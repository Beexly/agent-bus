Units: probability squared. Breakdown regime: as \(N_{\text{dropback}}\to 0\), the variance diverges and the estimate is not usable. A Wilson interval should be used instead of a normal approximation when counts are small.

Expected sacks per dropback:
\[
E[S]=\sum_{s=0}^{n} s\,\Pr(S=s)
\]
Units: sacks per dropback. Assumption: \(S\) is the discrete sack count on a dropback. This separates pressure from conversion; a team can have high pressure probability and low sack probability.

Run stuff rate:
\[
r_{\text{stuff}}=\frac{N_{\text{stuff}}}{N_{\text{rush}}}
\]
Units: probability, dimensionless. A “stuff” must be pre-defined, for example a rush gain at or below a fixed threshold. Breakdown regime: changing the threshold mid-sample invalidates comparability.

Offensive play-call entropy:
\[
H_{\text{play}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i\log_2 p_i
\]
Units: bits. \(H_{\text{play}}\to 0\) bits is the deterministic-tendency singularity: the defense can anticipate and tee off. \(H_{\text{play}}\to \log_2 k\) bits is maximum uncertainty for \(k\) play types.

Pressure model with covariates:
\[
\Pr(\text{pressure})=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\left(\beta_0+\beta_1 x_{\text{interior}}+\beta_2 x_{\text{edge}}+\beta_3 x_{\text{release}}+\beta_4 x_{\text{blitz}}\right)
\]
Units: the linear predictor is in log-odds; \(x\) variables are standardized covariates. Breakdown regime: complete separation occurs if a covariate perfectly predicts pressure; then the maximum-likelihood estimate diverges, \(\beta\to\infty\), and the model is unusable without regularization or priors.

Possession-count timing model:
\[
N_{\text{poss}}=\frac{T_{\text{game}}}{\mu_{\text{drive}}+\mu_{\text{transition}}}
\]
Units: possessions; \(T_{\text{game}}\) is in seconds, and \(\mu_{\text{drive}}\) and \(\mu_{\text{transition}}\) are mean seconds. Breakdown regime: as \(\mu_{\text{drive}}+\mu_{\text{transition}}\to 0\), \(N_{\text{poss}}\to\infty\) in the ideal model, which is physically bounded by the play clock and game rules; as the denominator grows, possessions collapse toward one.

Weather regime thresholds, stated as explicit assumptions rather than facts: if wind speed \(v_{\text{wind}}\gt 9\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) or precipitation rate \(R_{\text{rain}}\gt 5\,\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\), the deep-pass model is in a different regime, and deep-pass probability should be downgraded. Biomechanical analogy from continuum mechanics: stress \(\sigma=E\epsilon\), where \(\sigma\) is in pascals, \(E\) is in pascals, and strain \(\epsilon\) is dimensionless. It is an analogy for load, not a football metric. The exact football breakdown is statistical: small \(N\), complete separation, deterministic play-calling, and threshold-mismatched “stuff” definitions.

Trading Jeffery Simmons to CHI is a prior cause whose future effects are not uniform: it reduces Tennessee’s interior double-team absorption, changes the risk calculus for blitzing, raises Jacksonville’s incentive to slide protection inside, and alters Tennessee’s offensive clock preference. Scientific lineage: trench attrition analysis descends from several traditions. One is industrial load and fatigue analysis: repeated stress cycles reduce performance margin, and the relevant variables are stress amplitude, cycle count, and material/structural tolerance. That maps only analogically to offensive-line load, because football players adapt, rotate, and change technique. Another is queueing and possession economics: game clock and drive length determine how many chances each offense gets, so a defense’s weakness can be hidden by reducing opponent possessions. A third is information theory: disguise and tendency entropy determine whether a defense can anticipate pressure. A fourth is causal inference: the Simmons trade is an intervention, but the observed game is not a randomized experiment.

Trap two: compensation illusion. Blitzing more can raise short-term pressure while lowering coverage quality. If Tennessee replaces interior pressure with A-gap blitzes, Jacksonville can punish with hot routes, screens, and max protection. Trap three: clock illusion. Tennessee may shorten the game to protect its defense, but that also reduces its own comeback capacity and can hide rather than fix interior attrition. Trap four: weather overfit. The historical lesson is that interior defensive losses are often felt first in run-fit integrity and double-team absorption, then in coverage stress, and only later in sack totals. Sack totals lag pressure; pressure lags interior disruption; interior disruption lags the roster change. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under trench stress, coordinators often revert to familiar habits. Tennessee’s defensive coordinator may regress toward blitz-heavy simulated pressure because the four-man rush is less trusted without Simmons. Jacksonville’s offensive coordinator may regress toward quick game and max protection because the interior is the perceived threat. The quarterback may regress toward checkdowns if interior pressure arrives early. These habits are predictable, and predictability is exactly what the opposing coordinator attacks.

Counter-trap: Jacksonville can anticipate more A-gap pressure and use slide protection, running-back scans, and hot routes behind the blitz. Tennessee can counter by showing blitz and dropping out, using edge stunts to create interior movement without a true Simmons replacement, and playing coverage behind simulated pressure. The recursive levels are: level 0, Tennessee thinks “replace Simmons by committee”; level 1, Jacksonville thinks “Tennessee will blitz more”; level 2, Tennessee thinks “Jacksonville expects blitz, so we will simulate and drop”; level 3, Jacksonville thinks “Tennessee knows we expect blitz, so we will max-protect and take shots outside.” The equilibrium is mixed: Tennessee must blitz enough to generate pressure but not so much that Jacksonville’s protection and hot routes dominate.

Clock: the third player. If Tennessee’s offense sustains long drives, Jacksonville’s possessions fall and Tennessee’s defensive weakness is partially hidden. If Tennessee’s offense stalls, Jacksonville’s tempo rises and the interior attrition is exposed. Recursive theory of mind says Tennessee knows Jacksonville wants tempo, so Tennessee may slow the game; Jacksonville knows Tennessee wants to slow it, so Jacksonville may go no-huddle after first downs. The clock is not neutral; it is a strategic variable that changes the value of pressure and the cost of blitzing.

The pre-kickoff read is therefore conditional: Tennessee’s best path is credible four-man pressure plus moderate blitz rate plus clock control. Jacksonville’s best path is protection integrity plus tempo plus punishing blitzes with explosive passes. Crisis habit regression favors Jacksonville if Tennessee over-blitzes; counter-trap favors Tennessee if Jacksonville over-commits to max protection and loses receiving options. Clock favors whichever team can sustain drives. The decisive recursive question is whether Tennessee’s simulated pressure can preserve coverage integrity without Simmons.
