[1405.0352] Asymptotic Theory for Random Forests Asymptotic Theory for Random Forests Stefan Wager ?? Random forests have proven to be reliable predictive algorithms in many application areas. Not much is known, however, about the statistical properties of random forests. Several authors have established conditions under which their predictions are consistent, but these results do not provide practical estimates of random forest errors. In this paper, we analyze a random forest model based on subsampling, and show that random forest predictions are asymptotically normal provided that the subsample size s s scales as s ⁡ ( n ) / n = o ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ) − d ) s(n)/n=o(\log(n)^{-d}) , where n n is the number of training examples and d d is the number of features. Moreover, we show that the asymptotic variance can consistently be estimated using an infinitesimal jackknife for bagged ensembles recently proposed by Efron (2014). In other words, our results let us both characterize and estimate the error-distribution of random forest predictions, thus taking a step towards making random forests tools for statistical inference instead of just black-box predictive algorithms. But despite the ubiquity of applications, the statistical properties of random forests are not yet fully understood. Simple questions such as “What is the limiting distribution of random forest predictions as the number of training examples goes to infinity?” and “How can we get consistent estimates of the sampling variance of random forest predictions?” still remain largely open. This paper provides answers to these questions for a large class of random forest models. We study random forests with subsampling, defined as follows. Thus, our results let us take a step towards bringing random forest predictions into the realm of classical statistical inference. 1.1 Related Work Breiman originally described random forests in terms of bootstrap sampling: RF B ⁡ ( x , Z 1 , … , Z n ) = 1 B ​ ∑ b = 1 B T ⁡ ( x , Z b ​ 1 ∗ , … , Z b ​ n ∗ ) , \operatorname{RF}^{B}\left(x;\,Z_{1},\,...,\,Z_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}T\left(x;\,Z^{*}_{b1},\,...,\,Z^{*}_{bn}\right), (2) where the Z b ​ i ∗ ∼ iid { Z 1 , … , Z n } Z^{*}_{bi}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\text{iid}}}\,}\{Z_{1},\,...,\,Z_{n}\} form a bootstrap sample of the training data. Random forests of the form ( 2 ), however, have proven to be remarkably resistant to classical statistical analysis. As observed by Buja and Stuetzle 2006 , Chen and Hall 2003 , Friedman and Hall 2007 and others, estimators of this form can exhibit surprising properties even in simple situations. In this paper, we avoid these pitfalls by basing our analysis on subsampling rather than bootstrap sampling. Subsampling has often been found to be more easily amenable to theoretical analysis than bootstrapping [ 28 , e.g.,] . Although Breiman originally built random forests using bootstrap aggregation, or bagging [ 5 ] , subsampled random forests have also been studied and found to work well [ 9 , 30 , e.g.,] . Our present analysis is related to work by Hall and Samworth 2005 , who study the properties of subsampled k k -nearest neighbors predictors and show that they are consistent provided the subsample size s ⁡ ( n ) s\left(n\right) grows to infinity slower than n n . Most existing theoretical results about random forests aim to establish the consistency of random forest predictions [ 2 , 3 , 7 , 26 ] . There has been much less work, however, on understanding the sampling variance of random forests. One notable exception is a paper by Lin and Jeon 2006 that uses an adaptive nearest neighbors analysis of random forests to derive lower bounds for their variance. They then use this result to show that predictors of the form ( 2 ) converge slowly in n n if the leaf-size of the trees T T is small. Our work builds on recent contributions by Duan 2011 , Sexton and Laake 2009 and Wager et al. 2014 , who seek to move beyond a black-box analysis of random forests by providing variance estimates for their predictions based on ideas like the bootstrap and the jackknife. These papers, however, only gave heuristic and experimental support for their proposed approaches. Here, we provide rigorous results about the asymptotic distribution of random forest predictions. Finally, we note recent independent work by Mentch and Hooker 2014 , who establish asymptotic normality of generic subsampled ensembles in a small-subsample-size regime with s ⁡ ( n ) / n → 0 s(n)/\sqrt{n}\rightarrow 0 , assuming a non-degeneracy condition on the first-order effects of the base learner. Definition 4 . Consider a set of points X 1 , … , X s ∈ ℝ d X_{1},\,...,\,X_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} and a fixed x ∈ ℝ d x\in\mathbb{R}^{d} . A point X i X_{i} is a potential nearest neighbor (PNN) of x x if the smallest axis-aligned hyperrectangle with vertices x x and X i X_{i} contains no other points X j X_{j} . A predictor T T is a PNN predictor if, given a training set ( X 1 , Y 1 ) , … , ( X s , Y s ) ∈ ℝ d × 𝒴 \left(X_{1},\,Y_{1}\right),\,...,\,\left(X_{s},\,Y_{s}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y} and a test point x ∈ ℝ d x\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , T T always outputs Y i Y_{i} corresponding to a PNN X i X_{i} of x x . Similar distributions were also used for simulation experiments in [ 33 ] . 5 Conclusion Following Breiman’s lead, random forests have mostly been studied in terms of black-box metrics like cross-validation error or test-set error. Although measuring test-set error is of course important, a pure black-box evaluation can lack much of the richness of a more careful statistical analysis. It cannot answer questions like: “Are there some points at which the random forest is more stable than others?,” or “How much might the predictions change if we gathered more training data?” In this paper, we studied a random forest model based on subsampling with a subsample size satisfying s / n = o ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ) − d ) s/n=o(\log(n)^{-d}) . We established results about the asymptotic normality of random forest predictions, and showed how their asymptotic variance can be accurately estimated from data. Thus, our results help open the door to a more complete and nuanced statistical understanding of random forest models. References Bache and Lichman [2013] Kevin Bache and Moshe Lichman.
