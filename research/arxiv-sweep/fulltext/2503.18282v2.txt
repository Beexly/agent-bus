Abstract
Multi-object tracking, player identification, and pose estimation are fundamental components of sports analytics, essential for analyzing player movements, performance, and tactical strategies. However, existing datasets and methodologies primarily target mainstream team sports such as soccer and conventional 5-on-5 basketball, often overlooking scenarios involving fixed-camera setups commonly used at amateur levels, less mainstream sports, or datasets that explicitly incorporate pose annotations.
In this paper, we propose the TrackID3x3 dataset, the first publicly available comprehensive dataset specifically designed for multi-player tracking, player identification, and pose estimation in 3x3 basketball scenarios. The dataset comprises three distinct subsets (Indoor fixed-camera, Outdoor fixed-camera, and Drone camera footage), capturing diverse full-court camera perspectives and environments. We also introduce the Track-ID task, a simplified variant of the game state reconstruction task that excludes field detection and focuses exclusively on fixed-camera scenarios. To evaluate performance, we propose a baseline algorithm called Track-ID algorithm, tailored to assess tracking and identification quality. Furthermore, our benchmark experiments, utilizing recent multi-object tracking algorithms (e.g., BoT-SORT-ReID) and top-down pose estimation methods (HRNet, RTMPose, and SwinPose), demonstrate robust results and highlight remaining challenges. Our dataset and evaluation benchmarks provide a solid foundation for advancing automated analytics in 3x3 basketball.

https://github.com/open-starlab/TrackID3x3

Introduction
Recent advances in computer vision have developed sports analytics by enabling the automated extraction of critical information from video data, reducing manual intervention and enhancing analytical precision. Researchers have developed techniques for field registration (e.g.,
Theiner and Ewerth 2023
;
Gutiérrez-Pérez and Agudo 2024
;
Oo et al. 2023
), multi-object tracking (MOT, e.g.,
Yin et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Sun et al. 2024
), re-identification (Re-ID, e.g.,
Mansourian et al. 2023
;
Somers et al. 2023
;
Somers et al. 2024a
), and pose estimation (e.g.,
Xiong et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2022
;
Cheng et al. 2020
). These approaches also address common sports-specific challenges, such as frequent occlusions and the inherent similarity in visual features among players, which complicate accurate data extraction. These will also support applications ranging from automated decision making and scoring to comprehensive performance analytics by converting raw broadcast footage into structured data. However, the current research is predominantly focused on sports such as soccer, 5-on-5 basketball, and volleyball, while many sports—including 3x3 basketball—lack dedicated datasets and tailored methodologies.
Beyond soccer, the availability of tracking data enough for machine learning analytics in other team sports remains critically limited (note that, the datasets in sports computer vision areas are usually not sufficient for data analytics using machine learning). For instance, the only large-scale tracking dataset available for 5-on-5 basketball is derived from the 2015–16 NBA season, including 630 games; however, it has been outdated now and hinder further research progress. In contrast, soccer game state reconstruction
Somers et al. 2024b
—which integrates field detection, player tracking, and identification in broadcast videos—has been pioneering and remains essential for subsequent analyses such as event prediction
Decroos et al. 2019
;
Toda et al. 2022
;
Yeung et al. 2025b
;
Yeung et al. 2025a
, trajectory prediction
Teranishi et al. 2022
;
Lindström et al. 2020
;
Fujii et al. 2024a
, and reinforcement learning
Nakahara et al. 2023
;
Fujii et al. 2023
.
To enable such analytical techniques across sports, it is necessary to develop methods that can effectively process video footage captured by simple, fixed cameras (e.g., smartphones). Establishing robust image processing approaches for these conditions is expected to democratize automated analyses based on machine learning, including prediction with mathematical modeling
Spearman 2018
;
Kono and Fujii 2024
;
Iwashita et al. 2024
, evaluation
Umemoto and Fujii 2023
;
Ding et al. 2024
;
Fujii et al. 2024b
, and suggestion of optimal actions
Yeung and Fujii 2024
;
Nakahara et al. 2023
. In particular, 3x3 basketball offers a promising research opportunity: its reduced number of players simplifies occlusion problems and streamlines problem formulation in computer vision. However, a significant challenge remains in the absence of annotated datasets for player positions and pose estimation in this sport.
In this paper, we propose TrackID3x3 dataset and algorithm for multi-player tracking with identification and pose estimation in 3x3 basketball full-court videos (see Figure
1
and Table
1
).
Our dataset, the first publicly available resource specifically for 3x3 basketball, includes subsets captured from Indoor (fixed camera), Outdoor (fixed camera), and Drone (drone camera) scenarios, providing diversity in terms of camera perspectives and playing environments. We also propose a new task termed Track-ID task, a simplified version of the game state reconstruction (GSR) task
Somers et al. 2024b
, excluding field detection to specifically target fixed-camera scenarios, thereby enabling straightforward subsequent data analysis. Additionally, we establish baseline algorithms using a combination of recent tracking and identification methods. Our benchmark evaluation incorporates recent algorithms for MOT and 2D pose estimation.
The contributions of this paper are as follows.
(1) We propose a new comprehensive annotation dataset in 3x3 basketball full-court videos captured under diverse conditions—including indoor and outdoor with smartphone, drone, and consumer camera recordings—addressing the current lack of publicly available resources for this sport (Table
1
).
(2) We examined the feasibility of fully automating multi-player tracking with identification—a simplified variant of game state reconstruction
Somers et al. 2024b
—in 3x3 basketball, a sport that offers more favorable conditions compared to soccer.
Our experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of our baseline approach, achieving 80.75% in the Indoor dataset and 46.11% in the Outdoor dataset for the proposed TI-HOTA metric (described in Section
4
).
(3) We present and validate them as the pose dataset, an essential component for future subsequent sports analytics. We establish benchmarks for pose estimation on our dataset by evaluating a range of models from conventional methods to recent top-down transformer-based models, reporting over 77% in all datasets
in terms of mean Percentage of Detected Joints (PDJ).

Related Work
Multi-player tracking in sports.
In recent years, significant progress has been made in building datasets. Early efforts provided soccer player location data using multi-view camera
D’Orazio et al. 2009
, while subsequent datasets combined high-resolution panorama videos with local positioning system data
Pettersen et al. 2014
. Publicly available large-scale broadcast video datasets such as SoccerNet
Cioppa et al. 2022b
;
Somers et al. 2024b
and SoccerDB
Jiang et al. 2020
have further propelled research in player tracking. In basketball, datasets including APIDIS and SPIROUDOME
De Vleeschouwer et al. 2008
;
Lu et al. 2017
have been introduced, alongside other sports datasets for handball
Biermann et al. 2021
and volleyball
Ibrahim et al. 2016
. Recent datasets such as SoccerNet-Tracking
Cioppa et al. 2022b
and SportsMOT
Cui et al. 2023
utilize unedited broadcast footage, while SoccerTrack
Scott et al. 2022
and its extension TeamTrack
Scott et al. 2024
offer full-pitch multi-sport tracking. Furthermore, virtual environments like Google Research Football
Kurach et al. 2020
and Soccersynth
Qin et al. 2025
provide synthetic data that facilitate algorithm benchmarking.
In recent MOT, tracking-by-detection is a popular approach, where high-quality object detection forms the backbone for subsequent data association and tracking. The first step is the accurate identification of players and the ball in each video frame. Early methods relied on background subtraction, multi-scale feature detection, and edge detection techniques
Mackowiak et al. 2010
;
Cheshire et al. 2015
;
Direkoglu et al. 2018
;
Ali et al. 2012
. More recently, deep learning–based detectors such as RetinaNet
Lin et al. 2017
, CenterNet
Duan et al. 2019
, and various versions of YOLO
Redmon et al. 2016
have significantly improved detection accuracy. The second step associates detections across consecutive frames, which is challenging in sports due to frequent occlusions, rapid motion, and visual similarity among players. Motion-based methods, such as SORT
Bewley et al. 2016
and its extensions like BoT-SORT
Aharon et al. 2022
, employ Kalman filters and data association techniques to predict object trajectories. Deep appearance-based methods, exemplified by DeepSORT
Wojke et al. 2017
and techniques leveraging Deep EIoU
Huang et al. 2024
, use convolutional neural networks to extract robust visual features that help maintain consistent identities despite occlusion and similar uniforms. Recent work in soccer
Maglo et al. 2022
;
Majeed et al. 2024
, basketball
Arbués-Sangüesa et al. 2019
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2024
, hockey
Vats et al. 2023
, and multi-sports
Huang et al. 2023
, combines these strategies to tackle the unique challenges of sports tracking, including complex movements, similar appearances, and severe occlusions. In tracking and dataset diversity, 3x3 basketball reduces the number of players simplifies occlusion and other problems.
Player identification in sports.
Player identification, including Re-ID (the process of consistently matching and maintaining a player’s identity across different frames and camera views) is fundamental to sports analytics. In non-team sports, marathon, ultra running, and running datasets
Ben-Ami et al. 2012
;
Pei Xu et al. 2017
;
Penate-Sanchez et al. 2020
;
Suzuki et al. 2024
provide images or videos of athletes annotated with or without bib numbers. In team sports, re-ID datasets have been proposed in basketball
Van Zandycke et al. 2022
, ice hockey
Koshkina and Elder 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2020
, and soccer
Cioppa et al. 2022a
. Recently, the SoccerNet GSR dataset
Somers et al. 2024b
provided a comprehensive dataset integrating field registration, tracking, and player identification.
Player Re-ID methods have evolved from relying solely on traditional visual cues such as jersey color and number
Ivankovic et al. 2014
;
Napolean et al. 2019
. Early image-level methods focused on facial and local appearance features, while more recent work employs convolutional neural networks and part-based methods
Senocak et al. 2018
;
Akan and Varlı 2023
. Advanced techniques now incorporate contrastive learning and transformer-based models
Koshkina et al. 2021
;
Vats et al. 2022
;
Habel et al. 2022
, as well as multi-task frameworks for joint Re-ID, team affiliation, and role classification (PRTreID)
Mansourian et al. 2023
. In GSR
Somers et al. 2024b
baseline, player identification was performed with PRTreID
Mansourian et al. 2023
to produce team and role-aware ReID embeddings, and MMOCR
Kuang et al. 2021
for jersey number recognition. We use a general framework
(
Koshkina and Elder 2024
)
for jersey number recognition and color histograms for tracklet integration and team affiliation determination for both jersey and bibs situations.
Pose estimation in sports.
Pose information is fundamental in sports analytics because it provides detailed insights into players’ movements, enabling precise performance assessments. In general sports contexts, LSP dataset offers 1,000 images with pose
Johnson and Everingham 2010
, and the Sport Image dataset with 1,300 images
Wang et al. 2011
. The extensive MPII Human Pose dataset provides 25,000 images covering a wide range of human motions, including sports scenarios
Andriluka et al. 2014
, while the OCHuman dataset emphasizes occluded human poses with 4,700 images
Zhang et al. 2019
. For comprehensive whole-body pose annotations, the COCO-WholeBody dataset
Jin et al. 2020
has been developed, and domain-specific datasets such as 3DSP
Yeung et al. 2024
and the KTH Multiview Football Dataset II
Kazemi et al. 2013
provide valuable pose data for soccer players. In addition, the DeepSportLab dataset
Ghasemzadeh et al. 2021
offers a unified resource for ball detection, player instance segmentation, and pose estimation (672 frames) in basketball contexts. Our dataset provides 6701 frames of annotated pose (Table
2
).
Recent advances in 2D pose estimation have been primarily driven by top-down methods in sports (e.g.,
Yeung et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2024
), where the process begins with detecting individual persons followed by keypoint localization. The top-down paradigm consistently yields higher precision in keypoint estimation in sports scenes characterized by frequent occlusions and rapid motion. DeepPose
Toshev and Szegedy 2014
pioneered this approach, and subsequent work such as HRNet
Sun et al. 2019
further improved accuracy by preserving high-resolution representations throughout the network. More recent top-down systems, including RTMPose
Jiang et al. 2023
and SwinPose
Xiong et al. 2022
, leverage transformer-based architectures to further refine keypoint predictions. Although bottom-up methods like DeepCut
Pishchulin et al. 2016
and OpenPose
Cao et al. 2018
as well as emerging end-to-end approaches
Shi et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
provide efficient alternatives, we focus our evaluation solely on top-down methods because bounding boxes are given in our datasets.

Bounding boxes.
In the Indoor dataset, bounding boxes (bboxes) were provided by
Ichikawa et al. 2024

In Outdoor and Drone datasets, we also manually annotated bboxes about each player.
For all datasets, unique IDs for each player were assigned to enable tracking and identification over time.
Body keypoints.
In a subset of frames from three datasets, we manually annotated 10 human body keypoints per player. These keypoints include the head, shoulders, elbows, wrists, ankles, and the center, defined as the midpoint of the hips. Compared to commonly used 2D human pose estimation datasets that include 17 keypoints, we reduced annotation complexity and eliminated unnecessary keypoints by omitting the eyes and ears. Additionally, we simplified the lower body representation by retaining only the ankles and the hip midpoint.

TrackID3x3 Baseline Algorithms
Here, we introduce the TrackID3x3 baseline algorithms employed to evaluate the performance of tracking, identification, and pose estimation methods on our dataset.
First, we introduce a new Track-ID task, and its baseline algorithm, as well as tracking and 2D pose estimation baselines.
4.1
Track-ID Task
One of our experimental tasks is a Track-ID task to obtain the location and identification information of players on the court from fixed-camera videos. It is similar to the recent GSR task
(
Somers et al. 2024b
)
, but the most significant difference is that the camera is fixed and shows the entire court. Our task is relatively easy and accurate to carry out court localization by manually annotating keypoints on a single image in the video. Another important difference is that referees are not the object of this task; in 3x3 basketball, since the two referees only move back and forth in their respective areas, their movements have little effect on the state of the game, and there is less incentive to track them than in soccer. Since only on-court players are targeted, the maximum number of detections required in each frame is determined to be 6.
Note that there is a crucial difference between the Indoor and Outdoor datasets, in terms of player identification: the presence or absence of players’ jersey numbers. This is because the numbers were not printed on the bibs of the Indoor dataset, and this difference also accounts for the difference in sub-tasks performed between the two datasets. In addition, for the Outdoor dataset, the player is uniquely determined by two pieces of information: team affiliation and jersey number, because players on the same team wear the same color bibs with different numbers. Hence, the task formulation is also different in the two datasets.

4.2
Track-ID Baseline Algorithm
We propose a baseline to execute this task and verify its performance. Figure
2
shows an overview of the baseline algorithm. A detailed description is provided as follows.
Note that the court localization is manually performed and described in Section
3
.
People Detection and Tracking.
We use BoT-SORT-ReID
(
Aharon et al. 2022
)
with a pre-trained YOLOX
(
Ge et al. 2021
)
detecter without fine-tuning to track people in the videos. It is one of the strong image-based MOT methods in 5-on-5 basketball
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
.
Conversion to Court Coordinate System.
To convert the bboxes of the persons to court coordinates, homographic transformation was performed using the annotated court keypoints. The position coordinate of each tracklet is calculated using the midpoint of the bottom edge of the bboxes.
On-Court Player Classification.
Since the images include people who are playing and those who are not, it is necessary to distinguish them. We employ a rule-based heuristic method based on court position coordinates. For the Indoor dataset, in each frame, detections are filtered to those present in the court when the virtual court line parallel to the end line is moved one-meter away perpendicular to the end line, and if more than seven detections remain, the number is reduced to six by excluding those closest to the end line. For the Outdoor dataset, we exclude detections that never stayed in the court for more than 10 consecutive frames. Although this method often leaves seven or more detections without eliminating the referees, the subsequent process narrows the number of detections down to six.
Player Appearance Information Extraction.
As mentioned earlier, there is a difference between Indoor and Outdoor in terms of the presence or absence of jersey numbers. For the Indoor, the color histogram of the player image regions segmented by Dectron2
(
Wu et al. 2019
)
was calculated for each tracklet. Specifically, people segmentation was first performed for each frame, and the temporal median of the 3D color histograms (
8
×
8
×
8
8\times 8\times 8
bin) was calculated for the segmentation region with the largest area in the bboxes of each player’s tracklet. For the Outdoor, a tracklet-level jersey number recognition pipeline
(
Koshkina and Elder 2024
)
was used to predict the jersey number and calculate the color histogram of each tracklet using the intermediate product, the torso region images. This color histogram is similar in shape to Indoor and is the temporal median. The color histogram is the median value over up to 100 frames from the frame number with the smallest frame number, and its shape is the same as the Indoor. In both approaches, color histograms were used because of their lower computational cost than deep learning-based ReID features.
Tracklet Integration and Attributes Identification.
For the Indoor, the first step was the integration of tracklets: each frame after the first was scanned in chronological order, and if only one new tracklet appeared in a frame, it was merged with the missing tracklet ID; if two or more new tracklets appeared simultaneously, similar ones were merged based on the similarity of their 3D color histograms. Note that Jensen-Shannon divergence was used as the similarity measure.
Not only does the Indoor dataset not have information on the player’s jersey number, but all the players wear different colored bibs. Hence, the method of identifying the players’ team affiliation is limited to determining it practically from her relative position at the beginning of the video. Specifically, in the opening frame of the video, three pairs were created by pairing players detected at close distances to each other, with the one closer to the midpoint of the end line as the defense and the one farther away as the offense. The top, left, and right were determined relative to the midpoint of each pair’s position.
As for the Outdoor, it is more complicated: first, exclude tracklets whose jersey numbers could not be recognized. Next, the tracklet closest to a certain position in the first frame is the offense: the midpoint of the court line parallel to the end line in the case of videos where the offense starts at the top, or the midpoint of the free throw line in the case of videos where the offense begins with a free throw. Then, in the case of a video with the offense starting at the top, the tracklet closest to the determined offensive one is the defense, and then the two tracklets with the highest color histgram similarity to the offensive one are the offense and the rest are the defense. In the case where the video begins with a free throw, the two tracklets with the most similar color histograms to the first determined offensive tracklet shall be the offense and the remainder shall be the defense. For subsequent frames, if new tracklets appear, they will be assigned to the team with more player shortages (or defense if there is a tie). Finally, if the number of team members is greater than three in each frame, the tracklets are removed in order of length, starting with the shortest.
4.3
Tracking Baselines in Drone Dataset
In Drone dataset, we describe the tracking baselines. We extracted four sequences from a complete 3v3 basketball game video, capturing critical game moments from the initial ball possession to termination events such as fouls, violations, or out-of-bounds plays. The dataset comprises 4,999 frames across these four sequences, providing a comprehensive representation of dynamic player movements in competitive gameplay scenarios.
We implement two conventional baseline MOT algorithms: ByteTrack
Zhang et al. 2022
and BoT-SORT-ReID
Aharon et al. 2022
to track player movements.
For performance evaluation, we use HOTA
Luiten et al. 2021
metric due to its comprehensive ability to measure both detection and association performance. Additionally, we incorporate supplementary metrics including FN, FP, and ID switches to provide a more granular analysis of tracking performance.
4.4
Pose Estimation Baselines
In all datasets, we evaluate the performance of RTMPose
Jiang et al. 2023
, HRNet
Sun et al. 2019
, and SwinPose
Xiong et al. 2022
to estimate the human pose. These models are common baseline models for top-down 2D pose estimation with diverse architectures.
We use The Percentage of Detected Joints (PDJ)
Toshev and Szegedy 2014
, widely used for evaluating the accuracy of 2D human pose estimation. It measures the proportion of correctly detected keypoints based on a distance threshold relative to the torso diameter. This metric is useful for assessing pose estimation models with varying degrees of localization precision
Yeung et al. 2024
.
In this evaluation metric, a keypoint is deemed correctly detected if the normalized distance between the predicted and ground truth keypoints is less than 0.5. Furthermore, PDJ allows for the evaluation of pose estimation accuracy under different levels of localization precision by adjusting the threshold. The area under the PDJ curve (AUC) is computed to assess performance across various threshold values.
For normalization, we designate the nose as the head reference point and define the center point as the midpoint between the left and right hips. The torso length is determined as the Euclidean distance between the midpoint of the left and right shoulders and the center.

5.3
2D Pose Estimation Performance
We also evaluated the performance of 2D pose estimation models in our all datasets. We evaluated three models without fine-tuning as baselines. Since each player in our datasets is annotated with only 10 keypoints, while the baseline models output 17 keypoints in COCO
Lin et al. 2015
keypoints format, we select the corresponding 10 keypoints for evaluation to ensure consistency.
The evaluation results are summarized in Table
5
.
All models achieved their highest PDJ scores on the Outdoor dataset, but performance declined on the Drone dataset, with the Indoor dataset exhibiting the lowest accuracy. The lower performance observed in the Indoor dataset can be attributed to two primary factors.
First, the image resolution of the Indoor dataset is 1280 × 720, which is significantly lower than that of the Outdoor and Drone datasets (3820 × 2160). The reduced resolution may lead to blurred keypoints and reduced model accuracy. Second, the PDJ scores for head keypoints in the Indoor dataset are considerably lower. This discrepancy arises because the head keypoints in Indoor data are annotated on the upper forehead rather than the nose, which introduces a larger localization error than other keypoints.
A comparison between different keypoints across datasets, ankles, shoulders, and the center exhibit the most stable and high detection accuracy in all scenarios. In contrast, the elbows and wrists in the upper limbs demonstrate weaker performance, probably due to occlusion and motion blur.
In our TrackID3x3 dataset, RTMPose
Jiang et al. 2023
achieved the best performance in the Outdoor dataset, with an average PDJ of 89.43%. This result is highly comparable to the average PDJ of 89.51% reported for RTMPose in AutoSoccerPose
Yeung et al. 2024
.

6
Conclusion
In this paper, we proposed the TrackID3x3 dataset and its baseline algorithms in 3x3 basketball. Our benchmark experiments demonstrated robust results and highlight remaining challenges. Our dataset and evaluation benchmarks provide a solid foundation for advancing automated analytics in 3x3 basketball.
As a point to note in the experimental results, the biggest challenge in the Track-ID tasks and multi-object tracking in our dataset is ID switches due to frequent occlusions, especially track ID exchange between players or between players and referees, which is a major obstacle to accurate players’ data extraction. Addressing this challenge will be the highest priority. In addition, for the Track-ID task, both for prediction and ground truth, bbox-based position coordinates are not robust to arm extension and contraction and leg movement. This can be a hindrance in capturing the precise position and distance moved by the players. In future work, it may be a good idea to consider position coordinates that also utilize posture information
Yin et al. 2024
.
