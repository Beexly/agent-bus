[2604.21087] Model quality in football: Quantifying the quality of an Expected Threat model The recent growth in data availability in football has increased the risk of incorrect use of data-driven models, making guidelines on their validation and application necessary. The Expected Threat (xT) model is an accessible option for football organizations that start building in-house methods, yet little is known about how to assess its quality. The aim of this study is twofold: to examine how the model error depends on the number of game states and the number of training points, and to translate these results into guidelines for constructing and applying the model. Using the Markov chain underlying the model, we perform theoretical analyses and simulations to study the model error. These show that the model error is approximately log-normally distributed for a specified number of training points and game states. Additionally, we combine the simulations with expert consultation to establish the model error beyond which player evaluations based on the Expected Threat model become unreliable for scouting applications. From this, we derive rules of thumb to ensure the quality of an Expected Threat model before application, and we illustrate through an example how a validated model can be applied in practice. Because the approach generalizes to Expected Possession Value models, this paper illustrates a framework to systematically quantify model quality, despite the ground truth being unobservable in football analytics. This, despite the potential impact ranging from improved talent identification to supporting tactical analyses 10 ; 24 ; 18 . For football clubs starting to build in-house models, accessible models can be more easily integrated into their processes. However, the use of easily accessible methods poses the risk of incorrect use of data techniques 13 . Post hoc argumentation and drawing conclusions that might be based on insignificant differences can be possible pitfalls. Consequently, it is important that these models are accompanied by guidelines on their validation and how and when to use them 5 ; 13 . A data-driven model that has generally been embraced by practitioners at football organizations is the Expected Goal model, often shortened as xG 19 ; 2 ; 12 . It models the probability of a shot being scored given a specific in-game situation. This can be used to evaluate shooting performance, goalkeeper performance, or scoreline analysis. The Expected Threat (xT/xThreat) model 20 generalizes the xG model. The Expected Threat describes the probability of scoring a goal in the same possession chain given the current game state. As the game state is defined by the position of the ball, it models the progression of the ball during a possession chain. The Expected Threat model makes it possible to evaluate the quality of general open-play actions, whereas xG only considers actions related to shots. These action evaluations provide information for opponent analysis or player scouting. The framework uses procedures that are intuitive to practitioners 26 and can be visualized in its traditional low-dimensional setting as illustrated by 21 . This makes the Expected Threat model very accessible for football clubs starting to incorporate data into their decision processes. Despite its accessibility, little is known about the quality of the model’s estimations. Differences between model and and a game of football in reality arise both from modeling assumptions and from the model error. In this paper, we focus on studying issues related to the latter. More specifically, it is unclear how the number of different game states, indicative of the model complexity, and the size of the training set influence the errors of the threat values estimated by the model. Consequently, practitioners face challenges during model construction, application, and interpretation of the results. The aim of this paper is twofold: (1) to obtain insights into the dependence of the error of the Expected Threat model on the number of game states and the number of training points, and (2) to provide guidelines for practitioners to build and apply an Expected Threat model. First, we give a mathematical description of the model. We then use this to find theoretical results on the error of the model, which provide a rough error bound and insights into the model behavior. The error of the model is studied in more detail via simulations, which give an approximate distribution of the model error a for specified training sample size and number of game states. Additionally, we perform simulations to find what size of the model error is acceptable for applications in player scouting. Finally, we use these results to provide guidelines to obtain a validated model and we illustrate how such a model can be applied to compare player performances at the 2020 Euros. 2 Expected Threat model 2.1 Theoretical framework The Expected Threat, x ​ T ​ ( s ) xT(s) , is the probability that a goal is scored in the current possession chain in football, given the current game state s s . The concept was first introduced by 20 and later popularized in the blog post by 21 . In the traditional formulation, the game state is defined by the location of the ball during a possession. The possession chains in the game are described by a Markov chain. Then, the probability of scoring given the current state is a well-defined and can be estimated. Because the aim in football is to score goals, a good pass or dribble increases the likelihood of scoring. The quality of an on-the-ball action is therefore described in ( 1 ) as the difference in Expected Threat before and after an action. Here, the action a i = ( s i − 1 , s i ) a_{i}=(s_{i-1},s_{i}) moves the game from state s i − 1 s_{i-1} to s i s_{i} . In recent years, the quantity of collected data in football has increased, resulting in often proprietary data sets 17 ; 9 ; 13 . With historical data of in-game actions, the values of P ⁡ ( s ​ h ​ o ​ t | s ) P(shot|s) , x ​ G ​ ( s ) xG(s) , T s , s ′ T_{s,s^{\prime}} , or the vector g g and matrix T T can be estimated. This is generally done by counting occurrences and taking the empirical mean. The only unknowns in the model then remain the x ​ T xT -values, which are solutions to a linear system of equations. This can be solved with various methods, but, a specific version of the Value Iteration algorithm 3 is used that is intuitive to coaches and players. This Value Iteration algorithm calculates the probability of scoring within the next k k actions. Scoring a goal within one action can only be done by directly shooting and scoring successfully. Traditionally, the field is discretized and divided into different rectangles. The rectangle in which the ball-possessing player is located during a possession chain gives the game state 21 . A common discretization of the pitch is a 16 × 12 16\times 12 grid. This grid roughly follows the ratio between dimensions of the football pitch, and it results in a model with M = 16 ⋅ 12 = 192 M=16\cdot 12=192 game states. In this way, the discretization of the pitch in two dimensions determines the game states of the model. The discretization grid is a tuning parameter of the Expected Threat model. A model with a finer grid is better able to distinguish between different in-game situations and could be considered favorable. On the other hand, a finer grid results in more probabilities to estimate as the sizes of g g and T T grow. With a fixed size of the data set, this decreases the number of data points that can be used to estimate a single probability and lowers the quality of the estimation. As a result, the choice of the grid is a model tuning parameter that strongly influences the model precision and quality of the estimates. The traditional approach of defining game states solely by the ball-possessing player’s positions does not capture the full game dynamics that influence scoring probability 26 . 25 extended the traditional model by adding two extra dimensions: an indicator for a high or low ball and the number of defenders between the player and the goal. Because this rapidly increases the number of game states, they estimated the xG-values via an existing xG-model and used kernel density estimation to prevent overfitting. 8 added the xG-value as an extra dimension to the Expected Threat model. Based on the expected behavior of football players, they assumed extra structure on the transition probabilities. In this way, the Expected Threat model can be extended to better capture game dynamics. In a broader sense, the Expected Threat model is an Expected Possession Value (EPV) model. EPV models are often studied with continuous state spaces, and scoring probabilities are generally estimated through neural networks such as (convolutional) neural networks 6 ; 7 , graph neural networks 23 , or autoencoder-decoders 14 to estimate the probability of scoring. These models can be used to assess decision making 27 ; 15 ; 16 and assess defensive quality 11 . In these ways, they can provide extra insights for clubs with a dedicated data department. However, these more complex approaches require tracking data, which is less widely available. This makes it harder to apply those models in scouting processes, where tracking data is less accessible. Additionally, the described methods perform smoothing or regularization to deal with the increased number of the game states, which complexifies the mathematical analysis of the model error. Therefore, this paper focuses on the traditional formulation of the Expected Threat model, although most of is applicable to general EPV models. 2.4 Quantifying the model error The Expected Threat model aims to approximate the game of football as closely as possible. There are two factors that may cause a difference between model and reality. First, the model makes assumptions such as the Markov assumption and the definition of the game states. Players are influenced by the past, albeit slightly, and the threat on the field changes gradually instead of with few discrete steps. This causes a discrepancy due to the modeling assumptions. Second, an error is made within the context of the assumed model. Estimating g ^ \hat{g} and T ^ \hat{T} and applying Algorithm 1 can introduce deviations from the true values. Because this error arises within the context of the assumed model, we refer to it as the model error. Both influence how closely the Expected Threat model describes reality. Although we acknowledge that a finer grid decreases the influence of the model assumptions, our main focus in this paper is to study the model error, since only little is known about this aspect. To study the size of the model error, we assume that there exist true x ​ T xT -values for each set of game states S S of size M M . These values are the underlying probabilities of scoring a goal in each defined game state. When training an Expected Threat model, these quantities are estimated by taking empirical means and applying Algorithm 1 . The resulting model error is x ​ T − x ​ T ^ ( k ) ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] M xT-\widehat{xT}^{(k)}\in[0,1]^{M} , where x ​ T xT represents the true value and x ​ T ^ ( k ) \widehat{xT}^{(k)} the estimated value after k k iterations. The size of this error can then be measured with any vector norm: ‖ x ​ T − x ​ T ^ ( k ) ‖ \|xT-\widehat{xT}^{(k)}\| . In the context of this paper, the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm, defined as ‖ x ​ T − x ​ T ^ ‖ ∞ = max s ∈ S ⁡ | x ​ T ​ ( s ) − x ​ T ^ ​ ( s ) | \|xT-\widehat{xT}\|_{\infty}=\max_{s\in S}|xT(s)-\widehat{xT}(s)| , is a suitable candidate for three reasons. First, this norm does not require rescaling to compare models among different number of game states M M . Second, the probability of scoring a goal is close to 0 far away from the goal, and the error in estimating these xT-values is low in practice. By taking the maximal values, many small errors do not deflate the measured error when significant errors are made at critical states close to the goal. Finally, the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm provides an intuitive way of measuring the error of the model as it describes the maximal estimation error for each single state. This gives practitioners a clear handle to interpret the margin of error when applying the model at football clubs. The size of the model error is therefore measured as ‖ x ​ T − x ​ T ^ ‖ ∞ \|xT-\widehat{xT}\|_{\infty} . 3 Theoretical results The goal of training an Expected Threat model is to estimate the true underlying x ​ T xT -values, but errors can occur through the estimation process. The statistical error splits into the estimation error of the vector g g and the matrix T T . A probabilistic bound on these statistical errors can be obtained via concentration inequalities. These insights can then be combined to find one probabilistic bound on the size of the statistical error. For this purpose, a scenario is considered where there is a dataset of N N in-game actions, for instance, all actions of a season in a league. Let N g N_{g} denote the number of shots and let N m N_{m} be the number of actions that move the ball. Note that N = N g + N T N=N_{g}+N_{T} and that the proportion of shots is constant. This means N g ≈ p g ​ N N_{g}\approx p_{g}N and N m ≈ ( 1 − p g ) ​ N N_{m}\approx(1-p_{g})N , where p g ≈ 0.02 p_{g}\approx 0.02 is a constant that represents the proportion of shots in the dataset. Then, N N , N g N_{g} , and N T N_{T} are of the same order of growth. For the purpose of this analysis, it is assumed that the shots and actions are uniformly distributed over all in-game states, i.e., each state has on average N ¯ g = N g / M \bar{N}_{g}=N_{g}/M shots and N ¯ T = N T / M \bar{N}_{T}=N_{T}/M actions that move the ball on the pitch. 3.2.1 Split of statistical error The size of the statistical error can be split into a term representing the error in the goal probability and one representing the error in the transition probabilities. Concentration inequalities can be used to derive bounds on the estimation error of both. This results in bounds in terms of the number of game states M M and the size of the training data set N N . Combined, they provide a bound on the statistical error and can give insights into the impact of estimation errors in different model parameters. The goal-scoring vector, g g , gives the probability of scoring given ball possession in game state s s . These are then multiplied to find the value g ^ s \hat{g}_{s} . Equivalently, one could estimate g g by taking the empirical mean of all goals being scored from all visits to a state. Although the theoretical results provide insights into the behavior of the model, the typical behavior of the model error during application is unknown. 4 Simulations Two types of simulations were performed that provided insights into the typical behavior of the Expected Threat model. First, we performed a simulation study to obtain an approximate distribution of the model error given realistic values of both the training sample size N N and the number of game states M M . Second, we combined expert consultation with simulations to find which model errors result in reliable player ratings for scouting purposes. These simulations provided information on the size of the model error and the influence of model error on the quality of player ratings. With this, we will formulate rules of thumb to constrain the error of the Expected Threat model during application in Section 5 . 4.1 Methods For a specific grid of size M M , we trained an Expected Threat model on the openly available StatsBomb open data 22 . For the purpose of the analysis, these models were assumed to be the ground truth. Figure 1 : A visualization of the simulation scheme used in the study. Based on the Markov chain in the model, a new data set of size N N can be simulated by sampling different possession chains. A new model with the same grid can be trained, which estimates the ground truth model. As both the ground truth x ​ T xT and the estimated x ​ T ^ \widehat{xT} are known, the size of the model error can be calculated for the estimated model. The simulated model error is a bootstrap approximation of the model error. This process is visualized in Figure 1 and yields a situation where the error of the model can be assessed despite the general lack of ground truth in sports modeling 4 . To perform simulations, we used the openly available StatsBomb open data 22 to train the ground truth model. All available games from the Premier League, Ligue 1, Serie A, La Liga, and the Bundesliga were used. The events that did not describe passes, dribbles, errors, clearances, or shots were filtered out. This resulted in a dataset of approximately 4 million in-game events, or roughly 6.5 full seasons of a single league. The code for the simulations are available on GitHub . The computations were performed on the DelftBlue Supercomputer 1 . 4.1.1 Distribution of the model error Simulations were carried out to investigate the behavior of the model error in practice. Because the size of the model error ‖ x ​ T − x ​ T ^ ‖ ∞ \|xT-\widehat{xT}\|_{\infty} is the maximal absolute difference of all x ​ T xT values, it can only attain positive values. This means that an assumed statistical model for this error needs to have positive support. From the theoretical analysis, it is known that the error becomes larger for increasing values of M M and decreasing values of N N . As the error is positive, both the variance and mean can be expected to depend on N N and M M . A possible purpose of the Expected Threat model is to quantify the ability of a player to create threat within the setting of performance monitoring for a prospective transfer target. We used expert consultation together with simulations to determine when a model is of sufficient quality. The values of xT created per player are dependent on the context, like playing position and the league. The xT values of a specific player should thus be interpreted relative to players in a similar situation. Therefore, a natural way of quantifying the quality of players is to consider their quartile within a group of players playing at the same position and in the same league. In consultation with practitioners, it was established that a model error is acceptable if at most 10% of the players are assigned an incorrect quartile by the estimated model. Additionally, the difference between the assigned quartiles of the estimated and the ground truth model can at most be one quartile. To assure this is likely to hold, these situations should be satisfied with at least 0.90 0.90 probability. In short, a model is of acceptable quality if, with probability of 0.90 0.90 , less than 10% of the players are assigned the incorrect quartile with at most one quartile change. To find to which model error this corresponds, we performed simulations. Players playing as center forwards in the French Ligue 1 in the 2015/2016 season were considered. As these players played in the same competition and position, they roughly faced the same opponents and their values can be compared. Players with less than 300 minutes as a center forward were filtered out to ensure reliable estimates. This resulted in a group of 47 players whose ratings could be compared. A ground truth model with a 16 × 12 16\times 12 ( M = 192 M=192 ) grid was used to simulate 10,000 estimated models for different values of N N . Table 3 describes the different sizes of the training data set N N . For all resampled models, the xT created per 90 was calculated for each player. We then determined the quartile of this player performance and compared it to the quartile assigned based on the ground truth model. For the estimated models, the model error could also be determined. This resulted in a dataset containing 130,000 samples of the model error, number of incorrectly assigned player quartiles and the largest quartile change by a player rating. For instance, the model can be used to calculate the xT created per 90 minutes for different players. The xT created per 90 minutes is a performance metric that can be used to objectively compare transfer targets when trying to find a midfielder that is good at moving possession to high-threat situations. To responsibly use the Expected Threat model, one first has to ensure that the Expected Threat model is of sufficient quality and has an acceptable model error. The findings of this study can can be applied either to validate the quality of an existing model or to designing a new model of sufficient quality. For this purpose, we formulate rules of thumb based on the results in Section 4 . Lastly, it is illustrated how an Expected Threat model with an acceptable model error can be used for scouting purposes on publicly available StatsBomb data of the 2020 Euros 22 . 5.1 Model validity 5.1.1 Quality check of an existing model In case football clubs already have access to an existing Expected Threat model, it is beneficial to check whether the existing model is already of sufficient quality. The most likely scenario is that there is a specific data set available to train the model on. A natural question that arises is: what is the best number of game states M M given a training dataset of size N N ? When selecting the grid, there is a clear trade-off present. On the one hand, a small number of game states is desirable since it results in a smaller error in estimating the Expected Threat values. On the other hand, a model with more game states is better able to distinguish between different in-game situations, which reduces the error due to model assumptions as discussed in Section 2.4 . Hence, a finer grid could be favorable. Based on the results of the simulations in combination with expert consultation, we formulate the following rule of thumb. Select the most flexible model (highest M M ) such that the model error is smaller than M ​ E m ​ a ​ x = 0.0192 ME_{max}=0.0192 with 0.90 probability. This selection rule ensures that the model error is best able to distinguish between different in-game scenarios, while keeping the model error within an acceptable range. As an illustration, we consider a situation where 4 seasons of data are available for a specific league. This corresponds to 2.4 million data points. The quality of an on-the-ball action can be determined by the increase in threat or probability of scoring a goal, as described in ( 1 ). By evaluating the quality of the in-game actions performed by a player, ratings can be obtained describing the on-the-ball quality of a player. As an example, we use the Expected Threat model to find the midfielders who created the most goal-scoring threat at the 2020 Euros. This can, for instance, be used to aid in a scouting process to identify possible targets when searching for a threat-creating midfielder. The data used is part of the StatsBomb Open Data and is publicly available. For each on-the-ball event, the xT-value before and after an action is calculated. This was used to calculate the xT difference by an action as described in ( 1 ). The performance of the player was then obtained by summing the xT difference for every action of a player with a positive value and normalizing it to obtain the xT created per 90 minutes. A filter was applied that selected those who played most games as midfielder and played at least 300 minutes. Within this group, the quantiles were calculated for each player. The results are visible in Figure 10 . It shows that the xT created per 90 minutes varies between 0.055 and 0.336. Kai Havertz, Marcel Sabitzer, Mikel Damsgard, and Thomas Müller have the largest xT created and are in the fourth quartile (Q4), highlighted with dark green. Because of the relatively small number of games in the tournament, the differences cannot be interpreted as significant, especially not the small difference between Kai Havertz and Marcel Sabitzer. We conclude that these players were among the best at creating goal-scoring threat of all midfielders at the 2020 Euros. Figure 10 : The xT added per 90 minutes by midfielders with at least 300 minutes played at the 2020 Euros. On the other hand, Serhii Sydorchuk, Sebastian Larsson, and Albin Ekdal appear to be the worst-performing players according to this metric. However, possible biases in the data or the model should always be taken into account when evaluating the performances of players. In this specific situation, a selection bias is clearly apparent. Midfielders playing for countries that did not pass the group phase of a tournament most likely underperformed. Still, these were excluded because no reliable estimate of the xT created per 90 minutes could be made. Thus, the players not being included are likely the worst performing players, and the seemingly low-performing players in Figure 10 might have average performance. This highlights the importance of taking into account possible biases in the data. 6 Discussion The goal of this paper was (1) to gain insights into how the error of the Expected Threat model depends on the number of game states and the number of training points, and (2) to provide guidelines for practitioners to build and apply the Expected Threat model. We find that the model error behaves approximately like a lognormal random variable proportional to M 1.01 / N 0.51335 M^{1.01}/N^{0.51335} . In addition, simulations combined with expert consultations identify a maximal acceptable error of M ​ E max = 0.0192 ME_{\max}=0.0192 . Based on this, we established practical rules of thumb to find the appropriate number of game states ( M M ) or the desired number of training points ( N N ) when constructing a model. An application to the 2020 Euros further illustrates how player performance can be evaluated relative to comparable players and with awareness of potential biases. The theoretical analysis yields a worst-case bound on the model error of order M 3 / 2 ​ log ⁡ ( M ) / N M^{3/2}\log(M)/\sqrt{N} . Simulations showed that this bound is overly pessimistic. The model error is mostly determined by the errors in the goal-scoring probabilities, which are of a lower order than the errors in the estimated transition matrix. Consequently, the model error behaves approximately lognormal like ‖ x ​ T − x ​ T ^ ‖ ∞ = e − 2.0916 ​ M 1.01 ( N ) 1.0267 ​ e ε , where ​ ε ∼ N ⁡ ( 0 , 0.1782 ) . \|xT-\widehat{xT}\|_{\infty}=e^{-2.0916}\frac{M^{1.01}}{(\sqrt{N})^{1.0267}}e^{\varepsilon},\qquad\text{where }\varepsilon\sim N(0,0.1782). We conclude that the typical model performance is substantially better than the worst-case theoretical bound. The model error seems to be largely influenced by the estimation of the direct goal-scoring probabilities. High-threat states are rarely observed, because defending teams actively prevent ball possession there. With many game states M M and few data points N N , a lack of observed shots can cause the model to assign zero probability of scoring directly in these high-value states, causing large errors. Consequently, the most fruitful improvements to the Expected Threat model could be attained by enhancing the estimation of the goal-scoring probabilities. Leveraging existing shooting probabilities and xG values to estimate the corresponding quantities estimations will lead to more efficient estimators. Enforcing symmetry, applying smoothing techniques or incorporating prior knowledge could also boost the performance based on domain knowledge. The maximal acceptable error ( M ​ E max ME_{\max} ) of 0.0192 means that the absolute error of the x ​ T xT for each game state must be smaller than 1.92% for applications in player scouting. Existing models, such as that of 21 , might fall short of this threshold. Notably, the rules of thumb suggests that approximately 5.4 seasons of data are required for a 16 × 12 16\times 12 grid, far more than typically used in practice. Model validation methods such as visual inspection by domain experts and verification methods such as described in 4 can be performed to complement the rules of thumb described. Sufficient technical expertise is needed to perform this. Under these circumstances, less conservative values might be considered instead of M ​ E max = 0.0192 ME_{\max}=0.0192 . This highlights the need for caution when applying the Expected Threat or related possession value models. In the simulations, models trained on an open-access data set of roughly 4 million data points were assumed as the ground truth, which constitutes a limitation. These models can contain estimation errors and may be less smooth than the true underlying values. This might make the estimation task more difficult, and consequently, both the model error and the maximal acceptable error may be slightly overestimated. Future research with larger, probably proprietary, data sets could evaluate the exact impact of this effect. Within the broader set of Expected Possession Value models 6 ; 7 ; 23 ; 14 , there exist other models that incorporate additional in-game context. Like the Expected Threat model, these EPV models implicitly rely on the Markov assumption. The simulations and rules of thumb in this paper can be extended in a similar manner to such models as well, enabling a systematic assessment of model quality. In this way, this paper provides a framework to quantify model quality, despite the ground truth being an intangible object in football analytics. 7 Proofs of propositions and theorems The propositions and theorems in this paper were formulated with the ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} -norm.
