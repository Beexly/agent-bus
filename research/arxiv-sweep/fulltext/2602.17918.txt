[2602.17918] Distribution-Free Sequential Prediction with Abstentions Distribution-Free Sequential Prediction with Abstentions Jialin Yu Affiliation: Georgia Institute of Technology Email: yujl1@gatech.edu Moïse Blanchard Affiliation: Georgia Institute of Technology Email: mblanchard41@gatech.edu Abstract We study a sequential prediction problem in which an adversary is allowed to inject arbitrarily many adversarial instances in a stream of i.i.d. instances, but at each round, the learner may also abstain from making a prediction without incurring any penalty if the instance was indeed corrupted. This semi-adversarial setting naturally sits between the classical stochastic case with i.i.d. instances for which function classes with finite VC dimension are learnable; and the adversarial case with arbitrary instances, known to be significantly more restrictive. For this problem, [ 20 ] showed that, if the learner knows the distribution μ \mu of clean samples in advance, learning can be achieved for all VC classes without restrictions on adversary corruptions. This is, however, a strong assumption in both theory and practice: a natural question is whether similar learning guarantees can be achieved without prior distributional knowledge, as is standard in classical learning frameworks (e.g., PAC learning or asymptotic consistency) and other non-i.i.d. models (e.g., smoothed online learning). We therefore focus on the distribution-free setting where μ \mu is unknown and propose an algorithm AbstainBoost based on a boosting procedure of weak learners, which guarantees sublinear error for general VC classes in distribution-free abstention learning for oblivious adversaries. These algorithms also enjoy similar guarantees for adaptive adversaries, for structured function classes including linear classifiers. These results are complemented with corresponding lower bounds, which reveal an interesting polynomial trade-off between misclassification error and number of erroneous abstentions. 1 Introduction We study the classical online prediction problem in which a learner sequentially observes an instance x t ∈ 𝒳 x_{t}\in\mathcal{X} and makes a prediction about a value y t y_{t} before observing the true label. The learner’s objective is to minimize the error of its predictions y ^ t \hat{y}_{t} compared to the true value y t y_{t} . The problem has been extensively studied under two extreme scenarios: the stochastic setting where instances x 1 , … , x T x_{1},\ldots,x_{T} are assumed to be independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) usually from an unknown distribution μ \mu to the learner, and the general adversarial setting where no assumptions are made on the instance generation process. In the stochastic setting, also known as PAC learning [ 44 , 41 ] , simple algorithms can provably learn the best prediction function within function classes ℱ \mathcal{F} of finite Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension [ 43 , 44 , 41 ] , a combinatorial measure of complexity that is well controlled for many practical hypothesis classes, including linear classifiers. On the other hand, learning is severely limited for adversarial data. Specifically, learning in the adversarial setting requires the function class to have finite so-called Littlestone dimension [ 29 , 7 ] , which does not hold even for threshold function classes in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . 1 1 1 The threshold function class is defined as ℱ = { x ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] ↦ 𝟙 [ x ≤ x 0 ] : x 0 ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] } \mathcal{F}=\{x\in[0,1]\mapsto\mathbbm{1}[x\leq x_{0}]:x_{0}\in[0,1]\} . This significant gap has sparked substantial interest in intermediate learning models which aim to relax the i.i.d. assumption while preserving strong learning guarantees. This includes distributionally-constrained adversaries [ 37 , 10 ] and specifically the smooth adversarial model which imposes density ratio constraints on data distributions [ 37 , 25 , 6 ] . As an alternative model for non-i.i.d. data, another line of work focused on algorithms robust to adversarial corruptions in which an adversary may corrupt training [ 42 , 27 , 8 , 1 , 22 , 23 , 4 , 40 , 9 ] or test data [ 19 , 2 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 32 ] . A central challenge with adversarial corruptions is that achieving strong predictive performance typically requires imposing substantial restrictions on the adversary, such as limiting corruptions to a small fraction of the data or to specific classes of perturbations. To circumvent these limitations, allowing the learner to abstain on difficult or out-of-training instances has emerged as a fruitful workaround [ 17 , 14 , 21 , 26 , 15 ] . In practice, an abstention from the machine learning model could trigger a conservative default policy, for instance in the context of content moderation systems, or prompt human review/intervention in medical diagnoses or autonomous driving applications. In high-risk applications such as these, a cautious abstention is often significantly more desirable and cost-effective than a prediction error, and enables the model to selectively predict on instances for which it has high confidence—an approach sometimes referred to as “reliable learning” [ 28 , 26 ] . Distribution-free sequential learning with abstentions. We consider the sequential prediction model with abstentions introduced by [ 20 ] in which an adversary may inject arbitrary corruptions within the clean sequence of i.i.d. instances from a distribution μ \mu . In particular, the adversary may corrupt half (or all) of the data instances x 1 , … , x T x_{1},\ldots,x_{T} . On the other hand, the learner may abstain on instances which they suspect may be corrupted. For simplicity, we focus on the realizable binary classification case where all instances are labeled by a fixed in-class function f ⋆ : 𝒳 → { 0 , 1 } f^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\} . In other terms, we focus on a clean-label model [ 40 , 9 ] , where corrupted instances are still correctly labeled. In this context, the learner aims to always make correct predictions whenever it does not abstain, thereby ensuring reliability, while simultaneously avoiding abstentions on clean instances. Crucially, the learner incurs no penalty for abstaining on corrupted instances; in light of the fundamental limitations for adversarial learning even for realizable data [ 29 ] , this is essentially necessary to achieve meaningful learning guarantees when the data may contain a large fraction of corrupted examples. In this abstention sequential learning framework, [ 20 ] propose learning algorithms that guarantee both low misclassification error and few abstentions on clean instances for any function class ℱ \mathcal{F} with finite VC dimension, under the core assumption that the clean sample distribution μ \mu is known to the learner a priori. While these results provide strong theoretical evidence for the benefit of abstentions in the presence of adversarial attacks, the knowledge of μ \mu is an important practical limitation. Indeed, assuming access to the underlying data distribution significantly departs from classical results in the PAC learning and consistency literature, in which simple algorithms such as Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) or nearest-neighbor-based algorithms achieve desired learning guarantees without any prior distributional knowledge. Accordingly, recent work has aimed to remove distributional assumptions in other beyond-i.i.d. learning models, including smooth adversaries [ 13 , 11 ] . For the abstention learning problem itself, [ 20 ] show as a proof of concept that prior knowledge of the distribution μ \mu is not required for certain function classes: namely axis-aligned boxes and those with VC dimension 1 1 . This naturally leads to the following fundamental question for abstention sequential learning: Is learning with abstentions possible for general function classes (with finite VC dimension) without prior knowledge of the data distribution μ \mu ? Throughout this paper, we denote by 𝒳 \mathcal{X} the instance domain and let ℱ ⊆ { 0 , 1 } 𝒳 \mathcal{F}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}} be a class of measurable functions. We consider the sequential online learning problem with adversarial corruptions, where the learner may additionally abstain as introduced in [ 20 ] . Formally, the learning protocol proceeds as follows. Initially, an adversary fixes a distribution μ \mu over 𝒳 \mathcal{X} together with labeling function f ⋆ ∈ ℱ f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F} . The learner does not observe f ⋆ f^{\star} nor μ \mu . Then, at each iteration t ∈ [ T ] t\in[T] : 1. The adversary first decides whether or not to corrupt this round by selecting c t ∈ { 0 , 1 } c_{t}\in\{0,1\} . 2. If c t = 1 c_{t}=1 , the adversary selects an arbitrary instance x t ∈ 𝒳 x_{t}\in\mathcal{X} . Otherwise, if c t = 0 c_{t}=0 , the new instance is sampled x t ∼ μ x_{t}\sim\mu independently from the past. 3. The learner observes x t x_{t} and outputs y ^ t ∈ { 0 , 1 , ⟂ } \hat{y}_{t}\in\{0,1,\perp\} , where ⊥ \bot denotes an abstention. 4. Finally, the learner observes the (clean) label y t = f ⋆ ​ ( x t ) y_{t}=f^{\star}(x_{t}) . We emphasize that this setup corresponds to a realizable setting: the values y t y_{t} are always consistent with the fixed function f ⋆ ∈ ℱ f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F} even on corrupted rounds. In words, we consider mild corruptions which only affect the instance x t x_{t} but not the labelings. Remark 1 . As discussed within [ 20 ] , other models of feedback are possible. For instance, we will also consider a censored variant of this setup in Section 6 , where the learner only observes label y t y_{t} in step 4 when it has made a prediction i.e. y ^ t ≠ ⟂ \hat{y}_{t}\neq\perp . If the learner makes a prediction y ^ t ∈ { 0 , 1 } \hat{y}_{t}\in\{0,1\} , it incurs a misclassification cost if it makes a mistake: y ^ t ≠ y t \hat{y}_{t}\neq y_{t} . Conversely, if the learner abstains y ^ t = ⟂ \hat{y}_{t}=\perp , it incurs an abstention cost if the round was not corrupted: c t = 0 c_{t}=0 . We therefore focus on the misclassification error and abstention error on all T T iterations, defined by MisErr ≔ ∑ t = 1 T [ y ^ t ∉ { y t , ⟂ } ] and AbsErr ≔ ∑ t = 1 T [ c t = 0 ∧ y ^ t = ⟂ ] . \textsc{MisErr}\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\!\left[\hat{y}_{t}\notin\{y_{t},\perp\}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\textsc{AbsErr}\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\!\left[c_{t}=0\wedge\hat{y}_{t}=\perp\right]. In words, the learner aims to make few mistakes on the rounds where it predicts to minimize its misclassification error, while not abstaining too often on non-adversarial rounds to minimize its abstention error. Importantly, by construction, the learner is allowed to abstain on adversarially injected inputs for free. This allows for an arbitrary number of corruption rounds (e.g., T / 2 T/2 corruptions) without incurring a linear penalty in the objective, as opposed to the abstention model [ 16 ] , where there is a small but fixed cost on every abstention. Specifically, up to removing predictions of weak learners with large misclassification error, we may assume that all weak learners have at most M M misclassification error—for the abstention learning problem, we will use M = 𝒪 ⁡ ( d 2 ​ log ⁡ T ) M=\mathcal{O}(d^{2}\log T) since Theorem 7 guarantees the existence of a good weak learner with this misclassification error. With this property in mind, the high-level idea behind our procedure is as follows. Weak learners which do not abstain for at least s ≫ M s\gg M rounds make at least s − M s-M correct predictions and as a result, have small mistake per prediction average rate ≤ M / ( s − M ) \leq M/(s-M) . Therefore, assuming that we can focus on these weak learners with at least s s predictions, the fraction of incorrect predictions compared to correct predictions throughout all T T rounds is at most ≤ M / ( s − M ) ≪ 1 \leq M/(s-M)\ll 1 . In turn, majority-vote-inspired algorithms will achieve low misclassification error without having to abstain frequently. Since we do not know a priori which weak learners make s ≫ M s\gg M predictions, we instead use a deletion strategy: we ignore the first s s predictions of each algorithm for some adaptively chosen value of s s , which precisely removes weak learners with at most s s predictions. In practice, to achieve low misclassifciation error, our complete boosting procedure needs to perform log ⁡ L \log L rounds of such deletions. For completeness, we detail the algorithm in Section B.1 . 7 Conclusion In the sequential learning with abstention framework of [ 20 ] , we showed that achieving sublinear abstention and misclassification errors is possible for general VC classes without knowing μ \mu , for oblivious adversaries. We also identified structural properties of the function class ℱ \mathcal{F} —finite so-called reduction dimension —enabling learning against adaptive adversaries, which may be of independent interest for other abstention learning models. Together with corresponding lower bounds, these results show the existence of a polynomial-form tradeoff between abstention and misclassification errors. This work naturally leaves open the following two directions. Tight characterizations of tradeoffs between abstention and misclassification errors. At the high-level, our positive results for oblivious adversaries show that 𝒪 ⁡ ( T 4 ​ α ) \mathcal{O}(T^{4\alpha}) misclassification can be achieved while ensuring 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T 1 − α ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(T^{1-\alpha}) abstention error, leaving a gap compared to the corresponding Ω ⁡ ( T α ) \Omega(T^{\alpha}) misclassification error lower bound. The lower bound turns out to be tight for VC-1 classes for instances, but the situation is unclear for general VC classes. We note, however, that with further parameter information (e.g., which deletion parameter s ≤ s max s\leq s_{\max} to use within Delete procedures), slight modifications of the algorithm can yield stronger misclassification guarantees, opening potential opportunities for further improvements. Extending results to adaptive adversaries for general VC classes. Our guarantees for adaptive adversaries are limited to function classes with finite reduction dimension (and finite VC dimension). It remains open whether one can remove such constraints and achieve successful learning for all VC classes, which leads to the following open question: Can we achieve poly ​ ( d ) ​ T 1 − Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \text{poly}(d)T^{1-\Omega(1)} misclassification and abstention error for any function class with VC dimension d d , against adaptive adversaries? As a remark, the main limitation in our current approach is in achieving accurate estimations of the shattering probability from i.i.d. samples. Specifically, our approach reduces the problem to the case when the learner has a priori access to a few i.i.d. samples—say T 1 − Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) T^{1-\Omega(1)} —from the clean distribution μ \mu . If learning can be efficiently performed with few misclassifications in this setting, then our boosting procedure ensures learning guarantees for the general case in which the learner has no prior distributional information.
