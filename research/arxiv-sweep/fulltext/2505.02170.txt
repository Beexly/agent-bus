[2505.02170] Data-Driven Team Selection in Fantasy Premier League Using Integer Programming and Predictive Modeling Approach Data-Driven Team Selection in Fantasy Premier League Using Integer Programming and Predictive Modeling Approach Danial Ramezani Thanks: danialramezani988@gmail.com Abstract Fantasy football is a billion-dollar industry with millions of participants. Constrained by a fixed budget, decision-makers draft a squad whose players are expected to perform well in the upcoming weeks to maximize total points. This paper proposes novel deterministic and robust integer programming models that select the optimal starting eleven and the captain. A new hybrid scoring metric is constructed using an interpretable artificial intelligence framework and underlying match performance data. Several objective functions and estimation techniques are introduced for the programming model. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first study to approach fantasy football through this lens. The models’ performance is evaluated using data from the 2023/24 Premier League season. Results indicate that the proposed hybrid method achieved the highest score while maintaining consistent performance. Utilizing the Monte Carlo simulation, the strategic choice of averaging techniques for estimating cost vectors, and the proposed hybrid approach are shown to be effective during the out-of-sample period. This paper also provides a thorough analysis of the optimal formations and players selected by the models, offering valuable insights into effective fantasy football strategies. Keywords: Fantasy Football, Data-Driven Optimization, Simulation, Time Series Forecasting, Machine Learning, Robust Optimization, XAI 1 Introduction Fantasy sports are a billion-dollar industry, with the market size expected to grow to 84.98 billion US dollars by 2032 [ 1 ] . Fantasy football (soccer) is an online game played by over 11 million users in 2025 in the Fantasy Premier League (FPL) alone [ 2 ] . The goal is to select 15 players—two goalkeepers, five defenders, five midfielders, and three forwards—while not spending more than 100 million pounds. Each football player has a transfer cost, which is dynamically determined based on historical performance. The team with the highest total points from the starting eleven is the winning team of the week. Generally, points earned by reserve players are not counted, although special cards, which can be used once per season, offer additional privileges and can increase the team score occasionally, including bench boost card. Players compete against each other for rewards. While there are numerous strategies to approach this game, the most straightforward one is to select a reliable team that has performed well and ranked in the top percentile in the past, hoping that it will continue to perform well in the future. Interestingly, fantasy football has attracted attention from researchers across different disciplines. These include qualitative studies, such as examining the impact of fantasy games on the sales of the underlying products [ 3 ] , game attendance [ 4 ] , and the mental health of gamers [ 5 ] , as well as various quantitative studies. The emergence of publicly accessible datasets, facilitated by advancements in artificial intelligence and large language models, has significantly simplified the process of conducting quantitative research on this topic, allowing deeper insights into the impact of such games. 1.1 Literature Review Becker and Sun [ 6 ] presented a mixed-integer programming model for optimizing fantasy football draft selections and weekly lineup management in the NFL, using historical data and performance prediction. They proposed a comprehensive strategy to enhance a fantasy team’s chances of winning throughout a season. Lutz [ 7 ] employed neural networks and support vector machines to predict NFL Quarterbacks’ scores and suggested approaches to increase the prediction accuracy. Matthews et al. [ 8 ] presented a sequential approach for the FPL, utilizing belief-state Markov decision process and tractable Bayesian Q-learning algorithm, while modeling the problem using a multi-dimensional knapsack packing problem. Their automated approach achieved competitive performance, ranking around the top percentile when pitted against millions of human players. Steenkiste [ 9 ] deployed linear regression, random forests, and multivariate adaptive regression splines to predict the performance of the NFL quarterbacks for next week based on historical data. Gupta [ 10 ] proposed a linear mathematical model to select the total number of players in the squad. However, the formulation is simplistic and does not account for all constraints or the optimal formation. A hybrid time series model, a combination of a recurrent neural network and an autoregressive integrated moving average, is used to estimate the cost vector of the objective function. Bangdiwala et al. [ 11 ] studied machine learning models to predict the score of each player. They stated that linear regression is superior to other models. Bhatt et al. [ 12 ] analyzed Twitter posts for captain selection. Results indicate that the wisdom of the crowd system performed superior to expert analysts. Pokharel et al. [ 13 ] studied players’ selection and transfer strategy, considering return on investment, using an xgboost regression model. They also analyzed the impact of the midweek cup fixtures on the performance of the teams. Lombu et al. [ 14 ] compared the performance of a convolutional neural network and a long short-term memory on predicting the performance of the players using the data from their last five games. They indicated that the latter network performed better. Presenting an optimization approach and forecasting methods, Venter and van Vuuren [ 15 ] performed among the top 4% ranking in the 2021/22 season. Khamsan and Maskat [ 16 ] investigated methods for handling highly imbalanced output classes when predicting FPL virtual player price changes, using machine learning techniques. They used FPL as a real-world case study, where player prices fluctuate based on real-game performance and transfer activity, creating a dataset heavily skewed toward unchanged prices. Aribo [ 17 ] studied the application of the genetic algorithm to optimize team selection and budget management in FPL. 1.2 Contribution of the Paper Even though various papers have studied fantasy football, the majority focus on predicting players’ points for the upcoming week(s). Rarely is a solid mathematical model introduced to realistically model the player selection process. In this paper, novel deterministic and robust integer programming models are presented to select the optimal starting eleven, accurately modeling constraints of FPL. This study also analyzes the impact of various objective functions on the performance of the optimal teams and systematically compares them. Furthermore, another key contribution of this paper is the development of a hybrid cost estimation approach using explainable artificial intelligence to define a better score metric. Finally, this study provides a thorough analysis of the formations and players selected by the mathematical models. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first study to approach fantasy football through such an integrated and data-driven optimization framework. 1.3 Structure of the Paper The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides insights about the dataset used in this study. Although there are some common features for all positions, the performance of players in each position is evaluated with different metrics, as stated on [ 18 ] . Playing for more than 60 minutes, for example, grants two points to any player, regardless of the player’s position, or an equal amount of points will be deducted from players who scored own goals or received cards from referees. On the other hand, it is inappropriate to compare defenders with attackers using the same criteria; thus, each position is assessed using distinct metrics. The FPL dataset [ 19 ] is used in this study. All features are explained in [ 20 ] . For instance, BPS is defined as follows: ”The Bonus Points System (BPS) uses a range of stats to create a BPS score for each player. The three best performing players in each match will be awarded bonus points.” Given this definition, it is clear that some features not only do not contribute to the total points target—the target variable of interest—but are also influenced by it; therefore, they should be removed. Some other features are also irrelevant or or represent a combination of other features, such as the ICT index, which is defined as ”a Statistical index developed specifically to assess a player as an FPL asset, combining Influence, Creativity and Threat scores”. This correlation is weaker for defenders and stronger for goalkeepers. There is a stronger relationship between the goalkeepers’ value and their total points compared to other positions, indicating that higher-priced goalkeepers tend to contribute more points to teams. Unlike stochastic programming, which requires perfect knowledge of the distribution of the uncertain parameters and scenario generation techniques, robust optimization focuses on ensuring that the model solution remain feasible under the worst-case realization of uncertainty. The general form of a robust optimization problem is: min x ∈ 𝒳 ⁡ max u ∈ 𝒰 ⁡ f ⁡ ( x , u ) \min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\;\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}f(x,u) (9) Where x x is the decision vector in a feasible set 𝒳 \mathcal{X} , u ∈ 𝒰 u\in\mathcal{U} represents the uncertain parameters within the uncertainty set 𝒰 \mathcal{U} , and f ⁡ ( x , u ) f(x,u) is the objective function affected by uncertainty. 3.1.1 Robust Formulation with Box Uncertainty Let the actual expected points be uncertain and lie in a box uncertainty set: 𝒰 = { c ∈ ℝ n : c j ∈ [ c ¯ j − d j , c ¯ j + d j ] ​ ∀ j } , \mathcal{U}=\left\{c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:c_{j}\in[\bar{c}_{j}-d_{j},\bar{c}_{j}+d_{j}]\;\;\forall j\right\}, (10) Where c ¯ j \bar{c}_{j} is the nominal expected score and d j ≥ 0 d_{j}\geq 0 is the uncertainty margin for player j j . In this setting, the goal is to maximize the worst-case total score under uncertain c j c_{j} . The objective of the aforementioned model can be written as: max x , y ⁡ min ⁡ ∑ j = 1 n c ∈ 𝒰 ⁡ c j ​ ( x j + y j ) . \max_{x,y}\min_{c\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}(x_{j}+y_{j}). (11) This formulation ensures that the selected team maximizes the worst-case score, making the solution robust against possible overestimation in the expected scores. 4 Estimating Cost Vectors This section explains the methods and approaches used to estimate the cost vectors. The data used in subsections 4.1 , 4.2 , and 4.3 is a simplified version of the aforementioned dataset. For these methods, only the total points of each player in every gameweek are used, along with their value, position, name, and team. Additional features are incorporated in subsections 4.4 and 4.5 , which are described in detail in subsection 4.4 . The dataset used in this study contains each player’s records for all 38 gameweeks. A breaking point is selected to divide the data into in-sample and out-of-sample sets: The model is trained on the in-sample data, and the resulting decisions are evaluated using the out-of-sample data. Since the model requires a single value for each player’s performance, the following methods aim to generate one representative value for each player based on their performance up to the breaking point. 4.1 Averaging Methods Three averaging methods are used to estimate the expected points based on historical data. Given a time series { p 1 , p 2 , … , p N } \{p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc,p_{N}\} representing a player’s points in each week, two methods are considered for computing an average: 4.1.1 Simple Average The simple average treats all weeks equally and is computed as: Simple Average = 1 N ​ ∑ i = 1 N p i \text{Simple Average}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i} (12) Where N N is the total number of weeks. 4.1.2 Weighted Average The weighted average assigns more importance to recent performances. The weights increase linearly from the oldest to the newest week. Specifically: The weight i i is assigned to the i i -th point ( p i p_{i} ), where i = 1 , 2 , … , N i=1,2,\dotsc,N . The weights are, then, normalized so that they sum to 1: w i = i ∑ j = 1 N j w_{i}=\frac{i}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}j} (13) Then, the weighted average is: Weighted Average = ∑ i = 1 N w i ​ p i \text{Weighted Average}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}p_{i} (14) In this implementation the most recent week (week N N ) receives the highest weight, and the oldest week (week 1 1 ) receives the lowest weight. 4.1.3 Exponential Smoothing The third method uses Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing to estimate the expected points. This technique models the time series data by capturing level and trend components. Given a time series { p 1 , p 2 , … , p N } \{p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc,p_{N}\} representing a player’s points in each week, an additive trend model is fitted. After fitting the model, forecasts are generated for the remaining weeks (up to gameweek 38). The model uses an additive trend without a seasonal component; smoothing parameters and initial states are estimated automatically. Forecasts are generated for the remaining weeks until gameweek 38. 4.1.4 Simulation The performance of the players depends on their historical records. In this subsection, both parametric and non-parametric simulation techniques are employed to estimate total points. For the non-parametric approach, empirical bootstrapping is used to pick samples randomly with replacement from the historical scores to estimate future values. This method makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution of points. The formations chosen by the hybrid, weighted average, Monte Carlo, ICT and robust ICT teams are all 3-5-2, while the linear regression team prefers 3-4-3 and, surprisingly, the ARIMA team opt for 5-4-1 formation. This is unexpected, given that defenders generally contribute the least value to a team. Interestingly, the teams using the 3-5-2 formation tend to choose expensive players or those with higher expected goal contributions as captains (Bukayo Saka was selected as captain more often than any other player), while the other two teams preferred to captain defenders. Notably, Ollie Watkins is selected in all teams, and he is also selected as a captain in the proposed hybrid method. The official Premier League website even posted an article about Watkins’ performance in gameweek 27 with the title ”GW27 stats: Brave FPL managers rewarded for captaining Watkins [ 21 ] ”. Each model selects a different goalkeeper, except for the Monte Carlo simulation and the robust ICT team, both of which picked Chelsea’s goalkeeper. Keepers are generally selected from teams that conceded more goals and have a negative goal difference, except for the ARIMA team, for which Ederson is selected. This may be explained by the total number of saves, as goalkeepers from weaker defensive teams typically face more shots, assuming a positive correlation between shots on target and saves—which is not far-fetched. Defenders, on the other hand, are not selected from those teams in a radical manner, and models showed moderation in selection. Arsenal defenders were more interesting for models, and those who selected them performed better. Bukayo Saka and Phil Foden are also consistently picked by the models as midfielders. While most fantasy players tend to have Mohamed Salah or Erling Haaland on their team, it seems that these players are too expensive for models to select. Only the proposed hybrid method selected Haaland, whereas Salah is picked by both weighted average and Monte Carlo simulation teams. 6 Conclusion and Future Research In this study, a novel integer programming model, along with its robust formulation, is proposed that is capable of precisely modeling the team selection process in Fantasy Premier League. The integer model, by default, maximizes the expected score of players of the team, determining both players and the captain. In addition to the model, numerous forecasting, averaging, hybrid, and simulation methods—as well as two alternative functions—are presented to estimate or replace the objective function. The presented hybrid technique makes use of underlying features, identified using Shapley values, that are not included in the game’s official scoring and defines new scores based on them. Results indicate that the weighted average of expected points and Monte Carlo simulation technique (almost) consistently outperformed other methods in the out-of-sample period, and the novel hybrid approach produced the highest score throughout the period. Among various models, the 3-5-2 formation was the most favorable, and it appears that models prefer to pick more midfielders. The goal of this study was to draft a team that performs well above average without using transfers or special cards. In future studies, however, one can improve the model by modeling those cards. Additionally, it is possible to use a rolling horizon approach in combination with the model introduced in this paper to simulate transferring policies. This work did not account for dynamic captaincy approach as it was outside the defined scope; nevertheless, this may produce better results. More restrictive constraints for selecting goalkeepers might select better-performing goalkeepers. Last but not least, in this paper, the dataset is issued directly by the Fantasy Premier League. Additional third-party datasets containing match-level performance of players likely show great performance comparable to this paper, or even better, depending on the quality of the dataset. Therefore, it is strongly recommended to investigate the relationship between such external datasets and player point outcomes, using this paper’s hybrid method as a basis for comparison.
