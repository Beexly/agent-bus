[2504.01781] Proper scoring rules for estimationand forecast evaluation Proper scoring rules for estimation and forecast evaluation Kartik Waghmare Johanna Ziegel Thanks: ETH Zurich, Seminar for Statistics, Switzerland, kartik.waghmare@math.ethz.ch,ziegel@stat.math.ethz.ch Abstract Proper scoring rules have been a subject of growing interest in recent years, not only as tools for evaluation of probabilistic forecasts but also as methods for estimating probability distributions. In this article, we review the mathematical foundations of proper scoring rules including general characterization results and important families of scoring rules. We discuss their role in statistics and machine learning for estimation and forecast evaluation. Furthermore, we comment on interesting developments of their usage in applications. 1 Introduction In recent years, proper scoring rules have emerged as a powerful general approach for estimating probability distributions. In addition to significantly expanding the range of modeling techniques that can be applied in practice, this has also substantially broadened the conceptual understanding of estimation methods. Originally, proper scoring rules were conceived in meteorology as summary statistics for describing the performance of probabilistic forecasts ( Murphy and Winkler 1984 ) , but they also play an important role in economics as tools for belief elicitation ( Schotter and Trevino 2014 ) . A probabilistic forecast is a probability distribution over the space of the possible outcomes of the future event that is stated by the forecaster. The simplest and most popular case of probabilistic forecasts arises when the outcome is binary, so the probabilistic forecast reduces to issuing a predictive probability of success. Brier 1950 was the first to consider the problem of devising a scoring rule which could not be “played” by a dishonest forecasting agent. He introduced the quadratic scoring rule and showed that it incentivizes a forecasting agent to state his most accurate probability estimate when faced with uncertainty. McCarthy 1956 1 1 1 better known as the designer of the Lisp programming language. was the first to clearly state the concept of propriety as it is understood today and stated as Equation 1 . Consider a forecasting tournament which consists of a forecasting agent issuing a probabilistic forecast ℙ \mathbb{P} for a random variable Y Y and then receiving a penalty S ⁡ ( ℙ , y ) S(\mathbb{P},y) according to the forecast ℙ \mathbb{P} and the realization y y of Y Y . If the forecasting agent is rational and believes that Y ∼ ℚ Y\sim\mathbb{Q} for some ℚ \mathbb{Q} , then it would issue the forecast ℙ \mathbb{P} so as to mimimize the expected value S ⁡ ( ℙ , ℚ ) = 𝔼 Y ∼ ℚ ​ [ S ⁡ ( ℙ , Y ) ] S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathbb{Q}}[S(\mathbb{P},Y)] of its penalty. In order to compel the agent to reveal its true belief ℚ \mathbb{Q} , we must choose S S such that S ⁡ ( ℙ , ℚ ) S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}) is minimized at ℙ = ℚ \mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q} . It follows that we must have S ⁡ ( ℚ , ℚ ) ≤ S ⁡ ( ℙ , ℚ ) S(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q})\leq S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}) (1) for every ℙ , ℚ \mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q} (with equality if and only if ℙ = ℚ \mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q} ). In other words, S S should be a (strictly) proper scoring rule , see Definition 4 . Example 1 (Brier score) . For a binary outcome y ∈ { 0 , 1 } y\in\{0,1\} , the Brier or quadratic score S Brier ​ ( ℙ , y ) = ( p − y ) 2 , S_{\mathrm{Brier}}(\mathbb{P},y)=(p-y)^{2}, where p p is the predicted probability for the event { y = 1 } \{y=1\} , is a strictly proper scoring rule ( Brier 1950 ) . During the 1970s, proper scoring rules were developed into a principled approach for the assessment of probabilistic forecasts with applications in meteorology, economics and psychology ( Murphy and Winkler 1970 ) and as a foundation for the theory of subjective probability ( Savage 1971 ) . McCarthy 1956 produced a general characterization of proper scoring rules for finite outcome spaces. His characterization was later extended by Hendrickson and Buehler 1971 to absolutely continuous measures on the real line, which was followed by a complete characterization by Gneiting and Raftery 2007 for general measurable spaces, see Theorem 12 . Proper scoring rules have long been recognized as elicitation and aggregation mechanisms for subjective opinion in economics and other social sciences ( Jensen and Peterson 1973 ) . In fact, many interesting developments such as the spherical scoring rule originate there ( Roby 1964 ) . Tetlock 2005 provides a popular account of studies on the accuracy of probabilistic forecasts concerning political events by subject-matter experts. Another interesting application is the crowd-sourcing of forecasts from multiple human subjects via forecasting tournaments such as the Good Judgement Open and Metaculus, which evaluate forecasts using the Brier and logarithmic scores, respectively ( GJOpen 2025 ; Metaculus 2025 ) . A fascinating analysis of the literature on proper scoring rules in applications from 1950 to 2015 is given by Carvalho 2016 . The literature knows both orientations in that a proper scoring rule S ⁡ ( ℙ , ℚ ) S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}) should be minimized at ℙ = ℚ \mathbb{P}=\mathbb{Q} or that it should be maximized. The influential paper by Gneiting and Raftery 2007 assumes that better forecasts correspond to higher scoring rule values, but most of the more recent literature has adopted the convention in Equation 1 . With a negative orientation, scoring rules should maybe be called loss functions, but it is not in our power to change the naming convention. Some parts of the literature order the arguments of proper scoring rules differently, that is, it would be S ⁡ ( y , ℙ ) S(y,\mathbb{P}) instead of S ⁡ ( ℙ , y ) S(\mathbb{P},y) . We mention this since it may cause confusion when dealing with expected scores S ⁡ ( ℙ , ℚ ) S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}) . “Scoring rule” is sometimes abbreviated to “score”, which may cause confusion, since “score” typically refers to the gradient of the logarithmic density in statistics, machine learning and econometrics. However, for some classes of proper scoring rules such as kernel scores (Section 3.1 ) the term is established by now. 1.1 Proper scoring rules in estimation Proper scoring rules present a general and nuanced approach to the problem of estimating probability distributions, which allows for taking into account computational and statistical considerations relevant to the application. If we think of ℚ \mathbb{Q} as the data distribution and ℙ \mathbb{P} as a model distribution, then Equation 1 can be interpreted as saying that the expectation S ⁡ ( ⋅ , ℚ ) = 𝔼 Y ∼ ℚ ​ [ S ⁡ ( ⋅ , Y ) ] S(\cdot,\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mathbb{Q}}[S(\cdot,Y)] is minimized precisely when the model and data distributions are equal. It is reasonable to expect that given an iid sample { Y j } j = 1 n \smash{\{Y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}} from ℚ \mathbb{Q} with empirical distribution ℚ ^ n \hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{n} , the minimizer of the empirical mean S ⁡ ( ⋅ , ℚ ^ n ) = ( 1 / n ) ​ ∑ j = 1 n S ⁡ ( ⋅ , Y j ) S(\cdot,\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{n})=(1/n)\sum_{j=1}^{n}S(\cdot,Y_{j}) would give reasonably good estimates of ℚ \mathbb{Q} under appropriate regularity conditions. Scoring rule minimization can thus be employed for estimating probability distributions. For example, let { ℙ θ } θ ∈ Θ \{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta} be a parametric family of distributions and assume that ℚ = ℙ θ ∗ \mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{\ast}} for some θ ∗ ∈ Θ \theta_{\ast}\in\Theta . Consider the estimator θ ^ = arg ​ min θ ∈ Θ ⁡ [ 1 n ​ ∑ j = 1 n S ⁡ ( ℙ θ , Y j ) ] = arg ​ min θ ∈ Θ ⁡ S ​ ( ℙ θ , ℚ ^ n ) , \hat{\theta}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},Y_{j})\right]=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}S(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}), where S S is a strictly proper scoring rule. This is known as minimum scoring rule inference , of which the classical maximum likelihood inference is a special case where S = S log S=S_{\mathrm{log}} is the logarithmic score of Example 2 ( Dawid et al. 2016 ) . Strict propriety ensures that if θ ^ \hat{\theta} was minimizing the limit of S ⁡ ( ℙ θ , ℚ ^ n ) S(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\hat{\mathbb{Q}}_{n}) instead, which is the expectation S ⁡ ( ℙ θ , ℙ θ ∗ ) = S ⁡ ( ℙ θ , ℚ ) S(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{\ast}})=S(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q}) , then θ ^ = θ ∗ \hat{\theta}=\theta_{\ast} . Propriety can thus be thought of as the population counterpart of consistency for M-estimators of probability distributions ( Huber and Ronchetti 2009 ) . Minimization of strictly proper scoring rules can also be used to estimate conditional distributions. Let { ( X j , Y j ) } j = 1 n \{(X_{j},Y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n} be an iid sample of covariates and responses with ( X j , Y j ) ∈ 𝒳 × 𝒴 (X_{j},Y_{j})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y} , and let { ℙ θ , x : ( θ , x ) ∈ Θ × 𝒳 } \{\mathbb{P}_{\theta,x}:(\theta,x)\in\Theta\times\mathcal{X}\} be a parametric family of distributions on 𝒴 \mathcal{Y} that model the conditional distributions ℙ Y | X = x \mathbb{P}_{Y|X=x} of the response Y Y given covariate X = x X=x . We consider θ ^ = arg ​ min θ ∈ Θ ⁡ [ 1 n ​ ∑ j = 1 n S ⁡ ( ℙ θ , X j , Y j ) ] \hat{\theta}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}S(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,X_{j}},Y_{j})\right] (2) which yields the estimator ℙ θ ^ , x \smash{\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\theta},x}} of ℙ Y | X = x \mathbb{P}_{Y|X=x} for every x ∈ 𝒳 x\in\mathcal{X} . This approach estimates the conditional distribution simultaneously for every quantile and covariate as opposed to some classical methods such as quantile regression. In this sense, strictly proper scoring rules play the same role for estimating conditional distributions ℙ Y | X = x \mathbb{P}_{Y|X=x} , that the squared error loss plays for the conditional expectation 𝔼 ⁡ [ Y | X = x ] \mathbb{E}[Y|X=x] . Moreover, in a certain sense, these are the only loss functions which can consistently estimate conditional distributions (see Theorem 8 ). Although every proper scoring rule can identify the true model or the conditional distribution as its minimum in the population sense, they exhibit different inductive biases or regularization effects in finite samples. Furthermore, the computational tractability varies substantially depending on the chosen scoring rule, see Section 5 . 1.2 Proper scoring rules in forecast evaluation Gneiting and Katzfuss 2014 have argued that forecasts should be probabilistic, since future outcomes are uncertain and this uncertainty ought to be quantified for better decision making. Probabilistic forecasts are routinely issued in more and more application domains including but not limited to weather and climate prediction, flood risk prediction, renewable energy forecasts, financial risk management, epidemiological prediction, and preventive medicine. Given competing forecasters or forecasting methods, the predictive power is compared with a proper scoring rule S S . Suppose that there is data ( ℙ i , ℙ i ′ , y i ) i = 1 n (\mathbb{P}_{i},\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n} of competing probabilistic forecasts ℙ i \mathbb{P}_{i} , ℙ i ′ \mathbb{P}_{i}^{\prime} and corresponding verifying observations y i y_{i} for each forecasting instance i i (for example, each day for daily predictions). Then, one compares the average realized scores 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n S ⁡ ( ℙ i , y i ) and 1 n ​ ∑ i = 1 n S ⁡ ( ℙ i ′ , y i ) , \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(\mathbb{P}_{i},y_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{\prime},y_{i}), (3) and gives preference to the forecaster with the lower score. Justification of this approach, even in the absence of stationarity of forecast-observation pairs, can be found in Modeste et al. 2023 . Inference methods for differences in predictive performance are discussed by Diebold and Mariano 2002 ; Giacomini and White 2006 ; Lai et al. 2011 ; Henzi and Ziegel 2022 ; Choe and Ramdas 2024 but this list is not comprehensive. The mentioned works focus on assessing predictive performance comparatively between two forecasters based on proper scoring rules. A comprehensive review of inference methods for assessing predictive performance in relative and absolute terms goes beyond the scope of this paper. Clearly, the empirical scores in Equation 3 can be also be used as an evaluation metric (validation or testing loss) for comparing the performance of different estimates. While Equation 1 ensures that the optimal forecaster (or true model) will dominate all other forecasters (models or parameters) with regards to the comparison in Equation 3 in the long term, different proper scoring rules will rank competing imperfect forecasters differently even for infinite amounts of data, see for example Patton 2020 . While this may seem as a disadvantage of ranking forecasts with respect to proper scoring rules, it is neither surprising nor is there any alternative. Therefore, the scoring rule must be chosen carefully for the comparison of empirical scores to be relevant to the application at hand, since it determines when one imperfect probabilistic forecast should be preferred over another. As a result, there are numerous proposals to tailor proper scoring rules such that they are discriminative with respect to certain regions of interest of the predictive distributions and outcomes, see Section 5 . 1.3 Structure of the paper In Section 2 , we formally introduce proper scoring rules, discuss characterizations and interpret their geometric properties. It was originally derived as the limit of discrete ranked probability scores and by integrating the Brier score, until it was recognized as a member of a much larger family of kernel scores ( Staël von Holstein 1977 ; Gneiting and Raftery 2007 ) . Mathematically, it is essentially the only 1 1 -homogeneous translation invariant kernel score on ℝ \mathbb{R} (see Theorem 21 ). In addition to historical reasons, its popularity is explained by a range of advantages that it offers. The CRPS is an intuitive, interpretable, and convenient measure of forecasting accuracy. It is distance-sensitive, that is, it assigns better scores to forecasts, which put more probability closer to the observations. In contrast, the logarithmic score assigns harsh penalties regardless of the overall closeness of the forecast to the observations if the forecast density at any of the observations is too low. The CRPS is essentially the L 2 L^{2} distance between the CDFs of the forecast and the observations (see Example 11 ), and as the integral of the quadratic (Brier) score for binary outcomes, it elegantly connects to the conceptually simpler point forecasting setting. Indeed, it reduces to the absolute error loss when point forecasts are issued. Moreover, homogeneity implies that scaling the observations scales the predictive distribution accordingly even when the CRPS is away from the minimum. Furthermore, the CRPS exists for all forecasts with finite first moment, which means that the forecast measure need not necessarily have a density. It can be easily estimated by Equation 14 using samples drawn from the forecast, or explicitly computed if the forecast distribution has finite support. This is especially useful in applications such as ensemble forecasting in meteorology, where forecasts are routinely issued as discrete predictive distributions consisting of the empirical distributions of the ensemble of predictions. The CRPS has been extended to circular random variables ( Gneiting and Raftery 2007 ) . It has also spawned numerous other modifications, see Section 5.2 . Closed-form expressions for the CRPS have been obtained for many important parametric families such as the Gaussian and generalized extreme value (GEV) distributions ( Taillardat et al. 2016 ) . Closed-form expressions are also available for forecast CDFs which are linear splines ( Gasthaus et al. 2019 ) . In the absence of closed-form expressions, one can resort to estimating the score by sampling as in Equation 14 or numerical integration techniques such as kernel quadrature ( Adachi et al. 2025 ) . Estimation of such models using the logarithmic score in the form of maximum likelihood can, however, present an often insurmountable computational burden. Proper scoring rules can provide computationally viable alternatives. 5.1.1 Non-normalized Densities Although the logarithmic score yields closed-form expressions for many standard parametric families, it is often intractable for more complicated ones. This occurs frequently in statistics ( de Fondeville and Davison 2018 , see for example) and especially in some modern machine learning applications such as generative diffusion models ( Song and Ermon 2019 ) , where we need to model probability densities more flexibly, say as p θ ​ ( 𝐱 ) ∝ exp ⁡ η θ ​ ( 𝐱 ) p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\propto\exp\eta_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) , where η θ \eta_{\theta} could be a neural network. Estimating θ \theta via maximum likelihood for such complicated models would require evaluating the normalization constant c θ = ∫ exp ⁡ η θ ​ ( 𝐱 ) ​ 𝑑 𝐱 c_{\theta}=\int\exp\eta_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x} , which is prohibitively expensive even for moderately high dimensions. The Hyvärinnen score allows for a much more tractable estimation of such models by circumventing the need to evaluate the normalization constant c θ c_{\theta} and reducing the problem to minimizing the empirical risk corresponding to Δ 𝐱 ​ η θ ​ ( 𝐱 ) + 1 2 ​ ‖ ∇ 𝐱 η θ ​ ( 𝐱 ) ‖ 2 \Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\eta_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\eta_{\theta}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2} which depends only on η θ \eta_{\theta} . In this way, it complements methods like Langevin dynamics, which allow sampling from p θ p_{\theta} using only η θ \eta_{\theta} . This makes it particularly convenient for many statistical applications where normalization can be an issue such as estimation of high-dimensional graphical models ( Lin et al. 2016 ) , causal discovery ( Rolland et al. 2022 ) and estimation of point processes ( Cao et al. 2024 ) . In Bayesian statistics, the Hyvärinen score has been used for likelihood-free inference ( Pacchiardi and Dutta 2022 ) , model selection ( Dawid and Musio 2015 ) , the derivation of objective priors ( Leisen et al. 2020 ) , model comparison ( Shao et al. 2019 ) and sampling from Markov chain Monte Carlo and variational inference approaches ( Zhang et al. 2018 ; Modi et al. 2023 ) . The recent emergence of diffusion models as a powerful approach to generative modeling of images, audio, video and text, is also partly due to advancements in the implementation of score matching ( Dhariwal and Nichol 2021 , see for example) . The pseudo-spherical scoring rules also enable estimation with non-normalized densities due to their homogeneity and for this reason, they have been applied to estimating distributions on discrete spaces ( Takenouchi and Kanamori 2015 ) and training energy-based models ( Yu et al. 2021 ) . 5.1.2 Transformation Models In many settings in statistics and machine learning, such as that of structural equation models ( Dimitruk et al. 2007 ) or implicit generative models ( Mohamed and Lakshminarayanan 2017 ) , we model probability distributions { ℙ θ } θ ∈ Θ \{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta} (or conditional distributions { ℙ θ , 𝐱 } θ ∈ Θ \{\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\mathbf{x}}\}_{\theta\in\Theta} ) as the laws of parameterized transformations g θ ​ ( 𝐍 ) g_{\theta}(\mathbf{N}) (or conditionally as g θ ​ ( 𝐱 , 𝐍 ) g_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{N}) ) for θ ∈ Θ \theta\in\Theta , where 𝐍 \mathbf{N} is some standard random vector such as a multivariate standard Gaussian, instead of a parametric family of densities { p θ : θ ∈ Θ } \{p_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\} . The original approach to generative adversarial networks (GANs) achieved this via an innovative min-max formulation which however complicates training ( Goodfellow et al. 2014 ) . Although these scoring rules have been shown to be useful in evaluation, they have not been widely used in estimation contexts. 5.2.1 Sensitivity and Robustness Many scoring rules have been proposed in order to be sensitive or robust to particular statistical properties of the observations. For example, Scheuerer and Hamill 2015 proposed the variogram score in order to remedy the relative insensitivity of the energy scores to the correlation structure of multivariate distributions leading to poor discrimination ability ( Pinson and Tastu 2013 ) . In contrast, Bolin and Wallin 2023 considered a setting where observations are drawn from different distributions, where they show that common scoring rules such as the CRPS overweigh observations coming from more dispersed distributions. Motivated by local geometry considerations (see Section 2.3 ), they proposed the scaled CRPS to correct for this relative oversensitivity to observation variance which can be understood as a nuisance parameter. Another modification known as the scaled weighted CRPS has been proposed by Olafsdottir et al. 2024 in this setting to account for extremes. 5.2.2 Weighted Scoring Rules In many statistical applications such as risk management ( Holzmann and Klar 2017 ) and high impact weather prediction ( Allen et al. 2023 ) , certain outcomes are of considerably greater consequence to the user than others and naturally, we want the scoring rule to weigh forecast performance for such outcomes accordingly. To achieve this, Allen et al. 2023 propose using kernel scores with kernels modified using thresholding-weighting or vertical-rescaling , that is, h ′ ​ ( x , y ) = h ⁡ ( v ⁡ ( x ) , v ⁡ ( y ) ) h^{\prime}(x,y)=h(v(x),v(y)) and h ′ ​ ( x , y ) = h ⁡ ( x , y ) ​ w ​ ( x ) ​ w ​ ( y ) h^{\prime}(x,y)=h(x,y)w(x)w(y) , respectively, by using a chaining function v : 𝒴 → 𝒴 v:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y} or weighing function w : 𝒴 → ℝ w:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R} . By choosing h h to be a translation invariant kernel, we can control the weight exclusively through the chaining and weighting functions. This generalizes the threshold-weighted CRPS introduced by Gneiting and Ranjan 2011 , which also introduced a quantile-weighted version of the CRPS. Towards the same end, Diks et al. 2011 propose weighted versions of the logarithmic score based on which Holzmann and Klar 2017 propose a different approach to modifying scoring rules, which generally does not lead to kernel scores even if the original score was a kernel score. For binary outcomes, Buja et al. 2005 argued that scoring rules should be tailored to the relative cost implications of different types of errors. In a decision-theoretic context, Johnstone et al. 2011 proposed tailored scoring rules which are proper scoring rules adapted to the utility function of the decision maker. 6 Limitations and Extensions Proper scoring rules are versatile and important tools for estimation and forecast evaluation. However, they also have limitations. Proper scoring rules cannot assess tail properties of distributions. For example, under weak conditions on the scoring rule S S , Brehmer and Strokorb 2019 demonstrate that for a given distribution ℙ \mathbb{P} on ℝ \mathbb{R} , one can always find a distribution ℚ \mathbb{Q} such that the divergence d ⁡ ( ℚ , ℙ ) = S ⁡ ( ℚ , ℙ ) − S ⁡ ( ℙ , ℙ ) d(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})=S(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})-S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}) is arbitrarily small but ℙ \mathbb{P} and ℚ \mathbb{Q} are not tail equivalent. This negative result has further been strengthened by Allen et al. 2024 . In survival analysis, the classical approach to inference based on maximum likelihood (i.e. the logarithmic score) is complicated by the fact that observations are censored because this often renders the likelihood intractable. Consequently, many approaches based on alternative scoring rules such as the survival-CRPS ( Avati et al. 2020 ) and integrated Brier score ( Graf et al. 1999 ) have been devised over the years, see also Dawid and Musio 2014 . Unfortunately, these have been found to be improper by Rindt et al. 2022 , implying that they are unfit for evaluation or estimation. Rindt et al. 2022 establish the propriety of the classical right-censored log-likelihood and propose a tractable approach to survival analysis based on it. Moreover, Yanagisawa 2023 proposed proper extensions of the Brier and ranked probability scores to survival analysis. Hanson 2003 has proposed an interesting extension of proper scoring rules called market scoring rules for improving prediction markets. The basic premise of a prediction market is as follows: for a binary event of interest, say if it will rain on a given day, one can float a security in the prediction market which pays one dollar if (and when) the event takes place and nothing if it does not. The price of such a security can then be interpreted as the probability of the event (as forecasted by the market). However, obtaining the market forecast for a continuous quantity, such as the amount of rainfall on a given day, or a joint forecast for a multitude of related events, would require a very large number of such binary securities. Consequently, each security will barely have any buyers or sellers and the market will not function. This is known as the thin market problem . Hanson’s solution is to use instead a single security based on a proper scoring rule S S on the possible probability distributions of the outcomes and an automated market maker (AMM) which functions as follows: if a participant disagrees with the AMM forecast ℙ t \mathbb{P}_{t} at time t t , he can replace it with his own ℙ t + 1 \mathbb{P}_{t+1} and be paid 2 2 2 We use scores in their positive orientation here.
