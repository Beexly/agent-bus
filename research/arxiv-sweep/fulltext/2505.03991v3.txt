Abstract
Video event detection has become an essential component of sports analytics, enabling automated identification of key moments and enhancing performance analysis, viewer engagement, and broadcast efficiency. Recent advancements in deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Transformers, have significantly improved accuracy and efficiency in Temporal Action Localization (TAL), Action Spotting (AS), and Precise Event Spotting (PES). This survey provides a comprehensive overview of these three key tasks, emphasizing their differences, applications, and the evolution of methodological approaches. We thoroughly review and categorize existing datasets and evaluation metrics specifically tailored for sports contexts, highlighting the strengths and limitations of each. Furthermore, we analyze state-of-the-art techniques, including multi-modal approaches that integrate audio and visual information, methods utilizing self-supervised learning and knowledge distillation, and approaches aimed at generalizing across multiple sports. Finally, we discuss critical open challenges and outline promising research directions toward developing more generalized, efficient, and robust event detection frameworks applicable to diverse sports. This survey serves as a foundation for future research on efficient, generalizable, and multi-modal sports event detection.

II
Taxonomy of Sports Event Detection
In the domain of sports video analysis, three primary tasks relevant to event detection have been defined: TAL, AS, and PES. While these tasks share certain similarities, each addresses distinct objectives and annotation requirements, as summarized in Table
I
. The key differences among these tasks are discussed in detail in the following subsections.
II-A
Temporal Action Localization
Temporal Action Proposal Generation (TAPG) aims to identify temporal segments within videos that are likely to contain actions or events, without necessarily assigning specific action labels. Building upon TAPG, Temporal Action Localization (TAL)—also referred to as Temporal Action Detection (TAD)—extends the task by not only detecting these temporal segments but also classifying the actions occurring within them
[
12
,
13
,
11
]
, as illustrated in Figure
1
.
Among these tasks, TAPG is generally regarded as the most challenging, as accurately estimating the temporal boundaries of actions is inherently difficult. In contrast, once reliable proposals are available, action classification models tend to achieve relatively high accuracy
[
11
]
. This makes TAL particularly effective for recognizing everyday actions that unfold over extended periods.
II-B
Action Spotting
Introduced by Giancola et al. in 2018
[
14
]
, AS—also referred to as Event Spotting—addresses the ambiguity inherent in defining precise temporal boundaries for actions. Several factors contribute to this challenge: some actions occur extremely rapidly, others have continuous and indistinct endpoints, and many actions may overlap or conflict with one another. These ambiguities are especially pronounced in sports scenarios. For instance, in soccer, accurately determining the start and end times of events such as ”scoring a goal” or ”passing” is often nontrivial, due to the continuous nature of gameplay and the lack of sharply defined action boundaries.
To address these challenges, AS proposes an alternative to TAL by representing each action with a single keyframe rather than a temporal interval. While both TAL and AS aim to temporally locate actions within untrimmed videos, they differ fundamentally in annotation granularity. TAL annotates actions with explicit start and end times, making it suitable for longer-duration activities. In contrast, AS captures brief and rapidly occurring events using a single timestamp, thereby simplifying annotation efforts and mitigating ambiguities associated with temporal boundaries.
II-C
Precise Event Spotting
While AS facilitates coarser detection by tolerating errors within relatively broad temporal windows (typically between 5 and 60 seconds), certain applications in sports demand much higher temporal precision. Hong et al.
[
15
]
highlighted that in sports such as tennis
[
16
]
and figure skating
[
17
]
, even a margin of error of 1–2 frames can result in missing critical events, such as the precise moment of ball contact in tennis.
Similarly, consultations with Table Tennis Australia confirmed that frame-level precision is critical in table tennis, where even minor inaccuracies can significantly impact the determination of ball bounce locations, given the rapid motion of the ball across frames in professional-level matches, as illustrated in Figure
2
.
To meet these demands, PES was proposed as an extension of AS. PES requires extremely strict detection thresholds, typically constrained within 1–5 frames (approximately 33–200 milliseconds at standard frame rates of 25–30 FPS). PES thus ensures highly accurate event localization at the frame level, addressing the critical need for fine-grained temporal precision in professional sports analysis.
III
Video Event Detection
Generally, a video event detection model do this task in two step:
•
Temporal Segmentation: This involves identifying the timing of an event, either by locating its start and end timestamps within a continuous video (as in Temporal Action Localization, TAL) or by detecting a single representative keyframe (as in Action Spotting, AS, and Precise Event Spotting, PES)
•
Event Classification: Once an event is segmented, the model classifies it into predefined categories, such as team events (e.g., goals, time-outs) or individual actions (e.g., shots, fouls, serves).
In this section, we dive into the review of video event detection.
III-A
Temporal Action Localization
Current methods for TAPG typically fall into two main categories: Global-to-Local and Local-to-Global approaches.
Global-to-Local methods generate action proposals through sliding windows or pre-defined anchor structures, segmenting videos into fixed-length or hierarchically structured intervals to localize actions.
Local-to-Global methods independently evaluate each frame to predict the likelihood of action start, action end, and overall actionness. Proposals are subsequently created by connecting high-confidence start and end frames, followed by a confidence-based evaluation to confirm whether the proposal indeed contains an action.
The difference between these two approaches is shown in figure

.
The subsequent sections will explore these two approaches comprehensively, discussing their strengths, limitations, and practical applications in sports event detection.
Fig. 3:
Comparison of Global-to-Local (GTL, left) and Local-to-Global (LTG, right) approaches. In GTL, predefined temporal anchors are classified as action or background, followed by a regressor that refines the event’s time interval. In contrast, LTG first predicts per-frame start and end probabilities to define the temporal boundaries and then classifies the resulting interval.
J. Gao et al. proposed the Temporal Unit Regression Network (TURN) to address the temporal action proposal generation problem
[
18
]
. TURN first decomposes an untrimmed video into short video units, which serve as fundamental processing blocks, with visual features extracted from each unit using a base model C3D
[
19
]
. To construct meaningful clip-level representations, TURN pools a set of contiguous units across multiple temporal scales, forming a clip pyramid. Each clip is further enriched by concatenating features from both its internal and surrounding units, ensuring contextual information is preserved. At this stage, each clip is treated as a proposal candidate, and TURN outputs a confidence score indicating whether the clip contains an action instance, along with two regression offsets that refine the predicted start and end times of the action within the clip. Finally, Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) is applied to eliminate redundant proposals, improving localization accuracy and ensuring high-quality action boundaries.
They have also proposed a new metric for this task which is called Average Recall vs. Frequency of retrieved proposals (AR-F) which covers two criteria, (1) it should be capable of evaluating the performance of different methods on the same dataset efficiently; (2) it should be capable of evaluating the performance of the same method across different dataset.
Even though TURN has achieved the SOTA performance on two challenging datasets THUMOS-14
[
20
]
and ActivityNet
[
21
]
, with the highest AR at the time because of the sliding window nature where it covers all segments in the video however the temporal boundaries are not precise enough, indicating its flaws when accurately deciding the correct time frame for the event
[
22
,
13
,
23
]
.
On the other hand, Temporal Action Grouping (TAG)
[
24
]
was proposed as an alternative to fixed temporal scale methods, drawing inspiration from the watershed algorithm
[
25
]
. TAG groups continuous high-score regions into proposals rather than relying on predefined temporal scales. It applies a binary classification to each unit or snippet (a few contiguous frames) to generate actionness scores, which indicate the likelihood of an action occurring in that unit. This method is capable of producing high-precision temporal boundaries, provided that the actionness scores are accurate. However, its performance heavily depends on the quality of the predicted actionness scores, making it less effective when predictions are unreliable
[
22
]
.
To address these limitations, Complementary Temporal Action Proposal (CTAP) was introduced by J. Gao et al.
[
22
]
, leveraging the strengths of both actionness-based proposals and sliding-window proposals. CTAP first generates both types of proposals and then applies a proposal complementary filter, which collects missing correct proposals that TAG alone might overlook by retrieving them from the sliding-window proposals. In this stage, a binary classifier is used, taking a sequence of unit features as input and outputting a probability score that indicates whether a proposal can be accurately detected by the unit-level actionness model and TAG. Finally, a Temporal Convolutional Adjustment and Ranking Network is employed to rank proposals and refine their boundaries. Experimental results on THUMOS-14 and ActivityNet v1.3 demonstrate that CTAP outperforms TURN in AR, highlighting its effectiveness in improving proposal quality. However, the combination of multiple proposal generation methods can impact computational efficiency, potentially increasing processing time.

T. Lin et al. proposed the Boundary Sensitive Network (BSN), the first approach designed to predict the start, end, and actionness of each frame in a video
[
12
]
. Proposals are generated by pairing start and end points with high probabilities, while low-confidence candidates are discarded by an evaluation module. BSN significantly outperforms previous methods and achieves high performance even with relatively few proposals. On the THUMOS14 dataset, BSN surpasses all SOTA methods across a range of proposal counts (10 to 1000). Notably, when the average number of proposals is 50, BSN improves AR from 21.86% to 37.46%, demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing action boundaries with fewer proposals.
However, BSN suffers from inefficiencies due to its proposal feature construction and confidence evaluation processes, which are applied individually to each proposal, leading to high computational overhead. To address this issue, Boundary-Matching Network (BMN) was introduced
[
13
]
, incorporating a Boundary-Matching (BM) mechanism for more efficient confidence evaluation of densely distributed proposals. Instead of evaluating each proposal separately, BMN represents proposals as matching pairs of their start and end boundaries and constructs a two-dimensional BM confidence map. This map models densely distributed proposals by encoding continuous starting boundaries and temporal durations, allowing confidence scores to be computed simultaneously for all proposals. By leveraging this structure, BMN significantly improves efficiency while maintaining strong performance in temporal action proposal generation.
Building upon these advancements, BSN++ was introduced as an enhanced version of BSN to further refine proposal generation and confidence evaluation
[
23
]
. While BSN effectively captures action boundaries, it lacks a mechanism to assess the completeness of proposals, often leading to inaccurate segmentations. BSN++ addresses this by incorporating a proposal completeness modeling module, which evaluates whether a detected segment fully encapsulates an action instance. Additionally, BSN++ enhances feature representation by introducing context-aware proposal features, ensuring that both internal and surrounding contextual information contribute to more precise action localization. To improve proposal ranking, a global-local confidence fusion module is integrated, leveraging both local proposal confidence and global consistency to reduce false positives. Furthermore, BSN++ adopts the BM confidence map to evaluate multiple proposals simultaneously, significantly improving computational efficiency. These refinements enable BSN++ to generate higher-quality proposals while reducing redundancy, leading to superior performance in temporal action proposal generation.
However, Z. Qing et al. argue that existing methods struggle to effectively model temporal relationships, as neither convolution-based approaches nor global fusion mechanisms can fully capture long-range dependencies. Additionally, relying solely on internal or boundary context for regression is insufficient for generating proposals with precise boundaries
[
26
]
. To address these limitations, they proposed the Temporal Context Aggregation Network (TCANet) for high-quality proposal generation, which consists of two key components.
First, the Local-Global Temporal Encoder (LGTE) is introduced to capture both local and global temporal relationships in a channel-grouping fashion. The Local Temporal Encoder (LTE) is responsible for processing the first A groups of the input feature maps, which are linearly transformed and evenly divided into N groups along the channel dimension to enhance short-term temporal modeling. Simultaneously, the remaining N - A groups are handled by the Global Temporal Encoder (GTE), which captures long-range dependencies. By combining these two encoders, LGTE effectively integrates long-term contextual information while preserving structural and fine-grained local details, leading to richer proposal representations.
Second, the Temporal Boundary Regressor (TBR) is designed to refine proposal boundaries by leveraging both internal and boundary context. This is achieved through a dual regression approach: (1) Frame-level boundary regression, which refines the starting and ending locations of proposals with boundary sensitivity, and (2) Segment-level boundary regression, which adjusts the center location and duration of proposals based on a holistic perception of their structure. The final predicted proposal boundaries are obtained by fusing the outputs of both regression modules, ensuring precise action localization. Experimental results demonstrate that TCANet significantly enhances action proposal generation and action detection, outperforming existing methods by improving both boundary accuracy and proposal completeness.
Previous works typically use tIoU between the proposal and the ground truth action instance—referred to as the action score—to evaluate proposal confidence and develop high-quality temporal action proposals. However, H. Yang et al. argue that background information is also significant but was previously overlooked. Motivated by this observation, they proposed the Background Constraint Network (BCNet) to generate high-quality temporal action proposals
[
27
]
.
BCNet consists of two main modules: the Action-Background Interaction (ABI) Module and the Boundary Prediction (BP) Module. Inspired by the Transformer
[
28
]
, the ABI module first employs self-attention to capture relationships between frames and enhance feature representations. The enhanced frame features are then processed by the Difference-Attention Unit, which aggregates features based on the inconsistency between action and background features under supervision. Finally, a prediction head encodes both action and background features, followed by a Sigmoid layer to generate action and background probability sequences.
During training, unlike previous works that rely solely on action score loss, BCNet incorporates background score loss to further refine proposal confidence. Extensive experiments on the ActivityNet-v1.3 and THUMOS14 datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of explicitly modeling background information and leveraging attention mechanisms in TAPG.

III-B
Action Spotting & Precise Event Spotting
In contrast to TAL, which focuses on predicting temporal intervals for actions, AS and PES have gained significant attention in the sports analytics domain. AS and PES aim to identify actions or events using a single key frame, making them particularly suited for rapidly occurring events while also reducing annotation effort
[
43
,
46
]
. This contrasts with traditional temporal video understanding, where actions are defined by explicit start and end times. In sports scenarios, these boundaries often become ambiguous—for example, clearly defining the precise start and end of a goal event can be challenging.
To address the ambiguity in defining temporal boundaries in sports actions, the SoccerNet dataset introduced AS as a new task specifically tailored to the sports domain
[
14
]
, as illustrated in Figure
4
. Alongside the dataset, S. Giancola et al. also proposed the first baseline method for AS, which involved evaluating various temporal pooling strategies on pre-extracted features from video chunks.
Building on this initial work, they later introduced NetVLAD++
[
34
]
, a temporally-aware pooling module inspired by NetVLAD
[
47
]
. While standard NetVLAD aggregates frame-level features without considering temporal order—treating frames as an unordered set—NetVLAD++ explicitly incorporates temporal structure. This is particularly important in AS scenarios, where the sequence of frames before and after an event carries significant contextual information. By modeling both past and future contexts, NetVLAD++ achieved improved performance in AS.
A summary of recent methods developed for AS and PES, including their key techniques, modalities, and evaluation results on the SoccerNet benchmark, is provided in Table
II
.
O. Rongved et al.
[
30
]
trained a 3D ResNet model, inspired by the architecture proposed in
[
48
]
, and applied it directly to raw video frames using a 5-second sliding window approach. While 3D CNNs are effective at capturing spatiotemporal features, they are also known for their high computational cost, which can hinder real-time deployment and scalability. To address this limitation, Darwish et al. introduced the Spatio-Temporal Encoder (STE)
[
38
]
, which relies solely on 1D convolutions and fully connected layers. This design significantly reduces computational complexity while maintaining strong performance, demonstrating that efficient architectures can still effectively model spatiotemporal dynamics without the overhead of 3D convolutions.
K. Vats et al.
[
31
]
proposed a multi-tower CNN architecture designed to process video information at multiple temporal scales, addressing the uncertainty in AS. In parallel, Cioppa et al.
[
32
]
introduced a context-aware loss function that explicitly models the temporal context surrounding actions. Their method takes a different approach by predicting multiple action spots within each video chunk, achieved by regressing several temporal offsets per chunk to capture the potential positions of events.
A significant performance improvement was achieved by Zhou et al.
[
36
]
, who demonstrated that task-specific feature representations tailored to soccer broadcast videos can substantially enhance the effectiveness of AS models. They fine-tuned several pre-trained action recognition networks on soccer-specific data to extract high-level semantic features that better reflect the visual patterns and context of the sport. These features were then fed into Transformer-based encoding layers serving as the prediction head. Their approach achieved SOTA at the time, highlighting the importance of leveraging domain-adapted features for improving spotting accuracy in soccer video analysis.
Building on detection principles and self-supervised learning, Tomei et al.
[
35
]
presented RMS-Net, a model that combines regression and masking strategies. Inspired by object detection pipelines and pretraining techniques, RMS-Net enables the network to learn robust representations for spotting actions with improved precision.
J. Hong et al. later extended AS to PES by setting a stricter tolerance window
[
15
]
. Their proposed E2E-Spot model begins by extracting local spatial-temporal features using RegNet-Y
[
49
]
enhanced with Gate Shift Modules
[
50
]
. These features are passed through a single-layer bidirectional GRU
[
51
]
, which outputs per-frame event occurrence probabilities.
In parallel, methods originally developed for TAL have shown strong performance when adapted to AS tasks. For example, J. Soares et al. proposed a detection approach based on short temporal anchors (e.g., 0.5–1.0s) where each anchor is a pair formed by a time instant and action class, predicting confidence scores and fine-grained temporal displacements for each anchor
[
40
]
. Their framework, inspired by object detection, employs both a 1D U-Net
[
52
]
and a Transformer-based encoder, with the Transformer outperforming by effectively modeling both temporal and contextual information
[
53
]
.
Incorporating multi-modal input, especially audio, has also proven beneficial as audio data can contain many critical cues such as the sound when the player strikes the ball. Vanderplaetsen et al.
[
33
]
explored different fusion strategies and found that late fusion—integrating audio and visual features just before the fully connected layer—yielded the best performance.
With the rise of Transformers in computer vision
[
54
]
, their application has also extended to the PES task. SpotFormer
[
39
]
adopts this approach by leveraging multiple feature extractors—including Transformer-based models (VideoMAE
[
55
]
, SwinTransfrmer
[
56
]
)—to extract rich spatial and temporal features from soccer videos. These features are then fed into a Transformer-based spotting network to perform precise event spotting. This multi-extractor strategy achieved strong performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of Transformer architectures in learning from diverse feature inputs. However, the use of multiple feature extractors significantly increases computational cost and inference time, making it less practical for real-time or resource-constrained applications.
To address temporal modeling more efficiently, Zhu et al.
[
41
]
proposed the use of a multi-scale Vision Transformer for feature extraction, followed by a sliding window mechanism to improve temporal understanding. These enhanced temporal features are then processed using NetVLAD++, a pooling strategy designed to aggregate local features into a robust global representation, producing the final event spotting predictions. This approach strikes a balance between temporal context modeling and computational efficiency, offering an alternative to the heavier multi-extractor pipelines.
In a similar direction, Shi et al.
[
37
]
introduced a multi-scene encoding strategy to address the variability in action durations. First, video features are extracted as a chunk and then divided into multiple subsets, each of which is processed by separate Transformer encoders. By varying chunk sizes, their model captures actions of different temporal lengths more effectively, demonstrating a flexible and adaptive approach to temporal modeling in event spotting tasks.
Building on the success of transformer and audio fusion, Xarles et al. proposed ASTRA (Action Spotting TRAnsformer)
[
42
]
, a Transformer-based encoder-decoder model designed for soccer videos. ASTRA processes both visual and audio embeddings using learnable queries in the decoder, demonstrating significant performance gains on SoccerNet by leveraging multi-modal information with transformers.
Tran et al.
[
45
]
highlight a key limitation in traditional backbone models: they tend to rely heavily on global feature extraction, which often overlooks fine-grained details in the scene. As a result, these models struggle to detect events that occupy only a small spatial and temporal region of the video, such as ball positions or yellow/red card events. To address this issue, the authors proposed the Unifying Global and Local (UGL) Module, designed to capture both global environmental context and local features from task-relevant scene entities.
For extracting global features, UGL adopts the same approach as the E2ESpot model
[
15
]
, utilizing RegNet-Y with GSM. To capture local features, the model integrates GLIP
[
57
]
, a vision-language model that processes a single image and a textual prompt describing the target objects to detect (e.g., ”ball,” ”referee,” ”card”). This represents the first use of vision-language models in PES, enabling the model to better focus on semantically relevant entities within the scene.
By effectively combining global and local information, the UGL module achieved SOTA performance at the time of publication, demonstrating the efficacy of integrating vision-language models and hybrid feature modeling for fine-grained event detection in sports videos.
Recognizing the need for high temporal resolution in PES, A. Xarles et al. later introduced the Temporal Discriminability Enhancer Encoder-Decoder (T-DEED)
[
43
]
. T-DEED addresses the challenge of distinguishing between visually similar frames that occur in close succession by preserving fine-grained temporal resolution across multiple scales. It incorporates Scalable-Granularity Perception (SGP) layers
[
58
]
to boost intra-sequence feature discriminability.
Despite these advances, one major challenge persists: the reliance on large volumes of annotated data. To address this, S. Giancola et al. proposed the first active learning framework for AS
[
59
]
. Their pipeline begins with a model trained on a small labeled set, which is then used to generate predictions on unlabeled data. These predictions are ranked using uncertainty and entropy measures to identify the most informative samples. Selected clips are manually annotated and added back into the training loop. This iterative process significantly reduces the required labeled data while maintaining performance—achieving results comparable to state-of-the-art methods using only one-third of the dataset.
Most recently, J. Denize et al. proposed COMEDIAN, the first AS-specific framework that combines self-supervised learning (SSL) with knowledge distillation (KD) for initializing spatiotemporal transformers
[
44
]
. COMEDIAN features two Transformers: a spatial transformer that learns short-range context within video segments, and a temporal transformer that integrates longer-range context. The spatial transformer is trained using Momentum Contrast (MoCo)
[
60
]
, a contrastive learning method that promotes feature similarity across related clips. Both components are further enhanced using a Soft Contrastive loss (SCE)
[
61
]
, which distills knowledge from a bank of rich spatiotemporal features generated by a pre-trained video model. After pretraining, the entire framework is fine-tuned in a supervised manner for AS, achieving state-of-the-art results on the SoccerNet-v2 benchmark.

Tolerance Windows and AP Calculation in AS and PES
In contrast to TAL, which evaluates temporal intervals, both AS and PES treat detections as successful if the predicted timestamp falls within a predefined tolerance window centered around the ground-truth event. In AS, this window is typically broad, often ranging from 5 to 60 seconds, reflecting the nature of rapid, ambiguous actions where precise boundary annotation is difficult. PES, on the other hand, imposes a much stricter tolerance, typically constrained to 0–2 frames, requiring frame-level temporal precision.
Given this setup, the evaluation of AS and PES differs slightly from classical TAL evaluation. Rather than computing mAP over temporal intervals, AS and PES typically compute AP based on spot predictions relative to event anchors. Specifically, detections are first sorted by descending confidence scores. A prediction is considered a true positive if it falls within the defined tolerance window of any unmatched ground-truth event. Once a ground-truth event has been matched, it cannot be matched again.

Thus, while the AP formulation for AS and PES follows a similar procedure, the strictness of the tolerance window fundamentally shifts the difficulty and interpretation of the resulting scores.

IV
Challenges and Future Directions
In this section, we critically examine current challenges in sports event detection and outline specific, actionable future research directions to address these limitations.
IV-A
Generalization Across Diverse Sports
Most existing AS and PES models are developed and validated primarily within individual sports contexts, such as soccer (e.g., SoccerNet). These specialized approaches perform well within their target sports but often exhibit limited capability for generalization, as they rely on domain-specific characteristics like camera angles, event definitions, and player interactions.
Only a limited number of approaches have systematically evaluated their effectiveness across multiple sports datasets (see Table
II
), including tennis, figure skating, diving, and gymnastics. This gap highlights a significant limitation: current models struggle to generalize to sports with different visual characteristics, dynamics, and temporal structures. Future research must prioritize:
•
Development of universal AS and PES models leveraging cross-domain transfer learning, possibly employing large-scale pre-trained vision-language models (e.g., CLIP
[
75
]
), to enhance cross-sport adaptability.
•
Rigorous benchmarking across diverse sports to validate generalizability, robustness, and practical utility in varied real-world scenarios.
•
The creation and release of new annotated datasets spanning a wider range of sports—particularly underrepresented domains such as volleyball, hockey, fencing, and athletics—to support broader generalization and fair evaluation.
IV-B
Advancements in Unsupervised and Low-Supervision Methods
Creating extensive labeled datasets for sports event detection is costly, labor-intensive, and often demands expert domain knowledge, particularly in technical sports such as gymnastics, tennis, and figure skating. To mitigate these barriers, recent methods have begun exploring low-supervision learning paradigms such as knowledge distillation and active learning
[
44
,
59
]
. These approaches significantly reduce dependency on large annotated datasets by transferring knowledge from pretrained models or selectively labeling informative samples.
Nevertheless, fully unsupervised or self-supervised methods remain largely unexplored. Future research directions include:
•
Development of self-supervised frameworks that leverage temporal consistency, contrastive learning, and multimodal alignment to extract meaningful event representations from unlabeled or weakly labeled video data.
•
Integration of unsupervised learning with domain adaptation techniques to further boost model performance and generalization across multiple sports domains.
These strategies will be essential for building scalable, efficient, and broadly applicable event detection models suitable for diverse sports analytics.
IV-C
Enhanced Multimodal Fusion Approaches
Although existing AS and PES methodologies predominantly rely on visual data, audio cues can significantly enrich the detection of critical events in sports. Examples include sounds of ball impacts, crowd reactions, or figure skaters landing on ice—all of which provide valuable complementary temporal signals.
Currently, multimodal models predominantly employ straightforward fusion methods such as concatenation or late fusion
[
33
,
42
]
, which do not fully capture the complex interactions between modalities. To address these limitations, future research should explore more sophisticated fusion techniques:
•
Attention-based cross-modal transformers and gated attention mechanisms, capable of dynamically weighting and integrating audio and visual modalities.
•
Modality-specific encoders coupled with temporal alignment mechanisms to capture precise event timings, especially in noisy or visually ambiguous scenarios.
•
Exploiting commentary audio through automatic speech recognition and natural language processing to provide additional semantic context and weak supervision signals, aligning spoken descriptions directly with visual event occurrences.
Advancing multimodal fusion methodologies represents a crucial opportunity to significantly enhance the accuracy, robustness, and practical applicability of AS and PES frameworks in sports video analytics.
V
Conclusion
In this survey, we presented a comprehensive review of the current state of deep learning-based video event detection, with a particular focus on the critical tasks of TAL, AS, and PES within the sports analytics domain. By systematically categorizing and evaluating recent datasets and advanced methodologies, we identified key limitations, including poor generalizability across diverse sports contexts, reliance on extensive annotated datasets, and the underutilization of multimodal information sources.
Our analysis highlights the urgent need for the development of generalized frameworks capable of robust performance across multiple sports disciplines, potentially leveraging transfer learning and cross-domain evaluation strategies. Moreover, advancing unsupervised and low-supervision approaches, such as self-supervised learning and active learning, will be critical to reducing annotation costs and enabling scalable deployment. In parallel, future research should explore sophisticated multimodal fusion techniques that effectively integrate visual, audio, and textual modalities to enhance temporal precision, robustness, and event understanding in complex real-world environments.
By addressing these challenges, future efforts can significantly advance the capabilities of automated sports video analytics, leading to more comprehensive, accurate, and efficient systems capable of delivering actionable insights across a wide range of sports applications.
