[1701.03363] The Massey’s method for sport rating: a network science perspective The Massey’s method for sport rating: a network science perspective Enrico Bozzo Affiliation: Department Mathematics, Computer Science, and Physics Affiliation: University of Udine Email: enrico.bozzo@uniud.it Massimo Franceschet Affiliation: Department Mathematics, Computer Science, and Physics Affiliation: University of Udine Email: massimo.franceschet@uniud.it Abstract We revisit the Massey’s method for rating and ranking in sports and contextualize it as a general centrality measure in network science. 1 Introduction Rating and ranking in sport have a flourishing tradition. Each sport competition has its own official rating, from which a ranking of players and teams can be compiled. The challenge of many sports’ fans and bettors is to beat the official rating method: to develop an alternative rating algorithm that is better than the official one in the task of predicting future results. As a consequence, many sport rating methods have been developed. Amy N. Langville and Carl D. Meyer even wrote a (compelling) book about (general) rating and ranking methods entitled Who’s #1? [ 11 ] . In 1997, Kenneth Massey, then an undergraduate, created a method for ranking college football teams. He wrote about this method, which uses the mathematical theory of least squares, as his honors thesis [ 12 ] . Informally, at any given day k k of the season, Massey’s method rates a team i i according to the following two factors: (a) the difference between points for and points against i i , or point spread of i i , up to day k k , and (b) the ratings of the teams that i i matched up to day k k . Hence, highly rated teams have a large point differential and matched strong teams so far. Below in the ranking are teams that did well but had an easy schedule as well as teams that did not so well but had a tough schedule. In this paper, we review the original Massey’s method and explicitly provide the algebraic background of the method. Moreover, we interpret Massey’s technique in the context of network science. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the original Massey’s method. In particular, Section 2.1 provides a full example of the method, Section 2.2 embeds the method in the context of network science, and Section 2.3 gives the algebraic background of the Massey’s method for the curious reader. We review related methods for sport rating in Section 3 . Furthermore, in the following, we propose an electrical interpretation of the Massey’s rating. If we view the symmetric matrix A A as the adjacency matrix of an undirected graph G A G_{A} , then M M is the Laplacian matrix of the graph G A G_{A} . The rating vector r r defined in system ( 2 ) is then equivalent to the potential vector over a resistor network defined by A A with supply vector p p [ 5 ] . Following this metaphor, the resistor network has a resistor between nodes i i and j j as soon as teams i i and j j has matched with conductance (reciprocal of resistance) equal to the number A i , j A_{i,j} of matches between i i and j j . Sources, that are nodes i i with positive supply p i p_{i} through which current enters the network, are teams with positive point spread, while targets, that are nodes i i with negative supply p i p_{i} through which current leaves the network, are teams with negative point spread. Notice that current entering and leaving the network must be equal, indeed the point spread vector p p sums to 0. The potential r i r_{i} for node i i corresponds to the rating of team i i : teams with large potential are teams high in the ranking. Moreover, the current flow through edge ( i , j ) (i,j) is, by Ohm’s law, the quantity A i , j ​ ( r i − r j ) A_{i,j}(r_{i}-r_{j}) , which corresponds to the rating difference between teams i i and j j (which is also an estimate of the point spread in a match between i i and j j ) multiplied by the number of times they matched: current flows more intensely between teams of different strengths (as measured by Massey’s method) that matched many times. An n × n n\times n matrix A A is defined to be strictly diagonally dominant if | A i , i | &gt; ∑ j ≠ i | A i , j | |A_{i,i}|&gt;\sum_{j\neq i}|A_{i,j}| for i = 1 , … , n i=1,\ldots,n . By using Gers̆gorin theorem it is easy to prove that if A A is strictly diagonally dominant then it is nonsingular, see [ 6 ] . A matrix A A is defined to be diagonally dominant if | A i , i | ≥ ∑ j ≠ i | A i , j | |A_{i,i}|\geq\sum_{j\neq i}|A_{i,j}| for i = 1 , … , n i=1,\ldots,n . A matrix A A is defined to be irreducibly diagonally dominant if it is irreducible, it is diagonally dominant, and for at least one value of i i the strict inequality | A i , i | &gt; ∑ j ≠ i | A i , j | |A_{i,i}|&gt;\sum_{j\neq i}|A_{i,j}| holds. An irreducibly diagonally dominant matrix is nonsingular, see again [ 6 ] . With a non standard terminology, we define the matrix A A to be exactly diagonally dominant if | A i , i | = ∑ j ≠ i | A i , j | |A_{i,i}|=\sum_{j\neq i}|A_{i,j}| for i = 1 , … , n i=1,\ldots,n . We introduce this concept since the Laplacian and the signless Laplacian of a graph are exactly diagonally dominant. The signless Laplacian of a graph is defined as the matrix whose entries are the absolute values of the entries of the Laplacian [ 3 ] . For example, the matrix M = D − A M=D-A of system ( 2 ) is the Laplacian of the graph G A G_{A} , and the matrix N = D + A N=D+A of system ( 6 ) is its signless Laplacian. The following lemma points out an interesting property of a symmetric irreducible and exactly diagonally dominant matrix. Lemma 3 . Let n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , A A be a symmetric irreducible exactly diagonally dominant n × n n\times n matrix and k ∈ { 1 , 2 , … , n } k\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\} . The ( n − 1 ) × ( n − 1 ) (n-1)\times(n-1) matrix A ⁡ ( k ) A(k) obtained by deleting from A A the k k -th row and the k k -th column is nonsingular. Proof. First of all notice that A A cannot have diagonal elements equal to zero, since, being exactly diagonally dominant, this would imply that A A has a row completely equal to zero, and this conflicts with the assumption that A A is irreducible. The matrix A ⁡ ( k ) A(k) is the adjacency matrix of the subgraph G A ⁡ ( k ) G_{A(k)} obtained by the elimination of node k k and of the edges incident in that node from G A G_{A} . The matrix A ⁡ ( k ) A(k) can be transformed by a simultaneous permutation of rows and columns into a block diagonal matrix where each diagonal block is relative to one of the connected components of the subgraph. We want to show that each of these blocks is nonsingular. If the block has dimension 1 1 , then it contains one of the diagonal elements of A A , hence its only entry is different from zero. If the dimension of the block is bigger than 1 1 , then the block is irreducible because its graph is connected by construction. Moreover, since A A is exactly diagonally dominant, each of the blocks of A ⁡ ( k ) A(k) is diagonally dominant and the strict inequality holds for one of the values of the index in each block because at least one of the deleted edges was incident in one of the nodes of the connected component. Hence the block is irreducibly diagonally dominant so that it is nonsingular. Finally, the matrix A ⁡ ( k ) A(k) is nonsingular since the diagonal blocks are nonsingular. ∎ From Lemma 3 we deduce that a symmetric irreducible and exactly diagonally dominant n × n n\times n matrix A A has rank at least n − 1 n-1 and more specifically, if we delete an arbitrary row from A A , the remaining rows are linearly independent. Hence, either A A is nonsingular, or its nullspace has dimension one. For example the Laplacian of a connected graph is always singular, while its signless Laplacian is singular if and only if the graph is bipartite [ 3 ] . If A A is singular and v ≠ 0 v\neq 0 is such that A ​ v = 0 Av=0 then v v cannot have entries equal to zero since all the subsets of n − 1 n-1 rows, or equivalently columns, of A A are linearly independent. For example for the Laplacian of a connected graph v = e v=e while for its signless Laplacian, in the case where the graph is bipartite, v v has components equal to 1 1 or − 1 -1 in correspondence with the two parts of the graph. Keener’s method [ 8 ] is a recursive technique based on Perron-Frobenius theorem. Let S i , j S_{i,j} be the number of points, or any other relevant statistics, that i i scores against j j and A i , j A_{i,j} be the strength of i i compared to j j . The strength A i , j A_{i,j} can be interpreted as the probability that i i will defeat team j j in the future. For instance, A i , j = S i , j / ( S i , j + S j , i ) A_{i,j}=S_{i,j}/(S_{i,j}+S_{j,i}) or, using Laplace’s rule of succession, A i , j = ( S i , j + 1 ) / ( S i , j + S j , i + 2 ) A_{i,j}=(S_{i,j}+1)/(S_{i,j}+S_{j,i}+2) . Notice that in both cases 0 ≤ A i , j ≤ 1 0\leq A_{i,j}\leq 1 and A i , j + A j , i = 1 A_{i,j}+A_{j,i}=1 . The Keener’s rating r r is the solution of the following recursive equation: A ​ r = λ ​ r , Ar=\lambda r, where λ \lambda is a constant and A A is the team strength matrix, or r i = 1 λ ​ ∑ j A i , j ​ r j . r_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{j}A_{i,j}r_{j}. Hence, the thesis of Keener is that team i i is strong if it defeated strong teams. This intriguing recursive definition has been discovered and rediscovered many times in different contexts. It has been investigated, in chronological order, in econometrics, sociometry, bibliometrics, Web information retrieval, and network science [ 4 ] . In particular, it is the basis of the PageRank algorithm used by Google search engine to rank Web pages [ 1 ] . Assuming that matrix A A is nonnegative and irreducible (or equivalently that the graph of A A is connected), Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees that a rating solution exists. The offense-defense method is described in Langville and Meyer’s book [ 11 ] , where it is applied to the problem of rating and ranking of sport teams. A slightly modified method is proposed and investigated in a previous work by Knight [ 10 ] . The method lies on Sinkhorn-Knopp theorem. Let A A be a strength matrix as defined for Keener’s method, that is, A i , j A_{i,j} is the probability that i i will defeat team j j in the future. Assuming that matrix A A has total support (or equivalently that the graph of A A is regularizable), Sinkhorn-Knopp theorem guarantees that a rating solution exists. The offense-defense method can be regarded as a non-linear (reciprocal) version of Kleinberg’s method HITS [ 9 ] . Finally, the Elo’s system is an iterative method coined by the physics professor and excellent chess player Arpad Elo. Then, when teams i i and j j match, the new rank r i ​ ( n ​ e ​ w ) r_{i}(new) of team i i is updated as follows (and similarly for j j ): r i ​ ( n ​ e ​ w ) = r i ​ ( o ​ l ​ d ) + κ ⁡ ( S i , j − μ i , j ) , r_{i}(new)=r_{i}(old)+\kappa(S_{i,j}-\mu_{i,j}), where κ \kappa is a constant (for instance, in chess κ = 25 \kappa=25 for new players). Hence, beating a stronger player has a larger reward than beating a weaker one. Immagine a weighted directed network in which nodes have an advantage in having weighty edges exiting from the node, while they have a disadvantage in having weighty edges entering to the node. For instance, this holds when nodes are countries and edges corresponds to valued transfer of goods between countries: an edge weighted k k from A to B means that country A exports to country B for a value of k k (or B imports from A for a value o k k ). As another example, imagine if nodes are financial actors (like banks) and edges are money that have been loaned between banks: an edge weighted k k from A to B means that bank A loaned to bank B a value of k k (or B borrowed from A a value o k k ). We can view such a weighted directed network as a competition between nodes of the network as follows: • for any pair of nodes i i and j j for which there is an edge ( i , j ) (i,j) with weight k 1 k_{1} as well as the reciprocal edge ( j , i ) (j,i) with weight k 2 k_{2} , we have a match between i i and j j in which i i scores k 1 k_{1} points and j j scores k 2 k_{2} points; • for any pair of nodes i i and j j for which there is an edge ( i , j ) (i,j) with weight k 1 k_{1} but there is no reciprocal edge ( j , i ) (j,i) , we have a match between i i and j j in which i i scores k 1 k_{1} points and j j scores 0 0 points; • for any pair of nodes i i and j j that are not connected by an edge (in any direction), we have no match between i i and j j . Having defined the matches of the competition, we can apply Massey’s method (or any alternative sport rating method) to estimate the importance of nodes in the network. For instance, in the case of Massey’s method and the banking scenario illustrated above, we are looking for ratings of banks such that r i − r j = y k r_{i}-r_{j}=y_{k} , where y k y_{k} is the financial balance (credit minus debit) between banks i i and j j .
