Our main theoretical contribution is Transfer-Informed Betting (TIB), which warm-starts the WSR wealth process using a source domain’s risk profile, achieving tighter bounds in data-scarce settings with a formal dominance guarantee. We prove that the TIB wealth process remains a valid supermartingale under all source–target divergences, that TIB dominates standard WSR when domains match, and that no data-independent warm-start can achieve better convergence—the source-informed initialization is optimal among plug-in priors. The specific combination of (i) betting-based confidence sequences, (ii) LTT monotone testing, and (iii) cross-domain transfer is, to our knowledge, a three-way novelty not present in the literature. We evaluate all nine bound families on four benchmarks—MASSIVE ( n = 1,102 n{=}1{,}102 ), NyayaBench v2 ( n = 280 n{=}280 ), CLINC-150 ( n = 22,500 n{=}22{,}500 ), and Banking77 ( n = 13,000 n{=}13{,}000 )—across 18 ( α \alpha , δ \delta ) configurations. On MASSIVE at α = 0.10 \alpha{=}0.10 , LTT eliminates the ln ⁡ K \ln K union-bound penalty, achieving 94.0% guaranteed coverage versus 73.8% for Hoeffding—a 27% relative improvement. WSR betting with LTT achieves the tightest non-transfer bounds across all four datasets. On NyayaBench v2, where the small calibration set ( n cal = 134 n_{\text{cal}}{=}134 ) makes Hoeffding-family bounds infeasible below α = 0.20 \alpha{=}0.20 , Transfer-Informed Betting achieves 18.5% coverage at α = 0.10 \alpha{=}0.10 —a 5.4 × 5.4\times improvement over LTT + Hoeffding. We additionally provide a rigorous comparison with split-conformal prediction, showing that conformal methods produce prediction sets (avg. 1.67 classes at α = 0.10 \alpha{=}0.10 ) whereas selective prediction provides single-prediction risk guarantees—a fundamental distinction for deployment scenarios requiring point predictions. We apply these methods to selective prediction in agentic caching systems, where the guarantee determines when it is safe to serve cached responses autonomously, formalizing a progressive trust model. 1 Introduction Personal AI agents—voice assistants, smart home controllers, productivity bots—receive queries that are highly repetitive: the same user asks variants of “turn off the bedroom lights” or “what’s the weather in Delhi” dozens of times per week. Caching intent-classification outputs avoids calling a large language model (LLM) on every query, reducing cost from $0.01–0.05 per query to effectively $0.00 ( Bang et al. 2023 ; Zhang et al. 2025 ; Schroeder et al. 2025 ) . The safety-critical failure mode is the unsafe cache hit : the classifier assigns a query to the wrong intent, the system serves a cached response for that intent, and the agent silently performs the wrong action. For low-stakes queries (weather, factual questions), this is merely annoying. For high-stakes queries (financial transactions, device control, healthcare), it can cause real harm. Selective prediction. The standard defense is to augment the classifier with a confidence threshold τ \tau : serve the cached response only when the classifier’s confidence exceeds τ \tau , and defer to the LLM otherwise ( Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017 ) . This creates a coverage–safety tradeoff: higher τ \tau reduces unsafe hits but also reduces coverage (the fraction of queries served from cache). Prior work selects τ \tau by sweeping over a validation set and choosing a “good” operating point ( Chillara et al. 2026 ; Gill et al. 2025 ) . This provides no statistical guarantee on the unsafe rate at deployment. Finite-sample guarantees. Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets (RCPS; Bates et al. 2021 ) provide a framework for selecting τ \tau with a finite-sample certificate: given n n calibration examples and risk tolerance α \alpha , RCPS finds τ ∗ \tau^{*} such that the unsafe rate is bounded by α \alpha with probability 1 − δ 1{-}\delta . The standard instantiation uses a Hoeffding bound with a union bound over K K candidate thresholds. However, Hoeffding’s inequality is distribution-free and makes no use of the loss distribution’s structure, which can be highly favorable (binary losses with small variance when the classifier is accurate). Consider a cascade architecture where Tier 1 (a lightweight SetFit classifier) serves cached responses, and Tier 2 (an LLM) handles deferred queries. The RCPS threshold τ ∗ \tau^{*} determines the boundary: • At α = 0.10 \alpha{=}0.10 with LTT + Bernstein, MASSIVE achieves τ ∗ = 0.21 \tau^{*}{=}0.21 : queries with confidence ≥ 0.21 \geq 0.21 are served from cache, covering 94% of traffic with a guaranteed unsafe rate ≤ 10 % \leq 10\% . • The remaining 6% are deferred to the LLM, which acts as the “supervisor” for uncertain queries. • As more calibration data accumulates (Section 6.3 ), the bound tightens and τ ∗ \tau^{*} can be lowered, further increasing coverage. This is precisely the progressive trust model: cached chains start at low trust (high τ \tau , low coverage) and graduate to higher trust (lower τ \tau , higher coverage) as the guarantee tightens with more data. The RCPS certificate makes this graduation formal : the system can provably state “with probability ≥ 1 − δ \geq 1{-}\delta , the unsafe rate is at most α \alpha ” at each trust level. The progressive trust simulation (Figure 3 ) quantifies this graduation trajectory. All select thresholds empirically without finite-sample guarantees. Our work provides the missing statistical foundation. Agentic plan caching. APC ( Zhang et al. 2025 ) caches plan templates and adapts them with LLM calls. RAGCache ( Jin et al. 2024 ) caches retrieval-augmented generation outputs. Both treat caching as a systems optimization; we treat it as a selective prediction problem with formal safety constraints. Conformal prediction and RCPS. Split-conformal prediction ( Vovk et al. 2005 ) guarantees marginal coverage of prediction sets; RCPS ( Bates et al. 2021 ) generalizes this to risk-controlling prediction sets; LTT ( Angelopoulos and Bates 2022 ) extends RCPS with fixed-sequence testing that eliminates the union-bound penalty. Selective classification ( Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017 ) studies the coverage–accuracy tradeoff. A key distinction—often elided in the literature—is between set-valued conformal guarantees (true class ∈ \in prediction set) and point-prediction risk guarantees (risk ≤ α \leq\alpha on the selected prediction). We provide a rigorous empirical comparison (Section 6.6 , Table 5 ) showing that conformal prediction sets average 1.67–4.77 classes at α = 0.10 \alpha{=}0.10 – 0.20 0.20 , making them unsuitable for applications requiring single predictions. We ablate nine bound families for the selective prediction setting and introduce Transfer-Informed Betting as a novel cross-domain mechanism. Betting and e-processes. Testing by betting ( Shafer 2021 ) and confidence sequences ( Howard et al. 2021 ) provide anytime-valid inference. Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2024 show that betting-based bounds dominate classical concentration inequalities for bounded random variables. Our contribution is extending WSR betting to cross-domain settings via warm-started wealth processes (Transfer-Informed Betting, Theorem 1 ), which has not been studied previously. PAC-Bayes bounds. Classic PAC-Bayes theory ( Catoni 2007 ) provides bounds that can be tighter than Hoeffding when an informative prior is available. We apply PAC-Bayes with cross-domain transfer to selective prediction, and additionally show that betting-based transfer achieves comparable performance via a different mechanism. Calibration. Temperature scaling ( Guo et al. 2017 ) is the standard post-hoc calibration method. We use it as a diagnostic tool but apply RCPS to raw scores, since the guarantee is exact regardless of calibration. 9 Conclusion We have shown that the choice of concentration inequality, multiple-testing correction, and cross-domain transfer mechanism has a dramatic effect on the achievable coverage under finite-sample safety guarantees for selective prediction. Through a comprehensive ablation of nine bound families across four benchmark datasets, including novel contributions in testing by betting with cross-domain transfer, we establish the following practical recipe: • Large calibration sets ( n ≳ 500 n\gtrsim 500 ): Use WSR Betting + LTT or LTT + Empirical Bernstein. WSR betting adapts to the observed loss distribution; LTT eliminates the ln ⁡ K \ln K union-bound factor. On MASSIVE, both achieve 94.0% guaranteed coverage at α = 0.10 \alpha{=}0.10 . • Small calibration sets with transfer ( n ≲ 200 n\lesssim 200 ): Use Transfer-Informed Betting (our novel method, Theorem 1 ). The warm-started wealth process exploits source domain risk profiles to concentrate bounds faster, matching PAC-Bayes transfer coverage on NyayaBench v2 while providing the additional benefit of anytime validity. • Small calibration sets without transfer : Use PAC-Bayes- λ \lambda with an uninformative prior, which leverages its faster 1 / n 1/n rate. • Exact small-risk bounds : Use Clopper-Pearson + LTT when the classifier is highly accurate ( R ^ ≈ 0 \hat{R}\approx 0 ), achieving ≈ 2 × {\approx}2\times tightness over Hoeffding. • Distribution shift concerns : Use Wasserstein DRO, which explicitly budgets for shift at the cost of lower coverage. These guarantees formalize the progressive trust model for agentic systems: the RCPS certificate determines when a cached chain can safely graduate from LLM-supervised to autonomous execution. The progressive trust simulation (Section 6.3 ) quantifies this trajectory: LTT enables semi-autonomous operation (62% coverage) at n = 150 n{=}150 and autonomous operation (94% coverage) at n = 549 n{=}549 , while Hoeffding remains infeasible until n = 400 n{=}400 . As calibration data accumulates, the bounds tighten, coverage increases, and the system becomes simultaneously cheaper and safer. Limitations. The marginal risk formulation (Eq. 1 ) averages over the full query distribution; as shown in Section 6.5 , per-intent subgroup guarantees require substantially more data ( n cal ≥ 120 n_{\text{cal}}\geq 120 per class) and are infeasible for most intent classes at typical dataset sizes. PAC-Bayes transfer assumes the source and target risk profiles are related, which may not hold for domains with very different intent distributions. The one violation observed at n = 15 n{=}15 for PAC-Bayes transfer (Section 6.3 ) highlights the fragility of very small calibration sets. All bounds assume i.i.d. calibration data (except DRO, which relaxes this partially). Future work. Transfer-Informed Betting (Section 4.3 ) naturally extends to online settings: as new target queries arrive, the wealth process updates continuously, enabling anytime-valid trust certificates without fixed calibration sets. Multi-source transfer—blending risk profiles from multiple agent domains—may further tighten small- n n bounds. Hierarchical subgroup guarantees that pool information across related intents could bridge the gap between aggregate and per-class safety. Extending the betting framework to handle non-stationary query distributions (via discounted wealth processes) is a natural next step for production deployment. Keywords.
