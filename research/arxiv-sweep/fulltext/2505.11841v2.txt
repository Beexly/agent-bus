Abstract
Causal inference has become an accepted analytic framework in settings where experimentation is impossible, which is frequently the case in sports analytics, particularly for studying in-game tactics. However, subtle differences in implementation can lead to important differences in interpretation. In this work, we provide a case study to demonstrate the utility and the nuance of these approaches. Motivated by a case study of crossing in soccer, two causal questions are considered: the overall impact of crossing on shot creation (Average Treatment Effect, ATE) and its impact in plays where crossing was actually attempted (Average Treatment Effect on the Treated, ATT). Using data from Shandong Taishan Luneng Football Club’s 2017 season, we demonstrate how distinct matching strategies are used for different estimation targets - the ATE and ATT - though both aim to eliminate any spurious relationship between crossing and shot creation. Results suggest crossing yields a 1.6% additive increase in shot probability overall compared to not crossing (ATE), whereas the ATT is 5.0%. We discuss what insights can be gained from each estimand, and provide examples where one may be preferred over the alternative. Understanding and clearly framing analytics questions through a causal lens ensure rigorous analyses of complex questions.

Introduction
Over the past quarter century, statistical causal inference has made significant advancements, leading to its widespread adoption across diverse disciplines, including healthcare, economics, and education. These methods offer a framework to uncover relationships that go beyond associations, enabling researchers to address causal questions directly. However, many studies proceed with selecting estimation techniques without first clearly articulating the causal question or specifying the target of estimation
[
11
]
. This lack of clarity can undermine the reliability, interpretability, or comparability of conclusions.
Existing overview papers on causal inference often focus on the properties of specific methods but fail to address broader issues, such as whether the chosen technique aligns with the study’s goals or whether the necessary assumptions for causal validity are satisfied. This creates challenges for researchers navigating an expanding methodological landscape while attempting to apply these tools effectively to their questions. One such area is sports analytics, where causal inference is increasingly recognized as a powerful but underused tool for data-driven decision-making. See
Lopez 2020
,
Wu et al. 2021
,
Nakahara et al. 2023
,
Dona and Swartz 2023
,
Dona and Swartz 2024
,
Gibbs et al. 2022
, and
Yam and Lopez 2019
for some rare examples of causal analyses in sport. Sports provide a structured and well-defined setting for causal analysis, with clear objectives, fixed timeframes, and established rules. These characteristics facilitate the identification of confounding variables, a key challenge in causal inference, making sports a more tractable domain than many complex scientific fields. This clarity enhances the robustness and interpretability of causal estimates, reinforcing the value of causal methods in sports analytics.
In this article, we provide a tutorial for sports researchers in the form of a case study, focusing on framing causal questions and answering them using causal inference tools, illustrated through a soccer-related re-analysis of the dataset used by
Wu et al. 2021
to investigate whether crossing is an effective strategy for creating goal-scoring opportunities (shots) in soccer. In soccer or association football, a “cross” is defined as a long pass delivered from a wide area of the field toward a central area near the opponent’s goal. Crossing has traditionally been regarded as a key offensive strategy, based on the assumption of a positive correlation between the frequency of crosses and the number of goals scored. However, recent studies have challenged this belief, presenting evidence of a negative association between open-play crosses and goal-scoring effectiveness
[
24
,
20
]
.
The dataset that we shall employ to motivate this tutorial includes detailed tracking data on crossing opportunities and player movements in potential crossing zones from 30 regular-season matches of the 2017 season for Shandong Taishan Luneng Football Club in the Chinese Super League. It captures 2,225 crossing opportunities, of which 692 involved an actual cross and 1,533 did not. We take as our outcome the binary indicator of whether a shot resulted from the play. That is, we will focus on whether a shot was taken rather than whether the shot created a goal-scoring opportunity.
Additionally, several potential confounders – that is, variables that are believed to affect both the decision to cross or not and the probability of a shot being taken – are included which were derived from spatiotemporal tracking data.
Wu et al. 2021
analyzed spatiotemporal conditions leading to crossing under a potential outcomes framework and employed propensity score matching, a widely used causal inference technique, to estimate a causal effect. They concluded that crossing can be an effective strategy under specific contextual conditions. We will reproduce their analysis and provide an alternative analysis with a view to clearly defining inferential targets (estimands of interest) and demonstrating their implementation.
This paper provides a step-by-step guide to formulating the research question in the causal framework, emphasizing the importance of specifying precise causal questions, defining estimation targets, and evaluating assumptions before selecting an analysis method. By bridging the gap between theory and practice, this tutorial aims to equip practitioners in sports analytics with the tools to make rigorous and informed methodological choices tailored to their specific research goals.

Components of the potential outcomes framework for causal inference
The dominant statistical framework for causal inference over the last three decades is the potential outcomes framework, also known as the Rubin Causal Model
[
14
,
18
]
. This framework is built on the concept that each individual in a study has
potential outcomes
that correspond to each of the possible treatments of interest: in the case of a binary treatment, with would be the outcome that would be observed if they received the treatment, and the outcome that would be observed if they did not receive the treatment. The significance to this approach is that it forces the researcher to clearly specify the “treatment” alternatives (e.g., decisions, actions) under consideration, and to describe the effects as contrasts of outcomes attributable
only
to differences in these alternatives–all other factors are unchanged in defining this contrast. Any observed effect should then be attributable only to the differences in treatment alternatives, as there are no other factors which vary.
For the soccer crossing research, causal questions can be formulated in two fundamentally distinct ways: (a) What is the effect of crossing if applied to all plays? (b) What is the effect of crossing for plays where it was in fact used? The first question addresses the effectiveness of crossing to create shot opportunities across the entire population of plays or crossing opportunities, while the second question focuses on the effectiveness specifically in situations where crossing has already been attempted. (To consider a medical analogy which may help to clarify the distinction, consider a non-randomized study two competing treatments for smoking cessation - nicotine patches and hypnotherapy. The first question, the ATE, seeks to understand the impact of hypnotherapy versus pharmacotherapy across a target population such as runners experiencing pain. The second, the ATT, instead seeks to estimate the impact of hypnotherapy versus pharmacotherapy across a target population of all runners experiencing pain who are seeking relief via hypnotherapy. Notice, however, that the population of ‘all runners experiencing pain’ and ‘all runners experiencing pain who sought hypnotherapy’ may not be identical. For instance, those individuals who are skeptical of hypnotherapy may not have tried it and perhaps the skepticism would impact the effectiveness of the treatment so that the average effect across the whole population is smaller than the effect observed among those who sought hypnotherapy.
To empirically evaluate these questions, it is necessary to define the key estimands. In the following sections, we discuss the fundamental components needed to formalize the causal question in the crossing problem. Note that much of the causal inference language is described in terms of medical interventions; we clarify how this translates to sporting analyses.

Analysis of soccer data using propensity score matching: a causal inference approach
3.1
Confounding in observational soccer data
A central challenge in identifying causal effects from observational data is the presence of
confounding
. In the context of crossing decisions in soccer, a confounder is any factor that influences both the likelihood of attempting a cross (the “treatment”) and the likelihood of creating a shot (the outcome). Because crosses are not randomly assigned, players decide to cross (or not) based on situational factors such as the distance to the nearest defender or the ratio of offensive to defensive players in the box. If these factors also influence whether a shot is taken, failing to account for them can bias the estimated effect of crossing.

Of the 2,225 total “crossing opportunities” or plays (our units of analysis), 692 involved an actual cross and 1,533 did not. These opportunities span diverse match situations, from variations in score differential to differences in player positioning. As seen in Table

Cross attempts were more common when the sender had greater space from the nearest defender (mean 3.93 vs. 2.49 meters), better control of surrounding space (mean 0.79 vs. 0.54), and a shorter distance to the endline (mean 14.29 vs. 23.21 meters). Crosses also tended to occur when there were more favorable offensive-defensive player ratios in the penalty area (mean 0.43 vs. 0.20) and more often in the final ten minutes of a half (29.0% vs. 23.0%). Defenders were also more likely to attempt crosses (37.3%) compared to forwards (16.0%). The SMD is a measure of the difference in mean or proportion across the two treatment groups, scaled by a pooled measure of the variability; values of 10% or higher are considered indicative of poor covariate balance. 3.3
The propensity score
The propensity score is a function of covariates that reduces the vector
𝐗
\mathbf{X}
into a single value, capturing all measured information that is relevant to the choice of actions
[
17
]
. This score has the

Typically, the propensity score is estimated using a parametric model, such as logistic regression, or a non-parametric approach like tree-based classification
[
6
]
, to predict the probability that the sender will cross the ball given the confounders. Being a probability, the propensity score lies in the range [0,1].
Alam et al. 2019
demonstrated that a simple parametric approach such as logistic regression often outperforms more complex models for propensity score estimation in terms of covariate balance. See also
Austin 2011

This can be done by examining SMDs, variance ratios, and other distributional statistics in the matched sample, or using plots such as empirical cumulative distribution functions. 3.4
Propensity score matching
Propensity score matching is a statistical technique widely used in observational studies to reduce or eliminate confounding and estimate causal effects by mimicking the conditions of a randomized controlled trial. The method involves pairing the plays where a cross was taken and where it was not with similar propensity scores. The success of propensity score matching depends on accurate specification of the propensity score model; misspecification can result in imbalanced groups and biased estimates. Various algorithms are available to match cross and no-cross plays, differing in how they measure similarity or distance. Common approaches include exact matching, nearest neighbor matching, and caliper matching
[
4
]
. Matching can be performed on a one-to-one basis or extended to one-to-many matching configurations, and can be performed with or without replacement. Decisions concerning the closeness of the matching (for example, in caliper matching, the size of the caliper determines the similarity of the propensity scores that are considered a match) and whether to allow matching with replacement affect both the bias and variability of the resulting estimator. Requiring a very close match reduces covariate imbalance but may come at the cost of having the discard some units that cannot be matched this inflating variance and possibly changing the target population. Similarly, matching with replacement may allow for closer matches, but could inflate variance due to the re-use of some units (plays) multiple times
[
22
]
.

3.4.3
Implementation of matching in the crossing analysis
For our analysis, we used the
Matching
R package
[
21
]
to perform one-to-one nearest-neighbor propensity score matching. Nearest-neighbor matching pairs each cross-play with the no-cross-play whose propensity score is closest and – when the ATE is the estimand – also pairs each play without a cross to the closest (in propensity score) play where a cross was taken. We implemented this procedure with replacement to reduce bias, recognizing that it may increase variance
[
4
]
. Caliper matching is another commonly-used approach, however we chose not to use it in order to avoid discarding plays that lacked suitable matches. In addition, we allowed ties among matched pairs: if more than one no-cross-play had identical or nearly identical propensity scores to a given cross-play, all such no-cross-plays were retained. This strategy helps mitigate underestimation of the outcome variance that can occur when ties are not allowed
[
21
]
.
We evaluated covariate balance between plays with and without crossing by calculating the standardized absolute mean difference, expressed as a percentage. Recall from Table
1
that our original, unmatched data exhibited considerable imbalance, with most confounders demonstrating an SMD greater than 10%. We report both the Abadie–Imbens standard error and a bootstrapped standard error. The Abadie-Imbens standard error is derived assuming a known rather than estimated propensity score and hence may not accurately reflect the uncertainty in estimation, although it is widely used
[
2
]
. Specifically, using the estimated propensity score as if it were true can produce conservative confidence intervals for the average treatment effect (ATE), while its impact on the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) remains unclear. We also include a bootstrapped standard error, given its common application in practice
[
7
]
, despite evidence that bootstrap methods can fail under nearest-neighbor matching with replacement
[
1
]
.

2

4
Discussion and conclusion
This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the effectiveness of crossing in soccer through a causal inference framework. By applying propensity score matching, we aimed to estimate the Average Treatment Effect and the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated for crossing opportunities. The analyses yielded valuable insights into the impact of crossing on the probability of creating a shot under both general and specific conditions.
From a practical standpoint, these findings have direct implications for soccer coaching strategies. The overall modest effect of crossing, as reflected by the ATE, suggests that crossing is not universally effective across all situations. However, the larger effect observed in the ATT analysis indicates that crossing is more effective in plays where it was actually attempted, pointing to players’ intuition to identify favorable conditions for crossing in real-time. Coaches can leverage this information to refine training programs, emphasizing decision-making criteria that enhance crossing effectiveness, especially focusing on situational awareness and tactical execution.
Much like the hypothetical example of hypnotherapy for pain relief given above, it may be the case that the situations in which a cross occurred were ones where a success was somewhat more probable. This possibility leads to intriguing questions such as whether further investigations can discover situations or combinations of covariates
𝐗
\mathbf{X}
in which a cross is to be recommended, potentially informing future strategy. Again, analytic strategies from the causal inference framework and particularly that for precision interventions or individualized treatment rules may be relevant
[
25
]
.
The generalizability of the findings of this study should also be interpreted with caution. The data used consist of games from a single team in the Chinese Super League, which may limit the applicability of the results to other leagues or styles of play. Variations in playing styles, defensive strategies, and team tactics across different leagues could lead to different effects of crossing. Extending this analysis to data from multiple teams or leagues would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the causal impact of crossing in soccer.
A notable limitation of our analysis is that it disregards correlations within individual plays, treating each data point as independent. This assumption might not be valid and could potentially breach the no interference assumption, as soccer involves tactical interdependencies and interactions between players. Nonetheless, the use of multiple confounders may help to reduce the impact of any previous plays on a given play.
Propensity score matching effectively improved the balance of all measured covariates included in the propensity score between the cross-plays and no-cross-plays, as indicated by the reduction in standardized absolute mean differences. Nevertheless, low overlap and SMDs that remained near 10% after matching may result in residual confounding in the causal estimates. This highlights a key limitation of the analysis: although propensity score matching can mitigate confounding, it relies heavily on having measured all relevant confounders, on correctly specifying the propensity score model and on achieving adequate overlap in propensity scores.
Regression adjustment within a propensity-score matched sample is a method to address residual confounding and reduce bias due to covariate imbalance
[
19
]
. This approach can enhance the precision of causal effect estimates and make them doubly robust, meaning the estimator is consistent if either the propensity score model or the outcome model is correctly specified
[
5
]
. However, when sample size is limited, adjusting for all covariates may not be feasible. Evidence suggests that covariate adjustment is most beneficial for variables with standardized absolute mean differences exceeding 10%. In such cases, prioritizing adjustment for these covariates is recommended to mitigate imbalance
[
15
]
.
Several other analytic strategies could also be employed which avoid matching altogether, but instead rely on weighting or highly flexible modeling
[
11
]
. Matching is quite intuitive and hence attractive, but software exists to implement many other approaches including other doubly robust alternatives although many are primarily implemented for the ATE rather than the ATT
[
29
,
28
]
. It is worth noting that in
Dona and Swartz 2023
,
Dona and Swartz 2024
, and
Wu et al. 2021
the authors use the term ATE as the causal estimand whereas their measure would conventionally be described as the ATT.
Future work could explore analyzing the data within the framework of dynamic treatment regimes, which offers the potential to identify specific strategies valuable for planning purposes. While strategies in this framework are typically expressed as linear combinations of covariates, an interesting avenue for further research would be to investigate the use of decision rules. Incorporating decision rules could lead to strategies that are not only more interpretable but also more parsimonious, enhancing their practical applicability by potentially being more easily implemented for in-game play.
In conclusion, this tutorial demonstrates the importance of framing causal questions precisely and applying appropriate statistical methods to answer them. The ATE and ATT analyses provide distinct but complementary perspectives on the effectiveness of crossing. While the ATE suggests that crossing has a limited impact overall, the ATT highlights the potential for effectiveness when players choose to cross under favorable conditions.
Causal assessments of in-game tactics may in general be better answered by the ATT, whereas questions of, say, the effect of a given training regime, diet, or other less ‘dynamic’ action or intervention that could reasonably deployed on a wider scale (e.g., to all team members) is often more reasonably answered via an ATE. However, even in the case of investigating a tactic, there may also be value in estimating the ATE as well to see whether a given tactic might be more widely deployed. Evidence of effect heterogeneity should then be followed by a more in-depth analysis that would attempt to more formally quantify the settings under which a given tactic has the highest probability of success.
The insights found in this work underscore the value of integrating causal inference techniques into sports analytics to better understand and improve team strategies.
