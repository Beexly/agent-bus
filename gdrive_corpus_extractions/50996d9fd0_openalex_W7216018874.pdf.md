<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are metric operators and tracking-mechanism operators. Let \(B_t\) be the predicted bounding box at frame \(t\), \(G_t\) the ground-truth box, and \(c_t,c_t^\star\in\mathbb{R}^2\) their centers in image coordinates. The IoU operator is
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_t=\frac{|B_t\cap G_t|}{|B_t\cup G_t|}\in[0,1]\quad\text{(dimensionless)},
\]
with the assumption that both boxes are expressed in the same image coordinate system and that ground truth exists for frame \(t\). The source defines AUC as “Area Under the Curve of the success plot (varying IoU threshold)” [PAGE 3]. Formally, for \(N\) annotated frames,
\[
S(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{IoU}_i\ge\tau\},\qquad
\mathrm{AUC}=\int_0^1 S(\tau)\,d\tau\in[0,1],
\]
reported as a percentage by multiplying by 100. Breakdown regime: if ground truth is absent during occlusion, as stated in the figure caption “Grey shaded regions indicate periods of occlusion where ground truth annotations are absent” [PAGE 3], then AUC is computed only where annotation exists and does not measure invisible-interval certainty. The center-precision operator is
\[
P@20=\frac{100}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{\|c_i-c_i^\star\|_2\le 20\ \mathrm{px}\}\quad(\%),
\]
where the threshold is exactly 20 pixels, matching “Percentage of frames where the predicted bounding box center is within 20 pixels of the ground truth” [PAGE 3]. Its units are percent of annotated frames; its assumption is that a 20-pixel center error is meaningful at 1280×720 resolution, which is not scale-normalized. The restart operator is
\[
R=\frac{N_{\mathrm{restart}}}{N_{\mathrm{seq}}}\quad(\text{restarts per sequence}),
\]
matching “Avg Restarts / Seq: Frequency of tracker resets per sequence due to total loss” [PAGE 3]. It assumes the reset rule “re-initialized based on ground truth if they fail to predict the puck for 10 consecutive frames” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regime: changing that 10-frame clock changes \(R\) and can mask irreversible drift by converting it into a countable reset event.

The local-search operator is geometric. Let \(\hat c_t\) be the last estimated puck center and let
\[
\Omega_t=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^2:\|x-\hat c_t\|_2\le r_s\}
\]
be the local search region in pixels. A tracker restricted to \(\Omega_t\) can observe the target at \(t+1\) only if \(c^\star_{t+1}\in\Omega_t\). If \(c^\star_{t+1}\notin\Omega_t\), the local appearance likelihood is effectively zero for the true target and recovery is impossible without a global search or external reinitialization. This is the formal core of the source claim: “any tracking failure that pushes the true puck position outside this search space results in irreversible drift” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regimes: displacement after occlusion larger than \(r_s\); rapid camera pan; high puck velocity; or a missed frame that shifts the crop center. The paper’s global operator instead uses a full-frame encoder: “the Transformer backbone encodes the entire image at once and predicts object-specific filters in parallel” [PAGE 4]. Its assumption is that the puck remains visible somewhere in the frame; its breakdown regime is full occlusion or severe blur, because then global context has no puck-specific evidence.

The motion-blur operator is
\[
L_b=\bar v_{\mathrm{img}}\,\tau_e\quad[\mathrm{pixels}],
\]
where \(\bar v_{\mathrm{img}}\) is image-plane speed in pixels per second and \(\tau_e\) is exposure time in seconds, under the assumption of approximately constant image velocity during exposure. The source identifies the critical scale as a “3–5 pixel object” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: when \(L_b\gtrsim d_p\) for puck support \(d_p=3\)–5 pixels, the object becomes “aliased and elongated” [PAGE 4], and shape/appearance evidence is not identifiable. The sampling operator for a stride-\(s\) feature map has Nyquist cutoff
\[
f_c(s)=\frac{1}{2s\,p_{\mathrm{px}}}\quad[\mathrm{cycles\,pixel^{-1}}],
\]
where \(p_{\mathrm{px}}\) is the image sampling pitch in pixels and \(s=2\) in the source’s description of “early convolutional layers with a stride of two” [PAGE 4]. Original-lattice content above \(f_c(2)\) is discarded; for a 3–5 pixel puck, edge content is already near the original Nyquist limit, so stride-2 downsampling can remove the fine detail needed for detection. Breakdown regime: \(f_{\mathrm{edge}}>f_c(s)\). The probabilistic operator in PrDiMP is a conditional density \(p(x_t\mid z_{1:t})\) over state \(x_t\), with entropy
\[
H[X_t\mid Z_{1:t}]=-\int p(x_t\mid z_{1:t})\log p(x_t\mid z_{1:t})\,dx_t\quad[\mathrm{nats}],
\]
under a continuous-state assumption. The source says PrDiMP “spreads uncertainty over a larger spatial region” during occlusion [PAGE 4], which raises entropy; its breakdown regime is that a high-entropy density can maintain weak overlap while accumulating “small localization errors that eventually require reinitialization” [PAGE 4].

The paper’s lineage is explicitly staged: [PAGE 2] summarizes “Fixed-template similarity →online discriminative learning →probabilistic estimation →Transformer-based reasoning →global multi-object tracking.” SiamFC is described as learning “a generic similarity function between a template (the initial tracker object’s appearance) and a search region (a small region in the next frame that may contain the tracked object)” [PAGE 2]. Because it relies “solely on a fixed target template extracted in the first frame,” it struggles with “significant appearance changes, background distractors, and occlusions” [PAGE 2]. DiMP and SuperDiMP add an “online optimization module that learns a target-specific classifier during tracking” [PAGE 2]. PrDiMP moves to “predicting a full conditional density over the target state rather than a single confidence score” [PAGE 2]. ToMP “replaces the optimization-based model predictor with a Transformer that directly predicts the weights of both the classifier and the bounding-box regressor” [PAGE 2]. TaMOs extends this to “sharing global frame-level features and predicting object-specific correlation filters in parallel” [PAGE 2]. The hockey-specific lineage is also present: Yang et al. use nine consecutive frames for temporal inference; Pidaparthy et al. use player positions and optical flow; Li et al. use controlled/free-moving states; Vats et al. use PuckNet 3D heatmaps; Sarkhoosh et al. test Faster R-CNN and YOLOv8 [PAGE 2–3]. The physics/motion lineage includes Physics-Guided Fusion with “kinematic constraints, collision dynamics, and depth cues” and TrackNetV4 with “motion-attention maps and learnable motion prompts,” but the paper warns that “their reliance on domain-specific assumptions (e.g., known physical parameters, consistent scene structure, reliable motion differencing) limits direct transfer to ice hockey broadcast video, where aliasing, irregular occlusions, and extreme camera motion are frequent” [PAGE 2].

The multi-week traps are longitudinal evaluation traps, not week-specific source claims. First, the 10-frame reinitialization rule [PAGE 3] can turn catastrophic drift into a restart statistic, so a multi-week deployment that quietly reinitializes from ground truth would look better than an autonomous tracker. Second, the dataset’s visibility profile—typical clips with “16–40 visible-puck frames before an occlusion” and puck visible in only “\sim 68% of frames” [PAGE 3]—means multi-week data will be dominated by occluded and near-occluded intervals unless visibility is stratified. Third, the metric conflict between AUC and P@20 [PAGE 3] can produce rank reversals across weeks: a tracker that preserves coarse overlap can win AUC while losing sharp reacquisition, and a tracker that reacquires precisely can win P@20 while collapsing during occlusion. Fourth, frozen backbones and 30-epoch fine-tuning on 145,000 annotated frames [PAGE 3] may not transfer across broadcast style, compression level, ice glare, camera pan rate, or team uniform color. Fifth, the citation mismatch around SiamFC [PAGE 2] versus reference [11] on [PAGE 5] is a reminder that lineage claims should be audited against the reference list, not accepted from prose alone. The retrocausal lesson is that the modern global-reasoning branch did not eliminate the original small-object physics; it moved the failure from local-search drift to global-context collapse under invisibility and aliasing.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The tracker’s “crisis habit regression” is the reversion to its learned prior when evidence weakens. For local trackers, the habit is the cropped search region and the template/classifier update around the last known location. The paper’s failure vocabulary is exactly this: “irreversible drift, stick-locking, and blur-induced feature loss” [PAGE 1], and “when the puck passes behind a stick or merges with player clutter, these trackers frequently commit to the wrong object and never recover” [PAGE 4]. In recursive terms, the tracker’s model of the puck is not a stable belief state; under crisis it collapses to “the most target-like patch inside my current crop.” If the crisis is caused by an occluder, the tracker can lock onto the occluder itself: “visually similar distractors such as hockey sticks, skates, or patchy ice textures” [PAGE 4]. That is habit regression because the model does not expand its hypothesis space; it intensifies local matching.

The counter-trap is the global-reasoning alternative. TaMOs avoids the local crop trap by encoding the whole frame, which yields the best P@20 and fewest restarts [PAGE 3–4]. But its counter-trap is that global re-detection is not the same as continuous state estimation: “TaMOs tends to drop overlap quickly during occlusions—its predictions collapse when the puck is fully invisible—but once the puck reappears, the global frame encoding allows TaMOs to re-acquire it sharply” [PAGE 4]. The recursive theory-of-mind error for an analyst is to read TaMOs’ 55.65% P@20 as continuous certainty, when it is better interpreted as sharp reacquisition after visibility returns. Likewise, PrDiMP’s 31.25% AUC can be misread as reliable tracking, but the paper says its probabilistic head “spreads uncertainty over a larger spatial region” and “the same uncertainty causes PrDiMP to accumulate small localization errors that eventually require reinitialization” [PAGE 4]. The analyst’s model of the tracker must therefore distinguish “I know where it is” from “I have a broad overlap” and from “I can re-find it when it reappears.”

The clock is the evaluation and belief-update horizon. The source’s explicit clock is the 10-consecutive-missed-frame reset [PAGE 3]. The data clock is the “16–40 visible-puck frames before an occlusion” and the “\sim 68%” visibility rate [PAGE 3]. The metric clock is the 20-pixel center threshold [PAGE 3]. These clocks interact recursively: a tracker that fails for 10 frames is externally reset, so its internal model never has to represent long-duration uncertainty; a tracker that is evaluated only on annotated visible frames is not rewarded for correctly representing invisibility; and an analyst who sees a low restart count may infer stability when the low count is partly a product of reset policy. The correct counter-trap is not “always reason globally” or “always use probabilistic regression,” but “match the hypothesis space to the visibility regime and preserve fine detail when the object is only 3–5 pixels” [PAGE 4]. The clock also governs trust: P@20 rewards sharp reacquisition at the moment of reappearance, AUC rewards coarse overlap survival, and restarts reward not crossing the reset threshold. No single clock is the truth.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/optical layer vetoes any claim of fine-grained robust puck tracking that does not address resolution and sampling. The source’s own scale is “a 3–5 pixel object in broadcast footage,” and it links failure to “early convolutional layers with a stride of two, which discards fine-grained spatial detail” [PAGE 4]. Formally, if the object support is \(d_p=3\)–5 pixels and the feature sampling period is \(s p_{\mathrm{px}}\) with \(s=2\), then the high-frequency edge content needed to distinguish a puck from a stick tape, skate blade, or ice patch is at or beyond the stride-2 Nyquist cutoff \(f_c(2)=1/(4p_{\mathrm{px}})\) [PAGE 4]. If motion blur has length \(L_b=\bar v_{\mathrm{img}}\tau_e\gtrsim d_p\), the object becomes “aliased and elongated” [PAGE 4], so appearance likelihood is not identifiable. This layer vetoes the strongest universal claim: even TaMOs, the best P@20 model, “still suffers when the puck becomes heavily blurred or aliased” [PAGE 4]. The proof is not a ratio of unrelated quantities; it is a sampling-and-blur impossibility condition: below the pixel-support and above the blur-length threshold, the observation does not contain the spatial information required for reliable identity.

The biomechanical/occlusion layer vetoes local-search recovery after occlusion. The source says the puck “frequently disappears behind sticks, skates, boards, and players” [PAGE 3]. Geometric occlusion condition: for camera center \(C\), puck point \(P_t\), and occluder volume \(O_j\), full occlusion occurs when the segment \(L(C,P_t)\) intersects \(O_j\) and no alternative camera view is available. Partial occlusion creates distractor patches. Under local search \(\Omega_t\), if the puck reappears outside \(\Omega_t\), recovery requires a global search; the source states “any tracking failure that pushes the true puck position outside this search space results in irreversible drift” [PAGE 4]. This layer vetoes the claim that a better local classifier alone solves hockey tracking: the failure is a support mismatch between the target’s reappearance location and the tracker’s search support. It also explains stick-locking: the stick is a biomechanically plausible occluder and a visually similar distractor, so the local tracker’s crisis habit is to commit to the occluder [PAGE 4].

The weather/environmental layer must be adjudicated carefully. Ice hockey broadcast is indoor, and the source does not provide weather variables. Therefore any weather-based causal explanation is vetoed by absence of evidence and by the indoor setting. The environmental layer that the source does support is broadcast artifact and camera motion: “compression noise, and rapid camera pans” [PAGE 3], and “aliasing, irregular occlusions, and extreme camera motion” [PAGE 2]. Ice glare, lighting changes, and condensation could be plausible proxies, but they are not measured in the source; they cannot be used to override the measured physical/optical and occlusion mechanisms. The cross-layer verdict is: physical/optical veto is primary for fine detail and blur; biomechanical/occlusion veto is primary for local-search recovery; weather is not a validated causal layer for this indoor dataset and must not be substituted for compression, pan, glare, or occlusion variables.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
