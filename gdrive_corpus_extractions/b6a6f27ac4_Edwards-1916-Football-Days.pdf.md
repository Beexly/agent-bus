<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

The book's own genealogy is *retrocausal* in the strict sense: it is a 1916 document that writes *forward from* 1870s heroes and *backward into* 1916's "greatest team" claims, and it is also a *living transmission chain* — families in which football instinct is "passed down from father to son and from brother to brother" [PAGE 347]. Formalize transmission as \( \Gamma \in [0,1] \), the probability that a coaching or playing innovation survives one generation (roughly 5–15 yr for football). The book's Poe family, Hallowell family, Riggs family (five brothers), Young family (four at Cornell), Warner brothers (Bill and Glenn), Draper brothers (all three backs), and Hinkey brothers (Frank and Louis) are the empirical sample. The long-run trap here is *confusing survival with quality*: the text notes that in the Lamar fumble-run of 1885 ("Lamar who had not come across the field to back Toler up, had been able to get the ball on the bound," [PAGE 141]), Walter Camp's own account of the era holds the run to be almost accidental; the more interesting retrocausal claim is that the Yale players' *pre-commitment to the kick coverage* — running "down the field … to within a few yards of Toler" — is an early instance of a *specialized coverage unit*, which is a structural innovation that survives, while the "Lamar run" as a *heroic narrative* is discarded.

Four multi-week traps are worth flagging.

**Trap 1 — "More practice = better."** [PAGE 129] records the Corbin/George era policy: "if a man did not think he was in any condition to play he reported to the captain … I was told to take a football and run around the track, which was a half mile long and encircled the football field." The retrocausal chain runs from this to the present-day *athlete-monitoring* movement, but a naive reading of the book would invert the causality: the 1880s *did* produce "strong, hard and fast" teams [PAGE 129], so the temptation is to infer that the regime was net-positive. The correct genealogy is that the same policy produced Morris Ely's *unreported* broken ribs [PAGE 281] and Von Gammon's death [PAGE 285] — i.e., the *survivorship* sample is a biased estimate of the regime's true cost.

**Trap 2 — "Forward pass is the innovation."** The modernization from the pre-1889 phrase signals [PAGE 149] to the 1889 numerals to the Haughton/ Warner era is not a single jump; the book records Haughton's systematic *photographic* coaching [PAGE 317, 269 — "Haughton coaches a great deal by the use of photographs"], which is a *measurement* innovation, not a rules innovation. The retrocausal trap is to attribute the depth-3 ruse (red ball [PAGE 305]) to the rules change, when the actual cause is measurement.

**Trap 3 — "The Indian team was a gimmick."** [PAGE 419] records that Carlisle was the "champion travelers of the game," and that Warner sewed leather-football shapes onto jerseys [PAGE 305]; the trap is to read this as showmanship. The correct genealogy is that it is an *adversarial information-attack* family — the same family as Hodge stealing Corbin's cap [PAGE 149] and the same family that, decades later, produces opponent-signal-decoding [PAGE 441, 872]. The line runs through to modern scouting.

**Trap 4 — "Winning is a personality trait."** The book insists on "spirit" as causal ("If you won't be beat, you can't be beat" — Cochran's quote of Johnny Poe's 1896 message, [PAGE 493]). The retrocausal reading is dangerous: it underdetermines the *physical* content (in the 1896 Harvard game, "two of our best men … were seriously hurt," and the message "put iron in each man's soul" [PAGE 493]). The correct genealogy is that the message operated on *pain-reporting thresholds*, not on force production — which is exactly why later training regimes (Ely's ribs, Jarvis's tibia) produce the very injuries the "spirit" narrative is designed to hide.

</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

I model crisis behavior with a three-way choice: **regress** (habitual response), **counter-trap** (meta-level adversarial response), or **clock** (collapse to time-pressure action). The regress mode is the default: under contact, players default to the habits of their training. [PAGE 129]'s "reported to the captain" regime teaches the habit *not to report*, and Ely's ribs [PAGE 281] are the direct observable consequence: the player's default is "help it out by putting a shin guard under my jersey." [PAGE 279]'s Jarvis — who "danced several times" on a "complete fracture of the tibia" — is the extreme regress case: a *social* habit (return to normal function) overrides a *physical* signal.

The counter-trap mode is depth ≥ 2 opponent modeling. Warner's leather jerseys [PAGE 305] and Haughton's red ball [PAGE 305] are the cleanest pair: Warner traps the defense at depth 2; Haughton traps Warner at depth 3 by *pre-committing to a rule-legal counter* and by *signaling honestly* via the letter. The formal signature of the counter-trap is that the trap-*setter* pays a pre-commitment cost to make the response non-credible: Haughton's cost is the ball-painting, which is legible to the referee; Warner's cost is the sewn ball, which is legible to any competent observer.

The clock mode is where all three collapse. The 1899 Poe kick [PAGE 103] is the canonical instance: "thirty-six seconds left for play." The decision hierarchy is \((\)kick or not\() \to (\)who drops back\() \to (\)what protection form\()\), and each move compresses the available \( \Delta t \) to the next. The book records the collapse exactly: "Crowds had left the field before Poe made his great goal kick. They had accepted a Yale victory as inevitable" [PAGE 103] — the crowd's collapse to the *base rate* is an independent clock-mode observation, and it is the same collapse we see in [PAGE 481–483]'s end-of-game confusion, where a "custom" (referee recall) is mistaken for a rule and the second-half collapse follows.

The recursive structure is: **regress → counter-trap → clock**, and the switches are not voluntary. The Yerkes–Dodson-shaped behavior is visible when the source records *extreme* anxiety producing *reduced* performance: [PAGE 299] — "over-anxiety to score" caused two Princeton fumbles near the Harvard line ("Both errors were hardly excusable and were traceable to over-anxiety to score"). This is the formal signature that *habit regression* is dominant at high arousal unless counter-trap rehearsals have been layered in (the 1896 "message" [PAGE 493] is precisely such a rehearsal, pre-scripted for use at the half).

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

A "veto" here means a physical constraint that makes a tactical prescription inoperative regardless of skill, intent, or spirit. I adjudicate four layers.

**Physical veto — mass and impulse.** [PAGE 269] describes the offensive–defensive collision as "the crash of conflict." The force law \( F_c = m_{\text{eff}}\,\Delta v/\Delta t \) [N] is not a metaphor; it is the mechanism. Heffelfinger's recorded 25-pound gain in one season [PAGE 137] (from 178 to ~203 lb) is a *mass* increase; under fixed \( \Delta v \), this is a linear increase in collision momentum transfer. The veto is that a 60- to 80-pound disadvantage can be driven below zero only by a geometry change (lower pad level), which is the actual content of the "play low" coaching maxim [PAGE 481–483, Sanford to Moss]. Every "spirit" claim in the book that ignores mass-asymmetry must be downgraded.

**Biomechanical veto — joint thresholds.** Hillebrand's broken clavicle [PAGE 99] and Jarvis's tibial fracture [PAGE 279] are *threshold* events: once the strain energy exceeds the yield strength of the tissue, the fracture is a *material* fact that no amount of will changes. The book itself proves the veto by recording Ely's near-impossible recovery ("discharged by the doctor in less than three weeks" [PAGE 281]) — the recovery is verified, but only for a *young* athlete with a specific fracture pattern; it is *not* evidence that the training regimen was net-positive. The veto rule is: any claim that a player "played through" a fracture must be read as *reporting latency*, not as *healing rate*.

**Meteorological veto — thermal step and wind.** [PAGE 379]: the 1901 Michigan team traveled from Ann Arbor "12° below zero" to Pasadena "80° at 8 p.m." — a 39-K dry-bulb step. A human acclimation time constant for this magnitude is on the order of 10–14 days; the source's own record (Michigan won 49–0, "their first scrimmage since") shows the thermal step did *not* prevent victory, which is itself the veto: *thermal acclimation is not a first-order determinant of scrimmage outcome at the college level*. [PAGE 191]'s gale behind Odlin's punt is the other meteorological veto: the punt that carried "several yards beyond their goal line" is a *wind-assisted* kick, and any model of kicking that omits the wind field is over-fit to a calm day.

**Officiating and clock veto.** The 1908 New Haven second-half delay [PAGE 475–477] and the 1899 Poe kick with "thirty-six seconds" [PAGE 103] show the clock and the officials as *institutional* veto layers. The formal statement is: the on-field product is invariant under "more spirit" once \( \Delta t_{\text{eff}} \) or \( t_{\text{remaining}} \) cross a threshold. The 1887 "Bloody Angle" [PAGE 343] is the historical proof-of-concept: "an injured player was not allowed to leave the field of play without the consent of the opponents' captain" [PAGE 339] — this is an *adjudication veto* that, once in force, makes substitution-based recovery impossible, so the physical state of the eleven is fixed at kickoff. The correct cross-layer order is physical < biomechanical < meteorological < officiating, with the *strongest* veto at the top being the institutional layer, because the institution can *deny* the physical remedy.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

Each scenario below specifies a verifiable ground-truth condition that an RLVR policy is required to satisfy before its reward is credited. The predictions are stated so that they are falsifiable from the book *alone*, without any external game outcome.

**Scenario 1 — Thermal-step veto (Michigan, New Year's 1901).**
*Setup.* [PAGE 379] states "it was \(12^\circ\) below zero" at departure and "\(80^\circ\) at 8 p.m." at Pasadena. The verifier is the *temperature trajectory*: the difference between the two readings is \( \Delta T = 92^\circ\text{F} \approx 51\ \text{K} \) (or \( \sim 39\ \text{K} \) if the daily range reported is between \(-12^\circ\text{C}\) and \( 27^\circ\text{C}\)). *RLVR condition:* the policy must predict the *direction* of the outcome as a function of \( \Delta T \) and the acclimation interval (12 days). *Prediction:* if a counterfactual policy claims that thermal step *reverses* the expected outcome (i.e., the cold-acclimated side loses because of the heat), the verifier returns *fail*, because the book records the opposite direction: Michigan "won by a score of 49 to 0," and "was their first scrimmage since" [PAGE 379]. The policy is only credited if its prediction matches the *observed direction*, not the direction implied by a thermal-strain model. This is exactly a *cross-layer veto* on the thermal explanation.

**Scenario 2 — Clock veto (Poe's kick, 1899, New Haven).**
*Setup.* [PAGE 103]: "there were but thirty-six seconds left for play." *RLVR condition:* the policy must output whether the goal kick is a *rational* action at \( t_{\text{remaining}} = 36\ \text{s} \), given a *pre-kick* time budget of \( t_{\text{seal}} + t_{\text{release}} + t_{\text{flight}} \). *Prediction:* any policy that prescribes kicking as *rational* at \( t_{\text{remaining}} < 10\ \text{s} \) is *vetoed*, because the trajectory of the receipt (the ball passing "over the cross bar" [PAGE 103]) requires a minimum flight-and-setup window. Conversely, the policy is credited only if it reproduces the observed *shift* in the criterion: at 36 s, kick; at <10 s, the counterfactual action is a *hail mary* or a *scrum*. A second verifiable sub-condition: the book records that "Crowds had left the field before Poe made his great goal kick" [PAGE 103] — the policy must assign a lower *prior* to the goal than the crowd did, which is the correct depth-2 inference.

**Scenario 3 — Reporting-latency veto (Jarvis, Brown, Andrews Field).**
*Setup.* [PAGE 279]: "a complete fracture of the tibia," yet "He had gone to the training table for dinner," discarded crutches, "danced several times," and the X-ray showed the fracture only the *next morning*. *RLVR condition:* the policy must distinguish *injury* from *report* — two separate variables. The verifier checks whether the policy attributes the discrepancy to a *physical-healing* rate (false) or to a *reporting-latency* rate (true). *Prediction:* any policy that predicts a *healing* time consistent with dancing on a complete tibial fracture is *vetoed* — the physical state is invariant under the social behavior. The credited prediction is polar opposite: *the fracture was present throughout; the reporting channel was closed*, and the estimated reporting latency is on the order of 6–14 hours (from tackle to X-ray).

*Cross-check.* The combined scenarios form an RLVR triple: (1) cannot be credited unless the policy respects the *thermal* veto; (2) cannot be credited unless the policy respects the *clock* veto; (3) cannot be credited unless the policy respects the *reporting-latency* veto. The three are non-redundant — each vetoes a distinct wrong explanation — and each has a verifiable ground truth in the source: [PAGE 379], [PAGE 103], [PAGE 279]. A policy credited on all three must (i) allow the temperature to be a real variable, (ii) allow time to be a real variable, and (iii) allow observation to be a real variable, which is precisely the structure the book's own narrative routinely violates — and which the present adjudication therefore corrects.
