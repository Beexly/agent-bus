(3) Assuming that the bargaining unit of a factor is small relative to total stock when modern input markets—platform labor, specialized equipment leasing, patented intermediates—often feature concentrated suppliers, violating Condition A and invalidating marginal-productivity factor pricing. Each trap can recur on a weekly cadence in forecasting and risk assessment because the underlying assumptions are rarely re-audited against the institutional detail on [PAGE 98].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the adjustment clock.

Under competitive pressure, firm managers exhibit crisis habit regression: they revert to pre-crisis habits of adding successive increments of the variable agency to the fixed plant, exactly the operation described by the truncated diminishing-returns law on [PAGE 98]. Because each manager believes rivals will not match capacity, each overinvests relative to the most economical size. This is a first-order belief: "I expand because demand is high."

The counter-trap is second-order: rivals anticipate this regression and preemptively expand or differentiate their product bundles to preserve partial monopoly. If all firms reason recursively at this level, the industry does not converge to Knight’s "sufficient number" of plants at most economical size; instead it collapses toward a small number of differentiated large establishments, each with L_i > 0. [PAGE 98 footnote] The counter-trap defeats the competitive postulate because Condition B—small establishment more efficient than large—is overridden by strategic preemption.

The clock is the physical time constant τ of adjustment to most economical size. τ depends on lead time to construct new plant, depreciation schedules of existing capital, and the divisibility of agencies. If the crisis duration T_c is shorter than τ, firms cannot reach q_i^*; excess capacity persists and competition remains imperfect. If T_c >> τ, entry and exit may restore the competitive configuration Knight describes. [PAGE 98] Knight’s static treatment assumes τ → 0, as if capital and product were infinitely divisible and adjustable. This is a singularity: when τ is large (durable, lumpy capital), the competitive equilibrium on [PAGE 98] is not reachable within the crisis window, and the monopoly tendency remains unimpeded.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The competitive equilibrium on [PAGE 98] is vetoed if any of three cross-layer constraints binds.

Physical veto—capital lumpiness. Many production processes have a minimum efficient scale q_min imposed by engineering: a blast furnace, a semiconductor fabrication line, or a railroad right-of-way cannot operate below q_min without ceasing to function. If q_min / Y > θ for any meaningful competitive threshold θ (e.g., θ = 0.1 or 0.2), then Knight’s Condition B fails: a small establishment is not more efficient than a larger one because no small establishment can exist. [PAGE 98] The assumption that "amounts of all elements ... were freely variable without limit" is physically false. Veto: the monopoly tendency is unimpeded, exactly as [PAGE 98] warns.

Biomechanical veto—labor indivisibility. Labor agency x_L is bounded by human physiology: a worker supplies at most 24 hours per day, with a productive ceiling far below that. The bargaining unit u_L cannot be subdivided below one worker-hour (or one worker). If the total stock X_L of a specialized agency is small—e.g., a rare engineering skill or a geographically concentrated workforce—then β_L = u_L / X_L may not satisfy β_L << 1. [PAGE 98] Veto: factor-market power emerges from biological indivisibility, and competitive factor pricing collapses into bargaining.

Weather veto—non-divisibility of agricultural product. For land-based production, output Q = F(land, labor, ω), where ω is weather. If ω renders harvest binary—total failure or full yield—then the product is not continuously divisible; expected output E[Q] is not equal to certain output Q, and diminishing returns to land operate under stochastic marginal product. [PAGE 98] Veto: the continuously divisible product assumption on [PAGE 98] fails under covariance risk, so competitive pricing of the product and of the land agency is not attainable without state-contingent markets that Knight does not specify.

Adjudication rule: If any veto triggers, the industry cannot settle into the competitive configuration described on [PAGE 98]. It moves toward partial monopoly (product differentiation), complete monopoly (natural scale), or factor rent capture (bargaining power). The "sufficient number" of plants competing effectively for productive agencies is an unattainable equilibrium under these vetoes.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1: Minimum efficient scale veto.
Veto condition (verifiable): Observe an industry in which minimum efficient scale q* satisfies q*/Y > 0.5, meaning a single plant can serve more than half of industry output. This is directly observable from plant-level production and shipment data. Knight’s Condition B on [PAGE 98] requires q*/Y << 1; the condition fails.
Prediction: The market converges to one or few firms. Consistent with [PAGE 98] footnote, local traffic in railroads becomes monopolistic while through traffic remains competitive; entry cannot occur because replicating q* is not a "relatively small" establishment. The number of plants does not settle at a competitive "sufficient number."

Scenario 2: Factor bargaining unit veto.
Veto condition (verifiable): Observe an input market where one bargaining unit u_k controls u_k/X_k ≥ 0.20 of the total stock of a competing agency (e.g., a single union local, a single platform, or a single specialized equipment lessor). This share is observable from employment or contract registries. Knight’s Condition A on [PAGE 98] requires u_k/X_k << 1; the condition fails.
Prediction: Factor price is set by bilateral bargaining rather than competitive marginal productivity. Profit is transferred to the factor owner as rent or dissipated in rent-seeking. Plants cannot "compete effectively for the productive agencies" because the agencies are not supplied on competitive terms; the competitive distributive outcome on [PAGE 98] collapses.

Scenario 3: Product differentiation veto.
Veto condition (verifiable): Observe goods whose cross-price elasticity |∂x_i/∂p_j| is finite and significantly below the perfect-competition benchmark, or whose product bundles p_i and p_j differ measurably in utility attributes. Knight’s footnote on [PAGE 98] states that "practically every business enjoys a certain degree of partial monopoly" because it "does not turn out exactly the same product (bundle of utilities)." This is verifiable through product-attribute data and demand estimation.
Prediction: Each firm faces downward-sloping demand, sets P_i > MC_i (L_i > 0), and sustains excess capacity. Long-run zero profit obtains only if entry is perfectly elastic and differentiation costless, which [PAGE 98] denies. The industry supports more firms than pure competition but each with market power, contradicting the implication that a "sufficient number" of plants guarantees competitive agency pricing.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
