The paper’s core move is not a single formula but a chain of transformations: event counts become possession-adjusted defensive indicators, those indicators become SHAP-ranked features, the selected features become a UMAP embedding, the embedding becomes GMM clusters, and the clusters become “roles” and then “similar players.” The audit must therefore trace each non-invertible step and ask what evidence is lost. The source states the foundational distinction: “A player✣s role for a team can be distinct from their playing position” [PAGE 1], and it defines pressing as “a defensive tactic whereby players collectively exert pressure on the opponent with the ball, and the likely pass recipients, with the objective of winning the ball back, usually in the opponent✣s half” [PAGE 2]. That definition is collective and behavioural; the paper measures it through player event data. The first gray zone is therefore construct validity: a team-level label is derived from “passes per defensive action (PPDA; Trainor, 2014)” [PAGE 2], and then “teams laying within the lower quartile were classified as high pressing teams” [PAGE 8]. Players inherit a team label, but the paper later notes that team-level pressing intensity may change while individual pressing intensity can “stay constant or alter in an inverse direction to the team” [PAGE 14]. Thus, a high-pressing team label is not identical to an individual’s pressing role.

The second gray zone is feature selection. The paper uses “SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017” [PAGE 3] values from XGBoost to identify pressing KPIs. This is semi-supervised: the binary team label supervises the feature ranking, so the “optimal” variables are optimal for separating high- versus non-high-pressing team members, not necessarily for defining a universal pressing role. The paper itself admits “default parameters when implementing the XGBoost algorithm were used” [PAGE 14]. That matters because SHAP attributions are model-relative and can be unstable under correlated features, class imbalance, or hyperparameter changes. The top-four variables are then used in UMAP. The source says “UMAP seeks to preserve local neighbourhoods in a dataset” [PAGE 3-4], which is a local-neighbourhood claim, not a global-metric claim. Any Euclidean distance in the embedded space is therefore a representational artifact as well as a similarity measure. The paper uses a 50% GMM decision boundary: “A decision boundary was applied that classified each player to a role if that cluster probability was 50% or greater” [PAGE 8]. That converts soft posterior probabilities into hard labels and can hide boundary instability. The four-cluster solution is selected by NbClust, and the clusters are then named “Pressers,” “Passive,” “Ball Recovers,” and “Intermediate Pressers” [PAGE 10]. The naming is interpretive. The paper acknowledges that “it is up to the data scientist to produce labels that match the language used within each club” [PAGE 15], so the label is not a natural kind.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper cites standard methods but does not print all equations; the following are formal reconstructions with source anchors and breakdown regimes.

1. PPDA and high-pressing label. The source says the pressing variable was “Passes per Defensive Action (PPDA; Trainor, 2014)” [PAGE 8]. A standard formalization is
\[
\mathrm{PPDA}_i=\frac{N_i^{\text{opp passes}}}{N_i^{\text{def actions}}},
\]
with units of passes per defensive action, dimensionless. The percentile label is
\[
u_i=100\,\hat F_{\mathrm{PPDA}}(\mathrm{PPDA}_i),\qquad H_i=\mathbf{1}[u_i\le 25],
\]
where \(u_i\in[0,100]\) is a percentile and \(H_i\in\{0,1\}\) is a Bernoulli high-pressing indicator. The source states “teams laying within the lower quartile were classified as high pressing teams” [PAGE 8]. Singularity: if \(N_i^{\text{def actions}}=0\), PPDA is undefined or infinite; near zero it is unstable. Breakdown: provider-specific defensive-action definitions can change both numerator and denominator; possession duration and game state can confound PPDA; the lower-quartile cut is discontinuous at ties.

2. SHAP feature importance. The paper uses “SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017” [PAGE 3] values to select pressing KPIs. For feature \(j\), model \(f\), and feature set \(F\),
\[
\phi_j(f,x)=\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{j\}}
\frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}
\left[f(x_{S\cup\{j\}})-f(x_S)\right].
\]
Units are the output units of \(f\): probability if \(f\) is a probability, or log-odds if \(f\) is a margin. The efficiency identity is \(\sum_j\phi_j(f,x)=f(x)-\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\). Breakdown: with correlated event features, Shapley attributions are non-unique in interpretation; default XGBoost hyperparameters, class imbalance, and train-test leakage can change rankings. The paper admits “default parameters when implementing the XGBoost algorithm were used” [PAGE 14].

3. UMAP embedding. The source says UMAP “seeks to preserve local neighbourhoods in a dataset” [PAGE 3-4]. Let \(z_i=\mathcal{U}(x_i)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) be the embedding. A standard cross-entropy objective is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{UMAP}}
=\sum_{i,j}\left[
w_{ij}^{(h)}\log\frac{w_{ij}^{(h)}}{w_{ij}^{(l)}}
+(1-w_{ij}^{(h)})\log\frac{1-w_{ij}^{(h)}}{1-w_{ij}^{(l)}}
\right],
\]
where \(w^{(h)}\) and \(w^{(l)}\) are high- and low-dimensional edge weights, dimensionless. Units of \(z_i\) are embedding coordinates, not physical units. Breakdown: UMAP is stochastic, can distort global distances, can split or merge local neighbourhoods, and is not invariant under seed, \(n_{\text{neighbors}}\), or minimum-distance changes. A disconnected neighbour graph or too-large neighbourhood makes the local-preservation claim ill-posed.

4. Gaussian mixture model. For embedding points \(z_i\),
\[
p(z_i\mid\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(z_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k),
\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k=1,
\]
with posterior responsibilities
\[
\gamma_{ik}=
\frac{\pi_k\mathcal{N}(z_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}
{\sum_{m=1}^{K}\pi_m\mathcal{N}(z_i\mid\mu_m,\Sigma_m)}.
\]
Here \(\gamma_{ik}\in[0,1]\) is a probability and \(\pi_k\) is a mixing proportion. The paper uses seven broad positional groups and later four winger clusters; it states “A decision boundary was applied that classified each player to a role if that cluster probability was 50% or greater” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: if a component has fewer than \(d+1\) effective points or a rank-deficient covariance, \(\Sigma_k\) is singular; EM can drive a variance to zero and the likelihood unbounded; label switching makes cluster numbering non-identifiable; 0.5 is a hard decision rule, not a calibrated certainty threshold.

5. Role similarity. The paper says “The percentage similarity was declared as the proportion of the distance for each player✣s data point over the distance of the maximum datapoint away from Eze” [PAGE 9]. A transparent reconstruction is
\[
d_{ij}=\|z_i-z_j\|_2,\qquad
s_{ij}=100\left(1-\frac{d_{ij}}{\max_{m\in\mathcal{C}}d_{im}}\right),
\]
where \(d_{ij}\) is in embedding-coordinate units and \(s_{ij}\) is percent. The exact formula is not printed in the source, so this is an operator reconstruction, not a verbatim source equation. Breakdown: if the candidate set \(\mathcal{C}\) is empty or the maximum distance is zero, \(s_{ij}\) is undefined; Euclidean distance in UMAP space is not a physical or tactical metric; changing UMAP seed or feature set changes the ranking. The reported values, such as Wilfried Zaha “94.82851” [PAGE 11], are therefore representation-specific.

6. Association tests. The paper reports a chi-square test: “A chi-square test was performed for players from possession dominant teams and pressing teams, which established a significant association between the two variables (p < 0.001)” [PAGE 13]. The statistic is
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{r,c}\frac{(O_{rc}-E_{rc})^2}{E_{rc}},
\qquad E_{rc}=\frac{R_rC_c}{N},
\]
dimensionless, with \(df=(R-1)(C-1)\). Breakdown: expected cell counts below about five invalidate the asymptotic approximation; dependence across players in the same team violates independence. The two-sample \(t\)-test is
\[
t=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}},
\]
dimensionless. Breakdown: non-normal, heavily skewed count data, unequal variances, repeated players, and multiple testing across four variables can inflate false positives. The source reports \(p<0.001\) for Padj Pressures, Padj Tackles & Interceptions, Aggressive Actions, and \(p<0.01\) for Fhalf Ball Recoveries [PAGE 13], but those are marginal tests, not a causal model.

7. Aggressive Actions and ball recoveries. The source defines “Aggressive Actions” as “the number of tackles, pressure and fouls a player makes within 2 seconds of an opposition ball receipt” [PAGE 9]. Units are discrete action counts per event window. “Fhalf Ball Recoveries” denotes recoveries in the opposition half [PAGE 9]. These are count or rate-like variables; they are not SI physical units. Breakdown: provider event definitions, timestamp precision, and possession adjustment can change counts.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is visible in the paper’s citations and method choices. Early performance analysis separated football into repeatable moments such as established attack, established defence, offensive transition, and defensive transition, with set pieces added because they account for a large share of goals [PAGE 2]. PPDA then became a defensive-intensity proxy: the source cites “passes per defensive action (PPDA; Trainor, 2014)” [PAGE 2] and uses it to approximate high pressing [PAGE 8]. Statsbomb’s possession-adjusted metrics are also cited, including a “sigmoid function to adjust their defensive metrics” [PAGE 2]. The machine-learning lineage runs through PCA, t-SNE, UMAP, GMM, XGBoost, and SHAP. The paper cites “SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017” [PAGE 3], UMAP [PAGE 3-4], NbClust [PAGE 9], and GMM soft clustering [PAGE 4]. It positions itself against supervised role classification and against limited event sets, and it claims novelty in using SHAP values for feature selection of pressing roles [PAGE 13-14].

Retrocausally, later data layers change the meaning of earlier roles. The paper says “Future methods should combine physical data sources, such as from tracking data, to enable greater specificity in player role classification” [PAGE 1] and “Future methods should incorporate other data sources such as from positional/tracking data to establish more niche roles” [PAGE 14]. It also points to “Rest Defence (Peters et al., 2025)” and “sophisticated pressing models” [PAGE 14]. Those future operators retroactively reinterpret the current clusters: today’s “Intermediate Presser” may become a different construct once off-ball running intensity, rest-defence positioning, and physical load are measured. The retrocausal trap is treating the 2026 event-only role as a final ontology rather than a provisional compression.

Multi-week traps accumulate over scouting and performance cycles. First, role reification: a GMM label becomes a fixed identity despite the source warning that “roles will be specific to a manager or a style of play” [PAGE 15]. Second, possession-adjustment forgetting: analysts may quote Aggressive Actions because it is intuitive, even though the paper says high aggressive volume is associated with non-pressing teams and may be an artifact of low possession [PAGE 10, PAGE 13]. Third, similarity anchoring: the top-five list for Eberechi Eze, including Wilfried Zaha at “94.82851” similarity [PAGE 11], can anchor recruitment over weeks; but the metric is Euclidean distance in a UMAP space, not a transferable physical or tactical guarantee. Fourth, temporal leakage: using future-season information to validate past role evolution, or pooling seasons without manager covariates, can make Eze’s “94 % classification” [PAGE 12] look more stable than it is. Fifth, provider drift: pressure events are operationally defined differently across providers [PAGE 2], so a multi-week database built from mixed providers can drift silently. These are not one-off errors; they compound across repeated reports.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive loop is: analyst models coach, coach models scout, scout models recruitment committee, and all model the player’s adaptability. The paper’s role labels are meant to be intelligible to practitioners, but the source says “it is up to the data scientist to produce labels that match the language used within each club” [PAGE 15]. That means the analyst must anticipate how a coach will hear “Presser” versus “Intermediate Presser,” and the scout must anticipate how the coach will interpret “similar to Eze.” The paper’s similarity tool is useful for the question “who has a similar event signature?” but not automatically for “who can perform this role in our system next season?” The source’s own limitation says roles are manager- and style-specific [PAGE 15], and the Eze example shows a player can evolve while the team’s pressing intensity moves in another direction [PAGE 12-14].

Crisis habit regression appears under deadline pressure. The analyst may regress from possession-adjusted variables to raw pressure, tackle, and foul counts because they are legible and immediate. That is exactly the trap the paper flags: “players with high volume non-adjusted variables, but with low volume adjusted variables, may be an artifact of playing for low possession teams” [PAGE 10]. A scout may regress from the semi-supervised role similarity to reputation or position labels. A coach may regress from the model’s soft posterior to a binary “he presses” judgment.

The counter-trap is overcorrection: treating SHAP, UMAP, and GMM as objective and final. The model is semi-supervised, the XGBoost used default parameters [PAGE 14], the 50% GMM boundary is arbitrary [PAGE 8], and the cluster names are subjective [PAGE 15]. The counter-trap replaces human bias with opaque model bias. The clock is the temporal dimension. Roles change across seasons, managers, injuries, and team tactics; Eze moved from passive to near-Presser to “Intermediate Presser (94 % classification)” [PAGE 12]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore ask: what does the coach believe the role means now, what will the scout believe after the next data refresh, and what will the player believe about their own role? The paper’s method can inform those conversations but cannot close the loop without tracking, medical, and weather context.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto proof. Let \(P\) denote physical and biomechanical state, \(W\) weather and environmental context, \(T\) tactical context, and \(E\) the event-data signature. The paper observes only \(E\), so the implicit model is \(E=\Pi(P,W,T)+\varepsilon\). If \(\Pi\) is many-to-one—and it must be, because event counts compress continuous motion, fatigue, and environmental conditions—then there exist \(P_i\neq P_j\) with the same or near-same \(E\). Therefore high event similarity \(s(E_i,E_j)\) does not imply \(P_i\simeq P_j\). The source supports the physical gap: pressing is “a very active yet tiresome action” that “cannot be maintained during long spells of opposition possession” [PAGE 2]. Event counts are therefore censored by fatigue and physical capacity. The source also says “Future methods should incorporate other data sources such as from positional/tracking data to establish more niche roles” [PAGE 14], and it calls for combining “physical data sources, such as from tracking data” [PAGE 1]. Weather is not modeled at all.

Veto rule: any claim that two players have equivalent pressing roles, equivalent recruitment fit, equivalent injury resilience, or equivalent weather robustness is inadmissible from this paper alone unless physical/tracking/weather evidence is supplied. If independent biomechanical load, high-intensity running, acceleration count, or weather-conditioned event rates contradict the event-derived role, the event label must be downgraded from “role” to “event-data signature.” This is a proof by non-identifiability, not a claim that the paper is wrong. The paper’s event-only result is valid for its stated scope; the cross-layer claim is what gets vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
