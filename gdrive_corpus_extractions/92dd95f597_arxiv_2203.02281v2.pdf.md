<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let the video be a sequence of frames \(I_n\), sampled at frame rate \(f_s\) in reciprocal seconds:

\[
t_n = \frac{n}{f_s}, \qquad \Delta t = \frac{1}{f_s}, \qquad [f_s] = \mathrm{s^{-1}}.
\]

This matters because the review reports speeds in frames per second, for example “detection speed of 23.7 FPS and a tracking speed of 11.3 FPS” ([PAGE 16]). FPS is not merely throughput; it determines the maximum object displacement between observations.

For a planar field, camera calibration and homography are standard operators. In homogeneous pixel coordinates \(\tilde{p} = (u,v,1)^T\), the projective relation is

\[
\tilde{p} \sim H \tilde{q},
\]

where \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) is defined up to scale, \(\tilde{q}\) is a field-plane coordinate vector, and \(u,v\) are pixel coordinates. With full camera intrinsics and extrinsics,

\[
\tilde{p} \sim K [R \mid t] X,
\]

where \(K\) is the intrinsic matrix in pixel units, \(R\) is rotation, \(t\) is translation in meters, and \(X\) is a 3D world point in meters. The review states the necessity of calibration: “For a precise capture of the movements of the players, tracking the ball and actions of referees, etc. on the field or court, it is necessary to calibrate the camera [5, 8] and also to use an appropriate number of cameras to cover the field” ([PAGE 10]). Breakdown regime: planar homography assumes all relevant points lie on the field plane. A ball at height \(h\) in meters violates this assumption. The induced pixel displacement error scales approximately as

\[
e_{\text{height}} \approx \frac{f_{\text{pix}} h}{Z},
\]

where \(f_{\text{pix}}\) is focal length in pixels and \(Z\) is camera-to-object distance in meters. If \(e_{\text{height}}\) exceeds the ball’s projected diameter, trajectory reconstruction becomes non-identifiable.

For ball detection, the projected diameter in pixels is

\[
w_{\text{pix}} = \frac{f_{\text{pix}} d}{Z},
\]

where \(d\) is ball diameter in meters and \(Z\) is distance in meters. A detector has a minimum viable scale \(w_{\min}\) in pixels. If

\[
w_{\text{pix}} < w_{\min},
\]

the ball is below the detector’s effective aperture. This is the formal version of the review’s warning: “The proposed model is not robust and it does not detect the ball as it is small in size in far view frames” ([PAGE 18]).

For player tracking, a common state vector is

\[
x_n =
\begin{bmatrix}
X_n & Y_n & \dot{X}_n & \dot{Y}_n
\end{bmatrix}^T,
\]

with positions in meters and velocities in meters per second. A constant-velocity model is

\[
x_{n+1} = F x_n + w_n,
\]

with

\[
F =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & \Delta t & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 & \Delta t \\
0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\]

and process noise covariance \(Q\) derived from acceleration uncertainty. If acceleration noise has standard deviation \(\sigma_a\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), then position-noise variance scales as \(\sigma_a^2 \Delta t^4\) and velocity-noise variance scales as \(\sigma_a^2 \Delta t^2\). Measurement is

\[
z_n = H x_n + v_n,
\]

with measurement noise covariance \(R\) in \(\mathrm{m^2}\). The Kalman update is

\[
K_n = P_n^- H^T (H P_n^- H^T + R)^{-1}.
\]

Breakdown regime: if the true motion exceeds the process model, the normalized innovation squared

\[
d^2 = (z_n - H \hat{x}_n^-)^T S_n^{-1} (z_n - H \hat{x}_n^-)
\]

exceeds the gating threshold. The track is then rejected or fragmented. This aligns with the review’s statement that filters fail when the ball reappears after occlusion ([PAGE 49]).

During occlusion for \(m\) frames, covariance evolves as

\[
P_{n+m} = F^m P_n (F^m)^T + \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} F^k Q (F^k)^T.
\]

The spatial uncertainty radius is approximately

\[
r_{\text{unc}} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(P_{n+m})}.
\]

If \(r_{\text{unc}}\) exceeds the association gate \(G\), re-identification becomes ambiguous. The review identifies severe occlusion and identity switch as persistent failures ([PAGE 44] and [PAGE 45]).

Multi-object association can be written as

\[
\pi^* = \arg\min_{\pi} \sum_i
\left[
\|z_i - H \hat{x}_{\pi(i)}\|_{S^{-1}}^2
+
\lambda_A D_A(i,\pi(i))
\right],
\]

where the first term is motion consistency, \(D_A\) is appearance distance, and \(\lambda_A\) weights appearance evidence. If same-jersey players overlap, \(D_A\) loses discriminative power and identity switch probability increases. The review notes this directly: “when the player with the same jersey color is occluded, the ID of the player is switched” ([PAGE 16]).

Tracking performance can be formalized by MOTA:

\[
\mathrm{MOTA} =
1 -
\frac{FN + FP + IDSW}{\sum_t GT_t},
\]

where \(FN\) is false negatives, \(FP\) is false positives, \(IDSW\) is identity switches, and \(GT_t\) is ground-truth object count at frame \(t\). MOTA is dimensionless. The review reports MOTA and GMOTA results, for example “tracking accuracy of 96% and 60% on MOTA and GMOTA metrics respectively with a detection speed of 23 FPS” ([PAGE 16]). However, without common capture conditions, these numbers are not directly commensurable.

Real-time feasibility requires aggregate latency

\[
L_{\text{agg}} = L_{\text{capture}} + L_{\text{inference}} + L_{\text{association}} + L_{\text{output}},
\]

with units in seconds. For causal frame-by-frame operation,

\[
L_{\text{agg}} \leq \Delta t = \frac{1}{f_s}.
\]

If this inequality fails, the system drops frames or becomes non-causal. The review’s embedded-platform discussion is relevant here: “Among embedded AI computing platforms, NVidia Jetson devices provide low-power computing and high-performance support for artificial intelligence based visual recognition systems” ([PAGE 38]). The formal constraint is that edge deployment must satisfy the same latency inequality, not merely the same accuracy metric.

For classification or event recognition, a standard information-theoretic loss is cross-entropy:

\[
H(p,q) = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 q_k,
\]

with units of bits per sample. The review notes that algorithm performance depends on dataset annotation and evaluation metrics: “the performance of the algorithm depends on the type (annotation parameters) of dataset used, which is carried out based on loss functions and evaluation metrics” ([PAGE 47]). Breakdown regime: if annotation distribution differs from deployment distribution, the learned posterior \(q\) is miscalibrated and the expected loss increases.

Finally, background subtraction can be written as

\[
F_n(u,v) =
\begin{cases}
1, & |I_n(u,v) - B_n(u,v)| > \tau, \\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

where \(I_n\) is the current frame, \(B_n\) is the background model, \(\tau\) is an intensity threshold, and \(F_n\) is the foreground mask. The review states the limitation: “background subtraction based techniques generate foreground regions by subtracting the background frame from the current frame (i.e. by detecting moving objects in the frame), however they fails to detect the playfield lines” ([PAGE 11]). It also lists illumination, weather, and shadows as challenges ([PAGE 9] “The challenges here include distinguishing the color of the playfield from that of the stadium, lighting conditions and sometimes weather, viewing angles and the shadows.”). Breakdown regime: if illumination changes so that \(|I_n - B_n| > \tau\) over large non-object regions, the foreground mask becomes invalid.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source implies several interacting agents: the coach, the referee or umpire, the broadcast operator, the analyst, and the automated vision system. Each agent has a different belief-update problem. The coach wants tactical state; the referee wants event legality; the broadcaster wants highlights; the vision system must infer latent identities and events from noisy pixels. The review frames this as “higher level semantic interpretation” being useful when real-time analysis and minimal human intervention are required ([PAGE 2]). The recursive issue is that the system’s output changes the beliefs of the human agents, and the human agents’ requirements change the system’s objective.

A crisis occurs when visual evidence collapses: severe occlusion, identity switch, motion blur, or ball loss. In that crisis, the habit regression is to fall back to simple heuristics: jersey color, nearest-neighbor motion, or single-camera assumptions. The review identifies this regression explicitly: “when the player with the same jersey color is occluded, the ID of the player is switched” ([PAGE 16]). It also says that “Accurate pose detection as shown in figure 19 is still a major challenge to identify whether the player is running, jumping or walking as shown in figure 20, and also to handle the severe occlusions or identity switch among players” ([PAGE 45]). The recursive failure is that the system interprets ambiguous evidence as if it were certain, causing downstream tactical or event labels to become unstable.

The counter-trap is to maintain identity belief as a probability distribution over time rather than a hard label. Let \(P(i \mid z_{1:n})\) be the posterior probability that track \(i\) corresponds to player identity \(i\). The Shannon entropy

\[
\mathcal{H}_n = -\sum_i P(i \mid z_{1:n}) \log_2 P(i \mid z_{1:n})
\]

has units of bits. When \(\mathcal{H}_n\) is low, identity is stable. When occlusion removes measurements, \(\mathcal{H}_n\) increases. A counter-trap policy must reduce entropy by using temporal memory, multi-camera geometry, pose cues, and deep re-identification embeddings. The review points toward this direction: “a framework is needed to be proposed that learns object identities with deep representations and improves tracking using information pertaining to identity” ([PAGE 47]).

The clock is the third recursive constraint. A coach or official cannot wait indefinitely. The review says that real-time use is constrained by “accuracy, computation speed, size of the model etc.” ([PAGE 47]). If the system waits too long to resolve identity, the game state has already changed. If it decides too early, it may commit to the wrong identity. The optimal policy is therefore bounded by latency:

\[
L_{\text{agg}} \leq \frac{1}{f_s}.
\]

If this bound is violated, the system’s belief update is stale. The recursive trap is that high accuracy models often require more computation, which increases latency, which reduces effective temporal resolution, which increases identity uncertainty. Thus, the system must solve a joint estimation-control problem: how much computation to spend before committing to an identity or event label.

The source’s discussion of virtual umpires and assistant coaches shows the recursive stakes. For virtual umpires, the system must produce a decision that humans accept as authoritative ([PAGE 41]). For assistant coaches, the system must produce tactical suggestions that are robust to dynamic changes ([PAGE 42]). In both cases, the system’s credibility depends on calibrated uncertainty. If the system hides uncertainty, human agents may over-trust it during crisis frames. If the system exposes too much uncertainty, it becomes operationally useless. The correct recursive design is therefore not maximal accuracy alone, but calibrated confidence under a hard real-time clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The adjudication uses three veto layers: physical/optical, biomechanical/pose, and environmental/weather-illumination. If any layer is violated, the downstream claim is inadmissible regardless of reported accuracy.

Physical/optical veto. For a ball of diameter \(d\) in meters at distance \(Z\) in meters, the projected pixel diameter is

\[
w_{\text{pix}} = \frac{f_{\text{pix}} d}{Z}.
\]

Let \(w_{\min}\) be the minimum detector scale in pixels. If

\[
w_{\text{pix}} < w_{\min},
\]

then the ball is physically below the detector’s viable aperture. This is not a learning failure; it is an imaging failure. The review states the problem directly: “Due to the size, speed, velocity and unstructured motion of the ball compared to players and playfield in various sports, it is still an open issue to detect and track the ball” ([PAGE 46]). A second physical veto comes from motion blur. If exposure time is \(t_{\text{exp}}\) in seconds and object speed is \(v\) in meters per second, the blur length in object space is

\[
L_{\text{blur}} = v t_{\text{exp}},
\]

and in pixels,

\[
L_{\text{blur,pix}} = \frac{f_{\text{pix}} v t_{\text{exp}}}{Z}.
\]

If

\[
L_{\text{blur,pix}} > \alpha w_{\text{pix}},
\]

with \(\alpha\) a shape-integrity constant, the ball or player limb is smeared beyond reliable recognition. The review’s warning about motion blur ([PAGE 2]) therefore becomes a formal veto condition.

Biomechanical/pose veto. Pose-dependent event recognition, such as determining whether a player is running, jumping, or walking, requires sufficient visible skeletal evidence. Let \(\phi\) be the fraction of required joints visible in the frame. If

\[
\phi < \phi_{\min},
\]

then the pose class posterior is underdetermined. The review says: “Accurate pose detection as shown in figure 19 is still a major challenge to identify whether the player is running, jumping or walking as shown in figure 20, and also to handle the severe occlusions or identity switch among players” ([PAGE 45]). The veto proof is simple: if the necessary biomechanical observables are absent, the classifier cannot infer the action with bounded uncertainty. Any high-confidence action label under severe occlusion is therefore inadmissible.

A related biomechanical veto is identity ambiguity. If multiple same-jersey players occupy overlapping image regions, appearance evidence becomes non-identifying. Let \(D_A(i,j)\) be appearance distance between candidates. If

\[
D_A(i,j) < \delta_A
\]

for distinct players, then appearance alone cannot resolve identity. The review confirms this failure: “when the player with the same jersey color is occluded, the ID of the player is switched” ([PAGE 16]). The veto is not that the tracker is poorly trained; it is that the observation model is rank-deficient.

Environmental/weather-illumination veto. Background subtraction assumes approximate temporal constancy of background intensity. The foreground operator

\[
F_n(u,v) =
\begin{cases}
1, & |I_n(u,v) - B_n(u,v)| > \tau, \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

fails when global illumination changes. If the illumination-induced intensity change \(\Delta I\) exceeds \(\tau\) over non-object regions, the mask becomes invalid. The review states: “The challenges here include distinguishing the color of the playfield from that of the stadium, lighting conditions and sometimes weather, viewing angles and the shadows” ([PAGE 9]). It also notes that background subtraction fails to detect playfield lines ([PAGE 11]). Therefore, any pipeline that relies on naive background subtraction is vetoed under strong weather or lighting transitions.

Cross-layer proof. Suppose a system claims real-time ball tracking. The claim is admissible only if:

\[
w_{\text{pix}} \geq w_{\min},
\]

\[
L_{\text{blur,pix}} \leq \alpha w_{\text{pix}},
\]

\[
L_{\text{agg}} \leq \frac{1}{f_s},
\]

and identity association satisfies

\[
r_{\text{unc}} \leq G.
\]

If any inequality fails, the claim is vetoed. This adjudication preserves the source’s own warnings: calibration is necessary ([PAGE 10]), occlusion causes identity switch ([PAGE 44]), and real-time constraints limit deployment ([PAGE 47]).
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

When the uncertainty radius \(r_{\text{unc}} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(P_{n+m})}\) exceeds the association gate \(G\), re-identification is not unique. Prediction: identity switch count increases, and downstream event attribution becomes unreliable. The source directly states that “severe occlusions cause critical problem of identity switch among the players” ([PAGE 49]) and that “A player’s similar appearance to another and severe occlusions make it difficult to track and identify players, referees and goal keepers reliably” ([PAGE 49]). This veto is verifiable by measuring identity-switch rate under controlled overlap duration.

Scenario 3: Edge-device latency veto. Suppose an embedded platform executes detection and tracking at \(f_s = 25\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), so the frame budget is

\[
\Delta t = \frac{1}{25} = 0.04\ \mathrm{s}.
\]

If measured inference latency is \(L_{\text{inf}} = 0.08\ \mathrm{s}\), then

\[
L_{\text{agg}} > \Delta t.
\]

The system cannot maintain causal frame-by-frame processing. Prediction: dropped frames, increased effective inter-frame displacement, degraded association, and reduced real-time reliability. The source notes that embedded platforms are used for low-power visual recognition ([PAGE 38]) and that real-time deployment is constrained by accuracy, computation speed, and model size ([PAGE 47]). This veto is verifiable by measuring wall-clock latency on the target device.

These three vetoes are not arbitrary heuristics. They correspond to source-identified failure regimes: small/fast ball detection ([PAGE 18], [PAGE 46]), occlusion-induced identity switch ([PAGE 44], [PAGE 49]), and real-time compute constraints ([PAGE 47]). A system that fails any of these verifiable conditions should be rejected for the claimed deployment, regardless of offline benchmark performance. The source itself warns that cross-sport and cross-setup comparisons are difficult because “experiments, requirements; situations and infrastructure in every scenario differ” ([PAGE 50]). Therefore, RLVR-style verification must be local to the deployment regime, using physical measurement, latency measurement, and identity-switch accounting rather than relying on incomparable aggregate scores.
