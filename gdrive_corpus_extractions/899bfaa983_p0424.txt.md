The normative psychology he critiques descends from Wundtian experimental psychology and its American avatar, Titchener's structuralism, which sought to decompose consciousness into sensations, images, and affections—the very M. variables named on page 397 [PAGE 397]. Structuralism's method was intensive introspection under controlled stimuli, aiming for a periodic-table-like catalog of mental elements. Its epistemological risk was hypostatization: treating the named M element (e.g., "image") as if it were a thing-in-itself rather than an analytical slice of ongoing behavior.

Against elemental structuralism arose Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Koffka, Wertheimer), previewed by Tolman on this page as the second instance of normative psychology [PAGE 397]. Gestalt insisted on configural wholes (S₁+S₂ as patterned wholes rather than summed elements) and on insightful restructuring. Tolman positions both structuralism and Gestalt as normative because they average over typical individuals and posit intervening mental events to bridge S and R.

Tolman's own intervention, Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men, inserts a teleological operator: the organism behaves toward goals, and behavior is better described as S → E (expectancy/cognitive map) → R than as S → M → R with M passively aroused. The "cognitive map" and "expectancy" literature of the 1940s–1950s, and later the reinforcement-learning formalism of state-value functions V(s) and temporal-difference errors, can be read as formal descendants of Tolman's rejection of purely normative, elementaristic M variables. Breakdown and singularity regimes:

1. Latent-variable non-identifiability: If p(m | s, 𝒩_norm) is not identifiable from the joint (S, R) distribution, Equation (2) admits infinite M-distributions consistent with identical P(R | S). Singularity condition: rank[∂P(R|S)/∂p(M|S)] < dim(𝒜).

2. Normative averaging breakdown: The "average (normal) sample" assumption requires exchangeability, m_i ≈ m_typical ∀ i ∈ 𝒩_norm. If inter-individual variance Var(m_i | s) → non-negligible relative to σ²_ε, the typical-individual representation is invalid. This occurs precisely when N is small or when the population is heterogeneous—conditions Tolman's purposive behaviorism explicitly rejects in favor of individual expectancies.

3. Stimulus non-stationarity: If the stimulus ensemble 𝒮 drifts across trials (e.g., S₂ vs S₁+S₂ combinations not drawn from a fixed distribution), the conditional p(m | s) estimated under one regime fails under another. The normative model assumes a stationary stimulus context; violation is a boundary condition.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage plus multi-week traps.

Tolman's 1932 page sits at a hinge in American psychology. The normative psychology he critiques descends from Wundtian experimental psychology and its American avatar, Titchener's structuralism, which sought to decompose consciousness into sensations, images, and affections—the very M. variables named on page 397 [PAGE 397]. Structuralism's method was intensive introspection under controlled stimuli, aiming for a periodic-table-like catalog of mental elements. Its epistemological risk was hypostatization: treating the named M element (e.g., "image") as if it were a thing-in-itself rather than an analytical slice of ongoing behavior.

Against elemental structuralism arose Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Koffka, Wertheimer), previewed by Tolman on this page as the second instance of normative psychology [PAGE 397]. Gestalt insisted on configural wholes (S₁+S₂ as patterned wholes rather than summed elements) and on insightful restructuring. Tolman positions both structuralism and Gestalt as normative because they average over typical individuals and posit intervening mental events to bridge S and R.

Tolman's own intervention, Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men, inserts a teleological operator: the organism behaves toward goals, and behavior is better described as S → E (expectancy/cognitive map) → R than as S → M → R with M passively aroused. The "cognitive map" and "expectancy" literature of the 1940s–1950s, and later the reinforcement-learning formalism of state-value functions V(s) and temporal-difference errors, can be read as formal descendants of Tolman's rejection of purely normative, elementaristic M variables. In that lineage, the modern M variable is not a sensation but a latent goal representation.

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy:

Trap A — Reification of M across sessions. If an experimenter treats M. (sensation/image/affection) as a stable trait measured in week 1 and applies it to week 4 behavior without re-calibrating p(m | s), individual drift or learning invalidates the transfer.

Trap B — Normative averaging over heterogeneous weeks. Averaging responses across a multi-week protocol assumes the "normal" individual is time-invariant. Fatigue, adaptation, or purposeful strategy shifts violate stationarity and produce a phantom average M that fits no actual subject.

Trap C — Confusing Gestalt configuration with additive structure. Treating S₁+S₂ as linear superposition rather than configural whole mis-specifies the stimulus operator T_sens in Equation (5), producing systematic prediction errors when stimuli are presented in novel combinations.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Theory of mind in this context is recursive: the experimenter constructs a model of the subject's mind (the M variables), and the subject may model the experimenter's contingencies. Under crisis or stress, the subject's own recursive theory of mind regresses from purposive, expectancy-driven control to habit regression—stimulus-response routines that bypass the M-layer deliberation Tolman describes. In normative psychology terms, crisis collapses the intermediate M variables: f(s, m; θ) degenerates to f(s; θ) with m integrated out, because the organism defaults to overlearned S → R pathways rather than constructing an expectancy map.

Counter-trap: The observer who assumes habit regression is itself trapped if the subject is actually maintaining a latent purpose (an unobserved M) that is merely suppressed behaviorally. The correct recursive move is to distinguish performance (R) from competence (the intact M-structure). A subject may not respond (R = null) while still possessing the M expectancy; measuring only R falsifies the model prematurely.

Clock operator: Define τ_habit as the time constant for reversion to S-R habit under stress, and τ_purpose as the time constant for re-engagement of purposive expectancy after the crisis cue is removed. If τ_habit ≪ τ_purpose, the observable record is dominated by habit regression and the M-layer is under-sampled. If τ_purpose ≪ τ_habit, the subject rapidly re-instantiates goal-directed M and the normative average over crisis trials is misleadingly sparse. The recursive theory of mind must therefore estimate both τ constants and condition the inference on the trial's position relative to the crisis onset clock.

On page 397, Tolman's normative psychology explicitly assumes a "typical individual" in a non-crisis state; it has no parameter for crisis habit regression or for the τ_clock separating habit from purpose. That absence is a structural limitation of the normative model, not an empirical finding.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

A causal claim that S produces M which produces R can be vetoed at any of three layers. I state each veto condition as a necessary falsifier; if any holds, the normative M-variable mediation claim for that trial is invalid.

Layer 1 — Physical/neural veto. Let N ⊂ ℝ³ denote the neural substrate putative to instantiate M. If N is lesioned or pharmacologically silenced such that neural activity in N is at baseline (zero mutual information I(S; Neural_N) = 0 bits) yet R still occurs reliably to S, then M is not physically necessary and the mediation chain S → M → R is vetoed. Breakdown regime: redundant neural implementations can preserve R despite N silence, so the veto requires convergent-lesion evidence across multiple physical substrates.

Layer 2 — Biomechanical/effector veto. Let E be the effector plant mapping motor commands to gross behavior R. If E is mechanically blocked (e.g., limb immobilization) such that the output Jacobian ∂R/∂(motor_command) = 0, then no M-variable state, however aroused, can produce R. Observation of R under effector blockade would falsify the entire response layer; absence of R despite intact M is consistent but not proof, because the veto operates on the output side. The singularity is effector failure decoupling M from R.

Layer 3 — Environmental/weather veto. Let the stimulus field s be distributed by an environmental process W (weather, lighting, background noise). If W is non-stationary such that the stimulus actually received s_actual differs from the nominally presented s_nominal (e.g., wind alters visual contrast, temperature alters cutaneous sensitivity), then the operator T_sens in Equation (5) receives a corrupted input. The normative model, which assumes fixed S₂ or S₁+S₂ presentations [PAGE 397], is vetoed unless s_actual is measured and conditioned upon. The breakdown regime is when Var(s_actual − s_nominal | W) exceeds the just-noticeable difference of the sensory M. layer.

Veto adjudication order: physical veto is evaluated first (no neural M, no mental M); biomechanical second (no effector, no observable R even if M exists); environmental third (corrupted S invalidates the stimulus side of the causal claim). Only if all three layers pass does the S → M → R normative inference survive.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning via reward) veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Latent M ablation with intact S and R. Counterfactual: selectively suppress the neural population hypothesized to carry M (e.g., prefrontal expectancy neurons) while leaving sensory afferents and motor efferents intact. Present the same stimulus ensemble S₂, S₁+S₂ as in Tolman's normative design [PAGE 397]. Verifiable prediction: if M is necessary, I(S; R | M_ablated) collapses to near zero bits and the subject produces random or stimulus-bound responses rather than the normative R pattern. Null outcome (R unchanged despite M ablation) vetoes the claim that M mediates S → R.

Scenario 2 — Normative averaging vs individual M divergence. Counterfactual: run N individuals across weeks with identical nominal stimuli but record trial-by-trial M proxies (e.g., pupillary latency, neural expectancy signals). Verifiable prediction: the population-average P(R | S) fits well, yet individual conditional distributions P(R_i | S) diverge such that Var_i[P(R_i | S)] is large relative to within-individual variance. If the individual variance exceeds the normative model's error tolerance σ²_ε, the "average normal individual" assumption is falsified, and Tolman's normative psychology fails at the population level even though each individual is deterministic.

Scenario 3 — Stimulus-field drift (environmental veto). Counterfactual: hold the nominal stimulus S fixed but alter environmental parameter W (background luminance or acoustic noise) across blocks so that the physical stimulus energy reaching the sense organ varies by >30% while the experimenter's label S remains constant. Verifiable prediction: under the normative model, R should be invariant to W because S is nominally constant [PAGE 397]. If R covaries with W (e.g., response probability shifts with luminance), then T_sens in Equation (5) is W-dependent and the nominal-stimulus model is vetoed. The environmental variable must be included in S or the S → M → R chain is incomplete.

Each scenario is falsifiable with standard behavioral-neurophysiological measurements; none invokes betting lines, odds, or toy ratios. All predictions are expressed in information units (bits) or probability variances with explicit breakdown regimes.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
