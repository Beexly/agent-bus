Kolmogorov-based similarity distance [PAGE 4]. Exact Kolmogorov Complexity \(K(\cdot)\) is uncomputable; a standard computable surrogate is normalized compression distance:
\[
d_{\mathrm{NCD}}(x, y) \;[\dimensionless] = \frac{C(xy) - \min\{C(x), C(y)\}}{\max\{C(x), C(y)\}} \in [0,1],
\]
where \(C(\cdot)\) is compressed length in bits. Breakdown: \(\max\{C(x),C(y)\} \to 0\) (empty or incompressible strings) produces division by zero; requires sequence length above the entropy-compression threshold.

Cosine similarity between attribute vectors [PAGE 4]:
\[
\cos(\theta_{s,t}) = \frac{\mathbf{x}_s \cdot \mathbf{x}_t}{\|\mathbf{x}_s\|_2 \, \|\mathbf{x}_t\|_2} \in [-1,1].
\]
Breakdown: \(\|\mathbf{x}_t\|_2 = 0\) or \(\|\mathbf{x}_s\|_2 = 0\) (zero vector, e.g., new player with no games played) yields undefined similarity; must trigger a veto to base rate.

Similarity factor percentage [PAGE 4]:
\[
S(s,t) \,[\%] = 100 \cdot \sigma\bigl(-\lambda \, d_{\mathrm{NCD}}(\mathbf{x}_s,\mathbf{x}_t)\bigr), \quad \lambda > 0,
\]
where \(\sigma\) maps distance to a \([0,100]\%\) scale.

Non-parametric injury probability over window \(W \in \{7,14,21,30\}\) days [PAGE 4]:
\[
\widehat{P}(I_t=1 \mid W) = \frac{\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} K_h\bigl(S(s,t)\bigr) \, I_s}{\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} K_h\bigl(S(s,t)\bigr)}, \quad K_h(u) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2h^2}\right),
\]
with \(I_s \in \{0,1\}\) the observed injury indicator of similar athlete \(s\) in window \(W\), and bandwidth \(h\) in percentage points. Breakdown: denominator \(= 0\) when no similar athlete falls within bandwidth; sparse \(I_s = 1\) count produces high-variance estimates.

Reported operating characteristics [PAGE 5]:
\[
\mathrm{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = 0.70, \qquad \pi = \frac{P}{P+N} = 0.016.
\]
Positive predictive value is not identifiable from TPR and prevalence alone without FPR; Bayes form:
\[
\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi + \mathrm{FPR}\,(1-\pi)}.
\]
The 80% NBA accuracy claim [PAGE 5] is a single-vendor point estimate with no confidence interval or cohort size disclosed.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage plus multi-week traps.

The patent's intellectual ancestry runs from algorithmic information theory to sports-injury epidemiology. Kolmogorov Complexity (1965) supplies the distance metric for player-attribute comparison; associative memory architectures trace to Kohonen and Hopfield distributed associative retrieval; cosine similarity is a classical information-retrieval operator (Salton vector-space model). In sports science, the load–injury relationship builds on workload models (acute:chronic load ratios; Gabbett, Hulin, Blanch, Timpka) in which prior injury history is a dominant risk factor for recurrence. Kinduct Technologies of Halifax, Nova Scotia, is named as the NBA data source [PAGE 5], placing the system within athlete-management-system (AMS) deployments that centralize physiological, mechanical, and biomechanical telemetry.

Multi-week traps. The system restricts similarity computation to trailing windows of 7, 14, or 30 days [PAGE 4] and forecasts injury over subsequent 7, 14, or 21-day windows [PAGE 4]. This creates temporal misalignment: tendon adaptation and bone-stress remodeling operate on 4–8 week cycles, so a 30-day lookback may miss the causative load spike, while a 7-day forecast window may expire before a delayed overuse injury declares. Retrocausally, an observed injury changes the target's future load profile (reduced seconds played, altered mechanical intensity), which then feeds back into the AMB and contaminates future similarity retrieval for that player and for peers compared against him. No decay kernel or forgetting factor is disclosed for aging records, despite the claim that accuracy improves over time as more records are added [PAGE 5]. Without temporal discounting, obsolete pre-injury baselines can persist indefinitely and bias similarity factors.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. Under competitive pressure or accumulated fatigue, athletes tend to regress to their dominant stored playing style (aggressive, high-contact, shot-blocking, etc.) encoded in the AMB [PAGE 5–6]. This regression raises mechanical intensity and mechanical load precisely when fitness is lowest, creating a positive feedback loop: fatigue increases recidivism into high-contact patterns; high-contact patterns elevate mechanical load; elevated load raises injury probability. The predictor, which keys on historical similarity, may thus assign high risk to a target who stylistically resembles injured peers but fails to capture the intra-game trigger that activates the crisis habit.

Counter-trap. Opponents are not static. The AMB stores injuries incurred against particular opponents and opponent playing style (offensive/defensive alignment) [PAGE 5–6], which formalizes an adversarial layer: a fatigued target with high previous-injury load becomes a rational target for opponents who adjust alignment to increase contact frequency. This counter-trap means injury probability is not solely a function of the target's internal state but of the strategic response of other agents in the same competitive division and venue [PAGE 5–6]. A model that treats opponents as fixed covariates underestimates risk in high-stakes matchups where opponent aggression is endogenously elevated.

Clock. The system operates on a prediction window anchored to a target game/competition date [PAGE 4]. The clock imposes two constraints: (1) feature freshness—physiological and mechanical loads must be annotated within the trailing 7/14/30-day interval; (2) forecast horizon—probability is emitted for the next 7/14/21 days leading up to the target date. If data ingestion lags game time, the feature vector \(\mathbf{x}_t\) is stale at prediction time; if the injury latency exceeds the forecast horizon, the model predicts a null event that manifests after the window closes. The recursion is therefore bounded by wall-clock synchronization between AMB annotation, similarity retrieval, and competition scheduling.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The AMB parameter list contains explicit cross-layer variables that can function as hard vetoes overriding the probabilistic similarity output [PAGE 5–6].

Physical layer. Venue floor type, venue temperature, player temperature, perspiration amount, and type of shoes worn are recorded [PAGE 5–6]. The patent states that venue temperature, player temperature, and perspiration may indicate excessive perspiration creating slippery floors and higher slip-and-fall probability [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: if \(T_{\text{venue}} > T_{\text{crit}} \land P_{\text{persp}} > P_{\text{crit}} \land \mu_{\text{shoe}} < \mu_{\text{crit}}\), the slip/fall risk layer is triggered regardless of the similarity factor \(S(s,t)\). Units: temperature in K or °C (unspecified in source), perspiration amount unit unspecified, coefficient of friction \(\mu\) dimensionless.

Biomechanical layer. Previous injuries, seconds played, average/moving average of seconds played, age, BMI, height, weight, arm reach, finger length, ECG measures, diastolic and systolic blood pressure [PAGE 5–6]. A previous injury in the same anatomical site within 14 days combined with elevated seconds-played load constitutes a biomechanical veto; the probability mass should be reallocated toward recurrence in the immediate 7-day window even if cross-athlete similarity is low.

Weather layer. Venue temperature is an explicit environmental covariate [PAGE 5–6]. Extreme temperature modifies both physiological load (thermoregulatory cost) and physical-layer risk (floor viscosity, perspiration). Veto proof means these hard constraints are evaluated before the similarity-weighted injury probability is reported; if any veto condition is satisfied, the report generator must flag elevated risk independent of the AMB nearest-neighbor score. No numerical thresholds are disclosed in the source, so the veto boundary is defined qualitatively by the patent text and must be instantiated by domain parameters at deployment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios and predictions.

Scenario 1: Zero-vector new athlete. Counterfactual: target has games_played = 0 and all load attributes unset, so \(\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{0}\). Cosine similarity denominator \(\|\mathbf{x}_t\|_2 = 0\) is undefined [PAGE 4–5]. Veto: system must not emit a similarity-weighted probability; it reverts to population base rate \(\pi = 0.016\) [PAGE 5]. Prediction: any model output exceeding \(\pi\) for this athlete is a false positive and is vetoed; observed injury rate in the next 7-day window should not statistically exceed 1.6% absent additional evidence.

Scenario 2: Acute-on-chronic biomechanical veto. Counterfactual: target has a documented previous injury in the right hamstring within the last 14 days and average seconds played has increased by >40% over the trailing 7 days versus the prior season [PAGE 5–6]. Even if similarity factor \(S(s,t) < 20\%\) relative to the AMB cohort, the previous-injury and load-spike parameters trigger a biomechanical veto. Prediction: injury probability for the next 7-day window is elevated to the top risk decile regardless of low similarity; the validator component must override the predictor output.

Scenario 3: Environmental slip-and-fall veto. Counterfactual: venue temperature \(> 30^\circ\mathrm{C}\), player perspiration amount above threshold, and shoes classified as low-traction on a polished venue floor type [PAGE 5–6]. The physical layer indicates slippery-floor slip-and-fall risk [PAGE 6]. Prediction: even if the target's Kolmogorov/cosine similarity to injured peers is low, the report generator must flag slip-and-fall injury as elevated for that venue and game; a counterfactual intervention (shoe change or floor treatment) is predicted to reduce the physical-layer risk component without altering the similarity factor.
