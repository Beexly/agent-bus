<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s geometric operator is the homography between the field plane and the image. Let \(\mathbf{X}_f=(X,Y,1)^{\mathsf{T}}\) be a homogeneous field point with \(X,Y\) in metres, and let \(\mathbf{x}_i=(u,v,1)^{\mathsf{T}}\) be its homogeneous image point with \(u,v\) in pixels. Then, up to a nonzero projective scale \(s\),
\[
s\,\mathbf{x}_i = H\,\mathbf{X}_f,\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}.
\]
The source states that calibration uses “the homography between the ﬁeld plane and the image, which is valid under the assumption of a planar ﬁeld [18]” [PAGE 3]. \(H\) is defined only up to projective scale; individual entries are not independently physical. The inverse map is used for player localization:
\[
\mathbf{X}_f \;\sim\; H^{-1}\mathbf{x}_i,
\]
where \(\mathbf{x}_i\) is taken from “the middle point of the bottom of their bounding box” [PAGE 4]. Units: field coordinates in metres, image coordinates in pixels, and the homogeneous scale is absorbed projectively. Singularity regime: \(H^{-1}\) is undefined when \(\det H=0\); the source does not report such frames, so this is an out-of-scope mathematical breakdown rather than an experimentally characterized regime.

The dictionary-matching operator is a nearest-template search. Let \(S\) be the zone segmentation, let \(T_k\) be template \(k\), let \(\phi(\cdot)\) be the siamese encoder, and let \(H_k\) be the template homography. The rough estimate is
\[
H_0 = H_{k^*},\qquad k^*=\arg\min_k \left\|\phi(S)-\phi(T_k)\right\|_2^2.
\]
The source says: “The homography associated with the template encoding with the shortest L2 distance to the zone segmentation encoding is the rough estimate of the sought homography” [PAGE 3]. Units: the encoded vectors are dimensionless; the L2 distance is in the same learned feature units, not metres or pixels. The dictionary is large: “more than 1000 modes are found by the clustering algorithm” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: dictionary processing “scales poorly with the size of the dictionary” [PAGE 2], and poor zone segmentation can make the nearest-template match unreliable.

The refinement operator is a learned homographic correction. The source says: “the ﬁnal homography prediction is obtained by multiplying the regressed homography with the template homography” [PAGE 3]. Formally,
\[
H_{\mathrm{final}} = H_{\mathrm{reg}}\,H_{\mathrm{template}},
\]
where \(H_{\mathrm{reg}}\) is the spatial-transformer regression and \(H_{\mathrm{template}}\) is the dictionary template homography; the source does not fix a matrix-order convention beyond “multiplying.” The paper reports a training breakdown: “we notice vanishing gradient issues. To overcome this problem, we ﬁrst pre-train it with a MSE loss and use leaky ReLU activations instead of ReLUs” [PAGE 4]. Thus the refinement operator is not assumption-free: without MSE pre-training and leaky ReLU, the reported training procedure is not reproduced.

The relevance operator is a binary score:
\[
s(f)\in\{0,1\},
\]
where \(s(f)=1\) means “a plausible estimated calibration” [PAGE 4]. This is one bit of information per frame in the explicit sense. The source uses it to “discard cameras views that are not recorded by the main camera, such as close-up views, or public views” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if a close-up or public view receives \(s(f)=1\), the calibration is not credible under the paper’s own relevance definition.

Player filtering and graph construction add two more operators. Mask R-CNN supplies a bounding box and segmentation mask; a field mask removes boxes that “do not intersect the ﬁeld,” including “staff and detections in the public” [PAGE 4]. The graph edge condition is
\[
e_{ij}=1 \quad\text{iff}\quad \left\|\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\right\|_2 \lt 25\,\mathrm{m},
\]
because “Two players are linked to each other with an edge if their real-world distance is below 25 meters” [PAGE 5]. Units: metres. Breakdown regime: localization error near the 25 m threshold can flip edges; the source does not quantify that sensitivity.

Feature fusion is
\[
\mathbf{z}_t=\left[\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{SN}}_t;\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cal}}_t\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{304},
\qquad
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{SN}}_t,\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{cal}}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{152},
\]
because “We set d =152” and “The concatenation yields feature vectors of dimension 304” [PAGE 7]. Units: dimensionless feature dimensions.

The calibration metric is intersection over union:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\in[0,1],
\]
computed “between the top view projections of the ﬁeld model by the ground-truth camera and by the estimated camera,” with a second “IoU restricted to the part of the ﬁeld actually shown on the image” [PAGE 5]. IoU is dimensionless. The action-spotting metric is
\[
\mathrm{Average\text{-}mAP}
=
\mathrm{AUC}_{\delta\in[5,60]\,\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{mAP}(\delta),
\]
where a prediction is positive when it falls “within a margin δ of a ground-truth timestamp” and the Average-mAP is “the AUC of the mAP obtained at margins δ varying from 5 to 60 seconds” [PAGE 7]. Units: \(\delta\) in seconds; mAP and Average-mAP are percentages in the reported tables, with higher being better. The excerpt does not provide the CALF loss equation; no such equation is fabricated here.

Primary breakdown regimes from the source: planar-field assumption failure invalidates \(H\) [PAGE 3]; insufficient line markings restrict some vanishing-point methods to goal areas, requiring “at least two of both vertical and horizontal lines” [PAGE 2]; tracking-based calibration is restricted to “uncut, single-sequence video streams” [PAGE 2]; dictionary size causes processing-time scaling problems [PAGE 2]; spatial-transformer training suffers vanishing gradients [PAGE 4]; close-up and public views must be discarded by the binary score [PAGE 4]; distillation loses “6 to 12 points” [PAGE 6]; binary-channel top views yield lower performance [PAGE 8]; and kick-offs are the only actions where the proposed method degrades versus the original network, “most probably because those actions are regularly unshown in soccer broadcasts” [PAGE 7].

The paper’s related work frames the field as “most often planar,” making the homography “a convenient calibration rig” [PAGE 2]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The pipeline contains nested modelers. The commercial teacher models the field geometry; the student models the teacher; CALF models temporal action structure; the human analyst models the top-view or graph representation. Each level can misread the level below it. The paper’s own language exposes this recursion: the teacher supplies “pseudo-ground-truth calibrations” [PAGE 4], so the student’s objective is not truth but teacher imitation. The downstream network then treats concatenated calibration features as evidence, and the evaluator treats Average-mAP as evidence of utility. A recursive theory-of-mind audit asks: which agent’s belief is being trusted at each hop, and what happens when that belief is wrong?

“Crisis habit regression” is the failure mode in which an agent under distribution shift falls back to its prior habit. Here, the prior habit is generic ResNet-based action spotting without calibration. The paper reports that kick-offs are “the only actions for which our performances degrade compared to the original network, most probably because those actions are regularly unshown in soccer broadcasts” [PAGE 7]. When the camera does not show the event, the calibration branch cannot supply the expected player geometry, and the system regresses toward the generic temporal prior. A second crisis habit is the close-up or public view: without the binary relevance veto, the network would treat a non-main-camera view as if it were a calibrated field view, which the paper explicitly guards against by discarding such views [PAGE 4].

“Counter-trap” is the deliberate design that prevents a tempting but harmful use of calibration. The authors split the 17 classes into “patterned” actions, where occurrence is “systematically linked with typical player placements,” and “fuzzy” actions, which “may occur in many different player conﬁgurations” [PAGE 7]. The calibration branch is used for patterned classes; fuzzy classes use only ResNet features. This is a counter-trap against overfitting player geometry where geometry is not diagnostic. It is also a hand-built trap: if the class taxonomy or broadcast conventions change, the split may no longer protect the network. A recursive model of the annotator matters here: the labels are single-timestamp annotations, and the network must infer action occurrence from temporal context, not from a fully specified player configuration.

“Clock” is the temporal coordination layer. Calibration and player data are computed for the “2-fps-subsampled set of frames” [PAGE 3]; action spotting processes “2-minute video chunks” [PAGE 7]; and the metric accepts predictions within 5 to 60 seconds [PAGE 7]. The clock is therefore multi-scale: frame-level calibration, chunk-level temporal segmentation, and event-level tolerance. Broadcast cuts and replays break the clock’s continuity; the paper notes that several cameras are used and important actions are replayed, “breaking the continuity of the stream” [PAGE 3]. A recursive agent that assumes a single continuous camera stream will misread the clock. The correct counter-trap is to treat the binary calibration score and the main-camera assumption as clock guards: if the view is not the main calibrated field view, the temporal evidence is not admissible for geometry-aware spotting. The paper’s improvement to 46.8% Average-mAP [PAGE 7] is therefore not merely a feature-fusion result; it is a coordinated success of geometry, class gating, and temporal tolerance.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
