<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of decision-makers under pressure to revert to their most entrenched statistical prior. In Galtonian terms, that prior is often the pre-1889 belief that exceptional stature—or performance—is a stable, heritable essence rather than a draw from a distribution subject to regression. A coach, trader, or clinician in crisis will over-weight a recent extreme observation (the “hot hand” or “slump”) as if it shifts the constitutional mean of the squadron.

The counter-trap is the opposite overcorrection: assuming that every extreme observation must immediately regress toward the mean, thereby denying genuine regime shifts. If a population’s nutrition changes catastrophically (famine, war), \(\mu_P\) itself shifts; Galton’s assumption that all Stature-Schemes are “identical with that of the general Population” [PAGE 112] breaks down because the population is no longer stationary. Treating the shift as mere regression is a Type II error in detection.

The clock variable is the ratio of observation window \(T_{\text{obs}}\) to regression timescale \(T_{\text{reg}}\). In a bivariate normal kinship system, \(T_{\text{reg}}\) is effectively one generational step for parent-offspring pairs, but the recursive theory of mind complicates this: I model your stature as regressing toward my estimate of the mean, while you model my estimate as itself subject to regression. The nested expectations generate a fixed point only when the meta-variance of the mean-estimator converges. If \(T_{\text{obs}} \ll T_{\text{reg}}\), the observer mistakes a transient outlier for a new constitution; if \(T_{\text{obs}} \gg T_{\text{reg}}\), the observer mistakes a genuine secular trend for noise. Galton’s “1,000 identical Schemes” [PAGE 112] presumes the clock has already run to stationarity; applying the squadron to a non-stationary crisis window is the fundamental category error.
