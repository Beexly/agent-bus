<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when institutions face uncertainty and fall back on familiar certification heuristics: pass the 300 g drop test, use EPS, add a shell, ship the product. The paper explicitly identifies that habit: “Current cycling helmet test standards are not designed to evaluate concussion-relevant loading and do not incorporate youth-specific impact conditions [9]” [PAGE 3]. The authors anticipate a reviewer’s counter-move: they disclose that “NJC and DBC have a financial interest in SoftShox and are inventors on multiple pending patent applications that claim the design of the hydraulic helmet tested in this study” [PAGE 22]. That disclosure does not invalidate the data, but it changes the reader’s Bayesian posture: the HYDRAULIC result needs independent replication, shell-swap controls, and certification testing before it can be treated as a technology-class effect.

The counter-trap is more interesting than a simple pro-advanced-technology story. If a designer optimizes only PRA, a cellular or slip system can lower rotational load while raising PLA at low severity: “At 2.9 m/s, CELL and SLIP technologies lowered PRA even as PLA increased” [PAGE 17]. If a designer optimizes only the crown location, CELL increased predicted risk at the crown relative to both foams at 2.9 m/s while reducing risk elsewhere [PAGE 12–13]. If a regulator optimizes only a single linear threshold, a helmet can pass while remaining at high predicted concussion risk [PAGE 19–20]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that each stakeholder can rationally optimize a local proxy—manufacturer: certification; parent: “advanced” label; reviewer: statistical significance; regulator: legal threshold—while the rider’s actual exposure is a joint distribution over location, velocity, angle, surface, fit, and scalp friction. The paper’s own model is a partial corrective because it includes both displacement and relative rotation and reports independent contributions to risk [PAGE 14], but it still does not model stakeholder behavior or real-world exposure.

The clock is both experimental and institutional. Experimentally, the order was fixed: “FOAM-A, CELL, SLIP, SLIDE, HYDRAULIC, and FOAM-B” [PAGE 6], and each specimen was impacted once per location. The video-sensor clocks were synchronized by cross-correlation, with the sensor axis retained as reference [PAGE 9], and the reference was the first rising crossing of 15 g [PAGE 9]. Those choices are reasonable, but they mean “time zero” is a defined accelerometer threshold, not a universal contact instant; early air-gap motion is deliberately discounted. Institutionally, the clock is the certification cycle: CPSC 1203 is being contrasted with EN 1078 revisions that incorporate oblique impact and rotational criteria [PAGE 20]. A product can be tested, published, reviewed, certified, and marketed on different clocks. The recursive trap is that a favorable laboratory result can arrive before independent replication, while a certification failure can arrive after a manuscript is accepted. The only stable counter-trap is to treat the present result as a mechanism-discovery result with explicit boundary conditions, not as a finished product claim.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof for the linear mechanism starts with the normal energy budget:
\[
\int_0^{d_{\max}} F_n(s)\,ds=\frac12 m v_n^2,
\qquad [F_n]=\mathrm{N},\ [s]=\mathrm{m},\ [m]=\mathrm{kg},\ [v_n]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
\]
If \(F_n(s)\) is spread over a larger \(d_{\max}\) before densification, the peak transmitted acceleration can fall. The source states the mechanism conditionally: “increasing the distance over which the helmet absorbs energy can reduce transmitted force, provided the liner does not densify or bottom out [48,54]” [PAGE 18]. Therefore the physical veto is exact: once \(d_{\max}\) reaches available liner stroke and \(dF_n/ds\) rises sharply at densification, further displacement cannot be assumed protective; PLA can rise and shell-to-face contact risk can increase. The source also warns that designs should “retain enough available liner thickness to avoid bottoming out at higher energies” [PAGE 18–19]. This is a biomechanical veto, not a statistical preference.

Rotational veto proof starts with angular impulse:
\[
\int \tau(t)\,dt=\Delta L=I\,\Delta\omega,
\qquad [\tau]=\mathrm{N\,m},\ [L]=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}},\ [\omega]=\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
\]
A low-friction or shear-compliant interface can reduce the tangential torque transmitted to the head, so greater helmet-headform relative rotation can lower PRA. The source supports the sign: “Greater helmet headform relative rotation was associated with lower PRA, supporting our hypothesis that greater relative motion between the helmet and headform contributes to lower rotational head kinematics” [PAGE 19]. But the veto is saturation: “greater decoupling may improve rotational attenuation only until excessive motion compromises helmet stability, coverage, or fit” [PAGE 19]. If relative rotation becomes so large that the shell no longer covers the head or the fit is lost, the angular-impulse reduction is purchased by a coverage/fit failure. That is a cross-layer veto.

Weather and surface veto proof is absent from the source. The only surface control is “The anvil surface was covered with 80-grit sandpaper to represent the road surface [34,36]” [PAGE 6], and the tests are dry laboratory impacts. There is no temperature conditioning, wetness, UV aging, or hydraulic-fluid viscosity sweep. Yet both EPS and hydraulic fluid are rate- and temperature-sensitive. A cold-conditioned hydraulic unit can have higher fluid viscosity and a different force-displacement response; a hot-conditioned unit can have lower viscosity and less damping; wet sandpaper can change friction and therefore the tangential load that drives relative rotation. The paper itself limits generalization: “The controlled laboratory conditions represent only a subset of the impact velocities, angles, locations, and surfaces encountered in real-world bicycle crashes” [PAGE 20]. The weather veto is therefore categorical: without temperature/wetness conditioning, no claim of weather-invariant ranking is licensed. The neck/body veto is also explicit: “The experimental setup also excluded the neck and body mass” [PAGE 20]. Since neck inertia changes head kinematics, a technology that wins on a fixed headform may not win in a whole-body crash. These vetoes do not refute the paper’s internal comparisons; they define the domain where those comparisons hold.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
