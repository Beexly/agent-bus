The source is a 2016 M.S. thesis that asks whether “luck” in NFL team results predicts head-coach retention. The core chain is: define luck as short-run variance; operationalize neutral-luck performance with Pythagorean Win Expectation (PWE); compute a team-season differential between actual winning percentage and PWE-implied winning percentage; multiply by 16 to express the differential in wins; then enter that differential and four controls into a binary logistic regression whose dependent variable is 1 if the coach was fired and 0 otherwise. The model is stated verbatim as “(Coaching Retention) = a + b1(Diff16) + b2(Last5) + b3(Tenure) + b4(Play_Offs) + b5(Trend)” [PAGE 32]. The data set is 610 team-seasons from 1994–2013, with 133 firings, or 21.8% [PAGE 42]. The full-model regression reports Diff16 \(B=-.30\), \(S.E.=.09\), \(p=.001\); Play_Offs \(B=-1.76\), \(p=.000\); Last5 \(B=-.15\), \(p=.016\); Tenure \(B=.13\), \(p=.001\); Trend \(B=.00\), \(p=.981\) [PAGE 45]. The thesis concludes that “The results indicate that luck is a significant factor in deciding whether or not a head coach in the NFL is fired or retained” [PAGE 51].

The multi-hop derivation is not merely “bad luck causes firing.” It is: PWE assumes scoring inputs summarize neutral-luck performance; deviations are treated as luck; firing is a binary institutional response; therefore a negative coefficient on a good-luck-minus-bad-luck differential implies luck enters the retention decision. The source’s own PWE description says “Its only inputs are the number of points scored and the number of points allowed” [PAGE 17], and the thesis later notes that PWE “tends not to predict teams finishing with records to either extreme” [PAGE 17]. That is a real gray-zone: the luck measure is a residual from a deliberately simple baseline, not a direct observation of stochastic events. The source acknowledges that “If these aberrant results happen to cluster together, a team's win-loss record for a stretch of games may not reflect the true performance of the team” [PAGE 9], and that the relevant luck is “the short term variation that occurs at just the wrong point to have an outsized impact” [PAGE 25]. Thus the inference depends on the residual being luck rather than omitted physical, tactical, schedule, injury, or roster factors.

A second gray-zone is sign convention. Table 1 describes Diff16 as “The difference between the PWE expected winning % and the actual winning percentage multiplied by 16” [PAGE 31], but the descriptive table reports fired coaches’ mean Diff16 as \(-0.483\) and retained coaches’ mean as \(0.183\) [PAGE 42]. The regression reports a negative coefficient [PAGE 45]. To reconcile the reported signs, the operative variable must behave as actual-minus-PWE, not PWE-minus-actual: positive = good luck, negative = bad luck. If the thesis’s Table 1 wording were followed literally, the sign would flip and the substantive interpretation would reverse. This is a documented ambiguity, not a minor stylistic issue.

A third gray-zone is the “26% reduction” interpretation. The source says “A 26% reduction in the likelihood of getting fired for a one unit increase in the Diff16 variable” [PAGE 54]. In a binary logistic model, \(e^{\beta}\) is a multiplicative factor on the logit scale, not a 26-percentage-point reduction in firing probability. With \(\beta=-.30\), \(e^{-.30}=0.741\), so the exponentiated coefficient is about 0.74, i.e. a 26% multiplicative reduction in the modeled logit factor per one-win improvement in luck. That is not the same as a 26% absolute risk reduction. The source’s language is directionally correct but statistically imprecise.

A fourth gray-zone is the split-sample luck claim. The bad-luck regression reports Diff16 \(B=-.31\), \(S.E.=.18\), \(p=.087\) [PAGE 48], which passes the thesis’s stated \(\alpha=.10\) threshold: “For the purposes of this thesis, the standard for significance will be set at .10” [PAGE 30]. But the good-luck regression reports Diff16 \(B=-.29\), \(S.E.=.27\), \(p=.286\) [PAGE 49], which does not pass that threshold. The thesis nevertheless says the two sides are “very close to the same in predictive power” [PAGE 50]. That is weaker than the text implies: the full-sample significance may be carried mainly by the bad-luck side, or by pooling, or by specification choices. The equal-power conclusion is plausible but not established at the stated alpha in the isolated good-luck model.

A fifth gray-zone is dependence structure. The 610 cases are repeated team-seasons and coach-seasons, not 610 independent draws. The model uses ordinary binary logistic regression; repeated observations within teams and coaches can understate standard errors. The source also removed expansion teams’ first five years, reducing missing-history complications but also changing the sample [PAGE 40]. The Nagelkerke \(R^2\) is .20 [PAGE 45], so most variation in firing is unexplained. That is not fatal—firing is multi-causal—but it means the luck coefficient should be read as a conditional association, not a complete causal decomposition.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source does not print the algebraic PWE formula; it states only the inputs and use. A standard reconstructed PWE operator, consistent with the source’s description, is
\[
\hat p_i^{\mathrm{PWE}}=\frac{RS_i^{\gamma}}{RS_i^{\gamma}+RA_i^{\gamma}},\qquad
\hat W_i^{\mathrm{PWE}}=G_i\,\hat p_i^{\mathrm{PWE}},
\]
where \(RS_i\) = points scored by team \(i\) in a season, \(RA_i\) = points allowed, \(G_i\) = games played (16 in the NFL regular season), \(\gamma>0\) is a dimensionless sport-specific exponent, \(\hat p_i^{\mathrm{PWE}}\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless expected winning proportion, and \(\hat W_i^{\mathrm{PWE}}\) is in wins/games. The source supports the input structure: “Its only inputs are the number of points scored and the number of points allowed” [PAGE 17], and notes the PWE was “adapted to the NFL and other major sports” [PAGE 17]. Assumptions: scoring margin is an adequate neutral-luck summary; \(\gamma\) is stable across teams/seasons; games are exchangeable enough that season scoring maps monotonically to expected wins. Breakdown/singularities: if \(RS_i=RA_i=0\), the expression is \(0/0\) undefined; if \(RA_i=0\) and \(RS_i>0\), \(\hat p=1\); if \(RS_i=0\) and \(RA_i>0\), \(\hat p=0\); as \(\gamma\to0\) the ratio degenerates toward a constant; extreme records are poorly fit, as the source states: PWE “tends not to predict teams finishing with records to either extreme” [PAGE 17]. The source’s examples include the 2007 Patriots projected to 13.76 wins and the 2008 Lions projected to 2.78 wins [PAGE 18].

The luck operator used in the thesis is a scaled residual. To match the reported descriptive signs, define
\[
D_{16,i}=16\left(p_i^{\mathrm{actual}}-\hat p_i^{\mathrm{PWE}}\right),
\]
where \(p_i^{\mathrm{actual}}\) is actual winning proportion, \(\hat p_i^{\mathrm{PWE}}\) is PWE-implied winning proportion, and \(D_{16,i}\) is in wins per 16-game season. Positive values mean better-than-PWE results (“good luck”); negative values mean worse-than-PWE results (“bad luck”). This sign convention is required by Table 3, where fired coaches have mean Diff16 \(= -0.483\) and retained coaches have mean \(= 0.183\) [PAGE 42], and by the negative coefficient in Table 5 [PAGE 45]. The source’s Table 1 wording is order-ambiguous: it calls Diff16 “The difference between the PWE expected winning % and the actual winning percentage multiplied by 16” [PAGE 31]. The reported signs indicate the operational order is actual minus PWE. Assumptions: the PWE residual is a luck proxy, not a pure stochastic shock; 16-game scaling is linear and unit-preserving; the same PWE exponent is appropriate across the sample. Breakdown: if PWE systematically misprices extreme teams, \(D_{16}\) contains formula bias; if schedule strength, injuries, weather, or roster turnover are omitted, \(D_{16}\) contains physical/tactical signal; if the sign convention is reversed, all coefficient interpretations reverse.

The retention model is a binary logistic regression. Let \(Y_i=1\) if the coach was fired after season \(i\), \(Y_i=0\) otherwise. The source’s stated linear predictor is
\[
\text{(Coaching Retention)}=a+b_1(\text{Diff16})+b_2(\text{Last5})+b_3(\text{Tenure})+b_4(\text{Play\_Offs})+b_5(\text{Trend})
\]
[PAGE 32]. The statistically correct binary-logistic form is
\[
\eta_i=\operatorname{logit}(\pi_i)
=\beta_0+\beta_D D_{16,i}+\beta_L L_i+\beta_T T_i+\beta_P P_i+\beta_\tau \tau_i,
\qquad
\pi_i=\Pr(Y_i=1)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta_i}},
\]
where \(\eta_i\) is dimensionless logit, \(\pi_i\in[0,1]\) is firing probability, \(D_{16,i}\) is in wins, \(L_i\) is the Last5 term as coded in the table (proportion or wins/season depending on whether the \(\times16\) transformation is applied), \(T_i\) is tenure in years, \(P_i\in\{0,1\}\) is playoff indicator, and \(\tau_i\) is trend in consecutive seasons. The source’s Table 5 gives \(\beta_D=-.30\), \(S.E.=.09\), \(p=.001\); \(\beta_L=-.15\), \(p=.016\); \(\beta_T=.13\), \(p=.001\); \(\beta_P=-1.76\), \(p=.000\); \(\beta_\tau=.00\), \(p=.981\) [PAGE 45]. The exponentiated Diff16 coefficient is \(e^{-.30}=0.741\), consistent with the source’s “26% reduction” phrasing [PAGE 54], but that is a multiplicative logit-scale factor, not an absolute probability reduction. Assumptions: independent observations, linearity in the logit, no perfect separation, no influential collinearity, and correct coding of Last5. Breakdown: complete separation makes MLE coefficients diverge; clustered team/coach repeated measures bias standard errors; the Last5 scaling is inconsistent in the source—Table 7 reports mean .50 and SD .12 [PAGE 45], while Table 3 reports average wins last five of 8.103 and 7.621 [PAGE 42] and the text says the percentage “will be multiplied by sixteen” [PAGE 29]. That scaling inconsistency changes coefficient units and interpretation.

The source’s luck-splitting operator uses two subsamples:
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{bad}}=\{i:D_{16,i}<0\},\qquad
\mathcal{D}_{\text{good}}=\{i:D_{16,i}>0\}.
\]
The bad-luck regression gives \(\beta_D=-.31\), \(S.E.=.18\), \(p=.087\) [PAGE 48]; the good-luck regression gives \(\beta_D=-.29\), \(S.E.=.27\), \(p=.286\) [PAGE 49]. At the thesis’s \(\alpha=.10\) standard [PAGE 30], the bad-luck coefficient is significant and the good-luck coefficient is not. The source’s equal-power claim is therefore not verified by the isolated good-luck model alone. The descriptive split is 317 good-luck cases and 293 bad-luck cases; good-luck teams won 58% of games with PWE 51.7%, bad-luck teams won 42% with PWE 47.8% [PAGE 56]. Those are proportions and wins, respectively; the difference in PWE is less than one win per 16-game season.

One further source operator is Burke’s pure-luck comparison: “At 52.5% luck, his curve became statistically indistinguishable from the actual team win total curve” [PAGE 26], implying that “the better team wins only 74% of the time in the NFL” [PAGE 26]. This is not used as a regression term in the thesis, but it supplies the prior that NFL game outcomes contain a large stochastic component. Unit-wise, 52.5% is a dimensionless mixture proportion, not a win total; it should not be confused with the Diff16 residual in wins.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage runs from Bill James’s 1977 Baseball Abstract and the Pythagorean Win Expectation, through sabermetric adaptation to football and other sports, into commercial football analytics, and then into this thesis’s retention model. The source explicitly places the PWE in that lineage: James “introduced several statistics that he had designed himself,” including PWE, with “only inputs” being points scored and points allowed [PAGE 17]. It then cites Luo (2014) for the claim that the original PWE is “nearly as accurate as the best others have come up with” [PAGE 17] and Caro and Machtmes (2013) for the claim that PWE is “an accurate forecasting method for coaches and athletic directors to use when evaluating the performance of their football programs” [PAGE 20]. That lineage matters because the thesis is not inventing a luck metric from nothing; it is taking an established residual and asking whether a management decision responds to it.

The second branch is the regression-to-the-mean literature. The source cites Barnwell for the claim that teams with large actual-wins-minus-PWE differentials “usually predict that a team will regress towards the PWE predicted level the next season” [PAGE 16], and later that teams “who finish with a high differential between actual wins and the number of wins the PWE would have predicted are likely to regress towards the PWE the next season” [PAGE 21]. It also cites Barnwell on close games: teams that win a high or low percentage of games decided by seven points or less tend to regress toward 50% as a group [PAGE 27–28]. The source uses this to argue that short samples preserve luck. The retrocausal trap is obvious: if the residual is defined by PWE and then shown to regress toward PWE, that is partly mechanical. It is evidence that the residual is not permanent, but not independent proof that the residual is “luck” rather than omitted team quality.

The third branch is the “luck vs. skill” decomposition. The source summarizes Burke’s pure-luck league and chi-square exercise: a league where every team has a 50% chance in every game produces a binomial win-total curve; actual NFL win totals are a blend of skill and luck; at 52.5% luck the simulated curve becomes indistinguishable from the actual curve [PAGE 25–26]. It also cites Rudy on turnover margin: fumbles caused/recovered and interceptions are heavily influenced by random chance, while only interceptions thrown by the offense were positively correlated from first half to second half [PAGE 29]. This lineage supports a stochastic prior, but it also exposes a multi-week trap: short-window turnover and close-game effects can masquerade as coach skill or coach failure for multiple weeks or multiple seasons, especially when rosters turn over before variance balances.

The fourth branch is the coaching-retention literature. The source cites Adler, Berry, and Doherty on coaching replacements; Maxcy on college football; Hadley et al. on coaching efficiency; Porter and Scully on learning curves; Kahn on player quality and retention; and Breer on front-office power structures [PAGE 10–16, 29–30]. The thesis’s contribution is to add a luck residual to that retention literature. The retrocausal danger is that firing is not a stable natural outcome: management expectations, contract status, fan pressure, sponsor pressure, and front-office politics are all historical and institutional. A coach fired after an unlucky season may be a case of “crisis habit regression,” but the same observed firing could also be a rational response to a coach’s actual tactical limitations. The source itself acknowledges the omitted variables: “there are many factors that remain outside a coaches' control” [PAGE 13], and schedule differences “could, potentially, be a factor” [PAGE 29].

Multi-week traps in this genealogy include: (1) treating a 16-game season as a large sample when the relevant close-game or turnover subsample may be only a handful of games; (2) using a PWE residual that is known to misfit extreme teams [PAGE 17]; (3) attributing regression-to-PWE to luck when the baseline is constructed from the same scoring data; (4) inferring equal good/bad luck effects from two subsamples where only the bad-luck coefficient clears \(\alpha=.10\) [PAGE 48–49]; and (5) ignoring that the same coach can appear in multiple seasons, so “multi-week” streaks are nested in “multi-season” tenure clocks. The source’s own warning is apt: “If these aberrant results happen to cluster together, a team's win-loss record for a stretch of games may not reflect the true performance of the team” [PAGE 9].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has three agents: management, the coach, and the external audience of fans/sponsors. Management observes wins and perhaps PWE; the coach knows management observes wins; fans and sponsors observe official standings and may not observe PWE. The thesis’s own discussion supplies the recursion: “if a management team happens to make a decision based on data sets with small sample sizes and the data set has been skewed due to this luck factor, the decision could cause people to lose their jobs erroneously” [PAGE 9]. That is the crisis-habit regression: a bad-luck season produces a firing, which teaches the organization that the visible win total is the operative metric, which in turn reproduces short-sample firing behavior.

The counter-trap is that management may know luck matters but cannot act on that knowledge. The source states: “another possibility is that management teams well know the impact that luck can have, but cannot act on it due to outside influences” [PAGE 61]. It then specifies the mechanism: “Sponsors and fans may react to the official standings, necessitating action on the part of the management team” [PAGE 61]. In recursive terms, management’s private model may include Diff16, but its public action is constrained by the audience’s model, which is standings-based. The coach, anticipating this, may optimize for visible wins rather than process; the audience, anticipating management’s need to react, may intensify pressure after unlucky losses. The result is a second-order trap: even a luck-aware front office can rationally fire an unlucky coach because the sponsor/fan layer vetoes retention.

The clock is the one-season playoff demarcation. The model’s strongest coefficient is Play_Offs \(B=-1.76\), \(p=.000\) [PAGE 45], and the source says “making the play-offs is seen as success for most NFL head coaches” [PAGE 55]. Tenure is positive \(B=.13\), \(p=.001\) [PAGE 45], which the source interprets as longer-tenured coaches being more likely to be fired, possibly because a first-year grace period protects new coaches and later-tenure coaches face accumulated expectations [PAGE 55]. The clock therefore has two hands: a short playoff-or-else hand and a longer tenure/grace-period hand. The recursive theory predicts that a coach on the wrong side of the short clock is most exposed to luck, while a first-year coach is partially shielded. The data are consistent: first-year coaches were fired less than 10% of the time, rising to 22.5% in year two [PAGE 43]. The source’s conclusion that luck is “second only to whether or not a team made the play-offs in predictive power” [PAGE 60] fits a world in which management uses a coarse public clock and luck perturbs the clock’s reading.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer vetoes are conditions under which the Diff16 residual cannot be read as luck because physical, biomechanical, weather, schedule, injury, or roster layers have altered either the PWE baseline or the actual win total. The identification condition is: the luck coefficient \(\beta_D\) is interpretable only if \(D_{16,i}\) is conditionally independent of omitted performance-shifting factors \(Z_i\) given the model controls. If \(Z_i\) affects both \(D_{16,i}\) and firing \(Y_i\), then \(\hat\beta_D\) is biased. The source itself admits the scoring-input limitation: “Because that formula counts points scored for or against in any game situation as being of the same value” [PAGE 57], and it notes schedule differences “could, potentially, be a factor” [PAGE 29]. It also acknowledges that “there are many factors that remain outside a coaches' control” [PAGE 13].

A physical/biomechanical veto applies when injuries or fatigue change true team strength within the season. If a quarterback injury lowers scoring for several weeks, then actual wins fall below PWE and Diff16 becomes negative even if no stochastic “luck” changed. The PWE residual is then a joint product of injury and randomness. A weather veto applies when wind, precipitation, or temperature systematically suppress or inflate scoring in a way that is not symmetric across teams and games; because PWE treats all points as equal, weather-driven scoring changes can enter Diff16. A schedule veto applies when a team’s opponents are unusually strong or weak; the source explicitly flags that its turnover analysis “does not account for schedule differences” [PAGE 29], and the same concern transfers to the retention model. A roster-turnover veto applies when the team that produced the PWE residual is not the team that management evaluates at firing time.

Adjudication rule: accept the luck interpretation only if, after conditioning on injury exposure, schedule strength, weather, and roster continuity, the Diff16 coefficient remains negative and distinguishable from zero at the stated \(\alpha=.10\). If the coefficient attenuates to non-significance, the “luck” claim is vetoed and replaced by “unmeasured performance shock.” If the coefficient survives, the luck interpretation is not proven, but it is not vetoed by the cross-layer audit. The source’s own data provide a partial check: good-luck and bad-luck teams have PWE rates of 51.7% and 47.8%, respectively, a difference of less than one win per 16 games [PAGE 56]. That near-symmetry makes a pure “bad teams are just unlucky” story less likely, but it does not rule out physical-layer confounding.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Prediction: the reported good-luck coefficient is \(B=-.29\), \(S.E.=.27\), \(p=.286\) [PAGE 49], so it fails the thesis’s own significance threshold [PAGE 30]. Veto condition: if Diff16 loses significance under cluster-robust errors or under a reasonable alternative baseline, the causal “luck” interpretation is vetoed as specification-dependent.
