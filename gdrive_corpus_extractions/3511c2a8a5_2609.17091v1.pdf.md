\[
\sigma(e;b)=\max_{k\in[d]}\frac{e^{(k)}}{b^{(k)}}.
\]
The equivalence is
\[
\sigma(e;b)\leq\lambda \iff e^{(k)}\leq\lambda b^{(k)}\ \forall k\in[d]
\iff e\in\prod_{k=1}^d[0,\lambda b^{(k)}].
\]
Units: \(e^{(k)},L_k,U_k\) carry the response unit of target \(k\); \(\sigma,\lambda,\alpha,\gamma\) are dimensionless. The conformal quantile is
\[
q=\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil,\qquad
\hat\lambda(e_{n+1})=Q_q\bigl(\{\sigma_i(e_{n+1})\}_{i=1}^n\cup\{+\infty\}\bigr),
\]
where \(Q_q\) denotes the \(q\)-th order statistic [PAGE 2, PAGE 5]. The base thresholds are
\[
m'=\operatorname{clip}\bigl(\lceil\gamma(n+1)\rceil,2,n\bigr),\qquad
L_k=e^{(k)}_{(m'-1)},\qquad U_k=e^{(k)}_{(m')}.
\]
The paper requires “2 ≤m′ ≤n” so that \(L_k\) is well defined and \(U_k\) is finite [PAGE 5]. The transductive base is
\[
b^{(k)}_{n+1}=\operatorname{clamp}(e^{(k)}_{n+1},L_k,U_k),
\]
so \(L_k\leq b^{(k)}_{n+1}\leq U_k\) [PAGE 5]. The validity theorem defines
\[
R_\alpha=\{e_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^d_+:\sigma_{n+1}(e_{n+1})\leq\hat\lambda(e_{n+1})\},

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from split-conformal regression to multi-target joint regions. The ancestral operation is the split-conformal quantile: fit a predictor, compute calibration scores, take the \(\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil\)-th smallest score, and invert the score sublevel set [PAGE 2]. Papadopoulos et al. and Vovk et al. supply the split/inductive framework; Lei et al. supply the rank argument [PAGE 19]. The univariate normalized score divides by a local scale estimate [PAGE 3]. The multi-target problem changes the object from an interval to a region \(C(x)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^d\) with joint coverage \(\mathbb{P}(Y_{n+1}\in C(X_{n+1}))\geq1-\alpha\) [PAGE 1]. The simplest descendant is max aggregation \(s(x,y)=\max_k|y^{(k)}-\hat f^{(k)}(x)|\), which is valid but produces a hypercube “driven by the coordinate with the largest residuals” and is “over-conservative for the other targets” when scales are heterogeneous [PAGE 3]. Bonferroni is even more conservative [PAGE 3]. The copula branch uses rank transforms to put targets on a common \([0,1]\) scale, but “neither defines a nonconformity score in the split-conformal sense, so finite-sample coverage is not guaranteed”; empirical copula validity is only asymptotic [PAGE 3]. CHR is a valid split-conformal hyperrectangle method with per-target width adaptation, but it splits the calibration set in two, reducing the effective sample size for the conformal quantile [PAGE 3]. TSCP and TSCP-R are the closest antecedents: they standardize the residual vector by empirical mean and standard deviation in a transductive fashion, preserving exchangeability [PAGE 3]. The present paper’s lineage move is to replace mean/std standardization with calibration quantiles: “normalizing by calibration quantiles instead avoids this assumption, since a quantile depends only on the relative order of the observations rather than on every calibration point” [PAGE 2]. That is the retrocausal hinge: the base vector is a quantile-scale object, not a moment object. The four output types are a later branching: SCO as a closed-form valid rectangle, SCI as a tight but invalid diagnostic, \(R_\alpha\) as the exact implicit set, and SC2 as a computable valid staircase [PAGE 2, PAGE 11]. Multi-week traps follow from this genealogy. First, the exchangeability clock: if weekly data are treated as exchangeable but the joint law drifts, the rank-uniform argument fails; the paper explicitly postpones “non-exchangeable settings such as time series” [PAGE 18]. Second, calibration leakage: using future weeks to set \(L_k,U_k\) or \(\gamma\) breaks the split-conformal logic. Third, zero-residual degeneration: a near-perfect target can make \(U_k=0\), and the paper’s own Births-2 failure shows that \(\gamma=0.8\) is not safe when more than 80% of residuals are zero [PAGE 14]. Fourth, small-\(n\) high-\(d\) trap: OES10 and OES97 have \(d=16\) but only \(n=60\) and \(n=50\) calibration points, “too few for any rectangle, even the loosest one, to jointly cover 1 −α of calibration points across all 16 targets” [PAGE 17]. Fifth, the \(\gamma_{\rm rect}\) temptation: choosing \(\gamma\) to force \(\hat\lambda=1\) collapses the staircase and “does not improve consistently over the default γ = 1−α, suggesting it offers no systematic advantage” [PAGE 16]. Sixth, the SCI temptation: the inner rectangle is “tighter even than Rα, but without a formal coverage guarantee” [PAGE 2], and it “may under-cover since it is strictly contained in Rα” [PAGE 12]. Seventh, scale drift: the method is marginal, not conditional; if the target scale changes within a week, the base rectangle may no longer encode the relevant scale. Eighth, Pareto pruning must be done on calibration-only quantities; pruning after seeing test data would be a leakage trap. The lineage therefore recommends a conservative multi-week protocol: pre-register \(\gamma=1-\alpha\), recompute \(L_k,U_k\) on a trailing calibration window, check \(L_k>0\) and \(U_k>0\), report SCO or SC2 as the valid region, and treat SCI only as a diagnostic.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind audit asks what a practitioner, an auditor, and a downstream decision-maker each believe under crisis. The first crisis habit is regression to max aggregation or Bonferroni. Max is simple and valid, but it “ignores scale differences” and is over-conservative when targets have heterogeneous scales [PAGE 1, PAGE 3]. Bonferroni is “even more conservative” [PAGE 3]. Under deadline pressure, an agent may choose Max because it is easy to defend; the auditor will accept validity but penalize volume. The second crisis habit is regression to SCI because it is tight. The paper warns that SCI is “the most compact of the four, tighter even than Rα, but without a formal coverage guarantee” [PAGE 2]. An agent that reports SCI as if it were valid is trading validity for apparent efficiency. The counter-trap is overcorrection: choose \(\gamma=\gamma_{\rm rect}\) to force \(\hat\lambda=1\) and collapse the staircase, or choose a huge \(\gamma\) to make the base rectangle dominate. The paper reports that \(\gamma_{\rm rect}\) “does not improve consistently over the default γ = 1−α, suggesting it offers no systematic advantage” [PAGE 16]. A huge \(\gamma\) makes \(U_k\) outlier-sensitive and can push \(\hat\lambda<1\), where the staircase need not be contained in the outer rectangle [PAGE 12, PAGE 13]. The clock is the exchangeability clock. The guarantee is marginal and finite-sample under exchangeability; it is not a conditional or temporal guarantee [PAGE 4, PAGE 18]. An agent who recalibrates weekly but uses a random split can preserve exchangeability within the week; an agent who uses a rolling window with future leakage cannot. The recursive belief hierarchy is: the practitioner wants small volume; the auditor wants coverage at least \(1-\alpha\); the downstream decision-maker wants both but cannot inspect the cell decomposition. The correct counter-trap is to use the valid outer or staircase by default, report SCI only as a diagnostic, and pre-register \(\gamma=1-\alpha\). The paper calls \(\gamma=1-\alpha\) “a natural default” and says it “consistently matches or outperforms other values of γ across datasets and dimensions” [PAGE 13]. The clock also dictates a check: if \(U_k=0\), the valid response is not to ignore it but to use \(\gamma\geq1-\alpha\), because “if γ ≥1 −α and Uk = 0, then more than 1 −α of calibration residuals are zero, the transductive quantile remains finite, and coverage is maintained” [PAGE 14]. The recursive trap is that an agent may believe a zero-width target is “free efficiency”; in fact it is a singularity unless the gamma condition holds. A second clock trap is small \(n\) with large \(d\): the auditor will ask whether the staircase was actually pruned to a small active set. At \(d=16\) and \(n=50\) to \(60\), the answer can be thousands of rectangles [PAGE 17]. The agent should therefore report active-set size, calibration time, and whether \(L_k,U_k\) are positive. The final theory-of-mind rule is: do not let the downstream desire for tightness veto the auditor’s coverage requirement. Use SCO when a single rectangle is required; use SC2 when a valid union is acceptable; use SCI only to illustrate the lower bound. This is the counter-trap against both crisis habits: Max is too loose, SCI is invalid, and the staircase with \(\gamma=1-\alpha\) is the defensible middle.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Then Theorem 3 does not apply, because the proof requires the scores to be symmetric and the rank of \(\sigma_{n+1}\) to be uniform: “Under exchangeability, the rank of σn+1 among {σ1, . . . , σn+1} is uniformly distributed” [PAGE 21]. Let \(V_{\rm pos}=1\) if \(L_k=0\) for the outer region or \(U_k=0\) for the inner/staircase region. Then the score ratios are undefined or infinite, and the assumptions “Assume Lk > 0 for all k” [PAGE 9] and “Assume Uk > 0 for all k ∈[d]” [PAGE 10] fail. The veto proof is: if \(V_{\rm ex}=0\) and \(V_{\rm pos}=0\), then the exchangeability condition of Theorem 3 holds and the positivity conditions of Propositions 10 and 11 hold; therefore the coverage and containment conclusions hold without any additional physical, biomechanical, or weather premise. Conversely, if \(V_{\rm ex}=1\) or \(V_{\rm pos}=1\), the mathematical guarantee is not merely weakened; it is outside its hypothesis class. A weather covariate included in \(X\) does not by itself veto the method: if the calibration and test pairs remain exchangeable conditional on the design, weather is just a feature. A weather event that shifts the joint law between calibration and test does veto via \(V_{\rm ex}\). A biomechanical constraint that forces a target to be predicted near-perfectly can veto via \(V_{\rm pos}\) when \(U_k=0\), as in the Births-2 failure: “more than 80% of calibration residuals on that target are exactly zero, yielding Uk = 0” [PAGE 14]. A physical sensor calibration change that multiplies one target’s scale does not automatically veto coverage, because the scaling score is scale-adaptive; but it can make the base rectangle stale and inflate volume. The adjudication is therefore narrow: physical/biomechanical/weather layers have no independent veto authority over the conformal theorem. They can veto only by breaking exchangeability or by driving \(L_k\) or \(U_k\) to zero. If neither occurs, the valid outputs remain SCO and SC2, and SCI remains a diagnostic without a formal guarantee.

Three verifiable counterfactual vetoes follow. Each is stated as a checkable predicate, a predicted outcome, and a required action. They use only the paper’s reported quantities.

Veto 1: zero-residual base singularity. Dataset: Births-2, \(d=4\), \(\alpha=0.10\), SC2 with \(\gamma=0.8\). Verifier: compute \(U_k=e^{(k)}_{(m')}\) for each target; veto if any \(U_k=0\). The paper reports exactly this: “The one exception is SC2 with γ = 0.8 on Births 2 (d = 4): the random forest predicts one target near-perfectly, so more than 80% of calibration residuals on that target are exactly zero, yielding Uk = 0 and violating the assumption of Propositions 11 and 10” [PAGE 14]. Prediction: joint coverage falls to \(0.789\), below the nominal \(0.90\); Table 1 records this value [PAGE 15]. The other variants with \(\gamma\geq1-\alpha\) remain near \(0.90\), e.g. the table records \(0.905\) and \(0.903\) in the SC2 columns. Action: reject \(\gamma=0.8\) for this dataset; use \(\gamma=1-\alpha\) or larger, because “if γ ≥1 −α and Uk = 0, then more than 1 −α of calibration residuals are zero, the transductive quantile remains finite, and coverage is maintained” [PAGE 14]. This veto is verifiable by a single calibration pass and a coverage check.
