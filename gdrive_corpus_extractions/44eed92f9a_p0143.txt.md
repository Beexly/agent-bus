<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Rule XXIV sits in the 1915 Spalding's Official Foot Ball Guide [PAGE 143], a rulebook descended from the late-19th-century Intercollegiate Football Rules Association code shaped by Walter Camp and the rugby-to-American-football transition. In 1915, stadiums were often open enclosures without the modern separated team sideline boxes, coaching zones, or media areas; spectators and team personnel commonly stood along the field boundary. Rule XXIV therefore encodes an access-control boundary between the playing enclosure and the public: Section 4's requirement that all admitted persons remain seated and not walk up and down either side [PAGE 143] is a direct attempt to keep spectators off the sideline in an era when sideline crowding could physically interfere with play.

The 15-yard penalty [PAGE 143] was a severe field-position sanction in the low-scoring football of 1915, when 15 yards represented a substantial fraction of the 100-yard playing field and field position was a primary currency because scoring was infrequent. The offender-exclusion clause—banishment from the neighborhood of the field for the remainder of the game—functions as a partial ejection, predating modern disqualification rules while achieving a similar removal effect for field-access violators [PAGE 143].

A retrocausal trap arises when a modern analyst imports present-day sideline norms into a 1915 framework. Today, head coaches and assistant coaches occupy defined sideline areas; under Rule XXIV, such non-player, non-official, non-substitute persons would generally be unauthorized on the field without explicit Referee/Umpire permission [PAGE 143, Section 3]. Assuming that a 1915 coach could freely enter the field to attend a player or argue a call is historically false; Section 2 restricts field entry during an injury to one consented representative [PAGE 143]. Conversely, applying modern ejection-and-review standards to a 1915 sideline walker would be anachronistic: the 1915 remedy is a 15-yard loss with down unchanged, not automatic disqualification [PAGE 143].

The multi-week trap concerns cumulative exposure. A team that habitually sends unauthorized personnel onto the field—extra attendants, coaches arguing calls, or representatives entering without fresh consent—incurs repeated 15-yard penalties [PAGE 143]. Over a season, these losses compound in field-position terms, particularly because the down is not reset and no extra play is awarded. In retrocausal analysis, a team's 1915 loss streak might be partly attributable to undisciplined sideline access rather than on-field performance alone; the rule converts behavioral infractions into spatial penalties that persist across weeks. The scientific lineage is proto-queuing theory: each consent request is a service event at a two-server station (Referee, Umpire), and the field is a bounded buffer whose unauthorized occupancy triggers a penalty transition.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The enforcement spot \sigma_{\text{enf}} may lie within 13.716 m of the offending team's goal line. Rule XXIV specifies a fixed 15-yard loss [PAGE 143] but contains no half-distance-to-the-goal or end-zone truncation provision. Thus, when d(\sigma_{\text{offense}}, \text{goal line}) < 13.716 m, full enforcement is physically undefined; this is a goal-line singularity requiring an external rule not present on [PAGE 143].

Biomechanical layer. Section 2 permits one consented representative to attend an injured player [PAGE 143]. Obtaining consent takes non-zero time, during which the injured player remains on the field. If the injury involves suspected spinal trauma or hemorrhage, the biomechanical cost of delayed removal may exceed the rule-compliance cost of entering without consent. This creates a triage veto: medical necessity overrides the consent gate, but Rule XXIV does not specify the emergency exception, so the referee must adjudicate an undefined conflict between player safety and field-access control [PAGE 143, Section 2].

Weather layer. Section 4 requires all admitted persons to remain seated throughout the game and prohibits walking up and down either side [PAGE 143]. Under heavy rain, snow, or lightning, spectators may leave seats to seek shelter on or near the field, and officials may have reduced visibility of sideline violations. When visibility falls below the threshold required to enforce \operatorname{seated}(x,t)=1 and W(x)=0, Rule XXIV enforcement becomes operationally impossible; the veto passes to game suspension rather than penalty accumulation. Weather therefore couples to rule enforceability: severe conditions nullify the seated-and-stationary predicate by physical necessity.

Cross-layer coupling. The three layers cannot be optimized independently. A biomechanical emergency (injured player) demands field entry, but the physical layer (sideline boundary) and weather layer (poor visibility) make authorized entry and its verification harder. The referee's adjudication must privilege the highest-priority layer—player safety over field access—yet Rule XXIV provides no formal veto hierarchy, producing a cross-layer governance gap [PAGE 143].
