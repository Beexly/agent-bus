The paper’s central mechanism can be reconstructed as a multi-hop inference chain. First, broadcast frames produce player bounding boxes. Those boxes are used in two ways: one path extracts appearance descriptors for re-identification, and another path estimates a foot keypoint near the bottom of the box. The foot keypoint is then mapped by a homography into an overhead rink template. The graph tracker then treats players as nodes, and the relationships between players in consecutive frames as edges. The edge classifier decides whether two nodes in adjacent frames belong to the same player identity. Post-processing removes weak edges, resolves one-to-one assignment conflicts, and propagates identifiers across connected components.

The strongest explanatory claim is that homography reduces ambiguity when monocular appearance is weak. In hockey, players wear bulky equipment, team colors are similar, and rapid motion creates blur. The source explicitly states that “existing trackers encounter significant difficulties in dealing with occlusions, blurs, and agile player movements prevalent in telecast feeds” [PAGE 1]. Under these conditions, an appearance-only tracker may confuse two nearby players because their ReID vectors are close in feature space. The homographic footpoint adds a spatial coordinate that is less sensitive to upper-body overlap. If two players overlap in image space but their feet occupy different rink coordinates, the projected separation provides a discriminative signal. The figure caption makes this point directly: “At (t + 2), there is a clear distinction between overlapping players from the top view” [PAGE 2].

A second multi-hop step is the message passing network. The node embedding initially concatenates appearance and positional information. The edge embedding concatenates relative appearance and positional differences. Then the network repeatedly updates edges from endpoint nodes and nodes from neighboring edges. This allows the tracker to reason over a small temporal neighborhood instead of relying on a single pairwise distance. The source says “We run the MPN for L = 6 iterations” [PAGE 7], and the supplementary material states that “The no. of message passing steps for L, analogous to the receptive field of a CNN is best pronounced at L = 6 steps in our model” [PAGE 11]. This is important because homography alone does not solve identity continuity. The graph must propagate evidence across multiple frames so that a temporary occlusion does not force a new identifier.

A third step is confidence pruning. The classifier outputs an edge probability, and only edges above a threshold are retained. The source says, “For our experiments, we’ve used ξ = 0.9 to only retain the most strongest edges” [PAGE 6]. This is a conservative inference rule. It reduces false associations, but it also creates a gray-zone failure mode: if the true association probability is depressed by blur or partial occlusion, the correct edge may be removed. The result is tracklet fragmentation rather than identity switch. The paper’s evaluation emphasizes identity switches and IDF1 because these metrics measure association consistency more directly than detection-sensitive metrics. The source notes that “The FPs and FNs used to calculate MOTA relies solely on the detector’s quality” [PAGE 7], which explains why identity-oriented metrics are more diagnostic for this tracker.

The gray-zone audit must separate several failure regimes. First, homography assumes that the player foot contact point lies on a common plane, here the ice surface. The source says the foot keypoint “represents their exact point of contact with the ice” [PAGE 5], but this is an idealization. If the foot is occluded, if the bounding box bottom is poorly localized, or if the player is jumping, falling, or lying on the ice, the projected point may not correspond to a valid ground contact. Second, the homography matrix may become unreliable during camera cuts, extreme zoom, motion blur, or poor rink registration. The source acknowledges that broadcast feeds have dynamic camera movement [PAGE 1], but the graph tracker cannot distinguish a true player discontinuity from a projection discontinuity unless additional temporal checks are applied. Third, the edge threshold can discard valid associations during heavy occlusion. Fourth, the many-to-one violation rule enforces unique identity components, but if two players are extremely close in both image and projected space, the solver may still assign the wrong unique component. The ablation supports the core hypothesis: “only using appearance features underperforms by a noticeable margin” [PAGE 11], while adding homography improves identity metrics. Thus the model’s advantage is strongest when projected footpoints are accurate, temporally consistent, and not suppressed by the confidence threshold.

The source defines a broadcast sequence as \(S = \{F_i \mid i = 1, 2, \dots, n\}\), with \(n = t/\Delta t\) [PAGE 3]. Here \(t\) is sequence duration in seconds, \(\Delta t\) is frame interval in seconds, and \(n\) is dimensionless frame count. For each player node \(v_i\), the source lists frame identifier, player identifier, bounding box, confidence, and homographic footpoint coordinates [PAGE 3]. The node contains appearance features \(r_i^{id} \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\), bounding box \(b_i \in \mathbb{R}^4\) in pixel units, and projected footpoint \(p_i \in \mathbb{R}^2\) [PAGE 4].

The appearance operator is:
\[
r_i^{id} = \operatorname{ReID}(b_i|_{\mathrm{crop}})
\]
where \(r_i^{id}\) is dimensionless and \(b_i|_{\mathrm{crop}}\) is a cropped image region [PAGE 4]. The homographic footpoint operator is given as:
\[
p_i = H_i(f_l, f_r)
\]
where \(f_l\) and \(f_r\) are left and right footpoint estimates derived from the bounding box [PAGE 4]. The source writes this equation as “pi = Hi(fl, fr)” [PAGE 4]. The full projective form is:
\[
p_i =
s
\begin{bmatrix}
P_x' \\
P_y' \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\
h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\
h_{31} & h_{32} & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
P_{fx} \\
P_{fy} \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\]
[PAGE 5]. Here \(P_{fx}, P_{fy}\) are broadcast image coordinates in pixels, \(P_x', P_y'\) are projected template coordinates, \(s\) is the projective scale factor, and \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\) is the homography matrix [PAGE 5]. If the overhead template is calibrated to physical rink units, \(P_x', P_y'\) can be converted to meters using a metric scale \(k\) with units meters per template unit. If the template is only a raster, the coordinates are dimensionless template units.

The edge features are:
\[
\Delta r_{ij} =
\left[
\lVert r_i^{id} - r_j^{id} \rVert_1,
\cos(r_i^{id}, r_j^{id})
\right]
\]
[PAGE 5], where the first term is an appearance distance and the second is a cosine similarity. The positional edge feature is:
\[
\Delta p_{ij} =
\left[
\lVert p_i - p_j \rVert_1,
\lVert p_i - p_j \rVert_2
\right]
\]
[PAGE 5]. If \(p_i\) is metric, \(\Delta p_{ij}\) has units of meters; if \(p_i\) is template-raster, it has template units.

The initial node and edge embeddings are:
\[
h_{v_i}^{(0)} = f_v^{FE}\left(\left[r_i^{id}, p_i\right]\right)
\]
and
\[
h_{e_{ij}}^{(0)} = f_e^{FE}\left(\left[\Delta r_{ij}, \Delta p_{ij}\right]\right)
\]
[PAGE 5]. Table I gives the encoder dimensions: the node feature encoder maps a 515-dimensional input to 128 dimensions, and the edge feature encoder maps a 4-dimensional input to 8 dimensions [PAGE 6]. These embeddings are dimensionless.

The edge update is:
\[
h_{e_{ij}}^{(l)} =
f_e^{ME}
\left(
\left[
h_{v_i}^{(l-1)},
h_{v_j}^{(l-1)},
h_{e_{ij}}^{(l-1)}
\right]
\right)
\]
[PAGE 6]. The node update is:
\[
h_{v_i}^{(l)} =
\sum_{j \in N(v_i)}
f_v^{ME}
\left(
\left[
h_{v_j}^{(l-1)},
h_{e_{ij}}^{(l)}
\right]
\right)
\]
[PAGE 6]. The classifier outputs:
\[
\hat{y}_{e_{ij}} = f_{\mathrm{cls}}\left(h_{e_{ij}}^{(L)}\right)
\]
with a sigmoid final layer, so \(\hat{y}_{e_{ij}} \in [0,1]\) [PAGE 6]. The association uncertainty can be expressed in bits as:
\[
U_{e_{ij}} =
-\hat{y}_{e_{ij}}\log_2 \hat{y}_{e_{ij}}
-(1-\hat{y}_{e_{ij}})\log_2(1-\hat{y}_{e_{ij}})
\]
with maximum uncertainty 1 bit at \(\hat{y}_{e_{ij}} = 0.5\). This is a derived information-unit expression, not a source-reported metric.

The pruning operator is:
\[
\operatorname{Pruned}(\hat{y}_{e_{ij}}) =
\begin{cases}
\hat{y}_{e_{ij}}, & \hat{y}_{e_{ij}} > \xi \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
[PAGE 6]. The source uses \(\xi = 0.9\) [PAGE 6]. The identifier assignment rule is:
\[
\operatorname{ID}(h_{v_j}) =
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{SameID}, & h_{v_j} = h_{v_i},\ \forall j \in G_t,\ i \in G_{t-1} \\
\operatorname{NewID}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
[PAGE 6].

\[
L_{\mathrm{focal}} =
\sum_l
\sum_{e_{ij} \in G_{t-1} \cup G_t}
L\left(\hat{y}_{e_{ij}}^{(l)}, y_{e_{ij}}\right)
\]
[PAGE 7], where \(y_{e_{ij}} = 1\) for the same player and \(0\) otherwise [PAGE 7]. The loss is dimensionless.

\[
\mathrm{MOTA} =
1 -
\frac{
\sum_t FN_t + FP_t + IDsw_t
}{
\sum_t GT_t
}
\]
[PAGE 7], and
\[
\mathrm{IDF1} =
\frac{2 TP_{id}}{(2 TP_{id}) + FP_{id} + FN_{id}}

[PAGE 7]. Both are dimensionless ratios, often reported as percentages.

If the footpoint lies outside the rink template support, the projected coordinate is invalid. \lVert p_i - p_j \rVert_2 < 2\lVert \delta p \rVert_2

then the spatial cue is too weak to separate the two players. If the true edge probability satisfies \(\hat{y}_{e_{ij}} \leq 0.9\), the post-processing rule removes the association [PAGE 6]. If \(L\) is too small, information does not propagate sufficiently; if \(L\) is too large, overfitting or computational excess occurs. The source states that “L > 6 leads to possible overfitting on the training data” [PAGE 11].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits at the intersection of three historical streams: handcrafted sports tracking, deep re-identification, and graph-based data association. Early hockey tracking used boosted detectors and particle filters. The source notes that Okuma et al. used Adaboost detection with mixed particle filters, and Cai et al. added mean-shift stabilization and rink coordinates [PAGE 1]. Those systems were fragile under mutual occlusion, background change, blur, and lighting effects. The source also states that earlier works lacked quantitative evaluation: “there exists no quantitative evaluation in the above works to show the efficacy of their models” [PAGE 1].

The next historical step was the neural solver benchmark. Vats et al. created the first quantitative hockey tracking benchmark using message passing networks [PAGE 1]. The source acknowledges this as a benchmark but identifies two weaknesses: bounding box attributes as edge embeddings caused identity swaps under high overlap, and appearance-only node embeddings were ambiguous in hockey. The source says that bounding boxes “overlap significantly in the monocular view” and that appearance is weakened by “fully-covered bulk gear worn by players, similarly colored team jerseys, blur & lighting effects” [PAGE 1]. These are recurring traps for any sports tracking system trained on broadcast video.

The present method inherits from pedestrian tracking and multi-camera tracking. The pedestrian lineage includes SORT, DeepSORT, FairMOT, Tracktor, and ByteTrack [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. The multi-camera lineage uses camera geometry to map views onto a common ground plane. The source says that prior multi-camera graph methods “use the intrinsic camera parameters to map multiple camera views onto a common ground plane” [PAGE 3], but broadcast hockey feeds do not provide reliable camera intrinsics. Therefore the paper substitutes an off-the-shelf homography estimator trained on a top-view rink template [PAGE 3]. This is the key retro-causal adaptation: geometry is imported from multi-camera tracking, but the source of geometry is learned homography rather than known camera parameters.

A multi-week trap is metric selection. Detection-heavy metrics can be dominated by detector errors. The source warns that false positives and false negatives have “twice the weightage of IDsw in MOTA” [PAGE 7], so a tracker can appear weak because of detection quality even if its association logic is strong. The paper therefore emphasizes identity switches and IDF1. A second trap is assuming that appearance features alone can resolve hockey identities. The supplementary ablation shows that appearance-only tracking underperforms, while adding homography improves both identity metrics [PAGE 11]. A third trap is ignoring temporal context. Single-frame spatial cues are insufficient during occlusion; the graph must propagate evidence across neighboring frames. The chosen \(L = 6\) message-passing horizon is therefore not cosmetic; it is an empirical part of the method’s identity stability [PAGE 11].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In this domain, the tracker must model an adversarial visual environment, not a human opponent. The recursive belief problem is: given an image observation, what is the probability that node \(v_i\) at frame \(t\) corresponds to the same physical player as node \(v_j\) at frame \(t-1\)? The tracker’s first-level model estimates player identity from appearance and position. The second-level model must account for occlusion, camera motion, and projection error. The third-level model must decide whether to preserve an identity, split it, or create a new identity.

The “crisis habit regression” failure occurs when heavy occlusion causes the system to fall back to an older habit: associate by bounding-box overlap or appearance similarity. That habit is unstable in hockey because players cluster near the puck and wear similar gear. The source identifies this as a limitation of prior graph embeddings: bounding-box overlap increases under occlusion, causing “misassociation of players (identity switch) or a missed connection (lost tracklet)” [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is homographic disambiguation. By moving the footpoint to an overhead plane, the model changes the representational basis. Instead of asking whether two upper bodies look alike, it asks whether two ground-contact points occupy different rink locations. This is a recursive counter-strategy: the tracker does not merely improve appearance matching; it changes the coordinate system in which matching occurs.

The “clock” component is temporal propagation. A single frame is insufficient because occlusion can last several frames. The message-passing steps define a temporal receptive field. With too few steps, the tracker cannot propagate evidence across the occlusion interval. With too many steps, the model may overfit or propagate stale evidence. The source’s empirical optimum is \(L = 6\) [PAGE 11]. The clock also interacts with frame rate. The source reports cross-dataset results at different frame rates and argues that performance is not harmed by higher frame rate [PAGE 8]. This suggests that the graph’s temporal reasoning is not simply frame-count dependent; it is robust to sampling changes within the tested range.

A recursive theory-of-mind audit would therefore ask three questions. First, does the model believe that a missing player is absent, or merely occluded? If it chooses absence too early, it fragments tracklets. Second, does the model believe that a newly appearing player is new, or the reappearance of an old identity? If it creates new identities too readily, identity switches increase. Third, does the model believe that a projected coordinate is trustworthy during camera motion? If it overtrusts a bad homography, it can create false spatial separations or false merges. The paper’s post-processing encodes a conservative belief policy: retain only high-confidence edges and enforce unique connected components [PAGE 6]. That policy reduces false merges but can increase fragmentation if confidence is depressed. The best counter-trap is not a single trick but a coordinated stack: homography for spatial disentanglement, message passing for temporal continuity, and conservative pruning to prevent weak edges from corrupting identity propagation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

v_i(t) =
\frac{
k \lVert p_i(t+\Delta t) - p_i(t) \rVert_2
}{
\Delta t
}
\]
with SI units meters per second. If \(v_i(t) > v_{\max}\), where \(v_{\max}\) is an assumed biomechanical speed bound, the projected association is physically implausible and should be vetoed. The source does not provide \(v_{\max}\), so it must be treated as an external assumption, not a source-derived constant.

\[
a_i(t) =
\frac{
k \lVert p_i(t+2\Delta t) - 2p_i(t+\Delta t) + p_i(t) \rVert_2
}{
\Delta t^2
}
\]
with units meters per second squared. If \(a_i(t) > a_{\max}\), the projected trajectory is implausible under a smooth-skating assumption. This veto is especially useful during camera cuts or homography failures, because a bad projection can produce a large apparent jump even when the true player motion is continuous.

The homography itself is valid only under planar contact. The source says the foot keypoint “represents their exact point of contact with the ice” [PAGE 5]. That assumption breaks if the player is airborne, fallen, or occluded such that the estimated foot point is not on the ice plane. In that regime, the projection does not represent a ground-contact coordinate, and any speed veto based on that point must be suspended.

Environmental vetoes are weaker but still relevant. Indoor ice rinks are not weather-exposed in the same way as outdoor sports, but reflections, frost, skate spray, and condensation can alter the visual appearance of the ice and reduce homography registration quality. The source emphasizes “blurs and reduced visibility of players due to frequent camera motion” [PAGE 1]. These conditions do not change the physical ice plane, but they degrade the estimation layer that maps image footpoints to the rink plane. Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: if the homography is reliable and the projected motion violates kinematic bounds, veto the association; if the homography is unreliable, do not veto solely from projection, because the error may be geometric rather than physical.
