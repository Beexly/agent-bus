<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s central derivation chain is geometric rather than statistical. First, the pitch is asserted to be a planar structure, so the world point can be set as \(O_w=(X_w,Y_w,Z_w)^T\) with \(Z_w=0\) [PAGE 3]. That collapses the full perspective projection of Eq. (1) into the planar homography of Eq. (3), where \(H=K[r_1\ r_2\ T]\) and \(K\) contains focal length, principal point, aspect ratio, and skew [PAGE 3]. Second, the DLT stage estimates \(H\) from world-to-image correspondences; the paper states the hard condition verbatim: “DLT calibration method requires the coordinate data of at least four fixing points and they are not collinear” [PAGE 3]. This is the first gray-zone: four points are necessary, but they also must be well-conditioned; near-collinear points can satisfy the literal count while making the homography design matrix ill-conditioned. Third, the paper exploits a broadcast-production prior: “The position of main camera is standstill in the course of the competition” [PAGE 4]. If that prior is true, then \(T=(T_x,T_y,T_z)\) is known from stage one, and the stage-two unknown set shrinks. The paper says that when the camera position parameter is known, “the unknown parameters are reduced to 3” \(f,\alpha,\beta\), plus the scaling factor [PAGE 4]. That yields the PCC equation, Eq. (4), and the key operational result: calibration with “only two calibration points” [PAGE 4]. Because two points no longer overdetermine a homography, the paper explicitly falls back to nonlinear optimization: “In this case, we adopt LM algorithm to solve equation (4)” [PAGE 5]. The multi-hop conclusion is therefore: stationarity is not a cosmetic assumption; it is the degree-of-freedom reduction that makes two-point calibration identifiable. Remove stationarity, and the stage-two prior becomes a wrong-model bias.

The gray-zone audit must separate proven from asserted. The paper’s robustness evidence is synthetic: it describes simulated camera imaging and fixed parameters, including \(f=1000\) pixels, tilt \(100^\circ\), pan \(38^\circ\), and \(720\times480\) resolution [PAGE 5]. The noise study adds Gaussian perturbations to calibration-point coordinates and runs 500 trials over pan angles \(0,10,20,30^\circ\) and focal lengths \(500,1000,1500,2000\) pixels [PAGE 7]. It reports that parameter means are not associated with noise intensity while variances grow with noise, and that larger \(f\) improves calibration performance [PAGE 7]. That is useful but narrow: it does not test outliers, wrong line correspondences, lens distortion, rolling shutter, motion blur, rain, snow, shadows, occlusion by players, or real broadcast cuts. The claim that the method is “appropriate for real-time computation” [PAGE 2] is not backed by runtime measurements or LM convergence statistics. The superiority claim is conditional and the paper says so: “when the camera positional information is accurate, PCC is superior over DLT in terms of precision and robustness” [PAGE 8]. The audit therefore treats PCC as a well-motivated reduced-parameter estimator under a stationary-main-camera, planar-pitch, visible-line model, not as a universal broadcast calibrator. The most important singularity is not numerical noise but model breakdown: if stage one’s \(T\) is stale, if the broadcast cuts to a different camera, if the offside auxiliary lines are absent, or if the two points become coincident or nearly collinear, then the four scalar equations for \(f,\alpha,\beta,\lambda\) lose rank and LM can converge to a low-residual but physically wrong solution.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s projection operator is the standard pinhole mapping from world coordinates in metres to image coordinates in pixels. The paper defines world point \(O_w=(X_w,Y_w,Z_w)^T\), image point \(o=(x,y)^T\), aspect ratio \(\rho\), skew \(s\), principal point \((x_0,y_0)\), focal length \(f\), and scaling factor \(\lambda\) [PAGE 3]. A formal rendering of Eq. (1) is
\[
\lambda \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
K [R \mid T]
\begin{bmatrix} X_w \\ Y_w \\ Z_w \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\quad
K=
\begin{bmatrix} f/\rho &amp; s &amp; x_0 \\ 0 &amp; f &amp; y_0 \\ 0 &amp; 0 &amp; 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
Units: \(X_w,Y_w,Z_w,T_x,T_y,T_z\) are metres; \(x,y,x_0,y_0,f\) are pixels; \(\rho,s\) are dimensionless; \(\lambda\) is a homogeneous scale factor, fixed only by normalization. The paper states that \(\rho\) is aspect ratio, \(s\) is skew, \((x_0,y_0)\) is the main optical centre, \(f\) is focal length, and \(\lambda\) is a scaling factor [PAGE 3].

For a planar pitch, \(Z_w=0\), so Eq. (3) becomes
\[
\lambda \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
H \begin{bmatrix} X_w \\ Y_w \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\quad
H=K[r_1\ r_2\ T].
\]
Here \(H\) has eight degrees of freedom up to scale. DLT estimates \(H\) by least squares from \(N_p\) correspondences,
\[
\min_{H}\sum_{i=1}^{N_p}
\left\|
\pi\!\left(H\tilde O_i\right)-\tilde o_i
\right\|_2^2,
\]
where \(\pi\) is perspective division and residuals are in pixels. The paper’s exact condition is: “DLT calibration method requires the coordinate data of at least four fixing points and they are not collinear” [PAGE 3]. Thus the minimal DLT information count is four points times two scalar image coordinates, i.e. eight real-valued constraints for eight homography degrees of freedom. This is a constraint count, not a bit count; if each coordinate is quantized to \(b\) bits, the independent quantized information is at most \(2b\) bits per point under an independence assumption, but the paper reports no bit-level analysis.

The PCC stage uses the stationary-camera prior. The paper says: “when the camera position parameter … is known, the unknown parameters are reduced to 3” \(f,\alpha,\beta\), “with scaling factor” [PAGE 4]. A formal Eq. (4) expression is
\[
\lambda_i
\begin{bmatrix} x_i \\ y_i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
K(f,x_0,y_0)\,
R(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
\left(
\begin{bmatrix} X_{w,i} \\ Y_{w,i} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
-
\begin{bmatrix} T_x \\ T_y \\ T_z \end{bmatrix}
\right),
\]
with \(i=1,2\) in the minimal two-point case. The unknown vector is \(\theta=(f,\alpha,\beta,\lambda)\), assuming \(x_0,y_0,\rho,s,\gamma\) are fixed. Two image points give four scalar equations in pixels, so the minimal PCC problem is exactly determined before noise. The paper explicitly notes that with fewer points it is “not probable to acquire camera’s homography matrix H only based on two calibration points and thus unable to get the closed-form solution,” so it uses LM [PAGE 5]. The LM objective can be written as
\[
\min_{\theta}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_p}
\left\|
\pi\!\left(K(f)R(\alpha,\beta)
\left(
\begin{bmatrix} X_{w,i} \\ Y_{w,i} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}-T
\right)\right)
-
\begin{bmatrix} x_i \\ y_i \end{bmatrix}
\right\|_2^2,
\]
with squared pixel residuals. The paper’s noise model is effectively
\[
\tilde o_i=o_i+\epsilon_i,
\quad
\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I_2),
\]
where \(\sigma\) is in pixels; the 500-trial Monte Carlo study varies \(\sigma\) and reports mean and variance of \(f,\alpha,\beta\) [PAGE 7].

Breakdown regimes are exact and must be stated. DLT breaks when \(N_p\lt4\), or when the four or more points are collinear or near-collinear, because the homography design matrix has rank below eight and \(H\) is not unique [PAGE 3]. PCC breaks when \(N_p\lt2\), when the two points coincide, when their projected rays are nearly parallel, or when the Jacobian \(J_\theta\) has rank below four; then \(f,\alpha,\beta,\lambda\) are not identifiable and LM may stall or return a low-residual but incorrect solution [PAGE 5]. PCC also breaks when the stored \(T\) is stale or the main camera translates, because the model uses a false camera position; the paper’s superiority claim is explicitly conditional on accurate camera positional information [PAGE 8]. Euler-angle parameterizations have gimbal-lock singularities near tilt \(=\pm\pi/2\); an edge-on view with \(T_z=0\) or an optical axis parallel to the pitch plane makes the planar homography degenerate. The source does not model lens distortion, rolling shutter, motion blur, or line-detection outliers, and it does not test weather or occlusion; those remain unsupported regimes rather than proven capabilities.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits in the photogrammetry and sports-video calibration lineage: direct linear transformation and homography decomposition [PAGE 3], flexible camera calibration [PAGE 8, reference 3], extraction of physical camera parameters from the \(3\times3\) DLT matrix [PAGE 8, reference 5], court-model calibration for sport video [PAGE 8, references 6–7], kick-off-circle and vanishing-point approaches [PAGE 1], and Levenberg–Marquardt nonlinear least squares [PAGE 5]. The retrocausal lesson is that each later stage inherits the assumptions of the earlier one. DLT assumes a planar target and non-collinear correspondences; homography decomposition assumes a reasonable intrinsic model; PCC assumes the main camera’s translation is fixed across the sequence; LM assumes a differentiable residual and a good initialization. The multi-week trap is to validate stage one on goal-area frames, then assume stage two generalizes to midcourt or sideways frames merely because the algebra reduces the unknown count. In practice, the failure appears later: line detection selects a wrong offside auxiliary line, stage one’s \(T\) is slightly biased, and stage two’s two-point LM fit absorbs the bias into \(f,\alpha,\beta\). A second trap is historical: offside auxiliary lines are a modern pitch feature described as helping referees determine offside position [PAGE 2]; older or non-standard fields may lack them, so the method’s claimed non-goal-area coverage is field-dependent. A third trap is the “continuous” label: the abstract proposes continuous calibration, but the paper’s own production assumption is a stable main camera [PAGE 1, PAGE 4]; broadcast cutaways, reverse angles, and moving cameras are outside that genealogy.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three nested minds. The algorithm assumes the camera operator pans, tilts, and zooms but does not translate; the director assumes the main camera is the stable source; the detector assumes the visible pitch lines and offside auxiliary lines are the intended calibration objects. Crisis habit regression occurs when line detection is weak: the engineer reverts to the familiar four-point DLT ritual and forces four points even when the scene supplies only three lines or two useful intersections. That is the habit trap: satisfying the DLT point count while violating the non-collinearity and correspondence conditions [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap is the opposite overconfidence: because PCC can calibrate with two points [PAGE 4], the engineer treats any two points as sufficient, ignoring that the two-point fit is exactly determined and therefore highly sensitive to a stale \(T\), a wrong line identity, or a near-collinear pair. The clock trap is real-time pressure: stage one must run before stage two, and the temptation is to reuse an old camera position rather than re-estimate it. The paper’s own conditional wording is the correct guard: PCC is superior “when the camera positional information is accurate” [PAGE 8]. A recursive theory-of-mind audit should therefore ask not only “does the equation solve?” but “whose assumption is being trusted?”—the camera operator’s stationarity, the director’s camera selection, the referee-line visibility, and the line detector’s semantic labeling. If any of those nested assumptions is false, the correct response is to veto the calibration, not to add more LM iterations.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/optical layer vetoes calibration when the planar-pitch and visible-line premises fail. If the pitch plane is not the only calibrated plane, or if lens distortion and rolling shutter distort straight lines, the homography model is misspecified. If the offside auxiliary lines are occluded, shadowed, or absent, the non-goal-area method loses its added markers; the paper describes those lines as “offside auxiliary lines helping referees determine if there is offside position” [PAGE 2], so their visibility is a physical precondition, not a decorative detail. The biomechanical layer is mostly an occlusion layer: players, goalkeepers, and referees can block the very line intersections used as calibration points. The source does not model human occlusion, and it does not provide a biomechanical correction; therefore a claim of robust calibration under heavy player occlusion is vetoed by absence of evidence. The weather layer is similarly unsupported: rain, snow, fog, low sun, and long shadows reduce line contrast and can create false edges. The paper reports no weather experiments, so an all-weather robustness claim is not proven. The correct adjudication is layered: geometry can be valid while detection is invalid; detection can be valid while the camera prior is invalid; the camera prior can be valid while weather destroys contrast. The deployment veto should trigger on any layer that breaks the input contract—fewer than the required non-collinear points for DLT, fewer than two reliable points for PCC, an inaccurate \(T\), a camera cut, or a line-detection confidence collapse. The source supports the geometric reduction but not the cross-layer robustness claim.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Prediction: DLT’s homography design matrix becomes rank-deficient, \(H\) is not identifiable, and the decomposition into camera parameters becomes unstable; PCC with only one reliable point cannot supply four scalar equations. Veto criterion: if the system silently substitutes a false line or a collinear point and still reports calibration, the output is vetoed. This is the direct empirical test of the paper’s stated DLT requirement that four fixing points exist and “they are not collinear” [PAGE 3] and of the PCC requirement for at least two calibration points [PAGE 4].
