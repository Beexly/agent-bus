The anxiety construct splits into cognitive worry and somatic arousal per Morris et al. (1981) and Steptoe and Kearsley (1990) [PAGE 1], with Craft et al. (2003) documenting the negative correlation between cognitive anxiety and performance [PAGE 1]. Mindfulness is defined following Kabat-Zinn (2003) as present-moment, purposeful, non-judgmental attention [PAGE 1], and mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) in sport are contextualized through Gardner and Moore (2017) and Noetel et al. (2019) [PAGE 1]. Prior digital MBI evidence is mixed: favorable meta-analytic reports (Blanck et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2023) contrast with null RCTs in college athletes (Röthlin et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024) [PAGE 2]. Wang et al. (2026) attributes its positive divergence from null smartphone trials to three factors: sport-specific tailoring of content and language, an 8-week “dosage” sufficient for skill consolidation, and high adherence (82.9%) relative to typical digital health attrition exceeding 50% [PAGE 2; PAGE 9].

Multi-week traps embedded in the T0–T1–T2–T3 design. The assessment clock comprises baseline (T0), mid-intervention (T1, week 4), post-intervention (T2, week 8), and 4-week follow-up (T3, week 12), with electronic assessments targeted to the 24 hours before the nearest scheduled competition within each window [PAGE 2]. Event importance was recorded descriptively (club, collegiate/regional, championship) but not entered as a covariate [PAGE 2], so a T2 assessment embedded in a championship final is statistically equated with a low-stakes club time trial.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. In pre-competition crisis states, sprinters habitually regress to cognitive anxiety—negative thoughts, worries, and doubts about performance—and somatic anxiety—physiological arousal, muscle tension, butterflies [PAGE 1]. This regression is recursive: elevated anxiety erodes self-efficacy, and diminished self-efficacy amplifies threat appraisal, generating a vicious cycle of doubt and underperformance [PAGE 1]. The MindSprint intervention installs a counter-trap through mindfulness decentering: athletes learn to observe anxious thoughts as transient mental events without becoming entangled in their content, thereby reducing the impact of cognitive anxiety and regulating somatic arousal via greater bodily awareness [PAGE 1]. The mechanism operates across weekly modules: weeks 1–2 foundational attention (breath, body scan); weeks 3–4 mindfulness of thoughts and emotions; weeks 5–6 acceptance and self-compassion for setbacks; weeks 7–8 sport-specific mindful race visualization and mindfulness during movement [PAGE 2].

The recursive theory of mind unfolds across the study clock. Session frequency averaged 5.8 per week (SD 1.3) out of 7 recommended, with mean session duration 11.2 minutes (SD 2.1) and total practice 515.2 minutes [PAGE 1; PAGE 3 Table 2]. The acute phase (T0 → T2) produced large drops: cognitive anxiety 24.8 → 16.5, somatic anxiety 23.5 → 15.1 [PAGE 3 Table 3]. The maintenance phase (T2 → T3) showed near-stability: 16.5 → 17.1 cognitive, 15.1 → 15.8 somatic [PAGE 3 Table 3], suggesting the counter-trap held for four weeks post-intervention but did not deepen. Self-efficacy rose from 105.2 → 128.4 at T2 and held at 126.9 at T3 [PAGE 5 Table 4]. Figure 7 weekly heatmaps illustrate four response archetypes—rapid improvement with consistent use, gradual improvement, fluctuating response, and lower/variable engagement—demonstrating that the recursion is not uniform across athletes; some regress during high-stress competition weeks despite app access [PAGE 8]. The clock therefore imposes two constraints: the intervention must be frequent enough to compete with crisis habit regression (supported by 82.9% adherence), and the follow-up window is too short to confirm whether the counter-trap becomes automatic or requires booster sessions to prevent relapse beyond T3.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Physical, biomechanical, weather, and physiological veto proof. The study contains explicit hard vetoes against performance generalization. First, the physical performance layer: “Because objective sprint performance and physiological anxiety markers were not measured, the findings should be interpreted as evidence for changes in self-reported psychological outcomes” [PAGE 1 abstract]. No 100 m, 200 m, or 400 m race times, reaction times, split times, or velocity metrics were collected. Therefore any claim that MindSprint improves sprint performance is vetoed at the physical layer; the data support only psychological state change, not biomechanical output.

Second, the biomechanical layer: the introduction notes that excessive somatic symptoms can lead to “neuromuscular tightness and inefficient movement patterns, thereby impairing a sprinter’s explosive power and coordination” [PAGE 1], yet no electromyography, ground-contact force, stride length, or coordination metrics were measured. The somatic anxiety subscale (CSAI-2R) is a self-report Likert sum ranging 9–36 [PAGE 3], not a physiological or biomechanical index. A veto applies: reductions in self-reported somatic arousal do not establish reduced muscle tightness or improved force production.

Third, the weather and competition-context layer: assessments were anchored to the nearest scheduled competition within nominal windows, but “competition timing and event importance could not be fully standardized across athletes, and event level was not modeled as a covariate” [PAGE 2; PAGE 9]. Weather variables (wind velocity, temperature, humidity, precipitation) that directly affect sprint performance and pre-race anxiety were not recorded. A veto applies: intergroup differences at T1–T3 could reflect variations in competition schedule, weather, or race importance rather than the mindfulness intervention.

Fourth, the physiological anxiety layer: no heart-rate variability, salivary cortisol, or other autonomic markers were assayed [PAGE 1 abstract]. The mediation model links ΔFFMQ to Δ cognitive self-report anxiety, not to cortisol or HRV. A veto applies: the claimed anxiolytic mechanism is supported only by subjective report, not by objective psychophysiology.

Adjudication: The cross-layer veto stack leaves a single surviving inference layer—self-reported psychological change in cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety, self-efficacy, and dispositional mindfulness—bounded by the T0–T3 window. All extrapolations to race time, biomechanical efficiency, weather robustness, or autonomic regulation are formally vetoed absent additional measurement.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Current data show control stability (<1 point change), so the veto is not triggered by observed data, but the scenario remains empirically falsifiable in a replication with concealed post-trial access.

Because somatic anxiety was measured only by self-report (CSAI-2R, range 9–36) [PAGE 3], a participant may report lower worry while maintaining elevated autonomic arousal. Verification condition: compute Pearson correlation between Δ CSAI-2R somatic and Δ ln(HRV) or Δ cortisol at T2. Prediction: |r| < 0.30, indicating poor convergent validity; if confirmed, the somatic-anxiety reduction claim is vetoed at the physiological layer despite statistical significance on the self-report scale.

Counterfactual: restrict the mediation analysis to intervention participants completing fewer than 28 of 56 sessions (<50% adherence; observed range 28–56, mean 46.4) [PAGE 3 Table 2].
