<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The theoretical lineage of Knight's behavioral premium is anchored in Smith's observation of the "love of gain" and von Mangoldt's critique of utility. Knight's synthesis suggests that rational choice theory is inherently incomplete because it ignores the psychological drive to outbid competitors. This creates a multi-week trap: if an entrepreneur overpays for assets due to this premium, they must generate higher returns to compensate, locking them into high-risk strategies. In modern markets, this manifests as algorithmic high-frequency trading where bots with "restless energy" (Knight's term) continuously bid up prices beyond fundamental value. The trap deepens when liquidity dries up, revealing the extent of the irrational confidence that initially drove the bidding.

Here, $\alpha$ represents the dampening of time-value perception, and $\beta$ represents the amplification of uncertainty-bearing rewards under the "trait of tenacity."

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When facing a crisis, such as a sudden drop in asset valuations, the entrepreneurial psyche described by Knight undergoes a severe habit regression. Instead of recalibrating $Q$ toward $P$ (i.e., reducing optimism), the "trait of tenacity" triggers a counter-trap response: doubling down. Because the initial investment was justified by the "prestige of entrepreneurship," admitting a loss threatens the ego, leading to further irrational allocation of resources to "hold on to the last ditch."
In a recursive game against the market, the entrepreneur anticipates that rivals will panic sell. To maintain their status as "one's own boss," they attempt to absorb these distressed assets, misreading the systemic bust as a localized opportunity. However, if the market clock accelerates—liquidity vanishing faster than the tenacious holding period can sustain—the entrepreneur is trapped in a liquidity death spiral. Their internal model assumes time is on their side due to sunk costs, but the external market clock operates on immediate margin calls. This mismatch between the psychological clock (driven by tenure and pride) and the market clock (driven by volatility and cash flow) inevitably leads to forced liquidation at catastrophic levels.

The veto here is proof by exhaustion: the universe enforces conservation laws that override financial leverage. Thus, while Knight correctly identifies that prices are often set above what facts warrant, those facts ultimately include immutable physical constraints. When a cross-layer veto triggers, the "pure profit" evaporates instantly, exposing the structural fragility built upon the foundation of irrational confidence.
