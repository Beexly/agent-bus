<ORIGIN溯源>
The conceptual roots of Lebesgue's integration theory trace back to Riemann's partition-based approach, yet shatter its limitations through countable additivity and monotone convergence. The **mean fundamental sequence** concept, introduced by Banach and later refined by Hilbert in his study of function spaces, provides a metric framework for defining limits of approximating simple functions. Vitali’s covering theorem (1908) and Fréchet’s abstract metric space theory (1906) furnished the topological infrastructure necessary for a rigorous treatment of convergence in measure. Carathéodory’s outer measure construction (1914) and Lebesgue’s decomposition theorem laid the groundwork for extending the domain of integration beyond bounded intervals to more general measurable spaces. By 1950, when Halmos published *Measure Theory*, the field had matured under the combined efforts of Baire, Borel, Lebesgue, Jordan, and Radon. The shift from Riemann's crude finite-equipment paradigm to the modern, sigma-additive, limit-aware theory of integration necessitated an explicit resolution of the long-standing paradoxes surrounding non-measurable sets—culminating in Solovay's celebrated result (1970) that ZF + Dependent Choice + "Every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable" is consistent, thereby showing that certain pathological functions cannot be explicitly constructed without invoking the Axiom of Choice.
