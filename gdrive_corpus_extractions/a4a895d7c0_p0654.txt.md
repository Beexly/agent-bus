The lineage of this passage runs through Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, Chapter II, Section 8, which James quotes in the footnote at [PAGE 644]. Locke's point is that the mind can possess clear and distinct ideas—bitter versus sweet—even when the same external body produces different sensations under different bodily conditions. This places James in a historical tension between British empiricism (ideas derived from sensation) and rationalism (relations among ideas as necessary and a priori). The series of even difference and mediate comparison he invokes anticipates later work in the psychology of measurement and ordinal scaling, where immediate differences among stimuli are supposed to ground transitive, mediate judgments (if A differs from B and B differs from C, then A bears a rational relation to C).

The multi-week trap is longitudinal semantic drift. If a respondent is assessed over weeks with instruments that presuppose permanent meanings—say, repeated judgments that "black differs from white"—the analysis tacitly assumes the invariance axiom $\mathcal{C}(o_i, o_j; t) = \mathcal{C}(o_i, o_j; t')$. Because James's framework treats comparison results as immutable, it has no internal mechanism to detect this retroactive re-binding unless an external anchor fixes the identity of $o_i$ across time. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression operates when a subject under stress collapses the comparison space onto a small set of overlearned rational propositions. In James's terms, the subject regresses to the most immutable-seeming distinctions—black versus white, sweet versus bitter—and treats them as if they exhaust the rational layer. The recursive theory of mind here is second-order: the subject does not merely compare objects; they compare their own comparisons, verifying "I still mean the same by black," thereby using the invariance axiom as a self-confirming anchor. Under crisis, this verification loop becomes compulsive: the subject repeatedly checks that the same objects compared in the same way yield the same results [PAGE 644], not because new evidence supports it, because suspending the check feels epistemically dangerous.

The counter-trap is to mistake regressive rigidity for genuine stability. An observer may conclude that the subject's construct system is healthy because test-retest comparisons are invariant, when in fact the subject has frozen the comparison operator to prevent the anxiety of ambiguity. The clock is the temporal index $t$ in the invariance axiom. In normal cognition, successive moments allow the mind to mean the same while remaining open to revision; in crisis habit regression, the clock stops, so $t_1 = t_2$ functionally, and no new learning can update $\mathcal{O}$. The recursive theory of mind thus predicts a double bind: the subject's model of self as a possessor of fixed meanings protects against immediate uncertainty but blocks the very comparison updates that would allow adaptation when the external situation changes. Recovery requires restarting the clock—introducing a deliberate perturbation of $t$—so that $\mathcal{C}(o_i, o_j; t_{\text{new}})$ can be tested against $\mathcal{C}(o_i, o_j; t_{\text{old}})$ without the subject treating the discrepancy as a threat to identity.
