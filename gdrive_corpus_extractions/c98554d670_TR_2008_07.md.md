W = β_0 + β_{RS} RS + β_{SV} SV + β_{RA} RA + ε.
10-year: R² = 0.9321, R²_adj = 0.9314, MSE = 9.99941. 5-year: R² = 0.9272. 1-year: R² = 0.9121.
Units: W in wins; RS, RA in runs; SV in saves (dimensionless count). MSE in wins².
Breakdown: assumes run-differential stationarity across 1997–2006 and 2007; ignores park, weather, and bullpen-usage heterogeneity.

6. Pythagorean Expectation, [PAGE 12]:
\operatorname{WP\%} = \frac{RS^x}{RA^x + RS^x}, \quad x \in \{2,\; 1.83\}.
Units: RS, RA in runs; x dimensionless; output probability in [0, 1].
Breakdown: x ≤ 0 is physically absurd (a team outscoring opponents predicts a losing season) [PAGE 14-15]; invalid below RPG ≈ 4 without adaptation [PAGE 17].

7. Miller’s Weibull derivation of PE, [PAGE 13-15]:
X \sim \operatorname{Weibull}(α_{RS}, β, γ), \quad Y \sim \operatorname{Weibull}(α_{RA}, β, γ), \quad X \perp Y.
\operatorname{PE\ Theorem}: \quad P(X > Y) = \frac{RS^γ}{RA^γ + RS^γ}, \quad γ > 0.
Contingency test: 12×12 incomplete table, bins [0,1), …, [11, ∞), df = 109; modified χ² test; 13/14 AL teams satisfy independence [PAGE 13-14].
Exponent estimates: least squares x̄ = 1.79 (s = 0.09); maximum likelihood x̄ = 1.74 (s = 0.06) [PAGE 15].
Breakdown: Weibull shape γ > 0 required; independence of RS and RA is approximate because games cannot end in a tie [PAGE 13].

8. PythagenPort (Davenport, 1999), [PAGE 16-17]:
Poisson: P(X = k \mid λ) = e^{-λ} \frac{λ^k}{k!}.
Mixture mean: λ \in \{ RPG,\; RPG - 2(RPG)^{0.75},\; RPG + 2(RPG)^{0.75} \}, equal weights.
Exponent: x = 0.45 + 1.5 \log(RPG), \quad RPG = \frac{RS + RA}{G}.
Units: RPG in runs/game; x dimensionless. Validated over 4–40 RPG via 1,620-game simulation [PAGE 16-17].
Breakdown: Poisson assumes equidispersion (variance = mean); real run distributions are overdispersed (teams score zero more often and near the mean less often than Poisson predicts) [PAGE 16].

9. PythagenPat (Smyth / “US Patriot”), [PAGE 17]:
x = (RPG)^{0.287}, \quad \operatorname{WP\%} = \frac{RS^x}{RA^x + RS^x}.
Boundary condition: at RPG = 1, x = 1 ⇒ W = RS, L = RA for a 162-game season [PAGE 17].
Breakdown: only patches the exponent; inherits Poisson/Weibull run-distribution assumptions and does not model park or weather.

10. Squared-error playoff comparison, [PAGE 18, TABLE 3.1]:
SE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (W_i - \hat{W}_i)^2, \quad n = 30 \text{ teams}.
2007 errors (wins²): 1-year linear 358.96; 5-year 375.20; 10-year 366.00; PE-2 558.76; PE-1.83 503.86; Pport 529.02; Ppat 1324.29.
Breakdown: squared error rewards win-level accuracy but is not the project’s target; playoff qualification is a discrete classification problem.

11. Log5 single-game formula, [PAGE 21-22]:
WPAvB = \frac{WPA\,(1 - WPB)}{WPA\,(1 - WPB) + WPB\,(1 - WPA)}.
Derived by substituting RA_{League} for RAA and RAB in PE:
WPA = \frac{RSA^2}{RSA^2 + RA_{League}^2}, \quad WPB = \frac{RSB^2}{RSB^2 + RA_{League}^2}, \quad RAA = RSB.
Units: WPA, WPB ∈ [0, 1] dimensionless regular-season winning probabilities.
Breakdown: undefined (division by zero) if WPA ∈ {0, 1} or WPB ∈ {0, 1}; assumes equal average runs allowed for both teams and ignores park, weather, and starter matchups [PAGE 21].

12. Series win probabilities, [PAGE 23]:
Best-of-five: P_A = p^3 + 3p^3(1-p) + 6p^3(1-p)^2.
Best-of-seven: P_A = p^4 + 4p^4(1-p) + 10p^4(1-p)^2 + 20p^4(1-p)^3.
Units: p ∈ [0, 1]; output probability dimensionless.
Breakdown: assumes independent, identically distributed games with constant p; violated by pitcher rotations, travel, fatigue, and home–away alternation.

13. Simulation II blended probability, [PAGE 24]:
p = \frac{G_{AvB}}{162} WPAvB + \left(1 - \frac{G_{AvB}}{162}\right) WPRS.
Units: G_AvB ∈ [0, 162] games; WPAvB and WPRS dimensionless.
Breakdown: overweights small-sample head-to-head noise; e.g., New York’s 6–0 season series over Cleveland distorted p in Simulation II [PAGE 24-25].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

The intellectual genealogy begins with Bill James, who coined “sabermetrics” from SABR (Society for American Baseball Research) and emerged as its most prominent advocate [PAGE 1]. James’s 1981 Pythagorean Expectation, WP% = RS²/(RA² + RS²), was an empirical observation named for its visual resemblance to the Pythagorean theorem despite lacking geometric connection [PAGE 12]. Later refinements by James and others moved the exponent to 1.83 [PAGE 12]. Steven Miller’s 2007 Chance Magazine paper supplied a Weibull-distribution derivation, showing that under independent Weibull run-scoring and run-allowing processes, the Pythagorean form emerges as P(X > Y), with maximum-likelihood and least-squares exponent estimates of 1.74 and 1.79 respectively [PAGE 14-15, ref. 5].

Clay Davenport’s 1999 PythagenPort reacted to PE’s extreme-environment failures by modeling runs with a Poisson mixture and deriving x = 0.45 + 1.5 log(RPG), validated over a 4–40 RPG range [PAGE 16-17, ref. 3]. David Smyth and “US Patriot” then produced PythagenPat, x = (RPG)^{0.287}, specifically to cure the sub-4 RPG breakdown; Davenport publicly judged it “simpler” and “more elegant” and adopted it [PAGE 17, ref. 7]. Ray Ciccolella’s 2004 Log5 derivations and tests provided the single-game playoff probability engine used here [PAGE 21-22, ref. 2]. Finally, Nate Silver’s 2006 “Secret Sauce” introduced EqK9 (park- and league-adjusted strikeout rate), FRAA (Fielding Runs Above Average), and WXRL (Wins Expected above Replacement, closer leverage) as championship-relevant inputs absent from the source’s run-differential core [PAGE 26, ref. 9].

Multi-week traps specific to the 2007 season documented in the source: (1) the National League East and National League Central were “hotly contested races that came down to the final weeks and even days,” rendering long-run seasonal models nearly incapable of resolving them [PAGE 19-20]; (2) the National League West proved hard to predict because of team closeness, with Arizona scoring fewer runs than allowed and San Diego losing a one-game playoff to Colorado [PAGE 19]; (3) Cleveland–Boston regular-season winning percentages were identical (0.593), making that playoff series “basically a toss up,” while Colorado–Boston in the LCS had extremely similar percentages [PAGE 24]. These are not idiosyncratic noise; they are structural traps where a 162-game regular-season stationary model confronts short-series, high-leverage, late-season variance that the training data (1997–2006) cannot compress into a reliable exponent or coefficient set.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis-habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis-habit regression: The source’s models assume that regular-season run differential (RS, RA) and saves (SV) project linearly into the postseason [PAGE 11-12]. Under playoff crisis conditions, however, teams regress toward crisis-habit norms: rotations shorten to three or four starters, closers enter high-leverage situations earlier, and defensive replacements appear late in close games. None of these crisis-mode adjustments are captured by season-level RS, RA, or SV. Log5’s constant p therefore represents a regression to the regular-season mean precisely when teams are least likely to behave according to that mean.

Counter-trap: Simulation II’s blend, p = (G_AvB/162)·WPAvB + (1 − G_AvB/162)·WPRS [PAGE 24], is a counter-trap because it treats a small-sample head-to-head record as structural signal. The source documents that New York beat Cleveland 6–0 in regular-season meetings, giving that matchup the “largest impact” on Simulation II’s winning percentage and causing a misprediction in the Cleveland–New York series [PAGE 24-25]. The counter-trap is the inverse of the usual recency bias: instead of ignoring head-to-head, the model over-weights a six-game noise realization as if it were a stable matchup parameter, producing overconfidence in the wrong direction.

Clock mismatch: The source’s primary clock is the 162-game regular season, while the postseason clock is 5 or 7 games [PAGE 23-25]. The Monte Carlo simulation converges to Log5 expectations only as n_sim → ∞; at n = 1 it is a single Bernoulli draw, and the source concedes “either team can come out a winner” [PAGE 24-25]. This clock mismatch means that a model calibrated on 1,620-game simulations (Davenport) or 10-year regressions cannot resolve the entropy of a single short series. The recursive problem is that the model’s internal clock (season length) is mismatched to the external clock (series length), so confidence intervals derived from regular-season data are invalid for postseason prediction without an explicit short-series variance inflation term, which the source does not provide.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

Physical layer: The source’s core operators are purely statistical functions of runs scored, runs allowed, and saves [PAGE 11-12]. There are no explicit temperature, wind, precipitation, or atmospheric-density terms; the only environmental accommodation is Davenport’s RPG-dependent exponent in PythagenPort [PAGE 16-17]. Veto rule: if a postseason game is forecast in an environmental regime outside the 1997–2006 training envelope (e.g., extreme cold affecting ball flight, high wind at Wrigley or Coors, or marine-layer suppression at night games), the Weibull/Poisson run-distribution parameters are nonstationary and all model outputs are vetoed until park-weather covariates are added.

Biomechanical layer: Pitcher fatigue, closer leverage, and defensive range are biomechanical determinants of playoff outcomes that appear only in Silver’s “Secret Sauce” discussion (EqK9, FRAA, WXRL) and not in the RS/RA/SV regression [PAGE 26]. A postseason roster that deploys a shortened rotation or uses a closer in non-save situations violates the season-level SV regressor’s meaning. Veto rule: if playoff bullpen usage deviates from regular-season save patterns by more than one standard deviation, or if a starting pitcher is skipped on rest, the linear model’s SV coefficient is structurally invalid and the prediction is vetoed.

Weather veto: Because no weather covariates exist in the source [PAGE 2-17], any prediction for a game where temperature, wind speed, or humidity exceeds the historical range used to fit the Weibull shape parameter or Poisson λ mixture is automatically vetoed. The Weibull independence test and Miller’s χ² procedure were conducted on historical bins without weather stratification [PAGE 13-14]; therefore the independence assumption cannot be guaranteed under novel weather conditions.

Adjudication decision rule: Accept a playoff prediction only if (i) XᵀX is full-rank and well-conditioned for the chosen regressor set; (ii) RS/RA independence is not rejected at the 5% level for the teams involved; (iii) Log5 inputs lie strictly in (0, 1); (iv) RPG lies within the validated range for the chosen exponent model; and (v) no physical, biomechanical, or weather veto is triggered. If any layer fails, the model output is treated as null, not as a conservative probability.
