<THINK_TRACE>
The preprint’s evidentiary chain can be audited as a six-operator graph: image acquisition, edge extraction, track-marking detection, projective rectification, pose-based foot-contact detection, and metric distance reconstruction. The authors state that they combine “Canny edge detection, Probabilistic Hough Transform” with “homography” and “EfficientPose” [PAGE 2]. The central inferential claim is that foot contact points detected in pixels can be mapped into a measurable plane and converted into stride length or step length without physical markers [PAGE 2] [PAGE 8]. The audit must therefore distinguish geometric feasibility from measurement validity. A homography can always be fitted to four image-to-plane correspondences, but the result is metric only if the target plane has a known Euclidean scale. The paper says the homography maps “the projective image plane P2(pixel coordinates)” to “the Euclidean world plane R2 (metric coordinates)” [PAGE 5], but the provided text does not enumerate the metric coordinates assigned to the detected track intersections. This is the deepest gray zone. If the world coordinates are only inferred from the visual regularity of lane markings, then the absolute meter values depend on an unstated calibration assumption, such as a known lane interval or standard track width. If that assumption is absent, the method can recover relative geometry but not certified metric stride length.

The second gray zone is the definition of the measured quantity. The source describes using EfficientPose “to locate foot positions and measure the distance between consecutive steps” [PAGE 3] and later says “the distances between the support points were estimated using the Euclidean distance in the transformed plane” [PAGE 8]. In gait analysis, step length usually means distance between successive foot contacts, while stride length often means distance between successive contacts of the same foot. The paper’s language moves between “steps,” “support points,” and “stride length,” so the measured quantity is not fully disambiguated. If the reported values are consecutive support-point distances, they are step lengths; if they are same-foot intervals, they are stride lengths. This matters because the numerical values in Table 1, such as 1.66 m and 1.76 m for Athlete 1 [PAGE 8], may be interpreted differently depending on the gait definition.

The third gray zone is temporal sampling. The camera provides “1280×720 pixels, 30 frames per second” [PAGE 6]. The landing-point rule is that “if the keypoint remained stationary in the same pixel for two consecutive frames, it was considered a landing point” [PAGE 6]. At 30 Hz, two frames span approximately 0.0667 s. If true foot contact, or the visually detectable stationary interval of the toe keypoint, is shorter than that interval, or if occlusion and blur make the toe appear stationary while the foot is not actually planted, the contact set will be incomplete or false. This creates a speed-dependent bias: faster running may shorten contact duration and reduce the number of detectable stationary frames, while slower running may produce more detections. The paper acknowledges variability due to “running Conditions,” “Fatigue,” and “pacing” [PAGE 8], but those physiological explanations do not rule out measurement artifacts.

The fourth gray zone is robust estimation. The authors compute homographies from intersections involving “the first two lines, the second and third lines, and finally the fourth and fifth lines” and then take a “median of the matrices” [PAGE 6]. They also state that “distant lines appear unstable” but claim this does not matter because only four reference points from the fourth and fifth horizontal lines are required [PAGE 5]. This is risky. A homography is sensitive to intersection placement, especially when the intersections are distant, noisy, or nearly collinear. Taking an elementwise median of homography matrices is not the same as computing a geometric median on the projective manifold, and it may not preserve the most stable projective solution. Also, a homography is defined up to scale, so elementwise median across matrices with arbitrary normalization can be unstable. A more principled approach would be to collect all reliable correspondences, normalize coordinates, solve a robust DLT or least-squares problem, and propagate covariance into the final distance.

The final gray zone is validation. The authors explicitly state that “There is no ground truth (i.e., no objectively measured ‘true’ stride length)” [PAGE 8]. They therefore compare only consistency between two videos. Consistency is not accuracy. A biased homography, a systematically misassigned contact point, or a constant scale error can produce stable repeated values. The paper’s future work correctly identifies the need to compare against “motion capture systems or force plates” [PAGE 9]. Until that is done, the appropriate epistemic status is: feasible markerless pipeline, plausible coaching utility, but unquantified absolute metric error.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source contains several explicit operators. Each can be formalized with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Grayscale conversion, Gaussian smoothing, and Canny edge detection.
The paper says: “the scene was first converted to grayscale, followed by the application of a Gaussian filter. For edge detection, the Canny method has been employed” [PAGE 3]. A formal representation is:
\[
I_g(x,y) \in [0,1]
\]
where \(x,y\) are pixel indices. Gaussian smoothing is
\[
G_\sigma * I_g (x,y) = \sum_{m,n} G_\sigma(m,n) I_g(x-m,y-n)
\]
with kernel \(G_\sigma\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\) in pixels. The image gradient is
\[
\nabla I = (I_x, I_y)
\]
where \(I_x\) and \(I_y\) are partial derivatives with units intensity per pixel. The gradient magnitude and orientation are
\[
M(x,y) = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2}
\]
and
\[
\theta(x,y) = \operatorname{atan2}(I_y, I_x).
\]
Canny thresholding produces an edge set
\[
E = \{(x,y) : M(x,y) > T\}
\]
with \(E\) measured in pixel coordinates. Breakdown occurs when track-marking contrast falls below the noise floor, for example under rain, shadow, glare, or low illumination.

2. Probabilistic Hough Transform.
The source states that PHT “returns finite line segments rather than infinite lines” [PAGE 3] and provides tunable parameters “minLineLength and maxLineGap” [PAGE 4]. A classical Hough line model is
\[
\rho = x \cos\theta + y \sin\theta
\]
where \(\rho\) is distance from origin in pixels and \(\theta\) is angle in radians. For a segment \(s\), PHT returns endpoints
\[
s = ((x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2))
\]
with length
\[
\ell_s = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}.
\]
The parameter \(\text{minLineLength}\) imposes \(\ell_s \ge L_{\min}\), while \(\text{maxLineGap}\) permits gaps smaller than a pixel threshold. Breakdown occurs when curved, worn, or occluded markings produce fragmented segments that fail to merge into stable track geometry.

3. Vanishing point estimation.
The source says: “The vanishing point was derived as the intersection point of the two vertical lines” [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6]. If two image lines are represented as homogeneous line vectors \(l_1, l_2\), with each line satisfying
\[
l_i^T p = 0
\]
for points \(p = (x,y,1)^T\), then the intersection point is
\[
v = l_1 \times l_2.
\]
If \(v_3 \ne 0\), the finite vanishing point is
\[
(x_v, y_v) = \left(\frac{v_1}{v_3}, \frac{v_2}{v_3}\right).
\]
Units are pixels. If the two vertical lines are nearly parallel or incorrectly estimated, \(v\) becomes numerically unstable. If the true vanishing point lies far outside the image, small angular errors can produce large displacements.

4. Homography.
The paper gives the explicit projective relation:
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
x' \\ y' \\ w'
\end{pmatrix}
= H
\begin{pmatrix}
x \\ y \\ 1
\end{pmatrix}
\]
where \((x,y)\) are image coordinates and \((x'/w', y'/w')\) are world coordinates [PAGE 5]. The source also states: “The homography matrix \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}\) possesses 8 degrees of freedom (up to scale)” [PAGE 5]. Let \((u_i,v_i)\) be image coordinates and \((X_i,Y_i)\) be metric world coordinates. The mapping is
\[
X_i = \frac{h_{11}u_i + h_{12}v_i + h_{13}}{h_{31}u_i + h_{32}v_i + h_{33}}
\]
and
\[
Y_i = \frac{h_{21}u_i + h_{22}v_i + h_{23}}{h_{31}u_i + h_{32}v_i + h_{33}}.
\]
Each correspondence yields two linear constraints:
\[
h_{11}u_i + h_{12}v_i + h_{13} - h_{31}u_i X_i - h_{32}v_i X_i - h_{33}X_i = 0
\]
and
\[
h_{21}u_i + h_{22}v_i + h_{23} - h_{31}u_i Y_i - h_{32}v_i Y_i - h_{33}Y_i = 0.
\]
At least four correspondences are needed, and they must not be degenerate, for example not all lying on a single projective line. The source states that “at least four point correspondences” are required [PAGE 5]. Units require \((X_i,Y_i)\) to be meters if the final stride length is to be in meters. Breakdown regimes include: collinear correspondences, incorrect intersection selection, uncorrected lens distortion, camera motion, and absence of metric world coordinates.

5. Pose-based toe contact detection.
The source chooses EfficientPose because it is “optimized for real-time performance while maintaining accuracy” and predicts “a full set of 3D pose landmarks” [PAGE 6]. The authors use toe keypoints: “In our method, we used the positions of the toes as keypoints to determine the foot landing points” [PAGE 6]. The landing rule is:
\[
p_t = p_{t-1}
\]
for the toe keypoint in consecutive frames, where
\[
p_t = (u_t,v_t)
\]
is the pixel coordinate at frame \(t\) [PAGE 6]. With \(f_s = 30\) Hz, the frame interval is
\[
\Delta t = \frac{1}{f_s} \approx 0.0333\text{ s}
\]
and two-frame stationarity spans approximately \(0.0667\) s. Breakdown occurs if contact duration is shorter than the detectable stationary interval, if the toe is occluded, if motion blur causes apparent stationarity, or if the toe is not the true contact point.

6. Projective transformation and Euclidean distance.
After contact points are identified, the source says the “homography was applied to transform the points from the image plane to the real plane” and “the distances between the support points were estimated using the Euclidean distance in the transformed plane” [PAGE 8]. If \(p_k\) is the \(k\)-th detected support point and \(q_k = H p_k\), then
\[
L_k = \lVert q_{k+1} - q_k \rVert_2.
\]
If \(q_{k+1}\) and \(q_k\) are successive support points, \(L_k\) is a step length under common gait terminology. If they are same-foot contacts separated by one full gait cycle, \(L_k\) is a stride length. The source does not fully resolve this distinction [PAGE 3] [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s method sits at the intersection of four historical lineages: projective geometry, classical edge and line detection, vanishing-point reasoning, and deep pose estimation. Homography is a core operator from projective geometry and computer vision. It allows a planar scene to be rectified when camera geometry and plane correspondences are known. The Hough transform and its probabilistic variant belong to the classical era of feature extraction, where line segments are recovered from edge maps. Canny edge detection provides the upstream gradient-based boundary map. EfficientPose belongs to the modern learned-keypoint lineage, replacing handcrafted markers or manual annotation with neural landmark prediction.

The genealogical risk is that classical geometry and learned perception are coupled too tightly without enough calibration discipline. The paper’s pipeline inherits an old assumption from photogrammetry: a planar scene can be rectified if reliable correspondences are available. However, the source’s detected correspondences are generated from track markings, and the paper itself notes that “distant lines appear unstable” [PAGE 5]. Historically, unstable line intersections are a known failure mode in perspective recovery because distant features compress into small pixel regions, increasing angular uncertainty. If those unstable features are used for homography, the rectified plane can acquire a hidden scale or shear error.

Another genealogical trap is the transition from markers to markerless estimation. Marker-based systems make the contact point explicit. Markerless pose estimation must infer the contact point from anatomical landmarks. The paper’s choice of toe keypoints is reasonable, but the toe is not always the contact point across all running styles. Some runners land with a more pronounced heel strike, some with midfoot contact, and some with forefoot contact. The historical move away from markers therefore introduces a biomechanical ambiguity: the visual stationary point may not correspond exactly to the center of pressure or the true initial contact point.

A multi-week trap follows. If coaching staff collect data over several weeks without ground truth, the pipeline can accumulate systematic bias. Lighting changes, camera vibration, track wear, lane repainting, or lens drift can alter edge detection and line intersections. If the system is tuned to one session, later sessions may appear consistent while actually drifting. The paper’s future-work statement is relevant here: it proposes comparing results with “motion capture systems or force plates” to quantify error margins [PAGE 9]. Retro-causally, if that validation later reveals bias, the earlier reliance on “consistency between the two videos” [PAGE 8] will look insufficient. The genealogy therefore warns against treating short-term repeatability as proof of measurement validity.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive stakeholder model must distinguish coaches, athletes, sports scientists, and developers. Each has a different utility function. Coaches want immediate, actionable training feedback. Athletes want trustworthy feedback that does not destabilize technique. Sports scientists want calibrated measurement with uncertainty bounds. Developers want a pipeline that runs reliably on available video. The paper’s tool is attractive because it promises non-intrusive monitoring, but its epistemic status is fragile because “There is no ground truth” [PAGE 8].

Crisis habit regression is the tendency of coaches under time pressure to fall back on intuition. If the system produces uncertain or unstable values, coaches may ignore it and rely on visual judgment. This is not irrational; it is a rational response to an uncalibrated instrument. However, if the tool is deployed during a high-pressure training block, the desire for immediate feedback may cause staff to overinterpret small changes. For example, Athlete 1’s increase from 1.66 m to 1.76 m is described by the paper as “a noticeable increase” that “could suggest differences in running speed and fatigue” [PAGE 8]. In a recursive coaching setting, that interpretation may become a training intervention even though the measurement lacks ground-truth validation. The recursive loop is: uncertain metric, plausible physiological explanation, coaching adjustment, altered athlete behavior, and then apparent confirmation in later data.

Counter-trap is the opposite failure. Developers and staff may assume that because the pipeline uses homography, Canny, PHT, and EfficientPose, the result is automatically scientific. This can create a false authority loop. The presence of formal geometry can make an estimate feel objective even when the scale calibration is incomplete. The counter-trap is especially strong when the paper emphasizes “classical image processing” and “homography” as sources of precision [PAGE 2] [PAGE 9]. The missing element is not geometric sophistication; it is external validation.

Clock pressure is the training schedule. Coaches operate on weekly cycles: load management, technical drills, recovery, and competition preparation. If a stride-length estimate arrives on the coach’s clock, it may be used to modify training before validation catches up. Over multiple weeks, this can produce a habit loop: collect video, compute approximate stride, adjust training, and never measure true error. The recursion becomes stable because the loop is fast, cheap, and visually plausible. To counter this, the system should expose uncertainty, not merely output a single meter value. For example, the output should include confidence bounds derived from homography covariance, contact-detection certainty, and frame-rate limitations. Without such uncertainty, the recursive loop will tend to convert consistency into false confidence.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A valid metric stride estimate requires simultaneous satisfaction of physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints. The veto proof is as follows.

Physical layer: the camera must be static, the imaging model must be approximately projective, and the scene must be planar at the relevant contact and marking regions. Let camera extrinsics be constant over the observation window. If camera pose changes, the homography changes, and a single rectified plane is invalid. If lens distortion is significant, raw pixel coordinates are not suitable for projective line intersection without correction. If the track is not locally planar, or if the athlete’s foot contact occurs on a surface with vertical variation, the homography mapping is not valid. The paper’s use of a frontal camera view [PAGE 6] is consistent with a static-camera assumption, but the provided text does not explicitly state calibration against lens distortion or camera stability.

Biomechanical layer: the detected toe keypoint must correspond to the relevant foot-contact event. The source’s rule is stationarity: “if the keypoint remained stationary in the same pixel for two consecutive frames, it was considered a landing point” [PAGE 6]. This assumes that the toe is stationary in world coordinates during contact and that the camera is static. It also assumes that the pose estimator remains stable under motion blur, occlusion, and fast limb movement. At 30 Hz, the detectable stationary window is short. If contact duration is less than two frames, or if the toe keypoint is occluded by the other leg, the contact may be missed. If the toe moves slightly while the foot is planted, the stationarity test may fail. If the toe appears stationary due to blur but the foot is actually moving, a false landing point may be created. Therefore, the biomechanical layer can veto the estimate whenever contact detection is unreliable.

Environmental layer: lighting, rain, glare, shadows, and track-surface contrast affect Canny and PHT. The paper itself acknowledges unstable distant lines [PAGE 5]. If line intersections are unstable, the homography becomes unstable. If the homography is unstable, Euclidean distances in the rectified plane become unreliable. Weather can also create specular reflections that alter edge maps. A formal environmental veto condition can be written as: if the edge confidence or line-segment persistence falls below a threshold, then the homography covariance exceeds an allowed bound, and the stride estimate should be rejected or flagged.

The strongest veto is metric calibration. The source states that a planar homography requires correspondence between pixel coordinates and “Euclidean world plane R2 (metric coordinates)” [PAGE 5]. However, the provided text does not give the metric coordinates assigned to the four reference points. If those coordinates are not explicitly known, there is a scale ambiguity. Formally, if \(H\) is one solution, then a scaled world plane \(H' = S H\), where \(S\) applies a planar scaling, can preserve the same image-line geometry while changing all metric distances. Therefore, absolute stride values in meters are not identifiable without an external metric anchor. Consistency across two videos does not remove this ambiguity. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: the pipeline is feasible as a relative markerless estimator, but its absolute metric claims require explicit calibration and ground-truth validation. Until then, any claim of precise stride length must be treated as conditional.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
