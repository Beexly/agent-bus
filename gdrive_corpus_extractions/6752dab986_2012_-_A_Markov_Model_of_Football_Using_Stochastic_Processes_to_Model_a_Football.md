The source is Keith Goldner’s “A Markov Model of Football: Using Stochastic Processes to Model a Football Drive” [PAGE 1]. The reconstruction proceeds by multi-hop translation: first, a football drive is compressed into a finite state \(X_n=(d_n,y_n,z_n)\), where \(d_n\in\{1,2,3,4\}\) is down, \(y_n\) is distance-to-go in grouped 5-yard bins with all distances greater than 20 yards lumped together, and \(z_n\) is one of 20 five-yard field zones [PAGE 6]. The paper reports 340 transient states and 9 absorbing drive-ending states: touchdown, field goal, safety, missed field goal, fumble, interception, turnover on downs, punt, and end of half or game [PAGE 6]. Thus the full chain is \(340+9=349\) states [PAGE 6]. The transition matrix is estimated by direct counting: “if we were in state x 100 times, 40 times we went to state y, 60 times we went to state z then the transition probabilities are Px,y = 0.4, Px,z = 0.6” [PAGE 6]. The model then partitions the matrix into transient block \(Q\) and absorption block \(R\), computes \(B=(I-Q)^{-1}R\), and derives expected absorption time \(t=(I-Q)^{-1}c\) [PAGE 7, PAGE 11]. Expected points are computed by multiplying absorption probabilities by terminal point values: \(EP_{x,y,z}=\rho_{v,TD}7+\rho_{v,FG}3+\rho_{v,SAF}(-2)\), with a footnote adjustment that touchdowns are effectively 6.96 because extra points are made about 96% of the time [PAGE 12]. The reported example is \(EP_{1,10,20}=0.138\cdot 7+0.093\cdot 3-2\cdot 0.002=1.24\) expected points [PAGE 12]. Table 3 gives \(1\)st-and-\(10\) from own 20 as punt \(49.80\%\), field goal \(9.30\%\), touchdown \(13.84\%\), \(EP=1.24\), and expected remaining plays \(6.65\) [PAGE 17].

The gray-zone audit begins with the Markov property itself. The paper states \(P(X_{n+1}=x\mid X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_n=x_n)=P(X_{n+1}=x\mid X_n=x_n)\) [PAGE 4]. That is a strong conditional-independence claim. The author immediately qualifies it: “previous play-calls may affect future decision-making beyond the most recent play” [PAGE 5]. The reconciliation is: “If we disregard specific play calling—as run and pass are not variables in the model—only a team’s current down, distance, and yard line determine the probability of future events in a drive” [PAGE 5]. This is not a proof of sufficiency; it is a modeling restriction. Any hidden variable \(H\)—score differential, time remaining, timeouts, weather, fatigue, injury, personnel, opponent tendency—creates a possible violation if \(P(X_{n+1}\mid X_n,H)\neq P(X_{n+1}\mid X_n)\). The paper’s own conclusion concedes that “accuracy of the model can be increased slightly by separating late-game situations and adding more data to ensure higher frequencies” [PAGE 16]. That is a direct admission that clock/game context is a gray zone.

A second gray zone is state aggregation. The paper groups distance-to-go into 5-yard increments and says this was done “to ensure high enough frequencies for every state; if there were any states that never occurred in a game in the past 5 years, it would detract from the accuracy of the model” [PAGE 6]. The frequency range is “6 to 6624, with an average of about 550 visits to each state” [PAGE 6]. Aggregation is statistically necessary but mathematically hazardous: if microstates \(u\) and \(v\) are merged into one macrostate \(S\), the model uses an average transition law. If \(P(\cdot\mid u)\neq P(\cdot\mid v)\), the aggregated state is not lumpable and the Markov property holds only approximately. The paper tests this and finds that “3rd-and-1 to 3rd-and-5 is the most questionable” grouping, with average deviation \(5.5\%\), largest for punt \(11.2\%\), touchdown \(10.9\%\), and field goal \(9.2\%\) [PAGE 10]. For 3rd-and-1 from the opponent’s 15, the model estimates \(37.9\%\) touchdown absorption while actual drives produced \(45.1\%\), a \(7.2\%\) difference [PAGE 10]. This is a concrete validation of the aggregation gray zone.

A third gray zone is absorption semantics. “End of half or game” is an absorbing state, but the chain has no explicit clock variable. A drive ending because time expired is structurally different from a punt or turnover, yet all are absorbing. The paper’s maximum expected absorption time is “7.57 plays (for 1st-and-5 from a team’s own 16-20)” and the minimum is 1 play [PAGE 11]. That statistic is an expected play count, not a clock-aware duration. The model also reports the 4th-and-26 from the 25 as “a whopping 1 out of 175” for a successful field-goal ending [PAGE 3]. That number is a conditional absorption probability from a rare state, so its stability depends on sample size and aggregation. The model is a descriptive league-average chain, not a causal simulator of a specific game.

A fourth gray zone is nonstationarity and heterogeneity. The transition matrix is fit to 2005–2009 data: “about 200,000 plays, 30,000 drives, and 1,280 games” [PAGE 6]. The 2010 team NEP table is then interpreted through a model fit to earlier seasons [PAGE 13–15]. If league strategy, rules, or player populations shift, the matrix is stale. The paper notes that run and pass are not variables in the model [PAGE 5], so the matrix marginalizes over play calls. That is useful for average expected points but insufficient for counterfactual play-calling. The equations are valid only under the assumption that the estimated \(Q\) has spectral radius strictly less than 1 and that every transient state has a positive probability path to absorption. If \(I-Q\) is singular—equivalently, if a closed transient class exists or a row of \(Q\) sums to 1 with no absorption leak—then \(B\) and \(t\) are undefined. Sparse or zero-frequency states create the same breakdown because the counting estimator has no defined row.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core operator is the finite-state, discrete-time absorbing Markov chain. The Markov property is stated verbatim as:
\[
P(X_{n+1}=x\mid X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_n=x_n)=P(X_{n+1}=x\mid X_n=x_n)
\]
[PAGE 4]. The transition probability is “\(P_{x,y} =\) Probability of going from state ‘x’ to state ‘y’ in one step” [PAGE 5]. The matrix has \(349\) rows and columns, with the first \(340\) entries transient and the last \(9\) absorbing [PAGE 6]. The absorbing partition is \(P=(Q,R;0,I)\), where \(Q\) is \(340\times340\), \(R\) is \(340\times9\), \(0\) is \(9\times340\), and \(I\) is \(9\times9\) [PAGE 7]. The fundamental matrix is
\[
N=(I-Q)^{-1},
\]
and absorption probabilities are
\[
B=NR=(I-Q)^{-1}R.
\]
The paper writes this as “B = [(I – Q)-1]*R” [PAGE 7]. \(B\) is \(340\times9\); entry \(B_{i,a}\) is the probability, dimensionless in \([0,1]\), that a drive starting in transient state \(i\) is absorbed in absorbing state \(a\). The expected absorption time is
\[
t=Nc=(I-Q)^{-1}c,
\]
where \(c\) is a \(340\)-vector of ones [PAGE 11]. Each \(t_i\) is in plays, conditional on starting state \(i\). Expected points are
\[
EP_{x,y,z}=f(x,y,z)=\rho_{v,TD}7+\rho_{v,FG}3+\rho_{v,SAF}(-2),
\]
where \(\rho_{v,a}\) is the absorption probability into state \(a\) from state \(v=(x,y,z)\) [PAGE 12]. The unit is points per drive conditional on the state. Footnote 6 says “we use 6.96 for the touchdown since there is a 96% chance of making the extra point” [PAGE 12]. Equivalently, \(EP=B a\), where \(a\) carries terminal values: \(6.96\) for touchdown, \(3\) for field goal, \(-2\) for safety, and \(0\) for the other absorbing states [PAGE 12].

The empirical counting estimator is
\[
\hat P_{ij}=\frac{n_{ij}}{\sum_{k}n_{ik}},
\]
where \(n_{ij}\) is the observed count of one-step transitions from \(i\) to \(j\). The paper describes the logic: “if we were in state x 100 times, 40 times we went to state y, 60 times we went to state z then the transition probabilities are Px,y = 0.4, Px,z = 0.6” [PAGE 6]. The data are 2005–2009 play-by-play: “about 200,000 plays, 30,000 drives, and 1,280 games” [PAGE 6]. The state space uses 20 five-yard field zones and 5-yard distance bins, with all distance-to-go greater than 20 yards lumped together [PAGE 6]. Frequency range is “6 to 6624, with an average of about 550 visits to each state” [PAGE 6].

Validation numbers: for 1st-and-10 from own 20, the model predicts \(9.3\%\) field-goal absorption and reality gives \(10.3\%\), a \(1.0\%\) difference; average deviation is \(0.45\%\) [PAGE 10]. For 2nd-and-5 near the opponent’s 40, model touchdown \(30.0\%\) versus actual \(31.3\%\), difference \(1.3\%\), average deviation \(0.78\%\) [PAGE 10]. For 3rd-and-1 near the opponent’s 15, model touchdown \(37.9\%\) versus actual \(45.1\%\), difference \(7.2\%\), average deviation \(1.9\%\) [PAGE 10]. The 3rd-and-1 versus 3rd-and-5 grouping has average deviation \(5.5\%\), with punt \(11.2\%\), touchdown \(10.9\%\), field goal \(9.2\%\) [PAGE 10].

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. \(N=(I-Q)^{-1}\) exists only if \(I-Q\) is invertible; for an absorbing chain this requires that every transient state can reach an absorbing state with probability 1, equivalently that the spectral radius of \(Q\) is strictly less than 1. If a transient row has no absorption leak, or if a closed transient class exists, \(I-Q\) is singular and both \(B\) and \(t\) fail. If a state has zero observed visits, \(\hat P_{ij}\) is undefined or requires smoothing. If states are aggregated non-lumpably—e.g., 3rd-and-1 merged with 3rd-and-5—the matrix is a mixture, not a microstate Markov law; the paper’s own \(11.2\%\) punt deviation is evidence of that breakdown [PAGE 10]. If the process is nonstationary across seasons, the single matrix is a time-average, not a conditional law. If hidden variables such as clock, score, weather, fatigue, or personnel affect transitions, the Markov property is violated unless those variables are added to the state.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit. The paper defines a stochastic process by quoting Doob: “any process in nature whose evolution we can analyze successfully in terms of probability” [PAGE 4]. It places itself in a sports-Markov genealogy: Newton and Aslam’s tennis model predicts game, set, and match winners from serve and return inputs [PAGE 4]; Pankin’s baseball model uses outs and base runners to compute expected runs [PAGE 4]. The football analog is expected points, which the paper traces to Virgil Carter and Robert Machol’s “Operations Research on Football” and to *The Hidden Game of Football* by Carroll, Palmer, and Thorn [PAGE 11]. The later NEP lineage is also named: Alamar’s “Measuring Risk in NFL Playcalling” and the paper’s own 2010 “Ratio of Relative Importance” [PAGE 16]. The retrocausal reading is that modern expected-points and EPA-style metrics often treat this 2012 Markov paper as an ancestor, but the paper itself is a bridge between older empirical expected-point tables and matrix-based absorption probabilities.

Multi-week traps follow from that lineage. First, the “expected points” label can tempt analysts to treat \(EP\) as a causal value of a play call, but the model explicitly excludes run and pass as variables [PAGE 5]. A multi-week study that compares team NEP without holding play-call mix constant may attribute scheme effects to the Markov baseline. Second, the 2010 team table is evaluated against a 2005–2009 matrix [PAGE 13–15]; a multi-week replication must decide whether to refit the matrix or freeze it, and the two choices answer different questions. Third, the paper’s own warning about late-game separation [PAGE 16] means a multi-week analysis that pools all clock states will systematically blur end-of-half and end-of-game behavior. Fourth, the 3rd-and-1/3rd-and-5 aggregation is a known trap: the paper reports \(7.2\%\) touchdown underestimation for one 3rd-and-1 situation [PAGE 10], so a multi-week study that uses the pooled bin for short-yardage decisions will inherit that bias. Fifth, the “1 out of 175” rarity for 4th-and-26 from the 25 [PAGE 3] is a sample-size trap: rare-state probabilities are highly sensitive to smoothing, binning, and era. The correct genealogical use is to treat the matrix as a league-average prior, then test whether added state variables improve out-of-sample calibration, not to reify single-cell estimates.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem is a level-\(k\) game over a Markov baseline. Level 0 is the matrix: given \((d,y,z)\), the coach observes absorption probabilities and \(EP\). Level 1 is a coach who chooses the action with higher expected points, e.g., going for it on 4th down near the opponent’s 30 where the paper shows “a steep, inverse change between punt probability and field goal probability” [PAGE 8]. Level 2 is an opponent who anticipates that habit and prepares a return or a short-field defense. Level 3 is the coach who anticipates the opponent’s anticipation and may counter-trap by deviating from the base-rate punt. The model itself cannot represent this recursion because “run and pass are not variables in the model” [PAGE 5]; it supplies the base rates, not the strategic equilibrium.

Crisis habit regression appears when clock pressure is high. The model has an absorbing “EndHalf/Game” state, but no explicit time variable; the paper concedes that separating late-game situations could improve accuracy [PAGE 16]. Under a short clock, a coach may regress to the habit “punt on 4th down” even when the absorption probabilities favor aggression. The counter-trap is to exploit that habit: if the opponent expects a punt, a fake or a go-for-it call can have value beyond the Markov \(EP\) because it changes the opponent’s response distribution. The clock is the hidden state that adjudicates between these: with enough time, the base-rate \(EP\) is closer to sufficient; with little time, the value of surprise and the cost of consuming a play dominate. The 4th-and-26 from the 25 is the extreme case: the model gives “a whopping 1 out of 175” for a successful field-goal ending [PAGE 3], so a crisis decision is almost entirely about tail probability, not routine habit. The recursive theory of mind therefore says: use the Markov model for the prior, use clock and score to truncate the horizon, and use opponent beliefs only when the action changes the opponent’s conditional response.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is formal. Let \(X_n=(d_n,y_n,z_n)\) be the Markov state. Let \(W\) be a weather vector with wind velocity \(U\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), temperature \(T\) in \(\mathrm{K}\), and precipitation rate \(R\) in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\). Let \(B\) be a biomechanical/player-state vector including fatigue load in meters of high-speed running, injury status, and available personnel. Let \(H\) be a physical/venue vector including surface friction coefficient \(\mu\) (dimensionless) and altitude in meters. The Markov model assumes
\[
P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,W=w,B=b,H=h)=P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)
\]
for all \(w,b,h\). If that equality fails, the fitted matrix is a marginal average:
\[
P_{\text{obs}}(j\mid i)=\sum_{w,b,h}P(j\mid i,w,b,h)\,P(w,b,h\mid i).
\]
The veto condition is conditional mutual information
\[
I(X_{n+1};W,B,H\mid X_n)>0
\]
measured in bits or nats. A layer veto is proven if the conditional mutual information is statistically distinguishable from zero on held-out data, or if the induced change in \(EP\) exceeds a pre-registered tolerance. Physically, wind alters field-goal trajectories; rain and cold alter grip and ball flight; surface friction alters acceleration and cutting; altitude alters air density. Biomechanically, fatigue and injury alter acceleration, force production, and decision speed. The paper includes none of these variables in the state, so its matrix is a league-average marginal over them. The paper’s own admission that “accuracy of the model can be increased slightly by separating late-game situations” [PAGE 16] is a partial cross-layer veto: clock/game context is a hidden layer. The adjudication rule is therefore: no weather, biomechanical, or physical claim can be inferred from a single cell of the matrix unless the relevant conditional mutual information is shown to be negligible or the state is augmented. Otherwise the correct move is to stratify or add covariates, not to interpret the pooled absorption probability as a physical law.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. **Short-yardage aggregation veto.** Verifier: split 3rd-and-1 from 3rd-and-5 and refit on held-out seasons; compare log loss and Brier score for touchdown, field goal, and punt absorption. Prediction: the split model will improve calibration and will show higher touchdown absorption and lower punt absorption for 3rd-and-1. The paper already reports that the pooled model underestimates touchdown for a 3rd-and-1 from the 15 by \(7.2\%\) and has \(11.2\%\) punt deviation [PAGE 10]. Veto: if the split model does not improve held-out calibration beyond a pre-registered threshold, reject the aggregation-bias hypothesis. This is verifiable because the state definition and the three absorbing outcomes are fixed.

2. **Weather/venue veto.** Verifier: add wind speed \(U\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), precipitation, temperature, and venue altitude to the absorption model for field goals and punts; test conditional mutual information \(I(\text{absorption};\text{weather}\mid d,y,z)\). Prediction: high wind and precipitation will increase missed-field-goal absorption and decrease successful field-goal absorption from the same yard line, especially at longer attempt distances. Veto: if the weather-augmented model does not reduce held-out log loss or does not produce a conditional mutual information estimate distinguishable from zero, the weather veto is not proven. The paper’s nine absorbing states include “Missed FG” and “Field Goal” [PAGE 6], so the outcome is directly verifiable.

3. **Clock/score/late-game veto.** Verifier: add time remaining, score differential, and timeouts to the state and test whether “EndHalf/Game” absorption and 4th-down absorption probabilities become better calibrated. Prediction: late-game states will show materially different 4th-down behavior than the pooled matrix, and the pooled model will miscalibrate end-of-half absorption. The paper explicitly says accuracy “can be increased slightly by separating late-game situations” [PAGE 16], so the counterfactual is source-grounded. Veto: if the augmented model fails to improve held-out calibration for end-half/game and 4th-down outcomes, reject the late-game-veto claim. A second verifiable check is the rare 4th-and-26 from the 25: the paper reports “a whopping 1 out of 175” for a successful field-goal ending [PAGE 3]; a replication with clock and score strata should either narrow the uncertainty or show that the single pooled number is not stable. If the stratified estimate is unchanged within pre-registered intervals, the clock veto is not proven.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
