**Crisis habit regression.** The practitioner under time pressure does not read a model report; they read a number. Define the report as a tuple \(r=(\hat{R},\,\mathrm{CI},\,\mathrm{ECE},\,V_{\mathrm{ext}},\,\pi)\) — point risk, interval, calibration error, external-validation indicator, prevalence — and define a projection \(\mathcal{R}:\Delta(\mathcal{M})\to\mathcal{M}\) from a distribution over metrics to a single metric. Habit is the specific projection \(\mathcal{R}=\pi_{\mathrm{acc}}\). In crisis, decision latency \(\tau\) falls and the projection's sensitivity to its inputs collapses: \(\mathcal{R}\) becomes locally constant, so adding calibration and external-validation information changes nothing. This is not a hypothetical; the review documents the exact failure mode: "a model reporting a single accuracy value gives medical and coaching staﬀ no way to recognize when it is operating outside the conditions it was tested under" [PAGE 16]. The regression is therefore not a bias in the practitioner — it is a structural property of scalar reporting under latency constraints.

**Counter-trap.** The analyst who anticipates the regression tries to counter it by withholding the point estimate and forcing distributional reporting. But the counter-trap is that any scalar summary, including calibration error or a skill score, becomes the new \(\mathcal{R}\). Formally, if \(\mathcal{R}\) is idempotent and the identity on scalars, then the fixed points of \(\mathcal{R}\) are exactly the scalar reports; escaping the trap requires a set-valued or distribution-valued report, which cannot be a fixed point of an idempotent projection on a point space. The review's own remedies are consistent with this: calibration and probabilistic scoring rules are recommended specifically because they "improve transparency of model behavior beyond a single accuracy value" [PAGE 15]. The trap is escaped only if the report remains non-scalar at the moment of decision, which requires the decision interface — not just the model — to be redesigned.

**Clock.** The model has a validity horizon \(T^{*}\) in E14; the practitioner has a decision clock \(t_d\) measured in seasons or days. The misalignment \(\Delta=t_d-T^{*}\) is the governance quantity, and the veto condition is \(\Delta>0\): the decision is being made past the model's demonstrated support. Three concrete clock collisions appear in the corpus. First, injury models are used at return-to-play, which is weeks after the last training observation, so \(t_d\) exceeds the feature window. Second, match-outcome models are retrained annually, so \(T^{*}\) is implicitly assumed to equal one season without measurement. Third, selection and contract decisions occur across transfer windows, where the population changes discontinuously and \(T^{*}\) can be zero. The recursive structure is that the practitioner knows the analyst knows the practitioner regresses; the stable equilibrium is not better modeling but a shared clock — a pre-committed retraining and re-validation schedule that makes \(\Delta\le0\) by construction.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a rejection of a statistically valid model on grounds that a lower layer makes the association non-causal or non-realizable. Three layers are adjudicated here, each with a proof obligation.

**Physical layer — dimensional and sampling veto.** The model's inputs must be commensurable (E12) and sampled above the Nyquist rate for the phenomenon claimed. For a sprint-detection or acceleration-based load feature, \(f_s>2f_{\max}\); a 10 Hz GPS unit cannot resolve acceleration transients that occur at sub-100 ms scales without explicit smoothing assumptions, and the smoothing must be reported. Veto proof: exhibit two predictors whose units differ (metres of distance versus arbitrary-unit player load) entering a linear predictor without a stated conversion; the coefficient vector is then not identified up to scale, and the reported feature importance is arbitrary. The corpus supplies the premise directly, with "inconsistencies in GPS-derived load metrics" [PAGE 14].

**Biomechanical layer — sign and pathway veto.** For every predictor with an established mechanistic direction, the fitted partial derivative must carry the mechanistic sign (E11). The review's injury stratum "integrate[s] variables such as biomechanical load, heart rate, acceleration metrics, or match frequency" [PAGE 6], and the reference corpus includes explicit biomechanical targets, such as work that aims to "Accurately and eﬀectively predict the ACL force: utilizing biomechanical landing pattern before and after-fatigue" (Xu et al., 2023) [PAGE 19]. Veto proof: if the model assigns protective effect to a variable whose mechanism is injurious — for example, if increased peak tibiofemoral force in body weights reduces predicted risk — the model is fitting a confound (likely exposure or selection), not a pathway. The undecidable regime must be stated: where the mechanistic sign is contested in the literature, the veto cannot be adjudicated and the model should be reported as mechanistically unvalidated rather than as validated.

**Weather layer — confounding veto.** Heat stress is a plausible common cause of both load and injury, and wearable-based core-temperature estimation is an active measurement problem, as reflected in the review's use of Dolson et al. (2022) on wearable sensor technology to predict core body temperature [PAGE 14]. Veto proof via E10: if wet-bulb globe temperature (°C) predicts both the exposure (load) and the outcome (injury), and the model omits it, the estimated load effect is biased by \(\gamma_{\mathrm{WBGT}}\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{load}\sim\mathrm{WBGT}}\). The veto is discharged only by including the environmental term, stratifying by it, or demonstrating \(\delta\approx0\) within the training window. A second weather-layer obligation is temporal: heat acclimatization develops over roughly one to two weeks, so a model trained in a temperate month and deployed in a heat wave is outside its support even if all load variables are in range — an E14 violation expressed in the environmental covariate rather than in the outcome.

Adjudication order matters. Physical vetoes are decidable from the paper alone (units, sampling rate). Biomechanical vetoes require external mechanism knowledge and are therefore the most contestable. Weather vetoes require data the review does not extract, so they function as reporting obligations rather than as retrospective disqualifications. Stated in that order, the veto stack is falsifiable at each level and does not depend on any quantity the source does not contain.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
