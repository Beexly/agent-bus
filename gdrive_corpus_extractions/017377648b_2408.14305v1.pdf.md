They use SI time units, information units, probability units, and standard workload units. They are not source-reported measurements.

1. End-to-end tactical communication latency for method \(M\):
\[
L_{\text{e2e}}(M)=L_{\text{input}}+L_{\text{transmit}}+L_{\text{render}}+L_{\text{interpret}} \quad [\text{s}]
\]
Assumption: the coach creates or selects a tactical update, it reaches the player’s device, the player reads it, and the player converts it into an on-field intention. R1 asks for “quick and intuitive communication of strategies during dynamic practice sessions and critical game moments” [PAGE 3], and criticizes traditional methods as “too time-consuming” [PAGE 3]. The admissibility condition is \(L_{\text{e2e}}\le W_b\), where \(W_b\) is the available break or stoppage window in seconds. Breakdown regime: as \(W_b\to 0^+\), any nonzero \(L_{\text{e2e}}\) fails; at \(L_{\text{e2e}}/W_b\ge 1\) the method is outside the clock. Only precomputed, one-glance, or zero-input updates survive this limit.

2. Device disruption cost:
\[
D_{\text{device}}(M)=t_{\text{access/don}}+t_{\text{adjust}}+t_{\text{recover}} \quad [\text{s}]
\]
Assumption: access, adjustment, and recovery are sequential. R2 rejects “bulky wearable devices like Apple Vision Pro, Oculus Quest, or HoloLens during active gameplay or training” and notes “discomfort of wearing such devices, especially when sweating” [PAGE 3]. The condition is \(D_{\text{device}}\le W_b\) plus a comfort admissibility threshold. Breakdown regime: if \(D_{\text{device}}>W_b\), the device consumes the tactical window; if sweat or thermal load makes donning unacceptable, the method is vetoed regardless of visualization quality.

3. Cognitive load operator:
\[
\mathrm{CL}(M)\in[0,100] \quad [\text{NASA-TLX points}]
\]
or, in working-memory terms, \(K\) chunks required versus \(C_{\text{wm}}\) chunks available. R3 states that “complex diagrams can overwhelm players” [PAGE 3]. The condition is \(\mathrm{CL}\le \mathrm{CL}_{\max}\) and \(K\le C_{\text{wm}}\). Breakdown regime: \(K/C_{\text{wm}}\to 1^+\) produces overload; at \(K>C_{\text{wm}}\) the player cannot reliably decode the tactic under live pressure.

4. Visibility probability:
\[
p_{\text{vis},i}=\Pr(\text{player }i\text{ can read the display}) \quad [\text{dimensionless}]
\]
R4 criticizes solutions that “rely on a single display for multiple players” and asks for “simultaneous, clear visualization for all team members” [PAGE 3]. The condition is \(\min_i p_{\text{vis},i}\ge p_{\min}\). Breakdown regime: with one shared display and \(N\) players, occlusion and distance push \(p_{\text{vis},i}\to 0\) for far or obstructed players. Individual devices raise \(p_{\text{vis},i}\) conditional on device function, but do not by themselves guarantee shared interpretation.

5. Auditory clarity and text-conversion channel:
\[
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{dB}}=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{P_{\text{noise}}}\right) \quad [\text{dB}]

\[
C=B\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR}) \quad [\text{bits/s}]

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from physical tactic boards to digital tactical software to video analytics to XR. It begins with the board: “Tactic boards are the easiest and most common way coaches draw and communicate tactics during a game” [PAGE 1], but the board forces players to “translate the 2D tactics drawn on the board into a 3D spatial understanding on the field” [PAGE 1]. The next layer is digital: “Interactive whiteboards and software applications like Tactic Board[1] and TacticalPad[6]” allow real-time manipulation and data analysis [PAGE 2]. The third layer is video analytics, including Bozyer and the SoccerStories-style visual storytelling cited in the text [PAGE 2]. The paper then proposes mobile AR as the real-time, low-friction descendant of this lineage [PAGE 3].

Retrocausally, a future field deployment would rewrite which parts of this genealogy matter. If sweat, glare, rain, or device-access failure dominate, then the board’s simplicity is retroactively more valuable than the headset lineage, and the mobile-AR branch survives only as a ruggedized, pre-set, one-glance channel. If annotation time dominates, the coach-side tablet workflow becomes the true bottleneck, and the player-side visualization is secondary. If noise dominates, text conversion becomes the critical branch, and the auditory-clarity requirement R5 overrides visual novelty. The paper’s own conclusion that the system addresses “rapid tactical communication, minimizing disruption during games, reducing cognitive load, ensuring clear visualization for all players, and enhancing auditory clarity” [PAGE 4] is therefore a design aspiration, not a retroactively validated causal claim.

Multi-week traps are predictable. Week 1 novelty can inflate preference and compliance, especially after a survey that asks players to choose a future system [PAGE 3]. Weeks 2–3 can expose sweat and comfort failures, which the paper already flags for headsets [PAGE 3]. Weeks 3–4 can expose coach annotation burden: if the coach must draw complex tactics on a tablet during a short break, R1 collapses even if players like the phone view. Weeks 4–5 can expose device management, charging, network, and rain/glare failures. Week 5-plus can expose speech-to-text errors in crowd noise, directly threatening R5 [PAGE 3]. A longer deployment can also reveal a counter-trap: individualized player screens may improve local perspective while reducing shared common ground, so the team’s “shared understanding of strategic objectives” [PAGE 3] may not improve.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that the coach must model what each player understands, the player must model what the coach intends, and both must do this under a clock. Let \(d\) be recursive theory-of-mind depth: \(d=0\) is direct instruction, \(d=1\) is “I think you understand,” \(d=2\) is “I think you think I intend,” and so on. As the break window \(W_b\) shrinks, \(d\) collapses because recursive verification is costly. The paper’s R1 emphasizes “critical game moments” [PAGE 3], and R3 emphasizes “the heat of live gameplay” [PAGE 3], both of which compress the clock.

Crisis habit regression: when \(L_{\text{e2e}}>W_b\), coaches and players regress to the lowest-cost habitual channel, usually verbal instruction or a board. This is not irrational; it is a rational response to a violated latency constraint. The paper’s own criticism that traditional methods are “too time-consuming” [PAGE 3] cuts both ways: if mobile AR is not faster than the habit, the habit wins under crisis.

Counter-trap: a mobile AR system can reduce one cost while increasing another. Individual player devices satisfy R4’s concern about a “single display for multiple players” [PAGE 3], but they can fragment the coach’s ability to verify shared understanding. The coach may then spend extra metacognitive effort asking each player what they saw, which increases cognitive load and triggers regression. Similarly, converting speech to text [PAGE 3] can bypass noise, but if the text is long or ambiguous, it adds reading load under time pressure.

Clock: the binding variable is the break or stoppage duration \(W_b\). A design is admissible only if the coach’s input, transmission, player reading, and player interpretation fit inside \(W_b\). As \(W_b\to 0^+\), only pre-set, player-specific, one-glance displays survive. The paper’s proposal that “assistant coaches can pre-set their phones to display the corresponding player’s view” [PAGE 3] is a clock-aware countermeasure, because it moves cost off the critical break window. The paper does not test whether this preserves shared understanding; that remains a recursive ToM hypothesis.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto rule is:
\[
\text{Accept}\iff (V_{\text{phys}}=0)\land(V_{\text{bio}}=0)\land(V_{\text{weather}}=0)\land(V_{\text{acoustic}}=0)\land(L_{\text{e2e}}\le W_b)
\]
where each \(V\) is a Boolean veto in dimensionless form. This is an admissibility rule, not a measured model. It says that a high preference count cannot override a physical, biomechanical, weather, or acoustic failure.

Physical and biomechanical veto: the paper states the “impracticality of using bulky wearable devices like Apple Vision Pro, Oculus Quest, or HoloLens during active gameplay or training” and the “discomfort of wearing such devices, especially when sweating” [PAGE 3]. The proof is internal: if a proposed system requires a headset during active outdoor play, then it violates R2 as stated by the paper’s own participants. Therefore a headset-first design is inadmissible under the paper’s own premises, regardless of its visualization quality. Mobile phones and tablets avoid the donning/doffing problem, and the paper explicitly says they offer “a significant advantage over AR or VR headsets” [PAGE 3]. But phones still have a biomechanical access cost: pocket retrieval, handholding, sweat, and potential distraction during running. That cost must be measured against \(W_b\).

Weather veto: the paper is about “outdoor athletics” [PAGE 1], but it does not report rain, glare, heat, or sunlight testing. A mobile display can fail visibility in bright sun or rain, and a touchscreen can fail with wet fingers. Because the paper provides no weather-robustness evidence, the correct adjudication is “veto pending evidence,” not “proven safe.” The same applies to device thermal limits and battery drain.

Acoustic veto: R5 states that “noise from crowds and on-field activities can impede players’ ability to hear and understand the coach’s instructions” [PAGE 3]. The proposed text conversion [PAGE 3] is a valid countermeasure only if the speech-recognition channel is reliable. If crowd noise pushes \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{dB}}\) below the recognizer’s operating range, WER rises and the text channel fails. The paper does not report WER, noise levels, or comprehension under noise.

Thus the cross-layer proof is a consistency proof: the paper’s own R2 evidence vetoes headsets during active play, and its own R5 evidence makes acoustic robustness a necessary condition. Mobile AR is admissible only as a low-disruption, weather-tested, noise-tested, pre-set or one-glance channel. It is not admissible merely because 12 of 17 surveyed players preferred it [PAGE 3].
