Helmholtz argued that perception is an inference from ambiguous retinal signals to distal objects, using unconscious assumptions; James extends this to affective and social objects (enemies, ghosts, the lover’s bonnet) and to skilled reading, treating the prior not as a rare illusion-engine but as the default mode of perception. The direct intellectual descendants include (i) Helmholtzian unconscious inference in vision science; (ii) signal-detection theory (Green & Swets, 1966), which formalizes the criterion shifts James describes under fear and expectation; (iii) predictive coding and the Bayesian brain (Rao & Ballard; Friston), where prediction error is minimized by top-down predictions; (iv) cognitive penetrability debates (Pylyshyn), concerning whether beliefs and desires can alter perceptual content—James’s lover and the superstitious person are early case studies; and (iv) change blindness and inattentional blindness (Simons & Chabris), which show that observers miss obvious stimulus changes when attention and priors are elsewhere.

A “multi-week trap” emerges when this machinery is deployed in institutional settings over extended time. In proofreading, security screening, radiology, or quality inspection, repeated exposure to a stable context builds a strong prior that outputs are normal, threats are absent, or text is correct. Because \(p(\omega=\text{normal})\) is reinforced day after day, \(\alpha\) drifts upward and sensitivity to rare errors decays. Errors that would be obvious on day one become invisible by week three, not because acuity changed but because the prior \(p(\omega)\) has adapted to the base rate of normality. James’s observation that Latin, Greek, and Hebrew works were “more correctly printed” because “the proofs are better corrected” hints at the countermeasure: unfamiliar script lowers \(\alpha\), restoring bottom-up vigilance. Multi-week trap protocols therefore require periodic prior disruption—backward reading, font rotation, stimulus randomization, or insertion of known-error catch trials—to prevent the posterior from collapsing to expectation. Without such intervention, the system remains in a high-\(\alpha\) regime where the cost of a miss is paid only after the error has shipped, published, or been acted upon.

Crisis habit regression therefore operates fastest under short \(\Delta t\) and high affective prior, precisely the regime where the counter-trap is most needed and hardest to deploy.

This biomechanical bottleneck means the physical layer cannot deliver “every single letter” to cortex within the available fixation time; the system must supplement from the mental lexicon. James’s claim that “more than half of the words come out of their mind” is thus a behavioral readout of a biomechanical constraint, not a metaphysical claim.

Veto Scenario 1 — Fluency-dependent typo detection in proofreading.

Prediction: Typo miss rate in Condition A > 50% for words where James’s prior is strong; typo miss rate in Condition B < 20%. If the miss rate in A does not exceed that in B, James’s claim that practised readers generate words “from their mind” is falsified for this population.
