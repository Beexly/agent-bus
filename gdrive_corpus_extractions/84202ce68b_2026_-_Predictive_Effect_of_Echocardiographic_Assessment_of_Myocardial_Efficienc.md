<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central inference is a multi-hop chain: national-level martial arts training exposes athletes to repeated high-intensity intermittent loads; chronic exposure may produce cardiac remodeling; resting speckle-tracking and pressure-strain-loop echocardiography quantify deformation (GLS), constructive-to-total myocardial work efficiency (GWE), wasted work (GWW), and combined systolic-diastolic timing (MPI); these cardiac properties may co-vary with anaerobic power, agility, balance, reaction time, coordination, and competition index; therefore, echo indices may help stratify athletes. The first audit point is that the study does not test a non-athlete control comparison. It creates a “lower-performance internal reference group” and a “higher-performance group” from the top and bottom tertiles of a composite performance score. That design answers an internal-separation question, not a talent-identification question. The paper itself says: “The cross-sectional design limits inference to association and internal classification” [PAGE 583]. This directly conflicts with the Key points claim that GLS and GWE are “robust, non-invasive predictors of anaerobic power, agility, and technical performance in elite martial artists” [PAGE 585]. The stronger “predictor” language is not supported by a cross-sectional post hoc tertile analysis.

The second audit point is circularity. The composite performance score includes Wingate peak power, T-test agility time, and competition index. The paper admits: “These differences were expected because anaerobic power, agility time, and competition index contributed to the composite performance score used for tertile classification; therefore, the results describe the magnitude and statistical consistency of group separation rather than independent causal effects.” That means the primary group contrasts are partly definitional. The independent information is in the continuous regression models and the incremental AUC, not in the raw tertile differences. The regression models are more informative: GLS + GWE added ΔR² = 0.176 over covariates, and the logistic model AUC rose from 0.82 for covariates to 0.91 for GLS + GWE. But the model is still internal, with 120 athletes in the classification analysis and no external validation. The confidence intervals for AUC are wide enough that the incremental advantage needs replication before it can be treated as a selection rule.

The third audit point is measurement geometry. GWE is a ratio of constructive work to total work, derived from non-invasive pressure-strain loops calibrated by brachial blood pressure. It is not a direct metabolic efficiency measurement. The paper correctly says GWE and GWW were “derived from non-invasive pressure-strain loop modeling” and “were not treated as direct measurements of whole-body metabolic energy efficiency.” This matters because brachial cuff pressure, central-to-brachial pressure amplification, loading conditions, heart rate, and speckle-tracking quality all enter the calculation. GLS is signed: more negative values mean greater longitudinal shortening. The paper uses signed GLS in regression and magnitude in correlations. That sign handling is correct but easy to misread: a negative β for signed GLS can mean a favorable association if the outcome is coded higher-is-better. MPI is a timing ratio, MPI = (IVCT + IVRT)/ET; lower values mean shorter isovolumic intervals relative to ejection time, but ET is heart-rate and loading dependent. None of these indices measures combat skill, decision speed under feints, opponent modeling, or pacing. They are physiological correlates, not mind-reading instruments.

The fourth audit point is multiplicity and subgroup fragility. The paper reports many related echo and performance outcomes, applies Benjamini-Hochberg correction, and labels sex and discipline moderation as exploratory. The supplementary moderation table shows that the GWE × discipline interaction survived FDR (p = 0.012, q = 0.048), while the GLS × discipline interaction did not (p = 0.041, q = 0.082). That is not enough to build discipline-specific selection policy. The study also recruited through federation lists and national training centers, which may favor already-retained athletes. The 48-hour exercise abstinence and 24-hour caffeine/alcohol abstinence are good acute controls, but they do not control multi-week training load, weight cutting, sleep debt, infection, menstrual cycle, heat acclimatization, or seasonal blood-pressure shifts. The correct reading is therefore conservative: myocardial efficiency indices are adjunctive, internally associated markers in this cohort; they are not validated predictive markers for talent identification or training decisions.

The source itself states that it is a “machine conversion from the stored PDF via pdftotext + scripted reflow” and that “equations, tables and figures are NOT preserved.”

1. Composite performance z-score. For athlete \(i\) and performance domain \(k\),
\[
z_{ik}=\frac{X_{ik}-\bar{X}_{k}}{SD_{k}},
\]
where \(X_{ik}\) is the raw score, \(\bar{X}_{k}\) is the sample mean, and \(SD_{k}\) is the sample standard deviation. \(z_{ik}\) is dimensionless. T-test agility time was reverse-coded because shorter time is better. The paper states that the final composite was calculated after standardization, but the exact aggregation equation was not preserved in the conversion. The general form is
\[
S_i=\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k z_{ik},
\]
where \(w_k\) are aggregation weights. The source conversion does not preserve the \(w_k\); therefore no numerical weights should be invented. If equal weighting were used, \(w_k=1/K\), but that is an assumption, not a verbatim source fact. Breakdown regime: changing \(w_k\) changes tertile membership, which changes every group contrast and AUC.

2. Global longitudinal strain. The source defines GLS as “the average peak negative strain value from all 17 segments, expressed as a percentage, and calculated as the mean of three validated cardiac cycles per view” [PAGE 573]. Formally,
\[
\overline{\varepsilon}_{L}=\frac{1}{17}\sum_{s=1}^{17}\varepsilon_{L,s}\times 100\%,
\]
where \(\varepsilon_{L,s}\) is segmental Lagrangian longitudinal strain, dimensionless, and \(\overline{\varepsilon}_{L}\) is reported in percent. More negative values indicate greater longitudinal shortening. The paper also reports “GLS magnitude” as \(|\overline{\varepsilon}_{L}|\). Breakdown regime: if segmental tracking success is below 85%, or if more than 10% tracking deviation is not corrected, GLS is not valid under the stated protocol. Arrhythmia, poor acoustic windows, and foreshortened apical views also break the measurement model.

3. Myocardial work and GWE. Segmental myocardial work can be represented as a pressure-strain loop integral:
\[
W_s=\oint P(t)\,d\varepsilon_s(t),
\]
where \(P(t)\) is left ventricular pressure in mmHg and \(\varepsilon_s(t)\) is segmental strain, dimensionless. Thus \(W_s\) has units of mmHg, often reported as mmHg%. Global constructive work \(CW\), wasted work \(WW\), and total work are derived from segmental loops. The source states: “Global work efficiency was defined as the ratio of constructive work to the sum of constructive and wasted work, expressed as a percentage” [PAGE 573]. Therefore
\[
GWE=\frac{CW}{CW+WW}\times 100\%,
\]
dimensionless percent. GWW is reported in mmHg% in Table S1, so it should be treated as wasted work magnitude, not as a percent ratio unless the paper explicitly normalizes it. The source’s Table S1 labels GWW as “mmHg%” and reports \(13.2\pm4.8\) versus \(9.4\pm3.6\). Breakdown regimes: if \(CW+WW\to 0\), GWE is undefined; if brachial blood pressure is mis-cuffed or changes by more than the protocol’s repeatability criterion, the pressure calibration is biased; if loading conditions are not stable, GWE is not a clean efficiency estimate. The paper’s own protocol requires duplicate blood-pressure readings, a third reading if the difference exceeds 5 mmHg, and use of the mean of the two closest readings.

4. Myocardial Performance Index. The source states: “MPI was calculated using the formula: MPI = (IVCT + IVRT) ÷ ET” [PAGE 574]. Formally,
\[
MPI=\frac{IVCT+IVRT}{ET},
\]
dimensionless. IVCT is isovolumic contraction time, IVRT is isovolumic relaxation time, and ET is ejection time, all in seconds or milliseconds; the units cancel. Breakdown regime: as \(ET\to 0\), MPI diverges and becomes non-interpretable. Very high heart rate, poor Doppler alignment, lateral annular sampling error, and rhythm irregularity also break the timing model. Lower MPI indicates shorter combined isovolumic intervals relative to ejection time, but it is not a direct measure of agility, balance, or reaction time.

5. Regression and classification. Continuous outcomes were modeled as
\[
Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_{GLS}GLS_i+\beta_{GWE}GWE_i+\gamma^{\top}C_i+\epsilon_i,
\]
where \(C_i\) is the covariate vector: age, sex, discipline, training age, weekly training load, body fat percentage, and skeletal muscle mass. \(\beta_{GLS}\) has units of outcome per percent GLS; \(\beta_{GWE}\) has units of outcome per percent GWE; \(\Delta R^2\) is dimensionless. Logistic classification used
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(H_i=1)=\alpha+\beta^{\top}x_i,
\]
where \(H_i=1\) denotes higher-performance group membership. AUC is dimensionless and equals the probability that a randomly chosen higher-performance athlete receives a higher predicted score than a randomly chosen lower-performance athlete. The source reports base AUC 0.82, base + GLS 0.88, base + GWE 0.87, base + GLS + GWE 0.91, and full model 0.92 in Table S5. Breakdown regimes: post hoc tertile classification inflates apparent AUC; class imbalance, omitted external validation, and overfitting break the claim that the model is a predictive selection tool. Calibration slope should be near 1 and intercept near 0; the conversion does not preserve the calibration equation.

6. Reliability. The source reports inter-observer ICC for GLS 0.93, GWE 0.91, GWW 0.89, MPI 0.87; intra-observer ICC for GLS 0.96, GWE 0.94, GWW 0.92, MPI 0.90. ICC is dimensionless; CV is percent. These are acceptable research-grade reliability values, but they do not eliminate bias from BP calibration, loading, or group stratification. A reliable measurement can still be a biased or non-causal predictor.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from tissue-Doppler and speckle-tracking deformation imaging to pressure-strain-loop myocardial work, then from clinical cardiology to athlete monitoring. In clinical populations, GLS and MPI have been used to detect subclinical dysfunction in heart failure, myocardial infarction, amyloidosis, and post-COVID states; GWE and GWW have been used as pressure-adjusted work indices. The paper’s introduction explicitly notes that these indices “have demonstrated prognostic utility in disease contexts such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, and cardiac amyloidosis” but that their application to athletic performance “especially in anaerobic disciplines has not been systematically explored.” The retrocausal move is to take a clinical prognostic operator and reassign it to athletic talent stratification. That move is not automatically invalid, but it changes the outcome, the base rate, and the decision cost.

The athlete literature in the paper is mostly endurance or mixed-sport: Erevik et al. reported GWE correlated with VO₂max and treadmill duration; D’Andrea et al. reported preserved contractile reserve in power athletes; Kandels et al. reported GWI correlated with VO₂max in handball and football players while resting GLS changed little; Abibillaev et al. reported sport-related GLS differences with stable LVEF. None of these established a martial-arts-specific anaerobic or skill prediction model. The paper’s own literature review says “Combat-sport specific evidence remains absent in the existing literature.” The genealogy therefore has a gap: the clinical operator is borrowed before the combat-sport validation chain exists.

The multi-week trap is that cardiac remodeling is slow. Training age, weekly training load, and weekly sessions differ between the higher- and lower-performance groups: training age \(8.9\pm2.6\) vs \(6.8\pm2.3\) years, weekly sessions \(6.2\pm1.4\) vs \(4.8\pm1.2\), and weekly load \(11.4\pm3.6\) vs \(7.9\pm3.0\) hours. Those are not trivial covariates; they are potential causes of both cardiac adaptation and performance. A single resting echo after a 48-hour exercise abstinence cannot separate “efficient myocardium caused better performance” from “more training produced both a more efficient-looking myocardium and better performance.” The paper acknowledges this: the cross-sectional design “cannot determine whether GLS, GWE, GWW, or MPI preceded performance status, resulted from long-term training exposure, or changed in response to recent training load” [PAGE 583]. Other multi-week traps include weight cutting and dehydration, which alter plasma volume and blood pressure; sleep debt and infection, which alter resting heart rate and strain; menstrual-cycle phase, which can alter loading and autonomic tone; seasonal heat acclimatization, which alters plasma volume; and camp periodization, which changes training load before testing. The retrodictive illusion is strongest when a post hoc tertile is treated as a prospective class label.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression means that under fatigue, pain, or competitive stress, an athlete reverts to the most overlearned action pattern. A martial artist with better cardiac efficiency may recover faster between exchanges, but the echo index does not encode whether the athlete’s crisis habit is a reliable guard, a panic clinch, a lazy lead-leg check, or a predictable counter. The paper measures reaction time, BESS errors, FTNAT repetitions, and competition index, but those are not models of an opponent’s recursive beliefs. The “counter-trap” is the opponent or coach deliberately baiting the athlete’s crisis habit: feinting to draw the habitual reaction, forcing the athlete into a preferred clinch entry, or exploiting a fatigue-induced drop in stance. A resting GWE or GLS value cannot adjudicate whether the athlete recognizes the bait, delays the response, or changes level. The “clock” is the round timer and pacing structure: a fighter may look physiologically efficient at rest but mismanage the first 30 seconds, overcommit on a finish, or fail to recover between rounds. MPI is cardiac timing, not clock management.

The recursive layer is also institutional. Analysts may over-trust the AUC because 0.91 looks decisive; coaches may then select or cut athletes on an echo-derived score; athletes may internalize the label and either overtrain to “improve” a cardiac metric or hide symptoms to avoid being flagged. That is a Goodhart trap: once the marker becomes a selection target, behavior changes and the marker’s association with performance weakens. The paper’s own language should constrain this recursion: it says the findings “should be interpreted as cross-sectional association and internal classification, not as longitudinal prediction of competitive success or validation for athlete selection.” A theory-of-mind audit therefore treats echo indices as physiological context, not as mental-state evidence. The correct decision hierarchy is competition behavior and skill evidence first, medical/physiological screening second, and echo-derived stratification only as an adjunct that never overrides clinical or performance evidence. Counter-trap prediction: if an athlete has favorable GLS/GWE but poor opponent modeling, the echo model will overclassify; if an athlete has unfavorable echo but superior pacing and deception, the model will underclassify.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the study excluded diagnosed cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurological disorders; medications affecting cardiovascular output; and blood pressure \(\ge 140/90\) mmHg. Those exclusions mean the model is conditional on a medical clearance screen. Any athlete with syncope, exertional chest pain, unexplained dyspnea, arrhythmia, abnormal ECG, uncontrolled hypertension, or a family history of sudden cardiac death must be vetoed from “performance stratification” and referred clinically. An echo-derived GWE or GLS value cannot clear pathology; it is not a substitute for ECG, history, or stress testing. The paper’s use of resting echocardiography also means ischemia, exercise-induced arrhythmia, and dynamic obstruction are not captured.

Biomechanical veto: the T-test, BESS, FTNAT, and Wingate are all movement tasks. Ankle sprain, knee injury, hip restriction, lumbar pain, shoulder injury in grappling, or a recent musculoskeletal injury within three months can degrade agility, balance, coordination, and power independent of myocardial efficiency. The study excluded injury that could impair testing, but subclinical pain, guarding, tape, bracing, and weight-cutting fatigue can still veto the interpretation. A high GWE in an athlete with an unstable ankle does not predict T-test time; the biomechanical layer dominates. Likewise, technique proficiency, reach, weight class, and tactical style are not modeled by echo.

Weather/environment veto: the imaging room was controlled at 22–24°C and 40–60% relative humidity, but the performance tests were not necessarily in the same thermal state. Heat and humidity increase skin blood flow, shift plasma volume, alter heart rate and blood pressure, and can change GWE calibration if the athlete is dehydrated. Cold can slow reaction time and increase muscle stiffness. Altitude changes oxygen delivery. A 24-hour gap between echo and Wingate testing adds sleep, hydration, and recovery variance. If the athlete is heat-stressed, dehydrated, sleep-deprived, or training-loaded at performance testing, the echo–performance association is not identified as a myocardial efficiency effect. Cuff size, white-coat effect, caffeine/alcohol compliance, and the 48-hour exercise-abstinence rule are additional measurement vetoes. Statistical veto: post hoc tertiles, multiple related outcomes, and internal AUC estimation can produce optimistic classification. The paper used FDR, bootstrap, and sensitivity analyses, but it did not externally validate. If any of these layers is violated, the correct adjudication is “not admissible for selection or talent identification,” not “adjust the score.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Prospective external validation with pre-registered outcomes. Recruit a new national-level martial arts cohort, measure resting GLS, GWE, GWW, and MPI with the same blinded protocol, and predict a pre-registered 12-month competition index and Wingate/T-test outcomes. Verifiable prediction: the base covariate model plus GLS + GWE must improve AUC by at least 0.05 over the covariate-only model, with calibration slope between 0.8 and 1.2 and no loss of the GLS/GWE effect after FDR correction. Veto rule: if the incremental AUC is less than 0.03, the calibration slope is outside 0.8–1.2, or the GLS/GWE coefficients lose direction, the “predictive marker” claim is vetoed and the indices are reclassified as exploratory correlates.

Scenario 2: Blood-pressure calibration and measurement-perturbation stress test. Recompute GWE/GWW after a controlled ±10 mmHg brachial pressure perturbation or after comparing brachial versus central-pressure calibration. Verifiable prediction: GWE classification of higher versus lower performance must remain rank-stable, with Spearman \(\rho \ge 0.90\) and AUC drop \(<0.03\). Veto rule: if rank stability falls below 0.90 or the AUC drop exceeds 0.03, GWE is too calibration-sensitive to serve as a selection marker. This is a direct RLVR-style reward: the model either survives the perturbation or it does not.

Scenario 3: Stress/load and training-response counterfactual. Measure resting and post-fatigue GLS/GWE, or measure before and after an 8-week standardized training block, and test whether the change in myocardial efficiency tracks the change in Wingate peak power, T-test time, or competition index better than resting values. Verifiable prediction: stress or change in GWE should add at least \(\Delta R^2=0.05\) beyond the resting model; if resting GWE already explains the outcome, the stress model adds less. Veto rule: if stress/change GWE adds \(\Delta R^2<0.02\), the resting-only predictive claim is incomplete but not necessarily false; if training load improves GWE without improving performance, the causal direction is vetoed and GWE is reinterpreted as a training-load marker rather than a talent marker. A third possible veto is a causal-inversion cohort: athletes who reduce training load improve GWE but not competition output. That would prove the echo index is tracking recovery state, not competitive capacity.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
