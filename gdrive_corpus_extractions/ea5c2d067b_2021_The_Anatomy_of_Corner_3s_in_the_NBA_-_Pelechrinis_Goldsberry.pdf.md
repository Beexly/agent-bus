<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
All units below are stated explicitly. Probabilities are dimensionless; distances are in feet; expected points are in points per shot or points per possession; areas are in square feet; \(\alpha\) is a dimensionless double-team-impact parameter.

1. Expected points per shot. For a shot type \(z\) with make probability \(p_z\) and point value \(v_z\),
\[
E[S\mid z]=p_z\,v_z,
\]
where \(S\) is points scored on the shot. For C3s, \(p_{\rm C3}=0.388\), \(v_{\rm C3}=3\), so \(E[S\mid {\rm C3}]=1.164\) points per shot. For ATB3s, \(p_{\rm ATB3}=0.347\), so \(E[S\mid {\rm ATB3}]=1.041\) points per shot. The difference is \(0.123\) points per shot, or 12.3 points per 100 shots [PAGE 3; PAGE 1]. The paper also gives the 22-foot corner example as an expected value of 1.14 points and a 20-foot two-point jumper at 0.9 points [PAGE 1]. Assumptions: shot point value is fixed by court geometry; make probability is estimated from sample frequencies. Breakdown: if \(p_z\) is estimated with selection bias, or if fouls, offensive rebounds, turnovers, and shot-clock effects are included, the simple expected-point comparison is incomplete. No singularity exists unless \(v_z\) is undefined; the equation is bounded because \(p_z\in[0,1]\).

2. Distance-only logistic model. The paper says it builds “a logistic regression model for the probability of a shot being made based solely on the distance between the shooter and the basket” [PAGE 2-3]. Formally,
\[
\Pr(Y=1\mid d)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(\beta_0+\beta_1 d)]},
\]
where \(d\) is distance in feet, \(Y=1\) denotes a made shot, and \(\beta_0\) is dimensionless while \(\beta_1\) has units of \(\mathrm{ft}^{-1}\) so that \(\beta_1 d\) is dimensionless. The source’s implied result is a predicted C3-minus-ATB3 gap of about 1.8 percentage points using average distances of 25.1 feet for ATB3 and 23 feet for C3 [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: distance is the only covariate; the logit is linear in distance; the sample is representative. Breakdown: omitted-variable bias from contest, assist, shooter skill, and shot type; extrapolation outside the observed distance range; perfect separation if \(\beta_1\to\pm\infty\); no causal interpretation without randomization or a natural experiment.

3. Assist-rate comparison. The source reports “more than 90% of the C3 shots are assisted, while ATB3s are assisted at a lower rate of just above 70%” [PAGE 3]. Let
\[
A_g=\frac{1}{N_g}\sum_{i=1}^{N_g}\mathbf{1}\{\text{shot }i\text{ in group }g\text{ is assisted}\},
\]
with \(g\in\{\rm C3,ATB3\}\). Then \(A_{\rm C3}>0.90\) and \(A_{\rm ATB3}\approx0.70+\). Units: dimensionless proportion. Assumptions: assist coding is consistent across groups. Breakdown: assist rate is not the same as openness; a pass can be assisted but heavily contested, and an unassisted shot can be open. Therefore \(A_g\) is a mediator proxy, not a direct causal variable.

4. Radius of gyration. The paper defines
\[
r_C=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{x_i\in C}\left(x_i-x_C\right)^2},
\]
and describes it as “the standard deviation of distances between the cluster members and the centroid of the cluster” [PAGE 4]. In vector form, with Euclidean tracking coordinates,
\[
r_C=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{x_i\in C}\lVert x_i-x_C\rVert^2},\qquad
x_C=\frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{x_i\in C}x_i.
\]
Units: feet if \(x_i\) is in feet. Assumptions: Euclidean court coordinates; centroid is representative; clusters are spatially coherent. Breakdown: \(|C|\to0\) makes \(r_C\) undefined; multimodal or non-convex clusters can have low \(r_C\) while containing distinct sub-groups; outliers inflate \(r_C\); coordinate-system distortions or tracking smoothing alter the value. The paper uses \(r_C\) to argue that the “Stationed” clusters are “not only the most common ones, but they also have the smallest variability” [PAGE 4].

5. Defender payoff and double-team model. The defender’s strategy set is
\[
\Sigma_{\rm def}=\{1,2,3,\ldots,21\},
\]
where \(d\in\Sigma_{\rm def}\) is the distance in feet from the corner shooter [PAGE 4]. The offense has two strategies, drive or kick-out pass. The paper models a second defender at distance \(d\) from the corner, “and hence \(22-d\) from the player penetrating,” as reducing the drive’s expected points by
\[
F(d;\alpha)=1-\frac{1}{\alpha(22-d)},
\]
with \(\alpha\in[1,2]\) controlling double-team effectiveness [PAGE 4]. If \(D_0\) is the uncontested drive expected points, then
\[
D(d)=D_0\,F(d;\alpha)
=D_0\left[1-\frac{1}{\alpha(22-d)}\right].
\]
Units: \(d\) in feet; \(22-d\) in feet; \(\alpha\) dimensionless; \(F\) dimensionless; \(D(d)\) in points per possession or points per shot. Assumptions: linear court geometry; the relevant drive distance is \(22-d\) feet; \(d\) is quantized to one-foot bins; league-average finishing and shooting; no fouls, turnovers, offensive rebounds, or pass error in the base model. Singularity and breakdown regimes: \(d=22\) makes the denominator zero; \(\alpha=0\) is undefined; \(d\notin\{1,\ldots,21\}\) is outside the modeled strategy set; at the boundary \(d=21,\alpha=1\), \(F=0\), meaning the drive is fully neutralized in the model; if \(\alpha(22-d)<1\), \(F<0\), which is outside the intended parameter regime. The paper also notes a pass-completion adjustment, e.g. “if a player completes 80% of his passes to the corner, we can adjust the payoff \(\pi^{new}_{d,pass}=0.8\cdot\pi_{d,pass}\)” [PAGE 5]. More generally, \(\pi^{new}_{d,pass}=p_{\rm pass}\pi_{d,pass}\), where \(p_{\rm pass}\) is dimensionless.

6. Zero-sum Nash equilibrium. Let \(\Pi(d,q)\) be the offense’s expected points when the defender uses distance \(d\) and the offense uses action \(q\in\{\text{drive},\text{pass}\}\). Let \(p\) be a mixed strategy over \(\Sigma_{\rm def}\) and \(q\) a mixed strategy over the two offense actions. The value is
\[
v=\max_{p\in\Delta(\Sigma_{\rm def})}\min_{q\in\Delta(\{\text{drive},\text{pass}\})}p^\top \Pi q
=\min_{q}\max_{p}p^\top \Pi q.
\]
Units: points per possession or points per shot. Assumptions: zero-sum payoffs; both sides rational; the payoff matrix is correctly specified; one-foot quantization; only two offensive actions. Breakdown: if the matrix is misspecified, if there is no saddle point in pure strategies, if \(\alpha\) is uncertain, if defender reaction time or closeout biomechanics are binding, or if offensive actions are richer than drive-versus-pass, the equilibrium is not a sufficient normative guide. The paper reports that the equilibrium expected distance is “close to 13 feet” while the observed average is “12.3 feet,” but the observed distribution is “normal” rather than “bi-modal” [PAGE 5]. That distributional mismatch is a model-audit condition, not a validation.

7. Unit-disk influence model. The paper’s speculative cognitive model uses a defender influence disk of radius \(\rho\) [PAGE 6]. A weighted influence functional can be written as
\[
I(x_0)=\int_{\mathcal{C}\cap B(x_0,\rho)}w(x)\,dx,
\]
where \(\mathcal{C}\) is the court region, \(B(x_0,\rho)\) is the disk centered at defender position \(x_0\), and \(w(x)\) is the court-value weight. If \(w\) is measured in points per square foot, \(I\) has units of points; if \(w\) is dimensionless, \(I\) has units of square feet. The unweighted area heuristic is \(I_0=\pi\rho^2\) when the full disk lies inside the court. Assumptions: defender influence is isotropic and radius-limited; court value can be represented as a spatial weight. Breakdown: \(\rho=0\) gives zero influence; \(w\equiv\) constant reduces the model to raw area maximization, which the paper identifies as the fallacy; boundary clipping makes the raw disk area misleading; \(w(x)\) may be nonstationary and player-specific; the model is a mental representation hypothesis, not a measured performance law. The source states the correction directly: “the areas on the court are not all of the same value and the intersection between the court and the defender’s unit disk needs to be appropriately weighted by the court value” [PAGE 6].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has at least three levels. At level 0, the defender reacts to the drive: help at the rim or stay with the corner shooter. At level 1, the defender models the driver’s probability of finishing and the shooter’s catch-and-shoot threat. At level 2, the offense models the defender’s help habit: the drive is a bait to force a help decision, and the kick-out is the counter to that help. At level 3, the defense models the offense’s kick-out: it can counter-trap by staying home, by showing help and recovering, or by double-teaming the drive and trusting the rotation. The paper’s Nash equilibrium is the formal version of this recursion: the defender should commit to the corner or commit to the double-team, not linger [PAGE 4-5]. The empirical deviation—defenders lingering near 12.3 feet [PAGE 5]—is consistent with a crisis habit regression: under penetration stress, the defender falls back to ball-centric help and unweighted area coverage rather than the equilibrium commitment.

The counter-trap is the offense’s use of the drive as a threat to manufacture the corner pass. The paper’s generation evidence makes this concrete: the first two “Stationed” clusters include half of C3s, with the shooter “anchored in the corner” and the defender “lingering between the basket ... and the shooter” [PAGE 4]. If the defender overcommits to the corner, the offense can attack the rim; if the defender overcommits to the drive, the offense can kick out. The mixed equilibrium is precisely the indifference point. A defense can counter-trap by disguising its help: show a help step, then recover to the corner. But that reintroduces the “in-between” behavior the model criticizes, and it is vulnerable if the shooter is already anchored and the pass arrives on time.

The clock changes the recursion. The paper’s clustering window is the last four seconds before the shot [PAGE 4], and the “Stationed” cases involve a shooter anchored for at least four seconds [PAGE 2]. When the shot clock is short, the drive-and-kick sequence has less time to develop; the pass flight and catch-and-shoot preparation compress the corner payoff, shifting the equilibrium toward the drive or a contested shot. When the clock is long, the offense can run multiple drive-kick-re-drive cycles, stressing the defender’s recursive belief about help. The Ben Simmons quote captures the social-cognitive pressure: staying on the corner shooter “can look foolish for the unwashed” [PAGE 5]. That is a theory-of-mind problem, not just a spatial one: the defender knows that helping looks like effort, while staying home can look passive even when it is analytically correct. Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock therefore interact: stress favors the visible help habit; the offense exploits that habit with the kick-out; and late-clock compression reduces the offense’s ability to punish the help.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-geometry layer: the paper’s distance arithmetic survives physical audit. The NBA C3 is at least 22 feet from the basket, the average C3 in the data is 23 feet, and the average ATB3 is 25.1 feet [PAGE 3]. The FIBA geometry has a C3-versus-ATB3 distance differential only 28% of the NBA differential [PAGE 3]. These are court-geometry constraints, not modeling choices. The FIBA persistence of a 16-points-per-100-shots gap [PAGE 3] is a cross-geometry veto against treating distance as the sole cause.

Biomechanical layer: the paper does not measure lower-body alignment, closeout deceleration, hip orientation, pass velocity, or catch-and-shoot preparation time. Those variables could mediate the assist effect. A shooter anchored at the corner for at least four seconds [PAGE 2] has time to set his feet and balance, which plausibly raises make probability beyond the assist count alone. A defender at 6.5 feet for C3s versus a bit lower than 6 feet for ATB3s [PAGE 3] is a contest proxy, not a biomechanical measurement. Therefore the assist mechanism is not vetoed, but it is under-specified: an “assist-only” causal claim would be vetoed by the absence of closeout and balance controls. The correct reading is that assists are a proxy for ball movement, open catch-and-shoot conditions, and defensive rotation stress.

Weather layer: the dataset is indoor NBA and FIBA Champions League competition [PAGE 2]. There is no weather layer in the source, and no weather-based counterstory is supported. A weather veto would be a category error here. The only environmental factors are arena geometry, camera placement, and indoor lighting, not outdoor wind, precipitation, or temperature. The tracking system uses six cameras on the rafters at 25 Hz [PAGE 2], so occlusion and calibration are the relevant measurement layers, not weather.

Tracking and measurement layer: the 25 Hz sampling and one-foot strategy quantization [PAGE 2; PAGE 4] bound the precision of the 12.3-foot average and the 13-foot equilibrium expectation [PAGE 5]. A one-foot bin is coarse relative to closeout mechanics; a 12.3 versus 13.0 comparison should not be treated as exact agreement. The empirical distribution’s unimodality versus the equilibrium’s bimodality is the more robust distributional veto [PAGE 5]. Cross-layer adjudication: physical geometry supports a distance component; biomechanics and tracking support a mediated openness component but not a pure assist-only cause; weather has no veto; and the game-theoretic normative claim is not physically vetoed but is model-dependent. The paper’s own limitations—league-average shooting, no FIBA tracking, no pass-error base model, and a simplified two-action game [PAGE 4-5]—are the correct boundaries for any applied inference.
