The paper encodes this by combining target and receiver impulse responses into a probabilistic radiant power field and by modifying the volume-rendering weights to account for two-way transmittance [PAGE 5].

The third hop is beam divergence. The source says, “The size of the laser beams can become wider over distance, and typically not negligible in street scenes” [PAGE 3]. This invalidates an ideal zero-width ray model for two reasons. First, a diverging beam illuminates a footprint whose area grows with range, reducing irradiance. Second, different subrays within the same beam may hit different surfaces, especially at edges, producing multiple waveform peaks. The paper approximates the Gaussian beam with a finite set of subrays: “we ... approximate the Gaussian beam profile using M =37 rays” [PAGE 4]. This is not a cosmetic detail; it changes the range estimator from a single ray-surface intersection to a weighted beam-level measurement model.

The fourth hop is pulse formation and detection. The transmitted pulse is not a Dirac impulse. The received power is a convolution of transmitted pulse and system impulse response [PAGE 4]. The target response is modeled as Lambertian with a Dirac surface term, while the receiver response contains a two-way transmittance and inverse-square range term [PAGE 4]. Therefore, range estimation is not direct geometric intersection; it is a statistical/detection problem over a waveform. The paper acknowledges that the actual detector details are partly undisclosed: “this effect is hard to model in a fully physics-based way, because it depends on (usually undisclosed) details of the detection logic” [PAGE 5]. This is a gray zone: NFL is physically motivated, but it admits empirical learning where the sensor firmware is opaque.

The fifth hop is secondary returns and truncation. The source states, “we employ truncated volume rendering to estimate the radiant power beyond the first return” [PAGE 5]. Operationally, after estimating the first return, the transmittance is reset by zeroing densities up to a buffer distance, then rendering resumes. The supplementary sets “the buffer value ξ between two returns ... to 2 m” [PAGE 12]. If the buffer is mis-set, secondary returns are either merged with first returns or artificially created.

Notation and units used below: range ζ in meters [m]; time t in seconds [s]; speed of light c in meters per second [m s^{-1}]; density σ in inverse meters [m^{-1}]; transmittance T dimensionless; opacity α dimensionless; reflectance ρ dimensionless or radiometrically normalized; incidence angle θ in radians or degrees; beam divergence angle γ in radians; pulse width τ_H in seconds; received power P in watts or normalized power units; irradiance E in watts per square meter [W m^{-2}]; effective receiver area A_e in square meters [m^2]; loss terms scalar. Where the source uses an unspecified system constant C, C carries whatever units are required to make the equation dimensionally consistent.

1. Passive volume-rendering operators.

The source defines transmittance as the probability of traversing an interval without collision. Formally, for a ray r(o,d), with density σ_t along the ray:

\[
T_{\zeta_0 \to \zeta} \equiv \frac{T_{\zeta}}{T_{\zeta_0}} = \exp\left(-\int_{\zeta_0}^{\zeta} \sigma_t \, dt\right).
\]

This is source Eq. (1) [PAGE 3]. Units: σ_t [m^{-1}], ζ [m], exponent dimensionless, T dimensionless. Assumption: independent particle interaction and non-negative density. Breakdown regime: if σ_t is allowed to be negative, T can exceed one; if ∫σ_t dt becomes very large, numerical underflow can drive T to zero.

For a homogeneous segment [ζ_j, ζ_{j+1}] with constant radiance and density, the source gives:

\[
\mathrm{rad}(\zeta_j \to \zeta_{j+1}) = \mathrm{rad}_{\zeta_j} \int_{\zeta_j}^{\zeta_{j+1}} T_{\zeta_j \to \zeta} \sigma_{\zeta} \, d\zeta = \alpha_{\zeta_j} \mathrm{rad}_{\zeta_j},
\]

with opacity

\[
\alpha_{\zeta_j} = 1 - \exp\left(-\sigma_{\zeta_j}(\zeta_{j+1}-\zeta_j)\right).
\]

This is source Eq. (2) [PAGE 3]. Units: α dimensionless, radiance in radiometric units. Breakdown: segment length zero gives α=0; negative density gives negative opacity; extremely large optical depth gives α≈1 and numerical saturation.

Discretized passive volume rendering is:

\[
\mathrm{rad} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_j \mathrm{rad}_{\zeta_j},
\]

with

\[
w_j = \alpha_{\zeta_j} \prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(1-\alpha_{\zeta_k}).
\]

This is source Eq. (3)-(4) [PAGE 3]. The weights w_j are dimensionless and form a discrete probability-like decomposition if α ∈ [0,1]. Breakdown: if α leaves [0,1], weights can become negative or exceed one; if all α are zero, rendered radiance is zero regardless of radiance field.

2. Beam divergence operator.

The source models the beam footprint using a Gaussian profile in ray coordinates:

\[
E(\zeta,\gamma) = \frac{2I_0}{\sqrt{\pi}(\gamma_0 \zeta)^2} g(\gamma),
\]

where

\[
g(\gamma) = \exp\left(-2\frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma_0^2}\right).
\]

This is source Eq. (5) [PAGE 3]. Units: E [W m^{-2}] if I_0 is peak pulse power [W]; γ_0 is divergence half-angle [rad]; ζ [m]; g dimensionless. Assumptions: beam cross-section is approximately Gaussian; footprint area grows like (γ_0 ζ)^2. Singularity/breakdown: at ζ=0 or γ_0=0 the denominator vanishes; for γ outside the modeled beam support the Gaussian is only an approximation; for highly non-Gaussian laser profiles the operator is misspecified.

The source then approximates the beam with M=37 subrays: “we ... approximate the Gaussian beam profile using M =37 rays” [PAGE 4]. The total radiant power is a weighted sum over subrays. This introduces discretization error: too few subrays under-sample edges; too many increase compute and may amplify noise.

3. Pulse waveform and convolution operators.

The transmitted pulse is modeled as:

\[
P_e(t) \propto \left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^2 \exp\left(-\frac{t}{\tau}\right),
\]

with

\[
\tau = \frac{\tau_H}{1.75}.
\]

This is source Eq. (6) [PAGE 4]. Units: t and τ in seconds; P_e is proportional to power, often normalized in implementation. The pulse has finite width τ_H, so the return time is not a perfect step. Breakdown: if τ=0, the expression is singular; if t<0 is admitted, causality is violated; if the actual sensor pulse shape differs, range bias can remain even after correction.

The received power is modeled as a convolution:

\[
P(\zeta) = \int_0^{2\zeta/c} P_e(t) H\left(\zeta - \frac{ct}{2}\right) dt.
\]

This is source Eq. (7) [PAGE 4]. Units: with H as an impulse response over range, H may carry inverse-length distributional units; P_e(t) carries power units; the integral is over time. The source states that “The range-dependent received radiant power P(ζ) is the result of convolving the pulse power with the systems impulse response H(ζ)” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: if H contains a Dirac delta at an unmodeled surface range, the integral becomes a detection of that surface; if the pulse is truncated by thresholding, the linear convolution model is only an approximation to the actual detector.

4. Target and receiver impulse-response operators.

The source assumes a Lambertian target:

\[
H_T(\zeta) = \frac{\rho}{\pi}\cos(\theta)\delta(\zeta-\zeta_0).
\]

This is source Eq. (8) [PAGE 4]. Units: δ(ζ−ζ_0) is distributional with units [m^{-1}], ρ is dimensionless reflectance, cosθ dimensionless. Assumptions: Lambertian scattering, single surface at ζ_0, incidence angle θ defined relative to surface normal. Breakdown: specular reflection, retroreflection, translucent foliage, multi-layer vegetation, wet surfaces, or non-Lambertian BRDFs violate this operator. If θ ≥ π/2, cos(θ) becomes non-positive, which is physically invalid for a front-facing surface.

The receiver response is:

\[
H_C(\zeta) = T_{\zeta}^2 \frac{A_e}{\zeta^2}.
\]

This is source Eq. (9) [PAGE 4]. Units: T_ζ dimensionless, A_e [m^2], ζ^2 [m^2], so A_e/ζ^2 dimensionless. The square of transmittance accounts for the two-way trip. Breakdown: ζ=0 gives inverse-square singularity; strong atmospheric attenuation is not included unless T_ζ includes weather; receiver area assumptions may fail for non-ideal optics.

5. Active LiDAR radiant-power operator.

Combining target and receiver terms, the source defines a probabilistic radiant power field:

\[
P_{\zeta} = C \frac{T_{\zeta}^2 \cdot \sigma_{\zeta} \rho_{\zeta}}{\zeta^2} \cos(\theta).
\]

This is source Eq. (10) [PAGE 5]. Units: C absorbs dimensional normalization; σ_ζ [m^{-1}] or density-like extinction; ρ_ζ dimensionless; ζ^2 [m^2]. Assumptions: local density approximates probability of backscattering, reflectance is differentiable, incidence angle is known or regressed, and the target behaves approximately Lambertian. Breakdown: if σ_ζ is negative, P_ζ becomes negative; if ρ_ζ leaves [0,1], reflectance is unphysical; if θ is near or beyond grazing incidence, cos(θ) can vanish or become negative.

For a homogeneous segment, the integrated power is approximated as:

\[
P(\zeta_j \to \zeta_{j+1}) \approx \alpha_{\zeta_j} \rho'_{\zeta_j},
\]

where

\[
\alpha_{\zeta_j} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - e^{-2\sigma_{\zeta_j}\delta_j}\right),
\]

and

\[
\rho'_{\zeta_j} = C \rho_{\zeta_j} \frac{4\cos(\theta_j)}{(\zeta_j+\zeta_{j+1})^2}.
\]

These are source Eq. (11)-(12) [PAGE 5]. Here δ_j is the segment length. α is dimensionless; ρ' carries the radiometric scaling of returned power. The factor 1/2 arises because the source uses a two-way opacity-like term. Breakdown: α must lie in [0,1/2] for the subsequent LiDAR weights to remain nonnegative; if σ is negative, α becomes negative; if ζ_j+ζ_{j+1}=0, ρ' diverges.

The active LiDAR rendering equation is:

\[
P = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_j \rho'_{\zeta_j},
\]

with LiDAR-specific weights:

\[
w_j = 2\alpha_{\zeta_j} \prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(1-2\alpha_{\zeta_k}).
\]

These are source Eq. (13)-(14) [PAGE 5]. The weights are dimensionless. Assumption: 0 ≤ 2α ≤ 1. Breakdown: if α > 1/2, then 1−2α becomes negative and the weight product can change sign or become unstable. This is the central active-sensing replacement for passive NeRF weights.

6. Two-stage range operators.

The source estimates a coarse peak first. For N_c coarse samples, weights w_j^c are computed and the coarse peak index is chosen by maximum weight:

\[
p = \arg\max_j \{w_j^c\}_{j=1}^{N_c}.
\]

Then N_f fine samples are taken in a local interval around the coarse peak:

\[
\zeta_j \in [\zeta_p - \epsilon, \zeta_p + \epsilon].
\]

The refined range is a weighted average:

\[
\zeta_f = \sum_{j=1}^{N_f} w_j^f \zeta_j.
\]

The coarse/refined structure is described on [PAGE 5], and the sampling counts are given in the supplementary: “we sample N c = 768 points and in the second stage N f = 64 points” [PAGE 12]. The window size is “ϵ ... set to 0.8 m” [PAGE 12]. Units: ζ_f in meters; weights dimensionless and normalized. Breakdown: if the maximum coarse weight is low, the peak is unreliable. The supplementary says, “If the maximum weight at the first stage wc_p is below a predefined threshold η = 0.1, we assume that the network is uncertain” [PAGE 13]. In that regime, the method falls back to coarse rendering only.

7. Neural field parameter operators.

The neural field is:

\[
F:(x,d) \mapsto (\sigma,\rho,p_d).
\]

The source states, “We encode the scene as a neural field F : (x, d) 7→ (σ, ρ, pd)” [PAGE 4]. Input x ∈ R^3 [m], direction d dimensionless unit vector. Hash encoding maps x to features f_pos ∈ R^{32}; direction is projected to spherical harmonics f_dir ∈ R^{16}; density MLP also outputs geometry feature f_geo ∈ R^{15}. The source says, “we use a hash encoding [34] to map coordinates x to positional features fpos ∈R32 and project the view direction onto the first 16 coefficients of the spherical harmonics basis, fdir ∈R16” [PAGE 4]. These feature vectors are dimensionless learned representations. Breakdown: hash collisions can alias distinct geometry; spherical harmonic truncation can fail for highly specular view dependence; learned features can overfit static training trajectories.

8. Training loss operators.

The total objective is:

\[
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{range}} + \lambda_e \mathcal{L}_e + \lambda_d \mathcal{L}_d + \lambda_s \mathcal{L}_s.
\]

This is source Eq. (15) [PAGE 6]. The terms are scalar losses. Range loss has units of meters for refinement and dimensionless weight mismatch for coarse loss; intensity loss has units of intensity squared; classification losses are dimensionless. The source sets weights: “the weights in the loss function, i.e., λe, λd, and λs, are set to 50, 0.15, and 0.15, respectively” [PAGE 12].

Coarse range loss uses a Gaussian target weight:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{range}}^c = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|} \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \left(1 - \sum_{w_j \in \mathcal{X}_c^n} w_j \hat{w}_j + \sum_{w_k \in \mathcal{X}_c^e} w_k^2\right).
\]

This is source Eq. (16) [PAGE 6]. Here \hat{w}_j is obtained by integrating a Gaussian around ground-truth range. Units: dimensionless weighted loss. Breakdown: empty ray set makes 1/|R| undefined; if weights are not normalized, the loss can become inconsistent.

Refinement loss is L1 in range:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{range}}^f = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} |\hat{\zeta} - \zeta_f|.
\]

Units: meters. Reflectance/intensity loss is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_e = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} (\hat{e}-e)^2.
\]

Units: intensity squared. Ray-drop and two-return masks use classification losses:

\[
\mathcal{L}_* = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bce}}(p_*,\hat{p}_*) + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ls}}(p_*,\hat{p}_*)\right).
\]

This is source Eq. (17) [PAGE 6]. Probabilities p_* must satisfy 0 ≤ p_* ≤ 1. BCE is measured in nats if natural logarithm is used, or bits if base-2 logarithm is used; the Lovász surrogate is dimensionless and approximates intersection-over-union. Breakdown: sigmoid saturation causes vanishing gradients; severe class imbalance can dominate recall/precision tradeoffs; threshold choice changes ray-drop and second-return decisions.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the corpus is a convergence of three histories: neural radiance fields, LiDAR simulation, and active-sensing physics. NeRF is the direct ancestor. The source says, “Neural radiance fields (NeRFs) have led to unprecedented visual quality when synthesizing novel camera views” [PAGE 1]. It also notes that NeRF represents scenes as continuous density and radiance fields and renders images through volume rendering [PAGE 1]. The formal ancestor is therefore not just a neural network; it is a rendering integral. The paper explicitly builds on that integral but changes the measurement model because LiDAR is active: “In contrast to passive sensors like cameras that rely on ambient illumination, LiDAR actively illuminates the scene and measures the back-scattered radiance” [PAGE 5].

The second lineage is explicit LiDAR simulation. The source describes prior two-stage methods: “extract an explicit surface representation such as surfels or a triangular mesh ... Then, simulate LiDAR measurements ... by casting rays and intersecting them with the surface model” [PAGE 1]. It criticizes this because “the rendering assumes an idealised ray model and neglects the divergence of the LiDAR beams, which causes frequent second returns from distant surfaces” [PAGE 1]. This is the historical break: surfel/mesh reconstruct-then-simulate is fast and explicit but lacks differentiable sensor realism. LiDARsim is the main baseline, and NFL re-implements it for comparison [PAGE 6].

The third lineage is depth-supervised or LiDAR-supervised neural fields. The source mentions DS-NeRF and DenseDS-NeRF using sparse depth supervision, and Urban Radiance Field leveraging LiDAR for depth supervision [PAGE 2]. However, those methods still synthesize images or use LiDAR as a geometric prior. NFL differs because it renders LiDAR scans directly and models beam divergence, secondary returns, and ray drops. The source says, “Several NeRF extensions have utilized range measurements as additional supervision ... Yet, the output of those methods are synthetic images, not LiDAR scans” [PAGE 1]. This is the key genealogical distinction: NFL is not depth-supervised camera NeRF; it is a LiDAR-output neural field.

A fourth lineage is physical laser radar modeling. The source cites Gaussian beam decomposition and waveform digitization [PAGE 3], pulse waveform modeling [PAGE 4], and impulse-response convolution [PAGE 4]. This lineage supplies the non-ideal sensor physics: finite pulse width, beam footprint growth, target incidence angle, receiver area, two-way transmittance, and peak detection. NFL imports these operators into a differentiable scene representation. That is the central retro-causal move: instead of reconstructing geometry first and then simulating a sensor, NFL optimizes the scene representation directly through a learned approximation of the sensor measurement operator.

Multi-week traps emerge when researchers import the wrong historical operator. Trap one: treating LiDAR as a direct depth oracle. The source explicitly rejects this: “a laser scanner does not directly sense range, rather it measures the returned light energy per ray and determines the range based on the waveform” [PAGE 2]. Trap two: keeping camera volume-rendering weights. The source shows that passive weights are insufficient; active sensing requires two-way transmittance and modified opacity. The ablation says, “Our formulation improves range accuracy across all settings, without any hyper-parameter tuning” [PAGE 7]. Trap three: assuming a single ideal ray. The source says, “The size of the laser beams can become wider over distance, and typically not negligible in street scenes” [PAGE 3]. Trap four: ignoring secondary returns. The source models them explicitly and says NFL is “the first work to model the second return by combining beam divergence and truncated volume rendering” [PAGE 13]. Trap five: treating ray drops as random noise. The source says ray-drop behavior is hard to model fully physically because it depends on undisclosed detection logic [PAGE 5], so NFL learns a ray-drop probability. Trap six: assuming per-scene fidelity implies cross-scene generalization. The source admits, “requires per-scene optimization. Generalization across scenes and handling dynamic environments are key challenges” [PAGE 9]. These traps are historical because they correspond to older or adjacent assumptions that remain attractive under time pressure.

A final genealogical caution is weather. The source acknowledges adverse weather simulation in related work: “Weather conditions, such as fog or rain, can significantly impact the quality of LiDAR data” [PAGE 2]. NFL itself does not add weather attenuation to the core rendering operator. Therefore, its lineage is strongest for nominal sensor physics, not for fog, rain, snow, spray, or wet-road scattering unless those effects are learned from data or added externally.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory of mind for this corpus must model three agents: the physical LiDAR sensor, the NFL rendering system, and the downstream perception or planning system that consumes synthetic scans. In a crisis regime, such as a novel viewpoint, adverse geometry, or a safety-critical scenario, each agent has a habitual regression path. The physical sensor regresses to its firmware-level detection logic: thresholding, binning, pulse-shape correction, and undisclosed drop rules. The source captures this by saying ray-drop modeling is hard because it depends on “usually undisclosed details of the detection logic” [PAGE 5]. The NFL system regresses to per-scene optimization and learned masks. The downstream agent may regress to trusting synthetic geometry as if it were real, especially if the synthetic scans look realistic.

The crisis habit regression for NFL is especially important. When a system is uncertain, the source provides an explicit fallback: “If the maximum weight at the first stage wc_p is below a predefined threshold η = 0.1, we assume that the network is uncertain” [PAGE 13]. This is a rational counter-trap: do not refine a weak peak; fall back to coarse rendering. However, if the downstream agent is not told that the range is uncertain, it may treat the coarse estimate as high-confidence. The recursive failure is therefore not only geometric; it is informational. The simulator may know uncertainty, but the consumer may ignore it.

A counter-trap is to preserve the physical operators even when they are expensive. The source reports runtime: “Our central ray version takes 4.1 ms per frame to render the single returns on an RTX 3090 GPU, while other NeRF-style methods require 2.4 ms” [PAGE 13]. This is a clock constraint. Automotive LiDAR often operates near 10 Hz, and the source notes that all methods match or exceed normal LiDAR measurement frequency [PAGE 13]. The 4.1 ms central-ray rendering cost is compatible with real-time single-return synthesis on the reported hardware, but the diverged beam version adds cost. The recursive tradeoff is: physical realism versus clock budget. If the clock is too tight, the system may disable divergence, but that can degrade second-return realism. The source says diverged beam improves second-return estimation but “does slightly degrade the overall first-range accuracy likely due to imprecise two-return mask estimation” [PAGE 7]. Thus, disabling divergence is not automatically safe; it changes which error mode dominates.

The two-return mask is a recursive battleground. If the mask predicts two returns when there is only one, truncated rendering may hallucinate a second surface. If it misses a true two-return beam, secondary energy is lost. The source’s ablation shows that adding the range feature improves segmentation and second-range estimation, but performance is still not oracle-level: with ground-truth mask, first-range recall improves, indicating that mask quality is a bottleneck [PAGE 7]. A downstream planner that uses synthetic scans for vegetation, roadside objects, or pedestrian occlusion could be misled if the mask error correlates with safety-relevant geometry.

The clock also affects training. NFL is per-scene optimized. The source says it “requires per-scene optimization” [PAGE 9]. This means the simulator can be highly faithful for a fixed scene but slow to adapt to new scenes. If a crisis requires immediate synthesis for a new environment, the clock pressure may force reuse of an old scene model, creating a domain shift. The recursive counter-trap is to separate “scene-specific fidelity” from “cross-scene generalization,” and to use closed-loop protocols that test novel trajectories rather than only held-out frames. The source proposes Waymo NVS by shifting the sensor by [1.5, 1.5, 0.5] meters, with an overall displacement of about 2.18 meters [PAGE 12]. This is a counter-trap because it tests more difficult views than simple interpolation.

A deeper recursive risk is adversarial self-consistency. If downstream segmentation is trained on NFL-generated scans, it may learn the simulator’s systematic biases, such as range overestimation at high incidence angles or missing returns in certain regions. The source notes that the sensor tends to overestimate range in high-incidence-angle regimes [PAGE 4]. If NFL reproduces that bias, downstream models may internalize it. If NFL fails to reproduce it, downstream models may become brittle when transferred to real data. The correct recursive posture is therefore not “synthetic data is real enough” but “synthetic data must be audited for which sensor failure modes are preserved, which are smoothed, and which are newly introduced by the neural field.”

In crisis mode, the safest theory of mind is: treat NFL as a high-fidelity, physically motivated, but still approximate sensor simulator. Do not let the downstream agent assume zero uncertainty. Preserve masks, uncertainty thresholds, beam-divergence switches, and closed-loop evaluation. Respect the clock: 4.1 ms single-return rendering is useful, but full beam divergence and secondary-return modeling add cost and risk. The recursive counter-trap is to keep the physical model visible, auditable, and interruptible.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer adjudication asks whether a claim survives physical, biomechanical, and weather-layer checks. For NFL, the physical layer is explicit; the biomechanical layer is absent from the corpus; the weather layer is acknowledged but not fully formalized inside NFL.

Physical veto proof.  
Let a proposed NFL-derived claim be: “The synthesized LiDAR scan can replace a real scan for geometric validation without violating sensor physics.” For this claim to survive, the rendering must respect nonnegative density, nonnegative transmittance, nonnegative received power, finite pulse width, beam divergence, inverse-square receiver response, and two-way attenuation. The source provides these operators: transmittance is exponential attenuation [PAGE 3]; receiver response includes T_ζ^2 A_e/ζ^2 [PAGE 4]; active weights require a two-way opacity term [PAGE 5]. If a neural field predicts negative density or reflectance outside valid bounds, the claim is physically vetoed. If the model ignores beam divergence, it is vetoed for scenes where the source says divergence is “not negligible in street scenes” [PAGE 3]. If it ignores pulse width, it is vetoed for range-bias analysis because the source models the pulse with finite τ_H [PAGE 4]. The strongest physical veto is the active-weight constraint:

\[
w_j = 2\alpha_{\zeta_j} \prod_{k=1}^{j-1}(1-2\alpha_{\zeta_k}).
\]

If 2α leaves [0,1], the weight product is no longer a valid transmittance decomposition. Therefore, any claim of physical validity is vetoed unless the optimization constrains σ, ρ, θ, and segment lengths so that α remains valid.

Biomechanical veto proof.  
Let a proposed claim be: “NFL-validated LiDAR scans guarantee safe human driving behavior in the synthesized scene.” The corpus does not contain a biomechanical model of human perception, reaction time, visual acuity, cognitive load, or driver control. It only models LiDAR sensing and downstream machine perception experiments such as registration and semantic segmentation [PAGE 8]. Therefore, the corpus cannot entail a biomechanical safety guarantee. Formally, if a proposition requires a variable or operator absent from the source, the source does not support the proposition. The biomechanical veto is valid: NFL can support sensor-domain realism, but it cannot by itself certify human biomechanical response. The source’s own limitation statement reinforces the boundary: “handling dynamic environments are key challenges” [PAGE 9]. Dynamic human agents are part of that unresolved layer.

Weather veto proof.  
Let a proposed claim be: “NFL is robust to fog, rain, snow, or spray without additional modeling.” The source acknowledges adverse weather in related work: “Weather conditions, such as fog or rain, can significantly impact the quality of LiDAR data” [PAGE 2]. However, NFL’s core equations do not include a weather attenuation operator, scattering phase function, droplet distribution, or wet-surface BRDF. The transmittance T_ζ could in principle absorb weather, but the source does not specify how weather would be represented or learned. Therefore, a weather-robustness claim is vetoed unless external weather modeling or data is added. The veto is not that NFL fails in all weather; the veto is that the corpus does not prove weather robustness.

The adjudication result is: NFL survives the physical-layer audit if its learned fields are constrained to valid radiative ranges and if beam/pulse operators are retained. It does not survive a biomechanical-layer claim without additional human-perception evidence. It does not survive a weather-layer claim without explicit weather operators or validated adverse-weather data. This three-layer proof prevents overclaiming: NFL is a strong LiDAR novel-view synthesis model, but it is not a universal safety oracle.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
