The source defines a single technical trajectory: convert LiDAR acquisition from an explicit “reconstruct-then-simulate” pipeline into an implicit neural field optimized directly for novel LiDAR view synthesis. The first citation is explicit: “We present Neural Fields for LiDAR (NFL), a method to optimise a neural field scene representation from LiDAR measurements, with the goal of synthesizing realistic LiDAR scans from novel viewpoints.” [PAGE 1]. The reasoning chain is multi-hop. First, classical NeRF-style volume rendering represents a scene as density and radiance along rays [PAGE 2]. Second, LiDAR is not passive: “In contrast to passive sensors like cameras that rely on ambient illumination, LiDAR actively illuminates the scene and measures the back-scattered radiance. This two-way transmittance alters the volume rendering formulation.” [PAGE 5]. Third, the forward model must account for beam divergence, finite pulse width, peak detection, secondary returns, and ray drops [PAGE 3][PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. Fourth, the method turns these physical effects into differentiable operators: active volume rendering, truncated rendering for second returns, learned ray-drop probability, and classification of dual-return masks [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. Fifth, the evaluation is not only geometric but also closed-loop and perception-oriented: registration and segmentation are used to test whether synthesized scans are useful for downstream autonomous-driving perception [PAGE 6][PAGE 8][PAGE 9].

The gray-zone audit begins with the transition from passive to active rendering. In passive volume rendering, transmittance is a one-way survival probability, while in active LiDAR rendering the same transmittance appears squared because the emitted pulse and received echo both traverse attenuating medium [PAGE 3][PAGE 5]. The source derives an active radiant-power term proportional to two-way transmittance, density, reflectance, inverse squared range, and cosine incidence angle [PAGE 5]. This is not merely a cosmetic change: it changes the opacity definition and the discrete weights. In the source’s active weights, the effective segment opacity is multiplied by two, yielding weights of the form \(w_j = 2\alpha_j \prod_{k<j}(1-2\alpha_k)\), with \(\alpha_j = \tfrac{1}{2}(1-e^{-2\sigma_j\delta_j})\) [PAGE 5]. The factor of two is physically interpretable as two-way traversal, but it also creates a formal constraint: \(2\alpha_j\in[0,1]\), otherwise the weights cease to be probabilistic and can become negative or non-normalized. This is one of the most important breakdown regimes.

A second gray zone is range estimation. The method first samples coarse points along a ray, computes active weights, and selects the peak by \(p=\arg\max_j w_j^c\) [PAGE 5]. Then it samples locally around the peak and computes a normalized refined range. The source also describes a safeguard: if the maximum coarse weight is below \(\eta=0.1\), the network is considered uncertain and the refined stage is skipped [PAGE 13]. This reveals a hidden discontinuity: peak-based estimation is not smooth, and the method depends on the maximum weight being a reliable proxy for surface interaction. If the density field is under-constrained, the argmax can jump between surfaces, especially in sparse views or thin structures. The paper’s own qualitative figures show sensitivity around geometry, intensity, and dual-return masks [PAGE 7][PAGE 8].

A third gray zone is the dual-return mechanism. The source states that “we employ truncated volume rendering to estimate the radiant power beyond the first return” [PAGE 5]. Operationally, after estimating the first return \(\zeta_1\) and adding a sensor-specific buffer \(\xi\), the transmittance is reset by zeroing densities up to \(\zeta_1+\xi\). The implementation sets \(\xi=2\) m [PAGE 12]. This is a strong modeling choice: if the true second return lies closer than the buffer, it will be suppressed; if the buffer is too large, physically plausible near-second returns are discarded. The source acknowledges the dependence on the two-return mask: “However, diverged beam does slightly degrade the overall first-range accuracy likely due to imprecise two-return mask estimation.” [PAGE 7]. The oracle result with ground-truth mask improves both first- and second-return metrics, showing that the mask is a limiting factor rather than a cosmetic module [PAGE 7].

A fourth gray zone is ray dropping. The source explicitly says, “We empirically observe that the ray drop probability can be learned from LiDAR measurements.” [PAGE 5]. This is a pragmatic admission: ray drops are not fully derivable from first principles because the sensor’s detection logic is undisclosed. The method therefore augments the neural field with a learned drop probability. This creates a hybrid physical-statistical model. The physical part handles radiative transport; the learned part handles detector thresholding, proprietary logic, and unmodeled noise. The boundary between these parts is a source of domain gap: if training scans do not contain the same drop distribution as target viewpoints, ray-drop masks can become biased.

A fifth gray zone is evaluation. The source proposes a closed-loop protocol where the sensor is shifted by “[1.5, 1.5, 0.5] meters” to synthesize a changed trajectory, then re-optimizes on those novel views [PAGE 6]. The supplementary notes an overall displacement of approximately 2.18 m and says this corresponds to tasks such as lane changes or sensor-rig adaptation [PAGE 12]. This is important because ordinary interpolation benchmarks can hide geometric errors. A model can overfit nearby training rays and still fail under lateral translation. The paper’s closed-loop evaluation is designed to expose that failure mode. The residual risk is that even closed-loop synthesis remains per-scene: “our method is based on a NeRF-style representation and therefore requires per-scene optimization. Generalization across scenes and handling dynamic environments are key challenges” [PAGE 9].

The deepest audit is therefore not whether NFL “renders LiDAR,” but whether its physical operators remain valid under sparse supervision, approximate masks, learned ray drops, and imperfect sensor modeling. The method succeeds because it aligns the inductive bias with LiDAR physics: beam divergence, two-way attenuation, multiple returns, and detector dropout. It remains vulnerable wherever the physics is hidden, especially in proprietary ray-drop logic, dynamic scenes, adverse weather, and non-Lambertian materials.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The method’s genealogy is a chain of constraints inherited from earlier representations. NeRF established that continuous density and radiance fields can synthesize novel camera views through volume rendering [PAGE 2]. The source’s passive-sensor background directly inherits that formulation: density \(\sigma\) and transmittance \(T\) are interpreted probabilistically, and radiance is accumulated by discrete weights [PAGE 3]. This lineage matters because the original NeRF formulation assumes passive illumination and a camera-like measurement. The source explicitly contrasts this with LiDAR: “LiDAR actively illuminates the scene and measures the back-scattered radiance.” [PAGE 5]. The retro-causal constraint is therefore clear: any NFL model that ignores two-way attenuation is not merely approximate; it is using the wrong physical operator.

A second lineage comes from LiDAR simulation. The source discusses reconstruct-then-simulate methods, especially LiDARsim, which reconstructs explicit surfaces and then casts rays against them [PAGE 1][PAGE 6]. The paper criticizes this because explicit reconstruction “suffers from discretization artifacts and introduces noticeable errors” [PAGE 1]. It also notes that “the rendering assumes an idealised ray model and neglects the divergence of the LiDAR beams, which causes frequent second returns from distant surfaces.” [PAGE 1]. This creates a multi-week trap: a team may improve mesh reconstruction, surfel density, or ray-casting efficiency while still failing to reproduce the sensor’s waveform behavior. The NFL lineage responds by moving the sensing model into the optimization itself, rather than applying it after geometry reconstruction.

A third lineage comes from waveform LiDAR and time-of-flight modeling. The source cites full-waveform simulation and pulse-shape analysis, including the Gaussian beam approximation and peak detection [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. It states that real receivers use “radiant thresholding and binning to detect the return,” leading to discretization errors, range biases, and multiple returns [PAGE 3]. This lineage introduces a trap: even if a neural field can represent geometry, it must also reproduce detector-level artifacts. If the model outputs only a nearest surface, it will miss secondary returns from vegetation, edges, fences, or transparent/porous materials. The source highlights this with the statement that “Taking into account the beam divergence is important to reproduce two important phenomena: range biases and multiple returns” [PAGE 4].

A fourth lineage comes from depth-supervised NeRF methods. The source cites DS-NeRF and Urban Radiance Field as methods that use sparse depth or LiDAR supervision to regularize density [PAGE 2]. These methods improve geometry but still render camera-like outputs unless modified. NFL’s ablation replaces passive volume rendering in NeRF baselines with the active formulation and reports improved range accuracy “without any hyper-parameter tuning” [PAGE 8]. The historical lesson is that depth supervision alone is insufficient for LiDAR synthesis if the rendering operator remains passive.

A fifth lineage is implicit neural representation efficiency, especially hash encoding and fast MLP architectures. The source uses a hash encoding to map coordinates to positional features and spherical harmonics for view direction [PAGE 4]. This inherits the speed and expressivity of instant neural graphics primitives, but it also inherits sparse-view overfitting risk. The paper’s per-scene optimization limitation is therefore genealogical: the same representation that enables fast fitting also makes scene-level generalization difficult [PAGE 9].

The multi-week traps are recurrent. One trap is “clean simulation”: synthetic TownClean data with an idealized ray may look good numerically but hides divergence, dual returns, and detector dropouts [PAGE 6]. Another trap is “mask overconfidence”: a model may render plausible geometry but fail because the dual-return mask is wrong; the paper shows that a ground-truth mask significantly improves second-return range and prevents degradation of first-return accuracy [PAGE 7]. A third trap is “physics washing”: adding a physical equation without validating singularities, units, or sensor-specific constants. A fourth trap is “benchmark interpolation”: training and testing on nearby frames can conceal the need for lateral novel-view extrapolation, which is why the closed-loop Waymo NVS protocol is introduced [PAGE 6][PAGE 12]. A fifth trap is “ray-drop amnesia”: if ray drops are treated as random noise rather than learned detector behavior, downstream segmentation and registration degrade because vehicles, pedestrians, and vegetation often produce missing returns. The source’s downstream tables show that NFL improves vehicle segmentation recall and registration robustness on complex scenes [PAGE 8][PAGE 9].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem in this paper is not psychological but architectural: the model must represent the mental model of the sensor, the scene, and the downstream perception system. The LiDAR sensor has an implicit “belief” about surfaces: it reports a return when the waveform peak exceeds a threshold, drops a ray when the return is too weak, and reports a second return when enough energy returns from a farther surface within the beam footprint. NFL tries to encode that belief through differentiable operators. The source says, “NFL combines the rendering power of neural fields with a detailed, physically motivated model of the LiDAR sensing process” [PAGE 1]. The recursive layer is that the neural field must not only reconstruct geometry; it must predict what the sensor would infer from that geometry.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the model, faced with novel views or noisy data, falls back to habits learned from the training distribution. The dominant habit is nearest-surface regression: the model predicts the closest strong return and ignores weak secondary returns. Another habit is smoothness: the density field is regularized to be continuous, which helps rendering but can blur thin structures, vegetation edges, or glass-like surfaces. A third habit is ray-drop prior copying: if most training drops occur in certain classes or ranges, the model may reproduce that marginal distribution rather than the physical cause. A fourth habit is mask copying: the two-return mask may become correlated with scene texture rather than actual dual-return geometry. The paper’s own limitation admits that “using the density-based weights separately for each ray leads to improved performance,” even though real sensors perform detection on integrated beam radiant [PAGE 9]. This is a productive habit regression: it departs from strict instrument physics because it empirically improves synthesis.

The counter-trap is the set of mechanisms designed to prevent these regressions. Beam divergence counteracts the ideal-ray habit by forcing the model to account for footprint spreading and multiple hits [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. Truncated volume rendering counteracts the single-return habit by explicitly estimating power beyond the first return [PAGE 5]. The dual-return mask counteracts silent suppression of secondary returns, and the oracle study shows that better masks improve both first and second range estimates [PAGE 7][PAGE 9]. The learned ray-drop head counteracts naive thresholding by modeling detector behavior empirically [PAGE 5]. The closed-loop evaluation counteracts interpolation overfitting by shifting the sensor and re-optimizing on novel trajectories [PAGE 6].

The clock layer has three meanings. First, physical time: LiDAR range is determined by time of flight, \( \zeta = ct/2 \), and finite pulse duration \(\tau_H\) blurs the temporal waveform [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. If the pulse is long relative to surface separation, the clock of the sensor cannot resolve two returns. Second, optimization time: the Gaussian target standard deviation is annealed over iterations \(k/k_{\max}\), meaning supervision changes over training time [PAGE 13]. If the annealing clock is too fast, coarse supervision disappears before geometry stabilizes; if too slow, the model remains under-constrained. Third, scan-sequence time: the dataset uses 50 scans per scene, with every fifth frame held out in Waymo Interp., and closed-loop trajectories are generated by shifting the sensor [PAGE 6][PAGE 12]. The clock of evaluation determines whether the model is being tested on interpolation or extrapolation.

The recursive loop can be stated as follows: the neural field models the scene; the sensor model interprets the scene; the loss trains the neural field to match sensor observations; the evaluation asks whether downstream perception would trust the synthesized scan. If the loop is broken at any layer, the model can win one metric while losing the task. For example, a model may achieve low median range error but still produce dropped rays on vehicles, causing segmentation failure. The source reports that NFL achieves the highest vehicle-class recall in segmentation, a class “strongly affected by dual returns and ray drops” [PAGE 9]. This is evidence that the recursive loop is partially closed: better physical modeling improves not only geometric metrics but also perception-level usefulness.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A veto is a condition under which the model’s assumptions are physically, biomechanically, or environmentally invalid, causing the predicted LiDAR scan to become non-verifiable or unsafe for downstream use. The adjudication below separates physical vetoes, platform/biomechanical vetoes, and weather vetoes.

Physical veto 1: zero or near-zero range. The active power model contains inverse-square range terms,
\[
P_\zeta
=
C\frac{T_\zeta^2\sigma_\zeta\rho_\zeta}{\zeta^2}\cos(\theta),
\]
and the segment reflectance contains
\[
\rho'_{\zeta_j}
=
C\rho_{\zeta_j}\frac{4\cos(\theta_j)}{(\zeta_j+\zeta_{j+1})^2}
\]
[PAGE 5]. As \(\zeta\to 0\), these terms diverge; as \(\zeta\) becomes very small, the continuous approximation breaks because the sensor has a minimum measurable range, near-field blind zone, and receiver impulse response. Therefore, any rendered return at unrealistically small range without a sensor-specific blind-zone model is vetoed. The paper’s automotive setting reduces but does not eliminate this risk because close obstacles, bumper-level geometry, and self-returns can still occur.

Physical veto 2: low irradiance and ray drop. The received target response is proportional to \(\rho\cos\theta\), and the receiver response is proportional to \(T_\zeta^2 A_e/\zeta^2\) [PAGE 4]. If
\[
P_{\text{det}}
\propto
\rho\cos\theta\,
\frac{T_\zeta^2}{\zeta^2}
\]
falls below the detector threshold, no return is declared. The source notes that “beams that do not hit a surface do not return detectable signal” and that NFL learns ray-drop probability because the exact detection logic is not fully known [PAGE 3][PAGE 5]. Veto proof: if a synthesized scene contains a surface with nonzero density and reflectance but the learned ray-drop probability is near one without a physical justification, the output is not merely uncertain; it contradicts the radiative model. Conversely, if the model predicts a strong return for a low-reflectance, high-incidence, long-range surface, it must demonstrate that \(P_{\text{det}}\) exceeds threshold.

Physical veto 3: incidence-angle collapse. The Lambertian target response includes \(\cos\theta\) [PAGE 4]. For \(\theta\to \pi/2\), the target response tends to zero. In real scenes, high incidence angles occur on road surfaces at grazing angles, walls viewed obliquely, and sloped terrain. The source’s Figure 2 discussion notes that the LiDAR sensor tends to overestimate range in high incidence-angle regimes, especially at higher range [PAGE 4]. Veto proof: if the rendering model does not attenuate or bias returns according to \(\cos\theta\), it will overpredict energy from grazing surfaces. The model must either reproduce the low-return behavior or classify the ray as dropped.

Physical veto 4: dual-return buffer mismatch. The truncated-rendering operator resets transmittance after \(\zeta_1+\xi\), with \(\xi=2\) m in the implementation [PAGE 12]. Let the true first and second surface ranges be \(\zeta_1^\star\) and \(\zeta_2^\star\). A necessary condition for recovering the second return is
\[
\zeta_2^\star > \zeta_1^\star + \xi.
\]
If \(\zeta_2^\star - \zeta_1^\star \leq \xi\), truncated rendering cannot represent the second return. Veto proof: if the model predicts a second return with separation less than the buffer, it is inconsistent with its own truncation rule. If the true separation is larger but the dual-return mask predicts zero, the model also vetoes itself by suppressing a physically valid return.

Biomechanical/platform veto: ego-motion and scan-time distortion. LiDAR scans are not instantaneous snapshots; they are temporally extended measurements. If the platform moves or rotates during a scan, the static neural-field assumption is stressed. The source selects “mostly static” Waymo scenes for controlled evaluation [PAGE 12], and it identifies dynamic environments as a key future challenge [PAGE 9]. Formal veto: let \(T_{\text{scan}}\) be the scan period and \(v_{\text{ego}}\) the platform speed. If \(v_{\text{ego}}T_{\text{scan}}\) is comparable to the geometric tolerance of the task, then a single static radiance field cannot consistently explain all rays. For a 10 Hz sensor, \(T_{\text{scan}}=0.1\) s; at \(v_{\text{ego}}=10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), displacement during a scan is \(1\) m. This is large enough to matter for close-range registration or lane-level semantics. The paper’s registration evaluation uses approximately 1 m relative displacement between paired frames [PAGE 12], showing that centimeter-level geometric fidelity is sensitive to platform motion. Therefore, dynamic objects, moving pedestrians, other vehicles, and vibration-induced pose jitter are veto conditions for a purely static NFL field unless a dynamic or pose-aware extension is added.

Weather veto: fog, rain, snow, and spray. The source notes that “Weather conditions, such as fog or rain, can significantly impact the quality of LiDAR data” [PAGE 2]. The paper’s active model includes one-way transmittance \(T_\zeta\), squared for two-way travel [PAGE 4], but it does not fully model weather-dependent scattering. A formal weather veto can be stated using Beer–Lambert extinction. Let \(\alpha_{\text{ext}}(s)\) be the extinction coefficient along the path. Then
\[
T_\zeta
=
\exp\left(-\int_0^\zeta \alpha_{\text{ext}}(s)\,ds\right),
\]
and the two-way factor is \(T_\zeta^2\). If
\[
T_{\zeta_1^\star}^2
<
\frac{P_{\min}}{P_0},
\]
where \(P_{\min}\) is the detector threshold and \(P_0\) is the emitted power scale, the surface at \(\zeta_1^\star\) cannot produce a detectable return. Rain, snow, and fog also create distributed backscatter, which violates the assumption that returns come from discrete Lambertian surfaces. Snow particles can produce near-range spurious returns; fog can attenuate distant returns; spray can create transient low-confidence geometry. Therefore, a model trained only on clear-weather scans is vetoed when deployed in adverse weather unless it is retrained, adapted, or augmented with weather-consistent transmittance and scattering terms.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: a synthesized scan is admissible only if (i) range, power, and incidence angle are physically consistent; (ii) the dual-return mask and buffer do not contradict the truncation operator; (iii) the scene is static enough for the scan duration or explicitly dynamic; and (iv) weather-induced transmittance and scattering are either modeled or excluded from the evaluation claim. If any of these conditions fails, the output should be treated as non-verifiable for safety-critical perception.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario defines an intervention, a measurable prediction, and a veto condition based on the source’s reported operators and metrics.

Scenario 1: Ground-truth two-return mask oracle.
Intervention: Replace the predicted two-return mask with the ground-truth mask during rendering, while keeping all other components fixed. The source reports this as the “GT mask” condition in Table 1 [PAGE 7].
Prediction: If the two-return mask is the bottleneck, the GT mask should improve second-return segmentation and second-range accuracy, and it should also reduce the first-range degradation caused by imperfect mask prediction. The reported numbers are: with central-ray NFL, first-return MAE \(32.8\) cm, second-return MAE \(589.1\) cm; with diverged beam, first-return MAE \(36.1\) cm, second-return MAE \(505.1\) cm; with GT mask, first-return MAE \(29.7\) cm, second-return MAE \(116.0\) cm, and two-return segmentation recall/precision \(100.0/100.0\) [PAGE 7].
Veto condition: If replacing the predicted mask with the ground-truth mask does not improve second-return MAE or if it fails to improve the dual-return segmentation metrics, then the claim that mask prediction is a primary limiting factor is vetoed. The source’s own statement that “improving the mask prediction could lead to further improvements” is empirically supported by the oracle result [PAGE 9][PAGE 7].

Scenario 2: Beam-divergence ablation for dual-return rays.
Intervention: Compare a central-ray-only LiDAR field against a diverged-beam field with multiple subrays. The source implements the diverged beam with \(37\) subrays and divergence angle \(\gamma_0=2\) mrad for the TownReal simulation [PAGE 12]. In the Waymo experiments, the diverged-beam version should better capture multiple returns because different subrays can hit different surfaces.
Prediction: For rays with dual returns, the diverged beam should improve the error distribution of both first and second returns, as claimed by Figure 4 [PAGE 7]. Numerically, the second-return MAE should improve from \(589.1\) cm in the central-ray condition to \(505.1\) cm in the diverged-beam condition [PAGE 7]. However, the overall first-range accuracy may slightly worsen because the dual-return mask is imperfect. The source states: “However, diverged beam does slightly degrade the overall first-range accuracy likely due to imprecise two-return mask estimation.” [PAGE 7].
Veto condition: If the diverged beam fails to improve dual-return second-range MAE, or if it degrades overall first-range accuracy without a corresponding mask-estimation explanation, the physical claim that beam divergence is necessary for secondary returns is vetoed. The counterfactual is verifiable by comparing central-ray, diverged-beam, and GT-mask rows in the same evaluation table [PAGE 7].
