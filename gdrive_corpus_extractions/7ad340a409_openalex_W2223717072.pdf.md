The source is a master’s thesis examining neuropsychological performance in asymptomatic high school football players. The design is a two-group pretest-posttest comparison: 19 varsity football players and 9 varsity cross-country controls. The testing schedule in the methods is “testing on Mondays and Fridays after the second, sixth, and tenth football games” [PAGE 17], while the IRB protocol states “specifically on Monday’s and Friday’s after the first, fifth, and final game of the season” [PAGE 58], and the consent says “week 1, 5, and the final game of the season” [PAGE 64]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are measured in clinical, biomechanical, and probability units. Let \(G_i \in \{\mathrm{F},\mathrm{XC}\}\) denote football versus cross-country group membership; let \(T_j \in \{0,1,2,3\}\) denote baseline and three Monday in-season test occasions used in the repeated-measures model; let \(Y_{i,c,t}\) denote subject \(i\)’s score on composite \(c\) at time \(t\). The composites used in the model are verbal memory, visual memory, visual speed, and reaction time [PAGE 18]. Units are composite points for verbal memory, visual memory, and visual speed, and seconds for reaction time [PAGE 29]. Symptom burden \(S_{i,t}\) is measured in Post-Concussion Symptom Scale points [PAGE 21], and \(p\)-values are probabilities in \([0,1]\).

The RCI operator is a raw-change threshold operator, not a correlation ratio:
\[
\Delta_{i,c,t}=Y_{i,c,t}-Y_{i,c,0}.
\]
A deficit is flagged when \(\Delta_{i,c,t}\le -\delta_c\), where the 80% RCI thresholds are \(\delta_{\mathrm{VM}}=9\) points, \(\delta_{\mathrm{VisM}}=14\) points, \(\delta_{\mathrm{RT}}=0.06\) s, and \(\delta_{\mathrm{PCSS}}=10\) points; processing speed has a decline threshold of 3 points and an incline threshold of 7 points [PAGE 32]. If one writes the standardized RCI form, it is
\[
\mathrm{RCI}_{i,c,t}=\frac{Y_{i,c,t}-Y_{i,c,0}}{s_{\Delta,c}},
\quad
s_{\Delta,c}=\sqrt{2}\,s_{e,c},
\quad
s_{e,c}=s_{0,c}\sqrt{1-r_c},
\]
with \(s_{0,c}\) the baseline standard deviation in the same units as \(Y_{i,c,t}\), \(r_c\) the test-retest reliability, and \(s_{\Delta,c}\) the standard error of the difference in the same units. The source reports the raw Iverson thresholds rather than the full reliability inputs, so the standardized form is a reconstruction, not a reported statistic.

The repeated-measures multivariate model can be written as
\[
\mathbf{Y}_{ij}
=
\boldsymbol{\mu}
+
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}
+
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
+
(\boldsymbol{\alpha\beta})_{ij}
+
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{ij},
\]
where \(\mathbf{Y}_{ij}\) is the four-composite vector, \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\) is the group effect, \(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\) is the time effect, \((\boldsymbol{\alpha\beta})_{ij}\) is the Time x Group interaction, and \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{ij}\) is multivariate error with covariance \(\Sigma\). The reported three-way test is \(F_{12,312}=1.749\), \(p=0.129\), and the Time x Group test is \(F_{3,78}=4.463\), \(p=0.024\) [PAGE 20]. The post hoc verbal-memory comparison at time point 1 is \(p=0.020\), with football 89.00 and cross-country 80.89 [PAGE 20]. The independent-samples symptom comparison is
\[
t=\frac{\bar{S}_{\mathrm{F}}-\bar{S}_{\mathrm{XC}}}
{\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{F}}^2/n_{\mathrm{F}}+s_{\mathrm{XC}}^2/n_{\mathrm{XC}}}},
\]
reported as football \(1.39\pm 3.36\) versus cross-country \(2.56\pm 2.43\), \(p=0.384\) [PAGE 21]. The sample sizes are \(n_{\mathrm{F}}=19\), \(n_{\mathrm{XC}}=9\), total \(N=28\) [PAGE 10, PAGE 16].

The unobserved biomechanical dose operator is
\[
D_i=\sum_{k=1}^{N_i}\|\mathbf{a}_{ik}\|,
\]
where \(\mathbf{a}_{ik}\) is the translational acceleration vector in \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\) for impact \(k\), and a rotational analogue would use angular acceleration in \(\mathrm{rad/s^2}\). The source did not measure \(D_i\): “we were not able to quantify the number or magnitude of impacts that the football subjects encountered throughout the season” [PAGE 26]. Therefore any equation linking \(D_i\) to \(Y_{i,c,t}\) is a latent structural hypothesis, not an estimated source result.

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit. First, the causal estimand
\[
\tau(t)=E[Y_i(t;G=\mathrm{F})-Y_i(t;G=\mathrm{XC})]
\]
requires exchangeability \(Y_i(t;g)\perp G\mid X_i\). That condition fails structurally because football players were selected as “starters” by coach recommendation [PAGE 16] and because impact dose \(D_i\), effort \(E_i\), and body-size covariates are unmeasured or unbalanced. Second, small-sample MANOVA with \(n_{\mathrm{XC}}=9\) makes covariance estimation unstable; the Greenhouse-Geisser correction is reported because sphericity was not assumed [PAGE 18], but correction does not restore power. Third, multiplicity is a breakdown regime: four composites across repeated time points with \(\alpha=0.05\) a priori [PAGE 18] inflates family-wise error unless corrected. Fourth, the 80% RCI interval produces false-positive risk “as high as 10%-30%” [PAGE 22], so individual-level flags are not diagnostic by themselves. Fifth, unit and construct inconsistencies—abstract height/mass “69.9 ± 2.9 cm, 171.6 ± 28.3 kg” [PAGE 10] versus Table 1 values [PAGE 28], and “processing speed” [PAGE 17] versus “visual speed” [PAGE 18] versus “Processing Speed” [PAGE 32]—are reproducibility breakdowns. Sixth, the testing-schedule inconsistency between “second, sixth, and tenth football games” [PAGE 17] and “first, fifth, and final game” [PAGE 58] changes the recovery-window interpretation and is a protocol-operator breakdown.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from TBI epidemiology through high school football incidence, subconcussive impact telemetry, and computerized neurocognitive change detection. The thesis cites the high-school football burden and the underreporting problem: “over 50% of these football players do not inform appropriate medical personnel of SRC symptoms” [PAGE 14]. It inherits the HITS/impact-telemetry tradition and the Talavage/Bailes/Breedlove line that cumulative SCHI may produce deficits without overt symptoms [PAGE 13, PAGE 40]. It also inherits the Iverson RCI tradition for interpreting ImPACT change [PAGE 32]. The retrocausal trap is that later “asymptomatic concussed” language is projected backward onto data that did not measure the biomechanical exposure that supposedly caused the state.

The multi-week trap is the football week itself. The thesis states: “The interscholastic football season is unique in that there is one game per week with a routine practice schedule in between that includes a recovery day immediately following a game with a graduated practice schedule with regard to player–to-player contact” [PAGE 55]. It also warns: “Compounding the situation in football players is the fact that many times they participate in weekly full-contact practices in preparation for the next game incurring additional opportunities for SCHI; which could in turn delay recovery and worsen the signs/symptoms typically associated with SRC” [PAGE 14]. Thus Monday and Friday are not clean pre/post operators: Monday follows a game plus possible post-game processes; Friday precedes the next game but follows a week of practice exposure. The thesis reports that at “time point 3 (Monday ImPACT test) we found that three of our football subjects had ‘atypical’ NP scores, and two of these subjects had not returned to baseline levels by the end of the week, prior to their next competitive game” [PAGE 24], and at “time point 5 (Monday ImPACT test) we found eight of our football subjects had ‘atypical’ NP scores, and one of these subjects had not returned to baseline levels by the end of the week, prior to their next competitive game” [PAGE 24]. These are the cases that drive the clinical concern: “These particular subjects are of concern because they have diminished NP performance just prior to a competitive game where they are likely to encounter more SCHI” [PAGE 24].

The historical trap is category inheritance. The thesis compares its 53% two-or-more-deficit rate with Mulligan’s collegiate finding and with Talavage’s asymptomatic group [PAGE 24]. But the source’s own controls also show deficits: 56% with one or more deficits [PAGE 22] and 22% with two or more [PAGE 23]. That means the baseline rate of RCI flags in non-collision athletes is high enough that the football excess cannot be treated as a specific SCHI signature without an exposure model. The retest genealogy also matters: baseline was preseason, and repeated ImPACT administrations can produce practice, fatigue, effort, and regression-to-the-mean effects. The thesis acknowledges the effort assumption [PAGE 26] and the single-instrument limitation [PAGE 26]. Therefore the correct retrocausal reading is not “SCHI caused these flags”; it is “a subset of football players showed RCI flags, and a smaller subset failed to return to baseline by Friday, but the cause is not identified by this design.”
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive social model has four agents: the athlete, the clinician, the coach, and the parent or administrator. The athlete’s crisis habit regression is underreporting. The thesis reports: “McCrea et al. found that athletes most commonly did not report their symptoms because they felt their injury was not severe enough to warrant medical attention or that they did not want to leave the game” [PAGE 14]. The athlete’s belief is: “If I report, I lose playing time; if I do not report, I stay on the field.” That belief is reinforced by the sport’s selection logic: football players were chosen as starters [PAGE 16], and return-to-play pressures “come from a variety of sources including coaches, student-athletes, parents, and administrators” [PAGE 53]. The recursive loop is: hidden symptoms lead to continued participation; continued participation leads to more SCHI; more SCHI may produce NP flags; NP flags are invisible unless tested; the clinician sees no symptoms and allows play; the athlete learns that hiding works.

The counter-trap is clinician overreliance on symptom self-report. The thesis found that “more than half of them did not report a single symptom throughout the study” among football players, while “Eight of our nine control subjects reported symptoms at least one time” [PAGE 26]. The group symptom means were football \(1.39\pm 3.36\) versus cross-country \(2.56\pm 2.43\), \(p=0.384\) [PAGE 21]. A clinician who uses symptom scores as the gate would conclude the football group is safer, when the thesis’s own interpretation is the opposite: football players may “discount or hide concussion-related symptoms” [PAGE 26]. The counter-trap is therefore a false-safety signal: low symptom reporting is treated as evidence of low injury, but it may be evidence of high suppression.

The clock trap is the weekly game cycle. The athlete’s decision horizon is the next game, not the next month. The clinician’s decision horizon is the Monday or Friday test, not the cumulative season. The thesis’s own concern is exactly this mismatch: athletes with “diminished NP performance just prior to a competitive game where they are likely to encounter more SCHI” [PAGE 24]. The recursive theory-of-mind prediction is: if the athlete believes the clinician will only act on visible symptoms, the athlete will minimize symptoms; if the clinician believes the athlete is truthful, the clinician will under-test; if the coach believes the athlete is available, the coach will play the athlete; and the loop closes. The counter-counter-trap is objective testing with validity checks, multiple instruments, and return-to-play rules that do not depend on athlete self-report alone. The thesis points toward this by recommending BESS and King-Devick tools [PAGE 26-27], but it did not integrate them into the current design.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is impact dosimetry. A causal claim from football to SCHI to NP deficit requires measured impact frequency and magnitude. The thesis states: “we were not able to quantify the number or magnitude of impacts that the football subjects encountered throughout the season” [PAGE 26]. Therefore the physical layer vetoes strong causal attribution. The biomechanical layer also vetoes simple group comparison because body size differs: football 180.9 ± 7.6 cm and 91.7 ± 18.9 kg versus cross-country 174.4 ± 7.2 cm and 64.3 ± 6.9 kg [PAGE 28]. The thesis discusses “underdeveloped neck musculature in the youth population does not cushion head impacts well enough resulting in the brain taking more of an impact” [PAGE 45]. Mass, height, neck strength, position, and playing time are plausible modifiers of both impact dynamics and sport selection, and they were not adjusted in the reported MANOVA. The weather/environment layer is not directly measured. The testing environment is controlled: “All NP testing was performed in quiet, climate-controlled computer laboratory” [PAGE 10]. That controls measurement conditions, not outdoor practice or game weather, hydration, sleep, or academic load. No weather operator is reported, so weather cannot be ruled in or out as a modifier.

The cross-layer proof structure is as follows. Let
\[
Y_i(t)=f(G_i,D_i,X_i,E_i,W_i,\epsilon_i),
\]
where \(G_i\) is sport group, \(D_i\) is unobserved impact dose, \(X_i\) is body-size and position covariate vector, \(E_i\) is effort/validity, and \(W_i\) is weather/environmental load. The source observes \(G_i\), \(Y_i(t)\), and some \(X_i\), but it does not observe \(D_i\), does not model \(E_i\), and does not report \(W_i\). Therefore the conditional independence needed for a SCHI effect,
\[
Y_i(t;d)\perp D_i\mid G_i,X_i,E_i,W_i,
\]
is not established. The physical/biomechanical/weather veto is not a claim that SCHI is harmless; it is a claim that this dataset cannot adjudicate the causal pathway. The thesis itself supplies the honest veto: “Finally, we only utilized one concussion measurement tool to assess our subjects” [PAGE 26]. A single instrument with an 80% RCI and no impact telemetry is insufficient to prove the “asymptomatic concussed” mechanism, even if the clinical concern is reasonable.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
