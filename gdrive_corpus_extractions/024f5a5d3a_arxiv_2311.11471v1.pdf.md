\textbf{Detection and state operator.} YOLOv7 is used as “a pre-trained object detection model YOLOv7” and “performed with a mAP of 0.9 for detecting the boxers on a confidence score of 0.5” [PAGE 2]. A formal state is

\hat{\mathcal B}(t)=\{(\hat b_i(t),s_i(t))\}_{i=1}^{M(t)},\qquad
s_i(t)\in[0,1],
\]
where \(\hat b_i(t)\) is a bounding box in image coordinates and \(s_i(t)\) is a probability-like confidence. The ring-masked count is
\[
\hat N_{\mathrm{ring}}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{M(t)}\mathbf{1}\left[s_i(t)\ge 0.5\right]\mathbf{1}\left[\mathrm{centroid}(\hat b_i(t))\in \mathcal R\right],
\]
with \(\mathcal R\) the ring mask. Units: \(\hat b_i\) and centroids are in pixels, \(s_i\) is dimensionless probability, mAP is dimensionless, IDU and IDS are integer event counts, and temporal constants are in seconds or frames. The paper does not state a homography to metres, so no SI length unit is justified for the 40 threshold.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system has no explicit theory of mind, but its design embeds a recursive model of athlete and operator behavior. The first layer is crisis habit regression: under fatigue or clinching, boxers regress to close-range, low-separation, high-occlusion behavior. The descriptor tracker fails exactly there: “merging complexity when the boxer comes too close (clinching)” [PAGE 3]. The pose tracker is the counter-trap: instead of trusting appearance or a single centroid, it correlates shoulder and hip landmarks across mini-bouts, so the model’s “belief” about identity is updated from body configuration rather than attire. That is a recursive loop—tracked landmarks at the end of one mini-bout become the prior for the next mini-bout, and a mistaken match propagates forward. The paper’s “mini-bout integration module” [PAGE 2] is therefore a belief-propagation step, not a one-shot classifier.

The second layer is the counter-trap against the clock. The system knows “a bout takes for a period of 120s followed by a resting period of 60s” [PAGE 2], and it fuses that prior with visual cues by majority rule. If the clock prior is too strong, the system can segment by schedule rather than by observed behavior; if it is too weak, ringside noise dominates. The reported failure modes are precisely counter-traps: “boxers of the next bout enters the present bout within the time constraint” and “people moving along the side of the ring hangs onto the ropes resulting on line crossing” [PAGE 3]. In theory-of-mind terms, the system assumes that only the two boxers are relevant agents, but the scene contains referees, coaches, and ringside personnel. The person-count equation’s \(DO=3\) branch may even be encoding a referee, yet the re-identification target is the boxer. That is a recursive modeling error: the count cue and the identity cue disagree about who the agents are.

The third layer is the operator’s recursive expectation. The analyst wants continuous IDs because the ten-point-must traits are temporal; the system reports IDU and IDS as if identity is a persistent latent variable. But the paper’s own ground truth is “IDU=0, and IDS=0” [PAGE 3], and the pose branch reaches that ideal. The recursive danger is confirmation: once an ID is assigned, positional and pose costs bias future associations toward that ID. A single early switch can become self-reinforcing. The clock adds another recursion: track age 10,000 frames at 70 FPS is about 142.9 s, longer than a 120 s bout but shorter than a 180 s bout-plus-rest cycle. If tracks are not explicitly reset at bout boundaries, the system’s memory can leak across the rest interval; if they are reset, the “continuous” claim is only within-bout. The paper does not resolve this temporal recursion, so the correct interpretation is: pose-based mini-bout stitching is a strong counter-trap to clinching, but the clock and track-age constants remain unvalidated under schedule drift.

\textbf{Physical-layer veto.} The paper’s physical evidence is a single fixed top-view RGB camera at 70 FPS [PAGE 1]. There is no reported homography, lens calibration, depth, or metric scale. Therefore any claim about physical distance in metres, force, or energy is vetoed. The 40 threshold is a pixel-domain number; “the threshold of 40 was determined empirically across different bouts” [PAGE 2] does not make it physically invariant. The top view also causes “loss of anthropometric features” and “boxers in a bout wearing the same attire” [PAGE 1]. The ring-crossing cue depends on “four virtual lines” placed on the ropes [PAGE 2], so rope movement, camera vibration, or a changed ring setup invalidates the line geometry. Veto: no metric-space or cross-arena claim is supported without recalibration.

\textbf{Biomechanical-layer veto.} The paper directly documents the clinch failure: descriptor tracking suffers “merging complexity when the boxer comes too close (clinching)” [PAGE 3]. The pose branch uses only “two shoulder, two hip” landmarks [PAGE 2], so it cannot resolve identity when those four points are occluded, missing, or symmetrically ambiguous. The conclusion admits “line-of-sight calculation degrades when the pose estimation network fails to detect the landmarks” [PAGE 3]. Veto: a claim of universal ID continuity under prolonged clinch, grappling, or limb occlusion is not supported. The IDU=0 and IDS=0 results are conditional on the custom data and on pose estimation success.

\textbf{Weather and environment veto.} The source is an indoor boxing setup and reports no weather variables, no lighting measurements, and no environmental sensors. Weather is therefore not adjudicable from the paper. If the method were moved outdoors or under changing illumination, the appearance cost matrix and pose estimator would be exposed to photometric shift, but the paper provides no evidence either way. Veto: any weather-robustness claim is outside the source. The only defensible environmental statement is that ringside humans and rope contact are documented false-trigger sources: “presence of more humans on the ring side” [PAGE 3] and “people moving along the side of the ring hangs onto the ropes resulting on line crossing” [PAGE 3]. Those are physical-scene vetoes, not weather vetoes.

\textbf{Veto 1: pixel-scale / field-of-view rescale.} Counterfactual: keep the same physical ring and camera position but double the image resolution or change the field of view, and do not rescale the 40-pixel proximity threshold. The source’s cue is \(C_P=\mathbf{1}[ED\le 40]\) with \(ED\) in centroid pixels [PAGE 2]. Prediction: the effective physical proximity radius halves or doubles, so \(C_P\) will fire too rarely or too often; the majority rule will lose or gain a cue; transition accuracy will fall below the reported “90%” [PAGE 1] and over-segmentation will increase. Verification: rerun with a calibrated threshold \(40\cdot s\), where \(s\) is the pixel scale relative to the original setup; if accuracy returns to about 90% and over-segmentation falls, the universal-40 claim is vetoed and the scale-dependent claim is confirmed. This is a verifiable reward: count segmented bouts against ground-truth bouts across the 189-bout corpus [PAGE 1].

\textbf{Veto 2: frame-rate retiming.} Counterfactual: change capture from 70 FPS to 30 FPS while keeping “mini-bout segments of 120 frames” and “track age criteria ... 10,000 frames” [PAGE 2]. Prediction: the mini-bout becomes \(120/30=4.0\) s instead of \(120/70\approx1.71\) s, and track age becomes \(10000/30\approx333.3\) s instead of \(10000/70\approx142.9\) s. Longer mini-bouts will increase pose-stitching drift and ID switches; longer track age will extend identity memory beyond the 120 s bout plus 60 s rest cycle [PAGE 2]. Expected observable: IDU and IDS rise above the reported “IDU=0; IDS=0” [PAGE 3]. Verification: retune to \(120\cdot30/70\approx51.4\) frames and \(10000\cdot30/70\approx4285.7\) frames; if the retuned run restores near-zero IDU and IDS, the original constants are frame-rate-dependent and cannot be stated as frame-count invariants. This is a clean counterfactual because the paper gives both the FPS and the frame counts [PAGE 1, PAGE 2].

\textbf{Veto 3: ring-actor and rope-contact contamination.} Counterfactual: add or annotate ringside personnel who lean on the ropes, or compare bouts with and without a referee such that \(DO\) changes from 3 to 2. The source already reports over-segmentation because “people moving along the side of the ring hangs onto the ropes resulting on line crossing” and because “YOLOv7 failed to detect individuals at the time of transition” [PAGE 3]. Prediction: the ring-crossing cue \(C_R\) becomes a false positive, and the person-count cue \(C_C\) becomes undefined or invalid because Equation 3’s complement is malformed [PAGE 2]. With two independent false cues, the majority rule can satisfy “a minimal of two task’s condition” [PAGE 2] and declare a transition when none occurred. Expected observable: segmented bouts exceed ground truth, reproducing the paper’s “over-segments bout segments in some instances” [PAGE 3]. Verification: mask ringside regions, add line-crossing hysteresis, and stratify by \(DO=2\) versus \(DO=3\); if over-segmentation drops only when ringside actors are excluded, the 90% transition result is vetoed as scene-dependent rather than athlete-dependent.
