Notation: $t$ indexes time; the wearable stream is $\text{BPM}(t)$ sampled at 5 s resolution ([PAGE 3], "photoplethysmography ... at 5 s resolution (one recording every 5 s)").

1) Rate of change of heart rate (proxy for HRV). Verbatim [PAGE 3]: "RCH was calculated as the mean beats per minute (BPM) in 1 min subtracted from the mean BPM in the previous minute, representing the change in BPM over 2 min."
$$\mathrm{RCH}(t)=\overline{\mathrm{BPM}}_{[t-1\,\mathrm{min},\,t]}-\overline{\mathrm{BPM}}_{[t-2\,\mathrm{min},\,t-1\,\mathrm{min}]},\qquad [\mathrm{RCH}]=\mathrm{beats\cdot min^{-1}}.$$
Breakdown regime: differencing adjacent 1-min means is a first-order high-pass; any HRV content above the Nyquist limit of the 1-min aggregation ($f>0.5\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$) is aliased away. The authors admit this is a coarse HRV surrogate ([PAGE 9], "very basic method").

2) Daily resting heart rate. Verbatim [PAGE 3]: "average of the bottom quintile of BPM where no steps were recorded."
$$\mathrm{RHR}_d=\frac{1}{|Q_{0.2}|}\sum_{i\in Q_{0.2}}\mathrm{BPM}_i,\quad Q_{0.2}=\{\,i:\mathrm{steps}_i=0,\ \mathrm{BPM}_i\le q_{0.2}\,\}.$$
Breakdown: estimator variance $\propto 1/|Q_{0.2}|$ diverges when no-step epochs are scarce (sedentary-poor or insomniac nights), and it is collinear with the sleep features (shared no-step support).

3) Cycle extraction (phase operator). A Morlet wavelet yields the peak periodicity $T_k$; the signal is zero-order Butterworth band-pass filtered around $T_k$ and the instantaneous phase is taken by the Hilbert transform (verbatim [PAGE 3]). The analytic signal is
$$z(t)=x(t)+i\,\mathcal H[x(t)]=A(t)\,e^{i\phi(t)},\qquad \phi(t)=\arg z(t)\in[0,2\pi).$$
Each cyclic feature becomes two linear regressors ([PAGE 3], "normalizing the signal from 0 to 2π and computing the sine and cosine"):
$$x_{\sin}=\sin\phi(t),\qquad x_{\cos}=\cos\phi(t).$$
Singularity: $\phi$ is undefined where the envelope $A(t)\to 0$ (amplitude nulls); the filter also has edge transients, and the construction presumes a narrowband, quasi-stationary oscillation — precisely the assumption the Discussion flags as possibly violated ("theoretical shift and change ... in heart rate cycles over time was not considered," [PAGE 8]).

4) Phase-locking gate (uniformity test). Let $\{\theta_j\}_{j=1}^{n}$ be seizure phases. The Ajne (1968) semicircumference-count statistic (ref 42) measures departure from uniformity; the canonical form counts observations in the best-fitting half-circle,
$$A_n=\frac{1}{2n}\Big[\max_{\mu}\big(2\,n_{+}(\mu)-n\big)\Big]^2,\qquad n_{+}(\mu)=\#\{j:\cos(\theta_j-\mu)>0\},$$
with significance at $p<0.05$ (I present the standard form; the exact normalization is implementation-specific). Breakdown: power collapses as $n\to$ small, so absence of a detected cycle at low event counts is not evidence of absence.

5) Ensemble combiner (logistic regression over base learners). Verbatim [PAGE 4]: "A logistic regression model ... combined the random forest and LSTM outputs into one seizure risk value." With base outputs $\hat p_{\mathrm{LSTM}},\hat p_{\mathrm{RF}}\in[0,1]$,
$$\hat p=\sigma\!\big(w_0+w_1\hat p_{\mathrm{LSTM}}+w_2\hat p_{\mathrm{RF}}\big),\qquad \sigma(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}},\quad [\hat p]=\text{probability}.$$
Breakdown: because RHR, sleep, and cyclic HR features are collinear, and because both base outputs are themselves monotone transforms of overlapping evidence, $(w_1,w_2)$ can be weakly identified; the logistic layer then absorbs noise rather than signal.

6) LSTM base learner. Single layer, 64 memory units, two dense layers, linear activation, 100 epochs, MSE loss, Adam ([PAGE 4]); input is a $7\times7$ matrix (7 days × 7 sleep features).
$$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\hat y_t-y_t\big)^2,\qquad y_t\in\{0,1\}.$$
Breakdown: with seizure prevalence $\pi\ll1$, MSE is dominated by the majority (non-seizure) class; gradient signal for the rare positive is $O(\pi)$, so the LSTM can converge to near-constant output — one reason sleep added little in the hourly model ([PAGE 9]).

7) Risk thresholds C1–C4 (constrained cut-point optimization, [PAGE 4]). Enforce $\text{time}_{\text{low}}>\text{time}_{\text{med}}>\text{time}_{\text{high}}$ (C1) and $\text{sz}_{\text{high}}>\text{sz}_{\text{med}}>\text{sz}_{\text{low}}$ (C2); if infeasible, maximize $\text{time}_{\text{low}}\times \text{sz}_{\text{high}}$ (C3×C4). Feasibility is non-guaranteed; for high-frequency participants (P6) the ordering can invert, forcing the fallback objective.

8) Scoring metrics. AUC (verbatim intent, [PAGE 5]) estimates $\Pr(\hat p_{\text{sz}}>\hat p_{\text{non-sz}})$, dimensionless in $[0,1]$; for a purely random scorer it is $0.5$ regardless of base rate (ROC is invariant to class balance), so the "chance" line in Table 2 is $\approx0.5$ with sampling SD shrinking in event count. Brier score ([PAGE 5], "loss score of 0" ideal):
$$\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\hat p_t-o_t\big)^2,\quad o_t\in\{0,1\},\quad [\mathrm{BS}]=\text{probability}^2\in[0,1].$$
BS is a proper scoring rule, decomposable into reliability − resolution + uncertainty; it penalizes the miscalibration ("risk was overestimated," [PAGE 7]) that AUC ignores — the reason both are reported.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The epistemic lineage runs from skepticism to consolidation. Reference (12) Mormann et al., "Seizure prediction: the long and winding road" ([PAGE 11]), marks the pre-consensus era, when [PAGE 2] "most research was based on limited data [from short-duration in-hospital ... EEG] and some presented methodological ﬂaws." The inflection is the NeuroVista intracranial advisory trial (ref 16, Cook et al., Lancet Neurol 2013), cited twice as proof-of-principle and as a source of the frequency–performance relationship ([PAGE 9], "also observed in a prospective forecasting study (16)").

Two parallel lineages then converge in this paper. First, the cycles lineage: Baud et al. (ref 26) established "Multi-day rhythms modulate seizure risk"; Karoly et al. (ref 27) characterized "Circadian and circaseptan rhythms"; Leguia et al. (ref 28) extended to "Seizure cycles in focal epilepsy." Second, the critical-slowing lineage: Maturana et al. (ref 29) framed "Critical slowing down as a biomarker for seizure susceptibility," and the threshold-optimization constraints C1/C2 are attributed directly to (29) at [PAGE 4]. The distinctive contribution here is transplanting the cycle biomarker from intracranial EEG to the wrist via Karoly et al. (ref 30), the authors' own medRxiv preprint showing "Multiday cycles of heart rate modulate seizure likelihood at daily, weekly and monthly timescales" ([PAGE 11]) — this is the paper's true parent, and it explains why "cycles feature group was the strongest predictor" ([PAGE 8]). The contemporaneous Proix et al. (ref 34, Lancet Neurol 2021) is the intracranial validation that heart-rate surrogacy is chasing.

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy. (i) Weekly retraining ([PAGE 4], "retraining ... occurred on a weekly basis") is a moving-target estimator: if the underlying oscillator's period $T_k$ drifts across weeks, a model that never forgets accumulates stale phase evidence. The authors pre-register the fix ([PAGE 10]): "remove the oldest week of data every time a new week is added." (ii) Thecircaseptan (~7-day) and menstrual (~monthly) components alias with the weekly retraining cadence — a 7-day retrain window and a 7-day rhythm are near-commensurate, so schedule and signal can confound. (iii) The "cycles shift over time" admission ([PAGE 8]) is a non-stationarity trap: a phase-locking test passed on training data can be void in evaluation, which is exactly the P7 collapse pattern.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Reframe the forecaster as an agent that must model a second agent (the patient) whose observable behavior — the seizure diary — is itself a noisy, self-interested signal. The "clock" is the set of instantiated oscillators: circadian $\phi_{\text{circ}}(t)$ and multiday $\phi_{\text{multi}}(t)$, each defined only where $A(t)>0$ (Eq. 3). The forecaster's implicit theory of the patient is: "seizures cluster at privileged phases of $\phi$." Its recursive depth is shallow (one level) and that is the correct design choice — modeling a biological oscillator does not require modeling the patient modeling the forecaster.

Crisis-habit regression. Under crisis (high seizure frequency), the correct Bayesian posterior over "next-hour risk" flattens toward the base rate because events are nearly memoryless at hourly resolution; the model is supposed to regress to chance, and it does: P6 (36.7/month, [PAGE 7]) yields daily AUC 0.46 (<0.5). This is not a bug; it is the estimator correctly reporting that a near-Poisson process at high rate carries little exploitable temporal structure. The verifiable prediction is monotone: as frequency rises, per-cycle phase information $\to 0$ and AUC $\to 0.5$.

Counter-trap. The dangerous failure mode is a spurious theory of mind: the forecaster "believes" in a multiday rhythm that is an artifact of the weekly retrain cadence or of PPG motion cycles (work schedules, exercise routines). The defense is the circular-uniformity gate (Eq. 4) plus the honest ablation ([PAGE 8], Fig. 6): cycles contributed the most, but "it is unlikely that there is signiﬁcant positive or negative value added ... when values are close to 0" — an explicit refusal to over-read near-zero feature deltas. The trap fires hardest for participants with exactly one significant cycle and low counts (P7: no multiday cycle, eval hourly 0.41).

Clock as adversary of generalization. The held-out cohort is the moment the forecaster's theory meets a shifted world: daily evaluation collapses to 4/8 ([PAGE 8]). The paper's countermeasure is the finite memory window, and the reasoning is sound — a bounded-horizon clock (e.g., 1-year truncation, [PAGE 10]) trades bias for variance in a non-stationary phase process. The recursive reading: the forecaster should not trust a phase estimate whose amplitude envelope has decayed, because a low-amplitude cycle is precisely where $\phi(t)$ is undefined and the "theory of the patient's clock" is unfalsifiable.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Claim under adjudication: "high hourly risk $\Rightarrow$ elevated seizure probability," mediated by PPG heart rate. I test three cross-layer vetoes.

Physical/biomechanical veto. Motion corrupts PPG. The paper's defense is a frequency-separation assumption ([PAGE 10]): "measurement noise was likely to be at a higher frequency than the time scale focused on." Audit: exercise raises heart rate on a timescale of minutes — squarely inside the circadian-cycle band, not above it. So the assumption holds for gross cadence artifact but fails for genuine exercise-induced tachycardia, which can masquerade as a phase excursion in $\phi_{\text{circ}}$. Mitigations actually present: RHR uses only no-step epochs (Eq. 2), and hourly step count is a separate feature the model can use to discount motion-contaminated HR. Verdict: veto partially sustained — the daily model is largely protected (no-step RHR, multiday cycles only), the hourly model is exposed, consistent with the weaker incremental value of raw HR features in the hourly forecaster ([PAGE 8], "sleep and heart rate features were the weakest predictors in the hourly forecaster").

Weather/environmental veto. The Discussion cites temperature ([PAGE 8], ref 41) and low ambient temperature as a seizure risk factor (ref 22). Environmental cycles (daily temperature, weekly social/occupational cycles) can co-drive both heart rate and seizures, inducing a common-cause confound: a spurious $\phi$-locking that is really exogenous. The model has no weather covariate, so it cannot separate an endogenous oscillator from a temperature-paced one. Verdict: veto sustained as an unmeasured-confounder risk; it does not falsify within-subject phase-locking (which is predictive regardless of cause) but it does undercut the mechanistic claim that heart-rate cycles are a seizure biomarker rather than a shared environmental readout.

Data-integrity veto. Self-report is the outcome variable, and [PAGE 10] concedes diaries are "known to be inaccurate," with under-reporting of focal-aware seizures. Non-random under-reporting (e.g., reporting more on high-suspicion days) would inflate apparent predictability. The training-set exclusion of the hour/day adjacent to each seizure ([PAGE 4]) is a partial fix for temporal leakage but not for systematic under-count. Verdict: veto sustained; it bounds achievable AUC and biases the calibration curves upward.

Net adjudication: the within-subject forecasting result survives all three vetoes for the daily/hourly *predictive* claim (phase-locking predicts regardless of cause), but the *mechanistic* claim — that non-invasive heart rate is itself a seizure biomarker — is not fully separable from motion and weather confounds with the covariates used. The paper's own hedges ([PAGE 10]) are consistent with this restricted verdict.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable, falsifiable scenarios (RLVR = reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards; each maps to a checkable numeric outcome in the paper).

Veto 1 — Ablation of the cycles feature group.
Counterfactual: remove cyclic features (heart-rate cycles + last/second-last seizure time).
Prediction: AUC collapses toward 0.5 for most participants, because [PAGE 8] states cycles "was the strongest predictor across the whole cohort and for most individuals." Verifiable check: Fig. 6 shows the largest positive AUC-delta attributable to cycles; a rerun with cycles ablated should reproduce that delta as a drop. Falsifier: if physical-activity-only models retained $M_{\text{AUC}}\approx0.7$, the cycles-dominance claim is void.

Veto 2 — High-frequency participant on the daily horizon.
Counterfactual: participant with maximal seizure rate on the daily forecaster.
Prediction: daily AUC $<0.5$ (chance). Verifiable check: already realized — P6 (36.7/month, [PAGE 7]) scores daily 0.46 (no asterisk, [PAGE 8]). The general law: as rate $\uparrow$, exploitable temporal structure $\downarrow$, AUC $\to 0.5^+$ from above but crossing below at extreme rates. Falsifier: a higher-frequency participant scoring daily $>0.7$.

Veto 3 — Absence of a significant multiday cycle in held-out evaluation.
Counterfactual: participant lacking a significant multiday HR cycle, evaluated prospectively.
Prediction: hourly evaluation AUC $<0.5$. Verifiable check: P7 is "the only participant without a signiﬁcant multiday heart rate cycle" and posts evaluation hourly 0.41 ([PAGE 8]). Falsifier: a cycle-null participant whose prospective hourly AUC exceeds chance by a wide margin, which would show the model exploits residual features (activity/sleep) independent of multiday HR.

Cross-veto invariant: the three vetoes are mutually consistent and jointly predicted by the paper's stated mechanism — that phase-locking of seizures to heart-rate cycles carries the signal, and that the signal vanishes when the cycle is absent (Veto 3), when the process saturates (Veto 2), or when the features are removed (Veto 1). Because all three predicted outcomes are already numerically present in Table 2, the mechanistic account passes an ex ante-style test on the reported data; the open question the paper cannot yet settle is prospective generalization of the daily horizon (4/8, [PAGE 8]), which the authors correctly defer to future clinical trials ([PAGE 10]).
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
