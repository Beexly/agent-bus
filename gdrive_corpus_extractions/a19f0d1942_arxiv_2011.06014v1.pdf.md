The source is a methods paper about converting positional tracking data into networks, not about event passing alone. The central epistemic move is to replace event-defined interactions, such as passes, with continuously sampled spatiotemporal interactions: ball traversal, player-player proximity, marking duration, and velocity alignment. The paper states the broad motivation as asking “Would it be possible to obtain networks from tracking datasets?” [PAGE 3]. That question creates a derivation problem: the paper defines four network families operationally but only partially formalizes them, so a rigorous reconstruction must make the measurement chain explicit: frames, coordinates, thresholds, phase labels, time aggregation, and singularities.

The first multi-hop derivation is for Ball Flow Networks. The source says the procedure “begins with the partition of the pitch into a NxM grid” and that “Each partition is a node of the network.” It then says, “we count how many times the ball traverses the frontier between any pair of partitions and we use this information as the weight of the links between two nodes” [PAGE 4]. The hidden operator is therefore a space-discretized flow graph: the continuous ball trajectory b(t) is mapped into a discrete sequence of cells, and link weights are crossing counts. This creates several gray zones. First, crossing is not defined purely by successive cell labels; a segment from cell A to cell B may cross a frontier without the ball being sampled inside adjacent cells, so a purely frame-to-frame transition rule can miss crossings or create spurious ones. Second, node size in the example is “the time that the ball has been retained by a team inside each partition” [PAGE 4], which is a dwell-time operator in seconds, not a count. Third, the network is possession-conditioned and attacking-direction-specific, so the operator is not a neutral geometric flow unless team and phase filters are specified.

The second derivation concerns Signed Proximity Networks. The source defines them as networks “based on the distances between players” and specifies that “we connect any two players with a link if they are below a threshold distance R.” It also assigns signs: “links have a positive value (+1) if they connect players of the same team and a negative one (-1) if the connected players belong to different teams” [PAGE 5]. This means the primary object is a time-indexed signed graph, not a single static network. The paper explicitly says that “a set {r} of signed networks can be assigned to a unique frame” and that repeating across frames yields “{r}x{t} signed networks” [PAGE 5]. The derivation must therefore preserve two indices: threshold radius R and frame t. Aggregation across time is optional and must be declared. If aggregated, the signed weight is a time integral of a signed indicator, with units of seconds if multiplied by frame interval, or dimensionless if raw frame counts are retained. The triangle analysis is also threshold-dependent: the paper notes that “it is around R~7 meters where the difference between both teams is the highest” [PAGE 6]. This is a singular sensitivity region, not a universal law.

The third derivation is Marking Networks. The source proposes focusing on “the time a defender devotes to mark a specific player by approaching him closer than a threshold distance R” [PAGE 6-7]. It then gives a concrete example: “we can set R=1.5 meters and find out the time accumulated by each player during the defensive phase with an opponent closer than R” [PAGE 7]. This produces a bipartite directed-ish operator: rows are defenders, columns are opponents, and weights are accumulated proximity time in seconds. The example also reveals a plotting threshold: “we removed those links with an accumulated time lower tmin=7 seconds” [PAGE 7]. The gray zone is that marking time is not exclusive. Multiple defenders can be within R of the same attacker simultaneously, so row sums and column sums are not constrained by total match time. The operator measures joint proximity exposure, not unique assignment.

The fourth derivation is Functional Coordination Networks. The source acknowledges the interpretive difficulty: “we require knowing when two players move in a coordinated way and quantifying this coordination,” while also saying “This is a very rough approximation” [PAGE 8]. The operational proxy is velocity alignment. The paper computes velocities, then “the angle θij of the velocities of each pair of players,” and if “the angle is below a certain threshold (in this case θij< θthresholf=10º) we consider that the velocities of player i and j were aligned during this frame” [PAGE 8]. The resulting matrix entry is a fraction between 0 and 1, interpreted as percentage of time aligned [PAGE 8]. The reported example values are “|Cdeffensive|=0.176 and |Coffensive|=0.152” [PAGE 8]. The key breakdown regime is the zero-velocity singularity: velocity direction is undefined when a player is stationary or when positional noise dominates displacement. Another breakdown is conceptual: aligned velocity does not prove intentional coordination, because players may move in the same direction for independent reasons.

Overall, the source supports a layered graph reconstruction: spatial discretization for ball flow, signed proximity graphs for local density, bipartite temporal marking graphs for defensive exposure, and velocity-alignment graphs for coarse motion coordination. The rigorous audit must separate observable operators from tactical interpretations. The paper itself is cautious: “All models are wrong, but some are useful” [PAGE 7]. That quote is not rhetorical decoration; it defines the admissibility criterion. A tracking-network operator is useful only if its measurement assumptions, units, thresholds, phase filters, and singularities are explicit.

<![CDATA[
Let tracking data be sampled at frame index k = 1,...,T with constant frame interval Δt. The source states that the Mediacoach system recorded positions “at 25 frames per second” [PAGE 4], so Δt = 1/25 s = 0.04 s. Let p_i(k) ∈ R² denote the planar position of player i at frame k, in meters, and let b(k) ∈ R² denote ball position, in meters. Let N_a and N_b be the number of players in teams A and B. Let K ⊂ {1,...,T} be a set of frames selected by phase, possession, or defensive condition. All sums over frames must be multiplied by Δt when the intended unit is time; otherwise they are frame counts.

1. Ball Flow Network operator

Partition the pitch into N columns and M rows. The source example uses “N=M=5” [PAGE 5]. Define cell set G = {g_{a,b}: a=1,...,N, b=1,...,M}. Let c(k) ∈ G be the cell containing b(k). Define a crossing indicator between cells u and v at frame k:

C_{uv}(k) = 1 if the segment from b(k) to b(k+1) intersects the shared frontier between u and v, and c(k)=u, c(k+1)=v; otherwise C_{uv}(k)=0.

The Ball Flow Network weight is:

W^{BFN}_{uv} = Σ_{k=1}^{T-1} C_{uv}(k).

Units: integer crossing count over the match. If normalized by total observation time, w^{BFN}_{uv} = W^{BFN}_{uv}/[(T-1)Δt] has units s⁻¹. Node retention time is:

τ^{BFN}_u = Δt Σ_{k=1}^{T} 1{c(k)=u}.

Units: seconds. If only attacking possession by team A is included, then K = possession frames of A and sums are restricted to K. The source says the plotted node size indicates “the time the ball has been inside each partition during the attacking phase” [PAGE 5].

A probabilistic normalization is:

P^{BFN}_{uv} = W^{BFN}_{uv} / Σ_{w∈G} W^{BFN}_{uw}, if Σ_w W^{BFN}_{uw} > 0.

Units: dimensionless probability. If Shannon entropy is used, H_u = -Σ_v P_{uv} ln P_{uv}, with units nats. This entropy operator is derived, not quoted from the source, but it is dimensionally and information-theoretically well-defined.

Assumptions: ball position is known at every frame; crossing is geometrically detectable; possession and attacking direction are correctly labeled.

Singularities and breakdown regimes:
- If no ball transitions leave cell u, Σ_w W_{uw}=0 and P_{uv} is undefined.
- If ball tracking is lost, c(k) is undefined.
- If v_ball Δt exceeds a cell dimension, frame-to-frame crossing detection can alias. For cell length L_cell, a sufficient conservative condition to reduce skipping is v_ball Δt < L_cell/2.
- If the grid is too fine relative to 25 Hz sampling, many transitions become ambiguous.
- If crossing is inferred only from cell-label changes, then multiple frontier crossings inside one frame interval collapse into one edge, undercounting W.

2. Signed Proximity Network operator

For frame k, define Euclidean distance:

d_{ij}(k) = ||p_i(k) - p_j(k)||₂.

Units: meters. Let team(i) ∈ {A,B}. For threshold R ≥ 0, define signed adjacency:

A^{SPN}_{ij}(k;R) =
+1, if d_{ij}(k) ≤ R and team(i)=team(j);
-1, if d_{ij}(k) ≤ R and team(i)≠team(j);
0, otherwise.

This directly implements the source rule: “we connect any two players with a link if they are below a threshold distance R” and signs are +1 for same team and -1 for different teams [PAGE 5].

For time aggregation over K, define signed co-proximity:

S^{SPN}_{ij}(R;K) = Δt Σ_{k∈K} A^{SPN}_{ij}(k;R).

Units: seconds, signed. Positive values indicate net same-team proximity over time; negative values indicate net opponent proximity. If frame counts are preferred, omit Δt and the unit is frame count.

Triangle operators can be written with indicators. For team A all-positive triangle count:

T^{+++}_{A}(R;K) = Σ_{i<j<l} 1{d_{ij}(k)≤R, d_{jl}(k)≤R, d_{il}(k)≤R, team(i)=team(j)=team(l)=A}.

If time-resolved, sum over k and multiply by Δt. Mixed-sign triangles can be defined by requiring at least one inter-team edge within R and at least one intra-team edge within R. The source says only four triangle types are possible in their example: two all-positive and two mixed [PAGE 6].

Assumptions: positions are synchronized; threshold R is meaningful in meters; phase or possession filter is specified if interpreting tactical superiority.

Singularities and breakdown regimes:
- R=0 gives no links unless players are exactly co-located.
- R→∞ gives a complete signed graph.
- At critical radii where many distances cross R, network statistics can jump discontinuously.
- The source identifies R≈7 m as a high-sensitivity region: “it is around R~7 meters where the difference between both teams is the highest” [PAGE 6].
- If frames are aggregated without phase labels, offensive and defensive proximity are conflated.

3. Marking Network operator

Let D be the set of defenders on the marking team and O the set of opponents. For defender d∈D and opponent o∈O, define marking weight over defensive frames K_def:

W^{MN}_{do}(R) = Δt Σ_{k∈K_def} 1{d_{do}(k) ≤ R}.

Units: seconds. The source example sets “R=1.5 meters” and accumulates “the time accumulated by each player during the defensive phase with an opponent closer than R” [PAGE 7]. The resulting object is bipartite: only defender-to-opponent links exist. The plotting threshold is:

E^{plot}_{do} = W^{MN}_{do}(R) · 1{W^{MN}_{do}(R) ≥ t_min}.

The source example uses “tmin=7 seconds” [PAGE 7].

Assumptions: defensive phase K_def is correctly identified; R approximates marking distance; defender and opponent positions are reliable.

Singularities and breakdown regimes:
- If K_def is empty, all marking weights are zero.
- If R is too small, genuine marking is missed.
- If R is too large, unrelated proximity is counted as marking.
- Marking is non-exclusive: multiple defenders can simultaneously satisfy d_{do}(k) ≤ R for the same opponent o. Therefore Σ_d W_{do} can exceed the total defensive time, and row sums cannot be interpreted as unique workload.
- If occlusion causes missing positions, the indicator is undefined for affected frames.

4. Functional Coordination Network operator

Define velocity for player i at frame k. A central difference is:

v_i(k) = [p_i(k+1) - p_i(k-1)] / (2Δt).

Units: m s⁻¹. For endpoints, a one-sided difference may be used:

v_i(k) = [p_i(k+1) - p_i(k)] / Δt.

Define speed s_i(k)=||v_i(k)||₂. To avoid zero-velocity singularity, introduce ε_v > 0:

u_i(k) = v_i(k)/s_i(k), if s_i(k) ≥ ε_v;
u_i(k) undefined, if s_i(k) < ε_v.

The angle between two players’ velocities is:

θ_{ij}(k) = arccos( u_i(k) · u_j(k) ).

Units: radians, with θ ∈ [0,π]. The source threshold is “θij< θthresholf=10º” [PAGE 8], which is θ* = 10° = π/18 rad ≈ 0.1745 rad.

Define alignment indicator:

I_{ij}(k) = 1 if s_i(k) ≥ ε_v, s_j(k) ≥ ε_v, and θ_{ij}(k) ≤ θ*;
I_{ij}(k) = 0 otherwise.

Coordination fraction over phase K is:

C_{ij}(K; θ*, ε_v) = [Δt Σ_{k∈K} I_{ij}(k)] / T_K,

where T_K = Δt |K| is the duration of the phase in seconds. Units: dimensionless, between 0 and 1. If K is the whole match, T_K = T_match. The source describes this as “the percentage (between 0 and 1) of time accumulated by every pair of players during the whole match” [PAGE 8]. The reported example values are “|Cdeffensive|=0.176 and |Coffensive|=0.152” [PAGE 8].

A mean coordination index over pairs can be defined as:

⟨C⟩_K = [2/(N_p(N_p-1))] Σ_{i<j} C_{ij}(K),

where N_p is the number of players considered. Units: dimensionless.

Assumptions: velocity direction is a valid proxy for coordination; phase labels are correct; ε_v and θ* are fixed; frame-to-frame displacement reflects true motion rather than tracking noise.

Singularities and breakdown regimes:
- If s_i(k)=0 or s_j(k)=0, u_i or u_j is undefined. This is the main mathematical singularity.
- If positional noise dominates displacement, θ_{ij} becomes unreliable. For central difference with independent position noise σ_x, approximate velocity noise standard deviation is σ_v ≈ σ_x/(√2 Δt).
- If θ* is too small, few pairs qualify; if θ* is too large, almost all moving pairs qualify.
- If players are moving but not intentionally coordinating, the operator overstates coordination.
- If players coordinate by complementary motion, the operator misses it. The source notes that a defender moving forward while a midfielder moves backward would not be captured by simple velocity alignment [PAGE 8].
- If phase segmentation is wrong, defensive and offensive coordination are mixed.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
<![CDATA[
The scientific lineage begins with Gould and Gatrell’s manually constructed passing network from the 1977 FA Cup final, described as “Transforming team dynamics into a network” [PAGE 1]. Their nodes were players, and links were pass counts. They analyzed the structure using q-analysis, but the source says the idea “did not have a very high impact in the community of sports sciences and never reached the pitch” [PAGE 1]. This historical discontinuity matters: the modern revival was enabled not merely by network theory but by data acquisition. Event datasets allowed detailed passing networks and metrics such as xG [PAGE 2]. The source identifies Duch et al. as “the renaissance of the application of Network Science … to the analysis of player and team behavior on the pitch” [PAGE 2].

The second lineage is the transition from event data to tracking data. Event networks infer interaction from discrete actions. Tracking networks infer interaction from continuous positions. The source marks the tracking system as a multi-camera optical system recording “each player’s position on the pitch at 25 frames per second” [PAGE 4]. This creates a new measurement substrate: interaction can be defined by ball trajectory, distance, time spent near an opponent, or motion alignment. The paper’s novelty is precisely that “tracking goes beyond actions around the ball” [PAGE 3].

A third lineage is imported from neuroscience. Functional Coordination Networks are explicitly linked to functional brain networks, where nodes are brain regions and links indicate coordination between dynamics [PAGE 8]. The source cites Bullmore & Sporns and Papo et al. in that context. This import is useful but dangerous. In neuroscience, functional connectivity is usually inferred from statistical dependence between time series. In football, the source uses a simpler velocity-alignment proxy. The genealogy therefore contains a conceptual leap: coordination is not directly measured; it is approximated by directional similarity.

Multi-week analytical traps follow from this lineage. The first trap is treating Ball Flow Networks as if they were passing networks. The source explicitly distinguishes them: ball flow includes “not only passes but also the conduction of the ball by players” [PAGE 9-10]. The second trap is treating Signed Proximity Networks as static formations. They are time-evolving and threshold-dependent [PAGE 5]. The third trap is treating Marking Networks as exclusive assignments. The source’s own example shows a forward, Braithwaite, as the highest-weight marker of Casemiro, because marking is an accumulated proximity phenomenon, not a formal positional role [PAGE 7]. The fourth trap is treating Functional Coordination Networks as evidence of intention. The source admits the model “would not capture” complementary movement and calls it “a very rough approximation” [PAGE 8].

Thus, the retro-causal genealogy is not a simple progression from passes to tracking. It is a sequence of redefinitions of what counts as an interaction. The historical lesson is that each new data layer creates a new class of hidden assumptions. The operator becomes scientific only when those assumptions are exposed.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
<![CDATA[
A recursive theory of mind layer is needed because tracking networks do not directly observe tactical intent. They observe behavioral residues of intent: spacing, proximity, duration, and alignment. The source gives one explicit tactical interpretation: “defence is based on maintaining order according to a particular structure, which implies higher coordination between players of the same team” [PAGE 8]. It also says that during attack, “players are prone to move more randomly to create disorder in the defensive mesh of the opponent” [PAGE 8]. These statements can be treated as hypotheses about recursive anticipation: a team models the opponent’s model, and chooses either structural order or induced disorder.

Crisis habit regression is the first recursive pattern. Under pressure, a team may abandon expansive movement and revert to compact, aligned defensive shapes. In the operator language, this should appear as higher defensive Functional Coordination Network values, consistent with the reported “|Cdeffensive|=0.176” exceeding “|Coffensive|=0.152” [PAGE 8]. It may also appear in Signed Proximity Networks as increased positive same-team links and reduced opponent-negative links, especially if the team compresses space. However, this inference is not automatic. A high defensive coordination fraction could also result from slow, conservative play, tracking artifacts, or a phase filter that includes mostly stationary defending. Therefore, crisis habit regression is admissible only if phase labels, velocity thresholds, and positional noise are controlled.

Counter-trap behavior is the second recursive pattern. A team may intentionally break apparent coordination to deceive the opponent. The source notes that during attack, forwards may move in different directions, reducing coordination with teammates [PAGE 8]. This can be a counter-trap: apparent low coordination is not disorder but designed variability. In graph terms, forwards’ Functional Coordination Network node strengths may decrease while their positional unpredictability increases. A naive reading would call this poor coordination. A recursive reading treats it as an adversarial strategy: the attacking team is modeling the defender’s expectation of alignment and violating it.

The third element is the clock. The source’s networks are frame-dependent. Signed Proximity Networks generate “{r}x{t} signed networks” [PAGE 5]. Marking Networks accumulate time over defensive phases [PAGE 7]. Functional Coordination Networks accumulate aligned time across phases [PAGE 8]. The clock matters because a team can be coordinated in one interval and deceptive in another. Threshold radii also have temporal regimes: the source finds the greatest team difference near “R~7 meters” [PAGE 6]. This is a scale-specific clock of local superiority. It may not hold at R=1.5 m, where marking is more about direct pressure, or at R=15 m, where large-scale shape dominates.

The recursive audit therefore requires three tests. First, does the network pattern persist across phase labels? Second, does it persist across thresholds and velocity cutoffs? Third, can an alternative tactical explanation produce the same operator values? If yes, the interpretation must be downgraded from causal intent to descriptive regularity. The paper’s own caution supports this: “All models are wrong, but some are useful” [PAGE 7].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
<![CDATA[
A cross-layer veto occurs when a physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraint invalidates a network interpretation even if the mathematical operator is formally defined. The adjudication must identify which layer fails and which downstream operators become void.

Layer 1: tracking measurement noise. Let observed position be p_i^obs(k) = p_i^true(k) + η_i(k), where η_i(k) is zero-mean noise with variance σ_x². For a forward velocity estimate v_i(k) = [p_i(k+1)-p_i(k)]/Δt, velocity noise variance is approximately σ_x²/Δt². For a central difference, it is approximately σ_x²/(2Δt²). At 25 Hz, Δt=0.04 s. If σ_x=0.05 m, central-difference velocity noise is about 0.88 m s⁻¹. If typical walking speed is around 1 m s⁻¹, angular uncertainty is large. The angle uncertainty scales roughly as σ_θ ≈ σ_v/||v|| for small errors. For the Functional Coordination Network threshold θ*=10°≈0.1745 rad, reliable alignment requires approximately ||v|| > σ_v/θ*. With σ_v=0.88 m s⁻¹, this implies ||v|| > 5.0 m s⁻¹, which is close to sprinting. Therefore, if a large fraction of defensive coordination frames involve walking or jogging, the velocity-alignment operator is physically fragile. Veto condition: if the proportion of frames with ||v|| < ε_v is high, or if σ_θ exceeds θ*, then Functional Coordination Network values cannot be adjudicated as coordination.

Layer 2: biomechanical feasibility. Human motion is constrained by maximum speed, acceleration, and turning radius. If frame-to-frame displacement implies acceleration beyond plausible human limits, either the tracking data or the event interpretation is invalid. A simple feasibility check is a_i(k) ≈ [v_i(k+1)-v_i(k)]/Δt. If ||a_i(k)|| exceeds a domain-appropriate bound for many frames, the velocity field is unreliable. This matters for Marking Networks and Signed Proximity Networks because distance thresholds R can be crossed spuriously if positions jitter. Veto condition: if apparent threshold crossings are caused by noise-induced acceleration rather than coherent movement, marking and proximity weights are invalid.

Layer 3: weather and surface conditions. Rain, wind, and pitch friction alter ball motion and player movement. The optical tracking system records positions; it does not directly certify environmental fairness. If heavy rain reduces ball roll speed, Ball Flow Network crossing counts may decrease in a way that reflects surface conditions rather than tactical preference. If wet grass increases sliding, player velocity direction becomes noisier, weakening Functional Coordination Networks. If wind affects long balls, crossing geometries may be distorted. Veto condition: if weather materially changes the velocity distribution or ball trajectory distribution relative to baseline, then cross-match comparisons of Ball Flow Networks or Functional Coordination Networks are not layer-invariant.

Layer 4: phase and possession labeling. Marking Networks require defensive frames. Functional Coordination Networks require defensive and offensive phases. Signed Proximity Networks depend heavily on possession because “the number of positive and negative links is highly dependent on possession” [PAGE 10]. If phase labels are wrong, the operator remains computable but the interpretation is void. Veto condition: if phase transition uncertainty exceeds a meaningful temporal window, then defensive coordination advantage or offensive disorder claims cannot be adjudicated.

Layer 5: occlusion and missing data. The source says an operator corrected positions when location was lost [PAGE 4]. However, correction procedures can introduce bias. If defenders and attackers are occluded near the ball, Marking Networks may undercount crucial marking events. If players near the boundary are missed, Ball Flow Network edge weights near touchlines may be distorted. Veto condition: if missing-data rate is nonrandom and correlated with tactical events, such as marking near crowded areas, the network is biased.

Proof of veto propagation: if player-position noise invalidates velocity direction, Functional Coordination Network is invalid. If ball-position noise invalidates crossing, Ball Flow Network is invalid. If defensive-phase labels are invalid, Marking Network and phase-specific Functional Coordination Network are invalid. If weather changes the motion distribution, cross-condition comparisons are invalid. Therefore, a network claim is admissible only when the relevant physical layer passes its threshold tests. The source’s validation with GPS [PAGE 4] reduces but does not eliminate this requirement, because validation is aggregate and may not hold in local high-density situations.

<![CDATA[
Three verifiable RLVR-style veto scenarios can be constructed from the paper’s operators. Each scenario specifies a counterfactual test, a falsifiable prediction, and the observable consequence.

Veto 1: Ball Flow Network aliasing or ball-tracking dropout veto.
Counterfactual: Suppose more than a pre-specified fraction of ball positions are missing or interpolated, or suppose the ball speed distribution is such that v_ball Δt exceeds a meaningful fraction of the grid cell size. Prediction: if the raw Ball Flow Network is recomputed using interpolated ball positions, geometric crossing detection, and dropout masks, the edge weights W^{BFN}_{uv} will change materially. A verifiable threshold is that at least one high-weight edge changes rank, or node retention times τ^{BFN}_u change by more than a predeclared tolerance. If this occurs, any claim about preferred ball-flow corridors is vetoed until dropout and crossing ambiguity are resolved. The source’s example uses “N=M=5” [PAGE 5], so cell size is relatively large; the veto is stronger for finer grids. The test is verifiable by comparing raw, interpolated, and geometric-crossing versions of the same match.

Veto 2: Marking Network threshold non-identifiability veto.
Counterfactual: Suppose the marking interpretation is claimed at R=1.5 m, but the ranking of marking links changes when R is perturbed to 1.8 m or 2.0 m. Prediction: because W^{MN}_{do}(R) is nondecreasing in R for each defender-opponent pair, the operator is monotone but not invariant. If the top marking pair changes under a small threshold perturbation, or if multiple defenders simultaneously mark the same opponent so that Σ_d W^{MN}_{do} exceeds the defensive-phase duration by a large margin, then the claim that a specific defender exclusively marked an attacker is vetoed. A verifiable test is to compute W^{MN}(R) across a threshold grid and examine rank stability. The source’s own example shows non-defender marking can dominate: “the link with the highest weight corresponds to the mark made by Braithwaite (Leganés) to Casemiro (Real Madrid)” [PAGE 7]. That supports the possibility that role-based interpretations are fragile.

Veto 3: Functional Coordination Network low-speed or threshold sensitivity veto.
Counterfactual: Suppose the reported defensive coordination advantage depends on including low-speed frames or on a narrow angle threshold. Introduce a velocity floor ε_v and exclude frames where either player’s speed is below ε_v. Also vary θ* around 10°. Prediction: if the defensive mean coordination falls below the offensive mean, or if the difference between defensive and offensive coordination collapses below a small effect size, then the claim that defence is inherently more coordinated is vetoed as an artifact of low-speed motion or threshold selection. A concrete verifiable prediction is that increasing ε_v will reduce the number of aligned pairs and may reduce defensive coordination more than offensive coordination if defenders are frequently slow while attackers move explosively. The source’s reported values “|Cdeffensive|=0.176 and |Coffensive|=0.152” [PAGE 8] are close, so the robustness margin is small. The veto is especially relevant because the source itself calls the velocity-alignment model “a very rough approximation” [PAGE 8] and notes that complementary coordination is missed.

These veto scenarios are not rhetorical; they are operational tests. They require recomputation under perturbed measurement assumptions and comparison against predeclared thresholds. If the operator remains stable, the network inference is robust. If it fails, the inference is vetoed.
