The paper’s core derivation is a four-link causal chain that must be audited carefully. First, player workload and injury history are mapped into a risk-factor vector \(F_t\), and an XGBoost classifier is trained with binary cross-entropy to output calibrated injury probabilities \(\Theta_{a,t}\) [PAGE 5]. Second, the chosen team \(C\) changes the distribution of injury outcomes \(\Pr(o_t|C,\Theta_t)\); the outcome \(o_t\) updates the unavailability vector \(L_t\), whose entries \(l_a\) count games unavailable [PAGE 4]. Third, availability changes the feasible action set because “A player \(a\) cannot be selected in the team \(C\) if \(l_a>0\)” [PAGE 4]. Fourth, team value \(V(C,t)=f(C,\alpha_t)\) is converted into expected points by \(V(C,t)=3\cdot\Pr(win|C)+\Pr(draw|C)\), and the manager solves a discounted Bellman problem over a 38-game season [PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. The multi-hop insight is that resting a key player today is optimal only if the immediate expected-point loss is smaller than the discounted expected future gain from reduced injury unavailability. That comparison is mediated by \(Z_t\), the remaining games, and by \(\gamma\) in \(V(s)=\max_a(R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}\Pr(s'|s,a)V(s'))\) [PAGE 8]. The gray-zone audit: the injury event is rare—about 4% in the dataset [PAGE 11]—so calibration is fragile; the physical workload features are event-data proxies rather than direct tracking measurements [PAGE 5]; injury lengths are sampled from a Gaussian then truncated at zero [PAGE 5]; the MCTS rollout assumes “The first three rollout state transitions occur normally and then the remaining games are simulated under the assumption of no further injuries” [PAGE 10]; and the objective is single-season, which the authors admit creates “a slight increase in injury risk towards the season’s end” [PAGE 15]. These are not cosmetic caveats. If the injury classifier loses its small log-loss advantage over the heuristic, the Bellman reward is driven by a misestimated \(\Theta_t\). If long-latency injuries dominate beyond three rollout transitions, the MCTS value underestimates future unavailability. If the season terminal horizon is taken literally, end-of-season matches are discounted too heavily for player welfare. The paper’s claimed gains—expected points nearly equal to greedy, squad injuries reduced by about 5%, optimal-team injuries reduced by about 13%, and standard deviation in season-expected points reduced by about 17% [PAGE 12][PAGE 2]—therefore depend on calibration, proxy validity, rollout truncation, and horizon assumptions. A rigorous audit must treat those as explicit breakdown regimes rather than hidden assumptions.

The formal objects are defined as follows. Let \(A=\{a_1,\dots,a_N\}\) be the player set and \(T=\{t_1,\dots,t_K\}\) the season games, with \(K=38\) for the English Premier League [PAGE 4]. A selected team is \(C\subseteq A\) with \(|C|=11\) [PAGE 4]. Each player has skill \(\Omega_a\in\Omega\), learned from past games; in the match model this is the player’s total VAEP, a dimensionless value unit from “Valuing Actions by Estimating Probabilities” [PAGE 7]. Each game has difficulty \(\alpha_t\in\alpha\), a constant opponent-and-venue strength term independent of \(C\) [PAGE 4]. Team value is \(V(C,t)=f(C,\alpha_t)\), with units of expected points per game [PAGE 4]. Injury probability is \(\Theta_{a,t}\in[0,1]\), a dimensionless probability, and the matrix is \(\Theta\) [PAGE 4]. The stochastic injury outcome is \(o_t\in O_t\) with distribution \(\Pr(o_t|C,\Theta_t)\) [PAGE 4]. Unavailability is \(L_t=\{l_1,\dots,l_N\}\), where \(l_a\in\mathbb{N}_0\) is the number of games unavailable starting from game \(t\); \(l_a=0\) means available, and \(l_a\) decreases by one after each game [PAGE 4]. The timestep \(\tau_t\) is measured in days, with \(\tau_0=0\) at the first game [PAGE 4]. The injury-length sampler is a Gaussian fit to observed injury lengths; negative samples are set to zero games [PAGE 5]. The reward is exactly \(V(C,t)=3\cdot\Pr(win|C)+\Pr(draw|C)\), in points, where the win probability receives three points and the draw probability one point [PAGE 7]. The match model adapts the Maher Poisson regression by replacing club identity features with player-level VAEP sums \(V_i\) and \(V_j\), and opponent strength \(\alpha_t\) is the opposition club’s average team value across past real-world games [PAGE 7]. Reported match log-loss is \(0.915\pm0.020\) for the player-level model and \(0.910\pm0.018\) for the team-level model, in log-loss information units determined by the chosen logarithm base [PAGE 7]. Injury-model log-loss is \(0.1676\pm0.0005\) for XGBoost versus \(0.1700\pm0.0002\) for the heuristic baseline [PAGE 11]. The MDP is \(G=\langle S,\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma\rangle\) with transition \(P=\Pr(s'|s,a)\) and reward \(R(s,a)\) [PAGE 8]. A state contains \(t\), \(Z_t\), \(F_t\), \(\Theta_t\), and \(L_t\), where \(Z_t\subseteq T\) is the remaining season [PAGE 8]. The Bellman equation is \(V(s)=\max_{a}(R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}\Pr(s'|s,a)V(s'))\) [PAGE 8]. The single-game constraint problem is \(\max_{C}\sum_{a\in C}\Omega_a\) subject to \(|C|=11\), \(l_a=0\) for all \(a\in C\), \(\sum_{a\in C}(X_a==GK)=1\), \(3\le\sum_{a\in C}(X_a==DEF)\le5\), \(3\le\sum_{a\in C}(X_a==MID)\le5\), and \(1\le\sum_{a\in C}(X_a==ATT)\le3\) [PAGE 9]. Exact solution is infeasible because the paper reports “up to ~2000 possible actions at each timestep and up to 38¹¹ possible new states” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes: (i) calibration breakdown when held-out injury log-loss satisfies \(L_{XGB}\ge L_{heuristic}\), or when \(\Theta_t\) is miscalibrated under prevalence shift; the claimed injury reduction is then unsupported. (ii) Proxy breakdown when event-data estimates of distance covered and dribbles fail to preserve the mapping from physical workload to \(F_t\); the paper explicitly uses “cheaper on-ball event data” plus a location-prediction model rather than direct tracking [PAGE 5]. (iii) Rollout-truncation breakdown when injuries with latency beyond three transitions are non-negligible; the rollout sets future injuries to zero after three transitions [PAGE 10]. (iv) Terminal-horizon breakdown when welfare beyond \(t_K\) matters; the paper’s own Kanté case shows risk rising near season end because long-term injuries are “less impactful as the season concludes” [PAGE 15]. (v) Formation/substitution breakdown when within-game workload management or non-preferred formations matter; the paper fixes a preferred formation and lists substitutions as future work [PAGE 9][PAGE 16]. (vi) Distributional breakdown when injury durations are heavy-tailed rather than Gaussian-truncated [PAGE 5].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is unusually legible. The match reward descends from Maher’s 1982 Poisson goal-scoring model [PAGE 17], with player-level VAEP replacing club identity [PAGE 7][PAGE 18]. The injury model descends from workload and injury-history epidemiology: the acute:chronic workload ratio literature [3], previous-injury risk [4], and weather-condition risk [5] are explicitly cited [PAGE 2][PAGE 17]. The planning layer descends from MCTS and UCB1 bandit search [20], progressive widening [21,22], AlphaGo-style tree search [18], and coalition/team-formation work including robust and recoverable team formation [10,11][PAGE 9][PAGE 18]. The explainability layer uses SHAP [14], and the physical-metric layer uses a graph-neural-network plus LSTM player-location imputation model [13][PAGE 5]. The “retrocausal” character comes from the Bellman backup: future fixtures \(Z_t\), future injury probabilities, and the terminal state \(t_K\) are made present in the current team choice through discounted expected value [PAGE 8]. But that retrocausality is bounded by the single-season terminal condition. The model can rationally rest a key player before a congested run, yet near the season’s end it rationally takes more risk because future unavailability has little time to bite; the paper reports exactly this “slight increase in injury risk towards the season’s end” [PAGE 15]. Multi-week traps follow. First, a manager who optimizes only the next game follows the greedy policy, and the paper finds real managers “closely mirror our greedy model” [PAGE 12]. Second, a congested cup run or tournament schedule inflates injury risk; the high-risk multiplier experiment shows MCTS remains better in mean and lower-quartile performance, but “As the injury probability rises, the injury reduction margin compared to the greedy strategy narrows” [PAGE 13]. Third, long-latency injuries can escape the three-transition rollout and make the planner overconfident. Fourth, cross-season welfare is invisible to a single-season terminal reward. Fifth, proxy drift from event data to tracking data can silently invalidate the workload features. These traps are multi-week because they compound across fixture congestion, recovery windows, and injury cascades rather than appearing in one match.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The manager is modeled as an agent who must recursively represent three minds: the opponent’s strength \(\alpha_t\), the injury environment’s stochastic response \(\Pr(o_t|C,\Theta_t)\), and the future self who will inherit the updated \(L_t\) and \(\Theta_t\) [PAGE 4][PAGE 8]. Crisis habit regression occurs when the manager collapses this recursive representation into a myopic best response: choose the team maximizing \(V(C,t)\) today and ignore the transition kernel. The paper’s behavioral evidence is that human expert selections “closely mirror our greedy model,” indicating “a widespread managerial inclination toward prioritizing short-term results” [PAGE 12]. That is the regression: under high-pressure match cycles, the manager stops modeling the future injury state and optimizes the visible next-game reward. The counter-trap is MCTS: it deliberately accepts a near-zero expected-point difference of “-0.1% ± 0.3” while reducing optimal-team injuries “by around 13% on average” [PAGE 12]. It does so by resting key players at moments when their marginal immediate value is low relative to their future availability value—an explicitly recursive move because it prices the manager’s future action set. The clock is the season timestep and terminal horizon: \(\tau_t\) is in days, \(Z_t\) shrinks, and \(t_K\) is terminal [PAGE 4][PAGE 8]. The clock creates a predictable end-of-season bias: the same recursive manager who rests a player in midseason may play that player near the finale because future unavailability has little remaining season to affect [PAGE 15]. In high-risk regimes, the counter-trap can fail because frequent injuries remove the proactive option set; the paper notes that the injury-reduction margin narrows as injury probability rises [PAGE 13]. Thus the recursive theory of mind is not a personality claim; it is an operational claim: the manager must model the future self’s feasible actions, the environment’s injury response, and the clock’s remaining horizon. When any of those representations collapses—especially under crisis pressure—the policy reverts to greedy selection.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto must be adjudicated on the paper’s own evidence, not on an invented biomechanical ratio. The physical layer is represented by acute workload, number of past injuries, career total days injured, and distance covered; the paper reports mean absolute SHAP values of \(0.0033\), \(0.0029\), \(0.0023\), and \(0.0022\), respectively [PAGE 6]. Those are probability-scale contributions from the injury model, not SI force units. The measurement layer is a proxy: “Computing physical metrics that are used to calculate fatigue-related features typically requires expensive tracking systems,” so the authors estimate physical metrics from “cheaper on-ball event data” and a player-location prediction model [PAGE 5]. The weather layer is acknowledged only by citation: prior work links injury risk to “weather conditions [5]” [PAGE 2][PAGE 17]. The paper does not train a direct weather variable in the final feature set; it states that team form, days since the previous game, and other physical features were curated but removed when they had minimal performance impact [PAGE 6]. The biomechanical layer is therefore indirect at best. Veto proof: let \(Z\) be a stratum for weather, surface, or biomechanical load. If \(\Pr(y=1|F_t,Z)\ne\Pr(y=1|F_t)\), then the classifier’s \(\Theta_t\) is not conditionally calibrated for that stratum, and the Bellman reward inherits the bias through \(\Pr(o_t|C,\Theta_t)\). A policy that reduces injuries in the training distribution cannot be guaranteed to reduce them in the shifted stratum. The practical veto is exact: if the event-data proxy fails to preserve the rank or calibration of the physical workload features under weather or biomechanical stress, the MCTS injury-reduction claim is not transportable. The paper’s own caveats support this: it removed features with minimal impact [PAGE 6], it relies on an imputation model for location [PAGE 5], and it treats the model as adaptable when clubs have “more detailed health and training data” [PAGE 7]. The cross-layer adjudication therefore is not “weather always vetoes”; it is “unmodeled weather or biomechanical effect that changes \(\Pr(y|F)\) vetoes the calibrated injury probability, and therefore vetoes the claimed optimality of the rested team.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: injury-calibration veto. Verifiable test: on a held-out season, compare the XGBoost injury log-loss to the heuristic baseline. The paper reports \(0.1676\pm0.0005\) versus \(0.1700\pm0.0002\) [PAGE 11]. Veto trigger: if \(L_{XGB}\ge L_{heuristic}\) or if calibration slope is outside a pre-registered band around 1, then the \(\Theta_t\) input to the MDP is not demonstrably better than the heuristic. Prediction: the MCTS injury-reduction advantage over greedy should collapse toward zero, while expected-point parity may remain; the claimed ~13% optimal-team injury reduction [PAGE 12] should not replicate. Scenario 2: manager-greedy similarity veto. Verifiable test: using real team states, compute average shared-player similarity between manager selections and greedy selections versus manager selections and MCTS selections, as in the paper’s Experiment 2 [PAGE 11][PAGE 12]. Veto trigger: if similarity(manager, greedy) is not greater than similarity(manager, MCTS), the premise that managers exhibit crisis habit regression toward greedy selection is unsupported. Prediction: the behavioral justification for using greedy as the real-world benchmark weakens, and the MCTS advantage in a new league should shrink or reverse if managers are already forward-looking. Scenario 3: high-risk crossover veto. Verifiable test: sweep an injury-risk multiplier \(m\), measure \(\Delta Points=E[MCTS]-E[Greedy]\), \(\Delta Inj=Inj[MCTS]-Inj[Greedy]\) for the optimal team, and lower-quartile points. Veto trigger: if at any tested \(m\) the MCTS lower quartile is below greedy, or if \(\Delta Inj\ge0\) for optimal-team injuries, the resilience claim is vetoed. Prediction: the paper’s observed narrowing of the injury-reduction margin as injury probability rises [PAGE 13] implies a crossover; beyond that crossover, MCTS should lose its injury-mitigation advantage because frequent injuries remove the proactive rest option set. A fourth audit, useful as a robustness check, is the rollout-horizon veto: if the injury-length distribution has non-negligible mass beyond three games, the three-transition no-further-injury assumption [PAGE 10] should cause MCTS to overvalue immediate availability and the predicted injury reduction should attenuate in long-injury regimes.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
