<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of Tolman’s “means-end-field” stretches back to Gestalt psychology’s emphasis on holistic perception and forward-looking teleology, while also reacting against the dominant S-R behaviorism of Watson and early Skinner. Historically, Thorndike’s law of effect (1898) established that connections between stimuli and responses are strengthened by satisfaction, laying groundwork for trial-and-error learning. However, Thorndike did not account for sudden insight or reorganization of behavior, phenomena Tolman sought to explain. Köhler’s work with chimpanzees (1917) demonstrated insightful problem-solving, challenging pure associationism. Tolman synthesized these streams by proposing that organisms form expectations about the structure of their environment—the “cognitive map”.

Scientifically, the genealogy connects to modern developments in neuroscience and AI. O’Keefe and Morris (1978) discovered place cells in the hippocampus, providing a neural substrate for spatial mapping. Later, Hafting et al. (2005) found grid cells, offering a coordinate system for navigation. These findings validate Tolman’s intuition that animals maintain internal representations of space that go beyond stimulus-response chains. In AI, the concept of “options” ( Sutton & Precup, 1995) formalizes temporally extended actions, enabling hierarchical planning. More recently, world models ( Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018 ) learn compact representations of environment dynamics to facilitate planning, echoing Tolman’s idea of integrating partial experiences into a coherent whole.

The multi-week trap refers to the prolonged controversy over whether rats truly possess cognitive maps or merely use proximal cues ( Odour, etc.). Critics argued that simpler mechanisms like odor trails or egocentric vectors could explain performance. Tolman’s experiments were designed to rule out such alternatives—for instance, by using novel starting positions and blocking direct routes. Nevertheless, debates persisted for decades, influencing experimental designs in comparative psychology. Today, with advanced imaging techniques, we can directly observe hippocampal activity during navigation, largely confirming the existence of allocentric spatial representations. However, the exact format of these representations—whether they are Euclidean metrics, graph structures, or something more abstract—remains an active area of research, illustrating how foundational questions continue to evolve.
