If indoor preparation begins too early in winter, athletes risk staleness before the season; if it begins too late, the window W is too short to reach L* [from SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS]. The optimal phase φ* is singularly sensitive to the regional climate gradient ΔT = T_south − T_north. Because northern colleges cannot equal ΔT by travel, they are structurally forced to operate near the L* boundary, where small errors in load prescription produce large performance decrements.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap structure, and the season clock.

Under crisis—early-season losses, poor fielding, or a coach’s fear that the nine is “not in condition”—the decision-maker regresses to the most legible heuristic available. On [PAGE 194], Camp’s prescription (“systematic and well-directed use” of apparatus) is legible because it is visible, traditional, and equipment-based. The coach’s theory of mind models the players as responsive to apparatus exposure: more cage time, more dumb-bells, more Indian-clubs, more rope work. This is crisis habit regression: under uncertainty, revert to the institutional ancestor’s rule.

The recursion operates as follows:
1. Coach believes players believe apparatus causes readiness.
2. Coach increases apparatus load L_indoor.
3. Players accumulate mechanical load without corresponding skill transfer (η ≈ 0 or L_indoor > L*).
4. Coach observes fatigue, soreness, or stagnant performance.
5. Coach misattributes the stagnation to insufficient apparatus or insufficient player effort rather than to exceeded recovery capacity R or negative transfer.
6. Coach increases L_indoor further.

The fixed point of this recursion is the overtraining singularity L > L*, where additional indoor work reduces P_field. Because the coach cannot observe η directly (no throwing velocity, no exit velocity, no injury-rate telemetry is described in the source), the loop is not externally damped.

Counter-trap: Treat the coach’s own belief state as a controlled variable. Require a verification interval Δt_verify (e.g., 14 days) during which transfer is measured by field-relevant outputs—throwing velocity [m/s], batting exit speed [m/s], or fielding success rate [dimensionless]—before any escalation of apparatus load. If no statistically detectable transfer is observed, the apparatus regimen is vetoed and replaced by specific skill work outdoors or by a taper.

The clock: Let t = 0 be the first indoor practice and T_game be the first contest. The preparation window W = T_game − t is an absorbing boundary. The marginal value of indoor load MV(L) = η − 2κL declines as L approaches L*. As W → 0, coaches face pressure to raise L toward L*, but the risk of crossing into L > L* rises nonlinearly. The clock singularity occurs at W_crit where the expected value of additional indoor load becomes negative: any further apparatus session reduces E[P_field] because fatigue accumulates faster than skill can be transferred before opening day. At W_crit, the rational action is to stop indoor loading entirely, regardless of how “systematic” it was intended to be [PAGE 194].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto layers adjudicated against the source.

Physical veto (structural/environmental). [PAGE 194] describes “a rope fastened to the ceiling or a beam” and “another rope, on which a heavy ‘spool’ slides freely, stretched from a point about the height of a man’s shoulder.” If ceiling height h < h_min (approximately 2.4–2.6 m for a full overhead Indian-club arc or medicine-ball trajectory), the exercise is mechanically impossible and is vetoed. Beam load capacity C_beam [N] must satisfy C_beam > m_player · (g + a_max), where g = 9.81 m/s² and a_max is peak upward acceleration [m/s²]. Rope tension τ_rope [N] must remain below the ultimate tensile strength τ_ult [N]; the structural breakdown regime is τ_rope ≥ τ_ult, risking catastrophic failure.

Biomechanical veto (throwing/batting mechanics). The cage is “completely bounded by two walls and a wire netting” [PAGE 194]. Lateral confinement w_cage alters the throwing follow-through and batting swing plane. Elbow valgus torque during throwing is τ_valgus = F_ball · r_forearm [N·m], where F_ball is ball release force [N] and r_forearm is the moment arm [m]. If w_cage is less than the width required for full external rotation and deceleration, the arm’s deceleration path shortens, increasing τ_valgus and ulnar collateral ligament strain ε_UCL. The biomechanical breakdown regime is ε_UCL ≥ ε_fail (tissue failure strain). Veto rule: maximal-effort pitching or batting inside a cage with w_cage < 10–12 m is prohibited; substitute flat-ground throwing, medicine-ball rotational work, or outdoor field access.

Weather veto. [PAGE 194] positions indoor gymnasium work as the alternative to outdoor winter and early spring practice. Ambient temperature T_amb [°C] below a safe threshold (approximately 5–10 °C for bare-handed throwing and batting) increases soft-tissue viscosity and reduces grip, vetoing outdoor work. Professionals avoid this by traveling to Southern cities [PAGE 194]; college teams cannot. Indoor substitution must then replicate aerodynamic conditions: air density ρ_indoor ≈ 1.2 kg/m³ lacks outdoor wind velocity v_wind [m/s] and thermal gradients. The environmental transfer deficit is ΔP_env = k_ρ(ρ_out − ρ_indoor) + k_v·v_wind [field-performance units]. If ΔP_env exceeds the team’s tolerance and no outdoor acclimatization block is scheduled before T_game, the indoor-only regimen is vetoed.

Adjudication hierarchy. Safety vetoes (structural failure, tissue strain, thermal risk) are hard constraints and override performance aspirations. An apparatus may be used in the gymnasium only if it passes all three layers simultaneously. If any layer fails, the prescribed exercise is replaced by a lower-risk surrogate (e.g., medicine ball instead of overhead club; plyometrics instead of rope spool; outdoor travel if financially and academically feasible). No “systematic and well-directed” use [PAGE 194] can justify an exercise that violates a hard physical or biomechanical boundary.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios with explicit predictions. Each is falsifiable and tied to the formalism in SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS.

Scenario 1 — Ceiling-height veto.
Counterfactual: Gymnasium ceiling height h < 2.4 m, making full-overhead Indian-club and ceiling-rope spool exercises physically impossible [PAGE 194]. Veto rule: Overhead apparatus exercises are prohibited; substitute shoulder work at or below shoulder height. Prediction: After six weeks, there is no statistically significant difference in shoulder power output [W] between the vetoed group and a matched control using full-height facilities (repeated-measures ANOVA, p > 0.05), despite equal session attendance [dimensionless count]. Verifiable via force plate and motion-capture measurement of peak shoulder power.

Scenario 2 — Cage-width biomechanical veto.
Counterfactual: Indoor cage width w_cage < 12 m, insufficient for full baseball-throw follow-through [PAGE 194]. Veto rule: Maximal-effort pitching inside the narrow cage is vetoed; pitchers use flat-ground or outdoor throwing. Prediction: Pitchers restricted to the narrow cage exhibit mean elbow valgus torque τ_valgus > 60 N·m and pre-season fastball velocity below baseline by at least 0.9 m/s, measured by radar gun and dynamometer. Verifiable via biomechanical dynamometry and radar-gun velocity logs over a four-week pre-season block.

Scenario 3 — Overtraining singularity veto.
Counterfactual: Indoor load L_indoor exceeds L* = η/(2κ) during the final 14 days before opening day [SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS]. Veto rule: Mandatory taper; apparatus volume reduced by at least 50% in the final two weeks. Prediction: Teams that maintain high cage volume into the final week show a team batting average [dimensionless 0–1] at least 0.020 lower in the first five games and at least two additional days-lost-to-injury per team compared with a tapered control group. Verifiable via official game statistics and athletic-trainer injury logs. If the prediction fails (no performance decrement or injury increase), the veto rule is falsified and L* must be re-estimated.
