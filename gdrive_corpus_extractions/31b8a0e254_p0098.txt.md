Singularity/breakdown regimes: corner solution x_i=0 invalidates the interior FOC; indivisibility makes x_i integer-valued so demand is a step function and the derivative does not exist except at jumps; transaction costs \tau > 0 can exceed gains from reallocation, so no trade occurs at the predicted price; Knightian uncertainty about p or \bar X means no well-defined expected-demand schedule can be constructed from “given” prices.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight’s 1921 text sits in the marginalist lineage of Jevons, Menger, and Walras, but the specific page ([PAGE 83]) anticipates the ordinalist revolution without completing it. The footnote’s distinction between two demand propositions that “are often treated as equivalent” is a pre-Slutsky intuition of the income/substitution split: Slutsky’s 1915 formulation was not widely available in English until Hicks and Allen (1934), and Hicks’s compensated demand and Samuelson’s revealed preference (1938) formalized what Knight only argues verbally. Arrow-Debreu (1954) later supplies the general-equilibrium completion of Knight’s “fixed amount … sold at one price” institution, but only under complete markets, convexity, and probabilistic risk—conditions Knight’s broader book famously limits through the risk/uncertainty distinction.

Multi-week traps arise when this page is applied mechanically to time-series or panel data. In any given week after a relative price change, nominal income m_i is fixed but real income moves; estimating a Marshallian elasticity while assuming the two propositions are equivalent will misattribute quantity changes entirely to price, ignoring the -x_i(\partial x_i/\partial m_i) income term. In weeks following a staple-price shock, very low-income households may exhibit Giffen behavior, so a pooled weekly regression can deliver a positive slope even though each individual’s compensated demand is downward-sloping. For perishable goods with fixed weekly supply \bar X, prices can swing sharply week to week not because of speculative “bubbles” but because a vertical supply curve intersecting a shifting demand curve generates large p^* movements from small demand shocks. A citation trap is to treat Knight’s [PAGE 83] law of demand as universal; the footnote explicitly blocks that inference, and later theory identifies inferior goods, corner solutions, and non-convexities as regimes where the simple monotone schedule fails.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Agent i’s demand derivation presumes that every other agent j is simultaneously solving the same marginal utility condition and that all take prices as given. The single-price market institution ([PAGE 83]) is therefore a recursive expectations equilibrium: each individual reveals a quantity at price p^* only because they expect all others to face the same p^* and to obey the same law of choice. Under crisis conditions associated with Knightian uncertainty—where the probability distribution of prices or supply is not known—this recursion regresses to habit. Consumers stop re-solving MRS_{xy}=p_x/p_y after every relative price change and fall back on consumption routines (e.g., buying the same staple quantity regardless of price), which invalidates the smooth comparative-statics chain on [PAGE 83].

The counter-trap is policy or market design that treats the aggregate demand schedule as stable and imposes a single administered price. If the administered price diverges from the market-clearing p^*, agents regress to non-price allocation mechanisms: queuing, rationing by personal connection, favor-trading, and parallel markets. The institution “must all be sold at one price” then fails descriptively because multiple prices emerge legally and illegally, even though the formal model admits only one scalar p^*.

The clock dimension fixes the regime’s duration. Knight’s “fixed amount … in any given short space of time” defines a narrow window \Delta t within which supply is inelastic and the static schedule is meaningful. As the clock extends, supply becomes elastic through storage, imports, entry, or reduced waste; habits adjust; income changes alter m_i; and the demand curve X^d(p;\mathbf{m}) shifts. Thus the theory of market price on [PAGE 83] is a short-run, fixed-clock construct, not a timeless law; recursive out-of-equilibrium thinking collapses once \Delta t elapses and agents revise both their own plans and their beliefs about others’ plans.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: the theory assumes a fixed quantity of physical units \bar X available in a short time and “must all be sold at one price” ([PAGE 83]). If the good is storable, intertemporal arbitrage creates a price path p_t rather than one cross-sectional price, vetoing the single-price assumption. If the good is indivisible (e.g., one unit of a durable), demand is a step function in physical units and the marginal calculus breaks down. If the good is perishable, unsold stock is destroyed rather than carried forward, so excess supply cannot be reallocated across time and the clearing price becomes highly sensitive to the exact level of \bar X.

Biomechanical layer veto: the footnote proof on [PAGE 83] requires the consumer to detect that a utility ratio is “upset” and to divert resources accordingly. This presumes deliberate, continuous optimization and negligible cognitive or transaction cost. Bounded rationality, search costs, and the complexity of comparing many goods mean the marginal utility ratio is not computed for every price change; agents use heuristics, so the predicted reallocation from x to y does not occur. When transaction costs \tau exceed the utility gain from re-trading, no exchange happens at the theoretically predicted price, and the demand schedule is not revealed.

Weather layer veto: supply and demand shocks are treated as given in the text, but in practice \bar X is weather-sensitive (harvest, transport disruption) and preferences shift with environmental conditions. Write \bar X_t = \bar X + \xi_t^{weather}. If \xi_t is not insurable or probabilifiable—Knightian uncertainty rather than measurable risk—then prices cannot be treated as exogenously “given,” and no stable market-price scale can be inferred from individual choice alone. Formal veto condition: the model holds only if (i) preferences are convex and interior, (ii) \bar X is known and fixed over \Delta t, (iii) \tau \approx 0, and (iv) shocks are subject to a known probability measure (risk). Violation of any layer vetoes the clean single-price equilibrium on [PAGE 83].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto Scenario 1 — Giffen staple under a controlled price increase. Recruit a sample of very low-income consumers for whom a staple (e.g., rice) accounts for budget share s above a critical threshold s^*. Hold all other prices and nominal income m_i constant, then raise the staple price p_s in small increments. Verifiable prediction under the veto: the Marshallian own-price elasticity \eta_s = (p_s/x_s)(\partial x_s/\partial p_s) becomes positive for income below a critical m^*; quantity demanded increases as price rises. Null hypothesis inherited from a naive reading of [PAGE 83]: \eta_s \le 0 for all income levels. Decision rule: weekly purchase records showing \eta_s > 0 in the low-income band falsify the simple downward-sloping demand claim; compensated (Hicksian) elasticity may remain negative, confirming the Slutsky decomposition rather than Knight’s unqualified proposition.

Veto Scenario 2 — Binding price ceiling on a fixed perishable supply. Consider a market with fixed short-run supply \bar X of a perishable good and impose a legal ceiling p_c below the theoretical clearing price p^*. The [PAGE 83] institution predicts all \bar X units are sold at the single price p_c. Verifiable veto prediction: legal quantity transacted q_c satisfies q_c < \bar X; unsold or wasted stock \bar X - q_c > 0; excess demand X^d(p_c) - \bar X > 0; parallel-market price p_b exceeds p_c; and non-price rationing (queues, allocation by connection) is observed. Decision rule: market inspection or retailer ledger data showing unsold perishable stock coexisting with excess demand at p_c falsifies the “must all be sold at one price” clearing prediction.

Veto Scenario 3 — Indivisible durable good with corner solutions. Sell an indivisible unit (e.g., a single appliance) to individual consumers whose reservation prices are heterogeneous. Vary the posted price in fine grid increments over an interval [p_l,p_u]. Verifiable veto prediction: aggregate quantity demanded is a step function—constant over sub-intervals and dropping discretely at individual reservation prices—so dq/dp = 0 almost everywhere and undefined at jump points; the smooth marginal reallocation proof on [PAGE 83] has no empirical counterpart. Decision rule: individual purchase logs showing no quantity response to price changes within [p_l,p_u], followed by a discrete collapse to zero at a threshold, falsify the continuous downward-sloping individual demand schedule assumed by the text.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
