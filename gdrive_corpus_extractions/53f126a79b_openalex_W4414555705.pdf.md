**Operator O1: Bayesian information integration.** Let \(s_t\) be the latent game state at time \(t\), \(c_t\) the contextual cue vector, \(k_t\) the kinematic cue vector, and \(I_{1:t-1}\) prior task knowledge. A conditional-independence formalization consistent with the paper is
\[
p(s_t \mid c_t,k_t,I_{1:t-1})
\;\propto\;
p(s_t\mid I_{1:t-1})\,p(c_t\mid s_t)\,p(k_t\mid s_t),
\]
where probabilities are dimensionless, \(s_t\) is in state-space units, and \(c_t,k_t\) are in cue-specific units. The paper’s own terms are “likelihood,” “prior,” “joint probability distribution,” and “posterior” [PAGE 9]. For Gaussian cue models, precision weighting is
\[
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^2}
=
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{prior}}^2}
+
\frac{1}{\sigma_c^2}
+
\frac{1}{\sigma_k^2},
\qquad
w_i=
\frac{1/\sigma_i^2}{\sum_{j\in\{0,c,k\}}1/\sigma_j^2},
\]
with \(\sigma_i^2\) in squared state-variable units and \(w_i\) dimensionless. Assumptions: conditionally independent cues, Gaussian or log-concave likelihoods, no deception, and no saccadic sampling cost. Breakdown regimes: if \(\sigma_c^2\to\infty\), then \(w_c\to0\) and kinematic information dominates; if \(\sigma_k^2\to\infty\), prior/contextual information dominates; if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{prior}}^2\to0\), the player is prior-dogmatic and ignores sensory evidence; if all variances diverge, the posterior is flat and the decision is singular/undefined. This matches the report that “the reliance on contextual information becomes dominant before or in early stages of a movement execution. Then, when actions unfold, kinematic information is weighed more heavily” [PAGE 2], and the player report that against a random opponent “it’s definitely movement-specific” [PAGE 7].

**Operator O2: Gaze-policy selection.** Let \(g\in\{\text{foveal spot},\text{gaze anchor},\text{visual pivot}\}\), \(\tau\) be available time in seconds, \(\rho\) be decisional relevance on a dimensionless utility/importance scale, \(N_s(g)\) the number of saccades, and \(\lambda_s\) the saccade cost in utility units. A source-consistent policy is
\[
g^\star=
\arg\max_{g}
\left[
\mathbb{E}\{U(d)\mid g,\tau,\rho\}
-
\lambda_s N_s(g)
\right].
\]
Assumptions: gaze strategy is selected to improve decision utility; saccades suppress some information processing; \(\tau\) and \(\rho\) are exogenous task conditions. The source gives the mapping: visual pivot is usable only when “time pressure and decisional relevance are rather low” [PAGE 8]; “a gaze anchor is used under high time pressure and low decisional relevance, and a foveal spot is needed under high time pressure and high decisional relevance” [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\lambda_s\to\infty\), visual pivot is vetoed and anchor/foveal strategies dominate; if \(\tau\to0\), only low-saccade strategies are feasible; if \(\rho\to1\) and fine detail is required, foveal spot is favored even at the cost of peripheral breadth; if \(\lambda_s\to0\) and attention is broad, visual pivot may become viable. The paper’s physical rationale is explicit: “each saccade involves costs (e.g., the suppression of information processing)” [PAGE 9]. The player quote on broad peripheral positioning supports the foveal/anchor logic: “the more directly I look at the goal, the more overview I have of what is happening on the left and right” [PAGE 5].

**Operator O3: Decision-mode threshold.** Let \(D\in\{\text{intuitive},\text{elaborated}\}\), \(\tau\) be decision time, \(\tau_c\) the individual/task threshold, and \(F\in\{0,1\}\) familiarity/typicality. A threshold formalization consistent with the paper is
\[
D=
\begin{cases}
\text{intuitive}, & \tau\le\tau_c \ \text{or}\ F=1,\\
\text{elaborated}, & \tau>\tau_c \ \text{and}\ F=0.
\end{cases}
\]
Units: \(\tau,\tau_c\) in seconds; \(F\) dimensionless. Assumptions: familiar and high-time-demand situations trigger learned if-then automatisms; atypical and lower-time-pressure situations allow conscious elaboration. The source states: “while a familiar environment or high time demands evoke an intuitive response, a consciously elaborated process is afforded in atypical environments and less time pressure” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\tau_c\to0\), almost all decisions become elaborated; if \(\tau_c\to\infty\), almost all become intuitive; if \(F\) is misclassified, the threshold prediction fails. The paper also notes that intuitive decisions “rely partially on unconscious if-then automatism” developed through repeated experience [PAGE 7] and that players prefer “only a few options” for efficient decisions [PAGE 8].

**Operator O4: Risk/utility adjudication.** Let \(a\in A\) be an action, \(V(a)\) its outcome value, \(R(a)\) its risk, and \(C\) confidence. A formal decision-value expression is
\[
a^\star=
\arg\max_{a\in A}
\left[
\mathbb{E}\{V(a)\mid I\}
-
\lambda_R R(a)
+
\lambda_C C
\right],
\]
with \(V,R,C\) in utility units and \(\lambda_R,\lambda_C\) dimensionless trade-off parameters. Assumptions: risk and confidence are separable influences; utility is comparable across actions. The paper reports that players attempt an “optimal trade-off between outcome-related costs and rewards and thus maximizing the utility of the decision outcome” [PAGE 10]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\lambda_R\) is undefined or unstable, risk management cannot be modeled; if confidence is uncorrelated with actual skill, the confidence term becomes miscalibrated; if tactical constraints dominate, the unconstrained argmax is invalid. The source also cites “game management” and “confidence” as decision determinants [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs through at least four scientific strands. First, expert-performance and information-processing traditions: the introduction cites talent identification, information processing in motor skills, tactical development, and anticipation/decision making [PAGE 1][PAGE 11]. Second, gaze and peripheral-vision research: foveal spot, gaze anchor, and visual pivot are inherited from Vater and colleagues, with earlier expert-novice gaze work [PAGE 2][PAGE 11]. Third, Bayesian integration: the authors explicitly connect the players’ reports to Gredin et al., Körding, Tassinari et al., Helm et al., and Runswick et al. [PAGE 2][PAGE 11]. Fourth, dual-process and option-generation accounts: Kahneman and Klein, Ashford et al., Johnson and Raab, Cisek’s affordance competition, and Bieleke et al. on if-then planning [PAGE 11].

A retrocausal reading is useful: later formal ideas—precision weighting, affordance competition, if-then planning—are read back into earlier qualitative reports. The paper’s own retrocausal move is to reinterpret “less-is-more” option reduction through Bayesian integration rather than pure option elimination: “we prefer to link this finding to the Bayesian integration framework, which suggests that prior knowledge shapes the decision in the most likely direction without eliminating other actions” [PAGE 10]. That is a scholarly re-description, not a new experiment.

Multi-week traps: (i) treating “visual pivot” as a universal expert ideal, when the paper says it is usable only when “time pressure and decisional relevance are rather low” [PAGE 8] and that saccades carry suppression costs [PAGE 9]; (ii) overgeneralizing external/weather information, because “except for the two participants in beach volleyball” the players did not mention external information [PAGE 9]; (iii) confusing self-reported “feeling” for gaze strategy with eye-tracked fixation data, since players “struggled to explicitly define the type” [PAGE 5]; (iv) treating “intuition” as no information processing, when the paper ties it to if-then automatism and prior knowledge [PAGE 7]; (v) overreading the sample as universal, despite the Swiss cultural-training caveat and small-sample limitation [PAGE 10].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three mechanisms must be separated. **Crisis habit regression** occurs when time pressure is high and the player falls back to dominant if-then automatisms: “when the reaction time is short, or the perception time … is short … I believe then it is intuition” [PAGE 7]. Habit regression is not random; it is a low-dimensional policy selected by prior reinforcement. It predicts gaze anchor when relevance is low and foveal spot when relevance is high, because both avoid costly saccades [PAGE 9]. Its failure mode is predictability: the opponent can learn the player’s default if-then mapping.

**Counter-trap** occurs when the opponent exploits that predictability. The player must then use kinematic information as a “control mechanism” to check whether the anticipated state actually occurred: “I assume that, but I don’t know for sure yet if that will happen 100%. That’s why I still have to check if the opponent has indeed reacted that way” [PAGE 5]. In recursive terms, Player A holds a prior over Player B’s preference, but B may violate that preference; A therefore monitors B’s movement and updates the prior. The source supports this with “confirm, update, or override previous estimations” [PAGE 2] and with Player 2’s shift from game-specific prior to movement-specific reaction against an unknown opponent [PAGE 7]. A counter-trap succeeds when the kinematic cue contradicts the prior strongly enough that the posterior flips.

**Clock** is the exogenous timing variable that selects between habit and deliberation. Under a short clock, recursive deliberation is too costly; under a long clock, elaborated if-then planning is possible: “I often prepare a plan in my head and can then decide at short notice whether it makes the most sense” [PAGE 7]. The recursive theory of mind is therefore clock-bounded: with little time, the player cannot run a deep opponent model and must rely on a shallow prior-plus-cue check; with more time, the player can represent the opponent’s likely counter-move and prepare multiple branches. This is consistent with the paper’s claim that tactics “scaffold the game” [PAGE 2] and that players prefer a few options for efficient decisions [PAGE 8]. The gray-zone warning is that the paper does not measure recursive mentalizing directly; the inference is a theoretical reconstruction from reports about anticipation, checking, and adaptation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical veto.** The physical layer vetoes any claim that visual pivot is generally superior. The source states that visual pivot is usable only when time pressure and decisional relevance are low [PAGE 8] and that “each saccade involves costs (e.g., the suppression of information processing)” [PAGE 9]. Therefore, under high time pressure, a high-saccade strategy is physically dominated by gaze anchor or foveal spot. This is a veto proof by cost, not by preference.

**Biomechanical veto.** The biomechanical layer can veto a purely contextual prior. The paper defines kinematic information as equipment, ball flight, postural cues, and body motion [PAGE 2], and players report using “movement and postural cues from their opponents” [PAGE 6]. Kinematic information is said to “confirm, update, or override previous estimations” [PAGE 2]. Thus if a postural cue contradicts the contextual prior, the biomechanical evidence can override the prior. The player quote on run-up direction, speed, and upper-body movement supports this layer [PAGE 6].

**Weather veto.** Weather is a sport-specific external-information layer. The paper defines external contextual information to include “weather conditions” [PAGE 2], but reports that “except for the two participants in beach volleyball” the players did not mention external information [PAGE 9]. Therefore, weather can be a legitimate veto layer only where the sport exposes athletes to it—especially beach volleyball—and it is not a universal veto across indoor sports. A model that assigns weather a large weight in floorball, handball, ice hockey, or soccer would be cross-layer overreach.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto scenario 1: Gaze-mode counterfactual.** Manipulate \(\tau\) and \(\rho\) in an eye-tracking task: high time pressure/low relevance, high time pressure/high relevance, and low time pressure/low relevance. Verifiable predictions from the source: high time pressure/low relevance should produce gaze anchor; high time pressure/high relevance should produce foveal spot; visual pivot should be rare or absent under high time pressure, and should appear mainly in low-time/low-relevance conditions [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. Veto condition: if visual pivot is frequent and performance-superior under high time pressure/high relevance, the gaze-policy operator is falsified.

**Veto scenario 2: Contextual-reliability counterfactual.** Compare a known opponent with a stable preference against a random/unknown opponent. Verifiable prediction: with reliable contextual information, early action tendencies should follow the prior; with unreliable contextual information, reliance should shift toward kinematic information, as in Player 2’s report that against a random player “it’s definitely movement-specific because then you just have to react to what is happening” [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if players remain context-dominant when the contextual cue is explicitly unreliable, the Bayesian precision-weighting operator is vetoed.

**Veto scenario 3: Decision-clock counterfactual.** Manipulate time pressure and familiarity in a video or in-situ decision task. Verifiable prediction: short clock plus familiar patterns should yield fast intuitive/if-then responses; long clock plus atypical patterns should yield elaborated planning and more verbalized alternatives [PAGE 2][PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if high time pressure produces deliberative verbal reports with no loss of speed, or if ample time in a novel situation produces automatic if-then decisions, the threshold decision-mode operator is vetoed. A secondary measurable prediction is that tactical instructions will shift the prior, while kinematic cues will shift the likelihood; if instructions change only verbal reports and not gaze or movement, the proposed integration layer is not supported [PAGE 7][PAGE 8].
