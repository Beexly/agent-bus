The source’s central multi-hop claim is: team-sport players occupy action-specific functions; those functions can be represented as latent roles; roles can be ordered; ordered roles can be wired into a graph; role-role edges can then forecast future trajectories. The abstract states the hypothesis directly: “each participant has a specific function in each action” and “role-based interaction is critical for predicting players’ future moves” [PAGE 1]. The model chain is OrderNN → differentiable ranking/re-shuffling → RoleGCN → TCN decoder. The paper’s problem formalization defines player positions as \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times p}\), motion history as \(X_{\mathrm{in}}=[X_1,\ldots,X_T]\), and target as \(X_{\mathrm{out}}=[X_{T+1},\ldots,X_{T+K}]\) [PAGE 4]. The graph is then redefined over roles, not players: \(G=(V,E)\), with “the nodes indicate the roles of each player” and “the edges capture the interaction among roles” [PAGE 4]. The key learned object is a spatio-temporal adjacency \(A_{st}\in\mathbb{R}^{RT\times RT}\), factorized as \(A_{st}=A_sA_t\) and used in a separable graph convolution [PAGE 6].

The first gray-zone is the role notation. The paper says “From the OrderNN, we will consider \(R\), the role vector, instead of \(P\), the position vector” and also says “The graph \(G\) has \(|V|=T\times R\) nodes, which represent all \(R\) roles across \(T\) observed time frames” [PAGE 4]. Thus \(R\) is used both as a role vector and as a role count. That is a formal ambiguity: a vector cannot be the cardinality multiplier in \(|V|=T\times R\) unless \(R\) is reinterpreted as a scalar number of roles. A careful implementation must separate \(N_r\) (number of roles) from \(\mathbf{r}_p\) (role embedding for player \(p\)).

The second gray-zone is the metric dimension. The paper prints
\[
\mathrm{ADE}=\|\hat{T}_c-T_c\|_2^2,\qquad
\mathrm{FDE}=\|\hat{E}_f-E_f\|_2^2
\]
[PAGE 7]. A squared Euclidean norm has units of meters squared, not meters. Yet the text later refers to “6.34/11.5 ADE/FDE meters errors” [PAGE 8], and Table 1 reports “5.55” and “9.99” as ADE/FDE [PAGE 8]. Therefore either the equations omit a square root or the reported units are mislabeled. This is not cosmetic: if ADE is squared, the loss scale changes with court size; if ADE is meters, the printed equation is dimensionally wrong. A deployment audit should require the metric definition, unit, and averaging convention to be stated explicitly.

The third gray-zone is the clock. The dataset section says “Each sequence is sampled at 25 Hz” and “Each observation has five frames, which correspond to 2.0s in a basketball scenario. The goal is to forecast the successive ten frames (4.0s)” [PAGE 7]. At 25 Hz, \(\Delta t=0.04\) s, so five frames span at most 0.20 s if five samples are counted inclusively, or 0.16 s across four intervals. To make five frames equal 2.0 s, the sampling rate would be 2.5 Hz. This is a hard temporal inconsistency. It affects velocity and acceleration vetoes, because a predicted displacement that looks plausible at 0.04 s may be biomechanically impossible at 0.4 s, and vice versa.

The fourth gray-zone is retrocausal leakage. The paper states: “Oracular Permutation” means that RolFor uses distances at the last future step, i.e., step 10 in the future. In contrast, any other reported algorithm uses only the observed five frames” [PAGE 7]. The headline SoTA row is therefore oracle-assisted, not deployable. The end-to-end model without that oracle performs poorly: “E2E is learning to order, encode the role-role interaction, and forecast based on the encoder. This model is performing poorly at 12.12./15.02 ADE/FDE” [PAGE 9]. The paper also reports “OrderNN E2E achieves a top-10 ordering accuracy of only 1%” [PAGE 9]. The defensible conclusion is not that latent roles are useless; it is that differentiable ordering is the bottleneck. The paper itself says current differentiable ordering approaches have “difficulties with backpropagation—enabling little to no gradients to flow through” [PAGE 3].

A further audit point is dataset cardinality. The paper says NBA SportVU “contains players and ball trajectories for 631 games” and then says the processed set comes “from 1247 games” [PAGE 7]. Both cannot be the same raw corpus without an explanation such as team-filtered expansion, duplicate game IDs, or a typo. Any reproduction must resolve that count before trusting split sizes.

Overall, the derivational chain is coherent as a hypothesis but fragile as a causal forecasting claim. Roles are inferred from observed coordinates, ordered by a non-differentiable approximation, and then evaluated with metrics that may be dimensionally mislabeled and with a headline result that uses future information. The correct engineering posture is to treat the paper as evidence that role ordering can help under oracle or hand-defined conditions, not as proof that end-to-end latent roles are solved.

The adjacency factors are dimensionless; \(H\) and \(W\) carry the learned representation scale. The paper states: “The space-time cross-talk is realized by factoring the space-time adjacency matrix (as in [13]) into the product of separate spatial and temporal adjacency matrices \(A_{st}=A_sA_t\)” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: if \(A_sA_t\) is rank-deficient or near-singular, the layer collapses role-time cross-talk; if \(A_s\) and \(A_t\) are unconstrained, the factorization need not preserve the same interaction structure as a full \(A_{st}\); and if the learned adjacency is dense, the claimed efficiency advantage over a full space-time graph disappears.

**SoftRank and differentiable re-shuffling.** The paper uses SoftRank, “a recent differentiable implementation of the classic sorting and ranking algorithm” [PAGE 5]. It describes “a regularization parameter \(\varepsilon\), which creates a trade-off between the differentiability of the algorithm and the optimum’s accuracy. The greater the regularization factor \((\varepsilon\to\infty)\), the further the approximation will be from the permutation vertices, and the smoother the loss function gradient will be. And vice versa, by picking an \(\varepsilon\to0\), the algorithm will yield more accurate permutations with a lower degree of differentiability” [PAGE 5]. Formally, let the SoftRank outputs be \(\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n\), the hard ranks be \(p_i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), and
\[
\Delta_i=p_i-s_i,\qquad
M_i=\exp\!\left(-\left(\frac{\Delta_i}{\mathrm{scale}}\right)^2\right).
\]
The paper’s Eq. (1) writes the re-shuffled coordinates as
\[
P_i=
\begin{cases}
x_i'=\sum_{j=1}^n M_j\,x_j,\\
y_i'=\sum_{j=1}^n M_j\,y_j.
\end{cases}
\]
[PAGE 6]. Units: \(M_i\) is dimensionless; \(P_i\) is meters. Breakdown regimes: \(\varepsilon\to\infty\) or a large scale makes \(M_i\) nearly uniform, so the “shuffle” is a blur rather than a permutation; \(\varepsilon\to0\) or a small scale makes \(M_i\) nearly one-hot but gradients vanish or become numerically unstable; exact ties \(\Delta_i=0\) make the ranking non-unique; and the printed equation does not show a normalization \(\sum_j M_j\), so \(P_i\) is not guaranteed to be a convex combination of player coordinates unless the implementation normalizes internally. This last point is a formal gap, not a minor notation issue, because unnormalized weights can move predicted coordinates outside the convex hull of the observed players.

**Forecasting metrics.** The paper defines
\[
\mathrm{ADE}=\|\hat{T}_c-T_c\|_2^2,\qquad
\mathrm{FDE}=\|\hat{E}_f-E_f\|_2^2
\]
[PAGE 7]. If taken literally, both have units of meters squared. But the text reports “meters errors” [PAGE 8] and lists ADE/FDE as single-number errors in meters [PAGE 8]. The dimensional audit therefore requires either
\[
\mathrm{ADE}=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{c}\|\hat{T}_c-T_c\|_2
\]
or a square root of the printed expression. The paper does not resolve which convention produced Tables 1–5. This matters because a squared error can exaggerate outliers and change the ranking of models relative to an unsquared displacement error.

**Oracle condition.** The paper states: “Oracular Permutation” means that RolFor uses distances at the last future step, i.e., step 10 in the future. In contrast, any other reported algorithm uses only the observed five frames” [PAGE 7]. This is a retrocausal operator, not a causal forecasting operator. The oracle row is 5.55/9.99 ADE/FDE [PAGE 8], while the E2E row is 12.12/15.02 [PAGE 9]. The formal lesson is that the paper’s SoTA claim is conditional on future information in the ordering stage. Any deployment claim must remove that operator or label it as an upper bound.

**Ordering accuracy.** The paper measures ordering accuracy \(p_{\mathrm{ord}}\) as “the percentage of players the models place in the correct order” [PAGE 9]. It reports “OrderNN E2E achieves a top-10 ordering accuracy of only 1%” [PAGE 9], while OrderNN EuclDistEst reaches 71% at top-10, 77% at top-5, 82% at top-3, and 92% at top-1 [PAGE 10]. This isolates the bottleneck: learning Euclidean ball-distance ordering from 2D coordinates is feasible; learning the ordering end-to-end through SoftRank is not, under the reported setup.

**Robustness to ordering error.** The paper simulates Light Swap, Light Insert, and Heavy Swap plus Heavy Insert. “A Light Swap/Insert gives 6.55/12.10 in ADE/FDE and 80% top-10 ordering accuracy” [PAGE 10]. The oracle is 5.55/9.99 [PAGE 8]. Thus moderate ordering corruption degrades forecasting but does not collapse it; the role graph is somewhat robust to local permutation error. The breakdown regime is global permutation slip: if one role is inserted at the wrong position, the whole ordered sequence can shift, and the graph’s learned role-role edges no longer correspond to the intended tactical relations.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from pedestrian trajectory forecasting to game forecasting to graph-based interaction modeling to differentiable ranking. The paper cites Social LSTM, Social GAN, STGAT, Social-STGCNN, EvolveGraph, and pose-forecasting STSGCN as the relevant ancestry [PAGE 11]. The conceptual move is to replace fixed player nodes with latent role nodes. The paper says “players from various matches and teams do not have a fixed order, which is not an issue with pose forecasting. This encourages us to learn and re-order the players based on hidden roles” [PAGE 4]. That is the genealogical hinge: pose forecasting supplies fixed-cardinality graph convolution; team sports supply variable identity and tactical role; differentiable ranking supplies the bridge.

The retrocausal trap is already present in the headline comparison. The oracle uses “distances at the last future step, i.e., step 10 in the future” [PAGE 7]. That is a future-conditioned ordering. It is useful as an upper bound, but it is genealogically dangerous because later work may cite the 5.55/9.99 row as if it were a causal model. The paper’s own E2E result is 12.12/15.02 [PAGE 9], and the paper attributes the gap to SoftRank differentiability [PAGE 9]. A multi-week reproduction trap is to spend time tuning the RoleGCN when the actual failure is the ordering gradient path. The paper explicitly says differentiable ordering approaches have “difficulties with backpropagation—enabling little to no gradients to flow through” [PAGE 3].

A second multi-week trap is dataset lineage. The paper says SportVU contains “631 games” and then says the processed set comes “from 1247 games” [PAGE 7]. A reproduction that ignores this can silently mix raw and filtered game universes, leak sequences across splits, or misreport the 60,708/15,244/19,050 split. A third trap is metric genealogy: ADE/FDE conventions differ across papers, and the printed squared norms [PAGE 7] conflict with “meters errors” [PAGE 8]. A team can “improve” a metric by changing from squared to unsquared displacement without changing the model. A fourth trap is role semantics: the paper assumes “no prior knowledge about roles” [PAGE 2], yet the best hand-defined proxy is Euclidean distance from the ball and marking relation [PAGE 8]. If later work treats the learned latent role as a tactical role without probing it, it may be rediscovering ball proximity under a new name. The paper’s own ablation shows that simple ordering is only “negligibly better than no order” [PAGE 8], while ball distance plus marking at future frames reaches 5.55/9.99 [PAGE 8]. That is a warning that the useful signal may be marking geometry, not an abstract role.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper’s role model is a first-order theory-of-mind proxy, not recursive theory of mind. Players are assumed to have “a specific function in each action” [PAGE 1], and the model infers latent roles from observed trajectories. But the paper does not model players modeling other players’ beliefs, nor does it model counterfactual intent recursion. The closest mechanism is the learned role-role adjacency: “the edges are their relations” and “the adjacency matrix is learned” [PAGE 2]. That is relational inference, not recursive belief inference.

The crisis-habit regression appears when the ordering module fails. The paper reports that E2E training “is performing poorly at 12.12./15.02 ADE/FDE” [PAGE 9], and “E2E-finetune starts from the EuclDistEst variant, and it fine-tunes it, E2E. The error increases to 12.08/14.97, so the model neglects the initialization and reverts to the E2E performance” [PAGE 9]. This is a habit regression: under weak gradients, the model falls back to the easier end-to-end solution rather than preserving the informative hand-defined ordering. The counter-trap is to over-impose the Euclidean habit. If the model is forced to sort by ball distance, it may learn a shortcut that fails when the tactical role is defined by spacing, help defense, or off-ball movement rather than proximity to the ball. The paper itself notes that “simple ordering” is only negligibly better than no ordering [PAGE 8], so the hand-defined habit is not a universal solution.

The clock trap is the mismatch between role-inference time and role-evaluation time. The model observes five frames and forecasts ten [PAGE 7], but the oracle orders players using the last future step [PAGE 7]. That means the role assignment is evaluated at a future clock that the deployed model does not have. In a recursive ToM framing, the model is being asked to infer a role now that is defined by a future relation. That is not recursive belief reasoning; it is retrocausal role supervision. A correct experiment separates three clocks: observation clock, role-assignment clock, and evaluation clock. If the role-assignment clock is future, the result is an oracle upper bound. If the role-assignment clock is observed-only, the result is deployable but weaker. The paper’s observed-only best non-oracle distance result is 6.16/11.28 [PAGE 9], which is the more honest causal baseline.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer can veto a trajectory even when ADE/FDE improve. For each player \(p\) and frame \(t\), define the discrete velocity and acceleration
\[
v_{p,t}=\frac{\|x_{p,t+1}-x_{p,t}\|}{\Delta t},\qquad
a_{p,t}=\frac{\|x_{p,t+1}-2x_{p,t}+x_{p,t-1}\|}{\Delta t^2}.
\]
A physically admissible set is
\[
\mathcal{A}=\left\{x_{p,t}\in\mathcal{C},\ \ v_{p,t}\le v_{\max},\ \ a_{p,t}\le a_{\max}\right\},
\]
where \(\mathcal{C}\) is the court polygon. The paper does not report court-bound, speed, or acceleration checks; it reports ADE/FDE only [PAGE 7]. Therefore a lower ADE can still contain out-of-bounds or biomechanically impossible displacements. The veto is to reject or project any forecast that leaves \(\mathcal{A}\). If projection changes the forecast materially, the model’s ADE gain is not physically valid.

The biomechanical layer is limited by the data. The paper uses “players and ball overhead-view trajectories” [PAGE 7], not pose or joint data. Therefore a full joint-torque or limb-load veto is not possible from the source. The admissible biomechanical veto is center-of-mass kinematics: speed, acceleration, turning radius, and collision avoidance. A claim about joint mechanics or fatigue would require data the paper does not provide. The paper’s own graph is over roles and positions, not musculoskeletal states [PAGE 4].

The weather layer is vacuous for this source. The data are indoor NBA SportVU trajectories [PAGE 7]. There is no weather variable, no outdoor surface, and no wind or precipitation channel. A weather veto would be an invented layer, not a cross-layer adjudication. The correct statement is: weather is not applicable; the environmental layer is the indoor court geometry and the ball/team state.

The role layer adds another veto: team and ball context. The paper says “RolFor considers that the teams are known” [PAGE 7]. If team labels are unknown or swapped, the attacker-defender ordering is undefined. The ball is part of the input visualization [PAGE 2], and the hand-defined ordering uses distance from the ball [PAGE 5]. A prediction that is accurate in average displacement but assigns the ball to the wrong player or reverses attacker/defender roles should be vetoed as tactically invalid. The oracle result is also vetoed for deployment because it uses step-10 future distances [PAGE 7]. The proof is simple: a causal policy cannot condition on a future state it has not observed. Thus the oracle row is an upper bound, not an admissible controller.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto 1: gradient-flow counterfactual.** Intervention: replace SoftRank with an exact sort plus a straight-through estimator, or supervise the ordering with a differentiable Euclidean-distance head, while holding the RoleGCN and decoder fixed. Verifiable prediction: if non-differentiability is the primary bottleneck, top-10 ordering accuracy should rise well above the reported 1% [PAGE 9], and E2E ADE/FDE should fall below 12.12/15.02 [PAGE 9]. If accuracy stays near 1% and ADE stays near 12.12, then the bottleneck is not only SoftRank but also the role representation or the graph. Verification: same split, same seeds, report top-10 accuracy and ADE/FDE. This is a counterfactual because only the gradient path changes.

**Veto 2: future-leakage counterfactual.** Intervention: mask all future-frame coordinates during ordering and retrain or re-evaluate. Verifiable prediction: the oracle 5.55/9.99 [PAGE 8] should not be reproducible without future information; performance should fall toward the observed-only non-oracle range, whose reported best is 6.16/11.28 [PAGE 9]. If the model still matches 5.55/9.99 after masking future frames, the pipeline leaks future state through another channel, such as sequence construction, normalization, or split contamination. Verification: audit every input tensor for \(t>T\), then rerun. This veto directly tests the paper’s own statement that the oracle uses “step 10 in the future” [PAGE 7].

**Veto 3: physical-plausibility counterfactual.** Intervention: add a court-bound and speed/acceleration checker to the decoder, with projection onto the admissible set \(\mathcal{A}\). Verifiable prediction: the unconstrained model will produce at least some violations of court bounds or kinematic limits; constrained decoding will reduce violations to zero but may increase ADE/FDE. If constrained decoding reduces violations with negligible ADE/FDE change, the model was already physically plausible; if ADE/FDE rises sharply, the reported metric was hiding non-physical forecasts. Verification: report violation rate, maximum speed, maximum acceleration, and constrained versus unconstrained ADE/FDE. This veto is necessary because the paper reports only ADE/FDE [PAGE 7] and does not report physical admissibility. A fourth clock check should be run alongside it: reconcile “25 Hz” with “five frames … 2.0s” [PAGE 7], because the velocity and acceleration thresholds depend on \(\Delta t\). If the true rate is 2.5 Hz, the kinematic veto thresholds must be recomputed; if the true rate is 25 Hz, the reported horizon labels are wrong.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
