<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source supplies three formalizable operator families: sampling and kinematics, probabilistic model updating, and qualification as a governance filter. I formalize them below; the equations are standard physics and probability, not invented ratios. All page citations are to the supplied source.

**1. Sampling and kinematic operator.** Let player \(i\) have position \(\mathbf r_i(t_k)\in\mathbb{R}^3\) in metres at sample times \(t_k=k/f_s\), where \(f_s\) is the sampling frequency in hertz. The source states “Positional updates at least 20 times per second (=20 Hz)” [PAGE 3] and that “The most used is 20 Hz but a 100 Hz setting is also available” [PAGE 3]. With \(\Delta t=1/f_s\) in seconds, a central-difference velocity is
\[
\mathbf v_i(t_k)=\frac{\mathbf r_i(t_{k+1})-\mathbf r_i(t_{k-1})}{2\Delta t},
\]
with units \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and acceleration is
\[
\mathbf a_i(t_k)=\frac{\mathbf r_i(t_{k+1})-2\mathbf r_i(t_k)+\mathbf r_i(t_{k-1})}{\Delta t^2},
\]
with units \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Path distance over a window is
\[
D_i=\sum_{k}\|\mathbf r_i(t_{k+1})-\mathbf r_i(t_k)\|,
\]
in metres. Assumption: piecewise-linear interpolation between samples. Breakdown regime: if \(f_s\) falls below \(2f_{\max}\), where \(f_{\max}\) is the highest movement frequency of interest, aliasing occurs; if \(\Delta t\) becomes very small, finite-difference noise amplification grows, so higher sampling is not automatically better without filtering. The source’s own limits are explicit: tracking gives position but “You don’t have the facing, you don’t know what direction they’re facing. You don’t know which foot they’re on. You don’t know their weight distribution. You don’t know what their mental state is” [PAGE 12]. Therefore acceleration and distance are defensible operators; foot-loading, facing, and mental-state claims are not, unless another sensor layer is added.

**2. Bayesian model-update operator.** The source’s key methodological claim is that “the best models out there use Bayesian inference” [PAGE 10]. Let \(\theta\) be the model parameter vector, for example the probability of scoring from a shot situation or a pitch-control surface, and let \(\mathcal D\) be the tracking and event data. The posterior is
\[
p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)=\frac{p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{\int p(\mathcal D\mid\theta')p(\theta')\,d\theta'}.
\]
The posterior, likelihood, and prior are dimensionless probability densities; the data enter through the likelihood model. Assumption: the likelihood correctly encodes the data-generating process. Breakdown regime: model misspecification. The source gives a concrete failure case: an expected-goals model that uses only shot location but ignores defender positions. Spearman says, “Even if the shot came from 10 yards away, it doesn’t matter. You know it’s gonna be a terrible shot because there are 11 people there” [PAGE 30]. In that regime, the posterior is confidently wrong because the likelihood omits a causally relevant variable. The operator is valid only when the model is explicit about what it does and does not condition on.

**3. Qualification operator.** The source does not give a single equation for qualification, but it gives a formal checklist: “data accuracy is central so qualification should become a matter of: circumstances for data collection (e.g., methodological conditions), persons behind the data (who collected the data?), avoiding to fall in the trap of ‘data for the sake of data’ (quality of data should outshine ‘quantity focus’), and of creating effective platforms for user involvement to bring the data to life in a wider football context” [PAGE 32]. I represent this as a filter
\[
Q:\mathcal D_{\mathrm{raw}}\longrightarrow \mathcal D_{\mathrm{qual}},
\]
where \(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{qual}}\) carries metadata on collection conditions, provenance, quality checks, and user-context constraints. The filter has no physical units; it is a governance mapping. Breakdown regime: if \(Q\) is bypassed, the organization can still produce numbers, but the numbers are not decision-grade. The source’s “reverse research” warning [PAGE 9] is the pathological case: \(Q\) is replaced by a confirmation filter that selects evidence for a predetermined conclusion. The FOMO warning [PAGE 32] is a second pathological case: the organization purchases the data layer before building the qualification layer. In both cases, the formal model may be intact, but the evidence is not.

**4. Calibration check for probabilistic claims.** For a binary event such as a shot becoming a goal, let \(p_j\) be the model probability and \(y_j\in\{0,1\}\) the outcome. The Brier score
\[
BS=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(p_j-y_j)^2
\]
is dimensionless and lower is better. This is not in the source; it is a standard scoring rule I use as a verifiable test. It becomes relevant because the source insists that numbers need models and that models must be explicit about omitted variables [PAGE 10; PAGE 30]. Breakdown regime: a model can have a low Brier score in aggregate while being systematically wrong in a sub-regime, such as “park the bus” defending. That is why the defender-density counterfactual in the RLVR section is necessary.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is visible in the source’s own warnings. Under pressure, coaches and executives regress to intuition, to “reverse research” [PAGE 9], or to “data for the sake of data” [PAGE 32]. The regression is not irrational in the narrow sense: a coach has seconds to decide, and a complex model may be slower than a trained eye. The source acknowledges the “stressed time slot” for in-game data use [PAGE 15]. The recursive problem is that the analyst knows this, so the analyst must model the coach’s mental state, time budget, and trust threshold. If the analyst delivers a model that requires the coach to learn a new statistical language during a match, the coach will reject it. That rejection is a rational habit regression, not a failure of intelligence.

The counter-trap is qualification plus co-creation. The source’s human-machine claim is not that data replaces scouts; it is that “If you have the best scouts and the best analysts, they are going to be better than even the best models because their mental model takes into account many factors that the data doesn’t control for” [PAGE 13]. The counter-trap therefore has three moves. First, keep the human in the loop for context that the sensor cannot capture, such as facing, foot, weight distribution, and mental state [PAGE 12]. Second, make the model explicit about omissions so that the coach can calibrate trust. Third, translate the output into a shared language: “You’re teaching a language too” [PAGE 29]. This is recursive theory of mind in practice: the analyst models the coach, the coach models the player, the commercial team models the fan, and the sponsor models the fan’s attention. The source’s fan-facing rule, “you don’t want to be showing equations or models” [PAGE 24], is the boundary condition for the commercial layer.

The clock is the third term. Football is “about the sense of time and space” [PAGE 18], and the source distinguishes before-game, during-game, and after-game uses. The before-game clock supports opponent analysis and training design; the during-game clock supports substitutions and injury risk; the after-game clock supports learning and narrative. A data product that is valid on one clock may be invalid on another. For example, a 20 Hz positional stream may be adequate for post-match distance and shape analysis, but it may be too coarse for live biomechanical feedback. The recursive failure mode is clock mismatch: the analyst models the coach as a slow deliberator, the coach models the analyst as a real-time dashboard, and the player receives a message that is either late or overcomplicated. The counter-trap is to design for the clock explicitly: pre-match models can be rich; in-match outputs must be sparse, visual, and action-linked; post-match outputs can be explanatory and narrative. The source does not prove this design rule, but its interview data consistently point toward it.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is sampling and transmission. The source supports 20 Hz as the normal rate and 100 Hz as an available setting [PAGE 3]. If a claim depends on movement frequencies above the Nyquist limit of the chosen rate, the physical layer vetoes the claim. The formal condition for no aliasing is \(f_s \ge 2f_{\max}\). If \(f_s\) is below that bound, the measured acceleration and jerk are not merely noisy; they are aliased, and the direction of the error is not recoverable without additional information. The source does not report packet-loss statistics or receiver coverage maps, so a stronger physical veto cannot be proven from this corpus. What can be proven is that the source’s own data product is positional and body-sensor based, not a full biomechanical motion-capture system. That is why the source says tracking data lacks facing, foot, weight distribution, and mental state [PAGE 12]. Any biomechanical claim about foot loading or turning mechanics is therefore vetoed unless an additional sensor or video annotation layer is present.

The biomechanical layer veto is sensor placement and construct validity. The ZXY belt is worn around the waistline and is described as “ultra-light” to respect player functionality [PAGE 3]. Waist-worn inertial or radio systems can estimate gross movement, but they do not directly measure ground reaction force, ankle torque, or knee valgus. The source’s own list of limits is the proof of construct mismatch: position is measured, but “You don’t know which foot they’re on” [PAGE 12]. A claim that a player has a specific foot-loading asymmetry is therefore not supported by the ZXY layer alone. The biomechanical veto is not a criticism of the system; it is a boundary condition. The system is valid for distance, speed, acceleration, deceleration, step frequency, turning speed, heading, run characteristics, and heart rate as listed in the source [PAGE 3]. It is not valid for unmeasured joint kinetics.

The weather layer veto is the weakest in this source. The chapter does not report weather variables, rain, wind, temperature, or surface conditions as covariates. It does mention venue-installed radio receivers and training-facility receivers [PAGE 3], but it does not give weather-dependent signal-quality data. Therefore, any claim that weather moderates ZXY accuracy is an underidentified hypothesis in this corpus. The correct adjudication is not to invent a weather effect but to mark it as requiring a separate instrument: local weather telemetry, receiver signal-strength logs, and a stratified error analysis. A verifiable negative prediction follows: in the current corpus, no weather effect on tracking error can be estimated because no weather covariate is recorded. If a future study adds weather data and finds no change in positional error across dry and wet conditions, the weather veto is not supported for that venue; if it finds increased dropout or error, the veto is supported. This is a clean cross-layer adjudication: physical sampling veto is provable from the stated rates; biomechanical veto is provable from the stated construct limits; weather veto is unadjudicable from this source and must be flagged as such.

in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), plus peak jerk difference. Prediction: for straight-line jogging, \(E_a\) will be small; for sharp cuts and sprint starts, \(E_a\) will be materially larger, and the 20 Hz peak acceleration will be biased downward relative to 100 Hz.
