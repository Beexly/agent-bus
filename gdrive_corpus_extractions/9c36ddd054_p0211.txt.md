with sH in habs and N a dimensionless repetition count. The integrated form is a monotonic, concave-down approach to sH_max; thus sH is invertible over its range.

Summation operator (per [PAGE 202]):
N_i = sH^{-1}(sHR_i), for i = 1,...,5.
N_tot = \sum_{i=1}^{5} N_i.
sHe = sH(N_tot).

General n-case: Eq. 30 implements this operator for n values without explicit iteration, though its closed form is not printed in the excerpt. [PAGE 202]

Units: sHR and sHe are in habs (Hullian habit-strength units); d is in j.n.d.'s (just-noticeable-difference steps); N is dimensionless; c is dimensionless (fraction of potential per repetition). No SI units are invoked; the system is psycho-physical rather than metric-physical.

Breakdown / singularity regimes: (i) As sHR_i \to sH_max, N_i \to \infty; if any gradient crest sits at asymptote, N_tot diverges and sHe saturates at sH_max. (ii) If d exceeds the range of the source table, g_i(d) is undefined; the summation operator lacks inputs. (iii) If net habit includes inhibitory components (sHR < 0), the purely excitatory repetition inversion is invalid without an inhibitory complement. (iv) The procedure assumes temporal contiguity of the five tendencies; distributed delays or retroactive interference violate the static N-addition premise.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Hull's 1943 Principles of Behavior represents the apex of deductive neo-behaviorism, blending Watsonian stimulus-response orthodoxy with logical-positivist formalism. The habit-strength construct sH, the postulate system, and the mathematization of learning in Chapter VIII place Hull in direct lineage from the American quantitative behaviorism of the 1930s, anticipating Spence's discriminant-learning formulation and the later generalization-gradient literature (e.g., Guttman & Kalish; Hanson). The j.n.d. metric borrows from psychophysics (Fechner), while the diminishing-returns repetition law echoes Ebbinghaus-type savings curves recast in drive-reduction terms.

Multi-week trap: Hull's equation 30 treats the five generalization tendencies as contemporaneous summands. It contains no parameter for inter-session interval, consolidation, or spontaneous recovery. Applied across weeks, the model risks assuming that a habit strength computed from a single-session gradient remains stable. In reality, multi-week intervals introduce extinction, contextual drift, and circadian modulation of reinforcement efficacy—variables absent from Postulate 4 and Eq. 26/30. A second trap is the reification of "habs" as a conserved quantity: because the summation is algebraic, the model implies habit strength is fungible across stimuli, which fails if the organism configuralizes the compound stimulus rather than summing elemental gradients.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind here refers to the organism's model of the experimenter's contingency structure, layered over its own habit hierarchy. Three dynamical regimes interact: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression: Under aversive arousal or reinforcement omission, the organism may regress to the earliest-consolidated habit (largest historical N) rather than express the freshly computed sHe from Eq. 30. The summed gradient predicts a blended response; crisis behavior instead predicts a high-N, low-flexibility response locked to the original reinforcement crest.

Counter-trap: When five gradients overlap, the summed sHe may exceed threshold at stimulus points where no single gradient predicts response. This creates a "summation false alarm"—the organism responds in a between-crests region because habs from multiple crests summate above threshold. The counter-trap is that the experimenter may interpret this as evidence of a novel peak, when it is merely an artifact of linear N-addition over nonlinear gradients.

Clock: Hull's operator is atemporal; it computes sHe from a static set of sHR values. Biological clocks (circadian, ultradian, session-time) gate the c parameter and the expression of sH. If reinforcement occurs at a circadian phase with low c, the effective N_i are smaller than predicted. The clock thus modulates the repetition equivalence: five repetitions at one time of day are not equivalent to five repetitions at another.

The recursive loop: the organism anticipates whether the current contingency is stable (high express sHe) or a crisis probe (regress to high-N habit). This anticipation is not represented in Eq. 30.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Hull's formalism occupies the computational-psychological layer: inputs are j.n.d. distances and habs; output is sHe. It contains no terms for biomechanical state, metabolic exhaustion, or meteorological conditions. Therefore any cross-layer prediction must pass through veto predicates.

Physical/biomechanical veto: Let C(response) \in {0,1} denote the organism's capacity to emit the measured response. If C=0 (e.g., injury, fatigue-induced motor failure, sedative load), then predicted sHe is behaviorally null regardless of habs magnitude. Formal veto: sHe_observed = sHe_Hull \times C(response). Because C is external to Eq. 26/30, the psychology layer cannot override a physical veto.

Weather/sensory veto: The j.n.d. unit assumes a stable sensory discrimination scale. Weather (e.g., fog reducing visual contrast, wind altering auditory localization, temperature shifting cutaneous thresholds) changes the physical mapping between stimulus coordinate x and perceptual d. If |\Delta j.n.d.| > tolerance, the d values [0,10,20,30,40] are no longer valid inputs to the source table, and g_i(d) collapses. Veto: if sensory channel is degraded by weather, the habs inputs are inadmissible.

Adjudication: The nominal sHe from Eq. 30 is an upper-bound, layer-local estimate. The final behavioral prediction is min(sHe_Hull, Veto_physical, Veto_weather). Since Hull's equations provide no biomechanical or meteorological correction terms, these vetoes are decisive, not advisory.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual scenarios for testing the Hullian summation operator against an RLVR-style intervention:

Scenario 1 (Crest ablation). Intervention: Extinguish or physically block the reinforcement crest at 130 j.n.d.'s after the five gradients are established, leaving only four crests. Hullian prediction: Remove sHR_2 = 2.20 habs; recompute N_tot from {3.0, 1.61, 1.18, 0.87}; sHe must decrease monotonically relative to the five-crest baseline. Verifiable veto: If post-ablation responding at probe 120 does not decrease (or increases), the summation operator is falsified; this implies configural or non-additive credit assignment across crests.

Scenario 2 (Out-of-table probe). Intervention: Test at a probe stimulus 50 j.n.d.'s from the nearest crest, beyond the range of the source table. Hullian prediction: g_i(50) \approx 0 habs for all i; sHR_i undefined or zero; N_tot \to baseline; sHe \approx pre-training habit (near zero). Verifiable veto: Any robust responding at d=50 falsifies pure j.n.d.-metric generalization summation and indicates stimulus coding that generalizes beyond the tabulated gradient (e.g., categorical or rule-based perception).

Scenario 3 (Post-learning magnitude shift). Intervention: After the five gradients and sHe are computed, change reinforcement magnitude (e.g., double reward amount) without additional training trials. Hullian prediction: Because Eq. 26/30 maps repetition counts to habit strength independent of post-acquisition magnitude, sHe should remain fixed at the value derived from the original five N_i. Verifiable veto: If sHe scales with the new magnitude (e.g., higher response vigor), the repetition-equivalence assumption is incomplete; incentive magnitude must enter as a separate operator, violating the pure habs-summation model.

Each scenario yields a binary verification: the observed sHe either matches the recomputed Hullian value (model survives) or violates it (veto triggers model revision).
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
