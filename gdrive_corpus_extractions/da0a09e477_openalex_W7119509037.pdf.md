That is not a minor annotation detail; it is the core latent variable the classifier is asked to predict.

The abstract already states “Fleiss’ κ = 0.63, n = 5” [PAGE 1]. This is the key gray-zone audit: the labels are not ground truth in a physical sense. They are aggregated expert judgments with substantial but imperfect agreement. The paper itself warns that “performance above 0.9 must be interpreted considering the IRR of 0.63” [PAGE 11]. Thus an F1 score of 0.94 is a score against a consensus label, not a certificate of causal assist truth.

The third hop is preprocessing. A rule-based system identifies the preceding potential assist event and produces four assist types, but type 4 shots and ambiguous or double-chance shots are excluded, “leaving 422 shots: 344 with prior successful passes and 78 with unsuccessful prior passes” [PAGE 4]. Feature engineering produces “the 29 resulting features” [PAGE 6], including engineered angles, distances, frame differences, event counts, tracking coordinates, expected pass and expected goal values, pressure, and packing scores. Missing data are replaced with 0 because other imputing methods impaired performance [PAGE 7]. That choice is a gray-zone risk: zero is not a neutral missing-data marker when coordinates, distances, and frame intervals have physical meaning. The model can learn “missingness” as signal.

The fourth hop is model selection and interpretation. Four model families are trained across six scenarios and 12 feature configurations, producing 288 models [PAGE 3][PAGE 7]. The best reported Random Forest in Scenario 5 reaches F1 0.94 and precision 0.92 [PAGE 8], while XGBoost reaches AUC 0.97 and log loss 0.26 [PAGE 9]. SHAP identifies the squared interval between pass and shot as the most influential feature, with lower values increasing assist likelihood, but the relationship is not strictly linear [PAGE 9]. The final gray-zone audit is therefore threefold: label ambiguity bounds the target, zero imputation and scenario construction bound the inputs, and SHAP dependence plots describe model behavior rather than football causation. The paper’s own conclusion that “best F1 = 0.94” [PAGE 13] must be read alongside its own IRR caveat [PAGE 11].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The operators below are standard measurement and machine-learning operators used to interpret the paper. All quantities are stated with units. Where the source does not print an explicit formula, the formula is the standard definition of the metric named in the source, and the source citation identifies the reported value or procedural use.

1. Fleiss’ kappa for multi-rater nominal agreement. With \(N\) labeled scenes, \(m\) raters, \(k\) categories, \(n_{ij}\) the number of raters assigning scene \(i\) to category \(j\), \(P_o\) the observed agreement, and \(P_e\) the chance agreement:
\[
\kappa=\frac{P_o-P_e}{1-P_e},\qquad
P_o=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{ij}(n_{ij}-1),\qquad
P_e=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_j^2,\qquad
p_j=\frac{1}{Nm}\sum_{i=1}^{N}n_{ij}.
\]
Units: dimensionless. Assumption: fixed \(m\) raters per scene, nominal categories, independent ratings. The source uses five labelers and binary scenarios, reporting “Fleiss’ κ of 0.37 indicating fair agreement” on the first 50 scenes and “a substantial IRR of 0.66 (total IRR for all scenarios = 0.63)” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regimes: \(\kappa\) is undefined if \(P_e=1\), which occurs when all raters use one category with probability one; \(\kappa\) is also undefined for \(m=1\). Negative \(\kappa\) means agreement worse than chance. The denominator \(1-P_e\) approaches zero as category marginals become degenerate, so small label imbalances can produce unstable kappa values.

2. Precision, recall, and \(F_1\). For binary potential-assist classification, with \(TP\), \(FP\), and \(FN\) counts:
\[
P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\]
Units: dimensionless probabilities and dimensionless score. Assumption: the positive class is “potential assist.” Breakdown regimes: \(P\) is undefined when \(TP+FP=0\); \(R\) is undefined when \(TP+FN=0\); \(F_1\) is undefined when \(P+R=0\). \(F_1\) ignores true negatives and is not a proper scoring rule, so it can be high under class imbalance even when calibration is poor. The source prioritizes \(F_1\) “to address class imbalance” [PAGE 8] and reports the best Random Forest \(F_1=0.94\) with precision 0.92 [PAGE 8].

3. Receiver operating characteristic area under the curve. If \(s(X)\) is a model score and \(X^+\) and \(X^-\) are random positive and negative cases:
\[
\mathrm{AUC}=\Pr\!\left(s(X^+)>s(X^-)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr\!\left(s(X^+)=s(X^-)\right).
\]
Units: probability, dimensionless. Assumption: positive and negative classes both exist in the evaluation set; higher score means greater assist likelihood. Breakdown regimes: AUC is undefined with only one class; it is insensitive to absolute calibration and can remain high when log loss is poor. The source reports XGBoost with “highest AUC (0.97) and lowest Log Loss (0.26)” [PAGE 9].

4. Log loss, also called cross-entropy. For \(N\) cases with label \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) and predicted probability \(p_i\):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\log}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right].
\]
Units: nats per sample when natural logarithm is used; bits per sample if base-2 logarithm is used. Assumption: \(p_i\) is a calibrated probability for the positive class. Breakdown regime: the loss diverges to \(+\infty\) if a case with \(y_i=1\) receives \(p_i=0\), or a case with \(y_i=0\) receives \(p_i=1\); implementations must clip probabilities. The source reports Random Forest log loss 0.29 and XGBoost 0.26 in Scenario 5 [PAGE 8][PAGE 9].

5. Frame-to-time conversion. The tracking data have “a temporal resolution of 25 frames per second” [PAGE 3]. If \(\Delta f_i\) is the frame difference between pass and shot:
\[
\Delta t_i=\frac{\Delta f_i}{25\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}}.
\]
Units: seconds. Assumption: constant 25 frames per second and no dropped frames. Breakdown regimes: the conversion is undefined for missing frame indices; the quantization step is \(1/25=0.04\) s; and the paper notes that “defaults were assumed for miscellaneous events, potentially impairing accuracy” [PAGE 12]. The squared feature \(\Delta f_i^2\) has units of frames squared before transformation; after Yeo-Johnson transformation and scaling, the model input is dimensionless, so SHAP values are in model-output units, not seconds.

6. Euclidean overcome distance. If reception coordinates are \((x_r,y_r)\) and shot coordinates are \((x_s,y_s)\):
\[
d_i=\sqrt{(x_{r,i}-x_{s,i})^2+(y_{r,i}-y_{s,i})^2}.
\]
Units: pixels in the raw tracking frame; metres only after pitch calibration. Assumption: a common planar coordinate system and negligible perspective distortion after calibration. Breakdown regimes: \(d_i=0\) when reception and shot coordinates coincide; missing coordinates replaced by 0 [PAGE 7] can create artificial zero distances or artificial large distances depending on the coordinate origin. The paper defines overcome distance as “the Euclidean distance between ball reception and shot location” [PAGE 4].

7. Angle operators. For vectors \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\):
\[
\theta=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}\right).
\]
Units: radians. Assumption: nonzero vector norms and a defined direction. Breakdown regime: undefined when either norm is zero; numerically unstable when the cosine is near \(\pm 1\) and floating-point rounding pushes it outside \([-1,1]\). The feature table includes assist angle, shot angle, angle difference, and trajectory angle [PAGE 6].

8. SHAP additive attribution. For a model \(g\) with baseline \(\phi_0\) and feature attributions \(\phi_j\):
\[
g(x)=\phi_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j.
\]
Units: model-output units, typically log-odds for a probabilistic classifier before the inverse-link transform. Assumption: additive feature attribution with a defined coalition game. Breakdown regimes: correlated features violate the independence intuition of Shapley values and split credit unpredictably; interactions produce non-additive dependence plots. The paper reports that “SHAP values reveal sensitivity to feature interactions” [PAGE 9] and that the squared frame difference “had the greatest influence, with lower values increasing the likelihood of an assist” [PAGE 9].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from manual notational analysis to automated event detection to pass-quality valuation and then to potential-assist classification. The paper cites Charles Reep as a pioneer of notational and performance analysis in football [PAGE 13], then traces automatic event detection to Tovinkere and Qian in 2001 and rule-based tracking-data approaches by Khaustov and Mozgovoy [PAGE 2]. It then moves through neural-network event detection, SVM and k-nearest-neighbor pass and reception detection, variational autoencoders for corner-kicks and counter-attacks, and decision-tree event detection [PAGE 2]. The pass-quality branch includes expected passes by Anzer and Bauer, risk-reward pass measurement by Power et al., pass classification by Chawla et al., completion-rate extension by Szczepański and Mchale, expected pass impact by Bransen et al., and QPass by Gyarmati and Stanojevic [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. The paper positions itself as the first study focused on automatic detection of assists, because prior work detects passes or rates pass quality but does not explicitly identify the assist event [PAGE 12].

The retrocausal genealogy matters because it explains why the assist label is historically unstable. Manual notation could record a last pass, but it could not easily encode “significant” dribbles, deflections, ricochets, rebounds, or intended distribution. Vendor systems then encoded different conventions: Wyscout excludes assists after decisive dribbles or runs, while Opta and FIFA use final touch and overlook dribbling actions [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. The paper’s three-condition definition is a synthesis, not a universal standard, and the authors admit that “decisive dribbles and runs represent crucial criteria for defining an assist and often require subjective interpretation” [PAGE 3]. The historical trap is therefore multi-week and multi-vendor: a model trained on one vendor’s labels can look excellent internally and still fail when transplanted to another vendor’s event grammar.

A second multi-week trap is sample construction. The paper uses 500 shot scenarios, excludes ambiguous and double-chance shots, and ends with 422 shots, of which 344 have successful prior passes and 78 have unsuccessful prior passes [PAGE 4]. Scenarios 3 and 4 are small, and the paper reports that “performance in Scenarios 3 and 4 may be limited by the smaller sample size” [PAGE 12]. SMOTE is applied, but the authors warn that it “introduces the potential limitation of generating synthetic examples that may not fully reflect real-world football situations” [PAGE 12]. A long validation cycle must therefore test on untouched matches, not merely on resampled test folds. A third trap is the temptation to treat high \(F_1\) as ground truth. The paper’s own caveat is decisive: “performance above 0.9 must be interpreted considering the IRR of 0.63” [PAGE 11]. The genealogy therefore ends not with a universal assist metric but with a complement to expected assists, as the authors suggest that “our potential assist framework could complement metrics such as expected assists (xA)” [PAGE 12].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem is that the classifier is trained to imitate experts who are themselves inferring the intentions of players. The labeler must decide whether the passer intended to reach a teammate, whether a deflection preserved that intention, and whether a dribble was “significant.” The source states that “concepts of intention and ball control remain ambigi­uous” and that “some definitions require interpretation” [PAGE 3]. Statsbomb, for example, does not refer to the intention of the potential assist giver [PAGE 3]. Thus the model is not learning a physical force or a deterministic event; it is learning a human attribution of preparation.

Crisis habit regression appears when ambiguous scenes push labelers back to familiar vendor habits. The initial 50-scene calibration produces only fair agreement, Fleiss’ kappa 0.37 [PAGE 4]. After expert exchange and refinement, the remaining 450 clips reach 0.66, and the total is 0.63 [PAGE 4]. This is a regression toward shared annotation habit, not necessarily toward truth. A counter-trap is overfitting to the aggregated label. The final label is created by “rounding the average” of expert inputs [PAGE 4], so a model can achieve high agreement with the consensus while still failing on a held-out expert’s interpretation. The paper’s own warning about the 0.63 IRR is the counter-trap check [PAGE 11].

The clock is the temporal interval between pass and shot. The paper finds that the squared frame difference has the greatest SHAP influence and that lower values increase assist likelihood, but “some low values corresponded to negative SHAP values, showing no strictly linear relationship” [PAGE 9]. The clock is therefore not a simple countdown. A short interval often looks like a direct assist, but a very short interval can also reflect a deflection, a rebound, or a mis-touch. The recursive model must represent both the passer’s inferred intention and the shot-taker’s action, and it must not confuse temporal proximity with causal preparation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the data support only what the sensors record. Tracking is 1920 by 1080 pixels at 25 frames per second [PAGE 3], so the temporal quantization is 0.04 s and the spatial resolution is pixel-limited. The paper replaces missing data with 0 [PAGE 7], which can manufacture artificial coordinates, distances, and frame intervals. Any claim that a specific assist was physically caused by a particular force, foot placement, or body orientation is vetoed unless it is supported by raw tracking and video review. The only shot-foot variable in the feature table is categorical “Shot foot” [PAGE 6]; there is no force plate, marker-based motion capture, EMG, or inverse-dynamics measurement.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the paper does not measure joint angles, ground reaction forces, muscle activation, or ball impact impulse. It measures event and tracking proxies such as angle to goal, distance to goal, change in distance, trajectory angle, frame difference, packing scores, expected pass, expected goal, and pressure [PAGE 6]. A biomechanical explanation of assist quality would require variables that are absent. The correct adjudication is therefore a veto of any biomechanical causal claim, not a rejection of the paper’s classification result.

Weather-layer veto: the data description lists event data, tracking data, player positions, ball positions, and referee positions, but no temperature, wind, precipitation, pitch moisture, or visibility variable [PAGE 3]. The paper does not model weather. Any assertion that weather altered assist probability in this study is unsupported and must be vetoed. The physical-layer and weather-layer vetoes are especially important because the paper’s strongest reported result, \(F_1=0.94\), is a label-agreement result under a specific data pipeline, not a universal physical law [PAGE 8][PAGE 13].

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: leave-one-rater-out label audit. Manipulation: retrain the same Random Forest pipeline on consensus labels formed from four experts, then evaluate against the held-out fifth expert and against the full five-expert consensus. Verifiable prediction: within-consensus \(F_1\) remains high, but cross-rater \(F_1\) falls and disagreement concentrates in scenes with deflections, ricochets, rebounds, or “significant” dribbles. This follows directly from the reported total Fleiss’ kappa of 0.63 and the initial 0.37 on ambiguous scenes [PAGE 1][PAGE 4]. The reward signal is vetoed if it rewards consensus imitation while claiming latent assist truth.

Veto scenario 2: temporal-default ablation. Manipulation: remove the squared frame-difference feature, or replace the zero imputation for missing frame intervals with a documented missingness indicator, and retrain Scenario 5 and Scenario 6. Verifiable prediction: the SHAP rank of the frame-difference feature drops, log loss increases, and the degradation is larger in events containing miscellaneous actions because the paper states that “defaults were assumed for miscellaneous events, potentially impairing accuracy” [PAGE 12]. The paper already reports that the squared interval had the greatest influence [PAGE 9], so a causal claim about timing must survive this ablation.

Veto scenario 3: cross-vendor definition transfer. Manipulation: apply the DFB-Pokal-trained classifier to a sample labeled under Wyscout, Opta, and Sportec conventions, stratifying by decisive-dribble scenes. Verifiable prediction: cross-vendor \(F_1\) is lower than within-vendor \(F_1\), and the largest disagreements occur where Wyscout excludes a decisive dribble but Opta or FIFA uses final touch [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. The paper’s own definitional synthesis and its warning that “the term ‘significant’ is not defined” [PAGE 3] make this a direct, verifiable veto against any claim of vendor-neutral assist detection.
