Homography and its parameter count. PAGE 1 gives the projective map
$$\lambda\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}' = \mathbf{H}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},$$
with $\lambda$ a scalar (the homogeneous scale, dimensionless). $\mathbf{H}$ has 9 entries defined only up to scale, hence **8 degrees of freedom (DoF)**. Each point correspondence contributes 2 independent linear constraints, so the DLT minimum is $\lceil 8/2\rceil = 4$ correspondences — matching PAGE 1, "requires at least four point correspondences."

RANSAC sample-complexity (information/probability units). PAGE 2 and PAGE 3 invoke RANSAC [5]. The standard worst-case sample bound is
$$N_{\text{iter}} \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{\log(1-p)}{\log\!\big(1 - w^{\,s}\big)} \right\rceil,$$
with $p$ = target confidence (probability, dimensionless), $w$ = inlier ratio (dimensionless, $0<w<1$), and $s$ the minimal sample size: $s=3$ for the plane (Eq. 5), $s=4$ for the DLT homography (Eq. 7). **Breakdown regime:** as $w\to 0$, $N_{\text{iter}}\to\infty$. If dynamic-object removal (Eq. 4) fails to drop the player fraction below $\approx 50\%$ of surviving points, RANSAC on the plane becomes computationally divergent and statistically unreliable.

Plane normal (Eq. 5, PAGE 2). Verbatim:
$$\mathbf{n}_k = \frac{(\mathbf{p}_2-\mathbf{p}_1)\times(\mathbf{p}_3-\mathbf{p}_1)}{\lVert(\mathbf{p}_2-\mathbf{p}_1)\times(\mathbf{p}_3-\mathbf{p}_1)\rVert}, \qquad d_k = -\mathbf{n}_k\cdot\mathbf{p}_1.$$
Units: if $\mathbf{p}_i$ are in metres, $\mathbf{n}_k$ is dimensionless (unit normal) and $d_k$ is in metres. **Exact singularity:** if the three sampled points are collinear, the cross product is $\mathbf{0}$, the denominator is $0$, and $\mathbf{n}_k$ is undefined — the equation is exactly singular. Non-collinearity of the RANSAC sample is a hard precondition, not a soft one.

Plane flattening (Eq. 6, PAGE 3).
$$\mathbf{p}_{\text{plane}} = \begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_0)\cdot\mathbf{u}\\ (\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_0)\cdot\mathbf{v}\end{bmatrix},$$
with $(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})$ an orthonormal basis of the plane, $\mathbf{p}_0$ a point on it. Units: metres (same as the cloud). This is a rigid 2D isometry of the plane, so it preserves metric lengths within the plane up to the unknown global scale $s$ from the monocular reconstruction.

Normalization singularity (Eq. 12, PAGE 4). Verbatim:
$$(p_{\text{warp},x},\,p_{\text{warp},y}) = \left(\frac{\tilde{p}'_x}{\tilde{p}'_z},\, \frac{\tilde{p}'_y}{\tilde{p}'_z}\right).$$
**Breakdown regime:** $\tilde{p}'_z \to 0$. Geometrically, $\tilde{p}'_z=0$ is the image of the plane's horizon (vanishing line). Any pixel whose back-projected ray meets the fitted plane at infinity — i.e., image content on or above the rink horizon such as the upper boards, glass stanchions, or crowd — maps to $|p_{\text{warp}}|\to\infty$. The pipeline is only valid strictly below the horizon line.

(3) *Annotation-coupling trap*: the ground truth comes from Stathletes puck annotations (PAGE 5), and since PAGE 4 admits no temporally consistent homography ground truth exists, any future benchmark is entangled with this vendor's data.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Model the downstream consumer (a hockey analyst computing puck speed, possession, or expected goals in rink metres) as an agent whose inferences inherit every assumption above.

Crisis-habit regression. Under distribution shift — a new broadcast camera, an unusual angle, or a rink geometry far from the NHL template — the pipeline does not fail loudly; it regresses toward its single-frame ancestor. If Pi3's pose smoothness degrades (fast whip-pans, cuts between shots handled as one sequence), the per-frame plane term $\mathbf{H}^{(t)}_{\text{img}\to\text{plane}}$ reacquires jitter, and the "stability" quietly reverts to the Fani-style regime the paper claims to have escaped. The analyst, trusting the "temporally stable" label, will not detect the regression because no jitter metric was published.

Counter-trap. The intended countermeasure is the two-stage decomposition itself: quarantine per-frame noise in the plane term, and quarantine scale/orientation error in the one-shot $\mathbf{H}_{\text{plane}\to\text{rink}}$. This works precisely when the noise is separable in that way. It fails when errors are *coupled* — e.g., if an incorrect plane orientation (a bad $(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})$ basis) simultaneously corrupts the plane term and biases the aggregated segmentation, so both stages inherit the same systematic error and there is no independent term left to cancel it.

The clock (temporal window $T$). Stability is a function of the aggregation horizon $T$ (number of warped segmentations fused into $S_{\text{agg}}$). As $T$ grows within a shot, the variance of the aggregated marking estimate should fall (errors "average out," PAGE 4) roughly as $1/T$ for zero-mean independent noise — but only up to the point where the shot ends or the camera viewpoint distribution stops adding new coverage. Beyond that, additional frames add correlated bias, not independent noise, and $1/T$ improvement stalls. The unstated optimum is "as many frames as the shot provides, weighted toward viewpoint diversity, not count." A recursive agent that treats "more frames = more stable" monotonically will over-trust long sequences that are actually viewpoint-starved.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Physical layer. The entire method rests on the ice being planar. Ice is nominally flat, so the planarity assumption is physically sound (a real strength versus, say, a baseball field with a mound). Two physical vetoes remain: (i) the horizon singularity $\tilde{p}'_z\to 0$ (Eq. 12) is a hard projective wall — no amount of segmentation fixes a pixel above the vanishing line; (ii) the anisotropic per-axis scaling in Eq. 13 assumes the rink's long axis aligns with the template's $x$; a residual in-plane rotation of $S_{\text{agg}}$ would stretch both metric axes. Verdict: physical vetoes are narrow but real, concentrated at the horizon and at template-orientation coupling.

Biomechanical layer. The dominant occluders are players, officials, and goalies — dense, fast-moving, self-occluding bodies. The pipeline's defense is YOLOX detection + SAM2 segmentation (PAGE 2, Eq. 3–4), with the explicit precondition that masked player points are set to NaN and excluded from the plane fit. The biomechanical veto triggers when detection recall drops: motion-blurred or heavily overlapping players produce partial masks, leaving player-body 3D points in $P_{\text{cleaned}}$. Since these points sit *above* the ice plane, they inflate the RANSAC residual and can tilt the fitted normal $\mathbf{n}^\ast$, corrupting the flattening basis. This is the most likely real-world failure and it is a body-density problem, not a geometry problem.

Weather/environmental layer. The setting is an indoor arena, so meteorological vetoes (rain, wind, snow) are null by construction. The correct analogues are the environmental optics that break segmentation and depth: glare and specular reflection off wet ice, condensation or frost on the camera-side glass, arena lighting flicker, and zamboni-resurfaced wet patches that violate the Lambertian assumption Pi3 and the segmentation network implicitly rely on. These are "indoor weather": low-frequency in time but capable of spiking segmentation error exactly during the high-action moments (a cloud of snow spray from a hard stop) that analysts most want to measure.

Adjudication: no cross-layer veto is fatal to the method's validity *in principle*, but the biomechanical (player-residue) veto is the highest-probability real-world trigger and directly undermines the metric claim by biasing the very plane whose scale is later anchored to metres.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

Three falsifiable scenarios, each with a concrete verifiable predicate and a prediction.

Veto 1 — Super-physical puck speed (scale-recovery failure). Predicate: transform the Figure 5 puck trajectory to metres via Eq. 13, differentiate to speed $v(t)=\lVert \mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}_{\text{rink}}/\mathrm{d}t\rVert$. A regulation ice surface bounds a hard slapshot at roughly $v_{\max}\approx 45\ \text{m/s}$ ($\sim$160 km/h); a routine pass is $<20\ \text{m/s}$. Prediction: if the aggregated segmentation mis-registers the template scale (or if ε = 0.001 in native units left a residual scale error), $v(t)$ will exhibit isolated spikes exceeding $\sim 45\text{–}50\ \text{m/s}$ inconsistent with play context. Verifiable: any computed $v > 50\ \text{m/s}$ falsifies the "metrically consistent" claim for that sequence.

Veto 2 — Horizon blow-up (projective singularity). Predicate: for every tracked point, log $\tilde{p}'_z$ from Eq. 11. Prediction: if any on-ice point yields $\tilde{p}'_z$ within noise of $0$, the normalized $(p_{\text{warp},x},p_{\text{warp},y})$ will diverge and the metric coordinates will jump by orders of magnitude in consecutive frames. Verifiable: a single-frame jump $\lVert\Delta \mathbf{p}_{\text{rink}}\rVert$ far exceeding the physical per-frame puck displacement ($\le v_{\max}/f$, e.g. $\le 45/30 = 1.5\ \text{m}$ at 30 fps) that coincides with $\tilde{p}'_z\to0$ confirms the singularity rather than a tracking error.

Veto 3 — Jitter-reduction falsification (the stability claim). Predicate: compute a discrete jerk proxy $J = \sum_t \lVert \mathbf{p}_{\text{rink}}(t{+}1) - 2\,\mathbf{p}_{\text{rink}}(t) + \mathbf{p}_{\text{rink}}(t{-}1)\rVert^2$ (second temporal difference) for the two-stage homography and for a single-frame baseline (e.g., Fani-style with Hann smoothing) on the same footage. Prediction: the paper's central claim implies $J_{\text{two-stage}} \ll J_{\text{baseline}}$. Verifiable outcome: if $J_{\text{two-stage}}$ is not statistically below $J_{\text{baseline}}$ (or if the ratio is dominated by a few frames where Veto 1 or Veto 2 fired), the "eliminates the jitter characteristic of single-frame approaches" conclusion (PAGE 5) is falsified. Because PAGE 4 offers only qualitative evidence, this veto is currently *unresolved by the authors* and is the single most important experiment the paper leaves on the table.
