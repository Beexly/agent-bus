S_{\text{extent}}(W) \approx S_{\text{extent}}(0).
\]
This falsifies a load-invariant \(f\), showing that command-sensation gain is modulated by biomechanical context.

Assumptions: quasi-static movement; negligible soft-tissue creep; stationary sensorimotor prior within a single experimental session; normalized \(u\) commensurate across effectors. Singularity/breakdown regimes: (a) \(R_{\text{ext}} \to F_{\text{max}}(\theta)\), where displacement control is lost; (b) \(p(R_{\text{hard}}) \to 0\), where the prior overwhelms rare-likelihood updates (the "once in a million times" hard-rubber case) [PAGE 516]; (c) amputation, where the afferent negation channel is removed and \(H(S \mid \text{non-existent toes})\) is undefined, leaving zero error signal [PAGE 516].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The argument on [PAGE 516] sits at a crossroads of 19th-century associationism and early sensorimotor physiology. James explicitly invokes the "inveterate association of ideas," a lineage traceable to Herbartian association psychology and to James's own earlier formulation in *Principles* that will and movement are habitually paired. He cites the *Proceedings of the American Soc. for Psych. Research*, p. 249 [PAGE 516], for phantom-limb phenomena, embedding the discussion in the emerging American experimental psychology of the 1890s. Herr Loeb's counter-evidence comes from *Pflüger's Archiv*, xLrv. p. 1 [PAGE 516], placing the debate within the German physiological tradition of Helmholtzian unconscious inference, where perception is understood as inference from sensory signs rather than direct registration.

Scientifically, James's rejection of "feelings of innervation" prefigures, by negation, the 20th-century efference-copy and corollary-discharge frameworks of von Holst & Mittelstaedt (1950) and Sperry (1950), in which the brain predicts the sensory consequences of its own commands and subtracts them from incoming reafference. James's account is closer to a purely associative predictive prior: without reafference, the idea itself generates the sensation. This anticipates modern predictive coding and active inference (Friston, 2005), where perception is hypothesis-driven and prediction errors update a generative model. The phantom-limb discussion also links to Mitchell's (1871) "phantom limb" and to later mirror-box work (Ramachandran, 1990s), in which visual reafference can reshape or extinguish phantom sensations.

Multi-week traps arise because the sensorimotor prior is non-stationary. James's statistical claim that hard resistance occurs "once in a million times" [PAGE 516] assumes a fixed environmental distribution. Over weeks of repeated exposure to hard rubber, the subject's prior \(p(R_{\text{ext}})\) shifts upward, weakening the habituation-based illusion and potentially converting hits into misses. Nerve conduction velocity (roughly 50–70 m/s in human motor and proprioceptive fibers) varies with ambient temperature and circadian state, altering the efferent–reafferent binding latency; over multi-week protocols, unrecorded thermal drift can change the temporal contiguity required for association binding. Muscle tone and tendon stiffness also show circadian and training-related variation, so \(F_{\text{max}}(\theta)\) is not constant across sessions. Any replication of James's finger or teeth experiments over weeks must therefore re-calibrate the resistance prior and the binding window; treating the 1890 protocol as i.i.d. across weeks is a category error that produces false negatives (illusion disappears because prior adapted) or false positives (illusion appears because fatigue lowered \(F_{\text{max}}\) and raised effective resistance).
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the subject as an agent maintaining a recursive belief about her own motor output:
\[
B_t = P(\Delta\theta > 0 \mid \text{do}(u_t), \mathcal{H}_{t-1}, D_t)
\]
where \(\mathcal{H}_{t-1}\) is sensorimotor history and \(D_t\) is the experimenter's demand signal. This is a second-order model: the subject has a theory of her own motor system and of what the experimenter expects her to report.

Crisis habit regression occurs under novel resistance. When the subject bites on hard rubber—a rare event with prior near \(10^{-6}\) according to James's "once in a million times" [PAGE 516]—the current afferent channel signals hardness, but the lifelong association expects softness. The belief regresses to the habitual mean:
\[
B_t \to B_{\text{habitual}} \approx P(\Delta\theta > 0 \mid \text{soft resistance}) \simeq 1,
\]
overriding the null proprioceptive likelihood from the blocked finger or the anomalous hardness of the rubber. The subject reports movement because the generative model collapses to the dominant prior rather than updating on the current likelihood.

The counter-trap is social simulation. James's instruction—"try to flex the terminal joint ... You do not do it, and yet you think that you do" [PAGE 516]—embeds an experimenter expectation inside the trial. The subject may recursively model \(D_t\): "The experimenter wants me to report that I think I moved." The report becomes a metacognitive simulation of compliance rather than a perceptual hallucination. In this account, "I think that I do" is a statement about a simulated outcome, not about an experienced movement. The associationist account and the counter-trap are observationally equivalent on verbal report alone; they diverge only under implicit measures (skin conductance, residual EMG, or forced-choice agency attribution) that bypass verbal compliance.

Clock structure determines which layer wins. The sensorimotor binding window between efferent command and expected reafference is on the order of 100–300 ms. By requiring the subject to act "without looking," James removes the fastest error channel (vision, latency roughly 50–100 ms) and leaves only proprioception, which returns null because Δθ = 0. If the subject holds the command beyond the binding window without corrective feedback, the persistent associated idea swallows the null input, exactly as James describes for the amputee: "the life-long sensorial associate ... follows the idea and swallows it up" [PAGE 516]. Formally:
\[
B_{t+1} = \alpha B_t + (1-\alpha)\, \mathcal{L}(y_t \mid u_t) + \gamma D_t,
\]
with \(\mathcal{L} = 0\) under blocked movement or amputation. When \(\mathcal{L} = 0\), \(B_t\) is driven by \(\alpha\) (habit regression) and \(\gamma\) (demand counter-trap). The clock therefore favors crisis habit regression: the longer the null interval, the more the belief collapses to the invariant prior.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three veto layers independently falsify the claim that movement occurred.

Physical veto: The table edge imposes a rigid unilateral constraint on the forefinger. Force balance at the joint gives:
\[
F_{\text{flexor}}(\theta) \le F_{\text{max}}(\theta) < R_{\text{table}}(\theta)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Delta\theta_{\text{physical}} = 0 \;\text{exactly}.
\]
Any verbal report of terminal-joint flexion is falsified by rigid-body kinematics independent of psychology. This is the decisive veto: the movement did not occur in physical space.

Biomechanical veto: With the finger flexed back at the metacarpophalangeal joint, the flexor digitorum profundus operates at a length where active force is reduced by the force-length relation \(f_l(\theta)\), and the moment arm \(r(\theta)\) may approach a mechanical limit. Joint torque is:
\[
\tau(\theta) = r(\theta)\, F_{\text{muscle}}(\theta,u)\, f_l(\theta).
\]
At the extreme back-flexion, \(f_l(\theta) \to 0\) or \(r(\theta)\) becomes ineffective, so \(\tau \approx 0\) even for maximal \(u\). The terminal joint cannot flex because the actuator is mechanically disadvantaged. This veto operates at the tissue level and does not require invoking central association.

Weather veto: The experiment is conducted indoors at a tabletop, so ambient weather is nominally controlled. However, nerve conduction velocity \(v\) scales approximately with temperature \(T\), and if \(T < 15^\circ\text{C}\), conduction slows, increasing efferent–afferent latency. If the latency exceeds the association binding window (\(\tau_{\text{bind}} \approx 200\text{ ms}\)), the vivid associated idea may no longer be temporally bound to the command, weakening the illusion. At standard room temperature (\(T \approx 20\text{--}25^\circ\text{C}\)), \(v \approx 50\text{--}60\text{ m/s}\), and limb-loop latency is roughly 5–10 ms, well within the window; thus no weather-based veto is expected under ordinary conditions. Formal veto predicate:
\[
V = 1 \;\text{if}\; \bigl(R_{\text{ext}} > F_{\text{max}}(\theta)\bigr) \;\vee\; \bigl(|\Delta t_{\text{efferent}} - \Delta t_{\text{reafferent}}| > \tau_{\text{bind}}\bigr),
\]
otherwise \(V = 0\). In the finger experiment, \(V = 1\) because \(R_{\text{ext}} > F_{\text{max}}\) and \(\Delta\theta_{\text{measured}} = 0\). The felt movement is therefore a vetoed percept: physically impossible, biomechanically blocked, and only thermally vulnerable under non-standard cold.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a prediction and a physical readout.

Scenario 1: Tendon vibration without motor command.
Setup: Apply 80–100 Hz vibration to the flexor tendons of the forefinger to induce a kinesthetic illusion of movement while the subject makes no motor command (\(u = 0\), verified by surface EMG null). Verifiable readouts: goniometer joint angle \(\Delta\theta\), EMG, and verbal report.
Prediction: If the subject reports movement while \(\Delta\theta = 0\) and EMG \(= 0\), then reafference-like sensation can be generated by peripheral afferent noise alone, falsifying James's requirement that a "will to move" precede the hallucinatory association. If no movement is reported, James's account (will + association) is favored over a pure sensory-noise account.
Veto condition: Veto the pure-association model if report coincides with \(\Delta\theta \neq 0\) only; otherwise veto James's will-primacy claim.

Scenario 2: Exoskeleton-passive movement matched to report.
Setup: The subject attempts to flex the blocked terminal joint; a robotic exoskeleton passively flexes the joint by exactly the angle the subject reports having moved, synchronized with the attempt. Verifiable readouts: exoskeleton encoder \(\Delta\theta_{\text{robot}}\), EMG command profile, and agency attribution.
Prediction: If the sense of "I did it" strengthens when passive reafference matches the report, agency is bound to reafference congruence, not to the feeling of innervation per se, undermining Loeb's and James's will-impulse primacy. If the illusion vanishes because the movement is externally caused, then efferent responsibility is necessary for agency, supporting a corollary-discharge over a pure association account.
Veto condition: Veto the innervation-feeling model if subjects deny agency despite congruent reafference; veto the pure-re-afference model if agency persists without any motor command.

Scenario 3: Mirror-box visual feedback in an amputee.
Setup: An amputee attempts to move phantom toes while a mirror box reflects the intact foot moving, supplying visual reafference of phantom movement. Verifiable readouts: phantom-movement vividness on a 0–100 unitless visual analog scale, residual-limb EMG, and skin conductance.
Prediction: If congruent visual feedback reduces vividness reports, the phantom sensation depends on the absence of contradictory afference, confirming James's claim that the associate "is uncorrected by any opposite sensation" [PAGE 516]. If vividness is unchanged by congruent or incongruent feedback, the association is purely ideational and immune to sensory correction, indicating a strong hallucinatory prior rather than a correctable prediction error.
Veto condition: Veto the error-correction/predictive-coding account if vividness increases under incongruent feedback; veto the pure-ideational hallucination account if congruent visual feedback significantly suppresses vividness.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
