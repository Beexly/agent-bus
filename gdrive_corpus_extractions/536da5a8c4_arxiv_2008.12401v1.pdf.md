The source is an arXiv paper on automatic player identification in Dota 2, not a physical-sports or weather-data paper. The audit therefore has to keep three layers separate: (1) the paper’s actual operators—mouse-movement features, game-statistic rates, and itemization encodings; (2) the paper’s actual evaluation claims—95% same-player pair accuracy, 100% random-forest accuracy on game statistics in experiment 1, and degraded performance when some features are combined; and (3) claims the source cannot support—biomechanical causation, physical-device invariance, weather effects, or real-world deployment validity. The central multi-hop derivation is: identity is inferred from behavioral residual variance after conditioning on hero, team side, game mode, and patch. The paper holds hero constant and restricts data to All Pick, Radiant, and matches before 18 November 2018 to avoid patch 7.20 [PAGE 3]. That design controls confounders but also narrows generalization. Mouse data are not metric hand trajectories; they are replay cursor coordinates sampled on game ticks, with a 300 ms inactivity threshold defining movement sequences [PAGE 3]. The paper’s own table defines angle, curvature, velocity, acceleration, jerk, and angular velocity from coordinate and tick differences [PAGE 4]. Those derivatives are discrete finite differences, so curvature and jerk are singular or unstable when path-length increments approach zero. The paper notes move commands occur “multiple times a second,” making movement sequences very short [PAGE 6], which is exactly the regime where curvature and jerk estimates become noise-dominated.

The itemization branch is a proxy branch. The paper says item positions “can reﬂect a player’s keyboard bindings” but “It is not possible to extract a player’s personal settings from a replay, so using item positions are a close approximate” [PAGE 4]. That is a gray-zone admission: item position is not a direct biometric; it is an indirect, context-sensitive encoding. Starting items were more predictive than end-of-match items in experiment 1 [PAGE 7], but in experiment 2 the gap between starting-items-only and all-items one-hot narrowed, which the authors interpret as “a more diverse pool of players leads to more similarity in starting items between any two players” [PAGE 9]. So the strongest same-player claim is not “identity is in the mouse” but “identity is in the joint distribution of early build choices, aggregate performance rates, and command-linked mouse movement.”

The evaluation logic also has a label-noise trap. The paper explicitly assumes “all replays that were downloaded from the same account were matches by the same player” [PAGE 5]. That assumption is in tension with the paper’s own threat model: accounts “can be readily obtained, sold and shared between multiple people” [PAGE 1]. If an account is shared, then positive same-player pairs are contaminated. If one person uses multiple accounts, negative pairs are contaminated. The reported 95% is therefore an account-consistency result under an assumption, not a universal identity result. The pair-construction also creates a mirrored correlation matrix because matches are combined as {match0, match1} and {match1, match0}; the authors correctly say this “should not be taken as a meaningful result” [PAGE 8]. A careless downstream reader could mistake that symmetry for evidence of behavioral correlation.

The cross-layer audit must be veto-heavy. The paper does not measure physical mouse hardware, DPI, OS acceleration, grip force, joint kinematics, or weather. It does report that mouse dynamics data “tends to be inconsistent and error prone due to the varied hardware input devices” [PAGE 3]. That single quote vetoes any claim that the mouse operators are device-invariant. It also means the derived velocity, curvature, and jerk are screen-coordinate derivatives, not SI physical velocities. Any biomechanical interpretation would require calibration from cursor coordinates to hand displacement and force, which the source does not provide. Weather is even more clearly outside the operator set: there is no weather, temperature, humidity, network-latency, or ambient-environment variable in the feature list [PAGE 4]. The only admissible cross-layer statement is that input-device and account-sharing layers are explicit confounders, while biomechanical and weather layers are unsupported.

The recursive-theory-of-mind audit is about adversarial adaptation. Players know that behavior can be fingerprinted; the paper’s motivation includes cheating in amateur tournaments and account trading [PAGE 1]. A counter-trap would be deliberate behavioral alteration: changing mouse path shape, item slot placement, boot choice, or command style. The paper’s own feature hierarchy makes some counter-traps cheap: item positions are only a proxy for keybindings [PAGE 4], and boots were selected because they are near-universal but differentiated by active ability and player preference [PAGE 5]. Crisis-habit regression is the opposite hypothesis: under time pressure, players may revert to low-level habits, and the paper finds attack-linked mouse movements are the most predictive complex action even though they are less common than move actions [PAGE 6]. But the paper does not measure stress or crisis; it only observes that rare attack actions are informative. Clock effects are also only partially tested: more time-slices produced less accurate predictions, which the authors attribute to noise [PAGE 8], and individual time-slices showed little performance difference [PAGE 8]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s formal operators are the mouse-movement derivative features in Table 1 [PAGE 4], the sequence definition with threshold \(\tau\), and the classification metrics. The source does not report SI-calibrated physical units for cursor displacement; coordinates are replay cursor coordinates, \(t\) is game tick, and the inactivity threshold is in milliseconds. Thus the correct unit statement is: \(x,y\) are screen/replay coordinate units; \(t\) is game ticks; \(\tau\) is milliseconds; angle and angular velocity are in radians and radians per tick only if the coordinates are treated as angular or normalized quantities—otherwise the source leaves angular calibration unspecified. Accuracy, precision, and recall are dimensionless probabilities/rates in \([0,1]\).

The movement-sequence operator is defined by a threshold \(\tau\): a sequence ends if no cursor-position change occurs within \(\tau\). The paper states: “A threshold of 500 milliseconds was used in Feher et al.’s [11] study. This was reduced to 300 milliseconds for capturing Dota 2 mouse features as it was found a 500 millisecond threshold created fewer and longer mouse movement sequences than expected” [PAGE 3]. Each sequence is represented by three length-\(n\) vectors:
\[
t=\{t_i\}_{i=1}^{n},\quad x=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n},\quad y=\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n},
\]
where \(n\) is the number of game ticks [PAGE 3]. Table 1 then gives, as printed:
\[
\theta_i=\arctan\!\left(\frac{\delta y_1}{\delta x_1}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{i}\delta\theta_j,
\]
\[
c=\frac{\delta\theta}{\delta s},\qquad
\Delta c=\frac{\delta c}{\delta s},
\]
\[
V_x=\frac{\delta x}{\delta t},\qquad
V_y=\frac{\delta y}{\delta t},\qquad
V=\sqrt{\delta V_x^2+\delta V_y^2},
\]
\[
V'=\frac{\delta V}{\delta t},\qquad
V''=\frac{\delta V'}{\delta t},\qquad
w=\frac{\delta\theta_t}{\delta t}.
\]
These are the paper’s basic mouse-movement features [PAGE 4]. The notation is not fully normalized in the source; for example, the printed velocity expression uses \(\delta V_x^2\) rather than \(V_x^2\). A rigorous implementation must resolve that notation before treating \(V\) as a speed magnitude. The source’s own use is empirical: sequence-level derivatives are summarized by minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation, giving “a total of 38 features for complex mouse action” [PAGE 4].

Breakdown regimes are explicit in these operators. \(\arctan(\delta y_1/\delta x_1)\) is undefined when \(\delta x_1=0\) in a naive ratio implementation and must be handled as a quadrant-aware \(\operatorname{atan2}\) or limiting case; otherwise the angle jumps by \(\pi\). Curvature \(c=\delta\theta/\delta s\) is singular when \(\delta s=0\), i.e., when the cursor is stationary across a tick. Rate of change of curvature \(\Delta c=\delta c/\delta s\) is doubly singular because it inherits \(c\)’s singularity and divides by \(\delta s\) again. \(V_x,V_y,V\) are finite only if \(\delta t\neq 0\); game-tick quantization and duplicate timestamps create undefined or infinite derivatives. \(V'\) and \(V''\) amplify tick-level quantization, and \(w=\delta\theta_t/\delta t\) inherits angle wrapping. The paper’s observation that move commands are sent “multiple times a second, making the mouse movement sequence very short” [PAGE 6] places much of the Dota 2 data near the short-sequence, low-\(\delta s\) regime where curvature and jerk are least stable.

The classification metrics are:
\[
\mathrm{precision}=\frac{\mathrm{true\ positives}}{\mathrm{true\ positives}+\mathrm{false\ positives}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{recall}=\frac{\mathrm{true\ positives}}{\mathrm{true\ positives}+\mathrm{false\ negatives}},
\]
as stated on [PAGE 6]. Accuracy is described as “the number of correct predictions over the total number of predictions” [PAGE 6], i.e.,
\[
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{\#\{\hat y=y\}}{\#\{(\hat y,y)\}}\in[0,1].
\]
Breakdown regimes: precision is undefined when the denominator is zero (no positive predictions); recall is undefined when there are no positive ground-truth cases. The paper’s random-forest mouse result is exactly this gray zone: “The random forest classiﬁer has perfect precision but low recall, indicating a lack of false positives predicted” [PAGE 6]. Perfect precision with low recall is not evidence of a good detector; it is evidence of a conservative detector that may miss most true positives.

The feature-combination operator is a one-hidden-layer multi-layer perceptron that learns weights over separate feature models; the paper states that matches “split up into each feature and model separately before being combined” and that the MLP is used “so it can learn the relative weights for each feature” [PAGE 5]. There is no reported calibration loss, no uncertainty estimate, and no operator for device identity, player stress, network latency, or weather. The itemization operator is also a proxy: item positions “can reﬂect a player’s keyboard bindings,” but “It is not possible to extract a player’s personal settings from a replay, so using item positions are a close approximate” [PAGE 4]. Therefore any claim that item position is a direct biometric is vetoed by the source’s own approximation statement.

The main reported results must be read with their conditioning. Experiment 1 used “93 players with 356 matches” [PAGE 5]. The featured same-player pair result is “95%” accuracy from “a combination of starting items, game statistics and mouse movement features” [PAGE 9]. The paper also reports that “game statistics with a random forest classiﬁer can achieve high prediction rates but it could not combine poor features with good features without losing a lot of accuracy” [PAGE 10]. Table 2 shows why: mouse+stats random forest is 0.597, while stats+starting-items random forest is 1.0 [PAGE 8]. This is a feature-interaction breakdown: a high-performing block can be poisoned by a noisy block under a tree model. Conversely, logistic regression reaches 0.99 with stats+boots [PAGE 7]. The correct conclusion is not “mouse features are bad” but “model class and feature block interaction determine whether mouse features help or hurt.”

The paper’s scientific lineage is a fusion of behavioral biometrics and esports analytics. On the biometric side, it explicitly follows Feher et al.’s hierarchy of atomic and complex mouse actions [PAGE 3] and Gamboa and Fred’s feature construction from a memory game [PAGE 3]. It also cites Awad and Traore’s mouse-dynamics biometric work and Bhatnagar et al.’s comparison of mouse versus keyboard dynamics, noting that “mouse dynamics data tends to be inconsistent and error prone due to the varied hardware input devices” [PAGE 3]. On the esports side, it inherits Starcraft II player identification from Liu et al. [PAGE 2], Dota 2 role classification from Gao et al. and Eggert et al. [PAGE 2], and Dota 2 outcome prediction from Hodge et al. and Yang et al. [PAGE 2]. The key retrocausal shift is that earlier Dota 2 work often predicted roles, heroes, or match outcomes; this paper redirects the same replay-derived operators toward identity. That is a retrocausal inversion: features built to explain game outcomes become forensic identifiers of the person producing the outcomes.

The paper’s controls are historically specific. Data were restricted to “Game mode: All pick,” “Team: Radiant,” “Date: Before 18 November 2018 (to avoid changes from patch 7.20 affecting some of the existing work),” and “Hero id: Constant as the same hero was used for all the datasets” [PAGE 3]. These controls are not incidental; they remove confounders that would otherwise be attributed to identity. A multi-week trap is to deploy the model across a patch boundary or across heroes and assume the 95% transfers. The paper itself lists “Multiple heroes from the same player” as future work and warns that “the difference in playstyle of different heroes would interfere with the difference in player behavior” [PAGE 10]. Another multi-week trap is account drift: the paper assumes same-account replays are same-player [PAGE 5], but its own introduction says accounts can be “sold and shared between multiple people” [PAGE 1]. Over a multi-week window, account sharing, smurfing, and hardware changes can silently relabel the data.

A third multi-week trap is feature drift in itemization. The paper samples inventories “once at the beginning of a match and once at the end” to prevent variance [PAGE 5], and finds starting items more predictive than end-of-match items in experiment 1 [PAGE 7]. But it also notes that end-of-match items depend on “the strategy, composition and context of their team, the opposing team and how well they are doing” [PAGE 7]. Over weeks, meta shifts change which starting items and boots are popular, so a model trained on one meta can confuse “same player” with “same meta.” The paper’s selective one-hot encoding of starting items and boots reduces dimensionality from about 300 items to 30 and from 300×6 inventory locations to 42 boot features [PAGE 5], but that same selectivity makes the model sensitive to item-pool changes. A fourth trap is evaluation leakage through pair construction: the correlation matrix is mirrored because pairs are duplicated as {match0, match1} and {match1, match0}, which the authors say “should not be taken as a meaningful result” [PAGE 8]. Any downstream pipeline that treats both orderings as independent samples inflates apparent evidence. A fifth trap is time-slicing: more slices increased feature count but reduced accuracy, which the authors attribute to noise [PAGE 8]. Multi-week deployment would be tempted to add finer temporal resolution; the source warns that this can hurt.

The lineage also contains a positive lesson: the paper’s best same-player result is not from a single deep model but from a modular combination of heterogeneous blocks. That modularity is historically continuous with ensemble and feature-block models in behavioral biometrics, and it is why the paper can report both 0.99 logistic regression on stats+boots [PAGE 7] and 0.95 random forest on starting items+stats+mouse [PAGE 9]. The retrocausal genealogy is therefore not a straight line from “mouse biometrics” to “identity”; it is a branching tree in which game-state features, item proxies, and mouse dynamics each carry different failure modes.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive adversary model has three competing mechanisms. First, crisis-habit regression: under high cognitive load or time pressure, a player may revert to overlearned motor routines. The paper’s strongest mouse evidence is consistent with this: “mouse movements following an attack action were the most predictive, with movements following a spell cast being the least predictive” [PAGE 6]. Attack actions are 20% of complex actions, move actions 78%, and cast actions 2% [PAGE 6]. The interpretation is not that attack actions are intrinsically more “biomechanical”; it is that rare, intention-bearing command contexts carry more identity signal than ubiquitous move commands. If crisis compresses behavior toward habit, attack-linked sequences should become more, not less, identifiable—provided the player is not deliberately counteracting.

Second, counter-trap: a player who knows the fingerprinting scheme can alter the observable. The paper’s threat model makes this plausible: accounts can be sold and shared [PAGE 1], and cheating in amateur tournaments has occurred [PAGE 1]. Counter-traps include changing mouse path curvature, changing item slot placement, changing boot choice, or using another account. Item positions are especially vulnerable because they are only a proxy for keybindings: “It is not possible to extract a player’s personal settings from a replay, so using item positions are a close approximate” [PAGE 4]. A player who deliberately randomizes inventory slots defeats the item-position channel without changing skill. A player who switches boots defeats a boot-only model, though the paper notes boots are near-universal and differentiated by active ability and preference [PAGE 5]. The model’s random-forest precision-recall profile is relevant: perfect precision with low recall on mouse features [PAGE 6] means the detector is conservative. A counter-trap that makes behavior more variable may push true positives below threshold and increase false negatives rather than false positives.

Third, clock: identity is not static. The paper’s time-slice experiment found that additional slices “caused less accurate predictions” and that individual time-slices had “little effect on the accuracy of predictions” [PAGE 8]. This is a clock result in two senses. Within a match, phase changes do not produce a clean, slice-specific identity signal; across matches, the model is trained on a fixed patch and fixed hero [PAGE 3]. The recursive ToM implication is that an adversary can exploit the clock by waiting for a patch, changing hero, or changing hardware. The defender’s counter is not to add more features blindly—the paper shows that can add noise—but to condition on the clock and the device layer explicitly.

The recursive equilibrium is therefore: the defender observes command-linked mouse movements and early item choices; the adversary can alter both; the clock changes the meaning of both. The paper’s 95% is best interpreted as a point estimate under a cooperative labeling assumption [PAGE 5], not as an adversarial robustness result. A counter-trap-aware system would need to model the probability that the observed account is shared, the probability that the hardware changed, and the probability that the patch changed the item meta. The source provides none of those probabilities; it only provides the behavioral operators and the empirical accuracies.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the source does not record mouse model, DPI, polling rate, OS pointer acceleration, or physical desk setup. It explicitly warns that mouse dynamics “tends to be inconsistent and error prone due to the varied hardware input devices” [PAGE 3]. Therefore any claim that the mouse-derived \(V_x,V_y,V,c,\Delta c,V',V''\) are physical invariants of a player is vetoed. The equations are screen-coordinate finite differences; a hardware change can rescale \(x,y\) and \(\delta t\), shifting velocity and curvature without any change in the player. The physical layer is a confounder, not a controlled variable.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the paper has no joint-angle, force, EMG, anthropometric, or hand-trajectory data. It has replay cursor coordinates and command events. A right-click on terrain versus an enemy entity is encoded as a move versus attack command [PAGE 3], but that is game-intent labeling, not biomechanics. The paper’s item-position channel is also not biomechanical: it is a “close approximate” for keyboard bindings [PAGE 4]. Therefore any biomechanical causal claim—e.g., that a player’s tendon properties or grip force explain the classifier—is unsupported. The only admissible biomechanical statement is negative: the source cannot adjudicate biomechanics because the measurement layer is the game cursor, not the body.

Weather/environmental-layer veto: the feature list contains no weather, temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, network latency, time-of-day, or ambient-noise variable [PAGE 4]. The data collection parameters mention only game mode, team, date cutoff, and hero id [PAGE 3]. The paper mentions IP addresses are “rarely static [24] due to NAT gateways” [PAGE 1], which is a network-identity instability, not weather. Therefore any weather-effect claim is vetoed by absence of an operator. A weather hypothesis could only be tested by adding an exogenous weather covariate and re-running the same paired-match design; the source does not do that.

Cross-layer adjudication proof: the admissible cross-layer structure is a directed confounder graph in which hardware/device and account-sharing affect the observed feature blocks, while player identity is the latent target. The source gives direct evidence for the hardware edge [PAGE 3] and direct evidence for the account-sharing threat [PAGE 1]. It gives zero evidence for weather or biomechanics. Therefore the correct adjudication is: device and account layers can veto identity claims; biomechanical and weather layers cannot be invoked as explanations. The paper’s own result that “mouse + stats” random forest falls to 0.597 while “stats + starting items” reaches 1.0 [PAGE 8] is a cross-layer warning: a noisy low-level layer can overwhelm a high-level layer under a tree model. The veto is not that mouse data are useless; it is that mouse data are device-conditioned and model-dependent.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Hardware-swap counterfactual. Test: collect same-player, same-hero, same-account matches before and after a deliberate mouse/DPI/keybinding swap, with game statistics and starting items held as stable as possible. Veto condition: if mouse-only same-player pair accuracy falls to near the label-permutation baseline while stats+starting-items remains high, the claim that mouse features are a portable identity fingerprint is vetoed. Prediction from the source: mouse operators will shift because “mouse dynamics data tends to be inconsistent and error prone due to the varied hardware input devices” [PAGE 3]; complex attack actions, which are “the most predictive” [PAGE 6], should degrade most because they depend on fine path shape. A successful model would need an explicit device-normalization operator, which the paper does not provide.

2. Account-sharing counterfactual. Test: take one account used by two different players and compare same-account cross-player pairs against different-account cross-player pairs. Veto condition: if the model trained under the paper’s assumption— “all replays that were downloaded from the same account were matches by the same player” [PAGE 5]—assigns high same-player probability to cross-player pairs from the shared account, the 95% same-player result is vetoed as an identity claim and reinterpreted as an account-consistency claim. Prediction: label noise will inflate false positives; the effect should be largest for itemization and game-statistic blocks, because those are strategic and role-dependent, while mouse features may show a smaller but still nonzero shift. The paper’s own motivation that accounts can be “sold and shared between multiple people” [PAGE 1] makes this a direct, not hypothetical, stress test.

3. Patch/hero drift counterfactual. Test: train on the paper’s restricted regime—“Before 18 November 2018” and “Hero id: Constant” [PAGE 3]—and evaluate on post-7.20 matches and on multiple heroes. Veto condition: if the 95% same-player accuracy does not remain above a pre-registered deployment threshold, or if feature-importance rankings invert, the generalization claim is vetoed. Prediction: itemization features will drift most because starting items and boots are meta-dependent; mouse features will drift less in shape but may drift in command-context distribution because hero abilities change which complex spell actions occur. The paper itself flags multi-hero generalization as unknown: “it is not known how it would work in a dataset that contained multiple heroes” [PAGE 10]. A second prediction is that adding more time-slices will not rescue drift, because the paper already found that additional slices “caused less accurate predictions” [PAGE 8]. The correct RLVR reward is therefore not “higher accuracy on the old regime” but “calibrated accuracy under explicit device, account, patch, and hero shift,” with a veto if any of those shifts collapses the same-player signal to chance.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
