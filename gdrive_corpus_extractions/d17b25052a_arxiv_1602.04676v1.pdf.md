The paper reduces a two-player two-round zero-sum game to a pure-exploration stochastic bandit problem. Player A possesses K actions; for each action i, Player B can answer with K_i actions j. The unknown win probability for Player A is µ_{i,j} ∈ [0,1], observed through Bernoulli rollouts when arm P=(i,j) is pulled [PAGE 2]. Player A controls both sides during the learning phase and is not actually playing the adversary [PAGE 2]. The target is the maximin action i* = argmax_i min_j µ_{i,j} [PAGE 2], under the maintained assumption that Player B best-responds by picking j minimizing µ_{i,j} for any fixed i [PAGE 2]. The fixed-confidence δ-PAC criterion is P_µ( min_j µ_{i*,j} − min_j µ_{î,j} ≤ ε ) ≥ 1 − δ [PAGE 3, eq. (1)]. This criterion is strictly weaker than full best-arm identification because the algorithm need only certify that some arm in group i is above the worst arms of every other group; it does not need to rank µ_{2,1} against µ_{2,2} [PAGE 10].

Sample complexity is governed by H*(µ) in Theorem 1 [PAGE 5], which sums over all arms an inverse squared gap to either the within-group minimum µ_{i,1} or a “virtual arm” midpoint related to (µ_{1,1}+µ_{2,1})/2 [PAGE 6, Appendix A]. The virtual arm is explicitly called a proof artifact [PAGE 6], yet it inflates required draws relative to Maximin-Racing, whose Theorem 8 bound avoids it for arms outside P_1 [PAGE 9]. Gray-zone regimes where the theory breaks down include: (i) µ_{1,1} = µ_{2,1}, where the maximin gap vanishes and H* diverges as 1/Δ², so no finite-sample δ-PAC guarantee exists at ε=0; (ii) ε = 0 with non-distinct arms, for which Theorem 8 requires pairwise distinct means [PAGE 9]; (iii) Bernoulli means at the boundary 0 or 1, where the binary relative entropy d(x,y) becomes degenerate and Hoeffding-type intervals are overly conservative or invalid [PAGE 7]; (iv) non-stationary opponent response, which violates the fixed-µ bandit reduction and empties the δ-PAC guarantee because the learning phase assumes B plays the minimizing response [PAGE 2].

A second gray zone is the exploration-rate tradeoff. β(t,δ) must be large enough to protect event E = ∩_t {µ_P ∈ [L_P(t),U_P(t)]} [PAGE 6, Theorem 1] but small enough to keep T(µ,δ) = inf{t : 4H*(µ)β(t,δ) < t} small [PAGE 6]. The maximin problem therefore draws on pure-exploration best-arm identification literature: Even-Dar et al. (2006), Audibert et al. (2010) [PAGE 2], Kalyanakrishnan et al. (2012) LUCB [PAGE 4], Gabillon et al. (2012) UGapE and unified fixed-budget/fixed-confidence approaches [PAGE 3, 13], Jamieson et al. (2014) lil’UCB [PAGE 13], and Kaufmann and Kalyanakrishnan (2013) KL-Racing [PAGE 8–9]. The refined Chernoff/GLRT stopping rule adapts Garivier and Kaufmann (2016) on optimal best-arm identification with fixed confidence [PAGE 7, 11]. Competitive MDP context is supplied by Filar and Vrieze (1996) [PAGE 3].

Multi-week traps. In a multi-week hyperparameter sweep, three traps recur. First, defaulting to UCB/regret minimization: because the learning phase has Player A choosing arms for both players [PAGE 2], a regret agent over-values arms it wins and under-samples the adversary’s minimizing response, directly mis-targeting the max-min objective. Second, applying vanilla best-arm identification to all arms forces discrimination between µ_{2,1} and µ_{2,2}, which maximin identification does not require when one arm in group 2 is already certified below µ_{1,1} [PAGE 10]; Table 1 shows this inflates draws on arm (2,2) by roughly an order of magnitude relative to M-LUCB [PAGE 10]. Third, using Hoeffding confidence bounds near Bernoulli boundaries: Corollaries 2–3 provide explicit but conservative β(t,δ) [PAGE 6], while M-Chernoff in Table 1 reduces arm (1,1) draws from 1762 to 315 under µ_1 [PAGE 10], demonstrating that source-bound choice dominates runtime more than algorithm selection. A fourth trap is setting ε = 0 when µ_{1,1} ≤ µ_{2,1}, violating the stopping-rule assumption stated in Section 2 [PAGE 5] and producing non-termination or vacuous PAC guarantees.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. Under time or compute pressure, an agent recurses to its crisis habit: greedy selection of the arm with highest empirical mean, or standard UCB over all arms. That habit maximizes expected immediate win probability, not the maximin criterion min_j µ_{i,j}, and it ignores the second-order assumption that Player B best-responds by minimizing µ_{i,j} for each fixed i [PAGE 2]. The resulting theory-of-mind failure is two-layered: layer one mis-identifies i* because it ranks marginal arm means instead of group lower envelopes; layer two assumes the identified maximin action remains optimal against a real opponent who may not play the minimizing response j = argmin_j µ_{i,j} during actual gameplay [PAGE 2]. The bandit model is explicitly a learning phase in which A chooses actions for both players and is not playing a game [PAGE 2]; therefore the δ-PAC guarantee conditions on a stationary opponent whose response function is fixed and known only through µ.

Counter-trap: enforce the maximin sampling discipline encoded in eq. (2) [PAGE 5], which stops only when the lower confidence bound of the recommended action’s within-group minimum exceeds, up to slack ε, the upper confidence bound of at least one arm in every rival group. This bypasses intra-group worst-arm ranking and concentrates samples on H_t and S_t [PAGE 4–5]. The clock variable is the fixed-confidence stopping time τ versus a fixed-budget horizon. As δ → 0, Theorem 4 and Theorem 5 establish E_µ[τ_δ] / log(1/δ) ≤ 4H*(µ) (general) and a tighter two-action constant [PAGE 6–7], so the clock budget must scale logarithmically in inverse risk, not linearly in arm count. If the operational clock imposes a hard deadline smaller than τ(µ,δ), the agent must either increase ε (accepting an ε-maximin rather than exact maximin action) or relax δ, moving from fixed-confidence to fixed-budget mode where the optimality certificates of Theorems 1, 4, 5, 8 no longer apply.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical / biomechanical / weather veto proof. The source is abstract stochastic bandit theory with no physical actuators, but any embodied deployment (robotics, physical game simulation, real-time control) inherits five veto layers.

Veto 1 — Bernoulli boundary saturation: if any µ_{i,j} ∈ {0,1}, the binary KL d(x,y) diverges and Hoeffding confidence intervals become either degenerate or invalid; physical win/loss sensors that saturate remove the stochastic exploration required by eq. (3) [PAGE 5] and eq. (8) [PAGE 9]. Veto condition: all arms must have strictly interior means or the algorithm must use bounded-likelihood modifications not present in the paper.

Veto 2 — i.i.d. stationarity: every rollout X_t for arm P must be an independent Bernoulli draw with fixed mean µ_P [PAGE 2]. Weather, fatigue, or opponent policy drift introduce time-varying µ_P(t) or correlated noise, invalidating the event E = ∩_t {µ_P ∈ [L_P(t),U_P(t)]} [PAGE 6] and the change-of-measure lower bound [PAGE 11–12]. Veto condition: stationarity and independence must be verified per arm before deployment.

Veto 3 — compute/clock hard deadline: each sample is a rollout consuming compute; Theorem 1 gives τ ≤ T(µ,δ) only if 4H*(µ)β(T,δ) < T [PAGE 6]. If the physical game clock enforces a deadline T_clock < T(µ,δ), fixed-confidence δ-PAC identification is infeasible and the system must switch to fixed-budget logic, for which the paper provides no matching optimality guarantee [PAGE 3].

Veto 4 — hidden information: Poker and Scrabble are mentioned as motivating examples [PAGE 1], but the formal model is a two-player full-information two-round game [PAGE 2]. Hidden state breaks the arm reduction P = {(i,j)} unless belief states are augmented, which is outside the paper’s scope. Veto condition: full observability of the game configuration at each decision point.

Veto 5 — adversarial non-stationarity of Player B: the model assumes B picks j minimizing µ_{i,j} [PAGE 2]. If B adapts, the arm means µ_{i,j} are no longer fixed parameters but functions of B’s policy, invalidating the Bernoulli bandit reduction. Veto condition: opponent response stationarity over the identification window.
Deployment is permitted only if all five veto checks pass; otherwise the algorithm output is not δ-PAC and must be suppressed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Zero-maximin-gap singularity. Construct a 2×2 instance with µ_{1,1} = µ_{2,1} = 0.40, µ_{1,2} = 0.50, µ_{2,2} = 0.45. Then Δ = µ_{1,1} − µ_{2,1} = 0, so H*(µ) contains terms proportional to 1/Δ² and diverges [PAGE 5–6]. Prediction: for ε = 0, M-LUCB’s stopping time τ_δ grows without bound as δ → 0; no finite-sample δ-PAC identification is possible. Verification: run N = 10^4 repetitions at δ ∈ {0.1, 0.01, 0.001} and record median τ_δ; the ratio τ_δ / log(1/δ) must increase monotonically and exceed any fixed compute budget for sufficiently small δ. Veto prediction: if measured τ_δ exceeds the allocated rollout budget at δ = 0.01, the deployment is vetoed under Scenario 1.

Scenario 2 — Vanilla best-arm over-allocation trap. Compare M-LUCB against standard LUCB applied to all four arms under µ_3 = [[0.4, 0.5],[0.3, 0.6]] [PAGE 10]. Standard LUCB must discriminate arm (2,2) from arm (2,1) because best-arm identification ranks all arms, whereas maximin identification only requires certifying some arm in group 2 below µ_{1,1} [PAGE 10]. Prediction: E[τ(2,2)] under vanilla LUCB scales as Θ( 1 / (µ_{2,2} − µ_{2,1})² ) = Θ(1 / 0.3²) and will be orders of magnitude larger than under M-LUCB; Table 1 already shows M-LUCB draws arm (2,2) only 36 times while KL-LUCB draws it 32 times only because µ_{2,2} and µ_{2,1} are far apart [PAGE 10], but the asymptotic gap widens as the intra-group gap shrinks. Verification: measure arm-specific draws τ_{2,2} over N = 10^4 repetitions; if τ_{2,2}^{LUCB} / τ_{2,2}^{M-LUCB} > 50 at δ = 0.1, veto vanilla LUCB for maximin tasks.

Scenario 3 — Confidence-bound misspecification near boundaries. Use Hoeffding-based M-LUCB with β(t,δ) from Corollary 2 [PAGE 6] on means near the boundary, e.g. µ_{1,1} = 0.99, µ_{2,1} = 0.98, µ_{1,2} = 0.995, µ_{2,2} = 0.97. Hoeffding intervals are symmetric and sub-Gaussian, while the Bernoulli KL d(x,y) is asymmetric and tighter near 0 and 1 [PAGE 7]. Prediction: M-Chernoff (empirical) stops earlier than M-LUCB-Hoeffding; Table 1 demonstrates an analogous gap under µ_1 where M-Chernoff draws arm (1,1) 315 times versus M-LUCB’s 1762 [PAGE 10]. Verification: at δ = 0.1, ε = 0, compare stopping times over N = 10^4 repetitions; if τ^{Hoeffding} / τ^{Chernoff} > 3 and the Hoeffding run exceeds the real-time rollout budget, veto the Hoeffding configuration and require Chernoff/GLRT intervals per Section 2.2 [PAGE 7].
