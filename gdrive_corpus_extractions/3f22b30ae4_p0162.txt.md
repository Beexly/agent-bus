<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. The page is drawn from Clark L. Hull's Principles of Behavior (1943), the canonical statement of neo-behaviorist drive-reduction theory. The concept that reinforcement efficacy declines with delay—the delay-of-reinforcement gradient—was central to Hull's system and to earlier Hullian experiments on delayed reward. Anderson (1) and Yoshioka (17) are cited on this page as independent empirical verifiers of Corollaries V and VI; Yoshioka's use of alternate paths to a goal generalizes the finding beyond a single runway to a spatial choice maze. [PAGE 153] This tradition connects forward to modern delay-discounting formalisms (e.g., hyperbolic discounting models in which subjective value V = A / (1 + kD), with amount A, delay D, and discount rate k), where a larger delay ratio produces steeper differential discounting and faster choice polarization. The "coarser ratio" principle anticipates the ratio effect in intertemporal choice: widely separated delays are discriminated more readily than closely spaced delays.

Multi-week traps. In longitudinal or repeated-measures designs, the short-delay dominance established early under a coarse ratio is vulnerable to several traps over weeks. First, satiation and changes in drive strength can alter delay sensitivity, so a ratio that produced rapid dominance in week 1 may yield weak dominance by week 4. Second, explicit contingency detection may replace gradient-based learning: once the subject recognizes the contingency structure, choice may shift toward maximizing overall reinforcement rate rather than preferring the shorter delay, eroding the Corollary VI effect. Third, reversal or ratio thinning (moving from r = 3 toward r = 1.5 or r = 1) after dominance has crystallized produces a behavioral contrast transient in which the previously dominant short-delay act is temporarily over-selected before re-equilibration. Fourth, inter-trial interval manipulations over weeks can confound absolute delay with session pace, especially at the 10-30 second end of Table 4. These multi-week dynamics mean that a single-session finding of faster dominance under coarse ratios does not guarantee stable preference across a training epoch.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under reinforcement scarcity or response-contingent pressure, the organism (or modeled agent) regresses to the shortest-delay habit because Corollary VI implies that short-delay dominance is acquired with the fewest reinforcements. In a crisis, the default policy is therefore the act tied to the lesser delay, not necessarily the act with the highest long-run yield.

Counter-trap. A strategic opponent or system designer who knows the delay-ratio law can establish short-delay dominance quickly by imposing a coarse ratio (e.g., 30 s versus 90 s, r = 3; dominance between reinforcements 15 and 20) [PAGE 153], then covertly equalize or reverse the delays. Because the habit was built on fewer trials, it is also brittle: the subject persists with the now-suboptimal short-delay act for a measurable persistence window before regressing. The counter-trap consists of inducing dominance under a coarse ratio, then switching contingencies during the window in which the subject's internal model still expects the old delay structure.

Clock. The reinforcement count is the operative clock. For r = 3, dominance crystallizes between reinforcements 15 and 20; for r = 2, between reinforcements 35 and 40. [PAGE 153] An observer who misestimates the clock—for example, treating an r = 2 schedule as if it were r = 3—will intervene either before dominance has formed (wasting the intervention) or after the habit is locked (missing the exploitation window). Recursive theory of mind enters when the subject models the experimenter's manipulation of this clock: the subject may alter its response timing or choice variance to reset or stretch the dominance acquisition schedule, effectively playing a second-order game over the reinforcement-count clock itself.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The apparatus must physically impose distinct path delays without altering start-box release, goal-box access, or runway cues. If the maze or alley cannot generate the required D_l - D_s separation—for example, if 10 s versus 20 s requires a path-length difference below the resolution of the running surface or the animal's stride—then the delay manipulation is confounded with spatial or proprioceptive cues, and the experiment is vetoed at the physical layer.

Biomechanical layer. Running speed, fatigue, and the inter-trial interval (ITI) impose constraints. At short absolute delays (10 s, 15 s, 20 s), the ITI may be a substantial fraction of the total trial duration, so the experienced delay is not well approximated by the programmed delay alone; this violates the "other things equal" clause of Corollary VI. Across 35-40 reinforcements, cumulative fatigue can change running speed non-stationarily, further decoupling programmed delay from experienced delay. These biomechanical factors are most severe for the short-delay pairs in Table 4 and weaken the internal validity of the ratio law at that end.

Weather layer. The experiments described are indoor operant/maze studies with controlled ambient conditions; temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure are not reported as variables and do not enter the reported results. The weather veto is therefore null for this source. If the findings were extrapolated to outdoor or field settings, ambient temperature would alter locomotor speed and thus the experienced delay, triggering a weather veto unless climate is controlled or modeled.

Adjudication. The physical and biomechanical layers support validity within the tested envelope (approximately 10-360 s, controlled laboratory, [PAGE 153]), but the strongest veto is not environmental: it is documentary. The OCR-corrupted Table 4, the 84% versus 87% footnote conflict, and the absence of the generating theoretical equation on this page prevent exact quantitative replication from this source alone. Weather does not veto; physical and biomechanical layers veto only at the extremes of short absolute delay and large path-length difference.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Veto scenario 1 — Equality boundary (r = 1). Program equal delays (e.g., 60 s : 60 s) so that r = 1 exactly. Verifiable prediction: the proportion of choices for the nominally shorter-delay act remains at chance, P_s = 0.50 ± 0.05, across all reinforcement counts N = 1 to 50; no short-delay dominance emerges at any point. If empirical P_s exceeds 0.60 systematically, the ratio law is violated or the delays are not actually equated. This veto tests the r -> 1 singularity.

Veto scenario 2 — Absolute-delay floor violation. Use a coarse ratio r = 3 but with very short absolute delays (e.g., D_s = 2 s, D_l = 6 s) where D_s approaches or falls below the species' delay-discrimination threshold and the ITI dominates trial time. Verifiable prediction: even after 40 reinforcements, P_s does not significantly exceed chance, contradicting the Corollary VI expectation that a coarse ratio rapidly produces dominance. The breakdown is at the absolute-time scale, not the ratio scale.

Veto scenario 3 — Contingency reversal after habit lock. Train with r = 3 (30 s : 90 s) until short-delay dominance is reached in the 15-20 reinforcement window, [PAGE 153] then reverse the delays (90 s : 30 s). Verifiable prediction: choices for the act now associated with 90 s remain elevated for at least 5-10 post-reversal reinforcements due to habit inertia, then regress toward the newly shorter 30 s act; if the subject switches immediately and perfectly, the pre-reversal behavior was not delay-dominance habit but local stimulus control. This veto tests the recursive crisis-habit-regression clock.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
