**1. Core Judgment Operator:**
The organism maintains a posterior belief over the value \( V_i \) of each potential means \( i \) (e.g., a specific nipple shape or seed size). This belief is updated upon observing outcome \( O_t \) (satiation or not) after selecting means \( a_t = i \).
\[
P(V_i | \mathcal{O}_{1:t}) \propto P(\mathcal{O}_{1:t} | V_i) P(V_i)
\]
where \( P(V_i) \) is a prior reflecting innate biases (e.g., preference for larger seeds), and the likelihood \( P(O_t | V_i) \) models the probabilistic relationship between selecting \( i \) and achieving satiation. The units of \( V_i \) are dimensionless probabilities (\( [0,1] \)), representing the expected reward rate.

**2. Selection Policy (Softmax with Temperature):**
The probability of selecting means \( i \) given current sensory evidence \( E_i \) (which influences the posterior) and internal state \( S \) (hunger) is:
\[
P(a_t = i | E_i, S) = \frac{\exp(\beta(S) \cdot \mathbb{E}[V_i | E_i])}{\sum_j \exp(\beta(S) \cdot \mathbb{E}[V_j | E_j])}
\]
Here, \( \beta(S) \) is the inverse temperature parameter, which is a monotonic increasing function of hunger \( S \). High hunger (\( S \rightarrow S_{max} \)) makes \( \beta \) large, leading to greedy exploitation of the highest-value estimate. Low hunger (\( S \rightarrow 0 \)) makes \( \beta \rightarrow 0\), resulting in uniform random selection, consistent with Yoshioka's finding that preference disappears when not hungry [PAGE 30].

**3. Docility as Learning Rate Modulation:**
The "docile" nature—the adjustment based on past success—is captured by the learning rate \( \alpha \) in the posterior update. We posit that \( \alpha \) itself is a function of prediction error magnitude and perhaps the strength of the prior:
\[
\alpha_t = f(\delta_t, \|V_i - \bar{V}\|)
\]
where \( \delta_t \) is the TD-error. A high prior strength (strong innate bias) could lead to a smaller \( \alpha \), making the readiness "stubborn" and requiring "a lot of proving" to change [PAGE 30].

**4. Breakdown Regimes & Singularities:**
*   **Perceptual Gate Failure:** The operator assumes reliable \( E_i \). In darkness, \( E_i \) for size is absent (\( E_{size} = \emptyset \)). The policy then defaults to a baseline, potentially equal selection, as observed [PAGE 30]. Formally, if \( E_i \notin \mathcal{F} \) (relevant feature set), then \( \mathbb{E}[V_i | E_i] = \bar{V} \) (global mean).
*   **Saturation Singularity:** When \( \mathbb{E}[V_i] \rightarrow 1 \) for some \( i \), the softmax becomes deterministic (\( P \rightarrow 1 \) for that \( i \)), eliminating exploration. This represents the fully "attached" state.
*   **Prior Dominance Regime:** If the prior \( P(V_i) \) is sharply peaked (very strong innate bias), even substantial contradictory evidence may not shift the posterior significantly, explaining the difficulty in proving the infant "wrong."

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman's work sits at a critical juncture in the history of behavioral science, reacting against the dominant S-R (stimulus-response) paradigm of Watson and early Skinner. His proposal of "means-end-readinesses" and "cognitive maps" was a direct attempt to introduce purposeful, goal-directed organization into behavior without invoking unobservable mentalism. The lineage can be traced back to Gestalt psychology's emphasis on holistic perception and purposive action, and forward to modern reinforcement learning and predictive processing theories.

The "retrocausal" aspect is profound. Tolman posits that the *future goal state* (satiation) organizes present behavior. The organism doesn't just react to stimuli; it acts *in light of* what those stimuli mean for the goal. This creates a narrative where past successful actions are recruited as evidence for the current choice, giving the illusion that the goal pulls the past into service. Historically, this view was controversial because it seemed to violate strict temporal locality of cause and effect.

A multi-week trap emerges from this genealogy: the **"Intentional Stance Trap."** If we interpret all behavior through the lens of goals and beliefs (as Tolman encourages), we risk attributing cognition where there is only complex allostasis. For instance, a simple chemotaxis gradient-following bacterium exhibits goal-directed movement toward nutrients. Does it possess a "means-end-readiness"? Tolman's framework, especially with its emphasis on "judgment," draws a fuzzy line. The trap is assuming that because a system *appears* to optimize for a goal, it must internally represent that goal. This confuses the descriptive utility of the intentional stance with an explanatory causal mechanism. The docility of readinesses could be entirely explained by gradient ascent on a fitness landscape without any internal "judgment" about means and ends.

Furthermore, the historical progression from Tolman's purposive behaviorism to contemporary "active inference" shows a conceptual continuity. Both treat the organism as minimizing surprise or free energy, where priors (innate readinesses) are updated by data (experience). Tolman was essentially describing a biological implementation of Bayesian model selection decades before the mathematics existed. The danger is retrofitting modern computational concepts onto his phenomenological descriptions, thereby missing his original insight about the *organismic* nature of purpose—how physiology, environment, and history co-constitute a "ready" state.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

The organism must maintain a representation of its own motivational state (hunger/satiation) and map actions to predicted changes in that state. **Crisis Habit Regression:** Consider an infant who has strongly attached to a specific bottle (high \( \mathbb{E}[V_{bottle}] \approx 1 \)). If the caregiver suddenly introduces a different-tasting formula, the infant experiences a large prediction error: action (sucking bottle) does not lead to expected outcome (pleasant taste/satiation). Under crisis (persistent mismatch), the system may regress to the broader, pre-attachment readiness ("anything nipple-like"). This is a safe, high-entropy policy that guarantees some chance of finding a working means, at the cost of efficiency. It’s a fallback to a shallower cognitive map.

**Counter-Trap Mechanism:** To avoid being trapped by a false positive (the old bottle), the system needs a counter-trap: a process that actively discounts the prior value of the failed means and increases exploration. This could be mediated by neuromodulators like dopamine signaling negative prediction errors. The difficulty Tolman notes—"it may take a lot of proving"—suggests this counter-trap has a high activation threshold. The system resists updating because the cost of abandoning a once-reliable strategy is high (potential starvation during exploration). Thus, persistence in a mistaken readiness is not a bug but a feature of a conservative, survival-oriented optimizer.

**Clock Interaction:** The timescale is critical. Hunger operates on a circadian and post-prandial clock. The readiness is not static; its temperature \( \beta(S) \) oscillates with meals. An infant might be highly selective (low entropy choice) an hour after a feeding if presented with a novel object, but become indiscriminate (high entropy) after several hours of hunger. The "judgment" is therefore time-indexed, pulsing with metabolic need. This rhythmic modulation prevents permanent fixation and allows periodic re-evaluation of the environment.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cognitive/judgmental layer proposed by Tolman is always subject to veto from lower-level physical and biomechanical systems. These vetoes are non-negotiable constraints that can override a preferred choice.

**1. Biomechanical Veto (Mastication vs. Seed Size):**
While a tame albino rat may visually prefer a large sunflower seed due to higher estimated caloric yield (\( \mathbb{E}[V_{large}] > \mathbb{E}[V_{small}] \)), its actual consumption is gated by its gnathostomatic apparatus. If the seed exceeds the mechanical capacity of its incisors and jaw muscles to crack efficiently, a biomechanical veto occurs. The rat will either reject the large seed or fail to extract the kernel, rendering the visual preference meaningless. This veto is absolute: no amount of hunger or learned association can make the rat successfully consume a physically indigestible item. Yoshioka’s use of "large" and "small" seeds within the consumable range is crucial; his results hold only within the biomechanical operating window.

**2. Physiological Veto (Nausea/Toxicity Override):**
In the infant case, suppose the attached nipple delivers milk laced with a mild emetic. The gustatory and visceral receptors will signal nausea, triggering a strong physiological veto. The sucking behavior ceites immediately, regardless of the infant's hunger level and the strength of the learned association. This veto operates via brainstem circuits (e.g., area postrema) that bypass higher cognitive evaluation. It demonstrates that the "means-end-readiness" is subordinate to homeostatic emergency protocols.

**3. Environmental/Weather Veto (Resource Availability):**
Consider the rat in a maze where the goal box contains sunflower seeds. A severe cold snap reduces ambient temperature. The rat's thermoregulatory demands increase, raising its overall metabolic rate and thus its hunger level \( S \). While this might intensify the motivation to seek food, the cold also affects the rat's locomotor efficiency. Shivering or vasoconstriction may slow movement, altering the effective cost-benefit calculus of traveling to different goal locations. Furthermore, if the cold causes the seeds to freeze solid, their palatability and digestibility drop, potentially overriding the size-based preference. The weather acts as an exogenous variable that perturbs multiple layers simultaneously (motivation, motor execution, reward quality), demonstrating that the judgmental readiness is not autonomous but embedded in a dynamic ecological network.

These vetoes prove that Tolman’s cognitive constructs are not sufficient statistics for predicting behavior; they are necessary but not sufficient conditions, always mediated by a hierarchy of physical filters.
