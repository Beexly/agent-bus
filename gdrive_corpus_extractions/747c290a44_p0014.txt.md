A sophisticated user can exploit this by placing items in a cart to trigger desired similar-items lists, then abandoning the cart. Because the offline table generation process 66 is driven by community purchase history rather than cart-abandonment, the counter-trap operates mainly at the real-time inference layer: transient cart contents distort $K_u$ and thus the retrieved union $\mathcal{R}(u)$, creating a short-lived manipulation window that expires when the cart is cleared or the session ends.

Clock — session and batch temporal bounds. Recommendations are invoked “in real-time in response to an online action” [PAGE 9], yet table 60 is generated offline [PAGE 9]. The user’s decision clock (session duration, cart lifetime, e-mail open latency) competes with the batch refresh clock of process 66. If the user delays beyond the clock window, cart contents change, $K_u$ changes, and previously computed recommendations become stale. The system therefore carries an implicit half-life tied to session timeout and batch period, not to the timelessness of the lifetime purchase counts used to compute CI [PAGE 9].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/environmental veto proof across system layers.

Layer 1 — Physical / computational veto. Table 60 is produced exclusively by offline table generation process 66 from community purchase histories [PAGE 9]. There is no online incremental CI update path described for a single purchase event. Therefore, if the offline batch pipeline fails, is delayed, or operates with period $T_{\text{batch}}$ exceeding the real-time service-level latency, the physical layer vetoes personalized recommendations: $\mathcal{R}(u)$ must be served from a stale snapshot or suppressed entirely. Veto condition: $t_{\text{query}} - t_{\text{last\_batch}} > \tau_{\text{SLA}} \Rightarrow \text{stale-CI veto}$.

Layer 2 — Biomechanical / interaction-signal veto. User interest is inferred from implicit biomechanical traces (cart adds, page views, viewing duration) and explicit traces (ratings above threshold two) [PAGE 9]. The disclosure marks several of these as optional: viewing activities “could” be used; items not popular “can optionally be ignored”; ratings require a threshold [PAGE 9]. If a user’s session consists only of brief views that do not meet the optional extended-viewing criterion and no cart, purchase, or rating events occur, then $K_u$ collapses to $\emptyset$ and step 82 has no lists to retrieve [PAGE 9]. Veto condition: biomechanical signal sparsity $\Rightarrow K_u = \emptyset \Rightarrow \mathcal{R}(u) = \emptyset$.

Layer 3 — Environmental / macro-shock veto. CI aggregates lifetime community purchases [PAGE 9], integrating exogenous shocks (regional network outages, holidays, flash sales, weather-driven demand anomalies) into the same affinity matrix as stable item substitutions. A correlated purchase surge caused by an environmental event inflates CI for item pairs that are only situationally complementary. Because table 60 is not regenerated continuously [PAGE 9], the environmental layer can veto the validity of CI until the next offline refresh: post-shock, $CI(i \to j)$ reflects event noise rather than enduring affinity. Adjudication rule: upon detected distribution shift in purchase arrival rates, downweight or bypass $S(i)$ and fall back to non-personalized popular items until batch regeneration.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions. RLVR = counterfactual verification of recommendation-layer veto rules.

Scenario 1 — Cold-start item exclusion (popularity threshold veto).
Setup: A new item $i^*$ is introduced at time $t_0$. Over the following two weeks it is purchased by $k < 30$ distinct customers. Veto rule: $\text{Pop}(i^*)$ is False because $k \ngtr 30$ [PAGE 9]; therefore $i^* \notin \mathcal{P}$ and $i^*$ receives no entry in table 60 and no similar-items list. Prediction: Recall of $i^*$ from the item-to-item module is exactly zero for all users until its lifetime purchaser count crosses 30, regardless of content-based relevance or viewing behavior. Verifiable metric: proportion of recommendations containing $i^*$ remains 0% for the first $W$ weeks, where $W = \lceil 30 / \lambda_{purchase} \rceil$ and $\lambda_{purchase}$ is the weekly purchase rate.

Scenario 2 — Batch-staleness suppression during demand surge (offline refresh veto).
Setup: Table 60 is regenerated weekly by process 66 [PAGE 9]. At a flash sale in week 2, items A and B are co-purchased at 100× baseline rates for 48 hours. Veto rule: $CI(A \to B)$ used in real-time recommendations still reflects the pre-surge batch because $t_{\text{query}} - t_{\text{last\_batch}} < T_{\text{batch}}$ but the batch predates the surge. Prediction: Cross-sell lift for $B$ given $A$ during the surge is suppressed by a factor approximately proportional to $\Delta t / T_{\text{batch}}$, where $\Delta t$ is time since last batch; observed $CI(A \to B)$ will not capture the surge until the next offline regeneration. Verifiable metric: recommendation hit-rate for surge-correlated pairs remains at baseline until table refresh.

Scenario 3 — Anonymous user empty-known-interest null (session initialization veto).
Setup: A new, unauthenticated session begins with no purchase history, empty cart, no ratings, and no qualifying views. Veto rule: Step 80 yields $K_u = \emptyset$; step 82 finds no table entries [PAGE 9]; the recommendations-generation process returns no personalized output. Prediction: Conversion attributed to the item-to-item recommendation module for anonymous traffic is zero; any observed recommendation engagement for this cohort must originate from a non-personalized fallback (e.g., global popular list) rather than from $S(i)$ retrieval. Verifiable metric: A/B test comparing logged-in versus anonymous users shows anonymous rec-CTR ≈ 0% from the CF path while logged-in rec-CTR > 0%, conditional on identical catalog exposure.
