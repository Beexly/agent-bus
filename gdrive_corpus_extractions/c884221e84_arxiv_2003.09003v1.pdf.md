<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The benchmark’s formal operators are defined over frames $t=1,\dots,T$, ground-truth objects, tracker hypotheses, and bounding boxes in pixel coordinates. Units are explicit: frame index is dimensionless; bounding-box coordinates and width/height are pixels; frame rate is frames per second; crowd density is persons per frame; visibility is a dimensionless ratio in $[0,1]$; MOTA and MOTP are dimensionless fractions, reported as percentages. Table 4 states that visibility is “a number between 0 and 1” [PAGE 4]. Table 1 reports density values such as 194.98 for MOT20-05 [PAGE 3], with unit persons per frame.

The matching similarity is the Jaccard index. For predicted box $B_p$ and ground-truth box $B_g$:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=\frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|},
\]
which is dimensionless. The paper states: “the intersection over union (a.k.a. the Jaccard index) is usually employed as the similarity criterion, while the threshold td is set to 0.5 or 50%” [PAGE 6]. A match is admitted only when $\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g)\ge t_d$. The Hungarian algorithm supplies the optimal one-to-one assignment; the paper says “the optimal matching is found using Munkre’s (a.k.a. Hungarian) algorithm” [PAGE 5]. The temporal operator is not independent per frame: if object $i$ was matched to hypothesis $j$ at $t-1$ and the frame-$t$ dissimilarity is below $t_d$, the correspondence is carried over even if another hypothesis is closer [PAGE 5]. An identity switch is counted when a target’s last assignment was $k\ne j$ and the new assignment is $j$; the paper calls this definition “stricter than the original one” [PAGE 5].

MOTA is:
\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t\left(\mathrm{FN}_t+\mathrm{FP}_t+\mathrm{IDSW}_t\right)}{\sum_t \mathrm{GT}_t}.
\]
The numerator is a count of objects/alarms/switches; the denominator is a count of ground-truth objects; the result is dimensionless and reported in percentage on $(-\infty,100]$ [PAGE 6]. Singularity: if $\sum_t \mathrm{GT}_t=0$, MOTA is undefined. Breakdown: if the numerator exceeds the denominator, MOTA is negative; the paper explicitly says it “can also be negative in cases where the number of errors made by the tracker exceeds the number of all objects in the scene” [PAGE 6].

MOTP is:
\[
\mathrm{MOTP}=\frac{\sum_{t,i} d_{t,i}}{\sum_t c_t},
\]
where $c_t$ is the number of matches in frame $t$ and $d_{t,i}$ is the bounding-box overlap of target $i$ with its assigned ground-truth object [PAGE 6]. With $t_d=0.5$, matched overlaps lie in $[0.5,1]$, so MOTP lies in $[0.5,1]$ or 50 to 100 percent. Singularity: if $\sum_t c_t=0$, MOTP is undefined. The paper warns that MOTP “is a measure of localization precision” and “mostly quantifies the localization accuracy of the detector” [PAGE 6], so it is not a tracker-only operator.

Track-quality operators use a per-trajectory recovered fraction. For ground-truth trajectory $i$ of length $L_i$, let $R_i$ be the number of frames in which $i$ is successfully tracked. Then $\tau_i=R_i/L_i$ is dimensionless. The paper defines: “A target is mostly tracked if it is successfully tracked for at least 80% of its life span” and “If a track is only recovered for less than 20% of its total length, it is said to be mostly lost (ML)” [PAGE 6]. Fragmentation counts interruptions where tracked status changes to untracked and later resumes; the paper also reports relative fragmentations as $\mathrm{FM}/\mathrm{Recall}$ [PAGE 7]. Relative ID switches are $\mathrm{IDSW}/\mathrm{Recall}$ [PAGE 5]. These are source-defined normalization operators, not invented toy ratios.

Gray-zone operators: at each frame all result boxes are matched to ground truth via Hungarian; boxes overlapping more than 75% with distractor, static person, reflection, or person-on-vehicle classes are excluded; during final evaluation “only those boxes that are annotated as pedestrians are used” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown occurs when an output falls just below the 75% exclusion boundary or when an ambiguous class is neither rewarded nor penalized. The evaluation also does not explicitly handle re-identification: “when a target leaves the field-of-view and then reappears, it is treated as an unseen target with a new ID” [PAGE 5]. This is a protocol singularity for long-horizon identity claims. The paper recommends concatenating sequences rather than averaging per-sequence figures because of “the large variation in the number of targets” [PAGE 5]; unweighted per-sequence averaging is therefore a known breakdown regime for ranking claims.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy runs from CLEAR metrics and Wu–Nevatia track-quality measures to MOT15, MOT16, MOT17, and MOT20. The paper explicitly says it employs “The CLEAR metrics proposed by Stiefelhagen et al. [14], and a set of track quality measures introduced by Wu and Nevatia [16]” [PAGE 4]. That lineage matters retrocausally: later benchmark choices redefine what earlier numbers mean. A tracker that looked strong under less crowded MOT15 conditions is re-weighted by MOT20’s density, where “the mean crowd density reaches 246 pedestrians per frame which 10 times denser when compared to the first benchmark release” [PAGE 2]. The same nominal MOTA is therefore not transportable across releases without the density and class-filter context.

The detector lineage is Faster R-CNN with ResNet101, trained on MOT20 training sequences [PAGE 3]. Public detections are supplied as a baseline, and for the MOT20 challenge “we will only accept results on public detections” [PAGE 3]. This creates a multi-week trap: a team can over-optimize association on the public-detection distribution and mistake detector-localization limits for tracker quality. The paper itself warns that “most methods can be heavily overfitted on one particular dataset” [PAGE 6]. A second multi-week trap is leaderboard overclaiming: the abstract says “leaderboards and ranking tables should not be over-claimed” [PAGE 1]. A third trap is test-set adaptation: half the sequences are test, test annotations are not released, and one scene is reserved for test time “in order to challenge the generalization capabilities of the methods” [PAGE 2]. Thus MOT20 retroactively judges earlier habits—single-frame assignment, per-sequence averaging, absolute IDSW counts—as insufficient under crowding and hidden-scene shift. The paper’s historical contribution is not a new physical law; it is a recalibration of the evidence standard for multi-object tracking.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when the tracking community’s familiar habits—per-frame detection, local nearest-neighbor association, and one-number ranking—are replayed under extreme crowding. The paper anticipates this by noting that previous benchmarks “tremendously contributed” but MOT20 is “substantially more crowded” [PAGE 7], and by warning that methods can be overfitted to one dataset [PAGE 6]. The recursive model is: authors expect trackers to expect that local assignment is enough; the benchmark then supplies a temporal carry-over rule that punishes that expectation. Figure 3(c) is the proof: an “optimal single-frame assignment in frame 1 is propagated through the sequence, causing 5 missed targets (FN) and 4 false positives (FP)” [PAGE 5]. The habit regresses to local optimality precisely when the clock operator makes local optimality path-dependent.

The counter-trap is the class-filter policy. The authors know detectors will fire on people on bicycles, static persons, reflections, and similar classes. Instead of rewarding or penalizing those outputs, the protocol excludes boxes overlapping more than 75% with those classes and uses only pedestrian annotations in final evaluation [PAGE 6]. The paper states the intent: “we do not want to penalize a tracker with a set of false positives for properly following that set of detections” and “we do not want to penalize with false negatives a tracker that is based on motion cues and therefore does not track a sitting person” [PAGE 6]. This is a theory-of-mind counter-trap: the evaluator models the tracker’s likely behavior and removes the tempting but non-score-bearing targets. A tracker that treats every detector box as a target can waste identity slots and generate excluded or ignored outputs; a tracker that ignores static persons is not punished. The gray zone is the 75% boundary: near-threshold overlaps are protocol-sensitive.

The clock operator is temporal state. The evaluator’s model of identity is “last known assignment” plus carry-over, not an eternal object identity. Re-identification is explicitly not handled: a target that leaves and reappears is “treated as an unseen target with a new ID” [PAGE 5]. This means the tracker’s belief about persistence and the evaluator’s belief about trajectory continuity can diverge. Fragmentation counts interruptions, and relative fragmentation is normalized by recall [PAGE 7]. A tracker can therefore appear to have many ID switches simply because it recovers more trajectories; the paper notes that “a method that finds twice as many trajectories will almost certainly produce more identity switches” [PAGE 5]. The recursive audit must therefore compare IDSW to recall, not IDSW alone. Crisis habit regression is the belief that a single aggregate number is sufficient; the counter-trap is the ignore/neutral policy for ambiguous classes; the clock is the carry-over and re-identification boundary. All three are source-grounded protocol effects, not physical or weather causal effects.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the source measures image-plane geometry—pixel boxes, resolution, frame rate, visibility, and overlap. It does not measure crowd force, pressure, density in persons per square meter, or biomechanical load. The only density quantity is “pedestrians per frame” [PAGE 1, PAGE 3], with values such as 246 pedestrians per frame [PAGE 1] and table densities such as 194.98 [PAGE 3]. A claim that physical crowd pressure causes a specific MOTA drop is vetoed because the source provides no pressure variable and no causal design. The legitimate physical statement is narrower: higher persons-per-frame counts co-occur with the crowded sequences, and detection statistics include minimum and maximum box heights from 16 to 463 pixels [PAGE 3], which constrains detectability.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the target class is “moving pedestrians” and people who briefly bend or squat “shall remain in the standard pedestrian class” [PAGE 2]. Static people—sitting, lying down, or standing still over the whole sequence—are annotated but are not score-bearing; the paper says an algorithm is “neither penalized nor rewarded” for tracking such a person [PAGE 2]. People on bicycles or other vehicles are excluded from the target class [PAGE 6]. Therefore any claim about gait kinetics, joint torque, fatigue, or biomechanical efficiency is vetoed: the source supplies class labels and boxes, not biomechanical measurements. The only admissible biomechanical inference is annotation-scope inference: upright moving pedestrians are targets; brief bending or squatting does not automatically remove target status; sitting/lying/static persons are neutral.

Weather-layer veto: the source describes sequences as indoor/outdoor and day/night, for example “indoor” and “outdoor, night” in Table 1 [PAGE 3], and says the sequences “include day and night time shots” [PAGE 1]. It does not report temperature, rain, snow, wind, humidity, solar angle, or surface wetness. A claim that weather caused a tracker’s score is therefore vetoed. The admissible statement is categorical: MOT20 includes indoor and outdoor scenes and day and night scenes; any weather-causal claim requires additional covariates not present in the source.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: a claim may cross layers only if every term in the claim is either directly measured or explicitly defined by the benchmark protocol. MOTA, MOTP, IoU, IDSW, fragmentation, MT, ML, visibility, resolution, frame rate, and persons-per-frame satisfy this rule. “Force,” “fatigue,” “rain,” “wind,” and “biomechanical load” do not. The proof is exclusion by source scope: the paper’s own operators are annotation, detection, assignment, and metric aggregation; the paper’s physical descriptions are categorical scene labels, not measurement series. Thus the cross-layer veto is not a denial that weather or biomechanics exist; it is a denial that this source identifies their causal effect on tracking scores.
