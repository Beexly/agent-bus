**Step 1: Deconstructing the "Ideal Adjustment".**
The phrase "uncompensated losses" implies a negative-sum game for any deviation. This suggests a stable equilibrium point where the sum of factor payments equals total product. Footnote 1 confirms this by referencing Wicksteed's exhaustiveness theorem. Mathematically, if $P$ is price, $MP_L$ is marginal product of labor, and $w$ is wage, then $w = P \cdot MP_L$. The same applies to capital ($r = P \cdot MP_K$). If the production function is homogeneous of degree one (constant returns to scale), Euler's theorem dictates that $\sum (MP_i \cdot K_i) = Total Product$. Thus, the "added product" exactly pays out the entire output, leaving zero profit in the long run. This is the "theoretical result" Knight mentions.

**Step 2: Analyzing the "Continual Repricing" Mechanism.**
Knight offers an alternative formulation: "view the adjustment as a continual repricing of productive services" analogous to consumer goods markets [PAGE 108]. He introduces the agent behavior: producers enter with money to buy "productive power in the abstract." They prioritize purchases that yield the "largest price contribution to product for a given price outlay." This is an optimization problem: Maximize $R(x)$ subject to budget constraint $p \cdot x = M$. The solution drives up the price of efficient inputs until returns equalize.

**Step 3: The Critical Constraint - Fixed Endowments.**
The mechanism hinges on: "since the amounts of all agencies in existence are fixed." This is the anchor. If supply were elastic in the short run, price adjustments would be dampened. Because supply is rigid (fixed stock of land, existing capital), demand shifts translate directly into price volatility until a new clearing price is found. Competition ensures this happens "quickly."

**Step 4: The Gray-Zone Audit - Where Logic Frays.**
While the deduction is internally consistent, it relies on "abstract" conditions. 
*Gray Zone 1: Information Costs.* The model assumes agents know the "price contribution" instantly. Real-world search costs, bounded rationality, and imperfect information mean the "readjustment" is noisy and lagged.
*Gray Zone 2: Time Horizon.* "Quickly" is undefined. In sectors with long gestation periods (aviation, pharma), "quick" adjustment is impossible.
*Gray Zone 3: The Definition of 'Product'.* Measuring the marginal product of a complex input (like management) is notoriously difficult. Does "added product" account for coordination effects?
*Gray Zone 4: Stability vs. Instability.* The text assumes convergence ("force a readjustment"). However, cobweb models or speculative bubbles demonstrate that adaptive expectations can lead to divergence rather than equilibrium.

**Step 5: Synthesizing the Implications.**
By framing the equilibrium as a "continual repricing," Knight highlights that markets are processes, not states. However, the reliance on fixed quantities suggests this is a partial equilibrium analysis ignoring general equilibrium feedbacks (e.g., income effects). The "ideal" is portrayed as an attractor, yet the very "artificiality" Knight warns of—perfect knowledge, friction-free exchange—is what guarantees the attractor's stability. Let $L, K$ represent the fixed quantities of labor and capital agencies (units: hours, machine-hours).
The text states: *"total produce would be divided... by giving each his added product"* [PAGE 108].
Mathematically, this corresponds to the marginal productivity distribution under constant returns to scale:
$$ Y = F(L, K) $$
where $F(\lambda L, \lambda K) = \lambda F(L, K)$ for $\lambda > 0$.
The distributive shares are determined by the marginal products:
$$ w = p_Y \cdot \frac{\partial F}{\partial L} \quad \text{(Wage rate)} $$
$$ r = p_Y \cdot \frac{\partial F}{\partial K} \quad \text{(Return on Capital)} $$
According to Euler's Theorem for homogeneous functions:
$$ Y = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial L} \cdot L\right) + \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial K} \cdot K\right) $$
Thus, total revenue $p_Y Y$ equals total factor payments $wL + rK$. The residual profit $\pi$ approaches zero in this "ideal adjustment."
*Breakdown Regime:* If $F$ exhibits increasing returns to scale ($n > 1$), factor payments exceed output, leading to insolvency. If decreasing returns ($n < 1$), excess profit remains, contradicting the "uncompensated losses" condition.

$$ \max_{x_{ij}} \frac{p_{j} \cdot MP_{ij}}{c_{j}} $$
Where $x_{ij}$ is the amount of agency $j$ purchased by firm $i$, $p_j$ is the price of agency $j$, and $MP_{ij}$ is its marginal physical product.
The market clearing condition relies on fixed endowments:
$$ \sum_{i} x_{ij} = \bar{X}_j \quad (\text{Fixed}) $$
As agents bid for agencies offering the highest ratio, prices $c_j$ rise until ratios equalize across all firms and agencies.
