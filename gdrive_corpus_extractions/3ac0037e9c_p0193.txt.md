<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of primary stimulus generalization traces back to early experimental psychology. E.L. Thorndike's law of effect (1898) implied that responses become associated with specific situations, but did not address variation in stimuli. Ivan Pavlov's work on conditioned reflexes (1927) systematically demonstrated that similar stimuli elicit graded responses, establishing the gradient as a fundamental phenomenon. B.F. Skinner's operant formulations (1930s-40s) treated generalization as a separate topic from respondent conditioning, focusing on response-generalization. Clark L. Hull integrated these strands into a deductive system, positing generalization as a primary principle arising from the "fractional anchoring" of habits to multiple cue elements.

Historically, the concept evolved from qualitative observations (similar sounds cause similar reactions) to quantitative laws (gradient functions fitted to data). The use of psychophysical scales (Fechner, Stevens) provided metrics for stimulus distance, enabling precise modeling. Later developments include Rescorla-Wagner model (1972) where generalization is incorporated via a similarity matrix, and modern machine learning kernels that compute similarity in high-dimensional feature spaces.

Multi-week traps emerge when researchers assume that gradient shapes are invariant. In reality, they plasticity: after discrimination training, gradients sharpen (narrower $\sigma$) around rewarded stimuli and flatten around unrewarded ones. Also, the initial gradient shape depends on the organism's phylogenetic preparedness (e.g., humans generalize faces broadly early in life, then narrow with experience). Another trap is equating generalization with poor discrimination; instead, broad generalization can be adaptive in unpredictable environments. Finally, ignoring cross-modal generalization leads to incomplete models—e.g., fear conditioning to a tone may generalize to sights of the experimenter, complicating clinical applications.

Understanding this genealogy reveals that Hull's convergence metaphor captures the neural implementation but may obscure the dynamic learning processes that reshape generalization over time. Modern neuroscience shows that generalization gradients reflect population coding in sensory cortices, where tuning curves overlap, and decision circuits read out weighted sums.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis situations, organisms often exhibit **crisis habit regression**, reverting to earlier, broader generalization gradients. Under stress (high arousal, threat), the nervous system prioritizes speed over accuracy, leading to overgeneralization. For example, a soldier conditioned to flinch at loud noises may startle at any sudden sound, even quiet ones. This regression reflects a breakdown in inhibitory control and a shift toward primitive survival circuits.

Conversely, **counter-trap avoidance** requires maintaining sharp discrimination despite pressure to generalize. Skilled performers (surgeons, pilots) train to keep gradients narrow even under stress through deliberate practice and biofeedback. The **clock** here is the rate of change in environmental statistics. In stable environments, narrow gradients are optimal; in volatile settings, broader gradients reduce missed detections but increase false alarms. Organisms must dynamically adjust gradient widths based on predicted reward/punishment rates—a metacognitive calibration process.

Recursive theory of mind involves predicting others' generalization patterns. If I know you were conditioned to fear dogs, I anticipate your fear response to wolf-like animals. Misjudgments occur when I underestimate your gradient breadth (thinking only dogs scare you) or overestimate it (assuming all four-legged animals scare you). Social coordination relies on shared generalization structures; e.g., linguistic categories depend on mutual agreement about which stimuli belong together.

The interplay between regression and counter-trap forms a strategic game: in emergency response training, instructors deliberately induce mild stress to reinforce narrow gradients, preventing regression during real crises. The ultimate challenge is designing environments that promote adaptive plasticity—broadening gradients when needed (exploration phase) and narrowing them when expertise develops (exploitation phase).

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical constraints impose hard vetoes on stimulus generalization. 

**Physical Layer:** Energy thresholds limit detection. A weak light below the absolute threshold cannot evoke a response regardless of how close its wavelength is to a conditioned one. Similarly, mechanical limits (e.g., joint range of motion) restrict which effectors can be activated.

**Biomechanical Layer:** Muscle physiology imposes fatigue and refractory periods. Even if a generalized stimulus successfully activates a motor program, the muscles may be too fatigued to execute the movement. Also, synergistic/antagonistic muscle groups create trade-offs; activating one effector might inhibit another due to reciprocal innervation.

**Weather/Environmental Layer:** Ambient conditions can mask or alter stimuli. Background noise can raise the effective threshold for auditory stimuli, flattening the generalization gradient. Temperature affects nerve conduction velocity and muscle contraction speed, potentially delaying responses and causing mistiming relative to stimulus onset.

**Veto Proof Example:** Consider a conditioned reflex to withdraw hand from hot stove ($s_0$ = 80°C touch). Generalization predicts withdrawal to slightly cooler temperatures (75°C). However, if the hand is severely frostbitten (biomechanical veto), nerve endings may be damaged, and the stimulus might not reach threshold. Additionally, if wearing thick gloves (physical layer), even 80°C might not be detected. Thus, generalization predictions fail when lower-level systems are compromised. Any viable model must incorporate these veto mechanisms as gating functions that zero out response probabilities when preconditions aren't met.

This adjudication ensures that high-level cognitive principles like generalization remain subordinate to bodily integrity and environmental reality. It prevents the overreach of purely informational models that ignore embodiment.
