The paper’s central question is narrow but methodologically loaded: does the allocation of home and away matches in a single round-robin (sub)tournament change the number of points a club collects? The authors answer with a three-country club-season panel from Hungary, Scotland, and Serbia, using Football Club Elo Ratings measured at the start of the single round-robin phase—Round 23 in Hungary and Scotland, Round 31 in Serbia—and a Mundlak-augmented pooled OLS model with club-clustered HC1 standard errors. The abstract states the key result: “In contrast to the robust impact of relative team strength, the strength of schedule is found to have no significant effect on the number of points scored under the assumption of a league-specific home advantage” [PAGE 1]. The same page gives the two possible interpretations: “the theoretical unfairness is not a serious issue in practice, or the standard approach of modelling home advantage in Elo ratings might need to be reconsidered” [PAGE 1].

The multi-hop derivation is as follows. First, Elo converts a rating difference into a home-win probability via a logistic function with a home-advantage shift \(\Delta\). Second, the paper defines an average winning probability \(AWP_i(\Delta)\) for a fair double round-robin baseline and a strength-of-schedule measure \(SoS_i(\Delta)\) as the difference between the actual single round-robin average win probability and that baseline. Third, the regression asks whether \(SoS_i(\Delta)\) predicts points per match \(S_{it}\) after controlling for \(AWP_i(\Delta)\), country indicators, and club-specific temporal means. Fourth, the baseline coefficient on \(SoS\) is statistically indistinguishable from zero, while \(AWP\) is positive and significant. The reported interpretation is that a 10 percentage point increase in \(AWP\) for the same club is associated with about 0.16 additional points per match, whereas \(SoS\) has no significant within-club association [PAGE 11]. The sensitivity analyses with \(\Delta=25\) and \(\Delta=75\) leave the \(SoS\) conclusion unchanged [PAGE 12], and the extended Scottish sample gives the same qualitative result [PAGE 13].

The gray-zone audit begins with notation. The paper writes \(p_{ij}(\Delta)=1/(1+10^{(R_j-R_i-\Delta)/400})\) for team \(i\) at home against \(j\). Swapping indices gives \(p_{ji}(\Delta)=1/(1+10^{(R_i-R_j-\Delta)/400})\), which is \(j\)’s home-win probability against \(i\), not \(i\)’s away-win probability. The away-win probability for \(i\) at \(j\) is \(1-p_{ji}(\Delta)\). The printed \(AWP\) and \(SoS\) formulas therefore contain a notational gray zone: the stated property “\(AWP_i(\Delta)=0.5\) for each team \(i\in N\) if they are of equal strength” [PAGE 7] is consistent with a centered form such as \(\tfrac12[p_{ij}(\Delta)+1-p_{ji}(\Delta)]\), but not with the literal sum \(p_{ij}+p_{ji}\) when \(\Delta>0\), because equal teams would then give \(p_{ij}=p_{ji}=1/(1+10^{-\Delta/400})\), which exceeds 0.5 for positive \(\Delta\). Unless \(p_{ji}\) is implicitly redefined as the away-team win probability, the formulas are ambiguous. This matters because \(SoS\) is the treatment variable; a sign or centering error would change the estimated coefficient’s meaning, though it need not invalidate the null if the error is absorbed in the baseline.

A second gray zone is identification. The Elo snapshot is ex ante with respect to the single round-robin, which is good for avoiding reverse causality from the sub-tournament results. But Elo ratings are not exogenous to injuries, transfers, managerial changes, or promoted-team uncertainty; the paper explicitly notes that “Elo rating is a strictly performance-based measure that does not reflect the effect of injuries, managerial changes, or player transfers” [PAGE 5], and that promoted teams’ ratings are “equal at the beginning of a season and are an unreliable measure of strength” [PAGE 5]. The Serbian format is especially delicate: the last 11 rounds are determined after 22 rounds, so “more home games are positively correlated with team strength in Serbia” [PAGE 9]. That creates a selection channel: the teams that receive more home games are the stronger teams, so a simple “more home games” comparison is not causal. The paper’s \(SoS\) measure is designed to control for opponent strength, but any residual correlation between schedule allocation and unobserved team quality remains a threat.

A third gray zone is outcome and power. The dependent variable is points per match, not accumulated points, because the number of matches differs across countries [PAGE 8]. This is sensible for cross-country comparability, but it changes the policy question: a club-season ranking is determined by accumulated points and tie-breakers, not by points per match alone. The paper itself motivates the question with Balmazújváros and Diósgyőr finishing on 36 points and with Győr beating Ferencváros by one point [PAGE 2], yet the regression estimates average per-match performance. The null on \(SoS\) therefore does not directly prove that no ranking reversal can occur; it says that the average per-match point rate is not detectably associated with the Elo-based schedule imbalance in this sample. The paper acknowledges the scale: “the impact of scheduling asymmetry remains below 0.1 points per match as the absolute value of SoS never exceeds 1.5” [PAGE 14]. A null of that size is consistent with practical fairness, but it is also consistent with a small true effect that the design cannot resolve.

A fourth gray zone is model selection and interpretation. The paper reports that the Hausman test rejects conventional random effects, while the FE-versus-pooled and Breusch–Pagan tests do not clearly favor unit-specific effects; the authors therefore use a Mundlak-augmented pooled OLS specification [PAGE 9]. This is defensible, but the Mundlak correction only separates within-club and between-club variation in the observed regressors. It does not solve omitted team-specific home advantage, non-transitive rivalries, or weather/venue interactions. The discussion itself flags “home advantage has a team-specific component (Peeters and van Ours, 2021), which cannot be captured by this approach” [PAGE 14], and notes that “Non-transitive patterns in long-term rivalries ... bogey team relationships ... and circular triads of overperformance ... could also interact with the allocation of the field of the games” [PAGE 14]. Those are not decorative caveats; they are alternative causal structures under which the constant-\(\Delta\) \(SoS\) null could be a misspecification artifact rather than evidence of fairness.

The paper’s Elo operator is a logistic map from rating difference and home advantage to a home-win probability. With \(R_i,R_j\) in conventional Elo rating points and \(\Delta\) also in Elo rating points, the source defines
\[
p_{ij}(\Delta)=\frac{1}{1+10^{(R_j-R_i-\Delta)/400}},
\]
where \(p_{ij}(\Delta)\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless and \(400\) is the Elo scale constant in rating points [PAGE 6]. The parameter \(\Delta\) is a league-specific home-advantage shift; the paper uses \(\Delta=50\) as the benchmark and \(\Delta=25,75\) for sensitivity [PAGE 7]. The rating update is
\[
R_i'=R_i+k\left[Q_{ij}-p_{ij}(\Delta)\right],\qquad
R_j'=R_j+k\left[(1-Q_{ij})-(1-p_{ij}(\Delta))\right],
\]
with \(Q_{ij}=1\) for an \(i\) win, \(Q_{ij}=0.5\) for a draw, \(Q_{ij}=0\) for a \(j\) win, and \(k=20\) Elo points per match [PAGE 6]. The update preserves \(R_i+R_j=R_i'+R_j'\) [PAGE 6]. The draw is not a separate Elo probability; it enters only through \(Q_{ij}=0.5\), so the Elo win probability is a two-outcome abstraction used for rating updates and schedule measurement.

The average winning probability for a double round-robin baseline is
\[
AWP_i(\Delta)=\frac{\sum_{j\in N,\,j\neq i}\left[p_{ij}(\Delta)+p_{ji}(\Delta)\right]}{2(n-1)},
\]
where \(n=|N|\) [PAGE 7]. The paper states: “\(AWP_i(\Delta)=0.5\) for each team \(i\in N\) if they are of equal strength” [PAGE 7]. As noted in the audit, the literal index-swapped \(p_{ji}\) is the opponent’s home-win probability, so the formula is only unambiguous if \(p_{ji}\) is read as the away-side probability or if the expression is centered. The strength-of-schedule operator is
\[
SoS_i(\Delta)=100\left(
\frac{\sum_{j\in O_i^h}p_{ij}(\Delta)+\sum_{j\in O_i^a}p_{ji}(\Delta)}
{\left|O_i^h\right|+\left|O_i^a\right|}
-AWP_i(\Delta)
\right),
\]
where \(O_i^h\) and \(O_i^a\) are the home and away opponent sets [PAGE 7]. \(SoS_i\) is expressed in percentage points because of the factor 100; \(AWP_i\) is dimensionless probability. The paper proves two properties: “In a single round-robin tournament without home advantage \((\Delta=0)\), as well as in a double round-robin, \(SoS_i(\Delta)=0\) for each team \(i\in N\)” [PAGE 7]. These are the favorable theoretical properties: no home advantage implies no schedule bias in the metric, and a balanced double round-robin implies zero by construction. The metric also depends on the relative strength of home and away opponents, which the paper argues is essential because “a strong team is severely disadvantaged if it plays against the strongest half of its opponents away, and against the weakest half of its opponents at home” [PAGE 3].

The regression operator is a Mundlak-augmented pooled OLS:
\[
S_{it}=\beta_0+\beta_1 AWP_{it}+\beta_2 SoS_{it}
+\gamma_1 HUN_i+\gamma_2 SRB_i
+\theta_1 \overline{AWP}_i+\theta_2 \overline{SoS}_i+\varepsilon_{it},
\]
where \(S_{it}\) is points per match, the country dummies use Scotland as reference, and \(\overline{AWP}_i,\overline{SoS}_i\) are club-specific temporal means [PAGE 9]. Units: \(S_{it}\) is points per match; \(\beta_1\) is points per match per unit of \(AWP\) (so a 0.10 increase in \(AWP\) maps to approximately \(0.10\beta_1\) points per match); \(\beta_2\) is points per match per one-percentage-point increase in \(SoS\). The baseline estimate is \(\beta_1=1.597\) with \(AWP\) significant and \(\beta_2=0.006\) with \(SoS\) insignificant [PAGE 12]. The paper reports that a 10 percentage point increase in \(AWP\) is associated with about 0.16 points per match, and that “SoS exhibits no statistically significant within-club association with \(S\)” [PAGE 11]. The implied between-club \(AWP\) slope is approximately \(1.597+1.338=2.935\) [PAGE 11–12].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are exact. First, equation (1) assumes a constant league-specific \(\Delta\) added to the home team’s rating. If home advantage is team-specific, \(\Delta_i\) or \(\Delta_{ij}\), the operator is misspecified; the discussion explicitly warns that “home advantage has a team-specific component (Peeters and van Ours, 2021), which cannot be captured by this approach” [PAGE 14]. Second, the logistic has mathematical limits \(p_{ij}\to 0\) as \(R_j-R_i-\Delta\to+\infty\) and \(p_{ij}\to 1\) as \(R_j-R_i-\Delta\to-\infty\); in finite precision, extreme rating gaps can overflow or saturate, although the paper’s ratings are far from that regime. Third, \(SoS_i\) is undefined if \(O_i^h\cup O_i^a\) is empty or if \(|O_i^h|+|O_i^a|=0\); the tournament must contain at least one opponent. Fourth, the \(SoS=0\) properties require either \(\Delta=0\) in a single round-robin or a complete double round-robin; they do not hold for arbitrary unbalanced schedules with \(\Delta>0\). Fifth, the regression assumes that \(AWP\) and \(SoS\) are measured without classical error and are not correlated with omitted club quality; promoted-team Elo equality and the Serbian strength-correlated schedule are the clearest threats [PAGE 5, PAGE 9]. Sixth, the null is a statement about the sample and model: with 396 primary observations and an \(SoS\) range of roughly \(-2.15\) to \(+2.15\) percentage points [PAGE 8], a coefficient of 0.006 points per match per point implies an upper-bound practical effect of about \(0.006\times 2.15\approx0.013\) points per match at the observed extreme, far below one point. That is a precision statement, not a universal proof of fairness.

Model-selection diagnostics are part of the operator context. The paper reports an FE-versus-pooled OLS \(F(70,323)=1.276\), \(p=0.084\); a Breusch–Pagan LM \(\chi^2(1)=0.101\), \(p=0.751\); and a Hausman \(\chi^2(2)=13.930\), \(p<0.001\) [PAGE 8]. The authors conclude that “The Hausman test rejects the conventional RE orthogonality assumption” [PAGE 9] and use the Mundlak correction. The baseline and sensitivity models have \(R^2=0.426\) and adjusted \(R^2=0.417\) [PAGE 12]; the secondary Scottish sample has \(R^2=0.615\) [PAGE 13]. These are moderate explanatory fits: team strength explains a substantial share of points per match, but most variance remains unexplained, which is expected in football and which limits any claim that schedule effects are absent rather than merely undetected.

The paper sits in a retrocausal lineage: a theoretical fairness claim about single round-robin schedules is evaluated against later empirical data, and the null result retroactively changes the policy status of the theory. Wessel et al. (2026) prove that ranking-fair schedules exist when the number of contestants is a multiple of four and argue that contestants should face opponents in an alternating home-away pattern when ranked by strength [PAGE 3]. The source paper tests whether that theoretical distortion is detectable in European football. Its abstract conclusion—that strength of schedule has “no significant effect” under a league-specific home advantage [PAGE 1]—retroactively weakens the case for complex ranking-fair schedules in the leagues studied: “using the rather complex ranking-fair schedules proposed by Wessel et al. (2026) seems to be unnecessary in the football leagues considered here” [PAGE 14]. But the paper immediately qualifies that the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: “the lack of empirical evidence does not guarantee the fairness of these tournaments” [PAGE 14].

The scientific genealogy has several branches. The Elo branch runs from Arpad Elo’s chess rating system through Football Club Elo Ratings, which the paper uses because it incorporates home advantage and goal difference [PAGE 5]. The home-advantage branch runs through Pollard’s retrospective and worldwide analyses and the sports-economics literature on seasonal and team-specific home advantage [PAGE 2, PAGE 14]. The unbalanced-schedule branch runs through Lenten’s adjusted win-ratio and competitive-balance measures and Lee et al.’s unbiased estimator under unbalanced schedules [PAGE 3–4]. The panel-method branch runs through Mundlak (1978) and Wooldridge’s formulation of Mundlak regressions, which the paper uses to separate within-club from between-club variation [PAGE 9]. The chess analogy branch runs through Csató and Krumer (2026), where players with an extra white game score significantly more points in Swiss-system tournaments [PAGE 3]. The paper explicitly notes the analogy between home advantage in team sports and white advantage in chess, and says one “could anticipate a positive impact in our sample, too—but the results presented above do not support the straightforward expectation” [PAGE 14]. That is the retrocausal pivot: the chess result predicts a schedule effect, the football data do not confirm it, and the difference is attributed either to genuine practical fairness or to the constant-\(\Delta\) Elo model.

The historical data design also contains multi-week traps. The Hungarian league has used a triple round-robin with 12 teams since 2015/16; six teams play 17 home matches and six play 16, and the paper motivates this with the 2017/18 Balmazújváros–Diósgyőr relegation case and the 2025/26 Győr–Ferencváros title case [PAGE 2]. Scotland’s Premiership has a pre-split triple round-robin, and Serbia’s format determines the last 11 rounds after 22 rounds, with the top eight and bottom eight split into championship and relegation parts [PAGE 4, PAGE 9–10]. These are not interchangeable formats. In Hungary and Scotland the home-away patterns are fixed at the start of the season; in Serbia “the first half of the teams after 30 rounds ... are guaranteed to play more (four) home games” [PAGE 9]. That means the Serbian schedule is an endogenous treatment correlated with performance, while the Hungarian and Scottish schedules are pre-determined. Pooling them without country interactions risks conflating format effects with country effects, which is why the paper restricts the primary sample to the common 2015/16–2025/26 window and uses country dummies [PAGE 5]. The secondary Scottish sample extends to 2000/01 to increase power and check temporal stability [PAGE 6, PAGE 13].

Another multi-week trap is the “clock” of the single round-robin. The sub-tournament does not start at the beginning of the season; it starts after a double round-robin, at Round 23 in Hungary and Scotland and Round 31 in Serbia, with Elo ratings taken on the first day of that round [PAGE 6, Table A.1]. By that point, league positions, relegation pressure, European qualification, injuries, and managerial changes may have altered behavior. The Elo snapshot is ex ante with respect to the sub-tournament, which avoids reverse causality from sub-tournament points, but it is not ex ante with respect to the season. A club that has already secured safety or already been relegated may rotate players differently in the single round-robin. That is a time-varying omitted variable, and it is exactly the kind of multi-week adaptation that a schedule-fairness test should consider. The paper’s use of year indicators in a robustness check is jointly insignificant [PAGE 13], but year dummies do not capture club-specific strategic timing.

A further trap is the Danish exclusion. Denmark used a triple round-robin with 12 teams from 1995/96 to 2015/16, but “the Danish league started with a single round-robin of 11 rounds, followed by a double round-robin of 22 rounds (Rasmussen, 2008), which excludes using the Danish data” [PAGE 5]. This is a useful negative control: the Danish format places the single round-robin first, so pre-season Elo ratings would be the relevant strength measure, and promoted teams’ ratings are unreliable. The paper’s choice to exclude Denmark is therefore not arbitrary; it protects the identification of strength from being contaminated by the very sub-tournament being studied. The broader lesson is that format chronology matters: a single round-robin embedded after a double round-robin is a different causal object from a single round-robin at the start of a season.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer asks how clubs, coaches, and analysts form beliefs about the schedule and about each other’s beliefs, and how those beliefs could bias the estimated \(SoS\) effect. The paper’s model is non-strategic: it treats \(AWP\) and \(SoS\) as ex-ante Elo objects and regresses points per match on them. It does not model a coach who knows that his club plays the strongest opponents away and therefore chooses a different rotation policy, nor a club that knows its rival has an easier home set and therefore takes more risk. In a finite sub-tournament, those beliefs can produce “crisis habit regression”: when the schedule looks unbalanced, teams and commentators regress to the habitual explanation that home games are decisive, even if the actual point effect is small. The paper’s descriptive statistics show that home teams win about 15 percentage points more often than away teams in Hungary and Scotland and more than 20 in Serbia [PAGE 9–10], so the habit is empirically grounded. But the regression asks whether the allocation of those home games changes points per match after controlling for strength, and the answer is no. The habit regression is therefore a belief about a mechanism that the data do not detect at the league-average level.

The “counter-trap” is the strategic response: if a club expects its schedule to be unfavorable, it may compensate by prioritizing home matches, resting players in away matches it expects to lose, or altering tactics. Such compensation would attenuate the observed \(SoS\) coefficient toward zero, because the schedule disadvantage is offset by behavior. Conversely, a club with a favorable schedule may become complacent, which would also attenuate the coefficient. A third possibility is exploitation: a club with more home games against weak opponents may press its advantage, which would amplify the coefficient. The paper cannot distinguish these because it has no rotation, tactical, or effort data. The null is therefore compatible with “no effect,” “offsetting effects,” and “effect too small for the design.” The source’s own caveat about team-specific home advantage and non-transitive rivalries [PAGE 14] is a theory-of-mind caveat: clubs are not interchangeable agents with identical \(\Delta\).

The “clock” is the finite timing of the sub-tournament. The single round-robin is played late in the season, when the league table already encodes beliefs about relegation and qualification. In Serbia, the last 11 rounds are determined after 22 rounds, so the schedule itself is a function of the first-phase table; the top teams are guaranteed more home games [PAGE 9]. That is a formal counter-trap: the format rewards first-phase performance with a second-phase home advantage, and the teams know it. The paper’s Table 6 shows that the share of points collected by teams with more home games averages 50.11% in Hungary, 50.90% in Scotland, and 56.91% in Serbia [PAGE 11]. The Serbian average is consistent with the format favoring stronger teams, but it is descriptive, not a causal estimate. A recursive model would predict that the Serbian “more home games” effect should be larger than the Hungarian and Scottish effects if the dynamic schedule is exploitable; the paper’s country indicators and \(AWP\)-country interactions are insignificant [PAGE 12], which weakens that prediction but does not eliminate it because the regression uses \(SoS\), not a direct “more home games” treatment.

From a modeling perspective, the theory-of-mind layer suggests three predictions. First, if strategic compensation is strong, the \(SoS\) coefficient should be closer to zero in formats where clubs know the schedule far in advance (Hungary and Scotland) than in the dynamic Serbian format; the paper does not find a significant country difference, so either compensation is weak or the measure is too noisy. Second, if the “clock” matters, the effect should be larger in the final rounds, when relegation and title incentives are sharpest; the paper’s year dummies are jointly insignificant [PAGE 13], but round-level data are not used. Third, if analysts’ crisis habit is the main driver of the fairness concern, then the policy debate should focus on tie-breakers and ranking rules rather than on average points per match. The paper’s motivation with Balmazújváros and Győr [PAGE 2] is exactly a tie-breaker/ranking-rule problem, not a points-per-match problem. That is the recursive gap: the model estimates average performance, while the motivating cases are discrete ranking reversals.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether physical, biomechanical, or weather mechanisms could invalidate the paper’s statistical conclusion. The source contains no pitch-surface, travel-distance, altitude, crowd-density, player-load, injury, sprint, fatigue, temperature, precipitation, or wind variables. Its only physical proxy is the league-specific home-advantage parameter \(\Delta\), which is a single dynamic Elo shift per league [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. Therefore, no positive proof at the physical, biomechanical, or weather layers is possible from this paper. The correct adjudication is a conditional veto: the statistical null survives only if those omitted layers are either randomized with respect to the home-away allocation or absorbed into the league-specific \(\Delta\). If they are correlated with the allocation, the null is biased and cannot be read as evidence of practical fairness.

At the physical layer, travel and venue effects are the obvious omitted variables. A club that plays more away matches against distant opponents may face different recovery costs than a club whose away matches are local. The paper does not measure travel. If travel burden is correlated with \(SoS\), then \(SoS\) is a composite of opponent strength and physical burden, and the coefficient could be attenuated or inflated depending on the sign of the correlation. The paper’s use of Elo ratings as the strength measure means that travel is not in the model. The veto is therefore not that travel is proven important; it is that the paper cannot rule it out. A physical-layer replication would need travel distance, rest days, and venue characteristics, and would need to show that they are balanced across the home-away allocation or that they do not predict points per match after \(AWP\) and \(SoS\).

At the biomechanical layer, player load and injury are the key omitted variables. The single round-robin is played late in the season, after a double round-robin, so cumulative load may differ across clubs. A club with more home games may have lower travel load, but a club with a harder away set may have higher load. The paper has no minutes-played, sprint-distance, or injury data. It acknowledges that Elo does not reflect injuries or player transfers [PAGE 5], but that is a measurement caveat, not a biomechanical control. If load is correlated with schedule allocation, the \(SoS\) coefficient is not a clean schedule effect. The veto is that the paper’s null is an average reduced-form association, not a biomechanical proof. A biomechanical-layer test would need to instrument load or randomize the schedule; neither is present.

At the weather layer, the paper has no weather variables. Weather could change home advantage: rain, wind, heat, or cold may affect playing style and may interact with venue. The paper’s \(\Delta\) is league-specific and dynamic, and it notes that the home-advantage parameter “increases or decreases together with the Elo rating of the home team” and reached a minimum during Covid-19 seasons when matches were played behind closed doors [PAGE 5]. That is evidence that \(\Delta\) captures some environmental/crowd variation, but it is not a weather model. If weather shocks are random with respect to the home-away allocation, they add noise and reduce power; if they are correlated with venue or region, they bias the estimate. The paper cannot adjudicate this. The cross-layer veto is therefore symmetric: weather cannot be used to explain away the null without data, and the null cannot be used to rule out weather-mediated unfairness without data.

The strongest cross-layer veto internal to the paper is team-specific home advantage. The discussion states: “home advantage has a team-specific component (Peeters and van Ours, 2021), which cannot be captured by this approach” [PAGE 14]. If \(\Delta_i\) varies across clubs and is correlated with the schedule, then the constant-\(\Delta\) \(SoS\) is misspecified. A club with a large \(\Delta_i\) that receives more home games against strong opponents may have a different expected point total than the metric implies. The breakdown regime is clear: as the variance of \(\Delta_i\) grows relative to the range of \(SoS\), the constant-\(\Delta\) approximation loses validity. The paper’s sensitivity analysis over \(\Delta=25,50,75\) [PAGE 12] tests league-level shifts, not club-level heterogeneity. That is the cross-layer gap: the model can absorb a common physical/weather/crowd shift, but not a club-specific one. The correct conclusion is not that physical or weather effects are absent; it is that the paper’s null is conditional on a common-\(\Delta\) approximation and cannot veto club-specific physical, biomechanical, or weather mechanisms.

Scenario 1: parametric power simulation with the actual schedules. Use the paper’s Elo ratings at the Round 23/31 snapshot, the actual home-away opponent sets, and the constant-\(\Delta\) win probabilities \(p_{ij}(\Delta=50)\). Simulate match outcomes conditional on a draw model calibrated to the observed league draw rates, convert to points per match under the 3-1-0 rule, and re-estimate the same Mundlak-augmented pooled OLS with club-clustered HC1 standard errors. The verifiable prediction is: if the design has adequate power and the constant-\(\Delta\) Elo model is the true data-generating process, the simulated \(\beta_2\) on \(SoS\) should be positive and its 95% confidence interval should exclude zero at least at the nominal rate; if the simulated \(\beta_2\) is null, then the observed null is uninformative because the design cannot detect the theoretical effect. The reward check is pre-registered: sign of \(\beta_2\), coverage of the confidence interval, and the fraction of simulations in which the null is rejected. A finding that simulated power is low would veto the paper’s strong reading “the theoretical unfairness is not a serious issue in practice” [PAGE 1] and support the weaker reading that the sample is underpowered.

Scenario 2: team-specific home advantage. Replace the constant \(\Delta\) with a hierarchical or deterministic club-specific shift, for example \(\Delta_i=\Delta_0+\lambda(R_i-\bar R)\) or \(\Delta_i\sim\mathcal N(\Delta_0,\sigma_\Delta^2)\), holding the actual schedules fixed. Simulate points per match, estimate the same regression, and compare out-of-sample RMSE, AIC/BIC, and the \(SoS\) coefficient to the constant-\(\Delta\) model. The verifiable prediction is: if team-specific home advantage is correlated with schedule allocation, the \(SoS\) coefficient should become nonzero and the model fit should improve; if the constant-\(\Delta\) model fits equally well and the \(SoS\) coefficient remains null, the paper’s specification is robust to this alternative. The reward check is a pre-registered model-comparison threshold: \(\Delta\)RMSE, \(\Delta\)BIC, and the sign/CI of \(\beta_2\). A rejection of the constant-\(\Delta\) model would not prove unfairness, but it would veto the paper’s interpretation that the null is evidence of practical fairness rather than evidence of model misspecification.

Scenario 3: format-specific “more home games” treatment and rank-reversal audit. Replace \(SoS\) with an indicator for playing more home games than the opponent set average, or with the actual number of home games, and estimate country-interacted models on the Hungarian, Scottish, and Serbian subsamples. In parallel, simulate full league tables under permuted home-away allocations that preserve each club’s opponent set and home-game count, and compute the rate at which the champion, relegation, or European qualification order changes. The verifiable prediction is: if the Serbian dynamic schedule is exploitable, the “more home games” coefficient should be positive and larger in Serbia than in Hungary and Scotland, and the rank-reversal rate should be higher in Serbia; if the coefficients are null across formats and the rank-reversal rate is negligible, the paper’s practical-fairness conclusion is supported. The reward checks are the country-interaction sign and significance, the permutation \(p\)-value, and the simulated rank-reversal rate. This scenario directly tests the motivating cases—Balmazújváros’s relegation and Győr’s one-point title—without assuming that average points per match is the same object as a discrete ranking outcome [PAGE 2]. A nonzero rank-reversal rate despite a null average-points coefficient would veto the inference from “no average effect” to “no fairness problem.”
