<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Galton's 1889 text initiates a multi-generational epistemological trap that persists in modern genomic interpretation. By defining "highly consumptive" fraternities (≥50% mortality/morbidity, n≥6), he operationalizes family clustering without distinguishing maternal intrauterine exposure from paternal germline transmission. The "contagion vs malformation" dichotomy prefigures the heritability-environment interaction debate but collapses them into a single "hereditary" bucket. This creates a retrocausal illusion: observing clustered disease retrospectively infers genetic causality while ignoring time-dependent exposure matrices. Modern GWAS replicate this trap by treating pedigree-based phenotypes as purely genetic signals, thereby inflating SNP-heritability estimates when environmental sharing is unmodeled. The multi-week trap manifests when researchers use these inflated estimates to design gene-targeting therapies, only to find null effects because the true driver was shared environment (e.g., household crowding, socioeconomic status). Galton's "peculiarly exposed during all the time of its existence before birth" insight—maternal-fetal interface vulnerability—was prematurely subsumed under "inheritance," delaying recognition of transgenerational epigenetics and microbiome transfer for over a century.

If consumption were exclusively paternally inherited, we would observe equal disease incidence in children of affected mothers versus affected fathers, controlling for postnatal contact. Prediction: Maternal pulmonary function decline (FEV1 reduction) correlates more strongly with offspring TB incidence than paternal decline.

Counterfactual prediction: If genes alone determined risk, discordance rate would be <5%.

Veto condition: If inheritance were purely Mendelian, fostered pups would match biological mothers' susceptibility; if maternal care drives outcomes, they should match adoptive mothers' resistance. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Galton's cognitive architecture reflects Victorian crisis habit regression: confronting epidemic mortality with limited intervention options (no antibiotics, ineffective sanatoria), he regressed to hereditarian explanations that offered illusory control through eugenic selection. His counter-trap attempt—defining "highly consumptive" fraternities to avoid statistical noise—paradoxically deepened the trap by privileging extreme cases that conflated genetics and environment. The "clock" of his era ticked toward Pasteurian germ theory, yet Galton's framework remained static, unable to integrate contagion mechanisms. Modern researchers inherit this recursive flaw: facing complex polygenic diseases (e.g., diabetes, schizophrenia), they regress to "missing heritability" narratives that ignore dynamic gene-environment correlations. The trap persists because it satisfies a psychological need for linear causality, whereas reality requires stochastic differential equations modeling exposure-time-response interactions. Breaking this loop demands abandoning static "nature/nurture" binaries for continuous-time Markov models where environmental states modulate genetic effect sizes moment-to-moment.

<THINK_TRACE>
Galton’s passage operates at the intersection of pre-Germer scientific epidemiology and early biometric thought. He observes that "consumptive mothers produce highly consumptive families much more commonly than consumptive fathers" and proposes a dual mechanism: hereditary "malformation" (genetic predisposition) plus contagious/infectious exposure during prenatal and postnatal periods. The core gray zone lies in attributing familial aggregation primarily to maternal influence without disentangling the pathways—is it the womb (intrauterine infection, nutritional programming, stress-induced epigenetic marks), infancy (prolonged skin-to-skin contact, breastfeeding, co-sleeping), or childhood proximity ("breathing the tainted air")?

To audit this, we must consider the time-dependency of exposure. Tuberculosis has a long latent period (months to years); thus, early-life exposure may determine whether latency progresses to active disease later. Galton’s definition of "highly consumptive" fraternities (≥50% morbidity/mortality, n≥6) implicitly selects for high-transmission households, likely characterized by overcrowding, poor ventilation, and low socioeconomic status—all of which amplify environmental risk independent of genetics.

A multi-hop derivation reveals: (1) Maternal TB increases fetal exposure to *M. tuberculosis* via placental transfusion; (2) Neonatal immune systems are naïve, making primary infection more severe; (3) Breastfeeding from infected mothers may transmit bacilli orally; (4) Shared sleeping arrangements prolong aerosol exposure. Each step introduces non-genetic variance that Galton’s "hereditary malformation" concept cannot capture.

Furthermore, his assumption that "so far as it is contagious...the child is peculiarly exposed during all the time of its existence before birth" overlooks that paternal TB also poses risks during pregnancy if the father is cohabiting—though perhaps less directly. The asymmetry he notes may stem from cultural norms (mothers spending more time cradling infants) rather than biological imperative.

Modern extensions must formalize this as a competing-risk survival model: let \(T_{death}\) be time to TB death, \(E_m(t)\) maternal exposure intensity at time \(t\), \(E_f(t)\) paternal exposure intensity, and \(G\) genetic susceptibility. Then the hazard \(\lambda(t | G, E_m, E_f)\) depends on cumulative exposure \(\int_0^t E_m(\tau)d\tau + \int_0^t E_f(\tau)d\tau\) weighted by \(G\). Galton’s data collapse these integrals into a binary "affected parent" variable, losing temporal resolution.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
We define the **Familial Aggregation Hazard Ratio (FAHR)** operator to quantify excess risk in offspring of affected parents relative to population baseline. Let \(h_0(t)\) be the baseline TB-specific hazard rate in the general population (units: time⁻¹, e.g., year⁻¹). For an individual with genetic susceptibility \(G \in [0,1]\) and cumulative exposure profile \(E(t) = \int_0^t \left( \alpha_m I_m(\tau) + \alpha_f I_f(\tau) \right) d\tau\), where \(I_m, I_f\) are indicator functions for maternal/paternal infectiousness at time \(\tau\), and \(\alpha_m, \alpha_f\) are exposure efficiency coefficients (dimensionless, capturing proximity, duration, viral load).

The conditional hazard is:
\[
\lambda(t \mid G, E) = h_0(t) \cdot \exp\left( \beta_G G + \beta_E E(t) \right)
\]
where \(\beta_G\) (log-hazard ratio per unit genetic risk) and \(\beta_E\) (log-hazard ratio per unit cumulative exposure) are parameters estimated from cohort data.

Galton’s observation implies \(\alpha_m > \alpha_f\) due to greater prenatal/postnatal contact. To isolate genetic effects, we require instrumental variables (e.g., Mendelian randomization using SNPs associated with immune function) that affect \(G\) but not \(E(t)\).

**Breakdown Regimes:**
1. **High Exposure Saturation**: When \(E(t) \gg 1/\beta_E\), the exponential term dominates, masking genetic variation (\(\lambda \approx h_0 e^{\beta_E E}\)). This occurs in endemic settings (e.g., urban slums), rendering genetic contributions negligible—a regime Galton’s method cannot detect.
2. **Low Genetic Variance**: If \(Var(G) \approx 0\) within the sampled population (e.g., isolated founder populations), \(\beta_G\) becomes unidentified, leading to overestimation of \(\beta_E\).
3. **Time-Varying Confounding**: If parental illness severity (affecting \(I_m, I_f\)) correlates with unmeasured household factors (crowding, nutrition), standard adjustment fails. Negative control exposures (e.g., paternal TB history unrelated to current household) are needed.

**Units & Dimensionality:**
- \(h_0(t)\): hazard rate (time⁻¹)
- \(E(t)\): dimensionless cumulative exposure index
- \(\beta_G, \beta_E\): log-hazard ratios (dimensionless)
- \(G\): standardized genetic risk score (mean 0, SD 1)
- \(\alpha_{m,f}\): exposure scaling factors (time⁻¹·concentration⁻¹·duration)

Galton’s "highly consumptive fraternity" criterion corresponds to selecting families where observed failures \(D \geq 0.5n\) and \(n \geq 6\). Under a binomial model with probability of failure \(p\), this defines a biased sample with elevated \(p\), inflating apparent heritability.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
