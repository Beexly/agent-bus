Let habit stock evolve as \(H_t=(1-\rho)H_{t-1}+s_t\), where \(s_t\ge0\) is current stake and \(\rho\in[0,1]\) is depreciation. Let period utility be \(u(c_t)-\kappa H_t\) and let the agent have present bias \(\beta<1\) over future utility. A sophisticated agent anticipates that expanded access lowers transaction costs and raises the salience of betting, increasing \(s_t\). The counter-trap is a commitment device: self-exclusion, deposit limits, or pre-commitment reduces \(s_t\) at a utility cost. The clock is the survey recall window. If betting events arrive as a Poisson process with hazard \(\lambda\), then annual participation is \(P_1=1-e^{-\lambda}\) and lifetime participation is \(P_\infty=1-e^{-\lambda T}\to1\) as \(T\to\infty\). A mixture with a never-bettor share \(q\) gives \(P_T=(1-q)(1-e^{-\lambda T})\). This explains why Spain’s lifetime rate of 49.75% [PAGE 10] can coexist with much lower annual participation: the clock, not a stable national habit, drives the contrast. Crisis habit regression occurs when expanded access shifts the hazard \(\lambda\) upward for young males with high income, the demographic group identified on [PAGE 13] and [PAGE 14]. The paper notes that Spanish law includes consumer-protection measures for “children and gambling addicts” and bans operators from granting loans to gamblers [PAGE 18]. That is a counter-trap at the institutional layer. The recursive theory-of-mind prediction is that welfare effects are heterogeneous: sophisticated agents use commitment devices and gain from lower effective prices, while naive agents may escalate. The paper’s Pareto-improvement language [PAGE 17] does not model this heterogeneity, so it is an incomplete welfare operator.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The requested cross-layer veto is physical, biomechanical, and weather-based. The source does not estimate these layers, so the adjudication must be conditional. Define \(W\) as a vector of physical, biomechanical, and weather states that affect event supply and outcomes. The paper’s probit estimand is \(P(Y_i=1\mid X_i=x_i)\) for individual \(i\). A cross-layer veto would require \(P(Y_i=1\mid X_i=x_i,W)\neq P(Y_i=1\mid X_i=x_i)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,W)\neq0\) in the survey population. The source provides no evidence for either condition. Physical and biomechanical constraints affect athlete performance and injury risk; weather affects event cancellation and playing conditions. These factors can create supply shocks, and the paper acknowledges that organized leagues provide a regular schedule of events that enhances betting opportunities [PAGE 2]. But a supply shock that is independent of demographic covariates does not bias the demographic participation estimates. The veto proof is therefore a conditional-independence argument: if \(W\perp X\) and \(W\perp Y\mid X\), then the probit marginal effects are not vetoed. The only plausible veto is if weather or biomechanical factors systematically change participation by age, sex, or income—for example, if bad weather cancels the events that young males prefer. The source does not document such a mechanism. The paper’s game-fixing discussion is a corruption layer, not a physical/weather layer: it argues that “game fixing and point shaving appear to be rare in North American team sports” [PAGE 16]. That is an integrity veto, not a biomechanical one. The cross-layer conclusion is that the physical/biomechanical/weather veto is not triggered by the provided source, but it remains an untested assumption. A future test would require event-level cancellation data merged with individual participation data.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
