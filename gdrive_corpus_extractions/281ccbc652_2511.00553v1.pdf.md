<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three recursive regimes can be distinguished. The first is crisis habit regression: under high cognitive load, a judge falls back on automatic ingroup favoritism. The paper’s format description supports the load premise: “Breaking judges must therefore assess multiple performance aspects while processing two consecutive performances and retaining detailed evaluations of the first until the second concludes—introducing unique cognitive demands” [PAGE 3]. If crisis habit regression dominated, bias should increase when load is highest and should be broad across judges. The observed pattern is only partly consistent: bias is significant overall, but “not uniformly exhibited across judges” and is “primarily driven by two individuals” [PAGE 13, PAGE 18]. That is more consistent with stable individual habit than with a universal load-induced regression.

The second is counter-trap recursion: a judge models other judges’ expected national bias and adjusts strategically. The BAR test is the direct probe. If judges anticipated that both athletes were represented on the panel, they might amplify, suppress, or trade bias; instead, “The inclusion of this control did not indicate a statistically significant change in the level of national bias (𝛽𝐵𝐴𝑅 = 0.379; t(1,294) = 0.467; p = 0.641)” [PAGE 13]. The paper concludes that “Breaking judges do not manipulate scores strategically” [PAGE 17]. This does not prove the absence of all recursive modeling; it only vetoes a panel-visible BAR counter-trap at the estimated average.

The third is clock recursion: judges model stakes and adjust as the competition advances. H3 predicted increasing bias in later stages. The stage interaction is essentially null: “The results indicate no significant increase in national bias as the events progressed (𝛽𝑆 = 0.027; t(1,294) = 0.079; p = 0.937)” [PAGE 13]. The paper therefore supports neither strategic counter-trap nor clock escalation. The recursive interpretation should be minimal: level-0 performance evaluation is noisy; level-1 compatriot favoritism is present in a subset; level-2 anticipation of colleagues is not detectable; level-3 public/self-presentational strategy is not identified by these data. The judge-specific heterogeneity—two positive significant judges, one negative but non-significant judge, and three with no compatriot scores—is the strongest evidence that the mechanism is person-specific rather than a uniform panel strategy [PAGE 13-14].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto can be stated formally. Let the observed score be
\[
S_{jp}=\theta_p+\beta_{NB}\phi_{jp}+\gamma X_p+\eta Z_{jp}+\varepsilon_{jp},
\]
where \(\theta_p\) is the performance-comparison fixed effect, \(X_p\) is any performance-comparison-level physical, biomechanical, or environmental factor common to all nine judges in comparison p, and \(Z_{jp}\) is a judge-specific factor. Because \(X_p\) is constant within p, it is absorbed by \(\theta_p\); it cannot explain \(\beta_{NB}\) unless it interacts with \(\phi_{jp}\). The paper’s fixed-effect design therefore vetoes additive biomechanical/weather explanations that operate at the battle-round or performance-comparison level. The source does not report weather variables or biomechanical covariates, so a direct weather test is unavailable; the veto is an identification argument, not an empirical weather regression.

The color/physical-uniform layer is explicitly addressed. The paper notes that “unlike in combat sports, where color-coded uniforms have been shown to influence scoring … such effects are unlikely in Breaking, as athletes do not wear designated red or blue attire” [PAGE 7-8]. Thus red/blue coding is a data-entry convention, not a physical uniform treatment. A physical-order or fatigue explanation would need to be judge-specific and correlated with compatriot status within p to survive the fixed-effect veto. The source does not provide athlete order, fatigue, heart-rate, force, or kinematic variables, so those layers cannot be positively tested here; they remain unmeasured confounds only if they interact with \(\phi_{jp}\).

The main residual veto risk is therefore not weather or biomechanics as common p-level shocks, but judge-specific response heterogeneity: a judge may score athletes of a particular style, school, or body type differently, and that style may correlate with nationality. That would be an omitted \(Z_{jp}\) correlated with \(\phi_{jp}\), not a physical main effect. The paper’s own limitation language supports caution: judge-specific estimates are averages and “may not fully capture the variability of national bias at the level of individual scoring instances” [PAGE 19]. Cross-layer adjudication therefore returns: physical/biomechanical/weather main effects are absorbed or unavailable; they do not veto the national-bias coefficient as estimated; but they also cannot be ruled out as judge-specific interactions without additional data.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
