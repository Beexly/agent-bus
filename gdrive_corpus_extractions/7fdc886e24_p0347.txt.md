Frank Knight's seminal work did not emerge in a vacuum. His intellectual precursors stretch back centuries:

**Ancient Precursors.** Thucydides documented Athenian traders who cornered grain markets during the Peloponnesian War, manipulating futures prices to exploit famine predictions—a proto-example of predation-driven speculation. Roman agronomists like Columella discussed forward contracting and risk-bearing, though they lacked the systematic category of "expectation."

**Mercantilism and Commercial Revolution.** The rise of merchant capitalism in the 16th-18th centuries introduced structured speculation through bills of exchange and futures trading. Adam Smith criticized this in *Wealth of Nations*, noting that merchants often anticipated demand changes and manipulated supply to raise prices. Smith's concept of "profiting by occasion" is a conceptual ancestor of Knightian foresight advantage.

**Neoclassical Antecedents.** Carl Menger (late 19th century) and Léon Walras independently developed general equilibrium theory. Walras considered expectations as given parameters. Irving Fisher extended this in his 1907 work *The Rate of Interest*, introducing the pure rate of interest as a premium for patience. However, neither addressed endogenous expectation formation or its strategic uses. Frank Knight (after whom this analysis is named) read extensively in this tradition.

**Keynesian Inflection Point.** John Maynard Keynes shifted focus from long-run equilibrium to short-run uncertainty and animal spirits. His *General Theory* (1936) posited that investment decisions are driven by "animal spirits" rather than marginal productivity of capital. This acknowledged Knightian friction but emphasized psychological factors over legal-structural ones. Keynes identified "speculative efficiency" (betting on market psychology) versus "enterprise" (long-term productive investment), echoing Knight's distinction.

**Modern Developments.** Eugene Fama's Efficient Market Hypothesis (1970) attempted to rule out persistent abnormal returns, but empirical evidence showed momentum effects, overreaction biases, and bubble dynamics—precisely Knightian phenomena of mispriced capital. Robert Shiller's research on irrational exuberance demonstrated that asset prices sometimes deviate far from fundamental values due to social contagion and herding behavior. Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz proved that in imperfect-information economies, prices always reflect some imperfect information—and thus some room for predation remains.

The trap here is underestimating how legal structures can both enable and inhibit Knightian dynamics. By retroactively tracing the genealogy, we isolate the key diagnostic: whether an economy tolerates foresight asymmetries as competitive advantage or as criminal activity.
