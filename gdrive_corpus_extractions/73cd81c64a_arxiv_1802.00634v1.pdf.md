The source paper is a compact engineering study about making single-image human pose estimation reliable in an unusually hostile aquatic setting. The reasoning chain begins with a physical observation: a swimming channel permits a stationary camera to record a swimmer from the side while the athlete remains approximately fixed relative to the camera because the water current matches the swimming velocity [PAGE 1]. This creates long, temporally coherent video sequences, but it also creates a pose-estimation problem where the visual evidence is corrupted by water, bubbles, spray, refraction, and occlusion [PAGE 3]. The paper’s central hypothesis is therefore not “more generic pose estimation,” but “activity-conditioned and temporally conditioned pose estimation.” In other words, the swimmer’s stroke class and the temporal continuity of the stroke cycle are treated as information sources that reduce the effective pose hypothesis space.

The first reasoning hop is the baseline Convolutional Pose Machine. The baseline network takes an image patch centered on the person and outputs a stack of joint confidence maps. The predicted joint location is the spatial argmax of the final heatmap for that joint [PAGE 3]. This is a formal maximum-a-posteriori-like decision rule if the heatmap is interpreted as a likelihood or confidence field. The paper explicitly describes the heatmap as confidence that joint j is at image location z [PAGE 3]. The gray-zone issue appears immediately: when the water surface refracts the apparent joint position, or when a limb is occluded by the waterline or by another limb, the heatmap may become multimodal. The argmax operator is brittle under multimodality because it collapses a distribution over plausible locations into one point estimate without explicitly representing ambiguity.

The second hop is style conditioning. The four swimming styles partition the dataset into classes: backstroke, breaststroke, butterfly, and freestyle [PAGE 2]. The authors encode style as spatially replicated one-hot class label maps and add them as extra convolutional input channels [PAGE 3–4]. Mathematically, because the class map is spatially constant, its convolution effect reduces to a class-dependent bias shift on each filter [PAGE 4]. This is important: the architecture does not merely concatenate a label vector at the classifier head; it modulates convolutional feature extraction throughout selected layers. The paper’s explanation is that this allows filters to act as on-off switches depending on the swimming style [PAGE 4]. In probabilistic terms, the style variable reduces the prior entropy over feasible poses. If four styles were equiprobable and perfectly known, the label itself carries at most log2(4) = 2 bits of class information. The real benefit, however, is not the two bits alone; it is the induced conditional dependency structure between joints under each stroke class. For anti-symmetrical strokes such as freestyle and backstroke, left and right limbs can be independently visible or occluded, so style information helps resolve left-right ambiguity [PAGE 4].

The third hop is temporal refinement. The baseline treats each frame independently, ignoring the fact that consecutive poses are strongly dependent [PAGE 4]. The proposed temporal module receives the current frame plus a sequence of single-frame pose heatmaps from past and future frames. The sequence is defined as z_t = (ĥ_{t−2l}, ĥ_{t−2(l−1)}, …, ĥ_{t+2l}), with nominal sequence length k = 4l + 1 and effective sampled length k′ = 2l + 1 because only every other frame is used [PAGE 4]. This design avoids explicit recurrence and avoids optical flow. The architecture has separate past, present, and future branches, each trained to predict the current-frame pose, followed by a temporal pooling layer that learns joint-wise weights over the three branches [PAGE 5]. The learned average weights are close to equal across past, present, and future branches: approximately 0.289, 0.300, and 0.306 [PAGE 7]. This is evidence that the network is not merely smoothing the present prediction; it is genuinely using temporal context.

A gray-zone audit must examine whether the reported gains are robust or artifactual. First, the dataset is small: 24 videos, 7,146 annotated frames, with one video per style held out for testing [PAGE 5]. Second, for symmetric styles such as breaststroke and butterfly, only the left side is annotated and the right side is approximated by mirroring because of the side view [PAGE 2]. This may inflate performance for symmetric styles because the task is partially simplified. Third, the combined test set is biased toward backstroke and breaststroke because those styles have more test frames [PAGE 6]. Fourth, temporal refinement can become unstable for longer sequences, and performance declines for some styles beyond a certain k [PAGE 7]. Fifth, the temporal window can interpolate only when the joint is at least partially observable across the window; if a joint is occluded by the water surface for the entire window, temporal pooling cannot create missing visual evidence. The strongest interpretation is therefore conditional: style conditioning helps when the style label is correct and the style-specific biomechanical prior is valid; temporal refinement helps when motion is smooth and the joint is observable in neighboring frames; neither mechanism can overcome persistent occlusion or severe refraction unless additional physical or multi-view evidence is introduced.

Let the input video frame at time t be x_t ∈ ℝ^{H×W×3}, with image coordinate lattice Z ⊂ ℤ². For joint j ∈ {1,…,J}, with J = 14 in the paper’s body model [PAGE 2], the baseline CPM produces a stage-s heatmap ĥ_{s,j}(z) ∈ [0,1], interpreted as confidence that joint j is located at z [PAGE 3]. The final predicted location is

\[
\hat{y}_j = \arg\max_{z \in Z} \hat{h}^{S}_{j}(z),
\]

which is the paper’s Equation (1) [PAGE 3]. The units of z and \hat{y}_j are pixels unless a camera calibration maps them to SI units; in calibrated physical coordinates, z and y would be in meters.

The evaluation metric is Percentage of Correct Keypoints, PCK. The paper states: “The PCK metric counts a joint as correctly localized if the euclidean distance to the ground truth location does not exceed a fixed fraction α of the torso diameter” [PAGE 6]. Formally, for N test instances and J joints,

\[
\mathrm{PCK}_{\alpha}
=
\frac{1}{NJ}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\sum_{j=1}^{J}
\mathbf{1}
\left(
\left\|
\hat{y}_{i,j} - y_{i,j}
\right\|_2
\le
\alpha D_i
\right),
\]

where D_i is the torso diameter for instance i, defined as the distance between the left hip and right shoulder [PAGE 6], and α = 0.2 is used [PAGE 6]. The indicator is dimensionless; PCK is an empirical probability estimate in [0,1], often reported as a percentage. If D_i is measured in meters, then αD_i is a physical length threshold in meters. If D_i is measured in pixels, the threshold is in pixels.

For style conditioning, let M = 4 swimming classes. For class m, the one-hot class label map C_m(z) ∈ {0,1}^{H×W} is spatially constant over the valid region: C_m(z) = 1 for the active style and 0 otherwise. The paper states: “the respective map of a given swimming style is filled with ones, while the remaining three maps are zeroed” [PAGE 3]. For a convolutional layer with input feature maps I_i, filter weights W_{o,i}, class-map weights U_{o,m}, and bias b_o, the pre-activation at output channel o is

\[
a_o(z)
=
\phi
\left(
\sum_i
(W_{o,i} * I_i)(z)
+
\sum_{m=1}^{4}
U_{o,m} C_m(z)
+
b_o
\right),
\]

with ReLU activation φ(u) = max(0, u). Because C_m(z) is spatially constant, the class-map term is equivalent to a class-dependent bias:

\[
b_o(m)
=
b_o
+
\sum_{m'=1}^{4} U_{o,m'} C_{m'}(z).
\]

The paper explicitly says: “the new filter weights shift the filter activations by a constant but class dependent value … This is identical to an additional class dependent bias term learned for each filter” [PAGE 4]. This is the formal operator behind the claimed “on-off switching functionality” [PAGE 4].

In information units, if S denotes the swimming style variable and Y denotes the pose configuration, the style label can reduce pose uncertainty by

\[
I(Y;S)
=
H(Y) - H(Y \mid S)
\quad \text{bits}.
\]

Under an ideal equiprobable four-class prior, H(S) ≤ log₂4 = 2 bits [PAGE 2]. This is not a guarantee of a 2-bit performance gain; it is only an upper bound on the label’s standalone class information. The actual benefit depends on how strongly style constrains joint configurations and whether the style label is correct.

For temporal refinement, the input sequence is

\[
z_t
=
\left(
\hat{h}_{t-2l},
\hat{h}_{t-2(l-1)},
\ldots,
\hat{h}_{t+2l}
\right),
\]

which is the paper’s Equation (2) [PAGE 4]. The nominal length is

\[
k = 4l + 1,
\]

and because only every other frame is used, the effective number of pose maps is

\[
k' = 2l + 1
\]

[PAGE 4]. If the sampling rate is f_s in Hz, the nominal temporal span of the full index range is approximately

\[
\Delta t = \frac{k-1}{f_s}
\quad \text{seconds}.
\]

The paper says the videos are recorded at 50 Hz and that pose sequences use every other frame [PAGE 4]. For k = 29, l = 7, k′ = 15, and Δt ≈ 28 / 50 = 0.56 s under the 50 Hz interpretation. The units are seconds if f_s is in s⁻¹.

The temporal module has three branches: past, present, and future. The past and future branches receive only heatmap sequences, not raw image content, forcing them to infer forward or backward temporal interpolation [PAGE 5]. The present branch receives the current image and current single-frame estimate [PAGE 5]. The final temporal pooling layer computes, for each joint j, a learned weighted combination of the three branch heatmaps. The paper states: “For each joint separately, a single 1x1 convolution filter is learned on the respective heatmaps from all three branches. Each filter computes a weighted average to combine the prediction heatmaps for one specific joint” [PAGE 5]. Formally,

\[
h^{*}_{t,j}(z)
=
\phi
\left(
w_{p,j} h^{(p)}_{t,j}(z)
+
w_{c,j} h^{(c)}_{t,j}(z)
+
w_{f,j} h^{(f)}_{t,j}(z)
+
b_j
\right),
\]

where p, c, and f denote past, current/present, and future branches. The averaged learned weights reported in the paper are approximately w_p = 0.289, w_c = 0.300, and w_f = 0.306 [PAGE 7]. The weights are dimensionless filter coefficients; the heatmaps are confidence fields, so the pooled output is also a confidence field before final normalization or decoding.

Breakdown regimes follow directly from the operators. First, if the heatmap argmax is unstable because multiple modes have similar confidence, the point estimate can jump between modes. This is especially relevant for “Ambiguous joint locations due to refraction at the water surface” [PAGE 3]. Second, if the true error satisfies ||ŷ − y||₂ > αD, the PCK indicator is zero regardless of semantic plausibility. Third, if a joint is occluded across the entire temporal window, the mutual information between the target joint and the available heatmaps may approach zero; then temporal pooling cannot recover the joint except through a learned prior, which may be unreliable. Fourth, if k becomes too large, the paper reports that “performance starts to decline again for some of the swimming styles” and “the increasingly larger network input renders the training less stable” [PAGE 7]. Thus the temporal operator has a non-monotonic bias-variance regime: too little context fails to correct single-frame ambiguity, while too much context increases optimization instability and may incorporate irrelevant motion.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage begins with classical pictorial structures, where the body is modeled as interacting parts and the objective is to find the most probable part configuration given an image [PAGE 2]. That tradition supplied the idea that pose estimation is not merely local appearance classification but structured inference over body-part dependencies. The convolutional transition is explicitly traced through direct coordinate regression, such as DeepPose, and then to confidence-map regression, where networks predict likelihoods over all possible joint locations [PAGE 2]. The paper notes that spatial encoding of the learning objective “seems to be more natural to CNNs compared to the direct regression of image coordinates” [PAGE 2]. This is the conceptual bridge from coordinate regression to heatmap-based CPMs.

The direct ancestor of the baseline is the Convolutional Pose Machine of Wei et al. [30], cited as the source of the baseline architecture [PAGE 1–3]. The CPM’s staged design is historically important because it allows iterative refinement: early stages produce coarse heatmaps, later stages use larger receptive fields to resolve ambiguities [PAGE 3]. The swimming paper inherits this staged logic but extends it along two new axes: activity conditioning and temporal conditioning. The activity-conditioning branch is genealogically related to part-based models and class-specific priors, because swimming style acts as a high-level categorical constraint on feasible limb configurations. The temporal branch is related to video pose methods that extend spatial part interactions with temporal dependencies [PAGE 2]. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from optical-flow methods and recurrent networks: “In our approach we avoid the computational expensive extraction of optical flow and the data-intensive training of RNNs due to limited video material” [PAGE 2]. This is a crucial historical constraint: the method is shaped by small-data conditions in sports video.

The multi-week traps in this lineage are methodological. The first trap is benchmark overfitting: general pose datasets such as LSP and MPII emphasize varied appearances and unrestricted poses, but the swimming domain has limited background and pose variety, while adding severe water-based occlusion [PAGE 2–3]. A model tuned only for generic benchmarks may fail when the waterline removes visual evidence. The second trap is temporal leakage: because swimming motion is cyclic, similar poses recur within the same video. If frames from one video are randomly split into training and test, temporally adjacent frames can leak information. The authors avoid this by partitioning on video boundaries: “the partitioning is performed on video boundaries such that frames from one video are either all in the training set or in the test set” [PAGE 5]. The third trap is symmetry shortcutting: for breaststroke and butterfly, only one side is annotated and mirrored because of side-view occlusion [PAGE 2]. This reduces task difficulty and may make performance look stronger than it would be under full bilateral annotation. The fourth trap is sequence-length overconfidence: longer temporal windows are intuitively better, but the paper finds that beyond a certain k, performance can decline and training becomes less stable [PAGE 7]. The genealogy therefore teaches a recurring lesson: architecture must match the physical sensing modality, not merely the generic task label “pose estimation.”
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis treats the network, the coach, and the evaluator as agents with different belief states. The baseline single-frame CPM has a crisis habit: when a joint is occluded or refracted, it falls back on local appearance and generic body priors. This habit is a regression to the most common training pose, but in swimming, the most common pose may still be wrong for the specific stroke phase. For example, if the right wrist is occluded in freestyle, the baseline may produce a low-confidence or misplaced wrist heatmap. The paper reports a right wrist detection rate of 48.5% for freestyle in the baseline [PAGE 6]. That is the observable signature of the crisis habit.

The counter-trap is the architecture’s attempt to model the coach’s reasoning. A swimming coach does not infer a pose from one ambiguous pixel region alone; the coach uses stroke identity, rhythm, and recent motion. The style-conditioned network encodes this by adding one-hot swimming style maps, which act as class-dependent biases and allow stroke-specific filters [PAGE 4]. The temporal network encodes rhythm by using past and future heatmaps in separate branches and then pooling them [PAGE 5]. In theory-of-mind terms, the model is learning to ask: “Given the stroke class and the recent trajectory, what pose is physically plausible now?” This is not explicit biomechanical simulation, but it is a learned approximation of a coach’s temporal-interpolation habit.

The clock is the third agent variable. The paper states that pose changes between frames are limited, “especially … for the swimmer videos recorded at 50Hz” [PAGE 4]. The clock defines how much temporal context is useful. At high sampling rates, neighboring frames are highly redundant, so temporal interpolation can average out local noise. But if the effective temporal window becomes too long, the assumption of smooth local motion weakens, especially across stroke transitions or rapid limb reversals. The use of every other frame reduces input size while retaining a longer nominal span [PAGE 4]. This is a deliberate temporal aliasing trade-off: the model sacrifices some temporal resolution to gain a wider context.

The recursive loop among these agents can fail in three ways. First, if the evaluator assumes that higher PCK always means better physical plausibility, the model may be rewarded for exploiting annotation mirror shortcuts in symmetric styles [PAGE 2]. Second, if the coach assumes style labels are always correct, the model may become overconfident under mislabeled video. Third, if the clock assumes stationarity, the model may over-smooth rapid strokes. The paper’s empirical results show that the combined system improves to 95.7% PCK@0.2 on the combined test set [PAGE 8], but the improvement is not uniform: temporal refinement gains are concentrated in backstroke and freestyle, with +6.0 PCK each for k = 29 [PAGE 7]. This suggests that the temporal agent helps most where single-frame ambiguity is high and motion is sufficiently smooth, not everywhere.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto requires checking whether a proposed inference is compatible with physical optics, biomechanics, and environmental disturbance. The first veto layer is optical. The source lists “Ambiguous joint locations due to refraction at the water surface” as a core challenge [PAGE 3]. If refraction displaces the apparent joint location by a vector δ such that

\[
\|\delta\|_2 > \alpha D,
\]

then even a semantically correct joint interpretation will be counted incorrectly by PCK@α. In physical units, if D is torso length in meters and α = 0.2, the allowable error is 0.2 torso lengths. A refraction-induced displacement larger than this threshold vetoes any claim of reliable metric-level localization from that view alone. The paper’s qualitative examples show that the water surface can obscure or distort head and lower-leg joints [PAGE 3]. Therefore, a model that claims to recover those joints must either use a different view, exploit temporal continuity across frames where refraction changes, or explicitly model the water surface. The paper’s temporal refinement does not explicitly model refraction; it only exploits temporal consistency. Thus the veto is partial: temporal refinement can help if the ambiguity fluctuates over time, but it cannot veto-proof a persistent refractive bias.

The second veto layer is biomechanical. Swimming styles impose strong kinematic constraints. The paper notes that for freestyle, “the positioning of both arms on top of each other is unlikely for a freestyle swimmer” [PAGE 4]. This is a class-conditional biomechanical veto: if a single-frame estimate places both arms in a configuration inconsistent with freestyle, the style-conditioned model should suppress it. Formally, let P(y | s) be the feasible pose distribution under style s. If a candidate pose y has P(y | s) ≈ 0, then the posterior P(y | x, s) should also be near zero under a well-calibrated model. The one-hot class maps operationalize this by enabling style-specific filters [PAGE 4]. However, the veto fails if the style label is wrong, if the athlete performs an atypical motion, or if the stroke phase genuinely produces a rare but valid configuration. Therefore the biomechanical veto is valid only as a soft prior, not as a hard logical constraint.

The third veto layer is environmental weather and spray. The paper lists “Image noise due to bubbles and spray” and “Frequent self-occlusions” as challenges [PAGE 3]. If bubbles occlude the joint evidence in all frames of the temporal window, then the likelihood p(x_t | y_t) becomes weakly informative or non-informative for that joint. In information terms, if I(Y_t; X_window) ≈ 0 for an occluded joint, no deterministic post-processing network can reliably reconstruct Y_t without external assumptions. The temporal pooling layer can combine predictions, but it cannot manufacture missing evidence. The empirical baseline right-wrist freestyle score of 48.5% illustrates this failure mode [PAGE 6]. The combined model improves freestyle substantially [PAGE 8], but the improvement should be interpreted as recovery from partially observable motion, not recovery from total occlusion.

The final cross-layer judgment is therefore conditional approval. The architecture is physically admissible when: (1) the swimming style label is correct; (2) the temporal window is short enough that motion smoothness holds; (3) the joint is at least intermittently visible; and (4) the torso scale D is reliably estimated for PCK normalization. The architecture is vetoed when any of these conditions is violated persistently. In particular, any claim that the model can recover a joint hidden behind the waterline for the entire temporal window is vetoed by the absence of visual information.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable because they map directly to metrics, architecture switches, or data conditions described in the paper.

1. Incorrect style label veto.
Counterfactual: the one-hot swimming style map is wrong, e.g., a freestyle video is labeled as butterfly. The style-conditioned filters will apply the wrong biomechanical prior. The paper shows that style information produces large gains for anti-symmetrical strokes, especially freestyle: “Repeated addition of the swimming style labels to subsequent convolution layers proves to be the superior approach for freestyle, leading to a gain of +12.6 PCK” [PAGE 6]. The combined model reaches 95.7% combined PCK@0.2 and 95.7% freestyle when style and temporal refinement are combined [PAGE 8]. If the style label is wrong, the model should lose the anti-symmetrical disambiguation benefit. Verifiable prediction: under a controlled label-swap experiment, freestyle PCK@0.2 for the style-conditioned model should fall toward the baseline freestyle score of 79.0% or at least lose most of the +12.6 PCK style gain [PAGE 6, 8]. If the model still performs near 95.7% under wrong labels, the style-conditioning claim is suspect because the network may be relying mostly on temporal smoothing or dataset shortcuts.

2. Temporal window degeneracy veto.
Counterfactual: the sequence length k is set to 1, so there are no past or future pose maps. The paper states that k = 1 is a verification experiment: “With no sequential information at all, the outer network branches can be ignored and we only have a fourth network stage in the baseline CNN architecture” [PAGE 7]. The result with k = 1 “reach and slightly surpass the baseline performance” but does not provide the temporal refinement gain [PAGE 7]. Verifiable prediction: with k = 1, the temporal refinement network’s pooled output should be functionally close to the present branch or an extra single-frame stage, and the combined gain from temporal context should vanish. If k = 1 still yields the full k = 29 gain, then the temporal explanation is invalid. Conversely, if k is made very large, the paper reports declining performance for some styles and less stable training [PAGE 7]. Verifiable prediction: beyond the optimal k around 29, test PCK@0.2 for at least one style should decline, and training loss or variance should worsen.

3. Persistent occlusion veto.
Counterfactual: a joint is occluded by the waterline or self-occlusion for the entire temporal window, e.g., lower legs or a wrist hidden across all frames used by z_t. The paper lists “Occluded joints by the water surface” and “Frequent self-occlusions” as core challenges [PAGE 3]. Temporal pooling combines past, present, and future heatmaps, but if all those heatmaps lack reliable evidence for that joint, the pooled heatmap cannot be correct except by prior hallucination. Verifiable prediction: in a masking experiment where the occluded joint’s heatmaps are replaced by low-confidence or adversarial maps across the entire sequence, the temporal model’s PCK for that joint should collapse toward chance or baseline-occluded levels. The baseline freestyle right wrist detection rate of 48.5% provides a reference failure scale [PAGE 6]. The temporal gains reported for backstroke and freestyle, +6.0 PCK each for k = 29, should be concentrated in joints and frames with intermittent visibility, not in fully occluded intervals [PAGE 7]. If the model maintains high PCK for a joint that is continuously occluded, the evaluation likely contains leakage, annotation mirroring, or an overly permissive distance threshold.

These three vetoes form a minimal verification suite. A valid claim of “activity-conditioned continuous pose estimation” must survive them. If style labels are irrelevant, temporal context is irrelevant, or occlusion can be ignored, then the paper’s central causal story is not supported. The reported results pass the first two checks within the paper’s own experiments: style helps anti-symmetrical strokes [PAGE 6], and temporal refinement improves combined PCK from 90.1% to 93.8% before style combination, then to 95.7% when combined [PAGE 7–8]. The third check remains conditional: the method is powerful under intermittent observability, but it is vetoed under persistent, fully unobserved occlusion.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
