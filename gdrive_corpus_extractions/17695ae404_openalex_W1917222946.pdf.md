<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The coach models the athlete as possessing internal forward/inverse models and modular controllers that anticipate action effects [PAGE 8-9]. The athlete, in turn, models the coach’s evaluation function: which deviations will be penalized and which will be treated as style. Crisis habit regression occurs when, under pressure, the athlete reverts to an over-learned motor primitive that is locally stable but globally suboptimal relative to the current task constraints [PAGE 8-9]. The counter-trap is the coach’s attribution of an observed error to the currently executed sub-action when the root cause actually resides in a previous sub-action whose final state set the current initial state [PAGE 11]. Feedback then targets the symptom, not the cause, producing a recursive correction loop.

The clock is the temporal cascade $t_{\text{root}} \rightarrow t_{\text{obs}}$ mediated by feedback delay $\Delta t_{\text{fb}}$. If $\Delta t_{\text{fb}} > \tau_{\text{root}}$, temporal credit assignment fails and the athlete updates the wrong module [PAGE 11]. The recursion closes only when explicit instruction—such as gaze anchoring in balancing—is recognized as a conscious correlate of modular control rather than a replacement for it [PAGE 9]. Successful coaching therefore requires a second-order model: the coach must represent the athlete’s representation of the task’s functional structure, and update that model only when the “in order to” clause for each sub-action and modality remains intact [PAGE 6].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: athlete attributes—body height, coordinative competence, leg-muscle strength—constrain optimal solutions [PAGE 4]. Biomechanical layer: acceleration path, inter-segmental energy transfer, action-reaction [PAGE 3 Table 1]. Weather/environment layer: water resistance punishes speed fluctuations; snow, ice, air, and parcours impose demand-increasing conditions; an unpredictable squall in ski flying lies outside athlete influence and therefore cannot be an athlete-caused error [PAGE 2, 4]. Rule layer: a somersault over the long-jump flight phase may be biomechanically optimal for perpendicular distance but becomes non-functional once explicitly banned by the rule commission [PAGE 4].

Veto proof: a candidate movement $m$ is admissible iff $\forall c \in \mathcal{C}_{\text{hard}},\; c(m) = \text{true}$, where $\mathcal{C}_{\text{hard}}$ comprises safety rules, physical realizability, and official sport rules. If any hard constraint returns false, $m$ is vetoed regardless of kinematic elegance.

Illustrative vetoes: (1) one-handed basketball throw is vetoed when player-basket distance exceeds the strength threshold; two-handed or underarm throws become functionally necessary [PAGE 10]; (2) barbell half-squat with rounded back is vetoed due to backbone injury risk under repeated overload [PAGE 3 Table 2]; (3) eliminating the rounded-back Fosbury-flop landing is vetoed because, although it does not affect jumping performance, it prevents neck injury [PAGE 7].

Scenario 1 — Ski-jump arm-swing restoration. Counterfactual: reintroduce the early-20th-century upward arm swing at takeoff to increase vertical takeoff speed [PAGE 10]. Veto: functional analysis shows aerodynamic efficiency (arms held back) is biomechanically more important than takeoff support by arm movement [PAGE 10]. Prediction: athletes adopting forward-extended arms will exhibit increased drag and reduced flight distance despite marginally higher takeoff speed; falsifiable via aerodynamic force measurements and jump-length outcomes.

Scenario 2 — Child basketball one-handed mandate. Counterfactual: require a young child to use a one-handed overhand throw because it matches the elite textbook model [PAGE 10]. Veto: the child’s arm strength lies below the strength-related threshold, making the one-handed throw non-functional for the required distance [PAGE 10]. Prediction: forced one-handed attempts yield underachievement (ball fails to reach the basket), whereas two-handed or underarm throws are functionally optimal; falsifiable via shot-success rate and release-speed measurement.

Scenario 3 — Fosbury-flop landing-style correction. Counterfactual: remove the rounded-back landing because it deviates from an aesthetic ideal and appears non-functional for clearance [PAGE 7]. Veto: landing does not affect jumping performance, but a rounded back prevents neck injury [PAGE 7]. Prediction: removing the rounded-back strategy leaves bar-clearance kinematics unchanged while increasing cervical-injury incidence; falsifiable via injury logs and pre/post-landing kinematics.
