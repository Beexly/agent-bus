<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core operator is the Bradley–Terry paired-comparison model. For teams \(i,j\), match \(r\), and home-field indicator \(h_{ijr}\in\{-1,0,1\}\), the source gives
\[
\Pr(Y_{ijr}=1)=\frac{\exp(\tau h_{ijr}+\mu_i-\mu_j)}{1+\exp(\tau h_{ijr}+\mu_i-\mu_j)},
\tag{2.1}
\]
where \(Y_{ijr}=1\) means team \(i\) defeats team \(j\) [PAGE 3]. The indicator is defined as \(1\) if the match is at team \(i\)’s home, \(0\) if neutral, and \(-1\) if at team \(j\)’s home [PAGE 3]. The parameters \(\mu_i\) and \(\tau\) are dimensionless on the logit link scale; \(\Pr(\cdot)\) is dimensionless; \(h_{ijr}\) is dimensionless. The likelihood assumes independence among matches:
\[
\ell(\mu,\tau)=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{r=1}^{n_{ij}}
\left[
y_{ijr}(\tau h_{ijr}+\mu_i-\mu_j)
-\log\{1+\exp(\tau h_{ijr}+\mu_i-\mu_j)\}
\right],
\tag{2.2}
\]
measured in nats when the natural logarithm is used [PAGE 3]. Identifiability is through pairwise differences; the source uses the sum contrast \(\sum_{i=1}^k\mu_i=0\) [PAGE 3]. The ranking lasso is the constrained problem
\[
(\hat\mu_\lambda,\hat\tau_\lambda)
=
\arg\max \ell(\mu,\tau)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|\mu_i-\mu_j|\le s,
\tag{3.1}
\]
with pair-specific weights \(w_{ij}\) [PAGE 5]. The equivalent penalized form is
\[
(\hat\mu_\lambda,\hat\tau_\lambda)
=
\arg\min
\left\{
-\ell(\mu,\tau)+\lambda\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|\mu_i-\mu_j|
\right\},
\tag{3.2}
\]
for a one-to-one relation between \(\lambda\) and \(s\) [PAGE 6]. The reformulation uses \(\theta_{ij}=\mu_i-\mu_j\) [PAGE 6]:
\[
(\hat\mu_\lambda,\hat\tau_\lambda,\hat\theta_\lambda)
=
\arg\min
\left\{
-\ell(\mu,\tau)+\lambda\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|\theta_{ij}|
\right\}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\theta_{ij}=\mu_i-\mu_j.
\tag{3.3}
\]
The adaptive ranking lasso uses
\[
w_{ij}=|\hat\mu_i^{(mle)}-\hat\mu_j^{(mle)}|^{-1},
\tag{3.4}
\]
so large estimated differences receive small weights and near-zero differences receive large weights [PAGE 7]. To avoid divergent MLEs for undefeated or winless teams, the source adds \(\epsilon\sum_{i<j}(\mu_i-\mu_j)^2\) with \(\epsilon=10^{-4}\) [PAGE 7]. The Augmented Lagrangian objective is
\[
F_{\lambda,v}(\mu,\tau,\theta,u)
=
-\ell(\mu,\tau)
+\lambda\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}|\theta_{ij}|
+\sum_{i<j}u_{ij}(\theta_{ij}-\mu_i+\mu_j)
+\frac{v}{2}\sum_{i<j}(\theta_{ij}-\mu_i+\mu_j)^2,
\tag{3.7}
\]
with \(u\) the Lagrangian multipliers and \(v>0\) the quadratic penalty coefficient [PAGE 8]. The \(\theta\)-update is soft-thresholding:
\[
\hat\theta_{\lambda,ij}
=
\operatorname{sign}(\tilde\theta_{\lambda,ij})
\left(
|\tilde\theta_{\lambda,ij}|-\frac{\lambda w_{ij}}{v}
\right)_{+},
\qquad
\tilde\theta_{\lambda,ij}
=
\hat\mu_{\lambda,i}-\hat\mu_{\lambda,j}-\frac{u_{\lambda,ij}}{v},
\tag{9}
\]
as given on [PAGE 9]. The multiplier update is
\[
u_{\lambda,ij}^{(new)}
=
u_{\lambda,ij}^{(old)}
+v(\hat\theta_{\lambda,ij}-\hat\mu_{\lambda,i}+\hat\mu_{\lambda,j}),
\tag{10}
\]
and \(v\) is set to the maximum of squared updated multipliers [PAGE 9]. Model selection uses
\[
\mathrm{AIC}(\lambda)=-2\ell(\hat\mu_\lambda,\hat\tau_\lambda)+2df(\lambda),
\qquad
\mathrm{BIC}(\lambda)=-2\ell(\hat\mu_\lambda,\hat\tau_\lambda)+\log n\,df(\lambda),
\]
with \(df(\lambda)\) estimated as the number of distinct groups [PAGE 10]. The hybrid method uses the lasso only for group selection and then refits by maximum likelihood with equal abilities inside each group [PAGE 10]. For ties, the cumulative link model is
\[
\Pr(Y_{ijr}\le y_{ijr})
=
\frac{\exp(\delta_{y_{ijr}}+h_{ijr}\tau+\mu_i-\mu_j)}
{1+\exp(\delta_{y_{ijr}}+h_{ijr}\tau+\mu_i-\mu_j)},
\]
with \(-\infty\le\delta_0\le\delta_1\le\delta_2\equiv+\infty\); neutral symmetry requires \(\delta_0=-\delta_1\), and if ties are absent \(\delta_0=\delta_1=0\) [PAGE 18]. The NHL/NFL examples report \(\hat\tau^{(mle)}=0.322\) with standard error \(0.149\) for the NFL [PAGE 5], and \(\hat\tau^{(mle)}=0.402\) with standard error \(0.066\), \(\hat\delta_1^{(mle)}=0.288\) with standard error \(0.024\) for NCAA hockey [PAGE 19]. Units: log-likelihood is in nats; \(\mu_i,\tau,\theta_{ij},h_{ijr}\) are dimensionless logit-scale quantities; \(\lambda\) is numerical in nats per weighted L1 unit; \(s\) is a dimensionless weighted L1 bound; \(v\) is numerical in nats per squared logit unit; \(u_{ij}\) is numerical in nats per logit unit. Assumptions: conditional independence of matches given \((\mu,\tau)\) [PAGE 3]; connected comparison graph and a contrast such as \(\sum_i\mu_i=0\); no team with all wins/all losses unless ridge is used [PAGE 7]; static abilities over the observed season; a single home-field parameter rather than team-specific home effects [PAGE 3]; no ties for the basic Bradley–Terry model, with ties handled by the cumulative link extension [PAGE 18]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs backward from the ranking lasso to Thurstone’s paired-comparison law [PAGE 1], the Bradley–Terry model [PAGE 2], the lasso [PAGE 2], the fused lasso [PAGE 6], the adaptive lasso [PAGE 7], generalized fused-lasso and generalized-lasso path methods [PAGE 6]–[PAGE 7], and the Augmented Lagrangian method of Hestenes and Powell [PAGE 8]. The paper explicitly says the penalty is “a generalization of the fused lasso penalty” [PAGE 6], and that the lack of a natural order among teams creates computational difficulties absent in ordered fused-lasso problems. It also cites She’s clustered lasso, Bondell and Reich’s factor-level fusion, Gertheiss and Tutz’s categorical collapsing, Guo et al.’s clustering shrinkage, and Tibshirani and Taylor’s generalized-lasso path algorithm [PAGE 6]. The adaptive step is genealogically tied to Zou’s adaptive lasso and to the inconsistency critique of plain L1 shrinkage [PAGE 7]. The numerical genealogy is not incidental: the quadratic penalty method is rejected because “numerical instabilities may arise for large values of the penalty coefficient v even if the objective function is smooth” [PAGE 8], and the Augmented Lagrangian method is adopted because convergence can be assured without increasing \(v\) indefinitely if the multipliers converge [PAGE 9]. The multi-week trap is that the source’s NFL example spans 17 weeks and 256 matches [PAGE 3], but the model has one static ability per team and one home-field parameter; it is not a week-by-week dynamic rating model. A naive transfer to multi-week forecasting would treat week 1 and week 17 as exchangeable after conditioning on \(\mu_i\). The paper’s cross-validation randomly samples half the matches without replacement [PAGE 16], not by week, so it measures match-level predictive fit, not rolling future-week calibration. A second multi-week trap is schedule imbalance: the NFL schedule is division- and conference-structured [PAGE 3], and the NCAA hockey tournament is “highly incomplete,” with about 73.3% of possible pair matches unplayed and team match counts varying from 31 to 43 [PAGE 18]. Group labels can therefore reflect schedule connectivity and repeated pairings, not only latent ability. A third trap is criterion hysteresis: along the \(\lambda\) path, nonadaptive estimates show “many crossings between teams and groups along the clustering process” [PAGE 11], so a multi-week narrative built from one path point can reverse at a neighboring penalty. The adaptive path is smoother because it protects large differences and preserves the maximum-likelihood ordering [PAGE 13], but it still depends on the MLE seed and ridge stabilization [PAGE 7]. A fourth trap is the hybrid refit: it recovers MLE-scale abilities but, in the source’s cross-validation, predictions are “comparable to those based on maximum likelihood estimates with a very limited improvement” [PAGE 17]. Thus the genealogical lesson is that the predictive gain comes from shrinkage and grouping, not merely from selecting groups and refitting.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In this source, recursive theory of mind is best read as the interaction among three selection habits. The first is crisis habit regression: when the penalty is too strong or the path is nonadaptive, the procedure over-collapses teams and loses discrimination. The source’s nonadaptive path is “quite irregular with many crossings between teams and groups along the clustering process” [PAGE 11], and the nonadaptive image plots have “several spots because teams are frequently classiﬁed in different groups with respect to their closer neighbors” [PAGE 13]. In a crisis, an analyst who reacts to the latest match or the latest path point will regress to unstable group labels. The second is the counter-trap: adaptive weights are designed to protect true large differences, using \(w_{ij}=|\hat\mu_i^{(mle)}-\hat\mu_j^{(mle)}|^{-1}\) [PAGE 7]. This is a counter-trap because it prevents the shrinkage from swallowing a genuinely large ability gap; the adaptive image plots form “smooth blocks formed by spatially contiguous pixels, thus preserving the maximum likelihood ranking order” [PAGE 13]. The third is the clock: the penalty path and the information criteria decide when to stop fusing. AIC selects 9 groups and BIC 7 groups in the NFL analysis [PAGE 13]–[PAGE 14], so the “clock” is not a physical game clock but a model-selection clock over \(\lambda\), \(s\), or cross-validation folds. The recursive part is that different agents—selectors, analysts, and forecasters—hold different beliefs about whether a group is real. A selector may ask, “If I treat these teams as equal, how will future opponents behave?”; the model answers through predicted match probabilities and through the cross-validated negative log-likelihood, where AIC and BIC improve on MLE by about 15–20% in the NFL example [PAGE 16]. The trap is to let the clock overrule the counter-trap: if one selects a very sparse BIC solution and then refits by MLE, the hybrid predictions resemble MLE and lose most of the shrinkage gain [PAGE 17]. Conversely, if one ignores the clock and uses an arbitrary small \(s\), all teams collapse to one group and neutral-match probability \(0.5\) [PAGE 12]. The stable recursive rule is therefore: use adaptive weights to protect large differences, use AIC/BIC or cross-validation as the stopping clock, and do not reinterpret group labels as physical equivalence. The hockey example reinforces this: with 58 teams and 1083 matches, AIC selects 7 groups and BIC 6, and the larger, more irregular tournament makes “the usefulness of the grouping effect induced by the lasso” more evident [PAGE 19]. The recursive theory-of-mind failure mode is over-attributing intent or ability to a group label; the source only licenses probabilistic equivalence within a fitted group, not a claim that the teams are biomechanically or psychologically identical.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is negative but precise: the source contains no mass, force, velocity, acceleration, temperature, wind, precipitation, injury, fatigue, or biomechanical variables. The only physical/design variable in the equations is the home-field indicator \(h_{ijr}\in\{-1,0,1\}\), defined on [PAGE 3]. The probability model (2.1) and log-likelihood (2.2) contain only \(h_{ijr}\), \(\mu_i\), \(\mu_j\), and \(\tau\) [PAGE 3]. Therefore a physical, biomechanical, or weather mechanism cannot be inferred from the fitted model; it is an unmodeled layer. The home-field parameter \(\hat\tau^{(mle)}=0.322\) with standard error \(0.149\) for the NFL [PAGE 5] is a statistical association with the home indicator, not a biomechanical proof of causation. If weather modifies the home effect or team ability, the single \(\tau\) in (2.1) is misspecified unless weather covariates or interactions are added; the source does not add them. If biomechanical fatigue changes \(\mu_i\) within the season, the static ability assumption is violated; the source does not include week-varying \(\mu_i\). If injury or roster change alters ability, the model treats it as part of the latent ability estimate, not as an identified physical effect. The correct veto rule is: any proposed cross-layer claim that requires a variable absent from (2.1)–(2.2) must be either rejected as unsupported or promoted to an explicit covariate and re-estimated. The design layer is also not physical: the NFL division/conference schedule [PAGE 3] and the NCAA hockey conference and incomplete schedule [PAGE 18] affect which pairwise differences are observed, but they are not biomechanical causes. The tie layer is statistical, not physical: the cumulative link model adds cutpoints \(\delta_0,\delta_1\) and requires \(\delta_0=-\delta_1\) for neutral symmetry, collapsing to \(\delta_0=\delta_1=0\) when ties are absent [PAGE 18]. Weather and biomechanics are therefore vetoed as explanatory layers for this source; they may be future covariates, but they are not part of the proof structure. The only defensible cross-layer statement is conditional: given the fitted \(\mu\) and \(\tau\), the model outputs a probability for a neutral or home match; it does not output a physical mechanism.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
