The core multi-hop derivation begins with the three-dimensional array \(X_{spm}\), the value of metric \(m\) for player \(p\) in season \(s\), and decomposes observed variation into player, season, player-season, and sampling components. Page 5 states the linear mixed-effects template \(X_{spm}=\mu_m+Z_{sm}+Z_{pm}+Z_{spm}+\epsilon_{spm}\), with random effects for season, player, player-season, and individual player-season noise [PAGE 5]. The key conceptual move is that “for an infinitely long season, a player’s metric would have no such variability, thus ϵspm = 0” [PAGE 5]. The first hop is the law of variance decomposition: \(V_{sm}[X]=E_{sm}[V_{spm}[X]]+V_{sm}[E_{spm}[X]]\) [PAGE 6]. The second hop converts this into discrimination, \(D_{sm}=1-\frac{E_{sm}[V_{spm}[X]]}{V_{sm}[X]}\), which is the fraction of between-player variance not attributable to within-player sampling noise [PAGE 7]. The third hop defines stability as \(S_m=1-\frac{E_m[V_{pm}[X]-V_{spm}[X]]}{V_m[X]-E_m[V_{spm}[X]]}\), with \(0\le S_m\le1\) [PAGE 8]. The fourth hop is independence: a Gaussian copula maps each metric to a latent normal \(Z_{spm}\), and \(I_{mM}=\frac{Var[Z_{spm}\mid\{Z_{spq}:q\in M\}]}{Var[Z_{spm}]}=C_{m,m}-C_{m,M}C^{-1}_{M,M}C_{M,m}\) [PAGE 9]. The fifth hop is redundancy compression via PCA, \(F_k=\frac{\sum_{1}^{k}\lambda_i}{\sum_{1}^{M}\lambda_i}\) [PAGE 9]. Thus the full chain is metric \(\to\) variance components \(\to\) discrimination/stability \(\to\) latent dependence \(\to\) PCA/hierarchical clustering \(\to\) decision use.

The gray-zone audit is critical. The paper explicitly says “our meta-metrics only measure the internal quality of a metric. The meta-metrics are not designed to provide any information about how relevant the metrics are for the sport of interest” [PAGE 24-25]. The extreme example is that “an athlete’s birthplace zip code would be perfectly discriminative, stable and independent from all other metrics, but is clearly irrelevant” [PAGE 25]. Therefore high \(D\), high \(S\), and high \(I\) are necessary reliability diagnostics, not sufficient decision warrants. Relevance is a fourth, separate meta-metric suggested by the authors [PAGE 25]. Another gray zone is conditional interpretation: “there is less variation in the number of blocks and rebounds by NBA centers, and as such, these metrics are less discriminative and stable than they are for the league as a whole” [PAGE 24]. So marginal \(D\) and \(S\) can reverse inside a position subgroup. A third gray zone is the exchangeable additive model: the paper warns that “an exchangeable, additive model is not appropriate for many of the metrics we consider” [PAGE 5], because percentages are bounded and many counts are discrete and positive [PAGE 6]. The nonparametric bootstrap estimator avoids most parametric assumptions [PAGE 10], but it still depends on resampling games within a season and on finite samples. A fourth gray zone is empirical Bayes: the 3P% shrinkage estimate is “biased for all players,” yet expected mean squared error can fall because variance is reduced [PAGE 20]. That is a bias-variance trade, not an unbiasedness claim. Finally, the independence score is latent-scale, not raw-scale: it measures conditional variance after the copula transform, so it is not a raw correlation and can be unstable under near-collinearity or rank-likelihood degeneracy [PAGE 9-10].
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal operators are defined on \(X_{spm}\), with \(s\) indexing season, \(p\) player, and \(m\) metric. Units: \(X_{spm}\) carries the metric’s own unit; variances such as \(V_{spm}[X]\), \(V_{sm}[X]\), \(V_{pm}[X]\), and \(V_m[X]\) carry squared metric units; \(D\), \(S\), \(I\), and \(F_k\) are dimensionless proportions, ideally in \([0,1]\); \(C\) is a unitless latent correlation matrix; \(\lambda_i\) are unitless eigenvalues of a correlation matrix.

1. Linear mixed-effects template [PAGE 5]:
\[
X_{spm}=\mu_m+Z_{sm}+Z_{pm}+Z_{spm}+\epsilon_{spm},
\]
\[
Z_{sm}\sim[0,\sigma^2_{SM}],\quad Z_{pm}\sim[0,\sigma^2_{PM}],\quad Z_{spm}\sim[0,\sigma^2_{SPM}],\quad \epsilon_{spm}\sim[0,\tau^2_M].
\]
Assumption: additive exchangeable random effects. Breakdown: bounded supports, heteroscedasticity, nonstationarity, selection, and context-dependent skill changes violate the template; the paper itself says this model may not be appropriate for many metrics [PAGE 5].

2. Discrimination [PAGE 7]:
\[
D_{sm}=1-\frac{E_{sm}[V_{spm}[X]]}{V_{sm}[X]},\qquad D_m=E_m[D_{sm}].
\]
Under the mixed model,
\[
D_m=\frac{\sigma^2_{PM}+\sigma^2_{SPM}}{\sigma^2_{PM}+\sigma^2_{SPM}+\tau^2_M}.
\]
Breakdown: if \(V_{sm}[X]=0\), \(D_{sm}\) is undefined; if bootstrap variance exceeds the empirical between-player variance, the estimator can fall outside the ideal range; if player groups are highly homogeneous, denominator collapse creates unstable discrimination.

3. Stability [PAGE 8]:
\[
S_m=1-\frac{E_m[V_{pm}[X]-V_{spm}[X]]}{V_m[X]-E_m[V_{spm}[X]]}.
\]
Under the mixed model,
\[
S_m=\frac{\sigma^2_{PM}}{\sigma^2_{PM}+\sigma^2_{SM}+\sigma^2_{SPM}}.
\]
The appendix proves \(0\le S_m\le1\) using nonnegative variance terms [PAGE 31]. Breakdown: denominator zero when non-sampling variation vanishes; negative or noisy bootstrap variance estimates can make the empirical ratio unstable; players with few seasons make \(V_{pm}[X]\) poorly identified.

4. Independence via Gaussian copula [PAGE 9]:
\[
Z_{sp}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathrm{MVN}(0,C),\qquad X_{spm}=F_m^{-1}[\Phi(Z_{spm})],
\]
\[
I_{mM}=\frac{Var[Z_{spm}\mid\{Z_{spq}:q\in M\}]}{Var[Z_{spm}]}
=C_{m,m}-C_{m,M}C^{-1}_{M,M}C_{M,m}.
\]
Assumption: latent Gaussian dependence and monotone marginal transforms. Breakdown: \(C_{M,M}\) singular under exact collinearity or duplicated metrics; empirical CDF degeneracy under all-tied or zero-attempt metrics; missing-not-at-random breaks rank likelihood; discrete metrics require the semiparametric rank-likelihood treatment cited as Hoff [PAGE 10, PAGE 27].

5. PCA redundancy [PAGE 9]:
\[
F_k=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_i}.
\]
Breakdown: if all \(\lambda_i=0\), \(F_k\) is undefined; near-zero eigenvalues make eigenvectors rotate unstably; sign and rotation are nonidentifiable, so component “meaning” is interpretive rather than uniquely estimated.

6. Bootstrap estimators [PAGE 10]:
\[
\hat D_{sm}=1-\frac{\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}\widehat{BV}[X_{spm}]}{\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}(X_{spm}-\bar X_{s\cdot m})^2},
\]
\[
\hat S_m=1-
\frac{\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac1{S_p}\sum_{s=1}^{S_p}\left[(X_{spm}-\bar X_{\cdot pm})^2-\widehat{BV}[X_{spm}]\right]}
{\frac1P\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac1{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S_p}\left[(X_{spm}-\bar X_{\cdot\cdot m})^2-\widehat{BV}[X_{spm}]\right]}.
\]
Assumption: game-level resampling within team-season reconstructs the sampling distribution [PAGE 10]. Breakdown: small game counts, injuries, work stoppages, roster changes, and non-independent games weaken the bootstrap; the paper normalizes by minutes or possessions where appropriate to reduce anomalous-event impact [PAGE 11].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is explicit: the authors root their meta-metrics in analysis of variance and psychometric reliability, citing Fisher’s ANOVA tradition, Cronbach’s alpha, and Kuder-Richardson reliability theory [PAGE 4, PAGE 26-27]. The bootstrap estimator descends from Efron and Tibshirani [PAGE 10, PAGE 26]. The shrinkage example descends from Stein-type and Efron-Morris variance-reduction logic [PAGE 20, PAGE 26]. The Gaussian copula dependence model uses Hoff’s rank likelihood [PAGE 10, PAGE 27]. PCA redundancy uses Mardia, Kent, and Bibby [PAGE 9, PAGE 27]. The empirical Bayes 3P% model uses Kelly and Morris [PAGE 19, PAGE 27]. This genealogy explains why the paper reads like a retrocausal correction of earlier blog-level debates: the blog discussions of three-point stabilization and reliability [PAGE 4, PAGE 26-27] are reframed as variance decomposition and shrinkage problems, and the Corsi-versus-Fenwick debate is reframed as a discrimination/stability comparison [PAGE 16].

The multi-week traps are temporal and contextual. First, short windows confound chance with skill: “over 50% of the variation in three point percentage between players in a given season is due to chance” [PAGE 13]. A multi-week sample has even less information than a season, so raw percentages are especially unstable. Second, context drift is not sampling noise: a metric can be unstable because of teammates or because a player’s skill changes through offseason practice or injury [PAGE 7]. Third, injury and work-stoppage normalization matter: the authors normalized by minutes or possessions to reduce anomalous events [PAGE 11]. Fourth, player-type conditioning reverses marginal conclusions: blocks and rebounds are highly discriminative league-wide but less so among centers [PAGE 24]. Fifth, selection and missingness matter: players with fewer seasons have noisier \(V_{pm}[X]\), and metrics with zero attempts create undefined percentages. These are not “multi-week” in a wagering sense; they are multi-week sampling and context traps in longitudinal sports measurement.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when a decision-maker, facing metric clutter, retreats to familiar raw summaries: raw 3P%, plus-minus, or minutes-based aggregates. The source directly undermines that habit: raw three-point percentage is “the least discriminative and stable” of the studied NBA metrics in Figure 2’s discussion [PAGE 14], and “over 50% of the variation in three point percentage between players in a given season is due to chance” [PAGE 13]. In hockey, plus-minus has “low discrimination” because goals are scarce [PAGE 16]. The counter-trap is to replace raw familiarity with a three-question protocol: Does the metric discriminate (\(D\))? Does it persist after removing chance (\(S\))? Does it add information beyond the conditioning set (\(I\))? The recursive version adds a fourth question: Is it relevant for this player subgroup and this decision? The paper explicitly recommends conditional recomputation for player types [PAGE 24] and warns that reliability is not relevance [PAGE 24-25].

The clock is sample size and season length. At the limit of an infinitely long season, sampling noise \(\epsilon_{spm}\) is assumed to vanish [PAGE 5]. In finite data, the bootstrap estimates sampling variance by resampling games [PAGE 10]. So the analyst must reason recursively: at level 0, the coach sees a raw metric; at level 1, the analyst asks whether the metric is stable; at level 2, the analyst asks whether it is independent of other metrics; at level 3, the analyst asks whether the conditioning set and player subgroup change the answer; at level 4, the analyst asks whether the metric is relevant to wins, role, or acquisition. The counter-trap to habit regression is not “trust the model”; it is “match the variance decomposition to the decision.” Attribution tolerates context and chance as signal, while acquisition treats repeatability as central [PAGE 3]. The clock also creates a trap: a metric can be stable but non-discriminative, or discriminative but unstable. The paper’s per-minute versus minutes-based distinction is exactly this: minutes-based metrics are highly discriminative but less stable, while per-minute metrics are less discriminative but more stable [PAGE 12]. Recursive reasoning prevents collapsing those trade-offs into a single ranking.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
External layers can veto a metric’s decision use only by changing either the variance components or the relevance mapping. Formally, let \(L\) denote a physical, biomechanical, or weather layer. If \(L\) changes \(V_{spm}\), \(V_{sm}\), \(V_{pm}\), or \(V_m\), then \(D\), \(S\), or \(I\) must be recomputed. If \(L\) changes only the predictive or causal relation between metric \(m\) and outcome \(Y\), then \(L\) vetoes relevance, not internal reliability. The source supports this separation: “our meta-metrics only measure the internal quality of a metric. The meta-metrics are not designed to provide any information about how relevant the metrics are for the sport of interest” [PAGE 24-25].

Physical/biomechanical veto proof is admissible where the source identifies a physical mechanism. Height drives the correlation between blocks and rebounds: “blocks and rebounds in basketball are highly correlated due to their association with height” [PAGE 9]. Position changes discrimination: blocks and rebounds are less discriminative among NBA centers [PAGE 24]. Free-throw shooting is described as requiring “little athleticism (e.g., does not change with age or health)” [PAGE 12], while injuries “aﬀect total minutes or games played in a season, but generally have less eﬀect on per-game or per-minute metrics” [PAGE 12]. Therefore a biomechanical veto is valid when it explains why a metric’s sampling distribution or context changes—e.g., injury changes minutes-based availability but not necessarily per-minute skill. Weather veto is not supported by the source for NBA or NHL indoor data. No weather variable appears in the metric glossaries [PAGE 32-34], and the source’s variance components are player, season, player-season, and chance [PAGE 5]. A weather claim would therefore be an external relevance hypothesis requiring its own data link; it cannot override \(D\), \(S\), or \(I\) by assertion. The adjudication rule is: biomechanical variables may veto if they alter variance components or relevance; weather may veto only with a verified causal or predictive link to the outcome, otherwise it is out-of-scope noise.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Minutes-ablation veto. Claim: WS and VORP reliability is driven substantially by minutes played. Counterfactual: replace the minutes contribution in WS and VORP with a fixed or per-minute normalization, then recompute \(\hat D\) and \(\hat S\) with the same bootstrap. Prediction: \(D_{\mathrm{WS}}^{\text{ablation}}<D_{\mathrm{WS}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{VORP}}^{\text{ablation}}<D_{\mathrm{VORP}}\), while \(S_{\mathrm{WS}}^{\text{ablation}}>S_{\mathrm{WS}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{VORP}}^{\text{ablation}}>S_{\mathrm{VORP}}\), consistent with the source’s statement that minutes-based metrics are highly discriminative but less stable [PAGE 12]. Veto if the signs do not reverse in the predicted direction beyond bootstrap uncertainty.

2. Three-point shrinkage veto. Claim: empirical Bayes shrinkage improves both discrimination and stability for 3P%. Counterfactual: fit the hierarchical Bernoulli model with career-average prior and recompute \(\hat D\) and \(\hat S\) on the shrunken estimates, using the bootstrap for frequentist variability as the paper suggests [PAGE 19-20]. Prediction: \(D_{\mathrm{3P,EB}}>D_{\mathrm{3P}}\) and \(S_{\mathrm{3P,EB}}>S_{\mathrm{3P}}\), matching the source’s claim that empirical Bayes estimates improve both stability and discrimination [PAGE 14, PAGE 19-20]. Veto the claim if the shrunken estimator fails to improve both, or improves one only by collapsing discrimination.

3. Corsi-versus-Fenwick veto. Claim: adding blocked shots to Fenwick to form Corsi improves reliability. Counterfactual: compute \(D\) and \(S\) for \(CF\%=CF/(CF+CA)\) and \(FF\%=FF/(FF+FA)\) on the same player-seasons, with the same bootstrap and conditioning set. Prediction: \(D_{CF\%}>D_{FF\%}\) and \(S_{CF\%}>S_{FF\%}\), because the source says “Corsi metrics (e.g. “CF% rel” and “CF%”) are both more reliable and stable than the Fenwick metrics” [PAGE 16]. Veto if the block-addition does not improve both, or if the improvement disappears after conditioning on player type.
