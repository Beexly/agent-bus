<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(Y_i \in \{0,1\}\) be the shot outcome for shot \(i\), with \(Y_i=1\) for a goal, and let \(\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) be the pre-shot feature vector. The paper’s definition is [PAGE 1]: “This is defined as the probability of a shot ending up as a goal.” Formally,
\[
p_i=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid \mathbf{x}_i), \qquad p_i\in[0,1],
\]
\[
\mathrm{xG}_{\text{team}}=\sum_{i\in\text{team}} p_i \quad \text{with unit goals}.
\]
The probability \(p_i\) is dimensionless; the summed xG is a count in goals. A thresholded classifier adds
\[
\hat{Y}_i=\mathbf{1}\{p_i\ge \tau\},
\]
where \(\tau\) is a decision threshold, not a probability.

XGBoost is an additive tree model. With \(K\) trees \(f_k\), leaf weights \(w_k\), \(T_k\) leaves, and logistic link \(\sigma\), the score and objective can be written as
\[
\hat{y}_i=\sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad p_i=\sigma(\hat{y}_i)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\hat{y}_i}},
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}=\sum_i \ell(y_i,\hat{y}_i)+
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\gamma T_k+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w_k\|_2^2+\alpha\|w_k\|_1\right),
\]
\[
\ell(y,\hat{y})=-\left[y\log\sigma(\hat{y})+(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(\hat{y}))\right].
\]
Here \(\ell\) has units of nats per shot; \(\gamma,\lambda,\alpha\) are regularization weights; \(T_k\) is a leaf count. The paper’s description on [PAGE 4] matches this family: “Extreme Gradient Boosting is one kind of a gradient boosting algorithm. It is made up of a group of decision trees.” It also says the regularization term lowers variance and “accepts values that are equal to or greater than 0” [PAGE 4]. A later phrase calls the fitted model an “Expert Gradient Boosting Algorithm” [PAGE 5], which is a naming inconsistency, not a new operator.

Geometry enters through distance \(d\) and angle \(\theta\). If the goalposts are separated by width \(W\), the shooter is at \((d,y)\), and the posts are at \((0,\pm W/2)\), then the subtended angle is
\[
\theta(d,y)=\arccos\!\left(
\frac{d^2+\left(\frac{W}{2}-y\right)\left(-\frac{W}{2}-y\right)}
{\sqrt{d^2+\left(\frac{W}{2}-y\right)^2}\sqrt{d^2+\left(-\frac{W}{2}-y\right)^2}}
\right),
\]
with \(\theta\) in radians. If goal height \(H\) is included, the available target is a solid angle \(\Omega\) in steradians. The paper cites distance and angle on [PAGE 3]: “The parameters are the distance from which the shooter shoots the ball, and the angle between the shooting position and the goal posts.” The source does not supply \(W\) or \(H\), so any numerical geometric replication must declare those physical constants explicitly.

Pearson correlation is defined as
\[
r=\frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}
{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i-\bar{y})^2}},
\qquad r\in[-1,1],
\]
and is dimensionless. The paper states on [PAGE 4], “Pearson correlation coefficient is the measure of the strength of a linear connection between two variables.” But its interpretation is wrong: [PAGE 4] says, “where -1 indicates that the features have no correlation whereas +1 indicates that the features are strongly correlated.” Standard interpretation is −1 perfect negative linear relation, 0 no linear relation, +1 perfect positive linear relation. This is a formal error, not a stylistic one.

Classification metrics are
\[
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
F1=\frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},
\]
all dimensionless. PR-AUC is the area under the precision-recall curve and is bounded in \([0,1]\), or reported as a percentage in \([0,100]\). Cohen’s kappa is
\[
\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},
\]
dimensionless, where \(p_o\) is observed agreement and \(p_e\) is chance agreement. On [PAGE 5] the paper reports “71% of precision, 27% of recall and F1 score of 0.39.” On [PAGE 6] it reports “a PR-AUC of 47.35 and a Cohen Kappa of 0.35.” The PR-AUC value violates its bounded range unless it is a percent typo.

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit. As \(d\to 0\), the subtended angle approaches \(\pi\) and the geometry saturates; if \(d=0\) at a post, the arccos argument becomes undefined. As \(d\to\infty\) or \(\theta\to 0\), the goal mouth subtends almost nothing and empirical calibration becomes sparse and unstable. In logistic classification, extreme linear scores drive \(p_i\to 0\) or \(p_i\to 1\), so tails are extrapolations unless regularized and calibrated. Under severe class imbalance, empirical risk is dominated by the no-goal class; recall and PR-AUC can collapse even when accuracy is high. If \(Y_i\) or a post-shot label is included in \(\mathbf{x}_i\), the model enters a leakage regime where \(p_i\) can approach \(Y_i\) artificially and the xG interpretation is void. If train and test distributions differ by season or competition, the calibration map \(p_{\text{train}}\mapsto p_{\text{test}}\) is not invariant. Any reported PR-AUC outside \([0,1]\) is a formal breakdown.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the analyst’s retreat to accuracy as a comfort metric. The paper itself creates the trap on [PAGE 6]: “There are a lot more attempts than attempts that result in goals. Therefore, the accuracy attained will be already 89% if a shot is randomly predicted not to be a goal each and every time.” A model that appears to score “91%” [PAGE 6] is therefore only two points above a trivial baseline and can still be poor at the goal class. The paper admits the asymmetry on [PAGE 5]: “The model performs exceptionally well at no-goal (class 0) prediction but poor at goal prediction (class 1).” The recursive failure is that the modeler believes the stakeholder will see “91%” as validation, while the stakeholder believes the modeler has controlled for imbalance. Neither belief is justified by the stated metrics.

The counter-trap is overcorrecting with PR-AUC and kappa while ignoring calibration. PR-AUC and kappa are better than raw accuracy under imbalance, but they do not by themselves validate xG. A model can have a useful ranking score and still emit probabilities that are systematically too high or too low. The paper reports “a PR-AUC of 47.35 and a Cohen Kappa of 0.35” [PAGE 6], but 47.35 is outside the bounded PR-AUC scale, so the counter-trap is to trust a number that is formally impossible without checking whether it is a percent typo. The second counter-trap is to treat feature importance as causal. Selecting “is_goal” and “shot_outcome” [PAGE 5] can make a model look skillful while learning the label.

The clock is both temporal and in-match. The dataset spans “the 2011–2012 season to the 2016–2017 season” [PAGE 3], so a random split ignores season drift. The paper also gives contradictory split clocks: “A ratio of 80:20” [PAGE 3] versus “Two- thirds of the data set can be used for training and one-third for testing” [PAGE 4]. In-match, xG is not static: game state, minute, scoreline, and substitutions change shot selection. The recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore requires three clocks: a training clock that forbids future leakage, a calibration clock that is checked on held-out seasons, and an in-match clock that reports xG by game state. Without those clocks, the modeler and stakeholder can recursively reassure each other with the wrong number.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical veto proof begins with omitted variables. Let \(\mathbf{x}_i\) be observed event features, and let \(\mathbf{z}_i\) include ball velocity, spin, foot contact point, defender pressure, goalkeeper position, pitch surface, wind, rain, and player biomechanics. The true shot probability is
\[
p_i^*=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid \mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{z}_i).
\]
The learned model estimates
\[
q_i=\int \Pr(Y_i=1\mid \mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{z})\,dF(\mathbf{z}\mid \mathbf{x}_i).
\]
If \(F(\mathbf{z}\mid \mathbf{x}_i)\) varies by player, team, weather, or match state, then \(q_i\neq p_i^*\). The residual \(Y_i-q_i\) is then not a clean measure of finishing skill. This is the formal veto against causal player-ability claims from the paper’s xG residuals.

The geometric layer already constrains the model. The angle \(\theta(d,y)\) is a nonlinear function of distance and lateral offset, and its derivatives diverge near the posts and near \(d\to 0\). A tree ensemble can approximate that surface piecewise, but it cannot extrapolate reliably outside the training hull. If the model is trained mostly on central shots, it has weak physical support for tight-angle shots. The paper’s own related work admits the missing layers: [PAGE 2] says Link’s method has the drawback of “not accounting for elements such as player and ball movement dynamics, player gaze patterns, player positioning in respect to the ball, and the availability of teammates.” The present paper does not add those variables; it adds a ball-position feature and then selects outcome labels [PAGE 5].

Biomechanical veto: foot speed, spin, and contact quality determine ball trajectory and goalkeeper reaction time. If these are absent, a shot from the same distance and angle can have very different scoring probability. Weather veto: wind and rain alter aerodynamic drag and surface grip; a physically correct model would need a force term or at least a weather stratum. Without those, any claim that the xG residual isolates “finishing” is confounded. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: geometry and distance/angle are admissible as pre-shot covariates; biomechanics, goalkeeper positioning, defensive pressure, and weather are missing layers that veto strong causal interpretation; outcome labels “is_goal” and “shot_outcome” are not admissible covariates at all.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
