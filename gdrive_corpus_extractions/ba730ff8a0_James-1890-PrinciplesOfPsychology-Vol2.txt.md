The source’s central multi-hop move is to deny that “sensation” and “perception” are two separable mental substances. James says at the outset that they “do not carry very definitely discriminated meanings in popular speech” and that they name “different cognitive functions, not for different sorts of mental fact” [PAGE 1]. The analytic distinction is functional: the nearer the object is to a simple quality, the more it is sensation; the more it is classed, located, measured, related, the more it is perception [PAGE 1]. This blocks the atomistic inference that a perception is a cluster of little sensation-atoms. James reinforces the point by calling “a pure sensation ... an abstraction” [PAGE 3] and by insisting that “Pure sensations can only be realized in the earliest days of life” [PAGE 7]. Thus any adult report of a “pure sensation” is already contaminated by memory, association, and apperceptive habit.

The second hop is that perception is acquired and inferential only in a loose, non-syllogistic sense. James quotes Mill’s associationist account and then defines perception as consciousness of “farther facts associated with the object of the sensation” [PAGE 77]. His own formula is blunt: “Every perception is an acquired perception” [PAGE 78]. But he refuses the conclusion that perception is literally a hidden syllogism with an unconscious middle term; instead, perception and reasoning are “co-ordinate varieties” of association and habitual brain paths. That is why “all brain-processes are such as give rise to what we may call FIGURED consciousness” [PAGE 82]: ambiguous sensory input does not produce a blur of fragments but switches between whole object-hypotheses.

The third hop is the contrast debate. Helmholtz treats simultaneous contrast as a “DECEPTION OF JUDGMENT” [PAGE 17]; Hering treats it as a physiological interaction in the retina and nervous apparatus [PAGE 19]. James sides with Hering because after-images show that one retinal region modifies another even when the judgment-based “covering” story is impossible [PAGE 20]. This matters for the whole psychology: if contrast could be explained by judgment alone, the source could preserve immutable sensation-atoms plus a relating intellect. Hering’s after-image evidence vetoes that picture. The same anti-atomistic logic appears in James’s rejection of “eccentric projection”: he says there is “not a vestige of evidence” for the view that sensations are first subjective and then “projected” outward [PAGE 31], and concludes “there is no truth in the ‘eccentric projection’ theory” [PAGE 42]. Instead, “Space means but the aggregate of all our possible sensations” [PAGE 35], and in experience “we are constantly selecting certain of our sensations as realities and degrading others to the status of signs of these” [PAGE 41].

The fourth hop is the imagination-hallucination continuum. Sensations leave modified nervous organism, so copies can arise after the stimulus is gone [PAGE 44]. But an imagined object is still “one idea” at a moment, not a gang of atomic copies [PAGE 45]. James admits that the imagination-process “CAN then pass over into the sensation-process,” so “genuine sensations can be centrally originated” [PAGE 72]. Hallucinations are therefore not simply mental images mistaken for reality; they are “strictly sensational form of consciousness” and often “extremes of the perception process” [PAGE 115]. Pseudo-hallucinations are the gray zone: vivid, spontaneous, but still marked as mental rather than bodily [PAGE 116]. Binet’s “point de repère” explains many hypnotic and insane hallucinations: a peripheral dot or irritation gives objectivity, while the cortex supplies the form [PAGE 128]. James accepts this for many cases but denies it covers all, because centrally initiated hallucinations and multi-sense hallucinations remain possible [PAGE 129].

The gray-zone audit is therefore: do not treat “sensation,” “image,” “illusion,” “pseudo-hallucination,” and “hallucination” as separable substances with fixed borders. They are functional and intensity-graded outcomes of the same perception machinery. The danger is the “psychologist’s fallacy”: reading adult introspective distinctions back into the infant’s or patient’s mind. James repeatedly warns against this: the first sensation is “the Universe” to the infant, not a little subjective patch [PAGE 8]. Hence the correct audit question is not “which atom is present?” but “which object-hypothesis has the brain’s habitual paths made probable, and under what physical conditions?”

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source is prose psychology, not a mathematical treatise, so the following are formal reconstructions of its stated operators. They are not presented as James’s own equations. Units and breakdown regimes are given so that each operator is testable rather than metaphorical.

1. Percept as posterior object-hypothesis. Let \(S=s\) be the peripheral sensory signal in its physical modality units, e.g. luminance in \(\mathrm{cd\,m^{-2}}\), pressure in \(\mathrm{Pa}\), acoustic pressure in \(\mathrm{Pa}\). Let \(\Theta=\theta\) be the object-hypothesis. A minimal formalization of James’s claim that “whilst part of what we perceive comes through our senses from the object before us, another part (and it may be the larger part) always comes ... out of our own head” [PAGE 103] is
\[
P(\Theta=\theta\mid S=s)=\frac{P(S=s\mid\Theta=\theta)\,P(\Theta=\theta)}{P(S=s)}.
\]
Here probabilities are dimensionless; the likelihood \(P(S=s\mid\theta)\) is a density with inverse units of \(s\). Assumption: the likelihood encodes the incoming sensory evidence and the prior encodes learned association/apperception. Breakdown: if \(P(S=s)=0\), no percept is defined; if the prior is degenerate at one \(\theta_0\), the percept is prior-locked even when the likelihood is weak; if the likelihood is flat over \(\theta\), the percept is determined by prior alone. That last regime is the formal analogue of hallucination-like or illusion-like filling, consistent with James’s “FIGURED consciousness” [PAGE 82] and with his claim that hallucinations are often “extremes of the perception process” [PAGE 115].

2. Retinal adaptation and contrast. Let \(E_i(t)\) be illumination at retinal region \(i\) in \(\mathrm{cd\,m^{-2}}\), \(A_i(t)\) an adaptation state in the same units, and \(R_i(t)\) a response in spikes \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Hering’s claim that “the excitation of any portion of the retina ... depends not only on its own illumination, but on that of the rest of the retina as well” [PAGE 19] can be written
\[
\tau_A \frac{dA_i}{dt}=-A_i+\alpha E_i,
\]
\[
R_i=\phi\!\left(E_i-\beta A_i+\gamma\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}(E_j-E_i)\right),
\]
with \(\tau_A\) in \(\mathrm{s}\), \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) dimensionless, and \(\phi\) a saturating response nonlinearity. Assumption: local adaptation plus lateral interaction. Breakdown: as \(\tau_A\to0\), adaptation becomes instantaneous and no stable contrast memory remains; as \(\phi\) saturates, contrast differences are compressed and disappear; if the neighborhood term dominates, “simultaneous induction” replaces contrast, exactly the failure mode James describes when fixation is continued [PAGE 15]. Singularity: \(\tau_A=0\) is a formal breakdown of the differential model, not a physical claim.

3. Local-sign spatial relation. Let \(p,q\) be points on a sensitive surface \(\mathcal M\). James’s claim that “in the field of space the relations are facts of the same order with the facts they relate” [PAGE 149] can be represented as
\[
\mathcal R(p,q)=\operatorname{Line}(p,q),
\]
where \(\operatorname{Line}(p,q)\) is the felt line or extent joining the two local signs, measured in angular degrees on the retina or millimetres on skin. Assumption: localization is not a pure act of relating thought added to spaceless sensations; it is a further sensory line, angle, or transition [PAGE 149]. Breakdown: if the local signs of a region lack qualitative contrast, the line is weakly discriminated; James says on the back “consciousness can sunder two spots, whilst only vaguely apprehending their distance and direction apart” [PAGE 160]. Singularity: \(p=q\) gives a zero-length relation; and if no discriminative attention separates a part from the total vastness, the line collapses into undivided extensity [PAGE 145].

4. Hallucination threshold. Let \(I_c(t)\) be cortical excitation in a centre, in spikes \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\); let \(S(t)\) be source drive in spikes \(\mathrm{s^{-2}}\); let \(\lambda\) be leakage through association paths in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\); and let \(I_{\mathrm{crit}}\) be the explosion threshold in spikes \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). A discrete formalization of James’s statement that when “the normal forward irradiation of intra-cortical excitement through association-paths is checked,” an accidental activity may set up “a process of full sensational intensity” [PAGE 128] is
\[
I_c(t+\Delta t)=I_c(t)+\bigl[S(t)-\lambda I_c(t)\bigr]\Delta t,
\]
\[
\text{hallucination if } I_c(t)\ge I_{\mathrm{crit}}.
\]
Assumption: reduced leakage or increased drive can push a centre past the sensational threshold. Breakdown: if \(\lambda\to0\) and \(S>0\), \(I_c\) grows without bound in the linear model, so a nonlinear ceiling must be added; if \(S=0\), no hallucination occurs in this model; if a peripheral “point de repère” is absent, the result may remain only a conception, as Binet’s hypnotic subjects illustrate [PAGE 128]. The equation is thus a threshold model, not a claim that all hallucinations are peripheral.

5. Motion sensitivity exceeds position sensitivity. Let \(\delta_{\mathrm{motion}}\) and \(\delta_{\mathrm{position}}\) be threshold extents in mm or degrees. James states that “our sense of movement, being so much more delicate than our sense of position, cannot possibly be derived from it” [PAGE 172]. Formally,
\[
\delta_{\mathrm{motion}}<\delta_{\mathrm{position}}.
\]
Assumption: movement is a primitive quality of sensibility. Breakdown: at very slow motion, or when position separations are large, position cues can dominate; Exner’s temporal-order threshold of about \(0.044\,\mathrm{s}\) for sparks versus about \(0.015\,\mathrm{s}\) for apparent motion is the kind of quantitative boundary James cites [PAGE 172]. The inequality is ordinal and unit-bearing only when a specific task fixes the threshold definition.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Locke’s simple ideas and Berkeley’s visual signs through Reid’s natural signs, Mill’s associationism, Helmholtz’s unconscious inference, Hering’s physiological contrast, Lotze’s local signs, Wundt’s apperception, and Fechner-Galton individual differences. James’s retrocausal correction is to keep the empirical phenomena but reject the atomistic metaphysics that often travelled with them. Locke’s “simple ideas” become sensations that are “boundaries of our thoughts” [PAGE 6], yet James denies that adult perception is built by adding fixed sensation-particles. Berkeley’s signs become James’s “signs of realities” [PAGE 41], but without a duplicate spaceless sensation that must be “projected.” Helmholtz’s judgment theory is respected but vetoed by Hering’s after-image experiments [PAGE 20]. Lotze’s local signs are retained, but only as habitual tags whose relations are themselves sensory lines [PAGE 149]. Wundt’s apperception is renamed “assimilation” and treated as association, not a separate spiritual faculty [PAGE 107].

The multi-week traps are longitudinal. First, prism adaptation: James notes that the illusory sense of position “lasts until a new association is fixed, when on removing the prisms a contrary illusion at first occurs” [PAGE 88]. A one-session experiment mistakes the initial error for a fixed law; a multi-week design reveals recalibration and after-effect. Second, proof-reader and expectancy effects: the mind supplies “more than half of the words” from habit, so repeated proofreading over weeks trains a prior that can hide typographic errors [PAGE 96]. Third, Fechner and Galton show enormous personal diversity in imagery [PAGE 50]; a single observer’s introspection is a trap. Fourth, hallucination census work requires long time windows because sporadic hallucinations may occur once in a lifetime [PAGE 117]. The retrocausal lesson is that later experimental controls—after-images, prisms, hypnosis, census methods—retroactively expose earlier “pure sensation” claims as abstractions.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis, the mind regresses to habitual apperception: the prior eats the likelihood. James’s sportsman sees a woodcock because the bird’s size and color are enough to trigger the whole expected object [PAGE 95-96]; the lover “perambulating the streets with his preoccupied fancy” perceives his idol’s bonnet [PAGE 96]. The recursive model is: observer A models observer B’s prior; under stress, B’s prior becomes rigid and B’s report is less diagnostic of the stimulus than of B’s habitual object-set. The counter-trap is not to argue B out of the percept by pure instruction, but to change the physical point de repère: move the dot, magnify it, double it with a prism, or remove it, as Binet’s hypnotic experiments do [PAGE 128]. If the percept tracks the physical transformation, the mechanism is still tied to sensory evidence; if it does not, central initiation is more likely.

The clock matters because perception-time is not instantaneous. James refers perception-time, choice-time, and discrimination-time to the earlier chapter [PAGE 131], and he emphasizes that movement sensation is more delicate than position and can beat temporal-order discrimination [PAGE 172]. In a crisis, fast habitual paths fire before slower reflective correction; the observer’s “clock” is the latency of association. The recursive ToM should therefore predict: under time pressure, reports gravitate toward the most probable object, and only delayed inspection recovers the ambiguous sensory evidence. This is not mysticism; it is the temporal signature of “the more diffused cerebral excitement of the perceptive state” versus the more concentrated sensational state [PAGE 113].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: if contrast were only judgment, Hering’s after-image should not differ when the ground’s brightness difference disappears. James reports Hering’s finding that “the difference in brightness of the strips in the after-image is in general much greater than it appeared in direct vision” and that a phase occurs “in which the difference in brightness of the two halves of the background entirely disappears, and yet both after-images of the strips are still very clear” [PAGE 20]. That is a physical/retinal veto against a purely judgmental explanation.

Biomechanical veto: accommodation and eye muscles must be included. James describes micropsy under atropin: the accommodation strain suggests nearness, but the retinal image is not enlarged proportionally, so the object seems too small [PAGE 93-94]. Prism and eyeball-pressure illusions likewise depend on involuntary eye positions [PAGE 88]. Semicircular-canal stimulation explains giddiness and objective whirling [PAGE 89-90]. A model that ignores these biomechanical variables is vetoed because it cannot predict the direction and time course of the illusion.

Weather/atmospheric veto: the moon illusion is not a pure intellect act. James says the horizon moon is “seen throngh vaporous air, and looks dimmer and duskier than when it rides on high” [PAGE 92], and is seen over “fields, trees, hedges, streams” which “make us the better realize the latter’s extent” [PAGE 92-93]. Both causes make it seem more distant, and because its visual angle is not smaller, “we deem that it must be a larger body, and we so perceive it” [PAGE 93]. A clear-air versus hazy-air manipulation is therefore a cross-layer veto: if perceived size is unchanged when atmospheric cues and terrain cues are controlled, the association/probability account fails. The adjudication is physical and ecological before it is “mental.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
