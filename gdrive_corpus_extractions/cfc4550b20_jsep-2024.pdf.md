<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three regimes must be modeled recursively: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. In crisis habit regression, an athlete under pressure reverts to overlearned perceptual-motor routines. The source’s Quiet Eye construct is exactly such a routine: “the final fixation at a task-relevant location prior to the initiation of a motor action” [PAGE 5]. If that habit is competition-valid, pressure may stabilize performance; if the trained habit was learned in a low-ecological-validity task, pressure exposes the mismatch. The soccer result is the warning: multiple object tracking and Quiet Eye programs produced “no increases ... in studies of dribbling and shooting accuracy” [PAGE 7], “possibly due to the lack of ecological validity of the training tasks for the transfer skills” [PAGE 7]. Crisis regression is therefore not automatically adaptive; it is adaptive only when the trained response is the competition response.

Counter-trap concerns recursive expectations among athlete, coach, and researcher. A coach expects improvement, the athlete perceives that expectation, and the researcher may inadvertently reinforce it. Placebo control is the counter-trap. Yet only 12 training studies used placebo conditions, and only one used preregistration with placebo [PAGE 7]. Without that control, a gain can be a belief effect, a retest effect, or a genuine training effect. The recursive model predicts that active and placebo arms will converge when the outcome is subjective or near-transfer, and will diverge only when the trained function is tightly coupled to the competition task.

Clock is the time-pressure and persistence variable. The source reports that most programs lasted 4–8 weeks, with one 6-month program [PAGE 6], and that persistence was tested minutes, days, or months later in some studies [PAGE 7]. A short clock favors near transfer and novelty; a long clock tests consolidation and decay. The recursive prediction is conditional: if the athlete’s crisis habit matches the competition clock, short-term gains may appear in practice but not in competition; if the counter-trap is uncontrolled, the apparent gain will not survive placebo; if the clock is extended, the effect will shrink unless the trained skill is ecological and reinforced. The source supports this three-way caution because it finds mixed efficacy, weak far transfer, and a training literature that “did not include follow-up assessments to determine the persistence of training effects” in many cases [PAGE 7].
