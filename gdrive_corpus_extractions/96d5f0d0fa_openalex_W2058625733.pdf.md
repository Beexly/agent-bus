<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is visible in the reference chain. HOG comes from Dalal and Triggs [19] and is imported here to compensate for small, color-bleeding player regions [PAGE 3]. The Bhattacharyya coefficient in Eq. (5) is an older probability-distribution similarity measure repurposed as a HOG histogram distance [PAGE 3]. Method 1 is a standard particle filter benchmark [PAGE 4 and PAGE 6], reflecting the CONDENSATION lineage [22]. Simulated annealing comes from Kirkpatrick, Gelatt, and Vecchi [21] and is used as a nonconvex optimizer over the composite landscape [PAGE 4]. The elastic spring model is a physics metaphor: players are connected by springs, with same-group players getting stronger coupling and distant different-group players getting weaker coupling [PAGE 3]. The field-line wire-frame preprocessing comes from Watanabe, Haseyama, and Kitajima [17], and the tactical grouping comes from Kon and Haseyama’s player clustering [20]. The paper is an extension of Iwai et al. [16] [PAGE 2]. In retrocausal reading, the later ablation table re-values the earlier components: Method 3 beats Method 2, Method 4 beats Method 3, Method 5 beats Method 3, and the proposed method beats Method 6 [PAGE 6]. Those comparisons retroactively make hue-only tracking look insufficient, HOG look necessary, and formation-plus-direction priors look like the load-bearing additions. The paper’s own sentence “Thus, the effectiveness of introducing the HOG descriptor into potential energy in the previous method [16] (method 6) was verified” [PAGE 6] is exactly a retrocausal credit assignment.

The multi-week traps are also visible. First, parameter tuning is not autonomous: “We experimentally determined the best parameters by using a clip that was not included in the target clips” [PAGE 4], and “Introduction of an automatic parameter setting is our future work” [PAGE 4]. A multi-week project can overfit \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\eta,F,M\) to one validation clip and then mistake stability on 18 clips for generality. Second, manual correction is a hidden supervision channel: “If some target players were not tracked in a frame, we manually gave their correct positions” [PAGE 4], and corrections are counted in Table 5 [PAGE 6]. A team can spend weeks improving autonomous tracking while the reported curve is partly driven by human repair. Third, the evaluation clock is coarse: “we checked tracking results every 30 frames” [PAGE 4]. A transient identity swap that lasts fewer than 30 frames can be missed, while a persistent swap may be manually reset. Fourth, formation is a tactical prior, not a law: it is valid for organized possession but weak during corners, set pieces, substitutions, and chaotic transitions; the paper concedes “it becomes difficult to accurately track players when the proposed method is applied to highly complex scenes such as corner kicks” [PAGE 7]. Fifth, smooth direction over \(F=30\) frames is a temporal prior that fails at abrupt cuts, deflections, collisions, and camera motion. Sixth, the fixed uniform hue model assumes FIFA-style color separation [PAGE 3], but lighting, shadows, kit clashes, and signboards with similar colors are uncontrolled. Seventh, the unit ambiguity in \(E_s\) can survive many weeks because the code still optimizes; the wrong unit convention only shows up when comparing weights across camera calibrations. Eighth, real-time deployment is vetoed by 0.56 s per frame [PAGE 7] against a 30 fps stream. These are not minor implementation details; they are the research-program traps.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
I use “theory of mind” in the intentional-stance sense, not as a claim that the tracker has mental states. The tracker carries a model of each player: a visual possibility field from hue and HOG, an elastic model of team formation, and a movement-direction prior. The team carries a tactical model: players “play in such a way as to maintain a formation” [PAGE 1], and nearby players are treated as more tactically related than distant players [PAGE 3]. The human evaluator carries a model of the tracker: check every 30 frames, correct missed targets, and feed corrected positions back into subsequent tracking [PAGE 4]. That is a recursive loop: the tracker predicts, the human corrects, the correction becomes the next initial condition, and the next prediction is conditioned on the human’s model of what went wrong.

“Crisis habit regression” is the key failure mode. When visual evidence is weak, because “each player region becomes small, and video coding causes color bleeding” [PAGE 1], the optimizer regresses to its habits: the elastic formation term and the movement-smoothness term dominate. That is usually helpful, but in a crisis it can be harmful. If two same-team players cross, the potential term may be nearly symmetric, the elastic term prefers preserving prior pairwise distances, and the direction term prefers smooth trajectories; the composite can preserve the wrong identity with high confidence. The paper’s own contrast is that conventional methods “mix up them” when a target is close to another same-team player [PAGE 1], and the proposed method mitigates this, but the corner-kick limitation [PAGE 7] shows the regression is not eliminated.

“Counter-trap” is the adversarial-visual case. A signboard with colors similar to the target uniform is a trap for the potential term; the paper says performance improves in “scenes where the target players are close to signboards with colors similar to the uniform colors of the target team” after field-area restriction [PAGE 7]. The field-line mask is the counter-trap: it vetoes off-field color attractors. A second counter-trap is HOG: hue alone can be fooled by a similarly colored non-player region, but shape similarity suppresses some false positives [PAGE 3]. A third is the movement prior: an occluded player’s sudden direction jump is penalized, so the tracker prefers continuity [PAGE 3]. None of these is a semantic detector; they are soft or hard constraints on the cost surface.

“Clock” is the temporal architecture. The current frame index is \(t\), the movement prior uses the past \(F=30\) frames [PAGE 4], the evaluation clock checks every 30 frames [PAGE 4], and the search area is a per-frame \(4\times4\) pixel gate [PAGE 4]. The clock creates a mismatch: the optimizer updates every frame, but the human audits every thirtieth frame. A swap that begins and ends inside the audit interval is invisible to the reported Accuracy, even though it matters for tactical analysis. The clock also defines when the smoothness prior is valid: over 30 frames, a player’s direction may change; over a single frame, it usually should not. The recursive trap is that a correction at frame \(t\) resets the history used by \(\bar v_i^{t-1}\), so the human’s intervention changes the tracker’s future “habit” as well as its position. That is why manual correction counts must be reported alongside Accuracy; the paper does report them in Table 5 [PAGE 6], which is a strength, but it also means the system is a human-in-the-loop tracker rather than a fully autonomous one.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

\[

\]

The paper justifies the small gate by saying “a distance that a player can cover between two successive frames is limited” [PAGE 4], but that is an assumption about frame rate, camera scale, and player speed. At 30 fps and far-view scale it may hold; at a cut, a fast transition, or a mis-calibrated world-to-screen mapping it can fail. The second hard veto is the field-line mask: “we restrict the area for player tracking to the area within the field lines on the wire frame model” [PAGE 2]. If a player is outside the modeled field lines, or if the wire frame is wrong, the mask excludes truth. The paper handles camera-parameter error by manual modification: “we manually modify incorrect camera parameters estimated by the method [17] beforehand” [PAGE 2]. That is an explicit admission that the physical layer can veto the tracker unless a human repairs it.

The biomechanical layer is the shape-and-motion prior. HOG is meant to represent player shape [PAGE 3], and the movement term inhibits “a sudden change in the movement direction” [PAGE 3]. The biomechanical veto occurs when occlusion or same-team overlap destroys the shape signature and the motion prior is the only discriminator. In a same-team crossing, two players share hue, similar HOG, and similar plausible motion; the elastic term \(K=1\) within the group can make the formation prior strong. If the prior’s pairwise-distance preservation is wrong for that instant, the tracker can maintain a wrong identity. The paper’s claim that the proposed method is robust “even when a target player is close to another player of the same team or occluded” [PAGE 4] is therefore a soft claim, bounded by the corner-kick failure [PAGE 7]. The biomechanical layer also vetoes through the \(4\times4\) gate: a sprinting player can exceed the gate before the shape term has any chance to act.

The weather and illumination layer is not experimentally covered in the paper. The hue model uses fixed target uniform hues \(H_0^n\) [PAGE 3]. Under rain, snow, shadow, floodlight color shift, or strong glare, the observed hue \(H(y^t)\) is an illumination-transformed version of the uniform hue. If that transform moves the uniform hue toward the field hue or toward a signboard hue, \(D_{\rm color}\) becomes ambiguous. HOG may survive better than hue because it uses gradient orientation, but in far-view the player rectangle is only \(12\times24\) pixels [PAGE 4], so gradient estimates are noisy. The paper does not report weather tests, night matches, or illumination sweeps. A formal weather veto is: if the illumination-induced hue displacement is comparable to the between-team hue separation, the color term cannot identify the target team; any successful track is then driven by shape, formation, and motion, not by uniform color. The correct adjudication order is hard physical constraint first, then biomechanical identifiability, then illumination and color calibration, then soft priors. The paper implements the field mask and search gate as hard constraints, but the elastic and movement terms are soft; they can be outvoted by a strong but wrong visual attractor, and they can also outvote weak visual evidence. That is the cross-layer trade-off. The paper’s own evidence supports a limited adjudication: HOG improves over hue-only [PAGE 6], elastic improves over potential-only [PAGE 6], movement improves over potential-only [PAGE 6], and field restriction improves over the previous method [PAGE 7]. It does not prove robustness under weather, camera cuts, or corner kicks; those remain veto layers.

I propose three counterfactual tests that are verifiable by rerunning the same 18 clips, same \(720\times480\) frames, same 30 fps, same parameters \(\alpha=0.8\), \(\beta=0.2\), \(\gamma=0.3\), \(\eta=70\), \(F=30\), \(M=360\), and same check-every-30-frames protocol [PAGE 4]. Each test has a veto condition: if the prediction fails, the corresponding causal claim is vetoed or must be narrowed.

Scenario 1: field-mask ablation on signboard-similar scenes. Disable the field-line restriction while keeping hue, HOG, elastic, and movement terms unchanged. Prediction: on clips containing signboards with colors similar to the target uniform, Accuracy in Eq. (15) will fall below the full proposed method’s reported values, and the correction counts in Table 5 will exceed the proposed method’s 16 frames and 43 players [PAGE 6]. The paper states that improvement is “confirmed” in “scenes where the target players are close to signboards with colors similar to the uniform colors of the target team” [PAGE 7]. If disabling the mask produces no measurable drop on those scenes, the field-mask claim is vetoed. If it produces a drop only on one clip and not on the others, the claim is narrowed to that clip family.

Scenario 2: elastic-energy ablation by setting \(\alpha=0\). Keep the potential and movement terms and the field mask. Prediction: aggregate Accuracy will fall below the full proposed 0.969 [PAGE 6], and correction counts will exceed 16 frames and 43 players. The direct evidence is that Method 4, which uses potential plus elastic energy, beats Method 3, which uses potential alone, with aggregate Accuracy 0.851 versus 0.785 [PAGE 6]. The paper’s mechanism is that “method 4 can track players maintaining a formation by using elastic energy” when visual information is insufficient [PAGE 6]. If \(\alpha=0\) in the full proposed pipeline leaves Accuracy at 0.969 and corrections at 16/43, the elastic-energy causal claim is vetoed for this dataset. If it lowers Accuracy but not in same-team-proximity scenes, the claim is narrowed to a different scene family.

Scenario 3: movement-direction ablation by setting \(\beta=0\) or by reducing \(F\) from 30 to 1. Prediction: aggregate Accuracy will fall below 0.969, correction counts will exceed 16 frames and 43 players, and the degradation will concentrate in occlusion and same-team-proximity scenes. The direct evidence is that Method 5, potential plus movement, beats Method 3, potential alone, 0.805 versus 0.785 [PAGE 6], and the paper says Method 5 is better “in scenes where a target player is close to another player of the same team or occluded” [PAGE 6]. Reducing \(F\) to 1 makes \(\bar v_i^{t-1}\) a single-frame direction, increasing sensitivity to detection noise; it also changes the denominator singularity risk in Eq. (13). If \(\beta=0\) or \(F=1\) does not reduce Accuracy below 0.969 or increase corrections above 16/43, the movement-energy claim is vetoed. If it reduces Accuracy uniformly across all clips rather than in occlusion scenes, the mechanism claim is narrowed from “occlusion robustness” to a generic temporal smoothness effect.
