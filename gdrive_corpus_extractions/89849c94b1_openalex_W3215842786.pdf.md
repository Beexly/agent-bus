The multi-hop derivation is: (1) the observation sheet represents the volleyball court and divides it into a fixed grid; (2) each observed forward reception-to-setter connection is recorded as a dot pair and a line; (3) the collection of dots and lines is collapsed into a team-specific spatial distribution over cells and a directed transition kernel; (4) “spatial flexibility” is then formalized as entropy, effective area, convex-hull area, dispersion, and net-proximity statistics; (5) team comparisons follow from those statistics, not from any inferential test. The source states that “The pitch was divided into 18 equal areas (3 m × 3 m squares)” [PAGE 4], and that “only passes where the ball was moving towards the net were recorded on the sheet (no passes where the ball was directed backwards, towards the end line or outside the recording area)” [PAGE 5]. Those two statements are decisive: the measured object is a censored, forward-only, floor-position map, not a full three-dimensional reception model.

The gray-zone audit must separate what the paper demonstrates from what it interprets. It demonstrates that four named teams produced distinguishable spatial patterns in the observed matches. It does not demonstrate statistical separation, causal effects of “flexibility” on winning, or universal tactical laws. The sample is “Each of the teams was observed during the three matches” [PAGE 4], so the effective sample is twelve team-matches with repeated opponents and no reported randomization. The paper itself frames the work as “a qualitative study of unique cases” [PAGE 3] and uses “ideal mapping models” that “allow for some kind of re-creation and identification of the actions in the game taken by the observed players – representing the highest possible sports level (championship level)” [PAGE 2]. That is a descriptive, championship-level mapping claim, not a predictive model with out-of-sample validation.

The central interpretive claim is that “all selected teams try to receive the ball (serve) as close as possible to the net” [PAGE 11], and that “The main goal of all teams is to direct the ball into a space as close as possible to the net” [PAGE 13]. The gray zone is that “as close as possible” is not operationally defined by a threshold in the paper. The paper repeatedly uses approximately “1.5 m from the net” as a secondary band, and identifies a “perfect reception zone” or “zone 0” between zones II and III [PAGE 14], but it does not give a formal decision rule for red, green, and blue areas. Therefore, any quantitative flexibility index must be treated as an audit construct, not as a recovered source parameter.

A second gray zone is opponent quality. The paper says that “when the opponent effectively prevents by efficiently performing the service, then the space of cooperation in receiving-passing the ball decreases” [PAGE 13]. This means the observed support is jointly produced by team preference, opponent serve difficulty, camera framing, and the forward-pass filter. If one team faces stronger serving, its map can look narrower without being less tactically flexible. The source notes that Brazil’s area was larger in matches with a positive result and smaller in the loss, but that is a within-team contrast over three matches, not a controlled comparison. Similarly, the source’s statement that “The model of receiving-passing the ball of the Brazilian national team is characterized by the widest area of cooperation in this phase of the game” [PAGE 11] is a descriptive ranking of the observed maps, not a significance claim.

A third gray zone is reproducibility. The sheet is an “original observation sheet” [PAGE 3], and the paper says each selected game was observed twice, but it reports no inter-coder agreement, no coordinate error, and no adjudication rule for disputed dots. The single camera was placed “centrally behind one of the end lines” [PAGE 5], which introduces perspective compression near the net and possible occlusion. Thus the correct audit posture is: accept the qualitative pattern as a source-generated hypothesis; do not accept cell-level probabilities or exact zone boundaries as measurement-grade without an independent reliability study. This is why the formal equations below are written as measurement operators with explicit breakdown regimes rather than as claims that the paper already computed them.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are: an original court-grid observation sheet; dot placement for reception and second-contact floor positions; solid-line linking of those dots; visual red/green/blue area designation; and qualitative comparison across four teams. I formalize these operators as follows. These are audit equations, not equations reported by the paper.

**Coordinate and grid operator.** Let one side of the court be \(C=[0,9]\,\mathrm{m}\times[0,9]\,\mathrm{m}\), with the net at \(y=0\) and the 3 m attack line at \(y=3\,\mathrm{m}\). The source states that “The line in the middle of the figure (black in bold) is the center line of the court, dividing it into two halves. The bold black lines to the right and left of the center line are the 3 m lines” [PAGE 3]. The grid cell area is
\[
A_c = (3\,\mathrm{m})(3\,\mathrm{m}) = 9\,\mathrm{m}^2 .
\]
The full court has 18 such cells, consistent with “18 equal areas (3 m × 3 m squares)” [PAGE 4]; one team’s half-court has 9 cells. Units: \(A_c\) in \(\mathrm{m}^2\); cell coordinates in \(\mathrm{m}\).

**Empirical reception-target distribution.** For \(N\) observed forward reception-to-setter events, let \(r_j=(x_j,y_j)\in C\) be the recorded target position and let \(C_k\) be the \(k\)-th cell. Define
\[
n_k=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{r_j\in C_k\},\qquad
p_k=\frac{n_k}{N},\qquad \sum_k p_k=1 .
\]
Here \(n_k\) is a count (dimensionless integer) and \(p_k\) is a probability (dimensionless). Assumption: each retained forward pass is exchangeable within a team-match. Breakdown: if \(N=0\), \(p_k\) is undefined; if \(N\) is very small, \(p_k\) is a sparse frequency, not a stable probability. The source’s three matches per team [PAGE 4] make this a serious limitation for cell-level probabilities.

**Reception-to-setter transition kernel.** Let \(m_{kl}\) be the number of events with reception in cell \(k\) and second contact in cell \(l\). Define
\[
T_{kl}=\frac{m_{kl}}{\sum_l m_{kl}},\qquad \sum_l T_{kl}=1 .
\]
\(T_{kl}\) is dimensionless. It represents the empirical directed-edge weight of the mapping model. Breakdown: rows with zero denominator are undefined; the source’s figures show lines but do not report \(m_{kl}\), so this kernel cannot be reconstructed from the paper alone.

**Spatial-flexibility statistics.** For the target distribution \(p_k\), Shannon entropy is
\[
H=-\sum_{k:p_k>0}p_k\ln p_k \quad \mathrm{nats},
\]
or
\[
H_2=-\sum_{k:p_k>0}p_k\log_2 p_k \quad \mathrm{bits}.
\]
The effective number of occupied cells is
\[
N_{\mathrm{eff}}=e^{H},
\]
and the effective area is
\[
A_{\mathrm{eff}}=A_c\,e^{H}=9\,e^{H}\ \mathrm{m}^2 .
\]
The occupied area is
\[
A_{\mathrm{occ}}=A_c\left|\{k:n_k>0\}\right|\ \mathrm{m}^2 .
\]
The convex-hull area of target points is
\[
A_{\mathrm{hull}}=\frac12\left|\sum_i (x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i)\right|\ \mathrm{m}^2 ,
\]
where the sum runs over the ordered hull vertices. The root-mean-square dispersion about the centroid \(\bar r\) is
\[
R_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lVert r_j-\bar r\rVert^2}\ \mathrm{m}.
\]
Net proximity is
\[
\bar y=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_j\ \mathrm{m},\qquad
P_{1.5}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{y_j\le 1.5\,\mathrm{m}\},
\]
with \(P_{1.5}\) dimensionless. Zone share for a named zone \(Z\) is
\[
q_Z=\sum_{k\in Z}p_k .
\]
These statistics formalize “spatial flexibility” without inventing a single composite ratio. A team’s flexibility is a vector
\[
\mathbf{F}=(H,A_{\mathrm{eff}},A_{\mathrm{occ}},A_{\mathrm{hull}},R_{\mathrm{rms}},\bar y,P_{1.5},q_{\mathrm{II}},q_{\mathrm{III}}),
\]
not a single score. This matters because the source compares “width” of areas and proximity to the net as separate features: Brazil is described as having “the widest area of cooperation” while Russia is described as having “a narrow area to which the service is directed” [PAGE 11], and all teams are said to aim near the net [PAGE 11].

**Censoring operator.** The source records only forward passes [PAGE 5]. Let \(\pi_k=P(C=1\mid k)\) be the probability that a target in cell \(k\) is retained by the forward-and-in-frame filter. Then the observed distribution is
\[
p_k^{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{p_k\pi_k}{\sum_j p_j\pi_j}.
\]
If \(\pi_k\) is independent of \(k\) and team, censoring changes only sample size. If \(\pi_k\) depends on cell or team, then \(H^{\mathrm{obs}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{obs}}\), and team rankings are biased. This is an exact breakdown regime: the observed map is not the full reception map.

**Small-sample entropy bias.** For finite \(N\), the plug-in entropy estimator is biased downward. A standard first-order correction is
\[
\hat H_{\mathrm{MM}}=\hat H+\frac{|S|-1}{2N}\quad \mathrm{nats},
\]
where \(|S|\) is the number of occupied cells. This correction is illustrative; the source does not report it. Breakdown: when \(N\ll |S|\), no correction makes cell-level probabilities stable.

**Singularity and breakdown regimes.**
1. \(N=0\): \(p_k\), \(T_{kl}\), \(H\), and \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}\) are undefined.
2. One-cell concentration: \(H=0\), \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=1\), \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}=9\,\mathrm{m}^2\); “flexibility” is zero under this metric.
3. Uniform over 9 half-court cells: \(H=\ln 9\), \(N_{\mathrm{eff}}=9\), \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}=81\,\mathrm{m}^2\); uniform over 18 full-court cells: \(H=\ln 18\), \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}=162\,\mathrm{m}^2\).
4. Collinear or identical targets: \(A_{\mathrm{hull}}=0\), so hull area is singular as a flexibility measure even when the distribution has nonzero entropy.
5. Coder-boundary instability: if red/green/blue area assignment changes under a second coder, the qualitative “model” is not reproducible even if the raw dots are unchanged.
6. Camera/perspective distortion: floor coordinates near the net are compressed by the single end-line camera, so \(\bar y\) and \(P_{1.5}\) have unquantified measurement error.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is praxeology and sports-game theory rather than statistical learning. The paper explicitly grounds game models in “praxeology and the theory of sports games” and cites Pszczołowski’s praxeological model [PAGE 1]. It then uses Panfil’s taxonomy of ideal mapping, ideal designing, real mapping, and real designing models [PAGE 2]. The key ancestor is the ideal mapping model: it recreates championship-level action without influencing the observed players and is built from “various types of activity tables, graphics, charts, etc.” [PAGE 2]. The paper is also a continuation of Mazur’s earlier work on positioning-phase cooperation, and it cites Mazur and Superlak’s mapping-model studies in the discussion [PAGE 13]. The retrocausal move is that a 2021 publication organizes 2016 Olympic observations and then certifies the team set with 2018 FIVB ranking data [PAGE 4]. That is not a fatal flaw, but it creates temporal leakage if a reader treats the 2018 ranking as a pre-2016 selection variable.

The multi-week trap is to turn a three-match qualitative map into a multi-week training prescription. A coach could easily read “Brazil widest, Russia narrowest, USA and Italy most similar” as stable team identities and then train those patterns for weeks. But the source itself says the observed space decreases when the opponent serves effectively [PAGE 13], and it notes that areas farther from the net appear because “Cooperation in receiving-passing the ball is not always successful” [PAGE 13]. A multi-week program built on fixed red/green zones would therefore overfit opponent quality, match state, and the forward-pass filter. A second trap is reifying “zone 0.” The paper reports that the space between zones II and III “is often referred to by discipline experts as the ‘perfect reception zone’ or ‘zone 0’” [PAGE 14], but that is a disciplinary label, not an experimentally isolated optimum. A third trap is treating the four teams as independent replicates. They played each other, the sample is twelve team-matches, and the maps are descriptive. A fourth trap is assuming that “effective cooperation” means causal effectiveness. The conclusions say the models “could be treated as effective, because they show the cooperation of the best teams in the world” [PAGE 14]; that is selection on championship level, not a controlled effectiveness estimate.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is best read as three interacting mechanisms: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock/tempo constraint. Crisis habit regression means that under high-quality serve pressure, a team regresses toward its most overlearned reception target. The source gives a concrete version: “when the opponent effectively prevents by efficiently performing the service, then the space of cooperation in receiving-passing the ball decreases” [PAGE 13]. Russia’s map is described as narrow and concentrated between zones II and III near the net [PAGE 11], which is exactly the kind of habitual attractor that would intensify under stress. Brazil’s map is described as wider when the match result is positive and narrower in the loss [PAGE 13], suggesting that crisis compresses the available support rather than expanding it.

Counter-trap reasoning is the server’s model of the receiver’s model. If a receiver habitually targets zone 0 or the II/III border, the server can serve to force a deeper or wider pass, and the receiver can reposition to resist that force. The source does not measure beliefs, so this is an interpretive layer, but it is consistent with the paper’s emphasis on “surprise” and “flexible use of various combinations” [PAGE 2–3]. The setter also runs a recursive model: the setter’s choice depends on the pass quality, the pass quality depends on the receiver’s platform and the server’s placement, and the opponent’s block depends on the setter’s likely distribution. The paper’s “1, 2 and 3 tempo” language [PAGE 3] is the clock constraint in this system. Volleyball has no game clock in the usual sense, but the three-touch sequence imposes a hard temporal budget: a pass that is too far from the net or too fast reduces the setter’s feasible tempo set. Thus “clock” should be read as rally-phase timing and tempo, not as an external match clock. The recursive failure mode is a self-confirming loop: a team believes its habit is flexible, the opponent serves to the habit, the team’s map narrows, and the coach interprets the narrowed map as proof that the team must practice the habit even more.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is the claim that physical, biomechanical, and weather constraints can invalidate a purely tactical reading of the maps. The physical layer is ball flight under gravity. For a ball with vertical position \(z(t)\),
\[
z(t)=z_0+v_{z0}t-\frac12 g t^2,\qquad g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.
\]
The horizontal travel time to a target at distance \(d\) is approximately \(\tau=d/v_h\), where \(v_h\) is horizontal speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). If \(\tau\) is shorter than the setter’s reaction and repositioning time, the target is physically infeasible regardless of tactical preference. A simple feasibility envelope is
\[
\lVert r_p-r_s\rVert \le v_s\tau_p+\ell_{\mathrm{reach}},
\]
where \(r_p\) is the pass target, \(r_s\) is the setter’s position, \(v_s\) is setter speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\tau_p\) is available time in seconds, and \(\ell_{\mathrm{reach}}\) is reach in metres. If the observed target lies outside that envelope, the map records a constrained outcome, not a free choice.

The biomechanical layer adds platform angle, shoulder orientation, and error propagation. A reception platform that is angled more steeply relative to the incoming serve has less tolerance for contact-point error; this is why “as close as possible to the net” cannot be read as an unconstrained optimum. The net and 3 m line are also hard boundaries: the source’s grid uses the 3 m line [PAGE 3] and 3 m × 3 m cells [PAGE 4], so a pass cell near the net is not equivalent to a pass cell near the attack line in terms of setter options. The weather layer is a veto by absence: the tournament is indoor, and the paper reports no wind, rain, temperature, humidity, or air-pressure data. Therefore any weather-based explanation of the observed maps is non-identifiable. The strongest cross-layer conclusion is negative: because the study records only forward floor positions [PAGE 5], it cannot separate tactical preference from physical feasibility, opponent serve quality, and camera censoring. That is the veto proof against overinterpreting the red/green/blue areas as pure strategy.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Scenario 1: inter-coder reliability veto.** Verifier: stratify at least 20% of rallies across the twelve team-matches, have two independent coders assign reception and second-contact cells, and compute Cohen’s \(\kappa\) for named-zone assignment plus an intraclass correlation coefficient for \(x,y\) coordinates. Veto condition: \(\kappa<0.60\) for zone assignment or \(\mathrm{ICC}<0.75\) for coordinates. Prediction: if the veto triggers, the reported “widest” Brazil map and “narrowest” Russia map will not replicate at the zone level; red/green/blue boundaries will shift enough to change at least one team ranking in \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}\) or \(A_{\mathrm{occ}}\).

**Scenario 2: bootstrap stability veto.** Verifier: resample observed rallies within each team-match, recompute \(H\), \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}\), \(A_{\mathrm{hull}}\), and \(\bar y\) over at least 10,000 bootstrap replicates, and form percentile intervals. Veto condition: the 95% interval for \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathrm{Brazil})-A_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathrm{Russia})\) contains zero, or Brazil is not ranked widest in at least 95% of replicates. Prediction: because the source uses “three matches” per team [PAGE 4], the intervals will be wide; if the veto triggers, the claim that Brazil has the widest area and Russia the narrowest is a descriptive artifact of the twelve observed matches rather than a stable team property.

**Scenario 3: censoring audit veto.** Verifier: re-observe the same matches without the forward-only filter, count backward, end-line, and out-of-frame passes, and test whether missingness depends on team or zone. Veto condition: forward-filter loss exceeds 10% of all reception events and team-dependent missingness is significant at \(p<0.05\) under an exact test or logistic model. Prediction: if the veto triggers, including censored passes will reduce the Brazil–Russia contrast in \(A_{\mathrm{eff}}\) or change the USA–Italy similarity claim, because the observed maps are conditional on a filter that the source itself describes as excluding “passes where the ball was directed backwards, towards the end line or outside the recording area” [PAGE 5].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
