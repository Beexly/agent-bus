The theoretical lineage of this passage runs through the marginalist revolution and into Knight’s distinctive epistemic correction. Gossen (1854) and Jevons (1871) grounded utility in the equalization of marginal utilities across expenditures; Menger (1871) emphasized subjective value and the ordinal ranking of wants; Walras (1874) generalized the system to general equilibrium where all prices and quantities are simultaneously determined; and Pareto (1906) replaced cardinal utility with indifference curves and the marginal rate of substitution. Knight’s contribution on [PAGE 1] is to restate the Law of Choice not as a psychological hedonistic calculus but as a behavioral equilibrium condition linking preference ratios to physical equivalence ratios. This anticipates, without fully formalizing, the revealed-preference revolution of Samuelson (1938) and Hicks (1939), which would derive choice directly from observable selection behavior rather than from an assumed utility faculty.

More consequentially, Knight’s broader project in *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* distinguishes risk—events over which objective or intersubjective probability distributions can be formed—from uncertainty, where no such distribution exists. The passage on [PAGE 1] operates implicitly in the risk domain: preference ratios are assumed stable enough to be equated to physical ratios, and physical ratios are “externally given.” Under Knightian uncertainty, however, neither the preference ordering nor the technological substitution ratio can be treated as a stable parameter over relevant planning horizons.

Multi-week trap: Analysts applying this static Law of Choice to multi-week production cycles often assume that the physical equivalence ratio observed in week 1 remains valid in week 4. In reality, input availability, weather, labor supply, and machine breakdowns alter \(\mathrm{PER}_{xy}\) within the production period. Because Knight explicitly situates the equality “first in the market and then in production” [PAGE 1], a week-long lag between price discovery and production completion creates a vintage mismatch: producers commit resources based on week-1 physical equivalence ratios while market preferences in week 4 reflect new scarcity conditions. This is a structural trap of temporal misalignment, not a mere forecasting error. A second multi-week trap is the “plurality of productive agents” complication [PAGE 1]: over weeks, entry and exit change the industry supply curve, so the physical equivalence ratio facing any individual producer is endogenous to the aggregate decisions of rivals, invalidating the price-taking assumption embedded in the simple equality.

Biomechanical / cognitive layer veto: Computing \(\mathrm{MRS}_{xy}\) over a large alternative set requires simultaneous comparison of many bundles. Weather / exogenous shock layer veto: Knight treats physical equivalence ratios as “externally given” [PAGE 1], but weather shocks (drought, freeze, storm) alter agricultural yields, energy flows, and transport feasibility exogenously and discontinuously. Such shocks shift \(\mathrm{PER}_{xy}\) independently of market prices. Because market price adjustment requires time, while weather impacts production immediately, there is a period during which \(\mathrm{MRS}_{xy} \neq \mathrm{PER}_{xy}\) and the market cannot instantaneously re-equilibrate. This veto is absolute when the shock destroys a specific ultimate resource, making the physical equivalence ratio undefined (division by zero in input coefficients).

Adjudication proof: Suppose all three layers are active. Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be the set of competitive equilibria satisfying \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{xy} = p_x/p_y\) for all agents \(i\) and \(\mathrm{PER}_{xy} = p_x/p_y\) for production. The equality described on [PAGE 1] is therefore not a universal law but a conditional equilibrium valid only when all three veto layers are inactive: stable thermodynamics, cognitive capacity sufficient for the relevant dimensionality, and exogenous physical ratios fixed over the production clock.
