The source is a validation study of a vision-based optical player-tracking system, not a general proof that every downstream match metric is valid. The core data-generating chain is: eight stationary high-definition cameras cover a 105 m by 68 m field, with two cameras placed 10 m behind each corner at 20 m height [PAGE 5]; the detector emits observations of ground position and timestamp; low- and medium-level hierarchical association forms one-second tracklets [PAGE 3]; a piece-wise cubic polynomial is fitted using the midpoint of each one-second epoch [PAGE 3]; and displacement is computed from consecutive calibrated \(x,y\) positions by Euclidean distance [PAGE 5]. The source equation is \(\delta(n)=\sqrt{[x(n)-x(n-1)]^2+[y(n)-y(n-1)]^2}\), with \(x,y\) in metres and \(\delta(n)\) in metres per frame [PAGE 5]. Summing \(\delta(n)\) over a trial yields the optical distance estimate \(D_{\rm opt}\). The criterion is the tape-measured course length \(D_{\rm true}\), with timing gates used to define start and end frames [PAGE 3, PAGE 5]. Therefore the signed error is \(e=D_{\rm opt}-D_{\rm true}\) in metres and the relative signed error is \(100\,e/D_{\rm true}\) in percent, assuming \(D_{\rm true}\) is positive.

The first multi-hop issue is that the paper reports several related but non-identical error summaries. Page 1 states a mean absolute difference of \(0.79 \pm 0.56\) m and a difference of \(0.25\%\) [PAGE 1]. Page 6 states that the system “overestimated displacement by 0.25%” and that the “absolute mean difference … was 3.59% (0.79 ± 0.56m)” [PAGE 6]. Page 7 then says a key finding was that “the mean difference between estimated displacement and actual distance was -0.25%” while the next paragraph again says “overestimated displacement by only 0.25%” [PAGE 7]. This is a genuine sign-and-label gray zone: a positive signed bias means overestimation under \(e=D_{\rm opt}-D_{\rm true}\), whereas \(-0.25\%\) means underestimation under the same convention. The abstract also gives straight-line CV as \(4.09\%\), while page 6 gives \(4.08\%\) [PAGE 1, PAGE 6]. These are not fatal, but any reuse must choose one convention and avoid treating all “0.25%” statements as interchangeable.

The second gray-zone issue is criterion validity. The paper’s criterion is the nominal course length measured by tape, not the participant’s actual \(x,y\) trajectory. Page 8 explicitly says “actual position data (x,y coordinates) of the participants were not collected” [PAGE 8]. Thus the error metric contains detector error plus path-adherence error. This matters most at 90 degree turns: page 6 reports \(90^\circ\) CV of \(4.89\%\) versus straight-line CV of \(4.08\%\) [PAGE 6], and page 7 explains that “at higher running speeds and turning angles the corner may be cut or over-run because of an abrupt change of direction” [PAGE 7]. A cut corner makes the actual path shorter than the tape course; an over-run makes it longer. Therefore some of the turn-related variance is human path deviation, not necessarily optical error.

A third audit point is reliability semantics. The manual quality-control process was repeated twice per video [PAGE 5], yielding typical error \(0.25\) to \(0.36\) m, typical error as CV \(1.06\) to \(1.75\%\), and ICC \(0.88\) to \(0.93\) [PAGE 1, PAGE 7]. That is intra-operator repeatability for the same operator doing the same verification task. It is not inter-operator reliability, not automated-detector reliability, and not match-level identity-tracking reliability. The paper does show strong correlations \(r=0.986\) to \(0.988\) and no heteroscedasticity [PAGE 7], but correlation and ICC answer different questions from absolute accuracy in new match conditions.

A fourth issue is temporal resolution. The system samples “noisy detections at 25 frames per second” and smooths with one-second tracklets [PAGE 3]. The nominal frame interval is \(\Delta t=1/25\ \mathrm{s}=0.04\ \mathrm{s}\). Displacement over 0.04 s is small, so summed Euclidean distance is sensitive to positional jitter: independent zero-mean tracking noise tends to inflate the sum of displacement magnitudes, because \(E[\|\Delta \mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\|]\) is at least \(\|\Delta \mathbf{x}\|\) by convexity. The paper’s small positive bias is consistent with such a mechanism, but the paper does not isolate it. Also, one-second smoothing can hide within-epoch direction changes, so the validation of displacement does not automatically validate velocity or acceleration. Page 8 states directly: “Velocity and acceleration measures were not assessed” [PAGE 8].

The fifth issue is scope. The protocol uses three participants, five courses, three speeds, and three trials, producing “27 data points for each of the five running courses and resulting in 135 data points in total” [PAGE 3]. Those 135 points are repeated trials nested within three people, not 135 independent participants. The study also tests “single runs for one player at a time” and says future research should address “soccer match-specific scenarios with multiple players” [PAGE 8]. Prior hockey work tracked twenty players with manual operator input during occlusion [PAGE 8], but that is not the same as a validated multi-player soccer displacement study. The defensible conclusion is therefore narrow: in this soccer-field running protocol, with one player at a time, manual quality control, and a tape-based criterion, the optical system gave accurate and reliable displacement estimates. It is not a blanket validation for occlusion-heavy match tracking, indoor tracking, weather extremes, or biomechanical load inference.

\[
D_{\rm opt}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\delta(n),
\]
in metres. The signed error against the tape-measured course length \(D_{\rm true}\) is
\[
e=D_{\rm opt}-D_{\rm true}\quad [\mathrm{m}],

\[
\bar e=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} e_i\quad [\mathrm{m}],
\]
and the mean absolute difference is
\[
\mathrm{MAD}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}|e_i|\quad [\mathrm{m}].
\]

\mathrm{CV}=\frac{s}{\bar x}\times 100\%\quad [\mathrm{dimensionless\ percent}],
\]

- Calibration breakdown: oblique camera views or poorly conditioned pixel-to-metre maps at field edges can scale \(x,y\) unevenly. - Criterion breakdown: if the tape course is not the actual path, \(e\) is not a pure detector error; it is detector error plus path-adherence error [PAGE 7, PAGE 8].
- Noise-accumulation breakdown: summing positive Euclidean increments over many frames can create a small positive bias under independent positional jitter. The paper’s small positive overall bias is compatible with this, but the design does not isolate it.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The nested belief structure has four levels. At the player level, the participant is told to follow the course as closely as possible, but under high speed and sharp turning the player may “cut or over-run” the corner [PAGE 7]. At the operator level, the human verifier believes that a tracklet positioned at the feet is the same player; page 5 says the operator confirmed that “the tracklets remained positioned at the feet of the moving player” [PAGE 5]. At the system-designer level, the association and cubic-smoothing pipeline assumes that a one-second midpoint representation is an adequate summary of continuous motion [PAGE 3]. At the analyst level, the reader may assume that a tape-measured course length is the actual path and that accurate displacement implies accurate velocity and acceleration. Page 8 directly contradicts the latter: “Velocity and acceleration measures were not assessed” [PAGE 8]. A further recursive layer is the reader who treats the abstract’s \(0.25\%\) as a signed bias without checking page 7’s \(-0.25\%\) statement [PAGE 1, PAGE 7].

Crisis habit regression: when occlusion, high turn rate, or a noisy detection sequence appears, the pipeline can regress to the last stable tracklet or to a smoothed straight-line interpolation. The operator can regress to the habit of accepting a feet-position tracklet because it looks plausible in video. The analyst can regress to the habit of reporting a single CV or ICC because those are familiar reliability summaries, even when the criterion is a nominal course rather than a gold-standard \(x,y\) trajectory [PAGE 8]. These are “crisis habits” because they restore a coherent-looking track under pressure rather than resolving the underlying identity or path ambiguity.

Counter-trap: the opposite failure is overcorrection—declaring the system invalid because it was not tested with VICON, multi-player occlusion, rain, and indoor courts all at once. The paper’s evidence is strong for its narrow protocol: high correlations \(r=0.986\) to \(0.988\), mean absolute difference \(0.79 \pm 0.56\) m, and ICC \(0.88\) to \(0.93\) [PAGE 1, PAGE 7]. The correct counter-trap is staged adjudication: accept displacement in the tested single-player field protocol; demand separate evidence for multi-player, occlusion, weather, indoor, and derivative metrics.

Clock: there are at least four clocks. The frame clock is 25 Hz, or 40 ms [PAGE 3]. The tracklet clock is one second, because the cubic polynomial is fitted using the midpoint of each one-second epoch [PAGE 3]. The trial clock is the timing-gate interval, with start and end defined by the frame at which the gate light extinguished [PAGE 5]. The multi-week clock is the calibration and operator-drift interval, which the paper does not test. Confusing these clocks produces false confidence: a 25 Hz system can still have one-second smoothing that hides sharp direction changes, and a one-session reliability study cannot certify a multi-week deployment.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: multi-player crossing and occlusion. Test two players crossing within 1 to 2 m at a \(90^\circ\) turn, at sprint pace, with a VICON or manually digitized frame-by-frame ground truth. Verification metric: identity-switch count and signed displacement error per player. Prediction: without occlusion-specific manual input, the automated tracklet association will sometimes swap identities, producing a large positive or negative displacement error that is not present in the single-player validation. If the observed identity-switch rate is nonzero and the displacement error exceeds the single-player \(0.79 \pm 0.56\) m scale [PAGE 1], the claim of match-level multi-player validity is vetoed. This directly contradicts the paper’s stated limitation that only “single runs for one player at a time” were assessed [PAGE 8].

Scenario 2: center-versus-periphery calibration. Replicate the same 30 m straight and 20 m \(90^\circ\) courses at the field center, at a corner, and at a sideline, with ground-truth \(x,y\) from VICON. Verification metric: signed bias and CV by field region. Prediction: peripheral and oblique camera views will show larger calibration error and larger CV than the center, because the paper explicitly did not compare center with peripheral detections [PAGE 8]. If the peripheral CV exceeds the center CV by a pre-specified margin, the claim of uniform field accuracy is vetoed. This is a verifiable geometric counterfactual, not a rhetorical objection.

Scenario 3: high-speed turn with actual-path ground truth. Instrument a 20 m \(90^\circ\) turn with VICON and compare three quantities: the tape-defined course length, the actual path length, and the optical summed displacement. Verification metric: the decomposition of error into path deviation versus tracking deviation.
