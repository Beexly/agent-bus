**Genealogical Lineage of the Drive-Dualism: From Instinct Theory to Cognitive Maps**
E. C. Tolman's 1932 conceptualization of *appetites* and *aversions* represents a critical evolutionary branch in the lineage of behavioral science, situated between early本能 (instinct) theories and later cognitive mapping. 
* **Ancestral Roots (Pre-1900):** Darwinian evolutionary psychology provided the foundational soil, suggesting that behaviors evolved to solve survival problems. Early psychologists like James McKeen Cattell and William James posited that "emotions" or "sentiments" drove behavior, but lacked a rigorous physiological anchor.
* **The Stimulus-Response Bridge (1910s-1920s):** Thorndike’s Law of Effect and Pavlov’s conditioning established the primacy of external stimuli. However, these models struggled with goal-directed flexibility. Tolman’s genealogy diverged here by insisting that the *internal state* (the initiating physiological excitement) was the primary cause, not just an epiphenomenon of external cues.
* **Tolman's Divergence Point:** While contemporaries like Watson focused on S-R bonds, Tolman proposed that an *appetite* is a "demand for a certain complementary type of physiological quiescence." This moved the focus from the stimulus (*food*) to the organism's internal demand (*hunger*). The "means-end-readinesses" became the cognitive bridge, allowing an animal to select diverse means (objects) to achieve the single end (quiescence). This was the seed of his later "cognitive map" theory: the animal learns relations among stimuli (means-objects) in service of fulfilling drive states (goal-objects).
* **Future Trajectory & Trap Avoidance:** This dual-drive model directly prefigured the reinforcement learning frameworks of the 20th century. In modern RL terminology, an *appetite* is a negative reward signal (cost function) driving the agent toward a terminal state (quiescence), while an *aversion* is a large penalty signal for entering specific states (disturbances). The "trap" for future researchers is to reduce Tolman’s rich, purposeful entities back into simplistic associative chains. Tolman explicitly argued against this reductionism; his drives are not mere reflex arcs but generative sources of purposive planning. Recognizing this lineage prevents the regression to a purely mechanistic view of behavior.
