Kant argued that space is not derived from external experience but is an a priori intuition necessary for experience to occur. The "Platonizing school" cited by James is the direct descendant of this transcendental idealism, asserting that geometry and spatial relations are structures imposed by the intellect. Conversely, James’s reliance on Nunnely’s patient echoes Molyneux’s Problem, originally posed by William Molyneux to Locke in 1688 regarding whether a blind person born could visually distinguish a cube from a sphere upon suddenly gaining sight. James uses the reported "yes" from Nunnely’s empirical observation to challenge the necessity of a priori categories.
