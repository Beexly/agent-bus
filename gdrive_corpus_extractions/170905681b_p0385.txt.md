Retrocausal genealogy traces forward: Rescorla & Wagner (1972) formalized the very summation Hull gestures at, with associative strength $V_i$ updating by $\Delta V_i = \alpha\beta(\lambda - \sum_j V_j)$. Pearce's configural theory (1987, 1994) radicalizes Major Corollary IV by treating the compound as a unique configural stimulus rather than an elemental sum, which maps onto Hull's positive vs. negative patterning distinction but rejects the assumption that components automatically summate [PAGE 378]. Scenario 1 — Supra-additive inhibitory compound veto.
Setup: Train element A positively to reaction R. Train elements B and C negatively to R (reinforced for omission). Present compound {A,B,C}. Hull's diminishing-returns summation [PAGE 378] predicts $\mathcal{R}(\{A,B,C\}) < \mathcal{R}(\{A\})$ but still positive if $h_A$ dominates; Major Corollary IV negative patterning may reduce but not necessarily invert configural strength.
Veto condition: If neural interaction is supra-additively inhibitory such that $\Psi''$ effectively becomes positive in the inhibitory direction (or $\Psi$ has an inhibitory saturation floor), then $\mathcal{R}(\{A,B,C\}) < \mathcal{R}(\text{neutral})$, i.e., response suppression below baseline.
Verifiable prediction: Response probability to compound {A,B,C} will be significantly lower than response probability to a neutral stimulus alone, with response latency increased by more than two standard deviations and possible complete omission. This falsifies pure diminishing-returns summation and confirms an inhibitory floor not specified by Hull [PAGE 378].

Scenario 2 — Generalization-gradient cliff veto at the 50 percent singularity.
Setup: Condition reaction R to a pure tone. Test the same tone embedded in an arpeggio context where surrounding notes have been differentially reinforced [PAGE 378]. Hull states that under "< 50 per cent" gradient fall, the afferent impulse evokes the same reaction alone and within the compound [PAGE 378].
Veto condition: If psychophysical measurement shows gradient fall $\Delta\gamma \ge 0.5$ between the pure tone and the tone-in-arpeggio, the same-reaction invariance breaks at the boundary Hull leaves undefined.
Verifiable prediction: The generalization gradient will exhibit a cliff-like discontinuity at $\Delta\gamma = 0.5$, not a smooth decay; above this threshold, tone-alone evokes R but tone-in-arpeggio does not, even when the tone element itself is identical. Any finding of reliable differential responding at exactly 0.5 or above constitutes a veto of the invariance claim [PAGE 378].

Scenario 3 — Latent non-receptor determinant veto.
Setup: Reinforcement of reaction R is determined jointly by stimulus compound C and by an unobserved physiological state $z$ (e.g., cortisol level or circadian phase) that does not activate a specific receptor for C [PAGE 378]. Hull acknowledges that many reinforcement-determining elements lack receptor discharge, forcing the organism to gamble [PAGE 378].
Veto condition: If $z$ is manipulated independently of C while holding compound composition constant, and observed reinforcement rate tracks $z$ rather than $\mathcal{R}(C)$, then reaction potentiality cannot serve as the biological calculus of reinforcement probability.
Verifiable prediction: Within-subject correlation between presence of conditioned elements in C and occurrence of R will drop to near zero when $z$ is varied across levels; response rate will instead correlate with $z$ (e.g., high-cortisol blocks R regardless of compound). This confirms Hull's gamble regime and falsifies the sufficiency of receptor-visible compound summation [PAGE 378].

Each veto is designed to be falsifiable with standard behavioral or neurophysiological measurement and maps directly onto a breakdown regime identified in the source-operators section.
