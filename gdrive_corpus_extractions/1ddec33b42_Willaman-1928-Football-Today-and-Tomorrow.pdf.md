<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let the primary state variables be defined as follows, with units and source grounding.

1. Field position. Let \(x_{\rm yd}\) be field position in yards from one’s own goal line, with \(0 \le x_{\rm yd} \le 100\). In SI units,
\[
x_{\rm m} = 0.9144\,x_{\rm yd}\quad [\mathrm{m}].
\]
The opponent’s goal line is at \(x_{\rm yd}=100\). “Inside the ten yard line” is approximated by \(x_{\rm yd}>90\).

2. Practice load. Roper states the daily ceiling: “no more than two hours of football is permitted in one day” [PAGE 45 of 214]. Let \(D\) be seasonal practice dose in hours:
\[
D = \sum_{j=1}^{W}\sum_{k=1}^{4} \Delta t_{jk}, \qquad \Delta t_{jk} \le 2\ \mathrm{h}.
\]
With four days per week over approximately eight weeks,
\[
D \le 8 \times 4 \times 2\ \mathrm{h} = 64\ \mathrm{h}.
\]
This matches: “The modern football team puts in sixty-four hours of actual football practice during the season; two hours a day for four days a week over approximately eight weeks” [PAGE 29 of 214]. A later passage gives a slightly different accounting: “Last fall at Princeton football took up exactly sixty-six hours of the players’ time, while the University was in session” [PAGE 154 of 214]. The source therefore supports an empirical range:
\[
D_{\rm source} \in [64,66]\ \mathrm{h}.
\]
Breakdown regime: if \(D\) is inflated by extra sessions, hot-weather exposure, or spring practice, the source warns of injury, fatigue, and loss of enthusiasm. “Nothing takes the life out of a football player more than the heat” [PAGE 28 of 214]. “Scrimmaging is often overdone, and more teams are overworked than underworked” [PAGE 36 of 214].

3. Fatigue and injury hazard. Roper gives a qualitative monotonicity principle: “men are hurt far more easily when they are tired than when they are fresh” [PAGE 40 of 214]. Let \(H_I\) denote injury hazard in events per exposure, \(L\) cumulative load in hours, and \(R\) recovery time in hours. A conservative formalization is:
\[
\frac{\partial H_I}{\partial L} > 0, \qquad \frac{\partial H_I}{\partial R} < 0,
\]
with no numeric coefficient supplied by the source. Breakdown regime: when recovery is insufficient, extra practice minutes are vetoed even if the coach desires technical perfection.

4. Kick release time. Roper asserts: “If you can get your kicks off under two seconds, none of them will be blocked” [PAGE 59 of 214]. Let \(\tau_k\) be kick release time in seconds. The operational condition is:
\[
\tau_k < 2\ \mathrm{s}.
\]
Breakdown regime: \(\tau_k \ge 2\ \mathrm{s}\) raises block exposure. The source separately notes that a drop-kick is slower: “The drop-kick is of necessity much slower in getting off than the punt and is easier to block” [PAGE 68 of 214].

5. Kicker range. Let \(d\) be attempt distance in yards and \(R_k\) the kicker’s effective range in yards. Roper’s rule is:
\[
d \le R_k.
\]
He illustrates failure: “The Princeton drop-kicker was very accurate but his range was thirty-five yards—not forty-five” [PAGE 68 of 214]. The exception is score-state dependent: “There is a time to break this rule against trying to stretch the range of your drop-kicker, and that time is when you are holding the short end of the score, and three points will win or tie the game for you” [PAGE 69 of 214].

6. Fourth-down policy. Let \(L_s\) denote score state: leading, tied, or trailing. Roper’s rule is:
\[
u^*(down=4) =
\begin{cases}
\text{punt}, & L_s \ne \text{trailing},\\
\text{consider retain possession}, & L_s = \text{trailing}.
\end{cases}
\]
Source quote: “Always kick on the fourth down unless you are behind” [PAGE 61 of 214]. Breakdown regime: if the team is not trailing, a rushing attempt on fourth down forfeits field position without necessity. The Colgate example is the negative demonstration: “The failure of the Princeton quarterback to order a kick cost the Princeton team fifty yards and they were never able to overcome the distance” [PAGE 61 of 214].

7. Forward-pass restriction. Let \(t_r\) be remaining game time in seconds. The two-minute warning is \(t_r \le 120\ \mathrm{s}\). Roper’s rule:
\[
\text{forward pass vetoed if } t_r \le 120\ \mathrm{s} \text{ and } L_s = \text{leading}.
\]
Source quote: “You should never forward pass in the last two minutes of the game if you hold the long end of the score” [PAGE 62 of 214]. The proof is immediate: “It is intercepted, and your opponents score a touchdown. You are beaten by the score of 12-7, because you took an unnecessary chance” [PAGE 62 of 214].

8. Zone policy. Roper’s zone doctrine can be written as a piecewise policy:
\[
u^*(x_{\rm yd}) =
\begin{cases}
\text{kick on first down or attempt long gain, then kick if it fails}, & x_{\rm yd} < 25,\\
\text{kick on third down; probe defense before then}, & 25 \le x_{\rm yd} < 50,\\
\text{use ground-gaining plays; kick on fourth down; trick plays favored}, & 50 < x_{\rm yd} < 90,\\
\text{any gain; avoid center-line plays inside opponent ten; avoid goal-line forward pass on second/third down}, & x_{\rm yd} \ge 90.
\end{cases}
\]
Source quotes: “Between your goal line and your twenty-five yard line you should either kick on first down or try a long-gaining play and kick if it fails” [PAGE 55 of 214]; “Between your twenty-five yard line and mid-field, kick on third down, until then try out opponents’ defense” [PAGE 56 of 214].

9. Deception entropy. Let \(\mathcal{U}\) be a finite play set and \(p(u)\) the opponent’s predicted probability of play \(u\). The information-theoretic surprise of the play distribution is:
\[
H(U) = -\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} p(u)\log_2 p(u)\quad [\text{bits}].
\]
Roper’s principle is qualitative: “one unexpected play is better than ten obvious plays, for the expected play is always a weak play, and the unexpected play nine times out of ten is the ground-gaining play” [PAGE 70 of 214]. Assumption: the opponent assigns nonuniform probabilities to expected plays. Breakdown regime: if the offense telegraphs formation or repeats patterns, \(H(U)\) collapses toward zero.

10. Weather and surface modifiers. Roper includes environmental state variables: “The position of the sun and the direction of the wind are to be considered in football generalship” [PAGE 44 of 214]; “On a wet field he should play a little safer than usual” [PAGE 44 of 214]. These are not numerical equations in the text, but they function as constraints: if surface friction, visibility, or heat load exceed tolerance, high-risk plays are downgraded.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogical core of the text is the transition from mass football to speed-and-brain football. Roper explicitly dates the shift: “Twenty-five years ago, when I played football, the development and selection of the varsity was largely a survival of the toughest, the biggest and strongest men. Today the fastest, the brainiest and best conditioned players make up the eleven” [PAGE 26 of 214]. This is not merely a personnel preference; it is a retro-causal explanation of why earlier methods produced injuries and inefficiency. The old hardening process, especially “falling on the ball,” is treated as a historical error: “At its worst, it is among the most dangerous forms of early season training” [PAGE 31 of 214]. Roper’s own confession makes the lineage personal: “I may as well confess that it took me twenty-five years to see the light” [PAGE 32 of 214].

A second genealogical thread is rule evolution. The 1905 crisis and subsequent reforms are presented as the birth condition of the modern game: “When President Roosevelt called the representatives of Yale, Harvard and Princeton to the White House in October, 1905 to discuss the future of football, he not only saved the game by demanding that the rules be changed, but made possible modern football as it is known today” [PAGE 179 of 214]. The 1906 revisions are itemized: ten yards for a first down, an added down, forward pass, onside kick, quarter periods, and restrictions on interference [PAGE 187 of 214]. Later changes include the touchdown value, four downs, field width, kickoff position, and forward-pass restriction [PAGE 196 of 214]. This genealogy matters because Roper’s tactical rules are not timeless axioms; they are adaptations to a rule environment that rewarded openness, passing, and field-position management.

A third lineage is psychological. Roper connects football to institutional loyalty: “the game is played, not by eleven men, but by eleven hundred or eleven thousand—by the whole student body and graduate body of the institution” [PAGE 162 of 214]. He contrasts this with professional football, which he sees as lacking the communal motive force: “It takes something more compelling than a pay check to arouse the flaming courage, the grit and endurance manifested on the gridiron” [PAGE 162 of 214]. This is not sentimental filler; it identifies a hidden variable: collective identity as a performance multiplier.

The multi-week traps are equally explicit. First, overwork: “The worst fault a coach can be guilty of is working his men to death” [PAGE 36 of 214]. Second, premature peaking: Roper’s 1906 lesson is that “the players burnt themselves out” after an early climax [PAGE 192 of 214]. Third, overconfidence: “Nothing alarms me more than to have a team I am coaching go into a big game a favorite” [PAGE 124 of 214]. Fourth, spring practice: “There is one phase of the training of a modern football team which I believe should be abolished—Spring practice” [PAGE 43 of 214]. Fifth, scouting dependency: “Scouting should be abolished. It destroys initiative and when initiative and independent thinking go out of football it loses its greatest force as a collegiate game” [PAGE 208 of 214]. These traps are recursive because they appear attractive in the short term but degrade performance over weeks: extra practice seems rigorous but increases injury; scouting seems informative but dulls improvisation; spring work seems developmental but steals recovery and enthusiasm.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer concerns what each agent believes the other agent expects. Roper’s quarterback is not a mere play-caller; he is a field-level Bayesian agent managing time, field position, opponent alignment, and team morale. The source states: “There are twenty-two football players in a game. Some systems of football strategy seem to have been built on the assumption that there are only eleven—all on your side” [PAGE 57 of 214]. This is an explicit warning against one-sided modeling. The quarterback must model the opponent’s model.

Crisis habit regression is the first recursive failure mode. Under pressure, the quarterback may default to familiar patterns: “The average signal caller is inclined to work by standard methods, to follow a set pattern” [PAGE 52 of 214]. If the opponent knows this, the opponent can sit on the standard play. Roper’s remedy is not random trickery but disciplined inversion: use an expected action as a mask, then execute a different action. The Caulkins play is the canonical counter-trap: Yale expected a center rush, so Caulkins “ordered a forward pass hurled to him” [PAGE 66 of 214]. The Lourie fake place kick is another: “Eleven minds were concentrated on one play and every effort was made to prevent the goal from the field” [PAGE 72 of 214], after which Lourie ran for the score.

The clock introduces a second recursion. When time is short and the score state is leading, the opponent expects either conservative play or desperate passing. Roper’s rule resolves the recursion by removing risky uncertainty: “You should never forward pass in the last two minutes of the game if you hold the long end of the score” [PAGE 62 of 214]. The logic is not merely risk-avoidance; it is expectation management. A pass attempt in that state hands the opponent a high-leverage negative event. The opponent’s best counter becomes trivial: intercept and score.

The dressing room is a third recursive control surface. Roper treats halftime as a cognitive environment that must be protected from emotional contamination. He demands five minutes of undisturbed rest: “For five minutes after the whistle my men are guaranteed absolute and undisturbed rest and quiet” [PAGE 110 of 214]. He rejects artificial stimulation: “I dislike, on principle, all forms of artificial stimulation, from oratory to coffee” [PAGE 112 of 214]. The recursive insight is that panic propagates through social contact. If the coach is visibly rattled, the players infer danger; if the room is chaotic, the players’ attention fragments. The coach therefore models calm not as performance but as information.

The counter-trap is only valid when rehearsed. Roper warns against “miracle plays that work once in fifty attempts” [PAGE 70 of 214]. The unexpected play must be a standard action deployed at an unexpected time, or a practiced formation change. The huddle is relevant here: “The huddle too, offers great possibilities of quick change of formation. This, to my way of thinking, is its greatest advantage” [PAGE 200 of 214]. The huddle reduces information leakage: “Your opponents don’t know until just before the ball is snapped what you are going to do, and how you are going to line up” [PAGE 200 of 214]. Thus the recursive stack is: opponent expects habit; coach masks habit; quarterback executes inversion; clock constrains inversion; quiet dressing room preserves cognitive clarity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a strategically attractive action is rejected because a lower physical or environmental layer makes it unsafe or infeasible. Roper’s text supplies several veto proofs.

Physical veto 1: falling on the ball. The practice may look disciplined, but it produces injury. Roper reports a player “got up with a broken collar bone” during pregame falling drills [PAGE 32 of 214], and another “dislocated his shoulder—and it wasn’t a paper-weight shoulder either—by trying to fall on the ball under my personal direction” [PAGE 32 of 214]. The veto proof is: if the action reliably produces skin loss, bruises, fractures, or dislocations without tactical benefit, it is vetoed. The tactical benefit is especially weak because a player near a loose ball could often pick it up and run: “Any one of those men could have counted three and then picked up the ball and scored with it” [PAGE 32 of 214].

Physical veto 2: excess padding. Padding may reduce bruises but reduce speed. Roper states: “Pads retard speed, and speed is king in modern football” [PAGE 41 of 214]. The veto proof is: if protective equipment reduces the decisive variable (speed) below the opponent’s effective motion, the equipment is removed except where essential, such as shoulders.

Physical veto 3: fatigue. Roper’s rule is explicit: “men are hurt far more easily when they are tired than when they are fresh” [PAGE 40 of 214]. Therefore, extra minutes of scrimmage are vetoed when fatigue is visible. The source describes Fitzpatrick detecting “the drawn faces and lagging step of a wearied player” [PAGE 40 of 214] and sending him to rest. The veto condition is:
\[
\text{observed fatigue signs} \Rightarrow \text{stop high-risk work}.
\]

Weather veto 1: heat. “Nothing takes the life out of a football player more than the heat. Football was never intended to be a hot-weather game” [PAGE 28 of 214]. If practice occurs in severe heat, the load equation is vetoed because recovery and cognition degrade. The source’s preference for later starts is based on this constraint.

Weather veto 2: wet field. “On a wet field he should play a little safer than usual” [PAGE 44 of 214]. The veto applies to high-risk lateral or trick plays when footing is unreliable. The physical mechanism is reduced friction and increased fumble probability, even if the text states the prescription rather than the mechanism.

Weather veto 3: sun, wind, and slope. “The position of the sun and the direction of the wind are to be considered in football generalship” [PAGE 44 of 214]; “It’s a lot easier to run downhill than uphill” [PAGE 44 of 214]. If a play requires precise passing or sprinting into a severe environmental gradient, the play is vetoed or reversed.

Timing veto: kick release. The two-second rule is a clear veto threshold: “If you can get your kicks off under two seconds, none of them will be blocked” [PAGE 59 of 214]. If the operation cannot achieve \(\tau_k < 2\ \mathrm{s}\), the kick is vetoed. This applies especially to drop-kicks: “The drop-kick is of necessity much slower in getting off than the punt and is easier to block” [PAGE 68 of 214].

Range veto: drop-kick beyond effective distance. The Navy example proves the veto: the kicker’s range was thirty-five yards, but the attempt was made from a longer effective distance; the kick was blocked and recovered [PAGE 68 of 214]. The formal veto is:
\[
d > R_k \Rightarrow \text{veto drop-kick, except score-state emergency}.
\]
The emergency exception is explicit: when “three points will win or tie the game” and the team is otherwise without a path, the risk may be accepted [PAGE 69 of 214].

The adjudication principle is therefore: tactical imagination cannot override biomechanical, meteorological, or chronometric constraints. Roper’s coaching method is not anti-strategy; it is strategy subordinated to physical feasibility.
