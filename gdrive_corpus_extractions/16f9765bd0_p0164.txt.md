When central nervous system (CNS) drive declines due to metabolic byproduct accumulation (e.g., H+, Pi) and peripheral fatigue (sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release impairment), motor unit recruitment patterns become erratic. This degrades the precision of complex coordinative patterns, ingrain bad mechanics, and increases injury risk. Modern sport science defines this zone as the point where acute fatigue compromises movement quality—a core tenet of velocity-based training (VBT) and autoregulation.

Third, the warning against late-season overtraining and the prescription for "light work only" before a critical game aligns with the principle of tapering. The physiological rationale is to allow supercompensation: the reduction in training stress permits the dissipation of fatigue while maintaining or enhancing fitness components (aerobic capacity, strength). The text’s concern about players being "lame and wearied" versus "full of energy and go" highlights the trade-off between mechanical proficiency (skill maintenance) and systemic freshness (energy availability). Tapering optimizes the ratio of form (fitness) to fatigue, peaking performance at competition time.

Fixed meal times synchronize peripheral clocks (via insulin/glucose signaling), optimizing metabolic efficiency and hormonal rhythms (cortisol, growth hormone). The text’s era lacked understanding of nutrition periodization (carb loading, protein timing) and sleep architecture’s role in recovery, which are now critical for managing "ginger" and preventing overtraining.
