Multi-week traps arise because the patent's "time-related information" (claim 1(a)) is underspecified relative to business cycles. Trap 1: seasonality aliasing. If an experiment runs over weeks 1–4 and the optimal price is deployed in week 5, holiday or month-end effects confound the estimated price sensitivity; the model attributes temporal variation to price. Trap 2: carryover and session contamination. A visitor exposed to price p₁ in week 1 and price p₂ in week 3 violates the stable unit treatment value assumption; the dataset no longer supports clean causal estimates of each price's effect. Trap 3: merchant intervention mid-flight. Because configuration data can include continuous or discrete sampling intervals (claim 4), a merchant who alters sampling parameters during an experiment breaks the randomization protocol and invalidates the confidence intervals referenced in the preamble. Trap 4: cohort drift. The visitor population composition changes week to week; a model estimated on week-1 demographics (claim 12) applied to week-5 traffic suffers from covariate shift. Trap 5: threshold lock-in. If δ in claim 16 is set conservatively at week 1, the system may propagate no price updates for multiple weeks despite genuine demand shifts, creating a false appearance of stability while the model becomes increasingly stale.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the merchant's behavioral response when the environment shocks. Under normal operations the merchant may trust the algorithmic p*, but during a crisis—sudden demand collapse, competitor price war, or supply disruption—the human reverts to heuristic pricing rules (cost-plus markup, competitor matching, last-known-good price). This regresses the system to a pre-experimental state, violating claim 1(e)'s assumption that the optimal price determined by the processor will be displayed or propagated. The machine's model, calibrated on non-crisis sensitivities, is then overridden by human judgment not represented in the configuration data.

The counter-trap is that the algorithm's stated objective may conflict with the merchant's latent utility during crisis. Claim 13 optimizes profit; claim 17 revenue; claim 18 market share. If the algorithm computes a surge price p* to maximize short-term revenue or profit during a demand spike, customers may perceive unfairness, reducing future demand and customer lifetime value. The demand model estimated from pre-crisis experiments omits this goodwill externality, so optimizing the claimed objective produces a p* that is locally optimal but globally destructive. The counter-trap is therefore: the more rigorously the system follows claims 13–17 during a crisis, the more it erodes the very market sensitivities the model claims to measure.

The clock layer introduces three distinct temporal clocks that the patent does not synchronize. Clock 1: experiment duration T_exp from the time-related configuration data. Clock 2: market-sensitivity decay τ, the half-life after which θ(t) materially changes. Clock 3: website-update latency T_upd between computation of p* and display to visitors (claims 1(e), 14). If T_exp > τ, the model is obsolete before deployment. If T_upd ≫ T_exp, batch price updates miss intra-day demand waves. If T_upd < human decision time, visitors may not react within the offered price window, censoring purchase data. The absence of a constraint such as T_exp < τ and T_upd ≈ decision-time creates a real-time control hazard in which the system issues optimal prices against a clock it does not model.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The website operates on networked hardware with latency L and server response time S. Claim 1(e) requires displaying the optimal price to the merchant; claims 14–16 require automatically updating the website. If L + S exceeds the visitor inter-arrival time or the discrete sampling interval (claim 4), a visitor may be assigned to price bucket p_k but rendered a different price, or the price update may fail to propagate before the visitor leaves. Veto rule: if L + S > Δt_slice, freeze propagation and hold p_cur.

Biomechanical layer: Human purchase decisions have a reaction-time distribution T_dec with mean μ_dec and standard deviation σ_dec. If the experiment offers a price only for a discrete interval Δt (claim 4) shorter than the time required for a visitor to observe, evaluate, and transact, the outcome Y_i is right-censored: the visitor did not have biomechanical opportunity to convert. This biases the sensitivity model toward zero demand for short-interval prices. Veto rule: require Δt > μ_dec + k·σ_dec (e.g., k = 2) before accepting experimental outcomes from that interval.

Weather layer: Claim 20 allows optimization of shipping resources and manufacturing resources. Severe weather W (storm index, precipitation, disruption severity) alters marginal shipping cost c_ship and manufacturing capacity C_man. If W exceeds a critical threshold W_crit, the cost structure embedded in the profit objective changes: c → c + Δc(W) or capacity becomes binding. A price p* computed under normal weather becomes suboptimal or infeasible because it ignores the resource veto. Veto rule: if W > W_crit, suppress all automatic price propagation until logistics recover.

Adjudication order (veto stack): Physical connectivity veto has highest priority because no price can be displayed if the website layer is unresponsive. Weather veto is second because supply infeasibility dominates pricing. Biomechanical veto is third because censored outcomes invalidate the model. Algorithmic confidence veto (insufficient n, wide CI) is lowest priority because it reflects statistical uncertainty rather than physical impossibility. This ordered veto stack ensures that no price update crosses into production when any lower layer is compromised.
