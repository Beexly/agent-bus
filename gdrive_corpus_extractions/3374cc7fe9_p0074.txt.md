<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight's treatise builds upon earlier economic thought, particularly the marginalist revolution of Jevons, Menger, and Walras, which shifted focus from labor theory of value to subjective valuation. However, Knight extends this by emphasizing the temporal dimension of choice—moving beyond static snapshots to dynamic planning. His notion of "detached judgments of need or value" contrasts with the hedonistic calculus of Bentham, which often treated pleasures as instantaneous sums.

The genealogy also connects to Keynes' effective demand and liquidity preference, where long-term expectations shape current investment. Knight's "long-run point of view" can be seen as a precursor to intertemporal optimization models developed by Ramsey, Cass, and Koopmans. Moreover, his recognition of want conflict anticipates game-theoretic formulations of intra-personal decision-making (e.g., Strotz's time inconsistency).

Historically, Knight wrote during a period of industrialization and urbanization, where individuals faced complex choices about work, leisure, and consumption. His critique of "immediate desires" likely responded to both Marxist critiques of alienation (where workers consume only what they can afford immediately) and emerging consumer culture that stimulated impulsive buying.

A multi-week trap in interpreting Knight is to assume he advocates for perfect foresight or complete rationality. Instead, he acknowledges uncertainty and emphasizes the role of judgment under conditions where probabilities cannot be calculated. This nuance is crucial for understanding his later work on risk, uncertainty, and profit.
