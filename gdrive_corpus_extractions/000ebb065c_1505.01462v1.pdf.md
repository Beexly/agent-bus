<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy is retro-causal in the sense that later minimax theory retroactively explains why earlier pairwise-comparison systems succeeded or failed depending on their implicit comparison graph. The historical root is Thurstone’s law of comparative judgment [PAGE 2], [PAGE 5], followed by Bradley-Terry-Luce [PAGE 2], [PAGE 5], and later Plackett-Luce extensions for multi-item choices [PAGE 9]. These models treat latent quality differences as the signal and noise as a probabilistic distortion. The paper’s modern contribution is to replace heuristic rating updates with a minimax operator whose leading behavior is controlled by the Laplacian spectrum.

A second lineage comes from statistical decision theory. The lower bounds use Fano’s method and packing sets [PAGE 21]. The paper explicitly cites Tsybakov-style minimax analysis [PAGE 21]. The Gilbert-Varshamov bound supplies binary packings with controlled Hamming distance [PAGE 21]. The upper bounds use convex M-estimation, strong convexity, and Hanson-Wright concentration [PAGE 23]–[PAGE 25]. This genealogy matters because it shows that the error is not caused merely by “small samples”; it is caused by the interaction of design spectrum, curvature, and noise magnitude.

A third lineage is spectral graph theory. The paper uses the scaled Laplacian \(L\), its eigenvalues, pseudo-inverse, and trace constraints [PAGE 6], [PAGE 35]. The historical trap is to treat all connected graphs as equivalent. The source refutes this: complete graph, expander, and star can be optimal, while path, cycle, and barbell can be strictly suboptimal [PAGE 12]–[PAGE 13]. For example, the path has \(\lambda_2(L)=\Theta(1/d^3)\), producing an upper risk \(O(d^4/n)\) and a lower risk \(\Omega(d^3/n)\) [PAGE 12]. This is a multi-week trap for experiment designers: a graph may look connected and balanced, yet its low-frequency modes make certain quality contrasts extremely expensive to estimate.

A fourth lineage is crowdsourcing and human computation. The paper cites Amazon Mechanical Turk and other crowdsourcing settings [PAGE 1]. The retro-causal insight is that worker-level noise is not an inconvenience; it is part of the observation operator. The empirical MTurk section shows that ordinal tasks often have lower per-response error and lower completion time than cardinal tasks [PAGE 18]. This undermines the intuitive belief that cardinal responses are automatically richer. The historical trap is to equate response format richness with statistical efficiency. The paper shows that the relevant comparison is the per-observation noise parameter after accounting for the elicitation process.

The longest multi-week trap is the “data-processing inequality” illusion. If cardinal scores could be converted into ordinal comparisons without changing noise, ordinal estimators could never outperform cardinal estimators. The source says this conversion assumption is false [PAGE 16]. The genealogy therefore retroactively warns against design decisions made by assuming that one measurement modality dominates another solely by representational format. The correct lineage is: identify the generative operator, estimate its noise and curvature, inspect the comparison graph, then choose the elicitation mode.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under time pressure, experimental designers often revert to familiar elicitation habits. In this domain, the familiar habit is to ask for numeric scores because they feel precise, or to reuse a sparse comparison graph because it is cheap. The source warns that ordinal elicitation can be faster: in the MTurk comparison, ordinal tasks often had lower median completion time than cardinal tasks [PAGE 18]. The regression is therefore twofold: designers may choose cardinal responses because they fear binary data are too coarse, while simultaneously choosing a poor comparison topology because it seems operationally convenient. Both habits can increase error. The first habit ignores the cardinal-versus-ordinal noise tradeoff [PAGE 19]. The second habit ignores the spectral optimality condition \(1/\lambda_2(L)=\Theta(d)\) [PAGE 11].

Counter-trap: the counter-strategy is to make the design recursive. First, estimate or pilot the noise parameters \(\sigma\) and \(\sigma_c\). Second, verify that the response function \(F\) satisfies strong log-concavity on the relevant bounded interval [PAGE 4]. Third, compute the scaled Laplacian for candidate comparison graphs and reject graphs whose inverse spectral quantities are too large. Fourth, if both cardinal and ordinal modes are feasible, compare the decision quantities \(\sigma^2\overline{\ell}(\sigma,B)\) and \(\sigma_c^2\), rather than relying on intuition about “more information” [PAGE 19]. This recursive loop prevents the team from mistaking representational richness for estimation efficiency.

Recursive theory of mind also applies to worker behavior. Workers may choose faster strategies, produce correlated errors, or interpret tasks differently depending on interface format. The source’s raw MTurk experiments show that direct ordinal responses can have lower error than cardinal responses converted into ordinal comparisons [PAGE 18]. A sophisticated designer should therefore model the worker as part of the noise operator, not as an ideal scalar quantizer. If workers are fatigued, distracted, or strategically simplifying, the effective \(F\) and \(\sigma\) change. The counter-trap is to keep the comparison graph robust to such changes by avoiding low-spectral-gap designs that amplify worker noise into large latent-score variance.

Clock: time is a hard constraint. The paper reports that ordinal tasks often require less time than cardinal tasks [PAGE 18]. In a fixed wall-clock experiment, lower per-item latency can permit more comparisons, effectively increasing \(n\). However, the minimax theorems are stated for fixed design sample size \(n\) [PAGE 3], [PAGE 7]. Therefore the clock does not change the asymptotic \(d/n\) or \(d^2/n\) scalings; it changes the feasible operating point within those scalings. If the clock forces fewer samples, the safest countermeasure is not to switch blindly to cardinal scores, but to increase spectral quality: prefer complete-like, expander-like, or star-like graphs over path-like or barbell-like graphs [PAGE 12]–[PAGE 13].

A final recursive habit is publication pressure: teams may overgeneralize the theorem beyond its assumptions. The source explicitly limits guarantees to strongly log-concave \(F\), bounded \(w^\star\), connected fixed-design comparison graphs, and \(m=O(1)\) for multi-item choices [PAGE 4], [PAGE 5], [PAGE 10]. The counter-trap is to include assumption audits in every design review: boundedness, curvature, connectivity, sample threshold, and modality noise must all be checked before applying the scaling rules.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather factors do not directly appear as variables in the paper’s formal theorem. They can veto a transfer of the theorem only if they change the mathematical assumptions. The adjudication proof is by assumption-violation contrapositive.

Assumption 1: fixed design and connected comparison graph.  
The theorem requires a connected comparison graph, because otherwise the Laplacian has more than one zero eigenvalue and some contrasts are not identifiable [PAGE 6]. Formal proof: if the graph is disconnected, there exists a nonzero vector \(v\) supported on one component with \(Lv=0\) and \(\langle 1,v\rangle\ne 0\). Then \(w^\star\) and \(w^\star+cv\) induce the same comparison probabilities on all observed edges for suitable \(c\), so no estimator can separate them. Therefore any physical or weather condition that removes comparisons across components, or makes some cross-component comparisons unobservable, vetoes the theorem’s applicability.

Assumption 2: bounded latent scores.  
The source proves that without bounded scores the minimax risk is infinite [PAGE 36]. Formal proof: for any nonzero \(w^\star\), the event that the higher-quality item wins every comparison has probability at least \(2^{-n}\) [PAGE 36]. Under that event, \(w^\star\) is indistinguishable from \(cw^\star\) for all \(c\ge 0\). Since the error can be made arbitrarily large, expected error diverges. Therefore if physical or biomechanical conditions create arbitrarily large quality differences or unbounded latent states, the finite-risk conclusion is vetoed.

Assumption 3: strongly log-concave response function.  
The upper bound requires \(\gamma>0\) in
\[
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}[-\log F(t)]\ge \gamma
\]
on the relevant interval [PAGE 4]. If weather, fatigue, or biomechanical stress makes the response function heavy-tailed, flat-tailed, or asymmetric, then \(\gamma\) may become zero or undefined. In that case the curvature inequality used to prove
\[
v^\top\nabla^2\ell(w)v
\ge
\frac{\gamma}{\sigma^2}\|v\|_L^2
\]
fails [PAGE 24]. The MLE may no longer be strongly convex in the Laplacian geometry, and the upper bound cannot be certified.

Assumption 4: order consistency and known noise parameter.  
The model requires \(F(x)=1-F(-x)\) and a known \(\sigma>0\) [PAGE 4]. If weather creates home-field advantage, if fatigue creates asymmetric bias, or if biomechanical constraints create systematic direction effects, then the probability of \(a_i\) dominating \(b_i\) may depend on order. That violates the symmetry condition and changes the likelihood. If the effective noise is unknown or time-varying, the stated \(\sigma\)-dependent bounds no longer apply without re-estimation.

Assumption 5: \(m\)-wise restriction.  
For multi-item comparisons, the paper restricts attention to \(m=O(1)\) and assumes noise is incorporated into \(F\) [PAGE 10]. If a physical interface forces large menus, if worker working memory is overloaded, or if weather causes rushed choices with nonstationary error, the \(m\)-wise theorem cannot be extended by simply replacing \(m\) with a larger value. The source explicitly says a more refined model is required for precise tradeoffs governing \(m\) [PAGE 10].

Veto conclusion: physical, biomechanical, or weather variables do not automatically invalidate the theorem. They invalidate it only when they change the graph connectivity, destroy bounded-score identifiability, flatten the log-concavity curvature, break order symmetry, or make the noise nonstationary. If those assumptions remain valid after recalibration, the theorem’s scaling remains applicable. If any one fails, the reported minimax scaling is not guaranteed and must be re-derived under a new observation model.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifier-style veto scenarios are stated below. Each scenario gives a hypothesis, a counterfactual test, and a source-backed prediction.

1. Veto scenario: “bounded quality scores are unnecessary.”

The proof uses the event where the higher-quality item wins every comparison, which has probability at least \(2^{-n}\) [PAGE 36]. Prediction: as \(B\to\infty\), the worst-case expected squared \(\ell_2\) risk and Laplacian risk diverge. Any claim that ordinal pairwise estimation remains finite without a bound is vetoed.

2. Veto scenario: “any connected comparison graph has the same estimation difficulty.”  
Hypothesis: connectivity alone is sufficient for minimax optimality.  
Counterfactual test: compare a path graph, barbell graph, complete graph, and expander graph with the same number of items \(d\) and sample size \(n\). Compute the scaled Laplacian spectrum and run the Thurstone MLE on synthetic comparisons.  
Verification from source: the paper states that the scaled Laplacian’s spectral gap plays a fundamental role [PAGE 3]–[PAGE 4]. The path and cycle have \(\lambda_2(L)=\Theta(1/d^3)\), yielding an upper risk \(O(d^4/n)\) and lower risk \(\Omega(d^3/n)\) [PAGE 12]. The barbell also yields a suboptimal \(\Omega(d^3/n)\) lower bound [PAGE 12]–[PAGE 13]. Complete graph, constant-degree expander, and star graphs achieve \(\Theta(d^2/n)\) under the stated conditions [PAGE 12].  
Prediction: if the verifier computes \(\lambda_2(L)\), the path and barbell graphs will show error scaling worse than \(d^2/n\), while complete, expander, and star graphs will show \(d^2/n\) scaling when the sample-size threshold is satisfied. Any claim of universal connectivity-based optimality is vetoed.

3. Veto scenario: “ordinal pairwise data are always inferior to cardinal numeric scores.”  
Hypothesis: because a cardinal response carries more numerical detail, it must dominate ordinal elicitation after aggregation.  
Counterfactual test: run paired elicitation tasks where some workers provide direct ordinal comparisons and others provide cardinal scores. Convert cardinal scores to ordinal comparisons if needed, measure raw per-response error, and estimate latent quality under both modalities.  
Verification from source: the paper explicitly rejects the assumption that cardinal and ordinal measurements have the same statistical fluctuation [PAGE 16]. In the MTurk raw-data comparison, direct ordinal responses often had lower error than cardinal responses converted to ordinal [PAGE 18]. Proposition 5 shows that cardinal and ordinal minimax risks have the same \(d/n\) scaling, with different constants, so the better modality depends on \(\sigma\), \(\sigma_c\), and \(B\) [PAGE 19].
