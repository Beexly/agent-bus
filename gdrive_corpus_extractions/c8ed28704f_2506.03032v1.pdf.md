<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage runs from classical psychometrics into modern LLM agents. Item Response Theory and computerized adaptive testing are anchored in Lord’s Fisher-information selection (FSI) [PAGE 12, citing Lord, 2012] and Chang & Ying’s Kullback–Leibler information (KLI) [PAGE 12, citing Chang and Ying, 1996]. The MAAT baseline introduces active-learning informativeness [PAGE 6, citing Bi et al., 2020]. Cognitive diagnosis models extend this with DINA, matrix factorization, and neural CDMs [PAGE 12]. TestAgent positions itself as “the first application of LLMs in adaptive testing” [PAGE 1], inserting an LLM planning layer between the psychometric core and the human test-taker.

The genealogical trap is temporal: human internal states are not static. The paper assumes θ is constant during a single test [PAGE 12], but across multi-week deployments—mental-health screening, personality reassessment, or educational progress monitoring—θ itself drifts. A model trained on GPT-4-simulated interactions [PAGE 3] may capture a simulator’s stationary prior rather than a real test-taker’s evolving state, creating a retro-causal mismatch: the assessment infrastructure is optimized for a synthetic distribution that no longer governs the human subject by the time of re-testing. Fairness compounds this; the Impact Statement notes that differentiated question selection raises fairness concerns explicitly deferred to future work [PAGE 9], implying that longitudinal re-testing could systematically steer subgroups into different item exposure paths. The multi-week trap is therefore a two-way distribution shift: (1) simulator-to-real gap at deployment, and (2) human-state drift between testing occasions, both of which invalidate the fixed-θ assumption embedded in the IRT loss on page 4.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
TestAgent instantiates a recursive theory-of-mind loop. Level 0 is the test-taker’s true latent state θ_0. Level 1 is TestAgent’s cognitive-diagnosis estimate θ_t updated from S via cross-entropy [PAGE 4]. Level 2 is the test-taker’s model of TestAgent: aware that answers drive question difficulty, test-takers may guess, mislead, or overconfidently assert knowledge [PAGE 1–2, 4–5]. Level 3 is TestAgent’s meta-model of the test-taker’s strategy, implemented by AFM’s coherence checks and AM’s context-tracking for contradictions [PAGE 4–5].

Crisis habit regression occurs when stress or social pressure induces the test-taker to revert to socially desirable responses rather than authentic states [PAGE 2, citing Stein and Swan, 2019; McDonald, 2008]. The counter-trap is AM’s procedural escalation: brief or evasive answers are broken into smaller, easier probes [PAGE 18, Table 9], and overconfident answers are challenged for reasoning [PAGE 5]. However, this counter-trap consumes clock cycles. The clock is the finite test horizon T and the question bank |Q|; each AFM/AM re-probe burns a step without advancing θ estimation. If the test-taker repeatedly triggers anomaly handling, the clock expires before θ_T stabilizes, and the adaptive advantage—“20% fewer questions” [PAGE 7]—is eroded. The recursion is unstable precisely when the test-taker’s counter-model of TestAgent is most sophisticated: deliberate misdirection is detected only after context accumulates across multiple questions on the same topic [PAGE 5], which requires spare clock time that an abbreviated test may not provide.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
