<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s core claim is not that a second-year coach is magically better. The paper tests whether the Year-Two Effect is an observable statistical regularity after controlling for schedule difficulty, conference change, prior-recruit talent, school fixed effects, and season fixed effects. The derivation has five hops. First, the authors define the object: the “expected improvement by an athletic program during a head coach’s second season at the helm” [PAGE 1]. Second, they compute mean season-to-season changes by tenure: Year 1 is −1.84% in WIN% and −0.532 in SRS, while Year 2 is +5.76% in WIN% and +2.026 SRS rating points [PAGE 7]. Third, they estimate two-way fixed-effects panel models with school effects \(e_i\) and season effects \(h_t\), where the basic form is \(y_{it}=X'_{it}\beta+e_i+h_t+u_{it}\) [PAGE 6]. In the WIN% model, the Year-2 coefficient is 0.1042 with \(p \lt 0.001\); in the SRS model, it is 3.5429 with \(p \lt 0.001\) [PAGE 8]. The authors interpret this as: “a program can expect to improve on its winning percentage from the prior season by approximately 10% during the head coach’s second season” and “a second-year coach will generally see his team improve by 3.5 points in the ratings” [PAGE 8]. Fourth, they examine mechanisms: recruiting rank improves by about eight to nine spots in Year 2, while Year 1 recruiting rank worsens by three to four spots [PAGE 9]; second-year teams commit nearly 0.23 fewer offensive turnovers per game [PAGE 10]; penalties show no significant tenure effect [PAGE 10]. Fifth, they add replacement-type dummies and a regression-to-the-mean variable. The largest Year-2 effects occur after a coach was fired for on-field performance reasons: +13.42% WIN% and +4.9 SRS points [PAGE 11]. But after including REG MEAN, the Year-2 WIN% coefficient falls from 0.1042 to 0.0790 and becomes “just slightly larger than the significant effects also witnessed in years three” and four [PAGE 12]. REG MEAN itself is −0.4590 with \(p \lt 0.001\) [PAGE 12].

The gray-zone audit is therefore that the Year-Two Effect is real as a conditional association but is not a clean causal coach-magic variable. It is partly mean reversion, partly predecessor-exit composition, partly recruiting improvement, and partly turnover reduction. The paper explicitly frames the media belief against the “availability heuristic” [PAGE 15] and asks whether the public is “falling victim to an availability heuristic” [PAGE 2]. The replacement-type models are decisive: after a coach leaves for a new job, only Year 1 is significant, and it is negative: WIN% declines by 13.07% and SRS by 4.7 points [PAGE 12]. After a performance firing, Year 2 is the largest positive effect. After retirement, Year 2 is significant but Year 4 is slightly larger in WIN% [PAGE 11 and PAGE 12]. Thus the gray zone is not “does Year 2 exist?” but “for whom, relative to what baseline, and after what mean-reversion control?” The authors also note that penalties do not behave as a discipline proxy: the penalty model has within \(R^2=0.007\) and \(F=0.795\), and “none of the variables were significant predictors of a team’s penalties” [PAGE 10]. The turnover model is stronger, with Year-2 coefficient −0.229 and \(p=0.0005\) [PAGE 10]. The causal chain is therefore layered: predecessor exit type → prior performance extremity → mean reversion → recruiting class improvement → ball security → observed Year-2 improvement. Any single-layer claim that skips those layers overstates the evidence.

The paper’s measurement units are explicit. WIN% is a dimensionless proportion; when multiplied by 100 it is percentage points. SRS is points better or worse than an average team, with 0.0 average; SOS is points above or below average, also with 0.0 average [PAGE 5]. PENYDSPG is penalty yards per game; OTOVPG is offensive turnovers per game; FRRk, SORk, JRRk, and SRRk are ordinal national recruiting ranks, where lower rank is better [PAGE 5 and PAGE 9 note 5]. \(p\)-values are probabilities in \([0,1]\); \(R^2\) is dimensionless.

The general two-way fixed-effects specification is
\[
y_{it}=X'_{it}\beta+e_i+h_t+u_{it},
\quad t\in\{1,\dots,T\},\ i\in\{1,\dots,N\},
\]
where \(e_i\) is the school fixed effect and \(h_t\) is the season fixed effect [PAGE 6]. The within transformation is
\[
(y_{it}-\bar y_{i.})=(x_{it}-\bar x_{i.})'\beta+(\varepsilon_{it}-\bar\varepsilon_{i.}),
\]
with school means \(\bar y_{i.}\), \(\bar x_{i.}\), and \(\bar\varepsilon_{i.}\) [PAGE 6]. The paper states that “the two-way fixed effects model implies that unobserved heterogeneities in both the time periods (e.g., the seasons) and the programs were controlled for” [PAGE 6].

Equation 3 is the main performance model:
\[
\begin{aligned}
y_{it}-y_{i,t-1}
={}&\beta_1\mathrm{COACHTENURE}_{it}
+\beta_2(\mathrm{SOS}_{it}-\mathrm{SOS}_{i,t-1})
+\beta_3\mathrm{NEWCONF}_{it}\\
&+\beta_4(\mathrm{JRRk}_{it}-\mathrm{JRRk}_{i,t-1})
+\beta_5(\mathrm{SRRk}_{it}-\mathrm{SRRk}_{i,t-1})
+e_i+h_t+u_{it}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(y\) is the change in WIN% or SRS from the prior season [PAGE 7]. In Table 5, the WIN% Year-2 coefficient is 0.1042, S.E. 0.0217, \(t=4.9230\), \(p \lt 0.001\); SRS Year-2 is 3.5429, S.E. 0.7144, \(t=4.9590\), \(p \lt 0.001\) [PAGE 8]. where \(y\) is the change in FRRk [PAGE 9]. For PENYDSPG, Year 2 is −1.809 with \(p=0.3230\); no tenure dummy is significant, within \(R^2=0.007\), \(F=0.795\) [PAGE 10]. \[
\mathrm{REG MEAN}_{it}
=\mathrm{WIN\%}_{i,t-1}

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a collision between a media truism and panel econometrics. The paper begins from the claim that the Year-Two Effect is “recycled and repeated nearly any time a second-year Division I football coach manages to find themselves on the positive end of a year-two turnaround” [PAGE 1]. Its intellectual ancestors are threefold. First is the anecdotal/popular-press lineage: Connelly (2016) reported that second-year coaches improved by 3.0 adjusted points per game, but also observed that “teams that had suffered from bad performances in the past tended to improve dramatically, regardless of coach year” [PAGE 3]. Hokanson (2008) provided case-list reasoning about schemes, attitudes, and optimism [PAGE 3]. Second is the peer-reviewed coaching-succession lineage: Dohrn, Lopez, and Reinhardt (2015) found new coaches improved Sagarin ratings and winning percentages over one-, two-, and four-season windows, with mid-revenue programs showing the strongest second-year pattern [PAGE 3]. Maxcy (2013) found first-season declines after replacement and a reversal in recruiting rankings in year two after a fired coach was replaced [PAGE 3]. Third is the recruiting-to-performance lineage: Herda et al. (2009) found recruiting metrics explained up to 45% of the variance in teams’ Sagarin rankings [PAGE 4]; Caro (2012) linked star ratings to conference winning percentages; Bergman and Logan (2016) estimated that a five-star recruit was worth more than $150,000 in expected bowl game revenues [PAGE 4]. Huml et al. (2019) linked second-season coaching controls to recruiting improvements [PAGE 3].

The multi-week and multi-season traps are substantial. The first is the aggregation trap: this paper uses season-level observations, not week-level panels, so it cannot prove weekly momentum, weekly practice adaptation, or within-season learning curves. A coach could improve week to week while the season aggregate remains noisy. The second is the mean-reversion trap: a program coming off an extreme low has “nowhere to go but up,” and a program coming off an extreme high has “nowhere to go but down.” The paper explicitly warns that regression to the mean “has been positioned as a potential influencer of second-season success” [PAGE 10], and its REG MEAN model confirms that mean reversion absorbs much of the Year-2 magnitude [PAGE 12]. The third is the survivorship trap: only coaches who survive to Year 2 enter the Year-2 comparison; the sample contains over 230 coaching changes, with mean and median service lengths of 5.87 and 4 seasons [PAGE 13]. The fourth is the recruiting-lag trap: Year 1 recruiting rank worsens by three to four spots, then Year 2 improves by eight to nine spots [PAGE 9], but the on-field payoff of a recruiting class is delayed and heterogeneous. The fifth is the contract-clock trap: 25% of changes occurred after fourth seasons, 14% after third, and 13% after fifth [PAGE 13], so the “Year-Two Effect” is embedded in a survival process that selects which coaches are observed at all. The sixth is the multiple-comparison trap: many tenure dummies, four replacement types, two primary outcomes, and two mechanism outcomes create a large inferential surface; the Year-2 result is strong, but the Year-3 and Year-4 results are close enough that “Year 2 is uniquely special” is a fragile claim once REG MEAN is included [PAGE 12].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive belief loop is: media and fans expect a Year-2 jump; administrators write contracts and buyout clauses around that expectation; coaches know the benchmark and prioritize immediate recruiting and ball security; fans then update on Year 2 and either reinforce or punish the belief. The paper’s practical section makes this loop explicit: over 230 coaching changes occurred, 59% of head coaches replaced someone fired or resigned for on-field performance reasons, 26% replaced coaches leaving for new jobs, 11% replaced retirees, and 4% replaced non-performance firings [PAGE 13]. That distribution means most new coaches enter a crisis context where the Year-Two Effect is most applicable [PAGE 11]. The “crisis habit regression” is the organizational habit of firing after poor performance and then over-reading the inevitable mean rebound as coach quality. The paper’s results support a conditional version of that habit: after a performance firing, Year 2 shows +13.42% WIN% and +4.9 SRS points [PAGE 11]. But the counter-trap is equally important: after a coach leaves for a new job, Year 1 is negative (−13.07% WIN%, −4.7 SRS) and Year 2 is not significant [PAGE 12]. A rational administrator should therefore not apply the same Year-2 expectation to every transition type. The clock matters because Year 5 is negative in the basic model (−0.0117 WIN%, −0.5520 SRS, both non-significant) [PAGE 8], and the paper recommends that “initial contracts and buyout clauses should seldom extend beyond year four” [PAGE 14]. The recursive theory-of-mind failure mode is a shared belief that creates its own evaluation metric: the coach anticipates Year 2 as a referendum, the administrator anticipates donor and fan pressure, and the media anticipates a narrative. The paper’s best counter-trap is to condition expectations on predecessor exit type, prior three-year mean, and recruiting trajectory, rather than treating “Year 2” as a universal clock. The coach’s own theory of mind should also include the fact that Year 1 recruiting decline is normal and that Year 2 recruiting improvement is part of the mechanism, not proof of immediate tactical mastery [PAGE 9]. Finally, the contract clock should be evidence-contingent: escalating or de-escalating clauses based on whether the Year-Two Effect occurred may be rational, but only if the baseline includes mean reversion and replacement type [PAGE 14].

The physical/biomechanical/weather veto is decisive: the provided paper contains no weather variables, no temperature, no precipitation, no wind, no altitude, no field-surface, no travel-load, no injury, no player-load, no sprint-speed, no collision-force, and no biomechanical fatigue measure. Therefore any claim that the Year-Two Effect is caused by weather, biomechanical adaptation, injury recovery, or physical load is outside the identified model. The two-way fixed effects absorb time-invariant school differences and common season shocks [PAGE 6], but they do not absorb team-specific weather or team-specific biomechanical states. The paper’s own physical/behavioral proxies are limited: penalties are yards per game and turnovers are offensive turnovers per game [PAGE 5]. Thus the cross-layer adjudication is: behavioral discipline as measured by penalties is vetoed as a Year-2 driver; ball security as measured by turnovers survives as a candidate mechanism; weather and biomechanical explanations are vetoed from causal interpretation until the model includes those layers. A physical hypothesis could still be true in the world, but this source cannot adjudicate it. The correct proof standard is attenuation: if adding weather, injury, and load variables removes the Year-2 coefficient, the physical-layer explanation gains support; if the coefficient remains, the physical-layer explanation is demoted. The present evidence does not meet that standard, so the physical/weather layer is a veto, not a confirmed cause.
