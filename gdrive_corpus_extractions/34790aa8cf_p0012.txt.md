\frac{\mathbb E[\Pi(p^*)]-\mathbb E[\Pi(p_{\text{curr}})]}{\mathbb E[\Pi(p_{\text{curr}})]}\ge \tau,\qquad \tau=0.05,
\]

and in the example \((990.00-877.50)/877.50\approx0.128\), i.e., 12.8% [PAGE 12]. Under i.i.d. Bernoulli purchasing,

\[
\operatorname{SE}(\hat f_j)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat f_j(1-\hat f_j)}{n_j}},\qquad [\operatorname{SE}]=\text{dimensionless},
\]

so 95% confidence intervals are displayable as noted on [PAGE 11].

Breakdown/singularity regimes: (i) \(n_j\) too small such that confidence-interval half-width exceeds inter-price demand differences, making the argmax statistically indistinguishable from neighbors; (ii) \(\partial\hat f/\partial p>0\) (“quantity increases when price increases”) or \(\partial\hat f/\partial p<0\) when price decreases, defined on [PAGE 11] as an incorrect slope triggering automatic reexamination via additional sampling or finer intervals; (iii) \(c\ge p\), yielding non-positive unit margin; (iv) interpolation or extrapolation outside \([\min\mathcal P,\max\mathcal P]\), where the piecewise linear assumption is unverified; (v) segmentation so fine that per-segment \(n_j\) collapses and confidence intervals become uninformative.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The patent’s intellectual lineage descends from neoclassical demand theory (Cournot duopoly and Marshallian partial equilibrium), where price is the allocative variable and quantity demanded is a function of price. It inherits experimental design from Neyman–Pearson sample-size logic and Fisherian randomization: the dynamic sampling engine assigns customers at random to price treatments so that \(\hat f_j\) is an unbiased estimator of segment-level purchase propensity. The worked example’s 7% allocation and per-cell counts of 1,000 reflect classical power considerations for binomial proportions, though the patent leaves the confidence-interval methodology to “those skilled in the art” [PAGE 11]. It also belongs to the yield-management and online dynamic-pricing tradition (perishable inventory price optimization, A/B testing of web treatments), extended here to continuous re-sampling and automatic propagation to the webserver at step 435 [PAGE 11].

Multi-week traps arise because seasonality is listed among the supplemental variables conditioning propagated prices [PAGE 12]. A single-day or short-window experiment can alias a weekly demand cycle into a spurious price optimum; re-running with a different grid under decision box 450 [PAGE 12] across weeks risks sequential data-peeking, where the manager stops when a favorable price appears. The automatic-update threshold creates a bandit-like feedback loop: once \$8.75 is deployed to the non-experimental population, future observations no longer reflect the original experimental distribution, invalidating stationarity assumptions for subsequent re-estimation. Segment-specific optimal prices, if persisted over weeks, may also encounter reference-price effects in which customers anchor on prior prices, shifting the very demand function the system seeks to estimate.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The manager’s theory of mind about the system is recursive: the manager believes the dynamic sampling engine reveals the true profit-maximizing price, while the system assumes customers present a stable, piecewise-linear demand curve during the sampling window. Under crisis—e.g., an inconclusive experiment at decision box 450 where \$8 appears optimal among \$8–\$11 but the true optimum may be lower [PAGE 12]—the manager regresses to a crisis habit: reverting to the status-quo price of \$9.25 or intuitively compressing the grid downward (\$6.00–\$9.00) without a formal stopping rule. This local-search counter-trap chases a finer grid around a presumed optimum while ignoring that demand may have shifted, that the true optimum could lie outside the new range, or that repeated re-sampling inflates Type I error.

The system’s own counter-trap is the 5% auto-update threshold: it blocks marginal but genuine improvements and, conversely, may deploy a sample-level winner whose realized lift regresses to the mean after winner’s-curse attenuation. The clock is the daily visitor cycle—100,000 customers per day, 7% sampled, 1,000 per price point [PAGE 12]—which sets a roughly 24-hour data-accumulation cadence. If demand drifts slower than this clock, prices grow stale; if faster, each day’s experiment fits noise. The recursive loop closes when customers observe repeated price changes and adjust their reference prices, making \(f_i\) endogenous to the system’s own past deployments and violating the static-demand assumption embedded in \(\mathbb E[\Pi_i]=m\hat f_i(p_i-c)\).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: the webserver must generate and deliver distinct price-conditioned webpages to experimental versus standard-population customers at steps 470–435 [PAGE 11]. Veto triggers: CDN or edge-cache staleness serving a superseded price, inability to segment delivery by supplemental variables (visit duration, purchase history, seasonality [PAGE 12]), or latency that degrades the purchase funnel itself. If the webserver cannot guarantee that the intended \(\hat f_j\) is measured under the intended price exposure, the measurement layer is invalid and propagation to 435 is vetoed.

Biomechanical layer: the manager at display 436 must interpret the demand function, optimal price, and confidence intervals [PAGE 11]. Veto triggers: cognitive overload when confidence intervals are wide or overlapping, misreading of the “incorrect slope” alert, or manual override of the argmax based on heuristic pricing rules rather than the objective function. Because the patent automates reexamination of non-monotonic segments but leaves the final managerial call at decision box 450 [PAGE 12], human fatigue can produce either excessive re-running (analysis paralysis) or premature deployment of a noisy optimum.

Weather/exogenous layer: seasonality, competitor price moves, supplier cost changes (\(c\)), and traffic shocks are exogenous to the sampled demand function. Veto triggers: a detected shift in \(c\) between experiment and deployment, a seasonality regime change, or an incorrect-slope anomaly [PAGE 11] that persists after finer-interval re-sampling. Any such shock invalidates the \((p-c)\) margin term and the demand estimate simultaneously.

Adjudication: the veto functions as an OR-gate across layers; if the physical, biomechanical, or exogenous layer issues a veto, the system holds the standard price and does not update at step 435. The veto is lifted only after re-sampling restores statistical power, monotonicity, and layer-specific readiness, with a new confidence-interval calculation before any subsequent propagation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 (low-conversion noise): At \(p=\$9.50\), suppose the true purchase fraction is \(f=0.02\). With \(n_j=1{,}000\), expected purchases equal 20 and \(\operatorname{SE}(\hat f)\approx0.0044\), so the 95% confidence interval spans roughly \(\pm0.0087\) in \(f\), or \(\pm8.7\) units. If the point estimate ranks \$9.50 within 5% of the current price but its confidence interval overlaps the current price’s interval, RLVR vetoes auto-update despite a favorable point estimate. Prediction: post-deployment A/A measurement shows profit lift statistically indistinguishable from zero, with realized \(\Delta\Pi<2\%\).

Scenario 2 (incorrect-slope artifact): A segment exhibits \(\hat f\) increasing as price rises over two adjacent grid points, satisfying the patent’s incorrect-slope trigger on [PAGE 11]. RLVR vetoes propagation to 435 and mandates finer intervals or re-sampling before any price in that segment is deployed. Prediction: re-sampled demand restores \(\partial\hat f/\partial p<0\); the original interior optimum is revealed as a sampling artifact, and the deployed price shifts to the nearest monotone grid point.

Scenario 3 (exogenous cost shock): The experiment identifies \(p^*=\$8.75\) at \(c=\$6\), yielding sample profit \(\$990\) on 360 units [PAGE 12]. Before deployment, variable cost rises to \(c=\$8.50\). RLVR recomputes \(\mathbb E[\Pi]=m\hat f(p-c)\): at \$8.75 the unit margin is only \$0.25, giving profit of roughly \$90 for the same demand, while lower prices may produce negative margin. RLVR vetoes the \$8.75 deployment and requires re-optimization under the new cost. Prediction: deploying \$8.75 at \(c=\$8.50\) would earn near-zero margin; the veto preserves positive economic variable(s) and forces selection of the cost-adjusted argmax or no change.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
