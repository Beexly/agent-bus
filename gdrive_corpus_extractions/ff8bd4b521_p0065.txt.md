<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under pressure—protecting a lead, facing third-and-long, or trailing late—a defensive coordinator tends to regress to the most familiar aggressive device documented here: carrying a lineman back to create a momentary numbers advantage. [PAGE 44] This is a habit loop because the stunt feels proactive, but its recurrence makes it predictable to an offense that has film on the tendency.

Recursive counter-trap: the offense, anticipating that regression, runs the parallel medium-speed motion precisely to elicit s_i = 1. [PAGE 44] If the tackle is carried back, the runner drives by him via the right-angle turn [PAGE 44]. If the defense has learned and the tackle now holds to set the edge, the runner "noses back toward the center" into the guard/center vacancy [PAGE 44]. The second-order trap: once the defense expects the cutback, the tackle over-pursues inside, and the offense returns to the edge drive. The recursion is s_tackle = 1 ⇒ edge open ⇒ defense holds edge ⇒ s_tackle = 0 ⇒ inside cutback ⇒ defense crashes inside ⇒ edge open again. There is no fixed point; the play's value is entirely in the defender's lagged belief state.

Clock layer: the medium-speed parallel run consumes game clock without proportional yardage [PAGE 44], which is advantageous when protecting a lead but a veto condition in a two-minute drill. The right-angle turn is the clock-to-yards conversion event; if the clock is insufficient to complete the probe and turn, the play cannot run as prescribed. Therefore the same habit regression that creates the tactical opportunity also imposes a temporal constraint: the offense needs the clock to diagnose s_i before executing the turn.

Veto condition: precipitation such that \mu_s < v_y^2/(g\rho) or standing water within the kicking distribution.

Veto condition: N_rushers - N_p \ge 1 or \tau_p < t_k.
