Historical and scientific lineage of the [PAGE 104] argument, read backward from Knight's 1921 correction to its classical and marginalist sources, and forward to multi-week analytical traps.

Knight positions himself against a conflation in the marginalist tradition. On [PAGE 104] he cites Taylor, loc. cit., pp. 101, 102, for the demonstration that "the fall in specific or marginal contribution is an easy inference from the law of the variation of product per unit." Taylor uses both "diminishing returns" and "diminishing productivity" "in virtually the same sense," thereby failing to bring out "the contrast between the variation of physical product and that of value product." Knight further traces this through Wieser's theory of imputation, which he says "advances it in place of the specific productivity theory, applied to industries independently, which he repudiates," with the relevant discussion deferred to p. 110 of the same volume.

Retrocausal lineage: Ricardo's differential rent theory established that marginal land determines return, but in physical terms (corn on marginal land). Jevons, Menger, and Walras translated this into marginal utility and marginal productivity in value terms. Wieser's imputation attempted to distribute value backward from final products to productive agents. Taylor's instrumental law refined the physical side. Knight's innovation on [PAGE 104] is the explicit separation: physical diminishing returns is a technical law; diminishing value productivity is the sum of technical change plus exchange-value reallocation. By refusing "the misleading terminology of factors and margins," Knight attempts to block the retroactive projection of value language onto a purely physical law.

Multi-week traps: (T1) Analysts who observe a falling physical marginal product and infer a falling value marginal product without checking price movements will misdiagnose the source of profit changes over a production cycle. (T2) Because price adjustments lag physical output shifts, a week-to-week time series may show physical product rising while value product falls, or vice versa, creating spurious serial correlation if the researcher does not model the price channel. (T3) Knight's terminological refusal creates a trap for readers: if one imports Taylor's "marginal product of the factor" language back into Knight, one collapses the very distinction he is defending. The genealogical trap is to treat "diminishing productivity" as a synonym for "diminishing returns" exactly as Taylor does, thereby erasing the price-mediated value effect that makes profit theory nontrivial.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock in the producer's estimation problem on [PAGE 104].

Knight states that "producers naturally estimate product in terms of its exchange value and not of its physical magnitude." Recursive theory of mind models what producers believe other producers believe about value during a transfer. In equilibrium, producer i anticipates that withdrawing agency from A raises P_A and that adding agency to B lowers P_B; each producer therefore discounts the physical marginal product by the expected price reaction of rivals. This is a second-order belief: I reduce my output because I expect you to reduce yours, which raises price, which validates my reduction.

Crisis habit regression: Under stress or uncertainty, producers regress to estimating product "in terms of its physical magnitude" (tonnes, hours, units) rather than exchange value. This is a habit regression to the more concrete, observable technical margin. When markets are disordered, the "organized free exchange society" assumption fails; price signals become noisy or administered, and the physical magnitude becomes the only salient cue.

Counter-trap: The counter-trap is to assume that because producers regress to physical estimation during crises, the value-productivity channel is permanently inactive. Knight's argument implies the opposite: the divergence between physical and value productivity is largest exactly when price volatility is high. Treating crisis physical estimation as the permanent decision rule is itself a trap; it ignores the reversion to exchange-value calculation once organized exchange resumes.

Clock: The clock is the temporal mismatch between the physical transfer of agency and the price adjustment. Physical output changes immediately upon transfer; P_A and P_B adjust with market-clearing latency. During the latency window, producers may observe rising physical product in A (from withdrawal) while P_A has not yet risen, creating a transient where physical and value signals conflict. The recursive theory of mind must therefore track not just what producers value, but when they update their beliefs: physical first, exchange value second, and only after the clock ticks does Knight's "added" variation become fully realized.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
