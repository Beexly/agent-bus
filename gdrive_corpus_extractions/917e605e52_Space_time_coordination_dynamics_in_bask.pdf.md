\end{cases}
\]
The source states that positive values “are represented as unity (i.e. 1) and negative values as minus unity” [PAGE 9]. For phase extraction, let \(x(t)\) stand for either \(C_{k,d}(t)\) or \(S_{k,d}(t)\). The Hilbert transform is
\[
\mathcal{H}[x](t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x(\tau)}{t-\tau}\,d\tau,
\qquad [\mathcal{H}[x]]=[x],
\]
and the analytic signal is
\[
z_x(t)=x(t)+i\mathcal{H}[x](t),
\qquad A_x(t)=|z_x(t)|,
\qquad \phi_x(t)=\arg z_x(t).
\]
The phase \(\phi_x\) is dimensionless in radians, usually reported in degrees. The relative phase between teams is
\[
\Delta\phi_d(t)=\operatorname{wrap}\!\left(\phi_{A,d}(t)-\phi_{B,d}(t)\right),
\]
with wrap into \([-180^\circ,180^\circ)\) for the paper’s histograms. The paper reports longitudinal relative phase “mean ¼ 6.98, SD ¼ 10.58” and lateral “mean ¼ 74.98, SD ¼ 39.98” [PAGE 3], and it notes that the reported means and standard deviations were computed after forcing relative-phase data into one cycle [PAGE 3]. The histograms are distributed into 30-degree bins from approximately \(-180^\circ\) to \(+180^\circ\) [PAGE 5]. The relative stretch index is not a phase; it is a metric difference. The paper’s bi-stable peaks are reported as “bins 70.4 m and 0.6 m” longitudinally and “70.2 m and 0.4 m” laterally [PAGE 7], where the OCR glyph “7” represents a minus sign, so the intended values are approximately \(-0.4\) m and \(0.6\) m, and \(-0.2\) m and \(0.4\) m. Assumptions: five players per team; calibrated common court frame; equal player weights; directional axis decomposition; narrowband oscillatory behaviour for Hilbert phase; phase forcing into one cycle; negligible deadband at \(R_d=0\). Singularity and breakdown regimes: \(\Delta\phi_d\) is undefined if either analytic amplitude \(A_{k,d}(t)\) vanishes; Hilbert phase is not identifiable for stationary or strongly broadband non-oscillatory signals; phase branch cuts near \(\pm180^\circ\) make arithmetic means invalid without circular statistics; forcing can alter reported means and SDs; \(S_{k,d}=0\) makes stretch phase undefined; \(R_d(t)=0\) makes \(b_d(t)\) undefined and can cause sign chattering under noise; missing or occluded player coordinates break the centroid and stretch-index definitions; equal-weight averaging can mask role-specific information [PAGE 11]; and the sign-change-to-possession claim is a temporal association, not an identified causal operator [PAGE 9].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit in the source. It extends the Haken–Kelso–Bunz coupled-oscillator account of human rhythmic coordination [PAGE 3], Kelso’s dynamic-patterns framework [PAGE 3], McGarry and colleagues’ dynamical self-organizing account of sport competition [PAGE 2], Palut and Zanone’s Hilbert relative-phase analysis in tennis [PAGE 3], Frencken and Lemmink’s team-kinematics work in small-sided soccer [PAGE 3], and the companion Bourbousson, Sève, and McGarry article on player–player dyads [PAGE 2]. The retrocausal genealogy is therefore not a claim that later events physically cause earlier phase states. It is an analytical lineage: an observed outcome, such as a team shot attempt or a possession change, is used retrospectively to interpret the preceding phase relation. The paper does this when it says team shots were preceded by lateral relative-phase disruption [PAGE 8] and when it says sign changes in the relative stretch index occurred “in the main shortly following change in ball possession” [PAGE 9]. The historical trap is to convert this retrospective ordering into a forward causal law. Multi-week traps follow. Week 1 trap: treat longitudinal in-phase as a universal law of basketball rather than a finding from six sequences in one contest. Week 2 trap: collapse the player–player and team–team levels into one description, despite the paper’s warning that “a common description need not necessarily mean the same description” [PAGE 5]. Week 3 trap: reify the stretch index as a tactical truth rather than a mean absolute deviation about an equal-weight centroid. Week 4 trap: read lateral relative-phase disruption as always beneficial or always harmful; the paper distinguishes individual shots after defensive lapses, where no association was observed [PAGE 8], from team shots after lateral disruption [PAGE 8, PAGE 11]. Week 5 trap: ignore circular statistics and treat degree means as ordinary linear quantities. Week 6 trap: ignore the difference between relative phase and relative stretch index; the paper itself says the information obtained depends on the indicator used [PAGE 10]. The correct genealogical reading is therefore layered: coupled-oscillator theory motivates phase analysis; sport-performance analysis supplies event coding; the Hilbert transform supplies a phase estimator; the relative stretch index supplies a difference metric; and the binary sign supplies an event-clock variable. Each layer has its own validity domain and its own failure modes.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer treats each team as a social unit whose collective behaviour emerges from individuals cooperating toward a common goal [PAGE 2]. The first-order model is: each player responds to local attacker–defender information. The second-order model is: each team responds to the other team’s collective shape and to ball possession. The third-order model is: analysts infer team intent from phase and stretch dynamics, then link those dynamics to shot attempts and possession changes. The crisis habit regression is the return to the most stable collective pattern under perturbation. In this source the strongest stable pattern is longitudinal in-phase coupling of spatial centres: the abstract reports “in-phase stabilities in both the longitudinal and lateral directions, with more stability in the longitudinal than lateral direction” [PAGE 2]. The stretch indexes also show in-phase stability in the longitudinal direction only [PAGE 6]. Under a possession change or shot event, the default habit is therefore for both teams to continue travelling basket-to-basket in a coupled way and, under man-to-man marking, to expand and contract together longitudinally. The counter-trap is the lateral disruption. The paper expected an anti-phase attraction laterally if the attacking team widened and the defending team reduced width, but it did not observe that attraction in the stretch-index relative phase [PAGE 6]. Instead, lateral spatial-centre data showed weaker in-phase attraction and “symmetrical variability about in-phase for the lateral data demonstrates repulsion from anti-phase” [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap is to label every lateral destabilization as a defensive failure. The paper reports that team shots were preceded by lateral relative-phase disruptions [PAGE 8], and that team-shot attempts were produced “seemingly just after disturbances in the relative-phase of spatial centres when measured in the lateral direction” [PAGE 11]. That is a potential affordance-creation pattern, not automatically a failure. The clock is the event ordering. Possession changes and shot attempts define an event clock; the sign of the relative stretch index changes “shortly following change in ball possession” [PAGE 9]. The recursive trap is to infer intent from timing alone. The analyst must keep three clocks separate: the physical game clock, the possession-event clock, and the phase-sign clock. The paper does not measure shot-clock pressure, player intent, or tactical instruction; it measures coordinate-derived phase and stretch variables and associates them with coded events. The theory-of-mind conclusion is therefore conditional: under crisis, habitual longitudinal in-phase coupling is the default; lateral disruption can be either an attacking counter-trap or a defensive lapse; the event clock supports temporal association but not intention.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which a lower-layer measurement failure makes a higher-layer inference non-identifiable. Physical-layer veto: the spatial centre and stretch index are functions of calibrated court coordinates. If player positions are missing, occluded, or transformed into a rotating frame, \(C_{k,d}\) and \(S_{k,d}\) are biased; if a team’s centre is stationary, the analytic amplitude \(A_{k,d}\) can approach zero and Hilbert phase becomes undefined; if the signal is broadband because of a sharp possession change, instantaneous phase can slip. Therefore no phase-based claim is admissible unless the coordinate calibration, missing-data handling, detrending, and amplitude checks are specified. Biommechanical-layer veto: \(S_{k,d}\) is a mean absolute deviation in metres. It is not force, torque, fatigue, muscle activation, or injury risk. No equation in the source contains a biomechanical force term; the source calls the spatial centre a mean position and the stretch index a mean dispersion [PAGE 11]. Therefore any inference from stretch index to fatigue or force is vetoed. If player roles are unequal, the equal-weight centroid can mask the player who carries the most tactical information; the paper explicitly raises this limitation: the spatial centre is defined “without regard to the information content of each individual player” [PAGE 11]. Weather-layer veto: the source reports no weather variables, no wind, no rain, no temperature, and no outdoor venue covariates. For an indoor basketball contest, weather cannot be invoked as an explanatory layer; for an outdoor extrapolation, wind and rain would alter ball flight, traction, and player movement, so the same Hilbert and stretch operators would not be transferable without a weather covariate model. The veto proof is thus a conditional identifiability proof: if the measured coordinate process does not satisfy the physical calibration and oscillatory assumptions, the phase operator is not identified; if the hypothesis requires force or fatigue, the stretch operator is not identified; if the setting is outdoor and weather is unmodeled, the coordination operator is not identified. The source itself supports this caution by insisting that different metrics assess different properties of team behaviour [PAGE 10] and that further work on appropriate performance metrics is required [PAGE 11].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: phase-surrogate veto. Construct phase-randomized surrogates of each team’s spatial-centre time series that preserve the power spectrum but destroy cross-team coupling. Compute the circular resultant
\[
R_{\Delta\phi}=\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{i\Delta\phi_d(t_n)}\right|
\]
for longitudinal and lateral relative phase. Prediction: the observed longitudinal \(R_{\Delta\phi}\) should exceed the 95th percentile of the surrogate distribution, and the lateral value should be weaker, consistent with the reported stronger longitudinal in-phase stability [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. Veto rule: if the observed concentration is not above the surrogate threshold, the in-phase claim is an artifact of forcing, binning, or shared trend, and the phase interpretation is vetoed. Scenario 2: role-weight and leave-one-player-out veto. Recompute \(C_{k,d}\) and \(S_{k,d}\) with unequal player weights \(w_{k,i}(t)\), where \(\sum_i w_{k,i}=1\), and with each player removed in turn. Prediction: if the team-level in-phase longitudinal coupling and the relative-stretch bi-stability are robust, they should survive these perturbations; the reported bi-stable peaks near \(-0.4\) m and \(0.6\) m longitudinally and \(-0.2\) m and \(0.4\) m laterally [PAGE 7] should remain identifiable within uncertainty. Veto rule: if the bi-stability disappears under plausible role weights, then the effect is driven by the equal-weight averaging assumption rather than by team coordination. Scenario 3: possession-event-clock veto. For every possession change, record the lag to the next sign change of \(R_d(t)\). Compare the observed lag distribution with a null distribution built by placing pseudo-possession changes at random times matched for sequence duration and event rate. Prediction: observed sign changes should cluster after true possession changes more than after pseudo-events, matching the source statement that sign changes occurred “in the main shortly following change in ball possession” [PAGE 9]. Veto rule: if the observed lag distribution is indistinguishable from the shuffled-event null, the possession-to-shape-change association is vetoed. A fourth verifiable check, nested under Scenario 3, is to test whether team-shot attempts are preceded by lateral relative-phase disruption more often than individual-shot attempts; the source predicts team shots after lateral disruption [PAGE 8, PAGE 11], while individual shots after defensive lapses showed no association [PAGE 8]. If that contrast fails, the specific shot-type interpretation is vetoed. These counterfactuals are verifiable because each specifies a metric, a null model, a threshold, and a predicted direction of effect.
