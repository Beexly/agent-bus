Tolman's 1932 treatise fundamentally redefines the "image" not as a mental picture, but as a discriminanda-expectation generated by internal stimuli. On [PAGE 1] (p. 255), he states that when I introspect an image, I am indicating "the presence of unique momentary discriminanda-expectations" and declaring it to be like or unlike "such and such external stimuli." The core mechanism is behavioral reductionism: the subject "run[s] about in a discrimination-box," pairing internal stimuli with carefully chosen external ones to see where discrimination fails. This implies that an image has no unique essence; it is entirely defined by its functional equivalence to sensory inputs under specific conditions.

The gray-zone arises in his distinction between images and sensations. Tolman notes we call expectations "images" when "the stimuli which arouse them are obviously not peripherally present then and there." However, he immediately flags the breakdown case: hallucinations. In a hallucination, the internal stimulus produces discriminanda-expectations so potent that they mimic peripheral presence, collapsing the image/sensation boundary. Furthermore, the second point—that images differ by some "functional significance"—is left intentionally vague, suggesting a latent variable related to cognitive mapping or purposive behavior rather than raw sensation.

A multi-hop derivation links this to modern predictive coding: the brain maintains generative models (discriminanda-expectations) updated by prediction errors (external stimuli). An "image" is simply a high-confidence prior activated without bottom-up input, while a "sensation" involves strong top-down priors combined with intense bottom-up drive. Tolman’s "discrimination-box" is effectively a behavioral assay for measuring the strength and structure of these priors. The system breaks down when the prior is so strong it overrides sensory evidence (hallucination) or when sensory evidence is ambiguous enough that the prior dominates perception (illusion).

The historical lineage is crucial. Tolman was reacting against both Watsonian S-R psychology, which denied internal states, and Introspectionist psychology, which treated images as mystical entities. By reducing images to discriminanda-expectations, he paved the way for cognitive maps and latent learning. A multi-week trap for modern researchers is to revert to dualism—treating "imagery" as a separate neural module—when Tolman insists it's just a variation in the stimulus source within a unified expectational framework.

From a recursive theory of mind perspective, humans might use this framework defensively: labeling something an "image" allows us to compartmentalize it as non-threatening or hypothetical, whereas calling it a "sensation" forces immediate action. Crisis regression could involve treating memories (images) as if they were present threats (sensations), leading to maladaptive behaviors.

Finally, cross-layer vetoes prove critical. Biomechanical limits—like eye movement fatigue or muscle tension—can veto the generation of a stable image. If you try to visualize a complex object but your oculomotor system is exhausted, the discriminanda-expectation may fail to form. Weather or environmental noise can similarly veto the precision of external discrimination tasks, altering the baseline against which internal images are compared.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Tolman’s framework can be formalized using Information Theory and Bayesian inference, translating his behavioral terms into rigorous operators.

Let \( S \) be the space of all possible stimuli (both external \( S_{ext} \) and internal \( S_{int} \)). 
Let \( D \) be the space of discriminanda (perceptual outcomes/features).
An individual’s state at time \( t \) is characterized by a probability distribution over expected discriminanda, given a stimulus source.

**Definition 1: Discriminanda-Expectation Operator**
For any stimulus \( s \in S \), the organism computes an expectation:
\[ E_s = \mathbb{P}(D | s) \]
where \( E_s \) is a vector in the feature space of discriminanda. 

**Definition 2: Image vs. Sensation via Stimulus Provenance**
[PAGE 1] states images arise from internal stimuli absent peripherally. Let \( \delta(s) \) be a binary indicator: \( \delta(s)=1 \) if \( s \) is currently peripherally active, \( 0 \) otherwise.
\[ \text{Image}(E) \iff \exists s_{int} \in S \text{ s.t. } E = \mathbb{P}(D | s_{int}) \land \delta(s_{int}) = 0 \]
\[ \text{Sensation}(E) \iff \exists s_{ext} \in S \text{ s.t. } E = \mathbb{P}(D | s_{ext}) \land \delta(s_{ext}) = 1 \]

**The Discrimination-Box Metric**
Tolman describes pairing internal/external stimuli to find indistinguishable pairs. Define a distance metric \( \rho \) on the expectation space (e.g., Kullback-Leibler divergence or Hellinger distance):
\[ \rho(E_a, E_b) = D_{KL}(\mathbb{P}(D|a) \| \mathbb{P}(D|b)) \]
A subject "runs about in a discrimination-box" by searching for \( s_{ext} \) such that:
\[ \rho(E_{int}, E_{ext}) < \epsilon \]
where \( \epsilon \) is the subject's just-noticeable difference threshold. The image is thus operationally defined by the set of external stimuli \( \{s_{ext}\} \) that fall within this tolerance.

**Breakdown Regimes & Hallucination**
[PAGE 1] cites hallucinations where the distinction breaks down. Formally, this occurs when the internal expectation becomes indistinguishable from a strong external drive, violating the provenance constraint:
\[ \lim_{\text{prior strength} \to \infty} \mathbb{P}(D | s_{int}) = \mathbb{P}(D | s_{ext}) \quad \text{even if } \delta(s_{ext})=0 \]
In a Bayesian Predictive Coding framework, let \( y \) be sensory input and \( \hat{y} \) the prediction. Precision-weighted prediction error is \( \Pi(y - \hat{y}) \). A hallucination corresponds to the precision of the prior \( \Pi_{prior} \) overwhelming the precision of the sensory error \( \Pi_{sensory} \), causing the posterior to align entirely with the internal model regardless of peripheral silence.

**Units and Assumptions**
- Units: Expectations are distributions over feature vectors (dimensionless probabilities). Distance \( \rho \) is measured in nats or bits.
- Assumption: The mapping from stimulus to discriminanda-expectation is stationary during the short-term experimental trial.
- Singularity: The system becomes ill-defined if \( \epsilon \to 0 \) (perfect discrimination) or if the feature space is infinite-dimensional without regularization.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman's "purposive behaviorism" did not emerge in a vacuum; it was a direct retroactive correction to the impasses of 1920s psychology. Edward Titchener’s structuralism had attempted to dissect consciousness into elements like "images" and "sensations," relying on strict introspective protocols. Tolman, trained under this regime but influenced by Gestalt principles and behaviorism, realized that introspection was inherently unstable because it lacked a public, verificatory criterion. By introducing the "discrimination-box," Tolman grafted a behaviorist verification method onto mentalistic terminology. This created a genealogical loop: he used the language of inner experience ("image") but drained it of private substance, filling it instead with publicly observable conditional relations (discriminanda-expectations).

Historically, this move anticipated the cognitive revolution by three decades. B.F. Skinner would later reject any reference to mediators, but Tolman kept the mediator by making it functional rather than substantive. His concept of the "cognitive map" later grew directly out of this definition of imagery as a spatially organized expectation of discriminanda.

A persistent multi-week trap for contemporary neuroscience is the "neural correlate" fallacy. Researchers often search for a specific "imagery area" in the brain (e.g., early visual cortex reactivation). While neural overlap exists, Tolman’s framework warns that identifying the *source* of the stimulus (internal vs. external) cannot be done purely by looking at the resulting activation pattern alone; one must look at the behavioral history of discrimination and the absence/presence of peripheral input. The trap is assuming that similar firing patterns imply identical psychological phenomena, ignoring the crucial contextual variable of stimulus provenance.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When analyzing how subjects categorize their own experiences as "images" or "sensations," we enter a recursive theory of mind domain. The subject is not only generating a discriminanda-expectation but also modeling their own cognitive state to report it. Tolman’s phrase "I indicate to you... I run about in a discrimination-box" reveals this meta-cognitive layer.

**Crisis Habit Regression:** Under stress or trauma, the sophisticated ability to tag an expectation as "internal/image" degrades. The subject regresses to a primitive S-R mode where any vivid discriminanda-expectation is treated as an imminent external threat (a sensation). This is the mechanism behind flashbacks: a memory (internal stimulus) triggers a full autonomic response as if the event were happening now (external stimulus). The "provenance check" (\( \delta(s) \)) fails due to amygdala hijack overriding prefrontal monitoring.

**Counter-Trap Strategy:** To counter this, therapeutic techniques (like exposure therapy or grounding exercises) essentially force a recalibration of the discrimination-box. They deliberately pair the internal stimulus with safe external stimuli, teaching the nervous system that \( \rho(E_{int}, E_{safe\_ext}) \) is low, thereby re-establishing the "image" classification and reducing panic.

**Clock Dependency:** The stability of the image/sensation distinction relies heavily on temporal context. Rapidly flickering lights or strobe effects can blur the line, creating illusions where internal predictions and external flashes fuse. Similarly, in flow states, the "clock" of self-monitoring slows, and actions become automated; the distinct categorization of "this is an imagined movement" versus "this is an actual sensation" might temporarily dissolve, leading to highly efficient but dissociated performance.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
While Tolman’s logic is psychologically sound, its implementation in a biological agent is subject to hard physical and biomechanical vetoes.

**Biomechanical Veto:** Generating a stable "image" (discriminanda-expectation) often requires subtle motor engagement. For example, visualizing a distant object might involve unconsciously adjusting the lens accommodation or inducing micro-saccades. If the oculomotor muscles are fatigued or paralyzed, the internal stimulus may fail to generate the corresponding fine-grained discriminanda-expectation. The subject might attempt to "look inward," but the lack of proprioceptive feedback from the eyes results in a "black screen" rather than a vivid image. Thus, muscular capacity acts as a veto on the formation of certain classes of images.

**Environmental/Weather Veto:** Tolman assumes a standardized discrimination box. However, extreme environmental conditions can veto the validity of the comparison. Suppose an experiment relies on comparing an internal color image to external monochromatic lights. If the laboratory lighting suddenly shifts to a high-intensity sodium vapor glow (rich in long wavelengths), the external discrimination space is distorted. The subject’s ability to accurately pair internal stimuli with external ones collapses. The weather or ambient spectrum imposes a hard filter on the discriminanda-expectation space, potentially forcing an erroneous conclusion about the nature of the image.

**Neurochemical Veto:** Pharmacological interventions can chemically alter the mapping function \( \mathbb{P}(D|s) \). Psychoactive drugs might heighten the vividness of internal stimuli (increasing \( \rho \) sensitivity) or dampen external responses. If a sedative blunts all discriminanda-responses, even strong internal stimuli will yield weak expectations, failing the criteria for being classified as anything distinct from background noise.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
