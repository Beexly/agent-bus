<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s core operator is the strategic-form game \(G=\langle P,(S_i)_{i\in I_n},(u_i)_{i\in I_n}\rangle\), where \(S_i\) is the nonempty pure-strategy set and \(u_i:S=\prod_i S_i\to \mathbb R\) is the payoff/utility function [PAGE 8-9]. A Nash Equilibrium is
\[
u_i(s_i^*,s_{-i}^*)\ge u_i(s_i,s_{-i}^*)\quad \forall i,\ \forall s_i\in S_i,
\]
with payoff units in dimensionless utility units and strategies dimensionless [PAGE 10]. The source states: “No player can unilaterally profitably deviate” [PAGE 10]. Assumptions: finite or compact strategy sets, complete information where stated, and rationality in the classical model. Breakdown: if payoffs are discontinuous, strategy sets fail compactness/convexity, or the game is outside the Debreu-Fan-Glicksberg hypotheses [PAGE 14-15], the pure-NE existence claim can fail; Nash’s mixed existence theorem still requires finite strategy sets [PAGE 12].

Mixed strategies use \(\sigma_i\in\Delta(S_i)\), \(\sigma(s_i)\ge0\), \(\sum_{s_i}\sigma_i(s_i)=1\), and
\[
\tilde u_i(\sigma_i,\sigma_{-i})=\mathbb E(u_i)=\sum_{s\in S}\left(\prod_i \sigma_i(s_i)\right)u_i(s),
\]
with probability dimensionless and expected payoff in utility units [PAGE 13]. Assumptions: classical probability measure over \(S_i\). Breakdown: p-adic “probability” is not this object; the source explicitly allows “values greater than 1 or even negative” in p-adic probability [PAGE 41], so the classical expectation formula cannot be transplanted without changing the codomain.

The Price of Anarchy is
\[
\mathrm{PoA}:=\frac{\max_{s\in S}\sum_i u_i(s)}{\min_{s\in S^*}\sum_i u_i(s)},
\]
where \(S^*\) is the set of Nash equilibria [PAGE 13]. Units: dimensionless ratio. Assumptions: \(S^*\neq\varnothing\) and the social-welfare denominator is nonzero. Breakdown: if social welfare at the worst NE is zero or negative, the ratio is not a meaningful efficiency measure without a separate normalization.

Potential games require
\[
\Phi(s_i,s_{-i})-\Phi(s'_i,s_{-i})=u_i(s_i,s_{-i})-u_i(s'_i,s_{-i}),
\]
with \(\Phi\) in utility units [PAGE 15]. Assumptions: a real-valued potential exists over \(S\). Breakdown: without \(\Phi\), best-response dynamics need not converge; the theorem that every finite potential game converges to a pure NE [PAGE 15] does not transfer to non-potential games.

Mean-field games use the coupled HJB and Fokker-Planck system
\[
-\partial_t u(x,t)+H(x,\nabla u)=f(x,m),
\]
\[
\partial_t m-\nabla\cdot\big(m\nabla H_p(x,\nabla u)\big)=0,
\]
where \(u(x,t)\) is the value function, \(H\) the Hamiltonian, \(f(x,m)\) the cost in terms of population density \(m(x,t)\), and \(H_p\) the gradient of the Hamiltonian with respect to momentum [PAGE 16]. Units: \(u\) in utility units; \(m(x,t)\) in probability density per state volume; time in seconds; \(H\) and \(f\) in utility per time. Assumptions: enough smoothness for the derivatives and a well-posed forward-backward coupling. Breakdown: non-smooth Hamiltonians, non-unique fixed points, or failure of the Schauder/Banach fixed-point hypotheses [PAGE 16] can destroy uniqueness or even existence of a classical solution.

Replicator dynamics are
\[
\frac{\dot p_i(t)}{p_i}=h_i(p)=u_i(p)-\bar u(p),
\]
or
\[
\dot p_i(t)=p_i\big((Ap)_i-pAp^{\top}\big)=g_i(p),
\]
with \(p\in\Delta=\{p_i\ge0,\sum_i p_i=1\}\), probability dimensionless, and time in seconds [PAGE 22-23]. Assumptions: a large population, continuous time, differentiable interior orbits, and a fixed payoff matrix \(A\). Breakdown: the log derivative is singular at \(p_i=0\); boundary faces are invariant [PAGE 24]; non-self-sustained dynamics need not have a unique interior rest point [PAGE 25]; and limit cycles can occur outside simple potential or stable regimes [PAGE 50 reference list cites Hofbauer on limit cycles].

The p-adic norm is
\[
|r|_p=p^{-k}\quad \text{for }r=p^k\frac ab,\quad (a,b)=1,
\]
and it satisfies
\[
|x+y|_p\le \max(|x|_p,|y|_p).
\]
The source says: “whereas the p-adic absolute value satisfies the ultrametric (strong) inequality” [PAGE 38], and “all triangles in the p-adic space are isosceles” [PAGE 39]. Units: dimensionless. Assumptions: \(p\) prime, \(r\neq0\) in the valuation formula. Breakdown: equality occurs when the two norms are unequal; the Archimedean triangle inequality is replaced, so real-line intuition about additivity of distances fails.

For p-adic quantum games, the source replaces \(\mathbb C\) with \(Q_p(\mu)=\{z=x+y\sqrt\mu\mid x,y\in Q_p\}\) and defines
\[
|z|_{p,\mu}:=\sqrt{|z\bar z|_p},\quad z=x+y\sqrt\mu,\quad \bar z=x-y\sqrt\mu.
\]
It warns that “in general, the ultranorm does not stem directly from the inner product” [PAGE 43], and that the space may contain isotropic vectors with \(\langle z,z\rangle=0\) [PAGE 43]. Assumptions: \(\mu\in Q_p\) is non-quadratic, and the p-adic Hilbert space is separately specified. Breakdown: the classical identity \(\|z\|=\sqrt{|\langle z,z\rangle|}\) can fail, so any argument importing standard Hilbert-space geometry without checking isotropy is invalid.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The source’s genealogy runs from the Babylonian Talmud estate-division problem [PAGE 4] through Cournot’s best-response intuition [PAGE 11], von Neumann and Morgenstern’s founding book [PAGE 6, PAGE 11], Nash’s equilibrium and existence theorem [PAGE 10-12], Maynard Smith and Price’s evolutionary game theory [PAGE 18], Isaacs’s differential games [PAGE 30], Lasry and Lions’s mean-field games [PAGE 16], Meyer and Eisert-Wilkens-Lewenstein’s quantum games [PAGE 34], Hensel’s p-adic analysis [PAGE 38], and Aniello et al.’s p-adic quantum states [PAGE 42]. Retro-causally, the modern p-adic quantum game is not an isolated invention; it is a recombination of three older lineages: the Nash fixed-point lineage, the p-adic ultrametric lineage, and the quantum-information lineage. 

Multi-week trap one: conflating Nash existence with Nash computability. The source itself says existence “is only an existence result” [PAGE 12], while algorithmic game theory shows that convergence can be prohibitively long [PAGE 31]. A model that asserts unique computable equilibrium from Nash’s theorem alone is overreaching. Multi-week trap two: conflating Pareto efficiency with equilibrium stability. The source notes that the Prisoner’s Dilemma’s unique NE is Pareto inferior [PAGE 13], so stability and social optimality are distinct. Multi-week trap three: conflating quantum correlation with classical mixed randomization. The source defines superposition and entanglement as non-classical resources [PAGE 34], and the quantum Battle of the Sexes is claimed to yield a Pareto-optimal solution [PAGE 37]. Multi-week trap four: importing ordinary probability into p-adic probability. The source explicitly permits negative or greater-than-one p-adic “probability” values [PAGE 41]; treating them as Kolmogorov probabilities is a category error. Multi-week trap five: treating the ten American core values model [PAGE 44-47] as an empirically identified causal system rather than a formal socio-cultural game proposal. The source says this is an “upcoming research work” for the p-adic quantumization [PAGE 47], so the correct status is model candidate, not settled empirical law.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory-of-mind in this source is the interaction between a player’s model of the opponent, the opponent’s model of that model, and the clock. Crisis habit regression appears in replicator dynamics: if \(p_i(t)\) is driven toward a boundary face, the log derivative is singular at \(p_i=0\) [PAGE 22-24], so the population can “regress” to a pure or near-pure habit even when mixed play would be socially better. The source’s Harper duck experiment shows repeated tatonnement-like adjustment to a NE even by non-rational entities [PAGE 11], which is the habit-regression mechanism in miniature: local payoff chasing locks in a stable frequency distribution. 

The counter-trap is mechanism design and correlated equilibrium. The source defines correlated equilibrium by a joint distribution over recommendations [PAGE 14] and gives the traffic-light example: “no driver has an incentive to disobey a traffic light while everyone else is following it” [PAGE 14]. A counter-trap is therefore a trusted signal or payoff transformation that makes the old crisis habit unprofitable without requiring the player to become more rational. In Stackelberg security games, the defender commits first and the attacker reacts [PAGE 32-33]; that is a recursive-ToM counter-trap because the defender models the attacker’s best response and pre-commits to a mixed patrolling scheme. 

The clock is the differential-game time horizon. In the source’s infinite-horizon example,
\[
\max_{y(\cdot)}\int_0^\infty e^{-rt}\big[\beta\gamma(t)-0.5y^2(t)-0.5\gamma s^2(t)\big]dt
\]
subject to \(\dot s(t)=y(t)-\delta s(t)\), \(s(0)=s_0\), with discount rate \(r\) and assimilation rate \(\delta\) [PAGE 31]. The clock changes what “optimal” means: a high discount rate shortens effective memory, while a low rate makes future counter-traps more salient. Thus crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock are not three separate stories; they are the same dynamic game viewed at the individual, mechanism, and temporal scales.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof is negative for this source. The source defines a game by players, strategy sets, and utility functions \(u_i:S\to\mathbb R\) or \(u_i:S\to Q_p\) [PAGE 8-9]. It provides no coordinate for weather \(w\), no biomechanical load \(b\), no fatigue variable, no physiological state, and no environmental measurement equation. Therefore, under the source closure, a weather or biomechanical veto is not merely unsupported; it is undefined. Formally, if \(w\notin S\) and \(w\) is not an argument of \(u_i\), then \(\partial u_i/\partial w\) does not exist. One cannot infer \(\partial u_i/\partial w=0\); one can only infer that the model has not specified the map \(w\mapsto u_i\). 

A valid cross-layer veto would require an extended game
\[
G_w=\langle P,(S_i(w,b))_i,(u_i(s,w,b))_i\rangle
\]
with explicit units, domains, and measurement protocols. The source does not supply that extension. Its physical-layer content is limited to the general statement that a “Game refers to in any interactive situation leading to the sharing of clearly quantifiable benefits, the payoffs/utilities and costs” [PAGE 7]. That is a definitional umbrella, not a biomechanical measurement model. Likewise, the source’s examples such as traffic, ducks, cyber defense, and national parks are illustrative games [PAGE 10-11, PAGE 29-33], not calibrated physical field data. 

Therefore the only admissible cross-layer veto is a formal model-consistency veto: a proposed physical or weather claim must specify how it enters \(S_i\), \(u_i\), the information structure, or the transition law. Absent that, the claim is non-identifiable and must be rejected as an external override. The source’s own MFG equations require a state density \(m(x,t)\) and value \(u(x,t)\) [PAGE 16]; no meteorological field is supplied. The p-adic quantum layer requires \(Q_p(\mu)\) and a p-adic Hilbert space [PAGE 42-43]; no biomechanical operator is supplied. Thus the physical/biomechanical/weather veto is zero-admissible on this drive source. If a later model adds such variables, the veto becomes admissible only after the new equations and units are stated and tested.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: Prisoner’s Dilemma dominance check. The source gives the payoff ordering \(T>R>P>S\) for the Prisoner’s Dilemma [PAGE 25-26]. Verifiable prediction: for a one-shot simultaneous PD with that ordering, defection is a strictly dominant best response for each player, so mutual defection \((D,D)\) is the unique Nash equilibrium; if any model predicts mutual cooperation as the unique NE under those inequalities, the model is vetoed. This is directly checkable by comparing \(u_i(D,s_{-i})\) and \(u_i(C,s_{-i})\) for both opponent actions.

Veto scenario 2: Replicator interior rest point and Nash consistency. The source states that every interior rest point \(\hat p\in\dot\Delta\) is a Nash equilibrium, and that if the replicator dynamics are self-sustained then there is a unique interior rest point whose time averages converge to it [PAGE 24-25]. Verifiable prediction: for a matrix \(A\) with an interior rest point satisfying \((Ap)_1=\cdots=(Ap)_n\), a simulation of \(\dot p_i=p_i((Ap)_i-pAp^{\top})\) should have zero derivative at that point and should not drift away under the stated self-sustained condition. If a counterfactual claims that an interior rest point is necessarily unstable or non-Nash without additional eigenvalue evidence, it is vetoed. The source also warns that boundary \(\omega\)-limit points can make time averages fail to converge [PAGE 25], so any convergence claim must specify self-sustainedness.

Veto scenario 3: p-adic ultrametric and isotropic-vector check. The source gives \(|x+y|_p\le\max(|x|_p,|y|_p)\) [PAGE 38] and states that p-adic triangles are isosceles [PAGE 39]. Verifiable prediction: for any proposed p-adic distance computation, the strong inequality must hold; if a model uses ordinary additive triangle geometry without proving equality conditions, it is vetoed. A second check is the p-adic quantum Hilbert-space warning that “the ultranorm does not stem directly from the inner product” and that isotropic vectors can satisfy \(\langle z,z\rangle=0\) [PAGE 43]. Verifiable prediction: any p-adic quantum model that asserts \(\|z\|=\sqrt{|\langle z,z\rangle|}\) universally, without excluding isotropic vectors and without a separate norm definition, is vetoed. These three vetoes are page-verifiable, equation-verifiable, and independent of any external empirical claim.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
