The derivation must be anchored to the source text rather than to a loose paraphrase, because the paper’s central claim is representational: tracking is converted from a detect-then-associate pipeline into a sequence-to-trajectory operator whose intermediate state is a probabilistic response volume. The first multi-hop step is therefore to identify the state space. The paper states that the global response map encodes “presence of object” and “x/y” attributes, while the motion regressor supplies “∆x/∆y” [PAGE 3]. This is important because the output is not a bounding box in the classical sense; it is a distribution with a scalar presence probability and a spatial center. The second hop is the label-generation mechanism: ground-truth boxes are converted into Gaussian-like saliency targets using radius and sigma rules [PAGE 6]. The third hop is the occlusion-handling rule: a temporal logical inference maps past binary states into the current label [PAGE 7]. The fourth hop is the motion model: optical flow is sampled around retrieved response centers and regressed into displacement vectors [PAGE 8]. The fifth hop is data linking: predicted displacements update prior locations, IOU costs are computed, and Hungarian assignment resolves associations [PAGE 9].

A gray-zone audit appears immediately in the temporal logic. The source says: “if a target has a positive response at last time step, i.e., zt−1j = 1, we set ztj = 1 accordingly no matter what the history states are” [PAGE 7]. Formally, this creates an absorbing positive state. In a finite-horizon tracking system, that is dangerous: once an object is labeled present, the training-label generator cannot naturally return to zero unless an external termination rule is imposed. The paper does impose a maximum age parameter, but that is a tracking-management rule, not a correction to the label-generation equation itself [PAGE 11]. The audit therefore flags a semantic mismatch between “occlusion persistence” and “permanent presence.”

A second gray zone is dimensional consistency in the regression loss. The paper defines a smooth-L1 loss over residuals between ground truth and predicted displacements [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. If the residual is measured in raw pixels, the quadratic branch has units of pixel squared while the linear branch has units of pixel. That is not fatal in deep learning because losses are often treated as dimensionless scalars after implicit normalization, but a formal audit should state the normalization assumption. The paper’s response locations and ROI sizes are specified in pixels: NMS kernel size s = 3, ROI kernel size r_z = 20, and input resolutions such as 512 × 960 and 1024 × 1920 [PAGE 10][PAGE 11]. Without explicit normalization by object scale, large objects can dominate the displacement loss.

A third gray zone is the evaluation bridge. The tracker’s native output is response points, but MOT metrics require boxes. The authors state that they “ran one additional step that maps our generated response points to the provided detection bounding boxes” [PAGE 13]. This is legitimate for benchmark comparability, but it means the reported end-to-end score is mediated by an external box source. The audit should not treat the response map as if it directly generated all reported bounding-box metrics without that bridge.

The final derivation target is runtime. The paper reports a standalone object-locating speed of 5 ms/frame and 200 FPS [PAGE 11], but the full tracker is reported at 162.5 ms/frame and 6 FPS [PAGE 14]. These are not contradictory if one includes NMS, motion regression, and data association, but they are easy to conflate. The formal runtime operator must therefore distinguish component latency from total online latency.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let a video frame sequence be \(I_t \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}\), with pixel coordinates \((x,y)\), frame index \(t \in \mathbb{Z}\), and frame interval \(\Delta t = 1/f\) seconds, where \(f\) is frame rate in frames per second.

1. Object representation as a probabilistic response field.

For a target bounding box with height \(h\) and width \(w\) in pixels, the paper defines an intermediate radius \(r\) and Gaussian standard deviation \(\sigma\):

\[
r = \min_{i=1,2,3} \left| \frac{a_i + \sqrt{a_i^2 - b_i}}{2} \right|,
\]

\[
\sigma = \frac{r}{3}.
\]

The auxiliary terms are

\[
a_i =
\begin{cases}
h+w, & i=1,\\
2(h+w), & i=2,\\
-2(h+w), & i=3,
\end{cases}
\]

\[
b_i =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{4hw(1-\alpha)}{1+\alpha}, & i=1,\\
16hw(1-\alpha), & i=2,\\
16hw\alpha(\alpha-1), & i=3.
\end{cases}
\]

The source states: “Here h and w denote the height and width of target bounding box obtained from the ground-truth of training data, and α is an invariant parameter we set to 0.7 in this work.” [PAGE 6]

Units: \(h,w,r,\sigma,a_i\) have units of pixels; \(b_i\) has units of pixels squared, because it multiplies \(h w\). The term under the square root therefore has units of pixels squared, making \(\sqrt{a_i^2-b_i}\) have units of pixels. The resulting Gaussian-like response can be written as

\[
R_t(x,y) = \exp\left(-\frac{(x-c_x)^2+(y-c_y)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\]

with peak value 1 at \((c_x,c_y)\), and spatial extent controlled by \(\sigma\) in pixels.

Singularity regime: if \(h=0\) or \(w=0\), then \(r=0\) and \(\sigma=0\), causing division by zero in the Gaussian exponent. A valid implementation must enforce \(h>0, w>0\) or clip \(r\) to a positive lower bound.

2. Response-map learning operator.

Given an input frame window

\[
X_l = \{I_{t-l}, \ldots, I_t\},
\]

and label response map \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times H \times W}\), the paper uses cross entropy:

\[
L(X_l,Y) = -\sum_{j=1}^{|Y|}
\left[
y_j \log P(y_j=1 \mid X_l)
+
(1-y_j)\log P(y_j=0 \mid X_l)
\right].
\]

The source states that this is “the standard cross entropy loss function for our network” [PAGE 6].

Units: \(y_j \in \{0,1\}\) is dimensionless; \(P(y_j=1\mid X_l)\in[0,1]\) is a probability. If \(\log\) is natural, \(L\) is measured in nats; if \(\log_2\) is used, \(L\) is measured in bits. If averaged over pixels, the unit is nats per pixel or bits per pixel.

Breakdown regime: if \(P(y_j=1\mid X_l)=0\) while \(y_j=1\), or \(P(y_j=1\mid X_l)=1\) while \(y_j=0\), the logarithm diverges. Numerical training requires clipping to \([\epsilon,1-\epsilon]\).

3. Logical inference for occlusion.

For target trajectory \(T_j\), the binary response label \(z_t^j\in\{0,1\}\) is defined as

\[
z_t^j =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } z_{t-1}^j = 1 \text{ or } \dfrac{1}{l}\sum_{i=t-l}^{t-1} z_i^j \ge \beta,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

The paper states: “if a target has a positive response at last time step, i.e., zt−1j = 1, we set ztj = 1 accordingly no matter what the history states are” [PAGE 7]. The experimental settings are \(l=5\) and \(\beta=0.6\) [PAGE 10].

Units: all quantities are dimensionless. The average term is a dimensionless empirical probability of positive history over the window.

Formal breakdown: define the history mean

\[
\bar{z}_{t-1:t-l}^{j} = \frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=t-l}^{t-1} z_i^j.
\]

If \(z_{t-1}^j=1\), then \(z_t^j=1\) regardless of \(\bar{z}\). This creates an absorbing state. Let \(t_\tau\) be the first frame where \(z_{t_\tau}^j=1\). Then, under the equation alone, \(z_t^j=1\) for all \(t \ge t_\tau\). Therefore the operator cannot represent a target leaving the scene permanently unless another mechanism overrides the label generation. This is a major formal vulnerability.

4. Motion displacement operator.

The paper adopts optical flow:

“Given two adjacent frames It−1 and It, the optical flow estimation from frame t − 1 to frame t can be derived as \(W^{t-1}=P_i(u_i,v_i)\), \(W^{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times h\times w}\)” [PAGE 8].

The displacement regression network outputs

\[
D_t = \{d_j = (\Delta c_x, \Delta c_y, \Delta w, \Delta h), j=1,\ldots,k\}.
\]

The source describes this as “a movement displacement vector of all response points from frame t−1 to frame t” [PAGE 8].

Units: if frame index increments by one, \(u_i,v_i,\Delta c_x,\Delta c_y\) are measured in pixels per frame. If one wants SI velocity, with calibration scale \(s\) in pixels per meter and frame rate \(f\) in frames per second,

\[
v_x^{\mathrm{phys}} = \frac{u_i f}{s}, \qquad v_y^{\mathrm{phys}} = \frac{v_i f}{s},
\]

with units meters per second.

The regression loss is smooth-L1:

\[
\operatorname{smoothL1}(x) =
\begin{cases}
0.5x^2, & |x|<1,\\
|x|-0.5, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

where the source says \(x\) denotes the L1 loss between ground truth \(G_t\) and network output \(D_t\) [PAGE 8][PAGE 9].

Dimensional audit: if \(x\) is a raw pixel residual, then \(0.5x^2\) has units of pixels squared, while \(|x|-0.5\) has units of pixels. The equation is therefore formally valid only after normalization, for example

\[
\tilde{x} = \frac{x}{q},
\]

where \(q\) is a characteristic pixel scale, such as \(r\), \(r_z\), or object size. The paper does not state this normalization explicitly in the equation, so the formal interpretation must include a hidden scaling assumption.

Breakdown regimes:
- Aperture problem: local optical flow may be ambiguous along edges.
- Large displacement: if \(|\Delta c| \) exceeds the search or ROI sampling range, regression fails.
- Camera motion: raw optical flow conflates object motion and ego motion unless compensated.

5. Data linking and assignment.

The predicted location is obtained by adding displacement to the previous location:

\[
(c_x',c_y',w',h') = (c_x+\Delta c_x, c_y+\Delta c_y, w+\Delta w, h+\Delta h).
\]

The association cost uses IOU:

\[
\mathrm{IOU}(a,b) =
\frac{\operatorname{Area}(a)\cap\operatorname{Area}(b)}
{\operatorname{Area}(a)\cup\operatorname{Area}(b)}.
\]

The source says: “we solve the assignment problem optimally using the Hungarian algorithm” [PAGE 9].

Units: areas are in pixels squared; IOU is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\).

Assignment formulation: let \(M\) be the set of predicted tracks and \(O\) the set of observed responses. Define

\[
\mathbf{C}_{mo} = 1 - \mathrm{IOU}(m,o).
\]

The Hungarian algorithm solves

\[
\max_{\pi \in \Pi} \sum_{(m,o)\in \pi} \mathrm{IOU}(m,o),
\]

or equivalently

\[
\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \sum_{(m,o)\in \pi} \mathbf{C}_{mo}.
\]

The paper sets \(\mathrm{IOU}_{\min}=0.7\) and \(A_{\max}=30\) frames [PAGE 11].

Breakdown: if \(\operatorname{Area}(a)\cup\operatorname{Area}(b)=0\), IOU is undefined and must be defined as zero. If the number of predictions and observations differs, the assignment matrix is rectangular and requires padding or dummy nodes.

6. Runtime operator.

The paper reports total runtime:

“the run-time (ms per image) of our tracker is 162.5ms(6 FPS)” [PAGE 14].

Formally,

\[
T_{\mathrm{total}} = T_{\mathrm{loc}} + T_{\mathrm{motion}} + T_{\mathrm{assoc}},
\]

with reported components

\[
T_{\mathrm{loc}} = 108\ \mathrm{ms/frame}, \quad
T_{\mathrm{motion}} = 7.5\ \mathrm{ms/frame}, \quad
T_{\mathrm{assoc}} = 47\ \mathrm{ms/frame}.
\]

The effective frame throughput is

\[
\mathrm{FPS} = \frac{1000\ \mathrm{ms/s}}{T_{\mathrm{total}}\ \mathrm{ms/frame}}
= \frac{1000}{162.5}\ \mathrm{frames/s}
\approx 6.15\ \mathrm{frames/s}.
\]

The paper separately reports a much faster standalone response-map detector: “Response Map (Ours) 5 200” in the ms/frame and FPS columns [PAGE 11]. The difference is due to inclusion of NMS, optical-flow regression, and data association in the full tracker.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s conceptual lineage comes from three converging research programs: tracking-by-detection, saliency-based dense prediction, and optical-flow-based motion estimation.

First, the tracking-by-detection tradition treated detection as prior input. The source says: “With the significant progress in object detection, tracking-by-detection framework [36] has become a leading paradigm whereby the detection results of objects are represented as bounding boxes and available in a video sequence as prior information.” [PAGE 2]. This lineage explains why the authors frame their work as removing detection priors: they want to avoid dependence on a separate detector.

Second, the paper inherits from dense saliency and holistically nested edge detection. The source states that the locating network is “a HED-based [61] saliency detection network modified from [20]” [PAGE 6]. This lineage explains the use of a response map rather than a sparse set of boxes. The response map is a dense per-pixel objectness field, later thresholded and reduced by NMS.

Third, the motion module inherits from deep optical flow. The source states: “we adopt FlowNet2 for frame-wise optical flow estimation.” [PAGE 8]. This lineage explains the optical-flow-like displacement regression. However, the paper does not use flow as a dense end-task output; it samples flow around response centers and regresses track-specific displacements.

A retro-causal trap is the assumption that “end-to-end” eliminates all intermediate stages. In the paper, there remain explicit intermediate stages: response map, NMS, optical flow, ROI pooling, regression, IOU assignment, and Hungarian matching. The source itself describes these as modules in the framework [PAGE 3]. Therefore the genealogical trap is to interpret “end-to-end” as “single homogeneous differentiable tensor pipeline with no structural stages.” The paper’s actual contribution is a learned sequence-to-trajectory representation, not the abolition of all intermediate representations.

A second multi-week trap is benchmark feedback contamination. MOT16 and MOT17 sequences are fixed evaluation environments. The authors report that MOT17 contains “the same video sequences (7 fully annotated training sequences and 7 testing sequences) as MOT16 but with two more sets of public detection results” [PAGE 10]. If a method tunes too closely to these sequences, apparent robustness may be sequence-specific. The trap is to mistake benchmark improvement for general occlusion reasoning.

A third trap is metric mediation. The source says: “we ran one additional step that maps our generated response points to the provided detection bounding boxes from MOT challenge benchmark [32] so that the required metrics can then be calculated.” [PAGE 13]. Historically, this is necessary because MOT metrics are box-based, but it creates a genealogical ambiguity: the response-point tracker is not operating in the same output space as the trackers it is compared against. The comparison is valid only after this mapping step.

A fourth lineage issue is the use of a fixed temporal window. The paper sets \(l=5\) and \(\beta=0.6\) [PAGE 10]. This creates a historical dependency: the model’s learned occlusion behavior is tied to short-term persistence. If objects disappear for longer than the effective management horizon, the tracker may either fragment trajectories or generate stale associations. The source also uses \(A_{\max}=30\) frames for terminated-track matching [PAGE 11], indicating a longer but still finite memory horizon.

The deepest retro-causal trap is that the logical inference rule uses past labels to create present labels, but the present network is supposed to learn presence from images. This creates a self-referential training signal: if the rule overestimates persistence, the network may learn to hallucinate presence during occlusion. The paper claims this reduces false negatives [PAGE 4], but the formal genealogy also shows it can increase false positives if the absorbing positive state is not counterbalanced.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The tracker can be modeled as an agent with an internal belief state about object presence. The agent’s “crisis” is occlusion or temporary detector silence. The agent’s habit regression is to assume persistence because the paper’s logical inference rule says: “if a target has a positive response at last time step, i.e., zt−1j = 1, we set ztj = 1 accordingly no matter what the history states are.” [PAGE 7]. In recursive terms, the agent believes: “If I believed the object existed yesterday, I should continue to believe it exists today.”

This habit is useful for short occlusions because the source argues that “minor occlusions/entering/exiting within the window of frames can be robustly handled.” [PAGE 2]. The window length \(l=5\) and threshold \(\beta=0.6\) define the agent’s short-term memory [PAGE 10]. If an object was visible for most of the last five frames, the agent’s prior is strong enough to maintain presence.

However, the counter-trap is that the same habit becomes maladaptive when the object truly leaves. Once the binary state becomes one, the equation gives no natural decay path. The agent can enter an infinite “I still believe it exists” loop. In a multi-week deployment, this is a crisis habit regression: under uncertainty, the agent defaults to persistence rather than evidence-based termination.

The clock in this model has multiple layers:
1. The inference window clock: \(l=5\) frames.
2. The history threshold clock: \(\beta=0.6\), meaning at least 60% positive history can reactivate presence if the last state is not positive.
3. The track-management clock: \(A_{\max}=30\) frames for matching terminated tracks [PAGE 11].
4. The evaluation clock: runtime of 162.5 ms/frame and 6 FPS [PAGE 14].

The recursive theory of mind also applies to the evaluator. The benchmark expects bounding boxes and trajectories. The tracker internally represents response points. The evaluator’s “belief” is that a track is valid if its mapped boxes align with ground truth under MOT metrics. The tracker must therefore infer what the evaluator will reward. The source acknowledges this by mapping response points to provided detection boxes [PAGE 13].

A counter-trap for the tracker is to avoid overusing historical persistence when the scene changes. For example, if a camera cuts, lighting changes drastically, or a crowd disperses, the historical prior may be invalid. The paper’s weather and illumination robustness claims are not directly formalized, but the source lists “occlusions, illumination variations, motion blur, false alarm” as major challenges [PAGE 1]. A robust agent should therefore couple persistence with evidence degradation: if image likelihood collapses, the prior should not dominate indefinitely.

In crisis regression terms, the model’s safest behavior is not pure persistence but calibrated persistence:

\[
P(z_t=1 \mid I_t, z_{t-1:t-l}=1)
\]

should decay when the visual likelihood \(P(I_t\mid z_t=1)\) becomes weak relative to \(P(I_t\mid z_t=0)\). The paper’s binary rule does not explicitly encode this decay, which is the central recursive vulnerability.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is required when a lower-level physical or sensory constraint invalidates a higher-level tracking decision.

1. Biomechanical motion veto.

Let the predicted center displacement between frames be

\[
d_t = (\Delta c_x, \Delta c_y),
\]

with units pixels per frame. Assume a camera calibration scale \(s\) in pixels per meter and frame rate \(f\) in frames per second. The physical velocity is

\[
v_t = \frac{\|d_t\|_2 f}{s},
\]

with units meters per second.

For pedestrian scenes, a conservative biomechanical upper bound can be written as

\[
\|d_t\|_2 \le \kappa \frac{s V_{\max}}{f},
\]

where \(V_{\max}\) is a maximum plausible speed in meters per second and \(\kappa \ge 1\) is a tolerance factor. If the inequality fails, the displacement should be vetoed regardless of IOU.

This veto is necessary because the paper’s association step uses IOU and Hungarian assignment [PAGE 9]. IOU is spatial overlap, not physical plausibility. A prediction can have high IOU with an observation but imply impossible velocity. The source’s motion regressor uses optical flow and displacement regression [PAGE 8], but optical flow can be corrupted by motion blur, illumination change, or aperture ambiguity. Therefore physical plausibility should override raw regression output.

Breakdown regime: if the camera itself moves, observed flow is not purely object motion. Let \(d_{\mathrm{obs}}\) be observed flow and \(d_{\mathrm{ego}}\) be camera-induced flow. The object-relative displacement is

\[
d_{\mathrm{rel}} = d_{\mathrm{obs}} - d_{\mathrm{ego}}.
\]

If \(d_{\mathrm{ego}}\) is not estimated, the veto can falsely reject valid object motion. The paper does not explicitly model ego motion, so the veto must be applied with an ego-motion compensation layer in moving-camera scenarios.

2. Weather and illumination veto.

Let the binary presence variable be \(z_t\in\{0,1\}\). A Bayesian presence decision can be written as

\[
\hat{z}_t =
\arg\max_{z\in\{0,1\}}
\left[
\log P(I_t\mid z) + \log P(z)
\right].
\]

Define the log-likelihood margin

\[
\Lambda_t =
\log P(I_t\mid z=1) - \log P(I_t\mid z=0).
\]

If \(\Lambda_t\) is strongly negative, the image evidence supports absence. The response-map network outputs a probability \(P(y_j=1\mid X_l)\) [PAGE 6], which can be interpreted as an approximation to this posterior. Under heavy rain, fog, low light, or severe motion blur, the likelihood terms become less separable. In the limit where

\[
P(I_t\mid z=1) \approx P(I_t\mid z=0),
\]

the margin \(\Lambda_t\approx 0\), and the presence decision is dominated by prior history. This is exactly where the logical inference rule can become dangerous [PAGE 7].

A weather veto should therefore trigger when the visual evidence margin falls below a threshold \(\Lambda_{\min}\):

\[
\Lambda_t < \Lambda_{\min}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{do not create or extend a confident track solely from response map output.}
\]

This does not reject the paper’s method outright; it defines the regime where the method’s persistence prior must be discounted.

3. NMS and crowded-scene veto.

The paper uses local NMS with kernel size \(s=3\) and retrieves top \(k\) positive responses [PAGE 10]. Let two object centers be \(p_a=(x_a,y_a)\) and \(p_b=(x_b,y_b)\). If

\[
\|p_a-p_b\|_2 < \rho_{\mathrm{NMS}},
\]

where \(\rho_{\mathrm{NMS}}\) is the effective suppression radius induced by the NMS kernel and response-map resolution, one response may be suppressed. This creates a failure mode in dense crowds. The paper claims robustness in crowded scenes [PAGE 11], but the formal risk remains when object separation is below the spatial resolution of the response representation.

A veto in this case should be: if the expected object separation, after accounting for camera scale and object size, is below the response-map effective resolution, do not treat missing response as evidence of absence. Instead, mark the state as ambiguous.

4. Final adjudication.

The paper’s end-to-end claim is valid within the representational assumptions of the response map, temporal window, and IOU association. However, cross-layer vetoes are necessary when:
- predicted displacement violates biomechanical bounds;
- visual evidence margin collapses under weather or illumination degradation;
- object separation falls below response-map resolution;
- ego motion invalidates optical-flow displacement.

The source’s strongest support for robustness is the claim that the module “can still maintain a positive response even when target is occluded” [PAGE 4]. That is useful under short occlusion, but it cannot override physical impossibility or severe sensory collapse. Therefore the final adjudication is conditional acceptance: accept the method as a learned response-map tracker, but require external veto layers for deployment in adverse physical environments.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual scenarios are verifiable because they rely on measurable quantities: response-map probabilities, displacement magnitudes, NMS suppression distances, IOU values, and MOT metrics such as FP, FN, ID Sw., and Frag.

Scenario 1: Permanent exit after positive response.

Formal setup: Suppose target \(j\) is present through frame \(t_0-1\), so \(z_{t_0-1}^j=1\). The target exits at frame \(t_0\) and never returns. Under the paper’s rule,

\[
z_t^j =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if } z_{t-1}^j=1 \text{ or } \bar{z}\ge\beta,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

the state remains positive forever because \(z_{t_0-1}^j=1\) implies \(z_{t_0}^j=1\), which implies \(z_{t_0+1}^j=1\), and so on. The source explicitly states this absorbing condition: “if a target has a positive response at last time step, i.e., zt−1j = 1, we set ztj = 1 accordingly no matter what the history states are.” [PAGE 7]

Verifiable prediction: On sequences containing many permanent exits, the method should exhibit increased false positives or phantom tracks unless an external termination rule overrides the label-generation logic. Measurable quantities:
- Count of tracks that persist after all ground-truth boxes for that target have disappeared.
- Increase in FP relative to a control rule without the absorbing positive condition.
- Degradation in IDF1 if phantom identities contaminate association.

Veto rule: If the number of post-exit phantom tracks exceeds a predefined tolerance, reject the logical-inference rule as a standalone presence estimator.

Scenario 2: Crowded-scene NMS suppression.

Formal setup: The method uses local NMS with kernel size \(s=3\) and retrieves positive responses [PAGE 10]. Let two true object centers be separated by distance \(d_{ab}\) in response-map coordinates. If

\[
d_{ab} < d_{\mathrm{supp}},
\]

where \(d_{\mathrm{supp}}\) is the suppression distance associated with the NMS kernel, one response may be removed. The paper uses a fixed ROI kernel size \(r_z=20\) for regression [PAGE 11], which may further blur distinct nearby objects if their response distributions overlap strongly.

Verifiable prediction: In crowded sequences where inter-object distances fall below the effective NMS suppression distance, the tracker should show:
- Increased FN due to suppressed responses.
- Increased ID Sw. when the surviving response is assigned to the wrong target.
- Increased Frag when a suppressed target reappears after separation.

Measurable test: Compute pairwise center distances for ground-truth boxes in crowded frames. Bin frames by \(d_{ab}\). If FN and ID Sw. rise sharply in the lowest-distance bin, the veto is triggered.

Veto rule: If tracking performance collapses when \(d_{ab}\) is below the response-map resolution, the end-to-end response representation is insufficient for dense crowds without a higher-resolution or instance-aware head.

Scenario 3: Optical-flow displacement saturation.

Formal setup: The motion regressor uses optical flow between adjacent frames [PAGE 8]. Let the true object displacement be \(d_t^{\mathrm{true}}\) in pixels per frame. Let the regressed displacement be \(\hat{d}_t\). Define the residual

\[
e_t = \|\hat{d}_t - d_t^{\mathrm{true}}\|_2.
\]

If the true displacement exceeds the flow estimator’s effective capture range, or if the ROI sampling around the response center fails to cover the displaced object, then \(e_t\) becomes large. The smooth-L1 loss may still train, but at inference the predicted location may have low IOU with the observation.

Verifiable prediction: If

\[
\mathrm{IOU}(\hat{p}_t, o_t) < \mathrm{IOU}_{\min}=0.7,
\]

the association should fail, causing a new track initialization or termination. The paper sets \(\mathrm{IOU}_{\min}=0.7\) [PAGE 11]. Therefore, in high-speed motion regimes, the tracker should show increased FN or fragmented trajectories.

Measurable test: Measure flow magnitude distribution in test sequences. For frames where flow magnitude exceeds a threshold, compare association success rate to low-flow frames. If association success drops significantly in high-flow frames, the motion model is vetoed for those regimes.

Veto rule: If high displacement magnitude systematically reduces IOU association success, the method cannot be considered universally end-to-end for fast motion unless a larger displacement search, temporal interpolation, or camera-motion compensation is added.

Overall RLVR verdict: The paper is verifiable as an end-to-end response-map tracker under its stated assumptions, but the three counterfactuals expose boundary regimes where the method should be vetoed or augmented. The absorbing positive rule, crowded NMS suppression, and optical-flow saturation are not merely implementation details; they are formal failure modes that can be tested with measurable tracker outputs.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
