Third, frequency groups were defined by actual attendance rather than randomized allocation, so group membership is partially endogenous to child health, family schedule, and compliance over the 12 weeks [PAGE 1]. Fourth, kindergarten-based unstructured physical activity was not monitored or controlled, allowing environmental movement opportunities outside the intervention to covary with group assignment [PAGE 8 Limitations]. Fifth, sex data were unavailable and could not be reconstructed, so sex distribution across L/M/MH/H is unknown; unequal sex ratios could confound motor outcomes because boys and girls may differ in TGMD-3 item performance [PAGE 8 Limitations]. Sixth, no retention or follow-up testing is reported beyond the post-test within one week of program completion, so the durability of frequency effects across the critical 3–6 year window is unexamined [PAGE 3 Methods; PAGES 7–8 Discussion].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From the perspective of a coach or parent interpreting Ren et al. (2026), a crisis-habit regression loop is predictable: when illness, kindergarten events, or scheduling pressure arise, families regress from MH or H attendance down to M or L. The L group was explicitly defined as “up to 1 session/week, with intermittent participation” [PAGE 1], so the model treats intermittent attendance as a stable exposure when it may be the equilibrium outcome of repeated regression under constraint. A counter-trap follows from policy overreach: reading “at least twice per week” as a prescription for three or more sessions ignores the null H-vs-MH contrast and risks over-scheduling preschoolers, displacing unstructured play, sleep, or recovery without measurable motor benefit [PAGE 1 Abstract; PAGES 7–8 Discussion].

The clock layer imposes two limits. First, the intervention clock (12 weeks, 60 minutes per session [PAGE 1]) is short relative to the developmental clock of early childhood; gains observed immediately post-test may not persist or compound across the 3–6 year critical window [PAGE 1]. Second, the assessment clock (pre-test one week before, post-test within one week after [PAGE 3]) captures acute change but not retention, transfer to non-sport contexts, or long-term physical activity adherence, which are the actual outcomes in the Stodden et al. (2008) developmental model [PAGE 1]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore model how stakeholders update behavior: if they treat the study as proving “more is better,” they will over-invest in H; if they correctly read the saturation pattern, they may converge on M/MH as cost-effective, but only if they also resist crisis-driven regression to L and recognize that the evidence remains associational, not causal, due to missing control and randomization [PAGE 7 Limitations].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three veto layers are applied.

Physical layer veto (maturation): The absence of a non-intervention control group means the null hypothesis—that all pre-to-post improvements reflect normal growth rather than structured multisport participation—cannot be rejected [PAGE 3 Methods; PAGE 7 Discussion]. The L group improved significantly on the gross motor total (p<0.01 [PAGE 5 Results]), and without a maturation-matched comparator, the dose-response gradient between L and higher-frequency groups may partly reflect differential maturation correlated with family selection into attendance frequency rather than the intervention itself. Veto status: active; causal attribution to frequency is not proven.

Biomechanical layer veto (session dosing): The protocol specifies a circuit format with locomotor and object control each ~40% of content, balance ~20%, one recovery day between sessions for ≥2x/week attendance, and consistent curriculum across groups [PAGE 3–4]. This design successfully isolates frequency as the primary manipulated variable by holding duration, structure, and content constant [PAGE 4]. However, intensity is not quantified (no heart rate, accelerometry, or RPE), so the biomechanical dose \(D = f \times 60\) min is an exposure-time proxy, not a measured metabolic or mechanical load. Veto status: inactive for internal consistency but active for external dose generalizability; frequency effects cannot be translated into precise biomechanical load–response curves.

Weather/environmental layer veto: The study is based at an institution in Beijing offering multiple class time slots [PAGE 4], and sessions appear facility-based, but children’s daily kindergarten physical activity and outdoor movement opportunities were not monitored [PAGE 8 Limitations]. Seasonal variation in Beijing (temperature, air quality) could modulate both attendance and unstructured activity, confounding the frequency–outcome association. Veto status: active for ecological validity; observed effects may not replicate under different climatic or institutional constraints.

Adjudication: The study passes internal-consistency vetoes (baseline equivalence, TGMD-3 reliability W > 0.70 [PAGE 4], normality and homogeneity assumptions satisfied [PAGE 5]) but fails the causal veto because the physical-layer confound of maturation remains open. The appropriate epistemic verdict is an associational dose-response pattern consistent with a threshold near two sessions/week, not a proven causal prescription.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable predictions:

Scenario 1 (Maturation control). Introduce a non-intervention control group C (n≥18) matched on age, kindergarten, and baseline TGMD-3, measured pre/post over the same 12 weeks with no structured multisport. Veto condition: if \(\Delta_{\text{total}}^{C}\) is statistically indistinguishable from \(\Delta_{\text{total}}^{L}\) (within 2 raw TGMD-3 points) and the omnibus Time × Group interaction drops below significance when C is added or substituted. Prediction: L-group gains are predominantly maturation; the total-score interaction η²p falls from 0.280 to below 0.10, and H/MH advantages over L attenuate or vanish. This is directly testable by adding C and re-running the 2×5 mixed ANOVA [PAGE 3 power analysis; PAGE 5 ANOVA framework].

Scenario 2 (Randomization). Reallocate children randomly to L/M/MH/H frequency targets stratified by baseline TGMD-3 total, with equal n≈16 per cell. Veto condition: if the group main effect p-value rises from 0.111 to >0.20 and the interaction η²p falls below the large-effect benchmark of 0.14. Prediction: self-selection accounts for at least 50% of the observed H/MH advantage over L; random allocation removes the correlation between family motivation and frequency assignment, weakening the frequency–outcome association [PAGE 1 non-random classification; PAGE 5 Table 5 group main effects].

Scenario 3 (Frequency extension). Expand H into H2 (≥4 sessions/week, n≥20) with actigraphy-verified dose and weekly adverse-event monitoring (pain, fatigue, attendance dropout). Veto condition: if H2 shows no significant gross motor total gain over MH and injury or disengagement rates exceed baseline by a prespecified margin (e.g., >10% absolute increase). Prediction: the dose-response plateaus at 2 sessions/week; ≥3 sessions yields null marginal benefit and introduces non-trivial burden in preschoolers, falsifying a linear “more is better” model [PAGE 1 H group ≥3 definition; PAGES 7–8 Discussion on absent H>MH advantage and need for clearly defined higher-frequency groups].

Each scenario yields a binary veto outcome and a quantitative prediction that can be verified in a follow-up trial without relying on observational post-hoc frequency classification.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
