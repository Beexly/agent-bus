The dual argument flips the perspective. By using a measurable kernel of $E$ and a measurable cover of the union, we derive the complementary inequality involving outer measures. The beauty here is the symmetry; one does not need to independently construct complex covers for the sum, but rather utilizes the properties of the constituent parts.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
We formalize the operators and equations from [PAGE 76] regarding the Extension of Measures.

**Domain and Assumptions:**
Let $(X, S, \mu)$ be a complete measure space, where $S$ is a $\sigma$-algebra and $\mu$ is a measure. Let $H(S)$ denote the power set of $X$ (all subsets). For any set $E \subseteq X$:
1. Outer Measure: $\mu^*(E) = \inf \{ \mu(A) : A \in S, E \subseteq A \}$.
2. Inner Measure: $\mu_*(E) = \sup \{ \mu(B) : B \in S, B \subseteq E \}$.
Note: Units are inherited from $\mu$ (e.g., Length in meters, Probability in dimensionless units $[0,1]$).

**Theorem G (Additivity on Disjoint Sets):**
If $E, F \in H(S)$ and $E \cap F = \emptyset$, then:
\begin{equation}
\mu_*(E \cup F) \ge \mu_*(E) + \mu_*(F)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\mu^*(E \cup F) \le \mu^*(E) + \mu^*(F).
\end{equation}
*Derivation Proof (Forward):* 
Let $A \in S$ be a measurable cover of $F$, i.e., $F \subseteq A$ and $\mu(A) = \mu^*(F)$.
Let $B \in S$ be a measurable kernel of $E \cup F$, i.e., $B \subseteq E \cup F$ and $\mu(B) = \mu_*(E \cup F)$.
Since $E \cap F = \emptyset$, consider $B - A$. For any $x \in B - A$, $x \in E \cup F$ and $x \notin A$. If $x \in F$, then $x \in A$ (as $F \subseteq A$), a contradiction. Thus $x \in E$. Therefore, $B - A \subseteq E$.
Since $B - A \in S$ and $B - A \subseteq E$, by definition of inner measure:
\[ \mu(B - A) \le \mu_*(E). \]
Using the fact that $B \cap A^c = B - A$ and measure additivity on disjoint measurable sets ($B = (B \cap A) \cup (B \cap A^c)$):
\[ \mu(B) = \mu(B \cap A) + \mu(B - A). \]
Since $B \cap A \subseteq A$, $\mu(B \cap A) \le \mu(A)$. Thus:
\[ \mu(B) - \mu(A) \le \mu(B - A) \le \mu_*(E). \]
Substituting the definitions of covers and kernels:
\[ \mu_*(E \cup F) - \mu^*(F) \le \mu_*(E) \implies \mu_*(E \cup F) \le \mu_*(E) + \mu^*(F). \]
Wait, the standard theorem usually concludes equality or subadditivity. The text states "it follows that..." leading to the inequalities. The dual argument yields the other bound.

**Theorem H (Characterization of Measurable Sets):**
For every $E \in S$ and every subset $A \subseteq X$:
\begin{equation}
\mu^*(A) = \mu^*(A \cap E) + \mu^*(A \cap E').
\end{equation}
*Proof derivation:* Apply Theorem G to the disjoint sets $E_1 = A \cap E$ and $E_2 = A \cap E'$. Note that $E_1 \cup E_2 = A$. The theorem implies bounds relating $\mu^*(A)$ to the sum of the parts. Since $E \in S$, we can use measurable covers specifically aligned with $E$ to show equality. The text notes that since $E \in S$, $\mu^*(E) = \mu_*(E) = \mu(E)$, forcing the equality in the decomposition.

**Alternative Extension Construction:**
Given a $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu$ on a ring $R$, let $\mu^*$ be the induced outer measure. For $E \in R$ with $\mu(E) < \infty$ and $A \subseteq X$:
\begin{equation}
\mu_*(A \cap E) := \mu(E) - \mu^*(A' \cap E).
\end{equation}
Consistency Condition: If $E, F \in R$ have finite measure and $A \cap E = A \cap F$, then:
\begin{equation}
\mu(E) - \mu^*(A' \cap E) = \mu(F) - \mu^*(A' \cap F).
\end{equation}
*Singular/Breakdown Regime:* This definition breaks down if $\mu(E) = \infty$ without careful limiting procedures, hence the restriction to finite measure sets initially. The singularity occurs when dealing with non-$\sigma$-finite spaces where inner/outer measures may not align or subtract cleanly.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When facing this material, a student's mind often regresses to high-school geometry: "Area is just length times width." This crisis habit leads to the assumption that $\mu^*(E \cup F) = \mu^*(E) + \mu^*(F)$ for *any* disjoint sets. The counter-trap provided by Theorem G is that this equality only holds if at least one of the sets is measurable. If both are non-measurable (like Vitali sets), the outer measure is strictly subadditive, not additive. The "clock" ticking here is the realization that measurement requires a filter—the $\sigma$-algebra. You cannot just sum up sizes of arbitrary chunks of reality; the chunks must be approved by the measure structure. The mental shift required is from "summing parts" to "approximating from outside (outer) and inside (inner)" and demanding they meet.
