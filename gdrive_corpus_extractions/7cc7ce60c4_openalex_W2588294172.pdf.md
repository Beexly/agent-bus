<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source gives the following formal operators. I preserve the paper’s symbols and add unit bookkeeping; where the paper omits normalization, I flag it rather than inventing a hidden constant.

1. Gadd Severity Index, SI [PAGE 3]:
\[
\mathrm{SI}=\int a_{\max}^{\,n}\,dt \;\leq\; 1000 .
\]
Here \(a_{\max}\) is the maximum value of a head-injury-related parameter such as resultant acceleration, force, or pressure, and \(n\) is a weighting exponent. The paper states that the Wayne State tolerance curve has a slope of approximately \(-1/2.5\), so for resultant acceleration \(n=2.5\) [PAGE 3-PAGE 4]. Unit caveat: the source does not print the acceleration normalization. If \(a\) is in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and \(t\) in \(\mathrm{s}\), \(\mathrm{SI}\) is not dimensionless as written; the numerical threshold 1000 presupposes an implicit normalization such as \(g\) and seconds. The breakdown regime is that SI uses a maximum, so it is “データのノイズに影響を受けやすい” [PAGE 4].

2. Head Injury Criterion, HIC [PAGE 4]:
\[
\mathrm{HIC}
=
\max_{t_1,t_2}
\left[
\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}
\int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\,dt
\right]^{2.5}
(t_2-t_1).
\]
Here \(a(t)\) is the resultant translational head acceleration, and \(t_1,t_2\) are the integration-window endpoints chosen by optimization so that HIC is maximized [PAGE 4]. The paper uses HIC15, with a 15 ms duration ceiling: “本研究でも持続時間15［ms］のHIC を用いることとし，HIC15 と表記する” [PAGE 4]. HIC is dimensionless only under a stated acceleration normalization. If \(a(t)\) is continuous and finite, the window can collapse toward zero and HIC tends toward zero; if the measured signal contains a delta-like impulse, the average grows as \(1/\Delta t\) and the expression can diverge as \(\Delta t^{-1.5}\). That impulse singularity is not discussed in the source.

3. Rotational Injury Criterion, RIC [PAGE 4]:
\[
\mathrm{RIC}
=
\max_{t_1,t_2}
\frac{
\left[
\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}
\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \alpha(t)\,dt
\right]^{2.5}
(t_2-t_1)
}{
C_{\mathrm{RIC}}
}.
\]
Here \(\alpha(t)\) is the resultant angular acceleration in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\), \(t_1,t_2\) are optimized endpoints, and \(C_{\mathrm{RIC}}\) is a correction coefficient. The paper sets \(C_{\mathrm{RIC}}=10{,}000=10^4\) so that the RIC36 threshold sits near 1000, comparable to HIC36/HIC15 thresholds of 1000 and 700 [PAGE 5]. The paper uses a 36 ms ceiling because football impacts can exceed 15 ms: “RIC36 とPRHIC36 の持続時間（t2－t1）の上限を，最初にHIC に適用されていた36［ms］とした” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regimes: \(C_{\mathrm{RIC}}=0\) is undefined; a vanishing continuous window drives the metric toward zero; a delta-like angular impulse can diverge; and if \(\alpha(t)\) is treated as a signed vector rather than a resultant magnitude, positive and negative phases can cancel inside the integral.

4. Head Injury Power, HIP [PAGE 4]:
\[
\mathrm{HIP}
=
\sum_i m\,a_i\int a_i\,dt
+
\sum_i I_{ii}\,\alpha_i\int \alpha_i\,dt .
\]
Here \(m\) is head mass, \(a_i\) is component \(i\) of translational acceleration, \(I_{ii}\) is the component moment of inertia, and \(\alpha_i\) is component \(i\) of angular acceleration. The paper assumes an AM50 head with \(m=4.5\,\mathrm{kg}\) and moments \(I_{xx}=0.016\), \(I_{yy}=0.024\), \(I_{zz}=0.022\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) [PAGE 4]. The rotational part is isolated as:
\[
\mathrm{HIP}_{\mathrm{rot}}
=
\sum_i I_{ii}\,\alpha_i\int \alpha_i\,dt
=
0.016\,\alpha_x\int\alpha_x\,dt
+
0.024\,\alpha_y\int\alpha_y\,dt
+
0.022\,\alpha_z\int\alpha_z\,dt .
\]
The unit of \(\mathrm{HIP}_{\mathrm{rot}}\) is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,rad^2\,s^{-3}}\) if \(\alpha\) is in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\) and time in seconds. The sign issue is explicit: \(\alpha_i\int\alpha_i dt\) can be negative if the angular acceleration reverses, and the source does not print absolute-value bars.

5. Power Rotational Head Injury Criterion, PRHIC [PAGE 4]:
\[
\mathrm{PRHIC}
=
\max_{t_1,t_2}
\frac{
\left[
\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}
\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathrm{HIP}_{\mathrm{rot}}(t)\,dt
\right]^{2.5}
(t_2-t_1)
}{
C_{\mathrm{PRHIC}}
}.
\]
The paper sets \(C_{\mathrm{PRHIC}}=1{,}000=10^3\) [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: \(C_{\mathrm{PRHIC}}=0\) is undefined; a negative window-average of \(\mathrm{HIP}_{\mathrm{rot}}\) makes the real-valued 2.5 power undefined unless an absolute value or sign convention is imposed; a near-zero window can produce numerical instability under discretely sampled peaks; and a 36 ms ceiling truncates longer rotational events, which the paper itself warns may raise the relative value of peak angular velocity for longer events [PAGE 9].

6. Cumulative Strain Damage Measure, CSDM [PAGE 3, PAGE 11]:
\[
\mathrm{CSDM}_{\varepsilon}(\%)
=
100\,
\frac{\sum_{e:\,\varepsilon_1^{(e)}>\varepsilon} V_e}{V_{\mathrm{brain}}},
\]
where \(\varepsilon_1^{(e)}\) is the first principal strain of solid element \(e\), \(V_e\) is element volume, \(V_{\mathrm{brain}}\) is total brain volume, and \(\varepsilon\) is the threshold. The abstract defines CSDM as “the percent volume of the brain that exceeds a specified first principal strain threshold” [PAGE 11]. The source evaluates \(\varepsilon=10,15,20,25,30,35\%\) [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: \(\varepsilon\) at or below zero makes nearly the whole model “injured”; \(\varepsilon\) above the maximum strain makes CSDM zero; mesh refinement changes the element-volume sum; and a single-element maximum strain indicator is more mesh-sensitive than a volume-fraction CSDM [PAGE 6].

The paper’s empirical calibration values are: RIC36 50 percent MTBI tolerance 1030; PRHIC36 50 percent tolerance 870 [PAGE 7]. CSDM10 and CSDM30 50 percent tolerances are 18.2 vol% and 0.008 vol%, respectively [PAGE 7]. These are model-conditional logistic estimates, not universal constants. The source does not report confidence intervals in the extracted text, so the precision of 1030 and 870 is not established here.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The present metrics are retrocausally legible as a chain of engineering compromises. The lineage begins with the Wayne State tolerance curve for skull fracture, attributed to Patrick and colleagues, which related head impact acceleration to duration [PAGE 3]. Gadd converted that curve into a weighted-impulse severity index, SI, with the power 2.5 [PAGE 3-PAGE 4]. Versace then averaged the acceleration over duration to reduce noise sensitivity and produced HIC [PAGE 4]. NHTSA later imposed a 36 ms HIC ceiling in 1986 and changed it to 15 ms in 2003 on the basis of skull-fracture duration evidence [PAGE 4]. That 15 ms choice is a skull-fracture lineage, not a concussion lineage. The paper’s decision to retain 36 ms for RIC36 and PRHIC36 is therefore a deliberate divergence: football impacts can exceed 15 ms, so “RIC36 とPRHIC36 の持続時間（t2－t1）の上限を，最初にHIC に適用されていた36［ms］とした” [PAGE 4]. A multi-week trap here is to assume that because HIC15 is the modern standard, RIC36 should also be HIC15-like; the source explicitly rejects that transfer.

A second lineage is rotational concussion research. Ommaya and colleagues used primate heads to derive a concussion tolerance curve relating angular acceleration amplitude and duration [PAGE 4]. Gennarelli and colleagues later produced diffuse axonal injury in primates by rotational loading and reported that lateral rotational loading was especially severe: “側方の回転が強いと脳傷害が発生しやすい” [PAGE 9]. Newman and colleagues introduced Head Injury Power, HIP, which the paper uses for the rotational power component \(\mathrm{HIP}_{\mathrm{rot}}\) [PAGE 4]. Takhounts and colleagues introduced CSDM as a DAI evaluation method in the SIMon model [PAGE 3], and later BRIC work appears in the reference list [PAGE 11]. Kimpara and Iwamoto then proposed RIC36 and PRHIC36 as FE-free surrogates [PAGE 1-PAGE 2]. The retrocausal insight is that RIC36 is not a “new HIC”; it is a hybrid of Gadd’s weighted impulse, Versace’s duration average, Ommaya’s rotational tolerance logic, and Newman’s rotational power.

A third lineage is database genealogy. The 6DOF collegiate football database came from helmet-mounted six-degree-of-freedom sensors, with 4,709 records but no MTBI cases [PAGE 2]. The NFL database came from reconstruction of televised collisions using Hybrid III dummies, with 58 people in 31 collisions and 25 MTBI cases [PAGE 2]. The risk curves therefore inherit the dummy-neck problem: “ダミーの頚部は構造や剛性の点で実人体の頚部と異なるため，ダミーの頭部運動が実人体で予想される挙動と異なる可能性がある” [PAGE 9]. Multi-week traps include: (1) treating 6DOF as an injury database when it has no injury labels; (2) treating NFL reconstructed rotation as validated in-vivo rotation when the source says rotational validity was not evaluated [PAGE 2]; (3) treating CSDM10 and CSDM30 as the same “brain injury” when their 50 percent tolerances are 18.2 vol% and 0.008 vol% [PAGE 7]; (4) treating the 0.89 correlation cutoff as a natural boundary when it was chosen to make \(R^2=0.8\) [PAGE 6]; and (5) treating the 36 ms ceiling as universal when the paper warns that longer events may change which kinematic feature dominates [PAGE 9]. These are not one-day errors; each can consume weeks of reanalysis because the correction changes the database, the FE threshold, or the integration window rather than a single coefficient.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis pressure, the first-order analyst regresses to habit: concussion is present, so reach for the familiar HIC15 or peak linear acceleration. That habit is reinforced by the paper’s own statement that HIC15 is effective for skull fracture and brain contusion [PAGE 9]. But the source shows that translational metrics correlate weakly with FE strain predictors: translational injury indicators were at or below about 0.72 correlation, whereas rotational indicators were stronger [PAGE 6]. The habit regression is therefore a known trap: the analyst uses a skull-fracture metric as if it were a diffuse-axonal-injury metric.

The second-order analyst anticipates that trap and overcorrects: “use RIC36 instead.” This is the counter-trap. RIC36 is indeed strongly correlated with principal strain, shear strain, Hotspot, and CSDM10 [PAGE 6], and its 50 percent MTBI tolerance is 1030 [PAGE 7]. But the source does not say RIC36 is a universal concussion scalar. It says RIC36 and PRHIC36 are useful surrogates for CSDM and recommends adding them to HIC15 when simultaneously evaluating skull fracture and brain injury [PAGE 9-PAGE 10]. The counter-trap is to replace one single number with another single number, ignoring that RIC36 fails to track high-threshold CSDM and PRHIC36 fails to track Hotspot, principal strain, and shear strain [PAGE 7]. The recursive analyst must hold both: RIC36 is best for lower-threshold, broader-volume CSDM; PRHIC36 is best for higher-threshold, more localized CSDM.

The third layer is the clock. The integration window is not a neutral technical detail; it is the theory of time embedded in the metric. HIC15 uses 15 ms; RIC36 and PRHIC36 use 36 ms because football impacts can exceed 15 ms [PAGE 4]. The source’s case comparisons make the clock visible: Case077HD2 has a 36.0 ms duration versus 3.0 ms for Case059HD2, and the longer-duration case has larger RIC36 and larger CSDM10/CSDM30 despite similar peak angular acceleration [PAGE 7-PAGE 8]. The paper concludes that “角加速度の最大値が同程度の場合でも，角加速度の持続時間が長いほど，脳に与えられるエネルギーは大きくなり” [PAGE 8]. A recursive agent must therefore ask: whose clock is being optimized? If the event is longer than 36 ms, the 36 ms ceiling may truncate the relevant dose and peak angular velocity may become more informative, as the paper warns [PAGE 9]. If the event is shorter than the sampling resolution, the integral may be dominated by a single sample. The correct theory of mind is not “which metric is best?” but “which metric’s clock matches the impact physics and the available measurement?” The source’s own recommendation is conservative: use HIC15 plus RIC36 or PRHIC36, and validate further across impact modes [PAGE 10].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical measurement veto. The 6DOF database uses helmet-mounted sensors and is described as measuring head acceleration directly because the sensor is separated from the helmet outer layer [PAGE 2]. That supports the 6DOF translational and rotational kinematics as measured quantities. The NFL database does not have the same status: its rotational acceleration was reconstructed from dummy impacts, and the source says the rotational component’s validity was not evaluated [PAGE 2]. Therefore any claim that requires validated in-vivo NFL rotational kinematics is vetoed. The veto can be lifted only by a biofidelic dummy or volunteer measurement with rotational validation, which the paper recommends [PAGE 9].

Biomechanical model veto. The FE model is a THUMS head with 49,579 elements and 4.39 kg mass [PAGE 2], linear viscoelastic brain, linear elastic membranes [PAGE 3], and rigid skull in the football simulations [PAGE 3]. It was validated against cadaver head experiments: one translational and two rotational cases, with predicted intracranial pressure and brain motion matching experiment [PAGE 3]. That is meaningful validation, but it is not clinical validation. CSDM is a thresholded volume fraction, and the paper itself notes that maximum principal strain, shear strain, and Hotspot can be determined by “たった一つの要素” and are therefore mesh-sensitive, whereas CSDM reduces element-shape dependence [PAGE 6]. The veto: no claim of clinical concussion diagnosis or subject-specific injury location can be adjudicated from this model alone. The model supports relative ranking and correlation, not absolute clinical truth.

Directional veto. The scalar RIC36 and PRHIC36 do not encode rotation axis. The paper’s Case077HD2 versus Case157HD2 comparison is a direct veto against isotropic interpretation: RIC36 values are 1744 and 1825, close, but CSDM10 values are 45.8 and 29.3, far apart [PAGE 8-PAGE 9]. The paper explains that Case077HD2 has strong Z-axis rotation while Case157HD2 has strong Y-axis rotation, and links this to Gennarelli’s finding that lateral rotation is especially injurious [PAGE 9]. Therefore any cross-layer claim that treats RIC36 as direction-blind is vetoed. A direction-resolved model would need axis-specific terms, falx/tentorium geometry effects, and medical injury ground truth, none of which are fully available in the extracted text.

Weather veto. The source contains no weather variable, no temperature, no humidity, no field-condition measurement, and no environmental coefficient. Weather is therefore not an adjudicable layer in this corpus. Any weather-based inference is vetoed by non-identifiability: there is no operator, no unit, no measurement, and no page citation that could support it. The only defensible statement is that weather is an unmeasured confound and cannot be used to override the kinematic or FE evidence. To lift this veto, one would need synchronized weather metadata, helmet-surface condition, and impact reconstruction; the paper provides none.

Cross-layer adjudication rule. A claim survives only if it is consistent with the measurement layer, the FE layer, and the directional layer simultaneously. RIC36’s correlation with CSDM10 survives the FE and measurement layers within the stated databases, but it does not survive as a universal clinical scalar because of the directional counterexample and the NFL rotational-validation gap. PRHIC36’s correlation with CSDM30 survives within the model, but the 0.008 vol% tolerance is near the volume floor and should be treated as model-conditional. Scenario 1: Peak-angular-acceleration proxy veto.
Counterfactual: if RIC36 were merely a monotone proxy for maximum angular acceleration, then ranking NFL cases by peak \(\alpha\) should reproduce RIC36 and CSDM rankings.
Verifier: extract the Case077HD2 versus Case057HD2 comparison. The source states that maximum angular velocity is 37.0 rad/s in both, but Case057HD2 has the larger maximum angular acceleration while Case077HD2 has the larger duration, RIC36, CSDM10, and CSDM30 [PAGE 8]. It also states that “角加速度の最大値が同程度の場合でも，角加速度の持続時間が長いほど，脳に与えられるエネルギーは大きくなり” [PAGE 8].
Prediction: a model using only peak \(\alpha\) will misrank these cases; adding duration or the RIC36 integral will recover the source’s ordering. Any RLVR reward that rewards peak-\(\alpha\) agreement alone will be vetoed by this counterexample.

Scenario 2: CSDM-threshold equivalence veto.
Counterfactual: if CSDM10 and CSDM30 represented the same injury severity, then RIC36 and PRHIC36 should correlate similarly with both, and their 50 percent tolerances should scale smoothly.
Verifier: the source reports that RIC36 correlates with CSDM10 while PRHIC36 correlates with CSDM15 and above; the common strong pairs are RIC36-CSDM10 and PRHIC36-CSDM30 [PAGE 6]. It also reports 50 percent tolerances of 18.2 vol% for CSDM10 and 0.008 vol% for CSDM30 [PAGE 7]. Maxwell’s axotomy classification gives distinct strain bands: primary axotomy at 20 percent or more; secondary axotomy at 5-10, 10-15, and 15-20 percent [PAGE 7].
Prediction: a single-threshold CSDM model will fail to reproduce the dual correlation pattern. A verifier that collapses CSDM10 and CSDM30 into one label will be vetoed because the source contains two different tolerance values and two different metric pairings.

Scenario 3: Directional-isotropy veto.
Counterfactual: if rotational direction were irrelevant, then two cases with similar RIC36 should have similar CSDM.
Verifier: Case077HD2 and Case157HD2 have RIC36 values 1744 and 1825, but CSDM10 values 45.8 and 29.3 [PAGE 8-PAGE 9]. The source attributes the difference to axis-direction differences: strong Z-axis rotation in Case077HD2 versus strong Y-axis rotation in Case157HD2, and cites Gennarelli’s lateral-rotation result [PAGE 9]. The paper concludes that “頭部の回転方向の影響も無視できない” [PAGE 9].
Prediction: a scalar-only RIC36 model will predict nearly equal CSDM for these two cases, while a direction-resolved model with axis features will separate them. Any RLVR verifier that rewards scalar RIC36 fit while ignoring axis direction will be vetoed by the Case077HD2/Case157HD2 pair. The correct next verification target is an axis-resolved FE comparison, not another global correlation coefficient.
