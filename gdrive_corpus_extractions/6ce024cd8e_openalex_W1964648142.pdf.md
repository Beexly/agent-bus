The color distance \(\Delta(C_1,C_2)\) is “a configurable weighed distance function” [PAGE 5], but the exact weights and normalization are not given. The thresholds \(C_{\text{ThresG}}\), \(C_{\text{ThresL}}\), \(\text{SmallThresG}\), and \(\text{MediumThresG}\) are user-definable [PAGE 5–6], so reproducibility depends on operator choices. The fuzzy membership levels are discrete: “Not the color” \(0\), “Resembles the color” \(0.5\), “Is the color” \(1\), and “Is a seed color” \(1\) [PAGE 6]. This means the fuzzy model is not a continuous membership function; it is a four-level lookup table. The background threshold update is per-pixel and only occurs when the pixel is classified as background: “The threshold applied to each pixel is only updated if the pixel is classiﬁed as background, otherwise its value remains unchanged” [PAGE 6]. That design creates a predictable failure mode: stationary foreground can be absorbed into the background. The paper explicitly tests this: at high learning constants \(0.16\) and \(0.20\), “the two static players are miss detected from frame 82 onwards” [PAGE 14], while \(\alpha=0.02\) “was able to completely absorb the players’ shadows in a relatively short period of time (51 frames)” [PAGE 14]. Thus the background model is a gray-zone tradeoff between shadow absorption and player absorption. The homography stage is another gray zone: the paper reports “the error is less than 35cm for the 2 cameras for the total of 20 × 40m” [PAGE 10], with system resolution \(3.6\)–\(3.7\) cm [PAGE 10]. That is adequate for gross position/velocity maps, but it vetoes conclusions requiring sub-decimeter precision. The Kalman filter is also conditional: with \(TPW=1\), tracking is “around 99.44%,” and with \(TPW=20\), “around 99.70%” [PAGE 15], but higher TPW means the operator is prompted less often, not that the tracker is more veridical. The paper warns that “the operator must pay more attention to the entire process, otherwise the tracker may be lost and continue to rely on a misleading prediction” [PAGE 15]. The final gray zone is metric semantics: miss-detection rate, tracking rate, and operator correction rate are not interchangeable. Table 5 reports “a success that ranges from 95.44 to 99.90% (and a corresponding average of 98.79%)” [PAGE 16], but those figures include manual validation and operator corrections, so they are system-plus-operator performance, not autonomous perception. Any inference must preserve that distinction.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(C(x,y)\) be the color at pixel \((x,y)\). The paper expands in HSL to reduce shadow/light effects: “Color expansion is performed on the HSL (Hue, Saturation and Luminance) color space to minimize the effects of shadows and light variations” [PAGE 5]. Let \(\mathcal{S}_c\) be the color subspace for team \(c\), \(C(x_s,y_s)\) a seed color, \(n_8(x_p,y_p)\) the eight-neighborhood, and \(\Delta(\cdot,\cdot)\) a configurable weighted color distance. The region-growing admissibility rule is

\[
C(x_a,y_a)\in\mathcal{S}_c \iff
\forall (x_a,y_a)\in n_8(x_p,y_p)\ \wedge\
\Delta\big(C(x_a,y_a),C(x_p,y_p)\big)<C_{\text{ThresL}}
\ \wedge\
\Delta\big(C(x_a,y_a),C(x_s,y_s)\big)<C_{\text{ThresG}},
\]

with threshold units in the chosen color-distance scale and user-defined values [PAGE 5]. The membership operator is

\[
\mu_{\mathcal{S}_c}(C(x,y))\in\{0,0.5,1\},
\]

corresponding to “Not the color,” “Resembles the color,” “Is the color,” and seed colors mapped to full membership [PAGE 6]. The weighted center of mass is

\[
(x_{cm},y_{cm})=
\left(
\frac{\sum_x\sum_y \mu_{\mathcal{S}_c}(C(x,y))\,x}
{\sum_x\sum_y \mu_{\mathcal{S}_c}(C(x,y))},
\frac{\sum_x\sum_y \mu_{\mathcal{S}_c}(C(x,y))\,y}
{\sum_x\sum_y \mu_{\mathcal{S}_c}(C(x,y))}
\right),
\]

with \(x_{cm},y_{cm}\) in image pixels [PAGE 8]. The denominator is singular when the blob has no positively labeled pixels; the implementation must discard empty blobs rather than divide by zero. The background threshold update is

\[
\sigma^c_{t+1}(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\alpha\big(I^c_t(x,y)-B^c(x,y)\big)+(1-\alpha)\sigma^c_t(x,y), & I_t(x,y)\in B(x,y),\\
\sigma^c_t(x,y), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

where \(\sigma^c_t\) is an 8-bit intensity threshold level, \(I^c_t\) and \(B^c\) are 8-bit intensities, and \(\alpha\) is dimensionless; the paper sets \(\alpha=0.02\) and clamps the threshold between 4% and 23.5% of \(0\)–\(255\), i.e. approximately \([10.2,59.9]\) intensity levels [PAGE 6]. The persistence operator for auto-expansion is

\[
p_{\mathcal{S}_c}(R,G,B)=
\begin{cases}
C_{\text{Low}}\,t_{\exp}, & B_{\mathcal{S}_c}(R,G,B)=C_L,\\
C_{\text{Med}}\,t_{\exp}, & B_{\mathcal{S}_c}(R,G,B)=C_F,\\
C_{\text{High}}\,t_{\exp}, & B_{\mathcal{S}_c}(R,G,B)=C_S,
\end{cases}
\]

with \(t_{\exp}\) measured in frames and the constants dimensionless per-frame persistence increments [PAGE 7]. A color is removed when persistence reaches zero. If \(t_{\exp}\to0\), auto-expansion has no meaningful persistence window; if \(t_{\exp}\) is too large, adaptation is slow, and the paper finds \(t_{\exp}=30\) gives “a 6.95% global average of miss detections” in their case [PAGE 12]. Radial undistortion is

\[
\begin{aligned}
x_u&=x_d+(x_d-x_c)(k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6),\\
y_u&=y_d+(y_d-y_c)(k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6),
\end{aligned}
\qquad
r^2=(x_d-x_c)^2+(y_d-y_c)^2,
\]

with pixel coordinates and radial coefficients in inverse powers of pixels [PAGE 8]. The singularity at \(r=0\) is benign, but the polynomial becomes unstable at the image periphery if \(k_i\) are poorly conditioned; the paper calibrates these unknowns from field markings [PAGE 8]. The projective mapping is

\[
x=K[R|T]X \iff x=H_wX,
\]

where \(X\) is world coordinates in meters and \(x\) is image coordinates in pixels [PAGE 9]. The homography assumes planarity and is projected at average player height [PAGE 9]. The Kalman model is

\[
x_k=A x_{k-1}+B u_k+w_{k-1},
\qquad
z_k=H x_k+v_k,
\]

with \(x_k,z_k\in\mathbb{R}^2\) positions in meters, \(u_k=[v_x,v_y]^T\) in m/s, \(A,B,H=I_2\), and process/measurement noise in meters [PAGE 9]. The input velocity is

\[
u_k=
\left(
\frac{x_k-x_{k-1}}{\Delta t},
\frac{y_k-y_{k-1}}{\Delta t}
\right),
\]

with \(\Delta t\) in seconds [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: HSL has the “non removable singularity problem” cited by the paper [PAGE 6]; HSL hue is ill-conditioned for achromatic colors, so RGB background subtraction is used instead [PAGE 6]. The blob center is undefined if \(\sum\mu=0\). Homography error is bounded at “less than 35cm” for the \(20\times40\) m field, so sub-35cm attribution is not supported [PAGE 10]. Kalman tracking fails when missed detections exceed the Tracking Prediction Window; a large TPW raises the reported tracking rate but risks “a misleading prediction” [PAGE 15].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs from Zadeh’s fuzzy sets [PAGE 20, ref. 35] and Kalman’s linear filtering [PAGE 20, ref. 17] into sports-vision segmentation. The “retrocausal” reading is that later operational traps are already latent in the 2012 design choices. First, region growing inherits the seed problem: the authors state that “the initial seed choice (a well known problem of region growing methods) as well as the speciﬁc characteristics of the team’s uniform will inﬂuence on how well the color subspace adapts” [PAGE 11]. Experiment B, using poorer seeds, “presents higher miss-detection rates, particularly at the initial phase, only getting similar values after frame 600” [PAGE 14]. A multi-week trap is to calibrate once on a bright empty hall and then reuse those seeds across different venues, ceiling heights, uniforms, or lighting; the paper’s vari-focal lens is manually adjustable and the system is meant to be portable between sports halls [PAGE 4], so seed calibration is venue-specific. Second, the dynamic color subspace can drift: persistence prunes color triplets that are not detected, and the paper notes that for the red team the auto-expansion “adaptively ‘pruned’ those color triplets from the subspace” [PAGE 11]. If the pruning threshold or \(t_{\exp}\) is mis-set, true uniform colors can be removed during occlusion or low-light periods. Third, the background model creates a long-horizon trap: if a player stands still, high \(\alpha\) absorbs the player into the background, as shown by miss detections from frame 82 at \(\alpha=0.16\) and \(0.20\) [PAGE 14]. In a multi-week tournament, substitution patterns and dead-ball periods make this failure recurrent. Fourth, the system’s real-time clock is not closed: processing takes “about 160ms to process each frame” and “can go up to 1s during an auto-expansion process,” while a 30 fps frame requires 33 ms [PAGE 10]. Therefore any claim of live tactical inference must account for latency and auto-expansion overhead. Fifth, brightness changes are a known stressor: “For the Step and Ramp tests, the miss-detection rate increases specially when applying the −20 brightness delta” [PAGE 13]. Indoor weather is not a direct variable, but window-mediated daylight is. The genealogical lesson is that the paper’s fuzzy calibration is not a one-time truth; it is a recursive maintenance process over frames, halls, uniforms, and lighting.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system is a user-in-the-loop recursive belief loop. The operator encodes a prior about team colors by clicking seeds [PAGE 5]. The fuzzy subspace encodes that prior as membership values [PAGE 6]. The Kalman filter predicts where the tracker “believes” the player will be, and the Tracking Prediction Window decides when that belief is overridden by operator input [PAGE 9, PAGE 14]. Crisis habit regression occurs when miss-detections rise and the operator reverts to manual annotation or repeatedly re-seeds the current frame. That habit can overfit the color subspace to a single illumination condition and destroy the persistence mechanism that was designed to adapt over time [PAGE 7]. A counter-trap is to optimize the visible tracking rate by increasing TPW: the paper reports “around 99.70% for TPW=20 frames” versus “around 99.44%” for TPW=1 [PAGE 15], but the higher value reflects fewer operator prompts, not better perception. The paper itself warns that the operator must pay more attention “otherwise the tracker may be lost and continue to rely on a misleading prediction” [PAGE 15]. Another counter-trap is disabling auto-expansion too early because it is expensive; the paper advises that “It is convenient to disable the auto-expansion process when the color subspaces become stable and the detection rates high because the overhead time induced by the auto-expansion is high” [PAGE 10]. That is correct only if lighting is stable; if brightness steps occur later, the frozen subspace cannot adapt. The clock is therefore threefold: \(t_{\exp}\) controls adaptation cadence, TPW controls operator intervention cadence, and 30 fps versus 160 ms processing controls whether the loop is real-time. The recursive ToM rule is: never interpret a high tracking rate as the tracker knowing the player’s true state; it may mean the operator has been successfully modeled out of the loop, or that the tracker’s prediction is being trusted beyond its evidence.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the field is \(20\times40\) m, cameras are \(1024\times768\) at 30 fps, and reported homography error is “less than 35cm” [PAGE 10]. Any conclusion requiring sub-35cm spatial discrimination is vetoed. The system resolution is \(3.6\)–\(3.7\) cm [PAGE 10], but that pixel-scale resolution does not overcome homography, lens-distortion, and average-height projection error. Biomechanical veto: handball is “quite a dynamic game, with high physical contact among players and rapid movements (a player can achieve velocities higher than 5m/s)” [PAGE 3]. Table 6 reports “the maximum instantaneous velocity ranged from 4.92 m/s (player P2) to 9.41 m/s (player P3)” [PAGE 17]. If a derived track implies speeds far above the reported maximum, the correct adjudication is identity swap, homography error, or blob merge, not a new biomechanical fact. High contact also means merged blobs; the RLE/blob density filter is not an identity oracle. Weather veto: the paper explicitly models a “semi-controlled environment of an indoor game” [PAGE 6], so direct outdoor weather variables have no independent causal channel. The only weather-adjacent channel is lighting through windows: the conclusion cites “different ﬁeld zones, shadows, inﬂuence from outside conditions due to the presence of windows” [PAGE 17]. Therefore a direct rain/wind/temperature veto is inadmissible; a brightness-step or ramp veto is admissible, and the paper shows that “the miss-detection rate increases specially when applying the −20 brightness delta” [PAGE 13]. Cross-layer adjudication: if the lighting layer fails, veto color-based player detection before blaming tracking; if the static-player layer fails, veto background absorption before blaming Kalman; if resolution is halved, down-sampling “proved to induce a higher miss-detection rate” [PAGE 14], so fine-grained velocity or position claims are vetoed. Only when physical scale, biomechanical plausibility, and lighting conditions all pass should the tracking layer be adjudicated on its own terms.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — seed-quality counterfactual. Fix \(t_{\exp}=30\), cameras, and lighting; compare Experiment A (well-chosen seeds) with Experiment B (poorly chosen seeds). Prediction: B has higher miss-detection early and “only getting similar values after frame 600” [PAGE 14]; the red subspace in B is more condensed on frame 1600, while the green final subspaces are similar [PAGE 14, Table 4]. Veto: if B matches A before frame 600 or if the red final subspace is not more condensed, the paper’s seed-dependence claim is falsified. This scenario verifies operator-prior sensitivity rather than autonomous perception.

Scenario 2 — auto-expansion plus Kalman counterfactual. Fix seeds and \(TPW=5\); compare “without auto-calibration and without Kalman ﬁlter (noExp noKalman)” against “with auto-calibration and with Kalman ﬁlter (Exp Kalman)” [PAGE 16]. Prediction: Exp Kalman lowers miss-detections and produces tracking success “from 95.44 to 99.90% (and a corresponding average of 98.79%)” [PAGE 16]. Veto: if noExp noKalman equals or beats Exp Kalman, the claimed benefit of fuzzy auto-expansion plus Kalman is falsified. This scenario also checks that the reported tracking rate is not an artifact of operator correction.

Scenario 3 — static-player/background-learning counterfactual. Fix a sequence with two players standing still and vary \(\alpha\in\{0.02,0.08,0.16,0.20\}\). Prediction: high constants \(0.16\) and \(0.20\) absorb foreground and the players are “miss detected from frame 82 onwards”; \(\alpha=0.08\) detects only one at frame 82; \(\alpha=0.02\) absorbs shadows in “51 frames” while keeping detection high [PAGE 14]. Veto: if \(\alpha=0.16\) does not absorb the static players, or if \(\alpha=0.02\) fails to maintain detection, the background-threshold breakdown model is falsified. A secondary verifiable perturbation is down-sampling by \(1/2\): the prediction is a higher miss-detection rate because each player contains fewer pixels [PAGE 14]. If down-sampling does not degrade detection, the resolution-sensitivity claim is vetoed. These three counterfactuals are source-grounded, metric-bearing, and falsifiable without adding any unsupported sensor or biomechanical assumptions.
