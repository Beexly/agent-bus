The source operators are the inverse demand, contest-success function, profit-maximization first-order conditions, and the restricted attendance equation. Units are stated or inferred from the paper’s variable definitions and tables.

Inverse attendance demand [PAGE 20]–[PAGE 21]:
\[
p_{it}=\delta+\beta v_{it}+b w_{it}+\mathbf{c}\mathbf{y}_{it}-dA_{it}.
\]
Here \(p_{it}\) is average ticket price in dollars per ticket; \(v_{it}\) is fights per regular season; \(w_{it}\) is points earned divided by the maximum 152 points, so it is a dimensionless proportion; \(\mathbf{y}_{it}\) is a vector of demographic and economic variables with mixed units; \(A_{it}\) is attendance, either season attendance by the equation’s wording or per-game attendance by Table 3 [PAGE 25]. Parameter \(d\) has units of dollars per attendee if \(A\) is a head count; \(\beta\) has units of dollars per fight; \(b\) has units of dollars per unit of \(w\). The source states: “Season attendance for club i in year t is given by Ait” [PAGE 21].

Contest-success function [PAGE 22]:
\[
w_{it}=\gamma-e v_{it}+\mathbf{g}\mathbf{x}_{it}.
\]
The term \(\mathbf{x}_{it}\) includes power-play percentage, penalty-kill percentage, goals allowed, and number of 20-goal scorers [PAGE 24]. \(e\) has units of winning proportion per fight; \(\mathbf{g}\) has units of winning proportion per performance unit. The source states: “The term 𝒙𝒊𝒕 is a vector of team-specific performance measures that are thought to contribute towards winning percentage” [PAGE 22]. It also warns that a non-logistic probability model can predict outside \((0,1)\), but the sample is clustered near 0.5 [PAGE 21]–[PAGE 22].

Profit-maximization first-order condition for attendance [PAGE 22]:
\[
\Pi_{it}=p_{it}A_{it}-C_{it},\qquad
\frac{\partial \Pi_{it}}{\partial A_{it}}
=\delta+\beta v_{it}+b w_{it}+\mathbf{c}\mathbf{y}_{it}-2dA_{it}=0,
\]

A^*_{it}=\frac{1}{2d}\left(\delta+\beta v_{it}+b w_{it}+\mathbf{c}\mathbf{y}_{it}\right).

\frac{\partial^2 \Pi_{it}}{\partial A_{it}^2}=-2d \prec 0,

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is institutional, econometric, and cultural. The cultural prior is Slap Shot (1977), whose business premise is that violence brings fans back; the source quotes Reggie Dunlop’s “personal bounty” line [PAGE 1] and then notes that the movie reflected a violent period in professional hockey [PAGE 2]. The Philadelphia Flyers’ 1974-75 and 1975-76 teams participated in 105 and 106 recorded fights, and the 1977-78 Flyers set an NHL record with 145 fights before the Boston Bruins recorded 150 in 1979-80 [PAGE 2]. That history is the retrocausal seed: observed violence was interpreted as demand-generating, and later econometric work tried to test whether the interpretation survived controls.

The scientific lineage runs from Jones (1984) through Stewart et al. (1992), Jones et al. (1993), Jones et al. (1996), Paul (2003), and Rockerbie (2012). The source says Stewart et al. (1992) built “a model of a profit-maximizing club owner who considers violence to be an important input into the production of wins on the ice” and argued that violence shifts ticket demand through on-ice success and the “blood lust” of fans [PAGE 18]. Jones et al. (1993) found American fans responded positively to violence and Canadian fans negatively, using 1983-84 NHL data [PAGE 18]. Paul (2003) replicated the method with fights per game and found both American and Canadian fans preferred fighting, with American fans more responsive [PAGE 19]. Rockerbie (2012) extended the sample to 1997-2009 and found that doubling fights per season decreased attendance by about 1.63 percent [PAGE 19]. The AHL paper is therefore a corrective extension, not a freestanding claim.

The multi-week traps are four. First, season aggregation can hide game-level timing: Goldschmied and Espindola (2013) found fights were more likely early in regular-season games and in pre-season, but almost non-existent in playoffs when costs are large [PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16]. A season-level fight coefficient can average over very different clock states. Second, relocation and franchise instability create multi-year selection: the source reports 19 of 30 current AHL clubs have been relocated from another city [PAGE 12]–[PAGE 13], and the Adirondack Flames moved after $12 million in losses since 2010 [PAGE 15]. Fight rates may proxy for survival pressure rather than profit maximization. Third, rule changes are multi-week interventions: the 2014-15 AHL rule made two instigator determinations in a game result in expulsion [PAGE 10], a change after the 2002-2013 sample; pre/post comparisons must separate rule effects from league-wide trends. Fourth, the WHA merger and NHL salary escalation changed the player talent pool: the source notes the NHL-WHA merger in 1978 produced many marginal players, including “goon” players, and that NHL fighting peaked in the late 1980s before falling [PAGE 17]. The AHL’s higher fight rate cannot be read as a timeless profit rule; it is historically conditional on salary structure, roster size, and the absence of a collective agreement against fighters [PAGE 31].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three agents: owners, players, and fans. The owners’ crisis-habit regression is visible in the relocation record: AHL parents are “not particularly loyal to any city,” and relocations are “frequent and swift if the club does not perform well at the box office” [PAGE 12]. Management therefore has a short-horizon incentive to reach for the Slap Shot strategy even when the estimated attendance effect is one seat per fight [PAGE 29]. The owners’ belief is recursive: they think fans want blood, so they tolerate fighters; fans see fighting, so ownership reads attendance as validation; the loop persists even if the coefficient is insignificant.

The players’ recursive theory of mind is different. The source reports that in a 2011 poll, 98 percent of NHL players opposed a ban on fighting, arguing that fighting makes the game safer by deterring stick fouls, head hits, and violent boarding [PAGE 5]–[PAGE 6]. AHL players also have a promotion motive: the source says some AHL players “might use an aggressive style of play as the ticket to the NHL, believing that there is a role in the NHL for tough players to protect the more skilled players from intimidation by other teams” [PAGE 4]. The AHL is described as “a sort of purgatory for semi-professional hockey players,” with a few fighters having lengthy AHL careers [PAGE 31]. The counter-trap is explicit: “There is no strategic advantage to having a player whose main skill is fighting on your team, but if you do not have one, your team could be at a significant disadvantage” [PAGE 31]. That is a coordination trap: if every team carries a fighter, no team gains a relative edge; if one team abstains, it is exposed. The NHL escapes the trap through high salaries that leave no roster room for non-skilled fighters [PAGE 17], while the AHL cannot easily reach a league-wide no-fighter agreement [PAGE 31].

The clock layer changes the recursion. Within a game, the penalty clock is concrete: each combatant typically receives a five-minute major, an instigator receives an additional two-minute minor and a ten-minute major, and an aggressor receives a ten-minute major plus ejection [PAGE 9]–[PAGE 10]. The five-minute major does not reduce the skater count if both fighters are penalized, so the marginal deterrent is small when both are guilty; the instigator penalty is the real asymmetry, but the source says instigator penalties are “very rarely called” because players mutually agree to fight [PAGE 9]. Across the season, the clock runs from 76 regular-season games in October through April to the Calder Cup playoffs in June [PAGE 8]. Fights are more likely early in regular-season games and in pre-season, but almost non-existent in playoffs when the cost could be very large [PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore predict state-dependent violence: players fight when the perceived future cost is low and suppress fighting when the playoff clock makes each penalty expensive. That pattern is evidence for cost-benefit calculation, not for a constant blood-lust taste.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical layer vetoes a strong profit-max interpretation unless an omitted nonpecuniary benefit or promotion option is large. Fighting consumes penalty time, exposes hands, wrists, heads, and joints to impact and falls on ice, and can produce ejection under aggressor or repeated-instigator rules [PAGE 9]–[PAGE 10]. The economic model treats all costs as fixed and independent of attendance [PAGE 22], so it does not price injury risk, roster depletion, or the fatigue of repeated fights. If the marginal attendance gain is about one seat per fight per game [PAGE 29], and that estimate has a standard error of 1.678 [PAGE 29], the revenue side is statistically indistinguishable from zero. A physical-cost veto follows: for profit maximization to hold through blood-lust, the marginal revenue from the extra fight must exceed the marginal injury, penalty, and roster cost; the source does not estimate those costs, and the revenue estimate is too small and imprecise to clear them. The unrestricted estimate of 3.3644 is also insignificant [PAGE 30], so the veto is not rescued by relaxing the restriction.

The biomechanical veto is also strategic. If a team’s fighter is ejected or injured, the team loses a roster slot and may lose the deterrent that protects skilled players. The source’s own strategic-complement logic says an enforcer has no relative advantage when every team has one, but absence creates disadvantage [PAGE 31]. That is a prisoner’s-dilemma-like veto on the claim that fighting is chosen because it raises profit: the private incentive can persist even when the league-level profit effect is zero or negative. The NHL’s salary escalation is the cross-layer escape hatch: at an average NHL salary of $2.4 million in 2012, there is “no room for ‘goon’ players who are not also talented skaters, shooters and defenders” [PAGE 17]. The AHL’s lower salary cap, $70,000 maximum and $40,500 minimum [PAGE 8]–[PAGE 9], keeps the fighter role affordable. Thus the physical veto is weaker in the AHL because the opportunity cost of a roster slot is low, not because the blood-lust revenue is large.

The weather and travel layer is not formally identified in the source. The paper notes that most AHL clubs are near the eastern seaboard, only a few have existed west of Chicago and in Canada, “probably due to the higher costs of travel” [PAGE 8]. The Canadian zero-one variable is statistically insignificant in the attendance function [PAGE 29], and the model contains no weather variable. Therefore a weather veto cannot be proven from this source; the defensible statement is that weather and travel are unmodeled confounders, not estimated mechanisms. The source-supported veto is physical-biomechanical and statistical: the blood-lust coefficient is about one seat per fight per game and insignificant [PAGE 29], while the physical costs and penalty-clock costs are real and omitted. That combination is sufficient to reject a strong claim that AHL fighting is a proven profit-maximizing strategy.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
