<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual apparatus for analyzing "instinct" traces a direct line from Charles Darwin’s *On the Origin of Species* (1859), where instinct was reconceived not as a divine implant but as a trait shaped by natural selection—an adaptation. Herbert Spencer’s evolutionary psychology further secularized the notion, framing mental faculties as products of organic evolution. William James, writing in 1890, inherits this legacy but pushes it toward a more precise **physiological monism**. His insistence on avoiding "abstract purposes" is a reaction against Cartesian dualism and vitalist theories that posited non-physical "souls" or "impulses" guiding animal behavior.

This genealogical thread continues into the 20th century with B.F. Skinner’s radical behaviorism, which sought to eliminate even the appearance of internal states, reducing all behavior to stimulus-response contingencies modulated by reinforcement history. While Skinner rejected innate ideas, his mechanistic approach shares James’s commitment to explaining behavior without recourse to unobservable mental entities. Later, cybernetics and early artificial intelligence attempted to formalize these ideas with automata theory, creating models of simple machines that exhibited goal-directed behavior without understanding—a precursor to modern robotics and AI agents.

In contemporary neuroscience, the "predictive processing" or "active inference" framework provides a sophisticated descendant of James’s thought. Here, the brain is seen as a prediction engine minimizing "surprise" (free energy). What appears as instinct can be modeled as a deep prior—a highly weighted probabilistic assumption about the world embedded in the brain's generative model. These priors are so strong that they effectively bypass higher-level deliberation, producing automatic, seemingly "instinctive" actions. Thus, James’s "functional correlatives of structure" finds its modern expression in the architecture of hierarchical Bayesian inference networks shaped by phylogeny.
