<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s 1932 proposal emerges from a specific lineage: a direct revolt against Thorndike’s Law of Effect (1898) and Pavlovian conditioning paradigms, which reduced learning to mechanical S-R strengthening. The intellectual grandfather is Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Wertheimer, Koffka), particularly their emphasis on *insight* (*Einsicht*) in problem-solving. Tolman transplanted this Gestalt principle from perceptual fields to behavioral fields, arguing that organisms perceive *relationships* (means-end structures), not just isolated stimuli.

The retrocausal element appears in how the future goal influences present behavior. Tolman introduced *expectancies*: the rat in a maze doesn't just learn turns; it develops an expectancy for food at the goal, which then pulls the animal through the maze. This anticipatory state organizes past experiences. Historically, this was a "trap" for behaviorists because it smuggled mentalism back into psychology. They trapped themselves by insisting on methodological behaviorism, missing the cognitive revolution that Tolman was foreshadowing.

In modern contexts, the multi-week trap is the reinforcement learning community's initial fixation on Q-learning and TD-error minimization—local, incremental updates. While powerful, these can get stuck in local optima in sparse-reward environments. Tolman’s "cognitive map" prefigures model-based RL and latent variable modeling, where the agent builds an explicit world model. The "trap" is failing to recognize that efficient learning in complex tasks often requires a global restructuring of the policy space, akin to insight, rather than gradual gradient ascent. Recognizing Tolman’s legacy allows us to design algorithms that prioritize *map-building* (world-model acquisition) before *path-finding* (policy optimization), avoiding prolonged periods of aimless exploration.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression occurs when an agent relying on a cached, low-resolution cognitive map encounters an unexpected obstacle or a changed reward location. Its default response is to revert to a simpler, more robust S-R heuristic ("run toward the light")—a form of learned helplessness or superstitious behavior. This is the system's attempt to reduce cognitive load during stress by falling back on procedural memory.

The counter-trap is the ability to inhibit this regression and engage in deliberate, model-based planning. Tolman demonstrated this with his "latent learning" experiments: rats explored mazes without reinforcement and later performed better than those who learned only under reward, once they understood the goal. The counter-trap mechanism involves maintaining a separate, explicit representation of the environmental topology that can be queried independently of immediate drive states. When the habitual route fails, the agent can simulate alternative paths on this map, effectively "thinking ahead."

The clock measures the decay of confidence in the current hypothesis. Each prediction error injects noise into the belief state. After a critical threshold of accumulated error, the system triggers a "re-mapping" phase, suspending execution to explore and update the cognitive map. This prevents catastrophic forgetting of the global structure while allowing local adaptations. In human terms, this is the difference between a driver automatically taking their usual route (habit) and stopping to consult GPS when there's traffic (model-based reasoning).
