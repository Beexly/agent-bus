<THINK_TRACE>
The source is not a single forecast but a formal warning about how forecast quality is inferred after extreme outcomes. The multi-hop derivation begins with the joint law \([F,Y]=[Y][F|Y]\) in the likelihood-base-rate factorization on [PAGE 5]. Conditioning on the realized outcome \(Y\) is therefore not the same operation as conditioning on the forecast \(F\). The paper states that “stratifying by, and conditioning on, the realized value of the outcome is problematic” and that “stratifying by, and conditioning on, the forecast is unproblematic” on [PAGE 24]. This is the first grey-zone: an evaluator may believe that scoring only the cases where \(Y\) is extreme is a neutral filter, but it changes the target functional and the implied reward.

The second hop is the scoring-rule algebra. For a proper score \(S_0\), multiplying by a nonconstant outcome weight gives \(S(F,y)=w(y)S_0(F,y)\). The expected score is minimized by the tilted density \(f(y)\propto w(y)g(y)\), not by the true density \(g\); hence a forecaster who anticipates this reward is “encouraged to deviate from their true beliefs and misspecify their predictive densities” [PAGE 8]. The indicator weight \(w_r(y)=\mathbf 1\{y\ge r\}\) is the formal analogue of dropping non-extreme cases, because it assigns zero score below \(r\) and multiplicatively rescales the restricted score [PAGE 8-9]. This is not a toy ratio; it is a hedging theorem.

The third hop is the simulation in eq. (1.1): \(Y\mid \mu\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), \(\mu\sim\mathcal N(0,1-\sigma^2)\). With \(\sigma^2=2/3\), the perfect forecast is ideal, yet Table 2 and Table 3 show that the deliberately biased “extremist” forecast wins the restricted MAE, MSE, rCRPS, and rLogS when evaluation is confined to \(Y>1.64\) [PAGE 3-4, PAGE 12]. The conditional calculation on [PAGE 6] explains why: \(X_{\text{perfect}}\mid Y=y\sim\mathcal N((1-\sigma^2)y,\sigma^2(1-\sigma^2))\) and \(X_{\text{extremist}}\mid Y=y\sim\mathcal N((1-\sigma^2)y+5/2,\sigma^2(1-\sigma^2))\). In the extreme stratum, the bias can reduce conditional error even though the unconditional forecast is worse. The fourth hop is the remedy: proper weighted scores such as twCRPS, CL, and CSL with right-tail weights restore the correct ranking [PAGE 12], because the weight is integrated over thresholds \(z\), not multiplied on the realized outcome \(y\).

The fifth hop is Neyman-Pearson. The one-sided Diebold-Mariano test based on LogS has the same rejection region as the likelihood ratio test, up to an estimated critical value [PAGE 15]. This explains why weighted tail scores do not automatically buy power; the unweighted LogS can be competitive [PAGE 14]. The sixth hop is the case study: GDP growth and inflation rankings reverse under restricted rCRPS and rLogS [PAGE 23-24], illustrating the dilemma in real data. The final audit is about breakdown regimes: \(\sigma\to0\) gives a point-mass perfect forecast; \(\sigma\to1\) gives the zero-signal unconditional forecast; \(r>\max y\) makes twCRPS and CSL depend only on tail mass; CL can vanish when no observation exceeds \(r\) [PAGE 17-19]. The grey zone is therefore not “which score is best” in general, but whether the score was precommitted, whether the weight has support in the sample, whether the signal-to-noise ratio is low, and whether the evaluation conditions on outcomes or forecasts.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(Y\) be the observation in measurement units \(U\). In the macro case study, \(U\) is percentage points because GDP growth and inflation are “100 times the log difference” of the respective index [PAGE 19]. Let \(F\) be a predictive CDF and \(f\) its density. A scoring rule is \(S:\mathcal F\times\Omega_Y\to\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}\). Propriety is
\[
\mathbb E_G S(G,Y)\le \mathbb E_G S(F,Y)\quad\text{for all }F,G\in\mathcal F,
\]
with equality only for \(F=G\) in the strictly proper case [PAGE 7]. If the logarithm is natural, LogS and CL/CSL are in nats; if base 2, they are in bits. CRPS, MAE, and twCRPS are in \(U\); MSE is in \(U^2\). Probabilities and CDF values are dimensionless.

Core source equations:
\[
Y\mid\mu\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2),\qquad
\mu\sim\mathcal N(0,1-\sigma^2)
\tag{1.1}
\]
[PAGE 3]. The perfect, unconditional, and extremist forecasts are \(F_{\text{perfect}}=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), \(F_{\text{unconditional}}=\mathcal N(0,1)\), and \(F_{\text{extremist}}=\mathcal N(\mu+5/2,\sigma^2)\) [PAGE 3-4]. The calibration-refinement and likelihood-base-rate factorizations are
\[
[F,Y]=[F][Y\mid F],\qquad [F,Y]=[Y][F\mid Y]
\tag{2.1-2.2}
\]
[PAGE 5]. Auto-calibration is \([Y\mid F]=F\) almost surely [PAGE 5]. The log score and CRPS are
\[
\operatorname{LogS}(F,y)=-\log f(y),
\tag{2.6}
\]
\[
\operatorname{CRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(F(z)-\mathbf1\{y\le z\}\bigr)^2\,dz.
\tag{2.7}
\]
The product-weighted score and its hedging density are
\[
S(F,y)=w(y)S_0(F,y),\qquad
f(y)=\frac{w(y)g(y)}{\int w(z)g(z)\,dz},
\tag{2.9-2.10}
\]
so a nonconstant \(w\) renders \(S\) improper [PAGE 8]. The proper tail-weighted scores are
\[
\operatorname{CL}(F,y)=-w(y)\log\!\left(\frac{f(y)}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(z)f(z)\,dz}\right),
\tag{2.11}
\]
\[
\operatorname{CSL}(F,y)=-w(y)\log f(y)-(1-w(y))\log\!\left(1-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(z)f(z)\,dz\right),
\tag{2.12}
\]
\[
\operatorname{twCRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(z)\bigl(F(z)-\mathbf1\{y\le z\}\bigr)^2\,dz.
\tag{2.13}
\]
The threshold-weighted continuous ranked logarithmic score is
\[
\operatorname{twCRLS}(F,y)=-\int_{\mathbb R}w(z)\log|F(z)-\mathbf1\{y>z\}|\,dz.
\tag{2.14}
\]
For an indicator weight \(w(z)=\mathbf1\{z\ge r\}\), predictive distributions that are identical on \([r,\infty)\) but differ below \(r\) cannot be distinguished [PAGE 9]. The Diebold-Mariano statistic is
\[
t_n=\sqrt n\,\frac{\bar S_n^F-\bar S_n^G}{\hat\sigma_n},
\tag{2.16}
\]
with variance estimator
\[
\hat\sigma_n^2=
\begin{cases}
\hat\gamma_0,& k=1,\\
\hat\gamma_0+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\hat\gamma_j,& k\ge2,
\end{cases}
\tag{2.17}
\]
or HAC
\[
\hat\sigma_n^2=\hat\gamma_0+2\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(1-\frac jJ\right)\hat\gamma_j,\qquad J=\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor.
\tag{2.18}
\]
The test assumes a vanishing expected score difference and regularity conditions [PAGE 10-11]. The Neyman-Pearson rejection region is
\[
\sum_{t=1}^n \log f_1(y_t)-\sum_{t=1}^n \log f_0(y_t)>\log k,
\tag{3.1}
\]
and the one-sided LogS Diebold-Mariano test replaces \(\log k\) by \(\sqrt n\hat\sigma_n z_{1-\alpha}\) [PAGE 15]. The source states that “the unique most powerful test of H0 against H1 is the likelihood ratio test” [PAGE 15].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. \(\sigma\to0\): \(F_{\text{perfect}}\to\delta_\mu\), a point mass at the random mean; \(\sigma\to1\): \(F_{\text{perfect}}\to\mathcal N(0,1)\), the zero-signal unconditional law [PAGE 11-12]. The dilemma appears mainly at “moderate to low signal-to-noise ratios” [PAGE 13].
2. In \(w(y)S_0\), if \(w\) is nonconstant, the optimal quoted density is \(w g/\int w g\); if \(\int w g=0\), the CL denominator vanishes and the score is undefined. If \(f(y)=0\) at an observed \(y\), LogS is infinite.
3. For \(w_r=\mathbf1\{y\ge r\}\), if no sample observation reaches \(r\), CL mean scores vanish; the source notes “none of the 100 observations in the test sample exceed the threshold, and so the mean scores under both forecasts vanish” [PAGE 17]. If \(r>\max_i y_i\), then twCRPS reduces to a pure tail functional, approximately \(\int_r^\infty(1-F(z))^2dz\), and CSL likewise depends only on tail mass; the lighter-tailed forecast is favored even if misspecified [PAGE 18-19].
4. For DM tests, the \(k-1\) dependence assumption can fail; the HAC estimator uses \(J=\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor\). Weighted tests can exceed nominal level; the paper reports that “the level of these tests exceeds the nominal level of \(\alpha=0.05\)” for twCRPS and CL in the relevant threshold range [PAGE 16].
5. Neyman-Pearson optimality is exact for simple hypotheses; it is robust to minor Kullback-Leibler misspecification [PAGE 15], but not a universal bound for composite or outcome-conditioned evaluations.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicitly retrocausal. It opens with John Owen’s 1607 epigram and Bernoulli’s 1713 argument that a foolish prediction can attract praise when a rare event happens to materialize [PAGE 1, PAGE 25]. The modern statistical genealogy runs through Gumbel (1958), Embrechts et al. (1997), Coles (2001), Katz et al. (2002), and Beirlant et al. (2004) on extremes; Murphy and Winkler (1987) on the joint distribution framework; Diebold and Mariano (1995) on comparative predictive accuracy; Gneiting and Raftery (2007) on proper scoring rules; Diks et al. (2011) and Gneiting and Ranjan (2011) on tail-weighted scoring; Neyman and Pearson (1933) on optimal tests; and Tetlock (2005) and Denrell and Fang (2010) on the psychology and management of extreme predictions [PAGE 2, PAGE 7-10, PAGE 15, PAGE 25-26]. The retrocausal trap is that after a crisis, the public retroactively certifies whoever appeared to have predicted it, even if the ex ante forecast was biased or uncalibrated. The paper gives the financial crisis of 2007/08 and the L’Aquila earthquake of 2009 as examples [PAGE 2]. In the language of the source, “if forecast evaluation proceeds conditionally on a catastrophic event having been observed, always predicting calamity becomes a worthwhile strategy” [PAGE 2].

The multi-period trap is the repeated use of event-conditioned scorecards across weeks, quarters, or forecast horizons. The paper’s case study uses quarterly GDP growth and inflation from 1985Q1 to 2011Q2, with forecast horizons of one and four quarters [PAGE 19-20]. It also uses real-time vintages: “The data vintage t includes information up to time t−1” [PAGE 20], and realized values are taken from later vintages to reflect revision noise [PAGE 20]. A multi-week or multi-quarter evaluator who scores only crisis weeks or high-inflation quarters is not running a stable evaluation; the target changes with the realized tail. In the case study, restricting to GDP growth below \(0.1\) or inflation above \(0.98\), about 10% of observations, reverses the ranking and makes the baseline AR model look best while the AR-TVP-SV model looks poor [PAGE 23-24]. The paper calls this “a further manifestation of the forecaster’s dilemma” [PAGE 24]. The historical lesson is that extreme-event evaluation is a selection mechanism, not a neutral microscope; its genealogy runs from Bernoulli’s praise for lucky fools to modern tail-weighted scores that try to restore propriety without conditioning on outcomes.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the recursive belief update in which an evaluator observes an extreme outcome \(Y\ge r\), then infers skill from the forecasts that were extreme in the same direction. The forecaster anticipates this update and may choose an extremist predictive distribution because it minimizes the anticipated restricted score. This is the strategic loop: evaluator conditions on \(Y\), forecaster conditions on evaluator, and the public then reads the resulting extreme forecasts as prescience. The paper’s formal version is the hedging density \(f\propto wg\) [PAGE 8] and the statement that “always predicting calamity becomes a worthwhile strategy” [PAGE 2]. The recursive theory of mind therefore predicts a regression toward crisis habits: after each extreme event, attention concentrates on the forecasters who were extreme, and the incentive to be extreme increases.

The counter-trap is to change the conditioning variable. Instead of stratifying by \(Y\), stratify by the forecast \(F\) or by a point forecast \(X=T(F)\), where auto-calibration gives \([Y\mid F]=F\) and \(T([Y\mid X])=X\) [PAGE 5]. This is the decision-theoretically coherent move: the evaluator precommits to a proper score, uses all available cases, or uses a proper weighted score with the weight chosen ex ante. The paper states, “Arguably the only remedy is to consider all available cases when evaluating predictive performance” [PAGE 25]. The counter-trap also requires calibration diagnostics such as the probability integral transform \(F(Y)\), which should be uniform for a probabilistically calibrated forecast [PAGE 5]. The recursive belief is defused when the evaluator rewards calibration and sharpness jointly rather than rewarding the appearance of having called the crisis.

The clock is the forecast horizon and the evaluation window. In the source, the horizon is \(k=1\) or \(k=4\) quarters, and the variance estimator changes with \(k\): for \(k=1\), \(\hat\sigma_n^2=\hat\gamma_0\); for \(k\ge2\), it includes \(k-1\) autocovariances [PAGE 10-11]. In the Diks et al. simulation, the sample size is tied to the threshold \(r\) so that the expected count in the tail region is \(c=5\) [PAGE 14]. This clock matters because a threshold that looks reasonable at one horizon or sample size can become an empty tail at another. The case study’s counterintuitive result, that all four models seem less skillful at predicting current-quarter inflation than four-quarters-ahead inflation under restricted scores [PAGE 23-24], is a clock artifact: the restricted sample and horizon interact with the tail definition. The recursive theory of mind must therefore include three clocks: the forecast lead time, the data vintage, and the event-selection clock. Without all three, the evaluator mistakes a change in the selection window for a change in skill.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is a proof that a statistical score cannot certify a physical, biomechanical, or weather claim unless the observation space, units, support, and weighting region are admissible. Let \(\Omega_Y\subseteq\mathbb R\) have physical units \(U\) and support \(S\). A score \(S\) is admissible for cross-layer adjudication only if: (i) \(S\) is proper on the forecast class; (ii) for LogS, \(f(y)>0\) for \(Q\)-almost every physically possible \(y\); (iii) CL and CSL denominators are nonzero and their log arguments are positive; (iv) the weight \(w\) is nonnegative, precommitted, and has positive probability mass in the evaluation sample; and (v) no nonconstant product weight \(w(y)S_0\) is used as if proper. If any condition fails, the score is vetoed.

The proof of the weather/rare-event veto is direct from the equations. For \(w_r(z)=\mathbf1\{z\ge r\}\) and \(r>\max_i y_i\), every observation lies below \(r\). Then for twCRPS,
\[
\operatorname{twCRPS}(F,y)=\int_r^\infty\bigl(F(z)-1\bigr)^2dz
=\int_r^\infty\bigl(1-F(z)\bigr)^2dz,
\]
so the score is independent of \(y\) and depends only on the upper-tail survival function. The forecast with the thinner tail receives the lower penalty. The paper states that “when the emphasis lies on a low-probability region with few or no observations, the forecaster assigning smaller probability to this region will be preferred” [PAGE 18-19]. For CL, if no \(y\ge r\), then \(w(y)=0\) for every observation, and the mean CL score vanishes; the paper notes that “none of the 100 observations in the test sample exceed the threshold, and so the mean scores under both forecasts vanish” [PAGE 17]. This is a hard veto against using an empty tail as evidence of skill.

For a physical or weather layer, the same veto applies if the threshold is outside the observed or physically plausible range, or if the forecast assigns zero density to a possible event. For a biomechanical layer, no concrete biomechanical data or mechanism is present in the source; any concrete biomechanical claim is therefore vetoed as unsupported. The only defensible cross-layer extension is abstract: if a biomechanical variable has units \(U\), bounds \(S\), and enough tail observations, then the same proper-score conditions apply. If the threshold lies outside physiological bounds or the sample tail is empty, the score degenerates exactly as above. The paper’s own weather examples are media headlines about storms and floods [PAGE 3], not a weather model; the cross-layer veto prevents turning those headlines into a claim that a particular tail-weighted score proves meteorological skill. The admissibility rule is therefore: proper score, correct units, nonempty weighted support, positive density on possible observations, and all-case or properly weighted evaluation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto 1: Outcome-conditioned reward with low signal-to-noise ratio. Suppose an RLVR evaluator rewards only cases with \(Y>1.64\) in the model of eq. (1.1) with \(\sigma^2=2/3\). The restricted reward is minimized by the extremist forecast \(F_{\text{extremist}}=\mathcal N(\mu+5/2,\sigma^2)\), which has rCRPS \(0.79\) and rLogS \(1.88\), versus the perfect forecast’s rCRPS \(0.96\) and rLogS \(2.30\) [PAGE 12]. The verifiable prediction is that RLVR selection will converge to the biased extremist, not the ideal forecast. Counterfactual veto: replace the restricted reward with a proper weighted score such as twCRPS with \(w(z)=\mathbf1\{z\ge1.64\}\), where the perfect forecast scores \(0.018\) and the extremist scores \(0.575\), or use the unweighted CRPS and LogS [PAGE 12]. Verification is exact replication of Table 3 and Table 4: the proper weighted ranking restores the perfect forecast.

Veto 2: Threshold beyond the sample maximum. Suppose RLVR uses twCRPS or CSL with \(w(z)=\mathbf1\{z\ge r\}\), and \(r\) is above every observed \(y\) in the evaluation batch. Then the score depends only on tail probabilities, and the lighter-tailed forecast wins even if it is misspecified. The paper’s Scenario B shows that “the frequency of the desired rejections in favor of F decays to zero with increasing threshold value” and that for twCRPS and CSL “the frequency of the undesired rejections in favor of \(\Phi\) rises for larger threshold values” [PAGE 18-19]. Prediction: an RLVR policy rewarded with such an empty-tail score will learn to narrow the tail or choose a lighter-tailed but inferior forecast. Counterfactual veto: require \(P(Y\ge r)>0\) in the evaluation sample, or use the unweighted CRPS/LogS; verify by checking that \(r\le\max_i y_i\) and by replicating the decay in Figure 7.

Veto 3: Weighted-score power and level exploit. Suppose RLVR rewards low p-values from one-sided Diebold-Mariano tests based on twCRPS or CL with an indicator weight. The paper shows that for thresholds between 0 and 1 these tests can violate the theoretical power upper bound of the likelihood ratio test, and their level exceeds the nominal \(\alpha=0.05\) [PAGE 15-16]. Prediction: the RLVR loop will exploit inflated rejection rates rather than genuine predictive improvement; it will select forecasts that trigger false rejections. Counterfactual veto: use the unweighted LogS, which has the same rejection region as the likelihood ratio test up to an estimated critical value [PAGE 15], or require calibration and proper-score agreement before accepting a tail-weighted claim. Verification: compare empirical rejection frequency to \(\alpha=0.05\), compare power to the likelihood ratio bound, and confirm that the unweighted LogS remains competitive as reported in Figure 4 and Figure 5 [PAGE 14-16].
