\[
\|f\|_{C_{\rm Lip}}
=\sup_x|f(x)|+\sup_{x\ne y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|}.
\]
The parameter set is \(M\), “a bounded, convex, closed subset of another Banach space \(S\)” [PAGE 5]. The control set \(U\) is a subset of Euclidean space. The generator is
\[

\tag{3.2}
\]

\[
L[t,\mu]f(z)

\tag{3.3}
\]

\tag{3.4}

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a chain from deterministic dynamic programming to viscosity solutions and semigroup perturbation theory, then to mean-field games. The HJB equation (3.6)-(3.7) descends from the Bellman principle; the well-posedness proof uses Duhamel’s principle and a contraction in \(C^1\), with viscosity-solution references to Fleming–Soner [PAGE 9-11]. The operator-perturbation identity (2.2) is the semigroup analogue of variation of constants [PAGE 4]. The Lévy–Khintchine generator (3.3) extends the analysis beyond diffusion to stable-like processes, with the stable-like example (3.17) and Green-kernel smoothing (3.18)-(3.19) [PAGE 8-9]. The mean-field game lineage is explicitly Lasry–Lions and Huang–Malhamé–Caines: the forward kinetic equation (4.5) and backward HJB equation (4.6) form a coupled backward-forward system [PAGE 20-21]. The feedback-regularity condition (4.7) is described as “critical” for solving that system, and the paper notes it was used as condition (37) in Theorem 10 of [8] [PAGE 2, 21].

The retrocausal structure is literal: the HJB equation is backward in time from terminal data \(V^T\), while the measure flow is forward from initial data \(\mu_0\). The fixed point is not a single causal direction but a two-sided consistency condition: the backward value function generates a feedback control, and that control drives the forward measure flow, which in turn appears in the HJB coefficients. The sensitivity theorem is therefore a stability theorem for this loop: if the terminal data, Hamiltonian, and generator depend Lipschitz-continuously on the measure curve, then the value function and its gradient move Lipschitz-continuously with the curve, which is exactly what makes the feedback map regular enough to close the loop.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. Week 1 trap: treat the HJB solution as classical and differentiable in the naive sense; the paper instead proves a unique mild solution in \(C^1\) under (A1)-(A2) and terminal \(C^1\) data [PAGE 10]. Week 2 trap: assume constants are uniform in the horizon \(T\); the paper’s constant is \(c(T)\), and the contraction argument is local, with iteration for arbitrary finite \(T\) [PAGE 15, 18]. Week 3 trap: use the Euclidean norm on probability measures; the correct norm is the dual \((C^2)^*\) norm (4.3) [PAGE 20]. Week 4 trap: assume a unique maximizing control is automatic; Theorem 4.1 explicitly assumes the argmax is a singleton and that the resulting control is Lipschitz in \((x,\mu,p)\) [PAGE 21]. Week 5 trap: ignore the smoothing exponent \(\beta\); the estimates are not valid without (3.12)-(3.13), and the stable-like example can push \(\beta\to1^{-}\), where the constants degenerate [PAGE 8-9]. These are not cosmetic issues; each one changes the admissible function space or the uniformity claim.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In the mean-field game, the reference player “knows only his own position \(X_t\) and the empirical distribution of all players \(\mu_t\in\mathcal P(\mathbb R^d)\)” [PAGE 19]. That is the formal theory-of-mind constraint: an agent models the aggregate through \(\mu_t\), not through individual identities. The recursion is bilateral. The agent’s value function solves a backward HJB equation with terminal data \(V^T(\cdot;\mu_T)\); the optimal feedback control is substituted into the forward kinetic equation; the resulting measure flow changes the coefficients seen by every agent; and the HJB must be re-solved against that flow. Feedback regularity (4.7) is the consistency condition that prevents this recursion from becoming unstable: small changes in the future measure curve must produce small changes in the feedback control, uniformly in \((t,x)\) [PAGE 21].

Crisis habit regression appears when the loop is stressed. A common regression is to freeze the measure flow, treating \(\mu_t\) as an exogenous open-loop parameter and solving only the backward HJB. That is mathematically tempting because Theorem 3.3 gives Lipschitz stability in the whole curve \(\{\mu_t\}\), but it is not the same as solving the coupled fixed point. The counter-trap is to check the two Lipschitz bounds (3.39)-(3.40): they guarantee that the value function and its spatial gradient respond continuously to the curve, so the backward block is stable. The remaining burden is the forward block and the singleton/Lipschitz-control condition (4.8)-(4.9). A second regression is to assume the argmax is unique because the control problem “looks concave”; Example 4.2 shows that uniqueness can follow from strict concavity and a Legendre transform, but Example 4.1 shows the explicit quadratic case \(\hat u=\beta p/(2\theta)\) [PAGE 22]. The clock is the horizon and the smoothing exponent: the backward HJB runs from \(T\) to \(t\), the forward flow runs from \(0\) to \(T\), and the contraction clock is controlled by \(T^{1-\beta}/(1-\beta)\). Near \(\beta=1\) or for long \(T\), the clock slows and iteration is required. Thus the recursive theory of mind is not “what does one agent believe?” but “does the map from future aggregate beliefs to current control remain Lipschitz?” The paper’s answer is yes under (A1)-(A3), singleton argmax, and control Lipschitzness; otherwise the fixed-point loop is not certified.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
