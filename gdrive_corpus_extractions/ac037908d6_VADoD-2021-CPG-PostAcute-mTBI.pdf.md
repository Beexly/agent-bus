<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive clinical problem is that patient and provider can each model the other’s model incorrectly. A patient may believe that persistent symptoms prove ongoing brain damage and that more testing will find the lesion. A provider may believe that negative tests prove the symptoms are psychogenic. The guideline blocks both regressions. [PAGE 25] states: “Providers should acknowledge symptoms (i.e., not label them as psychogenic) and reinforce normalcy and wellness rather than impairment and self-labeling.” [PAGE 8] warns that providers should avoid “brain damage” or “brain injury” terms because they “may inadvertently reinforce misattribution of symptoms or insecurities about recovery.” The recursive trap is thus a labeling loop: symptom persistence leads to brain-damage labeling, which increases anxiety and test-seeking, which produces more negative expectations, which can worsen perceived disability.

The crisis habit regression is the repeated-testing and repeated-specialty loop. [PAGE 25] warns: “The presumption that all presenting symptoms are attributed to the mTBI event may lead to the consideration that all are ‘mTBI symptoms.’ This misattribution may lead to the patient being considered a lifelong patient with symptoms attributed to mTBI and, as a result, subjected to repeated evaluations that are unlikely to be helpful and may be potentially harmful (e.g., needless repeated exposure to radiation).” The counter-trap is not dismissal; it is structured validation. Recommendation 1 prefers primary care and symptom-focused management [PAGE 22], while Recommendation 4 vetoes routine neuroimaging, serum biomarkers, and EEG for diagnosis or care direction [PAGE 22]. The provider’s theory of mind must hold two propositions simultaneously: the symptoms are real and deserve care, and the symptoms are not automatically proof of ongoing mTBI pathology.

The behavioral-health recursion is especially important. Recommendation 12 at [PAGE 23] says patients with behavioral health conditions, including PTSD, substance use disorders, and mood disorders, should be “evaluated and managed the same whether they have had mild traumatic brain injury or not, according to the relevant existing VA/DoD clinical practice guidelines.” This prevents a recursive attribution loop in which every psychiatric symptom is treated as mTBI-specific and therefore managed outside the stronger behavioral-health evidence base. The patient focus group findings at [PAGE 65] add the patient-side model: participants wanted “individualized treatment approaches, maintain an open dialogue, and participate in shared decision making.” They also reported difficulty distinguishing mTBI symptoms from co-occurring conditions. The provider should therefore externalize the uncertainty rather than resolve it by fiat.

The clock is the third regulator. The recursive model must be time-indexed:
\[
\mathrm{Model}_{k+1}=U(\mathrm{Model}_k,\mathrm{education}_k,\mathrm{symptom\ course}_k,\mathrm{treatment\ response}_k),
\]
where \(U\) is a qualitative update, not a numerical Bayesian estimate. The source does not provide likelihood ratios or prior probabilities for this update. The safe clock rules are: acute \(0-7\ \mathrm{d}\) [PAGE 8]; post-acute \(1-12\ \mathrm{wk}\) [PAGE 8]; chronic \(\gt 12\ \mathrm{wk}\) [PAGE 8]; new symptoms after \(30\ \mathrm{d}\) get alternative-etiology work-up [PAGE 27]; comprehensive neuropsychological testing is not routine in the first \(30\ \mathrm{d}\) [PAGE 22]; persistent functionally limiting symptoms at \(30\ \mathrm{d}\) trigger further symptom-based treatment [PAGE 116]; cognitive rehabilitation is trialed for \(4-6\) sessions and prolonged ineffective treatment is discouraged [PAGE 105]. These clocks prevent the recursive trap from becoming timeless.

The counter-trap is a short-trial, goal-based, function-first stance. For cognitive symptoms, Recommendation 10 supports a “short trial of clinician-directed cognitive rehabilitation services” after other contributing factors such as sleep, pain, behavioral health, headache, and disequilibrium are managed [PAGE 23]. For vestibular symptoms, Recommendation 13 supports a trial of vestibular and proprioceptive exercise [PAGE 24], and the discussion at [PAGE 37] recommends “goal-based, functional re-assessment to determine treatment responsiveness.” For behavioral symptoms, the guideline routes care through existing behavioral-health CPGs [PAGE 23]. For tinnitus, visual symptoms, exertion-induced symptoms, and CIH, the guideline refuses to manufacture certainty: it uses “Neither for nor against” where evidence is insufficient [PAGE 24]. The recursive theory of mind is therefore not “reassure and stop”; it is “validate, educate, trial, reassess, and stop ineffective prolonged treatment without abandoning the patient.”

It authorizes mechanism-aware prevention and injury description, not mechanism-based treatment modulation.

The valid physical veto is the urgent red-flag set. [PAGE 20] Sidebar 1 lists “Declining level of consciousness/impaired alertness,” “Declining neurological exam/focal neurological symptoms,” “Pupillary asymmetry,” “Seizures,” “Repeated vomiting,” “Motor or sensory deficits,” “Double vision,” “Worsening headache,” “Slurred speech,” and “Marked change in behavior or orientation.” If any is present, the algorithm routes to emergency evaluation and treatment [PAGE 18]. This veto outranks the primary-care symptom-focused pathway. A second valid physical veto is moderate or severe TBI classification: the algorithm routes to consultation with a clinician with TBI experience [PAGE 18]. A third is medication-layer risk: [PAGE 98] advises to “Avoid medications that lower the seizure threshold (e.g., bupropion, traditional antipsychotic medications) or those that can cause confusion (e.g., lithium, benzodiazepines, anticholinergic agents).” A fourth is vestibular medication-layer risk: [PAGE 101] states that vestibular suppressants “are not recommended after concussion,” and “Meclizine is the preferred agent, followed by scopolamine and dimenhydrinate,” while “The use of clonazepam, diazepam, or lorazepam is discouraged.” A fifth is BPPV assessment: [PAGE 101] says clinicians should perform the Dix-Hallpike and supine roll tests, and “radiographic imaging, vestibular testing, and routinely treating BPPV with vestibular suppressant medications is not recommended.” These are cross-layer vetoes because they override routine symptomatic management with a specific physical or pharmacologic finding.

The weather layer is absent. No page in the source defines weather exposure, barometric pressure, temperature, humidity, seasonal variation, or meteorological triggering as a modifier of mTBI diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment. A weather veto would therefore be an external layer with no source support. The correct adjudication is to mark it as a null operator: it cannot veto a guideline action because the source supplies no weather operator to evaluate. This is not a denial of environmental physiology; it is a scope statement. Any future weather claim would need its own citation and its own evidence review, and it could not be retrofitted into this CPG’s GRADE table without new evidence.

The source also vetoes routine diagnostic-layer escalation: neuroimaging, serum biomarkers, and EEG are not to be used to establish mTBI diagnosis or direct care [PAGE 22]. It vetoes routine first-30-day comprehensive neuropsychological testing [PAGE 22]. It vetoes HBOT strongly [PAGE 43] and rTMS weakly [PAGE 44]. The cross-layer adjudication is thus not “biology wins” or “psychology wins”; it is a source-ordered decision tree in which physical red flags and severity outrank routine symptom care, while mechanism and weather do not create independent treatment branches.

The following are verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each is stated as a test that would force a guideline operator to change if the test were met under the source’s own evidence architecture. The current prediction is the source-supported state, not a betting claim.

Scenario 1: First-30-day comprehensive neuropsychological testing. Veto condition: a full-text randomized or controlled study with at least 20 patients, at least 85% mTBI, blinded outcome assessment, and a first-30-day testing arm shows statistically and clinically significant improvement on a critical outcome such as the Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory, Rivermead Post-Concussion Questionnaire, return to activity/duty/work/sports, or community reintegration. Current source prediction: Recommendation 6 remains “Weak against” because [PAGE 22] states: “We suggest against performing comprehensive neuropsychological/cognitive testing during the first 30 days following mild traumatic brain injury.” The discussion at [PAGE 29] notes early cognitive deficits but also “no clear correlation between self-reported cognitive symptoms and findings on formal neuropsychological testing beyond the initial 7 – 30 days.” The inclusion architecture at [PAGE 59] makes the veto verifiable: at least 20 patients, 85% mTBI, full-length clinical study, outcome of interest. If such a trial appeared and survived systematic review, the weak-against operator would be falsified and would need re-grading.
