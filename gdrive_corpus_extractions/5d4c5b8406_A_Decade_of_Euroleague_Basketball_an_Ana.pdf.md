<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses four formal operators. First, the possession estimator is stated verbatim as “NP = P2A + P3A + ½ FTA + TO (Erčulj et al., 2003)” [PAGE 2]. In LaTeX:

\[
N_P = N_{2A}+N_{3A}+\frac{1}{2}N_{FTA}+N_{TO},
\]

with units of possessions per game for \(N_P\) and counts per game for \(N_{2A}\), \(N_{3A}\), \(N_{FTA}\), and \(N_{TO}\). Assumptions: a pair of free throws approximates one possession; offensive-rebound continuations are not separately counted; the statistic is a game-level sum. Breakdown regimes: the formula becomes ambiguous when free-throw attempts are odd, when offensive rebounds extend a possession, or when the analyst wants “true” possession counts rather than the paper’s tactical-pace proxy. [PAGE 2] explicitly warns that “The number of possessions is considered an indicator of the tactical pace of a game, as opposed to the physical pace of a game, which would require measuring the players’ activities or physiological variables such as heart rate or lactate.”

Second, the trend model is a linear regression over seasons. [PAGE 3] says the authors “assumed linear trends and fit linear models to test the significance of the trends.” Formally:

\[
Y_t=\beta_0+\beta_1 t+\varepsilon_t,\qquad t\in\{2001/02,\ldots,2009/10\},
\]

where \(Y_t\) is a seasonal average statistic in its native unit, \(\beta_1\) has units of statistic unit per season, and \(\varepsilon_t\) is a residual. Assumptions: linearity or at least monotonicity, independent residuals, and stable league composition. [PAGE 6] states: “With the absence of any major rule changes during the 2001/02 - 2009/10 period, it was assumed that trends during this period, if any existed, would be linear or at least monotonic.” Breakdown regimes: a structural break such as the 2007/08 officiating memorandum makes a single linear trend misspecified; small season counts make estimates unstable; autocorrelation inflates apparent precision. The paper itself reports the steal series as non-linear and non-monotonic [PAGE 4], which is a direct breakdown of the linear-trend assumption for that variable.

Third, the between-season comparison uses Welch two-sample tests. [PAGE 3] states: “Welch two-sample tests for comparing the last season before the rule change and the first season after the rule change were used.” Formally, for seasons 2009/10 and 2010/11:

\[
t_w=\frac{\bar X_1-\bar X_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}},
\]

with Welch degrees of freedom

\[
\nu=\frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\frac{\left(s_1^2/n_1\right)^2}{n_1-1}+\frac{\left(s_2^2/n_2\right)^2}{n_2-1}}.
\]

The statistic \(t_w\) and \(\nu\) are dimensionless; \(\bar X_i\) is in the native statistic unit; \(s_i^2\) is a variance in squared units; \(n_i\) is games. Assumptions: independent game samples and approximate normality; equal variances are not assumed. Breakdown regimes: denominator zero when both variances are zero; small \(n_i\) or severe non-normality undermine the reference distribution; dependence among games within a season would overstate precision. Table 2 [PAGE 6] reports, for 2009/10 versus 2010/11, P2A \(t=-8.080\), \(p=9.00\cdot10^{-15}\); P3A \(t=4.871\), \(p=1.65\cdot10^{-6}\); NP \(t=-2.239\), \(p=2.57\cdot10^{-2}\).

Fourth, shooting proportions use a binomial Z-test. Formally:

\[
Z=\frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_2}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}},\qquad
\hat p=\frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2},
\]

where \(\hat p_i\) is a dimensionless proportion, \(x_i\) is made shots, and \(n_i\) is attempts. Assumptions: made/missed shots are independent Bernoulli trials under the null and share a common success probability. Breakdown regimes: denominator zero when \(\hat p=0\) or \(\hat p=1\); overdispersion and within-game clustering violate independence; attempts are not exchangeable across positions, defenses, and shot distances. Page 4’s “Two-point shooting effectiveness decreased from 52.69% to 50.75% (p = 1.10 • 10-3) and 3-point shooting effectiveness decreased from 35.29% to 33.54% (p = 2.44 • 10-2)” [PAGE 4] should therefore be read as proportion-test evidence, not as the same test as Table 2’s Welch season-average tests [PAGE 6].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three recursive mechanisms fit the source. First, crisis habit regression: under a new arc and reset rule, players can revert to previously reinforced habits. Shooters may still hunt the old three-point rhythm; defenders may revert to hand and forearm contact that the 2007/08 officiating memorandum was designed to remove. [PAGE 6] says “Special attention was given to the elimination of illegal use of hands, forearms, and legs during post play and illegal use of hands or forearms by a defensive player when facing the opponent.” That is a habit-regression channel: the rulebook changes, but trained motor and defensive habits lag.

Second, counter-trap: agents anticipate each other’s adjustments. [PAGE 5] argues that personal fouls became more rational, “with a decreasing share of irrational or reckless fouls and increasing share of intentional tactical fouls to break up fast-breaks and prevent the opponent from scoring easy points.” That is a counter-trap logic: a defender may deliberately trade a foul for tempo control, and an offensive player may anticipate the foul and alter the fast-break decision. The shot-clock reset rule creates another counter-trap: if a non-shooting foul in the frontcourt resets the clock only conditionally, a defender can model the referee’s reset threshold and the offensive team’s remaining time. The paper’s own wording shows a codification ambiguity: [PAGE 2] says reset occurs “if there are 13 or fewer seconds remaining” to 14 s, while [PAGE 4] says “14 seconds instead of 24 if there are 14 or fewer seconds left.” Any player or coach reasoning about the clock must resolve that threshold, and a one-second difference changes late-possession decisions.

Third, clock theory of mind: the 14 s reset changes the decision horizon, not just the possession count. [PAGE 2] connects the reset to pace: “This reduces the average time per ball possession and should increase the number of ball possessions per game.” But the paper also notes that the tactical pace and physical pace are not the same [PAGE 2]. A coach can model the opponent’s urgency after a reset; a shooter can model the defense’s expectation of a quick shot; a referee can model players’ attempts to draw or avoid the reset. [PAGE 6] captures this recursive anticipation: “the coaches and referees anticipated that players would quickly adapt to this new refereeing criteria, so an increase in the number of personal fouls was not a concern.” The correct reading is not “the rule mechanically changed behavior.” It is that players, coaches, and officials recursively updated beliefs about the new arc, the new reset threshold, and the new officiating criteria, and the season aggregate is the residue of those updates.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical-layer veto applies to any claim that tactical pace is physical pace. [PAGE 2] states that the possession count is “an indicator of the tactical pace of a game, as opposed to the physical pace of a game, which would require measuring the players’ activities or physiological variables such as heart rate or lactate.” Because the study has no heart-rate, lactate, acceleration, or distance data, it cannot prove that the 2010/11 rule changes increased physical intensity. It can only show a tactical-pace and statistical shift. That veto blocks overclaiming.

A biomechanical-layer veto applies to the shooting-efficiency interpretation. [PAGE 2] cites Miller and Bartlett and Tang and Shung and says “As expected, their experiments also showed that shooting accuracy decreases with increasing distance from the basket.” [PAGE 5] then admits: “the statistical data do not allow us to investigate if a part of a decrease in accuracy is due to changes in players’ shot kinematics to account for an increase in distance.” The observed P3% drop from 35.29% to 33.54% [PAGE 4] is therefore compatible with a distance-biomechanics mechanism, but the paper cannot separate that mechanism from shot selection, defensive closeout, or adaptation. The biomechanical veto is not that the rule effect is false; it is that the paper lacks the kinematic variables needed to prove the mechanism.

A weather-layer veto is essentially external and unsupported here. The source studies indoor Euroleague basketball and reports no meteorological, arena-climate, travel, or humidity operator across [PAGE 1] to [PAGE 7]. Weather cannot be used as a post hoc explanation for the 2010/11 shifts unless an external dataset shows arena or travel conditions changed systematically. The institutional-layer veto is stronger: the 2007/08 officiating memorandum [PAGE 6] can explain the steal break without any 2010/11 rule change, and the 2010/11 package bundles arc distance, no-charge semicircles, and reset rules. The adjudication is therefore: physical pacing is not proven; biomechanical mechanism is plausible but unmeasured; weather is not an operator in the source; officiating and bundled-rule confounding prevent single-rule causal proof.
