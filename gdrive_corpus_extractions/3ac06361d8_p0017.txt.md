<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual genealogy of Wiener's conflation begins with Claude Shannon's 1948 paper, which formalized communication theory but deliberately avoided semantic interpretation. Shannon credited Hartley (1928) for the logarithmic measure of communication capacity. However, it was Norbert Wiener himself, in *Cybernetics* (1948), who explicitly merged Shannon's mathematical information with Maxwell's demon thought experiment and Boltzmann's statistical mechanics. Wiener argued that information is a physical quantity that can reduce entropy locally at the cost of increasing it globally—this laid the foundation for Landauer's principle (1961) and Bennett's reversible computing (1973), establishing that erasure of one bit dissipates \(k_B T \ln 2\) heat.

Historically, the trap lies in treating "information" as a mystical force rather than a bookkeeping tool for constraints. The retrocausal narrative often arises in misinterpretations of Wheeler's "it from bit" (1990), where observation is posited to create reality—a notion that, if taken literally, suggests future choices affect past states. In practice, this manifests as multi-week research detours: teams pursuing "information conservation" laws analogous to energy conservation, only to discover that unlike energy, information is not a conserved current but a monotonic functional under certain channels (data processing inequality). Another lineage trap involves algorithmic information theory (Solomonoff, Chaitin), which defines information via shortest description length. While profound, this leads to incomputability and diverges from operational measures; researchers sometimes conflate Kolmogorov complexity with Shannon entropy, resulting in misguided attempts to compress uncompressible data.

The multi-hop historical risk emerges when combining Wiener's thermodynamic view with modern AI alignment concerns: if an AI system optimizes for information gain (e.g., curiosity-driven exploration), it might inadvertently perform experiments that maximize entropy production in the environment, violating ethical constraints. This connects to the "paperclip maximizer" scenario, where instrumental goals lead to catastrophic side effects. Thus, understanding the precise boundary conditions under which information behaves like a conserved resource versus a dissipative flow is critical for safe autonomous systems design.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis scenarios—such as rapidly evolving pandemics, financial market collapses, or adversarial cyberattacks—human decision-makers exhibit habit regression toward heuristic shortcuts that ignore probabilistic rigor. A typical pattern is "survivorship bias": focusing only on successful outcomes and neglecting the base rate of failures, effectively treating \(P(\text{success}) \approx 1\) and thus \(I(\text{success}) \approx 0\), even though actual probability may be low. This leads to overconfidence and underestimation of risk. Conversely, during black swan events, rare outcomes (\(P \ll 1\)) suddenly occur, yielding massive information shocks (\(I \gg 0\)) that overwhelm cognitive bandwidth, causing paralysis or impulsive reactions.

Recursive modeling of such dynamics requires tracking not just probabilities but also the agents' beliefs about others' beliefs. Consider a two-level game: Player A knows event \(E\) has \(P(E)=0.1\); Player B thinks A believes \(P(E)=0.5\); A anticipates B's action based on that mistaken belief. This creates a loop where information is not merely received but interpreted through layers of theory of mind. In cyber-security, an attacker exploits defender's habit regression by crafting attacks that appear probable (low information content) until execution, when they reveal hidden structure. The counter-trap is to institutionalize pre-mortems and red-team exercises that forcibly assign high probability to failure modes, thereby reducing their information surprise when they materialize.

A clock-based mechanism can mitigate these regressions: implementing periodic reviews where probability estimates are updated via Bayesian revision, ensuring that prior confidence decays over time unless reinforced by evidence. This dynamic calibration prevents the drift toward dogmatic certainty (\(P \to 1\)) or paralyzing doubt (\(P \to 0\)). Moreover, embedding uncertainty quantification into decision interfaces forces stakeholders to confront the logarithmic nature of information: a tenfold reduction in probability increases information content by a constant amount (e.g., \(\log_{10} 10 = 1\) hartley), making extreme shifts more salient than linear scales.
