The source pipeline is a multi-hop latent-state estimator, not a single formula. First, the paper assumes goals are Poisson and uses a Skellam probability mass function to map two predicted goal rates into a three-way match forecast: if the goal difference k is positive the first team wins, if k=0 the match is drawn, and if k is negative the second team wins [PAGE 1]. The means μ1 and μ2 are themselves produced by a LightGBM model trained on rolling goal averages, home/away splits, tournament and league categories, and a pandemic indicator [PAGE 2]. Second, the draw probability s from that model is injected into a modified Glicko-2 expectation in Eq. (3); the paper states: “s represents the draw probability derived from the LightGBM based model and d serves as a hyperparameter responsible for adjusting draw outcomes” [PAGE 3]. Third, the home advantage h is added to the home team rating inside the expectation, with a separate pandemic value hp; the paper imposes “h > hp” [PAGE 3]. Fourth, seasonal resets apply Eq. (7) for promotion penalties and Eq. (8) for league transitions and volatility expansion [PAGE 3]. Finally, all league-level parameters are fitted by minimizing log loss on match outcomes [PAGE 4], with the test set containing 60,091 matches [PAGE 4].

The gray-zone audit is where the model’s mathematical cleanliness must be separated from its empirical claims. Eq. (3) is not an algebraic singularity: for finite real arguments the denominator is positive because it is a sum of positive exponentials. Numerical breakdown occurs in floating-point arithmetic when g(φj)(μ−μj) or d+s becomes large positive, causing exp overflow, or large negative, causing underflow. In the limit d+s → −∞, Eq. (3) collapses to the original Glicko expectation in Eq. (2), so the modification is mathematically nested. But Eq. (3) is presented as an expectation, not as a normalized three-way probability mass. The paper does not explicitly define the loss probability or prove that win, draw, and loss masses sum to one. That is a gray-zone: using Eq. (3) as a complete forecast requires an additional normalization or a separate loss complement, otherwise the three probabilities are not guaranteed to form a simplex.

A second gray-zone is dimensional and statistical. s is a probability in [0,1], but it is inserted as exp(d+s), so s and d are treated as dimensionless exponent arguments. The model can identify the additive combination d+s but not independent meanings of s and d without external anchoring. If s is noisy, d absorbs bias; if s is miscalibrated by league, d becomes league-specific. The paper acknowledges that the Poisson assumption “is not entirely precise” and that low-scoring matches are underestimated, producing draw bias [PAGE 3]. That justifies a correction, but it also means the draw mechanism is partly a residual-bias fix, not a pure generative draw model.

A third gray-zone is temporal. Ratings are updated before each season using Eq. (7) and Eq. (8), and all ratings are normalized after each season so that “the average rating of all teams should be constant” [PAGE 3]. That normalization is essential to prevent inflation, but it also means absolute rating levels are identified only up to a league-level anchor and seasonal re-centering. The paper notes that initial league rating helps compare teams from different national leagues, but it “can inadvertently lead to rating inflation” [PAGE 3]. Thus any cross-league claim is conditional on the anchor, the promotion penalty μnew, and the transition term μl.

A fourth gray-zone is the test design. The train/test split is seasonal: 2010/2011 through 2020/2021 for training, 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 held out [PAGE 2]. This is a defensible temporal split, but the pandemic window is inside training and the separate hp is trained for March 2020 through June 2021 [PAGE 3]. A modeler who tunes d, hp, and seasonal resets against the test seasons can leak future regime information through hyperparameter selection. The reported improvement is small: modified Glicko-2 log loss 0.5832 versus LightGBM 0.5896 and original Glicko-2 0.5949 [PAGE 4]. That margin is meaningful only if the ablation and temporal controls are held fixed; otherwise the gain is fragile.

The deepest audit point is that the model is outcome-only. The paper explicitly states: “The primary limitation of the proposed approach stems from its sole reliance on match outcomes for calculations” [PAGE 4]. Therefore the ratings are a compressed summary of results, not a measurement of injuries, biomechanics, weather, tactics, or player availability. Any inference that claims to recover those layers from this source alone is vetoed unless an external covariate layer is added and identified. The correct interpretation is a probabilistic ranking and forecasting system with league-specific home, draw, and transition parameters, not a physical causal model.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Notation and units. The paper does not assign SI units to ratings. I therefore use the following consistent conventions: μ, μj, μinit, μnew, μl, h, hp are rating-point quantities on an arbitrary scale; φ, φj, φs, σ are rating-deviation or volatility quantities on the same arbitrary scale; μ1 and μ2 are expected goals per match; k is an integer goal difference; probabilities are dimensionless in [0,1]; log loss is reported in nats per match if natural logarithms are used, or bits per match if base-2 logarithms are used. The paper does not state the logarithm base in Table I [PAGE 4]. All exponential arguments must be dimensionless.

Skellam goal-difference operator. The paper writes:
\[
p(k,\mu_1,\mu_2)=\exp(-(\mu_1+\mu_2))\left(\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}\right)^{k/2} I_k\!\left(2\sqrt{\mu_1\mu_2}\right),
\tag{1}
\]
where \(I_k\) is the modified Bessel function, \(k\) is the score difference, and μ1, μ2 are Poisson means [PAGE 1]. The paper states: “if k > 0, the value of the probability mass function equals the probability of the first team winning, for k = 0 we received the draw probability, and finally if k < 0, we get the probability of the second team winning” [PAGE 1]. Assumptions: independent Poisson goal counts, positive μ1 and μ2, and a match outcome determined by the sign of the goal difference. Breakdown regimes: μ1 or μ2 equal to zero requires a limiting convention; very large μ1μ2 causes Bessel overflow; the Poisson independence assumption is imperfect, and the paper itself notes underestimation of low-scoring matches [PAGE 3]. The output p(k, μ1, μ2) is a probability mass, not a rating.

Original Glicko expectation. The paper gives:
\[
E(\mu,\mu_j,\phi_j)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(-g(\phi_j)(\mu-\mu_j)\right)}.
\tag{2}
\]
This is the original Glicko-2 win expectation [PAGE 2]. The paper states that “The mathematical expectation formulas utilized in Glicko-2 do not facilitate the assessment of draw probabilities” [PAGE 2]. Eq. (2) is a two-outcome expectation on a dimensionless scale; its denominator is positive for finite arguments and has no algebraic singularity. Numerical overflow occurs for very large positive exponent arguments; underflow occurs for very large negative arguments.

Modified Glicko expectation with draw and home terms. The paper modifies Eq. (2) as:
\[
E(\mu,\mu_j,\phi_j,d,s)=\frac{\exp\!\left(g(\phi_j)(\mu-\mu_j)\right)}
{1+\exp\!\left(g(\phi_j)(\mu-\mu_j)\right)+\exp(d+s)}.
\tag{3}
\]
The paper states: “We have added two parameters into the original formula: s represents the draw probability derived from the LightGBM based model and d serves as a hyperparameter responsible for adjusting draw outcomes” [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: \(d+s\) is dimensionless; s is a probability from the LightGBM model; d is a league-level correction. Breakdown regimes: the denominator is strictly positive for finite real inputs, so there is no algebraic pole; floating-point overflow arises when \(\max(g(\phi_j)(\mu-\mu_j),d+s)\) exceeds the exponential range; underflow arises when both arguments are very negative. In the limit \(d+s\to -\infty\), Eq. (3) tends to Eq. (2). In the opposite limit \(d+s\to +\infty\), the first-team expectation tends to zero unless the numerator also grows faster. Eq. (3) does not by itself specify a normalized three-way probability mass; the paper does not explicitly define the loss probability or prove that win, draw, and loss probabilities sum to one. This is a formal gray-zone requiring an external normalization convention.

Original Glicko update. The paper states:
\[
\mu'=\mu+\phi'^2 g(\phi_j)\left(s_j-E(\mu,\mu_j,\phi_j)\right),
\tag{4}
\]
where \(s_j\) is the observed score contribution [PAGE 3]. The update is in rating points because the deviation-squared and g factors scale the residual. Breakdown: if φ′ is large, updates are large; if the residual is large, the update can overshoot; if the observed score coding changes, the meaning of s_j changes.

Modified home-aware update. The paper gives:
\[
\mu'=\mu+\phi'^2 g(\phi_j)\left(s_j-E(\mu+h,\mu_j,\phi_j,d,s)\right),
\tag{5}
\]
with the home parameter h replaced by hp during the pandemic [PAGE 3]. The paper states: “The parameters h and hp are positive numbers representing the home ﬁeld advantage. Also we have imposed the following restriction: h > hp” [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: home advantage is a league-level additive rating shift; pandemic home advantage is lower than normal. Breakdown: if \(h \le h_p\), the stated constraint is violated; if h is allowed to vary per team or per match without regularization, the paper warns that a unique set per team “tends to result in model overﬁtting” [PAGE 4].

Initialization and promotion penalty. The paper writes:
\[
r(\mu,\phi,\sigma)=
\begin{cases}
r(\mu_{\mathrm{init}}+\mu_{\mathrm{new}},\phi,\sigma), & \text{if team was promoted},\\
r(\mu_{\mathrm{init}},\phi,\sigma), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\tag{7}
\]
and states “The parameter µnew should be negative” [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: promoted teams from lower-ranked leagues should start below the league average; μinit anchors the league. Breakdown: if μnew is positive, promotion inflates ratings; if μinit is estimated without a fixed normalization, the league average drifts.

Seasonal transition and volatility update. The paper writes:
\[
r(\mu,\phi,\sigma)=
\begin{cases}
r(\mu+\mu_l,\phi+\phi_s,\sigma), & \text{if team changed league},\\
r(\mu,\phi+\phi_s,\sigma), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\tag{8}
\]
and states “The parameter φs should be positive to introduce a degree of uncertainty. The parameter µl should be positive for promoted teams and negative for relegated because usually the club budget increases after promotion and decreases after relegation” [PAGE 3]. The paper also normalizes all ratings after each season: “we normalize all ratings after each season to prevent the inﬂation. Hence, the average rating of all teams should be constant” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: negative φs reduces uncertainty and contradicts the stated intent; wrong-sign μl reverses the promotion/relegation adjustment; missing normalization permits rating drift.

Training objective and empirical comparison. The paper states that parameters “are trained through the minimization of the log loss function associated with match outcomes” [PAGE 4]. I formalize the objective for three outcomes \(c\in\{W,D,L\}\) as:
\[
\mathcal{L}
=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c\in\{W,D,L\}} y_{ic}\ln p_{ic},
\]
where \(N=60{,}091\) in the test set [PAGE 4], \(y_{ic}\) is the indicator of the observed outcome, and \(p_{ic}\) is the forecast probability. Units are nats per match for natural logarithms. The reported Table I values are: Catboost based 0.5931, LightGBM based 0.5896, Original Glicko-2 0.5949, and Glicko-2 with modifications 0.5832 [PAGE 4]. The paper calls the improvement “marginally superior” [PAGE 1]. The exact empirical ranking is therefore modified Glicko-2, then LightGBM, then Catboost, then original Glicko-2, under the reported split and metric [PAGE 4].

Constraints and identified parameter set. The paper states: “we conduct training for all the parameters described across the entire league. As a result, each league possesses a distinct set of parameters, which includes µinit, µnew, µl, φs, h, hp. Furthermore, we need to optimize the initial values φ, σ, and d from (3)” [PAGE 3]. This is a league-level hierarchical fit, not a team-specific physical model. The identifiability caveat is that d and s enter Eq. (3) only through \(d+s\), so separate interpretation of d and s depends on the LightGBM draw model being fixed and calibrated.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a sequence of nested approximations. Elo supplies the idea of a scalar strength and a logistic expectation [PAGE 2, reference 1]; Glicko adds a deviation around that strength; Glicko-2 represents “the strength of each team as a probability distribution” and updates a posterior as match outcomes accumulate [PAGE 2]. TrueSkill and Club Elo extend the same family to team sports and league rankings [PAGE 2]. The paper’s retrocausal move is to take the original Glicko expectation Eq. (2) and embed it inside Eq. (3) as the \(d+s\to -\infty\) limit, then add home advantage inside Eq. (5) as the \(h\to 0\) limit of the original update Eq. (4). In that precise sense, the modified model retroactively reinterprets earlier Glicko-2 as a special case with no draw correction, no home shift, no promotion penalty, and no league-transition volatility.

The second lineage is football-score modeling. The paper begins from the Poisson assumption [PAGE 1], uses the Skellam distribution for goal differences [PAGE 1], and cites Dixon and Coles [13] for the low-scoring correction that motivates the d term [PAGE 3]. The genealogy therefore runs: independent Poisson goals → Skellam difference → three-way outcome probabilities → LightGBM goal-rate estimates → draw probability s → modified Glicko expectation. The weak link is that the Poisson-Skellam layer is trained separately from the Glicko layer; s is not jointly generated with the rating likelihood. The paper acknowledges the assumption “is not entirely precise” and that low-scoring matches are underestimated [PAGE 3].

The institutional lineage is UEFA versus Club Elo versus the authors’ league ratings. The paper criticizes UEFA because it “neglects to consider the strength of opponents, treating victories over favorites and weaker teams equally” and because “the rating points lack a robust mathematical foundation” [PAGE 1]. It also argues that UEFA country ranking is driven by the strongest teams and gives “no information concerning the performance of weaker teams in the first league, as well as the second and lower divisions” [PAGE 1]. That criticism is the retrocausal justification for a league-level Bayesian rating with second divisions included.

Multi-week traps are temporal regime traps. The first is the pandemic window: “due to the diminished impact of the home team advantage during the pandemic, we conducted separate training for the home team constant for the period from March 2020 to June 2021” [PAGE 3]. A model that ignores this window will misstate home advantage across weeks. The second is the transfer window and mid-season roster change: the paper warns that second-league teams “frequently experience signiﬁcant roster changes as the season progresses” [PAGE 4]. A weekly update rule that assumes stable strength will be stale. The third is the season boundary: Eq. (7) and Eq. (8) apply at the start of every season, so promotion, relegation, and league change are discrete clock events [PAGE 3]. The fourth is the test clock: training uses 2010/2011 through 2020/2021, while 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 are held out [PAGE 2]. Any multi-week backtest that tunes d, hp, or φs on the held-out seasons violates the paper’s temporal design. The fifth is cross-region scarcity: the paper states “it’s not feasible to compare clubs from Europe with those from South America due to the scarcity of match history between teams from these two regions” [PAGE 4]. Europe-South America ratings in Table III and Table IV are therefore on separate anchors and should not be treated as a single continuous scale without an explicit bridge.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind here means modeling the analyst who models the model. The first-order modeler predicts match outcomes from ratings. The second-order modeler predicts where the first-order modeler will fail under crisis. The third-order modeler predicts how the first-order modeler will overcorrect after seeing that failure.

Crisis habit regression. Under pandemic, promotion, relegation, or roster churn, a pressured analyst regresses to a familiar habit: use original Glicko-2 or UEFA-style points and ignore the draw, home, and transition corrections. The paper gives the reason this is tempting: original Glicko-2 has a simple expectation and a clean update [PAGE 2], while the modified model requires league-specific μinit, μnew, μl, φs, h, hp, φ, σ, and d [PAGE 3]. But the habit is demonstrably incomplete: original Glicko-2 has test log loss 0.5949 versus 0.5832 for the modified model [PAGE 4]. The habit also ignores the paper’s own warning that the original expectation “do not facilitate the assessment of draw probabilities” [PAGE 2]. Crisis habit regression is therefore a known failure mode, not a neutral simplification.

Counter-trap. The opposite failure is overcorrection. A modeler who sees the league-level parameters may try to assign a unique set to each team, add stadium capacity, distance between cities, and other complex home-advantage variables. The paper explicitly rejects this: “While it would be theoretically desirable to assign a unique set of parameters to each team, considering factors such as stadium capacity, distance between opponent cities, and other complex variables that inﬂuence the actual home-ﬁeld advantage, this approach tends to result in model overﬁtting” [PAGE 4]. The counter-trap is therefore recursive: the attempt to out-think the gray-zone creates a higher-variance estimator, which then looks worse under the same log-loss clock. The safe counter-move is ablation and temporal validation, not parameter explosion.

Clock. The third layer is the clock. The paper’s clock has three hands: the season boundary for Eq. (7) and Eq. (8) [PAGE 3], the pandemic interval March 2020 to June 2021 for hp [PAGE 3], and the test seasons 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 [PAGE 2]. A recursive analyst must ask not only “does the model fit?” but “does it fit at the correct clock position?” A model that learns hp from the pandemic and then applies it after June 2021 is clock-misaligned. A model that normalizes ratings after each season but evaluates mid-season without the same normalization is clock-misaligned. A model that trains on 2022/2023 and reports 2021/2022 as a test is retrocausally contaminated. The paper’s strongest defense is its explicit seasonal split and separate pandemic constant; the weakest point is that d and s are trained inside the same league-level optimization, so the draw correction can absorb league-specific clock effects rather than a universal draw law.

The recursive resolution is a three-way adjudication: habit regression is vetoed by the reported log-loss comparison; counter-trap is vetoed by the paper’s overfitting warning; clock misalignment is vetoed by the seasonal and pandemic boundaries. What remains is a league-level, outcome-only, temporally segmented rating model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical veto. The paper’s likelihood uses match outcomes only. It states: “The primary limitation of the proposed approach stems from its sole reliance on match outcomes for calculations” [PAGE 4]. The feature list contains home/away, pandemic, rolling goal averages, tournament, league, and home/away scoring and conceding averages [PAGE 2]. It does not contain player injury status, minutes played, sprint load, acceleration counts, collision data, travel distance, pitch condition, temperature, wind, or precipitation. The paper also notes that existing rating systems neglect “player injuries, player transfers, and more” [PAGE 2], and it handles transfers only through a season-level φs and μl update [PAGE 3]. Therefore a claim that the rating identifies a biomechanical mechanism is vetoed by non-identifiability: two teams with identical ratings but different injury or fatigue states receive the same forecast from this model. The model can correlate with physical state through outcomes, but it cannot separate physical cause from rating residual.

Weather veto. Weather is not a covariate in the LightGBM feature list [PAGE 2], and home advantage is a league-level constant h or hp [PAGE 3], not a match-specific weather response. The paper explicitly considered more granular home-advantage variables and rejected them because they “tends to result in model overﬁtting” [PAGE 4]. Thus a claim that the model captures rain, heat, altitude, or wind effects is vetoed unless an external weather layer is added and validated. The correct statement is weaker: weather may enter the residual, but the paper does not identify it.

Physical-layer veto proof. Let \(M\) be the observed match-outcome data and let \(\theta=(\mu_{\mathrm{init}},\mu_{\mathrm{new}},\mu_l,\phi_s,h,h_p,\phi,\sigma,d)\) be the fitted parameter set [PAGE 3]. The paper’s likelihood is \(P(M\mid \theta,s)\), with s from the LightGBM draw model. If a proposed physical variable \(X\) is not in the conditioning set, then \(P(M\mid \theta,s,X)=P(M\mid \theta,s)\) under the model’s stated measurement equation, except through unexplained residual correlation. Therefore any claim that \(\theta\) or the resulting rating “proves” an \(X\)-mechanism is not licensed. The veto is not a claim that physical effects are absent; it is a claim that this source cannot identify them. The paper’s own limitation statement and feature list are the proof obligations [PAGE 2, PAGE 4].

Biomechanical transfer veto. The player-selection application in the paper uses rating differences as numeric features for league transitions, for example the difference between Denmark Superliga and France Ligue 1 [PAGE 4]. That is a league-strength feature, not a player biomechanical measurement. Using it to infer injury risk, recovery, or physical readiness would commit a cross-layer leap. The paper’s intended use is “predicting soccer player metrics for forthcoming seasons” and “detailed analysis of a player performance in preceding matches” [PAGE 1], which is a statistical transfer adjustment, not a physiological model. The cross-layer veto therefore blocks any claim that the rating is a direct physical or medical signal.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
