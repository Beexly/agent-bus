Knight's foundational premise is expressed through the classical definition of probability:

\[
\boxed{P(E) = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}}} \quad [\text{Page } 233]
\]

Knight appeals to **Cournot's Principle of Insufficient Reason**, stating that whenever evidence offers absolutely no grounds for distinguishing among several possibilities, every rational agent must assign them equal probability. For instance, in a scenario where an urn contains an unknown proportion of red and black balls, but the drawer knows only that both colors exist, symmetry dictates \( P(\text{Red}) = P(\text{Black}) = 0.5 \).

Yet when additional information becomes available—such as knowing exactly how many balls of each color are present—the logic shifts. Knight rejects the notion that hidden causal factors force predetermined results as insufficient. Instead, he grounds probability in *ontic indifferibility*: the equality arises because the underlying mechanisms governing ball extraction are genuinely indifferent to the specific outcome, not because of our cognitive limitations.

Furthermore, Knight implicitly connects this to his understanding of **risk versus uncertainty** via the Law of Large Numbers. With a sufficiently large sample, empirical frequencies converge to theoretical probabilities—a procedure Knight considers valid only when the probabilistic structure is known *a priori* and holds constant over time. In contrast, genuine uncertainty transcends statistical describability.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight's engagement with the *Principle of Insufficient Reason* traces back centuries, crystallizing during the 18th century. It appeared explicitly in the works of **Jakob Bernoulli** (Ars Conjectandi, 1713) and **Pierre-Simon Laplace** (Théorie analytique des probabilités, 1812), who posited that symmetry in our knowledge obligates equal assignment of probabilities. However, Knight's critique breaks from this classical tradition: while Bayesians like de Moivre or Laplace used this principle to infer posterior beliefs, Knight anchors it in metaphysical indeterminacy.
The *Bernoulli-Laplace* framework dominated actuarial science and insurance mathematics throughout the 19th century. Yet Knight sidesteps potential fallacies common in that era—like conflating frequency-based probability with individual case judgments. His insight predates the Vienna Circle's logical positivism by decades; where Carnap later attempted to reduce probability to logical necessity, Knight insists that indetermination is an irreducible feature of market processes.
Crucially, Knight sidesteps the **St. Petersburg Paradox**, resolved centuries later by von Neumann-Morgenstern utility theory. He treats each outcome with equal weight, bypassing the need for diminishing marginal utility functions to explain finite gambles. Knightian uncertainty transcends neoclassical expected utility theory from its onset.
