The multi-hop derivation in arXiv:1603.05583v1 proceeds from an event log, not from continuous player tracking. Hop 1 is data provenance: the authors use “an event-based dataset generated by Opta [17] covering the 2012/13 season of La Liga” with “more than 300,000 passes and nearly 10,000 shots,” and “the precision of the time annotation of the dataset is one second” [PAGE 3]. Hop 2 is vectorization: each movement is represented as “a vector of seven: (x1, y1, x2, y2, T, s, b), where the movement starts at time T at location (x1, y1), ends at location (x2, y2), with speed s, while b denotes ball possession” [PAGE 3]. Hop 3 is aggregation by clustering: “we derive the most relevant K movement vectors of the competition using all the vectors of all the players” and “we use K = 200” with “mini-batch K-means clustering” [PAGE 4]. Hop 4 is player characterization: assign each movement vector to its nearest centroid, count frequencies, normalize by the player’s total movements. Hop 5 is comparison: “we use the cosine similarity to measure the distance between two players” [PAGE 6], then compute uniqueness and consistency. Hop 6 is application: similar-player retrieval, uniqueness ranking, consistency tracking, and chance-creation maps.

The gray-zone audit is where the paper must be read carefully. The core limitation is stated plainly: “the physical location of a player is only recorded when the player was involved in some ball-related event” and “the elapsed time between two events of a player can be as low as couple of seconds but it can reach several minutes too” [PAGE 4]. Therefore the movement vector is not a trajectory sample at fixed frequency; it is an event-triggered displacement. Any claim about continuous sprinting, acceleration, deceleration, change-of-direction load, or fatigue is underdetermined. The pass handling is another gray zone: “at the receiving end, the dataset does not contain a timestamp. To overcome this issues, and to increase the wealth of the extracted time-series, we use the timestamp of the previous event, i.e., the initiation of the pass” [PAGE 4]. That proxy may distort duration and speed estimates for passing sequences. Pitch geometry is also non-identical: “the length of the pitch shall be between 100 and 110 meters, while the width between 64 and 75 meters” and the paper gives Elche at 108x70 m and Rayo Vallecano at 100x65 m [PAGE 4]. The authors say they take field size into account, but no explicit transform is printed, so cross-stadium metric invariance is not fully auditable from the text. Human annotation is error-prone: event data “involves human operators who code the games” and “this technique is prone to human errors” [PAGE 2]. Tracking data would be finer, but “the trajectories of a player may be of within several meters” across systems [PAGE 2], and wearables are single-team only: “the dataset holds information only on the players of a single team, details on the ball and the opponent players are missing” [PAGE 3]. The empirical counts are large but not uniform: “660,848 movement vectors of 542 players,” mean 1,219 movements per player, maximum 4,998, mean length 19.4 m, maximum 100 m [PAGE 3]. Low-participation players are excluded from uniqueness: “we only consider players who had at least 500 movements across the season to avoid discrepancies due to players with small participation in the season” [PAGE 7]. The clustering itself is non-convex and depends on K=200; no uncertainty intervals are reported. The cosine distance is valid for nonnegative frequency vectors, but it is undefined for an all-zero characteristic. Consistency is game-wise; if N is small or if a player is a substitute, the metric can reflect participation and role changes rather than stable movement ability. The paper’s own Xavi example confirms this: “The two outliers at the beginning and at the end of the season are games where he was partially on the bench” [PAGE 8]. Finally, the market-value table is descriptive context, not a causal performance operator; the values are “based on the estimations of Tranfermarkt [21]” [PAGE 7]. Thus the valid inference space is coarse-grained, event-anchored movement similarity and season-scale role stability—not biomechanics, weather response, or continuous physical load.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(\mathcal E\) be the event log and let \(V_i=\{\mathbf v_{i,a}\}_{a=1}^{n_i}\) be the set of movement vectors for player \(i\). The paper defines the primitive movement vector as
\[
\mathbf v=(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2,T,s,b),
\]
where \(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2\) are pitch coordinates in metres, \(T\) is the start time in seconds, \(s\) is speed in \(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\), and \(b\in\{0,1\}\) is ball possession. Source quote: “We describe each movement as a vector of seven: (x1, y1, x2, y2, T, s, b), where the movement starts at time T at location (x1, y1), ends at location (x2, y2), with speed s, while b denotes ball possession” [PAGE 3]. The corpus has \(|\cup_i V_i|=660{,}848\) vectors over 542 players, mean \(n_i=1{,}219\), max \(n_i=4{,}998\), mean movement length \(19.4\ \mathrm{m}\), max \(100\ \mathrm{m}\) [PAGE 3].

The clustering operator is an approximate mini-batch \(K\)-means fit over all competition vectors:
\[
\{\boldsymbol\mu_k\}_{k=1}^{K}
=
\arg\min_{\{\boldsymbol\mu_k\}}
\sum_{\mathbf v\in\cup_i V_i}
\min_{k\in\{1,\dots,K\}}
\|\mathbf v-\boldsymbol\mu_k\|_2^2,
\qquad K=200.
\]
Source quote: “we derive the most relevant K movement vectors of the competition using all the vectors of all the players. We determine these features using the mini-batch K-means clustering algorithm [19]” and “Throughout this paper, we use K = 200” [PAGE 4]. The centroid \(\boldsymbol\mu_k\) is the representative movement of cluster \(k\). This is a non-convex, label-permutation-invariant fit; the paper does not report multiple restarts or cluster stability.

The player characteristic is a normalized frequency vector:
\[
c_i(k)=\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{\mathbf v\in V_i}\mathbf 1\!\left[k(\mathbf v)=k\right],
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{200}c_i(k)=1,
\]
where \(k(\mathbf v)=\arg\min_k\|\mathbf v-\boldsymbol\mu_k\|_2\). Units are dimensionless frequencies. The paper states that after assigning each movement to a cluster, “we aggregate the number of times a player applied each feature vector, which creates a frequency vector of the features. Finally, we normalize the frequencies with the total number of movements a player has” [PAGE 5]. Singularity regime: if \(n_i=0\), then \(c_i\) is \(0/0\) undefined; if \(n_i>0\) but all movements fall outside any valid cluster assignment, the normalization is still defined only under the implemented assignment rule. The paper’s stated low-sample guard is for uniqueness, not for characteristic construction: “we only consider players who had at least 500 movements across the season” [PAGE 7].

The similarity operator is cosine distance:
\[
D(c_i,c_j)=1-\frac{\langle c_i,c_j\rangle}{\|c_i\|_2\|c_j\|_2},
\]
with \(c_i,c_j\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{200}\). Source quote: “we use the cosine similarity to measure the distance between two players” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: if \(\|c_i\|_2=0\) or \(\|c_j\|_2=0\), the denominator is zero and \(D\) is undefined; if the vectors are nonnegative, \(D\in[0,1]\) under the paper’s use, but the paper does not print a numerical stability guard.

Uniqueness is
\[
U_i=\sum_{j=1}^{M}d_{ij},
\qquad
d_{ij}=D(c_i,c_j),
\qquad
M=5,
\]
where the sum is over the \(M\) most similar players. Source quote: “Let dij = D(ci, cj) denote the cosine distance between player i and j, where ci denotes the movement characteristic of player i. We compute the uniqueness of player i as:” and “The range of this metric is (0, M), the higher this value is the more unique a player is” [PAGE 6]. Units are dimensionless. Singularity: if fewer than \(M\) comparable players exist after filtering, the sum is not the paper’s intended quantity; if all \(d_{ij}\) are undefined due to zero vectors, \(U_i\) is undefined.

Consistency for player \(i\) in game \(k\) is
\[
C_i^k=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}D(c_i^k,c_i^t),
\]
where \(c_i^k\) is player \(i\)’s movement characteristic in game \(k\) and \(t=1,\dots,N\) indexes the games in which the player was involved. Source quote: “Let t = 1, . . . , N denote the games a player was involved in, while ck i denote the movement characteristic of player i for game k. The consistency of player i for game k is defined as the average pairwise distance of its movement characteristics” and “The range of consistency is (0, 1)” [PAGE 6]. Units are dimensionless. Breakdown regimes: \(N=0\) gives an empty sum and undefined \(C_i^k\); \(N=1\) gives \(C_i^k=0\) mechanically but provides no evidence of consistency; small \(N\) or partial-game participation can dominate the value, as the Xavi example shows [PAGE 8].

Two filters are explicitly defined. Ball-possession filtering keeps movements where both start and end events involve possession:
\[
V_i^{\mathrm{ball}}=\{\mathbf v\in V_i: b_{\mathrm{start}}=1\ \text{and}\ b_{\mathrm{end}}=1\}.
\]
Source quote: “we determine all the combinations of the events where a player has the ball (i.e., both the starting and ending events of the movement should involve ball possession)” [PAGE 5]. High-speed filtering keeps
\[
V_i^{\ge14}=\{\mathbf v\in V_i: s\ge 14\ \mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\}.
\]
Source quote: “We apply a threshold on the speed of the movements, i.e., the movements ought to be at least 14km/h. This is inline with the categories widely used in the soccer industry [23, 2]” [PAGE 5]. This is a binary threshold, not a continuous load variable.

The chance-creation operator selects movements in a pre-shot window:
\[
V_{\mathrm{shot}}(t_s)=\{\mathbf v: T\in[t_s-20\ \mathrm{s},\,t_s]\},
\]
where \(t_s\) is the shot time. Source quote: “The example presents the movements of a team up to 20 seconds earlier than taking a shot” [PAGE 10]. Units are seconds for the window and metres for coordinates. This is an exploratory operator; the paper calls it a preliminary step and says “A thorough analysis of these phenomena is a future work” [PAGE 10].

Exact breakdown and singularity regimes for the source operators are therefore: (i) \(n_i=0\) makes \(c_i\) undefined; (ii) \(\|c_i\|_2=0\) makes cosine distance undefined; (iii) \(N=0\) makes consistency undefined; (iv) \(N=1\) yields a degenerate zero that is not stability evidence; (v) fewer than 500 movements triggers exclusion from uniqueness, so rankings are not population-wide [PAGE 7]; (vi) \(K=200\) is fixed and mini-batch \(K\)-means is non-convex, so cluster labels and centroids are not uniquely identified; (vii) pitch dimensions vary from 100–110 m by 64–75 m, and the paper’s normalization transform is not printed [PAGE 4]; (viii) pass reception timestamps are proxied by the prior event, so duration and speed are aliased [PAGE 4]; (ix) event locations exist only for ball-related events, so continuous trajectories are unavailable [PAGE 4]; and (x) human event coding is “prone to human errors” [PAGE 2].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a progression from descriptive soccer statistics to event-based strategy analysis to movement profiling. The paper positions prior work as focusing on “the average number of shots, goals, fouls, passes” and on “the outcome of the strategies that teams apply” [PAGE 1], citing descriptive and network/strategy studies [1,5,18,16,13,11,9,24,14]. The movement-profiling step is retrocausal in the sense that later movement conclusions are projected back onto earlier event logs: the event log was not collected to measure physical movement, but the authors reconstruct movement characteristics from it. The enabling lineage includes Opta event coding [17], mini-batch \(K\)-means for web-scale clustering [19], cosine similarity for profile comparison, and prior work on estimating maximal speed from event data [10]. The paper also sits after FIFA’s 2015 authorization of wearable EPTS devices, but it explicitly notes wearable limitations [PAGE 3].

The multi-week traps are several. First, reputation and market value can be retrofitted as causes of similarity. The paper’s table reports Transfermarkt estimates [PAGE 7], but the method itself compares movement characteristics, not valuations. A multi-week analyst may see a low-cost similar player and mistakenly infer causal replacement value; the paper’s claim is narrower: similar movement profiles identify “potential candidates who may be able to replace a given player” [PAGE 2]. Second, uniqueness is not quality. The paper reports defenders who played “significant portions of the season on different sides of the field” as highly unique [PAGE 7], which may reflect role flexibility rather than superior performance. Third, consistency is not fitness. Xavi’s outliers are “games where he was partially on the bench” [PAGE 8], so a multi-week consistency trend can encode substitution patterns. Fourth, season aggregation hides within-season drift. A player may change role after a coaching change or injury; the season-level characteristic smooths that change. Fifth, the \(K=200\) representation is a modeling choice; different \(K\) or different clustering seeds can change the frequency vector and therefore similarity ranks. Sixth, pitch-size differences and the unpublished normalization can create cross-stadium artifacts [PAGE 4]. Seventh, weather is absent from the dataset description, so any multi-week weather narrative is external to the source. Eighth, the paper itself warns that “higher uniqueness comes at a price of consistency” [PAGE 9], so a single-week uniqueness spike should not be interpreted as a stable player trait.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The nested inference problem is: a scout infers a player’s role from movement characteristics; a coach infers whether that role is stable; the player responds to the game state and to the opponent; the opponent responds to the player. The event-based dataset observes only ball-related actions, so the observer’s belief about the player’s internal state or physical condition is underdetermined. In this setting, “crisis habit regression” is the analyst’s fallback to reputation or market value when the movement data are sparse or ambiguous. The paper’s market-value table makes this fallback tempting, but the method’s own logic is movement-profile similarity, not valuation [PAGE 7]. A concrete regression is to treat Cristiano Ronaldo’s high market value as evidence that his movement profile must be highly unique; the paper instead reports that he “seems to be just an average player in terms of the uniqueness and the consistency of his movements (0.55 and 0.51, respectively)” [PAGE 9]. The counter-trap is overcorrecting in the opposite direction: taking one distinctive route—for example, Messi’s preferred entries into the final third—as the player’s whole identity. The paper’s Messi figures show multiple movement subsets, including all movements, with-ball movements, and high-speed movements [PAGE 5], so a single filter is not the player.

The “clock” is the multi-week, game-wise structure. Consistency is defined over \(N\) games, and the paper explicitly shows Xavi’s season trace with two bench-related outliers [PAGE 8]. A recursive theory-of-mind reading is that the scout should update beliefs on the slow clock of season-scale consistency, not on a single match’s unusual movement set. Messi is the paper’s positive outlier with high uniqueness and high consistency (0.86 and 0.30) [PAGE 9], while Adriano is high-uniqueness but less consistent (0.75) [PAGE 9]. These are different agent models: one is a stable, hard-to-replace movement profile; the other is a flexible, role-varying profile. The counter-trap is to collapse both into “unique = better.” The correct recursive inference is conditional: unique and consistent suggests a stable distinctive role; unique and inconsistent suggests multi-role use or participation effects; consistent and non-unique suggests a reproducible, replaceable role. The clock also matters for replacement: a candidate who matches a player’s season characteristic may not match the player’s current role after a tactical change. Thus the model should maintain separate beliefs for season-level identity and recent-game role, and should not let a crisis (injury, transfer, bench) overwrite the slow-clock estimate without new multi-game evidence.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

The source can adjudicate movement similarity, coarse role stability, and event-anchored chance-creation patterns; it cannot adjudicate weather response, biomechanical load, or continuous physical condition.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: empty or degenerate player profile. Counterfactual: inject a player with \(n_i=0\) movement vectors, or a player with \(N=1\) game, into the uniqueness/consistency pipeline. Verifiable prediction: the characteristic \(c_i\) is undefined for \(n_i=0\), cosine distance is undefined if \(\|c_i\|_2=0\), and consistency is mechanically zero for \(N=1\) but carries no stability information. Veto: any ranking that reports a finite uniqueness or consistency for such a player without an explicit guard is an artifact, not evidence. This follows the paper’s normalization by total movements [PAGE 5] and its game-wise consistency definition [PAGE 6].

Scenario 2: pass-reception timestamp proxy. Counterfactual: on a subset where actual reception timestamps are available, recompute movement vectors using the true reception time instead of “the timestamp of the previous event, i.e., the initiation of the pass” [PAGE 4]. Verifiable prediction: pass-heavy players’ duration and speed estimates will shift, and their top-5 similarity lists or consistency values will change relative to the proxy-based pipeline. Veto: if the recomputation changes the top-5 replacement set or the uniqueness ordering beyond a pre-registered tolerance, then precise movement claims based on the proxy are vetoed for those players. The source itself calls the proxy “the best method for estimation” [PAGE 4], not an exact measurement.

Scenario 3: pitch-dimension normalization. Counterfactual: recompute all vectors after an explicit affine normalization to a common pitch, e.g., 105x68 m, and compare against the unnormalized or unspecified normalization pipeline. Verifiable prediction: players who played home games on extreme fields such as Elche’s 108x70 m or Rayo Vallecano’s 100x65 m [PAGE 4] will show larger rank shifts than players on near-standard fields. Veto: if the top-10 uniqueness list or the top-5 similarity lists change materially under explicit normalization, then cross-stadium uniqueness claims are vetoed unless the exact normalization transform is published and shown to be invariant. This is a verifiable stress test of the paper’s statement that field sizes are taken into account [PAGE 4], because the transform itself is not printed in the source.
