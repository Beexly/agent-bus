<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage of this corpus is a movement from electromechanical certainty to cyber-physical contingency. The preface frames the transformation: “energy supply systems are on the verge of a major transformation” [PAGE 8]. That sentence is the genealogical root. In the old regime, frequency stability, voltage support, and dispatchability were bundled into synchronous generators. In the new regime, those services are disaggregated: inverters must synthesize inertia, markets must price congestion and voltage security, demand response must substitute for reserve, gas networks must deliver fuel to power plants under pressure constraints, and restoration algorithms must recombine islands after blackout.

The first genealogical branch is control theory. Classical frequency control relied on governor droop and turbine dynamics. The self-triggered paper belongs to the networked-control lineage: event-triggered and self-triggered methods reduce communication by computing the next update from the current state [PAGE 12]. Its formal novelty is to make the triggering interval state-dependent and to include cyber cost in the control objective [PAGE 17]. The multi-week trap here is to treat communication as free. Once communication becomes scarce, the controller cannot increase update rate without paying an information-cost penalty. If a message contains \(B\) bits, the update rate \(R=B/\tau\) in bit/s becomes a design variable. A planner who ignores this will design a physically stable controller that is cyber-infeasible.

The second branch is power-electronics emulation. The virtual-inertia review traces the shift: “The modern power system is progressing from a synchronous machine-based system towards an inverter-dominated system” [PAGE 155]. The synchronverter lineage attempts full electromechanical replication. The Ise-lab lineage solves the swing equation directly. The VSG lineage responds to frequency derivatives. The droop lineage is older but can approximate inertia through measurement filtering [PAGE 165]. The trap is believing that all topologies are interchangeable. They are not: synchronverters and swing-equation topologies can operate as grid-forming units, while current-source VSG implementations are better suited to grid-following contexts [PAGE 159]. A system planner who ignores this will deploy the wrong topology for an islanded microgrid.

The third branch is probabilistic renewable planning. The congestion-management paper integrates wind probability into voltage-stability-constrained optimal power flow. The Weibull distribution [PAGE 34] and turbine power curve [PAGE 35] are not decorative; they define the feasible set of wind injection. The trap is replacing a distribution by its mean. Mean wind output can hide cut-out events, steep ramps, and congestion-driven locational mismatches. The paper’s voltage stability margin and line-outage contingency analysis show that renewable placement must be evaluated under probability, not merely under expected cost.

The fourth branch is gas-electric coupling. The simulation framework states that “steady state gas models do not account for the changes in linepack” [PAGE 115]. This is a genealogical correction: earlier integrated energy-system models often treated gas as an algebraic fuel source. In reality, gas has inventory, pressure dynamics, and delivery thresholds. The 30 bar-g GFPP minimum and 45-minute shutdown delay [PAGE 118] create a physical clock that can overrule market dispatch. The multi-week trap is to schedule power generation from gas plants without checking whether the gas network can sustain the required fuel pressure over the relevant transient horizon.

The fifth branch is restoration. The Korean black-start paper uses an enhanced Dijkstra algorithm to minimize generator start timing [PAGE 147]. Its genealogical lineage comes from graph search, unit commitment, and black-start planning. The trap is to optimize only generator capacity or cost, ignoring path connectivity, transformer stress, charging current, and cranking power. Restoration is not a static optimization; it is a sequence of feasibility checks under evolving topology.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory of mind is required because the energy system is not merely a physical plant; it is a multi-agent inference problem. The agents are system operators, market participants, gas operators, restoration crews, inverter controllers, and automated protection systems. Each agent forms beliefs about the others. The crisis is that habits formed under normal operation can become maladaptive under rare events.

The operator’s default habit is regression to deterministic schedules. In normal conditions, day-ahead unit commitment, fixed tariffs, pre-assigned restoration paths, and scheduled gas deliveries are efficient. Under stress, those habits can fail. The gas-power case shows why: if an operator assumes gas supply is always available at pressure, the operator will not anticipate GFPP shutdown when linepack declines. The source says the flow balance “is always negative” in the disrupted scenario [PAGE 122], meaning inventory is being consumed faster than it is replenished. A deterministic habit that ignores linepack will underestimate the risk of cascading failure.

The counter-trap is stochastic and event-aware reasoning. The demand-response paper models responsive load through elasticity and price signals. The multi-period elasticity relation is  
\[
DDR(t)=D(t)\left[1+\sum_h E(t,h)\frac{\rho(h)-\rho_0(h)}{\rho_0(h)}\right]
\]  
[PAGE 71]. Here \(D\) is demand in kW, \(\rho\) is price, and elasticity \(E(t,h)\) is dimensionless. This allows the operator to anticipate rebound peaks and price-induced shifting. The self-triggered paper similarly counters the habit of fixed sampling by computing the next control time from the current state [PAGE 12]. The virtual-inertia review counters the habit of treating inverter generation as passive by making the inverter responsive to \(df/dt\).

The clock is the third recursive dimension. The system contains multiple clocks: electromagnetic transients in milliseconds, frequency response in seconds, governor action in tens of seconds, secondary control in minutes, gas linepack dynamics in minutes to hours, restoration in tens of minutes to hours, and market clearing in hourly or day-ahead steps. The review states that governor response occurs “within the first few seconds (typically 10–30 s)” [PAGE 156]. The gas framework uses a 15-minute gas reference step and a one-hour power reference step in its case study [PAGE 118]. Restoration timing uses start-up delay plus cranking ramp plus line energization [PAGE 137]. A crisis habit that treats all clocks as one clock will either overact too early or act too late.

The recursive model can be expressed as a conditional feasibility policy. At time \(t\), the operator has information set \(\mathcal{I}_t\) containing forecasts, measurements, and contingency status. The operator chooses action \(a_t\) to minimize expected cost  
\[
\mathbb{E}[C(a_t,\omega_t)\mid\mathcal{I}_t]
\]  
subject to physical constraints. The adversary is not a person; it is the rare event that violates the operator’s default model. The rare event may be a gas supply disruption, a wind cut-out, a transmission outage, or a communication delay. The operator must maintain a counter-habit: check the binding physical constraint before applying the economic optimum.

The most dangerous recursion is overconfidence in optimization. If an operator believes the market solution is physically feasible without checking voltage, pressure, inertia, and path connectivity, the optimizer becomes a trap. The gas-power framework explicitly relaxes constraints during re-dispatch and then performs load shedding to restore feasibility [PAGE 114]. That is a recognition that economic dispatch may be infeasible under stress. The restoration framework similarly recalculates the next start unit at each step because system conditions change [PAGE 148]. A robust operator therefore treats every optimization result as provisional until cross-layer checks pass.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer adjudication asks whether any layer can veto an apparently optimal decision. The answer is yes: physical feasibility, weather-driven uncertainty, and biomechanical/human operational limits can veto economic or algorithmic optimality.

Physical veto. The first veto is power balance. The AC power-flow equations must hold: \(P_i(V)-P_{G,i}+P_{D,i}=0\) and \(Q_i(V)-Q_{G,i}+Q_{D,i}=0\) [PAGE 118]. If a dispatch violates these equations, it is not implementable. The second veto is voltage and thermal limits. The AC-OPF includes voltage magnitude bounds and line apparent-power limits [PAGE 118]. If a contingency causes voltage to fall below the minimum, the model applies load shedding until voltage recovers [PAGE 114]. The third veto is generator ramping and cranking. In restoration, a generator can be selected only if \(\sum P_{gen}-P_{start}\geq 0\) and if start time plus path energization satisfies the sequence [PAGE 137]. If cranking power is insufficient, the generator cannot start even if it has low fuel cost.

Gas-pressure veto. The gas layer can veto the power layer through fuel delivery. The GFPP fuel-gas equation \(L_{GFPP}=P_G/(\eta_T GCV)\) [PAGE 123] says that power output requires gas flow. But gas flow requires pressure above the delivery threshold. The case study sets \(p_{min}=30\) bar-g and a 45-minute shutdown delay [PAGE 118]. If transient gas simulation shows pressure below this threshold for the allowed delay, the gas layer vetoes the power schedule. The source demonstrates this: four GFPPs are shut down due to insufficient pressure, and the power system must re-dispatch or shed load [PAGE 121].

Weather veto. Weather enters through wind probability and load uncertainty. The Weibull wind model and turbine power curve define feasible wind injection [PAGE 34–35]. If wind speed falls below cut-in or rises above cut-out, wind output is zero. The congestion-management paper uses Monte Carlo simulation to account for wind and load uncertainty [PAGE 42]. A deterministic schedule that assumes mean wind can be vetoed by a cut-out event or by a steep forecast error. The ESS allocation paper similarly forecasts wind-load difference and uses three-point discretization of wind distribution to avoid relying on a single scenario [PAGE 101]. Weather therefore vetoes any plan that assumes average renewable output without tail-risk assessment.

Biomechanical/human veto. The restoration paper includes human and equipment timing through start-up delay, ramping, and path energization. Although the paper does not model fatigue explicitly, the formal timing constraint acts as a biomechanical veto: restoration crews, switching operations, and equipment start sequences cannot be compressed below physical limits. If \(t_{GEN,i}=ST_i+ETL+ETR\) exceeds the restoration deadline, the plan is invalid. In a crisis, human error risk increases, so the safest plan is not the one with the shortest nominal duration but the one with the largest margin against timing uncertainty.

Proof of veto priority. Let \(\mathcal{F}_{phys}\) be the set satisfying power-flow, voltage, thermal, generator, and gas-pressure constraints. Let \(\mathcal{F}_{weather}\) be the set satisfying probabilistic reserve or scenario feasibility. Let \(\mathcal{F}_{human}\) be the set satisfying switching and timing feasibility. The admissible set is  
\[
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{phys}\cap\mathcal{F}_{weather}\cap\mathcal{F}_{human}
\]  
If \(\mathcal{F}=\emptyset\), no economic optimum exists. If an economic optimizer returns a point outside \(\mathcal{F}\), the physical, weather, or human layer vetoes it. The corpus supports this priority: gas pressure shuts down GFPPs [PAGE 118], voltage violations trigger load shedding [PAGE 114], and restoration sequences require feasible start times [PAGE 137].

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios follow. Each is stated as a counterfactual, a prediction, and a verification method.

1. Cyber-cost veto in self-triggered frequency control  
Counterfactual: An operator selects a larger decay rate \(\beta\) to reduce frequency error, believing physical performance alone determines the best controller.  
Prediction: Because the minimal triggering interval shrinks as \(\beta\) increases, the communication-cost term \(\varrho/\tau(k)\) rises. The aggregate cost \(J\) can increase even though the physical state converges faster. The source supports this: “large β may bring a higher communication cost, because the controller has to reduce the triggering interval to satisfy higher physical cost requirements” [PAGE 11].  
Verification: Reproduce the self-triggered design and plot \(\tau_{min}\) against \(\beta\). Check whether the number of control actions rises. The paper reports about 530 control actions for the proposed method versus about 600 for the conventional method [PAGE 16], showing that trigger reduction is measurable. If a higher-\(\beta\) design increases message count enough to raise \(\int \varrho/\tau dt\), the cyber-cost veto is confirmed.  
Formal veto condition: If \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left[\int_0^{T_f}\|x\|_Q^2dt+\int_0^{T_f}\varrho/\tau(k;\beta)dt\right]>0\), the higher-\(\beta\) policy is vetoed by aggregate cost despite improved local convergence.

2. Gas-linepack veto in integrated gas-electric operation  
Counterfactual: A power operator dispatches GFPPs based on steady-state gas availability and assumes gas pressure will remain adequate because current supply equals current demand.  
Prediction: Under supply disruptions, linepack declines. The gas model shows that “the flow balance, which is the sum of inﬂow minus sum of outﬂow, is always negative” [PAGE 122]. As linepack falls, pressure approaches the GFPP minimum delivery threshold. Once pressure stays below 30 bar-g beyond the allowed 45-minute delay, GFPPs shut down, causing generation loss and possible load shedding.  
Verification: Run the integrated simulation with gas-side disruptions and compare steady-state gas assumptions against transient gas simulation. The source shows pressure reductions at CBI, production field, and LNG facilities, and the shutdown of GFPPs due to insufficient pressure [PAGE 121–123]. If the steady-state model predicts no GFPP shutdown while the transient model predicts shutdown, the gas-linepack veto is confirmed.  
Formal veto condition: If there exists a time interval \([t_1,t_2]\) such that \(p_{GFPP}(t)<p_{min}\) and \(t_2-t_1\geq 45\) min, then any power schedule relying on that GFPP without reserve replacement is vetoed.

3. Inverter-topology veto in weak grids  
Counterfactual: A planner selects a VSG topology for an islanded microgrid because it is simple and provides frequency-responsive power injection.  
Prediction: The VSG equation \(P_{VSG}=K_D\Delta\omega+K_I d\Delta\omega/dt\) [PAGE 163] depends on frequency derivative measurement. In weak grids, PLL-based frequency estimation and derivative amplification can produce noise, instability, or poor transient sharing. Synchronverter or Ise-lab topologies, which do not require frequency derivatives for inertia emulation, are more suitable for grid-forming islanded operation.  
Verification: Implement the same virtual inertia benchmark with synchronverter, Ise-lab, and VSG topologies under a step load. The source compares settling time, ROCOF, peak power, and energy exchange. It reports that the synchronverter, Ise-lab, and VSG all reduce ROCOF, but the VSG has higher settling time and energy exchange than the synchronverter in the presented benchmark [PAGE 159–160]. If the VSG implementation in a weak islanded case shows larger oscillations, higher energy use, or failure to maintain synchronism, the topology veto is confirmed.  
Formal veto condition: If the measured ROCOF derivative has signal-to-noise ratio below the threshold required for stable \(K_I\) action, or if the PLL frequency estimate has error variance that makes \(K_I d\Delta\omega/dt\) dominate \(K_D\Delta\omega\), the VSG topology is vetoed for that islanded application.

These three veto scenarios are verifiable because they rely on source equations, source simulations, and measurable quantities: trigger count, gas pressure and linepack, and frequency response metrics. They also demonstrate the frontier principle: integrated energy-system reasoning must not optimize one layer while ignoring the binding constraints of another layer. The physical plant, the communication channel, the weather distribution, and the human restoration clock each possess veto power over the final plan.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
