The paper transforms the open-target problem into a closed-set viability problem: with \(K=\mathbb{R}^N\setminus R\), Ursula wants the state to leave \(K\), while Victor wants the state to remain in \(K\) forever [PAGE 4]. The first multi-hop step is therefore to separate target reachability from set viability. The second step is strategic: Ursula is restricted to Carathéodory strategies \(\bar u(\cdot,\cdot)\), measurable in \(t\) and continuous in \(x\) for almost every \(t\) [PAGE 4]. Victor’s counter-strategy class is the set of time-measurable controls \(\mathcal N=\{v(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to V,\ \text{measurable}\}\) [PAGE 4]. This asymmetry matters: Victor’s admissible object is a control path, while Ursula’s admissible object is a feedback strategy. The third step is the definition of Victor’s retorting victory domain: initial \(x_0\in K\) such that for every Ursula strategy \(C\in\mathcal U\) there exists a solution of the differential inclusion \(\dot x(t)\in f(x(t),C(t,x(t)),V)\) remaining in \(K\) on \([0,\infty)\) [PAGE 5]. Ursula’s blind victory domain is the complement: she can find a Carathéodory strategy such that every solution leaves \(K\) in finite time [PAGE 5]. The alternative principle is then almost tautological under that definition, but the causality principle is not: the source states that Victor’s retorting definition “does not satisfy a priori the principle of causality” because Victor uses “not only the knowledge of Ursula’s past and present strategy, but also that of her future strategy” [PAGE 6]. That is the literal retrocausal hinge.

The fourth step is the causal repair. The paper does not give a fully rigorous causal victory definition, but identifies the causal domain with the largest set inside \(K\) from which Victor can ensure remaining in \(K\), and says that the closure \(D\) of such a set satisfies a tangential condition involving the contingent cone \(T_D(x)\) [PAGE 6]. The largest closed discriminating domain is the discriminating kernel \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\) [PAGE 7]. The measurable viability theorem gives the inclusion \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\subseteq\bigcap_{C\in\mathcal U}\mathrm{Viab}_{f(\cdot,C(\cdot,\cdot),V)}(K)\), but Proposition 2.1 asserts that for some admissible dynamics and closed \(K\) this inclusion is strict enough to destroy the alternative under causal play [PAGE 7]. The source’s own summary is that if Victor plays causally, “alternative property does not hold”: there are initial positions where Ursula cannot surely win with Carathéodory strategies and Victor cannot surely win causally [PAGE 7].

The fifth step is the counterexample. Take \(K=[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}\) in the plane, controls \(u,v\in[-1,1]\), and \(\psi(t)=e^t/(1+e^t)\) [PAGE 8]. The source notes that \(\psi\) is increasing, \(\psi(t)\gt\psi(-t)\) for \(t\gt0\), \(\lim_{t\to-\infty}\psi(t)=0\), \(\lim_{t\to+\infty}\psi(t)=1\), and \(\psi(t)\in[0,1]\) [PAGE 8]. The vertical coordinate is a clock: the proof uses \(y(t)=y_0+t\) explicitly on [PAGE 9]. The first step proves \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)=\varnothing\) [PAGE 9]. The argument is a boundary-pressure argument: if a point belonged to the discriminating kernel, then using \(u=1\) would force it eventually into \([-1,1]\times[T,\infty)\); but for \(y\ge T\) and \(x_0\lt 1/2\), Ursula can use \(u=-1\) and Gronwall’s lemma gives \(x(t)\lt -1\) for sufficiently large \(t\); symmetrically, for \(x_0\gt 1/2\), Ursula can use \(u=1\) to force \(x(t)\gt 1\) [PAGE 9-10]. Hence no closed discriminating domain survives; the causal Victor domain is empty.

The sixth step is the apparent contradiction: for every Carathéodory strategy, there is still a viable solution from some set of initial positions. Lemma 3.1 shows that for any Carathéodory strategy, any \(z\in[-1,1]\), and any \(t\ge0\), there are \(x_0\in[-1,1]\) and a solution from \((x_0,0)\) staying in \(K\) on \([0,t]\) and reaching \(x(t)=z\) [PAGE 10-11]. Corollary 3.1 then gives nonemptiness of the viable intersection at time infinity [PAGE 11]. Lemma 3.2 gives a point \((x_0,y_0)\in K\) with \(y_0\lt0\) such that for any Carathéodory strategy and any \(z\in[-1,1]\), a solution stays in \(K\) on \([0,|y_0|]\) and reaches \(x(|y_0|)=z\) [PAGE 12]. Concatenating that with the viable solution from \((z,0)\) yields a solution staying in \(K\) forever from \((x_0,y_0)\) [PAGE 12]. Thus \((x_0,y_0)\) lies in the retorting intersection but not in the causal kernel. The gray-zone audit is therefore precise: the paper does not claim that the game is “broken” in every sense; it claims that the Carathéodory/control asymmetry cannot simultaneously satisfy alternative and causality in general [PAGE 3-4]. Any inference that causal Victor can always win, or that Carathéodory feedbacks approximate the discontinuous-strategy class, is explicitly blocked by [PAGE 7]. Any physical, biomechanical, or weather interpretation is also outside the source: the paper gives no force, torque, energy, weather, or measurement model. The source’s formal core is the controlled state equation and the viability operator. On [PAGE 4] the system is
\[
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),u(t),v(t)),\qquad u\in U,\quad v\in V,
\]
with \(x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^N\), \(u\) Ursula’s control, and \(v\) Victor’s control. Time has units of seconds when \(t\) is interpreted physically; \(f\) has units of state-units per second; \(U\) and \(V\) are metric compact spaces and \(V\) is a convex subset of \(\mathbb{R}^d\) [PAGE 5]. The paper assumes \(f:B^N\times U\times V\to\mathbb{R}^N\) continuous, \(f(\cdot,u,v)\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz for every \(u,v\), and \(f\) is affine in \(v\) [PAGE 5]. In physical units, \(L\) has units of inverse seconds if the state is measured in state-units, while the controls are dimensionless only in the paper’s normalized example.

Definition 1.1 on [PAGE 4] defines a Carathéodory strategy as a map
\[
\bar u(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}_+\times\mathbb{R}^N\to U
\]
that is measurable in \(t\) and continuous in \(x\) for almost every \(t\gt0\). The set of such strategies is denoted \(\mathcal U\). Victor’s counter-strategy class is
\[
\mathcal N=\{v(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to V,\ \text{measurable}\}.
\]
For \(C(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathcal U\), a solution of
\[
\dot x(t)\in f(x(t),C(t,x(t)),V),\qquad x(0)=x_0,
\]
exists for almost every \(t\ge0\) if and only if there is \(v(\cdot)\in\mathcal N\) solving \(\dot x(t)=f(x(t),C(t,x(t)),v(t))\) [PAGE 5]. The solution set is denoted \(S_{f(\cdot,C(\cdot,\cdot),V)}(x_0)\) in the example [PAGE 8].

Victor’s retorting victory domain is the set of \(x_0\in K\) such that for every \(C\in\mathcal U\) there is a solution remaining in \(K\) on \([0,\infty)\) [PAGE 5-6]. Formally,
\[
\mathcal V_{\mathrm{ret}}(K)=
\{x_0\in K:\ \forall C\in\mathcal U,\ \exists x(\cdot)\in S_{f(\cdot,C(\cdot,\cdot),V)}(x_0),\ \forall t\ge0,\ x(t)\in K\}.
\]
Ursula’s blind victory domain is the complement in \(K\): there exists \(C\in\mathcal U\) such that every solution leaves \(K\) in finite time [PAGE 5]. The causal Victor domain is identified with the largest closed discriminating domain contained in \(K\), the discriminating kernel \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\) [PAGE 7]. The tangential condition quoted on [PAGE 6] is the requirement that the admissible velocity set meet the contingent cone \(T_D(x)\) at every boundary point of the candidate discriminating set \(D\); the contingent cone is cited to Aubin [PAGE 6, reference [1]].

The critical formal comparison is
\[
\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\subseteq\bigcap_{C\in\mathcal U}\mathrm{Viab}_{f(\cdot,C(\cdot,\cdot),V)}(K),
\]
which the source says “always holds true” by the Measurable Viability Theorem [PAGE 7]. Proposition 2.1 asserts that there are dynamics satisfying the assumptions and closed \(K\) where the causal and retorting domains separate; the source’s words are: “There are some initial positions for which Ursula cannot surely win if she plays Carath6odory strategies, and where Victor cannot surely win if he plays in a causal way” [PAGE 7]. The breakdown regime is therefore not a numerical singularity in \(f\); it is a well-definedness breakdown at the interface of alternative and causality. The paper states on [PAGE 3]: “the game is not well-defined, i.e., either the ‘alternative’ or the ‘causality’ is not satisfied for that game.” On [PAGE 4] it repeats: “Either the game does not satisfy the alternative principle ... or the game does not satisfy the causality principle.”

The counterexample uses \(K=[-1,1]\times\mathbb{R}\), \(u,v\in[-1,1]\), and
\[
\psi(t)=\frac{e^t}{1+e^t}.
\]
The source records \(\psi\) increasing, \(\psi(t)\gt\psi(-t)\) for \(t\gt0\), \(\lim_{t\to-\infty}\psi(t)=0\), \(\lim_{t\to+\infty}\psi(t)=1\), and \(\psi(t)\in[0,1]\) [PAGE 8]. The vertical coordinate is explicitly used as a clock: \(y(t)=y_0+t\) [PAGE 9]. The first-step conclusion is “\(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)=\varnothing\)” [PAGE 9, OCR: “Discf(Ii') is empty”]. The boundary mechanism is the use of constant Ursula controls \(u=-1\) or \(u=1\) near \(x=\pm1\), combined with Gronwall’s lemma: the source states that \(x(t)\lt -1\) for sufficiently large \(t\) when \(x_0\lt1/2\), and symmetrically \(x(t)\gt1\) when \(x_0\gt1/2\) [PAGE 9-10]. Lemma 3.1 and Corollary 3.1 restore nonemptiness of the viable intersection at infinity [PAGE 10-11]. Lemma 3.2 gives \((x_0,y_0)\) with \(y_0\lt0\) from which, for any Carathéodory strategy and any \(z\in[-1,1]\), a solution remains in \(K\) on \([0,|y_0|]\) and reaches \(x(|y_0|)=z\) [PAGE 12]. The concatenation proof then uses the viable solution from \((z,0)\) to remain in \(K\) forever [PAGE 12]. The final boundary repair is the projection \(x_1(t)=x(t)\) if \(x(t)\in[-1,1]\), \(x_1(t)=1\) if \(x(t)\gt1\), and \(x_1(t)=-1\) if \(x(t)\le -1\), after which the paper proves that \((x_1(\cdot),y_1(\cdot))\) is still a solution of the differential inclusion [PAGE 14].

Assumptions and units must be stated explicitly: \(x\) is in state-units, \(t\) in seconds if physical, \(f\) in state-units per second, \(L\) in inverse seconds, and \(u,v\) are dimensionless in the example because they lie in \([-1,1]\). The paper provides no force, torque, energy, power, weather, or measurement units. The exact breakdown regimes are: first, \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)=\varnothing\) while the retorting intersection is nonempty in the example [PAGE 9-12]; second, failure of Carathéodory approximation of discontinuous strategies, stated as “there is no way to approximate by a Carathkodory feedback such discontinuous strategies” [PAGE 7]; third, boundary breakdown at \(x=\pm1\), where the state is clamped into \(K\) [PAGE 14]; fourth, clock breakdown at \(y=0\) and at the threshold \(T\), where \(y(t)=y_0+t\) drives the concatenation argument [PAGE 9-13].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage inside this source is viability theory plus differential games. The paper cites Aubin’s 1991 “VIABILITY THEORY” for contingent cones and viability domains [PAGE 6, reference [1]], Cardaliaguet’s 1994 Paris IX thesis on “Domaines discriminants en jeux diffirentiels” for discriminating domains [PAGE 7, reference [2]], a forthcoming paper on a differential game for discontinuous strategies [PAGE 7, reference [3]], and the Measurable Viability Theorem of Frankowska, Plaskacz, and Rzeżuchowski [PAGE 15, reference [4]]. The abstract’s lineage is explicit: “Pursuit and Evasion Games, Viability Theory” [PAGE 3]. The retrocausal genealogy is not mystical; it is the paper’s own distinction between a retorting Victor who knows Ursula’s future strategy and a causal Victor who must decide from past and present information. The source says that in the retorting definition Victor uses “not only the knowledge of Ursula’s past and present strategy, but also that of her future strategy” [PAGE 6]. That is a future-information genealogy: the retorting domain is an omniscient benchmark, while the discriminating kernel is the causal object.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are long-horizon errors that survive short-horizon checks. Trap one is to verify only the differential inclusion and forget the quantifier order. Short-horizon viability may hold for each fixed \(C\), but the causal quantifier requires a single Victor policy that works against all \(C\) without future knowledge. The source’s Proposition 2.1 is precisely a warning that the two quantifier structures separate [PAGE 7]. Trap two is to treat the retorting intersection as if it were causal because it is nonempty for every fixed strategy. The counterexample shows that a point can be in the intersection over all \(C\) yet outside \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\), because \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\) is empty [PAGE 9-12]. Trap three is to overgeneralize Carathéodory feedbacks. The paper says that for some class \(\mathcal U'\) of discontinuous strategies there is equality in the relevant equation, but “there is no way to approximate by a Carathkodory feedback such discontinuous strategies” [PAGE 7]. Trap four is to ignore the clock. The vertical coordinate \(y(t)=y_0+t\) turns the game into a timed approach to \(y=0\); the concatenation at time \(|y_0|\) is not a cosmetic detail but the mechanism that creates the counterexample [PAGE 12-13]. Trap five is boundary clipping. The projection in [PAGE 14] is needed because a solution that reaches \([-1,1]\times\{0\}\) may previously have left \(K\); the clipped trajectory is proved to remain a solution, but only after a case analysis at \(x=\pm1\). In multi-week terms, these traps compound: a model can appear viable over a short window because \(T\) has not been crossed, then fail when \(y\) reaches the threshold or when \(x\) presses against the boundary. The paper’s genealogy therefore points forward to robust viability and discontinuous-strategy games, not to a simple feedback approximation.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind structure is asymmetric. Ursula chooses a Carathéodory strategy “at the beginning of the game, and cannot change it throughout the game” [PAGE 6]. Victor, in the retorting domain, is modeled as knowing Ursula’s future strategy [PAGE 6]. That is a first-order omniscient opponent. The causal Victor is lower-information: he must use only past and present strategy, which is why the causal domain is the discriminating kernel [PAGE 7]. The counterexample adds a second recursion: Ursula’s constant-control habit at the boundary is not a global strategy; it is a local crisis response. At \(x_0\lt1/2\), she uses \(u=-1\); at \(x_0\gt1/2\), she uses \(u=1\) [PAGE 9-10]. This is “crisis habit regression”: when the state approaches a boundary, the agent falls back to a simple saturated control that pushes the state out of \(K\). It works for the boundary-pressure proof, but it is not a uniform Carathéodory policy that simultaneously solves every initial condition. The paper’s Lemma 3.1, by contrast, reverses time and constructs a viable solution for any fixed Carathéodory strategy [PAGE 10-11]. That reversal is the counter-trap: the agent who thinks only in forward time sees the boundary as a threat; the viability proof uses time reversal to show that a viable preimage exists.

The clock is the third recursive layer. The vertical coordinate \(y(t)=y_0+t\) [PAGE 9] gives a countdown to \(y=0\). Lemma 3.2 says that for any Carathéodory strategy and any \(z\in[-1,1]\), a solution stays in \(K\) on \([0,|y_0|]\) and reaches \(x(|y_0|)=z\) [PAGE 12]. That is a timed counter-trap: the agent can choose the terminal horizontal position after the clock has run down. The concatenation with a viable solution from \((z,0)\) then extends the trajectory to infinity [PAGE 12]. In recursive terms, the agent who forecasts only one step sees a boundary; the agent who forecasts the clock sees a controlled rendezvous. The habit regression is the constant \(u=\pm1\) push at the boundary; the counter-trap is the \(|y_0|\)-time reachability to any \(z\); the clock is the \(y\)-coordinate that makes the two compatible. The source’s conclusion is that the Carathéodory causal model cannot resolve this recursion: either alternative or causality fails [PAGE 3-4]. A theory-of-mind model that assigns Victor future knowledge wins the retorting game from \((x_0,y_0)\), but that same model is not causal; a model that restricts Victor to causal information has empty \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\) in the example [PAGE 9]. The correct recursive conclusion is therefore not “Victor is stronger” or “Ursula is stronger,” but “the information order changes the victory domain.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

The state is a point \(x\in\mathbb{R}^N\), the controls are compact-space elements \(u\in U\), \(v\in V\), and the only regularity assumptions are continuity, Lipschitz continuity in state, and affinity in \(v\) [PAGE 5]. There is no force \(F\) in newtons, no torque in newton-metres, no energy in joules, no power in watts, no body-segment mass, no joint-angle limit, no fatigue state, no surface friction, no wind vector, and no precipitation variable. Therefore any claim that a physical body, biomechanical system, or weather process can execute the paper’s controls is underdetermined by the source. The physical veto proof is conditional: if an application imposes an admissible state set \(\mathcal A_{\mathrm{phys}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^N\), then the viable set must be replaced by \(K\cap\mathcal A_{\mathrm{phys}}\). If \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)\cap\mathcal A_{\mathrm{phys}}=\varnothing\), the causal Victor cannot guarantee remaining in the physically admissible region even if the mathematical \(K\) is nonempty. In the counterexample, \(\mathrm{Disc}_f(K)=\varnothing\) [PAGE 9], so no physical refinement can rescue a causal guarantee from that kernel; it can only restrict the already empty set.

The biomechanical veto is stronger. The paper allows measurable controls with no rate bound, no acceleration bound, and no energy cost. A biomechanical layer would require \(u(t)\in U_{\mathrm{bio}}(t)\) and \(v(t)\in V_{\mathrm{bio}}(t)\) with additional differentiability or torque limits. If those sets are empty along a required trajectory, the trajectory is vetoed regardless of the mathematical differential inclusion. The paper’s boundary clipping at \(x=\pm1\) [PAGE 14] is a mathematical projection, not a biomechanical saturation law; treating it as a muscle or joint limit would be an unsupported cross-layer import. The weather veto is similar: the source’s \(f\) is deterministic. A weather layer would add an exogenous disturbance \(w(t)\in W\), for example \(\dot x=f(x,u,v)+g(x)w(t)\), and would require a robust or stochastic viability statement. The paper provides no \(g\), no \(W\), and no noise model. Therefore a weather claim can only veto an application by showing that the required viable trajectory leaves \(K\cap\mathcal A_{\mathrm{phys}}\) under admissible disturbances. The source’s own breakdown regime is mathematical: empty discriminating kernel, noncausal retorting domain, and Carathéodory approximation failure [PAGE 7, PAGE 9]. Cross-layer adjudication must not convert those mathematical statements into physical guarantees. The only defensible cross-layer conclusion is: if the physical, biomechanical, or weather layer is not explicitly modeled, the paper’s victory domains are necessary but not sufficient for real execution.
