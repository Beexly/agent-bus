<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a two-player, imperfect-information, non-cooperative, repeated game-theoretic target-tracking paper. The central derivation chain is: continuous/discrete point-mass kinematics → noisy bearing measurements → cubature Kalman filter (CKF) state estimation for both players → one-step enumeration of admissible headings → min-max/max-min covariance-trace payoff → own-heading selection → threshold-based stopping or finite-horizon win declaration. The paper explicitly avoids a single common zero-sum game: “Rather than modelling a zero-sum game between the tracker and the evader, which presupposes the availability of perfect information, and therefore unrealistic, in this paper we model both the tracker and the evader as playing separate zero-sum games at each time step with an internal (and imperfect) model of the other player, which each player constructs based on the imperfect state information they have about the other player” [PAGE 1]. That sentence is the load-bearing assumption. Each player solves a local game against a simulated opponent; the simulated opponent is built from the player’s own CKF estimate, not from common knowledge. Consequently, the equilibrium object is not a Nash equilibrium of the true two-player game. It is a pair of one-step min-max/max-min choices under model misspecification.

The multi-hop derivation proceeds as follows. First, positions and velocities are defined as $\mathbf p_{T,k}=[x_{T,k},y_{T,k}]^\top$ and $\mathbf v_{T,k}=v_T[\cos\theta_{T,k},\sin\theta_{T,k}]^\top$, with analogous evader variables [PAGE 3]. The discrete dynamics are straight-line updates $\mathbf p_{T,k+1}=\mathbf p_{T,k}+\Delta\mathbf v_{T,k}$ and $\mathbf p_{E,k+1}=\mathbf p_{E,k}+\Delta\mathbf v_{E,k}$ [PAGE 3]. Second, bearings are nonlinear arctangent measurements with additive independent zero-mean Gaussian noise [PAGE 3]. Third, the CKF converts the nonlinear bearing into a Gaussian state estimate through eight cubature points, predicted measurement, innovation variance, cross-covariance, Kalman gain, and covariance update [PAGE 3–4]. Fourth, for each candidate own heading and candidate simulated-opponent heading, the player computes a one-step updated position-covariance block and evaluates its trace. The tracker’s game operator is $\arg\min_{\theta^{(l)}_{T,k}}\max_{\theta^{(m)}_{E,k}}\operatorname{tr}(P^{xy(l,m)}_{E,k+1|k+1})$; the simulated evader uses $\arg\max_{\theta^{(m)}_{E,k}}\min_{\theta^{(l)}_{T,k}}\operatorname{tr}(P^{xy(l,m)}_{E,k+1|k+1})$ [PAGE 6, eqs. (35)–(36)]. Fifth, only the real player’s own heading is applied: “only the heading angle θ∗ T,k for the tracker is of consequence” [PAGE 6]. The simulated opponent heading is discarded. Sixth, the process repeats until a covariance threshold or $k_{\max}=400$ is reached; the evader also wins if the tracker fails to win by the end [PAGE 6–8].

The gray-zone audit exposes several issues. The paper calls the framework “sequential,” but the simulation states: “At each step, both players must decide their next course of action without knowing the other’s choice” [PAGE 6]. That is a simultaneous-move repeated game, not an extensive-form sequential game with observed prior moves. The “sequential” label is therefore about repeated time indexing and internal simulation, not about perfect-order play. Second, the payoff is a covariance trace, not capture probability or physical distance; the paper uses three win metrics, and a tracker win occurs if any metric crosses a minimum threshold at any point [PAGE 7–8]. That path-dependent early-stopping rule can reward a transient favorable geometry rather than sustained tracking. Third, the stopping equation $\psi_e=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(P_{k|k})}<\eta$ [PAGE 4, eq. (18)] is dimensionally inconsistent if $P_{k|k}$ is the full four-state covariance: the position block is in spatial units squared, while the velocity block is in spatial units squared per time-step squared. The trace sums unlike units unless $\Delta$ and the spatial unit are numerically normalized. Fourth, bearing-only observability is not established; if both vehicles move nearly constant-course, range can remain weakly observable, and the CKF covariance may not reflect true error. Fifth, the min-max/max-min payoff can be degenerate: if $N=1$, if all headings yield identical trace, or if the payoff matrix is constant, the argmin/argmax is not unique and the “game-theoretic” label has no operational content. These are not minor implementation details; they define the domain in which the paper’s win-percentage claims are meaningful.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from von Neumann–Morgenstern game theory and Nash equilibrium, through zero-sum min-max and extensive-form/sequential/subgame concepts, into bearings-only tracking and nonlinear Kalman filtering. The paper cites Nash equilibrium as “a set of strategies in a strategic game where no player has an incentive to unilaterally deviate” [PAGE 2] and distinguishes simultaneous from sequential games: “a sequential game is an extensive-form game where players choose their strategies in a predeﬁned order” [PAGE 3]. It then inherits the bearings-only tracking lineage of EKF, modified polar EKF, UKF, CKF, pseudolinear, instrumental-variable, and particle filters [PAGE 2], choosing CKF for “the trade-off between the estimation performance and computational efﬁciency” [PAGE 2]. The maritime-defense lineage is explicit in the introduction: “the capability to efﬁciently track and predict the movements of target vessels is critical due to the complex nature of maritime environments” [PAGE 1]. The retrocausal move is that the tracker’s future win is treated as caused by current information quality, current internal model, and current heading choice; the paper’s title and abstract frame tracking as an “information-driven interaction” [PAGE 1].

The multi-week traps are mostly model-validity traps. Trap one: equating “game-theoretic” with globally optimal. The paper’s own construction uses separate zero-sum games with internal imperfect models [PAGE 1], so each player can solve the wrong game correctly. Trap two: treating covariance trace as capture. The paper uses covariance trace, estimation error, and true separation as separate metrics [PAGE 7–8], and a win can be declared by any one threshold crossing. A lower covariance does not imply smaller true separation; it can reflect favorable bearing geometry or a prematurely confident filter. Trap three: overgeneralizing the 400-step horizon. The paper notes that “if the experiments are simulated for a longer time period, then the linear escape action would not be as effective” [PAGE 11]. The evader’s apparent linear-escape advantage is partly a clock artifact: “the evader just needed to avoid losing” [PAGE 10] and a draw counts as an evader win. Trap four: assuming static decision rules. The paper’s own limitation is that “an agent is committed to a particular way of decision making, a certain decision making action or procedure, from beginning to end” [PAGE 12]. Mixed or adaptive switching is left for future work. Trap five: discrete speed ratios and constrained starting grids. The paper simulates $v_T,v_E\in\{1,2,3\}$ and random starts in $40\times40$ grids centered at fixed points [PAGE 6], so the win percentages are conditional on those distributions, not universal. Trap six: the “infinite dimentions” [PAGE 6] phrase could mislead a reader into thinking the state space is infinite-dimensional; it is a planar unbounded grid. Trap seven: reading “linear escape is most effective” as a general law. The paper says “a simpler linear escape action is most effective for the evader in most scenarios” [PAGE 1], but also that game theory helps the evader under severe speed disadvantage [PAGE 10]. The correct lineage is conditional: simple habits dominate when the clock and draw rule favor survival; recursive game reasoning matters when brute force is insufficient.

The paper states that the tracker “plays a zero-sum game with its internal model of the evader” and the evader “plays a zero-sum game with its internal model of the tracker” [PAGE 5–6]. This is not full common-knowledge recursion, because the tracker does not model the evader modeling the tracker modeling the evader, and the evader does not model the tracker modeling the evader modeling the tracker. It is one level of simulated opponent plus repeated time-step updating. That truncation is important: the resulting choices are local best responses to estimated opponents, not equilibrium strategies of the true coupled system.

Crisis habit regression appears in the evader’s results. Under ordinary conditions, the simpler linear escape habit is highly effective: “the evader is better off following a linear escape strategy, which gives the evader a win percentage of 55% or above for all speed ratios” [PAGE 10]. But under severe speed disadvantage, the evader benefits from game-theoretic reasoning: “the evader is better off playing game theory when ‘brute force’ (in this case, speed) is not in its favour” [PAGE 10]. That is the paper’s “where there is less brawn, there must be more brain” conclusion [PAGE 12]. The tracker shows the opposite habit profile: game theory helps in all tested regimes, and “for all speed ratios, the tracker is better off making decisions by using game theory” [PAGE 9]. The tracker is under a structural clock disadvantage because it must satisfy a win criterion before $k_{\max}$, while the evader wins a draw [PAGE 7–8]. So the tracker’s crisis is permanent within the simulation: it is always on the clock.

Counter-trap reasoning is not tested but is implied. If the evader knows the tracker is using a fixed internal model and a fixed one-step min-max rule, the evader could try to induce a false belief—for example, feint a linear escape and then turn, or choose headings that make the tracker’s estimated covariance trace fall while true separation grows. The paper does not test deception beyond the evader’s own internal game. Similarly, the tracker could attempt to exploit a predictable linear-escape evader, but the paper finds game theory still outperforms covariance minimization for the tracker [PAGE 9]. The counter-trap risk is that a player’s recursive model becomes self-confirming: the tracker’s internal evader is simulated from the tracker’s own noisy estimate, so if that estimate is biased, the tracker solves a coherent but wrong game.

The clock is decisive. The paper defines evader victory if the tracker does not win by the end of the simulation [PAGE 8], and it acknowledges that the evader “just needed to avoid losing” [PAGE 10]. When both parties use game-theoretic decision making, the evader’s win percentage “drops below 10% for speed ratios around unity” [PAGE 10], but the evader’s linear-escape habit remains strong because the finite clock rewards survival. The recursive model therefore has a temporal failure mode: deeper reasoning can be locally correct but globally dominated by a survival rule that exploits the horizon. The paper’s own future-work note—that longer experiments would weaken linear escape [PAGE 11]—is the right counterfactual: the recursion’s value depends on the clock, not just on the speed ratio.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is strong because the source assumes point-mass, constant-speed, straight-line motion between discrete steps [PAGE 3]. Real maritime platforms have finite turn rate, acceleration limits, drag, current, and wave-induced drift. If the feasible heading set is constrained by
\[
|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k|\le \omega_{\max}\Delta,
\qquad
|v_{k+1}-v_k|\le a_{\max}\Delta,
\]
then many of the paper’s enumerated headings are physically inadmissible. The game operator in eqs. (35)–(36) [PAGE 6] would then be optimized over an action set that the vessel cannot execute. The veto proof is direct: if the true feasible set $\mathcal A_k^{\mathrm{phys}}$ is a strict subset of the paper’s $\mathcal A_k$, the computed min-max heading may be infeasible; the reported win percentage is not transferable without re-solving the game on $\mathcal A_k^{\mathrm{phys}}$. The paper assumes constant speed, so any acceleration or current-induced speed change violates eqs. (1)–(2) [PAGE 3].

The mechanical/biomechanical layer is analogous rather than literal: the paper studies vessels, not biological bodies, but the same veto applies to platform dynamics and human-in-the-loop latency. If a human operator cannot perceive, decide, and execute a heading change within $\Delta$, the discrete-time action model is invalid. Bearing sensors have field-of-view limits, occlusion, sea clutter, rain, fog, and multipath; the paper instead assumes independent zero-mean Gaussian bearing noise [PAGE 3]. Weather can make that noise biased, heavy-tailed, or outlier-contaminated. In that case the innovation variance $P^{zz}$ in eq. (13) [PAGE 4] is mis-specified, the Kalman gain $W$ is wrong, and the covariance trace used as the game payoff is not a calibrated uncertainty measure. The veto condition is: if the measurement density is not approximately $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$, or if line-of-sight is frequently blocked, the CKF’s covariance trace cannot adjudicate the game.

The weather veto is particularly relevant to the paper’s “complex nature of maritime environments” [PAGE 1]. Add an unmodeled current $\mathbf c_k$ and the true dynamics become $\mathbf p_{k+1}=\mathbf p_k+\Delta(\mathbf v_k+\mathbf c_k)$; the linear-escape baseline and the game-theoretic predictions both change because the evader’s effective velocity is no longer $v_E[\cos\theta,\sin\theta]^\top$. If current or wind drift is comparable to the speed difference $|v_T-v_E|$, the speed-ratio conclusions [PAGE 9–10] lose meaning. The cross-layer veto is therefore: the paper’s conclusions are valid only inside a domain $\mathcal D$ of constant speed, straight-line motion, Gaussian bearings, no significant current/wind, no turn-rate saturation, and unoccluded line-of-sight. Outside $\mathcal D$, the correct procedure is to re-simulate with the physical feasible set, non-Gaussian measurement model, and environmental disturbance; otherwise the game-theoretic win percentages are conditional artifacts, not physical guarantees.
