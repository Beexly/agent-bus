The derivation begins by treating the paper as a measurement system rather than as a collection of architectural motifs. The system receives a broadcast image of a jump athlete, extracts visual features, queries arbitrary anatomical points, and emits heatmaps from which coordinates are decoded. The core inference chain is: image → HRNet features → visual tokens → keypoint query tokens → Transformer mixing → per-query heatmaps → DARK coordinate refinement. The paper’s central claim is that this pipeline can detect “arbitrary keypoints on the whole body of the athlete” by using “auto-generated segmentation masks of body parts” [PAGE 1]. That claim is not merely an architectural statement; it is a geometric claim about how unlabeled body regions become supervised targets.

The first multi-hop step is the conversion of segmentation masks into pseudo-ground-truth keypoints. For a body part with enclosing annotated keypoints \(k_1,k_2\), the paper constructs an intermediate point \(k_i\) by affine interpolation, then draws an orthogonal line and selects intersection points with the segmentation boundary. This creates a continuum of target points inside the mask. The key invariant is that the generated point must remain inside a coherent mask region. The source explicitly states: “We further add the constraint that cl and cr need to be part of a coherent area of the segmentation mask in order to deal with small errors in our automatically generated segmentation masks” [PAGE 3]. This is a robustness operator: it prevents the pseudo-label generator from jumping across holes, blur artifacts, or disconnected components caused by DensePose failures.

The second multi-hop step is the head problem. The head is not treated like a limb because the annotated head keypoint lies in the middle of the head, not at the crown. The authors therefore extend the neck-head line to boundary points \(k_t\) and \(k_b\). This solves the lower-half limitation but introduces a geometric singularity: near the crown, the orthogonal width collapses. The source states: “The distance between cl and cr is decreasing and even approaching 0, the closer ki is to kt” [PAGE 4]. In formal terms, if the local mask width \(w(k_i)=\|c_r-c_l\|_2\) approaches zero, the intersection-based thickness coordinate becomes ill-conditioned. A prediction of \(0.02\,\mathrm{m}\) error can represent a large fraction of the local body width when \(w\) is itself only a few centimeters.

The third multi-hop step is the angular head encoding. Instead of extending the neck-head vector, the method rotates a line around the head keypoint. This is better suited to the rounded geometry of the head. The source reports a large gain: “A significant difference is observed regarding the head keypoint. The angle strategy achieves an absolute improvement of over 10% for all experiments” [PAGE 6]. For the broadcast dataset, the gain is still larger: “The improvement is even slightly larger, with an absolute increase of over 11% for all variants” [PAGE 8]. The gray-zone audit here is that the angular method introduces a side-selection ambiguity: an unoriented line at angle \(\alpha\) is equivalent to the same unoriented line at \(\alpha+\pi\), but the chosen intersection point switches from \(c_r\) to \(c_l\). If the side convention is not stable, the model receives contradictory supervision for the same visual line orientation.

The fourth multi-hop step is bent limbs. Standard limb interpolation fails when elbows or knees are strongly bent, which is frequent in jumping. The paper introduces an anchor point on the inner side of the joint and then rotates a line around that anchor. This converts a simple orthogonal thickness problem into a constrained angular sweep between the upper and lower body parts. The source notes that elbows and knees are necessary for continuity: including them “enables retrieving continuous keypoints along the whole arms and legs” [PAGE 6]. However, the paper also identifies a failure mode: if \(c_l\) and \(c_r\) swap sides between the upper and lower body part, “we can encounter a collapse of the keypoints around the swapping location” [PAGE 6]. This is a topological failure of the side convention, not merely a local regression error.

The fifth multi-hop step is evaluation. The paper replaces a vague notion of “arbitrary” with a reproducible evaluation protocol: fixed keypoints plus generated points at specified thicknesses, yielding a large query set per image. The PCK threshold is tied to a body-scale reference, the left shoulder-right hip distance, with \(t=0.1\) and \(t=0.05\) corresponding approximately to \(6\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\) [PAGE 6]. This matters because it makes the metric interpretable in SI units. A model may be good at coarse localization but fail when the required precision falls below a few centimeters. The hardest parts are hands and feet, as the paper repeatedly notes, and the normalized-pose encoding is especially weak there because “the area of these body parts has the lowest size in the normalized pose template” [PAGE 7].

The final gray-zone audit concerns distribution shift. The jump-broadcast dataset includes “sports sites, lighting conditions and image quality” that vary, and it contains “a lot of extreme poses, especially during the jump phase” [PAGE 2]. Slow-motion replays are treated as new camera perspectives: “slow motion replays are mostly recorded with a different camera and therefore seen as a new camera perspective” [PAGE 2]. Therefore, any reasoning about model behavior must include broadcast artifacts, motion blur, compression, and unusual viewing angles. The strongest interpretation is not that the model “knows” anatomy, but that it learns a conditional distribution over mask-consistent query points under heterogeneous visual evidence.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let an input image be \(I:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3\), with pixel coordinates in meters after camera-scale normalization. The feature extractor \(F_{\mathrm{HR}}\) maps \(I\) to multi-resolution feature maps. Patchification and linear projection produce visual tokens:
\[
Z_v = \{z_i^v\}_{i=1}^{N_v}, \qquad z_i^v = W_v\,\mathrm{vec}(P_i(F_{\mathrm{HR}}(I))) + b_v,
\]
where \(W_v\in\mathbb{R}^{192\times d_p}\), \(d=192\) is the embedding dimension, and \(N_v\) is the number of visual patches. The model uses TokenPose-Base with input size \(192\times 256\), embedding dimension \(192\), and heatmap size \(48\times 64\) [PAGE 5].

A keypoint query code \(c_q\) is converted to a query token:
\[
z_q = E_q(c_q)\in\mathbb{R}^{192}.
\]
The full token sequence is
\[
X = [Z_v; Z_q]\in\mathbb{R}^{(N_v+N_q)\times 192}.
\]
Self-attention is
\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)V,
\]
with \(d=192\). Since ViT supports variable sequence length, the query count \(N_q\) can vary: “Since ViT architectures can deal with inputs of various length, it is possible to query for an arbitrary number of keypoints” [PAGE 5].

For each queried point, the output token is mapped by a shared MLP to a heatmap:
\[
\widehat{H}_q\in\mathbb{R}^{48\times 64}.
\]
The coordinate is decoded using DARK-style distribution-aware coordinate representation [PAGE 3]. If \(p_{x,y}\) is the decoded probability mass over heatmap locations, a coordinate expectation is
\[
\widehat{k}_q = \sum_{x,y} p_{x,y}\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix},
\]
then transformed from heatmap pixels to image meters by the person-scale factor.

For a body part with enclosing keypoints \(k_1,k_2\in\mathbb{R}^2\), define
\[
v_e = k_2-k_1,\qquad u=\frac{v_e}{\|v_e\|_2},\qquad n=R_{\pi/2}u,
\]
where \(R_{\pi/2}\) rotates by \(90^\circ\). The intermediate point is
\[
k_i = p\,k_1 + (1-p)\,k_2,\qquad p\in[0,1].
\]
The orthogonal line is
\[
\ell_o(s)=k_i+s\,n,\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}.
\]
Let
\[
S = \{s\in\mathbb{R}: \ell_o(s)\in \partial M_b\},
\]
where \(M_b\) is the segmentation mask for body part \(b\) and \(\partial M_b\) is its boundary. The left and right intersection points are
\[
c_l = \ell_o(s_l),\quad c_r = \ell_o(s_r),
\]
with side selection relative to the orientation of \(v_e\). The final keypoint is
\[
k_f = q\,c_s + (1-q)\,k_i,\qquad q\in[0,1],\quad c_s\in\{c_l,c_r\}.
\]
The thickness vector encodes \(q\). If \(c_r\) is selected, one may write
\[
\tau = [q,\,1-q,\,0],
\]
and if \(c_l\) is selected,
\[
\tau = [0,\,1-q,\,q].
\]
This matches the paper’s description that the middle entry is \(1-q\) and the first or last entry is \(q\), depending on the chosen side [PAGE 5].

For the torso, the reference is the neck and the midpoint of the hips:
\[
k_{\mathrm{hip,mid}}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{hip},L}+k_{\mathrm{hip},R}}{2}.
\]
The keypoint-vector entries are set so that the neck receives \(p\) and each hip receives \(0.5(1-p)\), reflecting the virtual midpoint [PAGE 5].

For the head extension method, let \(k_h\) be the head keypoint and \(k_n\) the neck keypoint. Extend the line through \(k_n,k_h\) to boundary points \(k_t,k_b\). Then
\[
k_i = p\,k_t + (1-p)\,k_b.
\]
The local orthogonal width is
\[
w(k_i)=\|c_r-c_l\|_2.
\]
The breakdown regime is
\[
\lim_{k_i\to k_t} w(k_i)=0.
\]
The source states this directly: “The distance between cl and cr is decreasing and even approaching 0, the closer ki is to kt” [PAGE 4]. If \(w(k_i) < \delta\), with \(\delta\approx 0.03\,\mathrm{m}\) corresponding to the stricter PCK threshold [PAGE 6], the thickness coordinate becomes numerically unstable.

For the head angle method, define an angular parameter
\[
\alpha\in[0,2\pi),\qquad u(\alpha)=R(\alpha)u_0,
\]
where \(u_0\) is the neck-head direction. The query line is
\[
L(\alpha)=\{k_h+s\,u(\alpha):s\in\mathbb{R}\}.
\]
The encoded angular percentage is
\[
p_\alpha = \frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\in[0,1).
\]
The final keypoint is
\[
k_f = q\,c(\alpha) + (1-q)\,k_h,
\]
where \(c(\alpha)\) is the selected boundary intersection. The paper notes that lines at angular percentages differing by \(0.5\) are geometrically identical as unoriented lines but differ in side selection: \(c_r\) is used for percentage \(p\), while \(c_l\) is used for \(0.5+p\) [PAGE 5]. The degeneracy is
\[
L(\alpha+\pi)=L(\alpha)
\]
as an unoriented set, but the ray-side label changes. Breakdown occurs if the side convention is unstable near \(\alpha=0\) or \(\alpha=\pi\), where the line coincides with the neck-head vector.

For bent elbows and knees, let \(k_j\) be the joint, \(k_p\) the proximal keypoint, and \(k_d\) the distal keypoint. Define unit vectors
\[
u_1=\frac{k_p-k_j}{\|k_p-k_j\|_2},\qquad
u_2=\frac{k_d-k_j}{\|k_d-k_j\|_2}.
\]
The bending angle is
\[
\beta = \arccos(u_1^\top u_2)\in[0,\pi].
\]
The anchor-generation line has half-angle geometry:
\[
\angle(u_{\mathrm{anchor}},u_1)=\angle(u_{\mathrm{anchor}},u_2)=\frac{\beta}{2},
\]
on the acute side of the joint. The anchor point is
\[
k_a = \partial M_{\mathrm{unified}}\cap \ell_i
\]
selected inside the acute wedge, where \(M_{\mathrm{unified}}\) is the merged upper/lower body mask. Then a rotating line \(\ell_a(\theta)\) around \(k_a\) is swept between the upper-part normal and lower-part normal. Its intersection with \(v_e\) gives \(k_i\), and the final keypoint is chosen between \(k_i\) and \(k_a\), or between \(k_i\) and an outer point \(k_o\). The failure regime is side collapse:
\[
\mathrm{sign}((c_r-k_i)^\top n_{\mathrm{upper}})
\neq
\mathrm{sign}((c_r-k_i)^\top n_{\mathrm{lower}}),
\]
which corresponds to the paper’s warning: “If cl and cr swap the side between the lower and upper body part, we can encounter a collapse of the keypoints around the swapping location” [PAGE 6].

The evaluation operators are:
\[
\mathrm{PCK}(t)=\frac{100}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}
\mathbf{1}\left(\|\widehat{k}_q-k_q\|_2\le t\,d_{\mathrm{ref}}\right),
\]
where \(d_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is the left shoulder-right hip distance. The source says: “The PCK uses the distance between the left shoulder and right hip keypoints times the threshold as the maximum distance for a correct prediction” [PAGE 6]. The thresholds are \(t=0.1\) and \(t=0.05\), corresponding approximately to \(6\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\) [PAGE 6].

The thickness metric is
\[
\mathrm{PCT}(t)=\frac{100}{|Q_{\mathrm{th}}|}\sum_{q\in Q_{\mathrm{th}}}
\mathbf{1}\left(|\widehat{\tau}_q-\tau_q|\le t\right),
\]
with \(t=0.2\) [PAGE 6]. Here \(\tau_q\) is a side-aware thickness coordinate in \([0,1]\), so PCT is dimensionless.

An information bound for querying a body part of visible area \(A_b\) in \(\mathrm{m}^2\) at precision \(\delta\) in meters is
\[
B_{\mathrm{req}} = \log_2\left(\frac{A_b}{\delta^2}\right)\ \mathrm{bits}.
\]
With \(\delta=0.03\,\mathrm{m}\), a \(0.01\,\mathrm{m}^2\) body region requires roughly
\[
B_{\mathrm{req}}\approx \log_2\left(\frac{0.01}{0.0009}\right)\approx 3.47\ \mathrm{bits},
\]
while a \(0.001\,\mathrm{m}^2\) region requires about \(6.8\) bits. This is not a learned loss; it is an information-theoretic accounting of the spatial resolution demanded by the metric. If the query embedding cannot distinguish that many effective modes, performance degrades.

Breakdown regimes include:
1. Empty or missing mask: \(M_b=\varnothing\), so \(\partial M_b\cap\ell_o=\varnothing\).
2. Disconnected coherent component: the constraint fails if \(c_l,c_r\) belong to different components, contrary to the stated coherent-area requirement [PAGE 3].
3. Head-top collapse: \(w(k_i)\to 0\) as \(k_i\to k_t\) [PAGE 4].
4. Angular side ambiguity: \(L(\alpha+\pi)=L(\alpha)\) but side selection changes [PAGE 5].
5. Bent-joint swap: \(c_l,c_r\) switch sides, causing collapse [PAGE 6].
6. Environmental degradation: motion blur, low resolution, compression, or lighting changes increase boundary uncertainty, consistent with “The sports sites, lighting conditions and image quality vary throughout our dataset” [PAGE 2].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits at the intersection of three lineages: video-based sports analysis, human pose estimation, and dense body-part segmentation. The sports lineage is explicit from the opening: coaches use video analyses to assess performance, derive improvements, and develop training routines, especially in triple, high, and long jump where athletes care about “step frequency, speed, and body posture during the in-run and the jump phase” [PAGE 1]. This creates the applied requirement: fine-grained body locations are not cosmetic; they are inputs to biomechanical assessment.

The pose-estimation lineage moves from fixed keypoint sets to queryable keypoints. The paper states that “Typical human pose estimation datasets provide only a very limited set of keypoints, which is not sufficient in this case” [PAGE 1]. The prior solution of adding more keypoints is rejected because it requires annotating all new keypoints in most images and remains bounded by a fixed set. The retro-causal trap is to treat keypoint detection as a classification problem over a finite list. Once that assumption is made, every new analytic need requires a new annotation campaign. The paper breaks the loop by converting keypoint specification into a query input.

The architectural lineage is TokenPose, ViT, HRNet, and DARK. TokenPose uses visual tokens plus keypoint query tokens; ViT patchifies images or feature maps; HRNet supplies high-resolution features; DARK decodes heatmaps. The paper’s novelty is not a new backbone but a replacement of learned fixed keypoint tokens with human-readable query encodings embedded into token space. This is why the method can be applied to “any TokenPose variant” [PAGE 3].

The segmentation lineage comes from DensePose and detectron2. The dataset uses automatically generated masks, but the authors admit that some masks are “completely or partly wrong” [PAGE 3]. Instead of discarding all imperfect masks, they retain partially correct masks and add a coherence constraint. This is a key historical move: dense segmentation becomes a substrate for pseudo-labeling even when imperfect. The genealogy therefore includes a shift from clean human annotations to noisy-but-usable masks.

A multi-week trap is to assume that better masks alone solve the problem. They do not. The paper still needs geometric operators to convert masks into queryable targets. Another trap is to assume that limbs generalize directly to head, hands, and feet. The head requires extension or angular methods; hands require extrapolating beyond the wrist-hand keypoint; bent elbows and knees require anchor points. Another trap is to ignore evaluation scale. The paper’s use of \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\) and \(6\,\mathrm{cm}\) thresholds reveals that “arbitrary” is only meaningful if the error tolerance matches the anatomical scale [PAGE 6]. A final trap is to ignore broadcast domain shift: slow-motion replays and varying camera perspectives are part of the problem structure, not incidental noise [PAGE 2].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive belief structure involves four agents: the coach, the annotator, the model, and the verifier. The coach believes that specific anatomical points are needed to evaluate jump mechanics. The annotator believes that fixed keypoints are the practical way to provide supervision. The model believes that a query code can stand in for a desired point. The verifier believes that metrics such as PCK and PCT can certify correctness. The paper’s design changes the beliefs of the annotator and model by making arbitrary queries possible without enumerating every point.

Crisis habit regression occurs when performance fails on hands, feet, elbows, or knees. The habitual response is to add more fixed keypoints. This is a regression because it increases annotation burden and still leaves the system trapped in a finite taxonomy. The source identifies the limitation: “Adding more keypoints to a fixed set requires annotating all new keypoints in most images, which is again very time consuming and the resulting model is still limited to a larger fixed set of keypoints” [PAGE 1]. Under crisis, the annotator may believe that more labels will solve the problem; the model may believe that more output channels will solve the problem. Both beliefs are recursive because they reproduce the same bottleneck.

The counter-trap is query-based supervision. The model is told which point to detect through an input encoding rather than through a fixed output head. The annotator no longer needs to annotate every possible point; instead, mask-based generation creates a continuum of pseudo-targets. The counter-trap succeeds only if the query encoding is expressive enough and the mask geometry is reliable enough. The paper compares vector encodings and normalized-pose encodings. The normalized pose is conceptually clean, but it underperforms for small parts: “The normalized pose approach has especially difficulties with the hands and feet which might be due to the fact that the area of these body parts has the lowest size in the normalized pose template” [PAGE 7]. This is a recursive failure: the canonical template compresses small parts, so the model’s belief about hand or foot location is degraded before inference begins.

The clock consists of evaluation discipline and data hygiene. The dataset is split so that each athlete appears in only one subset: “We split the dataset in 1805 images for training, 576 images for testing and 122 images for validation in such a way that each athlete is only included in a single subset” [PAGE 3]. This prevents identity leakage from inflating perceived performance. The evaluation protocol also creates a clock: fixed keypoints plus generated points at specified thicknesses produce a reproducible query set. The paper says this strategy “is reproducible and hence provides a comparable metric for future work” [PAGE 6]. The clock is not temporal; it is procedural. It forces the system to compare models under the same query distribution.

Recursive mind-reading appears in the head encoding. The annotator believes the head should be represented by a center and a direction. The extension method believes the neck-head axis is sufficient. The angular method believes that a radial sweep better matches the round head. The verifier observes that the angular method improves head keypoint scores substantially: over \(10\%\) absolute improvement on the triple and long jump dataset [PAGE 6], and over \(11\%\) on the jump-broadcast dataset [PAGE 8]. The model’s belief state must therefore include the geometry of the body part, not merely the presence of a segmentation mask. If the model assumes all parts behave like elongated limbs, it fails on the head; if it assumes all parts behave like radial blobs, it may fail on thin limbs. The counter-trap is part-specific query logic.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is valid when a lower-layer geometric or physical constraint proves that a higher-layer prediction is inadmissible. Let \(\widehat{k}\) be a predicted keypoint, \(M\) the observed segmentation mask, and \(K\) the kinematic chain containing \(\widehat{k}\). Define the feasible set
\[
\mathcal{F} = \left\{k\in M\cup\partial M \;:\; k \text{ satisfies local kinematic closure} \right\}.
\]
If
\[
\widehat{k}\notin \mathcal{F},
\]
the prediction is vetoed.

The first veto is mask inconsistency. If the predicted point lies outside the segmentation mask by more than an uncertainty radius \(\eta\), it is inadmissible:
\[
d(\widehat{k},M)>\eta \implies \text{veto}.
\]
Here \(d(\cdot,\cdot)\) is Euclidean distance in meters. The uncertainty radius \(\eta\) should include segmentation error, blur, and boundary ambiguity. Since the paper uses automatically generated masks and admits that some are partly wrong [PAGE 3], a nonzero \(\eta\) is required. If \(\eta\) is set to the stricter PCK tolerance, \(\eta=0.03\,\mathrm{m}\), then any prediction farther than \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\) outside the mask cannot be verified under the strict metric.

The second veto is side-collapse at bent joints. For a bent knee or elbow, define the side sign relative to the upper and lower segments:
\[
s_{\mathrm{upper}} = \mathrm{sign}\left((c_r-k_i)^\top n_{\mathrm{upper}}\right),
\]
\[
s_{\mathrm{lower}} = \mathrm{sign}\left((c_r-k_i)^\top n_{\mathrm{lower}}\right).
\]
If
\[
s_{\mathrm{upper}}\neq s_{\mathrm{lower}},
\]
then the side convention has swapped. The paper explicitly links this to collapse: “If cl and cr swap the side between the lower and upper body part, we can encounter a collapse of the keypoints around the swapping location” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, a prediction near the joint is vetoed if neighboring query predictions show sign reversal or if the decoded points cluster abnormally close to the joint in a way inconsistent with the anchor geometry.

The third veto is head-top width collapse. For the extension method, define
\[
w(k_i)=\|c_r-c_l\|_2.
\]
If
\[
w(k_i)<\delta,
\]
with \(\delta=0.03\,\mathrm{m}\), the local thickness coordinate is not reliably identifiable. The source states: “The distance between cl and cr is decreasing and even approaching 0, the closer ki is to kt” [PAGE 4]. A prediction in this regime is vetoed for the extension encoding unless the model can demonstrate stable boundary intersections. The angular encoding is preferred because it avoids the vanishing-width problem near the crown.

The fourth veto is environmental. The dataset includes varying “sports sites, lighting conditions and image quality” [PAGE 2]. Let \(\eta_{\mathrm{env}}\) be the boundary uncertainty induced by blur, compression, and low illumination. If
\[
\eta_{\mathrm{env}} \ge \frac{w_{\min}}{2},
\]
where \(w_{\min}\) is the minimum local body-part width, then left/right boundary points cannot be distinguished. In that regime, no thickness prediction can be certified below the \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\) threshold. This is a physical-layer veto: the image evidence does not contain enough spatial information to support the requested metric.

A formal proof sketch for environmental veto:
1. Assume the true boundary is \(B^*\) and observed boundary is \(B\).
2. Let Hausdorff distance satisfy \(d_H(B^*,B)\le \eta_{\mathrm{env}}\).
3. If the true width is \(w^*\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{env}}\ge w^*/2\), the observed intersections may lie on the wrong side or disappear.
4. Therefore the estimated thickness \(q\) has error bounded below by a fraction of \(w^*\).
5. If \(w^*\le 2\eta_{\mathrm{env}}\) and \(\eta_{\mathrm{env}}\ge 0.03\,\mathrm{m}\), the PCK@0.05 criterion cannot be guaranteed.
Thus the prediction is vetoed at the physical layer.

Biomechanical veto can also be expressed through segment length consistency. For a chain \((k_a,k_j,k_d)\), let observed segment lengths be
\[
L_1^{\mathrm{obs}}=\|k_a-k_j\|_2,\qquad
L_2^{\mathrm{obs}}=\|k_j-k_d\|_2.
\]
A predicted configuration \((\widehat{k}_a,\widehat{k}_j,\widehat{k}_d)\) should satisfy
\[
\left|\|\widehat{k}_a-\widehat{k}_j\|_2-L_1^{\mathrm{obs}}\right|\le \epsilon,
\]
\[
\left|\|\widehat{k}_j-\widehat{k}_d\|_2-L_2^{\mathrm{obs}}\right|\le \epsilon.
\]
If \(\epsilon\) is exceeded beyond plausible segmentation error, the prediction violates rigid-body segment consistency and is vetoed. This veto is conservative because broadcast pose can include foreshortening, but it remains useful as a coarse admissibility check.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios for a reward or verification layer. In each case, the verifier checks a measurable condition. If the condition fails, the predicted keypoint is rejected.

1. Hand-extension veto.
The paper handles hands by extending the wrist-hand line to the hand mask boundary because fingertip annotations are absent. The formal check is: the predicted hand point must lie on the extrapolated wrist-hand ray and inside the hand mask, or within an uncertainty margin. Let \(u_{wh}\) be the unit vector from wrist to hand keypoint. The predicted point \(\widehat{k}_{hand}\) must satisfy
\[
\widehat{k}_{hand} = k_{wrist} + \lambda u_{wh} + r,
\]
with \(\lambda\ge 0\) and residual norm \(\|r\|_2\le \eta_{hand}\). If \(\eta_{hand}=0.03\,\mathrm{m}\) and the residual exceeds this, veto. Counterfactual: if the model uses the normalized-pose encoding, the verifier should predict lower hand scores because the hand area is small in the template. The paper supports this: “The normalized pose approach has especially difficulties with the hands and feet” [PAGE 7]. Prediction: vector or angle encodings will outperform normalized pose on hand thickness metrics, while normalized pose may still be competitive for head or torso. If a counterfactual deployment observes normalized-pose hand predictions failing the extension check, the veto is confirmed.

2. Bent-joint side-consistency veto.
For elbow and knee queries, the verifier checks whether the decoded points remain on the same side of the upper and lower body-part axes. Define the side signs \(s_{\mathrm{upper}}\) and \(s_{\mathrm{lower}}\) as above. If
\[
s_{\mathrm{upper}}\neq s_{\mathrm{lower}}
\]
for adjacent thickness queries, or if predicted points collapse within a radius smaller than the expected local width, veto. The source predicts this failure when side swapping occurs: “If cl and cr swap the side between the lower and upper body part, we can encounter a collapse of the keypoints around the swapping location” [PAGE 6]. Counterfactual: if the anchor-based generation is removed and the model reverts to naive limb interpolation, the verifier should observe more collapse events near strongly bent knees and elbows, especially in jump phases. Prediction: anchor-based generation reduces collapse rate and improves continuity across the joint, even if aggregate PCK changes only modestly.

3. Head-width instability veto.
For head queries, the verifier computes the local boundary width \(w(k_i)\) for the extension method. If
\[
w(k_i)<0.03\,\mathrm{m},
\]
the extension encoding is vetoed unless the angular encoding is used. The verifier can also check prediction variance across nearby angular queries. Let
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{k}_{head}) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\|\widehat{k}_{head}^{(m)}-\bar{k}_{head}\|_2^2.
\]
If the standard deviation exceeds \(0.03\,\mathrm{m}\) in the high-curvature crown region, veto. The paper supports the preference for angular encoding on the head: “A significant difference is observed regarding the head keypoint. The angle strategy achieves an absolute improvement of over 10% for all experiments” [PAGE 6], and for the broadcast dataset, “The improvement is even slightly larger, with an absolute increase of over 11% for all variants” [PAGE 8]. Counterfactual: if a system trained with the extension method is queried near the crown, the verifier should predict high variance and low PCK@0.05. If switching to the angle method reduces variance and increases head PCK, the veto is confirmed.

These vetoes are verifiable because they rely on mask geometry, side conventions, and metric thresholds rather than subjective visual inspection. They also respect the paper’s own breakdown regimes: incoherent masks [PAGE 3], vanishing head width [PAGE 4], bent-joint collapse [PAGE 6], and environmental degradation [PAGE 2]. A reinforcement or verification layer should assign zero reward to predictions that fail these checks, even if the raw model confidence is high. The counterfactual prediction is that the best overall strategy will be part-specific: angle encoding for the head, vector encoding with careful side handling for limbs, and conservative abstention for hands and feet when mask width or environmental uncertainty exceeds the \(3\,\mathrm{cm}\) certification scale.
