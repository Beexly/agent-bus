The source’s core operator is binary logistic regression. The paper writes verbatim: “Log(p/1-p) = B0 + B1X1 + B2X2 + B3X3 + … + BiXi” [PAGE 18]. Formally, for player \(i\),
\[
Y_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i),\qquad 
\operatorname{logit}(p_i)=\ln\!\left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right)=B_0+\sum_{j=1}^{k}B_jX_{ij},
\]
\[
p_i=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid X_i)=\frac{\exp(B_0+\sum_j B_jX_{ij})}{1+\exp(B_0+\sum_j B_jX_{ij})}.
\]
Here \(Y_i=1\) means “depicted by an existing or commissioned subject-specific stadium/ballpark statue at the primary club or franchise,” and \(Y_i=0\) means not depicted. \(p_i\) is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). The coefficients \(B_j\) are in natural-log probability-ratio units, i.e. logit units or nats per one-unit change in \(X_j\). \(\exp(B_j)\) is a dimensionless multiplicative factor on \(p/(1-p)\) for a one-unit increase in \(X_j\), not a probability change. The paper states this operator explicitly: “a one unit increase in Xj will cause the … (p/1-p), to change by a factor of exp(Bj)” [PAGE 18].

The paper’s scientific lineage runs through social memory, BIRGing, heritage marketing, and nostalgia studies. Osmond et al. are quoted: “Public statues that commemorate the lives and achievements of athletes are pervasive and influential forms of social memory within the cultural landscape of Western society” [PAGE 3]. Cialdini et al.’s BIRGing and Holt’s “myth markets” are used to explain why clubs build statues: they project past glory and authentic tradition simultaneously [PAGE 7]. Seifried and Meyer’s facility audit and the Camden Yards retro-ballpark watershed of 1992 supply the institutional timeline: heritage-based stadium design became a strategic tool, and statues became one tactic within it [PAGE 6]. Russell’s “grammar of mourning” supplies the sympathy hypothesis, though the estimated models do not retain age of death [PAGE 5; PAGE 28]. The paper’s genealogy is therefore not a simple meritocracy narrative; it is a retrocausal genealogy in which present commemorative institutions select and canonize past careers.

The multi-week traps are several. First, the statue count is a stock with a pipeline: 2011 totals include commissioned-but-not-yet-erected statues, and 2012 totals are entirely commissioned statues [PAGE 3 footnote]. A short scrape that treats “commissioned” as “erected” will distort the outcome. Second, the era effect is not fixed. The authors warn that the optimum is a 30–40 year distance from the 1990s statue fashion, and “the apex and shape of the curvilinear effect is unlikely to remain fixed” [PAGE 26]. A modeler who hard-codes “1950s and 1960s” as the nostalgia era will be trapped when the window moves forward. Third, fan-led and club-led projects follow different logics: fan organizers can choose subjects “beyond televisual memory or the living memory of supporters” [PAGE 27], while clubs may prefer “future nostalgia” and more recent players [PAGE 26]. Fourth, franchise relocation creates a genealogical rupture: Jackie Robinson is commemorated by six statues across the US and Canada, “yet none of these is located at an MLB ballpark” [PAGE 29]. Fifth, Hall of Fame induction lags: the five-year retirement rule and elite-sample construction mean recent expansion franchises are underrepresented [PAGE 30]. Sixth, multi-subject statues are club-specific monuments rather than subject-specific honors, so treating every depicted body as an independent \(Y_i=1\) is a category error [PAGE 30–31]. These traps are not “one bad data point”; they are structural features of the commemorative process.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The selection process is recursive because each agent models the others’ memories and motives. Club owners and executives ask: what do supporters believe the club’s past was, and what version of that past will sell tickets and real estate? Supporters ask: does the club respect our history or is it commodifying us? Players ask: is a statue now the expected end-of-career honor? The paper supplies direct evidence for the third loop: “In 2011, basketball player Kareem Abdul-Jabbar openly raised the subject of how he deserved a statue outside the LA Lakers’ Staples Center” [PAGE 32]. That is a player belief about a club’s commemorative duty, not a physical performance variable.

Crisis-habit regression appears when a club faces a stadium move, redevelopment, or loss of a familiar ground. The paper describes fan-led projects as “an attempt to offset the losses described above” [PAGE 11], and the Swansea organizer’s quotation shows the habitual commemorative response: “how can we move forward by taking a part of our history, not just into the present but into the future, not to forget where we’ve come from?” [PAGE 11]. Under crisis, agents regress to the habit of the statue as a stabilizing object.

The counter-trap is that different agents can escape the same nostalgia clock in opposite directions. Clubs can move forward toward recent players to create “future nostalgia” [PAGE 26]. Fans can move backward beyond living memory to founders and pre-televisual heroes, as with the Bloomer and Keenor examples [PAGE 27]. The modeler’s counter-trap is to interpret the 1950s/1960s coefficient as a fixed cultural fact rather than a moving window. The clock is explicit in the variables: career midpoint, years since midpoint, career length, ballpark age, years of MLB presence in the city, and franchise relocation [PAGE 16–17; PAGE 21]. The recursive theory of mind therefore predicts that a statue is a joint product of club beliefs about fan nostalgia, fan beliefs about club commercialism, and player beliefs about honor—not a direct readout of athletic merit.
