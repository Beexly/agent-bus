Galton's core insight here is that while any specific condition might favor an extreme trait (a very tall player, a very fast runner), the *aggregate* of all possible conditions—the "characteristic and persistent" accidents of the environment—will most often favor the median or characteristic value. He uses the metaphor of flight through foliage: a taller bird might sometimes benefit from clearing dense branches, but on average, the most common obstacles are best navigated by a bird of medium size.
In sports, $P$ represents the archetypal, optimally efficient strategy or roster construction for a given league era. For example, in the modern NBA, $P$ might correspond to a roster with 3-and-D wings, a versatile big man, and a high pace. Deviations $x$ from this norm—like loading up on traditional post-up centers—are riskier. While a specific opponent's defense might be particularly vulnerable to a post-up (making that deviation favorable for that game), the *typical* defensive scheme across the league is designed to punish such inefficiencies. Thus, teams that deviate significantly from $P$ are more likely to encounter a "dense foliage" of counter-strategies, leading to a rapid decline in performance frequency $y$.
This directly challenges the narrative of "style has no holes." Galton shows that styles *do* have holes, but the holes are filled by the persistent characteristics of the competitive environment. The "law of frequency of error" dictates that errors (mismatches, bad shots) accumulate faster as you move further from the center of the favored distribution. Therefore, betting markets or fan sentiment that overvalue a unique style ($P+x$) are failing to account for the probabilistic dominance of the typical impediment ($P$). The key is to identify the current $P$ of the league and then assess whether a team's specific strengths are aligned with the "typical" conditions or are reliant on a rare "accident of foliage" that may not persist.
