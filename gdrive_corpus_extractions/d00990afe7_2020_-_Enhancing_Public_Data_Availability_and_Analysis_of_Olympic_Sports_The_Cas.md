The source is a 2020 Carnegie Mellon Sports Analytics Conference paper by Matthew Flancer and Konstantinos Pelechrinis on enhancing public data availability for Olympic sports using college swimming as the testbed [PAGE 1]. The multi-hop reasoning starts with the data-generating process: the authors build a Python scraper for swimcloud.com and collect ACC men’s and women’s data since 2012, including more than 2,000 swimmers and more than 17,000 event times in the ACC finals [PAGE 2]. The first modeling hop is swimmer growth: average points per event, \(\pi_s\), is modeled by a Bayesian linear regression on college attended \(c\) and recruiting Power Index \(r\) [PAGE 4]. The second hop is season-ending taper: percentage improvement \(\delta_s\) from qualifying time to conference-finals time is modeled by a Bayesian regression on school \(c\) and event type \(e\) [PAGE 6]. The third hop is interpretation: the paper explicitly warns that this is “purely observational analysis” and that “we cannot assign causality to these claims” [PAGE 4]. The gray-zone audit therefore must separate the paper’s legitimate contribution—open data, scraping infrastructure, and descriptive posterior summaries—from causal coaching or taper claims that the design cannot identify.

A key audit finding is that the growth model’s school coefficients are not automatically “coaching effects.” They are posterior associations conditional on a recruiting rank and a school label. The Power Index is an ordinal high-school recruiting rank from 1 to 100, with 1 the top prospect, and it is missing for some swimmers, mainly international swimmers, marked as \(-1\) [PAGE 2]. Treating \(-1\) as a numeric value would be a breakdown regime because it lies outside the stated support and can bias the slope. The paper does not print an explicit missing-data model. Also, the school variable is categorical; with an intercept, full one-hot coding creates a rank-deficient design matrix unless a reference level or sum-to-zero constraint is imposed. The paper’s coefficients are interpretable as relative effects only under such a constraint. The Miami men’s posterior is especially fragile: “there are only 10 data points for swimmers from the University of Miami (the next fewest number of data points is 81 for Duke)” [PAGE 5]. That sample-size asymmetry explains the wide Miami interval, from 4.36 to 17.3 in Table 1 [PAGE 5]. Any ranking that treats Miami’s 11.0 point-per-event posterior mean as a stable school effect is in a gray zone.

The taper analysis has a second gray zone: the sign and definition of “improvement.” The paper says the Improvement column “provides the percentage difference between the time achieved at the speciﬁc race and the qualifying time for the conference ﬁnals” [PAGE 2]. Table 2 interprets “a smaller coefficient \(b_c\) is better (ideally negative), since this corresponds to smaller percentage increase in the swimming time compared to the qualifiers” [PAGE 6]. Thus the model can be read as \(\delta_s\) increasing when finals times are slower relative to qualifying times, but the source does not print the exact signed formula. This matters because a modeler could invert the sign and reverse the ranking. The paper’s own conclusion for taper is cautious: the school effects are wider than in the points model, and it says the school attended “is not associated with any quantifiable beneﬁt (or setback) on a swimmer’s time” [PAGE 6]. Table 2 shows school credible intervals covering zero for both men and women, with missing cells marked “∗: no data for swimmers/event” [PAGE 8]. The correct inference is descriptive non-identification, not proof that tapering is irrelevant.

A third gray zone is the temporal and strategic structure. Tapering is defined as a period “ranging from a few days, up to 3 weeks” in which athletes “ﬁne-tune their swimming, while the volume of the work in the pool is cut drastically” [PAGE 6]. The dataset contains meet times, not weekly training load, sleep, illness, travel, or coach intent. The paper admits: “we cannot know if all teams include in their training season-ending taper (even though it is highly unlikely that any of them does not)” [PAGE 6]. Therefore the finals-time improvement is a compound of taper, rest, prelim/final round selection, event allocation, competition pressure, lane assignment, and measurement conditions. The paper’s future-work question—whether a swimmer should “participate in 4 events and perform slightly above average in all of them, or participate in 2 events and excel in both” [PAGE 9]—shows that event allocation is itself strategic. That strategic layer must be included in any theory-of-mind model of the data-generating process.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s data operators are explicit. The scraper functions are `getRoster`, which returns swimmer IDs for a season and team; `findEntries`, which returns a swimmer’s events in a competition; `getData`, which returns points gained by roster swimmers; and `getPowerIndex`, which returns a swimmer’s high-school recruiting rank [PAGE 3]. The three collected tables are swimmers’ information, swimmers’ times, and swimmers’ standings [PAGE 2]. The times table format is “Swimmer ID, Team, Year, Event, Event Type, Time, Improvement,” and the standings table includes season-by-season points, events, and points per event, plus Power Index [PAGE 2].

The first formal model is the swimmer growth curve. The source defines \(\pi_s\) as the average points per event earned by swimmer \(s\) [PAGE 3]. In compact notation, if \(P_s\) is total points and \(E_s\) is events, then
\[
\pi_s=\frac{P_s}{E_s},\qquad [\pi_s]=\text{points}\cdot\text{event}^{-1}.
\]
The paper’s Bayesian linear regression is
\[
\pi_s \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(a_0+a_c\,c+a_r\,r,\ \sigma^2\right),
\]
where \(c\) is the college attended, \(r\) is the recruiting Power Index, \(a_0\) is the intercept, \(a_c\) is the school coefficient, \(a_r\) is the Power Index coefficient, and \(\sigma\) is the residual standard deviation in points per event [PAGE 4]. The source states the priors as “\(\sigma\): Half Cauchy distribution with \(\beta = 10\)” and “\(a_0, a_c, a_r\): normal distribution with 0 mean and standard deviation of 5” [PAGE 4]. The model is fit by MCMC with “5 chains and 2000 samples each” [PAGE 4]. Table 1 reports posterior means and “CI (3%)” and “CI (97%)” quantiles, i.e. a 94% credible interval, in points per event [PAGE 5].

The second formal model is the taper/improvement regression. The dependent variable is the percentage change between qualifying time and finals time, \(\delta_s\), described as “the percentage improvement in each event as compared to their qualifying time for the same event” [PAGE 6]. Consistent with the paper’s sign interpretation, one may write the measured quantity as a percentage difference,
\[
\delta_s = 100\,\frac{T_s^{\text{final}}-T_s^{\text{qual}}}{T_s^{\text{qual}}},
\qquad [\delta_s]=\%,
\]
with \(T\) in swimming time units, conventionally seconds for the printed yard events; the paper itself does not print an explicit unit column for time. The regression is
\[
\delta_s \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(b_0+b_c\,c+b_e\,e,\ \sigma^2\right),
\]
where \(c\) is school, \(e\) is event type such as 50YFree or 200YIM, \(b_c\) is the school coefficient, \(b_e\) is the event coefficient, and \(\sigma\) is in percent [PAGE 6]. The same prior family is used: normal priors with mean 0 and standard deviation 5 for the coefficients, and a half-Cauchy prior for \(\sigma\) [PAGE 6]. Table 2 reports school and event coefficients with 3% and 97% posterior quantiles [PAGE 8].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are exact and auditable. First, categorical school and event variables require reference coding or a constraint with an intercept; if all levels are one-hot encoded alongside an intercept, the design matrix has linearly dependent columns and the posterior is non-identifiable. Second, missing Power Index marked \(-1\) [PAGE 2] must be treated as missing, not as a real rank; otherwise the support assumption \(r\in\{1,\dots,100\}\) is violated. Third, small-cell regimes break stable ranking: Miami men have 10 data points versus 81 for the next fewest [PAGE 5], producing the wide posterior interval 4.36 to 17.3 [PAGE 5]. Fourth, Table 2 contains missing event cells, including Miami men and women’s 50YBreast and 50YFly, marked “∗: no data for swimmers/event” [PAGE 8]. Fifth, the sign of \(\delta_s\) is inferred from the text rather than an explicit formula, so sign inversion is a model-risk regime. Sixth, no training-load time series is available, so taper effects cannot be separated from rest, round selection, or event allocation. Seventh, the paper explicitly says the analysis is observational and non-causal [PAGE 4]. No information-theoretic units such as bits or nats are reported; the probability densities are in the parameter scales of points per event and percent.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs from Olympic-team analytics and public-data scarcity to swimming-specific pacing and taper research. The introduction states that Olympic committees use data for athlete identification, funding allocation, and sports science, citing Ferguson and Kirwan [PAGE 1; PAGE 9]. It also cites pacing studies in running and swimming, including Foster et al., de Leeuw et al., McGibbon et al., and Menting et al. [PAGE 1; PAGE 9]. A key historical thread is the 2016 Rio lane-bias finding: “there was a lane bias, with athletes swimming faster in one direction as compared to the opposite one depending on the lane they were swimming” [PAGE 1]. That lineage matters because it shows that swimming performance is sensitive to physical and procedural conditions, not just athlete quality or coaching.

The Bayesian modeling lineage is Box and Tiao’s Bayesian inference in statistical analysis [PAGE 9, ref. 1]. The paper uses that tradition to produce posterior distributions and probabilistic rankings rather than point rankings. The taper lineage is Mujika and Le Meur et al., with tapering defined as a few days to three weeks of reduced pool volume to optimize performance at important competitions [PAGE 6; PAGE 9, refs. 7 and 10]. The paper’s retrocausal temptation is to read the posterior school coefficient backward as a coaching effect on an athlete’s development, and to read finals improvement backward as evidence of a successful taper. But the causal arrow is not identified: schools recruit different swimmers, swimmers select schools, coaches allocate swimmers to events, and qualifying times occur before finals in a different competitive context. The paper itself says “this is a purely observational analysis and we cannot assign causality to these claims” [PAGE 4].

Multi-week traps follow directly from the taper window. Because taper can last “a few days, up to 3 weeks” [PAGE 6], a finals time is a terminal observation after an unobserved multi-week training block. If the dataset aggregates all seasons and all events, the model cannot recover week-level periodization; if it treats each finals swim as independent, it ignores within-swimmer correlation across events and seasons. Another trap is survivorship: the dataset covers ACC finals participants, not all collegiate swimmers, so school effects are conditional on reaching the conference finals. A further trap is the use of Power Index as a talent proxy; it is a high-school recruiting rank, not a measured physiological capacity, and it is missing for some international swimmers [PAGE 2]. The historical lesson is that open-data infrastructure is a genuine contribution, but posterior school rankings should not be retroactively upgraded into causal coaching rankings without longitudinal training, selection, and lane/event controls.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three named hazards: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. “Crisis habit regression” is the analyst’s or coach’s fallback under sparse data: the analyst sees a posterior school coefficient and defaults to a causal coaching story; the coach defaults to habitual taper lore; the swimmer defaults to chasing a qualifying time without modeling prelim/final incentives. The paper’s own evidence resists that habit: the points model has a Miami men’s posterior mean of 11.0 but a 94% interval from 4.36 to 17.3 based on only 10 data points [PAGE 5], and the taper model’s school intervals cover zero [PAGE 6; PAGE 8]. The correct “counter-trap” is not to discard the data because causal identification fails. The counter-trap is to use the open scraper and descriptive posteriors for what they are—uncertainty-aware descriptions—while refusing to market them as causal coaching rankings. The paper’s valid contribution is the public-data footprint: the scraper, the three tables, and the interactive Dash application [PAGE 2; PAGE 7].

The “clock” is the season and meet clock. Qualifying times occur before conference finals; taper occupies a multi-week window; prelims, timed finals, and finals occur in a fixed sequence; and the future-work question about entering 4 events versus 2 events [PAGE 9] is a strategic allocation problem. A recursive model must represent at least three minds: the swimmer, who may pace prelims to reach finals and reserve effort; the coach, who chooses taper timing and event entries; and the analyst, who observes only the resulting times. Because the analyst does not observe the coach’s taper plan or the swimmer’s effort allocation, the school coefficient \(b_c\) can absorb strategy rather than conditioning. The paper acknowledges this gap: “we cannot know if all teams include in their training season-ending taper” [PAGE 6]. Thus the theory-of-mind audit says: use the posterior to generate hypotheses about program effects, but do not treat the school label as a mind-reading device for coaching quality.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is strongest at the lane and course level. The paper cites the Rio lane-bias result that athletes swam faster in one direction depending on lane [PAGE 1]. If lane assignment, pool course, timing system, or pool facility correlates with school or event, then both \(\pi_s\) and \(\delta_s\) can shift without any coaching or taper effect. The source does not include lane, pool, or timing covariates in the regression tables, so a school coefficient cannot be adjudicated as a conditioning effect until those physical variables are controlled or randomized. The biomechanical-layer veto is event mix and round structure. Table 2 includes event coefficients for 50YFree, 100YBack, 200YIM, 1650YFree, and other events [PAGE 8], which means the school coefficient is conditional on event type. If a school’s roster is concentrated in sprint versus distance events, or if prelims versus finals have different pacing demands, the school coefficient is confounded. The missing cells for women’s 50YBreast and 50YFly and for Miami men [PAGE 8] also show that event-specific identification is incomplete.

The weather-layer veto is weaker for indoor swimming but not absent. The paper provides no weather, travel, facility-temperature, or air-quality covariates. Indoor conference finals are largely climate-controlled, so a direct weather claim would be unsupported; but travel disruption and facility conditions could still enter the residual. The paper’s explicit non-causal caveat [PAGE 4] is the correct adjudication: no physical, biomechanical, or weather mechanism is proved. A school effect should survive only if, after conditioning on event, round, lane, season, course, and missingness, the posterior interval still excludes zero. The paper’s own taper result fails that veto: it says the school attended “is not associated with any quantiﬁable beneﬁt (or setback) on a swimmer’s time” [PAGE 6]. The points result is stronger descriptively, but it still cannot pass a causal physical/biomechanical veto without the missing controls.
