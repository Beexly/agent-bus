Mean ToGo is 8.90 yards while mean pass gain is only 8.10 yards [PAGE 10], so a single passing play statistically cannot convert a first down; coaches must sequence plays across downs, and psychological pressure rises as ToGo grows [PAGE 8]. Mean ToTD is 51.23 yards with max 99, so most passes occur far from the end zone, where touchdown probability is low (5.06% overall) [PAGE 10]. The recursive trap is that both offense and defense update beliefs after each play: a successful short pass changes down-and-distance, which changes the next play’s optimal distribution, which the defense anticipates. The ML models in the paper capture only first-order statistical tendencies (team identity, down, distance, direction) and explicitly lack starter-versus-substitute personnel context [PAGE 15], audio commentary, and player trajectories [PAGE 20–21]. Consequently they cannot model higher-order theory of mind—e.g., a quarterback audibling at the line based on a defensive disguise, or a coordinator calling a fourth-down pass because he believes the defense expects a punt. The models’ ceiling is bounded by this missing recursive layer of intentionality.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto adjudication.

Physical layer: The field imposes hard geometric constraints. A first down requires 10 yards within four downs [PAGE 8], but average pass gain is 8.10 yards versus average ToGo of 8.90 yards [PAGE 10], so no single passing play can be vetoed as a guaranteed first-down converter; the model must predict sequences, not isolated plays. Yards gained range from −21 (interception/negative return) to 93 [PAGE 10], confirming that physical turnovers and explosive plays exist in the support of the outcome distribution. Any model that outputs only non-negative yardage without modeling interception risk is physically incomplete.

Biomechanical layer: Passing success depends on quarterback throwing accuracy, which degrades with distance, and receiver vulnerability to defensive interference [PAGE 8, 21]. Deep passes are more likely intercepted but yield more yards when completed; short passes complete more easily but produce post-catch tackles by nearby defenders [PAGE 8–9]. These mechanisms generate the observed heteroscedasticity: prediction variance increases with actual yardage, and both SVM and XGBoost struggle on large yardage values [PAGE 15–16]. The standard deviation of 10.41 yards around an 8.10-yard mean [PAGE 10] is a biomechanical dispersion signature that no deterministic play-call model can remove.

Weather layer: The dataset includes 82 games with synchronized audio and 78 with video [PAGE 7], but the models use neither audio nor video trajectories [PAGE 20–21], and no weather variables (wind speed, precipitation, temperature, dome versus outdoor) appear in the feature list [PAGE 8–10]. Outdoor passing is physically sensitive to wind, especially on deep passes (19.32% of plays) [PAGE 10]. Veto: any claimed generalization to live games is invalidated unless the model is augmented with weather and venue state; otherwise deep-pass predictions are physically ungrounded. Adjudication: the paper’s models are conditionally valid only for structured, stadium-average passing plays under the implicit assumption that weather and biomechanical noise are exchangeable across the 2018–19 sample—an assumption that fails in extreme weather games and in any future season with different personnel.
