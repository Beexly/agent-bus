The paper is a pipeline paper, not a predictive-model paper: it estimates indoor volleyball player positions by mapping a monocular image point to a court-plane bird’s-eye coordinate. The multi-hop derivation is: image capture [PAGE 1] -> YOLOv9 person bounding box [PAGE 2] -> extraction of a ground-contact proxy point by Eq. (1), namely the bottom-center of the box [PAGE 2] -> manual visual tracing of court lines and net coordinates [PAGE 2] -> computation of line intersections, with 0 to 6 possible intersections [PAGE 2] -> selection of four reference points from A, B, E, F or A, C, D, F [PAGE 3] -> projective transformation to bird’s-eye view [PAGE 3] -> Euclidean comparison with tape-measured ground truth [PAGE 3] -> reported mean coordinate errors dx and dy and mean distance [PAGE 4]. The scientific claim is that a bird’s-eye view can be generated from arbitrary viewing angles with average distance error 0.30 m, which the authors compare heuristically to male shoulder width 0.4–0.45 m [PAGE 4].

The first gray-zone audit concerns automation. The abstract says the approach “automatically identifies reference points based on the coordinates of the net and court lines” [PAGE 1], but the methodology states: “The lines and the net in the image are visually inspected, and their coordinates are manually obtained” [PAGE 2]. Thus the reference-point selection is semi-automatic at best. The later conclusion says line and net detection by object recognition is future work “aiming for full automation” [PAGE 6]. This matters because manual tracing injects human coordinate error into a homography whose sensitivity can amplify small reference-point perturbations.

The second gray-zone audit concerns reference-point geometry. The paper says there can be “anywhere from 0 to 6 intersections within an image” [PAGE 2]. If fewer than four intersections exist, the system displays “Please change the angle of view” [PAGE 3]. If four or five exist, A, B, E, F are used; if six exist, A, C, D, F are used [PAGE 3]. Table 3 reports A, B, E, F: dx 0.21 m, dy 0.24 m, distance 0.32 m; A, C, D, F: dx 0.21 m, dy 0.15 m, distance 0.26 m [PAGE 6]. The improvement is mainly along y, consistent with the paper’s explanation that larger reference quadrilaterals and points inside the quadrilateral improve accuracy [PAGE 5]. But dx is unchanged at 0.21 m, so the six-point choice is not a universal cure.

The third gray-zone audit concerns the ground-contact assumption. Eq. (1) sets the player image position to the bottom-center of the bounding box: x is the midpoint of x_min and x_max, and y is y_max [PAGE 2]. This is valid only if the bottom of the box tracks the foot or ground-contact patch. The paper itself reports a failure: Camera 8 “did not capture the feet of the player in the center of the left column” and “the bottom edge of the bounding box was displayed around the player’s waist” [PAGE 5]. That makes y_max a waist point, not a ground point. The conclusion also admits: “this experiment assumed that players remained on the ground; when players are airborne, such as during jumps, the estimation error is expected to increase” [PAGE 6]. These are not minor caveats; they define the physical validity domain of the operator.

The fourth gray-zone audit concerns sampling and evaluation. The 351 plots are described as “successful plots” [PAGE 4], and plots outside the court were excluded to avoid coaches, managers, spectators, and other non-game individuals [PAGE 4]. This exclusion is reasonable for tactical analysis but creates a selection boundary: players near the court edge, airborne players, or heavily occluded players may be underrepresented. The paper reports means, not confidence intervals or tail quantiles, so 0.30 m average distance could hide severe outliers. Camera-level results show Camera 4 at 0.41 m distance and Camera 8 at 0.38 m, versus Camera 1 at 0.21 m and Camera 2 at 0.23 m [PAGE 5]. The monocular depth direction is explicitly identified as the weak axis: “Since this study uses monocular player position estimation, the error in the y-direction is larger than in the x-direction” [PAGE 4]. Thus the correct conclusion is conditional: the method works in the tested gym, for ground-contact players, with manually traced lines, under the tested camera angles, and with a mean error near 0.30 m.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines four formal operators.

1. Bounding-box ground-point operator. YOLOv9 produces a person box with image coordinates x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max in pixels [PAGE 2]. Eq. (1) is:
\[
x=\frac{x_{\min}+x_{\max}}{2},\qquad y=y_{\max}.
\]
Units: x and y are image pixels. Assumption: y_max coincides with the player’s ground-contact point. Breakdown regimes: occlusion truncates the box above the feet; the paper reports a waist-level bottom edge for Camera 8 [PAGE 5]; airborne players have no ground-contact point at y_max [PAGE 6]; loose or oversized boxes shift y_max [PAGE 4].

2. Court-line intersection operator. The paper obtains linear equations of court lines and the net, extends lines where only part is visible, and computes intersections [PAGE 2]. It states: “In general, there can be anywhere from 0 to 6 intersections within an image” [PAGE 2]. If a new intersection is detected after extension, “the point in question should be selected” [PAGE 2]. Units: image pixels. Breakdown regimes: near-parallel lines produce intersections at or near infinity in homogeneous coordinates; extended lines outside the image increase coordinate uncertainty; non-court linear features can create false intersections; fewer than four intersections trigger the message “Please change the angle of view” [PAGE 3].

3. Reference-point selection operator. For four or five intersections, the selected points are A, B, E, F; for six, A, C, D, F are used [PAGE 3]. The paper defines A as the point closest to the net line and to the left of the net, F as the other net-adjacent point, B as the point closest to A that is not F, E as the point closest to F that is not A, and C and D as points on line AB [PAGE 3]. Units: pixel coordinates. Assumption: the selected points are non-collinear and correspond to known court-plane locations. Breakdown regimes: collinear or near-collinear reference points make the homography rank-deficient or ill-conditioned; mislabeled C or D introduces a wrong correspondence; reference points outside the image or outside the acting court region increase extrapolation error. The paper supports the geometric conditioning argument: accuracy “tends to improve as the area of the quadrilateral formed by the reference points increases,” and points “inside the quadrilateral” are expected to be more accurate than points outside [PAGE 5].

4. Projective transformation operator. Let \(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=[u,v,1]^{T}\) be an image homogeneous point in pixels and \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=[X,Y,1]^{T}\) a court-plane homogeneous point in meters. A homography \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\) satisfies:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\sim H\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\qquad
u=\frac{h_{11}X+h_{12}Y+h_{13}}{h_{31}X+h_{32}Y+h_{33}},\qquad
v=\frac{h_{21}X+h_{22}Y+h_{23}}{h_{31}X+h_{32}Y+h_{33}}.
\]
With the gauge \(h_{33}=1\), four correspondences give the standard DLT rows
\[
\mathbf{a}_i=[X_i,Y_i,1,0,0,0,-u_iX_i,-u_iY_i,-u_i],
\]
\[
\mathbf{b}_i=[0,0,0,X_i,Y_i,1,-v_iX_i,-v_iY_i,-v_i],
\]
and the stacked system is solved by singular value decomposition. The estimated court point is
\[
\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\pi(H^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}),
\]
where \(\mathbf{p}=(x,y)\) is the Eq. (1) image point and \(\pi\) dehomogenizes. Error is
\[
e_i=\sqrt{(\hat{x}_i-x_i)^2+(\hat{y}_i-y_i)^2}
\]
in meters, with dx and dy as coordinate differences. The reported mean is dx = 0.21 m, dy = 0.21 m, distance = 0.30 m over 351 plots [PAGE 4]. The condition number \(\kappa(H)=\|H\|_2\|H^{-1}\|_2\) controls sensitivity: as \(\det H\to0\) or \(\kappa(H)\to\infty\), the inverse mapping is singular or unstable. Exact singularity occurs when the four reference points are collinear or when the homography matrix is rank-deficient. Near-singularity occurs when the reference quadrilateral has small area or when extended-line intersections lie near infinity; then small pixel errors in manually traced lines are amplified into large court-coordinate errors.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is projective geometry and homography estimation, applied through a modern object-detection front end. The paper cites Luo et al. on homography estimation [PAGE 6] and YOLOv9 for detection [PAGE 6]. Historically, the bird’s-eye transformation descends from perspective projection: a planar court is mapped to an image plane by a homography, and four non-collinear correspondences determine that homography. The paper’s novelty is not the homography itself; it is the reference-point selection heuristic for variable camera views, using net and court-line intersections [PAGE 1–3]. The lineage is therefore: manual tactical notation systems such as DataVolley [PAGE 1] -> camera-based player tracking -> monocular homography -> automatic reference-point selection -> multi-view fusion as future work [PAGE 6].

A retrocausal reading is useful: later failures reveal earlier assumptions. The occlusion failure at Camera 8 [PAGE 5] retroactively exposes the Eq. (1) assumption that y_max is a foot point. The airborne caveat [PAGE 6] retroactively exposes the same assumption as a ground-plane restriction. The manual tracing admission [PAGE 2] retroactively weakens the abstract’s “automatically identifies” claim [PAGE 1]. The multi-week traps are predictable. First-week trap: calibrate on one camera and assume the four-court-corner method generalizes; the paper explicitly says fixed angles require adjustment whenever the angle changes [PAGE 2]. Second-week trap: over-trust intersection count; six intersections are not automatically better because A, C, D, F improved dy but not dx in Table 3 [PAGE 6]. Third-week trap: ignore occlusion and let YOLO boxes truncate at the waist; Camera 8’s 0.38 m distance versus Camera 1’s 0.21 m is the visible cost [PAGE 5]. Fourth-week trap: treat jump frames as ground frames; the conclusion warns of increased error for airborne players [PAGE 6]. The correct genealogy is not a straight line from “camera” to “tactical insight”; it is a chain of geometric assumptions, each of which needs an explicit validity test.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer asks what the system believes about the analyst’s view, the player’s body, and the clock. Crisis habit regression appears when the view is poor: the system or analyst falls back to the fixed four-corner habit, or to manual mouse tracing of lines, even though the paper’s stated goal is arbitrary-view estimation [PAGE 1–2]. The paper’s fallback is explicit: when intersections are fewer than four, it displays “Please change the angle of view” [PAGE 3]. That is a safe regression, but it is still a regression from estimation to user correction. A stronger system would infer why the view is poor, for example whether the net is outside the frame or whether court lines are occluded. The paper notes that angles without the net were excluded from tactical analysis [PAGE 3], so the system already has a partial self-model of view adequacy.

The counter-trap is overconfidence in more reference structure. Six intersections sound better than four, but the paper’s six-point selection uses A, C, D, F, and Table 3 shows dx remains 0.21 m while dy improves from 0.24 m to 0.15 m [PAGE 6]. The trap is to believe that more intersections necessarily mean more accuracy. The paper’s own explanation is geometric: larger quadrilateral area and interior points improve transformation accuracy [PAGE 5]. So the counter-trap is not “six is bad”; it is “six is not a substitute for non-collinear, well-spread, correctly labeled correspondences.”

The clock layer is the least developed. The experiment uses still images to evaluate accuracy [PAGE 3], not live video latency. Tactical analysis in live volleyball needs timestamp alignment between the detected player box and the traced court geometry. If lines are traced once and reused while the camera pans, the homography no longer matches the frame. The paper mentions that live images can feature zoomed-in views and shifts in camera angle as the ball is tracked [PAGE 1], which is a clock-and-view problem, not only a geometry problem. A recursive system should maintain uncertainty over three nested beliefs: “I can see the court,” “I can see the player’s ground contact,” and “the player is on the ground.” If any belief fails, it should communicate uncertainty or request another view rather than emit a silent bird’s-eye point. The paper’s future multi-view fusion proposal [PAGE 6] is the correct counter-trap: use another viewpoint to resolve occlusion and airborne ambiguity instead of forcing a single monocular estimate.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The homography layer has a hard physical veto: if the four selected reference points are collinear or nearly collinear, or if the reference quadrilateral area is too small relative to court dimensions, the transformation is singular or ill-conditioned. The paper states that projective transformation “requires at least four reference points” [PAGE 1] and that accuracy improves with larger reference-quadrilateral area and with points inside the quadrilateral [PAGE 5]. Therefore any frame whose recovered H has a condition number above a pre-registered threshold, or whose reference points fail a minimum-area test, should be vetoed rather than averaged into results. The paper’s own fewer-than-four rule is an explicit physical veto: “When the number of intersections is less than four … ‘Please change the angle of view’ is represented” [PAGE 3]. Extended lines near infinity are also a physical veto because they produce unstable homogeneous intersections [PAGE 2].

Biomechanical veto. Eq. (1) treats y_max as the ground-contact point [PAGE 2]. That is a biomechanical assumption about posture and contact. The veto should trigger when the bounding box bottom is visibly above the foot region, as in Camera 8 where “the bottom edge of the bounding box was displayed around the player’s waist” [PAGE 5]. It should also trigger when the player is airborne: the conclusion states that “when players are airborne, such as during jumps, the estimation error is expected to increase” [PAGE 6]. The shoulder-width comparison, 0.4–0.45 m for a typical male versus 0.30 m mean distance [PAGE 4], is a heuristic tolerance, not a universal biomechanical law; a positional error of 0.30 m may be acceptable for coarse tactical spacing but not for fine net-contact or landing analysis. Therefore biomechanical vetoes should be task-dependent: ground-contact-only for Eq. (1), multi-view or pose-aware for jump frames.

Weather veto. The experiment was conducted in the gymnasium at the Awazu Campus of Komatsu University [PAGE 3], so direct weather layers such as wind and precipitation do not physically act on the court or player in this dataset. The weather veto is therefore null for outdoor meteorology, but not for indoor environmental illumination: lighting changes, floor glare, and crowd occlusion can degrade line tracing and YOLO detection. The paper does not report weather variables, so any weather-based rejection rule would be unsupported. The correct adjudication is: no direct weather veto; use a lighting/occlusion veto only if the image evidence shows line or foot degradation, and do not attribute indoor error to weather without measurement.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Homography-condition veto. Veto condition: reject a frame if the four selected reference points yield \(\kappa(H)\) above a pre-registered threshold or if the reference quadrilateral area is below a pre-registered fraction of the court area. Prediction: the accepted subset will have lower tail error than the full set, while coverage falls. This is verifiable from saved reference-point pixels, recomputed H, and tape-measured ground truth. It follows the paper’s geometric claim that larger reference quadrilaterals and interior points improve accuracy [PAGE 5] and the explicit fewer-than-four rejection rule [PAGE 3].

Scenario 2: Foot-visibility veto. Veto condition: reject or flag a frame when the YOLO bounding-box bottom is above the visible foot region, or when the box bottom is near the waist or knee. Prediction: on Camera 8, retaining only foot-visible frames should reduce mean distance error from the reported 0.38 m toward the Camera 1 and Camera 2 range of 0.21–0.23 m [PAGE 5], at the cost of fewer usable frames. This is directly testable because the paper identifies the waist-level box as the Camera 8 failure mode [PAGE 5].

Scenario 3: Ground-contact/airborne veto. Veto condition: reject Eq. (1) ground-plane output when temporal or pose evidence indicates that the player is airborne, and route the frame to multi-view fusion or a pose-aware estimator. Prediction: ground-contact frames will show smaller dy error than jump frames, and multi-view fusion will reduce jump-frame error relative to monocular Eq. (1). This is verifiable with annotated jump intervals and the paper’s own warning that airborne estimation error is expected to increase [PAGE 6]. A formal RLVR reward can be defined as \(R_i=1\) if the frame is accepted and \(e_i\leq\tau\), \(R_i=-1\) if accepted and \(e_i>\tau\), and \(R_i=0\) if vetoed, with \(\tau\) fixed before evaluation in meters. The reward is verifiable because \(e_i\) is computed from the bird’s-eye estimate and the tape-measured ground truth, and the veto conditions are recomputable from stored bounding boxes and reference points.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
