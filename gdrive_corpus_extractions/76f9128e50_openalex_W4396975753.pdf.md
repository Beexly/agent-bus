<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides no information-theoretic units such as bits or nats and no probability units beyond the standard measure-theoretic framework for stochastic PDEs. The document itself is a “Book of Abstracts” [PAGE 4 of 83]. The following are formal source-level operators, with dimensional caveats and breakdown regimes.

1. Elliptic hemivariational inequality, [PAGE 18 of 83]. The source states: “find \(u\in V_0\) such that \(a(u,v)+\alpha\int_{\Gamma_3}j^0(u;v)\,d\Gamma \ge L(v)\), \(\forall v\in V_0\),” where \(j^0\) “represent the generalized (Clarke) directional derivative.” Formal statement:
\[
u\in V_0:\quad a(u,v)+\alpha\int_{\Gamma_3}j^0(u;v)\,d\Gamma \ge L(v),\qquad \forall v\in V_0.
\]
Assumptions cited there include \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^d\), regular boundary \(\Gamma=\cup_{i=1}^3\Gamma_i\), \(\mathrm{meas}(\Gamma_i)>0\), \(g\in L^2(\Omega)\), \(q\in L^2(\Gamma_2)\), and \(j(x,\cdot)\) locally Lipschitz for a.e. \(x\in\Gamma_3\), “not necessary differentiable.” Units: the abstract assigns no physical units; a dimensional realization requires \(u\) in metres if it is displacement, \(a(u,v)\) and \(L(v)\) in joules, and \(\alpha\) chosen so that the boundary term is also in joules. Breakdown: the Clarke subgradient is multivalued and nonmonotone; the limit \(\alpha\to\infty\) is a separate asymptotic result, not an identity for finite \(\alpha\). The source explicitly discusses “when the parameter \(\alpha\) goes to infinity.”

2. Singular \(p\)-Laplacian problem, [PAGE 33 of 83]. Source equation:
\[
-\Delta_p u=\lambda f(x,u)+\mu g(x,u)\ \text{in }\Omega,\qquad u>0\ \text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\ \text{on }\partial\Omega,
\]
with \(\Delta_p u:=\mathrm{div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)\), \(p\in(1,N)\), \(\lambda,\mu>0\), \(f\) “mildy singular near zero,” and \(g\) of “subcritical growth.” Units: the abstract is dimensionless; if \(u\) is a physical potential or concentration, the \(p\)-Laplacian term requires a nontrivial dimensional scaling. Breakdown: \(f\) is singular near \(u=0\); for \(p<2\), \(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\) is singular at \(\nabla u=0\); for \(p>2\), the operator is degenerate at \(\nabla u=0\). The source says: “media with \(p > 2\) are called dilatant fluids, and media with \(p < 2\) are called pseudoplastics, while for \(p = 2\) they are called Newtonian fluids.”

3. Periodic Sturm–Liouville problem with discontinuous nonlinearity, [PAGE 37 of 83]. Source:
\[
-(p u')'+q u=\lambda f(x,u(x))\quad\text{in }]0,T[,\qquad u(0)=u(T),\quad u'(0)=u'(T),
\]
with \(p,q\in L^\infty([0,T])\), \(p(0)=p(T)\), \(q_0:=\mathrm{ess\,inf}_{[0,T]}q>0\), \(p_0:=\mathrm{ess\,inf}_{[0,T]}p>0\). Units: none specified; variables are abstract. Breakdown: the source states that “the set of the points of discontinuity of the nonlinear term \(f\) may also be uncountable.” Therefore classical differentiability of the energy functional cannot be assumed; the proof uses “critical point theory for non-differentiable functions.”

4. Stochastic fractionally dissipative quasi-geostrophic equation, [PAGE 66 of 83]. Source: 
\[
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}+(v\cdot\nabla)\theta=0,\qquad v=R\theta,
\]
and the stochastic version
\[
d\theta(t)+\big[(R\theta(t)\cdot\nabla)\theta(t)+\kappa(-\Delta)^\alpha\theta(t)\big]dt=G(t,\theta(t))\,dW(t),\qquad \theta(0)=\theta_0,
\]
with \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), \(R=(-R_2\theta,R_1\theta)\) in the typical Riesz-transform example. Units: \(\theta\) is temperature in kelvin, \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(\kappa\) must carry \(\mathrm{m^{2\alpha}\,s^{-1}}\) so that \(\kappa(-\Delta)^\alpha\theta\) has units of \(\mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}\). Breakdown: existence of a weak solution is claimed for \(\alpha\in(0,1)\); uniqueness is claimed only in the “sub-critical case, i.e. \(\alpha\in(1/2,1)\).” Uniqueness outside that range is not asserted.

5. Grounded shallow ice-sheet obstacle problem, [PAGE 14 of 83]. The source does not print the full PDE, but states: “Since the thickness of the ice sheet is constrained to be nonnegative, the problem under consideration is an obstacle problem,” and the model is “governed by a set of variational inequalities that involve nonlinearities in both the time derivative and in the elliptic term.” The recovered limit is a “doubly nonlinear parabolic variational inequality.” Units: ice thickness \(h\) in metres; time in seconds; the nonlinear elliptic and time-derivative terms require compatible diffusive and forcing units. Breakdown: the obstacle is \(h\ge0\); the coincidence set \(h=0\) is the grounded/contact region. The proof route uses “a penalized relaxation” and “a discretization in time,” so the result is an existence result for the relaxed/discretized problem, not a quantitative melt forecast.

6. Plate thickness optimization with penalization, [PAGE 11 of 83]. Source penalized function: \(u\mapsto\eta^{-1}\beta(u-\frac12 e-\Phi)\), “with a deflection \(u\), a thickness function \(e\) and an obstacle function \(\Phi\).” Units: \(u,e,\Phi\) are lengths in metres; \(\beta\) must be dimensionally consistent with an energy density after multiplication by \(\eta^{-1}\). Breakdown: \(\eta\to0\) is a penalization limit; the admissible set consists of limits of penalized solutions. The source warns that in the elastic case the acceleration term “has a form of a vector measure,” and that the restriction is due to “the lack of admissible estimates of accelerations of all possible states.” Therefore the optimal thickness is a limit of penalized optima, not an unqualified minimizer over all possible states.

7. Shell asymptotic reduction with contact, [PAGE 47 of 83] and [PAGE 49 of 83]. The shells have “thickness \(2\varepsilon\), clamped along their entire lateral face.” The asymptotic claim is that “when the small parameter \(\varepsilon\) tends to zero,” the three-dimensional solution converges to a two-dimensional limit independent of the transverse variable. Formal schema:
\[
u_\varepsilon \rightharpoonup u \quad(\varepsilon\to0),
\]
with error estimates under regularity assumptions. Units: \(\varepsilon\) in metres; displacements in metres; stresses in pascals. Breakdown: \(\varepsilon\to0\) is an asymptotic limit, not a finite-thickness identity. [PAGE 49 of 83] states the error proof assumes “a certain regularity for the normal component in the solution of the two-dimensional limit problem.” Without that regularity, the stated error estimate is not available.

8. Fixed-stress splitting for poromechanics, [PAGE 38 of 83]. The source says: “We show that the fixed stress split scheme is a contraction. We show this for both conformal and mixed finite elements in space and a backward Euler discretization in time.” Formal restatement:
\[
\|T(x)-T(y)\|\le \kappa\|x-y\|,\qquad \kappa<1,
\]
where \(T\) is the discrete fixed-stress iteration map. Units: \(x\) contains pressure in pascals and displacement in metres; the norm must be weighted or nondimensionalized for dimensional consistency. Breakdown: the source does not assert a universal contraction constant for all discretizations. It explicitly says “poorly designed schemes may lead to unstable schemes.” The contraction result is tied to the stated finite-element and time-stepping choices.

9. Frictional adhesion parameter, [PAGE 42 of 83]. The adhesion intensity \(\beta\) is “modelled by a nonlinear ODE” and has physical meaning in \([0,1]\): “\(\beta=1\) when the bonds are active, \(0<\beta<1\) when the bonds are partially active, and \(\beta=0\) when the bonds are severed.” Units: \(\beta\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: the mathematical difficulty is to prove \(\beta\in[0,1]\) “without making restrictions on the physical system.” The ODE is coupled to a nonlinear variational inequality; the bound is not automatic from the ODE alone.

10. Stochastic Reynolds equation with cavitation, [PAGE 57 of 83]. The gap is expressed as a Karhunen–Loève expansion; cavitation is incorporated through “a linear complementarity formulation, which can be equivalently written as a variational inequality.” Units: pressure in pascals, gap in metres, random field dimensionless or in metres depending on the KL modes. Breakdown: the cavitation region is “zero pressure value (coincidence set).” The source gives no numerical random-field covariance or bearing geometry, so it supports a stochastic variational-inequality formulation, not a specific lubrication design prediction.
