Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit.

The data pipeline operates as a sequence of information filters. Jeff Sackmann’s Grand Slam point-by-point files are joined with ATP/WTA match results to compute pre-match Elo [PAGE 2]. Rows are retained only if the match produced a valid non-tied final set score, the winner completed two or three sets, more than 20 points were recorded, and server/point-winner identities are unambiguous; this excludes 2,286 raw matches (21.8%) and leaves 8,222 matches and 1,505,355 point states [PAGE 2]. Each row is constructed strictly from information available before the next point, with the eventual match winner held in a separate target column to prevent leakage [PAGE 2]. Point labels (15, 30, 40, AD) are encoded numerically as 1, 2, 3, 4 for Markov compatibility [PAGE 2].

A multi-hop derivation then proceeds through three model strata before the final ensemble. First, Elo ratings are updated chronologically with a match-count-dependent gain K_i,t = 250/(m_i,t + 5)^0.4, so early-career matches move ratings more than late-career matches [PAGE 3]. Second, the Markov score recursion maps point-level serve probabilities p_a and p_b through tennis scoring rules (points → games → sets → match) via V(s) = q(s)V(T_1(s)) + (1−q(s))V(T_2(s)), with terminal conditions S_1 = m(b) or S_2 = m(b), where m(b) = ⌈b/2⌉ [PAGE 3]. Third, the serve-shrink layer blends live serve-point win rates r_i with pre-match Elo-derived priors π_i using Bayesian shrinkage: θ̂ = (n/(n+κ))θ̂_live + (κ/(n+κ))θ_prior, where κ is a pseudo-count controlling the prior’s influence [PAGE 4]. At 25% match progress κ=640, meaning the prior dominates until substantial in-match evidence accumulates; at 75% κ=40, ceding more weight to live observation [PAGE 4].

Trace stacks the Elo-asymmetric Markov probability p̂_M(s), the serve-shrink Markov probability p̂_S(s), their logits, their difference, and the raw feature vector z(s) into x(s), then passes x(s) to a histogram gradient-boosting classifier f_θ [PAGE 4]. The result is p̂_Trace(s) = f_θ(x(s)) [PAGE 4].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week evaluation traps.

The intellectual genealogy begins with Klaassen and Magnus (2003), who introduced a hierarchical Markov model updating win probability from current score [PAGE 2]. Gollub extended this by adding pre-match serve probabilities updated mid-match with a beta-binomial model, reaching 76.5% maximum accuracy [PAGE 2]. Lerner, Badri, and Monogue then proposed DeepTennis, an LSTM combining sequential point data with pre-match estimates for 79.5% test accuracy [PAGE 2]. Parallel to this, pre-match Elo was validated by Vaughan Williams et al. as superior to official rankings, and Kovalchik showed career-history Elo ranked second only to market consensus at ~70% accuracy [PAGE 2]. The serve-shrink component draws explicitly on Colin and Bechler’s Bayesian informative-prior binomial approach [PAGE 4]. Calibration assessment follows Guo et al.’s binning methodology [PAGE 6].

Critical multi-week traps emerge from temporal evaluation design. DeepTennis’s 2017 development / 2014 testing inversion is a look-ahead trap: matches after 2014 influenced training, contaminating the test period [PAGE 7]. The current study avoids this with a strict chronological split—train 2011–2021, validate 2022, test 2023–2024—but the authors still replicate DeepTennis’s non-chronological setup in Table 6 and warn it “should be interpreted cautiously because the split allows for potential data leakage” [PAGE 7]. A second trap is surface homogeneity: Grand Slam-only data mix hard, clay, and grass without surface-specific Elo, so a model trained across surfaces may misestimate a Roland Garros specialist on Wimbledon grass [PAGE 7]. A third trap is tour heterogeneity: WTA’s lower serve hold rate (~66% vs ATP ~79%) produces more service breaks and faster probability state transitions, meaning a single model trained on pooled ATP/WTA data may be miscalibrated for one tour [PAGE 7]. Finally, the 2023–2024 test window is short (two years), so multi-week form cycles, surface-season transitions, and player aging curves are underrepresented in the evaluation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock dynamics.

Crisis habit regression refers to the tendency of players to revert toward their established serve-quality baseline under pressure, rather than sustaining extreme live anomalies. The serve-shrink model operationalizes this: if a 70% serve player wins 9 of 10 early serve points (90% live), the shrinkage equation prevents an immediate jump to 90% because n is small relative to κ; at 25% progress κ=640 forces the estimate to remain close to the Elo-derived prior [PAGE 4]. This is a formalized reversion-to-the-mean for in-match performance.

The counter-trap is the HGBM’s vulnerability to overfitting recent noise. Because the HGBM learns directly from live features without the Markov structural prior, it can treat a short-run serving streak as a durable regime change. The paper notes HGBM is “extremely weak early” and falls behind symmetric Markov at the start, only becoming strong late [PAGE 7]. Trace avoids this counter-trap by feeding both Markov probabilities into the booster; when live noise is high early, the Markov terms anchor the prediction, and when live signal dominates late, the HGBM can reweight accordingly [PAGE 7].

The clock is the match-progress checkpoint system: 25%, 50%, 75% [PAGE 5]. Accuracy rises monotonically for all models as more of the match is observed [PAGE 5]. Trace’s largest advantage occurs in the middle stages, roughly 40%–70% progress [PAGE 5], exactly when pre-match priors are stale but live evidence is not yet decisive. Near match end, all models converge because the score state becomes deterministic [PAGE 5]. Thus the “theory of mind” embedded in Trace is recursive: it models player strength (Elo), models the opponent’s current serve quality (shrinkage), and models its own uncertainty about which of those two models is more trustworthy at a given point on the clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto layers.

Physical veto: The feature set lacks surface-specific performance. The paper admits the Elo system “does not directly account for different types of surfaces,” citing Nadal’s clay dominance as an example of surface-conditioned strength [PAGE 7]. A veto should trigger when a player’s surface-specific historical win rate deviates materially from their all-surface Elo prior.

Biomechanical veto: Handedness, injury, fatigue, and playing style are not represented [PAGE 7]. Although ace rate and rally length are tracked, raw serve velocity, unreturned-serve frequency, and return-quality metrics are absent from the listed feature groups [PAGE 2, Table 3]. A left-handed server with a wide slice serve on the ad court, or a player carrying a minor abdominal strain, is indistinguishable from a right-handed healthy server if their aggregate point-won rate is identical. The model can only detect the injury after it manifests in lost points, introducing lag.

Weather veto: Wind, heat, humidity, and precipitation are not features [PAGE 7]. Outdoor Grand Slam matches are materially affected by wind on serve toss and ball flight; heat affects serve speed and rally duration over long matches. Because these are exogenous to the point-state encoding, the Markov recursion and HGBM cannot condition on them.

Tour-structural veto: WTA matches exhibit greater fluctuation than ATP matches [PAGE 7], attributed to lower serve hold (~66% vs ~79%) and more frequent breaks [PAGE 7]. A veto layer should flag WTA states where games_diff changes by two or more games within a short point window, because the serve-based probability estimates may whipsaw faster than the feature update cycle can absorb.

The authors explicitly bound their claims to Grand Slam matches and state results “may not generalize well to lower-level tour events” [PAGE 8]. Any deployment outside Majors requires a veto unless retrained on ATP/WTA 250/500/1000 data.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
