<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of Binet’s theory—and James’s critique thereof—traces back to the late 19th-century debate between associationist psychology (Taine, Spencer) and emerging neurological reductionism (Charcot, Liébeault). Associationists explained hallucinations as overly strong memory traces activated by association, requiring no peripheral input. Neurologists like Binet, influenced by hypnosis research, sought a unitary physiology, arguing that even hypnagogic images stem from faint muscle twitches or eye movements (“peripheral”). This was a radical demotion of the “will” or “intellect” to mere epiphenomena of automatic nervous processes.

James, writing in 1890, stands at the precipice of two futures. He sympathizes with Binet’s materialist parsimony but remains skeptical of its completeness. His reference to prisms hints at an understanding of geometric optics that would soon inform Gestalt psychology. The “retrocausal” trap lies in assuming contemporary knowledge validates either side. We now know that thalamic reticular nucleus disinhibition can generate widespread cortical activation independent of periphery (explaining delirium tremens), yet optogenetic studies confirm that injecting artificial spikes into LGN reliably induces vivid percepts. Both mechanisms coexist. The historical genealogy shows how early scientists wrestled with the boundary condition between “internal” and “external” before neuroimaging provided dispositive tools. The multi-week trap for modern researchers is over-reliance on fMRI, which measures hemodynamics sluggish enough to miss the millisecond-scale “kindling” events James describes. Furthermore, the philosophical trap of “hard problem” dualism obscures the fact that even a purely computational system, given appropriate sensorimotor loops, would develop analogous distinctions—an insight anticipatory of active inference and enactivism.
