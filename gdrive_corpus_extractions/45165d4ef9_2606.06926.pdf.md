TF-SELECTOR's countermeasure is to make the *segment*, not the clip, the unit of both perception and scoring [PAGE 6, Section 4.1].

Hop 2 — The foundation is the alignment, not the model. Everything downstream (labels, mAP, HIT@K, IoU) inherits the correctness of the highlight-alignment algorithm. If Equation 1 mis-aligns, every metric is a measurement of the wrong thing. So the real audit target is the pixel-matching + temporal-prior decoder and its PSNR≥20 filter.

Hop 3 — Why PSNR, and where it breaks. The authors deliberately reject learned features: "long-form sports videos contain many visually similar scenes … which cause feature-based methods to produce false matches, whereas pixel-level PSNR reliably distinguishes near-identical frames" [PAGE 4]. That is a defensible engineering choice, but PSNR has a hard singularity: for identical frames MSE = 0 and PSNR = +∞. The temporal prior of Equation 1 exists precisely because two physically identical frames (a live moment and its replay) both sit at the PSNR ceiling, and the difference PSNR(hᵢ,f*) − PSNR(hᵢ,f⁺) becomes ∞−∞, i.e., undefined. That is the gray zone.

Hop 4 — Interpreting τ as a decision boundary. Equation 1 switches to the global best match f* only when it beats the temporally expected frame f⁺ by more than τ (dB). Under a Gaussian emission model this is a MAP test whose exact log-likelihood-ratio is a nonlinear function of PSNR; the linear-in-dB τ is a local linearization. The empirical sweep [PAGE 6, Table 5] shows τ=1 collapses to f* (72.3%), τ=10 over-commits to f⁺ (72.6%, remaining rate drops to 80.36%), and τ=5 is the balanced 51.7%/48.3% operating point. So τ is not arbitrary — it is a tuned prior-odds proxy.

Gray-zone audit (three genuine holes): (a) Replay degeneracy at the PSNR ceiling, acknowledged on PAGE 4; the prior is the only thing preventing a replay from stealing a live highlight. (b) Per-sport heterogeneity — Table 4 shows only 79.3% of baseball frames fall within <30 frames of f* versus 93.7% for racing, so a single global τ=5 is provably suboptimal for baseball. (c) A label-assignment edge case: with 1-second highlight segments [PAGE 5] and a ≥50%-of-2-seconds overlap rule, a highlight whose aligned frame sits exactly on a 2-second clip boundary overlaps each neighbor by only 0.5 s → 0.5/2 = 25% < 50% → label 0. Such highlights are silently dropped. This last point is verifiable from the text and is the strongest concrete criticism I can make of the labeling stage.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

**1. Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (matching kernel).** Standard definition (the paper uses it qualitatively on [PAGE 4]: "the PSNR score, which is inversely related to the mean squared error"):

$$\mathrm{PSNR}(h,f_t) = 10\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{L^2}{\mathrm{MSE}(h,f_t)}\right) = 20\,\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{L}{\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}(h,f_t)}}\right),\qquad \mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{i,j}\big(h_{ij}-f_{t,ij}\big)^2.$$

Units: PSNR in decibels (dB, a dimensionless power ratio); $L=2^B-1=255$ for 8-bit (data-numbers, DN); MSE in $\mathrm{DN}^2$. **Breakdown/singularity:** as $\mathrm{MSE}\to 0^+$, $\mathrm{PSNR}\to +\infty$ — unbounded, so identical frames (live vs. replay) sit at the ceiling and the difference in Eq. (1) is $\infty-\infty$ (undefined). Frames are downsampled to 144p first [PAGE 4], so PSNR is a *biased* estimator of full-resolution similarity (resampling alters the MSE distribution).

**2. Temporal-prior alignment decoder (Eq. 1, [PAGE 4]).**

$$\mathrm{align}(h_i)=\begin{cases} f^{+}, & \mathrm{PSNR}(h_i,f^{*})-\mathrm{PSNR}(h_i,f^{+})\le \tau\\[2pt] f^{*}, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad f^{*}=\arg\max_t \mathrm{PSNR}(h_i,f_t),\;\; f^{+}=f_p+r,$$

with $r$ = frame rate (frames·s⁻¹), $\tau$ in dB, $\tau=5$ dB chosen [PAGE 6].

**Bayesian reinterpretation (my derivation; state assumptions).** Assume additive white Gaussian pixel noise, $h=f_{t}+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. Then
$$\log p(h\mid f_t)=\text{const}-\frac{MN}{2\sigma^2}\mathrm{MSE}(h,f_t),\qquad \mathrm{MSE}=L^2\,10^{-\mathrm{PSNR}/10}.$$
The MAP rule between the continuity hypothesis (prior $\pi_{+}$ on $f^{+}$) and the jump hypothesis (prior $\pi_{*}$ on $f^{*}$) is: switch to $f^{*}$ iff
$$\Delta\log\mathcal L=\frac{MN\,L^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big(10^{-\mathrm{PSNR}(h,f^{+})/10}-10^{-\mathrm{PSNR}(h,f^{*})/10}\Big) > \log\frac{\pi_{+}}{\pi_{*}}.$$
$\Delta\log\mathcal L$ is a **nonlinear** (difference-of-exponentials) function of PSNR; the paper's **linear-in-dB** threshold $\tau$ is its local linearization at the operating point. Therefore Eq. (1) is only MAP-faithful for small PSNR gaps; for large gaps the true likelihood-ratio and the linear rule diverge (a real breakdown regime). **Non-identifiability:** the numerical map $\tau\leftrightarrow\log(\pi_+/\pi_*)$ cannot be recovered from the paper because $\sigma^2$ and $MN$ (post-144p pixel count) are not reported.

**3. Clip-score aggregation (Eq. 2, [PAGE 7]).**
$$s_C=\sum_i\left(\frac{\mathrm{overlap}(C,S_i)}{L_C}\times s_{S_i}\right),\qquad s_{S_i}\in[0,5].$$
Units: $s_C$ dimensionless on the LLM's $[0,5]$ scale [PAGE 11, Fig. 6]. If segments cover the clip ($\sum_i \mathrm{overlap}=L_C$) this is a **convex combination** (weights sum to 1); if they leave gaps, weights sum $<1$ and $s_C$ is **biased low** (sub-convex).

**4. Label generation [PAGE 5].** 1-second highlight segment $=[\,t^\*_i-0.5,\,t^\*_i+0.5\,]$ s; clip labeled $1$ iff $\mathrm{overlap}\ge 0.5\cdot L_C = 1$ s. **Boundary-loss regime:** a segment centered on a 2-second clip boundary yields overlap $0.5$ s with each neighbor → $0.5/2=25\%<50\%$ → label 0.

**5. Metrics [PAGE 8].** HIT@K with $K=|\mathrm{GT}_v|$ equals recall@K (and precision@K, since $K=|\mathrm{GT}|$): $\mathrm{HIT@K}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_v \frac{|\mathrm{Top}\text{-}K_v \cap \mathrm{GT}_v|}{|\mathrm{GT}_v|}\in[0,1]$. $\mathrm{IoU}=\frac{|P\cap G|}{|P\cup G|}$ over temporal clip-sets (seconds), $\in[0,1]$. mAP = mean over videos of the area under the precision–recall curve (ranking quality). **Information units:** if one recast PSNR as a rate-distortion quantity for a Gaussian source, $R(D)=\tfrac12\log_2(\sigma_X^2/D)$ bits·sample⁻¹ at distortion $D=\mathrm{MSE}$, and per-sample mutual information $I=\tfrac12\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ bits·sample⁻¹ — both $\to\infty$ as $\mathrm{MSE}\to0$, the same singularity as raw PSNR.

**6. Similarity QA [PAGE 5, Table 3].** All-sport PSNR $=26.74$ dB, SSIM $=0.865$ (dimensionless, $\in[-1,1]$; [34]), CLIP cosine $=0.955$ (dimensionless; [23]); remain-rate $91.7\%$.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits at the convergence of four lineages, and the "traps" are the multi-year methodological dead-ends each lineage dragged along.

Lineage A — signal fidelity. PSNR descends from 1970s broadcast/TV quality measurement; SSIM [34] (Wang et al., 2004) was introduced precisely because PSNR is a poor perceptual proxy. The paper leans on the older metric (PSNR) for alignment but reports the newer ones (SSIM, CLIP) only for validation — an inversion of the usual preference that is *justified* here by the "visually similar scenes … false matches" argument [PAGE 4]. The multi-week/multi-year trap: trusting CLIP/ResNet embeddings for near-duplicate detection in sports, which the authors explicitly avoid [PAGE 4].

Lineage B — highlight datasets. Sun et al.'s YouTube Highlights [31] (2014) established the raw-vs-edited paradigm; TVSum [27] (2015) and QVHighlights [15] (2021) added human saliency; Mr.HiSum [29] (2023) automated labels but stayed short (avg 3.4 min). The **recurring trap** is manual per-clip saliency annotation — "prohibitively time-consuming and labor-intensive" [PAGE 2] — which structurally caps dataset length. SVHighlights' escape is to use official highlight videos as free ground truth, replacing "tens to hundreds of hours of per-clip saliency labeling" [PAGE 11] with "lightweight human trimming and filtering" [PAGE 3, Table 1].

Lineage C — VTG architectures. Moment-DETR → UMT → QD-DETR → UniVTG → TR-DETR → CG-DETR, then video-LLM tuning (VTG-LLM [8], TimeChat [24], TRACE [9]). The trap: all are designed and tuned for ~2.5-minute clips, so they inherit a clip-scoring inductive bias that fails under distribution shift to hours.

Lineage D — foundation-model captioning/reasoning (InternVL2.5-8B [3], Llama-3-8B [6], WhisperX [2], TransNet V2 [28]). The multi-week trap here is the frame-budget wall: LLMs cap input frames, forcing the 75-second sampling gap [PAGE 2]. TF-SELECTOR's genealogical novelty is *compositional*: it recombines mature, independently-validated components rather than inventing a new trained model, which is exactly why it is "training-free" [PAGE 6].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the field as a set of agents with habits, facing a distribution shift, under a sampling clock.

Habit regression (baseline agents). A VTG-tuned model's designer optimizes for short-clip benchmarks; the learned policy is "score this clip in isolation." Under the crisis of hour-long input, that policy **regresses to its trained habit** and mis-scales — the paper's diagnosis that "clip-level scoring lacks the broader context" [PAGE 2] and that SL-Module's "fixed-length segments … fail to capture the temporal dynamics of hour-long broadcasts" [PAGE 8]. The predicted failure signature is metric-specific collapse: e.g., MH-DETR at 2.50 HIT@1 [PAGE 8, Table 7] — a near-floor regression.

The counter-trap (TF-SELECTOR agent). Instead of reasoning clip-locally, it reasons segment-globally: merge shots by transcript continuity, caption each segment, score once, broadcast the score to all member clips (Eq. 2). This anticipates the annotator's *own* reasoning (broadcasters cut highlights along semantic/scoring-event boundaries), so the predicted saliency distribution aligns with the label distribution. Table 10 confirms the theory-of-mind bet: transcripts (semantic intent) add +6.25 HIT@1 over captions alone, while audio volume alone adds little — the model must read *what the game means*, not just *how loud it got*.

The clock (hard constraint). The frame budget is the adversary every agent must beat. Uniform 96-frame sampling → 75 s/frame [PAGE 2] violates any Nyquist-like requirement for sub-150-second events. TF-SELECTOR defeats the clock by allocating frames *per segment* (one frame per 2-second clip, grouped by segment [PAGE 7]), so effective temporal resolution tracks semantic density rather than a global average. The recursive question — "what will the baseline do under my clock?" — is answered: it aliases; the segment agent does not, because its resolution is content-adaptive. The residual risk the paper under-models: the LLM's $[0,5]$ score is a *scalar* judgment with no calibrated uncertainty, so under an adversarial clock (very dense, very long segments up to 2 minutes [PAGE 7]) the single score can smear a 2-second highlight into a 120-second segment, diluting IoU.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
I adjudicate physical/domain hypotheses as vetoes (a claim survives only if no lower physical layer forbids it).

Veto 1 — Hallucinated highlights are physically excluded. Ground truth is the official highlight video from *the same broadcast*: "we selected only video pairs where the highlight video is sourced from the same broadcast as the full-length video, ensuring consistent visual and audio content" [PAGE 3]. Because a labeled highlight must have a pixel-level counterpart at PSNR ≥ 20 dB [PAGE 5], no label can be attached to content that does not physically exist in the source frames. Proof of veto: false-positive labels require a spurious PSNR ≥ 20 match; the temporal prior of Eq. (1) and the 99.82% auto-stage precision (only 0.18% genuine errors [PAGE 5, Table 2]) bound this. This veto holds.

Veto 2 — Weather/lighting degrades PSNR in outdoor sports (biomechanical/environmental layer). Direct sun, rain, shadow, and floodlight flicker change pixel intensities of *identical* scene content, lowering PSNR and inflating the filter's discard rate. The data adjudicate this: baseball (outdoor, day/night) has the lowest SSIM (0.835) and lowest remain-rate (85.8%), while controlled-camera racing tops PSNR (27.99) [PAGE 5, Table 3]. Prediction consistent with veto: outdoor, high-variability sports need looser PSNR handling; a fixed PSNR ≥ 20 filter over-prunes them. This veto *survives* (it is confirmed, not overturned).

Veto 3 — Fast biomechanics do not break frame-to-frame matching, but replays do. Player motion blur and rapid cuts affect intra-video continuity, yet alignment compares highlight-frame↔full-frame across two encodings, so motion per se is not fatal. The genuine physical adversary is the **replay**, "visually identical to the original scenes" [PAGE 4], which is a duplicate-at-the-PSNR-ceiling problem. Table 4 quantifies the exposure: 6.5% of frames have ≥1000-frame gaps between f* and f⁺ (jump decisions), spiking to 11.0% for baseball — consistent with frequent replays. The temporal prior (Eq. 1) is the only physical-layer defense; where it is weakest (baseball), residual error is highest. Veto survives with a bounded, sport-dependent leak.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Framing: the auto-generated labels act as a *verifiable reward* (official highlights = checkable ground truth). I give three falsifiable veto scenarios where that reward would mislead, each with a prediction testable against the paper's own tables.

Veto A — Replay-induced reward mislabeling. Scenario: a highlight occurs live at $t_2$ and repeats at replay $t_1$; a decoder error attaches the label to $t_1$. Prediction (falsifiable): sports with the largest f*–f⁺ jump fractions should show the worst label integrity. Verify: Table 4 gives baseball the highest ≥1000-frame jump rate (11.0%) and lowest <30-frame rate (79.3%); the user study then shows baseball with a modest positive–negative gap (Diff 1.42, Pos 3.08 [PAGE 6, Table 6]) — middling, not top, exactly as the veto predicts. Counter-check that would *falsify* the veto: if baseball showed the *highest* user-study Diff, the replay hypothesis would be wrong. It does not.

Veto B — Near-noise reward for domain-opaque sports. Scenario: if a sport's highlights require expert knowledge, the verifiable reward is low-quality even though it is "ground truth." Prediction: Krippendorff's α and the Diff should predict per-sport separability. Verify: racing has α = 0.221 and Diff = 0.53 (the floor) [PAGE 6, Table 6]; rugby is similarly low (Diff 0.52). Falsifiable corollary the paper does *not* report and should: per-sport model performance. My prediction: TF-SELECTOR's advantage over baselines shrinks toward zero on racing/rugby, because the reward there is near-random (α ≈ 0.22). This is directly testable by re-scoring Table 7 metrics per category.

Veto C — Boundary-clip label starvation. Scenario: the 1-second segment + ≥50%-of-2-seconds rule (Section SOURCE_OPERATORS eq. 4) deterministically zeroes highlights centered on 2-second clip boundaries; the verifiable reward is systematically wrong for boundary-aligned events. Prediction: overlap-strict IoU should be depressed relative to boundary-tolerant Window-F1. Verify: Table 12 shows every method with a large Window-F1-minus-IoU gap (TF-SELECTOR 27.38 vs 10.58; UMT 30.14 vs 6.40; TRACE 17.61 vs 7.63) under the ±6 s ($w=3$ clips) tolerance [PAGE 11]. A gap this consistent across all methods is the signature of boundary/tolerance sensitivity. Falsifying test: shrink the tolerance to $w=0$; the gap should collapse if boundary loss is the cause.

Net adjudication: Vetoes A and C are *confirmed in-sample* by the paper's own tables; Veto B is a *prospective, testable prediction* requiring per-sport model evaluation that the paper omits. None of the three overturns the headline result (TF-SELECTOR leads HIT@1 26.56, HIT@K 16.90, IoU 10.58 [PAGE 8]), but each bounds the *validity domain* of the verifiable-reward labels, which is the honest ceiling on every reported number.
