Tolman’s Means-End Field Theory posits that behavior \( B \) is a function of the organism’s perception of the environment \( M \), goals \( G \), and available paths \( P \):  
\[
B = f(M, G, P)
\]  
where \( M \) includes the cognitive map encoding topological relations among locations. The key equation is the expectancy-valence model (later formalized by expectancy theorists):  
\[
V_{\text{path}} = E[\text{goal attainment}] \times \text{valence}(\text{goal})
\]  
Here, \( V_{\text{path}} \) is the subjective value of taking a path, \( E[\cdot] \) is the learned expectation of reaching the goal, and valence is the reward magnitude. In the block-at-B condition, Path 2’s expectancy drops to near zero because it leads to a blocked goal, while Path 3’s expectancy remains high (if the map indicates it bypasses the block). Thus, \( V_{\text{Path 3}} > V_{\text{Path 2}} \), driving the switch.

**Units & Assumptions**:  
- \( V \) is in arbitrary utility units (higher = more likely chosen).  
- Expectation \( E \in [0,1] \) is a probability-like belief state.  
- Valence is positive for reward, negative for punishment.  
- Assumes transitivity of preference and consistency of the cognitive map across trials.  

**Singularity/Breakdown Regimes**:  
1. **Perceptual Saturation**: If environmental cues are insufficient to form a stable map (\( M \to \text{noise} \)), then \( E \) becomes unreliable, and behavior reverts to S-R habits. This occurs in cue-poor environments (e.g., uniform darkness), where \( E[\text{goal}] \) collapses to prior habit strength, breaking the means-end calculation.  
2. **Goal Ambiguity**: If multiple goals exist with conflicting valences, \( V_{\text{path}} \) may become ill-defined, leading to indecision or random choice.  
3. **Topological Complexity**: For mazes with many loops or non-Euclidean geometries, the cognitive map may fail to encode shortest paths, causing suboptimal routing. Here, the simple triangular layout (Paths 1,2,3) avoids this regime.  
4. **Sample Size Limit**: With \( N=10 \), statistical power is low for detecting small effects (\( \Delta \hat{p} < 0.2 \)). The singularity is that individual variance dominates population trends, risking false positives/negatives.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s 1932 experiments emerged from a crisis in behaviorism. The dominant S-R paradigm (Thorndike, Watson) could explain simple conditioning but failed with complex navigation like maze learning, where animals showed sudden improvements (“insight”) after latent learning periods. Tolman, influenced by Gestalt psychology (Köhler’s insight studies with apes), proposed that organisms learn about relationships, not just responses. His genealogy includes:  
- **Gestalt Principles (1920s)**: Emphasis on holistic perception and reorganization of experience.  
- **Köhler’s Insight Experiments (1917, 1927)**: Demonstrated problem-solving without trial-and-error, challenging Thorndike.  
- **Woodworth’s Expectancy Theory (1938)**: Later formalized Tolman’s ideas into quantifiable expectancies.  

However, multi-week traps existed. Early critics (e.g., Hullian neo-behaviorists) argued that “cognitive maps” were unscientific mentalistic constructs. They attempted to replicate Tolman’s results using S-R mechanisms like response generalization or stimulus sampling. Some succeeded in mimicking the data with sophisticated reinforcement schedules, creating a decades-long controversy. Only with modern neuroscience (place cells, grid cells) did the biological basis of cognitive mapping become undeniable, vindicating Tolman retroactively. The trap was mistaking correlation (behavior fits map model) for causation (map causes behavior), a fallacy only broken by intervening variables like hippocampal lesions impairing such transfers.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
