The paper’s core chain is not a static accounting identity; it is an equilibrium mapping from firm dynamics to mark-up dispersion and then to aggregate efficiency and growth. The first hop is the product-market pricing block. Final demand for variety \(i\) has unitary elasticity, \(y(i,t)=Y(t)/p(i,t)\) [PAGE 7], so Bertrand competition with perfectly substitutable brands forces the leader to limit-price at the follower’s marginal cost: \(p(i,t)=MC(q_F,t)=\psi(R,w)/q_F(i,t)\) [PAGE 7, eq. (5)]. The equilibrium mark-up is therefore the productivity gap \(\mu(i,t)=q(i,t)/q_F(i,t)\) [PAGE 7, eq. (6)]. This is the key identification move: high measured revenue productivity is interpreted as monopoly power, not necessarily as a binding input constraint.

The second hop is static aggregation. Factor demands are \(k(i,t)=\mu(i,t)^{-1}\alpha Y(t)/R(t)\) and \(l(i,t)=\mu(i,t)^{-1}(1-\alpha)Y(t)/w(t)\) [PAGE 8, eq. (7)]; profits are \(\pi(i,t)=(1-\mu(i,t)^{-1})Y(t)\) [PAGE 8, eq. (8)]; and marginal products satisfy \(MPK(i,t)=R(t)\mu(i,t)\), \(MPL(i,t)=w(t)\mu(i,t)\), with \(TFPR(i,t)\propto \mu(i,t)\) [PAGE 8]. Thus the entire cross-sectional dispersion of revenue productivity is mark-up dispersion. The aggregate wedges are \(M(t)=\exp(E[\ln \mu(i,t)^{-1}])/E[\mu(i,t)^{-1}]\) and \(\Lambda(t)=E[\mu(i,t)^{-1}]\) [PAGE 9, eqs. The third hop is endogenous firm dynamics. Productivity is a quality ladder, \(q=\lambda^n\), and the mark-up is \(\mu(i,t)=\lambda^{\Delta(i,t)}\) where \(\Delta=n_L-n_F\) [PAGE 10, eq. (13)]. The fourth hop is welfare. The paper writes welfare as \(W=\text{const}+\rho^{-1}(\ln c+\rho^{-1}g/(1-\alpha))\) [PAGE 15, eq. (23)] and, in the no-capital/no-reallocation case, \(W=\text{const}+\rho^{-1}(\ln M+g/\rho)\) [PAGE 15, eq. (24)]. First, limit pricing is not generic: footnote 6 says if demand elasticity exceeds unity, the leader may set the unconstrained monopoly price when its advantage is large enough [PAGE 7]. Second, the empirical proxy is the entry rate \(z\), not the theoretically required entry intensity \(x=z/I\): “I will use the rate of entry \(z\) as my measure of entry in the empirical exercise” [PAGE 24, footnote 33]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is Schumpeterian quality ladders plus misallocation accounting. The paper explicitly follows Aghion and Howitt (1992) and Grossman and Helpman (1991) and constructs a quality-ladder model in the Schumpeterian tradition [PAGE 5]. It imports limit pricing from Bernard et al. (2003) and Acemoglu and Akcigit (2012) [PAGE 5]. Its balanced-growth characterization is compared to Klette and Kortum (2004) and Lentz and Mortensen (2008) [PAGE 5]. Its static misallocation lineage is Hsieh and Klenow (2009), Restuccia and Rogerson (2008), and Bartelsman et al. (2013) [PAGE 5]. The Pareto tail is explicitly analogized to Gabaix (1999) city-size dynamics: mark-ups rise stochastically while a firm survives and reset upon exit [PAGE 13]. The empirical mark-up measurement follows De Loecker and Warzynski (2012) and De Loecker et al. (2012) [PAGE 20]. Entry costs are linked to Djankov et al. (2002) regulation-of-entry work [PAGE 17]. The paper also situates itself against trade-mark-up work such as Atkeson and Burstein and Edmond et al. [PAGE 5-6].

The retrocausal trap is that later empirical work can read the residual \(\ln(s_l^{-1})\) as “mark-up” only because the theory supplies the interpretation. Historically, the static misallocation literature treated wedges as exogenous; this paper reverses the causality by making entry and innovation shape the wedge distribution. The multi-week trap is temporal aggregation: the model is annualized and calibrated to annual Indonesian data; replacing annual rates with weekly rates requires compounding, \(g_{\text{weekly}}=(1+g_{\text{annual}})^{1/52}-1\), not \(g_{\text{annual}}/52\). The paper never estimates weekly dynamics, so any weekly extrapolation is outside the source. A second multi-week trap is cohort age: the Poisson age formula uses continuous time and annual innovation intensity \(I\); interpreting “age” as weeks without rescaling \(I\) changes the predicted mark-up-age slope. A third trap is using \(z\) for \(x=z/I\): the theory’s sufficient statistic is entry intensity, but the empirical work uses the entry rate \(z\) because \(I\) is poorly measured in sparse cells [PAGE 24, footnote 33]. A fourth trap is treating the Pareto shape as fixed; it is endogenous to \(x\), so a policy that changes entry costs changes both the distribution and the growth rate.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model’s agents have rational expectations, not explicit higher-order beliefs, but their decisions encode a recursive theory of mind. Incumbents choose innovation \(I\) knowing that entry flow \(z\) may replace them; the HJB equation subtracts the expected loss \(z(V(\Delta)-V(-1))\) and adds the option value of innovation [PAGE 17, eq. (28)]. Entrants choose entry knowing \(V(1)=\chi w\), so they anticipate incumbents’ mark-up and innovation environment [PAGE 18, eq. (31)]. The policymaker who lowers entry costs \(\chi\) must anticipate both the direct pro-competitive effect and the incumbent innovation response \(\omega=\partial I/\partial z\). The “crisis habit regression” is the incumbent’s fallback under competitive stress: if entry rises, the incumbent may either intensify innovation or be crowded out. The model’s sign is not predetermined; Proposition 4 gives \(\partial I/\partial \chi\) ambiguous and \(\partial x/\partial \chi<0\) [PAGE 18]. The “counter-trap” is the assumption that entry is always dynamically beneficial because it reduces static misallocation. Equation (26) shows that the counter-trap fails when \(x\omega\) approaches or exceeds one; Table 10 shows a negative dynamic multiplier at high R&D share [PAGE 32]. The “clock” is the age distribution: mark-ups rise with firm age according to \(E[\ln\mu\mid t]=\ln\lambda\,It\) [PAGE 13], while entry resets the clock. A policy’s short-run static gain can therefore differ from its long-run BGP growth gain. The recursive equilibrium is consistent because firms’ value functions and the free-entry condition are solved jointly; the theory-of-mind layer is the mutual anticipation of entry and innovation, not a behavioral bias.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
