Breakdown regimes: (i) visible set $\mathcal{V} = \emptyset$ for prolonged time $\Rightarrow$ absolute position unobservable, drift unbounded [PAGE 9]; (ii) $N_p < N$ or no initial association $\Rightarrow$ association problem infeasible without manual reset [PAGE 9]; (iii) soft-tissue artifact or skin-marker migration violates rigidity, invalidating the distance-invariant shape metric [PAGE 9]; (iv) $\Delta t$ beyond the short-interval MEMS regime $\Rightarrow$ cubic error growth invalidates the millimeter bound.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week failure traps.

Lineage. The disclosed method inherits from four technical lineages. (i) Inertial navigation and dead-reckoning trace to early 20th-century gyroscopic stabilization (Sperry) and Apollo-era inertial measurement units (Draper Laboratory), where double integration of accelerometers and gyroscopes was used to propagate position in the absence of external references. (ii) Recursive state estimation enters via Kalman filtering (1960s) and its inertial-navigation variants, which fuse IMU propagation with intermittent position updates to bound drift. (iii) Optical motion capture derives from marker-based photogrammetry systems (e.g., Vicon, Optitrack) that suffer from line-of-sight occlusion; the patent explicitly contrasts its fused approach with a stand-alone optical system that becomes “useless” after a two-second marker occlusion [PAGE 9]. (iv) Biomechanical linked-segment modeling follows Winter’s rigid-segment conventions (1979), where joint constraints and segment coordinates enforce anatomical plausibility; Xsens commercialized MEMS-based inertial motion tracking (MTw, MTi) that fuses these principles into wearable suits.

Multi-week traps. Over multi-week deployment horizons, the following latent failure modes accumulate outside the single-session scope of [PAGE 9]: (a) MEMS bias instability and temperature-dependent scale-factor errors cause $\mathbf{b}_g$ and $\mathbf{b}_a$ to drift, invalidating the cubic error bound for any gap longer than the calibrated short interval; (b) subject morphology changes (muscle fatigue, hydration, clothing) alter soft-tissue artifact magnitudes, breaking the rigid-segment distance invariance assumed in FIG. 6 [PAGE 9]; (c) marker or sensor-unit migration relative to bony landmarks invalidates the initial association and the fixed geometric offsets used in the optimization search space; (d) repeated entering and leaving the optical tracking volume creates “re-identification” ambiguity: after a prolonged absence, inertial drift is large enough that eq. (3) may associate a returning limb to the wrong positional unit, especially if the subject’s gait or limb configuration has changed between sessions. These are not explicitly modeled in the source text but are direct consequences of its stated assumptions.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

The patent embeds an implicit theory of mind about the human operator. The designers assume the user can perform an initialization step in which “a first correct association between inertial sensing units and corresponding positional units is provided,” for example via manual labeling during system calibration [PAGE 9]. This models the operator as a reliable, attentive agent capable of establishing ground-truth correspondences at session start.

Crisis habit regression. When an occlusion event breaks the marker-inertial association, the operator’s crisis habit is to regress to manual re-labeling or to restart the calibration routine [PAGE 9]. The system anticipates this by providing the eq. (3) association test [PAGE 9], but the test itself depends on the initial association being correct [PAGE 9]. Thus under crisis conditions the human–system loop regresses to the very manual intervention the inventors sometimes wish to eliminate.

Counter-trap. A counter-trap arises when the subject leaves the tracking volume and returns after a prolonged inertial dead-reckoning interval [PAGE 9]. The system’s implicit assumption is that the returning subject presents the same linked-segment geometry as before departure. The counter-trap is that the shape-matching optimization [PAGE 9] may lock onto a kinematically similar but incorrect association (e.g., left foot matched to right foot marker because both exhibit similar drift magnitudes), especially when $N < N_p$ and multiple candidates exist [PAGE 9]. The linked-segment constraint mitigates but does not eliminate this trap; it is a failure of the system’s theory of mind that the operator’s body configuration is invariant across absences.

Clock. The real-time constraint is explicit: the optical measurement period ranges from “some milliseconds to a few tens of milliseconds” [PAGE 9], and the demonstrated gap tolerance is a 2 s interval within a 15 s walking trial [PAGE 9]. The clock defines the critical horizon $\Delta t_{\text{crit}}$ beyond which cubic inertial error growth exceeds the 6 mm practical threshold [PAGE 9]. If the system’s association loop runs slower than the optical update rate, or if gap duration approaches or exceeds $\Delta t_{\text{crit}}$, the prediction degrades and the association test becomes unreliable. The interplay is therefore: manual initialization sets the clock origin; occlusion consumes the clock budget; crisis regression resets the clock only if the operator intervenes correctly.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof with adjudication hierarchy.

Physical-layer veto. The MEMS inertial layer is vetoed by integration drift. Bias instability $b_g$ and accelerometer noise $n_a$ make absolute position error grow as $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^3)$ for attitude-coupled gravity leakage [PAGE 9]. When the gap duration approaches or exceeds the regime where $\tfrac{1}{6} g b_g \Delta t^3$ exceeds several millimeters, the physical layer vetoes continued dead-reckoning without optical aiding. This is the dominant veto at the 1–5 second scale for walking.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The linked-segment model assumes rigid segments connected by ideal joints [PAGE 9]. Soft-tissue artifact (STA), skin-marker sliding, and joint constraint violations during impact, sprinting, or cutting change inter-marker distances by amounts comparable to or larger than the 6 mm tolerance [PAGE 9]. When STA distorts the underlying geometric shape, the rigid-body transformation observed in FIG. 6 [PAGE 9] no longer holds, and the shape-invariant optimization yields false associations. This veto dominates at high-dynamic, high-impact regimes even if optical markers remain visible.

Environmental / weather-layer veto. The source is silent on outdoor weather, but optical tracking requires line-of-sight within a tracking volume [PAGE 9]. Occlusion by clothing, equipment, or the subject’s own body acts as an environmental veto on the positional layer. Temperature drift of MEMS biases is an environmental physical-layer effect that shortens the valid dead-reckoning horizon over long sessions. No precipitation, wind, or atmospheric propagation model appears in [PAGE 9]; the system is implicitly indoor or controlled-environment.

Adjudication. Hierarchy of veto precedence: (1) Environmental zero-visibility ($\mathcal{V}=\emptyset$) triggers immediate fallback to pure inertial dead-reckoning with bounded-time validity [PAGE 9]; (2) Physical drift exceeding the millimeter-to-centimeter threshold vetoes absolute-position trust and forces reliance on relative linked-segment constraints [PAGE 9]; (3) Biomechanical non-rigidity vetoes shape-matching association and requires kinematic joint limits or re-initialization [PAGE 9]. If two vetoes fire simultaneously (e.g., prolonged occlusion plus sprinting dynamics), the system must declare an association fault and request manual re-calibration, because neither inertial drift bounds nor rigid-shape invariance can be guaranteed [PAGE 9].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards) counterfactual veto scenarios with explicit predictions. Each is falsifiable with sensor logs and motion-capture ground truth.

Scenario 1 — High-dynamic occlusion veto. Counterfactual: replace the walking trial [PAGE 9] with a sprinting trial containing a 2 s optical gap at the same 10–12 s window. Verifiable condition: peak leg angular velocity exceeds 300 deg/s and peak vertical acceleration exceeds 6 g. Prediction: position error during the gap exceeds $20\times 10^{-3}$ m (well above the 6 mm walking bound [PAGE 9]) because gyroscope bias-coupled attitude error projects large specific-force errors into the vertical channel. Veto triggers automatic re-initialization request.

Scenario 2 — Zero-visibility prolonged absence veto. Counterfactual: subject leaves the optical volume for 8 s and returns. Verifiable condition: visible marker count $|\mathcal{V}|=0$ for $\Delta t > 5$ s. Prediction: absolute inertial drift exceeds $50\times 10^{-3}$ m; upon re-entry, the eq. (3) association statistic [PAGE 9] selects an incorrect candidate with probability $>0.5$ among $N_p \ge 4$ markers [PAGE 9]. Veto disables automatic association and forces use of linked-segment shape matching [PAGE 9] or manual reset.

Scenario 3 — Soft-tissue artifact shape-variance veto. Counterfactual: lateral cutting maneuver with skin-mounted positional units. Verifiable condition: measured inter-marker distance variance exceeds $4\times 10^{-3}$ m$^2$ relative to the rigid-segment model. Prediction: the rigid-body transformation assumption of FIG. 6 [PAGE 9] is violated; the Procrustes-style association cost [reconstructed in SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS] yields multiple local minima within $5\times 10^{-3}$ m, causing false marker-to-inertial associations. Veto switches association to kinematic joint-limit filtering and raises a soft-tissue artifact flag.
