Historical and scientific lineage of Knight's demand conditions, and the multi-week trap structure implicit in [PAGE 145].

Knight's four conditions descend from the marginalist revolution. Condition 1's demographic stock echoes Jevons's (1871) treatment of population as the basis of aggregate utility, though Knight replaces Jevons's hedonic calculus with a compositional measure \(\pi_C(c)\). Condition 2's "psychic attributes" draw directly from Menger's (1871) subjective theory of value and from Veblen's (1899) institutional emphasis on habit, custom, and conspicuous standards; Knight's phrase "social inheritance" is a Veblenian concept translated into demand analysis. The institutional control examples—"authority of parents, sumptuary laws"—anticipate later work on intra-household allocation (e.g., Becker, 1965) and legal constraints on consumption.

Condition 3's income-distribution problem connects to Pigou's (1920) Economics of Welfare and to the marginal productivity distribution framework, but Knight adds a crucial reflexive twist: income is both an immediate demand condition and an equilibrium outcome determined by supply. This circularity prefigures Keynes's (1936) effective-demand principle, where income and demand determine each other, and Hayek's (1945) dispersed-knowledge argument, where no single mind holds all the "facts" about income and resources.

Condition 4's geographic distribution locates demand in space and environment, linking to von Thünen's agricultural location theory and to Marshall's (1890) industrial districts, but Knight's inclusion of "environmental conditions affecting desirability of sites for habitation" [PAGE 145] makes climate and carrying capacity explicit determinants of where consuming units can reside.

The chapter title, "CHANGE WITH UNCERTAINTY ABSENT," situates this analysis in a stationary world before Knight's famous risk/uncertainty distinction. Here probabilities over commodity characteristics are assumed knowable ("actual knowledge or beliefs as to the real characteristics"), yet the inclusion of beliefs as a demand condition plants the seed for later uncertainty: if beliefs are the object of demand, and beliefs can be erroneous or non-probabilizable, then the "absence of uncertainty" is itself a fragile assumption.

Multi-week trap: Because competitive adjustment is not instantaneous, the initial facts \((N,\pi_C,\psi,F_Y,\rho)\) persist as state variables over weeks. Habits \(h_i\) and mores \(m_i\) adjust slowly; income distribution \(F_Y\) responds to supply with a lag; geographic density \(\rho(x)\) is sticky because relocation costs and environmental desirability \(\varepsilon(x)\) change slowly. A shock to productive resources \(R\) therefore propagates through income \(Y\) into demand \(Q_d\) over a multi-week horizon, during which the economy may be trapped in a disequilibrium configuration sustained by psychic and institutional inertia. The trap is "multi-week" because price signals may move faster than habit, custom, or population geography, so short-run demand does not clear even when long-run supply conditions have shifted.

Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the adjustment clock in Knight's demand architecture.

Knight's condition 2 embeds a recursive theory of mind: consuming units hold beliefs \(K_i\) about commodity characteristics \(\chi\), and they are subject to control by other units via \(\mathcal R_{\text{control}}\) ("authority of parents, sumptuary laws, etc.") [PAGE 145]. Agent \(i\) models commodity \(q\); agent \(j\) models agent \(i\)'s consumption; institutions modulate both. This nesting creates multiple equilibria in expectations: if everyone believes a good has characteristic \(\chi'\), demand shifts as if \(\chi'\) were true, even if \(\chi\neq\chi'\).

Crisis habit regression: Under competitive or environmental shock, agents face cognitive overload in recomputing \(d_q(p;c_i,\psi_i,Y_i,x_i)\) from first principles. Knight's inclusion of "habit, custom, tastes, standards, mores" as inherited social attributes [PAGE 145] implies a regression to the social mean: agents fall back on \(\psi_i^{\text{habit}}\), the pre-crisis psychic configuration. Demand therefore does not re-optimize instantly; it reverts to customary bundles. This is habit regression as a crisis-stabilizing (or crisis-locking) mechanism.

Counter-trap: Sophisticated agents or institutions anticipate the regression and design around it. A producer knowing that tastes \(t_i\) are sticky can introduce new goods framed as extensions of old habits; a legislator knowing that sumptuary laws exploit inherited mores can pass restrictions that bind precisely because agents will not reprogram their psychic attributes quickly. The counter-trap is the deliberate manipulation of the regression: induce rivals to revert to outdated \(\psi\) while the manipulator advances on the new frontier of \(K_i\) (beliefs about real characteristics).

Knight's passage implies \(\tau_{\text{habit}}\gg\tau_{\text{price}}\): habits, mores, and geographic distribution are "given facts" that persist while prices and incomes fluctuate [PAGE 145]. If a crisis arrives on a timescale \(\tau_{\text{crisis}}<\tau_{\text{habit}}\), habit regression dominates and demand remains anchored to pre-crisis \(\psi\). If \(\tau_{\text{crisis}}>\tau_{\text{habit}}\), institutions have time to reshape \(m_i\) and \(K_i\), and demand can re-optimize. The clock thus determines whether the economy exits the crisis through price re-equilibration or through psychic/institutional lock-in.

If any layer vetoes, the economic layer must re-solve subject to the constraint; the psychic/social attributes \(\psi_i\) in condition 2 cannot override a hard physical, biomechanical, or meteorological boundary. This establishes a veto hierarchy: physical/biomechanical/weather \(\succ\) income \(\succ\) psychic/social \(\succ\) price.

Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards) counterfactual veto scenarios derived from Knight [PAGE 145], each with a falsifiable prediction.

Scenario 1 — Taste manipulation under income collapse.
Counterfactual: An RLVR agent assumes demand \(Q_d\) can be raised by shifting tastes \(t_i\) (condition 2) while holding income \(Y_i\) fixed. Veto: Knight states money income is an immediate demand condition and that its distribution ultimately depends on supply [PAGE 145]. If a supply shock drives \(Y_i\to0\) for a positive-measure subset of \(\Omega\), the income distribution \(F_Y\) develops a mass at zero; no taste adjustment can generate effective demand without purchasing power. Formal veto: \(Y_i=0 \Rightarrow d_q(p;\cdot)=0\) for \(p>0\), regardless of \(\partial d_q/\partial t_i\). Prediction: In markets where a verifiable income collapse occurs (e.g., resource-region shutdown), advertising or preference-shaping interventions will produce no measurable increase in quantity demanded; \(Q_d\) tracks \(Y_{\text{agg}}\), not \(\psi\).

Scenario 2 — Belief-reality information severance.
Counterfactual: An RLVR agent treats consumer beliefs \(K_i\) about commodity characteristics \(\chi\) as perfectly aligned with \(\chi\), so demand is modeled as a function of true quality only. Veto: Knight explicitly includes "actual knowledge or beliefs as to the real characteristics of commodities" as a separate demand condition [PAGE 145], meaning \(K_i\) and \(\chi\) can diverge. If the mutual information \(I(K_i;\chi)\) is verifiably low (misinformation regime), demand based on \(\chi\) is mis-specified. Formal veto: \(I(K_i;\chi)<\epsilon \Rightarrow \text{Cov}(d_q,\chi)\approx0\); demand tracks beliefs, not characteristics. Prediction: After a verifiable information correction (e.g., product recall revealing \(\chi\neq K_i\)), quantity demanded will exhibit a discontinuous drop proportional to the prior belief-error gap \(K_i-\chi\), not a smooth quality adjustment.

Scenario 3 — Geographic reallocation under environmental veto.
Counterfactual: An RLVR planner assumes population consuming units can be costlessly reallocated across space to match productive resources \(r(x)\), so local demand follows supply. Veto: Knight states geographic distribution is fixed by productive resources and "environmental conditions affecting desirability of sites for habitation" [PAGE 145]; weather and habitability \(\varepsilon(x)\) can veto occupancy. If \(\varepsilon(x)\) falls below a habitability threshold (e.g., verifiable climate event renders a site uninhabitable), \(\rho(x)=0\) and the population cannot be reallocated by price signals alone. Formal veto: \(\varepsilon(x)<\varepsilon_{\text{crit}}\Rightarrow\rho(x)=0\Rightarrow Q_d(x)=0\) for all local goods, irrespective of \(p\) or \(Y_i\). Prediction: In regions hit by a verifiable environmental desirability shock, local demand will not recover through price adjustment or income transfers; consumption units remain geographically fixed, and interregional trade cannot substitute for local presence of consuming units.
