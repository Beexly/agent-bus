The threshold-crossing repetition is included in the working-set count [PAGE 860]. FAIL sets continue to volitional failure, defined as inability to complete another concentric repetition with acceptable technique despite maximal intended effort [PAGE 860].

Because relative load is fixed at 30% of current 1RM and updated only after session 8, the repetition volume is not an independently randomized dose; it is a deterministic consequence of the termination strategy under a stationary relative-intensity prescription [PAGE 859-860]. This produces the observed working-set volumes of 55.91 ± 0.61, 74.26 ± 0.89, and 94.08 ± 0.93 repetitions per session for VL10%, VL40%, and FAIL respectively (all adjusted p < 0.001) [PAGE 863]. Total working repetitions over 16 sessions are 894.6 ± 9.8, 1188.2 ± 14.2, and 1505.3 ± 14.9 [PAGE 863 Table 4]. Session-averaged set RPE on the Borg 6-20 scale is 12.44 ± 0.10, 15.53 ± 0.31, and 16.74 ± 0.16 [PAGE 863].

Gray-zone audit. (1) Arterial occlusion pressure is not directly measured with Doppler ultrasound; cuff pressure is assigned from thigh circumference per Loenneke et al. (2015) and represents estimated, not confirmed, 50% arterial occlusion pressure [PAGE 859]. (2) The 5 calibration repetitions per session (80 total over the intervention) are excluded from working-set repetition totals yet still contribute to time under occlusion and metabolic fatigue [PAGE 860; PAGE 865 Limitations]. (3) Peak velocity is monitored with an Enode Pro accelerometer that shows systematic overestimation of +0.129 m·s⁻¹ and mean absolute percentage error of 9.99% versus a criterion displacement sensor during deep squats at 30% 1RM, with between-session ICC = 0.954 [PAGE 860]. Because the threshold is defined relative to the session reference measured on the same device, within-session termination is partly self-calibrating, but absolute velocity values are not interchangeable with studies using mean concentric or mean propulsive velocity [PAGE 863 Discussion]. (4) Sánchez-Medina et al. (2017) note that peak velocity shows a weaker load-velocity association below 40% 1RM; therefore the prescribed 30% 1RM does not guarantee velocity-based intensity equivalence across protocols [PAGE 863]. (5) The study tests multiple secondary outcomes (CMJ, SJ, SBJ) and interprets them cautiously, raising false-discovery risk in the jump domain [PAGE 862]. (6) Absence of a statistically significant difference between VL40% and FAIL for primary strength outcomes (1RM mean difference 2.33 kg, 95% CI -2.58 to 7.25 kg, adjusted p = 1.000; IMTP -70.2 N, 95% CI -167.48 to 27.08 N, adjusted p = 0.442) does not establish equivalence or noninferiority [PAGE 861-862].

Derivation of pre-to-post change scores from Table 1 [PAGE 861]: full back squat 1RM changes are VL10% +17.00 kg (+13.39%), VL40% +25.54 kg (+20.40%), FAIL +23.20 kg (+18.06%). The outcome-specific jump pattern contradicts a unitary "general adaptation" model and implies velocity- or contraction-specific transfer rather than hypertrophy alone.

Breakdown regimes: the inference collapses if (i) cuff pressure error exceeds roughly ±15-20 mmHg relative to true 50% AOP, uncoupling metabolic stress from the nominal BFR layer; (ii) Enode Pro velocity error drifts beyond its stated MAPE across 8 weeks, invalidating the velocity-decline threshold; (iii) squat depth (thighs below horizontal) is not enforced, altering the mechanical stimulus [PAGE 860]; or (iv) calibration-repetition fatigue materially affects working-set performance despite exclusion from volume totals [PAGE 860, 865].

The session-reference peak velocity is \(v_{ref}^{(s)}\) in m·s⁻¹, measured as the maximum of 5 calibration repetitions at 30% of current 1RM with cuffs inflated [PAGE 860]. The termination operator for velocity-based groups is:
\[
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}: \quad \text{terminate at first } r \in \{1,\dots,R_{\max}\} \text{ such that } v_{\text{peak},r} \le (1-\delta)\,v_{ref}^{(s)},
\]
with \(\delta = 0.10\) for VL10% and \(\delta = 0.40\) for VL40%; the threshold-crossing repetition is included in the count [PAGE 860]. For FAIL:
\[
R^{(s)} = \max\{r : \text{concentric repetition completed with acceptable technique}\}.
\]

Working-set volume per session excludes the 5 calibration repetitions:
\[
V^{(s)} = \sum_{k=1}^{4} R_{s,k} \quad [\text{repetitions}],
\]
and the 16-session total is \(V^{(s)}_{16} = 16\,V^{(s)}\) [PAGE 863]. Observed means [PAGE 863 Table 4]:

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week structural traps.

The paper situates itself in a lineage beginning with Cormie et al. (2011) and Suchomel et al. (2016), who establish maximal strength as a foundational determinant of athletic performance across populations [PAGE 858]. The BFR literature (Centner et al., 2019; Patterson et al., 2019; Pearson & Hussain, 2015; Scott et al., 2015) motivates low-load training when heavy loading is undesirable or when stress must be managed alongside other work [PAGE 858]. The failure-versus-nonfailure controversy draws on Morán-Navarro et al. (2017), Pareja-Blanco et al. (2017), and Sieljacks et al. (2019), showing that failure-based sets increase repetition exposure, fatigue, and discomfort, while nonfailure BFR may yield comparable adaptations with less discomfort [PAGE 858-859; PAGE 863 Discussion]. The velocity-regulation lineage includes Pareja-Blanco et al. (2017; 2020) and Galiano et al. (2022), demonstrating that velocity-loss thresholds individualize repetition exposure and may preserve performance qualities [PAGE 859]. Measurement lineage: Sánchez-Medina et al. (2017) on load-velocity estimation in the back squat; Rodríguez-Rosell et al. (2020) on velocity loss versus repetitions in reserve [PAGE 859]. The closest comparator is Sánchez-Valdepeñas et al. (2026), who used 55-70% 1RM, 3 sets, set-internal mean propulsive velocity via linear transducer, session-by-session load adjustment, and 50% of directly measured arterial occlusion pressure, and reported an inverted-U pattern in which low-to-moderate velocity-decline thresholds produced favorable strength and jump adaptations [PAGE 863-864].

Multi-week traps embedded in the 8-week, twice-weekly design (16 sessions) [PAGE 859]: First, the relative load is held at 30% of current 1RM but the absolute load is updated after session 8 to 30% of the reassessed 1RM [PAGE 860]. This makes the mechanical tension operator non-stationary across the mesocycle; the same nominal relative intensity represents different absolute bar masses in weeks 1-4 versus weeks 5-8. Second, the sample consists of resistance-trained males with baseline squat 1RM near 125-128 kg [PAGE 861], a population with high training age and limited adaptive ceiling, raising the possibility that observed gains partly reflect familiarization and regression toward a trained mean rather than a pure intervention effect. Third, cumulative BFR exposure is substantial: 64 working sets per participant plus 80 calibration repetitions excluded from volume totals [PAGE 860, 863 Table 4], creating a fatigue layer that co-varies with velocity decline and RPE (12.44 versus 16.74 Borg units) [PAGE 863]. Fourth, the study ends at week 8, before detraining, accommodation, or joint-tissue adaptation to repeated low-load occlusion can fully manifest; therefore the results describe an acute-to-intermediate block, not a longitudinal steady state. Fifth, because repetition volume is produced by the termination strategy and co-varies with terminal velocity, best-to-terminal decline (9.48% versus 59.70%), and perceived exertion, the adaptation differences cannot be causally isolated to volume [PAGE 863, 865]. The historical lesson from Sánchez-Valdepeñas et al. (2026) is that changing load (55-70% versus 30% 1RM), set number (3 versus 4), velocity metric (mean propulsive versus peak), reference (set-internal versus pre-session calibration), and pressure determination (Doppler versus circumference) alters both the volume produced by a nominal threshold and its physiological meaning; therefore the present thresholds are protocol-specific tools, not universal prescriptions [PAGE 859, 863-864].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the 8-week clock.

Crisis habit regression: when a coach or athlete perceives stagnation or feels time pressure inside an 8-week block, the default regression is toward volitional failure (FAIL) because high session-averaged set RPE (16.74 Borg units) is phenomenologically equated with maximal effort and, by faulty transitivity, maximal adaptation [PAGE 863]. FAIL does produce the numerically largest pre-to-post changes in IMTP net peak force (+20.14%) and full back squat 1RM (+18.06%) [PAGE 861 Table 1], which reinforces the narrative that discomfort is necessary. The recursive trap is to ignore that VL40% achieves statistically indistinguishable primary strength outcomes (1RM difference 2.33 kg, 95% CI -2.58 to 7.25 kg, adjusted p = 1.000; IMTP difference -70.2 N, adjusted p = 0.442) while completing 21.1% fewer working-set repetitions per session and reporting lower RPE (15.53 versus 16.74) [PAGE 861-862; PAGE 863].

Counter-trap: the opposite overcorrection is to adopt VL10% universally to preserve freshness, accepting smaller strength-oriented adaptations (squat 1RM +13.39% versus +20.40% for VL40%; IMTP +9.19% versus +15.80%) in exchange for favorable jump outcomes (CMJ +9.69% and SBJ +10.23% versus FAIL, with VL10% > FAIL on both) [PAGE 861-862]. This is only rational if the athlete's immediate priority is jump-specific expression rather than maximal strength; the paper explicitly frames the results as trade-offs among repetition volumes and fatigue exposures, not a universally optimal threshold [PAGE 863 Conclusion]. Applying VL10% to a strength-critical mesocycle would be a category error under this evidence base.

Clock: the intervention operates on a fixed 8-week, twice-weekly clock with a midpoint load reassessment after session 8 [PAGE 859-860]. The clock constrains how much fatigue can accumulate and how much adaptation can consolidate; it also means that the "current 1RM" is a moving target, so the 30% prescription is re-anchored mid-block. If an athlete extends the FAIL habit beyond this window, the cumulative RPE and metabolic load under BFR likely exceed the tolerated envelope observed here (no adverse events reported, but RPE near the top of the Borg scale) [PAGE 859, 863]. Conversely, truncating the block before week 8 would forfeit the strength gains that appear to consolidate across the full 16 sessions. The meta-cognitive trap is to mistake the endpoint of this specific clock for a steady-state truth about velocity-based termination under BFR.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, environmental, and veto conditions.

Physical layer: All groups perform 4 sets of full back squats twice weekly for 8 weeks with 3-minute inter-set rest; descent continues until the top surface of the thighs is below the horizontal plane before concentric initiation with maximal intended velocity [PAGE 859-860]. BFR is applied bilaterally with 5-cm-wide pneumatic cuffs at the proximal thigh using a portable intelligent pressure device (Yidongkang ZNJY-01); pressure is selected by thigh circumference as 100 mmHg (<51.0 cm), 130 mmHg (51.0-55.9 cm), 150 mmHg (56.0-59.9 cm), or 180 mmHg (≥60.0 cm), corresponding to estimated 50% arterial occlusion pressure per Loenneke et al. (2015) [PAGE 859]. Staged familiarization at 50%, 75%, and 100% of assigned pressure precedes occlusion each session [PAGE 859].

Biomechanical layer: Peak bar velocity is monitored with an Enode Pro accelerometer magnetically attached to the barbell and calibrated before each session; device evidence includes between-session reliability ICC = 0.954 and agreement with a criterion displacement sensor ICC = 0.900, but with systematic overestimation of +0.129 m·s⁻¹ and MAPE 9.99% at 30% 1RM [PAGE 860]. Jump height for CMJ and SJ is assessed with a Myotest Pro2 accelerometer-based device, previously reported to have acceptable field validity and reliability (Casartelli et al., 2010; Choukou et al., 2014) [PAGE 860]. IMTP is performed on a force plate with the bar fixed at mid-thigh height (knee 120-145°, hip 140-150°); three maximal 5-second pulls with 1-minute rest; net peak force equals peak force minus body-weight force [PAGE 859].

Environmental/weather layer: Testing occurs indoors on a standardized track surface for SBJ (one practice trial, 3 maximal attempts, 2-minute rest) and in a controlled laboratory for force plate and jump assessments; the same equipment and standardized procedures are used at pre- and post-testing by staff blinded to group allocation [PAGE 859-860]. Participants abstain from vigorous exercise for 48 hours, alcohol for 12 hours, and caffeine for 6 hours before testing [PAGE 859]. Because testing is indoors and environmentally controlled, ambient weather is not a measured variable; however, the absence of weather variation does not remove equipment- or protocol-level vetoes.

Veto adjudication: The causal inference is vetoed if (1) cuff pressure does not correspond to true 50% AOP because Doppler was not used, making BFR pressure a latent confounder rather than a fixed stimulus [PAGE 859; PAGE 865 Limitations]; (2) Enode Pro velocity error drifts beyond MAPE 9.99% over the 8 weeks, so the velocity-decline thresholds no longer produce the intended repetition volumes [PAGE 860]; (3) squat depth below horizontal is not enforced, changing the mechanical stimulus and potentially altering jump transfer [PAGE 860]; (4) the 5 calibration repetitions per session contribute unmeasured occlusion time and fatigue but are excluded from working-set totals, corrupting the exposure-dose layer [PAGE 860; PAGE 865]. Additionally, because IMTP and SJ change scores violated homogeneity of variance and required Welch's ANOVA with Games-Howell post hoc tests [PAGE 862], any veto that increases residual heteroscedasticity (e.g., pressure mismatch correlated with thigh circumference) specifically threatens the IMTP and SJ inference layers.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1: Equivalence re-analysis of VL40% versus FAIL for full back squat 1RM. Re-analyze the pairwise contrast using two one-sided tests (TOST) with an equivalence margin of ±5% of the baseline mean (~±6.3 kg). Observed mean difference is +2.33 kg (VL40% minus FAIL) with 95% CI [-2.58, 7.25] kg and adjusted p = 1.000 [PAGE 861]. Prediction: equivalence will NOT be established because the CI upper bound (7.25 kg) exceeds the +6.3 kg bound. Verifiable outcome: compute TOST p-values; predict failure to reject the null of non-equivalence despite the non-significant superiority test.

Scenario 2: Correction of Enode Pro systematic bias. The device overestimates maximum velocity by +0.129 m·s⁻¹ versus a criterion sensor at 30% 1RM [PAGE 860]. If raw data are reprocessed against a linear transducer criterion, true session-reference velocities fall from ~2.00-2.03 m·s⁻¹ to ~1.87-1.90 m·s⁻¹ [PAGE 863 Table 4]. Because VL10% terminates at 90% of the (biased) reference, the corrected threshold velocity is proportionally lower (~1.71 m·s⁻¹ versus ~1.83 m·s⁻¹ uncorrected). Prediction: participants would reach the true velocity threshold earlier than the device signals, completing fewer than 55.91 repetitions per session and showing strength gains further below VL40%. Verifiable outcome: reprocess Enode Pro files against displacement-sensor data; predict a negative correlation between sensor bias magnitude and VL10% repetition count, and a downward shift in VL10% 1RM change.

Scenario 3: Cuff pressure mismatch relative to true AOP. Suppose true 50% AOP differs from the circumference-prescribed pressure by ±20 mmHg (e.g., prescribed 130 mmHg but true individual 50% AOP corresponds to 110 or 150 mmHg) [PAGE 859]. Prediction: participants with underestimated pressure (lower true occlusion than intended) experience attenuated BFR metabolic stress and blunted strength response; participants with overestimated pressure experience greater discomfort and potentially earlier voluntary termination despite identical velocity thresholds. Verifiable outcome: measure Doppler AOP in a subsample and regress individual 1RM and IMTP change residuals on thigh circumference and prescribed pressure; predict increased residual heteroscedasticity in IMTP net peak force and squat 1RM, consistent with the observed need for Welch/Games-Howell corrections on IMTP and SJ change scores [PAGE 862], and with the study's own limitation that applied pressure represents estimated rather than confirmed 50% AOP [PAGE 859; PAGE 865].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
