<THINK_TRACE>
This source is a computer-vision measurement paper, not a biomechanical dose paper. The multi-hop derivation begins with the paper’s target quantity: soccer head-impact exposure, in which headers are the dominant counted event: “soccer heading represent approximately 90% of head impacts in soccer” [PAGE 1]. The paper’s operational definition is narrower than “head impact”: headers are “any contact between a player’s head and the soccer ball” [PAGE 2]. That definitional step is the first gray-zone audit: the output is a header-event detector, not a general head-impact detector. The authors later state that “other types of impacts such as head to head, head to body, and head to ground will not be identified by the current network” [PAGE 9]. Therefore, any inference that DeepImpact measures all soccer head impacts, or all sports head impacts, is out of scope before any model-performance discussion begins.

The second hop is the pipeline. A video is segmented into short clips; the paper reports that a segment length of 11 frames yielded the best cross-validated result [PAGE 3]. Inside each segment, YOLOv5l detects the ball; a Kalman filter with a constant-speed state \([u,v,\dot u,\dot v]^T\) smooths detections; frames are cropped to 10 times the bounding-box size; cropped frames are resized to \((640,480)\); a temporal shift module with ResNet-50 maps an \((11,3,640,480)\) tensor to a \(1\times2048\) feature vector; two fully connected layers produce a \(1\times2\) probability vector [PAGE 4]. The derivation therefore has serial dependency: ball detection → tracking → cropping → temporal aggregation → classification. If ball detection fails, the paper says “uncropped frames were used by the algorithm” in failure cases [PAGE 7]. That is a gray-zone mechanism: the classifier is then fed a different input distribution from the cropped training distribution. The paper’s ablation confirms the effect: without temporal information and cropping, test precision is 0.4%; with temporal information but no cropping, test precision is 1.4%; with both, test precision is 21.1% [PAGE 8]. Thus precision is not a free-standing property of the network; it is an operator-stack property.

The third hop is metric interpretation. Cross-validation gives “95.3% sensitivity and 96.0% precision” [PAGE 1], but the independent full-game test gives “92.9% sensitivity and 21.1% precision” [PAGE 1]. The authors explain the drop through class prevalence: training and validation included “7 nonheaders for each header event,” while the full-game test had “an average of 110 nonheader segments for every header segment” [PAGE 8]. This is a prevalence-shift audit. Precision is prevalence-dependent even when sensitivity and specificity are fixed. A detector can appear excellent on a balanced validation set and collapse in natural prevalence because false positives multiply against a much larger negative class. The paper reports an average of “379 nonheaders identified as header events in each game” [PAGE 7]. The correct downstream interpretation is therefore not “21.1% accurate”; it is “high-sensitivity candidate generation followed by manual review.” The reported review burden is “3.5 min of total film time” per test game [PAGE 1], not zero-labor automation.

The fourth hop is the gray-zone audit of physical meaning. The paper explicitly says video data “cannot be used to obtain accurate impact magnitude information” [PAGE 9]. The network outputs binary header/nonheader probabilities, not acceleration, angular velocity, impulse, or injury risk. It also does not identify the player involved: “The current solution does not identify the player involved in each header” [PAGE 9]. So a count of headers is not an individual exposure dose, not a severity measure, and not a clinical risk estimate. The paper recommends combining video with wearable sensors: video can assess frequency, while sensors can measure magnitude [PAGE 9]. That is the correct cross-layer bridge.

The fifth hop is generalizability. The test videos were “selected from similar professional-level broadcast soccer games that were used in training the network” [PAGE 9]. Limitations include “64% of videos in the dataset had low 720p video quality,” constant broadcast camera movement causing blur, and single-view blindness to distant or invisible events [PAGE 9]. Training diversity included different stadiums and lighting conditions [PAGE 3], but that is training diversity, not a weather-stratified validation. No meteorological covariate, weather station, or weather-stratified test is reported. The gray-zone conclusion is that the source supports a narrow claim: in professional broadcast soccer, DeepImpact can generate a high-sensitivity header-candidate set that still requires manual verification and does not measure impact magnitude. Any stronger claim is vetoed by the source’s own definitions, metrics, and limitations.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Define the binary classification counts as integers: \(TP\) true positives, \(FP\) false positives, \(TN\) true negatives, and \(FN\) false negatives. The paper defines the operational metrics as [PAGE 5]:

\[
\mathrm{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FP+TN+FN}.
\]

All four are dimensionless proportions in \([0,1]\) under non-empty denominators. Assumptions: a binary ground-truth label per video segment and a thresholded classifier decision. Exact breakdown regimes: sensitivity is undefined if \(TP+FN=0\); precision is undefined if \(TP+FP=0\); specificity is undefined if \(TN+FP=0\); accuracy is undefined if the total count is zero. These are mathematical singularities, not empirical caveats.

For prevalence-shift audit, let \(\pi\) be the prevalence of positive events in the evaluated population. From the paper’s definitions, and assuming fixed sensitivity \(Se\) and specificity \(Sp\), the precision at prevalence \(\pi\) is exactly:

\[
\mathrm{Precision}(\pi)=
\frac{Se\,\pi}{Se\,\pi+(1-Sp)(1-\pi)}.
\]

This is dimensionless. Assumptions: binary labels, the same threshold and same \(Se,Sp\), and a population prevalence \(\pi\). Breakdown regime: as \(\pi\to0\), precision tends to zero whenever \(Sp<1\); if \(Sp=1\), precision is 1 for any \(\pi>0\). The source reports validation with “7 nonheaders for each header event” and full-game test with “an average of 110 nonheader segments for every header segment” [PAGE 8], which is precisely the regime in which the reported precision drops from 96.0% to 21.1% [PAGE 1].

Ball detection uses intersection over union and average precision. Let \(B_p\) be a predicted bounding box and \(B_g\) the ground-truth bounding box. The standard IoU operator is:

\[
\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=
\frac{\mathrm{area}(B_p\cap B_g)}
{\mathrm{area}(B_p\cup B_g)},
\]

dimensionless, with image-plane areas in pixels\(^2\). The source reports \(AP_{\mathrm{IoU}=0.50}=0.90\) and “mean AP was 0.40” over \(\mathrm{IoU}=0.50:0.05:0.95\) [PAGE 6]. Assumptions: bounding-box annotation and thresholded detections. Exact breakdown regimes are stated in the source: “ball was fully or partially occluded by a player,” “other objects in the background such as a body part were detected as a ball,” and “ball size was extremely small” [PAGE 6]. These are not small perturbations; they are failure modes of the pixel evidence itself.

The ball tracker is a Kalman filter with constant-speed model. The state vector is [PAGE 4]:

\[
\mathbf{x}_k=[u,v,\dot u,\dot v]^T,
\]

where \(u,v\) are bounding-box center coordinates in pixels and \(\dot u,\dot v\) are velocities in pixels/s. A standard constant-velocity transition is:

\[
\mathbf{x}_k=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&\Delta t&0\\
0&1&0&\Delta t\\
0&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}+\mathbf{w}_k,
\]

with process noise \(\mathbf{w}_k\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},Q)\). The measurement is:

\[
\mathbf{z}_k=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&0&0\\
0&1&0&0
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{x}_k+\mathbf{v}_k,
\]

with measurement noise \(\mathbf{v}_k\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},R)\). The source states: “The parameters of the measurement covariance matrix were set to 15 pixels for horizontal and vertical coordinates” and “Process noise covariance matrix was initialized with 20 pixels for location parameters and 5 pixels/s for speed parameters” [PAGE 4]. These parameters carry mixed units by coordinate block; the location block is pixels\(^2\) in covariance terms, and the speed block is (pixels/s)\(^2\). Assumptions: approximately constant ball speed between consecutive frames, Gaussian detection noise, and at least five detected ball frames. The source states: “KF was only applied to video segments that had at least five frames with a detected ball” [PAGE 4]. Exact breakdown regimes: fewer than five detected ball frames; a zero or degenerate first-frame bounding-box width; prolonged occlusion with no measurement; abrupt camera motion or ball direction change that violates constant speed; and false detections outside the gate. The gate rule is: if either coordinate residual exceeds \(d\), the innovation is set to zero and no correction is applied; “d was set to five times the width of the bounding box in the first frame of the video segment” [PAGE 4]. The source also reports that “Outlier rejection happened in 47% of the video segments” [PAGE 6], so gating is a common, not rare, operator.

Frame cropping is a scaling operator: crop size is “10 times the size of the bounding box,” followed by resize to \((640,480)\) using bicubic interpolation [PAGE 4]. The TSM input tensor is \((11,3,640,480)\): 11 frames, 3 color channels, 640 by 480 pixels. The network maps this tensor to a \(1\times2048\) feature vector and then to a \(1\times2\) probability vector [PAGE 4]. Assumptions: 11-frame segments are sufficient; the header moment is centered in header segments; resizing preserves relevant visual information. Breakdown regimes: ball not detected in most frames, so uncropped frames are used [PAGE 7]; crowded scenes, corner-kick headers, and very distant headers [PAGE 7]; inconsistent frame rates because the dataset used “25, 30, and 50 frames per second” [PAGE 4] and the authors note that “Having a consistent frame rate could improve the performance” [PAGE 9].

The exposure operator is a candidate count, not a dose:

\[
\hat N_{\mathrm{header}}
=
\sum_{s=1}^{S}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat p_s(\mathrm{header})>\tau\right],
\]

where \(\hat N_{\mathrm{header}}\) is a count of candidate header segments in a video, \(S\) is the number of segments, and \(\tau\) is the decision threshold. \(\hat N_{\mathrm{header}}\) has units of events. It is not in newtons, m/s\(^2\), rad/s, or joules. Assumptions: one header event corresponds to one positively classified segment; overlapping segments do not double count; manual verification follows. The source reports 546 header events and 60,018 nonheader events in five test games, with 507 true positives and 39 false negatives, and average 379 false positives per game [PAGE 7]. The review clock is therefore nonzero: candidate review is “approximately 3.5 minutes” per game [PAGE 8]. Exact singularity: if \(\tau\) is lowered to include many candidates, recall may rise but review burden and false positives rise; if \(\tau\) is raised, precision may rise but false negatives rise. The source’s own high-sensitivity/low-precision test regime is the operating point.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a reaction to two prior measurement regimes: wearable impact sensors and manual video review. The source summarizes sensor studies in Table 1, including studies with reported sensitivity and precision such as “85.9”/“16.3,” “91.4”/“6.1,” “69.2”/“80.3,” “50.6,” and “43.1,” with one study reporting precision “20.0” [PAGE 2]. The paper’s opening critique is that sensors “suffer from high deployment cost and limited accuracy,” while “manual video analysis is a long and resource-intensive task” [PAGE 1]. The historical move is therefore: thresholded IMU detection → video confirmation → independent video analysis → automated video candidate generation. DeepImpact inherits this lineage but shifts the bottleneck from sensor hardware to video annotation and verification.

The manual-review lineage is explicit: “fourteen trained raters reviewed 163 hours of video to fully verify 217 head impacts” [PAGE 2]. This is the previous cost structure. The paper’s proposed improvement is not that human review disappears; it is that review is compressed to candidate segments. The test result says header candidates correspond to “3.5 min of total film time” per game [PAGE 1], and the authors describe “a quick secondary review” to mitigate false positives [PAGE 8]. Thus the genealogy is not “human out, AI in.” It is “human review relocated from full-game search to candidate verification.” That distinction is a multi-week trap: a longitudinal study that budgets zero reviewer time will fail operationally even if the model’s sensitivity is high.

The computer-vision lineage is also layered: YOLOv5 for object detection [PAGE 3], Kalman filtering for visual tracking [PAGE 4], ResNet-50 and temporal shift module for video understanding [PAGE 4], and Kinetics pretraining for action recognition [PAGE 5]. The paper reports that TSM compared favorably with LSTM and temporal segment networks in early trials [PAGE 9]. This lineage creates a second multi-week trap: benchmark gains on curated clips do not automatically transfer to natural full-game prevalence. The paper itself demonstrates the gap: cross-validation precision is 96.0%, but full-game test precision is 21.1% [PAGE 1], explained by the shift from 7:1 nonheader-to-header in validation to 110:1 in full games [PAGE 8]. A multi-week study that trains on balanced clips and deploys on full matches without recalibration will systematically overstate precision.

A third trap is definitional drift. The historical sensor literature often counts “head impacts,” including player-to-player and head-to-ground events. DeepImpact counts headers only, defined as head-ball contact [PAGE 2], and the authors explicitly exclude “head to head, head to body, and head to ground” [PAGE 9]. If a multi-week study imports DeepImpact counts into a sensor-based “all head impacts” table, it commits a category error. The correct lineage is not replacement of all impact counting; it is a specialized, high-recall header-event screen.

A fourth trap is environmental drift. The training data included “different stadiums, lighting conditions, camera positions/angles, ball size/color/location, and scenes with a partially covered ball” [PAGE 3]. But the test was “selected from similar professional-level broadcast soccer games that were used in training the network,” and the authors call for “Further testing using different video sources” [PAGE 9]. Over multiple weeks or venues, camera placement, broadcast style, lighting, pitch color, and weather can shift the pixel distribution. The source does not report weather-stratified validation, so a multi-week deployment that assumes all-weather robustness is unsupported. The genealogical lesson is that the paper’s automation is conditional on a broadcast-video niche, not a universal sports-vision system.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive stakeholder model has four levels. First, the authors know the model is high-sensitivity but low-precision in full games; they report “92.9% sensitivity and 21.1% precision in an independent test” [PAGE 1] and explain the prevalence shift [PAGE 8]. Second, the video raters know the candidate set contains many false positives: an average of “379 nonheaders identified as header events in each game” [PAGE 7]. Third, the downstream exposure analyst may mistakenly treat the candidate count as a verified count unless the review step is explicitly modeled. Fourth, the player, coach, or clinician may receive a simplified message—“the algorithm tracks headers”—and infer completeness or severity that the source does not support. Recursive theory of mind must therefore separate what the authors believe, what the algorithm outputs, what the reviewer verifies, and what a non-specialist audience hears.

Crisis habit regression appears in two forms. One is regression to sensor thresholding: under resource pressure, a team may prefer a single acceleration threshold because it is simple, even though the source notes that threshold choice is “highly subjective” and introduces bias [PAGE 1]. The other is regression to “AI says header, therefore header”: a high-sensitivity result is treated as a complete count, ignoring the 21.1% test precision and the need for secondary review [PAGE 8]. In a crisis-oriented brain-injury research program, this regression is tempting because the paper’s headline cross-validation numbers are strong. But the correct mental model is not “95.3% sensitivity means 95.3% of the exposure problem is solved.” It is “95.3% sensitivity in cross-validation; 92.9% sensitivity and 21.1% precision in full games; manual verification remains necessary.”

The counter-trap is to preserve the high-sensitivity screening role while refusing to optimize only for precision. If one raises the classifier threshold to suppress false positives, the false-negative rate may rise, and the source already reports 39 missed headers across five test games, an average of 8 missing headers per game [PAGE 7]. The authors translate this to “approximately 0.4 missing headers per player per game” for 20 field players [PAGE 8]. The counter-trap is therefore a two-stage design: stage one is a high-recall candidate generator; stage two is human review; stage three, if magnitude is needed, is sensor measurement. The paper explicitly recommends combining video frequency with wearable magnitude [PAGE 9]. That is the robust recursive contract: no single layer is trusted to answer frequency, identity, magnitude, and severity simultaneously.

The clock is the operational constraint that disciplines the recursion. The paper’s review estimate is “3.5 min of total film time” per game [PAGE 1], not 3.5 minutes for the entire season. A multi-week study must budget reviewer hours per game and per player. The source also states that the current solution “does not identify the player involved in each header” [PAGE 9], so individual exposure estimates require additional video analysis. The recursive theory-of-mind failure mode is a clock error: a study planner assumes the algorithm produces per-player counts automatically, while the actual output is a per-game candidate set requiring human attribution. The counter-trap is to schedule review, track reviewer fatigue, and audit a sample of verified positives and negatives. The clock is not an implementation detail; it is part of the measurement’s validity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto proof. The source’s output space is a \(1\times2\) probability vector for header versus nonheader [PAGE 4]. It contains no inertial measurement, no acceleration, no angular velocity, no impact duration, and no mass. The authors state that video data “cannot be used to obtain accurate impact magnitude information” [PAGE 9]. Therefore any claim that DeepImpact measures impact magnitude, impact severity, cumulative dose in biomechanical units, or injury risk is physically vetoed. The veto is not a matter of model accuracy; it is a missing observable. A valid physical claim would require a calibrated map from video pixels to units such as m/s\(^2\) or rad/s, and the source explicitly denies that such accurate magnitude information is available from its video-only method.

Biomechanical-layer veto proof. The paper defines headers as “any contact between a player’s head and the soccer ball” [PAGE 2]. It also states that “other types of impacts such as head to head, head to body, and head to ground will not be identified by the current network” [PAGE 9]. Therefore any claim that DeepImpact is a general head-impact monitor is biomechanically vetoed by scope. A second biomechanical veto applies to individual exposure: the paper says the solution “does not identify the player involved in each header” [PAGE 9], so per-player exposure estimates require further video analysis. The valid biomechanical claim is narrow: the system detects a subset of head-ball contact events in broadcast soccer video and can support header-frequency screening, not a full biomechanical exposure profile.

Weather and visual-environment veto proof. The source reports training diversity across “different stadiums, lighting conditions, camera positions/angles, ball size/color/location, and scenes with a partially covered ball” [PAGE 3]. That is training-set variability, not weather-stratified validation. The test set was “selected from similar professional-level broadcast soccer games that were used in training the network,” and the authors state that “Further testing using different video sources would be necessary” [PAGE 9]. The paper further limits single-view broadcast video: “64% of videos in the dataset had low 720p video quality,” the camera angle “changes constantly to follow the ball, which can cause blurry ball and players,” and “one video angle provides limited information for distant events” [PAGE 9]. No meteorological variable, weather station, rain/fog/snow label, or weather-stratified performance table appears in the source. Therefore any claim of all-weather robustness, weather-invariant detection, or outdoor generalization is vetoed. The only supportable weather statement is negative: the source does not establish weather robustness.

Cross-layer adjudication rule. A claim survives only if it is stated as a header-event candidate count in professional broadcast soccer, with manual false-positive review, and without magnitude or severity inference. If the claim includes “all head impacts,” it is vetoed by the header-only definition and the excluded head-to-head/body/ground impacts [PAGE 9]. If it includes “impact magnitude” or “injury risk,” it is vetoed by the missing kinematic layer [PAGE 9]. If it includes “per-player exposure” without further review, it is vetoed by the absence of player identity [PAGE 9]. If it includes “all-weather” or “any camera,” it is vetoed by the single-view broadcast limitations and the absence of weather-stratified validation [PAGE 9]. The surviving claim is the paper’s own: a low-cost, video-based header-candidate generator that streamlines manual review and can help collect large-scale header-frequency data [PAGE 1].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Natural-prevalence precision veto. Verifiable check: evaluate the same trained model on a balanced clip validation set and on full-game natural prevalence. The source’s observed counterfactual is already reported: cross-validation precision is 96.0%, while independent full-game precision is 21.1% [PAGE 1], with validation using “7 nonheaders for each header event” and test using “an average of 110 nonheader segments for every header segment” [PAGE 8]. Prediction: if a report claims high precision in full games without prevalence correction or manual review, the claim will fail the full-game audit. Veto condition: claimed precision \(\geq 90\%\) at natural full-game prevalence while the reported operating point is near 21.1% and no reweighting, threshold recalibration, or verification step is specified.

2. Ball-detection and cropping ablation veto. Verifiable check: rerun the classifier with and without ball tracking/frame cropping on crowded, corner-kick, and distant headers. The source reports that unsuccessful detections were mostly “crowded events with multiple players in the header’s scene” and that “Headers that happened after a corner kick were the most common case of the failed detections” [PAGE 7]. The source’s ablation gives a direct prediction: without cropping, test precision is 1.4%; with cropping, test precision is 21.1% [PAGE 8]. Prediction: removing ball-position cropping will sharply reduce precision and will disproportionately fail corner-kick and distant headers. Veto condition: a claim that frame cropping is unnecessary, or that the model is robust to occlusion and small-ball scenes, is contradicted by the source’s ablation and failure analysis.

3. Kinematic-severity veto. Verifiable check: inspect the model output schema and require a magnitude target. The output is a \(1\times2\) probability vector [PAGE 4]; there is no acceleration, angular velocity, impulse, or severity head. The paper states that video “cannot be used to obtain accurate impact magnitude information” [PAGE 9]. Prediction: the same binary header label will be produced for low- and high-magnitude head-ball contacts whenever the visual features are similar; any severity ranking from DeepImpact alone will be unverifiable against sensor magnitude. Veto condition: any claim that DeepImpact estimates impact severity, cumulative biomechanical dose, or injury risk without a separately validated sensor or kinematic mapping is vetoed. The only allowed cross-layer prediction is the paper’s proposed combination: video for frequency and sensors for magnitude [PAGE 9].
