<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading requires three layers: the player’s habit under crisis, the opponent’s counter-trap, and the clock that changes both. Crisis habit regression means that under pressure a player reverts to the surface-specific policy that has historically worked. On grass, that policy is serve dominance: the paper finds ace-rate and double-fault-rate both at 0.07, the highest serve-related importance on any surface [PAGE 7]. A player in crisis may over-control the second serve to avoid double faults; the opponent can model that regression and attack the cautious second serve. On clay, the habit is rally tolerance and controlled aggression; winner-rate ranks third [PAGE 6], but the unforced-error partial dependence still shows that winning probability falls as errors rise [PAGE 8]. On hard, the habit is balance; unforced-error-rate ranks third [PAGE 6], and the steepest decline in win probability is on hard courts [PAGE 8]. A crisis regression on hard may therefore be passive consistency, which an opponent can counter by changing pace and court position.

Counter-trap reasoning is where the player models the opponent modeling the player. The ace-rate partial dependence is a signature: “When the ace-rate exceeds 0.05, both clay and hard courts show an increase in winning probability, while the grass court curve unexpectedly dips before beginning to rise again around 0.075” [PAGE 9]. A counter-trap explanation is that forcing aces beyond the natural grass pattern requires sacrificing first-serve percentage or groundstroke preparation; the opponent can read the pattern and extend the point. The source’s own interpretation is selection-based: “players who rely heavily on aces might lack other essential skills—such as groundstrokes—leading to lower overall performance” [PAGE 9]. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive. On clay, the ace-rate curve remains relatively stable beyond 0.15 [PAGE 9], which is consistent with the idea that clay aces are harder to produce and therefore may mark a more well-rounded player, as the paper suggests [PAGE 9].

The clock is the third layer. The model has no serve clock, point clock, match duration, or recovery-week variable. It aggregates point rows into match outcomes and then into surface-specific forests. That means the partial-dependence curves are averages over all points, not late-match or high-pressure states. A player’s crisis habit may change with fatigue: first-serve speed drops, winner-rate declines, and unforced errors rise. The opponent’s counter-trap also has a clock: it may be deployed early to test the habit, or late to exploit fatigue. Without temporal features, the RF cannot distinguish “surface effect” from “fatigue effect” from “score-pressure effect.” The paper’s discussion concedes that it focuses on outcome statistics rather than technical attributes [PAGE 10]. So the recursive theory-of-mind interpretation is a hypothesis generator, not a tested mechanism. It predicts that if point-level pressure and fatigue were added, the apparent surface thresholds in winner-rate and ace-rate would become conditional rather than universal.

The biomechanical layer vetoes direct prescription from MDI.
