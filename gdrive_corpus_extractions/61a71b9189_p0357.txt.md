$$t_{\pi(1)} < t_{\pi(2)} < \dots < t_{\pi(n)} \quad \text{and} \quad \exists k: \; t_{\pi(k+1)} - t_{\pi(k)} > \tau_{\text{bind}}.$$

Positive patterning contingency [PAGE 1]:
$$\mathcal{R}(S_c, r) = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \forall i: \; \mathcal{R}(s_i, r) = 0,$$
where $\mathcal{R} \in \{0,1\}$ denotes reinforcement delivery. This yields configural excitatory habit strength $V(S_c) > 0$ while $V(s_i) \approx 0$.

Negative patterning contingency [PAGE 1]:
$$\forall i: \; \mathcal{R}(s_i, r) = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \mathcal{R}(S_c, r) = 0,$$
producing extinction of the compound’s tendency and, per Pavlov’s conversion described by Hull [PAGE 1], inhibitory transformation of components: $V(s_i) < 0$ while $V(S_c)$ is maintained.

$$I(R; S_c) > \sum_{i=1}^{n} I(R; s_i),$$
with mutual information measured in bits. Equality indicates purely additive elemental control; strict inequality indicates genuine patterning.

The theoretical descendants include configural cue theory in animal learning, the Rescorla-Wagner model’s treatment of compound stimuli and excitatory/inhibitory associative strengths, and Pearce’s configural theory of conditioning, all of which inherit Hull’s assumption that a compound can acquire control greater than the sum of its elements. Another trap is the extrapolation from Pavlovian preparation to operant goal-directed behavior without re-deriving the response-reinforcement contingency for each new stimulus aggregate, which Hull’s own traffic-light example warns against by showing that identical physical stimuli (red light) evoke opposite behavior depending on the aggregate context [PAGE 1].
