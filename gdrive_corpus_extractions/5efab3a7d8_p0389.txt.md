The core of the analysis rests on formalizing the "piling-up effect" and its absence within an information-theoretic framework. Let \( A \) denote the action taken by the subject (e.g., drawing a line of length \( L \)). Let \( E \) be the absolute error, defined as \( E = |L - 4| \). Let \( F \) represent the feedback signal received after the action. In the described experiment, subjects act with "eyes closed" and are "not informed which are right and which are wrong," implying \( F \) is absent or independent of \( E \). Therefore, the mutual information \( I(A; F) = 0 \).

Learning can be modeled as a Bayesian update of the internal policy \( \pi \), which governs the probability distribution over actions given a context. The update rule is typically driven by a gradient related to the expected reward or negative error. Formally, the change in policy parameters \( \theta \) follows:
\[
\Delta \theta \propto \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{A \sim \pi_\theta} [R(A)]
\]
where \( R(A) = -E(A) \). However, if \( F \) is missing, the estimate of \( \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[R] \) becomes purely stochastic with respect to the environment's hidden state, effectively reducing to random walk noise rather than directed optimization.

The "piling-up effect" refers to the accumulation of errors due to frequent practice without correction. Mathematically, if the expected value of the error after \( n \) trials is \( \mu_n \) and the variance is \( \sigma_n^2 \), then under pure trial-and-error with no feedback, we expect:
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \mu_n = \mu_0 \quad \text{(bias persists)}
\]
\[
\lim_{n \to \infty} \sigma_n^2 = \sigma_0^2 + n \cdot \epsilon \quad \text{(variance grows linearly with noise } \epsilon \text{)}
\]
This indicates that while individual errors may fluctuate, the mean error does not converge to zero, and the spread of errors actually increases over time—a literal "piling up" of uncertainty. This breakdown regime occurs when \( I(A; F) = 0 \); the learning algorithm hits a singularity where gradient signals vanish entirely. As cited from [PAGE 389], the experiment demonstrates subjects making responses "but Are Not Informed Which Are Right and Which Are Wrong, and Show No Piling-up Effect from Frequency," confirming the theoretical prediction that repetition alone cannot reduce bias without a feedback channel.

Tolman. Thorndike famously argued that learning occurred through the strengthening of stimulus-response (S-R) bonds via "law of effect"—rewards strengthen connections, punishments weaken them. The experiment described, where subjects draw lines blindly, serves as a critical stress test for this doctrine. If mere repetition (the "law of exercise") were sufficient, even without reinforcement, some improvement should occur simply through neural wear-in or muscle habituation. Tolman's citation of this result highlights its failure: without knowledge of results, performance stagnates. This finding retrocausally shaped the rise of Cognitive Psychology by proving that internal representations ("cognitions" or "maps") are necessary to interpret stimuli and guide behavior purposefully. The multi-week trap here is the lingering assumption in some behavioral traditions that "practice makes perfect" is universally true. In reality, "practice makes permanent"; only deliberate practice with feedback refines skills.
