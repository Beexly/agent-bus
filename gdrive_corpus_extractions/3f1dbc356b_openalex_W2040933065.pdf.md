The paper’s inference chain is multi-hop, not a single correlation. First, it establishes a base-rate puzzle: left-handers are about 10%–13% of the general population but are “overrepresented in the expert domain of many interactive sports” [PAGE 1]. Second, it separates two older explanations: an innate neuropsychological superiority and a strategic/tactical advantage [PAGE 1–2]. Third, it inserts a perceptual mechanism: because right-handers are the majority, athletes see right-handed stroke patterns more often, so “the greater frequency of tennis matches with right-handed opponents makes it possible to discriminate the stroke movements of right-handed players more precisely” [PAGE 1]. Fourth, it operationalizes this as the negative perceptual frequency effect: “the sport-type-specific ability to perceive movement patterns carried out with the left hand is less well developed” [PAGE 3]. Fifth, it tests the mechanism with temporal occlusion, mirrored video clips, and radial prediction error in meters [PAGE 3]. The key result is that “all three groups were better at predicting the direction of strokes by right-handed players” [PAGE 1], and “in all three groups, the directions of strokes by left-handers were more difficult to recognize than those of right-handers” [PAGE 4]. The expert group did not erase the asymmetry; instead, “this difference was largest in the expert group (Mdiff  43.59 cm)” [PAGE 4]. The author interprets this as “simple summation effects” from “more intensive training with right-handers (ca. 80%–90% of training time over 19 years)” [PAGE 5]. The final inference is therefore probabilistic and partial: the data support one additional strategic-perceptual mechanism, not a sole cause.

The gray-zone audit matters because the paper moves from a population-level overrepresentation to a laboratory prediction asymmetry. That move has several breaks. First, the experiment does not measure match wins or selection; it measures a mouse-click estimate of ball landing. The abstract says “partly due to perceptual frequency effects” [PAGE 1], and the conclusion says the factor “may help to account” for overrepresentation [PAGE 7]. That is weaker than a full causal mediation claim. Second, the demonstration set is small: four players, with post hoc variation “by as much as 56 cm” across players [PAGE 6]. Thus the mirroring manipulation controls horizontal side but cannot fully separate left-handed kinematics from person-specific technique. Third, the response is not the sport response: “these laboratory tasks basically make demands only on the ventral stream (vision for perception) and not on the dorsal stream (vision for action)” [PAGE 5]. Fourth, handedness is partly sport-specific rather than purely manual: the source notes that “it is unclear whether participants are consistent left-handers who perform all manual activities with their left hand or use the left hand only for that particular sport” [PAGE 8]. Fifth, the expert difference is compatible with both training summation and selection; the paper favors summation but does not fully exclude selection. Sixth, the own-handledness result complicates a simple “same-hand advantage”: left-handers “might be able to discriminate the movements of right-handers better” because they more often face right-handers [PAGE 6]. So the mechanism is exposure-sensitive, not identity-sensitive. The correct multi-hop conclusion is: majority exposure shapes perceptual tuning; that tuning makes left-handed patterns less predictable; that unpredictability is one strategic channel into interactive-sport overrepresentation.
