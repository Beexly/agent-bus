**1. Measurable Functions and Convergence ([PAGE 1])**  
Let $(X, \mathcal{M}, \mu)$ be a measure space. A function $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is *measurable* if for every Borel set $B$, $f^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{M}$.  

- **Pointwise Convergence**: $f_n \to f$ pointwise if $\forall x \in X, \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x) = f(x)$. Assumptions: None beyond measurability. Breakdown: May fail to preserve integrability (e.g., $f_n = n \cdot \chi_{(0,1/n)}$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to 0 but $\int f_n = 1 \not\to 0$).  
- **Convergence in Measure**: $f_n \to f$ in measure if $\forall \epsilon > 0, \lim_{n\to\infty} \mu(\{x : |f_n(x) - f(x)| \geq \epsilon\}) = 0$. Units: Probability measure often used ($\mu(X)=1$). Assumptions: Requires finite measure for some properties (e.g., Egorov’s theorem). Breakdown: Does not imply pointwise convergence everywhere (only almost everywhere along a subsequence); sensitive to null sets.

**2. Integration ([PAGE 1])**  
For a non-negative measurable function $f$, the Lebesgue integral is defined via simple functions: $\int f d\mu = \sup \left\{ \int s d\mu : 0 \leq s \leq f, s \text{ simple} \right\}$, where a simple function is $s = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \chi_{E_i}$ with $E_i \in \mathcal{M}$. The integral extends to real-valued functions by splitting into positive and negative parts. Assumptions: $f$ measurable; for finiteness, require $\int |f| d\mu < \infty$ (i.e., $f$ integrable). Breakdown: The Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT) requires a dominating integrable function; without it, even pointwise convergence does not guarantee convergence of integrals.

**3. Signed Measures and Radon-Nikodym ([PAGE 1])**  
A *signed measure* $\nu: \mathcal{M} \to [-\infty, \infty]$ is countably additive and $\nu(\emptyset)=0$. The Hahn decomposition splits $X$ into positive and negative sets for $\nu$. The Jordan decomposition expresses $\nu = \nu^+ - \nu^-$, where $\nu^+, \nu^-$ are mutually singular positive measures.  

- **Absolute Continuity**: $\nu \ll \mu$ if $\mu(E)=0 \Rightarrow \nu(E)=0$.  
- **Radon-Nikodym Theorem**: If $\nu \ll \mu$ and $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite, then there exists a measurable function $f = \frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$ such that $\nu(E) = \int_E f d\mu$ for all $E \in \mathcal{M}$. Units: $f$ has units of $\nu/\mu$ (e.g., probability density per unit Lebesgue measure). Assumptions: $\mu$ $\sigma$-finite; $\nu$ finite or $\sigma$-finite. Breakdown: If $\mu$ is not $\sigma$-finite, the derivative may not exist; if $\nu \not\ll \mu$, the singular part cannot be represented as an integral.

**4. Product Measures and Fubini’s Theorem ([PAGE 1])**  
Given measure spaces $(X_i, \mathcal{M}_i, \mu_i)$ for $i=1,2$, there exists a unique product measure $\mu = \mu_1 \times \mu_2$ on $\mathcal{M}_1 \otimes \mathcal{M}_2$ such that $\mu(E_1 \times E_2) = \mu_1(E_1)\mu_2(E_2)$.  

- **Fubini’s Theorem**: If $f: X_1 \times X_2 \to \mathbb{R}$ is $\mu$-integrable, then:  
  (i) For almost all $x$, $f_x(y) = f(x,y)$ is $\mu_2$-integrable.  
  (ii) The function $x \mapsto \int f_x d\mu_2$ is $\mu_1$-integrable.  
  (iii) $\int_{X_1 \times X_2} f d\mu = \int_{X_1} \left( \int_{X_2} f(x,y) d\mu_2(y) \right) d\mu_1(x)$.  
Assumptions: $f$ measurable and integrable (or non-negative, for Tonelli’s version). Breakdown: Without integrability, iterated integrals may differ or not exist; requires careful handling of $\sigma$-finite measures.

**5. Probability Framework ([PAGE 1])**  
In a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$, random variables are measurable functions $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. Expectation is $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X dP$.  

- **Independence**: Events $A,B$ are independent if $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Random variables $X,Y$ are independent if for all Borel sets $B_1, B_2$, $P(X^{-1}(B_1) \cap Y^{-1}(B_2)) = P(X^{-1}(B_1)) P(Y^{-1}(B_2))$. This corresponds to the joint distribution being the product of marginals.  
Assumptions: $P$ is a probability measure. Breakdown: Independence is global; local dependencies may still exist; checking pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence for more than two variables.

Arnaud Denjoy and others explored convergence in mean (L^1), while Maurice Fréchet generalized metric spaces and introduced convergence in measure in the 1920s. Pointwise convergence remained problematic due to counterexamples like typewriter sequences; hence, convergence in measure became vital for completeness results (e.g., Riesz-Fischer theorem for L^p spaces).

Chapter VI on *general set functions* builds on work by Hugo Steinhaus and Stefan Banach on additive set functions. The Hahn decomposition (Section 29) originates from Hahn’s 1915 paper, while the Jordan decomposition follows naturally. The Radon-Nikodym theorem (Section 31) was conjectured by Johann Radon in 1913 and proved rigorously by Ottoto Nikodym in 1930 (with Karolina Nikodym). Its impact was profound: it showed that any σ-finite measure absolutely continuous with respect to another has a density, unifying concepts from analysis (differentiation of integrals) and probability (likelihood ratios). Before this, differentiation of measures was heuristic; after, it became a cornerstone of modern integration theory.

*Product spaces* (Chapter VII) have combinatorial origins but were elevated by Lebesgue’s need to integrate over multiple variables. The rigorous construction of product measures required transfinite induction and careful handling of σ-algebras—developed by Marston Morse and others in the 1930s. Fubini’s theorem (Section 36), named after Guido Fubini (1899), originally dealt with Riemann integrals; Lebesgue extended it to his setting, but full generality (Tonelli’s theorem for non-negative functions) came later. Chapter VIII on *transformations* reflects the influence of group theory and dynamics. Measurable transformations (Section 39) are studied in ergodic theory, pioneered by Georg Hamel and Constantin Carathéodory, and later expanded by John von Neumann (mean ergodic theorem, 1932). The isomorphism theorem (Section 41) relates to classification of measure-preserving transformations, a topic explored by Aleksandr Khinchin and others.

Before Kolmogorov, probability was largely combinatorial or geometric; he showed that events are sets, probabilities are measures, and random variables are measurable functions.
