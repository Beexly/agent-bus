Waking event at sleep-to-awake transition:
\[ t_w = \inf \{ t > t_{\text{onset}} : \hat{s}(t) = 0 \land \hat{s}(t^-) = 1 \} \]
unit: seconds (s). If no such transition exists within the observation window, t_w is undefined.

\[ e^* = \arg\max_{e_i \in \mathcal{E}} \{ t_i + \delta_i : t_i + \delta_i < t_w \} \]
Breakdown regime S_0: \mathcal{E}_{<t_w} = \emptyset (no SWS precedes waking) \Rightarrow e^* undefined \Rightarrow recovery score undefined.

Data quality metric: Q([a,b]) \in [0,1], dimensionless probability that the segment is artifact-free, e.g., Q = \Pr(\text{artifact-free PPG} \mid [a,b]). Estimated from contact, perfusion, and motion. No threshold \theta_Q is specified in the claims [PAGE 52].

\[ W^* = [u^*, u^* + T_w] = \arg\max_{[u,u+T_w] \subseteq e^*} Q([u,u+T_w]) \]

\[ \operatorname{RMSSD} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} (\Delta_{i+1} - \Delta_i)^2 } \]

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage: HRV as an autonomic index descends from the Task Force standards (Heart rate variability: standards of measurement, physiological interpretation and clinical use, Circulation, 1996), which define time-domain (SDNN, RMSSD) and frequency-domain (VLF, LF, HF) metrics in milliseconds. Slow-wave sleep (N3) is associated with parasympathetic dominance and reduced sympathetic tone, making the most recent SWS before awakening a physiologically privileged window for an overnight recovery score. Wearable PPG heart-rate monitoring and consumer “recovery scores” (e.g., Whoop strain/recovery, Firstbeat) operationalize this by condensing autonomic state into a single dimensionless number.

The patent’s specific retrocausal structure is notable: the wake event t_w, which occurs after the SWS epoch, is used to select “the slow wave sleep period occurring most recently before the waking event” [PAGE 52]. Thus a later observable causally gates which past data enter the recovery calculation. This differs from a purely prospective HRV average over the whole night; it is a conditionally selected retrospective statistic.

Multi-week traps: Claim 2 authorizes “calculating additional recovery scores after one or more other waking events of the user for comparison to the recovery score” [PAGE 52]. Across weeks this creates three traps. First, within-subject baseline drift: HRV and sleep architecture drift with training load, illness, age, and sensor contact, so comparing nightly \mathcal{R} values without a subject-specific rolling baseline conflates true recovery change with measurement non-stationarity. Second, regression-to-the-mean: a single low \mathcal{R} after a hard session is likely to be followed by a higher value regardless of intervention, which can be misattributed to a recovery protocol. Third, circaseptan confounds: weekday versus weekday sleep timing, alcohol, and travel produce weekly periodicity in T_{\text{sleep}} and Q that masquerades as recovery cycles if the comparison window is not phase-aligned. Claim 10’s “sleep need score estimating an amount of sleep needed by the user in a next night” [PAGE 52] compounds this by issuing a prospective estimate from limited nocturnal data, an overfitting risk when Q varies nightly with skin perfusion and contact.

Cross-layer veto predicate V:
\[ V = \mathbb{1}\{ C_{\text{loss}} > \theta_c \lor M_{\text{idx}} > \theta_m \lor (T_a < T_{\min} \land Q < \theta_Q) \lor f_s < f_{\text{Nyq}} \} \]

Weather-induced vasoconstriction is a physical veto, not a recovery signal: a low \mathcal{R} caused by cold artifact is a measurement failure, not necessarily low physiological recovery. Without V, cross-layer contamination yields a false recovery score: either inflated (motion-free sub-window cherry-pick) or deflated (vasoconstriction artifact read as low HRV).
