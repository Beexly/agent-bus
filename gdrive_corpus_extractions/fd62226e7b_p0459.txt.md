<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This theory emerges from late 19th-century physiological psychology, reacting against Cartesian dualism. Genealogical lineage traces back to Darwin's "The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals" (1872), which emphasized adaptive value of expressions. Lange's 1885 "Emotions: An Essay on the Physiological Foundations" provided vascular evidence. James independently developed similar ideas in his 1884 Mind article "What Is an Emotion?".

Multi-week traps include: (1) Reductionism trap: equating complex emotions with simple autonomic responses, ignoring higher cortical processes. (2) Circular reasoning: using emotional reports to validate physiological correlations, then using correlations to define emotions. (3) Historical determinism: assuming James' framing was objective rather than influenced by contemporary materialist trends.

Modern retrocausal analysis reveals that James anticipated predictive coding models: the brain generates predictions about bodily states, and mismatches generate emotional signals. This connects to Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis (1994) and Barrett's theory of constructed emotion (2017). However, James' original formulation lacked mechanistic detail on how perception triggers specific physiological patterns—this gap was filled by Cannon-Bard theory (1927) and later neuroimaging studies showing thalamic-cortical pathways.

The genealogy also includes a false dichotomy: mind vs body. Modern psychoneuroimmunology shows bidirectional coupling, undermining the strict unidirectional arrow in James' model. Yet his core insight—that emotion arises from sensing the body—remains empirically supported in studies of patients with spinal cord injuries or anesthesia.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
