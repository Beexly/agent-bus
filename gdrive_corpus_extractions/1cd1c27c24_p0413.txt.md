<THINK_TRACE>
The derivation begins with Tolman’s central thesis from *Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men*, specifically Page 413. Tolman argues that learning is not merely a reaction to the nature of material (the content) but is critically dependent on the "manner of presentation" (the formal, temporal, or structural conditions). He explicitly critiques "pre-gestalt" and "trial-and-error" doctrines, which historically overemphasized these formal laws without considering individual cognitive capacities. The core operator here is the **Sign-Gestalt Formation**. In conditioned reflex learning, this is described as "simple sign-gestalt formation," requiring "requisite capacities in the individual." This implies that the presentation must allow for a simultaneous or highly integrated perceptual field—a *coup d’oeil*—where the stimulus and its potential significance are grasped as a unified whole.

A gray-zone audit reveals that Tolman's critique of "pre-gestalt" studies suggests a major historical trap: the assumption that formal conditions (like timing or proximity) operate independently of the subject's cognitive map. If an experiment fails to account for the "requisite capacities" or the specific spatial-temporal setup, it may misattribute a failure of perception to a failure of conditioning. For example, if an ape cannot use a stick because it was presented out of immediate visual range of the food, pre-gestaltists might conclude the behavior wasn't learned, whereas Tolman would argue the "sign-gestalt" (stick-in-relation-to-food) was never formed. Thus, the "manner of presentation" acts as a veto: even if the subject has the capacity, if the presentation disrupts the holistic field required for the expansion/contraction of the perceptual map (as cited via Koffka), learning cannot occur. This aligns with the necessity of a "clock" mechanism—an internal pacing that must synchronize with external presentation to form a stable expectation. Without this synchronization, the system defaults to crisis habit regression, reverting to trial-and-error scrambling rather than purposive insight.

**Operator:** Sign-Gestalt Formation Probability ($P_{SG}$).
**Formal Equation:**
Let $C$ be the requisite cognitive capacity vector, and $M$ be the matrix of formal presentation conditions (temporal, spatial). The probability of forming a sign-gestalt upon a single presentation event $E$ is:
$$ P_{SG}(E | C, M) = \sigma \left( \frac{w_1 \cdot \text{similarity}(C, E) + w_2 \cdot \text{holism}(M)}{\lambda} \right) $$
Where $\text{holism}(M)$ measures the degree to which the presentation allows for a *coup d'œil* grasp (a high value when stick and food are co-visible), and $\lambda$ represents the noise or fragmentation of the perceptual field.
**Units:** Dimensionless probability (0 to 1); $w_i$ are weighting factors derived from species-specific perceptual constraints; $\lambda$ is measured in bits of missing relational information.
**Assumptions:** The subject possesses minimal baseline capacities ($C \neq 0$); the environment provides a consistent metric for spatial relations.
**Breakdown Regime:** As the spatial distance between instrumental object (stick) and goal (food) increases such that they fall outside the simultaneous focal field, $\text{holism}(M) \to 0$. At this singularity, $P_{SG} \approx 0$, and the system reverts to non-purposive trial-and-error dynamics.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of the "manner of presentation" extends back to Wundt’s voluntarism and early Gestalt psychology (Koffka, Köhler, Wertheimer), which rebelled against the elementalism of Watsonian behaviorism. Tolman synthesizes this by arguing that "pre-gestalt" normative psychology isolated formal variables (e.g., inter-stimulus intervals) but failed to see them as embedded within a teleological "expectancy" framework. Historically, the "trial-and-error" doctrine (Thorndike) served as a multi-week trap for subsequent researchers: once an animal exhibited random movements, the "law of effect" was deemed sufficient, masking the prerequisite "coup d'œil" that actually triggered the solution. By retro-causally analyzing Tolman's rejection of "pre-individual-difference" laws, we see he identifies a generational error: ignoring that the same presentation ($M$) yields vastly different outcomes based on the latent cognitive map ($C$) already constructed during prior, unrecorded exploratory sessions. The "conditioned reflex" tradition similarly trapped itself by viewing the conditioned stimulus as a mere substitute, rather than a sign pointing to a gestalted future state.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In a crisis scenario where the expected sign-gestalt fails to manifest (e.g., the stick is moved out of sight before the food is visible), the subject experiences a breakdown in its predictive clock. Instead of the smooth "expansion" of the perceptual field toward the goal, there is a jarring contraction. A recursive Theory of Mind analysis suggests the subject does not simply fail; it actively models the experimenter's or environment's "intention" to withhold the tool. If the presentation consistently violates the *coup d'œil* requirement, the organism regresses from "purposive" planning to "crisis habit"—stereotypic, high-frequency trial-and-error behaviors aimed at restoring the necessary spatial configuration. The "counter-trap" occurs when the subject anticipates the violation: having learned the "formal conditions" of failure, it may refuse to engage, displaying learned helplessness or a shift to an alternative gestalts (e.g., waiting for the stick to be brought to the food). The "clock" here is the internal rhythm of expectancy; if the external presentation desynchronizes from this clock, the behavioral output collapses into noise.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Tolman’s text implicitly establishes a physical/biomechanical veto over purely psychological learning laws. Even if an animal has fully "learned" the conceptual relationship between a rake and food, the **manner of presentation** can physically veto the execution. 
*Proof:* If the stick lies too far forward, the biomechanics of primate reaching (limited arm span, joint angles) combined with visual field constraints make a simultaneous grasp impossible. The "requisite capacities" include not just neural processing but the somatic ability to execute the *coup d'œil*. Furthermore, environmental weather/lighting acts as a veto: if illumination changes drastically, the "formal conditions of presentation" (contrast, depth cues) degrade, collapsing the gestalt even if the objects remain in the optimal position. The "pre-gestalt" laws were often vetoed in real-world applications because they ignored these hard physical limits, assuming that if the temporal interval was correct, learning was inevitable. Tolman rescues the theory by embedding the formal laws within the "requisite capacities" and the physical "set up" of the field.
