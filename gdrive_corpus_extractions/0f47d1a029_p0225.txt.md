The biometrician–Mendelian conflict (Weldon vs. Bateson, ca. 1900–1915) seemed to reject Galtonian continuous regression in favor of discrete Mendelian factors, but R. A. Fisher's 1918 paper reconciled them through the infinitesimal model: many additive loci of small effect, environmental variance \sigma_E^2, and the narrow-sense heritability h^2 = \sigma_A^2/(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_D^2+\sigma_E^2), exactly the quantity Galton's mid-parent regression estimates under his stability assumptions. Subsequent layers: Lush and Henderson's animal-breeding selection index and BLUP (mid-20th c.), and modern GWAS/LDSC SNP-heritability and polygenic-score regression, all of which are formal descendants of the table on this page.

Multi-week traps embedded in the genealogy. (a) Causal misreading: regression was never a physical "force" pulling offspring back; it is a conditioning artifact of h^2 < 1 combined with selection on M. Treating it as a causal mechanism across weekly observation windows is a category error. (b) Stationarity trap: Galton's equal-variance assumption fails across generations if nutrition, disease, or measurement changes (the 20th-century secular trend in height was ~1–2 cm/decade), so a regression fit on one week/generation does not project to the next without re-estimating \sigma_C/\sigma_M. (c) Winner's-curse trap: applying the mid-parent regression to parents selected for extremity (the upper tail of the "Above 72.5" class [PAGE 208]) systematically overpredicts offspring means because selection error in M is not transmitted.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. Treat the observer as a recursive model of the system being measured. Under crisis (selection on an extreme mid-parent value, or an extreme observation in a time series), the subject's subsequent behavior regresses toward baseline habit — the statistical analogue of Galton's offspring regressing toward mediocrity [PAGE 208]. The first-order trap: the observer attributes post-crisis improvement to a specific intervention ("the crisis habit was broken by X") and attributes post-success decline to complacency. Both are misattributions of Galtonian regression to the mean; neither improvement nor decline need be caused by the intervening event.

Counter-trap. Because regression is expected, a sophisticated observer may overcorrect: seeing a high mid-parent value, they predict near-average offspring and thereby underestimate the true additive component h^2 M. Equivalently, after a crisis low they expect automatic recovery and fail to invest in genuine corrective causes. The counter-trap is thus the mirror image: denying the regression that the model actually predicts.

Clock. Galton's regression operates on the parent-to-child (generational) clock, not on an individual's intra-life timescale. Applying it week-to-week within a single developing organism is invalid unless the trait is re-sampled from an independent population each week. For height, the relevant clock is set by epiphyseal closure (~16–18 years) and generational turnover; any "weekly" regression-to-the-mean claim for an individual's stature is vetoed by biology. The page's data (adult children versus mid-parents [PAGE 208]) respect this clock; a weekly re-interpretation does not.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. Adult height is bounded by skeletal physiology: epiphyseal plate closure ends longitudinal bone growth, and the species ceiling is ~2.5 m (pathological extremes near 2.72 m). A linear mid-parent regression \mu_{C|M}(m) = \mu_C + \beta_{CM}(m-\mu_M) is unbounded in m, so for mid-parent values in the extreme right tail ("Above 72.5" [PAGE 208] and beyond) the linear predictor eventually violates the physical maximum. Veto: linearity is valid only over the observed support of M; beyond it the response must saturate, \partial \mu_{C|M}/\partial m \to 0.

Biomechanical layer. Height is the outcome of growth-plate mechanotransduction, nutrition (protein, vitamin D, calcium), disease load, and sleep, partitioned as \sigma_P^2 = \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_D^2 + \sigma_E^2 (+G\times E). Galton's page implicitly assumes \sigma_E^2 is stable between parent and child generations. If the child generation experienced different nutrition or disease ecology, \sigma_C \neq \sigma_M and the slope \beta_{CM} = r_{CM}\sigma_C/\sigma_M no longer estimates h^2; the equal-variance assumption is vetoed by an environmental shift. The visible median run (66.3 → 70.0 in [PAGE 208]) is too compressed by OCR noise to confirm or deny a parallel upward shift from secular trends, which is exactly the gray zone.

Weather/climate layer. Season-of-birth effects on adult height are on the order of millimeters to at most ~1 cm, far below the inch-scale regression gradient on this page; weather is a noise term, not a first-order veto for Galton's table, though it contributes to \sigma_E^2.

Veto proof / adjudication order. (1) Biological bounds veto unbounded linear extrapolation at extreme m. (2) Variance-stationarity vetoes identification of \beta_{CM} with h^2 across generations unless \sigma_C^2 = \sigma_M^2 is demonstrated (not possible from this OCR layer alone). (3) The OCR page itself cannot veto physics, but any estimate claiming \beta_{CM} > 1, \beta_{CM} < 0, or non-monotone medians ordered by m is vetoed by the internal logic of the table: medians must be weakly increasing in mid-parent class; the apparent "67.9, 67·7, 67.9" dip [PAGE 208] is therefore flagged as OCR/sampling noise until the original is re-read.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
