<THINK_TRACE>
Derivation summary and gray-zone audit. The source's core chain is not a collection of isolated formulas; it is a layered construction. First, Boole defines a sign as “an arbitrary mark, having a fixed interpretation, and susceptible of combination with other signs in subjection to fixed laws dependent upon their mutual interpretation” [PAGE 26]. That definition forces every later algebraic move to be tied to a fixed interpretation. Second, literal symbols denote classes or class-selecting acts: “by the combination xy shall be represented that class of things to which the names or descriptions represented by x and y are simultaneously applicable” [PAGE 20]. From repetition of the same selection Boole obtains “xx = x,” then writes “x2 = x” [PAGE 30]. This is the idempotence law. It is not a convention about arbitrary algebra; it is the formal trace of repeated conceptual selection. Third, Boole assigns 0 and 1: “Hence the respective interpretations of the symbols 0 and 1 in the system of Logic are Nothing and Universe” [PAGE 34]. Fourth, complementation gives \(x(1-x)=0\); Boole reads this as “it is impossible for the same individual to be at the same time a man and not a man” [PAGE 43]. This is the law of duality or non-contradiction. Fifth, development follows by evaluating a Boolean function at the two admissible values of a class symbol: “f (x) = f (1) x + f (0) (1 −x)” [PAGE 52]. For several symbols the same procedure yields a sum of constituents, each constituent multiplied by the value of the function when the constituent's factors are set to 1 or 0 [PAGE 54]. Constituents satisfy “t (1 −t) = 0,” distinct constituents multiply to 0, and their sum is 1 [PAGE 57]. Sixth, interpreting an equation \(V=0\) means developing it and equating to 0 every constituent whose coefficient does not vanish [PAGE 61]. Seventh, elimination is the product of boundary evaluations: “If f(x) = 0 be any logical equation involving the class symbol x, with or without other class symbols, then will the equation f(1)f(0) = 0 be true, independently of the interpretation of x; and it will be the complete result of the elimination of x from the above equation” [PAGE 75]. Boole also gives the philosophical reading: “what is equally true, whether a given class of objects embraces the whole universe or disappears from existence, is independent of that class altogether” [PAGE 85]. Eighth, systems are reduced to a single equation by addition when coefficients are positive, or by squaring first; a function already satisfying “V (1 −V ) = 0” needs no squaring [PAGE 93]. Ninth, secondary propositions are handled by the same formal laws but reinterpreted over time: \(x\) denotes the time for which proposition X is true, and “1 represents the universe, or whole of time” [PAGE 130]; the fundamental law remains \(x(1-x)=0\), now expressing “A proposition cannot at the same time be true and false” [PAGE 142]. The gray-zone audit has six main failure points. (i) The idempotence law is a law of class symbols or truth indicators, not of probabilities: a probability \(p\in(0,1)\) does not satisfy \(p(1-p)=0\). (ii) Boole's \(+\) is disjoint aggregation unless explicitly repaired; inclusive “either” requires a form such as \(x+y(1-x)\). (iii) Division by a class symbol is not generally valid, because that symbol may take value 0; Boole explicitly denies a formal equivalent to algebraic division [PAGE 32-33]. (iv) The coefficient \(0/0\) is an indefinite class symbol, not an arithmetic undefined quantity [PAGE 68]. (v) A coefficient \(a\) that fails \(a(1-a)=0\) forces its constituent to 0 under class interpretation [PAGE 74-75]. (vi) Secondary propositions require a time-indexed interpretation; otherwise “if” and “sometimes” are silently conflated. These are not cosmetic caveats. They separate formal theorem from interpretation and prevent the source from being overread as ordinary algebra, as probability calculus, or as a universal decision procedure.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal core. Let \(U\) be a universe of discourse and let \(\mathcal{B}\subseteq \mathcal{P}(U)\) be a Boolean algebra of classes. Boole's literal symbols \(x,y,z\) denote elements of \(\mathcal{B}\). The intended operations are:
\[
I(xy)=I(x)\cap I(y),\qquad I(1-x)=U\setminus I(x),
\]
with \(I(0)=\varnothing\), \(I(1)=U\). The symbol \(+\) is disjoint union: \(I(x+y)=I(x)\sqcup I(y)\) only when \(I(x)\cap I(y)=\varnothing\). For inclusive “either x or y,” Boole uses \(x+y(1-x)\) or equivalently \(x+y-xy\). The basic laws are:
\[
xy=yx,\qquad x+y=y+x,\qquad x(y+z)=xy+xz,
\]
\[
x^2=x \quad\text{or}\quad x(1-x)=0.
\]
Boole states “xx = x” and “x2 = x” [PAGE 30]; he states the duality law \(x(1-x)=0\) and interprets it as the impossibility of possessing and not possessing a quality at once [PAGE 43]. Units: logical truth values are dimensionless indicators in \(\{0,1\}\); class operations are set operations with cardinality or measure units inherited from \(U\). If \(P\) is a probability, then \(P\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless, and the information content \(-\log_2 P\) is in bits. The law \(x^2=x\) applies to event indicators \(1_A\), not to probabilities \(P(A)\).

Development theorem:
\[
f(x)=f(1)x+f(0)(1-x),
\]
[PAGE 52]. For two symbols,
\[
f(x,y)=f(1,1)xy+f(1,0)x(1-y)+f(0,1)(1-x)y+f(0,0)(1-x)(1-y),
\]
[PAGE 54]. A constituent \(t\) satisfies
\[
t(1-t)=0,\qquad t_i t_j=0\ (i\ne j),\qquad \sum_i t_i=1,
\]
[PAGE 57]. Interpretation rule for \(V=0\): “Develop the function V , and equate to 0 every constituent whose coefficient does not vanish” [PAGE 61]. Coefficient rules: coefficient 1 means the whole constituent class is taken; 0 means none; \(0/0\) means “some, none, or all” [PAGE 68]; \(1/0\) or any coefficient not satisfying \(a(1-a)=0\) forces the constituent to 0 [PAGE 74-75].

Elimination theorem:
\[
f(x)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ f(1)f(0)=0,
\]
and this is “the complete result of the elimination of x” [PAGE 75]. General form: expand \(V\) in constituents of the symbols to be eliminated and equate the product of their coefficients to 0. Reduction: any equation may be put in the form \(V=0\) with
\[
V(1-V)=0,
\]
and if a first member already satisfies this, it “does not need ... the process of squaring” [PAGE 93]. Systems with positive coefficients may be added; otherwise square and add.

Secondary propositions. Let \(T\) be a time universe. For a proposition \(X\), let \(T_X\subseteq T\) be the set of times at which \(X\) is true. Interpret \(x=T_X\), \(1=T\), \(0=\varnothing\). Then \(x=1\) says \(X\) is true throughout the discourse-time; \(x=0\) says \(X\) is false throughout. The conditional “If Y is true, X is true” is expressed by \(y=vx\), where \(v\) is an indefinite time symbol, i.e. \(T_Y\subseteq T_X\). Boole says “1 represents the universe, or whole of time” [PAGE 130], and the same duality law \(x(1-x)=0\) now expresses “A proposition cannot at the same time be true and false” [PAGE 142]. If \(\mu\) is a time measure, then \(\mu(T_X)\in[0,\mu(T)]\), with units of seconds or other duration units. Boole's probability definition is a case-ratio: numerator favorable cases, denominator total cases, all cases “equally likely” [PAGE 17]. That probability is dimensionless; do not substitute \(P(A)\) into \(x^2=x\).

Assumptions and breakdown regimes. Assumption A1: fixed interpretation within a discourse [PAGE 26]. Assumption A2: literal symbols are class or truth indicators, not arbitrary real numbers. Assumption A3: final expressions must be interpretable in the same system [PAGE 49]. Assumption A4: aggregation by \(+\) is disjoint unless explicitly corrected. Breakdown regimes: (B1) applying \(x^2=x\) to probabilities \(p\in(0,1)\), where \(p(1-p)\ne0\); (B2) dividing by a class symbol \(z\) when \(z=0\), since \(zx=zy\) does not imply \(x=y\) [PAGE 32-33]; (B3) treating \(0/0\) as arithmetic undefined rather than an indefinite class symbol [PAGE 68]; (B4) allowing a coefficient \(a\notin\{0,1\}\) without forcing its constituent to 0 under class interpretation [PAGE 74-75]; (B5) using inclusive “or” as bare \(x+y\), which is not generally interpretable unless the classes are disjoint; (B6) using secondary propositions without time limits or without distinguishing primary class relations from truth-over-time relations [PAGE 129-131].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage. Boole's 1854 project is retroactively legible as the immediate ancestor of Boolean algebra, switching theory, digital circuit design, SAT/SMT solving, relational query logic, and parts of probability and information theory. The lineage is not a straight line of identical meanings. Aristotle supplies the principle of contradiction and the syllogistic tradition; Boole formalizes the contradiction law as \(x(1-x)=0\) [PAGE 43] and later treats syllogism as “a species of elimination” [PAGE 186]. De Morgan, Jevons, Peirce, and Schröder extend and reinterpret the algebra. Shannon's later switching interpretation reads \(x^2=x\) as idempotence of circuit variables, but that is a later semantic layer, not a license to erase Boole's class interpretation. Modern probability theory inherits Boole's event algebra, but it separates event indicators from probabilities: \(1_A^2=1_A\) is Boolean, while \(P(A)^2=P(A)\) is false except for \(P(A)\in\{0,1\}\). Modern information theory adds \(-\log_2 P\) in bits, again a measure on events, not a class symbol.

Multi-week trap set. Week-1 trap: treating \(x^2=x\) as a strange algebraic equation whose only roots are 0 and 1, then forgetting that the law is interpretational, not a restriction on all quantities. Week-2 trap: treating Boole's \(+\) as ordinary inclusive OR. This produces false translations: “either x or y” may require \(x(1-y)+y(1-x)\) when exclusive, or \(x+y(1-x)\) when inclusive [PAGE 40-41]. Week-3 trap: cancelling a common class symbol from an equation. Boole explicitly denies the formal equivalent of algebraic division because the symbol may be 0 [PAGE 32-33]; a later worker who divides away a factor silently assumes a non-zero class, which is an extra premise. Week-4 trap: treating \(0/0\) as a numerical singularity rather than “some, none, or all” [PAGE 68]. Week-5 trap: overinterpreting coefficients. In Boole's system, \(1\), \(0\), \(0/0\), and \(1/0\) have distinct interpretive roles; a coefficient such as \(2\) is not an ordinary multiplier of a class but a signal that the constituent must be set to 0 under class interpretation [PAGE 74-75]. Week-6 trap: confusing primary and secondary propositions. The same equations serve both, but the interpretation shifts from classes to times of truth [PAGE 129-131]. Week-7 trap: probabilistic overreach. Boole himself defines probability as a fraction of cases [PAGE 17], but the formal law \(x^2=x\) governs events-as-indicators, not probabilities. A probabilistic model that imports \(p^2=p\) into \(p\in(0,1)\) is not Boole's system; it is a different and mostly trivial system. These traps are multi-week because each one can appear locally plausible while corrupting a chain of derivations at the interpretation boundary.

The retrocausal lesson is therefore disciplined: later algebra can clarify Boole's formal skeleton, but it cannot retroactively erase the source's interpretation rules. The invariant that survives is the Boolean algebra of classes and truth indicators; the contingent layer is the particular vocabulary, examples, and time-indexed reading.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. In a crisis, a reasoner often regresses to ordinary arithmetic habits: cancel common symbols, treat “or” as inclusive addition, and treat a class symbol as a number. Boole's text blocks that regression at each point. Common-symbol cancellation is blocked because division by a class symbol is not generally valid [PAGE 32-33]. Inclusive and exclusive “or” are separated by explicit symbolic forms [PAGE 40-41]. The idempotence law \(x^2=x\) [PAGE 30] is the signature that the reasoner is still inside class algebra rather than ordinary algebra. The counter-trap is the opposite failure: refusing all symbolic manipulation because one has seen symbol misuse, then falling back on verbal reasoning that cannot expose hidden middle terms. Boole's method is designed to avoid both: manipulate formally, but restore interpretation only at the end [PAGE 49]. The clock is the time-index repair. For secondary propositions, \(x\) is not a timeless class but the time for which \(X\) is true [PAGE 129-130]. “If Y is true, X is true” becomes \(y=vx\), i.e. the time of Y is contained in the time of X. If an agent models another agent's assertion without a clock, it may mistake a conditional for an equality or for a material implication. With a clock, the test is concrete: is there a time \(t\) with \(Y\) true and \(X\) false? If yes, the conditional fails. The recursive part is that an agent A must represent whether agent B is using primary class algebra or secondary time algebra. If A assumes B is using classes when B is using time-indexed propositions, A will overinterpret “if” as identity. If A assumes B is using time when B is using classes, A will miss the containment relation. Boole's same formal laws in both domains [PAGE 129] make the confusion tempting; the interpretation rule is what disambiguates. Crisis habit regression is therefore not merely “bad algebra”; it is a failure to track which interpretation layer is active. The counter-trap is not merely “distrust symbols”; it is a failure to use the source's own interpretability conditions. The clock is not an extra metaphor; it is the formal device that makes secondary propositions testable over time.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof. Let \(s(t)\) be a physical state vector at time \(t\), for example a weather field or a biomechanical state. For each atomic proposition \(X_i\), define the truth indicator
\[
x_i(t)=1_{\{s(t)\in A_i\}}\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Then for every \(t\),
\[
x_i(t)(1-x_i(t))=0,
\]
and every Boolean identity derived from \(x(1-x)=0\) holds pointwise. Therefore weather or biomechanics cannot veto the algebraic identity itself. They can only veto a premise by making \(s(t)\notin A_i\) while the premise asserts \(x_i(t)=1\). Example: “it rains” is a simple event and “it rains and thunders” is a compound event [PAGE 18]. The weather determines whether \(x_{\text{rain}}(t)=1\); it does not determine whether \(x(1-x)=0\). Likewise, a biomechanical threshold claim \(x=1_{\{F(t)\ge F_0\}}\) satisfies the Boolean law at each \(t\), even though the underlying force \(F(t)\) is continuous, measured in newtons, and may vary continuously. The threshold map is the interpretation layer; the Boolean law is above it. Secondary propositions add a clock: \(x\) denotes the time set \(T_X\), and \(T_X\subseteq T\) [PAGE 129-130]. Weather can change \(T_X\); it cannot change the set-theoretic identity \(T_X\cap(T\setminus T_X)=\varnothing\). Thus the veto hierarchy is: (1) algebraic identities are invariant under any valuation into a Boolean algebra; (2) premise truth is contingent on the physical or biomechanical state; (3) numerical coefficients are interpretive signals, not physical measurements. A cross-layer veto is legitimate only when it changes the interpretation from truth indicators to something else, for example when a measured frequency \(p\in(0,1)\) is inserted as if it were a class symbol. In that case \(p(1-p)\ne0\), and the class algebra has been abandoned. If the task is probabilistic, the correct layer is probability measure on events, with \(P(A)\in[0,1]\) and information \(-\log_2 P(A)\) in bits. If the task remains Boole's class or truth-indicator algebra, weather and biomechanics can only supply or refute premises; they cannot overturn the formal identities. This is the proof: for all physical states \(s(t)\), the indicator map is a Boolean valuation, so every derivable identity is satisfied; any apparent veto is either a false premise or a category error at the interpretation boundary.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable-reward counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario mutates one source operator or interpretation rule and predicts a detectable failure. No betting lines are involved.

Scenario 1: Remove idempotence and allow a class symbol to take value \(1/2\). Source law: “x2 = x” [PAGE 30]. Counterfactual: evaluate the development theorem at \(x=1/2\) for \(f(x)=x^2\). In the mutated system, the left side is \(1/4\). The Boole development formula gives \(f(1)(1/2)+f(0)(1/2)=1\cdot(1/2)+0=1/2\). Prediction: the verifier will detect a mismatch \(1/4\ne1/2\). If the verifier only tests \(x\in\{0,1\}\), it will not detect the mutation, because \(0^2=0\) and \(1^2=1\). Verification protocol: sample \(x=1/2\) and require exact rational equality. Expected result under Boole: mismatch is impossible; under mutation: mismatch occurs. Veto: the mutation is rejected.

Scenario 2: Permit cancellation \(zx=zy\Rightarrow x=y\). Source boundary: Boole denies the formal equivalent of algebraic division because the symbol may become 0 [PAGE 32-33]. Countermodel: set \(z=0\), \(x=1\), \(y=0\) in a two-element Boolean algebra. Then \(zx=0\cdot1=0\) and \(zy=0\cdot0=0\), so the antecedent \(zx=zy\) holds, but \(x=1\ne y=0\). Prediction: the verifier finds a countermodel and the cancellation rule fails. Verification protocol: enumerate all \(z,x,y\in\{0,1\}\), check \(zx=zy\), and check whether \(x=y\). Expected result under Boole: cancellation is invalid; under the mutated rule: the countermodel is a false positive. Veto: the mutation is rejected.

Scenario 3: Replace two-branch elimination with one-branch evaluation. Source theorem: \(f(1)f(0)=0\) is the complete result of eliminating \(x\) [PAGE 75]. Counterfactual: use only \(f(1)=0\) or only \(f(0)=0\). Take the standard indefinite-premise encoding \(f(v)=y-vx\), with \(y\) meaning “men,” \(x\) meaning “mortal,” and \(v\) an indefinite class symbol. The correct elimination gives \(y(1-x)=0\), i.e. no non-mortal men. If one uses only \(v=1\), one obtains \(y-x=0\), which is too strong: it rejects the valuation \(y=0,x=1\), even though \(y(1-x)=0\) is satisfied there. If one uses only \(v=0\), one obtains \(y=0\), also too strong: it rejects \(y=1,x=1\), which satisfies \(y(1-x)=0\). Prediction: the verifier will find that the one-branch rule excludes admissible valuations that the product rule admits. Verification protocol: enumerate \(x,y\in\{0,1\}\), compute the correct eliminated condition \(y(1-x)=0\), and compare against the one-branch conditions. Expected result under Boole: only the product rule is equivalent; under either one-branch mutation: false exclusions appear. Veto: both one-branch mutations are rejected. These three tests are verifiable because they are finite, exact, and reproducible on a Boolean valuation table.
