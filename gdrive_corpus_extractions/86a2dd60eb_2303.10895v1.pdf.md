<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the stochastic trajectory-prediction problem with past ego trajectory \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{T_p\times2}\), neighbor trajectories \(\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times T_p\times2}\), and future \(Y\in\mathbb{R}^{T_f\times2}\). The objective is
\[
\theta^*=\min_\theta\min_{\widehat{Y}_i\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}}D(\widehat{Y}_i,Y),\quad \text{s.t.}\quad \widehat{\mathcal{Y}}\sim\mathcal{P}_\theta,
\]
where \(D\) is a distance in meters when it is a Euclidean norm over 2D coordinates, and \(\mathcal{P}_\theta\) is a probability density over \(\mathbb{R}^{T_f\times2}\) with units \(m^{-2T_f}\) if coordinates are in meters [PAGE 3, Eq. (1)]. The paper’s own motivation is that “stochastic trajectory prediction requires a sophisticated multi-modal distribution of future trajectories” [PAGE 1].

The standard diffusion operator is
\[
Y^0=Y,\quad Y^\gamma=f_{\rm diffuse}(Y^{\gamma-1}),\quad \gamma=1,\dots,\Gamma,
\]
\[
\widehat{Y}^\Gamma_k\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),\quad \widehat{Y}^\gamma_k=f_{\rm denoise}(\widehat{Y}^{\gamma+1}_k,X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}}),\quad \gamma=\Gamma-1,\dots,0,
\]
with \(\Gamma\) a dimensionless integer step count [PAGE 3, Eq. (2)]. The noise variable is assumed dimensionless after normalization; \(\alpha_\gamma\) and \(\bar{\alpha}_\gamma\) are dimensionless diffusion schedule parameters. The denoising update is
\[
\widehat{Y}^\gamma_k=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_\gamma}}\left(\widehat{Y}^{\gamma+1}_k-\frac{1-\alpha_\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_\gamma}}\epsilon^\gamma_\theta\right)+\sqrt{1-\alpha_\gamma}z,
\]
where \(\epsilon^\gamma_\theta\) is the estimated noise and \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) [PAGE 5, Eq. (6)]. Breakdown regimes: \(\alpha_\gamma\to0\) makes \(1/\sqrt{\alpha_\gamma}\) singular; \(\bar{\alpha}_\gamma\to1\) makes the coefficient denominator \( \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_\gamma}\to0\); exact equality \(\alpha_\gamma=1,\bar{\alpha}_\gamma=1\) is a \(0/0\) schedule endpoint if not handled by the chosen schedule.

The LED leapfrog operator replaces the normal initialization by
\[
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}^\tau\sim^K\mathcal{P}(\widehat{Y}^\tau)=f_{\rm LSG}(X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}}),
\]
\[
\widehat{Y}^\gamma_k=f_{\rm denoise}(\widehat{Y}^{\gamma+1}_k,X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}}),\quad \gamma=\tau-1,\dots,0,
\]
where \(\tau\) is a dimensionless integer and is assumed \(\tau\ll\Gamma\) [PAGE 3, Eq. (3)]. The source states that “a large number of small denoising steps can be replaced by a single, yet powerful leapfrog initializer” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: \(\tau\to0\) makes the initializer approximate the full data distribution \(\mathcal{P}(Y^0)\), which the paper says causes worse prediction performance; \(\tau\to\Gamma\) means the initializer is approximating an almost-normal distribution, so the leapfrog advantage shrinks and inference time is wasted [PAGE 7].

The reparameterization is
\[
\mu_\theta=f_\mu(X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}})\in\mathbb{R}^{T_f\times2},\quad
\sigma_\theta=f_\sigma(X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}})\in\mathbb{R},
\]
\[
\widehat{S}_\theta=[\widehat{S}_{\theta,1},\dots,\widehat{S}_{\theta,K}]=f_{\widehat{S}}(X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}},\sigma_\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{T_f\times2\times K},
\]
\[
\widehat{Y}^\tau_k=\mu_\theta+\sigma_\theta\cdot\widehat{S}_{\theta,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_f\times2}.
\]
Here \(\mu_\theta\) is in meters; \(\sigma_\theta\) has meter units only if \(\widehat{S}_{\theta,k}\) is dimensionless. The paper does not explicitly state that normalization, so dimensional consistency is an assumption. The paper says this “allows us to avoid learning a raw sophisticated distribution, making the training much easier” [PAGE 4, Eq. (4)]. Singularity regimes: \(\sigma_\theta\to0\) is singular in the inverse-variance loss term; \(\sigma_\theta\to\infty\) is suppressed by \(\log\sigma_\theta^2\), but the paper does not give clip bounds. If \(\widehat{S}_{\theta,k}\) is not normalized, the interpretation of \(\sigma_\theta\) as a standard deviation is not identifiable.

The encoder operator is
\[
e^{\rm social}_{\mu_\theta}=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{f_q(X)f_k(\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}})^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right)f_v(\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}}),
\]
\[
e^{\rm temp}_{\mu_\theta}=f_{\rm GRU}(f_{\rm conv1D}(X)),\quad
\mu_\theta=f_{\rm fusion}([e^{\rm social}_{\mu_\theta}:e^{\rm temp}_{\mu_\theta}]),
\]
where \(d\) is the dimensionless embedding dimension [PAGE 4, Eq. (5)]. Breakdown regime: if the neighbor set is empty, \(L=0\), the attention softmax over an empty key set is undefined unless a masked or null-neighbor convention is implemented; the source does not discuss this edge case.

The two-stage losses are
\[
L_{\rm NE}=\Vert\epsilon-f_\epsilon(Y^{\gamma+1},f_{\rm context}(X,\mathbb{X}_{\mathcal{N}}),\gamma+1)\Vert^2,
\]
with \(\gamma\sim U\{1,\dots,\Gamma\}\), \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), and
\[
L=w\min_k\Vert Y-\widehat{Y}_k\Vert_2+\frac{\sum_k\Vert Y-\widehat{Y}_k\Vert_2}{\sigma_\theta^2K}+\log\sigma_\theta^2,
\]
as printed on [PAGE 5]. \(L_{\rm NE}\) is dimensionless if \(\epsilon\) is dimensionless; the distance term is in meters; the variance term requires dimensionless residuals or a squared norm for dimensional consistency. The paper says “the variance estimation controls the dispersion of the predictions, bridging scenery complexity and prediction diversity” [PAGE 5]. The explicit-supervision alternative is rejected because “we need to run \((\Gamma-\tau)\approx\Gamma\)-steps denoising for \(M\gg K\) samples to get statistics, resulting in unacceptable time and storage consumption” [PAGE 5]. The source concludes: “We thus do not use explicit supervision” [PAGE 5]. The paper’s implementation sets \(\Gamma=100\) and \(\tau=5\) on NBA [PAGE 6]; the ablation reports \(\tau=5\) as the best ADE trade-off at about \(46\) ms, while \(\tau=3\) gives about \(30\) ms and \(\tau=10\) about \(89\) ms [PAGE 7]. The paper’s limitation is also explicit: acceleration works “partially because the dimension of trajectory data is relatively small” [PAGE 8].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
“Crisis habit regression” here means the default behavior of a time-pressured designer who falls back to the standard diffusion habit: many denoising steps plus independent sampling. The source supplies the pressure: standard diffusion needs about 100 steps and costs “∼886ms to predict; while the next frame comes every 200ms” [PAGE 2]. The same source states that “less denoising steps usually cause a weaker representation ability of future distributions” [PAGE 3]. That is the regression trap: the designer knows fewer steps is faster but expects representation loss, so the habit is to keep the expensive schedule. LED’s counter-trap is the leapfrog initializer: it does not merely truncate the schedule; it learns the \(\tau\)-th denoised distribution and then runs only \(\tau\) refinement steps [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap is reinforced by correlated sampling, because \(K\) samples are generated “simultaneously from the same underlying feature” and are “dependent to intentionally allocate appropriate sample diversity” [PAGE 4]. But the counter-trap has its own failure mode: if the designer over-skips, the initializer must model too hard a distribution; if the designer under-skips, the initializer has already captured the denoised distribution and the extra steps waste time [PAGE 7]. The “clock” is the 200 ms frame interval [PAGE 2], and LED reports about 46 ms on NBA [PAGE 2], but the clock is hardware-bound to a GTX-3090 [PAGE 6]. A recursive model of the evaluator must therefore hold two nested expectations: the evaluator values real-time inference, but also values the best-of-\(K\) distance and diversity under a fixed \(K\). If the evaluator only checks latency, any truncation looks good; if the evaluator checks minADE/minFDE at \(K=20\), the initializer and correlated allocation matter. The paper’s two-stage training is itself a theory-of-mind move about trainability: it anticipates that jointly training the initializer and denoiser may be non-convergent, so it freezes the denoiser first [PAGE 5]. The remaining boundary is that the paper’s “mind” is a model of a computational designer, not a model of a physical agent’s intentions; the variance is called “scene complexity” [PAGE 8], not a psychological or biomechanical state.
