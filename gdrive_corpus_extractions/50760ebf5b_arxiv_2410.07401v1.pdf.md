<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a camera-calibration pipeline for soccer broadcast frames, so the derivation must run from image evidence to projective geometry, not from any invented sports-performance ratio. The first hop is the paper’s own framing: “Accurate camera calibration is essential for transforming 2D images from camera sensors into 3D world coordinates” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is the geometric operator stack: a point from 3D world coordinates maps through a 3x3 homography “containing 8 independent parameters,” and the matrix can be calculated from “at least 4 point pairs where no three of them are collinear” [PAGE 2]. The third hop is the paper’s central claim: existing point-matching approaches “have not exploited structural information to its maximum potential” [PAGE 2]. Therefore the pipeline does not stop at extremities; it manufactures correspondences from line-line intersections, line-conic intersections, conic tangencies, and homography-projected extra points. The key count is 57 landmarks: 30 line-line intersections, 6 line-conic intersections, 8 conic tangent points, and 13 additional structural points (9 along the longitudinal axis plus 4 quarter-turns on the central circle) [PAGE 3; PAGE 6 table caption]. A separate 23-channel line detector supplies lines and, when in-image points are insufficient, “predicted line intersection points, even beyond the image boundary, were considered” [PAGE 6].

The multi-hop derivation is therefore: heatmap point model + heatmap line model -> fitted lines and fitted ellipses -> analytic intersections and tangent points -> hypothesis subsets -> homography/camera calibration -> reprojection RMSE voter -> iterative confidence-threshold voter -> line-fusion completion -> challenge score. The gray-zone audit matters because each hop has a failure regime. Homography estimation is singular when correspondences are fewer than four or rank-deficient; near-parallel lines produce unstable finite intersections; line-conic intersection fails when the discriminant is negative; tangent construction from an external point fails if the point is inside the conic; heatmap maxima are unreliable when confidence collapses; and calibration with zero distortion is biased when radial distortion is strong. The paper openly flags one such gray zone: “Note that the computation disregarded the effect of geometric lens distortion” [PAGE 3]. It also flags the counter-trap: “In some cases where intersections were sufficient for reliable calibration, we found that adding extra points actually decreased calibration accuracy” [PAGE 6]. That is not a contradiction; it is a trade-off between accuracy and completeness. Table 1 shows exactly this: intersections-only gives \(L2=4.46\) px, \(Acc@5=0.7711\), \(CR=0.6034\), Score \(0.4653\); adding tangent points gives \(L2=4.79\), \(Acc@5=0.7527\), \(CR=0.6731\), Score \(0.5067\); adding extra points gives \(L2=4.89\), \(Acc@5=0.7446\), \(CR=0.7245\), Score \(0.5395\) [PAGE 6]. Accuracy falls while completeness and the product score rise. The audit must also note that \(CR\) alone is misleading: “the CR metric, without the context of accuracy results, provides little insight into the actual performance of an approach, as the value can reach its maximum of 1.0, for instance, when all images are provided with default camera parameters” [PAGE 5]. Thus any reasoning that maximizes completeness without the accuracy term is a gray-zone error. The final calibration is also vetoed by physical plausibility: below ground, above 100 m, more than 250 m from pitch center in either spatial dimension, or focal length outside \([10,20000]\) px is discarded [PAGE 6]. The remaining uncertainty is temporal: left-right ambiguity is hard “without temporal context” [PAGE 4], and temporal smoothing is left to future work [PAGE 8]. That is the clock layer the paper does not fully close.

Let world points be \(X_w=(X,Y,Z,1)^\top\) in metres and image points be \(x=(u,v,1)^\top\) in pixels. The pinhole projection is
\[
x \sim K\,[R\mid t]\,X_w,\qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}f_x&0&c_x\\0&f_y&c_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},
\]
with focal lengths \(f_x,f_y\) in pixels and principal point \((c_x,c_y)\) in pixels. The paper fixes the principal point to the frame center and assumes “zero astigmatism and distortions” [PAGE 6]. For a planar pitch, \(Z=0\), so
\[
x \sim H\,X,\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},\quad H\sim \lambda H,
\]
where \(H\) has 8 independent parameters. The source states: “The matrix can be calculated using at least 4 point pairs where no three of them are collinear from the model and image plane” [PAGE 2]. In direct-linear-transform form, stacking \(N\) correspondences gives
\[
A h=0,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 9},\quad h=\operatorname{vec}(H),
\]
and a unique finite homography requires \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=8\). Breakdown occurs when \(N<4\), when three or more correspondences are collinear, or when the configuration is otherwise rank-deficient; the nullspace then has dimension greater than one and \(H\) is not identifiable. Near-parallel pitch lines are a numerical gray zone because their intersection moves toward infinity in projective coordinates and becomes unstable in finite pixel coordinates.

The point detector is a heatmap operator. For keypoint \(i\) at pixel \((u_i,v_i)\), the target is a Gaussian peak
\[
G_i(u,v)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{(u-u_i)^2+(v-v_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\]
with \(\sigma=3\) px for points [PAGE 5] and \(\sigma=4\) px for line extremities [PAGE 6]. The network output is passed through Softmax; the maximum location is the predicted keypoint and the maximum value is treated as confidence [PAGE 5]. The paper also adds an inverse-max channel so the target tensor “summed to 1.0 at each spatial point” [PAGE 5]. Training uses MSE and Adam, then Adaptive Wing Loss, with augmentation by horizontal flip, Gaussian noise, and colour variation [PAGE 5]. The line model uses 23 heatmap channels, two peaks per line, and decodes by finding the highest-confidence point, masking it, finding the second peak, and producing the line equation [PAGE 4; PAGE 6].

Line fitting produces line equations. For two fitted lines \(l_1^\top x=0\) and \(l_2^\top x=0\), the line-line intersection is
\[
x_{12}\sim l_1\times l_2,
\]
with breakdown at parallelism \(l_1\times l_2\approx 0\). The paper uses a two-step fit: first fit all annotation points on a line, derive a coarse intersection, then refit only proximate points to refine the keypoint [PAGE 3]. For a conic written as
\[
C(x,y)=a x^2+bxy+c y^2+d x+e y+f=0,
\]
the line-conic intersection solves \(C(x,y)=0\) with \(l^\top (x,y,1)=0\). In quadratic form the discriminant
\[
\Delta = B^2-4AC
\]
determines the regime: \(\Delta>0\) gives two real intersections, \(\Delta=0\) gives tangency, and \(\Delta<0\) gives no real intersection. The paper fits conics with Halíř–Flusser least squares and derives line-conic intersections analytically [PAGE 3]. Tangent points from an external point \(P\) to conic \(C\) satisfy the polar relation
\[
P^\top C\,x=0,\qquad x^\top C\,x=0,
\]
and real tangents exist only when \(P\) is outside the conic; if \(P\) is inside, the construction breaks down. The paper uses known pitch-line intersections as external points and exploits the fact that “projective transformations preserve tangency” [PAGE 3].

Calibration is scored by polyline accuracy:
\[
Acc@t=\frac{TP@t}{TP@t+FN@t+FP@t},
\]
where a ground-truth field segment is a true positive if every annotated point is within \(t\) pixels of the predicted polyline; otherwise it contributes false positives or false negatives [PAGE 5]. The challenge fixes \(t=5\) [PAGE 5]. Completeness is
\[
CR=\frac{\#\{\text{frames with a predicted camera}\}}{\#\{\text{frames}\}},
\]
dimensionless on \([0,1]\), and
\[
Score=Acc@5\times CR
\]
[PAGE 5]. The raw keypoint \(L2\) metric is the average Euclidean distance in pixels between predicted and ground-truth keypoints [PAGE 5]. Camera hypotheses are voted by reprojection RMSE:
\[
e_{\mathrm{RMSE}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\left\|\pi(K,R,t,X_j)-x_j\right\|_2^2},
\]
in pixels. The voter chooses the lowest-RMSE parameter set and gives preference to all detected points when RMSE is less than \(5\) px [PAGE 6]. The Iterative Voter repeats this over three confidence thresholds and selects the highest-confidence acceptable set [PAGE 6]. Physical veto thresholds are explicit: below ground or above \(100\) m, more than \(250\) m from pitch center in either spatial dimension, or focal length outside \([10,20000]\) px causes the calibration result to be discarded [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes therefore include rank-deficient homographies, parallel-line intersections, negative conic discriminants, inside-conic tangencies, low-confidence heatmap maxima, and distortion-induced reprojection bias. The last is especially important because the paper says “the computation disregarded the effect of geometric lens distortion” [PAGE 3], and because endpoint closeness can hide midline error: “it is possible that the endpoints of a distorted line are close, but the distance between the middle points is large” [PAGE 5].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from pinhole projection and projective geometry to direct linear transformation, then to feature-based pitch registration, then to heatmap deep learning. Traditional soccer calibration used SIFT keypoints, Hough line detection, and advanced least-squares fitting for arcs and ellipses [PAGE 2]. Those methods are historically important but the paper says they are “often vulnerable to noise, shadows, lighting variations and occlusions” [PAGE 2]. Deep learning shifted the field toward semantic segmentation: Homayounfar et al. classified pixels into vertical lines, horizontal lines, side circles, middle circle, grass, and crowd [PAGE 2]; Citraro et al. added player mass centers but required multiple camera setups [PAGE 2]; Nie et al. used uniform grids [PAGE 3]; Chu et al. used instance segmentation with per-point labels [PAGE 3]. The paper’s retrocausal argument is that grid points “do not form a line or a corner” and lack connection with the physical structure of the pitch, which “decreases its generalization ability” [PAGE 3]. The correction is to derive points from the pitch’s structural geometry: line-line intersections, line-conic intersections, conic tangencies, and homography-projected structural points [PAGE 3]. The dataset lineage is SoccerNet-Calibration-2022 with 21,132 images and SoccerNet-Calibration-2023 with 25,506 images at 960x540 px from 500 games [PAGE 5], plus the Magera et al. polyline metric that considers distortion better than point-only error [PAGE 5]. The challenge result is the top entry with \(Acc@5=0.7322\), \(CR=0.7559\), Score \(0.5535\) [PAGE 7].

Multi-week traps appear at several layers. Trap one is annotation-as-truth: the paper explicitly says “We utilise computed keypoints instead of relying directly on annotation data, thus enhancing robustness against imperfections in the data” [PAGE 2]. A team that trains directly on noisy extremities will bake annotation error into the model. Trap two is the grid trap: dense grid points may increase apparent supervision but weaken physical correspondence [PAGE 3]. Trap three is the distortion trap: ignoring radial distortion can look acceptable at endpoints while failing along the polyline [PAGE 5]. Trap four is left-right ambiguity: when the camera aligns with the pitch long axis, “accurately differentiating between the left and right sides without temporal context becomes difficult” [PAGE 4]. Trap five is metric myopia: optimizing \(CR\) alone can be gamed by default camera parameters [PAGE 5], so the product \(Score=Acc@5\times CR\) is mandatory. Trap six is temporal instability: the paper leaves “temporal smoothing techniques and neural networks based on frame sequence processing” to future work [PAGE 8]. A multi-week effort that chases per-frame accuracy without a temporal state will regress on broadcast sequences with occlusion and camera motion.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the model’s tendency, under point scarcity, to fall back on the most visually salient and easiest-to-learn features: line-line intersections and extremities. The paper supports this: “The Acc@5 metric achieves the best result when only intersections were utilised” [PAGE 7], because intersections “are marked with clear geometric markings on the football pitch, thereby allowing for less ambiguous and more accurate neural network predictions” [PAGE 7]. That is a rational habit, but it is a regression when the camera field of view contains too few intersections. The counter-trap is to add tangent points and homography-projected extra points without noticing that accuracy can fall. Table 1 shows \(Acc@5\) dropping from \(0.7711\) to \(0.7527\) to \(0.7446\) as tangent and extra points are added, while \(CR\) rises from \(0.6034\) to \(0.6731\) to \(0.7245\) and Score rises from \(0.4653\) to \(0.5067\) to \(0.5395\) [PAGE 6]. The recursive theory-of-mind reading is that the pipeline must model three agents: the annotator, who is imperfect; the neural network, which is overconfident on easy intersections and underconfident on sparse conics; and the calibrator, which can be fooled by low reprojection error on a bad subset. The Voter and Iterative Voter are the paper’s answer: they recursively re-select subsets by confidence and RMSE rather than trusting one global point set [PAGE 6]. The clock layer is the missing temporal agent. The pipeline is per-frame; left-right remapping makes “the goal area closest to the camera consistently considered the left side” [PAGE 4], but that is a static convention, not temporal tracking. Without a clock, broadcast cuts, occlusions, and camera pans can produce inconsistent labels. The paper’s own future work names temporal smoothing and frame-sequence networks [PAGE 8]. Thus the correct counter-trap is not “add more points always” and not “trust intersections always”; it is to use the Score product, confidence thresholds, RANSAC filtering, line fusion, and a future temporal prior together.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/geometric veto is provable from the source. Homography identifiability requires at least four non-collinear correspondences and rank \(8\) in the DLT system [PAGE 2]. If a frame supplies fewer than four usable pairs and the line model cannot supply out-of-frame intersections, calibration must be vetoed because no unique \(H\) exists. If the fitted camera suggests it is below ground, above \(100\) m, more than \(250\) m from pitch center in either spatial dimension, or has focal length outside \([10,20000]\) px, the paper discards the result completely [PAGE 6]. The reprojection veto is softer: the voter prefers the lowest-RMSE set and gives preference to all detected points when RMSE is less than \(5\) px [PAGE 6], but a high-RMSE hypothesis can still be selected if no better set exists; that is exactly why \(CR\) must be multiplied by accuracy. The optical/weather veto is also source-backed: traditional low-level methods are “vulnerable to noise, shadows, lighting variations and occlusions” [PAGE 2], and line visibility controls whether line-line and line-conic operators exist at all. If shadows or occlusion remove the visible lines, the line-fusion rescue may fail and \(CR\) drops. The conic tangent operator can rescue frames where only the centre circle is visible, because tangent points from known external points augment the homography set [PAGE 3]. The biomechanical veto is not derivable from this paper. The source mentions player tracking, offside detection, distance covered, and speed as downstream uses [PAGE 1-2], but it contains no biomechanical state variable, no force, no fatigue, and no player-motion model. Asserting a biomechanical veto would be an invented layer, so it is rejected. The correct cross-layer adjudication is: geometric rank veto first, physical camera-range veto second, reprojection-RMSE preference third, visibility/weather veto fourth, and no biomechanical veto unless a separate biomechanical source is supplied.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Degenerate-correspondence veto. Take a frame with only three non-collinear point pairs and disable the line model. The verifiable predicate is \(\operatorname{rank}(A)<8\) in the DLT system [PAGE 2]. Prediction: no unique homography is produced; the frame contributes \(CR=0\) unless another camera hypothesis is available, and its per-frame Score is zero. This is verifiable by rank computation and by checking that the calibration output is absent or discarded. Scenario 2: Annotation-as-truth veto. Replace computed keypoints with raw annotation extremities and disable the line-fitting exclusion of failed points. The source’s stated design is the opposite: “We utilise computed keypoints instead of relying directly on annotation data, thus enhancing robustness against imperfections in the data” [PAGE 2], and “Points that failed the line fitting algorithm are excluded in training” [PAGE 4]. Prediction: raw-annotation training increases \(L2\) keypoint error and lowers \(Acc@5\) on the validation split relative to fitted-keypoint training, because annotation noise is no longer averaged out by line fitting. This is verifiable by a controlled ablation on the same split and by measuring both \(L2\) and \(Acc@5\). Scenario 3: Distortion-polyline veto. Apply the pipeline to frames with strong radial distortion while keeping the paper’s zero-distortion assumption [PAGE 6]. The verifiable predicate is the polyline metric: a segment is a true positive only if all annotated points are within \(5\) px of the predicted polyline [PAGE 5]. Prediction: endpoint-only inspection can look acceptable, but \(Acc@5\) falls because “it is possible that the endpoints of a distorted line are close, but the distance between the middle points is large” [PAGE 5]. This is verifiable by computing both endpoint \(L2\) and polyline \(Acc@5\) on the same distorted frames; the counterfactual veto is confirmed if endpoint error is low while polyline accuracy is lower.
