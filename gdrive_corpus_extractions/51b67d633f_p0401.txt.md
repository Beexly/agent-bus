<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of James's argument traces back to a fundamental rift in late 19th-century biology and philosophy. On one side stood the "mystical view" [PAGE 401], inherited from Aristotle's *De Anima* and perpetuated by pre-Darwinian natural theology, which saw instinct as a perfect, unerring divine implant—a teleological guarantee of species survival. This view demanded "invariable" and "mathematically 'true'" behavior. Its scientific counterpart was the rigid specificity of early neurophysiology, perhaps echoing Helmholtz's precise measurements of nerve conduction, which suggested deterministic mechanical chains.

James positions himself squarely against this lineage. His "retrocausal" insight—that modern observations of animal behavior invalidate the old definition—is rooted in the empirical revolutions of his contemporaries. He explicitly cites Darwin’s *Origin of Species*, whose gradualist, selectionist account rendered perfect, immutable instincts untenable. If instincts evolved by small variations, then imperfection and variability were not bugs but essential features. Similarly, George John Romanes’s *Mental Evolution in Animals* provided extensive comparative evidence of "mistake" and "transition," documenting how even fixed action patterns could be interrupted or modified by context.

Crucially, James leverages the emerging field of experimental psychology and neuroscience. His reference to the "reflex arc" connects to the work of Sherrington and others who were dissecting spinal cord functions, demonstrating that even simple reflexes could be modulated, inhibited, or facilitated by higher centers. The "physiological conception" is thus not just a philosophical stance but a commitment to explaining mental phenomena through known or knowable physical mechanisms—specifically, the interactions of excitatory and inhibitory currents in neural tissue.

The multi-week trap for a modern reader lies in overlooking the profound radicalism of this move. Today, we take for granted that all behavior has a neural basis and is subject to noise and interference. But in 1890, asserting that a "pure" psychological entity like instinct was reducible to a noisy, inhibitable reflex arc was a bombshell. It collapsed the mind-body dualism at the heart of Cartesian thought and challenged the very notion of free will if even our deepest urges were just electrical currents fighting for dominance. James is not merely describing animal behavior; he is laying the groundwork for a fully materialist science of consciousness, where the "struggle in his breast" is nothing more than a competition between dipoles in a network.

Thus, while James correctly identifies intra-neural competition as a major source of behavioral variability, his "physiological conception" is incomplete. It must be embedded within a larger cybernetic loop that includes the body as a filter and the environment as a constraint. The true "fatality" of behavior emerges only at the intersection of neural dynamics, bodily capacity, and physical possibility.
