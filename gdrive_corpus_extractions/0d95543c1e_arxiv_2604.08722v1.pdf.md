<THINK_TRACE>
The source describes a multi-stage measurement pipeline, not a single predictor. The causal chain is: raw elevated BePro video with dynamic zoom and ball-tracking movement [PAGE 4] → first-frame object detection → bounding-box center prompts → SAM2 segmentation and streaming-memory tracking [PAGE 7] → a multi-task CNN that predicts 12 field keypoints plus visibility [PAGE 8] → DLT homography from camera pixels to a top-down field template [PAGE 9] → K-Means team assignment from 5×5 RGB patches [PAGE 9] → downstream tactical quantities such as player speed, distance covered, positioning heatmaps, and team statistics [PAGE 1]. The paper’s own headline numbers are: YOLOv5x chosen with F1 0.8451, recall 0.7995, precision 0.8963, and IoU 0.7644 [PAGE 10]; keypoint validation visibility accuracy 97.18% and masked MAE 0.0138 normalized image coordinates, reported as 7.6531 pixels [PAGE 12]; full-system keypoint MAE 0.225 m for ground-truth points and 0.26 m for predicted points, with average projection error 0.499 m [PAGE 12]. The important multi-hop audit is that these numbers live on different layers and cannot be averaged into a single “system accuracy.” A missed detection prevents a player from ever entering SAM2, so recall failure becomes track omission. A keypoint localization error perturbs H, so pixel error becomes metric error. A glare-induced color error flips a team label, so appearance error becomes tactical misclassification. A re-identification failure changes the identity attached to a trajectory, so tracking error becomes a false speed or distance statistic. The gray-zone audit must therefore ask which layer first violates its assumptions. The source itself supplies several: DLT “assumes all input points are accurate and does not account for outliers, making it highly sensitive to keypoint placement” [PAGE 9]; the detector produces false positives such as a ballboy or referee [PAGE 12]; the clustering algorithm “mistakes players when they are affected by the glare or shadows, assigning them to the opposite team” [PAGE 13]; and the keypoint model “is not perfect, which can lead to innaccurate positions in the top down perspective or misleading stats” [PAGE 13]. The dataset is also narrow: 10 home games, 146 keypoint-labeled frames from two games, with 92 glare frames and 54 overcast frames, and typically 4 to 12 keypoints per frame [PAGE 5]. Ground truth is not independent: SAM2 generated annotations on 390 consecutive frames after YOLO prompts, and SAM2 masks were reverse-engineered into ground-truth boxes [PAGE 5]. That creates pseudo-label circularity. The system found 17/22 players without fine-tuning [PAGE 10], which is promising but also exposes the coverage gap. The deepest derivation is therefore conditional: metric coordinates exist only if at least four non-collinear keypoints are visible and H is well-conditioned; team labels exist only if illumination does not dominate the 5×5 color patch; player-level speed exists only if identity and frame timestamps are preserved. No layer should be allowed to inherit credibility from a higher-layer average. This is the central gray-zone finding: high keypoint visibility accuracy and reasonable detection F1 coexist with metric projection error, false positives, weather-sensitive clustering, and unresolved re-identification. The correct response is layered abstention, not a single composite score.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are: (O1) object detection with Faster R-CNN, YOLOv5x, YOLOv8x, or YOLOv11x; (O2) SAM2 segmentation and streaming-memory tracking; (O3) a multi-task CNN for 12 keypoint coordinates and visibility; (O4) Direct Linear Transformation homography; and (O5) K-Means team clustering on RGB patches. The paper states the goal is to obtain “player speed, distance covered, positioning heatmaps, and more complex team statistics” [PAGE 1], and that homography transforms camera-perspective positions to a “real-ground perspective” [PAGE 1]. The field registration pipeline is described as extracting keypoints, establishing correspondences to a 2D field template, and estimating the homography matrix [PAGE 4].

Detection metrics are dimensionless under a fixed matching rule:
\[
P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R},\qquad IoU=\frac{|B\cap B^*|}{|B\cup B^*|}.
\]
Here \(B\) is the predicted bounding box and \(B^*\) is ground truth. The source reports YOLOv5x as the chosen model with “the highest F1-score (0.8451)” while maintaining “strong recall (0.7995), precision (0.8963), and IoU (0.7644)” [PAGE 10]. Breakdown regime: low recall means players never receive SAM2 prompts; the paper notes that missed detections limit tracking completeness [PAGE 10]. High precision alone is insufficient: YOLOv11x has recall 0.6805 versus YOLOv5x recall 0.7995 [PAGE 11].

Keypoint localization uses the paper’s masked MAE:
\[
\mathrm{MAE}_{masked}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{C}v_{i,j}\left(|x_{i,j}-\hat{x}_{i,j}|+|y_{i,j}-\hat{y}_{i,j}|\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{C}v_{i,j}}.
\]
Units are normalized image coordinates, dimensionless in the range [0,1]; \(v_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}\) is visibility, \(C=12\), and invisible points are imputed as \((-1,-1)\) [PAGE 8]. Pixel error requires image width and height, so normalized MAE is not a physical unit. The paper reports training and testing values: accuracy 0.9989 and 0.9718; MAE as percent image 0.0107 and 0.0138; MAE in pixels 5.9648 and 7.6531 [PAGE 12]. Visibility accuracy is:
\[
\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{C}\mathbf{1}\left[\mathrm{round}(\hat{y}_{i,j})=y_{i,j}\right],
\]
dimensionless. Breakdown regime: visibility accuracy can remain high while coordinate regression errors dominate homography; the paper’s own keypoint model “is not perfect” [PAGE 13].

Homography maps field points to image points:
\[
\mathbf{q}_i=\begin{pmatrix}u_i\\v_i\\w_i\end{pmatrix}\sim H\mathbf{X}_i,\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},\qquad \mathbf{X}_i=\begin{pmatrix}X_i\\Y_i\\1\end{pmatrix},
\]
where \(X_i,Y_i\) are in meters on the field template and \(u_i,v_i\) are in pixels. The normalized image coordinates are
\[
u_i'=\frac{u_i}{w_i},\qquad v_i'=\frac{v_i}{w_i}.
\]
DLT solves
\[
A h=0,\qquad h=\mathrm{vec}(H),\qquad \hat{h}=\arg\min_{\|h\|_2=1}\|Ah\|_2^2,
\]
using SVD. The source states: “The DLT estimates the homography matrix by solving a linear system built from point correspondences, using Singular Value Decomposition. It assumes all input points are accurate and does not account for outliers, making it highly sensitive to keypoint placement” [PAGE 9]. Assumptions: at least four non-collinear point correspondences, accurate field-template dimensions, and a planar field model. Singularity and breakdown regimes: \(\det(H)=0\); \(w_i\approx 0\); rank deficiency in \(A\), especially with collinear or near-collinear keypoints; large condition number \(\kappa(H)\); or keypoint residuals comparable to the field scale. In those regimes, a finite-looking but arbitrary pixel coordinate can be produced, so all downstream metric coordinates must be vetoed.

Projection error in metric units can be written as
\[
E_{proj}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\pi\left(H_{pred}\mathbf{X}_k\right)-\pi\left(H_{gt}\mathbf{X}_k\right)\right\|_2,
\]
where \(\pi\) denotes projective normalization and the norm is in meters on the field plane. The paper reports “an average projection error of 0.499 meters in the keypoints transformed” and keypoint MAE of “0.225 meters for the ground truth points and 0.26 meters for the predicted points” [PAGE 12]. Breakdown regime: if field dimensions are wrong, or if the camera zoom changes between keypoint prediction and player mapping, the metric scale is biased even when pixel residuals look small.

Player kinematics require a time base:
\[
v_k=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{p}_{k+1}-\mathbf{p}_{k-1}\right\|_2}{t_{k+1}-t_{k-1}},\qquad s(t)=\int_{t_0}^{t}\left\|\dot{\mathbf{p}}(\tau)\right\|_2\,d\tau,
\]
with \(\mathbf{p}_k=(X_k,Y_k)\) in meters, \(v_k\) in m/s, and \(s\) in meters. Assumption: known frame timestamps and persistent identity. Breakdown regime: dropped frames, camera cuts, occlusion, SAM2 identity loss, or player re-entry without re-identification. The paper explicitly lists re-identification for players entering and leaving the frame as future work [PAGE 13].

Team clustering minimizes
\[
\min_{\{\mu_1,\mu_2\}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\min_{\ell\in\{1,2\}}\left\|\mathbf{c}_i-\mu_\ell\right\|_2^2,
\]
where \(\mathbf{c}_i\) is the mean RGB vector from a \(5\times5\) patch at the bounding-box center and the distance is in RGB intensity units. The source notes that “clustering algorithm mistakes players when they are affected by the glare or shadows, assigning them to the opposite team” [PAGE 13]. Breakdown regime: illuminant shift, white balance, shadow, glare, or jersey color similarity makes the two-cluster assumption invalid.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive belief structure has three agents: the analyst, the coaching staff, and the system. The analyst believes the system’s metric output; the coaching staff believes the analyst’s tactical summary; the system “believes” its first-frame detections, SAM2 memory, keypoint visibility, and color cluster. Crisis habit regression occurs when uncertainty rises and each layer falls back to its cheapest prior. Under occlusion, low resolution, or glare, the detector falls back to learned object priors and admits false positives such as a ballboy or referee [PAGE 12]. SAM2 falls back to memory and may preserve an identity after the player has left or changed appearance. The keypoint CNN falls back to visible-line patterns, but if too few keypoints are visible, DLT falls back to whatever correspondences remain, even when they are near-collinear. K-Means falls back to mean RGB and can assign a shadowed player to the opposite team [PAGE 13]. The counter-trap is that a locally “smart” choice can be globally worse. Choosing YOLOv11x for precision or IoU is a counter-trap for a SAM2 prompter because recall is lower: 0.6805 versus 0.7995 for YOLOv5x [PAGE 11]. Trusting 97.18% keypoint visibility accuracy is a counter-trap if the coordinate MAE and DLT conditioning still produce 0.499 m projection error [PAGE 12]. Trusting a high F1 is a counter-trap if the detector still finds only 17/22 players [PAGE 10]. The correct counter-move is not another aggregate score; it is abstention, re-prompting, and layer-specific confidence. Clock discipline is the temporal version of the same problem. SAM2’s streaming memory is a clock: it carries context across frames [PAGE 3]. Player speed and distance are clock-dependent integrals over time, so a frame-index clock is not enough unless dropped frames and camera cuts are handled. If a player leaves and re-enters, the system needs exit location, team, and previous coordinates to re-prompt only that player [PAGE 13]. Without that, the clock resets identity and the trajectory becomes a false path. The recursive theory-of-mind failure is therefore: the analyst assumes the system knows who the player is; the system assumes SAM2 memory knows; SAM2 assumes the prompt identity is stable; the detector assumes the first frame is representative. Each assumption is plausible locally and invalid globally. Crisis habit regression says “use the prior”; counter-trap says “optimize the visible metric”; clock says “preserve identity and time.” A robust system must let the clock veto the habit and let geometric conditioning veto the counter-trap.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The veto hierarchy is geometric, perceptual, appearance-based, temporal, then biomechanical. The proof is by dependency: every downstream statistic is a function of field coordinates, and every field coordinate is a function of H. If H is not identifiable, no later layer can repair it. The geometric veto is: require at least four visible non-collinear keypoints, a full-rank DLT system, and a bounded condition number \(\kappa(H)\). If not, \(H\) is singular or near-singular, and the projective normalization \(u'=u/w\), \(v'=v/w\) can explode as \(w\approx0\). The source explicitly warns that DLT “assumes all input points are accurate and does not account for outliers, making it highly sensitive to keypoint placement” [PAGE 9]. The perceptual veto is: if detection recall is low or if non-player objects enter the box set, player counts and team aggregates are invalid. The source reports false positives such as a ballboy or referee [PAGE 12] and only 17/22 players identified without fine-tuning [PAGE 10]. The appearance veto is: if the 5×5 RGB patch is contaminated by glare, shadow, or white-balance shift, the two-cluster team assignment is not trustworthy; the source says players are assigned “to the opposite team” under glare or shadows [PAGE 13]. The weather veto is not decorative: the primary dataset includes rainy conditions [PAGE 4], and the keypoint set includes 92 glare and 54 overcast frames [PAGE 5]. Rain, glare, and shadow change both keypoint visibility and color appearance, so they attack geometry and clustering simultaneously.

The biomechanical veto follows from error propagation. Let \(\delta p\) be field-coordinate error in meters and \(\Delta t\) the frame interval in seconds. A central-difference speed error scales approximately as
\[
\delta v \approx \frac{2\delta p}{\Delta t}.
\]
Using the paper’s 0.499 m average projection error [PAGE 12], at \(\Delta t=1/30\) s the propagated speed error is about 29.94 m/s; at \(\Delta t=1\) s it is about 1 m/s. Therefore a speed estimate is verifiable only if the time base is known and the coordinate error is small relative to the sport’s plausible speed and acceleration bounds. The paper does not provide a biomechanical model, so any biomechanical veto threshold must be externally specified; the formal veto is: if the claimed speed or acceleration is not separable from \(\delta v\) under the known \(\Delta t\), reject the metric. The physical veto also includes field scale: the paper uses Google Maps API and NCAA rules to define keypoint locations [PAGE 9]. If the field template is wrong, or if the camera zoom changes between calibration and tracking, the meter scale is biased. The cross-layer adjudication rule is: the lowest invalid layer vetoes every higher claim. A bad keypoint vetoes positions, speed, distance, and heatmaps. A bad color patch vetoes team labels but may still allow player presence. A bad identity vetoes player-level distance but may still allow team-level occupancy. A bad clock vetoes speed and distance even if positions look plausible. This is the only defensible adjudication because averaging a geometric failure with a detection success produces a number that has no physical meaning.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
