Knight begins his analysis by establishing the fundamental distinction between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty. Consider a commodity whose price follows a random walk with drift:

\[
dP = \mu P dt + \sigma P dW
\]

where \(P\) is price, \(\mu\) is expected return, \(\sigma\) is volatility, and \(dW\) represents Brownian motion. For investors operating within an ergodic stationary process, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are known constants, enabling risk-pooling strategies based on large numbers law. However, Knight argues that many economic decisions involve parameters that are not merely unknown but unknowable. Let the total capital be \(K\) and the investment amount be \(k_i\) in asset \(i\), with weights satisfying \(\sum w_i = 1\). The return \(R\) on the portfolio is:

\[
R = \sum_i w_i r_i,
\]

where \(r_i\) are asset-specific returns. In a Knightian scenario, at least one \(w_i\) must be assigned via a subjective measure \(\pi(w_i)\) rather than a frequentist count. This leads to a singular point in decision space where traditional mean-variance optimization breaks down.

The cornerstone equation characterizing Knightian uncertainty in production contexts, as applied to agricultural output \(Y\):

\[
Y = f(K,L,E,W)
\]

with inputs \(K\) (capital), \(L\) (labor), \(E\) (environmental conditions), and \(W\) (weather). Under Knightian uncertainty, the joint distribution \(F(Y|K,L,E)\) collapses into a set-valued belief \(\Pi(E,W)\). The enterprise value maximization problem then involves maximizing worst-case expected utility:

\[
\max_{x} \min_{(E,W) \in \Pi} \mathbb{E}[U(x,Y)|E,W],
\]

where \(x\) is the control variable and \(U\) is a concave utility function. In the specific context of risk versus uncertainty, Knight highlighted that while risk admits a finite variance \(\operatorname{Var}(R) < \infty\), Knightian uncertainty may have infinite variance, rendering standard deviation meaningless. This is captured by the Levy-Khinchin representation for stable distributions with exponent \(\alpha \in (0,2]\):

\[

which converges to Gaussian (\(\alpha=2\)) only when \(\beta=0\). For agricultural models, the extremal index \(\theta\) governs the clustering of extreme events and is given by:

\[

where \(M_n = \max(X_1,...,X_n)\). Under ergodicity, \(\theta = 1/e\); Knight suggested that natural disasters often violate this ergodicity, leading to \(\theta \neq 1/e\).
