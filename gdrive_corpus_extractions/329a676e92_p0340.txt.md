Hull’s lineage descends from Pavlovian reflex physiology, Thorndike’s law of effect, and Watson’s behaviorist manifesto, but diverges by treating “reaction potential” and “habit strength” as intervening variables inferred deductively from postulates rather than as merely descriptive summaries. The notation (sEk) in the text corresponds to Hull’s excitatory reaction potential, sE_r, a central construct in his deductive chain: drive (D) × habit strength (sHr) × incentive (K) − threshold (sIr) determines reaction tendency.

Scientific descendants include Spence’s discrimination-learning gradient theory, Logan’s motivational formulations, and, indirectly, the Rescorla–Wagner (1972) expectancy model and modern temporal-difference reinforcement learning, all of which replace Hull’s molar curves with asymptotic prediction-error rules. The Hullian insight that observed probability can saturate while latent strength continues to accrue anticipates the modern distinction between performance and learning in reinforcement-learning theory.

Multi-week trap: In longitudinal habit-formation protocols spanning weeks, the early acquisition phase produces rapid gains in p, but the later phase shows p pinned at 100 per cent while ℰ(n) still climbs slowly toward asymptote. A practitioner observing only evocation probability may conclude that the habit is “maximal” and thin or withdraw reinforcement. Because p is saturated, this decision is made without information about the true latent reserve. If reinforcement is withdrawn prematurely, extinction resistance reveals whether ℰ(n) was actually high or merely barely above threshold. The trap is therefore temporal: the ceiling in p arrives weeks before the asymptote in ℰ, and the intervening weeks are a vulnerability window in which the habit looks stable but is under-specified by the behavioral measure alone.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Recursive theory of mind here concerns two nested models: the learner’s model of the reinforcement contingency and the experimenter’s model of the learner’s latent state. Under normal conditions, p = 1.0 provides mutual reassurance: the learner emits the response consistently, and the experimenter infers strong habit strength. Crisis habit regression occurs when an external perturbation—acute stress, competing drive, or fatigue—raises the effective threshold θ or broadens the zone of oscillation ω. Because ℰ(n) − θ was only marginally positive during the p = 1.0 plateau, the crisis shrinks the margin below zero and evocation collapses. The observer, having relied solely on p, receives no early warning: the regression appears sudden even though the latent buffer was thin all along.

Counter-trap: measure latency L and extinction resistance R while p remains at ceiling. If L is already elevated relative to the theoretical minimum, or if R is low despite many reinforcements, the system is in a fragile ceiling state. These probes regress the observer’s belief about ℰ(n) backward from overt performance to latent strength, counteracting the trap of trusting p = 1.0 as proof of robustness.

Clock: The learning curve is not symmetrical; Hull’s data give approximately nine reinforcements for the 0→50 per cent transition and seventeen reinforcements for the 50→100 per cent transition ([PAGE 333]). The “clock” governing reinforcement therefore runs at two speeds: fast initial acquisition, then roughly twice as many trials for the final approach to ceiling. An agent recursively modeling the teacher must allocate approximately 65 percent of total acquisition reinforcements to the second half of the curve. Misestimating this clock leads to premature schedule thinning: the experimenter treats the 50 per cent point as the midpoint of effort, when it is actually the midpoint of probability but not the midpoint of required reinforcement.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof.

Any inference that habit strength is maximal solely because p = 1.0 is void if any of three independent layers issues a veto. The layers are physical/physiological, biomechanical/motor, and environmental/contextual (“weather” in the broad sense of situational stimuli).

Physical layer: the organism must be in a homeostatic state capable of supporting the reaction. If drive D falls below a minimum D_min, or if physiological arousal is incompatible with response execution, evocation is vetoed regardless of ℰ(n). Formal veto condition:
\[
D < D_{\min} \;\lor\; A < A_{\min} \;\implies\; \neg\text{evocation},
\]
where A is arousal/activation state. This overrides the behavioral p-series because p = 1.0 was measured under a different physical regime.

Biomechanical layer: the all-or-none reaction must be physically executable. If the required motor topography M_req exceeds current motor capacity M_cap (fatigue, injury, neuromuscular constraint), the response cannot be emitted even if reaction potential exceeds threshold:
\[
M_{\text{req}} > M_{\text{cap}} \;\implies\; \text{biomechanical veto}.
\]
This is distinct from a failure of learning; it is a failure of the output plant.

Weather/environmental layer: contextual stimuli shift the effective threshold. Let Δθ_env be the context-induced threshold displacement. Then
\[
\theta_{\text{eff}} = \theta + \Delta\theta_{\text{env}}.
\]
If ℰ(n) + ω < θ_eff, evocation fails despite a prior history of p = 1.0 in a neutral context. This layer proves that p = 1.0 is context-relative, not a context-free measure of habit strength.

Veto proof: Because each layer can independently suppress evocation, an observation of p = 1.0 in training does not entail p = 1.0 under transfer, stress, fatigue, or altered context. The only defensible inference is that ℰ(n) exceeded θ under the specific training conditions; all other claims require layer-specific verification (latency, extinction resistance, or direct threshold probes).

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1: Response-cost latency counterfactual. Manipulation: After p reaches 1.0 and remains there, impose a small but nonzero response cost (e.g., increased manipulandum displacement or added effort requirement) on a random subset of trials. Prediction: Reaction latency L will increase significantly on cost trials relative to no-cost trials, and p may drop below 1.0 if the cost raises the effective threshold above ℰ(n) + ω on some trials. Verification: Record L in seconds before and after cost imposition; a statistically significant latency increase, or any trial with failed evocation under cost, vetoes the claim that p = 1.0 reflects effort-independent maximal habit strength.

Scenario 2: Differential extinction-resistance counterfactual. Manipulation: Train two matched groups to p = 1.0 at different reinforcement counts (e.g., n = 28 versus n = 60, bracketing the text’s 50-to-100 per cent span), then withdraw reinforcement entirely. Prediction: The group with more prior reinforcements will exhibit greater resistance to extinction R, measured as the number of non-reinforced trials to criterion, despite both groups having identical p = 1.0 at withdrawal. Verification: Count R for each subject; a significant positive correlation between prior n and R falsifies the inference that p = 1.0 marks an upper bound on habit strength.

Scenario 3: Drive-state threshold-shift counterfactual. Manipulation: After stable p = 1.0 under satiated conditions, shift the organism’s motivational state (e.g., food deprivation versus satiety, or introduction of a competing drive) without additional reinforcement. Prediction: p will fall below 1.0 under the shifted drive because θ_eff moves relative to the fixed ℰ(n), demonstrating that p was threshold-relative, not an absolute index of potential. Verification: Re-measure p under both drive states within subjects; a significant drop in p under the drive shift vetoes the conclusion that p = 1.0 indicates maximal latent reaction potential independent of motivational context.
