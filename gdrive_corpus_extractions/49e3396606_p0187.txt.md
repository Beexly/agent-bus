Let \(C\) represent the total cost incurred in creating a product, including all explicit payments and implicit opportunity costs. Let \(R\) denote the gross revenue generated by selling the product. The net profit \(\Pi\) is then given by:
\[
\Pi = R - C
\]
In Knight's static-equilibrium argument, the necessary condition for competitive pricing is \(R = C\), yielding \(\Pi = 0\). However, in non-static states, \(R\) becomes a random variable \(R(t)\) depending on unpredictable events \(\xi(t)\).

Knight introduces the concept of "anticipatability" via a filter function \(F[\cdot]\):
\[
E[R | F[\xi] > \theta] = C
\]
Here, \(\theta\) is a threshold level of predictive accuracy, and \(F\) transforms environmental signals into actionable forecasts. Knight tacitly assumes a Gaussian likelihood model \(p(\xi)\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Under this model, the probability of correct prediction exceeds \(\theta\) scales as \(erfc((\mu - \theta)/\sqrt{2}\sigma^2))\).

Critical breakdown: The central limit theorem implies that \(F[\xi]\) converges to normality only in large samples. For small-scale innovation (e.g., a single startup), this approximation collapses, generating power-law tails in profit distributions that Knight's linear-cost framework cannot capture.

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial N} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log(P_i) \right) = 0
\]
where \(P_i\) is the profit of firm \(i\). In statics, \(N^* \rightarrow \infty\) ensures \(\Pi/N \rightarrow 0\). In non-statics, \(N^*\) depends on the volatility of \(\xi(t)\); Knight neglects this dependence, introducing bias.
