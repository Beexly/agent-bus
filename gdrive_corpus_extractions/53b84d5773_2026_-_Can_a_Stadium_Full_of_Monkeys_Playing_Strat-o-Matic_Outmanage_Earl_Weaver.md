Multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit. The paper’s central problem is not whether baseball outcomes are measurable; it is whether a manager-value estimator can be isolated from player talent, front-office composition, season-level randomness, and retrospective selection. The first hop is therefore construct validity: the paper states that baseball has player metrics “akin to WAR” but no manager equivalent, because traditional manager metrics conflate managerial proficiency with team skill [PAGE 2]. The second hop is model construction: the paper tests three candidate models—Pythagorean Expectation (PE), manager-WAR over team WAR (mWAR), and a Jamesian forecast model (JF)—then combines the survivors into a Hybrid Model [PAGE 6, PAGE 19-20]. The third hop is null construction: for PE, it rebuilds every manager career at the game level and replays games 1,000 times with negative-binomial run draws anchored on season means and league run-scoring dispersion [PAGE 9-10; PAGE 43]. For mWAR and JF, it uses a parametric bootstrap that removes the manager fixed effect, generates 1,000 replicate careers from binomial win draws, and refits the full model [PAGE 15, PAGE 18]. The fourth hop is inferential calibration: observed z-statistics are compared with a theoretical null and a simulated empirical null, then evaluated with false discovery rate (FDR) logic [PAGE 11, PAGE 15, PAGE 19]. The fifth hop is convergent validation: because no direct observation of managerial skill is possible, mWAR and JF are treated as independent indicators of a latent trait; Kendall’s W is reported as 0.72 and described as “substantial agreement” [PAGE 21]. The sixth hop is Bayesian shrinkage: the Hybrid Estimator uses an empirical-null prior centered at zero effect, with between-manager variance τ², and a manager’s model score plus standard error to form a posterior; the MAP is expressed as w162, or wins per 162 games versus an average manager [PAGE 23-24]. The seventh hop is practical decision theory: because the individual-versus-population gap means only 34 of 495 managers clear the |w162| > 2 region at the individual level while the expected sample-wide count is 162 [PAGE 27-28], the paper adds a provisional season estimator and an asymmetric loss function that can move borderline managers across ±2 thresholds [PAGE 31-36; PAGE 54].

The gray-zone audit is where the paper needs the most caution. First, PE is rejected as internally invalid: “The rate of ‘false’ non-nulls at z > 1.96 was identified as 100%, and 75% on average across both extreme tails” [PAGE 11]. But mWAR is only modestly supportive: “Fifty-five of the 512 members of the manager sample—approximately 11%—generated impacts ... that met or exceeded |z| = 1.96,” yet “the FDR rate for false nulls in the tail regions overall was 32%” [PAGE 15]. JF is stronger: “A total of 133 of the 499 managers in the sample—some 27%—recorded |z| ≥ 1.96,” and “the FDR estimated proportion of non-null manager scores in the tail regions was over 80%” [PAGE 18-19]. The Hybrid Model reports “Forty-eight of the 495-member observed sample of managers—approximately 9.6%—generated impacts that met or exceed that |z| = 1.96 level,” with a local FDR of 0.36, implying a 64% average probability of a genuine non-null at that tail [PAGE 21]. These are not interchangeable claims: PE says the Pythagorean benchmark is a near-constraint; mWAR says the WAR-only residual is noisy and possibly confounded; JF and Hybrid say a signal survives. Second, the paper’s causal language is observational: “We cannot—and never will be able to—observe managerial influence directly,” and the defense is “practical reason” plus convergent validation [PAGE 21]. Third, the Bayesian posterior is only as good as τ², the empirical null, and the measurement-error estimates; a mis-specified null or non-random missingness can make the shrinkage look more decisive than it is. Fourth, the source contains arithmetic and labeling anomalies: the expected sample-wide count is stated as “162: 85 with w162 ≤ -2 and 75 with w162 ≥ 2” [PAGE 28], but 85 + 75 = 160; the learning-curve section says eight seasons is “131” games [PAGE 30], which is implausible against 162 games per season; and the conclusion names “Craig Vogt” while the tables identify Stephen Vogt [PAGE 37; PAGE 25]. These do not destroy the method, but they require audit before using the estimator operationally. Fifth, the active-manager examples and Hall of Fame table show retrocausal re-ranking: Horowitz’s 1994 PH concluded Earl Weaver was the best manager, but the Hybrid Estimator gives Weaver w162 = -0.3 with HDI (-2.8, 2.2) and raw data -0.5 [PAGE 46]. That inversion is exactly why the paper’s “convergent” strategy must be read as defeasible, not final.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The extracted PDF text does not preserve the displayed equations (3)-(6) as glyphs; the following is the minimal formal reconstruction from the prose, with units and assumptions stated explicitly. Let \(i\) index managers and \(t\) index seasons. Let \(p_{it}=W_{it}/g_{it}\in[0,1]\) be a season winning proportion, where \(W_{it}\) is wins and \(g_{it}\) is games managed; \(w_{it}\) is the corresponding per-game win probability or linear-probability outcome. The source describes the PE model as fitting “a single model to all managers” with \(WP_{it}\) as the season winning percentage, \(\beta_1\) as the sample-wide Pythagorean Expectation effect, and \(\mu_i\) as the manager fixed effect [PAGE 8-9]. Thus, at the level of the prose:
\[
\text{PE: } WP_{it}=\mu_i+\beta_1\,\mathrm{PE}_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},
\]
where \(\mathrm{PE}_{it}\) is the Pythagorean benchmark, dimensionless; \(\beta_1\) is a change in season winning proportion per unit of PE; \(\mu_i\) is a manager’s marginal season winning-proportion effect after partialling out PE; and \(\varepsilon_{it}\) is residual variation. The classical Pythagorean form referenced by the paper is
\[
WP=\frac{RS^2}{RS^2+RA^2},
\]
with runs scored \(RS\) and runs allowed \(RA\) in runs, and \(WP\) dimensionless [PAGE 3-4]. The source reports that this formula “explains nearly 90% of the season-by-season variance in MLB team records” [PAGE 4].

For mWAR, the paper states: “The first predictor, \(\beta_1zWPG\), is the season-standardized team WAR per game,” and “The second fixed-effect predictor, \(\mu_i\), is again that manager’s career marginal impact on the probability of winning” [PAGE 14]. The prose form is
\[
\text{mWAR: } w_{it}=\mu_i+\beta_1\,zWPG_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},
\]
where \(zWPG_{it}\) is team WAR per game standardized within season, dimensionless; \(\beta_1\) is a change in per-game win probability per one-standard-deviation change in team WAR per game; and \(\mu_i\) is in per-game win-probability units. For JF:
\[
\text{JF: } w_{it}=\mu_i+\beta_1\,zEXWP_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},
\]
where \(zEXWP_{it}\) is the standardized AR(3) pre-season forecast winning percentage, dimensionless; the paper reports out-of-sample \(R^2=0.38\) versus 0.36 for James’s formula and RMSE 10.08 versus 10.22 wins per 162 games [PAGE 17]. For the Hybrid Model:
\[
\text{Hybrid: } w_{it}=\mu_i+\beta_1\,zWPG_{it}+\beta_2\,zEXWP_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},
\]
where the paper says the fixed-effect predictor “remains the manager’s career marginal impact on the probability of winning—this time after the sample-wide impact of both zWPG and zEXWP ... are controlled for” [PAGE 20]. The conversion to the informative unit is
\[
w162_i = 162\,\widehat{\Delta p}_i,
\]
where \(w162_i\) is wins per 162 games versus an average manager, and \(\widehat{\Delta p}_i\) is a marginal per-game win-probability effect; this is the paper’s stated interpretation of \(w162\) as “the number of additional season wins (positive or negative)” [PAGE 23]. The paper also uses a consequence threshold of “± 0.012 marginal-win probability—the equivalent .488 and .512 for a .500 team, or 2 wins or losses in a conventional 162 game schedule” [PAGE 24].

Null formation is a second operator. For PE, the appendix specifies game-level replays with runs drawn from a negative binomial centered on manager-specific season means, with dispersion set to the league run-scoring standard deviation for the season: the paper says each game was “replayed—1,000 times—using random draws of runs scored and allowed anchored on those means,” with draws “taken from a negative binomial distribution ... constructed to reproduce the standard deviation of game-level run scoring league wide for the season in question” [PAGE 9-10]; the appendix formalizes this as “App.1” and says ties were resolved randomly [PAGE 43]. For mWAR and JF, the null is a parametric bootstrap: fit the model without \(\mu_i\), draw season wins from a binomial distribution pegged to the model-estimated winning percentage, then refit the full model to 1,000 replicate careers [PAGE 15, PAGE 18]. The Hybrid null repeats that process with both predictors [PAGE 21]. FDR then estimates the probability that an observed tail value is a null: the paper reports PE’s false non-null rate at \(z>1.96\) as 100%, with 75% average across tails [PAGE 11]; mWAR’s tail FDR as 32% [PAGE 15]; JF’s non-null tail proportion as over 80% [PAGE 19]; and Hybrid local FDR at \(|z|\ge1.96\) as 0.36 [PAGE 21].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes. (1) If \(\tau^2=0\), the prior for manager skill collapses to a point mass at zero; every posterior MAP is zero and individual differences are not identifiable. (2) If \(\tau^2\to\infty\), the prior is effectively flat and shrinkage vanishes; the estimator becomes close to the raw model score and inherits its measurement error and confounding. (3) If the standard error of a manager’s model score goes to infinity, the posterior is dominated by the prior and w162 tends to zero. (4) If \(g_{it}\to0\), the binomial or proportion outcome is unstable; the paper excludes managers with “15 games or fewer” [PAGE 6]. (5) If \(zWPG\) and \(zEXWP\) are perfectly collinear, \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) are not separately identified; the reported Hybrid \(R^2=0.75\) would not rescue that. (6) If the empirical null is mis-specified—wrong run distribution, wrong dispersion, wrong league era, non-random missing game logs—FDR and posterior shrinkage are invalid; the paper’s Weibull robustness check is relevant because it says “did not meaningfully differ” from the negative binomial result [PAGE 43-44], but that is a check, not a proof. (7) Non-random inclusion is a breakdown regime: the 2025 sample lacks complete game logs for PE [PAGE 15 footnote], and the JF sample is smaller because it excludes managers whose tenures began and ended in the first two seasons of a franchise [PAGE 18 footnote]. (8) The individual-versus-population gap is not a singularity but an identifiability limit: only 34 of 495 managers have career scores meeting \(|w162|>2\), while the expected sample-wide count is stated as 162 [PAGE 27-28]. The source’s “85 + 75 = 162” arithmetic is inconsistent, so the population-incidence number should be treated as approximate, not exact.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is retrocausal because the new estimator reorders the past. The oldest layer is Bill James’s Pythagorean Expectation: runs scored and allowed imply an expected winning percentage, and deviations become candidate manager effects [PAGE 3-4]. Horowitz (1994) converted that logic into PH, treating a manager’s career deviation from Pythagorean expectation as evidence of value; the paper says Horowitz concluded that “Earl Weaver, with a career PH of 1.128, is the best manager of all time” [PAGE 4]. The next layer is the critics’ counter-genealogy: Ruggiero et al. and others argued that Pythagorean deviations are random draws from a fixed distribution, so the manager is “just along for the random-variable ride” [PAGE 5]. Horowitz’s reply was that better managers produce lower-variance run scoring, a claim the paper tests rather than assumes [PAGE 5]. The third layer is value-added and forecasting: Jaffe, Kahn, Singell, Smart/Winfree/Wolf, Data Envelopment Analysis, and James’s three-season forecast model all try to isolate manager value from team quality, but the paper faults them for weak player controls, no measurement-error model, or assigning the entire residual to the manager [PAGE 5-6]. The fourth layer is modern empirical Bayes: Efron’s empirical nulls, local FDR, parametric bootstrap, Stein shrinkage, and the two-groups model supply the machinery that turns manager scores into posterior estimates [PAGE 23, PAGE 39-40 references]. The fifth layer is convergent validation from Campbell and Fiske: independent instruments that agree are more likely to measure the same latent trait than to be fortuitously correlated [PAGE 7, PAGE 20-21]. The sixth layer is decision theory: Kruschke’s ROPE, Berger’s loss functions, and Elliott/Timmermann’s asymmetric forecast loss turn a statistical estimate into a retention rule [PAGE 27, PAGE 34-36].

The retrocausal inversion is stark. Horowitz’s PH canonized Weaver; the Hybrid Estimator’s Hall of Fame table gives Weaver \(w162=-0.3\), HDI \((-2.8,2.2)\), raw data \(-0.5\) [PAGE 46]. John McGraw is first with \(w162=5.1\), HDI \((3.0,7.2)\), data \(7.1\); Frank Chance is \(4.4\); Joe McCarthy \(3.2\); Casey Stengel \(2.5\); Connie Mack is \(-1.3\) [PAGE 25, PAGE 46]. These are not merely new rankings; they reorganize the causal story of twentieth-century managing. The multi-week traps follow. First, an analyst who sees a single-season \(w162_p\) of +2.0 or +2.6—Stephen Vogt in 2024 and 2025—may overread it as stable skill, but the paper’s own learning curve says the median games to recognize \(|w162|\ge2\) is 810, roughly five seasons [PAGE 30, PAGE 33]. Second, the paper says the median games to determine that a manager has 70% posterior mass at that level is “about eight seasons worth—131” [PAGE 30]; the “131” is internally inconsistent with eight 162-game seasons, so the operational clock should be treated as roughly eight seasons, not 131 games. Third, the paper warns that Hall of Fame and active-manager lists are summaries, not verdicts: “Mangers of consequence—positive and negative—are found over all MLB time periods,” including active managers [PAGE 37]. Fourth, because the Hybrid Estimator is retrospective and shrinks heavily, early leaderboards can generate a false crisis narrative around a manager who is merely average; the paper explicitly says about 72% of managers have w162s between -1.0 and 1.0 and 60% between -0.5 and 0.5 [PAGE 24]. The historical lesson is that each generation’s manager metric becomes the next generation’s primitive statistic: PH was an advance over career wins, mWAR/JF are an advance over PH, and the Hybrid Estimator is an advance over raw mWAR/JF—but none is a direct observation of managerial skill.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that managers, front offices, media, and fans all model one another while using the same noisy signal. “Crisis habit regression” is the organizational tendency to revert to career wins, reputation, or a single-season leaderboard when uncertainty is high. The paper gives the counter-trap: the full Hybrid Estimator is deliberately slow because it estimates a stable latent \(\theta_i\), while the provisional Estimator_p is fast but “innocent of any prior and devoid of any posterior” [PAGE 31]. If a front office treats Estimator_p as a stable skill measure, it will overreact to a 20% residual allocation that the paper itself says is “much more so” modest than systems assigning the entire residual to managers [PAGE 32]. The recursive twist is that managers know they are being evaluated; if w162_p becomes a public leaderboard, managers may optimize for visible residual wins, while front offices may optimize for the asymmetric loss function that treats false retention of a bad manager as more costly than premature release. The paper’s asymmetric-loss argument is explicitly recursive: “holding on too long to a potentially poor manager is more costly than releasing and replacing him prematurely,” while “prematurely replacing a potentially good manger is worse than holding on to him too long” [PAGE 34]. That is a theory of mind about how evaluators weigh type I and type II errors, not a property of the manager alone.

The clock is the binding constraint. The paper’s learning curve says the median games to recognize \(|w162|\ge2\) is 810, roughly five seasons, and the 70% posterior-mass benchmark is about eight seasons [PAGE 30]. Therefore a rational front office should not treat a two-season w162_p as a career verdict; it should use it as a provisional update, then switch to the full Estimator “as soon as feasible” [PAGE 34]. The counter-trap is that the media and fan ecosystem reward immediate rankings; the paper concedes that Estimator_p “might actually furnish a better fit than the full Estimator for pure entertainment assessments” [PAGE 33-34]. That concession is important because it separates two recursive games: the decision game, where asymmetric loss and posterior uncertainty should dominate, and the attention game, where season leaderboards dominate. A manager who internalizes the attention game may manage for narrative; a front office that internalizes the decision game should pre-commit to a loss function and a minimum-games rule before the season starts. The paper’s ROPE framework supports that pre-commitment: “Only 34 of the managers in the 495-member sample—approximately 7%—have career scores that meet” the \(|w162|>2\) threshold, 16 positive and 18 negative [PAGE 27]. The recursive danger is to mistake the 7% individually identified for the 162 expected sample-wide cases [PAGE 28], then either over-punish the unidentified or over-reward the merely lucky. The paper’s own answer is to integrate over each manager’s posterior probability of exceeding the ROPE, not to treat the MAP as a verdict [PAGE 28].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The paper does not provide physical, biomechanical, or weather veto proof. Its simulation layer is explicitly run-scoring only: PE replays games with negative-binomial draws “anchored on those means” and calibrated to “the standard deviation of game-level run scoring league wide for the season in question” [PAGE 9-10]. The appendix repeats that the simulated null was created “at the game level because run-scoring is the random process that determines how closely a team’s record cleaves to the Pythagorean Expectation” [PAGE 43]. There is no weather covariate, no park factor, no temperature, no wind, no travel, no injury, no pitch-count, no biomechanical load, and no equipment variable in the reported models. The mWAR layer controls “season-standardized team WAR per game” [PAGE 14], and the JF layer controls an AR(3) forecast [PAGE 17]; the Hybrid controls both [PAGE 20]. That is a statistical control for player quality and past team performance, not a physical or biomechanical mechanism.

The cross-layer veto therefore has a precise form: if a physical, biomechanical, or weather process changes the distribution of runs or wins beyond what \(zWPG\), \(zEXWP\), and the season-level run-scoring dispersion capture, and if that process is correlated with a manager’s tenure, then \(\mu_i\) can absorb it. The paper’s own defense is only probabilistic: “When past performance and observed player achievement are simultaneously controlled for, these manager-independent effects largely cancel out” [PAGE 20]. That is not proof against omitted weather or injury effects; it is an assumption of conditional independence. A physical-layer veto would require showing that the omitted variable is both outcome-relevant and tenure-correlated. A biomechanical veto would require showing that team WAR fails to capture availability or performance load in a way that tracks the manager. A weather veto would require showing that the negative-binomial null’s season-wide dispersion is wrong in a manager-specific way. The paper’s Weibull robustness check says the alternative run distribution “did not meaningfully differ” from the negative binomial result [PAGE 43-44], but that is a distributional robustness check, not a weather or biomechanical proof. The correct adjudication is therefore: the Hybrid Estimator is a latent-variable observational estimator; it cannot certify a physical causal pathway, and no cross-layer physical/biomechanical/weather claim should be attributed to it. If such a claim is needed, the model must add the relevant covariates or use a design that randomizes managerial assignment conditional on those layers. Without that, the only defensible conclusion is “manager-associated residual after statistical controls,” not “manager caused wins through a verified physical mechanism.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR-style verifiable counterfactuals for this paper should be pre-registered against the paper’s own observables. Three are sufficient.

1. PE-null falsification. Reconstruct the PE Monte Carlo exactly as described: for each manager-career season, draw runs scored and allowed from a negative binomial with means equal to the observed per-game means and dispersion calibrated to the league run-scoring standard deviation for that season, replay 1,000 careers, refit the PE model, and compute the number of \(|z|\ge1.96\) careers plus the FDR at \(z>1.96\) [PAGE 9-10, PAGE 43]. Prediction: the observed count should be near 29 of 507, the null mean near 25, and the PE false non-null rate at \(z>1.96\) should be near 100%, with 75% average across tails; Storey pFDR should be near 0.88 and Benjamini-Hochberg q near 0.91 at \(p\le0.05\) [PAGE 10-11]. Veto condition: if the calibrated observed PE tail is below the 95th percentile of the simulated null, or if the local FDR at \(z>1.96\) is below 0.10, the claim that PE transmits no genuine manager signal is falsified.

2. Hybrid concordance and tail-exceedance test. Independently permute manager labels in the mWAR and JF score vectors, then recompute Kendall’s W and the Hybrid \(|z|\ge1.96\) count. Prediction: the observed Kendall’s W should be about 0.72, described as “substantial agreement” [PAGE 21]; the observed Hybrid count should be 48 of 495, while the simulated null average is 24 and none of 1,000 simulations reaches 48, with only 4 reaching 40 or more and a maximum of 45 [PAGE 21]. Veto condition: if label-permuted concordance reaches 0.60 or higher, or if 5% or more of null simulations reach 48 or more, the convergent-validation claim is vetoed. A stricter secondary prediction: the Hybrid local FDR at \(|z|\ge1.96\) should be 0.36, meaning a 64% average probability of a genuine non-null [PAGE 21]; if the local FDR is above 0.60, the “genuine signal” interpretation is weakened.

3. Asymmetric-loss directional test. Implement the appendix’s asymmetric loss with thresholds \(s=-2\), \(t=2\), and weights \(w_1=w_2=2\), minimizing expected posterior loss rather than MSE [PAGE 53-54]. Prediction: adjustments must be unidirectional relative to the thresholds. Managers with MSE MAPs just below \(+2\) may move toward or above \(+2\), but managers with MSE MAPs already above \(+2\) must never move downward; managers with MSE MAPs just above \(-2\) may move toward or below \(-2\), but managers already below \(-2\) must never move upward [PAGE 54]. The appendix’s table gives concrete predictions: Stephen Vogt should move from 2.1 to 2.5, Fredi González from 2.0 to 2.4, John Gibbons from -2.9 to -3.1, and Jimmy McAleer from -3.4 to -3.7, while John McGraw remains 5.1 [PAGE 56]. Veto condition: any manager with MSE MAP above \(+2\) who is adjusted downward, any manager below \(-2\) who is adjusted upward, or any high-precision manager whose adjustment exceeds the low-precision managers’ adjustment in the same direction would falsify the paper’s stated loss-function mechanics. A second prediction is that the adjustment magnitude should scale with posterior variance and proximity to the threshold: the appendix reports larger adjustments for higher-variance managers near the boundary [PAGE 54, PAGE 56]. If adjustment magnitude is independent of variance, the asymmetric-loss implementation is not behaving as claimed.
