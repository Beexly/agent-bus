\[

Define the detection-information operator \(\mathcal{I}\):
\[
I_{\text{detect}}(t)=H(S_t)-H(S_t\mid M_t),\qquad [I]=\mathrm{bits},
\]
where \(S_t\) is symptom state and \(M_t\) is medical measurement. The source says “Education is still lacking” [PAGE 91] and that “subjective symptoms like headaches or dizziness often go unreported” [PAGE 44]. It also calls for a “centralized medical database” [PAGE 44]. Thus detection is not automatic; \(I_{\text{detect}}\to0\) is a breakdown regime in which exposure continues despite available knowledge.

Breakdown and singularity regimes: (i) \(E(t)\to\infty\) makes \(P(T\le t)\to1\), but the model becomes clinically invalid because competing causes and ceiling effects dominate. (ii) \(\beta\to\infty\) is singular; no finite data in the source calibrate such a limit. (iii) \(I_{\text{detect}}\to0\) is the “silent progression” regime supported by underreporting [PAGE 44]. (iv) The model is not calibrated to joules, radians per second squared, or tau burden; the source gives no such measurements. (v) The source’s own history warns against overclaiming: “The brain cannot be trained for this purpose, and, for heaven's sake, one should never try such an experiment” [PAGE 81]. That is a mechanistic caution, not an equation.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogical chain begins with Martland’s 1928 “Punch Drunk” work, which the source says “established the term in formal medical discourse” [PAGE 13] and described “slurred speech, memory loss, and impaired motor skills” [PAGE 13]. It then moves through Del Fontaine’s 1935 case, where “punch-drunk” was invoked at trial [PAGE 17], and through the later term “dementia pugilistica,” which “was introduced as a more respectful term” [PAGE 17]. Critchley’s work is presented as a nomenclature and recognition step: he “coined terms like ‘chronic progressive traumatic encephalopathy’” [PAGE 21]. Roberts’s 224-boxer study [PAGE 26] and the Q&A figure “17% of the boxers he examined had dementia pugilistica” [PAGE 127] belong to the epidemiological layer. Corsellis’s brain collection belongs to the pathological layer: his autopsy findings “revealed structural changes in the brain correlating with personality alterations and emotional disturbances” [PAGE 27]. The modern CTE layer is then retroactively attached to older cases: Paul Pender’s brain donation revealed “CTE instead of Alzheimer's” [PAGE 36], and McKee’s research found patterns “consistent with CTE across multiple boxers” [PAGE 38]. The source itself summarizes the renaming: “CTE, formerly known as ‘Dementia Pugilistica’ and ‘Punch Drunk Syndrome’” [PAGE 3].

The retrocausal trap is that modern labels can make earlier evidence look more precise than it was. The source says “CTE carries less stigma than its predecessor terms” [PAGE 57], which is a social fact, not a diagnostic one. A second trap is the short-window inference: acute recovery after a bout can create false reassurance, while the relevant horizon is longitudinal. The source notes “annual evaluations planned for over a decade” [PAGE 41] and that McKee’s findings challenged “the prevailing belief that significant brain deterioration could not occur decades after retirement” [PAGE 142]. A third trap is survivorship: the celebrated cases—Ali, Quarry, Ward, Graham—are visible, but the source also describes “38 individuals in urgent need” of care [PAGE 71] and fighters who “fall through the net” [PAGE 101]. Multi-week observation is therefore inadequate; the minimum credible audit window is multi-year, and the source’s own design uses annual MRI and cognitive testing [PAGE 41]. The genealogy does not prove a single continuous disease entity; it shows a historical sequence of recognition, renaming, and partial institutional uptake.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when stress collapses behavior to the most rehearsed identity script. The source’s script is explicit: “Real fighters don't quit. In fact, that's the dirtiest word in the sport” [PAGE 103]. Under that script, a fighter who admits dizziness risks being seen as weak, so the rational short-run move is concealment. The source says fighters are “discouraged from admitting vulnerability” [PAGE 44] and that “It's a taboo subject” [PAGE 100]. The recursive belief is: the fighter believes the trainer wants toughness; the trainer believes the fighter wants to continue; the commission believes the promoter will resist a stoppage; the doctor fears being blamed for an early stoppage. This is a nested belief trap, not a single bad decision. The counter-trap is that safety rules can intensify concealment if they are perceived as punitive. A fighter who thinks an honest symptom report will end his purse may hide the symptom, which defeats the rule’s purpose.

The clock layer is retirement timing. The source says “The legs are the first thing to go in a fighter” [PAGE 85], and it warns that telling a fighter to retire requires reasons: “when you're going to tell someone to retire and they ask why, you have to be able to give them reasons” [PAGE 92]. The clock is not chronological age alone; it is cumulative exposure, knockout history, sparring load, and post-fight recovery. The source’s frequency example—one fight per year versus three per year [PAGE 88–89]—makes the clock a variable-rate process. A good recursive model tracks three clocks simultaneously: biological recovery, career opportunity, and institutional patience. The counter-trap is “early stoppage” being socially punished: “You'd rather have the fight stopped a second too early rather than a second too late” [PAGE 91] is medically sensible but institutionally costly. The source also states the endgame bluntly: “When you are at the end of your career, there is no one there to protect you” [PAGE 91–92]. That is the recursive failure: each actor expects another actor to protect the fighter, and the expectation circulates until the exposure has already occurred.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical veto: the source does not provide impact thresholds, but it does provide a hard mechanistic caution: “The brain cannot be trained for this purpose, and, for heaven's sake, one should never try such an experiment” [PAGE 81]. A simple inertial-stress proxy is:
\[
\sigma(t)\approx \rho\,r\,\alpha(t),\qquad [\sigma]=\mathrm{Pa},\;[\rho]=\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},\;[r]=\mathrm{m},\;[\alpha]=\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}},
\]
where rotational acceleration \(\alpha\) is more relevant to shear than linear acceleration alone. A biomechanical veto would require \(\sigma(t)\) or accumulated strain to fall below tolerance for all relevant impacts; the source does not establish that any glove, headgear, or round change achieves this. It says headgear was discussed but “often dismissed” [PAGE 18], and later calls for “re-evaluating boxing gloves, rounds, and headgear” [PAGE 45]. It also records a practical proposal: “they should wear twenty-four-ounce gloves” [PAGE 101]. Those are exposure-reduction proposals, not proof of a biomechanical veto.

Institutional veto: a safety intervention is vetoed if it is not enforced. The source says injured fighters can “seek licenses elsewhere” [PAGE 48] and that “The lack of one overall federation puts a massive roadblock up...” [PAGE 96]. A centralized database would be a detection veto only if it is actually queried before licensing; otherwise it is information without force. The source’s “When in doubt, sit it out” [PAGE 91] is a valid clinical veto rule, but its causal effect depends on ringside authority and post-fight follow-up.

Weather-layer veto: no provided page documents weather, temperature, altitude, or atmospheric conditions as a causal variable in boxing brain injury. A meteorological veto is therefore unsupported and cannot falsify the core biomechanical pathway. The correct adjudication is: physical layer supports the mechanism; institutional layer can block or amplify it; weather layer has no evidentiary standing in this source. The only defensible cross-layer conclusion is that reducing exposure and improving detection are necessary but not sufficient unless the institutional layer enforces them.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — early medical stoppage veto. Trigger: a fighter shows concussion signs such as headache, dizziness, or unsteadiness. Veto: ringside physician stops the bout rather than allowing further rounds. The source supports the trigger because “subjective symptoms like headaches or dizziness often go unreported” [PAGE 44] and “Education is still lacking” [PAGE 91]. Prediction: a stoppage rule reduces subsequent impact count and lowers \(E(t)\) in the current bout; verifiable by round-by-round video, medical records, and the absence of further impacts after the trigger. Falsifier: if stoppage decisions are systematically overridden or if fighters conceal symptoms at the same rate, the predicted exposure reduction fails.

Scenario 2 — centralized database and license veto. Trigger: a fighter with documented impairment applies for a license in a new jurisdiction. Veto: the database flags the prior record and the license is denied or conditioned on neurological review. The source calls for a “centralized medical database” [PAGE 44] and warns that the current system “allows injured fighters to seek licenses elsewhere” [PAGE 48]. Prediction: license shopping falls, and fighters with prior impairment have fewer subsequent bouts; verifiable by database audit, commission licensing logs, and bout counts before versus after the policy. Falsifier: if jurisdictions do not query the database or if exemptions restore access, the predicted reduction fails.

Scenario 3 — sparring-load and glove veto. Trigger: a training camp exceeds a predefined sparring-round cap or uses competition-weight gloves for high-impact sparring. Veto: the camp must reduce sparring frequency or use larger gloves. The source says “the real threat often comes from sparring rather than actual matches” [PAGE 69], that Jeffries “estimates having absorbed tens of thousands of head impacts in his career” [PAGE 69], and that Roach favors “reducing sparring frequency while still recognizing its necessity” [PAGE 51]. Prediction: lower sparring exposure reduces \(E(t)\) and slows detectable MRI change over annual evaluations [PAGE 41]; verifiable by training logs, glove weight records, and longitudinal imaging. Falsifier: if sparring load is underreported or if fighters compensate with harder unsupervised sessions, the predicted exposure reduction fails. These three vetoes are verifiable because each has a documented trigger, a recorded action, and a measurable exposure or licensing outcome; none requires an unstated clinical threshold from the source.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
