Formal operators, LaTeX equations, units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes, sourced to [PAGE 329].

Variables and units (all quantities as tabulated on [PAGE 329]):
- M ∈ {0, 1, 2, ...}: number of preceding reinforcements, dimensionless count [Column I].
- sEB_M: effective reaction potential after M reinforcements, Hull reaction-potential units (h.u.) [Column II; title notation sER].
- sLR: reaction threshold, h.u. [Column III subtrahend].
- σ₀ = 10 h.u.: standard deviation of the oscillation of the reaction potential, constant across rows [Column IV header].
- z_M: dimensionless standard-deviation score, z_M = (sEB_M − sLR)/σ₀ [Column IV].
- p_M: probability of reaction evocation, dimensionless, 0 ≤ p_M ≤ 1 [Column V].

Verbatim basis [PAGE 329]: Table 10 derives "THE PROB-ABILITY OF REACTION EVOCATION AS A JOLNT FUNCTION OF THE REACTION THRESHOLD, THE STRENGTH OF EFFECTIVE REACTION POTENTIAL (sER), AND THE MAGNITUDE OF THE OSCILLATION OF THE REACTION POTENTIAL," and its values "PRESUPPOSE AN UNLIMITED SAMPLE OF HOMOGENEOUS BEHAVIOR." Column V is "Derived from Table 9 and Column IV," identifying Table 9 as the standard normal cumulative integral.

Core derivation:
Let the instantaneous reaction potential be a Gaussian random variable
X_M ∼ 𝒩(sEB_M, σ₀²), σ₀ = 10 h.u. (1)

Evocation is evoked iff the instantaneous potential exceeds threshold:
Evocation ⇔ X_M > sLR. (2)

Therefore
p_M = Pr(X_M > sLR) = 1 − Φ((sLR − sEB_M)/σ₀) = Φ(z_M), (3)
z_M = (sEB_M − sLR)/σ₀, (4)

where Φ is the standard normal CDF (Table 9 on [PAGE 329] context):
Φ(z) = (1/√(2π)) ∫_{−∞}^{z} exp(−t²/2) dt, dimensionless. (5)

Equivalently, p_M is the upper-tail area above sLR under a normal curve centered at sEB_M with spread σ₀.

Assumptions (explicit or implicit on [PAGE 329]):
A1. The oscillation of reaction potential is Gaussian with fixed σ₀ = 10 h.u.
A2. σ₀ is invariant across M (homoscedastic oscillation).
A3. sLR is fixed and does not drift with M, fatigue, or drive.
A4. The sample of behavior is unlimited and homogeneous (verbatim caveat, [PAGE 329]).
A5. Each row is treated as a stationary condition despite M indexing learning history.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
R1 (σ₀ → 0⁺): p_M collapses to a Heaviside step, p_M → H(sEB_M − sLR). At the measure-zero boundary sEB_M = sLR exactly, p is convention-dependent (0, 1/2, or 1); the Gaussian density is singular there and Φ is not defined by a unique value without an additional tie-breaking rule.
R2 (σ₀ → ∞): p_M → 1/2 for all finite sEB_M − sLR. The model loses all discriminative information (maximum entropy, 1 bit) because the oscillation swamps the potential–threshold gap.
R3 (Finite N): The [PAGE 329] caveat limits validity to an unlimited sample. With finite N, the observed proportion ṗ has binomial sampling variance Var(ṗ) = p(1−p)/N, so the tabulated p values are asymptotic only and will not be matched exactly by any finite experiment.
R4 (Nonstationarity): Because M increments habit strength sHr (and Hull's broader system lets drive D and reactive inhibition s_IR drift), the homogeneity assumption A4 fails across rows; applying one fixed σ₀ and sLR to a multi-trial sequence mis-specifies the data-generating process.
R5 (Non-Gaussian oscillation): If the true oscillation is skewed, bounded, or heavy-tailed, the Φ(z_M) mapping from Column IV to Column V is invalid regardless of correct z-scores.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage of the Table 10 formalism, and the multi-week traps latent in its static presentation.

Lineage. Hull's Principles of Behavior (1943) is a hypothetico-deductive behavior system. Its immediate psychological ancestry runs through Thorndike's Law of Effect, Pavlov's conditioned reflex, and Watsonian behaviorism, with Hull's own preceding work on drive and habit integration. The mathematical apparatus of Table 10 is older and distinct: the Gaussian error function and the normal curve trace to Gauss, Laplace, and de Moivre, passed through Quetelet and Galton's social physics, then into psychophysics via Fechner and Weber, where sensory threshold itself was modeled as a probabilistic boundary rather than a hard line. Yerkes-Dodson's drive–performance inversion supplies the motivational context in which Hull embeds sER. Table 9, invoked on [PAGE 329] to convert Column IV into Column V, is a standard normal probability integral — direct descent from the Laplace–Gauss error-function tradition. Hull's innovation is behavioral: he grafts that error function onto a motive–habit–threshold architecture so that the probability of an overt act becomes a computable integral over an unobserved oscillation.

Multi-week traps. Table 10 is a static cross-section: one σ₀ = 10, one sLR, one sequence of M, and a p for each row. Multi-week application introduces traps the page does not flag. First, sHr accumulates across reinforcements spread over days or weeks; M in Column I is not a calendar variable, so a row labeled M = 10 could represent one intensive session or ten weeks of spaced training, with different consolidation states. Second, reactive inhibition s_IR builds within a session and recovers over hours to days; ignoring its multi-day decay makes late-session sEB_M overestimated when the table is naively extended across weeks. Third, drive D fluctuates circadianly and with deprivation state across weeks; holding D constant across rows (implicit in fixed sEB_M per M) is a trap if the subject's deprivation or circadian phase changes between weekly tests. Fourth, sLR itself may shift with practice or fatigue, violating A3 over longitudinal spans. Fifth, because p hits the ceiling (1.000) at high z on [PAGE 329], multi-week ceilings in habit strength cannot be distinguished from Gaussian rounding error without re-estimating σ₀ per week. Any inference that treats a single Table 10 slice as stable across weeks confounds measurement granularity with behavioral change.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, the counter-trap, and the clock.

First-order modeling. An observer using [PAGE 329] represents the subject's behavior as p_M = Φ((sEB_M − sLR)/σ₀). The observer infers sEB_M from M (reinforcement history), fixes sLR and σ₀ = 10, and predicts the probability that response X is evoked on the next trial. This is a standard forward model: potential minus threshold, scaled by oscillation noise, passed through the normal CDF.

Recursive (second-order) layer. Under crisis, drive D rises and the habit hierarchy sharpens: the response with the largest accumulated sHr — hence the largest sEB_M — commands the highest p_M. Hullian crisis regression is therefore not random panic but a deterministic skew toward the dominant habit. Recursively, each agent models the other as subject to this same regression: I expect you to emit your strongest habit under pressure; you expect me to do the same; we both pre-position accordingly. The model predicts convergence on the highest-sHr response by both parties under mutual crisis, because each p_M is monotonically increasing in sEB_M (holding sLR and σ₀ fixed).

The counter-trap. The trap is assuming crisis elicits rational optimization or novel problem solving. Hull's formalism says the opposite: elevated activation releases the strongest pre-existing habit. The counter-trap for an adversary is therefore to destabilize the sHr hierarchy itself — vary reinforcement contingencies so that no single response dominates sEB_M — which flattens the z_M distribution across responses and makes p diffuse rather than peaked. If all candidate responses have similar sEB_M relative to sLR, then z_M values cluster and p_M values bunch near the center of Φ, removing predictability. This is a model-derived counter-strategy, not an ad hoc one.

The clock. Time enters Table 10 in three distinct clocks that the page does not unify. (i) The trial clock: M increments discretely with each reinforcement, so p_M changes trial-by-trial as sEB_M grows; this is a reinforcement-count clock. (ii) The within-session clock: moment-to-moment oscillation with σ₀ = 10 h.u. makes evocation a stochastic event at each instant, so there is a millisecond-to-second clock of Gaussian fluctuation overlaid on the discrete trial clock. (iii) The multi-day clock: drive D, reactive inhibition s_IR, and possibly sLR drift over hours and weeks, violating the homogeneity assumption on [PAGE 329] unless re-anchored. There is no single time constant in the table; applying it across real time requires coupling p_M to D(t), s_IR(t), and sHr(t). Without that coupling, the table's p is a snapshot, and the recursion "I model that you model that I regress" must be re-evaluated at each clock tick because z_M itself is time-dependent.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto layers that bound the Hullian probability on [PAGE 329], with an adjudication rule and breakdown statement.

The equation p_M = Φ((sEB_M − sLR)/σ₀) assigns a nonzero probability to evocation for every finite z_M, because the Gaussian tail never touches zero. That is a mathematical property, not a physical guarantee. Three veto layers truncate or modify the model before any behavior occurs.

Physical layer veto V_phys ∈ {0,1}: If the required response demands force, displacement, or velocity beyond the organism's musculoskeletal maximum, the response is anatomically impossible. Then p_eff = 0 regardless of how large z_M is. The Gaussian tail's p > 0 is over-prediction; the physical veto is a hard truncation outside the model's domain. V_phys = 1 ⇒ p_eff = 0.

Biomechanical layer veto V_bio ∈ {0,1}: Fatigue, injury, pain, or load can raise the effective threshold (sLR_eff = sLR + ΔsLR_bio) or depress the effective potential (sEB_eff = sEB − ΔsEB_bio). If the resulting sEB_eff ≤ sLR for a physically required displacement, the response cannot be generated and p_eff = 0. More generally the biomechanical veto rescales z to z_bio = (sEB_eff − sLR_eff)/σ₀ before the Gaussian integral; if the physical plant cannot produce the required dynamics, Φ is moot.

Weather/environmental layer veto V_env ∈ {0,1}: Temperature extremes, precipitation, wind, or visibility loss can block the response channel or inject a competing drive (e.g., thermoregulation) that reduces effective D and therefore sEB. If the environment makes the response topography impossible to perform, p_eff = 0 even when z_M is large and positive.

Adjudication rule. Let V_i ∈ {0,1} denote each veto. The admissible probability is
p_eff = p_M × ∏_i (1 − V_i). (6)
Any active veto forces p_eff = 0. When all V_i = 0, p_eff = p_M = Φ(z_M) from [PAGE 329].

Breakdown statement. Table 10 contains no veto terms; its validity is bounded to laboratory conditions where V_phys = V_bio = V_env = 0. In field conditions vetoes are common, so tabulated p values are conditional, not universal. The model breaks down precisely when any V_i activates, because the Gaussian tail will still yield p_M > 0 while the physical system yields zero evocations — a category error of applying a psychological integral beyond the plant's physical limits.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios derived from the formalism on [PAGE 329], each with a crisp prediction and a verification path. RLVR = rewards contingent on verifiable outcomes; all three scenarios produce observable, binary or distributional outcomes.

Scenario 1 — Oscillation (σ₀) counterfactual. Hold sEB − sLR fixed at whatever value produced a given row, but double the oscillation magnitude: σ₀′ = 20 h.u. instead of the tabulated σ₀ = 10. Prediction: the new z-score is z′ = z/2, so p′ = Φ(z/2). For z > 0 (above-threshold rows), p′ < p, moving toward 0.5 from above; for z < 0, p′ > p, moving toward 0.5 from below. The farther from threshold, the larger the absolute change. Verification: recompute Column V from Table 9 using z′ = Column IV / 2; the resulting column must lie strictly between the original p and 0.5 for every row. Any recomputation yielding p′ outside [min(p, 0.5), max(p, 0.5)] falsifies the Gaussian scaling assumption.

Scenario 2 — Physical threshold veto counterfactual. Set sLR above the maximum attainable effective reaction potential: sLR > sEB_max for the organism. The Gaussian tail on [PAGE 329] still yields p = Φ((sEB_max − sLR)/σ₀) > 0, however small. The physical veto imposes p_eff = 0 (response anatomically impossible). Prediction: repeated attempts yield zero evocations across N trials; the observed proportion is 0, not the tiny positive tail value. Verification: attempt the response under conditions enforcing sLR > sEB_max (e.g., required force beyond maximum voluntary contraction). Zero successes across N is consistent with p_eff = 0; any single success falsifies the hard veto and implies sEB can exceed the imposed threshold. Binomial confidence bounds apply, but the qualitative veto (exact zero vs. positive tail) is directly falsifiable.

Scenario 3 — Finite-sample / homogeneity veto counterfactual. Select one fixed row (fixed M, sEB, sLR, hence fixed p from Column V on [PAGE 329]) and run N = 40 trials instead of the "unlimited sample" the page requires. Prediction: the observed proportion ṗ will not equal the tabulated p exactly; it fluctuates with standard error SE = √(p(1−p)/40). For p = 0.810 (a visible mid-table value on [PAGE 329]), SE ≈ 0.062, so a 95% interval is roughly p ± 0.12; exact equality ṗ = p should be rejected. Verification: simulate or run 40 Bernoulli trials at probability p; the sample mean will almost surely differ from p, validating the [PAGE 329] caveat that exact p values presuppose an unlimited homogeneous sample. If the observed proportion matches p to more than two decimal places across many repetitions, the finite-sample assumption is violated and the table's precision is illusory.
