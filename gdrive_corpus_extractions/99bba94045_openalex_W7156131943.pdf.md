Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit:

The source document is a machine-learning paper about long-form sports video reasoning. The central problem is not generic video question answering, but temporal compositional reasoning: an answer depends on multiple sparse events that must be located and then composed into an explanation. The paper states the failure mode directly: current models “struggle to identify temporally dispersed evidence and compose it into reasoning” [PAGE 2]. This is a multi-hop issue because a single visual frame is insufficient; the model must first identify candidate moments, then verify what happened in those moments, then connect those verified observations to the question, and finally produce an answer. The proposed benchmark, SportsTime, is designed to supervise this process with step-wise temporal evidence annotations [PAGE 3], and the proposed method, Chain-of-Time Reasoning, is designed to make each reasoning step explicitly anchored to time [PAGE 8].

A useful derivation chain is as follows. If a question asks why a goal was scored, the causal answer may depend on a pass at time τ1, a defensive mistake at time τ2, and a shot at time τ3. Under a naive policy, the model may infer a plausible soccer narrative from language priors without retrieving the actual visual evidence. The paper diagnoses this as overreliance on semantic priors and weak grounding [PAGE 2]. To counter this, the authors factorize the answer generation process into temporal steps: each step is a pair of a textual statement and a time anchor, and the trajectory probability is factorized over those steps and the final answer [PAGE 8]. The formal advantage is that intermediate claims become auditable. If a step says “player X made a pass,” the model must also provide a time anchor that can be checked against the broadcast timeline.

The gray zone appears in the difference between apparent grounding and genuine grounding. The paper reports that time-prompted chain-of-thought increases the frequency of emitted timestamps, but those timestamps may be poorly aligned with the true evidence. The source says this gap shows that “simply ‘asking for timestamps’ encourages format compliance rather than evidence faithfulness” [PAGE 12]. This is crucial because a model can learn the syntactic surface of temporal reasoning without learning the underlying event localization. In other words, the presence of a timestamp is not proof of grounding. The reward design must therefore evaluate both coverage and correctness: coverage asks whether the reasoning steps include anchors, while correctness asks whether those anchors match the annotated evidence [PAGE 9, PAGE 10].

Another gray zone is the relationship between answer accuracy and process quality. A model can sometimes answer correctly even if its reasoning chain is weak, especially when the question is guessable from domain priors. Conversely, a model can produce a well-grounded chain but still miss the final answer because of subtle event interpretation. The paper addresses this with dual-track evaluation: open-ended answer accuracy under an LLM judge and step-wise grounding alignment evaluation [PAGE 11]. The empirical pattern supports the need for both tracks. In the main results, strong proprietary models remain limited, with GPT-5 reaching 40.72% average and Gemini-2.5-Pro reaching 29.37% [PAGE 11]. For the 4B backbone, the full method improves average accuracy from 25.15% to 29.24% [PAGE 12], but the grounding metrics are even more informative: temporal anchoring rises from 11.95% for the base model to 95.76% for the temporally rewarded model, while mean span intersection-over-union rises from 0.2601 to 0.5812 [PAGE 13]. This suggests that the main value of the method is not merely better answer matching, but a reduction in temporal drift.

The audit must also note confounds. The paper finds that accuracy does not decrease monotonically with video length; difficulty is likely driven by video type and event structure [PAGE 14]. This means that any claim that “longer video is harder” is incomplete. Similarly, frame budget matters, but the gain is model-dependent [PAGE 14]. The judge reliability is not perfect: average pairwise agreement is high, but Fleiss’ κ is only moderate at 0.57 [PAGE 14]. Therefore, the evidence supports the conclusion that SportsTime exposes weakness and that Chain-of-Time Reasoning helps, but it also implies that benchmark interpretation should remain cautious. The strongest defensible conclusion is that explicit temporal supervision improves both answer performance and step-wise grounding under the tested settings, not that the problem is solved.

Formal operators, units, page citations, and breakdown regimes:

Let \(V\) denote a long-form sports video, \(q\) a question, and \(a\) an open-ended answer. The reasoning trajectory is \(\mathcal{T} = [n_1, \ldots, n_T, a]\), where each intermediate step is \(n_t = \langle s_t, \tau_t \rangle\). Here \(s_t\) is a textual statement and \(\tau_t\) is a temporal anchor, either a timestamp or a temporal span [PAGE 8]. The temporal anchor has units of time, typically seconds or the display format mm:ss. The video duration \(D\) is in seconds; the number of sampled frames \(N\) is dimensionless; the sampling interval is \(\Delta = D/N\) seconds per sampled frame.

The paper’s trajectory factorization is:

\[
p_\theta(\mathcal{T} \mid V, q)
=
\left( \prod_{t=1}^{T} \pi_\theta(n_t \mid V, q, n_{1:t-1}) \right)
\cdot
\pi_\theta(a \mid V, q, n_{1:T}).
\]

This is the probability of generating the full anchored reasoning chain followed by the answer. The units of \(p_\theta\) are probability mass, dimensionless, bounded between 0 and 1. Log-probability can be measured in nats if using natural logarithms, or in bits if using base-2 logarithms. The integer \(T\) is the number of reasoning steps before the final answer. The conditioning history \(n_{1:t-1}\) means all previous anchored steps.

The aggregate reinforcement-learning reward is defined as:

\[
R(\mathcal{T})
=
\lambda_{\text{fmt}}\, r_{\text{fmt}}(\mathcal{T})
+
\lambda_{\text{acc}}\, r_{\text{acc}}(\mathcal{T})
+
\lambda_{\text{temporal}}\, r_{\text{temporal}}(\mathcal{T}).
\]

This appears on PAGE 9. The coefficients \(\lambda_{\text{fmt}}, \lambda_{\text{acc}}, \lambda_{\text{temporal}}\) are scalar weights, dimensionless. The structural reward \(r_{\text{fmt}}\) is binary, checking whether the output follows the required format [PAGE 9]. The answer reward \(r_{\text{acc}}\) is a task-level correctness reward, also naturally bounded in \([0,1]\) if implemented as a correctness indicator or normalized score. The temporal reward \(r_{\text{temporal}}\) evaluates temporal grounding quality.

The temporal reward is decomposed as:

\[
r_{\text{temporal}}(\mathcal{T})
=
\alpha\, r_{\text{cov}}(\mathcal{T})
+
(1-\alpha)\, r_{\text{cor}}(\mathcal{T}),
\]

with \(\alpha \in [0,1]\) [PAGE 9]. The coverage term \(r_{\text{cov}}\) is the proportion of reasoning steps that contain at least one explicit time anchor [PAGE 9]. If there are \(T\) reasoning steps and \(T_{\text{anchored}}\) of them contain at least one anchor, then:

\[
r_{\text{cov}} = \frac{T_{\text{anchored}}}{T},
\]

for \(T > 0\). This is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). The correctness term \(r_{\text{cor}}\) measures how well predicted anchors align with ground-truth anchors. For each ground-truth anchor, the model computes a best-match score against predicted anchors, using span intersection-over-union for span-span matches and a distance-aware similarity for point-span or point-point matches, then averages over ground-truth anchors to obtain \(r_{\text{cor}} \in [0,1]\) [PAGE 10].

For span anchors, the standard intersection-over-union operator is:

\[
\mathrm{IoU}(S_{\text{pred}}, S_{\text{gt}})
=
\frac{|S_{\text{pred}} \cap S_{\text{gt}}|}{|S_{\text{pred}} \cup S_{\text{gt}}|},
\]

with the convention that if the union is empty or undefined, the score is undefined or set to zero by implementation rule. The numerator and denominator are measured in seconds, but their ratio is dimensionless. The paper’s SGA evaluation uses mean span intersection-over-union, mIoU, and hit rate at threshold \(\tau\), written H@\(\tau\) [PAGE 13]. The hit-rate operator is:

\[
\mathrm{H}@\tau
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\mathbf{1}\left[ \mathrm{IoU}_i \ge \tau \right],
\]

where \(M\) is the number of examples, \(\mathrm{IoU}_i\) is the example-wise temporal alignment score, and \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is the indicator function. This is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\).

Breakdown regimes are important. First, if the ground-truth anchor set is empty, \(r_{\text{cor}}\) has no denominator and becomes undefined unless a special convention is defined. Second, if predicted anchors are empty, matching has no candidate set and the score collapses to zero or undefined. Third, if \(\alpha = 1\), the reward becomes coverage-only and may encourage many anchors without correctness. Fourth, if \(\alpha = 0\), the reward becomes correctness-only and may discourage emitting anchors when uncertain. Fifth, if a timestamp lies outside the valid video interval \([0,D]\), it is physically invalid. Sixth, if the sampled frame interval \(\Delta\) is larger than the duration \(\delta\) of a decisive event, the event may be missed by uniform sampling; under a simple uniform-sampling model, the chance of at least one sampled frame falling inside an event is approximately \(1 - \exp(-\delta/\Delta)\), which is small when \(\delta \ll \Delta\). Seventh, the timestamp overlay itself can fail if the visual overlay is occluded, cropped, low-resolution, or misread; the method depends on the model successfully reading the visual clock [PAGE 9].

The paper sits at the intersection of several research streams: long-video understanding, temporal grounding, chain-of-thought reasoning, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, and sports analytics. The related-work section traces temporal grounding to query-to-time alignment, where a language query is aligned with a temporal segment or moment proposal [PAGE 4]. It also identifies TOGA as predicting both an open-ended answer and supporting temporal spans, and it cites LongVT and VITAL as systems that use temporal localization inside a tool-augmented reasoning process over long videos [PAGE 4]. SportsTime extends this lineage by making each intermediate reasoning step explicitly anchored, not merely producing one final supporting span.

The benchmark lineage is also important. Earlier sports benchmarks moved from task-specific datasets toward broader multimodal understanding. SoccerNet established tasks such as replay grounding and camera-shot segmentation; later soccer efforts scaled multimodal soccer data [PAGE 4]. Multi-sport benchmarks such as Sports-QA and SPORTU broadened coverage, while SportR introduced chain-of-thought and spatial grounding supervision [PAGE 4]. The paper’s comparison table shows that many prior datasets have chain-of-thought annotations, but SportsTime uniquely emphasizes step-wise temporal annotations with timestamps and spans, denoted TS+S in the table [PAGE 7]. A multi-week trap in this lineage is the confusion between temporal-looking output and temporal understanding. Models can be trained to emit timestamps because the format is learnable, but the underlying alignment may remain weak. The paper’s time-prompted chain-of-thought result is a direct warning: anchoring frequency rises to 91.80%, yet mean intersection-over-union remains only 0.1244 and hit rate at 0.5 is only 12.12% [PAGE 13]. Another trap is direct supervised fine-tuning of reasoning chains: the ablation shows that adding supervised fine-tuning can reduce performance, from 25.15 to 17.61 average, with repetitive generation attributed to structural variability and non-uniqueness of temporal reasoning chains [PAGE 14]. The lineage therefore suggests that the field must move from answer-level supervision to process-level, verifiable supervision.

Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock:

Under long-form video reasoning, a model may experience a “crisis” when the answer depends on evidence that is far from the current context. The habit regression is to fall back on semantic priors: plausible sports commentary, typical event sequences, or language-model expectations. The paper describes this as models that “overrely on language priors while underutilizing visual and temporal evidence,” producing plausible but weakly grounded answers and hallucinations [PAGE 2]. In recursive terms, the model’s internal policy predicts what a sports answer usually sounds like instead of what actually happened in the video.

The counter-trap is to make the reasoning process externally checkable. Chain-of-Time Reasoning does this by requiring each step to carry a temporal anchor, then using reinforcement learning to reward both anchor coverage and anchor correctness [PAGE 9, PAGE 10]. During inference, anchor-triggered interactive observation retrieves local clips around each predicted anchor, verifies the step against those clips, and revises before proceeding [PAGE 10]. This creates a loop: anchor, observe, infer, revise. The model is no longer allowed to rely only on a global summary; it must repeatedly consult bounded local evidence.

The “clock” layer is the visual timestamp overlay. The paper states that it burns “an explicit timestamp overlay into the top-right corner of video frames with a consistent mm:ss format” [PAGE 9]. This converts latent temporal position into an observable visual signal. It is a clock because the model no longer needs to infer absolute time solely from narrative context. The ablation supports this: removing the timestamp overlay causes a clear drop, indicating that visually accessible timestamps help the model leverage temporal evidence [PAGE 14]. In recursive theory-of-mind terms, the model is given a persistent temporal landmark that reduces drift across long contexts.

However, the clock can be gamed. If the reward values timestamps too much without verifying alignment, the model may learn to place plausible-looking timestamps on plausible-looking statements. The countermeasure is the correctness term, which pushes predicted anchors toward annotated evidence spans [PAGE 10]. The empirical contrast between time-prompted chain-of-thought and the temporally rewarded model is the proof: high anchor frequency alone is insufficient, while high anchoring plus high mIoU indicates reduced temporal drift [PAGE 12, PAGE 13].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof:

A temporal reasoning system is not valid if the physical layer cannot support the claimed evidence. Let \(D\) be the video duration in seconds, \(N\) the number of sampled frames, and \(\Delta = D/N\) the sampling interval in seconds per sampled frame. Let \(\delta\) be the duration of a decisive event in seconds. If sampling is approximately uniform and independent of the event, the probability that at least one sampled frame intersects the event is:

\[
P_{\text{detect}}
=
1 - \left(1 - \frac{\delta}{D}\right)^N.
\]

For \(\delta \ll D\), this can be approximated by:

\[
P_{\text{detect}}
\approx
1 - \exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{\Delta}\right).
\]

When \(\delta \ll \Delta\), the probability is approximately \(\delta / \Delta\). This gives a physical veto: if the decisive event is shorter than the sampling interval, the sampled evidence cannot reliably support the event. The paper emphasizes that sports evidence is “sparse, brief, and widely separated in time” [PAGE 11], and that each reasoning step is usually tied to “an event timestamp or a short, second-scale span” [PAGE 7]. Therefore, a model evaluated with too few frames may fail not because of reasoning weakness alone, but because the physical sampling grid missed the event.

A biomechanical veto also applies. Fast player motion, motion blur, occlusion, camera panning, replay cuts, and broadcast editing can make the visual boundary of an event ambiguous. The benchmark construction acknowledges that the five sports differ in “camera conventions, editing styles, and event structures” [PAGE 5]. If the camera cuts away before the ball is kicked, or if a replay changes the apparent timing, a temporal anchor may refer to a broadcast moment that does not align with the game moment. The model’s timestamp overlay can identify broadcast time, but broadcast time is not identical to event semantics if editing or replay is present.

A weather veto can also invalidate visual grounding. Rain, fog, snow, low light, or glare can obscure the ball, player limbs, or jersey numbers. If the visual signal is below the threshold needed to identify the event, the correct epistemic action is abstention or reduced confidence, not confident temporal grounding. Formally, if visual evidence quality \(Q\) falls below a task-dependent threshold \(Q_{\min}\), then the likelihood of correct event identification collapses, and any temporal reward based on that event becomes unreliable. The paper’s semi-automatic annotation pipeline attempts to control domain correctness through expert review [PAGE 6], but at inference time the physical visibility layer remains a veto: without sufficient visual evidence, the reasoning chain cannot be trusted even if the language output is fluent.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning counterfactual veto scenarios and predictions:

1. Counterfactual veto: remove the visual timestamp overlay during training and inference.
Prediction: temporal anchoring becomes less learnable, coverage and alignment fall, and the benefit of temporal reward weakens. The paper’s ablation supports this: “removing the timestamp-overlay (w/o ts) causes a clear drop” [PAGE 14]. The specific measured drop is from 27.31 average with timestamp overlay to 26.12 without it, while temporal accuracy falls from 13.73 to 13.14 [PAGE 14]. Verification method: hold the backbone, data, and reward function fixed, and toggle only the overlay. If grounding metrics drop while language-only answer accuracy remains similar, the veto is confirmed.

2. Counterfactual veto: reward only anchor coverage, not anchor correctness.
Prediction: the model emits many timestamps, but temporal alignment remains poor. The paper’s time-prompted chain-of-thought result is a proxy: anchor frequency reaches 91.80%, but mIoU is only 0.1244 and H@0.5 is 12.12% [PAGE 13]. This shows that high anchor frequency without correctness is not genuine grounding. Verification method: set \(\alpha = 1\) in the temporal reward so that \(r_{\text{temporal}} = r_{\text{cov}}\). If the model increases anchoring frequency but fails to improve mIoU or H@0.5, the counterfactual is confirmed. The veto rule is that coverage-only reward is insufficient when the benchmark requires verifiable temporal alignment.

3. Counterfactual veto: use answer-only reinforcement learning without temporal reward.
Prediction: answer accuracy may improve modestly, but grounding remains weak because the policy is not required to localize evidence. The paper’s native GRPO comparison supports this: native GRPO improves accuracy to 26.72%, but anchoring is only 13.10% and mIoU is 0.3128; the temporal-reward version reaches 27.31% accuracy with anchoring 95.76% and mIoU 0.5812 [PAGE 13]. Verification method: train two policies with identical backbone and data, one with answer reward only and one with answer plus temporal reward. If the answer-only policy fails to improve temporal grounding metrics, the counterfactual is confirmed. The RLVR principle is that rewards must be tied to verifiable process evidence, not only to final answer text.

A fourth cross-check is human evaluation. The paper samples 100 examples and scores evidence-span accuracy, intermediate claim reliability, and faithful logical consistency. The reported scores are FLC 4.50/5, ICR 4.02/5, and ESA 3.65/5 [PAGE 13]. This indicates that the chain is generally coherent and reliable, but fine-grained localization remains improvable. The counterfactual veto is that a model can receive a high language-coherence score while still having weak evidence-span accuracy. Therefore, verification must include both automatic temporal alignment and human process evaluation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
