This creates a nested modeling problem: the experimenter assumes a metric space ($d_{\mathrm{aff}}$), while the organism’s nervous system may implement a non-metric or periodic map (octave peaks).

Crisis habit regression operates as follows. Under stress or novel probe conditions, the organism reverts to the strongest available habit in its hierarchy (Hullian habit-family dominance). If discrimination training is recent and has not reached the 75 per cent asymptotic criterion [PAGE 190], the crisis habit is broad generalization along physical dimensions. If training is asymptotic, the crisis habit becomes narrow differentiation according to j.n.d. distance. The counter-trap is for an observer or adversary to present probes at even multiples of the conditioned frequency [PAGE 190]. The organism’s internal j.n.d. model predicts weak responding because physical distance is large; its receptor physiology predicts strong responding because octaves tap overlapping channels. The trap is to trust the experimenter’s metric over the organism’s physiology.

The clock is the training-trial axis. Early in training, generalization gradients are broad and the 75 per cent differential criterion has not been established [PAGE 190]; late in training, the j.n.d. is estimated at asymptote and gradients narrow. A crisis occurring early on the clock recruits broad, physical-distance-based habits; a crisis occurring late recruits narrow, j.n.d.-based habits. Recursive theory of mind therefore requires the modeler to track (i) where the organism is on the training clock, (ii) whether the probe dimension contains receptor irregularities such as octaves, and (iii) whether the probe is elemental or configural. Misjudging any one layer produces a false prediction of the crisis habit.
