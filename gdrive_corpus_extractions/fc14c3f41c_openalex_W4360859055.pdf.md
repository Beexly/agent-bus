Physical layer: amputation changes the center of gravity, impairs walking and running ability, raises energy expenditure and heart rate, and decreases oxygen consumption [PAGE 2, Introduction]. The AF cohort’s numerically higher resting heart rate (81.66 ± 13.36 bpm versus 76.45 and 73.08 bpm) [PAGE 3 Table 1] indicates chronic sympathetic loading that can independently drive concentric wall thickening and diastolic stiffening.

Biomechanical layer: amputee football is played with forearm crutches; this produces intermittent upper-body loading, altered venous return through the thorax, and different skeletal-muscle pump mechanics compared with able-bodied football. Prosthetic socket interface pressure can impair venous return and augment afterload during the gait cycle. Because the study did not stratify by amputation level (transtibial vs transfemoral vs hip disarticulation) [PAGE 6], the biomechanical load is heterogeneous within the AF group; transfemoral amputees incur higher metabolic cost and cardiac stress per unit distance, potentially dominating the observed AF signal.

Weather veto: ambient temperature modulates systemic vascular resistance and heart rate; humidity affects sweating, prosthetic adhesion, and gait efficiency; cold exposure raises afterload and can unmask diastolic dysfunction. However, the TTE examinations were performed in a cardiology department with participants in the left lateral decubitus position in dim light [PAGE 4], so the measurement environment was thermoneutral and controlled. The weather veto therefore nullifies measurement-condition confounding but does not rescue external validity: resting echocardiograms obtained in a clinic cannot be extrapolated to match-play conditions in cold, wet, or hot environments without in-environment validation.

Verdict: The physical veto (concentric geometry with normal cavity size) is sustained: smaller LVEDD with higher PWT is incompatible with pure eccentric athlete’s-heart remodeling and indicates pressure-overload-type adaptation. The biomechanical veto is sustained only conditionally: without amputation-level stratification, the AF group is a biomechanical mixture, and the group-level ECHO differences may be driven by the highest-loading amputees. The weather veto is rejected for the clinic measurements but upheld for match-day generalization.

Scenario 1 — Generalization to able-bodied elite football. Veto condition: if a downstream user applies the AF reference ranges (PWT ≈ 10 mm, LVEDD ≈ 45.5 mm, A-wave ≈ 0.58 m/s) to non-disabled professional footballers to screen for pathology. Justification: the FP comparison group is amateur league, not elite professional [PAGE 3], and the AF pathophysiology is amputee-specific, not transferable. Verifiable prediction: able-bodied professionals with physiological athlete’s-heart remodeling (LVEDD > 55 mm, PWT < 11 mm, E/A > 1.5) would be misclassified as abnormal against AF-derived cutoffs, producing false positives and unwarranted exclusion from competition.

Scenario 2 — Clearance for competition based on resting TTE alone. Veto condition: if a clinician uses this study’s normal LVEF (54.50–59.70%) and normal chamber dimensions to clear an AF player for high-intensity match play without exercise stress testing. Justification: the paper explicitly states that patients with normal diastolic function at rest may have diastolic dysfunction during exercise [PAGE 6, citing refs. 24–25], and the data were collected out-of-season after ≥8 hours of rest [PAGE 4]. Verifiable prediction: an AF athlete with normal resting TTE will exhibit elevated E/Em ratio and elevated filling pressures during or immediately after a match, and resting clearance will fail to predict exercise-induced diastolic decompensation.

Scenario 3 — Causal attribution of PWT differences to amputation. Veto condition: if a model infers that amputation per se causes the higher PWT in AF (d=0.978 vs FP) based solely on this cross-sectional design. Justification: n=12 per group, nominal p-values without family-wise correction, no stratification by amputation level, no longitudinal data, and uncontrolled differences in training history and prosthetic use [PAGES 3–6]. Verifiable prediction: a matched longitudinal study controlling for amputation level, training load, and prosthetic interface will show that the PWT effect size attenuates toward zero, revealing that the observed group difference is confounded by loading modality rather than amputation status alone.
