The concept of "experimental extinction" itself dates back to Pavlov's observations that a conditioned response diminishes if the unconditioned stimulus is no longer presented. This formalization allowed for comparisons across different species, modalities, and experimental conditions, establishing extinction as a core dependent variable in learning theory.

Looking forward, this lineage has profound implications for contemporary neuroscience. Modern research views extinction not as erasure of the original memory trace, but as the formation of a new, competing inhibitory memory (extinction learning). The "inhibition of reinforcement" mentioned in the text can be retrospectively understood as the engagement of prefrontal cortical circuits that suppress amygdala-driven fear responses. The multi-week trap emerges when considering relapse phenomena: spontaneous recovery, renewal, and reinstatement. These occur because the original conditioned association persists, lying dormant beneath the surface-level extinction learning. This historical insight—that extinction follows a predictable curve yet leaves traces of the original habit—directly informs current treatments for anxiety disorders, PTSD, and addiction, where preventing relapse is critical. Understanding the precise shape of Hovland’s negative growth curve helps clinicians predict how quickly a patient might respond to exposure therapy and when they are most vulnerable to relapse.
