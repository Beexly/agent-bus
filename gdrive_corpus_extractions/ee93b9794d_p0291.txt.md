The page then collapses these into a purely behavioral decision rule: “We define the emotion as fear, whether it be in ourselves or in rats, when the organism is observed to run or hide, we define it as rage, when he is observed to fight or attack, and we define it as lust, when he is observed to caress or copulate” [PAGE 1], explicitly adding that this holds “irrespective of whether or not the visceral components, and hence the introspective feels of these different emotions, are or are not distinctive” [PAGE 1].

Gray-zone audit. The first gray zone is circularity: if fear is defined by running, and running is taken as evidence of fear, the definition is operationally closed and cannot falsify itself. The second gray zone is the “sign object.” Tolman treats the sign object as an intentional target of expectancy, yet in rats the inference from observed flight to “expectation of escape via sign object” is underdetermined by behavior alone; the organism could be executing a fixed action pattern without any representational means-end model. The third gray zone is cross-species extrapolation: applying the same “lust = caress or copulate” rule to rats and humans assumes homologized goal states (sexual analepsis) and homologous action repertoires, which may not hold across phylogenetic distance. The fourth gray zone is the breakdown regime: when the body cannot execute the designated action—paralysis, restraint, environmental blockade—the operational definition fails even if the emotional state persists. Tolman’s framework therefore has a singularity at the point where means-end execution is physically impossible, a regime his text does not address. Finally, the page offers no metric for “physiological disturbance” or “quiescence” in SI or information units, so the theory is qualitatively rich but quantitatively opaque at the precise point where it interfaces with physiology.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operators, units, page citations, and breakdown regimes.

Let the emotion alphabet be \(\mathcal{E} = \{f, r, \ell\}\) for fear, rage, and lust. Let \(\mathcal{P}\) denote physiological states, partitioned into disturbances \(\mathcal{D} \subset \mathcal{P}\) to be avoided and quiescences \(\mathcal{Q} \subset \mathcal{P}\) to be approached. Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be the set of sign objects and \(\mathcal{A}\) the set of observable actions. From [PAGE 1], Tolman’s hypothesis is that each emotion \(e \in \mathcal{E}\) is a tuple \((\tau_e, \mu_e)\), where \(\tau_e \in \mathcal{D} \cup \mathcal{Q}\) is the target physiological state and \(\mu_e: \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}\) is the means-end expectancy map.

Fear: \(\tau_f = \text{avoid}(d_{\text{pain/injury}})\), \(\mu_f(s) = \text{escape}(s)\) or hide. Verbatim [PAGE 1]: “an avoıdance of the physiological disturbance of pain or injury, plus an expectation that the way to avoid such pain or injury is by escaping from the sign object in question.”

Rage: \(\tau_r = \text{avoid}(d_{\text{blocking/interference}})\), \(\mu_r(s) = \text{destroy}(s)\) or attack. Verbatim [PAGE 1]: “an avoidance of the physiological disturbance of blocking or interference, plus an expectation that the way to avoıd such blocking is to destroy the sign object in question.”

Lust: \(\tau_\ell = \text{approach}(q_{\text{sexual analepsis}})\), \(\mu_\ell(s) = \text{intimate\_contact}(s)\) or copulate. Verbatim [PAGE 1]: “an approach to the physiological quiescence of sexual analepsis, plus an expectation that the way to achieve such sexual analepsis is by intimate bodıly contacts.”

The operational decision rule stated on [PAGE 1] can be written as an indicator:
\[
\hat{e}(a, s) = 
\begin{cases}
f, & a \in \{\text{run}, \text{hide}\},\\
r, & a \in \{\text{fight}, \text{attack}\},\\
\ell, & a \in \{\text{caress}, \text{copulate}\},\\
\varnothing, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
This rule is explicitly agnostic to visceral components: [PAGE 1] states the distinctions are made “irrespective of whether or not the visceral components, and hence the introspective feels of these different emotions, are or are not distinctive.”

Units and assumptions. Physiological disturbance/quiescence is treated as a homeostatic error signal, dimensionless or normalized to baseline; no SI unit is specified by Tolman [PAGE 1]. Action is measured in kinematic terms (velocity m/s, force N) only when biomechanically available. The mutual information \(I(E; A \mid S)\) is assumed high under the theory, but no numeric value is given.

Breakdown regime (singularity). If a constraint \(c\) renders \(\mu_e(s)\) physically inexecutable, the mapping from emotion to action is undefined: \(P(a = \mu_e(s) \mid e, c) = 0\). In that regime the indicator \(\hat{e}\) returns \(\varnothing\) despite possible persistence of the emotional state. The theory breaks down precisely when action is vetoed by physics, biomechanics, or environment, because Tolman’s definition is behaviorally anchored [PAGE 1].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

The lineage on [PAGE 1] runs from Cannon’s 1914 physiological researches, cited in the footnote as “W B Cannon The interrelations of emotions as suggested by recent physiological researches Amer 1 Psvchol 1914 25 256-282” [PAGE 1], through Dashiell’s 1929 Appleton synthesis (footnote: “ed (New York D Annleton and Company 1929)-,, p n 8 a5 ea (New York D Appleton and Company 192zy, 319 327” [PAGE 1]), and arrives at Tolman’s 1932 purposive behaviorism. Cannon and Dashiell dismantle the James-Lange assumption that distinct emotions possess distinct visceral signatures. Tolman does not retreat to pure introspection; instead he relocates emotion in the organism’s expectancy structure—its “means-end relation” to a sign object [PAGE 1]. This is a direct ancestor of cognitive expectancy theory, Tolman’s own later cognitive maps, and modern active-inference accounts in which emotion is organized around predicted homeostatic outcomes.

Multi-week trap. In longitudinal conditioning, an organism repeatedly exposed to a sign object that precedes pain or blocking may consolidate a fear or rage habit over weeks. The means-end contingency (“escape removes sign object,” “attack removes interference”) becomes a stable policy. Under stress, the policy regresses to this consolidated habit even when the original contingency has changed. The “clock” in this trap is the temporal contiguity window: if the expected physiological quiescence is delayed beyond the critical association interval, the means-end link weakens, producing extinction-like degradation. However, if the organism has undergone multi-week overtraining, the habit becomes resistant to contingency degradation and re-emerges under pressure, exactly because Tolman’s definition ties emotion to the *expectation* of a specific means-end path rather than to the instantaneous visceral feel [PAGE 1]. Thus the historical trap is that a weeks-old expectancy can outlast the physiological condition that originally generated it, making the emotion label persistent but potentially obsolete.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. Under acute threat, the organism is predicted by Tolman’s framework to default to the prepotent action tied to its emotion label: run/hide for fear, fight/attack for rage, caress/copulate for lust [PAGE 1]. This is a habit regression because the means-end map \(\mu_e(s)\) is invoked reflexively; the organism does not deliberate over whether escape will actually remove pain, it simply executes the historical solution. The regression is “crisis” because it is triggered by the presence of the sign object and the anticipated physiological disturbance.

Counter-trap. A counter-trap is a higher-order inhibitory intervention that suppresses the prepotent action. For example, freezing instead of running, or withholding attack despite blocking. Tolman’s text does not name this, but his expectancy formulation implies it: if the organism revises its expectation about which means leads to quiescence, the action can be suppressed. The counter-trap is cognitively expensive and requires an intact means-end model.

Clock. The clock is the temporal pressure imposed by the sign object’s approach. If pain or injury is imminent, the window for engaging the counter-trap collapses; the system must execute \(\mu_f(s)\) or suffer the disturbance. The clock therefore determines whether crisis habit regression is allowed to complete or whether a counter-trap can be inserted. In Tolman’s terms [PAGE 1], the emotion is defined by the *expectation* that a certain means avoids disturbance; when the clock runs out, expectancy collapses into pure reaction, and the behavioral label (run, fight, caress) is applied by the observer irrespective of whether the internal expectancy still holds. This creates a recursive theory-of-mind trap: an external observer attributes fear to a rat because it runs [PAGE 1], but the rat may be running without any representational expectancy; the observer’s second-order attribution is thus ungrounded in the organism’s actual means-end model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

Adjudication rule. Let \(\mathcal{C} = \{c_{\text{phys}}, c_{\text{bio}}, c_{\text{weather}}\}\) be the constraint set. An emotion label \(e \in \mathcal{E}\) is assigned only if the feasibility predicate \(F_e(s, c) = 1\) for all \(c \in \mathcal{C}\), where \(F_e\) returns 1 iff the action \(\mu_e(s)\) is executable in the current context. If any \(c\) forces \(F_e = 0\), the Tolmanian label is vetoed and the state is classified as undifferentiated arousal rather than fear, rage, or lust [PAGE 1].

Physical veto. If the organism lacks the anatomical apparatus for escape (e.g., no limbs, paralysis), then \(\mu_f(s) = \text{escape}(s)\) is physically impossible. \(F_f = 0\). Fear is vetoed even if autonomic signs of disturbance avoidance are present.

Biomechanical veto. If musculoskeletal damage or fatigue prevents attack, \(\mu_r(s) = \text{destroy}(s)\) cannot be executed. \(F_r = 0\). Rage is vetoed. The organism may show autonomic interference-avoidance physiology but cannot operationalize rage as destruction of the sign object [PAGE 1].

Weather veto. Environmental conditions alter sign-object accessibility and action feasibility. Flooding removes escape routes (\(F_f = 0\)); extreme heat or cold suppresses copulation (\(F_\ell = 0\)); high wind or noise degrades sign-object detection, making \(\mu_e(s)\) inexecutable because \(s\) cannot be localized. Weather is therefore a veto layer that acts before the emotion can be labeled.

Proof of veto consistency. Suppose an organism is restrained (physical constraint \(c_{\text{phys}}\)) and exposed to a shock sign object. It shows autonomic activation consistent with disturbance avoidance but cannot run or hide. By [PAGE 1], fear is defined by running/hiding. Since \(\mu_f(s)\) is blocked, \(\hat{e}\) returns \(\varnothing\). The state is not fear under Tolman’s operational definition; it is non-behavioral arousal. The veto is thus sound: no emotion label is assigned without an executable means-end action.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios and predictions.

Scenario 1: Immobilized subject exposed to threat sign object. A rat is physically restrained so it cannot run or hide, then presented with a shock-associated sign object. Autonomic fear responses (heart rate, corticosterone) are recorded, but no escape behavior occurs. Veto: biomechanical constraint blocks \(\mu_f(s)\) [PAGE 1]. Prediction: Tolmanian label “fear” is rejected; behavior is logged as “immobilization distress” rather than fear. Verifiable via synchronized video and telemetry; if running is absent, \(\hat{e} = \varnothing\) despite physiological disturbance.

Scenario 2: Rage without an external sign object. A human participant in a featureless isolation chamber reports intense anger but identifies no blocking or interference object to destroy. No sign object \(s \in \mathcal{S}\) is present, so \(\mu_r(s)\) is undefined. Veto: means-end map has no domain input. Prediction: the state fails Tolman’s rage criterion (“avoidance of ... blocking ... destroy the sign object” [PAGE 1]) and is reclassified as free-floating irritability or autonomic dysregulation. Verifiable via experience-sampling reports plus behavioral observation showing no attack behavior.

Scenario 3: Sexual analepsis without intimate contact. A subject under quarantine achieves sexual quiescence (analepsis) via non-contact pharmacological stimulation; caress and copulation are physically impossible due to isolation (weather/environmental constraint). Veto: \(\mu_\ell(s) = \text{intimate\_contact}(s)\) blocked. Prediction: under [PAGE 1], lust cannot be labeled because the defining action is absent; the state is classified as “sexual satiety without lust signature.” Verifiable via physiological measures of analepsis and confirmed absence of caress/copulation behavior.

Across all three, the counterfactual prediction is identical: when the means-end action specified on [PAGE 1] is vetoed by a verifiable constraint, the emotion label collapses to \(\varnothing\), demonstrating that Tolman’s definition is behaviorally executable rather than viscurally intrinsic.
