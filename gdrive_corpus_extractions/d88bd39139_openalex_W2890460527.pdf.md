<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses several formal operators. Herrnstein’s strict matching law is stated as:

\[
\frac{B_1}{B_1+B_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_2}
\]

where \(B_1,B_2\) are response counts and \(R_1,R_2\) are reinforcement counts or rates; all quantities are dimensionless, and the equation assumes simultaneous, stable, two-alternative choice with no unmodeled bias [PAGE 26]. Baum’s generalized matching equation is:

\[
\log\left(\frac{B_1}{B_2}\right)
=
a\log\left(\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)+\log b
\]

where \(a\) is the dimensionless sensitivity slope, \(b\) is the multiplicative bias, and \(\log b\) is the additive log-bias intercept [PAGE 26]. The source explains: “Strict matching is demonstrated by regression line (A) in Figure 1 with a slope (a) of 1.0 and bias (log b) of 0.0” [PAGE 27]. Overmatching is \(a \gt 1\), undermatching is \(0 \lt a \lt 1\), and bias toward \(B_1\) or \(B_2\) appears as \(\log b \gt 0\) or \(\log b \lt 0\) [PAGE 27]. The log base is not explicitly fixed; slopes are invariant to base, but the intercept’s numerical scale depends on the base, so \(\log b\) should be read as a dimensionless log-ratio offset rather than an SI quantity.

The football yards model is:

\[
\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pass}_{\mathrm{plays}}}{\mathrm{Rush}_{\mathrm{plays}}}\right)
=
a\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{Yards}_{\mathrm{pass}}}{\mathrm{Yards}_{\mathrm{rush}}}\right)+\log b
\]

[PAGE 56]. The football Success Rate model is:

\[
\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{Pass}_{\mathrm{plays}}}{\mathrm{Rush}_{\mathrm{plays}}}\right)
=
a\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{SuccessRate}_{\mathrm{pass}}}{\mathrm{SuccessRate}_{\mathrm{rush}}}\right)+\log b
\]

[PAGE 57]. Success Rate itself is a dimensionless proportion:

\[
\mathrm{SuccessRate}_i
=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{success},i}}
{N_{\mathrm{success},i}+N_{\mathrm{fail},i}}
\]

where success is defined by down-specific thresholds: 40 percent of needed yards on first down, 60 percent on second down, and a first down on third down [PAGE 9; PAGE 10; PAGE 43]. The response variable is a play-count ratio; the predictor is either a yardage ratio or a success-proportion ratio. The fitted line minimizes squared error in log-ratio space:

\[
(\hat a,\log\hat b)
=
\arg\min_{a,\log b}
\sum_i
\left[
y_i-(a x_i+\log b)
\right]^2
\]

where \(y_i=\log(B_{p,i}/B_{r,i})\) and \(x_i\) is the chosen reinforcement log ratio. \(R^2\) is the dimensionless fraction of variance accounted for, and RMSE is in log-response-ratio units [PAGE 64; PAGE 65]. The source also uses ANCOVA to compare slopes and intercepts [PAGE 61; PAGE 72] and paired t-tests for across-team slope comparison [PAGE 82].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are critical. The GME requires positive ratios; it breaks if \(B_2=0\), \(R_2=0\), \(R_1=0\), or if any reinforcement value is negative and a raw logarithm is taken. This is not hypothetical here: the source later notes that EPA and WPA can be negative and would require transformation before logarithmic GME use [PAGE 97], and football rushing yardage can be negative in a game even when season aggregates are positive. Success Rate is bounded in \([0,1]\), so \(\log(\mathrm{SuccessRate}_{\mathrm{pass}}/\mathrm{SuccessRate}_{\mathrm{rush}})\) breaks at zero or one and becomes unstable near the boundaries; a continuity correction or logit transform would be required for a fully specified model. The source does not report such a correction. The OLS fit also assumes independent errors, correct functional form, no omitted confounders, and no measurement error in play classification; audibles, run-pass options, and scrambles violate exact measurement of the called play [PAGE 25; PAGE 44]. The source checks normality and residuals and removes outliers with ROUT at \(Q=5.0\%\) [PAGE 59; PAGE 69], but it does not establish exogeneity. Model comparison is also limited: the author states “there was no statistical comparison of R2 values” [PAGE 94], and when slopes differ significantly, intercept comparison by ANCOVA is not licensed [PAGE 72]. Thus the formal model is valid as a descriptive log-ratio regression under positivity and independence assumptions, but its causal and boundary behavior is not established.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source models one agent’s response allocation, not a recursive strategic interaction. That omission matters in three ways. First, crisis habit regression: under late-game time pressure, a play-caller may fall back on overlearned biases. The source documents situational biases such as rushing when winning and passing when losing [PAGE 48], and it reports a passing bias when less than two minutes remain in a half [PAGE 46]. Those patterns are consistent with a habitual repertoire becoming dominant under high tempo and high consequence, but the GME treats the resulting \(a\) and \(\log b\) as fixed parameters rather than state-dependent policy outputs. Second, counter-trap risk: a defensive coordinator who models the play-caller’s bias can make the habitual response maladaptive. The source acknowledges “the type of defensive strategy employed by an opponent” as an omitted factor [PAGE 38] and lists “strength of opposing defense” among unmodeled variables [PAGE 94]. If the defender’s policy \(D\) changes the reinforcement ratio \(R_1/R_2\), then the GME’s predictor is endogenous to the opponent’s counter-strategy. A play-caller who assumes the defense expects rush may pass; a defense that expects that may defend pass; the recursive loop is not identified by a one-agent matching equation. Third, clock as motivating operation: the source argues that the reinforcing value of one yard changes with down-and-distance and time remaining, giving the example that ten yards in the first quarter differs from ten yards with one minute left [PAGE 53; PAGE 54]. The clock is therefore not a neutral covariate; it alters the effective reinforcement value of the same nominal yardage or Success Rate. The source’s static model cannot distinguish “habit regression,” “counter-trap adjustment,” and “clock-induced value change” because all three appear as residual variance or as shifts in the pooled slope and intercept. A sharper test would manipulate the clock and opponent tendency independently, or at least model them as interacting state variables.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can veto the source’s causal interpretation even if the statistical fit is good. The source explicitly admits that “weather, injuries” and many other play-calling determinants “are not accounted for in the current study” [PAGE 94]. Let the true data-generating process be:

\[
y=a x+\log b+\gamma W+\varepsilon
\]

where \(y=\log(B_p/B_r)\), \(x=\log(R_p/R_r)\), \(W\) is a dimensionless composite of physical or weather conditions, and \(\varepsilon\) is error. If the fitted model omits \(W\), the probability limit of the slope is:

\[
\hat a_{\mathrm{omit}}
\xrightarrow{p}
a+\gamma\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,W)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}
\]

This is the omitted-variable bias operator. Units: \(a\), \(\gamma\), \(x\), \(y\), and \(W\) are all dimensionless; \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\) is in squared log-ratio units. The proof is standard: regressing \(y\) on \(x\) alone loads the covariance between \(x\) and \(W\) onto \(\hat a\). The breakdown regime is \(\operatorname{Cov}(x,W)\neq 0\) with \(\gamma\neq 0\). Weather is a concrete case: wind or precipitation can reduce passing Success Rate, cold can affect grip and ball security, and field surface can affect cutting and rushing efficiency. Biomechanical cases include quarterback shoulder injury, receiver hamstring injury, offensive-line injury, and cumulative fatigue; these change the relative reinforcement value of passing versus rushing and are plausibly correlated with the observed reinforcement ratio. If coaches respond to these conditions by changing play-calling, \(x\) and \(W\) are correlated, and the estimated Success Rate advantage may be partly a proxy for omitted physical state. The veto is not that weather always reverses the result; the veto is that without the covariate block, the source cannot rule out \(\gamma \operatorname{Cov}(x,W)/\operatorname{Var}(x)\) being large enough to change the sign, magnitude, or significance of the slope difference. A full adjudication would add a pre-specified weather-injury-opponent-defense block and test whether the Success Rate advantage survives.
