Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit.

The paper’s central operator is the conditional track proposal classifier defined in Eq. (1) on [PAGE 3]: \( f(d_t, T_l(t-1); \theta) = p(d_t \in T_l(t) \mid T_l(t-1)) \). This estimates the probability that detection \(d_t\) belongs to track \(T_l\) given only that track’s own memory. The first multi-hop extension appears in Eq. (4) on [PAGE 4], where the conditioning set expands to all existing tracks \(T_{1:M}(t-1) = \{T_1(t-1), \dots, T_M(t-1)\}\), yielding \( f(d_t, T_{1:M}(t-1); \theta) = p(d_t \in T_l(t) \mid T_{1:M}(t-1)) \). This is not a mere notational convenience; it changes the information structure of the classifier from single-track to scene-aware.

The memory representation is governed by the Bilinear LSTM in Eq. (3) on [PAGE 3]. The prior LSTM memory vector \(h_{t-1}\) is reshaped into a matrix \(H^{\text{reshaped}}_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}\), and the new detection feature \(x_t\) is matched multiplicatively: \(m_t = g(H^{\text{reshaped}}_{t-1} x_t)\). Because the interaction is multiplicative rather than additive, each row of the memory matrix can specialize to a distinct appearance template, and the inner product serves as a correlation metric. The paper explicitly contrasts this with transformers [PAGE 3], noting that transformers require inner products between a new token and all previous tokens, which is memory-inefficient for online tracking over hundreds of frames. Bilinear LSTM keeps memory size fixed and automatically groups similar appearances into rows via the standard LSTM update rule.

The critical gray zone arises in the indefinite number of negative tracks. For a target track \(l\) and detection \(x_t\), the paper defines \(m^+_t\) as the positive matching and constructs \(M^-_t \in \mathbb{R}^{(M-1) \times 8}\) on [PAGE 4], where each row is the matching between \(x_t\) and one non-target track. Because \(M\) varies frame to frame, a fixed-size representation is required. Eq. (6) on [PAGE 4] applies max pooling element-wise: \(m^-_t(j) = \max_i M^-_t(i,j)\). This collapses an unbounded set of negative matchings into an 8-dimensional vector, which is concatenated with \(m^+_t\) in Eq. (7) on [PAGE 5] to form \(mall_t\). The softmax then outputs a binary decision.

Computational complexity creates another gray zone. Greedy data association runs in \(O(MN)\) time [PAGE 6], while a globally optimal scheme such as the Hungarian algorithm would cost \(O(MN^2)\) and require careful tuning. The paper accepts the myopia of greedy association in exchange for real-time operation (20–25 Hz on MOT 17/16, Tables 6–8). The association threshold is fixed at 0.5 [PAGE 6], and track termination occurs after more missing than actual detections or after \(N_{\text{miss}} = 60\) consecutive missing detections [PAGE 6]. These are hard operational singularities: if \(M=1\), the negative set \(M^-_t\) is empty and the model degenerates to the baseline Bilinear LSTM; if occlusion persists beyond 60 frames, the track is irrevocably terminated.

Training introduces further singularities. Long tracks exceed GPU memory, so the paper uses truncated BPTT with a temporal window of 10 [PAGE 11], processing entire sequences through multiple iterations with a moving window while sharing recurrent memories across iterations. (10) on [PAGE 5] to counter the severe class imbalance: for every positive track-detection pair there are \(MN-1\) negatives. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week temporal traps.

The intellectual genealogy begins with Hochreiter and Schmidhuber’s LSTM [16], cited on [PAGE 9], which introduced the gated recurrent cell that underlies every memory operator in this paper. Kim, Li, and Rehg’s Bilinear LSTM [18], from ECCV 2018 and cited extensively on [PAGES 2–3, 9], is the direct architectural ancestor: it replaced additive LSTM interactions with multiplicative input-memory products to enable template correlation and fixed-size multi-template memory. The present work inherits that memory structure but alters the conditioning set from \(T_l(t-1)\) to \(T_{1:M}(t-1)\).

The closest prior work is Sadeghian et al.’s “Tracking the Untrackable” [32], cited on [PAGES 2, 3, 6, 9], which trained recurrent networks to output joint track-detection probabilities using interaction cues. The paper is explicit about the distinction: [32] used “the most recent locations of other tracks, not appearances of other tracks” [PAGE 4]. Thus [32] modeled spatio-temporal interaction, whereas this paper models cross-appearance confusion. Fang et al.’s recurrent autoregressive networks [11], cited on [PAGES 2–3, 9], learn generative models of motion and appearance for data association, but again operate per-track or with limited cross-track conditioning. Brasó and Leal-Taixé’s neural solver [3], cited on [PAGE 2], uses relational or graph convolution over detections in the same and neighboring frames, but operates in a batch setting with full-video access, violating the online constraint. The authors position their multi-track pooling as “significantly simpler and faster than relational networks or graph convolutional networks which require multiple iterations of message passing” [PAGE 2].

A secondary lineage appears in social tracking: Pellegrini et al. [28] and Choi & Savarese [6] modeled interpersonal collision avoidance and activity cues. The present paper explicitly rejects those as orthogonal, stating it “focuses on incorporating multiple appearances from all tracks” rather than social behavior [PAGE 3]. The transformer comparison to Vaswani et al. [35] is also genealogical: both use multiplicative key-query interactions, but transformers scale poorly with sequence length, making them unsuitable for online MOT spanning hundreds of frames [PAGE 3].

The multi-week trap is the long-dependency training problem. Prior works [32, 18] trained on short, randomly clipped tracks, which bounds the temporal extent the recurrent model can learn. When tracks are long—spanning minutes of video, or effectively “weeks” of aggregated surveillance data—a model trained only on short segments cannot learn re-identification after long occlusion or appearance drift. This paper’s counter-trap is truncated BPTT with a 10-frame window [PAGE 11], applied across a moving window so that entire long sequences are processed over multiple training iterations while recurrent memories are shared. The trap is that gradients vanish beyond the window; the mitigation is cross-iteration memory persistence. A secondary trap is track termination at \(N_{\text{miss}}=60\) [PAGE 6]: if an object is occluded for more than 60 frames, the track dies and cannot be resurrected, creating a hard horizon beyond which the model has no memory. The track extension module [PAGE 6] attempts to bridge short gaps via Kalman-predicted boxes, but it cannot recover a track terminated by the 60-frame rule.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the real-time clock.

The tracker implements a crude theory of mind: each track classifier maintains not only a self-model of its target’s appearance memory but also a scene-model of all other tracks as potential confounders. This is recursive because the memory of track \(l\) is updated based on association outcomes, and those outcomes depend on the pooled appearance of all other tracks, whose memories are themselves being updated simultaneously. The system therefore reasons about “other minds” in the scene—other tracked objects that might be mistaken for the target.

Crisis habit regression occurs under heavy occlusion, crowding, or appearance similarity (e.g., “multiple men in black suits” [PAGE 1]). In such regimes, a tracker lacking multi-track pooling regresses to the habit of unilateral appearance matching: it compares the detection only against its own track memory, ignoring that a neighboring track may be a better visual match. This produces identity switches (IDS), which the ablation shows are reduced by pooling (11,940 vs 15,367 on MOT 19 val1, Table 2). The crisis is most acute when motion cues are disabled; then the appearance model does “heavy lifting” [PAGE 7], and without cross-track negative examples the classifier over-trusts its own template.

The counter-trap is the max-pooled negative memory \(m^-_t\). By injecting the strongest cross-track appearance response into the classifier input, the model learns to suppress detections that match another track too well. This is an explicit counter-measure to the identity-switch trap: it forces the classifier to discount a detection when a non-target track has high correlation with it. The training strategy reinforces this counter-trap by generating mini-batches with within-batch dependencies among tracks [PAGE 5], so the network experiences hard negative examples drawn from the same scene rather than isolated negatives.

The clock is the real-time constraint. Online MOT cannot use future frames or significant look-ahead [PAGE 1]. Greedy association at \(O(MN)\) [PAGE 6] is the fastest possible exact assignment per frame, and the 0.5 likelihood threshold [PAGE 6] is a hard temporal gate: associations must be irrevocable immediately. The near-online variant adds local smoothing [PAGE 7], which requires look-ahead frames and is therefore not strictly online. The recursion closes the loop: every frame’s association updates LSTM memories, which alter the next frame’s pooling and association. If the clock forces a wrong greedy choice at \(t\), the error propagates into \(h_t\), corrupting the multi-track pool at \(t+1\). There is no global revision mechanism, so the system’s theory of mind is strictly causal and myopic by design.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

The tracker stacks multiple veto layers that operate as hard constraints before any association is finalized.

(1) Association likelihood veto [PAGE 6]: every track-detection pair is scored by the softmax binary classifier, and the threshold is fixed at 0.5. Any pair below this is vetoed; no association occurs. This is the first and most direct veto, implemented at the output layer of the track proposal classifier.

(2) Motion gating veto [PAGE 6–7]: when the simple motion gating strategy is used (Tables 4–5), detections far from the predicted track location are excluded from consideration. This is a physical veto based on spatial proximity and kinematic plausibility, implemented as a separate learned motion model (traditional LSTM over bounding-box coordinates) combined with appearance.

(3) Track termination veto [PAGE 6]: a track is terminated if it accumulates more missing detections than actual detections, or if consecutive missing detections exceed \(N_{\text{miss}} = 60\). Once terminated, the track is excluded from data association and from the non-target memory matrix \(M^-_t\). This is a temporal veto that removes stale or lost objects from the scene model.

(4) Bounding-box coordinate correction veto [PAGE 6]: extended tracks generated by the Kalman filter may have inaccurate boxes. A bounding-box regressor corrects coordinates based on CNN features; false-positive detections are classified by an additional head [PAGE 11, Table 13]. This veto operates on the geometry of the detection before appearance matching.

(5) Max-pooling suppression veto [PAGE 4–5]: within the appearance classifier itself, the pooled negative vector \(m^-_t\) acts as an internal veto. If any non-target track produces a stronger correlation with the detection than the target track, the max-pooled response raises the negative evidence, and the softmax may reject the association even if the target-track match alone would pass 0.5.

Physical and environmental constraints not explicitly modeled: the paper does not contain a weather model, illumination-invariant preprocessing beyond ImageNet mean subtraction [PAGE 10], or explicit occlusion reasoning beyond track extension. Biomechanical plausibility is implicit in the motion model’s use of bounding-box location and scale, but there is no articulated human model or social-force constraint [PAGE 3 notes prior work [28] on collision avoidance is not used]. The absence of these vetoes defines the boundary conditions: in heavy rain, fog, or low light where ResNet-50 features degrade, or when objects touch and occlude for more than 60 frames, the veto stack has no recovery path.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
