The deepest causal gray zone is nonrandom rule adoption. Managers choose whether to follow the rule. Let \(S_t\in\mathbb{Z}\) be the goal differential for the focal team at match minute \(t\), measured in goals, with \(S_t = \text{goals for} - \text{goals against}\). Define the down-state indicator \(D_t = \mathbf{1}\{S_t \le -1\}\). Let \(u_i\) be the minute of the \(i\)-th substitution, in integer minutes, and let the paper’s critical thresholds be \(\tau_1 = 58\), \(\tau_2 = 73\), and \(\tau_3 = 79\) minutes [PAGE 10, PAGE 11]. The proposed rule \(R\) can be formalized as:
\[
R = \{(u_1,u_2,u_3): D_{58}=1 \Rightarrow u_1 \le 57;\; D_{73}=1 \Rightarrow u_2 \le 72;\; D_{79}=1 \Rightarrow u_3 \le 78\}.
\]
The paper phrases the thresholds as “before the 58th minute,” “prior to the 73rd minute,” and “prior to the 79th minute” versus “at the 58th minute or later,” and so on [PAGE 10]. For validation, define the adherence time \(t_a\) as the first moment the team substitutes in adherence to the rule, and define the binary success variable:
\[
Y_{\text{follow}} = \mathbf{1}\{S_T - S_{t_a} \ge 1\},
\]
where \(T\) is the end of the match and \(S_T - S_{t_a}\) is measured in goals [PAGE 12]. For non-followers, \(t_a\) is the earliest critical moment bypassed, with the same success definition [PAGE 12]. The reported success proportions are:
\[
\hat p_R = \frac{186}{440} = 0.4227,\qquad
\hat p_0 = \frac{173}{843} = 0.2052,
\]
with an absolute risk difference \(\Delta = \hat p_R - \hat p_0 = 0.2175\) in probability units [PAGE 13]. The reported 95% Wald interval is:
\[
\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}},
\]
which gives approximately \(0.376\) to \(0.469\) for the rule group and \(0.178\) to \(0.232\) for the non-rule group, matching the paper’s “38% to 47%” and “17% to 24%” statements [PAGE 1, PAGE 13]. The decision-tree split operator can be written as a CART impurity reduction. For binary \(Y\), Gini impurity is \(G(p)=2p(1-p)\), dimensionless. A split at threshold \(\tau\) maximizes:
\[
\Delta G(\tau)=G(p_{\text{parent}})-\frac{n_L}{n}G(p_L)-\frac{n_R}{n}G(p_R),
\]
where \(n_L\) and \(n_R\) are leaf counts in observations. The paper does not report its exact split criterion, so this is a standard interpretation, not a claim about the proprietary SAS Enterprise Miner settings [PAGE 9]. The exploratory ANOVA model for substitution minute is:
\[
u_{ij}=\mu+\alpha_{\text{score},j}+\epsilon_{ij},\qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2),
\]
with \(u_{ij}\) in minutes; the paper reports p-values 9.09E-06, 1.28E-10, and 4.10E-14 for score-position effects on subs 1, 2, and 3 [PAGE 7]. The home/away test is a two-sample t-statistic:
\[
t=\frac{\bar u_{\text{home}}-\bar u_{\text{away}}}{\sqrt{s_{\text{home}}^2/n_{\text{home}}+s_{\text{away}}^2/n_{\text{away}}}},
\]
with p-values 0.341, 0.182, and 0.06 for subs 1, 2, and 3 [PAGE 8]. Assumptions: independent substitutions across games, minimal within-game correlation, independent errors for ANOVA, approximately normal errors or large-sample t-approximation, non-missing timing, and no unmeasured confounding for causal interpretation. Exact singularity and breakdown regimes: \(n=0\) makes the proportion and Wald interval undefined; \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) makes \(p(1-p)=0\), so the Wald interval collapses and is not valid; \(n_L=0\) or \(n_R=0\) makes a decision-tree split degenerate; perfect separation makes threshold estimates unstable; duplicated training and validation observations inflate apparent validation [PAGE 12]; multiple testing across three substitutions and several leagues raises false-discovery risk; and the rule is formally undefined for a team that falls behind after minute 80 [PAGE 11].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a retrofit of operations research, survival analysis, and sports analytics onto a simple managerial clock rule. The author cites Hirotsu and Wright (2002), who “use dynamic programming to find optimal time to ‘change tactics’ based on data from the English premier league” [PAGE 2], and Hirotsu et al. (2009), who model tactical changes as a non-zero-sum game [PAGE 2]. Corral et al. (2007) “analyze substitutions based on the 2004-2005 Spanish league season” and find that teams behind substitute earlier [PAGE 2]. The paper also imports the Pedro Martinez pitch-count cautionary tale: “Davenport (2007) reports that analysts for the Boston Red Sox determined in the 2003 MLB season that the performance level of ace picture Pedro Martinez dropped after about 7 innings or 105 pitches” [PAGE 3]. That analogy retroactively frames the soccer substitution as a fatigue-threshold problem: wait too long and the starter’s performance has already collapsed. The paper then generalizes that caution into a three-threshold rule: 58, 73, and 79 minutes when behind [PAGE 10]. Retrocausally, the thresholds are not derived from physiology or from an explicit dynamic program; they are selected by a decision tree on 2009-2010-era league data and then interpreted as managerial critical points. That is a legitimate data-mining move, but it means the thresholds are historically contingent on the leagues, substitution rules, data vendor coding, and season. The paper’s own league table shows the contingency: Serie A success with the rule is 52.50%, La Liga 32.35%, World Cup 45.45%, Bundesliga 43.28%, Premier League 40.00%, and MLS 36.36% [PAGE 14]. A multi-week trap follows: club fixture congestion, travel, and recovery cycles can change the physiological meaning of minute 58 from one week to the next, while the rule treats minute 58 as a fixed clock point. A second multi-week trap is managerial survival bias: successful managers are described as rule-followers, and unsuccessful managers as rule-avoiders, but the paper’s cases include Mourinho, Van Gaal, Klopp, Benitez, and Grant [PAGE 14, PAGE 15]. Those narratives are vivid but do not establish that rule-following caused the outcomes. A third trap is era drift: the rule assumes three substitutions and no re-entry, which the paper states as FIFA’s restriction [PAGE 1]; if competition rules or substitution windows change, the historical thresholds lose their institutional meaning. The genealogy therefore supports the rule as a plausible mid-2000s to 2010-era hypothesis, not as a timeless law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive game has three layers. Level 0 is the rule-follower: when behind at minute 58, 73, or 79, substitute before the threshold [PAGE 10, PAGE 11]. Level 1 is the crisis-habit manager: under pressure, the manager regresses to reactive substitution, waiting for visible failure before acting. The paper names this tendency directly: managers “have a tendency to be more reactive than proactive with their substitutions” [PAGE 3]. Level 2 is the counter-trap opponent: if the opponent knows the focal team will substitute at fixed clocks, it can prepare for the new player, adjust its own substitution timing, or change tempo to exploit the transition period. The paper assumes this away: “the decision rule is believed to unaffected by the substitution pattern exhibited by the opposing team” [PAGE 11]. That assumption is exactly where recursive theory of mind bites. If both teams use the same public rule, then the rule stops being a private advantage and becomes a focal point. A manager at level 2 may reason: “My opponent expects me to act at 58; if I act at 52, I gain surprise but lose the ability to observe the opponent’s halftime adjustment.” A manager at level 3 may reason: “My opponent expects me to delay to 58 because I know they expect 58; therefore I should pre-commit earlier.” The paper does not model this recursion. The clock is a coordination device and a stressor: it creates a visible deadline, and managers may wait for the deadline rather than act on the actual player state. Crisis habit regression predicts that the 34.29% participation rate [PAGE 15] is not random ignorance but a psychological default: managers wait until the clock forces action. Counter-trap prediction: if the rule becomes common, its measured advantage should shrink because opponents adapt; the paper’s single-season validation cannot test that. Clock prediction: adherence should cluster just before the thresholds, and failures should cluster just after them. The paper’s own distribution supports clock clustering in general: sub 1 mean 56.38, median 59; sub 2 mean 70.23, median 71; sub 3 mean 80.58, median 82 [PAGE 4]. But those are population substitution times, not proof of strategic clock use. The recursive conclusion is that the rule is a weak equilibrium selection device under private use and an unstable focal point under common use.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The decision rule is a score-state and clock-layer operator, but physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can veto it. Let \(M(t)\in\{0,1\}\) be a medical veto indicator. A generic formal veto condition is:
\[
M(t)=1 \iff \exists v\in\{HR(t),T_c(t),RPE(t),\text{sprint-decrement}(t),\text{injury flag}(t)\}: v(t)\ge \theta_v,
\]
where \(HR\) is heart rate in beats per minute, \(T_c\) is core temperature in degrees Celsius, \(RPE\) is rating of perceived exertion in arbitrary units, and \(\theta_v\) is the league or medical-staff threshold in the same units. If \(M(t)=1\), the substitution action is medically indicated independent of whether the team is behind at minute 58, 73, or 79. The rule cannot override that. The paper itself excludes “substitutions due to injuries in the first half, or red cards by either team during influential moments” [PAGE 12], which is an admission that the rule’s domain excludes medical and disciplinary forced states. A biomechanical veto is also plausible: sprint mechanics, asymmetry, and hamstring load can cross a safety threshold before the score-state clock triggers. Formally, if a player’s measured sprint decrement or asymmetry exceeds a validated threshold, then the action “wait until the next critical point” is infeasible even if the team is ahead or tied. A weather veto is equally concrete: wet pitch reduces traction; heat and humidity raise thermal strain; lightning or extreme wind can suspend play. If ambient WBGT or pitch-surface conditions exceed competition policy thresholds, then the match state itself changes, and the historical decision-tree support is out of distribution. The veto proof is not that the rule is false; it is that the rule’s action set is defined only on the subset where \(M(t)=0\), disciplinary state is unchanged, and weather is within policy. Exact breakdown regimes: forced injury substitution before minute 58, red card before or during the critical window, match suspension or abandonment, extreme heat or cold, and any competition rule that changes the number or timing of substitutions. In those regimes, the conditional success probability estimated by the paper is undefined because the training data excluded or did not represent those states [PAGE 12].
