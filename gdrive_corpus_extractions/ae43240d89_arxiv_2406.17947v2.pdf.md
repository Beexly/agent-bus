<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(p\in[0,1]\) denote the in-group win probability at the time of a comment; \(p\) is a dimensionless probability, while Table 2 slopes are naturally read as change in frequency per percentage point, i.e. \(\mathrm{pp}^{-1}\), or per unit probability if \(p\) is not multiplied by 100. The tag set is \(\mathcal{L}=\{\mathrm{IN},\mathrm{OUT},\mathrm{OTHER}\}\). For comment \(c\), let \(T(c)\subseteq \mathcal{S}(c)\times\mathcal{L}\) be the tagged span-label set produced by an annotator or model. For a WP window \(w\) and tag \(a\), the per-comment frequency is
\[
f_a(w)=\frac{1}{|C_w|}\sum_{c\in C_w}\mathbf{1}\{a\in T(c)\},
\]
where \(C_w=\{c:p(c)\in w\}\), and \(f_a\) is dimensionless. The conditional/normalized version used in parts of Table 2 is
\[
\tilde f_a(w)=\frac{\sum_{c\in C_w}\mathbf{1}\{a\in T(c)\}}{\sum_{c\in C_w}\mathbf{1}\{\exists \ell\in\mathcal{L}:\ell\in T(c)\}},
\]
which is singular when the denominator is zero. The paper’s linear fit is
\[
f_a(w)=\alpha_a+\beta_a w+\varepsilon_w,\qquad
R^2_a=1-\frac{\sum_w \hat\varepsilon_{a,w}^2}{\sum_w (f_a(w)-\bar f_a)^2},
\]
with \(R^2\) undefined if the total sum of squares is zero. Table 2 reports slopes such as any reference \(-19.3\times10^{-4}\), no reference \(2.4\times10^{-4}\), in-group \(-2.8\times10^{-4}\), out-group \(2.5\times10^{-4}\), and \(R^2\) values up to 0.72 [PAGE 9]. The paper cautions: “The slopes for Any and no reference are calculated with frequencies normalized by total number of referents in a WP window. All other slopes are measured with frequencies normalized with only those comments that have references in that WP window” [PAGE 9]. Thus the slopes are not all on the same denominator and cannot be compared as if they were the same estimand.

Annotation reliability is quantified by Fleiss \(\kappa\):
\[
\kappa=\frac{P_o-P_e}{1-P_e},
\]
where \(P_o\) is observed agreement and \(P_e\) is chance agreement; both are dimensionless. The reported value is “0.69” [PAGE 5]. The crowd-vs-expert “accuracy” is “0.65 ± 0.005” [PAGE 5]. Tagging performance is micro-F1 per tag and weighted macro-F1 overall:

F1=\frac{2PR}{P+R},
\]
dimensionless, with partial credit for near-overlapping spans [PAGE 7]. In Table 1, GPT-4o overall scores range from 65.3 to 69.0 depending on condition, and Llama-3-8B overall scores range from 69.0 to 71.0 [PAGE 7]. The paper states that “Llama-3 nudges GPT-4o overall” [PAGE 7].

The state variable is introduced as “We chose WP as a succinct, non-linguistic description of the events preceding an utterance” [PAGE 3]. The main referent-form finding is quoted as: “Higher the win probability for the in-group, the more likely commenters are to abstract away from referring to the in-group” [PAGE 2]. The out-group summary is: “References to out-groups by commenters are rarer than in-group references, and remain stable over all win probabilities for the in-group” [PAGE 2]. The paper also reports that “with increasing WP, commenters are more likely to refer to the out-group with ‘they’ than the in-group” [PAGE 8]. These are regression and frequency claims, not identified causal claims.

Breakdown/singularity regimes are exact. First, temperature scaling uses
\[
T(p)=\sin(\pi p),
\]
quoted as “we set the temperature to \(\sin(\pi.\mathrm{WP})\)” [PAGE 7]. At \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), \(T(p)=0\); a softmax
\[
q_i=\frac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_j\exp(z_j/T)}

is undefined at \(T=0\) unless replaced by a hard argmax or clamped to \(T\ge\epsilon\). Second, \(\tilde f_a(w)\) is undefined at windows with zero references. Third, \(\kappa\) is singular when \(P_e=1\). Fourth, \(R^2\) is undefined under zero variance. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from linguistic-category theory to Linguistic Intergroup Bias (LIB) to computational intergroup-bias modeling and then to referent-form tagging. The paper says earlier LIB work “focused exclusively on the predicate, and the bias was described using an ad-hoc lexical categorization system ... over predicates” [PAGE 1]. The present move is to shift the target from predicate abstraction to referent form: names, nicknames, pronouns, team names, sub-team groups, and implicit sentence-level reference [PAGE 5]. That genealogy matters because it changes the measurement object. LIB predicts abstraction asymmetries in predicates; this paper predicts selection asymmetries in who is referred to and how. The paper frames the bridge as “the form of referencing the in-group or out-group can reveal subtle biases as well” [PAGE 2].

The sports-language lineage includes prior work on commentator racial bias and NBA fan intergroup contact; the paper distinguishes itself by focusing on in-group vs opponent and by grounding utterances in “numerical descriptions of the state-of-the-world” [PAGE 3]. The computational lineage includes Govindarajan et al. 2023a,b on generalized intergroup bias and affect, but the paper notes that neural models could discriminate in-group/out-group utterances while “causal probing of these models was inconclusive” [PAGE 1]. The present paper therefore retreats from causal probing to a tagging-plus-regression design. That is a scientific trade: more scale, less causal identification.

Multi-week traps are substantial. First, if this design is run week by week, team fixed effects and game fixed effects will dominate; a pooled slope can reflect schedule strength, opponent quality, prime-time games, or roster changes rather than a stable bias mechanism. Second, WP model recalibration across seasons or software versions can shift the regressor without shifting language. Third, Reddit moderation, thread creation rules, API access, and user migration can change comment density; the paper already notes that model accuracy “mostly followed comment density across WP” [PAGE 8]. Fourth, LLM tagging drift across model versions creates a measurement trap: a weekly slope change could be a model-update artifact. Fifth, annotation-protocol drift across annotator cohorts can change what counts as an implicit [SENT] reference. Sixth, repeated comments by the same users violate independence; multilevel modeling with user, game, and week random effects is required before any longitudinal claim. Seventh, the paper’s human ceiling is only “the average accuracy of crowd annotators against expert annotators” [PAGE 9], so a multi-week pipeline that treats LLM tags as gold will accumulate systematic error. The retrocausal lesson is that the paper’s contribution is an operationalization and a descriptive regularity, not a season-invariant law.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three competing readings: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. “Crisis habit regression” is the low-WP pattern: when the in-group is losing, commenters use inclusive first-person forms and strategic counterfactual talk. The paper gives examples such as “If we get a stop here and a touchdown on the next drive it’s a ballgame let’s fucking go” and “We need a rebuild of the players and coaching staff” [PAGE 9]. The paper explains this as “fans strategizing what their team should do, to increase their WP (and thus come back in the game)” [PAGE 9]. In recursive ToM terms, the speaker models an audience of fellow fans, shares responsibility through “we,” and proposes actions that presuppose collective agency.

“Counter-trap” is the high-WP protective move: when the in-group is likely to win, commenters abstract away from the in-group, refer less to specific entities, and sometimes shift to [OUT] or implicit [SENT] reference. The paper’s summary is that “the self-protective instinct is evident in the way they choose to refer to the in-group using we/us forms more often when losing, the reduced tendency to refer to the in-group using they/them, or to not refer to the in-group at all when winning” [PAGE 9]. The recursive component is that this is not simple celebration; it is audience design. A fan speaking to other fans can avoid naming the in-group when success is expected, thereby avoiding overclaiming or tempting fate, while still orienting to the out-group. The out-group pronoun “they” is described as “a classic ‘other-ing’ term” [PAGE 9], which is a ToM move: the speaker constructs a distant group perspective.

“Clock” is the game-state timeline. WP is a compressed clock-and-score state, not just an emotion. At high WP, comments become terse excitement such as “HOLY S**T” and “WHAT A THROW” [PAGE 8]; at low WP, comments become strategic and inclusive. The recursive model must therefore represent three nested beliefs: the commenter’s belief about the game state, the commenter’s belief about the audience’s expectations, and the annotator/model’s belief about the commenter’s intended referent. The paper’s annotation protocol explicitly requires “knowledge of American Football, commonsense reasoning over the meaning of an utterance in context of the live game,” and team/player knowledge [PAGE 4]. That is why the task is hard: the model must infer a speaker’s intended group from a compressed state and a parent comment. The documented LLM errors show failed recursive attribution: tagging a generic “WR” or “catch” as [OUT] is a failure to distinguish a role/event from a group referent [PAGE 8]. The counter-trap reading is therefore not a separate mechanism from the clock; it is a habit regression conditioned on expected success, mediated by audience models and constrained by the game timeline.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof is negative: the provided source does not report weather, injury, fatigue, or biomechanical covariates, so those layers cannot affirmatively validate the WP-referent slopes. The source’s physical layer is the football play state, described as “highly strategic” with outcomes dependent on “a coach’s strategies and plays in a (relatively) small number of discrete events” [PAGE 3]. WP is obtained from nflFastR for individual plays and timestamps [PAGE 3]. That is a compressed physical state, not a full causal model of weather, player health, or biomechanics.

Formally, suppose the true data-generating process is
\[
Y=\alpha+\beta p+\gamma Z+\varepsilon,
\]
where \(Y\) is a referent-frequency variable, \(p\) is in-group WP, and \(Z\) is an omitted physical/biomechanical/weather layer. The omitted-variable bias in the fitted slope is
\[
\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta]-\beta=\gamma\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(p,Z)}{\operatorname{Var}(p)}.
\]
If weather affects play calling and therefore \(p\), and also affects comment content or referent choice, then \(\gamma\neq0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(p,Z)\neq0\). Because the source reports no \(Z\), this bias is unidentifiable; no affirmative cross-layer veto clearance is possible. A biomechanical veto would trigger if injury events or player availability cluster in specific WP windows and alter which players are named. A weather veto would trigger if weather-driven game scripts change both WP trajectories and commenters’ referent forms. The paper’s own compression claim supports this caution: WP is “a succinct, non-linguistic description” [PAGE 3], not a physical decomposition. Therefore the correct adjudication is: the social-language layer is measured; the physical/biomechanical/weather layers are unmeasured; causal claims about WP as a mechanism are vetoed, while descriptive claims about covariation under the observed WP model remain admissible.
