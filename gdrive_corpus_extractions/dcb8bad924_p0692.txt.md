<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of the concepts presented on page 682 originates deeply in the mid-to-late 19th-century synthesis of evolutionary theory and physiology. Charles Darwin's *The Descent of Man* (1871) explicitly argued for the inheritance of acquired characteristics, observing things like the "shrug" in French children as vestiges of ancestral habituation. Jean-Baptiste Pierre Antoine de Monet, Baron de Lamarck, provided the original theoretical scaffold decades earlier, suggesting that use and disuse of organs directly altered heredity. 

Physiologists like Claude Bernard and later, Charles Richet, explored the "milieu intérieur," laying the groundwork for understanding how environmental conditions (bad living conditions causing scrofula/rickets) directly alter physiological structure. Brown-Séquard’s brutal experimental surgeries on the nervous system were quintessential examples of 19th-century reductionism—forcing extreme somatic shocks to test the boundaries of heritability.

James aggregates these diverse threads—Darwinian ethology, Lamarckian adaptation, and experimental neurophysiology—into a single psychological principle: that structure dictates function, and environmental impacts on structure can be heritable. Historically, this created a "multi-week trap" (or rather, a multi-decade trap) for genetics. The discovery of Mendelian laws and the subsequent Modern Synthesis violently rejected James's "principle is the same" assertion, branding it as soft inheritance/Lamarckism. It took nearly a century for the rediscovery of epigenetics and transgenerational trauma studies to validate the *phenomenology* of James's examples, even if the *mechanism* was not the direct genetic alteration he might have vaguely presumed, but rather methylation and histone acetylation. The retrocausal pull here is profound: our modern understanding of epigenetic memory literally looks backward to vindicate the intuitive observations of 1890.
