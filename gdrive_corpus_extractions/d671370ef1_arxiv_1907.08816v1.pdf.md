The source document describes a PTZ SLAM system whose central scientific claim is that a pure-rotation sports camera can be tracked without metric scene depth by representing landmarks as angular rays rather than 3D points. The derivation begins from the projection model in [PAGE 4 of 14]:  
“P = KQφQθ … PTZ … S[I|−C] prior”.  
This factorization separates a fixed prior extrinsic part, camera location C and tripod/base rotation S, from a time-varying PTZ part, pan θ, tilt φ, and focal length f. The formal consequence is that the observable dynamic state is low-dimensional. The paper states: “When camera location and base rotation are known, the two-point method provides the minimum-configuration solution for PTZ cameras” [PAGE 2 of 14]. In information-theoretic terms, the measurement model only constrains a three-dimensional intrinsic/extrinsic dynamic submanifold, not full 6-DoF camera translation. This is why the system chooses rays as landmarks: “Rays overcome the missing depth in pure-rotation cameras” [PAGE 1 of 14].

The gray-zone audit starts with the physical observability problem. For a camera rotating about its optical center, the image formation model lacks a baseline. The paper explicitly says: “Because no parallax is observed, a robust estimation of depth is almost impossible” [PAGE 1 of 14]. Therefore, a conventional EKF-SLAM state containing Euclidean landmark positions is either rank-deficient or forced into artificial assumptions. The paper’s ray representation avoids that by parameterizing each landmark as a direction in tripod coordinates. The formal ray projection is given as a normalized angular vector and the backprojection equations recover ray angles from pixel coordinates [PAGE 4 of 14]. The state vector is written as “pan, tilt and focal length and their velocities” [PAGE 4 of 14], with each landmark being “a ray in tripod coordinates” [PAGE 4 of 14]. This is not a cosmetic simplification; it changes the measurement Jacobian from depth-sensitive bearing-range estimation to angular reprojection estimation.

A second gray-zone issue is the relationship between PTZ tracking and homography-based mosaicing. Homographies are exact for a single plane or for pure rotation when all observed structure is effectively projected onto one reference plane. The paper contrasts its method with panoramic and keyframe-based systems and notes that “we use rays as landmarks for tracking and mapping, which facilitates the EKF SLAM system” [PAGE 3 of 14]. Baseline 3, called EKF-H, replaces the PTZ camera model by a homography and places landmarks on a mosaic plane [PAGE 7 of 14]. The empirical consequence is severe: “In baseline 3, more errors are accumulated compared with our method” [PAGE 8 of 14], and the qualitative comparison says “EKF-H loses the tracking at the first fast-moving period” [PAGE 10 of 14]. The formal failure mode is that a planar homography cannot absorb non-planar depth variation, changing player configurations, or rapidly varying focal length without introducing systematic reprojection residuals.

A third gray-zone area is dynamic foreground. Sports scenes contain players, referees, and equipment that move independently of the background. The paper integrates “player detection with the SLAM system” and removes keypoints inside player bounding boxes [PAGE 6 of 14]. The stated effect is that the system can “remove most of keypoints that are on player bodies while only sacrifice a few background keypoints” [PAGE 6 of 14]. This is a probabilistic trade: detection boxes create false negatives for players and false positives for background, but the net effect reduces outlier contamination in both tracking and relocalization. The quantitative comparison against Baseline 1, where player detection is removed, supports this claim, although not uniformly for every scalar metric [PAGE 8 of 14].

The final audit concerns breakdown regimes. The camera model assumes “square pixels, a principal point at the image center and no lens distortion” [PAGE 4 of 14]. If those assumptions fail, K is misspecified and the pan/tilt/focal decomposition becomes biased. The ray parameterization also has angular singularities: tan(θ) diverges near ±π/2, and the backprojection expression for tilt contains a denominator involving X and Z that becomes ill-conditioned when the ray direction approaches a degenerate vertical direction. The system also depends on initialization: “the system assumes that the camera location and the first-frame camera pose are known” [PAGE 2 of 14], and later acknowledges that “our system requires the camera pose of the first frame and camera base parameters to initialize the system” [PAGE 10 of 14]. Thus the claim of robustness is conditional on correct base calibration, stable feature tracking, sufficient static background visibility, and successful relocalization when tracking is lost.

The core projection operator is the PTZ camera matrix from [PAGE 4 of 14]:

\[
P = K Q_{\phi} Q_{\theta} S [I \mid -C].
\]

Units and assumptions: \(C\) is camera location in world coordinates, expressed in metric length units; \(S\) is a dimensionless rotation matrix for the fixed tripod/base; \(Q_{\theta}\) and \(Q_{\phi}\) are dimensionless rotation matrices for pan and tilt; \(K\) is the camera intrinsic matrix. The source states: “K is the camera matrix in which the focal length f is the only free parameter because we assume square pixels, a principal point at the image center and no lens distortion” [PAGE 4 of 14]. Thus \(f\) has units of pixels, while angles \(\theta,\phi\) are formally in radians in the derivation, although the paper reports degrees.

The dynamic camera state is written in [PAGE 4 of 14] as:

\[
x_v = [\theta, \phi, f, \delta\theta, \delta\phi, \delta f]^T.
\]

Units: \(\theta,\phi\) in radians, \(f\) in pixels, \(\delta\theta,\delta\phi\) in radians per second, and \(\delta f\) in pixels per second. A landmark ray is written as:

\[
r_i = [\theta_i, \phi_i]^T,
\]

with angular units. The ray projection equation from [PAGE 4 of 14] is:

\[
P_{\text{ptz}}(r_i)
=
K Q_{\phi} Q_{\theta}
\begin{bmatrix}
\tan(\theta_i) \\
-\tan(\phi_i) \\
\sqrt{\tan^2(\theta_i)+1} \\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The source explains that this represents the ray “as a 3D point in the plane of Z = 1” [PAGE 4 of 14]. The operator maps an angular landmark to a homogeneous image projection. The singularity regime includes \(\theta_i \to \pm \pi/2\), where \(\tan(\theta_i)\) diverges, and the representation cannot directly encode rays whose normalized direction would require division by zero in the backprojection step.

The inverse operator is the backprojection equation from [PAGE 4 of 14]:

\[
\theta_i = \arctan\left(\frac{X}{Z}\right),
\qquad
\phi_i = \arctan\left(\frac{-Y}{\sqrt{X^2+Z^2}}\right),
\]

with

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
X \\
Y \\
Z
\end{bmatrix}
=
Q_{\theta}^{-1}
Q_{\phi}^{-1}
K^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_i \\
y_i \\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Units: \(x_i,y_i\) are pixel coordinates; \(K^{-1}\) maps pixels to camera-plane coordinates scaled by focal length; \(X,Y,Z\) are dimensionless camera-plane coordinates after rotation; the arctangent outputs are radians. Breakdown occurs if \(Z=0\) in \(\arctan(X/Z)\), or if \(X^2+Z^2=0\) in the tilt denominator. These are exact singular configurations for the ray representation.

The EKF tracking component is described in [PAGE 4 of 14] as using “velocity models to predict camera poses and the projection of landmarks in the image.” A formal constant-velocity prediction can be written as:

\[
x_{v,t}^{-} = F x_{v,t-1}^{+},
\]

\[
P_t^{-} = F P_{t-1}^{+} F^T + Q,
\]

where \(F\) integrates the angular and focal velocities over time step \(\Delta t\), \(P\) is the state covariance, and \(Q\) is process noise. The measurement residual is the reprojection error:

\[
e_i = p_i - P_{\text{ptz}}(\hat r_i),
\]

with units of pixels. The optimization in [PAGE 6 of 14] is written as:

\[
\{\theta,\phi,f\}
=
\arg\min
\sum_i
\|p_i - P(\hat r_i)\|^2.
\]

The source adds that “we use the RANSAC method [14] to remove outliers” and “we use the two-point method [7] to estimate the initial camera pose inside of the RANSAC method” [PAGE 6 of 14]. The two-point method is described as a “minimum-configuration solution” when camera location and base rotation are known [PAGE 2 of 14]. The exact minimum-number regime is therefore tied to correspondences that are both geometrically stable and consistent with the known prior extrinsics.

The mapping/relocalization operator is the online pan-tilt forest. The regressor is defined as:

\[
\hat r = h(v),
\]

where \(v\) is an appearance descriptor, such as SIFT, and \(\hat r\) is a predicted ray [PAGE 5 of 14]. The tree split criterion is given in [PAGE 5 of 14] as:

\[
E(S_n,\pi_n)
=
\sum_{(x,r)\in S_n}
(r-\bar r)^2
-
\sum_{j\in L,R}
\left(
\sum_{(x,r)\in S_j^n}
(r-\bar r)^2
\right).
\]

Units: if \(r\) is angular, the variance has units of radians squared; if represented in degrees, degree squared. The split maximizes reduction in angular variance. The online update rule is given in [PAGE 6 of 14]: “The correctness is measured by thresholding the angular errors (0.1◦). When the correctness is higher than a threshold (50%), we update one randomly selected decision tree in ht(.) by adding new examples St+1. Otherwise, we add a new tree” [PAGE 6 of 14]. A formal version is:

\[
\rho_t
=
\frac{1}{|S_{t+1}|}
\sum_{s\in S_{t+1}}
\mathbf{1}
\left(
\text{angular error}(r,\hat r)
\leq
0.1^\circ
\right).
\]

If \(\rho_t > 0.5\), update an existing tree; otherwise add a new tree. The threshold \(0.1^\circ\) is approximately \(1.745\times10^{-3}\) radians.

Important breakdown regimes are explicitly or implicitly present in the source. First, pure rotation makes metric depth unobservable: “Because no parallax is observed, a robust estimation of depth is almost impossible” [PAGE 1 of 14]. Second, planar homography is invalid when the scene is not well modeled by one plane; EKF-H accumulates errors on the real basketball sequence [PAGE 8 of 14]. Third, feature constancy fails under severe motion blur and rapid rotation; the paper notes that sports cameras “move very fast so that images can be severely blurred” [PAGE 2 of 14], and later says “ORB matching is not stable when cameras rotate rapidly. On the other hand, SIFT is more reliable for rapid camera rotation” [PAGE 9 of 14]. Fourth, the EKF parameter settings are physically interpreted: “the variance of keypoints location, pan/tilt angles and focal lenth is 0.1 pixels, 0.001◦and 1 pixel” [PAGE 9 of 14], with focal length variance effectively measured in pixel units.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits inside a lineage that moves from general visual SLAM to rotation-only panoramic tracking and then to sports-specific PTZ calibration. The EKF-SLAM foundation is explicitly cited through MonoSLAM-style probabilistic feature mapping: the source says “EKF-SLAM uses a probabilistic feature-based map that represents the current estimates of the camera and all landmarks with uncertainty” [PAGE 3 of 14]. That lineage contributes the state-covariance update logic, but it cannot be transplanted unchanged because sports PTZ cameras violate the general-motion assumption by having fixed translation and rapid rotation.

The immediate ancestor is PTZ camera calibration. The source states: “Recently, Chen et al. [7] proposed a novel PTZ camera model. They decompose the projection matrix into two parts” [PAGE 3 of 14]. The first part is fixed camera location and base rotation; the second part is dynamic pan, tilt, and focal length. This decomposition is the retro-causal hinge: without it, the system would need either full 3D structure or a reference mosaic. The paper explicitly extends “this camera model shows superior performance in single frame calibration” into “single image calibration to multiple (sequential) image calibration and tracking” [PAGE 3 of 14].

A second ancestral branch is panoramic and mosaic SLAM. The source says “Our work closely relates to panoramic SLAM systems” and notes that those systems “assume rotation-only camera motion and track the camera in three degrees of freedom” [PAGE 1 of 14]. Earlier methods use plane-induced homographies or cylindrical images [PAGE 1 of 14]. The paper’s break from that branch is the use of rays rather than planar or cylindrical mosaic coordinates. This is not merely representational; it changes relocalization into a regression problem over appearance-to-ray pairs.

A third branch is random-forest relocalization. The paper develops an “online pan-tilt forest” as the global map and says the forest “predicts the landmark by its appearance in the image” [PAGE 5 of 14]. It adapts “the online random forest method from [35]” and states that “online training decreases the training time without decreasing the prediction accuracy” [PAGE 6 of 14]. This connects the work to scene coordinate regression forests and their descendants, but adapts them to angular ray outputs instead of full 3D coordinates.

The multi-week traps visible in the genealogy are recurring failure modes. The first trap is over-parameterization: if the camera state is expanded to full pose or if depth is introduced unnecessarily, estimation becomes less robust when outliers exist. The paper says the chosen representation “has the smallest number of degrees of freedom” and therefore “the estimation is more robust than over-parameterized methods when outliers exist” [PAGE 1 of 14]. The second trap is homography drift on non-planar sports scenes. The EKF-H baseline demonstrates this by accumulating errors and losing track during fast motion [PAGE 8 of 14], [PAGE 10 of 14]. The third trap is keyframe relocalization collapse under repeated patterns or outliers; the paper reports that nearest-neighbor search is unstable on basketball because “the background has repeating patterns that are difficult to distinguish in the feature space” [PAGE 10 of 14]. The fourth trap is descriptor instability under rapid rotation, which forces a choice of SIFT over ORB despite computational cost [PAGE 9 of 14].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system can be modeled as a recursive predictive agent whose “theory of mind” is not human intention but camera-operator behavior, player motion, and the physical limits of feature tracking. The paper says the cameras have “three degrees of control signals (speed of pan, tilt angles and zoom levels)” [PAGE 2 of 14]. That sentence is crucial: the broadcaster’s control loop is itself a dynamical agent. The SLAM system must infer the operator’s commanded motion from pixels while simultaneously rejecting the independent motion of players.

The crisis habit regression is the default failure mode when tracking becomes difficult. A conventional tracking habit would rely on local feature constancy and frame-to-frame continuity. Under rapid PTZ motion, this habit regresses because image blur and large interframe displacement weaken descriptor matching. The paper states that “Sports cameras move very fast so that images can be severely blurred, causing difficulty for feature matching/tracking” [PAGE 2 of 14]. It also reports that ORB becomes unstable under rapid rotation while SIFT remains more reliable [PAGE 9 of 14]. Thus the system must anticipate that the cheapest habit, fast descriptor matching, will fail precisely when the camera moves quickly.

The counter-trap is the paper’s multi-layer architecture. First, the PTZ camera model reduces the search space to physically meaningful parameters. Second, player detection removes foreground outliers before they contaminate the EKF update. Third, the online pan-tilt forest provides relocalization candidates when tracking is lost. Fourth, RANSAC and the two-point method provide a minimal robust estimator inside the optimization [PAGE 6 of 14]. The counter-trap is therefore not a single algorithm but a hierarchy of defenses: physical parameterization, semantic masking, regression-based relocalization, and robust estimation.

The clock layer is the computational and temporal budget. The paper notes that the implementation is not optimized and that “the speed can be improved by implementing with C++ and using a GPU to compute image features” [PAGE 9 of 14]. It also reports a Python implementation with player detection on a TITAN Xp GPU [PAGE 9 of 14]. In a broadcasting setting, the clock is not merely latency; it determines whether keyframes can be added, forest trees updated, and relocalization attempted before the camera moves into a new viewing configuration. The online forest update rule reflects this clock: if recent keyframes are predicted correctly above 50%, the system updates an existing tree; otherwise it adds a new tree [PAGE 6 of 14]. That rule is a recursive policy for managing model age versus model novelty.

A deeper recursive model is that the system must anticipate its own loss of tracking. The paper’s qualitative example shows EKF-H losing tracking during a fast pan and then recovering with drift, whereas the proposed method tracks through the fast-moving period [PAGE 10 of 14]. The proposed system’s advantage is not only better local estimation but better anticipation of the crisis regime: when appearance changes too quickly, it switches to a global regressor that can use multiple keyframes rather than relying on a single nearest keyframe. In recursive terms, the system models not only the scene but also the probability that its own current estimate is invalid.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto layer begins with the camera mounting constraint. The model requires a fixed camera location and base rotation. The source states that “the system assumes that the camera location and the first-frame camera pose are known” [PAGE 2 of 14] and that the prior part \(S[I\mid -C]\) “can be pre-estimated” because the tripod is fixed [PAGE 4 of 14]. If the camera location changes, if the tripod is bumped, or if the base rotation is misestimated, the prior extrinsic operator is invalid. In that regime, the PTZ decomposition becomes a biased approximation and the reprojection residual can no longer be attributed only to pan, tilt, and focal error.

The biomechanical veto layer concerns moving foreground agents. Players are not random noise; they are structured, high-contrast, independently moving objects that often cover large image areas. The paper says “moving objects (e.g. the players) occlude static objects in the background and inject many outliers for feature tracking” [PAGE 2 of 14]. The system responds by detecting players and removing keypoints inside bounding boxes [PAGE 6 of 14]. The formal veto is: let \(N_{\text{static}}\) be the number of validated static background correspondences after player masking and RANSAC. If \(N_{\text{static}}\) falls below the minimum required by the two-point or EKF update, pose observability collapses. The paper’s minimum-configuration claim makes this exact: “the two-point method provides the minimum-configuration solution for PTZ cameras” when location and base rotation are known [PAGE 2 of 14]. If foreground removal eliminates too many background points, or if player detection fails and moving points enter the estimator, the covariance update is corrupted.

The weather and visibility veto layer is not directly quantified in the paper, but it follows formally from the feature-constancy assumption. Sports cameras are exposed to rain, glare, shadows, low light, or atmospheric haze. These conditions reduce contrast and descriptor reliability. The source already notes severe blur from fast motion: “images can be severely blurred, causing difficulty for feature matching/tracking” [PAGE 2 of 14]. A formal visibility veto can be stated without empirical invention: if the number of stable keypoints \(N_t\) after detection and matching is less than the minimum required for the two-point initialization, or if the RANSAC inlier fraction is too low to distinguish the correct camera motion from outlier contamination, then the pose update is vetoed. The system then depends on relocalization.

The relocalization veto is adjudicated by the online forest. The paper reports that when tracking is lost, “the relocalization component recovers the camera pose using an online random forest which is gradually updated by the mapping component” [PAGE 2 of 14]. The forest outputs multiple ray candidates for a pixel and the optimizer chooses the candidate minimizing reprojection error [PAGE 5 of 14]. The veto condition is that the forest’s predicted rays must produce a RANSAC-consistent set of correspondences sufficient for the two-point or EKF optimization. If the background is repetitive, appearance alone may be non-discriminative; the paper acknowledges that nearest-neighbor search is unstable because “the background has repeating patterns that are difficult to distinguish in the feature space” [PAGE 10 of 14]. The random forest mitigates this because “random forest methods optimize tree parameters using both features and rays” [PAGE 10 of 14].

The final cross-layer proof is that the proposed method is robust only when all layers align: calibrated prior extrinsics, sufficient static background visibility, successful player masking, stable descriptor matching, and a relocalization map that remains discriminative under appearance change. The paper’s own limitations section confirms the dependence on initialization and dataset scarcity: “our system requires the camera pose of the first frame and camera base parameters to initialize the system” [PAGE 10 of 14], and “the experiments on real data are limited because there are no large public datasets available” [PAGE 10 of 14]. Therefore any claim of general robustness must be restricted to regimes where these cross-layer conditions hold.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Counterfactual veto 1: remove player detection and keep all other components unchanged. The verifiable prediction is that moving foreground keypoints will increase outlier pressure, especially in pan and focal-length estimation where players dominate the image. The source implements this as Baseline 1: “We remove the player detection component in our system and keep the remaining settings the same” [PAGE 7 of 14]. The measured consequence is reported in Table 3 [PAGE 8 of 14]. For basketball, Baseline 1 has pan error 0.50±0.46 degrees versus 0.17±0.14 degrees for the full method, and focal-length error 31.95±25.37 pixels versus 19.28±18.68 pixels. For soccer, Baseline 1 has pan error 0.28±0.32 degrees versus 0.08±0.07 degrees, and focal-length error 110.58±62.66 pixels versus 63.70±61.66 pixels. The tilt result on basketball is not uniformly worse for Baseline 1, but the overall claim that player detection improves accuracy is supported by the paper: “The result of baseline 1 shows that the player detection part improves the accuracy of our system” [PAGE 8 of 14]. The veto prediction is therefore verifiable: removing player masks degrades robustness when foreground motion is large.

Counterfactual veto 2: replace the PTZ ray model with a homography-based EKF under non-planar sports scenes and fast angular motion. The verifiable prediction is that reprojection error will rise sharply and tracking may be lost. The source implements this as Baseline 3, EKF-H, which “uses an off-line mapping for PTZ camera tracking” and places landmarks on a mosaic plane [PAGE 7 of 14]. On the synthetic sequences, Table 1 reports that EKF-H has maximum reprojection error 16.0 pixels on sequence 3, while the proposed method has maximum 0.5 pixels [PAGE 7 of 14]. On real data, Baseline 3 has much larger errors, for example basketball focal-length error 395.40±303.88 pixels versus 19.28±18.68 pixels for the proposed method [PAGE 8 of 14]. The qualitative comparison says “EKF-H loses the tracking at the first fast-moving period” [PAGE 10 of 14]. The formal veto is that a planar homography cannot represent non-planar structure or rapidly changing focal geometry without accumulating systematic residual error.

Counterfactual veto 3: use nearest-keyframe relocalization instead of the online pan-tilt forest when outlier contamination increases. The verifiable prediction is that correctness collapses for keyframe relocalization while the forest remains stable. The source compares “traditional keyframe-based method” with the proposed forest and reports: “Our method achieves much higher correctness than the keyframe-based method (e.g. 100% vs. 68% when outlier ratio is 40%)” [PAGE 8 of 14]. Table 2 gives the full trend: at 40% outliers, keyframe correctness is 67.7%, forest correctness is 100%; at 50% outliers, keyframe correctness is 47.0%, forest correctness is 99.7% [PAGE 7 of 14]. The formal explanation is that the forest uses multiple keyframes and appearance-to-ray regression, whereas nearest-keyframe relocalization depends on selecting one sufficiently similar reference frame. In scenes with repeated patterns or high outlier fractions, nearest-neighbor selection becomes unstable, as the paper notes for NNS on basketball: “the background has repeating patterns that are difficult to distinguish in the feature space” [PAGE 10 of 14].

These three vetoes are independently testable because each changes one operator while preserving the rest of the system. The first tests the semantic masking operator. The second tests the geometric camera-model operator. The third tests the relocalization memory operator. In each case, the source provides baseline ablations, numerical error metrics, or correctness thresholds that can be checked directly against the reported tables and text.
