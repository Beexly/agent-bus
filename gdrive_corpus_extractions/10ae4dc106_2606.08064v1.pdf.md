<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s core reasoning is a three-layer causal decomposition of a cooperative physical task: first, learn a reusable decentralized rope-turning skill; second, schedule that skill so its periodic rope phase aligns with a player’s jump phase; third, broaden the set of player behaviors used during training so the scheduler does not collapse to one narrow rhythm. The formalization begins with a partially observable Markov game, quoted on [PAGE 2] as  
“M = ⟨N, S, {Ai}i∈N , P, {Ri}i∈N , γ, {Ωi}i∈N , O⟩”,  
and the learning objective is the expected discounted return given on [PAGE 3]. The multi-hop derivation is: if each turner’s policy only receives a compact command rather than a full rope trajectory, then the turners can remain decentralized during execution; if the rope phase can be estimated from a subset of rope points, then a high-level scheduler can correct phase error; if the scheduler is trained against diverse jumping policies, then its phase correction remains valid under varied player morphology and rhythm.

The first gray zone is geometric. The rotation center and rotation axis are defined from the two turners’ base positions on [PAGE 4]. This assumes the two humanoids remain horizontally separated. If their horizontal separation collapses to zero, the normalization denominator in the rotation-axis equation becomes zero, so the command geometry becomes undefined. The second gray zone is temporal. The jump rhythm is represented by frequency, ground ratio, phase, and a binary clock on [PAGE 4]. The binary clock is a coarse contact indicator, but successful skipping depends on continuous phase alignment. The paper itself states on [PAGE 2] that  
“even small phase mismatches may result in rope-player collisions or failed jumps.”  
Thus the control problem is not merely trajectory tracking; it is a synchronization problem under partial observability.

The third gray zone is phase estimation. Equation (2) on [PAGE 5] constructs the rope rotation phase from an angular-velocity estimate and a set of rope points. The angular-velocity estimate is a least-squares fit of point velocities to rigid rotation about the estimated center. If the rope points are noisy, sparse, or nearly collinear with the estimated axis, the phase estimate can become ill-conditioned. The phase is also cyclic. A phase-tracking reward that does not respect modulo-one equivalence can create artificial discontinuities near the wrap point. The paper’s reward table lists a phase-tracking term on [PAGE 16], so the implementation must either use a wrapped phase error or accept instability near the phase boundary.

The fourth gray zone is player modeling. The scheduler observes the player as an oriented bounding box, including position, orientation, and size, on [PAGE 5]. This is a simplification: the player’s actual feet contact timing and body deformation are not directly observed. The diverse-player discovery stage addresses this by training a latent-conditioned jumping policy with an IPM-based diversity objective, quoted on [PAGE 5] as an objective that maximizes discrepancy between states visited under different latent variables. The practical effect is to prevent the scheduler from overfitting to one jumping style.

The fifth gray zone is real-world sensing. The real-world system depends on optical motion capture to obtain rope markers, turner base poses, and player body parts, as stated on [PAGE 6]. The paper explicitly identifies future work as “enabling humanoids to collaborate with diverse human partners in the wild via on-board sensors rather than MoCap system” on [PAGE 8]. Therefore, any deployment claim is conditional on controlled sensing. If marker visibility, calibration, or communication latency degrades, the phase and collision-avoidance guarantees weaken.

\[

Units: rewards may be task-scaled but are treated as dimensionless after weighting; \(\gamma\) is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: as \(\gamma \to 1\), variance of return estimates grows and credit assignment becomes harder; if rewards are unbounded and \(\gamma=1\), the return may diverge.

\[
\Pi_{xy}([x,y,z]^\top) = [x,y,0]^\top .

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of the paper moves from single-agent humanoid athletics to multi-agent coordination with a shared deformable object. The introduction places humanoid sports within embodied intelligence and cites prior work on running, dancing, parkour, and other athletic tasks [PAGE 1]. The key historical break is that long rope skipping is not an isolated whole-body skill; it is a joint activity requiring two turners and a player. The authors state that “long rope skipping requires two rope turners to cooperatively swing the rope while adapting to a player under different jumping rhythms” [PAGE 1]. This sentence is the conceptual pivot: the task is neither pure locomotion nor pure manipulation, but a coupled rhythmic coordination problem.

The first lineage is multi-agent reinforcement learning. The paper uses MAPPO under centralized training and decentralized execution [PAGE 3]. It also discusses value decomposition and decentralized policy gradient methods as ways to avoid the curse of dimensionality in the joint state-action space [PAGE 2]. The historical trap here is to treat the two turners as a single centralized controller. The ablation called Single Agent concatenates observations and actions and performs worse, which the authors interpret as introducing “redundant and highly coupled observation-action spaces” [PAGE 6]. Thus the genealogy shows a move from centralized single-agent control to structured decentralized control with a compact command interface.

The second lineage is motion tracking and replay. Early humanoid control often used trajectory replay or blind motion tracking. The paper explicitly argues that such methods are insufficient for rope manipulation because “the rope is a deformable and underactuated object whose intermediate configuration cannot be directly controlled” [PAGE 1]. The Open Loop baseline reproduces evaluation trajectories and achieves smoother actions but much worse rope accuracy; the paper says its “rope manipulation accuracy drops substantially” [PAGE 6]. This is a multi-week trap: a replayed motion may look locally smooth but fail when the rope’s state diverges from the recorded trajectory.

The third lineage is deformable-object modeling. The rope is approximated in simulation by many small rigid capsules connected by passive joints [PAGE 6]. The observation uses a subset of rope points, randomly sampled and ordered by distance to the attached end [PAGE 4]. This avoids assuming full rope state observability. The ablation “w/o Segment Sampling” shows mild degradation, supporting the claim that random segment sampling improves robustness to realistic sparse sensing [PAGE 6].

The fourth lineage is phase-based rhythm control. The jumping policy is trained to align a binary feet-contact mask with a binary clock, where the rhythm tuple contains frequency, ground ratio, phase, and clock signal [PAGE 4]. The scheduler then aligns the rope rotation phase with the jump phase. The paper states that ideal skipping requires the rope phase to pass through zero when the player is at the highest point [PAGE 5]. This lineage comes from periodic locomotion and clock-driven motion generation, but the novelty is coupling the clock to an external player and a deformable rope.

The fifth lineage is diversity discovery through information-theoretic objectives. The jumping policy is conditioned on a latent variable, and an IPM-based objective encourages different latent codes to produce different state visitation distributions [PAGE 5]. The implementation uses spectral normalization to approximate a Wasserstein-1 constraint [PAGE 5]. The multi-week trap here is overfitting to a single partner style. The ablation “w/o Player Diversity” underperforms, and the authors say “introducing diverse counterparts during training is crucial for improving the generalization and adaptability” [PAGE 7].

The final lineage is sim-to-real transfer. The system uses dynamics randomization and observation noise, including rope length, density, joint drive properties, friction, mass, and push disturbances [PAGE 14]. However, the real-world deployment still depends on motion capture and external rhythm signals delivered through DDS services [PAGE 13]. The limitation section identifies onboard sensing in the wild as future work [PAGE 8]. The genealogical trap is therefore not only algorithmic but infrastructural: the method is robust within a sensed and controlled environment, not automatically robust in an unconstrained physical environment.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system requires recursive belief modeling because each component acts under a different information set. The rope turners do not directly observe the player. The scheduler does not directly control the rope; it only outputs commands to the low-level manipulation policy. The player policy does not know the turners’ internal commands; it responds to its own rhythm clock and proprioception. The paper formalizes this separation by training a decentralized manipulation policy, a centralized scheduling policy, and a diverse player policy [PAGE 3]. The scheduler’s observation includes rhythm, rope morphology history, and player OBB history [PAGE 5]. The player’s observation includes rhythm and proprioceptive history [PAGE 4]. The turners’ observation includes command, rope morphology, and proprioception [PAGE 4].

Crisis habit regression occurs when a component falls back to a learned default under stress. For the low-level turners, the default is to track the commanded rotation center, width, and angular velocity. If the player drifts laterally or changes rhythm abruptly, the turners may continue tracking the last command because they cannot see the player. The scheduler must infer the player’s phase and correct the command. If the player state estimate becomes noisy, the scheduler may overcorrect, producing phase jitter. If the rhythm signal is delayed, the scheduler may chase a stale phase. The paper notes that rhythm signals are managed by a control terminal and transported with MoCap information through DDS services [PAGE 13]. Any latency or jitter in that transport chain becomes a belief error in the scheduler.

The counter-trap is the diverse-player discovery mechanism. If the scheduler is trained only against one jumping policy, it learns a narrow mapping from player state to rope command. The IPM objective expands the set of state visitation patterns by conditioning the jumping policy on latent variables [PAGE 5]. The curriculum enables diversity only after the jumping policy reaches a predefined task performance threshold, so diversity does not destroy the basic jumping behavior [PAGE 5]. This prevents a regression where the player policy produces degenerate motions merely to maximize latent discrepancy. The paper also includes regularization rewards for feet distance, joint symmetry, feet contact force, and undesired contacts [PAGE 16], which further constrain the diverse policies to remain physically plausible.

The clock is the central shared temporal operator. The binary clock \(c = \mathbf{1}(\phi \ge \kappa)\) gives a coarse ground-contact schedule, while the continuous phase \(\phi\) gives the timing signal for alignment. The scheduler uses the target phase relation on [PAGE 5] to align the rope’s rotation with the player’s ascent. The player policy uses the clock to learn periodic jumping [PAGE 4]. The turners implicitly follow the commanded angular velocity, which determines rope frequency. Thus the recursive structure is: player clock produces jump phase; scheduler infers jump phase and adjusts rope command; turners execute command and produce rope phase; rope phase feeds back into scheduler observation.

A failure mode is phase-lock regression. Suppose the scheduler learns that the easiest policy is to keep the rope rotating at the clock frequency without adapting to player drift. This may work when the player remains centered, but fails when the player moves horizontally. The paper’s player-tracking reward and overlap penalty are designed to counter this by incentivizing adaptation to “the slight horizontal drift of player and reduce the rope-player collision” [PAGE 13]. Another failure mode is collision avoidance regression: the scheduler may learn to slow the rope to avoid overlap, but then the player cannot complete the jump because the rope is not under the feet at the correct phase. The phase-tracking reward counters this by requiring the rope phase to pass through the target point.

The theory of mind is therefore not anthropomorphic; it is a formal multi-rate estimation problem. The scheduler must estimate the player’s phase from OBB history, predict the effect of command changes on rope phase, and select commands that keep the overlap indicator zero while maintaining phase tracking. The system’s robustness depends on the assumption that the rhythm signal, player observation, and rope observation share a consistent time base. If the clock, MoCap, and robot control loops are not synchronized, the recursive belief chain breaks.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which a higher-level or lower-level physical constraint invalidates the policy action, regardless of learned reward performance. The paper provides several formal veto conditions.

Physical veto 1: undefined rope axis. The rotation axis \(e_r\) requires horizontal separation between the turners [PAGE 4]. If  
\[
\|\Pi_{xy}(p^2_{\text{base}} - p^1_{\text{base}})\|_2 = 0,
\]
then the axis is undefined. The policy cannot compute the desired rotation command in a well-posed frame. This is a hard geometric veto. It can occur if the robots are commanded too close together or if one robot falls toward the other.

Physical veto 2: undefined rope phase. The phase estimate \(\bar\theta_{\text{rope}}\) depends on rope points and the estimated angular velocity [PAGE 5]. If the observed points do not provide transverse displacement relative to the rotation axis, then \(\xi_{y,i}\) and \(\xi_{z,i}\) may vanish or become noise-dominated. In that regime, the phase estimate is not reliably observable. The overlap and phase rewards become unreliable because the scheduler’s phase observation is ill-conditioned. This is especially relevant in real-world deployment where only a finite set of reflective markers is observed [PAGE 6].

Biomechanical veto 1: foot slip or undesired contact. The reward table penalizes “Slide with contact” and “Undesired contacts” [PAGE 16]. Formally, if a foot is in contact and its horizontal velocity is nonzero beyond the allowed tolerance, the contact constraint is violated. The low-level policy is trained to maintain stable foot contacts, and the paper lists feet slippage as a control-stability metric [PAGE 6]. If feet slip exceeds the trained tolerance, the humanoid cannot reliably serve as a turner because the base pose estimate and rope-end attachment point become invalid.

Biomechanical veto 2: joint limit violation. The reward terms include joint position limits and joint deviation penalties [PAGE 16]. If the commanded rope manipulation requires joint targets outside feasible limits, the PD controller will saturate or produce unsafe torques. This can happen when the desired width, height, or angular velocity is incompatible with the robot’s arm reach or balance envelope.

Weather or ambient veto: unmodeled disturbance. The real-world system depends on MoCap and controlled rhythm delivery [PAGE 13]. The sim-to-real randomization includes push velocities \(v_x, v_y \in U(-0.5,0.5)\) m/s and push intervals \(U(1.0,3.0)\) s [PAGE 14]. If an ambient disturbance produces base motion outside this distribution, the policy is outside its validated regime. If ground friction, restitution, or surface compliance leaves the randomized intervals, contact behavior is also outside the validated regime. Formally, if  
\[
|v_{\text{push},x}| > 0.5 \text{ m/s}
\]
or  
\[
|v_{\text{push},y}| > 0.5 \text{ m/s},
\]
the trained push-disturbance distribution is exceeded. If rain, wind, or loose ground changes friction outside the randomized friction ranges, the foot-contact model is invalid. Since the observation set does not include wind or surface state, the policy cannot adapt to these disturbances using its learned observation variables. This is an observability veto: the disturbance is unobservable, so no learned policy can guarantee phase alignment or collision avoidance under arbitrary weather.

The adjudication rule is therefore hierarchical: geometry first, then phase observability, then contact stability, then ambient distribution validity. If any lower-level veto triggers, the high-level scheduler’s learned objective cannot be certified. The paper’s metrics verify performance within the supported regime: rope tracking errors, overlap ratio, phase tracking error, complete rate, feet slippage, and action rate [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7]. Outside that regime, the formal operators themselves become singular, noisy, or unobservable.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The paper supplies verifiable reward predicates and experimental metrics that can be used as RLVR counterfactual vetoes. A veto triggers when a measurable predicate indicates that the policy would fail a required physical or coordination condition.

Veto Scenario 1: phase-collision veto.  
Verifiable predicate:  
\[
R_{\text{overlap}} > \tau_{\text{overlap}}
\]
or  
\[
\text{Phase Tracking Error} > \tau_{\text{phase}}.
\]
Here \(R_{\text{overlap}}\) is the fraction of timesteps where a rope point lies inside the player OBB [PAGE 7] [PAGE 16]. The source reports that the full method achieves overlap ratio \(0.090 \pm 0.049\) and phase tracking error \(0.397 \pm 0.115\), while the “w/o Scheduling” baseline degrades to overlap ratio \(0.392 \pm 0.140\) and phase tracking error \(1.092 \pm 0.273\) [PAGE 7]. The complete rate also falls from \(0.787 \pm 0.204\) to \(0.358 \pm 0.207\) [PAGE 7]. The paper states that “w/o Scheduling shows a significant performance degradation” [PAGE 7]. Counterfactual prediction: if the high-level scheduling policy is removed and replaced by a rule-based clock frequency, the system will produce more rope-player overlap, larger phase error, and fewer completed jumps. If the scheduler is restored but rhythm latency increases, the same veto should trigger because the phase estimate becomes stale.

Veto Scenario 2: deformable-rope tracking veto.  
Verifiable predicate:  
\[
E_{\text{rot}} > \tau_{\text{rot}}
\]
or  
\[
E_{\text{wid}} > \tau_{\text{wid}}.
\]
The source reports that the full method achieves \(E_{\text{rot}} = 1.719 \pm 0.332\) and \(E_{\text{wid}} = 0.063 \pm 0.011\), while the Open Loop baseline achieves \(E_{\text{rot}} = 6.754 \pm 2.018\) and \(E_{\text{wid}} = 0.284 \pm 0.135\) [PAGE 6]. The paper says Open Loop “achieves slightly smoother actions, but its rope manipulation accuracy drops substantially” [PAGE 6]. Counterfactual prediction: if the policy is replaced by replayed trajectories or blind motion tracking, the rope state will diverge from the commanded rotation pattern, causing width error and rotation-frequency error to increase. The veto should trigger before a physical deployment because the rope’s deformable state is not directly controlled [PAGE 1].

Veto Scenario 3: player-diversity generalization veto.  
Verifiable predicate:  
\[
\text{Complete Rate} < \tau_{\text{complete}}
\]
or  
\[
\text{Player Tracking Error} > \tau_{\text{player}}
\]
under a held-out player latent or unseen human partner. The source reports that removing player diversity changes overlap ratio from \(0.090\) to \(0.120\), phase tracking error from \(0.397\) to \(0.414\), player tracking error from \(0.116\) to \(0.172\), and complete rate from \(0.787\) to \(0.751\) [PAGE 7]. The paper states that “introducing diverse counterparts during training is crucial for improving the generalization and adaptability” [PAGE 7]. The PCA visualization also shows that diversity expands the visited local-frame end-effector pose distribution [PAGE 7]. Counterfactual prediction: if the scheduler is trained only with a narrow jumping policy, it will fail when the player’s rhythm, body size, or horizontal drift lies outside the narrow training distribution. The IPM diversity objective and curriculum threshold are designed to prevent this failure [PAGE 5] [PAGE 15].

These three vetoes are verifiable because they rely on metrics explicitly defined by the paper: overlap ratio, phase tracking error, complete rate, rotation tracking error, width tracking error, and player tracking error [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7]. They are counterfactual because each compares the deployed policy against an alternative or a held-out condition. They are physical because they correspond to collision, loss of rope control, or failure to synchronize with a player. They do not depend on speculative betting constructs; they depend only on measurable task predicates and the paper’s reported empirical regimes.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
