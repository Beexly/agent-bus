<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The selectors' cognition is nested. First-order theory of mind: W. A. Lambeth selects the best players he can observe. Second-order: he aggregates "the opinions of local observers and critics" [PAGE 1] because he anticipates that readers will judge the All-Southern Eleven against local consensus, not against an invisible objective standard. He picks names that critics will recognize, avoiding idiosyncratic omissions that would invite public correction.

Crisis habit regression operates under deadline pressure. A selector assembling a newspaper all-scholastic eleven [PAGE 1] regresses to familiar surnames, repeat performers, and schools with visible game coverage. A half-back from a well-covered program such as Asbury Park [PAGE 1] enjoys a availability heuristic advantage over an equally skilled player from a less-covered town.

The counter-trap: a sophisticated selector may intentionally omit an obvious pick to signal independent judgment. That contrarian omission then enters next year's reputational baseline, because future selectors consult prior all-star lists as informal rosters of "proven" talent. The counter-trap therefore metastasizes into a reputation cascade: an omitted player is harder to select next year not because he declined, because his absence from the 1915 guide [PAGE 1] becomes evidence of non-elite status.

Clock layer: the guide is annual, with a hard production cutoff after the 1915 season [PAGE 1]. Late-season injuries, late-blooming seniors, and postseason all-star games are invisible to the selector. Recursive theory of mind among players: knowing local observers are watching, a 1915 full-back may favor conservative inside plunges over high-risk outside runs to protect his all-star reputation. Habit regression under observational pressure skews the observed sample toward risk-averse play. The counter-trap is that if all backfield candidates regress to conservative carries, the selector's evidence base is strategically distorted, and the resulting all-eleven reflects durability and caution rather than true ability ceiling. The clock thus converts a single-season sample into a multi-year reputational artifact with no in-season correction mechanism.
