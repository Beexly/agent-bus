Users develop a recursive model of the search engine: they learn which positions, bold patterns, and background colors typically correlate with high-quality results, and they adjust clicking habits accordingly. The search engine, in turn, models the user’s model and manipulates presentation to elicit clicks. Under normal conditions this reaches a rough equilibrium, but under crisis—an abrupt SERP layout change, a new onebox format, or a sudden ad-load increase—users regress to habitual click positions (the “crisis habit regression”), clicking the top result or familiar bold patterns regardless of actual relevance. The system observes these clicks and may misattribute them to relevance rather than habit, thereby reinforcing a biased relevance signal.

The counter-trap is the system’s intentional perturbation of presentation to test whether clicks persist: varying indentation, changing background color, moving bold terms from beginning to end of sentence [PAGE 15], or temporarily suppressing an onebox. If clicks remain stable under presentation perturbation, the relevance signal is robust; if clicks collapse, the previous signal was predominantly presentation bias. This counter-trap requires the system to sacrifice short-term click metrics for long-term identification, a recursive meta-decision about whether to exploit current user habits or explore to break them.

The clock is the decay rate of the prior model’s validity. Because users adapt to new layouts and because query-type composition shifts weekly, the presentation-bias operator \(B(d)\) and the prior-model mapping \(g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) have a finite half-life. The clock sets the recalibration interval: if the system does not re-estimate presentation effects before the clock expires, the relevance signal drifts and the dual-signal combination in FIG. 4F becomes misweighted. The recursive loop therefore runs on three timescales: immediate habit regression (seconds to minutes), counter-trap probing (hours to days), and prior-model clock decay (weeks).

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: screen size, resolution, luminance, and ambient illumination govern whether presentation features are perceptible. Bold terms at the beginning versus end of a sentence [PAGE 15] only affect CTR if users can resolve them; on small mobile screens under high glare, the position-of-bold-terms effect may fall below visual acuity thresholds (~1 arcminute) and be vetoed. Background-color results (e.g., light blue background) depend on contrast ratio; outdoor sunlight or low-brightness modes can erase the color cue, nullifying the [PAGE 15] format effect.

Biomechanical layer: motor constraints impose Fitts’s-law bounds on position bias. Thumb-reach zones on handheld devices and saccade-length limits in eye-tracking make lower SERP positions physically costlier to select, amplifying position-dependent click fraction suppression independent of relevance. Indentation effects that signal deeper network-domain nesting [PAGE 15] are bounded by the user’s willingness to execute additional motor actions; beyond a fatigue threshold, indentation ceases to raise or lower CTR predictably.

Weather layer: meteorological conditions modulate mobile search context. Rain or snow increases distracted, short-session mobile usage; solar glare reduces snippet and URL readability, altering the measured effect of title/snippet/URL length [PAGE 15]. These are not direct ranking factors, but they inject noise into the implicit feedback signal that can masquerade as relevance change.

Veto proof: if cross-layer sensors (ambient-light sensor, device orientation, touch-tap latency, weather API) indicate low visibility, high motor cost, or distracted context, the system must veto raw click-fraction updates and either fall back to the prior model from FIG. 4E or withhold ranking changes. Example: a result on a light-blue background suffers a CTR drop during a sunny afternoon; a physical-layer veto prevents the system from interpreting this as relevance decay.

The prior model predicts \(\hat{f}_{\mathrm{prior}}=0.075\) based on IR score and position. Counterfactual query: if \(d_{new}\) were placed at position 1 rather than its observed position 12, would CTR equal 0.075? Veto condition: if observed position \(>10\), veto the prior-model boost because the prior was trained on mixed positions and does not adjust for extreme-rank presentation bias [PAGE 15 table, position feature]. Prediction without veto: the system over-ranks \(d_{new}\) and wastes exposure on a result whose predicted clickability does not survive repositioning. Prediction with veto: \(d_{new}\) remains at its current position until direct impression evidence accumulates.

Scenario 2 — Onebox suppression veto. A very topical onebox appears prior to normal results [PAGE 15]. Organic result \(d\) experiences CTR decline from 0.11 to 0.03. Naive model interprets this as relevance collapse and demotes \(d\). Counterfactual: remove the onebox; expected CTR reverts to 0.11 because the onebox effect is presentation-driven, not relevance-driven. Veto: block the demotion while the onebox is active. Prediction without veto: \(d\) is unfairly demoted for the duration of the onebox episode and may take weeks to recover. Prediction with veto: organic ranking is preserved; only the onebox’s own presentation features are adjusted.

Scenario 3 — Ad-quality cycle veto. Better quality ads lower CTR for all results [PAGE 15]. The system observes a global click-fraction decline and applies a across-the-board relevance recalibration. Counterfactual: hold ad quality constant; result-specific CTR changes are negligible, so the decline is an ad-layer presentation effect. Veto: prohibit global relevance recalibration; adjust only ad-position and ad-quality features. Prediction without veto: all organic results are under-scored during high-quality ad campaigns, distorting the ranking order. Prediction with veto: organic relevance ordering is preserved while ad presentation is independently modeled.
