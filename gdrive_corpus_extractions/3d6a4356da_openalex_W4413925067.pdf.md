The source’s core reasoning chain is a domain-bridging argument under out-of-distribution constraints. The authors observe that expert wheelchair tennis footage is monocular, broadcast-quality, and lacks reliable ball depth [PAGE 1]. Therefore, direct 2D-to-2D imitation is impossible, and direct 2D-to-3D reconstruction is also impossible without depth. Their proposed workaround is not to reconstruct the expert’s full 3D scene, but to exploit an asymmetric transfer assumption: the controlled motion of interest (wheelchair) is constrained to a 2D ground-plane motion, while the environmental motion (tennis ball) may be 3D [PAGE 3]. This leads to a loop: reconstruct ball and wheelchair in task space, project into expert-like 2D image space, learn or execute a diffusion imitation policy in that image space, then map the wheelchair command back to task space using inverse homography. The reasoning is elegant because it avoids the harder problem of lifting monocular broadcast footage into metric 3D, while still closing a closed-loop control path on a physical robot.

The multi-hop derivation proceeds as follows. First, data extraction converts broadcast footage into structured episodes: non-matchplay filtering, audio hit detection, court homography, ball tracking, player localization, and episode aggregation [PAGE 3]. Second, the learned policy is trained on image-space trajectories of the wheelchair and ball. Third, on the real court, multi-camera and onboard sensors reconstruct task-space ball and wheelchair states. Fourth, those task-space trajectories are warped into the expert image coordinate system. Fifth, the diffusion policy generates a wheelchair trajectory in image space. Sixth, the trajectory is inverted back into the task space. Seventh, a PD controller executes the trajectory on the wheelchair. The critical gray zone is that every stage introduces error: filtering can remove valid rally frames; audio detection can mislabel hits; homography can fail if camera motion is missed; ball tracking can lose high-speed or occluded balls; factor-graph/EKF estimation can be delayed; and inverse homography assumes the wheelchair remains on a valid ground plane. The paper explicitly acknowledges the transfer assumption: “The knowledge transfer is valid with the only assumption that MOI is 2D motion and Env-M can be 3D” [PAGE 3].

A second gray zone is the difference between trajectory fidelity and task success. The benchmark metrics—RMSE, DTW, ICP, jerk—measure geometric agreement with expert trajectories [PAGE 5]. However, the hybrid and real-court experiments use task success: “within 1.4 meters of the ball) before it bounces three times” [PAGE 5]. This is a navigation interception metric, not a hitting metric. The paper’s future work explicitly notes that the manipulator and return stroke are not yet part of the closed loop [PAGE 6]. Therefore, a low jerk score does not guarantee athletic return; it only indicates a plausible approach path. The gap between hybrid success and real-time success is the most important empirical signal: “Our pipeline achieves a navigation success rate of 97.67% ... and achieve a success rate of 68.49% in a real-world, real-time experiment” [PAGE 1]. That gap likely reflects estimation uncertainty, communication latency, and real-time ball-state prediction, as the authors state: “on-the-fly trajectory estimation in the large court has negative effects on the agile navigation task due to observation uncertainty and communication delays” [PAGE 6].

A third gray zone is the semi-automatic versus automatic dataset trade-off. The automatic pipeline is larger, but the semi-automatic dataset is manually filtered and therefore higher quality. The authors report that the automatic dataset’s prediction results are slightly worse despite “double size of data” [PAGE 5]. This matters because diffusion models can smooth over noisy supervision, but if episode boundaries or trajectories are systematically wrong, more data may amplify a biased distribution rather than correct it. The paper’s choice to use semi-automatic data for downstream evaluation is therefore a pragmatic inductive bias: quality over quantity. A deeper audit would ask whether the remaining manual filtering is confounded with court geometry, camera angle, player handedness, rally length, or broadcast cut timing. If so, the diffusion policy may be learning broadcast-editorial artifacts as well as athletic navigation strategies.

A final gray zone is temporal: the task is adversarial and fast. The paper states the robot has “less than two seconds to intercept an incoming tennis ball moving under its own dynamics” [PAGE 1]. Diffusion inference is reported at approximately 0.1 seconds with ten steps [PAGE 5], but the full loop includes perception, factor graph, EKF, network transport, homography inversion, and PD control. Thus, the true control delay is not merely diffusion inference; it is the full sense-plan-actuate path. The method’s success therefore depends on whether the predicted intercept point remains inside the admissible region after all delays. If the ball is fast and the intercept horizon short, even a small positional lag can dominate the error. This is the central reasoning risk: the method looks strong in replayed hybrid trials because the ball trajectory is known or replayed, but real-time estimation introduces a moving target under latency.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Mean-shift matchplay filtering operator  
The paper uses mean-shift clustering on preprocessed video frames to retain matchplay segments. The update rule is:

\[
x_i^{(t+1)} = \frac{\sum_j K(x_j - x_i^{(t)}) x_j}{\sum_j K(x_j - x_i^{(t)})}
\]

where \(K\) is a flat kernel [PAGE 3].  
Units: \(x_i\) are normalized frame feature vectors; if intensity-rescaled grayscale frames are used, the entries are dimensionless intensity features. The denominator is a kernel weight sum, dimensionless.  
Assumptions: feature modes corresponding to matchplay and non-matchplay are separable in the chosen frame representation; camera framing is stable enough that matchplay forms a coherent mode.  
Breakdown regime: if crowd shots, replays, and matchplay overlap in feature space, the flat kernel merges modes and the filter either removes valid rally frames or retains non-rally frames. If brightness or scale changes across broadcast cuts, the distance metric becomes nonstationary. The authors state: “where K is a flat kernel” [PAGE 3].

2. Audio hit-detection classifier  
The hit detector is a three-layer fully connected network trained on audio features such as MFCCs, chromagrams, and mel-spectrograms. The loss is binary cross-entropy:

\[
L = -\left[y \log(\hat{y}) + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})\right]
\]

where \(y \in \{0,1\}\) indicates a ball hit and \(\hat{y}\in(0,1)\) is predicted probability [PAGE 3].  
Units: \(L\) is dimensionless information loss, in nats if natural logarithm is used; per-sample average loss has units nats/sample.  
Assumptions: hit sounds are acoustically separable from crowd noise, chair movement, shoe scuffs, and line calls.  
Breakdown regime: log singularity occurs if \(\hat{y}\to 0\) when \(y=1\) or \(\hat{y}\to 1\) when \(y=0\), producing infinite or unstable gradients. Acoustically, false positives from crowd claps or false negatives from muffled wheelchair hits corrupt episode boundaries.

3. Court homography operator  
The paper defines the mapping between image coordinates and court coordinates:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}u' \\ v' \\ w\end{bmatrix}
=
H
\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
\]

with normalized image coordinates usually \(u = u'/w\), \(v = v'/w\) [PAGE 3].  
Units: if \((x,y)\) are court coordinates in meters and \((u,v)\) are image coordinates in pixels, \(H\) contains mixed-scale projective coefficients; the mapping is homogeneous and defined up to scale.  
Assumptions: the court surface is planar; camera intrinsics and extrinsics are constant or only recalculated when camera motion is detected; keypoint correspondences are accurate. The authors recalculate the matrix only when camera motion is detected: “we recalculate the matrix only when camera motion is detected, improving efficiency” [PAGE 3].  
Breakdown regime: undetected slow pan, zoom, vibration, lens drift, or incorrect keypoint matching produce a biased \(H\). Since the wheelchair trajectory is mapped by \(T_w = H(cT_w)\) and recovered by \(c\hat{T}_w = H^{-1}(\hat{T}_w)\) [PAGE 4], homography error directly corrupts both policy conditioning and command generation. The paper notes the inverse homography is possible “since the wheelchair always remains on the ground plane” [PAGE 4]; wheel slip or lifting violates this.

4. Diffusion forward noising operator  
For trajectory \(\tau_0\), diffusion timestep \(i\), and noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\):

\[
\tau_i = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_i}\tau_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_i}\epsilon
\]

The training objective is:

\[
L(\theta) = \left\|\epsilon - s_\theta(\tau_i, i, m)\right\|_2^2
\]

where \(s_\theta\) predicts the applied noise and \(m\) is conditioning [PAGE 4].  
Units: if \(\tau\) represents image-space trajectory coordinates in meters or court meters, the loss has units of squared meters; if trajectories are normalized for learning, it is dimensionless.  
Assumptions: the forward process is Markovian, noise schedule \(\bar{\alpha}_i\) is monotone, and the model sees enough in-distribution examples of expert wheelchair responses. The paper uses a cosine schedule [PAGE 4].  
Breakdown regime: if the real-world conditional distribution is far from the training distribution, denoising collapses to mean trajectories or produces physically inconsistent paths. The paper segments trajectories to “32 and 18 timesteps for the input and output respectively” [PAGE 4]; inappropriate horizon length can truncate tactical intent or create endpoint discontinuity.

5. Diffusion sampling and constraint projection operator  
At sampling time:

\[
\tau_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)
\]

and for \(i=T,\ldots,1\):

\[
(\mu_i, \Sigma_i) \leftarrow s_\theta(\tau_i), \quad
\tau_{i-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \Sigma_i), \quad
\tau_{i-1} \leftarrow C(\tau_{i-1})
\]

where \(C\) enforces constraints, including starting at the current wheelchair location [PAGE 4].  
Units: \(\mu_i\) and \(\Sigma_i\) are in trajectory-state units, e.g., meters or image pixels, with covariance in squared units.  
Assumptions: the constraint set is feasible; the conditioning stream is fresh; the denoising chain is short enough for real-time use. The paper reports “small diffusion steps (10 steps) and inference time (≈0.1s)” [PAGE 5].  
Breakdown regime: if the current position is outside the training manifold or the ball estimate is inconsistent, projection onto \(C\) can create jumps, jerky trajectories, or infeasible starts. If the policy is conditioned on stale ball states, the sampled trajectory is dynamically invalid.

6. PD control operator  
The executed controller is:

\[
v = k_{1p}(x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta) + k_{1d}\dot{d}
\]

\[
\omega = k_{2p}\left(\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\theta\right) + k_{2d}\dot{\theta}
\]

where \(v\) is driving speed and \(\omega\) is angular velocity [PAGE 4].  
Units: \(v\) is m/s, \(\omega\) is rad/s, \(x,y\) are desired position errors in meters in the robot frame, \(\theta\) is heading in radians, and gains have SI-consistent units.  
Assumptions: differential-drive conversion is valid; the robot can approximate the commanded twist; heading error is wrapped correctly; wheel slip is negligible.  
Breakdown regime: actuator saturation, wheel slip, nonholonomic constraints, or large heading error can make the commanded velocity unexecutable. If the desired point is behind the wheelchair, the controller may need to reorient, consuming time that is not available in a two-second interception task.

7. Trajectory metrics and task-success operator  
The paper evaluates RMSE, DTW, ICP, and jerk [PAGE 5]. A formal RMSE can be written:

\[
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|p_k - g_k\|_2^2}
\]

with units meters if positions are in meters. Jerk is root-mean-square third derivative:

\[
\mathrm{Jerk} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\frac{d^3 r_k}{dt^3}\right\|_2^2}
\]

with units \(\mathrm{m/s^3}\).  
Task success is defined probabilistically:

\[
\hat{p}_{\mathrm{succ}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{succ}}}{N_{\mathrm{trials}}}
\]

where success means the wheelchair is within 1.4 m of the ball before it bounces three times [PAGE 5].  
Units: \(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{succ}}\) is dimensionless probability.  
Breakdown regime: if \(N_{\mathrm{trials}}\) is small or not reported, the probability estimate has large sampling uncertainty; the source reports percentages but not trial counts in the excerpt. Also, the 1.4 m threshold is inherited from prior robotic manipulator research [PAGE 5], so it may not fully certify return feasibility.

8. Ball-state estimation and rollout operator  
The system estimates ball state through a factor graph and EKF-like fusion, then rolls out future predictions with an ODE solver [PAGE 6]. A generic state representation is:

\[
x_b =
\begin{bmatrix}
r_b \\
v_b \\
s_b
\end{bmatrix}
\]

where \(r_b\) is position in meters, \(v_b\) is velocity in m/s, and \(s_b\) is spin or angular state in rad/s or appropriate units. The paper says the factor graph estimates “position, velocity, and spin” [PAGE 6].  
Assumptions: multi-camera detections are asynchronous but associable; ball dynamics can be approximated by an ODE; spin and drag effects are sufficiently observable.  
Breakdown regime: occlusion, motion blur, insufficient camera baseline, or wind make the state estimate uncertain. If the covariance of \(r_b\) is large, the predicted intercept point is unreliable even if the diffusion policy is well trained.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs through three historical streams: imitation learning, diffusion generative models, and robotic sports navigation. The behavior-cloning root is ALVINN-style image-to-action learning [PAGE 7], where a policy maps observations to control. The paper notes the classic compounding-error problem in behavior cloning and that DAgger addresses it by requiring interaction with experts and the environment, which is contrary to their zero-shot objective [PAGE 2]. That refusal to use interactive expert correction is central: the method wants web video as a frozen dataset, not a closed-loop human-in-the-loop teacher.

The second stream is diffusion modeling. The authors cite diffusion probabilistic models and denoising generative models [PAGE 7], then apply a diffusion policy to trajectory generation. The key historical move is from global one-shot diffusion planning to feedback-compatible trajectory refinement. Prior diffusion planning can generate smooth paths, but the paper emphasizes in-task feedback: the policy starts from the current wheelchair location and is re-invoked as new ball estimates arrive [PAGE 4]. This is a correction to the limitation that earlier global diffusion plans “lack in-task feedback and real-time performance guarantees” [PAGE 2].

The third stream is robotic sports. The paper positions itself against fixed, locally moving, and globally moving athletic robots [PAGE 2]. It contrasts gantry-constrained table tennis or badminton systems with a mobile wheelchair base that must navigate the full court. It also builds on the same group’s wheelchair tennis manipulator work [PAGE 7], where the 1.4 m success radius is inherited. The retro-causal genealogy is therefore not just “learning from video”; it is the convergence of sports robotics hardware, broadcast-video extraction, and image-space motion planning.

The multi-week traps are mostly data-pipeline traps. The first trap is filter bias: mean-shift filtering may discard rare but valid rally segments, causing the diffusion policy to learn an overly conservative or editorially biased notion of “expert play.” The second trap is audio segmentation: if ball-hit sounds are confused with crowd noise or wheelchair impacts, episode boundaries shift, and the policy learns incorrect causal alignment between ball hit and wheelchair response. The third trap is homography drift: because the matrix is recalculated only when camera motion is detected [PAGE 3], a slow undetected camera drift can silently corrupt the mapping from task space to image space. The fourth trap is dataset size versus quality: the automatic dataset is larger but worse than the smaller semi-automatic dataset [PAGE 5], showing that more extracted data does not automatically improve imitation. The fifth trap is real-time estimation: the hybrid test uses recorded ball trajectories, while the real-court test requires live factor-graph estimation and communication [PAGE 6]. The observed drop from 97.67% to 68.49% is the empirical signature of that trap.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system involves at least four recursive agents or quasi-agents: the expert wheelchair tennis player in the broadcast video, the broadcast camera operator, the opposing far-side player, and the robot wheelchair policy. The paper’s imitation target is not merely “tennis,” but the expert player’s movement policy as filtered through broadcast presentation. This creates a recursive modeling problem: the policy may learn what the camera chose to show, not what the player would do under optimal control.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency under time pressure to fall back to familiar spatial habits. In this domain, the crisis is the adversarial timing: “less than two seconds to intercept an incoming tennis ball moving under its own dynamics” [PAGE 1]. Under that pressure, experts may regress to high-probability patterns: return to center after a shot, favor certain diagonal recoveries, or respond to familiar ball spin. The diffusion policy may also regress to the mean of training trajectories, especially if the current ball estimate is uncertain. This is beneficial if the expert prior is strong, but dangerous if the current rally requires a novel movement. A policy that regresses to broadcast-frequent paths may fail on rare but physically valid ball trajectories.

The counter-trap is the opposite error: assuming the future ball trajectory is known. The paper finds that conditioning on future ball observations, “Post 2D,” improves trajectory prediction compared with prior observations [PAGE 5]. However, in real-time deployment, future ball states are estimated, not observed. The counter-trap occurs when the policy becomes overconfident in predicted ball position and does not preserve enough contingency margin for estimation delay. The real-court drop to 68.49% suggests that the system is vulnerable to this counter-trap: the better the policy is at following a predicted ball path, the more it may suffer when the prediction is noisy or delayed.

The clock is the decisive adversarial variable. The policy must complete diffusion inference, state estimation, homography inversion, and PD control before the ball passes the admissible intercept window. The paper reports diffusion inference around 0.1 s [PAGE 5], but the total loop includes asynchronous camera detections, Ethernet transport, offboard factor-graph computation, and controller execution [PAGE 6]. A simple clock inequality is:

\[
t_{\mathrm{intercept}} > t_{\mathrm{estimate}} + t_{\mathrm{diffusion}} + t_{\mathrm{transport}} + t_{\mathrm{control}} + t_{\mathrm{margin}}
\]

with all times in seconds. If this inequality fails, the policy cannot succeed even if its image-space trajectory is close to expert data. The paper’s real-time result implies that the clock, not only the learned trajectory distribution, limits performance. The recursive theory of mind here is therefore not psychological guessing alone; it is a race between expert habit priors and estimation latency.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The framework must pass through four layers: image-space plausibility, task-space feasibility, biomechanical feasibility, and environmental physics. A veto at any layer overrides a good score at another layer.

Layer 1: image-space plausibility.  
The diffusion policy can produce low RMSE, DTW, ICP, and jerk [PAGE 5]. This proves the trajectory resembles expert image-space paths. It does not prove physical feasibility. If the policy learns broadcast camera framing artifacts, the image-space trajectory may be plausible but task-space invalid.

Layer 2: task-space homography feasibility.  
The wheelchair command is mapped by inverse homography:

\[
c\hat{T}_w = H^{-1}(\hat{T}_w)
\]

This is valid only if the wheelchair is on the court plane and \(H\) is accurate [PAGE 4]. If wheel slip occurs, if the wheelchair tilts, or if camera homography is stale, the inverse mapping produces a false task-space goal. Veto condition:

\[
\|H^{-1}(\hat{T}_w) - T_{w,\mathrm{true}}\| > \epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}
\]

where \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}\) is the allowable court-position error, in meters. If \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}\) exceeds the interception margin, success is impossible regardless of image-space imitation quality.

Layer 3: biomechanical and actuator feasibility.  
Let \(d\) be the distance from the wheelchair to the intercept point, \(v_{\max}\) the maximum effective speed in m/s, and \(a_{\max}\) the maximum acceleration or braking deceleration in m/s\(^2\). A conservative lower bound on time to reach the point from rest is:

\[
t_{\min} \approx \frac{d}{v_{\max}}
\]

A more conservative bound including acceleration is:

\[
t_{\min} \approx \frac{v_{\max}}{a_{\max}} + \frac{d}{v_{\max}}
\]

if the robot must first accelerate. If available time \(t_{\mathrm{avail}}\) satisfies:

\[
t_{\mathrm{avail}} < t_{\min}
\]

the trajectory is vetoed. Similarly, stopping distance imposes a veto:

\[
d_{\mathrm{stop}} = \frac{v^2}{2a_{\mathrm{brake}}}
\]

with units meters. If the wheelchair must stop within the 1.4 m success region but \(d_{\mathrm{stop}}\) exceeds the available margin, the motion is biomechanically infeasible. Nonholonomic turning adds another veto: if heading error \(\Delta\theta\) requires turning time

\[
t_{\mathrm{turn}} = \frac{|\Delta\theta|}{\omega_{\max}}
\]

and \(t_{\mathrm{turn}} + t_{\mathrm{translate}} > t_{\mathrm{avail}}\), the path is invalid. The paper’s PD controller does not model these hard saturation constraints explicitly; it assumes the commanded twist is executable [PAGE 4].

Layer 4: environmental ball-physics veto.  
The ball is 3D and affected by gravity, drag, spin, bounce, and possibly wind. The system estimates ball position, velocity, and spin [PAGE 6], but any unmodeled acceleration causes intercept error. For a simple constant unmodeled lateral acceleration \(a_w\), the position error after time \(T\) is:

\[
\Delta r \approx \frac{1}{2}\|a_w\|T^2
\]

with units meters if \(a_w\) is m/s\(^2\) and \(T\) is seconds. The success radius is 1.4 m before three bounces [PAGE 5]. A conservative environmental veto is:

\[
\frac{1}{2}\|a_w\|T^2 > r_{\mathrm{tol}}
\]

or:

\[
\|a_w\| > \frac{2r_{\mathrm{tol}}}{T^2}
\]

If \(r_{\mathrm{tol}}\) is less than 1.4 m because of tracking uncertainty, the allowable wind or aerodynamic disturbance shrinks. For example, with \(T=2\) s and \(r_{\mathrm{tol}}=1.4\) m, the threshold is \(0.7\) m/s\(^2\). If actual unmodeled lateral acceleration exceeds this, the predicted intercept point is displaced beyond the success region. Weather also affects traction: if wet court friction reduces effective braking, then \(a_{\mathrm{brake}} \approx \mu g\) drops, increasing stopping distance. The veto proof is therefore:

\[
\exists \text{ layer } \ell \in \{\text{homography}, \text{actuation}, \text{ball physics}, \text{weather}\} : V_\ell = 1
\]

where \(V_\ell=1\) means the physical layer invalidates the image-space plan. The paper’s strong hybrid result does not remove these vetoes; it only shows that the learned image-space policy is good when replayed trajectories and controlled conditions reduce real-time uncertainty.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement-learning-style verifiable counterfactual tests: if a proposed condition breaks a verifiable task invariant, the method should be vetoed. The verifiable reward is not image similarity alone, but physical interception within 1.4 m before three bounces [PAGE 5].

Scenario 1: Undetected camera drift corrupts homography.  
Condition: the wall camera slowly pans or zooms, but the system does not detect camera motion because the threshold is missed. The paper recalculates homography only when camera motion is detected [PAGE 3].  
Verifiable test: inject a synthetic homography error of known magnitude, or create a slow camera drift of known pixel velocity, then measure court-projected wheelchair error.  
Prediction: if the projected position error exceeds a fraction of the 1.4 m success radius, success rate falls sharply. A practical veto threshold is:

\[
\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}} > 0.2 \text{ m}
\]

because that is roughly one-seventh of the success radius and leaves insufficient margin for ball-state noise. If \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}} > 0.2\) m, the expected real-court success should fall below the observed 68.49%, and possibly below the TEB baseline of 35.36% if the bias is persistent [PAGE 6]. This scenario is falsifiable: measure homography reprojection error and correlate it with trial success.

Scenario 2: Audio hit detector false positives corrupt episode labels.  
Condition: crowd noise or chair impacts are classified as ball hits, causing episode boundaries to start too early or too late. The hit detector minimizes cross-entropy [PAGE 3], but label noise is not eliminated.  
Verifiable test: compare the automatic dataset with the semi-automatic dataset, or inject synthetic audio false positives. Measure trajectory prediction metrics and downstream success.  
Prediction: false-positive hit labels will increase RMSE, DTW, and ICP because the policy learns incorrect initial conditions. The source already shows that the semi-automatic dataset outperforms the larger automatic dataset despite the latter containing double the data [PAGE 5]. If automatic extraction false-hit rate causes more than approximately 0.07 m additional RMSE relative to the semi-automatic diffusion Post 2D result, that would match the observed gap between semi-automatic RMSE 0.277 and automatic RMSE 0.346 [PAGE 6]. A veto is triggered when label noise makes training episodes causally misaligned with the ball hit.

Scenario 3: Real-time latency exceeds the interception clock.  
Condition: the ball is fast, and the end-to-end delay from camera detection to wheelchair command exceeds the time remaining before the third bounce or before the ball leaves the 1.4 m intercept region.  
Verifiable test: measure total latency \(t_{\mathrm{lat}}\) and ball speed \(v_b\). Compute positional delay error:

\[
\Delta r_{\mathrm{lat}} \approx v_b t_{\mathrm{lat}}
\]

with units meters. If \(\Delta r_{\mathrm{lat}}\) plus ball-state covariance exceeds the 1.4 m success radius, success should collapse.  
Prediction: the real-time experiment should underperform the hybrid test whenever:

\[
v_b t_{\mathrm{lat}} > r_{\mathrm{eff}}
\]

where \(r_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is the effective margin after estimation uncertainty. The observed real-time success of 68.49% versus hybrid 97.67% is consistent with this veto [PAGE 1]. The paper itself attributes the gap to “observation uncertainty and communication delays” [PAGE 6]. A verifiable RLVR veto is: if added latency is measured to be greater than the time it takes the ball to travel more than the success radius, the policy must be considered invalid for that trial, regardless of diffusion trajectory quality.

These three vetoes are counter-traps for the paper’s optimistic interpretation. The diffusion policy is not judged only by expert imitation; it is judged by whether homography, audio segmentation, and real-time clock constraints preserve physical verifiability.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
