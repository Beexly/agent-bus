<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
First-order theory of mind: an agent selects R to maximize expected sHR given current S and reinforcement history N, implicitly computing m(w, f, τ) before acting. Second-order recursion: the agent models the opponent’s habit hierarchy and predicts that under crisis the opponent will regress to the habit with maximal historical ∆sHR (the highest-m attractor). Third-order counter-recursion: the agent anticipates that the opponent knows this regression tendency and may deliberately execute a low-m, high-asynchronism habit H₂ to induce the first agent to over-prepare against H₁.

Crisis habit regression operates as follows. Under load or threat, prefrontal monitoring weakens and behavior collapses onto the strongest cached sHR. If H₁ was trained with short f and short t′, it carries high m and becomes the default attractor. The counter-trap is to train H₂ under conditions where its sCr is preserved but its apparent strength is masked by a slightly longer f or longer t″, keeping its m below H₁ during observation while retaining sufficient absolute strength to execute under crisis. The opponent, expecting H₁, prepares a counter to H₁; when H₂ is deployed instead, the counter misses.

The clock variable f is decisive in this recursion. Because f defines a temporal credit-assignment window, the habit that receives the increment is the behavior occupying the effector at S onset, not necessarily the instrumental response. If reinforcement is delayed beyond the response-production window, the “clock” stamps contingent, adventitious behavior. Recursively, an agent can manipulate an opponent’s crisis attractor by arranging that, during the opponent’s own multi-week training, the behavior present at S onset under stress is a decoy rather than the optimal response. When crisis arrives, the opponent regresses to the decoy (high sHR for the wrong R), while the principal agent, having trained under veridical contingency, deploys the intact high-m target habit. The recursion terminates only when the clock f is aligned such that R itself, and not an adjacent behavior, occupies the contiguity window at reinforcement delivery [PAGE 1].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto rule: if any physical, biomechanical, or environmental layer imposes a hard constraint, ∆sHR = 0 and m is not approached, even when sCr = 1 and w > 0 [PAGE 1].

Physical layer: the postulate’s antecedent requires an actual effector activity r → R [PAGE 1]. If the effector anatomy cannot execute R within the mechanical response window imposed by S onset, no r → R event occurs, and Postulate 4 does not trigger. For example, if S signals a lever press but the limb is mechanically immobilized, the contingency is vacuous.

Biomechanical layer: neuromuscular transmission and spinal/voluntary reflex latencies (on the order of tens to over one hundred milliseconds) consume part of the delay f. If the reinforcement delay f exceeds the effective credit-assignment window set by effector refractory periods and motor conduction time, Ψ₂(f) → 0 [PAGE 1]. Fatigue further slows conduction and prolongs twitch duration, effectively stretching t″ when S is continuous, thereby driving Ψ₃(t″) toward zero [PAGE 1].

Weather/environment layer: temperature alters nerve conduction velocity and receptor threshold; cold increases sensory latency, inflating the effective f and t′ [PAGE 1]. Noise or precipitation degrades S detection, reducing receptor activity s and breaking the contiguity predicate sCr [PAGE 1]. If S is not reliably transduced, sCr = 0 and no increment accrues regardless of need reduction magnitude [PAGE 1].

Adjudication order: (1) verify sCr = 1 at the receptor layer; (2) verify r → R is physically executable at the effector layer; (3) verify f and τ fall within biomechanical credit windows; (4) only then compute m(w, f, τ). A veto at any earlier layer nullifies all downstream computation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Zero need reduction despite perfect contiguity. Setup: lever press (R) produces auditory/visual S onset (sCr = 1) and N successive trials, but no food, water, or drive reduction is delivered (w = 0). Verifiable: measure response rate and sHR proxy across N; confirm drive state remains unchanged. Prediction: Ψ₁(0) = 0 ⇒ m = 0 ⇒ ∆sHR_N = 0 for every N; sHR(N) remains at baseline. Exact singularity: no amount of contiguity or repetition can generate habit strength when the need-reduction factor is null [PAGE 1].

Scenario 2: Reinforcement delay beyond the critical window. Setup: brief S and brief R (small t′), but reward is delivered after a fixed delay f that is parametrically increased (e.g., 1 s, 5 s, 20 s). Verifiable: record asymptotic response rate and estimated m at each f. Prediction: m(f) = Ψ₁(w)·Ψ₂(f)·Ψ₃(t′); as f → ∞, Ψ₂(f) → 0, so m → 0 and response acquisition fails to asymptote. The breakdown regime is f exceeding the species-specific eligibility trace; beyond it, the temporal contiguity clock stamps behavior other than R [PAGE 1].

Scenario 3: Prolonged S overlapping R onset with excessive t″. Setup: S (tone/light) begins before R and continues after R begins for a parametrically varied overlap duration t″; R is emitted during S; reinforcement follows immediately after R onset. Verifiable: manipulate t″ (e.g., 0.5 s, 2 s, 10 s) while holding f and w constant; measure asymptotic sHR. Prediction: m(t″) = Ψ₁(w)·Ψ₂(f)·Ψ₃(t″), a negative growth function of overlap duration [PAGE 1]. If t″ exceeds the critical overlap ceiling, Ψ₃(t″) → 0, yielding ∆sHR = 0 even though R occurred within S. This isolates case (b) of Postulate 4: prolonged receptor stimulation at R onset suppresses habit increment rather than enhancing it [PAGE 1]. Each scenario is falsifiable by observing whether sHR tracks m as defined above or instead violates the multiplicative boundary conditions.
