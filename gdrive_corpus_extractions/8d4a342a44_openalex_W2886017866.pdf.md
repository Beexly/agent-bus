The source imposes a hard data constraint: the Challenge learning set describes matches by “date of match, team names, league, season, final score” [PAGE 2]. That means the admissible input object is \(D=\{\text{date},\text{home team},\text{away team},\text{league},\text{season},g_h,g_a\}\), with \(g_h,g_a\in\mathbb{N}_0\) goals. The paper’s central derivation is therefore not a new classifier but a feature-operator derivation: transform \(D\) into either a recency matrix or a rating matrix, then apply k-NN or XGBoost. For the recency operator, each match is represented by four feature groups per team — attacking strength, defensive strength, home advantage, and strength of opposition — each \(n\) levels deep, giving \(2\times4\times n\) features per match [PAGE 9–10]. With \(n=9\), the operator produced \(207{,}280\) matches and \(72\) features per match, losing \(216{,}743-207{,}280=9{,}463\) matches, or \(4.37\%\), because of the beginning-of-season problem [PAGE 11]. For the rating operator, each team receives four real-valued ratings: home attacking strength \(H_{hatt}\), home defensive weakness \(H_{hdef}\), away attacking strength \(A_{aatt}\), and away defensive weakness \(A_{adef}\); the goal-prediction model is a sigmoid pair, and the ratings are updated after each match by prediction-error rules [PAGE 11–14]. The rating operator produced \(31{,}318\) matches, \(14.4\%\) of the Challenge learning set, with eight features per match [PAGE 19].

The multi-hop model path is: domain knowledge \(\rightarrow\) feature operator \(\rightarrow\) supervised learner \(\rightarrow\) ranked probability score. The best pre-deadline combination was k-NN on rating features, \(RPS_{avg}=0.2054\) on the 206-match prediction set [PAGE 25]. After the deadline, the best overall was XGBoost on rating features, \(RPS_{avg}=0.2023\) [PAGE 24–25]. The paper explicitly says that “a key factor in soccer match outcome prediction lies in the successful incorporation of domain knowledge into the machine learning modeling process” [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit is where the real risk lies. First, the time-series structure is not automatically respected by the learning pipeline: the paper uses continuous-season views, super-league pooling, and random train/test splits [PAGE 23, PAGE 25]. Random splits can mix future and past matches, so reported hold-out scores are not pure temporal forecasts. Second, the continuous-season view is not uniformly valid: for Watford, the “previous” matches before 29/08/2015 include 2006/2007 matches, while Manchester City’s trajectory is smooth across 2014/2015 and 2015/2016 [PAGE 7–8]. Third, the paper notes that at the end of a season “some teams have nothing to play for anymore” [PAGE 9], so late-season matches may not be competitive. Fourth, five prediction matches lacked nine prior matches for at least one team and had to be imputed by league-average recency features [PAGE 23]. Fifth, the rating model’s optimization is computationally expensive: \(30{,}659\) s, about \(84\) h, for the recency operator at \(n=9\) [PAGE 11], and only a subset of leagues and seasons was used for rating features [PAGE 17–19]. The paper’s own caution is decisive: goals are rare, the home/away averages are \(\bar g_h=1.483\) and \(\bar g_a=1.111\), and “86.71% of all matches end either in a draw or a victory of either team by a margin of two or fewer goals difference” [PAGE 27]. That narrow margin is why the achievable \(RPS\) is near \(0.21\), and why the remaining sections must separate formal operators from unsupported causal claims.

The formal goal-prediction operator is defined by two sigmoid equations [PAGE 11]:
\[
\hat g_h(H_{hatt},A_{adef})=\frac{\alpha_h}{1+\exp\!\left(-\beta_h(H_{hatt}+A_{adef})-\gamma_h\right)},\qquad \hat g_h\in\mathbb{R}_0^+,
\]
\[
\hat g_a(A_{aatt},H_{hdef})=\frac{\alpha_a}{1+\exp\!\left(-\beta_a(A_{aatt}+H_{hdef})-\gamma_a\right)},\qquad \hat g_a\in\mathbb{R}_0^+.
\]
Units: \(\hat g_h,\hat g_a\) are predicted goals, so the output unit is goals per match; \(\alpha_h,\alpha_a\) are maximum-goal constants in goals per match, set to \(5\) in the study [PAGE 12]; \(H_{hatt},H_{hdef},A_{aatt},A_{adef}\in\mathbb{R}\) are performance-rating coordinates; \(\beta_h,\beta_a\) have reciprocal rating units so the exponent is dimensionless; \(\gamma_h,\gamma_a\) are dimensionless threshold constants. The rating-update rules are [PAGE 13–14]:
\[
T^{t+1}_{hatt}=T^{t}_{hatt}+\omega_{hatt}(g_h-\hat g_h),\qquad
T^{t+1}_{hdef}=T^{t}_{hdef}+\omega_{hdef}(g_a-\hat g_a),
\]
\[
T^{t+1}_{aatt}=T^{t}_{aatt}+\omega_{aatt}(g_a-\hat g_a),\qquad
T^{t+1}_{adef}=T^{t}_{adef}+\omega_{adef}(g_h-\hat g_h),
\]
where \(g_h,g_a\in\mathbb{N}_0\) are observed goals and \(\omega_\bullet\in\mathbb{R}^+\) are update weights; if ratings are in rating units and goal errors are in goals, each \(\omega_\bullet\) carries rating units per goal. The per-match goal error is [PAGE 16]:
\[
\epsilon_g=\frac12\left[(g_h-\hat g_h)^2+(g_a-\hat g_a)^2\right],
\]
with units \(\text{goals}^2\). The ranked probability score is [PAGE 20]:
\[
\mathrm{RPS}=\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}(p_j-a_j)\right)^2,
\]
where \(r=3\) outcomes, \(p=(p_1,p_2,p_3)\) is a probability vector with \(p_1+p_2+p_3=1\), and \(a\) is the one-hot observed outcome vector. \(\mathrm{RPS}\) is dimensionless on the probability simplex. The average is [PAGE 21]:
\[
\mathrm{RPS}_{avg}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{RPS}_i,
\]
also dimensionless. The k-NN distance over eight rating features is [PAGE 21–22]:
\[
d(X,Y)=\sqrt{\frac18\sum_{i=1}^{8}(x_i-y_i)^2},
\]
in rating units. The PSO update is [PAGE 17]:
\[
v_p(i+1)=\omega v_p(i)+c_1r(\cdot)(y_p(i)-x_p(i))+c_2r(\cdot)(z_k(i)-x_p(i)),
\]
\[
x_p(i+1)=x_p(i)+v_p(i+1),
\]
where \(x\) is a point in the eight-parameter space, \(v\) is its per-generation displacement, \(\omega\) is dimensionless inertia, \(c_1,c_2\) are dimensionless learning constants, and \(r(\cdot)\sim U[0,1]\). The swarm used \(n_{part}=50\), \(maxit=200\), hence at most \(10{,}000\) parameter sets [PAGE 17].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are exact, not metaphorical. In Eqs. 1–2 the denominator \(1+\exp(\cdot)\) is strictly positive for finite exponents, so there is no real singularity; breakdown occurs instead by saturation. If \(\beta_h=0\) or \(\beta_a=0\), the corresponding prediction is constant, \(\hat g=\alpha/(1+e^{-\gamma})\), so team ratings cannot discriminate. If \(\beta\to\infty\), the sigmoid becomes a hard step and gradients vanish away from the threshold. If \(\alpha=0\), the model predicts zero goals; if \(\alpha\to\infty\), outputs are unbounded. As \(H+A\to+\infty\), \(\hat g\to\alpha\); as \(H+A\to-\infty\), \(\hat g\to0\). In Eqs. 3–6, \(\omega_\bullet=0\) freezes ratings; too-large \(\omega_\bullet\) causes oscillation or nonstationarity, which is why the search space was bounded by \(\omega_\bullet\in[0,1.5]\) and \(\beta_\bullet\in[0,5]\), \(\gamma_\bullet\in[-5,5]\) [PAGE 17]. In Eq. 9, \(r=1\) makes \(1/(r-1)\) singular; the paper uses \(r=3\), so it is safe. In Eq. 10, \(n=0\) is singular. In Eq. 11, the fixed \(1/8\) avoids division by zero, but \(d=0\) makes neighbors tied. For k-NN, \(k=0\) is undefined, \(k=1\) is noise-sensitive, and \(k=N_{train}\) collapses to the training prior; the reported optima differ by learning set: \(k_{opt}=125\) and \(248\) for full recency/rating experiments [PAGE 23], but \(k_{opt}=70\) for the submitted rating model [PAGE 25]. For XGBoost, \(t=0\) gives no ensemble and \(\eta=0\) gives no additive learning; the submitted recency model used \(d_{max}=1,\eta=0.06,r_n=0.7,r_f=0.8,t=806\) [PAGE 25–26]. For the recency operator, \(n=0\) gives zero features; \(n=6\) is judged “too low” and \(n=12\) “too high” [PAGE 11].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicitly traced in the source. Reep and Benjamin studied a negative binomial fit to football scores but concluded that “chance does dominate the game” [PAGE 3]. Hill showed a significant correlation between expert predictions and final league tables [PAGE 3]. Maher modeled goals as Poisson variables and obtained a reasonable fit to four English divisions [PAGE 3]. Dixon and Coles observed that long-run team quality is easier to predict than an individual game [PAGE 3]. Elo’s chess rating system was adapted to football, with the caveat that a single rating per team cannot naturally produce draw probabilities [PAGE 4]. Goddard compared goals-based and direct-outcome models and found a hybrid approach best, though differences were small [PAGE 3]. Angelini and De Angelis proposed PARX, a Poisson autoregression capturing attacking and defensive abilities [PAGE 3]. The 2017 Soccer Prediction Challenge then posed a fixed-time-line prediction problem over \(216{,}743\) matches and \(206\) future matches [PAGE 1–2]. The paper’s novelty is retrocausal in the sense that it reads the historical record as a sequence of performance states: each past match updates ratings, and those ratings become features for future matches. The traps are multi-week and multi-season. The beginning-of-season trap loses \(4.37\%\) of data at \(n=9\) [PAGE 11]. The continuous-season trap treats a team’s first match in a new season as the next match after its last match in the prior season; for Watford that prior season is 2006/2007, years before 2015/2016 [PAGE 7–8]. The league-team-composition trap arises because lower leagues change membership through promotion and relegation: over 16 seasons, GER1 had 35 different teams, seven of which played all 16 seasons, while GER2 had 52 different teams and none played all 16 [PAGE 7]. The end-of-season trap is that “some teams have nothing to play for anymore” [PAGE 9]. The recency-depth trap is that a short window is noisy and a long window includes obsolete form [PAGE 11]. The random-split trap is that hold-out scores from random splits are not temporal forecasts even when the underlying feature operator is chronological [PAGE 23, PAGE 25]. These are the multi-week traps that any reanalysis must test before treating \(RPS\approx0.21\) as a stable limit.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is not a claim about player psychology; it is a claim about strategic response functions that the source partially encodes. Crisis habit regression appears in the sigmoid rationale: “teams usually will no longer make an effort to score more goals; instead, they tend to protect their lead” [PAGE 12]. That is a habit regression under a high-lead state: the team stops optimizing goal margin and shifts to risk minimization. The counter-trap is the opponent’s anticipatory response: if the leading team protects, the trailing team may press higher, which changes the goal-generating process but is invisible in a final-score-only data set. The source acknowledges that “single events, such as a red card, can be decisive” [PAGE 2], which is a state discontinuity that can override habitual patterns. The clock layer has two scales. At match scale, the sigmoid saturation at \(\alpha=5\) and the three-goal “almost a certain win” rationale [PAGE 12] compress late-game scoring incentives. At season scale, the end-of-season “nothing to play for” condition [PAGE 9] changes incentives for a subset of matches. A recursive model would need to represent each team’s model of the opponent’s model, but the Challenge data provide only goals, so the observable operator can learn average responses, not intentions. Therefore the correct counter-trap test is not “did the team intend to defend?” but “did the feature operator’s rating update change after a lead-state match in a way that predicts the next match?” The clock test is whether recency depth \(n\) is measured in matches, not weeks; the paper uses match time points \(t-1,\dots,t-n\) [PAGE 5], so calendar gaps and season boundaries are not equivalent to match gaps. This is why the Watford 2006/2007-to-2015/2016 example is a clock failure, not merely a data-sparsity issue [PAGE 7–8].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto can be stated formally. Let \(Y\) be the match outcome, \(D\) the Challenge variables, and \(L\) an external layer containing weather, pitch/ground state, player biomechanics, injury state, or physical condition. The source’s \(D\) contains only “date of match, team names, league, season, final score” [PAGE 2]. Therefore any model estimated from \(D\) alone targets
\[
P(Y\mid D)=\int P(Y\mid D,L)\,dP(L\mid D),
\]
not \(P(Y\mid D,L)\). If two external states \(L_1\neq L_2\) are observationally equivalent given \(D\), then no function of \(D\) can distinguish them. This is an identifiability veto: the data cannot confirm or exclude a biomechanical or weather mechanism. The source itself names “weather or ground conditions” and “red cards, injuries, penalties” among difficult-to-capture events that can produce goals independent of superior or inferior play [PAGE 28]. It also warns that “additional data will not solve the problem due to the small number of goals” [PAGE 28]. The veto proof is therefore: (i) \(L\notin D\); (ii) \(Y\) can vary with \(L\) at fixed \(D\); (iii) the estimator’s conditional distribution is invariant to \(L\) once \(D\) is fixed; hence (iv) any causal attribution to \(L\) from this model is unlicensed. A physical-layer claim is admissible only if \(L\) is measured and the model is re-estimated as \(P(Y\mid D,L)\). A weather-layer claim is admissible only with venue, date-time, and ground-state variables. A biomechanical claim is admissible only with player-load or movement variables. Absent those, the correct adjudication is veto, not a proxy ratio.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual vetoes follow directly from the source operators. First, veto the claim that a larger recency depth is automatically better. Counterfactual: set \(n=12\) instead of \(n=9\). Prediction: the feature dimension becomes \(2\times4\times12=96\), and the number of eligible matches must be smaller than \(207{,}280\), because the beginning-of-season requirement is stricter; the paper already says “\(n=12\) seems to be too high” [PAGE 11]. Verification: recompute the count of matches with at least 12 prior matches per team and check that the feature matrix has 96 columns. Second, veto the claim that continuous-season pooling is harmless for promoted or intermittent teams. Counterfactual: predict Watford versus Manchester City on 29/08/2015 under the continuous-season view. Prediction: Watford’s \(t-4,t-5,t-6,t-7\) features are drawn from 2006/2007, not 2014/2015, because Table 1 marks the red dashed line across seasons years apart [PAGE 8]. Verification: reconstruct the four prior rows for Watford and confirm the season labels. Third, veto the claim that rating features are informative without learned updates. Counterfactual: set all update weights \(\omega_{hatt}=\omega_{hdef}=\omega_{aatt}=\omega_{adef}=0\). Prediction: all ratings remain at their initial zero values [PAGE 15], so Eqs. 1–2 reduce to constants \(\hat g_h=\alpha_h/(1+e^{-\gamma_h})\) and \(\hat g_a=\alpha_a/(1+e^{-\gamma_a})\); every team’s four rating features are identical, Eq. 11 gives \(d(X,Y)=0\) for all pairs, and k-NN has no rating-based discrimination. Verification: simulate the rating table and check that every row is zero and every pairwise distance is zero. These three tests are source-verifiable and do not depend on any hidden variable.
