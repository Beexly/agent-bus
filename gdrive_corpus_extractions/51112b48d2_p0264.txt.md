Tolman’s argument on page 264 (labeled 240 in the scanned text) fundamentally challenges the behaviorist orthodoxy of his time, particularly Watson and Mead. He posits that ideation—the formation of cognitive maps or sign gestalts—typically precedes speech, rather than speech being the foundational mechanism for thought as claimed by radical behaviorists. The key insight is that behavior adjustments to mental representations come first; speech is a subsequent tool that can refine, hold, and sometimes even drive new ideation through automatic rhetorical habits. However, the primary direction of causality is from cognition to language, not vice versa. This implies that higher-order thinking and consciousness do not depend strictly on social symbolic interaction but emerge from individual purposive behavior navigating environmental fields.

The multi-hop reasoning starts with Tolman’s concept of “sign gestalts” as internal maps of environmental relations. These maps are formed through exploratory behavior and anticipation, which are pre-linguistic. The organism adjusts its behavior based on these internal representations before any verbal labeling occurs. Speech, once acquired, becomes an automatic tool that can manipulate these existing maps, allowing for more precise rehearsal and gap-filling in thought processes. Tolman uses this to critique Mead’s theory that taking the attitude of the “organized other” through speech is necessary for consciousness. Instead, Tolman argues that social attitudes and thus speech are outgrowths of already achieved cognitive structures.

Tolman carefully delineates that while speech can occasionally lead understanding, it is normally a consequence of prior ideation. The breakdown regime occurs when one conflates correlation with causation in the relationship between speech and thought, potentially leading back to a linguistic determinism that Tolman explicitly rejects. Furthermore, the application of this theory to modern AI systems raises questions about whether artificial “speech” or symbolic manipulation can generate genuine understanding without underlying cognitive maps—a point Tolman would likely argue against, maintaining that true intelligence requires a grounded, purposive engagement with the world.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s position emerges from the Gestalt psychology tradition, which emphasized holistic perceptual organization over elemental associations. His work represents a synthesis of behaviorism and cognitive science, anticipating later developments in information processing and cognitive mapping. The historical lineage includes Köhler’s insights into insight learning in apes, which demonstrated problem-solving without trial-and-error, supporting the idea of pre-verbal ideation. Tolman directly engages with Mead’s symbolic interactionism, which had gained prominence in the 1930s, arguing that Mead inverted the causal relationship. The multi-week trap arises when one assumes that because language shapes thought (as in Sapir-Whorf), it must originate it. Tolman warns against this regression, showing that many forms of intelligent behavior, including social coordination, exist in non-human animals without language. This perspective has been vindicated by modern neuroscience, which shows that hippocampal place cells form cognitive maps independently of linguistic areas.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis situations, such as encountering novel problems, organisms may regress to habitual verbal routines (“talking to oneself”) to regain cognitive control. This is a form of recursive self-modeling where the agent uses internal speech to simulate the perspective of others or to rehearse solutions. Tolman’s analysis suggests that this recursion is not the source of thought but a tool to stabilize and extend existing ideation. The counter-trap involves mistaking this stabilizing function for generative necessity, leading to an over-reliance on language at the expense of non-symbolic intuition. The clock metaphor: the pace of ideation is not bound by the speed of articulation; inner speech often runs ahead of or lags behind overt speech, indicating that the underlying cognitive processes operate on a different timescale. This supports Tolman’s claim that behavior adjustments to sign gestalts are primary and can occur in parallel with or independent of speech production.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical constraints provide a veto on theories that prioritize speech over ideation. For instance, infants and non-human animals exhibit complex navigational and social behaviors long before acquiring language, demonstrating that cognitive maps are built through sensorimotor experience. Weather and environmental variability further support this: animals adjust to changing conditions using flexible, map-based strategies without any linguistic mediation. A strict linguistic theory would struggle to explain how a bird migrates thousands of miles using celestial cues without any symbolic communication. Thus, the physical layer of behavior—grounded in embodiment and real-time interaction with the environment—must veto any account that makes language the foundation of consciousness. Tolman’s emphasis on “purposive behavior” aligns with this embodied view, where meaning arises from successful navigation of goal-directed paths.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
