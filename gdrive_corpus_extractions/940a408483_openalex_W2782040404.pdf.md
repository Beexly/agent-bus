<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that in-game margins are generated by coaches and players who are modeling each other. Let the state be \(s_t=(m_t,c_t,p_t,d_t,\tau_t)\), where \(m_t\) is score margin, \(c_t\) is remaining clock in seconds, \(p_t\) is possession, \(d_t\) is down and distance, and \(\tau_t\) is timeouts. A trailing team in crisis often regresses to a habitual high-variance pass-heavy policy \(\pi_{\text{crisis}}(a_P\mid s_t)\). The opponent anticipates this and counter-traps: it plays coverage, keeps the clock stopped only when advantageous, spies the quarterback, and forces sacks or turnovers. The resulting \(x_3\) yards-per-pass margin, \(x_5\) third-down margin, and \(x_6\) sack-yards margin are not exogenous causes; they are equilibrium outcomes of \(\pi_{\text{crisis}}\) and \(\hat\pi_{\text{opp}}\). The clock is the binding constraint: with \(c_t\) small, the number of remaining possessions is small, so the same statistical margin can imply very different win probabilities. The source model omits \(c_t\), \(p_t\), and \(\tau_t\). Formally, if
\[
\Pr(\text{win}\mid x,c_t)\neq \Pr(\text{win}\mid x),
\]
then the reported coefficients are not structural. The crisis habit regression is predictable, so a counter-trap can make the habit self-defeating: more passes produce more sack yards and more turnovers, which the model then reads as causes of losing. The correct recursive interpretation is that the opponent’s model of the habit changes the distribution of \(x\). A multi-week opponent can scout the habit and force the counter-trap; a single-game model cannot distinguish habit from adaptation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can veto the statistical model because they are omitted variables that are correlated with the included margins. Let \(Z=(W,B)\) collect weather \(W\) and biomechanical \(B\) states. The true conditional mean is
\[
E[Y\mid X,Z]=\beta_0+\beta^T X+\gamma^T Z.
\]
If the fitted model omits \(Z\), then
\[
\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta=\beta+(X^T X)^{-1}X^T Z\gamma.
\]
The bias is nonzero whenever \(X^T Z\gamma\neq 0\). A veto is declared when the adjusted and baseline coefficients differ by more than \(1.96\) standard errors, or when calibration slope leaves \([0.8,1.2]\). Physical example: aerodynamic drag on a thrown ball is
\[
F_D=\frac12\rho C_D A v_{\text{rel}}^2,\qquad v_{\text{rel}}=v-v_{\text{wind}},
\]
with \(\rho\) in kg/m\(^3\), \(A\) in m\(^2\), and \(v\) in m/s. Wind changes \(v_{\text{rel}}\), so it changes pass range and interception risk; the coefficient on yards-per-pass margin is not transportable across wind regimes. Biomechanical example: traction is bounded by
\[
F_f\leq \mu_s N,
\]
where \(\mu_s\) is the static friction coefficient and \(N\) is normal force in newtons. Rain or cold lowers \(\mu_s\), reducing acceleration \(a=(F_{\text{drive}}-F_f)/m\) in m/s\(^2\), which changes route separation, sack avoidance, and turnover risk. Weather example: precipitation, wind speed, and wet-bulb globe temperature shift both the mean and variance of passing and kicking outcomes. Therefore the model’s conditional independence assumption \(\Pr(Y\mid X)=\Pr(Y\mid X,Z)\) fails. The veto is not that the statistical fit is wrong in-sample; it is that the coefficients are regime-dependent. If a high-wind or high-precipitation stratum shows calibration slope below 0.8 or Brier score above 0.20, the all-weather use of the model is vetoed.
