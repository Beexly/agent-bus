An agent who assumes the ROM gain is purely structural and durable commits a counter-trap: they may treat a transient, superficial viscoelastic change as if it confers lasting mechanical advantage, when the study only establishes an immediate post-intervention effect in healthy, recreationally active young adults [PAGE 6].

The clock variable is temporal. The intervention is 1 minute; the control is 5 minutes of supine rest; measurements occur immediately post-intervention only [PAGE 2][PAGE 6]. No decay constant, no 5- or 10-min follow-up, and no longitudinal adaptation data exist [PAGE 6]. If the agent recurses this intervention daily or pre-competition based on a single acute study, they are operating without a clock model of tissue recovery or effect persistence. The high VAS (7–8) also introduces a cognitive distortion: under crisis, athletes may tolerate significant discomfort for marginal, short-lived gains, mistaking intensity for efficacy [PAGE 2]. Recursive theory of mind thus yields three nested states: (1) crisis habit regression toward cupping as a quick fix; (2) counter-trap of equating superficial stiffness reduction with neuromuscular protection or durable performance enhancement; (3) missing clock of temporal decay, meaning the agent cannot estimate how long the ROM window remains open before tissue stiffness regresses toward baseline.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Five veto layers are adjudicated against the manuscript’s physical and biomechanical evidence.

Veto 1 — Depth penetration (physical): The data show significant stiffness reductions only in deep fascia (DF) and superficial medial gastrocnemius (UMG); middle (MMG), deep (LMG), and deep intermuscular fascia (DIF) exhibited no significant condition × time interactions [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. The authors attribute this depth-dependence to limited deformation transmission, noting mean subcutaneous adipose tissue thickness in the posterior lower leg is approximately 6 mm (Gibney et al., 2010) and that cup-induced deformation likely attenuates with depth [PAGE 5]. Verdict: claims of deep tissue release are vetoed. The mechanical effect is bounded to the superficial fascial-muscle interface and does not generalize to deep muscle or deep intermuscular fascia under the M-size cup and 1-min protocol used.

Veto 2 — Neuromuscular mediation (biomechanical): EMG activity during passive dorsiflexion was low (1–5 %MVIC) and unchanged under both conditions; no significant interactions were found for TA, MG, or SOL at 0%, 30%, or 60% of maximum dorsiflexion [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. MVIC values also showed no significant condition × time interaction [PAGE 4]. Verdict: the hypothesis that ROM improvement is mediated by reduced protective guarding or altered maximal neuromuscular output is vetoed. Residual uncertainty: the pain-limited ROM endpoint leaves stretch tolerance as a non-neuromuscular sensory confound that was not directly measured [PAGE 3][PAGE 5].

Veto 3 — Dose standardization (physical): Suction pressure was not objectively quantified; application was manual and identical in procedure but not in measured pressure [PAGE 2][PAGE 6]. VAS scores ranged 7–8, indicating high and potentially variable mechanical load across participants [PAGE 2]. Verdict: reproducibility and dose-response claims are vetoed. Without quantified negative pressure (kPa gauge), the mechanical dose cannot be replicated, scaled, or compared across cup sizes or practitioners.

Veto 4 — Temporal asymmetry and time confound (physical/experimental): CUP lasted 1 min; CON lasted 5 min of supine rest [PAGE 2]. The authors acknowledge Limitation 6: uncertainty about whether pre-testing ROM timing differed materially between conditions [PAGE 6]. Verdict: the clean isolation of cupping-specific effect from mere passage of time or rest duration is weakened; the CON condition conservatively exceeded CUP duration, but the asymmetry remains an unbalanced design element.

Veto 5 — Environmental/thermal (physical): Measurements occurred in a controlled lab setting, but skin temperature and ambient conditions were not reported [PAGE 3]. Shear-wave velocity is temperature-dependent; without thermal monitoring, inter-session or inter-participant temperature drift could confound shear modulus readings, though the within-subject crossover design partially mitigates this. Verdict: not directly vetoed by the data, but flagged as an unmeasured physical variable that could affect the μ = ρv² relationship [PAGE 3].

Cross-layer synthesis: The cupping effect is real but superficially bounded, temporally immediate-only, and neuromorphologically silent. Any application protocol that claims deep fascial release, neuromuscular downregulation, or durable ROM alteration beyond the immediate post-intervention window is vetoed by the presented evidence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with testable predictions:

Scenario A — Pressure standardization counterfactual. If suction pressure were objectively quantified and held constant (e.g., −25 kPa gauge) rather than manually applied, and VAS were capped at ≤3/10 instead of the observed 7–8 [PAGE 2], then the mechanical dose would be reduced and standardized. Prediction: the Cohen’s dz for deep fascia and UMG shear-modulus reduction would fall below the large threshold (|dz| < 0.80), potentially to medium or small, and the condition × time interaction p-values could exceed α = 0.05 [PAGE 4]. Rationale: high pain (VAS 7–8) likely amplifies tissue deformation and nociceptive stretch tolerance; a comfortable, low-pressure dose may not reproduce the large effect sizes. Vetoes generalizability of the reported large effects to routine clinical dosing.

Scenario B — Temporal decay counterfactual. If SWE and ROM were re-measured at 5 and 10 minutes post-intervention—data the authors explicitly state were not collected (Limitation 5) [PAGE 6]—then the viscoelastic recovery curve could be estimated. Prediction: shear modulus of DF and UMG returns to within 5% of PRE values by 5 minutes, and ROM increase decays to < +2.0° by 10 minutes. Vetoes the implicit assumption that the acute ROM window persists long enough to alter movement mechanics during a training session or competition.

Scenario C — Adipose-depth stratification counterfactual. If participants were stratified by subcutaneous adipose thickness (e.g., ≥8 mm vs < 6 mm, referencing Gibney et al., 2010 [PAGE 5]) or if a larger-diameter cup were tested, then depth-specific engagement could be isolated. Prediction: the thick-subcutaneous subgroup shows no significant ROM or stiffness change (condition × time p > 0.05 for DF and UMG), whereas a larger cup might engage MMG but has not been tested [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. Vetoes the external validity claim that results apply across body compositions and that cup size can be freely selected to target deeper layers without direct comparative evidence.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
