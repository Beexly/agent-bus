The source is a holdout-season predictive-modelling study in elite Australian football, not a demonstration that training-load monitoring is useless. The multi-hop chain is: five load variables are measured on each session-day; rolling, exponentially weighted, monotony, strain, and acute:chronic features are constructed with 3-, 6-, and 21-day windows; 58 load features plus age and match-day indicator are passed to regularised logistic regression, generalised estimating equations, random forests, or support vector machines; hyperparameters are tuned by 10-fold cross-validation on 2014–2015; the frozen model is evaluated on 2016; AUC is computed on the untouched 2016 test set; and finally the decision utility is audited through false-negative versus false-positive cost ratios. The headline result is a limits result: “Predictive performance was only marginally better than chance for models of non-contact and non-contact time-loss injuries (AUC<0.65)” [PAGE 1], while “The best performing model was a multivariate logistic regression for hamstring injuries (best AUC=0.76)” [PAGE 1]. The authors conclude that single-club load-only models “showed poor ability to predict injuries when tested on previously unseen data, suggesting limited application as a daily decision tool for practitioners” [PAGE 1]. That conclusion is stronger than a mere association claim and weaker than a causal claim.

A second hop is the temporal geometry. For each workload variable \(w\), the paper uses 3-, 6-, and 21-day accumulation windows [PAGE 4], EWMA with 3-, 6-, and 21-day decay parameters [PAGE 5], 3:21 and 6:21 acute:chronic ratios [PAGE 5], and 7-day monotony and strain, except HSR where zeros make monotony and strain unstable [PAGE 5]. The EWMA construction is explicitly lagged: the value on day \(i\) has “no dependence on the training load that day” to avoid using injury-day information to predict the injury [PAGE 4–5]. The first 14 days of each season are removed so that chronic and EWMA features can be initialized [PAGE 7]. That is a defensible leakage-control choice, but it also means early-season observations are systematically absent. The study tests a 5-day lag outcome and finds no improvement: “Attempting to account for a possible lag time between training load spikes and injury occurrence ... did not improve predictive performance (mean AUC=0.50-0.57)” [PAGE 8]. Thus the multi-week trap is not that the authors ignored lag; they tested it and the data did not reward it.

A third hop is the class-imbalance and evaluation trap. Injury is rare: in the testing data, NC is 67 of 4,664 records, NCTL is 42 of 4,664, and HS is 13 of 4,664 [PAGE 8, Table 2]. The paper explicitly warns that error rate is misleading: “if the injury rate is 1%, a model always predicting no-injury is 99% accurate” [PAGE 7]. It therefore uses AUC and evaluates every model on exactly the same test set, with “no under-sampling or SMOTE applied to testing data” [PAGE 7]. Under-sampling generally degraded performance except for SVM, and SMOTE did not materially improve most models [PAGE 10]. PCA reduced 60 variables to 14 components explaining 95% of variance [PAGE 11], which shows that the load metrics are highly redundant; the authors note that “the different metrics contain largely overlapping information” [PAGE 14]. This redundancy is a plausible explanation for weak prediction, but it also means that adding more GPS/accelerometer derivatives is unlikely to rescue the model.

A fourth hop is the gray-zone audit of generalization. The 2016 test season had high player turnover: “of the 13 total hamstring injuries in the testing data; 10 were to new players and only 3 to returning players” [PAGE 11]. The returning-player hamstring result may look better, but the authors warn that “The inflated results for the returning players may be a consequence of the small sample size and not truly representative of expected future performance” [PAGE 11]. The learning curve also suggests underfitting for regularised logistic regression and overfitting for SVM; the paper estimates that “at least an order of magnitude (tenfold) more data may be needed” [PAGE 13]. Finally, staff in both training and testing seasons were aware of emerging load-injury research and adjusted training accordingly, which “may have influenced the distribution of training load variables recorded” [PAGE 15]. That is an intervention-feedback problem: the monitoring environment is not a passive observatory. The correct audit conclusion is therefore: load features carry association signal, but the source does not establish a deployable individual daily injury oracle, especially for NC/NCTL, and the HS result is fragile because of rare-event count and cohort turnover.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(w_i\) denote one training-load variable on day \(i\). The five variables and units are: distance above 3 km/h in metres; moderate speed running 18–24 km/h in metres; high speed running above 24 km/h in metres; session-RPE in arbitrary units, defined as rating of perceived exertion multiplied by session duration; and PlayerLoad in arbitrary units, a proprietary accelerometer-derived magnitude of rate of change of acceleration [PAGE 4, Table 1]. These are not interchangeable physical quantities; any model that mixes them without scaling has unit-dependent PCA behaviour.

Rolling accumulation for window \(c\in\{3,6,21\}\) days is operationalised as
\[
C_i^{(c)}=\sum_{j=i-c}^{i-1} w_j ,
\]
with units of \(w\). The source states: “Rolling averages (C) were calculated on each day of the season (i) using a 3, 6 and 21 day accumulation window” [PAGE 4]. The extracted formula is typographically imperfect, but the operative definition is the lagged window ending at \(i-1\). Breakdown regime: if the window is shorter than \(c\) or if the season burn-in is absent, \(C_i^{(c)}\) is not comparable across players; the source removes the first 14 days of each season for this reason [PAGE 7].

Exponentially weighted moving average for decay parameter \(N\in\{3,6,21\}\) is
\[
E_i^{(N)}=\lambda_N w_{i-1}+(1-\lambda_N)E_{i-1}^{(N)},\qquad
\lambda_N=\frac{2}{N+1}.
\]
The source gives the recursion and the lambda relation directly [PAGE 5]. Units are those of \(w\). The lag is deliberate: \(E_i^{(N)}\) uses \(w_{i-1}\), not \(w_i\), “to avoid information recorded on the day of an injury being used to try and predict that injury” [PAGE 4–5]. Singularity/breakdown: the recursion requires an initial \(E\), so early-season values are burn-in-dependent; the source discards 14 days [PAGE 7]. For \(N=3\), \(\lambda=0.5\); for \(N=6\), \(\lambda=2/7\approx0.2857\); for \(N=21\), \(\lambda=1/11\approx0.0909\). These are dimensionless decay weights, not physical load units.

Monotony over the previous 7 days is
\[
M_i=\frac{\bar w_{i-7:i-1}}{s_{i-7:i-1}},
\]
where \(\bar w\) is the mean daily load and \(s\) is the standard deviation over the same 7-day window; \(M_i\) is dimensionless. The source defines monotony as “the average training load in the previous 7 days divided by the standard deviation of daily loads over the same time” [PAGE 5]. Strain is
\[
S_i=\left(\sum_{j=i-7}^{i-1}w_j\right)M_i,
\]
with units of \(w\) times dimensionless, i.e. units of \(w\). The source defines strain as “the sum of load in the previous 7 days multiplied by the training monotony” [PAGE 5]. Singularity/breakdown: if \(s_{i-7:i-1}=0\), monotony is undefined or infinite, and strain inherits that breakdown. The paper avoids this for HSR because “players frequently accumulating zero HSR load in the previous 7 days” [PAGE 5]. This is a real zero-variance denominator, not a modelling nuisance.

Acute:chronic workload ratio for acute window \(a\in\{3,6\}\) and chronic window \(c=21\) is
\[
r_i^{(a,c)}=\frac{\sum_{j=i-a}^{i-1}w_j}{\sum_{j=i-c}^{i-1}w_j},
\]
dimensionless. The exponentially weighted analogue replaces the sums with \(E_i^{(N_a)}\) and \(E_i^{(N_c)}\). The source states that 3- and 6-day acute windows were used with a 21-day chronic window [PAGE 5]. Singularity rule: “When players had no chronic workload (and by definition zero acute load) they were assigned an acute:chronic workload ratio of zero” [PAGE 5]. That is a convention, not a mathematical limit: the ratio is undefined at zero denominator, and assigning zero is a domain decision. If the convention is changed, the feature distribution and model coefficients change. Breakdown regime also occurs when the chronic window is truncated by season boundaries or by the 14-day burn-in.

For the 60-variable model matrix \(X\), PCA is written via singular value decomposition
\[
X=U\Sigma V^\top,\qquad Z_m=X V_m,
\]
where \(m\) is the smallest number of components such that
\[
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sigma_k^2}{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sigma_k^2}\ge0.95.
\]
The source says PCA was calculated by SVD and “A 95% cumulative variance threshold was used to extract m principal components” [PAGE 7], and reports that 14 components were required [PAGE 11]. Assumption caveat: the extracted text does not report an explicit standardisation step. PCA variance is unit-dependent unless variables are scaled; because distance is in metres, session-RPE and PlayerLoad are arbitrary units, and age is in years, the 95% variance rule is not unit-invariant unless standardisation was applied. The paper’s correlation/factor map shows grouping of ACWR and EWMA ACWR variables and of 21-day versus 3-day cumulative loads [PAGE 11], which is consistent with strong redundancy, but the exact PC units are not identifiable from the supplied text.

Regularised logistic regression is
\[
p_i=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid X_i)=\sigma(\beta_0+\beta^\top X_i),\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
\]
with elastic-net penalty
\[
\hat\beta=\arg\min_{\beta}\left[-\ell(\beta)+\lambda\left(\alpha\|\beta\|_1+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2\right)\right].
\]
The source identifies elastic net regularisation for LR [PAGE 6]. AUC is
\[
\mathrm{AUC}=\Pr(\hat p_{\text{injured}}>\hat p_{\text{non-injured}})
+\frac{1}{2}\Pr(\hat p_{\text{injured}}=\hat p_{\text{non-injured}}),
\]
dimensionless, with 0.5 random and 1.0 perfect; the source states “A perfect model would have AUC=1.0 and random guessing would be expected to produce AUC=0.5” [PAGE 7]. GEE used a binomial link and autoregressive correlation structure for repeated observations [PAGE 6]; RF and RBF-SVM were the nonlinear alternatives [PAGE 6].

The decision-theoretic audit uses the reported cost trade-off. If \(C_{FN}\) is the cost of a missed injury and \(C_{FP}\) is the cost of an unnecessarily modified session, the source considered relative costs \(C_{FN}/C_{FP}=50,100,1000\) [PAGE 9]. Table 3 reports, respectively: TPR 0.08 and FPR 0.004; TPR 0.54 and FPR 0.11; TPR 0.92 and FPR 0.53 [PAGE 9]. Using the testing HS prevalence \(\pi=13/4664\approx0.00279\), the positive predictive value
\[
\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi+\mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi)}
\]
is approximately 0.05, 0.01, and 0.005, matching the source’s reported “Probability injury given positive prediction” values [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regime: these PPV values are prevalence-dependent; if prevalence rises or falls, PPV changes even with unchanged TPR and FPR. AUC alone does not adjudicate deployment; the cost-sensitive confusion matrix does.

The paper’s lineage is visible in its references and in the operators it inherits. Foster et al. (2001) supply monotony and strain [PAGE 5, PAGE 16]; Gabbett (2010) supplies an injury-prediction model for noncontact soft-tissue injuries in collision sport [PAGE 2, PAGE 16]; Colby et al. (2016) supply the complex-systems critique and non-linear pattern-recognition rationale [PAGE 2, PAGE 14]; and McCall et al. (2017) supply the warning about misuse of the word “prediction” in sport-science association studies [PAGE 3, PAGE 17]. The study is therefore a retrocausal checkpoint: it takes operators that were originally used to establish association and asks whether they survive a true future-season holdout. The answer is mostly no for NC/NCTL and only partially yes for HS.

The multi-week traps are explicit. First, the 3-, 6-, and 21-day windows overlap and induce collinearity; PCA shows that a few components capture most variance, and “the different metrics contain largely overlapping information” [PAGE 14]. Second, the 14-day burn-in removes early-season data and can hide early-season load spikes [PAGE 7]. Third, a 5-day lag was tested and failed to improve prediction (mean AUC 0.50–0.57) [PAGE 8]. Fourth, new-player turnover breaks the continuity assumption: 10 of 13 HS testing injuries were in new players, and the returning-player result is inflated by only 3 injuries [PAGE 11]. Fifth, the training environment was not passive: staff knew the load-injury literature and adjusted planning, which “may have influenced the distribution of training load variables recorded” [PAGE 15]. That is a retrospective contamination trap: later analyses cannot treat earlier load distributions as if they were generated under a fixed, blind protocol. Sixth, the single-club design limits sample size; the authors note that “There were an insufficient number of injury records of a particular type (other than hamstrings) to create different injury specific models” [PAGE 15]. The historical lineage therefore does not support a simple “more features, more prediction” narrative; it supports a narrower claim: load monitoring is a valid best-practice monitoring activity, but load-only individual injury prediction in this cohort was weak.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem is not “what is the injury probability?” but “what should the practitioner do with a probability under asymmetric costs, and how does that action change future data?” The source frames this directly: “how they weight the consequences (cost) of a false negative (missed injury) relative to a false positive prediction (unnecessarily cancelling or modifying a session by decreasing volume or intensity)” [PAGE 9]. Table 3 makes the recursion concrete. At a 50:1 cost ratio, the model achieves TPR 0.08 at FPR 0.004, so it misses most injuries but rarely modifies sessions unnecessarily [PAGE 9]. At 100:1, TPR rises to 0.54 but FPR rises to 0.11 [PAGE 9]. At 1000:1, TPR is 0.92 but FPR is 0.53, meaning more than half of non-injury sessions are flagged [PAGE 9]. There is “a trade-off between correctly predicting more injuries and incorrectly flagging non-injury sessions” [PAGE 9]. A practitioner who ignores this trade-off is not using the model; they are using an AUC number as a talisman.

Crisis habit regression appears when the decision environment becomes rare-event and high-consequence. The default habit is binary: either “always train” because injuries are rare, or “always modify” because the practitioner fears a missed injury. The source exposes the first habit with the 1% base-rate example: “if the injury rate is 1%, a model always predicting no-injury is 99% accurate” [PAGE 7]. The second habit is exposed by the conservative 1000:1 row: TPR 0.92 but FPR 0.53 [PAGE 9]. In a crisis, the practitioner may regress to a single threshold or a single weekly rule, ignoring the fact that the model’s positive predictive value at moderate risk is only about 0.01 [PAGE 9]. That is a habit regression from calibrated probability to moral certainty.

The counter-trap is that acting on the model changes the model’s future input distribution. If a coach modifies training after a high predicted risk, the next EWMA and ACWR values are no longer natural observations; they are intervened values. The source’s staff-awareness limitation is the empirical trace of this: “In both the model training and testing seasons physical preparation staff were aware of the emerging body of research on training loads and injury risk ... and gave consideration to this when planning training. This may have influenced the distribution of training load variables recorded” [PAGE 15]. A naive recursive loop—predict, modify, retrain, predict—can learn the coach’s policy rather than the athlete’s injury process. The counter-trap is especially sharp for false positives: modifying a session may reduce load, but it also removes the exposure that would have tested the model’s negative prediction.

The clock is the time-scale discipline that prevents the recursion from collapsing. The source’s clocks are daily prediction, 3/6-day acute windows, 21-day chronic windows, 7-day monotony/strain, 5-day lag outcomes, season boundaries, and the 14-day burn-in [PAGE 4–8]. The 5-day lag clock failed to improve prediction, which is evidence against a simple “spike then wait five days” habit [PAGE 8]. The season clock matters because the first 14 days are removed [PAGE 7]; the player-turnover clock matters because new players dominate the HS test injuries [PAGE 11]. A recursive theory of mind for this setting should therefore be: the practitioner’s action is a policy \(\pi(\hat p_i, \text{cost ratio}, \text{clock})\), and the data-generating process is \(P(\text{load}_{i+1}\mid \pi)\). Without accounting for that loop, the model is not a decision tool; it is a descriptive association surface. The source’s own practical conclusion is restrained: “Using individual training loads as a daily decision tool for athlete injury prediction has limited utility in this study” [PAGE 15].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is that load metrics are proxies, not tissue-state measurements. Distance, MSR, HSR, session-RPE, and PlayerLoad quantify external or perceptual load [PAGE 4, Table 1]; they do not measure hamstring fascicle strain, eccentric strength, neuromuscular fatigue, or tissue healing. The best HS model reaches AUC 0.76 [PAGE 1], but the source itself reports that at a 100:1 cost ratio the HS model has TPR 0.54 and FPR 0.11, with “Probability injury given positive prediction” equal to 0.01 [PAGE 9]. That is a physical-layer sufficiency failure for a low-tolerance daily decision: a positive flag is usually a false positive in this prevalence regime. The physical layer can veto a claim of deployable individual prediction even if the association is real.

The biomechanical-layer veto is direct because the variable is absent. The source states: “Running demands in relative speed zones, subjective wellness and fatigue markers and biomechanical screening data were not available” [PAGE 15]. Therefore any claim that load-only models have captured the biomechanical mechanism of hamstring injury is unsupported. The paper selected hamstring injuries because they were the most frequent specific injury in the cohort and common in Australian football [PAGE 5], but it did not include strength asymmetry, sprint mechanics, muscle architecture, or prior injury history. The omission is not a minor limitation; it is an omitted-layer veto on causal sufficiency. A load-only model can be a monitor, but it cannot adjudicate whether a given athlete’s hamstring risk is driven by tissue capacity, sprint exposure, or load progression.

The weather-layer veto is subtler. The source’s session-RPE is described as a subjective metric that “can take into account influences such as heat stress, residual fatigue and other contextual factors that the other objective measures cannot” [PAGE 4]. That means heat stress may be latent inside sRPE, but it is not measured, identified, or separable. There is no direct weather variable in the model. Therefore a weather-specific claim—e.g., that heat altered injury risk independent of load—cannot be tested from this source. The cross-layer adjudication is: if the required layer is absent, the model may still predict, but the prediction cannot be attributed to that layer. The veto is a claim-level veto, not a demand that every model include every variable.

A formal adjudication rule can be stated without inventing a new ratio. Let \(L\) be the load-only feature set, \(B\) the biomechanical feature set, and \(W\) the weather/heat feature set. The source provides evidence for \(L\) only; \(B=\varnothing\) and \(W=\varnothing\) in the reported model [PAGE 15]. If a deployment claim requires sufficiency of \(L\) for individual injury, the claim is vetoed unless a calibrated decision rule satisfies the practitioner’s false-positive tolerance. Table 3 shows that at moderate risk the PPV is about 0.01 with FPR 0.11, and at conservative risk the PPV is about 0.005 with FPR 0.53 [PAGE 9]. Those numbers are the proof of the practical veto: the physical layer alone does not meet a low false-positive standard in this rare-event regime. The correct cross-layer conclusion is not “loads do not matter”; it is “loads alone are insufficient for the claimed daily individual decision, and biomechanical and weather layers are unidentified in this source.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
