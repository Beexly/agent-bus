<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Hull’s formalization is deeply rooted in early 20th-century behaviorism’s quest for quantification. The use of “wats” echoes physical units (like watts), reflecting the era’s ambition to reduce psychology to physics-like laws. The normal ogive function traces back to Fechner’s psychophysics and Galton’s work on variation, importing statistical mechanics into behavior. The specific reference to Simley’s composite curves (Figure 22) indicates reliance on pooled human data, which may introduce artifacts—such as the “superficial indication” of a threshold—that later researchers might misinterpret as neurophysiological constants. Historically, this approach set traps for subsequent theories: by anchoring \(s_{ER}\) to latency curves, Hull conflated learning (trial-dependent change) with momentary stimulus efficacy, a distinction later clarified by Rescorla-Wagner models. The multi-week trap lies in assuming monotonic growth of \(s_{ER}\); extinction data often show recovery or spontaneous remission, violating the assumed irreversibility.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis habit regression, when primary reinforcement is threatened, organisms may revert to earlier learned responses. Within Hull’s framework, this could occur if \(s_{ER}\) for a dominant habit drops below its effective threshold (e.g., due to fatigue or interference), allowing a weaker but still active \(s_{ER}\) from an older habit to exceed its own threshold. Counter-trap mechanisms involve secondary reinforcement (drives like anxiety) boosting \(s_{ER}\) for escape behaviors. The “clock” aspect refers to circadian or arousal rhythms modulating \(s_{LR}\); for instance, sleep deprivation might lower \(s_{LR}\), making sub-threshold stimuli effective. Regression thus emerges not from memory loss but from dynamic shifts in the \(s_{ER}/s_{LR}\) ratio across competing response hierarchies.
