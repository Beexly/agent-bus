The paper states that RiteWeight “repeatedly changes the cluster boundaries and uses the resulting transition matrices to estimate stationarity rather than to propagate dynamics” [PAGE 2]. Therefore the clustering space is not a conventional MSM state space; it is a finite-iteration separation device. The second step is to see why representation quality matters: with finite data and finite iterations, configurations that remain close in the clustering representation may not be separated, so “an effective RiteWeight representation should distinguish configurations separated by important kinetic or energetic barriers” [PAGE 2]. The third step is the selection logic: the authors compute a coarse MSM on held-out data, partition it into 10 states with k-means, and use the sum of seven squared nontrivial eigenvalues as a proxy. They explicitly state that “the score serves as a proxy for whether kinetically distinct regions remain sufficiently resolved in the reduced feature space used for random clustering” [PAGE 3]. The fourth step is the independence check: “Importantly, RiteWeight performance itself was not used during model or hyperparameter selection” [PAGE 3]. Thus the downstream RiteWeight error is an external test, not a tuning target.

The gray-zone audit is where the claims must be graded. The strongest empirical claim is comparative and system-specific: under a 10-trial random search, DeepTICA gave lower folding-MFPT relative error than five-dimensional linear TICA and was more stable across three independent searches, while SPIB-VAE was more variable; after 50 trials, both learned representations beat linear TICA and SPIB-VAE improved most. The paper states “DeepTICA was both more accurate and more robust to hyperparameter selection” [PAGE 8] in the limited-search setting, and that “SPIB-VAE showed greater variability under the limited search but benefited substantially from broader hyperparameter exploration” [PAGE 10]. These are supported for the Trp-cage system and the tested iteration counts. The weaker claim is universal superiority or a causal guarantee that a higher coarse-MSM score always yields faster RiteWeight convergence. The evidence is qualitative: three optimization runs, one linear baseline, one protein system, fixed five-dimensional reduction, and a score constructed on equilibrium-like held-out data rather than on the source–sink NESS itself. The paper itself says “Future work will be needed to determine how well these observations generalize across biomolecular systems and alternative molecular representations” [PAGE 11]. A further gray zone is optimization-budget confounding: SPIB-VAE’s late improvement may reflect the 40 additional TPE-guided trials, not an intrinsic representation advantage at matched search effort. DeepTICA’s early saturation suggests its objective is easier to optimize in the explored space, but that is not the same as proving DeepTICA is always the better clustering space. Finally, the “VAMP2 score” terminology in the Figure 2 caption [PAGE 10] should be read as the same sum-of-squared-eigenvalue kinetic score described in the Methods, not as a new independent observable.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core operators are the transfer operator on molecular configurations, its finite-dimensional covariance approximation, the coarse MSM transition operator, and the RiteWeight weight-update operator. The representation map is
\[
Z(t)=f_\theta[X(t)],
\]
where \(X(t)\) is the minimal residue–residue distance descriptor and \(Z(t)\in\mathbb{R}^5\) is the learned latent coordinate. This is Eq. (1) on [PAGE 4]. The latent dimensionality is fixed at five: “the reduced-space dimensionality was fixed at five for all methods” [PAGE 4]. If \(X\) has length units, the raw covariance has length-squared units; the latent coordinates and generalized eigenvalues are dimensionless after the learned transformation.

DeepTICA solves the generalized TICA eigenvalue problem
\[
C_{\Delta t}v_i=\lambda_i C_0 v_i,
\]
Eq. (2) on [PAGE 4], where \(C_0\) is the instantaneous covariance and \(C_{\Delta t}\) is the symmetrized time-lagged covariance. The eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) are dimensionless. Training minimizes
\[
L_{\mathrm{DeepTICA}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{5}\lambda_i^2,
\]
Eq. (3) on [PAGE 4], so the objective is dimensionless and favors slowly decorrelating modes. Validation uses
\[
L_{\mathrm{val}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{5}\left(\lambda_{i,\mathrm{val}}\right)^2,
\]
Eq. (8) on [PAGE 5], with the improvement test
\[
L_{\mathrm{val}}^{\mathrm{current}}\lt L_{\mathrm{val}}^{\mathrm{best}}-\epsilon,
\]
Eq. (9) on [PAGE 5]. Here \(\epsilon\) is dimensionless and patience \(p\) is measured in epochs. The final training duration is
\[
e_{\mathrm{run}}=\max\left[e_{\mathrm{best}}^{(1)},e_{\mathrm{best}}^{(2)},\ldots,e_{\mathrm{best}}^{(10)}\right],
\]
Eq. (12) on [PAGE 6], also in epochs.

The coarse MSM score is
\[
S_{\mathrm{MSM}}=\sum_{i=1}^{7}\left(\lambda_i^{\mathrm{MSM}}\right)^2,
\]
Eq. (10) on [PAGE 5], and Optuna minimizes the negative
\[
L_{\mathrm{MSM}}=-S_{\mathrm{MSM}}.
\]
Both are dimensionless. The held-out features are partitioned into 10 states using k-means [PAGE 5], and the score is averaged over 10 train/validation splits and used as the Optuna objective [PAGE 5]. The paper describes this as a “MSM-based VAMP2 score” in the Figure 2 caption [PAGE 10].

The RiteWeight folding MFPT is computed from the reciprocal-flux relation
\[
\mathrm{MFPT}=\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{i_1\notin\mathrm{folded},\,i_2\in\mathrm{folded}}w_i\right)^{-1},
\]
Eq. (13) on [PAGE 7], with \(\tau=10\ \mathrm{ns}\), weights \(w_i\) dimensionless, and MFPT in ns. The relative error is
\[
\mathrm{Relative\ Error}\ (\%)=100\frac{\left|\mathrm{MFPT}_{\mathrm{RW}}(n)-\mathrm{MFPT}_{\mathrm{haMSM}}\right|}{\mathrm{MFPT}_{\mathrm{haMSM}}},
\]
Eq. (14) on [PAGE 7], in percent. For SPIB-VAE, the source describes a stochastic latent representation trained with a future-metastable-state decoder and a KL-divergence regulator [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 6], but does not typeset the full objective. The standard information-bottleneck form is
\[
L_{\mathrm{SPIB}}=\mathbb{E}\left[-\log p_\theta(s_{t+\Delta t}\mid z_t)\right]+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z_t\mid x_t)\parallel p(z_t)\right),
\]
with both terms in nats under natural logarithms and \(\beta\) dimensionless in that convention. This is a formal contextual statement, not a source-typeset equation. Probability masses, stationary weights, transition probabilities, and \(S_{\mathrm{MSM}}\) are dimensionless; KL divergence and entropy are in nats; time lags and MFPT are in ns.

Assumptions: time-lagged pairs at 10 ns carry transfer-operator signal; the equilibrium-like 208 \(\mu\)s trajectory is a usable input ensemble; the source–sink boundary condition is imposed by redirecting folded-sink transitions to the unfolded source [PAGE 7]; random clustering is applied only to intermediate configurations, producing 10 intermediate clusters plus folded and unfolded states, hence a \(12\times12\) transition matrix [PAGE 7]; the haMSM reference is accurate for this dataset [PAGE 7]; the coarse MSM score is computed on held-out equilibrium-like data, not on the source–sink NESS; k-means with 10 states is numerically stable; and the RiteWeight procedure is otherwise fixed: “the only change was the reduced feature space in which the 10 intermediate random clusters were constructed” [PAGE 7].

Singularity and breakdown regimes: if \(C_0\) is singular or ill-conditioned, the generalized eigenproblem \(C_{\Delta t}v_i=\lambda_i C_0v_i\) is undefined or numerically unstable, and the differentiable Cholesky/eigh path can fail. If the lag time is too short, \(C_{\Delta t}\) is dominated by noise; if too long, the finite trajectory loses transition statistics. If k-means produces empty or degenerate clusters, the MSM transition matrix is not row-stochastic or has zero rows. If the reciprocal-flux denominator is zero, MFPT is undefined or infinite; if it is near zero, MFPT is numerically explosive. If trajectory weights collapse onto a few segments, the effective sample size is too small and the relative error is unreliable. If the coarse MSM score is optimized too aggressively on held-out splits, it can overfit the proxy rather than improve the clustering space. If SPIB label refinement does not converge within \(n_{\mathrm{refine}}\), the learned state labels are not self-consistent. If the neural representation extrapolates outside the training distribution, the latent coordinates may separate configurations for reasons unrelated to kinetic barriers.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from linear TICA and MSM/VAMP model selection through DeepTICA and SPIB to RiteWeight and haMSM. TICA identifies slow linear order parameters by time-lagged covariance structure [PAGE 12, ref. 13]; VAMP-style scores use sums of squared singular values or eigenvalues to variationally rank kinetic models [PAGE 12, refs. 15 and 17]; DeepTICA replaces linear coordinates with neural networks trained on the generalized TICA eigenproblem [PAGE 12, ref. 12]; SPIB learns a predictive bottleneck for future metastable-state identity [PAGE 12, ref. 14]; RiteWeight removes fixed-discretization error by randomized iterative reweighting [PAGE 12, ref. 9]; and haMSM provides the history-resolved source–sink reference [PAGE 11, ref. 8]. The present paper is retrocausally constrained by that lineage: its selection score inherits the variational logic of MSM model selection, but it applies that score at a deliberately coarse resolution comparable to RiteWeight random clustering. That inheritance is useful but not innocent. A VAMP2-like score assumes a time-lagged stationary process and a meaningful coarse discretization; the source–sink NESS used for MFPT is not the same object as the equilibrium-like held-out data used for scoring. The paper is careful to say the score is a proxy [PAGE 3], but the proxy’s genealogy makes it easy to overread as a direct kinetic certificate.

Multi-week traps follow from that genealogy. Trap one: treating the coarse MSM score as if it were the RiteWeight objective. The paper explicitly did not use RiteWeight error in selection [PAGE 3], so replacing the score with downstream error would destroy the independence test. Trap two: comparing DeepTICA and SPIB-VAE at unequal search effort. The 10-trial and 50-trial panels show that SPIB-VAE benefits strongly from the extra 40 TPE trials; a one-shot comparison would confuse optimization clock with representation quality. Trap three: assuming the 10 ns lag, 10 intermediate clusters, and five-dimensional latent space are universal. They are fixed design choices for controlled comparison, not estimated optima. Trap four: assuming Trp-cage generalizes to all biomolecules. The paper limits generalization explicitly [PAGE 11]. Trap five: treating “RiteWeight does not require cluster-level Markovianity” [PAGE 3] as “no Markovian or transfer-operator assumptions remain.” The method still uses transition matrices and stationarity estimation. Trap six: ignoring finite-sample variance. Three independent optimization runs are informative but not a full uncertainty quantification. Trap seven: using predefined minimal residue–residue distances and then claiming the network learned directly from raw structure. The paper notes both learned methods were “constructed from predefined minimal residue–residue distance descriptors that were subsequently transformed by neural networks” [PAGE 11]. Trap eight: assuming a high score guarantees faster RiteWeight convergence at every iteration count; the evidence is qualitative agreement, not a theorem.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind reading is that three failure modes compete inside the model-selection loop. Crisis habit regression is the reversion to the familiar linear-TICA or fixed-MSM default when the learned representation is uncertain. The paper gives that habit a fair baseline: five-dimensional linear TICA is retained as a single reference because no neural hyperparameter optimization was performed [PAGE 8]. But the habit becomes a regression if one concludes that linear TICA is sufficient because it is simpler, or that DeepTICA is universally robust because it won under a limited search. The defensible statement is narrower: for this Trp-cage setup, DeepTICA provided a comparatively robust RiteWeight representation under limited hyperparameter exploration [PAGE 8], while SPIB-VAE needed broader search [PAGE 10].

The counter-trap is over-optimizing the proxy. If an agent optimizes \(S_{\mathrm{MSM}}\) harder and harder, it may select a representation that resolves slow modes in the coarse 10-state MSM but does not separate the configurations that matter for the finite-iteration RiteWeight clustering. The authors partially disarm this trap by keeping RiteWeight error out of the Optuna objective [PAGE 3] and by testing the selected representations downstream. But the counter-trap is not fully eliminated: the score is still a proxy, the held-out splits are finite, and the downstream test is only one protein system. A second counter-trap is the reverse: using RiteWeight error to select hyperparameters would make the reported agreement circular. The recursion here is that the reviewer can ask whether the score predicted RiteWeight, and the author can answer only because the score was not tuned on RiteWeight.

The clock is the third competitor. RiteWeight iteration count \(n\), Optuna trial count, training epochs, early-stopping patience, SPIB label-refinement cycles, and the 10 ns lag all define clocks. DeepTICA’s best-so-far kinetic score saturates early, while SPIB-VAE improves gradually during TPE search [PAGE 9]. That pattern is consistent with the downstream result, but it also means the comparison is clock-dependent. At a matched 10-trial budget, DeepTICA is more robust; at a 50-trial budget, SPIB-VAE becomes competitive and sometimes reaches particularly low errors at larger RiteWeight iteration counts [PAGE 9]. A careful agent should therefore report both budget regimes and avoid claiming an intrinsic ranking independent of search clock. The recursive conclusion is that the paper’s strongest evidence is the independence of the selection criterion, not a universal ranking of representations.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto is the only cross-layer veto that is fully identifiable from the source. It can be stated as a proof obligation. First, \(C_0\) must be positive semidefinite for the covariance operator to be physically admissible; if it is not positive definite, the generalized eigenproblem is singular or ill-conditioned. Second, the coarse MSM transition matrix must be row-stochastic with nonnegative entries; otherwise apparent stationarity is an artifact. Third, RiteWeight weights must be nonnegative and normalizable; negative weights would make the stationary solution unphysical. Fourth, the reciprocal-flux MFPT is finite and positive only if
\[
D=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{i_1\notin\mathrm{folded},\,i_2\in\mathrm{folded}}w_i\gt 0.
\]
Because \(\tau=10\ \mathrm{ns}\gt 0\) and \(w_i\ge 0\), a negative \(D\) is impossible; \(D=0\) gives an undefined or infinite MFPT, and \(D\) near zero gives numerical explosion. Thus a candidate representation that yields zero folded flux, non-stochastic transitions, or non-positive covariance is vetoed before any comparison of relative error. A high \(S_{\mathrm{MSM}}\) cannot override that physical veto.

The biomechanical and weather layers are not present in the source. There are no biomechanical loads, joint angles, gait variables, or tissue properties, and no meteorological forcing, pressure, temperature, or wind fields. Importing such a veto would require an external measurement model and external data; without them, the veto is unidentifiable and must be rejected as out-of-domain. The Trp-cage trajectory is atomistic molecular dynamics with a thermostat and solvent, not a weather system, and it has no biomechanical coordinate system. Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: physical and statistical constraints outrank the proxy score; biomechanical and weather vetoes are inapplicable and cannot be used to rescue or reject a representation. The paper’s own boundary is the source–sink NESS and the 10 ns transition lag [PAGE 7]; any cross-layer claim beyond that boundary is unsupported.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: time-pair shuffle veto. Retrain DeepTICA and SPIB-VAE with the same architectures, splits, and 10 ns lag, but randomly permute the time-lagged partner assignments so that \(C_{\Delta t}\) no longer represents true \(\Delta t\) dynamics. Prediction: the leading \(\lambda_i^2\) values collapse toward noise, \(S_{\mathrm{MSM}}\) falls, and RiteWeight folding-MFPT relative error remains high across iteration counts. If the shuffled models match the reported DeepTICA or SPIB-VAE performance, the claim that the learned representation captures slow transfer-operator kinetics is vetoed.

Scenario 2: untrained or random-feature veto. Replace the trained network with an untrained five-dimensional random projection or an untrained network of the same width, then run the identical 10-state coarse MSM score and RiteWeight procedure. Prediction: the random-feature representation has low \(S_{\mathrm{MSM}}\) and high RiteWeight error, while trained DeepTICA retains its advantage. If the random-feature baseline matches DeepTICA, then the reported improvement is attributable to dimensionality or clustering geometry rather than learned dynamical organization, and the causal interpretation is vetoed.

Scenario 3: selection-leakage veto. Repeat hyperparameter selection using RiteWeight relative error itself as the Optuna objective, then evaluate on held-out trajectory segments or a different lag time. Prediction: in-sample RiteWeight error improves, but the claimed independence of the coarse MSM score is lost, and cross-validation performance degrades relative to score-selected models. If the leakage-selected models do not generalize better, the paper’s practical criterion is supported; if they do generalize better, the claim that the coarse MSM score is the efficient selector is vetoed and must be replaced by a leakage-aware selection protocol.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
