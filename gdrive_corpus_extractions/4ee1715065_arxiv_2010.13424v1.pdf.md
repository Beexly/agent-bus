**Physical veto — camera ego-motion (proved).** From the derived envelope $\lvert d\rvert_{max}=2.94w$: at $w=40$ px (mid-field) the gate tolerates 118 px/frame = 3,540 px/s; at $w=12$ px (far-field) it tolerates 35 px/frame = 1,058 px/s. The paper’s geometry term is thus a pan detector, and its benefit is physically bounded by scene motion, not by feature quality.

**Physical veto — perspective and scale singularity.** $D_{bbox}\propto1/p_{track}$ diverges as boxes shrink. Veto: the thresholds cannot be globally optimal on a perspective scene.

**Physical veto — weather and photometry.** Rain streaks, fog scatter, and night-time low SNR raise the within-identity cosine distance. If the appearance separation margin satisfies $\Delta_{cos}\equiv\mathbb{E}[D_{cos}^{cross}]-\mathbb{E}[D_{cos}^{same}]<2\sigma_{noise}$, the gate is information-theoretically unable to separate identities and geometry must carry the assignment. The paper reports no weather stratification of MOT17, so this veto is untested rather than refuted.

**Biomechanical veto — gait and aspect deformation.** Training patches are square: “In general, when learning a pedestrian image, an input of 1: 2 or 1: 3 ratio is used, but the data we learn is not pedestrian-speciﬁc, and since it has various sizes and ratios, a 1:1 ratio is used” [PAGE 3]. Inference patches are pedestrian boxes at 1:2–1:3. Resampling a 1:3 box to 224×224 imposes a 3:1 anisotropic distortion relative to training geometry; the feature is evaluated off-manifold. Compounding this, a walking gait changes limb configuration at ~1 Hz — roughly every 30 frames — so silhouette shape is non-stationary on exactly the timescale of the EMA half-life (1.36 updates).

**Biomechanical veto — occlusion and crowd density.** Occlusion invalidates appearance within ~8–10 updates at $\beta=0.4$ (weight $0.6^{n}$), yet the paper’s own motivation for feature-based tracking is that it “is also robust in occlusion” [PAGE 2]. The EMA implementation undercuts the stated motivation: the memory is too short to bridge a multi-second occlusion.

**Weather/biomechanics interaction.** Under low light, motion blur lengthens the effective exposure; at 30 fps a 1/60 s shutter and a 1.4 m/s gait produce ~2.3 cm of smear per frame, which inflates $\lVert\Delta b\rVert$ (raising $D_{bbox}$) *and* blurs appearance (raising $D_{cos}$) simultaneously — a correlated double veto that no single-threshold design can absorb.
