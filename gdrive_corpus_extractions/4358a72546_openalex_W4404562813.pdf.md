<THINK_TRACE>
The source problem can be decomposed as a tri-modal inference problem: RGB appearance, depth geometry, and temporal motion. The paper states that DCTNet+ is “a three-stream network tailored for RGB-D VSOD, with an emphasis on RGB modality and treats depth and optical flow as auxiliary modalities” [PAGE 1]. This is the first gray-zone decision: modality hierarchy. The architecture assumes RGB is the most stable and semantically grounded stream, while depth and optical flow provide complementary evidence. That assumption is not physically universal. It is a design choice supported by ablation: when depth is removed, performance drops, and when depth or optical flow is promoted to primary modality, performance also drops [PAGE 12][PAGE 13]. The multi-hop reasoning is therefore: (1) RGB supplies appearance priors learned from large image corpora; (2) depth supplies geometric separation but can be noisy, missing, or domain-shifted; (3) optical flow supplies short-term motion but can fail under blur, occlusion, or fast displacement; (4) fusion must be conservative because an unreliable auxiliary stream can contaminate the primary stream.

The dataset layer adds a second gray zone: annotation. The RDVS dataset is not labeled by simple clicking or bounding boxes. The paper says the annotation process is “guided rigorously by gaze data captured from a professional eye-tracker” [PAGE 1]. The tracker “operates at a sampling rate of 90Hz” [PAGE 4], and participants viewed stimuli with heads maintained “at a distance of approximately 70 cm from the screen” [PAGE 4]. These details matter because they define the temporal and spatial resolution of the biological attention signal. Gaze points are sparse; the authors then apply a Gaussian filter to obtain continuous saliency maps [PAGE 4]. This introduces a smoothing operator whose bandwidth controls label sharpness. If the Gaussian is too narrow, masks become noisy; if too wide, small objects vanish. The paper also states that “only the RGB frames are displayed to participants as stimuli” [PAGE 4], which prevents depth from directly driving human attention during annotation. That is a principled separation between perceptual saliency and geometric saliency, but it creates a domain gap if downstream RGB-D models over-trust depth.

The model derivation proceeds from feature extraction to attention, refinement, fusion, and supervision. Each encoder stream is ResNet-34 based, with ASPP attached, and features are compressed to 64 channels [PAGE 7]. The multi-modal attention module (MAM) is restricted to higher-level features because the paper explicitly notes that “MAM only works on the higher three hierarchies” [PAGE 7]. This is a computational veto: full non-local attention at early high-resolution layers would create large affinity matrices and high memory cost. The refinement fusion module (RFM) then uses coordinate attention, spatial attention, and the universal interaction module (UIM). The HMAPs purify low-level features before RFM using a coarse map. This is another gray zone: low-level features contain fine boundaries but also noise. Refining them with a coarse high-level prediction can improve boundary precision, but if the coarse map is wrong, the error is broadcast into early layers. The paper’s ablation supports this risk: refining all five layers or moving HMAPs after RFM degrades performance [PAGE 13].

The deepest gray-zone audit concerns realistic versus synthetic depth. The paper introduces RDVS because realistic depth is needed, and it explicitly compares realistic and synthetic depth. It finds that “all models exhibit better accuracy when realistic depth is used” under matched fine-tuning [PAGE 12]. This suggests that synthetic depth, generated from RGB by DPT, lacks genuine complementary geometry. However, the paper also notes domain discrepancy: RGB-D SOD models may fail on RDVS without fine-tuning because their depth sources differ [PAGE 11]. Thus the correct inference is not “depth always helps,” but “calibrated, domain-aligned depth helps when it supplies information not already inferable from RGB and motion.” The final prediction uses the first decoder output, since the paper says “During inference, we take S1 as the final saliency prediction” [PAGE 9]. This choice assumes the deepest decoder layer provides the most reliable boundary map after progressive refinement.

X_i^r,\;X_i^d,\;X_i^f \in \mathbb{R}^{H_i \times W_i \times C_i}, \quad i=1,\dots,5,
\]
where \(H_i\) and \(W_i\) are spatial dimensions in pixels, and \(C_i\) is a dimensionless channel count. The compression module aligns channels to 64 for computational simplicity [PAGE 7]. Units: activations are dimensionless network tensors; spatial units are pixels at the corresponding feature-map resolution.

The multi-modal attention module first forms multi-modal features:
\[
X_i^h = \mathrm{BConv}([X_i^r, X_i^m]), \qquad m \in \{d,f\},
\]
where \([\cdot,\cdot]\) is channel concatenation and \(\mathrm{BConv}\) denotes convolution, BatchNorm, and ReLU [PAGE 7]. Embedded query and key tensors \(W_\theta\) and \(W_\phi\) produce an affinity matrix:
\[
W_a = \mathrm{softmax}(W_\theta W_\phi^{T}), \qquad W_a \in \mathbb{R}^{HW \times HW}.
\]
Units and probability interpretation: each row of \(W_a\) is a categorical distribution over \(HW\) spatial positions; entries are dimensionless probabilities satisfying \(\sum_j (W_a)_{ij}=1\). Assumption: query-key logits are finite and sufficiently calibrated. Breakdown regime: if logits grow without bound, softmax overflow/underflow occurs; if all logits collapse to a constant, attention becomes uniform and non-discriminative. Computational breakdown: memory and compute scale as \(\mathcal{O}((HW)^2)\), which is why the paper restricts MAM to higher, lower-resolution levels [PAGE 7].

The attended embeddings are transmitted by:
\[
\hat{X}_i^{rm} \leftarrow W_a W_g^r, \qquad
\hat{X}_i^{m} \leftarrow W_a W_g^m,
\]
followed by residual enhancement for auxiliary modalities and concatenation-based aggregation for RGB [PAGE 7]. Units: embeddings remain dimensionless; \(W_g\) are learned linear projections.

The universal interaction module is defined by four tensor interactions:
\[
\mathrm{UIM}(A,B)=\mathrm{BConv}\big([V_{\mathrm{cat}},V_{\mathrm{mul}},V_{\mathrm{max}},V_{\mathrm{sub}}]\big),
\]
where concatenation, multiplication, maximum, and subtraction are applied to \(A\) and \(B\) [PAGE 8]. Units: all intermediate tensors are dimensionless; concatenation temporarily increases channel width to \(4C\) before BConv restores \(C\). Breakdown regimes: subtraction can cancel useful signal; multiplication can suppress a tensor if the other is near zero; maximum is nondifferentiable at ties; channel concatenation can inflate parameters if not controlled.

For RFM, aligned features \(T_i^p\), \(p \in \{r,d,f\}\), receive coordinate attention:
\[
\mathrm{Att}_i^p = \mathrm{CoA}(T_i^p), \qquad
\hat{T}_i^p = \mathrm{Att}_i^p \odot T_i^p,
\]
where \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication [PAGE 8]. If coordinate attention outputs are normalized weights, they are dimensionless gains in \([0,1]\). Breakdown: if attention collapses to near-zero, features are erased; if it saturates to one, refinement becomes a hard gate.

Cross-modal coordinate attention is fused by UIM:
\[
\mathrm{Att}_i^{trm} = \mathrm{UIM}(\mathrm{Att}_i^r, \mathrm{Att}_i^m), \qquad m \in \{f,d\}.
\]
The RFM update equations are:
\[
\hat{C}_i^m = \mathrm{Att}_i^{trm} \odot T_i^m + \hat{T}_i^m + T_i^m,
\]
\[
C_i^{rm} = \mathrm{Att}_i^{trm} \odot T_i^r + \hat{T}_i^r,
\]
\[
\hat{C}_i^r = \mathrm{BConv}([C_i^{rf}, C_i^{rd}]) + T_i^r,
\]
where addition is element-wise [PAGE 8]. Assumption: residual connections preserve original signal and stabilize training. Breakdown: if auxiliary attention is erroneous, residual paths can still preserve noise.

Progressive fusion is:
\[
F_i = \mathrm{UIM}\big(Z_i^r, \mathrm{UIM}(Z_i^f, Z_i^d)\big),
\]
meaning auxiliary depth and flow are first interacted, then fused with RGB [PAGE 8]. Units: dimensionless fused feature tensor. Breakdown: if auxiliary modalities are highly noisy, the nested UIM can propagate auxiliary noise into the primary RGB path before RGB can veto it.

HMAPs generate a coarse map:
\[
CM = \mathrm{UIM}\big(L^r, \mathrm{UIM}(L^f, L^d)\big),
\]
and refine low-level features:
\[
f_i^p = f_i^p \odot CM + f_i^p,
\]
where \(f_i^p \in \{X_1^p, X_2^p, Y_3^p\}\) and \(\odot\) is broadcast multiplication [PAGE 9]. For stable gating, \(CM\) should behave like a saliency probability map in \([0,1]\). Breakdown: if \(CM\) exceeds one or becomes negative, early features can explode or be suppressed. The paper’s ablation supports restricting HMAP refinement to the first three layers [PAGE 13].

The supervision objective is:
\[
\ell = \sum_{i=1}^{5} 2^{-(i-1)} \ell(S_i,G) + \ell(CM,G),
\]
with
\[
\ell(S_i,G)=\ell_{\mathrm{BCE}}(S_i,G)+\ell_{\mathrm{IoU}}(S_i,G),
\]
where \(S_i\) is a predicted saliency map, \(G\) is ground truth, and \(CM\) is the coarse map [PAGE 9]. Units: BCE is dimensionless and measured in nats if natural logarithm is used; IoU is a dimensionless ratio in \([0,1]\). Breakdown: BCE diverges if predicted probabilities approach 0 for positive pixels or 1 for negative pixels; IoU is undefined if both prediction and ground truth have zero foreground area, requiring a convention or epsilon.

Dataset operators include eye tracking at 90 Hz, viewing distance approximately 0.70 m, and display resolution \(1920 \times 1080\) [PAGE 4]. Depth null values arise because “some depth maps of SBM-RGBD, acquired via Kinect structured light sensors, contain null values due to interference” [PAGE 4], requiring completion. Units: depth values are sensor-dependent length measurements, commonly meters or millimeters, but the paper treats them as aligned depth maps rather than a single canonical unit. Breakdown: missing depth, specular surfaces, sunlight, or reflective objects can invalidate depth completion.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage moves from RGB saliency to RGB-D saliency, then to video saliency, and finally to RGB-D video saliency. The paper frames RGB-D VSOD as an extension of both RGB-D SOD and VSOD [PAGE 1]. This genealogy matters because each parent field carries its own methodological traps. RGB-D SOD often assumes depth is a reliable geometric complement, but depth quality varies by sensor and scene. VSOD often assumes motion or optical flow is sufficient, but motion cues fail under occlusion, blur, or background clutter. The paper explicitly observes that RGB-D SOD and VSOD have traditionally been studied in isolation [PAGE 1].

Within RGB-D SOD, the paper reviews early fusion, late fusion, and middle fusion [PAGE 2]. Early fusion treats depth as an extra channel; late fusion predicts separate saliency maps and merges them; middle fusion performs staged cross-modal interaction. The paper argues that late fusion is insufficient because cross-modal interaction occurs too late to exploit complementarity [PAGE 2]. This is a recurring trap: models that only fuse predictions, rather than evidence, underuse modality-specific structure.

Within VSOD, the lineage includes recurrent networks, optical flow, ConvLSTM, transformers, and dynamic filters [PAGE 3]. The paper notes that optical flow is a common temporal cue and that DCTNet+ renders optical flow using RAFT [PAGE 7]. The historical trap here is to treat flow as a universal motion oracle. Flow is useful, but it is an estimator with failure modes, especially when frame-to-frame displacement exceeds reliable matching range or when blur destroys local texture.

The paper also inherits a methodological lineage from attention mechanisms: non-local networks, squeeze-and-excitation, coordinate attention, CBAM, and U-Net decoders [PAGE 7][PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. The DCTNet+ design does not simply concatenate these modules; it assigns them roles. MAM models cross-modal long-range dependencies; RFM refines and fuses; HMAPs purify low-level features. The retro-causal lesson is that module placement matters. Attention at high levels is affordable but semantically coarse; refinement at low levels is precise but vulnerable to noise.

A multi-week trap is benchmark overfitting. The paper compares against many VSOD and RGB-D SOD models [PAGE 10][PAGE 11]. If a researcher trains only on pseudo RGB-D datasets with synthetic depth, they may overestimate the usefulness of depth. The paper’s realistic-depth experiments expose this trap: synthetic depth can be generated from RGB and may encode the same appearance priors, reducing its complementary value [PAGE 12]. Another trap is annotation shortcutting. If labels come from naive clicking rather than gaze-guided observation, models may learn object-name priors rather than biological saliency. The paper’s use of eye tracking is intended to avoid that trap [PAGE 1][PAGE 4].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three agents: the human annotator, the learning model, and the evaluator. The annotator infers what a free-viewing participant would fixate on. The model infers which pixels are salient from RGB, depth, and flow. The evaluator infers whether the model’s predictions match socially or biologically grounded saliency. Each layer can develop a habit under crisis conditions.

Crisis habit regression occurs when input quality degrades. Under low contrast, occlusion, fast motion, or background clutter, a model may regress to center bias, because many training datasets contain centrally framed objects. The paper reports that RDVS exhibits center bias similar to other datasets [PAGE 6], but it also contains attention-shift cases [PAGE 4]. If a model overfits center bias, it will fail on saliency shift. Under depth noise, a model may regress to RGB-only behavior even when depth is available, because depth has become unreliable. Under flow noise, a model may regress to appearance-only behavior, ignoring motion. These are crisis regressions: the model retreats to the most stable modality.

Counter-traps are mechanisms that prevent regression. The eye-tracker annotation is a counter-trap against naive object-name labeling. It forces labels to follow biological fixation. The use of realistic depth is a counter-trap against synthetic-depth shortcutting. The paper shows that realistic depth improves performance under matched fine-tuning [PAGE 12]. MAM is a counter-trap against purely local feature enhancement, because it models cross-modal long-range dependencies. RFM with residual connections is a counter-trap against fusion-induced contamination. HMAPs are a counter-trap against noisy low-level features, but only if the coarse map is reliable.

The clock layer includes several temporal scales. The eye tracker samples at 90 Hz [PAGE 4], which defines the biological attention clock. Video frames define the visual stimulus clock. Optical flow estimates frame-to-frame motion, measured in pixels per frame. The RDVS dataset includes fast-motion cases defined by average per-frame centroid motion larger than 20 pixels [PAGE 5]. Training uses 30 epochs and input tensors resized to \(448 \times 448\) [PAGE 9]. Inference speed is reported as 0.0844 seconds for the overall model, with MAM at 0.0081 seconds and RFM at 0.0325 seconds [PAGE 13]. These clocks interact: a model trained on slow motion may fail when fast motion exceeds flow reliability; a model trained on high frame-rate gaze labels may overfit to fixation density rather than object boundaries.

The recursive loop is therefore: annotators encode human attention under uncertainty; models encode learned saliency under modality uncertainty; evaluators test whether the model’s implied theory of attention survives distribution shift. The safest policy is not to assume any modality is always reliable, but to estimate reliability and let RGB act as the primary anchor while depth and flow provide gated evidence.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather-related veto conditions can be adjudicated using information-theoretic and sensing constraints. Let \(Y\) be the saliency label, \(X^r\) RGB evidence, \(X^f\) flow evidence, and \(X^d\) depth evidence. A modality should be downweighted or vetoed if its conditional mutual information is non-positive:
\[
I(Y; X^m \mid X^{\text{other}}) \le 0,
\]
where \(m\) is the candidate auxiliary modality and \(X^{\text{other}}\) includes the remaining modalities. Units: bits if logarithm base 2 is used, or nats if natural logarithm is used. This is a formal veto: if an auxiliary modality provides no additional information about the label after conditioning on the other modalities, it should not be allowed to contaminate fusion.

Depth veto regime. Structured-light depth sensors can produce missing values. The paper states that “some depth maps of SBM-RGBD, acquired via Kinect structured light sensors, contain null values due to interference” [PAGE 4]. If missingness is high or completion is biased, then \(X^d\) becomes an unreliable estimator. In outdoor sunlight, infrared interference, reflective surfaces, or transparent objects can further invalidate depth. Under these conditions, the depth stream should be attenuated. The paper’s synthetic-depth experiment supports this indirectly: when depth lacks genuine complementary geometry, performance can degrade [PAGE 12]. Therefore, a physical veto is justified when depth noise exceeds the information gain threshold.

Flow veto regime. Optical flow fails when displacement is too large, when blur removes texture, or when occlusion breaks correspondence. The RDVS attribute table defines fast motion as average per-frame centroid motion larger than 20 pixels [PAGE 5], and motion blur as fuzzy boundaries due to fast motion [PAGE 5]. If frame-to-frame displacement exceeds the reliable matching range of RAFT or if blur destroys local gradients, then \(I(Y;X^f \mid X^r,X^d)\) can approach zero. In that regime, flow should be gated down. The paper’s observation that VSOD models rely on motion information [PAGE 11] does not contradict this veto; it only shows that motion is valuable when reliable.

Biomechanical veto regime. Gaze annotation depends on stable head pose, calibration, and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The paper reports that “a headrest is employed to maintain participants’ heads at a distance of approximately 70 cm from the screen” [PAGE 4], and that participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision [PAGE 4]. If head movement exceeds calibration tolerance, or if the eye tracker loses corneal reflection, gaze points become invalid. The 90 Hz sampling rate also imposes a temporal resolution: very short saccades may be undersampled relative to their perceptual importance. In such cases, the fixation-derived saliency map should be treated as uncertain, especially for small or fast-moving objects.

Weather veto regime. The RDVS dataset includes outdoor scenes [PAGE 5], and real-world deployment may include rain, fog, dust, or glare. These conditions degrade RGB contrast and can invalidate depth sensors, especially structured light or time-of-flight systems. If weather reduces RGB contrast below a usable threshold and depth returns become sparse, both appearance and geometry suffer. The paper’s inclusion of low contrast, occlusion, and complex backgrounds [PAGE 2] supports the need for veto logic. In such regimes, a robust system should fall back to temporal consistency or conservative boundary refinement rather than trusting a corrupted auxiliary map.

The cross-layer proof is therefore conditional, not absolute. Depth, flow, and RGB each have physical breakdown regimes. The architecture’s primary-RGB, auxiliary-depth/flow policy is rational only when the auxiliary modalities pass reliability tests. If they fail, the model must behave as if their conditional information gain is non-positive.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: synthetic depth can replace realistic depth. Counterfactual claim: a model trained and tested with synthetic depth on RDVS should match or exceed realistic depth because depth is only an auxiliary cue. Verifiable veto: under matched fine-tuning, realistic depth outperforms synthetic depth. The paper reports that “all models exhibit better accuracy when realistic depth is used” [PAGE 12]. For DCTNet+, realistic depth yields \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.814\), \(S_\alpha=0.869\), and \(M=0.034\), while synthetic depth yields \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.796\), \(S_\alpha=0.857\), and \(M=0.039\) [PAGE 12]. Prediction: any claim that synthetic depth is sufficient should be vetoed on RDVS fine-tuning unless the performance gap is closed by explicit depth denoising or domain adaptation.

Scenario 2: depth can be removed or demoted without meaningful loss. Counterfactual claim: because RGB is primary, depth is optional and removing it should not materially hurt RGB-D VSOD. Verifiable veto: the ablation without depth, denoted A1, drops RDVS performance to \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.765\) and \(S_\alpha=0.843\), compared with the full model’s \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.814\) and \(S_\alpha=0.869\) [PAGE 13]. The paper also states that incorporating depth boosts performance by notable margins, with the highest metric margins observed on RDVS [PAGE 12]. Prediction: removing depth or treating depth as equal to RGB will underperform on realistic RGB-D video, especially in scenes where geometry separates objects from cluttered backgrounds.

Scenario 3: HMAP refinement can be moved later or applied to all layers without penalty. Counterfactual claim: coarse-map refinement is a generic enhancement, so applying it after RFM or to all five layers should improve or preserve performance. Verifiable veto: variant F2 moves HMAPs from before RFM to after RFM and obtains RDVS \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.792\), \(S_\alpha=0.858\), while variant F3 applies HMAPs to all five layers and obtains \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.804\), \(S_\alpha=0.864\); the full model obtains \(F_\beta^{\max}=0.814\), \(S_\alpha=0.869\) [PAGE 13]. Prediction: coarse-map refinement placed after RFM or applied to all layers should degrade fine-grained saliency, because deeper features are less redundant to refine and late refinement cannot purify the features that RFM consumes.

These vetoes are verifiable because they rely on explicit ablation tables and dataset comparisons [PAGE 12][PAGE 13]. They also establish a general rule: in RGB-D VSOD, auxiliary modalities must earn their place through measurable conditional gain, and architectural placement must respect the noise structure of each layer.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
