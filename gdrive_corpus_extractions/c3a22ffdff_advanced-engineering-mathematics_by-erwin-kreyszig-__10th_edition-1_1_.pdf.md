It doesn't. The classic counterexample $y'=\sqrt{|y|}$, $y(0)=0$ has *both* $y\equiv 0$ and $y=\tfrac14 x|x|$ — existence without uniqueness.

**Hop 2 — Uniqueness (Ch. 1.7, Thm 2, p. 66).** Uniqueness needs the *extra* hypothesis that $f_y=\partial f/\partial y$ is continuous (hence bounded, $|f_y|\le M$) on $R$. This is a Lipschitz condition; it is what makes the contraction in Picard's iteration work. Note the logical shape: continuity buys existence, the *derivative* buys uniqueness, and Theorem 2 closes with "by Theorem 1 … precisely one solution." The gray zone is treating $f_y$ continuous as *necessary*; it is sufficient only. $y'=\sqrt{|y|}$ violates it at the origin, which is exactly why uniqueness dies there.

**Hop 3 — Basis and the Wronskian (Ch. 2.1 p. 76; Ch. 2.6 Thm 2 p. 101).** For linear $y''+py'+qy=0$ with $p,q$ continuous on $I$, existence of a *general* solution reduces to producing two **linearly independent** solutions $y_1,y_2$. The independence test is the Wronskian $W(y_1,y_2)=y_1y_2'-y_2y_1'$. Theorem 2 (p. 101) states the subtle equivalence: for solutions of the *same* linear ODE, $W=0$ **at one point** of $I$ forces $W\equiv 0$ on $I$ (hence dependence); contrapositively, $W\ne 0$ at a single point certifies independence everywhere. **Gray zone / most common trap:** the Wronskian vanishing at a point does *not* imply dependence for *arbitrary* functions — only for solutions of a common linear ODE with continuous coefficients. $x^2$ and $x|x|$ have $W\equiv 0$ yet are independent; they are not both solutions of one such ODE on a symmetric interval.

**Hop 4 — Reduction to systems and the eigenvalue picture (Ch. 4, p. 161, 166).** Theorem 1 (p. 161) converts any $n$th-order ODE to a first-order system $\mathbf{y}'=A\mathbf{y}$ by $y_1=y,\dots,y_n=y^{(n-1)}$. For constant $A$, solutions are $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}e^{\lambda t}$ with $(\lambda,x)$ an eigenpair (p. 166). Now the three "cases" of Ch. 2.4 (p. 91) are just the discriminant of $\lambda^2+\frac{c}{m}\lambda+\frac{k}{m}=0$: overdamped $c^2>4mk$ (two negative real $\lambda$), critically damped $c^2=4mk$ (double $\lambda$, needing the reduction-of-order companion $te^{\lambda t}$), underdamped $c^2<4mk$ (complex $\lambda=-\alpha\pm i\omega^*$). **Hop closes:** stability $\iff \operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ for every eigenvalue $\iff$ (for $2\times2$) trace $p<0$ and $\det q>0$ (Table 4.2, p. 176). The gray zone is the *degenerate* case where $A$ is not symmetric and lacks a full eigenbasis (Example 6, p. 171): $\lambda=3$ is a double root but only one eigenvector exists, so $e^{3t}$ must be supplemented by the generalized eigenvector solution.

The synthesis: **existence, uniqueness, and stability are three different theorems with three different hypotheses**, and every engineering error I can find in this material is a hypothesis silently dropped between hops.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
All equations below are standard results located at the cited pages; units are SI unless the source is explicitly imperial (Kreyszig's building/RL examples use °F, V, Ω, H).

**1. Newton cooling, worked (p. 41, Eq. 6).** $\dfrac{dT}{dt}=k(T-T_A)$. With $T_A$ frozen at its mean $45^\circ\text{F}$: separation gives $T(t)=T_A+ce^{kt}$. Data $T(0)=70\Rightarrow c=25$; $T(4)=65\Rightarrow e^{4k}=0.8\Rightarrow k=\tfrac14\ln 0.8=-0.0562\ \text{min}^{-1}$ (I retain the book's per-hour reading of the clock; the number is unit-agnostic). Prediction: $T(8)=45+25(0.8)^2=45+16=\mathbf{61^\circ F}$, matching Fig. 12. **Breakdown regime:** the model *assumes* $T_A$ constant; Kreyszig states $T_A$ actually fell $50\to40^\circ$F, so the single-exponential is a controlled idealization (p. 41: "even if a model seems to fit the reality only poorly … it may still give valuable qualitative information").

**2. RL circuit (p. 56).** $L\,I'+RI=E$ gives $I(t)=\dfrac{E}{R}+ce^{-(R/L)t}$. With $E=48\,\text{V}$, $R=11\,\Omega$, $L=0.1\,\text{H}$: $R/L=110\,\text{s}^{-1}$, $\tau=L/R\approx 9.1\times10^{-3}\,\text{s}$, steady state $E/R=48/11\approx 4.36\,\text{A}$, and $I(0)=0\Rightarrow I(t)=\tfrac{48}{11}(1-e^{-110t})$. **Breakdown/stiffness preview:** the rate $110\,\text{s}^{-1}$ is the exact quantity that later makes explicit Euler unstable unless $h<2/110$ (see §6).

**3. Resonance and its disappearance (p. 116).** For $my''+cy'+ky=F_0\cos\omega t$, steady state $y_p=C^*(\omega)\cos(\omega t-\eta)$ with
$$C^*(\omega)=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2}},\qquad \tan\eta=\frac{c\omega}{k-m\omega^2}.$$
Extremizing the radicand: $\frac{d}{d\omega}\big[(k-m\omega^2)^2+c^2\omega^2\big]=2\omega\big[2m^2\omega^2-2mk+c^2\big]=0$ gives
$$\boxed{\ \omega_{\max}^2=\frac{k}{m}-\frac{c^2}{2m^2}=\omega_0^2-\frac{c^2}{2m^2}\ }.$$
**Exact singularity regime:** a real interior maximum (practical resonance) exists **iff $c^2<2mk$**; for $c^2\ge 2mk$, $C^*(\omega)$ decreases monotonically (no resonant peak). As $c\to 0^+$, $\omega_{\max}\to\omega_0$ and $C^*\to\infty$ — the undamped resonance blow-up. (The OCR renders this "$2m^2(\omega_0^2+\omega^2)$" and "$\omega_0^2+c^2/2m^2$"; the sign is a scan artifact — the physical statement on the same page, "real solution iff $c^2<2mk$" and "$\omega_{\max}\to\omega_0$ as $c\to 0$," fixes the correct minus sign.)

**4. Laplace existence (p. 236, Thm 3).** If $f$ is piecewise continuous on every finite interval and of **exponential order**, $|f(t)|\le Me^{kt}$, then $F(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$ exists for $s>k$, with the bound $|F(s)|\le \dfrac{M}{s-k}$. **Exact nonexistence regime:** $e^{t^2}$ satisfies no such bound (it beats every $Me^{kt}$), so $\mathcal{L}(e^{t^2})$ does not exist — the abscissa of convergence is pushed to $+\infty$.

**5. Fourier convergence (p. 506, Thm 2).** For $f$ of period $2\pi$, piecewise continuous with one-sided derivatives, the series converges to $f(x)$ at continuity points and to $\tfrac12\big[f(x_0^+)+f(x_0^-)\big]$ at a jump. **Breakdown regime — Gibbs (p. 541):** at a jump the partial sums overshoot by a fixed fraction $\approx 8.95\%$ of the jump height that does **not** vanish as the number of terms grows; refinement only relocates the ripple toward the discontinuity. This is a genuine non-uniform-convergence singularity, not a numerical artifact.

**6. Bessel/gamma machinery (pp. 216–221).** $\Gamma(\alpha+1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}t^\alpha\,dt$, $\Gamma(\alpha+1)=\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)$, $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$; $J_\alpha(x)=\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,\Gamma(\alpha+m+1)}\big(\tfrac{x}{2}\big)^{\alpha+2m}$. Large-zero asymptotic (Eq. 14, p. 216): $j_{\nu,n}\approx\big(n+\tfrac{\nu}{2}-\tfrac14\big)\pi$. Check for $\nu=0$: $(n-\tfrac14)\pi$ gives $2.356,\ 5.498,\ 8.639$ vs. exact $2.405,\ 5.520,\ 8.654$ — the source's own numbers, confirming the formula.

**7. Heat equation from the divergence theorem (pp. 486–491).** Energy balance over $T$ with flux $-K\nabla U$ and $\iiint_T \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\,dV=\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dA$ yields $\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial t}=c^2\nabla^2 U$, $c^2=\dfrac{K}{\sigma\rho}\ [\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}]$. **Steady-state collapse:** $\partial_t U=0\Rightarrow \nabla^2U=0$ (Laplace) — the same equation met in §9.7 and §18.

**8. Condition number (pp. 891–896).** $\kappa(A)=\|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|\ge 1$, with relative-error propagation
$$\frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|}\le \kappa(A)\frac{\|\delta A\|}{\|A\|},\qquad \frac{\|\delta x\|}{\|x\|}\le \kappa(A)\frac{\|\delta b\|}{\|b\|}.$$
**Breakdown regime:** the worked system with $\kappa(A)=100$ (p. 896) shows a $30\%$ input perturbation bounded by $\kappa\cdot 30\%\approx 5.14$ in relative error — the bound is deliberately loose (actual $\approx$ tens of percent), which is *typical*, not a defect.

**9. Gerschgorin (p. 906, Thm 1).** Every eigenvalue of $A=[a_{jk}]$ lies in $\bigcup_j D_j$, $D_j:\ |\lambda-a_{jj}|\le\sum_{k\ne j}|a_{jk}|$. For the sample matrix the disks $\{|z|\le1\},\ \{|z-5|\le1.5\},\ \{|z-1|\le1.5\}$ split into $\{D_1\}$ and $D_2\cup D_3$; because two disjoint components each carry one/two eigenvalues, the eigenvalues are pinned to $\{-0.209,\ 0.904,\ 5.305\}$ — matching the text.

The text is a genealogy of named operators, and its own footnotes are the citation trail. **Calculus** (Newton, p. 41; Leibniz) → **exponential/Malthus growth** (Malthus 1766–1834, p. 31) → **d'Alembert's wave solution** (Ch. 12.4) → **Fourier series** (Ch. 11) → **Sturm–Liouville orthogonality** (Ch. 11.5) → **Bessel** (arising from the drumhead/Kepler problems, Ch. 5.4–5.5) → **Frobenius** (regular-singular extension, p. 206) → **Cauchy–Riemann–Weierstrass** (complex-analysis foundations, footnote p. 625) → **Gauss/Green/Stokes** integral theorems (Ch. 10) → **Lagrange** variation of parameters (p. 126) → **Runge–Kutta–Heun–Kutta** (p. 931, with the explicit 1895/1900/1901 references) → **Cholesky** (p. 881, with the Todd-at-King's-College history) → **Markov** (stochastic matrices, p. 296) → **Rayleigh, Hermite, Legendre, Jacobi, Kronecker** (special functions and notation). Reinterpreted for mathematics, "multi-week traps" are the **persistent hypothesis-errors that survive across a whole course**: (i) conflating a *sufficient* condition (continuity for existence, $f_y$ for uniqueness, $\kappa$ small for accuracy) with a *necessary* one; (ii) applying the Wronskian independence test to functions that are not common solutions of one linear ODE; (iii) forgetting that a general solution is *homogeneous + particular* (Ch. 2.7, p. 106), then being unable to satisfy initial conditions; (iv) reading an *a priori* Newton estimate (p. 831) as a guarantee rather than a heuristic that "with a bad $x_0$ the method may even diverge."

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Modeled honestly, this is not "crisis habit regression vs. clock" (no crisis, no clock in analysis); it is the **decision-metacognition the book trains the reader to run before every computation**:

1. **Which representation am I in, and what does it forbid?** Separable/exact/linear (Ch. 1) → power series/Frobenius (Ch. 5) → transform (Ch. 6) → eigenbasis (Ch. 4/8) → conformal map (Ch. 17). Kreyszig repeatedly models the *choice* ("Golden Rule: if you cannot solve your problem, try a simpler one," p. 41; "If you have a choice, use the previous method. It is simpler," p. 126). The expected internal monologue is: *check the equation's class first; the class dictates the operator.*

2. **Verify hypotheses before invoking a theorem.** The recursion the text wants is: state the theorem → check continuity / differentiability / exponential order / symmetry / connectedness → *then* conclude. The uniqueness rectangle (p. 66), the simply-connected hypothesis for path independence (Ch. 10.2, p. 446), and the symmetric-matrix eigenbasis guarantee (Thm 2, p. 366) are all "hypothesis gates."

3. **Predict the qualitative shape before computing the number.** Phase-plane classification (Ch. 4.4, Tables 4.1–4.2, p. 176) via $(p,q,\Delta)$ lets you *foresee* node vs. saddle vs. spiral vs. center from $\operatorname{tr}A$ and $\det A$ alone. The disciplined reader forms this expectation and treats a contradictory numeric answer as a signal to recheck — the mathematical form of a "counter-trap."

4. **Compare methods as mutual checks.** The Laplace solution of $y''-y=t$ (p. 241) is explicitly checked against the classical method; the two-tank eigenvector result (p. 156) is checkable against the phase-plane trace/det. The metacognitive habit is *solve twice, agree, then trust.*

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
"Biomechanical/weather veto" is **N/A for pure mathematics** — there is no physical substrate to veto. Its genuine analog is **cross-framework consistency adjudication**: when two independent mathematical routes to the same quantity disagree, at least one is wrong, and the disagreement *vetoes* the faulty one. Concretely:

- **Units/dimension veto.** Every derived equation must be dimensionally homogeneous. The heat diffusivity $c^2=K/(\sigma\rho)$ must carry $\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}$ (p. 486); the RL rate $R/L$ must carry $\text{s}^{-1}$ (p. 56); $k=\tfrac14\ln 0.8$ must be an inverse time. A result failing dimensional balance is vetoed before any further analysis.
- **Existence-before-uniqueness veto.** If a candidate "solution" is produced where Theorem 1's continuity hypothesis already fails, no uniqueness claim is admissible — the $y'=\sqrt{|y|}$ family vetoes any single-valued "the solution."
- **Transform vs. classical veto.** If $\mathcal{L}^{-1}$ of the subsidiary equation and the undetermined-coefficients solution of the same IVP disagree (Kreyszig's own cross-check, pp. 241), the algebra in one path is wrong.
- **Spectral veto.** Gerschgorin disks (p. 906) and the trace/determinant identities $\sum\lambda_j=\operatorname{tr}A$, $\prod\lambda_j=\det A$ (p. 371, Prob. 6) provide an *a priori* region and two invariants that veto any computed spectrum violating them. For the $A$ of Example 1 (p. 156), $\operatorname{tr}A=-2.8$, $\det A=1.6$ force $\lambda\in\{-2,-0.8\}$; any other pair is vetoed.
- **Convergence veto.** A term-by-term differentiated Fourier/heat series that fails the Weierstrass $M$-test (p. 726) is vetoed as a legitimate representation, even if it "looks" convergent pointwise.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three **verifiable** counterfactual checks (RLVR read as "reverse-lookup verifiable results"), each with a checkable prediction that vetoes the naive method:

1. **Stiff-ODE stability (pp. 926, 936, 946).** For $y'=-10y$ (or the system with eigenvalues $-1,-10$), explicit Euler $y_{n+1}=(1+h\lambda)y_n$ obeys $|y_{n+1}|=|1-10h|\,|y_n|$, which *diverges* for $h>0.2$ (the source tabulates Euler values $1.00,1.04,1.16,\dots,3168$ vs. exact $\to 0$). **Prediction/veto:** at $h=0.2$ the explicit method blows up while **backward Euler** $y_{n+1}=y_n/(1+10h)$ stays bounded and monotone for *every* $h>0$ (unconditional stability). If a computation shows explicit Euler stable at $h=0.2$ here, the code is wrong.

2. **Gibbs overshoot (p. 541; Fourier p. 506).** Expand a step (e.g., the $\pm1$ square wave, p. 511) in a Fourier series and sum $N$ terms. **Prediction/veto:** the maximum overshoot next to the jump tends to $\approx 1.0895$ (i.e., $\approx 9\%$ above the top value), *independent of $N$*, while the overshoot *location* migrates toward the discontinuity as $N$ grows. Any claim that "more terms remove the overshoot" is vetoed — it is a non-uniform-limit phenomenon, exactly as the text states.

3. **Ill-conditioned residual illusion (p. 891, Ex. 2).** Take the near-singular system $x_1+1.0001x_2=2.0001,\ 1.0001x_1+x_2=2.0001$ with exact $(2,0)$ vs. perturbed right side giving $(1,1)$; and the scalar analog $x^5-10^{-4}x=0$ near $x=0.1$ (p. 831). **Prediction/veto:** a small residual $\|b-A\hat x\|$ can coexist with a large error $\|x-\hat x\|$ (the source's $\hat x=(1.9999,0.0001)$ has residual $\sim10^{-4}$ yet is far off). Any inference "small residual $\Rightarrow$ accurate solution" is vetoed unless $\kappa(A)$ is controlled; the correct diagnostic is the *relative-error* bound $\kappa(A)\,\|\delta b\|/\|b\|$, not the residual.

Each veto is checkable in closed form or with a few lines of arithmetic, which is the only sense in which "prediction" is meaningful for a mathematics text.
