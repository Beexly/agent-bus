\[
B=\operatorname{conv}\{F_L,F_R\}\subset\mathbb R^2.

The stability margin is  
\[
\delta=\min_{p\in\partial B}\sqrt{(x_G-p_x)^2+(y_G-p_y)^2}\quad[\mathrm{m}].
\]
Static equilibrium requires  
\[
\sum_i N_i=mg\quad[\mathrm N],
\]
\[
\sum_i \mathbf r_i\times N_i\mathbf e_z+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{fric}}=\mathbf 0\quad[\mathrm{N\,m}].

Assumptions: quasi-static stance, planar support, foot contacts sufficient to prevent tipping.

2. Spiral pass flight under wind and drag.

\[
m_b\dot{\mathbf v}= -m_b g\mathbf e_z
-\frac12\rho C_D A\|\mathbf v-\mathbf w\|(\mathbf v-\mathbf w)
+\frac12\rho C_L A\|\mathbf v-\mathbf w\|^2\mathbf n(\boldsymbol\omega),
\]
with units \([\mathrm N]\). Here \(\rho\) is air density \([\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}]\), \(C_D\) drag coefficient, \(C_L\) lift/spin coefficient, and \(\mathbf n(\boldsymbol\omega)\) a spin-dependent direction.  
Assumptions: wind approximately uniform over flight; spin-induced lift represented by a lumped coefficient; ball not tumbling chaotically.  
Breakdown regime: if lateral wind acceleration \(a_x\) acting over flight time \(T\) produces displacement  
\[
\Delta x\approx \frac12 a_xT^2

The source notes the spiral is preferred because “The spiral pass travels faster, is more accurate in the wind” [PAGE 17]. \[
I\ddot\theta=\tau(t)\quad[\mathrm{N\,m}],
\]
\[
\omega_c=\frac{1}{I}\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\tau(t)\,dt\quad[\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}].
\]
At contact, the ball receives impulse  
\[
\mathbf J=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\mathbf F_{\text{foot}\to\text{ball}}(t)\,dt\quad[\mathrm{N\,s}],
\]
so  
\[
m_b\mathbf v_0=\mathbf J.
\]
If the foot strikes at an offset \(\mathbf e\) from the ball’s center, the induced spin error is approximated by  
\[
\Delta\boldsymbol\omega_b\approx\frac{\mathbf e\times\mathbf J}{I_b}\quad[\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}].

|x_{\text{bounce}}|\lesssim 2h\tan\theta_d\quad[\mathrm m].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The 1922 manual sits at a hinge point in American football’s evolution. Zuppke identifies the book as the substance of twenty-two lectures delivered in the University of Illinois Summer Session [PAGE 9], and the text explicitly presents “FOOTBALL TECHNIQUE AND TACTICS” as a first edition from 1922 [PAGE 7]. Its lineage is rugby-derived mass play, but it already absorbs the forward pass as a structural force. Zuppke writes that when the rules committee added the forward pass, “they built up the game with a new series of constructive fundamentals” [PAGE 114]. He also describes the “four threat” man who can run, throw, punt, or drop kick [PAGE 114]. This is an early formal recognition that offensive geometry expands when a credible pass threat forces the defense to protect deeper territory.

The book’s tactical genealogy runs through two competing line theories: quick opening and wedge. Zuppke says the older method is built on the theory that linemen must make quick openings through which the back darts, while the second method makes the line charge shoulder to shoulder as a tight or adhesive line forcing one man forward as the apex of a wedge [PAGE 71]. He treats both as legitimate: “I believe both have equal merits” [PAGE 71]. This is historically important because it records a pre-modern debate between gap-oriented blocking and mass-momentum attack. The wedge lineage later feeds into line-charging and mass plays, while the quick-opening lineage feeds into assignment blocking and eventually into more distributed modern schemes.

A second genealogy is the play-cycle concept. Zuppke warns, “Do not overload your team with plays” [PAGE 74], and proposes a group of related plays in which straights, slants, angle plays, and forward passes share initial formations and mannerisms [PAGE 74]. This anticipates modern concepts of play correlation, formation disguise, and constraint-based attacking. The text’s “angle play” is especially revealing: the backs start toward the sideline and then either dart in or step back to throw, giving the play a double threat [PAGE 74]. That is a direct ancestor of later option-like and run-pass conflict ideas.

The multi-week traps are explicit. Zuppke’s practice plan allocates early weeks to fundamentals: punting, kicking, tackling, blocking, stances, starts, passes, signals, and wind sprints [PAGE 175]. He then states an end-of-three-week benchmark: “At the end of three weeks a team should have the punt, a line smash, an end run, and three forward passes from the punt formation; a line smash, a tackle smash, a criss-cross, and two forward passes from the compact formation” [PAGE 176]. The trap is not merely insufficient practice; it is premature complexity. If coaches add shifts or elaborate pass concepts before fundamentals stabilize, the team loses decisiveness. Zuppke says complex methods develop indecision and destroy the positive mental attitude [PAGE 70]. The historical lesson is that tactical richness must be built on a stable primitive layer: stance, snap, timing, tackling, and safe ball handling.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Zuppke’s coaching problems are recursive belief problems. The offense does not merely choose a play; it chooses a play under the assumption that the defense is reading its formation, stance, eyes, and signal cadence. The defense, in turn, chooses a response under the assumption that the offense knows the defense is reading. The source makes this explicit when it says the secondary should watch the opponent’s quarterback and the telltale stances of the backs: “The opponent’s quarterback may give away his intentions by his eyes or movement of the body, while the other backs may point in the direction of the play by slightly changing their stances” [PAGE 86]. This is a second-order inference: the defense infers the offense’s intended action from subtle cues that the offense may or may not be trying to conceal.

The recursive model can be written as a belief hierarchy. Let \(b_t\) be the defense’s posterior belief over the offense’s play distribution. The defense updates  
\[
b_t(a)\propto b_{t-1}(a)\,p(o_t\mid a),
\]
where \(o_t\) is observed behavior such as stance shift, head turn, or signal timing. The offense attempts to reduce the likelihood ratio by making multiple plays share the same early cues. Zuppke’s play cycles do this: plays inherit mannerisms from one another so the defense cannot quickly classify the play from the initial movement [PAGE 74]. The shift is another recursive device: “The shift to be successful must contain the element of surprise. Its success hinges upon disorganizing the primary line of the opponents” [PAGE 144]. The shift forces the defense to update beliefs rapidly while also creating uncertainty for the offense itself, because the offense may not know the opponent’s exact alignment until motion is complete [PAGE 144].

Crisis habit regression occurs when fatigue, score pressure, or clock pressure forces players back to the most rehearsed policy rather than the optimal policy. Zuppke anticipates this by insisting on simple systems and repeated fundamentals. He says the coach should stress simplicity and avoid complex methods that cause indecision [PAGE 70]. In a late-game crisis, a fatigued back may revert to a familiar cutback lane even if the signal called for a different opening. A fatigued lineman may lunge rather than run into the opponent, violating the instruction to “run into rather than lunge into the opponent” [PAGE 43]. The counter-trap is to design plays that exploit the opponent’s predicted regression. If the defense is likely to overcommit to a favorite stunt because late-game fatigue reduces diagnostic accuracy, the offense can use a play that punishes that stunt.

Clock pressure modifies the recursive depth. Early in the game, both sides can afford deeper inference: the offense can study the defense, and the defense can study the offense’s tendencies. Late in the game, horizon shortens. Zuppke’s quarterback maxims reflect this: “If ahead, never take a chance” [PAGE 154], and “When in doubt, punt” [PAGE 153]. These are clock-conditioned policies. The recursive theory of mind therefore has a temporal cutoff: when remaining time is short, the optimal model is not the deepest possible inference chain but the most robust low-variance action. The source’s signal design supports this by making the charging number a shared temporal cue: “A charging number gives the offense an advantage in that they do not have to watch the ball” [PAGE 154]. The team synchronizes action without needing every player to infer the snap from visual ball movement.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a tactic is admissible at the strategic or psychological layer but inadmissible at the physical, biomechanical, or environmental layer. Zuppke’s text provides several necessary conditions that can be written as veto inequalities.

First, the high short pass veto. The source says short direct passes should be low to hide the ball from the secondary [PAGE 16]. If a pass is thrown with vertical rise \(h\), the minimum flight time is bounded by gravitational fall:  
\[
T_{\min}\approx\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}\quad[\mathrm s].
\]
If defensive visual-motor reaction time is \(\tau_r\), then a necessary condition for the pass to remain hidden is  
\[
T_{\min}<\tau_r,
\]
or equivalently  
\[
h<\frac12 g\tau_r^2.
\]
If \(h\) exceeds this critical height, the defense has enough time to locate the ball and the receiver. This is a physical veto against unnecessarily high short passes. It does not ban high passes in all contexts; it bans high trajectory when the tactical purpose is concealment and quick catch.

Second, the wet-ball and friction veto. Zuppke notes that a one-handed spiral passer is less accurate on a wet day when hand and ball are wet [PAGE 17], and that rainy conditions require wiping the ball [PAGE 16]. For blocking and tackling, friction is essential. If a block requires tangential force \(f_t\) and the available normal force is \(f_n\), the contact is feasible only if  
\[
f_t\le \mu f_n.
\]
Rain, mud, or wet turf can reduce \(\mu\). If a tactic requires \(f_t>\mu f_n\), the tactic is vetoed because the blocker or tackler will slip before transferring the intended impulse. This does not veto all contact play; it vetoes friction-dependent lateral blocks or sidewipes under low-friction conditions.

Third, the punt-in-wind veto. The punter is told to “kick high with the wind, low against the wind” [PAGE 24]. The source also says a wet ball will not carry as far as a dry ball [PAGE 25] and that nose-up orientation causes the ball to drop nearly straight down [PAGE 25]. A formal necessary condition is that the expected punt distance after wind and drag must exceed the minimum distance needed to avoid giving the opponent favorable field position. If  
\[
D_{\text{expected}}(\mathbf w,\text{ball state})<D_{\text{required}},
\]
then the selected punt type is vetoed. For example, a low driven kick into a strong headwind may produce insufficient carry; a high kick with the wind may be more stable but may also give the returner time. The source’s tactical instruction is therefore not stylistic; it is a conditional optimization under environmental constraints.

Fourth, the tackle-target veto. The source says the tackler should strike about four inches above the knee [PAGE 32] and that “it is just as bad to tackle too low as it is too high” [PAGE 34]. Let the effective target band be  
\[
z_t\in[z_{\text{knee}},z_{\text{knee}}+0.1016\,\mathrm m]
\]
as a coaching approximation, with the exact optimum depending on runner morphology and speed. If the intended contact point lies outside this band, the angular impulse may fail to produce the desired destabilization, or it may produce an unsafe rotational fall. The source’s safety constraint, “To avoid injury he must keep his head to one side” [PAGE 33], functions as an additional veto: any tackle form that places the head in the rotational path is rejected regardless of its expected stopping value.

Fifth, the information-delay veto. A signal or shift is admissible only if the offense can execute before the defense completes recognition. If the defense’s decoding time \(T_d\) is less than the offense’s initiation time \(T_o\), the tactic is vetoed. Zuppke warns that the shift can disorganize the opponent but also creates uncertainty for the offense if the defense does not line up as anticipated [PAGE 144]. Therefore a shift is valid only when  
\[
T_o<T_d
\]
and when the offense’s own members can maintain alignment despite the opponent’s varied defensive alignment.
