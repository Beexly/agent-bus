The supplied source is not a contest query, roster report, or event forecast; it is a computer-vision methods paper. I therefore treat the requested audit as an epistemic audit of the paper’s registration pipeline and of any downstream claim that might be attached to it. The paper’s core claim is precise: instead of relying only on feed-forward regression of a homography, it trains a second network to regress registration error, then differentiates through that error network to update the homography. The abstract states: “We propose an optimization-based framework to register sports ﬁeld templates onto broadcast videos” and “train a deep network that regresses the registration error, and then register images by ﬁnding the registration parameters that minimize the regressed error” [PAGE 1]. The Figure 1 caption gives the operational chain: “We first obtain an initial pose estimate from a feed-forward network that regresses directly to the homography parameterization h” and “We then differentiate through this network to obtain the direction in which the estimated error is minimized, and optimize our estimated homography accordingly” [PAGE 2].

Multi-hop derivation. First hop: the initial registration network \(f_\Phi\) maps image \(I\) to an eight-number control-point parameterization \(\hat h^{(0)}\), trained by squared error against ground-truth \(h_{\rm gt}\) [PAGE 3, PAGE 4]. Second hop: at iteration \(i\), the template \(m\) is warped by the current homography estimate \(\hat h^{(i)}\), and the warped template is concatenated channel-wise with \(I\). The error network \(g_\Psi\) maps that pair to a scalar registration-error prediction \(\hat\epsilon^{(i)}\) [PAGE 4]. Third hop: the scalar is differentiated with respect to \(\hat h^{(i)}\), and Adam is used to move the estimate in the direction of lower predicted error [PAGE 4]. Fourth hop: the loop continues until convergence or a maximum iteration count; the paper says it runs 400 iterations and returns the estimate with the lowest predicted error [PAGE 5]. The logic is not that the error network directly outputs a correction. The paper explicitly states: “our registration error network is not trained to give updates. It simply regresses to the correctness of the current estimate” [PAGE 4]. The correction emerges from the gradient of the learned error surface, not from a learned update rule.

Why decoupled training matters. The two networks regress different objects: pose and error. The paper argues that independent training prevents the error network from overfitting the initial network’s mistakes [PAGE 1]. This is a gray-zone issue because joint training is often assumed to be better. The paper reports the opposite: “Coupled training, however, performs worse than decoupled training, as shown in Table 2” [PAGE 6]. In the World Cup table, decoupled IoUwhole mean is 89.8 versus coupled 87.3; in Hockey, decoupled is 96.2 versus coupled 87.9 [PAGE 7]. The mechanism is a convergence-basin problem: if the error network is trained only on the narrowing mistakes of a converging initial network, it learns a narrow basin, and “The estimates that fall out of the convergence basin can not be optimized using the learned error” [PAGE 6].

Gray-zone audit. The geometric assumption is hard: “We assume a known planar sports ﬁeld template and undistorted images, so that we can represent the image-template alignment with a homography matrix” [PAGE 3]. If the field is nonplanar, the lens is distorted, or the visible field markings are too sparse, the homography model itself is invalid, and a low learned error is not proof of correct registration. The paper handles real broadcast videos and reports strong results, but its own evidence is bounded by the World Cup dataset of “209 images for training and validation, and 186 images for testing” [PAGE 4], and the Hockey dataset’s 1.67M images with only two 800-frame sequences used for validation and testing [PAGE 5]. The metrics are dimensionless IoU variants; the paper distinguishes “IoUpart” as visible-region intersection-over-union and “IoUwhole” as whole-template intersection-over-union [PAGE 5]. The target metric matters: regressing IoUpart does not guarantee the best IoUpart, and regressing IoUwhole gives the best performance in the reported cases [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. Spectral normalization is used to smooth error predictions “by constraining the Lipschitz constant of the model, which limits the magnitude of its gradients” [PAGE 5]. That stabilizes optimization but can also create a slow or flat gradient regime if over-smoothed. Runtime is also a gray zone: one frame takes 9.58 seconds, while a batch of 64 averages 1.36 seconds per frame [PAGE 7]. The main text does not integrate temporal state; temporal consistency is future work: “we can naturally embed temporal consistency by reusing the optimization state for consecutive images” [PAGE 8]. Thus the paper supports accurate offline or batched registration under its assumptions, not real-time, all-weather, player-level, or biomechanical inference.

The paper’s native units are mixed but explicit. Image coordinates are normalized dimensionless coordinates, image intensities are treated as image-valued fields, IoU is dimensionless in \([0,1]\), reprojection error is in pixels when pixel coordinates are used, and runtime is in seconds. The paper reports no joule, newton, bit, or information-theoretic quantity. The homography parameter vector is dimensionless under normalized coordinates.

Control-point parameterization [PAGE 3]:
\[
h_{\rm ref}=[-0.5,\,0.1,\,-0.5,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.5,\,0.1]^\top
\]
\[
h=[u_1,v_1,u_2,v_2,u_3,v_3,u_4,v_4]^\top
\]
The paper describes these as “the corners of the lower three-fifths of the image” in a normalized coordinate system where width and height are one and the centre is the origin [PAGE 3]. The actual transformation matrix \(T\) is obtained “from h and href through direct linear transformation” [PAGE 3].

Initial registration operator:
\[
f_\Phi:\ I\mapsto \hat h^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\rm init}
=\left\|h_{\rm gt}-\hat h^{(0)}\right\|_2^2
=\left\|h_{\rm gt}-f_\Phi(I)\right\|_2^2
\]
This is Eq. 5 of the paper [PAGE 4]. The units are squared normalized-coordinate units when \(h\) is in normalized coordinates, or squared pixels if pixel coordinates are used. The paper’s normalized setup implies dimensionless squared units.

Warping operator:
\[
W:\ (m,\hat h^{(i)})\mapsto W(m,\hat h^{(i)})
\]
The paper uses “a bilinear sampler [22] to preserve differentiability” [PAGE 4]. Bilinear sampling is defined only over the overlap between the warped template domain and the image domain. If the template is entirely outside the image, the sampled field is empty or constant and the gradient with respect to the homography is zero or uninformative.

Error-regression operator:
\[
g_\Psi:\ [I;W(m,\hat h^{(i)})]\mapsto \hat\epsilon^{(i)}
\]
\[
\hat\epsilon^{(i)}
=g_\Psi\!\left([I;W(m,\hat h^{(i)})]\right)
\]
This is Eq. 4 of the paper [PAGE 4]. The bracket denotes channel-wise concatenation. For IoU-based targets the output is constrained to be nonnegative and is passed through a sigmoid; for reprojection-error targets a squaring function is used [PAGE 5]. Thus for IoU targets \(\hat\epsilon\in[0,1]\) dimensionless; for reprojection targets the unit is pixels squared if the metric is squared pixel error, or pixels if the metric is an average pixel distance.

Error-network training loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\rm error}
=\left\|\mathrm{Err}\!\left(I,W(m,h_{\rm pert})\right)
-g_\Psi\!\left([I;W(m,h_{\rm pert})]\right)\right\|_2^2
\]
This is Eq. 6 of the paper [PAGE 4]. The target \(\mathrm{Err}\) is an error metric such as IoU. The units are squared metric units: dimensionless squared for IoU, squared pixels for pixel reprojection.

Perturbation model [PAGE 4]:
\[
\alpha_g\sim U(-\delta_g,\delta_g),\quad \alpha_g\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
\]
\[
\alpha_l\sim U(-\delta_l,\delta_l),\quad \alpha_l\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
\]
The paper sets \(\delta_g=0.05\) and \(\delta_l=0.02\) empirically from validation results [PAGE 5]. These are normalized-coordinate units. The global translation is added to all control points; the local translation is added individually to each control point [PAGE 4].

Optimization operator at inference:
\[
\hat h^{(i+1)}
=\hat h^{(i)}
-\eta\,\mathrm{Adam}\!\left(
\nabla_{\hat h^{(i)}}g_\Psi\!\left([I;W(m,\hat h^{(i)})]\right)
\right)
\]
The paper states: “In practice, we rely on Adam [26] for a stable optimization” [PAGE 4], and for inference it uses Adam with learning rate \(10^{-3}\), 400 iterations, returning the estimate with the lowest estimated error [PAGE 5]. The learning rate has units of parameter per iteration; under normalized coordinates it is dimensionless per iteration. If pixel coordinates were used, it would be pixels per iteration.

IoU metric [PAGE 5]:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}
\]
\[
\mathrm{IoU}\in[0,1]\quad\text{dimensionless}
\]
The paper defines \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm part}\) as intersection-over-union over only the visible region and \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm whole}\) as the same over the entire sports-field template [PAGE 5].

Singularity and breakdown regimes. First, the direct linear transformation is singular or ill-conditioned when the four control points are repeated, collinear, or otherwise rank-deficient in the correspondence system. Second, a projective map is undefined at points where the homogeneous denominator vanishes:
\[
h_{31}u+h_{32}v+h_{33}=0
\]
and the homography is non-invertible when \(\det(H)=0\). Third, the bilinear warping operator breaks down when there is no template-image overlap or when the field is occluded so heavily that the concatenated error input contains no usable field structure. Fourth, the learned error surface is only valid inside the perturbation support used for training; outside the support, the network is extrapolating. The paper’s basin argument makes this explicit: estimates outside the learned basin “can not be optimized using the learned error” [PAGE 6]. Fifth, optimization can stall on a plateau or local minimum if \(\|\nabla_{\hat h}g_\Psi\|\) is near zero, or if the learned minimum is displaced from the true minimum. Sixth, spectral normalization constrains the Lipschitz constant and limits gradient magnitude [PAGE 5]; if too strong, convergence is slow; if too weak, the error surface may be sharp and unstable. Seventh, \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm whole}\) can be depressed by a small visible region even when local alignment is good, while \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm part}\) can reward local alignment while ignoring global template consistency [PAGE 5].

The paper’s scientific lineage runs from classical direct image registration to learned error surfaces. Lucas and Kanade’s iterative image registration and the later Lucas-Kanade unification are the ancestral optimization frame: estimate a transformation by minimizing an image-alignment objective [PAGE 9, references 2 and 30]. Homography estimation adds the projective geometry lineage of Hartley and Zisserman and direct linear transformation [PAGE 9, reference 15]. The differentiable-warping operator descends from spatial transformer networks and bilinear sampling [PAGE 9, reference 22]. The learned-homography branch includes DeTone et al. and unsupervised deep homography [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. The “learned error as objective” branch includes deep value networks and optimization-in-the-loop methods such as BA-Net, LS-Net, and RegNet [PAGE 3]. Sports-field registration itself has a lineage from pre-calibrated reference-image methods through semantic segmentation, edge/line databases, and image-translation plus database search [PAGE 2]. The paper’s retrocausal move is to invert the role of error: error is not merely a post hoc diagnostic; it becomes the control signal that shapes the future estimate. The future objective—minimizing predicted error—retroactively determines what the error network must represent during training, which is why the perturbation distribution and decoupled training are central.

Multi-week traps. Trap one: treating a second feed-forward refinement network as equivalent to optimization through a learned error. The paper reports that feed-forward refinement gives only minor improvement over the single-stage feed-forward baseline; on World Cup, \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm whole}\) mean is 83.9 for SFF, 84.0 for FFR, and 89.8 for the proposed method [PAGE 7]. Trap three: choosing the target metric by intuition. Regressing \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm part}\) does not guarantee the best \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm part}\); regressing \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm whole}\) gives the best reported performance across cases [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. Trap four: over-reading the small-data result. The “209 images” result is real but dataset-specific; it does not establish robustness to new leagues, camera rigs, weather, or occlusion regimes [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In method terms, the pipeline has a three-level recursive structure. Level one is the initial network’s first-order belief: a homography hypothesis \(\hat h^{(0)}\) from image \(I\) [PAGE 3]. Level two is the error network’s second-order evaluation: given the image and the warped template, it estimates how wrong the current belief is [PAGE 4]. Level three is the optimizer’s action: it uses the gradient of that evaluation to revise the belief [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. A human analyst adds a fourth level: judging whether the error network’s evaluation is trustworthy outside its training basin. This is the recursive theory-of-mind analogue: the system models its own alignment error, then acts on that model, but it does not model players’ intentions, fatigue, injury, or tactical expectations.

Crisis habit regression. Under scarce data or narrow training, a feed-forward refiner can learn habitual corrections tied to the initial network’s typical mistakes. The paper’s decoupled training is designed to prevent exactly this: the error network is trained on independent perturbations rather than on the initial network’s evolving errors [PAGE 1, PAGE 4]. The failure mode is described as a narrow convergence basin: “The estimates that fall out of the convergence basin can not be optimized using the learned error” [PAGE 6]. In a crisis regime—large camera motion, occlusion, unusual view—the initial estimate may fall outside the basin, and the recursive correction can fail confidently.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is to make the error surface broad enough to rescue poor initial estimates. The paper’s NN and NNo variants test this: nearest-neighbor initialization alone gives weak World Cup \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\rm whole}\) mean 73.8, but optimization through the error network raises it to 86.3, still below the full pipeline’s 89.8 [PAGE 7]. That is evidence of a usable basin, not evidence of unlimited basin width. The counter-trap succeeds when the error network’s minimum is near the true homography; it fails when the minimum is displaced or when the input is outside the training distribution.

Clock. The clock is explicit: qualitative results show initial, step 20, step 40, and step 60 registrations, with alignment improving as iterations proceed [PAGE 6]. Inference runs 400 iterations and returns the lowest predicted error [PAGE 5]. Runtime is 9.58 seconds per frame for single-frame optimization and 1.36 seconds per frame for a batch of 64 [PAGE 7]. The main text has no temporal filter, no Kalman state, and no recurrent memory; temporal consistency is future work [PAGE 8]. Therefore the recursive model is bounded in time: it can refine a single frame’s pose, but it cannot infer a player’s crisis state, biomechanical fatigue, or weather-driven performance change from the supplied source.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
