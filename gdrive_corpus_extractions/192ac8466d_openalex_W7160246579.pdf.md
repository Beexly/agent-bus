The recursive theory-of-mind issue is how authors, reviewers, and downstream tactical analysts each model the other’s assumptions. **Crisis habit regression:** when occlusion, shadow, or pitch wear degrades line visibility, the conventional pipeline reverts to its habit: detect sparse lines or corners, fit a homography, and hope the visible features constrain the plane. The offset module is supposed to prevent that regression [PAGE 5–6], but the ablation inconsistency means the size of that protection is unclear. A downstream analyst should therefore ask not “does the model use attention?” but “when the offset branch is disabled or saturated, what is the failure mode?”

**Counter-trap:** the opposite error is to overcorrect by rejecting learned pose regression entirely and demanding a pure geometric solver. But pure PnP needs reliable 2D–3D correspondences, and TvCalib-style optimisation is explicitly sensitive to occlusions that compromise segmentation [PAGE 4]. The paper’s attention head is a reasonable attempt to aggregate distributed evidence. The counter-trap is to accept it without uncertainty calibration. The model outputs a point estimate \(\hat q,\hat t\) with no stated covariance, no quaternion-normalisation guarantee, and no per-keypoint confidence. In a tactical setting, a confident wrong calibration is worse than an abstention.

**Clock:** the tactical clock is short. The paper says tactical analysis requires “quick responses and limited preprocessing” [PAGE 3] and claims “real-time deployment” [PAGE 3], but it reports no latency, FPS, or end-to-end calibration time. A 1920×1080 HRNet plus transformer pipeline may be accurate but not real-time on the deployment hardware; without timing, the clock claim is unverified. There is also a match-long clock: “extended fixed recording exposes tactical cameras to illumination changes, shadows, and pitch wear” [PAGE 3], so a calibration that is correct at kickoff may drift as the sun moves or floodlights change. Recursive ToM says the reviewer should not assume the authors’ “real-time” means the same as the broadcaster’s real-time, and the downstream analyst should not assume a single pre-match calibration remains valid for the full match.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is triggered when a lower-layer physical or data condition makes the upper-layer pose claim unidentifiable, regardless of network capacity.

**Physical optics veto.** Under a pinhole camera, any plane-to-plane mapping is a homography; wide viewing angle alone does not invalidate it. The real physical veto is lens distortion and intrinsic drift. If the camera has non-negligible radial/tangential distortion, the projection is \(x'=\mathcal{D}(\pi(KX_c))\), and a single homography or a pure \((R,t)\) pose without distortion parameters cannot represent the mapping exactly. The paper speaks of “intrinsic and extrinsic camera parameters” [PAGE 2] but regresses only rotation and translation [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: systematic, spatially structured reprojection residuals remain after fitting \((R,t)\) and are correlated with image radius. That is evidence that omitted intrinsics/distortion, not keypoint noise, dominate.

**Visibility and occlusion veto.** The attention head can reweight tokens, but it cannot invent an occluded endpoint. If all annotated endpoints for a critical structure are hidden by players, shadows, or advertising boards, the likelihood of the correct pose is unconstrained along the direction that the missing line would have fixed. The paper shows qualitative robustness to player occlusion [PAGE 8], but it does not report occlusion-fraction versus error. Veto condition: for a given structure, the visible-endpoint fraction falls below the level needed to constrain both rotation and translation; in that regime, high JaC on other structures does not prove calibration of the occluded structure.

**Weather and illumination veto.** Rain, snow, fog, wet grass, deep shadow, and overexposure reduce line contrast. The training augmentation includes colour jitter and horizontal flipping [PAGE 7], but colour jitter does not simulate fog or specular wet turf. If the line-to-grass contrast falls below the detector’s noise floor, keypoints vanish. Veto condition: controlled degradation of contrast or added fog causes a sharp drop in keypoint recall and a rise in reprojection error that is not captured by the reported clear-weather benchmark.

**Biomechanical and dynamic-scene veto.** Players are not rigid field markings. Their motion creates occlusion boundaries and motion blur; the paper’s own motivation notes player-induced occlusion and limited resolution [PAGE 5]. A “biomechanical” veto here means that human-body occupancy can mask the exact line endpoints used by JaC. Veto condition: when player masks cover the penalty-box corners or centre-circle arc, the pose head’s global attention may still output a plausible pose, but the structural JaC for those markings should fail. If it does not fail, the metric is likely too forgiving.

**Camera-stability veto.** The method assumes a fixed elevated wide-angle tactical camera [PAGE 3]. Wind, stand vibration, or operator pan-tilt breaks that assumption. A pose regressed at time \(t_0\) is not a calibration for \(t_1\). Veto condition: frame-to-frame pose residuals on static field points exceed the reprojection tolerance, or calibration error drifts monotonically over a match. The paper reports no temporal stability test.

**Data-distribution veto.** The SoccerNet subset is filtered for elevated, near-central, at-least-80%-field coverage and excludes behind-goal, goal-line, and limited-sideline views [PAGE 7]. A real tactical camera outside that envelope is out of distribution. Veto condition: evaluating on unfiltered tactical feeds or on behind-goal views produces a failure rate not predicted by the filtered benchmark. The WC14 zero-shot set is only 200 frames [PAGE 7], so it cannot by itself adjudicate broad deployment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
