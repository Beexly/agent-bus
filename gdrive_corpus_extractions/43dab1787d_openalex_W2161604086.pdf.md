The source thesis frames monocular hand tracking as a model-based inverse problem: recover the kinematic parameters of a hand from one camera stream by fitting a generative hand model to each image. The core multi-hop derivation proceeds through five layers: kinematic parameterization, surface representation, camera projection, image discrepancy, and gradient-based inference. Each layer introduces a distinct mathematical object and a distinct failure mode. The first layer represents the hand as a tree-structured articulated body. The source states that the pose parameter vector encodes joint angles plus the global wrist pose, with a manifold dimension that depends on modeling choices; it later instantiates a model with 28 degrees of freedom for articulation and 29 ambient parameters when quaternion components are included [PAGE 66] [PAGE 76]. The kinematic equations are rigid transformations on SE(3), so the inference must respect both joint limits and the nonlinear geometry of rotations. This matters because naive Euclidean updates in quaternion or Euler coordinates can create singularities or leave the feasible pose manifold.

The second layer is the surface model. The thesis first uses ellipsoids and convex polyhedra for a silhouette-based method, then replaces them with a triangulated mesh controlled by linear blend skinning to support shading, texture, and finer occlusion boundaries [PAGE 70] [PAGE 130]. This shift is not cosmetic. The silhouette model can only enforce the projected hand region; it cannot explain internal intensity variation. The source explicitly notes that the silhouette-only objective “cannot discriminate between two pose that lead to similar silhouettes” [PAGE 133]. The later texture-and-shading model therefore adds an image formation process: albedo, Lambertian-like shading, illumination parameters, and visibility. This expands the observation model from a binary region likelihood to a per-pixel residual between observed and synthesized intensity.

The third layer is projection and occlusion. The camera model is pinhole projection, with image coordinates obtained by dividing camera-space coordinates by depth [PAGE 73]. In monocular video, depth is not directly observed. The thesis emphasizes that different 3D hand poses with different depth can produce very similar 2D projections, creating multiple low minima in the objective function [PAGE 22]. Occlusions make this worse because visibility changes discontinuously at hand-hand or hand-background boundaries. The source introduces “occlusion forces” to account for the motion of these boundaries when the pose changes [PAGE 152]. This is a key gray-zone derivation: the residual image is discontinuous across occlusion boundaries, but the integrated energy can still be differentiable if the boundary velocity and residual jump are included.

The fourth layer is the discrepancy functional. The silhouette method defines a log-likelihood ratio image and integrates it over the projected hand silhouette. The source first writes a discrete, piecewise-constant cost, then constructs a continuous cost by interpolation and Green’s theorem, enabling exact or analytically consistent gradients [PAGE 87] [PAGE 92] [PAGE 97]. The texture method defines a continuous image-domain residual between observed and synthetic images, with special antialiasing near occlusion boundaries [PAGE 144] [PAGE 158]. Both methods share a methodological commitment: optimization is only reliable if the implemented cost and gradient are continuous enough for quasi-Newton methods. The thesis repeatedly warns that discontinuous costs, finite-difference approximations, or binary visibility can stall optimization or produce invalid descent directions.

The fifth layer is inference over time. Because the pose space is high-dimensional and multimodal, the thesis uses local quasi-Newton optimization, constrained quadratic programming, particle filtering or smart particle filtering, and a Kernel PCA pose prior [PAGE 108] [PAGE 120] [PAGE 148]. The temporal model is simple but important: a constant-speed prediction is combined with observation likelihood, and the search is localized around predicted poses. The source also notes practical kinematic rates: hand translation can reach several meters per second and wrist rotation can reach hundreds of degrees per second, while common cameras operate near 30 Hz, creating motion blur and large inter-frame pose changes [PAGE 21]. These constraints bound the valid temporal search window.

The gray-zone audit therefore identifies several nontrivial traps. First, silhouette equivalence: any method that ignores internal edges, shading, or texture cannot infer poses that differ only inside the silhouette. Second, occlusion-induced non-differentiability: if the gradient omits boundary motion terms, the optimizer receives misleading information exactly when fingers occlude one another. Third, depth ambiguity: without shading or texture, the model may explain the image with incorrect depth. Fourth, discretization artifacts: pixel-grid clipping, ellipse-to-polygon approximation, and antialiasing choices can make the implemented energy differ from the continuous ideal. Fifth, biomechanical validity: linear joint constraints are useful, but self-collision constraints are nonconvex and difficult to enforce. Sixth, illumination and background assumptions: shading-based inference depends on a stable or estimable illuminant and a plausible background model. The thesis is strongest where it formalizes these layers and derives gradients explicitly; it is most vulnerable where assumptions remain implicit, such as manual initialization, limited collision handling, and incomplete cast-shadow modeling.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides a formal chain of operators. I state the main equations with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Articulated forward kinematics.
The thesis defines each bone pose by a homogeneous rigid transform:
\[
K_j(\theta) = K_{p(j)}(\theta) F_j(\theta_j),
\]
where \(K_j \in SE(3)\), \(p(j)\) is the parent bone, and \(F_j\) encodes the joint transform [PAGE 66]. If translations are measured in meters and angles in radians, then \(K_j\) maps local bone coordinates to world coordinates in meters. The root pose uses a unit quaternion and translation:
\[
\theta_1 = [q_{1:4}, t_{1:3}], \qquad q \in S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4,
\]
with a geodesic retraction used to keep the quaternion on \(S^3\) [PAGE 68]. Breakdown regime: Euler-angle parameterizations can introduce singularities where the rotation representation is not locally unique; the source explicitly warns that such singularities may cause the search to stop at saddle points [PAGE 35].

2. Joint constraints.
The thesis encodes static and dynamic joint limits as a linear inequality system:
\[
A\theta \le b,
\]
with \(A\) sparse and \(b\) in radians or degree-derived bounds [PAGE 77]. These constraints define a convex feasible set under linear inequalities. Breakdown regime: if omitted, the optimizer can generate anatomically implausible poses. A harder breakdown is self-collision: the source notes that the set of non-interpenetrating poses is not convex and is difficult to constrain in optimization [PAGE 13].

3. Pinhole projection.
The camera model maps 3D camera coordinates to image coordinates:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}\\ \hat{y}\\ \hat{z}\end{bmatrix}
=
P_c
\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}/\hat{z}\\ \hat{y}/\hat{z}\end{bmatrix},
\]
where \(x,y\) are pixel coordinates and \(X,Y,Z\) are camera-space coordinates, typically meters if the calibration is metric [PAGE 73]. Breakdown regime: monocular depth ambiguity. The source states that distinct 3D poses with different depth may lead to very similar 2D projections, creating multiple equally low minima or multiple posterior modes [PAGE 22].

4. Silhouette matching cost.
The first method defines a log-likelihood-ratio image \(f(x,y)\), equivalent to the logarithm of the likelihood ratio between hand and background color models [PAGE 33] [PAGE 87]. The continuous silhouette cost is
\[
L(\theta) = K + \iint_{\Omega(\theta)} f_c(x,y)\,dx\,dy,
\]
where \(\Omega(\theta)\subset\mathbb{R}^2\) is the projected hand silhouette and \(f_c\) is an interpolated version of the discrete log-likelihood-ratio image [PAGE 97]. If \(f_c\) is interpreted as a log-probability density per unit image area, the integral has information units, e.g. nats multiplied by image-area units; with unit pixel area it is numerically a log-likelihood cost in nats. The source emphasizes that the discrete version is piecewise constant and unsuitable for local optimization because its gradient is null almost everywhere [PAGE 96].

5. Green’s theorem reduction.
To compute the continuous silhouette cost efficiently, the thesis converts the area integral into a boundary integral:
\[
\iint_{\Omega} f_c(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=
\oint_{\Gamma=\partial\Omega}
\langle F(s), \hat{n}(s)\rangle\,ds,
\]
with \(F_y=0\) and \(F_x(x,y)=\int_0^x f_c(t,y)\,dt\) [PAGE 92]. Here \(s\) is arc length in pixels and \(\hat{n}\) is the outward unit normal. This operator reduces integration over a filled silhouette to integration over line segments and ellipse arcs or their polygon approximations.

6. Vertex force and silhouette gradient.
For a polygonal silhouette with vertices \(q_k\), the derivative of the cost with respect to a vertex is given formally as
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}
=
J(q_k-q_{k-1})
\int_0^1 f_c(tq_k+(1-t)q_{k-1})\,t\,dt
+
J(q_{k+1}-q_k)
\int_0^1 f_c(tq_k+(1-t)q_{k+1})\,t\,dt,
\]
where \(J\) is the 90-degree rotation matrix used to obtain edge normals [PAGE 104]. The units are cost change per pixel displacement of the vertex. Breakdown regime: if an adjacent silhouette edge aligns exactly with a pixel-grid line, the interpolated field or its derivative may fail to be well-defined under certain interpolation choices [PAGE 105].

7. Ellipse-to-polygon approximation.
Because exact ellipse-arc integration and differentiation are cumbersome, the thesis approximates each projected ellipse by an \(N\)-vertex polygon:
\[
p_n = V [a\cos(2\pi n/N), b\sin(2\pi n/N),1]^T + \mu,
\]
where \(a,b\) are ellipse semi-axes and \(\mu\) is the center [PAGE 100]. The source uses \(N=16\) in experiments. Breakdown regime: when the projected ellipse becomes a circle, \(d_1=d_2\) in the source’s notation, the angular parameterization becomes degenerate and the approximation may lose smooth dependence on pose [PAGE 100].

8. Texture-and-shading image formation.
The second method synthesizes an image using geometry, texture, shading, and illumination:
\[
I_{\mathrm{syn}}(x;\theta,\vartheta,L,T)
=
\begin{cases}
L(w;\theta,\vartheta,L)\, r(h(w);T), & x\in\chi(\theta,\vartheta),\\
I_{\mathrm{bk}}(x), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(\chi(\theta,\vartheta)\) is the projected hand region, \(r\) is texture albedo in RGB intensity units, and \(L\) is shaded irradiance determined by surface normals and illuminant parameters [PAGE 139]. The shading model uses a directional component plus an ambient component:
\[
L_v(i;\theta,\vartheta,L)
=
\langle \hat{n}_i(\theta,\vartheta), L_{1:3}\rangle + L_4,
\]
with luminance interpolated across mesh faces [PAGE 135].

9. Continuous residual energy.
The data-fidelity term is an integral over the image domain:
\[
E_c(\theta,\vartheta,T,L)
=
\int_{\Omega} R(x;\theta,L,T)\,dx,
\]
where \(R\) may be squared intensity error or a robust residual [PAGE 144]. If intensity is normalized to \([0,1]\) and \(\rho\) is squared error, the energy has units of intensity-squared times image area. Breakdown regime: if the residual is evaluated only on a discrete pixel grid without antialiasing, the cost becomes discontinuous when occlusion boundaries cross pixel centers [PAGE 141].

10. Occlusion-force gradient.
The central formal contribution of the texture method is the gradient in the presence of occlusion boundaries. Let \(\Gamma_\theta\) be the set of visible occlusion and self-occlusion boundaries. The derivative of the continuous energy with respect to a pose parameter \(\theta_j\) is
\[
\frac{\partial E_c}{\partial \theta_j}
=
\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma_\theta}
\frac{\partial R}{\partial \theta_j}(x;\theta,L,T)\,dx
-
\int_{\Gamma_\theta}
\left[
R^{+}(x;\theta,T,L)-R(x;\theta,L,T)
\right]
\hat{n}_{\Gamma_\theta}(x)\cdot v_j(x)\,dx,
\]
where \(R^{+}\) is the residual extended by continuity from the near-occluded side, \(\hat{n}_{\Gamma_\theta}\) is the boundary normal, and \(v_j\) is the boundary velocity induced by changing \(\theta_j\) [PAGE 152]. The second term is the occlusion force contribution. Units: boundary velocity is pixels per radian or pixels per unit pose parameter; the residual jump has intensity or residual units; the product integrates along a curve in pixels.

11. Antialiased differentiable discretization.
To implement a differentiable cost on a pixel grid, the thesis blends residuals near occlusion boundaries:
\[
\bar{R}(x;\theta,L,T)
=
w(x)R(x;\theta,L,T)
+
(1-w(x))R(p(x);\theta,L,T),
\]
where \(p(x)\) projects \(x\) onto the occlusion boundary and \(w(x)\) is a signed-distance-based blending weight [PAGE 158]. The discrete objective is then
\[
\bar{E}_c(\theta,T,L)
=
\sum_{x\in\Omega\cap\mathbb{N}^2}
\bar{R}(x;\theta,L,T),
\]
which is continuous in \(\theta\) and differentiable almost everywhere [PAGE 159]. Breakdown regime: if the antialiasing region is omitted, the gradient lacks the occlusion-boundary term and the first-order model becomes invalid near occlusions.

12. KPCA pose prior.
The pose prior is defined using Kernel PCA on training pose vectors:
\[
E_{\mathrm{prior}}(x)
=
\frac{1}{\lambda_\perp}
\left(
\bar{k}(x,x)-\bar{k}_x^T M \bar{k}_x
\right)
+
\bar{k}_x^T D \bar{k}_x,
\]
where \(\bar{k}\) is a centered kernel, \(M\) encodes projection onto the leading kernel principal components, and \(D\) encodes Mahalanobis weighting within the kernel subspace [PAGE 201]. If the kernel is normalized and inputs are scaled pose parameters, the prior is dimensionless. The combined objective is
\[
E(\theta,L,T)
=
E_c(\theta,L,T)
+
\beta E_{\mathrm{prior}}(\theta),
\]
where \(\beta\) controls the relative weight of prior and data terms [PAGE 148].

13. Temporal model.
The particle-filter dynamic model is a constant-speed Gaussian transition:
\[
P(X_{t+1}\mid X_t)
\propto
\exp\left(
-\frac{1}{2}
(DX-X)^T\Sigma_d^{-1}(DX-X)
\right)
\]
subject to \(A\theta_{t+1}\le b\), where \(X_t=[\theta_t,\dot{\theta}_t]^T\) and \(D\) advances pose by the frame interval \(\delta t\) [PAGE 119]. Units: \(\theta\) in radians, \(\dot{\theta}\) in radians per second, \(\delta t\) in seconds. Breakdown regime: if inter-frame motion exceeds the region where the local search and prediction are reliable, the tracker may lose the mode.

14. Exact breakdown regimes stated by the source.
The source identifies several precise failure regimes:
- High dimensionality: the hand has approximately 30 significant degrees of freedom, making exhaustive search computationally demanding [PAGE 20].
- Speed and frame rate: hand motion can reach up to 5 m/s translation and 300 degrees/s wrist rotation; at 30 Hz this permits about 10 degrees of inter-frame wrist rotation, causing motion blur and prediction uncertainty [PAGE 21].
- Depth ambiguity: distinct 3D poses with different depth can yield similar 2D projections, producing multiple minima or posterior modes [PAGE 22].
- Silhouette insufficiency: the silhouette-only cost cannot distinguish poses with the same silhouette [PAGE 133].
- Occlusion boundary nondifferentiability: residual discontinuities require occlusion forces for a valid gradient [PAGE 152].
- Pixel-grid degeneracy: binary masks and edge-aligned pixel boundaries create piecewise-constant costs and invalid finite-difference gradients [PAGE 96] [PAGE 105].
- Cast shadows and collisions: cast shadows are not modeled directly, and collision avoidance is not incorporated, both of which can bias the residual or permit implausible poses [PAGE 170] [PAGE 173].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the thesis is a recursive negotiation between three older traditions: inverse problems, generative image formation, and articulated-body optimization. The source explicitly formulates model-based tracking as an inverse problem in which one seeks \(\theta\) such that observed features equal synthesized features, and then relaxes this to a discrepancy minimization because noise and modeling error make exact inversion impossible [PAGE 23]. This is the classical regularization move: replace an ill-posed inverse operator with a penalized or probabilistic objective. The thesis later instantiates this principle in two different observation models: color-region likelihoods in the silhouette method and per-pixel shading/texture residuals in the second method.

A second lineage is the use of physical or mechanical metaphors for fitting. The state-of-the-art review describes generalized forces, Maxwell demons, ICP-like methods, and gradient descent on matching costs [PAGE 38] [PAGE 41]. The thesis preserves the metaphor but makes it mathematically explicit: forces on silhouette vertices are derivatives of an integrated log-likelihood ratio, and occlusion forces are derivatives of an integrated image residual with respect to moving visibility boundaries [PAGE 104] [PAGE 152]. This is a retro-causal genealogy: the later formal derivation shows why earlier force-based heuristics worked only when the underlying potential was continuous and the boundary motion was implicitly handled.

A third lineage is the evolution from silhouette and edge features to shading and texture. The source notes that edges and silhouettes are often insufficient for monocular depth disambiguation, and it cites the observation that human observers can use shading to infer local surface orientation [PAGE 22]. The thesis also records a pivotal remark from David Fleet: “if you needed to add shading onto your hand model to get a good visualization of your results, that means that you should add shading in the generative model you use for the tracking” [PAGE 4]. This remark is historically important because it identifies the missing generative variable. The earlier silhouette method had no mechanism to explain intensity variation inside the hand region; the later method adds illumination, albedo texture, and a triangulated surface, thereby turning shading from a visualization aid into an inference constraint.

The multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy are identifiable from the source’s limitations sections. The first trap is over-reliance on edges. The thesis states that edge-based matching costs may suffer discontinuities when small pose changes create or occlude edges [PAGE 59]. A researcher who optimizes only chamfer distance or edge alignment may spend weeks chasing local minima caused by edge birth and death rather than by actual pose error. The second trap is silhouette segmentation error. If the hand is segmented first, cluttered backgrounds or skin-color ambiguity can corrupt the cue before optimization begins. The thesis avoids this by using likelihood ratios directly inside the model silhouette, unifying segmentation and pose fitting [PAGE 62]. The third trap is binary visibility. If visibility is treated as a depth-buffer binary state, the cost changes abruptly when facets become occluded or visible, undermining quasi-Newton methods [PAGE 145]. The thesis addresses this by retaining a continuous image-domain formulation and deriving occlusion forces.

A fourth trap is finite-difference gradient implementation. The source discusses exact versus approximate matching cost and derivatives, showing that approximate derivatives can break first-order models used by variable-metric and trust-region methods [PAGE 115] [PAGE 117]. This is a classic multi-week trap: a method may appear theoretically sound but fail in implementation because the derivative is evaluated on a discontinuous discretized energy. The thesis’s exact or analytically consistent gradient methodology is a direct response to that trap.

A fifth trap is temporal overconfidence. Model-based trackers often initialize local search from the previous frame. The source shows that single-hypothesis local search fails when occlusion creates a strong local minimum, and that classical particle filtering can fail in high-dimensional pose spaces unless the number of particles is impractically large [PAGE 124]. The smart particle filter and KPCA prior are genealogical countermeasures: multiple hypotheses address local minima, while learned pose priors address implausible transitions. The historical lesson is that robust monocular tracking is not merely a better image term; it requires a joint model of kinematics, appearance, occlusion, and temporal uncertainty.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis treats the tracker as an agent whose beliefs about hand pose are updated by observations, priors, and temporal predictions. The crisis condition occurs when the observation model becomes ambiguous or discontinuous: self-occlusion, depth ambiguity, cluttered background, motion blur, or illumination change. In such a crisis, the default habit regression is to trust the previous pose and perform local gradient descent. This is efficient but fragile. The source notes that single-hypothesis methods fail the first time the hand gets occluded because the tracker cannot escape a local minimum [PAGE 124]. The habit regression is therefore: predict from the previous frame, linearize the cost, take a descent step, and repeat. This works when the inter-frame displacement is small and the cost basin is locally convex. It fails when the true posterior is multimodal or when the boundary of the basin is crossed.

The counter-trap is to model the crisis explicitly. The first counter-trap is multiple hypotheses. The smart particle filter combines local search with particle propagation: after propagating particles, each particle is duplicated and locally optimized, then reweighted so the represented posterior is not arbitrarily altered [PAGE 120]. This addresses the fact that the posterior may have several modes due to depth ambiguity or occlusion. The second counter-trap is a stronger observation model. Shading and texture reduce ambiguity because they constrain surface orientation and albedo continuity. The source demonstrates that removing occlusion forces makes the method unable to track displacements, while including them restores tracking through occlusions [PAGE 182]. The third counter-trap is a learned pose prior. Kernel PCA encodes a manifold of plausible poses; during the “CVPR” signing sequence, the method without the prior loses track after the first letter, while the prior helps maintain tracking through rapid transitions [PAGE 175].

The clock is the temporal layer. The source models state as pose plus velocity and uses a constant-speed transition [PAGE 119]. The clock is not merely a frame counter; it determines the valid search radius. The source notes that hand speed can reach hundreds of degrees per second and that 30 Hz cameras can observe up to about 10 degrees of inter-frame wrist rotation, creating motion blur and uncertainty [PAGE 21]. If the clock is too slow relative to hand motion, the prediction prior becomes weak and the observation likelihood may contain multiple competing modes. If the clock is too fast but noisy, the dynamic model may overconstrain the state and reject valid poses. The source also notes that professional interaction may require at least 100 Hz tracking speed to reduce lag and imprecision [PAGE 21]. Thus the clock has a dual role: it provides prediction, but it also defines the aliasing and blur regime of the sensor.

The recursive loop can be stated as a belief update:
\[
p(\theta_t \mid I_{1:t})
\propto
p(I_t \mid \theta_t)
\int p(\theta_t \mid \theta_{t-1})
p(\theta_{t-1}\mid I_{1:t-1})
d\theta_{t-1}.
\]
In the source’s implementation, this is approximated by particles or local modes, with observation likelihood derived from the matching cost [PAGE 118]. The crisis-habit regression corresponds to collapsing this posterior to one mode. The counter-trap corresponds to preserving multiple modes and adding prior structure. The clock corresponds to the transition kernel and sensor sampling rate. A robust tracker must know when to distrust the clock: during large inter-frame motion, occlusion onset, or illumination change, the prediction should widen and the search should globalize or re-initialize. The thesis’s architecture embodies this lesson by combining local optimization, particle filtering, and learned priors, but it also reveals residual limits: manual initialization, no cast-shadow forces, and no explicit collision avoidance mean that some crisis states remain outside the represented model [PAGE 170] [PAGE 173] [PAGE 185].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto proof asks whether a proposed pose or inference strategy is invalid because it violates a lower physical layer, a middle observation layer, or an upper temporal layer. I state the veto conditions formally.

1. Biomechanical veto.
Let \(\Theta_{\mathrm{valid}}\) be the set of poses satisfying static and dynamic joint constraints. The source encodes these as linear inequalities:
\[
\Theta_{\mathrm{valid}} = \{\theta : A\theta \le b\}.
\]
If a candidate estimate \(\hat{\theta}\notin\Theta_{\mathrm{valid}}\), it is vetoed because it violates anatomical joint limits [PAGE 77]. The source also notes that self-collision defines another admissible subset \(\Theta_c\subset\Theta\), but this subset is not convex and is difficult to encode with simple differentiable constraints [PAGE 13]. Therefore, if a method claims physical plausibility but does not enforce either \(A\theta\le b\) or a collision condition, its pose estimates are vulnerable to anatomical veto.

2. Kinematic-rate veto.
Let \(\omega_{\max}\) be the maximum plausible angular rate and \(\Delta t\) the frame interval. A temporal prediction is valid only if
\[
\|\theta_t-\theta_{t-1}\| \lesssim \omega_{\max}\Delta t.
\]
The source cites hand speeds up to 300 degrees/s for wrist rotation and notes that 30 Hz cameras can observe up to 10 degrees of inter-frame wrist rotation [PAGE 21]. If a tracker assumes a narrower search region than this bound during rapid motion, it is vetoed by the kinematic-rate layer because the true pose may lie outside the local basin. Conversely, if the tracker assumes a much larger region without adequate global search, it may be vetoed by computational or local-minimum failure.

3. Depth-ambiguity veto.
Under pinhole projection,
\[
x = f\frac{X_c}{Z_c}, \qquad y = f\frac{Y_c}{Z_c},
\]
so depth \(Z_c\) is not directly observed from a single view [PAGE 73]. If two poses \(\theta_a\) and \(\theta_b\) produce the same silhouette and the same internal edges are unobserved, the silhouette-only cost satisfies
\[
L(\theta_a)=L(\theta_b).
\]
The source explicitly states that the silhouette-only method cannot discriminate between poses with the same silhouette [PAGE 133]. Therefore, any claim of full 3D pose recovery using only silhouette evidence is vetoed unless additional cues such as shading, texture, motion parallax, or a strong prior break the equivalence.

4. Occlusion-differentiability veto.
Let \(E_c(\theta)\) be the continuous image residual. If the implementation omits the occlusion-boundary term, the computed gradient is
\[
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\theta}E_c
=
\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma_\theta}
\frac{\partial R}{\partial \theta}\,dx,
\]
but the true derivative contains the boundary term
\[
-\int_{\Gamma_\theta}
(R^+-R)\hat{n}_{\Gamma_\theta}v_\theta\,dx.
\]
The source derives this term and shows that neglecting it produces an invalid first-order model near occlusions [PAGE 152]. A method that uses such an incomplete gradient is vetoed in any scene where self-occlusion boundaries move, because descent directions will not correspond to the true energy decrease.

5. Illumination or weather-layer veto.
The shading model assumes that vertex irradiance can be written as a directional component plus an ambient component:
\[
L_v = \langle \hat{n}, L_{1:3}\rangle + L_4.
\]
This is a controlled-illumination assumption. If the scene contains uncontrolled illumination, interreflections, non-Lambertian reflectance, or moving cast shadows, the residual becomes biased. The source notes that cast shadows are not modeled directly and are effectively absorbed into texture or residual error [PAGE 170]. In a “weather” sense, if the illumination environment changes faster than the model can estimate \(L\), the shading cue can become adversarial. The veto condition is: if the illumination mismatch produces residual gradients that dominate the pose-dependent shading gradients, the pose estimate is no longer identifiable from the assumed generative model.

6. Background and clutter veto.
The silhouette method uses generative color models for hand, background, foreground, and body-behind-hand [PAGE 94]. If the background distribution is nonstationary, multimodal, or not represented by the learned model, the log-likelihood ratio image \(f(x,y)\) can assign misleading signs to pixels. The source acknowledges that arbitrary cluttered backgrounds are challenging and that many methods assume uniform, static, or distinct backgrounds [PAGE 23]. A tracker is vetoed if background contamination causes the silhouette boundary to align with a false hand-background boundary.

7. Discretization veto.
The continuous theory assumes an image domain \(\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^2\), but implementation uses pixels. If the cost is evaluated with a binary mask, the energy is piecewise constant and its gradient is null almost everywhere [PAGE 96]. If antialiasing or continuous interpolation is not used, quasi-Newton methods lose their first-order guarantee. The veto condition is: if the implemented cost fails a finite-difference consistency check against its analytic gradient, the optimizer’s descent claim is invalid.

The adjudication outcome is that a pose estimate is accepted only if it satisfies kinematic constraints, respects temporal motion bounds, explains the image under a continuous and differentiable observation model, includes occlusion-boundary motion terms, and remains stable under the assumed illumination and background regime. If any lower layer is violated, the estimate is vetoed regardless of its apparent image-fit quality.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I define three reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards counterfactual veto scenarios. In each case, the veto is triggered by a verifiable condition in the source or in a direct experiment derived from the source.

Veto Scenario 1: Silhouette-equivalence veto.
Counterfactual condition: suppose a tracker claims to recover full 3D hand pose while using only a silhouette-based likelihood ratio cost. The verifiable test is to construct two distinct hand poses, \(\theta_a\) and \(\theta_b\), whose projected silhouettes are identical but whose internal finger configurations differ. The source states that the matching cost in the first method “is only a function of the silhouette” and therefore cannot capture internal edges or shading [PAGE 133]. The predicted reward outcome is:
\[
L(\theta_a)-L(\theta_b)=0
\]
under the silhouette-only model, even if the true poses differ. The verifiable prediction is that the tracker will fail on sequences where fingers occlude the palm or where internal edges are required to disambiguate depth. The source confirms this failure mode when the hand closes and phalanges projecting inside the palm are not well estimated [PAGE 123]. The veto is triggered if the method’s reward, defined as negative pose error or negative endpoint error, fails to distinguish \(\theta_a\) from \(\theta_b\) in the silhouette-equivalence test.

Veto Scenario 2: Occlusion-force omission veto.
Counterfactual condition: suppose the gradient implementation omits the occlusion-boundary term from the continuous image-domain energy. The source derives the full gradient with an occlusion-force term and states that neglecting visibility changes near self-occlusion boundaries is a defect of existing formulations [PAGE 152]. The verifiable test is to compare the analytic gradient without the boundary term against a finite difference of the continuous energy for a pose sequence that causes an occlusion boundary to cross pixels. The predicted outcome is that the omitted gradient will fail the first-order consistency check:
\[
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}
\frac{E_c(\theta+\epsilon e_i)-E_c(\theta)}{\epsilon}
\neq
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\theta_i}E_c
\]
when the pose change moves an occlusion boundary. The source reports a dramatic failure when occlusion forces are removed: the algorithm becomes unable to track displacements [PAGE 182]. The veto is triggered if tracking reward collapses during occlusion events, especially when fingers bend over the palm or when self-occlusion boundaries move rapidly.

Veto Scenario 3: Temporal-prior or clock veto.
Counterfactual condition: suppose a tracker relies only on local search from the previous frame and has no pose prior or global re-initialization during rapid sign-language transitions. The source’s “CVPR” experiment provides a verifiable scenario. Without the KPCA prior, the method loses track after the first letter; with the prior, it tracks the sequence more robustly [PAGE 175]. The clock layer is relevant because the source notes that hand motion can be rapid and that 30 Hz sampling can create large inter-frame rotations and motion blur [PAGE 21].
