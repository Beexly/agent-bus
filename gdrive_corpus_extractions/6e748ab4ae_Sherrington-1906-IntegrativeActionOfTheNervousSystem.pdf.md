I start from the primitive composition stated on [PAGE 31]: “The reflex-arc is the unit mechanism of the nervous system when that system is regarded in its integrative function.” The same page defines the chain as “receptor, conductor, and effector — as a reflex-arc.” That gives the first multi-hop structure: stimulus → receptor transduction → afferent conductor → central synaptic/convergence apparatus → efferent final common path → effector. The receptor is not passive; [PAGE 37] says “the main contribution made to the mechanism of the reflex-arc by that part of it which constitutes the receptor is selective excitability.” Thus the receptor lowers threshold for an adequate stimulus and raises it for non-adequate stimuli. The next hop is conduction: [PAGE 43] states that in nerve-trunks the response interval “is strictly proportional to the distance” and gives frog nerve at 15°C “about 3 cm. per sigma (σ = .001 second).” But reflex arcs deviate: [PAGE 45] says “It is with mild stimuli that the difference in speed between reflex conduction and nerve-trunk conduction becomes most obvious,” and the contents summarize “Reflex latency inversely proportional to intensity of stimulation” [PAGE 17]. Therefore the formal operator must separate propagation time from synaptic/central delay and must include an intensity-dependent term that is not a toy reciprocal law but a monotone decrease with lower bound.

The next hop is temporal integration. Sherrington reports that “long series of subminimal stimuli ultimately provoke the reflex” and that “subliminal stimuli are summed” [PAGE 61]. This is a threshold-crossing operator over a finite summation window, not a single-shot all-or-none rule. The next hop is refractory phase: [PAGE 69] notes that in the eyelid-reflex for nearly a full second after initiation “the chance that a second stimulus then delivered will, though otherwise appropriate, excite the reflex, is fifty per cent less than it is one second later.” For the scratch-reflex, rhythmic response is independent of stimulus rhythm; [PAGE 73] says “Nor can this refractory state be overcome by simply increasing the intensity of the stimuli.” This creates a clock operator: motor output has its own refractory-limited frequency, not a one-to-one copy of stimulus frequency.

The next hop is reciprocal inhibition. [PAGE 83] calls the flexion-reflex a reflex “with simultaneous double-sign”: flexor excitation with extensor inhibition. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operators are stated below with units and breakdown regimes. The primitive arc is a composition
\[
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{C}\circ\mathcal{R},
\]
where \(\mathcal{R}\) is receptor transduction, \(\mathcal{C}\) is the conductor/synaptic chain, and \(\mathcal{E}\) is the effector. This is Sherrington’s “whole chain of structures — receptor, conductor, and effector — as a reflex-arc” [PAGE 31]. The input is a stimulus intensity \(I\) in physical units appropriate to the receptor—mechanical force \([\mathrm{N}]\), radiant power \([\mathrm{W}]\), chemical concentration \([\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}]\), or electrical current \([\mathrm{A}]\). The output is effector force \([\mathrm{N}]\), displacement \([\mathrm{m}]\), or secretion rate \([\mathrm{mol\,s^{-1}}]\).

Receptor selectivity is a threshold-ordering operator:
\[
\theta_R(s_a)<\theta_R(s_{\neg a}),
\]
where \(s_a\) is the adequate stimulus and \(s_{\neg a}\) is a non-adequate stimulus; \(\theta_R\) has the units of the stimulus. This formalizes “selective excitability” [PAGE 37]. Breakdown: if \(s_a\) is not present, the arc is not necessarily silent—electrical and noxious stimuli can force discharge—but the ordering explains why the reflex is normally tied to one stimulus class. The electrical-stimulus exception is itself source-marked: electricity “never constitutes the adequate stimulus for any receptor” [PAGE 37].

Nerve-trunk conduction is
\[
T_{\mathrm{trunk}}=\frac{L}{v},\qquad v\approx 3\,\mathrm{cm}\,\sigma^{-1}=30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
\]
at \(15^\circ\mathrm{C}\) in frog nerve, where \(\sigma=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 43]. Assumption: uniform myelinated fibre and no synaptic interruption. Breakdown: at a reflex arc the latency is not \(L/v\) alone; a central/synaptic term is added.

Reflex latency is
\[
T_{\mathrm{reflex}}(I)=T_{\mathrm{prop}}+T_{\mathrm{syn}}(I),
\]
with \(T_{\mathrm{prop}}=L/v\) in seconds and \(T_{\mathrm{syn}}\) in seconds. The source’s summary is “Reflex latency inversely proportional to intensity of stimulation” [PAGE 17]; formally this is the monotone regime
\[
\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{reflex}}}{\partial I}<0
\quad\text{for}\quad \theta<I<I_{\mathrm{sat}},
\]
with lower bound
\[
T_{\mathrm{reflex}}\to T_{\mathrm{prop}}+T_{\mathrm{syn,min}}
\quad\text{as}\quad I\to I_{\mathrm{sat}}.
\]
Breakdown: below threshold \(I<\theta\), no response occurs unless temporal summation crosses threshold; at very weak \(I\), reflex latency is long and variable, and the difference from nerve-trunk conduction is largest [PAGE 45]. The relation is not a universal reciprocal law; it is a regime-dependent monotone decrease.

Temporal summation is an integral threshold operator:
\[
\int_{t-T_{\mathrm{sum}}}^{t} e(s(\tau))\,d\tau \ge \Theta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{reflex discharge},
\]
where \(e\) is excitatory drive in \(\mathrm{Hz}\), the integral is in impulses, and \(\Theta\) is threshold in impulses. This formalizes “subliminal stimuli are summed” [PAGE 61]. Units are \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\times\mathrm{s}=\text{impulses}\). Breakdown: if inter-stimulus interval exceeds \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\), summation fails; summation time is shorter for distant receptive points than for one point [PAGE 147]. The reflex can appear only after many subminimal shocks, as in the source’s fortieth-successive-shock example [PAGE 61].

Refractory-phase operator:
\[
P(\text{response}\mid s,t_{\mathrm{post}})=
\begin{cases}
0, & t_{\mathrm{post}}<\tau_{\mathrm{abs}},\\
1-r(t_{\mathrm{post}}), & \tau_{\mathrm{abs}}\le t_{\mathrm{post}}<\tau_{\mathrm{rel}},\\
P_0, & t_{\mathrm{post}}\ge\tau_{\mathrm{rel}},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(P\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless and \(\tau\) is in seconds. This is a probability operator, not a deterministic toy ratio. For the eyelid-reflex, the source states that for nearly a full second the chance of a second response is “fifty per cent less than it is one second later” [PAGE 69]. For the scratch-reflex, a \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\) stimulus can yield flexions at about \(4\,\mathrm{Hz}\) [PAGE 73]; the motor clock is not the stimulus clock. Breakdown: increasing stimulus intensity does not overcome the refractory state [PAGE 73]; the operator must be reset by discharge, not by stimulus amplitude alone.

Reciprocal inhibition is a sign-vector operator:
\[
\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{flex}}=(+,-),\qquad
\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{cross\,ext}}=(-,+),
\]
where \(+\) denotes excitation and \(-\) inhibition; the entries are dimensionless signs applied to motor-neurone firing rates \(r_F,r_E\) in \(\mathrm{Hz}\). This is the “simultaneous double-sign” reflex [PAGE 83]. The inhibitory synapse is internuncial: “neither in the afferent neurone proper nor in the efferent neurone proper, but in an internuncial mechanism” [PAGE 105]. Under strychnine or tetanus toxin,
\[
\sigma_E:-\to+
\]
for selected arcs, not universally: “the effect across it was signalized by a different sign, namely minus prior to the strychnine or tetanus toxin, instead of plus, as afterward” [PAGE 135]. Breakdown: low doses can produce a stage in which “the reflex relaxation is diminished but is not replaced by excitation” [PAGE 109]; different inhibitory arcs convert with different facility; general convulsion is not the same operator as local sign reversal.

The final common path is a set-valued selection operator for antagonistic reflexes:
\[
\mathrm{FC}(t)\in\{R_i(t)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},
\]
not a linear sum. The source’s exact statement is: “The result is this reflex or that reflex, but not the two together” [PAGE 141]. For allied reflexes, the operator is coalescence/reinforcement:
\[
\mathrm{FC}(t)=R_i\oplus R_j,\qquad
\mathrm{effect}(R_i\oplus R_j)\ge \max\{\mathrm{effect}(R_i),\mathrm{effect}(R_j)\},
\]
with effect in \(\mathrm{N}\) or \(\mathrm{Hz}\). This matches “These widely separate reflex-arcs therefore reinforce one another” [PAGE 175]. Breakdown: algebraic summation at the final common path would produce a compromise movement, which the source rejects; the common path “is adapted to serve but one purpose at a time” [PAGE 233].

Prepotency is a dominance relation:
\[
R_k\succ R_j \quad\text{when}\quad A_k>A_j
\]
in the competition for the final common path, where \(A\) is reaction intensity in \(\mathrm{Hz}\) or normalized stimulus intensity. The source says intensity of reaction “is probably the most powerful” factor [PAGE 223], but not the only one: reflexes from receptors with strong affective tone “prevail over reflexes of other species” [PAGE 231]. Breakdown: fatigue, freshness, spinal induction, and functional species can reverse the dominance; strychnine can change the sign of an inhibitory path and thereby change the apparent dominance.

Spinal shock is a state-depression operator:
\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{post}}(t)=D(t)\,\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{pre}},\qquad D(t)\in[0,1],
\]
where \(D\) is dimensionless. It is not explained by trauma as such: shock “appears to take effect in the aboral direction only” [PAGE 241], and a second transection may cause little further shock because the relevant channels were already cut [PAGE 243]. Breakdown: in pronounced shock the scratch-reflex “fails to be elicitable by electrical stimulation at all, though still elicitable by rubbing” [PAGE 245]; in monkey and man the depression is peculiarly long-lasting, with suspected “isolation dystrophy” [PAGE 247]. Any fixed pre-transection parameter set is invalid in this regime.

Environmental rate operator:
\[
\left|\frac{dI}{dt}\right|<\dot I_{\mathrm{th}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{no receptor response},
\]
with \(\dot I_{\mathrm{th}}\) in stimulus units per second. The source illustrates this with “slow changes of barometric pressure on the body surface originate no skin-sensation, though they are much above the threshold value for touch” [PAGE 339]. Breakdown: an abrupt weak stimulus can be effective even when a slow large stimulus is not; the receptor responds to rate of change as well as intensity.

Breakdown regimes, stated exactly: \(I<\theta\) gives no reflex unless summation crosses \(\Theta\); \(t_{\mathrm{post}}<\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}\) gives refractory block; antagonistic reflexes have no simultaneous solution at the same final common path; strychnine/tetanus can flip the sign of selected inhibitory arcs; spinal shock invalidates pre-transection parameters; asphyxia and narcosis abolish reflexes sooner than nerve-trunk conduction [PAGE 103]; section of one lateral column abolishes ipsilateral decerebrate rigidity, and section of afferent roots makes the limb fall flaccid [PAGE 301]; static environmental variables fail to cross the rate threshold [PAGE 339].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogical line runs from Marshall Hall’s reflex concept and Pflüger’s spinal “laws” through Goltz, Ferrier, Hitzig and Fritsch, Cajal, van Gehuchten, and Sherrington’s synapse-centered synthesis. The supplied text explicitly cites Marshall Hall on the greater ease of eliciting reflexes from receptive surfaces than from afferent trunks [PAGE 33], and it treats Pflüger’s four “laws” as classical but partly non-concordant with mammalian data [PAGE 185–187]. The Silliman Foundation setting matters: the volume is a Yale lecture series, and the scanned title page prints “MDCCCCXX” [PAGE 9], while the Drive filename says 1906. That date discrepancy is a bibliographic trap: it should be logged, not silently resolved into a false citation.

The retrocausal lineage is not “Sherrington invented inhibition.” The source itself cites E. Hering and Breuer on respiratory vagal self-regulation, v. Cyon on depressor reflexes, and Meltzer/Kronecker on deglutition [PAGE 99]. What Sherrington does is move inhibition from a peripheral curiosity to a central co-ordinating operator at the common path. Later physiology inherits this as reciprocal innervation, Renshaw inhibition, presynaptic inhibition, and central pattern generators. The multi-week trap is to read the 1906/1920 text as if it already contains modern synaptic vocabulary in the exact modern sense. It uses “synapse” as a surface of separation and a threshold-bearing junction [PAGE 41, 155], not as the fully molecular cleft of later physiology.

Second multi-week trap: treating “reflex latency inversely proportional to intensity” [PAGE 17] as a clean reciprocal equation. The source is explicit that mild stimuli make the reflex/nerve-trunk difference most obvious [PAGE 45], that strong stimuli can bring reflex latency close to nerve-trunk latency [PAGE 43], and that the scratch-reflex has a characteristically longer latency than the flexion-reflex [PAGE 45]. The correct lineage lesson is regime-dependence, not a single constant.

Third trap: treating “final common path” as a single anatomical axon. The source’s common path is a functional convergence: “At the commencement of every reflex-arc is a receptive neurone … At the termination of every reflex-arc we find a final neurone” [PAGE 139]. Each internuncial path is itself usually a common path, and the final path is only the highest-degree communism [PAGE 145]. This matters for later motor-control models: convergence, not one-wire anatomy, is the operator.

Fourth trap: confusing fatigue with inhibition. The source explicitly contrasts them: reflex ceasing under inhibition fades “without obvious change in the frequency of repetition of the ‘beats,’” while fatigue shows “a slower rhythm and a sluggish course” [PAGE 221]. A model that labels every cessation “fatigue” will misread reciprocal inhibition and refractory phase.

Fifth trap: assuming strychnine simply lowers spinal resistance. Sherrington argues the opposite in the critical dose range: at a stage where the inhibitory arc is less able to affect the extensor, “on the spinal resistance view the resistance at this stage is actually heightened” [PAGE 109]. The lineage lesson is that pharmacological sign conversion is a phase transition, not a scalar gain change.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive observer problem is unavoidable in this text. An observer watches a decerebrate or spinal animal and is tempted to infer pain, anger, fear, or pleasure from mimesis. Sherrington blocks the easy inference: it is “as impossible to disprove as to prove” that psychical events accompany the reactions of the “spinal” animal [PAGE 235]. In the decerebrate preparation, motor reactions “play the pantomime of feeling; but no feeling comes to pass” [PAGE 267]. A recursive theory-of-mind model must therefore distinguish three layers: the experimenter’s inference about the animal’s inner state; the animal’s expressive motor pattern; and the underlying reflex machinery. The source’s own “pseudaffective” reflexes are the test case: they mimic affective expression without requiring the forebrain [PAGE 251–255].

Crisis habit regression: under spinal shock, decerebration, fatigue, or toxication, higher control is stripped and older reflex habits dominate. The source describes “retrogradation” by which a complex expressive movement “has in certain emotions passed into a simpler reflex-act” [PAGE 267]. Goltz’s dog still shows anger-like conduct after loss of much sensation; Sherrington notes that the expression can persist while the psychical state is doubtful [PAGE 261–263]. The recursive trap is to treat regression as proof of a preserved mind, when it may be a released reflex habit.

Counter-trap: inhibition and refractory phase can look like absence, fatigue, or “the animal stopped.” But reciprocal inhibition is an active sign vector: flexor excitation is paired with extensor inhibition [PAGE 83], and the common path serves “but one purpose at a time” [PAGE 233]. A naïve observer sees a pause and infers exhaustion; the source says the pause may be an inhibitory phase or a refractory clock. The counter-trap is to over-interpret a negative behavioral observation as a negative internal state.

Clock: the scratch-reflex has its own rhythm. Under a \(100\,\mathrm{Hz}\) stimulus it produces flexions at about \(4\,\mathrm{Hz}\) [PAGE 73], and the rhythm is independent of stimulus rhythm [PAGE 69]. The eyelid-reflex has a refractory window of nearly a second [PAGE 69]. Thus the “clock” is internal and refractory-limited, not a direct copy of the world. Recursive mind-modeling must allow that an animal’s timing is generated by central refractory mechanisms, not by the observer’s stimulus schedule.

The mind-model that survives is austere: behavior is a pantomime; affect is an inference; the reflex clock and the inhibitory sign are measurable; the psychical attribution is not settled by the source. That is the correct recursive stance.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: asphyxia and narcosis override reflex predictions. The source states that “interruption of the circulation in the frog extinguish reflexes in thirty minutes,” while the sciatic nerve “retain excitability and conductivity three to five hours in nitrogen” [PAGE 103]. Therefore a model that predicts a robust reflex from an isolated cord must be vetoed if oxygen delivery is compromised. Chloroform and ether, carried far, “quite abolishes” decerebrate rigidity [PAGE 301]. These are physical/chemical vetoes at the tissue level, not psychological variables.

Biomechanical veto: antagonistic muscles at the same joint cannot be driven to incompatible simultaneous contractions without confusion. The final common path is a mechanical bottleneck: “The result is this reflex or that reflex, but not the two together” [PAGE 141]. Reciprocal inhibition is the biomechanical solution: flexor excitation with extensor inhibition [PAGE 83]. Decerebrate rigidity is also mechanically vetoed by lesion: section of one lateral column in the upper lumbar region abolishes rigidity in the ipsilateral hind limb; section of one ventrolateral column in the cervical region destroys rigidity in ipsilateral fore and hind limbs [PAGE 301]. If afferent roots are severed after decerebration, “the limb at once falls flaccid” [PAGE 301]. A model that predicts rigidity from central drive alone after afferent-root section is vetoed by the preparation’s mechanics.

Weather/environmental veto: the supplied pages contain no meteorological equation, so no weather variable should be invented. The closest physical veto is the rate-of-change condition: slow barometric pressure changes “originate no skin-sensation, though they are much above the threshold value for touch” [PAGE 339]. Thus static weather is not a stimulus; only a sufficiently rapid change in the adequate physical variable crosses the receptor threshold. If a proposed inference requires a static pressure or temperature gradient to drive a reflex, the source vetoes it unless the gradient is converted into a rate of change or a noxious intensity.

Layer adjudication rule: the highest-priority veto is tissue viability (oxygen, narcosis), then biomechanical compatibility at the final common path, then receptor rate-threshold, then central state (shock, fatigue, refractory), then pharmacological sign conversion. The source supports this ordering because it repeatedly shows that the reflex is not a free-floating logical relation: it depends on living conduction, synaptic threshold, mechanical effector geometry, and state variables [PAGE 103, 141, 301, 339].
