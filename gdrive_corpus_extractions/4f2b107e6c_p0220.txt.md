<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Galton’s central mechanism on [PAGE 1]—the normal curve of frequency applied to inheritance—is a recursive statistical habit: extreme parental deviates produce offspring whose expected trait value regresses toward the population mean, E[O|P] = μ + r(P − μ) with |r| < 1. Under crisis conditions (selection pressure, environmental shock, or measurement outlier), the habit is to overreact to the extreme observation as if it were a new stable level. The regression effect says the next generation (or next observation period) will partially revert.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the regression fallacy: interpreting the statistical reversion as causal evidence that an intervention “caused” the return to average. If an agent observes an extreme value, applies a corrective, and then observes regression, the agent may falsely credit the corrective rather than the passive normal-correlation structure encoded in Table 4 and Table 5 on [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is especially acute when the agent mistakes Galton’s probable error 0.4769 for a standard deviation and therefore misjudges how extreme the initiating observation truly was.

Clock. The clock is generational recurrence time. Each application of the regression relation contracts the deviate by factor r; variance approaches an equilibrium σ²_O = r²σ²_P + σ²_E. If the clock is mismatched—e.g., applying a one-generation regression coefficient to weekly data—the predicted decay of shock persistence is wrong: shocks decay geometrically in generation units but may persist or accumulate in week units if the process has autocorrelation not captured by Galton’s independent-error assumption. The normal tables on [PAGE 1] assume iid errors; any serial correlation in the clock variable invalidates the single-step regression forecast.
