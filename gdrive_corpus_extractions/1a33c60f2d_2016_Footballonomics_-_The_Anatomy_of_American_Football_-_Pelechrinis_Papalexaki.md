<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive coach problem has three layers. First, crisis habit regression: under scrutiny, coaches revert to the status quo—kick the extra point, punt on fourth down—even when the expected-point calculation favors aggression. The paper frames this through status quo bias and notes that rational decisions are often “received with questions and are scrutinized,” which can discourage coaches from following them [PAGE 2]. The PAT decision is especially exposed because the expected gain is small per touchdown, so a single failed two-point attempt is highly visible while the long-run expected gain is invisible. The paper says the net gain per touchdown is “fairly small” and not all teams have the same success rates [PAGE 5]. That visibility asymmetry produces habit regression: the coach optimizes for immediate blame avoidance rather than expected points.

Second, counter-trap: if a coach adopts the aggressive policy, the opponent updates. The paper explicitly anticipates this for two-point conversions: practicing the conversion can raise \(s_{2pt}\) transiently, but “at some point the opposing defenses will also adopt and hence, \(s_{2pts}\) will converge to a steady value” [PAGE 6]. The same logic applies to fourth downs: if a team goes for it more often, opposing defenses can adjust their fourth-down expectations and their ensuing-drive scoring probabilities \(\Delta\pi_{fg}\) and \(\Delta\pi_{td}\). Thus the mean-field operator is a best response to current opponent behavior, not a fixed law. A recursive theory of mind must model the opponent modeling the coach: the more predictable the aggression, the more the opponent can prepare. The paper’s decision chart in Figure 7 is therefore a baseline, not a universal equilibrium [PAGE 11].

Third, clock and score-state recursion. The paper says the two-point decision “depends mainly on the score differential and the time remaining in the game, two supposedly irrelevant factors as per a rational economic model” [PAGE 5]. A rational expected-points model says those factors should not matter for the per-touchdown expected value, but a recursive game-theoretic model says they matter because they change the opponent’s incentives, the variance penalty, and the value of information. The paper concedes that coaching decisions may be based on “minimizing the variance of the outcome” or on risk-adjusted return [PAGE 22]. In that case the coach is not irrational; the coach is optimizing a different objective. The crisis habit regression is the behavioral failure mode; the counter-trap is the strategic adaptation mode; the clock is the state variable that changes which mode dominates. A robust audit must separate all three, because a decision that looks irrational under expected points may be rational under variance aversion, and a decision that looks rational in one clock state may be exploitable in another.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is straightforward: the paper’s operators are valid only over aggregate game statistics, not over physical, biomechanical, or weather layers. The authors themselves say that adjustments can be made using “information about the specific match-up (i.e., roster, weather forecast etc.)” [PAGE 3], and they admit the prediction engine does not account for “game-specific information (e.g., injured players, teams resting players during the last week, etc.)” [PAGE 22]. That is an explicit omitted-variable veto. Formally, if the true win probability is \(p(y\mid x,L)\), where \(x\) is the paper’s feature vector and \(L\) is a layer such as injury load, weather, or biomechanical fatigue, then the paper estimates \(p(y\mid x)=\int p(y\mid x,L)p(L\mid x)dL\). If \(p(y\mid x,L)\) varies with \(L\) and \(L\) is correlated with \(x\), the estimate is biased. The paper’s calibration result, slope confidence interval \([0.76,1.16]\) [PAGE 21], is conditional on the observed distribution of \(L\), not on counterfactual weather or injury states.

Physical veto: the paper uses only play-by-play aggregate statistics—total offensive yards, penalty yards, turnovers, possession time, \(r\), and ranking differential [PAGE 12, PAGE 14]. It has no player tracking, no collision load, no injury status, no roster availability, and no biomechanical fatigue state. If a key passer or kicker is unavailable, \(s_{kick}\), \(s_{2pt}\), and the passing-to-rushing ratio \(r\) can shift outside the empirical range \(r\in[0.3,0.98]\) [PAGE 15]. A prediction made inside that range but with an injured roster is an extrapolation in a hidden variable. Veto condition: if residual win-probability error correlates with injury reports or roster changes after controlling for \(x\), the model’s cross-layer claim is rejected.

Biomechanical veto: the PAT operator depends on \(s_{kick}\), which is a kicking mechanic and snap-distance variable. The 2015 rule moved the extra point snap from the 2-yard line to the 15-yard line, and the paper reports a roughly 5% drop in \(s_{kick}\) with p-value less than 0.001 [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. That is a physical-rule shock, not a tactical preference. If a model treats \(s_{kick}\) as a stable team constant, it will misprice the 2015 regime. The paper’s own Table 1 shows the break: 2015 \(s_{kick}=0.9416\) versus 2009-2014 values near 0.98-0.996 [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if kicker identity, snap distance, or weather explains a statistically significant share of \(s_{kick}\) residual variance, the fixed-\(s_{kick}\) mean-field calculation is incomplete.

Weather veto: weather is absent from the regression and from the bootstrap feature matrix [PAGE 12, PAGE 18]. Weather can alter passing efficiency, turnover propensity, kicking success, and the run-pass ratio \(r\). The paper’s validity range for \(r\) is \([0.3,0.98]\) [PAGE 15]; extreme wind or precipitation can push a team’s play calling toward the boundary or outside it. Veto condition: if out-of-sample calibration degrades in high-wind or precipitation games, or if the calibrated slope excludes 1 in those strata, the all-weather claim is rejected. The correct adjudication is not to discard the paper; it is to treat the paper’s output as a baseline anchor and require a layer-specific override when physical, biomechanical, or weather variables move the conditional distribution.
