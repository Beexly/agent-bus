1. Planar field homography. The chapter states that field registration “estimates a homography between the 3D sports ﬁeld plane and the image, which is limited to applications within the ﬁeld plane” [PAGE 2]. For normalized field coordinates \(u=U/L_x\), \(v=V/L_y\) with \(L_x,L_y\) in metres and image coordinates \(x,y\) in pixels, a homography is:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}} \sim H\tilde{\mathbf{u}},\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=(x,y,1)^\top,\quad
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=(u,v,1)^\top,\quad
H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}.
\]
After the scale normalization \(h_{33}=1\), \(H\) is dimensionless under normalized field coordinates; image coordinates remain in pixels. Assumptions: the field is planar, the camera is static over the estimation window, and lens distortion is either negligible or already removed. Exact singularity and breakdown regimes: \(H\) is singular if \(\det(H)=0\); the estimation is degenerate if the corresponding field points are collinear or duplicated; and the model is structurally invalid for off-plane points because the source limits homography to the field plane [PAGE 2]. The source contrasts this with the pinhole model, which “provides a complete 3D mapping that accounts for the camera’s intrinsic and extrinsic parameters” [PAGE 6].

2. Direct linear transformation and RANSAC. The chapter says field registration computes the mapping using “RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus) [48] and DLT (direct linear transformation) [60]” [PAGE 7]. For \(n\) point correspondences, DLT forms:
\[
A h=0,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 9},\quad h\in\mathbb{R}^9,\quad \|h\|_2=1,
\]
and solves by singular value decomposition \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\), taking \(h\) as the right singular vector associated with the smallest singular value. The reprojection residual is:
\[
r_i=\|\pi(H\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i)-\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\|_2\quad [\text{pixel}],
\]
with root-mean-square residual in pixels. DLT is exactly singular if \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\le 8\), which occurs for fewer than four non-degenerate correspondences or for degenerate configurations such as three collinear points. RANSAC success probability is:
\[
P_{\mathrm{succ}}(N,w,s)=1-(1-w^s)^N,
\]
where \(w\in[0,1]\) is the inlier probability, \(s\) is the minimal sample size, \(N\) is the iteration count, and \(P_{\mathrm{succ}}\) is dimensionless probability. Exact breakdown: \(P_{\mathrm{succ}}=0\) at \(w=0\); \(P_{\mathrm{succ}}\to 0\) when \(w^s\ll 1/N\). The source does not publish \(N\), \(s\), or \(w\) for its cited systems, so these must be treated as unverified implementation parameters.

\[

\quad

\begin{bmatrix}
f_x & \gamma & c_x\\
0 & f_y & c_y\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\]

If \(f_x f_y=0\) or the calibration constraints are rank-deﬁcient, the projection is singular. The source also warns that lens distortions and nonlinearities are often overlooked [PAGE 8], so a pinhole fit without distortion terms can carry systematic peripheral residuals even when nominal calibration succeeds.

\[

\mathbf{z}_k=H_k\mathbf{x}_k+\mathbf{v}_k,
\]
with state \(\mathbf{x}\) in metres and metres per second, process noise covariance \(Q=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^\top]\) in \(\mathrm{m}^2\) and \(\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}^2\), and measurement noise covariance \(R\) in \(\mathrm{pixel}^2\). The assignment problem is:
\[
\sigma^*=\arg\min_{\sigma\in S_n}\sum_i C_{i,\sigma(i)},
\]
where \(C\) is dimensionless if it is an IoU cost or in pixels if it is a centroid-distance cost. Exact breakdown: the Kalman filter diverges or becomes inconsistent if \(Q=0\) while the athlete changes direction, if occlusion invalidates the measurement model, or if the covariance matrix ceases to be positive semidefinite. The Hungarian assignment is undeﬁned if \(C\) contains \(\mathrm{NaN}\) or \(\infty\) from missing detections; practical systems require dummy rows and columns. IoU itself is:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=
\frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|},
\]
dimensionless in \([0,1]\), and is undefined when both boxes are empty. Under heavy occlusion or similar uniforms, the source’s stated tracking complications [PAGE 4] make these breakdowns operational rather than theoretical.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under occlusion, lighting failure, or unfamiliar footage, the pipeline tends to regress to the habits that are easiest to deploy: off-the-shelf detectors, manual annotation, and closed-world Re-ID. The source gives direct evidence for the detector habit: “off-the-shelf algorithms like YOLO tend to over-detect non-athlete individuals, such as spectators or staff around the ﬁeld” [PAGE 10]. It also gives evidence for the annotation habit: traditional data collection and labeling “relied heavily on manual effort, which is both time-consuming and costly” [PAGE 1]. And it gives evidence for the identity habit: most Re-ID studies remain closed-world [PAGE 13]. In a crisis, the model’s implicit theory of mind is “more labels will fix this,” while the analyst’s theory is “the metric already measures the game,” and the coach’s theory is “the skeleton is the biomechanics.” These beliefs are not equivalent.

Counter-trap: the source’s counter-moves are open-world processing, unsupervised fine-tuning, and active learning. The open-world setting processes raw images or videos directly and is “more suitable for real-world applications” [PAGE 14]. Unsupervised fine-tuning uses multi-view estimates as pseudo-labels for a monocular model, reducing manual calibration and multiple-camera requirements [PAGE 20] and [PAGE 21]. Active learning is offered to reduce annotation burden by selectively annotating informative samples [PAGE 25]. The counter-trap is to treat these as free wins. Pseudo-labels inherit multi-view calibration error; active learning inherits the selector’s blind spots; open-world Re-ID inherits the feature extractor’s domain bias. A recursive theory of mind must therefore ask not only “does the model believe it is accurate?” but “does the evaluator believe the evaluation distribution is the deployment distribution?”

Clock: the temporal layer is the discipline that prevents the recursive beliefs from collapsing into static snapshots. Tracking predicts future positions from “current trajectory and velocity” [PAGE 3]. Action spotting provides “precise timestamps for key actions” [PAGE 3]. Temporal action segmentation identifies start and end times [PAGE 3]. Without the clock, a detector can look accurate frame-by-frame while identity switches and event boundaries drift. The source’s action-spotting emphasis and the SoccerNet GSR integration [PAGE 21] imply that the correct recursive audit is temporal: check whether the same identity, same event, and same pose are coherent across time, not merely whether a single frame is classified correctly.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

The source lists “motion blur” and “fast-paced and complex movements” as capture problems [PAGE 4], and it states that “Weather conditions including lighting, camera angles, and obstructions can signiﬁcantly affect the quality of the captured footage” [PAGE 4]. Exact breakdown: if the homography is singular or the correspondences are degenerate, \(\bar r_g\) is not a reliable estimate at all; if lens distortion is unmodeled, the residual becomes spatially structured, so a low global mean can still hide large peripheral errors [PAGE 8].

Biomechanical veto: if joint-center error exceeds the biomechanical tolerance, technique and injury-risk claims are vetoed. The source says sports biomechanists “need highly precise joint center positions” and that current models “often fail to consistently provide the necessary level of precision” [PAGE 20]. Audit form: let \(\epsilon_J=\|\hat{\mathbf{J}}-\mathbf{J}\|_2\) in metres for a joint center. If \(\epsilon_J\gt\tau_b\), the biomechanical claim is vetoed regardless of pixel-level keypoint confidence. Exact breakdown: monocular 3D pose has depth ambiguity, so \(\epsilon_J\) can be small in the image plane and large in depth; multi-view triangulation fails if the joint is occluded in too many views or if camera rays are nearly parallel. The source’s multi-view and pseudo-label strategy [PAGE 20] and [PAGE 21] reduces but does not eliminate this veto.

Weather and visibility veto: if contrast, glare, rain, or obstruction removes the visual evidence needed for detection, the identity and action layers cannot recover it. The source’s environmental-factor statement [PAGE 4] is the proof of dependency. Audit form: let \(C=(I_{\max}-I_{\min})/(I_{\max}+I_{\min}+\varepsilon)\) be a dimensionless local contrast diagnostic, with \(\varepsilon\) a positive regularizer. If \(C\lt\tau_C\), detection confidence is not interpretable as presence probability. Exact singularity: if \(I_{\max}=I_{\min}=0\), contrast is undefined without regularization; if the image is saturated, contrast collapses while pixel values remain large. In either case, a high detector score is not evidence of a real athlete.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: a claim is admitted only if the geometry layer, identity layer, temporal layer, and biomechanical layer each pass their own veto. The source’s GS-HOTA description as “projection accuracy, tracking accuracy, and the quality of player identiﬁcation” [PAGE 22] supports this multi-axis logic, but the source does not provide the exact weighting. Therefore, exact GS-HOTA arithmetic cannot be reconstructed from this chapter alone; the audit must use the external metric definition if numerical adjudication is required.

1. Planar-homography counterfactual for off-plane structures. Counterfactual: take a calibrated multi-view or pinhole setup that can project goal posts and crossbars, then replace it with a planar field homography alone. Prediction: crossbar and goal-post reprojection RMSE increases beyond \(\tau_g\), and any 3D goal-mouth claim is vetoed. Verifier: measure crossbar keypoint reprojection error in pixels against held-out annotations. This is directly supported by the source’s distinction that homography is “limited to applications within the ﬁeld plane” [PAGE 2] while camera calibration can project “non-planar points, such as those belonging to goal posts or crossbars” [PAGE 7].

2. Closed-world Re-ID counterfactual on unseen identities. Counterfactual: train or select a Re-ID feature extractor on a closed-world gallery, then deploy it on unseen runners or players without open-world adaptation. Prediction: rank-1 identification accuracy falls toward chance \(1/N\), and identification entropy \(H_{\mathrm{ID}}\) approaches \(\log_2 N\) bits under a near-uniform posterior. Verifier: measure rank-1 accuracy, ID F1, and \(H_{\mathrm{ID}}\) on a held-out identity set. The source supports the distribution shift by contrasting closed-world training [PAGE 13] with the open-world setting that processes raw images or videos and is “more suitable for real-world applications” [PAGE 14].

3. Temporal-consistency ablation for tracking and spotting. Counterfactual: disable temporal consistency or velocity-based prediction in the tracking and homography refinement stages while keeping the same detector. Prediction: identity switches and track fragmentation increase, and action timestamps develop larger localization error in seconds. Verifier: measure ID switches, MOTA-style association errors, tracklet length distribution, and timestamp error against event annotations. The source supports the temporal dependency by describing tracking as detection plus association across frames [PAGE 3], by noting that some trackers predict future positions from “current trajectory and velocity” [PAGE 3], and by defining action spotting as “pinpointing the exact time at which a particular event occurs” [PAGE 3]. A second verifier can ablate temporal homography refinement, because the source notes temporal consistency between frames is used to reﬁne homography estimates [PAGE 7]. Exact veto condition: if the ablated system’s identity-switch rate or timestamp error exceeds the pre-registered tolerance, the temporal claim is rejected.
