<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s core object is not merely “RF ranging”; it is a latency-bounded, infrastructure-free graph realization problem. The multi-hop derivation must therefore proceed through five coupled layers: (1) physical two-way ranging, (2) weighted graph construction, (3) rigidity and topology estimation, (4) Euclidean distance matrix completion and embedding, and (5) absolute coordinate transformation. The source states the central problem explicitly: DynoLoc addresses “this latency-bounded infrastructure-free RF localization problem” [PAGE 1]. The first gray-zone issue is that accuracy is not a free function of ranging resolution. A UWB radio may have fine distance resolution, but if the network cannot collect enough ranges before the refresh deadline, the localization graph becomes sparse, non-rigid, or stale. The paper captures this by saying: “accurate tracking of mobile entities imposes a latency budget that not only affects the solution computation but also the collection of peer-to-peer ranges themselves” [PAGE 1].

The second layer is graph-theoretic. The authors define a weighted graph and a realization: “Let G = (V, E, d) be a weighted graph, where d is the set of range measurements (weights) for the —E— edges defined on the —V— nodes. A realization is a function x : V →R2” [PAGE 4]. The realization must preserve ranges: “∀(u, v) ∈ E, ||x(u) −x(v)|| = d(u, v)” [PAGE 4]. The critical invariant is rigidity. A rigid graph has only one realization “discounting any translation, rotation and flip” [PAGE 4]. This means that without rigidity, the localization solution is not merely noisy; it is fundamentally underdetermined. The paper’s use of 3-core subgraphs is therefore not an optimization detail; it is a geometric necessity. The source says: “It is known that a k-core sub-graph is rigid in k −1 dimensional space [43]. Hence, for rigidity in 2D, we seek to obtain 3-core sub-graphs” [PAGE 6]. This is a hard structural constraint. If the selected edges do not induce a 3-core, the problem becomes ambiguous.

The third layer is latency. The paper gives a concrete physical budget: “with a popular UWB hardware [31], it takes approximately 40 ms to complete a single range estimate, i.e. a two-way-ranging (TWR) operation. This inherently limits the number of ranges that can be estimated/collected per second to 25 to support a localization update rate of 1 Hz” [PAGE 5]. This creates a severe combinatorial issue. For 12 nodes, the complete graph has 66 undirected edges; for 20 nodes, it has 190 undirected edges. At 40 ms per sequential range, a 1 Hz refresh cannot collect all ranges. The system must therefore select edges adaptively. The paper’s aggregated ranging reduces this cost: “our aggregated optimized TWR (OPT-DS-TWR in Fig. 21) can range N nodes in (2N+2) time slots compared to the 4N slots for its sequential counterpart” [PAGE 14]. If each slot is 10 ms, as stated in Appendix A, the latency savings are material. However, even with aggregation, the selected edge set must fit within the epoch. If the selected set exceeds the epoch, the localization solution is stale or delayed. This is the central gray-zone tension: the algorithm must trade off geometric completeness against temporal freshness.

The fourth layer is mobility. The source says: “a 1 Hz refresh rate is appropriate to track nodes with speeds of 1-1.5 m/s, targeting a 1-2 m error” [PAGE 5]. This is a sampling-theoretic statement. If a node moves at speed v and the last localization occurred at time t0, then, without prediction, the position uncertainty cannot be smaller than the distance traveled since t0. For v = 2 m/s and a 1 s epoch, the displacement is 2 m. This does not invalidate DynoLoc’s claimed median error, but it constrains worst-case guarantees. The paper’s design uses the mobility metric to prioritize edges associated with nodes whose location uncertainty is growing. The mobility update is given as: “Mi(k) ←Mi(k −1) + vi(k) · ∆t, where node velocity vi(k) ←vi(k −1) + ai · ∆t” [PAGE 6]. The metric is reset after localization. This is a sensible mechanism, but it is also a gray zone: if IMU bias or drift is not controlled, the mobility metric can over-prioritize or under-prioritize nodes.

The fifth layer is link quality and NLOS. The paper defines an NLOS probability: “This NLOS probability is computed by as pNLOS = (f1 × f2 × f3 × f4)” [PAGE 6]. The link quality is the inverse of this probability. This is important because UWB ranging is not uniformly reliable. Body occlusion, concrete walls, glass, furniture, and multipath can bias range estimates. The paper shows that link quality matters: “introducing the L metric improves accuracy by 30 – 40% in two different NLOS scenarios” [PAGE 10]. The gray-zone audit must note that the four features f1–f4 are heuristic CIR-based indicators. They are not a full physical channel model, but they are operationalized as a selection prior.

The deepest audit is whether the claimed accuracy is algorithmically plausible under all these constraints. The paper reports: “median localization error of under 1 m (2 m)” for 12 (20) responders at 1 m/s (2 m/s), while “existing solutions suffer in accuracy (6m+ error) even for a node mobility of 1 m/s” [PAGE 3]. The plausibility comes from three mechanisms: adaptive topology estimation, aggregated/concurrent ranging, and rigid-subgraph EDM completion. The paper says DynoLoc “leverages the graph rigidity construct of k-core sub-graphs to identify maximal rigid sub-graphs of the topology, and applies EDM on these separately and combines them to provide a robust, accurate solution” [PAGE 2]. This is a strong countermeasure to incomplete EDMs. Without it, matrix completion methods such as OptSpace and SDR can fail because they are “not designed keeping in mind the geometrical implications relevant to a localization problem” [PAGE 5]. The gray-zone conclusion is that DynoLoc’s accuracy is credible for median indoor tracking under its stated assumptions, but it is not a worst-case guarantee unless the epoch, rigidity, and link quality constraints are simultaneously satisfied.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

1. **Weighted localization graph and realization**

The source defines the localization graph as:

\[
G=(V,E,d)
\]

where \(V\) is the set of nodes, \(E\) is the set of pairwise links, and \(d\) is the set of measured ranges. A realization is:

\[
x:V\to \mathbb{R}^{2}
\]

such that, for every measured edge,

\[
\forall (u,v)\in E,\quad \|x(u)-x(v)\|_2 = d(u,v)
\]

[PAGE 4]. In practice, measurements are noisy, so the operational condition is:

\[
\forall (u,v)\in E,\quad \|x(u)-x(v)\|_2 = d(u,v)+\eta_{uv}
\]

where \(\eta_{uv}\) is a range error term in meters. The source says: “The graph G = (V, E, d) is ‘rigid’ if there is only one realization, discounting any translation, rotation and flip” [PAGE 4].

**Units:** \(x(u)\) is a 2D coordinate in meters; \(d(u,v)\) is in meters; \(\eta_{uv}\) is in meters.  
**Assumptions:** Edges correspond to measurable pairwise ranges; node coordinates lie in a 2D plane.  
**Breakdown regime:** If the graph is not rigid, multiple realizations exist, and relative localization is non-unique. If range errors are large enough to change the feasible embedding set, the graph becomes effectively non-rigid even if topologically rigid.

2. **k-core rigidity operator**

DynoLoc uses k-core decomposition. A k-core subgraph is defined by:

\[
G_k=(V_k,E_k),\quad \deg_{G_k}(v)\geq k,\quad \forall v\in V_k
\]

The source states: “In a k-core sub-graph, every vertex has a degree of at least k. It is known that a k-core sub-graph is rigid in k −1 dimensional space [43]. Hence, for rigidity in 2D, we seek to obtain 3-core sub-graphs” [PAGE 6].

**Units:** \(k\) is dimensionless; graph degree is dimensionless.  
**Assumptions:** The underlying connectivity graph has enough edges; generic node positions are assumed by classical rigidity theory.  
**Breakdown regime:** If the remaining topology is only 1-core or 2-core, the 2D embedding is ambiguous. The source notes: “a 2-core sub-graph will not be rigid in 2D and will admit multiple localization solutions” [PAGE 6].

3. **Link quality and NLOS probability operator**

The source defines link quality through an NLOS probability:

\[
p_{\mathrm{NLOS}} = f_1 \times f_2 \times f_3 \times f_4
\]

[PAGE 6]. The four factors are described as:

- \(f_1\): average peak count before the detected first path in the preceding window;
- \(f2\): ratio of noise standard deviation to first-path amplitude;
- \(f3\): ratio of peak amplitude to first-path amplitude;
- \(f4\): ratio of total received power to first-path power.

The link quality is the inverse of this probability:

\[
L_{ij} = \frac{1}{p_{\mathrm{NLOS},ij}}
\]

or, more conservatively, a monotone decreasing function of \(p_{\mathrm{NLOS},ij}\).

**Units:** \(p_{\mathrm{NLOS}}\in[0,1]\), dimensionless; \(L_{ij}\) is dimensionless if defined as inverse probability.  
**Assumptions:** CIR features separate LOS from NLOS sufficiently well for scheduling.  
**Breakdown regime:** If \(p_{\mathrm{NLOS}}=0\), the inverse diverges; if \(p_{\mathrm{NLOS}}=1\), the link is effectively unusable. More importantly, if NLOS bias is present but not detected, the selected edge contributes a systematic range bias that rigidity cannot remove.

4. **Mobility metric operator**

The mobility metric is updated recursively:

\[
M_i(k) \leftarrow M_i(k-1) + v_i(k)\Delta t
\]

\[
v_i(k) \leftarrow v_i(k-1) + a_i \Delta t
\]

where \(M_i\) is location uncertainty since last localization, \(v_i\) is velocity, \(a_i\) is IMU acceleration, and \(\Delta t\) is elapsed IMU time [PAGE 6]. The metric is reset when the node is localized. For static nodes, the source assigns:

\[
M_i \leftarrow e^{T_{sL}} - 1
\]

where \(T_{sL}\) is time since last localization [PAGE 6].

**Units:** \(M_i\) is in meters if interpreted as accumulated uncertainty; \(v_i\) is in m/s; \(a_i\) is in m/s\(^2\); \(\Delta t\) and \(T_{sL}\) are in seconds.  
**Assumptions:** IMU acceleration is usable for short-term mobility indication; resets prevent unbounded growth after successful localization.  
**Breakdown regime:** IMU bias, scale error, or unmodeled rotation causes drift in \(M_i\). If a node is localized but the reset is delayed, the scheduler over-prioritizes it. If a node is moving but the IMU is clipped or stationary, the scheduler may under-prioritize it.

5. **Latency budget and ranging capacity operator**

Let \(T_{\mathrm{refresh}}\) be the application refresh interval and \(\tau_{\mathrm{range}}\) be the time required for one range estimate. The maximum number of sequential ranges per epoch is:

\[
N_{\mathrm{range,max}} = \left\lfloor \frac{T_{\mathrm{refresh}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{range}}} \right\rfloor
\]

The source states: “it takes approximately 40 ms to complete a single range estimate, i.e. a two-way-ranging (TWR) operation. This inherently limits the number of ranges that can be estimated/collected per second to 25 to support a localization update rate of 1 Hz” [PAGE 5].

For aggregated TWR, the source gives:

\[
T_{\mathrm{agg}}(N) = (2N+2)\tau_{\mathrm{slot}}
\]

compared with sequential TWR:

\[
T_{\mathrm{seq}}(N) = 4N\tau_{\mathrm{slot}}
\]

[PAGE 14]. The appendix says: “Given that each transmission slot time can be 10 ms, this substantial saving in turn allows for twice as many links to be ranged with the same latency budget” [PAGE 14].

**Units:** \(T_{\mathrm{refresh}}\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{range}}\), \(\tau_{\mathrm{slot}}\), and \(T_{\mathrm{agg}}\) are in seconds; \(N\) is dimensionless.  
**Assumptions:** Slots are non-overlapping; interference is avoided; control overhead is bounded.  
**Breakdown regime:** If the selected edge set requires more time than \(T_{\mathrm{refresh}}\), the epoch overruns. The localization solution becomes stale or delayed. This is the hard latency veto.

6. **EDM completion and rigid-subgraph optimization operator**

DynoLoc performs EDM completion only on rigid subgraphs. The source says: “DynoLoc performs EDM completion only on the rigid sub-graphs (3-core sub-graphs) of the topology, individually” [PAGE 7]. Algorithm 2 initializes the EDM with measured ranges, computes node locations by multilateration on the rigid graph, fills missing values, and iteratively updates node locations to reduce:

\[
\Delta D = |D - D_{\mathrm{cur}}|
\]

until \(\Delta D\) is no longer significant [PAGE 14].

A formal completion objective can be written as:

\[
\min_{X}\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\mathrm{meas}}} w_{ij}
\left(\|x_i-x_j\|_2 - d_{ij}\right)^2
\]

subject to missing entries being filled from the current node estimate:

\[
\hat{d}_{ij} = \|x_i-x_j\|_2,\quad (i,j)\notin E_{\mathrm{meas}}
\]

**Units:** \(D\) has units of meters; \(D^2\) has units of square meters; \(X\) coordinates have units of meters.  
**Assumptions:** The subgraph is rigid; measured edges are sufficiently accurate; multilateration error can be reduced by iterative geometric consistency.  
**Breakdown regime:** If the subgraph is not rigid, completion is ambiguous. If measured edges contain large NLOS bias, the completion objective fits the bias rather than the true geometry.

7. **Classical MDS operator**

The source states the MDS procedure as:

\[
B = -\frac{1}{2}JD^2J
\]

\[
J = I - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}
\]

\[
B = Q\Lambda Q^{T}
\]

\[
X = Q\sqrt{\Lambda}
\]

[PAGE 8]. The loss function is:

\[
L(X) = \|XX^{T}-B\|_F
\]

or equivalently:

\[
L(X) = \left\|XX^{T} + \frac{1}{2}JD^{2}J\right\|_F
\]

[PAGE 8].

**Units:** \(D^2\) is in m\(^2\); \(B\) is in m\(^2\); \(X\) is in meters; \(\Lambda\) has units of m\(^2\) for retained dimensions.  
**Assumptions:** The EDM is sufficiently complete; eigenvalues are nonnegative enough to form a real embedding; centering is appropriate.  
**Breakdown regime:** Noise can produce negative eigenvalues, making \(\sqrt{\Lambda}\) undefined without truncation. The source notes: “The CMDS can result in local minima, which are more likely to occur, when the dimension is low (2 as in our case)” [PAGE 8]. If the EDM is incomplete or biased, CMDS may converge to a geometrically inconsistent embedding.

8. **Absolute transformation operator**

DynoLoc transforms relative coordinates into absolute coordinates by translation, rotation, and possible flip. A compact formalization is:

\[
x_{\mathrm{abs}} = F R(\theta)x_{\mathrm{rel}} + t
\]

where \(R(\theta)\in SO(2)\) is a rotation, \(F\) is either the identity or a reflection across an axis, and \(t\in\mathbb{R}^2\) is translation. The source says: “DynoLoc needs to transform the localization solution from a relative coordinate system to a target absolute coordinate system. This is achieved by translating, rotating, and flipping the rigid graph derived in the previous step with the help of IMUs (on the nodes) or floormap information” [PAGE 8].

The rotation angle is derived from heading and edge slope:

\[
\theta = \theta_{23} - \theta'_{23}
\]

[PAGE 15]. The flip correction uses vertex ordering relative to the centroid.

**Units:** \(\theta\), \(\theta_{23}\), and \(\theta'_{23}\) are in radians; \(t\) and \(x_{\mathrm{abs}}\) are in meters.  
**Assumptions:** Heading estimates are meaningful; node motion direction approximates heading when moving; a reference node or floorplan is available.  
**Breakdown regime:** If heading is noisy, if nodes move sideways or backward, if magnetic heading is disturbed, or if the flip test fails, the absolute transform can add large errors. The source reports: “Conversion of relative to absolute localization incurs an additional error of 10–20%” [PAGE 10].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of DynoLoc is a convergence of three older technical traditions: anchor-based ranging, distance-geometry embedding, and graph rigidity theory. Anchor-based localization begins with the assumption that known reference points make localization easy once ranges are available. The source says: “Given a technology to perform accurate ranging, localization can be seen as a trivial extension” [PAGE 3]. This historical framing explains why much prior work focused on WiFi fingerprinting, antenna arrays, ultrasonic beacons, and UWB anchors. But the paper’s target environment removes the anchor assumption. That creates a retrocaual genealogy: the older anchor-based intuition that “ranging gives localization” must be replaced by the harder problem of inferring geometry from relative ranges alone.

The second lineage is distance geometry. The source says: “If all possible ranges between nodes are available, computing the relative localization is straight-forward in a static environment. The edge weights of the graph are maintained in the form of an adjacency matrix, EDM ... Then, an approach called Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) is applied on the EDM matrix” [PAGE 4]. This lineage inherits strong assumptions: complete graphs, accurate ranges, static nodes. The paper’s contribution is to break those assumptions by using rigidity and mobility.

The third lineage is graph rigidity. The paper cites Eren et al. and Dokmanic et al., and it explicitly uses k-core subgraphs. The key historical trap is that many network localization methods treat missing ranges as a matrix-completion problem. DynoLoc’s genealogy argues that matrix completion without geometric rigidity is insufficient. The source says: “matrix completion methods (e.g., SDR [40], OptSpace [41]) are used to complete the EDM before the nodes can be relatively localized. However, the latter are not designed keeping in mind the geometrical implications relevant to a localization problem” [PAGE 5].

The multi-week traps are concrete. The first trap is assuming a complete topology. In practice, “the physical communication range between nodes will limit the topology from being complete” [PAGE 5]. The second trap is ignoring latency. A system that waits for all ranges may be geometrically complete but temporally stale. The third trap is ignoring link quality. If NLOS edges are included, “inaccurate estimates of even a small set of ranges can lead to degraded accuracy for the entire topology” [PAGE 5]. The fourth trap is using CMDS on a noisy or incomplete EDM without checking rigidity. The fifth trap is ignoring absolute transformation error, especially heading and flip ambiguity.

Retrocaually, DynoLoc’s design is an answer to these traps. It does not try to force a complete EDM. It builds rigid subgraphs, completes them individually, and then combines them. It does not treat ranging as a passive measurement process. It treats ranging as a scheduling problem governed by mobility, link quality, and rigidity. This is why the paper’s historical position is not just “another UWB localization system”; it is a reframing of infrastructure-free localization as latency-bounded geometric inference.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind here concerns how responder behavior, system scheduling, and latency interact under crisis conditions. In a first-responder setting, personnel do not move like idealized benchmark nodes. They adopt crisis habits: crawling, pausing, hugging walls, clustering, moving through corridors, and orienting their bodies in ways that create NLOS links. These habits are not merely noise; they are structured behavior that changes the topology and link quality. If the localization system models only raw mobility without understanding this structured habit regression, it may over-trust stale edges or fail to anticipate sudden changes in connectivity.

Crisis habit regression means that under stress, responders revert to familiar patterns. They may follow known routes, stop in doorways, or group together. In graph terms, this can cause sudden transitions from a well-connected 3-core topology to a fragile 2-core or 1-core topology. DynoLoc’s counter-trap is to continuously re-estimate core decomposition. The source says: “DynoLoc identifies the maximal 3-core sub-graphs by starting with the connectivity graph and partitioning it into k-core subgraphs for k = 1,2 and 3 sequentially” [PAGE 6]. This prevents the system from assuming that yesterday’s rigid topology remains rigid during a crisis.

The counter-trap also includes link quality. In a fire drill, smoke, darkness, and body orientation can degrade UWB channel conditions. The source’s link quality metric is a direct counter-trap to naive ranging: “Lij captures the potential accuracy of ranging on the link based on the certainty of it being a LOS (direct) or NLOS (indirect) path” [PAGE 6]. If responders habitually orient their bodies toward each other, some links become temporarily reliable; if they turn away or crouch behind obstructions, links become unreliable. The scheduler must adapt.

The clock is the third recursive variable. Crisis behavior compresses decision time. The fire chief needs real-time location, not delayed post-hoc reconstruction. The source emphasizes refresh rate: “Accurate location of the nodes needs to be tracked at least every second (i.e. refresh rate of 1 Hz) for a node mobility of 1-1.5 m/s” [PAGE 5]. If the system’s internal clock is overcommitted by ranging overhead, the operator’s mental model and the system’s location model diverge. This is a recursive failure: the operator acts on stale locations, responders move according to those instructions, and the next localization cycle inherits the resulting topology change.

The recursive loop is therefore:

1. Responder habit changes mobility and NLOS.
2. Link quality and mobility metrics update.
3. Edge selection chooses critical ranges.
4. Ranging produces a new topology.
5. Rigid embedding updates locations.
6. Operator and responder behavior update again.

The failure mode is when the system’s recursive model is too slow. If the ranging schedule misses the refresh deadline, the loop breaks. The paper’s mitigation is aggregated and concurrent ranging: “It aggregates (and amortizes), the process of ranging (and associated overhead) for a node with all its neighbors into a single compacted process, while links that are spatially separated, can enable such ranging concurrently” [PAGE 2]. This is the temporal counter-trap. Without it, the system becomes a slow observer of a fast crisis.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof must show that physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints can invalidate an algorithmic claim even if the algorithm is internally consistent.

**1. Staleness veto: mobility versus refresh rate**

Let \(v_{\max}\) be the maximum node speed and \(T\) be the interval between localization updates. If no motion prediction is used, and the last known position is \(\hat{x}(t_0)\), then at time \(t=t_0+T\), the true position satisfies:

\[
\|x(t_0+T)-\hat{x}(t_0)\| \leq v_{\max}T
\]

and, in the worst case with unknown direction, the uncertainty radius is \(v_{\max}T\). If the target hard bound is \(e_{\mathrm{target}}\), then a necessary condition for a worst-case bound is:

\[
v_{\max}T \leq e_{\mathrm{target}}
\]

For \(v_{\max}=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(T=1\ \mathrm{s}\), and \(e_{\mathrm{target}}=2\ \mathrm{m}\), the bound is exactly marginal. Any additional latency makes the worst-case staleness exceed 2 m. Therefore, if the system claims a hard 2 m guarantee at 2 m/s with a 1 Hz refresh, it must either use motion prediction, reduce epoch latency below 1 s, or accept that only median error is guaranteed. The source reports median accuracy, not worst-case accuracy: “median localization error of under 1 m (2 m)” [PAGE 3]. This distinction matters for safety-critical use.

**2. Biomechanical veto: body-induced NLOS bias**

Human bodies attenuate and reflect UWB signals. If a link is body-blocked, the measured range can be biased. Let the measured range be:

\[
\tilde{d}_{ij}=\|x_i-x_j\|_2 + b_{ij}
\]

where \(b_{ij}\) is a NLOS bias in meters. If the selected edge has \(b_{ij}\neq 0\) and the EDM treats \(\tilde{d}_{ij}\) as exact, the embedding cannot satisfy all measured edges simultaneously. The resulting localization error is at least on the order of the induced geometric inconsistency. The source acknowledges this: “two key environmental factors, namely LOS blockages (due to body, concrete, etc.), and node mobility, can significantly affect the accuracy of the range estimates” [PAGE 5]. DynoLoc’s link quality metric is a countermeasure, and the paper reports: “introducing the L metric improves accuracy by 30 – 40% in two different NLOS scenarios” [PAGE 10]. But if link quality detection fails, the veto stands: NLOS-biased edges can dominate the embedding.

**3. Multi-floor veto: unmodeled vertical separation**

DynoLoc localizes in 2D. If nodes on different floors are connected through a stairwell and the system treats their ranges as horizontal 2D ranges, the model is inconsistent. Suppose two nodes have true horizontal separation \(r\) and vertical floor separation \(h\). The true 3D distance is:

\[
d_{\mathrm{3D}}=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}
\]

If the algorithm treats this as a 2D distance, the estimated horizontal separation becomes:

\[
r_{\mathrm{est}}=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}
\]

The horizontal error is:

\[
\varepsilon_h = \sqrt{r^2+h^2}-r
\]

At \(r=0\), the error equals \(h\). For a two-floor building with substantial floor height, this is a large systematic error. DynoLoc recognizes this and uses pressure sensors: “To determine the the current floor, the reading from the pressure sensors ... in conjunction with the available UWB connectivity information are used by DynoLoc” [PAGE 15]. If pressure sensing fails, the multi-floor veto applies.

**4. Weather-like environmental veto: pressure drift and atmospheric change**

Weather is not the primary indoor channel, but atmospheric pressure is a weather-coupled physical variable. The source says pressure data is used for floor identification and that longer-period changes “typically ≥20 minutes, which signifies atmospheric pressure change” [PAGE 15]. If an algorithm interprets atmospheric pressure drift as a floor change, it can switch localization frames incorrectly. The veto condition is:

\[
\Delta p_{\mathrm{atm}} \approx \Delta p_{\mathrm{floor}}
\]

within sensor resolution. If this occurs, the floor classifier can misclassify the current level. This is not a ranging error per se, but it is a cross-layer physical veto because it corrupts the absolute coordinate frame.

**Adjudication**

The physical veto is decisive when any of the following holds:

\[
T_{\mathrm{epoch}} > T_{\mathrm{refresh}}
\]

or

\[
G_{\mathrm{selected}} \text{ is not a 3-core}
\]

or

\[
p_{\mathrm{NLOS},ij} \text{ is high for selected edges}
\]

or

\[
\text{floor classification is ambiguous}
\]

In those cases, the algorithmic solution may be mathematically valid but physically invalid. The paper’s design reduces these vetoes through adaptive topology estimation, aggregated/concurrent ranging, rigid-subgraph EDM completion, and pressure-based floor identification, but it does not eliminate them.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

**Veto 1: Latency-overrun veto**

Counterfactual policy: range all selected edges sequentially without aggregated or concurrent ranging.

Formal veto condition:

\[
\sum_{e\in E_{\mathrm{selected}}}\tau_{\mathrm{range}} > T_{\mathrm{refresh}}
\]

For 12 nodes at 1 Hz, a complete graph has 66 edges. At 40 ms per range, the required time is:

\[
66 \times 0.040\ \mathrm{s} = 2.64\ \mathrm{s}
\]

This exceeds the 1 s refresh interval. For 20 nodes, the complete graph has 190 edges, requiring:

\[
190 \times 0.040\ \mathrm{s} = 7.60\ \mathrm{s}
\]

Therefore, a policy that ignores aggregation and concurrency is vetoed.

Verifiable prediction: if the controller logs epoch duration and the selected edge count exceeds the capacity implied by \(T_{\mathrm{refresh}}/\tau_{\mathrm{range}}\), the refresh rate will fall below 1 Hz. In that regime, localization error should degrade toward the baseline range reported by the paper: “existing solutions suffer in accuracy (6m+ error) even for a node mobility of 1 m/s” [PAGE 3]. If the counterfactual system instead uses aggregated TWR, the epoch time is bounded by \((2N+2)\tau_{\mathrm{slot}}\), and the veto is lifted only if that value remains below \(T_{\mathrm{refresh}}\).

**Veto 2: Rigidity-starvation veto**

Counterfactual policy: select edges only by mobility, or only by link quality, without ensuring maximal 3-core subgraphs.

Formal veto condition:

\[
V(G_{\mathrm{selected}})\not\supseteq V(G_{\mathrm{3-core}})
\]

or, more operationally, after core decomposition:

\[
\deg_{G_{\mathrm{selected}}}(v) < 3
\]

for nodes that should be in the rigid component.

The source’s ablation supports this veto. H-Agnos uses DynoLoc’s relative localization but “adopts a naive edge selection approach (ranges every pair of possible edges through round-robin) that does not account for node dynamics and link quality” [PAGE 9]. H-Dyn “accounts for node mobility by ranging on edges incident with the most dynamic nodes, but disregards the geometric rigidity requirement in its relative localization” [PAGE 9]. The measured result is: “H-Agnos and H-Dyn incur an error of 6–7 meters and 5–6 meters respectively” when all nodes are mobile at 1 m/s [PAGE 9].

Verifiable prediction: if the selected edge set fails to preserve a 3-core, the relative localization becomes non-unique. In controlled tests, the error should rise from DynoLoc’s sub-2 m regime to the 5–7 m regime unless additional IMU or floorplan constraints remove the ambiguity. A reproducible test is to disable core decomposition and force random edge selection; the resulting error should be materially worse than the full DynoLoc policy.

**Veto 3: Global EDM completion under incomplete topology veto**

Counterfactual policy: complete the entire EDM with OptSpace, SDR, or a generic low-rank method, without first restricting completion to rigid 3-core subgraphs.

Formal veto condition:

\[
\text{EDM is incomplete and non-rigid over the full graph}
\]

or:

\[
\operatorname{rank}(D^2) > 4
\]

for a 2D embedding after accounting for centering and noise, without a rigidity guarantee.

The source says: “DynoLoc performs EDM completion only on the rigid sub-graphs (3-core sub-graphs) of the topology, individually. The rigidity of the sub-graph (say, with n nodes) enables a more accurate EDM completion of n × n” [PAGE 7]. It also reports: “DynoLoc’s EDM-completion approach is able to bound the range estimation (for missing entries) to within 2m in most cases” [PAGE 10].

Verifiable prediction: if global completion is used on an incomplete, non-rigid topology, missing-range error will exceed the 2 m bound observed for rigid-subgraph completion, and relative localization error will grow with the number of missing edges. In an active-shooter geofencing test, this error becomes safety-relevant because “even a 2 m median localization error can lead to a few hundred sq. ft. of exposed area” [PAGE 11]. Therefore, the RLVR reward should veto any policy that permits global completion when the 3-core condition is not satisfied. The verifiable metric is the estimated range error for missing edges; if it exceeds 2 m in repeated trials, the policy is rejected.

</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
