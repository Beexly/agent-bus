<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The "what changed since yesterday" feed descends from three lineages. First, activity-feed HCI: mid-2000s social streams and mobile "What's New" modals established the expectation that a digital surface should summarize delta since last visit. Second, software changelog practice (e.g., Keep a Changelog), which separates human-readable change notes from internal commit hashes. Third, information-foraging theory, where users follow "scent of information" cues to decide whether a page contains relevant updates. [PAGE 1] operationalizes this lineage through its Copy Direction: "Human, direct, grounded, no fake certainty, no method leakage, no hype without proof," and its Public/Private Boundary, which mirrors changelog convention by exposing only summary cues to the public while reserving source weights for founder/admin.

Multi-week traps are specific to this lineage. Week 1 typically shows a novelty effect: users notice the feed because it is new. Week 2–3 risks staleness if the home and Player Lab surfaces do not genuinely change; an unchanging "what changed" feed becomes a credibility liability, contradicting [PAGE 1] business-value goals of trust and activation. Week 4+ risks banner blindness: if every trivial copy edit is marked "changed," users learn to ignore the feed, destroying 10-second clarity. Another trap is dependency rot: because this card extends BUILD-GV2-021, upstream drift in help/growth/design/trust work can invalidate assumptions made here without a corresponding version bump. A final trap is "last-verified cue decay": [PAGE 1] requires source cues where appropriate, but if those timestamps are not themselves kept current, the feed signals freshness it does not possess, violating the "confidence-safe language" mandate.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The user model is recursive: a visitor arrives at home or Player Lab and asks, "What changed since I was last here?" while simultaneously modeling what the product team wants them to notice. The feed must answer the first-order question without exposing second-order internal mechanics.

Crisis habit regression: under P0 execution pressure, the implementation team may regress to the easiest artifact—raw diffs, component version strings, or internal edit timestamps—because writing "reveal-less explanations" is cognitively harder than surfacing a changelog. [PAGE 1] explicitly rates method leakage as MEDIUM, so this regression is a live risk: a crisis habit of showing "what changed" in engineering terms leaks founder-only methodology to the public boundary.

Counter-trap: performative transparency. The team may mark trivial copy tweaks or token renames as "changed" to keep the feed looking active. This trains users to treat the feed as noise (the cry-wolf effect), which collapses the mutual-information condition \( H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \ge \gamma \) within \( \Delta t_{\text{clarity}} \le 10\ \text{s} \). Once users dismiss the feed, the trust and activation business value on [PAGE 1] is forfeited.

Clock constraint: the refresh cadence \( \tau_{\text{refresh}} \) must be coupled to the staleness operator \( \mathcal{A}(c) \). If \( \tau_{\text{refresh}} \) is much shorter than the typical inter-change interval, the feed polls empty updates and wastes the performance budget; if much longer, it misses its purpose and staleness cues age past \( \tau_{\text{stale}} \). The clock therefore operates as a governor: it must align \( \tau_{\text{refresh}} \) with observed \( \mathcal{A}(c) \) so the feed is neither spam nor silent.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: [PAGE 1] requires mobile layout works and reduced-motion support. Define \( V_{\text{phys}} = 1 \) if the feed component breaks on mobile viewports, ignores prefers-reduced-motion, or disrupts calm reading states. Breakdown regime: horizontal overflow, unclamped animations, or cinematic motion that cannot be disabled triggers \( V_{\text{phys}} = 1 \).

Biomechanical layer veto: [PAGE 1] requires accessibility basics pass and keyboard/focus checks. Define \( V_{\text{bio}} = 1 \) if \( CR < 4.5 \) for normal text or \( CR < 3 \) for large text (per the contrast equation in SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS), or if Fitts' law target width \( W < 44\ \text{CSS px} \), or if focus order traps a keyboard user. Breakdown regime: missing visible focus indicators, tap targets below 44 CSS px, or screen-reader-unlabeled "changed" icons trigger \( V_{\text{bio}} = 1 \).

Weather layer veto: network conditions are variable. Define \( V_{\text{weather}} = 1 \) if the feed lacks loading, empty, and error states, or if polling fails under intermittent connectivity and degrades the shell route. Breakdown regime: a hung spinner with no error fallback, or a poll that blocks route navigation on slow links, triggers \( V_{\text{weather}} = 1 \).

Composite veto: \( V = V_{\text{phys}} \lor V_{\text{bio}} \lor V_{\text{weather}} \). If \( V = 1 \), the change must not merge; rollback is isolated to the component/route/style per [PAGE 1], with no production data rollback required. Because [PAGE 1] rates Performance risk LOW but Legal risk LOW and Method leakage MEDIUM, the physical/biomechanical vetoes are the hard constraints most likely to fire during implementation.
