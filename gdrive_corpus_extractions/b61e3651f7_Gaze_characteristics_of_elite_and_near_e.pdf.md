<THINK_TRACE>
The audit frame is a live, on-ice vision-in-action study, not a film-based visual-search study. The source states that the authors used “a mobile eye tracker on ice” and that this “is the first attempt to determine the gaze of athletes in a live tactical setting” [PAGE 2]. That matters because the main inferential chain is not simply “elite athletes look longer” or “elite athletes look faster.” The paper tests three rival temporal-regulation hypotheses: early rapid fixations, late long fixations, or a combined “early rapid plus late sustained” cascade [PAGE 5]. The result supports the third: the elite group is distinguished by shorter early-to-mid situation-assessment fixations and both groups converge on a longer final fixation/tracking (F/T-1) before critical movement initiation [PAGE 1]. The abstract describes a “cascade of F/T” beginning with “short durations at the beginning of the play” and concluding with “a final gaze of long duration to a relatively stable target at the end” [PAGE 1]. The discussion then paraphrases this as a “quick then quiet cascade” [PAGE 22].

Multi-hop derivation: first, phase segmentation. The play is partitioned into pattern recognition (PR), situation assessment (SA), and final execution (FE), derived from Klein’s recognition-primed decision model [PAGE 7]. The SA phase is the informative interval: mean SA duration is about 5806.09 ms, far longer than PR at about 488.89 ms and FE at about 705.92 ms [PAGE 10]. Second, reverse-order F/T indexing: F/T-9 is earliest and F/T-1 is latest in SA [PAGE 12]. Third, duration contrast: F/T-1 has mean relative duration 18.9% with SD 13.35, significantly longer than F/T-2 through F/T-9 [PAGE 14]. Fourth, skill contrast: F/T-5 is shorter for elite than near-elite: elite M = 5.83%, SD = 5.07, versus near-elite M = 10.51%, SD = 9.80; in milliseconds, elite 346.74 ms versus near-elite 591.59 ms [PAGE 15]. Fifth, outcome interaction: elite are more successful when they take less time at F/T-5 on plus trials, while near-elite are more successful when they take more time on plus trials [PAGE 15]. The paper interprets this as a processing difference at a critical moment [PAGE 17].

The gray-zone audit is important. The paper’s elite and near-elite groups differ significantly in age and formal training: elite mean age 28.00, near-elite 21.67; elite formal training 11.16 years, near-elite 5.50 years [PAGE 5]. Thus “skill” is confounded with age and accumulated practice. The source also states that all 12 tried out for the Olympic team but none of the near-elite were successful [PAGE 5]. This is not a randomized expertise manipulation; it is a cross-sectional contrast with small n (six per group) and unequal retained trials: 87 trials, 45.3% of the total skated [PAGE 9]. A second gray zone is the no-shot constraint: “No shots were permitted on goal (backswing only)” [PAGE 8]. The authors themselves note that late adaptive gaze “may be found in conditions where this part of offensive play is allowed” [PAGE 19]. A third gray zone is that F/T-9 through F/T-7 are biomechanically contaminated by acceleration: “Fewer F/T were found at the beginning of the phase … due to the vigorous skating which occurred as the participants accelerated from a standing position to full speed at the onset of the SA phase” [PAGE 12]. The same page says gaze stability was “least from F/T-9 to F/T-7 and most stable from F/T-6 to F/T-1” [PAGE 12]. Therefore the defensible signal is the F/T-6 to F/T-1 window, not the entire nine-F/T sequence. A fourth gray zone is the apparent descriptive claim that F/T-1 was longer for elite than near-elite in the Discussion [PAGE 21], while the supplied Results text emphasizes that F/T-1 was significantly longer than earlier F/T, not necessarily a significant elite-versus-near-elite main effect [PAGE 14]. That distinction must be preserved.

Define the phase partition operator \(\Pi\) over trial time \(t\) in milliseconds: \(\Pi(t)=\mathrm{PR}\) on the PR interval, \(\Pi(t)=\mathrm{SA}\) on the SA interval, and \(\Pi(t)=\mathrm{FE}\) on the FE interval, with boundaries set by the first appearance of gaze, first skating movement to defend, first skating movement to engage, and first contact [PAGE 8]. Define phase duration \(D_{\Pi}\) in ms as \(D_{\Pi}=t_{\Pi,\mathrm{offset}}-t_{\Pi,\mathrm{onset}}\). Reported means are \(D_{\mathrm{PR}}=488.89\ \mathrm{ms}\) (SD 166.66), \(D_{\mathrm{SA}}=5806.09\ \mathrm{ms}\) (SD 1120.81), and \(D_{\mathrm{FE}}=705.92\ \mathrm{ms}\) (SD 430.77) [PAGE 10].

Define the reverse F/T index operator \(\rho\): within SA, chronological F/T events are re-labeled so that \(k=9\) is earliest and \(k=1\) is latest, with \(k\in\{1,\ldots,9\}\) when present [PAGE 12]. This operator is not always complete: Table 3 shows zero F/T-9 entries for some athletes, and the authors report that analyses were run “with missing cells and without (using means)” and the “more conservative missing cells results” are reported [PAGE 10]. Therefore any model using all nine levels has a missing-data breakdown regime.

Define onset fraction and duration fraction in percent: \(u_{s,o,k}=100\,t_{s,o,k}/D_{\mathrm{SA}}\) and \(r_{s,o,k}=100\,d_{s,o,k}/D_{\mathrm{SA}}\), where \(t_{s,o,k}\) and \(d_{s,o,k}\) are onset and duration in ms for skill group \(s\), outcome \(o\), and F/T index \(k\). Units: \(u\) and \(r\) are percent; \(t\) and \(d\) are ms; \(D_{\mathrm{SA}}\) is ms. The source states that F/T-1 mean relative duration is \(M=18.9\%\), \(s=13.35\), significantly longer than F/T-2 through F/T-9 [PAGE 14]. The Discussion also describes a “longer F/T duration on a relatively stable location during the final 30% of the phase” [PAGE 17]. These are not identical quantities: 18.9% is a mean duration percentage of SA, while “final 30%” is a late-phase window description. They should not be equated.

Define the gaze-location alphabet \(\mathcal{L}=\{\mathrm{O–D\ combo},\mathrm{offense},\mathrm{defense},\mathrm{positive\ space},\mathrm{negative\ space},\mathrm{puck}\}\), following the six-category reduction described on [PAGE 9]. For location probabilities \(p_{\ell}\), the Shannon entropy is \(H(\mathcal{L})=-\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}p_{\ell}\log_2 p_{\ell}\) in bits; mutual information with outcome \(O\) is \(I(\mathcal{L};O)=H(O)-H(O\mid\mathcal{L})\) in bits; information rate is \(R=I(\mathcal{L};O)/D_{\mathrm{SA}}\) in bits per second. These are formal audit metrics, not quantities reported in the paper. Empirical probabilities have breakdown when \(p_{\ell}=0\) because \(\log_2 0\) is singular; with 87 retained trials [PAGE 9], sparse cells make entropy and mutual-information estimates unstable.

Define the F/T detection criterion from the source: gaze is F/T when stable “for more than 99.9 ms (three or more frames) within 2" of visual angle” [PAGE 9]. At 30 frames per second, \(3f^{-1}=99.9\ \mathrm{ms}\). Saccade minimum is “66.66 ms or two frames” [PAGE 9], so \(2f^{-1}=66.66\ \mathrm{ms}\). Tracker precision is “accuracy of 2" of visual angle, precision of 1"” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown occurs when calibration drift exceeds the 2-degree acceptance window, when head/body movement pushes gaze into the “other” category [PAGE 9], or when the helmet/cable system constrains natural movement: the helmet had “a 30-metre cord attached to the waist” and “a total weight of 700 g” [PAGE 6].

Statistical operators. The paper reports factorial and mixed-model ANOVA with repeated measures, Greenhouse–Geisser epsilon for sphericity, Bonferroni adjustments, and omega squared for multi-mean effects [PAGE 10]. For an effect with sum of squares \(SS_{\mathrm{effect}}\), degrees of freedom \(df_{\mathrm{effect}}\), error mean square \(MS_{\mathrm{error}}\), total sum of squares \(SS_{\mathrm{total}}\), and \(MS_{\mathrm{error}}\), the omega-squared operator is \(\omega^2=(SS_{\mathrm{effect}}-df_{\mathrm{effect}}MS_{\mathrm{error}})/(SS_{\mathrm{total}}+MS_{\mathrm{error}})\), dimensionless. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy runs from closed-skill quiet eye research to open-skill tactical gaze research, then to this live ice-hockey study. The paper inherits the quiet-eye lineage from Vickers: in closed skills such as golf putting, basketball shooting, rifle and pistol shooting, and billiards, elite performers show longer final fixation on a target, with earlier onset and longer duration, and this has been treated as a perception–action variable [PAGE 3]. The paper also inherits the conflict between Helsen and Pauwels and Williams and Davids: Helsen and Pauwels reported lower search rates and longer fixations late in the play, while Williams et al. and Williams and Davids reported more frequent, shorter fixations early in the play [PAGE 4]. The live study is designed to adjudicate that conflict by allowing the whole play to unfold, because film-based protocols pre-select scenes and limit coupling between gaze and action [PAGE 19].

The retrocausal trap is to read the paper backward as if it settled the open-versus-closed taxonomy. It does not. The authors explicitly say the open skill of ice hockey used “a different type of gaze control” from closed skills [PAGE 17], but they also say F/T-1 had only “three of the same characteristics” of quiet eye: longer duration, a single location although moving, and occurrence before final action [PAGE 21]. That is a bridge, not identity. Another multi-week trap is the “ten years of preparation” lineage: the paper cites Bloom, Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Romer, and Simon and Chase for the minimum-preparation claim [PAGE 5], but the design is cross-sectional and the groups differ in age and training [PAGE 5]. A third trap is film-to-live overgeneralization: the live setting started as a 2-on-3 situation and evolved to 1-on-1 [PAGE 19], so prior 11-on-11, 3-on-3, and 1-on-1 film findings are not direct replicates. A fourth trap is outcome-label genealogy: plus/minus trials were retained only when two independent experts agreed 100%, and only 87 trials remained [PAGE 9], so the outcome contrast is expert-rated and selection-filtered, not a full population of defensive plays.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit-regression account is this: under temporal pressure, a less-expert performer falls back on a habitual search policy that is locally safe but globally costly. The near-elite F/T-5 pattern fits that reading. Near-elite are more successful when they take more time on plus trials: \(M=13.58\%\), \(s=12.38\), \(M=770.15\ \mathrm{ms}\), \(s=750.43\); on minus trials they are shorter: \(M=7.59\%\), \(s=5.38\), \(M=436.32\ \mathrm{ms}\), \(s=360\) [PAGE 15]. The paper concludes that elite are more successful when they take less time at the principal locations, while near-elite are more successful when they take more time, “suggesting processing differences distinguished the two skill groups at this critical moment during the play” [PAGE 17]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is not that near-elite are “panicking”; it is that their successful state requires a slower clock, and when the clock is unfavorable they may abandon the cue before it is fully processed.

The counter-trap is the elite policy: shorter F/T-6 and F/T-5 early-to-mid SA, followed by a longer F/T-1 that isolates a final target. Elite F/T-6 is heavily directed to the O–D combo at 65.6%, versus near-elite 27.0%, while near-elite allocate 32.4% to negative space [PAGE 16]. At F/T-1 both groups converge on the offensive player and puck: elite 34.9% and 41.8%; near-elite 45.5% and 43.2%; no F/T to the defensive player in either group at F/T-6, F/T-5, or F/T-1 [PAGE 16]. That is the “counter-trap”: do not over-monitor irrelevant space; compress early search and isolate the final object. The clock layer is phase duration and relative onset. Elite and near-elite did not differ significantly in PR, SA, or FE phase durations [PAGE 18], and onsets of F/T did not differ by skill or outcome [PAGE 13]. Thus the difference is not a gross skating-speed clock; it is a micro-clock of dwell time within SA. The recursive model is: clock pressure raises the cost of late cue acquisition; near-elite success depends on having enough time; elite success depends on buying time by rapid early encoding and then stabilizing on the final target. The breakdown regime is when the final object is moving unpredictably or when shots are allowed; the paper did not permit shots, so the final “quiet” target was a moving offensive player or puck, not a shot-stopping event [PAGE 21].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The measurement system itself can veto a clean perceptual interpretation. The mobile tracker was a monocular corneal-reflection system with miniaturized scene and eye cameras, an illuminator, and a visor mounted on a helmet, with “a total weight of 700 g” and “a 30-metre cord attached to the waist” [PAGE 6]. The gaze cursor had “accuracy of 2" of visual angle, precision of 1"” [PAGE 6]. F/T was coded only when gaze was stable “for more than 99.9 ms (three or more frames) within 2" of visual angle” [PAGE 9]. If helmet mass or cable drag increases head instability, the F/T duration distribution is biased downward, especially during acceleration. The source itself reports that stability was “least from F/T-9 to F/T-7 and most stable from F/T-6 to F/T-1” and attributes the early instability partly to “vigorous skating” and acceleration from standing to full speed [PAGE 12]. Therefore a physical/biomechanical veto applies to any claim that F/T-9 through F/T-7 are pure perceptual expertise markers. The defensible window is F/T-6 to F/T-1.

Biomechanical veto. The task begins with the participant standing with eyes closed at center ice, then responding to a whistle and skating rapidly to gain defensive position [PAGE 8]. The SA phase requires “scanning O1, O2, D1, Dt, the puck, and other locations on the ice” while skating [PAGE 8]. This is a dual-task gaze-stabilization problem. The paper’s own missing F/T at the start of SA and the “other” category for excessive head and body movement [PAGE 9] are biomechanical failure modes. A biomechanical veto would trigger if a replication showed that F/T-6 and F/T-5 differences disappear when head stabilization is externally supported or when skating acceleration is matched. That would not disprove expertise; it would relocate the effect from perception to gaze-stabilization capacity.

Weather/ice-layer veto. The source does not report air temperature, humidity, ice-surface temperature, barometric pressure, or rink microclimate. It is an on-ice hockey study with a mobile tracker [PAGE 1, PAGE 6], but no meteorological covariates are given. Therefore weather cannot be adjudicated from this source; it is an unmeasured layer, not a proven null. Any claim that weather or ice quality explains the elite-versus-near-elite F/T contrast would be unsupported. The only defensible cross-layer statement is that the study is ecologically live but physically instrumented, and its final action is truncated: “No shots were permitted on goal (backswing only)” [PAGE 8]. The authors concede that late adaptive gaze “may be found in conditions where this part of offensive play is allowed” [PAGE 19]. That is a validity boundary, not a refutation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto 1: shot-on-goal counterfactual. The source truncates the final action with “No shots were permitted on goal (backswing only)” [PAGE 8] and speculates that adaptive late gaze may require shots to be allowed [PAGE 19]. A verifiable RLVR test would add a live shot-on-goal condition and score the primary endpoint \(d_{\mathrm{F/T-1}}\) and a secondary endpoint: latency from F/T-1 offset to a corrective saccade or tracking adjustment toward the shot. Prediction: elite retain the shorter F/T-5 and longer F/T-1 cascade and show equal or faster late adjustment than near-elite. Veto: if elite lose the F/T-5 advantage or fail to show a late adjustment under shot permission, then the original cascade is partly an artifact of the no-shot constraint and the ecological claim is vetoed.

Veto 2: age-and-training matched counterfactual. The source reports elite mean age 28.00 versus near-elite 21.67, and elite formal training 11.16 years versus near-elite 5.50 years [PAGE 5]. A verifiable RLVR test would match or stratify elite and near-elite on age and formal training, or use a within-participant longitudinal design. The endpoint would be the F/T-5 plus-trial contrast: elite \(M=4.72\%\), \(s=2.12\), \(277.83\ \mathrm{ms}\), \(s=131.38\), versus near-elite \(M=13.58\%\), \(s=12.38\), \(770.15\ \mathrm{ms}\), \(s=750.43\) [PAGE 15]. Prediction: elite remain shorter than near-elite after matching. Veto: if the F/T-5 difference disappears or reverses after age/training matching, the “expertise” interpretation is vetoed and replaced by an age/training or accumulated-practice explanation.

Veto 3: temporal-compression and occlusion counterfactual. The source’s clock claim is that elite buy time by rapid early encoding and then stabilize on a final target, while near-elite succeed when given more time [PAGE 17]. A verifiable RLVR test would compress SA duration, occlude late visual field, or manipulate the time available after the first defensive read. The primary endpoint would be the sign of the plus-minus difference in F/T-5 duration within each group. Prediction: elite maintain the pattern of shorter F/T-5 on plus than minus, while near-elite’s advantage from longer plus-trial F/T-5 collapses under compression. Veto: if elite’s plus-minus pattern flips under compression, or if near-elite do not lose their longer-time advantage, then the temporal-pressure account is vetoed. A second prediction is that F/T-1 remains longer than F/T-2 through F/T-9 across compression, as reported at baseline [PAGE 14]; if F/T-1 loses its relative dominance under occlusion, the “quick then quiet” cascade is not robust.
