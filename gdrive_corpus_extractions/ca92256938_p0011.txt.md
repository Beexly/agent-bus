\[
\hat{Q}(P) = \hat{q}(p_{(k)}) + \frac{\hat{q}(p_{(k+1)}) - \hat{q}(p_{(k)})}{p_{(k+1)} - p_{(k)}} (P - p_{(k)}), \quad P \in [p_{(k)}, p_{(k+1)}].

[PAGE 9]: "plot the determined demand function—price versus quantity—in a piecewise linear form."

3. Weighted multi-criterion objective. Let \(\pi(P) = \hat{Q}(P)\,(P - c)\) be profit with unit variable cost \(c\), and let \(S(P)\) be a market-share criterion. The weighted objective:
\[
J(P) = w_\pi \,\pi(P) + w_S \,S(P), \qquad w_\pi + w_S = 1, \quad w_\pi, w_S \in [0,1].
\]

4. Profit maximization with accounting interface:
\[
P^* = \underset{P \in \mathcal{P}_{\text{feas}}}{\arg\max}\; \hat{Q}(P)\,(P - c),
\]
where \(\mathcal{P}_{\text{feas}}\) is the feasible price interval restricted by merchant cost floor and loss limits. [PAGE 5]: "By interfacing to appropriate accounting systems to determine variable cost, the objective of maximizing profit (Quantity \ (price—variable cost)) can be accomplished in real-time." <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The patent's intellectual lineage traces to classical price discrimination and empirical demand estimation. Pigou (1920) differentiated first-, second-, and third-degree price discrimination; this system operationalizes second-degree (menu of price points) and third-degree (segmented customer clusters) discrimination via A/B testing. The continuous sampling engine anticipates Thompson sampling and multi-armed bandit frameworks (Robbins, 1952; Lai & Robbins, 1985), where exploration across price arms trades off against exploitation of the current best price. The piecewise linear demand plot echoes early econometric demand-curve estimation (Marshallian partial equilibrium), but replaces structural estimation with nonparametric, real-time sampling.

Multi-week traps inherent in the architecture:

1. Demand non-stationarity trap. Experiments run "monthly, weekly, daily, or more often" ([PAGE 9]). Over multi-week horizons, competitor pricing, seasonality, inventory depletion, and macroeconomic shifts move the underlying demand curve \(Q(P,t)\). A price optimized in week 1 may be sub-optimal by week 3, yet the system's re-sampling cadence may lag the shift, causing persistent revenue leakage between experiment cycles.

2. Strategic customer anticipation trap. If customers learn that prices are dynamically varied, rational buyers delay purchases awaiting lower price samples. This induces a downward bias in observed demand at higher price points and an upward bias at lower points, systematically shifting the estimated argmax \(P^*\) downward relative to the true myopic demand optimum.

3. Segment spillover trap. Segmented pricing (heavy/light buyers, socioeconomic clusters) ([PAGE 9]) creates arbitrage and reference-price effects: customers in a high-price segment may observe lower prices offered to another segment, triggering perceived unfairness, churn, or cross-segment purchasing, invalidating the assumption of independent segment demand functions.

4. Early-stopping statistical trap. Permitting termination when "a particular price point is optimal" before planned completion ([PAGE 9]) capitalizes on random noise. With multiple price points compared sequentially, the probability of falsely declaring a spurious price optimal rises with the number of looks, degrading long-run profit across repeated weekly experiments.

5. Cost interface latency trap. Real-time profit maximization requires variable cost \(c\) from accounting systems ([PAGE 5]). If cost data updates slower than price experiments (e.g., weekly cost feeds vs. hourly pricing), the objective \(\hat{Q}(P)(P - c)\) uses stale \(c\), mispricing relative to true marginal economics.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
