The source passage [PAGE 434] presents William James’s two-stage critique of associationist psychology as applied to miserliness and collecting. First, James distinguishes misers who value gold not for itself (“in se”) but for the “power” it confers—specifically, the power to live rich while avoiding the risk of losing that power. The operative preference is “better the actual evil than the fear of it,” which inverts the usual hedonistic ordering: present poverty is tolerated to escape anticipatory dread of future loss. This already defeats a simple associationist account, because if value were built up by contiguity between gold and sensory pleasure, the miser would seek to spend or enjoy the gold rather than hoard it unused.

Second, James introduces a distinct population of misers in whom the preference for power over act is compounded by “the simple collecting propensity.” Here the associationist explanation fails entirely: hoarding is traced to an instinct shared with animals. The evidentiary pivot is Professor Silliman’s description of a California wood-rat’s hoard [PAGE 434], in which an empty stove contains symmetrically laid spikes pointing outward, a nest of hemp fibres, and an interlaced assemblage of cutlery, butcher’s knives, tobacco, an old purse with silver, tools, augers, and—crucially—the separated components of a silver watch: casing in one part, glass in another, works in a third. The spatial separation of functionally united watch parts implies a categorical or structural collecting operator that cannot be reduced to learned associations with edible or utilitarian objects. The rat transports these items “some distance,” incurring physical cost for zero caloric return, which breaks any purely energetic or associationist utility model.

James then extends the instinct to human pathology in lunatic asylums [PAGE 434], where patients spend “all their time picking pins from the floor and hoarding them,” or collect buttons, thread, and rags which they “prize exceedingly.” The popular “Miser” of melodrama, “the monster of squalor and misanthropy,” is identified with these mentally deranged persons whose “intellect may in many matters be clear” but whose instincts are captured by the hoarding compulsion. This yields a dissociation: cognitive clarity coexists with conative pathology.

Gray-zone audit. The text never specifies a threshold separating normal collecting from miserly pathology—no quantity, duration, or degree of social impairment is given—so the boundary between adaptive instinct and disorder is underdetermined. The wood-rat’s symmetry (spikes laid “with symmetry”) is offered as instinctive evidence without experimental controls isolating genetic from environmental influences. The miser’s counterfactual preference—“better to live poor now, with the power of living rich”—is never behaviorally tested because the power is never actualized; it may be a post-hoc rationalization of a compulsive posture rather than a stable utility function. Finally, James retrocausally treats the miser’s rationalization (“gold = power”) as secondary to the hoarding instinct, but he does not rule out that the rationalization subsequently reinforces the behavior, creating a feedback loop the text leaves open.

Let o ∈ O denote an object (coin, pin, watch part). The associationist valuation operator is
V_assoc(o) = Σ_i w_i · κ(o, q_i)
where q_i are sensory qualities, κ is a contiguity/similarity kernel, and w_i are learned weights; units are dimensionless preference weights. James’s refutation: for misers, ∂V/∂sensory_qualities ≈ 0 because “they care nothing for the gold in se” [PAGE 434].

Define the power/command potential operator
P(o) = sup_{a∈A} [ U(a | o controlled) − U(a | o not controlled) ],
measured in decision-relevant utility units (utils or bits of control information). Define actualization/use utility A(o) = U(use(o)). The miser’s preference ordering is P(o) ≫ A(o), even when P(o) is never exercised. The text’s dictum “Better to live poor now, with the power of living rich, than to live rich at the risk of losing the power” [PAGE 434] formalizes as a choice of low present A(o) over high anticipated variance in P(o).

The collecting operator C maps an environment E over time T to a hoard H = {h_1,…,h_n}. For the wood-rat hoard [PAGE 434], H contains disjoint watch components W = {casing, glass, works} with spatial partition Π = {P_1, P_2, P_3} such that d(casing, glass) > 0 and d(glass, works) > 0. Transport work W_transport = ∫ F · dx > 0 (joules) is expended for items with zero caloric value, yielding negative net energy balance.

(2) The pure instinct model breaks under extinction: if collecting persists with zero reinforcement over intervals T_extinction → ∞ (e.g., asylum patients hoarding valueless pins for weeks), the behavior is compulsive rather than adaptively regulated. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Lineage. James’s chapter (Principles of Psychology, Vol. 2, 1890) stands in the retrocausal shadow of British associationism—Locke, Hume, Hartley, James Mill, Bain, and Spencer—who explained complex conduct by the chaining of simple ideas through contiguity and frequency. James reverses the vector: the hoarding instinct is phylogenetically primitive and antecedent; the miser’s doctrine that gold represents “power” is a secondary rationalization imposed upon an already-existing conative discharge. The immediate scientific source for the animal comparison is Professor Silliman’s wood-rat observation, quoted in Lindsay, Mind in Lower Animals, vol. I, p. 151 [PAGE 434], itself part of late-19th-century comparative psychology’s attempt to locate instinct across species.

Multi-week traps. Hoarding is temporally extended. The wood-rat transports objects “some distance” [PAGE 434], implying repeated trips over days or weeks. Asylum patients “spend all their time” pin-picking [PAGE 434], a behavior that captures attention across weeks without consumption. The miser’s trap spans a lifetime: the hoard is never spent, so the original adaptive problem (resource scarcity) is absent while the behavior persists. The trap is that the behavior outlives its eliciting context, converting an instinct into a closed-loop compulsion that reorganizes the subject’s entire economy of time and attention.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under threat of loss, the miser regresses to hoarding as a primitive crisis response. The preference “better the actual evil than the fear of it” [PAGE 434] reveals that anticipatory dread dominates experienced reality; present poverty is accepted to avoid the subjective simulation of future dispossession.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the inversion of means and ends: hoarding is undertaken to preserve “the power of living rich,” yet by never actualizing that power the miser forfeits the very end he sought to protect. He lives poor to avoid the risk of losing wealth he never uses.

Clock. Dysfunctional temporal coupling operates across scales. The immediate clock (seconds to days) registers fear of loss; the accumulation clock (months to years) builds the hoard. Because the feared loss may never materialize, the subject discounts a certain present (poverty, isolation) for an uncertain future loss, producing a sunk-cost trap in which the hoard grows while life is foreclosed.

Recursive modeling. The miser models other people as threats who will appropriate his gold. His resulting conduct—“monster of squalor and misanthropy” [PAGE 434]—elicits social avoidance or hostility, which then confirms his second-order belief that others are dangerous. The theory-of-mind loop is self-fulfilling: belief in predation → isolating hoarding behavior → social rejection → corroboration of predation model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The wood-rat hoard in an empty stove [PAGE 434] requires transport work W_transport = ∫ F · dx > 0 over non-trivial distance for inedible metal and cloth objects. A purely caloric-foraging physical model returns negative energy and is vetoed; the behavior cannot be explained by subsistence energetics.

Biomechanical layer. Pin-picking by asylum patients entails repetitive flexion, grasping, and stooping “all their time” [PAGE 434], imposing cumulative biomechanical cost E_bio > 0 with zero caloric or instrumental return. A rational-choice biomechanical model that minimizes effort per unit gain is vetoed because the cost-benefit ratio is undefined (division by zero return).

Weather/microclimate layer. The stove provides a dry, enclosed microclimate, but the hoarded objects (watch parts, knives, tobacco) are not selected for weather resistance; they are weather-agnostic. If shelter from weather were the objective, any insulating material would suffice. The specific categorical accumulation is vetoed as a weather-adaptation explanation.

Cross-layer adjudication. Because all three lower layers yield zero or negative utility, explanation must pass to the conative/instinct layer. Associationism cannot supply the veto because it predicts valuation tracks sensory contiguity, which is explicitly absent [PAGE 434]. The instinct/collecting model is the only layer that survives the cross-layer veto.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Demonetization (verifiable). James writes, “Demonetize it, and see how quickly they will get rid of it!” [PAGE 434]. Experimental counterfactual: suddenly remove exchange power from gold tokens in a token economy while holding sensory properties constant. Prediction: subjects classified as misers divest within ≤ 7 days, whereas non-miser collectors retain comparable items. Veto: if hoards persist despite zero exchange power, the “power-over-act” account is falsified; only a pure object-independent collecting instinct remains.

Scenario 2: Channel extinction (verifiable). Block an asylum patient’s access to floor pins for 4 weeks while providing neutral substitutes. Prediction: collecting transfers to thread, buttons, or rags within 72 hours, and the daily count of collected items does not fall below baseline; total accumulation is preserved rather than extinguished. Veto: if collecting stops completely with no substitution, object-specific associationism is supported over James’s general hoarding instinct.

Scenario 3: Guaranteed access without ownership (verifiable). Provide a miser with a trust that funds all consumption without legal title to the underlying assets. Prediction: if the miser values command potential P(o) per se, accumulation of title continues despite no consumption benefit; if he values only security or use, accumulation ceases within 30 days. Veto: cessation of acquisition under guaranteed access falsifies the power-over-act theory and supports a purely utilitarian/security account.

Measurement: record time-to-divestment (days), daily collection counts (items/day), and acquisition attempts (attempts/30 days) as verifiable RLVR reward signals.
