<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s derivation chain is not “time-series forecasting” in the usual sense. It is a hierarchy of information-ordered optimization models. The first hop is the one-stage problem: a decision \(z\in Z\subset\mathbb R^n\) is chosen before \(\xi\) is observed, and the objective is the expectation functional \(\bar h_0(z)=\int_\Xi h_0(z,\xi)\,P(d\xi)\) [PAGE 5]. The paper immediately flags the computational gray zone: even this “simple” problem is not solvable by standard nonlinear programming algorithms because evaluating \(E h_0\) or its subgradient is the bottleneck [PAGE 6]. The solvable islands are separable univariate integration, finite support with small \(L\), and special multivariate distributions such as normal, Gamma, or Dirichlet [PAGE 6]. The audit question is therefore: does the proposed method require a high-dimensional integral that cannot be reduced to those islands? If yes, the method is not solving the stated problem; it is solving an approximation.

The second hop is the two-stage recourse model. A first-stage decision \(z_1\) is followed by a second-stage decision \(z_2(\xi)\) after information is obtained [PAGE 8]. The deterministic equivalent is a one-stage problem with an infimal value function \(f_0(z_1)=f_{10}(z_1)+\int \inf_{z_2}\{f_{20}(z_2,\xi): f_{2i}(z_1,z_2,\xi)\le0\}\,P(d\xi)\). That value function is generally infinite-valued when recourse is infeasible and nondifferentiable because it is the infimum of a mathematical program [PAGE 8]. The key structural hinge is the information field \(\mathcal G\). If \(\mathcal G\) is independent of \(z_1\), the feasible set is a linear subspace of measurable functions and the problem can be solved in sequence: first the finite-dimensional first-stage program, then deterministic recourse programs for each \(\xi\) [PAGE 10]. If \(\mathcal G\) depends on \(z_1\), the feasible set \(Z_{\mathcal G}\) “is no longer a linear space ... in general it is neither convex (not even connected), nor closed” [PAGE 11]. This is the central singularity: the moment the information available to the future decision-maker is endogenous to the first-stage decision, the problem leaves convex programming and enters a policy-dependent measurability regime. The paper then states the hard consequence: “The optimal first stage decision cannot be found without finding an explicit description of the associate (optimal) second-stage decision function” [PAGE 11].

The third hop is multistage nonanticipativity. The decision \(z_t\) must be \(\mathcal B_t\)-measurable, where \(\mathcal B_t\) is generated by observations up to stage \(t\) [PAGE 13]. The optimality condition uses multipliers \(p=(p_1,\dots,p_T)\) orthogonal to the nonanticipative subspace, i.e. \(\mathbb E\{p_t(\cdot)\mid\mathcal B_t\}=0\) a.s., with a pointwise argmin condition [PAGE 14]. The paper interprets those multipliers as “a price system associated with the nonanticipativity restrictions” [PAGE 14]. In partial information, the field is replaced by a coarser \(\mathcal G_t\), and the conditional-expectation condition becomes \(\mathbb E\{q_t(\cdot)\mid\mathcal G_t\}=0\); the difference \(q-p\) is an “information price-system” that could determine whether seeking full information is desirable [PAGE 15]. The gray-zone audit here is brutal: if the constraint multifunction \(\Gamma(t,\xi)\) is not nonanticipative, then future infeasibility induces hidden constraints on earlier decisions. The paper calls these induced constraints and distinguishes them from relatively complete recourse [PAGE 10]. Any solver that ignores induced constraints may produce a first-stage decision that is feasible stage-by-stage but infeasible as a policy.

The fourth hop is stochastic optimal control. The control model adds an explicit observation channel \(y_t=k_t(x_t,\xi_t^2)\) and a feedback law \(u_t=g_t(y^t)\), with \(u_t\) required to be \(\mathcal Y_t\)-measurable [PAGE 17]. The paper insists that full state-information is not the same as full information in the recourse sense [PAGE 20]. Under full state-information, dynamic programming yields a Markovian control law; under partial state-information, the state must be replaced by the conditional distribution \(\pi_t(dx\mid y^t)=P\{x_t^g\in dx\mid y^t\}\), called a hyperstate or information state [PAGE 20], updated by a filter operator \(S_t\) [PAGE 23]. The computational price is explicit: dynamic programming requires time-independence and independence of disturbances, plus finite state-space and discrete control space; otherwise “the solution obtained by solving the Markov decision model is, at best, an approximation of the problem at hand” [PAGE 25]. Continuous time is mostly a qualitative regime: beyond the linear-quadratic regulator, “there are essentially no closed-form solutions” [PAGE 26]. The gray-zone audit therefore tests seven assumptions: stage is not calendar time [PAGE 8]; full state-information is not full information [PAGE 20]; the information field may depend on decisions [PAGE 11]; the distribution may depend on decisions [PAGE 7]; the constraint multifunction may induce hidden constraints [PAGE 10]; dynamic programming may require independence and finite state [PAGE 22, PAGE 25]; and the filter may be unavailable unless a Separation Lemma applies [PAGE 21].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Units and conventions.** Probabilities and conditional probabilities are dimensionless on \([0,1]\). Information and entropy are measured in nats, with \(1\) nat \(=1/\ln 2\) bits. Continuous time \(t\) is in seconds, SI; discrete stage index \(t=1,\dots,T\) is dimensionless. Costs are in application-specific units \(U\), states in units \(X\), controls in units \(C\); dual multipliers inherit units \(U\cdot X^{-1}\) or \(U\cdot C^{-1}\) from the pairing in which they appear. No ratio of unrelated physical quantities is introduced.

**One-stage model.** The source form is
\[
\min_{z\in Z\subset\mathbb R^n}\ \mathbb E_P\{h_0(z,\xi)\}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
h_i'(z)\le0,\ i=1,\dots,s,\qquad
h_i'(z)=0,\ i=s+1,\dots,m,
\]
with random vector \(\xi\in\Xi\subset\mathbb R^N\) and distribution \(P\) [PAGE 5]. The expectation functional is
\[
\bar h_0(z)=\int_{\Xi} h_0(z,\xi)\,P(d\xi).
\]
If \(h_0(z,\cdot)\) is separable, the multidimensional integral reduces to univariate integrals; if \(\Xi=\{\xi^1,\dots,\xi^L\}\) is finite and \(L\) is not too large, then \(\bar h_0(z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}p_\ell h_0(z,\xi^\ell)\) with \(p_\ell=P[\xi=\xi^\ell]\) [PAGE 6]. If \(h_0\) is convex, smooth, easy to evaluate, and \(P\) is a multidimensional normal, Gamma, or Dirichlet distribution, special subroutines may apply [PAGE 6]. These are the paper’s “solvable” categories.

**Decision-dependent probability.** If \(P\) depends on \(z\), the objective is
\[
\bar h(z)=\int_{\Xi}h(z,\xi)\,dP(\xi;z).
\]
The paper warns that convexity of \(h(\cdot,\xi)\) for all \(\xi\) no longer implies convexity of \(\bar h\), and the usual subgradient formula no longer holds [PAGE 7]. The shift case is
\[
P(\xi;z)=Q(\xi+Hz)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\bar h(z)=\int h(z,\zeta-Hz)\,dQ(\zeta),
\]
so “The properties of \(E h\) will thus depend on the properties of \(h(z,t)\) viewed as a function of \((z,t)\) jointly” [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regime: stochastic quasi-gradient methods lose their standard almost-sure justification when the measure depends on the decision.

**Two-stage recourse.** The source form is
\[
\min_{z_1}\ f_{10}(z_1)+\mathbb E\{f_{20}(z_2(\xi),\xi)\}
\]
subject to \(f_{1i}(z_1)\le0\) for \(i=1,\dots,m_1\), and \(f_{2i}(z_1,z_2(\xi),\xi)\le0\) almost surely for \(i=1,\dots,m_2\), where \(z_2\) is measurably dependent on information consistent with the recourse stage [PAGE 8]. With full information, \(z_2\) is a function of \(\xi\). The deterministic equivalent uses
\[
f_0(z_1)=f_{10}(z_1)+\int \inf_{z_2}\{f_{20}(z_2,\xi): f_{2i}(z_1,z_2,\xi)\le0\}\,P(d\xi),
\]
which is generally infinite-valued and nondifferentiable [PAGE 8]. If \(\mathcal G\) is the observed subfield before recourse, then \(z_2\) must be \(\mathcal G\)-measurable; the induced-constraint distinction is: “If it does, one refers to them as induced constraints. Otherwise, the problem is said to have relatively complete recourse” [PAGE 10]. If the information field depends on \(z_1\), writing \(\mathcal G(z_1)\), the feasible space \(Z_{\mathcal G}\) is not linear and “in general it is neither convex (not even connected), nor closed” [PAGE 11]. This is the exact nonconvex, nonclosed breakdown regime.

**Multistage nonanticipativity.** The multistage problem chooses \(z_t\) that is \(\mathcal B_t\)-measurable, \(z_t\in\Gamma(t,\xi)\), to minimize \(\mathbb E\{h_0((z_1(\xi),\dots,z_T(\xi)),\xi)\}\) [PAGE 13]. Under a standard constraint qualification and a nonanticipative constraint multifunction, a necessary and, in the convex case, sufficient optimality condition is the existence of multipliers \(p=(p_1,\dots,p_T)\) with
\[
\mathbb E\{p_t(\cdot)\mid\mathcal B_t\}=0\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad t=1,\dots,T,
\]
and
\[
z^*(\xi)\in\arg\min\{h_0(z,\xi)-p(\xi)\cdot z:\ z_t\in\Gamma(t,\xi)\ \text{a.s.},\ t=1,\dots,T\}
\]
[PAGE 14]. In partial information with field \(\mathcal G_t\), the condition becomes \(\mathbb E\{q_t(\cdot)\mid\mathcal G_t\}=0\) a.s., and \(q-p\) acts as an information price-system [PAGE 15]. Breakdown regime: the number of realizations grows exponentially with the number of stages, the “curse of dimensionality” [PAGE 12]; decomposition and scenario aggregation, such as the hedging algorithm, are the paper’s proposed remedy [PAGE 15].

**Stochastic optimal control.** The model has state \(x_t\), control \(u_t\), disturbances \(\xi_t^1,\xi_t^2\), dynamics \(x_t^g=f_t(x_{t-1}^g,u_t^g,\xi_t^1)\), observations \(y_t=k_t(x_t^g,\xi_t^2)\), and feedback \(u_t=g_t(y^t)=g_t(y_1,\dots,y_t)\in U_t\), with \(u_t\) required to be \(\mathcal Y_t\)-measurable [PAGE 17]. The objective is
\[
\min_g\ \mathbb E\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_t(x_t^g,u_t^g,\xi_t^1)+C_T(x_T^g)\right\}.
\]
Full state-information dynamic programming uses a backward recursion of the form
\[
V_t(x)=\min_{u\in U_t}\left\{c_t(x,u)+\mathbb E\left[V_{t+1}\left(f_{t+1}(x,u,\xi_{t+1}^1)\right)\mid x_t=x\right]\right\},
\]
with terminal condition \(V_T(x)=\mathbb E\{C_T(x_T)\mid x_T=x\}\) [PAGE 22, PAGE 23]. Under partial state-information, the hyperstate is
\[
\pi_t(dx\mid y^t)=P\{x_t^g\in dx\mid y^t\},\qquad \pi_{t+1}=S_{t+1}(\pi_t,y_{t+1}),
\]
and the recursion is over the space \(\Pi\) of probability distributions [PAGE 23, PAGE 24]. The paper’s exact quote is: “These conditional distributions are sometimes called hyperstates or information states” [PAGE 20]. Breakdown regime: dynamic programming requires mutual independence of \(x_0,\xi_1^1,\xi_1^2,\dots,\xi_T^1,\xi_T^2\) [PAGE 22], time-independence, and finite state-space; otherwise the Markov decision model is only an approximation [PAGE 25].

**Continuous time.** The control problem is
\[
dx_t=f_t(x_t,u_t)\,dt+\sigma_1(x_t)\,d\xi_t^1,\qquad
dy_t=k_t(x_t)\,dt+\sigma_2(y_t)\,d\xi_t^2,
\]
with Wiener or semimartingale disturbances and \(u_t\) \(\mathcal Y_t\)-measurable [PAGE 25, PAGE 26]. Girsanov’s Lemma is used to give precise meaning to the information constraint [PAGE 26]. Breakdown regime: beyond the linear-quadratic regulator, “there are essentially no closed-form solutions” [PAGE 26].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a retrocausal one: later algorithmic feasibility retroactively selects which model formulations are treated as “natural.” The oldest layer is dynamic programming and the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman tradition, invoked as the main solution route for stochastic control [PAGE 21]. The next layer is stochastic programming with recourse, where two-stage and multistage models are analyzed through deterministic equivalents, nonanticipativity multipliers, and scenario decomposition [PAGE 8, PAGE 14, PAGE 15]. The paper also points to Witsenhausen’s “A counterexample in stochastic control” as the canonical warning that decision-dependent information can destroy the sequential finite-dimensional solution path [PAGE 11, PAGE 27]. That counterexample is the genealogical hinge between “control” and “programming”: it shows that an apparently small modeling change in the information field changes the mathematical species of the problem. Later references—Fleming and Rishel, Kushner, Davis, Vermes, Back and Pliska—mark the continuous-time branch, including piecewise-deterministic Markov processes and martingale methods [PAGE 26, PAGE 27]. The paper’s own diagnosis is that continuous-time models are an analyst’s delight but computationally thin beyond the linear-quadratic case [PAGE 26].

The multi-week traps are therefore not superficial. Trap one: treating “stage” as a calendar time unit. The paper explicitly says stages “do not necessarily refer to time units” and “correspond to stages in the decision process” [PAGE 8]. A multi-week research plan that maps stage index directly to weeks will mis-specify the information ordering. Trap two: conflating full state-information with full information. The paper warns that full state-information “should not be confused with what we have called full information” in the recourse model [PAGE 20]. Trap three: assuming the information field is independent of the first-stage decision. If it is not, \(Z_{\mathcal G}\) is not convex, connected, or closed [PAGE 11]. Trap four: ignoring induced constraints. The paper distinguishes induced constraints from relatively complete recourse [PAGE 10]; a solver that omits them can spend weeks tuning a policy that is infeasible after future information arrives. Trap five: applying stochastic quasi-gradient or plug-in gradient methods when \(P\) depends on \(z\). The paper states that the usual subgradient formula no longer applies [PAGE 7]. Trap six: assuming the Separation Lemma is general. The paper permits it only “in a few cases” [PAGE 21]. Trap seven: using dynamic programming after converting to a Markov decision problem without checking independence and finite-state conditions; otherwise the result is “at best, an approximation” [PAGE 25]. These are the multi-week traps because each one looks like a small implementation detail but changes the feasible mathematical object.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is the controller’s model of its own future information. In the full-state case, the future self is represented by \(x_t\); in the partial-state case, the future self is represented by the hyperstate \(\pi_t(dx\mid y^t)=P\{x_t^g\in dx\mid y^t\}\), updated by \(S_t\) [PAGE 23, PAGE 24]. That is a formal belief recursion: the agent reasons about what its future self will believe, not merely about the hidden state. When the information field depends on past decisions, \(\mathcal G_t(z^{t-1})\), the future self’s belief is endogenous: the current action changes the observation channel through which the future self will reason [PAGE 15]. This is the formal core of the recursive theory-of-mind problem.

“Crisis habit regression” is the failure mode in which, under uncertainty or time pressure, the policy regresses to an open-loop or rolling-horizon habit. The paper describes open-loop control as the case where no information is collected to adjust earlier decisions, and it warns that rolling-horizon use “cannot always be recommended, since it arbitrarily ignores feedback (or recourse) possibilities that are inherent to all stochastic optimization problems” [PAGE 19]. In recursive terms, crisis habit regression collapses \(\pi_t\) to a point estimate or collapses \(u_t=g_t(y^t)\) to \(u_t=g_t(y_t)\) without justification. The counter-trap is the opposite overcorrection: forcing a full-information or full-state policy when the observation channel is non-invertible, or restricting the feedback class to a parametric family before establishing error bounds. The paper notes that restricted feedback is computationally attractive but has “many unresolved theoretical questions” and requires error bounds [PAGE 21]. That is the counter-trap: a tractable surrogate can be optimal for the surrogate and suboptimal for the true information structure.

The clock is the stage index and horizon \(T\), not necessarily elapsed time. The recursive backward recursion runs from terminal condition at \(T\) to stage \(1\); if the clock is misread, the measurability condition is placed on the wrong field. The paper’s warning that stages are decision epochs [PAGE 8] is therefore a clock warning. In a correct recursion, the agent at stage \(t\) conditions on \(\mathcal Y_t\) or \(\mathcal G_t\), not on future observations; in a broken recursion, the agent anticipates future weather or future recourse, violating nonanticipativity [PAGE 13, PAGE 17]. The three-way distinction is: crisis habit regression collapses the belief recursion; counter-trap overfits a tractable surrogate; clock error misaligns the information field with the decision epoch. The paper’s Witsenhausen reference is the limiting case where these three errors become mathematically visible [PAGE 11, PAGE 27].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication starts from the admissible-policy set
\[
\mathcal A=\{g:\ g_t\ \text{is}\ \mathcal Y_t\text{-measurable},\ 
u_t^g\in U_t,\ 
x_t^g\in X_t\ \text{for all }t\ \text{almost surely},\ 
z_t\in\Gamma(t,\xi)\ \text{almost surely}\}.
\]
This is the intersection of four layers: physical state \(X_t\), biomechanical control authority \(U_t\), weather disturbance support \(\Xi_t^1\), and information field \(\mathcal Y_t\). If \(\mathcal A=\varnothing\), no policy can be optimal because no policy is feasible. The source’s control formulation includes system constraints as state-space and control constraints, and information constraints as \(u_t\) being \(\mathcal Y_t\)-measurable [PAGE 17]. That is the formal basis for the veto.

Physical veto proof. If a candidate policy \(g\) has \(\Pr\{x_t^g\notin X_t\ \text{for some }t\}>0\), then the policy violates the state-space constraint. In the source’s constrained control formulation, state-space constraints are part of the system constraints [PAGE 17]. Therefore \(g\notin\mathcal A\). If the objective assigns finite cost only on feasible states, the value is infinite or undefined; if the objective is bounded, the policy is still inadmissible. This veto cannot be repaired by tuning the cost function because it is a feasibility condition, not a preference.

Biomechanical veto proof. If \(u_t^g\notin U_t\) on a set of positive probability, the control is outside the actuator or physiological authority set. The source explicitly includes control constraints in the system constraints [PAGE 17]. Thus \(g\notin\mathcal A\). A biomechanical layer may impose \(U_t=U_t(x_t,\text{load},\text{fatigue})\), but the veto logic is the same: if the required control is outside the admissible set, the policy is vetoed before objective comparison. This is why actuator saturation is not a minor numerical issue; it changes the feasible set.

Weather veto proof. Let \(\Xi_t^1\) be the support of the state disturbance. If
\[
\Xi_t^1\nsubseteq
\{\xi:\ \exists u\in U_t\ \text{with}\ f_t(x_{t-1},u,\xi)\in X_t\},
\]
then there exists a weather realization of positive probability for which no admissible control keeps the state in \(X_t\). The policy is vetoed by the disturbance support, independent of the controller’s skill. The paper’s Markov decision formulation makes the transition kernel depend on \(Q(x_{t-1},u_t)=\{\xi:f_t(x_{t-1},u_t,\xi)\in D\}\) [PAGE 22]; the same support condition is the feasibility kernel. If the observation channel \(y_t=k_t(x_t,\xi_t^2)\) is non-invertible, a full-state policy is also vetoed by the information layer: it would require \(u_t\) to depend on information not in \(\mathcal Y_t\), violating the measurability constraint [PAGE 17]. The adjudication order is therefore: physical feasibility first, biomechanical authority second, weather support third, information measurability fourth. Any layer can veto the policy; no lower layer can overrule a higher-layer infeasibility.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
In an RLVR-style audit, a counterfactual veto is a verifiable test that rejects a policy or algorithm when a stated structural condition is violated. Three such vetoes follow.

**Veto 1: decision-dependent probability with a plug-in gradient.** Test condition: the model uses \(\bar h(z)=\int h(z,\xi)dP(\xi;z)\) but the algorithm samples \(\xi\sim P(\cdot;z)\) and uses the plug-in estimator \(\widehat{\nabla}=\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^M\nabla_z h(z,\xi_m)\) as if \(P\) were independent of \(z\). Counterfactual instance: take \(P(\xi;z)=Q(\xi+z)\), \(h(z,\xi)=\xi^2\), with \(Q\) a fixed distribution of finite variance. Then \(\bar h(z)=\mathbb E_Q[(\zeta-z)^2]\), whose true derivative is \(2(z-\mathbb E_Q\zeta)\), while the plug-in derivative of \(h\) with respect to \(z\) is identically zero. Prediction: the estimator has nonzero bias unless \(z=\mathbb E_Q\zeta\); the veto triggers when a finite-difference check on \(\bar h\) disagrees with the plug-in gradient by more than the Monte Carlo standard error. This is verifiable by running both estimators on the same seeds and reporting the paired difference in units \(U\cdot X^{-1}\).

**Veto 2: decision-dependent information field.** Test condition: the two-stage solver first optimizes \(z_1\) in the finite-dimensional program and then solves recourse for each \(\xi\), ignoring that \(\mathcal G=\mathcal G(z_1)\) depends on \(z_1\). Counterfactual instance: a finite-state, two-stage problem in which the observation channel after \(z_1\) is non-nested, so the feasible measurable functions change with \(z_1\). Prediction: the sequential solver returns \(z_1^{\text{seq}}\) with value \(J(z_1^{\text{seq}})\), while exhaustive policy enumeration over the finite horizon returns \(z_1^*\) with \(J(z_1^*)<J(z_1^{\text{seq}})\); the gap \(\Delta=J(z_1^{\text{seq}})-J(z_1^*)>0\). The veto is verifiable by enumeration and by checking the source’s structural warning that \(Z_{\mathcal G}\) is not linear, not necessarily convex, connected, or closed [PAGE 11]. If the gap is zero, either the information field is effectively independent of \(z_1\) or the instance has relatively complete recourse with a linear structure.

**Veto 3: Markov decision approximation under non-independent disturbances.** Test condition: the algorithm fits an independent-transition Markov decision model \(q(x'\mid x,u)\) to data generated by a process with autocorrelated \(\xi_t\), then applies dynamic programming. Prediction: the DP policy \(g_q^*\) has true value \(V_p(g_q^*)\) that exceeds the true optimal value \(V_p(g_p^*)\), and the excess is bounded below by a positive gap when the total-variation distance between \(q\) and the true conditional law \(p\) exceeds a tolerance. Verifiable prediction: under bounded cost \(|c_t|\le C_{\max}\), \(|V_p(g)-V_q(g)|\le C_{\max}\sum_t \mathrm{TV}(p_t,q_t)\), so a measured TV distance gives a certified upper bound on surrogate error; if the bound is below the reward tolerance, the veto does not fire. The source supports this veto directly: dynamic programming required mutual independence [PAGE 22], and without discrete state and independent random variables the Markov decision solution is “at best, an approximation of the problem at hand” [PAGE 25]. These three vetoes are counterfactual because each one can be run as a controlled test: perturb the distribution dependence, the information field, or the disturbance independence, and observe whether the claimed policy value degrades in the predicted direction.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
