The core analytic move is to treat exchange value and production cost as two descriptions of the same choice-theoretic equilibrium. Equilibrium is defined by the equality of cost and selling price; the intersection of the cost and demand curves determines both the equilibrium rate of production/consumption and the equilibrium price. Knight characterizes this as a straightforward deduction from the Law of Choice, which implies that economic actors select among alternative uses of resources until the marginal attractions are equated.

A multi-hop derivation proceeds as follows. First, under competitive conditions, value and cost are reciprocally identical: “a value involves an equal cost and a cost an equal value.” This identity is not causal in the sense of one determining the other temporally; it is a simultaneous resting point of relative choice. Second, cost is defined as “the value of the resources embodied in a thing,” equivalently the value of the best alternative use forgone for those resources. If no competing attraction exists for a resource, it is not economically a resource at all. Third, because increasing output of one good diverts resources from alternative productions, the value of those other goods rises while the conditions of the first good change; since resources are always valued at their best available use, marginal cost rises with output. Knight explicitly restricts the present analysis to an increasing-cost curve and defers decreasing costs to a later, more complicated stage. Let the cost of embodied resources be \(C(Q) : \mathcal{Q} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\), also in currency units per unit of \(Q\). Cost is the opportunity value of resources in their best alternative use:
\[
C(Q) = \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}(Q)} V_a\bigl(R(Q)\bigr),
\]
where \(R(Q)\) is the resource bundle embodied in output \(Q\), \(\mathcal{A}(Q)\) is the set of competing alternative uses, and \(V_a\) is the value (currency) obtainable in use \(a\). If \(\mathcal{A}(Q)\) is empty (no competing attraction), the resources cease to be economic resources and the cost basis collapses.

The equilibrium condition quoted from the text is
\[
P(Q^*) = C(Q^*),
\]
or equivalently \(D(Q^*) = C(Q^*)\), where \(D\) is the demand price schedule. The equilibrium price and quantity are
\[
P^* = P(Q^*) = C(Q^*), \qquad Q^* = \arg\{Q : P(Q) = C(Q)\}.
\]
The intersection of the curves reveals the equilibrium rate of production and consumption on one axis and the equilibrium price on the other [PAGE 107].

Knight’s footnote imposes the domain restriction

\frac{dC}{dQ} > 0 \quad \text{(increasing costs)},
\]

The footnote’s increasing-cost curve derives from the Ricardo-Malthusian intensive and extensive margin logic, where expanding output draws in progressively less favorably situated resources.

Because it treats equilibrium as a pure deduction from choice and explicitly parks decreasing costs and multi-agency complications for “a later stage,” it represents a short-run, single-commodity, single-agency closure. In historical time, this corresponds to a window of weeks to months during which plant, resource inventory, and technical coefficients are fixed, but marginal reallocation among existing uses is possible. The multi-week trap is that the model assumes reallocation is fast enough to establish \(P=C\) within the observation window, while Knight’s broader thesis holds that genuine uncertainty (as opposed to measurable risk) prevents agents from knowing the relevant cost and demand schedules at all. When scale economies or learning-by-doing dominate over weeks (the deferred decreasing-cost regime), the baseline itself becomes a trap: participants extrapolate increasing marginal cost from short-run experience into a long run where unit costs fall, over-supplying relative to the eventual equilibrium.

Regressing to cost=value during a crisis masks the fact that the relevant resource alternatives and their values are themselves being revised in real time.

If shocks arrive faster than this clock can complete a cycle (days versus weeks), the intersection \(P(Q^*)=C(Q^*)\) is never observed; only disequilibrium prices and quantity rationing appear. The page’s assumption that “only one kind of agency is used in making any one good” implicitly assumes the adjustment clock is short relative to the planning horizon, an assumption that fails precisely in the crisis situations where the theory is most likely to be invoked.

Biomechanical layer: Labor is a primary resource, and its supply is bounded by human physiological limits (hours awake, muscular endurance, cognitive load). If expanding output requires diverting labor from alternative uses beyond sustainable work duration, the marginal cost does not merely rise; it becomes undefined because the resource cannot be supplied at any price within the relevant interval. This imposes a hard \(Q_{\max}\) beyond which the increasing-cost curve ceases to exist as a continuous function.

Weather veto proof: Consider an agricultural good whose competing alternative use is another crop on the same land. A freeze or drought destroys the alternative crop, altering its value \(V_y\) discontinuously. Because cost is defined as the value of resources in their best alternative use, \(C(Q)\) shifts upward abruptly and non-monotonically. The smooth intersection \(P(Q)=C(Q)\) assumed on page 107 is then invalid until new information about surviving yields propagates through the market—a process measured in weeks, not instantaneously. The weather veto is proof that the cost curve’s increasing property is contingent on stable physical conditions; a shock that reorders the ranking of alternative uses invalidates the scalar cost schedule and, by extension, the equilibrium price deduced from it.
