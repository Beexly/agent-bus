<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Knight’s argument traces back to the Cambridge Capital Controversy and the foundational debates of marginalism in the late 19th century. Frank William Taussig and Alfred Marshall initially grappled with the problem of joint products—how to attribute cost to multiple outputs from a single process (e.g., beef and hide). Marshall’s "representative firm" was a heuristic to bypass the issue of indivisibilities.

Knight’s explicit reference to Professor Clark (John Bates Clark) situates him within the American Neo-Classical tradition, which sought to reconcile Ricardo’s distribution theory with the new marginalist calculus. Clark’s "Law of Wages" depended critically on the differentiability of the production function—a mathematical convenience that assumed labor and capital were perfectly divisible fluids.

Padan’s critique, cited by Knight, represents an early intrusion of what we now call "lumpiness" or integer programming constraints into economic thought. Padan argued that if the marginal unit is arbitrarily large, the marginal product—and thus the wage—is arbitrary. This is a precursor to the "Sraffian" critique that capital is not a homogeneous aggregate but a heterogeneous set of durable goods whose value depends on the distribution of income itself.

Historically, this debate created a trap for subsequent economists: the reliance on smooth, concave production functions (Constant Elasticity of Substitution - CES) effectively buried the problem of indivisibility under the assumption of large numbers. As firms grew in the 20th century (Standard Oil, U.S. Steel), the "exceptional cases" Knight mentioned became the rule in many sectors. The antitrust movements of the Progressive Era were, in essence, political responses to the economic reality that Knight described: when agencies become unique and indivisible, competition gives place to monopoly, and the "competitive adjustment" fails.

In the current era, the retrocausal link is clear: the digital platform economy has resurrected Padan’s "arbitrary size" critique. A social media platform’s "agency" is its user base. This agency is technically divisible (users can leave), but the network effects create increasing returns so potent that the platform becomes functionally indivisible in practice. We are living through the validation of Knight’s footnote: the "absurdity" of increasing returns is not a theoretical curiosity but a structural feature of modern tech monopolies, leading to the very negative productivity (stifled innovation, rent-seeking) he predicted.
