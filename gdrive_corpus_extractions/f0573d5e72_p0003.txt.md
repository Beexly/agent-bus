Taking the natural logarithm yields a sum of independent and identically distributed random variables: \(\log X_n = \log X_0 + S \log(1+f) + F \log(1-f)\). The expectation per trial is the growth rate coefficient: \(G(f) = p \log(1+f) + q \log(1-f)\), where \(p\) is the probability of success, \(q = 1-p\) is the probability of failure, and \(f\) is the fixed fraction wagered. This formulation assumes infinitely divisible capital, which redefines "ruin" not as hitting exactly zero, but as the probability that capital falls below any arbitrarily small positive threshold \(\varepsilon\) approaching one: \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \text{Pr}(X_n \le \varepsilon) = 1\). This is a crucial distinction from the classical Gambler's Ruin problem [PAGE 3].

Differentiating \(G(f)\) gives \(G'(f) = \frac{p}{1+f} - \frac{q}{1-f}\). Setting this derivative to zero reveals the stationary point at \(f^* = p - q\) [PAGE 3]. The second derivative \(G''(f) = -\frac{p}{(1+f)^2} - \frac{q}{(1-f)^2}\) is strictly negative for all \(f \in [0, 1)\), confirming that \(G(f)\) is concave and that \(f^*\) is the unique global maximum. The source explicitly notes that \(G'(f)\) is monotone strictly decreasing on \([0, 1)\), with \(G'(0) = p - q > 0\) (assuming an advantage) and \(\lim_{f \uparrow 1} G'(f) = -\infty\) [PAGE 3]. This guarantees that if \(p > 0.5\), the optimal betting fraction is positive.

The maximum achievable growth rate is \(G(f^*) = p \log p + q \log q + \log 2\) [PAGE 3]. Since \(p \log p + q \log q\) is the negative binary entropy, \(G(f^*) = \log 2 - H(p)\), which is non-negative only when \(p \ge 0.5\). The existence of a root \(f_e \in (0, f^*)\) such that \(G(f_e) = 0\) follows from the Intermediate Value Theorem because \(G(0)=0\), \(G'(0)>0\), and \(G(f)\) eventually goes to \(-\infty\) as \(f \to 1\). The singularity regime occurs at \(f=1\), where the logarithmic term \(\log(1-f)\) diverges to \(-\infty\), representing total loss in a single trial. In practice, the Kelly criterion is often scaled down (e.g., "half-Kelly") to reduce variance and account for estimation error in \(p\), which is a critical multi-week trap: overbetting relative to the true edge leads to exponential decay despite a positive expected value per bet. The gray-zone audit involves recognizing that while the mathematical optimum maximizes long-term growth, it does not minimize short-term volatility, creating a potential mismatch between theoretical optimality and psychological or liquidity constraints. The strategy's robustness relies heavily on the stability of the underlying probabilities; any misestimation can shift \(f^*\) into a region where \(G(f) < 0\), leading to inevitable ruin in the probabilistic sense. Thus, the core insight is not just the formula, but the recognition that optimal growth requires precise calibration of risk relative to perceived advantage, and even slight deviations can have catastrophic compounding effects over many trials.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The Kelly criterion originates from John L. Kelly Jr.'s 1956 work at Bell Labs on reducing noise in telegraph signals, framed as an optimal investment strategy in a horse-racing context. It was later independently rediscovered and popularized by Ed Thorp in the 1960s for blackjack and options trading, linking information theory (via Shannon's capacity) to financial economics. Historically, the criterion was viewed with skepticism by mainstream finance due to its aggressive growth focus and assumption of known probabilities, contrasting with Markowitz mean-variance optimization which prioritizes risk-adjusted returns. The genealogy shows a multi-week trap: practitioners often overestimate their edge \(p\), leading to overbetting (\(f > f^*\)) and severe drawdowns, as seen in the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) which employed high leverage akin to over-Kelly betting. The criterion also influenced modern risk parity and volatility-targeting strategies, though these often scale down position sizes further. A critical historical lesson is that while Kelly maximizes asymptotic growth, it does not guarantee short-term profitability, and its application requires robust estimation and often fractional scaling to survive the "gray zone" of parameter uncertainty.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A trader's crisis habit under stress is to either freeze (reduce \(f\) to near zero) or double-down (increase \(f\) beyond \(f^*\)) after a loss, both of which deviate from the optimal fixed-fraction rule. The Kelly framework prescribes a counter-trap: maintain \(f = f^*\) regardless of recent outcomes, as the law of large numbers ensures convergence to the maximal growth rate. However, this requires immense psychological discipline, as the variance of wealth under Kelly betting is high. The "clock" element refers to the time horizon: for very short horizons, the probability of significant drawdowns is substantial, making Kelly appear risky; for infinite horizons, it dominates all other constant-fraction strategies. Recursive thinking involves anticipating one's own future behavior: if one knows they will panic-sell during a drawdown, pre-committing to a lower fraction (e.g., half-Kelly) may be optimal to avoid deviation from the plan. The meta-level strategy is to recognize that the Kelly fraction is not a static number but a dynamic function of current edge estimates, and updating \(p\) based on new information is essential. Overconfidence in one's ability to estimate \(p\) accurately is a common cognitive bias that leads to overbetting and ruin.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

This increases variance without changing marginal \(p\), effectively reducing the geometric growth rate. Simulation shows that with pairwise correlation \(\rho = 0.7\) during stress periods, the effective growth rate drops significantly even if \(f = f^*\) computed under independence. Veto: Incorporate regime-switching models or reduce \(f\) proportionally to estimated tail dependence.

The optimal fraction shifts to \(f^*_c < f^*\). For \(c = 0.001\) (basis points), \(f^*_c \approx f^* - 2c/(p-q)\). Ignoring costs and using \(f^*\) results in suboptimal growth. Veto: Always incorporate realistic transaction costs into the growth rate function before optimizing; for frequent trading, this may make many opportunities uneconomical.
