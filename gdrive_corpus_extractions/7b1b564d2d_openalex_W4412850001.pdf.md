<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are best formalized as a real-time pipeline, a 2D landmark error metric, a monocular projection model, a confidence gate, and a bounded subjective fidelity score. All equations below are audit instruments, not claims added by the paper.

1. Full-pipeline latency budget. Define
\[
L_{\mathrm{pipe}}=
T_{\mathrm{cam}}+
T_{\mathrm{scale}}+
T_{\mathrm{det}}+
T_{\mathrm{lm}}+
T_{\mathrm{readback}}+
T_{\mathrm{render}}
\quad [\mathrm{ms}],
\]
where \(T_{\mathrm{cam}}\) is camera capture/exposure, \(T_{\mathrm{scale}}\) is image transform and affine sampling, \(T_{\mathrm{det}}\) is pose-detector scheduling, \(T_{\mathrm{lm}}\) is landmark refinement, \(T_{\mathrm{readback}}\) is tensor readback/clone, and \(T_{\mathrm{render}}\) is avatar/animation rendering. The real-time admissibility condition is
\[
L_{\mathrm{pipe}} \leq T_{\mathrm{frame}}=\frac{1000}{f_s}\ \mathrm{ms},
\]
where \(f_s\) is the display or capture rate in \(\mathrm{Hz}\). At \(f_s=30\), \(T_{\mathrm{frame}}=33.3\ \mathrm{ms}\); at \(f_s=60\), \(T_{\mathrm{frame}}=16.7\ \mathrm{ms}\). The source reports: “BlazePose with Unity Sentis exhibited the lowest latency (mean: 28ms, median: 26ms) compared to OpenPose (mean: 45ms, median: 43ms) and MoveNet (mean: 35ms, median: 33ms)” [PAGE 8]. Assumption: the reported value is a mean over trials under “Consistent environmental conditions and hardware” [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regime: if \(L_{\mathrm{pipe}}>T_{\mathrm{frame}}\), frame queues grow and motion-to-photon latency is no longer bounded by the reported mean; if \(L_{\mathrm{pipe}}\approx T_{\mathrm{frame}}\), jitter can cause intermittent frame drops even when the mean passes. The paper does not report variance, frame rate, resolution, or whether render/readback/capture are inside the 28 ms boundary.

2. Joint-position error. For \(N\) evaluated joints,
\[
\bar e=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\mathbf p}_i-\mathbf p_i\right\|_2
\quad [\mathrm{px}],
\]
where \(\hat{\mathbf p}_i\) is estimated image-plane position and \(\mathbf p_i\) is ground truth. The source reports: “BlazePose also demonstrated superior accuracy, with an average joint position error of 3.5 pixels, while OpenPose and MoveNet showed 5.2 and 4.1 pixels, respectively” [PAGE 8]. This is a pixel-domain metric. If a pinhole camera model with focal length \(f\) in pixels and depth \(Z\) in meters is assumed, the approximate physical error is
\[
\Delta X \approx \frac{Z\,\Delta u}{f},\qquad
\Delta Y \approx \frac{Z\,\Delta v}{f}
\quad [\mathrm{m}],
\]
where \(\Delta u,\Delta v\) are pixel errors. Assumption: known \(f\) and known \(Z\). Breakdown regime: if \(Z\) is unknown or varies per joint, the pixel error cannot be converted to a unique centimeter error; as \(Z\to 0\), the projection singularity appears; as \(Z\) grows or \(f\) decreases, the same pixel error corresponds to a larger physical error. Therefore 3.5 px is not a tennis-stroke accuracy guarantee.

3. Monocular projection and depth ambiguity. With camera intrinsics \(f,c_u,c_v\),
\[
u=f\frac{X}{Z}+c_u,\qquad
v=f\frac{Y}{Z}+c_v.
\]
For any positive scale \(k\),
\[
(X,Y,Z)\mapsto (kX,kY,kZ)
\]
leaves \((u,v)\) unchanged, so a single view has scale ambiguity. The paper states that the method is “designed to process 2D camera images into 3D pose data” [PAGE 6]. Assumption: BlazePose supplies learned priors that reduce, but do not eliminate, this ambiguity. Breakdown regime: exact singularity at \(Z=0\); ill-conditioning for large \(Z\) or near-frontal limb configurations where depth changes produce small image-plane changes. Thus the paper’s 3D avatar animation should be treated as prior-assisted reconstruction, not metric motion capture.

4. Confidence gating. Let \(s_j\) be the confidence for joint \(j\) and \(\tau\) be the threshold. The accepted set is
\[
\mathcal J_\tau=\{j:s_j\geq \tau\}.
\]
The source states: “If the confidence score surpasses a predefined threshold, m_PoseLandmarkerWorker refines joint positions into landmarks, completing the pose estimation” [PAGE 6]. Assumption: \(\tau\) is constant per frame. Breakdown regime: if \(\max_j s_j<\tau\), no pose is accepted and the avatar can freeze or hold the last pose; if \(\tau\) is too low, false-positive landmarks enter the rig. The paper does not report \(\tau\), per-joint confidence calibration, or false-accept/false-reject rates.

5. Fidelity score. The reported fidelity is a bounded ordinal score:
\[
F=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}q_r,\qquad q_r\in[0,10],
\]
where \(q_r\) is a rubric score for trial \(r\). The source reports: “The fidelity of movement translation was highest with BlazePose, achieving a score of 9.2 out of 10, compared to OpenPose (7.8) and MoveNet (8.5)” [PAGE 8]. This is not a physical unit and cannot be interpreted like milliseconds or pixels. Breakdown regime: without a prespecified rubric, blinded raters, and inter-rater reliability, the score is not reproducible; a 9.2/10 point estimate has no verified uncertainty interval.

6. Ball-validity predicate. Define
\[
p_{\mathrm{valid}}=\Pr\left[(\theta,\phi,v)\in\mathcal A\right],
\]
where \(\theta,\phi\) parameterize launch direction, \(v\) is speed, and \(\mathcal A\) is the admissible set of trajectories that are reachable and within game bounds. The source states: “using some calculations, it is almost certain that the ball is valid 100% percent of the time” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if randomAngle extremes produce directions outside \(\mathcal A\), if the shooter intersects scene geometry, or if high-speed modes cause collision/tunneling, the probability is less than one. The phrase “almost certain” already contradicts “100% percent.”

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/weather veto is real at the access layer. If precipitation or court unavailability blocks outdoor practice, the outdoor training set is empty for that interval. The paper’s motivating evidence is explicit: “during Wimbledon 2024, persistent rain caused 75 out of 91 scheduled matches to be canceled, forcing organizers to refund approximately 7,500 tickets and incurring an estimated financial loss of $275,000” [PAGE 1]. An indoor device-based game can bypass the weather predicate if a webcam, display, and compute device are available. That is a legitimate veto override for access and scheduling. It is not a veto override for physical tennis specificity, because the virtual ball dispenser and avatar do not reproduce court traction, racket–ball impact, ball spin, net interaction, or real proprioceptive load.

The biomechanical veto is stronger. Webcam pose estimation yields kinematics: joint positions, approximate angles, and timing. It does not directly measure racket force, ground reaction force, joint torque, muscle activation, or impact impulse. A simple dynamics relation makes the missing layer explicit:
\[
\tau = J^{T}F,
\]
where \(\tau\) is joint torque, \(J\) is the kinematic Jacobian, and \(F\) is the external force/contact wrench. Without \(F\), the system cannot compute load or injury-relevant torque. The paper’s claim is about movement mirroring, not force measurement: “This ensures that the player’s swings, jumps, and other physical actions are accurately mirrored in the program” [PAGE 4]. The exact breakdown regime is any claim about stroke force, racket-head speed at contact, footwork load, or injury risk. Those claims are vetoed unless additional sensors or validated biomechanical models are added.

The physical-environment veto applies to skill specificity. The paper describes a “ball dispenser” that “shoots tennis balls in different directions” [PAGE 2] inside a game, and the code description says the machine “randomly launch[es] a ball toward a player” [PAGE 5]. That is a virtual ball, not a physical ball with spin, bounce, and court interaction. The game can train reaction time, lateral movement, and some upper-body motion, but it cannot validate ball control or court-specific footwork without a transfer study. The paper’s own limitation statement is relevant: “One of the limitations of the project would be its inability to be run on web” [PAGE 9], which also means deployment scope is unsettled.

The latency/occlusion veto is the computational layer. The source’s confidence gate means that accepted landmarks depend on \(s_j\geq\tau\) [PAGE 6]. If the player is occluded, poorly lit, or outside the camera’s field of view, the gate can reject the pose and the avatar can freeze. The paper acknowledges that other approaches face “occlusions or varying lighting conditions” [PAGE 2], but it does not show that its single-webcam setup is immune. The exact veto is: if \(\max_j s_j<\tau\), no valid pose; if \(\tau\) is too low, false landmarks pass; if \(L_{\mathrm{pipe}}>T_{\mathrm{frame}}\), the motion-to-photon clock fails. The adjudication is therefore layered: the game passes as an accessible indoor movement adjunct; it is vetoed as a full replacement for physical tennis training, as a biomechanical load measurement system, and as a validated skill-transfer intervention.
