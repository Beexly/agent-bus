The core argument is that because the lower visual field habitually projects ground-plane objects at varying distances, the brain learns to automatically "enlarge" images falling on the lower retina to compensate for perspective foreshortening. **Multi-hop derivation:**
1.  **Physical Stimulus:** Light from a physical world with dominant ground-plane geometry strikes the retina.
2.  **Neural Encoding:** The lower retina receives images of the far ground; the upper retina receives images of overhead/near space.
3.  **Developmental Learning:** Through motor experience (walking, reaching), the perceptual system learns that identical-sized objects project larger retinal images when closer (upper field) and smaller when farther (lower field).
4.  **Inverse-Function Acquisition:** To maintain size/distance constancy, the system acquires an automatic scaling function `S(retinal_location)` that enlarges lower-field signals more than upper-field ones.
5.  **Misapplication Error (Illusion):** When viewing a 2D vertical line on paper, both halves are equidistant. The system erroneously applies `S`, over-enlarging the lower half's *perceived* content, causing the subjective midpoint to shift upward. The error magnitude (James notes "about 1/6") quantifies the strength of the entrenched habit.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The phenomenon can be formalized as a learned perceptual operator acting on raw sensory data.

Let `I(x,y)` be the intensity image on the retina, with `(x,y)` in normalized device coordinates, `y=0` at the fovea, `y>0` upwards. The physical world contains objects with true extent `E`. Due to perspective, the retinal image size `R` is a function of true size and distance `d`: `R ≈ E / d` (small angle approx).

The perceptual system estimates perceived size `P` by applying an inverse scaling function `S(y)` derived from lifetime statistics:
`P = S(y) * R`

For ground-plane dominance, the prior assumes `d ∝ 1/y` for `y<0` (lower field). Thus, to recover `E`, we need `S(y) ∝ 1/d ∝ |y|`. A simple linear model:
`S(y) = k * max(0, -y)^α`
where `k` is a gain constant and `α≈1` for a linear depth cue. For the upper field (`y>0`), `S(y) ≈ k₀` (constant, shallow depth).

**The Illusion Operator:** For a vertical line segment from `y₁` to `y₂` on paper (equidistant), the perceived extent of each half is integrated:
`Perceived Half-Extent = ∫_{half} S(y) dy`
Since `S(y)` is larger for negative `y`, the lower half contributes more to perceived length. The subjective bisection point `y_b` satisfies:
`∫_{y₁}^{y_b} S(y) dy = ∫_{y_b}^{y₂} S(y) dy`
Given `S(y)` is steeper below zero, `y_b > (y₁+y₂)/2`. The error `ε = y_b - (y₁+y₂)/2` is positive (too high).

**Units & Regimes:**
-   `S(y)`: Dimensionless scaling factor.
-   `y`: Normalized eccentricity (radians or deg).
-   Breakdown occurs when:
    1.  **Large Field:** `y` approaches ±π/2 (periphery), other cues dominate.
    2.  **Known Metric:** If the observer knows the line is on paper and consciously corrects, `S` is bypassed via top-down modulation.
    3.  **Non-Ground Context:** In virtual reality with explicit depth cues (stereopsis), `S(y)` is overridden by veridical `d`.

**Citation:** James [PAGE 264]: "...the lower half of the retina... ought to have acquired a habit of enlarging its pictures by imagination... if we try to bisect such a line we place the point of division about 1/6 of its length too high."

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This specific illusion lineage traces back through experimental psychology to foundational debates on perception.

1.  **Immediate Predecessor:** Charles Delboeuf (1879-1890s) empirically documented the bisection error, providing the quantitative hook. His work was part of the Belgian school's emphasis on precise measurement of mental phenomena.
2.  **James's Interpretation (1890):** He places it squarely in the "habits of thought" tradition, following his own principles of psychological continuity. The illusion is not a flaw but a byproduct of a useful adaptive mechanism. This opposes purely physiognomonic explanations.
3.  **The Wundt Counter-Hypothesis (cited †):** Wilhelm Wundt proposed a competing explanation based on "feeling of innervation"—the conscious sensation of eye muscle effort required to look up. This rooted the illusion in immediate sensorimotor feedback, not lifelong statistical learning.
4.  **Lipps' Refutation (also cited):** Theodor Lipps argued that if muscle effort were primary, the effect would scale with absolute eye displacement, not relative object parts. His observation that "only the halves of the same object look different" was a killer blow to Wundt's model and vindicated James's relational, object-centric view. This established that the prior operates on *segmented perceptual units*.
5.  **Modern Revival:** This logic directly informs Bayesian models of perception (Knill & Richards, 1996) and predictive coding frameworks. The "habit" is now termed a "prior distribution" over scene structure. The Delboeuf/Ponzo illusions are classic demonstrations of how priors distort metric judgments when conflicting with veridical cues.
6.  **Trap for Modern Researchers:** The danger is reifying the prior as a single static entity. In reality, it's a dynamic ensemble of cues (texture gradient, interposition, familiar size) that can be individually weighted or overridden. The historical debate between James (learning/habit) and Wundt (physiology) prefigures the modern nature vs. nurture debate in perception, reminding us that mechanisms are often layered.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
