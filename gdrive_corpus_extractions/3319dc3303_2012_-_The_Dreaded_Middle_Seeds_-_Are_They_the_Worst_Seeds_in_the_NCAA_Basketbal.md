Let \(W_i\in\{0,\ldots,6\}\) be the number of tournament wins for team \(i\), measured as a dimensionless count; let \(S_i\in\{8,9,10,11,12\}\) be the seed, measured as an ordinal rank. The paper’s advancement thresholds are \(r=2\) for Sweet Sixteen, \(r=3\) for Elite Eight, and \(r=4\) for Final Four, so the binary indicator is \(A_{i,r}=\mathbf{1}\{W_i\ge r\}\). Proportions are \(\hat p_{s,r}=X_{s,r}/n_s\), where \(X_{s,r}=\sum_{i:S_i=s}A_{i,r}\), and \(\hat p_{s,r}\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless. No SI physical units are used in the paper; the natural units are wins, seed rank, and probability. A binary game outcome carries at most \(\log_2 2=1\) bit of information, but the paper does not estimate entropy or information-theoretic quantities, so no bit-valued claim should be invented.

The mean-win operator is \(\bar W_s=n_s^{-1}\sum_i W_{s,i}\). The one-way ANOVA model is
\[
W_{s,i}=\mu+\tau_s+\varepsilon_{s,i},
\]
with \(s\in\{8/9,10,11,12\}\), \(i=1,\ldots,n_s\), and the working assumptions \(\varepsilon_{s,i}\sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) independently, though the 8/9 dependence is handled by the random half-sample for the all-team analysis. The test statistic is
\[
F=\frac{\sum_s n_s(\bar W_s-\bar W)^2/(k-1)}
{\sum_s\sum_i(W_{s,i}-\bar W_s)^2/(N-k)},
\]
with \(k=4\); \(F\) is dimensionless because wins squared cancel. The Dunnett one-tailed comparison against the 8/9 control uses
\[
t_s=\frac{\bar W_s-\bar W_{8/9}}
{\sqrt{MS_e\left(1/n_s+1/n_{8/9}\right)}},
\]
also dimensionless. The reported men’s all-team ANOVA is \(p=0.5529\), women’s all-team \(p=0.1840\); men’s first-win overall \(p=0.0179\) in Table 2b, women’s first-win \(p=0.1571\) [PAGE 9]. The men’s 10-seed Dunnett result is \(p=0.0098\), 11-seed \(p=0.0521\), 12-seed \(p=0.0967\) [PAGE 9].

\frac{(O_{s,y}-E_{s,y})^2}{E_{s,y}},
\]
with \(df=(4-1)(2-1)=3\), dimensionless. For the “yes” cell, \(E_{s,1}=n_s(X_{\cdot,r}/N)\), and for the “no” cell \(E_{s,0}=n_s(1-X_{\cdot,r}/N)\). P(W\ge r\mid do(S=s))=\sum_u P(W\ge r\mid S=s,U=u)P(U=u).
\]
These are equal only if \(P(U=u\mid S=s)=P(U=u)\) for all relevant \(u\), or if \(P(W\ge r\mid S=s,U=u)\) is constant in \(u\). Neither condition is established. Therefore the paper’s seed coefficients cannot be read as causal “worst seed” effects without the physical and biomechanical layers being measured or randomized.

Weather is a weak direct layer because NCAA basketball is indoor, but travel delays and arrival fatigue are still unmeasured. Biomechanics is stronger: injury and load affect both seeding information and tournament performance. The paper does not measure minutes, injuries, rest days, or travel. The physical bracket is fixed and finite: a 64-team single-elimination structure bounds wins and makes the second-round opponent path deterministic conditional on first-round survival. That physical structure is exactly why the 8/9 winner likely meets the 1 seed, but it also means the observed association is a bracket-path association, not proof of a seed curse. The cross-layer veto is thus: descriptive seed-win associations are admissible; causal “seed s is worse” claims are non-identifiable from these data.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
