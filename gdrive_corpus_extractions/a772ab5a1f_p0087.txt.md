<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage traces from Thorndike’s law of effect through Hull’s 1943 systematization to Pavlov’s conditioned-reflex framework as mediated by American elementary textbooks [PAGE 87]. Multi-week traps emerge from this lineage when short-term performance is mistaken for permanent habit change. Because Hull’s model bounds connection strength at 1 and permits superthreshold connections to persist while new de novo connections are formed [PAGE 87], an organism can show apparent acquisition under training and then relapse weeks later when the original superthreshold pathway reasserts itself. In applied behavioral settings, the trap is to schedule reinforcement densely over days, observe response suppression, and conclude that the old connection has been unlearned. Hull’s formulation implies instead that the old connection may remain superthreshold and merely masked [PAGE 87]. The genealogy is therefore retrocausal in the sense that later performance is governed by earlier-established superthreshold structures whose strength was never decremented, only bypassed.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when an organism under elevated drive reverts to previously superthreshold receptor-effector connections because those require less de novo computational scaffolding than newly formed connections [PAGE 87]. Hull’s account holds that selective learning and conditioned learning both instantiate new connections while strengthening already superthreshold ones [PAGE 87]. Under crisis-level drive, the fragile de novo pathways—dependent on precise temporal conjunction and nonzero drive decrements—are the first to degrade, leaving the robust superthreshold pathways to govern behavior. The observer may interpret this as a loss of learning, but under Hull’s formalism it is a regression to the high-strength baseline established before intervention [PAGE 87].

The counter-trap is the reverse inference: an outside observer sees a new response appear under low-stress training and infers that a permanent connection has been established de novo [PAGE 87]. Under the model, this inference is valid only if \(\mathcal{RF}=1\) repeatedly and if \(s_{ER}\) has measurably increased from its small initial value rather than remaining near zero while response frequency is temporarily inflated by drive suppression [PAGE 87]. The counter-trap is to confuse performance suppression (drive reduction without connection change) with learning (connection strengthening).

The clock is the conjunction window \(\tau_c\) measured in seconds, together with the post-reinforcement drive-decrement interval [PAGE 87]. If the afferent discharge and the reaction do not fall within \(\tau_c\), the receptor-effector connection cannot be established [PAGE 87]. If reinforcement (“abolition of the shock injury or need”) is delayed beyond the immediate post-conjunction period, the exact conformity with the law of primary reinforcement breaks down [PAGE 87]. Thus the clock sets a hard upper bound on the delay between neural discharge, response execution, and need abolition; behavioral procedures that ignore this clock will fail to produce the de novo connections Hull describes, regardless of how many trials are administered over weeks.
