Coase (1937), "The Nature of the Firm," supplies the transaction-cost mechanism: if insiders know each other's "personalities, motives, and policies tolerably well" [PAGE 359], they economize on the information and bargaining costs that would plague a diffuse public principal. Alchian and Demsetz (1972), "Production, Information Costs, and Economic Organization," formalize team production and the difficulty of observing individual marginal product—the exact "insuperable difficulty of coöperative production" [PAGE 359] Knight describes in psychological terms. Olson (1965), The Logic of Collective Action, supplies the group-size theorem: large groups cannot act in their common interest without selective incentives, which maps directly onto the \(N > N^*\) breakdown regime above. Ostrom (1990), Governing the Commons, offers the counter-lineage: common-pool institutions can survive at scale only with bounded groups, graduated sanctions, and low-cost monitoring—conditions absent in Knight's diffuse socialist principal.

Multi-week traps. In an organizational setting, the pathology on page 359 does not appear instantaneously. Week 1–2: insider monitoring remains salient; the manager still responds to the small clique. Week 3–6: as the public principal is asserted and insider oversight is formally diluted, the manager's marginal accountability signal decays; shirking accumulates as unobserved variance in \(\epsilon\). Week 7–12: productivity drift becomes visible, but because no single member of the public can attribute the decline to the manager's effort (the \(1/N\) marginal benefit is below perception threshold), no corrective sanction is applied. The trap is self-reinforcing: the more diffuse the ownership, the less information any one principal has to re-concentrate oversight, so the insider clique can quietly reassert informal control while maintaining the legal fiction of public accountability. The counter-trap is the belief—explicitly labeled superficial by Knight [PAGE 359]—that generations of political democracy will generate enough civic responsibility to operate industrial democracy. This is a trap because it substitutes a low-stakes political training ground for the high-stakes residual incentive structure that page 359 identifies as the actual binding constraint.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under pressure, the manager described on page 359 regresses to the cognitive and institutional mode in which he was actually formed: direct accountability to a small, legible group. When a crisis hits, the diffuse social principal cannot process information fast enough; the manager reverts to the insider clique logic because that is the only accountability structure in which his effort has a detectable marginal product. He "knows" the insiders' personalities, motives, and policies tolerably well [PAGE 359], so he can predict their reactions; he cannot predict "society." Thus crisis habit regression is not a moral failure but a Bayesian update: under high ambiguity, the manager re-weights his belief toward the low-ambiguity insider information set \(P_I\) and away from the public ambiguity set \(\mathcal{P}_{\text{amb}}\).

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the recursive misreading of Knight's text as an argument for gradual democratic preparation. Because Knight says political democracy encounters the same difficulty as coöperative production but its alternatives "relate to less vital matters" [PAGE 359], a theorist might conclude that extending democratic practice will eventually lower the difficulty. This is a trap: it treats responsibility as a transferable stock rather than as an incentive flow contingent on observability and residual claimancy. If the institutional design keeps \(\beta_S \approx 0\) because \(N > N^*\), then no amount of prior democratic training raises effort; the counter-trap consumes political energy without altering the payoff structure.

Clock. The decision-clock variable is \(\Delta t\), the latency from shock detection to managerial response. For the insider clique, \(\Delta t_I\) is short because mutual knowledge permits high-bandwidth, low-latency coordination among \(n \le 150\) actors [PAGE 359]. For the diffuse public principal, \(\Delta t_S\) scales with communication and aggregation cost, plausibly superlinearly in \(N\). Under crisis, the relevant clock is shock arrival time \(\Delta t_{\text{shock}}\). If \(\Delta t_S > \Delta t_{\text{shock}} > \Delta t_I\), the insider-controlled firm acts while the socially managed firm is still deliberating. The recursive theory of mind, therefore, is: the manager expects the public principal to be slow and diffuse, so he anticipates that only insider-level accountability will matter when decisions must be made; he therefore regresses to insider-mode decision-making precisely when the clock is fastest.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto. Information processing and monitoring are physical activities bounded by thermodynamic and communication constraints. Monitoring \(N\) principals requires signal acquisition, transmission, and computation whose energy cost scales at least linearly in \(N\), and plausibly superlinearly because each principal must compare manager effort against a common output \(Y\) to infer marginal contribution. Landauer's principle imposes a minimum energy per bit erased; aggregating \(N\) independent judgments about whether results depend on individual activity requires bit operations that grow with \(N\). Therefore the physical substrate cannot sustain the dense, low-latency accountability relation Knight attributes to insider groups at public scale. Veto condition: if monitoring energy per principal exceeds available institutional energy budget, diffuse social ownership is physically infeasible without selective incentive technologies (e.g., automated output attribution) not present in the text [PAGE 359].

Biomechanical layer veto. Human cognition imposes hard bounds on the size of a group whose members can "know each other's personalities, motives, and policies tolerably well" [PAGE 359]. Working memory, facial recognition, and social-bond maintenance constrain \(n\) to the Dunbar range (~150), with intimate knowledge clusters far smaller. A public principal of millions cannot satisfy the cognitive precondition for direct accountability. The individual manager, facing \(N \gg 150\), cannot model the principal as a single intentional agent; he experiences the "mass" as an unknowable environment, which produces the helplessness Knight describes [PAGE 359]. Veto condition: when \(N\) exceeds the cognitive bandwidth for shared intentionality, the premise that the manager is accountable to society as to insiders collapses.

Weather layer veto. Exogenous volatility—market demand shifts, input-price shocks, technological change—arrives as a stochastic process with realization times shorter than the deliberation cycle of a diffuse principal. "Weather" here is environmental non-stationarity: \(\epsilon\) in \(Y = \theta e + \epsilon\) has time-varying distribution. An insider clique with dense ties can re-estimate \(\theta\) and re-coordinate \(e\) on a sub-weekly clock. A diffuse public cannot aggregate new information fast enough to update the manager's incentive contract before the next shock. Veto condition: if shock frequency \(f_{\text{shock}} > 1/\Delta t_S\), the socially managed firm is permanently one step behind; the insider-controlled firm survives because \(\Delta t_I < 1/f_{\text{shock}}\).

Adjudication: All three layers veto the strong form of Knight's speculative hope (that political democracy can train responsibility sufficient for industrial democracy) and support his structural claim (that small-group insider control is the stable, observable accountability equilibrium). The physical layer vetoes monitoring at scale, the biomechanical layer vetoes shared intentionality at scale, and the weather layer vetoes coordinated response at scale. The only regime in which the text's socialist-manager scenario remains viable is one with \(N \le N^*\), selective individual incentives, and exogenous stability—none of which describe "the public at large" [PAGE 359].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Full diffusion without selective incentives. Counterfactual: A large firm transitions from insider control to complete social ownership in which every citizen holds an equal \(1/N\) residual share, no performance dashboard exists, and managerial pay is a fixed salary plus an equal dividend. Verifiable prediction: Managerial effort \(e\), measured by observable output per labor hour or by internal audit of decision velocity, declines by at least one standard deviation within two quarters of transition. Veto condition: If productivity and decision velocity remain statistically unchanged after controlling for market conditions, Knight's collective-action breakdown (\(N > N^* \Rightarrow e^*=0\)) is falsified for this context. RLVR signal: reward for predicting the direction of effort change; penalty for predicting stability.

Scenario 2 — Transparent dashboard under diffuse ownership. Counterfactual: The same socially owned firm installs a real-time, public dashboard linking manager actions to firm output, visible to all \(N\) principals, but residual shares remain \(1/N\) and no individual can capture more than a tiny fraction of the marginal product. Verifiable prediction: Effort does not increase with dashboard precision; the partial derivative \(\partial e / \partial (\text{dashboard resolution})\) is statistically indistinguishable from zero because \(\partial u_i / \partial e = \theta/N - \kappa e < 0\) for all \(i\) when \(N > N^*\). Veto condition: If effort rises proportionally to dashboard resolution, then the binding constraint was information asymmetry rather than residual-share dilution, falsifying the size-driven singularity. RLVR signal: reward for correctly predicting null effect; penalty for predicting effort increase.

Scenario 3 — Crisis decision-speed comparison. Counterfactual: An insider-controlled firm and a worker-cooperative (diffuse principal) face an identical, unannounced demand shock requiring a pricing decision within 48 hours. Verifiable prediction: The insider firm reaches and implements a decision in \(\Delta t_I < 24\) hours; the cooperative requires \(\Delta t_S > 48\) hours or fails to implement a consensus decision, because \(f_{\text{shock}} > 1/\Delta t_S\). Veto condition: If the cooperative implements a decision faster than the insider firm under high ambiguity, then the weather-layer veto is invalid and dense insider ties are not a necessary coordination advantage. RLVR signal: reward for predicting the insider firm's speed advantage; penalty for predicting cooperative speed parity or superiority.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
