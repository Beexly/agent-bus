The source is a 54-page honors thesis by Victor Luo, advised by Steven J. Miller, titled “Relieving and Readjusting Pythagoras.” The central multi-hop derivation is: (1) model team runs scored and runs allowed per game as independent Weibull random variables; (2) use the Weibull mean to connect scale parameters to observed season runs; (3) compute the probability that runs scored exceed runs allowed, which yields a Pythagorean-style win/loss percentage; (4) generalize from one Weibull to a convex linear combination of two Weibulls; (5) estimate the seven free parameters by least squares over binned run distributions; and (6) compare predictions against observed wins, single-Weibull predictions, and ESPN’s Expected WL. The thesis states the original James formula as “runs scored^γ divided by runs scored^γ plus runs allowed^γ,” with γ initially 2 but empirically better near 1.83 [PAGE 4]. The Weibull justification appears in Section 2.1, where the density is introduced and the single-Weibull predicted won-loss percentage is derived [PAGE 6]. The source explicitly fixes β = −1/2 so that integer scores sit at bin centers: “One other important assumption we will make is that β = −1/2 for a particular Weibull” [PAGE 7]. It also flags the literal falsehood of independence: “runs scored and runs allowed are independent. This obviously cannot be true, as a baseball game never ends in a tie” [PAGE 7]. The paper treats this as approximately true after structural-zero testing.

The first major empirical branch is sabermetric adjustment. Ballpark factor is defined as home runs divided by away runs, adjusted by home and away games, and then tested by dividing game scores by the factor [PAGE 10]. The result is not an improvement: “ballpark factors only slightly increase the predictive power of the Pythagorean W/L formula in some years; however, the minuscule increases observed in some years cannot be considered statistically significant by any means” [PAGE 12]. Game-state truncation is also tested. The author uses thresholds of 95% and 98% win expectancy, stops the game at that threshold, and uses the truncated score. WAR is defined as “the number of wins a player adds (or subtracts) compared to a replacement level player” [PAGE 19], but its standardization is arbitrary and harms fit.

The second branch is the linear combination of Weibulls. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Bill James published the Pythagorean Win/Loss Formula in his 1977 Baseball Abstract; the thesis says the formula was invented “in order to predict how many wins a baseball team should have won, given their runs scored and runs allowed” [PAGE 4]. The retrocausal lineage runs from James’s closed-form heuristic to Miller’s Weibull derivation, then to the MCGLP paper and this thesis. The source explicitly says previous work modeled runs scored and allowed by independent Weibull distributions and found that “runs scored and allowed were statistically independent and that the single Weibull with best fit parameters obtained from the maximum likelihood method and least squares method provide good fits for the observed data” [PAGE 4]. The thesis then extends the lineage by replacing the single Weibull with a linear combination and comparing it to ESPN’s Expected WL and baseball-reference’s Pythagorean WL [PAGE 30, PAGE 31]. The historical irony is that the exponent 1.83 emerges as a fitted Weibull shape mean: “Using the method of least squares, the mean γ over all 30 teams is 1.83 with a standard deviation of 0.18 (the median is 1.79)” [PAGE 27]. Thus the old Pythagorean exponent is retrospectively justified by a distributional model.

Multi-week traps are practical and computational. The thesis needed “each box score of every game in a season (so 2430 urls to go through)” [PAGE 13] and used Amazon EC2 to parallelize the culling. The code shows repeated URL checks, doubleheader handling, team-abbreviation normalization, missing-table guards, and manual remapping of codes such as NYY to NYA, LAD to LAN, CHC to CHN, and WSN to WAS [PAGE 36-39]. Structural zeros in the independence test are another trap: runs scored and allowed cannot be equal, so the diagonal of the contingency table is inaccessible, requiring specialized test theory [PAGE 7, PAGE 34]. There is also a model-selection trap: game-state truncation sounds physically sensible but discards comeback information and lowers R² [PAGE 14]. The thesis’s genealogy therefore warns that historical continuity is not the same as empirical improvement; the simplest inherited formula repeatedly survives added “whistles and bells” [PAGE 21].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when the analyst responds to residual error by continually adding variables: ballpark, then game state, then WHIP, then ERA+, then WAR. The source records the expectation and the failure: after testing WHIP, ERA+, and WAR, “the regular form of the Pythagorean W/L Formula is still a much better predictor of a team’s W/L percentage” [PAGE 17]. The counter-trap is to use the simplest robust model and verify it with out-of-sample or cross-season tests. The thesis does this with a t-test against the single Weibull and a χ² fit test [PAGE 29, PAGE 33]. The clock matters: a 162-game season and 2,430 total games wash out many game-level effects; the source notes that with so many games, ballpark factors “wash out over the entire season” [PAGE 12]. Game-state truncation is a clock error because stopping at 95% or 98% throws away late comebacks and good evaluating information [PAGE 14].

Recursive theory of mind also applies to benchmarking. ESPN’s ExWL uses the Pythagorean form with γ=2, while the thesis compares it to a fitted linear Weibull mixture and to baseball-reference’s γ=1.83 version. The recursive trap is overclaiming superiority against a public benchmark. The source’s own t-test says the 3.03 versus 3.09 difference is not statistically significant: “With a very large p-value, we fail to reject the null hypothesis” [PAGE 31]. The correct mental model is therefore opponent-aware but modest: the linear Weibull beats a single Weibull significantly [PAGE 29], but it does not decisively beat the best available Pythagorean benchmark. The “clock” also limits the c1 weight: because \(c_1\) has mean 0.21 and standard deviation 0.39 in 2011 [PAGE 35], a simple universal formula is not feasible. The counter-trap is not to force simplicity by ignoring parameter volatility.
