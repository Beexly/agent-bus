1. Proper scoring for binary outcomes. For \(N\) forecasts \(p_i\in[0,1]\) and outcomes \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\),
\[
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2,\qquad [\mathrm{BS}]=1,
\]
\[
\mathrm{LL}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right],\qquad [\mathrm{LL}]=\mathrm{nat}.
\]
Assumption: \(y_i\) are exchangeable within the evaluation window and \(p_i\) are out-of-sample. Breakdown/singularity: \(\mathrm{LL}\) is singular at \(p_i=0\) with \(y_i=1\) or \(p_i=1\) with \(y_i=0\); practical evaluation requires clipping \(p_i\in[\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]\). The review’s calibration-first result is explicit: “optimizing predictive models for calibration, rather than accuracy, leads to significantly higher betting profits, with a calibration-optimized model generating 69.86% higher average returns compared to an accuracy-optimized model” [PAGE 5].

2. Ordered-class scoring. For \(K\) ordered outcomes and cumulative forecast/observed distributions \(F_i(k),O_i(k)\),
\[
\mathrm{RPS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\left(F_i(k)-O_i(k)\right)^2,\qquad [\mathrm{RPS}]=1.
\]
Assumption: outcomes have a meaningful order, such as win/draw/loss or score bands. Breakdown: RPS loses interpretation if class order is arbitrary, if abandoned or tied matches are inconsistently encoded, or if probability mass is assigned to impossible classes. The review reports soccer RPS values such as 0.197 [PAGE 8] and 0.2101 with cross-entropy 1.0246 [PAGE 9], and basketball calibration with “a Brier score of 0.075” [PAGE 13]. 3. Calibration error. With \(M\) probability bins \(B_m\),
\[
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{N}\left|\frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i\in B_m}y_i-\frac{1}{|B_m|}\sum_{i\in B_m}p_i\right|,\qquad [\mathrm{ECE}]=1.
\]
Assumption: bins are large enough for stable conditional estimates. Breakdown: sparse bins make ECE noisy; extreme base rates make bin-wise calibration unstable. \]
\[
f^*=\arg\max_{0\le f\le 1}G(f).
\]
Assumption: \(p\) is the true win probability and \(r\) is a settlement payoff, not a sports-market handicap. Breakdown/singularity: \(f=1\) is singular if a loss event has positive probability; \(f^*<0\) when \(p\le 1/(1+r)\), meaning the position is not admissible; overbetting \(f>f^*\) can produce negative expected log growth. The review cites an “adaptive fractional Kelly method” [PAGE 5], which is precisely a shrinkage of \(f^*\) to control estimation error.

\max_{w}\ \mu^\top w-\lambda w^\top\Sigma w\quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{1}^\top w=1,\ w\ge0.
\]
Units: \(\mu\) and \(w\) are dimensionless per-stake quantities; \(\lambda\) has inverse-return units. Assumption: \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) are estimated from a stationary window. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer has three competing mechanisms: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock pressure.

Crisis habit regression. Under stress, an agent’s policy can collapse toward a low-entropy habit. Let \(Q_t(a)\) be the estimated value of action \(a\) at time \(t\), and let
\[
\pi_t(a)=\frac{\exp(\beta_t Q_t(a))}{\sum_{a'}\exp(\beta_t Q_t(a'))},\qquad [\pi_t]=1.
\]
If \(\beta_t\) decreases under crisis, the policy becomes more uniform or more prior-driven; if \(\beta_t\) increases, it becomes more deterministic. “Habit regression” corresponds to a shift toward a previously reinforced action rather than a fresh multi-step computation. The review’s live setting—“real-time and in-play bets” [PAGE 1]—creates exactly the conditions for this regression because computation time is bounded.

Counter-trap. A level-0 agent uses a prior; a level-1 agent best-responds to level-0; a level-2 agent best-responds to level-1. Formally, if \(a^{(k+1)}=\mathrm{BR}(a^{(k)})\), then an apparent improvement at level \(k+1\) can be reversed when the opponent moves to level \(k+2\). The review’s play-call prediction [PAGE 21] and quarterback decision modeling [PAGE 25] are examples where the “opponent” is a strategic actor. A model that predicts the previous equilibrium can be counter-trapped by adaptation. The review’s warning about “the dynamic nature of sports events” and “frequent model retraining” [PAGE 5] is a partial defense, but retraining on the last equilibrium can itself become the trap.

Clock. Let \(T_{\mathrm{budget}}\) be the live decision budget and \(\gamma\in(0,1]\) a discount factor per second. A value-of-information calculation should compare
\[
\Delta V=\gamma^{\Delta t}\left[V_{\mathrm{new}}-V_{\mathrm{old}}\right]
\]
against the cost of delay. If \(\Delta t\) is large or if the tail latency violates \(T_{\mathrm{budget}}\), the rational policy is to fall back to a simpler, pre-committed rule. The review’s real-time scope [PAGE 1] and its discussion of computational resources [PAGE 53] make the clock a first-class variable. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that a model should be vetoed if it cannot state which mechanism is active—habit, counter-trap, or clock—because the same accuracy number can arise from a stable signal or from a strategic artifact.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto is not a heuristic add-on; it follows from proper-scoring convexity. Suppose the true conditional probability is
\[
p_i=\sigma\left(\beta_0+\beta^\top X_i+\gamma W_i+\delta L_i\right),
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), \(W_i\) is weather severity, and \(L_i\) is biomechanical load. Let the model that ignores \(W_i,L_i\) produce
\[
\tilde p_i=\sigma\left(\beta_0+\beta^\top X_i\right).
\]
If \(\gamma<0\) and \(\delta<0\), and if \(W_i\ge W_c\) or \(L_i\ge L_c\), then
\[
\beta_0+\beta^\top X_i+\gamma W_i+\delta L_i<\beta_0+\beta^\top X_i,
\]
so \(p_i<\tilde p_i\). For the Brier score, the expected penalty of using \(\tilde p_i\) instead of \(p_i\) is
\[
\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde p_i-Y_i)^2\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(p_i-Y_i)^2\right]=(\tilde p_i-p_i)^2>0.
\]
Thus, under the stated monotonicity and measurement assumptions, ignoring the physical layer strictly worsens the proper score. This is the formal veto proof. The review gives empirical support: weather features are used in soccer prediction [PAGE 10], fatigue and injury are used in tennis [PAGE 14], “Home court advantage, Player tiredness” appears in basketball features [PAGE 12], body fat and speed are influential in rugby [PAGE 35], testosterone shows temporal persistence [PAGE 38], power-law intensity–duration relations are strong [PAGE 39], and one-on-one outcomes depend on speed and angle [PAGE 47]. The veto adjudication hierarchy is therefore: (1) data-integrity veto if features are missing or post-hoc; (2) physical veto if \(V_i=1\); (3) statistical veto if calibration or proper score fails; (4) strategic veto if counter-trap or clock failure is detected. Breakdown regimes: the proof fails if \(W_i,L_i\) are measured with differential error, if the veto threshold is misspecified, if load is endogenous to the outcome, or if the training support does not contain the veto stratum. In those cases the correct response is not to trust the model more; it is to widen the support or abstain.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
