<THINK_TRACE>
The source paper proposes a tightly coupled perception-tracking pipeline for indoor RGB-D multi-object tracking. The central causal chain is: RGB detection and segmentation produce object proposals; additional voxel and NOCS heads predict dense object shape and correspondence fields; depth plus camera intrinsics back-project the NOCS and depth patches into point clouds; a rigid alignment solves for a 7-DoF pose; then a graph neural network reasons over relative poses and geometry embeddings across frames to infer temporally consistent tracklets. This chain is explicitly described in [PAGE 1]: “We propose a novel approach for joint 3D multi-object tracking and reconstruction from RGB-D sequences in indoor environments.” and [PAGE 1]: “We leverage those correspondences to inform a graph neural network to solve for the optimal, temporally-consistent 7-DoF pose trajectories of all objects.”

A multi-hop derivation exposes several gray zones. First, the quantitative claim of superiority depends on a synthetic benchmark. The authors state in [PAGE 1]: “we introduce a new synthetic dataset comprising 2381 unique indoor sequences with a total of 60k rendered RGB-D images” and later in [PAGE 7]: “outperforming baselines in overall MOTA by 24.8% and F1-score by 0.117.” The evidence for real-world behavior is qualitative, not a full quantitative benchmark. On real-world sequences, the paper says in [PAGE 8] that its method “achieves more accurate reconstructions and more precise pose estimation over time,” but the main tabular evaluation remains on MOTFront. Therefore, the causal inference from synthetic MOTA gain to real-world reliability is incomplete.

Second, the pose operator is a rigid alignment between a back-projected depth point cloud and a predicted NOCS point cloud. The source states in [PAGE 5] that “the Umeyama algorithm [27] is employed to find the optimal 7-dimensional rigid transformation to align both cleaned point clouds.” This operator is exact only when the two point sets are non-degenerate, approximately correspond to the same object surface, and are not dominated by symmetry. If the NOCS prediction is noisy, if depth holes remove critical surface regions, or if the object is symmetric, the estimated scale, rotation, and translation become weakly identifiable. That uncertainty propagates into the edge embedding because the graph consumes relative translation, relative rotation, relative scale, and relative time. The source defines this in [PAGE 6] through the edge encoder input: relative translation, relative rotation, log relative scale, and time-step difference. A pose error therefore becomes an association error downstream.

Third, the temporal graph has a bounded receptive field. The paper says in [PAGE 5]: “we deﬁne a bidirectional graph neural network connecting object detections in consecutive frames within a window of 5 neighboring frames.” and in [PAGE 6]: “After a number of message passing steps nmp = 4.” If an object disappears for more than the effective temporal support of the graph, the model lacks the contextual edges required to maintain a unique tracklet. The source also describes a fallback rule: “Nodes with no prior connections create a new trajectory if their instance id does not already exist in any other trajectory” [PAGE 6]. Thus, long occlusion or missed detection can induce track fragmentation, which MOTA penalizes as misses or identity switches.

Fourth, the matching criterion is geometrically coarse but operationally decisive. The paper states in [PAGE 7]: “A match is considered positive if its L2 distance to the ground truth center is less than 40cm.” This means that center-distance accuracy below forty centimeters can preserve MOTA even if orientation or shape reconstruction is imperfect. Conversely, a symmetric object with correct center but ambiguous heading may still be counted as a match. This creates a gray-zone evaluation trap: MOTA can improve while pose observability for downstream robotics remains weak.

Fifth, the geometry representation is bounded by voxel resolution. The reconstruction head predicts a 32³ voxel grid, and the authors acknowledge in [PAGE 8]: “As our reconstruction is limited by the dense voxel grid resolution, ﬁne-scale details cannot be captured.” This matters because the geometry embedding is used as a node feature for graph association. If two objects of the same category have similar coarse voxel signatures, the graph may need to rely almost entirely on relative pose. If pose is noisy, association collapses. If objects are heavily occluded, the voxel head may complete implausible shapes, creating false geometry priors that mislead the tracker.

Sixth, the training losses reveal competing pressures. The object-level loss combines detection, NOCS correspondence, and reconstruction, while the tracking loss is a weighted binary cross-entropy over active and inactive graph edges. The source gives the object loss in [PAGE 6]: “Lobj = Ldet + 3 · Lnoc + 0.75 · Lrec” and the tracking loss as a weighted BCE. The weighting indicates that correspondence matching is emphasized more strongly than voxel reconstruction in the object-level objective. However, the graph classifier must handle severe class imbalance because active associations are rare relative to all possible non-active pairs. The source notes this imbalance and introduces a weighting factor for active edges in [PAGE 6]. If the classifier is under-calibrated, the system may prefer conservative new tracks over risky associations, reducing identity switches but increasing fragmentation.

In summary, the paper’s method is internally coherent, but the causal proof is strongest on synthetic data, weakest under real-world depth corruption, symmetric ambiguity, long occlusion, and fine-grained geometric requirements. The central audit conclusion is that the reported MOTA gain is credible as a benchmark improvement, but it is not sufficient by itself to establish robust physical-world tracking without additional verifiable constraints on pose error, temporal continuity, and geometry observability.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper supplies several formal operators that can be audited with explicit units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Repulsive sampling weight for synthetic object placement.
The source says in [PAGE 4]: “we weight the sampling direction with a factor σ(n, d(x, x′))” and gives the operator:
\[
\sigma(n,d(x,x')) = \frac{1}{2\sigma_0}\left(\frac{1}{d(x,x')}-\frac{1}{d^*}\right)^2
\]
for \(d(x,x') &lt; d^*\) and \(n &lt; N_{\max}\), otherwise \(\sigma=1\).
Here \(x,x'\) are 3D object positions in meters, \(d(x,x')=\|x-x'\|_2\) is a distance in meters, \(d^*\) is a repulsion threshold in meters, \(\sigma_0\) is a sampler scale parameter whose units are not fully specified in the source, and \(n\) is a trial index. The operator is used to discourage object-obstacle proximity during synthetic trajectory generation. Breakdown regime: as \(d(x,x')\to 0\), the term diverges, making the weight numerically unstable. If \(d^*\) is too small, the sampler rarely activates; if \(d^*\) is too large, the sampler may prevent plausible dense arrangements. The assumption is that distances are Euclidean and collision checking is reliable. The source also states in [PAGE 4]: “We randomly choose rooms that contain at least 5 objects.”

2. Depth and NOCS back-projection.
The method uses a depth patch and a NOCS patch to infer pose. The source says in [PAGE 5]: “we backproject depth patch IDepth and NOCs patch INOCs to point clouds Po and Pn.” A standard back-projection operator is:
\[
P = K^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} d,
\]
where \(u,v\) are pixel coordinates, \(d\) is depth in meters if the sensor provides metric depth, and \(K\) is the camera intrinsic matrix with units of pixels. The resulting point \(P\) is in camera coordinates, in meters under metric depth. Breakdown regime: invalid depth, missing pixels, reflective surfaces, or sensor noise produce outlier points. The paper addresses this with outlier removal, but the operator remains sensitive to depth holes because pose alignment requires sufficient surface support.

3. Umeyama rigid alignment.
The source states in [PAGE 5]: “the Umeyama algorithm [27] is employed to find the optimal 7-dimensional rigid transformation to align both cleaned point clouds.” The formal optimization is:
\[
(c^*, R^*, t^*) := \arg\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}, R\in SO(3), t\in\mathbb{R}^3} \left\| P_o - (c R P_n + t) \right\|.
\]
Here \(P_o\) is the observed depth point cloud, \(P_n\) is the predicted NOCS point cloud, \(c\) is a positive scale, \(R\in SO(3)\) is a rotation matrix satisfying \(R^T R = I\) and \(\det R = 1\), and \(t\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is translation. If \(P_o\) and \(P_n\) are in meters after normalization, \(t\) has units of meters and \(c\) is dimensionless. If NOCS points are normalized coordinates rather than metric points, the scale absorbs the unit mismatch. Breakdown regimes: (i) degenerate point distributions, such as planar or collinear clouds, make rotation weakly observable; (ii) symmetric objects create multiple minima; (iii) mismatched partial views reduce correspondence support; (iv) outliers can bias the closed-form solution. The paper mitigates outliers but cannot remove geometric ambiguity.

4. Pose-graph edge embedding.
The source defines an edge feature in [PAGE 6]:
\[
e_{ij} = N_{\text{edge-enc}}\left([t_j - t_i, R_j - R_i, \log(c_j/c_i), s_j - s_i]\right).
\]
Units: \(t_j-t_i\) is a relative translation in meters if poses are metric; \(R_j-R_i\) is a rotation difference, often represented as Euler angles in radians or another angular unit; \(c_j/c_i\) is dimensionless, so \(\log(c_j/c_i)\) is dimensionless; \(s_j-s_i\) is a temporal difference in frame indices. Breakdown regime: if \(c_i\) or \(c_j\) is non-positive, the logarithm is undefined. In practice, scale estimates from imperfect alignment may approach zero for small or distant objects, creating unstable features. The graph is defined over a window of five frames [PAGE 5] and uses four message-passing steps [PAGE 6], so the operator assumes that useful temporal evidence lies within that support.

5. Object-level loss.
The source gives the object loss in [PAGE 6]:
\[
L_{\text{obj}} = L_{\text{det}} + 3 \cdot L_{\text{noc}} + 0.75 \cdot L_{\text{rec}}.
\]
The NOCS loss is described as a symmetric smooth-\(L_1\) loss for tables:
\[
L_{\text{noc}} = \min_{i \in \{0^\circ,180^\circ\}} L_{\text{smooth-1}}(I^i_{\text{NOCS}}, I^{\text{gt}}_{\text{NOCS}})
\]
when the predicted class is table, otherwise a direct smooth-\(L_1\) between predicted and ground-truth NOCS patches. The reconstruction loss is a weighted binary cross-entropy:
\[
L_{\text{rec}} = w_{\text{occ}} \cdot \text{BCE}(O_{\text{occ}}, O^t_{\text{occ}}) + \text{BCE}(O_{\text{free}}, O^t_{\text{free}}).
\]
Units: losses are scalar dimensionless quantities. If logarithms are natural, information units are nats; if base-2 logarithms are used, bits. The weighting \(3\) for NOCS indicates that correspondence accuracy is prioritized over voxel reconstruction in the object-level objective. Breakdown regime: symmetric classes can make the minimum operation collapse distinct headings into one loss value, reducing rotation discriminability.

6. Tracking loss and graph classification.
The source gives in [PAGE 6]:
\[
L_{\text{track}} = w_{\text{act}} \cdot \text{BCE}(e_{\text{act}}, e^t_{\text{act}}) + \text{BCE}(e_{\text{non-act}}, e^t_{\text{non-act}}).
\]
The active edge class is rare relative to inactive edges, so \(w_{\text{act}}\) is used to counter imbalance. Breakdown regime: if the classifier becomes too permissive, it creates false connections and identity switches; if too conservative, it fragments tracks. The source also says in [PAGE 6]: “We discard graph connections between object detections which have a 3D IoU lower than a threshold τ = 0.05.” This is a hard gate; if the predicted 3D box has poor IoU due to pose or scale error, the edge may be discarded before classification.

7. MOTA metric.
The source defines MOTA in [PAGE 7]:
\[
\text{MOTA} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (m_t + fp_t + mme_t)}{\sum_t gt_t}.
\]
Units: \(m_t\), \(fp_t\), \(mme_t\), and \(gt_t\) are counts at time \(t\); MOTA is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: if \(\sum_t gt_t = 0\), the metric is undefined. MOTA is also insensitive to some pose errors if centers match within the threshold. The source states in [PAGE 7]: “A match is considered positive if its L2 distance to the ground truth center is less than 40cm.” This threshold is 0.40 m in SI units.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits inside a lineage that explains both its strength and its risks. The strongest ancestor is tracking-by-detection. The source says in [PAGE 3]: “A common approach for MOT is the tracking-by-detection paradigm” and notes that objects are localized first and then associated. This lineage supplies the assumption that detection quality gates tracking quality. If the Mask R-CNN backbone misses an object, the downstream graph cannot recover it except through temporal completion, which is limited by the five-frame window.

A second ancestor is object-level SLAM and dynamic reconstruction. The source reviews in [PAGE 2]: “Dynamicfusion [20] introduces the reconstruction and tracking of dynamic, non-rigid scenes in SLAM” and also cites Mask-Fusion and MID-Fusion. These works establish the idea that geometry can constrain tracking, but they also reveal a recurring trap: geometry-based association fails when surfaces are incomplete, textureless, or symmetric. The current paper inherits this risk because it uses voxel geometry and NOCS correspondences rather than rich appearance.

A third ancestor is the immediate predecessor method, Seeing behind objects. The source says in [PAGE 3] that Müller et al. “propose a framework that jointly performs object completion and pose estimation, where objects are associated in a frame-to-frame fashion levering hand-crafted heuristics for object assignment.” The new method’s contribution is to replace heuristic frame-to-frame association with learned message passing. The source states in [PAGE 3]: “In contrast, our method learns optimal object associations over time in an end-to-end fashion, using a neural message passing network while performing fully differentiable pose estimation and 3D reconstruction.” This is a clear genealogical break: from hand-crafted association to learned association.

A fourth ancestor is graph-based MOT. The source cites in [PAGE 3]: “Brasó et al. [4] propose a fully differentiable framework based on a message passing network modeling temporal dependencies to perform MOT for outdoor scenes” and “GNN3DMOT [34] introduces a graph neural network using 2D and 3D features for the MOT domain.” The current paper’s novelty is to use geometry embeddings derived from reconstructed voxels and relative pose features for indoor tracking. The genealogical risk is that graph methods can amplify correlated errors: if pose estimates for adjacent frames share the same NOCS bias, the graph may confidently link wrong detections.

A fifth ancestor is normalized object coordinate space, or NOCS. The source says in [PAGE 4]: “Several approaches [6, 30] propose to map object instances to a Normalized Object Coordinate Space (NOCs) to infer object poses by predicting NOCs maps.” NOCS gives dense correspondence, but it also introduces category-level canonicalization. For symmetric categories, canonicalization can erase heading information. The paper partially acknowledges this by using a symmetry-aware NOCS loss for tables [PAGE 6], but the acknowledgment does not remove the underlying ambiguity.

A sixth ancestor is synthetic indoor data generation from 3D-FRONT. The source says in [PAGE 3]: “MOTFront is created based on assets and scene layouts of the 3D-Front dataset [12].” It also says in [PAGE 3]: “To the best of our knowledge, a publicly available dataset for indoor 3D MOT currently does not exist.” This genealogical context explains why the benchmark is synthetic. However, the multi-week trap is that improvements on MOTFront may reflect adaptation to the renderer, camera sampler, and object sampler rather than robust physical tracking. The source’s own sampling operator [PAGE 4] and interest-score camera sampling can shape the distribution of motion, occlusion, and viewpoints. If the test sequences inherit those biases, the model may be evaluated on a distribution that is easier than real indoor environments.

The retro-causal audit is therefore: the paper’s claim is plausible because it improves the missing pieces of prior systems—differentiable pose estimation, learned association, and joint geometry. But the same lineage predicts failure modes: synthetic domain bias, symmetry ambiguity, sparse depth, and limited temporal context. A robust research program should treat the reported 24.8% MOTA improvement as a necessary but not sufficient signal. The next evidentiary layer should be real-world quantitative validation with depth-noise stress tests, rotation-error metrics, and long-occlusion stress tests.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit asks how different agents will interpret the paper’s claims over time, especially under pressure, deadline, or deployment constraints. The relevant agents are: the benchmark-oriented researcher, the robotics integrator, the reviewer or auditor, and the automated model itself.

The first failure mode is crisis habit regression. Under publication or deployment pressure, a team may regress to the most salient metric: MOTA. The paper emphasizes in [PAGE 1]: “our method improves the accumulated MOTA score for all test sequences by 24.8% over existing state-of-the-art methods.” A pressured agent may treat this as sufficient evidence of robustness. The regression is dangerous because MOTA is a count-based tracking metric, not a full physical-state metric. The paper itself defines matching by center distance [PAGE 7]: “A match is considered positive if its L2 distance to the ground truth center is less than 40cm.” Thus, a system can receive credit for a track even if orientation, scale, or fine geometry is wrong.

The second failure mode is benchmark over-trust. The paper’s main quantitative evidence is synthetic. The source says in [PAGE 1]: “we introduce a new synthetic dataset comprising 2381 unique indoor sequences with a total of 60k rendered RGB-D images.” A multi-week research trap is to continue optimizing on this synthetic distribution while assuming that gains transfer to real offices, homes, or robot environments. The paper does include real-world qualitative comparisons [PAGE 8], but qualitative figures are not a substitute for controlled quantitative stress tests.

The third failure mode is counter-trap formation. A sophisticated auditor should not reject the paper because it uses synthetic data; instead, the auditor should construct verifiable counter-traps. For example, one can test whether the graph’s five-frame temporal window is sufficient for realistic occlusion durations. The source says in [PAGE 5]: “we deﬁne a bidirectional graph neural network connecting object detections in consecutive frames within a window of 5 neighboring frames.” If an object is occluded for longer than the effective window, the model may create a new trajectory. The source’s fallback rule says in [PAGE 6]: “Nodes with no prior connections create a new trajectory if their instance id does not already exist in any other trajectory.” This is a precise counter-trap: long occlusion should produce fragmentation or identity switches.

The fourth failure mode is clock misalignment. The paper’s temporal design has a clock: sequences contain 25 frames [PAGE 4], the graph uses a five-frame window [PAGE 5], and message passing uses four steps [PAGE 6]. For online robotics, the relevant clock is not only the dataset frame index but wall-clock latency. If inference cannot process each frame within the sensor frame interval, the temporal graph becomes stale. The paper does not provide a full real-time latency analysis, so a deployment agent must verify that the pipeline satisfies the control loop. The formal timing condition is:
\[
\tau_{\text{inference}} \leq \Delta t_{\text{frame}},
\]
where \(\tau_{\text{inference}}\) is end-to-end latency in seconds and \(\Delta t_{\text{frame}}\) is the frame interval in seconds. If this inequality fails, temporal consistency claims degrade even if offline MOTA is high.

The fifth failure mode is physical-belief regression. A robotics integrator may assume that the 7-DoF pose is accurate enough for grasping or navigation. The source says in [PAGE 1] that the method predicts “temporally-consistent 7-DoF pose trajectories.” However, the paper also says in [PAGE 8]: “As our reconstruction is limited by the dense voxel grid resolution, ﬁne-scale details cannot be captured.” For grasping, fine geometry matters. Therefore, the correct counter-trap is to separate tracking-level success from manipulation-level success. A track can be correct at the MOTA threshold while still being unsafe for contact planning.

A Bayesian belief-update model clarifies the trap structure. Let \(H\) be the hypothesis that the method is robust enough for deployment. Let \(D_{\text{syn}}\) be synthetic benchmark evidence and \(D_{\text{real}}\) be real-world stress evidence. A rational posterior is:
\[
P(H \mid D_{\text{syn}}, D_{\text{real}}) \propto P(D_{\text{syn}}, D_{\text{real}} \mid H) P(H).
\]
If \(D_{\text{real}}\) is absent or weak, the posterior should remain uncertain. The crisis regression occurs when an agent assigns excessive likelihood to \(D_{\text{syn}}\) because it is abundant and easy to compute. The counter-trap is to require likelihood terms for depth holes, symmetric rotation ambiguity, long occlusion, and latency. The clock constraint prevents the belief update from ignoring temporal feasibility.

The final recursive prediction is this: if the research community follows only the MOTA headline, it will likely reproduce gains on MOTFront but encounter failures in real RGB-D deployments where depth noise, symmetric objects, and occlusion are more severe. If the community instead builds verifiable stress tests around pose error, track continuity, and latency, the paper’s method can be integrated more safely. The paper’s architecture is useful, but the evidence hierarchy must be recalibrated: synthetic MOTA is evidence of relative improvement, not proof of physical reliability.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraints invalidate the paper’s claims or a downstream deployment claim. The source domain is indoor RGB-D tracking, so weather is mostly indirect. The relevant veto layers are rigid-body physics, depth observability, robot interaction constraints, and illumination/appearance robustness.

First, rigid-body validity. The method estimates 7-DoF poses: 3 rotations, 3 translations, and scale. The source says in [PAGE 1]: “temporally consistent 7-DoF object poses (3 rotations, 3 translations and 1 scale).” A physical veto applies if the estimated transform leaves the rigid-motion manifold. Formally, for a valid pose:
\[
R^T R = I, \quad \det R = 1, \quad c &gt; 0.
\]
If the estimator returns a matrix that is not in \(SO(3)\), or a non-positive scale, the pose must be rejected. The paper’s Umeyama operator enforces \(R\in SO(3)\) and \(c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\) [PAGE 5], so the mathematical operator is valid. However, numerical optimization or downstream normalization can still produce invalid outputs if implementation errors occur. Veto condition: invalid \(R\) or \(c\leq 0\).

Second, depth-observability veto. The pose pipeline depends on depth back-projection. The source says in [PAGE 5]: “we backproject depth patch IDepth and NOCs patch INOCs to point clouds Po and Pn.” If the depth map has missing pixels, the observed point cloud \(P_o\) becomes incomplete. A formal observability condition is that the aligned point sets must have enough non-degenerate surface support. Let \(N_{\text{eff}}\) be the effective number of inlier correspondences after outlier removal. If \(N_{\text{eff}}\) is too small or the covariance of the point distribution is rank-deficient, the pose is not observable. The paper uses outlier removal: in [PAGE 15], it describes statistical outlier removal with neighboring points and RANSAC. But outlier removal cannot create missing evidence. Veto condition: rank-deficient geometry or insufficient inlier support.

Third, biomechanical or robotic interaction veto. The paper claims benefits for XR, service robots, navigation, and interaction [PAGE 8]. For grasping, the geometry must be accurate enough for contact planning. The paper acknowledges in [PAGE 8]: “As our reconstruction is limited by the dense voxel grid resolution, ﬁne-scale details cannot be captured.” If an object of characteristic length \(L\) is represented by a \(32^3\) voxel grid, the nominal voxel pitch is approximately:
\[
\delta_{\text{voxel}} \approx \frac{L}{32}.
\]
If a task requires geometric tolerance \(\epsilon_{\text{task}}\) smaller than \(\delta_{\text{voxel}}\), the representation is insufficient. For example, thin handles, small knobs, or sharp edges may be lost. Veto condition: \(\epsilon_{\text{task}} &lt; \delta_{\text{voxel}}\) for the required interaction.

Fourth, appearance and illumination veto. The method does not explicitly model texture. The paper states in [PAGE 8]: “one could consider an appearance-based object representation that also models textures.” This omission creates a vulnerability under strong illumination changes, shadows, or low-texture objects. The method may still track if depth and geometry are reliable, but RGB detection and segmentation can degrade. Veto condition: detection confidence falls below the paper’s filtering thresholds. The source says in [PAGE 5]: “we ﬁlter object detections utilizing non-maximum suppression between 2D bounding box predictions, as well as two thresholds κ = 0.35 and ν = 0.35.” If illumination or texture causes objectness or IoU-related confidence to fall below these thresholds, detections are discarded, and the tracker cannot recover them unless temporal propagation is sufficient.

Fifth, weather veto. Since the source is explicitly indoor, weather is not a primary layer. The paper says in [PAGE 1]: “indoor environments.” There is no rain, snow, fog, or outdoor wind model. Therefore, a direct weather veto does not apply to the paper’s claimed domain. However, an outdoor deployment would be vetoed by domain mismatch unless the system is retrained and revalidated. The source does not provide outdoor quantitative evaluation. Veto condition: deployment outside the validated indoor domain without additional evidence.

The cross-layer adjudication proof is as follows. The paper’s mathematical pose operator is valid on the rigid-motion manifold, and the indoor domain excludes direct weather effects. However, the physical veto remains active in three regimes: depth holes, symmetric ambiguity, and fine-scale geometry loss. If any of these regimes occurs, the tracking output may be numerically plausible but physically insufficient. Therefore, the paper’s claim is valid as an indoor synthetic benchmark improvement, but not automatically valid as a physical-world reliable tracking guarantee.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards style counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario defines an intervention, a measurable prediction, and a veto condition. These are not betting lines; they are formal stress tests derived from the paper’s equations and stated limits.

Scenario 1: Symmetric-table rotation ambiguity.
Intervention: select table sequences, because the paper’s NOCS loss explicitly handles table symmetry. The source says in [PAGE 6] that the NOCS loss chooses the minimal loss for target rotations \(i \in \{0^\circ,180^\circ\}\) when the predicted class is table. Rotate the ground-truth table by approximately \(180^\circ\) about its vertical axis while preserving its center and coarse shape.
Prediction: center-based MOTA may remain high because the matching rule uses center distance less than 40 cm [PAGE 7], but rotation error will increase. Define rotation error:
\[
e_R = \arccos\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(R_{\text{est}}^T R_{\text{gt}})-1}{2}\right).
\]
If the table is approximately symmetric, the NOCS alignment may choose the wrong canonical heading with little correspondence penalty.
Veto condition: \(e_R\) exceeds a task-specific angular tolerance, for example \(15^\circ\) or \(\pi/12\) radians, while center error remains below 0.40 m. This would show that MOTA alone is insufficient. The source’s class-wise table MOTA is reported as 58.4% for the full model in [PAGE 14], so table performance is not negligible, but rotation ambiguity remains a verifiable risk.

Scenario 2: Depth-hole or depth-noise corruption.
Intervention: take real or synthetic RGB-D sequences and remove depth values in object regions, or add Gaussian depth noise with standard deviation \(\sigma_z\). The paper’s pose pipeline depends on back-projected depth [PAGE 5] and outlier removal [PAGE 15]. The expected causal chain is: depth holes reduce inlier point count, Umeyama alignment becomes unstable, relative pose features degrade, and graph association fails.
Prediction: pose error increases, missed targets increase, and MOTA decreases. A verifiable metric is the inlier ratio after RANSAC, or the condition number of the point-cloud covariance used by Umeyama. If the covariance becomes rank-deficient, rotation estimation is unreliable.
Veto condition: the method’s MOTA drops below a predefined operational threshold, or the fraction of frames with pose error above 0.40 m exceeds a predefined fraction. This test directly probes the paper’s reliance on clean depth and geometric alignment. It is especially important because the main quantitative benchmark is synthetic, where depth is likely cleaner and more complete than many real RGB-D sensors.

Scenario 3: Occlusion longer than the five-frame graph window.
Intervention: occlude an object for \(k\) consecutive frames. The source states in [PAGE 5] that the graph connects detections within a window of 5 neighboring frames. The expected behavior is that if \(k\) exceeds the effective temporal support, the object has no prior connections and may start a new trajectory. The source says in [PAGE 6]: “Nodes with no prior connections create a new trajectory if their instance id does not already exist in any other trajectory.”
Prediction: for \(k \leq 4\) or \(5\), the graph may recover the track; for \(k\) substantially larger than 5, identity switches or new track fragments will increase. The verifiable metrics are number of misses \(m_t\), false positives \(fp_t\), identity switches \(mme_t\), and MOTA [PAGE 7].
Veto condition: a controlled increase in occlusion length produces a monotonic increase in identity switches or fragmentation after the five-frame threshold. If this occurs, the temporal design is not robust to realistic long occlusions, even if the average MOTA remains high. This scenario is especially relevant for indoor scenes with chairs, tables, sofas, and other furniture that can mutually occlude.

Additional audit note: the paper reports that graph-based tracking improves MOTA compared with a no-graph ablation. The source says in [PAGE 7]: “When comparing our association step with a graph neural network (Ours) against an approach with a L2-distance heuristic (no graph), we obtain an increase in MOTA score by 4.3% and a reduction of misses by 18.9%.” This supports the graph’s value under the benchmark distribution. However, the three counterfactual vetoes test whether that value survives distribution shift. If any veto scenario produces a large verified failure, the claim of robust temporal consistency should be weakened or conditioned on additional safeguards such as longer temporal memory, appearance features, uncertainty estimation, or explicit depth-completion modules.
