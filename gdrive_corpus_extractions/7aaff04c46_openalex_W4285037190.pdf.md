Cervical spine or vestibular pathology confounds balance and oculomotor tests, producing false positives that are not concussion. This is a hard veto: a positive BESS or tandem-gait result cannot be attributed to concussion without ruling out cervical/vestibular confounders, which sideline settings rarely can.

Weather/environmental layer: Page 1 defines side-line as pitch-side, rink-side, locker room, or assessment area immediately available but not clinical/hospital. Page 7 notes only one study used video assessment (Gardner et al. [39], National Rugby League match play), and page 8 observes that fixed cameras at amateur/club levels may be suboptimal in clarity. Lighting, noise, surface uniformity, and temperature vary across these environments and directly affect balance and visual tracking performance, yet no included study standardized or reported environmental covariates. This is a soft veto: environmental confounders invalidate the assumption of standardized test administration, but they do not formally exclude a study.

Adjudication hierarchy: When vetoes conflict, page 2’s consensus rule is the final arbiter—only a licensed medical practitioner can diagnose a concussion. Therefore, the medical-expert veto outranks the physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers. If no medical practitioner is present (the typical non-elite scenario, page 8), the chain breaks regardless of test modality: biomechanical and environmental vetoes become moot because no clinical diagnosis can be issued, and the sideline assessment remains research-only (page 7). The proof is structural: physical data are absent, biomechanical confounders are unmeasured at the sideline, environmental standardization is unreported, and medical authorization is gatekeeping. No layer can override the medical-expert requirement.
