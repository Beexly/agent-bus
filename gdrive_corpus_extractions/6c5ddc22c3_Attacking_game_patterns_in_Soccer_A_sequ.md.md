Let \(X_t\in\mathcal{A}\) be the attacking event at event-index \(t\), where \(\mathcal{A}\) is a finite event alphabet and \(t\) is an event count, not necessarily seconds. A first-order transition operator is
\[
P_{ij}=\Pr(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i),\qquad \sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}}P_{ij}=1,
\]
with \(P_{ij}\) dimensionless in \([0,1]\). The maximum-likelihood estimate is
\[
\hat P_{ij}=\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}},\qquad n_{i\cdot}=\sum_j n_{ij},
\]
where \(n_{ij}\) is an event-pair count. Breakdown regimes: if \(n_{i\cdot}=0\), \(\hat P_{ij}\) is undefined; if \(n_{ij}=0\), the unsmoothed model assigns zero probability to an unseen transition, and held-out log loss
\[
L=-\sum_{t}\log \hat P_{X_tX_{t+1}}\quad[\mathrm{nats}]
\]
diverges to \(+\infty\) on any held-out sequence containing that transition. A smoothed estimator is
\[
\tilde P_{ij}=\frac{n_{ij}+\alpha}{n_{i\cdot}+\alpha|\mathcal{A}|},\qquad \alpha>0,
\]
which is finite but assumption-dependent; \(\alpha\) must be reported.

A lag-\(k\) dependency audit uses mutual information
\[
I_k=\sum_{a,b}p_{ab}^{(k)}\log\frac{p_{ab}^{(k)}}{p_a p_b}\quad[\mathrm{nats}],
\]
where \(p_{ab}^{(k)}=\Pr(X_t=a,X_{t+k}=b)\), \(p_a=\Pr(X_t=a)\), and \(p_b=\Pr(X_{t+k}=b)\). \(I_k=0\) indicates independence at lag \(k\); the convention \(0\log 0=0\) is required. Singularity: if \(p_a p_b=0\), the log-ratio is undefined unless the zero-probability cell is excluded by an explicit support rule.

For possession duration \(T\), a hazard operator is
\[
h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Pr(t\le T<t+\Delta t\mid T\ge t)}{\Delta t}\quad[\mathrm{s}^{-1}],
\]
with survival
\[
S(t)=\exp\!\left[-\int_0^t h(u)\,du\right].
\]
Assumption: non-informative censoring. Breakdown: if censoring is tied to score state, or if the hazard is non-proportional across match states, the pooled estimate is biased. This matters because adjacent source pages report a mean final-third possession duration of “16.26 seconds” [PAGE 174] and a short-possession window of “0.5 – 2.5 seconds” [PAGE 173]; those two temporal scales cannot be treated as one homogeneous clock without an explicit model.

For categorical final-third entry methods, the Pearson operator is
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\frac{(O_c-E_c)^2}{E_c},
\]
dimensionless, with \(C\) cells and \(df=C-1-r\). Breakdown: if \(E_c=0\), the statistic is undefined; if cells are match-level repeated measures, independence is violated. The rank operator for two independent samples is
\[
U_1=R_1-\frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2},
\]
dimensionless rank units; breakdown under paired quarters or repeated matches. The standardized mean difference is
\[
d=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{s_p},
\]
dimensionless; singular if \(s_p=0\). All target-specific parameter values are **not attested** in the supplied text.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Crisis habit regression.** Under score-state pressure, a team may regress from its trained build-up pattern to a direct or aerial habit. Formally, let \(S_t\) be score state and \(C_t\) be clock state. The observed transition is
\[
P_{ij}(S_t,C_t)=\Pr(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i,S_t,C_t).
\]
If \(P_{ij}\) shifts when \(S_t\) changes from drawing to losing, that shift may be a crisis habit, not a stable game-pattern. Without the target body, no claim about the World Cup 2010 winner is allowed.

**Counter-trap.** The opponent may concede low-value possession and bait a direct entry, then attack the transition. In sequence terms, the observed high-frequency transition \(i\to j\) may be opponent-induced. A counter-trap audit requires conditioning on opponent shape and on the location of the ball recovery; otherwise the model confuses “what the winner did” with “what the opponent allowed.”

**Clock.** The clock changes urgency, risk tolerance, and substitution patterns. A model that pools the first and last 15 minutes can hide a time-varying transition matrix. The safe operator is \(P_{ij}(t)\), with a pre-registered time basis or change-point test. The adjacent source’s short-possession finding at [PAGE 173] and longer final-third possession mean at [PAGE 174] imply that the clock resolution must match the event definition; a one-second event cannot be modeled with a 16-second average as if they were the same unit.

Recursive theory-of-mind lesson: the analyst must model the opponent’s model of the analyst’s model. If a winner’s “habit” is really a response to an opponent’s counter-trap, then a first-order Markov chain is misspecified. The correct output is a veto on target-specific mental-state attribution unless the target pages supply the coding rules and conditional variables.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical veto.** Any claimed high-press or fast-break pattern must satisfy
\[
D_{\mathrm{HIR}}\le D_{\mathrm{total}},\qquad \int_0^T v(t)\,dt\le \bar v_{\max}T,
\]
where \(D_{\mathrm{HIR}}\) is high-intensity running distance [m], \(D_{\mathrm{total}}\) is total distance [m], \(v(t)\) is speed [m s\(^{-1}\)], \(T\) is match duration [s], and \(\bar v_{\max}\) is the athlete’s sustainable maximum [m s\(^{-1}\)]. If a sequence model implies a high-press rate that exceeds the recovery-constrained sprint budget, the tactical inference is vetoed. Adjacent source text illustrates the scale of physical demand: “top international female players covered an average of 1.7 km of high intensity running during a game” [PAGE 154]. The target is a different population, so this is a scaling check, not a target result.

**Biomechanical veto.** Ball-entry method depends on surface friction, ball rebound, and foot-surface interaction. A wet pitch changes the cost of aerial versus ground passes; a model that treats “pass to feet” and “aerial pass” as pure tactical choices without surface state can misattribute environmental effects to style. The breakdown condition is: if the playing surface or ball condition differs systematically across matches, the transition matrix must include a surface covariate or be stratified.

**Weather veto.** Heat and humidity alter high-intensity capacity and recovery. A formal environmental audit should record WBGT or equivalent heat-stress index [°C] and test whether the high-intensity sequence rate shifts above a pre-registered threshold. If weather is omitted and the high-press rate falls, the model cannot distinguish tactical adaptation from thermal constraint. The cross-layer veto is therefore: no causal “counter-trap” or “habit regression” claim is admissible unless physical, surface, and weather covariates are either measured or explicitly bounded.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
