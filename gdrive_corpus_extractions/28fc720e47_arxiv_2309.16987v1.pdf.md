The reasoning task is to adjudicate the SpikeMOT architecture as a formal event-based multi-object tracking system, not as a speculative sports or betting object. The source defines a two-clock system: a high-frequency tracker operating on event-derived voxels and a lower-frequency detector operating at an equivalent RGB frame period. The paper states that “the tracker maintains continuity of object identities across frame duration by updating the coordinates of the tracked objects at a higher frequency (1/δ)” [PAGE 3], while the detector “identifies objects of interest at frame rate” [PAGE 3]. In implementation, this becomes explicit: “The tracker executes periodic updates on each tracklet at 10 ms intervals, while the detector offers detections at 50 ms intervals” [PAGE 8]. These two clocks are the central temporal operators. They are not arbitrary hyperparameters; they define the information horizon available to the tracker between corrective detections.

The first gray zone is the meaning of “voxel duration.” The paper says the event voxel’s temporal axis is quantized into bins of duration δ [PAGE 3], and later says “both the template and search voxels comprise 100 slices, which represent the voxel duration. Each slice accumulates events from a temporal duration of 10 ms, which signifies the voxel granularity” [PAGE 8]. If 100 slices each span 10 ms, the nominal voxel window is 1.0 s. However, the runtime section reports “voxel duration of 100” and “voxel duration of 30” as operating points, with FPS and HOTA changes [PAGE 10]. The most coherent reading is that the ablation’s “voxel duration” is number of temporal slices, not milliseconds. Under that reading, duration 100 corresponds to 1.0 s at 10 ms granularity, and duration 30 corresponds to 0.3 s. This matters because the paper’s own ablation says longer voxel duration captures more temporal context but increases computational load [PAGE 11]. Therefore, the tradeoff is an information-computation Pareto boundary, not a free accuracy gain.

The second gray zone is the spiking neuron regime. SpikeMOT uses Spike Response Model neurons because they provide soft reset, longer-term dependency, and richer spiking patterns compared with leaky integrate-and-fire neurons [PAGE 3]. The formal neuron dynamics are given by response and refractory kernels: “The time constants of the response kernel τs and the refractory kernel τr are 10 ms and 1 ms, respectively. The spiking threshold is 1.0” [PAGE 8]. The breakdown regime is explicitly identified: “the influence is minimal when the threshold potential lies between 0.1 and 10.0,” but “the tracking accuracy is considerably hampered when the threshold potential exceeds 10.0” [PAGE 11]. This is a singularity-like operating boundary: beyond it, synaptic plasticity cannot compensate for excessively sparse firing, causing feature loss.

The third gray zone is association under occlusion. The dataset protocol excludes annotation for objects “occluded by at least 90% or disappear by more than 90% in the event domain” [PAGE 6], while retaining identifiers for later re-identification [PAGE 6]. That creates a hard evidentiary limit: if an object contributes less than roughly one-tenth of its observable event support, the system has no reliable spatial evidence for it. The tracker can propagate a prior, but the matcher needs detector support to validate or refresh the tracklet. The paper also says tracklets are removed if unmatched for “a continuous span of 100 frames” [PAGE 8]. With 50 ms detector intervals, this is a nominal 5-second disappearance budget, assuming detector frames are the relevant clock. If the disappearance exceeds the detector-refresh budget or the voxel context window, identity continuity must rely on re-identification after reappearance, not continuous observation.

The fourth gray zone is metric interpretation. The paper uses HOTA, CLEAR, and IDF1 because “HOTA explicitly balances the assessment of detection and association” [PAGE 7]. The reported SpikeMOT result on DSEC-MOT combined is HOTA 52.5, DetA 49.5, AssA 55.7, MOTA 54.7, IDF1 62.9, with only 7 identity switches [PAGE 9]. The unusually low identity-switch count relative to other trackers is the strongest evidence for the claimed benefit of sparse motion features plus recurrent SNN dynamics. But the FE240hz results are even more extreme: combined HOTA 87.4, DetA 87.4, AssA 87.5, MOTA 99.0, IDF1 99.5, and zero identity switches [PAGE 10]. That does not prove universal superiority; FE240hz is single-object tracking and easier in identity terms, so the DSEC-MOT result is the more diagnostic multi-object test.

The final audit is architectural. SpikeMOT’s matcher uses the Hungarian algorithm with Hausdorff distance to reduce dependence on the Siamese estimator and lower computational load [PAGE 5]. The Siamese estimator validates or dismisses associations [PAGE 5]. Negative training pairs teach the tracker not to drift toward other objects of the same class, other classes, or background when the target is occluded [PAGE 5]. The detector is YOLOv5 trained on event frames, with reported precision 0.87, recall 0.69, mAP@0.5 0.78, and mAP@(0.5:0.95) 0.54 [PAGE 5]. The detector is therefore not perfect; its recall limitation explains why occlusion and reappearance are still hard. In sum, SpikeMOT is best understood as a clocked, evidence-gated tracker: event voxels supply high-frequency motion evidence, SRM recurrence supplies temporal memory, detector refresh supplies spatial correction, and matcher logic prevents unsupervised drift.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the core operator set as a composition of tracker, detector, and matcher. Formally, with time interval \(t\) to \(t+\tau\), the paper states:

\[
P^{t+\tau} = f_T(Q^t, V^t, e_t^{t+\tau}; \theta_T)
\]
\[
D^{t+\tau} = f_D(e_t^{t+\tau}; \theta_D)
\]
\[
Q^{t+\tau} = f_M(P^{t+\tau}, D^{t+\tau})
\]

where \(f_T\) is the Siamese position estimator, \(f_D\) is the detector, and \(f_M\) is the matcher [PAGE 3]. Units: \(t\) and \(\tau\) are seconds; \(P\) is a set of estimated positions in pixel or metric coordinates; \(Q\) is a set of tracklets; \(D\) is a set of detections; \(V\) is a dense event voxel; \(e\) is a binary event tensor.

The event tensor is defined as a binary sparse tensor: “binary sparse tensor formed by setting 1 for the events” with shape \(2 \times H \times W \times (t_2-t_1)\) [PAGE 3]. For DSEC-MOT, event and image resolution are \(640 \times 480\) pixels [PAGE 7]. Thus \(H=480\), \(W=640\), and the two leading channels correspond to positive and negative event polarity. In implementation, positive events set the red channel to 1 and negative events set the green channel to 1 [PAGE 5]. Units: spatial indices are pixels; time is seconds; event values are dimensionless binary indicators.

A voxel \(V^t\) is a dense tensor converted from the sparse event tensor over duration \(\zeta\), with temporal bins of width \(\delta\) [PAGE 3]. Let \(M = \zeta/\delta\) be the number of temporal slices. In the reported implementation, “both the template and search voxels comprise 100 slices” and “each slice accumulates events from a temporal duration of 10 ms” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, under the literal slice interpretation:

\[
M = 100, \qquad \delta = 10\,\text{ms} = 10^{-2}\,\text{s}, \qquad \zeta = M\delta = 1.0\,\text{s}.
\]

If the ablation’s “voxel duration of 30” means 30 slices, then \(\zeta=0.3\,\text{s}\) at the same granularity. This yields an information horizon:

\[
\mathcal{H}_{\text{voxel}} = \zeta.
\]

The tracker clock is \(\delta=10^{-2}\,\text{s}\), or 100 Hz. The detector clock is 50 ms, or 20 Hz [PAGE 8]. If \(\tau\) is one detector frame, \(\tau=5\times 10^{-2}\,\text{s}\).

The spiking neuron operator is given formally as:

\[
u^l(t) = \int_0^\infty \varepsilon(t-\tau') o^l(\tau')\,d\tau' - \int_0^\infty \upsilon(t-\tau') s^{l+1}(\tau')\,d\tau'
\]

\[
\varepsilon = \frac{1}{\tau_s} e^{-t/\tau_s} H(t), \qquad
\upsilon = \frac{1}{\tau_r} e^{-t/\tau_r} H(t)
\]

where \(u^l(t)\) is membrane potential, \(o^l(t)\) is post-synaptic input from the CNN synapse network, \(s^{l+1}(t)\) is output spike train, \(H(t)\) is the Heaviside step function, \(\tau_s\) is response time constant, and \(\tau_r\) is refractory time constant [PAGE 5]. Units: \(t\), \(\tau_s\), \(\tau_r\) are seconds; \(H(t)\) is dimensionless; \(u^l\) is a dimensionless membrane-potential variable under the paper’s normalization; \(s^{l+1}\) is a spike train with units of \(\text{s}^{-1}\) in the Dirac-delta representation.

The threshold operator is:

\[
s^{l+1}(t) = f_s(u^l(t)), \qquad
s(t_i) =
\begin{cases}
\delta(t-t_i), & u(t_i) \ge V_{\text{th}} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

where \(\delta\) is the Dirac delta and \(V_{\text{th}}\) is threshold [PAGE 5]. In implementation, “the spiking threshold is 1.0” [PAGE 8]. The source’s breakdown regime is explicit: threshold values between 0.1 and 10.0 have minimal effect, but “tracking accuracy is considerably hampered when the threshold potential exceeds 10.0” [PAGE 11]. Thus the safe dimensionless regime is:

\[
0.1 \le V_{\text{th}} \le 10.0.
\]

The loss operator uses labels \(y_i\), weights \(w_i\), predicted scores \(v_i\), sigmoid \(\sigma\), peak position \(c_v\), target center \(c_y\), and radius \(R\):

\[
y_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \|u_i-c_y\| \le R \\
0, & \|u_i-c_y\| > R
\end{cases}
\]

\[
w_i =
\begin{cases}
0.5 / \sum [y_i=1], & \|u_i-c_y\| \le R \\
0.5 / \sum [y_i=0], & \|u_i-c_y\| > R
\end{cases}
\]

\[
L_{\text{neg}} = \sum -w_i \log(1-\sigma(v_i))
\]

and for positive pairs:

\[
\|u_i-c_v\| > R: \quad l_i = -y_i \log(\epsilon)
\]

\[
\|u_i-c_v\| \le R: \quad l_i = -\left[y_i \log(\sigma(v_i)) + (1-y_i)\log(1-\sigma(v_i))\right]
\]

\[
L_{\text{pos}} = \sum w_i l_i
\]

where \(\epsilon\) is infinitesimal [PAGE 6]. Units: \(u_i\) and \(c_v\) are spatial score-map coordinates in pixels; \(v_i\) is a real-valued logit; \(\sigma(v_i)\in(0,1)\) is a probability; \(y_i,w_i\) are dimensionless; \(L\) is in nats if natural logarithm is used, or bits if base-2 logarithm is used. The source does not specify log base; the standard implementation choice is natural log.

The assignment operator uses the Hungarian algorithm with Hausdorff distance as the association basis [PAGE 5]. A formal Hausdorff distance between sets \(A\) and \(B\) is:

\[
d_H(A,B) = \max\left\{
\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}\|a-b\|,
\sup_{b\in B}\inf_{a\in A}\|a-b\|
\right\}.
\]

If boxes are represented in pixels, \(d_H\) has units of pixels. If calibrated world coordinates are used, units are meters. The paper’s implementation uses image-domain bounding boxes, so pixel units are the direct interpretation.

Detection and tracking clocks produce a temporal Nyquist-like condition. Let \(\lambda_{\text{obj}}\) be the event rate produced by the target, with units \(\text{events}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\). The expected number of target-related events in one tracker voxel is approximately:

\[
N_{\text{obj}} \approx \lambda_{\text{obj}}\zeta.
\]

If \(\lambda_{\text{obj}}\zeta\) is too small, the tracker lacks motion evidence. If background event rate \(\lambda_{\text{bg}}\) dominates, the effective signal-to-interference ratio becomes:

\[
\Lambda = \frac{\lambda_{\text{obj}}}{\lambda_{\text{bg}}}.
\]

This is not a toy ratio; it is a formal interference measure under a Poisson-event approximation. The source acknowledges the problem: in real-world settings, “events from complex background and camera motion can easily obscure the true target motion” [PAGE 1], and DSEC-MOT sequences have “a substantial volume of background events mingled with object events” [PAGE 7].

The main breakdown regimes are:

1. Threshold breakdown: \(V_{\text{th}} > 10.0\) causes excessive spike suppression and feature loss [PAGE 11].
2. Voxel granularity breakdown: with fixed slice count \(M=100\), excessively small \(\delta\) reduces total voxel duration \(\zeta=M\delta\), causing loss of temporal context; the paper says “excessively fine voxel granularities shorten the time length of the voxel, leading to a decline in tracking effectiveness” [PAGE 11].
3. Detector refresh breakdown: longer refresh intervals reduce rapid re-detection after occlusion; the paper reports “a negative correlation between the detector’s refresh interval and the tracking accuracy” [PAGE 12].
4. Occlusion evidence breakdown: objects “occluded by at least 90% or disappear by more than 90% in the event domain are exempted from annotation” [PAGE 6], implying insufficient event evidence for reliable tracking.
5. Tracklet lifetime breakdown: tracklets are removed if unmatched “for a continuous span of 100 frames” [PAGE 8]. At 50 ms detector spacing, that is approximately 5 seconds of unsupported absence.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of SpikeMOT is a convergence of event sensing, spiking neural computation, Siamese tracking, and multi-object tracking evaluation. The event camera lineage begins with asynchronous brightness-change sensing: “event cameras respond asynchronously to brightness changes by generating event spikes at the time when and in the pixels where changes occur” [PAGE 1]. This creates sparse spatiotemporal traces of motion, unlike frame-based cameras that sample absolute brightness synchronously. The paper also emphasizes the practical advantage of “high dynamic range (in the order of 140 dB)” [PAGE 1], which situates event cameras in adverse illumination regimes where conventional RGB cameras fail. This is a physical lineage: event sensing is not merely a faster frame sensor; it is a change sensor.

The spiking neural network lineage follows because event data are naturally sparse and asynchronous. The paper states that SNNs are “naturally suitable for addressing event-driven vision challenges due to their inherent asynchronous computational abilities and adeptness in processing sparse spatiotemporal data” [PAGE 2]. It then distinguishes neuron models: Hodgkin-Huxley is too complex, LIF is simple but limited, and SRM provides “a simple yet versatile neuronal archetype” with soft reset and longer-term dependency [PAGE 3]. This is a key genealogical trap: choosing LIF would simplify hardware or implementation but would reduce the temporal memory needed for occlusion handling. The paper’s ablation supports this: SNN with SRM neurons outperforms CNN, LSTM, and SNN with LIF neurons across HOTA, DetA, AssA, MOTA, and IDF1 [PAGE 11].

The Siamese tracking lineage comes from template matching and similarity learning. The paper references SiamFC and related Siamese trackers as efficient balance points between accuracy and efficiency [PAGE 2]. It also references DeepSORT as an inspiration for combining motion and appearance cues [PAGE 2]. SpikeMOT inherits the Siamese template-search paradigm but replaces dense RGB appearance with event-derived motion voxels. This changes the lineage from appearance similarity to spatiotemporal motion similarity. The paper says the tracker “learns both motion coherence and appearance coherence to update tracklets” [PAGE 2], but the actual input is event-based, so the motion coherence is carried by spike-based spatiotemporal features rather than RGB texture.

The dataset lineage is equally important. The authors state that existing event-based trackers are “confined to controlled scenarios where object trajectories rarely intersect, the number of objects is limited, the background is absent, object contours are distinct and undeformable, scene durations are brief, and re-identification is not necessary” [PAGE 2]. DSEC-MOT is created to break that trap by providing real-world severe occlusions, trajectory intersections, and long-term re-identification [PAGE 2]. The dataset is built from DSEC, which lacked ground-truth boxes, and the authors introduce a semi-automated annotation tool and label 37,080 boxes for seven traffic classes [PAGE 2]. This genealogy explains why the benchmark is adversarial: it was designed to expose trackers that only work in clean event-based toy scenes.

There are several multi-week research traps implicit in this lineage. First, evaluating only detection metrics would hide association failure; the paper notes that previous event-based metrics were inadequate because they emphasized detection while overlooking association [PAGE 2]. Second, tuning only the SNN threshold without understanding synaptic compensation can produce misleading conclusions; the threshold sweep shows stability between 0.1 and 10.0 but collapse above 10.0 [PAGE 11]. Third, increasing voxel duration improves context but reduces FPS: with voxel duration 100, SpikeMOT runs at 12 FPS and HOTA 52.5; with voxel duration 30, it runs at 26 FPS and HOTA 51.1 [PAGE 10]. Fourth, ignoring detector refresh dynamics would miss the negative correlation between refresh interval and accuracy [PAGE 12]. These traps are not incidental; they are the reason the paper’s ablations are necessary.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis in event-based MOT is occlusion, background clutter, and rapid appearance change. A tracker that models only smooth motion will regress to a stale prior when the target disappears. The paper addresses this by training with antagonistic voxel pairs so the tracker learns not to drift toward surrounding objects when the target is occluded: “negative pair voxels arising from distinct categories serves to train the tracker the ability to circumvent unintended drift towards arbitrary surrounding objects when the target becomes completely occluded” [PAGE 5]. This is a counter-trap against habit regression.

The first recursive agent is crisis habit regression. Under occlusion, the tracker’s last estimated position becomes unreliable. If the model relies only on the previous search region, it may latch onto a nearby object with similar motion. This is especially dangerous in traffic scenes where cars and pedestrians intersect. The DSEC-MOT benchmark is explicitly built to expose this: it contains “heavy occlusions, extensive trajectory intersections, and long-term object re-identification” [PAGE 2]. A habit-regressing tracker would predict continuation of the last velocity, but velocity continuation is not identity preservation.

The second recursive agent is the counter-trap. SpikeMOT’s counter-trap has three layers. First, the SRM neuron’s soft reset and refractory dynamics provide longer-term dependency compared with LIF hard reset [PAGE 3]. The ablation says SRM neurons outperform LIF neurons because they can retain information and generate richer spiking patterns [PAGE 11]. Second, the matcher uses Hungarian assignment with Hausdorff distance before accepting Siamese validation [PAGE 5]. This reduces overreliance on the Siamese estimator and lowers computational load [PAGE 5]. Third, the detector refresh periodically re-grounds the tracker in global detections: “a shorter refresh interval allows the detector to capture new objects more quickly and handle occlusions more effectively by rapidly re-detecting the object once it reappears” [PAGE 12]. These are not decorative modules; they are adversarial countermeasures.

The third recursive agent is the clock. The tracker updates every 10 ms, while the detector updates every 50 ms [PAGE 8]. The SRM response time constant is 10 ms, and the refractory time constant is 1 ms [PAGE 8]. This creates a nested clock hierarchy:

\[
\tau_r = 1\,\text{ms} < \delta = 10\,\text{ms} < \tau_{\text{det}} = 50\,\text{ms}.
\]

The refractory clock prevents immediate repeated spiking. The tracker clock provides high-frequency motion updates. The detector clock provides slower global correction. If the crisis lasts longer than the voxel context, the recurrent memory alone is insufficient; detector re-identification becomes necessary. If the crisis is shorter than the voxel context, the SNN can bridge it using recurrent temporal features. If the crisis is longer than the tracklet removal horizon, the tracklet is deleted after 100 unmatched frames [PAGE 8], and identity must be re-established as a new or re-identified track.

There is also a recursive evaluation trap: if one evaluates only HOTA, one may miss whether identity switches are caused by detector failure or tracker failure. The paper provides both HOTA and ID switch counts. On DSEC-MOT, SpikeMOT has HOTA 52.5 and only 7 IDSw combined [PAGE 9], while GTR has HOTA 41.9 and 92 IDSw [PAGE 9]. The low IDSw count suggests the counter-trap works under occlusion. On FE240hz, SpikeMOT has zero IDSw across all sequences [PAGE 10], but that dataset is single-object tracking, so the result is less diagnostic for multi-object identity conflict. The correct theory-of-mind conclusion is: SpikeMOT reduces identity-switch risk by combining short-timescale motion recurrence with periodic detector re-grounding, but it cannot overcome evidentiary disappearance beyond the annotation and removal thresholds.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto proof begins with event generation. Event cameras respond to brightness changes, not absolute brightness alone [PAGE 1]. If an object is opaque-occluded, the sensor receives no photons from that object; the object-generated event rate tends to zero. Let \(T\) be optical transmittance from object to sensor. If \(T=0\), then object radiance contribution is zero, and the event rate from that object is zero regardless of dynamic range. The claimed “high dynamic range (in the order of 140 dB)” [PAGE 1] improves sensitivity under illumination variation, but it does not restore photons blocked by occlusion. Therefore, high dynamic range is not a veto override for opaque occlusion.

The biomechanical or scene-physical veto is stronger in traffic scenes. DSEC-MOT includes severe occlusion between vehicles, pedestrians, and other objects [PAGE 7]. The annotation protocol explicitly excludes objects “occluded by at least 90% or disappear by more than 90% in the event domain” [PAGE 6]. This is an empirical acknowledgment of the physical evidence limit. If visible object support is below approximately 10%, the event contribution is too weak for reliable spatial localization. Formally, let \(v\in[0,1]\) be visible fraction of the object’s event-producing surface. If \(v<0.1\), then:

\[
\lambda_{\text{obj}}(v) \approx v\lambda_0,
\]

and the likelihood ratio for target presence versus background-only becomes close to one. In information terms, mutual information between identity \(I\) and events \(E\) declines:

\[
I(I;E) \to 0 \quad \text{as} \quad v \to 0.
\]

Thus no tracker can reliably assert identity from direct event evidence when \(v\) is near zero.

The weather veto must be handled carefully. Event cameras are robust to lighting because they respond to changes and have high dynamic range [PAGE 1]. However, rain, fog, snow, or spray can create dense background events or reduce contrast. The source does not provide a specific weather benchmark, so any weather claim must be framed as a boundary condition, not as a measured result. The physical veto is: if weather reduces target-background contrast below the event camera’s contrast threshold, or if weather particles generate events that dominate the target’s event support, then the tracker’s signal-to-interference ratio collapses. Formally, if \(\lambda_{\text{weather}}\gg \lambda_{\text{obj}}\), then the target’s motion features are drowned out. The source’s FE240hz challenge with severe camera motion and background events is analogous: “The significant camera vibrations in these sequences result in the generation of a substantial volume of background events” [PAGE 8]. This supports the veto that background event dominance can defeat motion-feature tracking even without weather.

The detector layer provides a partial override. SpikeMOT uses YOLOv5 detection on event frames [PAGE 5]. If the detector sees the object after reappearance, it can reinitialize or update a tracklet. But detector recall is not perfect: on DSEC-MOT test set, YOLOv5 has recall 0.69 [PAGE 5]. Therefore, if the object is missed by the detector during reappearance, the tracklet may remain unmatched and eventually be removed after 100 frames [PAGE 8]. This is a formal failure chain:

\[
\text{occlusion} \rightarrow v\downarrow \rightarrow \lambda_{\text{obj}}\downarrow \rightarrow \text{detection probability}\downarrow \rightarrow \text{unmatched tracklet} \rightarrow \text{tracklet removal}.
\]

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: SpikeMOT can veto identity drift during moderate occlusion by using recurrent motion features and Hungarian validation, but it cannot veto physical disappearance. The system’s valid tracking envelope is bounded by the event evidence from the target, the detector refresh rate, and the voxel temporal context. Outside that envelope, the correct output is not a fabricated track but a delayed re-identification after sufficient evidence returns.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR-style counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable from the paper’s ablations and implementation details. Each scenario specifies an intervention, a mechanism, and a measurable prediction.

1. Threshold-potential veto.
Intervention: set the SRM threshold \(V_{\text{th}}\) above the safe range, for example \(V_{\text{th}}=20\), while keeping the network trained with the same synaptic weights.
Mechanism: neurons fire too rarely. The paper says the threshold effect is minimal between 0.1 and 10.0, but “tracking accuracy is considerably hampered when the threshold potential exceeds 10.0” [PAGE 11]. Synaptic plasticity can adapt to moderate threshold changes, but not to excessive threshold values.
Prediction: HOTA will fall below the baseline 52.5 on DSEC-MOT combined. The most likely failure is reduced spike diversity and weaker association features, causing AssA to drop more than DetA because detection is handled separately by YOLOv5. If the threshold is extremely high, the Siamese score map may become uninformative, increasing identity switches or track loss. Verifiable evidence: threshold sweep around Fig. 8 and the explicit textual breakdown [PAGE 11].

2. Granularity-context veto.
Intervention: reduce voxel granularity \(\delta\) from 10 ms to a much smaller value while keeping the number of temporal slices fixed at 100.
Mechanism: total voxel duration \(\zeta=M\delta\) shrinks. The paper warns that “excessively fine voxel granularities shorten the time length of the voxel, leading to a decline in tracking effectiveness” [PAGE 11]. With fixed slice count, finer granularity improves per-bin resolution but reduces total temporal context.
Prediction: HOTA will decrease relative to the 10 ms/100-slice baseline. The closest reference point is the lower-duration operating point: voxel duration 30 gives HOTA 51.1 versus voxel duration 100 giving HOTA 52.5 [PAGE 10]. If the shortened total duration is comparable to the lower-context regime, HOTA should fall toward or below 51.1. FPS may increase because fewer events are accumulated per voxel, but association accuracy will suffer. Verifiable evidence: voxel granularity ablation and runtime comparison [PAGE 10], [PAGE 11].

3. Detector-refresh veto.
Intervention: increase detector refresh interval from 50 ms to a longer interval, for example 500 ms.
Mechanism: the tracker has longer periods without global detector correction. The paper reports “a negative correlation between the detector’s refresh interval and the tracking accuracy” [PAGE 12] and explains that shorter refresh intervals help “rapidly re-detect the object once it reappears” [PAGE 12]. If occlusion duration exceeds the refresh interval, the tracker may rely on stale motion context.
Prediction: false negatives and identity switches will increase, especially in crowded DSEC-MOT sequences with frequent occlusions. DetA may decline if the detector misses newly appearing objects for longer, and AssA may decline if tracklets are propagated without correction. Tracklet removal risk increases if unmatched duration approaches the 100-frame removal rule [PAGE 8]. Verifiable evidence: detector refresh interval ablation and the explicit negative correlation [PAGE 12].

These veto scenarios are not speculative; they map directly to the paper’s stated operating regimes. They also define the safe deployment envelope: keep threshold within the stable range, preserve enough voxel duration for temporal context, and use a detector refresh interval short enough to recover from occlusions before tracklet removal.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
