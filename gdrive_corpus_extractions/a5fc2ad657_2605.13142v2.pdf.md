<THINK_TRACE>
The source defines the target predicate before any computation: “A team has clinched the playoffs if they are guaranteed to make the postseason regardless of the outcomes of any remaining games” [PAGE 1]. The central multi-hop derivation is therefore a refutation search. For a fixed team \(k\), the 0-day subroutine asks whether there exists any completion of the remaining schedule in which \(k\) is eliminated. The paper states: “The approach asks: ‘can team k be eliminated?’” [PAGE 4]. If such a completion is feasible, \(k\) has not clinched; if the CP model is infeasible, no elimination completion exists and \(k\) has clinched [PAGE 4–5]. This is the logical heart of the system: clinching is the negation of a satisfiable elimination counterexample, not a direct optimization of “best rank.”

The 0-day CP model decomposes the problem into a win-assignment layer and, only when necessary, a goal-assignment layer. In the win layer, the decision variables \(x^R_{ij},x^O_{ij},x^S_{ij}\) count regulation, overtime, and shootout wins by \(i\) over \(j\) in remaining games [PAGE 7]. Equation (5) forces every remaining game to have exactly one of the six outcomes: \(x^R_{ij}+x^O_{ij}+x^S_{ij}+x^R_{ji}+x^O_{ji}+x^S_{ji}=g_{ij}\) [PAGE 7]. For team \(k\), worst-case assumption constraints set \(x^R_{ik}=g_{ik}\) and all \(k\)-wins to zero, encoding that \(k\) loses every remaining game in regulation [PAGE 7]. Ranking is then encoded through \(b_{ij}\), with tie-breakers (2)–(4) folded into a disjunction over points, regulation wins, regulation plus overtime wins, and total wins, plus a guessed variable \(u_{ij}\) for ties beyond tie-breaker (4) [PAGE 8, Eq. (6)]. Division top-3 status \(t_i\), wildcard-relevant \(d_i\), and the qualification inequality \(\sum_{i\in C_k}(b_{ik}+d_i)\ge 8\) then encode the modern top-3-plus-wildcard structure [PAGE 8, Eqs. (7)–(11)].

The gray-zone audit is where the rigor matters. First, tie-breaker (5) is not pairwise: the paper says “tie-breaker (5) is different and more problematic than the others” because it applies to groups of arbitrary size [PAGE 5]. A naive pairwise head-to-head model can therefore certify a ranking that the actual group rule would reject. The paper avoids enumerating all tie groups by using guessed ranking variables and lazy no-good constraints: inconsistent ranking guesses are excluded and the model is re-solved [PAGE 5]. Second, the goal-assignment model is only invoked when a prospective elimination scenario remains tied through tie-breaker (5) [PAGE 8–9]. Its constraints force score margins to match the win types, match the previously found ranking, and prohibit a total tie through both goal differential and goals scored because the rules do not specify such a case [PAGE 9, Eqs. (15)–(17)]. Third, the score upper bound \(s_{ij\ell}\in[0,99]\) is a practical bound, not a theorem; it is chosen because exceeding it would be extremely unlikely [PAGE 9]. Fourth, the n-day tree search has a finite but exponential search space scaling as \(6^r\) for \(r\) relevant games [PAGE 15]; with a 30-minute timeout, not all \(n=2,3\) instances completed [PAGE 16]. Fifth, the footnote on [PAGE 10] notes that an overtime pulled-goalie concession is an extremely rare additional outcome left to future work. Finally, the model’s final-season ranking uses points \(p_i\) in Eq. (6); because the paper describes an 82-game season [PAGE 2], final point percentage and points are ordinally aligned when all teams complete 82 games, but a schedule imbalance would be a gray-zone audit item not explicitly modeled in Eq. (6).

The n-day layer adds another derivation hop: a node is an outcome of a particular game; root-to-node paths are partial future histories; the 0-day clincher tests the resulting league state. If clinching holds at a node, all descendants clinch, so pruning below is sound [PAGE 12]. If outcome orderings are monotone from \(k\)’s perspective, sideways pruning is also sound: stronger outcomes preserve clinching [PAGE 12–13]. The paper states: “The algorithm prunes sideways, yielding the single-literal clinching scenario” [PAGE 10]. The resulting DNF is “each root-to-leaf clinching path becomes a conjunction, and the disjunction of all such paths forms the scenario” [PAGE 14]. This is not a toy ratio; it is a constraint-satisfaction search with explicit pruning and explicit failure regimes.

The goal-differential and goals-for ranking must match the guessed ranking [PAGE 9, Eq. (16)], total ties are prohibited [PAGE 9, Eq. (17)], and unrealistically high scores are discouraged by [PAGE 9, Eq. (18)]:
\[
\min \sum_{(i,j)\in P}\sum_{\ell\in[1,g_{ij}]}s_{ij\ell}.
\]
The n-day search-space cardinality is
\[
|\Omega|=6^r,
\]
with \(r\) relevant games [PAGE 15]. Under a uniform completion assumption, the information content is
\[
H(\Omega)=\log_2(6^r)=r\log_2 6\approx 2.585r\ \text{bits}.
\]
Pruning efficiency is
\[
\eta=1-\frac{N_{\text{explored}}}{N_{\text{total}}},
\]
where \(N_{\text{total}}\) is the full tree size over all six outcomes of relevant games and \(N_{\text{explored}}\) is the number of explored nodes [PAGE 14]. Exact breakdown/singularity regimes are: (i) arbitrary-size tie-breaker (5) groups, where direct enumeration is combinatorial and lazy no-good constraints are used [PAGE 5]; (ii) total ties through goal differential and goals scored, prohibited because the rules do not specify them [PAGE 9]; (iii) the \(s_{ij\ell}\le 99\) score cap [PAGE 9]; (iv) omitted pulled-goalie overtime concession outcomes [PAGE 10]; (v) exponential \(6^r\) growth and 30-minute timeout exclusions for \(n=2,3\) [PAGE 15–16]; and (vi) the NP-complete classification [PAGE 2].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from early sports-elimination mathematics to modern constraint programming. The paper situates itself among baseball elimination work such as Schwartz, Robinson, and Wayne; soccer, racing, football, basketball, and hockey variants; and the most relevant prior NHL series by Russell and van Beek [PAGE 4, PAGE 17–18]. The historical contrast is explicit: prior NHL work covered 30 teams, six divisions, top-eight in-conference qualification, and three tie-breakers, whereas the modern league has 32 teams, four divisions, top-three-in-division plus wild cards, and seven tie-breakers [PAGE 4]. That lineage matters because the modern qualification structure changes the feasible region: Eq. (11) is not a simple “top eight by points” constraint, and Eq. (7) introduces division top-3 status [PAGE 8].

Multi-week traps are therefore not incidental. First, rule drift is a trap: a model or analyst trained on pre-2019-20 NHL rules will mis-rank teams because the tie-breaker set and qualification topology changed [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. Second, schedule drift is a trap: the 2021-22 season was shifted later due to the COVID-19 pandemic, and the 2022-23 Boston Bruins clinched early, producing visible shifts in the accumulation curves [PAGE 13–14]. Third, “games in hand” and point percentage are traps if one treats raw points as a complete ordering before the final state; the paper’s final-state model assigns all remaining games and uses points plus win-count tie-breakers [PAGE 7–8], but the schedule-imbalance edge case remains an audit point. Fourth, complexity growth is a trap: \(n=1\) produced a Boston Bruins scenario with 27 literals and 25,759.7 seconds elapsed [PAGE 14], while \(n=2\) had a longest run of 24,735.1 seconds without timeout and \(n=3\) excluded 35 searches under a 30-minute timeout [PAGE 16]. Fifth, the “published scenario” trap is subtle: the method matches NHL-published \(n\in\{0,1\}\) scenarios exactly [PAGE 3, PAGE 13], but exact match on those dates does not prove completeness for all \(n\); the paper itself reports timeouts and excluded searches [PAGE 16]. The genealogy is thus a progression from closed-form elimination numbers to CP-based counterexample search, with the modern contribution being the separation of win assignment, tie-group evaluation, goal assignment, and n-day tree search [PAGE 3–5, PAGE 10].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In the crisis window around March and April, the relevant “theory of mind” is not player psychology inside the CP model; the model treats game outcomes as combinatorial possibilities, not intentions. The human recursive loop is stakeholder reasoning: fans, media, and teams ask “what must happen tonight for our team to clinch?” while the league publishes daily scenarios for games later that evening [PAGE 2]. Crisis habit regression appears when analysts fall back to points-only “magic number” reasoning and forget that the NHL ranks by points and then by tie-breakers (1)–(7) [PAGE 2, Table 1]. The paper explicitly warns that “determining clinching scenarios manually has become increasingly time-consuming and error-prone” as tie-breaking rules have become more complex [PAGE 2]. The counter-trap is over-trusting a single counterexample or a single guessed ranking: a feasible win assignment may contain a tie group whose head-to-head ranking is inconsistent with the solver’s \(u_{ij}\) guess, which is why no-good constraints are added and the model is re-solved [PAGE 5]. A second counter-trap is assuming that a no-clinch result at one node implies no-clinch everywhere; the tree search instead expands children unless an inference algorithm proves pruning below and, when ordering is monotone, sideways [PAGE 10–12]. The clock is the operational constraint: 1-day scenarios are for games later that evening [PAGE 2], while \(n=2,3\) searches can hit 30-minute timeouts and exclude instances [PAGE 16]. The correct mental model is therefore recursive but disciplined: the analyst reasons about what the league’s rulebook would conclude, the solver reasons about whether an elimination completion exists, and the tree search reasons about whether a partial future history already forces the same conclusion. The model does not include team incentives, fatigue, injury, or weather; those can enter only through observed outcomes and standings parameters, not through a strategic utility function. This is a strength for rulebook rigor and a limitation for psychological or physical prediction.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather layers are vetoed from directly altering the CP feasibility predicate. Proof sketch: let \(z\) be any physical, biomechanical, or weather variable, such as force, skating speed, injury state, ice condition, temperature, wind, or travel fatigue. The CP model’s variables and parameters are \(p^0_i,p^0_{ij},w^0_i,w^0_{ij},w^{R,0}_i,w^{O,0}_i,w^{S,0}_i,g_i,g_{ij},x^R_{ij},x^O_{ij},x^S_{ij},b_{ij},u_{ij},t_i,d_i,\delta_i,f_i,s_{ij\ell},y^R_{ij\ell},y^O_{ij\ell},y^S_{ij\ell}\). None carries dimensions of force, length, velocity, acceleration, temperature, or physiological load; the only time dimension in the optimization is solver runtime in seconds, not game-clock or recovery time. Therefore the clinch predicate \(F_{\text{CP}}\) has zero partial derivative with respect to \(z\) except through the admissible projection \(\pi(z)\mapsto(p^0,w^0,g)\). Formally, if \(\pi(z_1)=\pi(z_2)\), then \(F_{\text{CP}}(z_1)=F_{\text{CP}}(z_2)\). Weather or biomechanics can change a game outcome, but once that outcome is realized it enters as points, wins, goals, or remaining-game counts; it does not enter as a separate causal force in Eqs. (1)–(18) [PAGE 7–9]. A postponement could change \(g_i\) or \(g_{ij}\), but that is a schedule-parameter update, not a biomechanical term. The source never models injuries, travel, ice quality, or weather; it models league state and game outcomes [PAGE 6–9]. Thus any claim that weather or biomechanics “vetoes” a clinch scenario must be translated into a changed parameter vector and re-solved; otherwise it is inadmissible. The only physical-layer exception visible in the paper is the rare overtime pulled-goalie concession, which is an additional game outcome node, not a continuous physical variable [PAGE 10]. The veto is therefore clean: physical and weather narratives cannot override the combinatorial proof; they can only update its inputs.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
