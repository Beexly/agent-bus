<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

The patent’s HRV-readiness architecture descends from the Task Force (1996) standards for HRV measurement and interpretation, which established time-domain metrics such as RMSSD and SDNN as indices of autonomic modulation. The “intensity score” and “recovery score” duality maps onto the Banister impulse-response model (fitness–fatigue paradigm), where accumulated strain generates a delayed fitness positive and a fatigue negative; readiness is the residual difference. The text’s reliance on “several days” for baseline HRV echoes Plews et al. and Kiviniemi’s work showing that daily HRV requires a multi-day rolling reference to isolate training-induced autonomic suppression from day-to-day noise.

The “evolving sleep need” construct—dependent on “recent strain, habitual sleep need, signs of sickness, and sleep debt” [PAGE 29]—lineages from sleep-homeostasis models (Borbély two-process model) extended with training-load covariates. The tactical-athlete / soldier example explicitly acknowledges dynamic sleep need under operational strain, which is historically significant: military fatigue models (e.g., the Sleep, Activity, Fatigue, and Task Effectiveness model) treat sleep debt as a cumulative state, not a nightly reset.

Multi-week traps embedded in the architecture:
1. **Baseline drift**: A multi-day baseline is treated as quasi-static, but over weeks, fitness adaptations shift resting HRV. A static \(T_{\text{base}}\) window will misclassify adapted athletes as under-recovered or over-recovered depending on window alignment.
2. **Illness confounding**: “Signs of sickness” enter sleep need, yet HRV is suppressed by subclinical illness independent of training load. Over multi-week horizons, an infection can be misread as training fatigue, prompting inappropriate rest or, conversely, training through illness if thresholds are adapted upward.
3. **Sleep-debt compounding**: Because sleep need is recursive (based on prior strain and prior debt), a small nightly under-sleep error compounds into a large sleep-debt state that the morning recovery score may not fully capture if HRV has partially rebounded.
4. **Soreness lag**: The intensity display claims “little to no soreness the next day” for sub-anaerobic thresholds [PAGE 29], but delayed-onset muscle soreness peaks 24–72 hours post-exercise, outside the next-day window used by the score. This creates a multi-day attribution error in the feedback loop.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock mismatch.

The system models the user as a rational recipient of a recovery score, but human behavior under physiological crisis exhibits habit regression: when fatigued, ill, or sleep-debt-laden, users often revert to prior training routines despite low recovery scores. The “CAUTIOUS” band [PAGE 29] explicitly delegates “careful not to overexert” to user discretion, which is precisely where crisis habit regression operates—an exhausted tactical athlete may interpret “careful” as “slightly lighter than usual” rather than “rest,” preserving the habitual high-load pattern.

The counter-trap is the system’s own adaptive threshold logic: “thresholds may be increased for higher planned intensity scores” [PAGE 29]. A user planning a high-intensity day raises the readiness bar, which can flip a READY classification into REST or CAUTIOUS. If the user then ignores the output and trains anyway, the system’s prescription is overridden by intention; if the user obeys, the down-regulation reduces next-day strain, potentially inflating the next morning’s recovery score and creating a false confirmation of the system’s calibration. This is a self-sealing loop: the intervention alters the input that validates the intervention.

The clock mismatch is temporal: recovery scores are generated “every morning” [PAGE 29], yet exercise may occur hours later. HRV and sleep metrics capture nocturnal autonomic state, but afternoon/evening exercise tolerance depends on acute factors—nutrition, hydration, circadian temperature peak, work stress accrued during the day—that are not in the morning score. A user cleared at 07:00 may be physiologically compromised by 17:00. The theory of mind therefore requires a second-order model: the system believes the user will act on the morning score; the user may act on a different internal state; and the system has no mechanism to update the score between morning issuance and evening execution.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The patent acknowledges physiological constraints but lacks a veto layer. The text states sleep need includes “signs of sickness” [PAGE 29], which is a physical veto candidate: acute infection, fever, or inflammatory illness depresses HRV independent of training load. A valid veto rule would be: if core temperature \(T_c > 37.8^\circ\mathrm{C}\) or illness flag is active, override \(\mathcal{D}(R)\) to REST regardless of \(R \ge \tau_{\text{high}}\), because HRV suppression in sickness is not training fatigue.

Biomechanical veto: the intensity display references “little to no soreness the next day” [PAGE 29], yet soreness is a lagging biomechanical signal. A veto rule: if the user reports delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) > threshold on a validated scale, or if joint-load metrics exceed tissue tolerance, downgrade READY to CAUTIOUS or REST. The current architecture has no soreness input into \(R\), so it can clear a user who is micro-traumatized from prior unaccustomed load.

Weather veto: heat and humidity increase cardiovascular strain and alter HRV independently of exercise intensity. The patent contains no environmental covariate. A veto proof requires: if wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) exceeds safety thresholds for the planned intensity class, block READY status even if \(R \ge \tau_{\text{high}}\), because thermoregulatory strain competes with the autonomic recovery signal.

Adjudication hierarchy (highest to lowest priority):
1. Physical veto (fever, acute illness, sensor dropout, missing baseline).
2. Biomechanical veto (DOMS, injury, excessive joint load, unresolved soreness from prior session).
3. Environmental veto (WBGT, extreme cold, altitude not acclimatized).
4. Algorithmic score \(\mathcal{D}(R)\) with thresholds \(\tau_{\text{high}}, \tau_{\text{low}}\).

Only if all vetoes are clear does the score-driven prescription propagate. The source text [PAGE 29] provides no such hierarchy, meaning the system can prescribe exercise during illness, injury, or hazardous weather solely because \(R\) crossed a numeric threshold.
