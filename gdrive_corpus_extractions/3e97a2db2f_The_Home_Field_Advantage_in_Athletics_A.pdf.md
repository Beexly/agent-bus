<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a meta-analysis of home-field advantage, not a primary biomechanical, meteorological, or physiological experiment. The central derivation is therefore a two-stage inference. First, each independent study or archival aggregation is converted to a proportion index \(p_i\), where \(p_i\) is the proportion of home-team wins among games played at home. The source explicitly defines this as “the consistent finding that home teams in sport competitions win over 50% of games played under a balanced home and away schedule” [PAGE 1]. The null value is \(p=.50\). Second, the \(p_i\) values are aggregated into an unweighted mean and compared with \(.50\). The reported “overall effect size (Mp = .604, SD = .065)” [PAGE 10] is therefore a mean of effect sizes, not a pooled game-level proportion, and this distinction matters: a 9-game effect and a 56,765-game effect receive equal weight in the mean unless a weighted model is used. The source says “a mean unweighted average p was calculated” [PAGE 10], so the inference is about the distribution of published/archival effect sizes, not about the pooled outcome of all games ever counted.

Multi-hop moderator reasoning then proceeds by decomposition. Sport type and competition level do not survive as moderators: individual versus group and collegiate versus professional are not significant. Time era, season length, game type, and sport do survive. But the sport and season-length findings are entangled. The source reports that “as the length of the season increased, the home-field advantage decreased (b = -.371, p = .024)” [PAGE 14], yet it also warns that “baseball was the only sport examined that plays seasons with more than 100 games” [PAGE 14]. Thus, the season-length ANOVA and the sport ANOVA are not clean orthogonal tests. The mediation claim is stronger than the moderator claim because the mediation model puts sport and season length into the same regression: “the effect of sport on the home-field advantage is mediated by season length” [PAGE 19], with Sobel’s \(Z=2.57, p=.01\). Still, mediation does not identify a biological or social mechanism; it only shows that the sport coefficient loses reliability when season length is included.

The pressure/championship finding is the most theoretically loaded. The source reports that “the home-field advantage is stronger for playoff/championship games (Mp = .630, SD = .090) than it is for regular-season games (Mp = .590, SD = .041)” [PAGE 16]. This directly contradicts a universal “home choke” model. However, the championship effect is not a direct measurement of anxiety, arousal, or explicit monitoring. It is an aggregate contrast between game types that also differ in crowd density, media attention, sample size, and selection. The source itself cannot dissociate these. Therefore the gray-zone audit must separate three claims: (a) home teams win more often overall; (b) the home advantage is larger in championship/playoff games on average; (c) the mechanism is threat-induced explicit monitoring of proceduralized skill. Claim (a) is strongly supported; claim (b) is supported by the meta-analytic contrast; claim (c) is a mechanistic extension imported from Beilock and colleagues, not a direct meta-analytic test.

The gray-zone audit also reveals structural weaknesses. The source excludes draws, excludes wild-card tennis matches, excludes matches between two host-country players, counts only home games to avoid double-counting, and requires expert athletes. Each exclusion improves independence but narrows generalization. The source reports that “archival effect sizes (Mp = .593, SD = .056) did not differ from those extracted from published studies (Mp = .610, SD = .069)” [PAGE 10], which supports pooling archival and published data. It also reports that “sample size (n) was not a reliable predictor of p (b = .15, p = .25)” [PAGE 10] among regular-season effects, but this does not fully resolve the game-type confound because playoff effects were not included in that regression. The result is a robust descriptive claim, a well-supported moderator pattern, and a mechanistically underdetermined pressure story. That is the correct inferential boundary.

Formal operators and units. For study \(i=1,\dots,k\), let \(W_i\) be the number of home-team wins in \(N_i\) games played at the home venue or home country, and let \(p_i\) be the proportion index:
\begin{equation}
p_i=\frac{W_i}{N_i},\qquad p_i\in[0,1],\qquad N_i\in\mathbb{N}.
\end{equation}
\(W_i\) and \(N_i\) are game counts; \(p_i\) is a dimensionless proportion. The source states that “a p computed from a sample of 20 games of which the home team won 15 would equal .75 (i.e., 15/20)” [PAGE 7]. Under the null \(p_i=.50\), the one-way chi-square operator is
\begin{equation}
\chi_i^2=\frac{(W_i-N_i/2)^2}{N_i/2}+\frac{((N_i-W_i)-N_i/2)^2}{N_i/2}
=\frac{(2W_i-N_i)^2}{N_i}\sim\chi^2_1.
\end{equation}
The aggregate operator is the unweighted mean
\begin{equation}
\bar p=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_i,\qquad k=87,
\end{equation}
with reported \(\bar p=.604\), \(SD=.065\), 95% CI \(.590\)–\(.618\), \(t(86)=14.98\), \(p<.001\), \(d=3.23\) [PAGE 10]. The CI operator is
\begin{equation}
\bar p\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,k-1}\frac{s_p}{\sqrt{k}}.
\end{equation}
The source reports that “the home team can be expected to win, on average, approximately 60% of athletic contests” [PAGE 11].

Moderator operators. For two independent groups \(A,B\),
\begin{equation}
t=\frac{\bar p_A-\bar p_B}{\sqrt{s_A^2/k_A+s_B^2/k_B}},
\end{equation}
and for multi-level moderators,
\begin{equation}
F=\frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}},
\end{equation}
followed by Tukey HSD. The reported moderator results are: sport type \(t(85)=0.57, p=.570, d=.12\); competition level \(t(85)=0.15, p=.880, d=.03\); time era \(F(3,66)=2.80, p=.046, d=.41\); season length \(F(2,80)=4.78, p=.011\); game type \(t(85)=2.82, p=.006, d=.61\); sport \(F(9,77)=5.21, p<.001\) [PAGES 11–19]. For time era, “the home-field advantage was significantly greater for games that took place before 1950 (Mp = .650, SD = .110)” [PAGE 12]. For game type, the source reports the championship contrast quoted above [PAGE 16]. For sport, “the home-field advantage for soccer (Mp = .674, SD = .091) was significantly stronger than that of any other sport” [PAGE 19], while “baseball exhibited a weaker home-field advantage (Mp = .556, SD = .051) than all sports” [PAGE 19].

Regression and mediation. The season-length regression is
\begin{equation}
p_i=\beta_0+\beta_1 L_i+\varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where \(L_i\) is season length in games and \(\beta_1\) has units of proportion change per game. The source reports \(\hat\beta_1=-.371, p=.024\) [PAGE 14]. The mediation operator is
\begin{equation}
M_i=\alpha_0+\alpha_1X_i+\varepsilon_{M,i},\qquad
p_i=\gamma_0+\gamma_1M_i+\gamma_2X_i+\varepsilon_{Y,i},
\end{equation}
with Sobel statistic
\begin{equation}
Z_{\text{Sobel}}=
\frac{\hat\alpha_1\hat\gamma_1}
{\sqrt{\hat\gamma_1^2\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\alpha_1)+\hat\alpha_1^2\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\gamma_1)}}.
\end{equation}
The source reports \(Z=2.57, p=.01\), and states that “the effect of sport on the home-field advantage is mediated by season length” [PAGE 19]. \(X\) is a categorical sport variable, \(M\) is season length in games, and \(p\) is dimensionless.

Assumptions: independence of effect sizes; no overlapping games; home-only counting to avoid double-counting; draws excluded; expert athletes only; win/loss as the only outcome; random-effects generalization; and exchangeability of effects within moderator levels. The source states that “games that concluded in draws were excluded from the effect-size calculations” [PAGE 9]. It also warns that “including all games (home and away) played by Teams A and B would count those overlapping games twice” [PAGE 7].

If game type is confounded with \(N_i\), the championship contrast cannot be read as a pure pressure effect. Finally, the aggregate is an unweighted mean of 87 effect sizes, so it is not a pooled game-level probability; large-\(N\) studies do not dominate the reported \(.604\).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from descriptive reviews to a feed-forward causal model to pressure-mechanism psychology. Courneya and Carron’s 1992 model is the ancestral operator: a “feed-forward model with five major components: game location, game location factors, critical psychological states, critical behavioral states, and performance outcome” [PAGE 2]. Game-location factors “represent four major conditions that differentially impact on teams competing at their own versus an opponent’s venue” [PAGE 2], namely crowd, familiarity, travel, and rules. Carron, Loughhead, and Bray’s 2005 update extends that framework. The present meta-analysis does not replace the model; it tests moderators that the model did not quantify.

The pressure debate is genealogically split. Baumeister and Steinhilber’s 1984 “home-field disadvantage” line argued that supportive audiences can impair performance under pressure; the source summarizes that researchers “have argued for a home-field disadvantage, whereby teams playing at home are hypothesized to choke and to perform more poorly in high-pressure games” [PAGE 15]. Schlenker et al. 1995 pushed back, asking “Where is the home choke?” and arguing that the advantage is not diminished in important games. The present meta-analysis adjudicates the average contrast in favor of the Schlenker side: championship/playoff \(Mp=.630\) exceeds regular-season \(Mp=.590\) [PAGE 16]. But the mechanism lineage is Beilock and Carr 2001 and Beilock et al. 2006: threat increases explicit monitoring, and “explicit monitoring broke down proceduralization, impairing execution” [PAGE 17]. That mechanism is conditional, not universal. The source’s own summary is careful: the data do not show that home teams never choke, only that on average they retain an advantage.

The crowd/referee lineage includes Nevill, Balmer, and Williams 2002 on crowd noise and referee judgments, plus Agnew and Carron 1994 and Schwartz and Barskey 1977 on crowd density. The familiarity lineage includes Pollard 1986 on unique playing surfaces and Pollard 2002 on reduced advantage after a stadium move. The travel lineage includes Pace and Carron 1992, Pollard 1986, Snyder and Purdy 1985, and jet-lag studies such as Recht, Lew, and Schwartz 1995 and Reilly, Atkinson, and Waterhouse 1997. These are mechanistic candidates, but the meta-analysis mostly measures their downstream aggregate, not their direct causal contribution.

Multi-week research traps: first, the aggregation trap. The overall \(.604\) hides sport-specific values from baseball \(.556\) to soccer \(.674\), so a single “home advantage” slogan overgeneralizes. Second, the season-length/baseball collinearity trap. The \(>100\)-game category is baseball-dominated, so the season-length effect cannot be cleanly separated from baseball’s specific rules, schedule, and crowd culture [PAGE 14]. Third, the championship-sample trap. Playoff effects have smaller \(N_i\), and the source’s regular-season check that “sample size (n) was not a reliable predictor of p (b = .15, p = .25)” [PAGE 10] does not automatically extend to playoffs. Fourth, the era trap. Pre-1950 effects rest on only 8 effect sizes with \(SD=.110\); the era finding is real in the meta-analysis but fragile to leave-one-out checks. Fifth, the independence trap. Overlapping home/away games, draws, wild-card entries, and host-country matches all require exclusion rules; without them, \(N_i\) inflates and significance tests become anti-conservative. Sixth, the publication/file-drawer trap. Large file-drawer \(N\) values reduce but do not eliminate concern about selective reporting.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the “home choke” model. The home athlete perceives high pressure, self-doubt rises, and the athlete explicitly monitors a proceduralized skill that should run automatically. The source’s threat account says that “when an athlete is threatened, there is an increased tendency to monitor proceduralized skills explicitly” [PAGE 17], and that this “impaired the execution of that behavior” [PAGE 17]. In recursive terms: I believe the crowd expects me to win; I believe failure will be publicly attributed to me; I monitor my technique to prevent failure; monitoring disrupts the technique; failure becomes more likely. This model predicts a home disadvantage in the highest-pressure games, especially for highly proceduralized skills. The meta-analysis does not support this as an average outcome: championship/playoff home teams still exceed regular-season home teams [PAGE 16]. So crisis habit regression survives only as a conditional, subgroup, or individual-difference model, not as the main effect.

Counter-trap is the opponent-side model. The away competitor experiences the dense, hostile crowd as a threat to identity and competence. The same explicit-monitoring pathway that can hurt the home athlete can hurt the away athlete: “when an athlete is threatened, there is an increased tendency to monitor proceduralized skills explicitly” [PAGE 17]. Meanwhile, the home competitor’s increased motivation can potentiate the prepotent response, and for expert athletes the prepotent response is often correct. The source notes that “proceduralized behaviors (e.g., a golf putting stroke) are facilitated by increased levels of motivation/arousal” [PAGE 4]. The counter-trap prediction is therefore the opposite of the home-choke prediction: high-pressure home environments should amplify the home advantage by facilitating home prepotent responses and impairing away explicit-monitoring-prone responses. The championship result \(Mp=.630\) versus \(Mp=.590\) [PAGE 16] is consistent with this counter-trap.

Clock is the temporal-importance model. It is not a single mental state but a recursive appraisal of how much each contest matters. A 162-game baseball season makes each game one of many; a 16-game football season makes each game urgent; a Game 7 makes the outcome nearly absolute. The source’s clock evidence includes season length and game type: longer seasons are associated with smaller home advantages [PAGE 14], and the NBA archive example says “home teams exhibit the highest winning percentages in Game 7 (i.e., the final and thus the most important game; 80.4%)” [PAGE 16]. The clock model predicts that championship/playoff games should show larger home effects, which the meta-analysis supports, but it also predicts heterogeneity by sport because the clock is set by schedule length and crowd density. The three models are recursive: crisis habit regression predicts home debilitation; counter-trap predicts away debilitation plus home facilitation; clock predicts that both effects scale with perceived importance. The meta-analytic average is most consistent with counter-trap plus clock, not with a universal crisis habit regression.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is valid only if the layer-specific variable is measured, temporally prior to the outcome, not collinear with home location, and strong enough to remove the moderator effect under a pre-specified model. Under that standard, the physical layer is partially admissible but not decisive. The source cites travel-distance evidence: “Studies that have examined sheer travel distance have found that the home-field advantage increased as the distance the away team traveled increased” [PAGE 3]. It also cites jet lag as a candidate: “jet lag, which is associated with long-distance, east–west travel, impacts game outcomes” [PAGE 3]. That is a legitimate physical mechanism. But the meta-analysis does not enter travel distance, travel mode, time-zone shifts, or circadian phase as covariates in the moderator models. Therefore a physical veto cannot overturn the aggregate \(Mp=.604\); it can only explain part of the residual heterogeneity. The physical layer would veto the home-location claim only if, after conditioning on measured travel and circadian load, the home effect disappeared with a confidence interval excluding the original effect. The source does not provide that test.

The biomechanical layer is also partially admissible. The source discusses proceduralized motor programs, including “a golf putting stroke,” and says such behaviors can be facilitated by motivation unless “debilitating explicit monitoring” occurs [PAGE 4]. It further reports that “explicit monitoring broke down proceduralization, impairing execution” [PAGE 17]. This is a real biomechanical/psychological bridge: pressure changes the control regime from automatic to explicit. But the meta-analysis measures win/loss proportions, not kinematic variability, reaction time, muscle activation, or movement error. A biomechanical veto would require a study showing that a specific home/away manipulation changes a measured movement variable and that this change mediates the win/loss effect. The source does not contain that mediation test. Thus biomechanics can support the pressure interpretation but cannot veto the meta-analytic descriptive claim.

The weather layer is not adjudicable from this source. There is no weather covariate, no temperature, wind, precipitation, altitude, or indoor/outdoor stratification in the reported moderator set. Weather could plausibly interact with sport, season, and venue, but the source provides no estimate. The correct adjudication is therefore “unadjudicated,” not “falsified.” A weather veto would require a pre-registered model with weather variables, venue fixed effects, and sport-specific controls; without that, weather cannot be used as proof against the home-field effect. The cross-layer verdict is: physical and biomechanical mechanisms are credible partial explanations; weather is an open layer; none of them has the evidence needed to veto the aggregate or moderator findings in this meta-analysis.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Duplicate-count veto. Recompute every \(p_i\) by counting both the home game and the corresponding away game for the same contest. The source’s independence rule says that “including all games (home and away) played by Teams A and B would count those overlapping games twice” [PAGE 7]. This is a verifiable RLVR check: stable \(p_i\) with doubled \(N_i\) is the expected signature; changed \(p_i\) is a veto.

If the negative coefficient remains reliable after excluding baseball and after controlling for sport, the season-length claim survives; if it does not, the claim is confounded and must be downgraded to “baseball-specific.”
