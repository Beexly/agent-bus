Hop one: Knight defines “saving” not as the retention of money but as the creation of a surplus of consumption goods—more goods produced than consumed by somebody at some time before capital goods come into existence [PAGE 1]. Hop two: this surplus is the resource base that permits the diversion of productive resources from consumption goods into producers’ goods; landlords, owners of capital goods, and laborers who produce capital goods are paid out of prior production or contemporaneous excess creation of consumption goods by others [PAGE 1]. Hop three: money intervenes as a mere medium of exchange and obscures the real transfer; students of economics often erroneously treat saving as saving of money and the income of capital-goods producers as a money income [PAGE 1]. Hop four: if saving is invested, real wealth is transferred to capital-goods producers, who “cash” that title in consumption goods; the transferred goods maintain those producers who would otherwise be making consumption goods for themselves or exchange [PAGE 1]. Hop five: interest arises specifically when saved wealth is not invested by the saver but transferred by loan to another person [PAGE 1].

Gray-zone audit. The first gray zone is the assumption that saved wealth can always be “cashed” at existing prices. Knight treats money as a veil over real transfers, but he does not specify the price-adjustment regime under which nominal savings translate into command over real consumption goods without changing relative prices; if prices are sticky or if the marginal propensity to save shifts discontinuously, the real transfer fails and the equation between money saving and resource diversion breaks down [PAGE 1]. The second gray zone is the homogeneity of “productive resources.” Knight assumes resources can be diverted from consumption goods to capital goods, but capital goods are heterogeneous and sector-specific; laborers, landowners, and capital owners are not perfectly fungible across sectors, so the diversion incurs retooling, retraining, and search costs that are absent from the verbal argument [PAGE 1]. The third gray zone is the temporal boundary: saving must occur “prior to the coming into existence of the capital goods,” yet Knight does not specify the length of the production period or the maturity structure of the loan that generates interest; if the loan matures before the capital good yields output, the saver’s claim cannot be realized in the expected real form [PAGE 1]. Let time be continuous, \(t \in \mathbb{T}\), measured in years. Define real output of consumption goods at date \(t\) as \(Y^C(t)\) in units of homogeneous consumption-good equivalents, and actual consumption as \(C(t)\) in the same units. Real saving is the accumulated surplus:

\[
S(t_0,t_1) \;=\; \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \big[ Y^C(\tau) - C(\tau) \big] \, d\tau ,
\qquad S \in [0,\infty) \ \text{consumption-good units}.
\]

This matches Knight’s definition of saving as “the production of more goods than are consumed … prior to the coming into existence of the capital goods” [PAGE 1]. The diversion of resources into producers’ goods is represented by the capital-goods accumulation equation:

\[
\dot{K}(t) \;=\; \Phi\big( S(t),\, L_K(t),\, \Lambda_K(t) \big) \;-\; \delta K(t),
\qquad K \in \text{capital-good units},
\]

where \(L_K(t)\) is labor directed to capital-goods production (persons or labor-hours per year), \(\Lambda_K(t)\) is land and existing capital employed in capital-goods production, \(\delta\) is the physical depreciation rate (year\(^{-1}\)), and \(\Phi\) is a transformation technology whose domain requires non-negative subsistence residuals. Knight states that producers of capital goods “are manifestly paid out of prior production or excess contemporary creation of consumption goods by other persons and equipment” [PAGE 1], which imposes the subsistence constraint:

\[
Y^C(t) - C(t) \;\ge\; \bar{c}\, L_K(t),
\]

where \(\bar{c}\) is the consumption-good subsistence requirement per worker per year (consumption units \(\cdot\) person\(^{-1}\) \(\cdot\) year\(^{-1}\)). If this inequality binds with equality, no real saving is available for diversion.

Money enters as a numeraire medium. Let \(M^s(t)\) be nominal saving balances and \(p^C(t)\) the price vector of consumption goods. Knight’s money-veil claim is:

\[
\frac{M^s(t)}{p^C(t)} \;\leftrightarrow\; S(t),
\qquad \text{“saving is erroneously thought of as the saving of money”} \ [PAGE 1].
\]

The real transfer occurs when saved wealth is invested; the title to consumption goods is transferred to capital-goods producers, who “cash” it in things they want [PAGE 1]. Formally, investment financing requires:

\[
M^s(t) \;=\; p^C(t) \cdot S(t)
\]

only if prices are flexible and there is no money illusion. The breakdown regime is \(\dot{p}^C(t) \neq 0\) during the diversion period, which changes the real value of nominal saving and invalidates the one-to-one mapping [PAGE 1].

Interest is defined on the loan margin. When saved wealth is transferred by loan rather than directly invested by the saver, a debt contract is written:

\[
D(t) \;\mapsto\; D(t)\big[1 + r\,\Delta t\big],
\]

where \(D(t)\) is the principal in nominal units, \(r\) is the nominal interest rate (year\(^{-1}\), equivalently dimensionless per annum), and \(\Delta t\) is the loan maturity in years. Knight states: “Interest arises when saved wealth is not invested by the saver, but transferred by loan to another person” [PAGE 1]. The ex-ante real interest rate is:

\[
\rho \;=\; r \;-\; \pi^e,
\qquad \pi^e \;=\; \mathbb{E}^e\!\left[ \frac{\dot{p}^C(t)}{p^C(t)} \right],
\]

with \(\rho\) in year\(^{-1}\). Singularity and breakdown regimes: (i) if Knightian uncertainty prevails—no objective probability distribution over the future marginal product of capital—then \(\mathbb{E}^e[\cdot]\) is not defined and \(r\) cannot be derived from an expected-return calculus; (ii) if \(S(t) = 0\) because \(Y^C(t) = C(t)\), the loanable-funds market is empty and \(r\) is undefined; (iii) if the subsistence constraint binds, \(\dot{K}(t)\) cannot become positive regardless of \(r\), so interest cannot command real resources into capital-goods production.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight’s argument sits inside a two-century lineage of capital theory. The idea that saving is a real surplus enabling roundabout production traces to Cantillon and Turgot, was refined by Nassau Senior’s abstinence theory, and reached formal maturity in Böhm-Bawerk’s agio theory of interest, where interest is the price of time preference across production periods of differing length. Knight’s 1921 contribution sharpens the distinction by emphasizing that money is “a mere medium of exchange” and that saving is “erroneously thought of as the saving of money” [PAGE 1], thereby anticipating Keynes’s later saving–investment identity and the liquidity-preference critique, while diverging from Keynes by treating the loan transfer as the origin of interest rather than the monetary rate set by liquidity preference. Schumpeter later added the innovation-entrepreneur margin, in which credit creates new purchasing power that is not merely a transfer of pre-existing saving.

The multi-week trap is this: because capital-goods production periods extend over months or years, a decision to divert resources today must be financed by saving accumulated over prior weeks, yet the monetary signal (interest rate, credit availability) is observed weekly. Agents and policymakers can therefore confuse a weekly increase in nominal money balances with a multi-week accumulation of real saving. If a central bank expands credit for several weeks without a corresponding increase in the physical surplus of consumption goods, the production structure is signaled to lengthen (more roundabout capital goods) while the subsistence fund for workers during that lengthening does not exist. The trap matures over multiple weeks: orders for capital goods are placed, labor is hired away from consumption-goods sectors, and only after the production lag does it become clear that the real resource transfer never occurred. The result is a cluster of malinvestments that must be liquidated once the nominal saving is revealed not to command real goods. This genealogical trap recurs whenever the monetary authority treats the weekly flow of credit as equivalent to the multi-week stock of real saving, precisely the money-veil error Knight identifies [PAGE 1].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under rising Knightian uncertainty, economic agents regress to the habit of holding money rather than transferring saved wealth by loan or direct investment. Knight notes that saving is mistaken for the saving of money [PAGE 1]; in a crisis this mistake becomes behavior. The saver refuses to part with title to consumption goods because the future return on capital goods cannot be probabilistically assessed. The borrower, facing uncertain demand for the output of the capital good, is unwilling to commit to a fixed nominal repayment \(D(t)[1+r\Delta t]\). Both sides retreat into liquidity, and the loan market that generates interest collapses not because the interest rate is too high, but because the underlying contingent claim is unpriceable.

Counter-trap: the observer (policymaker or analyst) sees the increase in money hoarding and interprets it as “excess saving,” then suppresses the nominal interest rate or expands credit to “encourage investment.” This is a counter-trap because it mistakes the symptom (money hoarding under uncertainty) for the cause (insufficient real surplus or unpriceable uncertainty). By lowering \(r\) while the subsistence constraint remains binding or while \(S(t)=0\), the counter-trap induces borrowing for capital-goods projects that cannot be maintained in kind. The recursion is: uncertainty reduces real saving → nominal money balances rise → policy interprets this as glut of saving → credit expands → resources are diverted without subsistence support → crisis deepens → further regression to money holding.

Clock: there are two clocks operating at different frequencies. The physical clock of capital-goods production runs in months to years, governed by \(\dot{K}(t)\), depreciation \(\delta\), and the time required to transfer subsistence goods to workers. The monetary clock of interest rates and credit conditions runs in weeks or days, governed by \(r\) and central-bank operations. When the monetary clock signals cheap credit faster than the physical clock can accumulate real surplus, the phase mismatch generates the boom–malinvestment–liquidation cycle. The recursive theory of mind must therefore track not only what agents believe about \(r\), but what they believe about other agents’ beliefs about the physical feasibility of the diversion, nested to the level of common knowledge regarding whether a real surplus actually exists [PAGE 1].

Veto condition: \(Y^C(t) - C(t) < \bar{c} L_K(t)\) implies \(\dot{K}(t) \le 0\) even if \(r\) is negative.

Weather layer veto: much of the baseline surplus of consumption goods historically derives from agricultural production. Weather shocks reduce \(Y^C(t)\) directly, shrinking \(S(t)\) without any change in monetary saving \(M^s\). Because Knight treats money as a veil over real transfers [PAGE 1], a nominal increase in saving cannot offset a weather-induced collapse in the real surplus. Veto condition: \(\Delta Y^C_{\text{weather}} < -\Delta S_{\text{nominal}}/p^C\) breaks the money–real parity.

Adjudication: any model that derives investment solely from the interest rate \(r\) and nominal saving \(M^s\) fails when any one of these three vetoes binds. The formal breakdown regime is the intersection where the physical retooling clock, the biomechanical subsistence floor, and the weather-driven output shock jointly constrain \(\Phi(\cdot)\). Knight’s text identifies the money veil as the analytical error, but it does not specify which layer binds first under particular empirical conditions; that determination requires institutional and physical data outside the excerpt [PAGE 1].
