<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s work sits at a nexus between Watsonian behaviorism and emerging cognitivism. His use of "purposive" and "cognitive" terminology challenged the strict S-R paradigm. The concept of "sign-gestalt" derives from Köhler’s Gestalt psychology, applied to perception. Tolman extended this to motor programs, suggesting innate perceptual-motor couplings.

Historically, this foreshadowed Tinbergen’s "sign stimuli" and Lorentz’s "fixed action patterns" in ethology. However, Tolman’s emphasis on the *chain* and its *blindness* prefigured later critiques of modularity in cognitive science (Fodor, 1983). The idea that low-level routines operate without access to high-level goals resonates with modern notions of "autonomic scripts" or "behavioral routines" in robotics (e.g., Brooks’ subsumption architecture).

Multi-week traps emerge when considering evolutionary time scales. A rigid nesting sequence might have been adaptive in stable Pleistocene environments but becomes maladaptive in fluctuating modern conditions. The trap is evolutionary: selection favors reliability of individual steps, but this can compromise overall flexibility. This creates a lag between environmental change and behavioral adaptation—a form of retrocausal pressure where past selective regimes constrain present behavior.

In neuroscience, this lineage connects to studies of habit formation (Balleine & O'Doherty, 2010), where actions become dissociated from outcomes. The basal ganglia circuitry for habits may implement something akin to Tolman’s blind chains. Dopamine signaling shifts from outcome-based to cue-based learning, effectively automating sequences. This neural implementation explains why animals continue nest-building even after the nest is destroyed—the habitual response is now cued by the residual context, not the current goal state.
