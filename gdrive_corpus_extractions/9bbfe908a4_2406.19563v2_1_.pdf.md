The derivation therefore has to move through four layers: (i) the Bradley–Terry–Luce likelihood for ordinal comparisons, (ii) the Partition-based Spike-and-Slab Fusion prior that ties equal worth values to equal ranks, (iii) the reversible-jump Gibbs sampler that traverses partition space, and (iv) the application-layer interpretation of rank-clusters under sparse or low-signal data. The central gray-zone issue is that rank-clustering is simultaneously a statistical device for uncertainty and a substantive claim about equality of objects. If the posterior mass for a cluster is high, the model says the data do not support separating the objects; if it is low, the model says the data support separation. This is why the paper emphasizes posterior rank-clustering probabilities rather than a deterministic rank order.

Starting from the Bradley–Terry pairwise operator, the paper states the probability that object \(i\) is ranked above object \(j\) as a ratio of positive worth parameters [PAGE 4]. This operator assumes \(\omega_i,\omega_j>0\). The Plackett–Luce extension gives a sequential product form for complete rankings [PAGE 4]. The important consequence is that only ratios of worth parameters matter: multiplying all \(\omega_j\) by a positive constant leaves every comparison probability unchanged. The paper notes that the data-generating BTL distribution is identifiable only up to scalar multiplication and that the Bayesian model avoids practical non-identifiability because the prior is non-uniform [PAGE 9]. The post hoc normalization \(\sum_j \omega_{0j}=1\) used in simulations makes this explicit [PAGE 15].

The PSSF prior is the formal bridge between worth parameters and rank-clusters. A partition \(g=\{C(1),\ldots,C(K_g)\}\) assigns each object \(j\) to a cluster, and each cluster receives one positive worth value \(\nu_k\) [PAGE 7]. If two objects are in the same cluster, their worth parameters are exactly equal; if they are in different clusters, their worth values are independently drawn from \(f_\nu\). The prior therefore places positive probability on the lower-dimensional sets \(\omega_m=\omega_n\), which is the spike component, while leaving positive density on \(\omega_m\neq\omega_n\), which is the slab component [PAGE 8]. This is not a thresholding rule applied after continuous shrinkage; the equality event is explicit in the prior.

A second gray zone is the data-generating mechanism for partial, incomplete, and groupwise comparisons. The model assumes judge-specific preference sets \(S_i\subseteq J\) and preference length \(R_i=|\Pi_i|\) are known [PAGE 9]. That assumption is essential. If the set of objects considered by a judge is itself endogenous—because some objects were unavailable, ignored, or structurally excluded—then the likelihood conditions on a possibly non-random comparison design. The paper acknowledges that interconnectedness among objects is not fully treated and is a future direction, especially for pairwise data where some pairs may never meet [PAGE 25].

A third gray zone is computational. The sampler alternates between partition updates via reversible-jump moves and worth updates via a closed-form Gibbs step after data augmentation. Births split one cluster into two; deaths merge two adjacent clusters; birth probability is fixed at \(b_g=0.5\) [PAGE 12]. The acceptance probability includes the posterior ratio, proposal ratio, proposal density for the deterministic split variable \(u\), and the Jacobian [PAGE 12–13]. The Jacobian is explicitly computed as \(2\nu_k/u\) [PAGE 13]. The paper warns that reversible-jump MCMC can be slow in high dimensions and recommends multiple chains and convergence checks [PAGE 11]. This matters because a chain that has not mixed can understate or overstate posterior rank-clustering probability.

A fourth gray zone is the distinction between “true equality” and “insufficient evidence.” The simulation section demonstrates that estimation improves as the number of judges increases and that rank-cluster recovery is best when the Poisson hyperparameter \(\lambda\) is near the true number of clusters [PAGE 15–16]. In low-information settings, the posterior may correctly report broad clusters simply because the data are weak. The NBA application is the clearest example: pairwise game outcomes provide less information than full rankings, and game outcomes are low-signal measurements of team ability because many factors influence results [PAGE 22]. The resulting posterior can legitimately contain many rank-clusters without implying that teams are truly equal in all respects; it implies only that the observed comparison data do not justify finer separation.

Finally, the audit must respect the model’s support constraints. Worth values are positive real numbers under the Gamma slab prior [PAGE 10]. If a worth value approaches zero, ratios involving that object can become extreme, and the paper recommends hyperparameters that place some density near zero to accommodate strong dominance [PAGE 11]. If a worth value is exactly zero, the BTL probabilities for events requiring that object become zero and the exponential data-augmentation rates can become undefined. The model is therefore well-defined on the open positive orthant but can break down at the boundary.

1. Strict preference operator and pairwise worth model.
The paper defines \(A\prec B\) as “object \(A\) is strictly preferred to \(B\)” [PAGE 3]. The Bradley–Terry pairwise probability is
\[
P[i\prec j\mid \omega_i,\omega_j]
=
\frac{\omega_i}{\omega_i+\omega_j},
\qquad \omega_i,\omega_j>0,
\]
as stated in Equation (1) [PAGE 4]. Here \(\omega_j\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\) is a dimensionless worth parameter. Probabilities are dimensionless and lie in \([0,1]\). Only ratios such as \(\omega_i/\omega_j\) are identified.

2. Plackett–Luce sequential ranking operator.
For a complete ranking \(\pi=\{1\prec 2\prec\cdots\prec J\}\), the Plackett–Luce probability is
\[
P[\Pi=\pi\mid \omega_1,\ldots,\omega_J]
=
\prod_{j=1}^{J}
\frac{\omega_j}{\sum_{j'=j}^{J}\omega_{j'}},
\]
Equation (2) [PAGE 4]. This assumes all worth parameters are positive. The denominators are strictly positive if every remaining object has positive worth.

3. Partition and PSSF prior.
A partition \(g=\{C(1),\ldots,C(K_g)\}\) of \(J=\{1,\ldots,J\}\) consists of disjoint nonempty clusters whose union is \(J\) [PAGE 7]. The PSSF hierarchical prior is
\[
G\sim f_G,
\qquad
\nu_k\mid g \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} f_\nu,
\qquad
\omega_j=\nu_{C_g^{-1}(j)},
\]
Equation (4) [PAGE 7]. The operator \(C_g^{-1}(j)\) returns the cluster containing object \(j\). If \(m\) and \(n\) are in the same cluster, then \(\omega_m=\omega_n\) exactly. If they are in different clusters, their worth values are independent draws from \(f_\nu\).

The marginal prior property is
\[
P[\omega_j]=f_\nu(\cdot),
\]
Equation (7) [PAGE 8]. This holds regardless of \(f_G\), because each object belongs to exactly one cluster and the cluster values are exchangeable [PAGE 8]. In information terms, if posterior rank-clustering probabilities are converted to surprisal, the unit is bits when using \(-\log_2 p\) and nats when using \(-\ln p\).

4. Rank-clustered BTL model.
The full model is

f_\nu=\text{Gamma}(\nu_k\mid a_\gamma,b_\gamma)\bigr),
\]
\[
\Pi_i\mid \omega \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim}
\text{Bradley-Terry-Luce}(\omega\mid S_i,R_i),
\qquad i=1,\ldots,I,

Equation (8) [PAGE 9]. The paper states that \(R_i=|\Pi_i|\) and \(S_i\) are “assumed known” [PAGE 9]. This is an assumption about the comparison design: the set of objects considered by each judge is treated as fixed and observed.

The partition prior is
\[
f_G(g)\propto \text{Poisson}(K_g\mid \lambda),
\]
Equation (9) [PAGE 10]. Thus all partitions with the same number of clusters receive the same prior probability [PAGE 10]. The continuous cluster-value prior is
\[
f_\nu(\nu_k)=\text{Gamma}(\nu_k\mid a_\gamma,b_\gamma),

Equation (10) [PAGE 10]. The Gamma prior is chosen because it supports closed-form Gibbs updates through data augmentation [PAGE 10]. The hyperparameters \(a_\gamma\) and \(b_\gamma\) are generally non-influential for the overall scale because worth is multiplicative invariant, but they matter near the boundary because extreme worth ratios may require density near zero [PAGE 11].

\nu'_{k_1}=u\nu_k,
\qquad
\nu'_{k_2}=u^{-1}\nu_k,
\qquad
u\sim \text{Unif}(0.5,1.5),

\nu_k=\sqrt{\nu'_{k_1}\nu'_{k_2}},

\frac{P(\nu',g'\mid \Pi)}{P(\nu,g\mid \Pi)}
\times
\frac{q(\nu,g\mid \nu',g')}{q(\nu',g'\mid \nu,g)}
P(u)
\left|
\frac{\partial(\nu'_{k_1},\nu'_{k_2})}{\partial(u,\nu_k)}
\right|,
\]
Equation (13) [PAGE 12]. The Jacobian is
\[
\left|
\frac{\partial(\nu'_{k_1},\nu'_{k_2})}{\partial(u,\nu_k)}
\right|
=
\frac{2\nu_k}{u},
\]
Equation (16) [PAGE 13]. The proposal ratio includes the number of splittable clusters and the binomial split count [PAGE 13].

The paper introduces auxiliary variables
\[

\text{Exponential}
\left(
\sum_{j\in S_i}\nu_{g^{-1}(j)}
-
\sum_{s=0}^{r-1}\nu_{g^{-1}(\pi_i(s))}

\]
Equation (17) [PAGE 13]. The rate is the remaining positive worth mass available at stage \(r\). The exponential rate must be strictly positive. If all remaining worth mass is zero, Equation (17) breaks down.

Equation (19) [PAGE 14]. The count \(c_k^i\) is the number of objects in judge \(i\)’s preference list that belong to cluster \(k\), and \(\delta_{irk}\) is the number of available objects in cluster \(k\) at stage \(r\) excluding previously selected objects [PAGE 14]. The units of \(y_{ir}\) are reciprocal to the exponential rate; if \(\nu_k\) is treated as a dimensionless positive worth scale, \(y_{ir}\) is a positive auxiliary quantity whose product with \(\nu_k\) enters the posterior.

7. Breakdown regimes.
The model has several exact breakdown or singularity regimes:

- Scale non-identifiability: \((\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_J)\) and \(c(\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_J)\) produce the same BTL probabilities for any \(c>0\). The paper handles this with a non-uniform prior and post hoc normalization [PAGE 9, PAGE 15].

- Boundary collapse: if \(\omega_j\to 0\), events in which object \(j\) is selected have probability approaching zero, and worth ratios involving \(j\) diverge. If \(\omega_j\to\infty\), object \(j\) dominates available comparisons. The paper notes that ratios can become very large when one object is strongly preferred [PAGE 11].

- Zero rate: the exponential augmentation in Equation (17) requires the remaining worth sum to be positive. If the remaining worth sum is zero, the augmentation distribution is undefined.

- Missing comparison coverage: if an object or pair is never observed in any \(S_i\), the likelihood contains no information about that object’s relative worth or that pair’s relative worth. The posterior for the unobserved contrast is then driven by the prior. The paper explicitly lists interconnectedness as an unresolved issue for pairwise data [PAGE 25].

- MCMC non-mixing: reversible-jump moves can converge slowly in high-dimensional partition spaces. The paper warns that “Since RJMCMC can be slow to converge in high dimensions, it is important to run multiple chains and assess for mixing and convergence” [PAGE 11].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the paper is a chain of solutions to the same problem: how to infer order from incomplete ordinal evidence without forcing false precision. Zermelo’s tournament problem and the Bradley–Terry model introduced positive worth parameters for pairwise comparisons [PAGE 4]. Plackett extended the logic to sequential choices, and Luce supplied an axiomatic justification through choice probabilities [PAGE 4]. This lineage created a durable habit: represent latent quality as positive continuous worth, then induce order by comparing worth values. The habit is useful, but it creates a retro-causal trap: once worth parameters are continuous, the analyst expects a unique total order unless an explicit mechanism for equality is added.

The Mallows lineage supplies a second historical operator: ranking distributions centered on a modal ranking with a distance metric [PAGE 6]. Mallows models are natural for complete rankings, but they become difficult when the number of clusters must be inferred because the normalizing constant can be intractable. The Clustered Mallows Model is described as requiring pre-specification of the number of rank-clusters and the number of objects per cluster [PAGE 6]. The paper points out that this creates model-space explosion: “given 7 objects there are 127 model specifications; given 10 objects there are 1023 model specifications” [PAGE 6]. This is a multi-week trap: an analyst may spend weeks comparing prespecified cluster structures, then mistake model-selection stability for inferential certainty. The current paper avoids that trap by making the partition random and estimating it jointly with worth values.

A third lineage is frequentist fusion. The fused lasso and related penalties encourage equal worth values by penalizing pairwise differences [PAGE 4]. This approach is computationally familiar, but the paper identifies four disadvantages: numerical instability, weak uncertainty quantification, difficult penalty selection, and inability to incorporate interpretable prior knowledge about cluster number or size [PAGE 5]. The retro-causal trap here is that penalty tuning becomes the hidden model. Different penalties can produce different rank-clusters, and the analyst may not have a principled way to express prior beliefs about how many clusters are plausible.

A fourth lineage is Bayesian variable selection through spike-and-slab priors [PAGE 5]. Traditional spike-and-slab priors place a point mass at zero and a continuous slab elsewhere. The PSSF prior adapts this idea to parameter fusion by placing the spike on equality manifolds \(\omega_m=\omega_n\) rather than only at zero [PAGE 8]. The historical trap avoided here is the requirement of known parameter order. The paper notes that the only other spike-and-slab fusion method it identifies requires the coefficient order to be known in advance [PAGE 5]. In ranking problems, that order is precisely the estimand, so requiring it in advance would reverse the causal direction of inference.

A fifth lineage is computational. Green’s reversible-jump MCMC provides a way to move between models of different dimension [PAGE 12]. Caron and Doucet provide a data-augmentation trick for Bayesian Plackett–Luce inference, later adapted by Mollica and Tardella [PAGE 13, PAGE 24]. The retro-causal trap here is that computational convenience can silently define the model. If the sampler cannot move efficiently between partitions, the posterior may reflect the proposal mechanism rather than the data. The paper’s recommendation to run multiple chains and inspect trace plots is a direct countermeasure [PAGE 11, PAGE 31–36].

The most recent lineage is the clustered Mallows comparison and the avoidance of selective inference. The paper states that Rank-Clustered BTL avoids selective inference because “parameter values and rank-clusters are estimated simultaneously” [PAGE 24]. It contrasts this with procedures that first select structure and then estimate parameters conditional on that structure. The historical lesson is that inference becomes invalid when the same data are used twice: once to discover clusters and again to estimate worth values as if the clusters were fixed. The current model treats the partition as a random variable, so uncertainty in the partition is propagated into worth estimation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem here is that the model is not merely estimating objects; it is also modeling how decision-makers will react to reported uncertainty. A crisis-oriented decision-maker may prefer a clean ranking because it reduces cognitive load. Under time pressure, the decision-maker may regress to habitual heuristics: choose the most familiar name, the most prestigious author, or the most visible candidate. The paper explicitly warns about this failure mode in funding decisions: “decision makers may be tempted to resort to unfair tie-breaking methods, e.g., selecting the proposal with the most famous author” [PAGE 25].

A naive crisis habit would treat a posterior rank-cluster as an inconvenience. If two proposals are tied, the decision-maker may ask the analyst to break the tie anyway. This creates a recursive trap: the analyst knows the posterior says the objects are not statistically distinguishable, but the decision-maker’s institutional clock demands a unique winner. If the analyst supplies a unique winner without preserving cluster uncertainty, the report silently converts “we cannot distinguish these objects” into “these objects have distinct worth.” That is an inference error.

The counter-trap is to make the cluster uncertainty visible and to use decision rules that respect it. The paper suggests fairer or more principled tie-breaking, such as a partial lottery, when rank-clusters appear among top proposals [PAGE 25]. In an election context, the same logic applies: if the winner is a singleton first-place cluster, the mandate can be described as stronger; if the winner is tied with other candidates, the mandate is weaker. The Minneapolis application uses this interpretation: Frey is a singleton first-place cluster and is described as having a relatively strong mandate [PAGE 20]. The recursive counter-trap is to let the decision-maker’s need for a winner coexist with an explicit representation of indistinguishability.

The clock has two meanings. First, there is the computational clock: the MCMC chain must run long enough for partition mixing. The paper specifies \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), recommends sufficient iterations, and warns about reversible-jump convergence [PAGE 11]. Trace plots for \(K\) and \(\omega\) are provided in the appendices, and the authors state that they find the trace plots demonstrate satisfactory mixing and convergence [PAGE 31]. Second, there is the institutional clock: elections, funding cycles, and policy deadlines do not wait for perfect posterior certainty. The recursive theory of mind therefore requires the analyst to forecast not only the posterior but also how the decision-maker will misuse the posterior if uncertainty is hidden.

A recursive belief chain can be stated qualitatively as follows. If the decision-maker believes the analyst will provide a hard ranking, the decision-maker may ignore cluster uncertainty. If the analyst anticipates this, the analyst should report posterior rank-clustering probabilities, credible intervals, and the number of plausible clusters. If the decision-maker understands that these summaries are robust, the decision-maker may accept a partial lottery or a transparent tie-break. If the computational clock is too short, the posterior summaries may be unreliable, so the analyst should report convergence diagnostics rather than overconfident clusters.

The most dangerous recursive failure is not bad arithmetic; it is performative certainty. The model can produce a rank-cluster with posterior probability 0.9, but a crisis report may compress that into “object A is above object B” because the report needs a sequence. The counter-trap is to preserve the cluster in the decision language: “A and B are not distinguishable under the observed comparisons.” This is especially important in low-signal settings. The NBA application states that game outcomes are low-signal measurements of team ability and that many factors influence outcomes [PAGE 22]. If a decision-maker treats the NBA posterior ordering as a deterministic causal ordering, the model is being asked to answer a question it cannot answer.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether physical, biomechanical, or weather information can veto or overturn the rank-clustered BTL inference. The answer, under the paper’s formal model, is no unless those variables are explicitly incorporated into the likelihood or treated as external constraints. The proof follows from the model’s conditioning structure.

Let \(\Pi\) denote observed ordinal comparisons, \(S_i\) the objects considered by judge \(i\), \(R_i\) the length of judge \(i\)’s preference, \(\omega\) the worth vector, and \(g\) the partition. The Rank-Clustered BTL posterior is
\[
p(\omega,g\mid \Pi,S,R)
\propto
p(\Pi\mid \omega,S,R)\,
p(\omega\mid g)\,
p(g).
\]
This is the formal content of Equation (8) [PAGE 9]. The likelihood depends on \(\Pi\) through the BTL or Plackett–Luce probabilities and on the comparison design through \(S_i\) and \(R_i\). It does not contain physical state variables such as injury status, biomechanical load, fatigue, travel, or weather unless those variables are added as covariates or used to modify \(S_i\), \(R_i\), or \(\Pi\).

Let \(X\) be a physical, biomechanical, or weather variable. If \(X\) is not included in the model and is assumed independent of \(\Pi\) conditional on \(\omega,g\), then
\[
p(X\mid \Pi,\omega,g)=p(X).
\]
In that case, the posterior over \(X\) is unchanged by the ranking data. Therefore \(X\) cannot veto a worth inference; it is simply outside the model’s information set.

If \(X\) affects \(\Pi\) but is omitted, the model’s likelihood becomes a marginal likelihood:
\[
p(\Pi\mid \omega,g)
=
\int p(\Pi\mid \omega,g,X)\,p(X)\,dX.
\]
In this case, the inferred worth parameter \(\omega\) may absorb the effect of \(X\). The model cannot separate “team quality” from “injury effect” or “weather effect” unless \(X\) is observed or its distribution is correctly specified. This is a confounding breakdown, not a valid veto. The inferred rank-cluster may reflect an unobserved physical shock rather than stable worth equality.

The paper provides a sports example that is directly relevant. In the NBA application, it says: “game outcomes provide low signal measurements of team ability” and that “many factors influence game outcomes, such as skill, home advantage, injuries, roster changes, and luck” [PAGE 22]. This sentence is an admission that the BTL worth parameter is a reduced-form construct. It is not a complete physical model of basketball performance. The rank-clusters estimated from regular-season game outcomes summarize comparative outcomes under the observed schedule, not biomechanical readiness, injury risk, or environmental conditions.

A formal veto would require a likelihood contribution such as
\[
p(\Pi\mid \omega,X)
\]
or a constraint such as
\[
\omega_j=0 \quad \text{if object }j\text{ is physically unavailable}.
\]
The supplied model does not contain either. The BTL operator remains
\[
P[i\prec j\mid \omega_i,\omega_j]
=
\frac{\omega_i}{\omega_i+\omega_j},
\]
with positive worth values [PAGE 4]. If a physical event makes an object unavailable, the object should not appear in \(S_i\). But the paper states that \(R_i\) and \(S_i\) are assumed known [PAGE 9]. If availability is endogenous, the assumption is violated and the likelihood is misspecified.

The weather layer is even more clearly outside the NBA application as described in the source. The source does not provide weather covariates for the basketball games, and basketball games are generally indoor events. Even if weather affected travel, rest, or attendance, those effects would have to enter through an explicit covariate model or through changes in comparison availability. Without that, weather cannot adjudicate the posterior worth ordering.

The biomechanical layer can be made precise as a boundary veto. Suppose a player’s biomechanical state implies zero probability of participation. Then the team’s roster changes, and the comparison outcome may be absent or structurally different. If the model still treats the team’s worth as a fixed positive parameter, it is estimating a mixture of availability and ability. The correct veto is not to reject the posterior worth ordering automatically; the correct veto is to declare the model incomplete for the decision question. The paper itself points toward this extension by noting that the PSSF prior could be imposed on more complex BTL models with covariates [PAGE 25]. That is the formal route by which physical or contextual variables could become admissible veto variables.

The final adjudication is therefore: within the source model, physical, biomechanical, and weather variables have no veto power because they are not in the likelihood. They can invalidate the interpretation of the worth parameters if omitted but relevant, but they cannot formally overturn a posterior rank-cluster unless they are encoded through the comparison design, the likelihood, or an external decision constraint.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable from the source equations, assumptions, and applications.

1. Nonpositive-worth veto.
Verifiable condition: a proposed cluster value \(\nu_k\) is zero or negative. The model specifies
\[
\nu_k\sim \text{Gamma}(\nu_k\mid a_\gamma,b_\gamma),
\]
with support on positive real values [PAGE 10]. The BTL probability requires positive worth values:
\[
P[i\prec j\mid \omega_i,\omega_j]
=
\frac{\omega_i}{\omega_i+\omega_j}.
\]
If \(\omega_j=0\) and \(i\neq j\), then \(P[i\prec j]=1\), while \(P[j\prec i]=0\). If both are zero, the pairwise expression is undefined. For Plackett–Luce, any stage requiring a zero-worth object to be selected has probability zero. In the data augmentation step, the exponential rate
\[
\sum_{j\in S_i}\nu_{g^{-1}(j)}
-
\sum_{s=0}^{r-1}\nu_{g^{-1}(\pi_i(s))}
\]
must be strictly positive [PAGE 13]. If the remaining worth mass is zero, Equation (17) breaks down. Prediction: any inference setting that produces or requires a nonpositive worth value is vetoed. The correct posterior must remain in the positive orthant, and any observed ranking that requires selecting a zero-worth object has zero likelihood under the model.

2. No-overlap comparison veto.
Verifiable condition: two objects never appear together in any judge’s comparison set, and no observed ranking contains information linking their worth values. In pairwise data, this means no head-to-head game or no indirect chain through other objects. The likelihood for such a pair is flat with respect to their relative worth. The posterior for their worth difference is then driven only by the prior. The PSSF prior assigns cluster probabilities through \(f_G\) and values through \(f_\nu\), but without data the equality event \(\omega_m=\omega_n\) is not informed by observed comparisons. The paper acknowledges this issue when it says future work should consider “the level of interconnectedness among the assessed objects” and notes that in pairwise comparison data “some pairs of objects may never experience a head-to-head match-up” [PAGE 25]. Prediction: if object \(m\) and object \(n\) are disconnected in the comparison graph, their posterior rank-clustering probability cannot be interpreted as evidence of equal ability. It is a prior-driven statement. Any claim that the model has empirically established equality between them is vetoed.

3. Heterogeneous-judge veto.
Verifiable condition: judges are not exchangeable draws from a single preference population, but the model is fitted as if they are. Equation (8) assumes
\[
\Pi_i\mid \omega \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim}
\text{Bradley-Terry-Luce}(\omega\mid S_i,R_i),
\]
for \(i=1,\ldots,I\) [PAGE 9]. If there are latent judge groups with different preference orders, the pooled likelihood can create artificial average worth values. The paper notes that existing heterogeneous Plackett–Luce work assumes known preference groups and object-level covariates, which is a different setting [PAGE 5]. It also subsets Eurobarometer respondents to Great Britain “to avoid heterogeneity and non-proportional sampling among respondents from different European countries” [PAGE 21]. That is a practical acknowledgment that pooling heterogeneous judges can distort inference. Prediction: if judges split into groups with conflicting rankings, a pooled Rank-Clustered BTL model may report broad clusters or unstable clusters that reflect mixture heterogeneity rather than object equality. The veto is triggered if judge-group labels are available and separate within-group posteriors disagree materially. The correct remedy is a heterogeneous or mixture model, not a single pooled worth vector.

These vetoes are verifiable because each one corresponds to an explicit equation, assumption, or limitation in the source. The first veto checks positivity of worth and positivity of exponential rates. The second veto checks comparison-graph connectedness and the likelihood’s dependence on unobserved pairs. The third veto checks the iid judge assumption and the model’s treatment of preference heterogeneity.
