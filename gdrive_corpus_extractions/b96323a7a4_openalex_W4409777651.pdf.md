The paper’s central reasoning chain begins with a binary image-classification formulation of football foul detection. The authors state that the study “presents a comprehensive comparative evaluation of eight state-of-the-art Deep Learning (DL) architectures” and that the models are evaluated using “a curated dataset comprising 7000 images,” split into “70% for training (4,900 images), 20% for validation (1,400 images), and 10% for testing (700 images)” [PAGE 1]. The immediate inference is that the paper is not modeling a temporal event, a physical collision, or a rule-based officiating decision; it is modeling static frame-level evidence. That distinction propagates through the entire analysis: every reported accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC value is a measure of visual pattern discrimination under a particular dataset construction, not a proof of causal foul detection.

A second reasoning hop concerns dataset semantics. The source says, “Divided into two main classes—Foul and Not Foul—the 7000 images used in this study are from actual football match video” [PAGE 3]. The class distribution is explicitly imbalanced: “35.9% (2513 photos) were classed as Not Foul, 64.1% (4487 images) belonged to the Foul class” [PAGE 3]. However, the authors also say that the test set was balanced: “the test dataset was balanced to ensure equal numbers of foul and non-foul images (with a target of 350 images per class, subjected to the availability of adequate data per class)” [PAGE 5]. This creates a gray zone: training and validation may retain the natural class imbalance, while test metrics may be computed on a reconstructed balance. If the test set is artificially balanced, accuracy becomes less informative about real-world deployment unless the deployment prior is also 50/50, which is not true for match video, where non-foul frames dominate. The paper itself warns that “accuracy by itself might be deceptive in normal match video as foul occurrences are less common than non-foul events” [PAGE 8]. This warning is methodologically important because it means the headline accuracy values cannot be interpreted as field prevalence estimates.

A third reasoning hop concerns the annotation asymmetry. The source says, “foul images accompany dedicated (_annotations.csv) files with comprehensive object-level annotations for entities including the fouling player, the victim player, and the foul action zone itself,” while “Not Foul images are devoid of annotation files” and therefore “the whole frame is used” [PAGE 4]. This asymmetry creates a potential shortcut-learning regime. The model may learn not only the visual appearance of contact but also the distribution shift between cropped foul regions and full-frame negatives. The authors acknowledge preprocessing: “The bounding boxes were taken out and cropped and isolated the pertinent foul areas for images labeled as such. On non-foul images, on the other hand, the whole image was utilized without cropping” [PAGE 5]. If the classifier sees cropped foul scenes but full-frame non-foul scenes, then background composition, field-of-view, player scale, and framing become partially predictive of the label. The model’s reported test performance may therefore reflect both foul evidence and dataset construction artifacts.

A fourth reasoning hop concerns the metric definitions. The paper reports “test accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC)” [PAGE 1]. It defines accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score using standard confusion-matrix expressions [PAGE 8]. However, the paper’s FPR formula on page 9 appears defective: it prints “FPR = FP / FP + FN” [PAGE 9], whereas the formal false-positive rate for ROC analysis is the proportion of actual negatives incorrectly classified as positive, i.e. false positives divided by all actual negatives. That breakdown matters because ROC and AUC are derived from threshold-dependent true-positive and false-positive rates. If the false-positive denominator is mis-specified, ROC interpretation becomes unstable. The corrected formal operator must be used in any downstream evaluation.

A fifth reasoning hop concerns model behavior. The source reports that “InceptionResNetV2 achieved the highest test accuracy of 87.57% and a strong F1-score of 0.8966” [PAGE 1], while “DenseNet121, which attained the highest precision of 0.9786 and an AUC of 0.9641” [PAGE 1]. The multi-hop interpretation is that DenseNet121 is conservative in positive prediction, InceptionResNetV2 balances precision and recall better, MobileNetV2 offers competitive performance with lower computational complexity, and EfficientNetV2 fails to adapt to this cropped-foul image distribution.

A final reasoning hop concerns deployment validity. The paper claims relevance to “Video Assistant Referee (VAR)” systems and says that “accurate and understandable decision support are essential” [PAGE 2]. Yet the evidence is single-frame, non-temporal, and dependent on annotations available only for foul images. The authors themselves identify future needs: “temporal modeling techniques, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), or Transformer architectures,” multimodal fusion, domain adaptation, and real-time optimization [PAGE 18]. Therefore, the gray-zone conclusion is not that the models are useless; it is that the paper establishes a baseline for frame-level foul/non-foul visual discrimination, not a verified causal foul-officiating system. Any claim stronger than that requires additional evidence: temporal validation, calibration, cross-domain testing, counterfactual ablations, and explanation auditing.

\]

[PAGE 8]

The source then states that ROC analysis uses TPR and FPR [PAGE 8]. TPR is formally:

\[
\text{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}
\tag{5}
\]

[PAGE 9]

The source’s printed FPR expression is:

\[
\text{FPR}_{\text{source}}=\frac{FP}{FP+FN}
\tag{6-source}
\]

[PAGE 9]

However, the formal ROC false-positive rate requires the denominator to be all actual negative events:

\[
\text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}
\tag{6-corrected}
\]

The corrected equation is necessary because ROC space is defined by actual-positive and actual-negative populations. If the denominator is not \(FP+TN\), the curve’s horizontal coordinate no longer represents the probability of false alarm among true negatives.

AUC is described qualitatively by the source as the area under the ROC curve, where “the higher the better the model’s ability to distinguish between classes” [PAGE 8]. Formally, if the ROC curve is parameterized by threshold \(\tau\), then:

\[
\text{AUC}=\int_{0}^{1} \text{TPR}(\text{FPR})\,d(\text{FPR})
\]

AUC is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). An AUC of \(0.5\) corresponds to random discrimination under independent score distributions; an AUC of \(1\) corresponds to perfect threshold-separable ranking. The reported DenseNet121 AUC is \(0.9641\) and InceptionResNetV2 AUC is \(0.9622\) [PAGE 9].

The training objective is binary crossentropy. The source says: “Using a binary crossentropy loss function, accuracy as the main evaluation parameter, and a 0.0001 learning rate Adam optimizer” [PAGE 5]. For a single image \(i\), with ground-truth label \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) and predicted foul probability \(\hat{p}_i\in[0,1]\), the standard binary crossentropy loss is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(y_i,\hat{p}_i)
=
-y_i\log(\hat{p}_i)
-(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)
\]

For a batch of size \(N\):

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}
=
-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[
y_i\log(\hat{p}_i)
+
(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)
\right]
\]

Units and assumptions: \(y_i\) is a dimensionless label; \(\hat{p}_i\) is a dimensionless probability; \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}\) has information units. If the logarithm is natural, the unit is nats per image; if base-2, bits per image. The source does not specify logarithm base, but TensorFlow/Keras convention typically uses natural logarithm internally, yielding nats. The optimizer learning rate is \(10^{-4}\), a dimensionless scalar controlling parameter update magnitude [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. Batch size is \(32\) images per optimization step [PAGE 5, PAGE 8]. Epoch is a dimensionless training-cycle count; maximum epochs is \(300\), with early-stopping patience \(10\) epochs [PAGE 8].

Breakdown and singularity regimes:

1. Precision breakdown: if \(TP+FP=0\), precision is undefined. This occurs when the model predicts no positives. Reporting precision as zero in that regime is formally incorrect unless a convention is explicitly defined.

2. Recall breakdown: if \(TP+FN=0\), recall is undefined. This occurs when the evaluation subset contains no positive ground-truth events. With a balanced target test set of 350 foul and 350 non-foul images, this should not occur if the target balance is achieved, but the source notes imperfect balance [PAGE 1, PAGE 5].

3. F1 breakdown: if \(\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}=0\), F1 is undefined. This occurs when there are no true positives and either no predicted positives or no actual positives.

4. Accuracy deception under imbalance: if \(P(\text{Foul})\) is small, a trivial majority classifier can obtain high accuracy. The source explicitly warns that “accuracy by itself might be deceptive in normal match video as foul occurrences are less common than non-foul events” [PAGE 8].

5. Test-set construction breakdown: the source’s Table 2 contains internal inconsistency, including “Validation Set [PHONE]” and “Test Set 350 (Actually 251) 350 (Actually 449)” [PAGE 4]. If the actual test set contains 251 foul and 449 non-foul images rather than 350/350, then accuracy, precision, recall, and AUC must be interpreted under the actual realized prior, not the intended balanced prior.

6. Cropping distribution shift: foul images are cropped to annotated regions, while non-foul images use the whole frame [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. The classifier’s empirical risk is therefore conditional on a mixed input-generation process:

\[
R_{\text{emp}}(f)
=
\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim \hat{P}_{\text{data}}}
\left[
\ell(f(X),Y)
\right]
\]

where \(\hat{P}_{\text{data}}\) is the constructed training/test distribution, not necessarily the deployment distribution \(P_{\text{deployment}}\). If \(P_{\text{deployment}}\) includes full-frame fouls, temporally unresolved incidents, broadcast compression, rain, night lighting, or unseen camera geometry, then \(R_{\text{emp}}\) may underestimate true operational risk.

7. Temporal breakdown: the models are image-based, with input size \(224\times224\) pixels [PAGE 5]. They do not directly observe frame order, velocity, acceleration, or impact impulse. Therefore, they cannot formally establish that a contact event satisfies the temporal and biomechanical conditions of a foul unless the visual appearance alone is sufficient, which is a strong and untested assumption.

8. AUC threshold independence but calibration blindness: AUC measures ranking discrimination across thresholds, not probability calibration. A model can have high AUC while producing poorly calibrated foul probabilities. The source reports AUC but does not report calibration error, reliability diagrams, or threshold-specific expected costs.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of the paper is a convergence of three genealogies: convolutional visual representation learning, sports officiating assistance, and explainable artificial intelligence. The model family selected by the authors is historically coherent. VGG16 descends from the principle that depth with small convolutional filters can increase representational capacity [PAGE 5]. ResNet50 introduces residual learning, where identity shortcut connections stabilize gradient propagation in deep networks [PAGE 5]. Xception factorizes spatial and channel-wise learning using depthwise separable convolutions [PAGE 5]. InceptionV3 uses parallel multi-scale convolutional branches to capture features at different receptive-field scales [PAGE 5]. InceptionResNetV2 combines residual connectivity with Inception-style multi-scale modules [PAGE 5]. DenseNet121 maximizes feature reuse by connecting each layer to subsequent layers [PAGE 5]. MobileNetV2 is designed for lightweight deployment using depthwise convolutions and inverted residuals [PAGE 5]. EfficientNetV2 uses compound scaling across depth, width, and resolution [PAGE 5]. The paper’s comparative logic is therefore genealogical: each architecture encodes a different historical hypothesis about how to allocate parameters, depth, scale, and connectivity for visual recognition.

The sports-officiating genealogy is shorter but important. The source says that football’s “dynamic architecture of the game, which is characterized by continuous action and complex player interactions, makes it difficult for officials to exactly spot and fix violations in real-time” [PAGE 2]. VAR is introduced as a human-in-the-loop technology, but the source also says “human errors and oversight still exist and lead to divisive opinions that might influence game outcomes” [PAGE 2]. This framing positions automated foul detection as a decision-support layer. The paper’s literature table cites prior work on tracking, spatiotemporal action recognition, referee gesture detection, and IoT-enabled VAR-like systems [PAGE 3]. The retro-causal trap is that historical foul-detection papers often report high accuracy on narrow datasets, which can create an illusion of officiating readiness. The present paper inherits that optimism but also partially checks it by comparing multiple architectures and by emphasizing GradCAM++ interpretability.

The explainability genealogy is also explicit. The authors state that “the study emphasizes the importance of model explainability through techniques such as Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping++ (GradCAM++)” [PAGE 1]. They add that GradCAM++ is used “to visualize the regions of an image that were critical to its final output” [PAGE 2]. This lineage matters because football officiating is not only a classification problem; it is an accountability problem. A decision must be reviewable, contestable, and visually justifiable. The paper’s use of GradCAM++ is therefore not decorative; it is a genealogical attempt to connect deep visual features with human-understandable evidence.

Several multi-week traps arise from this genealogy. First, validation-loss chasing: the authors note that “researchers want to achieve lower validation loss first and high validation accuracy second” and identify DenseNet121 epoch 30, InceptionResNetV2 epoch 37, and MobileNetV2 epoch 17 as trade-off points [PAGE 10]. This is reasonable for model selection, but it can become a trap if the validation set shares camera angles, lighting conditions, or dataset artifacts with the test set. Second, architecture prestige: InceptionResNetV2’s high accuracy may tempt deployment despite its “very complex, slower training and inference” limitation [PAGE 6]. Third, lightweight bias: MobileNetV2’s efficiency is attractive for VAR, but the source warns that lightweight models may have “lower accuracy on complex fouls” [PAGE 6]. Fourth, annotation asymmetry: because foul images are cropped and non-foul images are not, a multi-week experimental loop can accidentally optimize for dataset framing rather than foul semantics. Fifth, metric fixation: the paper’s headline metrics are strong, but they do not include temporal validation, calibration, cross-league generalization, or robustness to weather and occlusion.

The retro-causal conclusion is that the paper is best understood as a controlled architectural bake-off inside a constructed image-classification universe. Its historical ancestors are image classification and visual explanation; its officiating ambition is VAR; its current evidence is frame-level. The multi-week trap is to collapse those layers prematurely.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit must model at least four agents: the model, the referee, the analyst, and the deployment system. The model’s inferred objective is to minimize binary crossentropy on the constructed dataset. The referee’s inferred objective is to make consistent, fair, real-time decisions under uncertainty. The analyst’s inferred objective is to select an architecture that appears accurate, explainable, and efficient. The deployment system’s inferred objective is to integrate with VAR without introducing unacceptable latency, bias, or false interventions. These objectives are not identical, and the paper’s evidence supports only partial alignment among them.

Crisis habit regression is the first recursive pattern. Under time pressure, an analyst may regress to the highest headline accuracy. The source reports that “InceptionResNetV2 achieved the highest test accuracy of 87.57%” [PAGE 1], so the crisis habit would be to select InceptionResNetV2 immediately. A second crisis habit is validation-loss chasing: the source says DenseNet121 achieved “the greatest validation accuracy at 0.9764” and “the lowest validation loss (0.0621)” [PAGE 9], which may cause an analyst to prioritize DenseNet121 without asking whether validation loss reflects deployment risk. A third crisis habit is explainability theater: seeing GradCAM++ heatmaps and assuming that visual plausibility equals causal validity. The source states that GradCAM++ heatmaps can “show where the model made its decision” [PAGE 19], but a heatmap can be plausible while still relying on background, crop boundaries, jersey color, or camera angle.

Counter-trap reasoning requires the analyst to model the model’s possible shortcuts. The source’s annotation asymmetry creates a strong counter-trap hypothesis: the model may learn “cropped foul region” versus “full-frame non-foul” rather than “contact event” versus “no contact event.” The counter-trap response is to test counterfactual negatives: crop non-foul images in the same way as foul images, or pad/alter backgrounds, and measure whether false-positive rate increases. Another counter-trap is to audit temporal adequacy. Since the model sees single images, it cannot distinguish a foul from a benign pose if the decisive evidence is motion. The source acknowledges this limitation indirectly by proposing future “temporal modeling techniques” [PAGE 18]. A third counter-trap is domain shift: stadium lighting, camera angle, player scale, occlusion, and broadcast compression can all alter visual statistics. The source explicitly mentions “variation in player positions, angle of camera perspective, light conditions, partial occlusion” [PAGE 2], which means a model trained on one visual distribution may not transfer to another.

The clock dimension is twofold. First, the training clock: the source reports early stopping and epoch counts: Xception at epoch 16, MobileNetV2 at epoch 17, InceptionV3 at epoch 18, DenseNet121 at epoch 30, InceptionResNetV2 at epoch 37, EfficientNetV2 at epoch 44, VGG16 at epoch 78, and ResNet50 at epoch 134 [PAGE 10]. These values show convergence speed and computational demand. For real-time VAR, inference latency matters more than training epochs, but training time is still a proxy for optimization difficulty and resource cost. The source notes that MobileNetV2 is “extremely lightweight, mobile-friendly” but may have “lower accuracy on complex fouls” [PAGE 6]. Second, the officiating clock: VAR decisions must be timely. A high-accuracy but slow model may be less useful than a slightly less accurate but low-latency model, depending on the review workflow. The source mentions “real-time applications like VAR systems” and the need for “low-latency inference while maintaining accuracy” [PAGE 6, PAGE 19].

Recursive self-correction requires the analyst to ask: “If I were the model, what would I exploit?” Given cropped fouls and full-frame negatives, the model could exploit framing. Given static frames, it could exploit pose appearance rather than causal impact. Given a balanced test set, it could appear more robust than it is under natural prevalence. Given GradCAM++, it could produce heatmaps that look meaningful without proving rule compliance. The counter-trap is to require external validation: temporal sequences, counterfactual occlusions, cross-camera tests, calibration curves, and human-expert adjudication of heatmaps.

The final recursive inference is that the paper’s models are best interpreted as candidate perception modules, not autonomous officiants. The source’s own conclusion supports this: “InceptionResNetV2, DenseNet121 and MobileNetV2 represent the best trade-offs between accuracy, precision, recall and generalization capability and are good candidates for future developments and integrations into football officiating workflows” [PAGE 19]. The phrase “future developments” is critical. It signals that the authors do not claim complete deployment readiness. A recursive deployment policy should therefore treat the models as evidence-generating assistants whose outputs must pass physical, biomechanical, temporal, and environmental veto checks before influencing officiating.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication separates four layers: physical event layer, biomechanical plausibility layer, environmental robustness layer, and computational/statistical layer. A model output may be statistically accurate yet still vetoed if it fails at a lower or higher layer.

Physical event layer. A football foul is not merely a visual configuration; it is an event involving contact, timing, and rule interpretation. The source frames foul detection as differentiating fouls from regular physical contact, and notes “the ambiguity in differentiating fouls from regular physical contact” [PAGE 1]. A single image can show a body pose that resembles a foul, but it cannot by itself establish the temporal sequence required for many foul categories. Therefore, a physical-layer veto applies when the model’s decision relies on a static pose that could be equally produced by a legal challenge, a stumble, a celebration, or a non-contact incident. Formally, if \(f(X)=1\) but the frame lacks observable contact evidence or the annotated foul zone is empty/ambiguous, the decision should be vetoed for officiating use.

Biomechanical plausibility layer. The annotated foul action zone is part of the source’s ground truth for foul images: “comprehensive object-level annotations for entities including the fouling player, the victim player, and the foul action zone itself” [PAGE 4]. GradCAM++ is used to “create visual heatmaps” and to show “regions of the image that were critical to its final output” [PAGE 1, PAGE 2]. A biomechanical veto can be defined using attribution sets. Let \(A_f(X)\) be the high-attribution region produced by GradCAM++ for input \(X\), and let \(B\) be the annotated foul action zone. If \(A_f(X)\cap B = \varnothing\) while the model predicts foul, the visual explanation does not overlap the human-labeled foul evidence. In that regime, the decision is biomechanically inadmissible even if the model’s probability is high. This veto is especially important because heatmaps can be seductive: they may highlight a jersey, ball, or background pattern that correlates with fouls in the training set but does not represent the foul mechanism.

Environmental robustness layer. The source explicitly lists “different camera angles, diverse lighting conditions and occlusions” as challenges [PAGE 2]. It also proposes future work on “domain adaptation and data augmentation strategies, for example simulating difficult lighting or synthetic crowd occlusion” [PAGE 19]. A weather/lighting veto applies if the evaluation set does not contain representative environmental variation. Rain, night matches, stadium glare, wet pitch reflections, motion blur, and broadcast compression can change pixel statistics. If the reported AUC values are obtained only on in-domain frames, the expected deployment risk is not bounded by the in-domain risk. Let \(R_{\text{in}}\) be empirical risk on the paper’s test distribution and \(R_{\text{env}}\) be risk under environmental shift. Without assumptions linking \(P_{\text{in}}\) and \(P_{\text{env}}\), \(R_{\text{env}}\) cannot be inferred from \(R_{\text{in}}\). Therefore, claims of VAR readiness are vetoed unless environmental shift testing is performed.

Computational/statistical layer. The source reports strong metrics: InceptionResNetV2 test accuracy \(0.8757\), F1-score \(0.8966\), AUC \(0.9622\); DenseNet121 precision \(0.9786\), AUC \(0.9641\); MobileNetV2 test accuracy \(0.8686\), F1-score \(0.8889\), AUC \(0.9601\) [PAGE 9]. These are impressive for frame-level classification, but they are not sufficient for deployment because the paper does not provide calibration, latency distributions, temporal validation, or cross-domain confidence intervals. A statistical veto applies if the model’s probabilities are not calibrated. If \(\hat{p}=0.9\) does not correspond to approximately 90% empirical foul frequency in a relevant validation stratum, the output cannot be used as a trustworthy decision weight. A statistical veto also applies if the test set is not independent from training by camera, match, or time. The source’s random split language: “The dataset was then divided at random into three subsets” [PAGE 5], creates a potential leakage risk if multiple frames from the same clip appear across splits.

A cross-layer veto proof can be stated as follows. Suppose a model predicts foul with probability \(\hat{p}>0.5\) on a frame. For the prediction to be admissible for officiating support, it must satisfy: (1) visual evidence overlaps the contact zone; (2) temporal evidence supports contact or illegal challenge; (3) environmental conditions are represented or bounded; (4) calibration is acceptable; and (5) inference latency fits VAR workflow. If any condition fails, the prediction is vetoed. The paper’s evidence supports condition (1) partially through annotations and GradCAM++, but conditions (2), (3), (4), and (5) remain under-evidenced.

The strongest cross-layer conclusion is that DenseNet121’s high precision makes it attractive for high-confidence foul flagging, because the source says it is “quite confident in its positive predictions” and useful “in cases where avoiding erroneous foul calls is vital” [PAGE 16]. However, high precision alone does not prove physical causality. InceptionResNetV2’s higher recall and F1 make it attractive for balanced detection, but again only within the frame-level distribution. MobileNetV2’s efficiency makes it attractive for real-time support, but its lightweight nature may miss fine-grained contact cues [PAGE 6]. Therefore, no architecture should be granted unrestricted officiating authority on the basis of this source alone.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three reinforcement-learning-style verification counterfactuals are designed to test whether the reported foul-detection performance survives adversarial or distribution-shift challenges. Each scenario is verifiable with data derived from the paper’s annotation structure and each includes a prediction and veto rule.

1. Negative-crop leakage veto.
Operational test: Create a counterfactual negative set by cropping non-foul images to player-centered regions using the same approximate scale and framing as the foul crops described in the source. The original negative set uses whole frames because “Not Foul images are devoid of annotation files” and “the whole frame is used” [PAGE 4]. If the model has learned crop-framing rather than foul semantics, then cropped non-foul images should produce more false positives than full-frame non-foul images.
Formal measurable quantity:

\[
\Delta \text{FPR}
=
\text{FPR}_{\text{cropped negative}}
-
\text{FPR}_{\text{full-frame negative}}
\]

Prediction: If the model relies on dataset framing, \(\Delta \text{FPR}\) will be positive and statistically significant. A practical veto rule is: reject the model for deployment if \(\Delta \text{FPR} > 0.05\) with a 95% confidence interval excluding zero. This does not claim that 0.05 is universally sufficient; it is a pre-registered operational margin for detecting meaningful crop leakage. The underlying rationale is that foul images were cropped while non-foul images were not [PAGE 5], so any residual framing cue is a threat to validity.

2. Foul-zone ablation veto.
Operational test: For foul images with bounding-box annotations, occlude or neutralize the annotated foul action zone and evaluate whether the model’s foul probability remains high. The source states that foul images have annotations for “the fouling player, the victim player, and the foul action zone itself” [PAGE 4]. If the model’s decision is truly based on the foul zone, removing or masking that zone should reduce the foul probability.
Formal measurable quantity:

\[
\Delta p
=
\hat{p}_{\text{original}}
-
\hat{p}_{\text{occluded foul zone}}
\]

Prediction: For a causally meaningful model, \(\Delta p\) should be positive and large across foul images. A practical veto rule is: reject the model if the median \(\Delta p < 0.10\) or if AUC on occluded versus original foul images falls below \(0.80\). If the model maintains high foul probability despite removal of the annotated foul evidence, it is likely relying on spurious context, player appearance, or background cues. This test is especially relevant because GradCAM++ is supposed to highlight regions “critical to its final output” [PAGE 1]; if occlusion does not change the output, the explanation and prediction are inconsistent.

3. Cross-camera/lighting domain-shift veto.
Operational test: Evaluate the model on a held-out set that differs by camera angle, stadium lighting, weather, or league. The source identifies “different camera angles, diverse lighting conditions and occlusions” as core challenges [PAGE 2] and recommends future domain adaptation for “difficult lighting or synthetic crowd occlusion” [PAGE 19]. The paper’s reported AUC values are strong in-domain: DenseNet121 \(0.9641\), InceptionResNetV2 \(0.9622\), MobileNetV2 \(0.9601\) [PAGE 9]. The counterfactual test asks whether those values survive under domain shift.
Formal measurable quantity:

\[
\Delta \text{AUC}
=
\text{AUC}_{\text{in-domain}}
-
\text{AUC}_{\text{shifted domain}}
\]

Prediction: If the model generalizes, \(\Delta \text{AUC}\) will be small and bounded by a pre-specified non-inferiority margin. A practical veto rule is: reject the model for broad VAR use if \(\Delta \text{AUC} > 0.05\) or if calibration error in the shifted domain exceeds \(0.10\) expected calibration error. This veto is necessary because a model trained on one distribution can retain high in-domain AUC while failing under rain, night lighting, unusual camera geometry, or compressed broadcast frames. The source’s own future-work section supports this concern by calling for “domain adaptation and data augmentation strategies” [PAGE 19].

These three counterfactual vetoes are not decorative; they test the exact weaknesses implied by the source’s methodology. The first tests annotation asymmetry. The second tests whether foul predictions depend on the annotated foul evidence. The third tests whether the model’s statistical performance transfers beyond the constructed dataset. If the model passes all three, it becomes a stronger candidate for officiating support. If it fails any one, the reported metrics should be treated as in-domain diagnostic performance rather than proof of robust automated foul detection.
