<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under drawdown stress, human market-making desks habitually regress to inventory preservation: reduce position size, widen quotes, withdraw from risky names. In this architecture, that regression maps to the Inventory Control Module overriding the Speculation Module within the Arbitration Module. Because [PAGE 1] gives no priority ordering, the crisis outcome depends on the undisclosed implementation: if IC dominates, liquidity is withdrawn precisely when market quality should be maintained; if SPEC dominates, the firm doubles down on losing inventory.

Counter-trap. The system models the market as an exogenous state S_t, but other algorithmic participants are adaptive agents observing the same Electronic Market-Maker. A recursive theory-of-mind failure occurs when counterparties spoof or layer orders to induce r_MQC to provide liquidity into a one-sided flow, or to push q_t toward a boundary so that IC triggers forced liquidation. The Arbitration Module, lacking a model of counterparty intent, cannot distinguish informed flow from manipulation. It therefore cannot anticipate that its own recommendations change the state S_{t+1} adversarially.

Clock. Two timescales govern failure: τ_arb (arbitration cycle) and τ_book (book-update inter-arrival). During calm periods τ_book is long enough that r_i remains valid; during crises τ_book collapses (message rate spikes) and τ_arb may exceed the validity window. The “clock” mismatch means the most critical decisions are made on stale states. Additionally, human circuit-breaker decisions (cited NYT/WSJ on [PAGE 1]) operate on biomechanical timescales of seconds to minutes, far slower than τ_arb, so human vetoes arrive after electronic damage is already priced into inventory. The recursive failure is therefore temporal: by the time the slow human layer recognizes a crisis, the fast electronic layer has already executed stale recommendations into a moved book.
