Veto-proof boundary: The theory is defensible only when (i) \(\mathcal{M} \neq \emptyset\) and motor images are accessible, (ii) \(\mathcal{A}\) is biomechanically intact with full rank actuation, and (iii) the environment maintains a non-zero haptic-visual channel. Crossing any singularity boundary collapses the motor-image account and forces a different explanatory layer.

RLVR is interpreted here as a reinforcement-learning verification regime in which an artificial or human agent must recover writing/reading under manipulated constraints. Three verifiable veto scenarios follow, each with a precise prediction.

Scenario 1: Motor-image erasure with intact effector.
Setup: Train an agent to produce legible cursive script. After convergence, selectively suppress the latent motor-image code (the internal grapheme-trajectory representation) while leaving the actuator command layer fully functional and paralysis-free.
Prediction: The agent cannot generate legible letter sequences despite zero motor paralysis. It may produce random scribbles or stereotypic movements, but \(I(\text{motor image};\text{script output}) \approx 0\) bits.
Veto: Execution capacity alone is insufficient; motor imagery is a necessary generative substrate. This directly mirrors the agraphia case on [PAGE 1] where the hand is “in no way paralytic” yet writing is lost because “he no longer knows how.”

Scenario 2: Haptic channel ablation in word-blindness.
Setup: A word-blind agent has learned to read by tracing letters with a finger (the Charcot strategy). Anesthetize the fingertip or sever tactile afference while preserving vision and motor execution.
Prediction: The tracing strategy collapses immediately. Because the visual recognition channel \(V\) remains lesioned and the haptic channel \(H\) now has \(C_{haptic} = 0\) bits/s, the agent cannot access the lexical meaning of letters. Reading accuracy drops to chance.
Veto: Motor images require an intact afferent loop; without haptic feedback, the “reads by writing” mechanism on [PAGE 1] cannot close.

Scenario 3: Immediate post-cataract geometric naming.
Setup: A congenitally blind agent gains sight via cataract removal. Test naming of square and disk at \(t=0\) (hours after bandage removal) versus \(t=3\) weeks, where the \(t=3\) condition includes daily active touch of the shapes paired with vision.
Prediction: At \(t=0\), the agent fails to name shapes despite clear retinal input. At \(t=3\) weeks, after haptic-visual binding, naming accuracy rises above chance. The gain correlates with active-touch exposure hours, not with visual acuity alone.
Veto: Visual input by itself is insufficient for abstract geometric concept formation; the Franz case on [PAGE 1] shows that the patient needed to perceive sensations “in the points of his fingers” before forming the idea of a square or disk.
