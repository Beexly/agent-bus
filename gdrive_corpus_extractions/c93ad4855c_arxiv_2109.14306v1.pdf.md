<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s multi-hop logic begins with a problem diagnosis, not with a network. The paper states that fine-grained action classification differs from coarse-grained recognition because of “high intra-class similarity” and because the scene and manipulated objects are shared across videos [PAGE 1]. That forces the discriminative signal into movement. The paper then says that “all possible information should be extracted from the performed movement itself” [PAGE 2], and the abstract names the three modalities: “raw RGB data, the computed optical flow and the estimated pose of the player” [PAGE 1]. The first derivation hop is therefore: low inter-class variance plus shared context implies that appearance-only or context-only shortcuts are insufficient; motion and body configuration must be added. The second hop is architectural: RGB and optical flow are processed as spatio-temporal cuboids by 3D convolutions, while pose is processed by 1D temporal convolutions, and the three branches are fused late through bilinear layers [PAGE 4]. This is not a simple ensemble. The dependency graph is cascaded: optical flow estimates the region of interest; the ROI selects the RGB/OF cuboids and the nearest detected pose; the pose descriptor is normalized and fed into its branch; all three branches meet only at the final fusion and softmax. A failure in the first stage propagates through cropping, pose association, feature extraction, fusion, and finally the class probability.

The third hop is the weakest link in that graph: the ROI center is “estimated from the maximum of the OF norm and the center of gravity of non-null OF values” [PAGE 2]. The center-of-gravity equation divides by the count of pixels with non-zero optical-flow \(L_1\) norm. If a clip has no non-null optical flow, that denominator is zero, so the ROI is singular unless the implementation supplies a fallback that the paper does not formalize. Even when non-zero, the ROI is temporally smoothed by a Gaussian filter with kernel size 41, described as “1/3 second” at the paper’s 120 fps rate [PAGE 3]. That is a physical-time constant disguised as a frame count. At another frame rate, the same kernel is a different duration, so the smoothing no longer matches the stroke’s physical jitter spectrum. The fourth hop is pose: the descriptor is \(J(i)=(x_i,y_i,s_i)^T\), with \(x_i,y_i\) coordinates and \(s_i\) an associated score [PAGE 2]. The paper reports that “if no pose is detected (which is the case for 25% of the frames), the descriptor vector is filled with ROI center coordinates and a score of 0” [PAGE 5]. This is a nominal missing-data convention, not a learned abstention mechanism. A zero score may flag absence to a human, but the network is not shown to treat it as an epistemic veto.

The gray-zone audit must separate reported gains from causal claims. In pure classification, the Three-Stream Net with attention only on OF and RGB reaches 90.0 validation and 87.3 test accuracy [PAGE 5]. In joint classification and segmentation, the same model reaches 63.9 gross accuracy under the “Avg” rule, against 47.7 for T-STCNN with full attention [PAGE 5]. Without negative labels, the Three-Stream model reaches 85.6 against 67.9 [PAGE 5]. Those are substantial source-grounded effects. But the paper also reports, for the frame-wise evaluation, “a precision of 0.99 and a recall of 0.42” with respect to the negative class, leading to an F-score of 0.59 [PAGE 5]. That is a gray-zone signature: the model is conservative about calling a negative a stroke? Actually precision 0.99 for the negative class with recall 0.42 means it misses many negatives and labels them as strokes. The paper states that the model is “still more likely to classify as a stroke some frames belonging to a the negative class” [PAGE 5]. Thus gross accuracy is partly protected by the large negative prevalence, while the stroke-detection boundary remains biased. The pose-only ablation is another veto: “Pose alone could not achieve convergence and was able to classify with 22% of accuracy only” [PAGE 5]. The three-stream gain is therefore not evidence that pose reasons about intent; it is evidence that pose adds complementary observable geometry when OF and RGB are present.

The final multi-hop audit is about task decomposition. The paper solves two tasks: pure classification with known temporal boundaries, and joint classification and segmentation from untrimmed video using an overlapping sliding window and Vote/Avg/Gauss flattening rules [PAGE 5]. The second task is more sensitive to clock alignment, negative-class dominance, and window size. The paper’s own convergence observations also matter: the attention Three-Stream model reaches convergence at epoch 1176 after 736 effective epochs, while other models reach convergence after epoch 1400 [PAGE 5]. A replication that stops early may falsely reject the architecture. Conversely, a replication that tunes on the test set may falsely accept it. The gray-zone verdict is that the source supports a conditional multimodal gain, not a general claim of robust fine-grained reasoning under missing motion, missing pose, frame-rate shift, or expert-level stroke-quality assessment.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the optical-flow foreground operator as \(V=(v_x,v_y)\) and \(V_{FG}=V\odot(M_{FG},M_{FG})\) [PAGE 2]. Here \(V\) is a two-component displacement field in frame-indexed pixel units per frame; the paper does not calibrate it to metres per second. \(M_{FG}\) is a dimensionless binary foreground mask. The assumption is that the foreground mask and the 3 by 3 Gaussian smoothing remove background motion. Breakdown regimes: if \(M_{FG}=0\) everywhere, then \(V_{FG}=0\); if the mask is wrong, the ROI and pose association are wrong; if the normalization denominator is near zero, the flow is amplified or saturated.

The ROI operator is given by
\[
X_{\max}=(x_{\max},y_{\max})=\arg\max_{x,y}\lVert V\rVert_1,
\]
\[
X_g=(x_g,y_g)=\frac{1}{\sum_{X\in\Omega}\delta(X)}\sum_{X\in\Omega}X\,\delta(X),
\quad
\delta(X)=1 \text{ if } \lVert V(X)\rVert_1\neq 0,\; 0 \text{ otherwise},
\]
\[
x_{roi}=\alpha f_{\omega_x}(x_{\max},W)+(1-\alpha)f_{\omega_x}(x_g,W),
\]
\[
y_{roi}=\alpha f_{\omega_y}(y_{\max},H)+(1-\alpha)f_{\omega_y}(x_g,H),
\]
with \(\alpha=0.6\), \(\Omega=(\omega_x,\omega_y)=(320,180)\), and
\[
f_\omega(u,S)=\max\left(\min\left(u,\omega-\frac{S}{2}\right),\frac{S}{2}\right)
\]
[PAGE 2]. Units: coordinates and \(\omega\) are pixels; \(W,H\) are crop dimensions in pixels. The paper uses \((W,H,T)=(120,120,100)\), which is “a duration of 0.83s” at 120 fps [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: the non-null optical-flow set is non-empty; the crop size is valid relative to the frame size. Singularities and breakdowns: if the non-null set is empty, the \(X_g\) denominator is zero; if \(S\) exceeds \(\omega\), the clipping interval is empty and \(f_\omega\) degenerates to a constant \(S/2\); if the ROI is clipped at the border, the pose nearest to the ROI may not be the player of interest.

The pose descriptor is
\[
J(i)=(x_i,y_i,s_i)^T
\]
[PAGE 2]. Units: \(x_i,y_i\) are pixels before normalization and dimensionless after division by \(W_{img},H_{img}\); \(s_i\) is a dimensionless confidence score. The paper uses 13 joints and a descriptor length \(N_J=14\), including the pose mean [PAGE 5]. Missing-pose regime: “if no pose is detected … the descriptor vector is filled with ROI center coordinates and a score of 0” [PAGE 5]. Multiple-player regime: “only the closest pose from the previously computed ROI center is considered” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: the fallback can make a missing pose look like a valid low-confidence pose; lower-body joints are excluded because “knees and the ankles … are often hidden by the table” [PAGE 5], so footwork and weight transfer are not represented.

Normalization of optical flow is
\[
v'=\frac{v}{\mu+3\sigma},\qquad
v_N(i,j)=
\begin{cases}
v'(i,j), & \lvert v'(i,j)\rvert \text{ less than } 1,\\
\mathrm{SIGN}(v'(i,j)), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are the mean and standard deviation of the maximum absolute values distribution of each OF component over the whole dataset [PAGE 3]. Units: \(v_N\) is dimensionless in \([-1,1]\). Singularity: \(\mu+3\sigma=0\) when the flow distribution is degenerate, for example a zero-motion clip; near-zero denominator causes numerical amplification. The clipping is a saturation regime that discards magnitude information above 1.

The 3D convolution operator is
\[
\mathrm{out}(j)=\mathrm{bias}(j)+\sum_{k=0}^{C_{in}-1}\mathrm{weight}(j,k)\star X(k),
\]
where \(j\) is the output channel index, \(C_{in}\) is the input channel count, and \(\star\) is the valid 3D cross-correlation operator [PAGE 4]. The paper uses \(3\times3\times3\) filters, dense stride, padding 1, and \(2\times2\times2\) max pooling; the first two branches use 30, 60, and 80 filters [PAGE 3-4]. Units: feature maps are dimensionless tensors. Breakdown: if the temporal length is too small for three pooling stages, the feature map becomes empty; if padding and “valid” cross-correlation are implemented inconsistently, the spatial dimensions and the flattened length change, breaking the 500-unit fully connected layer.

The late-fusion operator is
\[
y=x_1^T A x_2+b,
\]
a bilinear fully connected layer of length \(N_{classes}\) [PAGE 4]. The three pairwise outputs are summed and passed to Softmax:
\[
p_c=\frac{\exp(z_c)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N_{classes}}\exp(z_k)}.
\]
Units: logits \(z_c\) and \(y\) are dimensionless; \(p_c\) is a probability. Assumptions: the two branch feature dimensions are compatible with \(A\); the bilinear matrix has enough rank to model cross-modal interactions. Breakdown: incompatible dimensions make \(x_1^T A x_2\) undefined; low-rank \(A\) collapses the interaction; late fusion cannot recover information already lost in the OF, ROI, or pose stages. Training uses “stochastic gradient descent with Nesterov momentum and weight decay,” cross-entropy loss, warm restarts, batch size 5, momentum 0.5, weight decay 0.05, and up to 1500 epochs [PAGE 4]. The small batch and long training schedule are a variance and overfitting regime.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is visible in its own citations. Coarse-grained action recognition is represented by Kinetics, AVA-Kinetics, UCF101, and FineGym [PAGE 1, PAGE 7]. Sport-specific fine-grained recognition is represented by sensor-based tennis and table-tennis stroke work, acoustic event detection, TTNet, and pose-based football and tennis studies [PAGE 2, PAGE 6-7]. The immediate architectural ancestors are the two-stream I3D model and the Twin-STCNN with attention [PAGE 4]. The three-stream model is a retrocausal response to a known limitation of the twin model: RGB plus optical flow captures appearance and motion, but not explicit body configuration. The paper adds pose as a third branch and fuses it late, then shows that the largest gain is in joint classification and segmentation, where the third branch “allows to boost the performance up to 18% compared to the model without pose information” [PAGE 5]. The later perspective section then projects forward to depth estimation and 3D skeletons, citing SharpNet and pose-depth combination [PAGE 6]. In that sense, the three-stream network is an intermediate historical step between two-stream video recognition and 3D biomechanical analysis.

The multi-week traps are numerous. First, the dataset is small: “129 of videos recorded at 120 fps” yield “1048 actions/strokes” and only “106 negative additional samples” [PAGE 4]. A replication that adds data or changes the negative ratio can change the gross accuracy without changing the underlying classifier. Second, the split is fixed at 0.7, 0.2, and 0.1 [PAGE 4]; tuning on the test split would invalidate the reported 87.3% test accuracy. Third, the optical-flow normalization uses dataset-level \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) [PAGE 3]; computing these on the test set leaks information. Fourth, the ROI smoothing kernel is 41 frames, described as 1/3 second at 120 fps [PAGE 3]; using a different frame rate without rescaling the kernel and the cuboid duration breaks the temporal semantics. Fifth, the pose branch depends on PoseNet and on the 25% missing-pose fallback [PAGE 5]; a different pose estimator with different joint conventions changes the descriptor length and the meaning of the score. Sixth, the attention configuration matters: the best joint-task result uses attention only on the OF and RGB branches, while full attention is lower in that table [PAGE 5]. Seventh, the paper itself notes that TTStroke-21 lacks qualitative stroke annotations and that “quality assessment needs to be built by experts in the field” [PAGE 6]. A multi-week project that treats the classifier output as coaching-quality feedback would overclaim the source.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer here is not mind-reading by the model. The model observes pixels, flow, and pose; it does not observe the athlete’s intention, tactical plan, or internal state. The paper’s target is “giving feedback to the players for improving their performance” [PAGE 1], but the implemented evidence is stroke class and temporal segmentation, not inferred belief or strategy. The expert annotators are table-tennis players and STAPS experts [PAGE 4], so the labels encode expert perceptual categories. A recursive theory-of-mind audit must therefore distinguish three agents: the athlete executing a stroke, the expert annotator labeling it, and the model predicting it. The model’s “theory” of the athlete is only a mapping from multimodal movement to a label.

Crisis habit regression is the athlete-level risk. Under pressure, a player may regress to a habitual service or defensive motion, compressing the very fine-grained differences the classifier is meant to detect. The paper’s class list includes eight service variants and six offensive and six defensive strokes [PAGE 4]; several differ by spin intention or wrist action rather than gross pose. If crisis regression makes two classes physically more similar, the model’s decision boundary is stressed even when RGB and flow are clean. The paper’s own pose-only result, 22% accuracy and failure to converge [PAGE 5], shows that body configuration alone is not a sufficient proxy for the athlete’s intended action. The counter-trap is anthropomorphic overreach: one must not infer “the player decided to change tactics” from a misclassified Forehand Loop versus Forehand Topspin. The source supports only observable movement classification.

The clock is the third agent-level constraint. The paper extracts \(T=100\) frames, which is 0.83 s at 120 fps [PAGE 3]. It temporally augments training samples by drawing \(T\) successive frames around the stroke center with standard deviation \(\sigma=(\Delta t-T)/N\), \(N=6\) [PAGE 4]. At test time, “no augmentation is performed and the \(T\) extracted frames are temporally centered on the stroke segment” [PAGE 4]. If the stroke duration \(\Delta t\) is shorter than \(T\), the formula produces a negative standard deviation, which is not a valid scale parameter; the paper does not discuss that regime. If the clock is shifted, the model may see preparation or recovery frames as the stroke. The recursive audit therefore says: the athlete’s habit can change the class-conditional distribution; the annotator’s expertise defines the label; the model’s clock defines what segment is actually classified. Counter-trap design should use abstention or confidence calibration when pose is missing, flow is degenerate, or the window is not centered, rather than treating the softmax output as a mental-state report.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the imaging chain can invalidate the input before the network reasons. The ROI estimator requires non-null optical flow; if the foreground mask or flow field is zero, the center-of-gravity denominator is zero and the ROI is undefined [PAGE 2]. If the player leaves the camera field of view, pose is missed; the paper reports missing pose in 25% of frames and fills the descriptor with ROI center coordinates and score 0 [PAGE 5]. That is a physical veto on pose-dependent conclusions, not a learned uncertainty estimate. If the camera frame rate changes, the 41-frame Gaussian smoothing is no longer 1/3 second [PAGE 3], and the 100-frame cuboid is no longer 0.83 s. Any claim of frame-rate invariance is therefore vetoed unless the temporal operators are rescaled.

Biomechanical veto: the pose descriptor uses 13 joints: nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, and hips [PAGE 5]. The paper explicitly excludes knees and ankles because they are “often hidden by the table” [PAGE 5]. Therefore any claim about footwork, weight transfer, lower-limb drive, or full-body kinetic chain is outside the source’s observable layer. The table itself is a physical occluder. Multiple players in the scene are another biomechanical ambiguity: only the pose closest to the ROI center is used [PAGE 5], so a nearby opponent or coach can be substituted for the target player. The network can still classify upper-body stroke appearance, but it cannot adjudicate lower-body mechanics or player identity from the given descriptor.

Weather veto: TTStroke-21 is “recorded indoors” [PAGE 4], and the equations contain no weather, temperature, humidity, wind, or lighting variable. Weather cannot directly veto a stroke classification because it never enters the model. The only indirect path is through the physical imaging chain: if weather changes indoor lighting, shadows, or visibility, the optical flow and pose estimator could degrade. The source provides no evidence for that path and no weather-stratified test. The correct adjudication is therefore asymmetric: physical and biomechanical vetoes are source-grounded and active; weather is not a modeled layer and cannot be used as a direct causal explanation. If a deployment claims weather robustness, the claim is unsupported rather than disproven. Cross-layer adjudication should force abstention when the physical or biomechanical veto is active, but the paper’s fallback of filling missing pose with ROI coordinates and score 0 [PAGE 5] is not a calibrated veto; it is a data-imputation convention.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The RLVR verifier here is a source-consistency check: an intervention is applied, the paper’s own operators and metrics are recomputed, and a claim is vetoed if the intervention makes the claimed capability false. Three verifiable counterfactuals follow.

1. Zero-flow intervention. Set \(V=0\) or set \(M_{FG}=0\) for a test clip. The verifier checks \(\arg\max_{x,y}\lVert V\rVert_1\) and the non-null set in the \(X_g\) equation [PAGE 2]. Prediction: the ROI is undefined or degenerate; the RGB/OF crop loses its motion anchor; if the pose stream is intact, pure classification collapses toward the pose-only regime of 22% test accuracy and non-convergence [PAGE 5]. For joint classification and segmentation, the three-stream result should fall below the reported 63.9% and toward or below the T-STCNN† 47.7% Vote score [PAGE 5]. Veto: any claim that the three-stream model is robust to absence of optical flow.

2. Pose blackout intervention. Set every pose score \(s_i=0\) and remove the pose branch, or mask the pose descriptor to the fallback state for all frames. The verifier checks the missing-pose fraction and the descriptor length \(N_J=14\) [PAGE 5]. Prediction: the “up to 18%” joint-task gain over the model without pose information disappears [PAGE 5]; the joint segmentation score reverts toward the Twin-STCNN range; pure classification remains close to the twin model because RGB and flow still carry appearance and motion. Veto: any claim that pose is a sufficient standalone signal, and any claim that the three-stream gain is independent of the pose branch.

3. Clock-warp intervention. Resample 120 fps video to 30 fps while keeping \(T=100\) and the 41-frame Gaussian kernel. The verifier computes the new cuboid duration \(100/30=3.33\) s instead of 0.83 s, and the new smoothing duration \(41/30=1.37\) s instead of approximately 1/3 s [PAGE 3]. Prediction: the temporal augmentation scale \(\sigma=(\Delta t-T)/N\) [PAGE 4] and the test-time centering no longer correspond to the stroke’s physical duration; ROI smoothing averages over a much longer physical interval; fine-grained service and offensive-stroke classification degrades, and joint segmentation becomes unstable across window sizes 150 and 201 [PAGE 5]. Veto: any claim of frame-rate invariance or physical-time equivalence without rescaling the temporal operators.
