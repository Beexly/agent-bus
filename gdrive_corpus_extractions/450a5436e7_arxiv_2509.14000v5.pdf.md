<THINK_TRACE>
The reasoning chain begins with the paper’s core operational premise: under moderate GNSS jamming, a low-cost receiver may continue to output a valid-looking position solution even though the position is biased. The source states this directly: “Under moderate jamming, low-cost receivers keep reporting a valid fix, shifted by up to several meters and often without an interference flag.” [PAGE 1] This is the crisis regime: not total denial, but silent degradation. The paper therefore formulates the problem not as raw-signal anti-jam processing, but as a post-solution regression and classification problem over the standard receiver output.

The first derivation hop is the observation model. At epoch \(t\), the receiver observes a set of satellites \(S_t\), each with features such as SNR, azimuth, and elevation, plus the receiver state \(r_t\). The observation set is written as \(O_t = \{r_t, o_{t,1}, \ldots, o_{t,|S_t|}\}\) [PAGE 5]. The paper then defines the learning target as a pair: a horizontal position deviation vector \(Y_t = (\Delta \text{lat}_t, \Delta \text{lon}_t)\) and a jamming setting class \(c_t\). The model is \(\left(\hat{Y}_t, \hat{c}_t\right) = \Phi(\{O_{t-k}, \ldots, O_t\})\) [PAGE 5]. This is a multi-task formulation: one head estimates the continuous position correction, the other classifies the interference condition. The classification set has 19 classes: no jamming plus three jammer types at six power levels [PAGE 5].

The second hop is structural. Because the number of visible satellites changes over time, the authors convert each epoch into a heterogeneous star graph \(G_t\). The receiver is the central node, and visible satellites are leaf nodes. The paper says: “the receiver–satellite constellation inherently forms a star graph, with the receiver as the central node connected to visible satellites” [PAGE 5]. This choice is crucial because jamming causes dropouts, reacquisitions, and abrupt SNR changes. A temporal graph can represent those topology changes without imputing missing satellites as if they were ordinary missing entries in a fixed-length table. The paper contrasts stable normal operation with jamming: “Satellite tracking status is intermittently lost, causing the edges between the receiver and satellites to disappear suddenly” [PAGE 5].

The third hop is temporal memory. JaGNet uses a single HeteroGCLSTM layer. For the receiver node, each gate fuses current receiver input, previous receiver hidden state, and mean-aggregated previous satellite hidden states. The gate equation is given as
\[
g^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} =
\sigma\left(
W^{(rec)}_{g,x} x^{(t)}_{\text{rec}}
+ W^{(rec)}_{g,rec} h^{(t-1)}_{\text{rec}}
+ W^{(rec)}_{g,sat} \frac{1}{|S_t|}\sum_{s \in S_t} h^{(t-1)}_s
+ b^{(rec)}_g
\right)
\]
for gates \(g \in \{i, f, o, \tilde{c}\}\), with \(\tanh\) replacing \(\sigma\) for the cell candidate [PAGE 6]. The mean over satellites is permutation-invariant and accommodates variable constellation size. The receiver cell and hidden state then update through standard LSTM dynamics:
\[
c^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} = f^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} \odot c^{(t-1)}_{\text{rec}} + i^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} \odot \tilde{c}^{(t)}_{\text{rec}},
\]
\[
h^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} = o^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} \odot \tanh\left(c^{(t)}_{\text{rec}}\right)
\]
[PAGE 6].

The fourth hop is outage memory. For satellites not currently visible, JaGNet does not zero the state; it freezes it. The paper specifies: “For any occluded or jammed satellite \(s \notin S_t\), the hidden and cell states are frozen rather than zeroed” [PAGE 6]. This matters under jamming because a satellite may drop and then reappear within the 10-second window. Freezing preserves pre-outage context. The paper justifies this by noting that within a 10-second window the orbital geometric shift is negligible, so the frozen state remains physically meaningful for tracking RF degradation [PAGE 6].

The fifth hop is gated correction. JaGNet does not always apply its estimated correction. At inference, “the reported position is corrected by \(\hat{Y}_t\) only when the most probable class is a jammed one, and otherwise it is passed on unchanged” [PAGE 6]. This is a safety gate: it prevents the correction head from injecting error into clean windows. The logic is important because the correction head is trained mainly on jammed windows, while clean windows should remain untouched.

The gray-zone audit must then ask where the method is strong, where it is fragile, and what its claims do and do not imply. The strongest evidence is in the reported detection and correction gains. The abstract says JaGNet detects “99.8 to 99.9% of the jammed 10-s windows with at most 1.0% false positives, against 64.7 to 66.2% for the receiver’s own monitor, and identifies the jammer type in 95.2 to 96.6% of them” [PAGE 1]. It also says the model “reduces the mean absolute position error by 67.5% at held-out locations and by 64.0% on held-out route repetitions, where its error is 20.2 cm below that of the strongest baseline” [PAGE 1]. Those are large gains, especially in the kinematic case, where the strongest baseline is separated by more than 20 cm.

But the gray zones are explicit. First, the method requires a valid fix. The paper states: “Under complete tracking loss, no position is available and JaGNet cannot operate” [PAGE 15]. Thus the method addresses silent moderate jamming, not full denial. Second, the kinematic correction is route-dependent. The paper says deployment on a new route calibrates the model with recordings of that route, and without calibration the transfer is weak: without route recordings, error reduction is only 3.3% on one bus route and 33.5% on one railway route; with ten recordings it rises to 56.2% and 47.3% respectively [PAGE 12]. Third, the static correction is not uniquely superior to all baselines: “In the static scenario, MAE of the attention-based baselines is within 0.4 cm of that of JaGNet” [PAGE 1]. The kinematic case is where the architecture’s receiver node and temporal recurrence matter most. Fourth, the laboratory data are conducted, not over-the-air, and contain no multipath or obstruction effects; the correction is not evaluated over the air because field datasets lack reference position [PAGE 15]. Fifth, the evaluation addresses accuracy, not integrity: “The evaluation addresses the accuracy of the corrected position, not its integrity” [PAGE 15]. Sixth, GPS-only operation is a deliberately severe feature set, but adding all constellations improves exact setting identification dramatically: exact setting rises from 63.5% to 95.4% static and from 60.1% to 93.2% kinematic when using GPS, Galileo, and BeiDou [PAGE 13].

Thus the deepest multi-hop interpretation is that JaGNet is not merely a jammer classifier. It is a receiver-domain error corrector that exploits the temporal graph structure of satellite visibility and SNR degradation to estimate a position-domain correction, but only when the receiver still produces a solution. Its strongest regime is moderate jamming with a valid but shifted fix; its weakest regimes are complete fix loss, unseen routes without calibration, and over-the-air correction without ground truth.

The source provides several formal operators. I restate them with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Observation operator.

At epoch \(t\), the observation set is
\[
O_t = \{r_t, o_{t,1}, \ldots, o_{t,|S_t|}\},
\]
where \(S_t\) is the set of visible satellites and each \(o_{t,i} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) contains satellite features such as SNR, azimuth, and elevation [PAGE 5]. In the main laboratory evaluation, satellite features are “C/N0, azimuth and elevation of the GPS satellites only” from standard receiver output [PAGE 7]. Units: C/N0 is reported in dB-Hz [PAGE 8]; azimuth and elevation are angular quantities, typically degrees; receiver position is latitude/longitude, with deviations later expressed in centimeters [PAGE 7].

Assumption: the receiver still provides a standard output and a valid fix. Breakdown: if no fix exists, the correction target is unavailable.

2. Target operator.

The model predicts
\[
\left(\hat{Y}_t, \hat{c}_t\right) = \Phi\left(\{O_{t-k}, \ldots, O_t\}\right),
\]
where \(Y_t = (\Delta \text{lat}_t, \Delta \text{lon}_t)\) is the deviation between reported and commanded position, and \(c_t\) is the jamming class [PAGE 5]. The class set has 19 values: no jamming plus three jammer types at six power levels [PAGE 5]. Units: \(Y_t\) is ultimately expressed in centimeters after inverse normalization [PAGE 8].

Assumption: ground truth is available during training. In the laboratory this is the commanded simulated position; in the field, no reference position is available, so correction is not evaluated over the air [PAGE 15].

3. Graph construction operator.

Each epoch is mapped to a heterogeneous star graph:
\[
G_t = (V_t, E_t, X_t),
\]
with
\[
V_t = \{\text{rec}\} \cup S_t.
\]
The receiver node carries receiver state, and each satellite node carries SNR, azimuth, and elevation [PAGE 5]. The paper states: “At each epoch \(t\), \(O_t\) maps to a heterogeneous star-graph snapshot \(G_t\)” [PAGE 5].

Breakdown: if \(S_t = \emptyset\), the satellite aggregation term has no terms. The model may still retain a receiver-only path, but if the receiver has no fix then the whole correction task is undefined.

4. Heterogeneous graph LSTM gate operator.

For receiver node type, each gate \(g \in \{i, f, o, \tilde{c}\}\) is
\[
g^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} =
\sigma\left(
W^{(rec)}_{g,x} x^{(t)}_{\text{rec}}
+ W^{(rec)}_{g,rec} h^{(t-1)}_{\text{rec}}
+ W^{(rec)}_{g,sat} \frac{1}{|S_t|}\sum_{s \in S_t} h^{(t-1)}_s
+ b^{(rec)}_g
\right).
\]
For the cell candidate \(\tilde{c}\), \(\sigma\) is replaced by \(\tanh\) [PAGE 6]. Units: hidden states are dimensionless learned representations after z-score normalization of inputs. Parameters are dimensionless learned weights.

Assumption: mean pooling is permutation-invariant and handles variable constellation sizes [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes:
- If \(|S_t|\) is small, the mean may be dominated by one or two satellites.
- If \(|S_t| = 0\), the satellite contribution disappears.
- If edges are removed, the receiver receives no satellite state from the previous epoch; the paper reports severe identification collapse in that ablation [PAGE 14].

5. LSTM state update.

For the receiver,
\[
c^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} = f^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} \odot c^{(t-1)}_{\text{rec}} + i^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} \odot \tilde{c}^{(t)}_{\text{rec}},
\]
\[
h^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} = o^{(t)}_{\text{rec}} \odot \tanh\left(c^{(t)}_{\text{rec}}\right).
\]
Here \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication [PAGE 6].

6. Frozen-state operator for absent satellites.

For each satellite \(s\),
\[
\left(h^{(t)}_s, c^{(t)}_s\right) =
\begin{cases}
\text{HGCLSTM}_{\text{sat}}\left(x^{(t)}_s, h^{(t-1)}_s, c^{(t-1)}_s, h^{(t-1)}_{\text{rec}}\right), & s \in S_t,\\
\left(h^{(t-1)}_s, c^{(t-1)}_s\right), & s \notin S_t.
\end{cases}
\]
This is the piecewise update that preserves temporal context during outages [PAGE 6].

7. Gated correction operator.

The reported position \(p_t\) is corrected only if the identification head predicts a jammed class:
\[
\hat{p}_t =
\begin{cases}
p_t - \hat{Y}_t, & \hat{c}_t \neq \text{no-jamming},\\
p_t, & \hat{c}_t = \text{no-jamming}.
\end{cases}
\]
The paper states: “the reported position is corrected by \(\hat{Y}_t\) only when the most probable class is a jammed one, and otherwise it is passed on unchanged” [PAGE 6].

8. Training loss.

The two-head model minimizes cross-entropy for identification and Huber loss for correction on jammed windows. The paper says: “the cross-entropy of the identification over all windows and the Huber loss of the correction over the jammed windows” [PAGE 8]. Formally, if \(z_t\) are identification logits and \(c_t\) is the true class,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = -\log \frac{\exp(z_{t,c_t})}{\sum_j \exp(z_{t,j})}.
\]
If natural logarithms are used, the unit is nats; if base-2 logarithms are used, bits. The source does not specify logarithm base, but the standard neural-network convention is nats.

For a scalar residual \(e = \hat{Y} - Y\), the Smooth L1 / Huber loss with threshold \(\beta\) can be written as
\[
\ell_\beta(e) =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{2}e^2, & |e| \leq \beta,\\
\beta\left(|e| - \frac{1}{2}\beta\right), & |e| > \beta.
\end{cases}
\]
The paper specifies \(\beta = 10^{-2}\) on normalized targets [PAGE 8]. Units are normalized target units during training; final metrics are inverse-transformed to centimeters [PAGE 8].

9. Accuracy and error metrics.

For \(M\) evaluation samples, latitude and longitude MAE are
\[
\text{MAE}_{\text{lat}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} |\Delta \text{lat}_i - \Delta \hat{\text{lat}}_i|,
\]
\[
\text{MAE}_{\text{lon}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} |\Delta \text{lon}_i - \Delta \hat{\text{lon}}_i|.
\]
The Euclidean MAE is
\[
\text{MAE}_{\text{euc}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}
\sqrt{
(\Delta \text{lat}_i - \Delta \hat{\text{lat}}_i)^2 +
(\Delta \text{lon}_i - \Delta \hat{\text{lon}}_i)^2
}.
\]
All errors are reported in centimeters [PAGE 9].

The error reduction is
\[
R = 1 - \frac{\text{MAE}_{\text{euc}}}{\text{MAE}_{\text{raw,euc}}},
\]
where \(\text{MAE}_{\text{raw,euc}}\) is the Euclidean MAE of the uncorrected position over the same windows [PAGE 9]. \(R\) is dimensionless.

10. Detection metrics.

Detection rate is the share of jammed windows identified as jammed. False-positive rate is the share of unjammed windows identified as jammed [PAGE 9]. These are probabilities or percentages, not physical units.

11. Real-time timing constraint.

The receiver output is 1 Hz, so inference should complete within one second. The paper reports JaGNet latency of 37.3 ms at rest and 37.7 ms in motion on a Raspberry Pi 5, with 99th percentile 38.9 ms [PAGE 14]. The real-time condition is
\[
\tau_{\text{inference}} \leq 1\,\text{s}.
\]
With \(\tau_{\text{inference}} \approx 0.0377\,\text{s}\), the model uses less than 4% of the one-second epoch [PAGE 15].

12. Breakdown and singularity regimes.

- Complete fix loss: no \(p_t\), no correction. “Under complete tracking loss, no position is available and JaGNet cannot operate” [PAGE 15].
- No satellite edges: the receiver cannot receive satellite state; identification collapses [PAGE 14].
- Single-epoch history \(W=1\): temporal context is lost; correction and identification degrade [PAGE 14].
- Missing receiver inputs in motion: route correction collapses; error reduction on FE–UKC falls from 69.1% to 8.2% without receiver inputs [PAGE 14].
- Over-the-air weak interference: detection depends strongly on C/N0 drop. JaGNet detects 97.7% of Jammertest windows with a drop of at least 10 dB, 65.4% with 6–10 dB, and 23.2% with 3–6 dB [PAGE 10].
- Distribution shift: laboratory-trained models may flag clean field data as jammed. TSMixer and Set Transformer have high false-positive rates on clean Jammertest windows, 60.6% and 8.8%, while JaGNet has 0.0% [PAGE 10].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual genealogy of this paper can be traced backward from JaGNet’s design choices to several earlier research strands.

First, the paper is descended from GNSS resilience and interference monitoring literature. It cites low-cost receivers lacking survey-grade countermeasures and notes that built-in defenses run inside closed firmware [PAGE 1]. The source says: “These built-in defenses, such as navigation filtering, integrity monitoring, multi-constellation processing and adaptive notch filtering, run inside closed firmware” [PAGE 1]. This lineage explains why the paper targets the standard output: the host application cannot inspect or modify the receiver’s internal anti-jam logic.

Second, the paper is descended from multipath position error correction. Prior works such as Kanhere et al., Weng et al., Zhu et al., Mohanty et al., and Liu et al. are summarized as correcting GNSS positioning using pseudorange residuals, satellite-wise MLPs, transformers, graph convolutional networks, or map context [PAGE 3]. The paper’s gap statement is that these methods are designed for multipath, not jamming, and often require raw pseudoranges or maps: “Existing PEC methods are designed for multipath rather than jamming, operate on single epochs, or require raw pseudoranges, which the NMEA output of commercial receivers lacks” [PAGE 1]. JaGNet’s genealogy is therefore a correction of the correction literature: it keeps the deviation-regression framing but removes raw-measurement assumptions.

Third, the paper inherits from graph learning for networks. It cites temporal link prediction, trajectory prediction, satellite network routing, scheduling, and distributed graph neural networks for resilient synchronization [PAGE 4]. The key retro-causal move is to treat the GNSS constellation not as a set of independent measurements but as a dynamic graph. The paper says: “To the best of our knowledge, no prior work on network resilience has formulated GNSS jamming mitigation as a dynamic temporal graph regression problem” [PAGE 4]. This positions JaGNet between network science and GNSS mitigation.

Fourth, the paper inherits from dynamic graph representation learning. It adopts the Discrete-Time Dynamic Graphs perspective and represents the system as a sequence of graph snapshots \(\{G_{t-k}, \ldots, G_t\}\) [PAGE 5]. The HeteroGCLSTM layer is drawn from spatiotemporal graph learning, and GraphSAGE-style aggregation appears in the receiver gate equation [PAGE 6]. The historical trap is to assume that attention or deeper graph networks are automatically superior. JaGNet explicitly rejects a second layer because the star graph has only one hop: “A second layer would reprocess the same one-hop messages with no additional structural information” [PAGE 6].

Fifth, the paper is descended from railway and transport positioning requirements. It cites EUSPA’s requirement of at most 2.5 m on each axis at 95% of the time for absolute train position [PAGE 1]. In the kinematic evaluation, JaGNet lowers the 95th percentiles of along-track and cross-track errors below that threshold on the nine railway routes: “from 4.81 and 2.95 m to 2.20 and 1.61 m, below the 2.5 m per axis at 95% of the time that EUSPA requires” [PAGE 11]. This gives the work a safety-relevant lineage.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are important:

Trap 1: Treating jamming like multipath. Multipath correction often assumes a stable set of measurements and geometry-based outliers. Jamming degrades many satellites simultaneously and can remove edges. The paper warns that traditional integrity logic “assumes that only a minority of the measurements is faulty, whereas jamming degrades all signals in its band at once” [PAGE 1].

Trap 2: Assuming raw measurements are available. Many prior correction methods require pseudorange residuals or carrier-phase data. JaGNet deliberately uses only standard NMEA-like output. The paper says this “emulates a worst-case scenario for the model” [PAGE 5].

Trap 3: Assuming temporal forecasting of absolute position is stable. The paper argues forecasting becomes unstable under non-stationary interference and prefers deviation regression [PAGE 1].

Trap 4: Assuming one epoch is enough. The ablation shows that removing history or edges severely harms identification: without edges or with one epoch, jammer-type accuracy falls to 35.9% at rest [PAGE 14].

Trap 5: Assuming laboratory detection transfers perfectly to the field. The field results show strong detection for large C/N0 drops but weaker detection for small drops: 97.7% for at least 10 dB, 65.4% for 6–10 dB, and 23.2% for 3–6 dB [PAGE 10].

Trap 6: Assuming a new route needs no calibration. The calibration study shows route-specific recordings are necessary for strong kinematic correction [PAGE 12].

Trap 7: Assuming accuracy equals integrity. The paper explicitly limits the claim: “The evaluation addresses the accuracy of the corrected position, not its integrity” [PAGE 15].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive model of the system must include four agents or layers: the jammer, the receiver firmware, JaGNet, and the downstream application.

Agent 1: jammer. The jammer’s observable strategy is defined by type and power. The paper evaluates CW, 3×CW, and wideband FM jamming at six powers from −45 to −70 dBm [PAGE 7]. CW is a narrow single tone; 3×CW uses three tones; FM raises a broad noise floor [PAGE 4]. The jammer’s effect is not just to deny service but to shift the receiver’s reported position while preserving a valid fix. The source says: “Under moderate jamming, low-cost receivers keep reporting a valid fix, shifted by up to several meters and often without an interference flag” [PAGE 1].

Agent 2: receiver firmware. The receiver’s crisis habit is to preserve continuity of navigation. It uses closed-firmware defenses such as notch filtering, RAIM-like integrity logic, and interference monitoring. These habits work against narrowband or isolated faults but fail under broadband jamming. The paper notes the receiver’s own monitor flags only a small fraction of FM windows: “the receiver’s own interference monitor reports a jamming state … and a window counts as flagged when any of its messages reports the state warning or critical” [PAGE 9]. In Table II, the receiver monitor detects 66.2% static and 64.7% kinematic overall, but only 6.9% and 3.7% under FM [PAGE 10]. This is the habit regression: the receiver’s internal monitor is not sensitive to the jamming profile that most silently biases the fix.

Agent 3: JaGNet. JaGNet’s counter-trap is to model the satellite-receiver interaction as a temporal graph and to separate detection, identification, and correction. Its gate structure allows it to exploit three signals that the receiver monitor ignores: per-satellite C/N0 trajectories, topology dropouts, and receiver state. The paper reports that “JaGNet detects 99.9% of the jammed windows at held-out locations without false positives and 99.8% on the routes with 1.0% false positives, and identifies the jammer type in 96.6 and 95.2% of the windows” [PAGE 10]. Under FM, JaGNet detects 100.0% of windows in both static and kinematic laboratory evaluations [PAGE 10].

The recursive counter-trap is that JaGNet must avoid two opposite failures: false negatives in moderate jamming, and false positives in clean conditions. The identification gate addresses false positives by leaving clean windows unchanged. The source says: “Because the correction is applied only to windows identified as jammed, the unjammed windows remain unchanged (false-positive rate 0.0%)” [PAGE 11].

Agent 4: application clock. The application requires timely position output. GNSS output is 1 Hz, and JaGNet processes a 10-second window in 37.3–37.7 ms on a Raspberry Pi 5 [PAGE 14]. This satisfies the real-time clock constraint:
\[
\tau_{\text{inference}} < 1\,\text{s}.
\]
The 10-second window is also a clock-level design choice. It is long enough to observe dropout and reacquisition patterns, but short enough to remain relevant for a moving receiver. The paper notes that within a 10-second window satellite orbital shift is negligible, making frozen satellite states valid for RF degradation tracking [PAGE 6].

Crisis habit regression:
- Receiver habit: keep reporting a fix.
- Firmware blind spot: moderate wideband FM does not trigger internal monitor strongly.
- JaGNet regression correction: infer deviation from temporal graph and apply only when jammed.

Counter-trap:
- If JaGNet overfits laboratory clean signals, it may false-positive in the field. The paper shows this risk for other models: TSMixer flags 60.6% of clean Jammertest windows [PAGE 10]. JaGNet avoids this in the reported field tests, with 0.0% false positives on clean Jammertest windows and 1.5% on clean cruise minutes [PAGE 10].

Clock trap:
- If the model window is too short, temporal structure is missed. Window 5 raises route MAE by 23.8 cm relative to the proposed model in the ablation [PAGE 14].
- If inference is too slow, the model cannot keep pace with 1 Hz output. JaGNet remains below 4% of the epoch [PAGE 15].

Multi-week field trap:
- Clean field signals are stronger and more varied than laboratory clean signals. The paper says GPS C/N0 is 41.6 ± 6.0 dB-Hz on clean cruise minutes versus 38.0 ± 4.2 dB-Hz in unjammed laboratory recordings [PAGE 10]. Models trained only in the laboratory may misclassify these stronger clean signals. JaGNet’s low false-positive rate is therefore a crucial recursive counter-trap, not merely a detection statistic.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof asks whether any lower layer invalidates the upper-layer claim that JaGNet can mitigate GNSS jamming. I adjudicate by physical, firmware, graph, motion, environmental, and integrity layers.

Layer 1: physical RF veto.

The method operates only when the receiver still produces a position. If jamming is strong enough to cause complete tracking loss, no position exists. The source states: “Under complete tracking loss, no position is available and JaGNet cannot operate” [PAGE 15]. Formally, let \(F_t\) be the event that a valid fix exists. If \(P(F_t) = 0\) over an interval, then \(\hat{p}_t\) is undefined because \(p_t\) is unavailable.

The method is therefore vetoed in the complete-denial regime. It is not vetoed in the moderate regime, where the fix remains valid but shifted. The paper’s central claim is explicitly about that silent regime: “Under moderate jamming, low-cost receivers keep reporting a valid fix, shifted by up to several meters and often without an interference flag” [PAGE 1].

Layer 2: receiver firmware veto.

The receiver’s internal monitor is not a reliable veto layer. It detects CW and 3×CW well but fails under FM. Table II shows receiver monitor detection of 6.9% static and 3.7% kinematic under FM, while JaGNet detects 100.0% [PAGE 10]. Therefore, any veto rule that relies solely on the receiver’s interference flag would incorrectly clear many FM-jammed windows. The paper’s JaGNet layer is necessary precisely because the firmware monitor has a blind spot.

Layer 3: graph topology veto.

The graph construction is not decorative; it is load-bearing. If the receiver node has no edges to satellites, the receiver cannot aggregate satellite state from the previous epoch. The ablation says: “Without edges, or with a single epoch (W = 1), this information therefore does not reach it. At rest, the edgeless or single epoch model identifies the jammer type in only 35.9% of the windows” [PAGE 14]. Thus, removing the graph edge layer vetoes identification and weakens correction.

The star topology also has a depth limit. The paper justifies one layer because the star graph has one hop: “the star graph has a single hop, so that attention can only reweight the satellites around one receiver” [PAGE 5]. A second layer would not add structural information. Therefore, a deeper model is not automatically a stronger veto-proof design.

Layer 4: kinematic state veto.

In motion, the receiver state is essential. The ablation reports: “without them, JaGNet reduces the error on the route by only 8.2%, against 69.1%, with the position only by 59.8%, and with the speed and heading only by 38.9%” [PAGE 14]. This is a strong veto: if the deployment setting cannot provide reliable receiver position, speed, or heading, the kinematic correction collapses. The method is therefore vetoed for applications that expose only satellite SNR and not receiver motion state.

Layer 5: environmental and data-collection veto.

The laboratory recordings are conducted, not over-the-air. The paper says: “The recordings are conducted, not over the air, so they contain no multipath, obstruction or antenna effects, and they cover one receiver model and one simulated date and time of day” [PAGE 15]. This creates an environmental veto against broad claims about urban canyons, weather, ionospheric variation, or changing satellite geometry over many days. The evaluation is controlled and single-date in the laboratory. The field tests are used for detection, not correction, because no reference position is available [PAGE 15].

Layer 6: integrity veto.

The paper evaluates accuracy, not safety integrity. It states: “The evaluation addresses the accuracy of the corrected position, not its integrity” [PAGE 15]. Therefore, any claim that JaGNet satisfies railway safety integrity would be vetoed unless additional integrity monitoring, fault trees, or certification analysis were provided. The EUSPA comparison is informative but not a full safety certification: JaGNet reduces the 95th percentile errors below the 2.5 m axis requirement on the evaluated railway routes [PAGE 11], but the paper itself notes that this requirement concerns absolute safe train position whose integrity the evaluation does not address [PAGE 12].

Proof summary:

Let \(C\) be the claim that JaGNet improves position accuracy under moderate jamming.

C is upheld when:
1. A valid fix exists.
2. The standard receiver output is available.
3. Temporal graph edges and history are preserved.
4. In motion, receiver state inputs are available.
5. The operating domain is similar to the evaluated domain or is recalibrated.

C is vetoed when:
1. No fix exists.
2. The receiver output lacks necessary fields.
3. The graph edges or history are removed.
4. The route is new and uncalibrated, especially in motion.
5. The environment differs strongly from the laboratory or field test conditions.
6. The required property is integrity rather than accuracy.

Thus the cross-layer adjudication supports JaGNet as a software-layer accuracy mitigation for silent moderate jamming, not as a universal replacement for receiver-level anti-jam hardware or safety integrity monitoring.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three veto scenarios are verifiable from the source’s reported experiments. Each includes a counterfactual condition, a predicted outcome, and a verification method.

Scenario 1: Complete-fix veto.

Counterfactual: Replace moderate-jamming windows with windows in which the receiver loses lock and stops reporting a position.

Prediction: JaGNet cannot produce a corrected position because the input \(p_t\) and the observation set \(O_t\) are unavailable or invalid. The paper states: “Under complete tracking loss, no position is available and JaGNet cannot operate” [PAGE 15]. The correct system behavior is not to output a fabricated correction but to signal loss of positioning and allow the application to switch sensors.

Verification:
- Use receiver logs with no NMEA/UBX position output or no satellite measurements.
- Attempt to run JaGNet.
- Expected result: undefined correction output; fallback flag should be raised.
- This veto is verifiable by a software test that checks fix availability before invoking the correction gate.

Scenario 2: Receiver-monitor-only veto under FM jamming.

Counterfactual: Use only the receiver’s internal jamming indicator as the detection layer, without JaGNet.

Prediction: FM-jammed windows will be missed at a high rate. The source reports that the receiver monitor flags only 6.9% of FM windows in the static laboratory test and 3.7% in the kinematic test [PAGE 10]. By contrast, JaGNet detects 100.0% of FM-jammed windows in those same categories [PAGE 10].

Verification:
- Parse UBX-MON-RF or equivalent receiver monitor messages.
- Mark a window as flagged if any message reports warning or critical, as defined by the paper [PAGE 9].
- Compare flagged windows against logged FM jamming intervals.
- Expected result: receiver-monitor-only detection is far below JaGNet detection under FM.
- This is verifiable directly from Table II and the receiver monitor definition.

Scenario 3: New-route calibration veto.

Counterfactual: Deploy a kinematic JaGNet model on a new route without using any recordings from that route.

Prediction: The error reduction will be small, especially for bus routes. The source reports: “Without recordings of the route (k = 0), the model of the ten other routes reduces the error by only 3.3% on the bus route FE–UKC and by 33.5% on the railway route T07” [PAGE 12]. With ten calibration recordings, reduction rises to 56.2% and 47.3% respectively [PAGE 12]. Thus, absence of route calibration vetoes strong correction transfer.

Verification:
- Train a model on all routes except the target route.
- Evaluate on the held-out repetition of the target route with \(k=0\).
- Then add \(k=10\) calibration recordings and retrain.
- Expected result: \(R\) increases from the low \(k=0\) values to the higher \(k=10\) values.
- This is verifiable from Table VII and the calibration protocol described on page 12.

Additional verifiable veto-like evidence:

Although only three scenarios are required, the source also provides a constellation-input veto. With GPS satellites only, exact jamming setting identification is 63.5% static and 60.1% kinematic. With GPS, Galileo, and BeiDou satellites as nodes, exact setting identification rises to 95.4% and 93.2% [PAGE 13]. This means that if the deployment is restricted to GPS-only features, power/type identification is substantially weaker, though detection remains strong. This can be verified by comparing Table IX rows for GPS versus all constellations.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
