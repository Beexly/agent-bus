<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when, under conditions of low \phi, decision-makers abandon model-based foresight and revert to social-proof heuristics: copying the nearest rival, assuming mean reversion of recent prices, or anchoring on the last observed cost structure. This regression is rational only if the environment is ergodic; under Knightian uncertainty it is a vulnerability because the copied behavior itself changes the state space, invalidating the heuristic.

The counter-trap operates at the reflexive level: if all agents anticipate that rivals will regress to copycat behavior during a crisis, each agent attempts to pre-empt the copycats by acting earlier. When this pre-emption is universally anticipated, the innovation is arbitrage-priced before it is implemented, and the would-be profit collapses to the normal return. The counter-trap therefore does not eliminate uncertainty; it converts potential entrepreneurial profit into a timing race whose expected excess return is zero once the recursion reaches common knowledge.

The recursive theory of mind thus predicts that the most durable profits accrue not to the most intelligent agent in isolation, but to the agent whose \Delta t_{act} remains shortest relative to the socially distributed \Delta t_{recog} during periods when \phi is exogenously depressed.
