James’s theory descends directly from Charles Darwin’s *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872), where Darwin proposed that many expressions are remnants of once-useful actions. James extends this by emphasizing the *symbolic* nature of the remnant—not just a vestigial twitch, but a metaphorical translation. The mention of Wundt and Piderit situates this within the late 19th-century German experimental tradition seeking physiological correlates of mental states. Their explanation of moral reactions as "symbolic gustatory movements" bridges the gap between physical sensation and ethical judgment, a precursor to modern embodiment theories.

The genealogy also includes Francisco Mantegazza’s attempt to extend symbolism to eyes and ears, though James dismisses it as unsuccessful. The footnote citing Professor Henle’s derivation of the negative head wag from an "incipient shudder" adds another layer: even the same gesture might have multiple evolutionary origins (rejection of objects vs. full-body rejection), hinting at convergence in symbolic systems.

However, contemporary research in mirror neurons, predictive coding, and enactive cognition strongly supports the idea that understanding others’ emotions involves simulating their sensorimotor states. James’s "symbolic" movements can be reinterpreted as minimal simulations: when we feel satisfied, we micro-simulate the act of tasting something good. Thus, his insight is not obsolete but foundational, awaiting modern mechanistic elaboration.
