<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage traced by Yost runs from indefinite Greek/Roman antiquity through English inter-town and county contests, crystallizing as a school game in the early eighteenth century despite Puritan disfavor [PAGE 1]. The decisive branch point was the school-ground size constraint: only Rugby's playground in 1850 permitted running and tackling, while Harrow, Eton, and others favored kicking, fair-catching, or dribbling [PAGE 2]. Codification began in 1863 among Rugby clubs; by 1871 London clubs agreed on a code that seeded both English Rugby and American intercollegiate football [PAGE 2]. American antecedents include Yale's 1840 class rushes (abolished for strenuousness, revived early 1870s) and the 1872 intercollegiate association of Yale, Rutgers, Princeton, and Columbia [PAGES 2-3].

The American divergence hinged on one rule innovation: permitting forwards to heel or pass the ball out from scrimmage to a back, replacing the English scrimmage-kick method [PAGE 4]. This created the snap-back, the specialized passer, and the differentiated line/backfield structure [PAGE 4]. The 1894 abolition of the flying wedge and mass play was a phase transition that suppressed high-momentum collision topology in favor of open-field play [PAGE 6].

Regional styles form a retrocausal genealogy: Princeton maintained a strict seven-man line with rare ball entrusted outside the backfield [PAGE 51]; Michigan under Yost (post-1901) developed shifting offense with ends drawn into the backfield and veiled points of attack [PAGES 53-54]; Harvard emphasized the "tandem" and tackle-back [PAGES 55-56]; Minnesota blended Yale lines with line shifts and tackle-backs into a wing-shift flavor [PAGES 57-58]; Yale relied on tackle-back and straight football [PAGES 59-60]; Wisconsin imported Princeton system via Phil King [PAGES 61-62]; Pennsylvania's Woodruff guards-back evolved into tackle-back [PAGES 63-64]; Chicago under Stagg featured the "whoa back" and general shifting [PAGES 64-65]; Army played Yale style, Navy Princeton system [PAGES 66-67]; Stanford inherited Yale, California inherited Princeton [PAGES 68-70]. Southern football diffused from eastern colleges after 1888, initially coached by easterners, later by western-trained coaches [PAGES 70-71].

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy: (1) Early-season weeks 1-10 must avoid regular scrimmage to prevent injury while hardening fundamentals [PAGES 155-156]; (2) Signal repetition over a season enables opponent decoding, necessitating key rotation [PAGES 178-181]; (3) Late-season overtraining degrades spirit and tone, so the day before a hard game uses light work only [PAGES 150-151]; (4) Weather-dependent strategy—wind at back favors punting; wind in face favors place-kicking or holding [PAGES 267-268]; (5) The "evening's work" weekly cycle (Monday criticism, Tuesday blackboard, Wednesday rules quiz, Thursday captain/quarterback, Friday rest, Saturday post-game release) is a recursive self-correction loop that prevents habit fossilization [PAGES 165-169].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the quarterback as a recursive agent engaged in level-k theory of mind against the defensive captain. Level 0 executes the drilled play without modeling the opponent. Level 1 anticipates the defensive formation and attacks its weakest point. Level 2 anticipates that the defense anticipates the offense's sequence plays; the offense therefore deploys fake attacks, delayed passes, and signal-key rotation to break the opponent's predictive model [PAGES 178-182, 240-241].

Crisis habit regression occurs under temporal and score pressure: agents revert to overlearned mass plays and repeated signal sequences. Yost warns against using sequence plays repeatedly because "it is impossible to tell what results any one play may produce" and the sequence may contain plays against which the team cannot make headway [PAGES 178-179]. The counter-trap is the fake kick, delayed pass, or formation shift executed at the moment the defense has committed to anticipating the base sequence [PAGES 240-256].

The clock is an explicit adversarial variable. With T = 4200 s and a 2-minute delay penalty [PAGE 288], latency \tau_{\text{dec}} directly converts into field-position risk. The "Hurry Ups" (pages 270-272) are procedural directives to compress \tau_{\text{dec}}: line up instantly, fall on every fumble, block hard, learn signals instantly, control temper. Failure to hurry when behind collapses the remaining time horizon and forces desperation plays with high turnover probability.

The evening's work implements a recursive self-model update: Monday written criticisms externalize individual and team-play errors [PAGE 166]; Tuesday blackboard drills spatial play simulations under compressed time [PAGE 167]; Wednesday rules quizzes enforce constraint propagation so that "no man can be a great football player unless he is an adept at the rules" [PAGE 168]; Thursday captain/quarterback sessions align the field generals' mental model of the opponents [PAGE 169]; Friday affective recovery prevents choking by removing contest rumination [PAGE 169]; Saturday post-game freedom prevents rumination from contaminating the next weekly cycle [PAGE 169]. This cycle mirrors the recursive theory-of-mind loop: observe opponent, update self-model, rehearse, recover, repeat.

\textbf{Physical layer (field geometry).} The captain and coach must inspect the field for slope, bare sod, wet spots, and footing [PAGE 263]. Wind direction determines goal selection: "the west goal will be selected by the captain who wins the toss" given a fresh breeze from the west [PAGE 30]. After every try-at-goal or field goal, teams change goals [PAGE 288]. Veto: if wind reverses at halftime, a kicker optimized for tailwind place-kicking loses the geometric advantage; the strategy set must pivot to punting or short yardage.

\textbf{Biomechanical layer (contact topology).} Tackling aim is "a point midway between the hips and the knees"; arms wrap legs below hips [PAGE 101]. Stiff arm directed at breast, head, or shoulder [PAGE 116]. Linemen must "play low, block low, charge low and tackle low"; a tackler caught "up in the air" cannot stop a mass play [PAGE 128]. Charging-machine pad height must be low; if players rise as they strike, benefits are nullified [PAGE 146]. Veto: any tackling or charging technique that elevates the center of mass above the opponent's knee-hip belt is invalidated by form failure and increased injury risk.

\textbf{Weather layer (exogenous stochasticity).} Wet vs. dry ball changes place-kicking technique; more care is required against the wind [PAGE 97]. End-over-end punt is almost useless against wind; spiral is preferred [PAGE 91]. Wet, slippery ground makes end runs "almost impossible"; ends are played close to tackles [PAGE 264]. Veto: precipitation or wind vectors outside the operational envelope remove entire play classes (end sweeps, long place-kicks) from the strategy space.

\textbf{Rule layer (formal constraints).} Offside before snap incurs 5-yard penalty [PAGE 286]. Forward pass toward opponents' goal is illegal [PAGE 295]. Piling up after whistle: 5-yard penalty; inside 10-yard line, half distance [PAGES 296, 304]. Veto proof: these rules are hard constraints; no training regimen or motivational exhortation can override them. If the defense piles up inside the 10, the half-distance rule may place the ball at the 5-yard line or half the distance to the goal, effectively granting an automatic first down [PAGE 304].
