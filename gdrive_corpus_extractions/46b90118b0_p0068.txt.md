<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman's 1932 design is a historical pivot between Thorndikean trial-and-error learning and modern cognitive expectancy theory. The food-to-water switch after nine days anticipates the outcome-devaluation paradigm later formalized by Balleine and Dickinson (1998) and the goal-directed versus habit dissociation articulated by Dickinson (1985). Hull's drive-reduction formalism (1943) supplies the competing lineage in which behavior is governed by multiplicative drive × habit dynamics rather than representational expectancy.

The nine-day food-training period constitutes a multi-week overtraining trap. Extended reinforcement can convert an initially goal-directed response into a stimulus-response habit that is insulated from current outcome value. The Day-10 contingency switch sits precisely at this inflection: if Group G persists in food-zone errors despite high thirst, the prior reinforcement history cages the current motivational state, demonstrating that habit can dominate drive. The retrocausal element is that contemporary habit-formation theory reads backward onto Tolman's page to explain why Group G was isolated—it pits a weak original drive (slight hunger) against a strong alternative drive (very thirst) while a nine-day food habit is already entrenched.

A further multi-week trap concerns the arbitrary nine-day cutoff. Without an asymptotic performance criterion (e.g., stable error rate over three consecutive days), Day 9 may fall in the acquisition, transition, or consolidation phase. If the experiment had continued for weeks after the switch, one would expect either gradual revaluation (errors decline as water expectancy strengthens) or permanent habit persistence (errors plateau at pre-switch levels). The source text provides no post-switch duration beyond the immediate change, leaving the multi-week trajectory unobserved.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Group G enters Day 10 under acute thirst (\(d_t \approx 1\)), creating a motivational crisis between executing the well-worn nine-day food run and switching to water seeking. Under stress, regression to the dominant habit is the default if the cognitive map is insufficiently flexible to represent the new goal-object.

Counter-trap: The experimenter assumes that because the rat is very thirsty, it will pursue water. This is a trap if maze running has become a conditioned response independent of reward identity. The counter-trap is to measure choice topology: does the rat run to the old food location (habit persistence) or to the new water location (goal-directed revaluation)? Without spatial choice data, error and time aggregates cannot adjudicate between these mechanisms.

Clock: The fixed nine-day interval functions as an external clock. It is unknown whether rats track trials-elapsed, whether circadian hunger/thirst oscillations interact with "throughout" deprivation, or whether the Day-10 switch aligns with an interoceptive rhythm. Without temporal control, the clock is confounded with the contingency switch, so any immediate post-Day-10 effect could reflect temporal anticipation rather than drive-based revaluation.

Recursive theory of mind: Level 0 is the rat running for food. Level 1 is the rat expecting the experimenter to deliver food. Level 2 is the rat expecting the experimenter will now deliver water after the switch. Level 3 is the experimenter expecting the rat to expect water. Tolman's design implicitly requires at least Level 2 for a purely purposive interpretation of the switch, but the source page offers no behavioral evidence (e.g., anticipatory approach to the water site before reward delivery) that the rats represented the experimenter's contingency change.
