A standard audit form is
\[
EI\,\frac{\partial^4 w(x,t)}{\partial x^4}+k_s b\,w(x,t)=q(x,t),
\]
where \(E\) is rail Young’s modulus [Pa], \(I\) is second moment of area [m\(^4\)], \(EI\) [N·m\(^2\)], \(w\) is vertical deflection [m], \(x\) is longitudinal distance [m], \(k_s\) is subgrade reaction modulus [N/m\(^3\)], \(b\) is effective beam width [m], and \(q\) is vertical load per unit length [N/m]. Breakdown: if \(k_s b\) is too low, deflections and differential settlements grow under repetitive traffic; the paper says soft support “will result in excessive deflections at rail level under dynamic loading, resulting in ballast mobilisation and loss of vertical alignment” [PAGE 3]. If \(k_s\) is too high, the paper warns of high modulus damage on shallow rock or bridge decks [PAGE 3-4]. This is a non-monotonic stiffness window, not a monotone “stiffer is better” rule.

2. Critical-velocity condition. The source says critical velocity problems involve “surface wave velocity, Rayleigh Wave velocity” [PAGE 1] and that the vehicle “starts to catch the wave up” [PAGE 4]. A standard Rayleigh-wave approximation is
\[
c_R \approx \frac{0.87+1.12\nu}{1+\nu}\sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho}},
\]
where \(c_R\) is Rayleigh wave speed [m/s], \(\nu\) is Poisson’s ratio [dimensionless], \(G\) is shear modulus [Pa], and \(\rho\) is density [kg/m\(^3\)]. Define the moving-load Mach number
\[
M=\frac{v}{c_R},
\]
where \(v\) is train speed [m/s]. Exact singularity/breakdown regime: in the ideal undamped elastic half-space, dynamic amplification becomes singular as \(M\to 1^{-}\); with finite damping the singular peak is bounded and shifted, but the near-critical amplification and geometry deterioration remain. The paper’s operational consequence is that the track “becomes difficult to maintain economically and results in the need for a speed restriction” [PAGE 4]. This is a physical veto, not a data-quality issue.

3. GPR depth inversion. The paper describes 2 GHz radar, reflections at material changes, and processing to radiograms [PAGE 5]. For a two-way travel time \(t\) [s],
\[
d=\frac{c\,t}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}},
\]
where \(d\) is depth [m], \(c\approx 3.0\times10^8\) m/s, and \(\varepsilon_r\) is relative permittivity [dimensionless]. Breakdown: the inversion fails when \(\varepsilon_r\) is unknown, when layers are thin relative to resolution, or when the reflection is not a clean interface. The paper uses GPR to infer ballast fouling via BFI, formation interface, theoretical ballast depths on structures, old dirty ballast, settlement at structures, and poor drainage [PAGE 5]. BFI is represented as a dimensionless index \(I_B\in[0,1]\) in audit notation, but the paper does not publish its closed-form equation, so no fabricated formula is asserted.

4. Rolling standard-deviation deterioration. The paper’s programme plots “a rolling SD for the previous 6 runs” and works out the rate of change “at 200mm intervals along the rail surface” [PAGE 6]. For run index \(n\), point \(j\), vertical standard deviation \(s_{j,n}\) [mm], and maintenance correction \(m_{j,n}\) [mm],
\[
\dot{s}_{j,n}=\frac{s_{j,n}-s_{j,n-1}+m_{j,n}}{\Delta N},
\]
where \(\dot{s}\) has units mm/run or mm per EMGTPA if \(\Delta N\) is traffic between runs. Breakdown: sparse runs, unrecorded tamping, or mixed track components such as jointed track [PAGE 5] can confound the rate; the paper’s own multi-source rule [PAGE 6] is the required guard.

5. Drainage probability operator. The paper defines good drainage as surcharging “not occur on average, more than one occasion in ten years” [PAGE 4]. If surcharge events follow a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\) [yr\(^{-1}\)], then
\[
P(N(10)=0)=e^{-10\lambda},\qquad \lambda\le 0.1\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}
\]
for the good-drainage audit bound. Breakdown: the bound is a frequency criterion, not a guarantee under changed rainfall or blocked drainage; poor drainage is separately defined by standing water within 0.5 m of sleeper base [PAGE 4].

6. Pile length and capacity. The paper specifies piles to “1m – 1.5m below the layer of soft material” [PAGE 8], a 140 mm threaded hollow core steel bar grouted to 220 mm, with a pile cap at “800mm below sleeper bottom level” [PAGE 8], installed in pairs “every alternate sleeper bay” [PAGE 8]. A standard static capacity audit is
\[
Q_{ult}=q_b A_b+\sum_i f_{s,i} A_{s,i},
\]
where \(Q_{ult}\) is ultimate axial capacity [N], \(q_b\) is end-bearing pressure [Pa], \(A_b\) is base area [m\(^2\)], \(f_{s,i}\) is shaft friction [Pa], and \(A_{s,i}\) is shaft area [m\(^2\)]. Breakdown: if the soft-layer depth is not known from ground investigation, the length constraint cannot be satisfied; the paper states it is “essential adequate ground investigation is undertaken” [PAGE 8].

7. Cost arithmetic. For 70 m, deep excavation is “£600K” and piling is “£160K” [PAGE 10], so the arithmetic difference is £440K per 70 m. This is not a geotechnical proof; it is only a cost comparator conditional on pile-depth and drainage preconditions.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is layered. The beam-on-elastic-foundation idea descends from Winkler-type subgrade reaction models and classical railway track mechanics; the critical-velocity idea descends from Rayleigh-wave elastodynamics and moving-load theory; GPR descends from radar reflection profiling; micropiles descend from grouted hollow-bar soil reinforcement. The paper’s own institutional genealogy is explicit in its references: “NTR-158 Technical Remit for the provision of Track Bed Investigation Site Works, Analysis and Reporting” and “NR/L2/TRK/4239 ... The Investigation, Design and Installation of Track Bed” [PAGE 10]. Retrocausally, a present-day failure identifies which earlier signal should have been causal. A “wet spot” at a dipped weld [PAGE 2] retroactively flags prior ballast fouling and pore-pressure retention; a post-reballast failure “within 6 months” [PAGE 3] retroactively invalidates the assumption that clean ballast alone fixes a pumping formation; a speed restriction after a line-speed increase retroactively exposes a critical-velocity screen that was missing. The multi-week and multi-year traps are concrete: the rolling SD uses “previous 6 runs” and 200 mm sampling [PAGE 6], so a short maintenance window can look like improvement; the tamping cycle on a stiff main line is “3 yearly” but worsens until tamping exceeds once a year [PAGE 2]; ballast life is “approximately 25 years” or “up to 50 years” [PAGE 1]; drainage surcharge is judged over ten years [PAGE 4]; pile design life is “120yr” [PAGE 8]; and the ballast-returns prediction study is only targeting “+/-10%” [PAGE 7]. The route-evaluation study’s “circa 15%” affected-route estimate [PAGE 7] is a planning prior, not a site verdict. The retrocausal lesson is that later condition data should rewrite the prior route schematic, but only when the data source is aligned and not isolated [PAGE 6].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive agent model has four roles: Track Bed Engineer, Route Asset Manager, Construction Planner, and Operations/possession controller. The engineer models the asset manager’s objective as minimizing speed-restriction risk and whole-life cost; the asset manager models the engineer as a source of evidence; the construction planner models operations as a gate on possession time; operations models passengers and safety as constraints. Crisis habit regression appears when a line-speed increase creates urgency: the familiar habit is “reballast and tamp,” because dirty ballast is the most common failure mode [PAGE 3]. But that habit regresses past the distinction between dirty ballast and sub-grade pumping: pumping “cannot be rectified by tamping or stone blowing” [PAGE 3], and re-ballasting on an inadequately investigated pumping site can fail within 6 months [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap is over-correction: demanding full physical investigation everywhere, or treating GPR as a total proof, despite the paper’s desk-study claim that analysis can occur “without any physical site investigation” [PAGE 4] and its simultaneous need for “accurate targeting of site investigation” [PAGE 7]. The balanced policy is targeted investigation, not either/or. The clock is multi-scale: 6-month post-reballast failure clock [PAGE 3]; 3-year tamping cycle [PAGE 2]; 10-year drainage surcharge clock [PAGE 4]; 25/50-year ballast life [PAGE 1]; 120-year pile design life [PAGE 8]; and sub-30-minute micropile mobilisation [PAGE 8] inside short possessions. Recursive failure occurs when the engineer assumes the asset manager will accept a long possession, the asset manager assumes operations will grant it, and operations assumes the track will remain maintainable at the enhanced speed. The paper’s own answer is a data-driven route schematic that lets the asset manager “prioritise and plan timely and effective renewals” [PAGE 6].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof: if \(M=v/c_R\to 1\), the moving-load dynamic amplification is near-singular in the undamped idealization; no amount of route-schematic optimism removes the need for speed restriction or stiffness treatment. The source explicitly ties critical velocity to Rayleigh wave velocity [PAGE 1] and says the vehicle “starts to catch the wave up” [PAGE 4]. If sub-grade pumping is present, slurry is “self-perpetuating” and the track bed becomes “un-maintainable” [PAGE 3]; therefore a ballast-only renewal cannot veto the formation/filter defect. Mechanical/biomechanical veto proof: the vehicle-track mechanical layer is non-monotonic. Soft support causes excessive rail-level deflection and ballast mobilisation [PAGE 3]; hard support causes high modulus, ballast breakdown, and even bedrock erosion [PAGE 3-4]. The pile design must therefore respect cap depth “800mm below sleeper bottom level” and alternate-bay arching [PAGE 8], not merely achieve a cost target. Weather veto proof: heavy rainfall saturation prevents pore-pressure dissipation, and the paper says performance is not significantly affected until fines reduce permeability so pore pressures “cannot dissipate under train loading, particularly when the ballast becomes saturated during heavy rainfall” [PAGE 2]. The drainage categories are adjudicative: poor drainage means standing water within 0.5 m of sleeper base [PAGE 4]; satisfactory drainage allows surcharging during heavy rainfall; good drainage has surcharge no more than once in ten years [PAGE 4]. Cross-layer adjudication rule: cost and possession advantages of micropiles [PAGE 8, PAGE 10] are valid only after physical, mechanical, and weather vetoes are cleared. A route schematic cannot overrule a known pumping interface, a near-critical Mach number, or a drainage defect.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — critical-velocity screen veto. Counterfactual action: raise line speed to 125 mph on a soft sub-grade without a \(v/c_R\) screen. Verifiable metrics: measured ground vibration, vertical SD rate over the top 35 m, broken clips/rails, and whether a speed restriction is imposed. Prediction: if \(M\to 1\), geometry deterioration and component damage accelerate; the paper’s own case shows a speed restriction of 80 mph before piling and line speed 125 mph after piling [PAGE 9]. Veto rule: if the pre-action route evaluation lacks a stiffness/critical-velocity check and the post-action vibration or SD rate exceeds the pre-agreed threshold, the action is vetoed and the cheaper “reballast only” reward is invalid.

Scenario 2 — sub-grade erosion / reballast veto. Counterfactual action: reballast a site with GPR formation-interface evidence and HSTRC wet spots but no blanket/filter renewal. Verifiable metrics: recurrence of wet spots, vertical SD before/after tamping, pumping slurry at the formation interface, and time to temporary speed restriction. Prediction: the paper states that where serious sub-grade erosion exists, the problem cannot be cured entirely without replacing or modifying the blanket [PAGE 3], and in severe cases the track bed can fail “within 6 months” [PAGE 3]. Veto rule: if wet spots recur and SD is not durably reduced after reballasting, the reballast action is vetoed; the correct action is formation/filter/drainage treatment or micropile/stiffness treatment as appropriate.

Scenario 3 — drainage/weather veto. Counterfactual action: accept a route schematic that predicts maintainability while the site has standing water within 0.5 m of sleeper base. Verifiable metrics: water level relative to sleeper base, surcharge frequency per decade, BFI/GPR drainage indicators, and wet-spot recurrence after heavy rainfall. Prediction: the site falls into “Poor Drainage” by the paper’s definition [PAGE 4], and the probability of at least one surcharge in ten years exceeds the good-drainage bound \(\lambda\le0.1\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}\). Veto rule: if the drainage layer is not renewed or the track-bed treatment is not made more robust, the “maintainable at enhanced speed” claim is vetoed. These three RLVR vetoes are externally verifiable because they use independent physical measurements—vibration, SD rate, wet spots, water level, and time-to-restriction—rather than the same desk-study score that proposed the action.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
