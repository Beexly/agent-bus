2. Fixed-effect inverse-variance pooling:
\[
\hat\theta = \frac{\sum_i w_i \hat\theta_i}{\sum_i w_i},\quad w_i = \frac{1}{v_i},\quad \mathrm{Var}(\hat\theta)=\frac{1}{\sum_i w_i}
\]

The source is a 2026 systematic review and frequentist network meta-analysis by Song et al. The multi-hop derivation begins with the search flow: “A total of 903 records were included,” 391 titles/abstracts screened, 21 full texts evaluated, and “Nine studies met the inclusion criteria” [PAGE 3]. Those nine RCTs involved “324 participants” and six intervention nodes: nonexercising control, land-based exercise, aquatic cognitive-motor exercise, aquatic aerobic exercise, aquatic HIIT, and aquatic strength training [PAGE 1; PAGE 4]. The analytic chain is: RCT-level pre-post cognitive change → standardized mean difference (SMD) → direct and indirect evidence synthesized in a frequentist NMA → rank probabilities summarized as SUCRA → clinical inference that aquatic cognitive-motor exercise is “most promising” [PAGE 1; PAGE 2; PAGE 6].

The first gray-zone audit is that SUCRA is a rank probability, not an absolute effect. The paper itself warns: “Although SUCRA rankings indicate relative probability of being the best intervention, they do not quantify absolute clinical effect” [PAGE 5]. The SUCRA ordering is aquatic cognitive-motor exercise 85.36%, aquatic aerobic exercise 61.44%, aquatic strength training 51.80%, aquatic HIIT 49.12%, land-based exercise 48.34%, and nonexercising control 3.94% [PAGE 1; PAGE 6]. But the control-referenced SMDs are not ordered identically: aquatic aerobic exercise has SMD = 0.51, 95% CI (-0.08, 1.11), whereas aquatic cognitive-motor exercise has SMD = 0.48, 95% CI (0.02, 0.95); “only Aquatic cognitive-motor exercise (C) demonstrated a statistically significant difference, as its confidence interval did not include zero” [PAGE 6]. Thus the highest SUCRA is not the highest point estimate versus control. The inference depends on the whole network and the ranking model, not on a single pairwise contrast.

The second gray zone is the fixed-effect model. The paper states: “A fixed-effect model was employed in the meta-analysis comparing the effects of exercise interventions versus the non-exercising control on cognition in older adults” [PAGE 6]. Yet it also reports “an I² statistic of 47.9% (95% CI: 0.0% - 75.8%), a Q statistic of 15.36 (df = 8), with a corresponding p-value of 0.0525” [PAGE 4]. A fixed-effect analysis alongside moderate heterogeneity and a very wide I² interval is a tension: the model assumes a common true effect, while the heterogeneity estimate suggests possible between-study variation. The paper calls this “moderate heterogeneity” but notes the interval ranges “from no heterogeneity (0%) to substantial heterogeneity (75.8%)” [PAGE 4]. This is not a fatal flaw, but it weakens precision claims.

The third gray zone is the confidence rating. The paper says: “Confidence in the evidence for all comparisons in this network meta-analysis was rated as High using the CINeMA framework” [PAGE 6]. That sits awkwardly with the paper’s own limitations: “only nine studies included and relatively small sample sizes,” “most individual effect estimates were imprecise, as reflected by wide confidence intervals,” and “the funnel plot's ability to detect publication bias is inherently constrained in a small, sparse network” [PAGE 10]. CINeMA includes imprecision and incoherence domains; a blanket “High” rating is difficult to reconcile with wide intervals and a sparse network. The more defensible reading is that the ranking is hypothesis-generating, not definitive.

The fourth gray zone is construct validity. The primary outcome pooled “global cognition measured by validated cognitive assessments,” including MMSE, MMSE-K, MoCA T-score, DSST, HDS-R, SDMT, Raven’s Progressive Matrices, and Stroop color-word test [PAGE 2]. The authors admit: “the aggregation of diverse cognitive outcomes into a single composite effect size using (SMD) introduces conceptual heterogeneity” [PAGE 10]. This is critical because global screening tools and domain-specific tests measure overlapping but non-identical constructs. A high SUCRA for aquatic cognitive-motor exercise could reflect executive/attention gains, global screening gains, or pooling artifact.

The fifth gray zone is the network’s sparsity. There are six nodes and “seven direct pairwise comparisons” [PAGE 4]. A complete six-node network would have fifteen unordered pairs; seven edges is sparse. The node-splitting results report no significant direct-indirect disagreement: “all p-values > 0.05” [PAGE 5]. But failure to detect incoherence is not proof of consistency; sparse networks have low power to detect it. The paper acknowledges “the limited size and density of the evidence network” [PAGE 1]. Therefore the correct adjudication is: the aquatic cognitive-motor signal is plausible but fragile; the ranking should not be treated as a stable clinical hierarchy.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
No invented ratios are used below; the equations are standard meta-analytic operators. The paper selected SMD because “all outcome measures in this study were continuous variables” and “the SMD with 95% CIs was selected as the effect size metric” [PAGE 2].

1. Study-level standardized mean difference. For study \(i\), with intervention mean change \(\bar D_{i,T}\), control mean change \(\bar D_{i,C}\), and pooled standard deviation \(s_{i,p}\):
\[
d_i=\frac{\bar D_{i,T}-\bar D_{i,C}}{s_{i,p}},\qquad
s_{i,p}=\sqrt{\frac{(n_{i,T}-1)s_{i,T}^{2}+(n_{i,C}-1)s_{i,C}^{2}}{n_{i,T}+n_{i,C}-2}} .
\]
Units: \(\bar D\) is in cognitive-test score points; \(s_{i,p}\) is in score points; \(d_i\) is dimensionless. Assumption: higher scores are oriented as better cognition, and the pooled SD is positive and comparable across arms. Exact breakdown: \(d_i\) is undefined if \(s_{i,p}=0\); sign is reversed if test direction is not harmonized; construct validity breaks if different cognitive instruments are pooled without a common latent metric. The paper’s own caveat is that pooling “global screening tools” and “domain-specific measures” assesses “overlapping, but distinct cognitive constructs” [PAGE 10].

2. Fixed-effect inverse-variance pooling. The paper used “A fixed-effect model” [PAGE 6]. For estimates \(\hat\theta_i\) with variances \(v_i\):
\[
\hat\theta=\frac{\sum_i w_i\hat\theta_i}{\sum_i w_i},\qquad
w_i=\frac{1}{v_i},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\frac{1}{\sum_i w_i}.
\]
Units: \(\hat\theta\) is dimensionless SMD; \(v_i\) is dimensionless squared SMD; \(w_i\) is inverse variance and dimensionless. Assumption: one common true effect, \(\theta_i=\theta\), and independent study estimates. Exact breakdown: if \(v_i\to 0\), weights diverge; if between-study variance \(\tau^2>0\), the fixed-effect variance is too small. The reported \(I^2=47.9\%\) [PAGE 4] is a warning against treating the fixed-effect precision as definitive.

3. Heterogeneity operator. With \(Q=\sum_i w_i(\hat\theta_i-\hat\theta)^2\) and \(df=k-1\):
\[
I^2=\max\left(0,\frac{Q-df}{Q}\right)\times 100\%.
\]
Units: \(Q\) and \(df\) are dimensionless; \(I^2\) is percent. Assumption: \(Q\) is interpreted under homogeneity, and \(k\) is large enough for stable estimation. Exact breakdown: with few studies, \(Q\) has low power and the \(I^2\) confidence interval is wide; the paper reports \(47.9\%\) with a 95% CI from \(0.0\%\) to \(75.8\%\) and \(Q=15.36\), \(df=8\), \(p=0.0525\) [PAGE 4]. This interval spans “no heterogeneity” to “substantial heterogeneity,” so the heterogeneity state is not sharply identified.

4. Node-splitting inconsistency operator. For comparison \(AB\), let \(\hat\theta^{dir}_{AB}\) be the direct estimate and \(\hat\theta^{ind}_{AB}\) the indirect estimate. The inconsistency factor is:
\[
\omega_{AB}=\hat\theta^{dir}_{AB}-\hat\theta^{ind}_{AB},\qquad
z_{AB}=\frac{\omega_{AB}}{s_{\omega_{AB}}}.
\]
Units: \(\omega_{AB}\) is in SMD units; \(s_{\omega}\) is the standard error in SMD units; \(z\) is dimensionless. Assumption: direct and indirect evidence estimate the same transitivity-preserving contrast. Exact breakdown: sparse loops give low power; “all p-values > 0.05” [PAGE 5] does not prove consistency. The paper reports direct estimates from \(-1.77\) to \(1.72\), indirect estimates from \(-1.34\) to \(1.89\), and network estimates from \(-1.26\) to \(1.76\) [PAGE 5], which shows wide uncertainty even where incoherence is not statistically detected.

5. SUCRA rank operator. For treatment \(k\) among \(K\) treatments, with rank probabilities \(P_{k,r}\) for rank \(r=1,\ldots,K\):
\[
\mathrm{SUCRA}_k=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{r=1}^{K-1}\mathrm{cum}_{k,r},\qquad
\mathrm{cum}_{k,r}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}P_{k,j}.
\]
Units: \(P_{k,r}\) and \(\mathrm{SUCRA}_k\) are probabilities in \([0,1]\), often expressed as percent. Assumption: the rank probabilities are valid posterior/frequentist summaries of the fitted network model. Exact breakdown: SUCRA is not an absolute effect; it can be unstable in a sparse network, and a high SUCRA does not require a statistically significant pairwise difference. The paper reports aquatic cognitive-motor SUCRA \(=85.36\%\) and aquatic aerobic SUCRA \(=61.44\%\) [PAGE 6], but also a non-significant aerobic versus control SMD of \(0.51\) with 95% CI \((-0.08,1.11)\) [PAGE 6].

6. Dose definition. The paper reports “session duration (20 - 60 minutes/session, predominantly 45 minutes), frequency (1 - 3 sessions/week, mostly 2 - 3 sessions), and intervention length (6 - 12 weeks, with 12 weeks as the most common duration)” [PAGE 4]. A minimal dose descriptor is:
\[
D=f\cdot t\cdot W,
\]
where \(f\) is sessions/week, \(t\) is minutes/session, \(W\) is weeks, and \(D\) is minutes. Assumption: the descriptor is only a volume proxy. Exact breakdown: \(D\) ignores intensity, water temperature, immersion depth, cognitive-task difficulty, and adherence; therefore equal \(D\) does not imply equal biological dose.

7. CINeMA certainty operator. CINeMA evaluates “six domains (within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence)” [PAGE 2]. It is a structured judgment, not a scalar equation. Exact breakdown: a “High” rating [PAGE 6] cannot be mechanically inferred from the absence of high-risk trials; the paper also reports imprecision and a sparse network [PAGE 10].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs through three scientific layers. First is demographic gerontology: “The United Nations' World Population Prospects 2022 predicts that the proportion of the elderly population will rise to 16% by 2050” [PAGE 1]. Second is exercise-neurobiology: physical exercise is said to mitigate cognitive decline through “enhanced neurogenesis, increased cerebral blood flow, reduced inflammation, and synaptic plasticity” [PAGE 1]. Third is aquatic physiology: “Immersion at chest level improves blood circulation, venous return, and cerebral blood flow,” while “buoyancy reduces joint impact, prolongs balance reaction time, and alleviates fall-related anxiety” [PAGE 1]. The later claim that “cognitive engagement, rather than exercise intensity alone, may be crucial” [PAGE 1] is a retrocausal interpretation: it projects a 2026 NMA ranking back onto mechanistic traditions that were not designed to isolate cognitive engagement.

The methodological genealogy is equally important. The authors use PRISMA-NMA and graph-theoretic NMA: interventions are nodes, direct trials are edges, and the network must satisfy connectivity [PAGE 2; PAGE 3]. They add node-splitting for incoherence, SUCRA for ranking, and CINeMA for certainty [PAGE 2; PAGE 5]. This lineage is legitimate, but it carries a known hazard: ranking methods can overstate confidence when the evidence network is sparse. The paper itself says “no head-to-head randomized controlled trials have compared their relative effectiveness” [PAGE 2], so the ranking is largely an indirect construction.

The multi-week trap is visible in the intervention windows. Durations in the included trials include 8 weeks for Kwok et al., 12 weeks for Ploydang et al. and Kooncumchoo et al., 16 weeks for Kang et al., 12 weeks for Jang et al., 10 weeks for Sato et al., 8 weeks for Dunlap et al., and 9 weeks for Carral et al. [PAGE 6; PAGE 7]. The summary states “intervention length (6 - 12 weeks, with 12 weeks as the most common duration)” [PAGE 4]. These windows are long enough for practice effects, differential adherence, seasonal changes, and regression to the mean, but short relative to cognitive aging. The paper concedes: “the absence of long-term follow-up data precluded assessment of the sustainability of cognitive benefits” [PAGE 10]. A retrocausal audit therefore cannot treat the post-intervention ranking as a durable life-course effect. It is a short-window signal.

A second multi-week trap is differential exposure to cognitive testing. Repeated MMSE, MoCA, DSST, SDMT, Raven’s, and Stroop testing can produce learning effects, especially in cognitively intact volunteers. Because the paper pooled “pre- to post-intervention difference” across instruments [PAGE 2], the estimate mixes true change, test-retest learning, and instrument-specific scaling. The authors acknowledge “conceptual heterogeneity” from aggregating distinct constructs [PAGE 10]. This is a retrocausal risk: the conclusion “aquatic cognitive-motor exercise may be the most effective” [PAGE 1] could become a historical artifact of short trials and repeated testing rather than a stable neurobiological hierarchy.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when older adults face a perceived threat—cognitive decline, fall risk, or loss of independence—and default to familiar avoidance patterns: less movement, less water exposure, less dual-tasking. The paper’s own framing supports this: aquatic exercise is proposed for older adults with “mobility limitations, osteoarticular diseases, or fear of falling” [PAGE 10], and buoyancy is said to alleviate “fall-related anxiety” [PAGE 1]. In a crisis, the habitual response may be land-based caution or non-exercise, not an aquatic cognitive-motor program. The recursive belief is: “If I am frail, water is risky; if dual-tasking is hard, I should avoid it.” That belief can suppress uptake and adherence even when the intervention is physiologically plausible.

The counter-trap is aquatic cognitive-motor exercise itself. It tries to interrupt the avoidance loop by making dual-tasking safer: “buoyancy and resistance, specifically augment cognitive-motor exercise by reducing joint stress and enabling safer dual-tasking compared to land-based equivalents” [PAGE 9]. The cognitive component then adds engagement: the paper concludes that “cognitive engagement, rather than exercise intensity alone, may be crucial” [PAGE 1]. But the counter-trap has a dose-response failure mode. If the cognitive task is too difficult, it becomes a stressor and triggers regression; if too easy, it ceases to be cognitive-motor training and becomes ordinary aquatic aerobic exercise. The paper does not report task difficulty, error rates, or progression rules, so the counter-trap cannot be verified at the participant level.

The clock is the trial duration and the post-trial horizon. The interventions run “6 - 12 weeks, with 12 weeks as the most common duration” [PAGE 4], and “the absence of long-term follow-up data precluded assessment of the sustainability of cognitive benefits” [PAGE 10]. On the aging clock, twelve weeks is brief. A participant may improve during the supervised window, then return to habitual inactivity after the pool program ends. The recursive theory-of-mind risk is that clinicians and participants over-learn the short-term signal: “I improved in water,” which then collapses when environmental support disappears. The paper’s recommendation for “adequately powered RCTs with long-term follow-up” [PAGE 10] is therefore not optional; it is required to distinguish a durable counter-trap from a transient supervised effect.

A further recursive layer is expectancy. The paper states: “In most studies, researchers were involved in the intervention delivery and outcome assessments, and the participants were not blinded to group allocation” [PAGE 3]. When participants know they are in an aquatic cognitive-motor arm, they may try harder, attend more, or report better subjective function. When assessors are not blinded, they may unintentionally favor the experimental group. The paper tried to manage risk of bias with RoB 2, reporting “none of the included studies were judged to be high risk of bias” [PAGE 3], but “some concerns” remained in six studies [PAGE 3]. The recursive belief loop—participant expectancy → adherence → outcome → clinician confidence → more referral—cannot be separated from the treatment effect in this evidence base. The clock also runs on the clinician: a positive 12-week ranking may become a habit of referral before long-term safety and durability are known.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer can veto the inference because aquatic immersion is not a neutral vehicle. The paper states: “aquatic immersion, due to hydrostatic pressure, redistributes blood toward the upper body and increases cerebral blood flow” [PAGE 9]. That is a plausible mechanism, but it also implies cardiovascular and thermoregulatory load. The paper does not report water temperature, immersion depth beyond general “chest level” [PAGE 1], session temperature control, or adverse events. A participant with cardiovascular instability, uncontrolled hypertension, open wounds, incontinence, infection, or vestibular sensitivity may be vetoed at the physical layer before any cognitive benefit can be tested. The paper’s phrase “safe and effective alternative” [PAGE 10] is therefore an inferential extension, not a measured safety result.

The biomechanical layer can veto because buoyancy changes both support and challenge. On one side, “buoyancy reduces joint impact” [PAGE 1] and “reducing joint stress and enabling safer dual-tasking” [PAGE 9]. On the other side, water alters postural control, gait mechanics, and balance reactions; the same buoyancy that reduces impact can reduce proprioceptive loading and change the dual-task demand. If a participant cannot maintain airway safety, cannot recover from perturbation, or cannot stabilize against water movement, the cognitive-motor task is not deliverable. The paper includes older adults “aged 60 - 90 years” [PAGE 4] and some with mild cognitive impairment or type II diabetes [PAGE 4], but it does not provide a biomechanical eligibility screen or adverse-event table. That omission is a veto against broad “safe for all older adults” claims.

The weather/environment layer can veto external validity. Pool access depends on transport, pool closure, water temperature, staffing, and seasonal conditions. None of these are reported as moderators. A 12-week aquatic program can fail not because the exercise is ineffective but because the participant cannot reach the pool. The paper’s funnel-plot caveat—publication bias detection is “inherently constrained in a small, sparse network” [PAGE 10]—is separate from, but compounded by, environmental selection. Participants who complete aquatic trials may be precisely those with reliable pool access, transport, and caregiver support. The resulting effect estimate is transportable only to that privileged access layer.

The cognitive layer can veto because severe impairment may make dual-tasking infeasible. The paper did not perform subgroup analyses for MCI: “the absence of long-term follow-up data precluded assessment of the sustainability of cognitive benefits, as well as subgroup analyses for specific populations such as those with MCI” [PAGE 10]. The study includes some MCI participants [PAGE 4], but the pooled SMD cannot tell whether the cognitive-motor advantage holds in mild impairment, moderate impairment, or healthy aging. If the task cannot be understood or remembered, the counter-trap collapses.

Adjudication: physical and biomechanical plausibility support the intervention, but they do not prove safety or universal delivery. Weather/access and cognitive severity are true veto layers because they can prevent the intervention from being administered or completed. The correct cross-layer conclusion is conditional: aquatic cognitive-motor exercise may be effective for older adults who can safely access and tolerate a pool, but the paper does not establish that condition. The “all aquatic exercise modalities demonstrated cognitive benefits at least comparable to those of land‑based exercise” claim [PAGE 10] is an efficacy comparison, not a safety or implementation guarantee.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR-style veto checks require externally verifiable outcomes, not narrative preference. The three scenarios below specify what would falsify or sharply downgrade the paper’s central ranking claim.

1. Random-effects and certainty re-analysis veto. Counterfactual: re-run the NMA with a random-effects model, Knapp-Hartung adjustment, and a formal CINeMA re-rating that incorporates the reported imprecision and sparse network. The current fixed-effect result gives aquatic cognitive-motor exercise SMD = 0.48, 95% CI (0.02, 0.95), with SUCRA 85.36% [PAGE 6]. Prediction: if the random-effects 95% interval for aquatic cognitive-motor versus control includes zero, or if its SUCRA falls below 70%, the claim that it is “the most promising” intervention is vetoed as model-dependent. A second verifiable marker: if aquatic cognitive-motor versus aquatic aerobic has a 95% interval that crosses zero after random-effects adjustment, the pairwise superiority claim is vetoed even if the SUCRA ordering remains numerically unchanged. This is verifiable by re-analysis of the published contrasts and variance structure.

2. Blinded active-comparator durability veto. Counterfactual: an adequately powered RCT with blinded outcome assessors, concealed allocation, aquatic cognitive-motor versus aquatic aerobic exercise, and 12-month post-intervention follow-up. The paper admits “researchers were involved in the intervention delivery and outcome assessments, and the participants were not blinded to group allocation” [PAGE 3], and it lacks long-term follow-up [PAGE 10]. Prediction: if the between-group SMD for aquatic cognitive-motor versus aquatic aerobic is below 0.20 at post-intervention, or if the cognitive-motor advantage decays by more than 50% at 12 months, the superiority and durability claims are vetoed. A further verifiable prediction: if dropout due to pool access exceeds 20% in the active comparator, the “safe and effective alternative” transport claim [PAGE 10] is vetoed for routine community implementation.

3. Construct-specific and domain-specific veto. Counterfactual: split the pooled outcome into global screening instruments (MMSE, MMSE-K, MoCA, HDS-R) and domain-specific instruments (DSST, SDMT, Raven’s Progressive Matrices, Stroop), then test whether the aquatic cognitive-motor advantage is concentrated in one construct family. The paper already warns that pooling these instruments “introduces conceptual heterogeneity” [PAGE 10]. Prediction: if aquatic cognitive-motor exercise loses its significant advantage on the domain-specific composite, or if the global-screening advantage disappears after adjusting for baseline cognition and practice effects, the construct-validity claim is vetoed. A second verifiable marker: if the effect is present only on subjective or global screening tests but absent on objective processing-speed, executive-function, and fluid-reasoning tasks, the clinical inference should be downgraded from “most effective for cognition” to “possibly effective on global screening performance.”

These three vetoes are independent: model-form veto, design/durability veto, and construct-validity veto. Any one would require a narrower conclusion than the paper’s headline ranking. All three together would reduce the finding to a hypothesis for future adequately powered, blinded, long-term aquatic cognitive-motor trials.
