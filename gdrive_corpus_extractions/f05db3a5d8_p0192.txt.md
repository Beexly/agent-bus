<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual architecture of "sequence plays" descends directly from military drill reforms of the late 19th century, particularly the German *Truppendiener* manuals that influenced American college football via coaches like Walter Camp and Amos Alonzo Stagg. The shift from improvised "mob" formations to rigid, signaled lines of battle necessitated methods to maintain cohesion without line-of-sight communication. 

Yost’s 1905 articulation is a retrocausal echo of the Prussian *Kriegsspiel* (war game) logic, where commanders had to issue standing orders (sequences) because battlefield smoke (fog of war/auditory noise) would inevitably sever command links. The specific limitation to "four or five plays" reflects the working memory constraints identified later by Miller's Law (7 ± 2 items), though Yost arrived at this empirically through the biomechanical reality of 1905 players who lacked the hyper-specialization of modern athletes.

Historically, this era represents a trap: the "Single-Wing" and "Double-Wing" dominant systems relied heavily on these short sequences. The trap was that defenses began to specialize in reading the *first* play of the sequence to deduce the entire string. By the 1920s, the complexity of defensive shifting outpaced the simplicity of 1-to-4 play strings, forcing a genealogical leap toward the "Notation Systems" of Glenn "Pop" Warner, who introduced alphanumeric combinations to exponentially increase the playbook size while maintaining the single-signal efficiency Yost pioneered. The "pernicious practice" of waiting for reiteration was eventually solved not just by better sequences, but by the invention of hand signals and, ultimately, radio earpieces—a technological escape from the Shannon limit Yost was bound by.
