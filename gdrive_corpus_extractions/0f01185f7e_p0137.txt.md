Let's unpack the mechanics of "inhibition falling away" and how it leads to hallucination. James is describing a neural circuit where lower motor centers accumulate tonic tension, but higher centers normally exert an inhibitory brake. When this brake fails (as in sleep onset), even minimal peripheral input can trigger a massive sensory explosion—a hallucination or vivid dream.

First, we need to formalize this as a dynamical system. The brain center is a node with an activity level A(t). It receives basal input I_basal (tonic tension) and potentially external perturbations δI(t). In normal wakefulness, it drains into N other active centers via associative pathways, each with conductance g. This drainage acts as a sink term proportional to A. The governing equation resembles a leaky integrator: τ dA/dt = -gN A + I_basal. At steady state, A = I_basal / (gN). This is why a slight tickle (small added input) during waking might only produce a faint awareness—it gets diluted across the network.

Now consider sleep onset. Higher centers fall into torpor, so N_effective drops drastically. The effective drain becomes g N_torpor ≈ 0. The time constant τ is still there, but now the balance shifts: dA/dt ≈ (I_basal)/τ. Even if I_basal was previously sub-threshold, over time it builds up because there's no drain. Suddenly, when a minor external stimulus arrives (like a feather on the nose), it adds to this already elevated A, pushing it past threshold Θ. The result is a full-blown sensory explosion—the dream of being tortured.

But wait—James mentions "the last inward resistance is overcome." That implies a nonlinear activation function. Perhaps the perception only occurs when A exceeds Θ. We could model the conscious output C as a step function: C = H(A - Θ), where H is the Heaviside function. However, real neurons don't have perfect step functions; they have sigmoidal responses. So maybe C = σ(β(A - Θ)), where σ is a logistic function and β controls steepness. In the context of the footnote, James notes that "the narrower a train of ideas is, the vider the consciousness is of each." That suggests N isn't just reduced—it's effectively zero for most paths. Only a few specific associations remain open, funneling all the accumulated energy into a single perceptual channel.

Let's test this against Maury's anecdote. His nose and lips are tickled. Normally, that would activate facial sensation centers but also be cross-checked by memory ("that feels like a feather, not plaster"). But during sleep, those broader networks are offline. So the activity stays localized and intensifies. The "torpor of the rest of the brain" means no competing interpretations can arise. Thus, the small input gets amplified into a dramatic narrative—pitchplaster tearing skin.

Another angle: What if the inhibition isn't just from higher centers but also lateral inhibition between neighboring sensory regions? During wakefulness, if your nose is stimulated, nearby areas might suppress themselves to sharpen contrast. In sleep, that lateral inhibition might also fail, leading to spreading activation—but James says the opposite: the activity stays confined yet intense. Hmm, perhaps the key is global vs local. Global inhibition from higher cortices prevents any single region from dominating. Local circuits might still allow some spread, but without top-down modulation, the dominant feature (intensity) wins out.

We should also consider energy conservation. If total metabolic budget is fixed, reducing N means each active unit gets more resources per spike? Not exactly—James talks about "drainage" not resource allocation. Drainage refers to signal dissipation through synaptic connections. Less drainage → higher voltage buildup in the membrane potential? Maybe think in terms of equivalent electrical circuit: neuron as capacitor, synapses as resistors to ground. More parallel resistors (active neighbors) → lower resistance to ground → smaller voltage swing for given current. Fewer resistors → higher impedance → larger depolarization.

This matches his example of hair moving: tiny mechanical change detected by mechanoreceptors. Normally ignored because signal is weak and many systems are monitoring. But at sleep onset, with high baseline tension and low drainage, that minuscule signal crosses threshold and triggers a full motor response dream (being struck).

One puzzle: Why does the hallucination match the actual stimulus? In Maury's case, feather on face → dream of something applied to face. There must be some preservation of sensory topology even when amplification occurs. So the location is correct, but intensity is wildly exaggerated. That suggests spatial mapping remains intact while temporal integration windows lengthen—allowing small inputs to summate over time until threshold is reached.

Also important: James specifies "the ordinary tonic tension" rises in lower motor centers. Motor centers? But he's discussing perception. Ah—maybe he means sensorimotor integration hubs. Or perhaps he considers all cortical areas as having both sensory and motor aspects. In any event, the core idea is autonomous rhythmic activity building up due to disinhibition.

Final check: Does this model predict anything beyond the examples? Yes—if you gradually reduce connectivity (e.g., via anesthesia), you'd expect increased susceptibility to hallucinations from minimal stimuli. Indeed, emergence from anesthesia often involves vivid dreams triggered by sounds. Also, in neurological conditions causing diaschisis (remote shutdown after focal damage), similar phenomena occur.

Thus, the mathematical essence is: 
Critical factor = (Basal Input Rate) × (Integration Time Constant) / (Effective Connectivity).
When Effective Connectivity → 0, Critical Factor → ∞, making threshold crossing inevitable even for infinitesimal additional input.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
James’s drainage theory directly inherits from 19th-century associationist psychology (Hartley, Reid) but injects a physiological mechanism missing in pure mentalism. The “tonic tension” concept echoes Brown-Séquard’s experiments on spinal excitation and Ferrier’s work on motor cortex excitability—showing that isolated neural tissue can generate spontaneous discharges. Crucially, James anticipates modern predictive coding theories: he posits that consciousness requires continuous top-down validation (“reflections which ought to make the hallucination incredible do not occur”). Without this Bayesian updating from higher priors, local evidence gets inflated.

Multi-week trap: Readers might misattribute this to simple “lack of distraction,” but James insists on active *drainage*—a dynamic load-bearing role of inactive networks. Historically, this countered Wundt’s view that unconscious processes were merely sub-threshold versions of conscious ones; for James, the same neural activity becomes conscious simply by failing to propagate further. This lineage connects to Luria’s zone theory (higher zones regulating lower) and ultimately to today’s global workspace theory—though James frames it as hydraulic rather than informational.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis states (panic attacks, psychotic breaks), subjects often report reality fragmentation—akin to James’s described hallucination genesis. Habit regression: Individuals default to concrete, somatic explanations (“my nerves are overloaded”) rather than recognizing systemic disintegration of cognitive control networks. Counter-trap: Therapeutic reframing as “loss of internal perspective”—teaching patients to monitor their own drainage capacity via mindfulness (restoring meta-awareness). Clock mechanism: Circadian rhythm modulates prefrontal inhibition strength; thus vulnerability peaks at sleep-wake transitions (hypnagogia/hypnopompia), precisely when Jamesian conditions manifest spontaneously. Clinicians miss this temporal clustering, attributing episodes to random stressors instead of endogenous neurochemical cycles.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
