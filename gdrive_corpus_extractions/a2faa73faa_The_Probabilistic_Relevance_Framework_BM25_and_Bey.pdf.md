The source is a formal information-retrieval monograph, not a sports or wagering document. The central derivation chain is: binary relevance variable, Probability Ranking Principle, Bayesian inversion, conditional term independence, restriction to query terms, logarithmic rank-preserving transformation, zero-removal arithmetic, term-weight functions \(U_i\) and \(W_i\), binary independence model, Robertson/Sprck Jones weight, IDF approximation, eliteness and 2-Poisson saturation, BM25 length normalization, BM25F stream aggregation, non-textual feature addition, and parameter optimization. The first multi-hop step is that the system does not observe relevance; it observes document/query properties. The source states the PRP as: “If retrieved documents are ordered by decreasing probability of relevance on the data available, then the system’s effectiveness is the best that can be obtained for the data” [PAGE 8 of 59]. The next step is Bayesian inversion of the posterior probability of relevance into document-generation probabilities conditioned on relevance and non-relevance, followed by dropping query-only factors that are document-independent [PAGE 10 of 59]. This dropping is rank-safe but not calibration-safe; the authors later warn that “some of the transformations involved dropping components which would not affect the ranking, but would be required for a good probability estimate” [PAGE 16 of 59]. That is the first gray-zone trap: a BM25 score is a ranking statistic, not a calibrated posterior probability.

The second hop is the independence assumption. The source explicitly says: “In Step (2.4) (term independence assumption), we expand each probability as a product over the terms of the vocabulary” [PAGE 11 of 59], and immediately concedes: “This is clearly a much more arguable step: in general terms will not be statistically independent in this fashion” [PAGE 12 of 59]. The model uses conditional independence given relevance, not unconditional independence. A weaker linked-dependence justification is also cited [PAGE 12 of 59]. The third hop is restriction to query terms: “we can make no such assumption about non-query terms” [PAGE 13 of 59], so the model sets the non-query probability ratio to one by a neutral prior. This is reasonable for query terms but becomes fragile under query expansion because added terms may be synonyms or correlated with original terms; the source notes that “this query expansion may worsen the term independence assumption” [PAGE 21 of 59]. The fourth hop is the zero-removal transformation: \(W_i(x)=U_i(x)-U_i(0)\). This makes absent query terms contribute zero and enables inverted-index scoring, but again it is a rank-equivalence operation, not a probabilistic reconstruction. The source calls the resulting form “simple linear models” whose evidence combination “is just by summation” [PAGE 15 of 59].

The fifth hop is BM25 itself. The binary independence model yields the RSJ weight with a pseudo-count of \(0.5\) to avoid infinities [PAGE 18 of 59]. With no relevance judgments, setting \(R=r_i=0\) reduces RSJ to an IDF-like weight [PAGE 19 of 59]. The eliteness model then postulates a hidden binary property: if a term is elite, the document is “about” that term; observed term frequency depends on eliteness, and relevance depends on eliteness, with \(tf\) conditionally independent of relevance given eliteness [PAGE 22 of 59]. Harter’s 2-Poisson mixture gives a saturating term weight: “any one term’s contribution to the document score cannot exceed a saturation point (the asymptotic limit), however, frequently it occurs in the document” [PAGE 26 of 59]. The asymptotic limit equals the binary-independence weight, except in the extreme case \(p_{i1}=1\), \(p_{i0}=0\), where the limit is infinite and the weight becomes linear in \(tf\) [PAGE 26 of 59]. BM25 replaces the messy 2-Poisson shape with the parametric saturation \(tf/(k+tf)\) and adds soft document-length normalization \(B=(1-b)+b\,dl/avdl\) [PAGE 27 of 59, PAGE 29 of 59]. The final form is “the classic BM25 term-weighting and document-scoring function” [PAGE 30 of 59].

The gray-zone audit has several exact breakdown regimes. First, probability ratios become singular if a conditioned probability is zero or one; RSJ pseudo-counts mitigate but do not make the model a full generative probability model. Second, term independence is false in natural language; BM25’s robustness is empirical, not a proof of independence. Third, blind feedback assumes top-ranked documents are relevant; the source says it “tends to fail on some queries, particularly on queries that are difficult to start with, where the top-ranked documents from the initial search may be poor” [PAGE 22 of 59]. Fourth, BM25F’s stream aggregation requires stream weights and stream-specific normalization; the simple version can be interpreted as replicating streams when weights are integers [PAGE 35 of 59], but the source says “The proper definition of IDF in this context requires further research” [PAGE 34 of 59]. Fifth, non-textual features are admitted under the assumption that they are “correlated with relevance for all queries” [PAGE 36 of 59]; a feature without that property should be vetoed or down-weighted. Sixth, positional information is hard to model formally and has shown small average gains; the source says none of the attempts “would qualify as a natural extension of the PRF” [PAGE 38 of 59]. Seventh, parameter optimization is not smooth: standard IR measures “have local maxima and plateaus, they are not smooth and they don’t have gradients” [PAGE 48 of 59]. These are the audit boundaries: BM25 is a rank-equivalent, conditional-independence, saturating, length-normalized scoring approximation, not a universal relevance probability.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(\mathrm{Rel}\in\{rel,\overline{rel}\}\) be the hidden binary relevance variable for a document \(d\) and query \(q\). The PRP ranks by \(\Pr(\mathrm{Rel}=rel\mid d,q)\). The source’s derivation begins with the probability ratio
\[
\frac{\Pr(rel\mid d,q)}{\Pr(\overline{rel}\mid d,q)}
=
\frac{\Pr(d\mid rel,q)\Pr(rel\mid q)}
{\Pr(d\mid \overline{rel},q)\Pr(\overline{rel}\mid q)}
\;\propto_q\;
\frac{\Pr(d\mid rel,q)}{\Pr(d\mid \overline{rel},q)},
\]
where \(\propto_q\) denotes rank equivalence for fixed \(q\) [PAGE 10 of 59, PAGE 11 of 59]. Probabilities are dimensionless; after taking natural logarithms, weights are in nats, or in bits if \(\log_2\) is used. The term-independence approximation is
\[
\Pr(d\mid rel,q)\approx
\prod_{i\in V}\Pr(TF_i=tf_i\mid rel,q),
\]
and similarly for \(\overline{rel}\); this is conditional independence, not unconditional independence [PAGE 11 of 59]. Restricting to query terms gives
\[
\approx
\prod_{i\in q}
\frac{\Pr(TF_i=tf_i\mid rel)}
{\Pr(TF_i=tf_i\mid \overline{rel})},
\]
with the non-query ratio set to one by a neutral prior [PAGE 12 of 59, PAGE 13 of 59]. The log transform defines
\[
U_i(x)=\log\frac{\Pr(TF_i=x\mid rel)}
{\Pr(TF_i=x\mid \overline{rel})},
\qquad
W_i(x)=U_i(x)-U_i(0)
=
\log\frac{\Pr(TF_i=x\mid rel)\Pr(TF_i=0\mid \overline{rel})}
{\Pr(TF_i=x\mid \overline{rel})\Pr(TF_i=0\mid rel)}.
\]
The document score is
\[
\sum_{i\in q,\;tf_i>0} W_i(tf_i),
\]
which is rank-equivalent to the full query-term sum but zeros absent terms [PAGE 13 of 59, PAGE 14 of 59, PAGE 15 of 59]. Exact breakdown: if any conditional probability is \(0\) or \(1\), the log ratio can be infinite; \(W_i(0)=0\) by construction. The zero-removal step preserves rank but not calibration [PAGE 16 of 59].

For binary presence/absence, \(TF_i\in\{0,1\}\), and the BIM weight is
\[
w_i^{BIM}
=
\log
\frac{P(t_i\mid rel)\,(1-P(t_i\mid \overline{rel}))}
{(1-P(t_i\mid rel))\,P(t_i\mid \overline{rel})},
\]
where \(t_i\) denotes term presence [PAGE 17 of 59]. The RSJ estimator uses \(N\) judged documents, \(R\) relevant documents, \(n_i\) documents containing \(t_i\), and \(r_i\) relevant documents containing \(t_i\):
\[
w_i^{RSJ}
=
\log
\frac{(r_i+0.5)(N-R-n_i+r_i+0.5)}
{(n_i-r_i+0.5)(R-r_i+0.5)}.
\]
The \(+0.5\) pseudo-count is introduced because “the combination of a ratio of probabilities and a log function may yield (positive or negative) infinities as estimates” [PAGE 18 of 59]. With no relevance information, setting \(R=r_i=0\) gives the IDF-like form
\[
w_i^{IDF}
=
\log\frac{N-n_i+0.5}{n_i+0.5}
\]
[PAGE 19 of 59]. Units are nats under natural log; \(N,n_i,R,r_i\) are document counts.

The eliteness model introduces hidden binary \(E_i\in\{elite,\overline{elite}\}\), with
\[
p_{i1}=P(E_i=elite\mid rel),\qquad
p_{i0}=P(E_i=elite\mid \overline{rel}),
\]
\[
E_{i1}(tf)=P(TF_i=tf\mid E_i=elite),\qquad
E_{i0}(tf)=P(TF_i=tf\mid E_i=\overline{elite}).
\]
Then
\[
P(TF_i=tf\mid rel)=p_{i1}E_{i1}(tf)+(1-p_{i1})E_{i0}(tf),
\]
and similarly for \(\overline{rel}\); the 2-Poisson assumption is \(E_{ie}(tf)\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_{ie})\) [PAGE 23 of 59, PAGE 24 of 59]. The eliteness weight is Equation (3.6) of the source [PAGE 24 of 59]. Its saturation limit is
\[
\lim_{tf\to\infty} w_i^{elite}(tf)
=
\log\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{(1-p_1)p_0}
=
w_i^{BIM}.
\]
If \(p_{i1}=1\) and \(p_{i0}=0\), the limit is infinite and the weight is linear in \(tf\) [PAGE 26 of 59]. The source’s warning is exact: “any one term’s contribution to the document score cannot exceed a saturation point (the asymptotic limit), however, frequently it occurs in the document” [PAGE 26 of 59].

BM25 uses the parametric saturation \(tf/(k+tf)\) for \(k>0\) [PAGE 27 of 59], then length normalization
\[
B=(1-b)+b\frac{dl}{avdl},\qquad 0\le b\le1,
\]
\[
tf'=\frac{tf}{B},
\]
\[
w_i^{BM25}(tf)
=
\frac{tf'}{k_1+tf'}\,w_i^{RSJ}
=
\frac{tf}{k_1\big((1-b)+b\,dl/avdl\big)+tf}\,w_i^{RSJ}.
\]
The document score is the sum over query terms present in the document [PAGE 29 of 59, PAGE 30 of 59]. Here \(tf,dl,avdl\) are token counts or token-count averages; \(B,b,k_1\) are dimensionless or normalized-count quantities; \(w_i\) is in nats. Exact breakdowns: \(avdl=0\) is a degenerate collection; \(b=1\) with \(dl=0\) gives \(B=0\); \(k_1=0\) removes saturation for \(tf>0\); and the \(p_{i1}=1,p_{i0}=0\) extreme removes the finite saturation ceiling.

BM25F aggregates streams \(s=1,\dots,S\) with stream weights \(v_s\), stream lengths \(sl_s\), and stream-specific normalization \(B_s\):
\[
\tilde{tf}_i=\sum_{s=1}^S v_s\frac{tf_{si}}{B_s},
\qquad
B_s=(1-b_s)+b_s\frac{sl_s}{avsl_s},
\]
\[
w_i^{BM25F}
=
\frac{\tilde{tf}_i}{k_1+\tilde{tf}_i}\,w_i^{RSJ}.
\]
The simpler version instead uses \(\tilde{tf}_i=\sum_s v_s tf_{si}\) and \(\tilde{dl}=\sum_s v_s sl_s\) [PAGE 34 of 59]. The source notes that integer \(v_s\) can be read as replicating streams [PAGE 35 of 59], but also that “The proper definition of IDF in this context requires further research” [PAGE 34 of 59]. This is an exact breakdown regime: a verbose stream containing most terms can make collection-wide IDF degenerate.

Non-textual features \(f=(f_1,\dots,f_F)\) are added under feature independence and query-independence assumptions:
\[
P(rel\mid d,q)\;\propto_q\;
\sum_{i\in q,\;tf_i>0} W_i(tf_i)
+
\sum_{j=1}^F \lambda_j V_j(f_j),
\]
\[
V_j(f_j)=\log
\frac{\Pr(F_j=f_j\mid rel)}
{\Pr(F_j=f_j\mid \overline{rel})}.
\]
The source states that “non-textual features are assumed to be correlated with relevance for all queries” [PAGE 36 of 59], and gives logarithmic, rational, and sigmoid \(V_j\) forms [PAGE 36 of 59, PAGE 37 of 59]. If a feature is not correlated with relevance, the theoretically consistent move is \(\lambda_j\to0\), not an arbitrary veto. Parameter optimization maximizes a measure \(M(\theta)\), with \(\theta=(k_1,b)\) for BM25 and more components for BM25F. The source warns that IR measures have local maxima and plateaus and are non-smooth with no gradients [PAGE 48 of 59]; the factorized BM25F schedule “may be repeated until no further increase in performance is obtained” [PAGE 52 of 59].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Maron and Kuhns’ 1960 probabilistic indexing model, through the Robertson and Sprck Jones relevance-weighting work of the 1970s, the unified probabilistic model of the early 1980s, Harter’s 1975 2-Poisson keyword-indexing model, the 1994 BM25 approximation to the 2-Poisson model, BM25F for multiple weighted fields in 2004, and later non-textual-feature and optimization work [PAGE 41 of 59, PAGE 55 of 59, PAGE 57 of 59, PAGE 58 of 59]. The retrocausal lesson is that today’s BM25 is a compression of several historical compromises: binary relevance, rank-equivalence, conditional independence, neutral non-query priors, fixed-length assumptions, and pseudo-count estimation. Those compromises became invisible because retrieval evaluation rewarded rank order, not calibrated probability. The source itself records the consequence: rank-preserving transformations dropped components “required for a good probability estimate” [PAGE 16 of 59]. Thus any modern system that uses BM25 as a probability, a causal effect, or a multi-week planning signal inherits a hidden historical debt.

Multi-week traps follow from that debt. Trap one: treating the IDF approximation as a stable semantic prior. The source obtains IDF by setting \(R=r_i=0\) in RSJ [PAGE 19 of 59], which is a no-feedback approximation, not a claim that rare terms are always more relevant. Trap two: repeated blind feedback. The source warns that blind feedback “tends to fail on some queries, particularly on queries that are difficult to start with” [PAGE 22 of 59]; iterating it can amplify early retrieval errors over multiple runs. Trap three: expanding queries with many terms. The source says query expansion “may worsen the term independence assumption” [PAGE 21 of 59]; over weeks, this can create a feedback loop where correlated expansion terms dominate the score. Trap four: BM25F stream tuning without rethinking IDF. The source says the proper IDF definition in the stream context “requires further research” [PAGE 34 of 59]; a multi-week experiment that tunes stream weights while leaving global IDF fixed can appear to improve in-sample ranking while degenerating on verbose streams. Trap five: optimizing \(k_1,b\) and stream weights on one collection and deploying them as universal constants. The source calls BM25 and BM25F “quite robust” but also says optimized parameters are collection-specific and may not transfer [PAGE 47 of 59]; a multi-week rollout can mistake local tuning gains for general improvement. Trap six: adding positional or proximity features as if they were natural PRF extensions. The source says the existing attempts do not qualify as a natural extension of the PRF and that gains are small [PAGE 38 of 59, PAGE 39 of 59]. The genealogical audit therefore says: preserve the rank-equivalence insights, but do not inherit the discarded calibration, independence, and stream-IDF assumptions without explicit re-derivation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive system-user loop is: the user has an information need; the query is an imperfect representation; the system estimates relevance from document/query evidence; the user judges retrieved documents; feedback updates the query or weights. The source defines a query as “a representation (not necessarily very good or very complete) of an individual user’s information need or perhaps search intent” [PAGE 6 of 59]. In true relevance feedback, the system can update RSJ weights from judged relevant documents and can add non-query terms using an offer-weight rule [PAGE 20 of 59, PAGE 21 of 59]. In blind feedback, the system substitutes an assumption for the user’s judgment: it takes the top-ranked \(y\) documents as relevant and proceeds [PAGE 21 of 59]. That is a crisis habit regression: when the user’s signal is absent, the system regresses to its own top-ranked output as if it were ground truth. The source predicts the failure mode: blind feedback “tends to fail on some queries, particularly on queries that are difficult to start with, where the top-ranked documents from the initial search may be poor” [PAGE 22 of 59]. Recursively, the user may then reformulate based on bad results, and the system may interpret the reformulation as a new need, compounding the error.

The counter-trap is query expansion. The system models the user’s need as broader than the literal query, so it adds terms from relevant or pseudo-relevant documents. But adding synonyms and related words can “worsen the term independence assumption” [PAGE 21 of 59], because those terms are correlated. The recursive trap is that the system believes it is modeling the user better while actually violating its own conditional-independence premise. The safe counter-trap is conservative expansion: add only a small number of high-offer-weight terms, and reweight rather than blindly appending [PAGE 21 of 59]. The clock dimension is parameter optimization. The system can tune \(k_1,b\), stream weights, and non-textual feature scales, but the source warns that optimization requires human evaluation, is collection-specific, and can be computationally costly [PAGE 47 of 59]. The recursive clock trap is that the system’s model of the user improves on the evaluation set at time \(t\), then becomes stale on the deployment collection at time \(t+\Delta\). The source’s recommendation is pragmatic: cache evaluations, use a grid, use robust line search and promising directions, and factor the search into batches [PAGE 48 of 59, PAGE 49 of 59, PAGE 50 of 59, PAGE 51 of 59, PAGE 52 of 59]. The theory-of-mind conclusion is: do not let the system’s self-model replace the user’s relevance signal; do not let expansion override the independence assumptions silently; and put a clock on tuned parameters so that local gains are re-verified after collection or query-distribution drift.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no biomechanical or weather layer. Its admissible layers are document text, query text, stream structure, and non-textual document features such as “the age of a file, its type or its link in-degree” [PAGE 35 of 59]. Therefore a physical, biomechanical, or weather veto is not admissible under the PRF unless it is encoded as a non-textual feature \(f_j\) in the document vector \(d=(c,f)\). Formally, if \(X\) is an external weather or biomechanical variable and \(X\notin(c,f,q)\), then under the source model
\[
\Pr(rel\mid d,q,X)=\Pr(rel\mid d,q),
\]
because the score depends only on the term-frequency vector \(c\), the feature vector \(f\), and the query \(q\). A weather veto can enter only through the non-textual term
\[
\lambda_j V_j(f_j)
=
\lambda_j
\log
\frac{\Pr(F_j=f_j\mid rel)}
{\Pr(F_j=f_j\mid \overline{rel})},
\]
and only under the source’s assumptions that non-textual features are independent with respect to relevance and “correlated with relevance for all queries” [PAGE 36 of 59]. If a weather variable is claimed to veto a retrieval result without being measured, transformed by a \(V_j\), and scaled by \(\lambda_j\), the claim is model-external and must be rejected. The same proof applies to biomechanical variables: they are not primitive in PRF; they are either features or they are noise. Physical constraints such as document length enter through \(dl\), \(avdl\), \(sl_s\), and \(B_s\) [PAGE 29 of 59, PAGE 34 of 59]; they do not authorize an independent veto outside the score. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: text-layer and feature-layer evidence may veto a ranking only through the equations; weather-layer or biomechanical-layer assertions have no standing unless they are explicitly encoded as features and validated. This is the exact veto boundary implied by the source’s feature-independence and query-independence assumptions [PAGE 35 of 59, PAGE 36 of 59].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Calibration veto against rank-equivalent BM25 probabilities. Counterfactual: a downstream adaptive-filtering system uses the BM25 score directly as \(\Pr(rel\mid d,q)\). The source says rank-preserving transformations dropped components “required for a good probability estimate” [PAGE 16 of 59] and that the model “has to be considerably modified if it is to be used in a situation which requires an explicit probability” [PAGE 16 of 59]. Prediction: raw BM25 scores will rank documents acceptably but will be poorly calibrated as probabilities; reliability diagrams and Brier scores will show systematic distortion. Verifiable veto: reject direct probability use if calibration error exceeds a pre-registered threshold; replace it with a post-hoc calibrator or a re-derived probability model that retains the dropped components.

Scenario 2: Blind-feedback veto on hard queries. Counterfactual: apply pseudo-relevance feedback to queries whose initial top-ranked documents are poor. The source states that blind feedback “tends to fail on some queries, particularly on queries that are difficult to start with, where the top-ranked documents from the initial search may be poor” [PAGE 22 of 59]. Prediction: on a paired hard-query set, expansion will reduce precision or relevance compared with no expansion more often than on easy queries. Verifiable veto: reject blind feedback for that query class if the paired improvement is not statistically significant or if the lower-tail degradation exceeds a fixed budget; use conservative term selection instead [PAGE 21 of 59].

Scenario 3: BM25F verbose-stream IDF veto. Counterfactual: compute IDF on the entire collection while ignoring streams, then apply BM25F stream weights. The source warns that this “can lead to degenerate cases (e.g., when a stream is extremely verbose and contains most terms for most documents)” and that “The proper definition of IDF in this context requires further research” [PAGE 34 of 59]. Prediction: injecting a verbose stream that contains most terms will flatten the IDF contrast and underweight discriminative short-stream matches such as title or anchor text. Verifiable veto: construct a synthetic collection with a verbose boilerplate stream and a discriminative short stream; if BM25F with global IDF fails to preserve the short-stream ranking advantage relative to stream-aware weighting, veto the global-IDF configuration and move to stream-specific IDF or an expected-IDF formulation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
