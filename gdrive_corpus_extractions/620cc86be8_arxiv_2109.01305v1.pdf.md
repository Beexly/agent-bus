\[
\phi_t \in \mathbb{R}^{2hw}.

The flow field has two channels, corresponding to horizontal and vertical displacement in pixel units. The source states that flow “is computed using RAFT [58] between \(x_t\) and \(x_{t-1}\)” and that the authors “clip to \(\pm 20\) pixels, and quantize into 8-bits” [PAGE 11]. \[

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of this paper can be reconstructed as a sequence of responses to pose fragility. The earliest relevant operator is the pose abstraction itself: represent human action by skeletal keypoints. The source situates this in “State-of-the-art skeleton-based deep learning techniques for action recognition” that “rely on accurate 2D pose detection” [PAGE 1]. This creates a dependency chain: action recognition depends on pose estimation, and pose estimation fails under blur, occlusion, and domain shift. The paper’s own formulation is a response to that chain: if the pose estimator is unreliable, do not use pose as a hard input; instead distill pose-like features from pixels when the teacher is reliable.

A second lineage is view-invariant pose embedding. The source connects VPD to “VIPE [53] and CV-MIM [72]” which “show that learned pose embeddings ... can be useful” [PAGE 2]. VPD extends this by replacing the unreliable 2D pose input with a learned pixel-to-embedding path: “VPD extends these works by using distillation to replace the unreliable 2D pose estimation step with a model that embeds directly from pixels to pose-embedding” [PAGE 2]. This is historically important because it shifts the unit of transfer from explicit skeleton coordinates to a learned descriptor that can be more robust than the teacher’s raw output.

A third lineage is end-to-end video recognition. The source lists dominant models such as TSN, TRN, GSM, and SlowFast-style approaches [PAGE 2]. These models avoid explicit pose but require more labels and can latch onto contextual or appearance shortcuts. The source explicitly says end-to-end models “tend to latch onto specific visual patterns” [PAGE 1]. VPD is positioned as a hybrid: it uses raw RGB and flow like end-to-end models, but it uses pose supervision like skeleton methods. The paper states that the strategy “retains the best of both pose and end-to-end worlds” [PAGE 1].

A fourth lineage is knowledge distillation. The source says VPD is “similar to [66] in that the main goal of distillation is to improve the robustness and accuracy of the student rather than improve model efficiency” [PAGE 3]. This matters because it rules out a common misinterpretation: VPD is not primarily a compression technique. The teacher is noisy, and the student is expected to become more domain-specialized and robust than the teacher for the target sport. The source adds that “our VPD student is fixed after distillation” [PAGE 3], which means there is no online teacher-student co-adaptation at test time.

A fifth lineage is tracking and athlete localization. The paper introduces subject tracking because sports frames contain judges, audiences, and other people. The source says, “Subject tracking is needed for VPD to ensure that the pose is of the correct person” [PAGE 8]. The implementation uses Mask R-CNN detections, heuristic selection, SORT, and OpenCV tracking [PAGE 16]. This lineage is crucial because many earlier pose systems implicitly assume a single centered person; sports video violates that assumption.

The multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. The first trap is treating the teacher as ground truth. The source warns that teacher outputs are noisy and that low-confidence frames are excluded for this reason [PAGE 4]. A team that ignores confidence calibration may spend weeks tuning the student while the real problem is corrupted supervision. The second trap is assuming that cropping solves identity. Cropping helps, but tracking can still fail; the source notes that “fast-moving athletes in challenging poses are often missed otherwise” [PAGE 4]. The third trap is distilling only on labeled action clips. The paper finds that “distilling with only the action video performs worse on our datasets” [PAGE 7]. A team that assumes only action segments matter may discard useful contextual motion. The fourth trap is evaluating only in the full-data regime. The source emphasizes that VPD’s more important advantage is label efficiency, especially when “labels are scarce” [PAGE 2]. The fifth trap is conflating explicit pose reconstruction with useful downstream features. The appendix explicitly says the distilled student “does not solve the explicit 2D pose estimation problem” [PAGE 14], even though its features can improve downstream tasks.

The historical lesson is that VPD is not a pose detector replacement in the strict sense. It is a representation-learning operator that uses pose as a weak supervisory signal. The lineage is therefore: explicit pose, pose embeddings, distillation, domain specialization, and downstream action understanding. The traps arise when practitioners collapse those distinctions and assume that a better pose estimator, a better crop, or a larger labeled set automatically solves the fine-grained sports problem.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis must model at least four agents: the teacher pose estimator, the student network, the downstream action model, and the evaluation protocol. Each has a different belief about reliability.

The teacher believes it can localize joints from RGB. The source acknowledges that this belief fails in sports: “pose estimators are often unreliable ... due to domain shift and factors such as motion blur and occlusions” [PAGE 1]. The teacher’s confidence score is its internal estimate of reliability, but confidence is not calibrated ground truth. The source uses mean estimated joint score as a filter, but it also notes that the optimal threshold depends on calibration and quality [PAGE 14]. Thus, the teacher’s belief is probabilistic and fallible.

The student believes it can infer pose-like structure from athlete crops and flow. It does not see the whole video; it sees localized RGB and flow [PAGE 4]. Its belief is constrained by the teacher only on selected frames. The source says the student learns to “fill-in the gaps” left by the noisy teacher [PAGE 2]. This is a crucial recursive move: the student is not merely copying the teacher; it is learning a smoother domain-specific mapping that can operate even when the teacher would fail. However, the student’s belief is valid only if the input crop is the athlete and the target domain is represented in the distillation corpus.

The downstream action model believes that pose-feature sequences contain enough signal for classification, retrieval, or detection. This belief is strongest in few-shot regimes, where pose features outperform end-to-end baselines [PAGE 5]. But the downstream model can be fooled by temporally misaligned features or wrong identity. The source’s use of DTW acknowledges that temporal alignment matters, and it also warns that “DTW is also sensitive to factors such as the precision of the temporal boundaries and the duration of the actions” [PAGE 12].

The evaluation protocol believes that held-out videos or few-shot subsets approximate deployment. The source controls few-shot variation by running “five randomly sampled and fixed subsets of the data, for each k” [PAGE 6]. This is a recursive safeguard: the evaluator knows that a single split could mislead. Still, the protocol cannot fully model open-world weather, new athletes, new camera rigs, or new occlusion patterns.

Crisis habit regression occurs when one of these agents falls back to a brittle default under stress. For the teacher, the crisis is fast motion, blur, occlusion, or unusual poses. Its regression habit is to emit a plausible but wrong skeleton. For the end-to-end model, the crisis is scarce labels. Its regression habit is to latch onto clothing, court, arena, or ball context [PAGE 1]. For the skeleton-only model, the crisis is unreliable pose. Its regression habit is to propagate teacher error into the classifier. For VPD, the crisis is wrong tracking or failed flow. Its regression habit would be to encode the wrong person or motion, but its training objective provides a counter-trap: it must predict pose and pose change, which discourages purely contextual shortcuts.

The counter-trap is explicit in the method. First, confidence gating prevents the student from imitating obviously bad teacher frames [PAGE 4]. Second, the temporal derivative forces the descriptor to encode motion, not just static appearance [PAGE 3]. Third, optical flow provides an explicit motion channel [PAGE 4]. Fourth, view-invariant teacher embeddings reduce camera-view ambiguity, although the source still notes that 2D pose estimates are “inherently ambiguous with respect to camera view” [PAGE 8]. Fifth, uncut-video distillation exposes the student to more frames than action-only clips, and the source finds that action-only distillation is worse [PAGE 7].

The clock is a separate recursive agent. Time enters in three ways. First, frame rate: the source targets 25 fps for consistent temporal derivatives [PAGE 11]. Second, action duration: a figure skating jump lasts about 1.6 seconds, or roughly 40 frames, while a tennis swing is defined as the 200 ms around ball contact, roughly 5 frames [PAGE 7]. Third, boundary precision: retrieval and detection depend on temporal alignment, and DTW is sensitive to boundary precision [PAGE 12]. If the clock is wrong, the pose sequence is not the same action even if the features are locally plausible.

The recursive equilibrium is therefore conditional. VPD works when the teacher’s confidence signal is useful, the athlete is correctly tracked, the flow is measurable, and the temporal sampling resolves the action. It becomes brittle when any of those conditions fail. The theory of mind is not that VPD magically knows the action; it is that VPD encodes a hierarchy of weak signals and relies on the evaluator to respect the conditions under which those signals are valid.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether a prediction from the VPD feature pathway should be suppressed because a lower physical, biomechanical, identity, or environmental layer invalidates the assumption chain. The answer is yes under explicit conditions.

First, define the identity layer. VPD requires an athlete-centered crop. The source states, “Subject tracking is needed for VPD to ensure that the pose is of the correct person” [PAGE 8]. Let \(B_t\) be the tracked bounding box and let \(B_t^\star\) be the true athlete region. Define identity validity as

\[
\mathrm{IoU}(B_t, B_t^\star) \ge \beta,
\]

where \(\beta\) is a domain-specific identity threshold. The paper does not provide a universal \(\beta\), but the operational requirement is clear: if the crop contains the wrong person, the feature \(z_t\) is not a valid athlete pose feature. The source also notes that “fast-moving athletes in challenging poses are often missed otherwise” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, if tracking confidence is low or the bounding box jumps between people, the prediction should be vetoed at the identity layer.

Second, define the teacher-confidence layer. The training loss is applied only to selected frames \(S_\tau\) where \(c_t \ge \tau\) [PAGE 4]. At inference, the student still produces a descriptor for any frame, but the descriptor’s training validity is strongest when the input resembles the selected distribution. If a test frame has teacher confidence far below the training threshold, the student is extrapolating. The source’s threshold study supports this: excluding low-confidence poses improves accuracy, but excessive exclusion harms supervision [PAGE 14]. A hard veto condition is therefore:

\[
c_t < \tau_{\text{min}}
\]

where \(\tau_{\text{min}}\) is a calibrated abstention threshold. If \(c_t\) is extremely low, the frame should not be used as reliable pose evidence.

Third, define the optical-flow layer. The student uses \(\phi_t\) from the previous frame, clipped to \(\pm 20\) pixels and quantized to 8 bits [PAGE 11]. Let \(\phi_t^{\text{true}}\) be the true displacement field. If

\[
\|\phi_t^{\text{true}}\|_\infty > 20 \text{ pixels},
\]

the measured flow saturates. Quantization then adds error, and the temporal derivative supervision \(\Delta p_t\) becomes unreliable. This is especially relevant for fast sports motion. The source identifies motion blur and fast motion as core challenges [PAGE 1]. A flow-saturation veto is therefore physically justified: if true motion exceeds the measurable range, the student’s motion channel is clipped, and the descriptor may encode incomplete motion.

Fourth, define the biomechanical temporal layer. The finite difference \(\Delta p_t = p_t - p_{t-1}\) assumes that adjacent frames capture meaningful pose change. For a jump, the source says the average action is about 1.6 seconds, or roughly 40 frames [PAGE 7]. For a tennis swing, the event is 200 ms, roughly 5 frames [PAGE 7]. If the effective sampling rate is too low, or if frames are dropped, the derivative may compare non-corresponding phases of the motion. A Nyquist-style condition can be stated informally: the sampling rate must be high enough to resolve the fastest discriminative joint motion. If the action’s discriminative phase changes faster than the sampling interval, the derivative becomes aliased. The source’s DTW warning reinforces this: alignment is sensitive to temporal boundary precision and action duration [PAGE 12].

Fifth, define the environmental layer. The source mentions tennis matches from Wimbledon and US Open [PAGE 4], implying different courts, lighting, and appearance conditions. The student is specialized to the target domain, which is beneficial when the distillation corpus covers that domain, but risky when environmental conditions are novel. The source applies color and noise jitter [PAGE 4], but augmentation does not guarantee invariance to weather, illumination, or venue shift. If the test environment lies outside the distilled corpus distribution, the student’s learned appearance-motion correlation may not hold.

The veto proof can be summarized as a chain of implications. If identity fails, then \(x_t\) is not the athlete crop; therefore \(z_t\) is not an athlete pose descriptor; therefore downstream action inference is invalid. If teacher confidence fails, then the student is extrapolating beyond its weak supervision; therefore the pose-matching constraint is weak; therefore the descriptor may encode non-pose appearance. If flow saturates, then \(\phi_t\) underestimates true motion; therefore \(\Delta p_t\) is unreliable; therefore motion-sensitive tasks degrade. If temporal sampling fails, then \(\Delta p_t\) and DTW alignment are distorted; therefore detection and retrieval boundaries become unreliable. If environment fails, then the student’s domain specialization may be invalid; therefore the descriptor may not transfer.

The adjudication rule is hierarchical. Hard vetoes apply when identity, flow saturation, or extreme temporal failure makes the feature physically meaningless. Soft vetoes apply when confidence is low, environment is novel, or action duration is near the sampling limit. In full-supervision regimes with unreliable pose, the source itself shows that an end-to-end model can outperform VPD: “On Diving48, VI-VPD trails end-to-end GSM (w/o crop) by 1.6%” [PAGE 6]. That result is not a failure of VPD; it is a cross-layer signal that when pose is too unreliable and labels are abundant, direct pixel modeling may be the safer layer. The correct adjudication is therefore not “VPD always wins,” but “VPD is valid when the physical and biomechanical preconditions are satisfied; otherwise veto or defer to another layer.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable from the source’s own ablations, definitions, and reported failure modes.

Veto Scenario 1: Low-confidence teacher collapse.
Counterfactual setup: suppose a deployment video contains many frames where the teacher’s mean joint score is below the training selection threshold. The teacher may still output a skeleton, but that output is likely to be wrong or unstable. The VPD student was trained mainly on frames satisfying \(c_t \ge \tau\), with \(\tau = 0.5\) by default and \(0.7\) for tennis [PAGE 4]. If the system forces the student to interpret frames from a lower-confidence regime, it is extrapolating beyond the weak-supervision distribution.

Verifiable prediction: downstream accuracy will be worse when low-confidence teacher frames are admitted than when they are excluded, especially on datasets with poor pose reliability. The source supports this in its threshold study: “Excluding the least confident poses improves accuracy; these poses are most likely to be incorrect” [PAGE 14]. The same source also warns against over-filtering: “setting the threshold too high also decreases accuracy if the supervision becomes too sparse” [PAGE 14]. The confidence distributions in Figure 4 show that floor exercise and diving have more uncertain poses; the caption says, “A flatter distribution with more mass to the left indicates greater uncertainty in the estimates” [PAGE 15].

Operational veto rule: if \(c_t\) is below a calibrated abstention threshold, do not treat \(z_t\) as reliable pose evidence. This veto is verifiable by comparing performance under different thresholds, as in the source’s Table 5 [PAGE 14].

Veto Scenario 2: Wrong-subject tracking.
Counterfactual setup: suppose the tracker locks onto a judge, an audience member, or the wrong athlete during a fast transition. The crop still produces an image \(x_t\) and a flow field \(\phi_t\), but they do not correspond to the action subject. The student may still output a descriptor because it was trained to map crops to pose-like embeddings, but the descriptor will not encode the intended athlete’s action.

Verifiable prediction: action recognition and detection will degrade when tracking is wrong. The source explicitly states, “Subject tracking is needed for VPD to ensure that the pose is of the correct person” [PAGE 8]. It also notes that with multiple humans, “fast-moving athletes in challenging poses are often missed otherwise: i.e., detected at lower confidence than static audience members or judges, or not detected at all” [PAGE 4]. The source further observes that tracking quality can change baseline results dramatically, citing large differences for prior methods when tracking is applied [PAGE 8].

Operational veto rule: if identity confidence is low, if bounding-box motion is inconsistent with the athlete’s kinematics, or if the tracker switches identity, veto the descriptor for that interval. This veto is verifiable by comparing tracked versus untracked inputs and by injecting synthetic tracking failures.
