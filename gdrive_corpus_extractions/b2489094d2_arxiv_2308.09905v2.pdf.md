<THINK_TRACE>
The first multi-hop step is to recover the paper’s causal reframing. Conventional MOT is defined as input-target pairs \((X_t,B_t,C_t)\), with each box \(B_t^i=(c_x^i,c_y^i,w^i,h^i)\) [PAGE 3]. TBD methods separate detection from association, while JDT methods attempt a unified pipeline [PAGE 1–2]. DiffusionTrack’s move is to replace “detect then associate” with “denoise paired boxes.” The abstract states that it “formulates object detection and association jointly as a consistent denoising diffusion process from paired noise boxes to paired ground-truth boxes” [PAGE 1]. The figure caption sharpens this: “DiffusionTrack formulates object association as a denoising diffusion process from paired noise boxes to paired object boxes within two adjacent frames \(t-1\) and \(t\)” [PAGE 1]. Thus the object of inference is not a single-frame box set but a paired object \(z_0=(B_{t-1},B_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 8}\) [PAGE 4]. The factor 8 is four box coordinates per frame across two frames; it is a count, not a physical unit.

The second hop is the forward/reverse diffusion derivation. The source gives \(q(x_t|x_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(x_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}x_{t-1},\beta_t I)\) [PAGE 3] and then prints \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+(1-\bar\alpha_t)\epsilon\) [PAGE 3]. The printed second equation is a gray-zone: standard DDPM uses \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon\), not \((1-\bar\alpha_t)\epsilon\). Taken literally, the printed noise variance is \((1-\bar\alpha_t)^2 I\), not \((1-\bar\alpha_t)I\). A reimplementation must audit this against the cited diffusion lineage [PAGE 3]. This is not a cosmetic issue: it changes signal-to-noise ratio and the denoising target.

The third hop is the tracker condition. The diffusion head receives a frozen backbone’s two-frame features and “takes a set of proposal boxes as input to crop RoI-feature” [PAGE 4]. The paired-box denoiser is \(f_\theta(z_s,s,X_{t-1},X_t)\) [PAGE 4], with an extra association score head whose output is used “to determine whether the paired boxes output belongs to the same object in the subsequent Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) post-processing process” [PAGE 4]. Therefore identity is not a separate Re-ID embedding in the main head; it is a learned confidence over paired-box noise refinement plus IoU association and later Kalman reassociation [PAGE 8–9].

The fourth hop is training and inference asymmetry. Training pads ground-truth boxes to \(N_{\text{train}}\), adds Gaussian noise under a “monotonically decreasing cosine schedule for \(\alpha_t\)” [PAGE 4], and minimizes a paired 3D GIoU/classification/regression loss [PAGE 5]. Inference instead uses prior boxes from frame \(t-1\) to initialize \(N_{\text{test}}\) noise boxes [PAGE 5], with the Figure 3 operator \(B=(1-\alpha_t)B+\alpha_t B_{\text{noise}}\) [PAGE 4]. Here \(\alpha_t\) is an interpolation coefficient, not necessarily identical to the training \(\bar\alpha_t\); the paper’s symbol reuse is a real audit trap. If \(\alpha_t=0\), the prior is preserved and no denoising is asked; if \(\alpha_t=1\), the prior is erased. The log perturbation schedule \(t=1000\cdot f(x)\), with \(f(x)=\log(x+1)/\log 2\) [PAGE 6], is only defined for \(x\ge 0\); \(x=0\) gives \(t=0\), and large \(x\) is unbounded unless clamped.

The fifth hop is the claim audit. The paper claims robustness to “detection perturbation” [PAGE 5–6], not weather robustness. It reports MOT17 MOTA 77.9 and MOT20 MOTA 72.8 [PAGE 7], and DanceTrack HOTA 52.4 [PAGE 7]. But the appendix says “our tracker does not work well for small objects on MOT20 due to the weakness of the diffusion model” [PAGE 8]. So any universal physical-layer claim is vetoed by the source’s own limitation. The gray-zone is that high aggregate MOTA/HOTA does not prove occlusion, small-object, or weather invariance; those require separate source evidence.

Forward noising operator, as printed [PAGE 3]:
\[
q(x_t|x_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}\!\left(x_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\right).
\]
Here \(t\in\{1,\dots,T\}\) is a dimensionless integer step; \(\beta_t\) and \(\alpha_t\) are dimensionless schedule coefficients; \(I\) is the identity covariance. The source then prints [PAGE 3]:
\[
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+(1-\bar\alpha_t)\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\]
with \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=0}^{t}(1-\beta_s)\). Audit note: the printed coefficient \((1-\bar\alpha_t)\) is not the standard DDPM \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\); if boxes are in pixel units, the printed equation is dimensionally inconsistent unless \(\epsilon\) is rescaled to coordinate units. This is a source-level breakdown risk, not an external correction.

Paired-box state and denoiser [PAGE 4]:
\[
z_0=(B_{t-1},B_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 8},\qquad
f_\theta(z_s,s,X_{t-1},X_t).
\]
The eight coordinates are two boxes times four coordinates each; \(N\) is a box count, not a physical quantity. The paper’s MOT box parameterization is \(B_t^i=(c_x^i,c_y^i,w^i,h^i)\) [PAGE 3].

Spatial-temporal fusion operator [PAGE 4]:
\[
P_1^i,P_2^i=\operatorname{Split}(\operatorname{Linear}_1(q_{\text{pro}}^i)),
\]
\[
\text{feat}=\operatorname{Bmm}\!\left(\operatorname{Bmm}\!\left(\operatorname{Concat}(f_{\text{roi}}^i,f_{\text{roi}}^j),P_1^i\right),P_2^i\right),
\]
\[
q^i=\operatorname{Linear}_2(\text{feat}),\qquad
(i,j)\in\{(t-1,t),(t,t-1)\}.
\]
All features are dimensionless embeddings; Bmm is batched matrix multiplication over \(N\) RoI features.

Training loss [PAGE 5]:
\[
L_{\text{cls}}(T_d,T_{\text{gt}})=\sum_{i=t-1}^{t}L_{\text{cls}}\!\left(\sqrt{C_d^i\times S_d},C_{\text{gt}}^i\right),
\]
\[
L_{\text{reg}}(T_d,T_{\text{gt}})=\sum_{i=t-1}^{t}L_{\text{reg}}(B_d^i,B_{\text{gt}}^i),
\]
\[
L_{\text{det}}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{pos}}}\sum_{(T_d,T_{\text{gt}})\in M}
\left[\lambda_1L_{\text{cls}}+\lambda_2L_{\text{reg}}+\lambda_3(1-\operatorname{GIoU}_{3D}(T_d,T_{\text{gt}}))\right].
\]
The source assigns \(\lambda_1=2,\lambda_2=5,\lambda_3=2\) [PAGE 5]. \(N_{\text{pos}}\) is a positive-sample count; \(S_d\) is association score; \(M\) is the Hungarian matching set. If \(N_{\text{pos}}=0\) or \(M=\varnothing\), \(L_{\text{det}}\) is undefined. \(L_{\text{cls}}\) is focal loss and \(L_{\text{reg}}\) is L1 loss [PAGE 5]; if boxes are pixels, L1 carries pixel units, while focal loss and GIoU are dimensionless.

3D GIoU operator [PAGE 8]:
\[
\operatorname{IoU}_{3D}(T_d,T_{\text{gt}})=
\frac{\sum_{i=t-1}^{t}\operatorname{Area}(B_d^i\cap B_{\text{gt}}^i)}
{\sum_{i=t-1}^{t}\operatorname{Area}(B_d^i\cup B_{\text{gt}}^i)},
\]
\[
\operatorname{GIoU}_{3D}(T_d,T_{\text{gt}})=
\operatorname{IoU}_{3D}
-\frac{\left|\sum_i\operatorname{Area}(D_{B_d^i,B_{\text{gt}}^i})-\sum_i\operatorname{Area}(B_d^i\cup B_{\text{gt}}^i)\right|}
{\left|\sum_i\operatorname{Area}(D_{B_d^i,B_{\text{gt}}^i})\right|}.
\]
The paper calls these “volume-extended versions” and “square frustums” [PAGE 5, PAGE 8]. \(\operatorname{IoU}_{3D}\in[0,1]\) and \(\operatorname{GIoU}_{3D}\in[-1,1]\) are dimensionless. If the enclosing convex object has zero volume, the denominator is zero; if both boxes are zero-volume, the ratio is ill-defined. If there is no intersection, GIoU still supplies a penalty through the enclosing object, which is the paper’s stated purpose [PAGE 8].

\[
B=(1-\alpha_t)B+\alpha_tB_{\text{noise}}.
\]
Here \(\alpha_t\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless interpolation coefficient. Breakdown regimes: \(\alpha_t=0\) means no perturbation and no denoising demand; \(\alpha_t=1\) erases prior geometry; \(\alpha_t\) outside \([0,1]\) is extrapolation not described by the source. The perturbation schedule [PAGE 6] is:
\[
t=1000\cdot f(x),\qquad f(x)=\frac{\log(x+1)}{\log 2},
\]
where \(x\) is “the average percentage of object motion cross two frames” [PAGE 6]. \(x\) must be nonnegative; \(x=0\) yields \(t=0\); \(x\to\infty\) yields an unbounded \(t\) unless clamped to \([0,T]\). The paper uses thresholds \(\tau_{\text{conf}}=0.25\), \(\tau_{\text{nms3d}}=0.6\), \(\tau_{\text{det}}=0.7\), and \(\tau_{\text{nms2d}}=0.7\) [PAGE 6]; these are dimensionless. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from Kalman/Hungarian MOT to learned association, then to diffusion-as-association. SORT used the Kalman filter with Hungarian assignment; DeepSORT added motion and appearance; StrongSORT integrated lightweight appearance-free association; ByteTrack used low-confidence detection similarities; OC-SORT revised the linear-motion assumption for occlusion and non-linear motion [PAGE 2]. Graph methods cast association as min-cost flow with GNN/GCN vertices and edges [PAGE 2]. JDT lineage includes query-based TrackFormer/MOTR/TransTrack/MeMOT, offset-based CenterTrack/PermaTrack/Tracktor++, and trajectory-based GTR/TubeTK [PAGE 2–3]. DiffusionTrack reads this history as a set of unresolved inconsistencies: “Harmful global or local inconsistency plagues both methods” [PAGE 2], “A suboptimal balance between robustness and model complexity is evident in both approaches” [PAGE 2], and “Both approaches also exhibit inflexibility across different scenes within the same video” [PAGE 2]. Its retrocausal claim is that association can be recast as paired-box denoising, not as a separate cost-flow or query-matching problem [PAGE 1–3].

The diffusion lineage is DDPM/score-based generative modeling to DiffusionDet and DiffusionInst: the paper says these “have successfully applied diffusion models to object detection and instance segmentation as noise-to-box and noise-to-filter tasks” [PAGE 3], and claims “this is the first work that adopts a diffusion model for the MOT task” [PAGE 3]. The genealogy is therefore not a clean replacement: DiffusionTrack retains older operators at the boundary. After denoising, “IoU is utilized as the similarity metric to connect the object tracklets” and “a simple Kalman filter is implemented to reassociate lost objects” [PAGE 8]. Algorithm 1 also uses IoU similarity and KalmanFilter for lost tracks [PAGE 9]. So the retrocausal story is hybrid: diffusion handles paired refinement, while classical association survives as a fallback.

Long-horizon traps follow directly from the source. Trap 1 is the consistency trap: separate detection/association training creates global inconsistency, but adding branches does not fully remove it [PAGE 2]. Trap 2 is the robustness-complexity seesaw: simple TBD is fragile to detection perturbation, while complex JDT is stable but compromises detection accuracy [PAGE 2]. Trap 3 is scene inflexibility: uniform video settings hinder adaptive strategies [PAGE 2]. DiffusionTrack’s answer is dynamic boxes and progressive refinement: “More sampling steps and proposal boxes in inference bring performance gain, but the effect is gradually saturated” [PAGE 5]. That is a real trade-off, not a free lunch: Table 1d shows accuracy gains accompanied by lower FPS [PAGE 6]. Trap 4 is the small-object trap: the appendix concedes poor small-object performance on MOT20 and calls multi-step denoising and longer training “intolerable” [PAGE 8]. Trap 5 is the inherited-Kalman trap: if the diffusion head fails under occlusion, the fallback is still a Kalman/IoU pipeline [PAGE 8–9], so the genealogy has not fully escaped classical assumptions.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
I use “theory of mind” here as an audit metaphor for the tracker’s implicit belief about object identity across time, not as a claim of cognition. The tracker’s belief state is the paired box plus association score: the association head decides whether paired outputs “belong to the same object” [PAGE 4]. Recursive belief is required because frame \(t\)’s decision depends on frame \(t-1\)’s prior boxes [PAGE 5], and lost tracks are re-associated after Kalman prediction [PAGE 9]. When the scene is easy, the model can behave as a one-step denoiser. When the scene is crowded or non-linear, it must recursively refine under occlusion.

Crisis habit regression appears at three points. First, under detection perturbation, the model can regress to thresholding: \(\tau_{\text{conf}}=0.25\), \(\tau_{\text{det}}=0.7\), and NMS thresholds [PAGE 6] determine which paired candidates survive. If the denoiser’s confidence collapses, the system falls back to IoU association and Kalman reassociation [PAGE 8–9]. Second, under occlusion, the classical habit is Kalman prediction; the paper keeps it [PAGE 9]. Third, under crowded MOT20 scenes, the source’s own limitation says small objects remain weak [PAGE 8], so the habit regression is not eliminated by diffusion.

The counter-trap is that more computation can look like more intelligence. More proposal boxes and more sampling steps improve aggregate metrics, but Figure 4a says the effect “is gradually saturated” [PAGE 5], and Table 1d shows 500 boxes/1 step at 21.05 FPS versus 500/2 at 10.47 FPS, 800/1 at 15.89 FPS, and 1000/1 at 13.37 FPS [PAGE 6]. A system that always chooses maximum boxes/steps is trapped: it buys small metric gains with large clock cost. The correct counter-trap is dynamic allocation: the paper explicitly says a single DiffusionTrack can be deployed to multiple scenes for a “desired speed-accuracy trade-off without retraining” [PAGE 6]. That is the counter-trap to fixed-compute habits.

The clock layer is therefore central. Sampling steps \(s\) are a temporal budget; the perturbation schedule \(t=1000\cdot f(x)\) [PAGE 6] is a scene-clock mapping from motion magnitude to diffusion time; FPS is the wall-clock output. Under a real-time constraint, the recursive belief must be computed with fewer steps; under an accuracy constraint, more steps are allowed. The source does not provide a formal controller for this trade-off, only the empirical observation of saturation and latency [PAGE 5–6]. Thus any claim that the model “adapts” without an explicit clock policy is overreach. The safe claim is narrower: the same trained network can be evaluated at different \(N_{\text{test}}\) and \(s\), and the operator can choose the setting externally. The internal recursion is not revealed as a planning process; it is a denoising recursion with a fixed number of steps.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
