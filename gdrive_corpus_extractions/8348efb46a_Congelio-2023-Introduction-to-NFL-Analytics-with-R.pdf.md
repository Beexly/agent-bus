Simple linear regression is stated as:
\[
Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1 X_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \varepsilon_i\sim(0,\sigma^2),
\]
where \(Y_i\) is the response in scoring points, \(X_i\) is the predictor in yards, and \(\beta_1\) has units of points per yard. Using the SI conversion \(1\text{ yd}=0.9144\text{ m}\), \(\beta_1\) can be rescaled to points per metre by multiplying by \(1/0.9144\). The book gives the equation as \(Y={\beta}_0+{\beta}_1X+\epsilon\) [PAGE 214]. A multiple linear regression extends this to:
\[
Y_i=\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j X_{ij}+\varepsilon_i.
\]
The design matrix \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p+1)}\) is estimable only if \(\operatorname{rank}(X)=p+1\); exact singularity occurs when \(\det(X^\top X)=0\), in which case at least one coefficient is not identified. The source shows this exact failure as “Coefficients: (1 not defined because of singularities)” [PAGE 227]. Near-singularity is measured by the condition number \(\kappa(X^\top X)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\); as \(\kappa\to\infty\), coefficient variance inflates. For predictor \(j\), the variance inflation factor is
\[
\operatorname{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2},
\]
where \(R_j^2\) is the coefficient of determination from regressing \(X_j\) on the other predictors. As \(R_j^2\to1\), \(\operatorname{VIF}_j\to\infty\), and the standard error of \(\hat\beta_j\) grows without bound. The book’s practical warning is: “The general rule is that two predictors become problematic in the regression model if the coefficient between the two is above 0.7 (or 0.8, given domain knowledge about the context of the data)” [PAGE 230–231]. The breakdown regime is therefore exact: rank deficiency gives non-identification; high pairwise correlation or high VIF gives unstable, sign-unstable estimates.

Binary logistic regression is stated as:
\[
P(Y=1\mid X)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\cdots+\beta_pX_p)]}.
\]
Here \(P(Y=1\mid X)\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless probability; the linear predictor and log-odds are dimensionless. The book presents this form on [PAGE 234]. For probabilistic scoring, log loss in bits is \(L=-\log_2 p(y\mid x)\), but the source itself uses probability filters rather than information-theoretic loss. The book reports the consequence: the filtered ranking changes materially [PAGE 100].

with both shares dimensionless on \([0,1]\) [PAGE 84]. Adjusted yards per attempt is:

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the analyst’s fallback when a model becomes unstable: revert to raw counting stats, anecdote, or eye-test authority. The book gives this habit a clear voice through Mike Tomlin: “I got a lot of respect for analytics and numbers, but I’m not going to make judgements based on those numbers. The game is the game” [PAGE 5]. In recursive terms, the analyst models the audience’s model of the analyst. If the audience distrusts adjusted metrics, the analyst may overcorrect toward familiar box-score quantities; if the audience overtrusts a leaderboard, the analyst may understate denominator fragility. The counter-trap is to make the model’s assumptions visible before the audience supplies its own. The book’s “directables” are exactly this: “most of my data visualizations include ‘directables’ within the plot” [PAGE 134], and the purpose is to let “a great quantity of data … be distilled into an easily consumable (and understandable!) format” [PAGE 133]. A directable is a theory-of-mind device: it anticipates misreading and installs an interpretation rule.

The counter-trap has three layers. Layer one is denominator honesty: show the minimum-attempt filter and the sample size, because “artificially-inflated statistics” appear when players have limited attempts [PAGE 80]. Layer two is filter transparency: show both the unfiltered and garbage-time-filtered rankings, because the source demonstrates that Pickett and Cousins move sharply when \(0.05<wp<0.95\) and `half_seconds_remaining > 120` are imposed [PAGE 99–100]. Layer three is identity transparency: state whether the analysis uses `passer` or `passer_player_name`, because the two definitions produce different attempt counts [PAGE 95–97].

The clock is both game-clock and model-lifecycle. Game-clock contamination enters through the two-minute warning, end-of-half, and win-probability filters. Model-lifecycle contamination enters through temporal leakage: using future-season aggregates, future roster outcomes, or post-hoc charting fields to predict earlier events. The book’s own warning about changing NFL culture across seasons [PAGE 226] is a clock warning: the data-generating process is non-stationary. A recursive theory of mind therefore requires the analyst to simulate three audiences—the skeptical coach, the casual fan, and the adversarial reviewer—and to pre-answer each with a visible filter, a visible denominator, and a visible uncertainty statement. The failure mode is not ignorance; it is a confident plot that has hidden its own clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Let \(C\) be the number of unique offensive-line combinations, \(Y\) a pressure or sack outcome, \(T\) quarterback time-to-throw, \(B\) running-back elusiveness or line cohesion, and \(W\) a weather/surface vector. A naive model estimates \(Y=\alpha+\beta C+\varepsilon\). If \(T\), \(B\), or \(W\) are omitted and correlated with \(C\), the probability limit is biased:
\[
\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta=\beta+\gamma_T\delta_T+\gamma_B\delta_B+\gamma_W\delta_W,
\]
where \(\gamma\) is the omitted variable’s effect on \(Y\) and \(\delta\) is the coefficient from regressing the omitted variable on \(C\). A sign-reversal veto occurs if \(\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta\) has the opposite sign from the hypothesized \(\beta\); in that regime the omitted layers dominate the claimed effect. A magnitude veto occurs if \(|\gamma_T\delta_T+\gamma_B\delta_B+\gamma_W\delta_W|\ge|\beta|/2\), because at least half the estimated association is attributable to omitted layers.

The source supplies concrete physical and biomechanical veto evidence. The play-by-play schema includes `roof`, `surface`, `temp`, `weather`, and `wind` [PAGE 91–92], so weather and stadium physics are not speculative add-ons; they are recorded variables that can condition passing, kicking, and traction. The source also shows release-time dominance: Roethlisberger averaged “just 2.19 seconds after the snap,” while Josh Allen averaged “3.04 seconds after the snap” [PAGE 34]. And it warns that “a team with a very good running back will appear higher no matter how bad their line, and a team with a great line will appear lower if the running back is terrible” [PAGE 38]. These are cross-layer vetoes: a quarterback who releases in 2.19 s can suppress sacks even behind a shuffled line; a running back who breaks tackles can inflate line yards even behind a poor line; wind, temperature, roof, and surface can alter both.

The variance side is equally formal. For predictor \(j\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j)=\sigma^2\operatorname{VIF}_j/\sum_i(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2\), so as \(R_j^2\to1\), \(\operatorname{VIF}_j\to\infty\) and the standard error diverges. Exact singularity occurs when \(\operatorname{rank}(X)<p+1\), equivalently \(\det(X^\top X)=0\); the source shows the symptom as “Coefficients: (1 not defined because of singularities)” [PAGE 227] and explains it as “the dreaded multicollinearity between two or more predictors” [PAGE 229]. Practical breakdown is flagged when pairwise correlation exceeds 0.7 or 0.8 [PAGE 230–231]. The adjudication rule is therefore: any claim that \(C\) causes \(Y\) must either condition on \(T,B,W\) or be labeled associational; any regression must report rank/condition diagnostics; any plot must show the conditioning layer or state that it is omitted. Without that, the physical/biomechanical/weather veto stands.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
