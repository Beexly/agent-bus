The algorithmic genealogy is: CFR and regret minimization (Zinkevich et al., 2007) → subgame solving (Burch et al., 2014; Moravcik et al., 2016; Brown & Sandholm, 2017) → knowledge-limited subgame solving (Zhang & Sandholm, 2021) → GS2 [PAGE 4, PAGE 7]. In parallel, Deep Monte Carlo (DMC) with self-play (DouZero) → DanZero → SDMC (expert-guided early self-play, soft action sampling) [PAGE 3, PAGE 7]. A multi-week trap is explicit: DanZero requires approximately 30 days of distributed self-play on a normal server to reach competitive performance [PAGE 7]. This creates a compute-time barrier to iterative research: each architectural ablation consumes weeks of GPU/CPU wall-clock before producing a verifiable win-rate delta in Table 1 [PAGE 8]. The second trap is evaluation elasticity: human-AI testing used only 16 volunteers across beginner/intermediate/advanced strata, and the authors warn win rates may be overstated by operational errors [PAGE 8]. Thus any counterfactual claim of "near-human" strength is retroactively vulnerable to small-sample bias. The third trap is the non-stationarity of team composition: because teammates may finish at different times, the residual teammate plays alone, dynamically altering the team structure mid-match [PAGE 4]. Agents trained on static teammate policies face distributional shift when the partnership dissolves, a failure mode not captured by pairwise round win rates alone. LLM-based agents (Suspicion-Agent, PokerGPT, Agent-Pro, ReTA) represent a third category but typically depend on fine-tuning with large-scale demonstration data generated more cost-effectively by RL and game-theoretic agents [PAGE 3], implying that the multi-week compute trap also governs LLM distillation pipelines for GuanDan.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In GuanDan, recursive theory of mind is obligatory because each player must infer private holdings of three others while coordinating with one partner. The mixed objective (cooperate with teammate, compete against two opponents) defeats purely adversarial or purely cooperative ToM models [PAGE 4]. Crisis habit regression appears when agents default to high-frequency, low-exploitability patterns under pressure—specifically in the tribute and back-tribute phases, where rank/suit selection of transferred cards alters future hand strength [PAGE 6]. A counter-trap is SDMC’s soft action sampling: instead of argmax over Q-values, it samples actions with Q-values close to the maximum, reducing exploitability and preventing opponents from converging on a deterministic counter-strategy [PAGE 7]. GS2 extends this by using SDMC as a blueprint and refining via real-time subgame solving, combining learned policy with online lookahead [PAGE 8]. The clock layer is the inter-round level-promotion mechanism: finishing order (Banker/Follower/Third/Dweller) sets tribute obligations and level advancement, meaning a tactical mistake in round t changes the information set and legal action space in round t+k [PAGE 4]. Agents that regress to myopic round-win maximization ignore this clock; for example, GS2 wins 97.1% against Rule Two but only 42.4% against advanced humans [PAGE 8], revealing that opponent modeling against adaptive humans outstrips static rule-based exploitation. The breakdown regime occurs when the belief state over opponents’ tribute strategies collapses under anti-tribute exceptions (both red jokers held by losing team waive tribute), because the agent’s ToM model must condition on a rare but game-state-changing discrete event [PAGE 4]. LLM-based ToM agents such as Suspicion-Agent (theory-of-mind aware GPT-4) show promise but require fine-tuning on demonstration data, which itself is generated more cost-effectively by RL agents [PAGE 3], creating a dependency loop where ToM performance is bottlenecked by the underlying RL teacher’s multi-week training wall.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the simulator is implemented in optimized Java on a single Intel Core i9-13900K (24 cores, 3.00 GHz), yielding ≈ 25 million steps/hour across 10 parallel environments [PAGE 7]. This hard throughput ceiling vetoes exhaustive tree search at GS2’s scale; real-time subgame solving must therefore operate over reduced infosets |I_i| and |I_{−i}| rather than the full 10^118 infoset manifold [PAGE 7]. Biomechanical veto: human-AI evaluation is bounded by volunteer operational familiarity; the authors explicitly state win rates may be overestimated because of operational errors during evaluation [PAGE 8]. With only ~106 rounds per agent and uneven matching (GS2/SDMC scheduled more against intermediate/advanced players), the sample size cannot veto the null hypothesis that agents remain below intermediate human performance [PAGE 8]. Weather veto (stochastic environment): the chance player c samples card deals with fixed probability, producing hidden-hand uncertainty and stochastic information sets; no deterministic plan can dominate because opponents’ private cards are latent [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. Adjudication: these vetoes compose multiplicatively. The physical compute veto limits self-play data volume; the biomechanical human-evaluation veto limits validation sensitivity; the weather stochasticity veto forces belief-space planning rather than state-space planning. An agent is adjudicated viable only if its win-rate advantage over rule-based baselines (e.g., GS2 100.0% vs Rule Four [PAGE 8]) persists under reduced parallelism (fewer than 10 environments) and under human operational noise, neither of which is currently stress-tested in the paper. The tribute/back-tribute sub-modules are identified as a limitation requiring more effective policies [PAGE 9], confirming that the cross-layer veto is not yet resolved.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto Scenario 1 (Equilibrium non-computability): Because GuanDan is a multi-player non-zero-sum imperfect-information game, CFR-based subgame solving does not guarantee convergence to a Nash equilibrium σ* satisfying u_i(σ*_i, σ*_{−i}) ≥ max_{σ_i} u_i(σ_i, σ*_{−i}) for all i [PAGE 4]. Verifiable test: if GS2’s blueprint strategy is fixed and opponents adapt via best response BR(σ_{−i}), the exploitability of σ* must remain bounded; otherwise the agent collapses against non-stationary human opponents. Prediction: any agent relying solely on equilibrium approximation without online opponent modeling will plateau below 45% against intermediate humans, matching GS2’s observed 40.0% [PAGE 8].

Veto Scenario 2 (Compute throughput wall): The 25 million steps/hour ceiling on a single CPU bounds self-play data generation [PAGE 7]. Verifiable test: halving parallel environments from 10 to 5 should linearly reduce data throughput and, after fixed wall-clock training, degrade win rate against SDMC by a statistically significant margin (e.g., >5 percentage points). Prediction: DanZero-style DMC agents require distributed training; restricting to a single-node CPU will extend the ~30-day training window [PAGE 7] beyond practical research cycles, producing agents that cannot exploit soft-action-sampling regularization fully.

Veto Scenario 3 (Inter-round credit-assignment veto): Tribute and level progression create cross-round dependencies where finishing as Dweller at Level A resets the team to Level 2 [PAGE 4]. Verifiable test: mask tribute/back-tribute and level-carryover state from the observation vector; if win rate against rule-based agents drops by >10 points while single-round mechanics remain intact, the Markov single-round reward hypothesis is falsified. Prediction: agents that ignore the Dweller/anti-tribute clock will win individual rounds but lose matches, reproducing the pattern where learning agents beat rule-based agents (>80% win rate) yet fail to reach superhuman performance (<50% vs humans) [PAGE 8].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
