<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central derivation is not “which model is best at sports video” but “which stage of the video-to-box-score chain has which kind of information structure.” The chain is decomposed on [PAGE 10] and [PAGE 11] into segmentation, event spotting, action classification, outcome determination, team assignment, player attribution, and aggregation. The multi-hop step is to add two latent axes to those seven stages: temporal scale and rule determinacy. Segmentation is a coarse temporal pattern over many seconds; event spotting is a fine kinematic pattern over a fraction of a second; outcome is often not perceptual at all because volleyball’s serve-next rule fixes it; team assignment is best solved by observing the server’s physical position; identity is a static optical fact about a garment, not a motor pattern distributed across time. This explains the apparent contradiction on [PAGE 1]: “A prompted model segments matches into plays about as well as anything we tested, yet a two-million-parameter trained model outperforms it at spotting individual contacts, at a small fraction of the cost.” It also explains why frontier reasoning improves event detection but not identity: reasoning can integrate coarse context and search for a legible frame, but it cannot create optical information that was never captured.

The gray-zone audit matters because the paper’s strongest claim is a negative identity result, and negative results are easy to misread. First, identity is not scored on all contacts; it is scored only on contacts that matched successfully at the action level and that carry a jersey number in the human label, as the protocol on [PAGE 30] states. Second, identity failure is decomposed on [PAGE 31] into no viable candidate, never legibly read anywhere in the play, and legibly read but not selected. The largest category is the third: “The single largest share of losses, larger than either of the two above, occurs where the acting player was legibly read somewhere in the play, and was present among the candidates offered to the selection step, but a different candidate was chosen instead” [PAGE 31]. That is a selection failure conditional on identity being available, not a pure reading failure. It is why the top-down track-then-read architecture on [PAGE 32] matters: “tracking allows a system to read a player’s identity once, at whichever moment across an entire play that player happens to be most legible, and then propagate that reading to every other contact belonging to the same track” [PAGE 32]. Third, “more context helps” has two different mechanisms. For action, temporal context integrates a redundant biomechanical signature. For identity, context is only a search over frames for the rare legible one; [PAGE 23] reports that “legibility rose sharply, roughly quadrupling for attacks and rising close to certainty for sets,” but that is search, not temporal integration. Fourth, the fine-tuning failure on [PAGE 19] is a checkable implementation trap, not a mysterious ceiling: loss was computed over the whole sequence and the vision tower stayed frozen, so loss “plateaued near 6.43 and never fell across training,” and the adapter scored “0.197 against 0.298” versus zero-shot. Fifth, abstention must be built from inter-reader agreement, not self-confidence; [PAGE 26] states: “build abstention from disagreement between independent estimates, not from a model’s own stated confidence in a single one.” Sixth, the evaluation protocol explicitly avoids letting a spotting error masquerade as an attribution error: “We do not score against timestamps directly, because doing so would let a segmentation or spotting error masquerade as an attribution error” [PAGE 30]. The gray-zone conclusion is that the paper’s identity ceiling is credible precisely because it separates reading, selection, tracking, and scoring, and because it reports coverage and precision separately rather than collapsing abstention into accuracy.

The cross-check with external evidence is also multi-hop. The paper’s identity result is not an isolated volleyball anomaly. Public benchmarks on [PAGE 8] show coarse video QA near 80.6 percent on LongVideoBench but fine temporal reasoning at 37.9 percent on TOMATO against a 95.2 percent human baseline, and agentic multi-turn video use under 60 percent on VideoGAIA. The same pattern appears in sports-specific work: jersey-number recognition is limited by fixed-camera pixel legibility, game-state reconstruction is in the low twenties even on broadcast footage, and action-to-player attribution remains short of reliable. The paper’s own single-pass readers on [PAGE 31] range from 0.488 to 0.585, with reasoning disabled collapsing to 0.220. The world-model probe barely beats a bounding-box-coordinate baseline once identity rather than action is asked [PAGE 1]. Thus the derivation is: action is temporally redundant and partially solved by motion models; identity is optically sparse and not recovered by scale, reasoning, or general video representation. The practical corollary is stage-specific investment: use prompting for coarse segmentation, small trained specialists for fine spotting and action, global rules for outcome and team state, track-level reading plus agreement-based abstention for identity, and never chain uncertain local identity decisions because [PAGE 33] reports: “Every local-chaining constraint we tried reduced accuracy relative to treating each contact’s attribution as an independent decision.”

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a seven-stage operator chain from video to statistics. Let \(V\) be a match video with time coordinate \(t\) in seconds and spatial coordinates in pixels. The chain is:
\[
\hat{R}=\mathcal{S}(V),\quad
\hat{E}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{R}),\quad
\hat{a}=\mathcal{A}(\hat{E}),\quad
\hat{o}=\mathcal{O}(\hat{R}),\quad
\hat{\tau}=\mathcal{T}(\hat{E}),\quad
\hat{\jmath}=\mathcal{I}(\hat{E},\hat{\tau}),\quad
\hat{G}=\mathcal{G}\left(\{(\hat{a}_i,\hat{\tau}_i,\hat{\jmath}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\right),
\]
where \(\mathcal{S}\) segments rallies, \(\mathcal{E}\) spots contacts, \(\mathcal{A}\) classifies action, \(\mathcal{O}\) determines outcome, \(\mathcal{T}\) assigns team, \(\mathcal{I}\) assigns identity, and \(\mathcal{G}\) aggregates. This decomposition is stated on [PAGE 10] and [PAGE 11]. The units are seconds for \(t\), pixels for spatial extent, and dimensionless probability for accuracy. The first assumption is that stages are scored conditionally: a predicted event is matched to a true event only if it falls within a tolerance window and carries the same action label, and identity is scored only on matched contacts with a jersey label [PAGE 30].

Identity accuracy is an exact-match probability over the jersey-labeled evaluation subset \(C_{\mathrm{jersey}}\):
\[
A_{\mathrm{id}}=\frac{1}{|C_{\mathrm{jersey}}|}\sum_{c\in C_{\mathrm{jersey}}}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat{\jmath}(c)=j(c)\right],
\]
with \(A_{\mathrm{id}}\in[0,1]\). This is the metric behind Table 3 on [PAGE 31] and Table 6 on [PAGE 32]. Coverage and precision are reported separately, because abstention makes a single accuracy number ambiguous:
\[
\mathrm{Cov}=\frac{|C_{\mathrm{ans}}|}{|C|},\qquad
\mathrm{Prec}=\frac{|C_{\mathrm{ans}}\cap C_{\mathrm{correct}}|}{|C_{\mathrm{ans}}|},
\]
both dimensionless probabilities. The protocol on [PAGE 30] explicitly requires reporting coverage separately from precision on the answered subset. For per-player statistics, the paper uses Spearman rank correlation:
\[
\rho=\frac{\operatorname{cov}\left(R(\hat{n}),R(n)\right)}
{\sigma_{R(\hat{n})}\,\sigma_{R(n)}},
\]
which is dimensionless. The source reports serve attribution at \(\rho=0.81\) across 303 true serves, while kill attribution falls to \(\rho=0.02\) across 98 true kills [PAGE 25]. This is not a modeling curiosity: it shows that a team-level aggregate can look plausible while the player-level ordering underneath is close to noise.

The rule-based operators are probabilistic invariants measured over the held-out corpus, not assumptions:
\[
P\left(S_{t+1}=W_t\right)=0.9996=\frac{8048}{8051},\qquad
P\left(\text{exactly one serve per rally}\right)=0.9998=\frac{8249}{8251}.
\]
These values appear in Table 2 on [PAGE 10]. The outcome operator \(\mathcal{O}\) therefore can often be replaced by observing the next server, because [PAGE 17] states: “the team that serves the next rally is, with vanishing exception, the team that won the last one.” The team operator \(\mathcal{T}\) is improved by observing the server’s physical position rather than assuming a court side; [PAGE 17] reports this raises team assignment from near chance to “accuracy in the low-to-mid nineties.”

The optical legibility condition is the key physical boundary. The corpus has a median player height “under two hundred pixels, and a jersey digit occupies a small fraction of that height, typically between fifteen and thirty pixels tall” [PAGE 9]. The figure caption on [PAGE 15] states: “Below roughly twenty pixels the digit is no longer reliably distinguishable from adjacent numerals, independent of any model’s capability; this is an optical limit rather than a modelling one.” Formally, define a frame-level legibility predicate \(\ell_t\in\{0,1\}\) for digit height \(h_{\mathrm{digit}}\) in pixels and viewing angle \(\theta\):
\[
\ell_t=0 \quad \text{when} \quad h_{\mathrm{digit}}\lesssim 20\,\mathrm{px},
\]
with the operational range \(h_{\mathrm{digit}}\approx 15\text{--}30\,\mathrm{px}\) [PAGE 9]. The information available to any identity reader is bounded by
\[
I(J;X_{1:T})\le H(J),\qquad
I(J;X_{1:T})=0 \quad \text{if} \quad \forall t,\ \ell_t=0,
\]
where \(I\) is in bits or nats and \(H(J)\) is the entropy of the jersey identity. This is the formal version of the paper’s statement on [PAGE 23]: “nothing about the motor sequence of a spike encodes which specific athlete is performing it, and no amount of additional temporal context, in principle, can extract information about a digit’s shape that is not optically resolvable in at least one available frame.” The breakdown regime is exact: if no frame in the track resolves the digit, identity cannot be recovered from pixels; any apparent recovery must come from roster priors, selection, or external metadata.

Camera axis is a second physical operator. Identity accuracy is not a monotone function of \(h\) alone but \(A_{\mathrm{id}}(h,\theta)\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between the jersey plane and the camera axis. The source reports the crossover on [PAGE 9]: “the larger of our two sideline matches, with a median player height of 148 pixels, scores the lowest identity accuracy of any match we measured, 0.340, while an end-line match with barely more than half as many pixels per player, 93, scores higher at 0.418, and the two end-line matches with still more pixels, 122 and 169, score 0.569 and 0.608 respectively.” The singularity is foreshortening: a sideline view can drive the effective digit width toward zero even when \(h\) is large, so a fixed pixel-height threshold is not portable across cameras [PAGE 33] and [PAGE 34].

Temporal sampling is a third operator. If a clip of duration \(T\) is sampled at \(f_s\) frames per second, then \(N_f=f_sT\). In the worked example on [PAGE 27] and [PAGE 28], a 13.33-second rally with seven true contacts is sampled at 1 frame per second, and the model returns zero contacts; at 3 frames per second, the same model with the same prompt returns ten contacts. The breakdown regime is sampling collapse: when \(f_s\) is below the contact duration scale, the contact is not delivered to the model at all. The paper states the one-frame-per-second response as “zero contacts found, on a rally that contains seven” [PAGE 27], and the three-frame-per-second response as finding “ten contacts” on the same rally [PAGE 28]. Cost is metered in USD per identical workload; Table 3 on [PAGE 31] shows nine readers from $0.025 to $3.585, while identity accuracy varies from 0.488 to 0.585, with the reasoning-disabled case at 0.220. The operator-level conclusion is that accuracy is stage- and condition-indexed, not a single portable constant.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a progression from identity-free perception to identity-saturated perception. At one actor, golf-swing analysis detects key events from ordinary video with high accuracy because there is no attribution problem; at two actors, tennis and padel use court geometry, since the net fixes which half of the frame belongs to which player; at two-person teams, beach volleyball weakens the geometric guarantee because teammates share a side and often share kit; at six or eleven actors, the guarantee disappears. The paper states the progression directly on [PAGE 5]: “detecting that something happened is close to solved; saying who did it is not.” The authors’ own earlier boxing work sat at the two-actor point and worked because a two-person combat sport removes the roster problem. The move to baseball, then basketball and volleyball, forced the architecture from a single clip classifier toward track-level, temporally integrated small models [PAGE 3].

The commercial genealogy is equally important. The market’s own documentation repeatedly reintroduces a human, a fixed rig, a pre-registered roster, or a wearable. Krossover built per-player statistics on paid human reviewers and its founder said “humans are still better than computer vision” [PAGE 6]. Hudl’s automated offering states that the core “relies on a team of trained analysts who manually tag every play, event, and statistic in a video” [PAGE 6]. SportsVisio’s capture guidance instructs filming “stable, elevated, and behind the end line so the full court and all six rotations stay in frame,” keeps “jersey numbers readable whenever possible,” and states that “poor capture habits cost more accuracy than cheap hardware does” [PAGE 6]. Balltime says submitting a player’s correct jersey number is “crucial” [PAGE 6]. Trace uses a wearable and states that a player who forgets the sensor does not appear in the statistics [PAGE 6]. Spiideo requires calibration and instructs that the rig not be moved [PAGE 6]. SmashVision’s own comparison argues that the right question is whether a customer can see and correct what the system got wrong [PAGE 6]. This is a retrocausal explanation of the paper’s negative result: the market already behaves as if computation alone does not close identity, and the paper measures why.

The scientific genealogy runs through jersey-number recognition, game-state reconstruction, and video benchmarks. Koshkina and Elder report that only a small single-digit percentage of player crops are legible enough to attempt a jersey reading under a fixed, unzoomed gymnasium camera, and attribute this to the fixed camera’s lack of pan and zoom rather than to the reading model [PAGE 5]. Their long-term tracking companion finds appearance re-identification confidence decays from near-certainty to near chance within a few hundred frames [PAGE 5]. SoccerNet game-state reconstruction reports low-twenties accuracy for the complete task even with professional camera work, and later challenge editions name jersey-number recognition as the bottleneck [PAGE 5]. Hu et al. report that multimodal systems remain short of reliable event-to-player attribution on broadcast basketball [PAGE 5]. The benchmark lineage on [PAGE 8] shows the same split: LongVideoBench at 80.6 percent, Video-MME-v2 at 49.4 percent, TemporalBench at 38.5 percent, TOMATO at 37.9 percent against a 95.2 percent human baseline, and VideoGAIA under 60 percent. These are not disconnected numbers; they are the general-case signature of coarse scene understanding succeeding while fine-grained temporal attribution lags.

The multi-week traps are specific. First, spend weeks scaling reasoning or context for identity, when [PAGE 23] says the digit is either optically resolvable in some frame or absent. Second, misread a sensitivity analysis: missed play boundaries are costly, but that does not mean a hand-engineered boundary rule will work; [PAGE 15] reports that the existing segmenter was already near the ceiling, so the “pure math” replacements were wasted effort. Third, fine-tune a vision-language model with a full-sequence loss and a frozen vision tower; [PAGE 19] reports loss plateauing near 6.43 and the adapter scoring below zero-shot, 0.197 against 0.298. Fourth, trust self-reported confidence; [PAGE 26] says confidence was statistically indistinguishable from overall accuracy. Fifth, chain local decisions; [PAGE 33] says every local-chaining constraint reduced accuracy. Sixth, transfer a fixed pixel-height threshold across cameras; [PAGE 33] and [PAGE 34] show focal length, distance, and axis confound the pixel count. Seventh, accept a team-level aggregate as validation; [PAGE 25] shows serve rank correlation at 0.81 but kill rank correlation at 0.02, so a plausible team sum can hide near-random player attribution. These traps are retrocausal because later evidence explains earlier failures: the failures were not insufficient effort but wrong layer of information.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive belief structure has three agents: the system, the coach or reviewer, and the vendor or market. The system’s belief about its own answer must include a belief about whether the answer is visually grounded. The coach’s belief about the box score must include a belief about which statistics are trustworthy. The vendor’s belief about deployment must include a belief about what the customer will tolerate correcting. The paper’s crisis habit regression is the market’s fallback under identity uncertainty: manual tagging, pre-submitted rosters, wearable sensors, fixed calibrated rigs, and analyst review. That is not irrational; it is a coherent response to a perception problem that has not been solved by computation alone. But the paper treats manual annotation as a fourth method with measurable cost and latency, not as free ground truth [PAGE 12]. The regression is visible in the vendor documents on [PAGE 6], where “automated” products still require a human somewhere between camera and box score.

The counter-trap is to avoid two false fixes. The first false fix is to let the model’s own confidence decide abstention. [PAGE 26] reports that the overwhelming majority of reads came back with high confidence, and accuracy among the high-confidence subset was statistically indistinguishable from accuracy overall. The counter-trap is agreement among independent readers: contacts where multiple passes agree are correct at a rate high enough to present without qualification, while disagreements are exactly what should route to a human [PAGE 26]. The second false fix is local chaining: excluding the previous attributed player, or feeding one uncertain decision into the next. [PAGE 33] states: “Every local-chaining constraint we tried reduced accuracy relative to treating each contact’s attribution as an independent decision.” Global constraints are different because they do not depend on a single upstream decision being correct; the serve-next rule and the one-serve-per-rally rule hold independently of processing order.

The clock layer is where recursive theory of mind becomes operational. The label clock runs to one-second resolution, so the evaluation matches within a tolerance window rather than demanding exact timestamp agreement [PAGE 9] and [PAGE 30]. The API clock is coarser than the task: [PAGE 27] shows a 13.33-second rally with seven contacts returning zero contacts at one frame per second; [PAGE 28] shows ten contacts at three frames per second; [PAGE 29] shows that a generic “Describe what is happening” prompt at the same frame rate produces fluent prose with no timestamp, no discrete event count, and no structure a box score could use. The deployment clock is the review loop: a coach reviews a minority of contacts, concentrated in the low-confidence tier the system itself identifies, and a correction to one contact propagates across the track [PAGE 34]. Recursively, the system must model that the coach can correct only what is surfaced; the coach must model that an unqualified number is not the same as a guessed number; the vendor must model that the customer’s capture habits are part of the model’s input distribution. The paper’s design response is tiered output: confirmed attributions, a short candidate list for one-interaction confirmation, and a review queue for contacts with no reliable candidate [PAGE 34]. That is a theory of mind about trust, not merely a UI choice.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer issues a hard veto against any claim that more reasoning, more parameters, or more temporal context can recover an illegible jersey digit. The optical evidence is explicit: median player height is “under two hundred pixels,” jersey digits are “typically between fifteen and thirty pixels tall” [PAGE 9], and “below roughly twenty pixels the digit is no longer reliably distinguishable from adjacent numerals, independent of any model’s capability; this is an optical limit rather than a modelling one” [PAGE 15]. The ball is even smaller: it “regularly measures under thirty pixels across” [PAGE 1]. Therefore, if the digit is not resolved in at least one frame of a track, the identity information is not present in the pixels. A model may still guess from roster priors, jersey-color context, or selection heuristics, but that is not visual reading. The correct veto is: do not attribute an identity improvement to reasoning unless the evaluation holds crops constant and demonstrates a legible-frame effect; otherwise the improvement is prior-driven.

The biomechanical layer distinguishes action from identity. A serve, dig, set, or attack is a repeated, trained motor pattern, so its signature is redundantly distributed across a short temporal window. That is why small temporal models can outperform prompted models at contact spotting, and why holistic reasoning can improve event detection. But identity is not a motor pattern. [PAGE 23] states: “nothing about the motor sequence of a spike encodes which specific athlete is performing it, and no amount of additional temporal context, in principle, can extract information about a digit’s shape that is not optically resolvable in at least one available frame.” The biomechanical veto is therefore against conflating “more context helps action” with “more context helps identity.” Context helps identity only as a search over frames for a legible moment, not as integration of a redundant signal. This is why the track-then-read architecture works: it reads once where legible and propagates, rather than reading at every contact instant [PAGE 32].

The camera-axis layer is a geometric veto against resolution-only claims. A sideline camera sees the jersey edge-on, foreshortened by perspective, while an end-line camera sees a flatter face. The source reports a direct crossover: a 148-pixel sideline match scores 0.340, while a 93-pixel end-line match scores 0.418 [PAGE 9]. Therefore, a system description that says it works “above some pixel threshold” is incomplete; the relevant condition is the joint pair of pixel height and viewing angle. This also vetoes transferring a fixed threshold from one camera to another: focal length and distance change the real-world meaning of a pixel count, and pose or digit reading depends on resolving a small structure rather than a silhouette [PAGE 33] and [PAGE 34].

The weather layer is not adjudicable from this source. The corpus is indoor amateur volleyball, and no meteorological variable, outdoor lighting condition, wind, precipitation, or temperature is reported. The paper’s physical claims are about optics, camera axis, occlusion, and crowding, not weather. The correct cross-layer adjudication is therefore a veto against importing weather as an explanation for the identity result: there is no measured weather covariate in the source, so a weather-mediated claim would be out of domain. This is not a claim that weather never matters in outdoor amateur sport; it is a claim that this source cannot support a weather inference, and the paper’s own transfer discussion on [PAGE 35] limits the specific numbers to volleyball and to the camera conditions described in [PAGE 9]. The admissible veto is against overgeneralization, not against weather as a physical phenomenon.
