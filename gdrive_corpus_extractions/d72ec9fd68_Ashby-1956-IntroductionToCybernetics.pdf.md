The source slice runs from the front matter of Ashby’s *An Introduction to Cybernetics* through the opening of Part III, ending at [PAGE 111]. The central derivation is not a loose metaphor about “control”; it is a staged reduction from behavior to transformation, from transformation to machine, from machine to coupled transducer, from variety to constraint, from constraint to entropy, and from entropy/requisite variety to regulation. The first move is explicit: “Such a set of transitions, on a set of operands, is a transformation” [PAGE 10]. Ashby then defines the determinate machine by behavior rather than matter: “A determinate machine is defined as that which behaves in the same way as does a closed single-valued transformation” [PAGE 17]. This is the load-bearing operator. If the transformation is not closed, higher powers are undefined: Ashby says “the transformation that represents a machine must be closed” [PAGE 19]. If it is not single-valued, then the “machine” is not step-determinate, and the protocol must be re-coded or the state definition enlarged [PAGE 91]. This is why the text can move from beads on springs to bacterial cultures to Tinbergen’s stickleback sequence without category error: the object is the transition structure, not the material substrate.

The next hop is the state/vector move. A state is “any well-defined condition or property that can be recognised if it occurs again” [PAGE 17], and a vector is “a compound entity, having a definite number of components” [PAGE 20]. Once states are vectors, coupling becomes tractable: the whole state is a vector of part-states, and a transducer is a machine whose behavior is indexed by parameters. Ashby’s “input” is the parameter set: “The parameter, as something that can vary, is its input” [PAGE 27]. Feedback is then defined structurally: “When this circularity of action exists between the parts of a dynamic system, feedback may be said to be present” [PAGE 31]. But the text immediately restricts the usefulness of feedback as a master concept, because richly cross-connected systems cannot be decomposed into independent feedback loops without loss. This is a gray-zone audit point: feedback is a local pattern, not a universal explanatory primitive.

The variety chain is the second major derivation. A set has “a variety of three elements” when it contains three distinguishable elements [PAGE 67]. Logarithmic variety is measured in bits: “When variety is measured in the logarithmic form its unit is the ‘bit’” [PAGE 69]. Constraint appears when actual variety is less than the independently possible variety [PAGE 70]. This is the conceptual bridge to information: a constraint is a reduction in the set of possibilities, and a machine is a constrained protocol. Ashby says a machine-like protocol is re-coded into “a statement of the transformation” plus “a statement of the actual input given” [PAGE 72]. Then transmission of variety is bounded: if T dominates U, U cannot gain more variety in one step than T possesses [PAGE 82]. Through a small channel R with r states, the rate is bounded by log2 r bits per step [PAGE 83]. This is the origin of channel capacity in the book: capacity is not a metaphor but a state-count/rate bound.

The entropy move must be handled carefully. Ashby defines Shannon entropy as “– p1 log p1 – p2 log p2 – … pn log pn” [PAGE 94], then warns: “The word ‘entropy’ will be used in this book solely as it is used by Shannon, any broader concept being referred to as ‘variety’ or in some other way” [PAGE 95]. This is a major gray-zone audit: thermodynamic entropy and Shannon entropy are not interchangeable here. Shannon’s calculation also assumes probabilities summing to one, Markovian transitions, and equilibrial weighting of column entropies [PAGE 95]. If those assumptions fail, the numerical entropy can still be computed algebraically, but its use as a channel/rate theorem becomes invalid. The book itself flags the danger: Shannon’s results “must therefore be applied to biological material only after a detailed check on their applicability has been made” [PAGE 95].

The regulation move is the culmination. The law of requisite variety states that only regulator variety can reduce outcome variety: “only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” [PAGE 110]. In logarithmic form, V_O cannot be less than V_D − V_R [PAGE 110]. This is not a physical law of matter; Ashby says “It has nothing to do with the properties of matter” [PAGE 111]. It is a combinatorial statement about tables of disturbance-regulator-outcome triples. If a column repeats outcomes k times, the theorem is modified to V_O ≥ V_D − log k − V_R [PAGE 111]. This is exactly the kind of gray-zone boundary that must be audited: the theorem is exact under the stated table conditions, and only approximately transferable when the table is a statistical or partially observed abstraction.

Two further audits matter for downstream reasoning. First, “memory” is observer-relative in Ashby: “to invoke ‘memory’ in a system as an explanation of its behaviour is equivalent to declaring that one cannot observe the system completely” [PAGE 64]. Thus a hidden variable can always be replaced by a past-history term, and vice versa, if the correspondence is one-one. Second, “noise” is not intrinsic: “noise is in no intrinsic way distinguishable from any other form of variety” [PAGE 100]. Only a recipient’s distinction between wanted and unwanted variety makes noise a meaningful category. These two points prevent the common error of reifying “memory” or “noise” as substances inside a black box.

The black-box derivation also has a non-uniqueness audit. The canonical representation identifies a mechanism only up to isomorphism, and “The behaviour does not specify the connexions uniquely” [PAGE 53]. A black box can be modeled by many internal networks with the same input-output behavior. Similarly, homomorphic simplification is not arbitrary: a many-one transformation must preserve determinacy, or the simplified machine is not a machine at all [PAGE 58]. This matters for any multi-hop inference: one may infer a quotient machine, but not a unique internal wiring diagram.

Finally, the equilibrium/veto derivation is exact for coupled parts. Ashby states: “the whole is at a state of equilibrium if and only if each part is at a state of equilibrium in the conditions provided by the other part” [PAGE 82]. Pictorially, each part has a “power of veto” over the whole’s equilibrium: “No state (of the whole) can be a state of equilibrium unless it is acceptable to every one of the component parts, each acting in the conditions given by the others” [PAGE 47]. This is the formal basis for cross-layer adjudication: a physical, biomechanical, or weather layer can veto a proposed stable outcome if its local equilibrium condition is not satisfied.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal core can be written as follows, with units and page anchors.

1. Transformation and closure. Let \(S\) be a finite set of operands/states. A single-valued transformation is a map
\[
T:S\to S,\qquad s\mapsto T(s).
\]
Ashby’s basic definition is: “Such a set of transitions, on a set of operands, is a transformation” [PAGE 10]. Closure means \(T(S)\subseteq S\); Ashby says: “When this occurs, the set of operands is closed under the transformation” [PAGE 10]. The unit of \(S\) is a count of distinguishable states; \(T\) itself is dimensionless. The iteration \(T^n(s)\) is defined only if closure holds at every step. Breakdown regime: an unclosed transformation “cannot be applied twice” in general; the text says “the transformation that represents a machine must be closed” [PAGE 19]. If closure fails, \(T^n\) is undefined beyond the last defined operand.

2. Determinate machine. A determinate machine is behaviorally equivalent to a closed single-valued transformation: “A determinate machine is defined as that which behaves in the same way as does a closed single-valued transformation” [PAGE 17]. A state is “any well-defined condition or property that can be recognised if it occurs again” [PAGE 17]. The trajectory from \(s_0\) is
\[
s_0,\ T(s_0),\ T^2(s_0),\ T^3(s_0),\ldots
\]
with dimension of discrete steps. Breakdown regime: if \(T\) is many-valued, the next state is not unique, and the trajectory is a set of possible trajectories, not a single line of behavior [PAGE 91].

3. Equilibrium and stability. A state \(a\) is an equilibrium if
\[
T(a)=a.
\]
For a displacement operator \(D\), Ashby’s stability condition is
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} T^n D(a)=a
\]
[PAGE 44]. The unit is discrete iteration count; \(D(a)\) is the displaced state. Breakdown regime: this test is meaningful only if \(a\) is already an equilibrium; otherwise “stability” has no well-defined return target. Also, stability must be specified relative to a class of displacements \(D\); a pencil may be stable to a 1° displacement and unstable to 5° [PAGE 45].

4. Vector state and coupling. If a whole has parts with states \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), the whole state is
\[
x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
\]
A vector is “a compound entity, having a definite number of components” [PAGE 20]. A transducer is a machine whose transformation is indexed by parameters: “The parameter, as something that can vary, is its input” [PAGE 27]. Coupling is parameter substitution: one machine’s state variables become the other machine’s parameter values. Breakdown regime: coupling is not uniquely determined by the parts alone; the same parts can be coupled differently, and the whole’s behavior changes accordingly [PAGE 53].

5. Variety and constraint. For a finite set \(S\),
\[
V(S)=\log_2 |S| \quad \text{bits}.
\]
Ashby says: “When variety is measured in the logarithmic form its unit is the ‘bit’” [PAGE 69]. For a vector with independent components,
\[
V_{\text{indep}}=\sum_i \log_2 |S_i| \quad \text{bits}.
\]
Constraint exists when
\[
V_{\text{actual}}<V_{\text{indep}}.
\]
Ashby defines constraint as the case “when the variety that exists under one condition is less than the variety that exists under another” [PAGE 70]. Breakdown regime: variety is observer-relative; the same object can support different sets of distinguishable states, and the “variety” is undefined until the set and discrimination are specified [PAGE 67].

6. Law of requisite variety. Let \(D\) be disturbance variety, \(R\) regulator variety, and \(O\) outcome variety, all in bits. Under the table condition that no column contains a repeated outcome,
\[
V_O \ge V_D - V_R.
\]
Ashby states: “only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” [PAGE 110]. If each column repeats outcomes \(k\) times, the modified bound is
\[
V_O \ge V_D - \log_2 k - V_R
\]
[PAGE 111]. Breakdown regime: the law is a combinatorial table theorem, not a claim about physical matter: “It has nothing to do with the properties of matter” [PAGE 111]. It does not forbid new devices; it forbids certain table arrangements.

7. Shannon entropy. For probabilities \(p_i\),
\[
H=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i \quad \text{bits/symbol}.
\]
Ashby defines the quantity as “– p1 log p1 – p2 log p2 – … pn log pn” and calls it “the entropy of the set of probabilities” [PAGE 94]. He explicitly restricts usage: “The word ‘entropy’ will be used in this book solely as it is used by Shannon” [PAGE 95]. For a Markov chain with transition matrix \(P_{ij}\),
\[
P_{ij}=\Pr(X_{t+1}=j\mid X_t=i),\qquad \sum_j P_{ij}=1,
\]
and the entropy rate is the equilibrium-weighted average of column entropies. Breakdown regimes: probabilities must sum to 1; transitions must be Markovian; the chain must be allowed to reach equilibrium densities [PAGE 95]. If transitions depend on earlier states, the chain is not Markovian and must be re-coded into vector states [PAGE 92].

8. Channel capacity. If a channel has \(r\) states and step duration \(\Delta t\),
\[
C=\frac{\log_2 r}{\Delta t}\quad \text{bits/s}.
\]
Ashby says a transducer with at most \(r\) states “cannot transmit Variety at more than \(\log_2 r\) bits per step” [PAGE 83]. The rate formulation is “the most natural way of measuring the capacity of a channel” [PAGE 97]. Breakdown regime: the capacity bound is a rate bound; it can be overcome in total quantity by longer sequences, but not in bits per step.

9. Equivocation and error-free transmission. If \(S\) is sent and \(R\) is received, equivocation is
\[
E=H(S,R)-H(R)\quad \text{bits/symbol},
\]
and received information is \(H(S)-E\) [PAGE 101]. Shannon’s theorem states that “Reliable messages can be transmitted over an unreliable channel” [PAGE 102], provided capacity is increased by at least \(E\), at the cost of delay. Breakdown regime: the theorem is asymptotic and requires sufficient accumulated symbols; it is not a claim that a single short message can be made error-free without redundancy or delay.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is unusually explicit in the source. Ashby begins with Wiener’s definition of cybernetics as “the science of control and communication, in the animal and the machine” [PAGE 5], then uses a geometric analogy: cybernetics stands to the real machine “much as geometry stands to a real object” [PAGE 6]. This is a retrocausal hinge: later control theory, information theory, systems biology, and machine learning often read back into Ashby a “feedback” or “information” vocabulary, but Ashby’s own move is more general and more austere. He is not primarily building a theory of servomechanisms; he is building a theory of possible machines, with terrestrial machines as special cases [PAGE 6].

The scientific genealogy then branches. One branch is Fisher and complex systems: Ashby notes that “Not until Sir Donald Fisher’s work in the ’20s” did it become clearly recognized that some complex systems “just do not allow the varying of only one factor at a time” [PAGE 7]. This matters for multi-week evaluation traps: a system that appears to have a single causal lever may actually be a coupled vector system in which intervention changes several components at once. A second branch is Shannon: Ashby explicitly says Chapter 9 is “devoted chiefly to introducing his Mathematical Theory of Communication” [PAGE 87]. The entropy and equivocation sections are therefore not Ashby-original physics; they are a careful application of Shannon to finite-state machines. A third branch is ethology and physiology: Tinbergen’s stickleback sequence is used as a determinate dynamic system [PAGE 18], and Cannon’s *Wisdom of the Body* is invoked for biological regulation [PAGE 104]. A fourth branch is mathematical structuralism: Bourbaki is cited for isomorphism and topology [PAGE 56], and the lattice of homomorphisms is used to order simplifications [PAGE 60].

Multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. Trap one: reading “entropy” as thermodynamic entropy. Ashby explicitly warns that the word is used “solely as it is used by Shannon” [PAGE 95]. Trap two: treating feedback as sufficient for instability or stability. Ashby gives a positive-feedback example that converges to equilibrium and says “arguments based on some short cut, e.g. by showing that the feedback is positive, may not be reliable” [PAGE 46]. Trap three: treating a black box’s internal diagram as unique. Ashby says “The behaviour does not specify the connexions uniquely” [PAGE 53]. Trap four: treating “memory” as a substance. Ashby says invoking memory is equivalent to declaring incomplete observation [PAGE 64]. Trap five: overgeneralizing the law of requisite variety into a physical prohibition. Ashby says it “has nothing to do with the properties of matter” [PAGE 111]. A multi-week evaluation that ignores these traps will overfit to vocabulary and miss the formal boundaries.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In Ashby’s terms, a recursive theory of mind is not a theory of introspection; it is a theory of an observer coupled to a black box, where the observer’s own distinctions become part of the system. The experimenter “is coupling himself to the Box, so that the two together form a system with feedback” [PAGE 50]. The observer collects a protocol, infers a canonical representation, and then uses that representation to predict. But the inference is always up to isomorphism or homomorphism: the observer may recover a quotient machine, not the unique internal wiring [PAGE 53]. This is the recursive loop: the observer models the box, the box’s behavior changes the observer’s distinctions, and the observer’s distinctions determine which homomorphism is seen.

Three modes are useful for adjudicating crisis behavior. First, “crisis habit regression.” Under repeated disturbance, a determinate system tends to a state of equilibrium or a cycle: “under repeated transformation, the representative point always moves either to some state at which it stops, or to some cycle around which it circulates indefinitely” [PAGE 16]. If the basin is maladaptive, the system can be stable in an undesirable state. Ashby warns: “Stability is commonly thought of as desirable… Nevertheless, stability is not always good” [PAGE 46]. In a crisis, an agent may regress to a low-variety habitual basin because that basin is stable under the current displacement class, even though it is not within the desired physiological or behavioral set. The observer with coarse discrimination sees only a homomorphism and may mistake the habitual stable pattern for successful regulation.

Second, “counter-trap.” A regulator R facing disturbance D must supply at least enough variety to reduce outcome variety into the acceptable set. The law of requisite variety gives the formal counter-trap: if \(V_D\) is large and \(V_R\) is small, then \(V_O\ge V_D-V_R\), so the outcome cannot be confined to a small acceptable set [PAGE 110]. In recursive terms, the agent’s model of the opponent/environment must preserve distinctions that matter; if it collapses them by a many-one homomorphism, it loses requisite variety. The counter-trap is not “try harder”; it is “increase the regulator’s distinguishable responses or accept a larger outcome variety.” The source also warns that the table condition matters: repeated outcomes per column modify the bound by \(\log_2 k\) [PAGE 111].

Third, “clock.” A clock is a deterministic cycle: \(T:n\mapsto n+k\) is the canonical clock transformation [PAGE 17], and a cycle is “a sequence of states such that repeated application of the transformation takes the representative point repeatedly round the sequence” [PAGE 43]. A clock is stable and predictable, but it is not adaptive regulation. The traffic-light example is instructive: the lights show only four combinations out of eight possible, so there is constraint/redundancy [PAGE 70], and the sequence can be re-coded to fewer components without loss of variety [PAGE 97–98]. In a crisis, a clock-like routine can preserve order but cannot respond to novel disturbance unless its parameter inputs or state set are enlarged. Thus the recursive theory of mind must distinguish: habitual regression to a stable basin, counter-trap escalation of regulator variety, and clock-like periodic behavior that is stable but non-adaptive.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof follows directly from Ashby’s equilibrium theorem. Let the whole state be a vector \(x=(x_p,x_b,x_w)\), where \(p\) denotes physical constraints, \(b\) biomechanical/actuator constraints, and \(w\) weather/environmental disturbance. A whole equilibrium requires
\[
T(x)=x.
\]
Ashby states: “the whole is at a state of equilibrium if and only if each part is at a state of equilibrium in the conditions provided by the other part” [PAGE 82]. Equivalently, each part has a “power of veto”: “No state (of the whole) can be a state of equilibrium unless it is acceptable to every one of the component parts, each acting in the conditions given by the others” [PAGE 47]. Therefore, if any layer’s local equilibrium set excludes the desired set \(\eta\), the whole cannot equilibrate in \(\eta\). This is the formal veto.

The physical layer vetoes by restricting the admissible table. If a proposed regulator requires a transition that is not in the physical table, it is not a possible \(R\). The biomechanical layer vetoes by limiting the number or rate of distinguishable regulator states: if the actuator/channel has at most \(r\) states and step time \(\Delta t\), its capacity is bounded by
\[
C\le \frac{\log_2 r}{\Delta t}\quad \text{bits/s}
\]
[PAGE 83, PAGE 97]. If the disturbance’s required regulation rate exceeds this, no control law can transmit enough variety per unit time. The weather layer vetoes by increasing \(V_D\): if \(V_D-V_R>\log_2|\eta|\), then \(V_O\ge V_D-V_R>\log_2|\eta|\), so the outcome cannot be confined to \(\eta\) [PAGE 110]. This is a proof of impossibility, not a heuristic. A proposed intervention that claims to hold \(\eta\) while violating the bound is vetoed by the table, regardless of material implementation.

The adjudication procedure is therefore: (1) specify the state vector and the desired set \(\eta\); (2) enumerate or bound \(V_D\), \(V_R\), and the physical table; (3) check the local equilibrium condition for each layer; (4) check the requisite-variety bound; (5) check channel capacity/rate. If any layer vetoes, the correct conclusion is not “the regulator is weak” but “the desired equilibrium is not admissible under the stated coupling.” This matches Ashby’s caution that a “system” is a list of variables chosen for an interest, not a unique material thing [PAGE 24–25].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Closure veto. Intervention: take a proposed machine trajectory and attempt to apply its transformation one step beyond the last recorded state. Prediction: if the transformation is unclosed at that operand, the next state is undefined and the trajectory cannot be extended. Veto condition: any claim of indefinite deterministic prediction from an unclosed \(T\). Source anchor: “the transformation that represents a machine must be closed” [PAGE 19]. Verifiable observable: the protocol terminates or requires a new operand definition; \(T^{n+1}\) cannot be computed without extending \(S\).

Scenario 2: Requisite-variety veto. Intervention: construct a disturbance-regulator-outcome table with no repeated outcome in any column, with \(|D|=9\), \(|R|=3\), and desired outcome set \(|\eta|=2\). Prediction: \(V_O\ge \log_2 9-\log_2 3=\log_2 3\approx 1.585\) bits, so \(|O|\ge 3\), and the outcome cannot be confined to two states. Veto condition: any regulator claiming \(|\eta|=2\) under those table conditions. Source anchor: “only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” [PAGE 110]. Verifiable observable: exhaustive marking of one cell per row will produce at least three distinct outcome symbols; if not, the table violates the no-repetition condition or the claimed \(R\) variety is larger than stated.

Scenario 3: Markov/entropy-assumption veto. Intervention: take a protocol in which the probability of a transition from \(B\) to \(A\) differs depending on whether the preceding state was \(A\) or \(B\). Prediction: a first-order Markov entropy estimate is invalid; the system must be re-coded into vector states such as \((X_{t-1},X_t)\), or the observer must retain a longer history. Veto condition: any channel-capacity or entropy-rate claim that ignores the prior-state dependence. Source anchors: Ashby’s re-coding procedure says the system “can be made so by a method… one re-defines the system” [PAGE 92], and Shannon’s assumptions require Markovian transitions and equilibrial weighting [PAGE 95]. Verifiable observable: conditioning the transition counts on the previous state changes the estimated probabilities; after pair-state re-coding, the new transition table becomes single-valued or Markov-stable. If the re-coded table still depends on earlier history, increase the memory order until the dependence disappears.
