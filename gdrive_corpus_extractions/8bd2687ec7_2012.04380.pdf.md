The retrocausal genealogy creates multi-week traps. First, preview narratives lag team state: a managerial change, injury recovery, or tactical shift may be visible in official team sheets before it is stabilised in journalistic language. A model trained on older previews can learn the old regime and then misread the first weeks of a new regime. Second, random 80/20 splitting across all 1770 games [PAGE 4] is weaker than season-forward evaluation because it allows same-season context to appear on both sides. Fourth, journalistic reputation and narrative herding can create multi-week autocorrelation: repeated phrases about a “crisis” or a “resurgent” team may be learned as team-name tokens rather than as causal state. The correct genealogy audit is therefore to hold out entire seasons, timestamp every article, and test whether the text gain survives when team-name memorisation and narrative repetition are controlled.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source model does not explicitly represent recursive theory of mind; it reads a journalist’s expressed model of a team. The allocation probability \(p(f(s)\mid \alpha)\) is a belief attribution: the journalist believes a sentence is about team \(\alpha\), and the model treats that attribution as a feature. The deeper recursion is that the journalist may be modelling the manager, the manager may be modelling the opponent, and the opponent may be modelling the journalist’s narrative. The baseline flattens this recursion into sentence vectors and counts, so it can capture expressed confidence, crisis language, injury news, and motivation, but it cannot distinguish first-order belief from second-order counter-belief.

Three failure modes follow. Crisis habit regression: under relegation threat, injury crisis, or managerial pressure, a team may regress to familiar habits rather than adapt. Journalists often encode this as “more to play for” or “must win,” but the same language can accompany a team that is tactically stuck. The model may learn a positive crisis signal when the true causal path is habitual and exploitable. Counter-trap: if the opponent expects the habit, the same preview language flips sign; the text says “team \(\alpha\) will fight,” but the opponent has prepared for exactly that pattern. The baseline has no explicit interaction between team \(\alpha\)’s habit and team \(\beta\)’s expectation, so it cannot adjudicate the flip. Clock: late-season time pressure changes incentives. The paper’s quote that a relegation-threatened team “may have more to play for” [PAGE 4] is a clock variable, but the baseline does not include an explicit gameweek-pressure feature; the 2.23% week-1-to-week-38 improvement [PAGE 4] may be a clock effect, a narrative effect, or a data-accumulation effect. A counter-trap audit would require interaction features for manager change, opponent style, and competition context; without them, the model’s human-expert signal is a plausible but under-identified proxy for recursive strategic belief.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source itself lists cross-layer uncertainties: “There are also a range of uncertainties that can inﬂuence match results including the team conﬁgurations, the health of players, the location of the match (home or away), the weather, and team strategies” [PAGE 2]. The baseline feature vector \(X=[\mu V(\alpha),V(\beta)]\) contains text-derived coordinates only. Therefore physical, biomechanical, and weather variables are omitted variables unless they happen to be mentioned in the preview. A cross-layer veto is justified when an omitted variable is necessary for the outcome to be in the model’s admissible domain.

Let \(N_\alpha\) and \(N_\beta\) be available players, \(M_\alpha\) and \(M_\beta\) be cumulative match minutes over a fixed window in minutes, \(R_\alpha\) and \(R_\beta\) be recovery days, \(W\) be wind speed in metres per second, \(P\) be precipitation in millimetres per hour, and \(T\) be temperature in degrees Celsius. A pre-registered feasibility domain can be written as
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{N_\alpha\ge 11,\;N_\beta\ge 11,\;W\le W_{\max},\;P\le P_{\max},\;T_{\min}\le T\le T_{\max},\;R_\alpha\ge R_{\min},\;R_\beta\ge R_{\min}\}.
\]
If \(\mathcal{D}\) is false, the text-only prediction \(\hat y\) is inadmissible. The proof is conditional and direct: the Random Forest computes \(q(y\mid X)\), but \(X\) has no coordinate for a late team-sheet exclusion, a referee safety decision, or a biomechanical overload that is absent from the preview. If the omitted variable changes the feasible outcome set, then \(q(y\mid X)\) is not the conditional distribution of interest; it is a distribution conditional on stale text. Physical veto therefore dominates text evidence. A player unavailable below the 11-player minimum, or a match postponed for unsafe weather, makes the outcome undefined or outside the model’s domain. A biomechanical overload measured in minutes and recovery days shifts the performance distribution even when the text still says “strong team.” Weather measured in wind speed and precipitation can suppress open play and increase variance even when the narrative is unchanged. The adjudication rule is: text supplies a prior; physical and environmental layers can veto, recondition, or force abstention; they cannot be overridden by a higher text probability.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
A verifiable-reward veto rule can be stated as
\[
R_{\mathrm{veto}}=
\mathbf{1}[V=1 \land a\in\{\text{abstain},\text{recompute}\}]
+\mathbf{1}[V=0 \land a=\text{predict}]
-\lambda\,\mathbf{1}[V=1 \land a=\text{confident predict}],
\]
where \(V\in\{0,1\}\) is a verified veto flag and \(R_{\mathrm{veto}}\) is dimensionless. The three scenarios below are verifiable from official records and are designed to test whether the model knows when its text-only signal is invalid.

Scenario 1: late team-sheet deviation. Verifiable evidence is the official team sheet compared with the preview used by the model. Veto condition: a key player named as available in the preview is absent, or the confirmed formation removes a role that the preview’s text treats as central. Prediction: on the veto subset, the model should abstain or recompute; if forced to predict, its confidence must be lower than on matched non-veto games. A text-only model that remains confident after a late exclusion fails the reward test.

Scenario 2: weather or pitch veto. Verifiable evidence is match-day wind speed, precipitation, temperature, and the official pitch inspection. Veto condition: \(W>W_{\max}\), \(P>P_{\max}\), or an unsafe-pitch declaration. Prediction: text-derived home-win confidence is inadmissible; the model should abstain or recondition toward a higher-variance outcome distribution. The paper’s own list of uncertainties includes “the weather” [PAGE 2], so this is a source-grounded veto, not an external invention.

Scenario 3: fixture-congestion and travel veto. Verifiable evidence is the official fixture list, player minutes, and travel distance. Veto condition: \(M_\alpha>M_{\max}\), \(R_\alpha<R_{\min}\), or travel beyond a pre-registered distance threshold for the relevant team. Prediction: the model should downweight a text signal that says “strong team” when the verified physical load contradicts it; on the veto subset, abstention or reconditioning should be rewarded, and confident prediction should be penalised. The paper notes that “Injuries and rotation also play a big part later in the season” [PAGE 4], which makes this a natural counterfactual test of whether the baseline’s text features are being treated as physical evidence rather than as a narrative proxy.
