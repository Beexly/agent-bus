<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs through price discrimination, revenue management, secondary-market theory, and information disclosure. The oldest layer is Pigou-style second-degree price discrimination: seat categories sort customers by willingness to pay, and Rosen and Rosenfield show that returns depend on willingness-to-pay variance across seat categories [PAGE 3]. Courty and Pagliero add concert-industry evidence, and Leslie’s Broadway study finds revenue improvements from seat-based pricing without large aggregate welfare changes [PAGE 3]. The paper’s twist is to move from vertical seat quality to horizontal event quality: different games in one season are the differentiated products [PAGE 3]. That genealogical move creates the key backdoor: if teams switch because their schedule or local demand is changing, the pricing switch is endogenous.

The second lineage is variable and dynamic pricing. The paper cites peak-load utility pricing, surge pricing, congestion pricing, and advance-selling models [PAGE 4]. It positions itself against Arslan et al.’s college-football pricing tool and Xu et al.’s dynamic-pricing forecasting work, arguing that empirical evidence on variable pricing is thin [PAGE 4]. The retrotausal trap is that a team might switch precisely when it expects demand to improve; hence the paper’s DiD with propensity-score weighting is not decorative, it is the historical correction for strategic selection [PAGE 2; PAGE 11].

The third lineage is secondary-market theory. The paper contrasts cannibalization with option value. In the source’s words: “When the seats are likely to sell out, customers can be reasonably certain that they can resell their tickets in the resale market. This option to resell provides additional value and can encourage customers to purchase tickets from the primary market, which is defined as the optionvalue effect. When sell-out is unlikely, customers can easily anticipate that tickets will be available in the resale market at lower prices. In that case, the cannibalization effect will take hold” [PAGE 5]. That lineage descends through Su, Cui et al., Sweeting, Leslie and Sorensen, Lewis et al., and Zou and Jiang [PAGE 5]. The multi-week trap is that resale activity is not a one-shot market: season-ticket holders can list later, single-game buyers can wait, and the team’s primary sales can be affected by what buyers believe other buyers will do.

The fourth lineage is information disclosure and quality signaling. Bagwell and Riordan’s high-price quality signal is the ancestor of the paper’s claim that a high-priced game signals attractiveness, while availability-information papers by Gaur and Park, Allon and Bassamboo, Cui and Shin, Gallino and Moreno, Tereyagoglu et al., and Cui et al. supply the disclosure analogy [PAGE 5]. The paper’s contribution is to treat the price schedule itself as a disclosed belief about game quality [PAGE 5].

Multi-week traps are structural in the NFL setting. “Each team plays 16 games (8 home and 8 away) in the regular season which runs from early September to late December” [PAGE 6], and the analysis focuses only on regular-season games because playoff pricing is conditional on appearance [PAGE 6]. The design absorbs 17 regular-season week indicators [PAGE 12] and checks pre-treatment trends over 34 weeks across 2012–2013 [PAGE 14]. Resale snapshots are taken on August 1 and September 1 [PAGE 8], so the resale equations are pre-season views, not week-by-week resale dynamics. The paper also excludes the 49ers’ new stadium, the Vikings’ temporary stadium after the Metrodome roof collapse, and the Titans’ broker issue [PAGE 9], plus nine games affected by international hosting or extreme weather [PAGE 9–10]. Those exclusions are the historical guardrails against venue and schedule shocks masquerading as pricing effects.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive belief structure has three layers: team belief about game attractiveness, customer belief about team belief, and customer belief about other customers’ beliefs. The team knows its schedule and local demand; it sets high or low prices. A low price says “this game is less attractive,” and “A low-priced event will signal that an event is less attractive and can prevent the deferral of purchases to the secondary market” [PAGE 2]. A high price says “this game is more attractive,” and “A high-priced event will signal that an event is more attractive and can activate the option-value effect” [PAGE 2]. Customers then infer quality and infer expected sell-out pressure. If they expect other customers to want the game, the resale option becomes valuable; if they expect no sell-out, they wait for resale relief. That is the paper’s mechanism: variable pricing changes the belief hierarchy, not merely the price level.

**Crisis habit regression.** Under fixed pricing, the customer’s crisis habit is deferral: when uncertain, wait for the resale market to avoid overpaying for an unattractive game. The paper states that price-sensitive customers “were more likely to defer their purchases to the resale market to avoid paying higher prices for games with unpredictable attractiveness” [PAGE 3]. Variable pricing interrupts that habit because the price schedule reveals the team’s own forecast. The evidence is demographic and game-quality heterogeneity: the effect is stronger in lower-income and higher-income-diversity hometowns, and stronger for less attractive games [PAGE 15; PAGE 16]. That is exactly where the fixed-price deferral habit should be strongest.

**Counter-trap.** The counter-trap is resale cannibalization: if variable pricing pulls more buyers into the primary market, those buyers can later list tickets and undercut the team. The paper tests this. For less attractive games, listings rise by about 646, but the minimum resale price does not fall: the coefficient is \(-0.0784\), \(p>.10\) [PAGE 20]. The source concludes that “the increase in the number of ticket listings in the resale market for the less attractive games does not seem to be a threat” [PAGE 20]. For attractive games, listings fall by about 435 and the minimum resale price rises by \(0.2370\) log-points, \(p<.001\) [PAGE 19; PAGE 20]. The recursive interpretation is that buyers see high primary prices as a popularity signal, buy early for the option value, and list at higher prices later; the counter-trap does not fire because the resale price floor does not collapse.

**Clock.** The clock layer is timing. The primary decision is made before the season, while the resale snapshots are August 1 and September 1 [PAGE 8]. The model absorbs 17 week fixed effects [PAGE 12] and season fixed effects, so common week shocks are removed; the pre-trend test covers 34 weeks [PAGE 14]. But the clock also limits the claim: the paper does not observe late-season resale cascades, and it excludes playoff pricing [PAGE 6]. The recursive theory therefore predicts a specific pattern: high-price attractive games should show stable primary sales and higher resale floors; low-price unattractive games should show higher primary sales and more listings without a lower resale floor. That is precisely the reported pattern [PAGE 18–20].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical/venue veto.** The physical layer is seat capacity and venue identity. The paper checks whether capacity or average price moved at the switch: “The difference between average seating capacities of teams across 2013 and 2014 is only 97.58, which is not statistically significant (p > .10)” [PAGE 10], and “the difference between average ticket prices of teams across 2013 and 2014 is only 1.38, which is not statistically significant (p > .10)” [PAGE 10]. It also excludes venue disruptions: the 49ers moved to Levi’s Stadium, the Vikings moved after the Metrodome roof collapsed, and the Titans had a broker-related sellout issue; “we excluded the home games of these three teams from our dataset” [PAGE 9]. If a venue shock were driving the 2.95%, the exclusions and capacity controls would have to fail simultaneously, and the pre-trend and placebo checks would have to pass by coincidence. They do not provide that pattern.

**Biomechanical veto.** There is no biomechanical treatment in this paper: the intervention is an organizational pricing schedule, not a player-load, injury, or movement intervention. A biomechanical veto would require a team-game injury or performance shock that is correlated with the switch and not absorbed by team fixed effects, opponent fixed effects, week fixed effects, season fixed effects, and team/game controls. The paper includes team- and game-related controls and fixed effects [PAGE 13], and the placebo switch is insignificant [PAGE 14]. That does not prove biomechanics are irrelevant to attendance, but it does mean a biomechanical story is not a competing identified operator in this design. The honest veto condition is: if treated teams had a treatment-timed injury shock that changed home attendance, the DiD would be biased; the paper’s pre-trend and placebo checks reduce but do not eliminate that possibility.

**Weather veto.** Weather is explicitly handled by exclusion. The source excludes games moved for international hosting or “unexpected extreme weather (i.e., a snow storm)” and reports “We found nine such games and excluded them” [PAGE 9]. It also identifies the Jets–Bills game played in Detroit “due to a snow storm in Buffalo” [PAGE 10]. Week fixed effects absorb common weather weeks [PAGE 12], and venue exclusions remove the roof-collapse case [PAGE 9]. A weather veto would require a treatment-correlated weather shock concentrated in treated home games after the switch, surviving the nine-game exclusion and week effects. The paper’s exclusions make that a narrow residual, not a demonstrated alternative. The cross-layer verdict is therefore: physical and weather layers are explicitly vetoed where observable; biomechanical layers are not part of the identified treatment and would need a new treatment-correlated injury measure to become a serious rival.

**Veto 1: pre-trend failure.** Verifiable check: estimate the pre-treatment interaction \(\lambda_2\) in the primary-sales trend equation over 2012–2013. Decision rule: if \(\lambda_2\) is statistically significant at the 5% level, veto the causal interpretation of the 2.95% effect. Prediction under the paper: \(\lambda^K_2=0.0002\), \(p>.10\), so this veto does not fire [PAGE 14]. If a replication finds a significant pre-trend, the correct prediction is that the DiD estimate is contaminated by differential team trajectories, and the 2.95% claim should be withdrawn until a stronger design is used.

**Veto 2: placebo-switch failure.** Verifiable check: relabel 2013 as a placebo switch for the eventual treatment teams and estimate the placebo \(VP\) coefficient. Decision rule: if the placebo coefficient is significant at the 5% level, veto the claim that the 2014 switch is the causal driver. Prediction under the paper: placebo \(=0.0104\), \(p>.10\), so this veto does not fire [PAGE 14]. If a replication finds a significant placebo, the prediction is that unobserved team-specific demand shifts, not variable pricing, explain the primary-sales increase.

**Veto 3: resale cannibalization failure.** Verifiable check: estimate the minimum-resale-price equation for less attractive games. Decision rule: if \(\beta^{MRP}\) is significantly negative, then the extra resale listings are undercutting the primary market, the counter-trap has fired, and the “no cannibalization” conclusion is vetoed. Prediction under the paper: \(\beta^{MRP}=-0.0784\), \(p>.10\), so this veto does not fire [PAGE 20]. A fourth auxiliary check would be the attractiveness classifier’s true-positive rate: if it falls materially below the reported 85.4% [PAGE 16], the attractiveness interactions become too noisy to support the mechanism, and the quality-signaling claim should be downgraded.
