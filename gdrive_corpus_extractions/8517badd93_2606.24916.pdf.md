The paper's spine is a single substitution: replace unweighted block-MSE (Eq. 14, [PAGE 4]) with latitude-weighted WS-MSE (Eq. 18, [PAGE 5]) inside the classic Kim–Kyung bit-truncation constraint. The multi-hop question is whether that substitution is (a) mathematically valid, (b) hardware-free, and (c) responsible for the headline savings. Tracing (a): the original constraint (Eq. 15) enforces $\log_{10}\sum_i\sum_t(\hat y_{i,t}-\hat x_i^*)^2 \le \xi_b$ with $\xi_b = 2\log_{10}255 + \log_{10}|B_b| - \theta_b/10$ (Eq. 16). I verified this is algebraically equivalent to $\mathrm{PSNR}\ge\theta_b$: $\mathrm{PSNR}=10\log_{10}(255^2/\mathrm{MSE})\ge\theta_b \Leftrightarrow \log_{10}\mathrm{MSE}+\theta_b/10\le 2\log_{10}255$, and since $\log_{10}\mathrm{MSE}=\log_{10}\Sigma-\log_{10}|B_b|$, rearrangement gives Eq. 16 exactly. The weighted version (Eq. 20) simply swaps $|B_b|\to W_b=\sum_i\cos\phi_i$ (Eq. 17) and inserts $w_i$ inside the sum — a clean, minimal generalization.

Tracing (b), the crux is Theorem 2 ([PAGE 5]): does $w_i$ change the optimal dummy pattern? The per-pixel cost $f(\hat x_i)=w_i\,\mathbb E[(\hat Y_i-\hat x_i)^2]$ has derivative $-2w_i(\mathbb E[\hat Y_i]-\hat x_i)$ (Eq. 22). Because $w_i=\cos\phi_i$ is a per-pixel positive constant, it factors out and the minimizer stays $\hat x_i^*=\mathbb E[\hat Y_i]\to$ binary $10\ldots0$. This is correct but almost trivial: a scalar multiplier cannot move the argmin of a convex parabola. The claim only fails at the poles where $w_i=\cos(\pi/2)=0$ — there the cost is identically zero and the optimum is undefined, which the paper quietly excludes ("for all non-polar ERP pixels (i.e., $|\phi_i|<\pi/2)$").

Tracing (c) exposes the gray zone. The Jensen argument (Eq. 23, [PAGE 5]) is valid: $\varphi=\!-\log$ is convex, so $\mathbb E[\mathrm{WS\text{-}PSNR}]\ge \mathrm{WS\text{-}PSNR}(\mathbb E[\mathrm{WS\text{-}MSE}])\ge\theta_b$. But this is a *conservative* (sufficient, not tight) bound — enforcing it may leave power on the table because the gap grows with $\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{WS\text{-}MSE})$, i.e., with texture. High-texture border tiles get truncated less than theoretically optimal. This partially explains why measured SPORT-B (47.9%) sits *below* the fixed-level theoretical 48.1% (Eq. 6, [PAGE 3]) despite adaptivity. The abstract's "3.1 percentage points more than a PSNR-based optimizer" ([PAGE 1]) directly contradicts Finding 4 ([PAGE 9]): "both … achieve 51.6% … Despite equal efficiency." I could find no table cell supporting 3.1 pp; Tables V and VIII show B4 = S1 = 51.6%. That is a live internal contradiction, likely a stale number.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model three "agents" whose incentives can conflict. (1) The optimizer's belief: it trusts $\mathbb E[\mathrm{WS\text{-}MSE}_b]\le\xi_b'$ (Eq. 20) as a guarantee of true WS-PSNR. This is safe only via Jensen (Eq. 23); its private failure mode is over-truncation on high-variance blocks, since the bound is one-sided and conservative. (2) The gaze predictor's belief: linear extrapolation holds "for prediction horizons below 100 ms" ([PAGE 2], citing [18],[19]). At the 9.33 ms horizon this is well-supported, but the *habit-regression* risk is fast, non-linear head flicks (saccadic onset), where angular acceleration violates the constant-$\omega$ assumption. The paper's counter-trap is the Border guard-band (45–60°) plus a "1.5 dB safety margin for gaze prediction error" ([PAGE 6]) — a deliberate hedge that trades power for robustness against its own predictor's model error. (3) The hardware's belief: it assumes $10\ldots0$ is optimal regardless of content (Theorem 2), which is true per-pixel but ignores that the *decision* of which $t_b$ to use depends on training data $\{\hat y_{i,t}\}$; content outside the training distribution (e.g., adversarial high-frequency equatorial texture in the FoV) could push a tile to a truncation that passes the expected-MSE test yet fails on a specific frame. The CDFs (Fig. 4, [PAGE 10]) are the empirical defense — curves "lie entirely to the right of the threshold … in every panel" over 100 frames — but 100 frames is a thin sample against the "multi-week" reliability a shipping headset needs. Recursive risk: each layer's safety margin is calibrated against the *previous* layer's nominal behavior, so a simultaneous worst case (max head velocity + high texture + polar-adjacent tile) is not jointly bounded in the paper.
