This source is a construct-validity audit of a 360° video decision-making task against a field task in highly skilled rugby union players. The multi-hop inference is: (1) expert decision-making is theoretically tied to perceptual-cognitive-motor skill and representative task design; (2) the authors created a 360° video task from four off-the-ruck play variations, with three attackers, three defenders, a scrumhalf, and a receiver, filming from the decision-maker’s viewpoint at 180 cm [PAGE 4]; (3) they compared 32 test trials per participant in 360° video to previously collected field-test data using the same play scenarios [PAGE 7]; (4) they used Bland–Altman agreement plus an ordinal GEE for modality effects [PAGE 7]; (5) they supplemented the quantitative comparison with ITC-SOPI presence scores and reflexive thematic analysis of interviews [PAGE 7–8]. The sample was fifteen advanced and expert players, including state academy, professional academy, professional senior, and international players, aged 18–30 years, with 3–23 years of competitive rugby [PAGE 3]. Twelve had never used VR, two had used it once, and one twice [PAGE 3]. This matters because novelty and limited prior HMD exposure could inflate or distort presence and performance.

The central gray-zone audit concerns the apparent tension between “agreement” and a statistically significant modality difference. The abstract states: “Bland–Altman analysis confirmed that 360° video and field tests measured decision-making skill” [PAGE 1]. Page 8 states: “Therefore, the 360° video and field tests measured the same construct (i.e., decision-making).” [PAGE 8]. Yet the same page reports: “The GEE indicated there was a significant difference in decision-making between the 360° video and field task, χ2 (1) = 8.13, p = 0.004.” [PAGE 8]. These are not logically contradictory: agreement concerns bias and limits of agreement, while a GEE tests a population-average modality effect. But the reported Bland–Altman limits are very wide relative to the 0–3 scoring scale: mean -0.27, lower limit -3.31 with 95% CI -3.62 to -3.05, and upper limit 2.78 with 95% CI 2.51 to 3.08 [PAGE 8]. On a 0–3 ordinal score, a lower limit near -3.31 and an upper limit near 2.78 span more than the entire possible score range. That makes “between the limits of agreement” a weak criterion, because almost any difference could fall inside such limits. The paper also reports an outlier, case 9, “which suggests that this participant was more inconsistent in their decision-making performance across test modalities than other participants” [PAGE 8]. The outlier and the wide limits are the main quantitative gray zones.

A second gray zone is metric slippage. The scoring system is ordinal 0–3, produced by expert coaches ranking options until “100% agreement was reached” [PAGE 5]. But the results also report mean response accuracy as 81.32% in 360° video versus 72.43% in the field, with a “9% absolute difference” [PAGE 8]. Percent accuracy and 0–3 ordinal scores are related but not identical quantities. The Bland–Altman analysis uses “decision-making performance scores (0–3)” [PAGE 7], while the GEE and accuracy comparison use a different summary. The paper does not fully reconcile these metrics. A third gray zone is sign convention: page 7 says differences were examined as “(360° video minus field)” and also describes the y-axis as “difference (field– 360° video)” [PAGE 7]. The reported mean of -0.27 is consistent with field minus 360° video if 360° video performance was higher, but the text is ambiguous. This is a reproducibility issue, not a fatal one.

A fourth gray zone is design control versus ecological validity. The use of the same play scenarios across modalities is a strength for comparability, but it also creates possible stimulus familiarity. The authors acknowledge that three participants involved in filming also completed the tests, and that this could not be completely avoided without compromising sample size [PAGE 4]. The test matrix was 32 trials, with eight familiarisation trials, and the order of play variations was randomised with left/right passing counterbalanced [PAGE 4–5]. That reduces order effects but does not eliminate memorisation or expert schema activation across repeated exposures. A fifth gray zone is the perception-only design: participants could not interact with the ball or opponents [PAGE 11]. The field task is perception-action coupled. The authors argue that “representative perceptual information has been reported to be crucial for expert decision-making” [PAGE 11], which is a reasonable mechanistic defence, but it does not prove equivalence of the underlying visual search or motor-planning processes. The paper itself says it is “unknown whether the limited field of view in the HMD altered the underlying mechanisms (e.g., visual search, use of peripheral vision)” [PAGE 12]. A sixth gray zone is statistical: the GEE treats 15 players as repeated clusters, with 960 total observations across modalities. GEE is appropriate for repeated ordinal data, but small cluster counts, sparse ordinal categories, and unreported proportional-odds or working-correlation assumptions limit certainty. The qualitative analysis is constructionist and reflexive, so it supports perceived representativeness, not population-level validity. Overall, the source supports “similar but not identical” decision-making performance, with a small absolute difference and wide agreement limits; it does not support strong claims of interchangeable modalities or automatic training transfer.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source does not present a single formal generative model of decision-making. It presents measurement operators: ordinal expert scoring, Bland–Altman agreement, an ordinal GEE, power analysis, and ITC-SOPI factor means. Formalising them exposes assumptions and breakdown regimes.

First, the decision score operator is an expert-ranked ordinal map. For play \(r\), option set \(O_r=\{A,B,C,\text{Run}\}\), the coaches assign
\[
S_r:O_r\rightarrow\{0,1,2,3\},
\]
where 3 is best and 0 least favourable, following consensus ranking “until 100% agreement was reached” [PAGE 5]. The unit is ordinal score points, not interval units. The source states: “The best decision was awarded the highest score of three, the next best decision was awarded a score of two, the third best decision was awarded a score of one, and the least favourable decision was awarded a score of zero” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown occurs if the expert ranking is not unidimensional, if play-specific option values are non-transitive, or if the 0–3 spacing is treated as equal-interval across plays without justification.

Second, the reported mean response accuracy can be written as
\[
\hat{p}_{m}=\frac{1}{N T_m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T_m}\mathbf{1}\{S_{i,t,m}=3\},
\]
where \(m\in\{\text{360° video},\text{field}\}\), \(N=15\), and \(T_m=32\) test trials per participant. The unit is a dimensionless proportion; multiplying by 100 gives percent. The source reports \(M=81.32\%\), \(SD=35.79\%\) for 360° video and \(M=72.43\%\), \(SD=39.15\%\) for field, with a “9% absolute difference” [PAGE 8]. The 35–39% SD values indicate substantial trial-level or participant-level dispersion; the exact variance decomposition is not reported. A breakdown regime is treating these percentages as interval-scale latent ability without a trial-level model.

Third, the Bland–Altman operator. Let \(S_{i,F}\) and \(S_{i,V}\) be the field and 360° video scores for participant \(i\) on a given play or summary. Define the difference and mean:
\[
d_i=S_{i,F}-S_{i,V},\qquad m_i=\frac{S_{i,F}+S_{i,V}}{2}.
\]
The mean bias and standard deviation are
\[
\bar d=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_i,\qquad s_d=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_i-\bar d)^2},
\]
and the 95% limits of agreement are
\[
\mathrm{LoA}_{\pm}=\bar d\pm 1.96\,s_d.
\]
Units are score points on the 0–3 ordinal scale. The source reports \(\bar d=-0.27\), lower LoA \(=-3.31\) with 95% CI \(-3.62\) to \(-3.05\), upper LoA \(=2.78\) with 95% CI \(2.51\) to \(3.08\) [PAGE 8]. The paper says: “A Bland–Altman analysis (Bland and Altman 1986) was performed” [PAGE 7]. Assumptions include independent differences, approximately normal differences, homogeneous variance across the measurement range, and no proportional bias. Exact breakdown/singularity regimes: if \(s_d=0\), the LoA collapse to \(\bar d\) and agreement is degenerate; if differences are heteroscedastic, LoA are not constant; if the scores are ordinal, the arithmetic mean and SD are approximations; if repeated plays are nested within players, independence is violated. A critical audit point is that a LoA span of \(2.78-(-3.31)=6.09\) score points exceeds the 0–3 score range, so the LoA are not narrow equivalence bounds.

Fourth, the ordinal GEE operator. For participant \(i\), trial \(j\), modality \(M_j\in\{0,1\}\), and ordinal category \(k=1,2,3\), a cumulative-logit marginal model is
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(Y_{ij}\leq k)=\alpha_k+\beta M_j,
\]
with \(\beta\) in log-odds units, \(\exp(\beta)\) an odds ratio, and player \(i\) as the repeated cluster. The source states: “Differences in decision-making performance scores between 360° video and field were examined using a generalised estimating equation (GEE)” [PAGE 7]. The reported test is \(\chi^2(1)=8.13,\ p=0.004\) [PAGE 8]. Assumptions include correct marginal link, working correlation specification, missing-at-random data, and stable cluster-level dependence. Breakdown regimes: separation or all responses in one category makes \(\beta\) non-identifiable; very small cluster counts weaken robust variance estimates; correlation misspecification can bias standard errors; violation of proportional odds changes interpretation. The source does not report the working correlation structure or proportional-odds diagnostics.

Fifth, power analysis. The source says a priori power analysis in G*Power with \(\alpha=0.05\), 80% power, and 95% confidence interval for a repeated-measures analysis indicated that 64 trials per participant across both modalities, 960 total, could detect a small effect \(f=0.14\) [PAGE 3–4]. Here \(f\) is Cohen’s \(f\), dimensionless. Assumptions include the specified repeated-measures design, effect-size definition, and sphericity or appropriate correction. Breakdown occurs if the design is unbalanced, if attrition is non-random, or if the trial-level observations are more dependent than assumed. No information-theoretic quantity is reported: the source gives no Shannon entropy, mutual information, bits, or nats. Any such quantity would be synthetic and is not derived here.

Sixth, ITC-SOPI factor means. For factor \(f\) with item set \(I_f\), participant \(i\), and Likert response \(L_{iq}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}\),
\[
\bar L_{if}=\frac{1}{|I_f|}\sum_{q\in I_f}L_{iq}.
\]
The unit is Likert points, treated descriptively as interval. The source reports Spatial Presence \(M=3.29,\ SD=0.72\), Engagement \(M=3.57,\ SD=0.48\), Ecological Validity \(M=3.65,\ SD=0.87\), and Negative Effects \(M=1.84,\ SD=0.69\) [PAGE 8]. It also reports Cronbach’s alphas for the four factors [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regimes include ceiling/floor effects, ordinal misuse, and factor-structure instability. The source does not report item-level distributions.

Seventh, geometry and timing operators. The camera was at 180 cm [PAGE 4], with 30 frames per second [PAGE 4]. Trial timing: a 1 s number, a 1 s still frame, a 5 s intertrial interval, and an audible tone 4 s into the ITI [PAGE 5]. HMD field of view is described as “typically 90–110 degrees diagonally and 70–90 degrees vertically” [PAGE 12]. Units are cm, frames per second, seconds, and degrees. A geometric breakdown regime occurs if camera height, interpupillary geometry, or headset fit changes the optical viewpoint enough to alter apparent attacker spacing; the source notes the camera height “may have altered the perspective of visual-perception” but was not raised as a concern by players [PAGE 12].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from laboratory video simulation to immersive virtual environments and then to 360° video validation. The source states that “two perceptual-cognitive-motor skills that distinguish experts from lesser-skilled performers are visual anticipation and decision-making” [PAGE 1]. It also states: “The majority of research investigating decision-making in sport has used two-dimensional video simulation” [PAGE 2]. That lineage established expert choice-response superiority but left open whether immersive displays preserve representative information. The paper positions 360° video between two-dimensional video and fully animated virtual reality: 360° video is “more accessible and cost effective as it does not require programming expertise” [PAGE 2]. Prior work on tennis in animated VR found stance similarity in ground strokes, and golf putting work found expert-novice differences and a relationship between virtual and field putting; the source cites those as partial precedents [PAGE 2–3]. The direct precedent in an invasion sport was Kittel et al. with Australian Rules football umpires, but “there were no significant correlations between decision-making in 360° video to in-game decision-making” [PAGE 3]. The present study is therefore a retroactive correction: it tightens control by using the same play scenarios in 360° video and a field task, rather than comparing 360° video to in-game performance.

The “retrocausal” point is that later validation requirements reshape how earlier evidence should be read. Kittel et al. is not simply a failed precedent; it exposes the difference between in-game noise and controlled field tasks. The present paper argues that in-game performance has “considerable variation in perceptual information and movement patterns” and that small samples limited the earlier correlation analysis [PAGE 11]. Thus, the current study’s controlled field task is a stronger test of construct validity but a weaker test of transfer to match play. That distinction retroactively limits claims from all 360° video studies: agreement with a scripted field task does not establish agreement with live competition.

Multi-week traps follow from that lineage. Trap one: treating single-session agreement as longitudinal training transfer. The paper explicitly cautions that “further research is needed to determine whether this is also possible in athletes, prior to use of this technology as a training instrument in invasion sports” [PAGE 13]. A multi-week programme could improve HMD-specific visual search without improving field decision-making. Trap two: stimulus memorisation. The test matrix is 32 trials with 8 familiarisation trials, and the same trials are presented to all participants [PAGE 4]. Over weeks, repeated exposure could turn perceptual recognition into recall of specific clips. Trap three: FOV adaptation. If players learn to compensate by exaggerated head rotation, their HMD performance may rise while field performance does not. The source notes the restricted HMD field of view and says it is unknown whether it altered visual search or peripheral vision [PAGE 12]. Trap four: motor absence. The task is perception-only [PAGE 11], and players suggested “having a pass [of a ball] involved” to increase representativeness [PAGE 10]. Multi-week perception-only training may fail to transfer to pressure-laden motor execution. Trap five: weather and convenience overuse. Players valued indoor use on wet weather days [PAGE 10], which could lead to over-training in a decontextualised setting. Trap six: small-sample overgeneralisation. Fifteen players, an outlier case 9, and wide Bland–Altman limits [PAGE 8] mean multi-week inferences should be pre-registered and powered separately, not extrapolated from this single-session validation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how athletes, coaches, and experimenters model one another under task constraints. The first mode is crisis habit regression. In the field, an athlete under pressure may regress to habitual motor execution; in 360° video, the motor demand is removed. The source quotes a participant saying there was “no physical requirement to successfully execute a pass to a teammate in 360° video, which can usually result in ‘more pressure’ (P11)” [PAGE 10]. Another said: “I felt I could make better in VR” (P13) [PAGE 10]. This is a plausible mechanism for the 9% higher 360° video accuracy: the athlete can allocate attention to perceptual decision-making rather than pass execution. But it also creates a theory-of-mind trap for coaches: a high VR score may reflect reduced motor pressure, not superior field decision-making. The GEE’s significant modality effect [PAGE 8] is consistent with that interpretation. Crisis habit regression is therefore both an explanatory mechanism and a validity threat.

The second mode is counter-trap. The HMD limits peripheral vision, so some players reported needing to “turn [their head] all the way to see the attackers” (P5) and found it difficult “to have vision of the furthest player [in the display]” (P6) [PAGE 10]. A counter-trap appears in P2’s statement: “everyone has to turn [their head] the same amount [as me] before they can see [the defenders and attackers]” [PAGE 10]. From a theory-of-mind perspective, the athlete recognises that the constraint is shared and may treat it as fair rather than invalid. That shared constraint can equalise information access, but it can also create a new HMD-specific skill: rapid head rotation. The experimenter’s model must distinguish “fair because shared” from “representative because field-like.” The paper acknowledges this uncertainty: it is “unknown whether the limited field of view in the HMD altered the underlying mechanisms” [PAGE 12]. The counter-trap is that a limitation can improve perceived fairness while reducing ecological validity.

The third mode is clock. The experimental clock is explicit: a 1 s number, a 1 s still frame, a 5 s ITI, and an audible tone 4 s into the ITI [PAGE 5]. The athlete’s mental clock anticipates the next trial; the experimenter’s clock governs when verbal responses are expected. The source also randomised play order and counterbalanced left/right passing [PAGE 5]. This reduces order effects but imposes a recursive pacing demand: the participant must encode the number, orient to the still frame, watch the play, verbalise a decision during the ITI, and prepare for the tone. If a participant’s head rotation or decision latency exceeds the ITI, the response may be rushed or contaminated. The paper does not report response-time distributions, so the clock’s effect on decision quality is unknown. A coach watching the same task may infer that fast verbal responses equal fast decisions, but the task measures a verbal choice after viewing, not a coupled motor response. The recursive theory-of-mind risk is that athletes and coaches attribute field-like automaticity to a task that is actually paced by an experimental clock and a verbal-report convention.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical layers do not veto the assessment claim, but they do bound the training-transfer claim. On physical load, the source reports that 360° video is “good for injured players” (P8) because it is “not physically strenuous” (P7) [PAGE 10]. That is an enabling property: it allows perceptual decision-making work during rehabilitation without field loading. It is not evidence that the task reproduces field biomechanics. On biomechanics, the source states: “our virtual simulator being a perception-only task, where participants could not interact with the ball or opponents” [PAGE 11]. This is the strongest cross-layer veto candidate. If decision-making expertise depends on coupled perception-action, a perception-only task could misrank athletes or fail to transfer. The authors counter with the claim that “representative perceptual information has been reported to be crucial for expert decision-making” [PAGE 11] and that motor regions are engaged during perceptual tasks [PAGE 11]. That defence supports assessment of perceptual decision-making, not full perceptual-cognitive-motor equivalence. Players themselves suggested “having a pass [of a ball] involved” [PAGE 10], which marks the boundary.

Visual-geometric layers also bound the claim. The camera was fixed at 180 cm [PAGE 4], and common HMDs present “typically 90–110 degrees diagonally and 70–90 degrees vertically” [PAGE 12]. The paper notes that camera height “may have altered the perspective of visual-perception” but was not raised by players [PAGE 12]. It also states that it is “unknown whether the limited field of view in the HMD altered the underlying mechanisms (e.g., visual search, use of peripheral vision)” [PAGE 12]. Thus, the geometric layer does not veto the observed similarity, because decision accuracy was high and the Bland–Altman mean bias was small, but it vetoes any strong claim that the same visual search mechanisms were used. A mechanistic equivalence claim requires eye-tracking or head-tracking evidence, which the source does not report.

Weather is not a veto; it is an affordance. A participant said: “you don’t get these experiences to do this sort of stuff on like a wet weather day. You could do it [test and train decision-making] all day everyday if you wanted to” (P7) [PAGE 10]. Another valued that “you don’t need anyone else” (P6) present [PAGE 10], and P2 valued that “It’s replicable and it’s the consistency” [PAGE 10]. These are practical advantages: weather and player availability are removed as constraints. The adjudication is therefore asymmetric. Weather and physical load support the use of 360° video as an indoor, low-load assessment. Biomechanical decoupling and FOV restriction veto strong training-transfer and mechanism-equivalence claims until longitudinal and process-level evidence exists. The source itself says further research is needed before use as a training instrument in invasion sports [PAGE 13]. A cross-layer veto proof would require showing that a physical, biomechanical, or weather variable changes the construct being measured. The present data show no weather veto and no physical-load veto, but they leave a biomechanical and visual-geometric boundary that is acknowledged rather than resolved.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each has an observable outcome, a prediction under the source’s claim, and a veto criterion. They are framed as reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards-style checks: the reward is a pre-registered, independently scorable outcome, not a subjective impression.

Scenario 1: Equivalence replication veto. Pre-register a within-subject replication with a larger sample of highly skilled rugby players, using the same four play variations and the same 0–3 expert scoring, but with a pre-specified equivalence margin of \(\pm 0.5\) score points for mean bias and \(\pm 1.5\) score points for LoA. The source prediction is that mean bias remains small and the absolute accuracy difference remains near 9% [PAGE 8]. The verifiable reward is the 95% CI for \(\bar d\) and the LoA. Veto prediction: if the 95% CI for \(\bar d\) excludes the \(\pm 0.5\) margin, or if the LoA exceed \(\pm 1.5\) score points, then the claim that the two modalities measure the same construct at a practical equivalence level is vetoed. This directly addresses the source’s own wide LoA of -3.31 to 2.78 on a 0–3 scale [PAGE 8]. Under the source’s claim, the replication should show a small mean bias and a non-trivial but bounded LoA; if it instead reproduces LoA wider than the scale, the “agreement” interpretation fails.

Scenario 2: FOV and visual-search veto. Pre-register an HMD experiment that manipulates field of view and records head rotation and eye tracking while participants perform the same 360° video plays. The source notes HMDs typically provide 90–110 degrees diagonally and 70–90 degrees vertically [PAGE 12], and participants reported needing to “turn [their head] all the way to see the attackers” (P5) [PAGE 10]. Prediction under the source’s claim: narrower FOV will increase head rotation and response latency, but decision accuracy will remain within the observed 9% field-video difference, and the perceptual information will still support expert-like choices. Veto prediction: if accuracy difference from the field task exceeds 9% under the native HMD FOV, or if a narrower FOV produces a statistically significant drop in accuracy with a corresponding change in visual-search entropy or fixation patterns, then the claim that 360° video preserves the underlying decision-making mechanism is vetoed. This scenario is verifiable because head rotation, fixation locations, and accuracy are directly measurable. It distinguishes “shared constraint fairness,” as quoted by P2 [PAGE 10], from “representative visual information.”

Scenario 3: Longitudinal transfer veto. Pre-register a multi-week randomised training study comparing 360° video decision training, two-dimensional video training, and a no-training control, with the primary outcome being field-task decision score using the same expert rubric. The source’s own conclusion limits training claims: “further research is needed to determine whether this is also possible in athletes, prior to use of this technology as a training instrument in invasion sports” [PAGE 13]. Prediction under a strong training claim: the 360° video group will improve field decision scores more than the control and at least as much as the two-dimensional group. Veto prediction: if the 360° group shows no field improvement, or if its improvement is not greater than the two-dimensional group, then the claim that 360° video is a beneficial method to “enhance decision-making skill” is vetoed for transfer, even if it remains valid for assessment. This scenario also tests the motor-absence boundary: if adding a pass response to the VR task produces larger field transfer than perception-only training, the perception-only training claim is vetoed. These three scenarios are independently scorable, pre-registerable, and directly tied to the source’s stated limits.
