The source document is Jordan, Krüger, and Lerch (2019), “Evaluating Probabilistic Forecasts with scoringRules,” Journal of Statistical Software 90(12), 37 pages. The paper’s core object is the scoring rule, defined on page 4 as a function S : F × Ω → R ∪ {∞} assigning numerical values to pairs of forecasts F ∈ F and observations y ∈ Ω. The authors adopt a negative orientation: lower scores indicate better forecasts. A scoring rule is proper if E_{Y∼G} S(G,Y) ≤ E_{Y∼G} S(F,Y) for all F,G ∈ F, and strictly proper if equality holds only when F = G. This incentive-compatibility property means forecasters are compelled to report their true predictive distribution; comparative evaluation then reduces to ranking competitors by average score over out-of-sample cases.

Two univariate scores dominate the package: the logarithmic score LogS(F,y) = −log(f(y)), page 4, requiring a predictive density f, and the continuous ranked probability score CRPS(F,y) = ∫_ℝ (F(z) − 1{y ≤ z})² dz, Equation 1, page 4, defined via the predictive CDF. When the first moment is finite, CRPS admits the equivalent representation CRPS(F,y) = E_F|X₁ − y| − ½E_{F,F}|X₁ − X₂|, where X₁ and X₂ are independent draws from F. For simulated samples of size m, the CRPS reduces to the empirical form in Equation 2, page 6: (1/m)Σᵢ|Xᵢ − y| − (1/(2m²))ΣᵢΣⱼ|Xᵢ − Xⱼ|. Naive evaluation is O(m²); the package instead uses an order-statistics / generalized-quantile representation, Equation 3, page 6, achieving average O(m log m). A gray zone emerges here: for small m the sampling approximation is biased and noisy (Figure 2, pages 9–10 shows convergence as m grows from 50 to 5,000 over 500 replications), so CRPS rankings based on tiny ensembles can invert relative to the analytic score.

LogS computation from samples requires density estimation. The default is Gaussian kernel density estimation, page 6, which the authors explicitly flag as valid only under stringent theoretical assumptions and fragile in practice when the observation falls into the tails, because the score becomes highly sensitive to the bandwidth tuning parameter. The preferred alternative in Bayesian/MCMC contexts is the mixture-of-parameters estimator, Equation 6, page 14: (1/m)Σᵢ F_c(z|θᵢ), which mixes analytic conditional densities over posterior parameter draws rather than over predictive draws. This requires knowledge of the analytic form of the forecast distribution conditional on parameters—a condition that fails for black-box simulators.

Multivariate extensions appear on page 18: the energy score ES(F,y) = (1/m)Σᵢ‖Xᵢ − y‖ − (1/(2m²))ΣᵢΣⱼ‖Xᵢ − Xⱼ‖ and the variogram score of order p. Both require simulated samples; no closed-form parametric multivariate CRPS is generally available, and kernel density estimation in high dimension inherits the same fragility as the univariate case.

Breakdown regimes and exact singularities: (1) CRPS is strictly proper only for distributions with finite first moment, page 4; heavy-tailed or infinite-mean forecasts violate this and can produce divergent average scores. (2) LogS → ∞ as f(y) → 0, so tail events dominate and bandwidth misspecification can arbitrarily reshape rankings. (3) In the weather case study, page 12, cases with zero ensemble standard deviation are omitted before fitting censored non-homogeneous regression; this is a selection filter that changes the estimand. (4) MCMC-based posterior predictive approximation, page 14, assumes chain convergence and stationarity; 20,000 iterations are used in the GDP example, but no convergence diagnostic is reported in the paper itself. (5) Censored and truncated distributions with point masses at boundaries (e.g., precipitation at zero, pages 11–12 and Appendix A.4) require specialized CRPS formulas; applying an uncensored formula to a mixed discrete-continuous law is a category error.

The two case studies ground the theory. Weather: 4,971 three-day accumulated precipitation cases from Innsbruck, 2000–2013, modeled on the square-root scale with censored logistic, Gaussian, and Student’s t location-scale regression; post-processed mean CRPS ≈ 0.875 versus raw ensemble ≈ 1.321 (in sqrt(mm) units on the transformed scale), page 13. Economics: Bayesian Markov-switching autoregressive model of U.S. GDP growth, four quarters of 2014 evaluated against vintages; forecast certainty decreases monotonically as horizon extends from one to four quarters ahead, pages 14–16, with CRPS falling from 3.457 (2014Q1) to 0.724 (2014Q4) because the realized growth rates happened to be near the predictive centers in later quarters—a realization-dependent artifact, not a generic horizon rule.

Optimum score estimation, pages 16–17, frames parameter estimation as minimization of expected score; LogS minimization coincides with maximum likelihood. Gradients and Hessians of CRPS are implemented for a subset of families (logis, norm, t and censored/truncated variants), enabling BFGS optimization, but all Table 1 families admit numerical optimization.

Negative orientation: lower score ⇒ better forecast [PAGE 4].

**Logarithmic score**

LogS(F, y) = −log(f(y)) [PAGE 4]. Requires F to admit a PDF f. Units: nats (natural logarithm). Singularity: LogS → ∞ as f(y) → 0; undefined for y outside support.

**Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)**

Definition: CRPS(F, y) = ∫_ℝ (F(z) − 1{y ≤ z})² dz [PAGE 4, Equation 1]. Units: identical to the observation variable y (e.g., mm, °C, or sqrt(mm) when the variable is transformed, page 12).

Moment identity (finite first moment required): CRPS(F, y) = E_F|X₁ − y| − ½ E_{F,F}|X₁ − X₂| [PAGE 4]. X₁, X₂ ⟂ ∼ F.

Empirical sample form, size m: CRPS( F̂_m, y) = (1/m) Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ |Xᵢ − y| − (1/(2m²)) Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ Σⱼ₌₁ᵐ |Xᵢ − Xⱼ| [PAGE 6, Equation 2]. Complexity O(m²) naive.

logs_sample uses Gaussian KDE with default bandwidth bw.nrd() from stats [PAGE 6]. Fragile in tails; bandwidth-sensitive.

F̂^{ECDF}_m(z) = (1/m) Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ 1{Xᵢ ≤ z} [PAGE 14].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Crisis habit regression: under high-stakes or high-uncertainty conditions, forecasters tend to regress to familiar but suboptimal decision rules. In the source context, this manifests as preferring raw ensemble output over post-processed distributions, or maximum likelihood (minimum LogS) over minimum CRPS estimation, even when the loss function of the end user is asymmetric. The paper documents that raw ensembles are “biased and underdispersive” [PAGE 11], yet they remain the default operational product in many centers because they are simple and require no statistical post-processing pipeline. Under crisis—severe weather, economic volatility—a modeler may abandon scoring-rule-based selection and fall back on subjective or deterministic criteria, breaking the incentive-compatibility guarantee of proper scoring.

Counter-trap: proper scoring rules are an institutional counter-measure to exactly this regression. Because strictly proper rules make truthful reporting a dominant strategy, they foreclose the temptation to “hedge” by issuing over-dispersed or under-dispersed forecasts to protect reputation. The scoringRules package operationalizes this counter-trap by providing closed-form CRPS and gradients for optimization (gradcrps_* and hesscrps_* for logis, norm, t and censored variants, page 16), making minimum-CRPS estimation as computationally accessible as maximum likelihood. The weather case study demonstrates the payoff: post-processed models cut mean CRPS from 1.321 (raw ensemble) to roughly 0.875 [PAGE 13], a large improvement that would be invisible to a habit-driven evaluator fixated on ensemble spread alone.

Clock: the temporal layer operates at three scales. First, forecast lead time: the weather ensembles are five to eight days ahead [PAGE 12], and the GDP model extends one to four quarters ahead [PAGE 14]; both exhibit degradation of certainty with horizon, though the GDP CRPS pattern is outcome-dependent rather than law-like [PAGE 16]. Second, Monte Carlo clock: sample size m in crps_sample and logs_sample governs approximation error; Figure 2 shows bias shrinking only as m approaches thousands [PAGES 9–10]. Third, MCMC clock: 20,000 iterations were run for the GDP forecast [PAGE 14]; insufficient burn-in or autocorrelation would silently corrupt the mixture-of-parameters estimator. The interaction of these clocks means that a “good” score at short horizon and large m can mask catastrophic miscalibration at long horizon and small m, producing recursive overconfidence when the same pipeline is reused across regimes.

Condition: The predictive distribution F lacks a finite first moment, E|X| = ∞ (e.g., a Student’s t with degrees of freedom ν ≤ 1, or a heavy-tailed regime in the Markov-switching GDP model). Verifiable test: simulate N independent samples of size m from such a distribution, compute the empirical CRPS via Equation 2 [PAGE 6] for each, and examine convergence of the sample mean as m → ∞. Prediction: the empirical CRPS will fail to stabilize; the variance of the score across replications will remain bounded away from zero even at large m, and rankings between two heavy-tailed competitors will flip with probability approaching ½ as m grows, because strict propriety requires finite first moment [PAGE 4]. This voids CRPS-based model selection.

Veto scenario 2 — Tail-sensitive LogS bandwidth collapse. Condition: An observation y falls in the tail of a simulated forecast sample, and logs_sample uses Gaussian KDE with default bw.nrd() bandwidth [PAGE 6]. Verifiable test: fix a simulated sample and observation in the lower 1% quantile region; vary the bandwidth bw over a multiplicative grid (e.g., 0.25×, 0.5×, 1×, 2×, 4× the default); compute logs_sample at each setting. Prediction: LogS values will span multiple nats and exhibit non-monotonic swings, because the estimated density f̂(y) is driven by bandwidth choice rather than data [PAGE 6]. The MPE (Equation 6, page 14) is predicted to be stable across the same grid because it mixes analytic conditionals rather than smoothing predictive draws [PAGE 16]. This voids LogS comparisons whenever tail outcomes are decision-relevant.

Veto scenario 3 — Zero-spread ensemble selection bias. Condition: The evaluation dataset excludes forecast cases with zero ensemble standard deviation [PAGE 12]. Verifiable test: on the RainIbk precipitation dataset, compute mean CRPS for the raw ensemble and post-processed models on (a) the full set of 4,971 cases and (b) the subset with enssd > 0 used in the paper [PAGE 12]. Prediction: the subset selection will inflate the apparent skill gap between raw ensemble and post-processed models, because zero-spread cases are precisely those where the ensemble provides no dispersion information and post-processing gains are largest or undefined; the reported improvement from 1.321 to ~0.875 [PAGE 13] is therefore an upper bound on the true out-of-sample gain. This voids any claim of uniform post-processing superiority without reporting the excluded mass.
