Layer 3—Weather: 1920-era leather balls lose friction when wet. Effective coefficient of friction μ_eff = μ_0(1 − αR), where R is rainfall rate [mm/h], μ_0 is dry friction [dimensionless], and α is a weather-sensitivity coefficient [h/mm]. If μ_eff < μ_crit [dimensionless], φ_weather = FALSE.

Composite veto:  
a* = advance iff φ_phys ∧ φ_bio ∧ φ_weather ∧ (E[EP|advance] > E[EP|fall_on]).

Proof of veto dominance: [PAGE 29] shows players falling even with “a clear field to the goal,” which means lower layers (habit, uncertainty about defenders, implicit weather/ball risk) dominated the higher-layer game-theoretic calculation. No expected-points advantage can override a physical impossibility such as μ_eff < μ_crit. Breakdown occurs at the singularity where μ_eff → 0 or τ_min → 0; in those regimes the policy collapses to fall_on with probability one.

Scenario 1—Weather veto. Model recommends pickup on a loose ball at R = 8 mm/h, giving μ_eff ≈ 0.20 < μ_crit. The weather layer vetoes and forces fall_on. Verifiable prediction: in verified games with comparable precipitation, attempted pickups show fumble/turnover rates > 35%, whereas fall-on shows turnover rates < 5%. If the model ignores the veto and advises pickup, verified loss in expected points is > 1.8 EP per occurrence.

Verifiable prediction: strip or fumble probability on attempted pickup exceeds 55%, versus < 15% for fall_on. Verifiable via player-tracking time-to-contact and post-play turnover logs.

The clock/risk layer vetoes because a turnover in own territory ends the game. Verifiable prediction: win-probability change ΔWP < −0.08 if the counter-trap is attempted versus ΔWP ≈ +0.02 if the team secures and runs the clock. Verifiable via win-probability models and fourth-quarter turnover outcomes.
