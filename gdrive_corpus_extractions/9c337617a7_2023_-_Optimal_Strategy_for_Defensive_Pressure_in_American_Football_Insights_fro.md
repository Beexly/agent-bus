The defensive coordinator’s decision problem is recursive: the defense selects pre-snap alignments to maximize sack probability, while the offense simultaneously selects protection schemes, quarterback depth, and release timing to minimize it. The model captures the first layer of this game tree—pre-snap features such as down, yards to go, yardline, formation, personnel, and alignment [PAGE 4-5]—but does not model the offense’s counter-response. Crisis habit regression appears in the feature importance of down_3 and down_4, which the paper associates with “specific pass-rushing packages and creative pass-rushing schemes employed by defenses” [PAGE 7]. Under pressure (late downs, long yardage), coordinators tend to regress to familiar blitz packages rather than experiment with novel alignments, because the perceived cost of a failed experiment is high. The counter-trap is the offensive response: if the offense correctly diagnoses a blitz, it can slide protection, chip the rusher with a running back or tight end, call a screen, or instruct the quarterback to throw quickly on a hot read, collapsing the sack probability regardless of how favorable the pre-snap alignment was. Because the model contains no quarterback time-to-throw variable, no protection-scheme indicator, and no in-play tracking beyond the snap frame, it cannot veto a high-probability alignment that is neutralized by post-snap offensive adjustment.

Clock structure compounds this recursion. The model includes yardline, down, and yards to go [PAGE 4], which proxy for game situation, but it does not include explicit game clock, score differential, or play-clock pressure. A defense trailing late in the fourth quarter may blitz aggressively despite suboptimal alignment probabilities, while a defense with a lead may play conservatively even when the model assigns high sack probability to a particular alignment. The recursive theory of mind therefore runs: the defense believes the offense will protect in a base scheme, so it aligns for a sack; the offense believes the defense will blitz because of down and distance, so it max-protects; the defense’s expected-sack probability from the model is high, but the realized probability is near zero because the recursion was not modeled. The dashboard described in the paper allows coaches to “experiment with the most optimal positions to put their defenders into” [PAGE 10], yet without simulating the offensive best response, the optimization is a unilateral calculation, not a Nash equilibrium. A counter-trap veto triggers whenever the offensive protection matches or exceeds the defensive pressure design, rendering the pre-snap expected sack probability an upper bound that is rarely realized in contested passing situations.
