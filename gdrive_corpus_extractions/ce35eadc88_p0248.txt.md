<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Frank H. Knight’s 1921 demarcation between "risk" (measurable) and "uncertainty" (unmeasurable) fundamentally severed economics from Laplacian determinism. Historically, this lineage traces back to the insufficiency of the Law of Large Numbers in dealing with unique, non-ergodic historical events—a problem heavily debated by Austrian economists like Carl Menger and Friedrich Hayek. Knight explicitly channels Adam Smith here; Smith had long ago observed the irrationality of gambling behavior, recognizing that human nature inherently discounts statistical reality in favor of emotional hope. 

From a scientific standpoint, Knight’s distinction anticipates the later development of John Maynard Keynes’s concept of "fundamental uncertainty," and directly prefigures the informational constraints identified by Herbert Simon ("bounded rationality"). However, a multi-week, multi-year trap exists within this genealogy: the "sorting method." By admitting that accidents might cancel out *to some extent*, Knight opens the door for subsequent generations of econometricians to aggressively force complex, heterogenous human behaviors into quantifiable regression models. The trap is assuming that because broad aggregates show constancy, the micro-decisions governing them can be safely subjected to a priori calculation. This is the origin of the catastrophic model failures seen in modern financial crises—the retroactive assumption that past frequency distributions (risk) perfectly map onto future structural shifts (uncertainty).
