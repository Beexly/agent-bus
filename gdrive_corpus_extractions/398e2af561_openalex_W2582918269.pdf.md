The thesis constructs a multi-hop pipeline for unedited user-generated video (UGV): identification of co-event recordings, temporal synchronisation, camera-motion detection, and coherent video composition. The source frames the problem at scale: “The increasing availability of smartphones is facilitating people to capture videos of their experience when attending events such as concerts, sports competitions and public rallies” [PAGE 5]. The central inference chain is: (1) visual content is unreliable across handheld cameras because of viewpoint, illumination, blur, and shake variation; (2) audio remains more correlated across co-located devices; (3) audio chroma, being a pitch-class energy distribution, is more robust than onsets or fingerprints under local degradations; (4) pairwise chroma matching yields a delay matrix whose dominant diagonal corresponds to temporal overlap; (5) a learned classification threshold turns pairwise matching into event clusters; (6) refined matching yields sample-accurate time shifts; (7) synchronised streams enable audio stitching, cut-point selection, quality ranking, and camera selection.

A critical gray-zone is the overlap boundary. The author states that “the minimum value of 8% overlap between a pair of recordings is required when performing feature matching” [PAGE 60]. This is not a universal physical constant but a corpus-specific statistical threshold arising from chroma dimensionality, recording length, and the minimum-count estimator over rows of the distance matrix. The thesis also acknowledges short-recording pathology: when one recording is much shorter, the minimum across each row can produce outlier matches, and the author lists block-wise matching as future work [PAGE 109]. Another gray zone is non-amplified sound. In non-amplified events, “usually the ambient noise is dominating while the audio clues of the sound sourceareweak” [PAGE 24]. This affects identification and synchronisation because the discriminative source signal may be below the ambient floor. The thesis nevertheless reports strong synchronisation performance, with errors “smaller than 0:03 sec” and “AC outperformed theother three methods as it was able to synchronise 262 out of 263 UGVs” [PAGE 86, PAGE 87].

The composition layer introduces another hierarchy: audio continuity, visual quality, and view diversity. The thesis uses audio ranking by spectral rolloff, stitched audio, and cut-points defined by agreement of root-mean-square, spectral centroid, and spectral entropy derivatives. The cut-point logic is motivated by film grammar: a cut should correspond to a meaningful transition. The source says “A segment longer than lmax isperceived asboring and asegment shorter than Imin may not beunderstandable” [PAGE 72]. This creates a temporal prior: shots must be long enough to be interpretable but short enough to avoid monotony. The gyro branch replaces expensive visual motion analysis with angular velocity. The key physical assumption is that handheld recording always contains involuntary body motion; the source says “The frequency of involuntary human body movement lieswithin fi = 20Hz” [PAGE 62] and that inertial logging at 50 Hz satisfies Nyquist. The final reasoning audit must therefore separate: (a) cases where chroma matching is physically valid, (b) cases where the estimator becomes statistically brittle, (c) cases where visual or inertial layers veto due to motion, blur, or light.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(C=\{C_m\}_{m=1}^{M}\) be a database of unsynchronised UGVs, and let \(E=\{E_k\}_{k=1}^{K}\) be events, with \(E_k=\{C_{k,n_k}\}_{n_k=1}^{N_k}\) [PAGE 24]. The event coverage duration is defined by the last recorded audio, video, or inertial sample:

\[
D_c=\frac{I^v}{s_v}=\frac{I^a}{s_a}=\frac{I^g}{s_g}\quad[\text{s}]
\]

where \(I^v\) is frames, \(I^a\) audio samples, \(I^g\) gyro samples, and \(s_v,s_a,s_g\) are rates in \(\text{s}^{-1}\) [PAGE 27]. The synchronisation operator between recordings \(i\) and \(j\) is

\[
\Delta t_{ij}=t_i^p-t_j^q+\varepsilon_{ij},
\]

with propagation correction

\[
\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{\Delta d_{ij}}{u_s},\qquad u_s=340\ \text{m/s}
\]

[PAGE 79]. If one frame tolerance at \(s_v=25\ \text{fps}\) is admitted, then \(|\varepsilon_{ij}|\le 0.04\ \text{s}\), implying \(|\Delta d_{ij}|\le 13.6\ \text{m}\), which the thesis approximates as \(14\ \text{m}\) [PAGE 79]. Breakdown regime: if \( |\Delta d_{ij}|>14\ \text{m}\), or if the propagation delay is not negligible relative to the desired temporal tolerance, the synchronisation estimate becomes biased.

Audio chroma is a 12-dimensional pitch-class representation. The pitch-class index is

\[
l=\left\lfloor 12\log_2\left(\frac{f(l)}{f_s}\right)\right\rceil+l_s,
\]

with \(f_s=440\ \text{Hz}\), \(l_s=69\) for A4, and \(v_d=12\) semitones [PAGE 35]. The chroma vector is

\[
\mathbf{v}=(v_0,\ldots,v_{11})\in\mathbb{R}^{12},
\]

where \(v_r\) is the energy assigned to pitch class \(r\). Units are dimensionless energy coefficients after spectral pooling; the input spectrum has units of magnitude or power per frequency bin, depending on the transform convention. Breakdown occurs if \(f(l)\le 0\) or if the spectrum is non-stationary over the analysis frame.

Feature extraction segments audio into frames. For audio signal \(A_n\), the chroma feature set is

\[
F_n=\{\mathbf{v}_n^p\}_{g=1}^{G_n},
\]

where \(p\) indexes frame time [PAGE 58]. Pairwise matching uses Euclidean distance:

\[
d_{ij}^{pq}=\left\|\mathbf{v}_i^p-\mathbf{v}_j^q\right\|_2.
\]

The distance matrix is

\[
\hat{\Delta}_{ij}=[d_{ij}^{pq}]\in\mathbb{R}^{G_i\times G_j}.
\]

The minimum-distance index per row is

\[
\mathbf{c}=\arg\min_s [\hat{\Delta}_{ij}]_{t,s},\qquad \forall t\in[1,G_j],
\]

and the matching histogram is \(H_{ij}(\Delta t)\), whose dominant peak estimates the relative time shift [PAGE 59]. Statistical breakdown: when recordings have unequal lengths, the row-minimum operation can create spurious diagonal mass; the thesis explicitly identifies this as an outlier problem and proposes block matching as future work [PAGE 60, PAGE 109].

The identification descriptor is built by normalising the histogram:

\[
\tilde{H}_{ij}(\Delta t)=\frac{H_{ij}(\Delta t)}{\max_{\Delta t}H_{ij}(\Delta t)}.
\]

A scanning threshold \(T_r\in[0,1]\) converts the histogram into a match-count curve \(P_{ij}(T_r)\), and the descriptor is its derivative:

\[
P'_{ij}=\frac{dP_{ij}}{dT_r}.
\]

The resulting vector is fed to an SVM to learn a classification threshold \(\theta\) [PAGE 80, PAGE 82]. The decision operator is

\[
\hat{y}_{ij}=\operatorname{sign}(f_\theta(P'_{ij})),
\]

where \(f_\theta\) is the learned separating function. Breakdown regime: match and non-match descriptors become non-separable when reverberation, channel noise, or insufficient overlap flatten the histogram peak.

Cut-point selection uses three audio derivatives: root-mean-square \(D_{\text{RMS}}\), spectral centroid \(D_{\text{SC}}\), and spectral entropy \(D_{\text{SE}}\). Within an analysis window \(W_a\), the threshold vector is

\[
\bar{t}_a=\bar{m}_a+hS_a,
\]

where \(\bar{m}_a\) is the mean vector, \(S_a\) the standard-deviation vector, and \(h\) a scalar weight [PAGE 71]. The binary outlier maps are

\[
b_{\text{RMS}}(g_C)=
\begin{cases}
0,& d_{\text{RMS}}(g_C)<t_a^{\text{RMS}},\\
1,& \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

and analogously for \(B_{\text{SC}}\) and \(B_{\text{SE}}\). The final cut-point signal is

\[
U^A=B_{\text{RMS}}\wedge B_{\text{SC}}\wedge B_{\text{SE}},
\]

where \(\wedge\) denotes logical AND [PAGE 71]. The thesis sets \(l_{\min}=3\ \text{s}\) and \(l_{\max}=10\ \text{s}\) [PAGE 72]. If spectral entropy is computed from a normalised probability distribution \(p_k\), then

\[
H=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k\quad[\text{bits}].
\]

Breakdown: if the audio is non-stationary within the frame, if \(W_a\) is too short, or if \(h\) is misscaled, cut-points become either absent or excessively dense.

The gyro layer filters angular velocity into low-pass and high-pass components. For pan and tilt, the source states that “we jointly analyse GLx(t) and GLy(t) by transforming them into polar coordinates” [PAGE 65]. The polar operators are

\[
G_r^L(t)=\sqrt{(G_x^L(t))^2+(G_y^L(t))^2},
\]

\[
G_\theta^L(t)=\arctan\left(\frac{G_y^L(t)}{G_x^L(t)}\right).
\]

Pan is detected when the angle is near \(0\) or \(180\) degrees; tilt is detected when it is near \(90\) or \(270\) degrees. Shake is detected by thresholding high-frequency magnitude:

\[
S(t)=
\begin{cases}
1,& G_r^H(t)>\beta,\\
0,& \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

The source selects \(\alpha=30^\circ\) for pan/tilt tolerance and \(\beta=0.06\) for shake magnitude [PAGE 65]. Units: angular velocity in \(\text{rad/s}\), angles in radians or degrees, time in seconds. Breakdown: if the camera is rotationally static, \(G_r\) may approach zero and motion detection becomes undefined; if \(\beta\) is too low, involuntary motion is misclassified as shake; if \(\beta\) is too high, genuine shake is missed.

The gyro-based quality score is derived from total angular velocity magnitude:

\[
|G_n|=\sqrt{G_{nx}^2+G_{ny}^2+G_{nz}^2}\quad[\text{rad/s}].
\]

After min-max inversion,

\[
Y_n=\frac{G_{\max}-|G_n|}{G_{\max}-G_{\min}},
\]

where \(Y_n\) is dimensionless quality, with higher values indicating lower motion-induced degradation [PAGE 94]. The source states: “The higher the magnitude, the lower is the perceived visual quality” [PAGE 94]. Singular breakdown occurs when \(G_{\max}=G_{\min}\), because the denominator vanishes.

For camera selection, the view-diversity constraint is

\[
M_j\in C_n\quad\text{s.t.}\quad M_j\neq M_{j-1}\ \text{and}\ M_j\neq M_{j-2},
\]

provided at least three recordings are available [PAGE 92]. The spatial occurrence score for camera \(n\) in segment \(u_j\) is

\[
O_{nj}^S=\frac{1}{K_j^v}\sum_{k=1}^{K_j^v}Q_{jk}^S,
\]

where \(Q_{jk}^S\) is the camera-selection indicator for frame sample \(k\) [PAGE 93]. Breakdown: if fewer than three recordings are available, the second-order diversity constraint must be relaxed; if all available views are degraded, diversity may reduce quality by selecting dissimilar but poor views.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of the thesis is a convergence of audio fingerprinting, music information retrieval, multi-camera synchronisation, and mobile sensing. Audio identification descends from landmark-based fingerprinting associated with Wang’s industrial-strength audio search and Shazam-style services [PAGE 32, PAGE 34]. Frame-based fingerprinting, such as Haitsma and Kalker’s sub-fingerprints, influenced multi-camera synchronisation by compact binary representations and bit-error matching [PAGE 32, PAGE 45]. Onset-based synchronisation descends from ERB-band transient detection, but the thesis shows that onsets are brittle under channel noise, ambient noise, and reverberation [PAGE 31, PAGE 46]. Chroma features come from music structure analysis, especially chord recognition and tonal matching, where pitch-class invariance provides robustness to loudness and timbre changes [PAGE 34, PAGE 46].

The visual lineage includes luminance projection correlation for pan and tilt estimation, optical flow for apparent motion, and template matching for shot classification [PAGE 34, PAGE 36, PAGE 48]. The inertial lineage enters through accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer sensing. The thesis identifies Cricri et al. as the only prior inertial-sensor camera-motion method and critiques its reliance on compass and unfiltered accelerometer data [PAGE 49]. The gyro branch is therefore a correction: direct angular velocity replaces indirect orientation inference.

A multi-week trap in this genealogy is the assumption that professional or near-duplicate video methods transfer directly to UGVs. The thesis explicitly separates near-duplicate video retrieval from UGV identification because UGVs have “varying ﬁelds of view, lighting conditions, cameramotions, devicesettingsand location of theusers” [PAGE 30]. Another trap is fixed-threshold classification. Earlier concert-organisation methods used fixed or mean-standard-deviation thresholds, limiting generalisation across events [PAGE 42, PAGE 55]. The thesis counter-traps this by learning a threshold from histogram descriptors [PAGE 82]. A further historical trap is treating audio as secondary. Prior composition methods focused on visual quality and ignored audio continuity; the thesis reintroduces audio stitching and cut-point selection as first-class operators [PAGE 52, PAGE 90].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how the system models the event, how the analyst models the system, and how both models fail under crisis conditions. In a concert or rally, the crowd is not a static scene: cheering, whistles, clapping, and non-amplified speech create global degradations that alter the audio channel. The source notes that global degradations are “common to some or all recording devices” and may even help synchronisation, while local degradations arise from device settings, channel noise, and reverberation [PAGE 43]. The system must therefore distinguish between event-level shared sound and device-level corrupted sound. This is a second-order model: the method models the recording as a noisy observation of a shared event, while the evaluator must model which degradations are informative and which are adversarial.

Crisis habit regression occurs when a pipeline under stress reverts to a simpler but brittle cue. In audio identification, the brittle habit is to trust a fixed threshold or a single fingerprint match. The thesis identifies this regression and replaces it with a learned descriptor and validation step. In synchronisation, the brittle habit is to trust the global minimum of a distance matrix. The thesis shows that this can fail for short recordings and introduces cluster membership validation through consistent time-shift histograms [PAGE 83]. In composition, the brittle habit is to select the visually highest-ranked camera without temporal diversity. The thesis introduces a two-step history constraint to avoid repetitive views [PAGE 92].

Counter-trap behaviour is the deliberate inversion of the expected crisis response. Instead of increasing visual processing when quality drops, the thesis can shift to gyro-based quality because gyro data is independent of luminance and moving-object clutter. Instead of accepting a single peak, it validates anti-symmetry and temporal consistency in the delay matrix. Instead of using a fixed classification threshold, it trains a boundary from match and non-match histogram descriptors. The clock dimension is literal: synchronisation depends on universal time alignment, cut-point selection depends on temporal thresholds, and subjective testing depends on presentation order. The source uses a Friedman two-way ANOVA by ranks, where rows are blocks and columns are treatments [PAGE 96]. The temporal order of test videos matters because subjects must compare all three videos within a test set; otherwise they lack a reference for quality. The p-value logic is probabilistic: “Generally, if thep-valueislessthan 0.05 or 0.01, it castsdoubt on the null hypothesis” [PAGE 103].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraint invalidates the algorithmic inference regardless of statistical confidence.

Physical veto 1: acoustic propagation distance. The thesis assumes \(u_s=340\ \text{m/s}\) and that cameras lie close enough for arrival-time error to be negligible [PAGE 79, PAGE 80]. With a one-frame tolerance at 25 fps, the maximum admissible source-distance difference is approximately \(14\ \text{m}\). If \( |\Delta d_{ij}|>14\ \text{m}\), the arrival-time term \( \varepsilon_{ij}=\Delta d_{ij}/u_s \) exceeds the temporal tolerance. Therefore, even a perfect chroma match cannot guarantee frame-accurate synchronisation. Proof: if \( |\Delta d_{ij}|=20\ \text{m}\), then \( \varepsilon_{ij}=20/340\approx0.0588\ \text{s}>0.04\ \text{s}\). Veto condition:

\[
|\Delta d_{ij}|>u_s\cdot\frac{1}{s_v}.
\]

Physical veto 2: insufficient acoustic overlap. The thesis identifies an empirical minimum of 8% overlap for reliable feature matching [PAGE 60]. If the temporal intersection \(D_o\) satisfies

\[
\frac{D_o}{\min(L_i,L_j)}<0.08,
\]

the matching histogram may lack enough diagonal mass for a dominant peak. This is a statistical-information veto: the available co-temporal samples are insufficient to estimate a stable shift.

Biomechanical veto: handheld assumption. The gyro method relies on involuntary human body movement to keep the gyroscope signal nonzero. The source states that “In the absence of intentional camera motion, the involuntary body movement results in low magnitude cameramotion” [PAGE 62]. If the camera is tripod-mounted or magnetically fixed, the gyro magnitude may collapse toward zero, and the assumption of nonzero motion fails. In that regime, pan and tilt detectors may return no positive evidence, and the gyro quality score can become ill-conditioned if \(G_{\max}=G_{\min}\). Veto condition:

\[
G_{\max}-G_{\min}\approx0.
\]

Biomechanical veto 2: motion outside the rotation model. The method assumes negligible translational motion and treats camera motion primarily as rotation around device axes. If the operator translates the phone through space, or if the scene motion dominates the image without corresponding device rotation, visual-based LPC or optical flow may detect motion that the gyroscope does not represent. Conversely, rapid rotation can produce motion blur that invalidates visual quality scores while gyro still detects rotation. Thus, the gyro layer can veto visual-only selection when blur is present, but visual quality may veto gyro-only selection if the scene contains non-camera motion.

Environmental veto: low luminance. The thesis notes that visual methods are less accurate in low brightness and that LPC-based gyro-visual synchronisation depends on luminance changes [PAGE 97]. If illumination is too low for reliable projection correlation, visual synchronisation or visual quality scoring becomes unreliable. The gyro layer can remain valid, but the composition framework must then rely more heavily on audio and inertial cues.

Weather-related veto: the corpus does not provide explicit weather variables, but the physical chain extends naturally. Wind, temperature gradients, rain, or crowd density can alter effective sound speed and attenuation. If effective sound speed changes from \(u_s\) to \(u_s+\delta u\), the arrival-time bias becomes approximately

\[
\delta\varepsilon\approx -\frac{\Delta d_{ij}}{u_s^2}\delta u.
\]

For nonzero \(\Delta d_{ij}\), this introduces a synchronisation bias. If wind also creates directional propagation or rain increases high-frequency attenuation, spectral centroid and rolloff statistics may shift, weakening audio ranking and cut-point detection. Therefore, any deployment with strong weather variability should be treated as outside the validated regime unless recalibrated.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Counterfactual veto 1: short-recording outlier veto.
Scenario: one UGV in a co-event cluster is much shorter than the others, for example under 30 seconds, with overlap below the practical 8% threshold. The row-minimum distance estimator creates spurious matches because the short recording contributes few reliable chroma frames.
Verifiable prediction: the synchronisation error for that short recording will exceed the typical error bound, or the time-shift histogram will lack a dominant peak. The thesis itself reports one failure out of 263 UGVs and identifies short recordings as a future block-matching problem [PAGE 86, PAGE 109].
Formal veto condition:

\[
\frac{D_o}{L_{\min}}<0.08
\quad\text{or}\quad
L_{\min}\ll L_{\max}.
\]

Counterfactual veto 2: rotationally static camera veto.
Scenario: the camera is placed on a fixed mount, or the user holds the phone with unusually perfect stabilisation such that angular velocity is near zero. The CMDG method assumes handheld involuntary motion and uses low-pass gyro magnitude for pan/tilt and high-pass magnitude for shake.
Verifiable prediction: pan and tilt detection precision/recall collapse because there are few true motion samples, while stable classification dominates. If the signal is perfectly constant, the gyro quality score denominator vanishes or becomes numerically unstable.
Formal veto condition:

\[
|G_n(t)|\approx0
\quad\text{for all }t,
\]

or

\[
G_{\max}-G_{\min}\approx0.
\]

Counterfactual veto 3: non-amplified ambient-noise veto.
Scenario: a protest, local gathering, or outdoor ceremony where the sound source is not amplified and ambient crowd noise dominates. The audio source may be weaker than background noise, and microphones may capture mostly diffuse environmental sound.
Verifiable prediction: audio onset and audio fingerprint methods will fail more often, while chroma may still succeed if there is sufficient overlap and stable tonal or rhythmic structure. If overlap falls below the 8% threshold, even chroma matching should fail. The thesis notes that non-amplified recordings contain weak source cues and high ambient noise [PAGE 24], and that existing methods did not consider non-amplified sources [PAGE 23].
Formal veto condition:

\[
\text{SNR}_{\text{source}}<\text{SNR}_{\text{ambient}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{D_o}{L_{\min}}<0.08.
\]

These three vetoes are verifiable because they depend on measurable quantities: overlap ratio, recording length ratio, gyro variance, and ambient-to-source energy dominance. They also provide operational guards: reject or downweight synchronisation hypotheses when overlap is too small, reject gyro-only quality when rotational variance is absent, and reject audio-only identification when non-amplified ambient noise dominates and overlap is insufficient.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
