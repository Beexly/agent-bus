The source is a 2017 arXiv preprint on concussion classification from whole-brain white-matter fiber strains. The central inference chain is: reconstructed NFL head-impact kinematics are applied to the Worcester Head Injury Model (WHIM); each DTI white-matter voxel receives a peak fiber-strain value, producing a 3D strain-encoded image volume with N = 64272 WM voxels [PAGE 3]; that voxel-wise field becomes the input to a five-layer fully connected deep network, with SVM and random forest as baselines and four scalar injury metrics as univariate-logistic-regression comparators [PAGE 1][PAGE 7]. The performance claim is not merely that deep learning has a high AUC; it is that feature-based classifiers preserve spatial-distribution information that scalar metrics collapse. The paper states this directly: scalar metrics “do not (and cannot) inform the location or distribution of brain strains that are likely critical for concussion” [PAGE 2]. This is the most important multi-hop operator: the input object is a field, not a scalar; the classifier is a learned map from field to probability; leave-one-out cross-validation is the audit that prevents the field classifier from being judged only on fitted data.

The first gray-zone audit is dataset size and class balance. The dataset has 58 reconstructed impacts, 25 concussions and 33 non-injury cases [PAGE 3]. Leave-one-out is appropriate for maximizing training data under small N, and the paper explicitly says: “Given the relatively small sample size (N=58), here we adopted a leave-one-out cross-validation for performance evaluation” [PAGE 7]. But LOO also creates 58 highly correlated training sets, so the testing AUC is less independent than a fresh external cohort. The paper acknowledges this limitation: only one head FE model and a single injury dataset were used, and “the deep learning classifier trained here may not be readily applicable to other injury datasets and a fresh training is necessary” [PAGE 13]. Thus any claim of deployment readiness is a gray-zone overreach. The source itself says the network “was by no means optimal or was ready for deployment in a more typical, general population” [PAGE 14].

The second gray-zone audit is feature-selection leakage. The paper is unusually careful: “To avoid the classical feature selection bias problem [56], independent feature selections were performed for each leave-one-out cross-validation trial. Specifically, only the training dataset (N=57), not including the cross-validation data point, were used for feature selection” [PAGE 7]. This means the reported 0.862 deep-learning accuracy, 0.840 sensitivity, 0.879 specificity, and 0.892 testing AUC in Table 4 are only interpretable under the N=57 rule. If a downstream analysis uses all 58 cases for feature selection and then reports LOO performance, it violates the paper’s protocol and becomes unverifiable against the source.

The third gray-zone audit is scalar-metric comparison. The paper compares against BrIC, CSDM-WB, CSDM-CC, and Peak-CC [PAGE 7]. The best feature-based classifier values in Table 4 are deep learning accuracy 0.862, SVM 0.828, RF 0.842±0.016; BrIC 0.776, CSDM-WB 0.741, CSDM-CC 0.776, Peak-CC 0.690 [PAGE 10]. The abstract summarizes the broader feature-based versus scalar contrast as “average accuracy of 0.844 vs. 0.746, and average area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) of 0.873 vs. 0.769, respectively, based on the testing dataset” [PAGE 1]. But there is an exception: “Only BrIC slightly outperformed deep learning in specificity when the F-score approach was used for feature selection (0.879 vs. 0.818; Tables 3 and 4)” [PAGE 11]. A valid answer must retain that exception and not claim absolute dominance in every metric under every feature-selection regime.

The fourth gray-zone audit is the physical meaning of the strain threshold. CSDM requires a threshold; the paper uses an “optimal” strain threshold of 0.2, chosen to maximize significance of the injury risk-response relationship for 50 deep WM regions [PAGE 7]. But the paper also warns that adjusting the threshold could improve scalar-metric fitting while creating inconsistency, and that “the strain threshold is related to the physical injury tolerance found from actual in vivo/in vitro injury experiments” [PAGE 13]. So the threshold is not a free hyperparameter if one wants physical interpretation. This is a key veto against any claim that scalar metrics were tuned unfairly or that deep learning’s advantage is merely threshold choice.

The fifth gray-zone audit is “black box” interpretation. The paper says deep learning “behaves much like a ‘black box’ without an obvious physical interpretation of the its internal decision mechanism” [PAGE 14]. Yet it also notes that RF-based feature selection identified SLF-R and EC-L as dominant regions, and SLF-R was previously injury-discriminative [PAGE 9][PAGE 12]. This creates a tension: the learned classifier’s performance is high, but the physical mechanism is not fully transparent. The correct inference is not “deep learning explains concussion”; it is “deep learning improves prediction on this reconstructed dataset, while feature-importance maps provide partial physical concordance.”

The sixth gray-zone audit is model dependence. The paper says even validated head models could produce discordant brain responses, and that further evaluation with other head injury models is important [PAGE 13]. It also notes WHIM uses isotropic, homogeneous brain material properties and a generic head model with neuroimages from one individual [PAGE 14]. Therefore, the deep-learning result is conditional on WHIM, its fiber-strain calculation, its mesh, and its boundary conditions. Any cross-model generalization claim must be tested or vetoed. The paper also notes that a high training AUC does not guarantee high testing AUC: “A high AUC in a training dataset does not necessarily indicate the same high level of AUC or other performance categories using the testing dataset” [PAGE 13]. That is the central clock: training-time fit versus testing-time validation.

The final gray-zone audit is cumulative exposure. The source explicitly says “this dataset does not consider the cumulative effects from repetitive sub-concussive head impacts, the importance of which is becoming realized” [PAGE 13]. Thus any multi-week or repeated-exposure inference is outside the evidence. The paper’s time-scale is impact reconstruction and LOO classification, not longitudinal clinical follow-up. This is a hard boundary.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the deep network as a sequence of fully connected layer transforms. For layer \(l\), the forward affine map is
\[
x_l = W_l a_{l-1} + b_l ,
\]
and the activation is
\[
a_l = \sigma_l(x_l),
\]
as reported on [PAGE 4]. Here \(a_{l-1}\) is the output vector from the previous layer, \(a_l\) is the output vector of layer \(l\), \(W_l\) is a 2D weight matrix, \(b_l\) is a 1D bias vector, and \(\sigma_l\) is the layer’s activation or normalization function. The weights and biases are dimensionless in the sense that the input features are dimensionless peak WM fiber strain values, so each affine pre-activation and activation is also dimensionless. The raw input \(x_0\) is the strain-encoded image volume; the paper says “Peak WM fiber strain, regardless of the time of occurrence during impact, was computed at each DTI WM voxel (N = 64272; [34]). For voxels not corresponding to WM, their values were padded with zeroes” [PAGE 3]. Strain is an engineering strain in \(m/m\), hence dimensionless; the voxel count \(N=64272\) is a count, not a physical unit. The paper reports that the network contained “over 1.31×108 independent parameters” [PAGE 4]. Table 1 on [PAGE 5] gives the dimensions: \(W_1: 2000\times 64272\); \(W_2: 1000\times 2000\); \(W_3: 500\times 1000\); \(W_4: 250\times 500\); \(W_5: 20\times 250\); \(b_1: 2000\), \(b_2: 1000\), \(b_3: 500\), \(b_4: 250\), \(b_5: 2\). The same table gives \(\sigma_1\): ReLU plus batch normalization; \(\sigma_2\): ReLU; \(\sigma_3\): ReLU; \(\sigma_4\): Sigmoid; \(\sigma_5\): “no normalization (i.e., using an identity matrix)” [PAGE 5].

The output classification uses Softmax. On [PAGE 18] the appendix states:
\[
S_x(j)=\frac{e^{x(j)}}{\sum_k e^{x(k)}},
\]
where \(x(j)\) and \(x(k)\) are the \(j\)-th and \(k\)-th elements of the final feature vector \(x\). This produces a probability vector \((p_1,p_2)\) with \(p_1+p_2=1\), where \(p_1\) is concussion probability and \(p_2\) is non-injury probability. The paper states: “Concussion was said to occur when \(p_1 \ge 0.5\)” [PAGE 5]. Probabilities are dimensionless in \([0,1]\). The Softmax denominator is positive for finite \(x\), but the numerical breakdown regime is exponent overflow or underflow when logits are large in magnitude; a second breakdown regime is a degenerate classifier that assigns probability 0 or 1 to the wrong class, because the cross-entropy logarithm diverges.

The training loss is cross-entropy. On [PAGE 18] the source gives
\[
E_x=-\sum_k\left[t(k)\log S_x(k)+(1-t(k))\log(1-S_x(k))\right],
\]
where \(t(k)\in\{0,1\}\) is the known label. If the logarithm is natural, the cross-entropy unit is nats; if base 2, it is bits; the paper does not specify the base in the excerpt, so the safe statement is “information units, nats for natural log.” The singularity regimes are \(S_x(k)\to 0\) when \(t(k)=1\), and \(S_x(k)\to 1\) when \(t(k)=0\). In practice the ADAM optimizer uses \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\) to prevent division-by-zero-like numerical failures; the paper reports “The default parameter values, \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) and \(\epsilon = 10^{-8}\), were used to prevent the weights from growing too fast” [PAGE 5]. The learning rate is \(2\times10^{-8}\), momentum 0.5, batch size 5, maximum epochs 5000 [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. The early-stopping rule is empirical: the paper says “the network always converged within [1000, 5000] epochs, which was set as an admissible range of epochs” [PAGE 6].

Backpropagation is defined on [PAGE 18]. For a network of \(L\) layers, the output-layer error gradient is
\[
\delta_L=\nabla_{x_L}E \odot \sigma'_L(x_L),
\]
and the recursive gradient is
\[
\delta_l=W_{l+1}^{T}\delta_{l+1}\odot \sigma'_l(x_l).
\]
The parameter gradients are
\[
\nabla_{b_l}E=\delta_l,
\qquad
\nabla_{W_l}E=\delta_l \otimes a_{l-1}^{T}.
\]
Here \(\odot\) is element-wise product and \(\otimes\) is tensor product. The breakdown regimes are: ReLU derivative zero for negative pre-activations, causing dead units; vanishing gradients when repeated derivatives and weight products are small; exploding gradients when repeated weight products are large; and batch-normalization instability if batch statistics are tiny, which is relevant because batch size is 5 [PAGE 5]. The source’s own architectural caveat is: “Likely, this was an indication that the adopted deep learning classifier architecture (Fig. 2) may not be optimal” [PAGE 12].

The performance operators are proportions and AUC. Accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity are dimensionless proportions on the 58 LOO testing predictions. Table 4 on [PAGE 10] reports deep learning accuracy 0.862, sensitivity 0.840, specificity 0.879, testing AUC 0.892; SVM 0.828, 0.760, 0.879, 0.872; RF 0.842±0.016, 0.787±0.027, 0.883±0.021, 0.856. The same table reports BrIC 0.776, 0.640, 0.879, 0.781; CSDM-WB 0.741, 0.640, 0.818, 0.786; CSDM-CC 0.776, 0.760, 0.788, 0.771; Peak-CC 0.690, 0.600, 0.758, 0.737. The abstract’s aggregate comparison is “average accuracy of 0.844 vs. 0.746, and average area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) of 0.873 vs. 0.769” for feature-based versus scalar metrics [PAGE 1]. AUC is dimensionless and threshold-free. The paper’s validation operator is leave-one-out: “only the training dataset (N=57), not including the cross-validation data point, were used for feature selection” [PAGE 7]. The breakdown regime for this operator is small-N correlation among LOO folds and absence of a fully external cohort; the source says fresh training is necessary for other datasets [PAGE 13].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage in the source runs from kinematic severity indices to finite-element tissue-response metrics to feature-based learning. The paper lists rotational injury criterion, PRHIC, BrIC, and RVCI as kinematic metrics, either alone or combined with linear acceleration [PAGE 2]. It then states the crucial scientific correction: “Kinematic variables, alone, do not provide regional brain mechanical responses thought to cause injury [9]” [PAGE 2]. That sentence is the retrocausal hinge: earlier scalar kinematics were useful for impact severity, but they cannot by themselves carry the spatial information that concussion likely requires.

The next lineage layer is finite-element brain modeling and response-based metrics. The paper cites peak maximum principal strain and CSDM as commonly used tissue-response metrics, and notes that WM fiber strain is a more recent candidate [PAGE 2]. The WHIM is described as created from high-resolution T1-weighted MRI of an individual athlete, with DTI providing averaged fiber orientations at each WM voxel [PAGE 3]. This genealogy is important because the deep-learning classifier is not starting from raw acceleration; it is starting from a model-estimated mechanical field. The classifier inherits the assumptions of the FE model: material properties, boundary conditions, mesh resolution, and fiber-orientation assignment. The paper itself notes that another model differed in “material properties (isotropic, homogeneous vs. anisotropic for the WM),” “brain-skull boundary conditions,” “mesh resolution,” and “method to calculate fiber strain” [PAGE 13]. Therefore the genealogy is not “deep learning replaces biomechanics”; it is “deep learning consumes a biomechanical field whose provenance matters.”

The third lineage layer is the scalar-metric trial-and-error tradition. The paper says studies using the same reconstructed NFL impacts found inconsistent “optimal” predictors, including maximum shear stress in the brainstem, strain in gray matter and CSDM0.1 in WM, peak axonal strain in the brainstem, or tract-wise injury susceptibilities in the superior longitudinal fasciculus [PAGE 2]. It calls these efforts “essentially ‘trial-and-error’ in nature” and says “no consensus has reached on the most injury discriminative metric or ROI” [PAGE 2]. This is the retrocausal trap: if many scalar ROIs can be tried, a high training AUC can be a selection artifact rather than a stable physical law. The paper’s remedy is cross-validation and feature-based classification.

The fourth lineage layer is machine learning in medical imaging and neuroscience. The paper cites SVM and RF as widely used classifiers, and deep learning as the recent advancement [PAGE 2]. It notes prior work using SVM for concussion but limited to kinematic variables and two injury cases, which “did not allow for cross-validation” [PAGE 2]. It also cites deep learning for Alzheimer’s detection and a fully connected Restricted Boltzmann Machine for mTBI categories based on DTI parameters [PAGE 3]. The current paper’s novelty is therefore a transfer of feature-based learning from neuroimaging classification to biomechanical tissue-response classification, using voxel-wise WM fiber strain rather than DTI parameters or conventional images.

The multi-week trap in this genealogy is that the source has no week-scale longitudinal endpoint. It reconstructs 58 head impacts and performs LOO classification; it does not model cumulative repetitive sub-concussive exposure. The paper explicitly says “this dataset does not consider the cumulative effects from repetitive sub-concussive head impacts, the importance of which is becoming realized” [PAGE 13]. Therefore a multi-week claim such as “this classifier predicts cumulative weekly injury risk” is a distribution shift, not a finding. The only defensible multi-week statement is: the source provides a cross-sectional impact-level classifier, and any weekly-horizon use would require new longitudinal data and fresh training. A second multi-week trap is model drift: because the classifier is trained on one FE model and one subject’s neuroimages, repeated deployment across weeks or populations without recalibration would violate the paper’s own warning that “a fresh training is necessary” [PAGE 13].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression in this paper is the researcher’s fallback to a single scalar metric under small-sample pressure. The paper describes the field’s habit: define a kinematic or response metric, fit univariate logistic regression, and report AUC from a single training dataset [PAGE 2]. That habit is cognitively attractive because it is interpretable and physically named, but the paper shows its information loss. The scalar peak or volume fraction “do not (and cannot) inform the location or distribution of brain strains” [PAGE 2]. The regression is therefore not just a statistical choice; it is a crisis habit that reduces a field to one number when the outcome is diffuse and spatially structured.

The counter-trap is overcorrecting into a black-box model and treating its score as physical explanation. The paper warns that deep learning “behaves much like a ‘black box’ without an obvious physical interpretation of the its internal decision mechanism” [PAGE 14]. A recursive theory-of-mind reading is that authors anticipate two audiences: biomechanists who demand a physical mechanism, and machine-learning reviewers who demand cross-validation. The paper tries to satisfy both by adding RF-based feature maps showing SLF-R and EC-L as dominant regions [PAGE 9][PAGE 12]. But the counter-trap is claiming that the network has discovered the injury mechanism. The source does not support that; it supports improved prediction and partial regional concordance.

The clock is validation time. The paper’s strongest methodological move is to separate training AUC from testing AUC and to use leave-one-out with independent feature selection. It reports that training AUCs are consistently higher than testing AUCs, and RF even reaches a perfect training AUC of 1.0 [PAGE 11]. That is a clock signal: if one only looks at training time, the model appears perfect; if one looks at testing time, the performance drops. The paper states: “A high AUC in a training dataset does not necessarily indicate the same high level of AUC or other performance categories using the testing dataset” [PAGE 13]. Recursive theory of mind here means anticipating one’s own future overconfidence. The early-stopping criterion is another clock: validation error initially decreases and then increases, indicating overfitting, so the authors stop within an admissible epoch range [PAGE 6].

There is also a social clock: the paper anticipates that an “optimal” model on this dataset may not generalize. It says “an ‘optimal’ neural network model with the ‘best’ performance may not be applicable when it is applied to a more typical general population” [PAGE 12]. This is a counter-trap against leaderboard thinking. The correct mental model is not “maximize LOO accuracy at all costs”; it is “preserve the validation protocol and report the conditions under which the result holds.” The paper’s own limitations section reinforces this: one FE model, one injury dataset, reconstructed kinematics errors, under-sampling of non-injury cases, no cumulative sub-concussive effects, generic head model, and one individual’s neuroimages [PAGE 13][PAGE 14]. A recursive theory of mind must hold all of these simultaneously: the result is promising, the protocol is rigorous for its scope, and the deployment claim is not established.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is direct. If a predictor uses only impact kinematics \(K\) and produces a scalar \(m(K)\), it cannot identify the regional strain field \(\epsilon(x)\) unless additional assumptions are supplied. The paper states that “Kinematic variables, alone, do not provide regional brain mechanical responses thought to cause injury [9]” [PAGE 2]. Formally, let two impact cases have the same kinematic scalar \(m(K_1)=m(K_2)\) but different WM fiber-strain fields \(\epsilon_1(x)\ne\epsilon_2(x)\). Any classifier that depends only on \(m(K)\) assigns the same probability to both cases, so it cannot distinguish regional injury-relevant differences. This is a veto against claiming that BrIC or any kinematic-only scalar is equivalent to the tissue-response field. The paper still uses BrIC as a comparator and even notes a specificity exception: “Only BrIC slightly outperformed deep learning in specificity when the F-score approach was used for feature selection (0.879 vs. 0.818; Tables 3 and 4)” [PAGE 11]. So the veto is not “BrIC is useless”; it is “BrIC cannot substitute for spatial tissue response.”

The biomechanical-layer veto concerns scalar response metrics. CSDM is a volume fraction above a strain threshold. For a region \(V\), a schematic source-consistent form is
\[
\mathrm{CSDM}_{\tau}(V)=\frac{1}{|V|}\int_V \mathbf{1}\{\epsilon(x)>\tau\}\,dx,
\]
with \(\tau=0.2\) in the paper’s CSDM comparison [PAGE 7]. This operator collapses the spatial field to a fraction. Two different strain fields can have the same CSDM but different locations of high strain, and the paper says scalar metrics “do not (and cannot) inform the location or distribution of brain strains” [PAGE 2]. Peak-CC is even more collapsed: it uses “peak WM fiber strain in the corpus callosum” [PAGE 7], a single ROI peak. Therefore any claim that Peak-CC or CSDM fully represents the diffuse fiber-strain field is vetoed by the source’s own information-loss argument. The CSDM threshold is also not freely tunable if physical meaning is desired: the paper says the threshold “is related to the physical injury tolerance found from actual in vivo/in vitro injury experiments” [PAGE 13].

The model-layer veto concerns generalization across head models. The paper states: “As even validated head models could produce discordant brain responses [60], it is important to further evaluate whether similar performance gains are possible with estimated brain responses from other head injury models” [PAGE 13]. It also notes that WHIM used isotropic, homogeneous brain material properties and a generic head model with neuroimages from one individual [PAGE 14]. Therefore a claim that the classifier is model-independent is not supported. The correct adjudication is conditional: the reported 0.862 accuracy and 0.892 testing AUC are conditional on WHIM, its mesh, its boundary conditions, its fiber-strain calculation, and the reconstructed NFL kinematics.

The weather-layer veto is an absence-of-source veto. No meteorological, weather, temperature, humidity, or environmental operator appears in [PAGE 1] through [PAGE 18]. The source’s layers are impact kinematics, FE tissue response, WM fiber orientation, feature selection, and classifier validation. Therefore a weather-based explanation or weather-adjusted injury claim cannot be adjudicated from this corpus; it is unsupported and should be rejected rather than invented. If a downstream system introduces weather as a feature, it is outside the source’s evidence and must be validated separately. The only environmental statement the source supports is that reconstructed impact kinematics contain errors and that model responses can differ across models [PAGE 12][PAGE 13]; those are measurement and model uncertainties, not weather operators.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: feature-selection leakage in leave-one-out validation. An RLVR verifier checks whether the claimed LOO result used the held-out case during feature selection. The source’s rule is explicit: “only the training dataset (N=57), not including the cross-validation data point, were used for feature selection” [PAGE 7]. Prediction: any claimed LOO accuracy that cannot demonstrate N=57-per-fold feature selection is rejected as non-verifiable. If leakage is present, the reported performance is expected to be optimistic relative to the source protocol, and it cannot be compared to the paper’s deep-learning accuracy of 0.862, SVM 0.828, or RF 0.842±0.016 in Table 4 [PAGE 10]. The verifier should require the exact protocol statement and the 58 independent feature-selection trials.

Veto scenario 2: universal-superiority claim for deep learning. An RLVR verifier checks whether a claim says deep learning dominates every baseline in every category. The source does not support that. Table 2 shows that without feature selection, RF sensitivity is 0.774±0.040 while deep learning sensitivity is 0.760 [PAGE 9]. The paper says: “The fact that RF with feature selection outperformed deep learning in sensitivity (when no features were explicitly selected) may indicate that the deep neural network architecture may not be optimal” [PAGE 14]. It also says BrIC slightly outperformed deep learning in specificity under F-score feature selection [PAGE 11]. Prediction: a claim of unconditional dominance is vetoed; the accepted claim must be scoped to the specific feature-selection condition and must retain the BrIC specificity exception and the RF sensitivity exception. A second prediction is that external validation may reverse rankings because the paper warns that no objective comparison can be made with a study that lacked cross-validation [PAGE 12] and that different head models can produce discordant responses [PAGE 13].

Veto scenario 3: deployment or cumulative-exposure claim. An RLVR verifier checks whether a claim says the classifier is ready for general population use or predicts cumulative multi-week injury. The source says the network “was by no means optimal or was ready for deployment in a more typical, general population” [PAGE 14]. It also says “this dataset does not consider the cumulative effects from repetitive sub-concussive head impacts, the importance of which is becoming realized” [PAGE 13]. Prediction: any deployment or cumulative-weekly claim is vetoed unless accompanied by fresh external training and longitudinal validation. A source-consistent prediction is narrower: on the 58 reconstructed NFL impacts, the best Table 4 deep-learning classifier achieved accuracy 0.862, sensitivity 0.840, specificity 0.879, and testing AUC 0.892 [PAGE 10], but those values are conditional on WHIM, the reconstructed kinematics, the N=58 dataset, and the LOO protocol. Any extension beyond that condition is a new hypothesis, not a verified result.
