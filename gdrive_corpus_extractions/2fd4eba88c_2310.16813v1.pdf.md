<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses ordinal draft mappings, count-based overlap, a geometric discount, and rank-aggregation scores. All variables are dimensionless or counts; no SI physical units are defined. The actual draft is \(D:P\to\{1,2,\dots,|D|,\phi\}\), and each mock is \(M_i:P\to\{1,2,\dots,|M_i|,\phi\}\), with \(\phi\) for undrafted [PAGE 3]. The source states “Usually, |D| = 60” [PAGE 2], and notes that mock drafts may include “anywhere from a handful of players up to about 150” while draft-eligible players are “usually 300 or so” [PAGE 3]. Missingness is treated as censoring: “One could consider this missing/censored data” [PAGE 3].

The overlap operator is
\[
X_d=\left|M^{-1}(\{1,\dots,d\})\cap D^{-1}(\{1,\dots,d\})\right|,
\]
the number of players selected in both lists through pick \(d\), so \(X_d\) has units of players/picks, while \(X_d/d\) is a dimensionless agreement fraction. Rank-biased overlap is
\[
\mathrm{RBO}(M,D,q)=\frac{1-q}{q}\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\frac{X_d}{d}q^d,
\]
with \(q\) dimensionless and \(0<q<1\) [PAGE 6]. The source says “For our purposes, we chose q = 0.98” and that this implies “about 89% of the metric’s weight is on the 60 picks in the draft (thus 11% to those who are undrafted), which includes 51% of the weight on the lottery/the first 14 picks” [PAGE 6]. The formal boundedness assumption is explicit: “for 0 < q < 1, the weighted sum is constrained to the [0,1] interval” [PAGE 6]. Rank-biased distance is
\[
\mathrm{RBD}=1-\widehat{\mathrm{RBO}},
\]
so RBD is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\) under the same restriction [PAGE 6].

For finite lists, the source writes extrapolated RBO with shorter length \(s\), longer length \(\ell\), and tuning parameter \(p\) in the equation:
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{RBO}}(M,D,p)=
\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\sum_{d=1}^{s}p^d\frac{X_d}{d}\right)
+\left(\frac{1-p}{p}\sum_{d=s+1}^{\ell}p^d\left[\frac{X_d}{d}+\frac{X_s}{s}\left(1-\frac{s}{d}\right)\right]\right)
+\left\{p^\ell\left(\frac{X_\ell}{\ell}+\frac{X_s}{s}\left[1-\frac{s}{\ell}\right]\right)\right\}.
\]
The extrapolation assumption is stated: “The other term in square braces assumes that the observed agreement of the short list is a good estimate of the agreement of the unobserved pieces” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: \(q=0\) makes the prefactor \((1-q)/q\) singular; \(q=1\) removes geometric damping and the infinite normalization; lengths \(s=0\) or \(\ell=0\) create division by \(s\) or \(\ell\); and RBOEXT is biased if the unobserved tail agreement differs from the observed short-list agreement. The source restricts \(0<q<1\) precisely to avoid the unbounded/undefined regime [PAGE 6].

Borda aggregation is
\[
\mathrm{BORDA}_p=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{author}}}\left[|D|-\hat M_i(p)+1\right],
\]
with
\[
\hat M(p)=
\begin{cases}
M(p), & M(p)\neq\phi,\\
|D|+1, & M(p)=\phi.
\end{cases}
\]
The score has dimensionless “rank points,” not SI units [PAGE 7]. The source warns: “One downside of Borda count is that an outlier mock draft, like Mock E here, can have an out-sized impact in the result” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown occurs when one author ranks an otherwise omitted player very high, as in the Robert Horry example, or when many authors omit a player and zero-point handling dominates.

RCA uses ballots \( \mathrm{ballot}_i=\arg\min_{p\in P^{\bullet\bullet}}M_i(p)\) and votes \( \mathrm{votes}_p=|\{i:\mathrm{ballot}_i=p\}|\) [PAGE 9]. A player is selected when \(\mathrm{votes}_{\tilde p}>\tfrac12 n_{\mathrm{authors}}\); otherwise the player with the fewest positive votes is dropped. The source’s tie rules include: “If there is a tie for fewest votes, the player with the highest average mock draft position is selected to be dropped in line 13” and “In the event that an author published a mock draft with tiers, then players would be tied and each player counted in line 5” [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: no majority with all positive vote counts tied; very small \(n_{\mathrm{authors}}\); correlated authors producing false majorities; and \(D>|P|\), which the algorithm’s note excludes by requiring \(D\le|P|\) [PAGE 9]. In-season aggregation has an additional protocol assumption: it “only looks at mock drafts that were posted in the past 10 days, and only use each author’s most recent mock draft” [PAGE 10]. Final-mock evaluation uses the last mock within 30 days, and regular authors are those with three or more seasons [PAGE 10, PAGE 16].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive reasoning appears at several levels. A mock-draft author forecasts what teams will do; teams forecast what other teams will do; an aggregator forecasts what authors will forecast; and the evaluator forecasts how well the aggregate forecasts the realized draft. The paper’s ranked-list assumption collapses team-specific preferences into a single league-wide ordering: “this effectively ignores team-specific preferences and assumes the actual draft reveals the single NBA-wide ordering of players” [PAGE 2]. A second counter-trap is to treat author independence as real: many authors read the same public information, so a majority can be a shared-information cascade rather than independent evidence. The clock layer is the pre-draft calendar: the 10-day rolling window and most-recent-author rule [PAGE 10], the 30-day final-mock window [PAGE 10, PAGE 16], the NCAA tournament kink [PAGE 10], and the lottery/combine/withdrawal deadlines marked in Figure 2 [PAGE 11]. The paper’s evidence supports the last channel strongly: mocks improve over the season and are stable near the draft [PAGE 10–12], but the source does not isolate hidden-preference modeling from information timing.
