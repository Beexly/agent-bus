Spalding's Official Foot Ball Guide belongs to the late-nineteenth-century tradition of standardized athletic annuals; the Spalding publishing line began codifying rules and records in the 1870s–1880s, making this 1915 page a descendant of early sports information systems and a distant ancestor of modern sports databases. Its epistemic lineage is annual, print-based, and post-season: results are compiled after play, not streamed in real time, and there is no play-by-play, down-and-distance, or player-level telemetry. The scientific lineage relevant here is therefore record-keeping and tabular data curation, not experimental measurement.

A multi-week trap is embedded in any attempt to project from this snapshot. The 1915 college football season was still in a transitional rules era (the forward pass had been legalized in 1906, but mass formations and limited substitution persisted), rosters turned over annually, coaching tenures were short, and travel was by railroad. A strength estimate inferred from these scores is non-stationary with respect to later weeks: a team dominant in early-October 1915 could be depleted by injuries or scheduling attrition by late November. Treating the printed guide as if it contained ex ante certainty is a retrocausal illusion—it mistakes a terminal, mediated record for a predictive state variable.

Moreover, the OCR mediation adds a second retrocausal layer: the researcher in 2025 observes a degraded digital surrogate of a 1915 print artifact. The "data" are not the games themselves but a lossy transcription of a summary of the games. Any causal claim about team quality that ignores both the historical season dynamics and the OCR channel conflates the record with the event.
