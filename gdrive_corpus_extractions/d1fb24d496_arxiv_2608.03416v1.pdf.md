The retrocausal lesson is therefore not that the benchmark is invalid, but that its causal claims must be time-indexed. The source’s own conclusion is appropriately narrow: the final table “should not be read as proof that one model simply ‘understands football’ better than all others” [PAGE 16]. The defensible genealogy is from scoring-rule theory to prospective evaluation, with AI World Cup as a fixed-snapshot, manually collected, path-weighted case study.

The recursive problem is that the evaluator must model the model’s model of the tournament. In this source, “crisis habit regression” appears as modal champion selection under uncertainty. What is measured is the distribution: four Brazil, three France, two Argentina, one Spain [PAGE 8]. The counter-trap is to avoid rewarding that modal habit merely because it is common. The benchmark’s knockout component does reward path construction, but it also over-rewards one realized bracket; the source warns that the ranking was “especially sensitive to knockout progression and the realised bracket” [PAGE 13].

The second recursive layer is self-confidence. A model’s stated confidence is a belief report about its selected scoreline, not a probability distribution. The source is explicit that “Each model supplied one confidence value for its selected scoreline rather than a probability distribution over possible outcomes” [PAGE 9], and “The reported figures therefore describe expressed certainty, not calibrated predictive probabilities” [PAGE 9]. The highest-confidence model, Mistral Medium 3.5 at 73.27%, was not the most accurate; the overall winner reported one of the lowest average confidence values, 60.68% [PAGE 9]. The correlation with outcome accuracy is \(r=-0.060\) [PAGE 9]. The recursive trap is to treat a model’s self-description as a second-order probability. The counter-trap is to demand proper scoring and repeated runs; the source recommends exactly that [PAGE 14].

The third layer is the clock. AI World Cup uses one pre-tournament snapshot and does not refresh before individual matches [PAGE 11]. That creates a clean prospective test but also a clock-dependent attribution problem: later errors may reflect stale information rather than weak reasoning [PAGE 11]. The source’s within-provider comparison illustrates the caution. GPT-5.5 Thinking scored 27 more aggregate points than GPT-5.5 despite lower group-stage outcome accuracy, 58.33% versus 62.50%, and a lower combined group-phase score, 502 versus 525 [PAGE 10]. The source calls this “one useful, although very limited, within-provider comparison” and says “one pair of submissions cannot establish that explanation” [PAGE 10]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore distinguish three clocks: the forecast clock, the tournament clock, and the evaluation clock. The counter-trap is to freeze the scoring rule before the forecast clock starts, preserve the raw response at the forecast clock, and evaluate only after the tournament clock resolves.
