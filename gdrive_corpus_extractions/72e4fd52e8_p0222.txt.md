Third, we analyze James’s counter-argument, which relies on immediate phenomenological observation rather than theoretical deduction. It contains adjectives that inherently imply depth, weight, and spatial occupation without requiring a learned correlation between eye muscle tension and object distance. If depth were purely an intellectual suggestion, the raw sensation of the sky would be flat; instead, it feels like a boundless void extending infinitely away from the observer.

Finally, the gray-zone emerges in the synthesis of these elements. James does not entirely discard muscular or retinal factors; he admits the judgment of distance results from their combination [PAGE 1]. However, he relocates the "singularity" of depth perception from the intellectual domain back into the sensory domain. The derivation concludes that while judgments of distance may be complex and sometimes erratic, the fundamental capacity to perceive the world as having volume is an innate, pre-conceptual feature of human vision, fundamentally severing the tie to Berkeley’s tactile causality.
