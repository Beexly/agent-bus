<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s reasoning chain has five links. First, a single shot \(i\) is modeled as a Bernoulli goal indicator \(Y_i\) with conditional probability \(p_i=P(Y_i=1\mid x_i)\). Second, \(p_i\) is converted into a weighted shot \(w_i=\hat p_i\), whose unit is expected goals per shot. Third, weighted shots are summed over skaters, goalies, and teams to produce expected-goals, adjusted save percentage, adjusted shooting percentage, and weighted-shots-per-60 metrics. Fourth, those derived metrics are tested for split-half reliability and for predictive correlation against traditional metrics. Fifth, weighted shots per 60 are used as the outcome in a ridge-regression adjusted plus-minus model.

The key gray-zone audit is that the paper distinguishes explanation from prediction. The single-shot model has area under the ROC curve \(0.764\), which the authors call good discrimination [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 6]. That is evidence that shot details help rank goal versus non-goal shots. It is not, by itself, evidence that adjusted save percentage is a superior predictor of a goalie’s future talent. The paper explicitly states that “the evidence is far from overwhelming, and firm conclusions should not be drawn from this analysis” [PAGE 2]. It also says, at the team level, “we did not find evidence that weighted shots per 60 minutes generated by a team’s offense or allowed by a team’s defense performed better than shots, Fenwick (shots + missed shots), or Corsi (shots + missed shots + blocked shots) per 60 minutes” [PAGE 2]. This is the central counter-trap: weighted shots are useful, but not a replacement.

A second gray zone is measurement. The model uses distance, angle, rebound status, shot type, strength situation, score differential, home indicator, angle changes, shooter fatigue, and time-on-ice variables. But rink recording bias can distort distance. The paper cites Ryder: “Alan Ryder found evidence of rink bias in the reporting of shot distance for shots taken at Madison Square Garden” [PAGE 12]. Therefore the authors state: “we will continue using performance in away games only throughout the rest of Section 5” [PAGE 12]. This is a serious audit point: all-games goalie rankings can be shifted by venue recording practices. The all-games table and away-only table differ: Lundqvist leads the 2008–2011 all-games table, but Thomas leads the away-only table [PAGE 11]. A causal talent claim from the all-games table is not adjudicable without the away-only correction.

A third gray zone is the fatigue and time-on-ice block. The paper reports “Our new variable “shooter fatigue” is significant and has the sign we would expect” [PAGE 5]. But it also reports “Interestingly, “Defending Team Time on Ice” is near zero and is marginally significant” [PAGE 5], and “Also, “Shooting Team Time on Ice” is positive” [PAGE 5]. The authors warn: “This variable may be quantifying offensive pressure rather than fatigue in a case like that” [PAGE 5]. Thus the biomechanical interpretation is not clean. A long shift can mean fatigue, but it can also mean sustained offensive-zone pressure, power-play structure, or deployment. The model’s coefficients are conditional associations, not isolated physiological effects.

A fourth gray zone is clustering and independence. Shots faced by the same goalie are not independent in an unmodeled sense. The paper’s own future-work section says: “One model extension we will explore is the use of random effects to address possible correlations in the data. Our first effort will be to fit a random effects model with the goalie as the cluster variable” [PAGE 18]. That admission implies that current standard errors and goalie rankings should be treated as provisional. A random-effects or clustered model could widen intervals enough to erase small adjusted-save-percentage differences.

A fifth gray zone is the adjusted plus-minus design. The paper uses ridge regression because of collinearity: teammates often appear together, making the design matrix rank-deficient or near-rank-deficient. The coefficients are interpreted as a player’s contribution “in terms of goals per 60 minutes, independent of all of the other players in the league” [PAGE 16]. But ridge shrinkage, zone indicators, and the choice of outcome variable all affect the estimates. The paper says “we use ridge regression similar to what was used in Sill (2010), Macdonald (2011b), and Macdonald (2012)” [PAGE 16]. Ridge solves singularity, but it does not create information that was absent from the design. If two players are almost always on the ice together, their separate effects remain weakly identified even after shrinkage.

The overall audit is therefore: the paper is strongest at the single-shot level, moderate at the goalie/team explanation level, and cautious at the prediction level. Its final conclusion is explicit: “We did not find any strong evidence that weighted shots outperforms shots” [PAGE 18], while still recommending weighted shots “in addition to, but not as a replacement for, shots” [PAGE 18]. That is the correct gray-zone verdict.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) be the goal indicator for shot \(i\), and let \(x_i=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{iK})\) be the covariate vector: distance, angle, rebound, own rebound, shot type, detailed strength, angle change, shooter fatigue, shooting-team time on ice, defending-team time on ice, score differential, home indicator, and interactions [PAGE 3–5]. The paper’s logistic model is reconstructed as
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p_i)
=
\log\!\left(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}\right)
=
\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\beta_j x_{ij},
\qquad
p_i=P(Y_i=1\mid x_i)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left[-\left(\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\beta_j x_{ij}\right)\right]}.
\]
Here \(p_i\) is dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). The coefficient \(\beta_j\) has units of logit change per one unit of \(x_j\). For continuous covariates, examples from Table 9 are distance \(\hat\beta=-0.054\) per foot, angle \(\hat\beta=-0.017\) per degree, shooter fatigue \(\hat\beta=-0.025\) per second, shooting-team time on ice \(\hat\beta=0.022\) per second, and defending-team time on ice \(\hat\beta=0.001\) per second [PAGE 18]. Converting distance to SI, \(-0.054\) per foot is approximately \(-0.177\) per metre because \(1\text{ ft}=0.3048\text{ m}\). Converting angle to radians, \(-0.017\) per degree is approximately \(-0.974\) per radian because \(1\text{ rad}=180/\pi\) degrees. Categorical variables are indicator contrasts, so their coefficients are dimensionless logit shifts. The fitted single-shot model reports \(0.764\) area under the ROC curve [PAGE 6]. Formally,
\[
\mathrm{AUC}
=
P(\hat p_i>\hat p_j\mid Y_i=1,Y_j=0)
+
\frac{1}{2}P(\hat p_i=\hat p_j\mid Y_i=1,Y_j=0),
\]
which is dimensionless. The paper says “A value over 0.7 is generally considered good discrimination meaning the model is useful for determining the response for a given shot” [PAGE 6].

The weighted-shot operator is
\[
w_i=\hat p_i \quad [\text{expected goals per shot}],
\qquad
W_g=\sum_{i\in I_g} w_i \quad [\text{expected goals}].
\]
Per-60 aggregation is
\[
\mathrm{WShots/60}_g
=
\frac{60}{\sum_{t\in T_g}\Delta t_t}
\sum_{i\in I_g}w_i,
\]
where \(\Delta t_t\) is ice time in minutes; if seconds are used, replace \(60\) by \(3600\). For a goalie \(g\), let \(n_g\) be shots against, \(GA_g=\sum_{i\in I_g}Y_i\), and \(\mathrm{ExpGA}_g=\sum_{i\in I_g}\hat p_i\). Then
\[
\mathrm{Sv\%}_g=1-\frac{GA_g}{n_g},
\qquad
\mathrm{ExpSv\%}_g=1-\frac{\mathrm{ExpGA}_g}{n_g},
\]
\[
\mathrm{AdjSv\%}_g
=
\overline{\mathrm{Sv\%}}
+
\left(\mathrm{Sv\%}_g-\mathrm{ExpSv\%}_g\right)
=
\overline{\mathrm{Sv\%}}
-
\frac{GA_g-\mathrm{ExpGA}_g}{n_g}.
\]
All save-percentage terms are dimensionless probabilities. The paper defines adjusted save percentage as “the league average save percentage plus DiffSv%, the difference in Sv% and ExpSv%” [PAGE 10]. Goals prevented is
\[
\mathrm{DiffGA}_g=\mathrm{ExpGA}_g-GA_g \quad [\text{goals}].
\]
For skaters, with \(EG_s=\sum_{i\in I_s}\hat p_i\), \(G_s=\sum_{i\in I_s}Y_i\), and \(n_s\) shots,
\[
\mathrm{ExpSh\%}_s=\frac{EG_s}{n_s},
\qquad
\mathrm{Sh\%}_s=\frac{G_s}{n_s},
\qquad
\mathrm{DiffG}_s=G_s-EG_s,
\]
\[
\mathrm{AdjSh\%}_s
=
\overline{\mathrm{Sh\%}}
+
\left(\mathrm{Sh\%}_s-\mathrm{ExpSh\%}_s\right).
\]
The paper describes adjusted shooting percentage as “league average shooting percentage plus DiffSh%, the difference in Sh% and ExpSh%” [PAGE 8]. These are dimensionless probabilities or goal counts as indicated.

For reliability and predictive checks, the paper uses split-half correlations: statistic \(A\) in one half of a season versus statistic \(B\) in the other half. The correlation coefficient
\[
r_{AB}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)}{\sigma_A\sigma_B}
\]
is dimensionless. The paper reports that “adjusted save percentage is slightly more consistent than save percentage” [PAGE 13], but also “we have not found the differences in these correlations to be statistically significant” [PAGE 13]. At the team level, “Adjusted save percentage does not seem to be a better predictor than save percentage at the team level” [PAGE 14], and “weighted shots per 60 minutes is much more consistent than goals per 60 minutes, but is not more consistent than shots, Fenwick (shots+missed shots), or Corsi (shots+missed shots+blocked shots) per 60 minutes” [PAGE 15].

For adjusted plus-minus, the paper uses ridge regression. Let \(y_t\) be the outcome for a no-substitution shift segment \(t\), either goals per 60 or weighted shots per 60, and let \(X_t\) contain player indicators and zone-start indicators. The estimator is
\[
\hat\beta
=
\arg\min_{\beta}
\sum_{t}\left(y_t-X_t\beta\right)^2
+
\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2,
\qquad
\hat\beta=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.
\]
The coefficient for a player is in goals per 60 minutes, or expected goals per 60 minutes when weighted shots are the outcome. The paper says “we use ridge regression similar to what was used in Sill (2010), Macdonald (2011b), and Macdonald (2012)” [PAGE 16], and interprets each coefficient as “an estimate of a player’s contribution to his team in terms of goals per 60 minutes, independent of all of the other players in the league” [PAGE 16].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes. The logistic model assumes independent Bernoulli shots conditional on \(x_i\), correct functional form, no unmodeled clustering, and unbiased measurement of \(x_i\). If quasi-complete separation occurs, the unpenalized maximum-likelihood estimate diverges and \(p_i\) saturates near \(0\) or \(1\); that is a singular regime. If \(X^\top X\) is singular in adjusted plus-minus because teammates co-appear in nearly every segment, ordinary least squares is non-identifiable; ridge with \(\lambda>0\) restores a unique solution, but as \(\lambda\to0\) the solution can become unstable again, and as \(\lambda\to\infty\) all coefficients shrink toward zero. If distance measurement error is classical and independent, the distance coefficient is attenuated toward zero; if the error is venue-dependent, as with rink bias, the bias can go in either direction and invalidates cross-venue comparisons [PAGE 12]. If shots within a goalie are correlated, Bernoulli standard errors are too small; the paper’s proposed random-effects model with “the goalie as the cluster variable” [PAGE 18] is the correct repair. Finally, if samples are below the paper’s goalie threshold of “a minimum of 1,000 shots” [PAGE 13], split-half correlations become unstable and should not be interpreted as talent.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a layered genealogy of shot-quality and plus-minus models. The shot-quality branch runs through Krzywicki’s logistic shot-quality models, Awad’s use of expected goals in plus-minus-style evaluation, and Schuckers’s non-parametric Defense Independent Goaltender Rating (DIGR) [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 7]. The paper positions itself as a Krzywicki-style logistic model with new fatigue and time-on-ice variables [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 18]. The rink-bias branch runs through Ryder’s 2004 and 2007 work, especially the warning about Madison Square Garden shot-distance recording [PAGE 12]. The team-control branch runs through Desjardins’s claim that teams do not control shot distance allowed very well [PAGE 12]. The adjusted plus-minus branch runs from basketball into hockey: Rosenbaum, Lewin, Ilardi and Barzilai, Witus, Sill, and Fearnhead and Taylor are cited as basketball precedents, while Macdonald’s hockey papers are the direct ancestors of the ridge model used here [PAGE 16] and [PAGE 23–24]. The paper’s own contribution is not a new shot-quality idea from nothing; it is a synthesis plus a reliability/predictive audit.

The retrocausal traps are multi-week and multi-season traps. First, the “AUC trap”: because the single-shot ROC area is \(0.764\), an analyst may retroactively infer that adjusted save percentage must be a better talent predictor. The paper blocks that inference: single-shot discrimination and future-talent prediction are different tasks, and the goalie evidence is only “some evidence” and not statistically decisive [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 13]. Second, the “rink-bias trap”: a few weeks or a single home-heavy stretch can make a goalie’s adjusted save percentage look different because the venue’s distance recording is biased. The paper’s away-only correction changes the 2008–2011 leader from Lundqvist to Thomas [PAGE 11]. Third, the “single-season trap”: goalie performance fluctuates, so one season’s goals-prevented table is not a stable talent estimate [PAGE 10]. Fourth, the “replacement trap”: because weighted shots correlate with goals and are more stable than goals, an analyst may replace shots, Fenwick, or Corsi with weighted shots. The paper explicitly refuses that replacement and recommends conjunction instead [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 18]. Fifth, the “collinearity trap”: adjusted plus-minus can produce dramatic player rankings, but teammates who always play together are weakly separated; ridge shrinks the estimates, and the shrinkage is not a causal identification strategy [PAGE 16]. The correct historical reading is that weighted shots are an explanatory and descriptive layer, not a universal upgrade over traditional counting statistics.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the evaluator’s fallback under uncertainty to a single metric as if it were a replacement. In one direction, the habit is “advanced metric supremacy”: because weighted shots use shot location and fatigue, treat adjusted save percentage or weighted-shots-per-60 as the true talent measure. The paper vetoes that habit: “the evidence is far from overwhelming, and firm conclusions should not be drawn from this analysis” [PAGE 2], and weighted shots should be used “in addition to, but not as a replacement for, shots” [PAGE 18]. In the other direction, the habit is “traditional-metric supremacy”: because weighted shots did not beat shots, Fenwick, or Corsi, dismiss weighted shots entirely. That is the counter-trap. The paper says weighted shots “still performed well” and are “reasonable to use … in conjunction with” traditional statistics [PAGE 18]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the analyst must model both overclaim and overcorrection: the model’s output is a conditional probability, not a talent verdict; the analyst’s interpretation is a belief about that probability; the critic’s interpretation is a belief about the analyst; and the auditor’s job is to prevent either belief from hardening into a replacement claim.

The clock matters because the paper’s claims are time-horizon dependent. At the single-shot clock, the model has good discrimination [PAGE 6]. At the half-season clock, adjusted save percentage is only slightly more reliable than save percentage, and the difference is not statistically significant [PAGE 13]. At the multi-season clock, the away-only goalie table changes the ranking [PAGE 11]. At the team clock, weighted shots per 60 is more reliable than goals per 60 but not more reliable than shots, Fenwick, or Corsi per 60 [PAGE 15]. A multi-week window is therefore too short for stable goalie or team talent inference; it is long enough only for descriptive shot-quality accounting. The recursive counter-trap is to confuse “this stretch looks adjusted” with “this is the underlying ability.” The paper’s own caution about goalie year-to-year fluctuation [PAGE 10] and its proposed random-effects extension [PAGE 18] are the correct clock-aware repairs.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The model’s physical inputs are distance from goal, shot angle, rebound status, angle change, and shot type [PAGE 3–4]. These depend on rink dimensions and \((x,y)\) recording. If \((x,y)\) is missing, the paper cannot apply the full model: for 2007–08 the authors say they used Krzywicki’s model because they did not have \((x,y)\) coordinates for each shot [PAGE 16, footnote]. If venue recording is biased, cross-venue adjusted save percentage is not ceteris paribus; the Madison Square Garden warning is the explicit veto [PAGE 12]. The away-only correction is a partial physical-layer proof, not a complete one. A claim that a goalie’s adjusted save percentage is venue-independent is vetoed unless home-rink recording bias is modeled or removed.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The model includes shooter fatigue and shooting/defending team time on ice [PAGE 5]. The shooter-fatigue coefficient is negative and significant, consistent with fatigue reducing goal probability [PAGE 5]. But the defending-team time-on-ice coefficient is “near zero and is marginally significant,” and the shooting-team time-on-ice coefficient is positive [PAGE 5]. The authors themselves warn that this may quantify “offensive pressure rather than fatigue” [PAGE 5]. Thus a causal biomechanical claim such as “longer shifts reduce goal probability through fatigue alone” is vetoed unless pressure, zone, power-play structure, and deployment are separated. The model supports a conditional association, not a physiological dose-response law.

Weather-layer veto. The paper is an indoor NHL study and contains no weather variable. Direct weather effects are therefore outside the model’s adjudication boundary. If the claim is extended to outdoor games, travel stress, ice quality, or environmental effects, the model has no evidence to support it. The correct veto rule is: any external claim that weather or environment changes shot quality must be tested with a venue/weather layer; the current weighted-shots model cannot adjudicate it. The same logic applies to unmodeled physical factors such as equipment, injury status, and score-effects beyond the linear score-difference term.

Cross-layer proof summary. A weighted-shots claim is admissible only if (i) shot location is measured without venue-dependent bias or the bias is corrected; (ii) fatigue and time-on-ice variables are not confounded with tactical pressure; (iii) goalie clustering is modeled or acknowledged; and (iv) the time window is long enough for the claimed reliability. The paper satisfies (i) partially through away-only analysis, satisfies (ii) only conditionally, and explicitly leaves (iii) for future random-effects work [PAGE 18]. Therefore the strongest admissible claim is descriptive: weighted shots explain past shot quality and can sit alongside traditional metrics. The strongest inadmissible claim is replacement or isolated causal talent attribution.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
