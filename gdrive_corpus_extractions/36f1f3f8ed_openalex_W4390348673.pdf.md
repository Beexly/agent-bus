<THINK_TRACE>
**Multi-hop derivation.** The paper’s elevation problem begins with a measurement chain: a wrist-worn GNSS receiver estimates pseudoranges from satellite signals; those are converted to coordinates and to an ellipsoidal height \(h\) referenced to WGS-84; the receiver or its geoid model converts that to an orthometric height \(H\) or a mean-sea-level height using the geoid undulation \(N\). The source states the relation \(H \approx h - N\) and defines \(H\) as “orthometric height to the geoid,” \(h\) as “ellipsoidal height,” and \(N\) as “undulation value (the difference between geoid and ellipsoid)” [PAGE 2]. The next hop is the reference surface: the study compares receiver heights against Poland’s Digital Terrain Model derived from LIDAR, with a stated mean elevation error “up to 0.2 m” and a recommendation that the reference system be “10 times better than the accuracy of the device being tested” [PAGE 3]. The third hop is the per-point error \(z_{\mathrm{rec},i}-z_{\mathrm{DTM},i}\), summarized by RMS and mean error in metres, and the fourth hop is the summed elevation-gain error expressed as a percent via the source’s TEG equation [PAGE 3]. The empirical result is conditional: the best three S9B units with barometric altimeters had RMS 3.6–4.1 m, the worst no-altimeter units had RMS 14.0–22.1 m, yet two no-altimeter units still obtained 6.4 and 7.6 m, and 90% of receivers underestimated TEG [PAGE 5]. Thus the derivation does not support a single-cause story: barometric support helps on average, but device model, firmware satellite selection, route geometry, and user settings remain in the causal chain.

**Gray-zone audit.** The study is an observational field comparison, not a randomized device trial. The paper says “A total of 20 tracks from among 295 race participants were collected, including tracks from seven athletes from the top 20 of the list” [PAGE 3]. It also states that “nineteen people (95%) used default settings (which in 14 cases—70%—was the GPS system, while in five—25%—GPS+Glonass)” [PAGE 4]. This creates a selection-and-setting confound: the receivers were configured by athletes, not by researchers. The recording frequency was “1/s for most of them,” yielding “about 2.2 thousand coordinate determinations per receiver” [PAGE 4], and the computations were corrected by “an offset of 1.0 m” for receiver location above ground [PAGE 4]. Those are sensible controls for mean vertical offset, but they do not control high-frequency wrist oscillation, horizontal route misregistration, barometric auto-calibration drift, weather-pressure change, or firmware differences. The paper itself lists limitations: device preparation and supervision by the researcher rather than the user, better horizontal route acquisition, investigation of “the (vertical) fluctuations of the wrist-worn receiver,” and more than one unit per model [PAGE 7]. A gray-zone trap is to read the Table 1 RMS ordering as a pure hardware ranking; the authors explicitly note “no advantage was noted for dual-system GNSS receivers as compared to the single-system ones” [PAGE 6]. Another trap is to equate TEG percent error with instantaneous vertical accuracy; TEG sums many increments and can cancel or accumulate errors. The safest interpretation is conditional: barometric altimetry is associated with lower RMS in this sample, but not with elimination of TEG underestimation, because “Neither the reception of two GNSS systems nor the use of a barometric altimeter appears to affect this value” [PAGE 5].

**Singularity and breakdown audit.** The geoid equation \(H \approx h-N\) breaks down if \(N\) is absent, if the geoid model and the ellipsoidal height use inconsistent datums, or if the local vertical datum is not the intended one; the source notes that a receiver must have or download “an accurate geoid model for a predetermined longitude and latitude” to provide height relative to MSL [PAGE 3]. The RMS is undefined at \(n=0\) and is contaminated if receiver and DTM samples are not matched in time and horizontal route. The TEG expression has an explicit mathematical singularity when \(\sum \mathrm{EG}_{\mathrm{pseudoref}}=0\); near zero the percent error becomes unbounded. It also breaks down if the receiver and pseudoreference elevation increments are not aligned to the same route segmentation or if sign conventions differ. There is no physical singularity in the geoid equation itself, but there is a datum/geoid-model breakdown regime. The paper’s second-half result is a useful gray-zone signal: higher mean elevation error occurred on the ridge section where satellite availability “should be (and is) better” [PAGE 5], so satellite visibility alone cannot explain the error profile.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**1. Geoid-to-orthometric-height operator.** The source gives
\[
H \approx h - N,
\]
where \(H\) is orthometric height to the geoid, \(h\) is ellipsoidal height, and \(N\) is geoid undulation, all in metres. The source defines these terms verbatim: “where H is the orthometric height to the geoid; h is the ellipsoidal height, and N is the undulation value (the difference between geoid and ellipsoid)” [PAGE 2]. Assumptions: a consistent geoid model and vertical datum; ellipsoidal height referenced to the WGS-84 ellipsoid. Breakdown regimes: missing or inaccurate \(N\); datum mismatch; receiver report of \(h\) or \(H\) without documenting which is used. The paper notes that the reading of a single height by a sports receiver “will not be identical to the height known from topographic maps” [PAGE 2].

**2. Vertical RMS reconstruction.** The paper uses RMS with a stated probability interpretation: “root mean square (RMS, associated with a probability level of 68%)” [PAGE 3]. A formal reconstruction consistent with that usage is
\[
\mathrm{RMS}_{z}
=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left(z_{\mathrm{rec},i}-z_{\mathrm{DTM},i}\right)^{2}},
\]
with \(z_{\mathrm{rec},i}\) and \(z_{\mathrm{DTM},i}\) in metres, \(n\) the number of matched observations, and output in metres. Assumptions: samples are paired in time and horizontal position; vertical datum is consistent; outliers are not removed unless stated. Breakdown: \(n=0\); horizontal misregistration; vertical-datum offset; route trimming mismatch. The source reports the best three same-model receivers at “3.6 to 4.1 m” and the highest RMS values for no-barometer units at “22.1, 19.7, and 14.0 m” [PAGE 1; PAGE 5].

**3. Mean elevation error.** The paper also uses mean elevation error in metres, which can be written
\[
\bar{e}_{z}
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left(z_{\mathrm{rec},i}-z_{\mathrm{DTM},i}\right).
\]
Units: metres. Assumptions and breakdown regimes are the same as for RMS; a near-zero mean can coexist with large RMS because positive and negative errors cancel. Table 1 reports separate mean elevation errors for “Start to checkpoint” and “Checkpoint to finish” [PAGE 5].

**4. Total elevation gain error.** The source prints
\[
\mathrm{TEG}(\%)
=
\left[
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{EG}_{\mathrm{rec},i}\times 100}
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{EG}_{\mathrm{pseudoref},i}}
\right]-100,
\]
where the source identifies \(\mathrm{EG}_{\mathrm{rec}}\) as the “single elevation gain error specified by the tested receiver” and \(\mathrm{EG}_{\mathrm{pseudoref}}\) as the elevation gain based on DEM for coordinates specified by the tested receiver, with \(n\) observations [PAGE 3]. Units: percent; dimensional consistency requires the numerator and denominator increments to be in metres before the factor 100. Assumptions: same route segmentation, same sign convention for ascent, and nonzero pseudoreference gain. Singularity: \(\sum \mathrm{EG}_{\mathrm{pseudoref}}=0\) makes TEG undefined; near zero it is unbounded. Breakdown: cancellation of gains and losses; route mismatch; horizontal DEM misregistration. The abstract reports a mean value of “−3.8%” and says it “does not appear to be affected by the reception of two global navigation satellite systems or the use of a barometric altimeter” [PAGE 1].

**5. Reference-quality condition.** The source states that the reference system “should be 10 times better than the accuracy of the device being tested” [PAGE 3]. Formally,
\[
\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}\le \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{DUT}}}{10},
\]
with both standard deviations in metres and DUT meaning device under test. The DTM’s mean elevation error “falls within a range of up to 0.2 m” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if the tested receiver’s true vertical error approaches the DTM error, the reference is no longer sufficient for adjudication.

**6. External physical operator for barometric adjudication.** The paper does not print a barometric equation, but barometric-altimeter veto logic requires the standard hypsometric relation
\[
h_{b}
=
\frac{R_{d}T_{v}}{g_{0}}
\ln\left(\frac{P_{0}}{P}\right),
\]
with \(h_b\) in metres, \(R_d\) in \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(T_v\) in kelvin, \(g_0\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and pressures in pascals. Assumptions: hydrostatic atmosphere, known reference pressure \(P_0\), and no rapid weather front. Breakdown: pressure and temperature changes, sensor drift, or unknown start calibration. This is a physical adjudication operator, not a source equation.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s elevation problem has a clear scientific lineage. It traces civilian GNSS to “GPS NAVSTAR, in the mid-1990s, particularly after the elimination of the so-called selective availability (a deliberate reduction in accuracy; May 2000)” [PAGE 1]. It then records the multi-system era: “full operational capability by three successive systems (Russian GLONASS in 2011, European Galileo 2016, and Chinese BeiDou 2020)” [PAGE 1]. That history explains why a modern wrist receiver can be configured for one, two, or four systems, but it also explains the paper’s caution: more constellations are not automatically a vertical-accuracy cure. The testing lineage runs from static and dynamic receiver-testing procedures and error measures such as RMS, CEP, SEP, and DRMS [PAGE 2] to high-accuracy national digital terrain models. The Polish DTM is a LIDAR-derived, 1-m-grid, PL-EVRF2007-NH reference with mean elevation error up to 0.2 m [PAGE 3]. The direct predecessor for elevation-gain consistency is the cycling study cited by the authors, where “results were relatively consistent among a group of devices of the same manufacturer, with differences only occurring between individual manufacturers” [PAGE 6]. This study partially echoes that manufacturer-clustering idea: “receivers of particular manufacturers had a characteristic repetitive profile” [PAGE 6]. But it does not fully reproduce the earlier claim, because the present receivers differed in model, firmware, settings, and recording frequency. The most probable mechanism offered is satellite selection: “the observed profiles are related to the manner in which the receivers of individual manufacturers ‘choose’ satellites for the determination of their geographical position, based on both hardware and firmware” [PAGE 6]. 

Multi-week traps follow directly from that lineage. First, if an athlete compares elevation gain across weeks, barometric auto-calibration at each start can change the baseline; the source says the barometer “either operates automatically (when starting the activity) or is initiated by the user” [PAGE 3]. Second, firmware and charge level can change receiver behavior, and the authors explicitly recommend researcher preparation of “navigation settings, up-to-date firmware, charge level” [PAGE 7]. Third, weather-pressure change can mimic vertical motion on a barometric altimeter, so a multi-week TEG series is not a clean device-accuracy series unless pressure and temperature are logged. Fourth, one unit per model cannot establish model-level accuracy; the paper states that “if the study aimed to determine the accuracy of a particular model, more than one unit would have to be gathered” [PAGE 7]. Fifth, horizontal route error matters over weeks because a few metres of lateral offset on a steep slope changes the DTM elevation sampled at each point; the authors recommend “acquiring an accurate (horizontally) route of the run” [PAGE 7]. These are not minor caveats; they are the retrocausal conditions under which the paper’s Table 1 result should be believed.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Crisis habit regression.** Under race conditions, the athlete’s mental model collapses to the default: start the watch, trust the display, and do not reconfigure. The paper confirms this: “nineteen people (95%) used default settings” [PAGE 4], and “most users used default settings, e.g. for the number of GNSS used” [PAGE 7]. The deeper belief error is lexical: “the term ‘GPS’ has become a synonym of a GNSSs in general, so that the typical user is unaware that there are other, equivalent GNSSs” [PAGE 7]. Thus the athlete is not making a deliberate single-system choice; the athlete may believe “GPS” means all satellite navigation. The coach’s mental model is also vulnerable: if the coach compares raw TEG from different watches, the coach treats a device-dependent estimate as an athlete-dependent load. The manufacturer’s model is different again: firmware and hardware determine satellite selection, producing “characteristic repetitive profile” behavior within a manufacturer group [PAGE 6]. The tester’s model must be recursive: the tester knows that the athlete did not control settings, the coach may not know the device model, and the manufacturer does not publish the geoid model or satellite-selection logic in a way the athlete can audit.

**Counter-trap.** The counter-trap is to pre-commit to a measurement protocol before the crisis: same model, same firmware, same sampling rate, barometer on/off as a recorded factor, start-point calibration documented, track exported under supervision, and horizontal route checked. The paper’s limitations point exactly there: receivers “should be prepared … and supervised … by the researcher, not the user” [PAGE 7]. A second counter-trap is statistical: do not infer individual device accuracy from a single unit; gather more than one unit per model [PAGE 7]. A third counter-trap is causal: do not treat the barometric altimeter as a magic sensor. The paper shows that no-altimeter units can still obtain 6.4 and 7.6 m RMS, and that TEG underestimation persists in 90% of receivers [PAGE 5]. The correct counter-trap is to separate instantaneous vertical RMS from accumulated elevation-gain bias.

**Clock.** The clock dimension is both sampling and event timing. Most receivers recorded at 1/s, giving about 2.2 thousand coordinate determinations per receiver [PAGE 4]. That is enough to compute a route-level statistic, but not enough to recover a lost start calibration, a firmware change, or a pressure front. The event clock is a single uphill run of about 8.5 km with about 1072 m of estimated elevation [PAGE 3]. Because the track was trimmed to the run duration [PAGE 4], the statistic is event-specific. A multi-week comparison across different clocks, different start calibrations, and different weather states would be a different experiment. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is therefore: the athlete’s default habit, the coach’s cross-device comparison habit, and the manufacturer’s firmware habit all interact; only a protocol that controls the clock, the setting, and the reference can adjudicate elevation accuracy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical-layer veto.** A claim that receiver model alone explains vertical error is vetoed by the geoid/datum chain. The source’s \(H\approx h-N\) relation means that an orthometric height is only as good as the geoid undulation model and the vertical datum. The paper says a receiver must have or download “an accurate geoid model for a predetermined longitude and latitude” to provide height relative to MSL [PAGE 3]. If the receiver reports ellipsoidal height while the athlete or software treats it as orthometric height, the error is not a GNSS satellite-count error. The DTM reference is strong, with mean elevation error up to 0.2 m [PAGE 3], but it is still a terrain model, not a surveyed ground truth at the wrist. Therefore any claim that the Table 1 RMS ranking is purely a receiver-hardware ranking is physically underdetermined.

**Biomechanical-layer veto.** The wrist is a moving platform. The authors corrected a mean 1.0 m offset between receiver location and ground [PAGE 4], but that correction does not remove stride-induced vertical oscillation. The paper explicitly leaves this open: “it should be investigated whether the (vertical) fluctuations of the wrist-worn receiver affect the altitude determination” [PAGE 7]. A biomechanical veto applies: if the wrist oscillates at stride frequency, the receiver’s vertical coordinate can contain a periodic component that the DTM cannot reproduce. A barometric altimeter may damp or filter this, but the paper does not prove that it does. Thus any claim that a 3.6 m RMS is a pure GNSS vertical-accuracy figure is vetoed unless wrist-mounting and filtering are controlled.

**Weather-layer veto.** A barometric altimeter is a pressure sensor, not a geoid sensor. The hypsometric relation shows that altitude inferred from pressure depends on temperature and pressure reference. The source says barometric support may operate automatically at activity start or be user-initiated [PAGE 3], but it does not report pressure or temperature logging. The paper’s ridge result is a concrete veto against a simple “more sky, better altitude” story: “Higher values of the mean elevation error were a characteristic of the second part of the run (checkpoint to finish), which is interesting because this section of the route is covered over the mountain ridge … and the availability of GNSS satellites should be (and is) better than that in the first part” [PAGE 5]. If satellite visibility were the dominant control, the ridge should be better; instead, mean error was higher. That does not prove weather caused it, but it vetoes the assumption that satellite geometry alone adjudicates elevation. Weather-pressure change is a live alternative explanation for barometric devices.

**Cross-layer adjudication rule.** The defensible statement is conditional: in this single uphill event, barometric-altimeter-supported units included the lowest RMS values (3.6–4.1 m), while several no-altimeter units were far worse (14.0–22.1 m), but no-altimeter units at 6.4 and 7.6 m and the 90% TEG underestimation prevent a universal rule [PAGE 1; PAGE 5]. Any stronger claim must survive physical datum, biomechanical wrist, and weather-pressure vetoes simultaneously.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**RLVR-1: Barometric-ablation veto.** Counterfactual: take the same receiver model, same firmware, same 1/s sampling, same route and start calibration, and alternate barometric altimeter ON versus OFF across matched trials. Primary verifier: vertical RMS against the 0.2 m-class DTM; secondary verifier: TEG percent error. Prediction from the source: ON trials should tend to lower RMS relative to the no-altimeter extremes, because the best three same-model units had RMS 3.6–4.1 m and the worst no-altimeter units had 14.0–22.1 m [PAGE 5]. Veto condition: if paired ON/OFF trials show no RMS improvement, then the source’s association cannot be upgraded to a causal “barometer fixes elevation” claim. Residual trap: no-altimeter units at 6.4 and 7.6 m already show that “acceptable results can also be achieved without using this sensor” [PAGE 5].

**RLVR-2: Constellation-count veto.** Counterfactual: same model and firmware, GPS-only versus GPS+GLONASS (or GPS+Galileo), on the same route and time window, with barometer state held constant. Verifier: difference in vertical RMS and difference in TEG percent error. Prediction from the source: do not expect a reliable vertical advantage from dual-system reception in this observational dataset, because “no advantage was noted for dual-system GNSS receivers as compared to the single-system ones” [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: any assertion that adding constellations necessarily fixes mountain-running elevation is vetoed by this dataset unless a controlled paired test shows a pre-specified RMS improvement. Important asymmetry: the source’s result vetoes using its data as proof of benefit; it does not prove that no benefit exists under controlled conditions.

**RLVR-3: Wrist-motion and horizontal-registration veto.** Counterfactual: same device and route, wrist-worn versus pole/backpack-mounted or with a documented low-pass filter; separately, recompute DTM elevations after shifting the receiver coordinates by 0, 5, and 10 m horizontally. Verifier: high-frequency vertical variance, RMS, and TEG error. Prediction: wrist mounting and horizontal misregistration should change the vertical statistic, because the authors recommend investigating “the (vertical) fluctuations of the wrist-worn receiver” and “acquiring an accurate (horizontally) route of the run” [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if wrist-mounted and rigidly mounted units are statistically indistinguishable and coordinate shifts do not move RMS or TEG, then the biomechanical/horizontal-registration explanation is not supported for that route. If they do move the statistics, then any claim that Table 1 isolates receiver quality alone is vetoed as under-controlled.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
