We formalize the Halmos isomorphism as a mapping between the space of probability measures and the space of valid temporal/spatial trajectories.

Let $(X, S, \mu)$ be the measure space of game-time, where $X = [0, T]$ (a quarter of basketball), $S$ is the Borel $\sigma$-algebra on $X$, and $\mu$ is Lebesgue measure representing uniform elapsed time.

**Operator 1: The Cumulative Impact Mapping (Theorem A)**
Given a finite measure $\nu$ on $S$ (representing, e.g., cumulative expected efficiency or spatial heat map density), we define the operator $\Phi$:
$$ \Phi(\nu)(t) = f(t) = \nu((-\infty, t]) $$
Properties enforced by $\Phi$ (as per Source Page 1):
1. Monotonicity: $t_1 \leq t_2 \implies f(t_1) \leq f(t_2)$ (efficiency/impact is non-decreasing).
2. Boundedness: $\exists M \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $|f(t)| \leq M$.
3. Left-Continuity: For any increasing sequence $x_n \uparrow x$, $\lim_{n \to \infty} f(x_n) = f(x)$.
4. Boundary Condition: $f(-\infty) = \lim_{x \to -\infty} \nu((-\infty, x]) = 0$.

**Operator 2: The Interval Differential / Measure Reconstruction (Theorem B)**
Conversely, given a valid function $f$ satisfying the properties above, we reconstruct the localized impact measure $\nu$ over any semi-closed interval $[a, b) \subset X$:
$$ \nu([a, b)) = \Delta_f[a, b) = f(b) - f(a) $$
This difference operator $\Delta_f$ acts as the foundational building block. Under the extension theorem referenced in the text ("results of § 8 are valid... hence the extension theorem"), this set function on intervals uniquely extends to a measure on the entire Borel $\sigma$-algebra $S$.

**Singularity and Breakdown Regimes:**
The mapping between $f$ and $\nu$ breaks down—or rather, undergoes a phase transition—if $f$ exhibits a jump discontinuity. By the fundamental theorem of Lebesgue integration regarding measures, if $f$ is differentiable almost everywhere, we can decompose $\nu$ into an absolutely continuous part and a singular part. 
A jump in $f$ at time $t_0$, denoted $\Delta f(t_0) = f(t_0) - f(t_0^-) > 0$, corresponds directly to a Dirac delta mass in the measure: $\nu(\{t_0\}) > 0$. In basketball terms, this singularity represents a discrete, high-magnitude event (like a made three-pointer) that instantaneously alters the state of the game, violating the smooth continuity assumptions often used in basic pace models. The "breakdown" occurs when analysts attempt to approximate $\nu$ solely via a probability density function (PDF) $p(t) = df/dt$, completely missing the Dirac components of massive plays.
