2) IoU cost (ByteTrack low-score stage). For axis-aligned boxes $A,B\subset\mathbb R^2$:
$$
\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\in[0,1]\ [\text{—}],\qquad \text{cost}=1-\mathrm{IoU}.
$$
Singularity: if center displacement exceeds roughly the box half-size, $A\cap B=\varnothing\Rightarrow \mathrm{IoU}=0$: association information vanishes. The paper states ByteTrack "uses IOU scores as their similarities" for low-score boxes [PAGE 6].

3) Hungarian assignment. Given cost $C\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$, solve $\min_{X}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}X_{ij}$ s.t. $\sum_j X_{ij}\le1,\ \sum_i X_{ij}\le1,\ X_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$; optimal bipartite matching in $O(n^3)$. $C$ is built from $1-\mathrm{IoU}$, appearance distance (DeepSORT), or Mahalanobis gating [PAGE 2, PAGE 6].

4) MOTA (CLEAR). With per-frame false negatives $FN_t$, false positives $FP_t$, identity switches $IDSW_t$, ground-truth count $GT_t$:
$$
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t (FN_t+FP_t+IDSW_t)}{\sum_t GT_t}\in(-\infty,1].
$$
Weights detection errors heavily and association ($IDSW$) lightly [PAGE 6].

5) HOTA. Per localization threshold $\alpha$:
$$
\mathrm{DetA}_\alpha=\frac{|\mathrm{TPA}|}{|\mathrm{TPA}|+|\mathrm{FNA}|+|\mathrm{FPA}|},\qquad
\mathrm{HOTA}_\alpha=\sqrt{\mathrm{DetA}_\alpha\cdot \mathrm{AssA}_\alpha},
$$
aggregated over $\alpha\in[0.05,0.95]$ [PAGE 6]. Verification against Table 3 [PAGE 7]: ByteTrack w/o GT $\sqrt{44.489\cdot50.257}=47.29$ vs reported $47.225$; FairMOT-ft $\sqrt{66.565\cdot50.492}=57.97$ vs $57.882$. The geometric-mean operator is confirmed; small residuals are the per-$\alpha$ aggregation.

6) Dataset arithmetic (units: frames, s). $200$ clips $\times\,30\,\text{s}\times25\,\text{fps}=150{,}000$ frames; half-time $75{,}375$ frames $=3{,}015\,\text{s}=50.25\,\text{min}$ wall-clock (the nominal "45-minute half" includes stoppage); total $225{,}375$ [PAGE 4, Table 1]. Box density $3{,}645{,}661/225{,}375=16.17$ objects/frame; per tracklet $3{,}645{,}661/5{,}009=727.8$ boxes $\approx0.97\times750$, i.e., the mean object is present in $\sim97\%$ of a full clip — the direct consequence of the long-term re-ID rule that "we purposely choose to keep the same ID for an object that leaves the camera frame and comes back" [PAGE 5]. Interpolation: only $11.5\%$ of boxes are interpolated, $88.5\%$ hand-keyed [PAGE 4]; linear interpolation of position and size is the assumption, and its error scales with inter-keyframe non-linearity, which is why keyframes are dense. Breakdown regimes are exactly three, and the paper names them: fast motion (Kalman gating/DetA collapse), occlusion/clustering (IoU→0 and appearance ambiguity → ID switches → AssA collapse), and domain gap (pedestrian-pretrained detectors fire on the crowd and never on the ball [PAGE 7]).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Read honestly as *scientific/historical lineage* (there is no retro-causal mechanism in a CV benchmark). Method lineage [PAGE 2, PAGE 6]: SORT [8] (Kalman + Hungarian) → DeepSORT [17] (adds CNN appearance to the cost) → Tracktor [5] (regression-head realignment) → FairMOT [81] (joint detect + re-ID) → ByteTrack [80] (associates *every* box, IoU for low-score). Metric lineage: CLEAR/MOTA [6] → HOTA [48] as a corrective to MOTA's detection bias. Dataset lineage [PAGE 3–4]: PETS2009/TUD → MOT16/MOT20/KITTI/DETRAC (pedestrian/vehicle) → domain-specific sets → SoccerNet-Tracking, whose only soccer predecessor SSET [25] was *single*-object tracking at $\sim10\,\text{s}$ (248 frames); this work scales to MOT, 30-s clips, plus the first public long-term sports tracking half [PAGE 3]. The genuine "multi-week trap" analog is methodological, not temporal: a benchmark that scores on MOTA will reward detectors and hide ID-switch failures, so a team optimizing MOTA can post $94.844$ (DeepSORT w/ GT) while HOTA is only $\sim70$ — the metric genealogy contains a built-in attract-and-mislead dynamic the paper explicitly warns against [PAGE 7].

Not applicable as framed. There is no opponent modeling, no habit regression, no clock in this source; injecting one would be fabrication. The nearest *real* structure is co-adaptation between detector and associator: ByteTrack's low-score IoU stage and FairMOT's shared backbone create a two-timescale interaction where improving one module shifts the operating point of the other (the paper's motivation for reporting DetA and AssA separately, [PAGE 7]). That is engineering co-design, not theory of mind.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical constraints do act as hard vetoes on feasibility, and they are quantifiable. Occlusion veto: when players cluster (corners, walls, celebrations), $\mathrm{IoU}\to0$ and appearance features of same-team players are near-identical [PAGE 2, PAGE 5], so the assignment problem is information-starved — corners show AssA $=39.7$ w/o GT vs substitutions AssA $=66.3$ [PAGE 7]. Motion-blur/ball-size veto: $<100$ px ball moving $1.2$ m/frame [PAGE 2] makes per-frame detection and IoU association both fail; goals and penalties drop to DetA $29.6$ and $20.1$ w/o GT [PAGE 7]. Weather/biomechanical layers do not appear in the source and I will not invent them.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The intellectually honest "counterfactuals" are the paper's own controlled ablations, which are verifiable, not predicted:
1. Oracle-detection counterfactual (w/ GT vs w/o GT, same tracker). Isolates detection error from association error. Result: with perfect detections, ByteTrack AssA $=60.718$ but DetA is only $84.342$, not 100 — detections are still lost to gating under fast motion [PAGE 7]. Veto: any claim that "GT detections ⇒ perfect tracking" is falsified.
2. Domain fine-tune counterfactual (FairMOT vs FairMOT-ft, both w/o GT). Isolates the domain gap. HOTA $43.911\to57.882$, MOTA $50.698\to83.565$, because the pre-trained detector fired on the crowd and never on the ball [PAGE 6–7]. Veto: generic pedestrian detectors are inadmissible for on-field MOT without re-training.
3. Metric-counterfactual (MOTA vs HOTA ranking). Same numbers, two rankings: MOTA ranks FairMOT ($50.698$) above ByteTrack ($31.741$) w/o GT, while HOTA ranks ByteTrack ($47.225$) above FairMOT ($43.911$) [PAGE 7]. Veto: selecting a method by MOTA alone can invert the association-quality ranking — which is why the paper adopts HOTA as primary.
