The family-talent examples—the Bachs, Bernoullis, Mozart—sit in the Galtonian tradition of Hereditary Genius (1869), where eminent lineages appear to concentrate talent, but James notes the line "quickly reaches a maximum" under intermarriage [PAGE 687], anticipating regression to the mean rather than indefinite Lamarckian accumulation.

Multi-week traps: (1) Anchoring on James/Weismann as if it were modern genetics. Weismann is pre-Mendelian; "germ-plasm" is not DNA, and modern evidence shows limited transgenerational epigenetic and small-RNA signaling that can transiently cross the somatic barrier under stress, usually resetting within a few generations. Treating the 1890 barrier as absolute misses this breakdown regime. (2) Conflating morphological mutilation with neural habit. The page explicitly separates "peculiarities of neural structure and habit" [PAGE 687] from "inherited mutilations in animals (dogs' ears and tails)" [PAGE 687]; a week-scale analysis may lump them together, but the tissue barrier, developmental timing, and channel capacity differ. (3) Mistaking Spencer's a priori indefinite-growth argument for an empirical forecast. James reports the opposite: talent reaches a maximum and disappears under miscellaneous intermarriage [PAGE 687], so any reasoning chain that projects unbounded familial gain from practice alone is falsified by the source. (4) Treating the blood-infection account of guinea-pig epilepsy [PAGE 687] as a general mechanism for acquired-trait inheritance; it is pathological and agent-specific, not a ratchet for adaptive neural habits.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Recursive model of an agent in crisis: an individual inherits no acquired parental neural habit directly [PAGE 687], but builds a crisis-response habit h_t within the lifetime. Let crisis indicator c_t ∈ {0,1}; habit strength obeys dh/dt = -λ h + β c_t, with decay rate λ (time^-1) and habit timescale τ_h = 1/λ, typically weeks. An observer recursing into the agent's mind sees family members display similar crisis habits and may infer transgenerational inheritance; the correct decomposition separates within-lifetime acquisition h_t from social/cultural transmission and from germline transmission τ = 0 [PAGE 687].

Crisis habit regression: under stress the agent regresses to the dominant habitual mode h_crisis. Counter-trap: deliberate inhibition I_t opposes the habit, net drive h_net = h - γ I, with inhibition efficacy γ. The counter-trap fails when γ < λ (inhibition weaker than habit decay is slow) or when crisis recurrence 1/τ_c exceeds λ, so the habit never fully decays and is continuously reloaded. Clock comparison: τ_h (habit decay, weeks) versus τ_g (generation, years) versus τ_epi (epigenetic reset, O(1–3) generations). Because τ_h << τ_g, a crisis habit acquired by a parent cannot accumulate across generations through germline inheritance; apparent familial crisis patterns that persist beyond τ_epi require cultural transmission or shared environment, not the Weismann-barrier-violating mechanism Lamarckism would require [PAGE 687]. Breakdown: if an observer mistakes the recurrence of h_t across a family for germline inheritance, they commit a category error between developmental timescale and generational timescale; the veto condition is verification of a germline allele or stable epimarker change accompanying the habit.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather/environmental veto layers.

Physical layer (Weismann barrier): the germline is sequestered from somatic acquired states; τ_{somatic→germ} = 0 for normal use/disuse [PAGE 687]. Veto: any claimed transgenerational adaptation without demonstrated germline DNA or reprogramming-resistant epimarker change is rejected.

Biomechanical layer: surgical amputation (parental tail cutting) removes tissue but does not rewrite developmental DNA or Hox patterning; offspring tail length at birth is unchanged across generations [PAGE 687]. Veto: inherited mutilation claims fail because a biomechanical intervention on the parent soma has no directed mechanism to alter gamete blueprint. Breakdown only under mutagenic trauma (radiation, chemicals), which produces random germline damage, not adaptive inheritance.

Weather/environmental layer: the text allows that degeneration may come "by good or bad nutrition" and blood poisoning [PAGE 687]. Maternal nutrition and ambient conditions modulate phenotypic provisioning and can produce maternal effects, but these are non-genetic, reversible, and decay after the provisioning window; they do not constitute a Lamarckian ratchet. Extreme teratogenic weather-linked exposures (e.g., temperature-dependent teratogens) may damage the germline randomly, but again without directed adaptive rewrite.

Adjudication rule: accept a transgenerational inheritance claim only if it passes all three layers—(i) physical-layer germline/epimarker change verified, (ii) biomechanical layer ruled out as mere somatic alteration, (iii) weather layer ruled out as maternal provisioning or shared environment—and the effect persists beyond the epigenetic reprogramming window (O(1–3) generations). Singularity: stable epimarker escape from reprogramming could create a persistent transgenerational signal, but this is rare and pathological, not the indefinite Spencerian talent growth James rejects [PAGE 687].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Veto scenario 1 (Weismann tail replication with sequencing): Breed lineages with parental tails cut for many generations, with matched uncut controls, and sequence germline DNA plus assay epimarks at tail-development loci (e.g., Hox). Veto condition: if E[L_offspring | cut] = E[L_offspring | uncut] = L₀ mm (ΔL = 0 mm) and no enrichment of germline mutations or stable epimarks in the cut lineage, veto inherited mutilation. Prediction: ΔL = 0 mm; per-generation mutation rate μ equal to baseline; H3K27me3/DNA methylation at developmental loci reprogrammed by generation 2. Verifiable from breeding records and molecular assays.

Veto scenario 2 (blood-exchange test of guinea-pig epilepsy explanation): Use parabiosis or blood transfusion to expose unoperated surrogates to blood from operated parents, and operate parents whose blood is never shared with offspring. Veto condition: if offspring epilepsy appears only when a transmissible pathogen is present and not merely from surgical trauma or blood sharing, veto Weismann's blood-infection account as a general mechanism of inherited trauma [PAGE 687]. Prediction: seizure incidence in offspring matches population baseline unless pathogen detected; narrow-sense heritability h² of seizure threshold unchanged by parental surgery status. Units: probability p dimensionless; h² dimensionless.

Veto scenario 3 (multigenerational talent/practice regression): Track a pedigree under sustained deliberate practice for the relevant faculty. Veto condition: if trait mean does not grow indefinitely but plateaus at T_max, and after practice stops the offspring mean regresses as E[T_t - μ] = r^t (T_0 - μ) with r < 1, veto Spencer's a priori indefinite-growth argument [PAGE 687]. Prediction: per-generation response R = h² i σ_P until additive variance V_A is depleted; singularity h² = 1 is never observed; family lineages converge to the population ceiling rather than accumulating without bound. Verifiable via longitudinal pedigree and psychometric data. Breakdown: if r ≈ 1 and V_A remains nondepleting indefinitely, the veto fails—but this regime is not supported by the source, which reports talent "quickly reaches a maximum" [PAGE 687].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
