where \hat{h} is perceived distal size (m), θ is retinal angular size (rad), and \mathbf{c} is the vector of depth/distance cues (binocular disparity δ in rad, vergence angle V in rad, accommodation A in diopters, motion parallax p in rad/s, occlusion, perspective, atmospheric contrast). P(h \mid θ, \mathbf{c}) ∝ P(θ \mid h, D) \, P(D \mid \mathbf{c}) \, P(h)   (3)

with \hat{h} = E[h \mid θ, \mathbf{c}]. Units: P(θ \mid h, D) is a likelihood over radians given m and m; P(D \mid \mathbf{c}) is a distance posterior in m; P(h) is a size prior in m.

When \mathbf{c} provides no information about D (e.g., isolated disk against a featureless homogeneous field, static monocular viewing, no motion parallax, no vergence/accommodation), P(D \mid \mathbf{c}) → uniform over a broad range. Then h and D trade off perfectly along h = D·θ [from (1)], and \hat{h} is unidentifiable from θ alone. James’s account sits in a genealogy that runs backward and forward in time, making the 1890 text both a synthesis and a pivot. Retrospectively, George Berkeley’s New Theory of Vision (1709) argued that distance is not itself seen but is suggested by visual signs; Thomas Reid (1764) challenged Berkeley by claiming perception of depth is immediate and native. James (1890) [PAGE 1] occupies the middle ground: retinal magnitude has no primitive absolute import, yet the observer is not condemned to permanent relativity because a “real” magnitude is selected by practical and “œsthetic” interests [PAGE 1]. This anticipates Hermann von Helmholtz’s “unconscious inference” (Handbuch der physiologischen Optik, 1866), in which the visual system infers distal properties from proximal stimuli using learned assumptions. John Ruskin’s “innocence of the eye” (Modern Painters, 1856–1860), which James quotes [PAGE 1], supplies the phenomenological target: a pre-linguistic, pre-conceptual mode of seeing color stains as such. James’s innovation is to treat that innocence as lost and replaced by an interest-relative canonical magnitude.

Prospectively, James’s argument seeds two opposing twentieth-century traditions. James J. Gibson’s ecological optics (The Perception of the Visual World, 1950; The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception, 1979) rejects the inference model: structure in the ambient optic array (optic flow, texture gradient) specifies distance directly, making Helmholtzian inference and Jamesian “lost innocence” unnecessary. Richard Gregory’s top-down, perceptual-hypothesis tradition (Nature, 1966; Eye and Brain, 1966) accepts the inference frame but treats constancy as active hypothesis testing, ill-posed without priors—formally close to equation (3) above. James’s claim that we “cannot say beforehand how much of a distant house or tree our finger will cover” [PAGE 1] is thus a proto-ill-posedness statement, later formalized in computational vision as the inverse-projection problem.

Multi-week traps: In longitudinal adaptation experiments, observers exposed for weeks to a systematically distorted visual environment (e.g., a room whose dimensions slowly contract by 5% per week, or prism lenses that shift perceived size) recalibrate their canonical “real” magnitude h_0 and propitious distance D_0. When the distortion is removed, a negative aftereffect occurs: ordinary objects transiently appear too large or too small because the recalibrated h_0 no longer matches the environment. This is a trap for any experimental design that assumes the “BEsL” magnitude [PAGE 1] is stable across sessions. A second multi-week trap concerns habit regression: under sustained task load, observers may stop computing full constancy and revert to reporting retinal angle; after the load ends, they fail to re-engage constancy immediately, producing a hysteresis in size judgments that can persist for days. James’s text underestimates this hysteresis by treating the “real” magnitude as a durable selection rather than a dynamically maintained set-point.

Recursive theory of mind models what Agent A believes about Agent B’s model of A’s perceptual strategy. In a crisis (high arousal, time pressure, or sensory degradation), Agent A undergoes habit regression: the computationally expensive constancy operator \mathcal{C}(θ, \mathbf{c}) [equation (2)] is down-weighted, and A reports raw retinal angle θ as if it were distal size. Example: an air-traffic controller under workload sees a converging aircraft; its retinal image expands rapidly, but without computing distance D from closure rate, A regresses to judging the aircraft as “close” purely because θ is growing, even if D is large and constant. The regression is not random; it is a collapse to the proximal layer.

The counter-trap: If A knows B is monitoring A’s size reports to infer A’s distance estimates, A can strategically misreport θ to manipulate B’s inference. B, anticipating this, discounts A’s reports and relies on independent telemetry; A then regresses further because the social channel is corrupted. The counter-trap is stable only if B has a veto on A’s report (independent rangefinder). Without that veto, the system enters a miscalibration spiral where neither agent’s size-distance map is trustworthy.

Clock: The time course of magnitude perception has three layers. (i) Millisecond layer: retinal transduction yields θ with latency ~40–70 ms; at this layer James’s “retinal space-feeling” dominates and no absolute import is assigned [PAGE 1]. (ii) Seconds layer: depth cues (disparity, vergence, motion parallax) are integrated and \hat{h} is computed via (3); size constancy stabilizes. (iii) Minutes-to-days layer: the “real” magnitude h_0 and propitious distance D_0 are set by task interests [PAGE 1]; this set-point drifts with fatigue, tool use (e.g., holding a pointer changes peripersonal space), and adaptation.

Recursive nesting: A models B’s clock. If A believes B operates only at the millisecond layer (raw θ), A may exaggerate angular size to make B think an object is nearer. If A believes B operates at the minutes layer (canonical h_0), A may instead manipulate context (lighting, viewing distance) to shift B’s D_0. Crisis habit regression collapses the nesting to layer (i), which is exploitable by an opponent who retains layer (ii) or (iii) computation.

The coverage ratio r ≈ D / d_f [equation (4)] therefore acquires variance Var(r) ∝ σ_d^2(t_g) / d_f^4. Under fatigue, r drifts systematically even when D and h are constant, so superposition fails as a pure retinal comparison. This veto proves James’s “experiment of superposition” is motor-contaminated, not a direct readout of visual magnitude.

Weather veto (atmospheric): At long distances, atmospheric turbulence creates scintillation and contrast loss. The retinal image of a fixed house at fixed distance D fluctuates in angular extent and position over time, violating James’s assumption that “the relation of the object to the eye is believed to be on the whole unchanged” yields a stable sensation [PAGE 1]. The effective visual angle becomes θ(t) = θ_0 + Δθ_turb(t), where Δθ_turb has variance proportional to the scintillation index and path length. Under strong turbulence, the observer ascribes less absolute import to the moment-to-moment retinal feeling, consistent with James, but for a physical reason (image motion) rather than a purely phenomenological one.

Adjudication proof: When any veto fires (θ < θ_min; proprioceptive σ_d high; scintillation active), the percept does not collapse to zero import as James’s strongest wording might imply [PAGE 1]. Instead, the perceptual operator degrades to a weighted cue combination

\hat{h} = \frac{\sum_i w_i \, \hat{h}_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \quad w_i = 1 / \text{Var}(\hat{h}_i)

where failed cues receive w_i → 0. The surviving cues (e.g., prior size P(h), known object identity, binocular disparity when available) maintain a non-zero, albeit uncertain, estimate. Thus James’s categorical statement that we “end by ascribing no absolute import whatever” [PAGE 1] is an over-claim; the correct regime is high-variance, veto-gated estimation, not total metric nihilism.
