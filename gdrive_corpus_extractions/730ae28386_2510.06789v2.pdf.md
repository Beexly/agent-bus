<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a statistics paper on rank aggregation, not a physical-performance forecasting paper. Its core multi-hop derivation is: (i) observe rater-specific scores \(y_{ik}\) on subsets \(C_k\); (ii) convert each within-rater pair into a signed comparison \(z_{ijk}=\mathbf 1\{y_{ik}\prec y_{jk}\}-\mathbf 1\{y_{jk}\prec y_{ik}\}\), where \(\prec\) denotes “strictly less than” in the observed score order; (iii) maximize the MASTER objective \(L(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^p\sum_{i<j\in C_k} z_{ijk}\mathbf 1\{\pi_i<\pi_j\}\) over permutations [PAGE 5]. The key assumption is Extended Weak Stochastic Transitivity: “There exists a global ranking \(\pi^*=(\pi^*_1,\ldots,\pi^*_n)\in S_n\) such that if \(\pi^*_i<\pi^*_j\), then \(\Pr(y_{ik}\prec y_{jk})>\Pr(y_{jk}\prec y_{ik})\) for all \(k\in[p]\)” [PAGE 6]. Under that assumption the expected objective is maximized at \(\pi^*\), because every discordant pair contributes a negative expected sign. The technical difficulty is that the objective is a weighted U-empirical process indexed by permutations; pairs sharing an item are dependent, and when \(L_k>2\) the standard empirical-process tools for \(L_k=2\) do not suffice. The paper’s Theorem 2.1 states \(\tau_m(\hat\pi,\pi^*)=O_p(L_{\max}n\log n)\) as \(p\to\infty\) [PAGE 8]. The modified Kendall loss is then mapped to ordinary Kendall tau through the non-uniform sampling Assumption 2.2, yielding Proposition 2.1’s bound “with probability at least \(1-\exp(-2n\log n)\)” [PAGE 9]. Proposition 2.2 gives the proportion of discordant pairs vanishing, \(\tau(\hat\pi,\pi^*)/\binom n2=o_p(1)\), under the signal condition \(\bar N q_{\min}^2\gg L_{\max}\log n/n\) [PAGE 10]. The lower bound in Theorem 2.2 is \(\gtrsim n/(\delta^2\log n)\) under \(\sum_k L_k(L_k-1)\le n(n-1)/(40\delta\log(n+1))\), and \(\gtrsim \sum_k L_k^2/\delta\) under \(\sum_k L_k\le n/\log(n+1)\) [PAGE 11]. The gray-zone audit is where overclaims usually enter. First, EWST is not “no assumption”: it requires a single global ordering and strict majority direction for every rater and every compared pair. Cyclic majority or rater-specific reversals break it. Second, \(q_{\min}>0\) is required for the Proposition 2.1 bound; if \(q_{\min}\to0\) or \(\bar N=0\), the bound is singular or vacuous. Third, the upper bound grows with \(L_{\max}\), so “multi-way” is not automatically better; within-rater dependence can dominate. Fourth, \(c_{\min}>0\) in Assumption 2.2 excludes sampling designs where some subsets are effectively impossible. Fifth, the objective is NP-hard, and the paper explicitly hedges: “we do not have to find the global maximum; instead, an approximate solution \(\tilde\pi\) satisfying \(L(\tilde\pi)\ge L(\pi^*)\) with high probability is sufficient” [PAGE 10]. Thus the theorem is conditional on optimizer quality, not on the existence of a polynomial-time global solver. Sixth, real-data top-ten lists are illustrative, not proof of asymptotic consistency; the tennis and sushi analyses show interpretable departures from parametric rankings, but they do not verify EWST. The correct reading is therefore: MASTER is a model-free maximum-score estimator with consistency and near-minimax guarantees under EWST plus sampling regularity, with explicit breakdown at vanishing signal, disconnected overlap, severe rater heterogeneity beyond EWST, and optimizer failure.

\[
z_{ijk}=\mathbf 1\{y_{ik}\prec y_{jk}\}-\mathbf 1\{y_{jk}\prec y_{ik}\},
\]
where \(\prec\) denotes strict less-than in the observed score order. Then the MASTER estimator is
\[
\hat\pi\in\arg\max_{\pi\in S_n}L(\pi),\qquad
L(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^p\sum_{i<j\in C_k}z_{ijk}\mathbf 1\{\pi_i<\pi_j\}.
\]
This is equation (1) on [PAGE 5]. Units: \(z_{ijk}\in\{-1,0,1\}\) is dimensionless; \(L(\pi)\) is a dimensionless count-like sum over rater-pair terms.

The central identification assumption is EWST:
\[
\pi^*_i<\pi^*_j\ \Longrightarrow\ 
\Pr(y_{ik}\prec y_{jk})>\Pr(y_{jk}\prec y_{ik})\quad\text{for all }k\in[p].
\]
The paper states this as “There exists a global ranking \(\pi^*=(\pi^*_1,\ldots,\pi^*_n)\in S_n\) such that if \(\pi^*_i<\pi^*_j\), then \(\Pr(y_{ik}&lt;y_{jk})>\Pr(y_{jk}&lt;y_{ik})\) for all \(k\in[p]\)” [PAGE 6]. The pair-specific signal gap is
\[
q_{ijk}=\left|\Pr(y_{ik}\prec y_{jk})-\Pr(y_{jk}\prec y_{ik})\right|\in[0,1],
\]
dimensionless probability units. The modified Kendall loss is
\[
\tau_m(\hat\pi,\pi^*)=
\frac{\left(\sum_{k=1}^p\sum_{(i,j)\in B(\hat\pi,\pi^*)\cap P_k}q_{ijk}\right)^2}
{\sum_{k=1}^p|B(\hat\pi,\pi^*)\cap P_k|},
\]
where \(B(\pi,\pi')\) is the discordant-pair set and \(P_k=\{(i,j):i<j\in C_k\}\) [PAGE 8]. Units: \(q_{ijk}\) is dimensionless, and \(\tau_m\) is a dimensionless count-like intrinsic loss; if all \(q_{ijk}=q_0\), then \(\tau_m=q_0^2\sum_k|B\cap P_k|\), so it scales with the number of observed discordant pairs.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is explicit in the paper’s references and framing. Thurstone’s paired-comparison tradition [PAGE 37] and the Bradley–Terry model [PAGE 35] supply the parametric pairwise baseline; Luce’s choice axiom [PAGE 37] and Plackett’s permutation model [PAGE 37] supply the multi-way parametric baseline. The maximum-score line runs from Manski’s maximum score estimator [PAGE 37] through Horowitz’s smoothed maximum score estimator [PAGE 37]. The paper itself says the MASTER objective “is closely related to the maximum score estimator for robust binary regression” [PAGE 6], but with two differences: the summation is over item pairs rather than observations, and the target is a global ranking rather than a regression coefficient. The noisy-sorting line is Mao, Weed, and Rigollet’s “Minimax rates and efficient algorithms for noisy sorting” [PAGE 37]; the paper explicitly says its pairwise special case reduces to an estimator used there [PAGE 6-7]. The matrix-estimation and Borda lines appear through Chatterjee’s universal singular value thresholding [PAGE 35], Shah and Wainwright’s Borda analysis [PAGE 37], and Chatterjee and Mukherjee’s monotone-constraint tournament estimation [PAGE 35]. The recent Plackett–Luce theory line appears through Han and Xu [PAGE 36] and Fan et al. [PAGE 36].

Retrocausally, MASTER reinterprets this lineage in three steps. First, it treats BT and PL as special cases of EWST rather than as the null model: the paper says EWST “covers the PL model, which further reduces to the BT model under the pairwise comparison setting” [PAGE 7]. Second, it treats noisy sorting as the \(L_k=2\), uniform-sampling special case and then generalizes to non-uniform sampling and multi-way comparisons [PAGE 6-7, PAGE 9]. Third, it reverses the usual modeling direction: instead of starting with a likelihood and proving consistency inside it, it starts with pairwise majority inequalities and proves a maximum-score estimator can recover the ordering without a parametric likelihood. That is the paper’s claim to novelty: “no nonparametric models or methods have been developed under general multi-way comparison settings” [PAGE 2].

The multi-week traps are not in the algebra but in the interpretation. Trap one: treating “model-free” as “assumption-free.” EWST still imposes a single global order and strict pairwise majority for every rater; a cyclic-majority data set is outside the theory. Trap two: treating the lower bound as if it applied to ordinary Kendall tau in every regime. The paper explicitly separates the modified metric from the ordinary metric and states that \(\tau\asymp n^2\) is possible in the sparse regime [PAGE 11]. Trap three: assuming the parametric baseline is always inferior. In the paper’s own simulations, when the data are generated by the Bradley–Terry model, BT is best; when generated by Plackett–Luce, PL-MLE is best. For example, in Table 2, under the correctly specified PL scenario at \(n=100\), PL-MLE has error \(2.37\) while MASTER has \(5.81\) [PAGE 17]. Trap four: ignoring the non-uniform-sampling condition. The tennis analysis notes that missingness is not uniform because “the likelihood of two professional tennis players meeting in a match depends on their ranking” [PAGE 18], which is exactly why the paper argues BT and MASTER are more appropriate than counting or USVT there. Trap five: overreading the real-data top-ten tables. The tennis and sushi results are interpretive checks, not proofs; the paper’s own sushi discussion uses pairwise preference proportions to explain why MASTER moves “maguro” to eighth and “kurumaebi” to tenth [PAGE 19-20]. Trap six: treating the privacy extension as established. The paper only says the approach has “natural compatibility with privacy-preserving randomization” and leaves formal development “for future investigation” [PAGE 21].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three competing policies: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. Crisis habit regression is the analyst’s default under sparse or noisy data: fall back to the familiar Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce likelihood because it is efficient and has standard software. That habit is rational when SST or PL is true. The paper’s simulations show the cost of the habit when it is false: in Table 1, scenario 2 at \(n=100\), BT has error \(8.65\) and MASTER \(1.66\); at \(n=500\), BT has \(5.39\) and MASTER \(0.32\) [PAGE 15]. The habit is not universally bad, however: in the correctly specified PL scenario in Table 2, PL-MLE has error \(2.37\) at \(n=100\) versus MASTER \(5.81\), and \(1.20\) versus \(3.29\) at \(n=500\) [PAGE 17]. The correct mental model is therefore not “nonparametric always wins”; it is “match the estimator to the strongest assumption that is credible.”

The counter-trap is the overcorrection: because raters are heterogeneous, allow every rater to have an arbitrary preference distribution and assume the data will sort themselves out. MASTER does allow “highly heterogeneous preference distributions across raters” [PAGE 1], but it still requires EWST. If raters are strategic, if their preferences cycle, or if ties dominate so that \(q_{\min}\) is near zero, the counter-trap fails. A second counter-trap is computational: because the objective is NP-hard, a local-search output can look like a model-free triumph while violating the condition \(L(\tilde\pi)\ge L(\pi^*)\) that Proposition 2.3 needs [PAGE 10]. The paper’s own hedge is the antidote: “we do not have to find the global maximum; instead, an approximate solution \(\tilde\pi\) satisfying \(L(\tilde\pi)\ge L(\pi^*)\) with high probability is sufficient” [PAGE 10]. That is a verifiable condition, not a slogan.

The clock is the asymptotic and finite-sample regime. Theorem 2.1 is a \(p\to\infty\) statement; Proposition 2.2 has two clocks, one with \(p\to\infty\) under \(\bar Nq_{\min}^2\gg L_{\max}\log n/n\), and one with \(n\to\infty\) for a single full rater [PAGE 10]. The simulations use \(p=n^2\) raters [PAGE 13]. The sushi application has \(5,000\) respondents and \(100\) sushi types, with each respondent ranking \(10\) types [PAGE 19]. The tennis application has \(875\) players and only \(7.13\%\) of player pairs having competed at least once [PAGE 18]. These are different clocks: in sushi, \(p\) is large and \(n\) is \(100\); in tennis, the “raters” are matches and the overlap graph is sparse. A recursive analyst must ask: which clock is running, and is the signal condition satisfied? If the clock says \(q_{\min}\) is tiny or \(\bar N\) is too small, the right response is to collect more overlapping comparisons, not to swap estimators. If the clock says PL is credible and \(p\) is large, the right response may be the parametric estimator. If the clock says EWST only, MASTER is the safer aggregation rule. The theory-of-mind recursion is thus: model the rater’s preference, model the analyst’s default under crisis, model the counter-trap of overflexibility, and then let the clock adjudicate.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is straightforward: the source contains no weather layer and no biomechanical layer. The data types are rater scores \(y_{ik}\), which may be “continuous, ordinal, or count” [PAGE 5]. The tennis data are “the results of all men’s matches organized by the Association of Tennis Professionals (ATP) from 2000 to 2018” [PAGE 17], and the paper explicitly says “each match is treated as a pairwise comparison event with \(L_k=2\)” [PAGE 18]. The sushi data are “preference records of 5,000 users regarding a set of 100 different types of sushi” [PAGE 19], with “each of the 5,000 respondents ranked 10 types of sushi” [PAGE 19]. Nowhere in the data-generating process or the parameter space \(\mathcal G\) are weather variables, biomechanical measurements, injury states, fatigue, or sensor covariates defined. The parameter space is distributions for \(D=\{(C_k,\pi_{{\rm obs},k})\}_{k=1}^p\) satisfying Assumptions 2.1 and 2.2 [PAGE 10]. Therefore any claim that weather or biomechanics explains the estimated ranking is outside the source model and must be vetoed as an inadmissible cross-layer adjustment. This is not a claim that weather is physically irrelevant to tennis; it is a claim that the paper’s likelihood has no weather coordinate and no biomechanical coordinate, so the paper cannot identify such an effect.

The physical/computational veto is separate and stronger. The MASTER objective is NP-hard [PAGE 3-4], and the paper’s guarantee depends on an approximate solution satisfying \(L(\tilde\pi)\ge L(\pi^*)\) with high probability [PAGE 10]. If a proposed cross-layer explanation requires a global optimum that the algorithm cannot certify, the physical computation layer vetoes the claim. The paper’s own limitation statement is explicit: “One limitation of the MASTER framework is the computational challenge posed by its discrete optimization objective” [PAGE 21]. A valid cross-layer proof must therefore show either (a) the optimizer condition holds on the realized data, or (b) the inferential claim is robust to local optima. Without that, the statistical layer’s theorem is not licensed.

The biomechanical/weather veto can be stated as a null-domain proof. Let \(X\) denote a weather or biomechanical variable. Under the source model, the likelihood is \(P(D\mid G)\) for \(G\in\mathcal G\); there is no \(X\) in \(G\). If one proposes \(P(D\mid G,X)\), then unless \(X\) is observed and the new conditional model is identified, the quantity \(P(D\mid G,X)\) is not estimable from the paper’s \(D\). The source therefore cannot support a conditional claim of the form “given weather \(X\), the ranking changes,” because the data contain no \(X\). The only admissible cross-layer statement is a scope restriction: the paper’s ranking is a preference/order aggregation from observed scores or match outcomes, and any physical or meteorological explanation is an external hypothesis requiring new data and a new identification argument. That is the veto proof: no variable, no likelihood coordinate, no identified effect.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios follow. Each is checkable against the paper’s equations and each yields a prediction that can falsify an overclaim.

Scenario 1: Cyclic majority violates EWST. Construct \(n=3\) items and many raters, each comparing all three, with \(\Pr(y_{1k}\prec y_{2k})=0.7\), \(\Pr(y_{2k}\prec y_{3k})=0.7\), and \(\Pr(y_{3k}\prec y_{1k})=0.7\). Then no global ranking satisfies the EWST inequalities for all three pairs: the required strict directions are \(1\prec2\), \(2\prec3\), and \(3\prec1\), a cycle. The paper’s Assumption 2.1 requires “There exists a global ranking \(\pi^*\)” with the stated strict probability inequalities for all \(k\in[p]\) [PAGE 6]. Prediction: MASTER’s expected objective has no \(\pi^*\) that simultaneously satisfies all pair directions; the output will be cycle-sensitive and the proportion of discordant pairs will not converge to zero as \(p\to\infty\). Veto condition: any invocation of Theorem 2.1 or Proposition 2.2 on a data set whose empirical pairwise majority graph contains a cycle. The veto is verifiable by checking the three pairwise inequalities directly.

Scenario 2: Vanishing signal or no overlap. Set \(q_{\min}=0\), or set \(\bar Nq_{\min}^2\le L_{\max}\log n/n\). The first makes the Proposition 2.1 bound singular because the denominator contains \(q_{\min}^2\); the second violates the sufficient condition in Proposition 2.2, which requires \(\bar Nq_{\min}^2\gg L_{\max}\log n/n\) [PAGE 10]. Prediction: the ordinary Kendall error \(\tau(\hat\pi,\pi^*)/\binom n2\) is not guaranteed to vanish; if \(\bar N=0\), no item pair is compared and the ranking is unidentified; if \(q_{\min}=0\), the pair is probabilistically tied and carries no directional signal. Veto condition: any claim of consistency when \(q_{\min}=0\), \(\bar N=0\), or \(\bar Nq_{\min}^2\le L_{\max}\log n/n\). This is verifiable from the observed overlap counts and estimated pairwise gaps.
