**Historical Lineage and the Genealogy of "g"**

To understand the systemic shift Tolman proposes on page 406, we must trace the **retrocausal lineage** of the constructs he is dismantling. The concept of a *general factor* (g) is not merely a psychological variable; it is the mathematical ghost of late 19th-century psychophysics and mental testing, haunting the architecture of early behaviorism.

1.  **The Spearmanian Inheritance:** Charles Spearman’s *two-factor theory* (1893-1927) posited that all cognitive tasks require a common *g* and a task-specific *s*. As Tolman notes [PAGE 406], Spearman did not clearly delineate the temporal integration of these factors, leading to the interpretation that *s* operates throughout the performance while *g* operates only in "higher, more central, or eductive steps." This reflects the **structuralist legacy** of treating cognition as a hierarchy of static faculties.

2.  **The Gestalt & Structuralist Rebuttals:** American structuralists (Titchener) attempted to reduce consciousness to elemental sensations, while Gestaltists (Köhler, Wertheimer) argued for holistic percepts. Tolman critiques both for lacking a **dynamic, purposive core**. They treated behavior as a series of stimulus-response bonds (S→R), ignoring the organism's internal state and future-directed aims.

3.  **Tolman’s Purposive Revolution:** On this very page, Tolman introduces his **"fourth type of independent variable": P**, the initiating physiological state. This is a genealogical pivot point. Unlike Watsonian behaviorism (which viewed S→R as a blind reflex chain), Tolman grafts a *teleological* vector onto the system. *P* initiates a sequence of "means-end readinesses," which generate "D" (demanded types of goal-objects).
    *   **Lineage:** This moves from Pavlovian conditioned reflexes → Hullian drive reduction → **Tolman’s cognitive map.** *P* is not just an instinct; it is a *state* that reconfigures the entire behavioral field, making specific goals (*D*) salient.
    *   **The Trap:** By 1932, the dominant paradigm was **mechanistic materialism**. Proposing that organisms act based on *future* goals (*D*) was seen as a return to mystical vitalism. However, Tolman uses the formal language of variables (*P, D, g, s*) to cloak his teleology in scientific objectivity. The genealogy shows a struggle between *deterministic physics* (S→R) and *purposeful biology* (P→D).

**Multi-Week Traps in Cognitive Development:**
This historical tension manifests in modern **"multi-week traps"**—extended learning phases where progress stalls despite reinforcement:
*   **Institutionalized Learning:** Students conditioned to seek immediate feedback (small *s*, large *g* in rote tasks) fail when encountering complex, open-ended problems requiring *P*-driven exploration (large *g* demand but no external reward).
*   **Algorithmic Optimization:** AI systems trained with dense rewards (high *s*, low *g* specialization) get trapped in local minima, unable to perform *eductive* leaps (the domain of *g*) required for generalization.
*   **Behavioral Rigidity:** In organizational settings, teams optimized for KPI compliance (high *s* specificity) become incapable of adaptive innovation when market conditions change, as they lack the *g* reservoir for novel synthesis. These are direct descendants of the S→R models Tolman critiqued—systems without a dynamic *P* to restructure goals.

Tolman’s introduction of *P* on page 406 acknowledges this. Extreme arousal states (panic, exhaustion) can veto higher cognitive functions. Under severe stress, the sympathetic nervous system overrides prefrontal cortex activity, shutting down access to *g* and forcing reliance on primitive, *s*-heavy reflexes. These cross-layer vetoes demonstrate that Tolman’s cognitive funds (*g*, *s*) are necessary but **not sufficient** conditions for performance. The ultimate arbiter is the integrity of the underlying biological hardware.

**Crisis Habit Regression vs. Counter-Trap Maneuvers: A Dynamic Clock Analysis**

Under extreme pressure (time scarcity, high stakes, threat), the system’s access to its general fund $F_g$ becomes constrained. This is not merely a deficit but an active **regression** to simpler, highly practiced specific routines ($s$-dominated behaviors).
- **Mechanism:** Stress hormones (cortisol, adrenaline) inhibit prefrontal cortex function, reducing executive control and working memory capacity—key substrates of $g$. Simultaneously, amygdala activation promotes fast, stereotyped responses mediated by basal ganglia circuits (high $s$).
- **Example:** A chess player under time pressure (blitz) abandons deep strategic calculation (high $g$) and relies on opening book memorization and tactical pattern recognition (high $s$). A programmer debugging under deadline reverts to trial-and-error editing (high $s$) instead of systematic root-cause analysis (high $g$).
- **Recursive Aspect:** The agent knows this regression is occurring ("I’m thinking too narrowly"). But the crisis *forces* the regression because the cost of engaging $g$ (slow, uncertain) exceeds the perceived benefit given the time clock.

**2. Counter-Trap Maneuvers: Exploiting Opponent’s Regression**

- **Mechanism:** Deliberately create time pressure, ambiguity, or emotional provocation to trigger the opponent’s shift from $g$-reliant to $s$-reliant processing. Then, prepare a counter-strategy specifically designed to foil the predicted habitual response.
- **Example:** In poker, a skilled player may make a aggressive bluff not because they have a strong hand, but to induce a weak player (prone to tilt/emotional stress) to fold a marginal hand out of fear (habitual avoidance of loss, high $s$) rather than calculating pot odds (high $g$).
- **Clock Dependency:** The counter-trap must be timed precisely. If the induced stress is too mild, no regression occurs. If too severe, the opponent may shut down entirely or resort to non-routine panic. There is an optimal "stress window" for triggering predictable habits.
