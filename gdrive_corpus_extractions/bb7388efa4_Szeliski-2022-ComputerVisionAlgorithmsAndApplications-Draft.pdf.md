The gray-zone audit therefore must track three layers simultaneously: the geometric forward model, the estimation objective, and the breakdown regime where the added assumptions stop being credible.

The first derivation layer is image formation. The text gives the calibrated camera model as a chain of transformations from world coordinates to pixel coordinates. On [PAGE 73] it says, “The 3 × 3 matrix K is called the calibration matrix and describes the camera intrinsics.” In formal terms, a world point in homogeneous coordinates is mapped through extrinsic rotation and translation, then through intrinsics, then through perspective division. The units are strict: rotation parameters are dimensionless or radian-based, translation is in meters, focal length is in pixels after calibration, and the image measurement is in pixel coordinates. This layer already contains a singularity: without additional constraints, the calibration matrix cannot be uniquely recovered from image measurements alone. The text notes that external measurement is required to disentangle sensor orientation, sensor spacing, and intrinsics [PAGE 73]. This is not a minor caveat; it is a veto condition for any system that claims metric accuracy from uncalibrated imagery alone.

The second layer is alignment and motion. The book connects feature-based alignment, parametric motion, dense optical flow, and Fourier methods. The key operator is incremental least squares. On [PAGE 421], the parametric motion update is formulated as a linearized intensity residual minimization, with a Jacobian formed by multiplying the image gradient by the motion Jacobian. The derivation exposes a recurring failure mode: if the image patch lacks texture in one direction, the Hessian becomes rank-deficient. The text names this directly as the aperture problem: on [PAGE 417] it states, “Only the normal component of the velocity (displacement) can be reliably recovered in this case.” That is a singular regime, not a soft performance issue. The matrix governing the displacement update has a near-zero eigenvalue along the ambiguous direction, so noise can produce arbitrarily large tangential estimates.

The third layer is global geometry. The text moves from two-frame structure from motion to factorization and bundle adjustment. The source pages support a formal operator stack with explicit units, assumptions, and singular regimes.

1. Calibrated projection and intrinsics  
On [PAGE 73], the text states: “The 3 × 3 matrix K is called the calibration matrix and describes the camera intrinsics.” A calibrated projection can be written as

\[
\tilde{x} = K [R \mid t] p_w,
\]

where \(p_w \in \mathbb{R}^4\) is a homogeneous world point with translation components in meters, \([R \mid t]\) is the extrinsic pose with \(R \in SO(3)\) and \(t\) in meters, \(K\) maps camera rays to pixel coordinates with units of pixels, and \(\tilde{x}\) is a homogeneous pixel vector. The equation assumes a pinhole or calibrated central projection. It breaks down when lens distortion is strong, when the sensor has significant non-central projection, or when intrinsics are not externally constrained. The text explicitly notes that external measurement is needed to separate intrinsics from extrinsics [PAGE 73].

2. Homography and planar or rotational alignment  
On [PAGE 453], the source describes the mapping between two images of a common plane or a rotating camera using a homography:

\[
\tilde{x}_1 \sim \tilde{H}_{10} \tilde{x}_0.
\]

Here \(\tilde{H}_{10}\) is a \(3 \times 3\) projective matrix defined up to scale. The operator assumes that the scene is planar, or that the camera motion is pure rotation, or that parallax is negligible. The breakdown regime is non-planar geometry with significant parallax. In that case, a single homography cannot align all pixels, and residuals will concentrate near depth discontinuities.

3. Epipolar constraint and essential matrix  
For calibrated two-view geometry, the source gives the relation between corresponding rays. On [PAGE 369], the text derives the constraint after eliminating depth by crossing with translation. In modern notation:

\[
\hat{x}_1^T E \hat{x}_0 = 0,
\qquad
E = [t]_\times R,
\]

where \(\hat{x}_i = N(K_i^{-1} x_i)\) are unit viewing directions, \(E\) is the essential matrix, and \([t]_\times\) is the skew-symmetric cross-product operator. The text later discusses recovering \(R\) and \(t\) from the essential matrix and notes that there are sign and rotation ambiguities. On [PAGE 373], it states that “we have to generate all four possible rotation matrices” and resolve them using chirality. Singular regimes include small baselines, noisy correspondences, pure planar scenes, and points near the plane at infinity. Chirality checks become unstable as points approach infinity because depth signs become error-prone.

4. Triangulation and conditioning  
The source explains triangulation as a homogeneous linear estimation problem. On [PAGE 369], it says that if homogeneous coordinates are used, the equations are “best solved as a singular value decomposition (SVD) or eigenvalue problem.” If one sets the homogeneous scale to one, “the resulting system may be singular or poorly conditioned, i.e., if all of the viewing rays are parallel.” This is a precise singularity: triangulation uncertainty grows without bound as the intersection angle between rays approaches zero. Units matter: image residuals are in pixels, camera matrices encode focal length in pixels, and reconstructed depth is in meters only after calibration and scale resolution.

5. Stereo disparity-depth relation  
On [PAGE 561], the book gives the rectified stereo relation:

\[
d = \frac{fB}{Z},
\]

where \(d\) is disparity in pixels, \(f\) is focal length in pixels, \(B\) is baseline in meters, and \(Z\) is scene depth in meters. The operator assumes rectified images, known calibration, and static scene geometry. It breaks down when \(Z \to \infty\) because disparity approaches the quantization floor; when rectification error introduces vertical parallax; when occlusion violates correspondence; or when exposure differences invalidate raw intensity matching.

6. Lucas–Kanade and brightness constancy  
The source presents incremental translational alignment as minimization of squared intensity differences. For a parametric motion model, the update on [PAGE 421] is linearized as

\[
E_{LK-PM}(p + \Delta p)
\approx
\sum_i
\left[
J_1(x_i') \Delta p + e_i
\right]^2,
\]

with

\[
J_1(x_i') = \nabla I_1(x_i') \frac{\partial x'}{\partial p}(x_i).
\]

Here \(p\) is the motion parameter vector, \(\Delta p\) is the incremental update, \(e_i\) is the intensity residual, and \(\nabla I_1\) has units of intensity change per pixel. The Hessian approximation is

\[
A = \sum_i J_{x'}^T
\left[
\nabla I_1^T \nabla I_1
\right]
J_{x'}.
\]

The singular regime is the aperture problem. On [PAGE 417], the text says: “Only the normal component of the velocity (displacement) can be reliably recovered in this case.” If the image patch contains only a one-dimensional edge, \(A\) has a near-zero eigenvalue along the edge tangent. Additional breakdown occurs under large displacement, because the Taylor linearization assumes small motion; under brightness change, because the constancy assumption fails; and under aliasing, because high-frequency texture can produce misleading local minima.

7. Normalized cross-correlation  
The source defines a normalized correlation score. On [PAGE 409], it states: “The normalized cross-correlation score is always guaranteed to be in the range [−1, 1].” In formal terms,

\[
E_{NCC}(u)
=
\frac{
\sum_i
\left[I_0(x_i)-\bar{I}_0\right]
\left[I_1(x_i+u)-\bar{I}_1\right]
}{
\sqrt{
\sum_i \left[I_0(x_i)-\bar{I}_0\right]^2
\sum_i \left[I_1(x_i+u)-\bar{I}_1\right]^2
}
}.
\]

The score is dimensionless. It is useful under affine intensity changes, but it is undefined when either patch has zero variance. The text notes that performance degrades in noisy low-contrast regions [PAGE 409].

8. Fourier alignment and convolution  
On [PAGE 157], the source lists Fourier properties, including the convolution theorem: convolution in the spatial domain becomes multiplication in the Fourier domain. This supports fast alignment methods. However, the source also warns that Fourier whitening or phase correlation is not universally superior. If signals have low signal-to-noise ratio at some frequencies, whitening can decrease performance. The breakdown regime is therefore noise-spectrum dependent: phase correlation helps when noise is concentrated in narrow bands, but can hurt when informative frequencies are weak relative to noise.

9. Parametric transformation hierarchy  
On [PAGE 61], the book lists 2D and 3D transformation families by degrees of freedom and preserved properties: translation, rigid, similarity, affine, and projective. These operators differ in invariance. Translation preserves orientation but not rotation; rigid preserves lengths; similarity preserves angles; affine preserves parallelism; projective preserves straight lines. The singular regime appears when a simpler model is forced onto data generated by a richer model. For example, an affine model cannot represent perspective foreshortening, while a projective model can overfit noise if the scene is nearly planar or the point configuration is degenerate.

10. Regularization and variational energies  
On [PAGE 197], the source defines smoothness functionals such as

\[
E_2 =
\int
f_{xx}^2 + 2 f_{xy}^2 + f_{yy}^2
\, dx\,dy.
\]

The mixed derivative term is required for rotational invariance. On [PAGE 201], it generalizes to robust penalty functions:

\[
E_r =
\sum_{i,j}
s_x(i,j)\rho(f(i+1,j)-f(i,j))
+
s_y(i,j)\rho(f(i,j+1)-f(i,j)).
\]

The family \(\rho(x)=|x|^p\) is called \(p\)-norms. When \(p < 2\), the solution becomes more piecewise continuous; when \(p < 1\), the objective becomes nonconvex and harder to optimize. The breakdown regime is that robustness can create many local minima, requiring continuation, graduated non-convexity, or global combinatorial methods.

11. Markov random fields and graph cuts  
The source formulates labeling problems as MRF energies. A representative prior is

\[
E_p(i,j)
=
s_x(i,j) V(l(i,j), l(i+1,j))
+
s_y(i,j) V(l(i,j), l(i,j+1)),
\]

where \(l(i,j)\) is a label and \(V\) is a compatibility function [PAGE 209]. On [PAGE 205], the text says that for binary images a better technique is to “re-formulate the energy minimization as a max-ﬂow/min-cut graph optimization problem.” The guarantee of exact minimization applies only to certain binary regularity conditions. If the energy is non-submodular, or if labels are high-order and unordered without a valid potential, the simple graph-cut construction may not yield the global optimum.

12. Recognition operators  
On [PAGE 685], the source describes boosting:

\[
h(x)
=
\operatorname{sign}
\left(
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \alpha_j h_j(x)
\right),
\]

where \(h_j(x)\) are weak learners and \(\alpha_j\) are learned weights. This operator assumes access to representative training data and a loss function that supports iterative reweighting. Its breakdown regime is distribution shift: if test imagery differs strongly from training imagery in illumination, viewpoint, sensor noise, or semantic context, the learned classifier may produce confident false positives. On [PAGE 677], recognition is presented as a distinct layer after geometry and photometric modeling, which reinforces the point that recognition failures cannot be repaired by geometric consistency alone unless the recognition hypothesis is explicitly coupled to scene constraints.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage in the source text explains why contemporary computer vision systems contain certain recurring traps. On [PAGE 33], Szeliski recounts the story that in 1966 Marvin Minsky asked Gerald Jay Sussman to “spend the summer linking a camera to a computer and getting the computer to describe what it saw.” The text then says, “We now know that the problem is slightly more difﬁcult than that.” This anecdote is not merely historical color; it identifies a multi-decade failure pattern: early confidence that perception can be reduced to a simple input-output mapping. The retro-causal lesson is that any project which treats vision as a short integration task will underbudget for ambiguity, calibration, occlusion, illumination, and statistical generalization.

A second lineage is the shift from pure image processing to three-dimensional recovery. The text distinguishes computer vision from digital image processing by a desire to recover three-dimensional structure [PAGE 33]. This creates a persistent trap: algorithms that look excellent on 2D appearance can become unstable when lifted into 3D. A feature matcher may produce visually plausible correspondences, yet the reconstruction may be projectively distorted, scale-ambiguous, or topologically wrong. The source addresses this through calibration, epipolar geometry, factorization, and bundle adjustment. The genealogy shows that the field repeatedly rediscovered the need for metric or quasi-metric constraints after projective reconstructions proved insufficient for measurement.

A third lineage is regularization and probabilistic modeling. On [PAGE 37], the text describes how variational methods were made well-posed through regularization and how Markov random fields enabled global search. This created a lasting trap: teams often treat regularization as a cosmetic stabilizer rather than a claim about scene statistics. If the prior is wrong, the optimizer will converge to a smooth but incorrect solution. The source’s discussion of robust penalties, continuation methods, and graph cuts is an attempt to make the prior explicit. The retro-causal warning is that optimization success can mask model error. A low energy value is not evidence of physical correctness unless the energy terms correspond to valid measurement and scene assumptions.

A fourth lineage is the move from sparse features to dense reconstruction. The source describes sparse correspondence, dense stereo, multi-view stereo, and internet-photo reconstruction [PAGE 393]. This transition creates a multi-week trap in applied projects: sparse feature demos can work for weeks, then dense reconstruction fails because small calibration errors, rolling shutter effects, moving objects, or repetitive textures corrupt the depth field. The source’s emphasis on feature tracks, bundle adjustment, and robust reprojection error is a direct response to that trap. The genealogy indicates that dense success depends on sparse hygiene. If feature tracks are contaminated, dense stereo will inherit the contamination.

A fifth lineage is the rise of recognition and learning. On [PAGE 677], recognition is presented as a separate chapter after geometry and motion. The source notes that learning became dominant because of large partially labeled image collections [PAGE 41]. This creates a contemporary trap: learned systems may bypass explicit geometry while still implicitly depending on it. A detector trained on web images may fail when camera pose, scale, or illumination differ. The retro-causal genealogy therefore warns that recognition must be evaluated as a statistical operator under distribution shift, not as a geometric oracle.

Across these lineages, the multi-week traps are predictable. In the first weeks, a system may succeed on controlled imagery. In later weeks, calibration drift, exposure changes, motion blur, occlusion, and rare object categories accumulate. The source text’s historical arc is a map of those traps: underestimation of ambiguity, premature metric claims, weak priors, sparse-to-dense error propagation, and overtrust in learned detectors.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis treats the reasoning system as an agent that must predict not only the scene but also its own future failure modes. The first mode is crisis habit regression. Under deadline pressure, a team tends to regress to familiar operators: raw SSD matching, fixed-threshold feature matching, simple optical flow, or a single calibration assumed to hold across all conditions. This regression is reinforced by early demos that appear successful. The source text warns against this implicitly by repeatedly emphasizing assumptions: calibration must be estimated, robust regression is needed, motion models must match geometry, and optimization requires initialization. The crisis habit is to skip these checks because they slow the demo.

The counter-trap is to install explicit self-audits before the crisis. One audit is observability: before estimating a motion or depth parameter, check whether the Jacobian or Hessian has sufficient rank. The source gives the aperture problem as the canonical example [PAGE 417]. A mature system should estimate parameter uncertainty, not just a point estimate. On [PAGE 797], the text notes that uncertainty estimation is often ignored but can be obtained from the inverse Hessian in locally quadratic problems. The counter-trap therefore is to compute uncertainty as a routine output. If the covariance is large, the system should abstain, request more texture, change viewpoint, or acquire calibration data.

A second crisis habit is to treat optimization convergence as correctness. Bundle adjustment can reduce reprojection error while still producing an incorrect reconstruction if the initial pair is weak, if the scene is nearly planar, or if outliers are not rejected. The source discusses robust non-linear minimization and the need for good initialization [PAGE 385]. The counter-trap is to maintain multiple hypotheses: different initial pairs, different motion models, or different robust thresholds. The agent must reason about what another competent observer would do if they saw the same residuals. If a rival hypothesis explains the data with fewer assumptions, the current hypothesis is suspect.

A third crisis habit is to assume that learned recognition is invariant. The source presents boosting and feature-based recognition as powerful but data-dependent [PAGE 685]. Under time pressure, a team may trust a detector because it works on the training set. The counter-trap is to test adversarial conditions: unusual pose, occlusion, weather, low light, and sensor noise. The recursive model must ask: if I were the detector, what image would make me fail? That question produces targeted evaluation rather than generic benchmarking.

The clock is the final agent in the model. A project clock forces tradeoffs between coverage and rigor. Early clock pressure favors simple operators. Later clock pressure favors patch-based corrections, post-hoc smoothing, or manual overrides. The dangerous pattern is to let clock pressure decide epistemic questions. The source’s sample syllabus and project-oriented structure [PAGE 49] suggest incremental validation: small programming tasks expose failure modes before the final system is assembled. The recursive theory of mind therefore recommends a staged clock: early phases test calibration and motion under controlled conditions; middle phases test dense estimation and robustness; late phases test semantic recognition and failure recovery. If the clock compresses these phases, the system should explicitly mark which estimates are provisional.

The recursive conclusion is that the agent must model its own regression tendencies. A computer vision system is not only estimating the world; it is estimating the conditions under which its estimates are valid. The strongest design is one that can say: “This estimate is invalid because the motion model is underconstrained,” “This reconstruction is invalid because the baseline is too small,” or “This recognition is invalid because the training distribution does not cover this illumination.” Those abstentions are not failures; they are the recursive safeguards that prevent a local habit from becoming a global error.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication is the process by which one physical or statistical layer rejects an estimate produced by another layer. The source text supports several veto proofs.

Physical optics veto: if the camera model is not central or the lens distortion is not modeled, calibrated geometric estimates are invalid. The source notes that wide-angle and fisheye lenses may require non-central or spline-based distortion models [PAGE 81]. Proof by contradiction: assume a pinhole model is valid for a fisheye image. The pinhole model maps every ray through a single center. A fisheye lens can collect rays over nearly 180 degrees and may not have a single effective center. Therefore the pinhole assumption contradicts the optics. The veto rule is: if the lens type violates the projection model, reject metric depth or pose estimates until the appropriate model is fitted.

Photometric veto: brightness constancy is required for raw intensity alignment. Suppose an image pair is related by an illumination field \(A(x,t)\). The observed images are

\[
I_0(x) = R(x) L_0(x),
\qquad
I_1(x+u) = R(x+u) L_1(x+u).
\]

The basic alignment model assumes \(I_1(x+u) \approx I_0(x)\). If \(L_1\) differs from \(L_0\) spatially, the equality fails. Therefore the residual contains illumination error, not only motion error. The veto rule is: if normalized correlation or robust photometric residual indicates spatially structured illumination change, reject raw SSD flow estimates. The source’s discussion of normalized cross-correlation and robust metrics supports this: NCC is more stable under affine intensity changes, but even NCC can fail when illumination changes are non-affine or occlusions alter visible surfaces [PAGE 409].

Geometric observability veto: if the scene and motion configuration make a parameter unobservable, any estimate of that parameter is not physically meaningful. For triangulation, parallel viewing rays produce no stable intersection [PAGE 369]. For two-view pose, a twisted pair and chirality ambiguity require additional checks [PAGE 373]. For stereo, disparity is inversely proportional to depth, so far points have subpixel disparity and fail quantization [PAGE 561]. The veto rule is: if the observability matrix or Hessian condition number exceeds a safe bound, reject the estimate. This is not a statistical preference; it is a proof that the data do not contain the parameter.

Scene-motion veto: structure from motion assumes a static scene and coherent camera motion. If independent objects move, the epipolar constraint is violated for those pixels. The source discusses layered motion and independent motion in dense motion estimation [PAGE 405], and it describes internet-photo reconstruction as requiring careful selection of image pairs with reliable parallax [PAGE 393]. Proof: assume a moving object pixel obeys the static epipolar constraint. Its image motion is generated by object motion plus camera motion. Unless object motion lies along the epipolar line, the constraint fails. Therefore the pixel must be vetoed from the static reconstruction or assigned to a separate motion layer.

Weather and environment veto: weather changes appearance statistics. Rain, fog, snow, and low light reduce contrast, add transient occlusions, and alter radiometry. The source’s discussion of exposure, noise, sampling, and camera response [PAGE 97] shows that sensor measurements depend on exposure and illumination. If weather adds a nonstationary additive or multiplicative component, brightness constancy and noise models are invalidated. The veto rule is: if environmental conditions violate the training or calibration distribution, reject or widen uncertainty. This is especially important for recognition, where detectors may rely on context and appearance cues that weather removes.

Biomechanical veto: for human pose or activity tracking, the source describes kinematic models and inverse kinematics [PAGE 629]. A proposed pose can be rejected if it violates joint limits, limb lengths, or contact constraints. Proof: a kinematic chain has fixed segment lengths and bounded joint angles. If an estimated configuration requires a limb length change or impossible joint angle, the estimate is physically invalid. The veto rule is: recognition or tracking outputs must be projected onto the feasible kinematic manifold or rejected.

The adjudication order should be conservative. First, check physical sensor and optics validity. Second, check photometric consistency. Third, check geometric observability. Fourth, check scene-motion coherence. Fifth, check semantic and biomechanical feasibility. If any lower layer vetoes the hypothesis, higher layers must not override it without new evidence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario defines an observable test, a veto condition, and a prediction.

Scenario 1: Aperture veto for optical flow  
Assume a synthetic sequence containing only a straight moving edge with no texture along the edge tangent. The Lucas–Kanade Hessian is

\[
A =
\sum_{x \in W}
\nabla I(x) \nabla I(x)^T.
\]

For an ideal one-dimensional edge, one eigenvalue of \(A\) is near zero. The source states that only the normal component can be reliably recovered [PAGE 417].  
Veto condition: if the smaller eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\min}(A)\) is below a noise-derived threshold, reject the tangential component of the flow estimate.  
Verification: inject known ground-truth motion with both normal and tangential components. Compute the covariance of the estimated flow over repeated noise realizations. The tangential variance should diverge as \(\lambda_{\min} \to 0\), while the normal variance remains bounded.  
Prediction: the recovered normal displacement will remain close to ground truth, while the recovered tangential displacement will have error increasing as the inverse square root of \(\lambda_{\min}\). If the system reports a confident tangential flow under this condition, it is invalid.

Scenario 2: Planar-scene homography veto for parallax-based reconstruction  
Assume a planar scene and a camera pair related by rotation and translation. Feature correspondences from the plane satisfy a homography exactly in the noise-free case:

\[
\tilde{x}_1 \sim \tilde{H} \tilde{x}_0.
\]

The source presents homography as the mapping for a common plane or pure rotation [PAGE 453].  
Veto condition: if a homography fit has reprojection residual below the sensor noise floor, reject a reconstruction that claims significant out-of-plane parallax from those same points.  
Verification: generate synthetic planar points with known depth variation set to zero. Fit both a homography and a projective depth model. If the homography residual is at the noise floor, the depth model should not produce a statistically significant improvement. Use an information criterion or cross-validation on held-out points.  
Prediction: any estimated depth variation will be dominated by noise and will not reduce held-out reprojection error. If a system reports stable 3D structure from purely planar correspondences, it is overfitting the motion model.

Scenario 3: Illumination-gain veto for raw SSD alignment  
Assume an image pair with a global gain change:

\[
I_1(x) = a I_0(x) + b,
\]

with \(a > 0\). Raw SSD alignment assumes \(I_1(x+u) \approx I_0(x)\). If \(a \neq 1\) or \(b \neq 0\), the SSD residual contains an intensity bias. Normalized cross-correlation removes affine intensity variation more effectively, but only if the spatial support has nonzero variance [PAGE 409].  
Veto condition: if the mean residual of raw SSD is correlated with image intensity or if NCC and SSD produce displacement estimates differing by more than the Cramér–Rao lower bound under estimated noise, reject the raw SSD estimate.  
Verification: create a controlled sequence with known translation and known gain changes. Compare SSD, NCC, and robust normalized SSD. Measure displacement bias as a function of gain error.  
Prediction: raw SSD bias will increase with the mismatch between the assumed constant-intensity model and the actual gain. NCC will reduce bias for spatially varying affine changes but will become unstable in zero-variance regions. If a system reports that raw SSD is unbiased under large gain change, the claim is invalid.

These counterfactual vetoes are verifiable because they rely on synthetic ground truth, eigenvalue thresholds, reprojection residuals, held-out validation, or controlled illumination changes. They convert abstract warnings from the source text into operational tests: aperture degeneracy must be detected through Hessian rank; planar degeneracy must be detected through homography sufficiency; illumination degeneracy must be detected through photometric model mismatch.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
