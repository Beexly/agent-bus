<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under the assumptions of [PAGE 95], agents’ theory of mind collapses to a first-order fixed point. Because personal powers and productive capacities are fixed and inalienable, agent \(i\) need not model agent \(j\)’s investment strategy, capacity expansion, or speculative demand for productive services. The recursive belief hierarchy \(B_i(B_j(\cdots))\) truncates at depth zero: each agent knows endowments, knows they are fixed, and trades finished commodities in a complete but static exchange. Crisis-habit regression under these conditions means agents revert to routine barter of known endowments; there is no counter-trap of entrepreneurial surprise because the entrepreneur, as defined in footnote 1, does not exist in the handicraft limit.

The counter-trap emerges the moment the assumptions crack. If one agent secretly accumulates alienable capital or develops a secondary division of labor (producing an intermediate good for another), the recursive structure reignites: agent \(i\) must now model whether \(j\) will withhold inputs, renegotiate terms, or appropriate quasi-rents. The clock of this regression is institutional: the transition from handicraft to enterprise operates on a timescale of weeks (contract renegotiation) to months (capital recomposition). In Knight’s framework, profit arises precisely when this recursive theory of mind becomes unavoidable—when agents must judge uncertain future demands and rival strategies without a stable probability measure. The static page [PAGE 95] is therefore the zero-clock baseline; any tick of the clock toward investment or cooperation reintroduces the infinite regress of expectations that defines genuine uncertainty.
