\[

Under crisis, the source predicts habit regression: teams fall back on mastered simple signals and rehearsed plays because “The first essential in any system of signals is simplicity” [PAGE 107 of 236]. The recursive model is: the quarterback holds a first-order belief about the opponent’s expected reaction, the opponent holds a second-order belief about the quarterback’s tendency, and the coach holds a third-order belief about which habits will emerge under stress. Formally, let \(B_Q^{(k)}(a\mid p)\) be the quarterback’s \(k\)-level belief that the opponent will take action \(a\) when play \(p\) is called. A decision-theoretic operator is
\[
\mathbb{E}[\Delta x\mid p,B_Q^{(k)}]
=\int \Delta x\, d\Pr(\Delta x\mid p,B_Q^{(k)}),
\]
with expected gain in metres. The counter-trap is to choose \(p\) that maximizes expected gain under the opponent’s predicted adjustment, not under the opponent’s previous adjustment. The source supplies concrete counter-traps: the delayed pass to the left half, where “The Q. pretends to pass to L.T. … then turns, hiding it the while, and passes to L.H.” [PAGE 153 of 236]; the fake kick; the wing shift; and the quarterback run that requires him to pass the line “at least five yards out from the point where the ball was snapped” [PAGE 37 of 236]. The clock operator is the sequence: use memorized successive plays “in the opponent’s territory about twenty-five yards from the goal, when quickness and speed of plays used is so essential to success,” especially because “the ‘cheering’ makes it hard to hear the signals” [PAGE 161 of 236]. Thus crisis does not merely cause regression; it creates a predictable regression that a counter-trap can exploit. The recursive theory of mind must therefore model both the stressed team’s habit distribution and the opponent’s model of that distribution; otherwise the counter-trap is indistinguishable from a lucky guess.
