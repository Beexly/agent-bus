The core postulate is that \( E_t \) is entirely determined by \( S_t \). There is no independent stochastic or agential input.
\[

*Breakdown Regime:* This model breaks down upon encountering any genuine indeterminacy (e.g., quantum collapse affecting neural pathways, or a logically novel conceptual synthesis not reducible to prior combinatorial rules). **Fatalism / Independent Variable Model:**
James suggests the fatalist operates with an implicit model where \( E_t \) *could* be an independent variable, potentially originating from a "fourth dimension" (a metaphor for a non-physical or extra-causal source).
\[
S_{t+1} = h(S_t, E_t^*), \quad \text{with } E_t^* \perp S_t \text{ (statistically/logically independent)}.
\]
However, the fatalist observes that their actual \( E_t \) consistently yields suboptimal outcomes due to weaknesses in \( S_t \) (e.g., poor habits, fatigue). They feel \( E_t^* \) is too weak to overcome the drift imposed by \( S_t \). Mathematically, they perceive:
\[
\| \nabla_{S_t} \text{(Outcome)} \| \gg \| \nabla_{E_t} \text{(Outcome)} \|
\]
i.e., the gradient of outcome with respect to circumstances dwarfs the gradient with respect to effort.
*Units/Probability:* Here, \( E_t^* \) could be modeled as a random variable from a distribution \( P(E^*) \) not derived from \( P(S_t) \). The "impotence" is the low conditional probability \( P(\text{Success} | E_t^*, \text{Weak } S_t) \approx 0 \).
*SINGULARITY/BREAKDOWN:* This model hits a singularity when trying to reconcile its two components. It posits \( E^* \) as independent yet consistently powerless. If \( E^* \) is truly independent, there must exist some \( S_t \) and some realization \( e^* \) such that success is possible. The fatalist's universal claim "it is hopeless" contradicts the existence of \( E^* \). The model collapses into either genuine determinism (if \( E^* \) is eliminated) or libertarian free will (if \( E^* \) is acknowledged as potent under different conditions).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of James's critique stretches back to the early modern debates on causal closure and the nature of the soul. Spinoza's geometric ethics presented a pure determinism where conatus (effort) was simply a mode of substance, denying any independent mental causation. Kant responded with his transcendental idealism, carving out a noumenal realm for practical reason (free will) precisely because the phenomenal world's deterministic laws made morality inexplicable. James, writing after Hume's skeptical associationism and amid the rise of strict physiological psychology, navigates between these poles. He inherits Hume's emphasis on habit ("hereditary habits") but rejects Humean skepticism about the self as a fiction. The "retrocausal" aspect lies in how James uses the future-oriented feeling of responsibility (the sense that we *ought* to have chosen differently) to diagnose the present structure of belief. The fatalist's regret implies a backward-looking causal judgment: "My past weakness caused this," but it also contains a forward-looking, counterfactual projection: "If I had been stronger, things would be different now." This projects a shadow of independence back onto the past moment of choice. The genealogical trap is adopting the language of scientific determinism (popularized by Comte, Spencer, and early neuroscience) while retaining the theological or moral intuition of a fallen will (from Augustine to Calvin). James exposes this hybrid as incoherent. The multi-week trap manifests when an individual, perhaps after a series of professional or personal failures, starts reading deterministic philosophy or pop-science genetics/neurology. They latch onto "hereditary habits" and "temporary bodily dispositions" as exculpatory factors, weaving a narrative of inevitable decline. This narrative, over weeks, alters their perception of their own agency, making future efforts feel increasingly futile, thereby *creating* the very determinism they claimed to merely describe.
