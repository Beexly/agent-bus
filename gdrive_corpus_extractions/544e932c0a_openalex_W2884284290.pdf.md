A scoring rule is proper if E_{Y∼G} S(G,Y) ≤ E_{Y∼G} S(F,Y) for all F, G ∈ F, and strictly proper if equality holds only when F = G. This property is the linchpin of comparative forecast evaluation: the lowest average score over multiple forecast cases identifies the best predictive performance while compelling forecasters to truthfully report their believed distribution [PAGE 4].

The first multi-hop derivation moves from properness to the continuous ranked probability score. The CRPS is defined for univariate observations with Ω = R as CRPS(F, y) = ∫_R (F(z) − 1{y ≤ z})² dz [PAGE 4, Equation 1]. When the first moment of F is finite, this integral admits the equivalent expectation form CRPS(F, y) = E_F |X₁ − y| − ½ E_{F,F} |X₁ − X₂|, where X₁ and X₂ are independent draws from F [PAGE 4–5]. This identity is computationally consequential: for a simulated sample of size m, the empirical CRPS reduces to Equation 2, CRPS( ˆF_m, y) = (1/m) Σ_i |X_i − y| − (1/(2m²)) Σ_i Σ_j |X_i − X_j|, which is O(m²) and therefore inefficient [PAGE 6]. The scoringRules package instead uses an algebraically equivalent representation based on order statistics and the generalized quantile function, Equation 3, achieving average O(m log m) performance [PAGE 6].

A second derivation concerns the logarithmic score, LogS(F, y) = −log(f(y)), which is strictly proper relative to distributions admitting densities [PAGE 4]. Unlike CRPS, LogS requires a predictive density. Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of forecasters, under pressure or uncertainty, to revert to familiar but inferior habits: preferring in-sample information criteria such as AIC or BIC [PAGE 2], trusting raw ensemble output without statistical post-processing [PAGE 11], or defaulting to maximum likelihood because it is computationally standard even when the decision context demands a proper scoring rule aligned with the loss function. The paper explicitly contrasts this with out-of-sample comparative evaluation using proper scoring rules [PAGE 2].

The counter-trap is the incentive structure of strict properness: because E_{Y∼G} S(G,Y) ≤ E_{Y∼G} S(F,Y) with equality only at F = G [PAGE 4], a forecaster who shades, hedges, or strategically misrepresents their predictive distribution earns a worse expected score than one who reports truthfully. This forecloses the temptation to game a leaderboard by issuing over-dispersed or biased forecasts; the lowest average score over cases identifies the best predictive performance [PAGE 4].

The clock operates on two timescales. Second is the forecast-horizon clock: precipitation ensembles lead by five to eight days for three-day accumulation [PAGE 11–12], while GDP forecasts extend one to four quarters ahead [PAGE 13]. As the horizon lengthens, forecast certainty decreases (visible in the GDP forecast-distribution histograms, Figure 4 [PAGE 15]), and proper scores must be interpreted within that expanding-horizon window rather than compared across mismatched lead times.

The adjudication is to enforce out-of-sample evaluation on a holdout period with proper scores only [PAGE 2, PAGE 12–14].

A forecast-scoring pipeline is invalidated if any of the following hold: (i) the predictive distribution lacks a finite first moment, because CRPS strict propriety is guaranteed only when E|X| < ∞ [PAGE 5]; (ii) the observation falls into the tails of a simulated forecast distribution evaluated by KDE-based LogS, because bandwidth choice then dominates the score [PAGE 6–7]; (iii) the ensemble spread is zero, because those cases are dropped and the remaining sample is no longer representative of the full forecast distribution [PAGE 11–12]; (iv) parametric family constraints are violated, e.g., Student's t degrees of freedom ν ≤ 1, GEV/GPD shape ξ ≥ 1, or censored boundary masses L + U ≥ 1 [PAGE 25, 29–30, 33]. Each condition acts as a hard veto: score comparisons computed under violation are not guaranteed to rank the true model first, because properness assumptions fail.

Scenario 1 (heavy-tailed finite-sample veto). Counterfactual: a forecaster compares two models using CRPS when the true observation-generating distribution is standard Cauchy (E|X| = ∞). Because the finite-first-moment condition fails [PAGE 5], CRPS is not strictly proper. Prediction: over 10,000 replications of n = 1,000 Cauchy observations, a deliberately miscalibrated heavy-tailed forecast will attain lower empirical CRPS than the true Cauchy forecast in more than 50% of replications, causing incorrect model selection. Verification: simulate Cauchy samples, compute crps() for true vs. miscalibrated Student's t with ν = 1.5, record sign of score difference. This is directly implementable with scoringRules parametric families because crps_t supports Student's t with location-scale generalization [PAGE 25].

Scenario 2 (small-MCMC ranking flip veto). Counterfactual: two Bayesian GDP forecast models differ in true CRPS by δ = 0.15 at horizon h = 1, but the analyst uses only m = 50 posterior predictive draws. Figure 2 shows that at m = 50 the empirical 90% confidence interval for Gaussian CRPS spans roughly [0.5, 0.9] in score units, while at m = 5,000 the interval collapses around the analytic value [PAGE 9–10]. Prediction: with m = 50, the ranking of the two models flips sign in over 50% of 500 independent replications because the Monte Carlo noise exceeds δ. Verification: run crps_sample() with method = "edf" on Gaussian(2, 3) predictive samples at observation y = 0 for m = 50 and m = 5,000 across 500 replications; compare the empirical coverage and sign-flip rate against the dashed analytic line [PAGE 9–10].

Scenario 3 (KDE boundary-tail LogS veto). Counterfactual: evaluating LogS for censored precipitation forecasts at y = 0 using logs_sample() with default bw.nrd() bandwidth versus undersmoothed and oversmoothed bandwidths. Page 6–7 states LogS requires an estimated density and is fragile when outcomes fall into tails; for censored distributions the lower boundary at zero is a tail-adjacent singularity. Prediction: the coefficient of variation of logs_sample across bandwidth choices bw ∈ {bw.nrd/10, bw.nrd, bw.nrd × 10} will exceed 50% for zero-precipitation cases, whereas the mixture-of-parameters estimator logs() with analytic normal mixture remains stable because it uses the known conditional Gaussian form [PAGE 7, PAGE 14–15]. Verification: apply logs_sample() to RainIbk cases with rain = 0 in the evaluation period and compare to logs_mpe from crps()/logs() with family "normal-mixture" [PAGE 15].
