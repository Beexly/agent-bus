This genealogy flows directly into Spence's discrimination-gradient formalism, Amsel's frustration theory (where non-reward induces drive), and modern computational accounts in which homeostatic deficit acts as a multiplicative gain on learned action values — the direct-proportion assumption f(D) ∝ D being a proto-gating signal.

The multi-week trap is explicit in the arithmetic: because D accumulates only until h = 120 h (five days), after which D is fixed at 100 motes, any longitudinal analysis treating week-n drive as n times week-1 drive is invalid. A subject food-deprived continuously for weeks does not accrue drive beyond the 120-hour ceiling; the model saturates, while real physiology would show adaptation, ketosis, or collapse — none represented in D(h). Conversely, retesting a habit formed at D' = 20 motes after weeks at a different deprivation level invokes d = |D − 20|; the generalization gradient predicts a progressive fall in sHz from 46.34 toward 30.94 as d widens. A multi-week practitioner who attributes performance decline to "habit decay" rather than to drive-state mismatch commits a retro-causal misattribution: the habit trace sHR may be intact, but the intensity-stimulus generalization decrement and the unmodeled f(D) modulation suppress expression. The second trap is the silent reversal at D = D': synthesis is "exactly the reverse of the analysis which took place between columns 2 and 3," so any cross-week comparison that ignores the D = D' anchor will misread the columns 9–13 recombinations as new learning when they are merely the identity case of the original compound.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the drive clock.

In a recursive theory of mind, an agent modeling another under crisis must represent two nested drive states: the original habit-formation drive D' and the current crisis drive D. Crisis habit regression occurs when stress shifts D away from D', invoking primary intensity-stimulus generalization. If crisis raises D above D' (e.g., heightened deprivation), d grows and sHz regresses from the 46.34 anchor toward lower values; the agent's behavior is then governed by habits whose compound structure matches the new drive stimulus, not necessarily the historically dominant habit. The regressed repertoire is a function of d and of the uncompleted f(D) gain, meaning crisis can both degrade the original habit (via d) and re-scale all expression (via f(D)).

The counter-trap is the analyst's assumption that observed behavior reflects stable habit strength. An analyst who sees performance fall during a crisis may infer habit weakening, when the true cause is the drive-state deviation d and the truncated f(D) modulation. The counter-trap is to regress behavior on d(h) and D first, attributing residual variance to habit loss only after the generalization gradient and drive gain are partialled out. Failing this, the analyst confounds a gradient effect with an erosion effect.

The clock is the 120-hour / 5-day ceiling embedded in D(h) = (5/6)h capped at 100 motes. Recursively, an agent can model the opponent's drive clock: beyond five days of privation, additional clock time does not increase D, so behavior becomes insensitive to further duration. If the analyst naïvely extends D linearly past 120 h, predictions drift; the recursive agent exploits this by recognizing that the drive clock has stopped, rendering multi-week threats or incentives based on "more deprivation = more drive" empty after the saturation point. The recursion closes on the boundary identity: at D = D', no distortion occurs, so a crisis that returns the organism to the original drive exactly restores the column-2 habit expression, making the drive clock a reset mechanism as well as a constraint.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto layers; adjudication rule.

Physical layer: D(h) = (5/6)h is a purely theoretical privation-to-drive map. It ignores water availability, thermoregulation, and metabolic substrate. A physical veto applies if water is withheld concurrently: food privation hours h no longer map to a single food-drive D because thirst constitutes a separate drive system not reducible to the 100-mote food ceiling. The equation is therefore void under mixed deprivation.

Biomechanical layer: At D = 100 motes (h = 120 h), the organism may be too weak to emit the response even if sHR is high. Reaction potential sER = f(D)·sHR assumes the musculoskeletal system can transduce habit into action; under starvation, glycogen depletion and muscle catabolism impose a hard output floor. A biomechanical veto invalidates the prediction of evoked action whenever the required force or endurance exceeds the degraded physiological capacity at high D, regardless of the computed sHR.

Weather layer: h is clock time, not physiological time. Ambient temperature alters caloric expenditure: cold increases metabolic rate, effectively accelerating drive accumulation per clock hour (effective h > clock h); heat reduces activity and may slow it. The conversion fraction 100/120 was presumably derived under controlled laboratory conditions. A weather veto voids D(h) under non-thermoneutral conditions unless h is corrected for effective energy expenditure.

Adjudication: The operator D(h) = (5/6)h holds only within the binding constraints of [PAGE 244]'s implicit experimental frame — ad lib water, otherwise healthy organism, thermoneutrality, h ≤ 120 h. Any physical, biomechanical, or weather condition outside this frame vetoes the equation; the habit-strength recomputation in columns 9–13 is then not falsifiable from this page alone because the physiological-summation rule (p. 223 ff.) and the f(D) complication are both unspecified and context-dependent.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Veto scenario 1 (saturation): Extend food privation beyond h = 120 h while holding all else constant. Verifiable prediction: D remains at 100 motes; sHz does not continue to decline linearly with additional hours, and behavior plateaus rather than scaling with h. Veto trigger: if measured drive or behavior continues to covary with h past 120 h (e.g., D > 100 motes or sHz falling below 30.94 with further hours), the linear D(h) law and the 100-mote ceiling are jointly falsified.

Veto scenario 2 (generalization null-test): Test a habit originally learned at D' = 20 motes at D = 0 (h = 0, no deprivation). Verifiable prediction: sHz = 30.94 (d = 20), not 46.34; the performance decrement is attributable to primary intensity-stimulus generalization across d = 20 motes, not to unlearning. Veto trigger: if performance at D = 0 remains at 46.34, the claimed generalization gradient (F = 1/50) and the linear Sp(D) assumption are falsified.

Veto scenario 3 (weather/biomechanical moderation): Hold clock privation h constant at, say, 24 h (D = 20 motes) but expose the organism to cold ambient temperature that raises energy expenditure. Verifiable prediction: effective drive accumulates faster than (5/6)h; behavior corresponds to a D greater than 20 motes and an sHz below the D = 20 anchor (toward the d > 0 generalization decrement). Veto trigger: if behavior at cold temperature matches the D = 20 motes prediction exactly, the weather/biomechanical layer is irrelevant (the clock-time assumption is upheld); if behavior shifts as predicted, D(h) is vetoed outside thermoneutral control and requires a temperature-corrected effective-h operator.
