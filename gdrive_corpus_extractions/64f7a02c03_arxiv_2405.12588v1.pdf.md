The source is a time-variant Bradley-Terry analysis of AFL match outcomes, not a direct physiological model. The core multi-hop derivation is: latent team strength \(\lambda_{ir}\) enters an exponential positive-part ratio, which is equivalent to a logit contrast between two teams; adding contest-specific effects introduces a home-designation shift; adding team-specific time-variant covariates makes \(\lambda_{ir}\) a linear predictor plus Gaussian error. The paper states the pairwise probability as
\[
\Pr(Y_{ijr}=1)=\frac{\pi_{ir}}{\pi_{ir}+\pi_{jr}},
\]
and explains that “\(\pi_{ir}=\exp(\lambda_{ir})\) and \(\lambda_{ir}\) is the strength of Team i in round r” [PAGE 9]. The standard model then gives “logit(P(Yijr = 1)) = λi −λj” [PAGE 10]. The contest-specific extension adds a bias term: “δ is the value of the bias and Z = 1 if i has the advantage, −1 otherwise” [PAGE 10]. The team-specific extension states “\(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) are Gaussian independent errors” [PAGE 10]. Thus the model’s inferential chain is: win/loss frequency to logit strength contrast; contest effect to home designation; covariates to time-varying strength; feature selection to final predictive model; accuracy and AIC to evaluation.

A gray-zone audit is necessary because the paper is explicit about what is and is not supported. The data cover 1,826 games from 2015 to 2023, but “draws were excluded as they amounted to only 14 games” [PAGE 9]. Network data were not used: “Network data was not retrieved as it was not publicly available, thereby discarding the use of tactical features” [PAGE 9]. Therefore any tactical-network claim is outside the fitted model. The standard BT model assumes independence of games, but repeated team appearances, Finals, and era shifts violate strict independence; the paper itself notes “team strengths change gradually in the medium term, possibly in a window of three seasons” [PAGE 21]. The probabilistic denominator cannot become exactly zero because \(\pi_{ir}\) and \(\pi_{jr}\) are positive exponentials, but numerical overflow can occur for extreme strengths, and non-identifiability occurs because adding a constant to all \(\lambda_i\) leaves differences unchanged; the paper fixes a reference: “The coefficient for the reference team is 0.00” [PAGE 13]. Separation can occur when a team has only wins or only losses in a window; the paper warns that “the binomial frequencies are expected to be low for all pairs” [PAGE 33]. Feature selection is another gray zone: “A model is trained on one season or window of seasons for each individual feature which, if found to be significant at the 5% level, is added to a set of final features” [PAGE 12]. This is not a causal identification strategy. The positive AT HOME coefficient is described as “an advantage to the Home Team under the BT model” [PAGE 15], and the paper states “teams playing interstate or away are at a disadvantage” [PAGE 16], but it does not isolate crowd, travel, sleep, weather, or biomechanics. The highest reported predictive result is “up to 71.5% of the games” [PAGE 23], while majority voting yields “stable accuracies above 60%, reaching up to 67.96%” [PAGE 24]. These are proportions, not deterministic laws. The model is best treated as an interpretable pre-game association engine, with explicit vetoes against overclaiming causality, tactical mechanisms, weather effects, and individual biomechanics.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operators and units. Let \(Y_{ijr}\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether team \(i\) beats team \(j\) in round \(r\), with \(i,j\in\{1,\ldots,18\}\). The probability is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). The BT probability operator is
\[
\Pr(Y_{ijr}=1)=\frac{\pi_{ir}}{\pi_{ir}+\pi_{jr}},
\qquad
\pi_{ir}=\exp(\lambda_{ir}),
\]
where \(\lambda_{ir}\) is a dimensionless log-strength parameter. The source states “where \(\pi_{ir}=\exp(\lambda_{ir})\) and \(\lambda_{ir}\) is the strength of Team i in round r (Tsokos et al., 2019)” [PAGE 9]. The standard time-invariant logit operator is
\[
\operatorname{logit}\!\left(\Pr(Y_{ijr}=1)\right)=\lambda_i-\lambda_j,
\]
with logit dimensionless. The source reports “logit(P(Yijr = 1)) = λi −λj” [PAGE 10]. The contest-specific operator is
\[
\operatorname{logit}\!\left(\Pr(Y_{ijr}=1)\right)=\lambda_i-\lambda_j+\delta Z,
\]
where \(\delta\) is a dimensionless logit shift and \(Z\in\{+1,-1\}\); the source says “δ is the value of the bias and Z = 1 if i has the advantage, −1 otherwise” [PAGE 10]. In AFL application, \(Z\) is the Home/Away designation and the paper interprets the positive estimate as “an advantage to the Home Team under the BT model” [PAGE 15]. The team-specific time-variant operator is
\[
\lambda_{ir}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\beta_k X_{ik}+\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
\]
where each \(X_{ik}\) carries its own unit—binary indicators are unitless, counts are counts, rates are dimensionless proportions, metres gained is in metres, points are in points—and \(\beta_k\) is in logit units per unit of \(X_k\). The source states “\(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\) are Gaussian independent errors” [PAGE 10]. The differential operator used in feature construction is \(D_{ij,k}=X_{i,k}-X_{j,k}\); the source says “The value is transformed into differentials following Robertson and Joyce (2018) as it is more predictive of game outcomes compared to the absolute value” [PAGE 11]. The cumulative operator is a sum over a season or over the last four games; the paper distinguishes “cumulative performance over the season” and “cumulative performance over the last four games” [PAGE 12]. Model fit is assessed with
\[
\mathrm{AIC}=-2\ell(\hat\theta)+2k,
\]
where \(\ell\) is the maximized log-likelihood in nats if natural logarithms are used and \(k\) is the number of estimated parameters; the source uses AIC for goodness of fit [PAGE 11]. Classification accuracy is
\[
A=n^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\hat p_m \ge 0.5\},
\]
dimensionless; the paper says “A team was predicted to win whenever their probability was higher than 0.5” [PAGE 11]. Assumptions: pairwise independence conditional on covariates; time-invariant strengths in the standard model; Gaussian independent errors in the team-specific expansion; fixed reference team; pre-game covariates only; draws excluded [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: non-identifiability under \(\lambda_i\mapsto\lambda_i+c\); numerical overflow for extreme \(\lambda\); separation under all-win or all-loss records; low binomial counts [PAGE 33]; collinearity and multiple testing in p-value selection [PAGE 12]; early-season zero differentials, since the Incremental strategy predicts “the first three rounds of the test data … with the model trained on the previous season since most of the differential features will amount to 0” [PAGE 13]; era nonstationarity [PAGE 7]; and Finals nonrepresentativeness, because “the worsening accuracy in the Finals Series might imply that the inclusion of these games for modelling is not optimal” [PAGE 24].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from pairwise-comparison models to sports forecasting. The paper positions BT as a model “designed to model pairwise comparisons” [PAGE 3] and extends it through R tooling and time-variant covariates, citing BradleyTerry2 and Tsokos et al. [PAGE 3]. Its scientific ancestors are earlier AFL performance-indicator studies: Robertson et al. on relative indicators and Forward 50 activity [PAGE 4], Young et al. on relative rather than absolute indicators [PAGE 4], Fahey-Gilmour et al. on pre-game machine learning and feature selection [PAGE 7], and Manderson et al. on Bayesian scoring models [PAGE 8]. The paper’s novelty is not inventing a new physiological law; it is applying an interpretable pairwise model to pre-game AFL outcomes and testing season windows and round-by-round updating.

Multi-week traps are central. First, the “era” trap: the source says the AFL has “groups of seasons or ‘eras’ … where the indicators are significantly different from each other” [PAGE 7], and that “the current game is faster, with heavier and taller players (Woods et al., 2017). Scoring has also declined in the latest seasons (Lane et al., 2020)” [PAGE 7]. A model trained across eras can average incompatible strategies. Second, the window trap: one-season, two-season, three-season, and four-season windows are tested, and the paper concludes “team strengths change gradually in the medium term, possibly in a window of three seasons” [PAGE 21]. Third, the previous-season-ladder trap: “the differentials in the previous season’s final ladder position … are included in more of the final models with a positive estimate” [PAGE 16], so last year’s rank can leak stale information into current predictions. Fourth, the cumulative-feature trap: season cumulative and last-four-game cumulative encodings are both tested, but neither is uniformly superior; the paper reports different test accuracies by season and encoding [PAGE 20]. Fifth, the fixture trap: cumulative wins can have counterintuitive signs because “a losing team has the advantage in cumulative wins if it faced weaker teams in the earlier rounds, or their opponent faced stronger teams” [PAGE 23]. Sixth, the Finals trap: Finals contain only the strongest teams, with “nine games in total” [PAGE 48], so they are a small and selected sample. Seventh, the Covid and schedule trap: 2020 had 162 games because the season was cut short, and 2023 had 216 games due to an added round [PAGE 9]; both affect cumulative features and window comparability. The retrocausal lesson is that the model’s apparent “strength” is a compressed history of outcomes, form, venue, and cumulative indicators, not a timeless team essence.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model is: the analyst believes the model believes team strength is latent; the model updates that belief from outcomes and covariates; the opponent’s belief is represented only through differential features and the pairwise contrast. Crisis habit regression appears when current-season evidence is thin. The paper’s Incremental strategy predicts the first three rounds with the previous season’s model because “most of the differential features will amount to 0” [PAGE 13]; that is a deliberate fallback to habit. The paper also finds strength is persistent over roughly three seasons [PAGE 21], which supports prior-season regression when current data are noisy. The counter-trap is overreacting to the most recent four games, ladder position, or a single significant coefficient. The source warns that early-season differentials and fixture skew can distort cumulative-win coefficients [PAGE 23], and it reports that ladder-position differential is often individually significant but “never included in the model” [PAGE 16]. A second counter-trap is treating AT HOME as a universal causal force; it is significant and positive overall [PAGE 15], but “teams can be designated as Home in games outside their home venue and state” [PAGE 10], so designation, venue, and travel are not identical. The clock is the updating schedule. Experiment 4 compares Addition, Substitution, Incremental, and Majority Voting; the paper says these are “the closest simulation of a BT model that would allow for time-varying coefficients” [PAGE 12]. The results are clock-dependent: the Incremental model reaches “70.05%” in 2015 under last-four encoding and “71.50%” in 2015 under season-cumulative encoding, but performs poorly in 2021 [PAGE 20]. Majority Voting is more stable, with “stable accuracies above 60%, reaching up to 67.96%” [PAGE 24]. Therefore the recursive policy should be: use prior-season regression only as a clock-aware prior; do not let recent-form counter-traps dominate; and prefer ensemble or round-by-round updating when the cost of a wrong update is high.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the fitted model’s match-difficulty variables are limited to “AT HOME,” “HOMEGROUND,” and “INTERSTATE” [PAGE 29]. Age and weight are discussed in the literature, where “older and heavier teams … are significantly (yet slightly) more likely to win their games” [PAGE 5], but they are not in the glossary of fitted features [PAGE 29–32]. Therefore the BT model cannot prove an age or weight effect; that claim is literature-backed, not model-backed. Biomechanical veto: the performance indicators are match actions and proxies—entries into Forward 50, metres gained, contested possessions, tackles, hitouts, rebounds—not direct kinematics. The paper itself says metres gained is only “hinting that teams where players move more are more likely to win” [PAGE 22]. The oval-ground description is “Defensive … Forward 50 … and Midfield” [PAGE 2], but the model does not measure force, fatigue, tendon load, or gait. Weather veto: no weather variable appears in the glossary across [PAGE 29], [PAGE 30], [PAGE 31], and [PAGE 32]. The travel discussion mentions time zones and sleep—“playing interstate games decreases the sleep of players” [PAGE 7]—but not weather. Thus any weather claim is outside the source and must be vetoed. Cross-layer adjudication: if a physical, biomechanical, or weather factor is not encoded, it cannot override the fitted BT result; if it is encoded only as a proxy, the result is associational and confounded. The admissible cross-layer statement is narrow: home designation and interstate travel are associated with win probability in this model [PAGE 15–16]; tactical networks were discarded because data were unavailable [PAGE 9]; and physiological mechanisms remain hypotheses for future covariates.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1, home-designation ablation. Refit the contest-specific BT model with and without the AT HOME term on identical windows. Prediction: the included model yields a positive \(\delta\), consistent with the reported overall estimate and the statement that the effect “was significant and positive when considering all available data” [PAGE 15], and it should not reduce AIC relative to the standard model in most windows, because the paper reports a “slight decrease” in AIC and improved test accuracy for most seasons [PAGE 15]. Veto condition: if removing AT HOME improves held-out accuracy or flips \(\delta\) negative across most windows, the home-advantage interpretation is vetoed.

Scenario 2, encoding swap. Hold the feature set and model class fixed, then swap last-four-game cumulative indicators for season-cumulative indicators. Prediction: no universal winner; the paper’s Table 6 and Table 7 show season-dependent reversals, with last-four and season-cumulative models each winning different test seasons [PAGE 20]. The reported maxima include 71.50% for the Incremental season-cumulative model in 2015 and 69.57% for last-four Majority Voting in 2016 [PAGE 20]. Veto condition: a claim that one encoding is uniformly superior is falsified by any season where the other encoding has higher test accuracy.

Scenario 3, round-by-round retraining. Compare full-season prediction against Addition, Substitution, Incremental, and Majority Voting. Prediction: retraining does not monotonically dominate; the paper states that models predicting the full season still perform best in some seasons [PAGE 18], while Majority Voting is stable above 60% and reaches 67.96% [PAGE 24]. The Incremental strategy is high-variance: it reaches the highest reported accuracy of 71.5% in one season but also performs poorly in another [PAGE 20]. Veto condition: if Addition or Incremental fails to beat the full-season model in every test season, the claim “round-by-round updating always improves prediction” is vetoed. These three checks are verifiable by refitting, comparing accuracy, AIC, and coefficient signs on the same page-reported windows.
