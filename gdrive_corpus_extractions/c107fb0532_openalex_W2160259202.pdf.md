<THINK_TRACE>
The monograph’s logical spine is a four-hop chain, and each hop exposes a distinct failure surface that must be audited before any practitioner relies on the downstream claims.

Hop 1 — moment truncation. Under the quadratic approximation \(u(w)=w-(\mathrm{RA}/2)w^{2}\), the expectation \(E[u]\) collapses to a function of mean and variance alone, because \(E[w^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}[w]+E[w]^{2}\) is linear in the second moment and \(E[w]\) is linear in the first; the Markowitz program [PAGE 17] then becomes the optimal response to risk-tolerance parameter \(\delta\). The equivalence is not merely formal: Kallberg and Ziemba (1983), summarized at [PAGE 20], prove that any concave \(u\) can be recovered to a cash-equivalent error of less than \(10^{-4}\) percent — and a portfolio-weight error below 1 percent — by *another* concave utility with the same Arrow–Pratt index, provided the data are monthly and RA is low. This is the license to reduce all utility functions to a single scalar.

Hop 2 — expected-utility relaxation. Replacing the single-period mean–variance objective with \(E_{\xi}[u(w_{0}\xi'x)]\) [PAGE 19] converts the problem to a statement about distribution functions, not moments. Hanoch–Levy single-crossing dominance [PAGE 26] then gives the exact regime in which the mean is the dominant sufficient statistic: if two cumulative distribution functions cross only once, the higher-mean one stochastically dominates for *every* increasing concave utility. That is the justification for Ziemba’s Point 1, "Means are by far the most important part of the distribution" [PAGE 177], and for the 20 : 2 : 1 error importance ratio [PAGE 10].

Hop 3 — scenario extension. The stochastic program substitutes \(\sum_{s}p_{s}u(w_{s})\) for \(E_{\xi}[u]\), with the tree \(S_{T}=(S_{1},\ldots,S_{T})\) [PAGE 55], which keeps concavity intact and (with a piecewise-linear shortfall cost [PAGE 77]) preserves linearity of the program. The Russell–Yasuda Kasai five-period, 256-scenario implementation [PAGE 92] is the canonical evidence: a US$42 bp (US$79 million) cumulative income gain over the mean–variance fixed-mix benchmark across fiscal 1991–92 [PAGE 84], and a documented refusal to hold any fixed-mix allocation that would have produced the 2000–03 losses.

Hop 4 — liability wedge and overbetting control. Liabilities \(L_{t}\) make the state variable surplus \(S_{t}=W_{t}-L_{t}\); the Rudolf–Ziemba continuous-time extension of Merton [PAGE 125] yields four-fund separation (market, state-variable hedge, liability hedge, riskless), with the liability hedge depending only on the funding ratio \(F\), and the market-portfolio weight ranging from 0 percent at \(F=1\) to 24.6 percent at \(F=1.5\) [PAGE 128, Table 4.12]. In the wagering dimension, the Kelly criterion \(f^{*}=p-q\) [PAGE 167] then anchors the upper bound: log is "the most risky utility function one should ever consider" and betting beyond it is stochastically dominated [PAGE 166].

Gray-zone audit. Four regimes break the chain. (i) The bond–stock yield differential model fires reliably only when both the long-bond yield and the earnings yield are non-negligible: "One had a close to 0–0 situation, so the model did not seem to apply to Japan in 1999" [PAGE 38]. At that singular point the differential is indeterminate. (ii) Exogenous-contagion crashes (Hong Kong October 1997, [PAGE 164]) fall outside the model because the S&P 500 was "not in the danger zone in October 1997, by my models" [PAGE 164] yet fell 10 percent. (iii) Stochastically different *ex post* data break the stochastic program itself: Fleten, Høyland, and Wallace (2002) show in-sample dominance far exceeding out-of-sample dominance [PAGE 68], because "the random input data are structurally different from those in sample." (iv) Kelly over-betting destroys the growth argument: at \(f=2f^{*}\) expected log growth returns to zero, and beyond it is negative — this is exactly the LTCM failure mode of \(\$1.25\) trillion positions against \(\$125\) billion borrowed [PAGE 161]. The audit therefore certifies the model only inside the open box \(\{(\text{yield gap non-degenerate}),(\text{no exogenous stampede}),(\text{scenario distribution stable}),(\delta\le 1)\}\) — everywhere else, the scenario-dependent correlation matrix, not the point forecast, must carry the risk-control load.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogical chain is Bernoulli (1738) → Kelly (1956) → Breiman (1961) → Thorp (1962, 1969) → MacLean–Ziemba (1986 on) → Ziemba–Hausch racing applications, running in parallel with Markowitz (1952) → Tobin (1958) → Sharpe–Lintner–Mossin (1964) → Merton (1969, 1973) → Rudolf–Ziemba four-fund (1998–2004), and bracketed below by Williams (1936) and Miller (1996). The Bernoulli letters establish logarithmic utility; Kelly’s information-rate theorem identifies log-optimal capital growth as the objective that maximizes asymptotic wealth; Breiman proves \(w_{KB}/w_{B}\to\infty\) for any essentially different competing strategy; Thorp converts theorem to practice in blackjack and then in Princeton Newport Partners, whose record [PAGE 149–150, Table 6.2] — 20 years without a losing year, Sharpe (monthly) near 3.0, US$1000 $\to$ US$148,200 versus US$64,500 for the S&P — is the empirical canonisation of the Kelly principle as *risk-control first*. In parallel, Markowitz’s frontier is generalized by Tobin’s separation theorem [PAGE 2]; Sharpe–Lintner–Mossin imposes CAPM factor structure; Merton extends to continuous-time intertemporal portfolio choice; Rudolf and Ziemba lift Merton one rung by replacing a single risky state with a surplus variable, producing four-fund separation [PAGE 125].

Multi-week traps lay in wait wherever a later generation forgets a singularity of a predecessor. Trap 1 — the LTCM trap: the 1997–98 carry trade + convergence trade is profitable in twenty out of twenty-one months, exactly as Kelly would predict at \(f^{*}=p-(1-p)/O\); but the magnification from leverage means that a single \(\sigma^{2}\to\) large regime (the 17 August 1998 Russia default, [PAGE 161]) pushes effective \(f\) above \(2f^{*}\), at which point expected log growth turns negative and the equity is consumed within weeks. Verbatim [PAGE 161]: "LTCM was unable to weather the storm of this enormous crisis of confidence and lost about 95 percent of its US$4 billion." Trap 2 — the 1/n trap: a 401(k) participant who allocates evenly across plan offers (Benartzi and Thaler 2001, [PAGE 124]) does *not* correct for the fact that human capital and own-company stock are 80-percent-correlated (Douglass, Wu, and Ziemba). The immediate temptation (Procter & Gamble, 94.7-percent own-company weight, Table 4.9 [PAGE 117]) looks like diversification because the plan offers a diversified menu; it is not. Trap 3 — the Boots trap: the UK pension scheme moving fully into bonds at depressed equity prices [PAGE 101] is a 2002–03 "safety" move that locks in the equity-bond correlation reversal of Points 5 [PAGE 177], and is structurally dominated by every InnoALM run in Table 4.7. Trap 4 — the log-optimal miscalibration trap: Leland’s \(B\) must replace \(\beta\) whenever the underlying is lognormal and the strategies involve options; without it, "the CAPM analysis penalizes strategies that buy options and biases them toward negative alphas" [PAGE 31], turning a genuine Kelly-optimal option-buying strategy into a reported loser.

The retrocausal thread is that the modern stochastic programming architecture is a *resurrection of the pessimistic branch* of portfolio theory. The 1950s–1980s line privileged expected return and covariance; the 2000–03 drawdown, the 1998 LTCM bailout, and 9/11 forced the re-injection of the tail-events, scenario-dependent-correlation, and over-betting vetoes that Bernoulli, Kelly, and Keynes had encoded but that the mean-variance descendants had suppressed. Ziemba’s own career trajectory — from 1974 UBC course (where Kusy and Ziemba 1986 was born) to Russell–Yasuda Kasai (1989–91, US$79 million in real money) to InnoALM (2000–03, Siemens Austria) — is exactly this resurrection in operational form.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three competing mental models fire under crisis, and their interactions determine whether a stochastic program actually gets used.

**Crisis habit regression** is the dominant 2000–03 behavioural response: *all* stress is mapped to a single lever with a fixed direction — sell equities, buy bonds. This is a learned habit from the 1973–74, 1980–82 and 2000–02 drawdowns, and it is what drove Boots Group fully into bonds [PAGE 101] and the University of Toronto pension fund to a US$484-million fiscal-year loss. The model falsifies the habit in two ways. First, at [PAGE 110] the *deterministically constrained* version (TMC: Austrian 40-percent equity cap) has the *highest* shortfall probability (16.1 percent versus 8.3 percent for the unconstrained TM tree) and the *lowest* expected terminal wealth (253.1 versus 342.8). Second, the correlation reversal documented at [PAGE 177, Point 5] — "When a large decline in the stock market occurs, the positive correlation between stocks and bonds fails and they become negatively correlated" — means bonds hedge exactly when the crisis is severe, which is when the habit most urgently recommends them; but the same negative correlation turns European bonds into a *positive* hedge inside the extreme-volatility regime of Table 4.4 [PAGE 107], not a general safe haven. The habit is thus right on average and wrong in the tail.

**Counter-trap** (the false-alarm lever) is the opposite failure mode: over-trusting the model’s mean forecast. In the InnoALM mean-sensitivity test [PAGE 111, Figure 4.3], a mean return of 12 percent for US equities yields an optimal weight of *100 percent equities*; a mean of 9 percent yields under 30 percent. The 3-percentage-point difference is inside any plausible estimation error, yet the model swings its recommendation from corner solution to corner solution. The counter-trap is therefore to treat the mean estimator as a decision rule — which is precisely what Point 1 [PAGE 177] warns against unless the mean is estimated *well*. The meta-rule the monograph actually adopts is the constrained one: optimise, yes, but wrap the optimiser in weight caps, James–Stein shrinkage, and long-horizon mean reversion — and accept that the optimum is near-flat in the neighbourhood, so the *cheapest* achievable portfolio on the near-optimal plateau dominates the unconstrained optimum.

**Clock** — the funding-ratio and spending clocks. The Rudolf–Ziemba continuous-time model [PAGE 128] makes risk-taking a function of \(F=W/L\): at \(F=0.9\) the portfolio holds 94.9 percent riskless assets and *negative* market weight, whereas at \(F=1.5\) it holds 65.6 percent riskless and 24.6 percent market. The clock is therefore not time but solvency distance; two funds with identical asset menus but \(F=1.2\) versus \(F=0.9\) should be doing opposite trades. This is corroborated on the individual side by Dybvig’s spending-protection rule [PAGE 139–141]: a "spend-never-decrease" policy forces a *higher* allocation to the protected real asset than a fixed-mix 4.5-percent real spending rule, and the composition chart in Figure 5.1 Panel B is the clock made mechanical. The recursion is that Ziemba’s Point 8 [PAGE 178] — stochastic programming will lose 5–15 percent when functional models lose 50–95 percent — is the meta-cognition that unifies the three: it is the habit that *replaces* crisis regression, the constraint that *bounds* the counter-trap, and the funding-ratio threshold that *resets* the clock each period.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The vetoes that override the model are of three kinds: physical (capital and liquidity), biomechanical (human biology and behavioural plumbing), and weather (exogenous shock calendars).

**Physical veto 1 — capital.** LTCM held US$1.25 trillion in gross positions against US$125 billion borrowed [PAGE 161], approximately 1 percent of the world derivatives book. No stochastic program, however well specified, can override the margin clerk: forced liquidation destroys the position before the scenario tree can be re-estimated. The only defence is the pre-trade constraint \(\sum_{j}|\pi_{j}|\le\kappa \cdot W\), a hard physical cap the monograph encodes as a "do not overbet" invariant and as 401(k) trading-constraint modelling [PAGE 124]. Verbatim [PAGE 171]: "Never overbet; it will eventually lead to trouble unless it is controlled somehow, which is hard to do!"

**Physical veto 2 — liquidity.** Askin Capital Management 1994 [PAGE 158]: when the handful of counterparties who could price the portfolio learned of the margin problem, bids collapsed. The bid–ask differential *widened endogenously* into the crisis, meaning the InnoALM-type optimisation (which assumes any weight vector is achievable at the quoted mid) mis-prices the terminal constraint set. Scenario generation must therefore append a "liquidity widening" state dimension; without it, the model has no admissible path back to solvency.

**Biomechanical veto — mortality and behaviour.** InnoALM’s liabilities are not scenario variables in the statistical sense; they are actuarily determined: Austrian mortality and marital tables, 60-percent widow’s pensions, retirement at 65/60 [PAGE 103]. Verbatim [PAGE 103]: "The wealth of the pension fund must grow by 7.5 percent a year to match liability commitments." Deaths, retirements, and widow percentages are exogenous — they cannot be optimised, and any model that treats them as flexible fails regulatory scrutiny. The behavioural analogue is the Yamaichi employee who was "obliged by moral suasion and peer pressure to buy stock in Yamaichi Securities, which went bankrupt in 1995" [PAGE 117]. This is a *biomechanical* constraint in the sense that it is a human social-mechanism, not a price signal: no expected-return estimate overrides the social obligation, and the model therefore cannot assume free re-allocation.

**Weather veto — exogenous shocks.** The 1998 list of "first-time" market events [PAGE 31] — 77 in one year vs a usual 4–5 — is the operational definition of weather in this domain. 9/11, Kobe 1995, the 17 August 1998 Russian default, the 27 October 1997 Hong Kong stampede, the 2002 accounting-confidence crisis, and 2003 Iraq are all deaths by calendar, not by mean. The veto is proved by the "imported crash" evidence: the S&P 500 was at 976 on Friday 24 October 1997, "way above 830," but a Hong Kong fall to 10,000 during the following Monday’s panic took the S&P futures through 7 percent to 906 and then 3 percent more to 876 [PAGE 164], exploding volatility and calling Niederhoffer’s short puts at US$16. The cash-option, option-price danger control that worked in 64 of 65 monthly plays from 1985–2003 [PAGE 165] failed exactly once — in this weather event. The stochastic-programming mitigation is to substitute *similar* past shocks (Russian default for a Brazilian crisis) and to make the correlation matrix scenario-conditional so that the same tree cannot simultaneously assume \(\rho_{\text{normal}}\) and a 40-percent implied volatility [PAGE 56]. The point of the veto is not to predict weather; it is to ensure the portfolio is still solvent when weather occurs. Verbatim [PAGE 178, Point 8]: "Those using stochastic programming models may lose 5, 10, or 15 percent, but they will not lose 50, 70, or 95 percent."

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

**Veto 3 — Kelly over-betting boundary.** *Setup.* Run the Ziemba–Hausch (1986) simulation from Table 6.3/6.4 [PAGE 170]: 700 independent wagers, all with a 14-percent edge, win probabilities \(p\in\{0.19,0.228,0.285,0.380,0.570\}\) in the proportions in Table 6.3, starting from US$1,000, repeated across 1,000 trials. *Prediction.* Full Kelly (δ = 1): final-wealth minimum \(\approx\) US$18, probability of finishing *below* the initial US$1,000 \(\approx\) 13 percent, probability of 100× gain \(\approx\) 16.6 percent, mean US$48,135. Half Kelly (δ = 0.5): minimum \(\approx\) US$145, probability of finishing below start \(\approx\) 4.6 percent, probability of 100× gain \(\approx\) 0.1 percent, mean US$13,069. Verbatim [PAGE 170]: "a Kelly bettor can make 700 independent wagers … and still lose more than 98 percent of his or her wealth." *Veto condition.* If any Monte Carlo trial with δ > 1 produces a higher expected log growth rate than δ = 1, the theoretical foundation of the entire over-betting prohibition collapses; in that case the LTCM narrative must be re-explained by exogenous capital constraints alone, not by Kelly.
