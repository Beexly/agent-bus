The event occurrence is the end of a sponsorship; censored observations are sponsorships still ongoing at the observation date. The first multi-hop inference is that estimator choice is not cosmetic: the abstract states that omitting censored cases "would result in the loss of 10 of the 27 TOP historical sponsorships" [PAGE 1]. With deletion, the complete-case mean is reported as "2.18 intervals (or 8.72 years, given the four-year duration of the Olympic quadrennial)" [PAGE 1]. With censored durations truncated at the present, the mean is "3.19 (12.76 years)" [PAGE 1]. With a life table, the median is "2.43 time periods, or 9.72 years" [PAGE 1]. A gray-zone audit must separate mean from median: the source's "range of lifetimes ... from 2.18 to 3.19 time periods" [PAGE 1] is an estimator range, not a confidence interval. Numerically, 3.19 minus 2.18 is 1.01 intervals, or about 4.04 years, so "one time interval" [PAGE 1] is a rounded statement. The life-table median 2.43 is a survival-threshold estimate, not a third mean.

The formal path is: let $T_i$ be the discrete duration in quadrennia for sponsorship $i$, with event indicator and censoring indicator. The survivor function is "the probability that individual i will survive past time period j" [PAGE 1], written $S_j=\Pr\{T_i>j\}$. The discrete hazard is the conditional end rate, defined by the source as "the rate in which the duration or event ends ... given that the target event or the duration has not ended prior to that particular time interval" [PAGE 1], written $h_j=\Pr\{T_i=j\mid T_i\ge j\}$. Under the standard discrete-time identity, $S_j=\prod_{k\le j}(1-h_k)$. The median solves $S_m=0.5$, with interpolation if the life table crosses 0.5 between integer intervals. The source also reports a "cumulative (overall) hazard function of 0.1977" [PAGE 1], interpreted as a "probability of 19.77% that a TOP sponsorship will end during each quadrennial" [PAGE 1]. That wording mixes average conditional hazard with cumulative hazard. If 0.1977 were a constant per-quadrennium discrete hazard, then $S_j=(0.8023)^j$, and the median would be $\ln(0.5)/\ln(0.8023)\approx 3.15$ quadrennia, or about 12.6 years. The reported life-table median of 2.43 quadrennia, or 9.72 years, therefore implies either a nonconstant hazard, interpolation, censoring effects, or that 0.1977 is an average rather than a constant hazard. This is a key gray-zone audit point.

A second audit point is the comparison with Payne's "renewal rate of greater than 90% for TOP sponsorships" [PAGE 1]. Renewal rate is the "percentage of sponsorships renewed in a particular time period" [PAGE 1]; the hazard is a conditional termination probability. They are not complements unless the interval, conditioning set, and event definition match. A 19.77% conditional end probability per quadrennium is compatible with varied renewal patterns and does not directly falsify a greater-than-90% renewal statement. The source calls the difference "significantly different" [PAGE 1], but the page provides no standard error, confidence interval, or test statistic; with 27 sponsorships and 10 censored cases, that inferential claim is not auditable from the page alone.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let $T_i$ be the discrete duration of TOP sponsorship $i$, measured in Olympic quadrennia, where $1$ quadrennium $=4$ years $=1.26144\times 10^{8}$ s. Let $C_i$ be the censoring time and let $T_i^{\mathrm{obs}}=\min(T_i,C_i)$. Let $\delta_i=1$ if the sponsorship end is observed and $\delta_i=0$ if censored. The survivor function is
\[
S_j=\Pr\{T_i>j\},
\]
which the source quotes as the "probability that individual i will survive past time period j" [PAGE 1]. The discrete-time hazard is
\[
h_j=\Pr\{T_i=j\mid T_i\ge j\},
\]
which the source describes as "the rate in which the duration or event ends ... given that the target event or the duration has not ended prior to that particular time interval" [PAGE 1]. Under independent censoring and the discrete-time life-table construction,
\[
S_j=\prod_{k=1}^{j}\left(1-h_k\right),\qquad
S_0=1.
\]
The median lifetime is
\[
m=\inf\{j:S_j\le 0.5\},
\]
with interpolation when a life table crosses $0.5$ between integer intervals. The source defines the median as "that value of T for which the value of the estimated survivor function is .5" [PAGE 1] and reports "a median lifetime of 2.43 time periods, or 9.72 years" [PAGE 1]. In continuous time, if $\lambda(t)$ is the hazard rate, then
\[
S(t)=\exp\left(-\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du\right),\qquad
\int_0^m\lambda(u)\,du=\ln 2.
\]
For a constant discrete hazard $h$, the implied median is
\[
m=\frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln(1-h)}.
\]
Using the source's "cumulative (overall) hazard function of 0.1977" [PAGE 1] as a constant $h=0.1977$ gives $m=\ln(0.5)/\ln(0.8023)\approx 3.15$ quadrennia, or about $12.6$ years. The reported life-table median of $2.43$ quadrennia therefore does not equal the constant-hazard median; the 0.1977 value cannot simultaneously be a constant per-quadrennium hazard and the sole generator of the reported median without additional time variation or interpolation.

The complete-case mean is
\[
\bar T_{\mathrm{CC}}=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{obs}}}\sum_{i:\delta_i=1}T_i,
\]
and the source reports $\bar T_{\mathrm{CC}}=2.18$ intervals $=8.72$ years [PAGE 1]. The truncation-at-present mean is
\[
\bar T_{\mathrm{trunc}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(T_i,C_i),
\]
and the source reports $3.19$ intervals $=12.76$ years [PAGE 1]. A restricted mean from a survivor curve would be
\[
E[T\wedge \tau]=\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}S_j,
\]
for integer-valued $T$ and restriction time $\tau$. The source's own censoring count is decisive: "the loss of 10 of the 27 TOP historical sponsorships" [PAGE 1]. Deletion changes the denominator from 27 to 17 and conditions on survival to the observation date; it is not a random sample of durations.

Revenue units follow from the source's arithmetic. In 2009-2012, "the 11 TOP sponsors yielded a total of $957 million in revenue for the IOC, an average of $87 million per sponsor" [PAGE 1]. If $N_s$ sponsors each contribute $r=87$ million USD per quadrennium for $\Delta m$ quadrennia, then
\[
R=N_s\,r\,\Delta m
\]
million USD under constant-average and full-exposure assumptions. For $N_s=5$ and $\Delta m=1$, $R=5\times 87\times 1=435$ million USD, matching the source's "$435 million" [PAGE 1].

Units and assumptions: $S_j$ and $h_j$ are dimensionless probabilities per discrete interval; $h_j$ is a conditional probability per quadrennium, while a continuous hazard $\lambda(t)$ has units of inverse time, such as year$^{-1}$ or quadrennium$^{-1}$. Revenue is in USD millions. The life-table and Kaplan-Meier interpretations require non-informative censoring, a common time origin, consistent interval width, and exchangeable risk sets; the complete-case mean additionally requires that censoring be ignorable, which is stronger and usually false for ongoing sponsorships. The page reports no risk table, no confidence intervals, no standard errors, and no raw durations.

Breakdown regimes and singularities: if $h_j=1$ for some $j$, then $S_j=0$ and all later risk sets collapse; if $h_j=0$ for all $j$, then $S_j=1$ forever and the median is undefined or not reached; if the at-risk count $n_j=0$, then $h_j=d_j/n_j$ is undefined; if the survivor function never crosses $0.5$ during the observed window, the median is not identified; if censoring is heavy before the median crossing, the median is non-identifiable without an extrapolation model; if censored durations are truncated and then treated as events, the estimator is a mixture of event and administrative censoring, not a pure lifetime mean; if $C_i$ is systematically related to sponsor type or contract quality, independent censoring fails and the Kaplan-Meier and life-table estimates are biased; if the interval is four years, annualized hazards require an explicit conversion and cannot be compared directly with annual renewal percentages. The source's "cumulative (overall) hazard function of 0.1977" [PAGE 1] also breaks if "cumulative" is read as $H(t)=-\ln S(t)$: a cumulative hazard of $0.1977$ over the whole study implies $S=\exp(-0.1977)\approx 0.821$, not a 19.77% per-quadrennium conditional end probability. The exact quoted interpretation—"probability of 19.77% that a TOP sponsorship will end during each quadrennial" [PAGE 1]—should therefore be treated as an average discrete hazard, not a cumulative hazard.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The method genealogy runs from renewal-rate accounting to event history analysis. The source says the renewal rate is "the percentage of sponsorships renewed in a particular time period" [PAGE 1] and that using a measure of central tendency is "inappropriate, given the presence of censored observations within the dataset (i.e., sponsorships that are currently ongoing)" [PAGE 1]. That is the retrocausal hinge: an older summary habit—report a renewal percentage—is re-read through censoring, which changes what the denominator and event mean. The paper explicitly invokes Kaplan-Meier (1958) survivor estimation, Singer and Willett (2003) for the survivor and median definitions, Box-Steffensmeier and Jones (1997) for life-table analysis and hazard rates, and Payne (2012) for the greater-than-90% renewal statement [PAGE 1]. The lineage is not a straight replacement of renewal rates by hazards; it is a change in the object of inference from a period renewal fraction to a conditional duration process.

The multi-week traps are therefore methodological, not merely numerical. Trap one is equating the source's "19.77% that a TOP sponsorship will end during each quadrennial" [PAGE 1] with "less than 90% renewal" or with "10.23% renewal." The two statements live on different conditioning sets: renewal is a period percentage, while the hazard is conditional on having survived to the start of that interval. Trap two is treating the three summaries—2.18, 3.19, and 2.43 intervals—as if they were lower bound, upper bound, and point estimate of one parameter. They are complete-case mean, censored-truncation mean, and life-table median. Trap three is deleting 10 of 27 sponsorships and then reporting the 2.18 mean as if it were representative; the source itself says "it is not wise to omit observations from a sample" [PAGE 1]. Trap four is truncating censored sponsorships at the present and reading the 3.19 mean as the true mean lifetime; truncation assigns an artificial end date. Trap five is ignoring the quadrennial clock: all conversions depend on "the four-year duration of the Olympic quadrennial" [PAGE 1], and a one-interval change is roughly four years, not one year. Trap six is revenue extrapolation: the "$87 million per sponsor" [PAGE 1] average and the "$435 million" [PAGE 1] five-sponsor figure are arithmetic exposures, not causal forecasts, and they assume full-quadrennium participation and constant average value. The historical lineage thus warns that the old renewal number and the new hazard number can both be correct while measuring different things.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the fallback to the familiar renewal rate when the data are sparse and censored. An IOC revenue forecaster, a sponsor, and a researcher can all prefer the simple statement "greater than 90% renewal" [PAGE 1] because it sounds like a stable planning parameter; the source's hazard estimate of "19.77% that a TOP sponsorship will end during each quadrennial" [PAGE 1] then appears threatening. The habit regression is to dismiss the hazard as anomalous rather than to ask whether the renewal percentage and the hazard answer different questions. The counter-trap is the opposite overcorrection: to treat the life-table median of "2.43 time periods, or 9.72 years" [PAGE 1] as a precise replacement for all renewal judgments, disregarding the 27-sponsorship sample, the 10 censored cases, the missing risk table, and the absence of confidence intervals. A second counter-trap is to read the 3.19-interval truncation mean as a causal mean lifetime and then price five sponsors at "$435 million" [PAGE 1] without asking whether those sponsors would actually remain for the full interval.

The clock is the quadrennial interval itself. The source converts intervals to years at four years per interval: 2.18 intervals to 8.72 years, 3.19 to 12.76 years, and 2.43 to 9.72 years [PAGE 1]. Recursive theory-of-mind must keep three clocks separate: the contract clock, the Olympic quadrennial clock, and the censoring clock at the present day. A sponsor deciding renewal is not only responding to elapsed time; the IOC is forecasting revenue; the researcher is modeling duration; and each party's belief about the others affects what gets observed. If the IOC treats ongoing sponsorships as censored, it preserves the information that those sponsors survived to the present; if it truncates them as if ended, it converts a survival belief into an event belief. If a researcher treats the average hazard as a fixed probability, the researcher implicitly assumes a memoryless sponsor process, which is a strong belief about sponsor behavior that the page does not test. The defensible recursive stance is: use the survivor and hazard functions to represent conditional survival, use the median only as a threshold crossing, use the mean only with an explicit censoring rule, and state that the page cannot identify sponsor intentions. The source's contrast between the hazard estimate and Payne's renewal statement is best read as a clash of clocks and conditioning sets, not as proof that one actor is irrational.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source page contains no physical-load, biomechanical, physiological, or meteorological variables. Its data objects are sponsorship duration, event end, censoring, quadrennial interval, and revenue. Therefore any claim that biomechanics or weather causally changes TOP sponsorship hazard is non-identifiable from this page. Formally, the page estimates a marginal hazard $h_j=\Pr\{T_i=j\mid T_i\ge j\}$ or a covariate hazard $h_j(x)=\Pr\{T_i=j\mid T_i\ge j,X_i=x\}$ only if $X_i$ is measured. No $X_{\mathrm{phys}}$, $X_{\mathrm{bio}}$, or $X_{\mathrm{weather}}$ vector is reported; the only observed dimensions are time and censoring, plus the revenue arithmetic. A physical-layer veto is thus proof by absence: a model cannot attribute sponsorship termination to athlete load, injury, or biomechanical fatigue when no such covariate enters the likelihood and no such mechanism is described. A weather veto is similarly absent for ordinary meteorological variation, but weather or schedule shocks could enter indirectly through the Olympic clock if a Games postponement or cancellation changed contract activation or the censoring date. That would be a time-clock and contract-event confound, not a biomechanical effect.

The veto adjudication is therefore: (1) physical and biomechanical explanations are vetoed for this source because the page provides no measurement, no exposure model, and no physiological mechanism; (2) direct weather explanations are vetoed for the same reason; (3) an indirect schedule or postponement explanation is not tested but is logically possible only as a change in the event or censoring clock, and it would need contract-level dates and Games timing to identify. The cross-layer proof is simple: with $T_i$ and $\delta_i$ alone, all layer-specific effects are collapsed into the marginal hazard and the censoring distribution. If a layer variable were correlated with both censoring and sponsorship end, the independent-censoring assumption behind the life table would fail; the page reports no such covariate adjustment. Thus the correct cross-layer conclusion is not that weather or biomechanics are unimportant in general, but that this one-page analysis cannot adjudicate them. Any stronger claim would require a covariate hazard model, explicit event definitions, and contract timing data that are not present in [PAGE 1].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto 1: Complete-case deletion audit. The source says deletion "would result in the loss of 10 of the 27 TOP historical sponsorships" [PAGE 1] and reports a complete-case mean of "2.18 intervals" [PAGE 1]. Verifiable counterfactual: reconstruct the 27 durations and censoring indicators; compute the elapsed duration of each of the 10 censored sponsorships at the observation date; compare their mean elapsed duration with 2.18 intervals. Prediction: if the censored cases are long-surviving sponsorships with mean elapsed duration above 2.18 intervals, the complete-case mean is downwardly biased relative to the truncated mean, consistent with the source's "3.19" [PAGE 1]. Veto: any claim that 2.18 intervals is an unbiased mean lifetime for all 27 sponsorships is rejected unless the 10 censored cases are missing completely at random and their inclusion would not change the mean. The source provides no such missingness proof.

Veto 2: Constant-hazard versus life-table median audit. The source reports a "cumulative (overall) hazard function of 0.1977" [PAGE 1] and a life-table median of "2.43 time periods" [PAGE 1]. Verifiable counterfactual: treat $h=0.1977$ as a constant discrete hazard and compute $S_j=(1-h)^j$. Prediction: the implied median is $m=\ln(0.5)/\ln(0.8023)\approx 3.15$ quadrennia, or about 12.6 years, not 2.43 quadrennia or 9.72 years. Therefore the reported 0.1977 cannot be a constant per-quadrennium hazard that alone generates the reported median; there must be time variation, interpolation, censoring effects, or a different meaning of "overall." Veto: any statement that equates 19.77% with a fixed annual or quadrennial termination probability and simultaneously accepts the 2.43 median is internally inconsistent without an explicit nonconstant hazard table. A further verifiable prediction: a proper life-table median of 2.43 requires the survivor function to cross 0.5 between analysis times 2 and 3, or at an interpolated point there; a printed risk table should show that crossing. The page does not print the risk table.

Veto 3: Revenue exposure audit. The source says 11 TOP sponsors yielded "a total of $957 million" and "an average of $87 million per sponsor" [PAGE 1], and that one interval for five sponsors "would equate to $435 million" [PAGE 1]. Verifiable counterfactual: obtain sponsor-level contract start and end dates and sponsor-level revenue for 2009-2012; compute exposure-weighted sponsor-quadrennia rather than assuming every selected sponsor remains for the full interval. Prediction: if any of the five sponsors exits before the full quadrennium or has below-average revenue, the realized five-sponsor revenue over one interval will be below $435 million; if all five remain and each equals the $87 million average, it will equal $435 million. Veto: any claim that one interval of duration "equates" to $435 million as a forecast is rejected unless full-exposure and constant-average assumptions are explicitly tested. The arithmetic is valid; the causal forecast is not identified by the page. A second verifiable prediction is that the $957 million total divided by 11 sponsors should equal $87 million per sponsor to the reported precision; any mismatch in a reconstructed table vetoes the average as stated.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
