The student models the director’s likely judgment (“he will tell me not to”), and the director models the student’s compulsive state; permission (“Go ahead and do it”) [PAGE 1] is a second-order theory-of-mind intervention that collapses the recursive loop: the student no longer needs to desire being stopped because stopping is no longer the frame. When stress returns, however, the student may regress to the original crisis habit if the counter-trap was not encoded as a new, stable schema.

Counter-trap vs clock: The “Go ahead and do it” injunction is a counter-trap because it accepts the unacceptable behavior as a way to remove the prohibition’s reactance. Its risk is the enabling trap: if the subject actually performs the deed, the intervention becomes catastrophic rather than curative. James reports instant quenching [PAGE 1], offering no longitudinal follow-up. The “clock” is the temporal structure of the urges: daily window passage, repeated tooth pressing, recurrent stink sniffing, and the jingle cycling “over and over … this very day” [PAGE 1]. These are refractory-period phenomena; the counter-trap must be delivered within the window of the urge’s buildup. If the director’s permission arrives after the crisis peak, or if the student encounters the cue outside the permission’s effective window, habit regression dominates. The recursive model therefore has three phases: (1) cue exposure triggers second-order modeling (I want to be stopped); (2) counter-trap collapses the model by redefining the act as permitted; (3) if the clock of the cue recurs before the new schema consolidates, the fixed idea reasserts itself as an attractor.

The “mawkish silliness” of the jingle adds a meta-cognitive strange loop: the subject judges the content as silly (negative meta-evaluation) yet the content repeats (positive behavioral persistence). This is a recursive theory-of-mind trap in which the self-model (“I loathe this”) fails to govern the object-level process (the jingle recurs), precisely because the object-level process is insulated by its fixed-idea status from the antecedent-affect requirement Bain posits [PAGE 1].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The defenestration temptation is biomechanically possible but carries a high injury/death cost; the text does not report the act being carried out, so the physical veto is behavioral inhibition rather than structural barrier. There is no mention of window height, ledge width, or locking hardware on [PAGE 1]; thus no physical veto can be confirmed from the source.

Biomechanical layer: The repeated behaviors James cites—pressing a sore tooth, sniffing a stink, subvocally reciting a jingle—impose negligible biomechanical loads (tooth contact in the millinewton-to-newton range; normal tidal ventilation; subvocal articulation with minimal phonatory effort). Because the force, work, and metabolic cost are low, there is no biomechanical veto that would naturally extinguish these behaviors through fatigue or tissue damage over the short term. They can be repeated indefinitely, which is exactly why the fixed idea persists without a hedonic antecedent [PAGE 1].

Weather layer: The source text [PAGE 1] contains no meteorological variable; weather is not part of James’s analysis. A weather veto is therefore null (N/A) for this page: there is no operator linking temperature, precipitation, or barometric pressure to the temptation to pass the window or to repeat the jingle. If one were to externalize the model, inclement weather could reduce the frequency of window-passing (a cue-exposure veto), but it would not veto tooth pressing, stink sniffing, or internal jingle repetition. No such externalization appears in the source.

Adjudication: For the behaviors actually described as recurring on [PAGE 1], neither biomechanical nor weather vetoes are operative. The only demonstrated veto is cognitive-reframing: the director’s paradoxical permission [PAGE 1], which instantaneously quenched the desire. Physical veto applies only if the defenestration idea is acted upon, at which point injury or death would terminate the behavior ex post—but the text concerns the pre-act temptation, not the act itself. Hence the cross-layer veto proof is: fixed-idea repetitions with low biomechanical cost are immune to somatic fatigue vetoes; they are vetoed only by higher-order cognitive or attentional interventions, and those interventions are temporally local (cued by the clock of the urge) and vulnerable to habituation across weeks.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a falsifiable prediction.

Veto Scenario 1 — Prohibition reversal. Counterfactual: Instead of permission, the Catholic director issues a stern prohibition, framing the act as painful (sin, bodily harm, divine sanction), thereby supplying a Bainian antecedent painful feeling. Veto condition: the painful sanction is the sole antecedent affect (V_ant < 0) while idea salience I remains high. Prediction: Under Bainian logic, the painful antecedent should either stimulate the will toward avoidance or inhibit the deed. Empirically, reactance theory predicts the opposite: urge intensity (visual-analog scale 0–100) remains high (>60) or increases relative to baseline, because the prohibition re-establishes the forbidden-fruit frame that permission dissolved. Verification: single-case ABAB reversal over 14 days (permission vs prohibition days), measuring daily urge ratings and window-passing rumination counts. Falsification: if urge drops below 20 under prohibition and stays there, the paradoxical-permission account is refuted.

Veto Scenario 2 — Cue-removal (environmental) veto. Counterfactual: The student’s route is changed or the window is covered/occluded so the visual cue that triggers the defenestration idea is absent. Veto condition: perceptual input I_cue = 0, so idea salience I cannot be evoked from that source; tooth, stink, and jingle remain unaffected because they have independent cues. Prediction: Daily defenestration temptations drop to zero during the occlusion phase; total fixed-idea episode count falls by the proportion attributable to the window cue; no cross-cue generalization to tooth pressing or jingle recurrence is expected within one week. Verification: counterbalanced 1-week occluded vs unblocked condition, with event logging. Falsification: if defenestration urges persist unchanged despite cue removal, the idea is not cue-dependent and the fixed idea is endogenous rather than stimulus-driven.

Veto Scenario 3 — Cognitive-load (working-memory) veto. Counterfactual: During exposure to the jingle or sore-tooth cue, the subject performs a high-load concurrent task occupying the phonological loop and visuospatial sketchpad (e.g., auditory 3-back plus spatial tracking), preventing maintenance of the fixed idea above threshold τ. Veto condition: working-memory load forces I(t) < τ during the exposure window. Prediction: Intrusion frequency (jingle repetitions per hour, tooth-presses per hour) falls significantly during load relative to a low-load control; a rebound spike occurs immediately after load removal if the fixed-idea attractor remains intact, indicating suppression rather than extinction. Verification: within-subject crossover with counterbalanced order; 48-hour monitoring per condition. Falsification: if intrusions do not rebound after load removal, the load produced extinction rather than temporary suppression, which would challenge the fixed-idea attractor model on [PAGE 1].
