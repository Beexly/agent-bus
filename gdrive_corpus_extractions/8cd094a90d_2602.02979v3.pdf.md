<THINK_TRACE>
The source defines a two-policy cooperative reinforcement-learning loop. Let the Coach policy be \(\pi^C_{\theta_t}\) and the Player policy be \(\pi^P_{\phi_t}\). At round \(t\), the Coach samples \(m\) task texts \(x_i \sim \pi^C_{\theta_t}(\cdot)\). For each task, the Player samples \(n\) independent answers \(y_{i,j}\sim \pi^P_{\phi_t}(\cdot\mid x_i)\). The pseudo-label is the majority-voted answer \(y_i^\star\), and the Player reward is the binary indicator \(r_{i,j}=\mathbf{1}[y_{i,j}=y_i^\star]\). The instruction-level Player reward is the empirical agreement rate \(R_i^{\text{Player}}=(1/n)\sum_{j=1}^n r_{i,j}=\hat p_i\). The paper then filters tasks to the “pedagogically optimal zone” \(0.2\le \hat p_i\le0.8\), citing this as the mechanism that keeps tasks “challenging enough to promote skill development yet solvable enough to avoid frustration” [PAGE 4]. The Player is updated with GRPO using a group-normalized advantage \(A_{i,j}=(r_{i,j}-\bar r_i)/\sigma_i\) in Eq. (9) [PAGE 5], though Algorithm 1 adds an \(\epsilon\) in the denominator [PAGE 12]. After the Player update, the paper computes the validation-accuracy gap \(\Delta_t=\operatorname{Acc}(\pi^P_{\phi_{t+1}};D_{\text{val}})-\operatorname{Acc}(\pi^P_{\phi_t};D_{\text{val}})\). The Coach receives the multiplicative reward \(R_i^{\text{Coach}}=R_i^{\text{Player}}\cdot\Delta_t\) and is updated by REINFORCE with \(\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=(1/m)\sum_i R_i^{\text{Coach}}\nabla_\theta\log\pi^C_\theta(x_i)\), as stated in Eq. (7) [PAGE 5].

A multi-hop derivation clarifies the intended credit assignment. The Player’s reward is purely self-consistency-based: correct means “agrees with the majority of its own rollouts,” not “matches a ground-truth label.” The paper explicitly notes that in the absence of labels, verification can be done “using self-consistency,” where “the majority-voted answer from a set of candidate outputs is treated as the correct answer” [PAGE 3]. Thus the Player gradient rewards consensus, not truth. The difficulty filter then uses the same consensus statistic: tasks with \(\hat p_i\) too high are likely trivial and produce near-zero GRPO advantage because all rewards are equal; tasks with \(\hat p_i\) too low are likely unlearnable and similarly produce near-zero advantage. The Coach reward multiplies local consensus by global validation progress. In expectation, this encourages the Coach to sample tasks whose local consensus is high and whose epoch-level validation gain is positive. However, \(\Delta_t\) is a single scalar shared by all \(m\) tasks in the round; it cannot identify which task caused the validation change. Therefore the multiplicative reward is a noisy, round-level credit assignment device rather than a per-task causal estimator. The “cooperative” interpretation holds only under the assumptions that (i) majority vote is a useful pseudo-verifier, (ii) \(\Delta_t\) is dominated by learning progress rather than validation noise, and (iii) the filter keeps the batch nonempty and within the learnable frontier.

The gray-zone audit must flag several source-internal tensions. First, “data-free” is explicitly narrowed: “Throughout this paper, ‘data-free’ refers only to the co-evolution stage after Coach–Player collaboration begins, and does not count any one-time model initialization performed beforehand” [PAGE 2]. The Coach is warmed up with “4K PRIME Eurus-2-RL-Data,” and the paper states, “Our setting is data-free Player training with an optionally warmed-up Coach” [PAGE 5]. So the headline claim “without relying on any external training data” [PAGE 1] is true for the Player co-evolution stage, not for the entire system. Second, AMC is used as the fixed held-out validation set for \(\Delta_t\): “We use AMC as the fixed held-out validation \(D_{val}\) during training” [PAGE 6]. The Table 1 caption further says OOD average excludes AMC “because RENT was trained on AMC and CPM¨obius validation also used AMC” [PAGE 6]. This is a legitimate evaluation choice, but it means the Coach’s reward is driven by labeled AMC performance even though no external training data is fed to the Player. Third, the equal-step comparison is not uniformly favorable: at the 60th step, the paper reports “our method slightly underperforms R-Zero on the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model but achieves stronger performance on OctoThinker-3B-Hybrid-Zero and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct” [PAGE 13]. Thus the strongest claim is model-dependent advantage, not universal dominance. Fourth, the pseudo-label can be systematically wrong if the Player’s majority is confidently incorrect; the filter would then accept tasks by consensus rather than by mathematical validity. Fifth, the GRPO denominator can be zero when all rollouts receive identical reward, making Eq. (9) undefined unless the algorithm’s \(\epsilon\) is used [PAGE 12]. These are not fatal, but they define the exact conditions under which the reported gains should be interpreted.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are all defined over finite batches of natural-language tasks and rollouts. Let \(t\in\{1,\dots,T\}\) index rounds, \(i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\) index Coach-proposed tasks, and \(j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\) index Player rollouts. In experiments \(m=16\) and \(n=16\): “We set the batch size as 16 and the number of rollout samples for each prompt as 16” [PAGE 6]. The Coach task operator is
\[
x_i \sim \pi^C_{\theta_t}(\cdot),\qquad i=1,\dots,m .
\]
The Player rollout operator is
\[
y_{i,j}\sim \pi^P_{\phi_t}(\cdot\mid x_i),\qquad j=1,\dots,n .
\]
The pseudo-label operator is the majority vote
\[
y_i^\star=\operatorname{MajorityVote}\bigl(\{y_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^n\bigr).
\]
The Player reward and instruction-level reward are
\[
r_{i,j}=\mathbf{1}[y_{i,j}=y_i^\star],\qquad
R_i^{\text{Player}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n r_{i,j}=\hat p_i .
\]
All \(r_{i,j}\) are dimensionless binary indicators; \(\hat p_i\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless empirical agreement fraction. The difficulty filter retains only tasks satisfying
\[
0.2\le \hat p_i\le0.8,
\]
which the paper calls “the pedagogically optimal zone” [PAGE 4]. The GRPO advantage in Eq. (9) is
\[
A_{i,j}=\frac{r_{i,j}-\operatorname{mean}(\{r_{i,1},\dots,r_{i,n}\})}{\operatorname{std}(\{r_{i,1},\dots,r_{i,n}\})},
\]
while Algorithm 1 writes \(A_{i,j}\leftarrow (r_{i,j}-\bar r_i)/(\sigma_i+\epsilon)\), adding \(\epsilon\) to avoid division by zero [PAGE 12]. The GRPO objective in Eq. (2) is
\[
J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{q,\{o_i\}}\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\left(
r_i(\theta)A_i,
\operatorname{clip}(r_i(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_i
\right)
\right]
-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}),
\]
with importance ratio \(r_i(\theta)=\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i\mid q)\) [PAGE 3]. Here the KL term is measured in nats; log-probabilities are in nats; \(\beta\) and \(\epsilon\) are dimensionless hyperparameters.

The environment-feedback operator is
\[
\Delta_t=
\operatorname{Acc}\bigl(\pi^P_{\phi_{t+1}};D_{\text{val}}\bigr)
-
\operatorname{Acc}\bigl(\pi^P_{\phi_t};D_{\text{val}}\bigr),
\]
which is a dimensionless accuracy difference and may be reported in percentage points after multiplication by 100. The Coach reward is
\[
R_i^{\text{Coach}}=R_i^{\text{Player}}\cdot\Delta_t,
\]
and the Coach REINFORCE gradient is
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^m R_i^{\text{Coach}}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi^C_\theta(x_i).
\]
This is the policy-gradient estimator for \(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\pi^C_\theta}[R^{\text{Coach}}(x)]\), with log-policy gradients in inverse-nat units per parameter. The source’s RLVR reward template is \(r(y)=\operatorname{verify}(y)\) [PAGE 3]; in CPMöbius the verifier is not a ground-truth checker but the majority-vote self-consistency rule. The paper says verification “can be performed in an unsupervised manner using self-consistency” [PAGE 3].

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. If \(\sigma_i=0\), Eq. (9) divides by zero; Algorithm 1’s \(\epsilon\) restores a defined value, but the numerator is also zero, so the advantage collapses to zero. This occurs when all \(n\) rollouts agree or all disagree, i.e. when \(\hat p_i\in\{0,1\}\). The difficulty filter is designed to remove such cases, but if the filter is disabled, the batch can become dominated by zero-advantage tasks. If the filter accepts no task, the resampling loop in Algorithm 1 does not produce \(m\) accepted tasks and the round is undefined [PAGE 12]. If majority voting ties, the pseudo-label is not unique; the paper does not specify a tie-breaking rule, so \(y_i^\star\) can be arbitrary. If \(n=1\), majority voting is trivial and the GRPO standard deviation is zero; the paper avoids this by using \(n=16\) [PAGE 6]. If \(D_{\text{val}}\) is empty, non-representative, or leaked into training, \(\Delta_t\) is undefined or biased. If \(\Delta_t=0\), all Coach rewards are zero and no Coach learning signal exists. If \(\Delta_t<0\) persistently, REINFORCE suppresses the sampled tasks even when the negative change may be evaluation noise. If the KL coefficient \(\beta\) is too small, the Player can drift outside the trust region; if too large, learning is suppressed. These are the formal boundaries of the reported operator system.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is visible in the paper’s own framing. The root ancestor is game-playing self-play: the paper cites “Mastering chess and shogi by self-play with a general reinforcement learning algorithm” [PAGE 1] as inspiration for self-evolving LLM reasoning. The next ancestor is RLVR: the paper defines “Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards” as training with “binary feedback derived from programmatic verifiers” [PAGE 2] and later states \(r(y)=\operatorname{verify}(y)\) [PAGE 3]. The third ancestor is GRPO: the paper reviews the critic-free group-relative advantage and clipping objective in Eqs. (1) and (2) [PAGE 3]. The fourth ancestor is self-consistency: majority voting over \(n\) answers is used as a pseudo-verifier because labels are absent [PAGE 3]. The fifth ancestor is curriculum learning and multi-agent collaboration: the Coach is a “curriculum designer” and the Player is a “reasoning solver” [PAGE 3], and the paper cites multi-agent collaboration work [PAGE 2]. R-Zero is the immediate adversarial cousin: it “initializes two roles of the same model that interact adversarially, with the challenger generating tasks and the solver attempting to solve them” [PAGE 6]. RENT is the entropy-minimization cousin: it treats “the model’s own confidence in its generated answers” as a reward signal [PAGE 6]. CPMöbius retrocausally reinterprets adversarial self-play’s instability as a reward-design failure and replaces “stump the other” with “maximize the Player’s progress” via \(R_i^{\text{Coach}}=R_i^{\text{Player}}\cdot\Delta_t\) [PAGE 5].

The multi-week traps are the long-horizon failure modes that appear after repeated rounds. Trap one is the “data-free” semantic drift: the paper’s own definition restricts data-free to the co-evolution stage and excludes one-time initialization [PAGE 2], and the Coach is warmed up on 4K PRIME data [PAGE 5]. Any external reproduction must therefore report the warm-up budget or the claim is not comparable. Trap two is validation coupling: AMC is the fixed \(D_{\text{val}}\) [PAGE 6], and the Table 1 caption says OOD average excludes AMC because “RENT was trained on AMC and CPM¨obius validation also used AMC” [PAGE 6]. This is a transparent control, but it means the Coach’s progress signal is not label-free; it is an AMC-labeled signal. Trap three is compute-matched comparison: at equal 60-step checkpoints, CPMöbius “slightly underperforms R-Zero on the Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B model” but is stronger on two other models [PAGE 13]. Thus the advantage is not a universal compute-for-compute dominance. Trap four is pseudo-label drift over long training: the Player is rewarded for agreeing with its own majority, so a stable but wrong consensus can be reinforced. Trap five is filter saturation: as the Player improves, the \([0.2,0.8]\) zone may require ever more resampling, and if the Coach cannot generate tasks in the zone, the round can stall. Trap six is length and entropy artifact: the paper reports that “the output length of the Coach model tends to increase, while that of the Player model tends to decrease” [PAGE 18]. This is presented as curriculum learning and concise reasoning, but it is also a potential confound because longer Coach problems and shorter Player answers change the effective token distribution. Trap seven is validation-environment sensitivity: the paper tests Minerva and OlympiadBench as replacements for AMC and reports continued gains [PAGE 14], but the magnitude varies, and one text claim says the 7B model improved AIME 2024 “from 9.0 to 21.2” [PAGE 14] while Table 6 reports 21.1 [PAGE 14]. Such small inconsistencies do not overturn the main result, but they matter for exact replication over many weeks.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
CPMöbius is not a formal theory-of-mind model, but it implements a recursive co-adaptation loop. The Coach must infer the Player’s current capability frontier from \(n\) rollouts per task; the Player must infer the task’s intended answer from its own consensus; and the designer must infer whether the Coach’s proposed curriculum is teaching or merely producing noise. The Coach’s only environmental observation is \(\Delta_t\): “The Coach never observes ground-truth solutions; instead, it receives only a scalar environment feedback signal, \(\Delta_t\), which captures the post-update performance change” [PAGE 4]. This is a low-bandwidth recursive belief channel. The Coach’s belief about the Player is compressed into the scalar product \(R_i^{\text{Player}}\cdot\Delta_t\), and the Player’s belief about the task is compressed into the majority vote \(y_i^\star\). The recursive loop is therefore: Coach models Player difficulty, Player models answer consensus, validation measures Player change, Coach updates its model of Player difficulty. The filter \(0.2\le\hat p_i\le0.8\) is the explicit “zone of proximal development” proxy [PAGE 4].

Crisis habit regression appears when the recursion degrades into a habitual shortcut. If the Coach is weak or unskilled, the paper warns that it “tends to generate ambiguous or unhelpful tasks, which makes the feedback noisy and undermines co-evolution” [PAGE 2]. Without warm-up, the ablation drops average accuracy to 23.7 percent and OOD to 21.2 percent [PAGE 8]. Without Coach updates, average drops from 28.8 to 25.3 and OOD from 26.8 to 23.1 [PAGE 8]. Without the instruction filter, average drops to 24.9 and OOD to 22.5 [PAGE 8]. These ablations are essentially measures of recursive habit regression: the Coach either stops adapting, starts from a bad prior, or stops calibrating difficulty. On the Player side, a crisis habit can be “majority-vote exploitation”: if the Player learns to produce internally consistent but mathematically wrong answers, the pseudo-label rewards the habit. The paper’s self-consistency framing makes this a real gray zone because the verifier is the model’s own consensus, not an external ground truth [PAGE 3].

The counter-trap is the cooperative reward product. In adversarial self-play the Coach would be rewarded for stumping the Player; here the Coach is rewarded by \(R_i^{\text{Player}}\cdot\Delta_t\). If the Coach proposes an impossible task, \(R_i^{\text{Player}}\) is low, so the Coach is not reinforced. If the Coach proposes a trivial task, \(\hat p_i\) is near 1 and the GRPO advantage is near zero; the filter also removes it. If the Coach proposes a task that coincides with positive validation change, the product is positive. This is a deliberate counter-trap against the “stump the solver” incentive that the paper attributes to adversarial self-play [PAGE 2]. The remaining weakness is that \(\Delta_t\) is global: a task can receive positive reward merely because the whole round improved, even if that task was not the cause. Thus the counter-trap is statistical, not a proof of per-task causal teaching.

The clock is the round index and the validation cadence. The paper trains for up to 1000 steps [PAGE 15], uses AMC as the fixed validation environment [PAGE 6], and reports smoothed validation curves in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 [PAGE 7, PAGE 8]. The clock matters because \(\Delta_t\) is a difference between successive Player checkpoints; if evaluation is noisy, the Coach’s reward is a clock-aliased signal. The paper chooses AMC because its difficulty is “typically moderate” and “neither trivial which would quickly saturate and yield a near-zero learning signal nor overly hard which would produce sparse, noisy feedback early on” [PAGE 6]. That is an explicit clock-and-difficulty calibration. Over long training, the clock also interacts with the length trends: Coach outputs grow while Player outputs shrink [PAGE 18], so the effective task distribution and answer distribution both drift. A recursive theory-of-mind audit should therefore treat the Coach’s “teaching” as a joint function of difficulty inference, consensus inference, and validation-clock noise, not as a clean pedagogical intention.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: disable the difficulty filter. If the filter is causally necessary for keeping tasks in the learnable zone, then removing it should reduce the Player’s average and OOD accuracy. The paper’s Table 2 reports exactly this prediction for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B: CPMöbius achieves average 28.8 and OOD 26.8, while “w/o Instruction Filter” achieves average 24.9 and OOD 22.5 [PAGE 8]. Verifiable prediction: a same-seed, same-budget rerun with the filter disabled should reproduce a drop of roughly 3.9 average points and 4.3 OOD points on this model, or at least a statistically distinguishable drop. If it does not, the filter’s claimed causal role is vetoed.

Veto scenario 2: freeze the Coach. If Coach adaptation is necessary for the co-evolutionary curriculum, then freezing the Coach should degrade performance relative to the full update. Table 2 reports that “w/o Coach Update” yields average 25.3 and OOD 23.1, versus 28.8 and 26.8 for full CPMöbius [PAGE 8]. The ablation text says that removing Coach updates “degrades average accuracy from 28.8% to 25.3%, with out-of-distribution (OOD) performance dropping from 26.8% to 23.1%” [PAGE 8]. Verifiable prediction: a frozen-Coach rerun under the same \(m=16\), \(n=16\) configuration [PAGE 6] should produce a lower AMC validation curve than the adaptive-Coach run. If the curves are indistinguishable, the Coach-update mechanism is vetoed as non-causal.

Veto scenario 3: test the AMC-label leakage hypothesis. If the reported gains were caused mainly by using labeled AMC validation feedback, then training on only 20 percent of AMC and testing on the remaining 80 percent should show little or no improvement. The paper reports the opposite pattern: with only 20 percent AMC data, CPMöbius reaches average 26.1 versus the base model’s 23.3, and on “AMC(remaining 80%)” it reaches 41.2 versus 38.8 [PAGE 14]. The source says, “We conduct an experiment, training with only 20% AMC data and testing over the remaining data” [PAGE 14]. Verifiable prediction: a leakage-only hypothesis predicts near-zero gain on the held-out 80 percent; the reported gain of 2.4 points on that split partially vetoes the strong leakage explanation. A stricter follow-up would replace AMC entirely with Minerva or OlympiadBench; the paper reports continued gains there as well [PAGE 14], which further weakens, though does not eliminate, the leakage veto. If a fully label-free validation environment produced no gain, the data-free claim would be substantially vetoed.
