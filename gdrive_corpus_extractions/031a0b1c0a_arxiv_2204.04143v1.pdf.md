The source document is a survey of optical tracking in team sports, and its central epistemic object is not a single algorithm but a data-production pipeline: video frames become detections, detections become associated tracks, and tracks become trajectory data for quantitative analysts. The paper states this dependency directly: “Tracking data, i.e., the exact location of the players and the ball on the field at each moment of the match, is the most important data for a quantitative model developer” [PAGE 3]. Therefore, any rigorous reasoning trace must model error propagation across layers: sensor and optics, detection, association, trajectory construction, and downstream analytics.

The first multi-hop derivation is from visual input to bounding-box detection. The survey separates traditional and deep-learning detection, noting that traditional methods require hand-specified features while deep methods learn features automatically [PAGE 3]. For traditional detection, the paper describes Histogram of Oriented Gradients, Hough transform, background modeling, edge detection, and supervised classifiers [PAGE 4–6]. Each of these creates a feature representation whose reliability depends on illumination, texture, occlusion, and camera geometry. For example, Gaussian Mixture Model background modeling is probability-based, but the source explicitly limits it: “This algorithm cannot recognize players in shadows” [PAGE 5]. That limitation is not merely empirical; it is a breakdown of the assumed observation model, because shadow pixels violate the assumption that foreground differs from the learned background distribution.

The second hop is from detections to tracking. Detection alone is insufficient because the survey states that “In these cases, the detection methods cannot provide the location of the same player and ball in consecutive frames to construct continuous trajectories” [PAGE 7]. Tracking therefore becomes a state-estimation and data-association problem. The source gives three canonical probabilistic operators: particle filtering, Kalman filtering, and graph-based or data-association methods. Particle filtering approximates a posterior distribution with weighted samples [PAGE 8]. Kalman filtering recursively updates a state under linear-Gaussian assumptions [PAGE 8]. Graph-based methods construct possible trajectories and select a path using optimization or dynamic programming [PAGE 13]. These are not interchangeable: each has a precise breakdown regime. Kalman filters degrade when nonlinear or non-Gaussian noise dominates; the survey’s cost table explicitly notes for one method that “non-linear, non-Gaussian noises are ignored, which decreases the accuracy of tracking” [PAGE 10]. Particle filters degrade when the number of particles is insufficient, weights collapse, or the motion/appearance model is misspecified.

The third hop is from tracking quality to method selection. The source emphasizes that selection is task-dependent: “model selection, i.e., DL or traditional, heavily depends on the task at hand” [PAGE 15]. This is a gray-zone audit point. The survey does not provide a universal performance scalar. Instead, it reports heterogeneous metrics across papers: precision, miss rate, accuracy, MOTA, mAP, AUC, MAPE, tracking rate, detection rate, solved occlusions, and identity switches [PAGE 4–16]. The table on deep learning explicitly defines metrics as “AUC: Area Under Curve; mAP: mean Average Precision; MAPE: Mean Absolute Percentage Error; MOTA: Multi Object Tracking Accuracy” [PAGE 16]. These metrics have different semantics and units. Therefore, any comparison that collapses them into a single “best method” claim without specifying task, camera setup, video quality, and ground-truth protocol is underdetermined.

A further gray-zone issue is dataset bias. The survey collected 125 papers, filtered to 50 eligible papers [PAGE 2], and reports that “74% of methods are applied on football videos” [PAGE 15]. This creates a domain-shift trap: a method optimized for broadcast football may fail for basketball, ice hockey, handball, or volleyball because camera motion, player density, court geometry, and object scale differ. The source also warns about camera setup: single fixed cameras require simpler preprocessing but produce blurry frames; multiple fixed cameras help occlusion but increase infrastructure; multiple moving cameras produce longer trajectories but require synchronization [PAGE 2]. Thus, a method’s reported accuracy cannot be audited independently of its acquisition layer.

The deepest gray-zone audit concerns observability. The survey repeatedly identifies occlusion and poor visual conditions as failure sources: “tracking usually fails in the cases of overlaps, poor light conditions, and low quality of the videos” [PAGE 1] and “continuous tracking fails due to e.g., occlusion, poor light, or posture changes” [PAGE 3]. Formally, if the observation likelihood becomes zero because the target is invisible, no downstream tracker can create reliable evidence from that frame alone. It may interpolate or hallucinate, but those are model priors, not optical measurements. This distinction is crucial for quantitative sports analytics: interpolated trajectory segments must be labeled as inferred, not observed, unless validated by another sensing modality.

Finally, the audit must separate computational cost from epistemological reliability. The source says “deep learning methods which require GPUs are more expensive than traditional methods with only CPUs” [PAGE 17], but cost is not the same as robustness. Deep methods reduce manual feature engineering and can generalize across object appearances, yet they remain vulnerable to distribution shift, annotation quality, and training-time bias. Traditional methods are more interpretable and debuggable, but they can be brittle under lighting, shadows, and crowded scenes. Therefore, the correct reasoning trace is layered: identify the physical sensing constraints, identify the probabilistic tracking assumptions, identify metric semantics, and only then adjudicate method suitability. The survey supports this layered audit because it repeatedly ties performance to camera setup, occlusion, computational resources, and annotation cost rather than to a single scalar score.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides several formal operators. I list them with assumptions, units, and exact breakdown regimes.

1. Gaussian Mixture Model background modeling.

Source equation:

\[
P(X_t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\omega_i \eta(X_t)
\]

Citation: [PAGE 5].  
Units: \(P(X_t)\) is a dimensionless probability or likelihood; \(X_t\) is a pixel value or feature value; \(\eta(X_t)\) is a Gaussian density; \(\omega_i\) is a mixture weight with \(\sum_i \omega_i = 1\).  
Assumption: background pixels are modeled by \(k\) Gaussian components, and foreground is detected by thresholding deviations.  
Breakdown: the source states, “This algorithm cannot recognize players in shadows” [PAGE 5]. Shadows violate the assumption that foreground pixels are separable from the background distribution. Another gray-zone breakdown is threshold sensitivity: the source says background subtraction is done “by setting arbitrary thresholds on the value of the pixels” [PAGE 5].

2. Particle filter approximation.

Source equation:

\[
P(x) \sim \sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i(x-x_i)
\]

Citation: [PAGE 8].  
Units: \(P(x)\) is a probability distribution or density over state \(x\); \(x_i\) are particles representing possible states, such as player position in image pixels or field meters; \(\omega_i\) are normalized weights.  
Assumption: the posterior state distribution can be approximated by a weighted set of \(N\) samples.  
Breakdown: particle-weight degeneracy. If all weights concentrate on very few particles, the effective sample size collapses. Formally, effective sample size can be audited as

\[
N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_i^2}.
\]

If \(N_{\text{eff}}\to 1\), the filter is fragile and likely to lose the target after a short occlusion. The source also notes that particle-filter variants differ by motion model and weighting method, such as Bhattacharyya coefficient or weighted average of particles [PAGE 8–10].

3. Kalman filter state transition.

Source equation:

\[
x_t = F x_{t-1} + B u_{t-1} + \omega_{t-1}
\]

Citation: [PAGE 8].  
Units: \(x_t\) is a state vector, for example position in meters and velocity in meters per second; \(F\) is a transition matrix; \(u_{t-1}\) is a control vector; \(B\) maps control to state; \(\omega_{t-1}\) is process noise, usually modeled as zero-mean Gaussian with covariance units matching the state.  
Assumption: state evolution is linear and noise is Gaussian.  
Breakdown: nonlinear dynamics, abrupt direction changes, or non-Gaussian noise violate the model. The survey’s Kalman-filter table explicitly states for one method: “non-linear & non-Gaussian noise are ignored, which decreases the accuracy of tracking” [PAGE 10]. A further breakdown occurs when no observation is available: without a measurement update, the predicted covariance grows, and the tracker becomes increasingly uncertain.

4. Intersection over Union for tracking association.

Source equation:

\[
\mathrm{IOU} = \frac{|BBA \cap BBB|}{|BBA \cup BBB|}
\]

Citation: [PAGE 14].  
Units: \(BBA\) is the ground-truth bounding box from the previous frame; \(BBB\) is the predicted bounding box in the current frame; intersection and union are measured in pixels, so IOU is dimensionless and bounded in \([0,1]\).  
Assumption: object location changes slowly enough that boxes overlap between consecutive frames.  
Breakdown: if boxes are disjoint, IOU becomes zero, and association fails unless another cue such as appearance, motion, or graph connectivity is used. If both boxes have zero area, the expression is undefined.

5. Ball compaction descriptor.

Source equation:

\[
C_d = \frac{P^2}{4\pi A}
\]

Citation: [PAGE 12].  
Units: \(P\) is perimeter, often measured in pixels; \(A\) is area in square pixels; \(C_d\) is dimensionless. The source states, “if Cd > 50%, the shape can be filtered as a ball” [PAGE 12].  
Assumption: the ball candidate is approximately circular or compact.  
Breakdown: the source notes that “the shape of the ball varies significantly in each frame, and does not look like a circle at all” [PAGE 12]. Motion blur, occlusion, low resolution, and shadows can make \(C_d\) unstable.

6. Ball eccentricity / form-factor descriptor.

Source description: eccentricity is “the ratio of the longest diameter to the shortest diameter of a shape” [PAGE 12]. The source also states that the form factor indicates circularity, and “if the result is between [0.2,0.65] they will consider it as a ball” [PAGE 12].  
Units: dimensionless ratios.  
Assumption: the ball appears approximately elliptical and isolated.  
Breakdown: occlusion, blur, court-line confusion, or low signal-to-noise ratio can push the descriptor outside the accepted range.

7. Derived physical motion-blur operator.

Not explicitly written in the source, but required for a physical audit. Let

\[
d = \frac{v \Delta t}{g}
\]

where \(v\) is object speed in meters per second, \(\Delta t\) is inter-frame time in seconds, and \(g\) is ground sampling distance in meters per pixel. Then \(d\) is displacement in pixels.  
Assumption: constant velocity, approximately planar motion, negligible rolling shutter or lens distortion.  
Breakdown: if \(d\) exceeds the object width \(w\) in pixels, frame-to-frame association by simple overlap becomes ambiguous. Similarly, motion blur length can be written as

\[
b = \frac{v \tau_{\text{exp}}}{g},
\]

where \(\tau_{\text{exp}}\) is exposure time in seconds. If \(b \gtrsim w\), shape features such as ball compactness or player silhouette become unreliable.

8. Information-theoretic association ambiguity.

A formal information unit can be defined as

\[
H(A|I) = -\sum_{a} p(a|I)\log_2 p(a|I),
\]

where \(H(A|I)\) is conditional uncertainty in bits about identity or association \(A\) given image evidence \(I\).  
Assumption: candidate identities or tracks are enumerated.  
Breakdown: if many indistinguishable candidates exist, \(H(A|I)\to \log_2 M\), where \(M\) is the number of candidates. In crowded scenes, this uncertainty increases, matching the source’s statement that “The biggest challenge in tracking is the overlapping of players, namely the occlusion” [PAGE 7].

9. Low-light signal-to-noise breakdown.

A formal photon-noise audit can be written as

\[
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{N_s}{\sqrt{N_s + N_d + \sigma_r^2}},
\]

where \(N_s\) is signal photoelectrons, \(N_d\) is dark-current electrons, \(\sigma_r\) is read noise in electrons, and SNR is dimensionless.  
Assumption: independent noise sources and approximately linear sensor response.  
Breakdown: in poor illumination, \(N_s\) becomes small and SNR collapses. The source repeatedly identifies poor light as a failure cause: “poor light conditions” [PAGE 1] and “poor light” [PAGE 3].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The survey’s historical lineage is a progression from handcrafted feature extraction to learned representation. In detection, the early lineage includes HOG and Hough transform for lines and player-like structures [PAGE 4], background modeling through Gaussian mixtures [PAGE 4–5], pixel-energy evaluation [PAGE 5], edge detection through Canny and Sobel operators [PAGE 5], and supervised classifiers such as SVM and Adaboost [PAGE 5–6]. The survey then marks the transition to deep learning through CNN-based jersey-number recognition, Spatial Transformer Networks, and Region Proposal Networks [PAGE 6]. This lineage is not merely chronological; it is a shift from analyzer-defined features to network-learned features. The source captures this by contrasting traditional feature description with automatic extraction through neural-network layers [PAGE 3].

In tracking, the lineage begins with point-based representations such as Point Distribution Models, Harris corners, and SIFT features [PAGE 7], then moves to probabilistic filters: particle filters and Kalman filters [PAGE 8–9]. Contour tracking follows, including centroid tracking, active contours, snakes, and contour filtering [PAGE 10–11]. Silhouette and shape analysis emerge when contour information is insufficient, especially for ball tracking using compaction, eccentricity, skeletons, and Euclidean distance transforms [PAGE 12]. Graph-based tracking then represents trajectories as nodes and edges and solves them via shortest path, dynamic programming, Viterbi, or mixed-integer programming [PAGE 13]. Data-association methods such as MCMC and JPDA address uncertainty when observations and tracks are ambiguous [PAGE 13]. Finally, deep-learning tracking enters through VGG-M, Cascade-CNN, YOLO, and Siamese networks, using learned features and IOU-based association [PAGE 14–15].

Multi-week traps recur across this genealogy. The first trap is annotation and labeling. Traditional supervised methods can require manual labeling, and deep methods require large training sets. The source notes that a support-vector-machine method required manually labeled training data, which is “time-consuming” [PAGE 6], and that deep methods may have “expensive manual force for labeling” [PAGE 18]. The second trap is domain expertise. Traditional methods depend on precise feature descriptions, while deep methods reduce that need but increase training and tuning complexity. The survey says, “Traditional models are time-consuming and require domain expertise” and “deep learning models are quite expensive to run in terms of computing resources” [PAGE 17]. The third trap is occlusion. The survey states, “The biggest challenge in tracking is the overlapping of players, namely the occlusion” [PAGE 7]. This problem recurs across traditional and deep methods, though the proposed mitigations differ: multi-camera views, contour deformation, graph-based hypothesis selection, appearance features, or learned detectors. The fourth trap is camera infrastructure. Single fixed cameras are cheaper but blurry; multiple fixed cameras improve occlusion handling; moving cameras require synchronization and produce more complex processing [PAGE 2]. The source states that moving-camera video “require[s] significant synchronization effort” [PAGE 2]. The fifth trap is metric heterogeneity. Different papers report precision, accuracy, MOTA, mAP, AUC, MAPE, tracking rate, solved occlusions, and identity switches, making direct comparison difficult without a common protocol [PAGE 4–16].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit must model how a tracking system behaves when the environment enters a crisis state, such as occlusion, blur, low light, or rapid camera motion. The survey’s crisis taxonomy is explicit: overlaps, poor light, and low video quality cause tracking failure [PAGE 1]; occlusion, poor light, and posture changes interrupt continuous tracking [PAGE 3]. In such crises, trackers often regress to habits. The most common habit is motion extrapolation: the tracker assumes the last observed state persists. Particle filters in the source often use “Linear uniform motion” to model movement [PAGE 8], and Kalman filters predict state forward using a transition matrix [PAGE 8]. This habit is useful during brief detection dropouts, but it becomes a trap during prolonged occlusion or abrupt direction changes.

A second crisis habit is appearance persistence: the tracker assumes color, edge, or shape features remain stable. The source describes particle weights based on color and edge features [PAGE 8], shape descriptors for the ball [PAGE 12], and Siamese networks comparing similarity between search areas and a target template [PAGE 15]. This habit fails when jersey color changes due to lighting, when players overlap, or when the ball is blurred beyond recognition. The source warns that the moving ball “does not look like a circle at all” [PAGE 12], which directly undermines shape-based crisis assumptions.

A third crisis habit is graph continuity: the tracker assumes the correct trajectory can be selected from a constructed graph of possible paths [PAGE 13]. This works when detection candidates are reliable and the graph includes the true path. But if detection fails or candidate generation is biased, the graph may not contain the true trajectory, causing identity switches or lost tracks.

Counter-trap strategies are visible in the survey. One is multi-view observation: multiple fixed cameras can resolve occlusion because an occluded player in one view may be visible in another [PAGE 2]. Another is learned appearance: deep networks can classify player, ball, or background and then use IOU or feature similarity for tracking [PAGE 14–15]. A third is probabilistic data association: MCMC and JPDA methods explicitly handle ambiguous mappings between observations and tracks [PAGE 13]. A fourth is optimization-based graph search, such as dynamic programming, Viterbi, or mixed-integer programming, which can enforce global trajectory constraints rather than relying only on frame-to-frame inertia [PAGE 13].

The clock dimension is critical. A tracker must operate within the temporal cadence of the video. If the end-to-end latency

\[
L = \tau_{\text{capture}} + \tau_{\text{detect}} + \tau_{\text{associate}} + \tau_{\text{output}}
\]

exceeds the frame interval \(\Delta t\), the system acts on stale information. Units are seconds. The survey distinguishes online and offline deep trackers: “online trackers are trained from scratch during the test and are not taking advantage of already annotated videos for improving performance, while offline trackers train on offline data” [PAGE 14]. Real-time feasibility is also method-dependent; for one particle-filter method, the survey’s table says “Not real-time” [PAGE 10]. Thus, a counter-trap must respect the clock: if latency is too high, more complex association may worsen performance because decisions arrive after the crisis has changed the scene.

The recursive mental model is therefore: in crisis, the tracker regresses to prediction; the counter-trap must inject new evidence, such as multi-view geometry, learned appearance, or global optimization; but the clock constrains which counter-trap is admissible. If the clock is too slow, the tracker’s prior becomes stale, and the system must either reduce processing cost, simplify the state model, or accept flagged uncertainty rather than emit a false continuous trajectory.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto proof must show when a physical or biomechanical constraint makes reliable tracking impossible regardless of algorithm class. Let visibility be \(V_t \in \{0,1\}\), where \(V_t=1\) means the target produces usable optical evidence at time \(t\), and \(V_t=0\) means it does not. If \(V_t=0\), the observation likelihood can be written as

\[
p(z_t \mid x_t) = 0
\]

for all object states \(x_t\). In Bayesian tracking, if no observation is available, the posterior collapses to the prior:

\[
p(x_t \mid z_{1:t}) = p(x_t \mid z_{1:t-1}).
\]

This is not an algorithmic preference; it is an observability proof. If the target is fully occluded, fully outside the field of view, or rendered indistinguishable by blur or low light, no tracker can create direct evidence for that frame. The survey supports this with repeated warnings: “tracking usually fails in the cases of overlaps, poor light conditions, and low quality of the videos” [PAGE 1], and “continuous tracking fails due to e.g., occlusion, poor light, or posture changes” [PAGE 3]. It also states, “The biggest challenge in tracking is the overlapping of players, namely the occlusion” [PAGE 7].

A second veto proof uses motion blur. Let \(b = v\tau_{\text{exp}}/g\), where \(v\) is object speed in meters per second, \(\tau_{\text{exp}}\) is exposure time in seconds, and \(g\) is ground sampling distance in meters per pixel. If \(b\) exceeds the characteristic object width \(w\) in pixels, then shape features are smeared across multiple pixels. For the ball, compactness and eccentricity become unreliable because the shape no longer approximates a circle or ellipse. The source states that the moving ball “varies significantly in each frame, and does not look like a circle at all” [PAGE 12]. Therefore, when \(b \gtrsim w\), shape-based ball tracking receives a physical veto.

A third veto proof uses displacement and association. Let \(d = v\Delta t/g\), with \(\Delta t\) the inter-frame interval. If \(d > w\), then the object may move farther than its own width between frames. If appearance cues are weak or players are crowded, IOU-based association can fail because predicted and true bounding boxes may not overlap. The source’s IOU operator assumes nonzero intersection for tracking [PAGE 14]. When \(d\) is too large and no reliable motion or appearance model exists, association becomes ambiguous. In information terms, the conditional uncertainty \(H(A|I)\) rises toward \(\log_2 M\) for \(M\) plausible candidates. This is a biomechanical and physical veto because fast players or balls exceed the temporal sampling capacity of the camera.

A fourth veto proof uses illumination. Let signal photoelectrons be \(N_s\), dark noise \(N_d\), and read noise \(\sigma_r\). The approximate SNR is

\[
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{N_s}{\sqrt{N_s + N_d + \sigma_r^2}}.
\]

If \(N_s\) is too low, detection thresholds fail and false positives rise. The source identifies poor light as a primary failure condition [PAGE 1, PAGE 3]. In low-light regimes, even a deep detector may receive insufficient photon evidence. This is a cross-layer veto: the failure occurs before feature learning or tracking association.

A fifth veto proof uses camera geometry and synchronization. For moving cameras, the source says such video requires “significant synchronization effort” [PAGE 2]. If timestamp error or homography estimation error exceeds the tolerance needed to project a player into a common field coordinate, trajectories become discontinuous. The survey also lists short extracted trajectories as a limitation for one point-tracking method [PAGE 18]. Thus, synchronization failure is a physical and geometric veto, not merely an engineering inconvenience.

The final adjudication rule is: if \(V_t=0\), if \(b \gtrsim w\), if \(d > w\) without reliable appearance or motion evidence, if SNR is below detection threshold, or if camera synchronization fails beyond tolerance, then a tracking claim based solely on that video layer must be vetoed or flagged as inferred. The survey’s own text supports this veto structure because it repeatedly ties failure to occlusion, blur, poor light, low video quality, camera setup, and synchronization [PAGE 1–3, PAGE 7, PAGE 14–18].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Below are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario states an observable condition, the formal reason for veto, and a measurable prediction.

1. Full-occlusion veto.

Observable condition: ground-truth annotation shows that a player or ball has zero visible pixels for at least \(K\) consecutive frames, or the visible fraction of the ground-truth bounding box is below a predefined visibility threshold.  
Formal reason: if \(V_t=0\), then \(p(z_t\mid x_t)=0\). The tracker has no direct observation.  
Prediction: any method relying only on single-view video will either lose the track, produce an identity switch after reappearance, or generate interpolated positions with no observation support. Verifiable metrics: MOTA drops during the occlusion interval; identity switches increase; reacquisition latency increases; interpolated segment length increases.  
Source support: the survey says occlusion is “The biggest challenge in tracking” [PAGE 7] and notes that traditional HOG-based detection cannot detect occluded players [PAGE 18].

2. Motion-blur / high-velocity ball veto.

Observable condition: measured ball speed \(v\), exposure time \(\tau_{\text{exp}}\), and ground sampling distance \(g\) satisfy \(b = v\tau_{\text{exp}}/g \gtrsim d_{\text{ball}}\), where \(d_{\text{ball}}\) is ball diameter in pixels. Alternatively, annotated ball blur length exceeds ball diameter.  
Formal reason: shape descriptors such as compactness \(C_d\) and eccentricity assume an approximately compact or elliptical blob. When blur destroys that geometry, the descriptor distribution separates from the training or threshold regime.  
Prediction: ball detection recall drops sharply; compactness and eccentricity filters reject true ball candidates; false positives from court lines or shadows increase. Verifiable metrics: ball recall, precision, false-positive rate, and descriptor threshold rejection rate.  
Source support: the survey states that the moving ball “does not look like a circle at all” [PAGE 12] and uses compactness and eccentricity as shape descriptors [PAGE 12].

3. Camera-synchronization / moving-camera homography veto.

Observable condition: multiple moving cameras are used, but timestamp synchronization error \(\Delta t_{\text{sync}}\) or homography reprojection error \(\epsilon_h\) exceeds the method’s required tolerance. For example, if projected player positions from different cameras disagree by more than one player width in field coordinates, cross-camera association is invalid.  
Formal reason: multi-view tracking assumes that observations can be transformed into a common geometric frame. If synchronization or homography fails, the observation model is inconsistent.  
Prediction: cross-camera association produces identity switches, duplicate tracks, or trajectory fragmentation. Verifiable metrics: cross-view association precision, trajectory length, fragmentation count, and reprojection error.  
Source support: moving-camera video requires “significant synchronization effort” [PAGE 2], and the survey lists “extracted trajectories are too short” as a limitation for a point-tracking method [PAGE 18].

These vetoes are counterfactual because they specify the condition under which a method’s reported success should not be trusted. They are verifiable because each requires measurable quantities: visibility annotation, ball velocity and exposure, timestamp error, reprojection error, MOTA, ID switches, recall, precision, or trajectory fragmentation. They do not rely on subjective ranking; they derive failure from observability, geometry, or information-theoretic ambiguity.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
