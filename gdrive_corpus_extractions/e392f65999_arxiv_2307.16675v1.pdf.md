The paper’s multi-hop argument is: large-scale 3D MOT datasets such as NuScenes contain multiple object categories, and those categories have different geometry and motion patterns; therefore, a single physical model and a single similarity metric create biased predictions and biased costs; biased costs produce erroneous trajectory–detection matches; those matches degrade downstream navigation and tracking. The first-hop evidence is stated in the abstract: “existing 3D MOT methods typically employ a single similarity metric and physical model to perform data association and state estimation for all objects” [PAGE 1], while real scenes contain “a variety of object categories that commonly exhibit distinctive geometric properties and motion patterns” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is the empirical fact that category sensitivity is not hypothetical: Table I reports that for Ped, gIoU3d gives 81.7 AMOTA and 175 IDS, while gIoUbev gives 81.2 AMOTA and 203 IDS; for Bus, gIoUbev gives 88.2 AMOTA and 2 IDS, while gIoU3d gives 88.1 AMOTA and 2 IDS [PAGE 2]. The paper concludes from this that “a single similarity metric cannot perform well in all object categories” [PAGE 2]. The third hop is the motion-model argument: “Most previous methods [5], [9] employ a uniform CA or CV model to predict the trajectories of all objects,” but “they fail to capture the highly nonlinear motion features of objects and ignore the differences in motion patterns across categories” [PAGE 3]. Poly-MOT therefore replaces a single model with category-conditioned models: CTRA for car-like objects and pedestrians, and Bicycle for objects that behave like bicycles [PAGE 3]. The fourth hop is association: first-stage category-specific metric/threshold matching, then second-stage re-association of unmatched detections and trajectories with a different metric and a strict threshold, with cross-category costs filled by invalid values [PAGE 5]. The fifth hop is lifecycle management: count-based initialization, max-age death, confidence decay, and output NMS at θ_nms = 0.08 [PAGE 6]. The sixth hop is the measured system result: 75.4% AMOTA on NuScenes test [PAGE 1, PAGE 5], 73.1% AMOTA on validation with CenterPoint and 75.2% with LargeKernel3D-L [PAGE 6], and 3 FPS on Intel 9940X without GPU [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit is where a careful reader must slow down. First, the paper calls gIoU3d, gIoUbev, and deucl “similarity metrics,” but the first-stage association thresholds are θ_fm = (1.6, 1.4, 1.3, 1.3, 1.3, 1.2, 1.7) for bicycle, motorcycle, bus, car, trailer, truck, pedestrian [PAGE 6]. A raw gIoU is bounded near [-1, 1], so a threshold above 1 cannot be a direct similarity cutoff. The paper does not explicitly state the sign convention or whether the cost is 1 − gIoU, or a distance-like transform. That is a reproducibility gap: the same numbers imply different accepted sets depending on the unstated cost transform. Second, deucl is introduced in Eq. (12) and Table I, but the final configuration uses gIoUbev for bus and gIoU3d for the other six categories [PAGE 6]; deucl is not the deployed first-stage metric in the reported final system. Third, the Bicycle slip-angle equation is printed as β(τ)=tan^{-1}(l_r/(γ l tan(δ(τ)))) [PAGE 4]; as typeset it has a δ→0 singularity, whereas the standard kinematic bicycle form is usually β=arctan((l_r/(γ l))tan δ). The paper’s exact parenthesization is ambiguous in the extracted text, so any reimplementation must resolve that ambiguity before trusting the singularity analysis. Fourth, the ablation is non-monotonic in IDS: “Os+Pre” has 374 IDS, “Os+Pre+Mo” has 443 IDS, and the full “Os+Pre+Mo+Ass” has 281 IDS [PAGE 7]. The paper explains that more accurate motion models shift the prediction–ground-truth bias distribution, so a single metric/threshold can no longer capture affinities and IDS rises until the association module is added [PAGE 7]. That is a real interaction effect, not a standalone module win. Fifth, NuScenes keyframes are sampled at 2 Hz, and the paper itself says “this keyframe frequency poses a challenge for precise motion model prediction, leading to significant inter-frame displacement” [PAGE 6]. Thus the motion-model gains are measured under coarse temporal sampling; higher-rate or lower-rate sensors will change the integration error and the usefulness of CTRA/Bicycle. Sixth, the paper includes “rainy days and nights” in NuScenes [PAGE 6], but it does not report a weather-stratified ablation, so claims about rain/night robustness are not empirically isolated. These gray zones do not invalidate the method; they define the conditions under which the reported numbers are reproducible.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Poly-MOT uses two 10-dimensional state vectors. In CTRA:
\[
T^{CTRA}=[x,y,z,v,a,\theta,\omega,w,l,h]^\top,
\]
and in Bicycle:
\[
T^{BIC}=[x',y',z,v,a,\theta,\delta,w,l,h]^\top.
\]
Here \(x,y,z,x',y',w,l,h\) are in metres; \(v\) is m·s\(^{-1}\); \(a\) is m·s\(^{-2}\); \(\theta,\omega,\delta,\beta\) are radians or rad·s\(^{-1}\) as appropriate. The paper states that \((x,y,z)\) are “the location of the geometric center of objects in the 3D space” and \((w,l,h)\) are the “3D size of objects” [PAGE 3]. For Bicycle, \((x',y')\) is “the location of the gravity center of the object on the ground” and \(\delta\) is “the steering angle” [PAGE 3]. The EKF prediction is
\[
T_{t,t-1}=f(T_{t-1}),\qquad P_{t,t-1}=F_tP_{t-1}F_t^\top+Q,
\]
where \(F_t=\partial f/\partial T_{t-1}\) and \(Q\) is “the process noise, which has an artificially set value” [PAGE 4]. Covariance units are squared state units: m\(^2\), (m·s\(^{-1}\))\(^2\), (m·s\(^{-2}\))\(^2\), rad\(^2\), (rad·s\(^{-1}\))\(^2\). The position transition is
\[
\hat x_{t,t-1}=\hat x_{t-1}+\int_{(t-1)\sigma}^{t\sigma}v(\tau)\cos\eta(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
\[
\hat y_{t,t-1}=\hat y_{t-1}+\int_{(t-1)\sigma}^{t\sigma}v(\tau)\sin\eta(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
with \(\sigma\) the LiDAR frame interval in seconds and \(\Delta t=\tau-(t-1)\sigma\) [PAGE 4]. The model branches are
\[
v(\tau)=
\begin{cases}
v_{t-1}+a\Delta t, & T=T^{CTRA},\\
v_{t-1}, & T=T^{BIC},
\end{cases}
\]
\[
\eta(\tau)=
\begin{cases}
\theta(\tau), & T=T^{CTRA},\\
\theta(\tau)+\beta(\tau), & T=T^{BIC},
\end{cases}
\]
\[
\theta(\tau)=\theta_{t-1}+\omega(\tau)\Delta t,
\]
\[
\omega(\tau)=
\begin{cases}
\omega_{t-1}, & T=T^{CTRA},\\
\dfrac{v(\tau)\sin(\beta(\tau))}{l_r}, & T=T^{BIC}.
\end{cases}
\]
The slip angle is printed as
\[
\beta(\tau)=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{l_r}{\gamma l\tan(\delta(\tau))}\right),
\]
where \(\gamma\) is “the ratio of the wheelbase to object length \(l\)” and \(l_r\) is “the distance between the gravity center and the rear tire,” set to 0.4–0.5 times the wheelbase [PAGE 4]. The paper explains that Eq. (7) is “the embodiment of retaining the rigid structure of the object” and that “the instantaneous center of the object in the Bicycle Model is not on the body of the object” [PAGE 4]. The similarity operators are
\[
gIoU_{3d}(B_i,B_j)=IoU_{3d}(B_i,B_j)+\frac{V(B_i\cup B_j)}{V_{3dhull}(B_i,B_j)}-1,
\]
\[
gIoU_{bev}(B_i,B_j)=IoU_{bev}(B_i,B_j)+\frac{A(B_i\cup B_j)}{A_{bevhull}(B_i,B_j)}-1,
\]
which are dimensionless, and
\[
d(B_i,B_j)=\gamma_{geo}\|B_i^{wlh}-B_j^{wlh}\|_2+\gamma_{dis}\|B_i^{xyz}-B_j^{xyz}\|_2,
\]
\[
d_{eucl}(B_i,B_j)=d(B_i,B_j)\,(2-\cos|\Delta\theta|),
\]
with \(d_{eucl}\) in metres if the state vectors are in metres and the \(\gamma\) factors are dimensionless; \(\Delta\theta\in[0,\pi]\) is in radians, so the penalty factor \(2-\cos|\Delta\theta|\) lies in \([1,3]\). The first-stage cost tensor is \(C_t^1\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{cls}\times N_{det,t}\times N_{tra,t-1}}\), and for each category the paper obtains \(C_{t,cls}^1\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{det,t}\times N_{tra,t-1}}\) [PAGE 5]. Matching is a one-to-one assignment, solved by the Hungarian algorithm, with category-specific thresholds \(\theta_{fm}=(\theta_{fm}^1,\dots,\theta_{fm}^{N_{cls}})\) [PAGE 5]. The second stage uses “gIoUbev for objects of all categories” unless the object used gIoUbev in the first stage, in which case gIoU3d is applied in the second stage; the paper says “the core of multi-stage association is to use different metrics to perform repeated associations” [PAGE 5]. Cross-category costs are “filled with invalid values” [PAGE 5].

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit and implicit. In the printed Bicycle slip formula, the denominator contains \(\gamma l\tan\delta\), so \(\delta=0\) gives a division-by-zero singularity as typeset; if the intended standard form is \(\arctan((l_r/(\gamma l))\tan\delta)\), then \(\delta=\pm\pi/2\) makes \(\tan\delta\) unbounded and drives \(\beta\to\pm\pi/2\). Also \(l=0\), \(\gamma=0\), or \(l_r=0\) are singular or degenerate in the Bicycle equations; \(l_r=0\) appears directly in the denominator of \(\omega\) for Bicycle. The gIoU formulas break down when \(V_{3dhull}=0\) or \(A_{bevhull}=0\), i.e., degenerate zero-volume or zero-area hulls, and they become numerically unstable for near-degenerate boxes. The EKF linearization is weak when \(\Delta t\) is large relative to turn rate or acceleration changes; the paper’s own warning is that 2 Hz keyframes create “significant inter-frame displacement” [PAGE 6]. The association thresholds are also a gray zone: the paper reports θ_fm values above 1 while gIoU is bounded, so the unstated cost transform must be resolved before the threshold semantics are reproducible. The deployed configuration is: “We employ Bicycle model with γ = 0.8 for (bicycle, motorcycle) and CTRA model for the remaining categories. The similarity metric for bus and (bicycle, motorcycle, car, trailer, truck, pedestrian) are gIoUbev and gIoU3d, respectively” [PAGE 6]. It also reports “θfm = (1.6, 1.4, 1.3, 1.3, 1.3, 1.2, 1.7) and max-age = (10, 20, 10, 15, 10, 20, 10) for bicycle, motorcycle, bus, car, trailer, truck, pedestrian and θsm = 1 for all seven categories” [PAGE 6]. Since NuScenes keyframes are 2 Hz, one frame is 0.5 s; max-age 10 frames is 5 s, 20 frames is 10 s, and 15 frames is 7.5 s, assuming keyframe-indexed lifecycle counting.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal genealogy runs from assignment and filtering theory to modern 3D MOT. Kuhn’s Hungarian method [PAGE 8, ref. 15] supplies the one-to-one assignment solver; the Kalman/EKF lineage supplies recursive state estimation; vehicle dynamics supplies the kinematic bicycle model and the CTRA turn-rate/acceleration model; Weng et al.’s 2020 TBD baseline supplies the modern 3D MOT skeleton with a Linear Kalman Filter and 3D IoU [PAGE 8, ref. 3]; CenterPoint supplies center-based 3D detection and a regression branch for inter-frame displacement [PAGE 2, ref. 8]; SimpleTrack supplies the four-step TBD decomposition and simple baseline improvements [PAGE 2, ref. 9]; EagerMOT supplies result-level 2D/3D fusion [PAGE 2, ref. 4]; CAMO-MOT supplies combined appearance–motion optimization and is the closest multi-modal competitor in Table II [PAGE 5, ref. 5]. Poly-MOT’s retrocausal claim is that earlier single-model/single-metric trackers were not merely under-tuned; they were structurally mismatched to multi-category data. The paper states the old habit directly: “Most previous methods [5], [9] employ a uniform CA or CV model to predict the trajectories of all objects,” and these models “assume that the movements of objects on each coordinate axis are independent, ignoring nonlinear motion patterns constrained by geometry and differences in motion patterns across categories” [PAGE 3]. Poly-MOT reframes that history: category-conditioned CTRA/Bicycle models and category-conditioned metrics are the missing variables.

Multi-week traps are the failure modes that appear after the first successful demo. Trap 1: validation-set threshold overfitting. The paper says “Hyperparameters are chosen based on the best AMOTA identified in the validation set” and “θSF is detector-specific” [PAGE 6]. Over weeks, θ_fm, max-age, and θSF can drift toward one detector’s calibration; the paper’s own Table V shows NMS+SF at 73.1 AMOTA and 0.055 s versus NMS-only at 71.8 AMOTA and 0.093 s, so preprocessing choices are not cosmetic [PAGE 7]. Trap 2: keyframe aliasing. NuScenes keyframes are 2 Hz, and the paper warns of “significant inter-frame displacement” [PAGE 6]; a motion model tuned on 0.5 s gaps may fail on 10 Hz LiDAR or on high-speed objects. Trap 3: category imbalance and metric coupling. Table I shows Ped and Bus have different best metrics, but the numerical gaps are small for Bus (88.2 vs 88.1 AMOTA) and larger for Ped (81.7 vs 81.2 AMOTA; IDS 175 vs 203) [PAGE 2]. A short experiment can wrongly conclude that metric choice is irrelevant because the aggregate is dominated by Car. Trap 4: interaction effects. “Os+Pre+Mo” improves AMOTA by +0.5% over “Os+Pre” but adds +69 IDS; only after the association module does the full system reach 73.1 AMOTA and 281 IDS [PAGE 7]. A week-long ablation that stops after the motion module can reject the correct module. Trap 5: runtime. The system runs at 3 FPS on Intel 9940X without GPU; SimpleTrack is 0.51 FPS and Minkowski Tracker 1.7 FPS [PAGE 6]. The half-parallel gIoU operator is still serial in convex-hull and rotation-IoU calculations [PAGE 6]. A deployment that assumes 10–30 FPS will fail even if AMOTA is strong.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In Poly-MOT, “theory of mind” is not anthropomorphic mental-state inference; it is a recursive, category-conditioned belief about how another object will move. The tracker predicts \(T_{t,t-1}\), then updates that belief with detections \(D_t\), then uses the residual mismatch to decide whether the object is a bicycle-like rigid body or a car-like CTRA body. The recursion appears in the two-stage association: the first stage forms a belief about which detections belong to which trajectories under the category-specific metric; the unmatched sets \(D_t^{1u}\) and \(T_{t-1}^{1u}\) are then re-evaluated under a different metric with a strict threshold [PAGE 5]. The system is recursively modeling its own failure: it assumes the first metric may have missed a true pair, so it runs a second, stricter pass. The paper states that the second stage uses a “distinct metric than before and a strict threshold” in the pipeline description [PAGE 3] and that “the core of multi-stage association is to use different metrics to perform repeated associations” [PAGE 5].

Crisis habit regression is the fallback under occlusion or detector miss. When detections vanish, a tracker tends to regress to the old habit: constant velocity/constant acceleration extrapolation and a single metric. Poly-MOT’s answer is not to abolish fallback but to condition it: CTRA for car-like and pedestrian categories, Bicycle for bicycle and motorcycle categories, with the Bicycle model retaining rigid structure through \(\beta\) and \(l_r\) [PAGE 3–4]. The paper explicitly warns that Bicycle is “susceptible to erroneous predictions caused by incorrect object structure information” and is “unsuitable for object categories where detectors tend to produce inaccurate detections” [PAGE 4]. That is the counter-trap: the more structured model is not universally safer; it is only safer when the detector’s size/structure estimate is reliable. The clock is the lifecycle and sampling clock. NuScenes keyframes are 2 Hz [PAGE 6]; max-age controls death at 10, 20, 10, 15, 10, 20, and 10 frames for bicycle, motorcycle, bus, car, trailer, truck, pedestrian [PAGE 6]; hitmin = 0, αpun = 0.05, Npun = 1, and output NMS θnms = 0.08 [PAGE 6]. These are clocked commitments: a trajectory that is not updated for max-age frames is discarded; a tentative trajectory that is hit for hitmin frames becomes active; a penalized trajectory is output for only Npun frames. The recursive theory-of-mind failure mode is clock mismatch: if the object’s real maneuver changes faster than the keyframe clock, the tracker’s belief lags; if max-age is too long, stale beliefs survive; if too short, occlusion causes premature death. The paper’s own evidence for this tension is the 2 Hz warning and the motion-model/association interaction that changes IDS [PAGE 6–7].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is a proof obligation: before a category-specific model or metric is allowed to govern association, three layers must not veto it. Layer 1, detector/preprocessing, vetoes low-quality boxes. The paper applies Score Filter before NMS to remove low-confidence detections and then NMS with IoUbev to remove duplicates; θSF is detector-specific and θnms = 0.08 [PAGE 6]. Table V is the quantitative veto: NMS+SF gives 73.1 AMOTA, 281 IDS, 0.055 s; NMS only gives 71.8 AMOTA, 320 IDS, 0.093 s; SF only gives 68.6 AMOTA, 354 IDS, 0.008 s [PAGE 7]. So removing SF vetoes the accuracy/runtime claim by −1.3 AMOTA and +39 IDS relative to NMS+SF, and removing NMS vetoes it far more severely. Layer 2, physical/rigid-body, vetoes Bicycle when the object is not wheeled or when the detector’s structure estimate is unreliable. The paper’s exact veto is: “Bicycle Model is susceptible to erroneous predictions caused by incorrect object structure information, thereby rendering it unsuitable for object categories where detectors tend to produce inaccurate detections” [PAGE 4]. Thus Bicycle is admissible only for bicycle/motorcycle-like categories with credible \(l\), \(l_r\), \(\gamma\), and \(\delta\); CTRA is admissible for car-like and pedestrian categories because “the directions of the heading angle θ, velocity v, and acceleration a of the object are on the same straight line” [PAGE 3]. Layer 3, association-metric, vetoes a metric when category geometry makes it unreliable. Table I shows Ped prefers gIoU3d (81.7 AMOTA, 175 IDS) over gIoUbev (81.2, 203) and deucl (81.0, 220); Bus prefers gIoUbev (88.2, 2) over gIoU3d (88.1, 2) and deucl (87.5, 1) [PAGE 2]. The metric veto is not “one metric is always better”; it is “the metric must be selected per category and the threshold must be category-specific.” The paper’s deployment follows that: bus uses gIoUbev; bicycle, motorcycle, car, trailer, truck, pedestrian use gIoU3d [PAGE 6].

Biomechanical veto: the paper does not use a biomechanical pedestrian model. It assigns pedestrian to CTRA, which is a rigid-body-like turn-rate/acceleration model, not a articulated gait model. That is a modeling choice, not a proof that CTRA captures human biomechanics. The veto condition is therefore: if pedestrian motion is dominated by lateral stepping, rotation in place, or non-rigid limb motion at the keyframe scale, CTRA’s “heading angle, velocity, and acceleration on the same straight line” assumption [PAGE 3] is physically weak. Weather veto: NuScenes includes “rainy days and nights” [PAGE 6], and Poly-MOT is LiDAR-only in the reported configuration [PAGE 5–6]. Rain and night can degrade LiDAR returns and detector size estimates; because Bicycle depends on size/structure, weather degradation can veto Bicycle even when the category label is correct. The paper does not report a weather-stratified ablation, so the weather veto is not empirically discharged; it remains an open cross-layer risk. A formal veto predicate can be written as
\[
\text{admit}(m,c)=\mathbf{1}\{\text{detector-quality}(c)\ge q_0\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\text{structure-error}(c)\le e_0\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\text{clock}(\sigma)\le \sigma_0\},
\]
where \(m\) is the motion model, \(c\) is the category, \(q_0\) is a detector-quality threshold, \(e_0\) is a structure-error threshold, and \(\sigma_0\) is a maximum keyframe interval. The paper supplies qualitative values for the middle term (“unsuitable” for inaccurate detections [PAGE 4]) and a concrete clock value (2 Hz [PAGE 6]), but not numerical \(q_0\) or \(e_0\). That missing calibration is the cross-layer adjudication gap: the system’s category policy is physically motivated but not fully instrumented as a per-object veto.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR-style verification here means a counterfactual prediction with a binary reward: 1 if the stated numeric direction holds on the same NuScenes validation protocol, 0 otherwise. Three verifiable veto scenarios follow.

1. **Motion-model swap veto for Bicycle categories.** Counterfactual: replace Bicycle with CTRA for bicycle and motorcycle while holding detector, preprocessing, association, and lifecycle fixed. Prediction from Table VI: Bicycle gives Bic 57.1 AMOTA, 0 IDS, 227 FP, 765 FN; CTRA gives Bic 55.4 AMOTA, 0 IDS, 256 FP, 747 FN. For Moto, Bicycle gives 77.0 AMOTA, 1 IDS, 121 FP, 464 FN; CTRA gives 73.6 AMOTA, 4 IDS, 154 FP, 537 FN [PAGE 7]. Veto rule: if CTRA does not reduce AMOTA by at least 1.0 point on Moto or does not increase IDS, the Bicycle-specific claim is not verified. This is a clean RLVR test because the paper already reports the exact counterfactual table.

2. **Second-stage association removal veto.** Counterfactual: remove the second association stage, i.e., compare full “Os+Pre+Mo+Ass” against “Os+Pre+Mo.” Prediction: full is 73.1 AMOTA and 281 IDS; without Ass it is 71.9 AMOTA and 443 IDS [PAGE 7]. Veto rule: if removing the second stage does not drop AMOTA by at least 1.0 point or does not raise IDS by at least 100, the claimed benefit of “a two-stage data association strategy” [PAGE 1] is not verified. The paper’s own pipeline says the second stage uses “a distinct metric than before and a strict threshold” [PAGE 3], so the counterfactual also checks the metric-diversity claim.

3. **Score-Filter removal veto.** Counterfactual: remove SF and run NMS only, holding detector and association fixed. Prediction from Table V: NMS+SF is 73.1 AMOTA, 281 IDS, 0.055 s; NMS only is 71.8 AMOTA, 320 IDS, 0.093 s [PAGE 7]. Veto rule: if NMS-only is not worse in AMOTA by at least 1.0 point or not worse in IDS or runtime, the preprocessing claim is not verified. This scenario is especially diagnostic because the paper claims SF “can efficiently remove apparent false-positive detections, improving the inference speed of the algorithm” [PAGE 3]. The verifiable prediction is therefore: removing SF should cost roughly 1.3 AMOTA, add roughly 39 IDS, and increase preprocessing runtime from 0.055 s to 0.093 s. If those directions fail, the cross-layer preprocessing veto is false.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
