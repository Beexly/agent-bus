This historical analysis aligns with modern sensorimotor integration theory. Proprioception arises from multiple receptors—muscle spindles encode length and velocity, Golgi tendon organs signal tension, while cutaneous mechanoreceptors provide joint-angle data. The raw chemical/mechanical signals from muscles themselves lack linear scaling to displacement; they reflect fatigue or strain rather than exact geometry. James’s qualitative observations anticipate the concept of **non-linear sensory encoding** where peripheral transduction must be decoded via learned priors.

Let \(X\) denote the true limb position and \(Y\) the neural response vector from muscular afferents. The mutual information \(I(X;Y)\) quantifies how much uncertainty about \(X\) is reduced by observing \(Y\). If the mapping \(f: X \to Y\) is many-to-one (i.e., different positions produce similar chemical/pressure patterns), then \(I(X;Y)\) saturates at low values, creating an **information bottleneck**.

Furthermore, Bayesian brain hypotheses suggest the central nervous system employs prior distributions over expected trajectories and uses likelihood functions derived from noisy proprioceptive inputs. When muscular signals are highly ambiguous (low Fisher information), posterior estimates rely heavily on priors, potentially leading to systematic biases if the environment changes unexpectedly. This explains why athletes often misjudge distances after prolonged exertion—muscle fatigue alters the stimulus-response function, degrading the reliability of efference copies.

Crucially, the cited passage reveals a deeper epistemological issue: the illusion of direct access to bodily state. What we perceive as “muscle movement” is actually a constructed representation built from multimodal cues, not a veridical readout of contractile events. This has profound implications for understanding agency and self-awareness—our sense of controlling limbs may be post-hoc inference rather than real-time monitoring.

In terms of predictive coding frameworks, the brain continuously generates top-down predictions about expected sensory consequences of motor commands. Mismatches between predicted and actual afferent feedback generate prediction errors used to update internal models. This could explain why skilled movements feel effortless—prediction errors are minimized through extensive training, reducing conscious awareness of muscular effort.

Finally, considering embodied cognition theories, the body schema is not merely a static map but a dynamic process shaped by interaction with the world. The fact that diaphragmatic movements escape conscious detection suggests that only salient deviations from baseline activity enter awareness—a filtering mechanism conserving cognitive resources. Thus, James’s early insights prefigure contemporary views on hierarchical processing and sparse coding in perceptual systems.
