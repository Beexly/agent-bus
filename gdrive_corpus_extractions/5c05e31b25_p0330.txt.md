<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage begins with Carl Menger’s subjective value theory (1871), which separated marginal utility from cost-of-production, but ignored temporal uncertainty. John Maynard Keynes’ *A Treatise on Probability* (1921) contemporaneously argued that many economic decisions lack numerical probabilities. Knight synthesized these in *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* (1921), creating the first systematic theory of entrepreneurial profit as reward for bearing unmeasurable uncertainty. This directly influenced Joseph Schumpeter’s “creative destruction” (1942): innovation requires overcoming both technical and market unknowns, with the latter being decisive. 

In the 1970s, Robert Lucas criticized Keynesian macro for ignoring rational expectations, inadvertently reviving Knightian ideas—rational agents face irreducible uncertainty. Later, Amilcar Getzler and Duncan Watts applied network science to show how information cascades amplify price volatility beyond fundamental values. The **multi-week trap** manifests when analysts use Monte Carlo simulations assuming Gaussian distributions for asset returns, thereby converting Knightian uncertainty into measurable risk—an ontological category error. Historical precedent: the 2008 crisis was modeled as low-probability tail risk, but it emerged from correlated uncertainties in mortgage-backed securities valuation that were inherently unquantifiable until they crystallized.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

3.  **Socio-Political Veto**: Supply chain optimizations assume stable trade policies. These vetoes demonstrate that uncertainty is layered: epistemic (knowledge gaps), aleatoric (stochastic variation), and agential (purposeful action). Knight focused on agential uncertainty in markets, but modern systems must account for cascading failures across layers.
