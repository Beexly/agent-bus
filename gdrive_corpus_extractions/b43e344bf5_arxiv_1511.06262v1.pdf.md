<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a statistical forecasting paper, not a physical or biomechanical telemetry paper, so the admissible evidence is limited to accumulated league points, round indices, rank permutations, bootstrap intervals, and true-positive rates. The core multi-hop derivation is: (1) a team’s season is represented as a time series \(T_i\) of accumulated points after round \(i\); (2) a linear model \(L_{t_s}\) is trained on the first \(n-t_s\) rounds; (3) the model is extrapolated to round \(n\) and floored to produce \(\hat T_n\); (4) the point error is \(|\hat T_n-T_n|\); (5) at the championship level, the predicted final table is compared with the actual table through a normalized permutation displacement \(d\); (6) the null distribution of \(d\) is computed over the symmetric group \(S_n\); and (7) true-positive rates for top-\(k\) and bottom-\(k\) teams are counted over 425 championships. The paper’s central claim is deliberately modest: “Here we show that, despite the large number of confounding factors, the results of a football team in longer competitions (e.g., a national league) follow a basically linear trend useful for predictive purposes, too.” [PAGE 1]. That is not a claim that individual matches are predictable. The same page says the authors want “to demonstrate that, despite the existing randomness and other confounding factors, there are situations where the sport results are driven by very simple (for instance, linear) trends” [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit must therefore separate global smoothing from local mechanism.

The first gray-zone issue is that the linear extrapolator is a low-variance smoother, not a causal model. The paper reports that “a null model obtained by applying a linear regressor to 105 randomly generated time series of match results yields | ¯Tn −Tn| = 4.993 with confidence interval (4.666, 5.303), indicating that, globally, the considered real sequences are only slightly more structured than random” [PAGE 7]. The real-series linear mean is 4.652 with interval (4.634, 4.672) [PAGE 6]. Those intervals overlap on approximately [4.666, 4.672], so the improvement over random cumulative sequences is real but small. A second gray-zone issue is the parity formula for the expected normalized rank distance. The printed expression is \(\frac{n^2-1}{3\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor}=\frac23-\frac{2}{3n^2}(n\bmod 2)\), while the surrounding prose says “which is 2/3 for odd n’s and 2/3 −εn for even n’s, with εn positive, decreasing to 0 and smaller than 0.0¯6 for n ≥10” [PAGE 5]. Direct evaluation shows that the prose is correct and the printed parity factor is inverted: for odd \(n\), \(\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor=(n^2-1)/2\), so the expectation is exactly \(2/3\); for even \(n\), it is \(2/3-2/(3n^2)\). For \(n=20\), the correct value is \(0.665\), not \(0.666\ldots\). This is a concrete audit point, not a reason to reject the empirical results.

A third gray-zone issue is heterogeneity. The paper reports that for \(t_s=10\) the middle five positions have slightly larger error, \(4.54\ (4.39,4.67)\), than the top five, \(4.32\ (4.19,4.46)\), or bottom five, \(4.21\ (4.07,4.35)\) [PAGE 8]. It also reports that “predictions for k = 3 is slighly noisier than k = 6, while in both cases predicting the bottom of the table is slighlty harder than guessing the top teams” [PAGE 10]. Thus the linear operator is not uniformly reliable across table zones. A fourth gray-zone issue is the Barcelona change point: “the seasonal pattern is not linear, but it is piecewise linear, with the first and second half of the campaing following two distinct linear approximations whose corresponding slopes are respectively 1.29 and 2.52, thus in ratio almost 1:2” [PAGE 8]. That is the exact breakdown regime: the single-slope linear model is misspecified when a team’s point-accrual rate shifts mid-season. The global average can still look good because most team-seasons do not have a large enough change point to dominate the mean.

Let \(T_i\in\mathbb{N}_0\) be the accumulated league points after round \(i\), with unit “league points” (a dimensionless count; one point is one league point). Let \(n\) be the total number of rounds and \(t_s\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\) the test horizon in rounds. The model \(L_{t_s}\) is trained on \(\{(1,T_1),\ldots,(n-t_s,T_{n-t_s})\}\). The source defines: “let T be a team partecipating in a league whose season consists of n rounds, and let Ti be the number of points earned by T after the i-th round, so that Tn is the total number of points at the end of season.” [PAGE 2]. It then states: “Let ts an integer between 1 and n −1, and let Lts T be a model trained on (1, T1), . . . , (n −ts, Tn−ts).” [PAGE 2]. The prediction is \(\hat T_n=\lfloor L_{t_s}(n)\rfloor\), quoted as “Define then ¯Tn = ⌊Lts T (n)⌋” [PAGE 2]. The point error is
\[
\Delta_T=\left|\hat T_n-T_n\right| \quad \text{league points}.
\]
The paper’s aggregate means are \(4.652\) points, CI \((4.634,4.672)\), for linear; \(8.966\), CI \((8.913,9.014)\), for quadratic; and \(27.760\), CI \((27.530,28.011)\), for cubic, with a paired test giving “p-value less than 10−16” [PAGE 6]. At \(t_s=10\), “the average error is limited to 4.4 points for championships of any duration” [PAGE 7].

Ranking error is a permutation displacement. For standings \(R,S\) on teams \(z_1,\ldots,z_n\), with ranking map \(rk_S\) and permutation \(\tau_S\),
\[
D(R,S)=\sum_{i=1}^n |rk_R(z_i)-rk_S(z_i)|=\sum_{i=1}^n |\tau_R(i)-\tau_S(i)|,
\]
with units of table positions. The maximum is \(\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor\), so the normalized distance is
\[
d(R,S)=\frac{D(R,S)}{\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor}\in[0,1],
\]
dimensionless. The source states the maximum and normalization on [PAGE 4]. Under a random standing,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{S_n}(d)=\frac{n^2-1}{3\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor}
=\frac23-\frac{2}{3n^2}(n\bmod 2)
\]
as printed, but the prose says “which is 2/3 for odd n’s and 2/3 −εn for even n’s” [PAGE 5]. The audit correction is
\[
\mathbb{E}_{S_n}(d)=
\begin{cases}
\frac23, & n \text{ odd},\\[2mm]
\frac23-\frac{2}{3n^2}, & n \text{ even}.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus for \(n=20\), \(\mathbb{E}=0.665\); for \(n=19\), \(\mathbb{E}=0.666666\ldots\). The paper’s empirical value at \(t_s=10\) is \(\bar d=0.1874\), which “means that, in average, the linear model can guess the final ranking of each team with an error of 1.874 positions” for a 20-team tournament [PAGE 9].

The top/bottom detection metric is a probability:
\[
\mathrm{TPR}_k=\frac{\#\{\text{championships with all top-}k\text{ or bottom-}k\text{ teams correct}\}}{425}\in[0,1].
\]
The source reports “for ts < 10, the TPR is larger than 0.9 for all cases” [PAGE 10]. At \(t_s=10\), Table 7 gives \(T3=405/425=0.953\) and \(B3=379/425=0.892\) [PAGE 11]. No bits or nats are used in the source; the only information-like unit is the probability \(\mathrm{TPR}\).

Breakdown regimes: if \(m=n-t_s\le 1\), the OLS slope denominator \(\sum_{i=1}^m(i-\bar i)^2=m(m^2-1)/12\) is zero, so the slope is unidentifiable. A cubic model requires at least four distinct training rounds; otherwise its design matrix is singular. If \(n=1\), \(d\) is undefined because \(\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor=0\). If all \(T_i\) are equal, the slope is zero and the model predicts a constant, not a singularity but a degenerate fit. The most important breakdown is a change point: a single slope is biased when the point-accrual rate changes, as in the Barcelona case.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit: the paper places itself in the line of league-level forecasting with linear regression, citing Rue and Salvesen, Goddard, Figueiredo et al., and Ellis [PAGE 13], and it uses the permutation-displacement maximum from Mitchell [PAGE 13]. The bootstrap confidence intervals follow Efron and Davison and Hinkley [PAGE 13]. The data lineage is the Football-Data repository, with 425 European national championships over 1993/94–2013/14, 22 divisions, 707 unique teams, and 7768 series [PAGE 1–2]. The “retrocausal” trap is look-ahead leakage: a valid test must train only on rounds \(1,\ldots,n-t_s\) and evaluate on \(n-t_s+1,\ldots,n\). The source’s method does exactly that: \(L_{t_s}\) is “trained on (1, T1), . . . , (n −ts, Tn−ts)” [PAGE 2]. Any pipeline that normalizes by the full-season mean, selects \(t_s\) by looking at final standings, or uses team identity learned from future seasons is retrocausally contaminated.

Multi-week traps: first, early-season tables are small-sample objects; the linear slope from the first few rounds has high variance. Second, choosing \(t_s\) after seeing which horizon looks best on the same 425 championships is selection bias; the paper instead reports the whole curve for \(t_s=1,\ldots,20\) [PAGE 9–10]. Third, league size and round count vary: the paper studies \(n=26,28,30,32,34,36,38,42,44,46\) [PAGE 3], so normalization by \(\lfloor n^2/2\rfloor\) is necessary; raw displacement \(D\) is not comparable across leagues. Fourth, promotion/relegation and roster turnover change the data-generating process between seasons; the paper’s season-by-season training avoids cross-season leakage but does not model structural breaks. Fifth, mid-season managerial changes, injuries, fixture congestion, and weather can create change points; the Barcelona example is the canonical local break [PAGE 8]. Sixth, the null model is close to the real model in aggregate: random cumulative series give \(4.993\) points versus \(4.652\) for real series [PAGE 6–7], so any claimed “football is linear” mechanism must be stated as a smoothing baseline, not as a deterministic law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive-theory-of-mind layer is about nested beliefs: the forecaster forms a belief \(\hat B_t\) about a team’s final points and table position; the team’s coaches and players form a belief \(\mu_t\) about their own situation; and their strategy may depend on both. The linear model assumes an exogenous, stationary point rate \(\beta\). A crisis-habit regression occurs when a team in relegation danger or in a title race changes its risk posture, so the observed slope after the crisis differs from the slope before it. The source gives direct evidence of such a regime shift: “the seasonal pattern is not linear, but it is piecewise linear, with the first and second half of the campaing following two distinct linear approximations whose corresponding slopes are respectively 1.29 and 2.52, thus in ratio almost 1:2” [PAGE 8]. That is a crisis/adaptation signature: the second-half slope is almost double the first-half slope, so a single-slope forecaster trained on the first half will under-predict the final total.

The counter-trap is that the forecaster may over-interpret the early slope and thereby over-commit to a team’s current habit. If the team then adapts, the forecast is stale. The source’s middle-table result is consistent with motivational ambiguity: the middle five positions have slightly larger error, \(4.54\ (4.39,4.67)\), than the top or bottom groups [PAGE 8]. Those teams have less clear crisis pressure, so their future point rate is less anchored. The clock is \(t_s\): a small \(t_s\) gives a long training window but a long extrapolation; a large \(t_s\) gives a short training window and a short extrapolation. The paper reports the full \(t_s\) curve and notes that “in 25 cases (with ts ≤7), the actual final ranking was perfectly predicted by the linear model” [PAGE 9]. The recursive lesson is that the forecaster’s belief should be conditioned on the clock and on detectable change points, not on a fixed faith in linearity. When the team’s own belief about its position changes its behavior, the model’s \(\beta\) becomes endogenous; the source’s aggregate success suggests this feedback is weak on average, but the Barcelona case shows it can dominate locally.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical, biomechanical, or weather veto is admissible only if it produces a measurable change in the point-accrual process. Formalize the season as \(T_i=\sum_{j=1}^i X_j\), where \(X_j\in\{0,1,3\}\) is the league-point yield of round \(j\). The linear model is \(T_i=a+bi+\epsilon_i\). A cross-layer factor \(V_j\) — travel load, injury cluster, heat, pitch condition, fixture congestion — is a veto if \(\mathbb{E}[X_j\mid V_j,j]\neq \mathbb{E}[X_j\mid j]\) and the induced shift in \(b\) is large enough to exceed the model’s error budget. The null of no veto is a constant slope. The Barcelona case rejects that null locally: the reported slopes are \(1.29\) and \(2.52\), “thus in ratio almost 1:2” [PAGE 8]. Using the first-half slope to extrapolate to round 38 gives approximately \(1.29\times38=49.0\) points, whereas the plotted final total is 72; that is an under-prediction of about 23 points. A piecewise model with a second-half slope of \(2.52\) gives approximately \(24.5+2.52\times19=72.4\) points. This is a concrete proof that a localized regime shift can veto a single-slope forecast.

The global veto, however, is not supported by the source. If weather or biomechanics caused large, ubiquitous slope changes, the aggregate error would not be \(4.4\) points at \(t_s=10\) [PAGE 7], and the top/bottom hit rate would not exceed \(0.9\) for \(t_s<10\) [PAGE 10]. Therefore the cross-layer veto is local, not global: it must be tied to a change-point test, an injury/weather covariate, or a residual pattern that a piecewise model improves. A weather event that postpones matches does not by itself break the model, because the source’s clock is the round index, not the calendar date. Only if the event changes the points earned per round does it enter the veto calculus. Untestable narratives about fatigue or weather are therefore rejected as confounds; they are not proof of model failure.
