In multimodal RAG, failure modes such as retrieval miss and context conflict require provenance and validation [PAGE 203]. In expert systems, rule traceability supports auditability [PAGE 147]. The clock is the temporal constraint. Engineering control often has a deadline \(T_{\text{deadline}}\), and the total decision latency is
\[
T_{\text{total}}
=
T_{\text{sense}}
+
T_{\text{retrieve}}
+
T_{\text{infer}}
+
T_{\text{verify}}
+
T_{\text{act}}.
\]
All terms are measured in seconds. If
\[
T_{\text{total}} > T_{\text{deadline}},
\]
the architecture must either simplify the model, precompute a fallback policy, or escalate to a faster deterministic controller. The source notes that real-time constraints are central in edge deployment and industrial systems [PAGE 222] [PAGE 213]. It also notes that meta-learning and adaptive control must operate under time pressure in robotics and physical systems [PAGE 110].

The recursive equilibrium is therefore not “maximum autonomy.” It is a negotiated equilibrium where the AI predicts human regression, the interface counters that regression with transparent evidence, and the clock forces a safe default when verification cannot complete in time. If the model cannot explain its uncertainty within the available time, the safe action is to reduce autonomy, request human confirmation, or invoke a rule-based fallback. The source supports this through the combination of transparent expert reasoning [PAGE 146], LLM checkpointing [PAGE 179], multimodal provenance [PAGE 203], and edge latency awareness [PAGE 222].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is required because predictive accuracy alone cannot certify physical safety. The source states that many deep architectures “operate purely as statistical data-driven approximators” and can produce unphysical anomalies when evaluated on out-of-distribution edge cases [PAGE 25]. PINNs reduce this risk by embedding governing equations into the objective [PAGE 23], but even PINNs can fail if boundary conditions, stiffness, or collocation coverage are inadequate.

Let \(a\) be a proposed action or prediction. Define the acceptance predicate:
\[
\operatorname{Accept}(a)
\iff
\begin{cases}
\left\|\mathcal{N}[u_\theta]\right\| \leq \varepsilon_{\text{phys}}, \\
\tau_j \leq \tau_{j,\max}, \quad
|\dot{q}_j| \leq \dot{q}_{j,\max}, \\
D_{\text{env}}(z_{\text{test}}, z_{\text{cal}}) \leq \varepsilon_{\text{env}}, \\
T_{\text{total}} \leq T_{\text{deadline}}, \\
\operatorname{Provenance}(r) = \text{verified}.
\end{cases}
\]
If any condition fails, the output is vetoed regardless of model confidence.

Physical veto proof.
Suppose a model predicts a state \(u_\theta\) for a fluid, thermal, or structural process. If the governing operator is \(\mathcal{N}\), physical admissibility requires
\[
\mathcal{N}[u_\theta] \approx 0
\]
within tolerance \(\varepsilon_{\text{phys}}\) in the appropriate SI units. If the residual exceeds the tolerance, the prediction may violate conservation of mass, momentum, or energy. The source states that PINNs embed such equations to avoid unphysical anomalies [PAGE 23] [PAGE 25]. Therefore, if a non-physical model outputs a high-confidence but residual-violating state, the physical layer must veto it.

Biomechanical or actuator veto proof.
If the AI output controls a robot, exoskeleton, valve, motor, or human-assist device, the action must satisfy actuator and biomechanical constraints. For joint \(j\), torque \(\tau_j\), velocity \(\dot{q}_j\), and power \(P_j\) must satisfy
\[
\tau_j \leq \tau_{j,\max}, \qquad
|\dot{q}_j| \leq \dot{q}_{j,\max}, \qquad
|P_j| \leq P_{j,\max}.
\]
These constraints are in SI units: newton-meters, radians per second, and watts. If a statistically plausible action exceeds these bounds, it is unsafe. The source’s emphasis on human oversight and checkpointing supports this veto because irreversible physical actions require approval [PAGE 179]. A model that ignores actuator limits is not merely inaccurate; it is unsafe.

Weather and environmental veto proof.
Engineering sensors and models are calibrated for environmental envelopes. Let \(z_{\text{env}}\) include temperature, humidity, wind speed, vibration baseline, precipitation, or electromagnetic interference. If the test environment lies outside the calibration distribution, the error bound can increase sharply. A simple covariate-shift diagnostic is
\[
D_{\text{env}}(z_{\text{test}}, z_{\text{cal}})
=
\left\|
\Sigma_{\text{cal}}^{-1/2}
\left(
z_{\text{test}} - \mu_{\text{cal}}
\right)
\right\|_2,
\]
where \(\mu_{\text{cal}}\) and \(\Sigma_{\text{cal}}\) are calibration mean and covariance. If this distance exceeds a threshold, the model is operating outside validated conditions. The source warns that multimodal and retrieval systems suffer modality gaps, outdated knowledge, and context conflicts [PAGE 203], and that synthetic or generated data can be invalid if domain consistency is not preserved [PAGE 121]. Therefore, weather or environmental excursion must trigger a veto or a fallback to conservative rules.

Adjudication rule.
The final adjudication is strict: physical residual, actuator safety, environmental validity, provenance, and latency are hard constraints. Statistical confidence is not a substitute for constraint satisfaction. If a model has high validation accuracy but violates any hard constraint, the output is rejected. This follows directly from the source’s distinction between data-driven approximation and physical compliance [PAGE 25].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR-style counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable because each defines an intervention, an observable trigger, and a predicted outcome. RLVR here means a reinforcement-learning or policy-evaluation setting where rewards are tied to verifiable engineering constraints, not to fluency or confidence alone.

Veto Scenario 1: Sensor dropout or retrieval miss.
Intervention: remove one or more critical sensors, or remove the relevant document from the retrieval corpus.
Trigger: missing modality fraction exceeds threshold \(m_{\text{crit}}\), or retrieval confidence falls below \(\tau_{\text{ret}}\). Formally, if
\[
\frac{m_{\text{missing}}}{m_{\text{total}}} > m_{\text{crit}}
\]
or
\[
\operatorname{Precision}@k < \tau_{\text{ret}},
\]
the system must not issue a high-confidence engineering recommendation.
Prediction: a well-designed system should reduce confidence, request missing data, or switch to an expert-system fallback. A poorly designed system will maintain confidence and produce a plausible but unsupported answer.
Verification: inject sensor dropout or remove documents, then measure confidence, fallback rate, and error rate. The source supports this because RAG failure includes “Relevant information is not retrieved” [PAGE 203], and multimodal systems can misinterpret conditions when modalities are not aligned [PAGE 203].
Veto decision: if the policy continues high-confidence action after critical sensor loss or retrieval miss, veto the action.

Veto Scenario 2: Physical residual violation under boundary-condition shift.
Intervention: change boundary conditions, load, flow rate, temperature gradient, or structural support beyond the training distribution.
Trigger: the PDE residual
\[
r_{\text{pde}}
=
\left\|\mathcal{N}[u_\theta]\right\|
\]
exceeds \(\varepsilon_{\text{phys}}\) in SI units.
Prediction: a PINN or physics-constrained model should show increasing residual as the system moves outside the training envelope. If the model still outputs a high-confidence control action while residual is high, it is violating the physical layer.
Verification: compare predicted states against a high-fidelity solver or physical conservation checks. The source states that PINNs are used to enforce physical compliance and that non-PINN models may generate unphysical anomalies [PAGE 25].
Veto decision: if residual exceeds tolerance, veto the action even if the data-driven loss is low.

Veto Scenario 3: Weather or latency envelope violation.
Intervention: place the system in an environmental condition outside calibration, such as temperature below the rated minimum, high humidity, wind gusts, or vibration beyond baseline; alternatively, inject network or compute latency.
Trigger: environmental distance
\[
D_{\text{env}}(z_{\text{test}}, z_{\text{cal}}) > \varepsilon_{\text{env}}
\]
or total latency
\[
T_{\text{total}} > T_{\text{deadline}}.
\]
Prediction: model error should increase, uncertainty should widen, and latency should rise. If the system continues to act as if nominal, it is unsafe.
Verification: use environmental chamber tests, hardware-in-the-loop simulation, or latency tracing. The source emphasizes edge constraints and practical deployability [PAGE 6] [PAGE 222], and warns that multimodal systems can fail when data are stale or misaligned [PAGE 203].
Veto decision: if environmental validity or latency validity fails, veto autonomous action and switch to conservative control or human approval.

These three vetoes are counterfactual because they ask what would happen under a specific failure intervention. They are verifiable because the trigger variables are measurable: missing modality fraction, retrieval precision, PDE residual, environmental distance, and latency. They are predictive because the expected behavior is known: confidence should decrease, fallback should activate, and unsafe actions should be blocked. If the system fails to exhibit these behaviors, the RLVR evaluation should assign a negative reward and reject deployment.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
