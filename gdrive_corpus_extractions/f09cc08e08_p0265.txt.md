<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from Haar's 1933 existence theorem for invariant measures on locally compact groups, through Weil's 1940 abstract integration and the Bourbaki formalization, to Halmos's 1950 Measure Theory [PAGE 250], where Chapter XI isolates the topological prerequisites. The notion of a full subgroup is not measure-theoretic in origin; it is a topological stepping stone that allows one to pass from a subgroup with a single interior point to an open subgroup, and thence to a discrete quotient. Von Neumann's work on almost periodic functions and Banach's earlier invariant means anticipate the concern, voiced on [PAGE 250], with excluding "measure theoretic pathology of the infinite."

Multi-week traps—persistent misconceptions that survive extended study—include the following. Trap 1: conflating "full subgroup" with "subgroup of full measure" (measure one). The definition on [PAGE 250] is purely topological (non-empty interior); a dense subgroup of measure zero can be full only if its interior is non-empty, which for ℚ ⊂ ℝ is false. Trap 2: assuming cosets are open directly from the subgroup property, skipping the intermediate lemma Z ∈ τ. Without the translation-by-z₀⁻¹ argument, openness of individual cosets is unjustified. Trap 3: believing the closedness conclusion requires a separate compactness or Hausdorff argument. On [PAGE 250], closedness is obtained for free by complementation because the family of unions of left cosets is closed under complementation. Trap 4: importing local compactness into Theorem A. The theorem holds for arbitrary topological groups; local compactness enters only in the subsequent promise of sufficiently small full subgroups. A learner who spends weeks proving Theorem A under added compactness assumptions is regressing to a stronger-than-necessary framework, obscuring the pure topological core.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: when a reasoner is under time pressure or cognitive load (the "crisis"), the default intuition regresses to naive algebraic thinking: "Z has an interior point, therefore Z is big, therefore its cosets are open." This skips the recursive requirement that openness of Z itself must be established before openness of xZ can be inferred. The regression manifests as an attempt to prove coset openness directly from z₀ ∈ Z^∘ without first proving Z ∈ τ, which stalls because xZ need not contain an open neighborhood of x unless Z contains an open neighborhood of e.

Counter-trap: the proof on [PAGE 250] is itself a recursive counter-trap to that regression. It forces a strict dependency chain: union → coset → Z → interior point → homeomorphism → neighborhood of e → openness of Z → openness of all cosets → clopen unions → discrete quotient. Each node is a veto gate: the skeptic blocks advancement to node n+1 unless node n is explicitly discharged. The critical veto gate is the homeomorphism property of left translation; without it, the interior point z₀ cannot be transported to the identity.

Clock: the logical clock of the proof is sequential and irreversible. Step 6 (closedness) depends on step 5 (openness) only through the complement symmetry, but step 5 depends on step 4 (cosets open), which depends on step 3 (Z open). Attempting to parallelize the proof—e.g., proving each coset open directly while leaving Z merely "full but not known open"—causes the clock to desynchronize and the argument to fail. In an oral-examination setting, temporal pressure further compresses the clock, making the examinee likely to omit the complement argument and assert only openness, thereby forgetting that Theorem A demands both open and closed.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Physical-layer veto: The formalism on [PAGE 250] contains no quantities with SI units (mass, length, time, current, temperature, amount, luminous intensity). The topological group (X, ·, τ) and its subgroup Z are abstract; therefore any veto predicated on dimensional analysis, force balances, or thermodynamic constraints is void by category error. The theorem is veto-proof against physical-layer intrusion because its domain is measure topology, not continuum mechanics.

Biomechanical-layer veto: There are no biological variables—joint torque, muscle activation, reflex latency, or fatigue accumulation—encoded in the statement or proof of Theorem A. A veto claiming that "biomechanical constraints limit the size of Z" has no referent in the source text [PAGE 250] and cannot affect the validity of clopen coset unions.

Weather-layer veto: No meteorological fields (pressure, wind velocity, precipitation rate, humidity) appear. The topological structure is invariant under environmental perturbation because the theorem is not a model of an atmospheric or fluid system. Any weather-based veto is inapplicable.

Adjudication: The only operative vetoes are algebraic and topological. The theorem is veto-proof against cross-layer physical, biomechanical, or weather objections, but it is not veto-proof against regime changes within its own domain: if Z^∘ = ∅, if translations are not homeomorphisms, or if the ambient object is not a topological group, the conclusion collapses. The breakdown regimes are purely internal to the formalism, not externally imposed by physical context.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 (Removal of fullness): Let X = (ℝ, +, usual topology) and Z = ℤ. Then Z^∘ = ∅, so Z is not full. The left coset 0 + ℤ = ℤ is closed but not open; hence the union U = ℤ of left cosets is not clopen. Prediction: Theorem A fails without the fullness hypothesis; specifically, openness of cosets and clopenness of their unions do not hold.

Scenario 2 (Loss of topological-group compatibility): Let X be a group endowed with a topology under which left translation L_x is not a homeomorphism (e.g., a group topology where multiplication is separately continuous but not jointly continuous). Even if Z ∈ τ and Z^∘ ≠ ∅, the image xZ = L_x(Z) need not be open because L_x is not an open map. Prediction: The chain Z ∈ τ ⇒ xZ ∈ τ breaks; unions of cosets need not be open, so Theorem A's conclusion is invalid.

Scenario 3 (Non-sigma-finite quotient pathology from absence of small full subgroups): Let X = (ℝ, +, usual) and Z = {0}. Then Z^∘ = ∅, so Z is not full and not "small" in the locally compact sense. The quotient X/Z ≅ ℝ carries the usual topology, not the discrete topology; the counting measure on uncountably many singleton cosets is not sigma-finite, and L²(X/Z) is non-separable. Prediction: Without sufficiently small full subgroups (as promised in the intro to [PAGE 250]), measure-theoretic pathology of the infinite emerges: non-sigma-finite quotient measures, failure of separability, and breakdown of standard Haar integration on X/Z.
