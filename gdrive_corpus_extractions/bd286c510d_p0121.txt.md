Galton’s “strictly interdependerit” results [PAGE 103] depend on the full range of deviation being represented; restricting the range breaks the interdependence he describes.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the strategic environment recursively. Let an agent’s baseline or “crisis habit” policy be \(\pi_0(s)\), the behavior emitted under stress when higher-order deliberation collapses. Galton’s regression logic maps directly onto a belief about the opponent’s next move: after an extreme deviation \(x_t\) from the opponent’s mediocrity, the conditional expectation of their next deviation is \(\mathbb{E}[x_{t+1}\mid x_t] = \rho x_t\) (in deviation units, assuming comparable variances). Because \(|\rho|<1\), the opponent is statistically expected to regress toward their own baseline habit \(\pi_0\).

The counter-trap arises when the opponent anticipates that you expect this regression. If you pre-position a response to \(\pi_0\), the opponent may deliberately deviate from \(\pi_0\) to exploit your preparation. But that deliberate deviation is itself a new, higher-order tell; if they expect you to expect the counter to the habit, they may revert to \(\pi_0\) after all. The recursion is:

\[
\pi^{(k+1)} = \text{BestResponse}\big(\text{Expect}[\pi^{(k)}]\big),
\]

with the statistical anchor that, absent continual injection of new extremes, the sequence of deviations contracts toward zero at rate \(|\rho|\) per stage. The “clock” is the number of stages required for an initial deviation to halve:

\[
n_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(0.5)}{\ln|\rho|},\qquad |\rho|<1.
\]

If an adversary manufactures a new extreme every \(n < n_{1/2}\) stages, the system never reaches mediocrity and regression is continually reset. If they wait \(n \gg n_{1/2}\), the deviation decays and the crisis-habit baseline reasserts itself. Galton’s page supplies the physical meaning of the anchor: deviations are measured “from the level of mediocrity” [PAGE 103], so the clock is calibrated to the statistical zero point of the population, not to an arbitrary recent peak. Any theory of mind that treats the last extreme observation as the new normal, rather than as a deviation subject to contraction, mis-specifies the recursion.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer (measurement and instrument): Human stature exhibits diurnal variation of roughly 1–2 cm from spinal disc compression, which is the same order of magnitude as Galton’s observed probable error \(Q \approx 1.00\) inch (\(\approx 25.4\) mm) [PAGE 103]. If stature is not measured at a standardized time of day, the observed scatter contains a systematic diurnal component that inflates \(Q_{\text{obs}}\) relative to the biological/error \(Q_{\text{true}}\). Veto condition: if measurement times are not locked within a narrow window, \(Q_{\text{obs}} \ge Q_{\text{true}} + \sigma_{\text{diurnal}}\), and the 7% gap between predicted \(Q=1.07\) inch and observed \(Q=1.00\) inch [PAGE 103] cannot be cleanly attributed to model adequacy versus protocol noise.

Biomechanical layer (support bounds): The normal curve assigns positive probability to \(x\in(-\infty,\infty)\), which is physically impossible for stature; negative stature and stature beyond structural limits have nonzero Gaussian density. From \(Q=1.07\) inch, \(\sigma_{\text{pred}}\approx1.586\) inch; at \(3\sigma\) the deviation is \(\approx4.76\) inch. Beyond \(\mu\pm3\sigma\), the Gaussian tail is physically implausible for human stature. Veto condition: for deviations \(>3\sigma\) (roughly \(\pm 4.8\) inch from the mean), the “true Curve of Frequency” claim [PAGE 103] breaks down; a bounded or truncated distribution is required. Because Galton’s ellipse analysis relies on the normal error law throughout [PAGE 103], tail truncation voids the model in the extreme regions where regression is most often invoked.

Weather layer: Ambient temperature changes induce thermal expansion in metal stadiometers (steel \(\approx 11\times10^{-6}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\)). Over a 2 m instrument, a \(10^\circ\text{C}\) swing alters length by only \(\approx0.22\) mm, negligible against \(Q\approx25\) mm. However, seasonal clothing, posture, and footwear can introduce mm-to-cm-scale offsets comparable to \(Q\). Veto condition: if seasonal or weather-driven posture/clothing variation exceeds \(\sim5\) mm without correction, the observed ellipse axes and inclination become contaminated; the “less than 2°” inclination tolerance [PAGE 103] is too tight to absorb such systematic rotation.

Composite adjudication rule: Accept the bivariate-normal ellipse model only if all four hold simultaneously: (i) axis-ratio change \(\le3\%\); (ii) inclination change \(\le2^\circ\); (iii) predicted and observed Q within the observed band around 1.00–1.07 inch [PAGE 103]; (iv) measurement protocol standardized so that diurnal, biomechanical, and weather noise are small relative to Q. Failure of any layer triggers a model veto and requires either a heteroscedastic/non-Gaussian replacement or a stratified subsample.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Correlation collapse. Counterfactual: Suppose the parental–filial correlation falls to \(\rho<0.2\) (e.g., in an adoptive or environmentally mismatched sample). The regression slope \(\beta=\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X\) then approaches zero, the major axis of the XY ellipse aligns with the coordinate axes rather than Galton’s predicted \(26^\circ36'\) [PAGE 103], and the axis ratio tends toward 1:1 if \(\sigma_X\approx\sigma_Y\). Prediction: In such a subsample, measured inclination \(<5^\circ\) and major:minor axis ratio within 5% of unity. Verification: compute the sample covariance for the low-\(\rho\) subsample; if inclination and circularity fall outside Galton’s tolerances, veto the claim that “the law of Error holds throughout with sufficient precision” [PAGE 103] for that subsample.

Scenario 2 — Inclination drift across cohorts. Counterfactual: Replicate the XY section for two adjacent generational cohorts separated by a secular trend in nutrition or health. If the inclination changes by more than \(2^\circ\) (e.g., from \(25^\circ\) to \(28^\circ\)), Galton’s stated stability bound is falsified [PAGE 103]. Prediction: sample covariance matrices \(\Sigma_A\) and \(\Sigma_B\) will show an ellipse-angle difference \(>2^\circ\) and an axis-ratio difference \(>3\%\), implying \(\Sigma\) is time-varying and a pooled ellipse is invalid. Verification: estimate \(\Sigma_A,\Sigma_B\) from \(n\ge100\) pairs per cohort; if \(\Delta\theta>2^\circ\) and \(\Delta(\text{axis ratio})>3\%\), veto the stationarity assumption underlying the footnote’s “true ellipses” [PAGE 103].

Scenario 3 — Probable error quantile failure. Counterfactual: Suppose the filial stature array’s central 50% width implies \(Q_{\text{obs}}=1.25\) inch rather than Galton’s observed \(1.00\) inch or predicted \(1.07\) inch [PAGE 103]. Under the Gaussian assumption, \(\sigma = Q/0.67449\), so \(Q=1.25\) inch implies \(\sigma\approx1.853\) inch versus Galton’s implied \(\sigma\approx1.483\)–\(1.586\) inch. Prediction: a chi-square goodness-of-fit test on the XZ frequency curve rejects normality at \(\alpha=0.05\) for \(n\ge200\), with expected \(p<0.05\). Verification: bin \(n\ge200\) filial deviations into quartiles, compute the empirical Q; if \(Q\) lies outside \([0.93,1.07]\) inch (\(\pm7\%\) around the observed 1.00 inch), veto the statement that calculation and observation are in “close accord” and that results are “strictly interdependerit” [PAGE 103].
