The derivation chain begins with sampling: twenty-nine (29) professional footballers from a Nigerian club met selection criteria out of thirty-two; players with injuries or comorbidities were excluded. The position distribution was twelve forwards, six midfielders, four goalkeepers, and seven defenders [PAGE 1], [PAGE 2]. The design is explicitly correlational, intended to reflect strength and direction of relationships between variables [PAGE 2].

The measurement hop proceeds as follows. Hand-eye coordination was assessed with the alternate hand wall toss test: participants stood two meters from a wall, threw a tennis ball underarm against it, caught it with the opposite hand, and repeated for thirty seconds; successful catches were counted [PAGE 2], [PAGE 3]. The test is cited as having validity 0.718 (good) and reliability 0.875 (okay) [PAGE 2]. Body composition was measured with a Beurer diagnostic scale (Model BG 42, Germany) using bioelectrical impedance; inputs included age, gender, height, weight, and physical-activity level (scored 1–5), outputs were percentage body fat (PBF) and muscle mass [PAGE 2], [PAGE 3]. Reliability of BIA is cited via Hashim et al. (2017): test-retest r = 0.99, interclass r = 0.96, intraclass r = 0.97 [PAGE 2]. PBF was classified using Gallagher et al. [17]; muscle mass using Janssen et al. [18] [PAGE 3].

The statistical hop uses SPSS Version 23.0, Pearson product-moment correlation, and linear regression; significance was set at p < 0.05 [PAGE 1], [PAGE 3]. Correlation magnitudes were interpreted per Akoglu [19]: ≤0.2 weak, 0.2 < r ≤ 0.5 fair, 0.5 < r ≤ 0.7 moderate, r > 0.7 strong [PAGE 3]. Results are reported in Table 1 [PAGE 4] and regression Tables 2–3 [PAGE 4–5]. The only nominally significant finding is for forwards: muscle mass versus coordination, r = 0.550, p = 0.044; abstract language states "For every one unit increase in muscle mass, an approximately 0.435 increase in coordination was observed among forwards" [PAGE 1]. Regression output: B = 0.435, SE = 0.209, Beta = 0.550, t = 2.080, Sig. = 0.044, 95% CI [-0.031, 0.901] [PAGE 5]. All other position-by-variable combinations were non-significant, including PBF versus coordination for every position [PAGE 4], [PAGE 5].

Gray-zone audit. Hop one: multiplicity. Eight hypothesis tests were conducted (four positions × two body-composition predictors) without any correction. Family-wise error rate under independence is approximately 1 − (1 − 0.05)^8 ≈ 0.337. Bonferroni-adjusted α = 0.05/8 = 0.00625; the forwards muscle-mass p = 0.044 does not survive. Hop two: per-position power. For forwards n = 12, df = 10; the two-tailed critical t at α = 0.05 is 2.228, yet the reported t = 2.080 corresponds to two-tailed p ≈ 0.065, placing the reported p = 0.044 in a borderline inconsistent zone (possible one-tailed reporting or rounding). The 95% confidence interval [-0.031, 0.901] straddles zero, so the null cannot be excluded at 95% confidence despite the starred p-value. Hop three: midfielders n = 6 show |r| = 0.552 (moderate magnitude by the paper’s own scale) with p = 0.256—a classic underpowered Type II error zone where a true moderate effect cannot be distinguished from null. Hop four: goalkeepers n = 4, df = 2; the minimum |r| required for p < 0.05 two-tailed is approximately 0.950, so observed |r| = 0.028 is structurally incapable of significance; any claim otherwise is impossible. Hop five: measurement error. Classical errors-in-variables attenuate observed correlations: r_obs = r_true · sqrt(ρ_M · ρ_C). With BIA reliability ≈ 0.97 and wall-toss reliability ≈ 0.875, attenuation factor ≈ 0.921, modest but real; unreliable measurement also inflates required sample size. Hop six: construct validity. The alternate hand wall toss is a closed, upper-limb catching task at a fixed two-meter distance [PAGE 3]; outfield forwards primarily require lower-limb foot-eye coordination under opponent pressure, so the test may not capture football-specific coordination. Hop seven: endogeneity. Position assignment is non-random; coaches may already select muscular, coordinated players for forward, meaning the correlation reflects selection into position rather than muscle mass causing coordination. Hop eight: temporality. The design is cross-sectional (one measurement occasion), so no temporal precedence can be assigned; muscle mass is a slow-changing stock while coordination is a fast-changing motor flow.

Synthesis: the only significant result is a marginal, uncorrected, small-subgroup finding with a confidence interval touching zero, embedded in a broader null pattern for PBF and for all other positions. The forward muscle-mass signal is best interpreted as a fragile winner’s-curse false positive pending layered replication.

\[
r_{MC} = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_P}(M_i - \bar{M}_P)(C_i - \bar{C}_P)}{\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_P}(M_i - \bar{M}_P)^2 \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_P}(C_i - \bar{C}_P)^2}} \in [-1,1].
\]

\[
t = r\sqrt{\frac{n_P - 2}{1 - r^2}}, \quad t \sim t_{\,n_P - 2} \text{ under } H_0.
\]

\[
C = \beta_{0,P} + \beta_{1,P} M + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_P^2), \quad [\beta_{1,P}] = \frac{[C]}{[M]}.
\]

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The study sits inside a multi-decade lineage of motor-science and soccer anthropology. Motor-learning foundations are drawn from Coker’s Motor Learning and Control for Practitioners [7] and Dogra’s sports-medicine text [24], both cited on [PAGE 7]. Fitness assessment in soccer traces to Reilly’s Fitness assessment in Science and Soccer [22], and body-composition/performance links to Silvestre et al. (NCAA Division I men’s soccer) [9] and Duthie et al. (anthropometry of elite rugby players, used here as a strength-to-weight analogy) [11]. Reference standards for the dependent and independent variables come from Wood’s Complete Guide to Fitness Testing (wall-toss norms) [16], Gallagher et al.’s healthy body-fat ranges [17], and Janssen et al.’s skeletal muscle mass reference in 468 adults [18]. Reliability of the field BIA method is anchored in Hashim et al. (2017) [15], and correlation interpretation follows Akoglu (2018) [19]. A prior bridge between muscle and coordination is Anusha et al. (2020), core strength versus hand-eye coordination in non-athletes with low back pain [23], which the discussion invokes while carefully noting that muscle strength and muscle mass are different entities [PAGE 5–6].

Tracing backward, the match-performance context cites Barros et al. (2007) and Sarmento et al. (2014) for distances covered (9–14 km, ~1330 activities per match) [PAGE 2], [PAGE 7], and Cabañas & Esparza’s kinanthropometry compendium [12] for muscle percentage and distance traveled [PAGE 1]. The historical arc therefore moves from general motor-control theory, through generalized body-composition references, into soccer-specific positional anthropology, landing on a narrow correlation in twenty-nine Nigerian professionals.

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy: (a) Hydration and glycogen status oscillate across a weekly microcycle; BIA-estimated muscle mass is exquisitely sensitive to total-body water, so a single measurement may capture a transient hydration state rather than stable muscle tissue [PAGE 2–3]. (b) Periodized resistance training causes muscle mass to rise and fall across mesocycles; a cross-sectional correlation cannot distinguish a player in a hypertrophy phase from one in a taper. (c) Injury and comorbidity exclusion [PAGE 2] removes players non-randomly; those removed may be precisely the ones for whom muscle mass and coordination diverged most, inducing survivorship bias. (d) Position assignment is retrocausal relative to the measured correlation: coaches designate forwards based on observed coordination and physique over seasons, so the outcome (position) precedes and shapes the predictor (muscle mass) via specialized training. The observed association may therefore read backward: players were made forwards because they already coordinated well and carried more muscle, not because muscle created coordination. (e) Maturation and relative-age effects operate over years, though the sample is professional adults; still, career longevity selects for physiques that tolerate load, confounding cross-sectional body-composition differences.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three recursive levels of theorizing about coaches, players, and the causal claim. Level one—crisis habit regression: under match pressure, athletes regress to overlearned, dominant motor patterns. For forwards, crisis habits are shooting, sprinting, and shielding—lower-limb, power-dominant behaviors. If higher muscle mass is associated with these power habits, then the observed correlation with an upper-limb wall-toss test may reflect regression to strength-biased behavior under stress rather than genuine coordination capacity. The motor system under crisis does not generalize from catching a tennis ball against a wall to receiving a pass under defensive pressure.

Level two—counter-trap: a coaching staff receiving \(r = 0.550\), \(p = 0.044\) may overcorrect by prescribing hypertrophy protocols for all forwards, believing muscle mass is a causal lever for coordination. This is a counter-trap because the evidence is a marginal, uncorrected, small-sample correlation with a confidence interval touching zero. Indiscriminate muscle gain can degrade the strength-to-weight ratio and acceleration—the very attributes the introduction identifies as impaired by excess mass [PAGE 1]. The trap is mistaking a fragile statistical signal for a training prescription, thereby inducing non-functional mass that harms the position’s actual physical demands.

Level three—clock mismatch: the study’s clock is a single cross-sectional occasion, whereas football coordination unfolds over a ninety-minute match clock with accumulating fatigue, dehydration, and glycogen depletion. Muscle mass is a slow-changing stock (weeks to months); hand-eye coordination is a fast-changing flow (seconds to minutes). Because the predictor and outcome are measured on incommensurate clocks, the regression coefficient \(B = 0.435\) cannot predict within-match coordination decay. Any intervention based on it operates on the wrong temporal scale: seasonal hypertrophy versus minute-by-minute motor control under fatigue. The recursive lesson is that a coach who updates beliefs on this study will regress to crisis habits (more muscle for forwards), spring the counter-trap (non-functional mass), and misread the clock (seasonal vs. match-time causality).

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto predicate \(V = 1\) if any layer fails its standardization condition; otherwise \(V = 0\). The source text reports no controls sufficient to set \(V = 0\).

Physical layer (bioelectrical impedance): The Beurer BG 42 estimates muscle mass from impedance assuming constant hydration, anatomy, and a population-specific equation [PAGE 2–3]. In the Nigerian training environment implied by the study, sweat loss, fluid intake, and recent exercise alter total body water between the euhydration baseline and measurement, shifting impedance and therefore the estimated muscle mass without any change in actual muscle tissue. Veto condition: participants were not standardized for food, fluid, and exercise prior to BIA (not reported). Because impedance error is systematic with respect to training timing, \(M_i\) contains structured noise that can manufacture or mask a position-specific correlation. Physical-layer veto: active unless euhydration protocol (e.g., urine specific gravity, controlled pre-test fluid and food) is demonstrated.

Biomechanical layer (coordination construct): The alternate hand wall toss uses a tennis ball, underarm throw, two-meter distance, and thirty-second count [PAGE 3]. It is a closed, upper-limb catching task. Forwards’ primary coordination demands are lower-limb foot-eye coordination during dribbling, shooting, and passing under opponent pressure [PAGE 1]. The test lacks ecological validity for football-specific coordination. Biomechanical-layer veto: active unless a football-specific coordination battery (e.g., passing accuracy under defensive pressure) replaces or supplements the wall toss.

Weather/environmental layer: Testing venue conditions—ambient temperature, humidity, wind, and surface—affect tennis-ball grip, bounce, tape adherence for the two-meter mark, and stopwatch-based timing [PAGE 3]. Heat and humidity also exacerbate the BIA hydration problem in the physical layer. Environmental-layer veto: active unless testing occurs indoors under climate control or environmental covariates are recorded and modeled.

Adjudication: Because the source reports a single outdoor or unspecified venue, no hydration standardization, and a non-football-specific coordination test, all three layer conditions fail or are unverified. Therefore \(V = 1\). The claim “a forward’s muscle mass affects their degree of hand-eye coordination” [PAGE 1] is vetoed at the physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers pending a replication that controls hydration, uses football-specific coordination, and standardizes testing climate.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios. Each is verifiable from the source statistics or by a specified replication, and each carries a falsifiable prediction.

Scenario 1 — Family-wise error correction. Verifiable by recomputing the eight reported p-values from Tables 1–3 [PAGE 4–5] with Holm–Bonferroni. Ordered p-values: 0.044 (forwards MM), 0.127 (forwards PBF), 0.256 (midfielders MM), 0.273 (midfielders PBF), 0.674 (defenders MM), 0.749 (defenders PBF), 0.943 (goalkeepers PBF), 0.972 (goalkeepers MM). Holm’s first step compares the smallest p = 0.044 against \(\alpha / 8 = 0.00625\). Prediction: 0.044 > 0.00625, so the null is retained for all eight tests; the nominally significant forwards muscle-mass result is a family-wise false positive and must be vetoed under error-rate control.

Scenario 2 — Blinded, protocol-controlled retest with attenuation. Verifiable by recruiting the same twelve forwards, administering wall-toss and BIA after a four-week wash-in, with blinded assessors and euhydration (no food or vigorous exercise four hours pre-test; urine specific gravity < 1.020; indoor climate control). Prediction: under classical measurement error, \(r_{\text{obs}} \approx r_{\text{true}} \sqrt{\rho_M \rho_C}\). With \(\rho_M \approx 0.97\) and \(\rho_C \approx 0.875\), the attenuation factor is \(\approx 0.921\), but the binding constraint is sample size: \(n = 12\), \(df = 10\), and the original standard error \(\text{SE} = 0.209\) remains large. Even if the true slope were preserved, the attenuated slope \(\hat{\beta}_1^* \approx 0.435 \times 0.921 \approx 0.401\) yields \(t \approx 1.92\), two-tailed \(p \approx 0.08 > 0.05\). Prediction: significance disappears in a blinded, protocol-controlled replication; the original finding regresses toward the null.

Scenario 3 — Construct substitution and small-n structural bound. (a) Verifiable by administering both the alternate hand wall toss and a football-specific foot-eye coordination test (e.g., passing accuracy under defensive pressure) to the same forwards. Prediction: because the wall toss measures upper-limb catching, not soccer-specific coordination, the correlation between muscle mass and football-specific coordination falls to weak magnitude, \(|r| < 0.20\), with \(p > 0.40\), while the wall-toss correlation remains the only nominally significant one. This isolates the construct-mismatch artifact. (b) Verifiable by computing the critical Pearson value for goalkeepers: \(n_G = 4\), \(df = 2\), so the minimum \(|r|\) for \(p < 0.05\) two-tailed is approximately 0.950. Any future dataset with \(n = 4\) goalkeepers claiming a significant muscle-mass–coordination correlation is structurally impossible unless \(|r| > 0.950\); such a claim must be vetoed by design. The joint prediction: construct substitution collapses the forward signal, and the goalkeeper cell is permanently underpowered.
