Weather / environmental layer: Knight’s automata argument applies only to a perfectly uniform environment [PAGE 294]. In open industrial systems, weather and material supply inject exogenous innovation, keeping H[𝒳] > 0 and D_KL(p_{t+1}‖p_t) periodically above ε. This guarantees that the boundary ∂γ_U/∂γ_D is non-empty and that “some part of the task will practically always be found to require conscious judgment” [PAGE 294]. Weather therefore regenerates the very condition that makes the human veto necessary.

Veto adjudication rule: A veto is admissible if and only if (i) the observed divergence at the boundary exceeds the learned threshold (σ_t ∉ 𝓛), (ii) the remaining time-to-commitment satisfies Δt > τ_conscious + τ_actuation, and (iii) the operator holds control location κ such that decision and responsibility are co-located [PAGE 294]. Breakdown occurs when any condition fails: perfect uniformity (H = 0) removes condition (i); excessive machine speed removes (ii); and delegated or ambiguous control removes (iii), at which point Knight’s indivisibility thesis no longer maps to an actionable human veto. </CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards) veto scenarios, each with a counterfactual and a testable prediction.

Scenario 1: CNC manufacturing line. The machine tender observes spindle vibration exceeding the learned envelope during a routine cutting cycle. The operator triggers an emergency stop before the tool reaches the depth-of-cut limit. Verifiable artifacts: accelerometer time-series, E-stop timestamp, post-stop spindle inspection showing incipient tool chipping. Counterfactual: no veto; the cycle completes and the tool fails mid-part. Prediction: Under the veto, defective-part count is zero for that cycle but planned downtime cost C_d is incurred; without the veto, expected cost is P(tool_failure)·C_scrap, which exceeds C_d when the anomaly rate is non-negligible. Verifiable test: compare defect rates and downtime across matched shifts with and without logged overrides.

Scenario 2: Harvesting machine tender under a weather front. Routine field operation proceeds until radar indicates an unforecasted rain cell moving onto the field. The operator diverts the harvester to the headland and shelters equipment rather than completing the row. Verifiable artifacts: weather radar reflectivity, GPS equipment track, fuel/log timestamps, post-event soil moisture readings. Counterfactual: continue the routine row and risk equipment immobilization in saturated soil. Prediction: The veto reduces expected equipment-recovery cost and crop-mud loss but increases idle-time minutes. Expected value of veto is positive when P(rain_immobilization)·C_recovery > C_idle_rate·Δt_idle. Verifiable test: instrumented fleet comparison across weather-front weeks.

Scenario 3: Expert assembly worker on a learned torque sequence. The task has “dropped below the threshold when learned” [PAGE 294], so the worker normally executes reflexively. A torque transducer flags an off-nominal fastener on unit #N. The worker consciously re-engages, breaks the reflex, and manually re-torques to specification. Verifiable artifacts: torque-angle curve, motion-capture or wearable IMU sequence showing pause-and-correct, error log, and field-failure trace for units that bypassed the check. Counterfactual: the worker completes the reflex sequence without checking, shipping an under-torqued fastener. Prediction: In nominal conditions, automaticity yields low variance and high speed; under transducer-flagged perturbation, reflex completion yields conditional field-failure probability p_fail|reflex ≫ p_fail|conscious, at the cost of increased cycle time Δτ. Verifiable test: A/B study of assembly cells with mandatory conscious verification versus pure reflex pacing, measuring field returns per 1,000 units. </RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
