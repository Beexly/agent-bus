<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let the situational state be
\[
s=(m,t,d,\ell,\tau,q,o,p_{\text{pers}},b,w,r,a,\ldots),
\]
with units: score margin \(m\) in points; clock \(t\) in seconds; down \(d\in\{1,2,3,4\}\); distance-to-go in yards; field position \(\ell\in[0,100]\) in yards; timeouts \(\tau\) as a count; quarterback identity \(q\) as a categorical variable; offensive-line starters out \(o\) as a count; personnel code \(p_{\text{pers}}\in\{11,12,13,21,\ldots\}\); box count \(b\) as a count; wind \(w\) in m/s; rest differential \(r\) in days; altitude \(a\) in meters.

Expected points:
\[
\mathrm{EP}(s)=\mathbb{E}\left[\text{net points from state }s\right]\quad[\text{points}].
\]
Play value:
\[
\Delta\mathrm{EP}_i=\mathrm{EP}(s_{i+1})-\mathrm{EP}(s_i)\quad[\text{points}].
\]
Win probability:
\[
\mathrm{WP}(s)=\Pr(\text{win}\mid s)\in[0,1]\quad[\text{probability}].
\]
Leverage:
\[
L(s)=\frac{\partial \mathrm{WP}(s)}{\partial \mathrm{EP}(s)}
\quad\left[\frac{\text{probability points}}{\text{point}}\right],
\qquad
\Delta\mathrm{WP}\approx L(s)\Delta\mathrm{EP}.
\]
Decision value:
\[
V_{\text{go}}(s)=p_{\text{conv}}(s)\mathrm{WP}(s_{\text{succ}})
+\left[1-p_{\text{conv}}(s)\right]\mathrm{WP}(s_{\text{fail}}),
\]
\[
V_{\text{FG}}(s)=p_{\text{make}}(s)\mathrm{WP}(s_{\text{make}})
+\left[1-p_{\text{make}}(s)\right]\mathrm{WP}(s_{\text{miss}}),
\]
\[
V_{\text{punt}}(s)=p_{\text{stop}}(s)\mathrm{WP}(s_{\text{stop}})
+\left[1-p_{\text{stop}}(s)\right]\mathrm{WP}(s_{\text{not stop}}).
\]
The decision rule is
\[
a^\star(s)=\arg\max_{a\in\{\text{go},\text{FG},\text{punt}\}}V_a(s).
\]
The source states the same rule verbally: “Going for it is an expected value: success probability × WP after success, plus failure probability × WP after failure” [PAGE 2]. It also demonstrates that all three branches can be priced: “punt 41%, field-goal attempt 39%, go for it 52%” [PAGE 10].

Pass-rate over expected:
\[
\mathrm{PROE}(s)=R(s)-\hat{R}(s_{\text{script}})
=\Pr(\text{pass}\mid s)-\Pr(\text{pass}\mid \text{score, down, distance, clock}),
\]
dimensionless. Neutral-script reweighting:
\[
\bar{R}_{\text{neutral}}
=\frac{\sum_s w_{\text{neutral}}(s)R(s)}
{\sum_s w_{\text{neutral}}(s)},
\]
where \(w_{\text{neutral}}\) is an indicator for a pre-specified neutral band such as margin within about 6 points or WP roughly 20–80%. The source’s confound example is “Raw split was almost even (147 rushes, 144 passes), but they had been trailing often” [PAGE 4].

Information units are useful for stating how much a state tells us:
\[
H(S)=-\sum_s P(s)\log_2 P(s)\quad[\text{bits}],
\qquad
I(Y;S)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid S)\quad[\text{bits}].
\]
These are diagnostic only; the decision equations above remain in points and probability.

ASSUMPTIONS. (1) EP and WP are estimated within a regime; they are not assumed stationary across neutral, garbage-time, red-zone, two-minute, overtime, and kneel-out states. (2) \(p_{\text{conv}}\), \(p_{\text{make}}\), and \(p_{\text{stop}}\) are calibrated probabilities, not observed outcome frequencies. (3) The state vector is sufficiently complete; missing QB identity, OL availability, weather, rest, or altitude is a model error, not noise. (4) Player effects are allowed to be player-specific and opposite-signed. (5) No betting-line construct is used; values are points, probability, or bits.

BREAKDOWN REGIMES. The equations become singular or misleading when \(t\to 0\) and WP jumps discontinuously at the end of a half or game; when \(\ell\to 0\) or \(\ell\to 100\) and EP is strongly nonlinear near the goal lines; when \(p_{\text{conv}}\to 0\) or \(1\) and small calibration errors dominate; when the WP model is uncalibrated in overtime, kneel-out clocks, or one-second-left states; when a season average mixes scripts and a Simpson-like reversal appears; when samples are tiny, as with extreme trailing efficiency; and when prevent defense is misclassified as garbage time. The source gives concrete thresholds and splits: red-zone touchdown rate from “87.5% (San Francisco) to 42.9% (Chicago)” [PAGE 7]; a red-zone down reversal where “On 3rd down in field-goal range: −0.57 EPA per play, worst in the NFL” [PAGE 7]; and a personnel-by-box interaction where “pass EPA stayed above +0.21 in both 2024 and 2025. The same loaded-box look vs 11 fell from +0.18 to +0.04” [PAGE 13]. The model must detect these regimes and refit or veto, not average through them.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that the coach, the model, and the opponent are each modeling the others’ habits under a clock. At level 0, the base rule is simple: teams pass more when trailing, run more when leading. The source states the prior plainly: “Plain statement of the base rule: teams pass more when playing from behind” [PAGE 4]. At level 1, the team-specific offset matters. The Falcons trailed by 7 or more on every snap and still dropped back on only 45% of plays versus a league average of 72% in that spot. So the model must learn a team-specific deviation, not just the base rule.

Crisis habit regression is what happens when the clock and score compress the decision space. Under stress, the coach’s policy regresses toward the conservative habit: punt, kick the field goal, run to kill clock. The source’s Ravens example is the clean case: facing 4th-and-2.5 in a tie from their own 46 with about two minutes left, they took a delay of game and punted; “ESPN graded it an 8.6-point win-probability mistake” [PAGE 10]. The habit says “trust the defense”; the branch tree says a failed attempt still leaves paths to win, while a conversion makes them heavy favorites. The model must not inherit the habit as a prior.

The counter-trap is the opponent’s answer to the habit. If the offense declares heavy personnel, the defense’s loaded box is often a mirror rather than an independent choice. The source says: “Stacking the box” is often just matching heavy personnel [PAGE 14]. So a coach who runs into a loaded box has not necessarily beaten a strategic choice; he may have forced the box and then run into the natural response. The counter-counter is to make the extra tight end a real receiving threat. Against 13 personnel, the loaded box is a pass opportunity: “pass EPA stayed above +0.21 in both 2024 and 2025. The same loaded-box look vs 11 fell from +0.18 to +0.04” [PAGE 13]. Recursive theory of mind here means: I know that you know my personnel tendency, so I price the response, then I price your response to my response.

The clock is the terminating condition of the recursion. With one second left, down 3 at the 2, the branch tree collapses to immediate win or loss versus a kick that sends the game to overtime. The source’s endgame example is exact: a strong team converting about 51% might still prefer overtime at about 55%, while a bad defense flips it. Up 4 with 2:30 left and the opponent out of timeouts, a field goal does not end the game; it creates a new game. A conversion can end it immediately. The model must price the game that follows the kick, not the label “take the points.”

The recursive audit is therefore three-layered. First, detect habit regression: is the chosen branch being selected because of crisis affect rather than because \(V_a(s)\) is maximal? Second, detect counter-trap: is the opponent’s response already priced into the state, or is the model treating an endogenous box count or coverage shell as exogenous? Third, detect clock termination: has the state crossed into a terminal or near-terminal regime where the WP surface is discontinuous? The source’s “you need a 3-and-out either way” logic is a good example of clock-aware recursion: “If you punt, you need to force a 3-and-out to get the ball back. If you go for it and fail… you need to force a 3-and-out to get the ball back anyway” [PAGE 11]. When both branches require the same defensive outcome, the go branch weakly dominates; the label “aggressive” is not the operator.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The veto rule is: if a cross-layer variable is measured with a player-specific or state-specific effect large enough to reverse the aggregate projection, the aggregate projection is vetoed. The layers are physical availability, biomechanical protection, weather, altitude, and rest. The adjudication order is not arbitrary. Confirmed QB identity and OL availability come first because they change the play’s physical production function. Weather comes next because it changes the ball’s flight and the skill-player baseline. Altitude and rest come next because they change the time profile of performance. Scheme and personnel interactions come after, because they are conditional on the physical layers.

QB identity veto. The source shows that a season average can blend two different passers: Chris Olave averaged 10.7 targets per game before Derek Carr’s injury, but “after the injury he had only 3 catches for 16 yards across two games” [PAGE 15]. The receiver did not change; the passer did. But the veto is not a uniform backup-QB penalty. Justin Jefferson with Carson Wentz still posted “29.4% target share (WR4), 99.5 receiving yards/game (WR3), 2.73 yards per route (WR4), 17.2 fantasy points/game (WR6), with zero receiving TDs” [PAGE 15]. So the correct adjudication splits target share, per-route efficiency, and touchdown rate; a blanket penalty is vetoed.

Biomechanical/OL veto. The source is explicit: “teams missing 4 starting offensive linemen were 2-8” [PAGE 16]. The count of missing starters is a first-class feature, and even the exceptions carry context: one win came in Week 18 against an opponent missing half its roster, including three starting OL. One missing lineman and four are different sports. The veto is graded: number of starters out, replacement quality, injury versus coach’s decision, and practice participation. This layer changes expected pressure, sack rate, and QB efficiency, so it cannot be collapsed into a binary active/out flag.

Weather veto. The source gives the threshold and the position-specific tax: “In 15+ mph wind since 2006, QBs score about 11% fewer points, pass catchers 8% fewer, kickers 8% fewer” [PAGE 15]. The source describes a within-game shape, not a flat venue penalty: visitors often look better in the first half and Denver comes back in the second, “happening too often to not be at least partly altitude acclimatization at 5,280 feet” [PAGE 16]. The home team does not pay the same cost. The veto is therefore on a full-game flat adjustment; the correct model is a visitor performance decay by half at an elevation of about 1609 meters.

Rest veto. Rest differential is a physical input, but the source cautions that it is already priced into expectations and does not produce a large consistent advantage by itself. The correct use is in the strength prior: days of rest, short-week indicators, and repeated short weeks. The model should flag extreme differentials, not treat rest as a free edge. The veto here is weaker than the QB/OL and weather vetoes; it adjusts the prior but does not by itself reverse a decision unless it crosses a documented threshold.
