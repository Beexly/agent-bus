The model is a discrete-time, daily-step SEIR process augmented with two detection-dependent compartments: confined (C, with sub-states E_C and I_C) and quarantined (Q). The discrete-time choice is not incidental; it mirrors the operational cadence of a football season, where tests and matches occur only on days that are integer multiples of N_test and N_match [PAGE 4]. Every random event is calculated once per day, which converts the continuous hazard of viral transmission into a sequence of Bernoulli trials governed by the per-day probabilities \beta_{\rm ext}, \beta_{\rm train}, and \beta_{\rm match} [PAGE 4]. A susceptible player can enter the Exposed class E through three independent daily channels: the external social circle (\beta_{\rm ext}, active every day), training contacts on non-match days (\beta_{\rm train}, active on N_{\rm match}-1 days per cycle), and match contacts on match days (\beta_{\rm match}, active every N_{\rm match} days) [PAGE 4-5].

The critical multi-hop derivation concerns the counterintuitive result that reducing the days between matches lowers the total epidemic burden. Consider one cycle of length N_{\rm match}. It contains exactly one match day and N_{\rm match}-1 training days. On a training day, an infectious player has, on average, k = pL = 0.2 \times 25 = 5 network neighbors in the team’s Erdős–Rényi contact graph [PAGE 6], and infects each with probability \beta_{\rm train} = 10^{-1} [PAGE 7], yielding roughly pL\beta_{\rm train} = 0.5 secondary exposures per infectious player per training day [PAGE 8]. On a match day, using a mean-field pitch assumption, one infectious player faces 21 opponents/teammates and infects each with \beta_{\rm match} = 10^{-2}, yielding 21\beta_{\rm match} = 0.21 exposures per match [PAGE 8]. Because the per-day training hazard exceeds the per-match hazard, each additional day of spacing between matches inserts a full day of high-hazard training exposure while only removing one low-hazard match event. Simultaneously, shortening N_{\rm match} compresses the total season length T \approx 11 N_{\rm match} days [PAGE 10], reducing the cumulative integral of the external hazard \beta_{\rm ext} over calendar time and reducing the total number of training cycles. Therefore the slope d\bar n/dN_{\rm match} is positive: wider spacing increases \bar n(t) [PAGE 10-11].

Counter-trap. The counterintuitive trap is that playing more frequently (every 3 days) reduces total infections. Naive reasoning suggests fewer contacts equal fewer transmissions, but here the dominant exposure channel is training (\beta_{\rm train} = 0.1 per neighbor per day) rather than matches (\beta_{\rm match} = 0.01 per contact), and the total calendar duration shrinks as N_{\rm match} falls [PAGE 8, 10-11]. The trap is that decision-makers may reject a compressed schedule out of fear of “more matches,” when the model indicates the schedule’s density protects by ending the exposure window sooner.

Clock. The operative clock is the 78-day season horizon [PAGE 12] nested within the latent period \sigma^{-1} = 3 days and infectious period \gamma^{-1} = 5 days [PAGE 7]. Daily PCR testing (N_{\rm test} = 1) compresses detection delay below these biological time constants, cutting off transmission chains before asymptomatic infectious players generate secondary cases. Testing every 7 days (Fig. 2B-C) allows multiple generations of spread within each inter-test interval [PAGE 10-11]. The recursion is therefore: crisis habit wants weekly matches; the model’s clock demands compressed matches and high-frequency testing to stay ahead of the 3–5 day viral generation interval.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Biomechanical/physical veto. The authors state explicitly that reducing time between matches “can be very physically demanding. The recovery time after a match would be reduced and the risk of injury would increase,” requiring increased player rotation to mitigate [PAGE 14]. This is a hard veto against the epidemiological optimum (N_{\rm match} = 3) unless squad depth and rotation protocols are simultaneously upgraded; without rotation, the injury layer overrides the epidemic layer.

Medical/test veto. Antibody and antigen detectors “are only reliable more than a week after the infection,” permitting infectious but unidentified players to spread for several days [PAGE 5]. This vetoes any protocol relying on low-sensitivity or symptom-triggered detection. PCR is required because \mu_I \approx 0.9 and \mu_E = \mu_I/3 still leaves exposed individuals under-detected; dropping below this sensitivity collapses the confinement feedback loop [PAGE 8-9].

Environmental/weather layer. The model treats \beta_{\rm train}, \beta_{\rm match}, and \beta_{\rm ext} as constant probabilities with no explicit weather, humidity, or ventilation terms [PAGE 4-7]. Because football is played outdoors, one might expect lower \beta_{\rm match} than indoor sport, but the model contains no parameter to adjudicate weather-dependent modulation; thus no weather veto is formally represented, and any real-world weather effect enters only through an ad hoc rescaling of \beta values outside the model’s domain.

Model singularity/breakdown regimes. The mean-field match assumption breaks if match-day contact is not well mixed or if rotations change network topology. The CPP reduction fails when inter-team jumps are no longer Poisson (e.g., after a superspreading match seeds many teams simultaneously). Most importantly, if \beta_{\rm ext} is non-stationary (second wave) or if N_{\rm test} exceeds the latent+infectious window, the confined compartment never catches the I class and the model’s low-burden predictions are invalid.
