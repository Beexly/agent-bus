The Tolman source (p. 285) establishes a fundamental proposition: an aversion like fright exhibits docility because the organism stops reacting to a specific stimulus when it is "proved" that the stimulus does not lead to injury. The spider example demonstrates this; after repeated exposure to a tuning fork that causes no harm, the spider ceases to drop from its web permanently. This is labeled "simple negative adaptation."

From a Frontier Reasoning perspective, we model this as a belief-update process where the sign-gestalt readiness (SGR), defined as $G_{\text{fear}}$, encodes a conditional probability of future injury given a sensory input pattern $x$. Initially, innate expectations assign a high prior probability to injury for certain classes (e.g., sudden loud noises). The SGR then triggers an avoidance motor program $M$ (dropping). Each trial updates the internal model. If the outcome variable $Y$ (injury) remains zero across trials, the posterior probability $P(\text{injury} | x)$ collapses toward zero, and the SGR threshold is no longer crossed. The key insight is that Tolman’s "unlearning" is not mere habituation at the receptor level but a propositional change: the organism "knows" that *this specific* instance of the stimulus is safe.

We must examine whether this simple negative adaptation scales to more complex environments with multiple stimuli and time delays. In the spider case, the temporal window between sound and potential injury is short. What if the absence of injury is only revealed after a delay? The system would need to maintain a suspended state of uncertainty, tracking the expectation versus actual outcome over time. This introduces the possibility of a "belief gap" where the organism continues to respond until the non-injury evidence accumulates beyond a critical mass. This gap could be exploited in multi-step traps: a predator might use a series of non-injurious sounds to desensitize prey, then introduce a novel sound that still triggers the fear response because the learning has not generalized beyond the exact conditioned stimulus.

Another gray zone: generalization versus specificity. Tolman says the spider unlearns fear for "that specific type of case." But what defines "specific"? Is it frequency? Amplitude? Spatial origin? The sign gestalt is a holistic representation; any change in one feature might break the match and reactivate fear. This suggests that the SGR is highly nonlinear and context-dependent. For prediction, if we alter the pitch slightly on day four, the spider may drop again, indicating the unlearning was narrowly tuned. This also implies that "docility" is fragile under parametric variation.

Furthermore, the text mentions innate expectations for loud noises and bright lights. These are hardwired priors. The learning process adjusts these priors locally. This creates a hierarchical model: global innate priors and local learned corrections. In a crisis, the system might regress to the global prior if the local correction is uncertain, leading to a burst of fear despite prior safety. This regression can be triggered by stress hormones or fatigue, which might lower the threshold for SGR activation.

Finally, we consider the role of the environment's stability. If the world changes—for instance, a new predator starts using the same sound—the previously safe stimulus becomes dangerous again. The organism must re-learn quickly. The speed of re-acquisition depends on how strongly the initial learning suppressed the innate prior. If the suppression was complete, re-learning might be slow; if the prior remained latent, re-learning is rapid. This "savings" phenomenon is crucial for survival.

Thus, the deep mechanism is a dynamic balance between innate threat detection and experience-based calibration, mediated by a representational structure that tracks the instrumental value of stimuli. This framework allows us to predict not just behavior but the conditions under which behavior will flip, persist, or generalize.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Operator 1: Sign-Gestalt Readiness (SGR)**
\[
\mathcal{G}_{\theta}(x_t) = \text{sigmoid}\left( w^\top \phi(x_t) - \tau \right) \in [0,1]
\]
where $\phi(x_t)$ is a high-dimensional feature vector (auditory spectral envelope, temporal onset, spatial location), $w$ are learned weights encoding past contingencies, and $\tau$ is a decision threshold. Units: dimensionless probability. Assumption: $\phi(x)$ captures all relevant perceptual dimensions of the stimulus. Breakdown regime: When $\phi$ fails to distinguish between safe and dangerous instances (high overlap in feature space), $\mathcal{G}$ becomes unreliable, leading to either false alarms or missed detections.

**Operator 2: Outcome Prediction Error (OPE)**
\[
\delta_t = y_t - \hat{y}_t, \quad \hat{y}_t = P(y=1 | x_t, \mathcal{H}_{t-1})
\]
where $y_t \in \{0,1\}$ indicates actual injury (painful shock, physical damage) and $\hat{y}_t$ is the predicted probability based on history $\mathcal{H}_{t-1}$. Units: probability mass. Assumption: Injury is binary and observable within a fixed horizon $H$. Breakdown: If injury is delayed beyond $H$ or is probabilistic (sublethal), the error signal is noisy, slowing learning.

**Operator 3: Weight Update Rule (Local Learning)**
\[
\Delta w = \eta \cdot \delta_t \cdot \nabla_w \log P(y_t|x_t;w)
\]
with learning rate $\eta$. Units: weight dimension (feature inverse). This implements gradient ascent on likelihood. The spider’s unlearning corresponds to $\delta_t < 0$ repeatedly, driving $w$ down so that $\mathcal{G}$ falls below $\tau$. Assumption: Linear separability of safe/unsafe stimuli in feature space. Singularity: When $\nabla_w \log P$ vanishes (plateau), learning stalls even if $\delta \neq 0$.

**Operator 4: Hierarchical Prior Integration**
Let $w = w_{\text{innate}} + w_{\text{learned}}$. The innate component $w_{\text{innate}}$ is fixed and large for broad classes (loud noise). Learning modifies $w_{\text{learned}}$ to counteract $w_{\text{innate}}$ for specific instances. The net response is:
\[
\mathcal{G} = \sigma\left( (w_{\text{innate}} + w_{\text{learned}})^\top \phi - \tau \right)
\]
Docility emerges when $w_{\text{learned}} \approx -w_{\text{innate}}$ for a particular subspace of $\phi$. If $w_{\text{learned}}$ is perturbed (e.g., by stress-induced forgetting), the innate prior dominates again, causing fear resurgance.

**Citation**: All operators derive directly from Tolman’s definition of fright as involving "a sign gestalt-readiness to the effect that the given disturbing object will... lead to injury" [PAGE 285]. The spider example illustrates the update dynamics when nothing "bad" results.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s concept of "sign gestalts" (c. 1932) sits at a crossroads between reflexive behaviorism and cognitive map theory. Historically, it inherits from Köhler’s Gestalt psychology (whole patterns vs. elements) and Pavlovian conditioning (stimulus-response bonds), but Tolman rejects the mechanistic S-R view. He proposes that organisms form expectations (gestalts) about distal events based on proximal signs. The "docility" of fright—that it can be unlearned—is evidence that the response is not a rigid reflex but a goal-directed hypothesis test.

This lineage reveals a multi-week trap: later researchers, especially radical behaviorists, dismissed cognitive interpretations, focusing on stimulus generalization gradients and extinction curves without acknowledging the underlying propositional change. They treated unlearning as decay of association strength, missing the crucial point that the organism discriminates between the specific instance and the class. Tolman’s own maze experiments showed that rats learn "place" rather than "response," supporting the idea of flexible, knowledge-based navigation.

In the context of the spider, the retrocausal view asks: why did evolution equip spiders with an innate fear of sudden vibrations? Because in ancestral environments, such vibrations often signaled predators (wasps, birds). The innate prior is a compressed statistical summary of past dangers. The ability to unlearn for a specific stimulus (like a tuning fork) is an energy-saving adaptation: constantly dropping wastes silk and exposes the spider to other risks. However, the retention of the innate prior ensures rapid reactivation if a truly novel threat appears.

A modern parallel is seen in machine learning: pre-trained models (with innate-like priors) fine-tuned on specific datasets. Overfitting to the training data (the specific tuning fork) can cause poor generalization to similar but distinct inputs. Tolman anticipated this trade-off nearly a century ago. His work warns against conflating low-level sensory adaptation with high-level belief revision—a confusion that still plagues some neuroscientific explanations of fear extinction.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
