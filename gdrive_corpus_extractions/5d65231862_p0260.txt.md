Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the clock.

Under crisis—defined here as the sudden re-activation of a primary drive such as hunger or thirst—the organism tends to regress to the habit hierarchy established under that drive. Because the drive modifies afferent input at the source (\(s \to s_h\) or \(s \to s_t\)) [PAGE 251], the crisis does not merely “energize” behavior; it changes the sensory cues to which the organism is attending. Corollary XII [PAGE 251] predicts that a subject will respond differently to the same objective situation when the active drive changes, and differently to the same drive when the situation changes. Thus, during a hunger crisis, a previously learned thirst-context response may be suppressed not because the subject “remembers” the rule, but because the afferent field is now \(s_h\), not \(s_t\).

Counter-trap: a recursive theory-of-mind error occurs when the modeler attributes explicit metarepresentation to the organism—as if the subject “knows” it is hungry and therefore selects a hunger-appropriate response. Hull’s account is mechanistic: drive alters afferent impulses; it does not require the organism to represent its drive state propositionally [PAGE 251]. Attributing such self-knowledge overestimates the cognitive architecture and mislocates the locus of control.

The clock is indexed by training trial \(\tau\), not calendar time per se, though in multi-week applications \(\tau\) maps onto session count. At \(\tau \approx 0\), differentiation is at chance (~0.50) [PAGE 251]. A crisis early in training therefore produces weak, noisy differential responding. A crisis after gradient steepening produces sharp, drive-specific responding. Recursive loop: the modeler’s assumption about whether the organism is “drive-aware” determines which test contexts the modeler selects; testing under a mismatched drive yields behavior that reflects afferent mismatch (\(s_h\) vs \(s_t\)) rather than a failure of learning, leading the modeler to revise the model of the organism’s “motivation” when the real variable is the afferent transformation.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto layers over the Hullian psychological law.

Physical layer (afferent transduction): For any drive-modulated discrimination to occur, the environmental stimulus \(S\) must be transduced into afferent impulses \(s\) [PAGE 251]. If receptors are saturated, nerves are blocked, or \(S\) is physically absent, then \(s\) is not generated and neither \(s_h\) nor \(s_t\) can be formed. Veto: any prediction of differential responding under sensory blockade fails at the physical layer, regardless of drive state.

Biomechanical layer (effector execution): Injury is listed among the primary drives in the source summary [PAGE 251], and intense stimuli can act as drives. Physical injury, fatigue, or biomechanical constraint can prevent the execution of a differentially learned response even when the central representation \(s_h\) vs \(s_t\) is intact. Veto: motor-output constraints supersede central drive modulation; observed absence of response cannot be interpreted as absence of drive differentiation without ruling out effector failure.

Weather layer (environmental stimulus integrity): Weather variables—temperature, humidity, precipitation, wind—alter the physical stimulus compound \(S\) and also shift physiological drives (e.g., heat intensifies thirst). If weather masks \(S\) or introduces competing afferents, the internal representations \(s\) and \(s_t\) cease to be the clean pair assumed in the Hull-Leeper design [PAGE 251]. Veto: predictions that assume an invariant \(S\) across weather conditions are invalid when weather changes the stimulus or the drive.

Adjudication order: Physical > Biomechanical > Psychological. A physical-layer veto nullifies all downstream inferences. A behavioral null result under, say, sensory blockade or extreme weather must not be interpreted as a failure of Hullian discrimination learning [PAGE 251].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios and predictions.

Scenario 1: Pharmacological drive nullification. Satiate both hunger and thirst so that drive intensity \(I_D \to 0\). Under the operator model, \(\mathcal{M}_D \to \mathcal{I}\), hence \(s_h \to s\) and \(s_t \to s\) [PAGE 251]. Prediction: choice probability remains at \(P \approx 0.50\) across sessions; the generalization gradient between \(s\) and \(s_t\) never steepens despite identical \(S\). Verification: compare pre-satiety and post-satiety choice frequencies and confirm via afferent recording that hunger- and thirst-specific neural signatures collapse to baseline.

Scenario 2: Afferent blockade. Transect or anesthetize the sensory pathway carrying \(S\) so that \(s\) is not generated. Prediction: no differential reinforcement can operate; performance stays at chance for all \(\tau\); the Figure 60 gradient never forms [PAGE 251]. Verification: electrophysiological confirmation of absent afferent volley during behavior, paired with behavioral chance-level responding.

Scenario 3: Multi-week extinction and drive-specific reinstatement. Withdraw differential reinforcement for three weeks (extinction), then reintroduce the original drive state. Prediction under Hullian habit persistence: responding reinstates selectively under the original drive (\(R(S,D_{orig}) > R(S,D_{other})\)) [PAGE 251, Corollary XII]. Prediction under trace decay: no differential recovery; performance remains undifferentiated. Verification: weekly probe tests measuring response rates under hunger versus thirst contexts across the extinction gap and at reinstatement.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
