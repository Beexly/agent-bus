Quantization is another gray zone: “The use of 4-bit NF4 quantization, while aligned with resource-constrained deployment goals, may introduce precision loss affecting observed performance ceilings” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, small compliance deltas should not be treated as architectural truth without uncertainty bounds. The defensible conclusion is conditional: a capability gap is strongly suggested around 3B parameters, few-shot calibration helps selected mid-sized short-context models, and fine-tuning helps some 1B lexical metrics but not compliance reliably. The unsafe conclusion is universal deployment guidance from a narrow ADR dataset with an automated judge.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper evaluates language-model outputs with dimensionless text-similarity scores, a bounded ordinal compliance score, a cosine-distance diversity score, and a parameter-efficient fine-tuning operator. No physical SI quantity is measured; the measured units are dimensionless scores, token counts, parameter counts, optimization steps, and cosine distances.

1. BERTScore F1. The paper states: “BERTScore (F1): Utilized as our primary measure of semantic accuracy, this metric evaluates the contextual embedding similarity between candidate and reference texts, capturing meaning beyond exact word matches” [PAGE 5]. For candidate tokens \(\hat y\) and reference tokens \(y\), with contextual embedding \(E(\cdot)\) and cosine similarity,
\[
P=\frac{1}{|\hat y|}\sum_{\hat t_i\in \hat y}\max_{t_j\in y}\cos\!\left(E(\hat t_i),E(t_j)\right),
\quad
R=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t_j\in y}\max_{\hat t_i\in \hat y}\cos\!\left(E(\hat t_i),E(t_j)\right),
\]
\[
F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}.
\]
Units: \(P,R,F_1\in[0,1]\), dimensionless. Assumptions: contextual embeddings are fixed and normalized; token alignment by maximum cosine similarity is an acceptable proxy for semantic matching. Exact singularity: \(F_1\) is undefined if \(P+R=0\); implementation-level breakdown occurs with empty reference sets, zero-vector embeddings, or tokenizer mismatch. Architectural breakdown: high \(F_1\) can coexist with invalid architecture, as the source explicitly reports for sub-2B models [PAGE 7].

2. ROUGE-N and ROUGE-L. The paper describes recall-oriented overlap: “ROUGE Family (1, 2, L): These recall-oriented metrics quantify the extent to which the generated text covers the content of the ground truth, measuring overlap at the unigram, bigram, and longest-common-subsequence levels” [PAGE 5]. For reference n-grams \(g\),
\[
\mathrm{ROUGE\text{-}N}=\frac{\sum_{g\in \mathrm{ngrams}_N(y)}\min\!\left(c_g(\hat y),c_g(y)\right)}
{\sum_{g\in \mathrm{ngrams}_N(y)}c_g(y)},
\]
where \(c_g(\cdot)\) counts occurrences. Units: dimensionless ratio in \([0,1]\). Exact singularity: denominator zero if the reference has no N-grams at that order. Breakdown regime: paraphrase, synonymy, or valid alternative architecture yields low recall despite valid reasoning.

3. BLEU. The paper states: “BLEU: This metric assesses the precision of the generation, penalizing the inclusion of irrelevant or hallucinated tokens that do not appear in the reference text” [PAGE 5]. With modified precision \(p_n\) and brevity penalty \(BP\),
\[
\mathrm{BLEU}=BP\cdot\exp\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n\log p_n\right),
\quad
BP=\min\!\left(1,e^{1-|y|/|\hat y|}\right).
\]
Units: dimensionless. Exact singularity: \(\log p_n\to-\infty\) if \(p_n=0\); practical implementations require smoothing. Breakdown regime: technical synonyms and valid architectural variants are penalized as if hallucinated.

4. METEOR. The paper states: “METEOR: To account for linguistic variability, this metric evaluates alignment by considering synonyms, stemming, and paraphrasing, providing a more flexible measure of correctness than strict exact-matching” [PAGE 5]. Formally, METEOR is an alignment-based \(F\)-mean with a fragmentation penalty,
\[
F_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{10PR}{R+9P},
\quad
\mathrm{METEOR}=F_{\mathrm{mean}}\,(1-\mathrm{Penalty}).
\]
Units: dimensionless. Breakdown: alignment resources are language- and domain-dependent; architectural validity is still not tested.

5. Architectural Compliance Score. The paper defines “a scalar score (0−100) based on the technical validity of the decision rationale and its alignment with architectural best practices” [PAGE 5], using “Gemini-2.5-Flash as an expert evaluator” [PAGE 5]. For model \(m\), validation set \(D_{\mathrm{val}}\), judge \(J_\theta\), candidate \(\hat y_{m,i}\), and ground truth \(y_i\),
\[
C_m=\frac{1}{|D_{\mathrm{val}}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|D_{\mathrm{val}}|}
J_\theta(\hat y_{m,i},y_i),
\quad
J_\theta(\cdot,\cdot)\in[0,100].
\]
Units: percentage points on a bounded ordinal scale. Assumptions: the judge rubric tracks architectural validity; judge identity and prompt are fixed; judge familiarity with the ADR corpus does not materially bias scores. Breakdown regimes: prompt sensitivity, verbosity bias, stylistic bias, and corpus familiarity. The source concedes that “The LLM-as-a-Judge mechanism for Architectural Compliance Score may harbor biases favoring verbose or stylistically similar answers” [PAGE 8], and that “a potential familiarity bias cannot be fully excluded” [PAGE 8].

6. Semantic Diversity Score. The paper states: “For each test input, we generated 3 distinct candidate solutions using nucleus sampling” [PAGE 5], and defines the score as “the mean pairwise cosine distance between all candidate solutions for a given input” [PAGE 5]. For \(K=3\) candidate embeddings \(v_a,v_b\),
\[
D_{m,i}=\frac{2}{K(K-1)}\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq K}
\left(1-\frac{\langle v_a,v_b\rangle}{\|v_a\|_2\|v_b\|_2}\right).
\]
Units: dimensionless cosine distance, typically in \([0,2]\). Exact singularity: undefined if any \(v_a=0\) or \(\|v_a\|_2=0\). Breakdown regime: near-zero scores indicate “mode collapse or repetitive generation” [PAGE 5]; high scores may indicate hallucination rather than productive exploration, as the paper concludes for small off-the-shelf models [PAGE 8].

7. LoRA fine-tuning operator. The paper reports “rank r = 16, alpha α = 32 , and a dropout” [PAGE 4] with “rate of 0.5” [PAGE 5], and states training used “10 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of \(2\times10^{-4}\) and a linear warmup over the first 10% of steps” [PAGE 5]. For frozen weight \(W\), LoRA uses
\[
W'=W+\Delta W=W+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA,
\]
where \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}\), \(B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}\), \(r=16\), \(\alpha=32\). Units: matrix entries and rank are dimensionless integers/counts. Breakdown regimes: rank too low underfits; dropout \(0.5\) with only 76 training pairs can over-regularize; narrow-domain fine-tuning can over-specialize. The source reports exactly this risk: “Phi-3-mini’s compliance regressed from 72.1% under few-shot to 47.8% after fine-tuning” [PAGE 8], and “OLMo-2-1b exhibited the most notable decline, with its BERTScore F1 dropping by −4.4 pp and compliance falling by −13.9 pp relative to zero-shot” [PAGE 6].

8. Dataset operator. The paper uses “95 domain-specific ADRs systematically collected from five GitHub repositories” [PAGE 3], with “80% training and 20% validation splits” [PAGE 4] and “76 Context-Decision pairs” [PAGE 4]. Units: counts of ADRs and pairs. Breakdown regime: small-sample variance and limited domain coverage; no human-expert calibration baseline.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from older surface metrics to later judge-based semantics, and the retrocausal trap is that later metrics reinterpret earlier “good” results as failures. Historically, ADR evaluation inherited ROUGE, BLEU, and METEOR from NLP summarization and translation. The paper explains the limitation of that lineage: “Traditional text similarity metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU exhibit fundamental limitations when applied to technical domains” because “a design decision may achieve high textual similarity while violating core architectural principles” [PAGE 2]. The next layer is BERTScore and COMET, then the “LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm” and multi-dimensional frameworks such as Meta-rater and AXCEL [PAGE 2]. The paper’s own contribution is the SLM-ArchBench framework, which “moves beyond textual similarity to assess architectural reasoning” [PAGE 2]. Retrocausally, this means a 2024-era ROUGE-only reading of a sub-2B model could have celebrated lexical overlap, while the 2026 judge-based reading says the same model has low architectural compliance. The source makes that reversal explicit: sub-2B models can have “competitive BERTScore F1 values (0.805–0.825) yet substantially lower compliance (45.4%–56.3%)” [PAGE 7].

Multi-week traps follow from deployment time. First, data-leakage trap: ADRs are public GitHub artifacts, and the paper warns that “Data leakage poses the risks that ADRs may have appeared in pre-training corpora” [PAGE 8]. A multi-week evaluation that reuses public repositories can overstate zero-shot capability. Second, judge-familiarity trap: Gemini-2.5-Flash may have seen the same open-source ADR repositories, creating “potential familiarity bias” that “may inflate compliance scores uniformly across all evaluated models” [PAGE 8]. Third, over-specialization trap: LoRA on 76 pairs for 10 epochs can improve lexical similarity while degrading compliance, especially for models with strong prompting priors, as Phi-3-mini’s drop from 72.1% to 47.8% shows [PAGE 8]. Fourth, diversity-interpretation trap: a multi-week workflow that rewards novel outputs may reward hallucination, because “high diversity in small models more often signals hallucination than creative exploration” [PAGE 8]. Fifth, context-window trap: the paper finds that few-shot prompting helps select short-context mid-sized models, contradicting a simple “longer context is better” assumption; “Few-Shot prompting serves as a highly effective calibration mechanism for select mid-sized models with short context windows” [PAGE 1]. The genealogical lesson is that later evaluation layers do not merely add detail; they can invert the apparent ranking produced by earlier layers.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how practitioners model the SLM’s latent “belief” about architectural norms, and how the evaluator models the model. The crisis-habit regression is lexical mimicry under pressure. When an SLM faces ambiguous architectural context, its habitual response is to reproduce ADR vocabulary and formatting rather than perform trade-off reasoning. The evidence is the divergence between fluency and compliance: Gemma-3-1b “achieved a competitive BERTScore of 0.805 but recorded the lowest Compliance Score in the cohort at 45.4%” [PAGE 5], and SmolLM2-1.7b “scored 51.1% in compliance despite a BERTScore of 0.815” [PAGE 5]. In crisis, the model does not “know” it is failing; it produces plausible text. That is the regression.

The counter-trap is the assumption that more adaptation always corrects the regression. The paper shows that adaptation is model- and architecture-dependent. Under few-shot, “Phi-3-mini’s compliance rose by +5.7 percentage points (pp), and OLMo-2-7b recorded the largest gain at +11.2 pp” [PAGE 6], but “Llama-3.2-1b experienced the sharpest decline (−9.8 pp)” and Mistral-7b-v0.3 “dropped by −4.9 pp” [PAGE 6]. Under fine-tuning, some 1B models gain BERTScore but not compliance, while Phi-3-mini regresses sharply. Thus the counter-trap is “if zero-shot fails, fine-tune”; the source’s own conclusion is more conditional: “Fine-tuning is essential for the smallest models but optional for larger ones” [PAGE 8]. The practitioner must model a second-order uncertainty: the model’s own uncertainty is not observable, and the judge’s score is a proxy.

The clock is the experimental schedule and scale boundary. The paper’s clock is zero-shot, then k=2 few-shot, then LoRA with “10 epochs” and warmup “over the first 10% of steps” [PAGE 5]. The scale clock is the 3B threshold: “a clear capability boundary exists around the 3B-parameter threshold” [PAGE 9]. But the clock is not a physical time measurement; the paper does not report latency, energy, or wall-clock deployment. Therefore a recursive evaluator should not infer operational speed from the schedule. The correct recursive stance is: model the model as a conditional reasoner whose behavior changes with scale, example calibration, and fine-tuning data; model the judge as a biased observer; and model the deployment context as a distribution shift over weeks. The counter-trap is replacing one proxy with another—lexical score with judge score—without a human calibration study, which the authors admit is missing: “the absence of a human-expert baseline” [PAGE 8].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no biomechanical variables and no weather variables. A biomechanical veto would be category-inapplicable: the paper studies software architecture text generation, not human motion, load, or injury. A weather veto is also inapplicable: the paper motivates efficiency by “substantial carbon emissions” [PAGE 1], but it does not measure weather, temperature, or energy consumption. Therefore no weather or biomechanical claim can be accepted as an empirical finding from this source.

The physical/computational veto is real, but it is a compute-and-precision veto rather than a biomechanical one. The paper loads models with “4-bit Normal Float (NF4) quantization” [PAGE 4] and warns that this “may introduce precision loss affecting observed performance ceilings” [PAGE 8]. Thus any compliance difference smaller than plausible quantization and judge noise should be vetoed as a strong architectural conclusion. The physical deployment constraint also includes the SLM definition: “Models must contain fewer than 7 billion parameters” [PAGE 3], and the study targets “consumer-grade hardware deployment” [PAGE 3]. That is a legitimate hardware-layer veto: results are conditional on 4-bit consumer-grade inference, not full-precision cloud inference.

The data-layer veto is stronger than any physical veto. The dataset is “95 domain-specific ADRs” [PAGE 3] with “80% training and 20% validation splits” [PAGE 4]. The paper admits that ADRs “from open-source projects [7] may not fully represent enterprise contexts” [PAGE 4]. Therefore enterprise-scale architectural claims are vetoed unless replicated on enterprise data. The evaluator-layer veto is also explicit: the judge may favor verbose answers and may have familiarity bias [PAGE 8]. The source states that Compliance Score and BERTScore have “moderate, setting-dependent correlation” and “capture complementary rather than redundant quality dimensions” [PAGE 8], so neither metric alone can veto the other. Adjudication: accept the qualitative threshold and mixed-adaptation findings as source-internal evidence; veto universal deployment rules, exact percentage-point rankings, and any claim that fine-tuning reliably improves architectural compliance. The cross-layer proof is textual: the authors themselves list quantization precision loss, judge bias, small-domain dataset, and missing human baseline as validity threats [PAGE 8].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement or evaluation with verifiable rewards, where the reward must be independently checkable rather than judge-only. Three counterfactual vetoes follow.

Scenario 1: Replace Gemini-2.5-Flash with a blind human-architect panel and a pre-registered rubric, holding model outputs fixed. Verifiable reward: inter-rater agreement and rank correlation between judge scores and human scores. Prediction: the broad 3B-versus-sub-2B compliance gap will survive because the source already shows a large separation, but exact percentage-point values and small few-shot deltas will shrink or reorder. If Phi-3-mini’s few-shot advantage and fine-tuning regression survive blind human scoring, the source’s adaptation claim is strengthened; if not, the judge-bias veto fires. This directly tests the admitted “absence of a human-expert baseline” [PAGE 8].

Scenario 2: Randomize the k=2 few-shot examples to invalid ADRs, non-ADR text, or examples from unrelated repositories, and compare against the paper’s golden examples. Verifiable reward: paired compliance difference on held-out ADRs across multiple seeds. Prediction: the gains for Phi-3-mini and OLMo-2-7b will disappear or reverse under invalid examples, because the source interprets examples as a “calibration signal” rather than mere context length [PAGE 8]. If invalid examples produce the same gain, then the effect is a generic context or formatting effect, not architectural calibration. This veto tests the causal claim behind “Few-Shot prompting serves as a highly effective calibration mechanism” [PAGE 1].

Scenario 3: Re-run fine-tuning with repository-level held-out splits, early stopping, and a lower dropout, then compare compliance and BERTScore. Verifiable reward: leave-one-repository-out validation and exact replication of the LoRA schedule. Prediction: the severe regressions reported for OLMo-2-1b and Phi-3-mini will attenuate, and the apparent 1B BERTScore gains may not transfer to compliance. The source already reports that “OLMo-2-1b exhibited the most notable decline, with its BERTScore F1 dropping by −4.4 pp and compliance falling by −13.9 pp relative to zero-shot” [PAGE 6] and that Phi-3-mini “regressed from 72.1% under few-shot to 47.8% after fine-tuning” [PAGE 8]. If those regressions persist under leakage-controlled, early-stopped training, then the over-specialization veto is confirmed; if they vanish, the original fine-tuning conclusion was an artifact of the narrow 76-pair training regime. In all three scenarios, the verifiable veto is not a new model score but a reproducibility check against the source’s own stated threats: judge bias, data leakage, quantization, and small-domain data [PAGE 8].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
