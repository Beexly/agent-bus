The text on [PAGE 518] sits inside a dense 19th-century genealogy of spatial perception and motor control. The immediate clinical predecessor is von Graefe's touch-localization test, cited by James as the method that exposes the patient's directional error: the finger approaches the extreme possible outward line of vision rather than the 20° line of sight. This is an ophthalmological tool for dissociating perceived direction from actual gaze. The theoretical foil is the tradition of "efferent feeling" or innervation theory, in which the conscious impulse of will is treated as the primary cue for direction—a view James attributes to unnamed authors whose conclusions rest on the "supposed absence" of fellow-eye afference.

The footnote on [PAGE 518] identifies Professor G. E. Müller's Zur Grundlegung der Psychophysik (1878), p. 818, as the first explanation "after the manner advocated" by James. Müller's psychophysics placed emphasis on the completeness of the afferent inventory, directly anticipating James's complaint that earlier writers omitted the fellow eye. Before Müller, Helmholtz's theory of unconscious inference (Handbuch der physiologischen Optik, 1866ff.) had already separated the physical stimulus from the perceptual inference, implicitly allowing that non-retinal signals—including motor commands—enter perception. Hering's law of equal innervation (formalized in the late 1870s) supplies the missing mechanistic link: the neural impulse sent to the paretic left external rectus is yoked to an equal impulse in the right medial rectus, moving the covered eye and altering its afferent state even under occlusion.

Retro-causally, this lineage reaches forward to the mid-20th-century efference-copy / corollary discharge theories of Sperry (1950) and von Holst & Mittelstaedt, which formalize what James intuits: the brain predicts the sensory consequences of its own commands and compares prediction with afference. The Jamesian claim that the "effort" is an efferent feeling is a pre-cortical version of corollary discharge, but James lacks the modern distinction between the copy of the command and the actual proprioceptive reafference.

Multi-week traps are embedded in the stone-cutter example on [PAGE 518]: "until experience has taught him wisdom." This is not an instantaneous correction. It maps onto prism-adaptation and motor-recalibration literature in which systematic sensorimotor errors require days to weeks of exposure to diminish. Trap 1: assuming that monocular occlusion permanently isolates the fellow eye's afference; chronic patching induces adaptive changes in the uncovered eye's motor plant that persist after patch removal. Trap 2: assuming that the error is purely perceptual; the stone-cutter's hammer error is a motor-execution error driven by the same faulty forward model, so perceptual recalibration without motor recalibration is insufficient. Trap 3: assuming the re-calibrated state is stable; if the paretic condition resolves or worsens over weeks, the previously learned gain $\lambda$ or $\mu$ becomes maladaptive, producing a re-emergent directional error when the patient returns to normal binocular viewing.

This is a first-order model of self-motion ($\theta \approx f(u)$) nested inside a second-order model of the world ("the object is at the extreme lateral position"). Because the fellow eye is covered and its afferent changes are inaccessible to introspection, the recursive model has no internal veto; the subject cannot "see" that his own model is missing the $A_f$ term.

Crisis habit regression: under time pressure, novelty, or threat, the subject regresses to the default, high-effort strategy. The stone-cutter, surprised by a sudden object, will again over-innervate the paretic rectus and misjudge direction, striking his hand instead of the chisel [PAGE 518]. Regression is the collapse of the corrected binocular model back onto the naive efferent-only model when cognitive resources are depleted.

Counter-trap: the fellow eye's afferent signal is the natural counter-trap. Even under occlusion, Hering's law moves the covered eye, generating proprioceptive and vergence-related afference that contradicts the extreme-lateral percept. The counter-trap fails only because the subject's attention is directed to the paretic eye and the cover removes the most salient retinal error signal (diplopia), so the counter-trap signal remains sub-threshold for conscious correction. The counter-trap is therefore present physiologically but absent phenomenologically.

Clock: the process is explicitly timed. At $t=0$ (initial paresis), the error is maximal and the touch line diverges from the sight line [PAGE 518]. Over $t = \text{days to weeks}$, repeated mismatch between touch and vision supplies an error signal that recalibrates the forward model; the stone-cutter's "wisdom" is a clocked reduction in $\Delta\psi$. If the clock is stopped—if no feedback is permitted after each movement—the recalibration does not occur and the error persists indefinitely. Thus the theory of mind is not static; it is a time-dependent recursion in which the agent revises his self-model only when the sensory consequences of his commands are available for comparison.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical layer veto: the paretic external rectus imposes a hard mechanical bound on rotation. Once the orbit reaches its anatomical limit, no increase in $u$ can produce further $\theta$; the force-length curve saturates and $\partial F_m/\partial \ell \to 0$. This physical veto cannot be overridden by conscious will: the patient may *feel* as if the eye has gone to the extreme, but the actual rotation is capped at $\theta = 20^\circ$ [PAGE 518].

Biomechanical layer veto: Hering's law of equal innervation couples the paretic muscle to its yoke in the fellow eye. The excessive $u$ that produces the illusion simultaneously drives the covered right eye, changing its position, retinal image $R_f$, and proprioceptive afference $A_f$. This biomechanical veto is active even during monocular occlusion; occlusion blocks retinal rivalry and diplopia but does not block extraocular muscle afference or the motor command broadcast to the yoke. Therefore the veto is not a retinal cue but a corollary-discharge/proprioceptive signal that should, in principle, flag the discrepancy between intended and actual gaze.

Weather/environmental layer veto: changes in illumination, contrast, or retinal illuminance alter the reliability of $p(R|\theta)$ but do not alter the motor command $u$ or the mechanical limit of the paretic muscle. Under low light, retinal signals become noisier, so a Bayesian observer should upweight the efference copy and the fellow-eye proprioceptive veto relative to the retinal image. Crucially, weather and lighting cannot "cure" the physical saturation of the paretic muscle; they can only re-weight the relative trust in the conflicting cues. The veto is thus weather-robust: whether the environment is bright or dim, the physical and biomechanical constraints on $\theta$ remain identical.

Adjudication: the combined physical + biomechanical veto forms a proof against the naive theory. Because the hand movement in von Graefe's test is guided by the same excessive motor plan that mislocates the eye, the touch vector diverges from the sight line exactly in proportion to the gap between saturated efference copy and bounded physical rotation [PAGE 518]. No amount of conscious attention to the paretic eye can close this gap; only re-integration of the fellow-eye afference (or removal of the paresis) can restore veridical direction.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verified-rewards (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Fellow-eye uncovering veto.
Condition: patient with left external rectus paresis, fellow eye covered as in James's experiment [PAGE 518]. Counterfactual: remove the cover and permit normal binocular viewing with intact retinal correspondence. Verified reward: verbal report or pointing response indicating perceived object direction. Prediction: perceived eccentricity shifts from "extreme lateral" toward veridical $20^\circ$ because $A_f$ and $R_f$ now provide a congruent binocular veto. Null-veto condition: replace clear viewing with a frosted lens over the fellow eye that blocks the retinal image but preserves extraocular muscle afference; prediction holds partially (proprioceptive veto remains). If the fellow eye is rendered totally unable to move (e.g., complete ptosis or external palsy blocking yoke motion), the naive extreme-lateral error returns, verifying that the veto requires active fellow-eye afference.

Scenario 2 — Active versus passive touch veto.
Condition: von Graefe localization test on [PAGE 518], where the patient actively touches the object with the forefinger. Counterfactual: the experimenter moves the patient's finger passively to the object while the patient maintains gaze; the active motor plan for the arm is removed. Verified reward: angular difference $\Delta\psi = \angle(\mathbf{v}_{\text{touch}}, \mathbf{v}_{\text{sight}})$. Prediction: active touch shows $\Delta\psi > 0$ because the arm path is yoked to the excessive innervation signal; passive touch reduces or eliminates $\Delta\psi$ because the touch vector no longer reflects the patient's efferent command. Verification: kinematic recording of finger trajectory; active trials show systematic overshoot toward the extreme lateral line, passive trials align with the 20° sight line.

Scenario 3 — Stone-cutter no-feedback veto.
Condition: stone-cutter with left external rectus paresis initially strikes his hand instead of the chisel [PAGE 518]. Counterfactual: after each hammer swing, vision and tactile outcome are masked before the subject can perceive a miss or hit (no verified reward signal). Verified reward: trial-by-trial error rate (hit chisel vs. hit hand). Prediction: with full visual/tactile feedback, error rate decays exponentially over trials as the forward model is recalibrated ("experience has taught him wisdom"); with post-movement sensory deprivation, the error rate remains flat because the efference copy cannot be updated without an outcome signal. This verifies that the correction is experience-dependent clocking, not spontaneous insight.

</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
