<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage. The page is from Hull, C.L. (1943), Principles of Behavior, the classic statement of deductive drive-reduction theory in which reaction potentiality sEr is posited as a function of drive D and habit strength H [PAGE 239]. The present passage explicitly imports Figure 52 from Skinner (1938), The Behavior of Organisms, p. 396 [PAGE 239], grafting Skinner's empirical food-deprivation/response-strength curve onto Hull's equation-based learning formalism. This creates a hybrid genealogy: Thorndike's law of effect → Hull's mathematical habit-drive algebra → Skinner's operant deprivation curves → the page's synthesized surface (Figure 51) → modern reinforcement-learning treatises of drive as homeostatic perturbation.

A key retro-causal link is the deferred threshold discussion: the negative intercept of ≈ 4 extinctive reactions is flagged as "a phenomenon of the reaction threshold which will be discussed in some detail later (p. 322)" [PAGE 239]. The backward extrapolation from observed extinction curves to a hypothetical N = 0 state defines the threshold by the very absence of responding it purports to explain—a self-referential operational loop that reappears in later learning theory as the distinction between performance and learning.

Multi-week trap. The text documents an experiment in which deprivation is extended day by day until "the animals died soon after" the sharp fall in potentiality [PAGE 239]. This is the archetypal prolonged-deprivation trap: as the experimenter lengthens the privation axis to test whether drive keeps raising response potential, the biological substrate exits the behavioral regime entirely. The right tail of the curve is not a measurable decline in motivation but a morbidity boundary. Any research program that treats "more deprivation = more drive = more response" as indefinitely extensible will re-run this trap, mistaking pre-mortem collapse for a drive-driven inversion. The "multi-week" character is implicit: once deprivation runs past one day into several days and toward death, the data-generating process switches from learning curves to survival curves.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. Under escalating deprivation (the crisis condition), the organism does not express a smoothly increasing reaction potential. Instead it regresses: first to high-rate responding at the training drive level (one day), then to a brief continued rise, and finally to sharp decline and death as exhaustion takes over [PAGE 239]. Habit strength itself is not what collapses; the organism's capacity to emit the habit collapses because viability fails. The recursion is that performance R(N; D) is read out through a dying substrate, so the measured curve conflates habit strength with physiological endurance.

Counter-trap. The experimenter's intuitive trap is monotonicity: assuming that because A(D) is an "increasing function" of privation over the observed low-D range, the increase continues indefinitely [PAGE 239]. The text explicitly identifies the counter-trap: the smooth Figure 49 curve "does not represent the functional relationship beyond ... 23" hours, and the Figure 52 fall "is evidently due to exhaustion" [PAGE 239]. Assuming the asymptote keeps rising with D past the exhaustion boundary is the counter-trap the page warns against.

Clock. Two temporal boundaries govern valid inference. The hard clock is D = 23 h, beyond which the Figure 49 function is invalid [PAGE 239]. The biological clock is the "died soon after" terminus following the multi-day peak [PAGE 239]. Data collection must close before these clocks expire; extrapolation past them is not forecasting but category error.

Recursive threshold. The rule that "a habit strength sufficient to resist four extinction reactions is necessary before reaction will be evoked" [PAGE 239] makes response evocation conditional on a history of non-response. The system must already be capable of withstanding ≈ 4 extinction trials before the first evoked reaction is counted—a bootstrapping condition in which the criterion for response is defined by prior resistance to non-reinforcement.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof, adjudicated in causal order.

Physical veto (mortality): The highest-priority veto is death. The text states the fall in potentiality "is evidently due to exhaustion, as the animals died soon after" [PAGE 239]. Any experimental condition driving D beyond the peak into the multi-day range is physically vetoed: no behavioral response can be emitted by a dead organism. This truncates the drive axis absolutely and invalidates any monotonic extension of A(D) or P(D) into high-D regimes.

Biomechanical veto (motor output): Even before death, starvation degrades the biomechanical capacity to emit extinctive reactions. R(N; D) is measured as a count of reactions [PAGE 239]; if motor vigor falls with depletion, a declining count reflects output failure, not reduced reaction potentiality. This veto applies most strongly at high D and confounds the interpretation of the sharp fall as purely motivational.

Environmental/ambient veto (generalization boundary): The source is a controlled laboratory preparation; ambient temperature, humidity, and thermal load are not reported but would modulate metabolic depletion rate and therefore shift the 23 h / multi-day exhaustion boundary if the preparation were moved outside the vivarium. Noted as an external validity veto for any field or cross-species generalization; not present in the text [PAGE 239].

Veto adjudication order: Physical (death) > Biomechanical (motor failure) > Model extrapolation (Figure 49 invalid beyond D = 23 h) > Behavioral interpretation (drive-potential monotonicity). A conclusion that potentiality rises without bound with food privation is vetoed at the first layer. The only non-vetoed regime is D ∈ [0, 23] h for the Figure 49 smooth curve, and the limited multi-day window before exhaustion for Figure 52, within which the reported positive intercept at D = 0 and the ≈ 4-reaction threshold hold.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards / counterfactual) veto scenarios, each with a condition, veto mechanism, and falsifiable prediction.

Veto Scenario 1 — Satiation and the naive-subject veto.
Condition: Test a fully sated subject (D = 0 h) with no prior reinforcement history (N history = 0). The page's surface extrapolated to D = 0 predicts "a positive growth function with n-values of considerable magnitude" [PAGE 239], which could be misread as implying responding at zero privation even without training.
Veto mechanism: The positive D = 0 intercept applies only to trained habits; with N = 0 there is no habit strength to exceed the ≈ 4-reaction threshold [PAGE 239]. Drive at zero cannot evoke a response in a naive organism.
Falsifiable prediction: Response rate at D = 0 for a naive subject ≈ 0 extinction reactions; only subjects with prior reinforcement (N above threshold) show nonzero n-values at D = 0. A nonzero response at D = 0 in a confirmed naive subject falsifies the threshold interpretation.

Veto Scenario 2 — Exhaustion veto beyond the validity boundary.
Condition: Extend food privation beyond the Figure 49 boundary (D > 23 h) into the multi-day range where Figure 52 peaks and then falls [PAGE 239]. A naive extrapolation of the smooth exponential curve would predict continued approach to, or maintenance of, a high asymptote.
Veto mechanism: The decline "is evidently due to exhaustion, as the animals died soon after" [PAGE 239]; mortality absorbs the regime, so no behavioral asymptote is reachable.
Falsifiable prediction: Reaction potentiality P(D) will decline precipitously after the peak (several days) and survival will drop toward zero; no stable high-D asymptote exists. Observation of sustained high response rates beyond the exhaustion boundary without mortality would falsify the reported exhaustion account.

Veto Scenario 3 — Threshold-buffer veto in early extinction.
Condition: Interrupt extinction after only 3 non-reinforced reactions in subjects trained to varying habit strengths. The backward extrapolation yields ≈ 4 extinctive reactions as the necessary buffer: "a habit strength sufficient to resist four extinction reactions is necessary before reaction will be evoked" [PAGE 239].
Veto mechanism: Subjects whose habit strength is below threshold cannot evoke responding during the first extinction trials, even under elevated drive D.
Falsifiable prediction: Weakly trained subjects will emit zero responses during the first 3–4 extinction trials regardless of D; responding emerges only after sufficient acquisition has built the ≈ 4-reaction buffer. Strongly trained subjects should respond from trial 1 of extinction. A weakly trained subject responding vigorously at extinction trial 1 under high D falsifies the threshold rule.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
