<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under pressure—late downs, trailing score, or noisy environment—the offense tends to regress to its most drilled script, here the off-tackle slight swing, because the conditional blocker assignments are overlearned and require minimal on-field diagnosis. The defense, in turn, regresses to edge contain: the defensive end widens and the safety funnels inside. Mutual regression collapses the interaction to a known fixed point where both sides execute conditioned responses.

Counter-trap: Because the offense expects the defense to play contain against the off-tackle swing, it can instead run the field-reversal play described at the top of [PAGE 62]. No. 3 takes the ball, cuts back across the grain while the defense flows to the off-tackle edge. The "removed end" blocking scheme then serves as a backside seal rather than a frontside wall. This is a second-order counter-trap: if the defense anticipates the counter-trap and plays disciplined contain, the straight off-tackle swing re-opens. The system therefore cycles rather than settling at a single equilibrium.

Clock layer: The snap-to-whistle interval imposes a hard clock. The delayed release of the removed end (\Delta t_{release}) creates a temporal window in which the secondary is unblocked. If the defense diagnoses the play before t_{release}, the defensive back can fill before No. 1 arrives; if not, No. 1 reaches the defensive back and the blocker advantage propagates to the second level. The clock converts the spatial "slight swing" into a race condition between blocker arrival time and defender diagnosis time. In this recursion, the offense's theory of mind is "the defense thinks I will swing, so I will reverse," while the defense's counter-theory is "the offense knows I expect the swing, so I will hold the edge."
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Layer 1—physical: Pinning force balance. The offensive lineman can apply maximum horizontal force F_{OL,max}; the defensive end applies F_{DE}. If F_{DE} > F_{OL,max} + F_{assist} (assist from tight end or back), the pin breaks and the defensive end crosses the line of scrimmage before the runner arrives. The off-tackle play is vetoed; the ball carrier meets an unblocked defender in the backfield.

Layer 2—biomechanical: The "slight swing" requires lateral acceleration a_{lat} = v^2 / \rho, where \rho is the radius of curvature of the runner's path. The maximum sustainable lateral acceleration before shoe-surface slip is a_{lat,max} = \mu g, where g = 9.81 m/s^2 and \mu is the shoe-turf coefficient of friction. If the required a_{lat} exceeds \mu g, the runner's feet slide, the swing straightens unintentionally, and the runner is funneled into defensive pursuit.

Layer 3—weather: Rain or snow reduces \mu and introduces liquid lubrication between shoe and surface. It also reduces ball grip, affecting the handoff to No. 3 and ball security during the reversal. Under low-\mu conditions, the Layer 2 biomechanical veto is triggered more easily, and the "slight swing" becomes an unpredictable arc rather than a controlled cut.

Veto hierarchy: A failed pin (Layer 1) vetoes the play before Layer 2 or 3 are reached. If the pin holds but \mu is low, Layer 2 vetoes execution. The offense's only non-vetoed branch is the conditional reassignment: No. 1 to defensive back, No. 2 to end. Exact breakdown regime: \|\mathbf{x}_{DE} - \mathbf{x}_{OL}\| > \epsilon_{pin} for t < t_{runner\_arrival}; no aperture exists; the play ends in a loss of yardage or no gain. These layers are physical and environmental, not wagering constructs.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
