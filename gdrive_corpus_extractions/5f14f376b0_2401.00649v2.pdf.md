The BLUE theorem's name is itself retro-causal: "David and Neyman (1938) used the name 'Markoff Theorem'. Lehmann (1951) appeared to first use the name 'Gauss–Markov Theorem'" [PAGE 53]. The robust covariance is a three-root convergence — "Eicker (1967) first proposed to use $\hat V_{\mathrm{ehw}}$. White (1980a) popularized it in economics," with "Cox (1961) and Huber (1967)" on misspecified parametric models [PAGE 82]. The misspecification spine (Huber 1967 → White 1980b → Buja et al. 2019a) is what the preface flags as the book's distinguishing feature: "This books introduces the theory of misspecified models and emphasizes its implications" [PAGE 20]. FWL-for-standard-errors is the newest graft: "Theorem 7.1 is well known for a long time but Theorem 7.2 is less well known … Ding (2021a) proved Theorem 7.2" [PAGE 96].

(ii) "Linear = linear in covariates" (violating R3, [PAGE 30]). (iii) Treating $R^2$ as fit quality — the whole of Chapter 13 is an antidote: "we cannot over-interpret the sample $R^2$, because it can be too optimistic" [PAGE 143]. (iv) Reporting default `lm` SEs under heteroskedasticity.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Reframed for inference: model the *practitioner under deadline* whose habits, counter-traps, and clock we must predict. **Crisis-habit regression:** faced with a `summary(lm(...))` table, the habitual read is the default homoskedastic SE (`se1`) and $R^2$. Figure 6.1 [PAGE 52–53] shows the pathology directly — under heteroskedastic errors "se2 is close to se0, but se1 underestimates se0," i.e., the habit is *confidently wrong* while the coefficient estimate remains symmetric and bell-shaped. The habit persists because Normality of errors is *not* what breaks (Simulation panels 1–2 vs 3–4, [PAGE 51–53]); homoskedasticity is, and that is invisible in a point estimate. **Counter-trap:** the trained move is to leave $\hat\beta$ alone and swap in HC3/EHW — "We do not need to modify the OLS estimator but only need to modify the covariance estimator" [PAGE 88]. Long & Ervin (2000) recommend HC3 in finite samples [PAGE 82]. A second-order counter-trap is leverage triage: because $h_{ii}$ feeds every LOO diagnostic ($\hat\varepsilon_{[-i]}=\hat\varepsilon_i/(1-h_{ii})$, [PAGE 135, Thm 11.3]; Cook's distance $\propto h_{ii}/(1-h_{ii})$, [PAGE 138]), a practitioner reasoning about influence must read $h_{ii}$ *before* trusting any residual. **The clock:** every robust claim is asymptotic, and [PAGE 88, L1] admits "We are often unsure whether the sample size is large enough." The theory-of-mind prediction: the deadline-driven analyst reaches for $R^2$ and default SEs; the counter-trap is EHW+leverage; the clock decides whether the asymptotic guarantee has "landed," and when $p/n$ is non-negligible (Chapter 13) it has not.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

A "physical-layer veto" here is an assumption violation that overrides a downstream conclusion.

**Veto 1 (homoskedasticity veto).** Claim: "OLS default SEs are valid." Under Assumption 6.1 ($\mathrm{var}(\varepsilon_i)=\sigma_i^2$, [PAGE 75]) the true covariance is $n^{-1}B_n^{-1}M_nB_n^{-1}\ne\sigma^2(X^tX)^{-1}$. The book states the veto plainly: "(L3) Under the heteroskedastic linear model, the Gauss–Markov theorem does not hold, so the OLS can be inefficient" [PAGE 88]. ∎ The default SE conclusion is vetoed; only the sandwich survives.

**Veto 2 (leverage/geometry veto).** Claim: "$\hat\beta$ is asymptotically Normal." Huber (1973) is cited for the iff: "all linear combinations of the OLS coefficient are asymptotically Normal if and only if the maximum leverage score converges to 0" [PAGE 133]. Hence a design with $\max_i h_{ii}\not\to0$ (e.g., $h_{ii}=1$, forcing $\hat\varepsilon_i=0$ and $\hat y_i=y_i$, [PAGE 141, Problem 11.5]) *vetoes* the CLT regardless of $n$. ∎

**Veto 3 (misspecification-interpretation veto).** Claim: "$\beta$ is the structural effect." Example 12.1 [PAGE 128] proves the best-linear-approximation slope is $F(x)$-dependent: for $y=x^2+\varepsilon$, $\beta(F_1)=0$ (Uniform$(-1,1)$), $\beta(F_2)=1$ (Uniform$(0,1)$). So "the best linear approximation depends on the distribution of x … This complicates the interpretation of $\beta$" [PAGE 127]. ∎ The *causal/structural* interpretation is vetoed — **but** the inferential layer is *not*: EHW remains consistent for the projection coefficient under $E\|x\|^4<\infty$, $E(y^4)<\infty$ [PAGE 130, Thm 12.7]. The adjudication: violations veto *interpretation* and *default variance*, never the projection estimand itself.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

**Scenario A — Anscombe's Quartet [PAGE 42–44].** Four datasets share $\bar x=9$, $\mathrm{var}(x)=11$, $\bar y\approx7.50$, and near-identical fits ($\hat\beta\approx0.500$, $R^2\approx0.666$, $F\approx17.99$). *Veto:* the fourth, supported on two $x$-values, "may suffer from severe extrapolation" [PAGE 44]. *Verifiable prediction:* any pipeline keyed on $R^2$/$t$/$F$ alone returns the *same* verdict on all four; only residual-vs-$x$ plots separate them. Falsifiable: compute the four `summary(lm)` outputs — they match to the printed digits.

**Scenario B — Boston housing heteroskedasticity [PAGE 62–63].** *Veto:* default SE vs EHW disagree sharply. *Verifiable prediction (from the printed table):* for `lstat`, $t_{\mathrm{ols}}=-10.35$ collapses to $t_{\mathrm{hc4}}=-4.93$; for `rm`, $9.12\to4.18$; the log-transform does *not* fix it ($-14.30\to-7.28$). Prediction: ranking covariates by naive significance reorders under HC — checkable directly against the reported $t$-tables.

**Scenario C — Freedman overfitting [PAGE 141–142].** Simulate $Y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with $\beta=(\mu,0,\dots,0)^t$, $n=100$, $p=50$, so $p/n=0.5$ and *no* covariate carries signal. *Veto:* $R^2$ as fit. *Quantitative prediction:* $R^2\sim\mathrm{Beta}\!\big(\tfrac{p-1}{2},\tfrac{n-p}{2}\big)$ [PAGE 141], so $\mathbb E(R^2)=\tfrac{p-1}{n-1}=\tfrac{49}{99}\approx0.495$ and $\mathrm{var}(R^2)=\tfrac{2(p-1)(n-p)}{(n-1)^2(n+1)}$; as $p/n\to\gamma\in(0,1)$, $R^2\to\gamma$ in probability [PAGE 143]. Falsifiable: the histogram centers at $0.5$, matching Figure 13.1's first panel; a two-step "select $p<0.25$ then test" flow produces *non-uniform* $p$-values (panel 2,2), confirming selective-inference invalidation.

**Cross-scenario invariant:** in all three, the point estimator (slope, fitted value) is *not* vetoed — only the *summary statistic* ($R^2$, default SE, naive $p$) is. The correct counter-move each time is the book's own: leave $\hat\beta$/$\hat Y$ intact, replace the covariance or the fit criterion (EHW, PRESS/GCV, adjusted-$R^2$). That is the single lesson the six layers converge on, and it is verifiable against every cited page.
