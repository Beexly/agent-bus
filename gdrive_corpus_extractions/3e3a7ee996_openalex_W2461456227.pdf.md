\[
I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\quad [\mathrm{bits}],
\]
or in nats with \(\ln\). Probability is dimensionless; entropy is in bits or nats. Assumption: labels are stable across the retrieval window. Breakdown/singularity: \(I=0\) under independence; terms are undefined if \(p(x)=0\) or \(p(y)=0\); retrieval collapses under label drift and nonstationary semantics. The source’s motivation is exactly this retrieval need: “how to effectively and quickly retrieve the part of coaches or athletes need from these data” [PAGE 1].

2. Marked-feature template matching. For an image patch \(W\) around a predicted joint and a template \(T\), a sum-of-squared-differences matcher is
\[
\hat{\mathbf u}=\arg\min_{\mathbf u}\sum_{\mathbf p\in W}\left[I_t(\mathbf u+\mathbf p)-I_{t-1}(\mathbf u_0+\mathbf p)\right]^2\quad [\mathrm{intensity}^2].
\]
Assumption: local brightness constancy and small displacement. Breakdown/singularity: the Hessian is rank-deficient in textureless windows, producing the aperture problem; motion blur, occlusion, specularity, and repetitive texture create false minima. The source explicitly uses “template matching method to detect feature” [PAGE 3] and warns that “Feature matching has been a very difficult problem” [PAGE 2].

3. Sum-of-Gaussians body/image representation. The source states: “Each input image is converted into a SoG representation” [PAGE 4]. A formal SoG is
\[
S(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\mu_i,\Sigma_i),
\]
with \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^2\) in pixels for images or \(\mathbb R^3\) in metres for body models; \(w_i\) dimensionless, \(\boldsymbol\mu_i\) in px or m, \(\Sigma_i\) in px\(^2\) or m\(^2\). Assumption: each component is non-degenerate and weights are non-negative. Breakdown/singularity: \(\det\Sigma_i=0\) makes a component singular; all-zero weights make \(S\) vanish; component collapse or occlusion destroys the representation. The source uses “a Sum of Gaussians” for the human body model [PAGE 3].

4. SoG overlap objective. A normalized overlap similarity can be written
\[
\mathcal O(\theta)=\frac{\int_\Omega \min\{S_M(\mathbf x;\theta),S_I(\mathbf x)\}\,d\mathbf x}{\int_\Omega \max\{S_M(\mathbf x;\theta),S_I(\mathbf x)\}\,d\mathbf x+\varepsilon},
\]
dimensionless after normalization; unnormalized integrals carry px\(^2\) or m\(^2\). Assumption: model and image are in a common calibrated frame. Breakdown/singularity: \(\varepsilon\to0\) with zero denominator; symmetric or occluded shapes make the maximum non-identifiable; high overlap can coexist with wrong depth or swapped limbs. The source’s objective is that tracking “optimizes the parameters such that the overlap similarity between the model and image SoG at the current frame is maximized” [PAGE 4].

5. Kinematic-chain transform. For joint \(j\) with parent \(p(j)\),
\[
T_j(\theta)=T_{p(j)}(\theta)\,\Delta T_j(q_j),\qquad
q_j=\exp\!\left([\boldsymbol\omega_j]_\times\alpha_j\right),
\]
with translations in metres, rotations in radians, and \(\alpha_j\) an axis-angle angle. Assumption: rigid bone offsets and a fixed parent hierarchy. Breakdown/singularity: axis-angle coordinates are non-unique at \(\alpha=0\) and \(\alpha=\pi\); zero or undefined parent offsets break the chain; clothing, soft tissue, growth, or marker slip violate rigid-offset assumptions. The source states: “Each joint is defined by an offset to its parent joint and a rotation represented in axis-angle form” [PAGE 3], and “The skeleton consists of 58 joints” [PAGE 3].

6. Reprojection residual. For camera projection \(\pi\), camera intrinsics \(K\), and observed image point \(\mathbf u_{ij}\),
\[
e_{ij}=\left\|\pi\!\left(K,T_j(\theta)\mathbf X_j\right)-\mathbf u_{ij}\right\|_2\quad [\mathrm{px}].
\]
Assumption: calibrated camera and correct correspondence. Breakdown/singularity: depth \(z\to0\) makes projection singular; calibration drift, rolling shutter, and wrong correspondences inflate \(e_{ij}\) without indicating pose error.

7. Rigid-bone physical constraint. For a bone of reference length \(L_j\),
\[
\left|\,\left\|\mathbf x_j(t)-\mathbf x_{p(j)}(t)\right\|_2-L_j\,\right|\le \epsilon_L\quad [\mathrm m].
\]
Assumption: rigid segment and stable marker placement. Breakdown/singularity: \(\epsilon_L\) must exceed measurement noise; if not, false vetoes occur. If the residual exceeds \(\epsilon_L\), the track is physically inadmissible under the model.

8. Multi-camera clock alignment. For frame period \(1/f_s\),
\[
\hat\tau=\arg\max_{\tau}\sum_t\left\langle S_1(t),S_2(t+\tau)\right\rangle,\qquad |\hat\tau|\le \frac{1}{2f_s}\quad [\mathrm s].
\]
Assumption: common event visible in both views and no dropped frames. Breakdown/singularity: periodic gait can alias when \(|\tau|>1/(2f_s)\); dropped frames and rolling shutter break the sampling model. The source mentions “cam n synchronized and calibrated video cameras” [PAGE 3], so synchronization is architecturally required.

9. Weather/photometric contrast. For marker mean \(\mu_m\), background mean \(\mu_b\), and background standard deviation \(\sigma_b\),
\[
\mathrm{SNR}=20\log_{10}\frac{\mu_m-\mu_b}{\sigma_b}\quad [\mathrm{dB}],
\]
or a dimensionless contrast \(C=(\mu_m-\mu_b)/\sigma_b\). Assumption: approximately stationary background noise. Breakdown/singularity: \(\mu_m=\mu_b\) gives \(\mathrm{SNR}\to-\infty\); rain, snow, glare, shadow, and motion blur create false blobs. The source’s active-marker discussion relies on “high-contrast background” [PAGE 2], so low contrast is an explicit failure mode.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has four agents: the athlete executing the movement, the coach interpreting the movement, the analyst tagging and retrieving it, and the system presenting evidence. At level 0, the athlete’s movement is constrained by biomechanics. At level 1, the coach observes a video and infers intent, fatigue, or technical error. At level 2, the analyst models the coach’s inference and the athlete’s self-model; the analyst asks not only “what happened?” but “what will the coach believe happened, and what does the athlete believe the coach expects?” At level 3, the system must model the analyst’s trust: a high-confidence SoG overlay can create false certainty, while a low-confidence overlay can be ignored. The source’s coach-facing aim is explicit: the method is meant to help “coaches in guiding the training under the guidance of empirical premise” [PAGE 4]. That makes trust calibration a first-class requirement, not a cosmetic interface issue.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under time pressure, injury uncertainty, or a poor competition block, the analyst regresses to familiar manual joint labeling or a single-camera judgment. This is not simply irrational: familiar habits have high perceived reliability, and the source’s own marked method is intuitive because “a subject joints affixed obvious markers” [PAGE 1]. The regression trap is that manual labels become the hidden reference even when the automated system is better calibrated. A counter-trap is the opposite: overcorrection into automation. The analyst sees a smooth SoG overlay and treats “overlap similarity … maximized” [PAGE 4] as proof of correctness. But overlap can be maximized by a wrong limb configuration under occlusion or marker swap. The recursive theory of mind must therefore represent both the analyst’s habitual prior and the system’s false-confidence prior. The clock is the third term. There are two clocks: the physical synchronization clock, which aligns multi-view frames, and the training-cycle clock, which determines when feedback reaches the athlete. A coach reviewing week-1 video in week-4 may attribute an old error to the current athlete state; the athlete may then overcorrect a problem that no longer exists. The system should timestamp every tag and display the delay between event, retrieval, and feedback.

The counter-trap versus clock distinction is important. The counter-trap is a belief trap: the analyst believes the automated pose because it looks coherent. The clock trap is a temporal trap: the analyst believes the retrieved video is contemporaneous with the athlete’s current state. Both are recursive: the coach’s belief changes the athlete’s belief, which changes future movement, which changes the next video. A robust design should expose uncertainty, calibration epoch, and retrieval time; it should also allow the coach to mark “this is historical” versus “this is current.” The source’s retrieval goal is to “save video of blind search time” [PAGE 4], but saving search time is not the same as saving interpretive time. If the system hides provenance, it accelerates the wrong feedback loop. The correct recursive stance is: the system models the analyst’s possible overtrust, the analyst models the coach’s possible overgeneralization, and the coach models the athlete’s possible overcorrection. Only then can a retrieved clip be used as evidence rather than as an authority signal.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is an ordered admissibility test. A high SoG overlap is necessary but not sufficient; the track must pass calibration/clock, physical, biomechanical, and weather layers before it can become a retrieval tag. The source supplies the model substrate: “connection type non-rigid motion constraints” [PAGE 2], “a kinematic skeleton” [PAGE 3], “58 joints” [PAGE 3], and SoG overlap maximization [PAGE 4]. The veto layers are external formal checks on that substrate.

Physical veto proof. For a rigid segment with endpoints \(\mathbf x_j,\mathbf x_{p(j)}\) and reference length \(L_j\), define \(L_j(t)=\|\mathbf x_j(t)-\mathbf x_{p(j)}(t)\|_2\). Under a common rigid transform, distances are preserved, so any observed change beyond measurement uncertainty \(\epsilon_L\) implies that at least one of the following is false: camera calibration, point correspondence, or rigid-body assumption. Formally, if \(|L_j(t)-L_j|\le \epsilon_L\) for all \(j,t\), the skeleton is physically admissible. If \(|L_j(t)-L_j|>\epsilon_L\), the track is vetoed or downweighted. The threshold must satisfy \(\epsilon_L\ge 2\max_t\|\delta\mathbf x(t)\|_2\), the worst-case endpoint noise. This is the proof obligation: a track that changes bone length by more than twice the endpoint uncertainty cannot be accepted as a rigid-body measurement. The source’s rod and multi-rigid model language [PAGE 2] makes this veto mandatory.

Biomechanical veto proof. Let \(\alpha_j(t)\) be a joint angle in radians and \(\dot\alpha_j(t)\) its angular velocity in rad/s. Physiological admissibility requires \(\alpha_j(t)\in[\alpha_j^{\min},\alpha_j^{\max}]\) and \(|\dot\alpha_j(t)|\le\omega_j^{\max}\). If the tracked pose exits these bounds, either the correspondence has jumped to a different limb or the model has collapsed. The proof is by contradiction: a true human joint cannot exceed its anatomical range in the observation window; therefore an out-of-range estimate is not a valid measurement of that joint. The veto should be asymmetric: a small excursion may be downweighted, while a large excursion or impossible angular velocity is rejected. This layer also catches marker swap, because a swapped left/right knee often preserves overlap while violating angular continuity.

Weather veto proof. Marker detection can be modeled as a hypothesis test between marker present, \(H_1:\mu=\mu_m\), and background, \(H_0:\mu=\mu_b\), with noise standard deviation \(\sigma_b\). The contrast is \(C=(\mu_m-\mu_b)/\sigma_b\), or \(\mathrm{SNR}=20\log_{10}C\) dB. For a fixed false-alarm rate, detection probability falls monotonically as \(C\) decreases. If \(C<C_{\min}\), the likelihood ratio is too weak to identify the marker, and rain streaks, snow, glare, or shadow can produce false positives. The proof obligation is: no tag may be accepted when \(\mathrm{SNR}<\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\) unless an independent modality confirms it. The source’s active-marker branch depends on “high-contrast background” [PAGE 2], so this veto is directly aligned with the paper’s own hardware assumption.

Clock veto proof. For frame rate \(f_s\), the Nyquist half-period is \(1/(2f_s)\). If multi-view offset \(|\hat\tau|>1/(2f_s)\), periodic gait can alias; a foot strike in view 1 may be matched to the wrong phase in view 2. The proof is the sampling theorem: once the relative delay exceeds half a frame period, the correspondence between periodic events is not uniquely determined by the sampled sequence. Therefore any tag depending on multi-view fusion must be vetoed when \(|\hat\tau|>1/(2f_s)\) or when the sync residual exceeds the calibration uncertainty. The source’s “cam n synchronized and calibrated video cameras” [PAGE 3] is a requirement, not a guarantee; the veto enforces it.

Adjudication order: clock/calibration first, because a skewed time base corrupts every downstream layer; physical second, because rigid-length violation invalidates the skeleton; biomechanical third, because joint-limit violation invalidates the movement interpretation; weather fourth, because low contrast invalidates the feature evidence; retrieval last. If layers disagree, the stricter admissibility layer wins. High SoG overlap cannot overrule a bone-length violation; a smooth trajectory cannot overrule an impossible joint angle; a confident marker detection cannot overrule a low-SNR weather veto.
