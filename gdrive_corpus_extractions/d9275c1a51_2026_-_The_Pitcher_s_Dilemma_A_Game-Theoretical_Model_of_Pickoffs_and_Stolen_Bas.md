<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is already embedded in the information sets: the pitcher observes lead distance but not intent, so the pitcher's information set contains one node for each \(b\in B\) [PAGE 2]. The baserunner must therefore model the pitcher's posterior over “steal” versus “stay” given the lead; the pitcher must model the baserunner's model of the disengagement budget. The source's intuition is explicit: if a pitcher always pitches, the baserunner “can lead off as far as they want and steal as frequently as they want”; if a baserunner never steals, “a pitcher can simply ignore a baserunner” [PAGE 11]. Equilibrium requires mixing to avoid being exploited.

Crisis habit regression is the failure mode where a player under pressure collapses to a simple heuristic instead of the mixed equilibrium. A runner may default to the two-foot rule or a fixed “green/red” steal card [PAGE 14–15]; a pitcher may default to pickoff after a stolen-base threat. That habit is exploitable because the opponent can condition on it. The paper's own warning asterisks on the strategy card—marking game states where the pitcher's equilibrium strategy calls for a pickoff [PAGE 14]—show that the equilibrium is not a fixed “always steal” rule.

Counter-trap is the recursive layer: the offense anticipates the pitcher's anticipation. The clearest example is Snell and the hit-and-run. In equilibrium, Snell mixes between two fastballs in the strike zone, which are good hit-and-run pitches; the offense's highest hit-and-run rate is 70%, not 100% [PAGE 18]. The source explains why: “if Snell knows the offense is hitting and running, he should throw a pitchout” [PAGE 19]. Thus, the offense cannot push the habit to 100% without creating the counter-incentive. This is a second-order belief trap: the offense wants the pitcher to believe the hit-and-run probability is low enough that he throws a hittable fastball, but high enough to gain value.

The clock is the disengagement count. The third failed pickoff is a balk [PAGE 1], so the pitcher has a finite pickoff budget; after each disengagement, the baserunner's risk of another pickoff falls, which is why the equilibrium lead increases [PAGE 13]. The 36-subtree, eight-stage recursion [PAGE 12] is a formal clock: balls, strikes, and disengagements advance the state, and the value of the current node depends on future subgames. Crisis habit regression ignores the clock; counter-trap exploits the habit; the clock vetoes infinite pickoff repetition. A good behavioral audit therefore asks whether a player is using the equilibrium mix, a stale habit, or a deliberate counter-trap, and whether the disengagement clock has advanced enough to change the posterior. The model does not include emotional arousal, fatigue, or opponent scouting memory, so those remain gray zones around the recursive layer.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof is an omitted-variable argument. The model's probabilities are functions of listed covariates only: pickoff \(p(h)\) depends on lead distance and a pitcher random effect [PAGE 5]; generic stolen-base probability depends on lead distance, sprint speed, jump speed, catcher pop time, and pre/post-2023 flag [PAGE 7]; pitch-specific stolen-base probability adds velocity, five-zone location, and handedness interaction [PAGE 8]; hit-and-run classifiers use pitch tracking, attack direction, and swing length [PAGE 10–11]. No weather variable, surface variable, biomechanical fatigue state, or injury state appears in those lists. Therefore the model assumes a conditional-independence statement of the form \(Y\perp W\mid X\), where \(W\) is weather or physical state. If the data show \(Y\not\perp W\mid X\), every downstream \(V^*\) and equilibrium strategy is biased. This is a logical veto, not a numeric threshold.

Physical veto: lead distance \(l\) enters the pickoff logit linearly, \(p(h)=\sigma(-13.091+0.777l+\gamma)\) [PAGE 5]. The sigmoid has no finite singularity; as \(l\to\infty\), \(p\to1\), and as \(l\to-\infty\), \(p\to0\). The breakdown is not a pole but a support failure: the linear predictor is only calibrated over observed MLB lead distances, and the source admits lead-distance data are “not openly shared by MLB” [PAGE 5]. A physical veto is triggered when \(l\) is outside the calibration support or when the runner's lead is constrained by a fielder's position, surface traction, or game situation not represented in the model.

Biomechanical veto: jump speed is engineered from a “power law curve” fitted to “90 foot running splits” in 5-foot increments from 0 to 90 feet [PAGE 7]. That operator assumes a smooth, athlete-specific acceleration curve. If a runner is injured, fatigued, or has an atypical acceleration profile, the power-law estimate of jump time and jump speed is invalid. The veto proof is residual-based: if the runner's observed split times deviate from the fitted power law beyond training residuals, the stolen-base probability input is not identified. The model also has no explicit fatigue clock across a season, so multi-week fatigue is an unmodeled biomechanical state.

Weather veto: the paper includes no rain, wind, temperature, humidity, or field-condition term. Weather can affect pitcher grip and command \(p(g,t)\), catcher pop time, runner acceleration, and batted-ball outcomes \(p_\chi(c)\). Because those terms are absent, the model's equilibrium cards are valid only under the implicit assumption that weather effects are zero or absorbed by the fitted probabilities. A weather veto is triggered when residual dependence on weather is detected in the same game states; the correct adjudication is to refit or to withhold the strategy card, not to extrapolate the card through the weather layer.

The cross-layer adjudication rule is therefore: first verify that the player and game state lie inside the paper's covariate support; second verify that weather, surface, and biomechanical residual terms are not statistically active; third, if any layer is active, veto the equilibrium recommendation and replace it with a layer-specific constraint. The paper itself gives strong evidence for this caution: it removes foul balls [PAGE 2], uses only two pitchers for pitch-specific command [PAGE 16], and relies on a selected hit-and-run swing population [PAGE 10]. Those are not cosmetic limitations; they are the exact seams where cross-layer vetoes enter.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
