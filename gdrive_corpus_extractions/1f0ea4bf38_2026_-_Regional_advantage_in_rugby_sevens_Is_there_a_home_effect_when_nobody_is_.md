<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central multi-hop derivation is not the naive claim that proximity predicts performance; it is the reversal of that claim under a symmetric panel. First hop: the raw match listing is asymmetric, because “Team 1 wins 67.3% of the time: the listing is not symmetric and is correlated with team strength” [PAGE 5]. A regression on Team 1 covariates therefore confounds proximity with listing order. Second hop: the authors convert each match into two focal-team rows so that “By construction the panel is symmetric: the mean margin is exactly 0 and the win rate is 0.495” [PAGE 6]. Third hop: within that panel, team and opponent fixed effects absorb quality, so the coefficient “identifies the Regional Differential off within-team variation in proximity across the venues a team visits, holding fixed both its own and its opponents’ quality” [PAGE 6]. Fourth hop: the aggregate coefficient collapses. The paper states “First, the aggregate Regional Differential is essentially zero” [PAGE 5], and “once team and opponent quality are absorbed through fixed effects in a symmetric team-match panel, no general regional advantage survives” [PAGE 1]. Fifth hop: the null aggregate is not the whole result; “this aggregate null conceals economically meaningful heterogeneity: a clearly identified group of teams (led by France) performs worse the farther a tournament lies from home” [PAGE 5]. Sixth hop: the mechanism is team-specific—France is pure displacement, Canada is east-west jet lag, and cultural proximity is a robust null [PAGE 11–12].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the Regional Differential as “the influence of the host region on team performance, net of team strength” [PAGE 3]. Its measurement operator is the symmetric team-match panel. For focal team \(i\) against opponent \(j\) in match \(m\) hosted in country \(h\), the outcomes are
\[
y^{\text{margin}}_{im}=S_i-S_j,\qquad
y^{\text{win}}_{im}=\mathbf{1}[S_i>S_j],\qquad
y^{\text{win/tie}}_{im}=\mathbf{1}[S_i\ge S_j],
\]
with \(S_i,S_j\) in points and the win indicators in probability units \(\{0,1\}\). The panel construction is justified by the listing asymmetry: “Team 1 wins 67.3% of the time: the listing is not symmetric and is correlated with team strength” [PAGE 5]. The symmetric panel has “mean margin ... exactly 0 and the win rate ... 0.495” [PAGE 6].

The proximity vector is
\[
\mathbf{p}_{im}=\big(d_{ih},\,j_{ih},\,H^{EW}_{ih},\,H^{NS}_{ih},\,L_{ih},\,R_{ih},\,\Delta F^{eth}_{ih},\,\Delta F^{ling}_{ih},\,\Delta F^{rel}_{ih}\big),
\]
where \(d_{ih}\) is distance in 1,000 km, \(j_{ih}=|tz_i-tz_h|\) is absolute UTC-offset difference in hours, \(H^{EW},H^{NS},L,R\) are binary indicators for shared east-west hemisphere, shared north-south hemisphere, shared language, and shared religion, and
\[
\Delta F^{q}_{ih}=|F^{q}_{i}-F^{q}_{h}|,\qquad q\in\{\text{ethnic},\text{linguistic},\text{religious}\},
\]
with \(F^q\in[0,1]\) the Alesina et al. (2003) fractionalization probability that two randomly drawn individuals in a country differ along dimension \(q\) [PAGE 5, 12, 16]. The workhorse specification is
\[
y_{im}=\beta\,\mathbf{p}_{im}+\alpha_i+\gamma_j+\delta_t+\rho_s+\varepsilon_{im},
\]
where \(\alpha_i\) is a focal-team fixed effect, \(\gamma_j\) an opponent fixed effect, \(\delta_t\) a year fixed effect, and \(\rho_s\) a round fixed effect; standard errors are clustered by tournament [PAGE 6]. The team-specific gradient specification is
\[
y_{im}=\beta_k\,(d_{im}\mathbf{1}[i=k])+\lambda d_{im}+\theta_k\mathbf{1}[i=k]+\gamma_j+\delta_t+\rho_s+\varepsilon_{im},
\]
so that \(\beta_k\) is team \(k\)’s own distance gradient [PAGE 7]. Units are points per 1,000 km for margin, probability per 1,000 km for win outcomes, and points per hour for jet-lag terms.

Assumptions: quality is absorbed by \(\alpha_i\) and \(\gamma_j\); calendar and stage effects by \(\delta_t\) and \(\rho_s\); proximity is identified from within-team variation across venues; and the panel is symmetric by construction [PAGE 6]. Robustness checks include clustering by team and HC1 errors: “the pooled distance coefficient with team and opponent fixed effects is −0.05 whether errors are clustered by tournament (p = 0.50) or by team (p = 0.40)” [PAGE 13]. The source also reports stability under dropping year fixed effects in Table 4 [PAGE 14].

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. If \(d_{im}\) or another proximity regressor is invariant within team \(i\), it is collinear with \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta\) is not separately identified; the paper’s design relies on within-team venue variation. If a host-level variable is common to both focal and opponent rows, it “differences out” in the symmetric panel: “host-level diversity cannot affect the score margin by construction in a symmetric panel (it is common to both teams and differences out)” [PAGE 12]. If cultural fractionalization dimensions are strongly collinear, the near-zero effect is split across correlated regressors: “this pattern is the signature of multicollinearity splitting an unstable, essentially zero effect across correlated regressors, not of a genuine cultural channel” [PAGE 12]. If distance varies only across teams within a host, the per-host gradient is descriptive and “blend[s] proximity with any host-specific selection of which teams attend” [PAGE 18]. Finally, flight-time and connection data are incomplete and “were therefore not used in the main specifications” [PAGE 15], so the estimates are not structural travel-fatigue parameters.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from classic Home Advantage work—Jones (2007), Thomas et al. (2008), Nevill et al. (1997), Jamieson (2010)—through crowd-absence tests during COVID-19 (Bryson et al., 2021; Bilalić et al., 2021; McCarrick et al., 2021; Leitner et al., 2023; Delbianco et al., 2023; Leota et al., 2022b; Szabó, 2022) to the neutral-venue problem in international rugby sevens [PAGE 1–2]. The paper’s retrocausal move is to reinterpret the folk wisdom about regional strongholds after the fixed-effects result: the raw pattern is real in descriptive win rates, but it is “driven by composition (strong teams of a given region meet weaker rivals at certain venues) and evaporate[s] under team and opponent fixed effects” [PAGE 14]. Thus later evidence reframes earlier “regional advantage” claims as schedule artifacts. The paper then reinterprets the folk claim again: “The folk wisdom is thus half right: regional strongholds are real for some teams, but the teams most often credited with them (the southern-hemisphere greats) are precisely those for whom location does not matter” [PAGE 10]. This is a double retrocausal correction: first from universal regional advantage to composition, then from composition-only null to team-specific gradients.

The scientific lineage also includes circadian and jet-lag research: Recht et al. (1995) on baseball, Nutting and Price (2017) on the NBA, Leota et al. (2022a) on eastward jet lag, and Smithies et al. (2021) on rugby sleep and recovery; the paper notes “long-haul transmeridian travel measurably degrades the sleep and recovery of professional rugby squads” [PAGE 11]. The cultural lineage runs through Alesina et al. (2003) fractionalization and Spolaore and Wacziarg (2013, 2016) cultural distance; the paper imports those measures and finds a “robust null” [PAGE 12]. 

The multi-week/longitudinal traps are important. Events are “typically staged over a single weekend, with teams arriving several days earlier so as to shed jet lag and acclimatize to local conditions” [PAGE 3], and “Consecutive rounds are, moreover, often geographically clustered, so that all teams face broadly comparable itineraries” [PAGE 3]. A season-level observer can therefore mistake a team’s itinerary clustering for a venue effect. A second trap is residual selection: “rotation policies that rest stars on long trips” cannot be fully excluded [PAGE 15]. A third trap is per-host selection: within a host, distance varies only across teams, so host gradients are descriptive [PAGE 18]. A fourth trap is gender pooling: the qualitative pattern is preserved, but for the United States and Australia the gradient is concentrated on the women’s side [PAGE 13]. These are multi-week traps because they arise from scheduling, rotation, and selection over a season, not from a single match.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression is the observer’s default inference: seeing France lose more often at distant venues, or seeing southern-hemisphere teams dominate in Oceania, the observer attributes the result to venue or region. The paper identifies this habit precisely: “The cross-sectional correlations that underpin descriptive accounts of regional strongholds are driven by composition ... and evaporate under team and opponent fixed effects” [PAGE 14]. The recursive theory-of-mind problem is that the observer does not know which opponent quality and which schedule generated the observed win rate; the observer sees the outcome but not the counterfactual itinerary.

The counter-trap is not to swing to the opposite false belief that “nothing matters.” The paper’s counter-trap is the symmetric team-match panel with team and opponent fixed effects plus team-specific interactions. It represents each team’s own perspective, absorbs both sides’ quality, and tests heterogeneity. The paper states “the heterogeneity is the substantive finding” [PAGE 14], and it warns that pooled estimation obscures team-specific gradients. A second counter-trap is cultural: the observer may infer that shared language or religion helps, but the continuous fractionalization measures produce “a non-result” [PAGE 12], and the binary indicators “vanish under team and opponent fixed effects” [PAGE 12]. A third counter-trap is mechanism: “The Regional Differential, where present, therefore has team-specific physiology” [PAGE 11].

The clock is both calendar and circadian. Calendar controls are year and round fixed effects [PAGE 6]; circadian controls are UTC-offset differences and east-west travel. The paper separates France’s pure displacement from Canada’s jet lag: “For France the effect is pure distance: the displacement gradient remains −1.05 (p &lt; 0.01) while the jet-lag term is insignificant” [PAGE 11], while “Canada is the opposite case: conditioning on distance, its margin falls by 1.5 points per hour of time difference (p &lt; 0.01)” [PAGE 11]. The recursive observer must also model anticipation: teams arrive days early to shed jet lag [PAGE 3], and coaches may rotate players on long trips [PAGE 15]. Thus the observed gradient is a joint product of physiology, scheduling, and selection. The correct mental model is a team-specific, clock-sensitive, opponent-adjusted belief update—not a universal regional prior.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
