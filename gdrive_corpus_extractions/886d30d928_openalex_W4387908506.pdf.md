<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The primary operator is a repeated-measures analysis of a continuous outcome \(y_{ij}\) for subject \(i=1,\dots,n_g\) in position stratum \(g\) at season wave \(j=1,2,3\). A formal one-way repeated-measures model is
\[
y_{gij}=\mu_g+\tau_{gj}+s_{gi}+e_{gij},
\]
with \(s_{gi}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{s,g}^2)\), \(e_{gij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{e,g}^2)\), and fixed wave effects \(\tau_{gj}\). The reported \(F\) statistic is
\[
F_g=\frac{MS_{\tau,g}}{MS_{e,g}}
=\frac{SS_{\tau,g}/(J-1)}{SS_{e,g}/\{(n_g-1)(J-1)\}},
\]
where \(J=3\). For defenders and midfielders, \(n_g=6\), so the error degrees of freedom are \((6-1)(3-1)=10\). For strikers, \(n_g=4\), so the error degrees of freedom are \((4-1)(3-1)=6\). The paper does not print these df values but the sample sizes in Table 1 imply them [PAGE 3]. The source’s statistical statement is explicit: “In the analysis of the data, the Repeated ANOVA test was used. Bonferroni post hoc analysis was applied… The significance level was accepted as p < 0.05” [PAGE 4].

To test the paper’s strongest verbal claim—that the annual plan “had different positive contributions to the players' physical performance according to their playing positions” [PAGE 1]—the required model is a position-by-time mixed model:
\[
y_{ij}=\mu+\alpha_g+\tau_j+(\alpha\tau)_{gj}+s_{i(g)}+e_{ij},
\]
with the null \(H_0:(\alpha\tau)_{gj}=0\) for all \(g,j\). The paper does not report this interaction. Instead, it reports separate ANOVAs by position and for the team. That is an operator mismatch: stratum-specific significance is not a differential-effect test. A formal interaction test is the correct gate for “different contributions.”

The post hoc operator is Bonferroni adjustment. For \(m\) pairwise comparisons,
\[
p_{\mathrm{adj},k}=\min(1,\,m\,p_k),\qquad
\text{reject if }p_{\mathrm{adj},k}<\alpha.
\]
The paper says Bonferroni was applied [PAGE 4], but it does not state \(m\). For the three within-wave pairwise comparisons, \(m=3\) is natural. If one accounts for 13 outcomes across four reported strata, \(m=52\), and
\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{FWER}}=\frac{0.05}{52}=9.615\times 10^{-4}.
\]
Under that stricter gate, several reported “\(p=.000\)” team results would survive, but boundary results such as “\(p=.001\)” and “\(p=.050*\)” would not. The midfielders’ “Arm Strength (kg) … 5,842 ,050*” and “20 m sprint (sec) … 4,060 ,050*” [PAGE 5] are especially fragile.

Effect size is absent. For a repeated-measures design, a standard dimensionless operator is
\[
\eta_p^2=\frac{SS_{\tau}}{SS_{\tau}+SS_e}
=\frac{F_g(J-1)}{F_g(J-1)+(n_g-1)(J-1)}.
\]
For example, defenders’ back strength \(F=18.161\) with \(J=3\), \(n_g=6\) gives \(\eta_p^2\approx 36.322/(36.322+10)=0.784\) if the reported \(F\) is exact [PAGE 4]. That is large, but the confidence interval is not reported and \(n=6\) makes it imprecise. No \(\eta_p^2\), \(\omega^2\), or standardized mean change is given.

Sprint is a distance-time operator:
\[
v=\frac{d}{t},\qquad [v]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\quad [d]=\mathrm{m},\quad [t]=\mathrm{s}.
\]
The tables report 10 m, 20 m, and 30 m times in seconds [PAGE 5–7]. Strength is reported in kg, not N. If the dynamometer reading is interpreted as kgf, then
\[
F_{\mathrm{N}}=m_{\mathrm{kg}}\,g_0,\qquad g_0=9.80665\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},
\]
so a 40 kg reading corresponds to \(392.3\ \mathrm{N}\). Heart rate is \(\mathrm{beats\,min^{-1}}\); reaction is reported as “msec,” but the source’s own statement “According to the device, 1 second is 100 milliseconds” [PAGE 3] creates a unit-scale ambiguity; flexibility is cm; age is years; height is cm; body mass is kg [PAGE 3].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes: (1) \(n_g=4\) for strikers gives only 6 error df, so power is low and estimates are unstable; (2) sphericity is not tested. If \(\varepsilon<1\), the uncorrected \(F\) is anti-conservative; a Greenhouse–Geisser correction uses \(df_1=\varepsilon(J-1)\), \(df_2=\varepsilon(n_g-1)(J-1)\); (3) multiple outcomes without a stated family inflate false positives; (4) \(p=.050*\) is a threshold-boundary singularity because rounding can move the result across \(\alpha=0.05\) [PAGE 5]; (5) zero within-subject variance makes \(F\) undefined; (6) missing or unbalanced waves break the stated repeated-measures operator; (7) uncalibrated reaction units and kg-based strength prevent cross-study SI comparison. The source’s normality check—“The Shapiro-Wilk test was used… and kurtosis and bulge values were examined” [PAGE 4]—does not cover sphericity, reliability, or unit calibration.

The historical lineage of the sport is explicit: “Amputee football was first invented by Don Bennett in 1982” [PAGE 2]. The paper itself reasons that “in athletes' back strength measurements, healthy football players use the dynamometer with both legs, while amputee football players use one leg” [PAGE 7]. That is a biomechanical genealogy, not merely a statistical one.

The scientific lineage is periodization and field-based performance testing. The annual plan is attributed to “Bompa & Buzzichelli, (2019)” [PAGE 3] and is organized as “Pre-Season (16 Week),” “First Part Season (10 Week),” “Season Break (8 Week),” “Last Part Season (10 Week),” and “Transition (8 Week)” [PAGE 3]. The pre-season block contains “Anatomical Adaptation, Max. Strength, Specific Endurance, Sprint, Anaerobic and Aerobic Power”; the in-season blocks contain “Maintenance of strength, endurance, sprint, and power” and “Technical and Tactical Training” [PAGE 3]. The measurement genealogy is also conventional: hand-grip and back/leg/arm dynamometry, a “Hubbard Scientific Reaction Timer,” wireless photocells for 10–30 m sprint, and a sit-and-reach bench [PAGE 3–4]. This is a field-test lineage, not a laboratory mechanistic lineage.

The multi-week traps are therefore substantial. First, the 8-week “Season Break” [PAGE 3] can produce detraining or altered loading between the mid-season and end-season tests. Second, the 16-week pre-season may produce most strength adaptation before the first test, so the first wave is already post-training; the design cannot isolate the pre-season effect from the in-season maintenance effect. Third, the tests occur at round 1, after round 9, and after round 18 [PAGE 3], so match congestion, travel, injury, and recovery are uncontrolled temporal covariates. Fourth, the same test order was used “in the same order in each trial after 10-15 min of individual athlete warm-up time before training” [PAGE 3], and athletes had “two attempts for each trial, and their best scores were recorded” [PAGE 3]. That introduces practice, pacing, and motivation effects. Fifth, the paper’s causal arrow is historically tempting but not identified: it attributes change to the “annual training plan” [PAGE 1], yet the design lacks a no-training or alternative-training comparator. The retrocausal genealogy thus reads the positive findings as “seasonal co-variation under a periodized program,” not as proof that the program caused position-specific adaptation.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three actors: the athlete, the coach, and the reader. The athlete’s crisis habit is regression under fatigue or competitive pressure: with crutches, grip and back strength are the immediate stability constraints, so a tired athlete may revert to habitual crutch reliance even if training improved isolated dynamometer values. The coach’s crisis habit is over-reading a significant \(p\)-value as a mandate for position-specific training. The reader’s crisis habit is treating “different positive contributions” [PAGE 1] as an interaction finding when no interaction was reported. The counter-trap is to overcorrect: because the sample is small and single-team, one might dismiss all observed changes, even though the within-team changes are large and directionally consistent for strength and sprint. The source itself warns that “the participants were exclusively drawn from one team, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results” [PAGE 8], but it also assumes that “the athletes perform both training and physical performance tests with maximum effort” [PAGE 8]. That assumption is a mental-state inference, not a measured variable.

The clock layer is decisive. The tests are timed at the start, after round 9, and after round 18 [PAGE 3]. The coach’s belief about “mid-season” may not match the athlete’s physiological state after nine rounds. The athlete’s belief about test importance may change across the season; early tests may be treated as baseline, late tests as selection or contract relevant. The paper notes that “the anatomy and flexibility of the body show changes at different times of the day” [PAGE 8], citing Reilly et al.; testing occurred “during the afternoon practice time” [PAGE 3], so circadian variation is a clock confound for flexibility and possibly reaction. The 8-week break [PAGE 3] is another clock: a coach may expect retention, while the athlete may de-train or change crutch use. The recursive failure mode is a false consensus: coach and athlete both infer that “same training” means “same response,” but positional demands differ and the paper itself says “All players did the same training (except the goalkeeper)” [PAGE 3]. The correct counter-trap is to model position as a moderator, not to assume it. The clock-corrected inference is: strength and sprint changes are plausible within the observed season, but the timing of testing, the 8-week break, and the unverified maximal-effort assumption are alternative explanations that must be stated alongside any performance recommendation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is partial but real. The sport’s physical rules make grip and back strength mechanically necessary: “These athletes use crutches during the match” [PAGE 2], and “The goalkeepers are people with arm amputations… they are not allowed to use it” [PAGE 2]. The study excludes goalkeepers because “the goalkeepers had arm amputations” [PAGE 3] and because “their amputations differ from the other players” [PAGE 8]. Therefore the physical layer supports the relevance of hand-grip and back-strength testing for field players, but it also vetoes generalization to goalkeepers. The biomechanical layer vetoes naive cross-study comparison: “healthy football players use the dynamometer with both legs, while amputee football players use one leg” [PAGE 7]. The sit-and-reach protocol is also nonstandard: “Athletes place the soles of their feet on the bench in a seated position, and the knees are bent forward with tension” [PAGE 4]. That is not the same as a straight-knee sit-and-reach, so flexibility values cannot be equated with standard norms. Prostheses and orthoses were removed “during the measurements” [PAGE 3], which further changes the biomechanical condition relative to match play.

The weather layer is a missing covariate, not a proven effect. Sprint tests were performed on “the artificial field where the athletes compete” [PAGE 4], and testing occurred “during the afternoon practice time” [PAGE 3]. The paper reports no temperature, humidity, wind, rain, or surface-moisture data. For sprint,
\[
t_{ij}=f(\text{training}_j,\text{weather}_j,\text{match load}_j,\text{skill}_i)+\epsilon_{ij}.
\]
If weather varies across the three waves and is omitted, the estimated seasonal change is not identified as a training effect. The same omitted-variable logic applies to match load and injury. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: accept the physical relevance of grip/back/leg strength; accept that sprint and flexibility are performance-relevant; veto causal attribution when weather, surface, match load, and biomechanical condition are uncontrolled; veto cross-group and cross-study equivalence when amputation level, residual-limb length, crutch setup, and dynamometer protocol differ. The paper’s own limitations support this: “Firstly, the participants were exclusively drawn from one team, potentially limiting the generalizability of the results” [PAGE 8]; “Moreover, it limits the number of people participating in the study” [PAGE 8]; and “Additionally, we excluded goalkeepers because their amputations differ from the other players” [PAGE 8]. These are cross-layer vetoes against overgeneralization, not against the existence of within-team seasonal change.
