<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicit: it positions itself against action-valuation methods such as expected threat, QPass, VAEP, expected possession value, and deep action-value models, citing Liu and Schulte on ice hockey, Liu et al. on soccer action values, Singh’s expected threat, Gyarmati and Stanojevic’s QPass, Decroos et al.’s VAEP, Fernandez et al.’s expected possession value, and Van Roy et al.’s decision-evaluation work [PAGE 22, PAGE 23]. Its claimed novelty is to move from valuing observed actions to deriving an optimal policy over the full pitch surface: “our work is the first attempt to use reinforcement learning to maximize the expected possession outcome for the full pitch surface in soccer” [PAGE 22]. The retrocausal genealogy is therefore not a straight line from “better pass metric” to “optimal pass metric.” It is a re-use of three older scientific lineages: expected-goals shot valuation from Lucey et al. [PAGE 23], off-policy evaluation from importance sampling and doubly robust methods [PAGE 11, PAGE 23], and policy-gradient reinforcement learning from Levine and Koltun, TRPO, and PPO [PAGE 23]. The paper imports those lineages into soccer by treating a possession as an episode and a ball destination as an action [PAGE 7]. That import creates a retrocausal trap: the “optimal” policy is estimated from historical behavior, then used to reinterpret the same historical possessions as if the players could have chosen otherwise. The paper says it can “directly evaluate the performance of following the optimal policy rather than performing counterfactual reasoning and analyzing what-if scenarios” [PAGE 15], but the evaluation methods are themselves off-policy counterfactual estimators [PAGE 11]. The claim is therefore best read as “model-based reweighting of historical support,” not as a guaranteed causal intervention. A second multi-week trap is team-specific fine-tuning. The league-wide model is trained on 2020-21, then “fine-tune[d]” for each of 18 teams “using the games of the 2021-22 season until the moment of writing this paper (October 2021)” [PAGE 15]. That is a short, early-season sample. The paper itself reports that top teams such as Union SG gain only 0.5 expected goals per game, while bottom teams such as Beerschot gain 3.2 [PAGE 16], and explains this by saying top teams are “quite often selecting the optimal actions” whereas bottom teams are farther from optimal [PAGE 16]. A multi-week adoption program would have to distinguish genuine tactical distance from early-season variance, opponent-quality drift, and regression to the mean. A third trap is reward hindsight. The phase rewards use score difference at the moment of the action [PAGE 9], but the eleven input channels do not include score or clock [PAGE 4]. A model trained across score states can therefore learn an average policy that is optimal for no particular score state. A fourth trap is the “discount trap” itself: the paper tunes \(\gamma=0.99\) with a method designed for deterministic MDPs [PAGE 10, PAGE 23], but soccer possessions are not deterministic and the horizon \(H\) varies by possession. A fifth trap is the headline “+2.5 goals” claim [PAGE 12, PAGE 17]. It assumes 100 possessions per team per game and perfect execution of the optimal policy; the paper concedes the latter is “not much realistic in the real-world” [PAGE 17]. Historically, this resembles the transition from expected-threat maps to full policy optimization: the map is a descriptive surface, the policy is a counterfactual surface, and the deployment is a causal intervention. Each step requires stronger assumptions than the previous one. The genealogical audit therefore should not ask only whether the network beats logistic regression or XGBoost on log-loss; Table 1 shows the proposed network achieving 0.18 selection log-loss and 0.22 success log-loss versus 0.65 and 0.58 for XGBoost [PAGE 6]. It should ask whether the off-policy support, reward identifiability, and state completeness are strong enough to justify the policy claim. Those are different validity layers, and the paper’s own “practical application” framing [PAGE 5] leaves them under-specified.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper is team-level, not player-belief-level. It models “the probability that the ball carrier selects the destination location of the ball on the field, given the current positions of all 22 players and the ball” [PAGE 7]. It does not contain an explicit opponent model, a belief over the opponent’s belief, or a game-theoretic equilibrium. The recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore has to distinguish three failure modes. First, crisis habit regression: under pressure, a team’s actual policy may collapse toward low-entropy habits, such as clearing long, forcing a pass to a star, or avoiding central zones. The paper’s behavioral policy is estimated from historical actions and is not conditioned on a pressure or crisis variable beyond the input channels and phase rewards. The transition reward uses K-Means pressure clusters [PAGE 8], but that is a spatial pressure proxy, not a psychological or game-state stress variable. The paper’s Table 2 is consistent with a crisis-regression story: bottom-table teams show much larger EPO differences, with Beerschot at 3.2 and Cercle Brugge at 2.8 expected goals per game, while Union SG shows 0.5 [PAGE 16]. But the paper’s own explanation is tactical distance, not crisis: “the optimal policy yields a smaller improvement in EPO and goal difference for the teams at the top of the table, and a larger improvement for the teams at the bottom of the table” [PAGE 16]. Crisis habit regression would predict that the gap widens specifically in trailing or late-game states, which the reported aggregate table cannot verify. Second, counter-trap: the optimal destination may be optimal only against the average historical defense. The success surface is a marginal probability \(P_{\text{success}}(g\mid s_t)\), not a minimax value against an opponent who anticipates the recommendation. If a coach deploys the suggested ball destination repeatedly, opponents can close the lane, shift the pressure cluster, or bait the pass. The paper does not model this recursive response; it treats the environment as historical data and says “the transition probabilities are not considered” [PAGE 10]. A counter-trap audit would ask whether the recommended action remains high-value after the opponent’s best response. The paper’s own distributional check is only a comparison of behavioral and optimal EPO under importance sampling, doubly robust estimation, and simulation [PAGE 11, PAGE 12]. Those estimators reweight old actions; they do not simulate an opponent modeling the new policy. Third, clock: the paper has a non-stationary phase structure and a score-dependent reward. It says “Due to the different objectives of the distinct phases of a soccer game (i.e., non-stationary policy), our reward engineering approach applies various reward functions in different phases” [PAGE 8]. In build-up, \(r_2\) changes when \(S_{\text{attack}}\le S_{\text{defend}}\) versus \(S_{\text{attack}}>S_{\text{defend}}\) [PAGE 9]. That is a clock/score-sensitive objective. But the eleven input channels do not include score, period, or match clock [PAGE 4]. The policy network therefore receives no explicit clock variable, while the reward function does. This is a recursive theory-of-mind problem because the “correct” action depends on what the team is trying to do at that moment: chase a goal, protect a lead, or manage fatigue. If the state does not encode that intention, the policy cannot represent it. The paper’s future-work line is revealing: it wants “to expand the current framework to capture the contributions of individual players such that they could adapt themselves to their team-specific optimal strategy” [PAGE 22]. That would require a second recursion: player models coach model opponent model. The current source stops at team behavior and team-specific optimal behavior. So the correct adjudication is: crisis habit regression is plausible but unproven; counter-trap is unmodeled and likely to shrink deployment gains; clock/score context is named in the reward but absent from the state, which is a concrete representational veto.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather veto evidence is mostly negative evidence from the input-channel audit. The source lists eleven channels: attacker locations, defender locations, attacker \(x\) and \(y\) velocities, defender \(x\) and \(y\) velocities, ball-to-zone distance, goal-to-zone distance, ball-goal angle, cosine, and sine [PAGE 4]. It does not list acceleration, body orientation, footedness, limb length, reaction time, fatigue, injury state, pitch friction, wind, rain, temperature, or visibility. The tracking data are “(x,y) coordinates of all 22 players and the ball on the pitch at 25 observations per second” [PAGE 3], with velocities as derived channels. That is enough to estimate a statistical success surface, but not enough to certify that a recommended pass is physically executable. A cross-layer veto must therefore be applied after the policy surface. For a candidate destination \(g=(x,y)\), let \(d(g)\) be the pass distance in meters and \(T_{\text{avail}}(s)\) the available time in seconds before an opponent can intercept. The required average ball speed is
\[
v_{\text{req}}(g,s)=\frac{d(g)}{T_{\text{avail}}(s)}\quad [\text{m/s}].
\]
A physical veto fires if \(v_{\text{req}}\) exceeds the player’s technique-specific maximum pass speed, or if the straight or curved ball path intersects an opponent’s reachable set within the reaction-and-travel time. The source provides neither a maximum-speed model nor an explicit opponent reachable-set model; it provides velocities and K-Means pressure clusters [PAGE 8]. Therefore any claim that the EPO-optimal zone is physically feasible is unidentifiable from the provided operators alone. A biomechanical veto fires when the pass requires a body orientation, weak-foot strike, first-touch control, or acceleration profile that is not in the state. The paper’s velocity channels are aggregate player velocities, not joint-level or kicking-mechanics variables [PAGE 4]. A weather veto fires when wind, rain, or surface condition changes the ball’s flight or roll, especially for the “long ball” action defined as “ball move over than 32 meter in length” [PAGE 20]. The success surface is a sigmoid trained on historical outcomes [PAGE 5]; if the match weather lies outside the historical weather distribution, the success probability is an extrapolation, not a verified probability. The paper’s own caveat supports this: the “+2.5 expected goal difference” league-wide result “is not much realistic in the real-world as it assumes that all players can perfectly follow the optimal policy” [PAGE 17]. Perfect following is exactly the physical and biomechanical assumption that the input channels do not justify. A rigorous deployment proof would need a three-layer veto: kinematic feasibility, biomechanical feasibility, and environmental feasibility. The source supplies none of the three as explicit constraints; it supplies a learned success surface that can be smooth and non-zero even in zones that are physically unreachable. The correct adjudication is therefore not “the model is wrong,” but “the model’s optimal surface is an unconstrained statistical surface until a physical veto layer is added.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I interpret RLVR here as a verifiable-reward/counterfactual audit: each proposed optimal action should be rejected if a traceable check cannot support the counterfactual claim. Three veto scenarios follow.

1. Support-overlap veto. Verifiable check: for a state \(s_t\) and destination \(g\), estimate the behavioral propensity \(\hat\pi_\theta(g\mid s_t)\) from held-out data and count the number of comparable historical actions \(n(s_t,g)\). If \(\hat\pi_\theta(g\mid s_t)=0\), or if \(n(s_t,g)\) is below a pre-registered minimum, the importance weight
\[
w_t=\frac{\pi_{\theta'}(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}
\]
is undefined or has explosive variance. The paper states that both importance sampling and doubly robust evaluation “take samples from the behavioral policy \(\pi_\theta\)” [PAGE 11], and that the agent learns “solely from historical data, without online interaction” [PAGE 10]. Prediction: when the optimal policy selects a zone that the behavioral policy almost never selected, the reported EPO improvement will be unstable, with wide or unbounded importance-sampling variance; the correct response is to veto the EPO claim unless a positivity/support check is reported. This is verifiable by recomputing the propensity and the effective sample size for the recommended zone.

2. Kinematic and weather feasibility veto. Verifiable check: for a recommended destination \(g\), compute \(v_{\text{req}}(g,s)=d(g)/T_{\text{avail}}(s)\) in meters per second. If \(v_{\text{req}}\) exceeds the tracked maximum pass speed for that player and technique, or if the ball path intersects an opponent’s reachable set, veto the action. If the action is a long ball over 32 m [PAGE 20] and the match wind or surface condition lies outside the training distribution, veto the success probability as an extrapolation. The source’s input channels include player velocities but not acceleration, body orientation, footedness, fatigue, wind, rain, or pitch friction [PAGE 4]. Prediction: the success surface will overestimate completion probability for long, high-wind, or awkward-body-orientation passes; the EPO gain will shrink or reverse when the physical veto is applied. This is verifiable by joining tracking-derived pass speed and weather data to the recommended zones and comparing predicted versus realized completion.

3. Markov score/clock-context veto. Verifiable check: find two states with identical eleven-channel tensors but different score or clock context, one with \(S_{\text{attack}}\le S_{\text{defend}}\) and one with \(S_{\text{attack}}>S_{\text{defend}}\). The reward \(r_2\) changes across those states: the paper uses \(P_{\text{success}}/d(\text{halfway line})\) in the first case and \(P_{\text{success}}\) in the second [PAGE 9]. But the state-channel list does not include score or clock [PAGE 4]. Prediction: the policy network cannot represent score-dependent optimal behavior; it will learn an average policy that is optimal for neither state, and the EPO difference in build-up will be biased. This is verifiable by stratifying held-out possessions by score state and testing whether the recommended action distribution is invariant when the eleven-channel state is matched but the score label differs. If invariance holds while the reward differs, the Markov-context veto fires. A further prediction is that the largest bias will appear in late-game or trailing/leading states where the score-dependent reward branch is most active.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
