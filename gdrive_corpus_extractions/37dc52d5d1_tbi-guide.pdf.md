\frac{dV_{\rm ic}}{dt}=\dot V_{\rm blood}+\dot V_{\rm edema}-\dot V_{\rm drain},

approaches \(0^+\) as the skull’s reserve is exhausted; at \(C_{\rm ic}=0\) the pressure response is singular in the model, meaning small added volume produces unbounded modeled pressure rise. <CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a constraint that cannot be overridden by motivation, encouragement, or a single good test. Physical veto: the injury triad of bleeding, tearing, and swelling [PAGE 7] means that “pushing through” can be vetoed by pressure effects. The guide says the skull does not expand, blood presses on brain tissue, and critical areas controlling breathing or heart rate can be affected [PAGE 7]. The ICP “relief valve” [PAGE 9] is proof that intracranial pressure is a physical layer, not a motivational one. Imaging veto is asymmetric: a positive CT/MRI may show bleeding, but a negative scan cannot veto injury because only 10–15% have positive findings [PAGE 9] and about 85% of the author’s patients have negative CT/MRI [PAGE 48]. Therefore the correct adjudication is: positive imaging can rule in a lesion; negative imaging cannot rule out the syndrome.

Biomechanical veto: acceleration/deceleration throws the brain forward and backward, producing contra coup injury [PAGE 8]. The neck is also a biomechanical layer: disks can be squished, nerves pressed, and muscles stretched into “knots” [PAGE 26]. The guide calls this “myofacial pain disorder” [PAGE 26] and says the pain can last for years, especially with re-injury or stress [PAGE 27]. Physical therapy may make pain worse before better: “But remember, it gets worse before it gets better.” [PAGE 28]. Thus a biomechanical veto blocks the inference “if you just relax, the neck pain will disappear.” It also blocks “no fracture, no problem,” because closed injury can build pressure [PAGE 9].

Weather/environmental veto: the guide identifies bright light as a trigger: going “from a dark building out into the bright sunlight” can cause instant headaches [PAGE 30], and visual overload is worse with bright lights [PAGE 37]. It recommends dark sunglasses, often the cheapest and darkest [PAGE 30, 37]. Temperature and respiration are also veto layers: a room that is too warm interferes with sleep, while a room that is too cold may cause “turtling,” pulling covers over the face and rebreathing carbon dioxide [PAGE 31, 39–40]. Cold-food headache is another environmental trigger [PAGE 31]. Dietary triggers include red wine and hard cheeses [PAGE 30]. Therefore environmental load is a vector constraint: photic load, thermal load, acoustic load, crowding, and chemical/dietary load can each veto a cognitive plan. The source-supported response is not “ignore it” but “modify it”: breaks, quiet, dark, ear plugs, sunglasses, temperature control, and dietary tracking [PAGE 30–31, 37, 39–40]. The veto proof is that these are external boundary conditions, not personality weaknesses.
