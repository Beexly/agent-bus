t_{\mathrm{on}} = 0\,\mathrm{s},\qquad t_{\mathrm{off}} = 5\,\mathrm{s},\qquad t_{\mathrm{US}} = 7\,\mathrm{s},\qquad \Delta t_{\mathrm{trace}} = 2\,\mathrm{s}.
\]

Receptor transduction operator:
\[
r_i(t) = \phi_i\!\big(s_i(t)\big), \qquad \phi_i : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0},
\]
where r_i is receptor activation (e.g., cochlear receptor potential in mV, slowly adapting mechanoreceptor firing rate in spikes/s).

Summation and response threshold:
\[
E(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{9} w_i\, r_i(t), \qquad w_i \in \mathbb{R},
\]
\[
P(\mathrm{CR}\mid \mathbf{S}) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big[-\beta\,(E(t)-\theta)\big]},
\]
with E in arbitrary excitation units, threshold \theta in the same units, and inverse slope \beta in (excitation units)^{-1}.

Breakdown and singularity regime:
The trial-wise design matrix \mathbf{X} with entries X_{ti} = r_i(t) must satisfy rank(\mathbf{X}) = 9 for unique weight estimation. The estimator
\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
\]
breaks down at the singularity \det(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}) = 0. In the Hull protocol this singularity is generically approached because s_insects(t) is an unobserved stochastic process, s_bands(t) exhibits uninstrumented viscoelastic creep, and s_L-ear(t) / s_R-ear(t) vary with unrecorded head orientation [PAGE 214]. Consequently, attributing salivation to the buzzer alone is an ill-posed inverse problem unless every component is measured or controlled.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage: The text is drawn from Hull’s Principles of Behavior (1943), which systematized Pavlovian conditioning within a drive-habit-incentive framework (E = D × H × K in Hull’s formalism). The immediate predecessor is Pavlov’s salivary conditioning (1927), in which a neutral stimulus is paired with food to elicit salivation. Hull’s innovation in this passage is the ontological expansion of the conditioned stimulus from a single “buzzer” to a compound of co-present sensory components [PAGE 214]. This anticipates mid-century developments: Rescorla–Wagner (1972) with prediction error \delta = \alpha\beta(\lambda - \sum V_i), Kamin’s blocking (1969), and Pearce’s configural theory (1987), all of which treat stimulus elements as competitive or configured units rather than isolated triggers.

Multi-week traps embedded in the protocol:
1. Surgical timeline trap: “Some weeks previous to the experiment one of its salivary ducts has been surgically diverted” [PAGE 214]. The fistula and capsule are chronic somatic contexts that predate the buzzer–meat-powder pairing by weeks. The experimenter implicitly treats the post-surgical state as equivalent to an intact baseline, but the diverted duct, fistula seepage, and capsule contact form a permanent interoceptive stimulus that may itself be conditioned or may modulate drive D independently of the buzzer.
2. Timescale mismatch trap: The text contrasts “some weeks” of surgical recovery and housing with “a few repetitions” of the buzzer–US sequence [PAGE 214]. The conditioned response may therefore reflect weeks of handling, restraint habituation, and experimenter–dog procedural learning more than the few acoustic pairings.
3. Drive-state trap: The 24-hour food deprivation [PAGE 214] establishes a short-term motivational state, but it is layered onto an unknown nutritional and stress history accumulated during the preceding weeks. Hull’s drive variable D is thus confounded: the dog’s hunger is acute, but its general arousal from surgery, restraint, and possible insect irritation is chronic and unmeasured.

Retrocausal reading: The experimental interpretation reaches backward to assume that the pre-conditioning dog is a neutral tabula rasa for the buzzer, while in fact the dog’s sensory manifold was reshaped weeks earlier by surgery and by chronic exposure to the restraint apparatus. The “conditioned reflex” is thus not an emergent property of the 7-s buzzer–US contingency alone; it is the product of a layered causal history in which the buzzer is only the most salient recent element.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
First-order theory of mind (experimenter’s model of the dog): The experimenter assumes the dog forms an association between the buzzer and meat powder, such that the buzzer alone eventually elicits salivation during the 7-s interval [PAGE 214].

Second-order theory of mind (dog’s model of the experimenter): The dog is not a passive receiver of an isolated acoustic CS. It is held by loose bands, fitted with a muzzle, sealed with a capsule, and exposed to an experimenter who manipulates a rubber tube [PAGE 214]. The dog may model the experimenter’s procedural rhythm: buzzer onset, buzzer offset, muzzle adjustment, capsule pressure change, and US delivery. In this recursive account, the capsule contact or muzzle pressure—present continuously and predictably before US delivery—may become a more reliable predictor than the transient buzzer.

Crisis habit regression: Under the combined stressors of 24-hour hunger, surgical fistula, loose restraining bands, and possible insect biting [PAGE 214], the dog’s salivation may regress from a specific anticipatory CR to a generalized non-specific arousal response. Stress-induced salivation and autonomic arousal would violate the assumption that the registered capsule pressure indexes food expectancy alone. The behavioral readout thus conflates conditioned expectancy with crisis-habit regression.

Counter-trap: If the capsule over the fistula is touched or adjusted by the experimenter immediately before meat-powder delivery, its pressure trace may acquire conditioned stimulus status and overshadow the buzzer. The experimenter’s belief that the buzzer is the active CS is the trap; the counter-trap is that the constant tactile context (capsule, muzzle, bands) is the true CS, and the buzzer is merely a redundant or blocked element.

Clock layer: Scalar expectancy theory implies that the dog’s internal clock represents the 5-s buzzer duration, the 2-s trace interval, and the 7-s CS–US interval as a timed expectancy peak at t_US. Because interval timing is subject to Weber’s law (standard deviation proportional to duration), the CR distribution should broaden with longer traces. If the dog’s clock is instead entrained to the experimenter’s manual sequence rather than to the buzzer per se, peak salivation may track capsule-muzzle events rather than acoustic onset. The recursive loop closes when the experimenter interprets any salivation in the 7-s window as evidence of buzzer conditioning, ignoring that the dog’s clock may be referencing a different, co-present stimulus chain.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The restraining bands are described only as “loose bands attached to a wooden framework” [PAGE 214]. Contact pressure P = F/A in pascals (Pa) is never instrumented; band tension may relax by viscoelastic creep over the session. Because skin receptors in the neck and thighs are continuously stimulated [PAGE 214], this pressure stream is a candidate unconditioned or conditioned stimulus that co-varies with the buzzer whenever the experimenter approaches to sound it. Unless band pressure is nulled or measured, the physical layer is not sealed.

Biomechanical layer veto: The dog’s head orientation determines the interaural level difference (ILD) and interaural time difference (ITD) of the buzzer at the two ears [PAGE 214]. Because the head is not clamped within the bands, orientation drifts freely, making s_L-ear(t) and s_R-ear(t) time-varying confounds. The cathode-ray oscillograph shows the buzzer wave is complex [PAGE 214], but complexity of the source does not eliminate the fact that the binaural cues reaching the cochlea are uncontrolled.

Environmental/weather layer veto: The text explicitly admits “the biting of a number of insects which may be hidden in the dog's hair” [PAGE 214]. Insect mechanosensory input is an uncontrolled stochastic process whose rate depends on ambient temperature, humidity, and air movement—weather-layer variables that are not recorded. A burst of insect biting coincident with buzzer onset would produce salivation or arousal falsely attributed to the acoustic CS.

Cross-layer adjudication: A veto is sustained unless all three layers are controlled simultaneously. The Hull protocol seals none of them: bands are loose and uninstrumented, the head is free to orient, and insect presence is possible. Therefore, the inference that the buzzer is the conditioned stimulus fails the cross-layer veto. The veto can only be lifted by (a) instrumenting band pressure in Pa, (b) fixing head position and calibrating ILD/ITD in dB SPL and microseconds, and (c) eliminating or monitoring insect contact with environmental logging. Absent these controls, the measured CR is attributable to the compound S, not to the buzzer in isolation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards) counterfactual veto scenarios. In each case, the verifiable reward signal is the registered capsule pressure transduced into collected saliva volume (mL) or peak salivation rate (mL/s) during the 7-s interval.

Scenario 1 — Restraining-band neutralization (physical-layer ablation).
Intervention: Replace the loose restraining bands with a rigid, pressure-zero head holder, or instrument the bands and regress out measured Pa pressure trial-by-trial.
Prediction: If restraining-band pressure was a dominant compound component, acquisition of the buzzer-alone CR will slow and the asymptotic saliva volume will decrease relative to the original protocol. Verifiable outcome: compare mean saliva volume per trial across 20 acquisition trials; a reduction greater than 50% in the band-controlled condition vetoes the buzzer-only attribution.
Breakdown condition: If saliva volume is unchanged despite band neutralization, the band component had negligible weight (w_bands ≈ 0), and the veto does not fire.

Scenario 2 — Interaural-cue equating (biomechanical-layer ablation).
Intervention: Deliver the buzzer via insert earphones at equal intensity and zero interaural phase difference, eliminating the natural free-field ILD/ITD produced by head orientation [PAGE 214].
Prediction: Under a configural compound model, removing the binaural-difference component should alter CR strength proportional to the lost weight w_{ear-difference}. If the original CR depended on asymmetric binaural cues, equal-phase presentation will reduce peak salivation rate during the 7-s interval. Verifiable outcome: peak mL/s in original free-field vs. equal-phase condition; a significant drop supports the compound-stimulus account and vetoes a pure monaural buzzer theory.
Singularity check: If the dog’s head was effectively fixed in the original protocol, ILD/ITD were constant rather than varying; in that case equal-phase presentation should produce no change, and the veto does not fire.

Scenario 3 — Trace-interval extension (temporal-layer ablation).
Intervention: Keep the buzzer and US identical, but increase the trace interval from 2 s to 10 s, yielding a total CS–US interval of 15 s.
Prediction: Scalar expectancy and temporal-gradient models predict exponential decay of responding with trace duration, P(CR) ∝ exp(−Δt/τ), with τ on the order of seconds. CR should fall below detection threshold. Verifiable outcome: registered capsule pressure signal-to-noise ratio during the extended interval. If a robust CR persists at a 10-s trace, then a persistent non-buzzer component (e.g., continuous capsule contact or muzzle pressure) is supporting the response, and the pure buzzer-trace account is vetoed.
Falsification boundary: If CR decays to zero at 10 s exactly as predicted, the temporal layer is consistent with a buzzer-mediated trace memory, and this particular veto does not fire.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
