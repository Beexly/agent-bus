John Bates Clark’s “dynamic” theory attempted to account for profit by attributing it to progressive changes like population growth or technological advancement, viewing these as external forces driving the system away from equilibrium. However, as Knight observed, even in the absence of such systematic trends, profit persists due to mere unpredictability. This necessitated a deeper ontological category beyond measurable probability.

This lineage connects to earlier philosophical debates about induction (Hume) and the problem of the criterion. In the 20th century, this influenced Keynes’ skepticism toward probabilistic forecasts during booms and busts, emphasizing that investors operate on convention rather than mathematical expectation. Later, G.L.S. Shackle developed “potential surprise” theory, explicitly rejecting probabilistic weights for unique events, directly inheriting Knight’s mantle.

A multi-week trap emerges from misapplying statistical tools designed for frequentist risks to singular uncertainties. Financial engineers might construct Value-at-Risk (VaR) models assuming normal distributions and stationary variances. During calm periods, these models appear accurate, reinforcing confidence. However, when a rare, high-impact event occurs (a "black swan"), the model’s tail estimates prove catastrophically wrong. The trap is self-reinforcing: successful navigation of routine fluctuations validates the use of simplified metrics, which then blinds actors to structural vulnerabilities until a crisis reveals the underlying Knightian uncertainty. This pattern repeats across decades, from the 1987 crash to the 2008 financial meltdown, illustrating a persistent failure to distinguish between variability that can be diversified away and fundamental ignorance of the future.
