Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit:

The paper by Niu and Harvey (2022) tests whether outcome feedback (OFB) attenuates lay over-forecasting of inflation and overconfidence in those forecasts. The derivation proceeds through three nested error layers. First, directional or constant error (CE) is defined algebraically as CE = \hat{y} - y, where \hat{y} is the judged inflation rate and y is the realized World Bank CPI value for 2019 [PAGE 12]. Across all four condition-by-session cells in Experiment 1, one-sample t-tests reject CE = 0 (p < 0.001), confirming H1 that laypeople overestimate inflation [PAGE 13]. Second, absolute error (AE) is |CE|, and variable error (VE) is operationalized as the absolute residual from a linear-plus-quadratic trend fit to each participant’s CE sequence within a session [PAGE 12]. The critical gray-zone result is that OFB reduces CE (bias) but increases VE (noise), so AE remains unchanged: “feedback had no effect on overall error (AE) because its effects on CE and VE were in opposite directions” [PAGE 12]. This is a classic bias–variance cancellation at the level of individual judgment, not a failure of feedback per se.

The learning-versus-incentivization distinction is established via the withdrawal design: Session 1 provides OFB to the Feedback group; Session 2 withdraws it for everyone [PAGE 10]. Because the bias reduction persists into Session 2, Annett’s (1969) incentive hypothesis is rejected in favor of a learning account [PAGE 17]. However, the “dose” of feedback does not matter: Experiment 2 shows no gradient across 2-, 5-, and 10-feedback conditions, only that any feedback beats none [PAGE 24]. This violates a naive expectation that more information monotonically improves calibration and suggests a threshold or “one-shot” knowledge transfer mechanism [PAGE 24].

A deeper gray zone concerns the hard–easy effect. Ferrell’s (1994) model decomposes bias against difficulty into an intercept (base-rate identification) and a slope (discriminability) [PAGE 28]. OFB shifts the intercept downward without changing the slope, meaning it improves average confidence calibration but not the ability to discriminate hard from easy series [PAGE 28]. The calibration ceiling remains poor: even with OFB, mean calibration score is 0.70, below the 0.75 achievable by a uniform judge who always assigns p = 0.50 [PAGE 28]. This indicates the feedback effect is shallow—learners grasp the base rate but not the series-specific uncertainty structure.

Another audit point is the increase in VE under OFB. The authors speculate that feedback makes series volatility seem larger than assumed, prompting forecasters to “simulate the noise” by scattering predictions more widely [PAGE 17]. This is plausible but not directly manipulated; the summarized-OFB condition in Experiment 2 does not increase VE, which the authors attribute to the explicit error comparison reducing one contributor to judgment noise [PAGE 25]. Whether this is a true processing-fluency effect or a demand-characteristic artifact remains unresolved.

External validity is limited by the stimulus set: 20 anonymized countries, annual CPI 2009–2018, forecasting a single year (2019) [PAGE 11]. Fourteen of twenty countries experienced falling inflation from 2018 to 2019, and cross-country dependencies were not disclosed to participants [PAGE 30]. The observed U-shaped trial pattern within sessions argues against base-rate learning driving the effect, but the paper does not run the counterfactual of systematically manipulated base rates [PAGE 30]. Finally, the persistence interval is unknown: sessions were adjacent, and the authors explicitly ask whether advantages survive hours or days [PAGE 30].

These multi-hop dependencies—bias reduction, noise increase, confidence recalibration, dose insensitivity, and unknown retention—form the causal core of the paper and define the boundaries for any downstream application.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage:

The intellectual ancestry of this paper runs through the judgment-and-decision-making feedback literature of the late 20th century. Hammond and colleagues established the multiple-cue probability learning (MCPL) paradigm in which outcome feedback often fails when more than two or three cues are present or when cue–criterion relations are non-linear [PAGE 4]. Brehmer (1980) argued that people lack the cognitive schemata for efficient probabilistic performance, whereas Todd and Hammond (1965) argued that feedback fails because it does not specify how to re-weight cues [PAGE 4]. The present paper’s time-series design sidesteps the MCPL curse by providing a single univariate series per trial, making the required cognitive operation simpler than multi-cue integration.

Annett (1969) provided the critical distinction between incentive effects (transient, effort-driven) and learning effects (persistent after feedback withdrawal) [PAGE 9]. Niu and Harvey explicitly adopt this two-session design to reject pure incentivization: the over-forecasting reduction and overconfidence reduction survive into Session 2 without feedback [PAGE 17]. This aligns with Winman and Juslin (1993), whose effects were fully maintained after feedback removal, indicating learning [PAGE 9].

The skill-acquisition literature adds a boundary condition. Schmidt and Bjork (1992) and related work (Schmidt et al., 1989; Wulf and Schmidt, 1989; Winstein and Schmidt, 1990) show that reduced feedback frequency during learning can improve retention performance because the task becomes harder and elicits greater effort [PAGE 18]. Niu and Harvey expected a similar “less is more” pattern but found instead that any feedback suffices and more feedback adds nothing [PAGE 24]. This constitutes a partial divergence from the guidance-hypothesis tradition: in their task, the knowledge conveyed by OFB appears to be captured after one or two exposures, so additional repetitions yield no marginal gain [PAGE 24].

Ferrell’s (1994) calibration model supplies the theoretical lens for the hard–easy null result [PAGE 28]. Feedback improves base-rate identification (intercept) but not discriminability (slope), explaining why calibration improves only modestly and remains below the uniform-judge benchmark of 0.75 [PAGE 28].

Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein (2021) provide the modern noise framing: overall error depends on bias plus scatter, and the same factor can move them in opposite directions [PAGE 26]. Niu and Harvey demonstrate this precisely: OFB cuts CE but raises VE, leaving AE unchanged [PAGE 12, 26].

Multi-week traps: The paper itself flags the principal temporal trap in its Limitations section: “a question remains about how long it would remain effective. If the interval between the first and second sessions … had been extended to hours or days, would the advantage of training still persist?” [PAGE 30]. A multi-week deployment of OFB in central-bank surveys would face decay risk; without scheduled booster feedback, the learning effect may regress. A second trap is dose insensitivity: if a practitioner assumes that weekly feedback outperforms monthly feedback, this paper’s null dose-response suggests that assumption is false [PAGE 24]. A third trap is population mismatch: training survey respondents alters the representativeness of the sample, so central banks cannot selectively train only lay respondents without biasing the aggregate expectation measure [PAGE 28].

These genealogical links show that the paper sits at the intersection of MCPL feedback failures, skill-acquisition guidance hypotheses, Ferrell-style calibration mechanics, and the contemporary noise-versus-bias decomposition.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock:

Crisis habit regression: When lay forecasters face inflation expectations without outcome feedback, they regress to a habitual over-forecasting mode. The availability heuristic makes price rises more cognitively available than price falls, and media coverage amplifies rises while neglecting declines [PAGE 26]. Niu and Harvey (2021) is cited for the expectation that inflation is more likely to rise than fall [PAGE 26]. This habit produces a positive CE of roughly +0.39 percentage points in the no-feedback condition of Experiment 1 [PAGE 13, Table 1]. Under crisis conditions—volatile prices, uncertain tenure—this habit likely intensifies rather than attenuates because the affective salience of rising prices increases.

Counter-trap: Outcome feedback is the counter-trap, but it is not a free lunch. The counter-trap operates by reducing CE (bias) while simultaneously increasing VE (scatter/noise), such that AE is unchanged [PAGE 12]. The mechanism proposed is that feedback convinces forecasters that series volatility is higher than they assumed, so they add scatter to simulate noise [PAGE 17]. This is a recursive theory-of-mind failure: the forecaster models the environment as noisier than it is and overcorrects by injecting random error into every subsequent judgment. The summarized-OFB condition in Experiment 2 partially escapes this trap because the explicit numerical difference between judgment and outcome reduces one source of noise [PAGE 25], but simple trial-by-trial feedback does not [PAGE 21].

Clock: The temporal structure is governed by session boundaries and trial position. Session 1 establishes the learning or incentive; Session 2 tests retention after feedback withdrawal [PAGE 10]. Within each session, confidence shows an early drop followed by stability, and VE follows an inverted-U across trials (low at beginning and end, higher in the middle) [PAGE 25]. The authors interpret the beginning-of-session caution as forecasts anchored near the series mean, mid-session adventurism as suspected trend-chasing, and end-of-session reversion to caution after experience convinces participants that no series trends [PAGE 25]. The “clock” therefore has three hands: session length (two sessions), feedback withdrawal (after Session 1), and intra-session trial position (1–10). A forecaster who ignores the clock—e.g., by assuming feedback benefits accumulate linearly across all ten trials—is contradicted by the null dose-response and the absence of trial-by-trial divergence within Session 1 [PAGE 17, 24].

Recursive theory-of-mind synthesis: The forecaster believes that experience improves performance even without feedback (Harvey & Fischer, 2005 [PAGE 17]); when no feedback is given, this belief inflates overconfidence across sessions. When feedback is given, the forecaster is disabused of that notion, overconfidence stops rising, and the correction persists after withdrawal [PAGE 17]. This is a second-order update: the agent learns not just about inflation but about the reliability of its own confidence judgments. The failure mode is that the agent updates the intercept (base rate) but not the slope (discriminability), leaving it unable to distinguish hard series from easy ones even after calibration improves [PAGE 28].

Veto Scenario 1 — Delayed feedback withdrawal beyond one session.

Verification condition: If the CE advantage of the prior-feedback group over the no-feedback group decays to non-significance (p > 0.05) by Session 3, while the no-feedback group’s CE remains stable, the persistence claim is falsified.
Prediction: Based on the limitation explicitly raised in the Discussion [PAGE 30], I predict the learning effect decays over hours-to-days; the CE gap narrows or closes, and overconfidence rebounds toward no-feedback levels because the knowledge trace is not consolidated without booster feedback.

Veto Scenario 2 — Multiple-cue probability learning with non-linear cue–criterion relations.
Counterfactual: Replace the univariate inflation time-series task with an MCPL task using four or more economic cues (e.g., unemployment, GDP growth, money supply, exchange rate) with a non-linear rule generating the criterion, as in the paradigms reviewed by Hammond et al. (1973) and Brehmer (1980) [PAGE 4]. Provide identical trial-by-trial OFB.
Verification condition: If OFB fails to reduce CE or increases AE relative to no-feedback, the generalization of the paper’s “OFB improves time-series forecasting” claim is vetoed for complex cue environments.
Prediction: Based on the cited literature [PAGE 4], I predict OFB will not improve accuracy and may impair it, because people lack schemata for efficient probabilistic integration (Brehmer, 1980) and feedback does not specify cue re-weighting (Todd & Hammond, 1965). The paper’s positive result is bounded to simple, single-series time-series tasks.

Veto Scenario 3 — Systematic base-rate manipulation of inflation direction.
Counterfactual: In Experiment 2, 14 of 20 countries showed falling inflation from 2018 to 2019, but participants were not informed of base rates [PAGE 30]. Suppose a new condition explicitly informs participants that “inflation fell in 70% of countries last year” before every forecast, with OFB provided.
Verification condition: If the feedback-induced CE reduction disappears or reverses (participants under-forecast because they overweight the communicated base rate), the claim that OFB transmits task knowledge independent of base-rate information is vetoed.
Prediction: Based on Ferrell’s model and the paper’s discussion [PAGE 28, 30], I predict that explicit base-rate information will shift the intercept (base-rate identification) directly, potentially making OFB redundant for bias reduction. Participants may rely on the stated base rate rather than learning from feedback, producing no additional CE benefit from OFB over the base-rate-only condition.

All three scenarios are verifiable with standard ANOVA or regression on CE, AE, VE, and confidence bias, using the same dependent variables and percentage-point units as the source [PAGE 12, 13].
