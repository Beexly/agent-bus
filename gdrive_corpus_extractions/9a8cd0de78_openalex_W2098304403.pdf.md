The source is a Bayesian hierarchical probit analysis of baseball fielding, not a predictive market document. The reasoning chain is: high-resolution ball-in-play (BIP) endpoints \((x,y)\), velocity \(V\), and fielder identity are used to estimate player-specific success probabilities; those probabilities are compared with a position-average curve; the differences are weighted by BIP frequency, run consequence, and shared responsibility, then integrated to a seasonal “SAFE” run value. The first multi-hop derivation is data-geometry: for flyballs/liners the paper models a two-dimensional travel problem, while for grounders it models a one-dimensional angular travel problem. The source explicitly says the actual starting location is not observed: “the actual starting location of the ﬁelder for any particular play is not included in the data. Instead, the starting location for each position is estimated as the location in the ﬁeld where each position has the highest overall proportion of successful plays” [PAGE 5]. That is a crucial gray-zone: \(D_{ij}\) or \(\theta_{ij}\) are measured from an estimated positional centroid, not from the fielder’s true jump/route origin. Thus the coefficient labeled “range” is conditional on a centroid approximation and can absorb unobserved positioning, adjacent-fielder coverage, and park geometry.

The second multi-hop step is hierarchical shrinkage. A no-pooling probit gives extreme \(\hat\beta_i\) when \(n_i\) is small; the paper notes that no-pooling estimates for low-sample CFs were “undeﬁned, did not converge or were clearly unrealistic” [PAGE 12], and that several no-pooling probability curves were “extreme in shape” [PAGE 15]. The hierarchical prior \(\beta_i\sim\mathrm{Normal}(\mu,\Sigma)\) borrows strength across players at a position [PAGE 7]. The observed posterior means for \(\sigma_k\) in the CF illustration were 0.15, 0.28, 0.28 and 0.17, with no mass near zero [PAGE 11], which argues against complete pooling. This is a precise statistical statement: the data support partial pooling, not “all fielders are the same” and not “each fielder’s raw split is truth.”

The third multi-hop step is aggregation. The SAFE integral multiplies four objects: BIP frequency \(\hat f\), run consequence \(\hat r_{\rm tot}\), positional shared responsibility \(\hat s_{\rm pos}\), and the probability difference \(\hat p_i-\hat p_+\). The units must be checked carefully. \(\hat f\) is a seasonal BIP density over space and velocity; \(\hat r_{\rm tot}\) is runs per unsuccessful BIP; \(\hat s\) is a dimensionless share; \(\hat p_i-\hat p_+\) is a dimensionless probability difference. Their product has units of runs per season. If any factor is silently omitted, the “SAFE” number changes meaning. The paper’s own aggregation is explicit: \( \mathrm{SAFE}^{\rm fly}_i=\int \hat f(x,y,v)\hat r_{\rm tot}(x,y,v)\hat s_{\rm pos_i}(x,y,v)[\hat p_i(x,y,v)-\hat p_+(x,y,v)]\,dx\,dy\,dv \) [PAGE 24]. The grounder form replaces \((x,y)\) with \(\theta\) [PAGE 24]. The integration is numerical over a grid, and the paper later admits the grid assumes “the maximal park dimensions” while actual parks differ, with Fenway’s left field as the dramatic example [PAGE 31]. That is a breakdown regime for park-specific interpretation.

The fourth step is validation. The paper uses posterior predictive residual checks: 95 of 100 observed binned residuals fell inside 95% posterior predictive intervals [PAGE 13], and removing fielding errors left hierarchical probability curves “essentially identical” while no-pooling curves were more sensitive [PAGE 18]. It also compares SAFE with UZR by correlation and between-year consistency. The between-year comparison assumes constant ability, which the authors explicitly flag: “by looking at consistency between years as a validation measure, we are assuming that player ability is actually constant over time, which may not be the case for many players” [PAGE 30]. This is where a gray-zone audit must separate descriptive conditional estimation from causal talent estimation. The model can be a good conditional predictor of observed BIP outcomes while still failing as a pure biomechanical skill measure because weather, actual starting position, sprint speed, route efficiency, injury, and park geometry are omitted or approximated. The physical veto is therefore not “the model is wrong”; it is “the causal interpretation of SAFE as intrinsic fielding ability is vetoed unless those layers are shown to be ignorable.” The source itself supports the descriptive claim, not an unqualified physical-causal claim.

\[
S_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{ij}),\qquad S_{ij}\in\{0,1\},
\]
where \(p_{ij}\) is dimensionless probability and \(S_{ij}=1\) denotes a successful play [PAGE 6]. The flyball/liner probit model is
\[
p_{ij}
=\Phi\!\left(\beta_{i0}+\beta_{i1}D_{ij}+\beta_{i2}D_{ij}F_{ij}
+\beta_{i3}D_{ij}V_{ij}+\beta_{i4}D_{ij}V_{ij}F_{ij}\right)
=\Phi(X_{ij}\cdot\beta_i),
\]
with \(\Phi\) the standard normal CDF, \(D_{ij}\) the modeled travel distance in feet, \(V_{ij}\) the ordinal velocity level \(1,2,3\) treated as continuous and dimensionless, and \(F_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{y_{ij}<324\}\) a forward/backward indicator [PAGE 6] [PAGE 10]. For grounders,
\[
p_{ij}
=\Phi\!\left(\beta_{i0}+\beta_{i1}\theta_{ij}
+\beta_{i2}\theta_{ij}L_{ij}
+\beta_{i3}\theta_{ij}V_{ij}
+\beta_{i4}\theta_{ij}V_{ij}L_{ij}\right)
=\Phi(X_{ij}\cdot\beta_i),
\]
where \(\theta_{ij}\) is the angle in degrees between the grounder trajectory and the line from home plate to the fielder’s estimated starting location, and \(L_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) is the left/right indicator [PAGE 7]. The parameter units are: \(\beta_{i0}\) on the probit scale; \(\beta_{i1}^{fly}\) ft\(^{-1}\); \(\beta_{i2}^{fly}\) ft\(^{-1}\); \(\beta_{i3}^{fly}\) ft\(^{-1}\) per velocity unit; \(\beta_{i4}^{fly}\) ft\(^{-1}\) per velocity unit; the grounder analogues are degree\(^{-1}\) and degree\(^{-1}\) per velocity unit. The velocity variable is dimensionless because it is ordinal, not a physical speed in ft/s. The source is explicit that velocity is “an ordinal variable with three levels (the velocity variable is estimated by human observation of video, not using any machinery)” [PAGE 4].

The hierarchical prior is

with noninformative hyperprior

with predictors rescaled to mean zero and standard deviation 0.5 [PAGE 10]. In that illustration there were \(N=138\) unique CFs, \(n_i\) ranging from 1 to 531, and 15,767 CF-eligible flyballs [PAGE 9]. The run-consequence operator is

and the total is the sum over BIP types for the player’s position [PAGE 24]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is visible in the paper’s own citations and comparisons. It descends from zone-discretized fielding metrics: UZR uses 64 large zones, PMR uses 18 five-degree pie slices, and Plus-Minus is a similar tabulation approach [PAGE 2]. The paper’s advance is to replace coarse zones with a continuous spatial model and then wrap that model in a hierarchical Bayesian structure. Its statistical genealogy runs through hierarchical regression and MCMC: Gibbs sampling [Geman and Geman], data augmentation for probit models [Albert and Chib], and Gelman’s prior recommendations for variance parameters [PAGE 7] [PAGE 8] [PAGE 31]. The run-conversion layer descends from classical linear weights: 0.5 runs for a single, 0.8 for a double, and 1.1 for a triple, attributed to Thorn and Palmer [PAGE 23] [PAGE 31]. The spatial-smoothing idea also has a cited sibling in Reich et al.’s spatial analysis of basketball shot charts [PAGE 2]. The longitudinal extension is explicitly connected to Glickman and Stern’s state-space model for football scores [PAGE 29] [PAGE 31]. Retrocausally, the paper anticipates later tracking-based fielding analysis by modeling continuous location, but it also exposes the exact missing layers that later systems would need: actual fielder starting position, route efficiency, sprint speed, weather, and park-specific geometry.

Multi-week traps recur across this lineage. The first trap is zone habit: after a few weeks of scouting, an analyst may mentally discretize a continuous field into “he makes that play” or “he does not,” losing the very resolution the model adds. The second trap is small-sample heroism: a few weeks of BIPs produce extreme no-pooling curves; the paper’s own no-pooling plots show “curves are extreme in shape” [PAGE 15], while partial pooling stabilizes them. The third trap is posterior-mean ranking without intervals: Tables 2–4 show overlapping intervals even among best/worst player-years [PAGE 26] [PAGE 27] [PAGE 28], so a multi-week ranking can overfit noise. The fourth trap is using between-year correlation as a gold standard while assuming constant ability; the paper explicitly warns that this assumption “may not be the case for many players” [PAGE 30]. The fifth trap is park myopia: a multi-week look at Fenway left field or another non-maximal park can confound geometry with fielder skill [PAGE 31]. The sixth trap is credit assignment: shared responsibility \(\hat s_{\rm pos}\) is estimated from relative frequencies of successful plays by position, not from a randomized credit experiment [PAGE 23], so a multi-week narrative that assigns all credit to one fielder can be a retrocausal error. The source’s historical contribution is therefore both a model and a checklist of what later data layers must repair.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has three modes. First, crisis habit regression: when uncertainty is high, evaluators regress to reputation and coarse habit. The paper’s Jeter discussion is the clearest example: Jeter “has been awarded several gold gloves despite being considered to have poor range by most other ﬁelding methods,” while the SAFE results place him among the worst shortstops and Alex Rodriguez among the best [PAGE 25]. Statistically, crisis habit regression is the no-pooling extreme: small \(n_i\) yields wild \(\hat\beta_i\), and the observer treats the wild curve as talent. The hierarchical prior is the counter-habit: it shrinks low-sample players toward the position mean, with posterior \(\sigma_k\) estimates showing that neither complete pooling nor no pooling is supported [PAGE 11]. The recursive point is that the evaluator’s prior about a player is itself a model; the hierarchical model makes that prior explicit and estimable.

Second, counter-trap: once SAFE or UZR rankings become deployment policy, the data-generating process changes. If a manager shifts a fielder based on a noisy ranking, or if opponents target the zone the model labels weak, then the BIP frequency \(\hat f(x,y,v)\), shared responsibility \(\hat s_{\rm pos}(x,y,v)\), and even run consequence \(\hat r_{\rm tot}\) are no longer fixed. A naive retrain then rewards or punishes the player for data generated by the model’s own reputation. This is a recursive theory-of-mind trap: the modeler models the player, the player/manager models the model, and the next season’s data reflect both. The paper’s fixed-integration equations [PAGE 24] are valid for historical description but not automatically for closed-loop policy. Counter-trap defenses are held-out seasons, position-balanced opportunities, explicit posterior intervals, and joint models of adjacent fielders rather than independent credit.

Third, clock: there are at least three time scales—play clock (weather, wind, sun, footing, jump), season clock (SAFE run value over a year), and career clock (aging and true ability). The paper fits year-specific models and uses between-year correlation as a validation clock, but it admits the constant-ability assumption [PAGE 29] [PAGE 30]. A multi-week hot streak is mostly play-clock and season-clock noise; a multi-year trend is career-clock signal mixed with park and role changes. The correct recursive behavior is to match the shrinkage and interval width to the clock: do not use a three-week sample as career truth, do not use a career average to erase a real injury-driven season, and do not deploy a ranking whose posterior interval crosses zero. The paper’s own tables show many intervals crossing zero even among extreme posterior means [PAGE 26] [PAGE 27] [PAGE 28], which is the clock warning made numerical.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The model’s causal-skill claim is vetoed if omitted physical, biomechanical, or weather layers are correlated with the modeled covariates and with player identity. Formally, the fitted model assumes
\[
P(S_{ij}=1\mid X_{ij},\beta_i)=\Phi(X_{ij}\beta_i).
\]
A cross-layer expanded truth is
\[
P(S_{ij}=1\mid X_{ij},O_{ij},B_{ij},\beta_i,\gamma,\delta)
=\Phi(X_{ij}\beta_i+\gamma O_{ij}+\delta B_{ij}),
\]
where \(O_{ij}\) is a weather/visibility/surface vector and \(B_{ij}\) is a biomechanical vector (actual starting position, acceleration, route, sprint speed, jump, injury state). In a linear-probability approximation,
\[
\mathbb{E}[S\mid X]=X\beta+\gamma O+\delta B,
\]
so the omitted-variable estimator is
\[
\hat\beta=\beta+(X'X)^{-1}X'(\gamma O+\delta B).
\]
Therefore, if \(\gamma\neq0\) or \(\delta\neq0\) and \(X\) is correlated with \(O\) or \(B\), the posterior \(\beta_i\) and hence \(\hat p_i-\hat p_+\) absorb non-skill variation. In the probit model the same logic applies through the score; the bias is nonlinear but does not vanish merely because \(\Phi\) is used. This is the formal veto: SAFE is a valid conditional description of observed BIP outcomes given \(X\), but it is not a pure biomechanical ability measure under cross-layer confounding.

The physical layer veto is strongest for flyballs. Wind, sun, rain, temperature, and air density change carry and catch difficulty; the source includes no weather covariates and models catch probability from distance, direction, and an ordinal human-observed velocity [PAGE 6]. The biomechanical layer veto is strongest because the paper explicitly lacks actual starting location: “the actual starting location of the ﬁelder for any particular play is not included in the data” [PAGE 5]. If the true start is \(r_{start,i,j}=c_{\rm pos}+a_i+u_{ij}\), then the modeled distance from centroid \(D_{\rm model}=\|r_{BIP}-c_{\rm pos}\|\) differs from \(D_{\rm true}=\|r_{BIP}-r_{start,i,j}\|\); a player-specific jump/positioning term \(a_i\) can be absorbed into \(\beta_{i1}\). Thus a fielder with better initial positioning or acceleration can be labeled as having better “range” even if the underlying route model is omitted. The weather/biomechanical veto is not a demand that the paper should have had 2026 tracking data; it is a precise adjudication: descriptive SAFE is supported by the source’s posterior predictive checks [PAGE 13] and error-removal robustness [PAGE 18], but causal SAFE is vetoed unless \(O\) and \(B\) are shown to be ignorable or are included.

The park layer is a separate physical veto. The source states that SAFE numerical integrations “are made over a grid of points that assume the maximal park dimensions, but individual park dimensions can be quite different” [PAGE 31]. If the true park boundary truncates or extends the playable region, then both \(\hat f(x,y,v)\) and \(\hat r_{\rm tot}(x,y,v)\) are misspecified near fences. A flyball that is a home run in a small park may be a catchable out in a large park; integrating over a maximal grid assigns nonzero weight to locations that do not exist in that park. This veto is park-specific and verifiable: refit with park-specific integration domains and compare SAFE. The source’s own Fenway left-field example [PAGE 31] is sufficient to prove that the maximal-park assumption is not universally harmless.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Error-removal hysteresis veto. The source reports that re-estimating the Bayesian hierarchical probability models with all fielding errors removed produced curves “essentially identical” to the curves with errors included, while no-pooling curves were “much more sensitive” [PAGE 18]. Verifiable test: rerun the full hierarchical SAFE pipeline on the error-removed dataset and compare posterior mean SAFE and 95% intervals for the top/bottom player-years. Prediction: hierarchical SAFE rankings should remain stable within posterior intervals, with high overlap in the top ten and bottom ten; no-pooling rankings should move substantially. Veto condition: if hierarchical SAFE shifts by more than its 95% interval half-width for a material fraction of players, the paper’s robustness claim is not supported and the outlying-error treatment becomes a model veto.

2. Park-geometry integration veto. The source uses maximal park dimensions for numerical integration and admits that actual park dimensions differ, especially Fenway’s left field [PAGE 31]. Verifiable test: replace the universal maximal grid with park-specific \(x,y\) domains and park-specific \(\hat f\) and \(\hat r_{\rm tot}\), then recompute SAFE for outfielders in parks whose fences deviate most from maximal dimensions. Prediction: left/right fielders in small or asymmetric parks should show the largest SAFE changes, especially near-fence locations; center fielders in symmetric large parks should change less. Veto condition: if park-specific integration changes posterior mean SAFE by more than the reported posterior interval for a substantial set of outfielders, the maximal-park assumption is a cross-layer veto for park-adjusted interpretation.

3. Ordinal-velocity treatment veto. The source treats \(V_{ij}\in\{1,2,3\}\) as continuous to reduce coefficients, even though velocity is an ordinal human-observed variable [PAGE 4] [PAGE 6]. Verifiable test: refit with velocity as two categorical indicators, or with a measurement-error/ordered-probit treatment, and compare \(\beta_{i3},\beta_{i4}\) and SAFE for players with many high-velocity BIPs. Prediction: players whose opportunities are concentrated in velocity level 3 should show the largest changes in velocity-adjusted probability curves and SAFE; the population-level distance coefficient should remain the dominant predictor. Veto condition: if categorical/measurement-error refitting changes posterior mean SAFE beyond the 95% interval for a material subset of players, the continuous-velocity simplification is not innocuous and must be treated as a model veto rather than a harmless parsimony choice.
