<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal source operators, with units and page citations.

1. Input and stream domains.
Let \(I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}\) be a broadcast thumbnail. [PAGE 5] fixes “input image resolution” at “256x128,” so \(H=256\), \(W=128\), in pixels. [PAGE 2] states the appearance extractor “outputs the appearance features a ∈H × W” and the part extractor “outputs the body part features p ∈H × W.” More explicitly, write
\[
A_\theta: I\mapsto a\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_a},
\qquad
P_\phi: I\mapsto p\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C_p}.
\]
The tensors are dimensionless feature maps; their spatial coordinates are pixel locations. The paper does not state \(C_a\) or \(C_p\).

2. OpenPose stage recursion.
[PAGE 2] describes OpenPose as multi-stage: earlier stages capture Part Affinity Fields, later stages produce confidence maps of “17 key points.” The paper writes:
\[
L_1=\phi_1(F),
\]
\[
L_t=\phi_t(F,L_{t-1}),\qquad 2\le t\le T_P,
\]
\[
S_{T_P}=\rho_t(F,L_{T_P}),\qquad \forall t=T_P,
\]
\[
S_t=\rho_t(F,L_{T_P},S_{t-1}),\qquad T_P \lt t\le T_P+T_C.
\]
Here \(L_t\) is a 2D vector field whose components encode orientation and spatial location in image coordinates; [PAGE 2] calls PAF “a 2D vector field that captures the orientation and spatial location of the body parts relative to the image domain.” \(S_t\) is a confidence map; under the usual sigmoid interpretation its entries are probabilities in \([0,1]\), but the paper does not explicitly state the activation or calibration. The notation \(S_{T_P}=\rho_t(F,L_{T_P}),\forall t=T_P\) is internally awkward; the intended meaning is the first confidence-map stage after the PAF stages.

3. Bilinear pooling and normalization.
[PAGE 2] gives
\[
f_{xy}=\mathrm{vec}(a_{xy}\otimes p_{xy}),
\]
\[
f=\mathrm{pooling}_{xy}\{f_{xy}\}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{xy} f_{xy},
\]
where \(x,y\) are spatial locations, \(S\) is spatial size, \(a_{xy}\) is appearance feature at \((x,y)\), \(p_{xy}\) is part feature at \((x,y)\), \(\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)\) flattens a matrix to a vector, and \(\otimes\) is the outer product. The pooled vector has dimension \(C_aC_p\) before compact projection. [PAGE 2] then defines
\[
\tilde f=\frac{f}{\lVert f\rVert_2}.
\]
The unit is unit-norm dimensionless feature space; \(\lVert f\rVert_2\) is the Euclidean norm. [PAGE 2–3] adds compact bilinear pooling: “random projections” approximate the outer product, using “tensor sketch” projections, producing a lower-dimensional \(\hat f\) with \(d\ll C_aC_p\). The paper does not give the projection matrix or the variance bound.

4. Triplet and identity objectives.
[PAGE 3] defines
\[
L_{\mathrm{tri}}=\max\bigl(d(a,p)-d(a,n)+\mathrm{margin},0\bigr),
\]
where \(d\) is L2 distance and the anchor \(a\), positive \(p\), and negative \(n\) are defined by same-player/same-action and different-player/same-action gallery relations [PAGE 3]. The margin value is not stated. If natural logarithms are used, the cross-entropy identity loss on [PAGE 3–4],
\[
L_{\mathrm{ce}}=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} y_i\log \hat y_i,
\]
is in nats per sample; the paper does not specify the logarithm base. \(y_i\) is ground-truth pid and \(\hat y_i\) is predicted pid [PAGE 4]. Layer-wise similarity is described only qualitatively: hidden-layer features are passed through fully connected layers and a “metric learning loss” is calculated at multiple layers [PAGE 3]. A source-faithful local form is
\[
L_{\ell}=\max\bigl(d(a_\ell,p_\ell)-d(a_\ell,n_\ell)+\mathrm{margin},0\bigr),
\]
but the paper does not specify the aggregation weights, layer indices, or exact composite objective. The phrase “The number of layers, choice, and fully connected layers are tuned in cross-validation” [PAGE 3] is the only selection rule given.

5. Evaluation metrics.
[PAGE 4] states “We use Rank-1 accuracy and mAP as evaluation metrics.” Rank-1 is the fraction of queries whose top gallery match has the same pid; mAP is the mean over queries of average precision over the ranked gallery. They are reported as percentages in [PAGE 5, Tables 1–3]. The query set is from action frames and the gallery from replay frames [PAGE 4], and matching is restricted to the same action [PAGE 4].

6. Exact singularity and breakdown regimes.
(i) Normalization singularity: \(\tilde f=f/\lVert f\rVert_2\) is undefined when \(f=0\). This occurs if the part stream returns an all-zero or numerically underflowed map, for example under missing keypoints or masked body parts. The paper gives no epsilon floor [PAGE 2].
(ii) Dimensional blow-up: the outer product has \(C_aC_p\) coordinates per spatial location; naive pooling costs \(O(HWC_aC_p)\). Compact bilinear pooling reduces this but introduces random-projection approximation error and collision variance [PAGE 2–3].
(iii) Pose breakdown: if fewer than the required keypoints are detected, or if PAF association by “bipartite graph matching” is underconstrained [PAGE 2], the part map is unreliable. An empty confidence map makes the “part-aligned” claim false for that image.
(iv) Triplet saturation: if \(d(a,p)-d(a,n)+\mathrm{margin}\le 0\) for all sampled triplets, the loss and gradient vanish. With few per-class samples [PAGE 1] and action-scoped identities [PAGE 4], hard-positive mining is fragile.
(v) Cross-entropy singularity: if \(\hat y_i=0\) for the true class, \(\log \hat y_i\) diverges; practical implementations need numerical stabilization, which the paper does not discuss.
(vi) Evaluation breakdown: because “player identity labels do not persist between actions” [PAGE 4], any evaluation that links a player across actions changes the label semantics and is not the paper’s protocol.

The metric-learning lineage is the triplet and identity-loss tradition cited in refs. [4, 5, 6] [PAGE 6], and the multi-scale and layer-wise similarity lineage appears in refs. Retrocausally, the later conclusion on [PAGE 6] that “low-level features had rich semantic information” and should be used in “layer-wise similarity scores” reinterprets the earlier architecture: the part stream is not merely an auxiliary pose branch; it is a way to impose local alignment on a representation that would otherwise collapse under same-team appearance similarity. The paper’s own history also contains a cautionary trap: the abstract’s initialization story [PAGE 1] and the methods’ initialization story [PAGE 2] do not match. A multi-week reproduction attempt that trusts the abstract will train an ImageNet-initialized appearance stream and a from-scratch part stream; one that trusts the methods section will use Zoo-initialized ResNet50 and COCO-initialized OpenPose. That difference alone can consume experimental cycles and produce non-comparable results.

Other multi-week traps are dataset-semantic rather than architectural. The first is treating SoccerNet-V3 as a persistent-identity benchmark. [PAGE 4] is explicit: “player identity labels do not persist between actions, and each action where a player has been seen has a separate identity.” A researcher who builds cross-action identity pairs will be optimizing a different task than the one evaluated. The second trap is comparing Table 1 and Table 2 without respecting the training fraction: [PAGE 5] reports 10% and 2% training subsets. On 10%, “Our” is 63.7 mAP and 52.8 Rank-1 versus OSnet 61.6 and 51.2; on 2%, OSnet is 55.5 and 45.1 versus “Our” 55.0 and 42.4. The universal-sounding claim on [PAGE 1] is therefore a conditional result. The third trap is the layer-wise arithmetic: [PAGE 5] says Rank-1 rose by 3.6%, while Table 3 implies 36.2 minus 32.8 equals 3.4 percentage points. The fourth trap is the small-subset protocol itself: the paper trains on “10% of the dataset” with batch size 32, Adam at 0.00001, and 256x128 inputs [PAGE 5]; these choices are not shown to be stable at full scale. The genealogy therefore points forward to compact bilinear and pose-guided ReID, but backward to a narrow, action-scoped experimental base.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when the part stream is unreliable. The model’s fallback is appearance-only ResNet50: [PAGE 5, Table 1] gives ResNet50 baseline 46.7 mAP and 32.8 Rank-1, and [PAGE 5, Table 3] gives ResNet 46.7 and 32.8. Under occlusion, missing keypoints, or low confidence, the part stream’s \(p_{xy}\) becomes noise or zero; bilinear pooling then either collapses toward the appearance-only signal or becomes singular at normalization. The “habit” is the model’s implicit regression to texture and jersey color, precisely the cue that [PAGE 4] says is weak because “Soccer players from the same team have strikingly similar appearances.” The paper’s own motivation on [PAGE 1] is that physical and jersey appearance are not enough, so a pose failure is a crisis: the system reverts to the cue it was designed to supplement.

The counter-trap is the compensatory move of adding layer-wise similarity and compact bilinear pooling. [PAGE 3] says hidden-layer features are given fully connected layers and a metric-learning loss, and [PAGE 5] reports a 3.7 mAP gain for layer-wise similarity. But this counter-trap can overfit to hidden-layer texture and can amplify part-stream noise: the bilinear outer product \(a_{xy}\otimes p_{xy}\) is sensitive to outliers, and compact random projections can collide distinct part-appearance combinations [PAGE 2–3]. A model that “rescues” mAP with more layers may be learning action-specific background or replay-frame artifacts rather than persistent body-part identity.

The clock is the action boundary. [PAGE 1] notes that “the temporal distance between images is not constant,” and [PAGE 4] defines the evaluation clock: query images come from action frames, gallery images from replay frames, and “only samples from the same action are matched against each other.” This is a recursive theory-of-mind problem among annotator, model, and evaluator. The annotator’s identity label is valid only inside one action; the model sees a pid that resets across actions; the evaluator ranks only within the action. If a researcher imagines a continuous player identity across a match, they are modeling a different agent’s task than the dataset defines. The correct mental model is: same action, different camera view, same pid; different action, even same human, different pid [PAGE 4]. The clock is not a calendar; it is the action window. That is why a model can look strong on the paper’s metric while failing at cross-action re-identification. The recursive trap is to read “player re-identification” as persistent identity when the source’s own protocol says otherwise.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical, biomechanical, or weather veto would require an explicit variable that forces the representation to be invalid. The paper contains no such layer. There is no camera calibration, homography, 3D skeleton, joint-angle constraint, friction model, ball-flight model, or weather channel. The only physical context is the statement that “the views are disjoint, the temporal distance between images is not constant, and lighting conditions and backgrounds are different” [PAGE 1], plus “Image resolution also varies significantly” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, physical and weather effects enter only as image-domain nuisance and pose-detector error, not as a certified veto.

Formally, let the deployed map be
\[
T(I)=\frac{\sum_{xy}\mathrm{vec}(A_\theta(I)_{xy}\otimes P_\phi(I)_{xy})}{\left\lVert \sum_{xy}\mathrm{vec}(A_\theta(I)_{xy}\otimes P_\phi(I)_{xy})\right\rVert_2}.
\]
There is no term \(C_{\mathrm{phys}}(I)\), \(C_{\mathrm{bio}}(I)\), or \(C_{\mathrm{weather}}(I)\) in the triplet loss or identity loss [PAGE 3–4]. Consequently, the only hard vetoes are mathematical: \(f=0\) makes \(T(I)\) undefined; missing OpenPose keypoints make \(P_\phi(I)\) uninformative; action mismatch makes the pid label invalid [PAGE 4]. Everything else is a soft distribution shift.

The biomechanical layer is likewise indirect. [PAGE 2] says OpenPose uses PAF and bipartite graph matching over “17 key points.” There is no explicit joint-limit check, no temporal smoothing equation, and no 3D lifting. The claim that body-part features “can address the misalignment in body parts” [PAGE 1] is a learned-alignment claim, not a biomechanical proof. If a limb is occluded, the confidence map can be empty or wrong; if two players have similar poses and similar jerseys, the appearance stream can dominate. The weather layer is not modeled at all; rain, shadow, motion blur, and broadcast compression affect \(A_\theta\) and \(P_\phi\) as unobserved covariates. The paper gives no weather-invariance operator. Thus a weather “veto” can only be asserted empirically: if weather corrupts pose confidence, the part branch degrades; if it corrupts appearance, the appearance branch degrades. But the source does not supply a physical equation that would justify a deterministic veto.

Adjudication: the paper’s representation is physically unconstrained but statistically conditioned. The correct cross-layer statement is conditional, not absolute: under missing keypoints or zero pooled features, the part-aligned feature is mathematically vetoed; under same-team appearance similarity [PAGE 4] and disjoint views [PAGE 1], the appearance branch is statistically weak; under action-scoped labels [PAGE 4], cross-action identity is semantically vetoed. No source page supports a stronger claim that the architecture enforces biomechanics or weather invariance.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
