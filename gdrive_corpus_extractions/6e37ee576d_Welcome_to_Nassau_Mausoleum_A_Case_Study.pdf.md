**Operator 1 — Salience reweighting (the frame), information units.** Define attribute universe X = {atmosphere, dilapidation, nostalgia, price, sightline, accessibility}. Prior q(x), framed p(x). Frame strength:

D_KL(p‖q) = Σ_{x∈X} p(x) · log₂[ p(x) / q(x) ]   [bits]

Assumptions: (i) X finite and exhaustive enough that residual mass is negligible; (ii) absolute continuity, q(x) > 0 ∀x with p(x) > 0. **Breakdown regime:** if the telecast introduces an attribute with zero prior mass (e.g. a viewer who has never heard of the Veterans Day dedication), q(x) = 0 and D_KL diverges; the correct repair is a mixture prior q′ = (1−ε)q + ε·Uniform(X), which caps D_KL at log₂(1/ε) bits. Illustrative parameterization for the observed split: if the prior over {atmosphere, dilapidation} is (0.5, 0.5) and the framed distribution is (0.9, 0.1), then D_KL = 0.9·log₂1.8 + 0.1·log₂0.2 = 0.763 − 0.232 = **0.531 bits**. This is a statement about attention allocation, not about outcomes.

**Operator 2 — Cumulative exposure (repetition).** Frame adoption over n exposures follows a saturating learning curve:

A(n) = A_∞ · (1 − e^{−λ n})   [A in probability units, n in exposures, λ in exposure⁻¹]

Assumptions: homogeneous exposure efficacy, no forgetting, no counter-frame interference. **Breakdown:** λ → 0 gives A(n) ≡ 0 (degenerate frame); n large gives A → A_∞ (saturation, so marginal exposures carry near-zero bits); and the model fails entirely once a counter-frame (the Dallas feed, [PAGE 13]) is co-present, because then the process is competitive rather than cumulative. The study's exposure budget is roughly 14 home feeds over 8 weeks [PAGE 6]; the "Tradition on Ice" / "43 Years of History" patches were "on their jerseys throughout the season" [PAGE 9], so n is plausibly large and A(n) plausibly near saturation.

**Operator 3 — Room acoustics (the physical substrate of the "loud" frame).** Sabine reverberation time:

T₆₀ = 0.161 · V / A   [T₆₀ in s; V in m³; A = Σᵢ Sᵢ αᵢ in m² sabins]

with room gain and critical distance r_c = 0.141 · √(Q·R), R = S·ᾱ/(1−ᾱ) in m². Incoherent crowd-source summation:

L_p(N) = L_p(N₀) + 10·log₁₀(N/N₀)   [dB SPL, N in persons]

so doubling an audience of 8,000 to the "16,000 in here" Okposo cites [PAGE 8] yields exactly **+3.0 dB** — a real but modest increment, insufficient by itself to explain the categorical language ("a library in Pittsburgh last night and a madhouse here" [PAGE 8]).

**Assumptions and breakdown.** Sabine assumes a diffuse field, ᾱ ≲ 0.3, and proportional absorption distribution. Nassau violates the diffuse assumption at the coupled bowl–concourse boundary (double-slope decay) and at the 97 ft ceiling [PAGE 7] if the mean free path is not large relative to the wavelength band of interest. **Exact breakdown:** A → 0 (T₆₀ → ∞, physically clamped by air absorption); ᾱ → 1 in the Sabine form gives R → ∞ and r_c → ∞, at which point the Norris–Eyring form T = 0.161V / (−S·ln(1−ᾱ)) must replace Sabine. Order-of-magnitude estimate for an occupied bowl: with A ≈ 1.0×10⁴ m² sabins (crowd absorption ≈ 0.5 m² per seated spectator × 16,000 ≈ 8.0×10³, plus structure) and V ≈ 1.5×10⁵ m³, T₆₀ ≈ 2.4 s. **This is an estimate with stated assumptions, not a measurement**; the paper supplies no SPL, no T₆₀, no impulse response.

**Operator 4 — Sightline veto geometry (second physical layer).** Solid angle subtended by the ice sheet (or by the two goal mouths) from a seat at position **r**:

Ω = ∫_A (cos θ / r²) dA   [steradians; θ = angle between the surface normal and the seat-to-element vector]

**Veto criterion:** a seat is view-obstructed if Ω < Ω*, where Ω* is the solid angle subtended by both goal mouths simultaneously. The paper asserts at Nassau that "Every seat is great for every fan anywhere you are in this building" (Haynes, [PAGE 13]) and at Barclays that there are "hundreds of seats with obstructed views" [PAGE 16]. These are opposite claims about Ω(x, y, z) in the same metropolitan market, and Ω is a geometric quantity that can be computed from seat maps and verified by survey.

**Operator 5 — Attendance demand with censoring.** Log-linear specification:

ln A_g = β₀ + β₁·F_g + β₂·W_g + β₃·S_g + ε_g,  observed A_g^obs = min(A_g, C)

with A_g in spectators, C ≈ 16,000 (capacity in persons), F_g ∈ {0,1} frame-intensity indicator, W_g in win fraction, S_g a sentiment index. The reported "10.7% jump in attendance" [PAGE 14] corresponds to Δ = ln(1.107) = **0.1017 nats**. Because 27 of 41 games hit the ceiling C [PAGE 14], OLS on A^obs is biased toward zero in the capacity regime and the naive decomposition of β₁ is unidentified — the correct estimator is a Tobit or a censored Poisson with a log link. **Breakdown:** the model is undefined in the limit β₂ → 0 *and* C binding (all variation absorbed by censoring); and it is structurally misspecified if F_g is chosen by the same management that chooses W_g (simultaneity).

**Operator 6 — Nostalgia as discounted reactivation.** Hyperbolic intertemporal valuation of a legacy asset:

V₀ = Σ_{t=0}^{T} u_t / (1 + κ·t)   [V in utility units, κ in time⁻¹]

with u_t the period-t utility of a dynasty memory reactivated at t = 0. **Breakdown:** κ → 0 collapses to linear accumulation (unbounded legacy value, which the $20 melted-ice result contradicts); the model fails entirely after t exceeds the asset's physical life, i.e. after the venue ceases to exist — which is precisely the constraint the paper names at [PAGE 16].

**Operator 7 — Brand-equity counterfactual.** Keller's differential effect made explicit:

BE = E[ Y | do(K = 1) ] − E[ Y | do(K = 0) ]

where K = 1 denotes knowledge of the venue brand, Y the behavioral response. **Exact breakdown:** the do-operator is unidentified whenever unblocked backdoor paths exist through winning (W) and final-season novelty (N) to both K and Y. The observed association E[Y|K=1] − E[Y|K=0] is not BE.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper's intellectual lineage runs in two convergent streams, and the convergence is the paper's actual contribution.

**Stream A — frame analysis.** Goffman (1974) supplies the organizing-experience concept; Entman (1993) supplies the operational definition the paper quotes verbatim — "the selection of some aspects of a perceived reality that make them more salient in a communicating text" [PAGE 3] — and Tankard (2001) supplies the empirical bridge. Gitlin (1980) contributes "persistent patterns of cognition, interpretation, and presentation of selection, emphasis, and exclusion" (p. 7) [PAGE 4]. The sports-telecast branch is dated precisely by the author: Real's 1975 Super Bowl study is the origin point, and Real himself reflected in 2013 on treating a megaevent with "kind of serious, sustained, critical analysis which it had not been receiving" (p. 31) [PAGE 13]. From there the branch runs through Olympic gender and ethnicity framing (Billings & Eastman 2003; Billings & Angelini 2007; Angelini & Billings 2010a, 2010b) and World Cup nationality framing (Billings & Tambosi 2004; Scott, Hill & Zakus 2012, 2014). The gap the paper claims is exact: prior work is near-exclusively megaevent-focused, and "scant attention has been paid to regional telecasts of games between teams in a major North American sports league" [PAGE 4].

**Stream B — venue as brand asset.** Aaker (1991) → Keller (1993) → Gladden & Milne (1999) → Ross, Russell & Bang (2008) carry brand equity into sport. Bitner's servicescape (1992) and Wakefield & Blodgett's (1994) Cincinnati/Cleveland footage experiment establish that the built environment itself moves stated willingness to attend — and the paper notes that the Cleveland footage "highlighted some of the same aspects for which Nassau Coliseum has been criticized, including older seats and dark and narrow spaces" [PAGE 3]. Place branding (Hankinson 2007; Kavaratzis & Hatch 2013; Zavattaro, Daspit & Adams 2015) supplies the "tangible, unique elements with affective, emotion-based elements" formula (p. 12) [PAGE 3]. Trumpbour (2007) supplies the cathedral analogy — the stadium as "the most visible and recognizable structure in many communities" (p. 2) [PAGE 3].

**The retrocausal hinge.** The genuinely unusual move is temporal. The telecasts do not merely describe the 2014–15 asset; they *revalue it by importing the 1980–83 dynasty*. Rose's line — "As the late cartoonist Bill Gallo coined, welcome to Fort Neverlose" [PAGE 11] — attaches a nickname minted during a stretch when "the Islanders won 19 consecutive playoff series and four Stanley Cups" (Ng, 2014) [PAGE 11] to a building that ESPN had just ranked last in stadium experience among 122 teams [PAGE 1]. The causal arrow is deliberately reversed: present marketing reaches backward to harvest past events, and the past events are then used to overwrite a present reputation. This is the paper's "retrocausal" content, and it has a built-in decay — once the building is vacated, the backward channel closes: "It may not be a mausoleum, but it's not Fort Neverlose either" [PAGE 16].

**The nickname's own genealogy is itself retrocausal.** Hirshon's earlier work (2015) is cited for the claim that "The 'Nassau Mausoleum' nickname gained popularity through repetition on sports radio" [PAGE 3] and, in the reference list, that the nickname is "a brainchild of the first all-sports radio station" [PAGE 20]. So the negative frame was also an artifact of mediated repetition, not of direct fan experience — the two frames (Mausoleum, Fort Neverlose) are symmetric products of the same mechanism, competing for the same prior mass.

**Multi-week traps.** Four are structural and must be respected by anyone extending this work:
1. **Sample horizon trap.** The corpus ends 6 December 2014 [PAGE 6]; the attendance and sellout outcomes are full-season [PAGE 14]. The interval between them contains the All-Star break, the trade deadline, injury shocks, and the relocation sentiment curve. Inferring full-season causality from a one-third-season text sample is a temporal external-validity failure.
2. **Novelty-decay trap.** "Last time here" framing is non-stationary and self-exhausting. Opponent feeds are explicitly *final-visit* coded (San Jose, Dallas, Winnipeg, Colorado, St. Louis) [PAGE 12–13] — the frame intensity is mechanically higher in the back half of the season, which is outside the sample.
3. **Venue-lifecycle trap.** Nostalgia requires a terminal condition. Wrigley Field and Fenway Park are cited as venues that "have built entire brands around them" [PAGE 14], but those brands are sustained by *non-terminality* plus continuous reinvestment. Transplanting the Nassau playbook to a venue with no announced end is a category error.
4. **Counter-frame trap.** The Dallas broadcast [PAGE 13] demonstrates that hostile framing is available at zero cost to an away network. Any home-framing strategy must be modeled against an adversarial co-present frame, and the paper's design (6 opponent feeds, only one hostile) cannot estimate that adversarial effect size.

**Lineage verdict.** The paper is a legitimate hybrid of Stream A and Stream B rather than a novel theory. Its contribution is scope (intraleague, regional, venue-focused) plus one memorable natural experiment: a venue ranked last in the world by one media outlet, framed near-first by another, in the same month.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Level 0 — raw perception.** The announcer reports the room: "I have to say, this place is bumping right now. It feels like a playoff game in there" (Hogan) [PAGE 7]. No model of any other mind.

**Level 1 — the announcer models the fan's desire.** Billings & Tambosi (2004) are cited for the proposition that "broadcasters are likely to describe the team in flattering terms to keep the viewers content" [PAGE 4]. The frame is now instrumental: the announcer selects atmosphere because he predicts the viewer prefers it.

**Level 2 — the announcer models the team's model of the network.** This is documented, not speculative: "commentators may risk dismissal if they do not frame the venue positively and anger the Islanders, who have final say over the hiring and retention of on-air talent" (Botta, 2010) [PAGE 2]. The announcer's utility function contains a termination hazard. Case Question 2 in the paper makes this explicit: "Should the team have mandated that its broadcasters speak positively about Nassau Coliseum on air?" [PAGE 16].

**Level 3 — the fan models the announcer's incentive.** Rader (1984) is cited for exactly this: commentators "trying to hold onto viewers by excitedly describing even the most routine plays, cheapening the games they are calling" [PAGE 4]. At Level 3 the frame is discounted on receipt. The paper cannot observe this discount — a structural blind spot.

**The egocentric-projection failure (the single most important ToM event in the corpus).** After the home-opener win, "the crowd shouted, 'Yes! Yes! Yes!' in unison, borrowing a popular chant from professional wrestling" and "Rose incorrectly interpreted the chant as a plea for the team to stay at Nassau Coliseum: 'Stay! Stay! Stay!' His misinterpretation promoted the notion that Islanders fans viewed the arena positively" [PAGE 8]. This is a Level-2 failure executed at Level-1 confidence: the announcer substitutes his own team-aligned prior for the crowd's actual communicative intent. It is the cleanest naturalistic specimen of motivated mishearing in the document, and it is *load-bearing for the argument* — the paper concedes the misinterpretation did framing work regardless of its falsity.

**Crisis habit regression.** Under relocation threat, the broadcast regresses to the dynasty repertoire: banners in the rafters [PAGE 7], alumni puck drops (Potvin, Nystrom, Smith) [PAGE 10], the "Rich Tradition / Bright Future" reel [PAGE 9], commemorative patches [PAGE 9], the Goring commercial — "This place, it's like my home. I know it like the back of my hand" [PAGE 13]. Regression here is *adaptive*: the habit repertoire is the cheapest available source of positive affect. But it is also self-limiting, because it recruits a fan base that remembers 1980–83 and excludes one that does not — a problem the paper itself raises in Case Question 5: "How might the telecasts have reached younger fans who were not alive for the Islanders' Stanley Cup dynasty?" [PAGE 16].

**Counter-trap.** The Dallas feed executes the mirror maneuver: Strangis's "What a grand old house and it's still standing, almost impossibly," with Reaugh "display[ing] exaggerated enthusiasm for never having to visit the Coliseum again by giving a thumbs-up and mouthing the word yes," then "It was built back in the early '70s, back when they were building these industrial, gray, cement venues around North America" [PAGE 13]. The counter-trap works by *pre-empting the home frame's sincerity*: it performs the nostalgia gesture and then deflates it, so that the home audience cannot accept the nostalgia frame naively. Note that Dallas nonetheless ran the dynasty footage — Reaugh "mention[ed] the Islanders' four Stanley Cups" only "in the final few seconds of the segment" [PAGE 13]. Even the hostile frame pays the history tax.

**Clock.** The countdown to 2015 is the frame's engine and its expiry date. Every nostalgic utterance is indexed to finality: "the last one at the Nassau Coliseum" [PAGE 13]; "It's the last time I'm going to be here at Nassau Coliseum unless these teams meet in the Stanley Cup Finals" (Baker) [PAGE 12]; "It's great to see this building full again" (Smith) [PAGE 10]; "I can see the old ghosts of the game for a moment there" (Potvin) [PAGE 10]. The clock converts ordinary observation into elegy, which is why the frames are so dense in a 20-game window — and why the design cannot be replicated in a non-terminal season.

**Recursive summary.** The corpus exhibits a three-tier nested-belief structure (announcer → fan, announcer → team → network, fan → announcer) with one documented Level-2 execution error (Rose), one documented adversarial counter-frame (Dallas), and one documented expiry condition (the relocation clock at [PAGE 16]).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Adjudication rule.** A framing claim survives only if the physical, biomechanical, and meteorological layers are all non-vetoing. A claim that survives rhetorically but fails a lower layer is a *transient narrative*, not a durable brand asset.

**Layer 1 — Physical/acoustic.**
*Survives:* the loudness frame. The 97 ft ceiling [PAGE 7] and the enclosed oval bowl support elevated T₆₀ and room gain under Sabine scaling; crowd-source summation gives a real +3.0 dB for a doubling of attendance, and Okposo's "you get 16,000 in here and the building gets rocking" [PAGE 8] is consistent with that magnitude. The claim is under-measured but not contradicted.
*Vetoed:* the general "fan-friendly environment" claim. The paper's own evidence contradicts it — "the Coliseum charged far more than the NHL average for hot dogs, soft drinks, and beer" (Greenberg, 2014) [PAGE 3] — while the telecast showed "fans … enjoying the concessions" [PAGE 8]. Price is a hard, verifiable, physical-transactional layer; the framed image fails it.
*Vetoed:* the structural-integrity subtext. A venue with "a leaky roof patched with temporary sealants and tape" and "faulty hoists supporting the above-ice scoreboard and sound system" [PAGE 3], plus a 2012 asbestos investigation [PAGE 2], cannot durably claim premium-quality status. These are code-compliance facts, not opinions.

**Layer 2 — Biomechanical/geometric (sightlines and egress).**
*Survives at Nassau:* the sightline claim. Haynes: "It's a great place to watch a game here. You don't have the huge corporate boxes. Every seat is great for every fan anywhere you are in this building" [PAGE 13]. Nassau's bowl is a compact, steeply-raked single-tier-dominant configuration; on a solid-angle criterion Ω ≥ Ω*, this claim is at least geometrically checkable and plausible.
*Vetoed at Barclays:* the paper concedes Barclays "has been criticized for hundreds of seats with obstructed views" [PAGE 16]. Under the Ω-criterion, hundreds of seats fall below Ω*. Therefore the sightline asset **cannot be transplanted** — the very attribute Haynes praised is the attribute the successor venue fails. This is the sharpest cross-layer veto in the document: the marketing playbook is venue-locked.
*Vetoed:* the concourse/e ingress claim. "Long lines for bathrooms and concessions in a narrow, crowded concourse" [PAGE 2] is a biomechanical throughput failure (persons per meter-width per minute), and no amount of framing changes occupancy load limits.

**Layer 3 — Meteorological/hydrological.**
*Vetoed:* tailgating, for a large fraction of the season. The paper shows "fans … tailgating in the Coliseum parking lot before games" [PAGE 8] and later, in the St. Louis feed, "a picture of himself outside the Coliseum with puddles on the old, uneven pavement in front of the arena" [PAGE 13]. Standing surface water implies a hydraulic-gradient failure — ponding where slope is below the drainage threshold and infiltration is blocked. Any surface with standing water is a slip-hazard layer: the tailgating frame is weather-contingent and, at this site, drainage-deficient even in the dry case.
*Vetoed absolutely at Barclays:* tailgating as a concept. An urban arena without a surface parking apron has no pre-game lot. The paper states this directly: "Islanders telecasts from Barclays Center will no longer be able to emphasize tailgating, a rich tradition, or a proud association with veterans" [PAGE 16]. Three assets, three vetoes — one physical (no lot), one historical (no dynasty there), one nominative (no "Veterans Memorial" in the name).
*Partially vetoed:* the Veterans Day frame. The arena's name and the monument inscription — "Dedicated to the courageous men and women who have fought to give us a peaceful tomorrow. Memorial Day, May 29, 1972" [PAGE 8] — are immutable to the venue but not transferable to the successor. The frame dies with the lease.

**Adjudicated scoreboard.** Of the two frames the paper identifies, **atmosphere survives** (acoustically grounded, measurably bounded, and third-party corroborated by the Colorado broadcast's remark on "the loudness of chants" [PAGE 13]). **Nostalgia survives only on a clock** — it is a terminal-condition asset whose physical referent (the building) is being demolished as an NHL venue. The composite "Fort Neverlose" brand fails all three layers at the successor site: no low ceiling, no unobstructed bowl, no lot. The correct verdict is that the Coliseum's brand equity was **real, non-fungible, and site-bound**, which is why the paper's own closing line — "It may not be a mausoleum, but it's not Fort Neverlose either" [PAGE 16] — is an adjudication, not a rhetorical flourish.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Each scenario below is stated so that it can be run against archived data and either confirmed or falsified. No outcome variables involving wagering are used or implied.

**RCV-1 — Performance confound veto (attendance).**
*Counterfactual:* re-estimate ln A_g = β₀ + β₁·F_g + β₂·W_g + ε with F_g omitted versus included, using the 41 Islanders home games of 2014–15, censored at capacity C (Tobit). **Prediction:** with W_g included, β̂₁ attenuates by at least 50% relative to the frame-only specification, and the residual β̂₁ is not distinguishable from zero at conventional confidence. **Falsifier:** if β̂₁ retains ≥80% of its magnitude after conditioning on win fraction, home/away, opponent quality, and month fixed effects, the framing-causation claim survives. **Why it matters:** the paper itself notes the team "had won 10 of their first 12 home games … and led the division" [PAGE 11], so the confound is documented in-source. **Verdict if prediction holds:** the 10.7% figure [PAGE 14] must be reported as a *coincident* rise, not a framing effect, and the paper's Discussion sentence must be rewritten.

**RCV-2 — Acoustic-mechanism veto.**
*Counterfactual:* measure T₆₀ and A-weighted SPL at matched occupied attendance (≈16,000) in (a) Nassau Coliseum and (b) a high-ceiling arena of comparable capacity, with a controlled crowd-excitation protocol. **Prediction:** Nassau shows T₆₀ ≥ 2.0 s at mid-frequencies and a 6–10 dB higher SPL between whistles than the control, while peak SPL during goals differs by less than 3 dB (source-dominated, not room-dominated). **Falsifier:** if inter-whistle SPL difference is under 3 dB, the room is not the mechanism and Rose's "a library in Pittsburgh last night and a madhouse here" [PAGE 8] is an artifact of excitation (score, rivalry, playoff memory), not architecture. **Secondary prediction:** at Barclays, with equal attendance and equal excitation, inter-whistle SPL is lower — falsifying any claim that the atmosphere brand transfers.

**RCV-3 — Dose-response veto (nostalgia → merchandise).**
*Counterfactual:* count nostalgia segments and on-air product mentions per telecast from the archived MSG+ feeds, then regress team-store sales of the "Tradition on Ice" patch, the arena-likeness puck, and the $20 melted-ice vial [PAGE 14] on lagged cumulative exposure, controlling for win streak, home-stand length, and holiday calendar. **Prediction:** cumulative exposure at lag 1–2 weeks predicts sales with a positive coefficient, but the coefficient collapses once the win-streak term enters; the melted-ice sellout is driven by the *terminal date*, not by exposure count. **Falsifier:** if exposure retains a stable positive coefficient across the win-streak reparameterization, the dose-response survives and the paper's causal language is warranted. **Breakdown regime:** with a single sellout event, n = 1 for the terminal outcome; the model is unidentified and must be reported descriptively.

**RCV-4 — Broadcast-amenity veto (opponent valance).**
*Counterfactual:* code each of the six opponent feeds [PAGE 6] for valence and for announcer working conditions; regress valence on (i) venue physical quality and (ii) booth amenity. **Prediction:** booth amenity dominates. Supporting in-source datum: Pang's sole complaint was the booth, where "you can't fit your notes" [PAGE 13] — not the seats, the concourse, or the ice. **Falsifier:** if valence is uncorrelated with booth amenity once venue quality is controlled, the "guests at a funeral" interpretation [PAGE 15] is sufficient and amenity is irrelevant. **Implication:** the paper's own explanation — announcers "generally have access to better amenities than fans, who must contend with worn seats, jammed concourses, crowded bathrooms and concessions" [PAGE 15] — is directly testable and should replace the untested "host/guest courtesy" alternative.

**Meta-veto across all four.** If RCV-1 and RCV-3 both return nulls, the paper's kausal chain (frame → affect → attendance and merchandise) must be demoted to a co-occurrence description, and the surviving contribution is the descriptive frame taxonomy plus the opponent-feed contrast — which is, on the evidence supplied, the safest reading of this document.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
