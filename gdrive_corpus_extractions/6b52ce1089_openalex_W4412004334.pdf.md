Physical layer veto: Younger athletes are vulnerable to growth-plate fractures, which require immediate attention to prevent long-term complications [PAGE 7]. Any training prescription that subjects open physeal plates to repetitive high-impact loading without monitoring is physically invalid and must be vetoed.

Biomechanical layer veto: Non-contact injuries arise from running, cutting, and landing with poor neuromuscular control, leading to ligament tears and muscle strains [PAGE 8]. Neuromuscular and proprioception training meaningfully reduces ACL injury incidence [PAGE 4]. Therefore, a strength-and-conditioning program that omits balance, agility, and body-control components fails to address the stated mechanism and is biomechanically vetoed. Hamstring and groin strains arise from explosive running and kicking; ankle sprains become chronic if mismanaged [PAGE 7]. Eccentric hamstring loading and ankle stabilization are necessary complements, not optional add-ons.

Environmental/surface layer veto: Indoor soccer has higher injury rates than outdoor soccer because of harder surfaces and smaller areas that increase collisions and falls [PAGE 8]. Artificial turf produces different abrasion and joint-loading patterns than natural grass [PAGE 8]. A veto condition: if the playing surface is hard or artificial, pivoting and cutting loads must be reduced or drill selection changed; applying outdoor grass load prescriptions to an indoor hard court is invalid.

Weather layer veto (extended from source’s environmental remarks): The source lists hydration education as a prevention strategy (x̄=4.51, item 6 [PAGE 8]) but does not model thermal stress. Heat and humidity elevate cardiovascular strain and impair neuromuscular control, while cold reduces muscle extensibility. A veto condition: under extreme thermal conditions, high-intensity interval training must be rescheduled or modified; hydration education alone does not veto the physiological risk.

Adjudication rule: The POLC Controlling layer must record surface type, athlete-exposure count, weekly load hours, and injury site per event before any guideline is deemed effective. Without these covariates, the model cannot distinguish a successful intervention from a favorable surface or light schedule. The veto stands until the data layer captures mechanical exposure, not merely administrative compliance.

Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 (load-volume veto). Condition: A team schedules more than five hours of training weekly without scheduled rest days, matching the overuse threshold identified in the source [PAGE 14]. Verifiable test: Over an 8-week competitive block, record athlete-exposures and time-loss injuries. Baseline from source: \lambda = 2.5 \times 10^{-3} injuries per athlete-exposure [PAGE 8]. Prediction: The high-load arm will exhibit \lambda > 2.5 \times 10^{-3}, with overuse injuries concentrated in the lower limb; the counterfactual arm with two rest days per week and periodized load will exhibit \lambda \le 2.5 \times 10^{-3}. Veto trigger: If the high-load arm’s rate exceeds baseline by a statistically meaningful margin while reporting no change in wellness questionnaires, the program is vetoed for load-management failure.

Scenario 2 (neuromuscular omission veto). Condition: A prevention program for 14–16-year-old female players excludes neuromuscular and proprioception training despite the knee’s 33.33% injury share [PAGE 1] and the documented 42% ankle-injury rate in young female players [PAGE 2]. Verifiable test: Randomize teams to (A) POLC documentation only, or (B) POLC plus twice-weekly neuromuscular warm-up consistent with Soligard et al. (2008) [PAGE 14]. Track ACL, MCL, meniscus, and ankle sprain incidence over one season. Prediction: Arm B will show a lower relative risk for knee and ankle injury than Arm A; Arm A’s knee injury proportion will remain near 0.3333 or higher. Veto trigger: If no neuromuscular component is present and knee/ankle injuries exceed 35% of total injuries, the protocol is vetoed as mechanistically incomplete.

Scenario 3 (controlling-latency veto). Condition: The controlling layer reviews injury data only monthly, with no real-time load or soreness monitoring, creating an information-overload and complacency risk flagged by doctors and sports scientists [PAGE 13]. Verifiable test: Compare teams using weekly wearable-load monitoring with 48-hour feedback to teams using monthly injury-log review. Count non-contact injuries from cutting, landing, and sudden direction changes over 12 weeks. Prediction: The real-time monitoring group will record fewer non-contact injuries because load spikes are corrected within the tissue-adaptation window; the monthly-review group will show clustering of non-contact injuries 10–14 days after unobserved load spikes. Veto trigger: If the monthly-review group’s non-contact injury count is statistically higher and the controlling logs show no mid-cycle adjustments, the controlling design is vetoed for excessive feedback latency.
