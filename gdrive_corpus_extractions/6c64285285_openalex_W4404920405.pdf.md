Units: \(z_{ij}\) dimensionless; \(x_{ij}\) retains the variable unit. Assumption: \(s_j>0\) and variables are commensurable only after standardization. The source reports “Standardized z scores” and a “Hierarchical dendrogram and agglomeration coefficients” but does not give the linkage rule or distance metric in the excerpt [PAGE 4].

2. Effect size.   
\[
d=\frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{s_p},\qquad 
s_p=\sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}}.
\]
\(d\) is dimensionless; the confidence interval is in the original variable unit. Assumption: approximately continuous, independent observations. Breakdown regime: \(n_2=8\), unequal variances, and non-normal variables make \(s_p\) unstable; the source used Cohen’s d but classified magnitudes with Rhea’s thresholds [PAGE 4], which is a convention mismatch, not a physical unit.

3. Isokinetic torque, power, work.   
\[
P_{\mathrm{iso}}(\dot\theta)=\tau(\dot\theta)\,\dot\theta,\qquad 
W=\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\tau(\theta)\,d\theta .
\]
Units: \(\tau\) in N·m; \(\dot\theta\) in rad·s\(^{-1}\); \(P\) in W; \(W\) in J. The source used 60, 180, and 300°/s [PAGE 2], i.e. approximately 1.047, 3.142, and 5.236 rad·s\(^{-1}\). Assumption: maximal concentric effort, correct knee-axis alignment, and standardized range of motion. Breakdown regime: \(\dot\theta=0\) gives the isometric limit; very high \(\dot\theta\) increases inertial/artifact error; fatigue across trials invalidates a single torque-velocity curve. The source states “Peak torque-velocity was normalized using the higher MVIC of the knee extensor muscle” [PAGE 3], which formally requires
\[
\tilde\tau=\frac{\tau}{\tau_{\mathrm{MVIC}}},\quad \tilde\tau\ \text{dimensionless},
\]
not N·m/kg. Yet Table 2 reports “Knee torque normalized 60°/s (N.m/kg)” [PAGE 5]. That is a documented unit inconsistency and a breakdown of the reported normalization operator.

4. Vertical-jump mechanics from force plates. A canonical force-plate operator is
\[
v_{\mathrm{to}}=\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{to}}}\left(\frac{F_z(t)}{m}-g\right)dt,\qquad
h=\frac{v_{\mathrm{to}}^2}{2g},\qquad
P_{\mathrm{peak}}=\max_t\left[F_z(t)v_z(t)\right],
\]
\[
P_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}P(t)\,dt .
\]
Units: \(F_z\) N; \(m\) kg; \(v\) m·s\(^{-1}\); \(h\) m; \(P\) W; \(g=9.80665\) m·s\(^{-2}\). Assumptions: rigid-body center-of-mass motion, no arm swing, valid takeoff/landing detection. The source required “hands on the hips” [PAGE 4] and used 200 Hz acquisition with a fourth-order Butterworth 10 Hz low-pass filter [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: filter cut-off too low attenuates peak power; incorrect takeoff detection biases \(v_{\mathrm{to}}\); body-mass changes make absolute and normalized power diverge. The source’s own table shows this divergence: absolute squat-jump peak power 7225 ± 132 W vs 7883 ± 406 W, but normalized 82.3 ± 8.5 W/kg vs 64.9 ± 8.4 W/kg [PAGE 5].

5. Morphological normalization.   
\[
\tilde L_f=\frac{L_f}{L_{\mathrm{femur}}},\qquad
\mathrm{PCSA}=\frac{m_{\mathrm{muscle}}\cos\theta}{\rho\,L_f}.
\]
\(\tilde L_f\) dimensionless; \(L_f\) and \(L_{\mathrm{femur}}\) in m; PCSA in m\(^2\); \(\rho\approx 1060\) kg·m\(^{-3}\) if homogeneous muscle density is assumed; \(\theta\) is pennation angle in radians or degrees with consistent trigonometric use. Breakdown regimes: \(L_f\to 0\) makes PCSA singular; \(\theta\to 90^\circ\) makes \(\cos\theta\to 0\); linear fascicle estimation has “an assumed error of 2-7%” [PAGE 3]. The source reports muscle thickness 2.5 ± 0.2 cm vs 3.0 ± 0.6 cm, p = .036 [PAGE 5], but no significant fascicle-length or pennation-angle difference in the excerpt.

6. Echo intensity and shear-wave elastography. A simple ROI mean is
\[
EI=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p\in\mathrm{ROI}}g_p,
\]
where \(g_p\in[0,255]\) is grayscale arbitrary units; \(EI\) is therefore A.U., not a ratio. The source says it was “corrected by subcutaneous fat tissue thickness” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: ROI includes fascia or fat, gain settings change, and anisotropy/preload invalidate comparisons. SWE stiffness is reported in kPa [PAGE 3]; in a linear isotropic approximation \(E=3\mu\), but the excerpt does not demonstrate that the tissue satisfies isotropy, homogeneity, or controlled preload.

No equation, weight vector, event universe, or unit is printed in the excerpt. Therefore DGI cannot be instantiated as a formal operator here; the reported values such as “Gerard Piqué (central defender, DGI: 48.602)” [PAGE 8] remain an unspecified index. Breakdown regime: absent weights and validation mean home/away and group/playoff rankings cannot be separated from index construction.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when an analyst under small-sample pressure defaults to the abstract’s shorthand: “Cluster 1 showed significantly higher peak power” [PAGE 1], dropping the word “normalized” that appears later [PAGE 4]. The counter-trap is equally wrong: because torque differences disappear after normalization, one might say there are no cluster differences at all. The correct recursive stance is to ask what each agent could know from the printed record. A coach sees cluster 2 as heavier, thicker, and absolutely stronger; a biomechanist sees cluster 1 as higher relative jump power and height; a statistician sees n = 22, two clusters, many tests, and a normalization-unit conflict. The clock layer matters: the lab visit is a single cross-sectional snapshot [PAGE 3], training history is “6.36 ± 4.54 years” [PAGE 2], and the second paper’s clock is 42 matches [PAGE 8]. These clocks are not interchangeable. The source’s forward-looking suggestion—“enhancing lower limb power output would be advantageous for players in skilled positions, while lineman players may derive greater benefits from a training program focused on maximizing strength” [PAGE 6–7]—is a hypothesis generated by the cluster pattern, not a tested longitudinal effect. The counter-trap is to treat that hypothesis as a causal law; the clock veto says a single visit cannot establish a multi-week adaptation trajectory.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Using the source’s own values, \(m_2/m_1=123.3/88.5=1.393\), \(P_{\mathrm{SJ},2}/P_{\mathrm{SJ},1}=7883/7225=1.091\), and \(\tilde P_{\mathrm{SJ},2}/\tilde P_{\mathrm{SJ},1}=64.9/82.3=0.789\); \(1.393\times0.789=1.099\approx1.091\). The same pattern appears in countermovement jump: \(8070/7300=1.105\), \(66.5/83.2=0.799\), and \(1.393\times0.799=1.113\) [PAGE 5].
