To understand why Tolman posited “mere explorativeness” as a genuine drive requiring explanation, we must reconstruct the intellectual lineage leading up to 1932. Historically, behaviorist orthodoxy dominated through Watsonian-Skinnerian paradigms emphasizing conditioned reflexes and operant contingencies. However, several precursor developments created fertile ground for Tolman’s revision:

First, Köhler’s (1925) insights on insight learning in apes demonstrated sudden restructuring of problem-solving strategies—not gradual habit formation—suggesting internal cognitive processes independent of reinforcement schedules. Similarly, Woodworth’s (1918) expectancy theory introduced the idea that organisms anticipate outcomes rather than merely reacting to stimuli.

Second, Pavlovian conditioning research revealed higher-order associations and discrimination learning that couldn’t be reduced to simple contiguity principles. These phenomena required auxiliary constructs like “cue validity” and “predictive value,” opening doors to representationalist interpretations.

Third, Gestalt psychology emphasized holistic perception and figure-ground organization—concepts naturally extendable to spatial cognition. Tolman synthesized these threads by proposing “cognitive maps” [Tolman, 1948], arguing rats mentally encode topological relations among locations, enabling flexible route selection beyond rigid stimulus-response chains.

Within this genealogical context, Tolman’s focus on *goal-directedness* emerges retrocausally: he worked backward from observed adaptivity (e.g., shortcut-taking, detour resolution) to infer underlying purposes (“purposive behavior”). His experimental design targeting elimination sequences was specifically crafted to refute S-R mechanism—which predicted uniform error patterns regardless of incentive salience—and instead demonstrate systematic modulation of search strategy based on motivational state.

Moreover, the historical trap embedded here involves conflating correlation with causation regarding drive reduction. Critics might argue that postprandial lethargy explains reduced exploration, not enhanced intrinsic interest. But Tolman countered this by showing that well-fed rats actively seek novel configurations (Dashiell’s checkerboard), implying energy availability permits—but doesn't necessitate—exploratory engagement. Thus, the causal arrow runs from decreased deficit-state to increased sensitivity to structural affordances, not vice versa.

Longer-term traps include misattributing this effect solely to arousal fluctuations or circadian rhythms. Later studies confirmed that pharmacological manipulation of serotonin/dopamine systems alters exploration-exploitation tradeoffs independently of feeding cycles [Douglas & Robbins, 1992], validating Tolman’s intuition about distinct neurochemical substrates for different types of “demands.”
