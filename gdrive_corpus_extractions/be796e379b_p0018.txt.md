Let's dissect Hull's argument step by step. He begins with a thought experiment: imagine a plastic (moldable) mass of matter with an elevation (hill) and adjacent depression (valley). Gravitational pressure from the hill pushes laterally into the valley, while there's no opposing pressure from the valley side. This imbalance causes flow, eliminating topography. Over time, the mass becomes spherical [PAGE 5]. This is a classic application of hydrostatic equilibrium principles, akin to Pascal's law in fluids. The key insight is that gravity acts isotropically toward the center of mass, so any protrusion increases potential energy, driving relaxation.

Hull then states that the significance lies in the convergence of two independent methods: theoretical deduction and empirical generalization. The theoretical method uses axioms (gravity, plasticity) to deduce "all heavenly bodies... are spherical." The empirical method observes many planets/moons and generalizes the same proposition. He asserts they never genuinely conflict if everything is correct. This is a strong claim about the unity of scientific knowledge.

But let's audit the gray zones. First, the definition of "plastic material." In geophysics, plasticity implies ductile flow over geological time. Not all materials behave plastically under their own gravity; rigid bodies can support topography indefinitely. So the antecedent condition "plasticity" is vague—is it a property of the material itself, or a function of temperature, pressure, and timescale? Second, "sufficient size" is undefined. There's a critical mass where self-gravity overcomes material strength (the "potato radius"). Below this, bodies remain irregular. Third, "homogeneity"—if density varies, equipotential surfaces aren't spheres; they're more complex shapes (Maclaurin spheroids). Rotation also introduces centrifugal effects, flattening poles.

The claim that theory and empirical methods never conflict ignores historical episodes like the precession of Mercury's perihelion, where Newtonian theory predicted one value, but observations showed another. This led to either ad hoc adjustments ( Vulcan hypothesis) or eventually General Relativity. So conflicts do occur, and they drive theory change. Hull's statement might be normative (how science should work) rather than descriptive (how it actually works).

Another layer: "scientific explanations tend to come in clusters constituting a logical hierarchy." This suggests theories are interconnected; changing one condition (e.g., assuming a body is not perfectly homogeneous) affects multiple related propositions. In our example, adding rotation modifies the shape theorem, which then impacts orbital dynamics, tidal interactions, etc. The hierarchy isn't linear but networked.

Thus, while Hull's illustration is elegant, it glosses over the messiness of real-world constraints and the iterative nature of theory refinement. The pressure tensor \(\mathbf{P}\) in a fluid satisfies \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{P} = -\rho \nabla \Phi\) in equilibrium. For an inviscid fluid, \(\mathbf{P} = p \mathbf{I}\), so \(\nabla p = -\rho \nabla \Phi\). This operator describes how pressure gradients balance gravity. Units: \(\nabla p\) in Pa/m, \(\rho\) in kg/m³, \(\nabla \Phi\) in m²/s² per m = m/s².

For a homogeneous incompressible fluid, the minimum occurs when the surface is an equipotential of the combined gravitational and centrifugal potentials (if rotating). Without rotation, \(\Phi = -GM/r\), so equipotentials are spheres.
