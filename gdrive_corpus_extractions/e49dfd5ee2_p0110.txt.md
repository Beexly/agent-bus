<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman's 1932 formulation of "discriminanda and manipulanda" emerges from a fierce historical lineage opposing Watsonian strict behaviorism. In the 1920s, Edward Tolman at Berkeley began running maze experiments with rats, observing that animals could navigate novel paths to food rewards without prior reinforcement on those specific routes. This contradicted the then-dominant view that behavior was a simple chain of stimulus-response associations forged solely by immediate reward [PAGE 91]. Instead, Tolman proposed the existence of *cognitive maps*—internal mental representations that allow for purposive, goal-directed action. The concepts of discriminanda (features perceived to guide action) and manipulanda (physical properties that allow for manipulation) are central to this map, representing the dual components of an organism's interaction with its environment: the information gathered and the physical capabilities employed.

This genealogy is fraught with multi-week traps of reductionism. The dominant paradigm of the era trapped researchers in viewing behavior as purely mechanistic, ignoring the *purposeful* nature observed in these experiments. Tolman's introduction of "expectational relationships" and "capacities" explicitly argued against this trap, suggesting that organisms do not just react but anticipate based on internal models. A modern parallel can be drawn to DeepMind's 2018 research on model-based reinforcement learning, where agents learn world models to plan actions, echoing Tolman's "cognitive map." However, the historical trap remains: over-emphasizing either pure cognition or pure mechanism, neglecting the embodied, situated nature of intelligence that Tolman's behavioral framework implicitly acknowledges through his focus on capacities like differentiation and expectation. The genealogical insight is that any analysis of adaptive behavior must account for both the informational structure of the environment and the organism's physical ability to interact with it, avoiding a purely symbolic or purely reflexive view.

The primary operator is the **Expectational Mapping Operator**, \(\mathcal{E}\), which maps a set of discriminanda \(D\) to a probability distribution over manipulanda \(M\), conditioned on the current goal state \(G\) and the organism’s history \(H\):
\[
P(M | D, G, H) = \mathcal{E}(D, G, H)
\]
Here, \(D\) represents the measured sensory features (e.g., shape, color), and \(M\) represents actionable properties (e.g., grippability, rigidity). The units for \(D\) and \(M\) are domain-specific (e.g., RGB values, Newtons of force), while the output of \(\mathcal{E}\) is dimensionless (probability).

The **Behavior-Support Operator** \(\mathcal{B}\) then maps a chosen manipulum \(m \in M\) to an action trajectory \(A\), also dependent on the organism’s motor capacity \(\mathcal{C}\):
\[
A = \mathcal{B}(m, \mathcal{C})
\]
\(\mathcal{C}\) embodies the second capacity mentioned: the physical ability to execute the manipulation. Its breakdown occurs when \(\mathcal{C}\) is insufficient for \(m\), leading to action failure despite correct expectation.

**Assumptions:** 1) The mapping \(\mathcal{E}\) is learned through experience, forming the "cognitive map." 2) The organism has a stable goal state \(G\) (e.g., hunger driving food-seeking). 3) Discriminanda and manipulanda are co-indexed in memory, allowing for transitive inference (if A looks like B, and B is pick-up-able, then A might be too).

**Breakdown Regimes:** The model fails under several conditions. First, **Sensory Overload**: When \(|D|\) exceeds the processing capacity, differentiation fails. Second, **Capacity Deficit**: If \(\mathcal{C}\) is damaged (e.g., paralysis), \(\mathcal{B}\) cannot produce \(A\). Third, **Expectational Corruption**: If \(\mathcal{E}\) learns spurious correlations (e.g., due to misleading cues), it will assign high probability to ineffective manipulanda. Finally, **Goal Instability**: If \(G\) fluctuates rapidly, the mapping \(\mathcal{E}\) becomes useless. These breakdowns manifest as hesitation, error, or perseveration in behavior.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
An agent operating under Tolman’s framework exhibits a recursive Theory of Mind regarding its own interaction with the world. It doesn't just perceive objects; it perceives them *for* certain actions. When faced with a novel object, the agent simulates potential interactions based on stored expectational relationships. For example, seeing a new utensil, it might simulate gripping it (testing the "pick-up-able-ness") before using it to eat. This simulation is a mini-theory of mind: "If I grasp this, what will happen?" The agent predicts the outcome (success/failure) and the resulting state change.

Crisis habit regression occurs when these simulations fail repeatedly. Imagine a robot whose gripper strength is suddenly reduced. Its previously reliable expectational link between "object size" and "grippability" becomes invalid. Instead of updating its model, it might regress to simpler, less efficient habits (e.g., pushing objects instead of lifting), abandoning the richer cognitive map. This is a failure to re-cognize the environment correctly.

Counter-traps arise when the environment exploits these expectational relationships. In a psychological experiment, a lever that previously delivered food now delivers a shock. The agent’s expectation ("lever press → food") is violated. A counter-trap would be an adaptive response that goes beyond simple avoidance: perhaps the agent starts inspecting the lever closely before pressing (enhancing discrimination) or seeks alternative ways to get food (using other manipulanda). This shows higher-order flexibility.

The "clock" element is the time available for these simulations and updates. In a time-critical situation (e.g., catching a falling object), the agent cannot afford detailed simulation; it relies on fast, low-level discriminanda-manipulanda mappings (reflexes). In a relaxed setting, it can engage in prolonged deliberation, testing multiple hypotheses about an object’s affordances. The balance between clock-constrained reflexes and open-ended deliberation defines the agent’s behavioral repertoire and its vulnerability to traps.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even with perfect expectational relationships, behavior can be vetoed by factors outside the cognitive map. This adjudication happens across three layers.

First, the **Physical/Biomechanical Layer**. An organism might correctly expect a branch to be climbable, but if it has a broken leg, the action is physically impossible. The veto here is absolute: the motor command cannot be executed. Similarly, a tool might be within reach (perceived as a manipulum), but if the arm is locked, no manipulation occurs.

Second, the **Contextual/Environmental Layer**. Consider a bird that expects a worm to be edible (a strong expectational relationship). But if a predator is nearby, the risk of foraging outweighs the reward. The context (predator presence) vetoes the consumption action, overriding the internal expectation. This is a dynamic veto based on competing goals.

Third, the **Weather/Physiological Layer**. Extreme cold might make even a familiar task (like turning a doorknob) impossible because fingers are numb. The physiological state directly impairs the capacity to interact with the manipulandum. Another example: fatigue can blur fine visual distinctions, causing discriminanda to merge, thus breaking the first capacity (differentiation).

These vetoes prove that Tolman’s model, while comprehensive for intentional behavior, operates within a larger system. The cognitive map provides the "script," but execution requires a viable body and a permissive environment. A complete analysis must therefore include these veto pathways, especially when diagnosing failures that occur despite seemingly correct perceptions and expectations.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
