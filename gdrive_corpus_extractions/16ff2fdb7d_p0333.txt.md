Performance under crisis is therefore determined by the maximum sER across the habit hierarchy, not by the most reinforced response per se. If the crisis response is poorly reinforced, p collapses once D normalizes, revealing regression to a lower stratum of the hierarchy.

The counter-trap is inferential: an observer who witnesses successful crisis performance may conclude that the relevant habit is strongly learned (high sHr). The counter-trap is that the success may be entirely due to transient D elevation, with sHr remaining low. Once drive returns to baseline, p falls back below sL, and the supposed “crisis learning” vanishes. The analyst must therefore disentangle drive gain from habit gain before assigning credit to reinforcement history.

The clock enters because the analysis assumes n is a sufficient statistic and that D is constant [PAGE 326]. In recursive theory of mind, the subject implicitly models its own drive state—time since last meal, circadian phase, fatigue—and selects responses accordingly. The observer must model the subject’s model of drive. A bar-press trained during a morning crisis may fail in the evening not because sHr decayed but because D crossed below the effective threshold, shifting sER below sL. Crisis prediction thus requires a joint state (sHr, D, clock phase, oscillation phase), not a univariate reinforcement count. Failure to recurse into the subject’s drive-state model produces systematic overprediction of p in off-peak hours and underprediction during peak arousal.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three orthogonal veto layers operate downstream of neural reaction potential and can render p(neural) unobservable in behavior.

Physical veto (musculoskeletal capability): The bar-press is all-or-none only at the response level; its execution requires lever displacement Δx and minimum force F_min. Let C ∈ {0,1} denote physical capability. The observed response R satisfies R = 1 only if neural evocation E = 1 AND C = 1. If fatigue, injury, or sedation sets C = 0, then p_obs = 0 even when p_neural = Pr[sER + ξ > sL] is high. This veto is independent of sER and of n; it is a peripheral hard constraint.

Biomechanical veto (effectors threshold): All-or-none responses possess a biomechanical activation threshold τ_bio. A neural command may exceed sL yet fail to generate sufficient motor-unit recruitment to overcome τ_bio (e.g., due to muscle atrophy or pharmacologic weakness). Formally:
\[ p_{\text{obs}} = p(s_{ER}) \cdot \sigma(F_{\text{muscle}} - F_{\min}) \]
where σ is a step or steep sigmoid. If F_muscle < F_min, the response is vetoed regardless of how far sER exceeds sL.

Weather veto (ambient modulation of drive and threshold): Hull’s derivation requires “drive remains constant” [PAGE 326]. Ambient temperature T alters thermoregulatory drive D(T) and can shift sL (cold increases general arousal; heat depresses motor output). A cold session may elevate D and p without any change in n; a hot session may depress D and sER despite high reinforcement history. Because T is exogenous to the reinforcement count, it violates ceteris paribus and makes p non-identifiable with respect to n unless T is held constant or modeled as a covariate.

Adjudication rule: These vetoes are multiplicative gain factors on the neural evocation probability. A valid test of Hull’s page-326 architecture must either enforce C = 1, F_muscle > F_min, and constant T, or explicitly estimate them as nuisance parameters. Otherwise, observed changes in p cannot be assigned to habit strength, drive, or threshold with any statistical confidence; the neural equation is under-identified by behavior alone.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
In an RLVR framing, reinforcements are verifiable rewards and the learner’s policy update is mediated by sER. Below are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a falsifiable prediction.

Scenario 1 — Drive-decoupling veto.
Setup: Train a lever-press response to a stable asymptote at n = N. Hold n fixed at N and independently reduce drive D via pre-session satiety, verifying D reduction with a consummatory measure (e.g., latency to eat free food).
Prediction under Hull: Because sER = κ·sHr(N)·D, lowering D scales sER downward. If the system operates far above threshold, p should decline approximately proportionally to D; if near threshold, p should collapse to the chance floor Pr[ξ > sL].
Veto condition: If measured p remains statistically unchanged despite a verified reduction in D, the multiplicative drive × habit assumption on p. 242 [PAGE 326] is falsified for this response.

Scenario 2 — Threshold-oscillation singularity veto.
Setup: Titrate conditioned-stimulus intensity in a pilot phase so that estimated sER ≈ sL for a fixed n and D. During the test, hold n and D constant across trials and record trial-by-trial evocation.
Prediction under Hull + oscillation: Near the singularity |sER − sL| ≤ scale(ξ), the oscillation component dominates [PAGE 326], so p should hover near 0.5 with high trial-to-trial variance and should not track n smoothly. A deterministic linear or smooth growth fit should fail.
Veto condition: If p instead increases smoothly and deterministically with n at this intensity, either the oscillation component is negligible or the threshold model is wrong; the page-326 all-or-none account is vetoed.

Scenario 3 — Modality-growth veto (graded versus all-or-none).
Setup: Use the same subject, same n, and same verified D for two responses: salivary secretion (graded) and bar-press (all-or-none, “Perin’s rats” [PAGE 326]).
Prediction under Hull: Salivary amplitude should show near-linear growth A_sal = α·sER(n) + ε [PAGE 326], whereas bar-press probability should follow a sigmoidal threshold function p_bar = 1 − Fξ(sL − sER(n)). Plotting performance versus n should therefore yield a roughly straight line for saliva and an S-shaped cumulative curve for the bar-press.
Veto condition: If both modalities show identical linear amplitude scaling with n, or if the bar-press exhibits graded trial-by-trial amplitude variation rather than binary evocation, the all-or-none threshold distinction asserted on p. 326 is invalid for this organism, and the probability model must be replaced by a graded amplitude model.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
