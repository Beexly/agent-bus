<THINK_TRACE>
I read the paper as a cascade of geometric and photometric vetoes rather than as a single detector. Multi-hop trace: first, court lines are found and reconstructed into a court model [PAGE 2]; the paper states that “some court lines often disappear during the zooming and panning of video camera” [PAGE 2]. Second, that model defines initial upper and lower court search areas using image-space coordinates \(X_{Pi},Y_{Pi}\) in pixels [PAGE 3]. Third, after a player is detected, its centroid is mapped from image space into real court coordinates by perspective transform; then a maximum real-world displacement is computed from “the speed of a player is around 2 - 7 meters per second” and “we use the maximum speed of 7 meters divided by the frame rate as the definition of the search window” [PAGE 4]. Fourth, the ROI is passed through field-specific non-dominant color extraction in HSV and a Sobel edge path; the paper explicitly says “some parts of player body are lost in non-dominant color extraction but preserved in edge map, and vice versa” [PAGE 6]. Fifth, refinement removes court lines, OR-combines horizontal and vertical edge maps, OR-combines the NDC map with the edge map, and applies cast-shadow removal [PAGE 8]. Sixth, the refined centroid becomes the next state, closing the loop.

The gray-zone audit turns on several unstated assumptions. The homography is assumed valid and nonsingular, but a planar court homography has a horizon singularity where the denominator of the projective division goes to zero; near that line, image-to-real and real-to-image mapping blow up. The speed bound assumes a fixed frame rate and a camera that is not cutting or zooming discontinuously; a broadcast cut invalidates the “same physical player” displacement constraint. The NDC equation compares a channel deviation to \(\alpha\sigma^2\), which is dimensionally suspicious if \(\sigma^2\) is a variance and \(P-\mu\) is a deviation: unless channels are normalized and the paper is using \(\sigma^2\) loosely for a scale, the threshold has squared-channel units on one side and linear-channel units on the other. The transcribed \(\alpha = 0.5 * \beta + \sigma_H^2/\sigma_H^2\) is even more fragile: read literally it collapses to \(0.5\beta+1\) and cancels the variance ratio, so either the OCR lost a denominator or the paper’s notation is nonstandard. The shadow rule in Eq. (2) has a similar mixed-units issue: \(-\alpha\sigma_H^2 \le p_H-\mu_H < 1/6\) mixes a variance-scaled lower bound with a fixed hue quantization step. The paper gives no numerical precision, recall, IoU, centroid-error distribution, or confusion matrix; it says only “promising results” [PAGE 1], “Generally the automatic tracking results are close to manual results” and “there are still a few mismatch errors” [PAGE 10-11]. That is a qualitative audit, not a calibrated one.

A second gray zone is operator interaction. Court-line removal can help the edge map, but page 11 says “players white clothes, sometimes mixing with court lines, are incorrectly removed and lead to misjudge of players centroid.” Thus the same photometric operator that suppresses court-line false positives can suppress the player. Edge fusion can compensate for NDC loss, but it also preserves court lines and shadows; the success of the cascade depends on ordering: remove court lines before OR-combining edge maps, then remove cast shadows only on edge pixels [PAGE 8]. If the order is reversed, the shadow and line pixels enter the player mask and corrupt the centroid. The adaptive window is also a feedback trap: a bad centroid produces a bad next ROI, which produces a bad next centroid. The paper’s counter is the court model and speed bound, not a probabilistic filter. No Kalman filter, particle filter, or learned appearance model is specified. The system is deterministic and hand-built, with \(\alpha\) as the main photometric adaptation knob. That makes the method interpretable but brittle under unmodeled weather, clothing, and camera changes.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s operators are geometric, photometric, and morphological. Units are mostly pixels for image coordinates, meters and seconds for real-court motion, and dimensionless or channel-dependent units for HSV thresholds. The paper gives no calibrated probability distribution; NDC and edge maps are Boolean fields.

1. Initial search areas from the court model. Let image width and height be \(W,H\) in pixels, and let \(P_i=(X_{Pi},Y_{Pi})\) be court-model points in image pixels. The paper defines upper-court search intervals as [PAGE 3]:
\[
U_L=\left[X_{P4}-\frac{1}{2}(X_{P4}-X_{P1})\right]\ \text{or }0,\qquad
U_R=\left[X_{P20}+\frac{1}{2}(X_{17}-X_{P20})\right]\ \text{or }W,
\]
\[
U_T=\left[\max(Y_{P4},Y_{P20})-\frac{2}{3}\max(Y_{P4},Y_{P20})\right],\qquad
U_B=[Y_{PC}],
\]
and lower-court intervals as [PAGE 3]:
\[
L_L=\left[X_{P1}-\frac{2}{3}X_{P1}\right]\ \text{or }0,\qquad
L_R=\left[X_{P17}-\frac{2}{3}(W-X_{P17})\right]\ \text{or }W,
\]
\[
L_T=[Y_{PC}],\qquad
L_B=\left[\max(Y_{P1},Y_{P17})-\frac{2}{3}(H-\max(Y_{P1},Y_{P17}))\right].
\]
Assumption: the court model is reconstructed well enough to supply \(P_1,P_4,P_{17},P_{20},P_C\). Breakdown: missing or misordered court lines make the intervals wrong; the fallback “or zero” and “or image width” is a heuristic, not a geometric proof.

2. Perspective transform and speed-derived adaptive window. Let \(\pi:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\) be the image-to-real homography. With homogeneous image point \(\tilde{\mathbf u}=(u,v,1)^T\) and real point \(\tilde{\mathbf r}=(r_x,r_y,1)^T\), \(\tilde{\mathbf r}\sim H_c\tilde{\mathbf u}\). The inverse mapping is singular on the horizon line \(h_{31}u+h_{32}v+h_{33}=0\). For frame rate \(f_s\) in frames per second and maximum speed \(v_{\max}=7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the per-frame real displacement is
\[
d_{\max}=\frac{v_{\max}}{f_s}\quad [\mathrm{m\,frame^{-1}}].
\]
If \(f_s=30\ \mathrm{fps}\), then \(d_{\max}=7/30\approx0.233\ \mathrm{m\,frame^{-1}}\). The paper says “we use the maximum speed of 7 meters divided by the frame rate as the definition of the search window” [PAGE 4]. With cardinal displacements \(D=\{(\pm d_{\max},0),(0,\pm d_{\max})\}\), the search-window union can be written as
\[
S_t=\bigcup_{\delta\in D}\operatorname{BBox}\left(\pi^{-1}(r_t+\delta)\right).
\]
The paper’s five-step procedure is: detect centroid, map centroid to real court, compute four possible displaced locations, map them back to image space, and use the minimum bounding boxes to obtain the new search window [PAGE 4]. Assumptions: fixed frame rate, continuous camera, planar court, and player speed at or below \(7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Breakdown: \(f_s\to0\) is undefined; a camera cut or zoom violates the homography; a player exceeding the speed bound leaves the search window; near the horizon the inverse homography is numerically unstable.

3. Non-dominant color extraction. For a selected court field, the paper computes channel mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) and defines [PAGE 6]:
\[
\mathrm{NDC}(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
1, & \|P_H-\mu_H\|>\alpha\sigma_H^2\ \text{or}\ \|P_V-\mu_V\|>\alpha\sigma_V^2,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Here \(P_H\) and \(P_V\) are pixel values in hue and value channels. The paper says “we select hue and value channels from HSV color space to detect non-dominant color pixels” [PAGE 5], and “Parameter \(\alpha\) is an adjustable parameter which is varied with the court conditions such as different courts or different lights of the same court” [PAGE 5]. The transcribed calibration is
\[
\alpha=0.5*\beta+\frac{\sigma_H^2}{\sigma_H^2},
\]
with “we quantize H into 6 dominant colors, so \(\beta\) is \(0.5 * 1/6 = 1/12\)” [PAGE 6]. Literal reading gives \(\alpha=0.5\beta+1\), which is not field-adaptive unless the ratio is an OCR error or the denominator is a different variance scale. Units: if H and V are normalized to \([0,1]\), then \(P-\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are dimensionless; if H is in degrees and V is normalized, the threshold is mixed-unit. Breakdown: \(\sigma^2=0\) makes the threshold zero and flags every non-mean pixel; if the intended denominator is \(\sigma_H^2\), then \(\sigma_H^2=0\) creates a \(0/0\) singularity.

4. Edge path. The paper applies a Sobel filter to produce horizontal and vertical edge images, binarizes each “by using \(\mu \pm \sigma\) as threshold,” and performs “1/2 sub-sampling to decrease edge width” [PAGE 6]. No exact binarization formula is printed. The operator is deterministic, with binary output in \(\{0,1\}^{W\times H}\). The natural information unit of a binary pixel is at most 1 bit under an equiprobable coding convention, but the paper reports no entropy or probability calibration. Assumption: edges from player body and court lines are separable after court-line removal. Breakdown: high-contrast court lines, shadows, and white clothing all survive edge detection.

5. Refinement and shadow removal. The refinement flow is: remove court lines, combine horizontal and vertical edge maps by OR and morphological Closing, label connected components to remove noise, combine the NDC binary image and the new edge map by OR, then remove cast shadow [PAGE 8]. The shadow rule is applied “to the edge pixels” and subtracted from the edge map [PAGE 8]:
\[
-\alpha\sigma_H^2\le p_H-\mu_H<\frac{1}{6},
\]
\[
p_S-\mu_S\ge-\alpha\sigma_S^2,
\]
\[
p_V-\mu_V\le-\alpha\sigma_V^2.
\]
The paper’s physical claim is: “The color of shadow is gray or black, which has high saturation (S), and low value (V) in HSV color space. In addition, the hue (H) value is greater than that of the court color” [PAGE 8]. The conjunction of these inequalities is the natural veto condition; if read as independent votes, over-removal risk rises. Breakdown: self-shadows are deliberately not handled; the paper says “Removing self shadow is error prone and frequently eliminates parts of player body as well” [PAGE 8]. White clothing can satisfy shadow-like conditions after court-line removal, as page 11 reports. Weather and lighting can shift \(p_S,p_V,p_H\) enough that the fixed \(1/6\) hue bound or variance-scaled bounds misclassify body pixels.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is visible in its own critique of prior sports-video work. Early temporal methods used “frame difference” and morphology; the paper says they are “simple and fast, but easily affected by spectator movement or camera view change” [PAGE 1]. Background subtraction then built “empty court image, mean of continuous frames, and mean of dominant color” models [PAGE 1]; the paper rejects the empty-court model because “The empty court image is hard to retrieve and thus unrealistic” [PAGE 1]. Dominant-color methods are computationally simple and robust across perspectives, but the paper says “dominant color selection and range determination are still open problems requiring more effort” [PAGE 2]. Han et al.’s RGB non-dominant color method is the direct predecessor; the paper argues that using “the average color of full court as dominant color” may deviate from “the color in players neighbor” [PAGE 5]. The corrective is field-specific mean and variance over inner and outer court regions, with HSV hue and value channels. Court-line detection and reconstruction come from the authors’ prior work [12], and shadow terminology comes from Prati et al. [13]. Edge detection is classical Sobel plus morphological OR/Closing.

Read retrocausally, the later requirement of a “complete player figure” explains earlier design choices: the paper says existing bounding boxes “do not provide a close fit to a player’s body” [PAGE 2], and high-level action recognition “still demand[s] a best fit window and a complete body” [PAGE 2]. That downstream requirement forces the adaptive window, the edge compensation, and the shadow removal. The genealogy is not a straight progress line; it is a sequence of failure-driven patches. Temporal differencing fails under camera change, so background subtraction is introduced; background subtraction fails under viewpoint change, so dominant color is introduced; global dominant color fails under local lighting, so field-specific HSV NDC is introduced; NDC loses body parts, so edge fusion is introduced; edge fusion preserves shadows, so cast-shadow removal is introduced.

The multi-week traps are drift traps. Over weeks, court paint, grass color, clay moisture, and stadium lighting change, so a fixed \(\alpha\) or a field mean learned from one broadcast is not stable. Player kits change, and white clothing can merge with court lines; page 11 explicitly reports “players white clothes, sometimes mixing with court lines, are incorrectly removed” [PAGE 11]. Broadcast camera packages change, so homography and zoom behavior change. Shadow direction and length change with sun angle and roof closure; Eq. (2) is a photometric rule, not a physical shadow model. A multi-week deployment would need recalibration of \(\alpha\), field masks, and court-line confidence, or the retrocausal chain collapses back to the very background-subtraction and dominant-color assumptions the paper criticizes.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Operationally, the system embeds a designer’s model of three agents: the player, the camera operator, and the color environment. The player model is a speed-bounded, deformable body: “the speed of a player is around 2 - 7 meters per second” [PAGE 4], but the body is non-rigid and can swing or serve. The camera model is a perspective projection that may zoom and pan; court lines may disappear [PAGE 2]. The color model is field-specific: court regions differ under light and shadow [PAGE 5]. The recursive loop is: court model predicts ROI; ROI detector predicts player mask; mask centroid predicts next ROI. If the detector is wrong, the next prediction can lock onto the error. That is the counter-trap: a too-small window loses body parts, while a too-large window “contain[s] too much noise and redundant information” [PAGE 8]. The paper’s own counter-trap is the edge map, because “some parts of player body are lost in non-dominant color extraction but preserved in edge map, and vice versa” [PAGE 6]. The edge channel acts as a second opinion against the color channel.

Crisis habit regression appears when the scene leaves the training envelope. Under a new court color, the NDC threshold may regress to a global dominant-color habit: it treats the local field mean as if it were the whole court. Under a camera cut, the speed prior regresses to a false continuity assumption: the system still expects the player within \(7/f_s\) meters per frame, but the image coordinate frame has changed. Under a shadow or white-clothing crisis, the refinement habit regresses to “remove anything court-line-like or shadow-like,” which can remove the player. The clock is \(f_s\): it converts meters per second into meters per frame and sets the search window. If the clock is wrong, or if the broadcast frame rate changes, the physical prior is mis-scaled. The recursive theory-of-mind lesson is that the algorithm’s expectations are only as good as its model of the other agents. It assumes a cooperative camera, a bounded player, and a locally stationary court color. When those assumptions fail, the loop does not become more cautious; it becomes more confident in a wrong ROI unless an external veto interrupts it.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is conditional, not empirical. Physical-geometric veto: if a detected centroid’s real-court displacement between consecutive frames exceeds \(d_{\max}=v_{\max}/f_s\), with \(v_{\max}=7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), then under the paper’s own speed model the detection cannot be the same player. The paper states “we use the maximum speed of 7 meters divided by the frame rate as the definition of the search window” [PAGE 4]. This veto is sound only if the homography is valid and the frame rate is known. Near the horizon \(h_{31}u+h_{32}v+h_{33}=0\), the inverse mapping is unstable, so the geometric veto must be suspended or replaced by court-line-confidence gating. If court lines are missing or reconstructed poorly, the homography is not trustworthy; page 2 says “some court lines often disappear during the zooming and panning of video camera” [PAGE 2].

Biomechanical veto: the player is deformable, so a rigid bounding-box prior is not sufficient. The paper criticizes existing windows that “do not provide a close fit to a player’s body” [PAGE 2] and demands a “complete body” for action recognition [PAGE 2]. A biomechanical veto can reject a refined mask that has lost a limb or torso segment: if the edge map has a continuous body contour but the NDC-plus-shadow mask has a hole, the hole is likely a color or shadow false negative. Conversely, if the edge map contains a long straight segment aligned with a court line, the court-line removal layer should veto that edge component before fusion. The page 11 failure mode is exactly this: white clothes mixing with court lines are removed, which “lead[s] to misjudge of players centroid” [PAGE 11]. That is a biomechanical-photometric conflict, and the safer adjudication is to prefer edge continuity over line-removal aggressiveness when the two conflict.

Weather and photometric veto: white court-line intensity changes with “weather, camera angle, different courts” [PAGE 2], so the court-line detector needs adaptive thresholding; the paper says an “adaptive threshold scheme is presented for adjusting color value of court lines” [PAGE 2]. The shadow layer has a physical color signature: “gray or black, which has high saturation (S), and low value (V)” and hue “greater than that of the court color” [PAGE 8]. Eq. (2) is the operational veto: subtract edge pixels only when the H, S, and V inequalities jointly indicate cast shadow. If a body pixel also satisfies them, the shadow veto over-removes; the paper’s own self-shadow caveat and white-clothing failure show the limit. Weather can change saturation and value through wetness, glare, and stadium lighting, so the veto should be gated by a court-color reference and a body-continuity check. The honest adjudication is: physical geometry has the strongest veto power when homography is stable; biomechanical continuity has the strongest veto power when color is ambiguous; weather/photometric rules are the weakest and should be treated as proposals, not final delete operations.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I define an RLVR-style verifier as a reproducible frame-level check against a counterfactual ablation. The reward is not a betting quantity; it is a detection or tracking score computed from manual masks, court model, and centroid traces. Three verifiable veto scenarios follow.

Scenario 1: Remove the adaptive search window and use only the initial fixed upper/lower court window. Verifier: compare automatic centroid to manual centroid and compute silhouette IoU against manual player masks on the same 12-video set. Prediction: the fixed window will include more spectators, court lines, and shadows, so precision falls and centroid variance rises; the paper’s own claim is that larger windows “contain too much noise and redundant information” [PAGE 8]. Veto prediction: if the ablation lowers IoU or raises centroid error beyond a pre-registered tolerance, the adaptive-window operator is causally necessary. A second prediction is that the failure will be worst during zoom and pan, because the initial window is not re-derived from the current homography.

Scenario 2: Replace field-specific HSV non-dominant color extraction with a single full-court RGB mean, the predecessor logic the paper criticizes [PAGE 5]. Verifier: measure player-pixel recall and background false-positive rate in inner versus outer court regions, and count mask fragments after edge fusion. Prediction: a global mean will bias the threshold on the field farthest in color from the global average; the paper says using “the average color of full court as dominant color” may have “large deviation with the color in players neighbor” [PAGE 5]. Veto prediction: if recall drops mainly in the outer field or under unequal lighting, the field-specific mean and variance are causally necessary. The edge path may partially compensate, so the verifier should report residual recall after fusion, not NDC recall alone.

Scenario 3: Disable cast-shadow removal Eq. (2) while keeping court-line removal and edge fusion. Verifier: compare refined silhouette area and centroid against manual masks for frames with strong cast shadows; compute area inflation and centroid shift vector. Prediction: shadow pixels attached to the edge map will enlarge the mask and pull the centroid toward the shadow, especially when the shadow is adjacent to the feet. The paper says “The merged result may contain shadows, thus we propose a new shadow removal technique” [PAGE 8]. Veto prediction: if area inflation and centroid shift are significantly larger in shadow frames than in non-shadow frames, the shadow operator is causally necessary. A complementary prediction is that the ablation will not fix the white-clothing failure on page 11, because that failure is caused by court-line removal and color ambiguity, not by cast-shadow removal. Thus the three counterfactuals separate geometric windowing, photometric field adaptation, and shadow refinement rather than treating the whole cascade as one opaque unit.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
