<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**1. In-game win-probability logistic model.** Let \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether the team eventually wins, and let \(x_i=(\text{Minutes Elapsed}_i,\text{Red Kills}_i,\text{Blue Kills}_i,\text{Red Towers}_i,\text{Blue Towers}_i,\text{Red Monsters}_i,\text{Blue Monsters}_i)\). The standard logistic form is
\[
\pi_i=P(Y_i=1\mid x_i)=\sigma(\beta^\top x_i)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta^\top x_i}},
\qquad
\operatorname{logit}(\pi_i)=\log\frac{\pi_i}{1-\pi_i}=\beta^\top x_i.
\]
Units: \(\pi_i\) is dimensionless probability; minutes are minutes; kills, towers, and monsters are counts; coefficients are on the logit scale, i.e., change in \(\log(\pi/(1-\pi))\) per one minute or per one count. The paper says it models “the log probabilities” with a linear combination of features weighted by coefficients [PAGE 18]. Training by stochastic gradient descent minimizes
\[
\mathcal{L}(\beta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[
y_i\log \sigma(\beta^\top x_i)
+(1-y_i)\log\bigl(1-\sigma(\beta^\top x_i)\bigr)
\right].
\]
Units: natural-log cross-entropy in nats per observation. Assumption: rows are treated as independent after sampling one random minute from each game; “we chose a random minute from each game, leaving us with millions of independent rows” [PAGE 18]. Breakdown regimes: perfect separation drives \(\|\beta\|\to\infty\); saturation \(\pi_i\to0\) or \(1\) makes event-level \(\Delta\pi\) numerically unstable; minute-granular features cannot resolve second-level simultaneity; patch changes violate the fixed-coefficient assumption.

**2. Full-game percentile mapping.** For player \(i\) in tier \(T\) and metric \(m\), let
\[
p_i(m)=100\cdot \frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{j\in T}\mathbf{1}\{m_j\le m_i\}
\]
be the tier-conditional percentile, in percent. The paper replaces the raw kills number by its percentile: “This kills number will be replaced by its percentile” [PAGE 18]. Then the smoothed win relation is
\[
\widehat{W}(\pi)=\Pr(Y=1\mid \lfloor p_i(m)\rfloor=\pi),
\]
estimated by averaging win/loss within each integer percentile bin [PAGE 18]. Units: \(\pi\in[0,100]\), \(\widehat{W}\in[0,1]\). Breakdown: ties create mass points; extreme percentiles have small \(n\); positive skewness means the right tail is sparse [PAGE 19]; percentile rank is tier-relative and not an absolute skill unit.

**3. Smart kill / worthless death event operator.** For team \(T\), event \(e\) at time \(t\), let \(\pi_T(t^-)\) be the team’s estimated win probability immediately before the event and \(\pi_T(t^+)\) immediately after. Define
\[
\Delta\pi_T(e)=\pi_T(t^+)-\pi_T(t^-).
\]
A death is a worthless death for \(T\) if \(\Delta\pi_T(e)\le 0\); a kill is a smart kill for \(T\) if \(\Delta\pi_T(e)>0\). This follows the paper’s definition: “Worthless deaths are ones that do not increase your team’s win probability. Smart kills are ones that do increase your team’s win probability” [PAGE 13]. Units: dimensionless probability change. Breakdown: if multiple events occur in the same minute, \(\Delta\pi\) is not a clean single-event attribution; if \(\pi\) is saturated, \(\Delta\pi\approx0\) even for strategically important actions; the model’s minute resolution limits second-level causal separation.

**4. Engagement and team-fight operator.** Let champion-on-champion damage events occur at times \(t_1<\cdots<t_k\). An engagement is a maximal sequence with
\[
t_{r+1}-t_r\le 5\ \text{s}
\]
for consecutive damage events; the engagement terminates when the gap exceeds \(5\) s. This is exactly the paper’s rule: “an engagement is a continuous sequence of champion-on-champion damage until 5 s elapse with no damage” [PAGE 19]. A champion is included in the team fight if it is “within experience range of any champion already deemed to be in the engagement” [PAGE 19]. Units: seconds for the gap; map-distance units for XP range. Breakdown: exactly \(5\) s is a knife-edge; 2 s fragments fights, 10 s merges separate fights; XP-range membership is not numerically specified in the source; champion overlap and stealth create missing-location errors.

**5. Tilt proxy.** Let \(D_i(t-180,t)\) be player \(i\)’s time spent dead over the preceding 3 minutes and \(D_T(t-180,t)\) the same for the team. The proxy is
\[
\tau_i(t)=\frac{D_i(t-180,t)}{D_T(t-180,t)},
\]
with the convention \(0.2\) when both numerator and denominator are zero [PAGE 20]. Units: dimensionless. The 3-min window is justified because “death timers range up to around 60 s, and it takes approximately 30 s to walk back to the middle of the map” [PAGE 20]. Breakdown: denominator zero forces an arbitrary convention; the proxy measures recent death share, not emotional state; it cannot distinguish intentional sacrifice from tilt.

**6. Role-detection pseudocode.** The source’s role classifier uses early minions killed between level two and level six; the jungler is the champion with the most neutral-jungle early minions; the support has the fewest overall early minions; the mid has the most early minions near map center; the ADC is most frequently near the support in the first 10 minutes, where “near” is within one-tenth of map width or height; the remaining champion is top [PAGE 26]. Breakdown: Riot’s own roles are sometimes wrong, and the paper says “The Riot API reports what Riot assumes are the roles of each person. Their results are sometimes incorrect” [PAGE 26]. The pseudocode is a patch- and meta-dependent heuristic, not an invariant class label.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit. The paper descends from traditional-sport win-probability and optical-tracking work: Cervone et al. on NBA optical tracking, Lock and Nettleton on NFL win probability, and Duch et al. on individual performance in soccer [PAGE 14]. In MOBA research it descends from Dota2 encounter detection and win prediction, including Schubert, Drachen, and Mahlmann’s encounter process and Hodge et al.’s in-game Dota2 win-probability models [PAGE 14]. It also inherits the fast-and-frugal-tree tradition through Phillips’s FFTrees implementation [PAGE 23]. The statistical backbone is McFadden’s conditional logit and likelihood-ratio pseudo-\(R^2\); Maymin reports a McFadden pseudo-\(R^2\) of 0.42, calling it an excellent fit [PAGE 22]. The data lineage is Riot’s public API, spectator client, OpenCV minimap processing, and direct observer-client hooks; the author notes that scripting software had been used by players to cheat, but his hooks were into the observer client, not a player client [PAGE 16]. The open-source release is part of the genealogy: “All of the code is open sourced” [PAGE 24].

The retrocausal move is the reclassification of past actions by forward-looking win probability. A kill is no longer just a kill; it is a kill whose estimated \(\pi\) moved upward. A death is no longer just a death; it is a death whose \(\pi\) failed to increase. That is a retrocausal genealogy from box-score counting to state-conditioned valuation. The paper also reverses the usual sports-analytics direction: because eSports has larger and cleaner data, try new metrics there and carry lessons back to traditional sports [PAGE 13]. The FFT rule is the distilled end of that lineage: “don’t die for no reason; if you do, try for some smart kills; if you can’t do that either, at least roam and have decent map coverage” [PAGE 23].

The multi-week traps are serious. First, patch drift: “Patches happen regularly in LoL, approximately every few weeks” [PAGE 15], and “new patches changing or modifying rules or champions approximately every few weeks” [PAGE 12]. A model trained on one patch can become mis-calibrated after champion, item, jungle, or tower changes. The author handles this by focusing on “the ∼150,000 games from the latest patch” [PAGE 17], but that is a within-patch solution, not a cross-patch guarantee. Second, spectator delay: “Riot broadcasts spectated games on a 3 min delay” [PAGE 15]. Any claim of truly live coaching or zero-latency decision support is vetoed by that delay. Third, iterative scraping only follows future games; the author says “Only future played games are spectated, so a player’s history is not imported” [PAGE 17]. That reduces history leakage but means the corpus is a moving window. Fourth, role evolution is a multi-week trap: roles “primarily refer to the laning phase” [PAGE 26], and role detection can fail when a top laner starts with bot lane or a jungler skips Smite [PAGE 26]. Fifth, the 3-min tilt window and 5-s engagement window are stable within a patch but can be perturbed by death-timer or item changes. The correct genealogy is therefore patch-conditional and client-conditional: the paper’s operators are strong within the described pipeline, but they are not eternal constants of League of Legends.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Crisis habit regression.** The paper’s tilt analysis is a model of habit regression under emotional load. “The most extreme form of tilt occurs when a player dies in lane then returns immediately and plays too aggressively and dies again” [PAGE 20]. The proxy is the share of recent team death time attributable to the player over the preceding 3 minutes [PAGE 20]. In recursive-theory-of-mind terms, the tilted player collapses the enemy’s model to a simple target: revenge. He stops updating on enemy cooldowns, jungler position, and wave state. The data consequence is a cluster of unfavorable fights and worthless deaths. The paper’s Figure 7 example shows a player first dying in a favorable fight, then a balanced fight, then three unfavorable fights in a row [PAGE 20]. That is not random variance; it is a habitual policy regression.

**Counter-trap.** The counter-policy is the smart-kill/worthless-death rule. A kill is not smart merely because it removes a low-health enemy. “If you chased a weak enemy for 2 min to kill them, that is almost surely not a smart kill, because it was a waste of time and you likely missed out on an objective” [PAGE 18]. The recursive model here is nested: the player must represent the enemy’s bait, the enemy team’s collapse timing, his own team’s objective window, and the opponent’s model of his greed. The FFT rule is the counter-trap in compact form: “don’t die for no reason; if you do, try for some smart kills; if you can’t do that either, at least roam and have decent map coverage” [PAGE 23]. This is not a generic “play safe” slogan; it is a priority ordering over death avoidance, win-probability-positive kills, and map presence.

**Clock.** The clock layer is both in-game and analytical. In-game clocks include the 5-s combat boundary [PAGE 19], death timers up to about 60 s, and roughly 30 s to walk back to mid-map [PAGE 20]. Analytical clocks include the minute-level win-probability model [PAGE 18], the 3-min tilt window [PAGE 20], and the multi-week patch cycle [PAGE 15]. Recursive theory of mind is constrained by these clocks: a player who respawns has a known travel time, a known death-timer penalty, and a known objective window. The enemy jungler’s threat is partly a belief about whether the laner has updated his model of fog-of-war risk: “If the enemy jungler emerges from the fog of war at this time, you will find yourself in a one-vs-two situation, and probably die” [PAGE 25]. The paper’s advanced metrics attempt to capture this recursion through favorable/unfavorable team fights, carry focus efficiency, vision denial, and map coverage [PAGE 19]. The honest limit is that the data see actions, not beliefs; recursive theory of mind is inferred from state-conditioned behavior, not from introspective reports.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical layer.** The physical data-generating process vetoes any claim of zero-latency, arbitrary-scale, or client-independent observation. The pipeline uses cloud Windows instances with game clients and an “expensive graphics processing unit (GPU)” because the client requires one [PAGE 16]. The observer client is on a “3 min delay” [PAGE 15]. Patches arrive “approximately every few weeks” [PAGE 15]. Optical tracking must resolve champion overlaps and minimap occlusion [PAGE 16]. Therefore, a claim that the model is a universal real-time oracle is vetoed: it is a patch-conditional, GPU-dependent, delayed-observer measurement system. This is not a minor caveat; it defines the admissible evidence.

**Biomechanical layer.** The paper does not measure human biomechanics directly. It measures in-game actions: attacks per minute, ability counts Q/W/E/R, combo damage per minute, damage taken, cooldowns, and positioning [PAGE 19]. Human reaction time, fatigue, injury, camera control, and mouse precision are not observed variables. Any biomechanical explanation must be translated into a tracked state variable or it is vetoed. The 5-s in-combat convention and 60-s death timers are game-mechanical clocks, not physiological clocks [PAGE 19; PAGE 20]. The 30-s walk-back estimate is a map-traversal clock, not a biomechanical measurement [PAGE 20]. Thus “fatigue” or “reaction speed” cannot be inserted as an unmeasured causal layer; it must appear as a change in attacks/minute, ability accuracy, combo order, or death timing.

**Weather layer.** There is no weather variable in the source. League of Legends is a digital environment with fog of war, brushes, wards, and stealth, but no meteorological state [PAGE 25]. External weather could in principle affect a human player, but the paper tracks “continuously, invisibly, remotely, and live” and “all without any impact on the user, or even their knowledge” [PAGE 11; PAGE 12]. It only tracks games that allow public spectating [PAGE 12]. Therefore, a weather-based explanation of smart kills, worthless deaths, or team fights is unverifiable in this dataset and is vetoed. The same veto applies to any physical-stadium claim: there is no stadium, no wind, no rain, and no field surface in the measured game state.

**Adjudication rule.** A cross-layer claim is admissible only if it maps to a tracked variable in the source’s extractable-data table: game state, resources, statistics, damage, or location [PAGE 16]. If it does not map, it is vetoed as an unmeasured layer. Under that rule, physical hardware and network delay are admissible constraints; biomechanical fatigue is admissible only as an action-rate or timing proxy; weather is inadmissible. This is the proof: the data-generating process is a spectator client and observer hooks, not a physiological or meteorological sensor.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Verifiable check: out-of-sample accuracy, false-positive rate, and McFadden pseudo-\(R^2\). Prediction: the raw K/D model will underperform the advanced model. The paper’s advanced FFT “makes the correct prediction about 80% of the time despite only using three metrics,” with “a false positive rate of only 13%” [PAGE 23], and the abstract states “regular kills and deaths do not nearly explain as much as smart kills and worthless deaths” [PAGE 11]. Veto condition: if raw K/D matches the advanced FFT’s 80% accuracy and 13% false-positive rate on the held-out 25%, the paper’s central claim is falsified. The prediction is directional: raw K/D accuracy \(<80\%\), or false-positive rate \(>13\%\), or both.

**Veto 2: Patch-invariant model versus patch-conditional recalibration.** Counterfactual: train the win-probability and smart-kill classifiers on patch \(P_t\) and evaluate on patch \(P_{t+1}\) without recalibration. Verifiable check: calibration slope, Brier score, and out-of-sample log loss by patch ID. Prediction: cross-patch performance degrades because “Our corpus of millions of games spanned several LoL patches, each of which can significantly affect gameplay” [PAGE 17], and patches change rules or champions “approximately every few weeks” [PAGE 12]. Veto condition: if the cross-patch calibration slope remains approximately 1 and Brier score does not materially worsen, the patch-drift veto is rejected. The expected result is a measurable degradation, forcing patch-specific or time-decayed coefficients.

**Veto 3: Fixed 5-s engagement boundary versus alternative boundaries.** Counterfactual: recompute engagements with a 2-s or 10-s no-damage gap instead of 5 s. Verifiable check: favorable/unfavorable fight counts, smart-kill counts, worthless-death counts, FFT split thresholds, and out-of-sample accuracy. Prediction: 2 s fragments single fights into multiple engagements; 10 s merges separate skirmishes. Both distort the favorable-fight and death bundles and reduce the FFT’s out-of-sample accuracy below the reported 80% [PAGE 23]. The paper justifies 5 s by an in-game mechanic: “Five seconds is consistent with the concept of ‘in-combat’ used within the game by certain powerups” [PAGE 19]. Veto condition: if 2 s and 10 s produce the same FFT accuracy and the same smart-kill/worthless-death rankings, the 5-s boundary is not load-bearing. The expected result is that 5 s is load-bearing because it matches the game’s combat clock; alternative boundaries change the event labels and therefore change the model’s predictions.
