The paper’s lineage is a convergence of four research programs. The first is transportation theory. Green and Brock (2000) established that narrative absorption shifts beliefs by suppressing counterarguing; the paper cites this directly and defines transportation as disengagement from the real world [PAGE 2]. The second is the Extended Elaboration Likelihood Model, which holds that “transportation into a narrative can result in positive responses to embedded persuasive messages, particularly by suppressing counterarguing against those messages” [PAGE 2]. The third is identification theory. Cohen (2001) defines identification as adopting a media persona’s perspectives [PAGE 3]; Klimmt et al. (2009) distinguish dyadic identification in non-interactive media from monadic identification in interactive media [PAGE 3]; Gee (2008) adds the embodiment argument that players take on “virtual minds and virtual bodies” [PAGE 3]. The fourth is stigma theory. Corrigan’s social-cognitive model links cues to stereotypes to discrimination [PAGE 2]; Link et al. (2001) specify labeling, negative attribution, us–them separation, and status loss [PAGE 2]. The paper’s contribution is to splice these lineages: interactive transportation is hypothesized to produce monadic identification, which is hypothesized to blur the in-group/out-group boundary and reduce social distance.

The retrocausal genealogy also contains a warning lineage that the paper acknowledges but does not fully integrate. Corrigan and Penn (1999) documented that anti-stigma interventions can produce a “boomerang effect, increasing the stigma they are attempting to reduce, either because of reactance or through the general resiliency of stereotypes” [PAGE 1]. Wahl and Lefkowits (1989) and Rubenking and Bracken (2015) showed that stigmatizing portrayals can increase stigma relative to sensitive portrayals [PAGE 2]. This lineage predicts a sign condition: if the portrayal activates danger or unpredictability stereotypes more than it humanizes, the total effect \(\partial \mathbb{E}[S]/\partial C\) can be positive even when \(\alpha_T>0\). The paper’s null on stereotyping is therefore not evidence against the boomerang; it is consistent with a mixture of humanization and stereotype activation that nets to zero.

The paper concedes: “participants in this study only played the first 45 min of the game” and “Effect sizes may have been larger had participants undergone the entire journey with Senua” [PAGE 8]. A dose-response curve with a lag implies that the 45-minute effect is a lower bound, but it also implies that the mechanism may change qualitatively later, when Senua’s self-acceptance narrative becomes salient. Second, the persistence trap: “we only examined the short-term impacts” [PAGE 8]. If the social-distance reduction decays over weeks, the intervention is not durable; a follow-up at 2, 4, and 8 weeks is required. Third, the stimulus trap: Hellblade is exceptional. It was “developed along with neuroscientists and those who experience psychosis” [PAGE 4], won a BAFTA for “Game Beyond Entertainment,” and used “binaural 3D microphones” [PAGE 4]. Generalizing from this stimulus to commercial games at large is unwarranted; the paper itself notes that the game “is not categorized as, e.g., an action game, simulation game, war game, or e-sports game” [PAGE 7]. The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how the player models Senua’s mind, the experimenter’s hypothesis, and the out-group. I formalize three levels. Level 1: \(B_P^{(1)}(M_S)\), the player’s belief about Senua’s mental state. Level 2: \(B_P^{(2)}(M_S \mid B_E(E))\), the player’s belief about Senua conditional on the player’s model of the experimenter’s expectation \(E\). Level 3: \(B_P^{(3)}(G \mid B_E(E))\), the player’s belief about the stigmatized group \(G\) conditional on the same experimenter model. Identification is the operator that maps Level 1 into the self-concept: \(I=\phi(B_P^{(1)}(M_S))\). Demand characteristics operate at Level 2: if the player infers that the study expects reduced stigma, self-report on \(D\) and \(S\) is biased downward independently of the treatment.

Crisis habit regression. Under cognitive load, the player regresses to default social heuristics. The paper’s own design creates load: “The game does not have a dedicated tutorial and does not show participants how to use controls or where to go” [PAGE 8]. Motor learning consumes attentional resources that would otherwise support perspective-taking. Let \(L\) be cognitive load and \(T\) transportation; a simple threshold model is
\[
I=\begin{cases}
f(T), & L<L^*,\\
0, & L\ge L^*,
\end{cases}
\]
where \(L^*\) is the load at which perspective-taking collapses. The paper reports that “there may have been some confusion for participants, making it somewhat difficult for them to play the game” [PAGE 8]. That is a crisis-habit regression: the player’s default response to failure is ego-protective distancing (“this is just a game,” “the voices are a mechanic”), which is a counter-trap to identification.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is that identification can increase self-stereotyping. The paper states: “even though participants can take on Senua’s thoughts, attitudes, and behaviors as though they were the players’, they may still stereotype their own self-concept” [PAGE 7]. Formally, if \(I\) expands the self-concept to include Senua, and if the stereotype \(S\) is activated, then the player may apply \(S\) to the expanded self. This predicts \(\beta_S\ge0\) even when \(\beta_I>0\). The observed null \(\beta_S\approx0\) is therefore ambiguous: it could mean no stereotype activation, or it could mean humanization and self-stereotyping cancel.

Clock. The 45-minute session is a temporal budget. Transportation follows a saturating curve \(T(t)=T_{\max}(1-e^{-kt})\); identification follows a lagged curve \(I(t)=I_{\max}(1-e^{-k'(t-\tau)})\mathbf{1}\{t>\tau\}\). If the lag \(\tau\) exceeds 45 minutes, the monadic relationship never forms. The low identification mean (2.59 or 2.69) is consistent with \(t<\tau\) for most participants. The clock also governs the narrative content: the first 45 minutes contain combat and confusion, while Senua’s self-acceptance comes “toward the end of the game” [PAGE 8]. A longer clock would increase \(T\) and \(I\), but it would also change the valence of the content. The recursive prediction is non-monotonic: social distance may fall further, but self-stigma may rise, and the net effect on stereotyping may reverse.

The recursive ToM also explains the watch condition. Watchers must model both Senua and the third-party player: \(B_W^{(2)}(M_S \mid M_P)\). The paper notes that “the watcher is not directly interacting with the character but is forced into a further layer of distancing brought on by the presence of a third party – the player” [PAGE 7]. This is a second-order model that consumes working memory and reduces transportation. The yoked design equalizes content but not recursive load; watchers carry an extra agent model. That is a structural, not incidental, difference between conditions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto asks whether physical, biomechanical, or meteorological constraints invalidate the causal claim. The physical layer includes the laboratory PC, the 45-minute session, the absence of a tutorial, and the binaural audio. The paper states that Hellblade used “binaural 3D microphones to give the illusion that the protagonist is hearing voices from different directions” [PAGE 4]. If participants did not use headphones, the directional-voice mechanism is degraded or absent. The paper does not report headphone use, audio settings, or volume. That is a physical-layer veto on the proposed mechanism: the treatment may have delivered a visual psychosis simulation without the auditory feature that experts called “one of the best representatives of these experiences” [PAGE 4]. The claim that the game accurately simulates psychosis is a property of the stimulus as designed, not necessarily as administered.

The biomechanical layer includes motor control, interface learning, posture, and fatigue. The paper admits that the game “does not show participants how to use controls or where to go” [PAGE 8]. Motor learning is a biomechanical demand that competes with narrative absorption. The single-item difficulty covariate (“This game was difficult”) [PAGE 5] is too coarse to capture latency, handedness, prior controller experience, or fatigue. A player who cannot navigate Senua will not experience the monadic relationship; the avatar becomes an obstacle rather than an extension. This is a biomechanical veto on the strong embodiment interpretation. The yoked design does not fix it because watchers do not face the same motor demand; the conditions differ in biomechanical load by construction.

The weather layer is non-operative. The experiment was conducted indoors on laboratory computers, and no meteorological variable enters the model. There is no basis in the source for a weather veto. The nearest weather-adjacent confound is seasonal recruitment from a subject pool, which could affect mood or stigma attitudes, but the paper does not report recruitment dates or season. I therefore adjudicate the weather layer as not applicable and explicitly non-vetoing; it cannot rescue or invalidate the causal claim.

The physical-layer veto proof is conditional. The causal path \(C\to T\to I\to D\) survives only if (i) random assignment is valid, which the paper states [PAGE 4]; (ii) yoking equalizes story exposure, which the paper states [PAGE 4]; (iii) difficulty is controlled, which is only partially true because the covariate is a single item [PAGE 5]; and (iv) the audio-visual delivery is as designed, which is unreported. The physical layer therefore does not fully veto the claim, but it narrows it: the effective treatment is “playing an unfamiliar narrative game with a psychosis portrayal under laboratory conditions,” not “embodying a mentally ill character” in general. The biomechanical layer further narrows it: the effect is estimated under motor-load conditions that may suppress identification for novice players. The paper’s own limitation section supports this reading: “there may have been some confusion for participants, making it somewhat difficult for them to play the game” [PAGE 8].

A second veto check concerns the outcome measurement. Social distance is self-reported willingness, not observed behavior. The physical layer of the survey—seven-point Likert items on a laboratory computer—introduces social desirability and demand characteristics. The disclaimer at the start of the game [PAGE 4] primes the study’s purpose. The physical layer of the measurement environment therefore competes with the treatment: participants may report lower social distance because they infer the hypothesis, not because their attitudes changed. This is not a full veto, but it is a plausible alternative pathway that the design does not rule out.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
