Knight dismantles the "given ends" prerequisite. His genealogy connects to Friedrich Nietzsche's concept of will-to-power and the creation of values, rather than the discovery of pre-existing ones. By framing economic motivation as akin to capturing an opponent's pieces in chess, Knight anticipates the 20th-century shift towards pragmatism and later, Herbert Simon's bounded rationality, though Simon focused on computational limits while Knight focused on ontological instability.

The multi-week trap inherent in this genealogy is the "rational actor" persistence bias. For nearly a century, despite Knight's warning that stable motives are doubtful, economic modeling defaulted to exogenous preferences because they were mathematically tractable. Models that attempted to incorporate Knight's dynamic preferences often collapsed back into static forms by assuming the "game" itself was optimized away. The trap lies in mistaking the mathematical convenience of a fixed utility function for a description of reality. In modern contexts, this manifests in the failure of traditional consumer demand models to predict the viral adoption of cryptocurrencies or NFTs—phenomena driven purely by the "game spirit" where the objective (holding a token) is arbitrary until the social momentum ($\lambda S$) makes it an absolute value. Understanding Knight's 1921 text retroactively exposes these modern failures not as anomalies, but as the expected behavior of systems governed by endogenous goal generation.

The social driving power $\lambda S$ cannot compensate for a missing physical input.
