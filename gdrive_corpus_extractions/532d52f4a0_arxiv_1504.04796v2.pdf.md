The paper’s core derivation is a multi-hop chain from edge-level infection times to a game-theoretic fixed point. At the edge level, [PAGE 5] defines “the time it takes for an infected node i to infect its susceptible neighbor j” as \(\mu(i,j)\), with rate \(\lambda(i,j)=1/\mu(i,j)\). The infection time of a path is the sum of edge times, and the infected set at observation time \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\) is the set of nodes whose shortest-path infection time from the source is at most \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\). The maximum hop radius is not a free parameter: [PAGE 6] defines \(\bar d(t)=\max\{k:\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\bar\lambda_m^{-1}\le t\}\), so the clock and the rate upper bounds jointly determine how far infection can have travelled. This is the first gray-zone audit point: any later claim about safety margin, source detection, or best response must be checked against \(\bar d(t_{\mathrm{obs}})\), not against an assumed number of hops.

The administrator’s estimator is the Jordan center of the observed infection graph. [PAGE 7] states that “a Jordan center is a minimax source estimator that minimizes the largest distance to any infected node.” The safety margin is \(d_s(\Lambda)=\min_{u\in JC}d(v^*,u)\), and the suspect set is \(V_{sp}(d_a)=\{v\in V_I:d(v,u)\le d_a\}\) around an arbitrarily chosen Jordan center. The utility functions link detection to cost: [PAGE 8] gives \(u_a(d_a,\Lambda)=-c_a(V_{sp}(d_a))+g_a(d_a,V_I)\mathbf 1_{d_a\ge d_s(\Lambda)}\) and \(u_s(d_a,\Lambda)=g_s|V_I|-c_s(d_a)\mathbf 1_{d_a\ge d_s(\Lambda)}\). The best-response logic is a two-step threshold argument. If \(d_s=0\), the administrator’s optimal radius is \(0\). If \(d_s\) is positive, any radius strictly between \(0\) and \(d_s\) pays probing cost without gaining detection reward, and any radius strictly beyond \(d_s\) pays more cost with non-increasing gain. Hence [PAGE 10] Theorem 1 restricts the administrator’s best response to \(d_a=0\) or \(d_a=d_s\), with the choice determined by whether \(g_a(d_s,V_I)\) exceeds \(c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0))\).

The source’s feasible strategy space is bounded by the observation clock. [PAGE 11] Theorem 2 proves that for a tree “the largest feasible safety margin is \(\bar d_s=\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor\).” The proof idea is a diameter argument: if the safety margin were larger, a far leaf in the infection graph would have distance at least \(2d_s\) from the source, contradicting the maximum hop radius. The Dominant Infection Strategy then maximizes infection subject to a chosen safety margin by selecting a dominant path, infecting along that path at maximum rates, and slowing branch infections enough to keep the Jordan center at least \(d_s\) away. [PAGE 15] gives the branch-rate construction \(\lambda_m=h_m/(t-t_m)\) with \(h_m=\bar d(t)-2d_s+m\), and [PAGE 16] uses a Bellman-Ford procedure to choose the dominant path that maximizes the weighted branch mass. The source then binary-searches the smallest \(t\) such that the infection size reaches \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\), which converts the observation threshold into \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\). This is the second gray-zone audit point: \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\) is not \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\); the paper explicitly derives \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\) from \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\) via the strategy’s infection-size monotonicity.

The game-theoretic closure is [PAGE 19] Theorem 4 and [PAGE 20] Theorem 5. For a given administrator radius \(d_a\), the source’s best response is either \(\Lambda_0\), the maximum-spread strategy, or \(\Lambda_{d_a+1}\), the strategy that sits just outside the suspect radius; the comparison is \(c_s(d_a)\) against \(g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_{d_a+1})|)\). At Nash equilibrium, if one exists, the administrator’s equilibrium radius is \(d_a=0\), i.e. the Jordan center estimator. [PAGE 20] states: “if a Nash equilibrium exists, then the strategy of the network administrator in equilibrium has \(d_a=0\), which corresponds to the Jordan center estimator.” The gray-zone limits are explicit. The proofs require a tree; [PAGE 21] notes that finding the best underlying tree in a general graph “is a NP-hard problem,” and [PAGE 26] says theoretical results for general networks seem unlikely because of loopy-graph difficulties. Incomplete observations also move the estimator: [PAGE 24] warns “\(JC(\alpha)\) may be different from the Jordan center of all infected nodes \(JC\).” Thus the formal claims survive only under the stated tree, rate-control, one-snapshot, known-\(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\), and full-observation conditions; outside them the paper offers simulations and heuristics, not theorem-level guarantees.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal objects, units, and page citations.

Let \(G(V,E)\) be an undirected graph, with a single source \(v^*\in V\). For edge \((i,j)\in E\), define infection time \(\mu(i,j)\) in time ticks per edge and infection rate \(\lambda(i,j)=1/\mu(i,j)\) in inverse ticks. [PAGE 5] states: “Let \(\lambda(i,j)=1/\mu(i,j)\) be the infection rate of \((i,j)\).” For an edge from distance layer \(m\) to layer \(m+1\), the rate is upper bounded by \(\bar\lambda_m\): [PAGE 5] states “we assume \(\lambda(i,j)\) is uniformly upper bounded by a maximum infection rate \(\bar\lambda_m>0\).” The distance \(d(v,u)\) is measured in hops, and the path infection time is
\[
\mu(v,u)=\sum_{(i,j)\in \rho(v,u)}\frac{1}{\lambda(i,j)}
\]
in time ticks, where \(\rho(v,u)\) is the shortest path. The observation set is
\[
V_I=\{u\in G:\mu(v^*,u)\le t_{\mathrm{obs}}\},
\]
with \(|V_I|\) measured in nodes. The maximum hop radius at time \(t\) is
\[
\bar d(t)=\max\left\{k:\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\bar\lambda_m^{-1}\le t\right\},
\]
measured in hops; this is [PAGE 6] equation (1). The model assumes \(G\) is sufficiently large so that \(\bar d(t_{\mathrm{obs}})\le \bar d(v^*,V)\), i.e. the observation occurs before the infection reaches the entire graph.

The Jordan center is
\[
JC=\{v:\bar d(v,V_I)=\min_{u\in G}\bar d(u,V_I)\},
\]
where \(\bar d(v,A)=\max_{u\in A}d(v,u)\), measured in hops. [PAGE 7] states: “a Jordan center is a minimax source estimator that minimizes the largest distance to any infected node.” For a chosen Jordan center \(u\), the suspect set is
\[
V_{sp}(d_a)=\{v\in V_I:d(v,u)\le d_a\},
\]
with estimation radius \(d_a\) in hops. The safety margin is
\[
d_s(\Lambda)=\min_{u\in JC}d(v^*,u),
\]
also in hops. The utility functions are [PAGE 8]:
\[
u_a(d_a,\Lambda)=-c_a(V_{sp}(d_a))+g_a(d_a,V_I)\mathbf 1_{d_a\ge d_s(\Lambda)},
\]
\[
u_s(d_a,\Lambda)=g_s|V_I|-c_s(d_a)\mathbf 1_{d_a\ge d_s(\Lambda)}.
\]
Here \(g_a,g_s,c_a,c_s\) are in utility units; \(|V_I|\) is in nodes; the indicator is dimensionless. The paper does not introduce an explicit information-theoretic entropy or bit unit. The only probability statement is the uniform-suspect example on [PAGE 8], where the administrator’s expected reward is inversely proportional to \(|V_{sp}(d_a)|\); this is a dimensionless probability weighting, not an information quantity.

Best-response estimation. [PAGE 10] Theorem 1 states: if \(d_s=0\), then the best-response estimation radius is \(d_a^*=0\). If \(d_s\) is positive, then
\[
d_a^*=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } g_a(d_s,V_I)\text{ is strictly less than }c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0)),\\
d_s, & \text{if } g_a(d_s,V_I)\text{ is strictly greater than }c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0)),\\
0\text{ or }d_s, & \text{if } g_a(d_s,V_I)=c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0)).
\end{cases}
\]
Feasible safety margin. [PAGE 11] Theorem 2: for a tree and observation time \(t\),
\[
\bar d_s=\left\lfloor \frac{\bar d(t)}{2}\right\rfloor.
\]
Thus any claim with \(d_s>\bar d_s\) is infeasible; no infection strategy can realize it. The proof uses a diameter and shows that otherwise \(d(v^*,l_2)\ge 2(d_s)\ge \bar d(t)+1\), contradicting the maximum hop radius.

Dominant Infection Strategy. For \(m\in[0,d_s]\), define
\[
h_m=\bar d(t)-2d_s+m,
\qquad
t_m=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\bar\lambda_k^{-1},
\qquad
\lambda_m=\frac{h_m}{t-t_m}.
\]
This is [PAGE 15] equations (20)-(21). The branch rate is [PAGE 15] equation (22):
\[
\lambda^{\mathrm{DIS}}_{m,j}=
\begin{cases}
\bar\lambda_{m+j}-\delta_{t,m}, & \text{if }j\in A_m\text{ and }0\le m\le d_s,\\
\bar\lambda_{m+j}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(A_m=\{j\le h_m:\bar\lambda_{m+j}>\lambda_m\}\) and \(\delta_{t,m}\ge0\) is chosen so that the path infection time equals \(t-t_m\) after subtracting the maximum-rate contributions outside \(A_m\). The dominant path is chosen by [PAGE 16] Algorithm 1, which assigns edge weight
\[
w(u,w)=|\{v\in T_u\setminus T_w:d(v,u)\le h(u)\}|,
\]
with
\[
h(u)=
\begin{cases}
\bar d(t)-2d_s+d(v^*,u), & d(v^*,u)\le d_s,\\
\bar d(t)-d(v^*,u), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
and then applies Bellman-Ford. The paper’s complexity statement is \(O(n^2)\) for Algorithm 1 and \(O(\beta^4 d_s n_{\mathrm{obs}}^2\log n_{\mathrm{obs}})\) overall under bounded degree \(\beta\).

Best-response infection. [PAGE 19] Lemma 4 says any maximum-infection strategy at smaller safety margin infects strictly more nodes than one at a larger safety margin. [PAGE 19] Theorem 4 then gives:
\[
\Lambda^*=
\begin{cases}
\Lambda_0, & \text{if }c_s(d_a)\text{ is strictly less than }g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_{d_a+1})|),\\
\Lambda_{d_a+1}, & \text{if }c_s(d_a)\text{ is strictly greater than }g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_{d_a+1})|),\\
\Lambda_0\text{ or }\Lambda_{d_a+1}, & \text{if equality holds}.
\end{cases}
\]
If \(d_a\ge\bar d_s\), no safety margin can exceed the estimation radius, so the source always incurs \(c_s(d_a)\) and maximizes \(|V_I|\), yielding \(\Lambda_0\). If \(d_a<\bar d_s\), the source compares \(\Lambda_0\) against \(\Lambda_{d_a+1}\).

Nash equilibrium. [PAGE 20] Theorem 5 gives conditions: \((0,\Lambda_0)\) is a Nash equilibrium iff \(c_s(0)\le g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_1)|)\) for all \(\Lambda_1\in M(1,t_{\mathrm{obs}})\); \((0,\Lambda_1)\) is a Nash equilibrium iff \(c_s(0)\ge g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_1)|)\) and \(g_a(1,V_I(\Lambda_1))\le c_a(V_{sp}(1))-c_a(V_{sp}(0))\); no other pure-strategy Nash equilibria exist. When equilibrium exists, \(d_a=0\), i.e. the Jordan center estimator.

Singularity and breakdown regimes. (i) The model assumes \(\bar\lambda_m>0\); if any \(\bar\lambda_m=0\), then \(\bar\lambda_m^{-1}\) is undefined and \(\bar d(t)\) is undefined. (ii) If \(d_s>\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor\), then \(M(d_s,t)\) is empty. (iii) In \(\lambda_m=h_m/(t-t_m)\), the construction requires the branch to be reachable, effectively \(t>t_m\) when \(h_m>0\); if \(h_m=0\), then \(\lambda_m=0\) and that branch contributes no further infection. (iv) Theorem 1 has tie cases; a deterministic verifier must accept both \(d_a=0\) and \(d_a=d_s\) at equality. (v) The tree assumption is not cosmetic: [PAGE 21] says finding the best underlying tree for general graphs “is a NP-hard problem,” and [PAGE 26] says obtaining theoretical results for general networks “seems unlikely” because of loopy-graph difficulties. (vi) Full observation is assumed for the theorems; [PAGE 24] notes that with partial observation, “\(JC(\alpha)\) may be different from the Jordan center of all infected nodes \(JC\).”
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits in a lineage that runs from graph-theoretic center estimators to rumor-source identification to game-theoretic obfuscation. The Jordan center is imported from social-network analysis [PAGE 7, citing reference 33] and is treated as a minimax estimator because it minimizes the largest distance to any infected node. Earlier source-identification work is represented by Shah and Zaman’s rumor centrality and by Luo, Tay, and Leng’s infection-source and region estimators [PAGE 35 references 14, 15, 18, 19]. Zhu and Ying’s SIR sample-path approach supplies the result that a tree has at most two Jordan centers [PAGE 7, reference 17]. Fanti, Kairouz, Oh, and Viswanath’s adaptive diffusion protocol [PAGE 35 reference 26] is the direct counter-lineage: it is designed for an administrator using the maximum-likelihood estimator and an infinite regular tree. The present paper’s DIS is positioned against adaptive diffusion: on an infinite \(r\)-regular tree with unit rates, [PAGE 18] shows that DIS infects at least as many nodes as adaptive diffusion almost surely, because adaptive diffusion’s infected set is bounded by the ball of radius \(\lfloor t/2\rfloor\), whereas DIS can use a larger radius \(t-d_s\) for the same safety margin. The influence-maximization literature [PAGE 36 references 27-32] is the adjacent lineage, but the paper distinguishes itself by fixing the source and maximizing infection subject to a safety-margin constraint rather than choosing a seed set.

The retrocausal turn is the reinterpretation of the Jordan center. In earlier work it was a robust estimator; here [PAGE 4] says the Nash-equilibrium result “gives a game-theoretic interpretation to the Jordan center estimator, in addition to being a universally robust estimator.” [PAGE 20] makes the point explicit: when equilibrium exists, the administrator’s equilibrium radius is \(d_a=0\), which “corresponds to the Jordan center estimator.” Thus a later equilibrium argument retroactively justifies an earlier minimax heuristic as the natural strategic choice under the paper’s cost and gain structure. A second retrocausal move is the comparison with adaptive diffusion: adaptive diffusion was order-optimal for obfuscation under maximum-likelihood estimation on infinite regular trees, but DIS shows that a deterministic, rate-controlled dominant-path strategy can dominate its infection count while preserving a chosen safety margin.

The multi-week traps are several. The first is the “tree-first” trap: treating the tree theorems as general-network guarantees. The paper explicitly extends DIS heuristically by building a breadth-first search tree [PAGE 21], but [PAGE 21] says finding the optimal underlying tree is NP-hard and [PAGE 26] says general-network theory is unlikely. The second is the “safety margin equals escape probability” trap. A safety margin \(d_s\) only places the source outside or inside a suspect radius; actual identification depends on \(d_a\), the gain-cost comparison in Theorem 1, and whether \(d_a\ge d_s\). The third is the “observation threshold equals observation time” trap. \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\) is a node-count threshold; \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\) must be recovered from the infection process, and [PAGE 11]’s Step 2 and [PAGE 16]’s binary search are essential. The fourth is the “adaptive diffusion is always the strongest obfuscation” trap; [PAGE 18] explicitly shows the opposite ordering on the infinite regular tree benchmark. The fifth is the “partial observation is harmless” trap; [PAGE 24] shows \(JC(\alpha)\) can differ from \(JC\), and [PAGE 25-26] reports that dense networks can push the administrator back to \(d_a=0\) because probing cost grows too quickly. These traps are multi-week because they recur whenever a practitioner transfers a theorem from trees to real networks without rechecking the feasibility bound, the observation model, and the cost asymmetry.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The strategic recursion is explicit. The administrator chooses \(d_a^*=\arg\max_{d_a}u_a(d_a,\Lambda)\); the source chooses \(\Lambda^*=\arg\max_{\Lambda}u_s(d_a,\Lambda)\); a Nash equilibrium is a pair where each is a best response to the other [PAGE 8-9]. This is a theory-of-mind loop: the source reasons about what radius the administrator will probe, the administrator reasons about what safety margin the source will choose, and the loop closes at a fixed point. The paper’s strongest simplification is that the loop collapses to two thresholds. Theorem 1 says the administrator’s best response is either \(0\) or \(d_s\), so the administrator does not need a continuum of beliefs about the source; it only needs to compare the gain of catching the source at radius \(d_s\) against the incremental probing cost. Theorem 4 says the source’s best response is either \(\Lambda_0\) or \(\Lambda_{d_a+1}\), so the source does not need to model arbitrary administrator strategies; it only needs to decide whether to maximize spread or to sit one hop outside the suspect radius.

“Crisis habit regression” appears when the cost or gain parameters push a player back to a simpler habit. If \(c_s(d_a)\) is low relative to \(g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_{d_a+1})|)\), the source regresses to \(\Lambda_0\), the maximum-spread strategy, and accepts detection because the reward from extra infected nodes dominates the penalty. If \(g_a(d_s,V_I)\) is low relative to \(c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0))\), the administrator regresses to \(d_a=0\), the Jordan center only, and accepts zero probability of catching a source with \(d_s>0\) because probing is too costly. These are not irrational habits; they are the best responses under the stated utility functions. The counter-trap is the opposite move: the source chooses \(d_s=d_a+1\) so that the administrator’s suspect set does not contain it, while the administrator chooses \(d_a=d_s\) so that the source is inside the suspect set. The paper’s equilibrium result resolves the counter-trap by forcing \(d_a=0\) in any pure Nash equilibrium, so the administrator’s equilibrium strategy is the minimal Jordan-center probe, and the source’s equilibrium safety margin is either \(0\) or \(1\) depending on the cost comparison in Theorem 5.

The “clock” is the observation time. The source knows \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\) but not \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\) [PAGE 8], so it must infer the clock from the infection process. [PAGE 11] Step 2 searches for the smallest \(t\) such that \(\|\Lambda_{d_s,t}\|\ge n_{\mathrm{obs}}\); [PAGE 16] Algorithm 2 binary-searches over \(t_m\) values. If the source ignores the clock and chooses a safety margin above \(\lfloor \bar d(t_{\mathrm{obs}})/2\rfloor\), Theorem 2 makes the strategy infeasible. If the administrator ignores the clock, it may set \(d_a\) based on an assumed \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\) rather than the actual infection radius. The recursive theory-of-mind failure mode is therefore a clock mismatch: both players can reason correctly about costs and radii yet still fail because they disagree about when the observation occurs. The paper’s remedy is structural: compute \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\) from \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\), then restrict the source to \(d_s\in[0,\lfloor \bar d(t_{\mathrm{obs}})/2\rfloor]\) and the administrator to the two-radius comparison in Theorem 1.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source paper contains no physical, biomechanical, or weather variables. It is a graph-dynamical game over social or computer networks. Therefore any cross-layer claim must be admitted only if it maps into one of the paper’s formal channels: the graph \(G\), the edge rates \(\lambda(i,j)\), the layer upper bounds \(\bar\lambda_m\), the observation threshold \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\), the observation time \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\), the observed fraction \(\alpha\), the gains \(g_a,g_s\), or the costs \(c_a,c_s\). A “weather” claim that does not change \(\lambda\), \(\bar d(t)\), \(G\), or the observation model has no formal channel and is vetoed. A “biomechanical” claim about a social network is category-invalid; if it is meant to alter transmission speed, it must be encoded as a change in \(\lambda(i,j)\) or \(\bar\lambda_m\). If it is meant to alter who is observed, it must be encoded as a change in \(\alpha\) or \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\). If it is meant to alter the graph, it must be encoded as a change in \(G\). Otherwise it cannot override the arithmetic of the model.

The proof of the topology veto is direct. [PAGE 11] Theorem 2 is stated “Suppose the underlying graph \(G\) is a tree.” Its bound \(\bar d_s=\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor\) follows from a diameter argument: if \(d_s\) were larger, a far leaf \(l_2\) would satisfy \(d(v^*,l_2)\ge 2d_s\ge \bar d(t)+1\), contradicting the maximum hop radius. A cross-layer event that does not change the tree structure or the rate bound cannot make that inequality false. The temporal-rate veto is equally direct: [PAGE 6] defines \(\bar d(t)\) as the largest \(k\) with cumulative minimum infection times at most \(t\). If a proposed infection reaches a node at distance greater than \(\bar d(t)\), it violates the sum of inverse upper bounds, independent of any external narrative. The safety-margin veto follows: if \(d_s>\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor\), then \(M(d_s,t)\) is empty. The observation veto follows from [PAGE 8] and [PAGE 11]: the administrator observes one snapshot when the infected count first exceeds \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\), and the source knows \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\) but not \(t_{\mathrm{obs}}\). A claim that assumes continuous full monitoring changes the model; [PAGE 3] notes that the problem “becomes trivial if the network administrator is constantly monitoring all the nodes in the network.” The cost veto follows from Theorem 1 and Theorem 4: the administrator’s best response is restricted to \(0\) or \(d_s\), and the source’s best response is restricted to \(\Lambda_0\) or \(\Lambda_{d_a+1}\). Any cross-layer claim that recommends an interior radius or an unrelated safety margin must show how it changes \(g_a,c_a,g_s,c_s\) or the infection graph; otherwise it is outside the theorem.

Thus the cross-layer adjudication rule is: physical, biomechanical, or weather evidence can veto a strategy only by changing a parameter that appears in the formal inequalities. It cannot veto a theorem by assertion. If a weather event changes \(\bar\lambda_m\), recompute \(\bar d(t)\) and rerun Theorem 2. If it changes the observation fraction, recompute \(JC(\alpha)\) and note that [PAGE 24] says \(JC(\alpha)\) may differ from \(JC\). If it changes the graph from a tree to a loopy network, the theorem-level guarantee is lost: [PAGE 26] says general-network theory “seems unlikely,” and [PAGE 21] says the optimal underlying tree problem is NP-hard. In the absence of such parameter changes, the topological, temporal, safety-margin, observation, and cost vetoes stand.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios follow. Each is designed so a reinforcement-learning verifier can check a candidate answer against the paper’s exact inequalities and page citations.

Scenario 1: Infeasible safety margin under the rate clock. A candidate claims that on a tree, with observation time \(t\), maximum rates \(\{\bar\lambda_m\}\), and \(d_s=\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor+1\), there exists a nonempty maximum-infection set \(M(d_s,t)\) with positive infection. Verifier procedure: compute \(\bar d(t)=\max\{k:\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}\bar\lambda_m^{-1}\le t\}\); compute \(\bar d_s=\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor\); if \(d_s>\bar d_s\), veto. Prediction: the claim is false; \(M(d_s,t)=\emptyset\). The exact check is [PAGE 11] Theorem 2: “the largest feasible safety margin is \(\bar d_s=\lfloor \bar d(t)/2\rfloor\).” If the candidate supplies an infection graph, the verifier can also test the contradiction from the proof on [PAGE 27]: a far leaf \(l_2\) would need \(d(v^*,l_2)\ge 2d_s\ge \bar d(t)+1\), which violates the maximum hop radius. This veto is robust to any narrative about “more aggressive spreading” because the upper bounds \(\bar\lambda_m\) fix the cumulative time.

Scenario 2: Administrator best-response radius veto. A candidate claims that for \(d_s>0\), the administrator’s unique best response is \(d_a=d_s\) even though \(g_a(d_s,V_I)\) is strictly less than \(c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0))\). Verifier procedure: compute the incremental probing cost \(\Delta c=c_a(V_{sp}(d_s))-c_a(V_{sp}(0))\); compare \(g_a(d_s,V_I)\) with \(\Delta c\). Prediction: if \(g_a(d_s,V_I)\) is strictly less than \(\Delta c\), then \(d_a^*=0\), not \(d_s\); the candidate’s utility is lower at \(d_s\) than at \(0\). The exact citation is [PAGE 10] Theorem 1. If \(g_a(d_s,V_I)\) is strictly greater than \(\Delta c\), the prediction reverses: \(d_a^*=d_s\). If equality holds, both \(0\) and \(d_s\) are best responses, so a verifier must not reject either. This scenario is explicitly RLVR-friendly because it needs only the observed infection graph, the suspect-set cardinalities, and the stated gains and costs.

Scenario 3: Source best-response and Nash-equilibrium veto. A candidate claims that for a given \(d_a<\bar d_s\), the source’s best response is \(\Lambda_0\) even though \(c_s(d_a)\) is strictly greater than \(g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_{d_a+1})|)\). Verifier procedure: compute the maximum-infection sizes \(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|\) and \(|V_I(\Lambda_{d_a+1})|\); compare \(c_s(d_a)\) with the gain difference. Prediction: if \(c_s(d_a)\) is strictly greater than that difference, then \(\Lambda^*=\Lambda_{d_a+1}\), not \(\Lambda_0\); if it is strictly less, \(\Lambda^*=\Lambda_0\); if equal, either is optimal. The exact citation is [PAGE 19] Theorem 4. A second verifier branch can test Nash claims: if \(c_s(0)\le g_s(|V_I(\Lambda_0)|-|V_I(\Lambda_1)|)\) for all \(\Lambda_1\in M(1,t_{\mathrm{obs}})\), then \((0,\Lambda_0)\) is a Nash equilibrium; any candidate pure Nash equilibrium with \(d_a>0\) is rejected by [PAGE 20] Theorem 5(c), which states “No other pure strategy Nash equilibria exist.” Prediction: the only pure Nash equilibria are of the forms \((0,\Lambda_0)\) or \((0,\Lambda_1)\) under the exact cost inequalities. This veto is decisive because it checks both the source’s best-response inequality and the administrator’s zero-radius equilibrium condition.
