The existence of differences in costs in different establishments in an industry is proof, when not due to differences in accounting practice, that the competitive adjustment is imperfect." The proof is conditional on observing accounting practice, which is itself difficult to verify, so the footnote shifts an empirical burden onto the researcher that the text does not resolve. The same footnote states that "The current conception of marginal cost necessarily falls away through the same reasoning" and that "The producer's calculations are made in terms of cost per unit and selling price per unit" [PAGE 1]. This creates a determinacy tension: if marginal cost falls away as an operative concept, the page offers no explicit rule for the output margin, relying instead on average unit-cost parity.

Finally, the chapter functions as a static baseline. Because Knight's book is ultimately a theory of profit under uncertainty, the zero-profit identity on [PAGE 1]—where costs, distributive shares, and prices are "different names for the total income of the society"—is the equilibrium foil against which uncertainty is later introduced. Reading this page as a complete description of real, dynamic markets is a category error: it describes a long-run tendency, not a short-run price-determination mechanism.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The page sits at the intersection of classical political economy and marginalism. The stationary-state reasoning echoes Ricardo and Mill, in which normal price gravitates toward cost of production. The graphical reference to p. 91 likely reflects Marshallian partial-equilibrium apparatus, while the treatment of productive services as agencies valued by contribution anticipates Walrasian general equilibrium and marginal-productivity distribution theory [PAGE 1]. Knight's distinctive move is to use this static, zero-profit configuration as the baseline from which uncertainty-generated profit is later derived; equilibrium here is a counterfactual reference point, not a historical endpoint.

Multi-week traps. First, importing Knight's constant-cost assumption into short-run industry analysis when a good is actually a considerable fraction of demand for specialized services mis-specifies supply elasticity [PAGE 1]. Second, treating "long-time normal price" as a short-run forecasting device ignores the footnote's warning that unit-cost equality requires perfect competitive adjustment [PAGE 1]. Third, collapsing Knight's later distinction between insurable risk and true uncertainty into a single probability calculus commits a category error: this page describes the pre-uncertainty equilibrium, so any model that calibrates risk parameters here and projects them into Knight's uncertainty theory misreads the argument across weeks of model development.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under stress, producers regress to the accounting habit Knight explicitly describes: calculating "in terms of cost per unit and selling price per unit" [PAGE 1]. In a downturn, the operative decision rule becomes whether p covers AC(q), not whether p equals long-run marginal contribution. Firms can persist in production while p ≥ AC(q) even when static-equilibrium conditions are violated, generating output and employment hysteresis.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is to infer from the aggregate identity—costs equal distributive shares equal prices, "different names for the total income of the society" [PAGE 1]—that profit cannot exist as a residual. Knight's broader argument is that uncertainty profit emerges precisely when this static equivalence fails in real time. A modeler who assumes the identity holds period-by-period will systematically underestimate entrepreneurial profit and overestimate competitive stability.

Clock. The long-run tendency is clock-like only in logical time. In calendar time, productive services are hired under multi-week contracts and technological adjustment lags output decisions. Cost conditions observed this week reflect services committed weeks earlier, so the equilibrium price p = AC(q) is a moving target. The discrepancy between the clock of contractual input fixation and the clock of market price clearing is the temporal wedge in which Knightian uncertainty operates.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto. Productive services include factors with hard physical upper bounds (land, specialized capital). If limited-agency supply S_L reaches S_max, output q cannot expand without asymptotically rising cost; the constant-cost regime for negligible-demand goods is vetoed once q approaches physical capacity [PAGE 1]. Formal veto predicate: V_phys = 1 if q ≥ q_phys(S_max), where q_phys is the maximum feasible output flow [units of good per time].

Biomechanical layer veto. If production relies on human labor, physiological bounds on work hours, recovery, and skill acquisition introduce non-convexities and fixed time costs not represented in Knight's smooth cost function, vetoing the assumption that agencies can be varied proportionally without limit.

Weather layer veto. For weather-exposed production, cost is C(q, ω(t)) where ω(t) is a non-stationary weather process. A single long-time normal price p = AC(q) is undefined when weather shocks shift the cost curve unpredictably [PAGE 1]. Formal veto predicate: V_weather = 1 if the variance of ω(t) over the production cycle exceeds the bandwidth around which normal price is defined.

Adjudication. Knight's static equilibrium is admitted only if V_phys = 0, V_biomechanical = 0, and V_weather = 0 simultaneously [PAGE 1]. Any binding physical or stochastic layer collapses the cost-per-unit equivalence and invalidates the long-run tendency as a short-run prediction.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1. Verifiable condition: an industry produces a good that is a negligible fraction of demand for productive services, so [PAGE 1] implies practically constant cost. Counterfactual intervention: a regional weather shock destroys a fixed share of the limited agency (e.g., frost damaging orchard land). Prediction: the cost function shifts from ∂AC/∂q ≈ 0 to a kinked, near-vertical segment at reduced capacity; market price rises above prior AC and output falls below the pre-shock level. The constant-cost equilibrium is vetoed; verifiable via crop reports and wholesale price series.

Scenario 2. Verifiable condition: Knight's footnote holds that cross-establishment cost differences, "when not due to differences in accounting practice," prove imperfect competitive adjustment [PAGE 1]. Counterfactual intervention: an industry exhibits apparent cost dispersion solely because of divergent depreciation accounting methods, while true economic costs are identical. Prediction: prices converge to a common unit cost despite reported AC differences, and high-reported-cost establishments do not contract output. The footnote's inference from cost differences to imperfect competition is vetoed; verifiable by reconciling financial accounts to economic cost.

Scenario 3. Verifiable condition: Knight asserts "The producer's calculations are made in terms of cost per unit and selling price per unit" and that marginal cost "falls away" [PAGE 1]. Counterfactual intervention: firms in the industry actually use marginal-cost pricing and shut down when p < MC even if p > AC. Prediction: output contracts at price levels where Knight's unit-cost rule predicts continued operation; the observed shutdown point is determined by MC, not AC. The average-cost behavioral assumption is vetoed; verifiable via firm-level pricing surveys and shutdown microdata.

Each scenario is falsifiable with industry cost data, weather records, or firm-level behavioral observations, and each produces a sharp directional prediction that contradicts a specific clause of [PAGE 1].
