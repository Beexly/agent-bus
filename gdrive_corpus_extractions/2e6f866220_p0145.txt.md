<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The argument sits in the Radon–Nikodym lineage: Lebesgue’s differentiation and integration (1902–1910), Radon’s 1913 theorem for \(\sigma\)-finite measures, Nikodym’s 1930 extension to finite signed measures, and Halmos’s 1950 textbook presentation in §31 of *Measure Theory* [PAGE 1]. Theorem A invoked in the contradiction is the Hahn decomposition theorem (Hahn, 1913) restated for the signed residual \(\nu_0\); the maximality device traces to the Fréchet–Riesz representation style of proof later abstracted in the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem. The finite-partition lattice trick (\(g_n=\max f_j\) on a disjoint measurable partition) is a standard device proving that the admissible class is closed under finite maxima, which is what allows the monotone sequence \(g_n\uparrow f_0\) to remain inside \(\mathcal K\) and invoke §27.B [PAGE 1].

Multi-week traps embedded in this page: (i) a reader who regresses to finite additivity will try to verify \(\nu_0(E_1\cup E_2)=\nu_0(E_1)+\nu_0(E_2)\) only for disjoint sets and miss that countable additivity of \(\nu_0\) is what makes “identically zero” a global statement; (ii) the symbol \([\mu]\) is routinely read as “everywhere” rather than “\(\mu\)-almost everywhere,” breaking the step \(f_0=f\,[\mu]\); (iii) item (2a)’s reference measure is easy to misread because the OCR shows “\(\mu=\mu+\nu\)” [PAGE 1] where the intended \(\lambda=\mu+\nu\) yields the sharp bound \(0\le d\nu/d\lambda<1\), whereas \(d\nu/d\mu\) need not be \(<1\) (e.g. \(\nu=2\mu\) gives density 2); (iv) item (2b) is truncated at the page edge, so the exact quantifier (“for every nonnegative measurable \(g\)”) and its conclusion are only partially recoverable from [PAGE 1].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: When a reader stalls on the Halmos proof, the habitual regression is to verify pointwise inequalities \(f_n\le f_{n+1}\) or to recompute finite Riemann sums, treating the supremum \(\alpha\) as a limit of ordinary calculus rather than a lattice supremum in \(L^1(\mu)\). That regress fails because the proof is not constructive pointwise; it is order-theoretic, relying on the closure of \(\mathcal K\) under finite maxima and on monotone convergence (§27.B) [PAGE 1].

Counter-trap: The correct recursive move is to inspect the strictness of the perturbation. The contradiction is not \(\int g\,d\mu\ge\alpha\); it is \(\int g\,d\mu=\alpha+\varepsilon\mu(A)>\alpha\), which is possible only because \(\mu(A)>0\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). If a reader assumes \(\nu_0\neq0\) but cannot produce an \(A\) with \(\mu(A)>0\), the counter-trap is precisely the singular case: the residual lives on a \(\mu\)-null set, Theorem A’s geometric alternative fails, and the Radon–Nikodym derivative cannot be obtained by this maximality argument alone.

Clock: There is no physical time variable, but there is a proof-theoretic “clock”: the increasing sequence \(g_n\uparrow f_0\) indexed by \(\mathbb N\), whose limit is justified by monotone convergence, and whose associated real numbers \(\int g_n\,d\mu\uparrow\alpha\) form a Cauchy-convergent bounded sequence. The recursion terminates at the contradiction step, not at an asymptotic fixed point; the “clock” is the well-ordered index set \(\mathbb N\) plus the completeness of \(\mathbb R\) bounding \(\alpha\).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather layer: null. The content of [PAGE 1] is pure measure theory on an abstract measurable space \((X,\mathcal S,\mu)\). There are no bodies, no forces, no atmospheric variables, and no biomechanical degrees of freedom; consequently no physical, biomechanical, or weather veto can be engaged. The only admissible vetoes are measure-theoretic and exact:

Veto 1 (measure annihilation): \(\mu(A)=0\) removes the strict gain \(\varepsilon\mu(A)>0\), so the perturbation \(g=f+\varepsilon\mathbf1_A\) cannot beat the supremum. This veto fires exactly when the residual measure is carried on a \(\mu\)-null set.

Veto 2 (absolute-continuity failure): \(\nu\not\ll\mu\) yields a nonzero singular part \(\nu_s\perp\mu\); then \(\nu_0\) is not identically zero but no set \(A\) with \(\mu(A)>0\) can support the one-sided inequality required by Theorem A, so the contradiction is unreachable.

Veto 3 (finiteness/compactness failure): \(\alpha=\infty\) or \(\nu\) not totally finite destroys the ordered supremum structure; \(f_0\) may fail to be integrable, so the passage to a finite-valued \(f\) with \(f_0=f\,[\mu]\) [PAGE 1] breaks down.

Adjudication: since the physical/biomechanical/weather veto set is empty, the mathematical veto set above is the complete adjudication layer. The proof is valid iff \(\mu\) is \(\sigma\)-finite (or at least the relevant measures are finite), \(\nu\ll\mu\), \(\alpha<\infty\), and the measure space is complete enough that \([\mu]\)-equivalence classes are well behaved. All other regimes are singularity/breakdown regimes in which the theorem’s conclusion either requires a different proof (Lebesgue decomposition) or is false.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions:

Scenario 1 — Non-\(\sigma\)-finite counting measure. Let \(X\) be uncountable, \(\mu\) the counting measure, \(\nu=\mu\). Then \(\nu\ll\mu\), but \(\mu\) is not \(\sigma\)-finite. The constant function \(f\equiv1\) lies in \(\mathcal K\) with \(\int_X f\,d\mu=\mu(X)=\infty\), so \(\alpha=\infty\) and there is no finite supremum to contradict; the integrability step producing finite-valued \(f\) with \(f_0=f\,[\mu]\) [PAGE 1] fails. Prediction: the maximality proof as written halts at the assumption \(\alpha<\infty\); the Radon–Nikodym derivative \(d\nu/d\mu=1\) exists but is not certified by this argument. Verifiable check: compute \(\sup\{\int f\,d\mu:f\in\mathcal K\}\) directly; it is \(+\infty\), so no contradiction of the form \(\alpha+\varepsilon\mu(A)>\alpha\) can arise.

Scenario 2 — Singular measure (Dirac vs Lebesgue). Let \(\mu\) be Lebesgue measure on \([0,1]\), \(\nu=\delta_0\). Then \(\nu\not\ll\mu\). For every \(E\) not containing \(0\), \(\nu(E)=0\), forcing \(f=0\) on \(E\); since \(\{0\}\) is \(\mu\)-null, \(f=0\,[\mu]\) and \(\alpha=0\). The residual \(\nu_0=\delta_0\neq0\), but there is no \(A\) with \(\mu(A)>0\) satisfying the Theorem-A lower bound \(\nu_0(E)\ge\varepsilon\mu(E)\), because \(\nu_0\) sees only the \(\mu\)-null point. Prediction: the hypothesis “\(\nu_0\) not identically zero \(\Rightarrow\) \(\exists\varepsilon>0,A,\mu(A)>0\) ...” from [PAGE 1] has an empty witness set; the proof cannot rule out \(\nu_0\), correctly signalling the necessity of \(\nu\ll\mu\). Verifiable check: exhibit \(E=\{0\}\); \(\nu_0(E)=1\) while \(\mu(E)=0\), so no \(\varepsilon>0\) can satisfy the bound for all \(E\).

Scenario 3 — Reference-measure misreading in item (2a). OCR shows “\(\mu=\mu+\nu\)” [PAGE 1], but the mathematically consistent reading is \(\lambda=\mu+\nu\). Take \(\mu=\) Lebesgue on \([0,1]\), \(\nu=2\mu\). Then \(\lambda=3\mu\) and \(f=d\nu/d\lambda=2/3\), so \(0\le f<1\,[\lambda]\) holds. If a counterfactual reader instead enforces the bound with respect to \(\mu\), then \(d\nu/d\mu=2\not<1\), falsifying the stated inequality. Prediction: the bound \(0\le f<1\) is valid only against \(\lambda=\mu+\nu\), not \(\mu\); swapping the reference measure is a veto that invalidates item (2a) as OCR-misread. Verifiable check: compute \(\int_E 2\,d\mu=2\mu(E)\le\nu(E)=2\mu(E)\) with equality, so \(f=2\) is admissible w.r.t. \(\mu\) but violates \(f<1\); this confirms the reference measure in [PAGE 1] must be \(\lambda=\mu+\nu\) for the theorem to be true.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
