\subsection*{1. Normal Form Games (NFG) and Mixed-Strategy Profiles}
\textbf{Definition 1 [PAGE 17]:} A Normal Form Game is defined as the tuple $G = \langle P, (S_i)_{i \in P}, (u_i)_{i \in P} \rangle$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P = \{1, 2\}$ is the finite set of players ($|P| = 2$ for bimatrix games: kicker and goalkeeper), indexed by $i \in P$.
    \item $S_i$ is the non-empty, finite set of pure strategies for player $i$ (e.g., $S_{\text{kicker}} = \{N, NN\}$ or $\{L, C, R\}$).
    \item $u_i : \prod_{j \in P} S_j \to \mathbb{R}$ is the utility (payoff) function mapping joint pure action profiles $(s_1, s_2) \in S_1 \times S_2$ to real-valued payoffs (empirical scoring probabilities $p_{\text{score}} \in [0, 1]$, which are dimensionless).
\end{itemize}
A mixed strategy $\sigma_i \in \Delta(S_i)$ is a probability distribution over $S_i$ lying in the $(|S_i|-1)$-dimensional probability simplex:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta(S_i) = \left\{ \sigma_i \in [0, 1]^{|S_i|} \;\middle|\; \sum_{s_i \in S_i} \sigma_i(s_i) = 1 \right\}.
\end{equation}
In bimatrix games, payoffs are stored in matrices $A, B \in [0, 1]^{|S_1| \times |S_2|}$, where $A_{s_1, s_2} = u_1(s_1, s_2)$ and $B_{s_1, s_2} = u_2(s_1, s_2)$ [PAGE 17, Figure 6].

\subsection*{2. Nash Equilibrium and $\epsilon$-Nash Equilibrium}
\textbf{Definition 2 [PAGE 17, Equation (1)]:} A strategy profile $\sigma^* = (\sigma_1^*, \sigma_2^*)$ is a Nash equilibrium if and only if:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[u_i(\sigma_i^*, \sigma_{-i}^*)\right] \ge \mathbb{E}\left[u_i(s_i', \sigma_{-i}^*)\right] \quad \forall i \in P, \; \forall s_i' \in S_i.
\end{equation}
Expanding the expectation over the mixed strategy $\sigma_{-i}^*$, for a $2 \times 2$ bimatrix game with payoff matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{pmatrix}$, the indifference condition equates the expected utility of row actions $s_1 \in \{1, 2\}$ against the column player's mixed strategy $\sigma_2^* = (q, 1-q)^\top$:
\begin{equation}
    q a_{11} + (1-q) a_{12} = q a_{21} + (1-q) a_{22} \implies q^* = \frac{a_{22} - a_{12}}{(a_{11} - a_{12}) + (a_{22} - a_{21})}.
\end{equation}
\textbf{Definition 3 [PAGE 18, Equation (2)]:} A strategy profile $\sigma^*$ is an $\epsilon$-Nash equilibrium if there exists $\epsilon > 0$ (dimensionless probability mass, expressed as a percentage in [PAGE 20, 26]) such that:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[u_i(\sigma_i^*, \sigma_{-i}^*)\right] \ge \mathbb{E}\left[u_i(s_i', \sigma_{-i}^*)\right] - \epsilon \quad \forall i \in P, \; \forall s_i' \in S_i.
\end{equation}
Formally, the minimal $\epsilon$ for a profile $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)$ is the maximum unilateral gain from deviation:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \max_{i \in P} \left( \max_{s_i' \in S_i} u_i(s_i', \sigma_{-i}) - u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}) \right).
\end{equation}

\subsection*{3. Information-Theoretic Divergence: Jensen-Shannon Divergence ($D_{\text{JS}}$)}
To quantify the distance between two discrete probability distributions $P = (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ and $Q = (q_1, \dots, q_k)$ over the $k$ pure strategy actions (e.g., $k=2$ or $k=3$ or $k=6$), the paper uses the Jensen-Shannon divergence [PAGE 20, 21, 22, 24, 26]:
\begin{equation}
    D_{\text{JS}}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M), \quad \text{where } M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q),
\end{equation}
and $D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) = \sum_{i=1}^k p_i \ln\left(\frac{p_i}{m_i}\right)$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence (in nats; or $\log_2$ for bits, where $0 \le D_{\text{JS}} \le \ln 2 \approx 0.6931\text{ nats}$).
\textit{Units and Breakdown:} Reported as percentages on [PAGE 20] ($0.84\%$ Nash, $1.2\%$ empirical, $0.087\%$ per-action in Table 2d, $0.75\%$ in Table 5b, and $0.03\%$ to $0.57\%$ in Table 7). Note that raw percentage scaling of $D_{\text{JS}}$ compresses the dynamic range; a $0.57\%$ $D_{\text{JS}}$ corresponds to substantial strategy shifts in low-entropy distributions.

In the $3 \times 3$ Left/Center/Right game (Table 4b), $p_{R,R} = 0.000011$ and $p_{L,L} = 7.791136 \times 10^{-7}$ reject equivalence.

\subsection*{5. Player Vector Generation: Spatial Grid Counts and Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)}
\textbf{Pipeline [PAGE 24, 32]:}
1. \textbf{Grid Partitioning:} For player $p$ and action type $t \in \{\text{pass, dribble, shot, cross}\}$, the $105\text{ m} \times 68\text{ m}$ pitch is partitioned into a $60 \times 40$ grid ($2,400$ cells of area $1.75\text{ m} \times 1.70\text{ m} = 2.975\text{ m}^2$), producing a non-negative integer count matrix $M_{p,t} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{60 \times 40}$.
2. \textbf{NMF Compression:} Each matrix $M_{p,t}$ is vectorized to $\mathbf{m}_{p,t} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{2400}$. Stacking all player vectors for action type $t$ into $V^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{N \times 2400}$, NMF approximates:
\begin{equation}
    V^{(t)} \approx W^{(t)} H^{(t)}, \quad \text{s.t. } W^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{N \times k_t}, \; H^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{k_t \times 2400},
\end{equation}
minimizing the Frobenius norm $\min_{W, H \ge 0} \|V^{(t)} - W^{(t)} H^{(t)}\|_F^2$.
3. \textbf{Concatenation and Filtering:} Each player's compressed vectors across the 4 action types are standardized (zero mean, unit variance) and concatenated into an 18-dimensional vector $\mathbf{v}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{18}$ ($\sum_{t} k_t = 18$). Players with $< 50$ Premier League appearances (2016–2019) are filtered out, yielding $635$ vectors [PAGE 24].

\subsection*{6. K-Means Clustering and PCA Visualization}
Players are clustered into $K = 6$ clusters via K-means [PAGE 24], minimizing within-cluster inertia:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J} = \sum_{c=1}^K \sum_{\mathbf{v}_p \in \mathcal{C}_c} \|\mathbf{v}_p - \boldsymbol{\mu}_c\|_2^2, \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_c = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_c|} \sum_{\mathbf{v}_p \in \mathcal{C}_c} \mathbf{v}_p,
\end{equation}
where $K=6$ is chosen at the largest drop ("elbow") in inertia. Dimensionality reduction to 2D/3D for visualization in Figure 11 [PAGE 25] uses Principal Component Analysis (PCA).

\subsection*{7. LSTM Trajectory Prediction and L2 Loss for Counterfactual Ghosting}
\textbf{Architecture [PAGE 28, Section 5.4]:} A centralized Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network with 2 layers of 256 hidden units each ($h \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$) takes as input raw trajectories of players and the ball over a context window and outputs step-wise 2D coordinate displacements $\Delta \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1} = (\Delta \hat{x}_{t+1}, \Delta \hat{y}_{t+1}) \in \mathbb{R}^2\text{ (m/frame)}$ for defensive players:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{L2}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{T_{\text{pred}} \cdot N_{\text{def}}} \sum_{t=t_0}^{t_0 + T_{\text{pred}}} \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{def}}} \left\| \Delta \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j, t+1}(\theta) - \Delta \mathbf{x}_{j, t+1} \right\|_2^2 \quad [\text{SI units: } \text{m}^2/\text{frame}^2].
\end{equation}
\textit{Simulation Parameters [PAGE 28]:} Input length = $240$ frames at $25\text{ fps}$ ($9.6\text{ s}$ total), downsampled to $12.5\text{ fps}$ ($120$ frames, $\Delta t = 0.08\text{ s}$), split into $60$ context frames ($4.8\text{ s}$) and $60$ prediction frames ($4.8\text{ s}$). Attacking and defending roles are randomized during training to prevent role-assignment bias.

\subsection*{8. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning and Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR)}
\textbf{Hierarchical RL Formulation [PAGE 29, Figure 14]:} The paper frames football as a 3-level hierarchical RL problem with shared top-down and bottom-up context:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Action Level:} Optimize action values $Q_V(A, E_A)$ for discrete events $E_A$ (pass, cross, shot, corner kick) to score goals (e.g., VAEP [PAGE 30]).
    \item \textbf{Match Level:} Optimize values $V_M(E_M)$ of match events $E_M \in \{\text{scored, conceded, loss, draw, win}\}$ to win the match.
    \item \textbf{Championship Level:} Optimize values $V_C(E_C)$ to win the championship.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Biophysical Workload Operator (ACWR) [PAGE 16]:} For daily workload $w_\tau$ (dimensionless arbitrary units or meters of high-speed running in $\text{m}\cdot\text{week}^{-1}$):
\begin{equation}
    \text{Workload}_{\text{acute}}(t) = \sum_{\tau = t - 7\text{ d}}^{t} w_\tau, \quad \text{Workload}_{\text{chronic}}(t) = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{k=0}^{3} \text{Workload}_{\text{acute}}(t - 7k\text{ d}),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{ACWR}(t) = \frac{\text{Workload}_{\text{acute}}(t)}{\text{Workload}_{\text{chronic}}(t)} \quad [\text{dimensionless}].
\end{equation}
\textit{Breakdown Regime [PAGE 16]:} The paper notes that binary ACWR classification is limited because preventive measures cause injuries to occur infrequently (severe class imbalance), and binary thresholding fails to capture predictive uncertainty or injury severity.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of Tuyls et al. (2020) traces back across four distinct intellectual traditions that converged in late 2020 to form the football AI microcosm.

\subsection*{1. The Game-Theoretic Lineage: From Minimax at Wimbledon to Empirical Game-Theoretic Analysis (EGTA) of 22 Agents}
The formal study of sports as zero-sum games began in the early 2000s with Walker and Wooders (2001) ("Minimax play at Wimbledon," [PAGE 2, 36]) and Chiappori, Levitt, and Groseclose (2002) ("Testing mixed-strategy equilibria when players are heterogeneous: The case of penalty kicks in soccer," [PAGE 33]). Palacios-Huerta (2003, "Professionals Play Minimax," [PAGE 38]) crystallized the paradigm by analyzing 1,417 penalty kicks from 1995–2000, proving that professional kickers and goalkeepers follow the $2 \times 2$ Natural/Non-Natural mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium. Over the subsequent 17 years, two retro-causal extensions became necessary:
First, scaling the dataset by an order of magnitude ($12,399$ kicks from 2011–2017 across 58 global competitions, [PAGE 18-19]) allowed the 2020 DeepMind-Liverpool FC team to resolve a historical statistical artifact: Palacios-Huerta (2003) assumed footedness invariance in the $2 \times 2$ game. Figure 9 and Figure 10 [PAGE 22-23] prove retroactively that apparent footedness asymmetries in small samples are a finite-sample artifact of low-experience kickers, which is "trained away" in high-appearance professionals.
Second, parallel to classical NFGs, Wellman (2006, "Methods for empirical game-theoretic analysis," [PAGE 41]) and later Omidshaﬁei et al. (2019, "$\alpha$-rank: Multi-agent evaluation by evolution," [PAGE 38]) and Tuyls et al. (2020, "Bounds and dynamics for empirical game theoretic analysis," [PAGE 40]) developed EGTA to estimate meta-strategies from multi-agent simulations or large interaction datasets. Historically, sports analytics lacked the multi-agent volume for EGTA outside set pieces, creating a multi-week to multi-season trap where researchers overfitted small-sample payoff matrices. The 2020 paper breaks this trap by coupling EGTA with statistical representation learning (Frontier 1: GT & SL).

\subsection*{2. The Computer Vision and "Ghosting" Lineage: From SportVU Basketball to Broadcast Occlusions}
In 2013, Grant Lowe's documentation of SportVU optical tracking in the NBA introduced the concept of "Ghosting" ([PAGE 27, 37]: "Ghosting refers to the prescription of the trajectories the players in a sports team should have executed, in contrast to what they actually did"). Le et al. (2017, "Data-driven ghosting using deep imitation learning" and "Coordinated multi-agent imitation learning," [PAGE 36]) and Yeh et al. (2019, "Diverse generation for multi-agent sports games," [PAGE 41]) adapted deep neural networks to predict basketball player trajectories.
The retro-causal transfer to football encountered a multi-week data acquisition barrier: unlike indoor basketball with fixed overhead cameras capturing all 10 players, football is played outdoors on a $105\text{ m} \times 68\text{ m}$ pitch with 22 players, broadcast via non-overlapping camera shots [PAGE 7]. Over 2015–2020, CV pipelines (Faster-RCNN [PAGE 39] + G-RMI top-down pose estimation [PAGE 31, Figure 2]) were developed to extract keypoints from occluded broadcast video, feeding low-dimensional tracking data into LSTM trajectory models [PAGE 28].

\subsection*{3. The Statistical Valuation Lineage: From Minsky's Credit Assignment to VAEP and EPV}
Minsky (1961, "Steps toward artificial intelligence," [PAGE 36]) formalized the temporal credit assignment problem in sequential decision-making. Because football averages only $2.72\text{ goals/game}$ in the Premier League [PAGE 5], sparse terminal rewards prevent direct credit assignment to intermediate passes or off-ball runs. Decroos et al. (2019, VAEP, [PAGE 30]) and Fernández, Bornn, and Cervone (2019, Expected Possession Value, [PAGE 13]) bridged this gap by estimating state-action value functions, while Decroos and Davis (2019, "Player vectors," [PAGE 34]) introduced NMF-compressed spatial player embeddings.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive Theory of Mind (ToM)—the hierarchical nesting of agent beliefs about the beliefs, strategies, and future actions of other agents—is the core cognitive and algorithmic engine behind Frontier 1 (GT & SL), Frontier 3 (GT & CV), and the Automated Video Assistant Coach (AVAC) vision. We dissect recursive ToM across three regimes: (1) dyadic zero-sum mentalization in penalty kicks, (2) cluster-conditioned higher-order modeling, and (3) multi-week strategic counter-traps between opposing coaching staffs and AI systems.

\subsection*{1. Dyadic Mentalization and Habit Regression in Penalty Kicks}
In the penalty kick scenario [PAGE 10, 20], both the kicker ($i = S$) and goalkeeper ($i = G$) engage in simultaneous-move recursive reasoning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level-0 (Zero-Order Strategy):} Play a fixed unconditional action frequency (e.g., a kicker always shooting to their natural side $P(N) = 1.0$). In a zero-sum game, a pure Level-0 strategy is strictly exploitable if observed by an opponent.
    \item \textbf{Level-1 (First-Order ToM):} The kicker models the goalkeeper's base dive distribution $\hat{\sigma}_G^{(1)}$ and best-responds. The goalkeeper models the kicker's natural-side bias $\hat{\sigma}_S^{(1)}$ and adjusts dive probability toward the natural side.
    \item \textbf{Level-2+ (Recursive Fixed-Point / Nash Equilibrium $\sigma^*$):} In a symmetric zero-sum game, the unique fixed point of infinite recursion is the Minimax / Nash equilibrium $\sigma^* = (\sigma_S^*, \sigma_G^*)$ [PAGE 17].
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Empirical Audit of Recursive Collapse:} The 12,399-kick dataset on [PAGE 20, Table 2d] demonstrates that elite professionals have effectively internalized infinite-order recursion, converging to $\sigma^*$ with $D_{\text{JS}} = 0.087\%$ and $\epsilon = 0.4\%$. However, the 3-action ($L, C, R$) breakdown on [PAGE 21-22] reveals a massive **Crisis/Habit Regression Trap** at the goalkeeper node:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nash equilibrium prescribes the goalkeeper stay in the center ($C\text{-}G$) with probability $\sigma_G^*(C) = 0.178$, exploiting the kicker's center shot ($C\text{-}S$) at a Nash frequency of $\sigma_S^*(C) = 0.116$.
    \item In reality, kickers rarely shoot down the center ($\hat{\sigma}_S(C) = 0.061$) because of the psychological/physical habit of striking with power/placement to the sides. Reading the kicker's Level-0/Level-1 center-avoidance habit, goalkeepers regress to a Level-1 heuristic of never staying central ($\hat{\sigma}_G(C) = 0.089$), leaving the $(C\text{-}S, C\text{-}G)$ cell completely unguarded.
\end{itemize}
Here, the goalkeeper's ToM model of the kicker ($\text{ToM}_G(\text{kicker})$) assumes the kicker is a habitual Level-0 side-shooter rather than a Level-2 Nash player who could exploit an open goalmouth center by softly panenka-chipping the ball into $C\text{-}G$.

\subsection*{2. Cluster-Conditioned ToM and the Clock/Score Trap in Live Play}
Extending ToM to the Premier League 2016–2019 Player Vector clusters [PAGE 23-27] shows that recursive mentalization is conditioned on opponent identity (clustering):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Kickers in Cluster 1 (86.2% success rate, 167 shots) play very close to the Nash equilibrium of their cluster ($\epsilon = 0.08\%$, $\sigma_{\text{kicker}}^* = (0.423, 0.577)$ vs. $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{emp}} = (0.485, 0.515)$).
    \item By contrast, goalkeepers facing Cluster 2, 4, and 5 kickers over-diverge toward the kickers' non-natural sides: $\hat{\sigma}_G(NN\text{-}G) = 0.629$ (Cluster 1), $0.582$ (Cluster 2), $0.562$ (Cluster 4), and $0.600$ (Cluster 5), whereas Nash recommends $(0.621, 0.570, 0.625, 0.683)$ [PAGE 26, Table 9]. This induces a Level-2 counter-trap where kickers in Clusters 2, 4, and 5 continue shooting to their natural side ($\hat{\sigma}_S(N) \approx 0.48 - 0.52$) and achieve sub-optimal scoring rates ($73.3\% - 80.7\%$) because their in-game ToM fails to realize how far the opposing goalkeeper's Level-1 dive bias has shifted toward $NN\text{-}G$.
\end{itemize}
In live play [PAGE 15], the authors highlight an explicit temporal **Game Clock & Score ToM Trap**: trajectory prediction models trained via imitation learning treat the game as a memoryless black-box dynamical process conditioned solely on on-pitch coordinates:
"such models do not yet account for the game-theoretically driven counterfactual responses of players to one another (e.g., taking into account the current game score, time remaining, relative strength of the two teams, impact of current game decisions on upcoming matches, etc.)" [PAGE 15].
When a team is leading $1-0$ in the 88th minute ($t_{\text{rem}} = 2\text{ min}$), a Level-0 trajectory model predicts league-average pressing, failing to model the opponent's Level-2 strategic shift to high-risk committing of defenders forward (an extensive-form game with asymmetric terminal utilities).

In the GSE + GSI Frontier Foundry, a **Cross-Layer Physical/Biomechanical/Weather Veto** occurs when a higher-layer strategic or AI recommendation (Layer 2: Predictive/Strategic Prescriptions, or Layer 3: Human Factors Models) violates strict lower-layer physical, biomechanical, or environmental conservation laws. Below we prove four mathematical veto bounds derived from the source text and physical reality.

\subsection*{Veto 1: The Spatiotemporal Nyquist-Sampling & Locomotor Kinematic Veto in LSTM Ghosting}
\textbf{Source Context [PAGE 7, 9, 28]:} The trajectory model on [PAGE 28] uses tracking data downsampled from $25\text{ fps}$ ($\Delta t_{\text{raw}} = 0.04\text{ s}$) to $12.5\text{ fps}$ ($\Delta t_{\text{sample}} = 0.08\text{ s}$). Context and prediction use $60$ frames each ($4.8\text{ s}$ per window).
\textbf{Physical Proof:} An elite outfield footballer has a maximum sprint velocity $v_{\max} \approx 9.5\text{ m/s}$ ($\approx 34.2\text{ km/h}$) and maximum ground reaction acceleration $a_{\max} \approx 6.0\text{ m/s}^2$. Over a single sampled frame step $\Delta t_{\text{sample}} = 0.08\text{ s}$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta x_{\max} = v_{\max} \cdot \Delta t_{\text{sample}} = 9.5\text{ m/s} \times 0.08\text{ s} = 0.76\text{ m}.
\end{equation}
In broadcast football video [PAGE 7], players frequently occlude one another ("multiple occlusions of players by one another"). If a defender sprints laterally across an occluded attacker during the $0.08\text{ s}$ sampling gap, the $L_2$ trajectory loss on [PAGE 28] assumes Euclidean interpolation $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_t + \Delta \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}$. Because human motion has bounded jerk $\dddot{x} \le 25\text{ m/s}^3$, any unconstrained generative model (Frontier 3, [PAGE 10-11]) that synthesizes video by interpolating at $12.5\text{ fps}$ without kinematic regularization will physically veto non-physical velocities $\|\Delta \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}\|_2 / \Delta t > 9.5\text{ m/s}$.

\subsection*{Veto 2: The Penalty Kick Reaction-Time & Ball-Kinematics Biomechanical Veto}
\textbf{Source Context [PAGE 9, 10, 18-20]:} In the penalty kick game, the kicker's ball placement and the goalkeeper's dive are modeled as simultaneous moves.
\textbf{Physical Proof:} A regulation penalty kick is taken at $D = 11.0\text{ m}$ from the goal line. Empirical ball speeds for professional penalty kicks range from $v_{\text{ball}} \in [25, 32]\text{ m/s}$ ($90 - 115\text{ km/h}$). The physical time of flight $T_{\text{flight}}$ from foot contact to crossing the goal plane is:
\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{flight}} = \frac{D}{v_{\text{ball}}} \in \left[\frac{11.0\text{ m}}{32\text{ m/s}}, \frac{11.0\text{ m}}{25\text{ m/s}}\right] = [0.344\text{ s}, \; 0.440\text{ s}] \quad [\text{SI units: s}].
\end{equation}
Subtract the goalkeeper's visual-motor neuromuscular reaction latency $\tau_{\text{react}} \in [0.180\text{ s}, 0.250\text{ s}]$ and limb swing execution time $\tau_{\text{swing}} \approx 0.150\text{ s}$. The available decision window is:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta T_{\text{decision}} = T_{\text{flight}} - \tau_{\text{react}} - \tau_{\text{swing}} \le 0.344 - 0.180 - 0.150 = 0.014\text{ s}.
\end{equation}
\textbf{Veto Adjudication:} No goalkeeper can visually track the ball's mid-flight trajectory after foot contact and physically redirect their center of mass to the opposite side. Therefore, any AI pose-estimation intent-prediction model [PAGE 9-10, Figure 2] that attempts to predict kick direction solely from ball departure frames ($t > t_{\text{contact}}$) is physically vetoed. Intent signatures must be extracted during the kicker's pre-contact run-up ($t < t_{\text{contact}}$).

\subsection*{Veto 3: The Pitch-Control vs. L2-Trajectory-Loss Misspecification Veto}
\textbf{Source Context [PAGE 9, 28]:} The paper combines LSTM trajectory predictions trained with an $L_2$ loss ($\ell_2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum \|\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \mathbf{y}\|_2^2$) with pitch control models (Spearman, 2016 [PAGE 9]).
\textbf{Physical Proof:} In multi-agent trajectory prediction, defenders' positions $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{22 \times 2}$ are correlated through physical pitch control constraints (non-overlapping player volumes $\pi r_{\text{body}}^2 \approx \pi (0.35\text{ m})^2 = 0.385\text{ m}^2$). When the training script on [PAGE 28] states: "we used the l2-loss on the tracking data for training, and randomized the order of attacking and defending players to avoid the role-assignment problem," randomizing player order breaks permutation-equivariant spatial volume exclusion. Under $L_2$ regression, predicting the conditional mean of a multimodal interaction (e.g., a 50/50 physical 1v1 duel where either Player A or Player B wins the ball) places the predicted mean position directly inside the physical body of the opponent, violating impenetrability. Furthermore, the court/pitch control probability $P_{\text{control}}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ [PAGE 9] sets a hard upper bound on pass completion; any strategic recommendation (Frontier 1) that prescribes a pass into a zone with pitch control probability below the opponent's intercept threshold is physically vetoed.

\textbf{Source Context [PAGE 3, 7, 16]:} The paper notes on [PAGE 3] that "Football takes place under far less controllable settings than other sports due to its outdoor and highly dynamic nature, a larger pitch, a large number of players involved, a low number of player changes and longer non-interrupted game sequences."

Consequently, counterfactual models trained on dry-weather league matches suffer an out-of-distribution physical veto when extrapolated to wet or high-altitude conditions.
