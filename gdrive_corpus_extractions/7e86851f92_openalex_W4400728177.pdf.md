<THINK_TRACE>
The source is not a play-calling or forecasting artifact; it is a methodological audit of how sports-analytics indicators and the models beneath them should be evaluated. The multi-hop chain is: raw sensing and annotation data produce features; features train a probabilistic or regression model; the model produces state values or event probabilities; those outputs are aggregated into an indicator; the indicator informs a practitioner decision. The paper explicitly rejects collapsing these hops: “we draw a distinction between evaluating the developed indicators themselves versus the underlying models that power them” [PAGE 1 of 34]. A correct analysis therefore must ask four separate questions: whether the data partition respects dependency structure, whether the model is calibrated and robust, whether the indicator is reliable and useful, and whether verification reveals behavior outside the observed data.

Hop one is dependency. Random cross-validation assumes exchangeability of examples, but the paper states that “sports data are often not independent and identically distributed” [PAGE 11 of 34]. Temporal ordering means sessions, matches, and events are correlated; subject consistency means the same athlete or team appears repeatedly. The remedy is group-aware partitioning: one must “force all data from one subject to be either in the training set or the testing set but not both” [PAGE 12 of 34], and temporal partitions must use non-overlapping periods or walk-forward splits. If a model trained with random folds claims generalization to new athletes, venues, competitions, or genders, the gray-zone audit fails unless subject-level, venue-level, or leave-one-competition-out partitions were used.

Hop two is action valuation. The central operator is \(C(s_i,a_i)=V(s_{i+1})-V(s_i)\), where “V(.) is the value of a game state and si+1 is the game state that results from executing action ai in game state si” [PAGE 7 of 34]. This is not a causal counterfactual unless \(V\) is a causal model. The paper later warns that counterfactual explanations from non-causal models are not causal counterfactuals [PAGE 24 of 34]. Thus an action-value indicator inherits the assumptions of its state representation, its value model, and its action-boundary annotations.

Hop three is metric choice. The paper distinguishes classification metrics, proper scoring rules, calibration, and regression metrics. The key methodological point is that Brier score and logarithmic loss aggregate errors differently: “the Brier score combines individual prediction errors by summing them while the logarithmic loss combines individual prediction errors by multiplying them” [PAGE 21 of 34]. Consequently, “we recommend using the Brier score to build a predictive model if the resulting probabilities will be summed” [PAGE 21 of 34], which matters for action-value indicators that sum or difference probabilities. But proper scoring rules alone are insufficient: “Proper scoring rules might not be sufficient to evaluate if a model is calibrated since a high discriminative power can obscure a poor calibration” [PAGE 21 of 34].

Hop four is verification. Standard test metrics aggregate over observed data; verification queries the model over constrained regions of instance space. The paper states that “a verification algorithm either constructs one or more instances that satisfy the constraints, or it proves that no such instance exists” [PAGE 24 of 34]. This is the strongest audit operator in the source because it can expose label bias and model artifacts that aggregate metrics miss. The two worked examples are decisive. In the xG audit, verification generated high-xG shots from outside the penalty box; checking a 5 m square touching the corner found “11 shots and 8 goals, yielding an extremely high 72% conversion rate” [PAGE 24 of 34]. The paper interprets this as “unexpected labeling behavior by the human annotators” [PAGE 24 of 34], because annotators likely label an action as a shot only when it results in a goal or save. In the VAEP audit, verification fixed all features except match time and found that “about 27 min into the first half the probability of scoring dramatically spikes” [PAGE 25 of 34]. Aggregate calibration can be fine while local behavior is unacceptable.

The gray-zone audit must also list known non-identifiabilities: no ground truth for tactical plans or player ratings [PAGE 9 of 34], credit assignment ambiguity [PAGE 9 of 34], noisy labels and subjective qualifiers [PAGE 10 of 34], small samples [PAGE 10 of 34], non-stationarity [PAGE 10 of 34], counterfactual defense evaluation [PAGE 10 of 34], and interventions by staff that alter training and selection [PAGE 11 of 34]. Any claim that ignores these is not merely imprecise; it is under-identified. The audit therefore requires a written partition scheme, a stated metric and calibration check, a context-stratification statement, and a verification query log. If any is absent, the claim should be vetoed or downgraded to exploratory.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides formal operators for action value, classification evaluation, probabilistic scoring, regression evaluation, and verification. I preserve the paper’s notation where possible and state units and breakdown regimes.

Action-value operator. For a game state \(s_i\), action \(a_i\), and state value \(V\), the paper defines
\[
C(s_i,a_i)=V(s_{i+1})-V(s_i),
\]
with the source explanation: “where V(.) is the value of a game state and si+1 is the game state that results from executing action ai in game state si” [PAGE 7 of 34]. The unit of \(C\) is the unit of \(V\): dimensionless probability if \(V\) is a goal probability or win probability; points if \(V\) is expected points. Assumptions: the state representation is sufficient for the value claim; \(s_{i+1}\) is observed or imputed; the same \(V\) is applied consistently across states; and the action boundary is annotated consistently. Breakdown regimes: \(s_{i+1}\) is unobserved for defensive actions that prevent events; annotation changes the action boundary; \(V\) is misspecified or non-stationary; and the model is not causal, so \(C\) is not a causal counterfactual. The paper notes that approaches differ in how they value states, with “scoring-based and win-based” paradigms [PAGE 8 of 34], so the unit and interpretation must be stated explicitly.

Partition operator. Let \(g(i)\) be the group of example \(i\) (subject, venue, competition, or season). A valid subject-level partition enforces that all examples from the same subject are in one fold. The paper’s instruction is to “force all data from one subject to be either in the training set or the testing set but not both” [PAGE 12 of 34]. Formally, for a training set \(\mathcal{T}\) and test set \(\mathcal{S}\), one requires \(g(i)=g(j)\) to imply that \(i\) and \(j\) are on the same side of the split. A valid temporal partition requires non-overlapping periods, for example \(\max t_{\mathcal{T}} \le \min t_{\mathcal{S}}\) for a single split, or a walk-forward scheme. Breakdown regimes: random example-level folds when temporal or subject dependence exists; overlapping windows; leakage of future matches into training; and pooling competitions with different scoring rates or rules.

Classification metrics. The paper defines accuracy, precision, recall, and F1:
\[
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},
\quad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{F1}=2\times\frac{\mathrm{Precision}\times\mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\]
These are dimensionless. Precision is relevant when false positives are costly; recall when false negatives are costly; F1 when both are comparable [PAGE 19 of 34]. Breakdown regimes: precision is undefined when \(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}=0\); recall is undefined when \(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}=0\); F1 is undefined when precision and recall are both zero; accuracy is misleading under class imbalance; and all except AUROC depend on class distribution [PAGE 21 of 34].

Probabilistic scoring. The source prints
\[
\mathrm{LL}=\frac{1}{|c|}\sum_{i=1}^{|c|} y_i \log \hat p_i,
\]
and
\[
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{|c|}\sum_{i=1}^{|c|}(\hat p_i-y_i)^2,
\]
where \(|c|\) is the number of classes, \(\hat p_i\) is the forecast probability, and \(y_i\) is a binary indicator [PAGE 20 of 34]. The Brier score has squared-probability units, hence dimensionless in probability space. The printed log-loss expression lacks an explicit leading minus sign in the OCR text; conventionally a negative sign makes lower values better, and the information unit is nats if \(\log\) is natural and bits if \(\log\) is base two. The ranked probability score is
\[
\mathrm{RPS}=\frac{1}{|c|-1}\sum_{k=1}^{|c|-1}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}(\hat p_l-y_l)\right)^2,
\]
with squared-probability units and ordinal-category assumptions [PAGE 20 of 34]. Breakdown regimes: \(\hat p_i=0\) with a positive outcome causes an infinite logarithmic penalty in the conventional negative log-loss; \(|c|=1\) makes RPS undefined; Brier and RPS require mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories; and calibration must be checked with reliability diagrams or expected calibration error [PAGE 21 of 34].

Regression metrics. The paper writes error-based metrics as
\[
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(y_i-\hat y_i),
\]
with MAE using absolute error, MSE using squared error, and MAPE using absolute percentage error [PAGE 21 of 34]. MAE and MSE carry the target’s units; MAPE is dimensionless but breaks down when \(y_i=0\). The coefficient of determination is
\[
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i-\bar y)^2},
\]
which is dimensionless and can be negative for out-of-sample predictions [PAGE 22 of 34]. Breakdown regimes: \(R^2\) is undefined when all \(y_i=\bar y\); MAPE is unstable for near-zero targets; and error-based metrics and \(R^2\) answer different questions, so both should be reported.

Verification operator. Let \(\mathcal{M}\) be a learned model and \(\phi\) a constraint formula over features and predictions. Verification returns either a witness instance \(x\) satisfying \(\phi\) or a proof that no such instance exists, as the paper states: “a verification algorithm either constructs one or more instances that satisfy the constraints, or it proves that no such instance exists” [PAGE 24 of 34]. The unit is logical satisfiability, not probability. Breakdown regimes: the feature space is unbounded or the constraint set is too weak; the model is queried outside its training support; and a witness is not a causal counterfactual. The paper explicitly warns that the constructed instances “are not restricted to being in the currently available data” [PAGE 24 of 34], which is the source of both power and risk.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage in the source runs from event counting to expected value to action value to counterfactual tactics and verification. Expected goals (xG) is presented as a binary classifier over shot outcomes using features such as distance, angle, assist type, and passage of play [PAGE 2 of 34]. The lineage then moves to holistic action valuation: the paper cites VAEP, xT, and Atomic-VAEP as successive indicator families, and Figure 5 reports that “the Atomic-VAEP (Decroos, 2020) indicator is more reliable than its VAEP (Decroos et al., 2019) predecessor and xT (Singh, 2019)” [PAGE 18 of 34]. It also notes that “omitting [shots] increases an indicator’s reliability” because shots are infrequent and variable [PAGE 18 of 34]. The scoring-rule lineage includes the ranked probability score introduced by Epstein in 1969 [PAGE 20 of 34], Brier score and logarithmic loss, and calibration work such as reliability diagrams and expected calibration error [PAGE 21 of 34]. The verification lineage includes Veritas and tree-ensemble verification [PAGE 24 of 34], and the paper frames reasoning about model behavior as a needed complement to aggregate metrics [PAGE 26 of 34].

This genealogy creates a retrocausal trap: later indicators are often validated against earlier labels, but those labels may be endogenous to the earlier data-collection regime. The xG corner example is the clearest proof. Verification surfaced high-xG shots from corner regions; a 5 m square touching the corner contained “11 shots and 8 goals, yielding an extremely high 72% conversion rate” [PAGE 24 of 34]. The paper attributes this to “unexpected labeling behavior by the human annotators” [PAGE 24 of 34], because annotators likely record a shot only when it produces a goal or save. A later model that learns from those labels can appear to validate against them while encoding the annotation policy. The retrocausal audit therefore requires checking whether the target label is a natural outcome, a human qualifier, or a post-hoc selection.

Multi-week traps are explicit in the source. Sports is non-stationary: “data that is more than one or two seasons old may not be relevant” [PAGE 10 of 34]. Team style changes with personnel; player skill changes with age and training; tactics evolve. The paper adds that “there has been little work on investigating if data goes out of date” [PAGE 12 of 34], so retraining schedules are not settled. Within a season, “games missed by injury will be clustered” [PAGE 18 of 34], and “the difficulty of a team’s schedule is not uniformly spread over a season” [PAGE 18 of 34]. Also, “injuries and fatigue tend to accumulate over the season” [PAGE 15 of 34]. A multi-week evaluation that ignores these structures can confuse a schedule effect, an injury cluster, or an aging trend with a skill change. Walk-forward validation, season-level splits, and context stratification are therefore not optional. A second multi-week trap is annotation drift: event definitions and qualifiers can change over time [PAGE 10 of 34], so a model trained across seasons may learn a mixture of label policies. A third trap is indicator reliability: the paper recommends splitting data and correlating indicator values, but warns that chronological splitting is “likely suboptimal” for reliability because injuries cluster, schedule difficulty is uneven, and aging effects appear over long horizons [PAGE 18 of 34]. The historical lesson is that the newest metric is not automatically the most valid; it must be traced back through its label genealogy, its season window, and its retraining clock.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind here means the analyst’s model of the coach’s or practitioner’s model of the indicator. The paper is explicit that indicators must be intuitive, trusted, and contextualized, and that domain experts need to understand when an indicator is useful [PAGE 3 of 34]. A recursive audit therefore asks: what will the practitioner infer when the indicator disagrees with conventional wisdom, and what will the practitioner do after a surprising verification witness?

Crisis habit regression is the failure mode in which a surprising model output causes stakeholders to revert to counting statistics or raw outcomes. The paper warns that “counting statistics ignore a number of important contextual factors such as the amount of playing time a player receives or the pace of the game” [PAGE 14 of 34]. It also warns that high correlation with existing success indicators can miss the point: if a new indicator merely reproduces goals, salary, or playing time, it adds no new information, and some of those existing indicators are not merit-based [PAGE 16 of 34]. Crisis habit regression is understandable but wrong when it discards context. The counter-move is not to defend the model unconditionally; it is to show face validity, reliability, and context-stratified evidence. The face-validity test is a useful brake: “if a metric blatantly contradicts most conventional wisdom, there is likely a problem with it” [PAGE 16 of 34]. But face validity is strongest for offensive contributions and weaker for defense because “good defense is a collective endeavor and more heavily reliant on tactics” [PAGE 16 of 34].

Counter-trap is the opposite failure: treating every verification witness as a fatal flaw or as a new training target. The paper states that “When a reasoner identifies unexpected behaviors, there are at least two possible causes” [PAGE 26 of 34]: training-data errors that warp the decision boundary, or peculiarities of the model, learning algorithm, or representation. The xG corner cluster is likely a data-labeling cause; the VAEP time spike is likely a model-representation cause [PAGE 25 of 34]. A counter-trap occurs when an analyst sees one witness and either overfits the model to that exact instance or declares the entire indicator invalid without diagnosing the cause. The correct recursive response is to ask what the practitioner will believe after the diagnosis, then to run a targeted check: inspect the labels for the xG case, and change the time representation for the VAEP case. The paper notes that correcting a model that has learned an unwanted pattern is difficult and iterative: “this is an iterative guess-and-check approach” [PAGE 27 of 34].

Clock is the temporal frame that governs both in-match behavior and multi-week trust. In-match, the VAEP example shows that time can have an “unexpected” and “undesirable” effect: “about 27 min into the first half the probability of scoring dramatically spikes” [PAGE 25 of 34]. The paper suggests “time should probably be handled differently in the model, e.g., by discretizing it to make it less fine-grained” [PAGE 25 of 34]. Multi-week, the clock governs retraining, season boundaries, injury accumulation, and schedule difficulty. The recursive theory-of-mind adjudication is therefore: if the practitioner’s trust depends on a stable weekly meaning, the indicator must be tested on a walk-forward clock, not a random clock. If the practitioner’s trust depends on in-match plausibility, the indicator must be tested with fixed-feature time sweeps. If either clock is missing, the model’s social meaning is underdetermined, and the claim should be treated as a hypothesis rather than a settled measurement.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source gives enough physical, biomechanical, and environmental structure to define a context-veto proposition. Let \(K\) be a claim that indicator \(I\) measures a stable skill or capability. Let \(X_{\mathrm{phys}}\) denote external and internal load variables, \(X_{\mathrm{bio}}\) denote biomechanical or physiological state variables, and \(X_{\mathrm{env}}\) denote temperature, altitude, field condition, home or away status, and pitch dimensions. If \(K\) asserts context-invariance but the evaluation does not condition on \(X_{\mathrm{phys}}\), \(X_{\mathrm{bio}}\), and \(X_{\mathrm{env}}\), and those variables are known to affect the measured actions, then \(K\) is not identified. The proof is by counterexample: construct two contexts that differ only in a relevant layer and show that the indicator changes while the underlying skill is held fixed. The paper supplies multiple such mechanisms.

Physical-layer veto. The paper distinguishes external load, “the amount and type of activities an athlete performs,” from internal load, “the body’s physiological response to the activity” [PAGE 5 of 34]. It also notes that staff interventions alter training regimes and selection: “The data is observational and teams constantly make decisions that affect what is observed” [PAGE 11 of 34]. For injury-risk and load-management claims, this is a veto condition: if a player is rested because staff fear injury, the observed load and the observed injury label are both selected by the intervention. The paper says this creates “ambiguous labels” because a non-playing player may be healthy, rotated, or held out near injury [PAGE 11 of 34]. Therefore any naive causal claim from observed load to injury risk is vetoed unless the intervention policy is modeled or the comparison is within a designed stratum. The physical layer also includes fatigue, where some measures are invasive, such as blood lactate or creatine kinase [PAGE 9 of 34], so an indicator that claims to measure fatigue without validating against those measures is under-identified.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The paper notes that biomechanical analyses often require lab setups such as motion capture and force plates, and that there is interest in “performing such analyses in the wild using cheaper and more portable sensors such as inertial measurement units” [PAGE 6 of 34]. This creates a domain-shift veto: a model trained on lab-grade biomechanics cannot be transferred to portable IMU data without calibration, because the measurement error and signal content differ. Similarly, \(\mathrm{VO2Max}\) is described as a gold standard that is arduous to measure and can disrupt training, so it is measured sporadically [PAGE 6 of 34]. A claim that a submaximal proxy estimates \(\mathrm{VO2Max}\) must therefore report the validation protocol and the training-disruption constraint; otherwise the proxy may be measuring effort, fatigue, or measurement conditions rather than aerobic fitness.

Weather and environmental veto. The paper states: “Physical parameters are also affected by environmental variables. For example, outside temperature is relevant when considering body measurements like temperature and altitude difference is important to assess heart rate when running” [PAGE 14 of 34]. It also states that “Sizes of fields can vary in sports such as soccer and baseball” [PAGE 14 of 34], and that pitch control and crossing counts change with pitch dimensions [PAGE 14 of 34]. Home or away status and field condition also affect physical and technical indicators [PAGE 14 of 34]. A weather/environment veto is therefore warranted when an indicator is compared across venues or seasons without conditioning on temperature, altitude, field dimensions, or surface condition. The proof is direct: the same player can have a different heart-rate response at altitude, a different pitch-control total on a larger pitch, and a different crossing count on a wider pitch, while the underlying technical skill is unchanged. If the indicator does not stratify or adjust, the observed difference is confounded.

Tracking-layer veto. The paper adds that occlusions and low-resolution cameras produce errors in player and ball locations, and these errors propagate to speed, acceleration, and derived indicators [PAGE 10 of 34]. This is a cross-layer veto because physical load metrics often depend on tracking-derived distance and speed. If the tracking error is not quantified, a physical-load claim can be an artifact of occlusion rather than a physiological change. The adjudication rule is: physical, biomechanical, and weather layers veto a claim when the claim is context-invariant, the layer is known to affect the target, and the evaluation does not condition, stratify, or calibrate. The claim may survive as a within-stratum description, but not as a universal skill measure.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I define an RLVR-style check as a verifiable reward: a counterfactual evaluation is run, and the reward is pass only if the predicted witness, metric drop, or instability is observed under a pre-specified protocol. These are not wagering constructs; they are falsification tests.

Scenario 1: subject-level partition veto. Counterfactual: compare a model evaluated with random example-level folds against the same model evaluated with subject-level folds. Verifiable prediction: if subject consistency is present, the random-fold performance or rank stability will be higher than the subject-level result because the same athlete appears on both sides. The paper’s rule is to “force all data from one subject to be either in the training set or the testing set but not both” [PAGE 12 of 34]. Veto condition: any claim of generalization to new athletes that was validated with random folds is vetoed if the subject-level score drops materially. Pass condition: the claim reports subject-level or leave-one-subject-out results and the drop is disclosed. This is verifiable because the partition labels and the two scores are directly auditable.

Scenario 2: label-bias verification veto. Counterfactual: query an xG model for the highest-probability shots from outside the penalty box and inspect the corner region. Verifiable prediction: verification will concentrate high-xG instances near the goal, but it will also generate a corner cluster; checking a 5 m square touching the corner should recover approximately “11 shots and 8 goals, yielding an extremely high 72% conversion rate” [PAGE 24 of 34]. Veto condition: if the model assigns high xG to that corner region and the labels are produced by annotators who record shots only when they result in a goal or save, the model is encoding label selection, not shooting skill. The paper calls this “unexpected labeling behavior by the human annotators” [PAGE 24 of 34]. Pass condition: the verification witness is traced to a documented annotation rule, and the model is retrained or the region is excluded from skill claims. This is verifiable by recounting the shots and goals in the specified region.

Scenario 3: fixed-feature time-perturbation veto. Counterfactual: take a VAEP-style action sequence, fix all features except match time, and sweep time across the match. Verifiable prediction: the probability of scoring should vary smoothly or in a domain-justified way; instead, for at least one instance, the probability will show a sharp spike. The paper reports that “about 27 min into the first half the probability of scoring dramatically spikes” [PAGE 25 of 34], even though aggregate test calibration can be good [PAGE 25 of 34]. Veto condition: if a fixed-feature time sweep produces a large, non-monotone, non-domain-justified jump, the model’s robustness claim is vetoed; the paper suggests “time should probably be handled differently in the model, e.g., by discretizing it to make it less fine-grained” [PAGE 25 of 34]. Pass condition: after discretization or re-representation, the sweep is smooth or the remaining variation is explained by domain evidence. This is verifiable by rerunning the same constrained query and comparing the probability curve before and after the fix. A fourth optional check is calibration-plus-local-instability: a model can be well calibrated in aggregate while failing a local sweep, so the veto should be triggered by the local witness even when the aggregate Brier score is acceptable.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
