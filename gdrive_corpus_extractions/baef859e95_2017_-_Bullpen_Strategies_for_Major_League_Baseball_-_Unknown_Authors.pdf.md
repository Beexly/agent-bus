The paper has two coupled derivations. The first is a measurement-correction problem: raw inning-by-inning run differentials \(d_i=s_{hi}-s_{vi}\) are biased in the 9th and 10th because the home team does not bat to three outs when leading. The paper states this directly: “the negative differentials in Figure 1 for innings 9 and 10 arise because when the home team wins, it doesn’t use all of its outs in the bottom of the last inning” [PAGE 3]. The correction is Eq. (3): estimate the latent home scoring \( \hat{s}_{hi} \) by taking visitor scoring plus the probability that the inning is played times the average differential from innings 3 through 8. The authors report \(d_{ave}=0.0419\) and \(\Pr(\text{10th})=0.1973\) [PAGE 4]. That yields a corrected total home-field advantage of roughly 0.429 runs/game rather than the raw 0.133 runs/game [PAGE 1]. The first inning’s \(d_1=0.09\) runs/game [PAGE 2] is then about 21% of the corrected advantage, not 67.5% or 58% [PAGE 5]. This is a multi-hop inference: raw differential → selection/truncation bias in last innings → probability weighting → corrected denominator → revised attribution. The gray-zone is that the correction assumes the innings 3–8 average differential is a valid proxy for the unobserved home half-inning potential in innings 9–10; that is plausible but not directly observed. It also assumes extra-inning probability is stable and that the 10th-inning compilation is homogeneous despite different extra-inning lengths. The second derivation is operational: the “first-batter starter” computes an adjusted first-inning differential by mixing one batter at the inflated \(d_1\) with the remaining \(\bar{PA}_h-1\) batters at \(d_{ave}\). The paper’s Eq. (5) gives \(\hat d_1=0.053\) runs/game, a reduction of about 0.037 runs/game or 8.6% of the corrected home-field advantage [PAGE 5]. The gray-zone is causal identification: the paper’s mechanism is “cooling down” in the dugout, supported by time-to-score correlation \(R^2=0.86\) and an extra run per 90 seconds beyond six minutes [PAGE 3], but those are observational associations, not a randomized test. A second gray-zone is the rule constraint: pitching substitutions mid-at-bat are allowed only if the replaced pitcher has faced at least one complete batter or is injured [PAGE 4]. Thus the strategy is a one-complete-batter strategy, not an arbitrary mid-count hook. The third derivation is the strike-to-ball (STB) monitoring layer: \(Y=\beta_0+\beta_1 B\), where \(Y\) is strikes and \(B\) is balls. The slope is the STB coefficient, but the paper warns “hits are counted as strikes, so is it theoretically possible to have an excellent STB while still being ineffective as a pitcher” [PAGE 8]. The league fit is \(Y=1.687+1.603B\) [PAGE 11]; starters and non-starters differ, \(Y=3.824+1.534B\) versus \(Y=1.416+1.245B\) [PAGE 11]. The paper also reports an average pitcher-level STB of 1.32 [PAGE 9], lower than the league slope coefficient because low-count events dominate the fit. This is a classic aggregation artifact and a reminder that the slope is not the mean ratio. The STB layer is used for trigger points: local drops in mean STB at high pitch counts appear around pitch 100 for Lester, around 80 for Hendricks, and near 65 and 90 for Hammel [PAGE 12]. The gray-zone is sparse high-pitch-count data and role selection: relievers are removed quickly, so their observed high-pitch-count profiles are conditional on success. Also, the paper’s own caveat that local drops require “a grain of salt” because pitchers may regress to the mean [PAGE 8] blocks a naive deterministic trigger rule.

The source defines inning-wise home scoring as \(s_{hi}=\frac{\text{total runs scored in inning }i\text{ by home teams in }n\text{ games}}{n}\), with \(n=80,554\), and similarly for visitors \(s_{vi}\), for \(i=1,\dots,10\) [PAGE 2]. The raw differential is \(d_i=s_{hi}-s_{vi}\) in runs/game [PAGE 2]. The paper reports \(d_1=0.09\) runs/game [PAGE 2] and later says this “is actually only 21% of the total home-ﬁeld advantage” [PAGE 5]. The corrected total advantage is “roughly 0.429 runs/game, rather than the 0.133 runs/game suggested by the scoring data” [PAGE 1]. The key correction is
\[
\hat{s}_{hi}=s_{vi}+\Pr(\text{inning }i\text{ is played})\left(\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=3}^{8}d_j\right),\qquad i=9,10,
\]
where the parenthetical term is \(d_{ave}\), with \(d_{ave}=0.0419\) and \(\Pr(\text{10th})=0.1973\) [PAGE 4]. Units: \(s\), \(\hat{s}\), and \(d\) are runs per game; \(\Pr(\cdot)\) is dimensionless. Assumptions: innings 3–8 are a stable proxy for latent home scoring in the last inning; extra-inning appearance is correctly weighted; the home team’s un observed bottom-half potential is not systematically different from the 3–8 average. Breakdown regimes: \(\Pr(\text{inning }i\text{ is played})=0\) makes the correction collapse to visitor scoring; a changing extra-inning selection rate biases the 10th-inning estimate; heterogeneous lineups or park effects inside innings 3–8 violate the proxy. The paper also flags an alternative error: “Several works actually make this error by calculating the number of runs they feel a source of the home-ﬁeld advantage is worth, and then simply taking a ratio against actual scoring” [PAGE 4]. The first-batter starter adjustment is
\[
\hat{d}_1=\frac{d_1}{\bar{PA}_h}+\frac{d_{ave}(\bar{PA}_h-1)}{\bar{PA}_h},
\]
with \(\bar{PA}\) defined as
\[
\bar{PA}=\frac{3}{PO}(AB+BB+IBB+HBP+SF+SH+CI),
\]
where the denominator uses put outs by the opposing team and the numerator sums batting events; the paper reports “\(\bar{PA}_h=4.30\)” and “\(\bar{PA}_v=4.21\)” [PAGE 5]. Units: \(\bar{PA}\) is plate appearances per inning; \(\hat{d}_1\) is runs/game. The source reports \(\hat{d}_1=0.053\) runs/game and a reduction of “approximately 0.037 runs/game (about 8.6%)” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: \(PO=0\) or non-baseball \(\bar{PA}_h\) values make the quotient undefined or uninterpretable; if the first batter is not subject to the full \(d_1\) penalty, the first term overstates the cost; if the effective starter is not equivalent to a home starter, the \(d_{ave}\) term is mis-specified. The STB fit is
\[
Y=\beta_0+\beta_1 B,
\]
where \(Y\) is strikes, \(B\) is balls, and \(\beta_1\) is the STB coefficient. The source gives league \(Y=1.687+1.603\,\text{Balls}\) [PAGE 11], starters \(Y=3.824+1.534\,\text{Balls}\), and non-starters \(Y=1.416+1.245\,\text{Balls}\) [PAGE 11]. It also reports “average value of 1.32” for pitcher-level STB [PAGE 9] and “STB coefﬁcient of 1.603 for the entire league” [PAGE 9]. Units: \(Y\) and \(B\) are counts; \(\beta_1\) is strikes per ball, dimensionless. Breakdown regimes: \(B\) near zero makes the intercept dominate and inflates slope estimates; high pitch counts are sparse; hits are counted as strikes, so an excellent STB can coexist with poor effectiveness [PAGE 8]. The paper’s own guardrail is that “this ratio is not the only pitching statistic that matters” [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from sabermetric adoption in MLB [PAGE 1], through Smith’s home-field work [16] and Zimmerman’s first-inning velocity/K-BB observations [19], to the 2016 postseason’s early removals of starters [PAGE 1]. The paper retroactively re-reads those results: Smith’s first-inning emphasis is corrected downward from “58%” to about 21% [PAGE 5], while the time-based mechanism—an extra run per 90 seconds beyond six minutes for the top of the first [PAGE 3]—is preserved as a causal hypothesis. The “opener” proposal from Grosnick [5] is re-engineered into the more specific “first-batter starter,” precisely because an opener who sits in the dugout during the top half would share the cooling-down problem [PAGE 4]. The forward genealogy is the paper’s future menu: closers as starters, three-inning pitchers, and “pitch once through the lineup” [PAGE 14]. Multi-week traps are embedded in this lineage. First, a club could overfit to the first-inning correction and ignore that the estimate depends on the 3–8 differential proxy. Second, a multi-week STB monitoring regime can mistake normal mean reversion for a fatigue trigger; the paper warns early superior or inferior signs “should be taken with a grain of salt” [PAGE 8]. Third, role changes over weeks alter the comparison set: a reliever used as a three-inning pitcher is no longer the same selection population as a one-inning reliever, so prior STB slopes and ERA comparisons do not transfer automatically. Fourth, opponents adapt: if the first-batter starter becomes common, hitters and managers may change first-inning preparation, and the original \(d_1\) penalty may shrink. The historical lesson is that a clever accounting correction is not the same as a robust game-theoretic strategy.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The manager’s decision is a recursive model of pitcher state, opponent adaptation, and organizational criticism. Crisis habit regression appears when a manager, under pressure, reverts to traditional roles: the closer in the ninth, the starter left in because of sunk cost, or the bullpen hierarchy regardless of the matchup. The paper acknowledges this tension: managers “still feel that their ‘gut instinct’ is more valuable at times than all the data in the world,” and the authors speculate that good managers internalize data that analytics has not fully captured [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap is over-correcting into a rigid ritual: using a first-batter starter every road game even when the opposing lineup, weather, or pitcher’s warm-up state makes the cooling-down mechanism irrelevant; or treating a local STB drop as an absolute removal trigger despite sparse high-pitch-count data. The clock operates at three scales. The in-game clock is the pitch count and the first-inning top-half duration; the paper’s time result is that the home team gains an extra run for every 90 seconds beyond six minutes in the top of the first [PAGE 3]. The role clock is the times-through-the-order penalty: hitters “tend to do better the third time facing a pitcher in a game” [PAGE 14]. The multi-week clock is recovery and role adaptation: the paper’s “three-inning pitcher” proposal would alter pitching schedules and training regimens [PAGE 9], which changes the manager’s model of availability over a season. A recursive manager must also model the opposing manager modeling him: if the first-batter starter is used, the opposing manager can adjust the top of the lineup or instruct hitters to extend the first plate appearance, which changes the very top-half duration that drives the effect. The healthy policy is a conditional trigger: use the first-batter starter when the mechanism is plausible, monitor pitcher-specific STB against his own baseline, and preserve the option to abandon the ritual when the clock and the opponent’s counter-model change.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical vetoes are strong here because the paper’s causal claim is about warm-up timing, not just run accounting. If a first-batter starter is physically unable to be ready for one complete batter, or if the required trip to the mound increases injury risk because the reliever has not completed a full warm-up cycle, the strategy is vetoed regardless of the 0.037 runs/game estimate. The rule layer reinforces this: mid-at-bat substitution is allowed only if the pitcher has faced at least one complete batter or is injured [PAGE 4]. A biomechanical veto also applies to the “closers as starters” and “three-inning pitchers” proposals: the paper itself distinguishes relief pitching from starting pitching “just as running a marathon is different from a 100-meter dash” [PAGE 14]. If a max-effort reliever’s mechanics degrade when extended, the STB profile is not a sufficient proof. Weather is a cross-layer confounder not modeled in the source: rain delays, extreme heat or cold, and grip changes can alter warm-up timing, first-inning velocity, and STB. The data are Retrosheet game logs and 2015 play-by-play [PAGE 1][PAGE 6], with no weather covariates in the reported equations. Veto proof requires a design that controls for top-half duration, temperature, park, umpire, travel, and lineup quality; absent that, the first-inning mechanism remains a hypothesis. The STB trigger layer is also vetoed if the local drops are artifacts of low sample size at high pitch counts; the paper concedes “lower conﬁdence is expected in the results due to less data” near the tail [PAGE 12]. The correct adjudication is conditional: the accounting result can stand as a measurement correction, while the causal strategy claim is vetoed unless the physical, biomechanical, and weather layers are shown not to dominate.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. First-batter starter road-game counterfactual. Treatment: in road games, use a first-batter starter for one complete batter, then bring in the effective starter. Prediction: first-inning home differential falls from \(d_1=0.09\) runs/game toward \(\hat d_1=0.053\) runs/game, with an aggregate reduction near 0.037 runs/game and roughly 3 runs over 81 road games [PAGE 5]. Verifiable veto: if a randomized or matched road-game sample shows no reduction in first-inning home scoring, or an increase, the causal strategy is vetoed. A second veto is operational: if the treatment cannot be executed without violating the complete-batter rule or injury rule [PAGE 4], the counterfactual is infeasible.

2. Pitcher-specific STB trigger counterfactual. Treatment: remove a pitcher when his rolling STB falls below his own empirical baseline at a given pitch count, rather than waiting for a conventional pitch-count limit. Prediction: the trigger reduces runs allowed over the next inning relative to waiting one additional batter, especially where the paper identifies local STB drops around pitch 100 for Lester, pitch 80 for Hendricks, and near 65 and 90 for Hammel [PAGE 12]. Verifiable veto: if trigger-based removal produces the same or worse next-inning run allowance after controlling for batter quality and base-out state, the trigger is vetoed. A second veto: if the apparent local drop is not reproducible out of sample, it is mean reversion, not a fatigue signal [PAGE 8].

3. Opener or relief-first-inning counterfactual. Treatment: use a relief pitcher for the entire first inning, or use a first-batter starter, rather than a conventional starter. Prediction: first-inning runs allowed are lower or equal, total game runs allowed are not worse, and the opposing top of the order gets fewer third-time-through exposures later in the game [PAGE 14]. Verifiable veto: if the relief-first plan increases total runs allowed, increases pitcher injury or workload failure, or fails to reduce first-inning scoring after controlling for lineup quality, the strategy is vetoed. A third veto is adaptation: if opposing managers systematically extend first-inning plate appearances or alter the top of the order so that the measured first-inning advantage disappears, the original prediction is falsified.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
