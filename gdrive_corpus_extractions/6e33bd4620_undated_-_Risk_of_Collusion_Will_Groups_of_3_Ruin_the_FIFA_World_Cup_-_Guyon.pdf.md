The source is a tournament-design paper, not a physical-performance paper. Its central causal chain is multi-hop: the 2026 format creates 16 groups of three [PAGE 1]; in a three-team single round-robin, the team designated A plays the first two matches, B plays the first and last, and C plays the last two [PAGE 2]; after Match 2, B and C know the exact result set that qualifies both at A's expense [PAGE 1-2]; therefore ex ante qualification states can be enumerated from points and goal difference, yielding Proposition 1's collusion cases [PAGE 5-6]; then probability of those states is computed from win/draw probabilities [PAGE 6-7]; then tournament-level risk is \(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{RMF}})^{16}\) [PAGE 9]; then schedule and point-system counterfactuals are evaluated [PAGE 9-21]; then alternative formats are compared under match-count and duration constraints [PAGE 23-26]. The gray-zone audit is that "risk of match fixing" is an opportunity measure, not an observed-fixing measure. The paper explicitly says "The two teams who play the last game in the group know exactly what results will let them advance to the knockout stage" [PAGE 1], but it does not claim that every such opportunity is exploited. It treats tacit coordination as possible without explicit agreement: teams "refusing or doing little to attack each other" [PAGE 2]. Thus the derivation is combinatorial and probabilistic, not a detection algorithm.

The most important non-obvious step is that schedule assignment dominates point-system reform. Table 5's plausible example reports "about 15% in any given group, if Team A is the a priori strongest in the group, but it climbs to around 50% otherwise" [PAGE 10]. Tournament aggregation makes even the favorable case severe: "more than 90%" probability of at least one risky group and "2.3 groups" expected in the best schedule, versus "eight groups on average" otherwise [PAGE 10-11]. A second non-obvious step is that banning draws is not a clean fix: Proposition 9 says "forbidding draws indeed always decreases the risk of collusion, but it actually increases the probability of an aggravated risk of collusion" [PAGE 12]. A third is the counterintuitive ordering: the 3-2-1-0 system is not better than 3-0 at reducing global risk because \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{3210}=p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{30}+\frac14 d_{AB}d_{AC}\ge p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{30}\) [PAGE 20]. A fourth is that the last-game information clock is structurally unavoidable in odd groups: FIFA's simultaneous-final-match safeguard "is not possible with groups that have an odd number of teams" [PAGE 2]. The audit therefore must separate (a) formal opportunity states, (b) assumed probability model, (c) empirical calibration limits, and (d) institutional fixes. The source itself flags that its illustrative probabilities "are made up but plausible" [PAGE 9], and that one cannot directly estimate them from past World Cups because 2026 will include many weaker finalists [PAGE 9]. That is the main epistemic gray zone: the combinatorial theorem is robust, but numerical percentages are scenario-dependent.

Another gray zone is the tie-breaker layer. The paper uses ordered criteria: goal difference, goals scored, then head-to-head if exactly two teams remain even [PAGE 5]. It notes that head-to-head "has no effect in groups of three" [PAGE 5], but in groups of four it enabled the Denmark-Sweden Euro 2004 suspicion [PAGE 3,5]. Thus the model's exact case split depends on the ranking rule. The breakdown regime is not a mathematical failure of Proposition 1; it is an institutional mismatch: if FIFA changes tie-breakers, the set of qualifying scorelines changes. Also, the probability model assumes "the result of Match 2 is independent of the result of Match 1" [PAGE 6] and equal penalty-shootout chances with no goal-difference impact [PAGE 12]. If teams strategically regulate effort before Match 2, or if penalty shootout skill is unequal, the numerical probabilities shift. The formal opportunity logic remains, but the ex ante percentages are conditional on those assumptions.

Risk of match fixing occurs exactly when: (1) \(A\) has one draw and one loss; (2) \(A\) has two draws; (3) \(A\) has one win and one loss and \(GD_A\leq 0\) [PAGE 5]. Aggravated risk occurs when \(A\) has one win and one loss and either \(GD_A\lt 0\), or \(GD_A=0\) and \(i\lt j\), where \(i\) and \(j\) are the goal counts defined in the source's example [PAGE 5]. The proof is an exhaustive state audit: two wins or one win and one draw already qualify \(A\) [PAGE 5]; in the one-win-one-loss case, sign and tie-break structure determine whether \(B\) and \(C\) can both finish ahead [PAGE 5-6]. The breakdown regime is a change in ranking criteria or points; the combinatorial cases are conditional on the 3-1-0 system and stated tie-breakers.

Perfect-balance risk operator. Under \(p:=p_{AB}=p_{BA}=p_{AC}=p_{CA}=p_{BC}=p_{CB}\leq 1/2\), \(d_{AB}=d_{AC}=d_{BC}=1-2p\), and the source states
\[
p_{\mathrm{RMF}}=2p(1-2p)+(1-2p)^2+2p_{\leq 0}p^2
=1-2p+2p_{\leq 0}p^2 .
\]
This is [PAGE 7]. Numerically, with \(p=1/3\) and \(p_{\leq 0}=0.6\), \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}=7/15\); with \(p=3/8\), \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}=67/160\approx 42\%\) [PAGE 7]. Tournament aggregation is
\[
p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{(16)}=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{RMF}})^{16},
\]
yielding \(1-(1-7/15)^{16}\approx 0.99996\) and \(1-(1-67/160)^{16}\approx 0.99983\) [PAGE 9].

Singular and breakdown regimes. The maximum is \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}=1\) when \(d_{AB}=d_{AC}=1\): "The probability that there is a risk of match fixing is maximal, equal to 1, in the case where dAB = dAC = 1" [PAGE 8]. The zero regime is stated in Remark 5: \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}=0\) if one of three sufficient conditions holds, e.g. \(p_{AB}=1\) and either \(p_{CA}=0\) or \(p_{\leq 0}=0\), or \(p_{AC}=1\) with analogous conditions, or \(p_{BA}=p_{CA}=1\) [PAGE 8]. Realistically, Corollary 6 says positive probabilities \(p_{AB},p_{BA},p_{AC},p_{CA},p_{\leq 0}\), or positive \(d_{AB},d_{AC}\), imply \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}\gt 0\) [PAGE 8]. The numerical breakdown regime is model dependence: the illustrative probabilities "are made up but plausible" [PAGE 9], and the model assumes independence of Match 2 from Match 1 [PAGE 6]. The penalty-system breakdown regime is explicit: equal penalty-shootout chances and no goal-difference effect are assumed [PAGE 12].

Schedule operator. Table 5's illustrative result is \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}\approx 15\%\) when \(A\) is the strongest team, versus around \(50\%\) otherwise [PAGE 10]. Tournament consequence: at least one risky group has probability "more than 90%" in the favorable schedule, with 2.3 expected groups, and about eight groups otherwise [PAGE 10-11]. The source states: "the risk of collusion is about 15% in any given group, if Team A is the a priori strongest in the group, but it climbs to around 50% otherwise" [PAGE 10]. The mechanism is that a strong \(A\) is more likely already qualified after Match 2, so \(B\) and \(C\) cannot collude to eliminate \(A\) [PAGE 10].

Point-system operators. The 3-0 system gives 3 points to a penalty-shootout winner and 0 to the loser, with draws after 90 minutes resolved by penalties [PAGE 12]. Proposition 9 says banning draws decreases risk but increases aggravated risk [PAGE 12]. The 3-2-1-0 system gives 2 points to a penalty-shootout winner and 1 to the loser; the source states
\[
p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{3210}=p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{30}+\frac14 d_{AB}d_{AC}\ge p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{30}
\]
[PAGE 20]. Thus 3-2-1-0 is not better than 3-0 for global risk, though the aggravated-after-90 risk equals the 3-1-0 system; banning draws still creates a new "aggravated risk of match fixing on penalties" because a team may deliberately lose a shootout at no cost [PAGE 12,19-20]. This is a formal incentive singularity: the shootout is a zero-goal-difference lottery, so the ranking can be manipulated without changing goal difference.

Format operators. Format 3 (16 groups of three, only group winners advance) "would eliminate the risk of collusion but has several serious pitfalls" including dead rubbers and fairness problems [PAGE 24]. Format 5 seeds the knockout bracket by cross-group performance, "thus significantly decreasing the risk of collusion" [PAGE 24]. Format 7 uses paired groups of three followed by groups of four with carried-over results: "Since results of the first group stage are carried over, teams have no incentive to collude" [PAGE 25]. The assumptions are match-count and duration constraints: total matches should not exceed about 100, and the current and three-team formats can be completed in 32 days, while some alternatives require extra weeks [PAGE 23-24].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper's lineage runs from historical collusion scandals to modern format incentives. The 1982 "disgrace of Gijon" is identified as "certainly the most famous example of match fixing in the history of soccer" [PAGE 2]. West Germany and Austria played out a 1-0 Germany win that advanced both at Algeria's expense; FIFA then required simultaneous final group matches to prevent repetition, but the source notes that this safeguard "is not possible with groups that have an odd number of teams" [PAGE 2]. That historical fix is the direct retrocausal trap: the 2026 three-team group reopens the exact information condition that simultaneous kickoffs were meant to close. The paper also cites Denmark-France 0-0 in 2018, Japan-Poland 2018, Peru-Colombia 2017, and Denmark-Sweden Euro 2004 as tacit-collusion precedents [PAGE 2-3]. These are not betting-market cases; they are institutional-incentive cases in which a qualification result made non-attacking mutually convenient.

The scientific lineage is tournament design and incentive compatibility. The paper cites Kendall and Lenten on unintended rule consequences [PAGE 3], Csato on perverse incentives [PAGE 3], Glickman and Glickman-Hennessy on adaptive knockout design [PAGE 3-4], Scarf-Yusof-Bilbao and Scarf-Yusof on tournament metrics [PAGE 4], fair round-robin draws [PAGE 4], and strategy-proofness literatures [PAGE 4]. It also places itself beside Chater et al., Truta, and Krumer et al., who argue that in groups of three with two qualifiers the a priori best team should be passive in the last round [PAGE 3]. The 2026 decision itself is traced to FIFA's January 10, 2017 council decision, with political and financial motives reported by The New York Times [PAGE 1]. The paper says the press release "does not motivate the decision" [PAGE 1]. That is a retrocausal genealogy: the format was chosen first, and the fairness risk was quantified afterward.

Multi-week traps. First, the rest-day trap: \(B\) has more rest between its two matches than \(A\) and \(C\); \(A\) gets more rest before the knockout round if it advances; \(C\) gets neither benefit [PAGE 2]. Over a multi-week tournament this compounds schedule inequality independently of collusion. Second, the final-match information trap: \(B\) and \(C\) play after \(A\)'s second result is known, so the last match is a common-information game [PAGE 1-2]. Third, the bracket-seeding trap: if group winners are not rewarded, teams may prefer second place to reach a preferred bracket half, as the paper notes for England and Belgium in 2018 [PAGE 4]. Fourth, the point-system trap: banning draws looks like a fix but creates penalty-shootout manipulation risk [PAGE 12,19-20]. Fifth, the format-duration trap: 12 groups of four would produce 104 games and last at least one more week; 16 groups of three produce 80 games; Format 7 produces 96 games and requires extra match days [PAGE 24-25]. The historical lesson is that a rule can solve one visible problem while creating a less visible one; the paper's genealogy is a sequence of such fixes and regressions.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive strategic structure is: \(B\) and \(C\) know \(A\)'s points and goal difference after Match 2; each knows the other knows; each knows that a set of Match 3 scorelines qualifies both; and each knows the other has the same incentive to avoid attacking if the required result is already on the board. The paper describes this as possible without explicit agreement: "It may simply take the form of two teams satisfied with the current score more or less late in a game and refusing or doing little to attack each other" [PAGE 2]. The Denmark manager's quote is the crisis-habit regression in plain form: "We just needed one point, we were up against one of the best teams in the world at counterattacks, so we would have been stupid to open up a lot of space. We stood back and got the result we needed" [PAGE 2]. Japan-Poland is the same recursive logic under a different scoreline: "Japan and Poland then suddenly stopped attacking each other, in scenes reminiscent of the disgrace of Gijon" [PAGE 3]. Peru-Colombia is the same with a draw that sent both forward [PAGE 3]. These are not necessarily prearranged; they are equilibrium-like mutual best responses under qualification incentives.

Counter-trap design must change the payoff of the last match. Format 5 seeds the knockout bracket by cross-group performance, so teams cannot safely coast on a narrow qualification result; the paper says this would "significantly decreasing the risk of collusion" [PAGE 24]. Format 7 pairs groups of three and carries results into a second group stage; the source says "Since results of the first group stage are carried over, teams have no incentive to collude" [PAGE 25]. Format 3, where only the group winner advances, eliminates the two-team-advance collusion channel, but creates a dead-rubber and fairness problem: a team can be eliminated after one game, and an already-eliminated team may not give full effort against another [PAGE 24]. The counter-trap is therefore not merely "more incentives"; it is a change in the information and carryover structure so that a cooperative last-match result cannot jointly improve both teams at the third team's expense.

The clock is the odd-group timing constraint. In groups of four, simultaneous final matches reduce the information advantage; in groups of three, the final match necessarily follows \(A\)'s second match, so \(B\) and \(C\) are on the clock with full information [PAGE 2]. \(B\)'s rest advantage and \(A\)'s potential rest advantage are also clock effects [PAGE 2]. The deepest recursive failure is the penalty-shootout clock: in the 3-0 and 3-2-1-0 systems, a team that is already assured of topping the group can deliberately lose the shootout at no goal-difference cost to eliminate \(A\) [PAGE 12,19-20]. That is a second-order theory-of-mind trap: \(A\) anticipates that \(C\) can choose to lose; \(C\) knows \(A\) knows; and the formal rules make the choice costless. The source calls this "extremely problematic" and says FIFA "certainly needs to avoid by all means this situation where a team can decide to eliminate another team at no cost by deliberately loosing a penalty shootout" [PAGE 15].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather layers are not measured in the source. The paper provides no weather data, no biomechanical load data, no injury model, no heat-stress model, and no physiological force or fatigue metric. Therefore no weather or biomechanical veto can be asserted from this source as a proven causal factor. The only physical-schedule layer documented is rest-day asymmetry: "Team B will enjoy more rest days between their two group matches than Teams A and C; Team A, if they advance to the knockout round, will enjoy more rest days than the other advancing team; Team C will have none of these benefits" [PAGE 2]. That is a recovery-clock variable, not a biomechanical measurement. It can affect performance probabilities, but the source does not quantify it.

The correct adjudication is conditional. Proposition 1 is combinatorial: it depends on points, goal difference, goals scored, and the order of matches, not on weather or biomechanics [PAGE 5-6]. A physical or weather variable can only veto a numerical claim by changing the input probabilities \(p_{XY}\), \(d_{XY}\), or \(p_{\leq 0}\). It cannot by itself invalidate the existence of an opportunity state. For example, if weather changes \(p_{XY}\), then \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}\) changes through the probability operator, but the set of scorelines that lets \(B\) and \(C\) eliminate \(A\) remains [PAGE 5-7]. The model's stated assumptions are the relevant breakdown conditions: independence of Match 2 from Match 1 [PAGE 6], equal penalty-shootout chances and no goal-difference impact [PAGE 12], and the simplified tie-breaker hierarchy [PAGE 5]. Weather or biomechanics would be an omitted variable in the probability calibration, not a refutation of the combinatorial theorem.

A physical veto would need three verifiable elements: a measured weather or biomechanical variable, a demonstrated effect on match outcome probabilities, and a demonstration that the effect changes the qualification opportunity set or the equilibrium choice of \(B\) and \(C\). The source supplies none of the first two and does not need the third because its result is conditional. Thus the cross-layer verdict is: physical and weather layers are non-vetoing for the formal result, and parameter-shifting for the numerical tables. The rest-day asymmetry is the only physical-layer evidence, and it reinforces rather than weakens the fairness critique because it adds schedule imbalance to the collusion risk [PAGE 2]. Any stronger physical claim would be out-of-source and must be labeled as external.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: schedule-assignment counterfactual. Claim to test: "It does not matter which team is assigned to \(A\)." Verifiable test: hold the same illustrative probability matrix and point system, enumerate Table 3's states, and compare \(A=S\), \(A=M\), and \(A=W\). The source predicts \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}\approx 15\%\) when \(A\) is the strongest, versus around \(50\%\) otherwise, with tournament risk "more than 90%" and 2.3 expected risky groups in the favorable case, versus about eight groups otherwise [PAGE 10-11]. Veto condition: reject the claim if the model enumeration does not produce a substantial ordering \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}(A=S)\lt p_{\mathrm{RMF}}(A=M/W)\). Prediction: the ordering holds and the expected-group count difference is large.

Scenario 2: point-system counterfactual. Claim to test: "The 3-2-1-0 system is strictly better than the 3-0 system at reducing global collusion risk." Verifiable test: compute \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{3210}-p_{\mathrm{RMF}}^{30}=\frac14 d_{AB}d_{AC}\) from the stated formula [PAGE 20]. Prediction: the difference is nonnegative, so 3-2-1-0 is not better than 3-0 at global risk; with \(d_{AB}\gt 0\) and \(d_{AC}\gt 0\), it is strictly worse. Additional prediction: aggravated-after-90 risk in 3-2-1-0 equals the 3-1-0 system, and penalty-shootout aggravated risk is positive [PAGE 19-20]. Veto condition: reject the claim if the computed difference is negative or if the deliberate-shootout-loss state is absent.

Scenario 3: format counterfactual. Claim to test: "A three-team group with two qualifiers can always be made safe by schedule choice alone." Verifiable test: under Format 3, only the group winner advances, so the two-team-advance collusion channel disappears; the source says this "would eliminate the risk of collusion" but creates dead rubbers and fairness problems [PAGE 24]. Prediction: exhaustive state enumeration gives zero \(p_{\mathrm{RMF}}\) in Format 3, but a dead-rubber state appears when \(A\) has two wins and is already qualified. Under Format 7, carried-over results remove the incentive: "teams have no incentive to collude" [PAGE 25]. Veto condition: reject the claim if Format 3 still contains a state where \(B\) and \(C\) can both advance at \(A\)'s expense, or if Format 7's carryover does not change the last-match payoff. Prediction: Format 3 has zero two-qualifier collusion but nonzero dead-rubber risk; Format 7 reduces the last-match collusion incentive relative to plain groups of three.
