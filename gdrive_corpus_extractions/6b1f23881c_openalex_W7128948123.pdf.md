<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock.

Athletes facing performance crisis (missed hits, lost points) possess a recursive theory of mind about the coach: they expect corrective verbal input, observable authority, and social reinforcement. Under traditional training, crisis habit regression returns them to coach-dependent verbal guidance, which operates on a seconds-to-minutes latency loop and lacks quantitative keypoint resolution. The LAFPose closed loop replaces this with machine vision feedback at 28.5 FPS and 35 ms motion latency in field deployment [PAGE 17, Figure 6], theoretically tightening the perception–action cycle. However, the athlete's model of the AI as an intentional coach can misalign: if the system highlights wrist deviation that the athlete cannot proprioceptively verify, cognitive dissonance arises and trust decays.

Counter-trap: The false-feedback control group receives identical presentation (skeleton animation, keypoint highlighting) but semantically random suggestions [PAGE 10–11, Section 4.1]. Early in the six weeks, expectancy-value mechanisms may inflate motivation (placebo lift), but as athletes detect the irrelevance of scoring prompts to actual racket angle and trunk torsion, the counter-trap triggers: perceived contingency between action and feedback collapses, motivation reverts toward baseline (observed Δ0.23, d=0.21, p=0.34), and the novelty effect is exhausted. Real AI intervention avoids this because feedback is contingent on measured keypoint error, preserving the expectancy–outcome contingency required by EVT.

Clock: The intervention clock comprises eighteen sessions over six weeks. Real-time performance demands that inference latency remain below the inter-frame interval of the source video (33.3 ms at 30 fps). On RTX 4090 latency is 5.4 ms/frame [PAGE 17, Table 8], satisfying the clock; on CPU latency is 71.4 ms/frame, violating it by >2×, so feedback arrives after the next movement has already begun, destroying temporal contiguity critical for motor learning. On Jetson Xavier NX at 28 FPS, the system is marginally below the 30 fps clock, risking dropped frames during multi-athlete scenarios [PAGE 18, Figure 7]. Athletes with low self-efficacy or low skill may over-rely on AI prompts during this clock, creating dependency; if the clock slips (latency > frame interval), they experience incorrect or delayed corrections and regress to habitual (often faulty) motor patterns, negating competence gains predicted by SDT.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
