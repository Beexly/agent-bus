<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a seven-page case study of Reward-Adaptive Iterative Discovery (RAID), an automated playtesting method for EA SPORTS NHL 26. The multi-hop chain starts with the baseline RL failure: a standard Soft Actor-Critic agent is trained to score in a one-versus-goalie setting, and because the game contains a small set of clearly superior high-reward behaviors, independent seeds collapse to the same final-state patterns. The paper states that the naive baseline finds “9 snapshot strategies and 11 backhand strategies” across 20 independent iterations [PAGE 4]. That is the first hop: RL optimizes expected return, so without a diversity pressure it selects the dominant scoring exploit rather than exploring distinct exploits. The second hop is RAID’s reward masking: each new agent receives the base reward only when its evaluated final-state strategy is not similar to any prior strategy. The paper’s Algorithm 1 expresses this as “ri(z) ←rbase(z) IF !similar(z, Zprev) ELSE 0” [PAGE 3]. The third hop is the domain-specific similarity predicate: two strategies are similar when they use the same shot type and their shot positions lie within a 2 m Euclidean radius. The paper says “we define similar(z, Zprev) as the shot position of a strategy being within a 2 m radius of a previous strategy that used the same shot type” [PAGE 3]. The fourth hop is evaluation: the strategy is summarized by the average shot position and the most common shot type over 100 goals, and only strategies above a 10% scoring chance are added to the prior-strategy set [PAGE 3]. The fifth hop is the empirical result: RAID found “six hockey scoring exploit strategies that were qualitatively similar to those that playtesters had found in hours-long manual testing sessions” [PAGE 1]. The sixth hop is the gray-zone: the representation of a strategy by an average position is not a complete policy representation. The paper itself reports multimodality: “Around one third of all shot positions lie outside of the 2 m radius around the average of all shot positions” [PAGE 5], and it describes a hypothetical case where “a single agent can learn to shoot from two positions that are more than 4 meters apart, going for either of the two shot positions at a rate of 50% depending on where the agent is spawned” [PAGE 5]. That is a formal breakdown regime: if two modes are farther than 4 m apart, the midpoint average can be more than 2 m from both modes, so neither mode is masked. The seventh hop is validation: because RL exploration is stochastic and policies overfit to environment dynamics, “the same exploit not appearing in a second run of RAID after the fix is not a sufficient guarantee that the fix was successful” [PAGE 5]. The eighth hop is cross-layer: the action space is deliberately human-controller-like, the agent acts every 5 frames, and stick actions are smoothed with an exponential moving average of factor 0.2 “to make sure that the agent does not leverage any super-human movement capability or reaction time” [PAGE 3]. A human-executability veto therefore applies before any discovered strategy can be treated as a valid exploit candidate. The ninth hop is operational: runtime “grows (at least) linearly with the number of strategies we aim to find” [PAGE 6], and the paper’s 30-iteration run took 48 h while the 10-iteration run took 14 h [PAGE 4]. The gray-zone audit is therefore not whether RAID can find exploits—the paper demonstrates that it can—but whether its final-state, average-position mask is a sufficient certificate of diversity, whether human review is correctly placed, and whether retesting after a goalie update can distinguish a fixed exploit from an untested one. The central singularity is the hard threshold at distance exactly 2 m: the reward mask is discontinuous there, and the average-position summary is undefined or misleading when the policy is multimodal, when no shot occurs, when shot-type modes tie, or when the strategy set is warm-started with incomplete manual knowledge. These are the relevant audit surfaces.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal setup. Let a strategy be \(z=(s,p)\), where \(s\) is the shot type from a categorical set \(S\) and \(p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\) is the shot position in the ice plane with SI unit metres. The paper defines the shot position as “the last position of the puck before the puck is shot or, if the puck is not shot, the last puck position before a goal” [PAGE 3]. Let \(Z_{\mathrm{prev}}\) be the set of previously accepted strategies. The novelty predicate is

\[
\mathrm{Novel}(z,Z_{\mathrm{prev}})
=
\forall z'\in Z_{\mathrm{prev}}
\left[
s\neq s'
\;\lor\;
\lVert p-p'\rVert_{2}>r
\right],
\qquad r=2\ \mathrm{m}.
\]

Equivalently, similarity is

\[
\mathrm{similar}(z,Z_{\mathrm{prev}})
=
\exists z'\in Z_{\mathrm{prev}}
\left[
s=s'
\;\land\;
\lVert p-p'\rVert_{2}\leq r
\right],
\qquad r=2\ \mathrm{m}.
\]

This matches the paper’s statement that a strategy is novel if “∀z′ ∈Zprev zshottype̸ = z′ shottype ∨l2(zshotpos, z′ shotpos) > 2 m” [PAGE 3]. The RAID reward operator is

\[
r_i(z)=
\begin{cases}
r_{\mathrm{base}}(z), & \mathrm{Novel}(z,Z_{\mathrm{prev}}),\\
0, & \mathrm{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

matching “ri(z) ←rbase(z) IF !similar(z, Zprev) ELSE 0” [PAGE 3]. Units: \(r_i\) and \(r_{\mathrm{base}}\) are reward scalars; their numerical scale is game-defined, not an SI unit. The base reward includes “every goal” plus shaping for puck proximity and “the scoring chance of every shot taken, based on an NHL-internal calculation” [PAGE 3]. The mask is a hard constraint, not an entropy bonus: the paper contrasts its method with “hard constraints on state difference, enforced through reward masking of prior behaviors” [PAGE 2].

Evaluation. For the NHL case, RAID evaluates a converged agent until it scores \(G=100\) goals. The stored strategy is

\[
\bar p_i=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}p_{i,j},
\qquad
s_i=\operatorname{mode}\{s_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{G},
\]

with \(G=100\), \(\bar p_i\) in metres, and \(s_i\) categorical. The paper states: “we evaluate the agent until it scores 100 goals and describe the strategy z as the average shot position and the most common shot type over those 100 goals” [PAGE 3]. A candidate is skipped if its scoring chance is below 0.10: “we skip adding strategies with a scoring chance of less than 10% after convergence” [PAGE 3]. Scoring chance is a probability in \([0,1]\), dimensionless. Training stops when the agent “either reaches a 90% scoring chance or does not improve its performance for 50k training steps” [PAGE 3]. The 90% value is a probability; 50k is a step count.

Spatial exclusion area. For radius \(r=2\ \mathrm{m}\), the excluded disk area per prior strategy and shot type is

\[
A=\pi r^{2}=4\pi\ \mathrm{m}^{2}\approx 12.57\ \mathrm{m}^{2}.
\]

This is a geometry fact, not a performance ratio. The paper’s diversity criterion is “a 2 m radius” [PAGE 3], and it notes that a user could increase “the area used for the shot similarity criterion to a 4 m radius” [PAGE 2]. The hard boundary at \(\lVert p-p'\rVert_2=r\) is a singularity for reward gradients: at equality the strategy is similar and receives zero mask reward, so the indicator is discontinuous across the boundary. That discontinuity is intentional, but it means gradient-based learning sees a step change rather than a smooth diversity distance.

Information interpretation. RAID does not maximize an entropy objective over strategies; it filters final states. If one wanted an information audit, the shot-type entropy over evaluated goals is

\[
H(S)=-\sum_{s\in S}\hat p_s\log_2 \hat p_s
\quad \mathrm{bits},
\]

where \(\hat p_s\) is the empirical frequency of shot type \(s\) among the 100 goals. This is an audit quantity, not a RAID operator. The source’s diversity mechanism is spatial plus categorical masking, not mutual-information maximization; related work is described as optimizing “mutual information between options” and “entropy optimization” [PAGE 2].

Assumptions. A1: the goalie AI is fixed during one iteration, and the environment is not updated between sequential agents within a run. A2: the strategy summary \(z=(s,\bar p)\) is sufficient for reward masking. A3: shot position is Euclidean in metres. A4: the mode shot type is well defined. A5: no-shot goals are represented by the last puck position before the goal. A6: the action interface is the human controller interface, with action every 5 frames and exponential moving-average smoothing of factor 0.2 [PAGE 3].

Breakdown regimes. (i) Threshold discontinuity: at \(\lVert p-p'\rVert_2=2\ \mathrm{m}\) with \(s=s'\), reward is exactly masked; arbitrarily close outside the radius it is unmasked. (ii) Multimodal average failure: if a policy has two modes \(p_A,p_B\) with \(\lVert p_A-p_B\rVert_2>4\ \mathrm{m}\) and equal mass, the average \(\bar p=(p_A+p_B)/2\) has \(\lVert p_A-\bar p\rVert_2>2\ \mathrm{m}\) and \(\lVert p_B-\bar p\rVert_2>2\ \mathrm{m}\); neither mode lies inside the exclusion disk. The paper reports exactly this risk: “Around one third of all shot positions lie outside of the 2 m radius around the average of all shot positions” [PAGE 5], and describes the “more than 4 meters apart” hypothetical [PAGE 5]. (iii) No-shot strategy bug: the paper says the agent “also finds strategies to score without shooting” and “This initially led to a bug in our setup, since previous shot strategies were only recorded when the agent actually shot the puck” [PAGE 5]. If no-shot strategies are not recorded, the mask can be bypassed. (iv) Mode tie: if two shot types are equally common over 100 goals, “most common shot type” is not uniquely defined by the text [PAGE 3]. (v) Low-score omission: strategies below 10% scoring chance are not added, so \(Z_{\mathrm{prev}}\) may omit a local optimum that a future seed could rediscover [PAGE 3]. (vi) False convergence: the 50k-step no-improvement stop and 90% scoring-chance stop can halt before a different high-chance strategy is found [PAGE 3]. (vii) Environment-change invalidation: policies are “highly overfitting to the exact dynamics of the environment” and can be “thrown off by slight changes in the goalie’s behavior” [PAGE 5]. (viii) Warm-start incompleteness: RAID can be warm-started with “manually defined strategies” [PAGE 4], so if that list is incomplete the masking is incomplete. (ix) Runtime: \(T_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}T_i\), and the paper states runtime “grows (at least) linearly with the number of strategies we aim to find” [PAGE 6]; the 30-iteration experiment took 48 h and the 10-iteration experiment took 14 h [PAGE 4].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from quality-diversity search and novelty search into intrinsic-reward RL and then into RAID’s hard reward masking. The paper places quality diversity in the evolutionary tradition that “aims to generate diverse populations of high-performing solutions” [PAGE 2], citing novelty-search and MAP-Elites-style work in its references [PAGE 2, PAGE 7]. It then moves to intrinsic-reward diversity: Gregor et al. and Eysenbach et al. are described as using mutual information and entropy optimization to discover diverse options or states [PAGE 2], while DOMiNO is described as combining “the intrinsic diversity reward and an extrinsic reward” using successor features and Lagrange multipliers [PAGE 2]. RAID is a retrocausal simplification of that lineage: instead of learning a moving diversity target in parallel with policies, it fixes the diversity measure for each individual agent’s training and enforces it by masking prior strategies. The paper explicitly says prior quality-diversity methods can be “brittle in complex domains, one reason being a moving diversity target learned in parallel to the policies” [PAGE 1], and that DUPLEX highlights instability when successor-feature representations are learned in parallel [PAGE 2]. RAID’s historical move is therefore not to out-optimize DOMiNO but to make the diversity constraint static, interpretable, and sequential.

The multi-week trap is that this simplification trades parallel diversity for sequential run time and representational fragility. In week one, a naive baseline run collapses to the dominant snapshot/backhand set: the paper reports “9 snapshot strategies and 11 backhand strategies” over 20 independent iterations [PAGE 4]. In week two, RAID is run and finds six exploits that human playtesters had found, but on an “outdated version of the game” [PAGE 4]. In week three, an engineer fixes one exploit. In week four, a second RAID run does not rediscover it, and a team may wrongly conclude the fix is verified. The paper blocks that inference: “the same exploit not appearing in a second run of RAID after the fix is not a sufficient guarantee that the fix was successful” [PAGE 5], and playtesters “still have to validate that an exploit is indeed fixed by imitating the strategy found by the approach before the fix” [PAGE 5]. In week five, the team may discover that a bimodal policy was only partly masked, because the average position does not cover both modes: “Around one third of all shot positions lie outside of the 2 m radius” [PAGE 5]. In week six, the team may discover that no-shot goals were not recorded as strategies, causing a setup bug [PAGE 5]. These are retrocausal traps because each later failure changes the interpretation of earlier “success”: a prior strategy list is only as complete as the strategy representation that generated it. The paper’s own future work points toward “more elaborate representations of a policy’s strategy, for example through clustering methods such as k-means” [PAGE 5], which is the genealogical next step beyond a single average point.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under exploit pressure, a development team tends to regress to the habit that produced the first discovery: fix one visible exploit, retrain the same base RL agent, and expect a new exploit. The baseline result explains why this habit fails: standard RL “tends to converge to one single ‘best’ solution” [PAGE 1], and the 20-seed baseline keeps returning the same small set [PAGE 4]. RAID changes the team’s belief state by making the prior strategy list explicit, but it also creates a new regression risk: the team may treat the list \(Z_{\mathrm{prev}}\) as a complete inventory of exploits. It is not complete if strategies below 10% scoring chance are omitted [PAGE 3], if no-shot goals are not recorded [PAGE 5], or if a multimodal policy is summarized by an average position that does not cover all modes [PAGE 5]. Crisis habit regression is therefore the shift from “run one agent” to “run RAID and trust the list,” without auditing the representation.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the recursive belief that absence of rediscovery proves a fix. The playtester believes the developer has changed the goalie AI; the developer believes the agent would rediscover the exploit if it remained; the agent, however, only optimizes the current reward mask under the current environment dynamics. The paper warns that policies “can not be used for this purpose, since RL is highly overfitting to the exact dynamics of the environment and can therefore be easily thrown off by slight changes in the goalie’s behavior, without proving that the exploit is fixed” [PAGE 5]. The correct counter-trap is interventional: replay or imitate the pre-fix strategy against the updated goalie. RAID’s own candidate list is a set of hypotheses for human review, not a proof of repair. The paper also withholds which strategies matched known exploits “to not reveal any information that allows players to further exploit game mechanisms” [PAGE 5], which means external observers cannot verify the match from the paper alone; internal review must carry that burden.

Clock. The clock has two faces. The first is training time: the 30-iteration experiment took 48 h and the 10-iteration experiment took 14 h [PAGE 4], and runtime grows “at least linearly” with the number of strategies sought [PAGE 6]. The second is the iteration limit: the paper reports that “The longer the experiment goes on, the more iterations do not make the cut” [PAGE 5], and it manually stops after 30 iterations because only three valid strategies were found in the last 11 iterations [PAGE 5]. The recursive theory-of-mind lesson is that the team must coordinate three clocks: agent training time, developer fix time, and human review time. If the review clock is slower than the RAID clock, the team accumulates an unreviewed candidate queue; if the fix clock is faster than the validation clock, the team accumulates false “fixed” beliefs. RAID’s sequential design is simple for non-RL practitioners, but it makes the human review step the binding constraint. The paper’s limitation is explicit: “telling high chance scoring strategies intended by game designers and actual exploits apart is not covered by the method” [PAGE 6]. The recursive loop is therefore agent discovers, human classifies, developer fixes, human imitates, and only then can the team update its belief.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The simulated environment contains “a single goalie, controlled by the game’s AI we want to test, as well as a forward player, controlled by RL” [PAGE 4]. The observation includes normalized position, velocity, and orientation of puck, net, and goalie relative to the agent, plus a stack of the 8 last actions [PAGE 3]. A discovered exploit is physically admissible only if it is representable in this state-action geometry. The shot position is a point in the ice plane, and the diversity criterion uses the Euclidean distance \(\lVert p-p'\rVert_2\) in metres [PAGE 3]. Physical veto: if a claimed exploit depends on a state variable or contact geometry not present in the simulation, RAID has not validated it. The paper does not model weather, crowd, or outdoor ice; the game is indoor hockey, and the source gives no weather layer. Weather veto is therefore not applicable inside the reported simulation; any claim that RAID tested weather-dependent behavior would be outside the source.

Biomechanical veto. The action interface is explicitly human-controller-like: “Discrete actions: the 4 face buttons and the left trigger button that execute so-called dekes” and “Continuous actions: the two sticks for player skating and hockey stick control, each represented by continuous x and y axes” [PAGE 3]. The agent can use at most “one discrete action at a time” [PAGE 3]. To prevent super-human execution, “we only let the agent act every 5 frames and smooth the stick actions using an exponential moving average with a smoothing factor of 0.2, to make sure that the agent does not leverage any super-human movement capability or reaction time” [PAGE 3]. Formally, for stick action \(a_t\) and smoothed action \(\tilde a_t\),

\[
\tilde a_t=(1-\alpha)\tilde a_{t-1}+\alpha a_t,
\qquad
\alpha=0.2,
\]

with \(\alpha\) dimensionless. The action interval is \(\Delta t=5\) frames; the source does not state the frame rate, so a seconds conversion would be an unsupported assumption. Biomechanical veto: if a discovered strategy requires simultaneous discrete actions, unsmoothed stick jerk, or reaction latency finer than one action interval, it is not a human-executable exploit under the reported setup. This veto is a proof obligation, not a claim that all RAID outputs are automatically human-executable; smoothing constrains the command interface, but it does not prove that a human can reproduce the timing under pressure.

Cross-layer adjudication. The layers are: physical geometry (metres, Euclidean shot position), biomechanical controller (discrete buttons, continuous sticks, 5-frame action interval, EMA smoothing), and environmental dynamics (goalie AI, puck, net, forward). A strategy passes all three only if it is a valid state-action trajectory in the simulation, uses only the human controller interface, and is robust to the goalie AI version being tested. If the goalie changes, the policy can be “thrown off by slight changes in the goalie’s behavior” [PAGE 5], so a failed rediscovery is not a physical veto of the exploit; it is a policy-environment mismatch. The correct cross-layer proof is interventional: record the pre-fix trajectory, replay it or imitate it under the updated goalie, and check whether the scoring chance remains above the review threshold. The source’s 10% cutoff for adding strategies [PAGE 3] and 90% training stop [PAGE 3] are probability thresholds, not physical thresholds; they govern acceptance and training, not human biomechanics. Weather is outside the model; physical geometry and biomechanics are inside; the goalie AI version is the temporal layer that can invalidate a prior proof.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto 1: Reward-mask ablation veto. Counterfactual: train the same base Soft Actor-Critic setup for \(n\) independent seeds with \(Z_{\mathrm{prev}}=\varnothing\) and no mask, then train RAID with the same base setup and the 2 m same-shot-type mask. Prediction: the unmasked baseline will concentrate on the dominant final-state modes, consistent with the paper’s “9 snapshot strategies and 11 backhand strategies” across 20 iterations [PAGE 4], while RAID will produce accepted strategies satisfying \(\lVert p_i-p_j\rVert_2>2\ \mathrm{m}\) for same shot type, as in the reported seed-1 and seed-2 runs [PAGE 4]. Veto condition: if the unmasked baseline produces the same number of accepted diverse strategies under the same acceptance rule, or if RAID violates the same-shot-type 2 m separation, the claimed diversity effect is not verified. Verification: compute pairwise same-shot-type distances in metres from the 100-goal evaluation sets and count accepted strategies above the 10% scoring-chance cutoff [PAGE 3].

Veto 2: Bimodal-average veto. Counterfactual: take an accepted strategy whose 100-goal shot positions are bimodal with two modes \(p_A,p_B\), \(\lVert p_A-p_B\rVert_2>4\ \mathrm{m}\), and apply the RAID mask using only the average \(\bar p=(p_A+p_B)/2\). Prediction: because both modes are more than 2 m from the midpoint, the mask disk \(D(\bar p,2\ \mathrm{m})\) does not cover either mode; a succeeding agent can still receive base reward for shots at \(p_A\) or \(p_B\), so the prior strategy is not fully excluded. This is the paper’s reported risk: “Around one third of all shot positions lie outside of the 2 m radius around the average of all shot positions” [PAGE 5], and the “more than 4 meters apart” hypothetical [PAGE 5]. Veto condition: if a single average-point mask is claimed to exclude a multimodal strategy whenever one mode is outside the radius, the claim is false. Verification: fit a two-component clustering to the 100 shot positions, compute \(\lVert p_A-\bar p\rVert_2\) and \(\lVert p_B-\bar p\rVert_2\) in metres, and check whether either is \(\leq 2\ \mathrm{m}\). If neither is, the mask is incomplete.

Veto 3: Rediscovery-absence veto. Counterfactual: fix a goalie behavior that previously allowed a RAID-discovered exploit; run RAID again with the same seed and with at least one different seed; also replay or imitate the recorded pre-fix trajectory against the updated goalie. Prediction: absence of the exploit in a second RAID run is not sufficient to prove the fix, because policies “can not be used for this purpose, since RL is highly overfitting to the exact dynamics of the environment” [PAGE 5], and the paper explicitly says “the same exploit not appearing in a second run of RAID after the fix is not a sufficient guarantee that the fix was successful” [PAGE 5]. The interventional replay is the stronger test: if the recorded strategy still scores at a high chance against the updated goalie, the fix is not verified; if replay fails while a fresh RAID run also fails to rediscover, the evidence is stronger but still requires human review because “telling high chance scoring strategies intended by game designers and actual exploits apart is not covered by the method” [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: if a team treats one absent rediscovery as proof of repair, the RLVR claim is vetoed. Verification: compare pre-fix and post-fix scoring chance for the imitated trajectory, and report both the replay result and the fresh-run result separately. Prediction: the replay test will be more decisive than the fresh-run test because it controls the policy while changing only the goalie behavior.
