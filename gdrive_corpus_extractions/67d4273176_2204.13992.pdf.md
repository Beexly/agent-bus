Source-grounded audit first: the paper’s central object is not an expected player position but a possibly reachable region. The abstract states, “Most existing models of football player motion have not been validated yet with respect to actual data,” and the authors frame validation as measuring “the smallest predicted area of reachable positions that encompasses all observed reached positions up to a manually defined threshold” [PAGE 1]. This immediately prevents a common category error: a model can be “correct” while being useless if its predicted region is enormous. The validation logic therefore separates correctness—does the observed endpoint fall inside the predicted reachable set?—from precision—how small is that set? The trail abstraction is the bridge between raw positional frames and model scoring. A trail is a quadruple \((\vec{x}_0,\vec{v}_0,\vec{x}_t,\Delta t)\), with units meters, meters per second, meters, and seconds respectively [PAGE 3]. The model receives only the initial state and horizon; the observed endpoint \(\vec{x}_t\) is held out for containment testing. This is a clean out-of-sample logic even though the paper is not a machine-learning paper in the usual sense.

Multi-hop derivation: first, containment indicator \(I_\tau=\mathbf 1[\vec{x}_t\in R_m(\tau)]\) is dimensionless and acts as an empirical probability over trails. Second, aggregating \(I_\tau\) over a trail sample gives the hit ratio \(H(m,T)\), which the paper uses as a correctness indicator [PAGE 5]. Third, the precision is the inverse of the mean area of correctly predicted regions, so its SI unit is \(\mathrm{m}^{-2}\) [PAGE 5]. Fourth, the score gates precision by a manually selected minimum hit ratio \(h_{\min}\): if \(H(m,T)<h_{\min}\), the score is zero; otherwise the score equals precision [PAGE 6]. Fifth, the implementation can stop early when the number of incorrect predictions exceeds \((1-h_{\min})|T|\) [PAGE 7]. That algebraic shortcut matters because the denominator \(|T|\) and threshold are known before evaluation.

The model family comparison is also structurally clear. Model (a) is a disk centered at \(\vec{x}_0\) with radius \(v_{\max}\Delta t\) [PAGE 9]. Model (b) is a disk centered at \(\vec{x}_0+\vec{v}_0\Delta t\) with radius \(\frac12 a_{\max}\Delta t^2\) [PAGE 9]. Model (d), the authors’ proposed approximation, moves inertially for a short interval \(t_{\text{inert}}\) along \(\vec{v}_0\), then switches to a constant-speed disk of radius \(v_{\text{final}}(\Delta t-t^*_{\text{inert}})\) [PAGE 10]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is retrocausal in the following sense: later validation changes how earlier kinematic assumptions should be read. Earlier space-control and dominance models often assumed constant equal speed, producing Voronoi partitions, or accelerated motion with friction or velocity-dependent acceleration [PAGE 3]. The paper records that “Controlled space is often defined as the area that a player is able to reach before any other player, given a specific model of motion for each player” [PAGE 3]. It also notes that Spearman et al. assumed accelerated motion with limits on acceleration and velocity for ground passes [PAGE 3]. Those assumptions were useful generative priors, but this paper’s validation routine retroactively audits them as reachable-region estimators. The key retrocausal shift is from “what position is expected?” to “what extreme positions remain possible?” The abstract states that models are “usually phrased in a way that emphasises possibly reachable positions rather than expected positions” [PAGE 1]. That rephrasing makes earlier constant-speed and constant-acceleration models testable, but also exposes their failure modes: constant speed ignores initial kinematic state, and constant acceleration without a speed limit overestimates reachable space at high speed [PAGE 12]. The paper’s own conclusion is that constant speed or constant acceleration with unlimited speed is “particularly inappropriate for an accurate distinction between reachable and unreachable locations” [PAGE 1].

Multi-week traps in this genealogy:
- Trap 1: treating a high hit ratio as sufficient. The paper warns that “extreme values are typically rare and particularly likely to include a component of measurement error” [PAGE 2]. A model can pass containment by widening the region; precision is the counterweight.
- Trap 2: copying \(h_{\min}=99.975\%\) across populations. The paper says the threshold “has to be set for each distinguished population,” because “players who sprint regularly during the game produce more positional extrema (and thus outliers)” than goalkeepers [PAGE 13].
- Trap 3: transporting model (d) below its temporal validity. The optimized \(t_{\text{inert}}=0.22\,\mathrm{s}\) means the model is not applicable for horizons at or below that scale [PAGE 13].
- Trap 4: over-reading optimized parameters as physical laws. The paper itself says the optimized acceleration in model (c) is “physically unrealistic” [PAGE 12].
- Trap 5: ignoring the discrete-parameter search structure. Bayesian optimization is run separately for discrete combinations such as \(\text{keep\_initial}\), so the reported optimum is conditional on that enumeration [PAGE 11].
- Trap 6: comparing scores without units. Precision and score are inverse areas in \(\mathrm{m^{-2}}\); reporting “score” without the inverse-area unit obscures that model (d)’s advantage is an area-narrowing advantage [PAGE 5, 12].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory-of-mind here must be handled as an explicit extension, not as a source-measured psychological claim. The source models player motion kinematically; it does not measure beliefs, pressure, or opponent anticipation. Still, three audit modes are useful.

Crisis habit regression: under acute time pressure, a player’s short-horizon motion may regress toward maintaining the current velocity direction before a new direction is selected. Model (d) is a formal analogue: it preserves \(\vec{v}_0\) for \(t_{\text{inert}}\) and then allows an arbitrary constant-speed direction [PAGE 10]. The optimized \(t_{\text{inert}}=0.22\,\mathrm{s}\) is short, so the model says the inertial habit window is brief. This is not proof of a psychological crisis habit; it is a kinematic prior consistent with short-horizon persistence. The formal time scales are \(t^*_{\text{inert}}=\min(t_{\text{inert}},\Delta t)\) and \(t^*_{\text{acc}}=\min(t_{\text{acc}},\Delta t)\), with \(t_{\text{acc}}\) determined by \(| \vec{v}_0|+a t_{\text{acc}}=v_{\max}\) [PAGE 9–10]. If \(\Delta t\) is near \(t_{\text{inert}}\), the model degenerates toward a point; if \(\Delta t\gg t_{\text{inert}}\), the constant-speed disk dominates. That is the “clock” condition.

Counter-trap: the main counter-trap is threshold gaming. A modeler can widen \(R_m\) until \(H(m,T)\ge h_{\min}\), gaining containment at the cost of precision. The score formula blocks that only if \(h_{\min}\) is set above the outlier prevalence and below the desired correctness floor [PAGE 6]. A second counter-trap is recursive anticipation: a defender who estimates an attacker’s reachable set with an over-wide model will cover space the attacker cannot actually threaten, while an attacker who knows the defender uses a narrow model may exploit the omitted region. The source does not test this game-theoretic layer; it only provides a one-agent reachable-set estimator. Therefore any recursive ToM claim must be labeled as an extension requiring opponent-position and decision data. The clock remains central: at \(\Delta t=1\,\mathrm{s}\), the optimized model (c) reaches its speed limit in roughly half a second from zero speed, while model (d) uses a \(0.22\,\mathrm{s}\) inertial segment [PAGE 10, 12]. The ordering \(0<t_{\text{inert}}<\Delta t\) is the validity window for model (d); outside it, the model’s “habit” interpretation is not licensed [PAGE 13].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

\[

\]
where \(\mu_s\) is dimensionless static friction and \(g\approx9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The paper’s model (c) optimum \(a_{\max}=19.42\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) is about twice \(g\), and the authors already call it “physically unrealistic” [PAGE 12]. That is a biomechanical veto against literal interpretation, even if it remains a useful curve-fitting parameter. r_a=v_{\max}\Delta t,\qquad r_b=\frac12 a_{\max}\Delta t^2.
\]
Thus \(R_{\text{phys}}(W)\subset R_m(P_{\text{dry}})\) when \(W\) is more restrictive. The model then overestimates reachable area; precision is artificially worsened if the observed endpoint still lies inside, and containment may fail if the player’s actual motion is constrained below the predicted boundary. Wind is less direct for player motion because aerodynamic drag on a player is small at football speeds compared with traction and propulsion, but wind can affect ball trajectories, not the player-motion model in this paper. The source’s data are 25 Hz video-based positional data with no weather metadata [PAGE 8], so a weather-layer veto is a proof of non-transportability: the paper’s calibrated \(v_{\max}\) and \(a_{\max}\) cannot be assumed invariant across surface and weather conditions without re-validation.

1. Threshold-fraction counterfactual. Fix \(T\) at the reported \(5\cdot10^5\) trails and vary \(h_{\min}\in\{99.9\%,99.975\%,99.99\%\}\). The allowed incorrect count before score zero is \((1-h_{\min})|T|\), giving \(500\), \(125\), and \(50\) respectively. Prediction: if model (d) has fewer than 50 incorrect predictions, it remains nonzero and likely top-ranked at \(99.99\%\); if it has between 50 and 125, it is vetoed to zero at \(99.99\%\) but survives at \(99.975\%\). This directly tests whether the reported ranking is threshold-robust rather than an artifact of the manual \(99.975\%\) choice [PAGE 6–8]. Verifiable prediction: the score ordering changes if and only if the incorrect count crosses one of these integer boundaries.

2. Initial-velocity permutation counterfactual. Randomly permute \(\vec{v}_0\) across trails while preserving \(\vec{x}_0,\vec{x}_t,\Delta t\). Model (a) ignores \(\vec{v}_0\) and should be unchanged; model (d) with \(\text{keep\_initial}=\text{True}\) should lose containment and/or precision because its first segment shifts by \(v_{\text{inert}}\frac{\vec{v}_0}{|\vec{v}_0|}t^*_{\text{inert}}\) [PAGE 10]. Prediction: the hit ratio of model (d) drops below the threshold or its precision worsens, while model (a)’s hit ratio and precision remain approximately stable. This is a verifiable ablation of the kinematic-state claim; if model (d) is unchanged under velocity permutation, the validation routine is not actually sensitive to the initial velocity it claims to use.

3. Physical acceleration-cap counterfactual. Re-optimize model (c) and model (d) subject to \(a_{\max}\le 9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) instead of the reported \(19.42\) and \(19.14\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\) [PAGE 12]. Prediction: model (c) cannot reproduce its reported score because its reachable radius scales with \(a_{\max}\); either its hit ratio falls below \(h_{\min}\) or its precision worsens. Model (d) is less structurally dependent on \(a_{\max}\) because it enters only through \(v_{\text{final}}=\min(v_{\text{inert}}+a_{\max}t_{\text{inert}},v_{\max})\) [PAGE 10]; if \(v_{\max}\) is not binding, model (d) should retain much of its advantage, but if \(v_{\text{final}}\) drops enough, its hit ratio will fall. Verifiable prediction: the physical cap either preserves model (d)’s top rank with a smaller predicted area or vetoes the claim that the optimized parameters are physically meaningful. In either case, the counterfactual separates predictive fit from biomechanical plausibility.
