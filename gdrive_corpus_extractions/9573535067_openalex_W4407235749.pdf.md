Standard zones are intervals in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) from Table 1 [PAGE 2]:
\[
Z_1=[0.5,1),\ Z_2=[1,2),\ Z_3=[2,3),\ Z_4=[3,4),\ Z_5=[4,5),\ Z_6=[5,10].
\]
High-stress set \(H=\{4,5,6\}\). The source says “Training zones are used to give athletes a set intensity at which they should be working during an activity to avoid injuries and perform better” [PAGE 2].

q_{p,a,j}=F_{p,a}^{-1}\!\left(\frac{j}{6}\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,5,
\]
with zones \([0,q_1),[q_1,q_2),\dots,[q_5,\infty)\). This is exactly the source’s statement that demarcation was “conducted by dividing the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) of the optimal distribution into six equal segments (1/6, 2/6, 3/6, 4/6, and 5/6 percentiles)” [PAGE 2]. Units of \(q_{p,a,j}\) are \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The CDF values and KS statistic are dimensionless.

For day \(t\), let
\[

\]
where \(H_{p,a}\) is the high-stress set under either standard or personalized zones. The source describes \(S_t\) as “the sum of the high stress zones accelerations (4th-6th) for day t” [PAGE 4]. W_{2,t}=\sum_{d=7}^{13}S_{t-d},\quad

\qquad

\]

If the denominator instead excludes the current week, the formula must be rewritten as

That is the retrocausal trap: once an injury is known, an earlier spike can be named “pre-injury,” but the same spike must be evaluated against non-injury windows to become a prospective predictor. The paper does not report a held-out injury-free comparison set, so the “pre-injury pattern” language on [PAGE 12] is post hoc pattern labeling.

The multi-week traps are several. First, the acute week and chronic window overlap. If the current week is inside the denominator, the ratio has a built-in upper bound and the “spike” is partly an artifact of weighting. Second, rolling daily windows create overlapping observations: consecutive days share six of seven acute days and up to twenty-seven chronic days. This induces strong autocorrelation and makes visual “sudden spikes” and “falls” dependent on window alignment. Third, the paper compares equal and unequal weights but reports that “using different chronic workload weights does not seem to lead to significant deviations in the ACWR patterns” [PAGE 12]. That may be because the current-week inclusion dominates the denominator, or because the visual scale hides differences; either way, it is not evidence that weighting is irrelevant. Fourth, the source considers multiple axes, multiple planes, two zone definitions, and two weighting schemes. Even a single player can generate many ACWR series; across five players the number of visual series is large enough that post hoc selection of a spike near injury is a serious multiple-comparison risk. Fifth, the claimed injury lag is “at most five days away from the first and largest spike” [PAGE 12]. A five-day tolerance around a rolling seven-day window is wide relative to the daily resolution, so many spikes can satisfy it. Sixth, the paper’s own alternative lineage—exponentially weighted moving averages—was designed to address training decay and recent-load sensitivity [PAGE 5, PAGE 13], yet the source uses fixed four-week weights rather than estimating a decay constant. The historical lesson is that ACWR is a monitoring heuristic whose predictive value depends on outcome definition, window choice, and validation; the source’s genealogy supports hypothesis generation, not a new validated injury law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision loop has three minds: athlete, coach, and medical/performance staff. The athlete experiences fatigue and may alter movement or training intensity in response to perceived load. The coach observes training plans and may react to a high ACWR by reducing or increasing load. The medical staff may intervene when a spike appears. The paper’s ACWR is therefore not a passive measurement of an unobserved system; it is an input to the same system it measures. This creates a counter-trap: if staff see a personalized ACWR spike and reduce training, the subsequent injury may be prevented, censoring the very outcome the model was meant to predict. Conversely, if staff ignore the spike because personalized zones always show roughly uniform occupancy, the model provides false reassurance. The paper’s crisis habit regression is the default to standard zones: when uncertainty is high, practitioners may prefer fixed thresholds in Table 1 [PAGE 2] because they are simple and comparable across players. But the source shows that those fixed zones produce almost no high-stress observations—less than 0.02% under standardized definitions [PAGE 4]—so the habit yields numerically sparse and potentially erroneous ratios. The counter-trap is the opposite habit: adopt personalized zones because they produce roughly uniform zone occupancy, as in Table 4 [PAGE 3], and then mistake occupancy for validity. The percentile construction guarantees that each player has data in every zone; it does not guarantee that zone 6 is a tissue-injury threshold.

The clock is the rolling daily window versus the weekly training microcycle. The source calculates ACWR “on a rolling daily window basis” [PAGE 1, PAGE 4], but football training and injury mechanisms are not daily-independent. A hard session on Monday may alter Tuesday recovery, Wednesday availability, and weekend match exposure. The five-day injury-lag claim [PAGE 12] means the model’s effective prediction horizon is short and overlapping. A recursive theory-of-mind audit should ask: what does the athlete know about the monitor, what does the coach do with the ratio, and how does that action change the next ratio? If the athlete modifies behavior after a spike, the observed ACWR-injury relationship is confounded by feedback. If the coach uses the ratio to taper load, the model may appear protective even if the underlying biological signal is weak. The source does not model these feedbacks, does not report blinding, and does not describe whether staff saw the ACWR before injury. That omission is a major interpretive veto: the claimed “pre-injury pattern” could be a pattern in monitoring-and-response behavior rather than a causal mechanical precursor.
