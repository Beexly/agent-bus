The source page [PAGE 1] (printed p. 108, §26 of Halmos, *Measure Theory*) presents Theorem C, a characterization of convergence in the mean (i.e., convergence in $L^1(\mu)$). The logical structure is an iff: $\{f_n\}$ converges in $L^1$ to $f$ if and only if (i) $\{f_n\}$ converges in measure to $f$, and (ii) the indefinite integrals $\lambda_n(E) = \int_E |f_n|\,d\mu$ are uniformly absolutely continuous and equicontinuous from above at $0$.

The proof of necessity begins by quoting two prior results: convergence in measure follows from 25.A [PAGE 1], and uniform absolute continuity follows from 24.C [PAGE 1]. The only remaining item is equicontinuity from above at $0$. Mean convergence supplies, for every $\varepsilon>0$, an index $n_0$ such that for all $n\ge n_0$,
$$
\int_X |f_n - f|\,d\mu < \varepsilon .
$$
Because $|f|$ is integrable, its indefinite integral $\lambda_f(E)=\int_E |f|\,d\mu$ is a finite measure by 23.I [PAGE 1]; by 9.E [PAGE 1], $\lambda_f$ is continuous from above at $0$. Hence for every decreasing sequence $\{E_m\}$ of measurable sets with $\bigcap_m E_m = \varnothing$ (the text writes $\lim E_m = 0$ [PAGE 1]), there exists $m_0$ such that $\lambda_f(E_m) < \varepsilon$ for $m\ge m_0$.

The page [PAGE 1] truncates at "Hence, if $m\ge m_0$, then we have," omitting the displayed inequality chain. The standard reconstruction is the triangle inequality on $E_m$:
$$
\int_{E_m}|f_n|\,d\mu \;\le\; \int_{E_m}|f_n-f|\,d\mu \;+\; \int_{E_m}|f|\,d\mu
\;\le\; \|f_n-f\|_1 + \lambda_f(E_m).
$$
For $n\ge n_0$ and $m\ge m_0$, both terms are small, giving $|\\lambda_n(E_m)|<\varepsilon$ uniformly in $n\ge n_0$. For the finite initial block $n=1,\dots,n_0-1$, each $\lambda_n$ is individually continuous from above at $0$ (again by 23.I and 9.E), so a finite intersection of the corresponding $m_0$ indices covers them. Taking the maximum of these indices yields a single $m_0$ valid for all $n$, which is precisely equicontinuity from above at $0$ [PAGE 1].

Second, the OCR truncation removes the exact inequality and the subsequent handling of $n<n_0$, creating a reconstruction risk: if one wrongly assumes $m_0$ may depend on $n$, one obtains only pointwise continuity of each $\lambda_n$, not equicontinuity, and the theorem's uniform quantifier ($\exists m_0\;\forall n$) collapses. Third, uniform absolute continuity (control over arbitrary small-measure sets) and equicontinuity from above (control over a prescribed decreasing nest shrinking to $\varnothing$) are distinct conditions; on finite measure spaces they interact because $\mu(E_m)\to0$, but they are not logically interchangeable without additional hypotheses. Fourth, "finite valued" set function [PAGE 1] means $\nu(E)\in\mathbb{R}$, excluding $+\infty$, which is essential for the $|\nu_n(E_m)|<\varepsilon$ formulation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operators, LaTeX, units, and page citations.

Let $(X,\mathcal{E},\mu)$ be a measure space. A set function $\nu:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{R}$ is finite valued.

**Definition (continuity from above at $0$) [PAGE 1].**
$$
\forall \{E_n\}\subset\mathcal{E},\; E_{n+1}\subseteq E_n,\;\bigcap_n E_n=\varnothing
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lim_{n\to\infty}\nu(E_n)=0 .
$$

**Definition (equicontinuity from above at $0$) [PAGE 1].**
For a sequence $\{\nu_n\}$,
$$
\forall \{E_m\}\downarrow\varnothing,\;\forall\varepsilon>0,\;\exists m_0\in\mathbb{N}\;\text{s.t.}\;\forall m\ge m_0,\;\forall n\in\mathbb{N}:\quad |\nu_n(E_m)|<\varepsilon .
$$
Note the quantifier order: $m_0$ is independent of $n$; $\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^+$ is a dimensionless tolerance.

**Theorem C [PAGE 1].** For integrable $f_n,f:X\to\mathbb{R}$,
$$
f_n \xrightarrow{L^1(\mu)} f
\iff
\Bigl( f_n \xrightarrow{\text{measure}} f \Bigr)
\;\land\;
\Bigl( \{E\mapsto \int_E |f_n|\,d\mu\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ uniformly absolutely continuous and equicontinuous from above at }0 \Bigr).
$$

**Mean-convergence hypothesis [PAGE 1].**
$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists n_0:\; n\ge n_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \int_X |f_n-f|\,d\mu < \varepsilon .
$$
Units: $\mu$ is a nonnegative extended-real measure; integrals are Lebesgue integrals in $\mathbb{R}$; $\varepsilon,\delta$ are positive reals.

**Supporting citations used in the proof [PAGE 1].**
- 23.I: indefinite integral of a non-negative integrable function is a finite measure.
- 9.E: such an indefinite integral is continuous from above at $0$.
- 25.A: yields convergence in measure.
- 24.C: yields uniform absolute continuity.

**Reconstructed missing inequality [PAGE 1].**
For the decreasing nest $\{E_m\}\downarrow\varnothing$,
$$
\int_{E_m}|f_n|\,d\mu
\le
\int_{E_m}|f_n-f|\,d\mu + \int_{E_m}|f|\,d\mu
\le
\|f_n-f\|_1 + \lambda_f(E_m).
$$
**Singularity / breakdown regime.** The argument requires each $|f_n|$ and $f$ to be integrable so that $\lambda_n,\lambda_f$ are finite measures (23.I). If $\mu(X)=\infty$ and the $f_n$ are not integrable, 23.I fails and the equicontinuity conclusion is not available. If $m_0$ is allowed to depend on $n$, equicontinuity is lost and Theorem C's sufficiency direction can fail.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage.

Theorem C in Halmos (1950), §26 [PAGE 1], is the measure-theoretic Vitali convergence theorem. Its ancestors are Vitali's 1905 theorem on uniform integrability, the Hahn–Saks theorem (1922–1924) on limits of sequences of measures, and the Vitali–Hahn–Saks theorem. Halmos chains his own earlier sections: §23.I (indefinite integrals are finite measures), §9.E (continuity from above), §24.C (uniform absolute continuity from $L^1$ convergence), and §25.A (convergence in measure). The theorem asserts that $L^1$ convergence is exactly convergence in measure plus two compactness-type conditions on the family of indefinite integrals; in modern language this is the Dunford–Pettis criterion for relatively weak compactness in $L^1$ specialized to convergent sequences.

Multi-week traps embedded in this material. (1) Trap of quantifier order: a reader may prove continuity from above for each fixed $\lambda_n$ and mistakenly claim equicontinuity, missing that $m_0$ must not depend on $n$ [PAGE 1]. This error surfaces weeks later when one tries to pass to the limit under an integral over a shrinking set and finds the bound blows up with $n$. (2) Trap of measure space finiteness: assuming $\mu(E_m)\to0$ from $E_m\downarrow\varnothing$ without verifying that the relevant set functions are finite measures; on non-$\sigma$-finite or infinite-measure spaces the continuity-from-above step via 9.E [PAGE 1] is not automatic for arbitrary set functions. (3) Trap of conflating conditions: treating uniform absolute continuity and equicontinuity from above as redundant; they control different geometries (arbitrary small sets versus prescribed nested exhaustion), and dropping either breaks the iff.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock.

Crisis habit regression: under pressure, an analyst regresses to the habit that "convergence in measure plus domination implies $L^1$ convergence," or worse, that convergence in measure alone suffices. This is the default mode when the dominating function is missing or only locally integrable. The regression produces a false positive: one believes $f_n\to f$ in mean while mass escapes through narrowing spikes or through the tails of an infinite-measure space.

Counter-trap: Theorem C [PAGE 1] is the counter-trap. It blocks the regression by requiring (i) convergence in measure, (ii) uniform absolute continuity of $\{\lambda_n\}$, and (iii) equicontinuity from above at $0$. Condition (ii) prevents mass from concentrating on sets of arbitrarily small measure; condition (iii) prevents mass from hiding in a nested sequence of sets $E_m\downarrow\varnothing$ whose indices $m$ can be chosen adversarially after seeing $n$. Together they form a tightness-and-uniform-integrability barrier that the habit-based regressor ignores.

Clock: the proof runs on two synchronized clocks. The mean-convergence clock emits $n_0(\varepsilon)$ [PAGE 1], after which $\|f_n-f\|_1$ is small. The set-function clock emits $m_0(\varepsilon,\{E_m\})$ from continuity from above of $\lambda_f$ and the finite initial block $n<n_0$. $L^1$ convergence is certified only when the two clocks overlap: for all $m\ge m_0$ and all $n$, the nest $E_m$ is small simultaneously for every $\lambda_n$. If the $m_0$-clock cannot be set independently of $n$, the equicontinuity condition fails and the mean-convergence conclusion is vetoed.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof.

Physical layer (measure-theoretic substrate): The indefinite integral $\lambda_n(E)=\int_E|f_n|\,d\mu$ must be a finite measure [PAGE 1, citing 23.I]. Veto if any $f_n\notin L^1(\mu)$, because then $\lambda_n(X)=\infty$ and the inequality $|\nu_n(E_m)|<\varepsilon$ is not defined in $\mathbb{R}$. On infinite-measure spaces, continuity from above of $\mu$ itself can fail, but the proof routes through the finite measures $\lambda_n$ via 9.E [PAGE 1], so the physical veto is specifically non-integrability, not infinite $\mu(X)$ per se.

Biomechanical/structural layer (anatomy of the sequence): The sequence $\{f_n\}$ must have uniformly controlled "tissue" across $n$. Uniform absolute continuity rules out concentration on arbitrarily small sets; equicontinuity from above rules out concentration along any decreasing nest to the empty set. A structural veto is triggered if
$$
\exists \{E_m\}\downarrow\varnothing,\;\exists\varepsilon_0>0:\;\forall m_0\;\exists m\ge m_0,\;\exists n:\;\int_{E_m}|f_n|\,d\mu\ge\varepsilon_0 .
$$
This is the negation of equicontinuity from above [PAGE 1]. Biomechanically, it means the sequence develops a stress fracture along some escaping nest that no uniform reindexing can close.

Weather layer (environmental/exogenous sets): The decreasing nest $\{E_m\}$ is the weather field—an adversarial but fixed sequence of measurable sets with empty intersection. On finite measure spaces $\mu(E_m)\to0$ automatically; on infinite measure spaces the nest may have $\mu(E_m)=\infty$ for all $m$ (e.g., $E_m=[m,\infty)$ on $\mathbb{R}$). The theorem's proof remains valid because each $\lambda_n$ is finite, but if the $f_n$ carry mass that rides the weather front to infinity (e.g., translating bumps), the uniform bound over $n$ fails.

Adjudication: A proposed $L^1$ convergence claim passes only if all three layers clear: integrability (physical), uniform-in-$n$ control over all nests (structural), and robustness to the adversarial nest $\{E_m\}$ (weather). Failure in any layer invokes the veto and Theorem C [PAGE 1] denies mean convergence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario A — Equicontinuity-from-above veto (sliding hump).
Space: $X=[0,1]$, $\mu$ Lebesgue. Let $f_n = n\,\mathbf 1_{(0,1/n)}$ and $f=0$. Then $f_n\to0$ in measure because $\mu(|f_n|>\varepsilon)=1/n\to0$. However the indefinite integrals are not equicontinuous from above at $0$ [PAGE 1]: choose the decreasing nest $E_m=(0,1/m)\downarrow\varnothing$. For $\varepsilon_0=1/2$, for any $m_0$ take $m=n=\max(m_0,1)$; then
$$
\int_{E_m}|f_n|\,d\mu = \int_0^{1/m} m\,dx = 1 \not< \tfrac12 .
$$
Prediction: Theorem C [PAGE 1] denies $L^1$ convergence; indeed $\|f_n\|_1=1$ for all $n$, so $f_n\not\to0$ in mean. Verifiable: compute $\|f_n\|_1=1$ and exhibit the nest $E_m$ defeating the equicontinuity quantifier.

Scenario B — Convergence-in-measure veto.
Space: $X=[0,1]$, $\mu$ Lebesgue. Let $f_n=1$ for all $n$ and $f=0$. The indefinite integrals are all $\lambda_n(E)=\mu(E)$, which is uniformly absolutely continuous and equicontinuous from above at $0$ [PAGE 1] (take $m_0$ from continuity of $\mu$). But $f_n$ does not converge to $f$ in measure because $\mu(|f_n-f|>\varepsilon)=1$ for $\varepsilon<1$. Prediction: Theorem C [PAGE 1] denies $L^1$ convergence; $\|f_n-f\|_1=1$. Verifiable: the structural conditions hold, yet mean convergence fails solely due to the missing measure-convergence hypothesis.

Scenario C — Finite-measure / integrability veto.
Space: $X=(0,1]$, $\mu$ Lebesgue. Let $f_n(x)=\min(n,1/x)$ and $f(x)=1/x$. Each $f_n$ is integrable, $f_n\to f$ a.e. (hence in measure on this finite space), but $f\notin L^1$ since $\int_0^1 x^{-1}dx=\infty$. The indefinite integrals $\lambda_n$ are finite measures by construction, but the limit's indefinite integral is not finite, so the step invoking 23.I [PAGE 1] for $f$ is vetoed. Moreover for the nest $E_m=(0,1/m)$, $\lambda_f(E_m)=\infty$, so no $\varepsilon$-bound can hold for the limiting object. Prediction: Theorem C [PAGE 1] is inapplicable because $f$ is not integrable; there is no $L^1$ limit in the theorem's sense. Verifiable: $\int_X f\,d\mu=\infty$ and $\lambda_f(E_m)=\infty$ for every $m$, breaking the finiteness precondition [PAGE 1].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
