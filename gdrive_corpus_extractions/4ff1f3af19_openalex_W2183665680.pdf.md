The source separates three mathematical objects that are easy to conflate: an admissible control process, the value function, and an optimality certificate. Start with \(P_{\infty}(\tau,z)\) on [PAGE 1]: minimize \(g(\xi)\) subject to \(\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),u(t))\) almost everywhere, \(x(\tau)=z\), \(u\in U\), \(x(t)\to\xi\) as \(t\to\infty\), and \(\xi\in S\subset\mathbb{R}^n\). The endpoint \(\xi\) is a decision variable, not a fixed terminal condition. The paper explicitly warns that “the value of the optimal cost depends not only on the equilibrium point, but also on the specific trajectory reaching it” [PAGE 1]. That sentence is the first trap: a finite-horizon instinct treats stabilization as steering to a fixed point in minimum time. The paper contrasts its problem with “time-optimal stabilization, i.e., finding a control that steers the state of the system to the origin in minimum time” [PAGE 1]. Thus, a proposed solution that only minimizes time to \(S\) has changed the objective.

The convergence notion is also not merely pointwise. The source states that \(x_{\bar u}(t)\to\xi\) means \(\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\tau}^{t} e^{\gamma s}\|x(s)-\xi\|\,ds<\infty\) for some \(\gamma>0\) [PAGE 1]. This is an exponentially weighted \(L^1\) condition. It implies a rate of convergence in an averaged sense; pointwise convergence alone does not supply it. This is a gray zone: the displayed problem says \(x(t)\to\xi\), but the formal definition adds exponential weighting. If a candidate trajectory converges only like \(1/t\), the formal definition may fail for every positive \(\gamma\). The proof must use the weighted condition or state an additional assumption.

Next, the Hamiltonian is defined as \(H(t,x,\eta)=\sup_{v\in\Omega}\langle\eta,f(x,v)\rangle\) [PAGE 1]. The HJB equation is written \(\varphi_t(t,x)-H(t,x,-\varphi_x)=0\) [PAGE 2]. Because \(H(t,x,-\varphi_x)=-\inf_{v}\langle\varphi_x,f(x,v)\rangle\), the equation is equivalent to \(\varphi_t+\inf_v\langle\varphi_x,f(x,v)\rangle=0\). The proximal form uses \(\eta_0-H(t,x,-\eta)=0\) for every \((\eta_0,\eta)\in\partial_P v(t,x)\) when the proximal subgradient is nonempty [PAGE 2]. This sign convention is a second trap. A verifier should check whether a candidate uses the proximal subgradient or supergradient and whether the inequality direction matches the minimization problem.

The verification-function theorem is the sufficiency bridge. A lower semicontinuous local verification function \(\varphi\) on a tube \(T(\bar x,\epsilon)\) must satisfy four conditions [PAGE 2]: for proximal subgradients, \(\eta_0+\min_{u\in\Omega}\langle\eta,f(x,u)\rangle\ge0\); for all \(\xi\in S\), \(\liminf_{t\to\infty}\varphi(t,\xi)\le g(\xi)\); a limit-consistency condition for \(\xi\in S\cap[\bar\xi+\epsilon B]\); and \(\varphi(\tau,z)=g(\bar\xi)\). Condition 1 makes \(\varphi\) nondecreasing along admissible arcs: if \((\eta_0,\eta)\in\partial_P\varphi\) and \(u\) is chosen, then \(\eta_0+\langle\eta,f(x,u)\rangle\ge\eta_0+\min_u\langle\eta,f(x,u)\rangle\ge0\). The terminal lower bound then yields \(g(\xi)\ge\varphi(\tau,z)=g(\bar\xi)\). This is the rigorous route from a certificate to optimality.

The feedback synthesis algorithm is a dynamic-programming implementation. It uses a partition \(\pi=\{t_k\}\) with diameter \(h_\pi=\sup_k\Delta_k\), a proximal point \(p_S(x)\), and the set \(U(x)=\{u\in\Omega:\langle N_S^P(p_S(x)),f(x,u)\rangle\le0\}\) [PAGE 3]. The control at step \(k\) maximizes \(\varphi(t_k,x_k+\Delta_k f(x_k,u))\) over \(u\in U(x_k)\), and the trajectory is obtained by integrating the dynamics on the subinterval [PAGE 3]. The theorem asserts a cluster point and an associated optimal process. This is not a continuous-time exact feedback law; it is an approximating recursive scheme. A gray zone is the passage to a cluster point: the theorem gives existence of a convergent subsequence, not necessarily uniqueness of the limit.

The necessary conditions in Section 3 concern a “variant” of \(P_{\infty}(\tau,z)\) with the mixed constraint \(h(x,u)=x^{T}f(x,u)/\|x\|^{2}+\gamma\le0\) [PAGE 3]. The pseudo-Hamiltonian is \(H(x,p,q,u)=p^{T}f(x,u)+qh(x,u)\). The maximum principle gives \(-\dot p(t)\in\operatorname{co}\partial_x H(x^*(t),p(t),q(t),u^*(t))\) almost everywhere, the transversality condition \(\lim_{s\to\infty}p(s)\in-\lambda\partial_x g(\xi^*)-N_S(\xi^*)\), \(q(t)\le0\) and \(q(t)h(x^*(t),u^*(t))=0\) almost everywhere, and pointwise maximization of \(H\) over \(\Omega\) [PAGE 3]. The proof outline uses finite-horizon truncation, Ekeland’s variational principle, and limit extraction [PAGE 4]. A critical audit point is that these necessary conditions are for the mixed-constraint variant, not automatically for every original stabilizing process. Also, \(h\) has a denominator \(\|x\|^2\): at \(x=0\) the expression is singular. If the target equilibrium is not the origin, the displayed \(h\) does not transparently certify convergence to \(\xi\); a shifted form \((x-\xi)^T f/\|x-\xi\|^2+\gamma\le0\) would be the natural analogue. This is a source-level gray zone that should be flagged rather than silently repaired.

Additional breakdown regimes: if \(f\) fails local Lipschitz continuity in \(x\) or the linear growth bound \(f(t,x,u)\in c(1+\|x\|)B\) [PAGE 2], the compactness and limit arguments can fail. If \(f(t,x,\Omega)\) is not convex-valued, the HJB theorem’s hypotheses are not met [PAGE 2]. If \(\Omega\) is not compact or \(S\) lacks an equilibrium point, the feasible set may be empty or compactness may fail. If the uniform interior condition \(H6\) fails, mixed-constraint multiplier behavior can degenerate. If the proximal subgradient is empty, the proximal HJB equation is vacuous at that point, so a verifier must not infer a contradiction from an absent subgradient.

Formal operators, units, page citations, and breakdown.

The source problem [PAGE 1] is
\[
P_{\infty}(\tau,z):\quad \min g(\xi)
\]
subject to
\[
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),u(t))\ \text{a.a. }t\ge\tau,\quad
x(\tau)=z,\quad u\in U,\quad
x(t)\to\xi\ \text{as }t\to\infty,\quad \xi\in S\subset\mathbb{R}^n,
\]
where
\[
U:=\{u\in L^{\infty}[\tau,\infty):u(t)\in\Omega\ \text{a.a. }t\ge\tau\}
\]
and \(\Omega\) is compact in \(\mathbb{R}^m\) [PAGE 1]. Unit bookkeeping: \(t\) has time units, \(x\) has state units, \(f\) has state per time, \(u\) has control units, \(g\) has cost units, and \(\gamma\) has inverse-time units. If variables are normalized, all units may be treated as dimensionless.

The asymptotic convergence definition is explicitly weighted:
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\gamma s}\|x(s)-\xi\|\,ds<\infty
\quad\text{for some }\gamma>0
\]
[PAGE 1]. This is stronger than bare pointwise convergence and imposes an exponential weighted \(L^1\) rate. The source’s Hamiltonian is
\[
H(t,x,\eta):=\sup_{v\in\Omega}\{\langle\eta,f(x,v)\rangle\}
\]
[PAGE 1]. The HJB equation is
\[
\varphi_t(t,x)-H(t,x,-\varphi_x)=0
\]
for all \((t,x)\in[\tau,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^n\) wherever the relevant derivatives exist [PAGE 2]. In proximal form, a lower semicontinuous extended-valued \(v\) satisfies
\[
\eta_0-H(t,x,-\eta)=0
\quad\forall(\eta_0,\eta)\in\partial_P v(t,x)
\]
whenever \(\partial_P v(t,x)\neq\varnothing\) [PAGE 2]. The verification-function conditions [PAGE 2] include
\[
\eta_0+\min_{u\in\Omega}\langle\eta,f(x,u)\rangle\ge0
\quad\forall(\eta_0,\eta)\in\partial_P\varphi(t,x),
\]
\[
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\varphi(t,\xi)\le g(\xi)\quad\forall\xi\in S,
\]
\[
\liminf_{t\uparrow\infty,\xi'\to\xi}\varphi(t,\xi')
=\liminf_{t\uparrow\infty}\varphi(t,\xi)
\quad\forall\xi\in S\cap[\bar\xi+\epsilon B],
\]
and
\[
\varphi(\tau,z)=g(\bar\xi).
\]
The source states that existence of this local verification function is necessary and sufficient for a strong local minimizer [PAGE 2]. A subtle operator is the proximal normal cone used in the synthesis algorithm:
\[
U(x):=\{u\in\Omega:\langle N_S^P(p_S(x)),f(x,u)\rangle\le0\}
\]
[PAGE 3], where \(p_S(x)\) is the proximal point of \(x\) at \(S\). The recursive control is
\[
\bar u_k^\pi\in
\arg\max_{u\in U(x^\pi(t_k^\pi))}
\varphi\left(t_k^\pi,x^\pi(t_k^\pi)+\Delta_k f(x^\pi(t_k^\pi),u)\right)
\]
[PAGE 3]. The theorem gives a cluster point and an optimal process under hypotheses \(H1\)–\(H5\) [PAGE 3].

For the necessary-condition variant, the source adds the mixed inequality
\[
h(x,u):=\frac{x^{T}f(x,u)}{\|x\|^{2}}+\gamma\le0,
\]
with \(\gamma>0\) [PAGE 3]. The pseudo-Hamiltonian is
\[
H(x,p,q,u):=p^{T}f(x,u)+qh(x,u).
\]
The maximum principle states that for an optimal process \((x^*,u^*)\) there exist an absolutely continuous \(p:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^n\), an \(L^1\) function \(q:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\), and \(\lambda\ge0\) such that
\[
-\dot p(t)\in\operatorname{co}\partial_x H(x^*(t),p(t),q(t),u^*(t))\quad\text{a.e.},
\]
\[
\lim_{s\to\infty}p(s)\in-\lambda\partial_x g(\xi^*)-N_S(\xi^*),
\]
\[
q(t)\le0\quad\text{a.e.},\qquad
q(t)h(x^*(t),u^*(t))=0\quad\text{a.e.},
\]
and
\[
u^*(t)\text{ maximizes a.e. the mapping }
v\mapsto H(x^*(t),p(t),q(t),\lambda,v)\text{ on }\Omega
\]
[PAGE 3]. Here \(N_S(\xi)\) and \(\partial f(\xi)\) are in the Clarke sense [PAGE 4]. The proof outline is truncation to \([0,T]\), Ekeland’s variational principle, and limit extraction [PAGE 4].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit and implicit. First, \(h\) is singular at \(x=0\) because of \(\|x\|^2\); if the target set contains the origin, the constraint must be interpreted on trajectories away from the singularity or in a shifted coordinate system. Second, the displayed \(h\) is not obviously a contraction condition toward a nonzero \(\xi\); the natural shifted version is \((x-\xi)^{T}f/\|x-\xi\|^2+\gamma\le0\). Third, the HJB theorem requires \(H1\)–\(H5\), including local Lipschitz continuity, linear growth, convex-valuedness of \(f(t,x,\Omega)\), compactness of \(\Omega\), and lower semicontinuity of \(g\) [PAGE 2]. Fourth, the maximum-principle theorem requires its own \(H1\)–\(H6\), including Borel measurability, closed bounded \(S\) and \(\Omega\), an equilibrium in \(S\), convexity of the lifted set \(\{(f(x,u),h(x,u)+v):u\in\Omega,v\ge0\}\), and a uniform interior condition \(\inf\{h(x,u):u\in\Omega\}\le-\delta\) [PAGE 3]. If these fail, the theorem is not a valid certificate. Fifth, the first section says \(S\) is closed [PAGE 1], while the later set hypothesis says \(S\) is closed and bounded [PAGE 3]; compactness is therefore an added requirement for the maximum-principle section. Sixth, the synthesis theorem gives a cluster point, not a uniqueness or global convergence statement for every partition sequence.

The infinite-horizon value-function lineage is represented by Baumeister, Leitao, and Silva [PAGE 4]. This genealogy matters because it shows that the paper is not inventing a new calculus; it is splicing dynamic programming, nonsmooth analysis, and mixed-constraint maximum principles under an asymptotic target-set constraint.

The multi-week traps are predictable. Week-one trap: treating \(x(t)\to\xi\) as ordinary pointwise convergence and ignoring the weighted integral [PAGE 1]. Week-two trap: replacing the trajectory-dependent objective with time-to-target minimization; the paper explicitly distinguishes its problem from “time-optimal stabilization” [PAGE 1]. Week-three trap: assuming the HJB equation has a classical \(C^1\) solution; the paper uses viscosity and proximal subgradient notions precisely to avoid that assumption [PAGE 2]. Week-four trap: proving optimality from the maximum principle alone. The maximum principle is necessary, not sufficient; the sufficiency certificate is the verification function [PAGE 2]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive verifier should model three possible regressions by an analyst under complexity pressure. The first is crisis habit regression to finite-horizon optimal control: the analyst replaces the infinite-horizon target-set problem with a terminal-time problem and imposes a terminal constraint at \(T\). The source’s truncation logic does use finite-horizon auxiliary problems [PAGE 4], but only as an approximation device followed by limit extraction; it does not replace the original objective with a fixed-\(T\) objective. The counter-trap is to preserve the infinite-horizon transversality condition and the weighted convergence definition [PAGE 1, PAGE 3]. The second regression is time-optimal stabilization: the analyst treats the goal as reaching \(S\) as fast as possible. The paper explicitly says this is “substantially different” from the usual optimal stabilization, which it identifies with time-optimal stabilization [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is to retain \(g(\xi)\) and the trajectory-dependent cost, not merely the hitting time. The third regression is heuristic penalty replacement: the analyst adds \(e^{\gamma t}\|x(t)-\xi\|\) to the cost and declares the problem solved. The paper does use a penalized auxiliary problem \(P_{\infty}^{l}(\tau,z)\) with \(\int_{\tau+l}^{\infty}e^{\gamma t}\|x(t)-\xi\|\,dt\) [PAGE 2], but the penalty vanishes as \(l\to\infty\); it is a proof and synthesis device, not a license to change the original objective permanently.

The “clock” is not a single wall-clock stopping time. It is the triple \((\gamma,\pi,T)\): \(\gamma\) sets the exponential rate, \(\pi=\{t_k\}\) sets the synthesis partition with diameter \(h_\pi\) [PAGE 3], and \(T\) is the finite truncation parameter used in the necessary-condition proof [PAGE 4]. A recursive verifier should ask whether a proposed solution respects all three clocks. If it uses a fixed \(T\) as the actual horizon, it has collapsed the infinite horizon. If it uses a partition but ignores the constraint \(U(x)\) built from the proximal normal cone [PAGE 3], it has broken the target-set geometry. If it uses the mixed constraint \(h\) without checking the sign of \(q\) and the complementarity condition [PAGE 3], it has broken the multiplier logic. The counter-trap is not to add more penalties but to demand a verification function or a maximum-principle certificate that is source-faithful. The paper’s own recursive structure is clear: verification function gives sufficiency [PAGE 2], the feedback algorithm recursively maximizes the certificate over admissible descent controls [PAGE 3], and the maximum principle supplies necessary conditions for the rate-constrained variant [PAGE 3].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto must be stated as a logical admissibility test, not as a narrative preference. The proof structure is: if a proposed control or claim violates a hypothesis or constraint required by the cited theorem, then the theorem’s conclusion is not entailed for that proposal. This is a veto on inference, not a claim that the physical system is impossible.

Physical-layer veto. The synthesis theorem requires \(H1\)–\(H5\): local Lipschitz continuity, linear growth \(f(t,x,u)\in c(1+\|x\|)B\), convex-valuedness of \(f(t,x,\Omega)\), compactness of \(\Omega\), and lower semicontinuity of \(g\) [PAGE 2]. The maximum-principle theorem additionally requires closed bounded \(S\) and \(\Omega\), an equilibrium in \(S\), convexity of the lifted mixed-constraint set, and the uniform interior condition \(H6\) [PAGE 3]. If an actuator command lies outside \(\Omega\), the control is inadmissible by definition of \(U\) [PAGE 1]. If unmodeled actuator dynamics alter \(f\) so that the growth or Lipschitz hypotheses fail, the theorem cannot be invoked. The paper’s application list includes MEMS and rigid-body systems [PAGE 1], but those names do not override the mathematical hypotheses; they only illustrate possible domains.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The mixed constraint \(h(x,u)=x^{T}f/\|x\|^{2}+\gamma\le0\) is a rate condition [PAGE 3]. If a biomechanical model cannot satisfy that rate within its actuator limits, the rate-constrained maximum principle does not certify the process. If the state approaches the singular set \(x=0\), the constraint is not evaluable as written; a shifted or limiting formulation is required. If the target equilibrium is not the origin, the displayed \(h\) does not transparently certify convergence to \(\xi\), so a biomechanical claim that cites \(h\) to prove convergence to a nonzero target is under-justified. The source mentions “biological, medical, and health care systems [7]” as an application area [PAGE 1], but that does not supply a biomechanical model; the model must still satisfy the formal assumptions. A veto applies when a proposed biomechanical interpretation uses the theorem while violating the contraction rate, the control bounds, or the singularity domain.

Weather-layer veto. Weather is not a formal operator in this corpus. The source’s dynamics are \(f(x,u)\) [PAGE 1], not \(f(t,x,u,w(t))\) with an exogenous weather state. Therefore, a weather disturbance can enter only if it is modeled as part of the state, control, or time-varying dynamics satisfying the source’s hypotheses. If weather is an unmodeled external disturbance, the asymptotic convergence theorem does not automatically guarantee robustness. The correct adjudication is: a weather-based claim cannot veto a mathematical result by assertion, but a mathematical certificate cannot be transferred to a weather-disturbed system unless the disturbance is included in \(f\) and the hypotheses \(H1\)–\(H6\) are checked, or a robust extension is proved separately. The source gives no weather layer, so any such claim is outside the cited evidence.

The cross-layer veto proof is therefore a three-part check: (1) domain constraint, meaning \(u(t)\in\Omega\) and \(x\) stays in the domain where \(h\) is defined; (2) hypothesis constraint, meaning all cited \(H1\)–\(H6\) conditions hold; (3) certificate constraint, meaning the proposed solution satisfies the verification-function inequalities or the maximum-principle equations, not merely a physical narrative. If any part fails, the theorem’s conclusion is vetoed for that proposal.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
