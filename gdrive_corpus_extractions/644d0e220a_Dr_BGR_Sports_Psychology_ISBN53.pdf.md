\]
where \(C\) is challenge and \(S\) is skill; \(\varepsilon\) is a small tolerance. Anxiety if \(C\gg S\); boredom if \(S\gg C\) [PAGE 145–146].

7) Mental toughness 4Cs vector (Clough et al., 2002; described [PAGE 101–102]):
\[
\mathbf{M} = [C_{\mathrm{ontrol}},\,C_{\mathrm{ommitment}},\,C_{\mathrm{hallange}},\,C_{\mathrm{onfidence}}]\in[0,1]^{4}
\]

8) Lazarus and Folkman (1984) coping selection [PAGE 117–119]:
\[
\sigma^{*} = \arg\min_{\sigma\in\{\mathrm{problem},\,\mathrm{emotion}\}}\mathcal{L}(\text{controllability},\sigma)
\]
Problem-focused coping when the stressor is perceived controllable; emotion-focused when uncontrollable [PAGE 117–119].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage traced across the text’s cited genealogy, with attention to multi-week temporal traps.

Lineage. The text locates roots in Norman Triplett’s 1898 social-facilitation experiment with cyclists [PAGE 26], Pierre de Coubertin’s 1903 emphasis on psychological preparation for the modern Olympics [PAGE 26], and Coleman Griffith’s 1925 University of Illinois laboratory and work with the Chicago Cubs [PAGE 27]. Institutionalization is marked by the 1965 formation of the International Society of Sport Psychology (ISSP) and the 1967 Rome World Congress [PAGE 28]. Theoretical foundations invoked later include Yerkes and Dodson (1908) for the Inverted-U [PAGE 111], Lazarus and Folkman (1984) for problem-/emotion-focused coping [PAGE 117–119], Bandura (1986) for social learning [PAGE 87], Deci and Ryan (1985) for Self-Determination Theory [PAGE 56], Locke and Latham for Goal-Setting Theory [PAGE 63], Csikszentmihalyi (1990) for flow [PAGE 145], Hanin for IZOF [PAGE 139], and Clough et al. (2002) for the 4Cs mental-toughness model [PAGE 101].

Multi-week traps. (1) PST integration is staged—education, acquisition, practice, application [PAGE 190–193]—but the text supplies no week-level dwell times; premature transition from acquisition to application can leave skills unconsolidated under competitive pressure. (2) Injury rehabilitation proceeds through graded return-to-play with psychological readiness scales [PAGE 197–198]; return-to-play decisions made before physiological tissue healing complete create a multi-week reinjury trap. (3) Burnout is described as chronic, long-term pressure [PAGE 107–108], meaning acute weekly monitoring may miss slow-onset depletion. (4) Team development from storming to norming requires conflict mediation over repeated interactions [PAGE 154–159]; a single intervention cannot collapse the stage transition. (5) Trait anxiety is stable over time while state anxiety fluctuates moment-to-moment [PAGE 109–110]; interventions targeting state spikes without addressing trait floors produce short-lived effects and retrocausal misattribution (blaming today’s slump on last week’s anxiety without lag quantification). (6) Personality traits are described as stable and enduring [PAGE 85–86], yet coaching expectations assume within-season malleability; this timescale mismatch is an unexamined retrocausal trap: current performance is explained by prior traits, but no decay constant or lag operator is specified.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock mechanisms embedded in coach–athlete mutual modeling.

Crisis habit regression. Under acute stress, attentional narrowing and reliance on dominant, overlearned habits increase [PAGE 130–131]. Athletes high in trait anxiety perceive competitive environments as more threatening [PAGE 109–110], elevating state anxiety \(c\). When \(c>c_{\mathrm{crit}}\) and physiological arousal \(a\) crosses the fold, Catastrophe Theory predicts a discontinuous performance drop rather than gradual decline [PAGE 112–113]. Regression manifests as loss of focus, negative self-talk, and abandonment of task-oriented cues [PAGE 130–131, 186–187].

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is a recursive Theory of Mind loop: the coach models the athlete’s internal state (confidence, anxiety, focus) and selects an intervention; the athlete models the coach’s intent and updates self-regulation. PST tools—self-talk, imagery, cue words, pre-performance routines—operate as counter-traps designed to interrupt regression [PAGE 135–136, 186–190]. Leadership style must match the recursive state: autocratic direction during acute crisis [PAGE 164–165], shifting to democratic or transformational styles once the athlete’s modeled autonomy stabilizes [PAGE 165–168]. Misattribution—reading stress as insufficient effort and responding with autocratic pressure—can push \(c\) above critical and trigger the catastrophe fold [PAGE 112–113, 164–165].

Clock. Time is an exogenous constraint that couples all layers. Pre-performance routines impose fixed temporal structure (e.g., a basketball player’s fixed free-throw sequence [PAGE 135]); countdown to competition elevates state anxiety [PAGE 109]; simulation training rehearses decisions under time pressure [PAGE 136]; PST’s application phase explicitly occurs in competitive, time-constrained environments [PAGE 192]. The clock converts slow trait vulnerability into acute state risk: as \(t\rightarrow t_{\mathrm{competition}}\), \(c\) rises, narrowing attention and reducing the effective IZOF band [PAGE 109–110, 139–140]. Recursive modeling under deadline pressure can amplify error: coach and athlete each update beliefs about the other’s readiness with lagged, noisy observations, producing oscillations in confidence and effort that degrade cohesion [PAGE 160–162, 171–173].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, weather, and psychological veto layers; formal adjudication and proof that psychology cannot veto hard constraints.

Veto layers. Define \(\mathcal{V}=V_{\mathrm{phys}}\lor V_{\mathrm{bio}}\lor V_{\mathrm{weather}}\lor V_{\mathrm{psych}}\), where each \(V\) is a Boolean veto.

Physical layer veto \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}\): injury, illness, fatigue, sleep deprivation [PAGE 130–132; 194–198]. Proof of non-overridability: Psychological rehabilitation addresses emotional coping, imagery, self-talk, and return-to-play confidence [PAGE 194–198], but cannot accelerate tissue healing. An athlete may score high on psychological-readiness scales while the repaired tissue remains mechanically insufficient; \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}=1\) nullifies \(\neg V_{\mathrm{psych}}\).

Biomechanical layer veto \(V_{\mathrm{bio}}\): faulty technique, motor-skill deficit, limited range of motion [PAGE 130–132; 187–188]. Proof: Imagery and visualization reinforce motor patterns and build confidence [PAGE 121–123; 187–188], but they cannot correct incorrect biomechanics without physical correction. The SMART "Achievable" criterion must respect current biomechanical capacity [PAGE 68–69]; a goal that violates \(V_{\mathrm{bio}}\) is unrealistic regardless of motivation.

Weather/environmental layer veto \(V_{\mathrm{weather}}\): heat, cold, wind, precipitation, noise, altitude [PAGE 130–132]. Proof: External distractions and weather conditions divert attention and degrade concentration [PAGE 130–132]. Arousal regulation and mindfulness cannot fully compensate for hypoxia, hyperthermia, or high wind altering projectile flow.

Psychological layer veto \(V_{\mathrm{psych}}\): anxiety, attentional lapse, low confidence, poor self-talk [PAGE 105–124; 186–190]. This is the layer the text addresses most intensively, but it is subordinate to hard layers.

Adjudication rule. Priority in hard-veto systems:
\[
V_{\mathrm{phys}}\succ V_{\mathrm{bio}}\succ V_{\mathrm{weather}}\succ V_{\mathrm{psych}}
\]
If \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}=1\), no amount of \(V_{\mathrm{psych}}=0\) (psychological readiness) yields performance clearance. Example: a golfer executing a precise putt in high wind [PAGE 130–132] cannot use flow state [PAGE 145–148] to override the aerodynamic veto on ball trajectory. Example: an injured athlete cleared psychologically but failing isokinetic strength symmetry (\(<90\%\)) carries a vetoed return-to-play decision [PAGE 197–198].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Sprinter catastrophe veto. Counterfactual: an elite 100 m sprinter with high trait anxiety [PAGE 109–110] starts a final; if pre-race cognitive anxiety \(c>c_{\mathrm{crit}}\) and arousal \(a\) exceeds the athlete’s IZOF upper bound \(A_i^{+}\), Catastrophe Theory predicts a discontinuous finish-time degradation [PAGE 112–113]. Veto: \(V_{\mathrm{psych}}=1\) via anxiety–arousal interaction. Verifiable metrics: 100 m split time, pre-race HRV and heart rate, state-anxiety inventory score, false-start incidence. Prediction: races in which measured \(c\) is above threshold and \(a>A_i^{+}\) exhibit finish-time degradation \(>0.15\) s relative to personal best, with a nonlinear drop after the fold; no gradual Inverted-U decay is observed.

Scenario 2 — Return-to-play physical veto. Counterfactual: a post-ACL athlete demonstrates high psychological readiness (imagery, self-talk, SMART rehab goals) [PAGE 194–198], but biomechanical landing symmetry \(<90\%\) and quadriceps strength deficit persist. Veto: \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}\lor V_{\mathrm{bio}}=1\) overrides \(\neg V_{\mathrm{psych}}\). Verifiable metrics: isokinetic symmetry index, single-hop distance symmetry, biomechanical landing kinematics, psychological readiness scale score, 12-month reinjury incidence. Prediction: athletes cleared by psychological scales but failing biomechanical benchmarks show reinjury risk within 12 months statistically indistinguishable from—or higher than—psychologically unready athletes; psychological variables alone cannot predict return success without physical benchmarks.

Scenario 3 — Team storming-stage leadership veto. Counterfactual: a newly formed team remains in Tuckman’s storming stage (conflict, role ambiguity, power struggles) [PAGE 154–156] beyond four weeks; leadership applies only laissez-faire or purely transformational style without structured conflict mediation [PAGE 164–169]. Veto: \(V_{\mathrm{psych}}=1\) via relationship/process conflict blocking task cohesion [PAGE 160–162; 171–173]. Verifiable metrics: Group Environment Questionnaire cohesion score, weekly conflict-log frequency, communication-network density, win/loss or outcome record. Prediction: teams unmediated past the four-week storming threshold show task cohesion \(<0.6\) (normalized) and outcome-variance increase; introducing autocratic mediation in crisis followed by democratic norming shortens the stage and raises cohesion above 0.7 within two additional weeks.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
