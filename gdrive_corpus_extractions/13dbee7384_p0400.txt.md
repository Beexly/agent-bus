Veto proof: if the boy’s upper limb is injured, if no stone is present, or if the toad is outside the effective throwing range, then $\mathcal{A}_t = 0$ regardless of the magnitude of $X_t$ or the sign of $\mathbb{E}(R)$. The source text under-specifies the actuator, so any inference from impulse to stone-throwing is biomechanically underdetermined.

Weather/environmental layer. Toads are temperature- and moisture-sensitive; boys’ access to ponds is weather-dependent. James’s analysis is stimulus-contingent on the toad’s presence but never models environmental availability. Veto proof: if rain drives toads into burrows or keeps boys indoors, then $s_t = \varnothing$, $\mathcal{I}(s_t) = 0$, and no expectation can form. The psychological mechanism is vacuous without an environmental trigger.

Adjudication: James’s theory is valid only as a conditional psychological model: given a stimulus, given memory of consequences, and given an intact actuator and permissive environment, expectation re-enforces or inhibits impulse. [PAGE 400] Outside those conditions, lower-layer (biomechanical) and upper-layer (weather/institutional) vetoes dominate, and the text supplies no parameters to calculate their thresholds. The breakdown regime is cross-layer: predicting real-world behavior from the text alone without embodiment data is invalid.

Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. In each, a veto operator $\mathcal{V}$ interrupts the memory–expectation link; the prediction is stated as a conditional probability over observable behavior $\mathcal{A}_{t+1}$.

Scenario 1 — Pharmacological consolidation veto. Administer a beta-adrenergic blocker immediately after the boy witnesses the dying toad, blocking emotional memory consolidation. Veto: prevents formation of the inhibitory expectation $\mathbb{E}(R \mid s_{\text{toad}})$. Prediction: At a toad re-encounter one week later, $P(\mathcal{A}_{t+1}=\text{smash} \mid \mathcal{V}) > P(\mathcal{A}_{t+1}=\text{smash} \mid \neg \mathcal{V})$, and protective behavior is absent. Verifiable by blind behavioral coding of approach, stone selection, and striking across repeated encounters. This tests James’s claim that “something in the expression … suggests the meanness” and later “prompts kindly actions.” [PAGE 400]

Scenario 2 — Sensory cue veto. During the first smashing, prevent the boy from viewing the toad’s dying expression (e.g., opaque screen) while allowing the act and its auditory consequences. Veto: removes the specific visual cue that triggers the association with human-like suffering and the recalled saying about animal pain. Prediction: $P(\mathcal{A}_{t+1}=\text{kindness} \mid \mathcal{V}) \approx P(\mathcal{A}_{t+1}=\text{kindness} \mid \text{baseline impulse})$, i.e., no above-baseline kindness on later encounters. Verifiable by measuring kindness frequency across a cohort with occluded versus unoccluded first encounters. This isolates whether the visual “clasped hands” representation is necessary for the revaluation James describes. [PAGE 400]

Scenario 3 — Temporal contiguity veto. Delay the visible death/suffering display of the toad by 24 hours after the smashing act. Veto: breaks the temporal contiguity required for associative credit assignment, so the boy does not attribute the outcome to his prior impulse. Prediction: $P(\mathcal{A}_{t+1}=\text{smash} \mid \text{delayed outcome}) > P(\mathcal{A}_{t+1}=\text{smash} \mid \text{immediate outcome})$ at the next same-day encounter. Verifiable by comparing impulse re-expression between immediate-feedback and 24-hour-delay groups. This tests the model’s breakdown regime at outcome latency $\to \infty$, where $\mathcal{M}$ cannot bind stimulus to consequence. [PAGE 400]

Each scenario yields a falsifiable inequality and identifies a precise regime in which James’s qualitative claim fails when the memory–expectation loop is experimentally severed.
