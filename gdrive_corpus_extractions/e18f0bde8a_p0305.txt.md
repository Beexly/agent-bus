<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From the organism’s perspective, the recursive theory of mind unfolds across three layers: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression: when drive or motivational intensity rises (a crisis state), ER increases. Because sER = ER − [IR + sIR], a sufficiently large ER can push sER back above threshold despite the presence of sIR. The organism therefore regresses to the previously reinforced habit under stress, not because the inhibitory habit vanished, but because the excitatory term temporarily overwhelms it. This is a regression to the pre-extinction attractor, mediated by high drive rather than erasure of sIR.

Counter-trap: extinction in context A conditions sIR to the stimuli present at cessation of action [PAGE 298]. If the organism is later tested in context B, the conditioned inhibitory stimuli may be absent, so sIR does not transfer (or transfers only partially via stimulus generalization). The counter-trap is therefore renewal: the subject “expects” reinforcement in B because the inhibitory cue configuration of A is missing, and the habit re-emerges. An agent reasoning recursively about this would predict relapse whenever the test context differs from the extinction context along dimensions that were present during response cessation.

Clock: IR dissipates spontaneously through the passage of time [PAGE 298], while sIR does not [PAGE 298]. The clock variable is t since last response/extinction trial. Short t: IR high, sER deeply suppressed, no recovery. Intermediate t: IR decayed, sIR still present, partial spontaneous recovery. Long t: same partial asymptote unless new learning occurs. The clock thus separates transient state inhibition (fast) from persistent inhibitory habit (slow), and a recursive agent must track both time-since-extinction and context similarity to predict response probability.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
