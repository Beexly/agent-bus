This source must be treated as a formal claim about latent tactical segmentation, not as a betting or market-style inference object. The central reasoning chain begins with the paper’s assertion that football phases are “intention-driven temporal processes rather than static spatial configurations” [PAGE 2]. That claim converts an apparently spatial football problem into a sequence-latent variable problem: at each tracked frame, the true tactical intention is unobserved, and the model must infer it from player graphs, global context, event context, and temporal dependencies.

The first multi-hop derivation is: tracking frames at 25 Hz [PAGE 8] are transformed into frame-level player-interaction graphs. Each frame produces player embeddings through a graph neural network, and those embeddings are pooled by role-conditioned attention into a frame representation. The frame representations are then contextualized by a Transformer encoder, with action features and event-biased attention influencing the temporal sequence. Finally, a classification head outputs frame-wise probabilities, followed by deterministic filtering and rule-based correction. This pipeline means that phase recognition is not a single-step spatial classifier; it is a hierarchy of relational encoding, temporal contextualization, probabilistic classification, and post-hoc temporal regularization.

The second multi-hop derivation concerns evaluation. The paper explicitly warns that “conventional frame-level evaluation is insufficient” [PAGE 2] because frame-level F1 can remain high while predicted segments are temporally fragmented. The authors therefore introduce temporal Intersection over Union, tIoU, defined as temporal intersection divided by temporal union for overlapping ground-truth and predicted segments [PAGE 9]. This creates a gray zone: a model may classify many individual frames correctly but still fail to produce coherent tactical segments. The source demonstrates this by showing that filtering can increase mean class-wise tIoU substantially, for intentions from 0.44 to 0.67 and for phases from 0.40 to 0.57 in aggregate [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit must therefore distinguish three different objects: frame correctness, segment coherence, and post-processed usability.

The third derivation is about model specialization. The paper finds that Transformer-based sequence modeling is the main driver of coherent segmentation, while graph-based relational modeling is most beneficial for Counter Attack [PAGE 1]. This is not a trivial performance note; it implies an operator decomposition. Stable phases such as Build Up, Progression, Maintenance, and Sustained Threat depend heavily on temporal continuity. Counter Attack depends on rapid relational reconfiguration after possession regain. Finishing is event-anchored and short, so temporal boundary precision dominates the error. The source supports this by noting that “Counter Attack is the phase where T-GAN shows its clearest advantage” [PAGE 13] and that Finishing remains the most difficult phase because “small boundary shifts can strongly reduce tIoU” [PAGE 14].

A deeper gray-zone audit concerns the validity of labels themselves. The annotations were performed by a single researcher [PAGE 8], and the authors acknowledge that this prevents inter-rater reliability assessment [PAGE 19]. Thus, even a perfect model could only approximate the operational label distribution, not necessarily an objective tactical truth. The source also uses a deterministic filter that removes short isolated fluctuations, with k = 10 frames for finishing and k = 50 frames for other intentions and phases [PAGE 8]. This creates a possible bias: rare but genuine short phases may be erased. The paper’s own misalignment analysis admits that missed pre-shot Finishing is a known failure mode [PAGE 14], although a lenient criterion recovers most shot-related cues.

The final reasoning layer is the downstream-use question. The authors compare observed, identified, and fixed labels using phase distribution, duration, movement, and spatial indicators, and report no corrected significant differences after Holm correction [PAGE 15]. This supports the claim that aggregate tactical profiling may be robust to boundary errors, but it does not prove frame-exact tactical validity. Therefore, the safest reasoning conclusion is: the framework is credible for coarse phase-structure profiling and automated annotation assistance, but fragile for fine-grained event-boundary tasks, especially Counter Attack, Finishing, and intention-ambiguous circulation.

The model begins with a frame-level graph operator. For frame \(t\), the player interaction graph is \(G_t\), and the graph neural network produces player embeddings:
\[
H_t = \mathrm{GNN}(G_t),
\qquad
H_t = \{h_{t,1}, h_{t,2}, \ldots, h_{t,N_t}\},
\]
where \(N_t\) is the number of valid player nodes in frame \(t\) [PAGE 26]. Each \(h_{t,i}\) is a vector in the learned GNN embedding space; its units are dimensionless learned representation coordinates, not SI physical quantities.

Role-conditioned attention is then defined. For player \(i\), with positional role \(r_i\) and role embedding \(e_{r_i}\), the unnormalized attention score is:
\[
s_{t,i} = \mathrm{MLP}_{att}\left(\left[h_{t,i} \parallel e_{r_i}\right]\right),
\]
where \(\parallel\) denotes concatenation [PAGE 26]. Team-normalized attention weights are:
\[
\alpha_{t,i} =
\frac{\exp(s_{t,i})}
{\sum_{j \in G(c_i)} \exp(s_{t,j})},
\]
where \(c_i \in \{0,1\}\) indicates defending or possessing team membership, and \(G(c_i)\) is the valid player set of the same team group [PAGE 26]. The frame representation is the weighted sum:
\[
f_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N_t} \alpha_{t,i} h_{t,i}.
\]
These equations are probabilistic in the attention-weight sense: \(\alpha_{t,i} \geq 0\) and, within each team group, weights normalize to unity. If interpreted as information weights, the entropy of the attention distribution may be measured in bits:
\[
H(\alpha_t) = -\sum_i \alpha_{t,i} \log_2 \alpha_{t,i}\ \mathrm{bits}.
\]
This entropy is not reported in the source; it is a formal uncertainty diagnostic derived from the provided softmax attention operator.

Action encoding is:
\[
e_t = \mathrm{MLP}_{act}\left(\mathrm{Emb}(a_t^{type}) \parallel a_t^{cont}\right),
\]
where \(a_t^{type}\) is the discrete action type and \(a_t^{cont}\) contains continuous action features [PAGE 27]. The graph frame embedding and action embedding are projected into Transformer input space:
\[
\tilde{e}_t = W_p \left[f_t \parallel e_t\right] + b_p,
\]
followed by positional encoding:
\[
z_t^{(0)} = \tilde{e}_t + p_t.
\]
Event gating is:
\[
g_t = \mathrm{Sigmoid}\left(\mathrm{MLP}_{gate}(e_t)\right),
\]
\[
\bar{z}_t^{(0)} = z_t^{(0)} + m_t\left(g_t \odot z_t^{(0)}\right),
\]
where \(m_t \in \{0,1\}\) marks event frames and \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication [PAGE 27]. Event-biased self-attention uses:
\[
b_t = m_t \cdot \mathrm{MLP}_{bias}(e_t),
\]
\[
s_{t,r} = \mathrm{Attn}\left(\bar{z}_t^{(0)}, \bar{z}_r^{(0)}\right) + b_r,
\]
\[
\alpha_{t,r} =
\frac{\exp(s_{t,r})}
{\sum_{r'=1}^{T} \exp(s_{t,r'})},
\]
where \(T\) is possession-sequence length [PAGE 27]. The final frame prediction is:
\[
y_t = \mathrm{MLP}_{cls}\left(z_t^{(l)}\right).
\]
For multi-class intention and invasion-phase tasks, softmax probabilities are used; for scoring phases, sigmoid outputs are used [PAGE 8]. If \(p_c\) is the predicted probability of class \(c\), then class uncertainty may be measured as:
\[
H(p) = -\sum_c p_c \log_2 p_c\ \mathrm{bits}.
\]

The sequence metric is temporal Intersection over Union:
\[
\mathrm{tIoU}(g,p) = \frac{|g \cap p|}{|g \cup p|},
\]
where \(g\) is a ground-truth segment and \(p\) is a predicted segment [PAGE 9]. This metric is dimensionless, bounded in \([0,1]\), and evaluated over overlapping segment pairs rather than only the best match [PAGE 9]. A breakdown regime occurs when \(|g \cup p| = 0\), making tIoU undefined; this can happen if both ground truth and prediction contain no valid temporal support.

The deterministic filter is a sequence-decoding operator: if a predicted segment is shorter than a minimum duration \(k\), and it is surrounded by a dominant neighboring phase within a \(2k\)-frame window, it is replaced by that dominant phase [PAGE 8]. The source states that “k is set to 10 frames for the finishing phase” and 50 frames for other phases [PAGE 8]. In seconds, \(k_{\mathrm{fin}} = 10 \times 0.04 = 0.40\ \mathrm{s}\), while \(k_{\mathrm{other}} = 50 \times 0.04 = 2.00\ \mathrm{s}\). This filter can improve temporal coherence but introduces a bias against genuinely short phases.

Training uses a “weighted Focal loss” to mitigate class imbalance [PAGE 10]. The source does not provide the focal-loss equation or the focusing parameter \(\gamma\); therefore no explicit parameterization is asserted here. The imbalance is evident in Table 2: Counter Attack has 14,624 frames and 67 sequences, whereas Build Up has 178,588 frames and 424 sequences [PAGE 9]. Finishing has only 2,558 frames and 95 sequences [PAGE 9], which explains its high sensitivity to boundary error.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of this paper runs from trajectory intention recognition into football-specific tactical segmentation. The introduction frames trajectory analysis as a general problem in which intention must be inferred from high-dimensional, continuous, temporally evolving movement data [PAGE 1]. Football is presented as a “reduced complexity experimental environment” because rules, roles, and tactical objectives constrain behavior sufficiently for analysis [PAGE 1]. This genealogy is important: the paper is not merely applying computer vision to sport; it is inheriting a trajectory-intention tradition and transplanting it into a domain with explicit tactical grammar.

The immediate football lineage proceeds through possession-sequence analysis. The authors define the unit of analysis as a stable possession episode, borrowing from Link and Lang [PAGE 4]. Earlier phase frameworks are described as too coarse because they rely on observable spatial or structural cues without explicitly modeling intention [PAGE 3]. Tenga et al. are cited for counterattack versus elaborate attack distinctions [PAGE 2]. Bauer et al. are cited as a key reference for frame-level phase recognition from tracking data, but the source notes that their categories were oriented more toward formation contextualization than intention-based in-possession segmentation [PAGE 3]. Biermann et al. are cited for hierarchical tactical periodization and graph-based phase annotation [PAGE 3-4].

The graph-learning lineage is also explicit. Raabe et al. are associated with tactical graph networks, Dick et al. with pass-availability graph models, Wang et al. with geometric graph neural networks for tactical prediction, and Lee et al. with graph-oriented match outcome prediction [PAGE 3-4]. The source then adds the Transformer lineage, citing attention-based sequence models for match outcome, event prediction, playing-pattern recognition, and player evaluation [PAGE 4]. The paper’s contribution is the synthesis: frame-level relational graphs plus Transformer temporal modeling plus deterministic tactical repair.

Multi-week traps in this genealogy are substantive. First, the trap of frame-level sufficiency: a model may achieve strong frame F1 while producing incoherent segments, because phase identity is temporally extended. The source explicitly demonstrates that “sequence-level evaluation captured fragmentation and boundary errors missed by frame-level metrics” [PAGE 1]. Second, the trap of annotation authority: the labels are produced by a single annotator [PAGE 8], so model validation is constrained by the internal consistency of one operational schema. Third, the trap of post-processing dependency: filtering and rule-based correction improve tIoU, but they can also conceal model weakness. The source’s Counter Attack improvement from 0.23 to 0.59 after filtering [PAGE 12] is evidence of genuine noise removal, but it also shows how sensitive the raw model is to short segment fluctuations.

A further historical trap is overgeneralization from rare phases. Counter Attack and Finishing are structurally important but underrepresented [PAGE 9]. In small samples, rare phases can dominate error statistics while contributing little to aggregate frame accuracy. The source acknowledges that rare borderline situations may be underrepresented in only seven matches [PAGE 14]. Therefore, the genealogical lesson is that tactical phase models must be judged not only by average accuracy but by the stability of rare, high-leverage phases and by the validity of deterministic constraints.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer must model at least four agents: the attacking team, the defending team, the annotator, and the machine learner. The attacking team’s observable behavior is assumed to reflect tactical intention, but intention is not directly observable. The defending team’s organization changes the phase: a team may retain possession because it chooses to, or because pressure forces it to. The source captures this by defining Maintenance as retaining possession “whether willingly or forced” [PAGE 5]. The annotator therefore infers intention from context, and the model then learns to mimic that inference from spatiotemporal operators.

Crisis habit regression appears when pressure or uncertainty pushes agents toward familiar patterns. In football, teams under high pressure may regress to Maintenance or hurried progression rather than structured Build Up. The practical example in the source shows Paderborn spending nearly half of possession time in Maintenance under Bayern pressure [PAGE 18]. This is a recursive crisis response: the team’s tactical habit becomes more defensive or retention-oriented when the opponent’s pressure raises the cost of exploration. The model may also exhibit a similar regression through its filter: when a rare phase is surrounded by a dominant neighbor, the deterministic decoder replaces it with the dominant label [PAGE 8]. This stabilizes output but can erase genuine crisis-like counterattacks or short finishing bursts.

The counter-trap is to recognize when regression is misleading. A short Counter Attack after possession regain is not necessarily noise; it may be a valid high-speed response to defensive disorganization. The source emphasizes that Counter Attacks occur when defensive organization is incomplete [PAGE 13], and that graph-based modeling helps capture transitional configurations. If the model’s temporal prior is too strong, it will misclassify these as Progression or Maintenance. The counter-trap is therefore to preserve event-anchored anomalies: possession-regain context, rapid forward ball movement, and defensive imbalance.

The clock is a formal recursive constraint. At 25 Hz, each frame is 0.04 seconds. The minimum episode is 3 seconds, or 75 frames [PAGE 4]. Filtering uses 10-frame and 50-frame windows [PAGE 8]. These temporal units define the scale at which tactical intention becomes inferable. If the model’s attention window is too short, it cannot distinguish a rapid Counter Attack from a momentary forward pass. If it is too long, it may smooth over legitimate phase changes. The Transformer’s event-biased attention is designed to amplify event frames, such as passes, carries, or shots [PAGE 8], but the source shows that Finishing still suffers because “small boundary shifts can strongly reduce tIoU” [PAGE 14].

The recursive adversarial loop is therefore: defenders compress space; attackers probe width or depth; the annotator labels the observed intention; the model learns the annotator’s priors; the filter imposes temporal plausibility; the analyst then uses the output to judge tactical behavior. The model’s attention analysis suggests it relies heavily on wingers, midfielders, and collective width rather than only central forwards [PAGE 15]. This is consistent with a theory-of-mind approximation: the model does not read minds, but it learns which positional configurations historically correlate with intention categories. The danger is circularity: if the annotator’s habits are narrow, the model will reproduce those habits, and the filter will make them look temporally stable.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto must be adjudicated separately for physical, biomechanical, and weather layers. The source supplies strong physical and kinematic features, but it does not supply weather features. The feature table includes player coordinates, velocity, acceleration, ball coordinates, ball velocity, ball acceleration, distances, angles, Voronoi area, action features, and possession-context variables [PAGE 24]. Therefore, any weather veto is not empirically derivable from the provided source unless external weather variables are introduced.

Let \(x_i(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be player position in meters, \(v_i(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be velocity in \(\mathrm{m/s}\), and \(a_i(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be acceleration in \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\) [PAGE 24]. Let the ball position be \(x_b(t)\), ball velocity \(v_b(t)\), and ball acceleration \(a_b(t)\). For a possession interval \([t_s,t_e]\), the duration is:
\[
L = (t_e - t_s + 1)\Delta t,
\]
with \(\Delta t = 0.04\ \mathrm{s}\). The source requires an episode to be at least \(3\ \mathrm{s}\), so:
\[
L \geq 3\ \mathrm{s}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
t_e - t_s + 1 \geq 75\ \text{frames}.
\]

For Counter Attack, the source defines the phase as an attack “with high speed immediately after regains possession” and usually when there is no organized defence [PAGE 5]. A formal physical veto can be constructed without inventing a single universal threshold by treating the threshold as a parameter \(\theta_{CA}\). Let \(\tau_{CA}\) be the evaluation window after possession regain. Define observed forward displacement of the ball as:
\[
D_b(\tau_{CA}) =
\left\|x_b(t_r + \tau_{CA}) - x_b(t_r)\right\|_2,
\]
where \(t_r\) is the regain time. Let \(v_{b,\max}\) be the maximum ball speed in the window and \(a_{b,\max}\) be the maximum feasible ball acceleration. Under a conservative kinematic bound, the maximum physically plausible displacement over \(\tau_{CA}\) is:
\[
D_{\max} =
v_{\max}\tau_{CA}
+
\frac{1}{2}a_{\max}\tau_{CA}^2.
\]
If a proposed Counter Attack label requires displacement greater than \(D_{\max}\), or if the observed displacement is below a calibrated minimum \(D_{\min}\) while the label asserts high-speed transition, the physical veto is triggered:
\[
V_{CA}^{phys} =
\mathbf{1}
\left[
D_b(\tau_{CA}) < D_{\min}
\ \text{or}\
D_{req} > D_{\max}
\right].
\]
The breakdown regime occurs when \(\tau_{CA} < \Delta t\), because then displacement is measured over less than one frame and becomes numerically unstable. It also breaks down when tracking data are missing, because the inequalities cannot be evaluated.

Biomechanical veto operates on player acceleration and velocity. If a label requires a player or team to achieve acceleration above a physiologically plausible bound \(a_{\mathrm{phys}}\), then:
\[
V^{bio} =
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\max_{i,t \in S} \|a_i(t)\|_2 > a_{\mathrm{phys}}
\right].
\]
This is not a toy equation; it is a formal constraint using SI acceleration units. The source provides acceleration as an input feature [PAGE 24], so the model can represent such states, but the source does not provide explicit physiological thresholds. The veto is therefore a formal adjudication rule requiring external calibration.

Weather veto is not supported by the source feature set. Let \(W\) be a weather vector containing variables such as precipitation, wind, temperature, or surface condition. The source’s model assumes, implicitly, that phase labels are conditionally independent of \(W\) given the provided tracking and event features:
\[
P(Y \mid X, W) = P(Y \mid X).
\]
Because \(W\) is absent from Table A1 [PAGE 24], the source cannot adjudicate a weather veto. If external weather data are added, a weather-based reliability penalty may be defined as:
\[
R(W) =
\begin{cases}
1, & W \in \mathcal{W}_{train},\\
\delta, & W \notin \mathcal{W}_{train},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(\delta \in [0,1]\) is a reliability discount. Without \(W\), no empirical weather veto can be proven from this corpus.

Thus, the cross-layer adjudication proof is: physical and biomechanical vetoes are formally expressible from the provided kinematic features, but require calibrated thresholds; weather veto is not identifiable from the source and must be treated as an external assumption. The strongest source-grounded veto is temporal: any predicted phase that violates the stable possession episode constraint or the deterministic filtering constraints should be rejected or repaired before downstream inference.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes are verifiable from the source because they rely on explicit predicates, event anchors, or deterministic rules described in the manuscript.

Veto 1: Possession-regain veto for Counter Attack.  
The source’s rule-based baseline identifies Counter Attack when the last possession belongs to the opponent and the possession gap is less than 25 frames [PAGE 25]. The feature table also includes “Last possession opponent” and “Possession gap” measured in frames [PAGE 24]. Define \(O(t_s)=1\) if the previous possession belonged to the opponent, and let \(G\) be the possession gap in frames. A verifiable veto is:
\[
V_{CA}^{regain} =
\mathbf{1}
\left[
O(t_s)=0
\ \text{or}\
G \geq 25
\right].
\]
If \(V_{CA}^{regain}=1\), the Counter Attack label should be rejected or reassigned unless stronger evidence overrides it. Counterfactual prediction: applying this veto should reduce Counter Attack / Progression inconsistency, which currently accounts for 3.48% of misalignments [PAGE 14], without collapsing the filtered Counter Attack tIoU of 0.59 [PAGE 12]. Falsification condition: if the veto reduces Counter Attack recall so severely that downstream phase distribution changes significantly after Holm correction, the veto is too strict.

Veto 2: Shot-anchored Finishing veto.  
The source states that if no Finishing label is detected within ten frames before a shot inside the penalty area, those ten frames are reassigned to Finishing [PAGE 15]. Let \(t_{shot}\) be the shot frame and define the pre-shot window:
\[
W_{fin} = [t_{shot}-10, t_{shot}].
\]
If no predicted Finishing frame exists in \(W_{fin}\), then:
\[
V_{fin}^{shot} =
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\nexists t \in W_{fin}: \hat{y}_t = \text{Finishing}
\right].
\]
When \(V_{fin}^{shot}=1\), the final segment should be repaired by assigning Finishing to the pre-shot window. Counterfactual prediction: missed Finishing before a shot, currently 1.81% of misalignments [PAGE 14], should decrease, while lenient pre-shot Finishing recovery should remain at least near the reported 79.80% recovery level [PAGE 14]. Falsification condition: if the repair creates Finishing labels without verifiable shot events or inside non-penalty-area contexts, the veto is invalid.

Veto 3: Transition-adjacency veto between Counter Attack and Progression.  
The source’s fixed-label rule states that “Progression and Counter Attack were not allowed to appear directly adjacent to each other” [PAGE 15]. Define a predicted sequence segment pair \((\text{CA}, \text{Prog})\) or \((\text{Prog}, \text{CA})\) as adjacent if their boundary frames differ by one frame and no intervening Build Up or Maintenance segment exists within the same possession. The veto is:
\[
V_{adj} =
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\text{CA} \rightarrow \text{Prog}
\ \text{or}\
\text{Prog} \rightarrow \text{CA}
\ \text{without intervening Build Up/Maintenance}
\right].
\]
When \(V_{adj}=1\), the prediction should be repaired by inserting a plausible transition, merging the shorter segment, or suppressing the less supported label. Counterfactual prediction: Counter Attack / Progression inconsistency should decrease, and aggregate phase duration, movement, and spatial indicators should remain statistically indistinguishable from manual labels after Holm correction, as reported for the fixed labels [PAGE 15]. Falsification condition: if the veto materially distorts Counter Attack duration, especially because identified labels already showed a visible decrease before correction [PAGE 15], the adjacency rule must be relaxed.

These vetoes are verifiable because they map directly to source predicates: possession-regain status, shot timing, penalty-area context, and phase adjacency. They are also counterfactual in the RLVR sense: a training or inference example receives a binary verify signal, and predictions violating the signal are penalized, filtered, or repaired. The main risk is over-vetoing rare but valid tactical transitions, especially when Counter Attack emerges from ambiguous regain conditions or when Finishing occurs without a clearly annotated shot due to event-label noise.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
