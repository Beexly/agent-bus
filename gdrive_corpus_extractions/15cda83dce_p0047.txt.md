<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The technical lineage in this patent traces from early 20th-century photoelectric plethysmography (Hertzman, 1930s) through pulse oximetry (Aoyagi, 1970s) and modern wrist-worn PPG arrays. US 9,743,848 B2 [PAGES 21–22] operationalizes a power-aware sensor fusion policy: optical emitters dominate during expected motion, while piezo and infrared sensors take over for low-motion sleep intervals [PAGE 22]. This echoes earlier actigraphy sleep-detection methods (Kripke et al., 1978) but adds autonomic features—skin temperature, galvanic skin response, and sub-resting heart rate—as classifier inputs [PAGE 22]. The multi-week traps are rooted in the non-stationarity of the biometric-sensor interface rather than in the radio link. First, strap tension and wrist geometry drift over days, altering contact pressure \(C\) and thereby the baseline perfusion term that the brightness controller assumes when selecting among 1–10 mA, 11–30 mA, and 80–120 mA drive levels [PAGE 21]. Second, cutaneous optical properties change with tanning, topical agents, or epidermal turnover; a user whose skin becomes more reflective will trigger the “first lower level” brightness rule, while less reflective skin triggers the “second higher level” [PAGE 21], both of which shift the effective \(\sigma_{\text{meas}}\) away from the factory-calibrated 2 BPM assumption. Third, the user-specific sleep classifier is trained on an initial baseline of movement, temperature, GSR, and heart rate [PAGE 22]; travel across time zones, illness, or altered sleep schedules shift those priors, causing the classifier to retroactively relabel historical quiet-wakefulness as sleep or vice versa. A particularly pernicious retrospective-causality trap occurs when the duty cycle downswitches during motionless wakefulness (e.g., reading in bed): the resulting sparse PPG samples depress computed HRV metrics, and an analyst may then attribute the low RMSSD to “poor sleep quality” when the true cause is the sensor power policy itself. Because the wireless transmission threshold \(\Theta_Q\) governs when data leave the device [PAGE 22], gaps in the uplink can be misread as missing nights rather than as duty-cycle-induced downsampling. Any longitudinal comparison of HRV or sleep staging across firmware updates is therefore invalid unless the duty-cycle, wavelength, and brightness policies are frozen or explicitly modeled as covariates.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes a user under autonomic stress who repeatedly interrogates the device’s HRV readout. Each voluntary wake check or wrist movement raises the motion feature \(M(t)\), potentially pushing the system across \(\Theta_M\) into the 10% duty-cycle walking profile [PAGE 21] and commanding higher emitter brightness [PAGE 21]. The user then observes a changed HRV number and interprets the device’s own power-policy response as a physiological signal, deepening the checking loop. The counter-trap operates at night: normal sleep includes “small bursts of movement (such as shifting in bed)” [PAGE 22]. If a burst crosses the motion threshold, the system may switch from piezo/infrared back to optical PPG and momentarily increase duty cycle; the user, observing a sensor-mode change or a data artifact, may infer an apnea event or cardiac disturbance that is actually a policy transition rather than a clinical marker. The clock layer operates on the battery depletion timeline. As the cell ages, the system has incentive to deepen duty-cycle reduction and wavelength downshifting (green→red→infrared) [PAGE 22] to preserve runtime, which silently inflates \(\sigma_{\text{tot}}\) toward or beyond the stated 5–6 BPM ceilings [PAGE 21]. Nocturnal charging creates uplink/data gaps; the sleep classifier must interpolate across them, and the interpolated sleep-onset latency feeds back into the next night’s baseline training data, producing a recursive drift in what the device defines as “typical resting heart rate when the user is awake” [PAGE 22]. In crisis states, this drift is amplified: elevated resting heart rate from anxiety may be misread by the classifier as a failure to meet the asleep criterion (“heart rate that is below the typical resting heart rate when the user is awake”) [PAGE 22], causing the system to remain in high-power optical mode through the night and accelerate battery drain, which then triggers more aggressive downsampling on subsequent nights—a positive feedback loop between user anxiety, sensor policy, and perceived device unreliability.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Define the veto predicate \(V(\cdot)\) as the disjunction of physical, biomechanical, and environmental failure modes that invalidate the optical HRV chain:
\[
V = V_{\text{contact}}\lor V_{\text{perfusion}}\lor V_{\text{saturation}}\lor V_{\text{classifier}}.
\]
\(V_{\text{contact}}\): contact quality \(C<C_{\min}\), e.g., a loose strap during walking or running. Even at the maximum 120 mA emitter current and 10% duty cycle [PAGE 21], poor optode-skin coupling reduces detected photon flux below the shot-noise floor, so the 2 BPM measurement-error assumption is invalid. \(V_{\text{perfusion}}\): cold-induced vasoconstriction or low cardiac output reduces arterial pulse amplitude \(A_{\text{PPG}}<A_{\min}\); the brightness controller may compensate by raising current [PAGE 21], but if \(\mu_a(\lambda,\text{skin})\) absorption is too high, no legal current level restores SNR. \(V_{\text{saturation}}\): high ambient illuminance \(E_{\text{amb}}\) drives the detector into saturation despite the “brightness set at a first higher level” rule for high ambient light [PAGE 21]; clipping destroys the pulsatile component and biases HRV downward. \(V_{\text{classifier}}\): the user-specific sleep model returns high entropy \(H(S|\mathbf{x})>H_{\max}\) when skin temperature and GSR are confounded by fever, alcohol, or circadian shift [PAGE 22].

Adjudication across layers: when \(V=1\), the optical layer must surrender control to the piezo/infrared fallback for low-motion periods [PAGE 22], or flag the segment as invalid rather than emit a heart-rate estimate that pretends to meet the 5 BPM / 6 BPM standard [PAGE 21]. The veto must propagate to the wireless layer: invalid segments should either be compressed into a minimal “data gap” flag or transmitted with an invalidity bit, because the threshold-based upload policy [PAGE 22] would otherwise batch corrupted PPG with valid samples and contaminate downstream HRV features. Weather vetoes are explicit: extreme cold (perfusion veto), bright sunlight (saturation veto), and high humidity (GSR baseline drift corrupting the sleep classifier) each independently satisfy \(V\). The singularity occurs when \(V_{\text{contact}}\) and \(M(t)>\Theta_M\) coincide during vigorous exercise: optical PPG is unreliable due to motion artifact and poor contact, while piezo/infrared sleep sensors are unsuited to high-motion cadence, yielding a total data gap. The system’s only correct action is to declare the epoch unusable rather than interpolate through it.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 (Ambient-light saturation during a daytime nap). A user falls asleep in a brightly lit room. Per the brightness rule, the system raises LED current because ambient light is at a “first higher level” [PAGE 21]. However, the detector still saturates because \(E_{\text{amb}}\) exceeds the front-end dynamic range. Veto: \(V_{\text{saturation}}=1\); mark the optical segment invalid. Prediction: compared to a simultaneous reference ECG and ambient-lux log, the computed nighttime RMSSD is biased downward by at least 30%, sleep-onset latency is overestimated because the HR-drop signature is clipped, and the device reports a “restless” night despite actigraphy confirming stillness. Verifiable by toggling a physical shade over the sensor and observing SNR recovery within one duty-cycle frame.

Scenario 2 (Rest-to-exercise transition latency). A user begins running; the accelerometer crosses \(\Theta_M\) but the system retains the 1% duty cycle and the longer “rest” wavelength for several seconds before up-switching [PAGE 21–22]. Veto: \(V_{\text{contact}}\lor V_{\text{classifier}}\) triggered by motion artifact; fallback to accelerometer cadence only. Prediction: heart-rate error exceeds the 6 BPM walking bound [PAGE 21] during the first 8–10 s, likely surpassing 10 BPM, and the inter-beat interval series shows >20% missing or ectopic beats because the 10 ms optical window cannot track the rising heart rate. Verifiable against a chest-strap reference with millisecond R-peak annotation.

Scenario 3 (Skin-reflectivity misclassification). The user has a fresh wrist tattoo or elevated melanin at the optode site. The classifier determines skin is “highly reflective” [PAGE 21] and sets brightness to a lower level, but actual absorption \(\mu_a(\lambda,\text{skin})\) is high, so photon flux is below detection threshold. Veto: \(V_{\text{perfusion}}=1\); optical path invalid despite duty cycle remaining at 1%. Prediction: effective \(\sigma_{\text{meas}}>2\) BPM, HF HRV power is underestimated, and the sleep classifier labels quiet wakefulness as sleep because the noisy or flat optical trace is interpreted as sub-resting heart rate [PAGE 22]. Verifiable by manually overriding brightness to the 80–120 mA band and observing restoration of the pulsatile signal and recovery of RMSSD to reference levels.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
