<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(i\) index a chat message. Let each binary indicator be \(I_{\cdot,i}\in\{0,1\}\). The paper’s manual schema assumes positive/negative exclusivity: “When the IsPositive variable is true the the IsNegative variable must be false and vice versa.” [PAGE 8]. Define \(P_i=I_{\mathrm{pos},i}\), \(N_i=I_{\mathrm{neg},i}\), \(S_i=I_{\mathrm{specific},i}\), \(Q_i=I_{\mathrm{noob},i}\), \(B_i=\max(I_{\mathrm{bad},i},I_{\mathrm{filt},i})\), and \(R_i=I_{\mathrm{rac},i}\). Let \(m_i\) be the 1-based count of negative messages by the same author within the same match, and let \(r_i=\max(0,m_i-1)\). The source’s cyberbullying score is piecewise:

\[
CS_i =
\begin{cases}
-1 - 3S_i, & P_i=1,\\
1 + Q_i + 2B_i + 2R_i + 3S_i + r_i, & N_i=1.
\end{cases}
\]

Units: points per message, ordinal and dimensionless in the paper’s scoring convention. Assumptions: \(P_i+N_i=1\); \(B_i\) is an OR-style union, not a sum; \(S_i\) changes sign across polarity; repetition is counted only for negative messages by the same author in the same match. The paper states “Every message will have as a score between -4 and +9 points.” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes: if \(P_i=N_i=1\), the piecewise definition is undefined; if \(B_i\) is implemented as \(I_{\mathrm{bad}}+I_{\mathrm{filt}}\), double counting breaks the +2 term; if \(m_i\ge 10\), the stated +9 upper bound fails because \(r_i\) is unbounded; if target specificity is uncertain, the sign flip produces discontinuous scores.

The proxy score is:

\[
PCS_i = N_i + Q_i + 2\max(I_{\mathrm{bad},i},I_{\mathrm{filt},i}) + 2R_i + \rho_i,
\]

where \(\rho_i\) is the SQL-derived repetition increment. The source writes: “PCS = (IsNegative * 1) + (NoobRelated * 1) + (HasBadLanguage|FilteredText * 2) + (IsRacist * 2) + RepetiveMessageScore” [PAGE 13]. Units: points per message. Assumptions: SQL lexical detection is a valid proxy for the manual indicators. Breakdown regimes: PCS omits the positive \(-1\) branch and omits SpecificTarget \(+3\); therefore PCS is not equivalent to CS and cannot identify targeted high-intensity abuse. If bad language occurs without negativity, PCS can still be positive, which departs from the CS polarity logic.

The classifier comparison uses the DOR statistic:

\[
DOR=\frac{N_{TP}N_{TN}}{N_{FP}N_{FN}},
\]

with counts of messages and dimensionless units. The paper says: “By collecting the classified results, including false positives and false negatives we could calculate the DOR.” [PAGE 12]. Reported values include IsRacist \(DOR=2{,}259\) [PAGE 12], HasBadLanguage \(DOR=946\) [PAGE 12], IsNegative \(DOR=1{,}627\) [PAGE 12], NoobRelated \(DOR=46{,}101\) [PAGE 13], CS/PCS \(DOR=284\) [PAGE 13], Twinword sentiment \(DOR=3\) [PAGE 13], and Microsoft sentiment \(DOR=6.6\) [PAGE 14]. Breakdown regimes: any zero cell makes DOR zero or infinite; rare false positives can inflate DOR without high recall; DOR is not a calibrated probability. The paper itself notes for racist detection that “SAC classification identified less than 45% of the manually identified cases.” [PAGE 12]. No information-theoretic bits or shannons are measured; sentiment vendor scores are vendor-specific dimensionless scales, not SI units.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogical line runs from survey-based cyberbullying research to lexical scoring, legal definitions, platform tribunals, and finally automated sentiment analysis. The paper inherits the broad behavioural definition: cyberbullying as “being cruel to others by engaging in socially aggressive behaviour using the Internet or other digital technologies” [PAGE 3]. It also acknowledges stricter definitions requiring “the repetition of the behavior over time, an imbalance of power between the perpetrator and the victim and finally the intention to do harm” [PAGE 3]. That lineage matters because the CS operator operationalizes repetition and target specificity, but does not measure power imbalance or intent. The legal genealogy is also explicit: UK law emphasizes repetition, and the paper notes Section 1 of the Protection from Harassment Act 1997 [PAGE 3]. The platform genealogy includes League of Legends’ “The Tribunal,” which gave researchers a large toxic-chat dataset but was later closed [PAGE 4]. The paper’s contribution is therefore retrocausal in a precise sense: it reads current platform chat through categories built by earlier legal, psychological, and platform-governance debates.

Multi-week traps are structural. First, the collection system is designed for long horizons: “the goal is to create a dataset with hundreds of millions of chat messages over many years” [PAGE 2]. But the reported run covers “approximately 15,000 replays” [PAGE 8]. A multi-week or multi-year claim cannot be stabilized from a few months of uploads. Second, player statistics are snapshots: “Since player statistics change continuously it is an important quality control to know what the time interval is between when the match was played and when the player’s statistics were fetched from the WOT API.” [PAGE 7]. Third, the learned-behaviour hypothesis requires longitudinal tracking: “Cyberbullying seems to turn up after the players have been playing for a while. This could be an indication that cyberbullying within World of Tanks is a learned behaviour from other more experienced players.” [PAGE 9]. The word “indication” is doing heavy epistemic work; cross-sectional XP bins cannot separate learning from selection, match exposure, or vehicle progression. Fourth, uploads to WotReplays are community-curated, not a random sample of all matches. Fifth, the paper excludes non-English servers [PAGE 7], so any cross-cultural genealogy is vetoed.

The policy genealogy is especially trap-prone. The post-death concentration is observed retrospectively, then converted into a prospective intervention: freeze chat “for a short period after the player is killed within a match” [PAGE 1]. If deployed, players can adapt: they may become toxic before death, use other channels, or change match behavior. A multi-week A/B test would be required to detect adaptation, displacement, and habituation. The paper’s own language is cautious: “it is quite feasible” [PAGE 14], not “proven.” The retrocausal trap is treating a timestamp correlation as an intervention effect.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer asks what the speaker believes about the target’s reaction, what the target infers, and what observers infer. In World of Tanks, chat is public to the team: “Private messages cannot be sent to an individual within a game so even messages sent to a particular player have to be broadcast to the whole team.” [PAGE 7]. Thus an insult is also a social performance. The CS schema tries to capture this with SpecificTarget: abuse aimed at “Tiger tank you suck” is scored higher than “this team is useless” [PAGE 8]. But the source cannot identify intent from text alone; it only flags lexical and targeting features. The paper’s own definitions include “intention to do harm” [PAGE 3], yet the classifier has no intent model.

Crisis habit regression is the paper’s implicit mechanism: death produces frustration, blame attribution, and a short outburst. The evidence is the clock result: “63% of all cyberbullying messages occurred after the player’s death in the game.” [PAGE 10]. The proposed mechanism is “At the point of death adrenaline and stress levels are probably higher, giving a higher probability of some sort of outburst.” [PAGE 10]. This is a regression-to-habit story: under sudden loss, the player reverts to toxic chat. But the source has no physiological measurement, no player self-report at the moment of death, and no exposure-normalized rate. It does not show whether dead players simply have more opportunity to chat, or whether post-death messages are more likely to be classified as abusive conditional on message volume.

The counter-trap is that muting dead players may suppress the visible symptom while leaving the living chat channel toxic. The paper notes that filtering alone is weak: “there is not much difference between the filtered phrase ‘Just die you useless piece of ****’ and the unfiltered version.” [PAGE 3]. A brief post-death mute could reduce the post-death spike, but it could also remove a cooling-off outlet, shift abuse earlier, or punish dead players who are coordinating or responding to harassment. The source does not test these counterfactuals. The clock layer is therefore double-edged: \(\Delta t=t_{\mathrm{message}}-t_{\mathrm{death}}\) in game seconds identifies the post-death window, but the source does not report \(N_{\mathrm{abuse,post}}/T_{\mathrm{dead}}\) versus \(N_{\mathrm{abuse,pre}}/T_{\mathrm{alive}}\). Without that exposure denominator, the 63% figure is a composition statement, not a rate statement. The recursive ToM conclusion is that the paper identifies a plausible crisis window, but not a validated counter-trap. The strongest defensible design is a short, randomized, within-match mute with checks for displacement and coordination loss.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer vetoes. The source is constrained by API access and server geography. It “processed just 1000 files on every data collection run” [PAGE 8], and it rejected non-European/North-American servers because the focus was English-language cyberbullying [PAGE 7]. Replay files are encrypted with Blowfish and decoded through third-party parsers [PAGE 6]. The collection counts themselves have an unresolved inconsistency between “approximately 44,000” snapshots in prose [PAGE 8] and “443150” in Table 3 [PAGE 8], which vetoes any precise reuse of that count without source verification.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The paper’s stress/adrenaline explanation is speculative. It says “At the point of death adrenaline and stress levels are probably higher” [PAGE 10], but no cortisol, heart-rate, reaction-time, or self-report measure is collected. There is no biomechanical instrument linking death events to typing behavior, message latency, or physiological arousal. Therefore the biomechanical causal claim is vetoed: the data support a temporal association between death and abusive messages, not a demonstrated adrenaline mechanism. The “63%” figure [PAGE 10] also lacks an exposure-normalized denominator, so it cannot adjudicate whether dead players are more abusive per unit time or simply more present in the chat channel.

Weather/environment-layer veto. The source contains no weather, climate, local-time, or environmental covariate. It logs American and European servers [PAGE 2], excludes Russian and Asian servers [PAGE 2], and does not translate non-English chat [PAGE 7]. Any claim about weather, seasonality, regional culture, or environmental stress is outside the measured layers and is vetoed. The only geographic conclusion permitted is the source’s own scope statement: the system “currently logs data from both the American and European servers” [PAGE 2].

Cross-layer adjudication. The valid inference is narrow: within uploaded English-language NA/EU World of Tanks replays, manually and SQL-labelled chat shows a post-death concentration of abusive messages and lexical baselines outperform off-the-shelf sentiment services. The invalid inferences are: universal cyberbullying prevalence, global platform toxicity, physiological causation, weather/environment effects, and proven policy effectiveness. Those require layers the source does not measure.
