<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage begins with Hume, whom James invokes explicitly on [PAGE 499]: since Hume, psychology has treated volition as known only through its “outward results,” not through any impression of hidden inner machinery. This is the classic Humean denial that we have an impression of necessary connection or of the hidden cause; applied to motor control, it denies an impression of innervation. James inherits this skepticism and sharpens it into a positive empirical demand: defenders of innervation feelings must produce direct, conclusive introspective evidence, which he says “does not exist.”

The next node is Wundt, whom James notes “makes such enormous use of these hypo-” [sic, hypotheses] on [PAGE 499], citing *Physiologische Psychologie*, 1st ed., S. 316. Wundt’s experimental psychology treated apperception and voluntary acts as analyzable into elementary feelings, implicitly requiring an introspectible motor component. James’s argument undercuts the Wundtean program by showing that the supposed elementary motor feeling decomposes into peripheral sensory images—“how the raised arm or the crooked finger feels.”

The historical trail also runs through Jaccoud’s 1864 clinical work on paraplegia and ataxia of movement (cited p. 591) and the Leidesdorf–Meynert psychiatric quarterly of 1867 (cited S. 8–7). These sources document patients whose outward movement is disrupted while intention remains, precisely the population in which one might expect a “feeling of innervation” to appear if it existed independently of afference. James’s argument implies that even in paraplegia and ataxia, the patient should not report a muscle-specific innervation feeling; rather, he reports the absence of the expected sensory consequence.

The lineage continues forward into the 20th century: Helmholtz’s unconscious inference, Sperry’s (1950) efference copy and von Holst’s reafference principle, and the modern forward-models literature (Wolpert, Miall, Kawato). Each accepts that the brain carries an internal copy of the motor command for control and stability, but none claims that this copy is a conscious sensation. The multi-week trap emerges in rehabilitation and motor learning: clinicians who assume patients can introspect their muscle-by-muscle innervation will design cueing strategies (“feel your vastus medialis fire”) that fail over weeks because the representational format of the command (vector-valued, distributed, unconscious) does not match the format of conscious imagery (holistic, delayed, sensory). Adaptation to prisms or force fields proceeds on a timescale of days to weeks via error-driven recalibration of \(\mathcal{F}\), not via immediate introspective access to \(\mathbf{e}(t)\). James’s page thus anticipates a chronic trap in applied motor psychology: mistaking the computational necessity of an efference copy for its phenomenal availability.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind here concerns what an agent believes about its own volitional states under pressure. James’s position generates a habit-regression structure: under normal conditions, the subject experiences volition only through its outward sensory results. Under crisis or high-stakes conditions, the agent regresses to habitual, feedback-dependent control loops because the conscious mind has no direct access to the fast, muscle-level motor command \(\mathbf{e}(t)\). The “crisis habit regression” is therefore a regression to peripheral afference and to the slow, image-based representation of the limb (“how the raised arm … feels”), not to a purported direct feeling of innervation.

The counter-trap is the opposite error: an athlete, musician, or patient under stress attempts to consciously reinstate control over individual muscles—“how many muscles he contracts, in what order of sequence, and in what degrees of intensity” [PAGE 499]. Because James demonstrates that no such introspectible feeling exists, the attempt produces conscious processing of automatic skills (reinvestment), disrupting the forward model \(\mathcal{F}\) and degrading performance. The subject is trying to introspect a null channel; the resulting effort is not motor control but meta-cognitive noise superimposed on an already unconscious motor discharge.

The clock variable is the temporal binding between intention and action. Haggard’s intentional-binding literature shows that voluntary actions and their sensory consequences are bound in subjective time, compressed toward each other. If a distinct “feeling of innervation” existed as James describes it, the binding would occur at motor-command onset, before movement. Instead, the bound episode is anchored to the sensory consequence (the outward result), consistent with James’s claim that we know volition through its results, not its hidden machinery. The recursion closes: the agent models its own will by observing the delayed sensory shadow of the act; under stress it regresses to that shadow; if it tries to penetrate the shadow to the muscle command, it triggers the counter-trap of conscious reinvestment; and the clock shows that the subjective “now” of willing is yoked to the afferent consequence, not to the efferent onset.

Biomechanical layer veto: Muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs, and spinal reflex loops operate on millisecond timescales far below introspective latency (introspective report typically >200–300 ms). Environmental / “weather” layer veto: Perturbations—unexpected loads, slippery surfaces, inertial changes—force the system to rely on feedback \(\mathbf{s}(t)\) and rapid recomputation of \(\mathcal{F}\), because the pre-computed efference copy no longer matches the sensory consequence. If the agent believed in a felt innervation channel, he would expect the feeling to update instantaneously with the perturbation; instead, he experiences a delay while the sensory consequence is re-affered and compared. The environment thus vetoes any model in which innervation is an immediate, self-intimating feeling independent of the outward result.

Adjudication: All three layers converge on James’s conclusion on [PAGE 499]: the “feeling of innervation” has no direct introspective existence. The physical layer shows command without feeling; the biomechanical layer shows distributed unconscious control; the environmental layer shows that felt agency is tethered to sensory consequence, not to motor onset. No single layer can rescue the innervation-feeling hypothesis without contradicting the others.
