The source is a systems/deployment paper, not a formal physical-model paper. It reports two tools, Watch-DMLT and ViSeDOPS, and a classroom deployment with 65 students and up to 16 Fitbit Sense 2 smartwatches [PAGE 1, PAGE 9]. The paper explicitly states that Watch-DMLT enables “real-time collection, synchronization, and storage of physiological and motion data across multiple devices” [PAGE 2], but the operational meaning of “real-time” is constrained by the reported one-minute transmission interval: intervals shorter than 30 seconds “risked overloading the communication queue,” while intervals longer than two minutes “led to memory overflow,” and a one-minute interval “was found to offer a reliable balance between performance and data integrity” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, “real-time” in this source is better interpreted as near-real-time periodic batch forwarding, not continuous raw streaming.

The first gray-zone audit concerns sampling. The paper says Watch-DMLT provides “high-frequency access to sensor data such as heart rate, acceleration or orientation” [PAGE 2], yet it does not report numerical sampling frequencies for heart rate, accelerometer, gyroscope, or orientation in the deployment. It does report that commercial Fitbit heart-rate sensing “often samples at low frequencies (e.g., one reading every five seconds) and may experience intermittent data loss” [PAGE 4]. That sentence describes a general commercial limitation, not necessarily the Watch-DMLT deployment rate, so any claim that the study achieved beat-to-beat heart-rate variability at 1 Hz would be unsupported. The CSV schema is explicit: “Each file contains the date, timestamp, and the corresponding sensor-specific measurement for each entry” [PAGE 5], with beats per minute for heart rate, angular velocity on three axes, linear acceleration on three axes, and quaternion components \(w,x,y,z\) for orientation [PAGE 5]. This proves timestamped raw-ish logging, not clock synchronization quality.

The second gray-zone audit concerns synchronization. The paper says successful transfer requires both devices “connected to a Wi-Fi network and synchronized before and after each monitoring session” [PAGE 6], and that failure to synchronize promptly can “result in transmission delays or data loss” [PAGE 6]. ViSeDOPS then allows “Selecting a timestamp in the heart rate graph automatically aligns and plays the corresponding video frame” [PAGE 8]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source contains no numbered equations. The following are audit formalizations derived from its stated constraints; they are not presented as original equations of Becerra et al.

**Sampling and storage operator.** For sensor stream \(s\in\{\mathrm{HR},\mathrm{ACC},\mathrm{GYRO},\mathrm{ORI}\}\), let the sample times be
\[
t_{s,k}=t_{s,0}+k T_s+\varepsilon_{s,k},\qquad T_s>0\ \mathrm{s},\qquad f_s=\frac{1}{T_s}\ \mathrm{Hz},
\]
where \(\varepsilon_{s,k}\) is timing jitter in seconds. If \(b_s\) is bytes per sample, the stream rate is
\[
R_s(t)=\frac{b_s}{T_s}\ \mathrm{B\,s^{-1}},\qquad
R_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=\sum_s \frac{b_s}{T_s}\ \mathrm{B\,s^{-1}}.
\]
Buffered storage is
\[
S_s(t)=S_s(0)+\int_0^t R_s(u)\,du\ \mathrm{B}.
\]
The device constraint is \(S_s(t)<M_s\), with \(M_s\) the available storage in bytes. Breakdown occurs at \(S_s(t)\ge M_s\); the paper reports that intervals “longer than two minutes led to memory overflow” [PAGE 6]. The exact \(M_s\), \(b_s\), and \(T_s\) are not reported.

**Queue operator.** In file units or bytes, let \(Q_n\) be queue occupancy at transmission epoch \(n\), \(A_n\) the arrivals during the interval, and \(C_n\) the successfully forwarded amount. The Lindley recursion is
\[
Q_{n+1}=\max(0,Q_n+A_n-C_n),
\]
with queue-overflow veto when \(Q_n>Q_{\max}\). The utilization condition is
\[
\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[A_n]}{\mathbb{E}[C_n]}.
\]
If \(\rho\ge 1\), the queue is unstable in expectation; if \(\rho\to 1^{-}\), queue length diverges slowly and latency grows. The source gives a practical interval constraint: intervals “shorter than 30 seconds risked overloading the communication queue,” while intervals “longer than two minutes led to memory overflow,” and 60 s was selected [PAGE 6]. Thus the empirically supported operating set is approximately
\[
\Gamma_{\mathrm{ok}}\subseteq\{\tau:30\ \mathrm{s}<\tau<120\ \mathrm{s}\},\qquad \tau^\ast=60\ \mathrm{s},
\]
with the caveat that the paper uses “risked” and “led,” not exact threshold equations.

**Synchronization and clock operator.** Let \(t_i(e)\) be the timestamp assigned by device \(i\) to event \(e\), and \(t_{\mathrm{ref}}(e)\) the reference time. The offset and drift are
\[
\delta_i(t)=t_i(t)-t_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)\ \mathrm{s},\qquad
\dot{\delta}_i(t)=\frac{d\delta_i}{dt}\ \mathrm{s\,s^{-1}},
\]
so
\[
|\delta_i(t)|\le |\delta_i(0)|+|\dot{\delta}_i|\,t.
\]
If video frame period is \(T_v=1/f_v\) seconds and annotation resolution is \(T_a\) seconds, semantic alignment is valid only when
\[
|\delta_i(t)|<\frac{1}{2}\min(T_v,T_a).
\]
Breakdown occurs when \(|\delta_i(t)|\ge \frac{1}{2}\min(T_v,T_a)\): the dashboard can display a heart-rate timestamp beside the wrong video frame even though both carry timestamps. The source supports timestamped files [PAGE 5], pre/post synchronization [PAGE 6], and timestamp-to-video alignment [PAGE 8], but it does not report \(\delta_i\), \(\dot{\delta}_i\), \(T_v\), or \(T_a\).

**Latency and RF operator.** Total delivery latency is
\[
L_{\mathrm{tot}}=L_{\mathrm{sense}}+L_{\mathrm{buffer}}+L_{\mathrm{queue}}+L_{\mathrm{BT}}+L_{\mathrm{HTTPS}}+L_{\mathrm{server}}\ \mathrm{s}.
\]
For Bluetooth path loss,
\[
L_p(d)=L_0+10n\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{d_0}\right)+X_\sigma\ \mathrm{dB},
\]
with \(d\) in metres and \(X_\sigma\) a shadowing term in dB. The link is vetoed when
\[
P_{\mathrm{rx}}=P_{\mathrm{tx}}+G_{\mathrm{tx}}+G_{\mathrm{rx}}-L_p(d)<P_{\mathrm{sens}}.
\]
The paper directly supports this veto class: phone-to-watch distance increased “risk of data loss and message queue overflow” [PAGE 7], and Wi-Fi/HTTPS synchronization was required before and after sessions [PAGE 6]. It does not report \(d\), \(P_{\mathrm{rx}}\), or packet-loss rates.

**Heart-rate and motion operator.** Instantaneous heart rate from inter-beat interval \(\mathrm{IBI}\) is
\[
\mathrm{HR}(t)=\frac{60}{\mathrm{IBI}(t)}\ \mathrm{bpm},\qquad \mathrm{IBI}\ \mathrm{in\ s}.
\]
An observational model is
\[
\mathrm{HR}_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)=\mathrm{HR}_{\mathrm{true}}(t)+\eta_{\mathrm{PPG}}+\eta_{\mathrm{motion}}+\eta_{\mathrm{sampling}},
\]
where each \(\eta\) is in bpm. If \(\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{\mathrm{motion}})\ge \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{HR}_{\mathrm{true}})\), inference from heart rate to stress/engagement is vetoed. The source records heart rate and motion [PAGE 7] and calls heart rate a stress/attention proxy [PAGE 3], but it does not report motion-artifact rejection or signal-to-noise ratios. For a sampling period \(T_s\), the Nyquist frequency is \(f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}=1/(2T_s)\). If commercial heart rate is sampled every 5 s, then \(f_{\mathrm{Nyq}}=0.1\ \mathrm{Hz}\), which cannot resolve beat-to-beat variability; this is a conditional limitation, not a proven property of the deployment [PAGE 4].

**Information-theoretic audit.** If heart rate is used as a predictor of an engagement label \(Y\), the usable information is
\[
I(\mathrm{HR};Y)=\sum_{h,y}p(h,y)\log_2\frac{p(h,y)}{p(h)p(y)}\ \mathrm{bits}.
\]
The source reports no such value; it reports only feasibility and visualization. Any claim of predictive validity from this deployment therefore requires external validation.

\operatorname{Var}(\eta_{\mathrm{motion}})\ge \operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{HR}_{\mathrm{true}})

\text{HR-to-stress/engagement inference vetoed}.

The source reports no motion-artifact correction, no accelerometer saturation check, no quaternion normalization quality metric, and no criterion validation against stress labels. Therefore a weather veto is non-identifiable: there is no measured weather exposure and no reported weather-related failure mode.
