The source document describes a full visual analytics stack for team affiliation in hockey video. The reasoning chain must therefore be reconstructed as a pipeline with dependent stages, not as an isolated classifier. The first hop is detection and segmentation: Mask R-CNN detects people and applies masks; only masked player images are passed downstream [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. The second hop is referee rejection: a supervised referee/non-referee CNN removes referees because referee uniforms are stable across games [PAGE 2][PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. The third hop is representation learning: an embedding CNN is trained with triplet loss so that player images from the same team become closer and images from opposing teams become farther apart [PAGE 4]. The fourth hop is unsupervised label generation: soft k-means confidence scores from an initial colour-histogram representation select high-confidence pseudo-labels; triplets are sampled from those pseudo-labels; the embedding network is then iteratively retrained while pseudo-labels are regenerated [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. The fifth hop is novel-game adaptation: for a new game, the first \(n_{\text{burn}}\) frames are used to estimate two cluster centres in the learned embedding space, and subsequent players are assigned to the nearest centre [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. The sixth hop is geometric rectification and heatmap generation: detected player footpoints are mapped through a learned homography to rink coordinates, and Gaussian kernel density estimation produces team-conditioned position maps [PAGE 5][PAGE 7][PAGE 8].

The central multi-hop derivation is that accuracy is not determined by the classifier alone but by the interaction of four quantities: (i) representation discriminability, (ii) pseudo-label precision, (iii) burn-in duration \(n_{\text{burn}}\), and (iv) geometric validity. The source explicitly shows this interaction in Table 1: when \(n_{\text{burn}} = 1\), the embedding CNN obtains \(0.94 \pm 0.009\) accuracy, while colour histogram obtains \(0.87 \pm 0.031\), bag-of-colours obtains \(0.76 \pm 0.032\), pretrained bag-of-colours obtains \(0.86 \pm 0.099\), and autoencoder obtains \(0.70 \pm 0.076\) [PAGE 7]. When \(n_{\text{burn}} = 512\), the colour-based methods rise to near parity, with colour histogram and bag-of-colours both reaching \(0.97\), while the embedding CNN remains at \(0.97 \pm 0.011\) [PAGE 7]. This is not merely a reporting difference; it reveals a formal bias-variance structure. Colour histograms have low representation variance but high sensitivity to the colour statistics of the specific novel game. The embedding CNN has higher pretraining cost but lower effective variance with respect to \(n_{\text{burn}}\), because the representation was learned across many training games and therefore transfers more stable team-discriminative structure [PAGE 7].

The gray-zone audit concerns where the method can silently fail. First, pseudo-label generation is recursive: initial k-means on RGB histograms supplies labels for triplet training, and those labels are later replaced by labels from the evolving embedding [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. If the initial pseudo-label noise is high, triplet training can reinforce incorrect team geometry. The source quantifies this with a threshold study: \(p_{ij} > 0.5\) yields error \(0.134\) with \(77\%\) of training samples; \(p_{ij} > 0.7\) yields error \(0.037\) with \(72\%\); and \(p_{ij} > 0.9\) yields error \(0.031\) with \(60\%\) [PAGE 10]. Thus the high-confidence regime reduces noise but also reduces sample count, creating a precision-versus-coverage tradeoff. Second, the method relies on “both colour and spatial features” [PAGE 4]. If two teams wear visually similar uniforms and also occupy similar pose configurations, the embedding may lack sufficient discriminative directions. The source notes that colour-only approaches fail “when the two teams are wearing similar colours” [PAGE 1], but the embedding is not immune; it merely reduces the probability of failure by adding spatial/configural information. Third, the heatmap layer has its own singularity: the homography assumes a stationary camera and a valid rink model. If camera parameters change or the homography is ill-conditioned, team affiliation may be correct while spatial aggregation becomes invalid. Fourth, the evaluation clock matters. Accuracy is evaluated over “the 30 annotated frames immediately following the burn-in interval,” which corresponds to roughly 10 seconds because every tenth frame is annotated [PAGE 7]. This means that the practical value of \(n_{\text{burn}}\) is tied to a short post-burn-in evaluation window, not to indefinite adaptation. Finally, runtime constraints matter: the embedding method learns team appearances from a single frame in “21 miliseconds” and performs inference in “11 miliseconds” per subsequent frame [PAGE 8], but Mask R-CNN detection runs at roughly 5 fps [PAGE 8]. Therefore the system is not fully real-time unless detection is replaced. The reasoning trace therefore concludes that the paper’s strongest claim is not simply “94% accuracy,” but that a pretrained contrastive representation collapses the burn-in clock and stabilizes team clustering under short adaptation horizons.

Let \(x_i\) denote a feature vector for player image \(i\). In the initial colour-histogram seeding stage, \(x_i\) is a normalized RGB histogram with 8 bins per channel [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. In the learned embedding stage, \(x_i = f_\theta(I_i)\), where \(f_\theta\) is the embedding CNN output and the final feature vector has length \(1024\) [PAGE 4][PAGE 7]. The source uses soft k-means confidence scores to rank pseudo-label reliability:

\[
p_{i1} = \frac{\lVert x_i - c_2 \rVert}{\lVert x_i - c_1 \rVert + \lVert x_i - c_2 \rVert}
\tag{1}
\]

\[
p_{i2} = \frac{\lVert x_i - c_1 \rVert}{\lVert x_i - c_1 \rVert + \lVert x_i - c_2 \rVert}
\tag{2}
\]

where \(c_j\) is the centre of cluster \(j\) [PAGE 4]. The quantities \(p_{ij}\) are dimensionless confidence scores in \([0,1]\). The Euclidean distance \(\lVert x_i - c_j \rVert\) has units of feature-space distance; when \(x_i\) is a histogram, the units are normalized histogram-distance units, and when \(x_i\) is an embedding, the units are learned embedding units. A singularity occurs if \(c_1 = c_2 = x_i\), because the denominator becomes zero. A degeneracy also occurs if \(c_1 = c_2\), because then \(p_{i1} = p_{i2} = 0.5\) whenever the denominator is nonzero, making team assignment maximally ambiguous.

The training objective is a triplet loss over anchor \(a\), positive \(p\), and negative \(n\). A formal form is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{triplet}}(a,p,n) =
\max\left(0,
\lVert f_\theta(a) - f_\theta(p) \rVert_2^2
-
\lVert f_\theta(a) - f_\theta(n) \rVert_2^2
+ \alpha
\right)
\tag{3}
\]

where \(\alpha \ge 0\) is a separation parameter in squared embedding-distance units. The source states that the triplet loss “drive[s] the network to decrease the distance in the embedding space between the anchor and positive images, while increasing the distance between the anchor and negative images” [PAGE 4]. The source also states that “we randomly convert 50% of training triplets to grayscale” to avoid exclusive reliance on colour [PAGE 4]. The assumption is that positives and negatives are generated from pseudo-labels; if those pseudo-labels are wrong, the gradient sign can be wrong.

The unsupervised pseudo-label iteration can be written as a recursive mapping:

\[
\mathcal{P}^{(t+1)} = \mathcal{K}\left(f_{\theta^{(t)}}(I), c^{(t)}\right),
\qquad
\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{triplet}}\left(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{P}^{(t+1)})\right)
\tag{4}
\]

where \(\mathcal{K}\) is k-means clustering, \(c^{(t)}\) are cluster centres, \(\mathcal{T}\) is triplet sampling, and \(\mathcal{P}^{(t)}\) is the pseudo-label set. The source describes this process: “we regenerate these pseudo-labels and training triplets, but replacing the histogram representation with the evolving embedded representation” [PAGE 5]. Training continues “until convergence” or a maximum of 30 epochs on initial histogram pseudo-labels, followed by new pseudo-label generation every 10 epochs [PAGE 5]. The breakdown regime is self-reinforcing collapse: if early pseudo-labels are biased, the network may learn a representation that makes the bias more stable.

For inference on a novel game, the first \(n_{\text{burn}}\) frames estimate two centres \(c_A, c_B\) in the learned embedding space [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. A player image \(i\) in a later frame is assigned by:

\[
\hat{y}_i = \arg\min_{j \in \{A,B\}} \lVert f_\theta(I_i) - c_j \rVert_2
\tag{5}
\]

This operator is valid only if the novel-game embeddings occupy a region where the two team clusters are separable. The practical measure of separability is the confidence score \(p_{ij}\) from equations (1)–(2). If most samples have \(p_{ij} \approx 0.5\), cluster assignment is unstable.

The referee classifier uses binary cross-entropy:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ref}} =
-\left[y \log q + (1-y)\log(1-q)\right]
\tag{6}
\]

where \(y \in \{0,1\}\) and \(q\) is the predicted referee probability [PAGE 4]. Units are nats per example if natural logarithms are used. The source reports mean referee accuracy of \(98\%\) with \(93\%\) precision and \(96\%\) recall [PAGE 7]. This layer matters because referee leakage into player clustering adds noise; the source explicitly notes that “some referees will be incorrect classified as players and will add noise to the contrastive learning process” [PAGE 7].

For heatmaps, player image coordinates are mapped to rink coordinates by a homography \(H\):

\[
\tilde{x}_{\text{rink}} \sim H \tilde{x}_{\text{image}}
\tag{7}
\]

where \(\tilde{x}\) denotes homogeneous coordinates. The source says the homography was computed from 19 corresponding point pairs and estimated by least-squares reprojection [PAGE 5][PAGE 10]. The input is the midpoint of the lower bounding-box boundary [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. The output density is estimated by Gaussian kernel density estimation:

\[
\hat{\rho}(x) =
\frac{1}{N h^2}
\sum_{j=1}^{N}
K\left(\frac{\lVert x - X_j \rVert}{h}\right)
\tag{8}
\]

where \(X_j\) are back-projected player positions, \(h\) is the bandwidth, and \(\hat{\rho}(x)\) has units of players per square metre per frame when the coordinate system is metric [PAGE 5][PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. The source uses Silverman’s rule and reports a bandwidth “roughly 30 pixels” on a template rink image of size \(496 \times 240\) pixels [PAGE 8]. Heatmap quality is measured by KL divergence:

\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) =
\sum_x P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
\tag{9}
\]

with units of nats per heatmap cell when natural logarithms are used. The source reports mean KL divergence values of \(0.072\) for colour histogram, \(0.069\) for bag-of-colours, and \(0.047\) for the embedding CNN [PAGE 8].

Illumination normalization is formal affine per-channel intensity mapping:

\[
I'_i(x,y) = a I_i(x,y) + b,
\qquad
\min_{x,y,i} I'_i = 0,
\quad
\max_{x,y,i} I'_i = 255
\tag{10}
\]

for \(i \in \{R,G,B\}\) [PAGE 5]. Units are 8-bit intensity levels. The assumption is that illumination changes are approximately affine across channels. If illumination is non-affine, shadows, specular glare, or colour casts violate the model. The source also resizes player images to \(62 \times 128\) pixels [PAGE 5], which imposes a fixed input resolution; severe occlusion or unusual pose may reduce the usable silhouette. The breakdown regime for runtime is also explicit: “21 miliseconds” to learn team appearances from a single frame and “11 miliseconds” per inference frame, while Mask R-CNN runs at roughly \(5\) fps [PAGE 8]. Thus the learned team-assignment operators are lightweight, but the detection operator is the bottleneck.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the source paper is a convergence of four older research streams: colour-based sports player labelling, supervised CNN classification, contrastive metric learning, and deep clustering. The paper explicitly situates itself against prior unsupervised colour methods and supervised CNN methods. It states that earlier unsupervised approaches “rely solely on colour-based features such as colour histograms” [PAGE 1] and that supervised CNN approaches require labelled data or fine-tuning [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. The genealogical move is therefore retro-causal: the authors do not merely add a new classifier; they reinterpret the task as representation learning before clustering. The causal ancestor is the triplet loss and contrastive embedding tradition, cited through Hadsell, Chopra, and LeCun [PAGE 9], and the implementation uses triplet loss from Hoffer and Ailon [PAGE 9]. The clustering ancestor is deep clustering, where networks learn features and cluster assignments jointly [PAGE 2]. The sports-vision ancestor is Mask R-CNN detection and segmentation [PAGE 2][PAGE 9], followed by referee classification and player clustering.

A multi-hop genealogy of failure modes appears over repeated game-week adaptation. In week one, a colour histogram may work because team uniforms are distinct and illumination is stable. In week two, if lighting shifts, histogram distances become unreliable. In week three, if teams wear similar colours, histogram clusters collapse. In week four, if the camera changes, homography-based heatmaps become invalid even if team labels remain correct. The source anticipates these traps by stating that colour-only methods are sensitive to illumination and similar colours [PAGE 1][PAGE 2], and by arguing that a learned representation “may include both colour and confgural information” can improve generalization to “novel teams, jerseys, lighting and camera parameters” [PAGE 1]. The contrastive embedding is thus a countermeasure to a recurring weekly trap: the system must adapt to new visual statistics without labelled data.

Another multi-week trap is pseudo-label drift. The source trains initially on colour-histogram pseudo-labels, then regenerates pseudo-labels from the evolving embedding [PAGE 5]. If this regeneration were delayed or applied too aggressively, the system could either remain colour-bound or chase noisy embeddings. The source’s schedule—maximum 30 epochs on initial histogram pseudo-labels, then new pseudo-labels every 10 epochs until convergence—is a temporal safeguard [PAGE 5]. It reflects an implicit genealogy of self-training: early reliable labels bootstrap later labels, but only if confidence filtering prevents noise amplification. The confidence threshold table is the quantitative memory of this trap: lower threshold \(p_{ij} > 0.5\) yields higher error \(0.134\), while higher threshold \(p_{ij} > 0.9\) yields lower error \(0.031\) but fewer samples [PAGE 10]. This is a lineage of label-noise control, inherited from semi-supervised learning, adapted to sports video.

The heatmap lineage adds another causal layer. Even if team classification is perfect, coaching value depends on geometric correctness. The source computes heatmaps by homography and KDE [PAGE 5][PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. The genealogical trap is that team classification and spatial rectification can fail independently. A camera calibration error can make correct team labels produce wrong spatial densities. The KL-divergence evaluation is the paper’s way of auditing this trap: the embedding CNN reduces divergence from ground truth to \(0.047\), compared with \(0.072\) for colour histogram and \(0.069\) for bag-of-colours [PAGE 8]. Thus the retro-causal genealogy is not merely “better features”; it is a layered defense against recurring weekly failures: illumination drift, uniform collision, camera change, pseudo-label noise, and geometric projection error.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer asks how the system models the mental state of the game environment under crisis. The “crisis” is not a single event but a compound condition: few frames available, imperfect masks, referee contamination, variable pose, motion blur, and possibly similar uniforms. The naive habit regression would be to fall back on colour histograms because they are immediate, lightweight, and require no deep representation [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. However, the source demonstrates that this habit is brittle when the clock is short. With \(n_{\text{burn}} = 1\), the colour histogram obtains \(0.87 \pm 0.031\) accuracy, bag-of-colours obtains \(0.76 \pm 0.032\), while the embedding CNN obtains \(0.94 \pm 0.009\) [PAGE 7]. The habit regression is therefore penalized by the short-clock environment. The system must “know” that immediate colour evidence is unreliable and must instead rely on a pretrained representation that has already learned team-discriminative structure across many games.

The counter-trap is the contrastive embedding. It does not merely classify players; it constructs a geometry in which same-team images are close and different-team images are far [PAGE 4]. This geometry acts as a model of team identity prior to seeing the novel game. The counter-trap becomes recursive because the system uses its own current predictions to generate training pairs for the next version of itself. The source states that pseudo-labels and triplets are regenerated as training proceeds, replacing histogram representation with the evolving embedded representation [PAGE 5]. This is a self-modeling loop: the network predicts cluster confidences, selects high-confidence examples, trains on them, and then re-estimates cluster centres. The recursive mind is stable only if the confidence filter suppresses self-confirmation. The threshold study formalizes this: \(p_{ij} > 0.5\) includes \(77\%\) of samples but produces error \(0.134\); \(p_{ij} > 0.9\) includes \(60\%\) but produces error \(0.031\) [PAGE 10]. The system’s theory of its own reliability is therefore explicitly probabilistic: it trusts only examples whose soft assignment confidence is high.

The clock is the third recursive operator. The source defines burn-in as the number of initial frames \(n_{\text{burn}}\) used to learn cluster centres before inference [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. Evaluation is performed over 30 annotated frames following burn-in, corresponding to roughly 10 seconds at 30 fps [PAGE 7]. This clock changes the expected behavior of each method. Colour methods improve continuously with more burn-in frames; the embedding CNN performs well even with one frame and improves only modestly thereafter [PAGE 7]. In theory-of-mind terms, the embedding CNN anticipates the future game state better because it has already learned a representation that generalizes across games. It does not need many frames to infer the local team split; it only needs enough evidence to place the two team modes in an existing representation space. The source says the advantage comes from learning “an embedding space that is more effective for discriminating teams than colour histograms,” allowing “well-separated cluster centres to be learned very quickly from the novel game” [PAGE 7].

The recursive model also includes the opponent environment’s hidden variables: jersey design, lighting, camera pose, referee presence, and occlusions. The system does not label these variables explicitly, but it must implicitly separate them. Grayscale augmentation is a counter-trap against colour overfitting: converting 50% of triplets to grayscale forces the network to use shape and spatial configuration, not only hue [PAGE 4]. Affine illumination normalization is another counter-trap against lighting shifts [PAGE 5]. Referee classification removes a hidden confounder before clustering [PAGE 2][PAGE 4]. The resulting recursive mind is therefore not a single classifier but a layered belief-updating loop: detect, filter referees, embed, cluster, filter by confidence, retrain, re-cluster, assign, rectify, and aggregate. The crisis habit regression would collapse this loop into colour matching; the counter-trap preserves the loop through representation learning; the clock determines how much evidence the loop is allowed to consume before decisions must be emitted.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a downstream decision must be blocked because an upstream physical, biomechanical, geometric, or environmental assumption fails. The first veto layer is detection. The pipeline uses Mask R-CNN with a confidence threshold of 0.6 [PAGE 5]. Let \(D = \{d_i\}\) be detections with confidence \(s_i\). If \(s_i < 0.6\), the detection is rejected. If no detection survives for a player, no embedding can be computed. This is a hard veto: team classification cannot occur without a masked image. The source states that Mask R-CNN is used to “detect and segment all people on the playing surface” [PAGE 2], and that referee and player labelling begins from those detections [PAGE 3].

The second veto layer is segmentation quality. The network receives player images with segmentation masks applied [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. If the mask area is zero, or if the visible silhouette is too small or occluded, the resulting feature vector is not reliable. The source resizes inputs to \(62 \times 128\) pixels [PAGE 5], which means that severe truncation can destroy discriminative information. A formal veto condition is:

\[
A_i = \sum_{(x,y)} M_i(x,y) = 0
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
\text{veto}(i)
\]

where \(M_i\) is the binary mask for detection \(i\). If \(A_i\) is nonzero but below a task-specific minimum, the confidence in \(f_\theta(I_i)\) should be reduced. The source does not specify a minimum area threshold, but the dependence on segmentation masks makes this a necessary audit condition.

The third veto layer is biomechanical visibility. The player image location is defined as the midpoint of the lower boundary of the bounding box [PAGE 4]. For hockey, this approximates the skates or contact points with the ice. If the lower boundary is occluded by other players, boards, or camera crop, the spatial point is invalid. The source excludes coaches and bench players by a heuristic based on detections close to the bottom of the frame with bounding-box height less than twice the width [PAGE 3]. This is a biomechanical veto: if the detected person is not plausibly standing on the playing surface in the expected pose geometry, the location used for heatmaps is suspect.

The fourth veto layer is geometric homography. The heatmap assumes a mapping from image coordinates to a model rink surface [PAGE 5][PAGE 10]. Let \(H\) be the homography estimated from 19 point pairs [PAGE 5][PAGE 10]. If the reprojection residual exceeds a tolerable geometric error, the mapping is invalid. Formally, for corresponding image points \(u_k\) and rink points \(v_k\):

\[
\epsilon_H =
\frac{1}{K}
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\left\|
v_k - \pi(H \tilde{u}_k)
\right\|_2^2
\]

where \(\pi\) dehomogenizes the projective output. If \(\epsilon_H\) is large, team labels may be correct but the heatmap is spatially false. The source uses a stationary 4K camera or two-camera virtual blend [PAGE 3]; if the camera moves, the homography becomes invalid. This is a hard physical veto for heatmap generation, though not necessarily for team classification.

The fifth veto layer is environmental illumination. The source applies affine per-channel normalization to reduce illumination variation [PAGE 5]. The assumption is:

\[
I'_c(x,y) = a_c I_c(x,y) + b_c,
\quad c \in \{R,G,B\}
\]

with per-channel affine parameters chosen to map intensities to \([0,255]\) [PAGE 5]. If illumination variation is non-affine—for example, strong shadows, specular ice glare, or localized colour casts—the normalization cannot fully remove it. The veto condition is not explicitly quantified in the source, but the paper’s own motivation acknowledges that colour features are sensitive to illumination [PAGE 2][PAGE 4]. Therefore, under extreme illumination, the initial colour-histogram pseudo-labels may be unreliable, and the system should rely more heavily on the pretrained embedding and high-confidence filtering.

The sixth veto layer is cluster separability. For novel-game inference, the system estimates two cluster centres from \(n_{\text{burn}}\) frames [PAGE 5]. If the centres are too close or the confidence scores are near 0.5, team assignment is not reliable. A formal veto condition is:

\[
\min_{i \in A, j \in B}
\lVert f_\theta(I_i) - f_\theta(I_j) \rVert_2
\le
\max_{i,j \in A}
\lVert f_\theta(I_i) - f_\theta(I_j) \rVert_2
\]

or symmetrically for team \(B\). This states that if the nearest different-team distance is not larger than the farthest same-team distance, the embedding geometry does not support a clean split. In such a regime, the system should either collect more burn-in frames, lower confidence requirements cautiously, or abstain.

A final veto layer is computational timing. The embedding assignment is fast: “21 miliseconds” to learn team appearances from a single frame and “11 miliseconds” per inference frame [PAGE 8]. However, Mask R-CNN detection runs at roughly 5 fps [PAGE 8]. If the application requires real-time operation, the detection layer vetoes full-system real-time performance unless replaced by a faster segmentation network. The source explicitly says this replacement is left for future work [PAGE 8]. Thus the cross-layer adjudication is hierarchical: detection and segmentation veto all downstream inference; homography and player geometry veto heatmaps; illumination and pseudo-label confidence veto clustering stability; runtime vetoes real-time deployment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes are verifiable against the source’s reported tables and described mechanisms. They are not forecasts of unknown experiments; they are audit predictions that would be confirmed or falsified by rerunning the source’s evaluation protocol.

1. Low-burn-in veto for colour-only methods.
Counterfactual scenario: a novel game provides only one frame for cluster-centre learning, \(n_{\text{burn}} = 1\). In this regime, the colour-only methods lack enough samples to estimate stable team colour distributions. The source reports that when \(n_{\text{burn}} = 1\), the embedding CNN achieves \(0.94 \pm 0.009\), while colour histogram achieves \(0.87 \pm 0.031\) and bag-of-colours achieves \(0.76 \pm 0.032\) [PAGE 7]. The veto is therefore against colour-only pipelines in the ultra-short burn-in regime. Prediction: if the evaluation is repeated over the same four test games with \(n_{\text{burn}} = 1\), the embedding CNN should retain approximately 0.94 accuracy, while colour-only methods should remain materially lower. The verifiable reward is team-classification accuracy over annotated frames immediately following burn-in [PAGE 7]. The source also states that the contrastive method “achieves 94% accuracy after unsupervised training on only a single frame” [PAGE 1]. This veto is robust because it is directly supported by Table 1 [PAGE 7].

2. Pseudo-label threshold veto for noisy bootstrapping.
Counterfactual scenario: the initial pseudo-label confidence threshold is lowered from \(p_{ij} > 0.9\) to \(p_{ij} > 0.5\). More samples enter training, but label noise increases. The source’s supplementary table reports error \(0.134\) at \(p_{ij} > 0.5\) with \(77\%\) of training samples, error \(0.037\) at \(p_{ij} > 0.7\) with \(72\%\), and error \(0.031\) at \(p_{ij} > 0.9\) with \(60\%\) [PAGE 10]. The veto is against low-threshold pseudo-label selection when the goal is stable clustering. Prediction: if the threshold is reduced, the initial training set grows but the resulting embedding error increases, because triplets are more likely to contain false positives and false negatives. The source’s mechanism is explicit: “We consider only high-conﬁdence samples” for training “to limit the label noise” [PAGE 4]. The verifiable reward is error rate and training-set size as a function of threshold [PAGE 10]. This veto is also supported by the recursive nature of pseudo-label regeneration: noisy early labels can be amplified during self-training [PAGE 5].

3. Colour-collision veto for histogram and bag-of-colours features.
Counterfactual scenario: two teams wear similar jersey colours, so colour-only representations have low inter-team separation. The source states that colour-only unsupervised methods “fail when the two teams are wearing similar colours” [PAGE 1], and that performance may suffer “when teams are wearing jerseys with similar colour profiles” [PAGE 4]. The embedding CNN is motivated by learning “both colour and spatial features” [PAGE 4], which should preserve some discriminative information even when colour is weak. Prediction: in a controlled test where uniform colours are made similar across teams, colour histogram and bag-of-colours accuracy should decline relative to the embedding CNN, while the embedding CNN’s degradation should be smaller because spatial/configural cues remain available. The verifiable reward is the accuracy gap between colour-only methods and the embedding CNN under the same \(n_{\text{burn}}\) and annotation protocol [PAGE 7]. This veto is not a claim that the embedding CNN is immune to colour collision; it is a claim that the colour-only layer is vetoed earlier and more severely when hue evidence is insufficient.

4. Autoencoder feature veto under long burn-in.
Counterfactual scenario: a convolutional autoencoder is trained for reconstruction and then used as the feature extractor for clustering. The source reports that with \(n_{\text{burn}} = 512\), the autoencoder obtains \(0.92 \pm 0.099\), while the embedding CNN obtains \(0.97 \pm 0.011\) [PAGE 7]. The veto is against reconstruction-only features when the task is team discrimination. Prediction: even with many burn-in frames, autoencoder features should lag the triplet-trained embedding because reconstruction preserves general appearance but does not explicitly optimize inter-team separation. The source states that the embedding CNN’s advantage derives from learning a more discriminative space for teams [PAGE 7]. The verifiable reward is accuracy at large \(n_{\text{burn}}\), where colour-only methods catch up but the autoencoder still lags [PAGE 7].

5. Heatmap geometric veto under invalid homography.
Counterfactual scenario: the camera is not stationary, or the rink keypoints are incorrectly matched. The source computes heatmaps using a homography from image coordinates to a rink model [PAGE 5][PAGE 10]. If the homography is invalid, team affiliation may still be correct but the spatial density map will be wrong. Prediction: KL divergence between estimated and ground-truth heatmaps will increase even if team-classification accuracy remains unchanged. The source reports mean KL divergence values for valid homography conditions: \(0.047\) for the embedding CNN, \(0.072\) for colour histogram, and \(0.069\) for bag-of-colours [PAGE 8]. The verifiable reward is KL divergence after intentionally perturbing the homography or using a moving-camera clip. This veto separates classification success from geometric success and prevents overclaiming coaching utility when spatial calibration fails.

These five counterfactual vetoes are consistent with the source’s own operators and reported measurements. They also define the safe operating envelope: the embedding CNN is strongest under short burn-in, high-confidence pseudo-labeling, and valid geometry; it is weakest when colour evidence collapses, pseudo-labels are noisy, or spatial rectification is invalid.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
