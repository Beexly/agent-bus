<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper’s controller does not implement an explicit recursive theory of mind, but its behavior depends on an implicit model of other agents. The tracker forecasts an obstacle with a constant-velocity holonomic model [PAGE 8], and the controller steers toward the obstacle’s current position when that position is predicted to be vacated: “the controller selects a velocity that avoids collision by steering toward the current position of the other vehicle since that position will be unoccupied in the future” [PAGE 14]. That is a first-order prediction: ego models obstacle future position, but not obstacle’s model of ego. In a recursive multi-agent setting, the other agent may slow, turn, or react to ego, so the predicted vacancy can fail. The human stop-start real-world test is the clearest counterexample: the human occasionally stops and starts again [PAGE 16], which breaks constant-velocity prediction.

Crisis habit regression appears when uncertainty rises. If association fails or the EnKF velocity estimate is noisy, the obstacle cost can jump toward the hard margin, and Algorithm 6 returns infinity when \(mindist\le config.obstacle\_margin\) [PAGE 10]. The robot’s habit then regresses to reactive DWA behavior: stop, reverse, or steer away. The paper even tests a reverse-direction penalty and shows that a small penalty leads to reverse motion, while a large penalty leads to lateral divergence [PAGE 15]. That is a controller-habit regression under perceived crisis, not a principled recursive negotiation.

The counter-trap is to avoid overcommitting to predicted vacancy. A safer counter-trap is uncertainty inflation: when the obstacle’s velocity variance is high, or when the obstacle is a human with stop-start behavior, the controller should increase effective clearance and reduce speed rather than aim at the projected empty point. The paper’s own limitation section supports this direction by calling for dynamic motion models beyond only holonomic ones [PAGE 17]. The clock trap is latency. The controller reports less than 10 ms [PAGE 15], but the LiDAR response is at least 95 ms [PAGE 12], and shared hardware increases controller delay [PAGE 16]. If prediction is computed on stale state, the recursive model is temporally invalid: ego predicts a future that has already changed. The sliding window \(W\) adds further lag [PAGE 8 and PAGE 9]. Therefore the recursive-theory-of-mind audit should require a deadline-aware fallback: if tracker covariance, association ambiguity, or control-loop latency exceeds threshold, the system must downgrade from predictive steering to conservative yield/stop.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The safety invariant for the cross-layer audit is
\[
d(t)= \|p_{\rm ego}(t)-p_{\rm obs}(t)\|-R_r-R_o \ge margin
\]
for all \(t\) inside the prediction horizon \(T\), with distances in meters and \(margin\) in meters [PAGE 9 and PAGE 10]. A layer veto is any verified condition that makes this invariant unprovable under the paper’s assumptions. 

Physical-layer veto. The tricycle model uses steering angle, traction-wheel linear velocity, wheel radius, and orientation [PAGE 4], and DWA samples only admissible velocities within the dynamic window [PAGE 3 and PAGE 9]. If the required stopping distance \(d_{\rm stop}=v^2/(2a_{\max})+v\tau_{\rm react}\) exceeds the available clearance \(d(t)-margin\), no feasible velocity command can satisfy the invariant. This is a physical veto, not a tuning issue. Sensor physics also vetoes: the LiDAR has 275-degree scanning angle and 0.39-degree angular resolution [PAGE 12], so objects outside the field of view or hidden behind occlusion are not observed. Reflective surfaces and low visibility are explicit limitations [PAGE 1]. If detection probability \(P_D\) falls below the level needed to maintain the track, the predictive claim is vetoed because the state estimate is no longer grounded in measurement.

Biomechanical-layer veto. A human is not a holonomic constant-velocity particle. The paper’s real-world test has a human moving in the same direction and “occasionally stopping and starting again” [PAGE 16]. Under acceleration \(a_h\), the position deviation from constant velocity over horizon \(\Delta t\) is approximately \(\frac{1}{2}a_h\Delta t^2\), in meters. If that deviation exceeds the obstacle margin, the projected obstacle position used in Algorithm 6 is invalid [PAGE 10]. Human limb motion and partial occlusion can also change the cluster extreme points and the radius \(R=\sqrt{W^2+H^2}/2\) [PAGE 6], which changes the ensemble spread and therefore the tracker uncertainty. A biomechanical veto occurs when human stop-start or occlusion-induced radius instability is large enough to make the constant-velocity projection unsafe.

Weather-layer veto. Rain, fog, dust, and reflective surfaces reduce signal-to-noise and can create false or missing points. The paper explicitly warns of “low-visibility situations or when faced with highly reflective surfaces” [PAGE 1]. Formally, if the measured detection probability or cluster stability is below the threshold required to keep tracker error within the obstacle margin, the system must veto predictive collision avoidance for that region. In information terms, if the conditional entropy \(H(X_t\mid Z_{1:t})\) of the obstacle state given measurements exceeds the entropy implied by the safety margin, the estimate is too uncertain for the claimed guarantee. The correct adjudication is hierarchical: any physical, biomechanical, or weather veto overrides the nominal DWA score and forces a conservative fallback, such as speed reduction, increased clearance, or stop. The paper’s own local-planner limitation and future-work calls for global planning, MPC, and additional sensors [PAGE 16 and PAGE 17] are consistent with this adjudication.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

1. Reflective-surface and low-visibility veto. Verifiable setup: replay or inject LiDAR scans from a reflective or low-visibility region and compare the EnKF position estimate against ground truth or a surveyed landmark. Veto condition: the actual obstacle displacement over the prediction horizon deviates from the linear projection \(o_x+o_{\dot x}\Delta t\cdot ii\), \(o_y+o_{\dot y}\Delta t\cdot ii\) [PAGE 10] by more than the obstacle margin. Prediction: the ego robot steers toward the obstacle’s current position because the model predicts that position will be unoccupied [PAGE 14], but the obstacle stops there, converting the predicted-vacancy path into a collision path. The human stop-start real-world test [PAGE 16] is a direct stress case. This veto is verifiable by comparing projected and actual trajectories over the same \(T\).

The paper reports “the controller response time was consistently found to be less than 10 ms” [PAGE 15], but also that with shared hardware “we see an increased delay in the controllers” [PAGE 16].
