The provided source is a computer-vision and sports-analytics paper, not a wagering document. Its pipeline is a multi-hop transformation from broadcast video to a scalar defensive-success estimate. The first hop is detection and tracking: “150 clips, each lasting 10 seconds, featuring one-on-one encounters between players” are processed with “our custom-trained YOLOv10 object detection model” [PAGE 3]. The second hop is geometric normalization: “The bounding boxes were used to determine player footprints, defined as the midpoint of the lower bounding box edge,” and “Homography allowed for the transformation of video frames to account for perspective distortions” [PAGE 3]. The third hop is feature construction: the paper defines “Distance between the forward and the net, denoted as d1,” “Distance between the forward and the defenseman, denoted as d2,” “Distance between the defenseman and the shooting lane, denoted as d3,” and “Angle of the forward with respect to the center line of the net, denoted as θ” [PAGE 3]. The fourth hop is supervised regression: expert reviews assigned an ISM score, described as “a quantifiable measure of the likelihood the forward were to score if they were to shoot the puck at that instant” [PAGE 4], and an XGBoost model maps \((d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta)\) to that score [PAGE 4]. The fifth hop is deployment: the paper proposes “line-matching strategies” based on “analyzing the peak ISM allowed by each defenseman against various forwards” [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit begins with the label. The ISM is an expert-derived latent score, not a directly observed goal probability. The source says “Each one-on-one encounter was evaluated using expert reviews, which assigned a score known as the ISM” [PAGE 4]. That means the model is trained to imitate expert judgment under broadcast-video constraints, not necessarily to estimate a physical scoring probability. The source also uses inconsistent acronym expansions: “Instantaneous Success Metric (ISM)” on page 4 and “Using the ISM (Individual Scoring Metric)” on page 5. This is a terminology red flag: instantaneous frame-level danger and individual player quality are different estimands.

The second gray zone is sample size and domain. There are 150 ten-second clips [PAGE 3], only one-on-one situations, and a private NHL dataset. The model therefore has limited support for team contexts, odd-man rushes, screens, rebounds, goalie positioning, fatigue, handedness, stick reach, and defensive partner coverage. The paper itself notes the broader limitation: broadcast footage has a “limited field of view” and makes it “difficult to consistently track all players on the ice simultaneously” [PAGE 1]. A low ISM may indicate good defense, a bad forward decision, a camera-angle artifact, or a tracking failure.

The third gray zone is model behavior. The paper reports that “training RMSE steadily decreases” while “the validation RMSE plateaus after an initial decline” [PAGE 5]. That is the classic overfitting/early-stopping warning: additional boosting may memorize clip-specific noise. More importantly, “Feature d1 has the highest importance, with a significantly higher F-score than other features” [PAGE 5]. Because \(d_1\) is forward-to-net distance, the model may largely learn a distance-to-goal prior rather than a defenseman-specific skill. If \(d_2\) and \(d_3\) have weak incremental value, the ISM should not be marketed as a pure 1v1 defense metric without ablation and calibration.

The fourth gray zone is temporal. The paper says “Currently, we must manually input the frame number when the forward shoots the puck” [PAGE 5]. Manual shot-frame entry can bias peak-ISM extraction, highlight selection, and clip-level aggregation. A model can be per-frame accurate yet still produce unstable defensive rankings if the peak frame is chosen inconsistently. The source also has figure-reference inconsistencies on page 5: the RMSE discussion and feature-importance discussion are cross-labeled relative to the figure captions. These are not fatal, but they reduce reproducibility.

Multi-hop formalization: detection gives \(\mathbf p_F(t),\mathbf p_D(t)\); homography maps them into rink coordinates; Euclidean and projection formulas produce \(d_1,d_2,d_3\); arctangent produces \(\theta\); XGBoost produces \(\hat y(t)\); aggregation produces peak or mean ISM. Each hop can invalidate the next. A homography error changes all distances; a track ID switch swaps forward and defenseman; a footprint midpoint error shifts \(d_3\); a manual shot frame changes which \(\hat y(t)\) is treated as the encounter outcome. Therefore the correct audit target is not just RMSE but invariance, calibration, ablation, and uncertainty. The source reports low RMSE despite a small sample, but it does not report confidence intervals, calibration curves, temporal cross-validation, or player-level held-out splits. Those omissions are the central evidentiary gaps.

Let the homography-corrected rink coordinates at frame \(t\) be
\[
\mathbf p_F(t)=(x_F(t),y_F(t)),\quad
\mathbf p_D(t)=(x_D(t),y_D(t)),\quad
\mathbf p_N=(x_N,y_N),
\]
where \(F\) is the puck-carrying forward, \(D\) the defenseman, and \(N\) the net center. The source states that homography is used “to account for perspective distortions” [PAGE 3]. Units are rink coordinate units; if the homography is calibrated to metres, \(d_1,d_2,d_3\) are in metres. The source does not state an explicit metric calibration scale [PAGE 3], so any claim of physical metres requires external calibration.

The four source features are:
\[
d_1(t)=\|\mathbf p_F(t)-\mathbf p_N\|_2,
\]
\[
d_2(t)=\|\mathbf p_F(t)-\mathbf p_D(t)\|_2,
\]
\[
\mathbf u(t)=\frac{\mathbf p_N-\mathbf p_F(t)}{\|\mathbf p_N-\mathbf p_F(t)\|_2},
\]
\[
s(t)=\operatorname{clip}\!\left((\mathbf p_D(t)-\mathbf p_F(t))\cdot \mathbf u(t),\,0,\,\|\mathbf p_N-\mathbf p_F(t)\|_2\right),
\]
\[
\mathbf q(t)=\mathbf p_F(t)+s(t)\mathbf u(t),
\]
\[
d_3(t)=\|\mathbf p_D(t)-\mathbf q(t)\|_2,
\]
\[
\theta(t)=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(y_N-y_F(t),\,x_N-x_F(t)\right).
\]
This matches the source’s statement: “To calculate d3, the perpendicular distance from the defenseman to the shooting lane between the forward and the net, we used vector projections to locate the nearest point on this line segment” [PAGE 4]. The angle is measured relative to the net center line, with \(\theta\) in radians if standard programming-library conventions are used; the source does not specify degrees versus radians [PAGE 3].

Let the feature vector be
\[
\mathbf z(t)=\big(d_1(t),d_2(t),d_3(t),\theta(t)\big).
\]
The XGBoost prediction is an additive ensemble:
\[
\hat y(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(\mathbf z(t)),
\]
where \(\hat y(t)\) is the predicted ISM. The source gives the objective
\[
L(\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n} l(y_i,\hat y_i)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Omega(f_k),
\]
with squared loss
\[
l(y_i,\hat y_i)=(y_i-\hat y_i)^2
\]
and regularization
\[
\Omega(f_k)=\gamma T+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{T}w_j^2.
\]
The source states that \(T\) is “the number of leaves in the tree,” \(w_j\) is “the weight of leaf \(j\),” \(\gamma\) penalizes leaf count, and \(\lambda\) penalizes “the L2 norm of the leaf weights” [PAGE 4]. The fitted configuration is \(\eta=0.1\), maximum tree depth \(6\), subsampling rate \(0.8\), and “100 boosting rounds” [PAGE 4].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes:
1. \(d_1>0\). If \(d_1\to 0\), then \(\mathbf u(t)\) is undefined, the shooting lane collapses, and \(\theta(t)\) is numerically unstable. Predictions in this regime are extrapolation, not measurement.
2. \(d_2>0\). If \(d_2\to 0\), forward and defenseman footprints overlap; detection and identity assignment become ambiguous, and the model may confuse contact with separation.
3. The shooting-lane projection assumes the relevant lane is the segment from forward to net center. If the forward is behind the goal line or outside the rink-coordinate valid region, the segment and angle branch cut can misrepresent the actual shooting geometry.
4. \(\theta\) has a \(2\pi\) branch cut. Angular wrapping must be normalized; otherwise the same physical direction can appear as \(+\pi\) and \(-\pi\).
5. XGBoost is a tree ensemble and does not extrapolate smoothly outside the training support. If a test clip has \((d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta)\) outside the convex-hull-like training region, the prediction is a piecewise-constant extension and should be vetoed or flagged.
6. The source’s ISM scale is not formally calibrated as a probability. It calls ISM “a quantifiable measure of the likelihood” [PAGE 4], but it does not report a calibration map. Therefore \(\hat y\) should be treated as an ordinal/latent danger score unless a calibration study proves \(\hat y\in[0,1]\) and \(\mathbb E[y\mid \mathbf z]=\hat y\).

A first-order uncertainty propagation, assuming a locally differentiable surrogate \(f\) for the ensemble, is
\[
\delta \hat y
\approx
\frac{\partial f}{\partial d_1}\delta d_1+
\frac{\partial f}{\partial d_2}\delta d_2+
\frac{\partial f}{\partial d_3}\delta d_3+
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta}\delta\theta.
\]
If \(f\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz on the valid feature domain, then
\[
|\delta \hat y|
\le L\big(|\delta d_1|+|\delta d_2|+|\delta d_3|+|\delta\theta|\big).
\]
A defensible veto rule is: if the right-hand side exceeds a pre-registered tolerance \(\tau\), the ISM value is not admissible for scouting or line-matching. The source does not report such uncertainty bounds, which is a major gap.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from sports performance indicators to computer-vision tracking to tree-based regression. The paper frames hockey analytics as historically offensive-biased: “Traditional metrics predominantly focus on goals, offensive plays, and game wins” while “defensive metrics have received less attention” [PAGE 1]. It also cites scouting-bias problems: “bias can significantly influence scouting and player selection” [PAGE 2]. The ISM is therefore a corrective attempt: it tries to quantify defensive play in one-on-one encounters rather than reduce defense to goals against or subjective eye tests.

The computer-vision lineage is explicit. The pipeline depends on multi-object tracking, hockey-specific MOT datasets, homography, YOLOv10 detection, and XGBoost [PAGE 3; PAGE 4]. The paper’s references include prior work on player tracking, jersey-number recognition, puck localization, event detection, and homographic projections [PAGE 7–8]. The methodological genealogy is: broadcast video → detection → tracklets → homography → geometric features → expert labels → XGBoost → ISM → line matching. Each stage inherits assumptions from the previous one. Homography assumes a planar rink and stable camera calibration. Tracking assumes identity persistence. Expert labels assume that a human can reliably assign instantaneous danger from limited broadcast views. XGBoost assumes that the training distribution covers deployment.

Retrocausally, the intended final use shapes the earlier data design. If the metric is used for “line-matching strategies” and “analyzing the peak ISM allowed by each defenseman against various forwards” [PAGE 5], then the model should be optimized and validated for ranking defensemen and for peak-event detection, not merely for average frame-level RMSE. A model with low average RMSE can still rank players poorly if it smooths away peak danger. Conversely, a model tuned for peak ISM can be noisy if the shot frame is manually entered, as the source admits: “Currently, we must manually input the frame number when the forward shoots the puck” [PAGE 5]. The final deployment therefore retroactively determines which labels, frames, and validation splits matter.

Multi-week traps are predictable. Week 1 trap: label leakage through clip-level expert review; if the same expert sees the model output or the clip outcome, the label is contaminated. Week 2 trap: random frame-level train/test splits; adjacent frames from the same clip leak near-duplicate information, inflating apparent generalization. Week 3 trap: continuing boosting until training RMSE is low while validation RMSE has already plateaued; the source itself reports this pattern [PAGE 5]. Week 4 trap: deploying a \(d_1\)-dominated model as a defenseman-skill metric; “Feature d1 has the highest importance” [PAGE 5] means the model may mostly measure how close the forward is to the net. Week 5 trap: treating ISM as a probability without calibration; the source calls it a “likelihood” [PAGE 4] but reports no calibration curve. Week 6 trap: rink-side and camera-angle drift; a left-side net and right-side net may not be normalized identically, and broadcast camera angles vary. Week 7 trap: concept drift across teams, seasons, and officiating; the private NHL dataset is not a stable population. These are not hypothetical; they are standard failure modes of video-derived sports metrics.

The genealogical lesson is that the ISM is not a raw physical quantity like velocity or distance. It is a composite of measurement, geometry, expert judgment, and model regularization. Its historical value is that it moves defensive evaluation away from purely offensive box-score thinking. Its historical risk is that it replaces one subjective scouting bias with a new algorithmic bias: camera-visible, geometrically simple, \(d_1\)-dominant danger.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer involves three agents: the forward, the defenseman, and the analyst/coach who consumes the ISM. The forward chooses actions to increase scoring probability; the defenseman chooses actions to decrease it; the analyst chooses interpretations and line matchups based on the model. The model is trained on expert labels, so it contains a fourth agent: the expert labeler’s implicit theory of danger. The recursive loop is: expert labels danger → model learns danger → coach uses model to rank defenders → defenders and forwards adapt → future labels and future video distribution change.

Crisis habit regression: under time pressure, coaches and scouts regress to familiar habits. The paper’s background notes the historical emphasis on “goals, offensive plays, and game wins” [PAGE 1] and the persistence of subjective scouting biases [PAGE 2]. In a crisis, an analyst may abandon the ISM and revert to points, goals, plus-minus, or eye-test reputation. That regression is understandable but dangerous: it swaps a noisy geometric metric for an unmeasured offensive proxy. A second habit regression is “peak hunting”: manually selecting the most dramatic frame and treating it as the player’s true skill. The source’s manual shot-frame input makes this easy: “Currently, we must manually input the frame number when the forward shoots the puck” [PAGE 5]. A third habit regression is overfitting to the visualization: “after the forward shoots the puck, the ISM display changes from white to red font” [PAGE 5]. The color change is a display convention, not a validated causal event marker.

Counter-trap: the counter-trap is overtrusting the automated ISM as an objective authority. If the model says a defenseman “allowed” a low ISM, the analyst may infer defensive skill, but the model has not controlled for forward quality, zone entry, goalie position, backchecking pressure, or score effects. The counter-trap is especially sharp because XGBoost can fit nonlinear interactions and produce confident-looking numbers even when feature support is thin. A second counter-trap is Goodhart’s law: once defensemen are ranked by peak ISM, players and coaches may optimize the proxy rather than the underlying objective. A third counter-trap is label circularity: if the expert labeler already believes a certain defensive style is correct, the model learns that style and then appears to confirm the expert.

Clock: the clock layer is both literal and procedural. Literally, each clip is “10 seconds” [PAGE 3], and the relevant instant is the shot frame. The paper’s ISM is “instantaneous” [PAGE 4], so the metric is a frame-level danger score, not a full-sequence defensive outcome. Procedurally, the clock is the scouting calendar: a coach needs a lineup decision before enough clips, labels, and calibration data exist. The source’s small sample—150 clips [PAGE 3]—means the clock outruns the evidence. Under that mismatch, the analyst must choose between an uncalibrated automated score and an uncalibrated subjective judgment. The correct recursive stance is to treat ISM as one weak signal, preserve uncertainty, and require player-level held-out validation before using it for “line matching” [PAGE 5].

A further recursive trap is model-mediated self-fulfilling evaluation. If coaches use ISM to select defensemen, then only players who fit the model’s geometric profile receive high-pressure repetitions. The next dataset is then generated by players selected under the model, creating a selection bias that makes the model look more predictive than it is. This is a classic feedback loop in algorithmic scouting. The countermeasure is to keep an exploration set of clips selected independently of ISM and to evaluate on players and game states not used for model selection.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto must distinguish measurement validity, biomechanical validity, and environmental validity. The source’s feature layer is purely geometric: \(d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta\) [PAGE 3]. It does not include skating acceleration, edge control, stick length, reach, handedness, fatigue, ice friction, puck roll, or goalie positioning. Therefore any claim that ISM measures all of defensive skill is a non-identifiability claim. The paper is explicit that broadcast footage has “limited field of view” and causes tracking difficulty [PAGE 1]. It also states that “Homography allowed for the transformation of video frames to account for perspective distortions” [PAGE 3], but homography does not recover occluded limbs, stick position, or contact forces.

Physical-measurement veto: Let the true rink coordinates be \(\mathbf p^\star\) and the estimated coordinates be \(\hat{\mathbf p}=\mathbf p^\star+\boldsymbol\epsilon\). Then
\[
|\hat d_1-d_1^\star|\le \|\boldsymbol\epsilon_F\|+\|\boldsymbol\epsilon_N\|,
\]
\[
|\hat d_2-d_2^\star|\le \|\boldsymbol\epsilon_F\|+\|\boldsymbol\epsilon_D\|.
\]
For \(d_3\), because it is a projection distance, the error is bounded by the same coordinate errors plus a projection-angle error. If the propagated feature error exceeds the model’s local tolerance, the ISM is vetoed. The source does not report tracking error distributions, homography residuals, or identity-switch rates, so the default adjudication should be “unproven” rather than “validated.”

Biomechanical veto: A defenseman’s effective control volume is not a point footprint. It includes stick reach, body angle, skating direction, and acceleration. Two players with identical \(d_2\) can have very different defensive efficacy. If the model assigns a low danger score while the defenseman is outside stick reach but the forward is off-balance, the model is missing a biomechanical variable. A formal veto condition is: if the residual variation in ISM after conditioning on \(d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta\) is explained by an omitted biomechanical variable \(B\), and \(B\) varies materially within the deployment population, then the model’s conditional prediction is biased:
\[
y=f(d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta)+g(B)+\varepsilon,\qquad
\mathbb E[y\mid d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta]\neq f(d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta)
\]
when \(\operatorname{Cov}(B,\mathbf z)\neq 0\). The source provides no \(B\), so the model cannot represent it.

Weather/environmental veto: Ice hockey is indoor, but arena temperature, humidity, ice quality, and puck friction can affect skating and puck control. The source contains no weather or arena-environment variables. A strict veto proof is therefore a proof of non-identifiability: because the model’s input vector excludes environmental state \(W\), it cannot distinguish a change in ISM caused by \(W\) from a change caused by geometry. If a deployment claims weather-invariance or arena-invariance, that claim is unsupported. The correct handling is to log arena, period, ice condition, and camera feed, then test whether ISM residuals cluster by those variables. If they do, the model must be stratified or recalibrated.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: admit an ISM value only if (i) detection confidence, track continuity, and homography residual are within pre-registered bounds; (ii) the frame lies inside the training feature support; (iii) the shot frame is either automated or manually audited; and (iv) the claim being made matches the estimand. A frame-level “instantaneous danger” claim may be admissible under weaker conditions than a season-long “defensive player quality” claim. The source’s conclusion that ISM can support “line-matching strategies” [PAGE 5] is a high-level claim that requires player-level validation, not just low frame-level RMSE.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards-style adjudication: a claim or model update is rewarded only if it passes a counterfactual test with a measurable pass/fail criterion. The following three veto scenarios are verifiable and directly target the source pipeline.

1. Mirror/rink-side invariance veto. Counterfactual: reflect the rink coordinates across the center line, swap left and right net orientation, and recompute \((d_1,d_2,d_3,\theta)\). Under a correctly normalized geometric model, the reflected feature vector should produce the same ISM within a pre-registered tolerance \(\tau_{\text{mirror}}\). Verifiable statistic:
\[
\Delta_{\text{mirror}}=\left|\hat y(\mathbf z)-\hat y(\mathbf z_{\text{mirror}})\right|.
\]
Veto if \(\Delta_{\text{mirror}}>\tau_{\text{mirror}}\) on a held-out set. Prediction: the source’s features are Euclidean distances plus a net-relative angle [PAGE 3], so a properly implemented pipeline should pass this test; if it fails, the implementation is using unnormalized rink-side coordinates or the angle branch cut is wrong. This is a high-value engineering veto because the source mentions “defensive net’s side on the rink” as a recorded variable [PAGE 3].

2. Feature-ablation placebo veto. Counterfactual: preserve \(d_1\) and \(\theta\) but randomly permute \(d_2\) and \(d_3\) across held-out frames, then recompute RMSE. Verifiable statistic:
\[
\Delta_{\text{ablate}}=\operatorname{RMSE}_{\text{permuted}}-\operatorname{RMSE}_{\text{baseline}}.
\]
If \(\Delta_{\text{ablate}}\approx 0\) or is negative, then \(d_2\) and \(d_3\) are not contributing defenseman-specific information beyond the forward-to-net distance. Veto any claim that ISM measures 1v1 defense rather than a distance-to-net prior. Prediction: the source reports that “Feature d1 has the highest importance, with a significantly higher F-score than other features” [PAGE 5], so there is a real risk that \(d_2\) and \(d_3\) add little. The reward should be granted only if the ablation shows a meaningful, pre-registered RMSE increase and a player-level ranking change.

3. Manual-shot-frame jitter veto. Counterfactual: perturb the manually entered shot frame by \(\pm k\) frames, recompute the peak ISM and the clip-level ranking, and compare to the original. Verifiable statistic:
\[
\Delta_{\text{peak}}=\left|\max_t \hat y(t)-\max_{t\in[t_s-k,t_s+k]}\hat y(t)\right|,
\]
plus the rank correlation between original and jittered defenseman rankings. Veto if peak ISM or player ranking changes beyond tolerance. Prediction: the source says “Currently, we must manually input the frame number when the forward shoots the puck” [PAGE 5], so this is an immediate reproducibility risk. If the model is genuinely instantaneous and the peak is broad, jitter may be harmless; if the peak is a single noise spike, the metric is brittle. This veto directly tests whether the proposed “peak ISM” line-matching use case is stable [PAGE 5].

A fourth optional veto, useful as a robustness check, is track-identity swap: counterfactually swap \(F\) and \(D\) labels for a subset of frames and require that the ISM changes in the expected direction. If swapping identities does not change the score materially, the model is not using the defensive relationship \(d_2,d_3\) as intended. This is a verifiable diagnostic of whether the model has learned the 1v1 geometry or merely the forward’s location.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
