Let \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) be the goal indicator and \(X_i\in\mathbb{R}^d\) the pre-shot covariates. The source estimand is
\[
p_i=\mathbb{P}(Y_i=1\mid X_i=x_i),\qquad p_i\in[0,1],
\]
with probability unit dimensionless. The paper states: “xG can be seen as a probability, ranging between 0 and 1” [PAGE 1]. The logistic baseline is
\[
p_i=\sigma(\beta_0+\beta^\top x_i)=\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(\beta_0+\beta^\top x_i)]},
\]
where the logit \(\beta_0+\beta^\top x_i\) is dimensionless (natural-log units, nats). The fitted parameters are obtained by minimizing binary cross-entropy:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\beta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right],
\]
with units nats per shot. The Hessian is
\[
H=X^\top W X,\qquad W=\operatorname{diag}\{p_i(1-p_i)\}.
\]
Breakdown regime 1: if \(X\) is rank-deficient or quasi-separated, \(H\) is singular or ill-conditioned; the maximum-likelihood estimate diverges, \(\|\beta\|\to\infty\), and \(p_i\to\{0,1\}\). This is the formal counterpart to the paper’s “never 0 or 1” statement [PAGE 1]: finite-sample clipping can produce boundary values even when the mathematical model is open-interval.

The gradient-boosting model is an additive logit:
\[
F_M(x)=F_0+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\nu\,\gamma_m h_m(x),\qquad p(x)=\sigma(F_M(x)),
\]
where \(\nu\) is a dimensionless learning rate, \(\gamma_m\) a dimensionless leaf/step scale, and \(h_m\) a weak learner. The stagewise fit is
\[
(\gamma_m,h_m)=\arg\min_{\gamma,h}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\!\left(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)+\gamma h(x_i)\right).
\]
The paper describes this as combining weaker models by gradient descent and reports the proposed model at “y = 0.00+0.92x and a correlation of 0.902” versus StatsBomb [PAGE 7]. Accumulated xG is
\[
G_{\mathrm{xG}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_i,
\]
with unit expected goals. Under conditional independence of shot outcomes, \(\operatorname{Var}(G_{\mathrm{xG}})=\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\), with unit goals\(^2\). The paper’s comparison is baseline \(1866\), proposed \(1870\), StatsBomb \(1751\), exact goals \(1887\) [PAGE 7, Table 2].

Position adjustment is a transfer contrast:
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{pos}}
=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}f_{\theta_{\mathrm{pos}}}(x_i)
-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}f_{\theta_{\mathrm{gen}}}(x_i),
\]
measured in expected goals. The reported values are \(+86\) for Forward, \(-142\) for Midfield, and \(-473\) for Defender [PAGE 9, Table 4]. Player adjustment is
\[
\Delta_{M}
=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}f_{\theta_{M}}(x_i)
-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}}f_{\theta_{\mathrm{gen}}}(x_i),
\]
reported as \(+347\) for the Messi-trained model [PAGE 11, Table 5]. This is not automatically a causal skill effect. A causal version would require potential outcomes and assumptions:
\[
\Delta_{M}^{\mathrm{causal}}
=\mathbb{E}\!\left[Y(M)-Y(\bar a)\mid X\in\mathcal{D}\right],
\]
with consistency, exchangeability, and positivity \(P(X=x\mid M)>0\) for all target \(x\). If target shot contexts lie outside Messi’s support, \(\Delta_M\) is extrapolation.

Calibration is distinct from correlation:
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y\mid p(X)=s]=s,\qquad
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2,
\]
with Brier score dimensionless. The source reports a fitted line and correlation [PAGE 7], but those do not by themselves establish calibration by technique, position, player, or match state. Breakdown regime 2: covariate shift, \(P_{\mathrm{train}}(X)\neq P_{\mathrm{target}}(X)\), invalidates direct transfer. Breakdown regime 3: omitted cross-layer variables, \(Y\not\!\perp U\mid X\), so
\[
P(Y=1\mid X=x)=\int P(Y=1\mid X=x,U=u)\,dP(u\mid x)
\]
depends on the distribution of \(U\). If weather, surface, body orientation, or goalkeeper reach shift, the source model’s \(p_i\) is not transportable. Breakdown regime 4: small strata; the Goalkeeper row has “2 0 0” shots, goals, and shot/goal entries [PAGE 8], so binomial variance and model identifiability fail. Breakdown regime 5: temporal or match-level leakage; shots are clustered within matches and players, so random shot-level splits overstate generalization unless grouped by match/season/player.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a sequence of attempts to move xG from shot geometry to context. Tippett’s *The Expected Goals Philosophy* is presented as “one of the seminal pieces that started a certain data revolution” [PAGE 2]. Herold et al. are used to diagnose the earlier gap: “They either did not acknowledge or capture opponent positioning and thus, failed to provide context that coaches and analysts can apply to the match” [PAGE 3]. Lucey et al. add spatio-temporal build-up; Brechot and Flepp use logistic regression over 7304 matches; Madrero uses Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and Neural Networks and finds that “Distance to goal proves to be the most influential feature, regardless of player quality” [PAGE 3]. Fairchild et al. use MLS shot coordinates and categorical shot/assist/play types; Cavus and Biecek scale to 315,430 shots with tree ensembles and explainable AI; Umami et al. combine distance and angle and suggest “surface type and condition, role play, and confidence” [PAGE 4]; Tureen and Olthof use hierarchical GLMMs for Estimated Player Impact [PAGE 4]. This paper’s retrocausal position is that later player/position adjustments reinterpret earlier average-shot models: the same average xG that looked “objective” is now seen as a mixture over position, player, technique, and game state.

The multi-week traps are temporal and institutional. First, the narrative trap: because Messi is known to be elite, a Messi-trained model that outputs \(+347\) can be read as confirmation rather than as a transfer test. Over weeks of analysis, confirmation accumulates unless the test is pre-registered and player-held-out. Second, the benchmark trap: matching accumulated xG to goals in one match or one season can hide local miscalibration. The overhead-kick case is the retrocausal correction: “the proposed xG shows a 0.131 value whereas StatsBomb is at 0.022” [PAGE 13] retroactively weakens the claim that the model’s technique handling is generally valid. Third, the stationarity trap: a model trained across competitions and seasons assumes stable shot-selection and defensive pressure distributions. Over multi-week international breaks, transfer windows, weather shifts, and tactical changes, \(P(X)\) and \(P(Y\mid X)\) can drift. Fourth, the small-stratum trap: positional subsets look like clean comparisons, but the Goalkeeper row (“2 0 0”) shows how quickly a subgroup becomes inferentially empty [PAGE 8]. Fifth, the leakage trap: if shots are split at random rather than by match/player/season, near-duplicate contexts from the same match can inflate apparent performance. The paper’s own move from 26 to 40 features and its Table 2 closeness are best read as a engineering lineage, not as a causal genealogy of finishing skill.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that the model is trained on behavior generated by agents who are themselves modeling the game. A player’s shot policy is \(\pi(a\mid x,s)\), where \(s\) includes score state, pressure, fatigue, and beliefs about the goalkeeper. Under crisis, the policy can regress to a habit \(h(a\mid x)\): players revert to familiar shot locations or techniques. If the xG model omits \(s\), it estimates a mixture
\[
P(Y=1\mid X=x)=\int P(Y=1\mid X=x,S=s)\,dP(s\mid x),
\]
so “crisis habit regression” appears as a residual drift, not as a new skill level. The counter-trap is the Messi adjustment: training on Messi data learns \(P(Y=1\mid X,M)\), which includes Messi’s selection policy and finishing. Applying it to others asks a different question, \(P(Y=1\mid X,A\neq M)\) or \(P(Y(M)=1\mid X)\). Without overlap, the model mistakes “Messi’s shot diet” for “Messi’s boot.” The paper’s Table 6 pattern—after adjustment, nearly every listed player is below adjusted xG [PAGE 11–12]—is consistent with a prior shift rather than a universal skill verdict.

The clock is the third axis. Match minute, season, and competition are not cosmetic. If \(P(Y=1\mid X,t)\) changes with clock—late-game fatigue, chasing a scoreline, set-piece urgency—then an omitted \(t\) averages over regimes. The paper includes game-minute as a feature only via its literature review of Cavus and Biecek [PAGE 4], not as a central operator in its own Table 1. The recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore asks three questions. First, does the model learn habit or skill? Check whether residuals correlate with pressure and score state. Second, does the counterfactual pass positivity? Check whether target shot contexts have support in the player-specific training set. Third, does the clock leak? Check grouped cross-validation by match and season, and calibration by minute bucket. Without those checks, “player-adjusted xG” is a recursive belief about a player, not a stable measurement of the player.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is justified when the probability model is not identifiable or transportable across physical, biomechanical, or weather layers. The formal condition is omitted-variable dependence:
\[
Y\not\!\perp U\mid X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P(Y=1\mid X=x)=\int P(Y=1\mid X=x,U=u)\,dP(u\mid x).
\]
If the training distribution \(P_{\mathrm{train}}(u\mid x)\) differs from the target distribution, the model’s \(p_i\) is biased even if its training fit is good. Weather is the clearest physical layer: wind vector, rain, temperature, and surface condition alter ball trajectory and goalkeeper movement. The paper’s own literature review flags “surface type and condition” as a missing variable class [PAGE 4], and the model’s feature table does not list weather or surface. A match played in strong wind or on a wet surface is therefore an extrapolation, not a validated prediction. The physical trajectory equation is not needed to prove the statistical point: if \(U\) shifts and \(Y\not\!\perp U\mid X\), the conditional probability shifts.

Biomechanical veto is empirically visible in the source. The overhead-kick goal is assigned \(0.131235\) by the proposed model versus \(0.022605\) by StatsBomb [PAGE 15, Table 8], and the paper itself says this “likely suggests an over-prediction of the xG value of overhead kicks, which are known to be a very tough skill” [PAGE 13]. That is a biomechanical layer veto: body orientation, acrobatic technique, and balance constraints are not exchangeable with normal volleys or ground shots. A model that treats “shot technique” as a categorical label without enough overhead-kick examples can overfit the category mean. The goalkeeper stratum is a second veto: “Goalkeeper 2 0 0” [PAGE 8] means the position-adjusted estimate is a two-shot extrapolation, not a stable skill estimate. The adjudication rule is therefore: if a stratum has \(n<n_{\min}\), or if the technique-specific calibration slope is not distinguishable from zero, or if cross-layer variables lie outside training support, the model must abstain or output a widened interval rather than a point xG. The source’s aggregate match result—proposed \(3.4\) versus StatsBomb \(2.7\), FBRef \(3.4\), infogol \(3.59\) [PAGE 12]—does not override the local veto, because aggregate agreement can be produced by offsetting stratum errors.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
