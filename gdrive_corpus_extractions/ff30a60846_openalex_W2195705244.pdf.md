The paper quotes that prior to 1997, “goalkeepers were required to remain still until the ball was struck” [PAGE 3]. Hop 2: that rule change enlarged the goalkeeper’s strategy set, increasing save probability and forcing shooters into new strategic behavior [PAGE 3]. Hop 3: because prior literature blended pre- and post-1997 data, the authors isolate a post-1997 panel comprising the 2002, 2006 and 2010 World Cups and the 2004 and 2008 European Championships for match play, plus 2000 for shootouts [PAGE 9]. The resulting sample contains 63 match-play penalties across 254 matches (0.248 per match) and 120 shootout penalties across 13 shootouts [PAGE 10–11, 16].

A gray-zone audit must isolate where inference breaks down. The temporal distribution shows 36.5% of penalties in Q4 versus 9.5% in Q1 [PAGE 12], yet conversion rises to 82.6% in Q4 from 50.0% in Q3 [PAGE 13]. This contradicts a naive fatigue model, which would predict monotonic decline; the authors therefore reject fatigue as a significant driver [PAGE 13]. However, the aggregate quarter test is only a trend (p = 0.120) at the α = 0.10 practical-significance threshold, not significant at α = 0.05 [PAGE 12]. Further breakdowns: match-play penalties in extra time number only two (3.2%) and were excluded to avoid sample-size bias [PAGE 11]; the 2000 EURO match-play data were unavailable [PAGE 9]; goalkeeper save data were unavailable for EURO shootouts [PAGE 18]. Shooters 6–9 (n = 6) were excluded from statistical comparison because of low power and skill confounding [PAGE 17]. Skill was never directly measured; prior proxies used playing position, which the authors call inadequate [PAGE 6]. Valence was coded objectively, but the authors concede objective valence may not match subjective experience [PAGE 8]. Self-selection means habitual takers are psychologically non-exchangeable with random players [PAGE 7]. These regimes define the singularity boundaries: small-n cells, unobserved covariates, and the α = 0.05 / 0.10 decision boundary.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy begins with the 26 October 1863 Freemason’s Tavern meeting that codified football’s rules, followed by incorporation of the penalty kick in 1891–1892 and the penalty spot in 1902 [PAGE 3]. The 1997 amendment allowing goalkeeper lateral movement is the pivotal inflection point: it changed the information structure of the penalty subgame by giving the goalkeeper a pre-kick maneuver option [PAGE 3].

Scientific lineage proceeds through Table 1 and the discussion. McGarry & Franks (2000) used 1982–1998 World Cup and 1996 European Championship data, reporting 85.2% match-play and 75.6% shootout conversion, and identified an inverted-U arousal hypothesis from Yerkes & Dodson (1908) [PAGE 4, citation 3 on PAGE 22]. Jordet et al. (2007) examined World Cup, European Championship and Copa America shootouts, finding 78.9% overall conversion and a stress effect by shooting order [PAGE 5]. Dohmen (2008) used the German Bundesliga from 1963 to 2003/2004 and found home teams missed more, experience reduced missing, and close scorelines late in matches lowered miss rates [PAGE 7]. Jordet & Hartman (2008) introduced valence, showing negative-valence shots produced 30% fewer goals and more avoidance behavior [PAGE 7–8]. Kuss et al. (2007) studied self-selection in the Bundesliga [PAGE 6–7]. The present study’s contribution is restricting analysis to post-1997 tournaments only, thereby avoiding the pre/post-rule contamination noted in prior work [PAGE 8, 20].

Multi-week traps: tournament knockout architecture imposes path dependence. Winning the World Cup requires four elimination victories; the European Championship requires three [PAGE 16]. Because 13 of 254 matches (18.8%) went to shootouts, and finalists had roughly a 50% probability of shootout involvement en route (World Cup finalists 56.5%, EURO finalists 46.5%) [PAGE 2, 16], a team’s progression is not independent of prior penalty exposure. Fatigue would nominally accumulate across weeks of a tournament, yet the study found no significant fatigue effect on conversion [PAGE 13, 21]. The scheduling trap is therefore that late-stage penalties cluster in match time (Q4) rather than being driven by accumulated tournament fatigue [PAGE 12, 20].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The penalty kick is a recursive shooter–goalkeeper game. At level 0, the shooter selects a target. At level 1, the goalkeeper predicts that target and commits a dive. At level 2, the shooter anticipates the goalkeeper’s prediction and selects the complementary target. The 1997 rule change expands the goalkeeper’s level-1 strategy set because lateral movement before the kick reduces the shooter’s time advantage [PAGE 3]. The data partially support a counter-trap rather than a simple stress regression: fifth shooters converted 100.0% of penalties, while fourth shooters converted only 45.8% [PAGE 17]. This produces a normal U-shape across order (high at 1st and 5th, low at 4th), contradicting the inverted-U stress hypothesis inherited from McGarry & Franks and Yerkes–Dodson [PAGE 4, 18].

Crisis habit regression appears under negative valence: when a miss means instant defeat, players exhibit avoidance behavior and score roughly 30% fewer goals than on positive-valence shots [PAGE 7–8]. However, the present study’s fifth shooters—mostly positive valence (71.4%)—were perfect, suggesting that elite shooters selected for late order do not regress under pressure; rather, self-selection filters for stress-resistant shooters [PAGE 5, 17]. The clock layer is the match clock: penalties cluster at 69–90+ minutes (36.5% of awards) [PAGE 12], creating temporal pressure for defenders and referees, yet conversion remains high (82.6%) in that window [PAGE 13]. Thus the crisis is not execution failure under clock pressure but the awarding of the penalty itself late in matches.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: the penalty is taken from a stationary ball at the penalty mark; historically the ball was placed anywhere along a 12-yard line before the spot was introduced in 1902 [PAGE 3]. Because the ball is stationary, run-up variability is bounded by the shooter’s approach mechanics, and the goalkeeper’s reaction window is bounded by human visual-motor latency. The source does not quantify ball speed or goalkeeper reaction time, so any numerical biomechanical bound would be exogenous to the archive.

Biomechanical layer: post-1997 goalkeeper lateral movement shortens the effective aiming window for the shooter [PAGE 3]. The source treats goalkeeper save/miss as a binary outcome without modeling joint kinematics, approach velocity, or foot–ball contact dynamics; therefore biomechanical confounders (ankle plantarflexion, hip rotation, strike precision) are unobserved.

Weather layer: the Methods section records no weather, pitch condition, or temperature covariates [PAGE 9–10]. If a match is played in heavy rain or crosswind, ball flight and run-up footing are disturbed, but the dataset’s goal/miss label cannot attribute cause. Veto rule: if an official match report lists adverse weather at the time of the penalty, the conversion probability \(P(G \mid \text{weather})\) is unidentified from this source alone, and the pooled quarterly rate (e.g., 82.6% in Q4 [PAGE 13]) cannot be transported to that condition.

Substitution layer: goalkeeper substitutions before a penalty alter the save-probability layer, yet the study’s goalkeeper analysis covers only World Cup shootouts and does not track goalkeeper identity [PAGE 18–19]. If a specialist goalkeeper is introduced immediately before a kick, the 17.1% average save rate [PAGE 18–19] is not exchangeable.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
