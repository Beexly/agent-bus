The first derivable hop is the stationarity premise: “with the effective habit strength and primary drive substantially constant, behavior evoked by successive repetitions of the same stimulus presents a close approximation to a normal probability curve” [PAGE 318]. That statement fixes two control variables—effective habit strength, call it H, and primary drive, call it D—as approximately invariant across repetitions. It then asserts that the observed response variable is normally distributed around a central tendency. In formal terms, this is an ergodic and stationarity assumption: the ensemble over repeated trials is treated as exchangeable with respect to H and D, so that sample averages converge to population expectations.

The second hop concerns the oscillation variable. Hull locates the oscillation in “the effective reaction potentiality (sER)” [PAGE 318] and asserts that “the oscillations of different effective habit tendencies are essentially asynchronous” [PAGE 318]. Asynchrony is a strong independence condition: for distinct habit tendencies i and j, trial-by-trial fluctuations eta_i and eta_j must have near-zero covariance. If that condition holds, the central limit logic supports treating the aggregate evoked behavior as Gaussian; if it fails, common drive modulation or shared neural substrates can couple the oscillations and produce skewness, bimodality, or state-dependent switching that a single normal curve cannot represent.

The third hop connects the Gaussian oscillation to the empirical learning curve. Hull notes “the sigmoid shape of certain learning curves when plotted in terms of the per cent (or probability) of reaction evocation” [PAGE 318], and attributes the “characteristically more protracted upper portion” to “the progressively slower rate of habit-strength acquisition as the physiological limit is approached” [PAGE 318]. Multi-hop implication: as H approaches a finite ceiling H_max, the increment dH per reinforced trial must decline toward zero. That bounded acquisition generates an asymptotic, sigmoid probability curve rather than an unbounded linear increase. The Gaussian approximation remains useful only while the system is far enough from the boundary that probability mass is not piled against 0 or 1.

The fourth hop is response intensity generalization. Hull explains the paradox of novel evoked reactions through oscillation’s tendency “to modify the intensity of every muscular contraction involved in every coördinated reaction; this makes the act evoked more or less different from any act involved in the original reinforcement. This amounts in effect to response intensity generalization” [PAGE 318]. This is a biomechanical claim: noise is not merely additive at the decision level; it rescales the motor execution vector, so the topography of the response drifts even when the stimulus and reinforcement history are fixed.

The fifth and most restrictive hop is the epistemic limit. Hull states that “no theory of organismic behavior can ever be expected to mediate the precise prediction of the specific behavior of any organism at a given instant” [PAGE 318], while prediction of “approximately the central tendencies” remains possible for organisms under “approximately the same antecedent factors” sharing “substantially the same neural and receptor-effector equipment” [PAGE 318]. The physiological limit H_max is named but not quantified. The normal approximation breaks down under nonstationary drive, correlated oscillations, bounded probabilities near 0 or 1, small samples, and equipment mismatch. Response intensity generalization further implies that “the same behavior” may not be topographically identical across trials, threatening response definition in quantitative measurement. These are exact breakdown regimes, not mere caveats: if any one obtains, the central-tendency license from [PAGE 318] is locally suspended.

P_t = Pr(evocation | H_t, D_t, theta) = Phi((sER_t - theta) / sigma_eta),

Habit acquisition near the physiological limit is represented by a bounded-rate assumption:

dH/dt = alpha (H_max - H) * I_reinforced,

where alpha is in reinforced-trial^-1, I_reinforced is an indicator equal to 1 on reinforced trials and 0 otherwise, and H_max is in HHU [PAGE 318]. Cov(eta_i, eta_j) approx 0,

with covariance in RPU^2 [PAGE 318]. Breakdown regimes include common motivational arousal, shared effector fatigue, circadian drive coupling, and crisis states that recruit a single dominant habit, all of which induce nonzero covariance and invalidate the independent-oscillation assumption.

bar B = E[B | H, D, equipment] approx sample mean over N trials,

Retrocausal reading of later relapse and renewal research suggests that apparent “spontaneous recovery” after multi-week extinction may be partly the unmasking of asynchronous oscillations when contextual cues change, not recovery of a single stored trace.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Recursive theory of mind here means modeling the organism while recognizing that the organism’s behavior is generated under the same antecedent and equipment constraints the modeler assumes [PAGE 318]. Three dynamical postures must be distinguished.

Crisis habit regression. Under acute crisis, primary drive D is no longer “substantially constant” [PAGE 318]; a dominant threat or homeostatic emergency monopolizes the organism’s effector budget. The oscillation terms of competing habit tendencies cease to be asynchronous because a common crisis signal couples them: Cov(eta_i, eta_j) becomes significantly positive [PAGE 318]. Behavior regresses toward the highest-strength available habit, not because the organism chooses it, because lower-strength alternatives are biomechanically suppressed by muscular contraction reorganization [PAGE 318]. Prediction shifts from population central tendencies to an attractor dominated by the crisis habit.

Counter-trap. The modeler’s counter-trap is to assume that because crisis produces regression to the dominant habit, the dominant habit’s average strength predicts the crisis response. Hull’s response-intensity generalization undermines this: crisis modifies “the intensity of every muscular contraction involved in every coördinated reaction,” making the evoked act “more or less different from any act involved in the original reinforcement” [PAGE 318]. Weak or latent habits can be recruited through intensity modulation, producing novel hybrid acts absent from the reinforcement history. Averaging pre-crisis data therefore underestimates crisis novelty and overestimates topographic stability.

Clock. Biological time imposes a third layer. Drive D_t is modulated by circadian and homeostatic clocks, so D_t = D_bar + A sin(omega t + phi) plus stochastic perturbation. Hull’s “given instant” impossibility [PAGE 318] is sharpened: precise prediction would require knowing not only H and D_bar but also the clock phase phi, frequency omega, and the organism’s current position in its oscillatory cycle. If the organism recursively models the modeler, it may vary response timing or intensity to reduce predictability, turning the modeler’s central tendency into a moving target. Breakdown occurs when clock phase is unobserved, when crisis couples all oscillations, or when response-intensity generalization makes the same nominal response physically different across trials [PAGE 318].

Simultaneously, extreme heat or cold changes muscle contractile kinetics and evaporative capacity, shifting I_m,t and sigma_eta. Veto condition: |T_a - T_neutral| > Delta T_max or RH outside the band permitting stable motor output. Under weather veto, the distribution of evoked behavior becomes skewed or bimodal because thermoregulatory behavior competes with the target response, violating “essentially asynchronous” oscillations and the single-normal assumption [PAGE 318].

Adjudication order: physical veto is absolute (no metabolic substrate, no behavior); biomechanical veto is next (substrate exists but effector cannot transduce); weather veto is contextual (modulates both drive and biomechanics). If any veto fires, the modeler must revert from central-tendency prediction to layer-specific state estimation rather than force a Gaussian average [PAGE 318].

Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards counterfactual veto scenarios and predictions.

Scenario 1: Drive nonstationarity veto. Hold the stimulus and effective habit strength H approximately constant across two blocks, but manipulate primary drive D so that Block A uses short deprivation (D_A low) and Block B uses long deprivation (D_B high), violating “primary drive substantially constant” [PAGE 318]. Verifiable rule: reject the single-Gaussian central-tendency model if the pooled response distribution fails a normality test and shows variance inflation or bimodality at p < 0.01. Prediction: Block A and Block B will have distinct means and variances; the pooled distribution will be better fit by a mixture of two Gaussians than by one normal curve; response evocation probability P_t will shift systematically with D_t according to the threshold operator [PAGE 318].

Scenario 2: Oscillation synchrony veto. Train two competing habit tendencies to stimuli on the same continuum, then introduce a common motivational arousal cue during test trials to couple their oscillations. Verifiable rule: compute trial-by-trial trial-wise response intensity or latency residuals after removing H and D effects; reject asynchrony if Cov(eta_i, eta_j) is significantly greater than zero. Prediction: under common arousal, Cov(eta_i, eta_j) will exceed zero, response-intensity generalization will generate hybrid acts not present in the original reinforcement set [PAGE 318], and topographic mismatch between evoked and reinforced acts will increase relative to a no-arousal control.

Scenario 3: Physiological-limit saturation veto. Extend reinforced training well past the point at which performance appears asymptotic. Verifiable rule: fit acquisition increments Delta H per reinforced trial and test for a negative quadratic term in H; reject the linear/normal-growth approximation if the quadratic term is significant and the ceiling proportion of trials at maximal response exceeds 90 percent. Prediction: Delta H -> 0 as H -> H_max, producing the protracted upper portion of the sigmoid learning curve [PAGE 318]; the distribution of reaction evocation probability will pile against P_t = 1, violating the unbounded normal approximation; and prediction of central tendencies will remain approximately valid while single-instance prediction remains impossible [PAGE 318]. Each scenario is falsifiable with standard behavioral time-series measures and directly maps to a stated limitation or assumption on page 318.
