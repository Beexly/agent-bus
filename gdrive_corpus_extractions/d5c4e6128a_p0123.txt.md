Explicit reasoning, James claims, traverses indirect association paths—those "which would lead to practically the same conclusion as the straighter ones"—whereas in perception such paths are "in all probability closed" ([PAGE 113]). This is not merely a speed distinction; it is a topological claim about the permeability of the associative graph. If we denote the set of associative paths by Γ and a traversal operator T: Γ → {0,1}, then T_reasoning(γ_indirect) ≈ 1 while T_perception(γ_indirect) ≈ 0. The inference is that perception operates under a gating constraint absent in deliberate inference.

The gray zone lies in James's modal hedging: "in all probability" and "no ground whatever for assuming them to be traversed now" ([PAGE 113]). He does not prove the closure of indirect paths in perception; he infers it from appearances. This is an abductive leap from phenomenology to neurophysiology, and its breakdown regime is any case where perception demonstrably recruits indirect associations—e.g., perceptual learning, expertise, or cross-modal completion—without conscious reasoning.

Hamilton's "great law," quoted on [PAGE 113], asserts that "Knowledge and Feeling,—Perception and Sensation, though always coexistent, are always in the inverse ratio of each other." Spencer reframes this as mutual exclusivity of states rather than coexistence within one state. James accepts the law only when sensation is restricted to hedonic tone—pleasure or pain. His corollary is structural: "the more concentrated a state of consciousness is, the more vivid it is" ([PAGE 113]). Isolated qualia—feeling a color, listening to a tone per se—are noticed more intensely than when embedded in a total object because "the more diffused cerebral excitement of the perceptive state is probably incompatible" with that intensity ([PAGE 113]).
