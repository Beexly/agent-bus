First, I must extract the formal components. The theorem operates on sequences of *integrable simple functions*, denoted {f_n} and {g_n}. These are finite linear combinations of characteristic functions of measurable sets with finite measure, ensuring all integrals involved are well-defined finite numbers. They converge in measure to a measurable function f. Convergence in measure means that for any ε > 0, the measure of the set where |h_n - f| ≥ ε tends to zero as n → ∞, for h_n being either f_n or g_n.

The critical hypothesis is equation (7): lim_{n,m→∞} sup_E |v_n(E) - λ_m(E)| = 0. Here, v_n(E) = ∫_E f_n dμ and λ_n(E) = ∫_E g_n dμ are the indefinite integrals—set functions derived from the simple functions. The supremum is taken over all measurable sets E. This condition states that the family of set functions {v_n} ∪ {λ_n} is uniformly Cauchy with respect to the total variation norm (supremum over E). This is much stronger than pointwise convergence of integrals; it implies the sequence of measures themselves converges in the strong operator topology on the space of signed measures.

The proof proceeds by first showing v(E) = λ(E) for sets E of finite measure. It uses the triangle inequality: |v(E) - λ(E)| ≤ |v(E) - v_n(E)| + |v_n(E) - λ_n(E)| + |λ_n(E) - λ(E)|. To bound this, one typically uses absolute continuity arguments, but f might not be integrable yet. Instead, Halmos references "the uniform absolute continuity proved in Theorem C." So the proof is essentially showing these two limits coincide.

Because any σ-finite set can be written as a countable union of disjoint finite measure sets, and additivity allows reconstruction.
