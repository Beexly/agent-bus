Tolman’s 1932 work sits at the confluence of several intellectual traditions. His rejection of pure S-R mechanisms echoes Kant’s distinction between phenomena and noumena—the organism posits unobservable purposes ("quiescences") based on observable signs. This teleological bent was controversial in the empiricist-dominated behaviorism of the era.

Scientifically, he built on Hull’s drive-reduction theory but added cognitive mediation. Hull (1943) later formalized drive as a multiplicative factor in performance equations ($sEr = V \cdot D \cdot K$), but Tolman insisted that expectations—not just drives—govern choice. The lineage continues into modern predictive coding theories (Friston, 2010), where the brain minimizes surprise by maintaining generative models of the world.

A multi-week trap emerges when researchers retroactively apply Tolman’s "purposeful" label to any complex behavior without empirical validation. For example, claiming that a rodent navigating a maze has a "goal" might overlook simpler explanations like gradient-following or habit formation. Such overattribution can lead to flawed experimental designs—for instance, testing for "expectancies" in tasks where animals rely on procedural memory.

Historically, Tolman faced criticism from both Watson (for being mentalistic) and Skinner (for not operationalizing "purposes"). Today, his ideas resonate with embodied cognition and enactivism, which emphasize the role of the body in shaping perception and action. However, these fields often neglect the precise mechanistic details Tolman sought—how exactly do "innate readines" arise developmentally? Are they fixed at birth or shaped by prenatal experience?

Another genealogical thread connects to cybernetics (Wiener, 1948): the idea of feedback loops regulating behavior toward goals. But cybernetics typically assumed engineering-designed controllers, whereas Tolman emphasized evolved biological implementations. This distinction matters for AI—if we build agents with "Tolman-like" architectures, should we program explicit goal representations or allow them to emerge from reinforcement learning?

In summary, tracing this lineage reveals that Tolman’s contribution was to insist that behavior requires interpretation of signs relative to ends. Any modern implementation must grapple with the origin of these interpretations—are they truly innate, or constructed through interaction?</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
