**Formal Definitions & Units**  
Let $sE_R$ denote *reaction-evocation potential*, symbolically constructed to quantify an organism's tendency to emit a specific response $R$ under stimulus conditions $s$. It is defined via a product rule (Hull, p. 239):  
\[
sE_R = k \cdot f(sH_R) \cdot g(D)
\]  
where:  
- $sH_R$ = habit strength (dimensionless, scaled 0–100 wats).  
- $D$ = primary drive intensity (scaled 0–100 wats).  
- $f(\cdot)$, $g(\cdot)$ are monotonic transformation functions mapping raw physiological/psychological quantities into the standardized wat scale.  
- $k$ is a normalization constant ensuring maximum $sE_R = 100$ wats when both operands are maximal.  

**Drive Functional Forms**  
As specified in the source, $D$ is context-dependent:  
\[
D_{\text{hunger}} = \alpha \cdot h + \beta \quad (\text{linear in privation hours } h)
\]  
\[
D_{\text{sex}} = \gamma \cdot c^\delta + \epsilon \quad (\text{nonlinear in hormone concentration } c)
\]  
Units: $h$ [hours], $c$ [mol/L]; $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon$ empirically determined coefficients.  

**Reaction Potential Singularity Regimes**  
Breakdown occurs under extreme parameter values:  
1. **Satiation Limit**: If $h=0$ or $c \to 0$, then $D \to 0$, implying $sE_R \to 0$ irrespective of $sH_R$. This predicts complete behavioral suppression even for well-practiced responses—a critical testable hypothesis.  
2. **Habit Ceiling**: For fixed high $D$, increasing $sH_R$ may saturate due to neural refractoriness; if $sH_R \to 100$ (maximum wat), further learning yields diminishing returns. The multiplicative model does not inherently capture such saturation unless $f(sH_R)$ is bounded nonlinearly.  
3. **Measurement Singularity**: The 100-wat scale assumes a universal physiological ceiling, but inter-individual variability (e.g., metabolic rates) could cause scale compression/expansion, violating comparability across subjects.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Hull’s conceptual triad—drive, habit, reaction potential—emerges from early 20th-century behaviorism’s push toward mathematical precision, reacting against mentalistic explanations. Historically, it inherits from Thorndike’s law of effect (strengthening connections via satisfaction) but formalizes it via continuous variables rather than discrete trials. The "wat" unit honors J.B. Watson, linking directly to Watson’s 1913 behaviorist manifesto that rejected introspection. Yet, the genealogy contains traps: over-reliance on laboratory rats/pigeons creates a multi-week trap of extrapolating findings to complex human cognition (e.g., emotional regulation involves neurochemical cascades far richer than simple hormone concentrations). Moreover, the era’s reductionism often ignored genetic predispositions (now central to behavioral genetics) and epigenetic modulation of drive expression. The retrocausal insight here is that Hull’s axiomatic elegance inadvertently constrained future research by framing behavior solely through mechanistic inputs, delaying integration of cognitive mediators until the 1950s–60s. Contemporary neuroscience now views "habits" as basal ganglia-thalamocortical loop plasticity and "drives" as hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis outputs—both dynamically interactive, not multiplicatively separable.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Analyzing an organism’s decision-making via Hull’s model requires simulating recursive state updates: given current $h$ and $c$, compute $D$; retrieve stored $sH_R$ from memory traces; multiply to estimate $sE_R$; compare against alternative actions’ potentials. Crisis habit regression occurs when acute stress (elevated cortisol) suppresses prefrontal inhibition, causing maladaptive habits (e.g., substance use) to dominate despite low instrumental value—this reflects a breakdown in $g(D)$ where pathological $D$ overrides rational cost-benefit analysis encoded in $sH_R$. Conversely, counter-trap strategies involve metacognitive awareness: recognizing one’s habitual pattern and deliberately engaging competing goals (activating opposing $sH_R'$). Clock dependency is evident in circadian rhythms modulating $D$ (e.g., melatonin affecting sleep drive); thus, time-of-day becomes a veto layer over predicted $sE_R$. In humans, theory of mind extends recursively: predicting others’ $sE_R$ based on inferred $D$ (their hunger/thirst) and observed $sH_R$ (their routines), enabling social coordination. Hull’s scalar lacks intentionality but captures heuristic proxies for goal-directedness.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical constraints impose hard vetoes on any calculated $sE_R > 0$. Example: An organism may have immense thirst drive ($D \approx 90$ wats) and strong learned habit to drink from a specific fountain ($sH_R \approx 95$ wats), yielding high $sE_R$, but if its jaw muscles are paralyzed (stroke) or the water pipe is frozen solid, action fails utterly. Weather provides another veto: extreme cold increases thermoregulatory drive to seek shelter, yet blizzard conditions (visibility <1m, wind chill -30°C) physically prevent movement regardless of $sE_R$. Biomechanics further dictates response feasibility: a bird cannot fly with broken wings even if migration drive peaks. These layers operate hierarchically above psychological potentials—they are necessary (but insufficient) conditions for behavior. Thus, $sE_R$ represents motivational readiness, not execution capability; the true probability of action $P(A)$ equals $sE_R$ only after confirming absence of veto signals from proprioceptive feedback and environmental affordances.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
