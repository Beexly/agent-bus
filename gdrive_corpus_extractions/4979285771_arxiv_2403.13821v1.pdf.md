The source defines a \(p\)-Wasserstein distance between discrete probability masses \(\mu\) on \(X=\{x_1,\dots,x_m\}\) and \(\nu\) on \(Y=\{y_1,\dots,y_n\}\):
\[
W_p(\mu,\nu):=\left(\min \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{ij}P_{ij}\right)^{1/p},
\]
where \(P\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\) is the transportation plan, \(P_{ij}\) is probability mass transported from \(x_i\) to \(y_j\), and \(D_{ij}\) is the ground distance; the source states that \(P_{ij}\) is “the quantity (probability mass) to be transported from \(x_i\) to \(y_j\)” [PAGE 5]. For \(p=1\) this is Earth Mover’s Distance. In this study \(d=9\) principal components are used and \(p=1\), with Euclidean ground distance [PAGE 5]. Units: probability masses are dimensionless; if the ground distance is in meters, \(W_1\) is in meters; because the 9 coordinates are PCA scores from standardized mixed-unit features, the PCs are not pure SI meters, so numerical \(W_1\) values are in PCA-score units, not directly in meters. Breakdown regimes: \(p<1\) does not give the stated metric form; if a player’s shot distribution is empty the empirical distribution is undefined, which is why the paper limits to players with 30 or more shots and retains 325 players [PAGE 5]; if two distributions have disjoint support, the Wasserstein formulation remains usable, unlike a naive overlap-dependent divergence, as the paper notes by contrasting it with Kullback-Leibler divergence [PAGE 5].

Ward clustering minimizes the within-cluster sum of squared deviations when merging; the source uses it because it gives relatively even cluster sizes [PAGE 5]. Silhouette validation uses:
\[
a(i)=\frac{1}{|C_{in}|-1}\sum_{x(j)\in C_{in}}d(x(i),x(j)),\quad
b(i)=\frac{1}{|C_{near}|}\sum_{x(j)\in C_{near}}d(x(i),x(j)),
\]
\[
s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max(a(i),b(i))}.
\]
Here \(s(i)\in[-1,1]\), with larger values indicating better cohesion and separation [PAGE 6]. Singularity: if a cluster is a singleton, \(|C_{in}|-1=0\), so \(a(i)\) is undefined; the method assumes non-singleton clusters or a convention for singleton cases. The paper reports mean silhouette coefficients but chooses 13 clusters partly by interpretability [PAGE 6].

Shooting efficiency validation uses
\[
\mathrm{TS\%}=\frac{\mathrm{PTS}}{2\times(\mathrm{FGA}+0.44\times \mathrm{FTA})}\times 100,
\]
with stated range 0 to 150 [PAGE 7]. This is a percentage-like scoring-efficiency statistic, not an SI unit. For offensive roles, Fuzzy C-means minimizes
\[
J=\sum_{k=1}^{c}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_k^{(i)})^q\|x^{(i)}-v_k\|^2,
\]
subject to
\[
\sum_{k=1}^{c}u_k^{(i)}=1,
\]
where \(u_k^{(i)}\) is the membership coefficient, \(v_k\) the centroid, \(q\ge1\) the fuzziness parameter, and \(c\) the cluster count; the paper uses \(q=1.2\) under \(c=10\) [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes: \(q\to1\) approaches hard assignment; \(q\to\infty\) makes memberships increasingly uniform; coincident centroids or degenerate data can make memberships unstable. Approximately 66% of players have maximum membership at least 0.9, and about 97% at least 0.5 [PAGE 8].

For soft-role lineup combinations,
\[
\mathrm{Combos}_{kk'}^{(i)}=
\begin{cases}
\sum_{a\in A(i)}\sum_{a'\in A(i),a'\ne a}u_k^{(a)}u_{k'}^{(a')}, & k\ne k',\\
\frac12\sum_{a\in A(i)}\sum_{a'\in A(i),a'\ne a}u_k^{(a)}u_{k'}^{(a')}, & k=k',
\end{cases}
\]
where \(A(i)\) is the set of players in lineup \(i\) [PAGE 10]. Because each player’s memberships sum to 1, the total combination mass in a five-player lineup remains \(\binom{5}{2}=10\) [PAGE 9-10]. The number of explanatory combination types is
\[
\binom{\binom{c}{2}}{}=\binom{c+1}{2},
\]
with \(c=5\) giving 15 and \(c=10\) giving 55 [PAGE 9, PAGE 17, PAGE 20]. The OFFRTG adjustment is
\[
\mathrm{adjustedOFFRTG}=\mathrm{LineupOFFRTG}\times(\mathrm{MIN}/300)+\mathrm{TeamOFFRTG}\times(1-\mathrm{MIN}/300),
\]
where MIN is simultaneous minutes and OFFRTG is points per 100 possessions [PAGE 10]. Units are points per 100 possessions; MIN is minutes. Breakdown: at MIN=300 the lineup value is unadjusted; at MIN=0 the value collapses to team OFFRTG, but the paper only analyzes lineups above 50 minutes, or approximately 100+ possessions [PAGE 10]. The Bayesian hierarchical model is
\[
y^{(i)}\sim \mathcal N(\mu^{(i)},\epsilon^{(i)2}),\quad
\mu^{(i)}=\alpha_t+x_t^{(i)T}\beta_t,
\]
\[
\alpha_t\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\alpha,10^2),\quad
\beta_t\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\beta,\sigma_\beta^2),\quad
\mu_\beta\sim\mathcal N(0,10^2),
\]
\[
\sigma_\beta\sim\mathrm{HalfNormal}(10),\quad
\epsilon^{(i)}\sim\mathrm{HalfNormal}(10),
\]
with team index \(t\), adjusted OFFRTG \(y^{(i)}\), and lineup explanatory vector \(x_t^{(i)}\) [PAGE 11]. The authors report \(\hat R<1.1\) for convergence [PAGE 17]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\hat R\ge1.1\), posterior summaries are not trusted; if a team has too few lineups, team-specific coefficients are weakly identified; the model is not a player-specific compatibility decomposition, which the paper explicitly names as future work [PAGE 22]. The individual PPP validation is
\[
\mathrm{PPP}=\frac{\mathrm{PTS}}{\mathrm{FGA}+0.44\times\mathrm{FTA}+\mathrm{TO}},
\]
where TO is turnovers [PAGE 28]. This is points per possession, not SI.

The “clock” trap is temporal: shooting style is from 2015-16, role data span 2015-16 to 2022-23, and lineup windows differ [PAGE 4]. A reviewer must also anticipate team-style confounds: SAS lineups are underpredicted despite high true OFFRTG, GSW has the largest NLL, and DEN is poorly predicted because the role clusters miss Jokic’s hybrid post-up/passing style [PAGE 15, PAGE 19]. The correct theory of mind is therefore: do not read cluster labels as causal identities; read them as measurement devices whose validity is conditional on the data window, clustering method, and validation checks. The authors themselves anticipate this by comparing against Kalman & Bosch, reporting predictive metrics, examining uncertainty, and taking medians across team-specific estimates [PAGE 10-11, PAGE 15].
