<THINK_TRACE>
The source text must be read as a proof architecture, not merely as an estimator comparison. The central multi-hop chain is: (i) the Bradley-Terry-Luce model assigns each item a scalar latent quality parameter and maps parameter differences to win probabilities through a logistic link [PAGE 2 of 47]; (ii) the maximum-likelihood estimator is defined as a constrained convex optimization over a box-constrained, centered parameter space [PAGE 2 of 47]; (iii) the box constraint is not innocuous: it creates a “clipping” effect at the boundary, causing systematic under-estimation for items near +B and over-estimation for items near −B [PAGE 3 of 47]; (iv) the unconstrained estimator removes the box constraint but introduces a different pathology: with positive probability an item may dominate all comparisons, producing an infinite estimate and unbounded mean squared error [PAGE 4 of 47]; (v) the proposed stretched estimator replaces B by A > B, thereby reducing the frequency and severity of boundary clipping while preserving enough boundedness to avoid the unconstrained estimator’s divergence [PAGE 4 of 47]. The theoretical bridge between these observations is the pair of bias theorems: the standard MLE has bias at least on the order of 1/sqrt(dk), whereas the stretched MLE has bias at most on the order of (log d + log k)/(dk), under sufficiently large d and k [PAGE 6 of 47]. The accuracy theorem then shows that the stretched estimator remains minimax-optimal for mean squared error at rate 1/k [PAGE 8 of 47].

A gray-zone audit reveals several delicate points. First, the improvement is asymptotic in both d and k: the theorem explicitly requires “for all d ≥ d0 and all k ≥ k0” where d0 and k0 depend on B, and in the stretched case on A and B [PAGE 6 of 47]. Therefore, finite-sample regimes with small d or small k are not covered by the worst-case guarantee. Second, the proof of the stretched-MLE bias upper bound proceeds indirectly through the unconstrained MLE. The authors first prove a high-probability ℓ∞ concentration bound for the unconstrained estimator, then take expectations using a second-order mean value theorem, and finally show that, with high probability, the unconstrained estimator does not violate the stretched box constraint and therefore coincides with the stretched MLE [PAGE 18 of 47][PAGE 21 of 47]. This means the result is not a simple deterministic inequality; it depends on a high-probability event E0 whose complement contributes a remainder of order 1/(dk) [PAGE 21 of 47]. Third, the lower bound for the standard MLE is obtained by reducing the general d-item problem to a two-item core problem using an oracle construction [PAGE 24 of 47][PAGE 27 of 47]. The oracle reduction is valid only because the authors construct a specific worst-case parameter vector and then show that the oracle and actual MLE differ by a smaller-order term [PAGE 29 of 47][PAGE 30 of 47]. Fourth, the paper’s sparse-observation discussion is explicitly not a full theorem: it is presented as an open problem, with conjectured dependence on the observation probability p_obs [PAGE 11 of 47]. Fifth, the estimator requires knowledge or choice of A > B; the paper notes that B is often unknown in practice and recommends estimating B leniently because an underestimate can cause significant bias [PAGE 11 of 47]. These gray zones do not invalidate the formal results inside their stated assumptions, but they sharply limit extrapolation to small samples, dependent comparisons, non-stationary items, or severe observation sparsity.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal objects in the source are dimensionless statistical operators, not physical SI quantities. The parameter vector θ* ∈ R^d represents latent quality on a logistic scale. The logistic win probability is

\[
\mu^*_{ij} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\theta^*_i - \theta^*_j)}}
\]

which is a probability in [0,1] and is invariant to adding a constant to all θ_i because only differences appear [PAGE 2 of 47]. The admissible parameter set is

\[
\Theta_B = \left\{ \theta \in \mathbb{R}^d : \|\theta\|_\infty \le B,\ \sum_{i=1}^d \theta_i = 0 \right\}
\]

where B > 0 is a finite constant [PAGE 2 of 47]. The ∞-norm constraint is the box constraint; the sum-to-zero constraint is a centering or gauge-fixing constraint. The source states: “A box constraint is necessary, because otherwise the estimation error can diverge to infinity” [PAGE 2 of 47]. The unit of θ is a logit, i.e. a dimensionless log-odds. Consequently, bias β has logit units, while mean squared error α has squared-logit units.

For k comparisons per pair, let W_ij be the number of times item i beats item j. The negative log-likelihood is

\[
\ell(\theta)
=
-\sum_{1 \le i < j \le d}
\left[
W_{ij}\log\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\theta_i-\theta_j)}}\right)
+
W_{ji}\log\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\theta_j-\theta_i)}}\right)
\right]
\]

[PAGE 2 of 47]. If natural logarithms are used, ℓ is measured in nats; if another logarithm base is used, it is measured in corresponding information units, but the argmin is unchanged by a positive multiplicative constant. Equivalently, using normalized empirical win fractions μ_ij = W_ij/k, the likelihood can be written as

\[
\ell(\theta)
=
k \sum_{1 \le i < j \le d}
\left[
\log(e^{\theta_i} + e^{\theta_j}) - \mu_{ij}\theta_i - \mu_{ji}\theta_j
\right]
\]

[PAGE 15 of 47]. The gradient operator used in the proof is

\[
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_m}
=
k \sum_{i \ne m}
\left(
\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\theta_m-\theta_i)}} - \mu_{mi}
\right)
\]

[PAGE 15 of 47].

The standard MLE is

\[
\hat{\theta}^{(B)}
=
\arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta_B} \ell(\theta)
\]

[PAGE 2 of 47]. The unconstrained MLE is

\[
\hat{\theta}^{(\infty)}
=
\arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta_\infty} \ell(\theta),
\qquad
\Theta_\infty = \left\{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d : \sum_{i=1}^d \theta_i = 0\right\}
\]

[PAGE 4 of 47]. The stretched MLE is

\[
\hat{\theta}^{(A)}
=
\arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta_A} \ell(\theta),
\qquad
\Theta_A = \left\{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d : \|\theta\|_\infty \le A,\ \sum_{i=1}^d \theta_i = 0\right\},
\qquad A > B
\]

[PAGE 4 of 47].

The two evaluation operators are

\[
\alpha(\hat{\theta})
=
\sup_{\theta^* \in \Theta_B}
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta} - \theta^*\|_2^2\right]
\]

for accuracy, and

\[
\beta(\hat{\theta})
=
\sup_{\theta^* \in \Theta_B}
\left\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta^*\right\|_\infty
\]

for worst-case bias [PAGE 2 of 47][PAGE 3 of 47]. The bias operator is worst-case across items because fairness is interpreted as avoiding systematic over- or under-estimation of any item.

The main formal rates are:

\[
\beta(\hat{\theta}^{(B)}) \ge \frac{c}{\sqrt{dk}}
\]

for constants depending only on B and sufficiently large d,k [PAGE 6 of 47]; and

\[
\beta(\hat{\theta}^{(A)}) \le c \frac{\log d + \log k}{dk}
\]

for any finite A > B and sufficiently large d,k [PAGE 6 of 47]. The corresponding accuracy bounds are

\[
\alpha(\hat{\theta}) \ge \frac{c}{k}
\]

for any estimator, and

\[
\alpha(\hat{\theta}^{(A)}) \le \frac{c}{k}
\]

for the stretched MLE [PAGE 8 of 47]. Thus the stretched estimator improves the bias exponent from d^{-1/2}k^{-1/2} to approximately d^{-1}k^{-1} up to logarithmic factors, while preserving the minimax mean-squared-error exponent k^{-1}.

The proof introduces several auxiliary operators. The event E0 is the intersection of high-probability events ensuring finite unconstrained MLE existence and concentration of empirical win fractions; it satisfies

\[
\mathbb{P}(E_0) \ge 1 - \frac{c}{dk}
\]

[PAGE 18 of 47]. On E0, the unconstrained estimation error obeys an ℓ∞ deterministic bound:

\[
|\hat{\theta}^{(\infty)}_m - \theta^*_m|
\lesssim
\sqrt{\frac{\log d + \log k}{dk}}
\]

for every coordinate m [PAGE 20 of 47]. The proof then defines

\[
\Delta_m = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{(\infty)}_m \mid E_0] - \theta^*_m
\]

and proves

\[
|\Delta_m|
\lesssim
\frac{\log d + \log k}{dk}
\]

[PAGE 21 of 47]. Finally, on E0, for sufficiently large d and k,

\[
\|\hat{\theta}^{(\infty)} - \theta^*\|_\infty < A - B
\]

so that

\[
\|\hat{\theta}^{(\infty)}\|_\infty < A
\]

and therefore

\[
\hat{\theta}^{(A)} = \hat{\theta}^{(\infty)}
\]

conditioned on E0 [PAGE 21 of 47]. This identity is the key bridge from unconstrained concentration to stretched-MLE bias control.

Breakdown regimes are explicit. If the comparison graph is not strongly connected, a finite unconstrained MLE may fail to exist [PAGE 33 of 47]. If an item beats all others, the unconstrained MLE estimates that item’s parameter as infinite, causing unbounded mean squared error [PAGE 4 of 47]. If d or k are below the implicit thresholds d0,k0, the theorem’s constants and asymptotics do not apply [PAGE 6 of 47]. If A is chosen too close to B, the bias improvement is weak; if A is chosen too large, the estimator behaves more like the unconstrained MLE and may lose robustness, although the paper reports that mean squared error is relatively insensitive to A once A ≥ 1 in the simulations [PAGE 9 of 47][PAGE 10 of 47]. In sparse random-design settings, the source conjectures similar rates but does not prove them, and disconnected graphs make the problem non-identifiable [PAGE 11 of 47].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the source is a convergence of three older traditions: paired-comparison modeling, minimax estimation from partial rankings, and bias correction in likelihood theory. The BTL model itself is the classical paired-comparison operator: “The BTL model assumes that each item is associated to an unknown real-valued parameter representing the quality of that item, and assumes that the probability of an item beating another is the logistic function applied to the difference of the parameters of these two items” [PAGE 1 of 47]. This places the paper in the lineage of Bradley-Terry-Luce models, Elo-style rating systems, peer grading, crowdsourcing, and AI bot tournaments [PAGE 1 of 47].

A second lineage is the minimax accuracy tradition. The source repeatedly notes that past work established the standard MLE as minimax-optimal in mean squared error [PAGE 2 of 47]. The stretched estimator’s accuracy theorem is not proved from scratch; it follows by modifying the earlier minimax proof: “The proof of Theorem 2.2 closely mimics the proof of Theorem 2(b) from [31], replacing the steps involving the domain Θ_B by the stretched domain Θ_A” [PAGE 8 of 47]. The appendix makes the same point explicitly: the only place where the box constraint is used in the earlier proof is a curvature bound, and that bound continues to hold when Θ_B is replaced by Θ_A, with constants allowed to depend on A and B [PAGE 47 of 47]. Thus the historical minimax result acts as a preservation theorem: changing the feasible box from B to A does not destroy the optimal mean-squared-error rate.

A third lineage is the bias-correction literature. The source contrasts its approach with finite-sample corrections such as Jackknife and other Taylor-expansion methods. It says those methods “operate in a low-dimensional regime (small d) where the MLE is asymptotically unbiased” and produce bias expansions of the form f1(θ*)/n + f2(θ*)/n^2 + ... [PAGE 5 of 47]. The source’s objection is that eliminating the first term does not guarantee a low bias rate, and because the functions f_i are implicit in θ*, such corrections do not directly yield explicit worst-case guarantees [PAGE 5 of 47]. This explains why the paper chooses a structural modification of the constraint set rather than a post-hoc correction.

The paper also positions itself against two possible historical misreadings. First, it distinguishes its result from high-dimensional logistic regression bias results, where unconstrained MLE can show the opposite direction of bias: “the paper [37] shows that the large positive coefficients are overestimated, and the large negative coefficients are underestimated” [PAGE 5 of 47]. In the constrained BTL setting, the standard MLE underestimates large positive parameters and overestimates large negative parameters because of clipping. Second, it distinguishes its result from regression toward the mean. Regression toward the mean concerns repeated measurements of random variables and order statistics, whereas the BTL parameters are fixed item indices: “we consider items whose indices are fixed (and are not order statistics)” [PAGE 5 of 47]. The source concludes that for fixed indices, the bias direction is opposite to regression toward the mean: under-estimation of large true values rather than over-estimation of large observed values [PAGE 5 of 47].

Multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. The first trap is to assume that asymptotic unbiasedness of MLE in classical logistic regression transfers to finite-sample BTL rating. The source explicitly operates in a finite-sample regime where the MLE is still consistent but biased [PAGE 5 of 47]. The second trap is to treat regularization bounds as applying to unregularized MLE. The source notes that the ℓ∞ bound for regularized MLE does not carry over because the prior proof relies on strong convexity of the regularizer; the paper instead provides an ℓ∞ bound for the unregularized MLE in the complete-graph regime [PAGE 5 of 47]. The third trap is to ignore the boundary. The source’s central empirical observation is that bias is largest near ±B: “the bias is the largest when the true values of the parameters are near the boundaries ±B” [PAGE 3 of 47]. The fourth trap is to treat the box constraint as a harmless numerical guard. The source proves it is a bias-generating mechanism, not merely a computational convenience. The fifth trap is to over-extrapolate to sparse graphs. The source leaves random-design sparse observations as an open problem and only provides simulations [PAGE 11 of 47].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The estimator can be interpreted as an agent facing a constrained decision problem under noisy comparisons. The “crisis” is small k or extreme θ*: the empirical win fraction μ is noisy, and the true parameter lies near the boundary of the feasible set. The standard MLE’s “habit regression” is to return a safe interior or boundary value that satisfies the known box constraint. This habit reduces catastrophic unboundedness but creates a systematic regression toward the center of the feasible interval. In the two-item intuition, the standard MLE clips at B: “the box constraint requires that the estimate shall never exceed B. We call this phenomenon the ‘clipping’ effect, which introduces a negative bias” [PAGE 7 of 47]. The habit is rational under misspecification risk or unboundedness risk, but it is biased.

The stretched-MLE is a counter-trap. It does not abandon boundedness, because the unconstrained estimator can diverge when an item wins all comparisons [PAGE 4 of 47]. Instead, it enlarges the safe set by a constant A > B. This allows the estimator to express stronger evidence for extreme items without immediately saturating at B. In the same two-item picture, the unconstrained estimator is convex in μ on (0.5,1), so Jensen’s inequality creates a positive bias; the standard MLE creates a negative clipping bias; the stretched estimator “balances out the negative bias from the ‘clipping’ effect and the positive bias from the convexity of the estimator function” [PAGE 7 of 47][PAGE 8 of 47]. The counter-trap is therefore not “remove all constraints,” but “choose a less brittle constraint.”

The “clock” is the pair of asymptotic scales d and k. For the standard MLE, the bias clock ticks at 1/sqrt(dk), because the worst-case boundary item suffers from fluctuations of order 1/sqrt(k) aggregated over d−1 opponents, yielding the d and k dependence in the lower bound [PAGE 6 of 47][PAGE 27 of 47]. For the stretched MLE, the bias clock ticks faster, at (log d + log k)/(dk), because the estimator’s expectation is controlled by a second-order expansion and the residual conditioning event has probability deficit O(1/(dk)) [PAGE 20 of 47][PAGE 21 of 47]. The accuracy clock is different: the mean-squared-error rate is 1/k, because each item is compared indirectly through d−1 opponents but the minimax topology-dependent bound for the complete graph yields a k-only leading exponent after d-dependent constants are absorbed [PAGE 8 of 47][PAGE 47 of 47].

The proof itself exhibits recursive theory of mind. The authors must reason about how the estimator behaves not only in expectation, but also on high-probability events and on conditional events. They first condition on E0, where concentration and finite existence hold [PAGE 18 of 47]. Then, in the lower-bound proof, they condition further on events E_v where the fraction of wins by item 1 takes a fixed value v. This pointwise conditioning is necessary because two unconstrained estimators being close in expectation does not guarantee that their constrained counterparts are close in expectation; the box constraint can act differently on different sample paths [PAGE 29 of 47]. Thus the proof anticipates the estimator’s pathwise behavior, not merely its average behavior.

The recursive evaluator also matters. Fairness is modeled as worst-case bias over all θ* in Θ_B [PAGE 3 of 47]. This is a stronger requirement than average-case unbiasedness. The standard MLE fails this requirement because it systematically underestimates top items and overestimates bottom items [PAGE 3 of 47]. The stretched MLE improves the worst-case bias operator while retaining the accuracy operator. In a multi-agent rating ecosystem, this changes incentives: top items are less penalized by boundary clipping, while bottom items are less artificially lifted by the same clipping mechanism. The estimator is therefore not only statistically better; it is institutionally more even-handed under the paper’s definition of fairness.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source’s guarantees are conditional on a formal data-generating layer. The BTL layer assumes independent Bernoulli comparisons with probabilities determined solely by fixed latent parameters: “When any item i is compared with any item j in the BTL model, the item i beats item j with probability ... independent of all other comparisons” [PAGE 2 of 47]. The proof layer further assumes a complete league format where every pair is compared k times [PAGE 2 of 47], and the probabilistic lemmas rely on independence or bounded-difference inequalities [PAGE 16 of 47][PAGE 34 of 47]. A cross-layer physical, biomechanical, or weather veto applies when the actual outcome-generating process violates these assumptions.

A formal veto can be stated as follows. Suppose the true outcome process is not

\[
X^{(r)}_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\mu^*_{ij})
\]

with fixed μ*_{ij}, but instead

\[
X^{(r)}_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p^{(r)}_{ij}),
\]

where p^{(r)}_{ij} varies with time, fatigue, weather, venue, patch version, or biomechanical state. If the variation is independent of item identity and has zero mean on the logistic scale, it may inflate variance and reduce effective sample size. But if the variation is correlated with item identity, then the observed win probability cannot be represented by a single fixed θ*_i - θ*_j. In that case, the parameter estimated by the MLE is a pseudo-parameter, not the true quality parameter. The bias guarantees, which are worst-case over θ* in the correctly specified BTL class, no longer apply because the model class itself is misspecified.

A simple veto proof by contradiction is possible. Assume the source’s theorem applies to an observed tournament. The theorem requires that there exists θ* ∈ Θ_B such that all pairwise win probabilities equal μ*_{ij} = 1/(1+exp(-(θ*_i - θ*_j))). Now suppose weather or fatigue introduces an item-specific additive term η_i(t) that is not constant over time. If two item pairs have identical θ differences but different weather exposure, their observed win probabilities differ. Therefore no single θ* can simultaneously satisfy the BTL equations for all pairs and times. Hence the assumption of the theorem is false. If the assumption is false, the conclusion β(θ̂) ≤ c(log d + log k)/(dk) cannot be asserted as a bias bound with respect to the true latent quality.

The breakdown regime can be made explicit. Let the effective comparison outcome be governed by a time-varying logit gap

\[
\Delta_{ij}(t)
=
\theta_i - \theta_j + \eta_i(t) - \eta_j(t) + \xi_{ij}(t),
\]

where η represents persistent state effects and ξ represents transient environmental shocks. The BTL proof requires η and ξ to be absent or absorbed into independent noise with fixed mean. If the supremum of the uncontrolled state term satisfies

\[
\sup_t \|\eta(t)\|_\infty \gtrsim A - B,
\]

then even the stretched estimator’s high-probability equivalence to the unconstrained estimator can fail, because the effective parameter may leave the stretched feasible set. If the environmental shock induces dependence across comparisons, then the bounded-difference proof of concentration fails: McDiarmid-type inequalities require independent or appropriately bounded coordinates, and the source’s proof uses exactly such independence or bounded-change structure [PAGE 34 of 47]. If the comparison graph becomes effectively disconnected because weather cancels matches or bots cannot play, then finite MLE existence can fail, since strong connectivity of the comparison graph is required [PAGE 33 of 47].

For sports or embodied AI competitions, the veto is practical: injuries, fatigue, weather, map selection, and patch changes are not mere noise if they correlate with teams or bots. For peer grading or crowdsourcing, the analogous veto is rater mood, rubric drift, or fatigue. In all such cases, the source’s mathematical result remains true inside its assumptions, but the application layer vetoes the inference from estimated θ to true quality. The correct adjudication is therefore: no veto of the theorem within the BTL model; a decisive veto of any claim that the theorem’s bias reduction automatically holds in non-stationary, dependent, or physically confounded comparison environments.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. In each case, the veto is empirical or formal: if the stated observation occurs, the claimed guarantee is not supported in that regime.

1. Small-k clipping veto.
Scenario: Fix d and set k very small, especially k = 1, with a worst-case parameter vector θ* = [B, -B/(d-1), ..., -B/(d-1)]. The standard MLE is heavily clipped at B, but the stretched MLE may still suffer high variance because there is insufficient evidence to justify extreme parameter values.
Verifiable prediction: For k below the implicit threshold k0(A,B), the empirical bias of the stretched MLE need not obey the asymptotic rate (log d + log k)/(dk). In finite simulations, one may observe that the stretched MLE’s bias is not monotonically smaller than the standard MLE’s bias for all A, particularly when A is too large and the estimator overfits rare undefeated or winless items. The source’s own simulations show that the optimal A depends on k and that smaller k can require different tuning [PAGE 9 of 47][PAGE 10 of 47].
Veto condition: If repeated experiments show

\[
\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{(A)}] - \theta^*\|_\infty
\ge
\|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{(B)}] - \theta^*\|_\infty
\]

for the intended A and small k, then the bias-reduction claim is vetoed in that finite-sample regime.

2. Underestimated-B veto.
Scenario: The practitioner does not know B and chooses a stretch parameter A based on an underestimated B_hat < B_true. If some item truly has θ*_i near B_true but A is less than or close to B_true, the estimator still clips that item.
Verifiable prediction: The bias for the maximum-magnitude item remains negative and of order approximately 1/sqrt(dk), not the improved stretched rate. The source warns that B is often unknown and that “an estimation smaller than or equal to the true B causes significant bias” [PAGE 11 of 47].
Veto condition: If, in a controlled simulation where θ* contains an item equal to B_true and the estimator uses A < B_true, the maximum item bias does not improve relative to the standard MLE, then the practical fairness claim is vetoed for that choice of A. This veto is verifiable by checking whether the empirical bias at the extreme coordinate tracks the clipped estimator rather than the stretched one.

3. Sparse or disconnected comparison veto.
Scenario: Comparisons are observed randomly with probability p_obs, and p_obs is too low to ensure a connected or strongly connected comparison graph. Some items may have no wins, no losses, or no path of comparison outcomes linking them to the rest of the graph.
Verifiable prediction: The unconstrained MLE may fail to exist, and the stretched MLE may become unstable or non-unique if the graph is disconnected. The source explicitly says an iteration is discarded if the graph is not connected because “the problem is not identifiable under such a graph” [PAGE 11 of 47]. The formal existence lemma also depends on strong connectivity of the comparison graph [PAGE 33 of 47].
Veto condition: If a dataset or simulation produces a disconnected comparison graph and the estimator returns finite but arbitrary values without regularization or additional constraints, then the bias and accuracy guarantees are vetoed because the identifiability assumption fails. The correct prediction is that no reliable bias-rate claim can be made until the comparison graph is connected or strongly connected with high probability.

These vetoes do not contradict the source’s theorems inside their assumptions. They identify the exact counterfactual boundaries where the theorems cease to be applicable: insufficient k, misspecified or underestimated B, and broken comparison-graph connectivity.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
