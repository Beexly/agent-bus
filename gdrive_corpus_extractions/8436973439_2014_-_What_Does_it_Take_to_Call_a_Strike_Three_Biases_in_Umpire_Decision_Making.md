The source builds a two-stage argument. First, it estimates a location-only strike probability surface \(p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})\) using a bivariate kernel regression with a Gaussian kernel and Silverman bandwidth, then tests whether residuals from that surface covary with at-bat states. The quoted design is explicit: “We call an umpire inconsistent when he makes different calls on pitches that cross the plane at the same location; we call him biased when these differences correlate with extraneous (non-location) factors” [PAGE 2]. This distinction is important: the paper is not claiming that umpires are physically unable to see pitch location. It is claiming that conditional on location, the probability of a strike call changes with count and prior-call states.

The semi-parametric model is \(y_{iuh}=p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})+\omega_i x_i^T\beta+\varepsilon_i\), where \(y_{iuh}\in\{0,1\}\) is a strike-call indicator, \(p_{uh}\) is a baseline probability in \([0,1]\), \(x_i\) contains state indicators, \(\beta\) is in probability points when \(\omega_i=1\), and \(\varepsilon_i\) is IID mean-zero [PAGE 6]. The weighting operator is \(\omega_i=1-2|p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})-0.5|\). Thus \(\omega_i=0\) for certain balls or strikes and \(\omega_i=1\) at baseline probability \(0.5\) [PAGE 6]. This is a deliberate identification device: the state coefficients are defined at the gray zone where location does not determine the call. It also means the model is silent about state effects at obvious pitches; the source says the probability “does not change either in the center of the strike zone or on the periphery” [PAGE 2]. The gray-zone audit must therefore focus on the 10% to 90% transition ring, which the source says is “a foot wide” [PAGE 2].

The structural stage converts call behavior into state-specific strike thresholds. The source assumes “a utility of 1 for making a correct call, a disutility of \(\gamma_{s,u}\) for calling a strike incorrectly, and a disutility of \(\gamma_{b,u}\) for calling a ball incorrectly” [PAGE 7]. If \(p\) is the known baseline probability that a strike is correct, expected utility for calling strike is \(p-(1-p)\gamma_{s,u}\), and for calling ball is \((1-p)-p\gamma_{b,u}\). Equating these gives \(p_j=(1+\gamma_{s,u})/(2+\gamma_{s,u}+\gamma_{b,u})\). This reproduces the source’s sign restrictions: if \(\gamma_s=\gamma_b\), \(p_j=0.5\); if \(\gamma_s \lt \gamma_b\), \(p_j \lt 0.5\), a strike bias; if \(\gamma_s \gt \gamma_b\), \(p_j \gt 0.5\), a ball bias [PAGE 7]. The average umpire needing “64% sure of a strike in order to call strike three half the time” [PAGE 1] is therefore a statement about asymmetric state-specific disutilities, not about visual acuity.

The main gray-zone vulnerabilities are stated by the authors themselves. The nonparametric surface assumes smoothness; the source warns: “The smoothing nature of the estimator may obscure a sharper boundary, though the bandwidth is small enough to minimize this concern” [PAGE 2]. The state design assumes “invariant strike zones across umpires, invariance in each umpire’s strike zone between left and right-handed hitters, and zero correlation between states” [PAGE 5]. The zero-correlation assumption is immediately qualified: “This is obviously false for 3-ball counts which can only be first achieved when the last pitch was a ball” [PAGE 5]. Causal identification also “rests on an assumption of exogeneity with respect to omitted states” [PAGE 6]. Finally, the paper’s own robustness check says “the apparent effect of a ball on the previous pitch is spurious but that sizable biases manifest in the other three states” [PAGE 2]. A rigorous audit should therefore treat the last-ball effect as a rejected candidate, not as evidence, and should treat the 2-strike, 3-ball, and prior-called-strike states as the surviving set.

The time-pressure test is the paper’s adjudication between deliberative and intuitive mechanisms. The source says: “If under time pressure the bias weakens, then the bias is likely deliberative... If under time pressure the bias strengthens, then the bias is likely intuitive” [PAGE 3]. The observed result is that “time pressure accentuates the 2-strike bias” and “the graph depicts a deeper moat” [PAGE 4]. That supports the intuitive interpretation for the 2-strike state. The audit caveat is that time pressure is not randomized; it is inferred from a game situation—3-ball counts with a runner on first, excluding 2-strike and 2-out calls [PAGE 3]. The footnote adds: “Since time pressure implies a 3-ball count, we cannot observe the difference in the strike zone under time pressure between counts with 3 balls and counts with fewer than 3 balls” [PAGE 4]. Thus the time-pressure comparison is within-count, not a clean experimental contrast across all counts.

The semi-parametric equation is
\[

\]
with \(y_{iuh}\in\{0,1\}\), \(p_{uh}\in[0,1]\), \(x_i\) a \(k\times 1\) state-indicator vector, \(\beta\) a \(k\times 1\) coefficient vector, and \(\varepsilon_i\) IID mean zero [PAGE 6]. The source defines
\[
\omega_i=1-2\left|p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})-0.5\right|.
\]
The units are dimensionless probabilities for \(p\) and \(\omega\); \(\beta_j\) is a percentage-point change in strike-call probability at baseline \(p=0.5\) [PAGE 6]. The source reports the 2-strike and 3-ball effects in probability language: “In 3-ball counts, borderline calls are called strikes 58% of the time; in 2-strike counts, borderline calls are called strikes only 31% of the time” [PAGE 6]. It also reports a full-count moderation: “the probability of a strike decreases by about 13 percentage points \((0.081-0.19-0.025)\)” [PAGE 6]. The prior-ball effect is near zero with a 95% interval \((-0.001,0.011)\), while a prior called strike lowers the borderline strike probability by 17 percentage points [PAGE 6].

The structural utility primitives are
\[
U_u(\text{strike})=p-(1-p)\gamma_{s,u}+\nu_s,\qquad
U_u(\text{ball})=(1-p)-p\gamma_{b,u}+\nu_b,
\]
where \(p\) is the known probability that a strike is correct, \(\gamma_{s,u}\) is the disutility of incorrectly calling a strike, and \(\gamma_{b,u}\) is the disutility of incorrectly calling a ball [PAGE 7]. With \(\nu\) IID type I extreme value, the call probability is the logistic transformation of \(U_u(\text{strike})-U_u(\text{ball})\) [PAGE 7]. The threshold solves \(E[U_u(\text{strike})=U_u(\text{ball})]\), giving
\[
p_j=\frac{1+\gamma_{s,u}}{2+\gamma_{s,u}+\gamma_{b,u}}.
\]
This equation has the source’s stated sign regime: \(\gamma_s=\gamma_b\) gives \(p_j=0.5\); \(\gamma_s \lt \gamma_b\) gives \(p_j \lt 0.5\) and a strike bias; \(\gamma_s \gt \gamma_b\) gives \(p_j \gt 0.5\) and a ball bias [PAGE 7]. Under nonnegative disutilities the denominator is at least 2, so the expression is finite. A formal singularity appears only if one permits the utility scale to degenerate at \(\gamma_s+\gamma_b=-2\); that is outside the paper’s disutility interpretation. The more relevant identification singularity is the weight regime: “For certain balls or strikes \((p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})\in\{0,1\})\), \(\omega_i=0\); for locations in which balls and strikes are equally probable \((p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})=0.5)\), \(\omega_i=1\)” [PAGE 6]. At \(\omega_i=0\), state effects are not identified because the location surface already pins the call.

Breakdown regimes include omitted-state correlation, violation of the per-handedness invariance assumption, and non-IID or non-extreme-value errors. The source explicitly warns that “causal identification rests on an assumption of exogeneity with respect to omitted states” [PAGE 6]. It also states that the initial assumptions include “invariant strike zones across umpires, invariance in each umpire’s strike zone between left and right-handed hitters, and zero correlation between states” [PAGE 5]. The last assumption is false for 3-ball counts [PAGE 5]. Finally, the paper reports that the prior-ball effect is spurious [PAGE 2], so any model that treats that coefficient as causal breaks the paper’s own robustness verdict.

No information-unit conversion is reported in the source. The paper’s quantities are probabilities, percentage points, call counts, utility units, and a binary time-pressure indicator. The reported length is “a foot wide” for the 10% to 90% ring [PAGE 2]; the source does not report seconds, bits, or joule-scale quantities.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The “retrocausal” structure here is not literal backward causation. It is decision-theoretic feedback: a present call is conditioned on the anticipated future consequence of that call. The source states that “umpires are influenced by what they have called previously and how their current decision bears on the outcome of the at-bat” [PAGE 1]. That is a genealogy in which the future state—third strike, fourth ball, at-bat termination—shapes the present threshold. The 2-strike contraction means the umpire behaves as if the anticipated cost of calling strike three is higher than the cost of calling ball three; the 3-ball expansion means the anticipated cost of ball four changes the threshold in the opposite direction.

The scientific lineage is behavioral and reference-dependent. The paper cites behavioral economics [4], prospect theory [6], loss aversion [5], and prior sports studies on losing leads, par putts, and round-number goals [1]-[3]. The mechanism is not a generic “pressure” story; it is state-dependent asymmetric loss. The structural model formalizes this by allowing \(\gamma_{s,u}\) and \(\gamma_{b,u}\) to vary by state and to differ from each other [PAGE 7]. That is a direct descendant of prospect-theoretic reference dependence: the count is the reference state, and miscalling a strike versus miscalling a ball has different psychological cost depending on that reference.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are mostly data-construction traps. First, pooling 2009-2011 and over one million calls is powerful, but the source restricts to “umpires with at least 7500 calls,” leaving “75 umpires and 1.03M calls” [PAGE 2]. That selection rule changes the inferential population: conclusions apply to high-volume umpires, not necessarily all umpires. Second, the states are correlated. A 3-ball count can only be first reached after a prior ball, so a naive decomposition of “3-ball effect” and “prior-ball effect” can double-count the same path [PAGE 5]. Third, the prior-ball effect is spurious under robustness [PAGE 2], which is a warning against treating every visible “moat” or “mountain” as causal. Fourth, time pressure is a small and nonrandom subset: “For about 2% of calls, the umpire must make his call immediately” [PAGE 3]. A multi-week sample can therefore contain enough calls to estimate the average state effect while leaving the time-pressure contrast vulnerable to game-situation selection. The correct genealogy keeps the surviving states—2 strikes, 3 balls, prior called strike—and demotes the prior-ball state to a rejected hypothesis.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The umpire is not only classifying a pitch; he is producing a public signal that another agent will act on. The source describes the time-pressure case: the call “tells the catcher whether to attempt a play on a potential baserunner” [PAGE 3]. The footnote explains that with 3 balls and a walk advancing runners, “the call tells the catcher how he should address a potential steal” [PAGE 3]. This is a two-agent recursion: the umpire models the catcher’s contingent action, and the catcher models the umpire’s call threshold. The batter’s presence is implicit in the count and handedness, but the source’s explicit recursive loop is umpire-to-catcher-to-baserunner.

“Crisis habit regression” is the reversion to an intuitive prior under clock pressure. The source’s test is explicit: if time pressure weakens bias, the bias is deliberative; if it strengthens bias, the bias is intuitive [PAGE 3]. The observed result is “time pressure accentuates the 2-strike bias” and “the graph depicts a deeper moat” [PAGE 4]. That is habit regression in the precise sense that the 2-strike avoidance habit gets stronger when the umpire has less time to deliberate. The counter-trap would be the opposite: an umpire who consciously tries to avoid a 2-strike bias should show a weaker contraction under time pressure. The data reject that counter-trap for the 2-strike state. The clock variable is not a general seconds measure; it is a game-state indicator for immediate-call situations [PAGE 3], and the source cannot compare time pressure across 3-ball and fewer-than-3-ball counts because time pressure implies a 3-ball count [PAGE 4].

The recursive model also predicts heterogeneity in where the bias appears. Obvious balls and strikes should be immune because the catcher’s action is already determined; borderline pitches should carry the state signal. The source is consistent: the probability “does not change either in the center of the strike zone or on the periphery” [PAGE 2], while the moat and mountain appear in the transition ring. The 3-ball-plus-prior-called-strike case is a useful counterexample: the source says “there is no apparent bias with or without time pressure” [PAGE 4]. That prevents an overgeneralized “all pressure makes all bias worse” theory. The recursive theory of mind must be state-specific: the umpire’s model of the catcher’s urgency interacts with the count-specific cost of a miscall.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical-layer veto would require showing that the state effects are artifacts of pitch measurement or pitch location. The source’s measurement layer is strong: “Stereoscopic cameras take a sequence of 60 images of each pitch from release until it crosses home plate” [PAGE 1], and the baseline surface is estimated from the crossing-plane coordinates. If a physical variable changes only the pitch’s location, then \(p_{uh}(z_{1i},z_{2i})\) absorbs it. The source’s strongest physical defense is that obvious pitches are unaffected: the probability “does not change either in the center of the strike zone or on the periphery” [PAGE 2]. That pattern is what a location-only physical miscalibration would not predict; a general measurement error should blur all regions, not selectively move the borderline ring.

A biomechanical-layer veto would require showing that batter handedness, catcher setup, or umpire positioning explains the state coefficients. The source addresses handedness partly by estimating the baseline for each \((u,h)\) tuple [PAGE 6], and it notes that “the strike zone shifts sideways based on the handedness of the hitter because the umpire positions himself differently behind the catcher” [PAGE 5]. The initial nonparametric figure pools across umpires, but the semi-parametric model is more granular. The remaining vulnerability is the stated assumption of “invariance in each umpire’s strike zone between left and right-handed hitters” [PAGE 5]. If that invariance fails in a way correlated with count, the state coefficients could be contaminated. The source does not provide a full biomechanical decomposition, so a biomechanical veto is not proved; it is only partially blocked by the \((u,h)\) baseline.

A weather-layer veto is the weakest supported of the three because the provided source pages do not report weather covariates. If weather affects pitch movement and therefore location, it is absorbed by \(p_{uh}\). If weather affects the umpire’s perception conditional on location and is correlated with count or prior-call state, it is an omitted state and the source’s exogeneity assumption fails. The paper itself states: “causal identification rests on an assumption of exogeneity with respect to omitted states” [PAGE 6]. That is the load-bearing sentence. The correct adjudication is therefore: the source establishes a location-conditional state association and argues for a behavioral mechanism, but it does not provide a weather or full biomechanical veto proof. A cross-layer veto would require external pitch-level weather and biomechanical controls; without them, the physical-layer defense is strong, the biomechanical defense is partial, and the weather defense is unverified.
