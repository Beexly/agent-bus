Let \(C_{ij}\) be the plasma concentration for player \(i\) at time \(j\), with \(C_{ij}\) in pg/mL for NFL and in ng/mL for S100β when reported in Figure 4 [PAGE 17]. The source’s log operator is not fully specified; define
\[
Y_{ij}=\log_b\!\left(\frac{C_{ij}}{C_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right),
\]
where \(Y_{ij}\) is dimensionless. If \(b=e\), slopes are in nats per assessment; if \(b=10\), slopes are in decades per assessment. The source does not state the base, so the slope unit is log-base-dependent. The operator is undefined at \(C_{ij}\le0\); assay floor, censoring, or missing values are breakdown regimes. The source labels the axis “Change in log NFL levels (pg/mL)” [PAGE 15], which is dimensionally inconsistent as written and should be treated as a transformed concentration, not a raw pg/mL change.

The primary statistical operator is a multilevel model for repeated measures [PAGE 8]:
\[
Y_{ij}=\beta_0+\beta_1 G_i+\beta_2 t_j+\beta_3 (G_i t_j)+u_i+\epsilon_{ij},
\]
where \(G_i\in\{0,1\}\) is lower/higher impact group, \(t_j\) is the linear time trend over baseline, pre-practice, and post-practice, \(u_i\sim N(0,\tau^2)\) is a player random intercept, and \(\epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\) is residual error. \(Y_{ij}\) is dimensionless; \(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3\) are dimensionless per time unit; \(\tau^2\) and \(\sigma^2\) are dimensionless variance components. The interaction \(\beta_3\) tests whether the NFL trend depends on impact group. The source reports the interaction as \(F(1,113)=3.97, p=0.04\), the high-group slope as \(0.25\) [SE \(0.06\)], \(p<0.0001\), and the low-group slope as \(0.04\) [SE \(0.09\)], \(p=0.61\) [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: \(\tau^2\to0\) or \(\sigma^2\to0\) gives singular/boundary variance estimates; missingness not at random breaks the model; unequal time spacing breaks a simple linear trend; and a post-hoc extreme-group split can inflate group contrasts.

For S100β, the same operator gives a group-by-trend interaction of \(F(1,99)=13.58, p=0.0004\) [PAGE 9], with high-group slope \(0.07\) [SE \(0.008\)], \(p<0.0001\), and low-group slope \(0.02\) [SE \(0.01\)], \(p=0.02\) [PAGE 9-10]. For tau, the interaction is \(F(1,113)=4.72, p=0.03\), but only the lower-impact group declined: \(B=2.16\) [SE \(0.29\)], \(p<0.0001\) [PAGE 10]. These are probability statements; \(p\in[0,1]\), not a ratio or an information unit.

The rank association operator is Spearman’s \(\rho_s\). With midranks \(R_X,R_Y\),
\[
\rho_s=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_X,R_Y)}{\sigma_{R_X}\sigma_{R_Y}},
\]
which is dimensionless. The source reports \(\rho_s=0.66, p<0.001\) for NFL change versus S100β change [PAGE 17] and \(\rho_s=0.38, p=0.03\) for S100β change versus NPC change [PAGE 10]. Breakdown regimes: small \(n\), ties, outliers, and monotone-but-nonlinear associations; \(\rho_s\) measures rank concordance, not causal direction or agreement. With only 24 players, the rank null distribution is coarse.

For biomechanical exposure, the paper uses number of hits, peak linear acceleration (PLA, in g; \(1\,\mathrm{g}=9.80665\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\)), peak rotational acceleration (PRA, in rad/s²), and HIC. A conventional HIC form is
\[
\mathrm{HIC}=\max_{t_1<t_2}\left[(t_2-t_1)\left(\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a(t)\,dt\right)^{2.5}\right],
\]
with \(a(t)\) in g and time in seconds; the paper treats HIC as a conventional dimensionless injury criterion. The formula is singular at \(t_2=t_1\). The sensor system uses “a triaxial accelerometer ... with 200g maximum per axis” and a “preset threshold for a peak linear acceleration (PLA) magnitude exceeded 10.0 g,” with low-pass filtering “at 180 and 40 Hz cutoff” and a “standard hit duration of 93.75 milliseconds” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes include clipping above 200 g, exclusion of sub-10 g hits, filter-induced attenuation, and mouthguard fit variability. The cumulative exposure variables are sums, not ratios: \(D_{\mathrm{PLA}}=\sum_k \mathrm{PLA}_k\) in g and \(D_{\mathrm{PRA}}=\sum_k \mathrm{PRA}_k\) in rad/s²; they are collinear with hit count, so frequency and magnitude cannot be cleanly separated without a pre-specified model.

Assay operators are also part of the source: NFL by “digital array technology (Simoa; Quanterix Corporation) using an in house kit”; S100β by “sandwich-based ELISA”; tau by Simoa commercial kit [PAGE 7]. Preanalytical breakdown includes hemolysis, delayed separation, freeze-thaw, and assay lot variability; these are not modeled in the reported equations.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the default inference: “no concussion symptoms means no injury.” The source directly undercuts that habit because “Participants sustained no concussions during the course of the study” [PAGE 5], yet high-impact players showed a significant NFL rise [PAGE 9]. A clinician who anchors on the symptom checklist can therefore miss a biological signal; an athlete who underreports symptoms can reinforce that anchor. The counter-trap is to use objective, time-resolved biomarkers plus kinematic exposure, but not to over-trust a single marker. The paper’s multiple-marker design—NFL, S100β, tau, NPC, symptoms—is the counter-trap: “Results from our studies highlight the need for assessing multiple biomarkers within an individual or within groups of individuals sustaining head impacts” [PAGE 11].

The clock is the decisive variable. NFL and S100β are framed as acute markers, while NPC is framed as more sensitive “over a longer duration” [PAGE 11]. A pre/post-practice sample captures acute flux; a symptom checklist captures current subjective state; NPC captures a functional ocular response with a different time constant. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that each observer has a different information set: the athlete knows symptoms but may have incentives to minimize them; the clinician sees the checklist and may treat it as ground truth; the researcher sees group-level slopes and may overgeneralize to individual diagnosis. The correct adjudication is to keep the clocks separate: an acute NFL–S100β concordance does not imply that NPC or symptoms should move at the same moment, and a null NPC/symptom correlation does not invalidate the acute plasma signal.

The “what does this mean?” answer is therefore layered. For acute subconcussive exposure, NFL and S100β appear to be coupled markers of mechanical strain and cellular release. For tau, the null NFL–tau correlation plus the lower-group tau decline [PAGE 10] argues against using tau as a simple acute dose meter in this cohort. For NPC and symptoms, the absence of change–change correlation with NFL argues against using them as acute proxies for plasma protein flux, even though high-impact players had higher group-level NPC and symptom values [PAGE 10]. This is a counter-trap against both nihilism (“no symptoms, no injury”) and overdiagnosis (“any NFL rise is a concussion”).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Weather is not adjudicable from this source. The paper reports no meteorological variable, no wet-bulb globe temperature, no field surface, and no hydration metric. The defensible cross-layer vetoes are sensor saturation, threshold exclusion, filter attenuation, group confounding, and plasma-versus-CSF specificity. The paper’s own conclusion is appropriately narrow: NFL and S100β are acute peripheral markers associated with impact exposure, not validated diagnostic tests.
