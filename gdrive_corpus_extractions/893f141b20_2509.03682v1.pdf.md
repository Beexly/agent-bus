<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer distinguishes three failure modes. Crisis habit regression occurs when a learned policy, under distribution shift, latency pressure, or opponent novelty, falls back to a scripted or low-entropy attractor. The source’s own critique of finite state machines and behavior trees—repetitive behaviors and game bugs [PAGE 2]—describes the attractor. In commercial settings, the industry preference for “handcrafted, predictable, and manageable AI behaviors” [PAGE 18] makes this regression especially likely.

Counter-trap occurs when an opponent models the habit and exploits it. AlphaStar’s league training is the clearest source example: “Main exploiters are specifically trained to identify and exploit weaknesses in the main agents to improve their adaptability and resilience,” and “League exploiters, on the other hand, target weaknesses in the overall league” [PAGE 15-16]. This is recursive: the main agent models the opponent, the exploiter models the main agent’s model, and the league exploiter models the population’s shared weaknesses. A fixed crisis habit is therefore not merely suboptimal; it is a target. The source’s nonstationarity claim supports the same point: multiple agents make the environment nonstationary [PAGE 6], so any policy that treats the opponent as stationary is vulnerable.

Clock is the real-time deadline that constrains recursive reasoning. The source gives concrete rates: Dota 2 runs at 30 frames per second and OpenAI Five acts every fourth frame, yielding roughly 20,000 steps per episode [PAGE 6]; StarCraft II professionals perform up to 500 APM, “meaning a decision is made every 125 milliseconds” [PAGE 6]; FTW agents had a 258 ms reaction time versus 559 ms for humans [PAGE 15]; Roller Champions uses “15 Unity's FixedUpdates,” described as “0.02 seconds per update under default settings” [PAGE 10]. The clock can force habit regression because a slow deliberate opponent model cannot be evaluated within the deadline. It can also enable a counter-trap: a fast exploiter can punish a slow deliberator before the deliberator finishes its model update.

The three modes interact. A tight clock induces habit regression; an adaptive opponent detects the habit and sets a counter-trap; the counter-trap increases effective time pressure, further tightening the clock. The mitigation is not “think harder.” It is architectural: action masking to remove infeasible actions [PAGE 11], opponent pools and league exploiters [PAGE 15-16], curriculum self-play [PAGE 17], and measured latency budgets. The audit prediction is straightforward: if the clock is tightened by a large factor, learned-policy performance should degrade before a scripted baseline does; if the opponent is allowed to adapt, a fixed habit should be exploited more than a mixed strategy; if exploiters are removed from evaluation, reported robustness should overestimate deployed robustness.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication treats a performance claim as valid only if it survives three necessary checks: physical execution, biomechanical human baseline, and environmental or “weather” stochasticity. These are veto conditions, not sufficient proofs. A claim can pass all three and still fail for other reasons, but failure of any one blocks the superhuman or human-like interpretation.

Physical veto: can the policy execute within the game’s deadline? The source supplies deadline evidence. Dota 2 is described as running at 30 frames per second with an action every fourth frame [PAGE 6]; StarCraft II has 16 time-steps per second and professional play at 500 APM [PAGE 6, PAGE 15]; Roller Champions uses a 15-FixedUpdate decision interval [PAGE 10]. A proof obligation is to measure the policy’s p99 inference latency \(L_{\text{infer}}\) and compare it with the decision deadline \(D\). If \(L_{\text{infer}}\gt D\), the policy is not deployable regardless of offline win rate. The source does not report p99 latency for most systems, so many claims are execution-conditional. The veto is especially strong for systems trained with large GPU batches but deployed on game servers or client hardware.

Biomechanical veto: is the advantage due to strategy or to superhuman motor precision? The FTW result is the critical case. The source reports “a reaction time of 258 milliseconds, compared to 559 milliseconds for humans” and “a tagging accuracy of 80%, substantially higher than the 48% accuracy of human players” [PAGE 15]. It also reports that “Even when the FTW agents' tagging accuracy was deliberately reduced to match human levels, they still maintained a higher win probability” [PAGE 15]. That is evidence that tagging accuracy alone does not explain the win probability, but it does not control reaction time. A human-like claim requires matching both reaction time and accuracy. A strategic-superiority claim requires either matching both or explicitly modeling them as confounding variables. The veto predicts that clamping reaction time to 559 ms will reduce the advantage; if the advantage remains large, the strategic component is supported; if it collapses, the superhuman claim is motor-confounded.

Weather veto: does the result generalize across stochastic environment instances? The source has no meteorological weather, so “weather” here means map instance, random seed, and nonstationary environment. Eq. 16 conditions on \(\omega\), “the specific map instance and random seeds, which are stochastic in learning and testing” [PAGE 14]. The FTW evaluation used “procedurally generated maps that neither the agents nor the humans had previously encountered” [PAGE 15], which is a strong environmental control. The proof obligation is to report the distribution of performance over \(\omega\), not a single tournament or seed. If the lower confidence bound over held-out maps is below the baseline, the claim is vetoed. The source’s nonstationarity claim [PAGE 6] makes this veto necessary: an agent that wins on one map distribution can fail when the distribution shifts.

The adjudication order should be physical first, biomechanical second, weather third. Physical veto asks whether the policy can act at all. Biomechanical veto asks whether the action advantage is humanly matched. Weather veto asks whether the result survives the declared environment distribution. The source’s own evidence supports this order: reaction-time and tagging numbers are reported separately from win probability [PAGE 15], and map randomness is explicitly parameterized [PAGE 14]. A claim that ignores these layers is not a proof of MARL competence; it is a conditional result.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes are pre-registered interventions with measurable predictions. They are designed to falsify a claimed mechanism, not merely to reproduce a win rate.

Scenario one: reward-shaping ablation in Google Research Football. Source anchor: “the SCORING function rewards agents based on successful goals, while CHECKPOINT addresses the reward sparsity of SCORING by offering intermediate rewards as the ball progresses toward the opponent’s goal” [PAGE 10]. Intervention: train the same algorithm family, seeds, network budget, and evaluation schedule under two conditions—SCORING only versus SCORING plus CHECKPOINT. Prediction: the SCORING-only condition will show lower sample efficiency and higher variance in goal differential before reaching a fixed win-rate threshold. Verification: pre-register the threshold, evaluate at least 1,000 held-out matches per seed, and report confidence intervals. If SCORING-only matches or exceeds the shaped condition, the reward-sparsity rationale is falsified for that environment. This veto tests whether the claimed complexity dimension is causally active.
