Level 0 is the physical ball state governed by impact and aerodynamics. Level 1 is the embedded estimator: camera clusters set an initial state, INS propagates between visual updates, and the orbit processor accumulates statistics [PAGE 7]. Level 2 is the broadcast graphic rendered from the estimated orbit. Level 3 is the viewer’s belief that the graphic coincides with the physical ball. Level 4 is commentator and analyst reaction to the graphic, which can retroactively shape the narrative of play.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the camera layer fails—during occlusion by player bodies [PAGE 7], or under rainfall and snow that obscure optics [PAGE 7]. The operator and viewer regress to unaided visual estimation or legacy commentary because the system can no longer justify sub-5 cm precision. The counter-trap is the patent’s fusion architecture: the camera state seeds the INS propagator, so short outages are bridged by dead reckoning rather than total blackout. However, if the bridge exceeds the covariance breakdown regime, the counter-trap becomes a false-precision trap: a confident but drifting "ghost ball" contradicts the viewer’s unaided perception, collapsing trust.

The clock is both the update-rate clock and the match clock. Preferred updates of 10–20 Hz [PAGE 7] imply a 50–100 ms latency budget per cycle; overruns cascade into dropped frames. Over a four-hour match [PAGE 7], that is roughly 144,000 to 288,000 state updates, demanding sustained compute, RF, and battery performance without thermal throttling or voltage sag. The recursive theory of mind thus predicts that the system’s credibility is a function of the minimum of (camera availability, INS drift bound, weather visibility, RF latency, battery SoC); any layer crossing its veto threshold forces regression to Level 0 unaided perception.
