The passage from Tolman (1932), [PAGE 364], isolates a distinction that functions as a logical wedge between acquisition and expression. Tolman argues that learning can accumulate without any strong differential effect; it remains latent until differential consequences are introduced, at which point the immediate drop in the performance curve reveals that learning was “just as great” as it would have been under continuous strong differential effects [PAGE 364]. This is not a claim about motivation alone, nor about motor capacity alone. It is a claim about the causal architecture linking three variables: the learned representation of consequences (L), the momentary demand or appetitive-aversion state (D), and the observed selection/performance (P).

The gray zone begins at the mapping between L and P. Tolman explicitly states that differential effects are “necessary for selective performance but they are not necessary, or at the most in only a very minor degree, for the mere learning qua learning which underlies such performance” [PAGE 364]. That means a flat performance curve during non-rewarded exploration cannot be interpreted as a flat learning curve. The multi-hop derivation is: (1) the organism samples alternative responses; (2) it registers what consequences each alternative leads to; (3) it stores this as an expectancy or cognitive representation of the means-end structure of the environment; (4) performance is gated by whether one consequence is “demanded-for” relative to others under the current appetite-aversion conditions [PAGE 364]. If the demand differential is null, no response is selected even though L is nonzero. This is a singularity: ΔD = 0 decouples P from L completely.

A second gray zone concerns the “immediate drop” Tolman cites. He does not specify the exact time constant of the drop, nor whether it is measured in errors per trial, latency, or path-ratio. Without an operational definition of the performance curve, one cannot distinguish a sudden retrieval of latent knowledge from a sudden change in motivation or from the removal of a response-competition artifact. The audit therefore treats “immediate” as an experimental observation requiring temporal resolution, not as a theoretical primitive.

The third gray zone is the trial-and-error rejection. Tolman denies that correct connections are “stamped in” and incorrect ones “stamped out” [PAGE 364]. Instead, learning consists in discovering or refining what all alternative responses lead to. This implies that errors are not mere noise to be extinguished; they are informative probes of the consequence-space. A system that only takes the correct path may learn less than a system that samples incorrect paths, because the latter builds a richer representation of alternative outcomes. This inverts the naive “practice makes perfect” assumption and creates a trap: an exclusive focus on error-free repetition may starve the learner of the very comparisons needed to discriminate “demanded-for” from “demanded-against” consequences [PAGE 364].

Gray-zone audit summary: latent L is invisible until ΔD ≠ 0; P is a function of L, D, and biomechanical affordances; the null-demand regime is an exact singularity where P reveals nothing about L; and error-free training may be suboptimal for learning even while it maximizes short-run performance. Eliot and remarked by Lashley (1929, pp. 524–563) [PAGE 364].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(R \in \{r_1,\dots,r_n\}\) denote the set of alternative responses available in the maze or problem space [PAGE 364]. Let \(C(r)\) be the consequence associated with response \(r\). Learning is the construction of an expectancy map \(E : R \to \mathcal{C}\) that refines the organism’s representation of what each alternative leads to [PAGE 364]. Formally, after exposure over time \(t\),

\[
L(t) \;=\; \int_0^t \mathcal{I}\big(R_a \to C_a\big)\, d\tau ,
\]

where \(\mathcal{I}\) is the Shannon information, measured in bits, gained about the consequence \(C_a\) produced by alternative \(R_a\), including incorrect alternatives. Tolman’s claim that “some, and perhaps considerable, taking of the incorrect path also is necessary” [PAGE 364] implies that restricting \(R\) to the correct path truncates the integral and degrades \(L\).

Performance is not \(L\). Define \(P(t)\in[0,1]\) as the proportion of selections of the response whose consequence is currently most “demanded-for.” Define the demand differential

\[
\Delta D \;=\; D_{\text{for}} - D_{\text{against}} \in \mathbb{R},
\]

where \(D_{\text{for}}\) and \(D_{\text{against}}\) are the appetitive/aversive demand intensities attached to competing consequences under the conditions of the moment [PAGE 364]. A formal choice operator consistent with Tolman’s verbal model is

\[
P(t) \;=\; \sigma\!\big(\beta\,\Delta D\big)\cdot L(t) \;+\; \epsilon(t),
\]

where \(\sigma\) is a monotonic selection gate (e.g., logistic), \(\beta\) is a gain parameter translating demand into response bias, and \(\epsilon(t)\) captures biomechanical and sensory noise. This is an assumption, not a law quoted from Tolman; Tolman supplies the qualitative conditional: “if there be no such difference in demands there will be no such selection and performance of the one response, even though there has been learning” [PAGE 364].

Breakdown/singularity regime: \(\Delta D \to 0\) implies \(\sigma(\beta\Delta D)\to \tfrac12\) (or chance-level selection), so \(P\to \tfrac12\) regardless of how large \(L\) is. This is the exact regime in which latent learning is behaviorally invisible. A second breakdown regime is \(L=0\) with \(\Delta D\neq0\): performance reflects pure motivation or bias, not learning. A third regime is biomechanical saturation: if the organism cannot execute \(r\) (injury, fatigue, obstacle), then \(P=0\) even when \(L>0\) and \(\Delta D\gg0\).

Units: \(L\) in bits (information about response-consequence contingencies); \(\Delta D\) in utility or demand units (not probability); \(P\) dimensionless proportion; \(t\) in trials or seconds depending on the recording convention. The source does not provide SI units; these are imposed for formal clarity and must be operationally anchored in any replication [PAGE 364].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs backward from [PAGE 364] to the trial-and-error doctrine of Thorndikian stimulus-response psychology, against which Tolman directs his “final criticism” [PAGE 364]. In that doctrine, correct S-R bonds are “stamped in” by the Law of Exercise and incorrect bonds “stamped out”; learning is equated with the incremental strengthening of connections through repetition and differential satisfaction [PAGE 364]. Tolman rejects the mechanism: stimuli do not “call out responses willy nilly” [PAGE 364]. Instead, the organism purports to discover the consequence-structure of alternatives, a position that anticipates cognitive-map and expectancy models.

The footnote on [PAGE 364] identifies Dr. M. H. Eliot as the first to point out the performance-learning distinction to Tolman, and cites Lashley (1929), *Brain Mechanisms and Learning*, pp. 524–563, as having made a related remark [PAGE 364]. This places the argument in a pre-Hullian, pre-spatial-cognitive-map moment, yet one already dissatisfied with pure contiguity. Forward in time, the passage anticipates Tolman & Honzik’s latent-learning experiments, in which non-rewarded rats showed no performance advantage until reward was introduced, after which their error curves dropped sharply relative to continuously rewarded controls. It also anticipates the distinction between model-based and model-free reinforcement learning: Tolman’s organism “refines what all the respective alternative responses lead to,” which is a model-based representation of transition consequences, not a cached value of a single stimulus-response pair.

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy: (1) the acquisition-curve trap, in which experimenters equate the slope of early practice with the amount of learning, ignoring latent phases; (2) the extinction-as-unlearning trap, in which a decline in performance is interpreted as loss of S-R strength rather than as a change in demand \(\Delta D\) or expectancy; (3) the error-avoidance trap, in which trainers eliminate incorrect responses to speed performance, thereby depriving the organism of the comparative consequence sampling Tolman deems necessary for learning [PAGE 364]. Each trap can persist across weeks of data collection because the performance curve continues to look “smooth” while the underlying representational state \(L\) is either accumulating invisibly or being misread.

The counter-trap is the probe-trial inversion: the observer must introduce a demand differential \(\Delta D\neq0\) after a period of latent exposure and look for an immediate performance shift, rather than waiting for gradual change. If the subject suddenly selects the correct path at above-chance levels, the observer must retroactively attribute learning to the earlier non-differential period. This requires a second-order model of the subject’s mind: the subject knows more than it is currently demonstrating, and the observer must know that the subject knows. Failure to run this recursion yields the “latent-learning blind spot,” where a capable organism is misclassified as an incapable one because the current appetite-aversion conditions do not favor the learned response [PAGE 364].

The clock variable is the mismatch between two time scales: \(\tau_L\), the consolidation time of the consequence representation during exploration, and \(\tau_P\), the expression time once \(\Delta D\) is applied. Tolman’s “immediate drop” implies \(\tau_P \ll \tau_L\): expression is fast once gated, while accumulation was slow and silent [PAGE 364]. If an experiment samples only at intervals longer than \(\tau_P\), it will see a step function and may misdate the learning event. If it samples only during the latent phase, it will see noise. A recursive observer therefore schedules demand-probes at multiple phases and treats the absence of selection under \(\Delta D=0\) as consistent with either \(L=0\) or \(L>0\), never as evidence for \(L=0\) alone [PAGE 364].

Crisis-habit regression under stress: when an organism is tired, hungry, or threatened, \(\Delta D\) may be dominated by aversive terms, causing it to regress to a previously “demanded-for” response even if a better-learned alternative exists. The observer may then conclude the better response was never learned, when in fact the demand configuration has simply suppressed its expression [PAGE 364].

Veto: any performance decrement concurrent with physical trauma is attributed to the physical layer, not to a loss of learned expectancy, unless a post-recovery probe under identical \(\Delta D\) shows recovery of the prior selection pattern.

Failure to satisfy any condition voids the inference; the result is indeterminate between learning, motivation, and affordance explanations.
