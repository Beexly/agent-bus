<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s formal core is a Bernoulli match model driven by Elo rating differences. All probability quantities below are dimensionless, in \([0,1]\). Elo ratings and scaling factors are in Elo rating points, not SI units. The paper does not assign an information-theoretic unit to the rating scale.

**1. Pairwise win operator.**
\[
W_{ij}=\mathcal{E}(E_i,E_j;s)=\frac{1}{1+10^{-(E_i-E_j)/s}},\qquad W_{ij}\in[0,1].
\]
Here \(E_i,E_j\in\mathbb{R}\) are Elo points and \(s>0\) is a scaling factor in Elo points. The paper states: “The winning probability of team i is Wij = 1/(1 + 10−(Ei−Ej)/s)” [PAGE 7]. It also states: “Note that Wij + Wji = 1, thus draws are prohibited” [PAGE 7]. Consequently, the operator is a two-outcome logistic map. Its limits are exact: as \(E_i-E_j\to+\infty\), \(W_{ij}\to1\); as \(E_i-E_j\to-\infty\), \(W_{ij}\to0\); as \(s\to\infty\), \(W_{ij}\to1/2\); as \(s\to0^+\), \(W_{ij}\to1\) for \(E_i>E_j\), \(W_{ij}\to0\) for \(E_i<E_j\), and \(W_{ij}=1/2\) for \(E_i=E_j\). The formula itself is undefined at \(s=0\); the step-function behavior is only a limiting regime. There is no finite-rating singularity for \(s>0\). The main breakdown regime is the no-draw assumption: if draw probability is non-negligible, the two-outcome operator misstates the match distribution.

**2. Home-field operator.**
For World Football Elo Ratings, the home team’s rating is adjusted as
\[
E_h' = H_{100}(E_h)=E_h+100,
\]
where 100 is in Elo points. The paper states: “Its scaling factor is s = 400 and the rating of the home team is increased by a fixed amount of 100” [PAGE 8]. The FIFA version “accounts neither for the margin of victory nor for home advantage” [PAGE 8]. Thus the home operator is active only in the World Football Elo variant. Breakdown occurs if home advantage is heterogeneous by venue, travel, altitude, or crowd conditions; the source has no such variables.

**3. Two-legged knockout operator.**
For a two-legged matchup treated as one long neutral game,
\[
W_{ij}^{(2L)}=\frac{1}{1+10^{-\sqrt{2}(E_i-E_j)/s}}.
\]
The paper states: “these matchups are assumed to be played at a neutral field and the difference of the Elo ratings is multiplied by the square root of 2” [PAGE 8]. The factor \(\sqrt{2}\) is dimensionless. Breakdown occurs if the two legs are not exchangeable, if aggregate score and away-goal-type rules alter incentives, or if fatigue and travel make the legs non-independent.

**4. Monte Carlo qualification estimator.**
For simulation run \(r\), define \(D_{ir}=1\) if team \(i\) qualifies directly and \(I_{ir}=1\) if team \(i\) reaches the inter-confederation playoff. The estimated qualification probability is
\[
\hat p_i=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\left(D_{ir}+\frac{1}{2}I_{ir}\right),\qquad N=10^7.
\]
The paper states: “A random number ε is generated between 0 and 1. If ε < Wij, the winner is team i, otherwise, the winner is team j. The ranking in groups depends on the number of wins, and all ties in the number of wins are broken randomly” [PAGE 10]. It also states: “The whole process is repeated 10 million times to get reasonable expected values” [PAGE 10] and “3.5 qualifying slots are allocated in every iteration” [PAGE 10]. Units: \(\hat p_i\) is a probability; \(N\) is a count; 3.5 is a slot count per simulated tournament. Breakdown regimes include finite-\(N\) Monte Carlo error, random tie-breaking variance, and model misspecification from fixed strengths.

**5. Synthetic strength model.**
For the theoretical exercise, the \(i\)-th ranked team is assigned
\[
E_i^{(\Delta)}=1300+(18-i)\Delta,\qquad \Delta\ge0,
\]
with the 18th team fixed at 1300 Elo points, \(s=400\), and home advantage 100 [PAGE 15]. Here \(\Delta=0\) is the fully competitive case, while \(\Delta=20\) and \(\Delta=40\) represent increasingly unbalanced contests [PAGE 15]. The model’s exact qualitative breakpoints are the 6th/7th boundary in the divided format and the 5th/6th boundary in the undivided format [PAGE 15]. The paper reports that “the divided tournament design is strategy-proof with respect to the initial ranking of the teams” [PAGE 15]. A breakdown regime for this synthetic model is that real strength distributions need not be linear in rank; the exercise isolates design properties, not real-team forecasts.

**Assumptions and page-anchored caveats.**
The divided format’s lower-seeded draw procedure was never announced, so the paper assumes “a similar policy” to prefer higher-ranked countries [PAGE 6]. The ranking basis was intended to be June 2020, but June and July 2020 are the same because “no international football matches were played in the summer of 2020” [PAGE 8]. The FIFA rating’s recent adoption may be imperfectly adjusted because “the number of matches played since the summer of 2018 is probably insufficient for the necessary adjustment of the ratings” [PAGE 8]. The paper itself cautions that the numerical results are “primarily for comparative purposes” [PAGE 10].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Crisis habit regression.** The COVID-19 disruption created a decision environment in which organisers reverted to a familiar hybrid: first-round groups, then knockout ties, then a final group [PAGE 4-5]. The paper frames this as a return to “a more traditional tournament design” after the pandemic forced the original divided plan to be updated [PAGE 4]. The habit-regression risk is epistemic: under time pressure, a governing body may equate “traditional” with “safer” or “fairer” without simulating the alternative. The source’s counterweight is that the divided design had two non-trivial properties: it increased competitiveness for the strongest and weakest teams, and it avoided misaligned incentives because no team preferred being ranked 7th in order to enter the lower-seeded path instead of the Hexagonal [PAGE 15][PAGE 17]. The paper states: “the divided tournament design is strategy-proof with respect to the initial ranking of the teams” [PAGE 15]. That does not prove the divided format is best; it proves that the traditional-format default is not automatically justified.

**Counter-trap.** The opposite error is to over-correct: to assume the abandoned novel format was superior, or to read the simulation as a precise forecast. The paper’s own caveats block that move. The model prohibits draws [PAGE 7], assumes fixed strengths [PAGE 3], assumes a lower-seeded draw policy that was never announced [PAGE 6], and counts the inter-confederation place as half [PAGE 10]. The correct counter-trap is to compare designs under the same fixed-strength model and to treat the percentages as comparative, not predictive. The paper says the numerical results are “primarily for comparative purposes” [PAGE 10]. It also recommends a probabilistic seeding rule—5th with 60%, 6th with 30%, 7th with 10% in the illustrative final-group case—to reduce the sharp nonlinearity rather than to pretend that the nonlinearity is a natural law [PAGE 16-17].

**Clock.** The decision clock has several layers. The real calendar clock is the multi-week qualifier schedule and the COVID-19 disruption that forced the format change [PAGE 3-4]. The ranking clock is the June/July 2020 equivalence caused by no international matches [PAGE 8]. The simulation clock is the 10 million-run Monte Carlo loop [PAGE 10]. The institutional clock is the July 2019 announcement, June 2020 revision, and July 2020 publication sequence [PAGE 3-4]. Recursive theory of mind then has four nested perspectives. The organiser anticipates competitive balance, fairness, and commercial interest. The team anticipates seeding cliffs and may chase a particular ranking position. The analyst anticipates that teams’ incentives depend on the announced rule, so the rule must be evaluated as a mechanism, not as a fixed schedule. The fan or stakeholder anticipates that small ranking differences imply large qualification differences, which is why the paper worries about perceived fairness [PAGE 15]. The lottery proposal changes the teams’ anticipation: if the 5th–7th positions are probabilistically mapped into the final-group slot, the cliff is softened and the incentive to chase an exact rank is reduced [PAGE 16-17]. The clock also bounds the claim: because the model holds strengths fixed, it cannot tell us how real teams would have adapted over the multi-week calendar; it can only tell us how the rules map fixed strengths into qualification probabilities.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether physical, biomechanical, or weather claims can be used to explain the paper’s simulated probability differences. The answer is no: those layers are outside the source model and are therefore vetoed for causal attribution. The source’s input set is essentially \(X_{\text{model}}=\{E_i,E_j,s,\text{home flag},\text{format},\varepsilon\}\). It contains no fatigue load, injury state, sprint or jump biomechanics, temperature, humidity, altitude, travel distance, pitch condition, or recovery variable. The paper explicitly states that it quantifies the external shock “under the assumption that their strengths do not change at all” [PAGE 3]. The match outcome is generated from a random number compared with the Elo probability [PAGE 10]. The FIFA variant “accounts neither for the margin of victory nor for home advantage” [PAGE 8]. Therefore any claim of the form “Canada’s probability doubled because of weather” or “El Salvador’s probability halved because of fatigue” is not supported by the source. Such a claim may be true in the real world, but it is not identified by this model.

**Physical veto proof.** The model has fixed Elo ratings across the simulated tournament. If physical training load, injury, illness, or recovery changed a team’s strength during the multi-week qualifiers, then the fixed-strength assumption would be violated. The paper’s comparative result would still isolate the format effect under fixed strengths, but it would not estimate the total real-world effect. The veto condition is: a physical causal claim is admissible only if the source contains a physical state variable and an equation linking it to \(E_i\) or \(W_{ij}\). No such variable or equation appears. The only “home” adjustment is a rating increment of 100 points in the World Football Elo variant [PAGE 8], which is a statistical rating convention, not a biomechanical measurement.

**Biomechanical veto proof.** The source contains no injury, load, force, kinematic, or fatigue measurement. It cannot adjudicate whether a two-legged tie is more physically demanding than a single group match, nor whether the Hexagonal’s travel pattern changes soft-tissue risk. The two-legged tie is explicitly modelled as a neutral “long” game with a \(\sqrt{2}\) rating-difference scaling [PAGE 8]. That is a probabilistic compression, not a biomechanical claim. A biomechanical explanation that assigns a sign or magnitude to the reform’s effect is therefore unfalsifiable within the source and must be vetoed.

**Weather veto proof.** The source has no match-level weather, venue, altitude, or temperature data. It also has no travel-distance variable. The only external shock named is the COVID-19 pandemic, and it enters as a reason for the rule change, not as a measured weather or physiological variable [PAGE 3]. A weather-based explanation of the 5–6 percentage-point shifts would therefore be a layer violation. The admissible statement is narrower: weather and biomechanics are unmodelled external-validity caveats that could affect real outcomes, but they cannot explain the paper’s simulated differences, because the simulation varies format and rating inputs while holding strengths fixed.

**Adjudication rule.** For any cross-layer claim \(C\), accept \(C\) as an explanation of the paper’s \(\Delta p_i\) only if \(C\) is expressible as a change in \(E_i,E_j,s,\text{home flag},\text{format}\), or the random tie-breaking rule. Otherwise veto \(C\) as an unsupported external confounder. This rule protects the source’s actual contribution: the paper demonstrates that tournament design alone can move qualification probabilities by more than 5 percentage points under fixed strengths [PAGE 1], not that physical or weather layers have been measured.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
