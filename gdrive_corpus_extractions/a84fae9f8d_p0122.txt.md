Figures 21 to 24, cited as the empirical typology, provide the observational base; Figure 21 specifically shows the accepted approximation by "the nearness of the circles to the curve which was generated by the equation" [PAGE 122]. This is a retrospective causal framing: past reinforcements are treated as the determinant of present habit strength.

However, the retro-causal structure contains a recursive trap. In a multi-week habit-formation protocol, the subject's current expectancy—a logical construct from Chapter XVIII (p. 326 ff. [PAGE 122])—modulates the efficacy of each newly delivered reinforcement. The value of reinforcement at trial t is therefore not exogenous; it depends on the subject's model of the experimenter's schedule, which itself is updated across weeks. The causal arrow is not simply n_past → H_present; it is a loop in which expected reinforcement changes the impact of received reinforcement. Counting reinforcements as a scalar n presumes that each reinforcement contributes identically, an assumption that fails when the subject detects non-contingency or schedule shifts.

Multi-week traps specific to this lineage:
Trap A (early-acquisition extrapolation): Weeks 1–3 typically show steep gains in H; experimenters may extrapolate this slope indefinitely and overestimate H_phys, mistaking transient acquisition velocity for permanent structure.
Trap B (late-plateau misreading): Apparent stabilization in weeks 6–8 may be read as the physiological limit, but could reflect satiation, fatigue, or reduced drive rather than an absolute ceiling. If reinforcements resume after a rest interval, H can rise again, falsifying the claim that the limit is permanent.
Trap C (constant non-transferability): The fitted constants .141, .033, and 3.1 from the Hovland data (page 120, cited on [PAGE 122]) are local to that dataset; applying them to a different species, apparatus, or reinforcement magnitude without re-estimation violates the trial-and-error caution that "the greater part of the trial and error involved in its determination has yet to be performed" [PAGE 122].

Because the text frames the procedure as ongoing and indirect, these traps are not anomalies to be excluded; they are expected failure modes of the method Hull himself describes.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression introduces a direct challenge to the monotonicity claim on [PAGE 122]. Under crisis conditions—high drive, novelty, threat, or pain—an organism may express behavior governed by an earlier, weaker habit stratum rather than the current asymptote. The effective expressed habit strength H_eff(n, crisis) can fall below H(n) even while acquisition remains monotonic. An experimenter measuring only response probability would observe regression and could falsely conclude that additional reinforcements failed to increase H, mistaking a retrieval/performance deficit for an acquisition ceiling.

The counter-trap is the sufficiency assumption for n. Two subjects with identical reinforcement counts n can exhibit different H if their inter-reinforcement intervals τ differ. A massed schedule (small τ) can induce peripheral and central satiation, depressing the marginal gain ΔH(n) before any structural habit ceiling is reached. A distributed schedule (large τ) permits consolidation and continued gains. Thus n is not a sufficient statistic; the required tuple is at least (n, τ, drive state). The text on [PAGE 122] says nothing about τ, creating a theoretical gap: the physiological limit may be reached sooner under massed training not because habit strength cannot grow, because the organism's processing buffer is temporarily exhausted.

The clock is the session timeline and inter-trial interval. If the clock runs too fast, the system hits a metabolic or attentional ceiling that mimics the "physiological limit" of conclusion 2 but is actually a performance veto. If the clock runs too slow, extraneous learning, extinction, and contextual drift between trials contaminate the count n, so H is no longer a clean function of reinforcement number alone. H_phys is therefore a function of clock speed and metabolic rate: H_phys = H_phys(τ, metabolic_rate), not a universal constant.

Recursive theory-of-mind nesting operates on both sides. The experimenter models the subject as accumulating H monotonically with n. The subject models the experimenter's contingency structure and updates its expectancy of reinforcement (the probability-of-reaction-evocation construct referenced at Chapter XVIII p. 326 ff. [PAGE 122]). If the subject detects that reinforcements are non-contingent or predictable independent of response, habit strength may not track n at all; superstitious or expectant responding breaks the Hullian mapping between objective reinforcement count and internal habit strength. In this loop, each agent's model of the other alters the very variable—n—that the experimenter treats as an exogenous input.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: Habit strength is bounded by physiological substrates—ATP, glucose, neurotransmitter pools, synaptic vesicle recycling, and mitochondrial output. If the reinforcement rate n per unit time exceeds the metabolic budget available for plasticity-related processes, then dH/dn = 0 regardless of the contingency. Veto condition: metabolic_supply(n) < plasticity_demand(n). Breakdown: at high reinforcement density, the system saturates at an energy ceiling rather than a habit-strength ceiling, so the "physiological limit" on [PAGE 122] is really a resource limit.

Biomechanical layer veto: If H is indexed through observable response probability (lever pressing, running, maze speed), peripheral fatigue can suppress measured P_evoke while central H remains elevated. This produces a false negative: the experimenter concludes that H has reached its physiological limit when in fact the musculoskeletal or effecter apparatus, not the habit construct, has reached its output ceiling. Veto condition: response rate declines while central drive and reinforcement contingency remain intact. The measured curve then underestimates true H(n), invalidating the monotonic fit to behavioral data.

Weather layer: Although [PAGE 122] describes laboratory conditions, ambient temperature and humidity alter metabolic rate and thermoregulatory load. High ambient temperature diverts energy to cooling, shifting H_phys downward; cold stress increases metabolic demand for thermoregulation, reducing the surplus available for habit consolidation. Veto condition: ambient conditions outside the thermoneutral zone used for Figures 21 to 24 invalidate the H_phys estimated under standard lab protocol. Any field or uncontrolled application must be vetoed until constants are re-estimated by the trial-and-error procedure Hull mandates.

Adjudication: The two propositions on [PAGE 122] hold only within the controlled regime implicit in the source. Cross-layer vetoes apply whenever physical, biomechanical, or environmental variables move the organism outside that calibration window. Under veto conditions, the claim that H is solely an increasing, bounded function of n is not merely inaccurate; it is categorically unsupported until a new indirect determination is performed.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR Veto 1 — Satiation-induced decrement. Counterfactual: reinforcements continue past the satiety threshold such that homeostatic drive falls below the level required for approach or instrumental behavior. Formal veto condition: ∃ n > n_sat such that ΔH(n) = H(n+1) − H(n) < 0. Prediction: the H(n) curve exhibits an inverted-U (initial rise followed by decline) rather than a monotonic ascent to an asymptote. Conclusion 2 on [PAGE 122] ("no more increase is possible") is wrong because active decrease occurs beyond n_sat, violating even the bounded-plateau claim.

RLVR Veto 2 — Latent reinforcement and spacing. Counterfactual: after n reaches the apparent asymptote on [PAGE 122], reinforcements are withheld for an interval T, after which H is probed. Formal veto condition: H(n, T) > H(n, 0) for T > 0, meaning habit strength changes without any additional reinforcements. Prediction: H is not a function of n alone; time-dependent consolidation means the physiological limit is reached only at a specific combination of n and T, and the limit estimated from massed data does not transfer to spaced protocols. Verifiable by measuring response probability or an H-index after fixed n with variable post-training intervals.

RLVR Veto 3 — Partial-reinforcement extinction divergence. Counterfactual: two groups reach identical n under continuous reinforcement; Group A continues continuous reinforcement, while Group B switches to a partial schedule. Formal veto condition: during extinction (Δn = 0), response probability for Group B remains significantly above Group A despite equal H(n) at the switch point. Prediction: the observable expression of habit strength depends on schedule history, so H(n) cannot be a unique scalar bounded by a physiological ceiling. The bounded limit on [PAGE 122] does not cap resistance to extinction; therefore the construct H as defined there fails to predict behavior under changed reinforcement contingencies. Each veto is verifiable by standard acquisition, spacing, and extinction protocols measuring response rate as a proxy for the logical construct H.
