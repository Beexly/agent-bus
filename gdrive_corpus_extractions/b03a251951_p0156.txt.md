The formal framework involves measurable spaces \((X,\mathcal{S})\) and \((Y,\mathcal{T})\), where \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{T}\) are σ-algebras on \(X\) and \(Y\) respectively. The Cartesian product space is \(X \times Y\), equipped with the product σ-algebra \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), defined as the smallest σ-algebra containing all measurable rectangles \(A \times B\) with \(A \in \mathcal{S}, B \in \mathcal{T}\).

For any subset \(E \subseteq X \times Y\) and \(x \in X\), the \(X\)-section of \(E\) is defined as:
\[
E_x = \{ y \in Y : (x,y) \in E \}.
\]
Similarly, for \(y \in Y\), the \(Y\)-section is \(E^y = \{ x \in X : (x,y) \in E \}\).

If \(f: E \to \mathbb{R}\) is a function, its \(X\)-section determined by \(x\) is the function \(f_x: E_x \to \mathbb{R}\) given by:
\[
f_x(y) = f(x,y).
\]

**Theorem A (Halmos [PAGE 1])**: Every section of a measurable set is a measurable set. That is, if \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\), then for every \(x \in X\), \(E_x \in \mathcal{T}\), and for every \(y \in Y\), \(E^y \in \mathcal{S}\).

*Proof sketch*: Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be the class of all subsets \(E \subseteq X \times Y\) such that for each \(x \in X\), \(E_x \in \mathcal{T}\) and for each \(y \in Y\), \(E^y \in \mathcal{S}\). One verifies that \(\mathcal{E}\) is a σ-algebra:
- If \(E = A \times B\) is a rectangle, then \(E_x = B\) if \(x \in A\), else \(\emptyset\); both are in \(\mathcal{T}\). Similarly, \(E^y = A\) if \(y \in B\), else \(\emptyset\), in \(\mathcal{S}\). Hence rectangles are in \(\mathcal{E}\).
- \(\mathcal{E}\) is closed under complements: For \(E^c\), \((E^c)_x = (E_x)^c \in \mathcal{T}\) because \(\mathcal{T}\) is a σ-algebra. Similarly for \(Y\)-sections.
- \(\mathcal{E}\) is closed under countable unions: \(\left( \bigcup_n E^{(n)} \right)_x = \bigcup_n (E^{(n)})_x \in \mathcal{T}\).

Since \(\mathcal{E}\) contains all rectangles and is a σ-algebra, it contains \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\). Thus any \(E \in \mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) has measurable sections.

**Necessary assumptions**: 
- \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{T}\) must be σ-algebras (closed under complements and countable unions). If they are only rings, \(\mathcal{E}\) may not be closed under complements, and the theorem fails.
- The product σ-algebra is generated by rectangles; this generation relies on the existence of \(X \in \mathcal{S}\) and \(Y \in \mathcal{T}\) (which hold for σ-algebras).

**Breakdown regimes**:
1. **Ring case**: If \(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{T}\) are rings but not σ-algebras, the complement step fails. Example: Let \(X = Y = \mathbb{N}\), \(\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{T} =\) finite subsets of \(\mathbb{N}\). Consider \(E = \{(n,n) : n \in \mathbb{N}\}\). Each singleton \(\{(n,n)\}\) is a rectangle (finite sets), so \(E\) is a countable union of rectangles, hence in the σ-algebra generated by rectangles. But for any \(x\), \(E_x = \{x\}\) is finite, so in \(\mathcal{T}\)? Wait, \(\mathcal{T}\) contains finite sets, so \(\{x\} \in \mathcal{T}\). However, the complement of a rectangle may not be a finite union of rectangles. More critically, if we take \(E = (\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}) \setminus \{(1,1)\}\), then \(E\) is not necessarily in the ring generated by rectangles, but it is in the σ-algebra. Its section at \(x=1\) is \(\mathbb{N} \setminus \{1\}\), which is cofinite. If \(\mathcal{T}\) is only finite sets, then \(\mathbb{N} \setminus \{1\} \notin \mathcal{T}\). So \(E\) would not have measurable sections. But is \(E\) in \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\)? Since \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(\mathcal{T}\) are not σ-algebras, the product σ-algebra is still defined as the σ-algebra generated by rectangles. In this case, \(E\) is indeed in that σ-algebra because its complement is a singleton rectangle. But its section is not in \(\mathcal{T}\). Hence Theorem A fails when \(\mathcal{T}\) is not a σ-algebra. This confirms that the σ-algebra condition is essential.

2. **Completion pathologies**: When dealing with complete measures (e.g., Lebesgue measure), the product measure is often completed. Then there exist sets in the completion of \(\mathcal{S} \otimes \mathcal{T}\) whose sections may not be measurable in the original \(\mathcal{T}\). For instance, if \(\nu\) is a measure on \(\mathcal{T}\), and we consider the completion \(\overline{\mathcal{T}}\), a set \(E\) in the completed product σ-algebra may have a section that is not in \(\mathcal{T}\) but only in \(\overline{\mathcal{T}}\). This is why Fubini's theorem for complete measures requires careful handling, often assuming one space is σ-finite to ensure that sections are almost everywhere measurable.

3. **Non-σ-finite case**: Even with σ-algebras, if the spaces are not σ-finite, product measures may not be unique, but Theorem A still holds because it is purely about σ-algebras, not measures.

Units and information: The objects are sets and σ-algebras; no physical units. Information-theoretically, the σ-algebra represents the observable information; sections correspond to conditioning on an event, and measurability ensures that conditioned observations remain within the information structure.

**Exact singularity**: The theorem breaks down precisely when \(\mathcal{T}\) is not closed under complements, i.e., not a σ-algebra. This is a sharp boundary.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The concept of sections in product spaces originates from the need to define iterated integrals rigorously. Before Carathéodory's extension theorem (1928), measure theory was largely based on outer measures and Lebesgue's construction. The product measure problem was addressed by Saks (1933) and later by Halmos in his 1944 book "Measure Theory". Theorem A is a key lemma leading to Fubini's theorem, which allows swapping integrals under certain conditions. The historical lineage traces back to Fubini (1907) who proved the theorem for Riemann integrals under continuity assumptions, and Tonelli (1909) who extended it to non-negative functions with Lebesgue measure.

Multi-week traps commonly encountered by students include:
- Assuming that the product σ-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) coincides with the Borel σ-algebra; this is true for finite dimensions but false for infinite-dimensional products.
- Believing that every subset of a product space is measurable; this leads to paradoxes like the Banach-Tarski decomposition.
- Overlooking the necessity of σ-finiteness in Fubini's theorem; without it, counterexamples exist where iterated integrals differ.
- Confusing sections with projections; projections of measurable sets may not be measurable (e.g., analytic sets).
- Thinking that completion of product measures preserves all properties; in fact, completion can introduce non-measurable sections.

The retrocausal analysis highlights how modern measure theory resolves these issues through precise definitions of σ-algebras and careful construction of product measures. The emphasis on σ-algebras rather than rings ensures that complements and countable operations are available, which is crucial for the monotone class arguments used in proving Theorem A.
