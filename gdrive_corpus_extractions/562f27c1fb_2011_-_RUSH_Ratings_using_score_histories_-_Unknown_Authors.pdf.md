<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
RUSH’s f operator is a clock-and-score theory of mind in reduced form. It asks: if the margin changes by s at time t, how much does the baseline probability of winning change? That is a recursive calculation because the value of a score depends on the remaining time, the current margin, and the distribution of future scores. The source’s “Family of hypothetical 28-0 games” makes this concrete: “Consider games that the visitor wins 28-0, with 7-point touchdowns at evenly spaced intervals,” e.g. scores at 2,4,6,8 minutes; 5,10,15,20 minutes; or 15,30,45,60 minutes [PAGE 12]. The same final margin is not the same score history. Early scores occur when the margin is low and the win-probability increment is large; late scores occur when the margin is already high and the increment is small. That is the clock layer: f is the clock, and it converts points into probability. The crisis habit regression layer is what the model does not explicitly include. Under late-game stress, teams may regress to habitual behavior: a leading team may play prevent defense, a trailing team may hurry up, a coach may kneel, spike, or call timeouts. Those habits change λ and π_s, not just α. If the trailing team’s late score comes against a prevent defense, RUSH correctly gives it low f-weight, but the score rate λ may be elevated by the strategy itself. If the leading team’s habit is to run clock and punt, the observed late scoring rate may fall even though ability is unchanged. The counter-trap layer is recursive: an opponent can anticipate the habit and deliberately concede a low-value score to burn clock, or force a high-value score by attacking a predictable tendency. RUSH’s f assigns low value to the conceded score when win probability barely moves, which is exactly the desired counter-trap resistance. But if the concession changes the score-type distribution—for example, a team allows a touchdown but also changes its own risk-taking—then π_s and λ are endogenous and the model’s fixed-π assumption breaks [PAGE 13], [PAGE 28]. The clock layer also has a breakdown regime: if a team can manipulate the clock, the time of a score is not exogenous. A kneel-down at the goal line, a deliberate safety, or an onside kick changes the score process in a way that is not captured by exp{α f}. The source’s likelihood treats t_i as observed and m(u) as piecewise constant [PAGE 25]; it does not model strategic timeouts or clock-stopping decisions. Thus the recursive theory-of-mind reading is: RUSH is strongest when teams are playing a relatively stationary scoring process and the main recursion is “score, margin, time remaining.” It is weakest when the crisis habit itself changes the intensity, because then the observed score history is a mixture of ability and strategy. The proper counter-trap is not to reinterpret every late score as clutch or garbage; it is to test whether λ and π_s are stable across game states. If they are not, the clock operator f is doing too much work.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is a misspecification test, not a narrative objection. Under RUSH the conditional intensity for a score of type s at time t and margin m is proportional to λ π_s exp{α f(t,m,s)} [PAGE 14]. If a physical, biomechanical, or weather layer changes λ, π_s, θ_j, or T, then the true intensity is not the RUSH intensity. The likelihood on [PAGE 25] is then evaluated under the wrong baseline. A physical veto is an injury or fatigue shock: if a team loses a key player midgame, θ_j is no longer constant, and the game’s early and late scoring rates are generated by different abilities. The homogeneous-Poisson statement “with evenly matched teams, scores occur according to a homogeneous Poisson process” [PAGE 15] is exactly what an injury shock violates. A biomechanical veto is a change in scoring technology: kicking mechanics, field surface, altitude, or wind can change the probability of a 3-point score relative to a 7-point score. The source fixes π_s as known constants [PAGE 13] and precomputes ξ_k(m) only “if the πs are assumed fixed and known” [PAGE 28]. If wind suppresses field goals and increases punts or touchdowns, the observed score-type product ∏π_{s_i} is misspecified, and the integral Σπ_σ exp{...} uses the wrong score-type weights. A weather veto is the same argument applied to λ and f: rain or wind can change the aggregate scoring rate, the score-type mix, and the baseline win-probability increments. Because f is computed under α=0 with fixed π_s, a weather-induced change in π_s also changes the ξ_k(m) table. The veto proof is simple: let π_s^*(w) be the weather-conditional score-type distribution. If π_s^*(w)≠π_s, then the true likelihood contains ∏π_{s_i}^*(w) and an integral with π_σ^*(w), while RUSH uses ∏π_{s_i} and π_σ. No choice of α can generally absorb a change in the baseline score-type distribution, because α multiplies f inside the exponent but does not alter the multiplicative π_s factors. Similarly, if λ(t) is nonstationary because of fatigue or weather, the constant-λ integral cannot match both early and late scoring rates. If θ_j(t) changes because of injury, a constant θ_j cannot match pre- and post-injury segments. Adjudication therefore requires stratification or covariates: indoor versus outdoor venue, wind speed, precipitation, temperature, injury indicators, and time-varying λ. If those layers are stationary or negligible, RUSH’s calibration target on [PAGE 16] can be tested directly. If they are not, the cross-layer veto is decisive: the rating is not a clean ability estimate. This is especially important because the source itself treats π_s as fixed and known and uses a homogeneous Poisson baseline for precomputation [PAGE 13], [PAGE 15], [PAGE 28]. The veto is not that RUSH is invalid everywhere; it is that RUSH’s likelihood has no free parameter that can repair a weather-induced change in the score-type distribution or a biomechanical change in scoring probability.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
