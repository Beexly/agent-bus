<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a count-based performance-analysis tradition. It cites earlier work on scoring first and home advantage, including Lago-Peñas et al., where “teams that scored first ended the match scoring around the double of their opponents” and “home teams scored first around 60% of the matches” [PAGE 1]. It cites Hughes and Franks on passes before goals in the 1990 FIFA World Cup, where “successful teams scored more goals after longer passing sequences” [PAGE 1]. It cites Yiannakos and Armatas on the 2004 European Championship, who “reported a higher percentage of goals in the second part (57.4%) than in the first part of the match (42.6%)” [PAGE 1]. It also cites Redwood-Brown, who observed that “during the five minutes before a goal, the number of passes was higher than the average” and “during the five minutes after a goal, the number of passes was lower” [PAGE 1–PAGE 2]. The present paper’s retrocausal move is to take these two lineages—pass-volume correlates with scoring, and second-half goal rates rise—and place them in one league-season dataset to produce the “pass-goal paradox” [PAGE 1].

The first multi-week trap is aggregation drift. A season-level team correlation can be stable while weekly match-level relationships move in the opposite direction. If a team has high pass volume and high goal output across the season, but in its low-pass weeks it faces weak opponents and scores more, the season aggregate will hide the weekly reversal. The source does not provide weekly rolling estimates. The correct multi-week audit would estimate \(C_h(i,w)\) for week \(w\), then test whether the sign of the half asymmetry is stable across weeks. A single season’s 20-team aggregate cannot answer that.

The second multi-week trap is schedule congestion and opponent strength. The source does not include opponent-adjusted passing or goal rates, rest days, travel, or fixture density. A team with a congested schedule may show lower second-half pass counts and higher goal counts because both teams are fatigued or because it is chasing a result. The paper itself says “other variables, such as playing at home or away, have been shown to influence the total number of passes and goals” and should be included [PAGE 5]. Multi-week analysis without home/away, opponent, and rest controls can reproduce the paradox mechanically.

The third trap is outcome coding. The source counts “completed passes” [PAGE 2], not attempted passes, progressive passes, final-third entries, or pass distance. A second half can have fewer completed passes but more dangerous final-third actions; the count ratio \(C_h\) will fall even if the team’s attacking efficiency is unchanged. The fourth trap is selection on rank categories. The Top 4, Middle Ranking, and Relegated groups are season-final categories; comparing them after the season is retrospective. The paper’s sampling of “114 values at each group” and 1000 repetitions [PAGE 3] addresses unequal group sizes but not the retrospective category construction. A multi-week design would need pre-match rank or pre-match expectation, not final rank.

The fifth trap is arithmetic provenance. The Table 3 goal-difference values and the “only two teams” versus three-team list [PAGE 4] show that the published count chain has not been fully reconciled. Any downstream genealogy that treats the reported ratios as exact must first reconcile Table 1, Table 3, and the text. Otherwise the “paradox” may inherit a typo as if it were a mechanism.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source gives two explicit candidate mechanisms: physical-performance decline with “a higher probability of making mistakes,” “fatigue” causing “tactic disorganization,” and “the proximity of the end of the game” causing “taking more risks” [PAGE 5]. A recursive theory-of-mind reading separates three layers.

First-order crisis habit regression: under fatigue or pressure, players regress to low-complexity habits—fewer short exchanges, more direct clearances, more individual duels. If the habit is “do not risk the extra pass,” then second-half completed-pass counts fall. If the same regression increases loose-ball recoveries near goal, goal counts can rise. This is consistent with the source’s fatigue conjecture, but the source does not measure habits, pressures, or pass risk.

Second-order counter-trap: a coach or team anticipates the opponent’s second-half risk-taking. If the opponent is likely to press higher or commit more players forward, the counter-trap is to bypass the press with fewer, longer passes and to attack the space behind. That also lowers pass volume while raising goal probability for both sides. The source’s phrase “probability of scoring of any of the two teams” [PAGE 5] is compatible with this symmetric counter-trap, but the paper does not separate own-team from opponent effects.

Third-order clock recursion: as remaining time \(T\) decreases, the utility of a goal relative to the utility of avoiding a concession changes. A trailing team may accept more turnover risk; a leading team may accept lower pass volume to protect space. The opponent anticipates that and may press or drop. The clock is not just a physical variable; it is a shared common-knowledge variable that changes both teams’ risk preferences. The source’s “proximity of the end of the game” [PAGE 5] is the clock layer, but the paper does not condition on score state or time remaining.

The recursive failure mode is that all three layers predict the same aggregate sign: lower second-half pass counts and higher goal counts. Crisis habit regression predicts it through fatigue; counter-trap predicts it through opponent anticipation; clock recursion predicts it through end-game risk. Without game-state, substitution, tracking-load, and pass-location controls, the observed asymmetry cannot adjudicate among them. The correct theory-of-mind audit is therefore a veto test: if the asymmetry survives within tied matches, within low-fatigue players, and within final-third pass counts, then a pure habit or clock story is insufficient; if it disappears under those controls, the source’s speculation is supported but still not proven.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
