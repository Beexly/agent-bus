<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of profit theory reveals a profound retrocausal trap wherein modern financialization has overwritten the original epistemological boundaries drawn by economists like Frank H. Knight. Historically, pre-19th century merchant capitalism treated profit as a direct, albeit perilous, function of venture—the physical voyage across oceans involved tangible, highly volatile risks (shipwrecks, piracy) that were largely actuarial once sufficient maritime data accumulated. 

Classical economists (Ricardo, Mill) grappled with profit as a surplus determined by the organic composition of capital or diminishing marginal productivity of land, often treating it as a passive residue. The "dynamic" explanations mentioned by Knight [PAGE 41] emerged from Marxian and Austrian traditions attempting to inject time, change, and innovation into the static Walrasian general equilibrium. However, these dynamic theories frequently collapsed back into wage-labor analogies (i.e., the manager is just a highly paid worker), failing to isolate the unique premium demanded for bearing uncalculable futures.

Hawley’s intervention in the early 20th century—an era of unprecedented industrial scaling and monopolistic consolidation—attempted to rescue the entrepreneurial function by elevating "enterprise" above land, labor, and capital [PAGE 41]. His insistence on the "irksomeness" of risk was psychologically astute but mathematically barren. By failing to formally decouple insurable variance (standard deviation) from uninsurable ambiguity (Knightian uncertainty), Hawley inadvertently provided the intellectual scaffolding for the mid-century rise of Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT). MPT, pioneered by Markowitz and Sharpe, took Hawley's "actuarial losses" and formalized them as diversifiable beta risk, essentially declaring that perfect markets eliminate the need for unique entrepreneurial judgment through efficient indexing. 

This represents a devastating multi-week (multi-decade) trap: by not rigorously defining the boundary between risk and uncertainty, early theorists allowed the financial industry to conflate the two. Today's algorithmic trading and derivatives markets operate under the illusion that Knightian uncertainty can be continuously modeled, hedged, and priced away via complex variance swaps. When "black swan" events occur, the resulting systemic shocks demonstrate that the "irksomeness" Hawley described was not a mere psychological quirk to be compensated by a small premium, but the fundamental ontological friction of a universe that refuses to yield its probability distributions to human estimation. The current crisis of global debt deflation and algorithmic flash crashes is the retrocausal manifestation of ignoring Knight's warning: you cannot build a stable economic superstructure on the foundation of calculable risk while pretending true uncertainty does not exist.
