The FE model is an LS-Dyna explicit-dynamics simulation of a Hybrid III 50th percentile male anthropomorphic head-neck model struck by a 15.6 kg impactor (nylon spherical cap, vinyl nitrile foam, metal backing plate, translational ram) mounted on a 17.7 kg sliding carriage [PAGE 5]. The impact design space spans head orientation angles \alpha \in [-180^\circ, 0^\circ] in 30^\circ increments, \beta \in [-30^\circ, 30^\circ] in 10^\circ increments, head vertical position z determined by trial-and-error to avoid neck contact, initial ram offset of 5–20 mm, and three impact velocities v_0 \in \{2, 5, 8\} m/s representing low-, medium-, and high-energy events [PAGE 5]. This produced N_sim = 1065 unique initial configurations with approximately even impact-location distribution [PAGE 5]. Each simulation ran for T_sim = 150 ms with \Delta t = 0.01 ms sampling, outputting head center-of-gravity linear and rotational acceleration, velocity, and displacement in the SAE J211/1 head coordinate system [PAGE 5][PAGE 6].

The machine-learning operator is a deep 1D convolutional neural network architecture adapted from Domel et al. [PAGE 6]: two blocks of [1D convolution (64 then 128 filters; 5x1 then 10x1 kernels; ReLU), max pooling (pool=2), batch normalization, 40% dropout], followed by a reshaped 2D combination, a 2D convolution layer (64 filters, 3x15 patch, 3x1 stride, ReLU), global average pooling, batch normalization, 40% dropout, and a softmax output [PAGE 6]. Six training strategies were compared, including training on field data only, field plus 25% synthetic, field plus 100% synthetic, and pre-training on synthetic data followed by re-training on field data [PAGE 6]. Augmentation added up to five time-shifted copies per signal with offsets of 1–5 ms, expanding the training set to roughly 5100 true and 55,000 false signals [PAGE 7]. Class weights were introduced into the loss function to counter the 1:10 imbalance, though the exact weighting coefficients are not published in the text [PAGE 7].

Performance was evaluated with positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the F_X score [PAGE 7]. The F_X formulation given in the source weights precision and recall by a factor X; X=1 yields F1 and X=2 yields F2, the latter chosen explicitly to minimize false negatives (missed true impacts) at the expense of tolerating more false positives [PAGE 7]. On the held-out real-world test set of 88 video-verified events, the best detector identified 61 true impacts and 20 false impacts, with 7 false positives and 0 false negatives [PAGE 11][PAGE 13]. Thus TP=61, TN=20, FP=7, FN=0, giving PPV = 61/68 ≈ 0.897, NPV = 20/20 = 1.000, accuracy = 81/88 ≈ 0.920, F1 ≈ 0.95, and F2 ≈ 0.98 [PAGE 13]. Processing speed exceeded 10^6 events per hour for the algorithm versus roughly 20 events per hour for manual video analysis, saving over 12 hours on the modest dataset [PAGE 10][PAGE 13].

Gray-zone audit. The authors acknowledge that the vast majority of mouthguard triggers occurred off-camera and were labeled “unknown,” likely arising from chewing or mishandling of the device [PAGE 13]. A fully automated deployment without human oversight would flag these device-misuse events as impacts, inflating the operational false-positive burden beyond the vetted 88-event subset. The FE synthetic generator uses a passive dummy head-neck with no muscle activation or sex/age-specific anatomy, so its validity outside the simulated envelope (v_0 > 8 m/s, angles outside the sagittal-plane restriction) is an unquantified extrapolation risk [PAGE 5][PAGE 13]. Finally, all training and testing used the same athletes in the same sport; cross-sport or cross-population generalization is explicitly listed as a limitation [PAGE 13]. The exact class-weighting formula in the loss function is absent from the manuscript, making exact gradient dynamics unreproducible from the text alone [PAGE 7].

Formal operators, units, page citations, and breakdown.

Measurement and trigger operator. The mouthguard measurement vector at sample i is
\mathbf{m}_i = \big[a_x(i),\,a_y(i),\,a_z(i),\,\dot{\omega}_x(i),\,\dot{\omega}_y(i),\,\dot{\omega}_z(i)\big]^T,
where linear acceleration components a_j are recorded in units of g (1 g = 9.80665 m/s^2) and angular-rate components \omega_j are recorded in degrees/sec [PAGE 4]. Page 6 refers to the model input as “angular acceleration,” creating a unit-consistency gray zone because the hardware gyroscope measures angular velocity; any angular-acceleration channel is therefore derived by numerical differentiation [PAGE 4][PAGE 6].

The trigger operator is a binary indicator
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{m}) = \mathbb{1}\!\left\{\max_j |a_j(t)| \ge 10\,\mathrm{g}\right\}.
Events failing this inequality are not stored, producing a left-censored observation process [PAGE 4]. The acquisition window operator is
\mathcal{W}: t \in [t_{\mathrm{trig}} - 50\,\mathrm{ms},\; t_{\mathrm{trig}} + 150\,\mathrm{ms}],\qquad \Delta T = 200\,\mathrm{ms},
with f_{s,a} = 1000 Hz for acceleration and f_{s,\omega} = 8000 Hz for angular rate [PAGE 4][PAGE 6].

Finite-element simulation operator. The LS-Dyna explicit FE solver maps impact parameters to head-center-of-gravity kinematics
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{FE}}: (\alpha,\,\beta,\,z,\,v_0,\,\mathrm{offset}) \mapsto \{\mathbf{x}_{CG}(t),\,\mathbf{v}_{CG}(t),\,\mathbf{a}_{CG}(t),\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{CG}(t)\}_{t=0}^{150\,\mathrm{ms}}.
The parameter domain is \alpha \in [-180^\circ, 0^\circ] with \Delta\alpha = 30^\circ; \beta \in [-30^\circ, 30^\circ] with \Delta\beta = 10^\circ; v_0 \in \{2, 5, 8\}\,\mathrm{m/s}; vertical position z set by trial-and-error to distribute impacts and avoid the neck; and translational offset \in [5, 20] mm [PAGE 5]. The total number of simulated configurations is N_sim = 1065 [PAGE 5]. The solver uses single precision with shared-memory processing, a time step \Delta t = 0.01 ms, and outputs kinematics in the head coordinate system per SAE J211/1 [PAGE 5][PAGE 6].

Class imbalance and augmentation. Verified field counts are N_{\mathrm{true}} = 1024 and N_{\mathrm{false}} = 10990, giving a class ratio r = N_{\mathrm{false}}/N_{\mathrm{true}} \approx 10.73 [PAGE 4][PAGE 7]. Time-shift augmentation applies offsets \delta t \in \{1,\dots,5\}\,\mathrm{ms} with a maximum multiplicity of 5 per original signal, yielding approximately N_{\mathrm{true}}^{\mathrm{aug}} \approx 5100 and N_{\mathrm{false}}^{\mathrm{aug}} \approx 55000 training samples [PAGE 7]. The manuscript states that class weights were applied to the loss function but does not publish the exact weighting coefficients [PAGE 7].

Classification metrics. Let TP, TN, FP, FN denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. The source defines
\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},\qquad
\mathrm{NPV} = \frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN}}.
The F_X score is given on page 7 as
F_X = (1+X^2)\,\frac{\mathrm{Precision}\cdot\mathrm{Recall}}{X^2\cdot\mathrm{Precision} + \mathrm{Recall}},
with Precision = PPV and Recall = TP/(TP+FN) [PAGE 7]. X=1 gives the balanced F1 score; X=2 gives F2, weighted toward precision/recall trade-offs that penalize missed true impacts [PAGE 7].

Real-world test breakdown. On the Spring 2021 cohort of six participants, N = 88 mouthguard events were video-verified as 61 true impacts and 27 false impacts [PAGE 10][PAGE 13]. The impact detector returned 68 positive predictions and 20 negative predictions, with 7 false positives and 0 false negatives [PAGE 13]. Therefore:
\mathrm{PPV} = \frac{61}{61+7} \approx 0.897,\qquad
\mathrm{NPV} = \frac{20}{20+0} = 1.000,
\mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{61+20}{88} \approx 0.920,
F1 \approx 0.95,\qquad F2 \approx 0.98.
Processing throughput is approximately 20 events/hour (≈5.56\times10^{-3} events/s) for manual video analysis versus greater than 10^6 events/hour (>277.8 events/s) for the algorithm, with training times of roughly 2 hours for manual workflow setup versus 5 hours for the automated workflow [PAGE 10].

Singularity and breakdown regimes. The trigger exhibits a dead-zone singularity for \|a(t)\|_2 < 10 g; impacts below this threshold generate no data and are invisible to the detector [PAGE 4]. The FE synthetic generator is bounded to v_0 \le 8 m/s and the specified angular envelope; extrapolation to higher-energy or differently constrained impacts lies outside the validated simulation domain [PAGE 5][PAGE 13]. The “unknown” offscreen event class represents an unmodeled mixture of device misuse and non-impact kinematics that the detector cannot distinguish without video context [PAGE 13].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week exposure traps.

The paper sits at the intersection of three lineages. First, the computational-mechanics ancestry: the FE head-neck model is derived from an open-source, experimentally validated helmet test apparatus originally developed by Biomechanics Consulting and Research (Biocore), with the helmet components removed to expose the bare head-neck response [PAGE 5][ref 43]. This connects to prior Stanford FE helmet work such as Giudice et al. 2020 and Decker et al. 2020 [refs 17, 18], and to the broader physics-informed machine-learning tradition of Raissi et al. (physics-informed neural networks) [PAGE 3][ref 33]. Second, the wearable-sensing lineage: Stanford’s instrumented mouthguard program traces to Camarillo et al. 2013 and Liu et al. 2020, which validated mouthguards for measuring linear and angular head kinematics in American football [PAGE 1][refs 9, 15]. Third, the prior detection-algorithm lineage: Domel et al. 2020 established an open-access mTBI data platform and a convolutional neural network architecture that this work adapts [PAGE 6][ref 13]; earlier detectors include Wu et al. 2018 (SVM, F1=0.90, F2=0.88) and Gabler et al. 2020 (AdaBoost CART, F1=0.89, F2=0.84) [PAGE 11-12][refs 14, 16]. The present PIML detector reports F1=0.95 and F2=0.98, positioned as the best published result for American-football mouthguard impact detection [PAGE 11-12].

Multi-week traps emerge when this static, single-season prototype is exposed to longitudinal reality. The data were collected over 17 practice and game days across one collegiate team and three high schools in a bounded window (Spring 2021 for the real-world workflow test) [PAGE 4][PAGE 10]. There is no multi-week temporal validation. Over a full season, several distribution-shift traps are predictable. Mouthguard placement is unstable: the authors note improper placement, such as carrying the device in a sock during running, produces false impact signals [PAGE 2]. Chewing and device malfunction generate signals too complex for standard filtering to separate reliably [PAGE 2]. As players accumulate weeks of exposure, tackling technique, fatigue, and contact intensity change, shifting the kinematic signature of true impacts away from the synthetic FE envelope (v_0 \le 8 m/s, fixed impactor geometry) [PAGE 5]. The offscreen “unknown” event problem [PAGE 13] would compound over weeks: without a mechanism to verify whether a player is on camera at trigger time, an automated system deployed season-long would systematically mislabel device-misuse events as impacts. Because retraining requires new video-verified field data—a slow, manual bottleneck [PAGE 10]—the model risks catastrophic forgetting of early-season impact modes as late-season kinematics drift. No evidence in the manuscript addresses week-over-week calibration drift of the MiG2.0’s ±400 g accelerometer or ±4000 deg/s gyroscope, nor bias drift that could move the effective trigger threshold away from the nominal 10 g [PAGE 4].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock-layer analysis.

Crisis habit regression. When a high-stakes concussion event is missed or mismanaged, athletic-medical staff experience a crisis of confidence in automation. The documented regression habit is to return to manual video analysis as the epistemically “safe” ground truth, even though manual review is slow (~12 hours for a modest dataset, ~20 events/hour) and cognitively limited [PAGE 10][PAGE 13]. The algorithm’s aggregate F1 = 0.95 and F2 = 0.98 [PAGE 11-12] do not protect against this regression because a single salient false negative (FN = 0 in the 88-event test, but unobserved sub-10 g impacts are invisible by design [PAGE 4]) triggers institutional risk aversion. The loop is recursive: the more the algorithm is trusted, the less video review is performed, so the fewer ground-truth labels are collected to retrain it, increasing the probability that the next crisis is an undetected distribution shift.

Counter-trap. The inverse failure mode is automation bias induced by the detector’s high NPV and PPV on the vetted subset. Because NPV = 1.000 and PPV ≈ 0.90 in the real-world test [PAGE 13], staff may stop verifying sensor events with video entirely. The counter-trap is the offscreen event class: the authors state the vast majority of mouthguard impacts occurred offscreen and were labeled unknown, likely from chewing or mishandling [PAGE 13]. If the detector runs without human oversight, these non-impact device artifacts are classified as impacts, flooding the clinical workflow with false positives. The trap is self-reinforcing: high throughput (>10^6 events/hour [PAGE 10]) means the algorithm generates more flagged events than staff can manually triage, so the false-positive deluge is not caught by the very video-analysis workflow the algorithm was meant to replace.

Clock-layer mismatch. Two clocks operate at incompatible timescales. The sensor clock is fixed by the 10 g trigger and the 200 ms acquisition window (-50 ms to +150 ms) [PAGE 4]; the algorithm’s inference clock runs at >10^6 events/hour [PAGE 10]; the human validation clock runs at ~20 events/hour [PAGE 10]. Because inference is orders of magnitude faster than verification, the system can label thousands of events before a human reviews one. If the game clock, video timestamp, and mouthguard trigger timestamp are not precisely synchronized, the 200 ms window may capture pre-impact noise or post-impact recovery rather than the impact centroid, degrading the CNN’s time-shift-invariant assumptions (the model was augmented only by ±5 ms shifts [PAGE 7]). The recursive risk is that the algorithm’s speed creates a backlog of unverified predictions, and the human clock can never catch up, causing the “ground truth” used for performance reporting to become stale relative to the live data stream.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical layer veto. The synthetic training population is a Hybrid III 50th percentile male anthropomorphic dummy head-neck model [PAGE 5]. This is a hard physical veto for application to pediatric, female, or older athletes whose head-neck mass, neck stiffness, and brain morphometry differ from the 50th percentile male. The field data included high-school players of mixed positions (except kicker) [PAGE 4], but the synthetic physics prior does not span that anatomical variance. Any deployment outside the tested demographic must be treated as out-of-distribution until the FE model is re-parameterized.

Biomechanical layer veto. The impactor is a nylon spherical cap backed by vinyl nitrile foam and a metal plate, driven by a translational ram of total mass 15.6 kg against a 17.7 kg sliding carriage [PAGE 5]. This apparatus does not include helmet-shell compliance, face-mask interaction, neck-muscle co-contraction, or helmet-to-helmet reduced-mass dynamics. The authors themselves concede the scope of real impact types is larger than this modest setup can reproduce and that more motor-functional control would be needed for true-to-life impacts [PAGE 13]. Veto condition: real-world impacts with effective closing velocity above 8 m/s, or oblique contact geometries outside the sagittal-plane-restricted (\alpha \in [-180^\circ,0^\circ], \beta \in [-30^\circ,30^\circ]) envelope [PAGE 5], lie beyond the simulated training manifold. The detector’s NPV and PPV are not guaranteed under those conditions.

Weather and environmental layer veto. The manuscript contains no modeling of temperature, humidity, or thermal drift in the MiG2.0 IMUs or in the LS-Dyna material parameters [PAGE 4][PAGE 5]. Accelerometer bias drift can shift the effective trigger threshold away from the nominal 10 g [PAGE 4]; in extreme cold or heat, the probability of missing a true impact (false negative) or triggering on noise (false positive) changes without a corresponding recalibration event in the training data. Because the device triggers at a fixed acceleration magnitude and stores only a 200 ms window, environmental sensor drift is not observable from the saved CSV files alone. Veto condition: deployment in uncontrolled outdoor stadium conditions without pre-game IMU bias calibration invalidates the nominal 10 g trigger assumption.

Adjudication outcome. The physics-informed model passes internal cross-validation on the same-athlete, same-sport, Spring 2021 test set (F1 = 0.95, F2 = 0.98 [PAGE 11-12]), but it fails the cross-layer veto for (a) non-50th-percentile-male anatomy, (b) impact energies or contact mechanics outside the 2–8 m/s dummy-impactor envelope, and (c) uncalibrated environmental sensor drift. The model may be used alongside manual video analysis as a triage filter, but it cannot replace video review without violating the physical and biomechanical preconditions of its training data.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-via-verifiable-rewards veto scenarios with predictions.

Veto Scenario 1 — Sub-threshold clinical impact (trigger singularity). Verifiable protocol: instrument a cohort with a reference-grade head-accelerometer recording continuously from 0 g alongside the MiG2.0, then correlate concussion diagnoses with all head-acceleration events, including those below 10 g. Prediction: because the MiG2.0 only stores data once \|a(t)\|_2 \ge 10 g [PAGE 4], any concussive event below threshold is structurally absent from the detector’s input space. If 15% of clinically relevant head impacts in the cohort occur below 10 g, the algorithm’s recall on all clinically relevant impacts falls below 0.85 despite the reported triggered-event F1 of 0.95 [PAGE 11-12]. The veto is triggered if reference-sensor data show a non-zero rate of sub-10 g impacts associated with injury symptoms.

Veto Scenario 2 — Offscreen device-misuse flood (operational false-positive veto). Verifiable protocol: timestamp every mouthguard trigger over a full fall season and tag each event as onscreen or offscreen using synchronized video; compare algorithm labels to video ground truth separately for the two strata. Prediction: the authors report the vast majority of mouthguard impacts occurred offscreen and were labeled unknown, likely from chewing or mishandling [PAGE 13]. If more than 50% of season triggers are offscreen device misuse, the fully automated workflow will generate a false-positive volume that exceeds manual triage capacity (>10^6 events/hour algorithm vs. ~20 events/hour human [PAGE 10]), collapsing the operational PPV below 0.50 even though the vetted 88-event subset showed PPV ≈ 0.90 [PAGE 13]. The veto is triggered when the offscreen false-positive rate exceeds the staff’s review bandwidth.

Veto Scenario 3 — High-energy domain shift (biomechanical extrapolation veto). Verifiable protocol: collect an independent test set of helmet-to-helmet and helmet-to-ground impacts with measured closing velocities up to 10–12 m/s (e.g., from collegiate or NFL impact sensors), then evaluate the pre-trained detector without retraining. Prediction: the FE synthetic data are limited to v_0 \in \{2,5,8\} m/s and a single nylon-cap impactor geometry [PAGE 5]; real high-energy helmet impacts involve greater effective mass and different contact compliance. Under this domain shift, the detector’s NPV falls below 0.70 and F1 below 0.80 because high-energy impulse signatures were never represented in the synthetic training manifold. The veto is triggered when the independent high-energy test set yields NPV < 0.70.
