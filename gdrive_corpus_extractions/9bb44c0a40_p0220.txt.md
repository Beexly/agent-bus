<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

The theoretical lineage tracing back to Hull's foundational 1943 Principles of Behavior chapter on compound conditioned stimuli interweaves multiple scientific genealogies. The core hypothesis—that the combined elicitation magnitude \(R_{r+t}\) of two distinct conditioned stimuli \(S_r\) and \(S_t\) presented simultaneously falls short of their arithmetic sum \(R_r + R_t\), instead approaching \(\frac{2}{3}(R_r+R_t)\)—resonates most directly with:

1. **Ivan Pavlov's "Actual Dynamics of the Nervous System" (1935)**: Pavlov himself observed such "temporal dynamics" where complex stimuli produced less than predicted simple effects. He termed it "internal contradiction," implying interference during summation. Hull cites him extensively—particularly regarding the dissociation of excitatory and inhibitory gradients—and aligns this concept with his own notation (\(R_1 + R_t - \Delta R\)). Yet while Pavlov emphasized physiological tension or ambiguity, Hull frames it quantitatively via subtractive decrement \(\Delta R\).

2. **Donald T. Campbell's Interactionism (1940s)**: Building on Hull's published data, Campbell's theory of "random capitalized partial reinforcement" would later refine the operational definition of extinction. Campbell argued that reinforcers ("R") in conditioning could interact multiplicatively rather than additively due to attentional competition or processing capacity limits. The 0.67 constant thus may foreshadow power-additivity models in cognitive psychology's dual-process theories.

3. **B.F. Skinner's Operationism**: Simultaneously developing independent laboratory methodologies, Skinner rejected unobservables like "neural dynamics." For him, the apparent shrinkage represented *reinforcer substitution*—the compound might act as a single "supra-stimulus." This contrast highlights Hull's attempt to situate the constant within mechanistic laws governing habit strength.
