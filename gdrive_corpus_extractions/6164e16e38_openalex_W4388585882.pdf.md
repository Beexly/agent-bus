<THINK_TRACE>
The drive source is a computer-vision paper on Sports Ball Detection and Tracking, not a probabilistic market document. Its central derivation is: small, fast, occluded balls in sports video create severe foreground-background imbalance; therefore the authors replace low-resolution encoder-decoder fusion with high-resolution HRNet-style feature extraction, replace binary ground-truth maps with real-valued position-aware maps, mine hard-to-localize samples, and add temporal consistency at inference. The source states the task as “our goal is to detect a (x,y)-coordinate of ball location from each image in a given video clip” [PAGE 3], and the motivating difficulty is “small size of a sports ball, its high speed, occlusion, blending in with surroundings, and camera motion [96]” [PAGE 1]. The multi-hop chain is: tiny ball in a large image → heatmap prediction with class imbalance → binary GT map destroys sub-pixel positional information → quality focal loss on real-valued maps improves localization sensitivity → HLSM applies real-valued maps only to hard samples → CoH uses heatmap mass rather than blob geometry → online tracking filters inconsistent candidates using Eq. 4 → oversampling gives more MIMO candidates. Each step is coupled: higher-resolution features increase compute cost; real-valued maps require a distance kernel with a threshold; temporal filtering requires prior detections; oversampling improves candidate diversity but lowers throughput. The paper’s own ablation supports the chain: removing the high-resolution stem modification lowers soccer F1 from 88.3 to 81.7, tennis F1 from 94.0 to 85.5, badminton F1 from 91.6 to 86.8, volleyball F1 from 86.5 to 84.3, and basketball F1 from 80.6 to 77.4 [PAGE 10, Table 3]. Adding HLSM, CoH, online tracking, and Step=1 changes soccer AP from 80.1 to 86.2, tennis AP from 88.5 to 94.2, and badminton AP from 85.5 to 91.6 [PAGE 10, Table 4].

The gray-zone audit has several layers. First, benchmark construction is not neutral: the authors introduce two datasets, manually re-annotate Soccer and Basketball, and re-implement six SOTA methods [PAGE 2, PAGE 6]. They explicitly say the original Soccer annotations “are collapsed and do not localize ball position correctly” [PAGE 6]. Second, validation of the re-implementations is incomplete: only TrackNetV2 on Badminton is checked against the original paper, where their implementation scores 85.6 versus 85.2 in the original Table IV [PAGE 7]. Third, the metric threshold is fixed at τ = 4 pixels in Table 2, then swept in Figure 6; the paper claims WASB “consistently achieves higher performance with most of the threshold settings” [PAGE 9], not every setting. Fourth, runtime is a coupled tradeoff: Step=1 raises soccer AP from 83.6 to 86.2 but lowers FPS from 55.7 to 23.6; tennis AP rises from 91.0 to 94.2 while FPS falls from 58.2 to 35.2; badminton AP rises from 88.5 to 91.6 while FPS falls from 70.4 to 34.3 [PAGE 8, Table 2]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(p=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\) be an image-plane location in pixels, and let \(p^{GT}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\) be the ground-truth ball location in pixels. Let \(d\) be a distance threshold in pixels. The binary GT map used by prior methods is [PAGE 4]:
\[
y^{bin}_{p}=
\begin{cases}
1, & \|p-p^{GT}\|_{2}\le d,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Here \(y^{bin}_{p}\in\{0,1\}\) is dimensionless; \(d\) has units of pixels. Breakdown regimes: if \(d\) is smaller than the pixel-grid spacing, no pixel may be labeled positive; if \(d\) is too large, the map labels a neighborhood and destroys exact localization. The paper uses \(d=2.5\) pixels in its implementation [PAGE 8].

The position-aware real-valued GT map is [PAGE 4]:
\[
y^{real}_{p}=
\begin{cases}
\min\left(C\exp\left(-\frac{\|p-p^{GT}\|_{2}^{2}}{d^{2}}\right),1\right), & \|p-p^{GT}\|_{2}\le d,\\
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(y^{real}_{p}\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless, \(C\) is dimensionless, and \(C\) is set so that the nonzero minimum value equals \(c_{\min}\). The paper uses \(c_{\min}=0.7\) [PAGE 8]. The exponent is dimensionless because both numerator and denominator are squared pixel lengths. Exact singularities and breakdowns: at \(d=0\) the exponent is undefined; if no grid point lies within \(d\), \(C\) is undefined; at \(\|p-p^{GT}\|=d\) the first branch is generally nonzero while the second is zero, creating a boundary discontinuity unless \(C\exp(-1)=0\); if \(c_{\min}\) is outside the allowed heatmap range, the map cannot be formed as specified.

The quality focal loss is [PAGE 5]:
\[
\mathcal{L}
=
\sum_{p}
\left[
-|y_{p}-\sigma_{p}|^{\beta_{n}}
\left((1-y_{p})\log(1-\sigma_{p})+y_{p}\log\sigma_{p}\right)
\right],
\]
where \(\sigma_{p}\in[0,1]\) is the sigmoid output probability at \(p\), \(y_{p}\in[0,1]\) is the GT value, and \(\beta_{n}\) is a dimensionless down-weighting parameter. If natural logarithms are used, \(\mathcal{L}\) is in nats; as a sum over pixels it is an aggregate dimensionless information loss. The paper notes Equation 3 “is equivalent to the focal loss [49] if GT is binary” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\sigma_{p}=0\) and \(y_{p}>0\), or if \(\sigma_{p}=1\) and \(y_{p}<1\), the log terms diverge; if \(y_{p}=\sigma_{p}\), the prefactor is zero; if \(\beta_{n}=0\), the loss reduces to binary cross-entropy-like terms without quality down-weighting.

The local motion model is [PAGE 6]:
\[
\hat p_{t+1}=p_{t}+v_{t}+\frac{a_{t}}{2},
\quad
v_{t}=p_{t}-p_{t-1}+a_{t},
\quad
a_{t}=p_{t}-2p_{t-1}+p_{t-2}.
\]
Here \(p_{t}\) is in pixels; under a discrete frame index, \(v_{t}\) is in pixels per frame and \(a_{t}\) is in pixels per frame squared, though the written equation is only dimensionally homogeneous if the frame step is normalized to one and the acceleration term is interpreted in discrete units. Breakdown regimes: the equation requires \(t\ge2\); it is undefined at the start of a sequence; it fails if detections at \(t-1\) or \(t-2\) are missing; it mispredicts under camera cuts, rapid zoom, or variable frame rate. The paper uses this only to “filter out inconsistent detection candidates” and explicitly says “we do not use filtering algorithms such as Kalman filter ... and particle filter ..., since any performance improvement was not observed with them” [PAGE 6].

Other operators: the MIMO input tensor is \(X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3N}\) and output is \(Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times N}\), with \(N=3\) and image resize to \(288\times512\) [PAGE 8]. CoH computes a ball position as “the center of heatmap values” and confidence as “a sum of heatmap values in the blob” [PAGE 6]. HLSM runs after a pre-defined epoch, uses inference over training sequences to find images where predicted positions are far from GT, then applies Equation 2 only to those images [PAGE 5]. Metrics are F1, Accuracy, and Average Precision at a distance threshold \(\tau=4\) pixels [PAGE 8], all reported as percentages; FPS has units of frames per second; parameter counts are dimensionless counts. The reported average ball displacement in Table 1 has units of pixels per frame [PAGE 7].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from classical tracking-by-detection to CNN heatmap detectors to high-resolution representation learning. The paper describes classical methods as “based on tracking-by-detection paradigm: Ball candidates are first detected from each video frame, then a true trajectory is recovered by associating the candidates through time” [PAGE 2]. That lineage then moves to encoder-decoder CNNs such as DeepBall, BallSeg, TrackNet, and MonoTrack, which produce heatmaps but often lose spatial resolution or treat temporal dynamics only inside a mini-batch [PAGE 2]. WASB retroactively reinterprets this history: the missing ingredients are high-resolution features from HRNet-style modules, position-aware GT maps from quality focal loss, and inference-time temporal consistency. The paper cites HRNet-family work [82, 92] for high-resolution modules, focal loss [49] and quality focal loss [44, 45] for imbalance and localization quality, and TrackNetV2 [75] and MonoTrack [50] for MIMO temporal stacking. The “retrocausal” reading is that later components explain earlier failures: if a tiny ball occupies few pixels, low-resolution decoder fusion is a bottleneck; if binary GT maps make position obscure, localization is insensitive; if inference ignores temporal consistency, ball-like objects cause false positives. The paper’s result that “WASB results dominate the best and the second-best SBDT performance over the most metrics” [PAGE 8] is therefore a synthesis of older lines, not a completely isolated invention.

Multi-week experimental traps are visible in the source. Trap one is annotation comparability: Soccer’s original annotations are “collapsed and do not localize ball position correctly” [PAGE 6], so historical Soccer numbers cannot be mixed with the re-annotated benchmark. Trap two is re-implementation drift: only TrackNetV2 is validated against an original score, 85.6 versus 85.2 [PAGE 7]; DeepBall, BallSeg, MonoTrack, DeepBall-Large, and ResTrackNetV2 lack comparable reference implementations or public benchmarks [PAGE 8]. Trap three is metric-threshold drift: Table 2 fixes \(\tau=4\) pixels, while Figure 6 sweeps thresholds from 2 to 5 pixels [PAGE 9]; a multi-week campaign that changes \(\tau\) midstream can manufacture apparent progress. Trap four is throughput drift: Step=1 oversampling improves AP but collapses FPS on Soccer, Tennis, and Badminton [PAGE 8, Table 2], so an AP-only dashboard hides the real-time cost. Trap five is training-clock drift: HLSM is run at the beginning of epoch 20 and more trials show no improvement [PAGE 8]; changing the epoch schedule changes the effective model. Trap six is temporal-clock drift: Volleyball and Basketball FPS are “N/A” in Table 1 [PAGE 7], yet Eq. 4 assumes a consistent frame-to-frame motion model. Trap seven is domain-width drift: the paper validates five sports but limits itself to one ball per frame and standard HD/FHD, 25-30 FPS video [PAGE 10]. These traps do not invalidate the baseline; they define the conditions under which its genealogy can be reproduced.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when a small-ball detector fails on noisy backgrounds, occlusion, or camera motion. The habitual response is to add a Kalman filter, particle filter, or tracking-by-detection post-processor. The source itself warns that this habit is not automatically useful: “we do not use filtering algorithms such as Kalman filter ... and particle filter ..., since any performance improvement was not observed with them” [PAGE 6]. The regression risk is therefore not that filters are forbidden; it is that a crisis response adds a familiar layer without proving it improves the actual benchmark. A second regression is reverting to binary GT maps and focal loss when real-valued maps are hard to tune; the paper shows HLSM and CoH improve AP on the tested datasets [PAGE 10, Table 4], but HLSM is only applied to hard-to-localize samples, not all data [PAGE 5]. A third regression is treating “more frames” as automatically better; MIMO with \(N=3\) is a design choice, not a physical law [PAGE 8].

The counter-trap is the evaluator’s model of the model. The evaluator may assume ground truth is correct, but the paper’s error analysis says model detections “seem to capture true ball positions ... more correctly than manually annotated ground truths” [PAGE 12]. That reverses the usual hierarchy: the model can surpass the annotation, so a counter-trap is to optimize against noisy labels and call the result error. Another counter-trap is threshold gaming: \(\tau=4\) pixels in Table 2, threshold sweep in Figure 6, and “most of the threshold settings” rather than all [PAGE 9]. A third is dataset-specific tuning: Soccer, Tennis, Badminton, Volleyball, and Basketball have different average displacements and resolutions [PAGE 7, Table 1]; a single hyperparameter set may hide per-domain regressions. A fourth is oversampling: Step=1 produces more candidates and higher AP but lower FPS [PAGE 8, Table 2]; an agent that only sees AP will over-oversample.

The clock layer is both temporal and operational. Eq. 4 predicts \(\hat p_{t+1}\) from \(p_t,p_{t-1},p_{t-2}\) [PAGE 6], which assumes a stable frame clock; Volleyball and Basketball FPS are unknown in Table 1 [PAGE 7], and Basketball has “camera frequently moves and zooms in rapidly” [PAGE 7]. The training clock is epoch 20 for HLSM [PAGE 8]. The inference clock is FPS: WASB is “over 30 FPS on 4 out of 5 datasets” with Step=3 [PAGE 9], but Step=1 drops below that on several datasets [PAGE 8]. Recursive theory of mind therefore has three agents: the designer assumes smooth ball motion; the evaluator assumes annotation truth; the user assumes wide applicability. Each assumption can fail independently. The correct counter-trap is not to add more habits, but to verify which clock and which annotation regime the claim depends on.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical veto requires a calibrated image-plane displacement bound. Let \(s\) be the camera scale in pixels per metre, \(v_{\max}\) the maximum plausible ball speed in metres per second, and \(f\) the frame rate in frames per second. A candidate can be physically vetoed when
\[
\|\hat p_{t+1}-p_t\|_{2} > \frac{s\,v_{\max}}{f}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{cam}},
\]
where the left side is in pixels per frame, \(s v_{\max}/f\) is in pixels per frame, and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{cam}}\) is a camera-motion compensation term in pixels per frame. This is a real kinematic bound, not a toy ratio. The source does not provide \(s\), \(v_{\max}\), or a full calibration; Table 1 provides only average ball displacement in pixels per frame [PAGE 7], and FPS is unknown for Volleyball and Basketball. Therefore a strict physical veto cannot be certified from the paper alone. It can only be certified for fixed-camera, known-FPS clips with calibration and camera-motion compensation.

A biomechanical veto requires player/limb/occluder segmentation. The source’s Soccer error analysis says detection is “not precisely aligned due to the noisy background (e.g., player shoes)” [PAGE 12]. Thus a candidate near a player’s foot, leg, or equipment can be vetoed if a player-instance mask and a ball-appearance model both reject it. The paper does not provide player bounding boxes or pose labels in its SBDT benchmark, so this veto is qualitatively motivated but not quantitatively reproducible from the source. A weather/visibility veto requires radiometric or contrast measurements. The paper mentions “blending in with surroundings” [PAGE 1] and “noisy background” [PAGE 12], but it does not report rain, glare, shadow, or lighting labels. There is no weather variable in Table 1 or the experimental protocol [PAGE 7, PAGE 8]. Therefore a weather-layer veto cannot be proven from the source; it can only be asserted as an unvalidated regime.

Adjudication: Eq. 4 provides a temporal-consistency veto for candidates far from the predicted position, but the paper only says it filters inconsistent candidates and does not use Kalman or particle filters [PAGE 6]. The threshold for “farther” is not specified in the main text. HLSM provides a training-time veto by flagging images where predicted positions are far from GT and then switching those images to real-valued maps [PAGE 5]. CoH provides a confidence veto by using heatmap mass rather than blob size [PAGE 6]. The cross-layer proof is therefore conditional: physical veto is valid only with calibration and known FPS; biomechanical veto only with player masks; weather veto only with visibility labels. The source’s own limitation—single ball, standard HD/FHD, 25-30 FPS [PAGE 10]—means a universal cross-layer veto cannot be adjudicated from this paper. The in-distribution benchmark survives; the universal physical claim does not.
