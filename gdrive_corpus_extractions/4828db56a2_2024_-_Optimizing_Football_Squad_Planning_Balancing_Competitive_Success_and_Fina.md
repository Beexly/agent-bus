The author states the Sporting Director’s role is “to assemble the strongest squad” [PAGE 1 of 27], but also concedes that “these decisions are often made based on subjective evaluations, with no clear structure that integrates qualitative and quantitative analyses” [PAGE 1 of 27]. Multi-hop derivation. Module 1 collects basic data, performance statistics, and financial attributes; the performance data are “from the last two seasons of the Premier League (2022/2023 and 2023/2024)” and “event data provided by StatsBomb,” while market data are obtained through “a Web Scrapping to TransferMarkt web site” [PAGE 7 of 27]. Module 2 defines a score \(Score_{p,t}\) as a position-weighted linear combination of offensive and defensive OBV percentiles:
\[
Score_{p,t}=w^{f_p}_{\mathrm{for}}\,OBV^{for}_{p,t}+w^{f_p}_{\mathrm{against}}\,OBV^{against}_{p,t}.
\]
The weights are position-specific; Appendix A gives, for example, \(w_{\mathrm{for}}=0.1,w_{\mathrm{against}}=0.9\) for GK and \(w_{\mathrm{for}}=0.9,w_{\mathrm{against}}=0.1\) for CF [PAGE 23 of 27]. The considered weights were “proposed and discussed between Sporting Director and the club’s staff” [PAGE 9 of 27], so the score is not purely data-derived. Module 3 then projects score and log market value using age, age-squared, and position:
\[
\widehat{score}_{p,t+1}\sim score_{p,t}+age_p+age_p^2+position,
\]
\[
\widehat{\log(market\ value)}_{p,t+1}\sim \log(market\ value)_{p,t}+age_p+age_p^2+position.
\]
The paper reports LASSO as best for score with RMSE 13.2 and MAE 10.6, and best for log-market-value with RMSE 0.41 and MAE 0.31 [PAGE 14 of 27 and PAGE 15 of 27]. Module 5 maximizes \(\sum_{p,t}x_{p,t}q_{p,t}\) subject to budget, youth, squad-structure, age, and score-trajectory constraints [PAGE 11 of 27 and PAGE 12 of 27].

The first gray-zone audit is statistical propagation. If per-player score RMSE is \(13.2\) score-points and a squad has \(n\) independently predicted players, the aggregate prediction standard deviation is approximately
\[
\sigma_{\Sigma}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_i^2}=\sqrt{n}\,\sigma_i.
\]
For \(n=23\), \(\sigma_{\Sigma}\approx 63.3\) score-points per season; for four seasons and independence, \(\sqrt{23\cdot4}\cdot13.2\approx126.6\) score-points. Meanwhile the reported budget sensitivity covers only \(7820-7751=69\) total score-points between EUR 100 million and EUR 10 million [PAGE 19 of 27 and PAGE 20 of 27]. Thus the model’s claimed budget effect can be smaller than the propagated prediction noise unless scores are averaged, shrunk, or otherwise regularized. The paper does not report this propagation, so the “optimal” squad differences may be statistically fragile.

The second gray-zone audit is constraint logic. The paper says “Each season the overall score must increase” [PAGE 12 of 27], but the displayed inequality in the source is a \(\leq\) relation between the current and prior season aggregates. As transcribed, that enforces non-increase, not increase. If the RHS is indeed \(t-1\), the solver can only produce flat or declining later-season totals, conflicting with the first-season increase reported on PAGE 18 of 27. If the RHS is \(t+1\), the notation is ambiguous. This is an exact breakdown regime: with a reversed sign, the feasible set excludes the claimed monotone-improvement policy. A second logic issue is the position-count constraint: the source writes \(\sum_p x_{p,t} f_p \leq k_f\) [PAGE 12 of 27], but \(f_p\) is a categorical position. A mathematically valid count requires an indicator \(1[f_p=f]\), otherwise the inequality sums position codes rather than players. Third, the paper promises budget constraints for “player acquisitions and salaries” [PAGE 11 of 27], but the displayed budget uses \(m_{p,t}\), described as market value, not salary. Fourth, “Min. Contract Length 2” appears in the parameter table [PAGE 16 of 27] but no corresponding minimum-contract constraint appears in the formulation. Fifth, the text mentions “maximum number of foreign players” [PAGE 11 of 27] but no foreign-player constraint is shown.

The third gray-zone audit is the optimality claim. The paper states “The results of the first instance show that the problem is solvable in just 5 seconds with an optimality gap of 0.1%” [PAGE 18 of 27]. That is a solver-gap statement, not a proof that the model is correctly specified. A gap of 0.1 percent on a total objective near 7820 is roughly \(7.82\) score-points, which is smaller than the LASSO RMSE but not negligible relative to the EUR 20 million versus EUR 10 million difference of \(7792-7751=41\) points [PAGE 20 of 27]. The derivation therefore separates three error layers: measurement error in OBV percentiles, prediction error in future score and market value, and optimization error from the 0.1 percent gap plus constraint misspecification.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are formal and position-conditioned. Let \(p\in P\) index players, \(t\in T\) seasons, \(f\in F\) field positions, and \(e\in E\) the ten event types listed in Table 1 [PAGE 8 of 27]. The score is
\[
Score_{p,t}=w^{f_p}_{\mathrm{for}}OBV^{for}_{p,t}+w^{f_p}_{\mathrm{against}}OBV^{against}_{p,t},
\]
with \(Score_{p,t}\in[0,100]\) as reported [PAGE 7 of 27]. The OBV aggregates are
\[
OBV^{for}_{p,t}=\sum_{e}v^{f_p}_{e,\mathrm{for}}\,\overline{OBV}^{for}_{p,t,e},
\qquad
OBV^{against}_{p,t}=\sum_{e}v^{f_p}_{e,\mathrm{against}}\,\overline{OBV}^{against}_{p,t,e},
\]
where the overline denotes the percentile-relative contribution. The paper defines the index as the player’s average contribution per action minus the average contribution of same-position league players [PAGE 8 of 27]:
\[
\overline{OBV}^{for}_{p,t,e}=\overline{OBV}^{player,for}_{p,t,e}-\overline{OBV}^{position,for}_{e},
\]
\[
\overline{OBV}^{against}_{p,t,e}=\overline{OBV}^{player,against}_{p,t,e}-\overline{OBV}^{position,against}_{e}.
\]
Units: \(Score\) is dimensionless on a 0–100 scale; \(OBV\) and the weights \(v,w\) are dimensionless. Assumption: Appendix A weights sum to one by position and Appendix B weights sum to one by event and position; this preserves the 0–100 scale. The exact singularity regimes are: if the same-position peer set has size zero, the position mean is undefined; if position assignment by modal minutes is tied, \(f_p\) is undefined; if minutes are zero, position and percentile are undefined; if any event count is zero for all same-position players, the percentile contrast is undefined or zero by convention, but the paper states no convention.

Module 3 operators are predictive regressions:
\[
\widehat{score}_{p,t+1}\sim score_{p,t}+age_p+age_p^2+position,
\]
\[
\widehat{\log(market\ value)}_{p,t+1}\sim \log(market\ value)_{p,t}+age_p+age_p^2+position.
\]
Units: age is years; score RMSE and MAE are score-points; log-market-value errors are log-units, nats if the natural log is used. The paper does not state the log base [PAGE 10 of 27], so the back-transform is ambiguous. If natural log is assumed, RMSE \(0.41\) corresponds roughly to a multiplicative error factor \(\exp(0.41)\approx1.51\), and MAE \(0.31\) to \(\exp(0.31)\approx1.36\). Exact breakdown regimes: \(\log(market\ value)\) is undefined for \(m\leq0\); missing TransferMarkt values break the regression; perfect collinearity between \(age\) and \(age^2\) is not exact unless age is constant, but a narrow age range can make the quadratic unstable; LASSO can zero out position dummies, which the SHAP section reports as minimal impact [PAGE 15 of 27].

The integer program is:
\[
\max \sum_{p\in P}\sum_{t\in T}x_{p,t}q_{p,t}
\]
subject to
\[
\sum_p hireaux_{p,t}m_{p,t}\leq CAP_{hire}\quad \forall t,
\]
\[
\sum_p x_{p,t}h_{p,t}\geq num_{youth}\quad \forall t,
\]
\[
\sum_p x_{p,t}1[f_p=f]\leq k_f\quad \forall f,t,
\]
\[
\sum_p x_{p,t}=\sum_f k_f\quad \forall t,
\]
\[
x_{p,t}age_{p,t}\leq max\_age\quad \forall p,t,
\]
\[
\sum_p x_{p,t}q_{p,t}\geq \sum_p x_{p,t-1}q_{p,t-1}\quad \forall t,
\]
\[
x_{p,t}+y_{p,t}=1,\qquad y_{p,t}-y_{p,t-1}\leq hireaux_{p,t-1}.
\]
The source displays the score-trajectory constraint with a \(\leq\) sign [PAGE 12 of 27]; I have written the logically intended \(\geq\) form to expose the sign problem. Units: \(q_{p,t}\) is score-points; \(m_{p,t}\) and \(CAP_{hire}\) are EUR; \(k_f\) and \(num_{youth}\) are player counts; \(age\) is years; \(x,y,hireaux\) are binary dimensionless. Exact breakdown regimes: infeasibility when \(CAP_{hire}\) is below the least-cost feasible squad; infeasibility when \(k_f\), squad-size equality, youth minimum, and max age cannot be jointly satisfied; infeasibility when \(t=1\) has no prior season for the trajectory constraint; incorrect raw \(f_p\) summation can produce meaningless or infeasible position counts; omitted minimum contract length and foreign-player constraints allow solutions that violate the stated policy; and if \(hireaux\) can be set to one without an actual hire, the budget can be wasted without changing the objective.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicit. It begins with workforce planning as “analyzing the current workforce, determining future needs, and identifying the gap” [PAGE 3 of 27], citing Anderson [1] and the four-step supply-demand-gap-solution process. It imports Bruecker et al. [2] on hierarchical and categorical skills, then NAPA [3] on “Linkage to Other Plans” so that workforce plans are financially viable [PAGE 4 of 27]. The football adaptation appears in Tworek et al. [4] on workforce diversity and performance [PAGE 4 of 27]. Player-selection lineage comes from Boon and Sierksma [5] and Ahmed et al. [6] on integer and multi-objective team selection [PAGE 5 of 27]. Performance-measurement lineage moves from Rösch et al. [7] on physical dimensions through Pappalardo et al. [8] on public event data, He et al. [9], Pariath et al. [10], Onwuachu and Enyindah [11], and Sayan and Hançer [12] [PAGE 5 of 27 and PAGE 6 of 27]. The source thus retrocausally joins operations research, sports economics, event-data analytics, machine learning, and contract management.

The retrocausal trap is that future projections are fed backward into present choices. A player’s current \(score_{p,t}\) and \(\log(market\ value)_{p,t}\) determine projected future values [PAGE 10 of 27]; those projected values then determine current hiring, retention, and sale decisions [PAGE 11 of 27]. This creates a multi-week and multi-window trap: TransferMarkt values are scraped at one time [PAGE 7 of 27], but transfer windows and contract negotiations unfold over weeks; agents and rival clubs can observe or infer the club’s valuation model and adjust demands. A second trap is age-curve extrapolation: the model uses \(age\) and \(age^2\), so a player near a projected peak can be bought at a premium just as the quadratic turns downward. A third trap is the two-season training base: the score model is trained on 2022/2023 and 2023/2024 Premier League event data [PAGE 7 of 27], so a tactical regime change, league switch, or injury return can make the projected profile stale. A fourth trap is contract-clock asymmetry: the parameter table includes “Min. Contract Length 2” [PAGE 16 of 27] but the formulation does not encode it, so the optimizer can recommend one-season fixes that look good in the horizon and fail in the next window.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The decision environment is recursive because the Sporting Director models the staff, the staff model the Director, agents model the club’s budget and urgency, and rival clubs model the club’s likely targets. The paper’s own admission that decisions are “often made based on subjective evaluations” [PAGE 1 of 27] means the model enters an organization with pre-existing beliefs. Crisis habit regression occurs when budget pressure or a poor run of results causes the Director to abandon the five-module pipeline and return to eye-test judgment; the model’s weights were already “proposed and discussed between Sporting Director and the club’s staff” [PAGE 9 of 27], so the regression can be invisible because the subjective prior is embedded in the weights. The counter-trap is over-trust: if the Director treats the LASSO projection as a precise forecast, an agent can exploit the club’s known threshold by demanding a premium for a player whose projected score is just above the selection cutoff. The clock trap is contract expiry and window timing: the model’s horizon is finite, but negotiations have deadlines; waiting for a projected market-value peak can collapse into a forced sale or a lost renewal. The correct recursive policy is to maintain an explicit belief state over model reliability, staff disagreement, agent reservation prices, and contract clocks, then veto any recommendation whose expected value is not robust to those beliefs. The paper’s first-season concentration of spending [PAGE 18 of 27] is especially exposed to this trap: the model front-loads improvement, which signals urgency to agents and weakens the club’s bargaining position in later windows.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source model has no physical, biomechanical, or weather variables in its player-characteristic list [PAGE 7 of 27] and no such constraints in the integer program [PAGE 11 of 27 and PAGE 12 of 27]. The veto proof is therefore exogenous. Let \(\alpha_{p,t}\in[0,1]\) be the probability that player \(p\) is available in season \(t\), and let \(\eta_{p,t}\in[0,1]\) be a bounded attenuation factor for weather and biomechanical load. The effective contribution is
\[
u_{p,t}=q_{p,t}\,\alpha_{p,t}\,\eta_{p,t}.
\]
If \(\alpha_{p,t}=0\), then \(u_{p,t}=0\) regardless of \(q_{p,t}\); selecting that player consumes budget and squad space while contributing zero expected score. If a feasible substitute \(p'\) has \(u_{p',t}>0\) and no greater cost, the selection of \(p\) is dominated. That is a physical veto. For biomechanics, let \(L_{p,t}\) be match load in minutes or seconds and let \(\lambda_{p,t}\geq0\) be a hazard per unit load with units \(\mathrm{season}^{-1}\); a simple survival model gives \(\alpha_{p,t}=\exp(-\lambda_{p,t}L_{p,t})\). If load exceeds a tissue-specific threshold, the hazard rises and the expected score falls. For weather, let \(w\) be WBGT in degrees Celsius; a bounded attenuation such as \(\eta(w)=\max(0,1-\gamma\max(0,w-w_0))\) with \(\gamma\) in \(\mathrm{^\circ C}^{-1}\) shows that heat stress can veto a high-score player who is not acclimatized. The paper’s score is therefore a fair-weather, available-player score, not an expected realized score. A cross-layer veto is warranted whenever the model’s top recommendation has \(\alpha_{p,t}\eta_{p,t}\) below an available alternative’s value, or when medical, load, or weather evidence places the player in a known breakdown regime.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
