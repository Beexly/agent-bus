<THINK_TRACE>
The source chain is clear and text-only. MALLET converts an input text \(x_i\) into a six-emotion probability vector \(p_i\) with a BERT classifier, defines a scalar stimulus score \(S_i\), aggregates a corpus-level High-Impact Rate \(HIR\), and computes an Emotion Balance Index \(EBI\) [PAGE 2]. It then rewrites high-stimulus texts into BALANCED and COOL modes with GPT-4.1-mini, re-scores each mode, and compares RAW, BALANCED, and COOL in a within-subject design over 800 AG News texts [PAGE 3, PAGE 4]. The headline result is a graded reduction: “BALANCED achieved 13.1% reduction in ¯S and 20.6% in HIR, while COOL achieved 19.3% and 26.0%, respectively” [PAGE 4]. The second core claim is independence of stimulus reduction and semantic preservation: SBERT similarity is 0.846 for BALANCED and 0.827 for COOL, NLI contradiction rates are 1.9% and 3.6%, and the reduction–similarity correlations are “r = −0.039 (BALANCED) and r = −0.046 (COOL)” [PAGE 5]. The paper’s own abstract states: “Near-zero correlation between stimulus reduction and semantic preservation confirmed that the two are independently controllable” [PAGE 1].

A multi-hop audit must not stop at those summary numbers. First hop: the classifier is the measurement instrument. The paper says “The softmax output of the model is renormalized to sum to 1, and the input is truncated to a maximum of 128 tokens” [PAGE 2]. Thus \(S_i\) is a function of a renormalized six-way distribution over at most 128 tokens. Any content beyond that prefix cannot affect the reported score. Second hop: \(S_i\) is clipped: \(S_i=\min(1,p_i(\text{anger})+p_i(\text{fear})+p_i(\text{surprise}))\) [PAGE 2]. Saturation at 1 is a real breakdown regime: above that point, further anger/fear/surprise mass cannot increase \(S_i\), so the metric is insensitive to extreme escalation. Third hop: HIR uses “θ = 0.6” and an indicator \(1[S_i>\theta]\) [PAGE 2]. The strict inequality means \(S_i=0.6\) is excluded. Fourth hop: EBI is \(1-\frac12\sum_c|p_i(c)-1/6|\) [PAGE 2]. Its mathematical range with six categories is \([1/6,1]\), not \([0,1]\): uniform gives 1, one-hot gives \(1/6\). The paper’s verbal gloss “close to 1 indicates that the six emotions are evenly distributed, while a low value indicates a bias” [PAGE 2] is directionally correct but omits the nonzero floor. Fifth hop: the semantic checks are not absolute truth guarantees. SBERT cosine and NLI contradiction rates measure embedding similarity and an NLI model’s three-way judgment, with the NLI input capped at “maximum 256 tokens” [PAGE 2]. The paper itself notes: “The evaluation of stimulus reduction depends on the output of the emotion classifier, and verification through human subjective emotional judgment is necessary” [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit also must separate within-text sanitization from longitudinal behavioral inference. The weekly monitor uses five pseudo-consumers and “100 texts per consumer are randomly sampled without replacement from a pool of emotion-scored texts to simulate five pseudo-consumers” [PAGE 3]. That is a simulation, not real browsing-log evidence; the limitation is explicit: “The evaluation of the Balance Monitoring Agent is based on pseudo-consumers, and longitudinal verification using real consumer browsing logs remains a task for future work” [PAGE 6]. Similarly, category-level analysis shows World behaves differently: “World category showed almost no effect (−0.1% for BALANCED)” [PAGE 4]. The paper explains that World contains inherently high-stimulus facts such as conflicts and terrorism, where terms like “explosion” and “attack” are factually necessary, so lexical neutralization cannot remove the stimulus without damaging factual content [PAGE 4]. Therefore the correct inference is conditional: MALLET reduces stimulus when the stimulus is carried by sensational modification, not when the fact itself is the stimulus. That is the key gray zone.

A further trap is mistaking “anger reduction” for general emotional detoxification. The paper reports that anger decreases from 0.34 to 0.24, joy increases from 0.37 to 0.42/0.45, and “Fear, sadness, love, and surprise showed no substantial changes” [PAGE 4]. It attributes the joy increase to softmax redistribution as anger probability mass falls [PAGE 4]. This means the classifier’s neutral-expression region is not emotion-neutral; it is closer to the joy label. A claim of broad emotional normalization would therefore overreach. The defensible claim is narrower: the main measured mechanism is selective anger reduction, with limited fear reduction and near-zero change in other categories. This matters for recursive user modeling because a “calmer” text may still contain fear-laden factual content, especially in World news.

Finally, the paper’s own effect sizes require restraint. Although all six paired comparisons are significant at \(p<.001\) with Holm correction [PAGE 4], the stimulus-score effects are small: “The effect sizes for stimulus score reduction were d = 0.210 (BALANCED) and d = 0.282 (COOL), both small by Cohen’s criteria” [PAGE 4]. The readability cost is larger for BALANCED: FRE falls to 30.23, a 24.3% decrease, with \(d=0.609\) [PAGE 4]. COOL partly compensates through supplementary text, with \(d=0.339\) [PAGE 4]. Thus the strongest defensible operator is not “detoxification” in a clinical sense; it is a measured, modest, classifier-referenced shift in a six-emotion distribution with a semantic-preservation constraint and a readability trade-off.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s formal objects are text sequences, probability vectors, indices, and similarity/classification rates. The six emotion categories are \(C=\{\mathrm{anger},\mathrm{fear},\mathrm{sadness},\mathrm{joy},\mathrm{love},\mathrm{surprise}\}\), with \(p_i(c)\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_{c\in C}p_i(c)=1\) after renormalization [PAGE 2]. The source states: “The softmax output of the model is renormalized to sum to 1, and the input is truncated to a maximum of 128 tokens” [PAGE 2].

Stimulus score:
\[
S_i=\min\!\left(1,\;p_i(\mathrm{anger})+p_i(\mathrm{fear})+p_i(\mathrm{surprise})\right)\in[0,1].
\]
This is dimensionless probability mass. Assumption: the classifier’s renormalized six-emotion distribution is a valid measure of stimulus intensity. Breakdown regimes: (i) saturation at \(S_i=1\), where additional anger/fear/surprise mass cannot increase the score; (ii) truncation at 128 tokens, so content beyond the prefix is ignored; (iii) classifier dependence, since the score is not a human emotional judgment. The source defines this as “the risk level of an individual text” and uses the three components “anger, fear, and surprise” [PAGE 2].

High-Impact Rate:
\[
HIR=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[S_i>\theta],\qquad \theta=0.6.
\]
This is a dimensionless proportion in \([0,1]\). Assumption: \(N>0\) and the same classifier/threshold is applied to every text. Singularity/breakdown: \(N=0\) makes HIR undefined; the strict inequality \(S_i>\theta\) excludes \(S_i=0.6\); a threshold change alters the proportion discontinuously at the boundary. The source states: “In this study, θ = 0.6” [PAGE 2]. Table I reports HIR values of 0.446, 0.354, and 0.330 for RAW, BALANCED, and COOL [PAGE 4].

Emotion Balance Index:
\[
EBI_i=1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in C}\left|p_i(c)-\frac{1}{6}\right|.
\]
This is a dimensionless index. Assumption: the uniform distribution \(p_i(c)=1/6\) is the balanced reference. Exact range with six categories: \(EBI_i\in[1/6,1]\). Uniform gives \(EBI_i=1\); a one-hot distribution gives
\[
1-\frac12\left(\frac56+5\cdot\frac16\right)=1-\frac12\cdot\frac{10}{6}=\frac16.
\]
Thus the formula cannot produce 0. The source’s gloss is “An EBI value close to 1 indicates that the six emotions are evenly distributed, while a low value indicates a bias toward a specific emotion” [PAGE 2]. The nonzero floor is a formal boundary condition that any reimplementation must respect.

Readability:
\[
FRE \in \text{readability score units},
\]
not an SI physical unit. The source defines “Flesch Reading Ease (FRE)” as a readability metric and reports RAW 39.91, BALANCED 30.23, and COOL 34.54 [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. Assumption: FRE is comparable across the same text population. Breakdown: the paper does not state a FRE formula or a validity range; FRE is affected by sentence length and vocabulary, so a lower FRE indicates greater reading difficulty but does not by itself prove semantic loss.

Semantic preservation operators:
\[
\mathrm{SBERT}(x_i,y_i)=\cos\!\left(e(x_i),e(y_i)\right)\in[-1,1],
\]
where \(y_i\) is the rewritten text and \(e(\cdot)\) is the SBERT embedding. This is dimensionless cosine similarity. Assumption: embedding proximity tracks semantic fidelity. Mathematical singularity: if either embedding is the zero vector, cosine similarity is undefined; the source does not report such cases. Table IV reports 0.846 for BALANCED and 0.827 for COOL [PAGE 5].

NLI contradiction rate:
\[
CR=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\arg\max_{c\in\{\mathrm{entailment},\mathrm{neutral},\mathrm{contradiction}\}}q_i(c)=\mathrm{contradiction}\right],
\]
where \(x_i\) is the premise and \(y_i\) is the hypothesis. This is a dimensionless proportion. Assumption: the NLI model’s three-way label is a valid factual-consistency proxy. Breakdown: the NLI input is capped at “maximum 256 tokens” [PAGE 2], so long texts are judged on a truncated pair. Table IV reports contradiction rates of 1.9% for BALANCED and 3.6% for COOL [PAGE 5]. The source states: “The contradiction rate was low for both modes (1.9% and 3.6%)” [PAGE 5].

Experimental operator:
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{mode}} = \frac{\bar S_{\mathrm{RAW}}-\bar S_{\mathrm{mode}}}{\bar S_{\mathrm{RAW}}},
\]
a dimensionless relative reduction. The source reports “BALANCED achieved 13.1% reduction in ¯S and 20.6% in HIR, while COOL achieved 19.3% and 26.0%, respectively” [PAGE 4]. Assumption: paired within-subject comparison over the same 800 texts. The source uses “Two-tailed paired t-tests with Holm correction” and reports \(n=800\) [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: significance does not remove classifier circularity, because the same family of emotion classifier measures both input and rewritten output. The paper acknowledges the need for human subjective validation [PAGE 6].

LLM rewrite constraints:
\[
\left|\frac{|y_i|-|x_i|}{|x_i|}\right|\le 0.20,
\]
with “maximum output token count is set to 150” and “temperature parameter is set to 0.2” [PAGE 2]. This is a length-ratio constraint, not an emotional metric. Breakdown: if the original is very short, a ±20% length window may be too tight for a supplementary COOL sentence; if the token cap is reached, the output may be truncated before the constraint is satisfied. The source states: “Both modes are constrained to keep the length within ±20% of the original” [PAGE 2].

Weekly aggregation operator:
\[
M_{u,w}=\left(\bar S^{(u)}_{w},\;HIR^{(u)}_{w},\;EBI^{(u)}_{w},\;\{p^{(u)}_{w}(c)\}_{c\in C}\right),
\]
for consumer \(u\) in week \(w\). This is a vector of dimensionless summary statistics. Assumption: the week is the relevant aggregation window; the source uses “weekly” aggregation and “100 texts per consumer” sampled without replacement to simulate five pseudo-consumers [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: \(w\) is not justified as the correct behavioral time constant; the pseudo-consumer pool is not real browsing data; the source explicitly says longitudinal verification with real logs remains future work [PAGE 6].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicit. It situates itself in the attention economy and “informational health,” where excessive emotional stimulation is analogized to unhealthy consumption and where balanced information intake is claimed to support mental well-being [PAGE 1]. It cites sensational-content exposure, anger/anxiety effects on judgment, stress and polarization, filter bubbles, fake-news detection, text style transfer, high-arousal emotion and misinformation spread, digital nudging, and multi-agent LLM pipelines [PAGE 1]. The reference list anchors this lineage: Li et al. on delete-retrieve-generate style transfer, Luo et al. on prompt-based editing, Zhang et al. on dual emotion for fake-news detection, Callaway et al. on optimal nudging, Qian et al. on multi-agent collaboration, Savani’s BERT emotion model, SBERT all-MiniLM-L6-v2, DistilBERT MNLI, GPT-4.1-mini, and AG News [PAGE 6]. Retrocausally, MALLET is a descendant of style transfer and nudging, but it changes the objective from polarity conversion to graded stimulus-intensity control: the paper says prior style-transfer work “aimed at polarity conversion of emotions,” while MALLET claims “two-level adjustment” with BALANCED and COOL [PAGE 5].

The multi-week trap is the most important genealogical hazard. The system’s weekly monitor is designed to detect “cases in which a consumer chronically continues to consume content biased toward high-stimulus material” [PAGE 2]. But a weekly cadence can create three failure modes. First, avoidance masquerading as regulation: if a high-HIR consumer follows advice to diversify away from conflict content, HIR may fall because exposure changed, not because the consumer’s reactivity changed. The source’s consumer 0 example has “high HIR of 0.53” and anger at 39.8%, and the advice is to diversify and switch to COOL or BALANCED [PAGE 5]. That is a behavioral recommendation, not evidence of improved regulation. Second, mode lock-in: the Personal Guide recommends an initial mode, and the system “iteratively update[s] the recommended mode by referencing the weekly indicators accumulated by the Balance Monitoring Agent” [PAGE 3]. If the update function is slow or sticky, a consumer can remain in COOL long after the acute need passes, producing over-neutralization. Third, classifier drift and label redistribution: because neutral expressions are closest to joy in the classifier, repeated COOL exposure can raise joy mass without proving improved well-being; the paper reports joy increases from 0.37 to 0.42/0.45 as anger falls [PAGE 4]. A multi-week deployment could therefore mistake classifier redistribution for emotional recovery.

Another trap is the World-category limit. The paper reports “World category showed almost no effect (−0.1% for BALANCED)” [PAGE 4] and explains that conflict and terrorism facts are inherently high-stimulus. A system that promises “detoxification” across all news will fail exactly where the need is greatest. The genealogical correction is to treat MALLET as a modifier of sensational style, not as a neutralizer of factual threat. That distinction should propagate into product claims, evaluation design, and user messaging. The paper itself recommends “alternative intervention strategies such as staged information disclosure” for inherently high-stimulus content [PAGE 4]. That is the correct descendant of the nudging lineage: change presentation architecture, not just vocabulary.

A final lineage hazard is the pseudo-consumer simulation. The weekly results in Table V are generated from sampled emotion-scored texts, not real logs [PAGE 3]. The paper’s limitation is direct: “The evaluation of the Balance Monitoring Agent is based on pseudo-consumers, and longitudinal verification using real consumer browsing logs remains a task for future work” [PAGE 6]. Retrocausally, this means the weekly-advice operator should be read as a feasibility demonstration, not as evidence that personalized advice changes real behavior. The historical lineage from digital nudging to MALLET is plausible, but the causal link from weekly JSON summaries to durable consumer welfare is not established.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three distinguishable regimes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. Crisis habit regression is the case where the consumer’s own attention habit reinforces high-stimulus exposure. The paper’s consumer 0 example is the clearest instantiation: “Your dominant emotion this week is anger at 39.8%, combined with a high HIR of 0.53, indicating frequent engagement with conflict-driven news” [PAGE 5]. In source variables, the condition is approximately
\[
HIR^{(u)}_{w}>0.5 \;\land\; p^{(u)}_{w}(\mathrm{anger})\text{ is the dominant or near-dominant component}.
\]
The threshold 0.5 is not a formal equation in the paper, but it is used in the advice generation: consumer 0 is above 0.5 and gets a mode-change recommendation, while consumer 3 is “below 0.5” and is told to continue the current display mode [PAGE 5]. The recursive hazard is that the consumer may use high-arousal content to regulate negative affect, so the system’s advice can be experienced as an unwanted restriction. The paper’s framing is consumer protection “without restricting access to the original text” [PAGE 1], which is the correct countermeasure: preserve RAW as an option.

Counter-trap is the opposite failure: the system over-protects and thereby violates the consumer’s preference model. The Personal Guide persona 3 is the test case. The paper describes Persona 3 as a “stimulus-seeking type who enjoys bold expressions while also valuing accuracy” [PAGE 3]. Table VII recommends RAW with the rationale: “Preserves the boldness and dramatic expression of the original while maintaining accuracy, matching the stimulus-seeking preference” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, under the paper’s own persona semantics, a recommendation of COOL for Persona 3 is a counter-trap. It is not merely suboptimal; it contradicts the stated preference model. Formally, if sensitivity order is
\[
P1\succ P2\succ P3
\]
in sensitivity, then the recommendation order must satisfy
\[
R(P1)=\mathrm{COOL},\quad R(P2)=\mathrm{BALANCED},\quad R(P3)=\mathrm{RAW}.
\]
The paper reports exactly this graded mapping: “A graded recommendation of COOL →BALANCED →RAW was made in order of decreasing sensitivity” [PAGE 5]. Any inversion is a theory-of-mind failure: the system has modeled the consumer’s sensitivity but has not modeled the consumer’s reaction to being over-sanitized.

Clock is the temporal recursion. The system uses an initial Personal Guide recommendation and then “iteratively update[s] the recommended mode by referencing the weekly indicators accumulated by the Balance Monitoring Agent” [PAGE 3]. A schematic transition is
\[
R_{u,w+1}=g\!\left(R_{u,w},M_{u,w};\phi\right),
\]
where \(g\) and \(\phi\) are unspecified in the source. The source does not define the update function, so any claim that the system guarantees convergence to the correct mode is unsupported. The clock trap is twofold. If the week is too short, noise in \(HIR_{u,w}\) and \(EBI_{u,w}\) causes mode oscillation; if the week is too long, the system reacts after a crisis has already consolidated into a habit. The paper’s own evaluation does not resolve this: it uses pseudo-consumers and weekly summaries, and the limitation section calls for longitudinal real-log verification [PAGE 6]. A robust recursive design would therefore separate acute mode selection from chronic habit tracking: use per-text adjustment for immediate stimulus, weekly summaries for trend diagnosis, and a slower multi-week layer for persona-level mode changes. The source supports the first two layers but not the third.

The recursive theory-of-mind also has an epistemic layer. The consumer knows the system is rewriting. If the rewrite is too aggressive, the consumer may seek RAW elsewhere, making the monitor’s HIR look better while the actual exposure moves off-platform. The paper’s design avoids forced restriction, but it does not measure off-platform leakage. That is a counter-trap at the system boundary: the sanitizer’s success metric can improve because the user exits the sanitized environment. A complete recursive model must therefore include a trust variable and an off-platform exposure variable, neither of which appears in the paper’s equations. Without those, the weekly advice should be treated as a within-platform diagnostic, not a total-exposure model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto is a scope proof, not an empirical proof about the world. The source’s state space is textual and probabilistic: \(x_i\), \(p_i\), \(S_i\), \(HIR\), \(EBI\), \(FRE\), SBERT similarity, and NLI labels [PAGE 2, PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. The paper states that “The Emotion Analysis Agent quantifies stimulus intensity using a 6-emotion BERT classifier” [PAGE 1] and that “it applies a pre-trained 6-emotion BERT model” to each input text [PAGE 2]. There is no weather covariate, no biomechanical load variable, no physical actuator model, and no sensor stream in the equations or tables. Therefore, for any external variable \(q\in\{\text{weather},\text{biomechanical load},\text{physical workload}\}\),
\[
\frac{\partial S_i}{\partial q}=0
\]
as a statement about the source model’s fixed structure, unless \(q\) is first mapped into the token sequence \(x_i\) or into a retrained classifier. That is the veto: a claim that weather or biomechanics modulates \(S_i\) cannot be inferred from MALLET. It may be true in an expanded model, but it is not source-supported.

The physical-layer veto is especially strict because the only admitted input is text up to 128 tokens: “the input is truncated to a maximum of 128 tokens” [PAGE 2]. If a proposed physical variable is not represented in those tokens, the classifier cannot see it. The biomechanical-layer veto is symmetrical: no equation contains joint angle, posture, muscle load, or movement variable. The weather-layer veto is also symmetrical: no table reports temperature, pressure, humidity, season, or location. The paper’s “World” category contains conflict and terrorism content, not meteorological context [PAGE 4]. Therefore any cross-layer claim of the form “weather changes the detoxification effect” or “biomechanical fatigue explains the HIR drop” is a category error under the source. It must be re-specified as a new hypothesis with new variables and new calibration.

The classifier-to-LLM layer also requires a veto. The Emotion Analysis Agent and the Emotion Adjustment Agent are different models: BERT-family emotion classification and GPT-4.1-mini rewriting [PAGE 2]. There is no shared latent calibration between them. The paper’s own limitation says: “The evaluation of stimulus reduction depends on the output of the emotion classifier, and verification through human subjective emotional judgment is necessary” [PAGE 6]. Therefore a claim that MALLET reduces a consumer’s “true” anger is not licensed; the licensed claim is that the classifier’s anger probability mass decreases. This is a cross-layer veto because classifier output is being used as both treatment target and evaluation instrument. The same circularity applies to EBI and HIR: they are re-computed after rewriting with the same classifier family.

The final veto is temporal and demographic. The weekly summaries come from pseudo-consumers sampled without replacement, not real logs [PAGE 3], and the paper says “longitudinal verification using real consumer browsing logs remains a task for future work” [PAGE 6]. It also says “the study targets only English texts” and multilingual applicability “has not been verified” [PAGE 6]. Thus cross-layer claims about real-world consumer protection, cross-cultural emotional norms, or multi-week behavioral change are outside the evidence. The valid adjudication is: accept the within-text, within-classifier, English AG News demonstration; veto claims that require physical, biomechanical, weather, real-log longitudinal, or multilingual evidence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
