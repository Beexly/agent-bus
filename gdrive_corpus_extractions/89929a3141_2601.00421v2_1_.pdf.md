A real veto would require an external constraint such as “do not use high-intensity pressing below \(A_8<0.35\)” or a biomechanical/medical override.

The second gray-zone is statistical redundancy. The source reports “A7–A9 (r = 0.98) and A3–A6 (r = 0.90)” and says the corresponding VIFs are “35.1 (A7), 37.0 (A9), 21.6 (A3), and 18.4 (A6)—well above the conventional threshold of 10, indicating severe multicollinearity” [PAGE 14]. Euclidean distance then double-counts shared variance. The authors retain the attributes for conceptual interpretability and report that a consolidated 12-attribute space gives mean Kendall \(\tau=0.91\), top-1 match 94%, and top-3 match 89% [PAGE 15]. That is reassuring for stability but not a proof of causal validity. A Mahalanobis alternative is explicitly deferred because \(\Sigma\) is unavailable for the prototype [PAGE 14]. In formal terms, \(d_M(x,y)=\sqrt{(x-y)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-y)}\) requires \(\Sigma\) to be invertible; near-perfect collinearity makes \(\Sigma\) ill-conditioned or singular, so the very redundancy that motivates Mahalanobis is also its breakdown regime.

The third gray-zone is operational validity. The source repeatedly limits its claims: “operational validity in live coaching contexts remains an open question for future prospective validation” [PAGE 1]; the experiments demonstrate “internal consistency rather than operational validity” [PAGE 41]. The pilot is a single match, retrospective, with only 5 unique DSS dimensions observed [PAGE 46, PAGE 52]. The source even reports moderate robustness “stability dropping to ∼60–70% under high-pressure or low-energy conditions” [PAGE 54]. So the correct audit stance is: the model is internally auditable, but the burden of proof for deployment remains prospective, multi-match, and external.

A fourth gray-zone is normalization and missingness. The min-max map \(\tilde{x}=(x-x_{\min})/(x_{\max}-x_{\min})\) is undefined when the denominator is near zero; the source assigns 0.5 when \(x_{\max}-x_{\min}<\epsilon\) [PAGE 7]. Missing data are imputed by correlated source, historical baseline, or neutral 0.5 with reduced confidence [PAGE 9]. The model can therefore produce a smooth recommendation even when information is weak. That is a feature for graceful degradation and a risk for overconfidence. The source’s own threshold is explicit: “When > 4 attributes are imputed, recommendations should be flagged as ‘low confidence’” [PAGE 38]. The audit should enforce that flag as a deployment gate.

Finally, the opponent-aware term \(d_{\text{comb}}=d_{\text{adapt}}(V_{\text{team}},V_S)-\alpha d_{\text{adapt}}(V_{\text{opp}},V_S)\) [PAGE 23] is only one-step. It rewards strategies that fit us and poorly fit the opponent, but it does not model the opponent’s counter-reaction to our choice. That is the recursive-theory-of-mind gap: the source supplies a relative-advantage score, not a fixed-point game. The audit must therefore treat opponent modeling as a confidence-weighted heuristic, not an equilibrium proof. The source itself reduces \(\alpha\) when opponent confidence is low via \(\alpha_{\text{eff}}=\alpha\cdot\bar{c}_{\text{opp}}\) [PAGE 24]. That is the right safeguard, but it is not a substitute for a counter-trap model.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Formal operators and units.** All macro-attributes \(A_j\), strategy components \(s_i^{A_j}\), weights \(w_j\), distances \(d\), and time fractions \(t,\tau_t\) are dimensionless. The source normalizes attributes to \([0,1]\) and strategy values to a floor of 0.2 and ceiling of 0.9 [PAGE 7, PAGE 18, PAGE 20]. Latency is reported in milliseconds, tracking frequency in Hz, and heart rate in bpm [PAGE 9, PAGE 29].

**Normalization.** The source defines
\[
\tilde{x}=\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}},
\]
with clipping and optional percentile bounds \(x_{\min}=P_5(D)\), \(x_{\max}=P_{95}(D)\). It states: “If xmax −xmin < ϵ (indicating near-constant values), the attribute is assigned the default value 0.5 to avoid division instability” [PAGE 7]. Assumption: benchmarks are computed only from information available at decision time; the prototype uses fixed synthetic benchmarks “by construction” [PAGE 8].

**Aggregation.** The general macro-attribute aggregation is
\[
A_j=\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_{ij}a_{ij},\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{ij}=1,
\]
where \(a_{ij}\) is player \(i\)’s contribution to attribute \(j\) and \(w_{ij}\) is a role-based weight [PAGE 13]. The appendix gives concrete formulas, e.g. \(A_1=0.7\cdot xG_{P_{FW}\cup P_{CM}}+0.3\cdot dribbling_{P_{FW}\cup P_{CM}}\) and \(A_8=0.7\cdot stamina_{\text{all}}+0.3\cdot resilience_{\text{all}}\) [PAGE 58-59].

**Strategy vector.** Each strategy is
\[
V_{\text{strategy}}^{(i)}=\left[s_i^{A_1},s_i^{A_2},\ldots,s_i^{A_{14}}\right],\qquad s_i^{A_j}\in[0,1],
\]
with a non-zero floor “We adopt a floor of 0.2 rather than 0” [PAGE 16, PAGE 18]. The source justifies this by tactical realism, geometric regularization, and robustness to measurement error [PAGE 18].

**Base and adapted distance.** The baseline is
\[
d_{\text{eucl}}(x,y)=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{14}(x_j-y_j)^2},
\]
and the context-adapted distance is
\[
d_{\text{adapt}}(x,y;w)=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{14}w_j(x_j-y_j)^2}.
\]
The source explains the metric choice: “Euclidean distance captures this absolute capability gap, penalizing large deviations quadratically” [PAGE 22]. Assumption: attributes are additive and independent enough for weighted Euclidean geometry; the source later acknowledges this is a linear approximation [PAGE 54].

**Dynamic weights.** The energy multipliers are
\[
m_5=1-\gamma_e\delta_e,\qquad m_{10}=1+\gamma_e\delta_e,\qquad m_{13}=1-0.5\gamma_e\delta_e,
\]
with \(\delta_e=\max(0,\tau_e-e)\), \(\tau_e=0.5\), \(\gamma_e=1.5\) [PAGE 27]. Gap multipliers include \(m_2=1+\gamma_g\max(0,-\Delta_{\text{tech}})\) and \(m_{11}=1+\gamma_g\max(0,-\Delta_{\text{phys}})\), with \(\gamma_g=1.0\) [PAGE 27]. Time-pressure multipliers use \(\delta_t=\max(0,\tau_t-t)\mathbf{1}[s\le 0]\), \(\tau_t=0.25\), \(\gamma_t=2.0\), giving \(m_4=1+\gamma_t\delta_t\) and an increase to \(m_1\) [PAGE 27]. Multipliers are clamped and normalized: “with mmin = 0.3 and mmax = 2.5” [PAGE 28].

**Opponent-aware adjustment.** The source defines
\[
d_{\text{comb}}(S)=d_{\text{adapt}}(V_{\text{team}},V_{\text{strategy}}(S))-\alpha\,d_{\text{adapt}}(V_{\text{opp}},V_{\text{strategy}}(S)),
\]
with \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) [PAGE 23]. It motivates this by symmetry: “A strategy that the opponent can execute well creates symmetry—both teams can play similarly, reducing differential advantage” [PAGE 23]. Confidence-adjusted opponent influence is
\[
\alpha_{\text{eff}}=\alpha\cdot\bar{c}_{\text{opp}},\qquad \bar{c}_{\text{opp}}=\frac{1}{14}\sum_{j=1}^{14}c_{\text{opp},j}.
\]
[PAGE 24]. Assumption: distances for own team and opponent are comparable because both use the same normalization protocol [PAGE 23].

**Uncertainty and robustness.** The source reports interval distance
\[
d_{\text{interval}}=\left[d(V_{\text{team}}^{-},V_{\text{strategy}}),\,d(V_{\text{team}}^{+},V_{\text{strategy}})\right],
\]
where bounds are obtained by perturbing each \(A_j\) by \(\pm\epsilon_j\propto(1-c_j)\) [PAGE 9]. The robustness index is
\[
R=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{S}_k=S^*\},\qquad R\in[0,1],
\]
with \(R>0.9\) treated as satisfactory [PAGE 30]. The source reports “89.3% top-1 consistency across plausible measurement error” [PAGE 9].

**Singularity and breakdown regimes.**
1. \((x_{\max}-x_{\min})<\epsilon\) in min-max normalization: division by near-zero; source fallback is \(0.5\) [PAGE 7].
2. \(d=0\) in the derivative \(\partial d/\partial x_j=w_j(x_j-y_j)/d\): the gradient is undefined at exact match; ranking ties or zero-distance degeneracy require tie-breaking.
3. Mahalanobis extension requires invertible \(\Sigma\); near-perfect collinearity \(r=0.98\) makes \(\Sigma^{-1}\) ill-conditioned or singular [PAGE 14].
4. Weight normalization requires \(\sum_k m_k>0\); the clamp \(m_{\min}=0.3\) prevents zero weights, but the normalization still changes all relative contributions [PAGE 28].
5. Sparse missingness: when more than four attributes are imputed, the source says to flag “low confidence” [PAGE 38].
6. Distribution shift: strategy vectors encode European tactical norms; the source reports expert agreement falling to 82% under youth shift and 78% under style shift [PAGE 38].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is semantic vector-space reasoning carried from computational linguistics into tactical decision support. The source explicitly grounds itself in semantic-distance models, citing cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, KL and Jensen–Shannon divergence, and contextual embeddings such as BERT and Sentence-BERT [PAGE 4]. It then adapts “Recommending Actionable Strategies” from general analytical frameworks and decision heuristics to football-specific macro-attributes and canonical tactics [PAGE 4]. This genealogy explains the design: words become players or attributes, documents become team profiles, and tactical concepts become vectors in a shared semantic space. The paper states the analogy openly: players act as words and collective play conveys meaning [PAGE 1].

The football-specific lineage is the strategy/tactics distinction. The source defines strategy as long-term identity and tactics as operational choices: strategy answers “What do we want to achieve?” and tactics answer “How do we achieve it?” [PAGE 3]. That distinction is why the DSS operates on tactical templates while using a strategic team vector. The 20 templates include high press, counterattack, positional defense, build-up play, gegenpressing, and many variants [PAGE 16, PAGE 60]. The historical scientific lineage also includes sports DSS and tracking/event-data systems such as Wyscout, InStat, StatsBomb, SciSports, and SkillCorner [PAGE 5]. The model is therefore a hybrid of NLP semantic geometry, performance analysis, and coaching heuristics.

Retrocausally, several future traps are already visible in the source’s own design choices. The first is synthetic-benchmark leakage. The source describes a production protocol with temporal ordering, held-out validation, and same-match exclusion [PAGE 8], but the prototype uses fixed synthetic benchmarks “by construction” [PAGE 8]. That means early demonstrations can look stable while never confronting the real leakage risk of rolling weekly updates. A multi-week deployment trap is to update benchmarks weekly without freezing the test window, thereby letting future match data influence past recommendations. The source warns against exactly that [PAGE 8].

The second trap is expert-vector provenance. Strategy vectors were elicited from three experts with European football backgrounds; the source admits “Cultural bias: All experts had European football backgrounds” [PAGE 61]. It also reports only moderate agreement on psychological attributes and says vectors reflect tactical understanding circa 2023–2024 and may need periodic re-elicitation [PAGE 61]. A multi-week trap is to treat the 20 vectors as timeless ground truth. They are historically situated constructs. If the tactical culture shifts, the vector library becomes a frozen prior masquerading as a measurement instrument.

The third trap is floor and perturbation sensitivity. The source reports that “The top-ranked strategy remained unchanged across all floor values for 3 of 4 scenarios” and that in the Fatigued and Inferior scenario, “the ranking between Positional Defense and Compact Zonal Defense alternated for f < 0.15” [PAGE 21]. This is a contained instability because the two strategies are tactically similar, but it shows that borderline recommendations are encoding-dependent. A multi-week trap is to over-interpret small rank changes near decision boundaries.

The fourth trap is distribution shift. The source reports expert agreement of 94% at baseline, 82% under youth shift, 88% under lower-division shift, and 78% under style shift [PAGE 38]. It recommends recalibration of thresholds or re-elicitation of strategy vectors [PAGE 38]. A multi-week trap is to deploy a professional-European model into youth or stylistically distinct football and mistake confident output for valid output. The source’s own caution is decisive: the experiments demonstrate “internal consistency rather than operational validity” [PAGE 41]. The genealogy therefore supports a disciplined reading: the model is a formal bridge from semantic geometry to tactical reasoning, but its historical priors and calibration population must travel with it.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer has three distinct failure modes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock pressure. They should not be collapsed into one “adaptation” variable.

**Crisis habit regression.** Under fatigue or stress, a coach may revert to a habituated identity rather than the distance-minimizing recommendation. The pilot gives a concrete example: after the DSS recommended Build-up Play, “the team continued with an aggressive, transition-heavy approach despite declining energy reserves and defensive organization” [PAGE 49]. The observed second-half profile diverged on defensive strength, transition speed, and residual energy [PAGE 51]. This is not necessarily irrational; it may be identity-preserving or belief-preserving. But it is a theory-of-mind problem: the DSS models the team’s capability, not the coach’s attachment to a habitual tactic. A recursive model would need a prior over coach policy, not just a vector over team attributes. The source’s diagnostics can support that conversation—e.g., “energy reserves constrain high-intensity options” [PAGE 53]—but the source does not estimate coach compliance or habit strength.

**Counter-trap.** The opponent-aware term is a one-step counterfactual: \(d_{\text{comb}}(S)=d_{\text{adapt}}(V_{\text{team}},V_S)-\alpha d_{\text{adapt}}(V_{\text{opp}},V_S)\) [PAGE 23]. It rewards a strategy that we can execute and the opponent cannot. But a recursive opponent may induce us into a trap. The source’s strategy library includes “Inducing Build-up Errors” [PAGE 16, PAGE 60], which is precisely a counter-trap template: the opponent invites a build-up pattern and then presses the error. The source’s formula does not model the opponent’s response to our chosen \(S\). It assumes “Independence of execution” and acknowledges that match dynamics may create indirect effects not captured [PAGE 23]. The correct audit is therefore: the opponent-aware score is a relative-fit heuristic, not a Stackelberg or fixed-point equilibrium. If opponent confidence is low, \(\alpha_{\text{eff}}=\alpha\bar{c}_{\text{opp}}\) reduces the exploitation term [PAGE 24]; that is a safeguard against overconfident counter-trap reasoning, not a solution to recursion.

**Clock.** The clock is formalized through time pressure. Let \(t\in[0,1]\) be fraction of match time remaining and \(s\in\{-1,0,+1\}\) score state. The source defines \(\delta_t=\max(0,\tau_t-t)\mathbf{1}[s\le0]\) with \(\tau_t=0.25\), \(\gamma_t=2.0\), and then increases \(m_4\) for transition speed and \(m_1\) for offensive strength [PAGE 27]. This is a rational urgency operator: late and not winning ⇒ upweight transition and offense. But it interacts with habit regression. A team that is losing late may revert to high-intensity vertical play even when \(A_8\) is low. The DSS may simultaneously upweight urgency and penalize pressing due to energy. The source’s scenario “High Temporal Pressure” is designed to test this and recommends Fast Counterattack [PAGE 34]. The recursive risk is that the coach uses clock pressure to justify abandoning energy constraints. The audit should require the diagnostic to show both the clock-driven weight increase and the energy-driven constraint, so the trade-off is visible rather than hidden in a single distance.

In sum, the source supports a first-order theory of mind—own team versus opponent—but not a second-order recursion in which the opponent models our model. Crisis habit regression is visible in the pilot but not parameterized. Counter-trap is represented as an opponent-fit subtraction but not as an adversarial response. Clock pressure is represented as an explicit weight multiplier but can be hijacked by habit. Those three should be separate audit axes.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto must decide when a physically or environmentally impossible recommendation overrides a semantically close one. The source’s distance is soft; it does not contain a hard feasibility constraint. Therefore the adjudication rule is: use the DSS for ranking, then apply external veto layers when the physical, biomechanical, or weather state invalidates the strategy’s assumptions.

**Physical veto.** The primary physical layer is residual energy \(A_8\), with secondary physical base \(A_{13}\). The source defines \(A_8\) as “Current stamina reserves across the squad” and computes it from stamina weighted by playing time with resilience as a moderator [PAGE 11-12]. In the pilot, projected \(A_8=0.35\) versus Build-up Play’s ideal \(0.60\) is called “the primary limitation” [PAGE 48]. For a high-press template with \(s_8=0.70\), the same \(A_8=0.35\) creates a larger gap. Formally, for \(d>0\),
\[
\frac{\partial d_{\text{adapt}}}{\partial V_{\text{team}}[A_8]}
=\frac{w_8\left(V_{\text{team}}[A_8]-s^{A_8}\right)}{d_{\text{adapt}}}.
\]
If \(V_{\text{team}}[A_8]<s^{A_8}\), increasing energy lowers distance; decreasing energy raises distance. At \(d=0\), the derivative is undefined. This proves a monotone soft penalty, not a hard veto. A hard physical veto requires a threshold rule: if \(A_8<0.35\) and the candidate strategy has \(s^{A_8}\ge0.70\) plus high \(s^{A_5}\), the recommendation should be blocked or escalated to top-k with a “physical infeasibility” flag. The source’s own fatigue scenario supports the direction: “the system automatically penalized energy-intensive attributes (A5, A8) and shifted toward Positional Defense” [PAGE 34].

**Biomechanical veto.** The source includes biomechanical observables such as sprint distance, high-intensity runs, recovery state, rest days, match load, and in-match fatigue [PAGE 6], plus tracking data at 25 Hz and physiological monitoring [PAGE 9]. It also applies quality control: implausible heart rates “¡ 40 or ¿220” are discarded and imputed [PAGE 9]. But the 14-attribute model does not contain an explicit injury-risk or tissue-load state. Therefore a biomechanical veto cannot be read directly off \(V_{\text{team}}\). It must be an external constraint: if recent high-intensity load, recovery state, or medical status violates a club policy, the strategy is vetoed regardless of semantic distance. The source’s missing-data pattern M1—missing \(A_4,A_8,A_{13}\)—is especially dangerous because those are the physical attributes most relevant to such a veto; the source reports only 78.5% top-1 match under M1 [PAGE 37]. A deployment should therefore treat physical-data outage as a confidence veto, not merely an imputation problem.

**Weather veto.** The exhaustive attribute set in Table 1 contains no weather, pitch, temperature, wind, or surface variable [PAGE 10]. The data-source list also contains no weather stream [PAGE 8]. Therefore weather effects are structurally omitted. This is a specification gap, not a modeling nuance. If weather affects traction, ball speed, or fatigue, it must enter either as a modification to \(A_4\) (transition speed), \(A_{13}\) (physical base), or \(A_5\) (press capability), or as a hard external veto. The source cannot adjudicate weather internally. A proof by exhaustion is straightforward: all 14 attributes are listed in Table 1, and none is meteorological [PAGE 10]. Any claim that the DSS “accounts for weather” would be unsupported by the source.

**Adjudication order.** The safest order is: (1) physical/medical hard veto; (2) biomechanical load veto; (3) weather/surface veto; (4) semantic ranking; (5) uncertainty/top-k presentation. The source already supplies the uncertainty layer: interval distances and top-k when intervals overlap [PAGE 9]. It also supplies the low-confidence flag when more than four attributes are imputed [PAGE 38]. Those should be treated as gates. If a high-intensity strategy is top-ranked while \(A_8\) is low and physical data are missing, the correct output is not a single recommendation but a vetoed or low-confidence set. That is the cross-layer proof: the semantic model can rank, but it cannot certify physical feasibility without an external constraint layer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Recompute \(m_5,m_{10},m_{13}\) from \(\delta_e=\max(0,0.5-0.30)=0.20\), yielding \(m_5=0.70\), \(m_{10}=1.30\), and \(m_{13}=0.85\) [PAGE 27]. Veto condition: if a high-intensity pressing template remains top-1 with no diagnostic warning and no top-k uncertainty flag, the model fails the physical-coherence test.
