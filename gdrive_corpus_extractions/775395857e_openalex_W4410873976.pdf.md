But Table 1.4 reports goalkeeper intake as \(2,178.077\) kcal/day [PAGE 3], which, against the same \(2,137.6\) kcal/day expenditure [PAGE 2], implies a positive balance of about \(+40.477\) kcal/day. This is a qualitative sign reversal, not a rounding issue. Midfielder intake also shifts from \(2,213.2\) [PAGE 2] to \(2,268.406\) [PAGE 3], a difference of \(55.206\) kcal/day. The source does not supply a reconciliation table, measurement model, or exclusion rule. Therefore any strong claim about “inadequate fuelling” for a position is underdetermined by the reported tables alone.

Second, macronutrient tables are inconsistent with the abstract and with each other. The abstract says “defenders consuming the highest amounts of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats” [PAGE 1], but Table 1.1 reports protein highest in midfielders at \(67.6 \pm 26.3\) g/day versus defenders at \(64.3 \pm 21.2\) g/day [PAGE 2]. Table 1.8 later reports defenders at \(62.575\) g/day and goalkeepers at \(66.437\) g/day [PAGE 4], while Table 1.1 reports goalkeepers at \(57.4\) g/day [PAGE 2]. Carbohydrate for goalkeepers is \(248.9\) g/day in Table 1.1 [PAGE 2] but \(293.504\) g/day in Table 1.6 [PAGE 3]. Fat for goalkeepers is \(68.5\) g/day in Table 1.1 [PAGE 2] but \(78.428\) g/day in Table 1.10 [PAGE 4]. These are not trivial because the paper’s recommendations are built on rank orderings. The statement “Forwards consistently show the lowest intake across all nutrients, potentially indicating inadequate fuelling strategies” [PAGE 3] may be true in the reported means, but the causal interpretation “inadequate” requires body mass, fat-free mass, exercise energy expenditure, and reporting validity, none of which are reported.

Third, the Atwater consistency check shows why the gray zone matters. Using standard factors \(4C+4P+9F\) with the Table 1.6/1.8/1.10 values gives goalkeeper macronutrient energy of \(4(293.504)+4(66.437)+9(78.428)=2,145.616\) kcal/day, which is closer to Table 1.4’s \(2,178.077\) kcal/day [PAGE 3–4] than to Table 1.3’s \(1,985.7\) kcal/day [PAGE 2], but it still does not match exactly. For forwards, the reconstructed value is \(4(179.703)+4(38.815)+9(52.117)=1,343.125\) kcal/day, far below Table 1.4’s \(1,496.528\) kcal/day [PAGE 3–4]. This could reflect unmeasured alcohol, fiber, rounding, or table transcription errors; the source does not say. The audit conclusion is that the paper is useful as a hypothesis-generating position-difference report, but not as a standalone causal proof of position-specific energy requirements.

The ANOVA layer also needs care. The reported F statistics — \(14.997\) for energy [PAGE 3], \(29.299\) for carbohydrate [PAGE 3], \(21.571\) for protein [PAGE 4], and \(13.253\) for fat [PAGE 4] — are dimensionless test statistics, and the p-values are probabilities in \([0,1]\). They establish that group means differ more than expected under the null model; they do not establish that position causes intake, because position is confounded with training load, body mass, playing time, menstrual status, dietary reporting, and team culture. The source itself later states that “Sample size variations among playing positions may influence the generalizability of results” and that “Longitudinal studies are needed to track changes over time” [PAGE 5]. That is the correct gray-zone boundary: the data support descriptive position differences, not individualized prescriptions.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(i\) index an athlete and \(j\in\{F,M,D,G\}\) index position (forward, midfielder, defender, goalkeeper). The source reports means in kcal/day, g/day, and mg/day [PAGE 2–4]. Convert source kcal to SI by \(1\ \mathrm{kcal}=4.184\ \mathrm{kJ}\).

Energy balance operator:
\[
E_{\mathrm{bal},i}=E_{\mathrm{in},i}-E_{\mathrm{exp},i}\qquad [\mathrm{kcal\,day^{-1}}]
\]
Assumption: \(E_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(E_{\mathrm{exp}}\) are measured over the same averaging window. Using Table 1.3 [PAGE 2], the means imply defenders \(2,324.3-3,512.4=-1,188.1\), forwards \(1,496.5-3,421.1=-1,924.6\), midfielders \(2,213.2-3,613.1=-1,399.9\), and goalkeepers \(1,985.7-2,137.6=-151.9\) kcal/day. Using Table 1.4 intake [PAGE 3] with the same expenditure gives defenders \(-1,188.145\), forwards \(-1,924.572\), midfielders \(-1,344.694\), and goalkeepers \(+40.477\) kcal/day. The goalkeeper sign reversal is a breakdown regime for any inference that requires the sign of energy balance.

Macronutrient energy reconstruction:
\[
E_{\mathrm{macro},i}=4C_i+4P_i+9F_i\qquad [\mathrm{kcal\,day^{-1}}]
\]
where \(C_i,P_i,F_i\) are g/day and the coefficients are Atwater factors in kcal/g. This is an assumption, not a source-stated equation. With Table 1.6/1.8/1.10 values [PAGE 3–4], goalkeepers reconstruct to \(2,145.616\) kcal/day, which does not equal either reported intake table. Breakdown: unexplained residual energy and table-to-table inconsistency.

Relative protein and energy availability require denominators that the source does not report:
\[
P_{\mathrm{rel},i}=\frac{P_i}{m_i}\qquad [\mathrm{g\,kg^{-1}\,day^{-1}}]
\]
\[
EA_i=\frac{E_{\mathrm{in},i}-E_{\mathrm{exercise},i}}{FFM_i}\qquad [\mathrm{kcal\,kg\,FFM^{-1}\,day^{-1}}]
\]
Singularities: \(P_{\mathrm{rel}}\) is undefined as \(m_i\to 0\); \(EA_i\) is undefined as \(FFM_i\to 0\). More importantly, the source provides no body mass \(m_i\), no fat-free mass \(FFM_i\), and no separate exercise energy term. Therefore absolute g/day comparisons cannot be converted into g/kg/day or energy-availability states. A ranking of absolute intake can invert after normalization if body mass differs by position.

ANOVA operator:
\[
Y_{ij}=\mu+\tau_j+\epsilon_{ij},\qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)
\]
\[
F=\frac{MS_{\mathrm{between}}}{MS_{\mathrm{within}}}\qquad [\mathrm{dimensionless}]
\]
The reported values are \(F=14.997\) for energy [PAGE 3], \(F=29.299\) for carbohydrate [PAGE 3], \(F=21.571\) for protein [PAGE 4], and \(F=13.253\) for fat [PAGE 4], with p-values reported as probabilities; for example, “F = 21.571, p = 0.0001” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: a significant F does not identify causal direction, effect size, or individual-level applicability.

Information operator:
\[
H(J)\leq \log_2 4 = 2\ \mathrm{bits}
\]
The position label carries at most 2 bits, while body mass, menstrual phase, training load, weather, and biomechanical load are absent. Therefore position alone is informationally insufficient to specify a multi-week diet.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is visible in its reference list: Bangsbo, Mohr, and Krustrup on “Physical and metabolic demands of training and match-play in the elite football player”; Burke et al. on carbohydrate guidelines; Clark et al. on Division I female soccer players; Devries et al. on “Nutritional considerations for female football players”; Hargreaves et al. on team-sport nutrition; Holway and Spriet on sport-specific nutrition; Maughan et al. on sports nutrition science and practice; Mohr, Krustrup, and Bangsbo on fatigue in soccer; and Tarnopolsky on protein requirements for endurance athletes [PAGE 5]. The paper’s retrocausal move is to take general team-sport nutrition and male position-specific dietary variation, then read the observed female group means backward as evidence of position-specific need. The source itself notes that “male athletes exhibit position-specific dietary variations (Hargreaves et al., 2002), yet similar data for female athletes are scarce” [PAGE 2]. That is a reasonable hypothesis-generation lineage, but it is not a longitudinal causal lineage.

The multi-week traps are therefore threefold. Trap one: converting a single cross-sectional survey into a multi-week prescription. The paper recommends tailored plans [PAGE 5], but it also says “Longitudinal studies are needed to track changes over time” [PAGE 5]. Trap two: treating absolute g/day as if it were relative intake. Without body mass and fat-free mass, a forward’s low absolute protein or carbohydrate intake may reflect lower body mass, lower playing time, underreporting, or a genuinely low intake; the source cannot distinguish these. Trap three: retrocausal performance attribution. A future performance outcome can be used to “confirm” a nutrition story that was never measured prospectively. The correct genealogy is descriptive position comparison → measured energy availability and training load → prospective intervention → outcome. The paper supports only the first step strongly, and even that step has table inconsistencies.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when a coach or athlete, under time pressure, reads “Forwards consistently show the lowest intake across all nutrients” [PAGE 3] and “Forwards may require dietary interventions to increase intake and balance energy needs” [PAGE 5] as an individual diagnosis: “the forward is underfuelled; increase intake now.” That is a regression to a simple group-mean habit. The counter-trap is to ask what the athlete believes the coach believes. If the athlete perceives that coaches expect forwards to eat more, she may increase intake, underreport intake, or change eating behavior in ways that contaminate the next measurement. The researcher’s recursive model must include that possibility; otherwise the observed position effect can be partly an expectancy effect.

The clock layer is decisive. A cross-sectional survey has a short measurement clock; a multi-week nutrition plan has a long intervention clock; a season has an even longer adaptation clock. The paper’s own limitations state that “Individual metabolic variations should be investigated to develop personalized dietary strategies” [PAGE 5]. That means the correct recursive stance is: position label is a weak prior; body mass, fat-free mass, training load, menstrual phase, and reported versus measured energy intake are the updating evidence. The counter-trap to avoid is over-updating on the position label and under-updating on the individual’s measured energy availability. The clock also warns against immediate crisis interventions based on one table: the goalkeeper energy-balance sign already flips between Table 1.3 and Table 1.4 [PAGE 2–3], so a rapid “corrective” feeding decision would be premature.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the source reports no body mass, fat-free mass, menstrual status, injury history, or measured resting metabolic rate. It reports only group means and ANOVA [PAGE 2–4]. Therefore claims requiring \(P_{\mathrm{rel}}\), \(EA\), or individual energy balance are vetoed. Biomechanical-layer veto: the source uses position labels as proxies for movement demands, saying midfielders operate over larger areas and goalkeepers require “high levels of agility and strength” [PAGE 2], but it reports no sprint distance, change-of-direction count, jump load, goalkeeper dive count, or external load. Any biomechanical causal claim is vetoed. Weather-layer veto: no temperature, humidity, altitude, surface, kit, or environmental variable appears on any page [PAGE 1–5]. Any weather-mediated inference is vetoed.

Formally, let a claim \(C\) require measurement validity \(M\), confounder control \(X\), biomechanical load \(B\), and weather/environment \(W\). The veto predicate is
\[
V_C = M \land X \land B \land W.
\]
The source supplies partial \(M\) and partial \(X\), but no \(B\) and no \(W\); therefore \(V_C=0\) for causal performance claims. The paper’s own limitations reinforce this: “Sample size variations among playing positions may influence the generalizability of results” and “Longitudinal studies are needed to track changes over time” [PAGE 5]. The adjudication is not that the descriptive table is useless; it is that the cross-layer proof is absent.

Scenario 1 — body-mass normalization veto. Collect body mass and fat-free mass for all 170 players and recompute \(P_{\mathrm{rel},i}=P_i/m_i\) and \(EA_i\). Prediction: the absolute protein ranking that places forwards lowest at \(38.815\) g/day [PAGE 4] will not necessarily remain the lowest after normalization; if a mass-adjusted model removes the position main effect at \(\alpha=0.05\), the claim of position-specific protein requirement is vetoed. Verifiable check: pre-register the mass-adjusted ANOVA and compare the position \(F\)-value against the reported \(F=21.571\) [PAGE 4].

Scenario 2 — energy-expenditure measurement veto. Measure expenditure by doubly labeled water and intake by weighed food records in the same athletes. Prediction: if measured goalkeeper expenditure is below \(2,137.6\) kcal/day, then Table 1.3’s negative balance is supported; if measured intake exceeds measured expenditure, Table 1.4’s positive balance is supported. Because the source already gives a sign reversal — \(1,985.7-2,137.6=-151.9\) [PAGE 2] versus \(2,178.077-2,137.6=+40.477\) [PAGE 3] — the veto is verifiable from arithmetic alone. Prediction: within-athlete expenditure will explain more variance than the static position label, and the position main effect will shrink; if the time-varying expenditure term is significant while position is not, the causal position-specific prescription is vetoed. Verifiable check: compare a mixed model with athlete random intercepts and time-varying expenditure against the reported cross-sectional ANOVA. The source’s own statement that “Longitudinal studies are needed to track changes over time” [PAGE 5] makes this the cleanest counterfactual test.
