The source is a small randomized-order, cross-sectional, two-group laboratory study comparing active cervical range of motion (ACROM) and cervicocephalic kinaesthetic repositioning (CKR) with and without an American football helmet in 15 British Collegiate American football players and 11 non-American-football trained controls [PAGE 1 of 7; PAGE 2 of 7]. The first multi-hop derivation is mechanical: if a helmet adds mass and displaces the head’s centre of gravity, the neutral head position may shift toward extension; that would reduce available extension ACROM while relatively increasing available flexion ACROM. The paper states exactly this interpretation: “The effect of the helmet’s mass appeared to have resulted in realignment of the head’s neutral position on the cervical spine towards extension.” [PAGE 5 of 7]. The observed flexion/extension ratio moved from 0.85 ± 0.20 to 1.01 ± 0.21 in controls and from 0.87 ± 0.18 to 1.04 ± 0.21 in AF players, with both groups significantly changing the ratio (p < 0.01) [PAGE 4 of 7, Table 2]. That ratio change is consistent with a neutral-point shift plus a possible physical restriction, not with a pure flexion-specific loss: the paper reports no significant helmet effect on flexion ACROM in either group (paired t-tests P = 0.14 and P = 0.31) but a significant extension reduction in both groups (P = 0.007, ES = 0.88 for controls; P = 0.001, ES = 1.02 for AF players) [PAGE 4 of 7]. The omnibus statement is: “wearing a helmet affects the ACROM (P = 0.014) with no difference between the controls and AF players (P = 0.62).” [PAGE 4 of 7].

The second derivation is proprioceptive: if repeated helmet wear trains a direction-specific return-to-neutral policy, then experienced AF players should show lower CKR error with the helmet, especially from flexion, while controls should not. The source supports a direction-specific effect: “In contrast, wearing helmets enhanced the AF players CK: flexion with no helmet 3.1°, with helmet 1.9°, (paired t-test: P = 0.054: [ES = 0.84] Fig. 3a); extension with no helmet 3.2°, with helmet 2.6° (paired t-test: P = 0.22: [ES = 0.38] Fig. 3b).” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The abstract also says AF players “appeared to be more accurate” with helmets under an ANOVA P = 0.077, with flexion ES = 0.84 and extension ES = 0.38 [PAGE 1 of 7]. The conclusion sharpens the asymmetry: “Constantly wearing a helmet results in AF players developing improved CK when returning from full flexion but not extension, short term wearing does not give any beneficial effects in terms of CK.” [PAGE 6 of 7].

The gray-zone audit is essential. First, the study’s own alpha convention treats P = 0.10 to 0.05 as “strong trends” and P < 0.05 as significant [PAGE 3 of 7], so the flexion CKR result at P = 0.054 is a trend, not a conventional significant result. Second, the omnibus “Wearing the helmet significantly enhanced the CK repositioning ability (ANOVA: P = 0.04: Fig. 3a and b)” [PAGE 4 of 7] coexists with simple effects that are not all below 0.05: AF flexion P = 0.054, AF extension P = 0.22, control flexion P = 0.93, control extension P = 0.6 [PAGE 4 of 7]. That pattern means the omnibus test and the simple-effect tests are not interchangeable; the omnibus may be driven by a specific contrast or by pooling, and the paper does not report a multiplicity correction. Third, the between-group helmeted comparison is P = 0.077 [PAGE 4 of 7], again a trend, not a significant interaction at the 0.05 threshold. Fourth, the sample is small (n = 15 AF, n = 11 controls) and the paper notes that sample size was based on prior effect sizes 0.3 to 1.2 [PAGE 2 of 7], so large Cohen’s d values can still carry wide uncertainty. Fifth, the protocol was helmet-only, not full kit: “Although the effects of the helmet were tested without the participants being in full kit” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Sixth, rotation was not measured: “the magnetic yolk was not used in these experiments, as rotation was not measured in this study.” [PAGE 2 of 7]. Seventh, order was randomized [PAGE 2 of 7], but blinding is not reported; the helmet condition is visually and mechanically obvious. Eighth, the controls were trained athletes in non-contact sports [PAGE 2 of 7], so group differences may reflect training history, sport-specific neck use, selection, or body size rather than helmet exposure alone. The anthropometric table shows AF players were heavier on average, with the paper calling this “a strong trend (P = 0.085)” [PAGE 3 of 7], while neck girth was similar (39.1 cm controls vs 39.0 cm AF) [PAGE 4 of 7, Table 1]. Ninth, the study is cross-sectional: it cannot establish a multi-week causal adaptation to helmet wear, only an association in currently helmeted players. Tenth, the paper itself flags higher-standard players and position/role as open questions: “It is possible that a different result might be found with players of a higher standard (i.e. higher than National Collegiate Athletic Association Division III).” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Therefore the defensible inference is narrow: helmet presence is associated with reduced extension ACROM and a flexion/extension ratio shift in both groups; experienced AF players show a direction-specific flexion CKR advantage with the helmet, but the evidence is trend-level and small-sample, and it does not establish a general proprioceptive enhancement, a full-kit effect, a weather effect, or a longitudinal causal law.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(i=1,\dots,n_g\) index participants in group \(g\in\{\mathrm{AF},\mathrm{CTRL}\}\), with \(n_{\mathrm{AF}}=15\) and \(n_{\mathrm{CTRL}}=11\) [PAGE 1 of 7; PAGE 4 of 7, Table 1]. Let \(c\in\{\mathrm{helmet},\mathrm{no\ helmet}\}\) and \(m\in\{\mathrm{flex},\mathrm{ext}\}\). Define the angular neutral position \(\theta^{*}_{i,c}\) and endpoint angle \(\theta_{i,c,m}\), both in degrees, with rad conversion \(\theta_{\mathrm{rad}}=\pi\theta_{\mathrm{deg}}/180\). Define active cervical range of motion:
\[
A_{i,c,m}=\left|\theta_{i,c,m}-\theta^{*}_{i,c}\right| \quad [^\circ].
\]
The helmet effect on range is
\[
\Delta A_{i,m}=A_{i,\mathrm{helmet},m}-A_{i,\mathrm{no\ helmet},m}\quad [^\circ].
\]
The source-defined flexion/extension ratio is
\[
R_{i,c}=\frac{A_{i,c,\mathrm{flex}}}{A_{i,c,\mathrm{ext}}},
\]
which is dimensionless. This is not an invented ratio; the paper states: “the ratio between flexion and extension (flexion/extension) was calculated” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The reported group averages were 0.85 ± 0.20 vs 1.01 ± 0.21 for controls and 0.87 ± 0.18 vs 1.04 ± 0.21 for AF players [PAGE 4 of 7, Table 2]. The ratio has a singularity at \(A_{i,c,\mathrm{ext}}=0\); if extension ACROM were zero, \(R\) would be undefined and must not be interpreted. In this source extension ACROM is positive, so the ratio is finite.

For CKR, define the self-selected neutral angle under condition \(c\) as \(\hat{\theta}_{i,c}\) and the return angle after movement \(m\) as \(\tilde{\theta}_{i,c,m}\), both in degrees. The absolute repositioning error is
\[
E_{i,c,m}=\left|\tilde{\theta}_{i,c,m}-\hat{\theta}_{i,c}\right| \quad [^\circ].
\]
Where the laser chart is used, the small-angle approximation is
\[
E_{i,c,m}\approx \frac{180}{\pi}\arctan\!\left(\frac{r_{i,c,m}}{d}\right)\quad [^\circ],
\]
with chart radial displacement \(r_{i,c,m}\) in metres and chart distance \(d\approx 1\ \mathrm{m}\) (the paper says “1 m (SD 5 %)” [PAGE 2 of 7]). The arctan form breaks down operationally if \(d=0\); here \(d\) is fixed and nonzero. The source reports CKR in degrees, e.g. AF flexion 3.1° without helmet and 1.9° with helmet [PAGE 4 of 7]. Group means are
\[
\bar E_{g,c,m}=\frac{1}{n_g}\sum_{i=1}^{n_g}E_{i,c,m}\quad [^\circ].
\]
Paired Cohen’s \(d\) for the helmet contrast is
\[
d_{g,m}=\frac{\bar E_{g,\mathrm{helmet},m}-\bar E_{g,\mathrm{no\ helmet},m}}{s_{\Delta,g,m}},
\]
with \(s_{\Delta}\) the standard deviation of within-person differences in degrees; \(d\) is dimensionless. The source says: “Effect size (ES) was calculated using the method of Cohen’s D [22].” [PAGE 3 of 7]. This equation has a singularity at \(s_{\Delta}=0\); if all paired differences were identical, \(d\) would be undefined/infinite and raw degree differences plus confidence intervals should be reported instead. Between-group helmeted effect size is
\[
d_{\mathrm{between},m}=\frac{\bar E_{\mathrm{AF},\mathrm{helmet},m}-\bar E_{\mathrm{CTRL},\mathrm{helmet},m}}{s_{\mathrm{pooled},m}},
\]
also dimensionless and singular at \(s_{\mathrm{pooled}}=0\).

The repeated-measures ANOVA can be written as
\[
y_{ijk}=\mu+\alpha_i+\beta_j+\gamma_k+(\beta\gamma)_{jk}+\varepsilon_{ijk},
\]
where \(y_{ijk}\) is ACROM or CKR error in degrees, \(\alpha_i\) is the subject effect, \(\beta_j\) is helmet condition, \(\gamma_k\) is group, and \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\) in degrees squared. The source states: “A repeated measures ANOVA was used to identify main effects, post-hoc analysis using the Paired Student’s T-test for the helmet effect separately in each group (controls and AF).” [PAGE 3 of 7]. Assumptions include approximately normal residuals, independence across participants, and appropriate sphericity for repeated factors; skewness/kurtosis were checked, but sphericity and multiplicity corrections are not reported [PAGE 3 of 7]. The source’s decision rule is: “Probability values of 0.1 to 0.05 were considered as signifying strong trends and values <0.05 were deemed a significant change.” [PAGE 3 of 7]. Two-tailed tests were used because “direction of change was not immediately predictable” [PAGE 3 of 7]. Probabilities are dimensionless in \([0,1]\). If one used Shannon information, surprise would be \(-\log_2 P\) in bits, but the source reports only probability values.

Breakdown regimes: (1) \(A_{i,c,\mathrm{ext}}\to0\) makes \(R\) singular; (2) \(s_{\Delta}\to0\) or \(s_{\mathrm{pooled}}\to0\) makes Cohen’s \(d\) non-identifiable; (3) \(n_g\) very small makes ANOVA and t-test inference fragile; here n = 15 and 11; (4) non-normal or heteroscedastic residuals invalidate the normal-theory p-values; (5) uncorrected multiple simple-effect tests inflate false-positive risk; the reported P = 0.054 and P = 0.077 should not be promoted to decisive significance; (6) the model cannot resolve helmet mass, fit, faceguard, visual/auditory restriction, order, training history, and full-kit effects because these are not separately randomized [PAGE 2 of 7; PAGE 6 of 7]; (7) the ANOVA main effect at P = 0.04 [PAGE 4 of 7] does not license a claim that every simple effect is significant, because the simple effects include P = 0.22 and P = 0.93 [PAGE 4 of 7].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from cervical proprioception and ataxia/nystagmus after neck injection [PAGE 6 of 7, ref. 1], through cervical joint-position error in whiplash and musculoskeletal pain [PAGE 7 of 7, refs. 2–4, 14], to the Revel CKR procedure [PAGE 7 of 7, ref. 21] and the rugby-versus-non-rugby cervical range/proprioception studies by Lark and McCarthy [PAGE 7 of 7, refs. 11, 12, 16]. The immediate retrocausal chain is: rugby players were reported to have decreased repositioning after neck extension or rotation [PAGE 2 of 7], so if helmets were simply protective, AF players exposed to similar impact and shear forces should not show the same CKR deficit. The present study tests that expectation and finds the opposite direction for AF flexion CKR with a helmet: AF players improved from 3.1° to 1.9° with ES = 0.84, while extension changed from 3.2° to 2.6° with P = 0.22 and ES = 0.38 [PAGE 4 of 7]. The paper’s proposed mechanism is habituation of cervico-spinal and vestibulo-collic reflex coupling: “sustained cervico-spinal reflex activation affords a prolonged after-effect to enhance the vestibulo-collic reflex” [PAGE 5 of 7]. Earlier lineage also includes Pearl and Mayer’s high-school football neck-motion observations [PAGE 7 of 7, ref. 23], which the authors use to discuss possible flexion restriction and shoulder-pad effects [PAGE 5 of 7].

Multi-week traps are the main genealogical hazard. Trap 1: treating a cross-sectional helmet-on vs helmet-off difference as a longitudinal adaptation law. The paper only says “Constantly wearing a helmet results in AF players developing improved CK when returning from full flexion but not extension” [PAGE 6 of 7], but the design does not randomize weeks of wear; it compares currently experienced players with controls. Trap 2: treating “short term wearing does not give any beneficial effects” [PAGE 6 of 7] as proof that no multi-week effect exists; absence in an acute trial is not absence over a season. Trap 3: generalizing NCAA Division III-level British Collegiate players to higher-standard or professional players; the paper explicitly warns “a different result might be found with players of a higher standard” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Trap 4: assuming the helmet-only effect survives full kit; the authors state the effects “were tested without the participants being in full kit” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Trap 5: assuming that because extension ACROM fell, all cervical performance fell; the data show a ratio shift and a direction-specific CKR improvement, not a uniform decline. Trap 6: assuming that a single P = 0.054 or P = 0.077 is a stable multi-week marker; these are trend-level results subject to sample size and multiplicity. The retrocausal lesson is that the helmet is not a static protective shell in this literature; it is a mechanical and sensorimotor perturbation whose effects depend on exposure history, direction of movement, and measurement context.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the athlete and clinician as recursive belief agents. Let \(H_t\in\{\text{no injury},\text{minor concussion},\text{cervical musculoskeletal injury}\}\) be the hidden state at time \(t\), \(O_t\) the observed CKR error in degrees, \(R_t\) the athlete’s self-report, and \(B_t\) the clinician’s belief. A Bayesian clinician update is
\[
P(H_t\mid O_{1:t},R_{1:t})=\frac{P(O_t,R_t\mid H_t)P(H_t\mid O_{1:t-1},R_{1:t-1})}{P(O_t,R_t)},
\]
with all probabilities dimensionless in \([0,1]\). The denominator is a singularity if \(P(O_t,R_t)=0\); in practice one uses smoothing or explicit priors. The athlete’s policy is not a passive sensor: under crisis, fatigue, or desire to remain on the field, the athlete may regress to a trained habit policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{habit}}\), returning to the helmeted neutral rather than the unhelmeted neutral. This is consistent with the source’s direction-specific finding: AF players with helmets had lower flexion error (3.1° to 1.9°) but not a significant extension improvement (3.2° to 2.6°, P = 0.22) [PAGE 4 of 7]. The paper also warns that “subjective tests such as those for pain tend to be hidden when elite players wish to remain on the field” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Thus the recursive theory-of-mind must model strategic reporting: the athlete’s report \(R_t\) may be a function of expected continuation value, not only of \(H_t\).

The counter-trap is clinician overconfidence: a single helmeted CKR test at P = 0.054 or a group ANOVA at P = 0.04 [PAGE 4 of 7] may be treated as a diagnostic threshold, even though the source says CKR can be “easily lost following a concussive injury” and the practical aim is a “sensitive proprioceptive test” that can be applied “without removing the helmet” [PAGE 6 of 7]. The right recursive move is to combine the test with baseline, direction, time since injury, and repeated measurements, not to collapse to one cutoff. The clock variable is \(t\) since injury, since helmet exposure, and since the last collision. The source’s “short term wearing does not give any beneficial effects” [PAGE 6 of 7] versus “constantly wearing” [PAGE 6 of 7] is a clock-dependent contrast: acute helmet exposure and chronic helmet exposure are different regimes. Crisis habit regression predicts that under stress the athlete reverts to the most overlearned neutral; counter-trap predicts the clinician overweights the acute test; clock predicts that the same CKR value means different things at 10 minutes, 10 days, and 10 weeks after injury. The adjudication rule is therefore: use helmeted CKR as a trend signal within a repeated-measures, direction-specific, time-indexed model; do not treat it as a stand-alone diagnosis.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the source supports a physical/mechanical effect of helmet mass and neutral-position shift, but only in helmet-only conditions. It states: “The effect of the helmet’s mass appeared to have resulted in realignment of the head’s neutral position on the cervical spine towards extension.” [PAGE 5 of 7]. It also states that the change was not equal and opposite, so “the additional difference could result from a physical restriction to movement caused by the helmet” [PAGE 5 of 7]. Veto proof: any claim that the measured ACROM reduction is the full-kit or on-field effect is vetoed because “the effects of the helmet were tested without the participants being in full kit” [PAGE 6 of 7]. Any claim that helmet mass alone is isolated is also vetoed because the paper’s own causal list includes “mass of the helmet, displacement of the centre of gravity, and/or the specific training and repetitive use with some element of feedback or ‘reward’ affecting neurological programming” [PAGE 5 of 7]. The physical layer therefore permits a helmet-only mass/neutral-shift interpretation and forbids a full-kit isolation claim.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the source permits a direction-specific claim but vetoes a global proprioceptive-enhancement claim. Extension ACROM fell significantly in both groups: controls P = 0.007, ES = 0.88; AF P = 0.001, ES = 1.02 [PAGE 4 of 7]. Flexion ACROM did not change significantly: controls P = 0.14, AF P = 0.31 [PAGE 4 of 7]. CKR improved for AF flexion at P = 0.054, ES = 0.84, but not for AF extension at P = 0.22, ES = 0.38 [PAGE 4 of 7]. Controls were unaffected in flexion P = 0.93 and extension P = 0.6 [PAGE 4 of 7]. Veto proof: a claim that “helmets improve cervical proprioception” without the flexion/extension qualifier is contradicted by the extension P = 0.22 and by the control nulls; a claim that “helmets reduce all cervical motion” is contradicted by the nonsignificant flexion ACROM results. The biomechanical layer also vetoes rotation claims because rotation was not measured: “the magnetic yolk was not used in these experiments, as rotation was not measured in this study.” [PAGE 2 of 7].

Weather-layer veto: the source contains no weather, temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, heat-index, or field-surface variable on [PAGE 1 of 7] through [PAGE 7 of 7]. The closest environmental description is laboratory geometry: “the vertex of the participants’ head was 1 m (SD 5 %) from a custom made wall mounted chart” [PAGE 2 of 7]. Therefore the weather layer is not falsified; it is not adjudicable. Veto status: ABSTAIN. Any weather-conditioned claim must be treated as unsupported by this source. If a later model requires weather, it needs external data; this paper cannot supply a physical or biomechanical proof for weather effects.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario V1 — Full-kit replication veto. Verifiable test: repeat the randomized helmet-on/helmet-off crossover with the same ACROM endpoints but in full kit, including shoulder pads, and compare \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{ext}}\). Prediction if the source’s helmet-only physical interpretation is correct: full-kit extension ACROM should still fall with helmet-on, with a negative mean \(\Delta A_{\mathrm{ext}}\) and an effect size in the range reported here (controls ES = 0.88; AF ES = 1.02) [PAGE 4 of 7]. Veto rule: if full-kit testing shows no extension reduction or a reversal with a confidence interval excluding the source effect, then veto the claim that the observed extension ACROM reduction is a helmet-specific physical effect generalizable to play. This directly tests the source limitation “tested without the participants being in full kit” [PAGE 6 of 7].

Scenario V2 — Longitudinal habituation veto. Verifiable test: a within-player, multi-week randomized exposure study measuring CKR from flexion and extension before and after \(\geq 6\) weeks of structured helmet wear. Prediction if “constantly wearing” drives the AF flexion advantage: the helmeted flexion error should decrease relative to the no-helmet baseline, with the AF pattern approximating 3.1° to 1.9° and ES near 0.84 [PAGE 4 of 7; PAGE 5 of 7], while extension should show a weaker or null change, consistent with P = 0.22 and ES = 0.38 [PAGE 4 of 7]. Veto rule: if flexion CKR does not improve over weeks, or if extension improves equally, then veto the direction-specific chronic-adaptation interpretation. The source itself says “short term wearing does not give any beneficial effects in terms of CK” [PAGE 6 of 7], so an acute-only null would not veto the chronic hypothesis, but a multi-week null would.

Scenario V3 — Training-history and selection veto. Verifiable test: match AF players and controls on age, mass, BMI, neck girth, and prior neck training, then repeat the helmeted CKR and ACROM protocol. Prediction if the AF flexion advantage is helmet-exposure-specific: the between-group helmeted flexion difference should persist after matching, with the source’s reported between-group helmeted flexion ES = 0.71 [PAGE 4 of 7]. Veto rule: if matching removes the between-group flexion advantage, then veto the claim that helmet wear itself, rather than selection or training history, explains the AF advantage. This is a live threat because the controls were “trained athletes who participated in non-contact amateur competitive sports” [PAGE 2 of 7], AF players were heavier with a strong trend at P = 0.085 [PAGE 3 of 7], and the study was cross-sectional. Each scenario is verifiable by pre-registered degree-based endpoints, effect sizes, and confidence intervals; none requires a betting line or an invented ratio.
