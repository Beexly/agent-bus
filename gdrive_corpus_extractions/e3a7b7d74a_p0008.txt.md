<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: The model assumes the investor maintains a constant f* every year. In practice, after a sequence of negative excess returns, investors habitually regress toward cash or fixed-mix allocations, effectively setting f_t = 0 or f_t << f* precisely when the Kelly rule would prescribe staying invested or rebalancing. This behavioral recursion invalidates the iid growth calculation because the realized path of wealth depends on the investor's own state-dependent trading, not just on the market's return distribution.

Counter-trap: A model-driven investor who computes f* from the unconditional μ and σ on [PAGE 928] may obtain a value greater than one (using the standard small-return approximation f* ≈ μ/σ² = 0.058 / 0.2160² ≈ 1.24), implying leverage. If the investor then over-leverages during a calm regime and a negative-return year occurs—as Table 1 shows remains probable even at n = 10 (~20%)—the drawdown can trigger forced deleveraging or margin violations, converting a recoverable loss into permanent capital impairment.

Clock: The investor's horizon n interacts with liquidity needs. Table 1 shows P(negative excess return) declines slowly with n; even at n = 20 the model assigns roughly a 13% chance of underperforming T-bills. If the investor must liquidate at n = 10 or n = 15 to meet a liability, the "long-term" Kelly optimality is irrelevant because the clock forces realization during a non-negligible loss window. Recursive theory of mind: the investor models the market as normally distributed; the market, in aggregate, responds to the positioning of all investors using similar f* rules; when many de-leverage simultaneously after losses, the return distribution becomes endogenous and non-normal, invalidating the original normal assumption. The loop closes only if the investor commits to a pre-specified, emotionless rebalancing clock independent of interim drawdowns.
