The identification problem at the core of this paper is multi-hop and cascading: raw plus-minus conflates individual skill with teammate quality, opponent quality, and coaching context (Rosenbaum, 2004; [PAGE 3]). Adjusted Plus-Minus (APM) reframes this as a multivariate linear regression where each lineup stint is an observation, each player is an indicator (+1 offense, −1 defense), and the response is net points per 100 possessions; however, NBA rotations are stable, so the design matrix X is nearly rank-deficient and ordinary least squares produces unbiased but explosively variable coefficients (Sill, 2010; [PAGE 3]). Ridge regression resolves the singularity by adding an L2 penalty λ‖β‖₂², yielding a unique positive-definite solution (XᵀWX + λI)⁻¹XᵀWy for any λ > 0 ([PAGE 25]). The Bayesian interpretation casts λ = σ²/τ² as a zero-mean Gaussian prior on player impact, shrinking limited-exposure players toward league average ([PAGE 26]).

The historical barrier is absolute: the NBA did not systematically record possession-level stint data until the late 1990s, rendering RAPM analytically inaccessible for 1984–85 through 1995–96 ([PAGE 1]). The paper’s solution—manual video reconstruction of 2,179 games, 435,760 logged possessions, and 1,012 player-seasons ([PAGE 1])—introduces gray zones that must be audited. First, box-score unreliability is not random noise but systematic home-scorekeeper inflation of subjective categories (steals, blocks, assists, offensive rebounds) ([PAGE 6]). The hard logical constraint STL_team ≤ TO_live,opponent (Equation 2; [PAGE 7]) is a matter of physical necessity: dead-ball turnovers cannot yield steals. The February 15, 1988 Atlanta-at-Chicago game violates this: film shows 7 live-ball Atlanta turnovers and 3 dead-ball turnovers, yet the official box score credits Chicago with 10 steals, inflating Jordan from 2 to 5 steals and under-crediting Oakley from 4 to 3 ([PAGE 8]). Because every inflated home steal implies an inflated visiting-team turnover, the Oliver possession estimator POSS = FGA − OREB + TO + 0.44·FTA (Equation 1; [PAGE 5]) propagates upward bias into visiting-team possession counts, systematically flattering home defensive profiles ([PAGE 8]).

Second, the video protocol itself contains approximation gray zones. Split possessions—possessions underway at a substitution boundary—are double-counted then fractionally apportioned 1/2 to each adjacent stint (Equation 4; [PAGE 16]). Equation (7) — Penalized objective ([PAGE 25]):
\[
\hat{\beta}_\lambda = \arg\min_\beta \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_2^2
\]

Equation (8) — Closed-form weighted ridge estimator ([PAGE 25]):
\[
\hat{\beta}_\lambda = (X^\top W X + \lambda I_{2P+1})^{-1} X^\top W y
\]
Positive definite for all λ > 0; singularity regime λ → 0 recovers near-rank-deficient OLS with explosive variance when X is collinear ([PAGE 3], [PAGE 25]).

Equations (9)–(11) — Bayesian posterior ([PAGE 26]):
\[
\beta \mid y, \sigma^2 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\beta}_\lambda, \sigma^2 (X^\top W X + \lambda I)^{-1}\right), \qquad \lambda = \sigma^2 / \tau^2
\]

Equation (12) — Coverage-scaled regularization ([PAGE 26]):
\[
\lambda = \frac{G_{\text{logged}}}{G_{\text{season}}} \times 5000
\]
Units: dimensionless penalty. Regimes: G_logged → G_season gives λ → 5000 (full-season shrinkage); at 17% coverage λ ≈ 850 (strong prior shrinkage); stub seasons 1993–94 (λ = 90) and 1994–95 (λ = 45) are effectively unregularized and inferentially unreliable ([PAGE 26], [PAGE 28]).

Equations (13)–(20) — RAPM extraction and mean-centering ([PAGE 29]–[PAGE 30]):
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{ORAPM}}_j = \hat{\beta}_{j,\mathrm{off}} - \bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{off}}, \qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{DRAPM}}_j = \hat{\beta}_{j,\mathrm{def}} - \bar{\beta}_{\mathrm{def}}, \qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{RAPM}}_j = \widehat{\mathrm{ORAPM}}_j + \widehat{\mathrm{DRAPM}}_j
\]
Units: pts/100 poss relative to league average.

Equation (21) — Multi-season pooling ([PAGE 31]):
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{RAPM}}_j = \frac{\sum_s \mathrm{poss}_{j,s} \cdot \widehat{\mathrm{RAPM}}_{j,s}}{\sum_s \mathrm{poss}_{j,s}}
\]
Units: possession-weighted mean, pts/100 poss.

Equation (22) — Residual variance ([PAGE 31]):
\[
\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N/2 - 2P - 1} (y - X\hat{\beta}_\lambda)^\top W (y - X\hat{\beta}_\lambda)
\]
Realized multi-season value σ̂ = 9.53 pts/100 poss ([PAGE 33]); single-season 1991–92 value σ̂ ≈ 9.17 pts/100 poss ([PAGE 45]).

Equation (23) — Posterior covariance ([PAGE 32]):
\[
\Sigma_\beta = \hat{\sigma}^2 (X^\top W X + \lambda I)^{-1}
\]
Dimension: (2P+1) × (2P+1); for P = 1,012 player-seasons this is 2,025 × 2,025 ([PAGE 19]).

Equation (24) — Exact RAPM variance ([PAGE 32]):
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{RAPM}}_j) = \Sigma_{k_1 k_1} + \Sigma_{k_2 k_2} + 2\Sigma_{k_1 k_2}
\]
Equation (25) — Reported standard error omits cross-term ([PAGE 32]):
\[
\mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{RAPM}}_j) = 1.96 \sqrt{\Sigma_{k_1 k_1} + \Sigma_{k_2 k_2}}
\]
Breakdown: conservative only if cross-covariance small; exact interval requires Equation (24).

Equation (26)–(28) — Interval width and sample size ([PAGE 33]–[PAGE 34]):
\[
h_j \approx \frac{1.96 \cdot \hat{\sigma} \cdot \sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{n_j \bar{w} + \lambda}}, \qquad
n_j \bar{w} \geq \frac{2(1.96)^2 \hat{\sigma}^2}{(h^*)^2} - \lambda
\]
With σ̂ = 9.53 and λ ≈ 863: target half-width ±2.0 pts/100 poss requires 416 logged possessions; ±3.0 requires 58; ±4.0 is achieved by the λ floor alone ([PAGE 34], Table 6).

Equation (29) — Predicted team rating ([PAGE 36]):
\[
\hat{R}_{t,s} = \frac{\sum_{j \in R_{t,s}} \mathrm{poss}_{j,s} \cdot \hat{\beta}_{j,s}}{\sum_{j \in R_{t,s}} \mathrm{poss}_{j,s}}
\]

Equation (30) — Calibration regression ([PAGE 36]):
\[
R_{t,s} = \alpha + \gamma \hat{R}_{t,s} + \eta_{t,s}
\]
Well-calibrated: γ̂ ≈ 1, α̂ ≈ 0, high R².

Win-loss estimators ([PAGE 37]):
\[
\hat{W}^{\mathrm{MLE}} = \frac{w}{n} \cdot 82, \qquad
\hat{p}^{\mathrm{Bayes}}_{t,s} = \frac{w+5}{n+10}, \qquad
\hat{W}^{\mathrm{Bayes}} = \hat{p}^{\mathrm{Bayes}}_{t,s} \cdot 82
\]
Units: wins over 82-game season.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of this work traces through four generations of basketball impact measurement. Rosenbaum (2004) introduced Adjusted Plus-Minus as a multivariate linear regression controlling for co-occurring teammates and opponents ([PAGE 3]). Sill (2010) replaced OLS with ridge regression to tame multicollinearity in near-rank-deficient lineup design matrices, creating the RAPM standard ([PAGE 3]). Oliver (2004) supplied the foundational box-score possession estimator with the 0.44 free-throw coefficient ([PAGE 5]), later derived and era-tested by Kubatko et al. (2007), who noted the coefficient varies across rule environments ([PAGE 5]). The present paper extends this lineage by attacking the data precondition rather than the estimator: because play-by-play records do not exist before the late 1990s, the paper reconstructs stint data from broadcast video, making RAPM applicable to 1984–85 through 1995–96 ([PAGE 1]).

Parallel box-score composite metrics—Hollinger’s PER ([PAGE 9]), Oliver’s Win Shares ([PAGE 9]), and Myers’s Box Plus-Minus ([PAGE 9])—provide historical coverage but cannot control for lineup context and inherit the scorekeeper bias documented here ([PAGE 6]–[PAGE 8]). Cervone et al. (2016) demonstrated that spatiotemporal tracking enables finer-grained impact estimation than stints alone, but such data does not exist for the pre-modern era; this project therefore lays the stint-level foundation for any future spatial reconstruction from historical video ([PAGE 9]).

Multi-week traps are embedded in the project’s trajectory. The stub-season trap: 1993–94 (20 games) and 1994–95 (10 games) produce λ values of 90 and 45, effectively unregularized estimates that look like results but carry no inferential content ([PAGE 28]). The denominator trap: using 943 games as the schedule size for post-expansion seasons (1,025–1,189 games) falsely indicated over-regularization ratios of 1.087–1.261; correcting G_season eliminates the discrepancy ([PAGE 56]). The coverage-expansion trap: credible interval width scales with 1/√(n_j w̄ + λ), so each additional reconstructed game nonlinearly improves precision, yet footage availability is exogenous and not a designed probability sample ([PAGE 34], [PAGE 21]). The single-coder trap: no inter-rater reliability assessment exists yet, so systematic interpretation biases in ambiguous substitution events are unquantified ([PAGE 48]). The informative-prior trap: Section 10.3.2 proposes borrowing strength from box-score metrics for low-exposure players, but those inputs are themselves contaminated by the home-scorekeeper inflation this project seeks to escape ([PAGE 50]). The playoff-reconstruction trap: future postseason RAPM would address regular-season/postseason divergence, but playoff footage availability, while higher, remains non-random ([PAGE 50]).
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the analytical reflex to fall back on complete but biased box-score composites (PER, Win Shares, BPM) when possession-level data is absent; these metrics are available for every historical game but cannot disentangle individual impact from team context and are systematically distorted by home-scorekeeper inflation of steals and blocks ([PAGE 9], [PAGE 6]–[PAGE 8]). The counter-trap is the video-reconstructed RAPM database itself: it offers possession-level identification immune to scorekeeper theft, but at 17.2% aggregate coverage it reintroduces a new crisis of confidence—estimates for the bottom half of players (<500 logged possessions) are prior-dominated with credible intervals of roughly [−5.7, +5.7] pts/100 poss, carrying no individual inferential content ([PAGE 35], Table 4).

The clock operates on three time scales. First, the archival clock: broadcast footage from the 1984–96 window is finite and degrading; each un-reconstructed game is a permanently lost observation, and coverage is driven by footage survival rather than sampling design ([PAGE 11], [PAGE 21]). Second, the precision clock: Equation (28) shows required possession counts fall as coverage increases (λ scales with G_logged/G_season), but narrowing intervals for marginal players requires many more games than currently logged ([PAGE 34]). Third, the stationarity clock: multi-season pooling (Equation 21) trades cross-season stability for sample size, assuming player quality is stationary enough to pool; this assumption is violated for rapidly improving or declining players, creating a temporal boundary beyond which pooling introduces bias ([PAGE 4]).

Recursive theory of mind applies to model users: an evaluator seeing Jordan’s +8.86 RAPM with CI [+5.80, +11.91] ([PAGE 42], Table 8) must reason that the estimator itself is reasoning about a collinear system where Jordan’s coefficient absorbs credit that might otherwise diffuse to teammates; conversely, seeing Pippen’s 1991–92 defensive RAPM of −0.08 with CI [−5.83, +11.02] ([PAGE 46]) requires recognizing that the model cannot separate his defensive contribution from Jordan’s because they share >90% of stints, and that the wide interval is an honest admission of non-identification rather than evidence of average defense. The crisis habit is to overinterpret point estimates; the counter-trap is to inspect the posterior covariance and credible interval width before drawing defensive conclusions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: possession conservation. Proposition 1 (Equation 4; [PAGE 16]) requires |W_home − W_away| ≤ 1 (or ≤ 2 generally) because possessions alternate between teams with at most one extra possession following the opening tip or period-end change. Any reconstructed game violating this bound triggers review; irreconcilable score discrepancies lead to exclusion ([PAGE 17]–[PAGE 18]).

Biomechanical layer veto: the steal-turnover physical constraint. Equation (2) ([PAGE 7]) is not statistical convention but physical necessity: steals can only be credited on live-ball turnovers. The February 15, 1988 game film review shows 7 live-ball Atlanta turnovers yet 10 reported Chicago steals—a direct veto of the official box score’s defensive statistics for that game ([PAGE 7]–[PAGE 8]). Because RAPM’s response variable is net scoring margin per possession counted directly from video, it is immune to this class of box-score error ([PAGE 8]).

Weather layer veto: not applicable. NBA games are indoor climate-controlled events; no meteorological variable affects possession counting or lineup detection. The sole environmental layer is footage quality: late-1980s and 1990s broadcasts are generally sufficient for unambiguous substitution detection, while some 1970s footage is lower quality ([PAGE 11]). The 1984–96 window studied here lies in the generally sufficient band, so weather-equivalent degradation does not trigger exclusion on those grounds.

Quality-control veto pipeline ([PAGE 17]–[PAGE 18]):
1. Score reconciliation: sum of stint points must match official final score; discrepancies ≥1 point trigger full re-review; unreconcilable games are excluded ([PAGE 17]).
2. Lineup consistency check: running active lineup must match visible broadcast players at quarter breaks and game end; irresolvable discrepancies exclude the game ([PAGE 17]–[PAGE 18]).
3. Minutes reconciliation: reconstructed minutes vs. official box-score minutes; discrepancies exceeding 2.0 minutes trigger re-review ([PAGE 18]).
4. Possession plausibility check: corrected W_k vs. Oliver estimate; |W_k − POSS_k| > 10 flags manual review to distinguish box-score error from reconstruction error ([PAGE 18]).
5. Exclusion criteria: (i) score irreconcilable after re-review; (ii) lineup inconsistency requiring substantive imputation; (iii) footage quality insufficient to detect substitutions ([PAGE 18]).

Breakdown regime: if a game fails any hard exclusion criterion, it is removed from the design matrix, reducing N and altering the ridge penalty λ via Equation (12) because G_logged changes ([PAGE 26]).
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) veto scenarios with counterfactuals and predictions.

Scenario 1 — Stub-season player-level inference (1994–95).
Veto condition: Any claim of individual defensive or offensive impact for the 1994–95 season (10 logged games, λ = 45; [PAGE 28], Table 5).
Counterfactual: Treat the coverage-scaled λ = 45 as sufficient regularization and interpret player coefficients as data-driven.
Prediction: With λ = 45 the prior variance τ² = σ²/λ is large relative to the effective sample, so estimates collapse toward the ridge prior at zero. Credible intervals will span approximately ±10 pts/100 poss, and out-of-sample win-loss prediction for teams in this season will have MAE exceeding 30 wins (consistent with the <15% coverage franchise error band of >110 wins over 12 seasons; [PAGE 38]). Verification: Hold out 20% of the 10 games, recompute λ, and confirm coefficient instability under λ perturbation; the correlation between RAPM and actual team net rating will be statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Scenario 2 — Jordan–Pippen defensive collinearity in 1991–92.
Veto condition: Attributing defensive RAPM to Scottie Pippen separately from Michael Jordan in the single-season 1991–92 regression (5,644 shared offensive possessions, >90% of stints shared; [PAGE 46]).
Counterfactual: Force separation of their defensive coefficients despite the collinearity.
Prediction: The off-diagonal posterior covariance Σ_{k_Pip,def,k_Jor,def} is large and negative relative to diagonal variances ([PAGE 46]); Pippen’s single-season defensive RAPM is −0.08 with CI [−5.83, +11.02], which straddles zero and spans ±8.5 pts/100 poss ([PAGE 46], Table 9). Sign reliability fails: the interval includes strongly positive, zero, and strongly negative defensive impacts. Verification: Trace the ridge solution path as λ varies; Pippen’s defensive coefficient should exhibit high sensitivity (non-monotonic jumps), whereas Jordan’s defensive coefficient (+1.01, CI well above zero) remains stable ([PAGE 46]).

Scenario 3 — Low-coverage franchise win projection (Utah Jazz, 12.4% sampled).
Veto condition: Using the MLE win-loss estimator Ŵ^MLE = (w/n)·82 for a franchise-season with coverage below 15% ([PAGE 37], Table 7).
Counterfactual: Trust the MLE projection as an unbiased estimate of full-season wins.
Prediction: Game-importance bias causes logged games to over-represent high-stakes road contests against playoff-caliber opponents, systematically understating true win rates ([PAGE 39]). The Utah Jazz show MLE error of −200.7 wins summed over 12 seasons (~16.7 wins/season) at 12.4% coverage ([PAGE 38], Table 7). Bayesian shrinkage toward 0.500 (Beta(5,5)) reduces but does not eliminate this bias ([PAGE 39]). Verification: Compare MLE predictions to actual full-season wins across all franchises below 15% coverage; the mean absolute error will exceed 15 wins per season, and the error will correlate negatively with franchise quality (better teams under-projected more severely).
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
