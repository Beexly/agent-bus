The printed factor 100 is a source-level inconsistency that should be quoted and corrected in any reproduction.

A third gray-zone is loss-curve interpretation. Page 6 states “the nano model always has a higher amount of residual” [PAGE 6], while Page 8 states “The smaller models, like the nano, has lower residuals in the loss functions due to its smaller representational ability” [PAGE 8]. These are contradictory. The safe GSI inference is that model-capacity explanations are post hoc and should be treated as hypotheses. A fourth ambiguity is the homography success predicate: the source states success only if “minimal detected keypoints are four with less geometric alignment” [PAGE 4]. The mathematically necessary condition is at least four non-collinear correspondences, and Page 8 confirms: “Since a minimum of four non-collinear correspondences is mathematically required to compute a projective transformation, RANSAC could not produce a valid homography” [PAGE 8]. The phrase “less geometric alignment” is imprecise; the defensible operator is rank and non-collinearity, not a vague alignment score.

The deeper derivation is therefore conditional: medium’s lower MPE reduces the probability that RANSAC’s 3 px inlier set falls below four usable correspondences, but the dominant failure mode in broadcast is not outlier contamination alone; it is insufficient keypoint count. Page 8 states both medium failures “share the same cause: fewer than four keypoints were detected” [PAGE 8]. Thus the medium claim is a conditional optimum over 514 images, fixed 640×640 input, 500 epochs, SGD lr0=0.01, and mosaic disabled [PAGE 5]. It is not a universal law across weather, camera cuts, multi-camera arrays, or larger keypoint vocabularies. The general scientific inference is that the operator should be a feasibility envelope, not a single leaderboard rank.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines four core evaluation operators. For detection accuracy, mean average precision is
\[
\mathrm{mAP}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{AP}(\tau),
\]
where \(\tau\) is an intersection-over-union threshold, \(\mathrm{AP}(\tau)\in[0,1]\), and \(\mathrm{mAP}\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless [PAGE 4]. The source uses “mAP at two standard thresholds (mAP@0.5 and mAP@0.5:0.95)” [PAGE 4]. Assumption: AP is computed per keypoint class or aggregated per the Ultralytics pose protocol; the paper does not print the full matching rule, so exact reproduction requires the code repository.

Mean pixel error is
\[
\mathrm{MPE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{(x_i-\hat{x}_i)^2+(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2},
\]
with \(x_i,y_i,\hat{x}_i,\hat{y}_i\) in pixel coordinates and \(\mathrm{MPE}\) in px [PAGE 4]. The reported values are nano 76.31 px, small 60.00 px, medium 35.67 px, large 55.59 px, and x-large 67.35 px [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: if \(N=0\), MPE is undefined; if a keypoint is invisible but still included without a visibility mask, MPE conflates localization error with annotation visibility.

Computational efficiency is printed as
\[
\mathrm{Latency}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{total}}}{N}\times100,\qquad
\mathrm{FPS}=\frac{N}{T_{\mathrm{total}}}\times100,
\]
where \(T_{\mathrm{total}}\) is the sum of inference duration over \(N\) test frames [PAGE 4]. Units are source-ambiguous. The table-consistent interpretation is \(T_{\mathrm{total}}\) in seconds, \(\mathrm{Latency}\) in ms after multiplying by \(10^3\), and \(\mathrm{FPS}\) in \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\); the medium row is 19.7 ms, 50.8 FPS, 52.2 MB [PAGE 7]. The abstract’s 49.6 FPS [PAGE 1] differs from Table 6’s 50.8 FPS [PAGE 7]. Exact breakdown regime: if \(N=0\), both latency and FPS are undefined; if timing includes pre/post-processing, the operator measures pipeline throughput, not pure model inference.

Homography reliability is
\[
R_{\mathrm{map}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{success}}(i),
\]
dimensionless and in \([0,1]\), where success is binary per image [PAGE 4]. The geometric operator is DLT: for homogeneous source points \(\mathbf{x}_j=(x_j,y_j,1)^\top\) and destination points \(\mathbf{x}'_j=(x'_j,y'_j,1)^\top\), estimate \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\) up to scale such that \(\mathbf{x}'_j\sim H\mathbf{x}_j\). Equivalently, \(A_j\mathbf{h}=0\), where \(\mathbf{h}=\operatorname{vec}(H)\) and \(A_j\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times9}\). A unique homography up to scale requires \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=8\). If \(n&lt;4\), then \(A\) has fewer than 8 rows and the system is underdetermined. If the four or more correspondences are collinear, rank deficiency persists and \(H\) is not unique. If \(h_{31}x_j+h_{32}y_j+h_{33}=0\), the projected point is at infinity and the Euclidean residual is undefined; if \(\det(H)=0\), the projective map is singular. RANSAC adds the inlier rule \(e_j=\|\mathbf{x}'_j-\pi(H\mathbf{x}_j)\|_2&lt;\tau_{\mathrm{rep}}\), with \(\tau_{\mathrm{rep}}=3\) px, stated as “about 0.47% at 640 × 640 resolution” [PAGE 7]. The source also filters detections by “confidence level more than 0.8” [PAGE 4]. The exact breakdown regime for \(R_{\mathrm{map}}\) is therefore: fewer than four confident keypoints, rank-deficient geometry, fewer than four RANSAC inliers, or degenerate projection. Page 8 confirms the hard failure: “Both failures occur in broadcast images and share the same cause: fewer than four keypoints were detected” [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from classical pitch registration to learned keypoint detection. Earlier approaches used “Hough-based line detection, ellipse fitting center circle, and template matching penalty box” [PAGE 3]; these worked in controlled scouting data but were weaker under broadcast variation. The next lineage is CNN segmentation: FCNs and DeepLab segmented field lines or regions, but segmentation was annotation-costly and struggled with real-time pipelines [PAGE 3]. The third lineage is heatmap-based pose estimation: Sportlight, the 2023 SoccerNet camera-calibration winner, used HRNetV2-w48 for pitch landmarks and accurate homography, but HRNet was “computationally costly and might not be applicable to real-time analytics” [PAGE 2]. The fourth lineage is lightweight pose detection: Spiideo used YOLOX-pose for player pelvis keypoints, showing that speed-oriented pose detectors can be effective [PAGE 2]. The present paper sits at the fifth node: retrain YOLOv8-pose variants for pitch landmarks, then test DLT+RANSAC homography reliability. It claims “the first reproducible baseline of YOLO-pose model varaints used in soccer homography mapping” [PAGE 2].

Multi-week traps are embedded in that lineage. Trap one: optimizing only mAP or MPE and ignoring \(R_{\rm map}\). The paper explicitly separates detection accuracy, computational efficiency, and homography reliability [PAGE 3]. Trap two: over-reading the medium variant. The authors themselves warn that “the limited dataset and fixed resolution (640 × 640) may favor models of medium capacity since larger models may not fully exploit their potential” [PAGE 7]. Trap three: ignoring broadcast/scouting imbalance. Testing has 52 images total, with only 20 broadcast and 32 scouting [PAGE 5]; scouting is almost always solvable because of fixed vantage and full-field coverage [PAGE 7]. Trap four: treating the 500-epoch schedule as converged for all variants. The source says medium becomes clearly lowest “after around 450 epochs” [PAGE 6], so a shorter schedule would change the rank. Trap five: assuming RANSAC alone solves missing keypoints. It cannot: Page 8 states four non-collinear correspondences are mathematically required. Trap six: confusing “best in this test” with “best in deployment”; weather, camera cuts, multi-camera arrays, and temporal smoothing were not tested, and temporal smoothing is listed as future work [PAGE 9].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when an analyst under deadline pressure collapses the three-axis evaluation into one scalar. The habitual move is “pick the lowest MPE” or “pick the fastest FPS.” Here that habit would select medium on accuracy and nano on speed; neither is sufficient. The source’s own design resists this by reporting accuracy, efficiency, and homography reliability separately [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap is to avoid over-generalizing medium’s 96.2% overall success. The difference between medium and small on broadcast is 18/20 versus 17/20 [PAGE 7], one image. A recursive model of the authors’ inference is: they observed medium lowest MPE, competitive latency, and highest overall \(R_{\rm map}\); they then inferred “best balance.” That inference is valid only inside the tested envelope. The counter-trap is to demand a broadcast-only, seed-replicated, threshold-sensitivity test before declaring medium universally superior.

The clock layer is both training time and inference time. Training uses 500 epochs, batch size 32, image size 640×640, SGD lr0=0.01, and mosaic 0.0 [PAGE 5]. The medium model’s advantage emerges after roughly 450 epochs [PAGE 6], so the clock is part of the causal story: a 100-epoch experiment might rank differently. Inference clock is 19.7 ms and 50.8 FPS for medium [PAGE 7], but the printed equation’s factor 100 and the abstract’s 49.6 FPS create a clock-measurement gray zone [PAGE 1], [PAGE 4]. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is: the authors are not irrational; they are selecting a conditional optimum under a fixed clock, fixed resolution, small dataset, and one RANSAC threshold. The correct counter-trap is to preserve their conditional claim while vetoing any unconditional “medium is always best” claim.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The hard physical veto is geometric feasibility. Define \(n_{\mathrm{vis},i}\) as the number of detected keypoints in image \(i\) with confidence \(c_k&gt;0.8\). DLT requires at least four non-collinear correspondences; therefore if \(n_{\mathrm{vis},i}&lt;4\), then \(R_{\mathrm{map},i}=0\) for every YOLOv8-pose variant, regardless of model capacity. Page 8 provides direct proof: “Both failures occur in broadcast images and share the same cause: fewer than four keypoints were detected. Since a minimum of four non-collinear correspondences is mathematically required to compute a projective transformation, RANSAC could not produce a valid homography” [PAGE 8]. This is not a model-ranking issue; it is a rank/feasibility issue.

The camera and occlusion veto is also explicit. Broadcast data contains “varying camera angles, zoom levels, and occluded pitch regions” [PAGE 1]. When zoom or occlusion removes pitch landmarks, the keypoint count falls below the geometric minimum. Scouting data is more stable because of “the fixed vantage point and full-field coverage” [PAGE 7]. Weather is an untested extrinsic veto: rain, fog, snow, glare, or low contrast can reduce visible keypoints and increase localization error, but the source does not report weather-stratified results. The correct adjudication is not to claim a weather effect from this paper; it is to state that weather is a plausible veto layer because it can force \(n_{\mathrm{vis},i}&lt;4\) or push residuals above the 3 px RANSAC threshold [PAGE 7]. Biomechanical/downstream veto: the paper does not integrate player tracking; it states that accurate keypoint detection and stable homography “cannot stand independently without player tracking” [PAGE 9]. Therefore any tactical or biomechanical claim beyond pitch registration is vetoed by missing downstream validation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: broadcast-only replication with paired image-level success. Veto condition: the claim “medium is uniquely superior on broadcast reliability” is rejected if the medium-vs-small difference is not stable beyond one image. Source counts are medium 18/20 and small 17/20 [PAGE 7]. Prediction: a paired exact test on 20 broadcast images will show a wide interval and a non-significant difference; the medium claim survives only as “best observed,” not “proven unique.” Verifiable by rerunning the same 20 broadcast images and recomputing \(R_{\rm map}\) with paired indicators.

Scenario 2: RANSAC threshold sensitivity. The source fixes \(\tau_{\mathrm{rep}}=3\) px [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if changing \(\tau_{\mathrm{rep}}\) to 1 px or 6 px reverses the medium ranking, the “best balance” claim is threshold-dependent. Prediction: at 1 px, all models lose success because fewer correspondences qualify as inliers; medium may still lead because its MPE is lowest, but the absolute broadcast success rate should fall below 90%. At 6 px, success should rise for all models, but high-MPE variants may gain more because their outliers become inliers. Verifiable by fixed-seed reruns across \(\tau_{\mathrm{rep}}\in\{1,3,6\}\) px.

Scenario 3: temporal smoothing with camera-cut masking. The source lists “temporal smoothing between frames based on the adjacent frame information” as necessary to reconstruct lost keypoints [PAGE 9]. Veto condition: if smoothing is applied without a camera-cut detector, it should propagate wrong keypoints across cuts and reduce \(R_{\rm map}\). Prediction: with cut-aware smoothing, broadcast success should increase above the medium’s 90% baseline; without cut-aware smoothing, broadcast success should decrease or produce false homographies. Verifiable by inserting a cut detector, comparing smoothed vs unsmoothed \(R_{\rm map}\), and reporting per-sequence rather than per-image success. These three vetoes test whether the paper’s conditional optimum is robust or merely an artifact of one dataset, one threshold, and one temporal assumption.
