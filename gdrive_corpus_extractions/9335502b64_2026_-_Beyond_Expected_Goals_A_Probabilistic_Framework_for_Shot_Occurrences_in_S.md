Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit of xG+.

The core statistical object is a conditional decomposition of the instantaneous scoring intensity. At frame t, the paper defines xS_t ≔ P_t(Shot within next second) and xG_t ≔ P_t(Goal | Shot occurs at t). The joint metric is xG+_t ≔ xS_t · xG_t ≔ P_t(Shot within next second) × P_t(Goal | Shot) [PAGE 5]. This product is the probability of scoring in the next second whether or not a shot is realized, which directly cures the conditioning-on-shots limitation of standard xG. The derivation is logically sound under the assumption that shot occurrence and shot quality can be estimated from the same snapshot feature vector at 30 fps. However, the product rule implicitly assumes conditional independence between the shot-decision process and the conversion process given the observed state, an assumption not explicitly tested.

Possession-level aggregation must respect the hard physical bound that only one goal can be scored per possession. The paper adopts xG+_poss ≔ 1 − ∏_{t=1}^{n} (1 − xG+_t) [PAGE 5]. This is the probability of at least one scoring event over n frames, bounded by one by construction. A secondary operator, max_t(xG+_t), is also evaluated [PAGE 10-11]. The at-least-one form grows mechanically with possession length n, because each additional frame multiplies the survival probability (1 − xG+_t) < 1, driving xG+_poss toward one even after the optimal shooting moment has passed [PAGE 11-12]. The max operator avoids this duration artifact by anchoring on the single most dangerous frame, and it performs identically or better in MSE/MAE (2.84 vs 2.84, 1.86 vs 1.86) [PAGE 11].

Gray-zone audit. First, selection bias: standard xG is trained only on observed shots, so players who generate more shots than their quality would predict dominate the training distribution. The paper documents this via SOE (shots minus xS), where Haaland, Kane, and Salah show +40 to +49 shots over expected in single seasons [PAGE 14]. Second, the independence violation: summing xG across rapid-fire rebounds yields 1.63 xG for a sequence that produced exactly one goal [PAGE 4-5], violating the unit bound inherent to a single possession. Third, temporal boundary effects: the one-second horizon for xS makes labels sensitive to sub-second timestamp misalignment, and the authors themselves concede boundary effects near the decision window [PAGE 16]. Fourth, feature-space approximation: openGoal models defenders as uniform 75 cm circles and ignores reach, height, and jumping [PAGE 16; formal definition on PAGE 19], which vetoes aerial duels and goalkeeper-rush geometry. Fifth, snapshot Markovianism: the framework scores frames with snapshot features and omits recovery runs, second balls, and pass–shoot chains [PAGE 16]. Sixth, statistical inference: the Binomial(114, 0.5) test assumes independence across matchdays, but matchday results are serially correlated through squad form, injuries, and fixture congestion, so p = 0.015 and p = 0.009 may be anti-conservative [PAGE 12]. Seventh, the 90% squared-error intervals overlap across all specifications ([0.800, 2.25] vs [0.819, 2.32]), meaning the mean MSE advantage of xG+ (2.84 vs 2.94) is not separable at the 90% level on every matchday [PAGE 11-12]. Eighth, selection on opportunity persists: stronger teams reach dangerous states more frequently, and exposure is not explicitly modeled [PAGE 16].

These gray zones do not falsify xG+, but they delimit its regime of validity: it is reliable for team-level forecasting over full seasons, unstable for single-match boundary events, and requires recalibration outside the 2022–2025 EPL tracking regime.

Let t index video frames at 30 fps (Δt = 1/30 s ≈ 0.0333 s). Define the shot-occurrence probability:
\[ xS_t \equiv P_t(\text{Shot within } [t, t+1\,\text{s}]) \in [0,1] \]
and the conditional conversion probability:
\[ xG_t \equiv P_t(\text{Goal} \mid \text{Shot at } t) \in [0,1] . \]
The joint second-horizon scoring probability is:
\[ xG^{+}_t \equiv xS_t \cdot xG_t = P_t(\text{Shot within next second}) \times P_t(\text{Goal} \mid \text{Shot}) . \]
This is quoted verbatim from [PAGE 5]: “xG+ₜ = Pₜ(Shot) × Pₜ(Goal|Shot) = xSₜ · xGₜ.”

For a possession of n frames, the at-least-one aggregation is:
\[ xG^{+}_{\text{poss}} \equiv 1 - \prod_{t=1}^{n} (1 - xG^{+}_t) , \quad xG^{+}_{\text{poss}} \in [0,1] . \]
Verbatim from [PAGE 5]: “xG+poss = 1 − Π_{t=1}^{n} (1 − xG+ₜ).” This equation enforces the physical bound that one possession yields at most one goal. The max operator is:
\[ xG^{+}_{\text{poss,max}} \equiv \max_{t \in \{1,\dots,n\}} (xG^{+}_t) . \]

Player-season over-performance measures:
\[ GOE_{xG} \equiv \text{Goals} - xG, \qquad SOE \equiv \text{Shots} - xS, \qquad GOE_{xG^{+}} \equiv \text{Goals} - xG^{+} . \]
Per-match scaling:
\[ GOEpm_{xG^{+}} \equiv \frac{GOE_{xG^{+}}}{\text{MP}}, \qquad GOEpm_{xG} \equiv \frac{GOE_{xG}}{\text{MP}} , \]
where MP is matches played [PAGE 15].

Two-stage inference:
Stage 1 (mixed-effects Poisson in lme4):
\[ \text{metric} \sim (1|\text{season}) + (1|\text{season:team}) + (1|\text{season:opp}) + \text{home} . \]
Stage 2:
\[ \text{goals} \sim \text{home} + \text{season} + \text{team\_off} + \text{opp\_def} . \]
[PAGE 10-11]

Evaluation units: log loss in nats (natural logarithm), MSE in goals², MAE in goals. Table 4 reports mean MSE: xG+ at-least-one 2.84, xG+ max 2.84, xG sum-of-shots 2.90 [PAGE 11]. Table 5 reports MAE: xG+ 1.86, xG 1.89 [PAGE 11]. Table 6 reports 90% squared-error intervals: xG+ at-least-one (0.800, 2.25), xG sum-of-shots (0.819, 2.32) [PAGE 12]. Stability correlations from Table 8: GOE_xG ≈ 0.12, SOE ≈ 0.63, GOE_xG+ ≈ 0.35 [PAGE 13]. Binomial test over 114 matchdays: X ∼ Binomial(114, 0.5); P(X ≥ 69) = 0.015 for at-least-one xG+, P(X ≥ 70) = 0.009 for max xG+ [PAGE 12].

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
1. Possession-length singularity: as n → ∞ with any xG⁺_t > 0, the product ∏(1 − xG⁺_t) → 0, so xG⁺_poss → 1 mechanically, rewarding duration over danger [PAGE 11-12].
2. Temporal boundary: if timestamp misalignment |δt| approaches or exceeds the frame interval but labels are assigned to a rigid 1-second window, xS labels flip near the boundary; the authors note “boundary effects near the decision window” [PAGE 16].
3. Tracking-noise singularity: if position error σ_pos exceeds the defender radius r_def = 0.375 m (75 cm diameter), the tangent-line openGoal construction becomes geometrically ill-posed [PAGE 19; approximation critique on PAGE 16].
4. Snapshot Markov failure: when sequential dependence (recovery runs, second balls, pass–shoot chains) dominates, frame-level features omit state history, violating the Markov assumption implicit in scoring each frame independently [PAGE 16].
5. Clear-possession filter veto: requiring “clear possession in the attacking third” excludes dangerous crosses or through balls with no nearby attackers, truncating the support of the shot-generation process [PAGE 16].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage of the shot-occurrence problem, plus multi-week trap identification.

The intellectual genealogy begins with Ensum et al. (2004), who used logistic regression to estimate shot-to-goal probabilities [PAGE 1]. Macdonald (2012) transplanted the idea to ice hockey, building an expected-goals adjusted plus-minus for NHL players [PAGE 1]. Heuer and Rubner (2012) and Mead et al. (2023) established that xG predicts future goals better than actual goals [PAGE 1]. Lucey et al. (2015) introduced spatio-temporal tracking into xG [PAGE 1]. Fernández et al. (2019) and (2021) decomposed possessions into decisions and actions and modeled the probability of pass, dribble, or shot at possession end, though they emphasized passing over shot probability [PAGE 1-2]. Singh (2018) and Statsbomb (2021) extended this into expected threat (xT) and on-ball value (OBV), valuing buildup actions by the xG of eventual shots [PAGE 2]. Poropudas and Inkilä (2021) embedded a shot-decision model into xT [PAGE 2]. Matano et al. (2018), Kharrat et al. (2020), and Zhang (2022) attempted soccer adjusted plus-minus using xG, constrained by data limitations [PAGE 2]. FiveThirtyEight’s SPI used xG alongside actual goals in predictive ratings [PAGE 2]. Brill et al. (2025) exposed selection bias in American football expected-points models: better offenses run more plays near the end zone, contaminating expected-points certainty [PAGE 2].

Pipping-Gamón et al. (2026) synthesize this lineage by modeling the missing left-censored variable: the probability that a shot is attempted at all. Where prior work conditioned on observed shots, xG+ conditions on possession state and asks, “Will a shot occur in the next second, and if so, what is its quality?”

Multi-week traps:
- Trap A (finishing variance): Over any 3–4 matchday window, GOE_xG has correlation ≈ 0.12, so a striker “outperforming xG” is overwhelmingly noise; betting on regression to the mean is the structurally correct habit [PAGE 13].
- Trap B (rebound inflation): In chaotic sequences, independent sum-of-shots xG can exceed 1.0 (the Orlando sequence totaled 1.63 xG for one goal), so cumulative xG over a 7-day period with multiple rebound clusters systematically overstates expected goal volume [PAGE 4-5].
- Trap C (shot-creation confounding): Elite creators (Mbappé, Haaland) receive high xG on realized shots because they manufactured the opportunity, not because they finish exceptionally; a 4-week sample cannot disentangle creation from conversion without xS [PAGE 3-4, 13].
- Trap D (league-transfer decay): Models trained on 2022–2025 EPL Gradient Sports tracking may not generalize to other leagues without recalibration, so cross-league player valuation over a multi-week window risks systematic mispricing [PAGE 16].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock modeling in the xG+ framework.

Crisis habit regression. Under defensive pressure, attacking teams regress to low-probability habits: speculative long shots (Rodrygo, 35 yards, xG ≈ 0.03 [PAGE 3]) and rushed finishes under closing defenders. The paper shows that shot-creation ability (SOE) is far more stable year-to-year (r ≈ 0.63) than finishing luck (GOE_xG, r ≈ 0.12) [PAGE 13]. This implies a recursive theory of mind in which elite attackers do not rely on terminal finishing variance; they regress to a stable habit of manufacturing shot opportunities. When a crisis (defensive pressure, compact block) occurs, the habit regresses toward creation, not conversion.

Counter-trap. The defensive counter-trap is shot suppression: reducing xS by denying the temporal-spatial conditions for a shot. openGoal quantifies the unobstructed goalmouth; defenders modeled as 75 cm circles collapse the angular window [PAGE 16, 19]. The counter-trap succeeds when it forces the attacker into low-xS regions (long distance, high ball speed, low openGoal), where even high xG conditional on a shot cannot compensate because xS ≈ 0 [PAGE 8-9].

Clock. The clock enters through the one-second horizon of xS and the possession-length n in the product aggregator. As possession time elapses without a shot, the defensive counter-trap consolidates; the mechanical duration bias of the at-least-one rule rewards merely keeping the ball, whereas the max operator respects the clock by isolating the single most dangerous instant before the window closes [PAGE 11-12]. The boundary effect near t + 1 s means the clock’s granularity (30 fps) interacts with label assignment: a shot at t + 1.05 s is a negative xS label, creating a cliff at the decision window [PAGE 16].

Joint recursion: An attacker with high SOE repeatedly resets the clock by creating new shot opportunities; a defender with strong shot suppression compresses openGoal and shortens the effective decision window; under crisis, the stable skill is shot creation (high SOE persistence), while finishing above xG is a mean-reverting habit (low GOE_xG persistence) [PAGE 13].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto conditions for xG+ deployment.

Physical layer. The ball’s distance to goal r (m), bearing θ (rad), height z (m), and speed (m/s) are primary features [PAGE 6-9]. xS and xG both decay monotonically with distance: xS over 8–30 m, xG over 8–20 m [PAGE 8]. Ball speed has opposing signs: higher speed raises xS (fast transitions create shots) but lowers xG (less controlled finishes), dropping sharply from near-zero speed [PAGE 8]. Veto: if ball speed exceeds the regime where the XGBoost partial dependence was trained, or if z implies an airborne body part not represented in training, the prediction leaves the validated manifold.

Biomechanical layer. openGoal computes the unobstructed share of the goal line by treating every non-goalkeeper defender as an identical 2D circle of 75 cm diameter and taking tangent-line pairs from the ball [PAGE 19]. This ignores reach, height, jumping, and goalkeeper positioning differences [PAGE 16]. Veto: in aerial duels, crosses to tall attackers, or goalkeeper rushes, the 2D occluder model systematically misestimates openGoal, invalidating both xS and xG inputs. The possession filter requiring “clear possession in the attacking third” further vetoes dangerous through balls and crosses where no attacker is proximal [PAGE 16].

Weather layer. The source does not include weather covariates (rain, wind, pitch temperature), and Gradient Sports tracking noise is acknowledged as a measurement-error source [PAGE 16]. Veto: heavy rain alters ball roll and flight, degrading ball-speed and height estimates; strong wind affects long-ball trajectory; these are outside the training distribution for 2022–2025 EPL matches and would introduce heteroskedastic error in xS labels.

Snapshot veto. The framework scores each frame with snapshot features and omits sequential dependence such as recovery runs, second balls, and pass–shoot chains [PAGE 16]. Veto: any possession where the decisive danger unfolds over multiple seconds (e.g., a rebound scramble) is underweighted relative to a single-frame max. The one-second horizon also renders xS labels sensitive to timestamp misalignment, producing boundary effects [PAGE 16].

Adjudication rule: xG+ is admissible for team-level seasonal forecasting on EPL tracking data with clear attacking-third possession and frame-accurate timestamps. It is vetoed for (i) aerial/goalkeeper-rush events where openGoal’s 2D circle assumption fails, (ii) multi-league transfer without recalibration, (iii) matchdays with severe weather distorting tracking, and (iv) sub-possession windows shorter than the one-second decision horizon.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios with counterfactual predictions.

Scenario 1: Rebound-cluster sum veto.
Verifiable condition: A single possession contains k ≥ 2 shots within 5 seconds (e.g., Orlando City vs. Philadelphia Union, 78th minute, four shots in 4 seconds) [PAGE 4-5].
Veto rule: The traditional independent sum-of-shots xG is capped at 1.0 for that possession; any reported value > 1.0 triggers a veto.
Counterfactual prediction: The recorded sequence yielded 1.63 xG from four shots but exactly one goal [PAGE 5]. Under xG+ at-least-one aggregation, possession value ≤ 1.0; the counterfactual error reduction is ΔMSE ≥ 0.63²/1 per such sequence relative to uncapped sum-of-xG, and max-per-possession xG+ should dominate on matchdays with high rebound frequency.
Falsifiable signature: On matchdays where > 10% of possessions contain ≥ 2 shots within 5 s, xG+ max MSE < sum-of-xG MSE by > 0.05 goals².

Scenario 2: Non-shot near-opportunity veto.
Verifiable condition: A cross into the six-yard box is defended just before contact, producing no shot event (Real Madrid vs. Manchester City, Rodrygo sequence) [PAGE 3].
Veto rule: Standard xG assigns zero to non-shots; xG+ must assign strictly positive xS_t · xG_t > 0 based on tracking features (ball distance, openGoal, defender proximity).
Counterfactual prediction: xG+ captures nonzero scoring probability for the near-cross, whereas traditional xG contributes 0. The cross scenario had “almost-sure goal” conditional on the attacker winning the footrace [PAGE 3]. Prediction: xG+ for that frame > 0.20 (reflecting high shot quality conditional on occurrence multiplied by non-trivial shot probability), while xG = 0 exactly.
Falsifiable signature: Frame-level xG+ values for no-shot high-danger crosses correlate with subsequent defensive clearance quality but not with recorded shot xG.

Scenario 3: One-second boundary timestamp veto.
Verifiable condition: A shot occurs at t + 1 s + ε, where ε ∈ (0, 0.1 s] and timestamps are subject to sub-frame misalignment [PAGE 16].
Veto rule: If |ε| < frame interval (1/30 s ≈ 0.033 s) but the labeler assigns the shot to the wrong second, the xS label flips from 1 to 0 or vice versa.
Counterfactual prediction: The paper notes “boundary effects near the decision window” [PAGE 16]. Under a smoothed hazard model with survival analysis, xS_t would replace the hard 1-second indicator; the counterfactual is a reduction in log-loss for xS by at least the current gap between XGBoost (0.0227) and best logistic (0.0251), i.e., Δlog loss ≥ 0.0024 nats, concentrated in frames within ±0.1 s of shot occurrence.
Falsifiable signature: Re-labeling shots occurring within ±0.05 s of the second boundary and retraining xS should shift feature importance away from ball speed (which ranks second for xS) toward temporal-proximity features, reducing out-of-sample log loss on boundary frames by > 5%.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
