<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week deployment traps.

Lineage. PlayGen-MoG inherits from a chain of multi-agent trajectory work. Social LSTM [1] and Social GAN [6] introduced spatial pooling and adversarial diversity for crowd simulation but lack structured role modeling for team sports [PAGE 1–2]. Trajectron++ [13] used a CVAE with spatio-temporal graphs for heterogeneous agents [PAGE 2]. AgentFormer [20] and Scene Transformer [12] brought transformer attention to joint forecasting, yet treated spatial relationships implicitly rather than encoding geometry directly [PAGE 1–2]. In sports specifically, Yeh et al. [18] demonstrated diverse multi-agent sports generation with a VRNN and graph interactions, and Zhan et al. [21] used programmatic weak supervision with macro-action variables for long-term coordination [PAGE 1–2]. The mixture component draws on Mixture Density Networks [2] and MultiPath++ [16], extended here with shared weights across agents to avoid O(N^2) covariance [PAGE 2]. Relative spatial attention adapts Shaw et al. [14] to 2D player coordinates [PAGE 2–3]. Non-autoregressive prediction follows non-autoregressive translation [4], predicting absolute displacements from formation in one forward pass [PAGE 2–3].

Multi-week traps. In a live sports-analytics deployment, several temporal generalization hazards arise. First, scheme evolution: offensive concepts shift week-to-week as coordinators install new packages, so a model trained on 2021–2022 tracking data may underrepresent emergent route trees and personnel usages in later weeks [PAGE 4, 8]. Second, personnel drift: injuries, trades, and snap-count changes alter which physical player occupies each role slot; role identifiers alone cannot capture speed or route-running differences, so generated trajectories may be role-correct but player-incorrect [PAGE 3]. Third, opponent adaptation: defenses adjust coverages and blitz rates based on formation, but PlayGen-MoG conditions only on offensive formation and generates offense-only trajectories, so it cannot model the strategic counter-adjustment that defines a real play [PAGE 8–9]. Fourth, distribution shift between seasons: the NFL Big Data Bowl datasets (2021 and 2022) are distinct [PAGE 8–9 references]; a model frozen on one season will degrade as league-wide spacing and tempo trends migrate. Fifth, discrete-start assumption: American football offers a clean pre-snap formation, but continuous-flow sports (soccer, basketball) lack this, so extending PlayGen-MoG requires extracting set-play situations; open-play generation from a single static frame is not valid without an equivalent “set” event [PAGE 8]. Sixth, weather and surface drift across weeks: rain, wind, and turf conditions alter route depth and acceleration, none of which are inputs [PAGE 4–8]. These are not addressed by the architecture and would require retraining or conditioning extensions before multi-week reliability.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock structure.

Recursive theory of mind models what the offense believes the defense believes the offense will do. PlayGen-MoG captures only the first order: it generates offensive trajectories from formation, implicitly learning the empirical distribution of play concepts associated with each alignment. It does not model defensive trajectories or condition on defensive alignment, play call, or coverage [PAGE 8–9]. Therefore it lacks second-order beliefs entirely: it cannot represent a defense disguising Cover 3 as Cover 1, nor an offense audibling at the line in reaction to a defensive look.

Crisis habit regression. Under pressure—late-game, two-minute drill, or third-and-long—coaching staffs tend to regress to a narrow, practiced habit set of trusted concepts rather than sampling broadly from the mixture distribution. PlayGen-MoG’s entropy regularization keeps all 8 components active [PAGE 6], but real crisis behavior likely collapses effective component entropy toward 1–2 high-frequency scenarios. The model would over-generate exotic deep-shot concepts that coaches suppress in crisis moments, while under-generating the high-repetition, low-variance scripts actually called under stress.

Counter-trap. Because component selection is shared across all players [PAGE 3], the model learns pairings such as “go route with QB dropback” or “slant with QB rollout” [PAGE 6]. A defensive coordinator with access to the same model could invert this mapping: given an observed formation, sample the most probable components and pre-position defenders to intercept the high-probability route tree. PlayGen-MoG has no defensive counter-modeling, so its outputs are exploitable as a static scouting report rather than a dynamic equilibrium prediction. The counter-trap is that the model’s “play scenario” is legible to an opponent; without game-theoretic response modeling, the generated play is transparent.

Clock. The play clock in American football imposes a hard latency budget for pre-snap analytics. PlayGen-MoG generates the full trajectory in a single forward pass under 10 ms [PAGE 1], which fits comfortably within the 40-second play clock and even within a broadcast graphic pipeline. The non-autoregressive design means there is no sequential decoding latency that scales with T; frame count does not slow generation [PAGE 5]. The temporal boundary condition is not play-clock duration per se but the requirement that all 50 frames be produced before the snap, which the architecture satisfies by construction. However, because the model cannot revise its output after defensive shifts late in the play clock, the “clock” creates an information asymmetry: the formation used as input may be a pre-shift look that changes before the ball is snapped, and PlayGen-MoG has no mechanism to update conditionally on motion or defensive adjustment.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto adjudication.

Physical layer veto. The model predicts displacements in normalized coordinates then maps back to yards via half-field constants (60 yd × 26.65 yd) [PAGE 4]. It contains no explicit field-boundary constraint, sideline collision penalty, or out-of-bounds rejection layer. A large covariance draw from L_ik can place a receiver beyond the sideline or behind the end zone. Because generation is non-autoregressive and each frame is independent conditional on the shared component [PAGE 5], there is no physics integrator to enforce momentum conservation or impenetrability between players. Trajectories may cross or occupy identical coordinates at the same timestamp without violation. Any deployment must be vetoed when predicted positions fall outside the field of play or when inter-player distance falls below a physical collision threshold; the architecture supplies no such veto internally.

Biomechanical layer veto. Player kinematics are governed by maximum acceleration, deceleration, and change-of-direction limits. PlayGen-MoG enforces continuity only through the L2 smoothness loss on best-component means [PAGE 4, Eq. 8], which penalizes frame-to-frame jumps but does not bound instantaneous speed or acceleration. The Cholesky covariance permits anisotropic uncertainty but encodes no mass, force, or actuator limit. Linemen are generated with the same displacement formalism as wide receivers despite radically different mass and acceleration profiles; role embeddings [PAGE 3] do not carry physical attributes. A veto is required if the generated trajectory implies acceleration beyond human limits or if offensive linemen release downfield before the snap-blocking engagement is physically complete. The loss has no term for ground-contact or blocking physics.

Weather layer veto. Temperature, wind, precipitation, and surface friction are absent from the input representation [PAGE 4–8]. Passing plays in high wind would exhibit altered route depth and ball flight, but PlayGen-MoG sees only formation and role IDs. Deep-ball concepts generated on a calm day may be physically unrealizable in a headwind, and route splits may be compressed or elongated by rain-affected cuts. Because the model is sport-agnostic but formation-conditioned [PAGE 8], it assumes the discrete starting configuration is the dominant conditioning variable; this assumption breaks when weather dominates route geometry. Veto condition: any game-time weather advisory (wind gust threshold, precipitation) invalidates the unconditional formation-to-play mapping unless weather embeddings are injected.

Veto proof summary. The cross-layer stack is incomplete: physical bounds, biomechanical limits, and weather are not represented in the computation graph. The model can only be deployed operationally under a veto protocol that checks (i) in-bounds positions, (ii) kinematic plausibility, and (iii) environmental compatibility. Without these external checks, PlayGen-MoG generates statistically coherent but physically ungrounded trajectories.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) veto scenarios with counterfactual predictions.

Scenario 1: Illegal-formation distribution shift.
Setup: An offensive formation is submitted with five linemen but split such that the tackle-eligible alignment is outside the training distribution (e.g., both tackles split wide to the same side, or fewer than seven players on the line of scrimmage). PlayGen-MoG was trained on legal 11-player offensive formations centered on the Center [PAGE 4]. Because the role embeddings and relative spatial attention assume the canonical offensive structure, an illegal or heavily shifted formation lies outside the support of the training density.
Verifiable veto: The formation encoder output and relational bias B_rel will be driven by out-of-distribution pairwise distances. The prediction is rejected before generation if the normalized pairwise distance matrix contains values beyond the 99th percentile of the training set, or if any player coordinate after denormalization falls outside the field boundary.
Counterfactual prediction: Without veto, ADE degrades to >10 yards and FDE >15 yards on such formations; entropy collapses toward 1 component because the shared π cannot resolve a coherent play scenario from an illegal geometry. With veto, no trajectory is emitted and a fallback rule-based generator is invoked.

Scenario 2: High-wind passing game.
Setup: A formation implies a deep-pass play concept (one mixture component pairs a go route with QB dropback [PAGE 6]), but game-time conditions include a sustained crosswind exceeding 20 mph. PlayGen-MoG has no weather input [PAGE 4–8]; its covariance floor ε = 0.01 only adds isotropic noise and does not model wind drift.
Verifiable veto: An environmental veto gate receives wind speed and direction as external telemetry. If wind speed exceeds a threshold and the selected component’s mean displacement contains downfield yardage beyond 20 yards for wide receivers, the generation is vetoed because the route depth is physically unreliable under wind.
Counterfactual prediction: Without veto, the model generates deep routes whose endpoint FDE exceeds the calm-condition baseline by >2 yards and whose actual completion probability (unmodeled) drops sharply. With veto, the system switches to a screen-or-run-biased component subset (short-to-intermediate concepts), reducing downfield exposure and keeping FDE within the observed 3.98-yard envelope.

Scenario 3: Pre-snap defensive blitz disguise.
Setup: The offense is in 11 personnel and PlayGen-MoG samples a mixture component that pairs a QB dropback with WR deep routes [PAGE 6].
