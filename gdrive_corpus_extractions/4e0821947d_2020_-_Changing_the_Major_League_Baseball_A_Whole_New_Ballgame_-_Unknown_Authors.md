The source is a 20-page MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference 2020 research paper, anonymized as “Baseball 1548715,” that studies physical changes in Major League baseballs across four periods: pre-2014, 2016-2018, the 2019 regular season, and the 2019 postseason. The deep inference chain is not “home runs rose, therefore the ball was juiced.” The paper’s chain is more conditional: manufacturing change alters a measurable ball property; that property can alter seam geometry or spherical symmetry; those can alter the aerodynamic wake and drag coefficient \(C_D\); \(C_D\) can alter carry distance for a given launch velocity, spin, and atmospheric state; and aggregate home-run-per-ball-in-play rates can then shift. Each link is separately evidenced or flagged as unresolved. The paper states that the 2018 committee found “the increase in home runs was the result of a ball with a lower drag coefficient. However, they were unable to find a physical difference that could account for this change” [PAGE 2]. That is the central gray-zone: lower drag was inferred aerodynamically, but the physical cause was not isolated by the committee. The paper then supplies candidate physical causes from disassembly and metrology.

The first multi-hop derivation is lace thickness to seam bulging to symmetry. The author “systematically disassembled two populations of baseballs—twelve from 2014 and fourteen from 2016-2017” and “recorded sixteen independent variables” [PAGE 3]. The only statistically significant structural difference was lace thickness: “the red cotton laces; those used to stitch the seams on the 2016-2017 balls were 9.0% thicker than those on the 2014 balls” [PAGE 4]. The measured values were “0.78±0.030mm” for 2014 and “0.85±0.023mm” for 2017 [PAGE 4]. The proposed mechanism is that “Increased lace thickness will produce slightly prouder stitches, creating a ‘bumpier’ seam” [PAGE 5], yet “thicker laces might lead to a more spherically symmetric ball” [PAGE 5]. That sounds contradictory only if one conflates lace thickness with seam height. The paper’s physical proposal is tension-dependent: wet cotton laces dry under tension, thicker laces stretch less, and less stretching reduces seam-adjacent bulging. The gray-zone audit must therefore separate three variables: lace thickness, seam height, and seam-adjacent bulge.

The second derivation is the 2019 regular-season ball. Here the paper reports lower static friction, lower seam height, and near-zero bulge. It measured “the coefficient of static friction (µs = tanθ) as a proxy for relative smoothness” [PAGE 8] and found that the 2019 coefficient was “27.6% lower” [PAGE 9]. It found that “the seams on the 2019 balls were only 54.6% ± 15.0% that of the average from previous seasons” [PAGE 9]. It found that “the 2000-2014 balls showed average bulging of 0.66% ± 0.34% and the updated 2018 population showed 0.28% ± 0.33%, those from 2019 deviated from spherical by only -0.04% ± 0.31%” [PAGE 10]. The paper then notes an apparent inconsistency: 2019 lace thickness returned to pre-2015 levels, yet the 2019 ball was rounder. It concludes that “lace thickness was no longer affecting shape” [PAGE 11] and proposes a drying-process change: “cotton dried under a hot air flow shrinks back to original shape” [PAGE 12]. This is a process operator, not a direct material operator. The audit point is that a later process change can erase the diagnostic value of lace thickness while leaving evidence in roundness and seam height.

The third derivation is the 2019 postseason. The paper’s retail acquisition is a major gray-zone. It purchased “36 baseballs—12 stamped ‘2019 Postseason’ and 24 stamped ‘2019 World Series’” [PAGE 13]. The paper’s own evidence therefore cannot by itself prove on-field postseason composition; it proves that the retail “authenticated” sample is not a clean field sample. That is a crucial boundary condition for any inference from retail balls to postseason drag.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are metrological and inferential. They are not decorative ratios; they are measurable quantities with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Disassembly and structural metrology. The paper’s first operator is destructive sampling: remove leather by unstitching, cut the cotton layer, unwind yarn layers, and measure components [PAGE 3]. The sample is \(n=12\) from 2014 and \(n=14\) from 2016-2017, with sixteen independent variables [PAGE 3]. The percent difference for a component is
\[
\Delta_x = \frac{x_{\mathrm{late}}-x_{\mathrm{early}}}{x_{\mathrm{early}}}\times 100\%,
\]
with dimensionless percent units. For lace thickness, \(\Delta_t = (0.85-0.78)/0.78\times 100\% \approx 8.97\%\), consistent with the paper’s “9.0% thicker” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: handcrafted variation, unstitching deformation, glue removal, and small \(n\) mean that a single significant variable out of sixteen needs multiplicity control.

2. Wraps-per-centimeter lace thickness. The paper uses a fiber-arts method: \(w=N_w/L\), where \(w\) has units \(\mathrm{cm}^{-1}\), \(N_w\) is a count, and \(L\) is length in cm. A calibrated thickness relation is
\[
t = \frac{\alpha}{w},
\]
where \(t\) is length in mm and \(\alpha\) is a dimensionless calibration constant. The paper reports “40 times over 3 cm” for 2014 and “36 times over 3 cm” for 2017 [PAGE 4], yielding \(w_{2014}=13.33\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}\) and \(w_{2017}=12.00\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}\). The quoted thicknesses are \(0.78\pm0.030\ \mathrm{mm}\) and \(0.85\pm0.023\ \mathrm{mm}\) [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: \(w\to 0\) would imply infinite thickness, so the model is only valid for many wraps over a fixed span; at low wrap counts, one-wrap quantization dominates.

3. Seam bulging and spherical symmetry. The paper measures average diameter \(D_a\) and seam-adjacent diameter \(D_s\) and compares them as
\[
b = \frac{D_s-D_a}{D_a}\times 100\%,
\]
with \(b\) in percent and diameters in mm or cm. The paper reports \(0.66\%\pm0.34\%\) for 2000-2014, \(0.28\%\pm0.33\%\) for 2018, and \(-0.04\%\pm0.31\%\) for 2019 [PAGE 10]. It also reports “0.66% (~0.5mm)” for pre-2015 and “0.19%, or roughly ~0.15mm” for 2018 [PAGE 6]. Breakdown: \(D_a\to 0\) is unphysical; the equation is a small-deformation approximation. Caliper placement within “~2mm of the seams” [PAGE 5] makes the operator sensitive to seam-adjacent location and to leather puckering.

4. Static friction as a smoothness proxy. The paper measures the angle \(\theta_s\) at which a folded leather cover slides and uses
\[
\mu_s = \tan\theta_s,
\]
where \(\mu_s\) is dimensionless and \(\theta_s\) is in radians or degrees [PAGE 8]. The 2019 result was “27.6% lower” [PAGE 9]. Breakdown: \(\tan\theta_s\) has a singularity at \(\theta_s\to \pi/2\); near vertical, the measured angle becomes ill-conditioned and \(\mu_s\) diverges. The proxy also breaks down if mud, scuffs, or puckering alter the surface; the paper explicitly eliminated scuffed or excessively puckered balls [PAGE 8].

5. Seam height from cover thickness. The operator is
\[
h_s = \bar{t}_{\mathrm{edge}}-\bar{t}_{\mathrm{body}},
\]
with \(h_s\) in mm, \(\bar{t}_{\mathrm{edge}}\) the average edge thickness, and \(\bar{t}_{\mathrm{body}}\) the average cover-body thickness [PAGE 9]. The paper reports that 2019 seams were “only 54.6% ± 15.0% that of the average from previous seasons” [PAGE 9]. Breakdown: if the interior cover surface is not smooth or if edge compression differs from body compression, the difference is not purely geometric seam height. The paper states the interior surface is smooth, which is the key assumption [PAGE 9].

6. Drag force and Reynolds regime. The aerodynamic operator is
\[
F_D = \frac{1}{2}\rho C_D A v^2,
\]
where \(F_D\) is in \(\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\), \(\rho\) is air density in \(\mathrm{kg}\,\mathrm{m}^{-3}\), \(C_D\) is dimensionless, \(A\) is cross-sectional area in \(\mathrm{m}^2\), and \(v\) is speed in \(\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). The regime parameter is
\[
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho v D}{\mu},
\]
where \(D\) is diameter in m and \(\mu\) is dynamic viscosity in \(\mathrm{Pa}\,\mathrm{s}\). Breakdown: \(C_D\) is not a constant; it changes with Reynolds number, seam geometry, spin, and wake transition. The paper’s central claim that lower drag can increase carry is physically consistent with \(F_D\propto C_D\), but the paper does not provide a closed-form \(C_D(\mathrm{Re},\text{seams})\) law. At the drag-crisis regime, small surface or seam changes can shift boundary-layer transition and wake width, so a linear extrapolation from seam height to \(C_D\) is invalid.

7. Projectile motion with drag. The minimal dynamics are
\[
m\dot{\mathbf v} = -\frac{1}{2}\rho C_D A |\mathbf v|\mathbf v + m\mathbf g,
\]
with \(m\) in kg, \(\mathbf v\) in \(\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), and \(\mathbf g\) in \(\mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}\). Breakdown: this omits Magnus lift from spin, so it cannot by itself translate a \(C_D\) change into a home-run probability. The paper’s evidence is about physical ball properties and drag, not a full pitch-to-hit trajectory model.

8. Difference-of-means inference. For sample means \(\bar{x}_1,\bar{x}_2\), standard deviations \(s_1,s_2\), and sample sizes \(n_1,n_2\),
\[
z = \frac{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}},
\]
which is dimensionless. For \(m=16\) independent structural variables, a Bonferroni-style family-wise threshold is
\[
\alpha_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{\alpha}{m}.
\]
Breakdown: the test assumes approximate normality and independence; handcrafted balls, shared suppliers, and multiple comparisons violate both. This is why the paper’s single significant lace result is suggestive but not automatically causal.

9. Batch and date operators. The paper identifies batch codes such as BEBRBSR, ERBSLAK, ERBSLAO, and EOBSLAO [PAGE 13] and reads code structure as a date proxy: six characters before a 2017 transition to seven, middle-character shifts O to R to S, and “B[x]BR[x][x]” as a 2019 pattern [PAGE 14]. This is a qualitative operator, not a physical equation. Breakdown: Rawlings’ official batch rubric is not public, so the code-to-date mapping is inferential and vulnerable to transition-designation ambiguity [PAGE 14].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. A claim is vetoed if it violates units or aerodynamic regime. Drag force must satisfy \(F_D=\frac{1}{2}\rho C_D A v^2\) in N, and any claimed \(C_D\) change must be interpreted with \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho vD/\mu\). A claim that “a 9% lace-thickness change directly caused an 11% home-run record” is vetoed because the source does not provide that direct chain; the paper’s mechanism is lace thickness to seam bulging to symmetry to drag, and the 2019 ball breaks the lace-to-shape link [PAGE 11]. A claim that “seam height explains all 2019 drag variation” is vetoed by the committee’s own 35%/65% split [PAGE 17]. A claim that “roundness is irrelevant” is vetoed because the paper notes prior work connecting roundness to “effective seam height” and therefore drag [PAGE 12].

Biomechanical-layer veto. A claim is vetoed if it conflates pitcher grip with ball aerodynamics. The paper’s blister mechanism is mechanical: “Increased lace thickness will produce slightly prouder stitches, creating a ‘bumpier’ seam” [PAGE 5], and blisters are associated with gripping or rubbing seams [PAGE 5]. That is a hand-contact pathway, not a drag pathway. A claim that “thicker laces juiced the ball by increasing drag” is internally inconsistent with the paper’s symmetry argument, because thicker laces were proposed to reduce bulging and increase symmetry [PAGE 5]. A claim that “exit velocity proves the ball changed” is vetoed as input-output conflation; the paper lists “higher exit velocities” as one of several competing theories, not as a physical ball measurement [PAGE 2].

Weather-layer veto. Air density changes drag at fixed \(C_D\): \(F_D\propto \rho\), and \(\rho\) depends on pressure, temperature, and humidity. The paper lists “even climate change” among the theories considered for the Home Run Surge [PAGE 2]. Therefore, a claim that raw home-run counts isolate a ball change is vetoed unless atmospheric state, park, and launch conditions are controlled. The veto is not that weather caused the changes; it is that the source does not license a single-cause attribution from aggregate offense alone. The paper’s evidential strength is physical metrology and batch provenance, not a full weather-controlled causal model.

Provenance-layer veto. A claim is vetoed if it treats retail “authenticated” postseason balls as on-field postseason balls. MLB said the field balls were “produced in the first quarter of 2019” [PAGE 15], while Rawlings said the purchased balls were “commercial” and “not balls that would be used on field” [PAGE 15]. The paper itself concludes that the retail balls “cannot even be expected to have been from 2019, let alone part of the postseason production process” [PAGE 16]. This is a hard cross-layer veto: no aerodynamic inference from that retail sample can be transferred to the on-field postseason population without independent provenance.
