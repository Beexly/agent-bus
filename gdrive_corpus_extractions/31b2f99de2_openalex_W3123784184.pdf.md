<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s causal chain is multi-hop: pre-game market-implied expectations form a rational reference point; the realized game outcome generates a probability surprise \(y-p\); that surprise enters a gain-loss function with loss aversion \(\alpha>\beta\); the surprise raises or lowers the conditional probability \(h\) that a conflictual interaction escalates to violence; and selection into watching the game changes which couples are exposed. The observable object is not a single couple’s \(h\), but an agency-Sunday count of police-reported intimate partner violence, modeled as Poisson with agency fixed effects. The key identification claim is that, conditional on the pre-game market-implied expectation, the game outcome is “as good as random” [PAGE 12], so a within-expectation-bin win/loss contrast can be interpreted causally. The source reports that an upset loss—defined by the ex ante expectation that the home team would win—is associated with an approximately 8 percent increase in at-home male-on-female intimate partner violence [PAGE 5]. The source also states that “negative emotional cues – benchmarked relative to a rationally expected reference point – make a breakdown of control more likely” [PAGE 2]. That is the load-bearing behavioral interpretation: payoff-irrelevant emotional shocks move violence, which is hard to reconcile with a purely instrumental, strategically optimizing account.

The gray-zone audit matters. First, the empirical contrast is conditional on a market-implied expectation, so the result is only as strong as the assumption that no omitted variable is correlated with the realized outcome within expectation bins. Weather is a real source of violence variation, but the source reports that “although other factors, such as the weather, have a significant effect on the rate of family violence” [PAGE 12], the outcome coefficients are stable across weather and audience controls. Second, the timing evidence is a strong discriminator: the source finds that “the spike in violence after an upset loss is concentrated in a narrow time window surrounding the end of the game” [PAGE 27]. A pre-outcome placebo window should not show the same spike; Table 5 reports small and insignificant pre-outcome coefficients for the early window and larger post-outcome coefficients for 1 PM games in the 3–6 PM window [PAGE 44]. Third, the source’s own selection logic predicts attenuation and asymmetry: “the most violence-prone families will avoid watching a game together (or take other protective action) when a loss is likely” [PAGE 5]. This is not a nuisance; it is a recursive-theory-of-mind mechanism. Fourth, the gender-specific result is a gray zone: female-on-male violence does not show the same large positive coefficient [PAGE 47], which the authors interpret as possible differences in emotional engagement or aggression, not as proof of a universal mechanism. Fifth, “all forms of IPV rise following an upset loss” [PAGE 30] is a summary reading, but the precision varies by severity and alcohol involvement. Sixth, non-intimate “friend” violence also rises by a similar magnitude [PAGE 48], which complicates strict intimate-partner specificity while supporting a broader expressive-cue account. The correct inference is therefore not “football causes all family violence”; it is that a measurable fraction of police-reported intimate partner violence behaves like cue-triggered loss of control, with instrumental motives left intact for other incidents.

Formally, the model separates conflict probability \(q\) from escalation probability \(h\), so the observed per-couple risk is \(qh\). The source’s equation is \(h=h_0-\mu(y-p)\), with \(\mu(y-p)=\alpha(y-p)\) for \(y-p<0\) and \(\beta(y-p)\) for \(y-p>0\), and loss aversion \(\alpha>\beta\) [PAGE 7]. Because \(y\) is binary, the loss and win risks are \(h_L=h_0+\alpha p\) and \(h_W=h_0-\beta(1-p)\) [PAGE 8]. Selection gives a cutoff \(q^*(p)=[p u_W+(1-p)u_L-u_0]/[(\alpha-\beta)p(1-p)v]\) [PAGE 9]. The loss-versus-win risk difference is \(\Delta(\text{risk}\mid p)=[\beta+(\alpha-\beta)p]\int_0^{q^*(p)}q\,dF(q)\) [PAGE 10]. The predicted sign is strongest when ex ante victory probability is high, because both the emotional cue and the selection of higher-\(q\) watchers increase. The empirical Poisson model is \(\log(\mu_{jt})=\theta_j+X_{jt}\gamma+g(S_{jt},y_{jt};\lambda)\) [PAGE 22], where \(\mu_{jt}\) is the expected count per agency-Sunday, \(\theta_j\) is a log baseline agency effect, \(\lambda\) are log-rate coefficients, and \(S_{jt}\) is a pre-game market-implied expected-outcome scalar mapped monotonically to \(p_{jt}\). A coefficient \(\lambda=0.080\) corresponds to \(100(e^{0.080}-1)\approx 8.3\%\). The singularity audit is exact: \(p=0\) makes a loss fully anticipated and \(h_L=h_0\); \(p=1\) makes a win fully anticipated and \(h_W=h_0\); \(\alpha=\beta\) makes the selection denominator zero and the cutoff formula inapplicable, with the decision rule becoming type-independent; \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) also degenerates the cutoff expression; \(v=0\) removes the utility cost of violence and again breaks the cutoff; and \(q^*(p)>1\) must be truncated to the support of \(F\). The Poisson mean-variance equality is another breakdown regime, which the source checks with negative-binomial and date-fixed-effect alternatives [PAGE 48].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(q\in[0,1]\) be the probability of a conflictual interaction, with distribution \(F(q)\); let \(h\in[0,1]\) be the conditional probability that a conflictual interaction escalates to violence; let \(p\in[0,1]\) be the ex ante probability of a home-team victory; let \(y\in\{0,1\}\) be the realized outcome; and let \(c=y-p\in[-1,1]\) be the emotional cue in probability-deviation units. All probabilities are dimensionless. The per-couple per-period violence risk is \(r=qh\), a dimensionless probability; population rates are counts per 100,000 person-days; Poisson log-rate coefficients are dimensionless log-count contrasts.

The source operator is
\[
h=h_0-\mu(y-p),\qquad
\mu(c)=
\begin{cases}
\alpha c, & c<0,\\
\beta c, & c>0,
\end{cases}
\]
with \(\alpha>\beta\) for loss aversion [PAGE 7]. Hence
\[
h_L=h_0+\alpha p \quad (y=0),\qquad
h_W=h_0-\beta(1-p)\quad (y=1)
\]
[PAGE 8]. Expected utility by action \(j\in\{0,W,L\}\) is \(u_j-qh_jv\), with \(v>0\) the utility cost of a violent episode [PAGE 9]. The relative payoff of watching is
\[
G(p;q)=p u_W+(1-p)u_L-u_0-(\alpha-\beta)p(1-p)qv.
\]
The zero-payoff threshold for conflict-free types is
\[
p_c=\frac{u_0-u_L}{u_W-u_L},\qquad 0\le p_c<1,
\]
and the watching cutoff is
\[
q^*(p)=\frac{p u_W+(1-p)u_L-u_0}{(\alpha-\beta)p(1-p)v}.
\]
The loss-versus-win risk difference is
\[
\Delta(\text{risk}\mid p)=[\beta+(\alpha-\beta)p]\int_0^{q^*(p)}q\,dF(q)
\]
[PAGE 10]. The source states that “the effect of a loss relative to a win is predicted to be most negative when the ex ante probability of victory is high” [PAGE 25]. The empirical Poisson operator is
\[
\log(\mu_{jt})=\theta_j+X_{jt}\gamma+g(S_{jt},y_{jt};\lambda),
\]
where \(\mu_{jt}\) is the expected count for agency \(j\) on Sunday \(t\), \(\theta_j\) is an agency log baseline, and \(g\) is a function of the pre-game market-implied expected-outcome scalar \(S_{jt}\) and realized outcome \(y_{jt}\) [PAGE 22]. The source’s main estimate is that an upset loss “leads to an 8 percent increase in the number of police reports of at-home male-on-female intimate partner violence” [PAGE 5]. The source also reports that “an upset loss is comparable to the effect of a hot day” [PAGE 34].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes: (i) \(p=0\) gives no loss surprise, so \(h_L=h_0\); (ii) \(p=1\) gives no win surprise, so \(h_W=h_0\); (iii) \(\alpha=\beta\) makes \((\alpha-\beta)p(1-p)v=0\), so \(q^*(p)\) is undefined and the model reduces to a type-independent watching decision; (iv) \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) also breaks the cutoff formula; (v) \(v=0\) breaks the cutoff by eliminating the violence-cost channel; (vi) if the formula returns \(q^*(p)>1\), the economically relevant cutoff is \(\min\{1,q^*(p)\}\); and (vii) the Poisson variance-equals-mean restriction can fail under over-dispersion, which the source checks with negative-binomial and saturated-date models [PAGE 48]. The source’s own identification caveat is that the outcome must be “as good as random” conditional on the pre-game expectation [PAGE 12].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from Becker’s rational-choice crime model to instrumental family-violence models such as Chwe (1990), Tauchen, Witte, and Long (1991), Bowlus and Seitz (2006), Bloch and Rao (2002), and Aizer’s work on intra-family incentives [PAGE 3]. Against that lineage sits the sociological family-conflict paradigm of Straus, Gelles, and Steinmetz (1980) and Gelles and Straus (1988), which treats violence as expressive escalation rather than fully strategic control [PAGE 3]. The source’s bridge is the psychology of self-regulation failure—Baumeister, Heatherton, and Tice (1994) and Baumeister and Heatherton (1996)—combined with Bernheim and Rangel’s cue-triggered decision model and Köszegi and Rabin’s reference-dependent preferences [PAGE 7]. The retrocausal element is precise: the reference point is not the outcome itself but the rationally expected outcome, so the same realized win can be good news or bad news depending on prior expectation. That is why the source can say negative cues are “benchmarked relative to a rationally expected reference point” [PAGE 2]. The historical lineage also includes weather-and-crime work such as Jacob, Lefgren, and Moretti (2007) and sports-spectator aggression work such as Branscombe and Wann (1992) [PAGE 5; PAGE 8].

The multi-week traps are not directly estimated by the one-period model. First, expectations update week by week: a team’s prior win probability is revised after each result, so the same loss can be a larger or smaller surprise depending on the season’s history. Second, high-risk couples can learn to avoid exposure; the source explicitly predicts that “the most violence-prone families will avoid watching a game together (or take other protective action) when a loss is likely” [PAGE 5]. Third, playoff contention changes salience: the source finds that upset losses in playoff-contention games have larger effects [PAGE 45]. Fourth, rivalry and frustration markers—rivalry games and games with unusual sacks, turnovers, or penalties—raise the estimated upset-loss coefficient, with the highly salient category reaching about 15 percent [PAGE 45]. Fifth, repeated exposure could produce either habituation or sensitization across weeks; the one-period cutoff model cannot distinguish those dynamic paths. Sixth, holidays and weather create seasonal baselines against which football cues are interpreted, and the source’s holiday comparisons are large: Christmas +17%, Thanksgiving +22%, Memorial Day +30%, New Year’s Day +19%, New Year’s Eve +32%, and Fourth of July +28% [PAGE 34]. The retrocausal trap is therefore that observed violence on a given Sunday reflects not only that day’s surprise but also the accumulated reference point from prior weeks, the couple’s defensive history, and the salience of the season.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three mechanisms compete. The first is crisis habit regression: a negative cue raises the probability that a conflictual interaction loses control, with no strategic targeting. The source supports this with the payoff-irrelevance of the game outcome, the post-game timing concentration, and the stronger response to upset losses than to upset wins. The second is counter-trap: partners and potential offenders anticipate each other. The agent decides whether to watch; the partner may leave, avoid the room, or reduce provocation; the agent may anticipate that avoidance and alter his own exposure. The source’s model embeds this through the watching cutoff \(q^*(p)\), and the source notes that “partners and children of higher-risk men leave the home or take other defensive actions” [PAGE 8]. This counter-trap can attenuate estimated effects, especially when a loss is likely, and it can produce asymmetric responses because the highest-risk couples self-protect most. The third is clock: the effect should be tied to event time, not to the calendar day as a whole. The source’s timing evidence is the adjudicator. For 1 PM games, the upset-loss coefficient is small and insignificant in the 12–3 PM window, larger in the 3–6 PM window, and not significant later; for 4 PM games, the large coefficient appears in the 6–9 PM window [PAGE 44]. The source concludes that “the spike in violence after an upset loss is concentrated in a narrow time window surrounding the end of the game” [PAGE 27]. That clock pattern favors crisis habit regression over a pure day-level confound. The counter-trap is not eliminated; it is made into a selection channel. The recursive-theory-of-mind audit therefore says: if the effect were purely instrumental and strategically timed, it should not be so tightly locked to the game’s end and so asymmetric between unexpected losses and unexpected wins. If it were purely a clock artifact, the pre-outcome windows should show the same spike; they do not. The remaining ambiguity is whether the post-game window captures loss of control, alcohol-mediated escalation, or couple re-entry into the home after an away outing; the source’s away-from-home negative point estimate is consistent with a venue-shift story, but not decisive [PAGE 47].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The weather/physical veto is the first cross-layer test. Weather genuinely affects violence: the source reports that “an upset loss is comparable to the effect of a hot day” [PAGE 34], and the models include hot, high heat index, cold, windy, rainy, and snowy indicators [PAGE 43]. If local heat, cold, rain, or snow were correlated with the realized game outcome conditional on the pre-game expectation, the outcome coefficient could be biased. The source’s defense is conditional independence: the realized outcome is “as good as random” conditional on the pre-game expectation [PAGE 12], and the estimated upset-loss coefficient is stable across specifications: .083 baseline, .077 with year/week/holiday controls, .080 with weather controls, .074 with audience controls, and .076 in the fullest model [PAGE 43]. That stability is the veto proof against a weather-confounder explanation. The hot-day effect is real but separately estimated; it does not absorb the upset-loss effect.

The biomechanical layer includes alcohol/drug involvement, assault severity, and the physical venue of violence. Alcohol-related incidents show a larger upset-loss coefficient (.127) than the overall estimate (.080), while minor and serious assaults show .096 and .063 respectively [PAGE 46]. That pattern is consistent with alcohol as a moderator or mediator, not a confounder: the source warns that “does not necessarily mean that upset losses lead to greater alcohol use that causes more violence” [PAGE 30]. The physical-venue layer is also probed: away-from-home male-on-female violence has a negative point estimate, while at-home violence is positive [PAGE 47]. That is compatible with couples returning home earlier after an upset loss, shifting the venue of subsequent conflict; it is not a clean falsification. The audience layer is controlled with local television ratings: the source reports that violence rises by almost 1% for each percentage-point increase in households watching, but the game-outcome coefficients are unchanged when this control is added [PAGE 25; PAGE 43]. The cross-layer veto therefore fails to overturn the source’s main claim: weather, audience composition, holiday/week structure, and agency fixed effects are either controlled or shown not to destabilize the outcome coefficient. The remaining physical caveat is that police-reported NIBRS data are voluntary and cover only about 25% of the U.S. population by 2006, disproportionately excluding the largest cities [PAGE 13]; that limits external validity but does not by itself produce the within-agency, within-Sunday pattern.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: pre-outcome placebo veto. Prediction under the source’s causal clock: for 1 PM games, the upset-loss coefficient in the 12–3 PM window should be near zero and smaller than the 3–6 PM post-outcome coefficient. The source reports .024 (SE .071) for 12–3 PM and .142 (SE .066) for 3–6 PM [PAGE 44]. A verifiable veto would trigger if a replication finds a pre-outcome upset-loss coefficient of at least .08 with a 95% interval excluding zero, or if the post-outcome coefficient falls below .03. Such a result would indicate day-level confounding or reverse timing rather than event-locked loss of control.

Scenario 2: weather/heat suppression veto. Prediction: adding heat index, cold, wind, rain, and snow controls should leave the upset-loss log-rate coefficient in the .06–.10 range. The source reports .080 with weather controls [PAGE 43]. A verifiable veto would trigger if the weather-adjusted coefficient drops below .03 or if its 95% interval includes zero while the uncontrolled coefficient remains above .06. That pattern would imply that the apparent emotional-cue effect is largely a weather-channel artifact. A second weather-veto check would trigger if the hot-day indicator alone absorbs the entire upset-loss contrast.

Scenario 3: relationship-specificity veto. Prediction: at-home male-on-female violence should show a positive upset-loss coefficient near .08, female-on-male violence should be close to zero, and non-intimate family violence should not show the same large positive effect. The source reports .080 (SE .027) for at-home male-on-female, .0162 (SE .0585) for female-on-male, and .002 (SE .030) for non-intimate family violence [PAGE 47; PAGE 48]. A verifiable veto would trigger if female-on-male violence shows a statistically significant coefficient of at least .06, or if non-intimate family violence shows at least .06 with a tight interval. Such a result would falsify the claim that the cue is specifically acting on intimate-partner escalation rather than on a broad household-conflict propensity. A weaker, non-vetoing anomaly is already present: friend/acquaintance violence shows a similar .077 coefficient [PAGE 48], which suggests a general expressive-cue channel and should be modeled explicitly in any successor theory.
