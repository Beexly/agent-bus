Let total joint product be \(Q = F(x_1,\dots,x_n; O)\), where \(x_i\) is the service flow of agency \(i\) and \(O\) is the organizational state. The marginal product of agency \(i\) is
\[
\mu_i \;=\; \left.\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x_i}\right|_{O,\;x_{-i}} ,
\]
with units of output-units per service-unit per period.

Wieser's objection formalizes as a non-separability condition. If one unit of agency \(i\) is withdrawn, the output loss is
\[
\Delta Q_{-i} \;=\; Q(x) - Q(x_{-i},\,0) .
\]
The objectors claim
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n \Delta Q_{-i} \;>\; Q(x),
\]
because organizational dislocation makes each individual withdrawal cost more than that agent's separable marginal contribution. Equivalently, the imputation residual
\[
R \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i x_i - Q(x)
\]
cannot be driven to zero under lumpiness, and no exact additive decomposition into factor-specific parts exists.

Knight's scaling correction introduces organizational granularity. Let agency \(i\) be divisible into \(m_i\) blocks of size \(b_i = x_i / m_i\). As \(m_i \to \infty\) (fine blocks relative to total industrial scale),
\[
\frac{Q(x) - Q(x - b_i e_i)}{b_i} \;\to\; \mu_i ,
\]
and the pointwise imputation error
\[
\varepsilon_i \;=\; \Bigl| Q(x) - Q(x - b_i e_i) \Bigr| - b_i \mu_i
\;\to\; 0 .
\]
Thus marginal productivity imputation is an asymptotic property of large, finely grained organizations, not a theorem for small organizations with large productive blocks.

Breakdown/singularity regime: The approximation fails when \(m_i\) is small (few, large blocks), when \(F\) is non-convex in \(O\) (team production, complementarities), or when withdrawal of \(x_i\) changes \(O\) discontinuously. In that regime \(R \neq 0\) and \(\varepsilon_i\) is economically significant.

Footnote operator (Taylor/Knight): Across industries \(k = 1,\dots,K\), let factor proportions be \(p_{ik} = x_{ik} / \sum_j x_{jk}\). Taylor's claim is that specific productivity \(\pi_i\) is identified from cross-industry variation in \(p_{ik}\) alone, even if within-industry proportions \(\partial p_{ik} / \partial t\) are invariant. Knight amends this: both cross-industry proportion differences and within-industry variability contribute to imputation. This is an identification assumption, not a physical necessity; it breaks down if factor proportions are fixed across all industries (no variation in \(p_{ik}\)) or if the "factor" concept itself is ill-defined (Knight's cross-reference to p. 104).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage on this page runs backward to Carl Menger's productivity theory of distribution and forward into Knight's own theory of profit. Wieser, in Der Natürliche Werth (3rd section, "Die natürliche Zurechnung des Produktiven Ertrages"), attempts to refute Menger by arguing that marginal productivity cannot divide the total product because factor withdrawal dislocates organization. Hobson, in The Industrial System (chap. v, appendix, pp. 112–20), extends the attack, citing Wicksteed, and declares the distribution problem impossible. Padan offers a quasi-mathematical variant in the Journal of Political Economy (March 1901). F. M. Taylor supplies the counter-exposition that Knight partially endorses: specific productivity can be imputed from cross-industry differences in factor proportions alone.

The multi-week trap embedded in this genealogy is the "small-organization fallacy." An analyst operating over a multi-week horizon in a concentrated industry—few firms, large capital blocks, key-personnel dependencies—who applies marginal productivity shares as if \(m_i \to \infty\) will systematically misattribute output losses. When a large block is withdrawn (a specialized machine, a lead engineer), the observed output drop \(\Delta Q_{-i}\) exceeds the imputed marginal share \(\mu_i b_i\). The residual is then either blamed on the wrong factor or left as unexplained profit. Knight's resolution is that this residual is precisely the domain of uncertainty: not a measurable risk with an insurable probability distribution, but a dislocation cost arising from non-convex organizational structure. Historically, the trap is to treat Taylor's cross-industry proportional identification as a universal law; it is valid only when cross-industry proportion variation exists and when the "factor" concept is coherent—both of which Knight questions.
