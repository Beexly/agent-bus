Marx argued that property relations (slavery, feudalism, capitalism) are determined by the needs of the prevailing mode of production. Knight agrees with this functionalist perspective—that property forms evolve to serve economic efficiency—but vehemently disagrees with Marx's teleological view of history and class conflict. Where Marx saw inherent contradictions leading to revolution, Knight sees a gradual, evolutionary adaptation of institutions (like private property) to handle uncertainty and spur innovation.

The reference to the Middle Ages and common ownership invokes a long-standing Whig historiography of economics, contrasting static, guild-controlled medieval craft production with the dynamic, competitive industrial capitalism emerging in Knight's era. His genealogy also includes Alfred Marshall's distinction between "economic man" and real human behavior, and the Chicago School's precursor ideas about entrepreneurship. The "desire to increase wealth" is a direct counterpoint to the neoclassical assumption that capital is merely deferred consumption (as in Irving Fisher's model). Knight was pulling from a rich vein of Austrian-inspired thought (like Böhm-Bawerk) that emphasized time preference and the unique role of the entrepreneur, but he grounds it in a more sociological, institutional framework.

The entrepreneur's "irrational" urge is the fuel that the institution of private property harnesses to overcome the stagnation of routine. The trap is mistaking Knight's descriptive account of a historical engine for a prescriptive endorsement of all outcomes produced by that engine. He is explaining why capitalism exists and grows, not declaring it morally optimal. His critique of the "instinct" theory suggests he views it as a complex social-psychological phenomenon, not a simple animal drive.
