For example, higher heart rates may prompt more frequent and real-time streaming transmission of data” [PAGE 55]. This is a second-order response: the device infers user distress from h(t) and preemptively streams before the user manually requests it. The counter-trap is intended to reduce user anxiety and ensure data capture during the crisis. However, it introduces a recursive trap: if the classifier confuses exercise tachycardia, caffeine, or fever with emotional crisis, the device enters high-frequency streaming unnecessarily, draining E_{bat} and raising device temperature. The user, noticing a dead or warm device during the next crisis, loses trust and reverts to manual on-demand checks, which the device then interprets as renewed crisis signals, closing the loop.

The clock layer imposes an exogenous forcing function independent of the user’s mental state: “the system may be set to stream automatically in the morning and following a time stamp” [PAGE 55]. This periodic transmitter acts as a zeitgeber for data flow but not necessarily for physiology. If the user’s internal clock is misaligned with the device’s scheduled uploads, the system may transmit during sleep inertia or low-HRV-value windows while remaining silent during the actual physiological crisis. The recursive theory of mind thus contains three coupled clocks: the user’s crisis-checking habit, the device’s heart-rate-triggered telemetry, and the system’s scheduled morning upload. Misalignment among any two generates a veto condition: either battery exhaustion during user-perceived need, or data silence during device-perceived need. Stability requires that \alpha in f_{\text{tx}}(t) be bounded and that the morning upload not coincide with the user’s usual sleep period; otherwise the recursion diverges toward device failure at the moment of maximal user dependence.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The wearable telemetry stack must be adjudicated by a veto hierarchy that overrides the nominal transmission policy. Four layers are considered: physical, biomechanical, weather, and informational.

Physical layer. The radio draw is bounded by battery energy E_{bat} and thermal dissipation. Sustained 3G transmission at P_{tx} = 1.73 W [PAGE 55] conducts heat to the skin-contact surface. If the steady-state skin temperature T_{skin} exceeds the burn threshold (approximately 41 °C for prolonged contact), the physical layer vetoes transmission: \mathcal{T} = \emptyset. The governing inequality is
\[
P_{tx} + P_{\text{metabolic}} \le h_{\text{conv}} A (T_{\text{skin}} - T_{\text{amb}}),
\]
where h_{\text{conv}} is the convective heat-transfer coefficient (W/m^2/K), A is the contact area (m^2), and T_{\text{amb}} is ambient temperature (K). Singularity: as T_{\text{amb}} \to T_{\text{skin}}, the right-hand side vanishes and no continuous transmission is thermally permissible.

Biomechanical layer. Movement data m(t) is recorded as raw input [PAGE 55], but high-variance motion corrupts the PPG-derived RR interval signal. Define a signal quality index SQI(t) \in [0,1]. A biomechanical veto fires when SQI(t) < SQI_{\min}; low-quality RR intervals are not transmitted, conserving bandwidth and avoiding false HRV alerts. This is distinct from the patent’s generic storage rule (“Regardless of the data transmission scheme, the system stores all the data it collects” [PAGE 55]) because storage may continue while transmission is suppressed.

Weather layer. Ambient conditions affect both battery chemistry and RF propagation. At T_{\text{amb}} < 0 °C, lithium-ion internal resistance rises, reducing maximum sustainable P_{tx}. If P_{tx,max} falls below the level required to maintain R = 960 kbps on 3G, the effective energy per bit \epsilon = P_{tx}/R rises toward the 2G value of 3.25\times10^{-6} J/bit or worse. Rain and foliage add attenuation, further lowering R. The weather veto shifts the system to store-and-forward mode until the link budget recovers.

Informational layer. The backlog constraint B(t) \le S_{\max} vetoes new acquisition when local storage is full, unless the system overwrites oldest data (FIFO dropout), which creates an irrecoverable gap in the HRV record.

Adjudication priority stack:
1. Physical/thermal veto (user safety).
2. Biomechanical SQI veto (data integrity).
3. Battery-reserve veto (maintain minimum E_{reserve} for emergency timestamps).
4. Nominal adaptive streaming per [PAGE 55].

If any veto fires, \mathcal{T}_{\text{mode}} is replaced by local storage only; no over-the-air transmission occurs until the veto condition clears. This prevents the failure mode in which the device streams continuously during a cardiac crisis, overheats, and shuts down exactly when the user needs the data most.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable predictions are specified below. RLVR denotes reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards; each scenario includes an observable metric and an expected outcome under the veto.

Scenario 1 — Proximity veto (store-and-forward).
Setup: User sleeps with the wearable on but leaves the linked phone in another room; no WiFi access point is within range. Condition: “the system may avoid data transmission when it is not within close proximity of a streaming outlet” [PAGE 55]. Veto: Bluetooth and cellular uplink are suppressed because no streaming outlet is proximate. All raw and processed data are stored locally because “Regardless of the data transmission scheme, the system stores all the data it collects” [PAGE 55]. Prediction: Cloud-side HRV timestamps will be absent for the entire sleep period, while on-device storage occupancy increases linearly at R_raw until the morning auto-stream trigger fires [PAGE 55]. Verifiable metric: delta between local storage fill level and cloud-uploaded record count; expected value is a positive storage backlog with zero cloud uploads during the proximity-null interval.

Scenario 2 — Cardiac-driven thermal veto.
Setup: User performs high-intensity interval training, driving h(t) above the adaptive threshold so that “higher heart rates may prompt more frequent and real-time streaming transmission of data” [PAGE 55]. The radio transmits continuously at P_{tx} = 1.73 W (3G) [PAGE 55]. Veto: Skin temperature reaches T_{\text{skin}} > T_{\max}; the physical layer throttles or pauses the radio despite high HR. Prediction: A gap appears in the real-time cloud HRV feed precisely during the interval of maximal heart rate, followed by a burst upload after cool-down. Verifiable metric: cloud data latency \Delta t_{\text{cloud}}(t) spikes concurrently with h(t) > h_{\text{crisis}} and device temperature telemetry crossing T_{\max}; the real-time stream resumes only after T_{skin} falls below the hysteresis threshold.

Scenario 3 — Cold-weather capacity veto.
Setup: User wears the device outdoors during sleep with T_{\text{amb}} < 0 °C. Veto: Lithium-ion internal resistance increases, lowering sustainable P_{tx}; the 3G link cannot maintain R = 960 kbps at P_{tx} = 1.73 W [PAGE 55]. The system either drops to 2G (higher \epsilon per bit) or reverts to store-and-forward. Prediction: Upload success rate per minute decreases, energy per successfully delivered bit increases from 1.80\times10^{-6} J/bit toward the 2G value of 3.25\times10^{-6} J/bit, and total nocturnal battery depletion rises despite unchanged data volume. Verifiable metric: ratio of uploaded bits to battery discharge (bits/J) measured before and during cold exposure; expected to decline by at least the computed \epsilon_{2G}/\epsilon_{3G} \approx 1.81 factor if the link degrades to 2G-equivalent efficiency, or to near zero if store-and-forward dominates until morning.
