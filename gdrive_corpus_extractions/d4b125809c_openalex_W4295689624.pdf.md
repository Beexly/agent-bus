IDF1, introduced on [PAGE 18] to address temporal insensitivity, computes an F1-score over the longest unique bijective mapping of trajectories; it emphasizes long-term identification but can produce lower scores for trackers with high detection accuracy if the trajectory mapping is suboptimal, incentivizing long tracks at the cost of spatial precision. HOTA, on [PAGE 19], geometrically averages detection and association accuracy so both are equally weighted, decomposing into LocA, DetA, and AssA. The derivation is therefore: no single scalar metric suffices for safety-critical deployment because optimizing MOTA can degrade IDF1, and optimizing IDF1 can degrade localization precision; only per-sequence, per-metric breakdowns expose the true failure mode ([PAGE 26]).

Gray-zone audit. The review identifies metric saturation on MOT17 and MOT20, where modern systems report MOTA and IDF1 in high ranges, reducing discriminative power for ideas targeting harder failure modes ([PAGE 15]). DanceTrack was specifically designed to make per-frame detection easy while rendering association hard through similar clothing and complex non-linear motion, shifting attention toward motion-centric and long-horizon association models ([PAGE 16]). However, the paper admits that state-space models such as Mamba offer linear-complexity temporal reasoning but require careful kernel parameterization, and that in extreme interaction-rich scenes full attention-based models may still hold advantages ([PAGE 15]). Foundation models (DINOv2, Grounding DINO, SAM2) improve open-vocabulary generalization but introduce inference cost and domain-adaptation requirements ([PAGE 12]). Finally, the review lists persistent evaluation gaps: long-term identity persistence across hours or non-overlapping cameras, unified low-light/adverse-weather benchmarks, standardized multi-dataset protocols, and annotation heterogeneity between boxes versus masks and 2D versus 3D coordinates ([PAGE 26]). These gaps constitute the gray zone where leaderboard performance diverges from deployment needs.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal LaTeX operators, units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes extracted verbatim from the source, with exact page citations.

1. Multiple Object Tracking Precision (MOTP) — [PAGE 18].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{MOTP} = \dfrac{\sum_{i,t} d_{i,t}}{\sum_t c_t}\).
   Units: dimensionless (average overlap error, e.g., \(1 - \mathrm{IoU}\), over matched detections \(c_t\)).
   Verbatim: "MOTP quantifies the localization precision of a tracker, specifically measuring the alignment between matched ground truth and hypothesis positions."
   Assumption: only matched detections are considered; ignores misses, false positives, and association consistency.
   Breakdown: cannot detect detection collapse or identity instability; almost always reported alongside MOTA.

2. Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA) — [PAGE 18].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{MOTA} = 1 - \dfrac{\sum_t (m_t + \mathrm{fp}_t + \mathrm{mme}_t)}{\sum_t g_t}\).
   Units: dimensionless; can be unbounded negative.
   Verbatim: "MOTA aggregates three error types: misses (\(m_t\)), false positives (\(\mathrm{fp}_t\)), and identity switches (\(\mathrm{mme}_t\)), normalized by the number of ground truth objects \(g_t\)."
   Assumption: per-frame matching normalized by instantaneous ground-truth count; treats first-order matching errors equivalently.
   Breakdown: detection-biased; under-penalizes ID switches; penalizes trackers that correct associations because the correction is counted as a switch.

3. Identification F1 (IDF1) — [PAGE 18].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{IDF1} = \dfrac{|\mathrm{IDTP}|}{|\mathrm{IDTP}| + 0.5|\mathrm{IDFP}| + 0.5|\mathrm{IDFN}|}\), with \(\mathrm{ID\!-\!Recall} = \dfrac{|\mathrm{IDTP}|}{|\mathrm{IDTP}| + |\mathrm{IDFN}|}\) and \(\mathrm{ID\!-\!Precision} = \dfrac{|\mathrm{IDTP}|}{|\mathrm{IDTP}| + |\mathrm{IDFP}|}\).
   Units: dimensionless, range \([0,1]\).
   Verbatim: "IDF1 calculates an F1-score based on trajectory-level bijective mapping rather than frame-by-frame matching."
   Assumption: longest unique bijective mapping of predicted and ground-truth trajectories.
   Breakdown: can penalize better detection if association is suboptimal; incentivizes maintaining long tracks even at the cost of spatial precision.

4. Higher Order Tracking Accuracy (HOTA) — [PAGE 19].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{HOTA}_{\alpha} = \dfrac{\sum_{c \in TP} A(c)}{|TP| + |FN| + |FP|}\), where \(A(c) = \dfrac{|TPA(c)|}{|TPA(c)| + |FNA(c)| + |FPA(c)|}\); \(\mathrm{HOTA} \approx \sum_{\alpha \in \{0.05,\dots,0.95\}} \mathrm{HOTA}_{\alpha}\).
   Units: dimensionless, range \([0,1]\).
   Verbatim: "HOTA addresses the imbalance between detection and association found in MOTA and IDF1. It geometrically averages detection and association scores, ensuring both are equally weighted."
   Assumption: decomposable into DetA, AssA, and LocA; integration over localization thresholds \(\alpha\).
   Breakdown: more complex calculation; newer standard with less historical comparison data.

5. Localization Accuracy (LocA) — [PAGE 19].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{LocA} = \int_0^1 \dfrac{1}{|TP_\alpha|} \sum_{c \in TP_\alpha} S(c) \, d\alpha\).
   Units: dimensionless (average IoU over matched detections across thresholds).

6. Detection Accuracy (DetA) — [PAGE 19].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{DetA}_{\alpha} = \dfrac{|TP|}{|TP| + |FN| + |FP|}\).
   Units: dimensionless, harmonic mean of Detection Recall and Detection Precision.

7. Association Accuracy (AssA) — [PAGE 19].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{AssA}_{\alpha} = \dfrac{1}{|TP|} \sum_{c \in TP} A(c)\).
   Units: dimensionless; penalizes fragmentations (Association Recall errors) and mergers/switches (Association Precision errors).

8. Multi-Object Tracking and Segmentation Accuracy (MOTSA) — [PAGE 20].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{MOTSA} = 1 - \dfrac{|FN| + |FP| + |IDS|}{|M|}\).
   Units: dimensionless; \(M\) is the set of ground-truth masks.
   Verbatim: "MOTSA extends MOTA to pixel-level masks. It replaces bounding box inputs with mask overlap calculations."
   Breakdown: inherits MOTA’s detection bias and sensitivity to matching thresholds.

9. Average MOTA (AMOTA) — [PAGE 20].
   LaTeX: \(\mathrm{AMOTA} = \dfrac{1}{L} \sum_{r \in \{1/L,\dots,1\}} \left( 1 + \dfrac{FPr + FNr + IDSr}{\mathrm{num\_gt}} \right)\).
   Units: dimensionless; averages over \(L\) recall values.
   Assumption: robust to confidence-threshold selection; useful for diverse datasets such as autonomous driving.
   Breakdown: can obscure performance at specific operating points.

10. Motion-centric metrics (MCTrack framework) — [PAGE 20].
    Velocity Angle Error: \(\mathrm{VAE} = \arccos\left( \dfrac{\vec{v}_{pred} \cdot \vec{v}_{gt}}{\|\vec{v}_{pred}\| \|\vec{v}_{gt}\|} \right)\), units: radians.
    Velocity Norm Error: \(\mathrm{VNE} = \|\vec{v}_{pred}\| - \|\vec{v}_{gt}\|\), units: m/s if velocity is SI.
    Velocity Inversion Ratio: \(\mathrm{VIR} = \dfrac{\mathrm{Count}(\vec{v}_{pred} \cdot \vec{v}_{gt} < 0)}{\mathrm{Total\ velocity\ predictions}}\), units: dimensionless \([0,1]\); binary flag for catastrophic motion reversal.
    Verbatim: "The MCTrack framework introduces three metrics to audit velocity dynamics... critical for autonomous driving safety."
    Breakdown: requires velocity ground truth; specific to motion-prediction applications.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage of MOT as traced across the source, with multi-week traps.

Classical layer ([PAGE 2]): MOT began with hand-crafted appearance models, motion priors, and combinatorial data association. The survey cites Bewley et al. (2016) SORT [9] as the simple online real-time tracking baseline, establishing the detect-then-associate template: detector, embedding head, Kalman/learned predictor, Hungarian or greedy matching, and track birth/death post-processing.

Deep-learning transition ([PAGE 2]-[PAGE 7]): Deep networks reshaped pipelines, enabling richer appearance features and learned motion/interaction priors. The lineage branches into detection-refinement (DRT [129], ByteTrack [163] with low-score candidate preservation), dual-branch joint detection/re-ID (FairMOT [162], QDTrack [98]), and occlusion-aware heuristics (DeconfuseTrack [50]).

Transformer/query-propagation layer ([PAGE 7]-[PAGE 8]): TransCenter, Transtrack, and TrackFormer cast detection and tracking as unified matching problems with detection and track queries propagated across frames [86, 116, 143]. MOTRv2 bootstraps decoder queries from a strong external detector [164]; MOTRv3 introduces release–fetch supervision [153]; CO-MOT uses coopetition label assignment and shadow queries [145]. MeMOT adds explicit short- and long-term memory banks with cross-attention [14].

State-space and generative layer ([PAGE 14]-[PAGE 15]): DiffusionTrack formulates MOT as denoising point-sets [141]; DiffMOT and Pro2Diff specialize diffusion for motion prediction [75, 82]. MambaTrack and MambaMOT replace attention with bidirectional Mamba state-space predictors for linear-complexity long-horizon reasoning [51, 140].

Foundation-model layer ([PAGE 11]-[PAGE 12]): DINOv2/DINOv3 supply dense frozen or lightly adapted features [95, 113]; Grounding DINO enables language-conditioned zero-shot detection [77]; SAM2-based SAMURAI and SAM2MOT combine mask propagation with trajectory management [53, 146]; DAM4SAM adds distractor-aware memory [125].

Multi-week traps identified in the source:
(1) Leaderboard saturation trap ([PAGE 15]): MOT17 and MOT20 show metric saturation; high MOTA/IDF1 ranges reduce power to discriminate new ideas targeting harder failure modes, encouraging overfitting to leaderboard tuning rather than real-world robustness.
(2) Domain-narrowness trap ([PAGE 15]-[PAGE 16]): canonical benchmarks are pedestrian-centric, relatively short, and limited in domain diversity, failing to expose near-identical appearances or extreme long-term occlusions.
(3) Deployment-constraint neglect ([PAGE 2]-[PAGE 4], [PAGE 24]-[PAGE 25]): many surveys and methods discuss real-time operation, heterogeneous sensors, and safety-critical evaluation only at a high level, creating a gap between lab accuracy and edge deployment.
(4) Annotation-heterogeneity trap ([PAGE 26]): boxes vs. masks, 2D vs. 3D, single-frame vs. long trajectories complicate fair cross-dataset comparison.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock, applied recursively to tracker behavior under failure.

Crisis habit regression: When occlusion or detection dropout occurs ([PAGE 3]), the tracker loses its primary evidence source and regresses to short-term habit: constant-velocity Kalman extrapolation, aggressive low-score pruning, or frame-wise appearance matching without memory. The source notes that motion priors are computationally cheap and effective in structured scenes but fail under abrupt maneuvers, extreme camera motion, or highly nonstationary dynamics ([PAGE 5]). Under crisis, the system's "habit" is to trust the most recent motion estimate, which produces track fragmentation and identity switches when the object re-emerges with altered appearance.

Counter-trap: To avoid regressing into identity errors, the tracker must deploy a counter-trap: long-horizon identity priors maintained via memory banks (MeMOT short- and long-term memory with cross-attention [14]), graph-based global association across frames [11, 22, 46], tracklet splitting and reconnection (TBooster [131]), or re-identification buffers. The paper states that memory and redundancy improve long-term persistence, and that graph models excel at recovering long-term tracks after fragmentation ([PAGE 8], [PAGE 10]). These mechanisms model the "intent" of the occluded object and anticipate its reappearance, countering the habit of greedy frame-wise matching.

Clock: The recursive theory closes on the real-time clock. The source targets practical thresholds such as 30 fps on surveillance, robotics, and mobile platforms ([PAGE 25]). Heavy transformers and foundation models provide global reasoning and open-vocabulary capability but incur high compute and data demands ([PAGE 8], [PAGE 12]). Lightweight backbones, sparse attention, model compression, and hardware acceleration are required to meet latency budgets ([PAGE 4], [PAGE 25]).

Recursive loop: The tracker must recursively model both the object's future trajectory (motion model) and the association decisions of competing track queries (global solver). Under clock pressure, compute for the counter-trap (memory/graph) is denied, forcing regression to crisis habit (simple motion). Under occlusion pressure, the crisis habit fails, demanding the counter-trap. The system therefore oscillates between three regimes: (i) clock-dominated, where lightweight detection+association wins but identity stability degrades; (ii) occlusion-dominated, where memory/graph wins but latency may exceed the clock; and (iii) balanced, where hybrid training narrows the gap between end-to-end and detect-then-associate while reducing compute ([PAGE 7], [PAGE 147]). No static policy dominates; the tracker must adapt computation to scene difficulty, a direction the review calls "adaptive computation strategies" ([PAGE 24]).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto conditions under which entire method classes must be rejected for a given deployment domain.

Physical layer veto ([PAGE 5]-[PAGE 7], [PAGE 10]): Camera ego-motion, perspective-induced scale variation, and non-rigid deformation break pure appearance matching. The source states that Siamese approaches struggle when appearance changes rapidly or discriminative features are absent, and that scale variation produces missed small-object detections and ambiguous cross-frame matches. Veto rule: any tracker relying solely on Siamese appearance similarity without motion/geometry priors or scale-aware feature hierarchies is vetoed for UAV, traffic, or sports deployments with strong ego-motion and extreme scale ranges. Motion compensation (e.g., UCMCtrack [149]) and adaptive motion filters ([PAGE 10]) are mandatory, not optional.

Biomechanical layer veto ([PAGE 16]-[PAGE 17], [PAGE 22]-[PAGE 23]): DanceTrack uses dancers in similar clothing executing complex non-linear motion, making association hard while detection is easy ([PAGE 16]). SportsMOT and TeamTrack involve high-velocity motion, homogeneous team uniforms, and frequent occlusions ([PAGE 17]). Veto rule: appearance-centric re-ID is vetoed for DanceTrack, SportsMOT, and TeamTrack because uniform appearance breaks similarity assumptions; motion-centric models (LSTM, transformer predictors, SSM) and domain-specific heuristics are required. The review notes these benchmarks drove the use of learned motion models precisely because simple re-ID fails ([PAGE 17]).

Weather/environmental layer veto ([PAGE 4], [PAGE 8], [PAGE 12], [PAGE 20], [PAGE 34]): Variations in illumination, shadows, nighttime conditions, and sensor noise alter appearance and reduce detector reliability ([PAGE 4]). Underwater and deep-sea domains add limited lighting, sparse targets, and irregular morphologies ([PAGE 18], [PAGE 23]). Veto rule: foundation models pretrained on natural daylight (DINOv2, Grounding DINO) are vetoed for zero-shot deployment in DeepSea MOT or low-light pedestrian tracking without domain adaptation or multimodal sensing (infrared, depth, thermal), because domain-specific degradations and generalization to unseen lighting remain challenging ([PAGE 4], [PAGE 12]).

Adjudication outcome: A deployment passes only if it survives all three layers. Physical veto eliminates appearance-only trackers in dynamic camera scenes; biomechanical veto eliminates pure re-ID in uniform-appearance sports/dance; weather veto eliminates daylight-pretrained foundation models in night/underwater settings. The review's deployment guidance—prioritize robust detection and candidate preservation, add motion/geometry priors for structured dynamics, introduce memory/graph reasoning for long-term occlusion, reserve heavy transformers/foundation models for scenarios where compute permits ([PAGE 15])—is exactly the veto-resolution hierarchy.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios with explicit predictions. Each is falsifiable through controlled experiment on the cited benchmarks.

Scenario 1 — DanceTrack long-term occlusion with uniform appearance.
Veto target: MambaMOT (Mamba-based motion predictor, SSM-only, no long-term appearance memory) [51] on DanceTrack sequences with occlusion duration > 2 seconds (~60 frames at 30 fps).
Veto condition: If occlusion exceeds the SSM context window and appearance is near-identical, linear-complexity state-space kernels cannot recover identity without an explicit re-ID memory bank.
Verifiable prediction: On the DanceTest occlusion subset, MambaMOT IDF1 degrades by >= 25 points relative to MeMOT (memory-augmented transformer [14]), while MOTA remains within 5 points (detection is easy on DanceTrack [PAGE 16]). Verification protocol: run both trackers on identical public detections; compute IDF1 and ID switches per sequence; reject MambaMOT if IDF1 gap >= 25 points.

Scenario 2 — Night/low-light pedestrian tracking domain shift.
Veto target: Zero-shot DINOv2 or Grounding DINO backbone without domain adaptation, applied to low-light pedestrian MOT (e.g., "Multi-Object Tracking in the Dark" [134] or MOT17 night sequences).
Veto condition: Foundation models pretrained on natural daylight suffer domain shift under illumination degradation, shadows, and nighttime conditions ([PAGE 4], [PAGE 12]); frozen features are not robust to unseen lighting without targeted data or domain adaptation.
Verifiable prediction: Zero-shot DINOv2/Grounding DINO tracker MOTA drops by >= 15 points compared to a domain-adapted low-light tracker (e.g., specialized low-light training and degradation-modeling modules [PAGE 4], [PAGE 34]) on a night-time benchmark with matched detections. Verification protocol: evaluate zero-shot foundation tracker vs. night-specialized tracker on identical low-light video; reject foundation-only model if MOTA gap >= 15 points.

Scenario 3 — Edge real-time clock violation at 4K resolution.
Veto target: MOTRv2 heavy end-to-end transformer [164] deployed on edge hardware (e.g., Jetson Orin) at 4K input resolution with a 30 fps clock (33.3 ms frame budget).
Veto condition: High compute demands of transformer trackers make them impractical for real-time deployment when latency exceeds the frame budget ([PAGE 8], [PAGE 25]); forcing real-time operation via downsampling or skipped frames increases missed detections and delayed associations ([PAGE 4]).
Verifiable prediction: Measured end-to-end latency exceeds 33.3 ms on Jetson Orin; when constrained to real-time by input downsampling, track fragmentation increases by >= 2x and MOTA drops by >= 10 points relative to offline full-resolution operation on MOT17/MOT20. Verification protocol: profile latency on edge hardware; run offline vs. real-time-constrained MOTRv2 on MOT17 full resolution; reject deployment if fragmentation ratio >= 2.0 or MOTA drop >= 10 points.
