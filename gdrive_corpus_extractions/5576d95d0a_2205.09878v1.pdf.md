I traced the source as a multi-stage probabilistic assignment chain rather than as a single detector. The paper states the goal directly: the team is trying to implement computer vision algorithms “capable of assigning detected helmet impacts to correct players via tracking information” [PAGE 1]. That sentence forces the reasoning order: first detect helmets, then maintain temporally coherent tracks, then associate detections with player tracking coordinates, then resolve team identity, then output final player labels. The paper makes that order explicit in the staged pipeline: “Use YOLOv5 for detecting helmets,” “Use deepsort to track the helmet,” “Use ICP (Iterative Closest Point) and the Hungarian algorithm to assign helmet boxes to the tracking data,” and “Divide helmets into two clusters using k-means based on the color of the helmet” [PAGE 3]. Later stages add temporal regularization through homography: “We modify homography transform in a preceding frame to get that of current frame” [PAGE 4].

The first derivation step is detection-to-track consistency. YOLOv5 produces helmet boxes with confidence values, and DeepSort converts those boxes into temporally coherent tracks. The source says DeepSort “mainly consists of 2 algorithms. They are Kalman filter and Hungarian Algorithm,” and that the Kalman filter “helps us to track the helmets and also helps us to estimate the exact position of helmet even when the detected Helmet in frame Number N gets hidden in frame number N+1” [PAGE 4]. This matters because helmet impacts are often visually degraded. The source admits: “Since helmets with impact are often only partially visible, we had to set lower thresholds for confidence and IOU” [PAGE 3]. Therefore, the detection layer is not merely a detector; it is a recall-preserving operator. If the impact helmet is missed in the frame where contact occurs, all downstream assignment layers inherit that loss.

The second derivation step is geometric association. The source uses tracking CSV data containing player coordinates, orientation, speed, and acceleration [PAGE 2], and then uses ICP and Hungarian assignment to match helmet boxes to player tracking data [PAGE 4]. The cost matrix is built from “shape context” and “cosine distance matrix between this sensor information and bounding box information” [PAGE 4]. This implies a formal assignment problem: each detected helmet box is matched to a player track under a distance or similarity cost, subject to one-to-one constraints. The Hungarian algorithm then produces a minimum-cost assignment. If the cost matrix is noisy, the assignment becomes unstable. The gray zone is that adjacent players may have similar coordinates, especially in pileups or near the line of scrimmage, so the cosine or Euclidean cost may not separate them cleanly.

The third derivation step is team disambiguation. The source says: “We divide these helmet boxes into two clusters based on the color of helmets using kmeans” [PAGE 4]. This is not just cosmetic; team identity constrains the final label. If two helmet detections belong to opposing teams but are close together, color clustering can break the symmetry. However, k-means with two clusters is fragile when helmet colors are similar under stadium lighting, when mud or shadows alter color statistics, or when one team’s helmets appear in multiple color classes. The paper does not provide a formal color-space model, so this stage should be treated as a soft prior, not a deterministic truth source.

The fourth derivation step is temporal propagation. The source uses homography because “player positions of adjacent frames are similar, the homography matrix should be also similar” [PAGE 4]. It applies the previous frame transform to current tracking data, then searches among “homography matrices in 60 preceding frames and selected one with best cost” [PAGE 5]. This is a multi-frame regularizer. It helps when a helmet is occluded for a short interval. It becomes dangerous if the field plane assumption is violated, if the camera motion is large, or if the selected homography is based on outlier player tracks. The source also acknowledges frame-level misalignment between views: “25 plays out of 60 doesn’t match and the difference is mostly 1 frame but there are 7 frame difference also” [PAGE 2]. That is a direct temporal gray zone. A seven-frame offset can materially change which player is closest to an impact, especially because movement after the snap is high [PAGE 2].

The final derivation step is label selection. The source says: “we created the cost matrix based on percentage of labels in the same deepsort helmets” [PAGE 6]. This means the final label is not chosen only from the current frame; it is chosen from historical co-occurrence. Labels that frequently appear in the same DeepSort track receive priority. This is a sensible prior, but it can lock in early errors. If a helmet is misassigned early, the historical cost matrix may reinforce that mistake. The source also notes that there are often fewer than 22 players in the video and that they remove players by cropping tracking coordinates with a rectangle [PAGE 6]. That is a spatial prior, but it can fail if the true player is outside the chosen rectangle or if non-player persons are included in the tracking data.

The gray-zone audit therefore has five fault lines. First, detection recall for partially visible impact helmets [PAGE 3]. Second, temporal misalignment between sideline and endzone views [PAGE 2]. Third, ambiguous player proximity in the assignment cost [PAGE 4]. Fourth, color cluster instability in k-means team separation [PAGE 4]. Fifth, historical label reinforcement in the final cost matrix [PAGE 6]. The evaluation metric intensifies these faults because impact helmets are heavily weighted: “The helmets that are involved in an impact are 1000x more weighted to calculate the accuracy metric” [PAGE 7]. The formal weighted accuracy equation on [PAGE 8] confirms that impact assignment dominates non-impact assignment. Therefore, the model must prioritize impact recall even when ordinary helmet detection appears adequate. The paper also reports that the best confidence range for their data was between 0.2 and 0.6 [PAGE 9], which is consistent with a recall-oriented regime rather than a precision-only regime.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source can be formalized as a sequence of operators acting on image, coordinate, and probability spaces. All equations below use units explicitly: pixel coordinates are in pixels unless normalized, time is in video frames, probabilities and confidences are dimensionless, and costs are dimensionless assignment costs unless a log-likelihood interpretation is stated.

1. Detection operator. Let \(I_t\) be the image at frame \(t\). YOLOv5 produces a set of helmet detections
\[
D_t = \{(b_i, c_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_t},
\]
where \(b_i=(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i)\) is a bounding box in pixel units and \(c_i\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless confidence score. The source trained “YOLOv5x6 with an image size of 1280” [PAGE 3] and used frames after the snap [PAGE 3]. Assumption: the detector is conditioned on helmet appearance and does not directly encode player identity. Breakdown regime: if an impact helmet is occluded or partially outside the image, the detector may fail to produce a box; the source acknowledges that impact helmets are “often only partially visible” and therefore lowers confidence and IoU thresholds [PAGE 3]. If \(D_t=\emptyset\), all downstream association operators receive no evidence.

2. Detection losses. The source names “box loss,” “Obj loss,” and “cls loss” during training analysis [PAGE 8]. A formal decomposition is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{box}} = 1-\mathrm{IoU}(b,\hat b),
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{obj}} = -\sum \left[y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\right],
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} = -\sum y\log p,
\]
where \(y\) is the ground-truth indicator and \(p\) is predicted probability. If natural logarithms are used, the units are nats; if base-2 logarithms are used, the units are bits. Breakdown: box loss is undefined when predicted and ground-truth boxes do not overlap; objectness loss becomes unstable under severe class imbalance; classification loss assumes calibrated class probabilities, which the source does not prove.

3. Intersection-over-union evaluation. The source states the competition IoU rule as
\[
\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{A\cap B}{A\cup B} \ge 0.35
\]
[PAGE 8]. Here \(A\) and \(B\) are planar regions in pixel space, and IoU is dimensionless. Assumption: boxes are axis-aligned image regions. Breakdown regimes: (a) if \(A\cup B=\emptyset\), IoU is undefined; (b) if the union is very small, IoU becomes hypersensitive to one-pixel changes; (c) if a helmet box is truncated by the frame boundary, IoU can penalize correct localization because the visible evidence is incomplete. The source’s lower thresholds are therefore consistent with a partial-visibility regime [PAGE 3].

4. Weighted accuracy operator. The source defines a weighted accuracy where impact helmets receive a weight of 1000 [PAGE 7], [PAGE 8]. Using non-source symbols for clarity, define
\[
W = \frac{C_{\text{nonimp}} + 1000\,C_{\text{imp}}}{N_{\text{nonimp}} + 1000\,N_{\text{imp}}},
\]
where \(C_{\text{nonimp}}\) and \(C_{\text{imp}}\) are correctly assigned non-impact and impact helmet counts, and \(N_{\text{nonimp}}\) and \(N_{\text{imp}}\) are corresponding reference counts. \(W\) is dimensionless. Assumption: impact boxes are valid events and their labels are reliable. Breakdown regimes: (a) if \(N_{\text{imp}}=0\) and \(N_{\text{nonimp}}=0\), \(W\) is undefined; (b) if impact labels are rare, one missed impact changes \(W\) sharply; (c) if impact annotation is noisy, the metric can reward assigning the wrong player to a high-value event. This is why the source emphasizes cross-validation: “testing data is a subset of training data, the chance for us to overfit the models is very high” [PAGE 7].

5. Kalman filter operator. DeepSort uses a Kalman filter to estimate helmet positions during occlusion [PAGE 4]. A linear Gaussian form is
\[
x_t = F x_{t-1} + w_t,
\]
\[
z_t = H x_t + v_t,
\]
where \(x_t\) is the latent track state, \(z_t\) is the observed helmet box or feature measurement, \(F\) is the state transition matrix, \(H\) is the observation matrix, and \(w_t,v_t\) are process and measurement noise. Units depend on state definition: pixels, pixels per frame, or normalized field coordinates. Assumptions: local motion is approximately linear over short intervals, and noise is approximately Gaussian. Breakdown regimes: (a) long occlusion beyond the motion model horizon; (b) abrupt direction changes such as collisions; (c) identity switches when multiple helmets cross; (d) missed detections that cause the filter to drift. The source says they delete helmets that cannot be tracked for longer than a certain frame span [PAGE 4], which is a practical guard against unbounded drift.

6. Hungarian assignment operator. The assignment problem is
\[
\min \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} x_{ij}
\]
subject to
\[
\sum_j x_{ij} \le 1,\qquad \sum_i x_{ij} \le 1,\qquad x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Here \(c_{ij}\) is the dimensionless cost of assigning detection \(i\) to track or player \(j\), and \(x_{ij}\) is a binary assignment variable. The source uses the Hungarian algorithm after computing a cosine distance matrix between tracking sensor information and bounding-box information [PAGE 4]. Assumption: costs are additive and one-to-one matching is appropriate. Breakdown regimes: (a) unequal numbers of detections and tracks require dummy nodes; (b) if many false positives exist, the solver may force bad assignments unless dummy or ignore rows are added. The source explicitly adds “rows full of zero in distance matrix” to ignore false-positive bboxes [PAGE 5].

7. Cosine distance operator. The source uses cosine distance for assignment costs [PAGE 4]. For feature vectors \(u,v\),
\[
d_{\cos}(u,v) = 1-\frac{u^{\top}v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}.
\]
This is dimensionless. Assumption: direction of feature vectors is more informative than magnitude. Breakdown regimes: (a) if \(\|u\|=0\) or \(\|v\|=0\), the expression is undefined; (b) if features are dominated by background rather than player shape, cosine distance can conflate different players; (c) if color or texture changes due to lighting, the feature geometry changes.

8. ICP operator. Iterative Closest Point is used to align helmet point clouds or player tracking points to a reference surface [PAGE 2], [PAGE 4]. A formal objective is
\[
\min_{R\in SO(3),\,t\in\mathbb{R}^3} \sum_j \rho\left(\left\|R p_j + t - q_{c(j)}\right\|^2\right),
\]
where \(p_j\) are source points, \(q_{c(j)}\) are corresponding target points, \(R\) is a rotation matrix, \(t\) is a translation vector, and \(\rho\) is an optional robust loss. Units are meters if field coordinates are metric, pixels if image coordinates are used, or normalized units if the source normalized the coordinates. The source says it normalized players into X-Y coordinates before applying Hungarian matching and ICP [PAGE 4], and it used Euclidean distances for initial rotation estimation [PAGE 2]. Assumptions: enough overlapping points exist, and the initial transform is close enough to the true transform. Breakdown regimes: (a) local minima; (b) insufficient correspondences; (c) degenerate geometry such as collinear points; (d) large initial misalignment; (e) scale ambiguity if units are not fixed.

9. K-means team-color operator. The source divides helmet boxes into two clusters using color features [PAGE 4]. A two-cluster objective is
\[
\min_{\mu_1,\mu_2} \sum_{m=1}^{2}\sum_{x\in C_m} \|x-\mu_m\|^2,
\]
where \(x\) is a color or appearance feature and \(\mu_m\) is a cluster centroid. The objective is dimensionless if \(x\) is normalized. Assumption: two team identities correspond to two separable color modes. Breakdown regimes: (a) empty clusters; (b) lighting-induced color shift; (c) helmets with similar palettes; (d) motion blur causing color smearing; (e) k-means local minima depending on initialization.

10. Homography operator. The source applies homography to propagate tracking information across frames [PAGE 4], [PAGE 5]. In homogeneous image coordinates,
\[
\tilde{p}' \simeq H\tilde{p},
\]
where \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\) is a projective transform and \(\tilde{p}\) is a homogeneous point. The source searches over homography matrices from the previous 60 frames and selects one with best cost [PAGE 5]. Units are homogeneous pixel coordinates. Assumption: the scene motion can be approximated by a dominant planar transform, such as the field plane. Breakdown regimes: (a) camera motion too large for planar approximation; (b) insufficient point correspondences; (c) collinear points; (d) non-planar scene elements such as players standing at different depths; (e) frame misalignment between sideline and endzone views, which the source quantifies as mostly one frame but sometimes seven frames [PAGE 2].

11. Temporal misalignment operator. Let \(t_{\text{side}}\) and \(t_{\text{end}}\) be the nominal frame indices for the two views. Define
\[
\delta_t = |t_{\text{side}}-t_{\text{end}}|
\]
in frames. The source reports that “25 plays out of 60 doesn’t match and the difference is mostly 1 frame but there are 7 frame difference also” [PAGE 2]. Assumption: downstream models need synchronized frames. Breakdown regime: if \(\delta_t>0\) and is not corrected, any temporal homography, collision-angle calculation, or impact assignment can be shifted. A seven-frame offset is especially hazardous after the snap because movement is high [PAGE 2].

12. Final label operator. The source creates a cost matrix from label frequency in the same DeepSort tracks [PAGE 6]. A formal version is
\[
C_{i\ell} = 1-p_{i\ell},
\]
where \(p_{i\ell}\in[0,1]\) is the empirical probability that helmet track \(i\) has been associated with player label \(\ell\) in prior frames. The final assignment solves a linear assignment problem, possibly after cropping out-of-bounds or non-player points [PAGE 6]. Assumption: historical co-occurrence is informative. Breakdown regimes: (a) early misassignment becomes self-reinforcing; (b) sparse tracks yield noisy probabilities; (c) ties produce unstable labels; (d) cropping can remove the correct player if the spatial rectangle is too aggressive.

13. Ignore-cost acceptance rule. The source says: “If increasing ignore number from N to N+1 helped decreasing cost function by 10, the increase was accepted” [PAGE 5]. Formally, let \(J(N)\) be the assignment cost with \(N\) ignored false-positive detections. The rule accepts \(N+1\) if
\[
J(N)-J(N+1) \ge 10.
\]
The threshold 10 is a dimensionless cost margin. Breakdown regime: if cost scales are not calibrated, this fixed threshold can either over-ignore real helmets or under-ignore false positives.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s genealogy is not merely technical; it is shaped by the competition structure and by the retrospective pressure of final evaluation. The source begins with the NFL-AWS collaboration and says the NFL wants to assign players to helmets to identify exposures [PAGE 1]. That institutional goal creates the first retro-causal constraint: the final label must be player-centric, not helmet-centric. A helmet detection that is accurate but unassigned to a player is insufficient for the stated safety objective. The paper also notes that the competition is connected to a previous year’s competition: “This competition will be a subset of the previous year’s competition from kaggle.com” [PAGE 1]. That creates a lineage trap: prior solutions, prior datasets, and prior visual cues may not transfer cleanly to the new data.

The technical lineage is visible in the chosen operators. YOLOv5 is used for detection [PAGE 3]. DeepSort is used for tracking [PAGE 3]. The paper explicitly decomposes DeepSort into Kalman filtering and Hungarian assignment [PAGE 4]. ICP is drawn from classical shape registration, and the source cites Besl and McKay [PAGE 11]. The Hungarian algorithm is treated as the general assignment solver [PAGE 10]. Detectron2 is mentioned as an alternative lineage that was explored but not fully implemented because of time and resource constraints [PAGE 12]. This genealogy matters because each operator carries historical assumptions. YOLOv5 assumes appearance-based detection from images. DeepSort assumes short-term motion continuity. ICP assumes geometric correspondence. Hungarian assumes explicit one-to-one costs. Homography assumes planar or dominant-plane motion. The source’s pipeline is therefore a layered inheritance of older assumptions, not a single unified model.

The multi-week traps are induced by the competition clock. The source says that after submission the model will be run on “15 unseen plays that is a part of a holdout training set” [PAGE 1], and later repeats that the submission will be executed on “15 unseen gameplay videos that are not available to us” [PAGE 7]. This creates a generalization trap: any tuning that improves public leaderboard performance may not survive the hidden evaluation. The source acknowledges the danger: “testing data is a subset of training data, the chance for us to overfit the models is very high” [PAGE 7]. That is a leakage trap. If the team tunes on data that is statistically too close to the hidden distribution, the private score can collapse.

A second trap is metric-induced. The source states that helmets involved in an impact are “1000x more weighted” [PAGE 7]. This causes a retro-causal inversion: ordinary helmet detection accuracy is less valuable than impact detection accuracy. A model that is excellent at detecting stationary, fully visible helmets but misses a partially occluded impact helmet may perform poorly. The source’s decision to lower confidence and IoU thresholds is therefore not a local engineering choice; it is induced by the final metric [PAGE 3]. The same pressure explains the focus on collision detection and angle tracking: “Using the speed, direction, and distance of two players can help us predict the collision” [PAGE 7].

A third trap is data augmentation asymmetry. The source flips endzone coordinates for augmentation but does not flip sideline coordinates because “a lot of frames in sideline have outsiders and audience” [PAGE 6]. This is a domain-specific countermeasure, but it also reveals a hidden assumption: the sideline view contains distractors that are not present in the endzone view. If that assumption is false for some plays, the model may underperform on sideline videos. The source also extracts frames after the snap because movement is highest after the snap [PAGE 2]. This is a temporal cropping prior. If an impact or pre-snap motion is relevant, cropping may remove evidence. The source’s own exploratory data analysis shows why: “Before snap players change their position a bit but after snap they start to run” [PAGE 2].

The final retro-causal trap is leaderboard psychology. The source reports scores: “Our maximum Leaderboard score achieved so far from the competition is 0.713(Private) and 0.797(Public)” [PAGE 8]. The gap between public and private scores is a warning that the team’s public tuning may have overfit visible test slices. The paper also says the team reached “a position of 34 (private leaderboard)” [PAGE 10]. That ranking pressure likely encouraged incremental improvements to an existing pipeline rather than a full redesign. The choice to mention Detectron2 as future work rather than deployed method supports this interpretation: the team recognized a stronger detection lineage but did not have the multi-week budget to realize it [PAGE 12].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source contains a recursive evaluation game between the modeling team, the competition evaluator, and the invisible holdout set. The team knows that the evaluator rewards impact helmets heavily, so it lowers detection thresholds and prioritizes recall [PAGE 3], [PAGE 7]. The evaluator, implicitly, rewards labels that are both accurate and player-specific, because the competition is about assigning impacts to players rather than merely detecting helmets [PAGE 1]. The holdout set, represented by the 15 unseen plays, punishes overfit tuning [PAGE 1], [PAGE 7]. Each actor’s expectation shapes the next actor’s behavior.

The first recursive layer is crisis habit regression. Under time pressure, teams revert to familiar operators. The source shows this in its use of YOLOv5 and DeepSort, which are well-known and easier to deploy than a custom architecture. It also shows it in the admission that Detectron2 was not implemented because of “constraints in time and resources” [PAGE 12]. Crisis habit regression is visible in the choice to keep the pipeline modular: detection, tracking, assignment, team clustering, homography, final label. This modularity is robust, but it also means errors can be localized rather than jointly optimized. The team also says it modified DeepSort parameters: “The DeepSort model’s default parameters that will impact the performance ... are selected and altered” [PAGE 7]. That is a regression toward familiar defaults, but with competition-specific tuning.

The second recursive layer is the counter-trap. The team anticipates overfitting and uses cross-validation: “To prevent this cross validation is used to test the models and their collaborative output” [PAGE 7]. It anticipates occlusion and uses Kalman filtering [PAGE 4], [PAGE 7]. It anticipates frame misalignment and uses homography over many preceding frames [PAGE 5]. It anticipates false positives by adding zero rows to the distance matrix and adaptively ignoring detections [PAGE 5]. It anticipates distractors in the sideline by avoiding endzone-style flipping for sideline frames [PAGE 6]. These are not isolated hacks; they are recursive defenses against known failure modes.

The third recursive layer is the clock. The competition timeline creates pressure to submit before full understanding is achieved. The source says the submission will be executed on unseen videos [PAGE 7], and the final score is revealed later. This creates a two-phase belief update: public leaderboard feedback is available during the competition, but private feedback arrives after the clock stops. The team’s reported public score of 0.797 and private score of 0.713 [PAGE 8] suggests that public feedback may have encouraged some over-specialization. The model’s final label operator, which uses historical co-occurrence, is especially vulnerable to this because it can amplify early leaderboard-favored patterns [PAGE 6].

The recursive theory of mind also applies to the visual domain. The model must infer player identity from helmets, but helmets are not player identities. The source must therefore infer identity from context: tracking coordinates, orientation, speed, acceleration, color clusters, and historical track labels [PAGE 2], [PAGE 4], [PAGE 6]. This is a theory-of-mind problem because the system must model unobserved player states from partial observations. If two players are near each other, the system must infer which helmet belongs to which player using motion continuity and team color. If an impact occurs, the system must infer which player was involved even if the helmet is partially visible. The source’s collision-detection discussion makes this explicit: “Using the speed, direction, and distance of two players can help us predict the collision” [PAGE 7]. That is an inference over latent player trajectories, not merely object detection.

The counter-trap against recursive overconfidence is the paper’s own evaluation section. The source reports confusion-matrix analysis and says “89% of the helmet (clearly visible) class are correctly classified out of actual ground truth” [PAGE 9]. It also reports that “setting the confidence between 0.2 to 0.6 would yield a overall better score for the given data” [PAGE 9]. These statements show the team checking its own beliefs against empirical metrics. Still, the strongest counter-trap is the hidden holdout. The model cannot see the 15 unseen plays, so any recursive belief that public performance equals generalization is unsafe. The correct stance is to treat the pipeline as a set of conditional operators whose failure modes are known: detection miss, track drift, assignment ambiguity, color cluster error, homography degeneracy, and historical label lock-in.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which a higher-level or independent layer rejects a proposed helmet-to-player assignment even if an earlier layer produced a plausible detection. The source’s architecture naturally creates four layers: photometric detection, geometric association, temporal/biomechanical plausibility, and environmental visibility. I adjudicate them as follows.

Layer 1: Photometric detection veto. If a helmet impact is not detected with sufficient visual evidence, the assignment should be vetoed or marked low confidence. The source acknowledges that impact helmets are often partially visible and that lower confidence and IoU thresholds are required [PAGE 3]. It also reports that confidence values between 0.2 and 0.6 gave better overall scores for their data [PAGE 9]. Formal veto condition: let \(c_i\) be detection confidence and \(v_i\) be an estimated visible-fraction proxy. If \(c_i\) is below the calibrated recall threshold or \(v_i\) is near zero, the detection should not be treated as a definitive impact. Breakdown: if the threshold is too high, true impacts are vetoed; if too low, false positives enter the assignment matrix. The source’s lower thresholds are a deliberate tradeoff toward impact recall because impact helmets are heavily weighted [PAGE 7].

Layer 2: Geometric assignment veto. If the ICP-Hungarian cost is too high, or if the homography residual is inconsistent with player motion, the assignment should be vetoed. The source uses ICP to minimize distance between helmet points and tracking data [PAGE 4], and it uses homography to make adjacent-frame player positions consistent [PAGE 4], [PAGE 5]. Formal veto condition: let \(J^*\) be the minimum assignment cost after dummy rows and ignored false positives. If \(J^*\) exceeds a calibrated rejection threshold, or if the assigned player track implies an impossible jump, reject the label. The source’s ignore rule, “If increasing ignore number from N to N+1 helped decreasing cost function by 10, the increase was accepted” [PAGE 5], shows that cost reduction is used to decide whether to ignore detections. That is a geometric veto mechanism, but it depends on cost calibration. If costs are not comparable across frames, the veto becomes unstable.

Layer 3: Biomechanical plausibility veto. The source uses player speed, direction, and distance to infer collisions [PAGE 7]. A biomechanical veto can be defined without inventing constants. Let \(p_i(t)\) and \(p_j(t)\) be player positions, \(v_i(t)\) and \(v_j(t)\) their velocities, and \(d_{ij}(t)=\|p_i(t)-p_j(t)\|\). If an impact is assigned to player \(i\), then over the impact interval \(\Delta t\), the position change must satisfy
\[
\|p_i(t+\Delta t)-p_i(t)\| \le \bar v_i \Delta t + \epsilon,
\]
where \(\bar v_i\) is a plausible maximum speed and \(\epsilon\) is measurement slack. If the assigned track violates this, the assignment is biomechanically implausible. A collision between two players also requires a distance threshold and an angular condition. The source says angle and direction are used as metrics to detect collision [PAGE 7]. A formal collision cone can be written as
\[
\cos\theta_{ij} = \frac{(p_j-p_i)^{\top}(v_j-v_i)}{\|p_j-p_i\|\,\|v_j-v_i\|},
\]
where \(\theta_{ij}\) is the relative approach angle. If \(\theta_{ij}\) lies outside a plausible collision cone, an impact assignment should be vetoed. Breakdown: if tracking coordinates are noisy, the velocity estimate can be wrong; if the impact is lateral or involves a player not moving toward the other player, a simple cone may falsely veto. Therefore the biomechanical veto should be probabilistic, not absolute.

Layer 4: Environmental and weather veto. The source does not provide weather metadata. It provides video frames and tracking CSV data [PAGE 2]. Therefore, within the source alone, there is no direct weather veto operator. However, weather can enter indirectly through image quality. If rain, glare, low light, or mud reduce helmet visibility, the detector’s confidence distribution should collapse. The source’s class analysis says detection depends on classes and image quality [PAGE 9]. A formal environmental veto would require external visibility metadata \(W_t\) and a detection-confidence model. If \(W_t\) indicates severe occlusion and \(c_i\) is low, the assignment can be vetoed. But because the source does not include \(W_t\), the only valid source-grounded statement is: weather cannot be directly adjudicated from the provided text and data description; it can only be inferred from confidence, class confusion, and visibility analysis [PAGE 9]. This is a proof of absence: the source contains no weather variable, so no weather-specific veto can be formally instantiated from the source alone.

The final adjudication rule is conjunctive. A proposed impact assignment is accepted only if:
1. the detection has enough visual evidence;
2. the geometric assignment cost is below threshold;
3. the temporal homography is consistent;
4. the biomechanical motion is plausible;
5. no environmental proxy indicates severe visibility failure.

If any layer vetoes, the assignment should be downgraded. The source’s heavy weighting of impact helmets [PAGE 7] means that false impact assignments are expensive, but missed impact assignments are also expensive. The cross-layer veto should therefore be tuned to preserve recall while rejecting physically impossible labels. The most dangerous failure is a high-confidence but biomechanically impossible assignment, because it can dominate the weighted metric.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfact-based veto scenarios. Each scenario identifies a failure mode, states the veto condition, and gives a measurable prediction. These are not speculative odds; they are testable conditions grounded in the source.

Scenario 1: Temporal misalignment veto. The source reports that sideline and endzone videos do not always share the same frame: “25 plays out of 60 doesn’t match and the difference is mostly 1 frame but there are 7 frame difference also” [PAGE 2]. Counterfactual: suppose a play has a seven-frame offset and the model uses unsynchronized frames for homography or collision-angle estimation. Veto condition: if \(\delta_t\) is not corrected and the homography residual or player-assignment cost increases beyond threshold, the temporal association should be vetoed. Prediction: if frames are artificially shifted by one to seven frames, the Hungarian assignment cost will rise, the ICP distance residual will increase, and impact labels will become less stable. Verification: measure assignment cost and label consistency before and after shifting endzone frames relative to sideline frames. The source’s reliance on adjacent-frame similarity makes this veto plausible: “player positions of adjacent frames are similar, the homography matrix should be also similar” [PAGE 4]. If the adjacency assumption is broken by frame offset, the homography prior becomes invalid.

Scenario 2: Color-cluster failure veto. The source separates helmets into two teams using k-means on helmet color [PAGE 4]. Counterfactual: suppose two teams wear visually similar helmet colors, or stadium lighting shifts the color distribution. Veto condition: if the k-means silhouette or color separation margin falls below a threshold, team-cluster assignments should not be used as strong evidence. Formal margin:
\[
M = \|\mu_1-\mu_2\| - (\sigma_1+\sigma_2),
\]
where \(\mu_m\) and \(\sigma_m\) are cluster centers and spreads. If \(M\le 0\), the clusters overlap and the team assignment is ambiguous. Prediction: when \(M\) is small, final labels will show higher disagreement between color-cluster labels and historical DeepSort labels. The source’s final label operator uses historical co-occurrence [PAGE 6], so if color clustering fails, the model should rely more on track history and less on color. Verification: compare final label accuracy under high-margin and low-margin color clusters. The source’s statement that color clustering helps determine team membership [PAGE 4] implies that this veto is testable.

Scenario 3: Impact visibility and confidence veto. The source says impact helmets are often partially visible and that lower confidence and IoU thresholds are needed [PAGE 3]. It also reports that confidence between 0.2 and 0.6 gave better scores for their data [PAGE 9]. Counterfactual: suppose the model uses a high confidence threshold, such as 0.8, to avoid false positives. Veto condition: if impact helmets have low visible fraction and low confidence, a high threshold will veto them. Prediction: raising the confidence threshold will increase precision on ordinary helmets but reduce impact recall. Because impact helmets are weighted by 1000 in the evaluation metric [PAGE 7], the weighted accuracy \(W\) will fall even if non-impact precision improves. Verification: run a confidence sweep and plot \(W\) against threshold. The source’s own observation that lower thresholds were necessary [PAGE 3] predicts that the curve will favor a lower operating point. The formal reason is that the metric’s denominator and numerator are dominated by impact counts when impact events exist:
\[
W = \frac{C_{\text{nonimp}} + 1000\,C_{\text{imp}}}{N_{\text{nonimp}} + 1000\,N_{\text{imp}}}.
\]
If \(C_{\text{imp}}\) decreases because impact detections are thresholded out, \(W\) drops sharply. This is a verifiable veto scenario because it can be tested directly on the training and validation subsets.

These three counterfact-based vetoes cover the main failure surfaces of the source: temporal alignment, team-color separation, and impact visibility. A robust deployment should implement all three as runtime checks rather than relying only on the final leaderboard score. The source’s architecture is capable of supporting these checks because it already contains confidence scores, tracking histories, color clusters, homography costs, and weighted evaluation logic [PAGE 3], [PAGE 4], [PAGE 5], [PAGE 7], [PAGE 8]. The missing piece is explicit threshold calibration and abstain-or-reject behavior when the veto conditions are triggered.
