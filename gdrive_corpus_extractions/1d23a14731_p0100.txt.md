<THINK_TRACE>
The source text describes two distinct phenomena: post-rotational vertigo and optical (induced) motion illusions at railway stations. The first involves a sensory conflict between vestibular input and visual processing following sudden deceleration of body rotation. James explains that the eyes normally maintain fixation through a compensatory inertia; when the head turns left, the eyes roll rightwards to stabilize the field of view. After whirling stops, this reflex continues unmodulated, causing the eyes to drift rightward while the brain interprets this retinal slip as external objects moving leftward. Crucially, James notes that voluntary corrective saccades “simply confirm and intensify” the illusion—the brain misattributes the cause of these movements.

This mechanism can be formalized as an error in internal model updating within a predictive coding framework. The brain maintains a forward model that predicts expected retinal motion based on motor commands (efference copy). When self-generated movement ceases unexpectedly (as after stopping whirling), the persistent vestibular-driven eye drift generates retinal motion signals that are not cancelled by the efference copy, leading to a prediction error interpreted as external motion. The second phenomenon—railway station illusion—operates via a different pathway: prolonged exposure to a moving visual field (train A starting) creates adaptation in motion-sensitive neurons tuned to the direction of train A’s motion. When the stimulus (the adjacent train) suddenly disappears, the adapted neurons rebound with activity opposite to their tuning preference, producing the sensation that one’s own train is moving backward. This is a negative afterimage of motion perception, as hinted in the footnote referencing Chapter XX.

The gray zone lies in distinguishing true neural adaptation from active feedback loop errors. In the whirling case, the persistence of eye movements suggests a mechanical or biomechanical inertia of the oculomotor system itself—a physical veto where the muscles do not instantly stop. However, James attributes the false perception primarily to the unconscious referral of retinal movement-feelings to external objects, implying a cognitive interpretation error rather than a purely sensory one. The railway example, by contrast, is almost entirely neural: it relies on the differential fatigue of direction-selective cells.

A multi-hop derivation reveals a deeper principle: the brain does not passively record sensations but actively constructs percepts by integrating conflicting inputs under uncertainty. When high-level expectations (e.g., “I am stationary in my seat”) conflict with low-level sensory data (retinal flow), the brain may privilege the sensory data if the expectation is weakly held (as in sudden deceleration) or may suppress it if the expectation is strong (as when closing eyes). This Bayesian weighting is the crux of the illusion—it is not the absence of a signal but its misattribution.

Furthermore, the mention of “optical vertigo” resulting from brain trouble or intoxication points to a vulnerability in the integration pathways. Damage to the vestibular nuclei or cerebellar connections could disrupt the cancellation of self-generated motion signals, causing chronic illusions. Thus, these everyday experiences are windows into the brain’s real-time calibration of self-motion versus world-motion—a process that, when faulty, leads to dizziness or nausea.

where \( \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t \) is sensory noise. The brain's posterior belief about external motion is:
\[
P(\mathbf{z}_t | \mathbf{y}_{1:t}, \mathbf{u}_{1:t}) \propto P(\mathbf{y}_t | \mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{u}_t) P(\mathbf{z}_t | \mathbf{u}_t)
\]
In normal conditions, the prior \( P(\mathbf{z}_t | \mathbf{u}_t) \) encodes the expectation that self-movement causes predictable retinal flow, allowing cancellation. During post-rotational nystagmus, however, the vestibular system sends a signal indicating continued slow-phase eye drift (\( \mathbf{u}_t^{\text{eye}} \neq 0 \)) despite cessation of whole-body rotation. If the efference copy of this drift is not properly subtracted (i.e., \( \mathbf{u}_t^{\text{eye}} \) is not included in the generative model for \( \mathbf{y}_t \)), then the residual \( \mathbf{y}_t \) is attributed to \( \mathbf{z}_t \neq 0 \), i.e., object motion.

For the railway illusion, we invoke a neuronal population code with adaptation. Let \( r_i(t) \) be the firing rate of neuron \( i \) selective for direction \( \theta_i \). Under sustained stimulation in direction \( \theta_0 \), the response adapts:
\[
r_i(t) = \frac{R_{\text{max}} \cdot g(|\theta_i - \theta_0|)}{1 + A \cdot t} \quad \text{(simplified decay)}
\]
Upon offset, there is a transient "rebound" due to disinhibition or recovery dynamics, leading to a net population vector pointing opposite to \( \theta_0 \). This corresponds to a negative afterimage of motion.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
These illusions have been studied since antiquity; Aristotle described the apparent counter-rotation after spinning. Helmholtz later analyzed them in terms of unconscious inference. James situated his account within the emerging field of physiological psychology, reacting against structuralist introspection by emphasizing dynamic sensorimotor loops. His reference to "retinal movement-feelings" being referred to objects echoes earlier theories of projection in vision.

Scientific lineage connects to modern studies of the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) and its role in stabilizing gaze. Békésy's work in the mid-20th century showed that the VOR has a latency of ~10 ms and operates via a three-neuron arc. Post-rotational nystagmus arises because the fluid (endolymph) in the semicircular canals continues to move after head rotation stops, deflecting cupulae and generating neural activity corresponding to continued rotation. This matches James’s description of eyes rolling rightwards after turning left—that is, the fast phase of nystagmus opposes the direction of previous rotation.

The railway illusion was famously demonstrated by Hering and others as an example of induced motion. It has been linked to the motion aftereffect (MAE), first described by Watson and Crick in 1965? Actually, it goes back to von Helmholtz and even earlier to Plateau’s stroboscopic effects. The key advance came with Hubel and Wiesel’s discovery of direction-selective simple cells in V1, providing a cellular basis for adaptation-based illusions.

Multi-week trap: One might assume that repeated exposure to such illusions would lead to habituation and reduced effect size. However, some forms of motion sickness show sensitization over days, suggesting complex long-term plasticity in brainstem circuits. Also, individuals vary greatly in susceptibility, possibly due to genetic differences in vestibular hair cell density or cerebellar granule cell connectivity.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under stress or fatigue, higher-order cortical control over attention and expectation weakens. For instance, after sleep deprivation, the brain may rely more heavily on automatic bottom-up signals (like retinal slip) and less on top-down priors (like knowing you're sitting still). This could amplify the intensity of motion illusions. In extreme cases, this regression might contribute to symptoms of anxiety disorders where bodily sensations are catastrophically misinterpreted as danger.

Counter-trap: An individual trained in mindfulness or meditation might consciously recognize the illusion ("my eyes are moving, not the room") and thereby reduce the subjective experience of vertigo. This meta-cognitive intervention works by strengthening the top-down prior that contradicts the erroneous percept, effectively increasing the precision weighting on the prior relative to the likelihood in the Bayesian update.

Clock: The temporal dynamics matter. The fading out of the illusion after seconds reflects short-term adaptation of the underlying neural populations. But if the illusion persists beyond minutes, it may indicate pathological dysfunction (e.g., benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, BPPV) requiring medical evaluation. The clock here serves as a diagnostic marker distinguishing normal physiology from disease.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical veto: James mentions that fixing eyes on a point often stops the illusion. This is a behavioral compensation strategy that overrides the automatic reflexes. By voluntarily holding gaze fixed, one eliminates the involuntary drift component of nystagmus, reducing the source of misleading retinal motion. This demonstrates that conscious action can veto subconscious sensorimotor processes.

Weather veto proof: Consider performing the whirling experiment outdoors on a sunny day versus indoors. Outdoors, rich landmark information provides stronger cues for absolute orientation, potentially suppressing the illusion because the visual scene is less ambiguous. Indoors, with fewer distinctive features, the same nystagmus might produce a more vivid sense of rotation. Similarly, wind gusts outside a train window could add additional optic flow vectors, either enhancing or canceling the induced motion depending on direction—thus environmental context modulates illusion strength.
