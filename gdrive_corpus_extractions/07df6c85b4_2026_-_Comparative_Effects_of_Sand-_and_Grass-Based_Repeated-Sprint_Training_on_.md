<THINK_TRACE>
The study is a three-arm randomized controlled trial in 42 male collegiate soccer players, with 14 participants per arm, comparing six weeks of repeated-sprint training (RST) on sand versus grass, against time-matched technical training [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. The first multi-hop inference is: if both active arms improve relative to control, the RST stimulus is effective; if SAND and GRASS differ despite identical sprint structure and volume, the surface is a plausible moderator. The protocol was three sets of six 30-m maximal sprints, 30-s between-sprint recovery, 4-min between-set rest, two sessions per week [PAGE 2]. That yields \(2 \times 3 \times 6 \times 30 = 1080\) m/week, or 6480 m across six weeks. This is a low-to-moderate sprint dose, so the six-week window likely captures early neural adaptations more than deep structural remodeling.

The second hop is internal-load adjudication. The paper reports that RPE and blood lactate increased over sessions, but the group-by-time interactions were not significant for RPE \((p=0.43)\) or lactate \((p=0.22)\), and HRmax did not differ \((p=0.40)\) [PAGE 4]. That weakens a simple “sand is globally harder” explanation. The authors themselves interpret the null internal-load findings as “comparable systemic demand,” while attributing performance differences to “surface driven neuromuscular and mechanical constraints rather than uniformly greater internal load on SAND” [PAGE 7]. That is a reasonable but incomplete inference: comparable RPE, lactate, and HRmax do not prove comparable local muscle loading, ground contact mechanics, or eccentric stress.

The third hop is the outcome pattern. SAND beat GRASS for squat jump, minimum power, mean power, and fatigue index, while CMJ, peak power, and best sprint time did not differ [PAGE 1][PAGE 5]. This pattern is mechanistically coherent with a compliance-induced concentric demand: an unstable, yielding surface may reduce elastic return and force greater concentric/stabilizing work, which could preferentially benefit a concentric-biased test such as the squat jump. But CMJ, which depends more on stretch-shortening-cycle function, did not separate surfaces, which limits a blanket “sand improves explosive performance” claim.

The gray-zone audit is substantial. First, all post-intervention tests were conducted on grass [PAGE 4]. That favors transfer to match-relevant conditions, but it also creates a test-specificity asymmetry: GRASS trained and tested on grass, while SAND trained on sand and tested on grass. The authors acknowledge that “the SAND group did not perform post-tests on the trained surface” [PAGE 4]. This asymmetry is conservative for SAND’s observed advantages, but it still prevents clean separation of training-surface and testing-surface effects.

Second, fatigue index is ratio-derived: \(FI=(P_{\max}-P_{\min})/\sum t_i\) [PAGE 4]. If minimum power rises while peak power is unchanged, FI necessarily falls. The authors explicitly warn that “the observed reduction in fatigue index may partly reflect the concurrent increase in minimum power rather than an independent physiological adaptation in fatigue resistance” [PAGE 8]. Thus, the fatigue-resistance claim is partially coupled to the minimum-power claim, not an independent construct.

Third, there are reporting inconsistencies. The abstract reports SAND greater squat jump at \(p<0.01\), mean power at \(p=0.03\), minimum power at \(p<0.01\), and fatigue index at \(p<0.01\) [PAGE 1]. Page 5 first reports SAND greater squat jump \((p<0.01)\), minimum power \((p<0.01)\), and mean power \((p=0.03)\) [PAGE 5], but later on the same page states SAND had greater changes in squat jump \((p=0.03)\), minimum power \((p=0.01)\), and fatigue index \((p=0.03)\) [PAGE 5]. These are not identical p-value mappings. That does not erase the direction of effects, but it is a transparency and reproducibility flag.

Fourth, the statistical model is baseline-adjusted ANCOVA with post-intervention value as dependent variable, group as fixed factor, and baseline as covariate [PAGE 4]. That is appropriate only if regression slopes are homogeneous. VO2max violated that assumption \((p<0.05)\), so the authors used mixed-design ANOVA and simple effects for VO2max [PAGE 5]. Running economy had no significant group-by-time interaction \((F=2.0, p=0.15)\) [PAGE 6], so the within-group RE improvements cannot be attributed to a surface-specific effect. The paper correctly states this, but the practical risk is over-reading “RE improved” as a training effect rather than a time effect.

Fifth, small samples and multiple outcomes inflate the gray zone. The authors concede that “the sample size may have been insufficient to reliably detect small-to-moderate between-surface differences” [PAGE 8–9]. Non-significant surface differences are therefore not proof of equivalence. Effect-size confidence intervals are wide, especially for peak power, and the paper warns that peak-power effects “should be interpreted cautiously” [PAGE 8]. The correct inferential posture is: strong evidence for RST versus control; moderate evidence for SAND versus GRASS on a narrow set of outcomes; weak-to-moderate evidence for mechanism; no evidence for surface-specific aerobic superiority.

**Operator 1: RAST sprint power.** The source defines:

\[
P_i=\frac{m d^2}{t_i^3}
\]

with \(m\) in kg, \(d=35\) m, \(t_i\) in s, and \(P_i\) in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-3}}=\mathrm{W}\). The paper states verbatim: “Power = body mass × distance2 / time3” [PAGE 4]. This is a field-test index, not a direct force-plate mechanical power; it assumes a simplified acceleration/time relation and is sensitive to timing-gate precision. Breakdown regime: as \(t_i\to 0\), \(P_i\to\infty\); near-zero sprint times are physically impossible but timing error, distance error, or start-gate misalignment disproportionately inflates power. The expression also omits any explicit acceleration constant, so between-study comparisons require identical distance and timing methods.

**Operator 2: RAST aggregate powers.**

\[
P_{\max}=\max_{i=1,\dots,6}P_i,\qquad
P_{\min}=\min_{i=1,\dots,6}P_i,\qquad
\bar P=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6}P_i
\]

Units are W. The source states: “Peak power = the highest value among the 6 powers,” “Minimum power = the lowest value among the 6 powers,” and “Mean power = sum of the 6 power / 6” [PAGE 4].

**Operator 3: Fatigue index.**

\[
FI=\frac{P_{\max}-P_{\min}}{\sum_{i=1}^{6}t_i}
\]

Units are \(\mathrm{W\,s^{-1}}=\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-4}}\). The exact source wording is: “Fatigue index = (Peak power - Minimum power) / total time of the 6 sprints” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown and singularity regimes: \(FI\) is undefined if the aggregate sprint time is zero; in practice it is never zero, but a small denominator amplifies noise. More importantly, \(FI\) is not normalized to peak power, so a rise in \(P_{\min}\) mechanically lowers \(FI\) even if peak power and pacing are unchanged. This is why the authors caution that the fatigue-index reduction “may partly reflect the concurrent increase in minimum power rather than an independent physiological adaptation in fatigue resistance” [PAGE 8]. A decrement score normalized to best sprint time would be less coupled to minimum power.

**Operator 4: Surface stiffness ratio.** The Clegg Impact Hammer was used with a 2.25-kg mass dropped from 0.457 m; ten random samples plus ten high-use samples were taken per area [PAGE 3]. The reported peak impact deceleration forces were:

\[
F_{\mathrm{SAND}}=393.6\pm70.3\ \mathrm{N},\qquad
F_{\mathrm{GRASS}}=1173.0\pm104.7\ \mathrm{N}
\]

with \(p<0.01\), “confirming that GRASS was the firmer surface” [PAGE 4]. The ratio is:

\[
R_s=\frac{F_{\mathrm{GRASS}}}{F_{\mathrm{SAND}}}
=\frac{1173.0}{393.6}\approx2.98
\]

dimensionless. The paper states that “the grass surface was approximately 2.98 times stiffer than the sand surface” [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regime: \(R_s\to\infty\) as \(F_{\mathrm{SAND}}\to0\); \(R_s\to1\) as surfaces become mechanically similar. The Clegg index measures vertical impact deceleration only; the authors explicitly note that it “does not directly quantify sand-specific mechanical properties such as depth-dependent deformation or shear resistance” [PAGE 3]. Therefore \(R_s\) is a comparative stiffness indicator, not a full substrate constitutive law.

**Operator 5: Training volume.**

\[
V_{\mathrm{wk}}=
2\ \frac{\text{sessions}}{\text{wk}}
\times 3\ \frac{\text{sets}}{\text{session}}
\times 6\ \frac{\text{sprints}}{\text{set}}
\times 30\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{sprint}}
=1080\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{wk}}
\]

and

\[
V_{6\mathrm{wk}}=1080\times6=6480\ \mathrm{m}
\]

The source states a “weekly sprint volume of approximately 1080m” [PAGE 2]. This is a modest sprint dose; it constrains generalization to higher-volume RST programs.

**Operator 6: Aerobic outcomes.** \(\dot V\mathrm{O}_{2\max}\) is reported in \(\mathrm{ml\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); anaerobic threshold (AT) is also in \(\mathrm{ml\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\); running economy (RE) is the average oxygen uptake during the final 30 s at 12 km·h\(^{-1}\) [PAGE 4]. The paper reports nearly identical \(\dot V\mathrm{O}_{2\max}\) improvements: “SAND 7.29% vs. GRASS 7.28%” [PAGE 8]. The RE group-by-time interaction was not significant \((F=2.0,\ p=0.15)\) [PAGE 6], so no surface-specific RE effect is established.

**Operator 7: ANCOVA model.** The primary between-group model was:

\[
Y_{i,\mathrm{post}}
=\alpha+\beta Y_{i,\mathrm{pre}}+\gamma_{g(i)}+\varepsilon_i
\]

where \(Y_{i,\mathrm{post}}\) is the post-intervention outcome, \(Y_{i,\mathrm{pre}}\) is the baseline covariate, \(g(i)\) is group, and \(\gamma_{g(i)}\) is the group effect. The source states: “post-intervention value as the dependent variable, group as a fixed factor, and the corresponding baseline value as a covariate” [PAGE 4]. Assumptions include normality, homogeneity of variance, and homogeneity of regression slopes. The slope-homogeneity assumption was violated for \(\dot V\mathrm{O}_{2\max}\) \((p<0.05)\) [PAGE 5]; therefore ANCOVA-based surface inference for VO2max is not clean, and the mixed ANOVA/simple-effects results carry the inference.

**Operator 8: Mixed ANOVA and effect size.** The time-by-group model is:

\[
Y_{ij}=\mu+\tau_j+\pi_i+(\tau\pi)_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ij}
\]

where \(\tau_j\) is time, \(\pi_i\) is group, and \((\tau\pi)_{ij}\) is the interaction. Hedges’ \(g\) is dimensionless and reported with 95% CI; the paper interprets \(g\) as trivial \(\le0.2\), small \(0.2–0.6\), moderate \(0.6–1.2\), large \(1.2–2.0\), and very large \(\ge2.0\) [PAGE 4]. Probability values \(p\in[0,1]\) are not effect magnitudes; wide CIs mean the surface contrasts are imprecise.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage is clear. Earlier work established that sand is not merely a visual variant of grass: Strydom et al. (1966) examined metabolic cost of marching over firm versus sandy surfaces; Lejeune et al. (1998) analyzed mechanics and energetics of human locomotion on sand; Pinnington and Dawson (2001a, 2001b) quantified energy cost and running economy on soft dry beach sand versus grass; Zamparo et al. (1992) examined walking/running energy cost on sand [PAGE 11]. That lineage created the prior that sand raises energy cost and changes kinematics. Binnie et al. (2013a, 2013b, 2013c, 2014) extended this to interval training, acute physiological responses, surface-specific sprint performance, and practical sand-training applications [PAGE 9–10]. Impellizzeri et al. (2008) is a direct ancestor of the present hypothesis: plyometric training on sand versus grass in soccer players, with jumping and sprinting outcomes and muscle soreness [PAGE 10]. Pereira et al. (2021, 2023a, 2023b) and Zhang et al. (2024) then pushed the literature toward meta-analytic and individual-response comparisons of sand versus hard/grass surfaces in team-sport players [PAGE 11]. The present study is retrocausally shaped by that genealogy: it takes the established “sand changes mechanical and physiological cost” premise and asks whether six weeks of RST on sand produces different adaptations from grass when sprint structure and session frequency are matched.

The multi-week trap is that the nine-week design is not nine weeks of uniform training. It contains a two-week familiarization phase, a one-week testing phase, and a six-week training phase [PAGE 2]. Familiarization reduces novelty but does not eliminate surface-specific learning. The testing phase schedules session one (jumps and RAST) and session two (graded treadmill) at least 48 h apart, but the authors admit that “some residual fatigue from session one may not be fully excluded” [PAGE 2]. That is a plausible carryover trap for aerobic outcomes, especially since VO2max and AT are sensitive to prior high-intensity work. The six-week training phase is also short relative to tendon, fascicle, and mitochondrial remodeling timelines; early gains may be neural and task-specific. The dose is only 1080 m/week [PAGE 2], so a null surface difference could reflect insufficient cumulative stimulus rather than true equivalence.

A second trap is the control condition. CON did time-matched standard technical soccer training during the same two weekly sessions [PAGE 2]. Therefore the SAND/GRASS versus CON contrast estimates “RST in place of technical training,” not “RST versus no training.” The surface-specific inference is restricted to SAND versus GRASS under identical sprint structure and volume, a point the authors explicitly make [PAGE 2][PAGE 6–7]. That is methodologically correct but often lost in translation to practice: the trial supports replacing two technical sessions with RST, not adding RST on top of all existing work.

A third trap is surface standardization. The sand was artificial, approximately 20 cm deep, raked before each session, and sessions were “conducted under predominantly dry (occasionally variable due to weather) sand conditions” [PAGE 3]. Sand moisture, grain size, compaction, and shear resistance can change stiffness and energy cost [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. The Clegg hammer captured vertical impact stiffness, but not shear or depth-dependent deformation [PAGE 3]. Thus the “sand” condition is a family of substrates, not a universal material. A wet-sand replication could produce a different stiffness ratio and different fatigue outcomes.

Finally, the post-test surface is grass for all groups [PAGE 4]. This is ecologically valid for soccer but creates a transfer-specificity trap: SAND is tested off its trained surface, while GRASS is tested on its trained surface. The fact that SAND still showed greater squat jump, minimum power, mean power, and fatigue index makes the result more rather than less interesting, but it does not fully resolve whether the effect is training-surface adaptation, testing-surface familiarity, or an interaction. The paper’s own recommendation—alternate sand and grass in a periodized approach—is a sensible hedge against overfitting to one substrate [PAGE 9].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Crisis habit regression.** Under maximal RST, athletes face high RPE and lactate, and the paper reports that RPE and LA rose from post-warm-up to post-training on both surfaces, with no significant group-by-time interaction for RPE \((p=0.43)\) or LA \((p=0.22)\) [PAGE 4]. The psychological risk is that under fatigue, athletes regress to overlearned grass-specific coordination: familiar stride length, familiar ground-contact timing, familiar SSC strategy. On sand, that habit is maladaptive because the substrate yields and slips; the athlete must either adopt a more concentric, stabilizing strategy or protectively co-contract. If the athlete chooses protection, the training stimulus is diluted; if the athlete persists, the local mechanical demand may be higher than the systemic RPE suggests. The paper did not measure kinematics or EMG, so this remains a plausible but unverified mechanism. The authors themselves note that “ground contact time, step frequency, and sprint kinematics were not measured” and that biomechanical explanations “should be considered speculative” [PAGE 7].

**Counter-trap.** The obvious counter-trap is pacing and effort distribution. The RAST is six maximal 35-m sprints with 10-s recovery [PAGE 3–4]. If SAND athletes unconsciously hold back early to avoid the unstable surface, they could preserve minimum power later and artificially lower fatigue index. That would look like “fatigue resistance” but be a pacing artifact. The paper’s data cannot fully exclude this because sprint-by-sprint times are not presented in the text; only aggregate best time, peak, minimum, mean, and fatigue index are reported [PAGE 6]. The fatigue-index coupling to minimum power makes this counter-trap especially serious: if SAND athletes simply produce a higher last-sprint power because they paced differently, \(FI\) falls without any change in the physiological rate of fatigue. The authors acknowledge the ratio problem [PAGE 8], but they do not provide the sprint-by-sprint decomposition that would adjudicate pacing versus fatigue resistance.

A second counter-trap is expectancy. Participants were randomized after baseline, but the intervention is not blinded: athletes know whether they are sprinting on sand or grass. Coaches also know. If the shared belief is “sand is harder,” athletes may give greater effort or interpret soreness differently. The null RPE/LA/HRmax differences argue against a large systemic expectancy effect [PAGE 4], but RPE was measured 30 s before and 30 min after training [PAGE 4], not during each sprint. Session RPE can obscure within-session pacing and recovery. A coach-facing theory-of-mind loop therefore exists: coach expects sand to be more demanding, athlete perceives sand as more demanding, athlete regulates effort accordingly, and the observed performance difference becomes partly a self-fulfilling pacing strategy rather than a pure tissue adaptation.

**Clock.** The adaptation clock is six weeks, two sessions per week, with 18 sprints per session and 1080 m/week [PAGE 2]. That is enough to change neural drive and jump performance, but probably not enough to fully differentiate tendon stiffness, muscle architecture, or mitochondrial density between surfaces. The testing clock also matters: session one includes jumps and RAST; session two is at least 48 h later but may still carry residual fatigue [PAGE 2]. A six-week window plus a single post-test on grass means the study captures a snapshot, not a trajectory. If the coach’s goal is match transfer, grass remains the specificity anchor; if the goal is concentric power and impact reduction, sand is a useful block. The paper’s practical recommendation to alternate surfaces is consistent with a clock-aware, non-dogmatic interpretation [PAGE 9].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical layer.** The physical difference is confirmed: SAND peak impact deceleration was \(393.6\pm70.3\) N versus GRASS \(1173.0\pm104.7\) N, \(p<0.01\), and the stiffness ratio was about 2.98 [PAGE 4][PAGE 7]. This vetoes any claim that the two surfaces were mechanically equivalent in vertical impact stiffness. But the Clegg hammer does not measure shear resistance, depth-dependent deformation, or traction [PAGE 3]. Therefore a strong claim that “sand’s compliance caused the performance difference” is only partially supported; the physical contrast is real, but the causal pathway is not fully characterized.

**Biomechanical layer.** The paper did not measure ground contact time, stride frequency, joint kinematics, EMG, or force-plate kinetics during sprinting [PAGE 7][PAGE 9]. It proposes that sand’s instability and compliance reduce ground reaction forces and elastic energy return, increase concentric demand, and raise trunk/hip stabilization requirements [PAGE 7–8]. Those are plausible mechanisms, but they are not directly tested. Veto rule: if a claim requires a specific biomechanical mediator and that mediator was not measured, the claim survives only as a hypothesis, not as proof. The squat-jump advantage is the most biomechanically coherent outcome because SJ isolates concentric strength; the CMJ null and peak-power null fit a story in which SSC-dependent and maximal-velocity expressions are less surface-sensitive. But even that story is inferential, not measured.

**Weather and substrate layer.** The sand was raked and predominantly dry, but “occasionally variable due to weather” [PAGE 3]. Moisture content, grain size, and compaction can alter stiffness and energy cost [PAGE 2]. The study did not report sand moisture or shear resistance. Veto rule: generalization to wet sand, beach sand, deeper sand, or differently graded artificial sand is not licensed. The 20-cm layer and raking protocol define a specific substrate condition [PAGE 3]. A wet-sand replication could attenuate or reverse the stiffness ratio and change the fatigue result.

**Internal-load layer.** RPE, LA, and HRmax did not differ significantly between SAND and GRASS [PAGE 4]. This vetoes a simple “sand produced uniformly greater systemic cardiometabolic stress” explanation. It does not veto local mechanical stress differences, because RPE and lactate are systemic/psychophysiological aggregates, not measures of Achilles tendon load, foot slip, or per-muscle activation.

**Aerobic layer.** \(\dot V\mathrm{O}_{2\max}\) improved nearly identically: 7.29% versus 7.28% [PAGE 8]. AT improved in both active groups versus CON, but no significant SAND-versus-GRASS difference was reported [PAGE 6][PAGE 8]. RE had no significant group-by-time interaction \((p=0.15)\) [PAGE 6]. Veto: any claim of a surface-specific aerobic advantage is not supported. The correct statement is “both surfaces improved aerobic markers relative to control; no surface difference was detected.” Because the study was underpowered for small-to-moderate differences [PAGE 8–9], “no difference detected” is not equivalence.

**Fatigue-resistance layer.** The fatigue-index result is partially vetoed as an independent construct because \(FI\) is ratio-derived and coupled to minimum power [PAGE 8]. If the question is “did SAND improve fatigue resistance beyond raising minimum power?”, the paper does not answer it cleanly. The stronger empirical claim is narrower: SAND produced higher minimum and mean power and a lower \(FI\) under this protocol. The mechanistic claim “sand training improves fatigue resistance” needs a decrement score normalized to peak or best sprint time, plus sprint-by-sprint data.

**Adjudication summary.** Survives: RST versus control improves jump, repeated-sprint indices, and aerobic fitness; SAND outperforms GRASS on squat jump, minimum power, mean power, and fatigue index in this sample; internal systemic load was similar. Partially survives: SAND-specific concentric/neuromuscular adaptation. Does not survive: surface-specific aerobic superiority; uniform greater systemic load on sand; strong biomechanical causation without direct kinematics/EMG; generalization to all sand conditions.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Define the verifiable reward as a binary pre-registered test: reward \(=1\) if the directional prediction is confirmed with the specified analysis and threshold; reward \(=0\) otherwise. The three scenarios below are designed to veto overbroad interpretations, not to re-litigate the observed trial.

**Scenario 1: Dual-surface, counterbalanced post-testing with sprint-by-sprint decomposition.** After the six-week intervention, test both SAND and GRASS groups on both sand and grass in randomized order on separate days. Primary outcomes: squat jump, mean power, minimum power, and a normalized sprint decrement:

\[
S_{\mathrm{dec}}=
\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{6}t_i}{6\,t_{\mathrm{best}}}-1\right)\times100\%
\]

Prediction: if SAND’s advantage is a true surface adaptation, squat jump and mean/minimum power should favor SAND on grass and should not reverse on sand; the fatigue-index advantage should persist after adjusting for minimum power. Veto prediction: if the SAND-versus-GRASS fatigue-index difference disappears when \(S_{\mathrm{dec}}\) is used, or if it attenuates by more than 50% after covarying minimum power, then the “fatigue resistance” claim is vetoed as a ratio artifact. If the squat-jump advantage disappears on both test surfaces, the surface-specific SJ claim is vetoed.

**Scenario 2: Direct biomechanical mediation with force plate, IMU, and EMG.** Measure ground contact time, vertical stiffness, concentric impulse, stride frequency, and lower-limb/trunk EMG during RST and during SJ/CMJ. Prediction: if sand’s effect is mediated by greater concentric impulse or greater stabilizer activation, those variables should differ between surfaces and should statistically mediate the SAND advantage in SJ and mean/minimum power. Veto prediction: if there is no between-surface difference in concentric impulse, contact time, or relevant EMG, then the proposed “sand forces greater concentric/stabilizing work” mechanism is vetoed.
