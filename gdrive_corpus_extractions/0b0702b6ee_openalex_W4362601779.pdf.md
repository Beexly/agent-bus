<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Operators. Let \(g\in\{E,N\}\) index expert and novice groups; let \(k\in\{2,3,4,5,6\}\) be the number of MOT targets; let \(d\in\{\text{pass},\text{dribble},\text{shoot}\}\) index decision type. The reported MOT variable is “tracking accuracy” (AVTN), and the reported SDM variable is “decision accuracy” (ASDM); the correlation variables are SVTN and SSDM. These are dimensionless proportions or correlation coefficients, except decision rate.

Binomial accuracy operator:
\[
X_{g,k}\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_{g,k},p_{g,k}),\qquad
\hat p_{g,k}=\frac{X_{g,k}}{n_{g,k}},\qquad
\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_{g,k})=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_{g,k}(1-\hat p_{g,k})}{n_{g,k}}}.
\]
Here \(X_{g,k}\) is the number of correct trials, \(n_{g,k}=24\times 30=720\) trials per group and target load [PAGE 5], \(p_{g,k}\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless probability, and \(\mathrm{SE}\) is dimensionless. Assumptions: independent Bernoulli trials within a subject-load cell, constant \(p_{g,k}\) within the cell, and no overdispersion. Breakdown regimes: \(\hat p=0\) or \(\hat p=1\) makes the binomial variance zero and the normal approximation degenerate; small \(n\) or strong within-subject clustering invalidates the independent-Bernoulli assumption. The reported expert 6-target value is \(240/720=0.333\), and novice is \(96/720=0.133\) [PAGE 6].

Chi-square operator:
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}},\qquad
E_{ij}=\frac{R_iC_j}{N},
\]
with \(\chi^2\) dimensionless, \(O_{ij}\) observed cell count, \(R_i\) row count, \(C_j\) column count, \(N\) sample size, and \(df=1\) for a 2×2 table. Assumptions: independent observations and expected cell counts \(E_{ij}\ge 5\). Breakdown: if any \(E_{ij}=0\), the statistic is undefined; if \(E_{ij}&lt;5\), the chi-square approximation is unreliable. The paper reports \(\chi^2=59.693\), \(P=0.000\) for overall MOT and \(\chi^2=31.975\), \(P=0.000\) for overall SDM [PAGE 1]. Note the paper’s phrase “Pearson’s χ-squared test” [PAGE 6] refers to this test, not to Pearson’s \(r\).

Pearson correlation operator:
\[
r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar x)^2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2}},
\qquad
t=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}},
\]
with \(r\) and \(t\) dimensionless, \(df=n-2\). Assumptions: bivariate linear association, independent observations, no extreme outliers, and roughly continuous scores. Breakdown: if either variance is zero, the denominator is zero and \(r\) is undefined; if \(|r|\to1\), the \(t\) denominator \(1-r^2\to0\); if \(n\le2\), \(df\le0\). The reported \(r=0.711\) for expert 4-target passing with \(n=24\) gives \(t\approx4.73\), consistent with \(P=0.000\) [PAGE 7]. The reported \(r=0.403\), \(P=0.051\) for expert 2-target shooting is borderline and should be treated as fragile [PAGE 7].

Decision-rate operator:
\[
f_g=\frac{N_g}{T_g},
\]
where \(f_g\) has units \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), \(N_g\) is the number of decisions, and \(T_g\) is exposure time in seconds. The paper reports \(f_E=0.74\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) and \(f_N=0.52\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) [PAGE 6]. Assumption: the denominator is the same effective decision window for both groups; the paper does not fully specify that denominator, so this is a gray-zone operator. Breakdown: \(T_g=0\) makes \(f_g\) undefined; unequal effective exposure or stoppage time makes group comparison non-identical.

A formal resource bound can be stated only as a theoretical constraint, not as a fitted source equation. Let \(C_{\text{att}}\) be attentional capacity in bits per trial and \(H_i\) the entropy demand of target \(i\) in bits. A necessary condition for reliable parallel tracking is
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{k}H_i \le C_{\text{att}}.
\]
Breakdown occurs when \(\sum_i H_i&gt;C_{\text{att}}\), \(C_{\text{att}}=0\), or \(H_i=0\). This is consistent with the paper’s statement that “the capacity of attention resources is limited; as the number of targets increases, the resources allocated to each target decrease, which leads to a decline in tracking accuracy” [PAGE 8]. It is not a substitute for the empirical binomial and correlation operators above.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Pylyshyn and Storm’s 1988 multiple-object tracking paradigm [PAGE 10, ref. 16] through Alvarez and Franconeri’s resource-limited attentive tracking account [PAGE 10, ref. 18], then into sport expertise work: Faubert’s professional-athlete visual-learning studies [PAGE 10, ref. 17], Mangine et al.’s NBA visual tracking speed study [PAGE 11, ref. 21], Qiu et al.’s expertise-level MOT study [PAGE 11, ref. 25], Jin et al.’s basketball dynamic visual attention work [PAGE 10, ref. 2], and Romeas et al.’s 3D-MOT passing-decision training study in soccer [PAGE 11, ref. 33]. The paper explicitly places itself against conflicting results: “3D MOT training did not significantly improve SDM [30,31]. More research supports that professional players have better MOT ability [17,32]” [PAGE 2]. It also notes that “no significant differences were found between intermediate players and nonplayers” and “no difference was observed among the three groups when tracking 2 targets [25]” [PAGE 3]. That lineage creates the central retromausal trap: the same MOT label has been used for laboratory tracking, 3D-MOT training, in-situ gaze, and game-coded decisions, but these are not interchangeable operators.

Multi-week traps. First, the training-transfer trap: Romeas et al. reported 3D-MOT training improved passing decisions in soccer [PAGE 11, ref. 33], while Harenberg et al. found 3D-MOT training did not significantly improve soccer decision-making [PAGE 11, ref. 30]. A single cross-sectional correlation cannot adjudicate that conflict. Second, the expertise-confounding trap: expert players had more than 9 years of experience and over 15 hours per week of training [PAGE 4], so any expert–novice difference is a bundle of skill, coaching, competition, and selection. Third, the null-result trap: the paper cites evidence that “expert players had no obvious advantage in MOT ability [20]” and that “3D MOT training did not significantly improve SDM [30,31]” [PAGE 2]. Fourth, the multiple-comparison trap: many target-load by decision-type correlations are examined; the 4–5 target passing correlations are strong, but the 6-target negative correlations are small and nonsignificant [PAGE 7]. Fifth, the ecological-validity trap: the laboratory used a 15.6-inch display, 1920×1080 pixels, 60 Hz, 12 white balls of 0.66° diameter moving at 5°/s for 10 s [PAGE 4], while the game study used two Canon HFG50 cameras about 10 m from the court and 5-minute 3 vs. 3 matches [PAGE 5]. These are different perceptual tasks. The paper’s own future-work statement, “Future imaging or EEG research will help us explain this neural process” [PAGE 9], marks the mechanism as unresolved rather than established.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three interpretations must be separated. Crisis habit regression: under high load, a player may fall back to overlearned action habits rather than flexible scanning. The data are only partially consistent: expert dribbling accuracy is 94.8% versus novice 94.6%, P = 0.886 [PAGE 7], so the expert advantage is absent for dribbling even though experts made 658 dribble decisions versus 278 for novices [PAGE 6]. That could mean dribbling is a habitual, less discriminative channel, or that both groups hit a ceiling. It does not prove “crisis regression” because the study did not manipulate crisis, fatigue, or score pressure.

Counter-trap: the player over-allocates attention to tracking too many objects and loses the decision-relevant passing structure. The paper’s own interpretation is that “having to attend to too many tracking targets (more than 6) interfered with players’ decisions” [PAGE 8], and at 6 targets the expert passing correlation is r = 0.005, P = 0.982, while novice passing is r = −0.199, P = 0.350 [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is therefore plausible as a load-dependent interference effect, but the experiment only measured 6 targets, so “more than 6” remains an extrapolated label. The strongest counter-trap evidence is the contrast between the positive 4–5 target passing correlations and the flat or negative 6-target correlations.

Clock: expert decision rate is 0.74 s⁻¹ versus novice 0.52 s⁻¹ [PAGE 6]. This is a temporal operator, but it is not a reaction-time clock and the paper did not impose a controlled time-pressure manipulation. The statement that expert players “exhibited fast and accurate SDM” [PAGE 9] is therefore an observed rate difference, not proof of a speed–accuracy tradeoff. Recursive theory of mind—modeling teammates’ and opponents’ intentions—is not directly measured. The paper’s ecological framing says players must monitor “multiple teammates, opponents, and their positions at the same time” [PAGE 3], which is a ToM-adjacent demand, but the MOT task uses abstract balls, not agents. Thus the ToM interpretation is inferential: expert passing decisions may benefit from better allocation to social affordances, but the present data cannot separate that from general attentional capacity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof. The MOT display is fixed: 15.6-inch screen, 34.4 cm × 19.4 cm, 1920×1080 pixels, 60 Hz, gray background 38.20°×21.3°, 12 white balls of 0.66° diameter, 2–6 targets, movement at 5°/s for 10 s, with possible occlusion [PAGE 4]. Any claim about tracking speeds outside this range, target counts outside 2–6, or displays outside this geometry is vetoed by the apparatus. The game layer is also bounded: two Canon HFG50 cameras, placed in the auditorium about 10 m from the court, 5-minute 3 vs. 3 matches, 28 matches, each player in 7 games for 35 minutes [PAGE 5]. Any claim about 5 vs. 5, full-court, or outdoor play is unsupported.

Biomechanical veto proof. All participants were female, right-handed, with normal or corrected vision; experts were 18–22 years, novices 18–21; experts had more than 9 years of experience and over 15 hours per week of training [PAGE 4]. There is no force-plate, kinematic, jump-load, or injury data. Therefore claims about biomechanical efficiency, left-handed transfer, male transfer, or clinical populations are vetoed. The only biomechanical claim licensed is that the observed accuracy differences occurred in this handedness, sex, age, and vision stratum.

Weather veto proof. The MOT experiment was in a laboratory, and the 3 vs. 3 matches were in the Basketball Hall of Northwest Normal University from August 29 to September 1, 2021 [PAGE 5]. There is no temperature, humidity, wind, altitude, or outdoor-surface measurement. Any weather or environmental-effect claim is therefore vetoed. The closest physical stressors are indoor fatigue and occlusion; the 27-minute MOT session included 1-minute white-screen breaks between blocks [PAGE 5], but fatigue was not modeled. Cross-layer adjudication therefore favors a narrow claim: under these indoor, female, right-handed, corrected-vision, 2–6 target, 3 vs. 3 conditions, expert performance is higher at moderate-to-high MOT loads and passing/shooting decisions, with a 4–5 target correlation to passing. Broader physical, biomechanical, or weather claims are vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Target-count extension veto. Test the paper’s “more than 6” claim by running the same MOT protocol with \(k=7\) and \(k=8\) in 24 experts and 24 novices, using the same 12-ball display and 5°/s speed [PAGE 4]. Prediction if the claim is valid: expert passing correlation at \(k=7\) and \(k=8\) is negative or near zero, and expert 7-target MOT accuracy is lower than the 6-target value of 33.3% [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: if expert 7–8 target passing \(r\ge0\) with 95% CI excluding a negative value, or if 7-target accuracy is not lower than 6-target accuracy, then “more than 6 interferes” is not supported.

2. Training-transfer counterfactual veto. Run a randomized 10-session 3D-MOT training study with an active-control group and measure passing decision accuracy in 3 vs. 3. Prediction if the transfer claim is valid: the trained group’s post–pre passing-accuracy gain exceeds the active-control gain, with 95% CI excluding zero, consistent with the Romeas et al. claim [PAGE 11, ref. 33]. Veto condition: if the trained group does not outperform active control on passing accuracy, the causal transfer interpretation is vetoed, especially given the conflicting null result cited as ref. 30 [PAGE 2].

3. Expertise-confound veto. Replicate the expert–novice comparison while matching or statistically adjusting for dribbling skill, weekly training hours, competitive level, and weekly video-game time; the paper already reports that video time “did not exceed 4 hours” [PAGE 4]. Prediction if the expert MOT–SDM advantage is not merely a skill bundle: the group effect on 4–5 target passing correlation remains positive and significant after adjustment. Veto condition: if the expert advantage on passing accuracy or the 4–5 target correlation becomes nonsignificant after adjustment, the causal “expertise improves SDM through MOT” reading is vetoed, leaving only a confounded association.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
