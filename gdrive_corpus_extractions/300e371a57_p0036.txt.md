Formal operators and equations. Luminance of target field: \(L_t(\mathbf r,t)\) [cd m\(^{-2}\)]. Luminance of surround field: \(L_s(\mathbf r,t)\) [cd m\(^{-2}\)]. Photopic illuminance at the cornea: \(E_v\) [lx] or spectral irradiance \(E_e(\lambda)\) [W m\(^{-2}\) nm\(^{-1}\)].

Weber contrast operator (dimensionless):
\[
C_W(\mathbf r,t) = \frac{L_s(\mathbf r,t) - L_t(\mathbf r,t)}{L_s(\mathbf r,t)}
\]
Valid when \(L_s > 0\); singularity at \(L_s \to 0\) (scotopic or near-dark surround) where Weber contrast diverges and the small-field signal becomes noise-limited.

Retinal adaptation state \(A(\mathbf r,t)\) (dimensionless, \(0 \le A \le 1\), 1 = fully sensitive):
\[
\tau_a \frac{\partial A(\mathbf r,t)}{\partial t} + A(\mathbf r,t) = A_0 - \alpha \int_{0}^{t} L_s(\mathbf r,s)\, e^{-(t-s)/\tau_a}\, ds
\]
where \(\tau_a\) is the adaptation recovery time constant [s], \(\alpha\) is the fatigue coupling coefficient [m\(^2\) cd\(^{-1}\) s\(^{-1}\)], and \(A_0\) is the dark-adapted baseline. Breakdown regime: if the inter-stimulus interval is much shorter than \(\tau_a\), \(A\) does not return to \(A_0\) and “equilibrium” is not restored, contradicting James’s claim that simultaneous contrast vanishes instantly on field removal [PAGE 26].

Lateral-inhibition contrast operator (center-surround):
\[
R(\mathbf r,t) = K\left[ I_t(\mathbf r,t) - \gamma \int w(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')\, I_s(\mathbf r',t)\, d^2\mathbf r' \right]
\]
\(R\) is retinal ganglion output [spikes s\(^{-1}\)], \(K\) is gain [dimensionless], \(\gamma\) is surround strength [dimensionless], and \(w\) is a normalized spatial kernel [m\(^{-2}\)] with characteristic spread \(\sigma_w\) [m]. Breakdown: when surround extent exceeds several \(\sigma_w\), additional surround area produces negligible extra inhibition (spatial summation saturation).

Opponent-process channels (dimensionless cone excitations \(L,M,S\)):
\[
C_{LM} = L - M,\qquad C_{S} = S - \frac{L+M}{2},\qquad C_{\text{lum}} = L + M
\]
Color contrast is computed primarily in \(C_{LM}\) and \(C_S\); breakdown occurs under scotopic conditions where \(L,M,S\) cone signals are absent and rods dominate (no chromatic contrast).

Information-theoretic bound. Let stimulus luminance random variable be \(\mathsf L\) and neural response \(\mathsf R\). Contrast discriminability is bounded by mutual information:
\[
\mathsf I(\mathsf L;\mathsf R) = \int p(\ell,r)\log_2\frac{p(\ell,r)}{p(\ell)p(r)}\,d\ell\,dr \quad [\text{bits}]
\]
Photon shot noise imposes \(\sigma_{\text{shot}} = \sqrt{N_{\text{photons}}}\), so the minimum detectable Weber fraction scales as \(\Delta L/L \gtrsim 1/\sqrt{N_{\text{photons}}}\) (dimensionless). Below this bound, no retinal or cortical mechanism can recover contrast.

Exact singularity/breakdown regimes: (a) \(L_s \to 0\) makes \(C_W\) undefined; (b) inter-field interval \(\ll \tau_a\) prevents adaptation recovery, so simultaneous contrast may persist beyond “an instant”; (c) temporal modulation above the critical fusion frequency (~30–60 Hz) abolishes separable contrast percepts; (d) spatial frequencies above the photoreceptor Nyquist limit alias the contrast signal.

The deeper historical trap is the conflation of retinal fatigue with cortical color constancy. James correctly notes that simultaneous contrast collapses immediately when the inducing field is removed [PAGE 26], which fits a short-latency retinal adaptation mechanism. However, later phenomena such as the McCollough effect—a contingent aftereffect induced by colored gratings—can persist for weeks or even months, far beyond any retinal fatigue time constant. If a research program runs contrast sessions across multiple weeks without controlling for long-term adaptation history, cumulative retinal and cortical plasticity will masquerade as “permanent” simultaneous contrast, violating James’s instantaneous-recovery observation.

Another multi-week trap is adaptation drift in longitudinal psychophysics. Observers tested daily on the same small-field/large-field paradigm undergo progressive changes in \(\tau_a\) and \(\alpha\) (from the adaptation equation above) as their visual systems tune to the laboratory illuminant. A null result in week four may reflect adapted equilibrium rather than absence of contrast, while a week-one effect may reflect unadapted retinal irritability. The genealogical lesson is that James’s [PAGE 26] physiology account is valid only within a single-session, short-adaptation window; extending the experimental clock to weeks recruits plasticity mechanisms outside the retinal-fatigue model he describes.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when an observer encounters an ambiguous or degraded color signal and regresses from local retinal reporting to object-based inference. James’s Von Kries example on [PAGE 26] is the canonical case: walking through snow-covered evergreens, the observer does not report the local retinal excitation of the bough interstices but infers “deep blue of pine-clad mountains.” Under perceptual crisis—low contrast, anomalous illumination, or occlusion—the visual system abandons the raw contrast channel and substitutes a generative model of the world.

The counter-trap is the opposite error: assuming that all contrast phenomena are purely retinal and therefore immune to top-down modulation. James explicitly rejects this exclusivity on [PAGE 26] when he admits that “our perception of a color is never in any degree modified by our judgment” is false; he cites color illusions due to wrong inferences about objects. A recursive theory of mind must therefore nest three levels: (Level 0) the retinal irradiance and adaptation state; (Level 1) the lateral-inhibition contrast computation; (Level 2) the observer’s model of the object and illumination. Failure to recurse between these levels produces misattribution: a Level-2 judgment (snowy forest implies blue mountain) is mistaken for a Level-1 retinal contrast effect, or vice versa.

The clock variable is the adaptation time constant \(\tau_a\) [s] from the equation in SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS, relative to the decision or report time \(T_{\text{report}}\) (~300–500 ms for a forced-choice contrast judgment). If \(T_{\text{report}} \ll \tau_a\), the observer reports while the retina is still fatigued, and the contrast percept is dominated by physiological irritation. If \(T_{\text{report}} \gg \tau_a\), equilibrium has restored [PAGE 26], and any remaining contrast must be explained by Level-2 inference or cortical constancy, not retinal fatigue. James’s claim that simultaneous contrast “never lasts for an instant on removal” [PAGE 26] implies \(\tau_a\) is shorter than the introspective reporting window for simple fields; the Von Kries case implies that when the clock is extended to naturalistic scene interpretation, Level-2 regression dominates.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. Photon shot noise sets an absolute floor on contrast detection: \(\sigma_{\text{shot}}/N = 1/\sqrt{N_{\text{photons}}}\). If the luminance difference between small and large fields produces \(\Delta L/L < 1/\sqrt{N_{\text{photons}}}\), no retinal mechanism can signal the contrast, regardless of “irritability” or fatigue [PAGE 26]. Any claim that simultaneous contrast is observed below this physical threshold is vetoed by quantum optics.

Biomechanical veto. Fixation is never perfectly stable; microsaccades of ~1–2 degrees occur at 1–2 Hz and continually refresh the retinal image. Because adaptation is local and spatial, microsaccades move the small and large fields across different retinal patches, resetting the fatigue integral in the adaptation equation. If an experiment does not control eye position (e.g., with a bite bar or video eye-tracking), the measured simultaneous contrast may reflect retinal image motion rather than a stable surround suppression. A claim that the large field “seems unchanged” while the small field shifts [PAGE 26] is biomechanically incomplete unless fixation stability is verified.

Weather veto. Von Kries’s snowy-forest example [PAGE 26] takes place under an outdoor illuminant with strong atmospheric path radiance and high-albedo snow (\(\rho \approx 0.8\)–0.9). Skylight is relatively blue-rich; snow reflects broadly but shifts the chromaticity of the scene. The “deep blue” inferred through bough interstices may be produced by the spectral power distribution of the open sky visible through gaps, not by retinal fatigue induced by the surrounding evergreen. Without spectroradiometric measurement of the interstice and surround, a weather/atmospheric layer veto applies: the apparent color contrast is confounded by outdoor illumination geometry.

Veto proof. The pure-retinal account on [PAGE 26] (“physiological processes, and not deception of judgment”) survives only if (1) luminance is photometrically above the shot-noise floor, (2) fixation is stabilized so microsaccades do not reset local adaptation, and (3) the spectral composition of the surround is controlled so that chromatic adaptation, not atmospheric path radiance, drives the effect. If any one of these layers fails, the retinal-fatigue explanation is vetoed in favor of a higher-order or environmental explanation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
