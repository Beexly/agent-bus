- **Independence:** Mutual independence is used to assert that `Var(∑_{i=1}^n f_i) = ∑_{i=1}^n Var(f_i)`. Without this, the key estimate in 46.B (likely a maximal inequality) fails. Pairwise independence is insufficient for series convergence.
- **Zero Means:** If means are non-zero, say `μ_n`, then `(1/n)∑ f_i = (1/n)∑ (f_i - μ_i) + (1/n)∑ μ_i`. The first part might converge if centered variances sum, but the second part requires `(1/n)∑ μ_i → 0`. Theorem D's hypothesis explicitly sets `μ_n=0`, simplifying matters. If `μ_n ≠ 0` but `(1/n)∑ μ_i → 0`, a generalized version could hold, but the given theorem does not cover it.
- **Measure Space:** `μ` is a probability measure. If `μ` is infinite (e.g., Lebesgue on ℝ), "almost everywhere" needs careful interpretation, and variances might not be defined relative to a finite total mass. The context implies probability.

**4. Adjudication Verdict:**
No cross-layer veto successfully invalidates Theorem D under its stated assumptions. The theorem is mathematically sound within measure-theoretic probability. However, its applicability to real-world scenarios hinges on verifying independence and variance decay, which are often idealizations. The sharpness result (Remark 3) confirms that relaxing the variance summability can lead to failure, so the condition is not arbitrary. Potential pitfalls include misapplying the theorem to dependent data or assuming it covers i.i.d. cases with non-decaying variance. Educators should emphasize these boundaries to prevent misuse.

In summary, Theorem D stands as a robust result in its domain, but its power comes with precise prerequisites that must be respected.
