<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s primitive operators are comparison, differentiation, integration/averaging, gain, and recursive prediction-error propagation. Formal statements, units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes follow.

1. Temporal mismatch and prediction. Let \(\tau_a\) be the agent’s sense-act latency and \(\tau_e\) the environment’s stable-state duration, both in seconds. The source’s qualitative condition is: if \(\tau_a \leq \tau_e\), sense-and-act can remain appropriate; if \(\tau_a \geq \tau_e\), the agent responds to a state that has already changed [PAGE 22-23]. Assumption: \(\tau_e\) is a characteristic state duration, not a universal clock. Breakdown: when motion shortens the effective environmental timescale, the agent needs lower latency or prediction; if neither is available, the control loop is structurally vetoed.

2. Closed-loop prediction error. The source gives the loop:
\[
E=R-P,\qquad P'=\Phi(E,P),
\]
where \(P\) is prediction, \(R\) is reality/target, and \(E\) is error [PAGE 31]. Units: \(P,E,R\) share the represented variable’s units. Assumption: \(R\) is observable or inferable at the comparison time. If \(\Phi(x,y)=x+y\), then \(P'=R\), but only under the assumption that \(R\) changes slowly relative to the loop [PAGE 31]. Breakdown: noise, delay, nonstationary \(R\), or a noninvertible plant makes simple error correction unstable.

3. Gradient and Taylor extrapolation. The basic gradient is
\[
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\approx \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x},
\]
with units \([y]/[x]\). The Taylor forecast is
\[
f(x_1)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)\Delta x+\frac{f''(x_0)}{2}\Delta x^2+\frac{f'''(x_0)}{6}\Delta x^3+\cdots
\]
[PAGE 35]. Assumption: local smoothness and a sufficiently small \(\Delta x\). Breakdown: large \(\Delta x\), discontinuities, nonstationarity, or noisy derivatives make higher-order terms unreliable; in practice the source notes that “many practical applications of the Taylor series only use the first derivative” [PAGE 35].

4. Weighted average and predictive combination. The weighted average is
\[
\hat{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k x_k,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k=1,
\]
with units of \(x\) [PAGE 40]. A source-style prediction combines a gradient term and an average term:
\[
P_{t+\Delta t}=\Phi(\vec S_t)=k_g S_t+k_g\frac{\Delta S_t}{\Delta t}+k_a\sum_{j=0}^{M}w_j S_{t-j\Delta t}.
\]
The source writes this form with gains \(k_g,k_a\) [PAGE 41-42]. Unit assumption: the timestep is normalized to one simulation step; otherwise \(k_g\) on \(\Delta S_t/\Delta t\) must carry seconds to preserve state units. Breakdown: stale gradients, over-weighted old averages, or gain imbalance causes overshoot or sluggish response.

5. PID control. The discrete PID law is
\[
u_t=k_p e_t+k_d\frac{\Delta e_t}{\Delta t}+k_i\sum_{j=0}^{t}e_j,
\]
where \(e_t=G_t-S_t\) is control error [PAGE 42-43]. Units: \(u\) has control units; \(k_p\) is dimensionless if \(u\) and \(e\) share units; \(k_d\) has seconds; \(k_i\) has inverse timestep under a discrete-sum convention. Assumption: a plant model \(S_{t+\Delta t}=\Omega(u_t,S_t)\). Breakdown: actuator saturation, delay greater than the controlled timescale, and integral windup.

6. Predictive coding. A source-style hierarchy uses
\[
\xi_k=x_k-W_kF(x_{k+1}),\qquad \frac{\partial x_k}{\partial t}=U_k\xi_{k-1}-\xi_k,
\]
where \(x_k\) are target/representation activations, \(\xi_k\) are prediction errors, \(W_k\) are top-down weights, and \(U_k\) are bottom-up weights [PAGE 112-114]. At equilibrium,
\[
\xi_k=U_k\xi_{k-1},\qquad x_k=W_kF(x_{k+1}).
\]
Units: activations are arbitrary real-valued activity variables; weights are dimensionless in the source’s simulations. Assumption: weights are trainable and the network reaches quasi-equilibrium. Breakdown: zero-error collapse, symmetric or degenerate weights, missing error neurons, and the biological implausibility of exact transpose-weight symmetry.

7. Boltzmann and free-energy operators. The Boltzmann distribution is
\[
p(s_i)=\frac{e^{-E(s_i)/kT}}{Z},\qquad Z=\sum_j e^{-E(s_j)/kT},
\]
often abstracted to \(p(s_i)=e^{-E(s_i)}/Z\) [PAGE 65-66]. Units: if \(kT\) is retained, \(E\) is in joules and \(kT\) in joules; if \(kT=1\), energy is in nats. Breakdown: \(Z\) is intractable for large state spaces; \(T\to 0\) produces deterministic ground states; large negative energies can underflow.

8. Free energy and variational free energy. The source defines
\[
F_g(d)=-\ln p_g(d)=\langle E_g(s;d)\rangle_g-H_g(s|d)
\]
[PAGE 75-76], and the variational bound
\[
F_g^r=-\ln Z+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r\parallel p_g)=F+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r\parallel p_g)
\]
[PAGE 77-79]. Units: nats when using natural logarithms. Assumption: \(p_r\) is a tractable recognition distribution. Breakdown: a poor \(p_r\) makes the bound loose; minimizing the bound does not guarantee minimizing the true free energy.

9. Contrastive Hebbian and Helmholtz updates. The Boltzmann contrastive rule is
\[
\Delta w_{jk}=\lambda\left(\langle x_jx_k\rangle_{d\in D}-\langle x_jx_k\rangle_{a\in S}\right)
\]
[PAGE 70]. The Helmholtz Delta rule is
\[
\Delta w_{kj}=\lambda x_k(x_j-s_j)
\]
[PAGE 86]. Units: activations and weights dimensionless in the source’s binary/sigmoid models. Breakdown: sampling error, slow mixing, and mismatch between wake and sleep distributions.

10. Temporal-difference error. The basal-ganglia/RL operator is
\[
\delta_t=V(s_{t+1})+r-V(s_t),\qquad V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha\delta_t,
\]
where \(r\) is reward/penalty and \(V\) is value [PAGE 56]. Units: \(r\) and \(V\) share reward units; \(\alpha\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: sparse reward, bootstrapping bias, and delayed credit assignment.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Retrocausal genealogy here means tracing later predictive-network formalisms backward to earlier scientific commitments, then checking which commitments remain load-bearing. The lineage begins with Helmholtz’s distinction between sensation and percept and his view of perception as statistical inference [PAGE 61]. It passes through Attneave’s redundancy-reduction theory, which treats perception as an information-handling process in which “much of the information received by any higher organism is redundant” [PAGE 93]. It then passes through Hopfield’s spin-glass energy formulation, the Boltzmann machine’s probability-energy link, the Helmholtz machine’s wake-sleep recognition/generation cycle, and Friston’s free-energy principle [PAGE 62-90]. Predictive coding itself is retroactively assembled from Attneave’s compression, Srinivasan’s retinal predictive coding, Rao and Ballard’s cortical model, and Whittington and Bogacz’s backpropagation approximation [PAGE 94-115]. The evolutionary branch runs from facilitated variation and neural Darwinism to cortical-column modularity, beta-gamma callup, and hippocampal phase precession [PAGE 118-147]. The synthetic branch runs from EANNs and EPANNs to CoDeepNEAT and predictive POE networks [PAGE 151-173].

Multi-week traps in this genealogy are persistent reasoning failures that survive surface inspection. Trap one: treating predictive coding as identical to backpropagation. The source says they mirror each other in dual flow and recursive errors, but “the main difference between the algorithms is procedural” [PAGE 115]. The trap is to assume local \(\xi\) equals global \(\delta\); it does not outside equilibrium or under arbitrary nonlinear dynamics. Trap two: treating free-energy minimization as empirical proof of a brain mechanism. The mathematics is a bound, not a measurement; the source repeatedly notes approximation and intractability [PAGE 77-79]. Trap three: over-reading cortical columns as clean predictive modules. The source warns that the brain is a heterarchy and that clean mapping “will surely fail” [PAGE 134]. Trap four: assuming evolution can simply replace gradient descent. The source presents EANNs as promising but notes that intricate developmental approaches “rarely produce networks capable of reaching the upper echelons of performance” [PAGE 151]. Trap five: assuming oscillations are a universal clock. Beta-gamma callup and phase precession are compelling, but the source’s account is a hypothesis with species and task constraints [PAGE 141-145]. Trap six: assuming procedural prediction is inferior to declarative prediction. The source distinguishes them but treats procedural prediction as evolutionarily effective in cerebellum and basal ganglia [PAGE 58]. Trap seven: assuming the hippocampus is only for navigation. The source discusses conceptual embedding and grid-cell triangulation but labels the broader cognitive extension as unproven [PAGE 51-55]. These traps matter because they convert a rich, multi-level theory into a single-level slogan.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind appears in the source as hierarchical prediction of another level’s plans and errors. The controller hierarchy predicts not raw sensory bits but the lower level’s control output: the higher level “tries to predict only what its lower neighbor is doing” [PAGE 135-136]. In predictive-coding terms, a higher level sends a prediction \(p_i\), a lower level computes error \(e_i\), and the gain \(g_i\) controls attention to that error [PAGE 135-139]. Recursive ToM adds another agent: an observer models the target’s model of the observer. The source’s machinery supports this because prediction errors can be generated at multiple levels, with higher levels representing slower, more abstract expectations [PAGE 36-37, 135-140].

Crisis habit regression is the first regime. Under stress, time pressure, or unstable working memory, an agent can regress from declarative prediction to procedural prediction. The source contrasts procedural prediction in cerebellum and basal ganglia with declarative prediction in hippocampus and neocortex [PAGE 58]. Procedural prediction is fast but rigid: it maps contexts to actions through tuned if-then associations. In a crisis, high gain on immediate error can suppress slower declarative model revision, producing rote sequence completion. The biological basis is plausible: cerebellar Purkinje cells and basal-ganglia actor-critic loops compute local prediction errors and temporal-difference signals [PAGE 39-43, 55-57]. The regression is not a moral failure; it is a shift in the precision-weighted hierarchy.

Counter-trap is the second regime. If an agent’s crisis habit is predictable, an adversary can exploit the habit by creating a cue that triggers the habitual prediction and then violating the expected consequence at the last moment. In source terms, the adversary manipulates the conditional stimulus or context that drives the agent’s generative model, raising prediction error at the agent’s lower level while the agent’s higher level still expects the habitual action to confirm its hypothesis. Friston’s active inference makes this especially sharp: action is chosen to “increase predictive accuracy” by producing sensory inputs predicted by favored hypotheses [PAGE 89]. A counter-trap therefore targets the agent’s prior, not merely its stimulus-response link. The defense is to increase gain on disconfirming error, reduce gain on habitual prediction, or introduce a deliberate model update before acting.

Clock is the third regime. The source’s oscillatory mechanisms provide temporal binding: gamma and beta rhythms gate when predictions and errors are compared, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity requires presynaptic and postsynaptic events within a narrow window [PAGE 141-145]. In a crisis, clock misalignment can make a correct prediction arrive too early or too late, converting a good model into a bad one. Phase precession in place cells is the clearest example: after learning, “the place cells fire predictively, indicating the locations that the animal will soon visit” [PAGE 145]. If the clock is wrong, the predictive field skews in the wrong direction. The counter-trap against a clock-dependent agent is to vary tempo, delay, or rhythmic context so that the agent’s learned temporal order no longer matches the world. The source’s beta-gamma callup and hippocampal theta-gamma coupling suggest that timing is not a cosmetic detail but a core predictive operator [PAGE 141-143].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a proof that a higher-level prediction cannot override a lower-level physical, biomechanical, or weather constraint. Formalize the plant as
\[
S_{t+1}=\Omega(u_t,S_t,\omega_t),
\]
where \(S_t\in\mathbb{R}^n\) is body/world state, \(u_t\in U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m\) is feasible control, and \(\omega_t\in\Omega\) is exogenous disturbance such as weather or terrain. The source’s control model uses \(S_{t+\Delta t}=\Omega(u_t,S_t)\) and action \(u_t=\Psi(E_t)\) [PAGE 22-23]. Let \(G_t\) be the goal and \(P_t\) the prediction. The predictive controller chooses \(u_t\) to reduce an error such as \(E_t=G_t-P_t\).

Veto theorem. Suppose there exists \(\varepsilon>0\) such that for every feasible \(u\in U\),
\[
\|G_{t+1}-\Omega(u,S_t,\omega_t)\|\geq \varepsilon.
\]
Then no predictive policy can force the next-state error below \(\varepsilon\) at time \(t+1\). Proof: the set of achievable next states is \(\{\Omega(u,S_t,\omega_t):u\in U\}\). If the goal ball \(B(G_{t+1},\varepsilon)\) does not intersect that set, the minimum achievable error is at least \(\varepsilon\) by definition of distance. Prediction can change the policy’s internal estimate, but it cannot enlarge \(U\) or change \(\Omega\) at that instant.

Physical veto. If the actuator is saturated, \(u_t\) lies on the boundary of \(U\), and \(\partial\Omega/\partial u=0\) in the saturated direction, then additional predictive certainty has zero control authority. Biomechanical examples include joint-angle limits, muscle-force ceilings, friction limits, and reflex latencies. The source’s spinal reflex discussion shows that lower-level loops can handle many corrections without cortical intervention [PAGE 125-127]; this is efficient, but it also means a cortical prediction cannot arbitrarily override a spinal or muscular constraint.

Weather veto. If \(\omega_t\) changes faster than the controller’s bandwidth, then even a correct prediction at time \(t\) may be obsolete by \(t+\Delta t\). This is the source’s \(\tau_a \geq \tau_e\) problem applied to exogenous disturbance [PAGE 22-23]. If the disturbance pushes \(S_t\) outside the controllable set
\[
C=\{S:\exists u\in U,\ \Omega(u,S,\omega)\in B(G,\varepsilon)\},
\]
then the error bound is unavoidable until the disturbance abates or the feasible set expands. Predictive coding can “explain away” expected sensory input, but it cannot explain away a physical state that remains outside the goal set. This is the cross-layer veto: high-level expectation yields to low-level feasibility.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a claimed predictive improvement is accepted only if a counterfactual, measurable test distinguishes it from degenerate or confounded alternatives. Three veto scenarios follow.

Veto 1: degenerate zero-error predictive coding. Claim: a predictive-coding network has minimized error because all \(\xi_k\) approach zero. Verifiable counterfactual: clamp a held-out input and measure whether \(x_k\) remains informative; also test whether \(W_k\) and \(U_k\) are nonzero and whether the error nodes respond to novel inputs. Prediction: if zero error arises because activations collapse to a constant, because weights vanish, or because the network ignores the input, then held-out error will rise sharply and mutual information between input and representation will fall. The source’s equilibrium condition \(x_k=W_kF(x_{k+1})\) and \(\xi_k=x_k-W_kF(x_{k+1})\) makes this test direct [PAGE 112-114]. Veto the reward if the zero-error state is degenerate rather than predictive.

Veto 2: free-energy confirmation trap. Claim: an active-inference agent has reduced variational free energy by acting to confirm its hypotheses. Verifiable counterfactual: compare the agent’s accuracy term against an independent sensory outcome and measure whether complexity \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r(s|\phi)\parallel p_g(s))\) increased without a corresponding external accuracy gain. Prediction: if the agent only samples expected data, it can lower the bound while failing on disconfirming probes. The source’s FEP decomposition \(F_g^r(d)=-\ln p_g(d)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_r\parallel p_g)\) and the complexity-accuracy form [PAGE 86-89] predict that a policy which ignores unexpected data will show brittle generalization. Veto the reward if the improvement disappears under a counterfactual observation that the agent did not choose.

Veto 3: evolved-architecture compute confound. Claim: an evolved predictive network, such as a CoDeepNEAT-style system, outperforms a baseline because evolution discovered a better predictive architecture. Verifiable counterfactual: match training compute, parameter count, data budget, and random seeds, then test on held-out tasks. Prediction: if the reported gain vanishes under compute matching or fails to replicate across seeds, the apparent architectural advantage is a search-budget or selection artifact. The source notes that CoDeepNEAT performs well on several data sets but is “unable to beat the best hand-designed” networks on popular benchmarks [PAGE 154]. Veto the reward if the evolved system’s advantage is not compute-normalized and held-out verified.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
