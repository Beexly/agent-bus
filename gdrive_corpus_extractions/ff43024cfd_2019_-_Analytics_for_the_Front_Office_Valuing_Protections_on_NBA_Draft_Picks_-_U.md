The valuation chain in Foster and Binns (2019) decomposes a protected NBA draft pick into a four-stage stochastic pipeline. First, the Team Performance Module simulates remaining scheduled games using an Elo rating update with k = 16 and a home-court advantage of 100 Elo points [PAGE 7]. The win probability for the home team is P(Home) = [1 + 10^(−ΔGameElo/400)]^(−1), where ΔGameElo = (Elo_H + HomeAd) − Elo_A [PAGE 7, Eqs. 1–2]. After simulating the full slate, team records are ranked, and lottery probabilities convert standings rank into a pick-position distribution for the current season. Second, if the simulated pick falls inside the protection interval, it rolls over to the following season. Because no games can be simulated for a season that has not yet occurred, the model applies a conditional probability matrix P(Rank_{X+1} | Rank_X) estimated from 1980–2017 historical standings [PAGE 8]. This introduces an intertemporal dependency: a team that finishes poorly in Year X is statistically likely to finish poorly again in Year X+1, so a top-1 protection in Year X can paradoxically raise the probability of securing the #1 pick in Year X+1 relative to a top-1 protection alone [PAGE 15]. Third, the Pick Position Valuation Module maps each pick position to a probability distribution of win shares over five years (WS/5yrs), drawn from 36 years of Basketball-Reference data [PAGE 9]. Raw distributions are smoothed by a monotonicity filter: if a lower pick position dominates a higher pick at any cumulative probability step, the two values are averaged to enforce the prior that earlier picks are always weakly more valuable [PAGE 10, Eq. 5]. Fourth, the Monte Carlo simulation draws from the finishing-position distribution, applies lottery odds, checks protections, rerolls if protected, and finally draws a player-value outcome from the filtered WS distribution; after N = 10,000 trials this yields an empirical distribution of asset value [PAGE 6, PAGES 12–14]. Module 3 then applies the Wang transform, distorting the cumulative outcome distribution by a risk parameter γ (Sharpe ratio / market price of risk) and taking the expectation of the distorted density to produce a single price in WS/5yrs [PAGE 12, Eq. 7].

Gray-zone audits reveal several singularity and breakdown regimes. (i) Elo does not account for how a trade itself alters roster strength; the authors acknowledge this computational limitation [PAGE 7]. If a traded pick is part of a multi-player deal, the acquiring team’s Elo should shift, but the model holds Elo fixed, biasing projected standings and thus pick-position probabilities. (ii) γ is calibrated from only two draft-pick-only trades: the 2017 Kings–Trail Blazers exchange (pick 10 for picks 15 and 20) and the 2017 Lakers–Jazz exchange (pick 28 for picks 30 and 42), yielding γ = 0.413 and γ = 0.436 respectively [PAGE 12]. With only two calibration points, γ is not identified for classes #1, #2–3, #4–5, #6–10, #11–18, #19–24, or #25–30; any mis-specification of γ non-linearly reweights tail outcomes because the Wang transform is an exponential distortion of the cumulative distribution. (iii) The model deliberately ignores time discounting: a protected pick that produces a player in years 2–6 is valued identically to an unprotected pick producing a player in years 1–5 [PAGE 21]. (iv) Rookie salary scale is omitted, so a top-10 protected pick that conveys late may carry a $3.4M starting salary versus an $8.1M salary if it conveys early [PAGE 22, footnote 14]; excluding cost-side cash flows makes the “price” a gross production value, not a net present value. Psychological anchoring on top-5, top-10, and top-14 protections further suggests market prices deviate from model-implied true values [PAGE 1], creating exploitable mispricing only if γ and the conditional transition matrix are correctly specified.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s intellectual lineage begins with financial asset pricing theory, citing Friedman’s price theory to argue that NBA trade prices need not reveal true value because of low liquidity, regulatory constraints, and asymmetric information [PAGE 4]. It imports the Elo simulation methodology directly from Silver and Fischer-Baum (2015), which models NBA team strength as a dynamic rating updated after every game [PAGE 7]. The player-production layer relies on 36 years of Basketball-Reference data and win shares as the valuation metric [PAGE 9], a choice justified by rookie-contract retention windows and comparability to incumbent players [PAGE 9]. The pricing kernel is the Wang (2002) universal framework for pricing financial and insurance risks via cumulative-distribution distortion [PAGE 12]. Finally, the authors gesture toward the NFL’s Jimmy Johnson Draft Pick Trade Value Chart as an analog for a standardized negotiation language [PAGE 21, footnote 13].

A multi-week trap is embedded in the protection rollover mechanism. Because protections condition conveyance on pick position in a single season, and unprotected conveyance in Year X+1 depends on the team’s Year X finish through the conditional probability matrix, a team’s short-term tanking decision cascades into next season’s lottery odds [PAGE 8]. The paper demonstrates this with Phoenix: a team in 5th-to-last place with the worst Elo has roughly a 20% chance of an unprotected #1 pick; under a top-1 protection that probability drops to near zero in Year 1, but if the pick rolls over, the 2nd and 3rd picks—which would have been protected—can become #1 in Year 2 [PAGE 15]. This creates a multi-week (indeed multi-season) incentive structure where protecting a pick this year can increase the probability of a premium pick next year, but only if the team remains at the bottom of the standings. The trap is that teams may overvalue top-1 protections because they anchor on the psychological “lottery-protected” label, even though, post-lottery, a top-14 protection has no special value by virtue of the lottery itself [PAGE 2, footnote 5]. Historically, this misalignment between psychological anchors (5, 10, 14) and conditional probability reality implies that protections are systematically mis-valued in the marketplace [PAGE 1].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when front-office decision-makers under pressure revert to psychologically salient protection levels—top-5, top-10, and top-14—rather than protection levels derived from the conditional standings distribution [PAGE 1]. The clustering in Figure 1 confirms that protections are not spread continuously across pick positions but bunch at round numbers [PAGE 1, Figure 1]. This habit generates a predictable counter-trap: a team using Foster and Binns’ model can identify where the market overpays for anchor protections and where it underpays for non-anchor intervals. For example, the Knicks’ unprotected pick is valued approximately the same as a top-8 protected pick, with the minimum at top-1 protection, because rolling outcomes into Year 2 increases the chance of a high lottery pick in a regime where teams are risk-seeking for low picks [PAGE 16]. A counter-traitorous front office would offer top-8 protection instead of top-5, capturing nearly the same value while conceding less downside protection than the anchor suggests.

The clock layer operates at two frequencies. First, the trade-deadline clock fixes the valuation snapshot; all results in Section 5 are from the 2018 trade deadline and value 2018 picks [PAGE 15]. As the deadline approaches, Elo uncertainty shrinks because fewer games remain, collapsing the pick-position distribution and changing the marginal value of each additional protection increment. Second, the rollover clock converts a one-year asset into a multi-year asset: a pick protected in Year 1 produces a player in Years 2–6 rather than Years 1–5, but the model does not discount those later contributions [PAGE 21]. This temporal mismatch means that a team facing an immediate win-now window will systematically undervalue a rolled-over pick relative to a team with a multi-year horizon, creating a recursive theory-of-mind gap where each party imagines the other’s time preference differently. The model’s exclusion of rookie salary scale exacerbates this: a top-10 protected pick that conveys late carries a $3.4M starting salary, whereas early conveyance can mean an $8.1M salary [PAGE 22, footnote 14], so the “clock” on salary-cap space interacts non-linearly with the protection horizon.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The model treats win shares as an ex-post production metric and does not model player availability, injury, or load management. A high-Elo team that loses a star to injury mid-season will not see its Elo adjusted for roster depletion, so the simulated pick-position distribution remains conditioned on pre-injury strength [PAGE 7]. This is a hard veto: any valuation produced after a major roster injury is physically invalid until Elo is re-seeded.

Biomechanical / athlete layer: Not directly modeled. Rookie contract length and re-signing expectations are acknowledged as potential adjustments [PAGE 14], but the physiological depreciation curve of a draftee over years 1–5 is outside the WS/5yrs accounting frame [PAGE 9]. The absence of age, minutes restrictions, or workload metrics means the model cannot veto a pick based on projected physical breakdown.

Weather layer: NBA games are played indoors, so weather is a null veto; unlike outdoor sports, temperature, wind, and precipitation do not introduce game-level variance into the Elo simulation [PAGE 7 implicit]. This removes a source of stochastic noise but also removes a potential hedge: weather-driven outcome anomalies cannot explain away Elo mispricing.

Regulatory veto: The CBA prohibits trading first-round picks in consecutive years [PAGE 3]. The model’s conditional probability matrix does not encode this hard constraint; if a team has already traded its next year’s first-rounder, a rollover protection is legally impossible, yet the simulation may still generate a rolled-over pick position [PAGE 8]. This is a regulatory veto that invalidates the output for any team lacking a tradable first-rounder in the rollover year.

Model-internal veto: The filtering algorithm vetoes any cumulative-probability crossing where a lower pick position dominates a higher one, enforcing monotonicity by averaging [PAGE 10, Eq. 5]. If the raw historical data contains too many inversions at the extremes, the filtered distribution drifts from empirical reality, and the Wang transform prices a smoothed fantasy rather than the observed cross-section.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
