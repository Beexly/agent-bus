The source is a 26-week randomized controlled trial comparing NASM-OPT periodization against traditional linear periodization in 42 adolescent athletes. The first multi-hop step is to reconstruct the intervention timeline: [PAGE 3] states, “In the NASM-OPT model, training across the macrocycle was organized into three sequential phases: a stability phase (weeks 1 - 4), a strength phase (weeks 5 - 22), and a power phase (weeks 23 - 26).” This means the performance endpoints are not just pre/post comparisons; they are cumulative products of stability, strength-endurance, hypertrophy, maximal-strength, and power exposures. The second hop is to connect the training phase sequence to the volume-load table and the outcome trajectories. [PAGE 5] reports that “the CON group accumulated nearly 1.85 times greater total training volume than the OPT group (2,393,472 kg vs. 1,294,494 kg),” despite OPT showing larger post-intervention gains in 1RM, CMJ, SJ, PSAP, and EUR. That creates the central inferential route: if OPT achieved more with less reported external volume load, then either the phase structure improved efficiency, or the volume-load metric omitted bodyweight/time-under-tension work, or group differences in maturation, technique, testing, or coaching expectation created part of the signal. The third hop is to unpack the jump-derived indices. [PAGE 4] gives “PSAP = (CMJ - SJ) × SJ - 1 × 100%” and “EUR = CMJ × SJ - 1.” Formally, these are normalized transformations of two jump heights, so they inherit all measurement error in CMJ and SJ and all validity assumptions about the squat jump having no countermovement. The fourth hop is to compare the reported phase trajectories. [PAGE 7] says, “the OPT group continued to improve through the 22th week, whereas the CON group plateaued by the 16th week.” This is not a simple uniform superiority: [PAGE 6] shows CON had higher CMJ at week 16 (40.12 ± 4.05 cm vs OPT 37.42 ± 4.76 cm) and higher squat 1RM at week 10 (107.24 ± 18.45 kg vs OPT 103.95 ± 19.76 kg). Thus the final OPT advantage is partly a late-phase phenomenon, consistent with the power phase and maximal-strength phase timing, but it also warns against retroactively reading the final result as if OPT were superior at every intermediate time point.

The gray-zone audit must separate statistically significant endpoint differences from causal mechanism. [PAGE 4] reports, “No statistically significant interaction effect of sex was found on intervention outcomes (1RM, CMJ, SJ, PSAP, EUR) across any training phase in either the OPT or CON group (p &gt; 0.05).” That supports pooling sexes, but [PAGE 8] also notes, “The limited sample size prevented the detection of potential sex-based differences within either model.” So the absence of a sex interaction is not strong evidence of equivalence. Maturation is another gray zone: [PAGE 2] states, “The mean bone age was 14.50 ± 1.10 years (range: 12.8 - 16.2 years),” and also admits, “the lack of direct PHV/PWV measurement represents a limitation of this study.” Bone age is not the same as somatic maturity, and a 26-week window in adolescents can overlap with growth spurts, coordination changes, and strength gains independent of periodization model. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(H_{\mathrm{CMJ},i}\) and \(H_{\mathrm{SJ},i}\) be the best countermovement-jump and squat-jump heights for athlete \(i\), measured in centimetres. The source’s printed formulas are reproduced verbatim as “PSAP = (CMJ - SJ) × SJ - 1 × 100%” and “EUR = CMJ × SJ - 1” [PAGE 4]. In standard notation, the intended operators are:

\[
\mathrm{PSAP}_{i}=100\,\frac{H_{\mathrm{CMJ},i}-H_{\mathrm{SJ},i}}{H_{\mathrm{SJ},i}}\quad [\%]
\]

\[
\mathrm{EUR}_{i}=\frac{H_{\mathrm{CMJ},i}}{H_{\mathrm{SJ},i}}\quad [\text{dimensionless}]
\]

Assumptions: \(H_{\mathrm{SJ},i}&gt;0\); CMJ and SJ are measured with the same device/calibration and maximal intent; SJ contains no deliberate or undetected countermovement; the best of three attempts is used for each condition [PAGE 4]. The source adopts reference values: “a PSAP of approximately 10% (range: 8 - 12%) and an EUR of approximately 1.1 were adopted as reference thresholds” [PAGE 4]. These are interpretive benchmarks, not mechanistic equations. The source also states that “These indices were treated as indirect markers of neuromuscular efficiency” [PAGE 4].

For external training load, [PAGE 4] defines “Volume Load = 1RM × %1RM × repetitions.” With \(L_{1\mathrm{RM},s}\) in kg, \(f_s=\%1\mathrm{RM}/100\in(0,1]\) dimensionless, and \(n_s\) as repetition count, the operational form is:

\[
\mathrm{VL}_{s}=L_{1\mathrm{RM},s}\,f_s\,n_s
\]

and for a phase:

\[
\mathrm{VL}_{\mathrm{phase}}=\sum_{s\in\mathrm{phase}}L_{1\mathrm{RM},s}\,f_s\,n_s
\]

Units are formally \(\mathrm{kg}\cdot\text{repetitions}\), although the source labels the phase table in “(Kg)” [PAGE 5]. That label is an operational simplification. The metric also does not automatically capture bodyweight exercises, time-under-tension, or plyometric repetitions; Table 2 lists bodyweight entries such as “BW，3×30s” and “15%，3×10” for OPT stabilization [PAGE 3]. Therefore, any “efficiency” inference from the 1.85-fold volume difference [PAGE 5] must be read as external-load efficiency, not total mechanical work or total physiological stress.

Singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. \(H_{\mathrm{SJ}}\to 0^{+}\): both PSAP and EUR diverge; at \(H_{\mathrm{SJ}}=0\) they are undefined. Human jump heights are positive, but the ratio is numerically unstable when \(H_{\mathrm{SJ}}\) approaches measurement resolution.
2. \(H_{\mathrm{CMJ}}&lt;H_{\mathrm{SJ}}\): PSAP becomes negative and EUR falls below 1; this is mathematically valid but flips the usual “pre-stretch augmentation” interpretation.
3. SJ countermovement contamination: [PAGE 8] warns that “squat jump compliance with the “no countermovement” instruction was verified only via standardized verbal cues and visual observation, so small, sub‑visual unweighting phases (≤ 2% of body mass) may still have occurred.” If larger unweighting inflates \(H_{\mathrm{SJ}}\), PSAP and EUR are biased downward. The source cites evidence that “squat jump unweighting amplitudes ≤ 2% of body mass do not meaningfully increase jump height, whereas larger amplitudes do” [PAGE 8].
4. Device/calibration mismatch or different jump protocols invalidate the ratio because \(H_{\mathrm{CMJ}}\) and \(H_{\mathrm{SJ}}\) no longer share the same measurement scale.
5. 1RM validity depends on squat depth and technique. [PAGE 4] specifies depth assessed by “visual inspection by two experienced strength and conditioning coaches observing from the sagittal plane”; inconsistent depth across groups or time points breaks load comparability.
6. Volume-load breakdown occurs when \(f_s\) is not referenced to the current 1RM, when repetitions are to failure or partial, or when bodyweight/plyometric work is omitted. Thus the reported “2,393,472 kg vs. 1,294,494 kg” [PAGE 5] should not be treated as total work equivalence.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from general adaptation stress theory through periodization models to youth athletic development and SSC testing. [PAGE 7] invokes “the general adaptation syndrome theory” and states that “overly linear loading or repeated training exposure without novel stimuli may reduce neuromuscular responsiveness and lead to plateaued or excessive fatigue states.” That lineage explains why the OPT model’s planned variation is hypothesized to avoid accommodation: stability, strength-endurance, hypertrophy, maximal strength, and power are sequenced rather than mixed concurrently [PAGE 3]. The NASM-OPT model itself is described on [PAGE 1] as organizing training into “three levels: stability, strength, and power,” with phase-specific targets. The youth-development lineage is also explicit: [PAGE 1] connects the study to the Long-Term Athlete Development model and to the idea that adolescence is a critical stage for motor-skill acquisition and strength foundation. That matters because the intervention window is only 26 weeks, while the biological maturation window is longer and partially unmeasured; [PAGE 2] admits that PHV/PWV were not directly measured.

Retrocausally, the final OPT advantage can be traced backward to three candidate ancestors: (a) the late maximal-strength and power phases, (b) the lower external volume load and possibly lower accumulated fatigue, and (c) the complex-training/PAP pairing in the power phase. [PAGE 8] states, “Complex training combines resistance exercises with plyometrics, leveraging post-activation potentiation (PAP) to concurrently develop strength and power,” and “The OPT model integrates these elements in the power phase, refining the force-power curve and improving SSC function.” The control group, by contrast, accumulated higher volume earlier and then plateaued: [PAGE 7] reports, “the OPT group continued to improve through the 22th week, whereas the CON group plateaued by the 16th week.” The multi-week trap is therefore early-signal misattribution: [PAGE 6] shows CON ahead at some intermediate points, including CMJ at week 16 (CON 40.12 ± 4.05 cm vs OPT 37.42 ± 4.76 cm) and squat 1RM at week 10 (CON 107.24 ± 18.45 kg vs OPT 103.95 ± 19.76 kg). A naive analyst could stop at week 16 and conclude the traditional model is superior for jump performance. The later OPT surge is consistent with the power phase, but it also means the study’s causal story is phase-dependent, not a uniform throughout-trial advantage.

Another genealogical trap is proxy genealogy: PSAP and EUR entered the study from SSC and eccentric-utilization research, but [PAGE 8] cautions that they are “indirect performance ratios derived from jump height measurements.” Historically, jump-height ratios were convenient field proxies; retrocausally, the study’s mechanistic language can outrun the measurement lineage. The correct backward reconstruction is: training phase → external load and movement velocity → strength and jump height → ratio change. It is not: ratio change → direct proof of reflex potentiation. The source itself recommends future “force–time variables and EMG” [PAGE 8] to verify mechanisms. Thus the strongest genealogy is performance-based, not mechanistic.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three decision loops matter. First, crisis habit regression: when a coach or athlete sees a plateau or a PSAP/EUR value below the reference threshold, the default under uncertainty is often to add familiar volume or intensity. The source creates a counterexample: the CON group had substantially higher volume [PAGE 5] but plateaued earlier [PAGE 7], while OPT achieved larger endpoint gains with lower reported external volume. A recursive coach who models the athlete’s fatigue must avoid the habit-regression trap of “more load equals more adaptation.” Second, counter-trap: the coach expects the athlete to respond to phase-specific stimuli, and the athlete expects the coach to judge progress through visible metrics. Because [PAGE 4] provides explicit reference thresholds, and [PAGE 8] says EUR and PSAP can provide “dynamic feedback for timely program adjustment,” there is a proxy-chasing risk: the athlete may optimize jump strategy or effort on test day rather than underlying SSC capacity. The counter-trap is not deliberate deception; it is a shared belief loop in which both parties treat the proxy as the goal. Third, clock: the training clock is 26 weeks, but the biological clock is adolescent maturation. [PAGE 2] gives bone age 14.50 ± 1.10 years and a range of 12.8–16.2 years, yet admits no direct PHV/PWV measurement. A coach’s mental model of “phase timing” can therefore be confounded by growth timing.

The recursive theory-of-mind audit should ask: who knows what, and when? The source reports randomization and supervised sessions [PAGE 3], but it does not report assessor blinding or coach blinding. If coaches knew group assignment, encouragement and technical cueing could differ. If athletes knew they were in the novel OPT arm, expectancy could influence maximal intent on jump tests. These are not proven biases, but they are recursive belief channels that a strict causal audit must flag. The safest interpretation is behavioral: the OPT protocol produced better late-phase test performances under supervised conditions, while the mechanism and the precise contribution of coach/athlete expectancy remain underidentified.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Define a veto as a measured layer that breaks the identifying assumption needed for a causal claim. Physical-layer veto: maturation. The study uses bone age but not PHV/PWV; [PAGE 2] states, “the lack of direct PHV/PWV measurement represents a limitation of this study.” Because the sample spans 12.8–16.2 years bone age, growth-related changes in lever arms, body mass, and neural coordination could veto any strong claim that the 26-week program alone produced all observed jump changes. The physical layer does not veto the randomized comparison, but it vetoes overconfident individual-level causal attribution.

Biomechanical-layer veto: jump and squat validity. The 1RM squat used visual depth inspection by two coaches [PAGE 4], and the SJ used verbal cueing plus visual monitoring; [PAGE 8] explicitly allows “small, sub‑visual unweighting phases (≤ 2% of body mass).” If unweighting exceeded the stated threshold, \(H_{\mathrm{SJ}}\) would be inflated and PSAP/EUR would be biased. The biomechanical layer therefore vetoes any claim that PSAP/EUR changes are pure SSC adaptations. It also vetoes treating the ratio as device-independent unless CMJ and SJ calibration are identical. The source’s own limitation statement supports this veto: PSAP and EUR “do not directly measure neuromuscular mechanisms such as motor unit recruitment, firing frequency, or muscle fiber type activation” [PAGE 8].

Weather/environment-layer veto: the source does not report temperature, humidity, indoor/outdoor status, or seasonal conditions. It does report that “All athletes were based at the same school and maintained similar dietary and rest habits, which ensured uniformity of experimental conditions” [PAGE 2], but that is not a weather measurement. Therefore any weather-specific inference is vetoed by absence of data. The defensible statement is narrow: the trial was single-center and sport-specific, so environmental generalizability is limited; [PAGE 8] states, “This was a single-center investigation involving athletes from a single sports school specializing in wrestling and badminton.” Cross-layer adjudication therefore returns a split verdict: strength and jump performance differences are credible within the trial, but mechanistic SSC and broad environmental claims are vetoed or heavily discounted.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Force-plate and EMG mechanistic veto. Verifiable test: repeat the 26-week design with force plates and surface EMG during CMJ/SJ, pre-registered outcomes including eccentric braking impulse (N·s), eccentric phase duration (s), peak concentric power (W), and normalized preactivation EMG (RMS). Prediction if the SSC claim is non-vetoed: OPT should show greater post-intervention eccentric braking impulse and preactivation EMG than CON after adjustment for baseline. Veto condition: if force-plate and EMG variables show no meaningful OPT advantage while PSAP/EUR still differ, then the “enhanced SSC function” interpretation is vetoed; the ratio change would be better explained by jump strategy or measurement variance. This directly addresses [PAGE 8]’s statement that “PSAP and EUR are indirect performance ratios derived from jump height measurements.”

2. Volume-matched or volume-clamped RCT veto. Verifiable test: randomize OPT versus CON with total external volume load matched within a pre-specified tolerance, e.g., ±5% of phase \(\mathrm{VL}_{\mathrm{phase}}\), while preserving each model’s exercise sequencing. Prediction if the “structure/efficiency” claim is non-vetoed: OPT should retain a post-intervention advantage in 1RM and CMJ at matched volume load. Veto condition: if the OPT advantage disappears when volume load is matched, then the efficiency claim is vetoed as a volume-confounded effect rather than a periodization-structure effect. This is necessary because [PAGE 5] reports that CON accumulated “nearly 1.85 times greater total training volume” but the bodyweight and plyometric components are not fully captured by “Volume Load = 1RM × %1RM × repetitions” [PAGE 4].

3. SJ unweighting correction veto. Verifiable test: use force plates to quantify SJ unweighting as a percentage of body mass on every trial, with pre-registered correction or exclusion for trials exceeding 2% body mass. Prediction if the PSAP/EUR claim is non-vetoed: after correction, OPT should still show a significantly larger post-intervention PSAP than CON. Veto condition: if correction for greater-than-2% unweighting removes the OPT–CON PSAP difference, then the SSC-index claim is vetoed. This follows [PAGE 8]’s own boundary: “squat jump unweighting amplitudes ≤ 2% of body mass do not meaningfully increase jump height, whereas larger amplitudes do.” These three counterfactuals are verifiable, pre-specifiable, and capable of overturning specific causal claims without relying on the original ratio endpoints alone.
