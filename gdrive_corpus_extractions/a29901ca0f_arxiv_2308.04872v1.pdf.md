<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Operator 1: CSRT track maintenance.** The paper treats CSRT as the top-view tracker: “we utilize the Channel and Spa-tial Reliability Tracking (CSRT) technique” [PAGE 4]. Formally, if \(I_k\) is the top-view image at frame \(k\), \(B_k\) is the bounding box, and \(F_k\) is the CSRT filter state, the operator is
\[
(B_{k+1},F_{k+1}) = \mathrm{CSRT}(I_{k+1},B_k,F_k),
\]
with \(B_k\) expressed in pixels. CSRT is not a measurement of world position; it is a pixel-domain track-maintenance operator. The paper’s claim that the top view “aim[s] to tackle obstructions and overlapping” [PAGE 3] is an assumption about camera geometry, not a guarantee: a top view can still be occluded by equipment or by a player’s body if the mount is not exactly overhead.

**Operator 2: HOG plus SVM target classification.** The paper says “We employ the Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) technique to describe image features” [PAGE 4] and “We incorporate a Support Vector Machine (SVM) detector” [PAGE 4]. Let \(P\) be an image patch from the rear-view camera. A standard formalization is
\[
\phi_{\mathrm{HOG}}(P) \in \mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad
\hat{y}(P) = \operatorname{sign}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\phi_{\mathrm{HOG}}(P)+b\right),
\]
where \(\phi_{\mathrm{HOG}}\) is a dimensionless gradient-orientation histogram, \(\mathbf{w}\) is the learned hyperplane normal, and \(b\) is a scalar bias. The paper states that “SVM optimizes the position and shape of the hyperplane by minimizing the objective function, aiming to separate the target and non-target regions as effectively as possible” [PAGE 4]. The decision value \(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\phi_{\mathrm{HOG}}(P)+b\) is unitless; it is not a probability. The source does not provide a calibrated posterior, so any claim that a given SVM output is “90 percent certain” is unsupported.

**Operator 3: ReID matching and threshold decision.** The paper says that “If the matching score exceeds a certain threshold and matches one of the players on the record, the ReID model assigns the corresponding original ID” [PAGE 4]. Let \(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{new}}\) be the feature of a newly detected player and \(\mathbf{f}_i\) the stored feature for roster entry \(i\). A defensible formalization is
\[
i^{*} = \arg\max_i s(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{f}_i),
\]
\[
\text{assign ID } i^{*} \text{ if } s(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{new}},\mathbf{f}_{i^{*}}) \ge \tau,
\quad \text{otherwise assign a new ID.}
\]
Here \(s\) is a unitless similarity score and \(\tau\) is a unitless threshold. The paper does not define \(s\), does not state whether it is cosine similarity, Euclidean distance, or a learned metric, and does not state the numerical value of \(\tau\). This is a major reproducibility gap. If \(s\) is converted to a log-likelihood ratio, its natural unit would be nats; if converted to a calibrated posterior, \(p \in [0,1]\). The paper provides neither conversion.

**Operator 4: Perspective transformation and the world frame.** The paper selects four court points in each view. For the rear-view module, “Sidepts1 represents the co-ordinates of four selected points in the rear-view video, corresponding to the four corners of the badminton court in the rear-view video. Sidepts2 represents the coordinates of the four points in the world coordinate system, corresponding to the known standard corners of the badminton court” [PAGE 5]. For the top-view module, the analogous matrices are `Roofpts1` and `Roofpts2` [PAGE 5]. The paper uses “the cv2.getPerspectiveTransform function to obtain the perspective transformation matrices” [PAGE 5].

For an image point in homogeneous coordinates,
\[
\mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\]
with \(x,y\) in pixels, a homography \(H\) acts as
\[
\mathbf{q}' = H\mathbf{p},\qquad
H = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{bmatrix}.
\]
The Euclidean world coordinates are obtained by perspective division:
\[
X = \frac{x'}{w'},\qquad Y = \frac{y'}{w'}.
\]
If the court dimensions are supplied in metres, then \(X,Y\) are in metres; if the authors use a court-coordinate abstraction, the unit is a court unit rather than SI. The source says only “known real dimensions” [PAGE 4], so metres are an assumption, not an explicit statement.

The paper’s printed side-view relation is
\[
[x',y',1'] = \mathrm{SideM}[x,y,l],
\]
and its printed top-view relation is
\[
[x',y',w'] = \mathrm{RoofM}[x,y,1],
\]
both on [PAGE 5]. A corrected homogeneous reading is
\[
\mathbf{q}_W \sim \mathrm{SideM}\,\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{rear}},
\qquad
\mathbf{q}_W \sim \mathrm{RoofM}\,\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{top}},
\]
where \(\sim\) means equality up to a nonzero scale. To display a rear-view point in the top-view image, the composition would be
\[
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{top}}' \sim \mathrm{RoofM}^{-1}\,\mathrm{SideM}\,\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{rear}},
\]
provided \(\det(\mathrm{RoofM}) \ne 0\). The paper’s later statement that “RoofM2 is the inverse matrix of RoofM” [PAGE 5] is consistent with the need for an inverse in a display composition, but the printed formula using `SideM2` and `[x,y,l]` is not consistent with the preceding definitions.

**Assumptions and singularity regimes.** The homography method assumes: a planar court; four non-collinear and non-repeated correspondences; a static camera; negligible lens distortion or prior undistortion; and accurate court-corner selection. The four-point homography has eight degrees of freedom after scale normalization. If the correspondence matrix has rank below eight, the solution is not unique; if three of the four points are collinear, the estimate is degenerate. If \(w' = 0\), the projected point lies at infinity and cannot be assigned finite court coordinates. If \(\det(H) = 0\), the inverse \(H^{-1}\) does not exist. A practical calibration audit should compute
\[
e_j = \left\| \pi(H\mathbf{p}_j) - \mathbf{q}_j \right\|_2,
\]
where \(e_j\) is a reprojection error in pixels or world units, and reject the calibration if \(e_j\) exceeds a stated tolerance. The paper does not report \(e_j\), \(\det(H)\), or the condition number of \(H\).

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer has three regimes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

**Crisis habit regression.** Under occlusion, overlap, or boundary crossing, the system tends to regress to its most familiar habit: keep the last CSRT box, keep the last feature match, or flip `idState` according to a threshold. Algorithm 1 does exactly this: it sets `idState` to “logout” when a player leaves the top-view field and to “login” when a feature matches an existing entry [PAGE 3]. The habit is reasonable in calm conditions, but in a crisis it can silently merge two players or assign a new ID to the same player. The human operator has a parallel regression: when the colored trajectory looks smooth, the coach may trust it even when the two-camera geometry is stale. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the coach’s belief about the system’s reliability is itself an input to how the output is used; a smooth line is not evidence of identity correctness.

**Counter-trap.** The counter-trap is to force disagreement between appearance and physical continuity before accepting an ID. If the rear-view HOG-SVM says “same player” but the top-view world coordinates say two distinct bodies are simultaneously present, the correct action is to output “uncertain” or “requires review,” not to collapse the two trajectories. The paper’s own Figure 2 versus Figure 5 contrast is the counter-trap case: the side view misidentified both players as the white-shirt bending player, while the top view plus perspective transformation separated the red and blue trajectories [PAGE 6]. A robust system should use that cross-view disagreement as a veto, not as a tie-breaker to be averaged away.

**Clock.** There are two clocks: event time and processing time. Event time requires the two cameras to be synchronized within a tolerance; otherwise the composition \(\mathrm{RoofM}^{-1}\mathrm{SideM}\) is applied to points from different instants. Processing time creates a different failure: if the system must run live, a deadline can force a lower ReID threshold or a skipped ReID check, which increases ID switches. For post-hoc coaching, the clock can be relaxed and trajectories can be smoothed; for live feedback, the system must expose uncertainty rather than pretending to be certain. The paper does not report latency, frame rate, or synchronization error, so the clock layer remains unquantified.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

**Physical layer.** The physical veto is the strongest because the court is planar and its real dimensions are known [PAGE 4]. A candidate world trajectory must lie inside the court polygon \(\mathcal{C}\) up to measurement uncertainty:
\[
\mathbf{X}_k \in \mathcal{C} \quad \text{for all } k,
\]
with \(\mathbf{X}_k = (X_k,Y_k)\) in metres or court units. Two distinct players cannot occupy the same physical point within a body-radius margin:
\[
\left\| \mathbf{X}_{i,k} - \mathbf{X}_{j,k} \right\|_2 \ge r_{\min}
\quad \text{for } i \ne j,
\]
where \(r_{\min}\) is a calibrated body-separation margin in metres. If the rear-view identity assignment says two simultaneous top-view blobs are the same player, and the top-view world separation exceeds \(r_{\min}\), the identity assignment should be vetoed. The physical layer also includes calibration admissibility: \(\det(H) \ne 0\), four non-collinear points, and reprojection error below tolerance. The paper’s top-view camera is mounted with “a downward angle of 90 degrees towards the ground” [PAGE 4], and the rear-view camera with “a downward angle of 45 degrees” [PAGE 4]; if those angles are not maintained, the physical projection assumptions fail.

**Biomechanical layer.** The biomechanical veto is a continuity check. For a candidate trajectory,
\[
v_{k\to k+1} = \frac{\left\| \mathbf{X}_{k+1}-\mathbf{X}_k \right\|_2}{\Delta t},
\qquad
a_{k} = \frac{\left\| \mathbf{v}_{k+1}-\mathbf{v}_k \right\|_2}{\Delta t},
\]
with \(v\) in metres per second and \(a\) in metres per second squared. If a proposed ID swap requires a velocity or acceleration beyond a calibrated human bound, the swap should be vetoed. The paper’s “fast back-and-forth movements” [PAGE 1] make this layer necessary, but the paper does not estimate \(v\) or \(a\). The biom

echanical layer also explains why pose alone cannot decide identity: the white-shirt player “bending over” in Figure 2 [PAGE 6] changes the HOG feature without changing identity. The physical top-view continuity should outrank the pose-sensitive appearance cue.

**Weather layer.** The weather layer is not admissible from the source. The paper concerns an indoor badminton court and supplies no weather, airflow, temperature, or lighting measurements. Weather can affect a shuttle’s flight, but the paper tracks players, not the shuttle, and it provides no weather covariates. A weather veto would therefore be an invented layer. If venue airflow or lighting is later measured, it could be added as a covariate, but it cannot be asserted from these seven pages.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
