c_k^\top=\mathbf e^\top D_kAE_{k-1},\qquad E_k=D(c_k)^{-1},

\[
c=D(A^Tr)^{-1}\mathbf e,\qquad r=D(Ac)^{-1}\mathbf e,
\]

c_{k+1}=D(A^Tr_k)^{-1}\mathbf e,\qquad r_{k+1}=D(Ac_{k+1})^{-1}\mathbf e.
\]
The symmetric variant on [PAGE 5 of 18] is
\[
x=D(Ax)^{-1}\mathbf e,\qquad x_k=D(Ax_{k-1})^{-1}\mathbf e.
\]
The stopping estimate on [PAGE 4 of 18] is

\mathrm{err}_k=\|c_k\circ d_{k+1}-\mathbf e\|_1,\qquad d_{k+1}=D(c_{k+1})^{-1}\mathbf e,
\]
where \(\circ\) is the Hadamard product.

\[
d(x,y)=\log\max_{i,j}\frac{x_iy_j}{x_jy_i},\qquad

\]

\]
The paper states this is an a priori bound and “can be very weak in practice” [PAGE 4 of 18]. The sharper theorem is Theorem 4.4 and Theorem 4.5 on [PAGE 8 of 18]. For a symmetric fully indecomposable matrix, Theorem 4.4 gives the alternating-subsequence contraction
\[
\|d_{k+2}\|\le |\lambda_2|^2\|d_k\|,
\]
where \(\lambda_2\) is the subdominant eigenvalue of the limiting doubly stochastic matrix \(P\). For the general SK algorithm on a fully indecomposable matrix, Theorem 4.5 gives
\[

-\begin{pmatrix}r^*\\ c^*\end{pmatrix}\right\|
\le \sigma_2^2
\left\|\begin{pmatrix}r_{k}\\ c_{k}\end{pmatrix}
-\begin{pmatrix}r^*\\ c^*\end{pmatrix}\right\|,
\]
where \(\sigma_2\) is the second singular value of \(P\). The source says on [PAGE 8 of 18]: “If A is fully indecomposable then the SK algorithm will converge linearly to vectors \(r^*\) and \(c^*\) such that \(D(r^*)AD(c^*)=P\) where P is doubly stochastic.”

\]
and [PAGE 13 of 18] gives the eigenvalue statement \(\sigma(P_\alpha)=\{1,\alpha\lambda_2,\ldots,\alpha\lambda_n\}\). Corollary 5.2 gives the doubly stochastic singular-value version: if \(P\) has singular values \(1,\sigma_2,\ldots,\sigma_n\), then \(P_\alpha\) has singular values \(1,\alpha\sigma_2,\ldots,\alpha\sigma_n\). c_{k+1}=D(G^Tr_k+\gamma\|r_k\|_1\mathbf e)^{-1}\mathbf e,

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is unusually long for a simple alternating normalization. [PAGE 2 of 18] says the method was “claimed to have ﬁrst been used in the 1930’s for calculating trafﬁc ﬂow [4]” and appeared in 1937 for telephone traffic prediction [14]; it is also called the RAS method [1] and Bregman’s balancing method [15]. The numerical-analysis name comes from Sinkhorn and Knopp’s 1960s convergence results [19]. The reference list on [PAGE 18 of 18] records Bacharach’s 1970 input-output book [1], Bregman’s 1967 convergence proof [4], Kruithof’s 1937 telephone-traffic paper [14], Lamond and Stewart’s Bregman balancing paper [15], Sinkhorn and Knopp’s 1967 Pacific Journal paper [19], Soules’s 1991 rate paper [20], Franklin and Lorenz’s 1989 scaling paper [10], Kalantari and Khachiyan’s complexity paper [12], and Tomlin’s 2003 web-ranking paper [21]. The retrocausal point is that the later web-ranking application in §5 is what makes the earlier convergence-rate question urgent: PageRank supplies the dominant random-walk habit, and the SK algorithm supplies a two-sided balancing alternative whose cost is comparable because it is dominated by two matrix-vector multiplies.

Multi-week traps are easy to predict from this lineage. The first trap is to conflate “has total support” with “is fully indecomposable.” [PAGE 3 of 18] gives total support as the necessary and sufficient condition for existence and for SK convergence, but [PAGE 8 of 18] and [PAGE 9 of 18] make the sharp rate theorem require full indecomposability. A total-support matrix can be permuted into block diagonal form; then each block has its own rate and the global upper bound is a maximum, not necessarily an attained value. The second trap is to treat the symmetric iteration as a convergent point iteration. [PAGE 6 of 18] says the symmetric algorithm can oscillate between scalar multiples of the fixed point; only alternating subsequences are controlled by \(|\lambda_2|^2\). The third trap is to take the Franklin-Lorenz bound as sharp for sparse matrices. It is an a priori positive-matrix bound; as the smallest element approaches zero, the bound approaches one, and [PAGE 4 of 18] calls it weak in practice. The fourth trap is to mix the PageRank damping parameter with the SK rank-one perturbation parameter. In the doubly stochastic special case, \(P'=P+\gamma\mathbf e\mathbf e^T\) maps to \(P_\alpha\) with \(\alpha=(1+n\gamma)^{-1}\), so \(\gamma\) is not \(\alpha\) and the rate is \((1+n\gamma)^{-2}\), not \(\alpha^2\) without the substitution. The fifth trap is to forget dangling nodes. PageRank requires preprocessing for empty columns, while [PAGE 10 of 18] argues that the SK ranking needs “no need to treat dangling nodes differently to any other” because the rank-one perturbation and the reciprocal formulation avoid an explicit dense perturbation. The sixth trap is to ignore the source’s practical range. [PAGE 17 of 18] recommends choosing \(\gamma\) so that \(.01\le \gamma n\le 1\), balancing speed and ordering quality; outside that range, the empirical comparison to PageRank is not supported by the reported experiments.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. An agent under time pressure reverts to the most familiar web-ranking habit: make columns stochastic, run the power method, and read the stationary distribution. But [PAGE 1 of 18] warns: “if we use this approach then the stationary distribution is absolutely useless for ranking purposes. However, in §5 we argue that the entries of D and E can be used as alternative measures.” The SK ranking is not a one-sided column-scaling method; it is a two-sided balancing method, and the two diagonal factors are interpreted as authority and hub tendencies. A crisis response that normalizes only one side destroys the very object that the paper proposes to measure.

The counter-trap is overcorrection: having learned that SK is not PageRank, an agent may overapply the sharp rate theorem and assume every nonnegative matrix with total support has rate \(\sigma_2(P)^2\). The source does not support that. Theorem 4.5 on [PAGE 8 of 18] is conditioned on full indecomposability; for total support without full indecomposability, [PAGE 10 of 18] has blockwise rates and only the upper bound \(\max_i\sigma_2^2(P_i)\), with the explicit warning: “However, we may not see this upper bound reached.” A second counter-trap is to call the symmetric oscillation a divergence. It is not divergence; it is a two-cycle in scalar multiples. [PAGE 6 of 18] says: “Eventually we oscillate between a pair of vectors that are scalar multiples of the fixed point.” The right counter is to examine the alternating subsequences and the distance to the fixed line, not the raw pointwise difference.

Clock adjudication concerns timing and what is measured per iteration. Theorem 4.4 gives \(|\lambda_2|^2\) for two symmetric steps; Theorem 4.5 gives \(\sigma_2^2\) for one SK step. These are consistent because one SK step equals two symmetric steps on the bipartite matrix \(S\). An observer who compares a per-two-step symmetric contraction to a per-one-step SK contraction will double-count the square. Likewise, the asymptotic theorem says there exists a \(K\) after which the bound holds, not that the bound is visible from iteration one. The stopping criterion \(\mathrm{err}_k\) on [PAGE 4 of 18] provides a computable clock, but it measures the row-balance residual after a row-normalization step, not the full spectral asymptotic. In web experiments, the clock is also affected by \(\gamma\): [PAGE 14 of 18] shows convergence speeding up as \(\gamma\) varies through \(0.01/n\), \(0.1/n\), \(0.5/n\), \(1/n\), \(2/n\), and \(4/n\), and [PAGE 13 of 18] gives the special-case rate \((1+n\gamma)^{-2}\). Thus the recursive theory of mind must hold three separate clocks: the mathematical contraction clock, the stopping-rule clock, and the empirical ranking-quality clock. Collapsing them into one “converged” judgment is the hidden error.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Prediction: the unmodified algorithm fails to produce finite positive scalings and cannot satisfy the row-sum and column-sum residuals within tolerance; the RLVR reward rejects the claim. If the empty column is replaced by ones, the test may pass, but then the modified matrix, not the original, is being balanced.

Scenario 2. Counterfactual claim: “For every matrix with total support, the measured asymptotic SK rate is exactly \(\sigma_2^2(P)\).” Veto: Theorem 4.5 on [PAGE 8 of 18] is stated for fully indecomposable matrices. A total-support matrix can be a direct sum of fully indecomposable blocks; [PAGE 10 of 18] gives the upper bound \(\max_i\sigma_2^2(P_i)\) and warns that the bound may not be reached. Verifiable test: construct a block diagonal matrix whose first block is a positive fully indecomposable 2 by 2 block with a known \(\sigma_2<1\), and whose second block is the 2 by 2 identity, which is already doubly stochastic. The global second singular value can be 1, making the global upper bound vacuous, while the measured error decay follows the non-doubly-stochastic block. Prediction: the observed contraction factor is below 1 and tracks the active block, not the global maximum; an RLVR checker that demands exact equality to the global \(\sigma_2^2(P)\) rejects the universal-rate claim.

Scenario 3. Verifiable test: run the symmetric iteration on a positive symmetric fully indecomposable matrix that is not already doubly stochastic. Track \(\mathrm{dist}(x_k,\operatorname{span}(x^*))\), the ratio of consecutive iterates, and the alternating subsequences \(x_{2k}\) and \(x_{2k+1}\). Prediction: the distance to the fixed line decreases at rate \(|\lambda_2|^2\), while the raw point error does not go to zero; the even and odd subsequences approach different scalar multiples.
