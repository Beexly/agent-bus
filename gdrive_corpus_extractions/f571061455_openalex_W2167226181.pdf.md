\[

=e(\bar{\pi})
-E[d(\bar{\pi},\pi_\gamma)]

The paper explicitly notes that “the grand probability (aka climatological frequency) of the target will always be a reliable forecast” but is not very informative [PAGE 2]. =E[d(\bar{\pi},\pi_1)]
-E[d(\pi_2,\pi_1)].

The paper’s lineage is explicitly retrocausal in the sense that later formalizations make earlier forecast-verification ideas legible as special cases. The introduction cites Good (1952) and Brier (1950) as pioneering work, then Brown (1970), Savage (1971), and de Finetti (1970) on scoring rules as devices for eliciting probabilities [PAGE 1]. It cites Murphy and Winkler (1977) for reliability and Murphy and Winkler (1987) and Murphy (1993, 1996) for forecast-verification frameworks [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. It cites DeGroot and Fienberg (1982) for sufficiency and refinement, and says their binary-target decomposition “seems not to be widely known in the atmospheric sciences community” [PAGE 3]. It cites Gneiting et al. (2005a) for the conjectured sharpness principle, Gneiting and Raftery (2007) for strictly proper scoring rules, and Bröcker and Smith (2008) for ensemble-to-predictive-distribution work [PAGE 2, PAGE 8]. The retrocausal reading is this: the Brier-score decomposition is the binary special case; DeGroot-Fienberg is the binary proper-score precursor; Bröcker’s Equation (13) generalizes both to finite-valued targets; and the sharpness principle is reinterpreted as the statement that \(\pi_\gamma\) is optimal among forecasts for which \(\gamma\) is sufficient [PAGE 8].

The multi-week traps are mostly traps of stationarity and scope. The paper’s reliability discussion assumes “weather forecasts are often issued over a long period of time under (more or less) stationary conditions, allowing for archives of forecast-observation pairs to be collected” [PAGE 2]. Over a multi-week horizon, a forecast system may pass through regimes in which \(\bar{\pi}\) and \(\pi_\gamma\) drift. If \(\bar{\pi}\) is treated as fixed while the true climatology changes, the uncertainty term \(e(\bar{\pi})\) is mis-specified and the resolution term \(E[d(\bar{\pi},\pi_\gamma)]\) is measured against a stale baseline. If \(\pi_\gamma\) drifts because the forecast model adapts or because the weather regime changes, then an archive-based estimate of reliability can be systematically wrong. The paper explicitly excludes seasonal effects [PAGE 3, footnote 2], so seasonal drift is outside the formal scope. A second multi-week trap is the confusion between resolution and reliability: a forecast can remain highly reliable by regressing to climatology, but that regression destroys resolution [PAGE 2]. A third trap is overfitting a short archive: a forecast can appear to have high resolution on the sample used to fit \(\pi_\gamma\), while the sufficiency relation that justified the ranking does not hold out of sample. The correct retrocausal audit is therefore not “which forecast scored best last week” but “which forecast has a verified sufficiency relation, a stable climatology, and a reliability term estimated over a window long enough to justify the stationarity assumption.” The paper’s own language supports this caution: it says reliability becomes a sensible property to ask for only “if a large archive of forecast–observation pairs is available” [PAGE 2].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three failure modes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. Crisis habit regression is the forecaster who, under uncertainty or evaluation pressure, collapses to the climatology \(\bar{\pi}\). This is a perfectly reliable forecast: \(\pi_\gamma=\bar{\pi}\), so the reliability term \(E[d(\gamma,\pi_\gamma)]\) is zero. But the resolution term is also zero, because \(E[d(\bar{\pi},\pi_\gamma)]=d(\bar{\pi},\bar{\pi})=0\). The expected score is exactly \(e(\bar{\pi})\). The paper makes this point plainly: the climatological frequency “will always be a reliable forecast” but is not very informative [PAGE 2]. In recursive terms, if the evaluator rewards reliability alone, the forecaster learns to regress to climatology, and the evaluator’s reliability metric becomes a habit-reinforcement device. The countermeasure is to audit the resolution term and the sufficiency relation, not just calibration.

Counter-trap is the opposite strategic response: the forecaster anticipates the evaluator’s score and tries to manufacture sharpness by making \(\gamma\) extreme or overconfident. If \(\pi_\gamma\) remains fixed while \(\gamma\) deviates from it, the reliability term \(E[d(\gamma,\pi_\gamma)]\) increases. The paper says that if \(\gamma\) changes “but so that \(\pi_\gamma\) remains constant,” then “any deviation of \(\gamma\) from \(\pi_\gamma\) has adverse effects on the score” [PAGE 7]. So the counter-trap is formally self-defeating under a strictly proper score when the conditional distribution is held fixed. If the forecaster instead changes \(\pi_\gamma\) by overfitting a small archive, the counter-trap can appear to work in-sample: the estimated resolution rises while the reliability term is underestimated. The out-of-sample audit must recompute \(\pi_\gamma\), \(\bar{\pi}\), and \(R\) on held-out data. The paper’s sufficiency result gives the correct partial order when the relation is real: if \(\gamma_1\) is sufficient for \(\gamma_2\), then \(R(\gamma_1)\ge R(\gamma_2)\) [PAGE 7]. If that relation fails out of sample, the claimed sharpness is not a stable information advantage.

Clock is the time-horizon failure. The paper explicitly excludes time-dependent problems and seasonal effects [PAGE 3, footnote 2], so the decomposition is a stationary-window statement. In a recursive setting, the forecaster models the evaluator’s archive length, and the evaluator models the forecaster’s adaptation rate. If the evaluator’s clock is longer than the forecaster’s regime, stale reliability estimates can reward a forecast that has already degraded. If the evaluator’s clock is shorter than the forecaster’s adaptation, sampling noise can reward a lucky deviation. The paper’s warning that unreliable forecasts cannot be ranked merely from sufficiency [PAGE 8] is the formal guardrail: a recursive evaluator must not infer a total order from a partial one. Strict propriety aligns incentives only when the forecaster’s reported probability is the forecaster’s actual belief and the score is strictly proper; the paper frames scoring rules as devices “to elicit probabilities from humans” [PAGE 1]. If the score is not strictly proper, or if the forecaster is optimizing the score rather than reporting belief, the recursive loop can stabilize on strategic distortion rather than truth. The correct counter-trap is not more confidence and not more climatology; it is a strictly proper score, a verified sufficiency relation, and a clock long enough to test stability.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Reliability is defined through \(P(Y=k|\gamma=p)=p_k\) [PAGE 4], and the paper’s empirical interpretation requires an archive collected under “more or less stationary conditions” [PAGE 2]. If the weather regime shifts, the conditional \(\pi_\gamma\) shifts, and the archive-based reliability term is no longer the quantity in Equation (13). The paper explicitly excludes seasonal effects [PAGE 3, footnote 2], so a seasonal or multi-week drift is a veto on the stationary decomposition, not a minor correction.

The strongest cross-layer proof is the climatology proof. Let \(\gamma=\bar{\pi}\) be the constant climatological forecast. If it is reliable, then \(\pi_\gamma=\bar{\pi}\). Therefore
\[
E[d(\bar{\pi},\pi_\gamma)]=d(\bar{\pi},\bar{\pi})=0,
\]
and
\[
E[d(\gamma,\pi_\gamma)]=d(\bar{\pi},\bar{\pi})=0.
\]
Equation (13) then gives
\[
E[S(\gamma,Y)]=e(\bar{\pi}).
\]
This proves that perfect reliability and zero reliability penalty can coexist with zero resolution. No amount of reliability auditing can turn climatology into an informative forecast. The only way to improve the score is to change \(\pi_\gamma\) so that it departs from \(\bar{\pi}\), which increases the resolution term. That is a physical-information veto: if the forecast carries no conditional information about \(Y\), the resolution term is zero regardless of how well calibrated it is.
