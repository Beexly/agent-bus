Hop 5: successful participants eventually become alumni, closing an intergenerational feedback loop in which today's subsidized athletes become tomorrow's fund contributors.

Gray-zone audit. First, the phrase "not run for profit" is normatively descriptive, not financially dispositive. If gate revenue exceeds direct staging costs, a surplus exists by accounting identity; the text simply denies that profit maximization is the objective. Second, the claim that contributions are made "without protest" [PAGE 175] assumes voluntary consent, yet the ticket price is bundled: a spectator cannot purchase admission to football while exempting the cross-subsidy to other sports. Third, "wisely used" [PAGE 175] is an unverifiable principal-agent assertion; the spectator principal has no audit mechanism over athletic-agent allocation across sports. Fourth, the treasury is described as chronically insufficient—"there is never enough" [PAGE 175]—which creates a permanent expansionist pressure on the schedule. The only explicit lever to grow the fund is to expand the spectacle (more contests, larger crowds), yet doing so risks converting football from an "incidental" spectacle into a "primary" commercial enterprise, eroding the amateur rationale for the subsidy. Fifth, the assumption of infinite absorptive capacity—"if we could double our funds we should still be able to find a useful and helpful application for every penny of them" [PAGE 175]—implies unbounded marginal utility of athletic expenditure, which breaks down under diminishing returns. The breakdown regime is reached when marginal cost of schedule expansion exceeds marginal revenue, producing a deficit singularity despite the text's confident assertion of unlimited absorptive capacity.

The schedule itself is the variable that resolves or aggravates this tension. Because the athletic treasury cannot cover all desirable sports, the football schedule becomes the residual claimant: additional dates are added not for competitive balance but to close the gap between aggregate sport costs and available receipts. This makes the schedule a financial instrument as much as a sporting calendar, and it exposes the enterprise to spectator-goodwill risk: if the public perceives the spectacle as primary, the "without protest" consent condition fails.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(G \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\) denote gate receipts per contest in 1920 USD, and let \(\mathcal{G}\) be the set of scheduled contests. Let \(\mathcal{S}\) be the set of sponsored sports, with annual cost \(C_s \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\) for sport \(s\). Let \(D \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\) denote non-gate donations. The athletic-fund balance is
\[
A = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} R_g + D - \sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} C_s ,
\]
where \(R_g\) is net revenue from contest \(g\). The source asserts chronic insufficiency: "there is never enough in the athletic treasury to finance as many sports as would be desirable" [PAGE 175], formalized as the inequality regime \(\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} C_s > \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} R_g + D\), i.e., \(A<0\) in the steady state.

Spectator cross-subsidy. Let \(p\) be the ticket price and \(\alpha \in [0,1]\) the fraction allocated to non-football sports. Per-attendee subsidy is \(\alpha p\). Spectator utility can be written as
\[
U = v(\text{game}) + \beta \sum_{s \neq \text{football}} \phi_s(C_s),
\]
where \(\phi_s\) maps dollars to participant welfare and \(\beta\) weights spectator concern for non-spectated sports. The text's claim that such sports benefit participants "almost or quite as much" [PAGE 175] implies \(\phi_s(C_s) > 0\) over the relevant domain.

Information-theoretic schedule operator. Let \(\mathcal{T}\) be the set of feasible dates. Schedule entropy is
\[
H(\mathcal{T}) = -\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} p(t) \log_2 p(t) \quad \text{bits}.
\]
Units: \(G, p, C_s, D, A\) in 1920 USD; attendance in persons; time in hours/days; \(H\) in bits; probabilities dimensionless in \([0,1]\).

Singularity and breakdown. Regime \(\alpha=0\): non-revenue sports lose the cross-subsidy and collapse. Regime \(G=0\) (zero attendance): fund insolvency. Regime \(\sum_s C_s \to \infty\) with fixed \(G, D\): deficit singularity where no finite schedule can close the gap. The text's unbounded absorptive-capacity claim fails when marginal cost of an additional contest exceeds marginal gate revenue.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. The 1920 passage is an early articulation of the revenue-sport subsidy model that defines modern intercollegiate athletics. The claim that football is "not run for profit" [PAGE 175] anticipates the NCAA's amateurism doctrine, while the pooling of football receipts into a "general athletic fund" [PAGE 175] is the direct ancestor of today's football/basketball revenue funding Olympic and non-revenue sports. The intergenerational loop—current athletes subsidized by spectators, later becoming alumni donors—forms the backbone of the collegiate athletic development pipeline.

Multi-week traps. In the seasonal schedule, early-season non-conference contests function as revenue generators to seed the treasury; late-season conference contests determine championships and alumni engagement. The trap is schedule creep: because the treasury is "never enough" [PAGE 175], athletic directors add dates to close deficits, which raises operating costs and player exposure, necessitating still more revenue. This recursive loop, visible in 1920, reappears in later eras as conference expansion, television-package chasing, and playoff enlargement. Each expansion solves the short-run treasury gap while aggravating the long-run structural deficit, producing a multi-week trap in which short-term fiscal relief locks in long-term commercialization pressure that contradicts the "incidental spectacle" framing.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. When the athletic treasury faces a shortfall, the institutional habit is to regress to the most legible revenue lever: add football games. Because football is the spectacle that draws paying spectators [PAGE 175], additional dates appear to promise proportional gate growth. This is a habit regression because it bypasses the harder governance problem of allocating the existing fund across sports.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is schedule restraint in defense of the amateur/subsidy framing. If the athletic department holds the line on contest count to preserve football as "incidental" [PAGE 175], it forgoes the marginal gate receipts needed to close the \(A<0\) gap, thereby deepening the deficit and forcing cuts to the very non-football sports the spectator subsidy was designed to protect. Restraint thus self-sabotages the communal justification.

Clock. The academic calendar imposes a hard temporal bound: contests must fit within the term and avoid examination periods, limiting the feasible schedule to a narrow autumn window. In 1920, November weather in the Northeast further contracts the playable dates. The clock is therefore a veto agent independent of finances: once the calendar and weather eliminate feasible Saturdays, no amount of treasury pressure can create additional revenue dates without violating physical or academic constraints.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. Early-20th-century football rosters were small, substitution was limited, and medical infrastructure was minimal. A feasible contest schedule must enforce a minimum inter-game recovery interval \(\Delta t \ge \tau_{\min}\), where \(\tau_{\min}\) is on the order of seven days given era-specific conditioning and travel by rail. Any schedule proposing \(\Delta t < \tau_{\min}\) is physically vetoed regardless of treasury need.

Biomechanical layer. Full-contact collisions accumulate microtrauma nonlinearly with exposure count. Injury hazard \(h(n)\) increases with the number of contests \(n\) such that \(\partial h / \partial n > 0\). A veto rule \(h(n) > h_{\max}\) removes dates from the feasible set.

Weather layer. Outdoor stadiums of 1920 lacked modern drainage and heating. Let \(\mathbb{P}(\text{unplayable} \mid t)\) be the probability of rain, snow, or frozen ground on date \(t\). If \(\mathbb{P}(\text{unplayable} \mid t) > \theta\) for an acceptable risk threshold \(\theta\), date \(t\) is vetoed.

Adjudication. These layers form a veto proof: financial models cannot override an unplayable field or an unfit athlete. The schedule must be the intersection of financially desirable dates and physically/biomechanically/weather-allowed dates. Because the source treats the schedule as the treasury's growth lever [PAGE 175], any veto that removes dates shrinks the feasible revenue set and pushes the fund closer to the deficit singularity defined above.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Double-header Saturday. Counterfactual: schedule two full football games on the same Saturday to double gate receipts in one date. Veto: violates \(\Delta t \ge \tau_{\min}\) and the biomechanical hazard rule; also contradicts the "spectacle incidentally" framing [PAGE 175]. Prediction: spectator fatigue reduces marginal utility below marginal cost; combined daily revenue falls short of double, and injury attrition degrades roster quality for the remainder of the season.

Scenario 2: Football-only fund concentration. Counterfactual: redirect all football surplus exclusively to football and eliminate non-revenue sports. Veto: contradicts the text's explicit justification that funds support sports spectators "do not care to watch" yet which benefit participants [PAGE 175]. Prediction: loss of alumni goodwill and athlete-development pipeline; long-term donor base contracts because the intergenerational feedback loop is broken.

Scenario 3: Examination-week contest. Counterfactual: schedule a rivalry game during final examinations to capture a high-attendance date. Veto: academic mission implicit in college athletics [PAGE 175] and the calendar clock constraint. Prediction: faculty and board intervention, possible athletic sanctions, reputational damage to the institution, and a decline in voluntary alumni contributions that outweighs one-game gate revenue.
