<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a repeated-measures pre/post study of 19 NCAA Division I American football players who completed drop jump (DJ) and repeated jump (RJ) testing before and after a 10-week offseason strength and conditioning program [PAGE 3]. The first multi-hop step is mechanical: vertical ground reaction force \(F_z(t)\) is sampled at 1 kHz, and the study derives velocity, power, position, jump height, and reactive strength index from that signal [PAGE 3]. The second step is statistical: dependent-samples t-tests are used for DJ metrics and jump 1 of the RJ, while time-by-jump repeated-measures ANOVAs are used for jumps 2–4 and for propulsive/performance metrics across all RJ jumps [PAGE 7]. The third step is inferential: the authors conclude that DJ and RJ performance increased after 10 weeks and that coaches may use these assessments to monitor program effectiveness [PAGE 12].

A gray-zone audit begins with body mass. The paper reports body mass increasing from \(102.73 \pm 8.79\) kg to \(104.35 \pm 9.33\) kg, with \(p=0.015\) and Hedge’s \(g=0.607\) [PAGE 8]. Because many metrics are mass-normalized, absolute force production is not directly comparable without care. The DJ mean propulsive force increased from \(21.17 \pm 1.59\) to \(22.54 \pm 1.57\) N/kg, \(p=0.001\), \(g=0.886\) [PAGE 8]. That is not a toy ratio; it is a force divided by body mass, and the gain is visible even against a mass increase. Still, the lack of a no-training control means the counterfactual “what would have happened without the program” is unobserved. The study design is explicitly repeated-measures, not randomized or controlled [PAGE 3].

A second gray zone is the RSI narrative. The abstract states that for all RJ jumps, peak propulsive power, mean propulsive force, jump height, and RSI increased with \(p \le 0.049\), \(\eta^2 \ge 0.134\) [PAGE 1]. Page 7 is more specific: RSI showed a time-by-jump interaction, with increases after training for jumps 3–4, while jump 1 RSI did not significantly change in the table (\(p=0.664\), \(g=0.101\)) [PAGE 7, PAGE 8]. Thus the broad abstract wording should not be read as “every jump’s RSI improved.” This is a classic gray-zone overgeneralization: a significant interaction and subgroup improvements are converted into a simpler headline.

A third gray zone is measurement and event identification. The DJ used a nominal 30 cm box, but “actual drop heights calculated by touchdown velocity ranged from 30.00 – 32.43 cm” [PAGE 3]. The braking-phase endpoint is based on a threshold equation whose typeset expression is not visible in the extracted text, though the variables are given: \(p_{\downarrow}\), \(m\), \(g\), and \(h\) [PAGE 3]. Manual identification of landing, toe-off, and contact points, plus 1 kHz sampling, introduces event-timing uncertainty. Within-session reliability was stronger for DJ metrics than RJ metrics: DJ ICCs \(\ge 0.757\) and CVs \(\le 12.23\%\), while RJ ICCs \(\ge 0.625\) and CVs \(\le 16.47\%\) [PAGE 7]. Those are acceptable in sport science but not negligible, especially for ratio metrics such as RSI whose denominator is contact time.

A fourth gray zone is program interpretation. The authors describe a “rigorous 10-week program,” but week 1 had only two training days, week 10 had only one day, and “Week 6 was considered a recovery week and included a drastic reduction in overall training load and volume” [PAGE 4]. Training emphasis also changed across weeks: weeks 1–5 emphasized speed and acceleration on Monday, while weeks 7–10 emphasized speed and acceleration on Monday and Thursday [PAGE 5]. A 10-week pre/post contrast therefore bundles training, recovery, testing familiarity, body-mass change, and seasonal factors. The data support “performance changed across the program,” not a clean isolated causal effect of one training ingredient.

A fifth gray zone is contact time. The discussion states contact times “ranged from 234-624 ms,” which the authors interpret as possibly greater effectiveness for slow stretch-shortening cycle performance rather than fast stretch-shortening cycle performance [PAGE 11]. That is plausible, but it also means the DJ was not behaving like a very fast reactive hop for many athletes. Any claim that the program improved fast reactive ability is therefore underdetermined.

Finally, there is a unit and label trap. The abstract reports “body mass=102.73±8.79 cm,” an obvious unit transposition for kilograms [PAGE 1]. Table 4’s heading says “before and after 8 weeks” even though the program and methods are 10 weeks [PAGE 8]. These do not invalidate the data, but they require the reader to distinguish typographical noise from substantive evidence.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The force-plate observable is the vertical ground reaction force \(F_z(t)\), sampled at \(f_s = 1\ \mathrm{kHz}\), so sample times are \(t_n = n/f_s\) with \(\Delta t = 1\ \mathrm{ms}\) [PAGE 3]. The source states: “During all jumps, the z-axis, vertical ground reaction forces were sampled at 1 kHz using The Motion-Monitor” [PAGE 3]. Let \(m\) be body mass in kilograms and \(g\) the acceleration due to gravity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The center-of-mass vertical acceleration is
\[
a_z(t)=\frac{F_z(t)-mg}{m}\quad [\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}],
\]
and the vertical velocity follows by integration:
\[
v_z(t)=v_z(t_0)+\frac{1}{m}\int_{t_0}^{t}\left(F_z(\tau)-mg\right)d\tau\quad [\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}].
\]
The paper’s own operational wording is: “velocity-time tracings were calculated by taking the integral of the force-time curve divided by mass” [PAGE 3]. That wording omits the body-weight subtraction explicitly, so the baseline convention must be inferred from the phase definitions; this is a breakdown risk if baseline force is mis-zeroed.

Instantaneous mechanical power is
\[
P_z(t)=F_z(t)\,v_z(t)\quad [\mathrm{W}],
\]
and normalized power is \(P_z(t)/m\) in \(\mathrm{W\,kg^{-1}}\). The tables define peak braking power and peak propulsive power as the peak of the product of force and velocity signals, expressed relative to body mass [PAGE 4]. Jump height by the impulse-momentum method is
\[
h_{\mathrm{imp}}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{TO}}^2}{2g}
=\frac{\left(J_{\mathrm{net,prop}}/m\right)^2}{2g}\quad [\mathrm{m}],
\]
where the source says jump height equals “the velocity at take-off (calculated as net propulsive impulse divided by body mass) squared, divided by the constant acceleration of gravity multiplied by 2” [PAGE 4]. Net propulsive impulse has units \(\mathrm{N\,s}\), and \(J_{\mathrm{net,prop}}/m\) has units \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Reactive strength index is a real dimensionless ratio:
\[
\mathrm{RSI}=\frac{t_{\mathrm{flight}}}{t_{\mathrm{contact}}}\quad [\mathrm{s\,s^{-1}},\ \text{dimensionless}],
\]
where for the DJ and RJ jumps 2–4 the denominator is “the duration from landing to take-off,” and for RJ jump 1 it is “the duration from initiation of unweighting to take-off” [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. This is not an invented ratio; it is the source’s defined operator. Its singularity is exact: as \(t_{\mathrm{contact}}\to 0^{+}\), \(\mathrm{RSI}\to\infty\). In practice, toe-off or landing misidentification can produce a near-zero or spuriously short denominator, so RSI is more fragile than jump height when contact time is small.

Braking-phase impulse normalized to body mass is
\[
\frac{J_{\mathrm{brake}}}{m}=\frac{1}{m}\int_{t_{\mathrm{land}}}^{t_{\mathrm{bottom}}}F_z(t)\,dt
\quad [\mathrm{N\,s\,kg^{-1}}],
\]
and mean braking force normalized to body mass is
\[
\frac{\bar F_{\mathrm{brake}}}{m}
=\frac{1}{m(t_{\mathrm{bottom}}-t_{\mathrm{land}})}
\int_{t_{\mathrm{land}}}^{t_{\mathrm{bottom}}}F_z(t)\,dt
\quad [\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}].
\]
Braking rate of force development is
\[
\mathrm{RFD}_{\mathrm{brake}}
=\frac{\Delta F}{\Delta t}\quad [\mathrm{N\,s^{-1}}],
\]
normalized in the tables to \(\mathrm{N\,s^{-1}\,kg^{-1}}\) [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. Braking stiffness is defined as absolute peak braking force divided by countermovement depth:
\[
k_{\mathrm{brake}}=\frac{F_{\mathrm{brake,peak}}}{d_{\mathrm{cm}}}\quad [\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}],
\]
with exact singularity as \(d_{\mathrm{cm}}\to 0^{+}\). The source defines countermovement depth as the “nadir of the position signal” [PAGE 4]. Position is obtained by integrating velocity, so any velocity baseline error propagates into depth and stiffness.

The DJ braking-phase endpoint is thresholded. The source says: “The following equation was used to calculate the end of the braking phase for each attempt (Bobbert et al., 1987)” and then identifies \(p_{\downarrow}\) as “the vertical force at the end of the braking phase,” \(m\) as body mass, \(g\) as gravity, and \(h\) as drop height [PAGE 3]. The extracted text does not render the typeset equation. The formal operational statement that is safely supported is
\[
t_{\mathrm{brake,end}}
=\min\left\{t>t_{\mathrm{impact}}:\ F_z(t)=p_{\downarrow}(m,g,h)
\ \text{after the initial peak braking impact force}\right\},
\]
with \(p_{\downarrow}\) in newtons. Because the exact function \(p_{\downarrow}(m,g,h)\) is not visible in the extraction, inventing a closed form would violate source fidelity.

Statistical operators: dependent-samples t-tests compare pre versus post for DJ metrics and RJ jump 1; time-by-jump repeated-measures ANOVAs compare RJ braking metrics for jumps 2–4 and propulsive/performance metrics for jumps 1–4, with Bonferroni corrections [PAGE 7]. The alpha level is \(p \le 0.05\) [PAGE 7]. Effect sizes are partial \(\eta^2\), with \(\eta^2 \ge 0.14\) considered large, \(0.06 \le \eta^2 < 0.14\) moderate, \(0.01 \le \eta^2 < 0.06\) small, and \(\eta^2 < 0.01\) negligible, plus Hedge’s \(g\) categories [PAGE 7]. These are dimensionless effect-size quantities; p-values are probabilities in the interval \([0,1]\).

Breakdown regimes and assumptions:
1. Force baseline drift or incorrect body-weight subtraction changes \(v_z(t)\), power, jump height, and impulse.
2. Manual identification of landing, bottom, and toe-off changes phase durations; at 1 kHz, timing quantization is 1 ms, but human event calling can be larger [PAGE 3].
3. Drop height is not exactly nominal: “actual drop heights calculated by touchdown velocity ranged from 30.00 – 32.43 cm” [PAGE 3]. A 2.43 cm range alters impact velocity and the braking threshold.
4. RSI is singular as contact time approaches zero; braking stiffness is singular as countermovement depth approaches zero; mass-normalized force and power are singular as \(m\to 0^{+}\), although physiologically irrelevant.
5. Statistical assumptions include normality, independence, and sphericity for repeated-measures ANOVA. The paper reports Shapiro-Wilk normality \(p \ge 0.055\), but sphericity and multiple-comparison behavior are not fully detailed [PAGE 7].
6. Missing post-test data for three subjects from 22 recruited to 19 analyzed is a potential selection issue [PAGE 3].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from classical drop-jump mechanics to modern force-plate monitoring. The paper cites Bobbert, Huijing, and van Ingen Schenau (1987) for the drop-jump braking-phase threshold [PAGE 3, reference 2]. That lineage treats the lower limb as a spring-mass system: the discussion says “thinking of the muscle as a spring,” where landing and countermovement compress the spring and the propulsive phase releases stored elastic energy [PAGE 11]. The RSI operator is another historical inheritance: flight time divided by contact time, intended as a practical index of reactive strength [PAGE 4]. The present study extends that lineage from single CMJ and DJ assessments to repeated jumps, with the explicit rationale that American football plays require repeated maximal efforts and that most collegiate plays last roughly 4.86–5.60 s [PAGE 2].

Retrocausal genealogy matters because current results can be back-projected onto older theory too easily. The finding that DJ jump height and propulsive force increased is consistent with the spring-model story, but the study is not a direct test of elastic-energy storage. The authors themselves use cautious language: improvements in DJ performance “can be hypothesized” to reflect sport-specific improvement [PAGE 10], and DJ performance “may act as a surrogate indicator” of on-field improvement [PAGE 11]. The lineage is therefore plausible but not deterministic.

Multi-week traps are built into the 10-week window. Week 1 was introductory with two training days, week 10 was a final testing/recovery week with one training day, and week 6 was a recovery week with “a drastic reduction in overall training load and volume” [PAGE 4]. The training emphasis shifted between weeks 1–5 and weeks 7–10 [PAGE 5]. A pre/post design over this window cannot separate the effects of resistance training, speed work, recovery, testing practice, body-mass gain, and seasonal maturation. The body-mass increase itself is a known confounder in vertical-jump monitoring, and the paper acknowledges body mass as “a known confounding factor” [PAGE 11].

Another genealogical trap is assessment selection. The DJ attempt with the highest jump height was used for comparisons, and the RJ attempt with the highest average jump height across all four jumps was used [PAGE 4]. This is defensible for best-performance testing, but it means the reported changes describe best observed performances rather than average session behavior. The low RJ reliability compared with DJ reliability, ICCs \(\ge 0.625\) versus \(\ge 0.757\) [PAGE 7], makes the repeated-jump lineage more sensitive to trial selection and day-to-day variability.

</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit-regression model asks what an athlete does when fatigued or under evaluation pressure. In a repeated-jump task, a regressed habit is likely to be a slower, more protective stretch-shortening cycle: the athlete self-selects a deeper countermovement, prolongs contact, and sacrifices reactive quality for jump height. The paper’s contact times of 234–624 ms support the interpretation that many trials were not fast reactive hops, and the authors suggest the program may have improved “slow stretch-shortening cycle performance” rather than fast performance [PAGE 11]. The RSI pattern reinforces caution: before training, RSI for RJ jumps 2–4 was greater than for jump 1 with very large effects, and after training RSI for jumps 2–4 remained greater than jump 1 [PAGE 7]. Jump 1 is a different motor problem because it begins with unweighting rather than a drop or preceding jump, so a coach should not treat jump 1 as a direct habit-regression index for the later jumps.

The counter-trap is the athlete-coach belief loop. If the athlete believes the assessment rewards maximum jump height, the rational strategy is to trade contact time for height; if the athlete believes it rewards RSI, the rational strategy is to minimize contact time. The study attempted to constrain the task with hands on hips, no knee flexion during flight, and valid force-plate contact [PAGE 3], but it could not eliminate strategic pacing. The coach, in turn, may overread a best-attempt jump-height gain as proof of improved on-field ability. The recursive theory of mind is therefore: coach expects monotonic improvement, athlete experiences fatigue and adjusts strategy, coach observes a mixed signal, and both may converge on a simplified narrative unless contact time and RSI are displayed alongside jump height.

The clock layer is the 10-week offseason itself. Testing occurred at the beginning of week 1 and the end of week 10, with week 6 as a recovery week and week 10 as a final testing/recovery week [PAGE 4]. The athlete’s internal clock includes cumulative fatigue and recovery, while the coach’s external clock is the program calendar. A performance gain at week 10 may reflect recovery from week 6 and reduced training in week 10, not only chronic adaptation. The safe interpretation is conditional: if the athlete is fresh at post-testing, the observed jump-height and propulsive-force gains are credible performance markers; if the athlete is still fatigued, the gains are conservative. The paper does not report readiness, sleep, soreness, or training-load markers, so this remains a theory-of-mind layer rather than a measured covariate.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the force-plate chain is only as good as calibration, zeroing, and event detection. The source reports one AMTI AccuGait force plate and 1 kHz sampling [PAGE 3], but it does not report calibration residuals, drift checks, or automated versus manual event-detection agreement. Because velocity is an integral of force, a small baseline offset accumulates over a jump. The paper’s own reliability data—DJ ICCs \(\ge 0.757\), RJ ICCs \(\ge 0.625\), and RJ CVs up to \(16.47\%\) [PAGE 7]—are the physical-statistical veto: any single-athlete inference from one RJ trial is weak unless repeated trials are averaged or reliability is modeled.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the program is confounded by body-mass gain, and the task is constrained but not fully standardized. Body mass increased from \(102.73 \pm 8.79\) kg to \(104.35 \pm 9.33\) kg [PAGE 8], and the authors acknowledge body mass as a confounder [PAGE 11]. The DJ box was nominally 30 cm but actual drop heights were 30.00–32.43 cm [PAGE 3]. Hands-on-hips and no-knee-flex rules improve standardization, but self-selected countermovement depth in the RJ remains a movement-strategy degree of freedom [PAGE 3]. Therefore a claim that the program improved “explosiveness” in a pure, position-independent sense is vetoed; the supported claim is narrower: selected DJ and RJ force-plate metrics changed across the program.

Weather-layer veto: the source provides no meteorological variables, no surface condition, no footwear, and no indoor/outdoor temperature or humidity record. The testing is laboratory-based with a force plate, but the paper does not explicitly state that environmental conditions were controlled. Any claim that weather moderated the results, or that the results transfer unchanged to outdoor field conditions, is therefore inadmissible. The weather layer is absent, so the cross-layer adjudication must default to “not identified” rather than “no effect.”

Cross-layer synthesis: the physical, biomechanical, and weather vetoes do not erase the observed changes; they limit the causal claim. The strongest evidence is for DJ mean propulsive force, peak propulsive power, peak braking power, and jump height, and for RJ propulsive and performance metrics across several jumps [PAGE 8, PAGE 9, PAGE 10]. The weakest evidence is for a generalized RSI improvement across every RJ jump, given jump 1 RSI \(p=0.664\) and the interaction-specific wording on page 7 [PAGE 7, PAGE 8]. The paper’s conclusion that coaches “may benefit from including DJ and RJ assessments” is supportable as monitoring guidance, not as proof of on-field causal transfer [PAGE 12].

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means a reward or judgment is tied to a verifiable measurement rather than a narrative. Each scenario below has a falsifiable prediction and an explicit veto condition.

Scenario 1: Add a concurrent no-training or delayed-training control group over the same 10-week offseason window. Verifiable metric: pre/post change in DJ mean propulsive force, DJ jump height, and RJ jump 3 RSI. The present study observed DJ mean propulsive force rising from \(21.17 \pm 1.59\) to \(22.54 \pm 1.57\) N/kg [PAGE 8], and RJ jump 3 RSI rising from \(1.77 \pm 0.23\) to \(2.00 \pm 0.26\) [PAGE 9]. Prediction: a true training effect should produce a significantly larger pre/post gain in the trained group than in the control group, with the trained group gaining at least about \(1.0\ \mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\) in DJ mean propulsive force and at least about \(0.15\) in RJ jump 3 RSI. Veto condition: if the control group shows equal or greater gains on those same metrics, the causal claim that the 10-week program produced the changes is vetoed.

Scenario 2: Reanalyze with body-mass adjustment and contact-time stratification. Verifiable metric: ANCOVA or mixed model with body mass as a covariate, plus subgroup effects for contact times below and above the sample median. The paper reports contact times from 234 to 624 ms and suggests slow stretch-shortening cycle adaptation [PAGE 11]. Prediction: if the program improved relative force production, DJ mean propulsive force and jump height gains should survive body-mass adjustment; if the adaptation is predominantly slow stretch-shortening cycle, the largest RSI and propulsive-force gains should appear in the longer-contact subgroup. Veto condition: if the apparent gains vanish after body-mass adjustment, or if gains are concentrated in the shortest-contact subgroup contrary to the slow-cycle interpretation, then the mechanism claim is vetoed and only an unadjusted association remains.

Scenario 3: Blindly reprocess the raw force traces with automated event detection and compare against the manual phase identification. Verifiable metric: intraclass correlation and Bland-Altman limits of agreement between manual and automated estimates of jump height, contact time, and RSI. The source reports manual identification of landing, toe-off, and contact points [PAGE 3], and the RJ reliability is weaker than the DJ reliability [PAGE 7]. Prediction: if the reported effects are robust, automated and manual estimates should agree strongly for jump height and the pre/post effect for DJ mean propulsive force should remain significant; RSI may show wider agreement because it is a ratio with contact time in the denominator. Veto condition: if the automated pipeline reverses the significance of the DJ propulsive-force gain, or if RSI classification flips for jumps 3–4, then the original event-detection-dependent result is vetoed as method-sensitive.

</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
