The source is a five-season observational study of 1,900 Spanish professional football matches, with events counted by pitch zone and then reduced by principal components analysis into four factors before a mixed linear model tests game location and final outcome. The first multi-hop step is the zone operator: each event is mapped to a pitch region, so a “goal” in Zone 5.1 and a “goal” in Zone 5.2 are not the same tactical object. The paper states: “Factor analysis with principal components was applied to the game-related statistics recorded from the games, which limited the analysis to four factors (Factor 1: Turnovers in zone 5.2 and Crosses in zone 4; Factor 2: Goals and shots in zone 5.1, Turnovers in zone 4, and Ball recover in zone 2; Factor 3: Goals and shots in zone 5.2; and Factor 4: Turnovers in zone 5.1).” [PAGE 2]. The zone definitions are also explicit: “Zone 2 was between the defensive semi-circle area and midfield circle, Zone 4 was between the midfield circle and offensive semi-circle area, Zone 5.1 was the offensive goal area, and Zone 5.2 was the offensive small area).” [PAGE 2]. Thus the causal chain the authors infer is not directly estimated as a path model; it is reconstructed from factor loadings: recover in Zone 2, progress through Zone 4, penetrate to Zones 5.2 and 5.1, shoot, score. The paper later summarizes this as “winning teams exhibit different and consistent profiles from drawing and losing teams, mainly discriminated by their ability to recover the ball in Zone 2 and to organize the offence through long passing sequences (penetrative passes) to Zones 5.2 and 5.1 to increase the number of shots and consequently goals.” [PAGE 6]. That is a plausible tactical grammar, but it is an interpretation of covariance, not a demonstrated mediation.

The second hop is the measurement model. The paper reports that “The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was adequate (0.65)” [PAGE 4], that “Analysis of the commonalities revealed values below an acceptable level of 0.5” [PAGE 4], and that “The principal components model obtained accounted for 22.3% of the total variance.” [PAGE 4]. It also admits “difficulties in creating factors with a substantial amount of explained variance was evident” [PAGE 5]. This is the gray zone: the factors are statistically usable but weak. Factor variances are only 4.1–8.2% each [PAGE 5], so a factor score is a noisy composite. The loadings are sometimes strong—Goals 5.1 at 0.89 and Shots 5.1 at 0.88 in Factor 2; Goals 5.2 at 0.79 and Shots 5.2 at 0.73 in Factor 3; Turnovers 5.1 at 0.71 in Factor 4; Turnovers 5.2 at 0.70 and Crosses 4 at 0.63 in Factor 1 [PAGE 4]—but the total explanatory envelope is small. A mixed linear model can produce very small p-values with n=1,900 even when the standardized effect is trivial. Table II reports, for example, a game-location effect on Factor 1 with F=513.78 and ES=0.11 [PAGE 5], and a final-outcome effect on Factor 3 with ES=0.16 [PAGE 5]. Under the paper’s own Hopkins criteria, those are below the 0.20 “trivial” threshold [PAGE 4]. So the correct audit is: the main effects are statistically detectable, but practically tiny. The claim that “home and winning teams having better values” [PAGE 2] must be read as a small distributional shift, not a deterministic tactical law.

The third hop is the ambiguity of “better.” The authors themselves note that “home teams had higher values in all studied variables (Sasaki et al., 1999; Tucker et al., 2005), including those for which a higher frequency meant performing worse (e.g. turnovers).” [PAGE 5]. That is a serious interpretive problem. A factor such as Factor 1 mixes Turnovers in Zone 5.2 with Crosses in Zone 4; Factor 2 mixes Goals and Shots in Zone 5.1 with Turnovers in Zone 4 and Ball recover in Zone 2. A higher factor score is not uniformly “good.” If home teams are more assertive and therefore produce more actions of every kind, including turnovers, then the location main effect partly measures action volume and risk appetite, not superior execution. The same issue applies to final outcome: “winning teams do not lose the ball in Zone 5.2, and consequently have more shots and goals” [PAGE 6], but winning teams also “had more shots and goals and less turnovers in Zone 5.1 (goal area) and more turnovers in Zone 4 and ball recovering in Zone 2.” [PAGE 6]. The profile is coherent, but the direction of causality is not identified. Final outcome is post-treatment. The authors are describing the statistical signature of teams that won, not proving that those actions caused the win.

The abstract says “The interaction LocationOutcome was only significant for Factor 4 (Turnovers in zone 5.1).” [PAGE 2], and the results repeat: “The interaction between game location and final outcome was statistically significant only for Factor 4, which mainly refers to Turnovers in Zone 5.1.” [PAGE 5]. The location and outcome main effects are consistent, but the contingency structure is weak. The safe conclusion is a descriptive one: home teams show slightly higher action frequencies, and winning teams show slightly different zone-specific profiles; the evidence does not support a strong home×outcome tactical law.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(e\) index a match event, \(z(e)\in\{1,2,3,4,5.1,5.2,\ldots,5.5\}\) be the pitch zone, and \(m\) index a match. The count operator is
\[
N_{z,m}=\sum_{e\in E_m}\mathbf{1}\{z(e)=z\},
\]
with units \(\mathrm{actions}\cdot\mathrm{match}^{-1}\). The source’s event definitions include “Goals: scored when the ball completely crosses the goal line, when no rule has been broken, and when the referee awards a goal.” [PAGE 3], “Turnovers: actions that lead offensive players to lose ball possession.” [PAGE 3], “Ball recovers: when a defensive team steals the ball or intercepts a pass.” [PAGE 4], and “Crosses: when offensive players flight the ball from one side of the pitch to in front of goal.” [PAGE 4]. These are categorical count operators; they have no continuous SI unit beyond actions per match.

The PCA operator is applied to standardized counts. Let \(X_{m j}\) be the count for variable \(j\) in match \(m\), and
\[
Z_{m j}=\frac{X_{m j}-\bar X_j}{s_j},
\qquad
R=\frac{1}{n-1}Z^\top Z,
\qquad
R v_k=\lambda_k v_k,
\qquad
F_{m k}=\sum_j Z_{m j}v_{j k}.
\]
Here \(F_{m k}\) is a dimensionless factor score in standardized units, \(\lambda_k\) is an eigenvalue in the same dimensionless correlation metric, and \(v_{jk}\) is a loading. The source used “Four factors were extracted with eigenvalues above 1.5” [PAGE 4] and “a criterion of j0.60j for identifying substantial loadings on factors” [PAGE 4; OCR for \(|0.60|\)]. The model “accounted for 22.3% of the total variance” [PAGE 4], with factor variances of “4.18.2%” [PAGE 5; OCR for 4.1–8.2%]. Assumptions: approximately linear correlation structure, adequate sampling, and interpretable simple structure. Breakdown regimes: if the correlation matrix \(R\) is singular, some \(\lambda_k=0\); if communalities \(h_i^2<0.5\), the factor solution explains less than half of a variable’s variance; if eigenvalues are nearly equal, factor scores are rotationally indeterminate; if KMO falls below 0.50, the correlation matrix is not factorable. The source’s KMO of 0.65 [PAGE 4] is adequate but not high, and its commonalities below 0.5 [PAGE 4] place it near the weak-structure boundary.

The mixed linear model operator is
\[
F_{m}=\mu+\alpha_{L(m)}+\beta_{O(m)}+(\alpha\beta)_{L(m),O(m)}+u_{T(m)}+\varepsilon_m,
\]
where \(L(m)\in\{\text{home},\text{away}\}\), \(O(m)\in\{\text{win},\text{draw},\text{lose}\}\), \(u_{T(m)}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^2)\) is a random team or match effect, and \(\varepsilon_m\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)\). The response \(F_m\) is in dimensionless factor-score units; \(\mu,\alpha,\beta,(\alpha\beta)\) are also in factor-score units; \(\sigma_u^2,\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) are factor-score-squared units. The source states: “A mixed linear model was applied to identify the effects of game location and final outcome on the previously identified factors.” [PAGE 2]. Assumptions: independent errors conditional on random effects, homoscedasticity, linearity, and a full-rank design matrix. Singularity regimes: \(\operatorname{rank}(X)<p\) or \(\operatorname{rank}(Z)<q\); exact collinearity between location and outcome; zero-variance cells; or a random-effect covariance matrix with a zero eigenvalue. In those cases the mixed-model solution is not unique. The source’s design is not empty—Figure 2 gives home win/draw/lose counts of 880/485/535 and Figure 3 gives away counts of 535/485/880 [PAGE 5]—but the interaction remains low-information because its effect size is 0.01 [PAGE 5].

Effect size is
\[
\mathrm{ES}=\frac{\bar F_A-\bar F_B}{s_p},
\]
with \(s_p\) the pooled standard deviation in factor-score units, so ES is dimensionless. The source reports “a stronger effect size (ES 0.11) for Factor 1” [PAGE 5] and a final-outcome effect on Factor 3 that is the strongest of the outcome effects [PAGE 5]. Under its own criteria, 0.11 and 0.16 are below the 0.20 trivial threshold [PAGE 4]. Thus the formal model supports “detectable but small” effects, not large deterministic separation.

For cross-layer adjudication, suppose the true model is
\[
F=\beta_L L+\beta_O O+\beta_C C+\varepsilon,
\]
where \(C\) is a physical, biomechanical, or weather covariate. If \(C\) is omitted, the probability limit of the location coefficient is
\[
\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta_L^{(0)}
=
\beta_L+\beta_C\delta_L,
\]
where \(\delta_L\) is the coefficient of \(L\) in the auxiliary regression of \(C\) on \(L,O\). Units: \(\beta_C\) is factor-score per \(C\)-unit; \(\delta_L\) is \(C\)-unit per location contrast; their product is factor-score per location contrast. Veto rule: if \(|\hat\beta_L^{(0)}-\hat\beta_L^{(1)}|>0.5|\hat\beta_L^{(1)}|\), or if the adjusted confidence interval includes zero, the location attribution is vetoed. A parallel multi-group PCA test compares loading matrices across weather or surface strata; if \(\|\Lambda^{(1)}-\Lambda^{(2)}\|_F\) exceeds a pre-registered tolerance such as 0.10 in loading units, the factor construct is not measurement-invariant and the mixed-model comparison is not interpretable.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is the shift from descriptive notational analysis to situational-variable performance analysis. It cites the performance-analysis tradition—Carling, Williams, and Reilly; Greghaine, Godbout, and Bouthier—and the home-advantage literature—Pollard; Pollard and Gómez—while also drawing on match-status work by Lago, Lago and Martin, and Lago-Peñas and colleagues [PAGE 2, PAGE 5, PAGE 6]. Its retrocausal move is to read the past from the present: final outcome is known, and the analyst reconstructs the tactical profile that “must have” produced it. The paper says the aim was “to identify the independent and interactive effects of game location and final outcome on football game-related statistics according to the zone of the pitch in which they occurred.” [PAGE 2]. That is retrospective classification, not prospective causation.

The historical chain is important. Early home-advantage work established that home teams perform better in several technical frequencies; Sasaki and colleagues recorded more goal attempts, shots blocked, shots on target, shots wide, successful crosses, and goal kicks at home, and Tucker and colleagues observed more corners, crosses, dribbles, passes, and shots at home [PAGE 2–3]. Match-status work then showed that score-line changes possession duration: “Jones and colleagues (Jones, James, & Mellalieu, 2004) reported variations in ball possession durations according to score-line, with possessions being longest when teams were losing and shortest when winning.” [PAGE 3]. Zone-based work added spatial context: “Lago (2009b) found more playing time in the attacking zone of the pitch when playing at home. Moreover, when behind, ball possession was less in the defensive zone and more in the attacking zone, than it was when winning or drawing.” [PAGE 3]. This paper’s contribution is to combine location, outcome, and zone into one PCA-plus-mixed-model design. The retrocausal danger is that the winning profile is selected after the outcome is known; a coach who copies the profile over a multi-week block may be fitting noise, opponent quality, schedule, weather, or squad rotation rather than a causal tactic.

Multi-week traps are therefore substantial. First, five seasons pool tactical evolution, so a “winning profile” estimated across 2003–2008 may not be stable across later rule changes, squad turnover, or league style. Second, the paper reports that “performance in drawn games is always closer to performance in losing than it is to performance in winning games.” [PAGE 6]. That nonlinearity is compatible with threshold effects or score-state dynamics, but a multi-week training intervention that treats “winning” as a continuous target may over-train the middle of the distribution. Third, repeated weekly testing creates multiplicity: with many zone variables and many weeks, a club can find a “significant” home or outcome effect that is just regression to the mean. Fourth, zone definitions are not natural kinds; a small shift in the Zone 5.2 boundary can change the Goals/Shots factor and therefore the claimed winning signature. Fifth, the paper’s factors explain only 22.3% of total variance [PAGE 4], so a multi-week program that chases factor scores may optimize a noisy proxy rather than the game itself. The genealogical lesson is that the paper is best read as a descriptive epidemiology of elite football, not as a causal prescription for a training block.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading separates three regimes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. In crisis habit regression, a losing team experiences negative score state and increasing time pressure; its players regress to habitual, low-complexity actions. The paper’s cited match-status evidence is consistent: possessions are “longest when teams were losing and shortest when winning” [PAGE 3], and when behind, “ball possession was less in the defensive zone and more in the attacking zone, than it was when winning or drawing.” [PAGE 3]. The habit regression is not necessarily adaptive. It can produce more crosses into Zone 4 and more turnovers in Zone 5.2—the exact mixture in Factor 1—because the team is chasing the game with a predictable delivery rather than breaking the opponent’s block.

The counter-trap is the opponent’s recursive response. A winning team can invite pressure, compress Zone 4, and organize defensive pressure to recover in Zone 2: “winning teams had their defensive pressure organized to promote recovering the ball in Zone 2.” [PAGE 6]. The losing team expects the winning team to sit deeper; the winning team expects the losing team to overcommit; the losing team’s players may even anticipate being counter-trapped and still lack the time to solve it. This is recursive because each side is modeling the other’s model of the score and clock. The paper’s Factor 2 is exactly the mixed signature of that recursion: Goals and Shots in Zone 5.1, Turnovers in Zone 4, and Ball recover in Zone 2 [PAGE 2]. A team that recovers in Zone 2 can spring into Zone 4; a team that turns over in Zone 4 concedes the counter; a team that reaches Zone 5.1 gets the high-value shot.

The clock is the third regime. The paper uses final outcome, not time-varying score, so it cannot distinguish early-game structure from late-game desperation. That is a major limitation for a theory-of-mind account. As remaining time decreases, the entropy of strategic beliefs should fall—teams become more predictable—and the value of a risky penetrative pass rises relative to a safe side pass. In information terms, if \(p_t(s)\) is the belief that the opponent will choose strategy \(s\) at time \(t\), then
\[
H_t(S)=-\sum_s p_t(s)\log_2 p_t(s)
\]
is in bits. A crisis regime should show \(H_t\) collapsing toward a few habitual actions, while a counter-trap regime should show the winning team retaining higher \(H_t\) and switching between pressing and dropping. The paper cannot test this because it collapses all clock states into “win,” “draw,” or “lose.” Its conclusion that winning teams “do not lose the ball in Zone 5.2, and consequently have more shots and goals” [PAGE 6] is therefore a final-state description, not a clock-resolved mechanism. A recursive account would predict that the losing team’s habit regression is strongest in the final third of the match, that the winning team’s counter-trap is strongest when protecting a lead, and that the draw state is the most cognitively ambiguous—consistent with the paper’s finding that drawn-game performance sits closer to losing than to winning [PAGE 6].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The paper’s own explanation of home advantage opens the door to cross-layer vetoes: “When playing at home, teams had higher frequencies for all analysed variables, probably resulting from home advantage factors such as facility familiarity and/or crowd.” [PAGE 6]. It also says home teams have “increased knowledge regarding the environment (e.g., crowd, pitch area, pitch lightening) and may decide to play with more risky decisions, having the consequence of increasing the frequencies of the performed actions.” [PAGE 5]. These are environmental and psychological confounds, not pure tactical location effects. A physical/biomechanical/weather veto is therefore necessary before accepting any location or outcome interpretation.

The physical layer includes fatigue and metabolic power. If a team’s high-intensity running capacity declines, its ability to press in Zone 2 and recover the ball changes; that alters Factor 2 and the inferred “winning profile” without any change in tactical intention. The biomechanical layer includes kicking mechanics, traction, footwear-surface interaction, and pitch firmness. Crosses are flighted balls from wide areas; wet or soft ground changes plant-foot stability and delivery accuracy, which changes Crosses in Zone 4 and therefore Factor 1. The weather layer includes precipitation rate \(P\) in \(\mathrm{mm}\cdot\mathrm{h}^{-1}\), wind speed \(W\) in \(\mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), and temperature \(T\) in \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Wind and rain can reduce pass precision and shot accuracy, inflating turnovers and depressing shots independently of location or final outcome.

The formal veto is an omitted-variable and measurement-invariance test. Fit the nested models
\[
M_0:F\sim L+O,
\qquad
M_1:F\sim L+O+C,
\]
where \(C\) is a vector of physical, biomechanical, and weather covariates. If the addition of \(C\) reduces the location coefficient by more than 50%, or makes its confidence interval include zero, then the location effect is not adjudicated as tactical. In factor-score units, a weather coefficient of, say, \(0.05\) factor-score per \(\mathrm{mm}\cdot\mathrm{h}^{-1}\) with a home-away precipitation contrast of \(2\ \mathrm{mm}\cdot\mathrm{h}^{-1}\) produces a \(0.10\) factor-score location bias—comparable to the reported ES of 0.11 for Factor 1 [PAGE 5]. That is enough to veto the claim that the location effect is a clean tactical signal. Likewise, multi-group PCA should test whether the loading matrix is invariant across dry/wet or home/away strata; if the Factor 1 loadings for Turnovers 5.2 and Crosses 4 change materially, the construct is not stable. The source did not report these controls, so the cross-layer veto remains open rather than closed. The correct adjudication is conditional: accept the descriptive location and outcome associations, but do not accept a causal tactical interpretation until physical, biomechanical, and weather covariates are shown not to absorb the effect.
