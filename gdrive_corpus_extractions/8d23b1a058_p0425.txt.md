\]

with \(\lambda\) in \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) (decay rate), \(\eta\) in \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) (exercise recharge rate), and \(t\) in seconds (SI). For constant exercise, the solution is

\[
h(t) = h_{0} e^{-\lambda t} + \frac{\eta}{\lambda}\bigl(1 - e^{-\lambda t}\bigr).
\]

If the instinct is never exercised (\(\eta = 0\)), then \(h(t) = h_{0} e^{-\lambda t} \to 0\) as \(t \to \infty\), which is the formal counterpart of James’s statement that the hunting instinct “may even entirely die out” [PAGE 415]. Breakdown regime: \(\lambda > 0\) and \(\eta = 0\) for an extended interval produces asymptotic extinction; if \(\lambda \to 0\), the instinct is effectively permanent and does not die out.

Uncertainty reduction by collateral circumstances. James writes that the choice between fear and ferocity is decided by collateral circumstances [PAGE 415]. Formally, the information gained is

\[
\Delta\mathcal{I} = H[A \mid o] - H[A \mid o, c] \ge 0 \quad \text{bits},
\]

where \(H\) is Shannon entropy. When \(\Delta\mathcal{I} \approx 0\), the reaction remains uncertain, which James notes is found in higher brutes as well as men [PAGE 415] and is not evidence of diminished instinctiveness.

Motor-expression gating. Because anger may be inhibited “from expressing itself in blows” [PAGE 415], the motor output \(M\) is a gated function:

\[
M = \Theta\!\Bigl( \alpha\mathcal{R} - \beta\mathcal{F} - \gamma h_{\mathrm{social}} - \delta \Bigr),
\]

where \(\Theta\) is a Heaviside or sigmoid gate, \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\) are dimensionless weights and threshold, and \(h_{\mathrm{social}}\) is social-inhibition habit. Breakdown regimes: (i) \(\alpha\mathcal{R} \approx \beta\mathcal{F} + \dots\) produces indecision; (ii) \(\gamma h_{\mathrm{social}}\) dominates and \(M=0\) despite high \(\mathcal{R}\), corresponding to inhibited rage; (iii) \(\mathcal{F}\) alone drives \(M\) toward flight or freeze.

Units and assumptions. All activations \(\mathcal{R},\mathcal{F},h\) are dimensionless probabilities in \([0,1]\). Time \(t\) is in seconds (SI). Rates \(\lambda,\eta\) are in \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). Information \(\Delta\mathcal{I}\) is in bits (Shannon). Probabilities are dimensionless. These are phenomenological latent-variable operators; no metabolic force, no toy ratio such as force-over-fatigue, and no betting quantities are introduced. Assumptions: James’s faculties are treated as latent dynamical variables; collateral circumstances are treated as context variables that change the posterior over actions; extinction is modeled as exponential decay, not as an empirically measured biological half-life from this text. The source page does not provide numerical values for \(\lambda\), \(\eta\), or \(\delta\); they must be estimated externally.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

James’s 1890 chapter sits at the intersection of Darwinian evolutionary psychology and the nascent experimental psychology of his own laboratory. The direct antecedent is Darwin’s 1872 *Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, which treats fear and rage as continuous across species; James inherits this cross-species assumption when he states that uncertainty in the fear-ferocity reaction is “found in the higher brutes as well as in men” [PAGE 415]. The theory then flows forward to McDougall’s 1908 *Introduction to Social Psychology*, which systematizes instincts as inherited dispositions, and to classical ethology (Lorenz and Tinbergen), where fixed action patterns are released by sign stimuli and can be modified by habituation. James’s claim that the hunting instinct is “restricted by habit to certain objects” [PAGE 415] is a proto-ethological releasing-mechanism claim: the object class “game” is learned, not fixed.

In affective neuroscience, the fear channel \(\mathcal{F}\) maps onto the amygdala-centric threat system described by LeDoux, while the ferocity channel \(\mathcal{R}\) maps onto hypothalamic-periaqueductal gray attack circuits described by Panksepp. The gating equation above is a crude phenomenological shadow of prefrontal top-down inhibition over subcortical defensive systems. The active-inference or predictive-processing tradition (Friston) formalizes the “collateral circumstances” as precision-weighted contextual priors that flip the posterior between fight and flight, exactly the role James assigns to collateral circumstances [PAGE 415].

Multi-week trap. If a civilized subject avoids genuine fear occasions for weeks, James’s logic implies the fear channel does not disappear; it seeks discharge on surrogate objects. Because “inanimate things, combinations of color and sound, bad bills of fare” can produce “real ebullitions of rage” [PAGE 415], a multi-week deprivation of proper fear occasions creates a displacement trap: trivial inanimate stimuli become rage triggers. The trap is recursive because the subject interprets the resulting irritation as evidence of an irascible temperament rather than as a fear-deprivation artifact. Over weeks, \(h_{\mathrm{fear}}\) for genuine threats decays toward zero while the sensitivity to color, sound, and domestic disorder rises, producing a pseudo-instinctive profile that James would likely misread as moral degeneration rather than ecological mismatch.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression. Under acute threat, top-down social inhibition \(\gamma h_{\mathrm{social}}\) in the motor gate is bypassed or overwhelmed. The agent regresses to the brute-like antagonism James describes: “we both fear, and wish to kill, anything that may kill us” [PAGE 415]. The recursive theory-of-mind content is first-order: I model the object as a killer; I simultaneously model my own fear and my own ferocity; the choice is not hard-wired but is decided by collateral circumstances. Because James locates this uncertainty in higher brutes as well as men [PAGE 415], the regression is not to a subhuman state but to a shared mammalian decision architecture.

Counter-trap. The modern belief “I am civilized, therefore I am less instinctive” is itself a trap. James explicitly blocks this inference: the uncertainty introduced by collateral circumstances “ought not to be taken as proof that we are less instinctive than they” [PAGE 415]. The counter-trap is second-order: an agent who believes she has transcended instinct may be more vulnerable to displacement rage because she fails to monitor the fear channel’s search for a proper occasion. She attributes her irritation to “bad bills of fare” rather than to fear deprivation [PAGE 415].

Clock. Three timescales must be held distinct. (1) Acute crisis: seconds to minutes, where \(\mathcal{R}\) and \(\mathcal{F}\) compete in real time and collateral circumstances flip the output. (2) Habit decay: weeks to months, governed by \(\lambda^{-1}\); if hunting is not exercised, \(h(t)\) approaches zero and the subject may spare a creature he could kill [PAGE 415]. (3) Civilizational fear deprivation: years to a lifetime, where the frequency of genuine fear occasions trends toward zero and many people pass “from the cradle to the grave without ever having had a pang of genu-[ine fear]” [PAGE 415]. The recursive theory-of-mind failure occurs when an agent mistakes the timescale of (2) or (3) for the timescale of (1), expecting a week-long habit change to resolve in seconds, or expecting a lifetime of civilized safety to prevent acute rage when a proper threat finally appears.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, environmental veto proof, and hierarchy.

Layer 1 — Physical. Fear has “bodily expressions of an extremely energetic kind” [PAGE 415]. If the subject’s physiology cannot mobilize the energetic expression (injury, disease, exhaustion), the fight action is physically vetoed regardless of \(\mathcal{R}\). Formal veto: \(V_{\mathrm{phys}} = 1\) if \(\text{physically capable}(a=\text{fight})\), else \(0\).

Layer 2 — Biomechanical / motor. Women’s anger is inhibited by fear “from expressing itself in blows” [PAGE 415]. The frequency of offense is high, but the extent of motor consequences is low. Formal veto: \(V_{\mathrm{bio}} = \mathbb{I}\{\alpha\mathcal{R} - \beta\mathcal{F} - \gamma h_{\mathrm{social}} > \delta\}\); if \(\mathcal{F}\) is sufficiently high relative to \(\mathcal{R}\), \(V_{\mathrm{bio}} = 0\) and no blow is struck. The extent, not the frequency, of pugnacity is constrained [PAGE 415].

Layer 3 — Weather / environment. The text mentions “combinations of color and sound” as rage triggers [PAGE 415], but it contains no meteorological variables (temperature, pressure, precipitation, wind). Therefore no weather veto can be adjudicated from this page; \(V_{\mathrm{weather}} = \text{null}\) unless external data are introduced. Absence of weather variables in the source is not evidence that weather is irrelevant in nature, but it is a hard bound on what this page can prove.

Hierarchy of vetoes. The complete action predicate is

\[
V = V_{\mathrm{phys}} \cdot V_{\mathrm{bio}} \cdot V_{\mathrm{habit}} \cdot V_{\mathrm{social}},
\]

where \(V_{\mathrm{habit}} = \mathbb{I}\{o \in \mathcal{G}\}\) (legitimate game only) [PAGE 415] and \(V_{\mathrm{social}} = \mathbb{I}\{\text{morally permitted}\}\), the latter importing the “moral monster” judgment [PAGE 415] as a social constraint rather than a physical law. A proposed kill is executed only if all layers pass; any single zero vetoes the motor expression. Breakdown: if \(V_{\mathrm{bio}} = 0\) because \(\mathcal{F}\) dominates, then \(\mathcal{R}\) may remain high while \(M = 0\), producing suppressed rage that can later displace onto inanimate objects [PAGE 415].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Habit-extinction veto. Counterfactual: a subject is physically capable of killing a wild creature but has not exercised the hunting instinct for \(T > 3\lambda^{-1}\). Veto condition: \(h(T) < \epsilon\), so \(V_{\mathrm{habit}} = 0\) for non-game objects and the creature is spared. Prediction: zero killing events despite repeated opportunity; measured as count per observation week. Verification: behavioral log over \(N\) weeks. Breakdown regime: if the subject is a “child of nature,” the normative “moral monster” judgment applies [PAGE 415], but this is a social veto, not a physical one, and does not change the behavioral count.

Scenario 2 — Collateral-circumstance tie veto. Counterfactual: \(\mathcal{R}(o) = \mathcal{F}(o)\) within a tolerance \(\delta\) for a given threat object \(o\). Veto condition: no deterministic fight-or-flight outcome; the result is decided by \(c\). Prediction: trial-to-trial switching probability near \(0.5\) across \(N\) repeated exposures, yielding \(H[A \mid o] \approx 1\) bit; outcome flips with minor collateral changes (presence of witnesses, time of day, escape route visibility). Verification: controlled stimulus-response experiment; entropy estimated in bits from action frequencies. Breakdown: if \(c\) is held constant across trials, variance collapses and the tie is broken by a fixed, unobserved parameter.

Scenario 3 — Civilized fear-deprivation veto. Counterfactual: a subject passes \(W\) weeks without any genuine fear occasion, so \(P(\text{genuine fear}) = 0\) over that interval. Veto condition: fear channel displacement to inanimate stimuli. Prediction: rage ebullitions toward color, sound, or domestic disorder increase with \(W\); measured as incidents per week. Verification: daily incident diary correlated with fear-exposure log. Breakdown: if the subject engages in controlled fear exposure (simulated threat, sport, or narrative fiction) during the interval, the displacement is vetoed and incident rate remains at baseline. James’s text predicts this displacement because “inanimate things … may … produce real ebullitions of rage” [PAGE 415] when proper occasions are absent.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
