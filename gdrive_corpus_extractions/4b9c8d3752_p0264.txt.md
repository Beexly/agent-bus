Historical and scientific lineage. Hull's 1943 Principles of Behavior synthesizes (i) Pavlovian conditioned-reflex physiology, (ii) Watsonian behaviorism's rejection of mentalism, (iii) Tolman's purposive behaviorism (which Hull stripped of cognitive intermediaries), and (iv) the quantitative learning tradition of Lashley and the Hamilton–Ainsworth curve-fitting school. The deductive system on [PAGE 255]—drive D derived from deprivation h, habit strength N growing as a positive function, reaction potential n as their product—is the core of Hullian drive-reduction theory. It directly spawned Spence's discrimination-learning theory, Logan's motivational work, Crespi's reversal effect, and the broader "Hull-Spence" dominance of mid-century American learning theory. Multi-week traps specific to this architecture:

1. Habit accrual lag. N(t; h) accumulates over sessions/weeks. A researcher testing after only a few days observes N far below A(h); applying the asymptotic multiplicative formula n = D(h)N overestimates reaction potential because G(t) ≠ 1. This is a multi-week trap: the model's asymptote is valid only after sufficient training trials under the same D′.

2. Drive-history mismatch. D′ is fixed at habit formation; D varies with current h. Over weeks of changing deprivation schedules, D − D′ = d′ drifts, producing apparent "spontaneous recovery" or "forgetting" that is actually drive-state mismatch, not memory decay. The model predicts performance collapses when |d′| grows, even if N is physiologically intact.

3. Generalization-gradient narrowing. With extended training, Γ(δ) sharpens (c increases). A stimulus that generalized early in training may fail to generalize after weeks, causing sHz → 0 and n → 0 despite unchanged D and N. This is a counterintuitive multi-week trap: more training can reduce cross-stimulus performance.

4. Saturation of D(h). If D(h) is concave or logarithmic, adding privation hours beyond a point yields diminishing drive returns; beyond a biological ceiling, h no longer increases n linearly. Testing animals at vastly different h without estimating φ(h)'s saturation produces systematic prediction error.
