The multi-hop derivation begins with the paper’s fairness axiom, not with a raw probability table. The source states that “The integrity of a sport can be seriously undermined if its rules punish winning as this creates incentives for strategic manipulation” [PAGE 1], and it identifies the relevant normative test as the Aristotelian principle that “the winning probabilities should be ordered according to the players’ ranking” [PAGE 2]. Therefore the audit object is not “are all teams equal?” but “does the qualification-probability ordering preserve the ex ante strength/ranking ordering?” The first hop is a mechanism mapping: UEFA assigns teams to Leagues A–D by coefficient rank [PAGE 4], then League D group winners are guaranteed a play-off path if they do not qualify directly, because “If no team qualifies through the qualifying group stage, then the group winners of the league form an own path” [PAGE 6]. The second hop is path formation: “This requires that no group winners face any team from a higher-ranked league” [PAGE 6], so a top League D team can avoid stronger leagues in the play-off semifinal structure. The third hop is the inversion: a bottom League C team has a low direct-qualification probability and a difficult route to a League C group-win guarantee, while a top League D team has a high probability of winning its weaker group and then entering a protected path. Hence the source’s abstract claim: “being a top team in the lowest-ranked League D of the 2018/19 UEFA Nations League substantially increases the probability of qualifying compared to being a bottom team in the higher-ranked League C” [PAGE 1]. The fourth hop is the policy counterfactual: changing only path formation, not team selection, can reduce or eliminate the inversion; the source says “The unfairness can be remarkably reduced or even eliminated with slightly changing the path formation policy of the UEFA Euro 2020 qualifying play-offs” [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit is therefore structural, not merely empirical: the paper’s simulation uses “Match outcomes are modeled such that the probability with which a given team defeats its opponent is fixed a priori” [PAGE 7], prohibits draws [PAGE 7], ignores some draw restrictions, and avoids a rules contradiction [PAGE 7]. These are simplifications, but they do not erase the protected-path mechanism. The source also reports “one million iterations have been considered to obtain exact expected values” [PAGE 9], so the numerical claims are Monte Carlo estimates with small simulation error. The key audit warning is that the paper does not claim all lower-ranked teams are advantaged; the inversion is local, especially at the C/D boundary, and the remedy is a path-formation policy change. The “tanking” gray zone is real: the source notes preliminary work showing “losing could improve the chances of participating in the 2020 UEFA European Championship” [PAGE 3], and it discusses historical coefficient manipulation including a case where “Cyprus would have been only the 40th after kicking 1740/20 = 87 own goals against Belgium” [PAGE 17]. \]
where \(W_e\) is dimensionless probability and \(d,s\) are in Elo rating points [PAGE 7]. The match rule is: draw \(r\sim U[0,1]\); if \(r<w_i\), team \(i\) wins, otherwise team \(j\) wins, with \(w_i+w_j=1\). The source explicitly states: “Draws (ties) in the matches are prohibited as further arbitrary assumptions would be necessary to obtain the probability of that outcome from formula (1)” [PAGE 7]. Assumptions: fixed team strengths, fixed home advantage, independent match outcomes, no draw state, random tie-breaking in group/league rankings, and no strategic response inside a single simulation. Singularity and breakdown regimes: if \(s=0\) and \(d=0\), the exponent \(d/s\) is undefined; for \(s\to0^+\) and \(d>0\), \(W_e\to1\), while for \(d<0\), \(W_e\to0\); for \(s\to\infty\), \(W_e\to1/2\) for fixed \(d\); for \(d\to\pm\infty\), \(W_e\) saturates at \(1\) or \(0\). The source itself notes the scaling choice is consequential: with \(s=400\), “the best team, Germany defeats the 10th (Croatia) with a probability of 81.1% at a neutral field, and wins against the 30th (Bulgaria) with a probability of 93.4%” [PAGE 11]. Thus the model is a monotone probability operator, not a physical-force model.

The qualification metric is
\[
P_q(i)=P_i(\text{direct qualification})+P_i(\text{qualification through play-offs}),
\]
with both terms in probability units on \([0,1]\). The source decomposes the probability exactly this way in Figure 2 [PAGE 10] and reports boundary cases: “Being in a lower-ranked league decreases the probability of qualification by about 2.3% for the Netherlands and by about 8.9% for Turkey, but the same metric increases by more than seven-fold for Lithuania” [PAGE 11]. Define the fairness inversion metric for a lower-ranked team \(j\) and higher-ranked team \(i\):
\[
\Delta_{i,j}=P_q(j)-P_q(i),\qquad R_{i,j}=\frac{P_q(j)}{P_q(i)}.
\]
\(\Delta_{i,j}\) is in probability units; \(R_{i,j}\) is dimensionless. Fairness requires \(\Delta_{i,j}\le0\) when \(i\) is ranked above \(j\). \(R_{i,j}\) has a denominator singularity when \(P_q(i)=0\); the source’s reported factors avoid that degenerate case. The Monte Carlo precision for \(N=10^6\) iterations is
\[
\mathrm{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}},
\]
in probability units, with maximum \(0.0005\) at \(p=0.5\); the Bernoulli information is \(H_2(p)=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)\) bits. The source’s “one million iterations” statement [PAGE 9] supports the reported probabilities to roughly \(10^{-3}\) or better for moderate \(p\). The regular operator preserves league separation for League D; random partitions the 16 teams randomly; seeded uses the traditional pot regime [PAGE 12]. The operator changes only the play-off component of \(P_q\), not the direct-qualification component [PAGE 12]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive game has three levels. At team level, a team reasons: “If I win now, my coefficient may rise, my league may improve, my group may become harder, and my playoff protection may disappear; if I lose or draw strategically, I may fall into a weaker league with a protected path.” The source’s crisis-habit regression is exactly this: under a misaligned clock, a team may regress to historical losing habits because “losing could improve the chances of participating in the 2020 UEFA European Championship” [PAGE 3]. At administrator level, UEFA reasons: “If teams anticipate the trap, they may tank, so we need a counter-trap.” The source’s counter-trap is seeded path formation: it preserves the Nations League’s value because “all contestants remain interested in performing well in the UEFA Nations League” [PAGE 14]. At researcher level, the simulation reasons about both: it holds team strengths fixed, samples match outcomes, and compares regular, random, and seeded path formation [PAGE 12]. The clock is the decisive variable. The action occurs in a match window; the consequence appears years later in the playoff path. The source’s “years before” phrase [PAGE 1] and the 2016–2017 coefficient examples [PAGE 17] show that the feedback delay is long enough for strategic habit formation and for public trust damage. The counter-trap is not “make every league equally likely”; it is “remove the protected-path inversion while keeping group-winner incentives.” The source reports that seeded path formation reduces the C/D boundary factor from \(7.14\) under regular formation to \(1.09\) [PAGE 14], which is the quantitative signature of a successful counter-trap. The remaining recursive risk is that teams may still target coefficient thresholds, but the source argues that blatant tanking “can be easily detected and probably sanctioned by the UEFA” [PAGE 17].

Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: no physical/biomechanical/weather veto is admissible unless it is operationalized as a measured change in \(w_i\) large enough to flip \(\Delta_{39,40}\), and the source’s sensitivity analysis shows the inversion is robust to plausible changes in competitive balance.
