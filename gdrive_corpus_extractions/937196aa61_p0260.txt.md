The core problem James identifies is why identical objective conditions—lines of equal length—yield opposite perceptual distortions depending on the sensory modality (vision vs. touch).

First, we analyze Helmholtz’s "law of contrast." He argues that lines cutting another line make it seem more inclined. This is framed as a sensationalistic law—a native function of the seeing-apparatus, akin to color after-images, rather than an association of ideas derived from past experiences. We can model this using information theory. If we define a perceptual magnitude $M$ as a function of a baseline stimulus $S_0$ and contextual noise or intersecting stimuli $\Delta S$, Helmholtz implies an amplification factor for distinct contrasts: $M = k \cdot S_0 \cdot (1 + \alpha |\Delta S|)$, where $\alpha$ represents the sensitivity to local gradients. This suggests a logarithmic scaling of perceived angles based on the density of intersections, similar to how Weber-Fechner laws apply to intensity.

However, Wundt’s critique exposes a singularity in this logic. If contrast increases the perceived magnitude of the central space, then by the same token, the spaces *between* dividing lines should appear smaller. Yet, the Müller-Lyer illusion (implied by "subdivided angles") shows the opposite: the subdivided space often appears expanded or contracted depending on arrowheads, while empty skin feels longer when punctured. We introduce a second operator, a fatigue-based attenuation for continuous tracking, such as moving a finger along punctures. If movement requires motor effort, the perceived distance might be inversely proportional to the cumulative muscular exertion $E$. Thus, $D_{perceived} \propto 1/E$. Since punctures provide frequent rests or clear markers, they reduce the continuous tracking effort, making the path feel shorter compared to a smooth, uninterrupted glide over an empty half.

The critical gray-zone emerges at the intersection of these models. Vision uses static fixation, whereas touch relies on active exploration. James notes that fixing the eyes dispels the illusion, implying that micro-saccades (involuntary eye movements) are necessary to generate the "muscular feelings" that cause the distortion. When the eyes are forced still, the dynamic feedback loop breaks. This leads us to a unifying hypothesis involving predictive coding. The brain predicts the sensory input required to traverse a space. For vision, intersecting lines create high-frequency spatial errors between the predicted trajectory and actual retinal image, inflating perceived size. For touch, punctures act as discrete error-correction points. A continuous path without markers generates accumulating prediction error, elongating the subjective experience of time and distance. Therefore, the "unanalyzable laws" James mentions are actually differential equations governing the minimization of free energy across different effector organs—the extraocular muscles versus the proprioceptive system of the finger.

Finally, checking for boundary breakdowns: Helmholtz’s contrast law fails if $\Delta S$ becomes uniform, leading to adaptation. The tactile puncture experiment fails if the punctures become so dense they mimic a continuous ridge, reversing the effect. Thus, the resolution lies not in a single geometric law, but in a multi-modal divergence of the brain's priors regarding surface topology and motor cost.
