Frolov’s experiment, in which “salivary secretion accompanied every secondary reinforcement of the black square” [PAGE 91], represents an early demonstration that a neutral stimulus can acquire autonomic CR properties through pairing with food. Cowles’ ape experiments [PAGE 91] and Wolfe’s controls [PAGE 91, p. 16] bridge comparative psychology and sensory specificity: the brass token control demonstrates that secondary reinforcement is not driven by geometric shape alone but by food-correlated stimulus energy. Bugelski’s extinction observation [PAGE 88] provides the theoretical bridge to modern Rescorla–Wagner and temporal-difference models: a predictor loses control over behavior when the expected primary reinforcer is omitted. Grindley’s chicken runway [PAGE 91] anticipates later work on conditioned reinforcement and Pavlovian–instrumental transfer, where a stimulus previously paired with food can energize a new instrumental response even when food is temporarily unavailable.

Modern descendants treat secondary reinforcers as learned value predictors encoded in dopaminergic prediction-error signals. In that lineage, \(V_{\text{sec}}\) above corresponds to the expected discounted value of the token, and extinction corresponds to a negative prediction error when rice is omitted. The multi-week trap arises because secondary reinforcement acquired in days can persist for weeks only if periodically backed by primary reinforcement; without backup, it undergoes gradual extinction, spontaneous recovery after delays, renewal when returned to the original acquisition context, and reinstatement after re-exposure to the primary reinforcer. Grindley’s design, if run only within a single session as the excerpt suggests, is blind to these multi-week dynamics: a chicken may show a runway-time effect on day 1 that vanishes by day 14 if rice is never delivered again, or may show renewal if tested in a different room. The text’s admission that “further evidence will be required” [PAGE 91] is therefore a recognition that single-session runway indices cannot establish the temporal stability or necessity of the CR component across the lifespan of the association.

Wolfe’s brass-token control shows the CR is specific to food-associated tokens, but specificity does not equal necessity. The counter-trap is to assume that because the CR co-occurs with secondary reinforcement, it must cause it. Hull’s own position [PAGE 91] is agnostic: further evidence is required. The counter-trap therefore demands dissociation: extinguish the CR while preserving \(S_{\text{sec}}\), then test whether \(S_{\text{sec}}\) still reinforces a new response.

The contiguity kernel \(K(\Delta t)\) requires that token and rice be close in time; if the intertrial interval is too short, extinction trials blend into acquisition trials and \(CR\) fails to decay. If the interval is too long, the animal may forget the association entirely. Spaced extinction across days produces different CR decay curves than massed extinction within one hour. The runway index \(I_G\) also depends on the clock: traversal time measured in seconds is only interpretable relative to the chicken’s baseline locomotor speed and the duration of food deprivation. A “crisis” on a fast clock (massed trials, minutes apart) looks different from the same crisis on a slow clock (spaced trials, hours apart), and the recursive theory of mind must model how the animal revises its expectation of rice availability as the clock ticks.
