Multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit. The source begins from a physical measurement layer: NFL Next Gen Stats uses RFID chips in the football and shoulder pads to produce “spatial location and trajectory information for all 22 players on the field during any given play at a rate of 10 Hz (i.e., 10 frames per second)” [PAGE 3]. At that cadence, each frame interval is nominally 0.1 s. The first inferential hop is geometric: for player coordinates \((x_t,y_t)\), the bearing is \(b_t=\operatorname{atan2}(y_{t+1}-y_t,x_{t+1}-x_t)\), and the turn angle is the change in bearing, \(\phi_t=b_t-b_{t-1}\) [PAGE 8]. The second hop is probabilistic: \(\phi_{ijt}\) is treated as a circular outcome from a von Mises distribution with player-frame mean \(\mu_{ijt}\) and concentration \(\kappa_{ijt}\) [PAGE 9]. The third hop is structural: both the mean and concentration are linked to covariates, and the concentration gets a ball-carrier random intercept \(u_j\) with position-specific variance \(\sigma^2_{p[j]}\) [PAGE 9]. The fourth hop is inferential: the posterior distribution of \(u_j\) is used to rank players by turn-angle variability, where lower \(u_j\) means lower \(\kappa\), hence more variable turning [PAGE 13-14]. The fifth hop is interpretive: the paper hypothesizes that “higher variability in turn angle is beneficial from a performance evaluation point of view, as this may be indicative of the ability to perform shifty movement with more sudden, unpredictable turns” [PAGE 3]. Each hop has a failure mode: RFID noise, 10 Hz discretization, zero-displacement frames, the \(\operatorname{atan2}\) branch cut, the tan-half link singularity at \(\mu=\pi\), the \(\kappa=0\) uniform boundary, multimodality of turn angles, autocorrelation within plays, hierarchical shrinkage, and small position-group samples. The paper itself reports uncertainty: the tight-end fixed effect has a 95% credible interval “[−0.021, 0.151]” that “includes zero” [PAGE 12], tight ends have “wider 95% credible intervals” [PAGE 15], and “a number of the posterior distributions in Figure 4 are not entirely above or below zero” [PAGE 15]. Thus the player rankings are posterior summaries, not deterministic athletic truths. The gray-zone audit must also flag omitted layers: the model includes speed, acceleration, cumulative distance, play type, position, and defender/teammate geometry [PAGE 6], but it does not include weather, field surface, biomechanical pose, game clock, score state, injury status, or explicit strategic defender anticipation. Therefore any claim that a player is “shiftier” because of a specific biomechanical or weather mechanism is outside the identified model. The correct reading is conditional: \(u_j\) is a player-specific shift in log turn-angle concentration after adjustment for the listed covariates, not a pure latent agility trait.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the frame-level turn angle as the change in bearing between successive displacement vectors. With \((x_t,y_t)\) observed at frame \(t\), the bearing is
\[
b_t=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(y_{t+1}-y_t,\;x_{t+1}-x_t\right)\in[-\pi,\pi],
\]
and the turn angle is
\[
\phi_t=b_t-b_{t-1}.
\]
The source states: “the turn angle \(\phi_t\) is the change in bearing between two successive time intervals \([t-1,t]\) and \([t,t+1]\)” and “\(\phi_t=b_t-b_{t-1}\)” [PAGE 8]. The angular units are radians; the frame interval is \(0.1\) s under the 10 Hz tracking rate [PAGE 3]. The model is
\[
\phi_{ijt}\sim \operatorname{vonMises}(\mu_{ijt},\kappa_{ijt}),
\]
\[
\tan\!\left(\frac{\mu_{ijt}}{2}\right)=\alpha_0+x_{ijt}^{\top}\beta,
\]
\[
\log \kappa_{ijt}=\gamma_0+z_{ijt}^{\top}\psi+u_j,
\]
\[
u_j\sim N\!\left(0,\sigma^2_{p[j]}\right),
\]
as given in the source [PAGE 9]. The von Mises density is
\[
p(\phi\mid \mu,\kappa)=\frac{1}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)}\exp\{\kappa\cos(\phi-\mu)\},
\quad \phi,\mu\in[-\pi,\pi),\quad \kappa\ge 0,
\]
with probability density units of \(\mathrm{rad}^{-1}\), \(\mu\) in radians, and \(\kappa\) dimensionless. The source explains: “\(\kappa\) denotes the concentration parameter of a von Mises distribution, illustrating a reciprocal measure of variability. In other words, a higher \(\kappa\) value corresponds to a more concentrated (i.e., less dispersed) turn angle distribution” [PAGE 9]. The log link enforces \(\kappa_{ijt}>0\). Covariates include “Speed (yards/second),” “Acceleration (yards/second2),” “Cumulative distance covered (yards),” play type, and position [PAGE 6]. Thus \(\psi_s\) has units of log-concentration per yard/second, \(\psi_a\) per yard/second\(^2\), and \(\psi_{\text{dis}}\) per yard; indicator coefficients are dimensionless shifts in log \(\kappa\). The source uses “vague half-\(t_3\) priors” for standard-deviation parameters [PAGE 10] and reports 4 chains, 3,500 iterations, 1,500 burn-in draws, yielding 8,000 posterior samples [PAGE 10].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. \(\operatorname{atan2}(0,0)\) is undefined when successive coordinates are identical; zero-displacement frames from stationary players, duplicate rows, or missing tracking produce undefined bearings.
2. The source writes \(\phi_t=b_t-b_{t-1}\) with \(b_t\in[-\pi,\pi]\); if the difference is not wrapped modulo \(2\pi\), reversals near the \(\pm\pi\) branch cut are misrepresented. Circular wrapping is \(\phi_t=[(b_t-b_{t-1}+\pi)\bmod 2\pi]-\pi\).
3. The tan-half link \(\tan(\mu/2)\) diverges as \(\mu\to\pi\pmod{2\pi}\); finite linear predictors cannot represent the boundary exactly.
4. \(\kappa\to0\) is the uniform circular distribution; \(\kappa\to\infty\) is a point mass. If turn angles are multimodal, a single \(\kappa\) is not a complete variability description.
5. \(\sigma_{p[j]}\to0\) creates non-identifiability between the fixed intercept and player random effects; small position groups amplify shrinkage and funnel geometry in MCMC.
6. The model assumes conditional independence across frames given covariates and random effects, but frame-level turn angles are temporally autocorrelated; the paper partially addresses persistence by including “the turn angle at the previous frame \(\phi_{ij,t-1}\)” in the mean model [PAGE 10].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit: the paper “draw[s] inspiration from the animal movement literature” and cites Hooten et al. (2017) for a complete overview [PAGE 7; PAGE 19]. That lineage runs from telemetry-based animal movement models, where turning angles and step lengths are circular/positive continuous outcomes, into sports tracking. The statistical lineage is circular statistics plus Bayesian mixed-effects modeling: von Mises likelihood, tan-half mean link, log concentration link, position-nested random intercepts, and half-\(t_3\) priors [PAGE 9-10]. The computational lineage is Stan/brms and no-U-turn sampling [PAGE 10]. The sports-analytics lineage includes NFL Big Data Bowl tracking data [PAGE 19], within-play expected points and route detection [PAGE 3], and prior STRAIN work on pass rush [PAGE 20]. The paper’s own contribution is to move change-of-direction evaluation from combine drills—three-cone and short shuttle—to in-game tracking, because the drills “fail to capture how players respond in live, real-time scenarios where movement is influenced by spatial constraints and different moving pieces on the field” [PAGE 2]. 

Multi-week traps: the empirical window is “the first nine weeks of the 2022 NFL regular season,” with “9,480 total plays across all 136 games” [PAGE 4]. A player’s random effect \(u_j\) is constant across those weeks; the model has no week random effect, no time-varying player effect, and no opponent-strength interaction. Therefore week-to-week changes in role, injury, fatigue, weather, surface, scheme, or opponent leverage are not identified. The paper notes “our limited sample of considered plays” [PAGE 15] and uses minimum play thresholds—running backs at least 25 plays, tight ends at least 10, wide receivers at least 15 [PAGE 14 caption]. Those thresholds make multi-week rankings sensitive to shrinkage and to which games are included. A retrocausal reading should therefore treat the rankings as within-window posterior summaries, not stable multi-season traits.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three competing stories can be posed, but only one is partially represented in the model. First, crisis habit regression: under fatigue or pressure, a ball carrier reverts to a dominant movement pattern, which would predict higher \(\kappa\) and lower turn-angle variability late in plays or late in games. The model has cumulative distance as a proxy, but the estimated coefficient is negative, \(\hat\psi_{\text{dis}}=-0.015\), 95% CI \([-0.016,-0.014]\), and the paper interprets this as players perhaps adjusting “their pace and direction more erratically in later moments of a play” [PAGE 11]. That is evidence against a simple habit-regression story, but cumulative distance is not a direct fatigue measure. Second, counter-trap: a ball carrier anticipates that a defender anticipates a cut, so the carrier uses more variable turns to disrupt prediction. The model includes closest-defender distance, angle, speed, and relative position [PAGE 6], but the reported fixed-effect table focuses on speed, acceleration, distance, play type, and position [PAGE 11-12]; there is no explicit strategic equilibrium or defender-belief model. Third, clock/game-state: the model does not list game clock, score differential, quarter, or time remaining in Table 2 [PAGE 6], so clock-dependent intent is not identified. The only “habit” term is the previous turn angle in the mean model, which “encodes the notion of directional persistence, capturing player tendency to make successive turns in a similar manner” [PAGE 10]. That is a low-level persistence term, not a theory of mind. Recursive theory-of-mind claims should therefore be vetoed unless new covariates or experiments are added.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer adjudication: the model does contain physical proxies—speed, acceleration, and cumulative distance—and the source estimates that “concentration in directional movement increases as speed increases” with \(\hat\psi_s=0.709\), 95% CI \([0.705,0.713]\), while acceleration is negative, \(\hat\psi_a=-0.094\), 95% CI \([-0.101,-0.087]\) [PAGE 11]. So the model can adjudicate a limited physical claim: faster motion is associated with more consistent turning, and higher acceleration with less consistent turning. It cannot adjudicate force production, ground reaction force, joint torque, or fatigue biochemistry because those variables are absent. Biomechanical-layer veto: the paper explicitly says that “with access to other data sources such as biomechanics data (or pose data), besides capturing what a player does with our model (e.g., variable turning), we can also understand how they do it (e.g., through hip or foot positioning)” and that “these data are currently not open to the public” [PAGE 17]. Therefore any hip-orientation, foot-placement, or neuromuscular mechanism claim is outside the identified model and is vetoed. Weather-layer veto: Table 2 lists speed, acceleration, distance, play type, position, defender geometry, and teammate counts, but not temperature, precipitation, wind, field surface, or footwear [PAGE 6]. Since weather is absent from \(x_{ijt}\) and \(z_{ijt}\), a weather explanation is confounded with any correlated included covariate and cannot be causally identified. The adjudication rule is: if a claim requires a layer not represented in the model and that layer is plausibly correlated with included covariates, the claim is vetoed as an identified explanation; it may remain a hypothesis for future data collection.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
