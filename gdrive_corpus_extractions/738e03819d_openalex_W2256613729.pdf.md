<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper implicitly models three agents: the statistical model, the coaching staff, and the observer or analyst. The recursive problem is that each agent’s behavior can change the distribution the other agent observes.

First, crisis habit regression. Teams that are trailing late in a game often shift toward more aggressive passing to conserve clock and generate yardage quickly. Teams that are leading late often shift toward conservative rushing to use clock and reduce risk. The paper identifies this exact possibility: a leading team’s reduced passing fraction could artificially depress the observed passing fraction, while a trailing team’s increased passing could inflate turnover risk [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. The authors test this by examining quarter-by-quarter evolution of \(r\) and by comparing turnovers through the third quarter. They conclude that the passing fraction is stable after the first quarter and that they can more confidently reject strong reverse causality for \(r\) [PAGE 9]. They also find that winning teams commit fewer turnovers than losing opponents by the end of the third quarter, supporting the view that turnover avoidance is associated with winning [PAGE 10].

Second, counter-trap behavior. If a coach treats the regression as a causal optimization rule, the largest coefficient becomes tempting. The paper explicitly warns against this. The passing-fraction coefficient has the largest absolute magnitude, but one cannot infer that calling only running plays maximizes win probability. The model is valid only within the range of observed input values [PAGE 7]. If a coaching staff pushes \(r\) below the observed support, the fitted linear trend may no longer apply. This is a counter-trap because the model’s own strongest coefficient becomes a trap when interpreted outside its empirical domain.

Third, clock effects. The paper notes that turnovers spike toward the end of each quarter and especially near the two-minute warning [PAGE 11]. This is not merely noise; it reflects strategic urgency. A trailing team may attempt risky passes because the clock is about to stop or the half is ending. A leading team may become conservative. Thus, the same turnover count can have different meanings depending on game state. The model uses full-game turnover differential, so it collapses timing into a single scalar. That is acceptable for baseline prediction but insufficient for causal coaching.

Fourth, recursive model awareness. If the model becomes public, teams may alter behavior to exploit or avoid its apparent preferences. For example, if analysts believe that a lower passing fraction is associated with winning, teams may attempt to game the metric by running more even when passing is optimal. If teams believe that turnover avoidance is the dominant lever, they may become overly conservative and reduce offensive variance. The model then becomes endogenous: the statistic it measures is no longer stationary because agents know it is being measured. This is the core recursive theory-of-mind problem. The paper’s descriptive model is calibrated on historical behavior, but future behavior may change if the model influences decisions.

Fifth, the clock creates a horizon problem. Early in a game, feature distributions are noisy and unstable. Late in a game, feature distributions are shaped by score state, clock, and urgency. The bootstrap engine partially addresses this by using recent games and block dependencies, but it does not explicitly condition on current score, time remaining, or win probability state. Therefore, a recursive agent should treat FPM as a pre-game anchor, not as an in-game causal controller. The safe interpretation is: FPM estimates a baseline distribution of outcomes from historical performance; it does not know whether a team is in crisis mode, whether a turnover is garbage-time noise, or whether a weather event has invalidated the historical feature distribution.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can veto the statistical layer whenever they materially change the feature distribution that the model assumes. The paper’s model is observational and descriptive. It says the regression captures merely correlations rather than cause-effect relations [PAGE 7]. It also says the system is agnostic to game-day decisions such as roster decisions, and that there is room for improvement by adjusting the bootstrap module [PAGE 15]. Finally, it explicitly mentions that adjustments can be made using matchup-specific information such as roster and weather forecast [PAGE 3]. These statements establish that the authors themselves recognize the model’s physical-context layer is incomplete.

A formal veto proof can be stated as follows. Let \(W\in\{0,1\}\) be the game outcome, \(Z\) the vector of statistical features, and \(V\) a physical or environmental state such as injury availability, biomechanical limitation, surface condition, wind, precipitation, or temperature. The model estimates:

\[
p(z)=\Pr(W=1\mid Z=z)
\]

But a coaching or matchup decision often requires:

\[
p_v(z)=\Pr(W=1\mid Z=z, V=v)
\]

If \(V\) affects both \(Z\) and \(W\), then omitting \(V\) produces confounding. The observed association between \(Z\) and \(W\) may not equal the causal effect of changing \(Z\). If a veto variable shifts the expected feature vector by \(\Delta z_v\), the first-order change in predicted probability is:

\[
\Delta p_v
\approx
p(z)\left(1-p(z)\right)\beta^\top \Delta z_v
\]

where \(\beta\) is the vector of fitted coefficients. If \(|\Delta p_v|\) exceeds the predictive uncertainty band from the bootstrap or cross-validation, the physical layer should veto the raw model output. This is not a toy equation; it is the standard first-order sensitivity of a logistic model to an exogenous feature shift.

For biomechanical vetoes, consider an injury to a starting offensive line or quarterback. Such an event can reduce passing efficiency, increase pressure, increase turnover probability, and reduce possession time. The historical bootstrap matrix contains games played under different health conditions. If the current roster state is materially different from the sampled rows, the bootstrap distribution is no longer representative. The model may still return a probability, but that probability is conditional on an obsolete data-generating process. The veto is therefore not merely qualitative; it is a support mismatch between the resampled empirical distribution and the current physical roster state.

For weather vetoes, consider wind or precipitation. These can alter passing accuracy, rushing commitment, turnover probability, and coaching strategy. If the historical sample contains mostly neutral-weather games, the bootstrap distribution underrepresents adverse conditions. The paper’s own discussion allows weather as an adjustment factor [PAGE 3]. Therefore, a weather veto is valid when the forecast shifts the expected passing fraction, turnover propensity, or possession-time distribution beyond the historical bootstrap envelope.

For schedule-strength vetoes, the paper admits that raw performance may be inflated by weak opponents [PAGE 15][PAGE 16]. A team can accumulate offensive yards because opponents have poor defenses. The model’s raw feature extrapolation then overstates future performance. The proposed remedy is propensity-score reweighting, where resampling probabilities are biased toward teams similar to the upcoming opponent [PAGE 16]. Until that adjustment is implemented, the physical-contextual layer can veto naive extrapolation.

The adjudication is therefore: the statistical model may estimate a baseline probability, but a physical, biomechanical, or weather veto can override it whenever the exogenous state invalidates the historical feature distribution. The veto is strongest when the omitted state changes the expected value of high-coefficient features, especially turnovers and passing fraction, because those features have large logistic sensitivity. The veto is weaker when the exogenous state affects low-coefficient features such as penalty yards, unless the shift is extremely large.

The following three reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards style counterfactual veto scenarios are designed to test whether the model’s apparent predictive skill survives interventions that expose its assumptions. Each scenario includes a veto condition, a counterfactual prediction, and a verifiable test.

**Scenario 1: Extreme passing-fraction extrapolation veto**

The model’s largest absolute coefficient is associated with the passing fraction \(r\). Suppose a team’s historical \(r\) is near the lower observed boundary, approximately \(0.3\), and the opponent’s \(r\) is near the upper observed boundary, approximately \(0.98\). The differential is large and negative. Because the coefficient is \(-3.18\), the model predicts a substantial increase in home win probability when the home team’s passing fraction is much lower than the opponent’s. Now impose the counterfactual: the home team attempts an extreme run-heavy game plan that pushes \(r\) below \(0.3\), while the opponent remains above \(0.98\). The model will extrapolate beyond the training support.

Veto: The paper states that the model is valid only within the range of input values covered by the data, and for \(r\) the approximate support is \([0.3,0.98]\) [PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. Therefore, the model’s extrapolated probability should be vetoed as a reliable causal or predictive estimate.

Prediction: If the model is used outside this support, its predicted probability will be overconfident relative to observed outcomes. The calibration curve will depart from the diagonal in the extreme-\(r\) region, and proper scoring metrics such as Brier score or log loss will worsen relative to a support-restricted model.

Verifiable test: Collect all games with \(r\) near or outside the observed support. Compare predicted win probability to realized win frequency. If the model’s predicted probability exceeds realized frequency by more than sampling noise, the extrapolation veto is confirmed. The Brier score is:

\[
\mathrm{BS}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(p_n-y_n)^2
\]

with units of probability squared. Log loss in bits is:

\[
\ell
=
-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\left[
y_n\log_2 p_n+
(1-y_n)\log_2(1-p_n)
\right]
\]

If extreme-\(r\) games produce higher \(\mathrm{BS}\) or \(\ell\) than in-support games, the veto is empirically supported.

**Scenario 2: Turnover-timing veto**

The model uses full-game turnover differential. Suppose two games have identical final turnover differential, but in Game A the home team commits two early turnovers, while in Game B the home team commits two turnovers during the final two-minute warning of a game that is already effectively decided. The model predicts the same turnover-related probability penalty in both games because the feature value is identical.

Veto: The paper shows that turnovers spike near the end of halves and games, with the highest density during the two-minute warning [PAGE 11]. It also shows that winning teams commit fewer turnovers by the end of the third quarter [PAGE 10]. This suggests that timing matters, but the full-game feature collapses timing into a single count. Therefore, the turnover feature should be vetoed as a sufficient statistic when timing is likely to be non-ignorable.

Prediction: If early turnovers are more causally damaging than late garbage-time turnovers, then games with equal full-game turnover differential but worse early turnover timing will have lower actual win rates than the model predicts. Conversely, if late turnovers are mostly symptoms of desperation rather than causes of loss, the model will over-penalize teams that committed late turnovers in already-lost games.

Verifiable test: Stratify games by turnover timing: early-quarter differential, third-quarter differential, and fourth-quarter differential. Fit an extended model with timing-specific turnover coefficients. Compare out-of-sample log loss or Brier score against the full-game turnover model. If timing-specific coefficients improve predictive accuracy, the full-game turnover feature is insufficient and the timing veto is confirmed. A formal likelihood comparison can be made using information criteria or cross-validated log loss:

\[
\Delta \ell
=
\ell_{\text{full-game}}
-
\ell_{\text{timing-aware}}
\]

If \(\Delta\ell>0\) consistently out-of-sample, timing-aware features dominate.

**Scenario 3: Schedule-strength inflation veto**

The model bootstraps a team’s raw historical statistics without fully adjusting for opponent quality. Suppose Team A accumulated high offensive yardage against weak defenses, while Team B accumulated similar yardage against strong defenses. The model treats their offensive-yard distributions as comparable. The paper acknowledges that teams can accumulate offensive yards because they played poor defenses [PAGE 16], and it proposes propensity-score weighting as a remedy [PAGE 16].

Veto: Until schedule strength is properly incorporated, the raw bootstrap extrapolation of inflated statistics should be vetoed for teams with systematically weak schedules. The paper explicitly says that one limitation is that the system does not incorporate schedule-strength information except indirectly through SportsNetRank [PAGE 15].

Prediction: A schedule-adjusted model will lower the predicted probability for teams whose raw statistics were inflated by weak opponents, and raise the predicted probability for teams whose raw statistics were suppressed by strong opponents. The raw model will overpredict future success for weak-schedule teams and underpredict for strong-schedule teams.

Verifiable test: Construct an opponent-adjusted performance metric. For each team \(T\), compute a weighted bootstrap where historical games are weighted by the similarity between past opponents and the upcoming opponent. The source suggests using a propensity score \(\gamma_i\) for past opponent \(T_i\), where \(\gamma_i\in[0,1]\) measures similarity to the upcoming opponent [PAGE 16]. The resampled probability for a historical row from opponent \(T_i\) is proportional to \(\gamma_i\). Then compare out-of-sample accuracy, Brier score, and log loss between the raw FPM bootstrap and the schedule-adjusted bootstrap. If the adjusted model improves calibration and reduces error, the raw schedule-blind model is vetoed for that matchup class.

These three scenarios are verifiable because they require only observable game data, predicted probabilities, and realized outcomes. They do not require hidden causal identification. They test whether the model’s apparent performance survives interventions that stress its support, timing assumptions, and opponent-quality assumptions. If any veto scenario produces systematic miscalibration, the corresponding model component should be downweighted, recalibrated, or replaced with a context-aware layer.
