Hull’s theoretical derivation hinges on the interplay between habit strength (sHᵣ), drive (D), and incentive motivation (K). When reinforcement stops, the response potential drops, but not uniformly—due to afferent interaction (the blending of S₁ and S₂ at the neural level), the response curves become asymmetric, creating a “pattern” even without explicit differential conditioning.

The multi-hop reasoning proceeds as follows: First, we formalize Hull’s core equation sEr = D × sHᵣ × V × K, where sEr is the manifest response strength. In patterning, V (stimulus effectiveness) decays with each non-reinforcement, following an exponential forgetting curve. For a compound, the effective stimulus is not simply additive; due to generalization gradients overlapping, the net excitation at any point is the sum of activations from S₁ and S₂, minus inhibitory interactions (afferent inhibition). This leads to a composite gradient that peaks at the original training point (where both stimuli are present) and falls off toward the edges. When reinforcement ceases, the decay is faster for stimuli that were less strongly associated, causing the peak to sharpen—a spontaneous pattern emerges.

The gray-zone audit reveals several ambiguities. Hull’s “potentially negative” could imply that the stimuli elicit avoidance responses when no longer paired with reward, introducing a secondary drive (e.g., anxiety) that interacts with the primary drive (hunger). There is also the unresolved question of whether the patterning is truly “spontaneous” or merely an artifact of pre-existing differences in stimulus intensity or prior exposure. Finally, the role of time since last trial is implicit but not modeled—could affect V through recency effects.

Expanding further, we consider the neurophysiological substrate: simultaneous presentation of S₁ and S₂ likely causes temporal summation in the cortex, enhancing the response beyond what either alone would produce. When reinforcement stops, synaptic depression may occur more rapidly in pathways with weaker initial activation, again sharpening the pattern. This aligns with modern concepts of long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD), though Hull’s era lacked such terminology.

In summary, the theoretical derivation is a sophisticated attempt to explain how complex behaviors can arise from simple associative mechanisms without explicit programming, foreshadowing later theories of emergent complexity in systems neuroscience.

G(S) = A \cdot e^{-\frac{(S - S_0)^2}{2\sigma^2}}

\[
A_{net}(x) = G_1(x) + G_2(x) - I_{aff}(x)
\]  
where \(I_{aff}(x)\) represents afferent inhibition (negative interaction).

\[
{}_sH_r(t) = {}_sH_{r,0} \cdot e^{-\lambda t}
\]

\[
{}_sV_r(n) = {}_sV_{r,0} \cdot (1 - c)^n

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This derivation sits within a lineage of behaviorist attempts to quantify learning. Key antecedents include Pavlov’s work on conditioned reflexes, Thorndike’s law of effect, and Watson’s emphasis on observable behavior. Hull synthesized these into a postulational system, influenced by logical positivism and the cybernetics of Wiener. The concept of “patterning” evolved from earlier studies by Kimble and others on discrimination learning, but Hull uniquely emphasized that patterns could emerge spontaneously from the mathematics of stimulus interaction, without needing explicit reinforcement schedules.

Historically, similar ideas appeared in Gestalt psychology (e.g., Köhler’s insight learning), but Hull provided a mechanistic account. Later, Rescorla-Wagner (1972) formalized blocking and overshadowing, which relate to afferent inhibition. Modern predictive coding theories (Friston) echo the idea that prediction errors (from unreinforced stimuli) shape future responses. The “multi-week trap” here is overgeneralizing Hull’s model to all learning: it fails for cognitive maps, latent learning, and social learning, where internal representations play a larger role.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis situations (e.g., sudden loss of expected reward), the subject’s behavior regresses to simpler, more global response tendencies (e.g., exploration or freezing) rather than the finely tuned pattern. This regression is predicted by the equations: as K drops sharply, sEr falls below threshold for the specific response, allowing competing habits (e.g., escape) to dominate. However, if the subject has learned to anticipate unreinforcement (via cues), it might exhibit a counter-trap: pre-emptively reducing responding before the full pattern emerges, thereby conserving energy. The clock variable (time since last trial) modulates this: immediate post-extinction tests show sharper patterns due to residual V, while delayed tests show flatter gradients as forgetting sets in. Thus, the theory must account for meta-learning—the animal learns not just S-R bonds but also the reliability of reinforcement, altering future D and K values dynamically.

Three verifiable scenarios where Reinforcement Learning Verifier (RLVR) logic vetoes Hull’s predictions:

1. **Overjustification Effect**: If an external reward is added to an already intrinsically motivated behavior, Hull’s model (which assumes rewards always increase K) predicts stronger responding. However, human subjects often show reduced intrinsic motivation, weakening the pattern. Prediction: Adding small extrinsic rewards to a well-established food-seeking pattern will decrease response amplitude over trials.

2. **Latent Learning**: Rats explore mazes without reward, forming cognitive maps. When reward is introduced, they perform well immediately. Hull’s model requires repeated S-R reinforcements to build H; thus it cannot explain rapid performance improvement without prior reinforcement. Prediction: Animals with prior unsystematic exposure to the maze will show steeper learning curves than naive animals, even if no reward was given during exploration.

3. **Observational Learning**: An animal watches another receive reward for a response and subsequently performs the response without direct reinforcement. Hull’s framework lacks a mechanism for vicarious reinforcement. Prediction: Subjects observing a conspecific being reinforced will acquire the pattern faster than those in isolation, violating the assumption that only direct reinforcement shapes H.
