Full extraction of “all that they can pay” requires approaching common knowledge, i.e., an infinite regress in which every bidder knows that every other knows that every other knows reservation values and budgets.

Crisis habit regression occurs when time or stress collapses the stack. Under deadline pressure, bidders regress from second-order to zeroth-order thinking: they attend only to their own willingness-to-pay and stop modeling the opponent’s bluff. The result is either underbidding (leaving surplus on the table) or overpaying (falling for a bluff), breaking Knight’s predicted full extraction.

The counter-trap is the deliberate inflation of first-order beliefs. A bidder who signals a higher budget than she possesses induces opponents to second-guess their own second-order models, causing them to bid below their true capacity. Because the underlying values are imperfectly communicable [PAGE 286], the signal cannot be immediately falsified, so the counter-trap can persist across several bargaining rounds.

The clock variable is bargaining time-to-deadline $T-t$. Let recursion depth be $d(t)\in\mathbb{N}$. As $t\to T$, cognitive and informational constraints force $d(t)\to 0$. At the singularity $t=T$, all second-order and higher beliefs vanish; the agent reverts to her own budget constraint only, and the “forced to pay all that they can pay” result fails. Any prediction of complete surplus extraction must therefore be conditioned on $d(T-)\ge 2$; otherwise the clock vetoes the equilibrium.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The “scope of enterprise one man can deal with effectively” [PAGE 286] is bounded by pre-modern communication latency and travel speed. An entrepreneur cannot oversee a variety of situations distributed across distance faster than the speed of sail, horse, or foot. Weather—monsoon, storms, seasonal wind shifts—imposes hard information blackouts and enforcement delays. These are physical vetoes on arbitrage: if price signals and contracts cannot circulate, the competitive erosion of exploiters’ profits cannot occur within the theoretical window.

Biomechanical layer: Diminishing returns from management arise because one man’s attention, working memory, and fatigue tolerance are finite. Sleep deprivation, cognitive load, and decision fatigue reduce the entrepreneur’s effective theory-of-mind depth below the second order required for wage extraction [PAGE 286]. When $d<2$, the recursive bargaining mechanism collapses regardless of the economic incentives. Biomechanics therefore sets an upper bound on $n$ and on the quality of each entrepreneur’s judgment; it is not merely a cost shifter but a structural limit on competitive entry.

Weather and disease layer: In the colonial-exploiter scenario, malaria, yellow fever, and tropical disease environments physically cap the number of Europeans who can be present and productive. If the disease environment fixes $n_{\max}$ below the critical $n^*(U)$ implied by the steepness of diminishing returns [PAGE 286], then prices will not reach competitive level even though the exploited masses are willing to accept larger offers. This is a hard, exogenous veto on the entry-erosion mechanism.

Adjudication rule: Any forecast of zero economic profit or full wage extraction is vetoed if (i) weather destroys the communicability channel such that $I(\Theta;S)\approx0$, (ii) fatigue or cognitive load drives theory-of-mind depth $d<2$, or (iii) disease/geography bounds $n_{\max}<n^*(U)$. Only when all three layers permit recursive belief formation and sufficient entry can Knight’s convergence result obtain.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1 — Zero-communicability veto. Set channel capacity $C=0$ so that $I(\Theta;S)=0$: bidders receive no signals about others’ powers or budgets. Verifiable condition: shut off information flow between bargaining parties and record wage dispersion across repeated contracts. Prediction: wages do not converge to $\max_j B_j$; instead variance remains high and bluffing becomes unexploitable because there are no signals to condition on. Veto rule: $I=0$ vetoes the “forced to pay all that they can pay” extraction [PAGE 286].

Scenario 2 — Physical entry-cap veto. Fix disease/weather constraints such that the maximum number of exploiters $n_{\max}$ is below the critical entry count $n^*(U)$ required to erode profit. Verifiable condition: compare extractive outposts with high vs. low settler mortality and measure persistence of above-competitive returns. Prediction: high-mortality locations maintain profit wedges despite natives accepting larger offers, because $n$ never reaches the threshold where competitive pressure binds. Veto rule: $n_{\max}<n^*(U)$ vetoes competitive erosion.

Scenario 3 — Radical-uncertainty scope veto. Let uncertainty $U$ be so steep that entrepreneurial scope collapses, i.e., $\alpha(U)\le0$ or $\partial\Pi/\partial n>0$ over the feasible range. Verifiable condition: in frontier markets with no prior data, observe whether adding entrants increases concentration and profit rather than dissipating it. Prediction: winner-take-all entrepreneurial monopoly emerges; more competitors intensify rather than reduce profit because no single agent can scope the variety of situations [PAGE 286]. Veto rule: radical uncertainty vetoes the standard diminishing-returns convergence assumption.
