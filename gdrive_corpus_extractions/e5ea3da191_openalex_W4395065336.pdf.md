<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retro-causal genealogy of TeamTrack begins with the older MOT benchmark lineage: MOT16 and MOT20 are pedestrian-centric and crowded-scene benchmarks [PAGE 2], while KITTI is autonomous-driving oriented [PAGE 2]. These datasets shaped the tracking-by-detection paradigm: detect first, then associate using motion and appearance [PAGE 2]. The paper explicitly notes that “Current methods typically employ detection followed by re-identification (ReID), relying on appearance-based features for temporal association” [PAGE 2]. That lineage creates a multi-week trap: a research program trained on MOT17 may assume that appearance ReID is sufficient because pedestrians usually have distinguishable clothing and motion statistics. TeamTrack invalidates that assumption by emphasizing “targets with similar appearance, dynamic movements, and frequent occlusions” [PAGE 3].

A second lineage comes from sports-specific tracking. SoccerNet-Tracking, SportsMOT, SoccerTrack, and SSET are cited as sports or partial-field resources [PAGE 2] [PAGE 3]. Broadcast-footage datasets introduce camera motion, zoom, and scene transitions, which require registration and complicate direct comparison [PAGE 3]. TeamTrack avoids that specific broadcast problem by using full-pitch drone and fisheye side views [PAGE 3]. However, it creates a different trap: researchers may assume that full-pitch stability eliminates camera motion issues, while in reality drone altitude, fisheye distortion, and viewpoint change create new scale and geometry problems. The annotation pipeline also has a lineage effect: CVAT and Labelbox interpolation reduce labor, but the source says annotation was focused “on approximately every 1 to 50 frames” [PAGE 3]. This is efficient but dangerous if dynamic events occur between annotated frames. The model or annotator may interpolate across an identity switch, producing a false smooth track.

A third lineage is the DanceTrack comparison. The paper follows DanceTrack methodology for appearance similarity, adjacent IoU, and relative-position switch [PAGE 3] [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6]. DanceTrack has high position-switch frequency and low HOTA relative to MOT17 [PAGE 8]. TeamTrack is compared with DanceTrack, MOT17, and MOT20 [PAGE 5]. The trap is to assume that TeamTrack must be uniformly harder because it has sports motion and similar appearance. The source rejects that simplification: average TeamTrack HOTA is 61.9, MOT17 is 63.1, and DanceTrack is 47.1 [PAGE 8]. The real signal is variance: Basketball SideView reaches 76.2 HOTA for ByteTrack, while Basketball SideView2 drops to 42.9 [PAGE 8].

A fourth multi-week trap is annotation economics. The source reports “over 600 person-hours, translating to roughly 2 hours for every 30 seconds of video” [PAGE 4]. It also says semi-automatic detection can fail because “the time saved was often offset by the need to correct false positives/negatives and manage ID switches” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, any future dataset-building plan that assumes automated annotation will scale linearly is historically naive. The retro-causal lesson is that annotation tools can create false smoothness, and benchmark scores may reflect interpolation quality as much as algorithm quality.

The final genealogical trap is metric inheritance. MOT researchers often inherit HOTA and MOTA as summary scores. TeamTrack shows that aggregate scores hide view-specific failures [PAGE 8]. A future method that wins on average TeamTrack may still fail on Soccer TopView or Basketball SideView2. The historical lineage therefore forces a shift from dataset-level leaderboard thinking to condition-level diagnostics: sport, view, object scale, annotation density, and association stress must be audited separately.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer treats the tracker as an agent reasoning about players who are also trying to avoid being tracked. In team sports, players cluster, cross, occlude, and change relative position. The source supports this by stating that the dataset includes “similar appearance, dynamic movements, and frequent occlusions” [PAGE 3] and that frequent position switches can arise from “highly non-linear motion patterns” [PAGE 6]. The tracker must therefore model not only where a player is, but also how a player can make identity ambiguous.

A formal belief state can be written as:
\[
p_{tk} = P(I_t = k \mid Z_{1:t}),
\]
where \(I_t\) is the true identity at frame \(t\) and \(Z_{1:t}\) is the observation history. The associated uncertainty is:
\[
H_t = -\sum_{k=1}^{N_t} p_{tk}\log_2 p_{tk},
\]
with units bits per object-frame. Breakdown regime: if \(N_t = 0\), if the detector misses all players, or if the identity posterior is not normalized, \(H_t\) is undefined. High \(H_t\) means the tracker cannot confidently assign an identity. TeamTrack’s appearance-similarity result implies that appearance alone often produces high \(H_t\), especially among same-team players [PAGE 5].

Crisis habit regression occurs when the tracker, under high identity uncertainty, falls back to a simple habit: nearest-box association, constant velocity, or appearance similarity. This regression is dangerous because the source shows that motion is non-linear and position switches are common [PAGE 6]. If the habit prior is too narrow, the tracker will predict smooth continuation where the players actually cross or occlude. The paper’s trajectory-forecasting experiment supports this warning: constant velocity has much larger long-horizon RMSE than LSTM, for example 7.99 versus 6.50 for football at 144 frames, 7.68 versus 5.19 for basketball, and 6.08 versus 4.07 for handball [PAGE 7]. A habit-based forecaster is therefore vulnerable in TeamTrack.

The counter-trap is to replace habit regression with structured inference: multi-view geometry, team labels, pitch dimensions, player count priors, and interaction-aware models. The paper says the multi-view full-pitch setting enables “the use of prior information (e.g., player count, pitch dimensions) not feasible with traditional broadcast videos” [PAGE 3]. It also notes that incorporating team labels and interaction models could improve forecasting [PAGE 7]. In theory-of-mind terms, the tracker should ask: given that opponents may cross or occlude, which association hypotheses remain physically plausible? This shifts the problem from passive feature matching to adversarial trajectory reasoning.

The clock layer is temporal. The source’s annotation clock is sparse: manual annotation was focused on approximately every 1 to 50 frames [PAGE 3]. The forecasting clock is explicit: observe 3.2 seconds and predict 4.8 seconds, or 144 frames [PAGE 7]. The tracking clock is frame rate dependent; low frame rate can reduce adjacent-frame IoU even if motion is smooth [PAGE 6]. Therefore, a counter-trap policy must condition on the clock: if annotation is sparse, interpolation may invent smooth identity paths; if frame rate is low, low IoU does not necessarily imply chaotic motion; if forecasting horizon is long, short-term motion models degrade.

The recursive equilibrium is this: players try to make identity ambiguous; trackers should not rely on a single cue. Appearance is weak because same-team features are entangled [PAGE 5]. Motion is useful but confounded by frame rate and local clustering [PAGE 6]. Spatial priors are strong because full-pitch geometry is available [PAGE 3]. The best counter-trap is therefore a multi-cue posterior that explicitly models occlusion, position swaps, and view-dependent scale. If the tracker ignores any of these, the opposing team’s cooperative or adversarial movement can induce crisis habit regression.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

The cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether a proposed tracking hypothesis survives physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints. The source gives enough structure to prove several conditional vetoes, although it does not provide all numerical constants.

First, physical continuity veto.
Let \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be the ground-plane position of an object at frame \(t\). For any finite biomechanical maximum speed \(v_{\max}\), frame interval \(\Delta t\), and localization error \(\epsilon_{\text{loc}}\), a physically plausible track must satisfy:
\[
\|x_{t+1} - x_t\|_2 \le v_{\max}\Delta t + \epsilon_{\text{loc}}.
\]
If a proposed identity assignment requires one player to move from box \(B_{ti}\) to box \(B_{t+1,j}\) with displacement greater than this bound, and no occlusion or detection miss explains the gap, the hypothesis is vetoed. Units: meters if \(x\) is in meters, \(v_{\max}\) in meters per second, \(\Delta t\) in seconds, and \(\epsilon_{\text{loc}}\) in meters. Assumptions: ground-plane projection is valid, the player is visible, and the camera calibration is adequate. Breakdown regime: severe fisheye residual distortion, drone motion, or rolling-shutter effects can make apparent displacement violate the inequality even when physical motion is plausible. The source notes fisheye correction using Zhang’s method [PAGE 3], so the veto is valid only to the extent that calibration is accurate.

Second, scale-based detection veto.
For a pinhole approximation, the projected pixel height of a player is:
\[
h_{\text{px}} = \frac{f_{\text{px}} H_{\text{player}}}{Z},
\]
where \(f_{\text{px}}\) is focal length in pixels, \(H_{\text{player}}\) is physical player height in meters, and \(Z\) is camera-to-player distance in meters. Units: pixels. If \(h_{\text{px}}\) falls below the effective detector stride or below the scale range learned during training, detection confidence collapses. The source supports the qualitative version: lower top-view mAP is “largely attributed to the smaller appearance of players from high-altitude drone footage” [PAGE 7]. Therefore, a proposed tracker that claims top-view performance without addressing small-object detection is vetoed unless it provides a super-resolution, tiling, or scale-aware detection module. Breakdown regime: exact proof requires altitude, focal length, and detector stride, which are not fully specified in the source; therefore the veto is structural rather than numerically complete.

Third, biomechanical ambiguity veto.
If two players cross or occlude, the tracker may propose an identity swap. A swap is physically possible, but it must be consistent with the relative-position-switch statistic and with motion continuity. The source defines \(sw(\cdot)\) to capture left-right or top-down swaps between adjacent frames [PAGE 6]. If a proposed swap occurs while adjacent-frame IoU is high and no occlusion or detection miss is present, the swap should be vetoed unless there is evidence of rapid non-linear motion. Conversely, if the swap statistic is high in a dense cluster, the tracker should not assume identity stability. This is especially relevant in Basketball SideView2, where detection is not the main failure and association is weak [PAGE 8].

Fourth, weather and environmental veto.
The source identifies some capture sites as outdoor, such as University of Tsukuba for soccer and Wuhu Institute of Technology for some basketball views, while others are indoor [PAGE 4]. Weather is therefore a latent environmental variable for outdoor sequences. A weather veto would require a model such as:
\[
P(D_t = 1 \mid W_t) < P(D_t = 1 \mid W_t = \text{clear}),
\]
where \(D_t\) is detection success and \(W_t\) is weather state. If rain, glare, wind-induced drone motion, or low light reduces detection or increases localization error, a tracker that ignores \(W_t\) may fail. However, the source does not provide weather logs or environmental covariates. Therefore, any weather-based veto is not fully verifiable from the source alone. The adjudication must mark weather as a non-identifiable confounder unless external metadata is supplied.

Final adjudication:
- A hypothesis that requires impossible displacement is vetoed.
- A hypothesis that claims high top-view tracking while ignoring small-object scale is vetoed.
- A hypothesis that assumes stable identities through high-density crossings without occlusion evidence is vetoed.
- A hypothesis that uses average TeamTrack scores to claim uniform robustness is vetoed because the source shows large per-view variance [PAGE 8].

The following are three verifiable RLVR-style veto scenarios. “RLVR” here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a proposed policy or claim is vetoed if it contradicts a measurable source constraint, and the veto is followed by a concrete prediction.

Veto Scenario 1: Appearance-only association is sufficient.
Claim under test: A tracker can solve TeamTrack primarily by appearance ReID because players are visually distinguishable.
Source constraint: TeamTrack has high appearance similarity. The paper says the dataset features “targets with similar appearance, dynamic movements, and frequent occlusions” [PAGE 3], and the re-ID cosine metric shows higher appearance similarity than pedestrian MOT datasets [PAGE 5]. The t-SNE analysis also says same-team players are highly entangled [PAGE 5].
Formal veto: If appearance likelihood \(P(Z_t \mid I_t=k)\) is weakly separable, then identity posterior entropy \(H_t\) remains high, and appearance-only association cannot reliably minimize ID switches.
Prediction: An appearance-only tracker will show a large drop in AssA and IDF1 in TeamTrack settings with dense same-team players, especially Basketball SideView2, compared with motion- or geometry-aware trackers. The drop should be larger than any improvement from appearance alone.
Verification: Ablate motion, spatial priors, and team labels. If AssA does not collapse when appearance is removed, the claim survives; if it collapses, the claim is vetoed.

Veto Scenario 2: High detection mAP guarantees high HOTA.
Claim under test: Because fine-tuning improves mAP, tracking performance must improve proportionally.
Source constraint: The source reports high Basketball SideView2 detection but poor tracking. For ByteTrack on Basketball SideView2, DetA is 54.7, AssA is 33.7, and HOTA is 42.9 [PAGE 8]. Using the canonical HOTA identity:
\[
\mathrm{HOTA} = \sqrt{\mathrm{DetA}\cdot \mathrm{AssA}},
\]
with percentage-point values, the reported value is consistent with the low association term. The paper explicitly says SideView2 posed significant challenges [PAGE 8].
Formal veto: If a policy increases DetA but does not increase AssA, HOTA is bounded by the association term. For example, with DetA fixed near 54.7 and AssA fixed near 33.7, HOTA cannot exceed approximately:
\[
\sqrt{54.7 \times 33.7} \approx 42.9.
\]
Any counterfactual claim of HOTA above 60 in that setting without a corresponding AssA increase is vetoed.
Prediction: A detector-only intervention on Basketball SideView2 will improve mAP or DetA but will leave HOTA near the lower 40s unless the tracker adds motion interaction, multi-view consistency, or occlusion handling. If AssA rises above 50, HOTA should rise accordingly; if AssA remains below 35, HOTA remains vetoed below roughly 43.

Veto Scenario 3: Constant-velocity forecasting is sufficient for occlusion recovery.
Claim under test: A simple constant-velocity model can maintain identity through occlusion or predict future positions well enough for tracking.
Source constraint: The source evaluates constant velocity and LSTM trajectory forecasting. At 144 frames, constant velocity RMSE is worse than LSTM for all sports: football 7.99 versus 6.50, basketball 7.68 versus 5.19, and handball 6.08 versus 4.07 [PAGE 7]. The source also says errors accumulate over time and LSTM generally outperforms, validating that TeamTrack has diverse and non-linear movement [PAGE 7].
Formal veto: For long-horizon occlusion recovery, if the motion model’s expected error exceeds the spatial separation between candidate identities, the tracker should not maintain identity by motion extrapolation alone. Let \(\mathrm{RMSE}_k\) be the forecast error at horizon \(k\). If \(\mathrm{RMSE}_{144}\) is large relative to inter-player spacing, identity hypotheses become non-identifiable.
Prediction: A constant-velocity occlusion-recovery module will produce more ID switches than an LSTM or interaction-aware module at long horizons. The performance gap should be largest in sports or views with high position-switch frequency and low adjacent IoU, such as Soccer TopView or dense basketball scenes [PAGE 6] [PAGE 8].
Verification: Measure IDF1 and AssA after occlusion events. If constant velocity matches learned forecasting, the claim survives; if IDF1 degrades while detection remains stable, the claim is vetoed.
