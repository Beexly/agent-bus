This occurs when an animal, initially guided by cognitive readiness and past success, becomes fixated on a particular route or position (e.g., a "position-habit" in rats choosing the right alley regardless of changing cues). The initial expectation loses its cognitive character, becoming an almost blind, habitual persistence that requires significant "disproving" to break down. This phenomenon highlights a transition from flexible, goal-directed behavior to rigid, stimulus-bound responding.

To analyze this deeply, we must consider several angles:

This mirrors modern distinctions between model-based and model-free reinforcement learning. Model-based RL uses internal representations of the environment's dynamics to plan actions, akin to sign-gestalt-expectation. Model-free RL relies on cached values or habits, akin to fixation. The transition can occur due to over-reliance on successful strategies, even when they become suboptimal.

2. **Persistence and Resistance to Change**: Position-habits persist despite contradictory evidence, suggesting a high degree of stability once established. This resistance may stem from neural mechanisms that reinforce habitual responses, such as striatal pathways. The "tremendous lot of 'disproving'" needed implies that the fixation has created a strong predictive framework that filters out conflicting information, similar to confirmation bias or perceptual set.

3. **Experimental Design and Operator Challenges**: Tolman notes that operators often balance correctness across left and right alleys to avoid bias, yet fixation still emerges. This indicates that early successes or extraneous events (like fear) can disproportionately influence behavior, creating a path dependency. The irregular shifting of cues might inadvertently reinforce the habit if the animal focuses on spatial position rather than the cue itself.

4. **Implications for Learning Theory**: Fixation challenges the view that learning is always adaptive and flexible. It suggests that under certain conditions, learning can lead to maladaptive rigidity. This has implications for understanding disorders like OCD or addiction, where habitual behaviors override goal-directed control.

5. **Broader Context**: Tolman's work prefigured debates about cognition in animals, arguing for map-like representations. Fixation represents a failure mode of such representations, where the map becomes too static. Modern neuroscience identifies dorsolateral striatum involvement in habits, while dorsomedial striatum supports goal-directed actions; fixation might reflect a shift in dominance between these systems.

In summary, fixation as described by Tolman is a robust phenomenon where initial cognitive strategies degrade into mechanical habits, resisting updating even with clear disconfirming evidence. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s discussion of fixation emerges from a rich historical context:
- **Pre-Tolman Behaviorism**: Watson and others emphasized S-R bonds, where stimuli elicit fixed responses. Tolman, however, argued for intervening variables like "expectations." Fixation represents a hybrid: it begins cognitively (sign-gestalt) but ends mechanically (S-R bond), challenging pure S-R theory.
- **Gestalt Psychology**: Köhler and Wertheimer focused on perceptual wholes and insight learning. Tolman adopted "gestalt" to describe integrated expectations about environments. Fixation arises when such integration breaks down, leading to fragmented, stimulus-driven behavior.
- **Early Experiments**: Studies by Lewin (1935) on topological psychology described "vectors" pulling toward goals; fixation might occur when a vector becomes overly dominant. Also, Hull’s habit strength theory (1943) later incorporated similar ideas, but Tolman identified the phenomenon earlier.
- **Modern Neuroscience**: Research on dorsolateral vs. dorsomedial striatum (e.g., Yin et al., 2004) shows that habits rely on different neural circuits than goal-directed actions. Fixation likely involves a takeover by dorsal-lateral striatal circuits, which encode stimulus-response associations resistant to outcome devaluation.

This lineage shows how Tolman’s observational insights anticipated debates about cognitive vs. associative learning, influencing later dual-system theories in psychology and neuroscience.

- **After Several Successes**: If the right alley (say, white) is often correct initially, the rat may form a stronger association: “Right is good.” This starts as part of the gestalt but can detach.

- **During Disproving Trials**: When right is incorrect, the rat experiences frustration but persists. From its perspective, the mismatch might be attributed to noise or error, not to invalidating the strategy. The habit provides certainty in an uncertain world.

Breaking fixation requires overwhelming evidence or a change in motivational state (e.g., hunger reduction).

This recursive analysis reveals fixation as a self-reinforcing loop: success → confidence → reduced monitoring → automaticity → resistance to update. Clock-wise, fixation might accelerate under stress or fatigue, as cognitive resources dwindle, favoring habitual responses.

Thus, fixation isn’t purely cognitive; it interacts with bodily states and environmental nuances.
