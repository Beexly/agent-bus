The clock itself is the final recursive object. Teams do not merely consume time; they model how officials will classify the reason for stoppage. The source’s timing architecture is thus a recursive control system: each team models the official’s classification, and the official’s classification is designed to defeat unfair exploitation.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof proceeds from the observation that some rule outcomes are not merely adjudicative but physical or environmental. A physical veto occurs when the ball, field, equipment, or weather makes the prescribed action impossible or unsafe. A biomechanical veto occurs when the human body or protective equipment cannot satisfy the safety predicate. A weather veto occurs when environmental conditions require roof closure or suspension.

First, weather veto. The source states: “The roof must be closed if (from 90 minutes before the game until the end of the game) precipitation or lightning is within the vicinity of the stadium, the temperature drops below 40°F (4°C), or wind gusts are greater than 40 miles per hour (64 km/h)” [PAGE 19]. Define the weather veto predicate:

\[
W = P \lor L \lor (T < 4^\circ\text{C}) \lor (v > 64\ \text{km/h})
\]

where:

- \(P\) = precipitation in the vicinity;
- \(L\) = lightning in the vicinity;
- \(T\) = temperature in degrees Celsius;
- \(v\) = wind gust speed in km/h.

The rule is:

\[
W \implies \text{RoofClosed}
\]

If the stadium has no roof, or if the roof cannot be closed, then the game may become unplayable. The source separately states that “The referee may suspend the game temporarily when conditions warrant such action” [PAGE 63]. Therefore, the environmental veto is not discretionary in the roof-closure condition; it is mandatory for a retractable-roof stadium. The breakdown regime is clear: if \(W\) is true and no compliant enclosed playing environment exists, play cannot lawfully continue under the stated condition.

Second, equipment and ball veto. The source requires the ball to be inflated to 12.5–13.5 psi [PAGE 23] and to weigh 14–15 ounces [PAGE 23]. If either condition fails, the ball fails the legality predicate. The source also prohibits equipment modified in a way that reduces protection [PAGE 28]. Thus, a physical veto occurs if mandatory equipment is missing, illegal, or unsafe. The source says a player wearing illegal equipment or failing to wear mandatory equipment must leave the game for at least one down unless corrected or replaced, subject to timeout provisions [PAGE 29]. This is a direct physical-layer veto: the player cannot participate until the equipment predicate is satisfied.

Third, biomechanical veto. The targeting rule uses a geometric definition of the crown: a 6-inch radius from the apex [PAGE 128]. This converts a biomechanical event into an administrable region. Let the helmet apex define a local coordinate system. The crown region is:

\[
C = \{(u,v): u^2 + v^2 \le (0.1524\ \text{m})^2\}
\]

If the initial contact point lies in \(C\) and the contact is forcible, the targeting predicate is triggered. The source does not provide a numerical force threshold; it uses “forcible contact” as a qualitative standard [PAGE 128]. The exact breakdown regime is therefore: if contact is not forcible, no targeting foul under that predicate; if contact is outside the crown and outside the head/neck region of a defenseless player, the specific targeting predicate may not apply; if contact is to a non-defenseless opponent and not with the crown, other personal-foul rules may apply instead.

Fourth, field-geometry veto. The source specifies hash marks at 60 feet from the sidelines [PAGE 19]. In SI units:

\[
60\ \text{ft} = 18.288\ \text{m}
\]

This geometry affects where the ball may be snapped and how side-zone restrictions operate. The source states that the ball may not be snapped in a side zone and must be transferred to the nearest hash mark if the starting point is in a side zone [PAGE 100]. This is a geometric veto on formation and snap location. If the ball spot is in a side zone, the prescribed snap location is invalid unless relocated.

Fifth, medical veto. The source states that an injured player must leave the game and may not return until approved by medical personnel [PAGE 64]. This is a medical veto over participation. Define:

\[
M_i = 1
\]

if medical personnel approve player \(i\) for return. If \(M_i=0\), the player cannot return, regardless of competitive need. The source also says officials should give special attention to concussion signs [PAGE 64]. This creates a safety veto that overrides competitive incentives.

The cross-layer veto proof is therefore:

1. If weather predicate \(W\) is true and no compliant enclosure exists, play is vetoed or suspended [PAGE 19] [PAGE 63].
2. If ball pressure or weight fails specification, the ball is vetoed [PAGE 23].
3. If mandatory equipment is missing or illegal, player participation is vetoed until corrected [PAGE 29].
4. If medical approval is absent after injury, player return is vetoed [PAGE 64].
5. If the ball spot is in a side zone, the prescribed snap is vetoed until relocated to the nearest hash mark [PAGE 100].

These vetoes are not secondary considerations. They are boundary conditions that determine whether the rule system can be executed at all. The source’s safety architecture depends on these physical and environmental predicates being enforced before competitive adjudication proceeds.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
