<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are formal probability, decision, survival, and optimization operators. Units are stated where the paper defines them; where the paper does not assign SI units, I mark the quantity as dimensionless or objective-scale dependent rather than inventing a physical unit.

1. Posterior update. The paper states Bayes’ rule as
\[
p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(D)},\qquad
p(D)=\int p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta .
\]
[PAGE 6]
Here \(p(\theta\mid D)\) is a posterior density, \(p(D\mid\theta)\) is a likelihood, and \(p(D)\) is the evidence. The operator is valid only when the prior is proper and the evidence is positive and finite. Breakdown regimes: an improper prior with a non-integrable likelihood yields an improper posterior; a prior-likelihood conflict can make the evidence numerically unstable; and MCMC diagnostics such as \(\hat R\) and effective sample size are required because the integral is usually intractable [PAGE 9]. The paper uses this operator with \(\theta=(\beta_0,\beta)\) for the logistic model.

2. Posterior predictive operator. For a new outcome \(Y^\ast\) at \(x^\ast\),
\[
p(y^\ast\mid x^\ast,D)=\int p(y^\ast\mid x^\ast,\theta)p(\theta\mid D)\,d\theta .
\]
[PAGE 6]
This is a probability, hence dimensionless and in \([0,1]\) for binary \(Y^\ast\). Assumptions: conditional independence given \(\theta\), a correctly specified likelihood, and a proper posterior. Breakdown: if the new \(x^\ast\) lies outside the training support, the integral can be mathematically finite but scientifically unsupported; if the model is misspecified, the predictive distribution is calibrated to the wrong data-generating mechanism.

3. Logistic link and likelihood. The binary model is
\[
\Pr(Y_i=1\mid x_i,\beta)=\sigma(\eta_i),\qquad
\eta_i=\beta_0+x_i^\top\beta,\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]
[PAGE 9]
The likelihood factorises as
\[
p(y\mid\beta_0,\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^n
\sigma(\eta_i)^{y_i}\left[1-\sigma(\eta_i)\right]^{1-y_i}.
\]
[PAGE 9]
Units: probabilities are dimensionless; \(\beta_j\) has units reciprocal to \(x_j\). Breakdown: under complete or quasi-complete separation, the unregularised maximum-likelihood estimate diverges; the Bayesian model with a proper Gaussian prior keeps the posterior proper, but the prior scale \(\tau\) controls how strongly separation is suppressed. The paper’s weakly informative prior is
\[
\beta_0\sim N(0,\tau_0^2),\qquad \beta_j\sim N(0,\tau^2).
\]
[PAGE 9]

4. Monte Carlo posterior predictive mean. The paper approximates the predictive probability by
\[
\hat p^\ast=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S
\sigma\left(\beta_0^{(s)}+x^{\ast\top}\beta^{(s)}\right).
\]
[PAGE 9]
This is dimensionless. Breakdown: small \(S\) gives Monte Carlo error; posterior draws from a poorly converged chain give biased intervals; the approximation does not repair model misspecification.

5. Calibration bin operators. For decile bin \(b\),
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{Pred}}_b=\frac{1}{n_b}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{bin}_i=b}\hat p_i,\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Obs}}_b=\frac{1}{n_b}\sum_{i:\,\mathrm{bin}_i=b}y_i.
\]
[PAGE 10]
Both are dimensionless proportions. Assumption: \(n_b\gt 0\). Breakdown: empty bins make the ratio undefined; small \(n_b\) makes the observed proportion high-variance; bin choice can hide localized miscalibration. The paper’s own Pima table shows a localized anomaly: “the fifth decile (mean predicted risk ≈0.23) exhibits a substantially higher observed prevalence (≈0.44)” [PAGE 29].

6. Bayesian decision threshold. With false-positive cost \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}\) and false-negative cost \(C_{\mathrm{FN}}\), the paper states: “The Bayes-optimal decision is to screen if \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}(1-\hat p^\ast) \gt C_{\mathrm{FN}}\hat p^\ast\)” [PAGE 10], equivalently
\[
\hat p^\ast\ge t^\star=\frac{C_{\mathrm{FP}}}{C_{\mathrm{FP}}+C_{\mathrm{FN}}}.
\]
[PAGE 10]
The threshold is dimensionless because costs cancel. Assumptions: costs are non-negative, additive, and correctly specified; the denominator is positive. Breakdown: if \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}+C_{\mathrm{FN}}=0\), the threshold is undefined; if costs change, the decision rule changes even when the posterior is unchanged. The paper’s example uses \(C_{\mathrm{FN}}=9\), \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}=1\), giving \(t^\star=0.10\) [PAGE 30].

7. Cox hazard and partial likelihood operator. The Cox model is
\[
h(t\mid X_i)=h_0(t)\exp(X_i^\top\beta),
\]
[PAGE 11]
where \(h(t\mid X_i)\) and \(h_0(t)\) have units of inverse time, and the exponential factor is dimensionless. Assumption: proportional hazards. Breakdown: if hazard ratios are time-varying, the model is misspecified; if the baseline hazard is estimated on a sparse event grid, variance can be large.

8. Elastic-net penalty operator. The paper defines
\[
P_{\alpha,\lambda}(\beta)=\lambda\left\{
\alpha\sum_{j=1}^p|\beta_j|
+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^p\beta_j^2
\right\},\qquad
\lambda\gt 0,\quad \alpha\in[0,1].
\]
[PAGE 11]
The paper says “\(\lambda\) controls the overall strength of regularisation, and \(\alpha\) balances between ridge (\(\alpha=0\)) and lasso (\(\alpha=1\)) components” [PAGE 11]. \(\alpha\) is dimensionless. \(\lambda\) is expressed on the objective or partial-likelihood scale; the paper does not assign it an SI unit, so it should not be interpreted as an inverse-time physical rate. Breakdown: \(\lambda=0\) removes penalisation; \(\alpha=0\) is pure ridge; \(\alpha=1\) is pure lasso; strong correlation among covariates can still produce unstable coefficient paths.

9. Concordance operator. Harrell’s C-index is defined over comparable pairs \(P\):
\[
\hat C=\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{(i,j)\in P}
\mathbf{1}\{T_i\lt T_j,\delta_i=1,r_i\gt r_j\}
+\frac{1}{2|P|}\sum_{(i,j)\in P}
\mathbf{1}\{T_i\lt T_j,\delta_i=1,r_i=r_j\}.
\]
[PAGE 36]
This is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). Assumptions: there is at least one comparable pair; censoring is handled by the comparable-pair definition. Breakdown: \(|P|=0\) makes the index undefined; very small \(|P|\) gives high variance; ties receive half credit; censoring that is informative can bias the estimand.

10. Gaussian-process surrogate and acquisition operator. The paper assumes
\[
f(\theta)\sim\mathcal{GP}\left(m(\theta),k(\theta,\theta')\right),
\]
[PAGE 12]
and uses the upper-confidence-bound rule
\[
a_T(\theta)=\mu_T(\theta)+\kappa s_T(\theta).
\]
[PAGE 12]
Here \(f(\theta)\) is the validation C-index, dimensionless; \(s_T(\theta)\) has the same units as \(f\); \(\kappa\) is a dimensionless exploration parameter. Assumptions: \(\Theta\) is a compact hyper-rectangle, the kernel encodes relevant smoothness, and noise is zero-mean. Breakdown: a badly chosen kernel or non-stationary surface can mislead the surrogate; repeated use of the same validation set can overfit the acquisition process; \(\kappa=0\) removes exploration, while very large \(\kappa\) over-explores.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicit. It descends from Bayesian decision theory and Bayesian data analysis [PAGE 6], generalized linear models [PAGE 6], Cox survival analysis [PAGE 7], Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization [PAGE 7], and calibration-focused machine learning [PAGE 4–5]. It also inherits the AutoML and clinical-risk-prediction line through AutoPrognosis and AutoPrognosis 2.0 [PAGE 3], and the epidemiological forecasting line that emphasizes probabilistic forecasts and decision-relevant metrics [PAGE 4]. The paper’s novelty claim is a synthesis: Bayesian inference governs the predictive layer, while Bayesian optimization governs the model-search layer [PAGE 5]. The retrocausal genealogy matters because each ancestor carries a different failure mode. Bayesian decision theory assumes a known loss function; if the cost ratio changes, the threshold changes. GLM theory assumes the link and likelihood are adequate; if separation occurs, MLE breaks. Cox theory assumes proportional hazards; if hazards cross, the C-index can still rank but the model is misspecified. GP optimization assumes a smooth, stationary-ish response surface; if the validation C-index is noisy or the validation set is reused, the surrogate can chase noise. Calibration theory assumes the evaluation distribution matches deployment; if prevalence shifts, a once-calibrated model can become miscalibrated. Multi-week traps are therefore not abstract. Week 1: fit on Pima and observe good aggregate calibration. Week 2: a local clinic has different prevalence and the fifth-decile gap becomes larger than the original table. Week 3: a cost review changes \(C_{\mathrm{FN}}\) and \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}\), so the old \(t^\star\) is no longer Bayes-optimal. Week 4: a new covariate or missingness pattern shifts the support. Week 5: repeated Bayesian optimization on the same validation split selects a hyperparameter that looks good because of validation reuse rather than because of stable concordance. The paper’s own numbers illustrate the modesty of the survival gain: oracle 0.777, baseline 0.774, BO 0.776 [PAGE 17]. That is not a mandate for unlimited tuning; it is evidence for careful, bounded tuning. The correct genealogical lesson is to inherit the formal operators but also inherit their audit requirements: separation checks, calibration checks, comparable-pair checks, cost sensitivity, and out-of-sample validation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has at least four nested beliefs: the patient’s latent risk, the clinician’s belief about that risk, the clinician’s belief about the model’s uncertainty, and the policymaker’s belief about the clinician’s decision rule. The paper makes the first two explicit through posterior predictive intervals. Table 5 shows “intermediate-risk individuals with wider intervals, where the posterior explicitly encodes epistemic uncertainty” [PAGE 27–28]. That is the counter-trap resource: uncertainty can prevent a false collapse to a binary label. But crisis habit regression is the opposite pattern: under time pressure, a clinician or triage team reverts to a point estimate and a habitual cutoff. The paper’s threshold \(t^\star=C_{\mathrm{FP}}/(C_{\mathrm{FP}}+C_{\mathrm{FN}})\) [PAGE 10] is exactly the kind of habituated rule that can be used well or badly. Used well, it forces cost explicitness. Used badly, it becomes a ritualized number detached from the current cost structure. The recursive theory-of-mind failure is that the clinician may treat the model’s credible interval as a property of the patient rather than a property of the model-data relation; the policymaker may then treat the clinician’s threshold as a property of the disease rather than a property of the loss function. The counter-trap is overcorrection: demanding near-certainty before acting, which can be worse than a calibrated expected-loss rule when false negatives are costly. The clock is the temporal dimension: screening decisions have deadlines, model calibration drifts, and Bayesian optimization has a finite evaluation budget. The paper’s GBSG2 example uses an initial space-filling design and subsequent acquisition steps [PAGE 30], which is a clock-bounded search. The correct recursive policy is: update the posterior, compute expected loss, check calibration and support, then act at the deadline. Do not let a wide interval automatically veto action; do not let a point estimate automatically authorize action. The decision should be governed by posterior expected loss and verified calibration, not by the emotional salience of uncertainty.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is admissible only if the external layer is represented in the model’s support, prior, likelihood, or loss. The paper’s Pima model includes pregnancies, plasma glucose, diastolic blood pressure, triceps skinfold thickness, two-hour serum insulin, BMI, diabetes pedigree function, and age [PAGE 8]. The GBSG2 model includes hormonal therapy, age, menopausal status, tumour size and grade, number of positive nodes, progesterone receptor, and oestrogen receptor measurements [PAGE 8]. There is no weather variable, no biomechanical load variable, and no physical exposure variable in either model. Therefore a weather veto or biomechanical veto cannot be adjudicated from the paper’s conditional predictive distribution; it is an out-of-support claim unless it is encoded as an additional covariate, prior, or loss term. The formal proof is simple: the model estimates \(p(y\mid x,D)\) for the listed \(x\). If an external variable \(z\) affects \(y\) and is correlated with \(x\), then \(p(y\mid x,D)\) need not equal \(p(y\mid x,z,D)\); the difference is a omitted-variable bias. The paper’s calibration diagnostics can detect the symptom but cannot identify the cause. A physical or biomechanical veto would require a constrained model, a different likelihood, or a new covariate set. A weather veto would require temporal or spatial covariates and out-of-distribution validation. The only cross-layer vetoes that the source can support are statistical: support failure, calibration failure, coverage failure, separation failure, comparable-pair starvation, and cost-rule mismatch. For example, if the calibration slope departs from 1 or the observed prevalence in a bin departs from the predicted mean beyond sampling error, the deployment should be vetoed until recalibrated. If \(|P|=0\) in the C-index calculation, the survival objective is undefined. If the cost ratio changes, the threshold \(t^\star\) must be recomputed. These are verifiable within the paper’s own operators. Claims about weather or biomechanics would require evidence not present in the source.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I define an RLVR-style veto as a verifiable-reward counterfactual: a deployment is rejected if a specified diagnostic fails under a counterfactual perturbation that can be checked from the paper’s own outputs or from rerunning the pipeline. Three scenarios follow.

Scenario 1: Logistic separation veto. Counterfactual: a Pima covariate or a linear combination of covariates separates diabetes status in the training split, so the unregularised MLE coefficient grows without bound. Verification: check the R glm convergence warning, coefficient magnitude, standard error, and calibration slope. Prediction: the MLE log-loss becomes unstable and the calibration slope collapses below 1, while the Bayesian model with the Gaussian prior remains finite but may still be overconfident if the prior scale is too large. Veto rule: do not deploy the MLE model if separation diagnostics fire; use the Bayesian or penalised model and report posterior intervals. This is verifiable because the paper already contrasts Bayesian and MLE behaviour and reports MLE instability in the high-dimensional regime: log-loss 1.308 with standard deviation 1.293 [PAGE 23].

Scenario 2: Comparable-pair starvation veto. Counterfactual: the GBSG2 validation split has very few comparable pairs because of heavy censoring, so Harrell’s C-index is either undefined at \(|P|=0\) or extremely noisy at small \(|P|\). Verification: compute \(|P|\), bootstrap the C-index, and inspect the width of the validation interval. Prediction: Bayesian optimization will chase noise, and the selected \((\log\lambda,\alpha)\) may have a C-index near 0.5 or a spuriously high value that does not replicate. Veto rule: reject BO-selected hyperparameters when the comparable-pair count is too small to support the claimed concordance; refit with a larger validation set or a different survival metric. This is verifiable from the C-index definition [PAGE 36] and from the paper’s use of validation C-index as the BO objective [PAGE 11–12].

Scenario 3: Calibration-shift and cost-threshold veto. Counterfactual: deployment prevalence differs from the Pima test split, or the cost ratio changes from the paper’s \(C_{\mathrm{FN}}=9\), \(C_{\mathrm{FP}}=1\) example that yields \(t^\star=0.10\) [PAGE 30]. Verification: recompute decile calibration, compare observed and predicted proportions, and recompute \(t^\star\) under the new costs. Prediction: the Pima fifth-decile anomaly, where predicted risk is about 0.23 but observed prevalence is about 0.44 [PAGE 29], will worsen or move to another bin; the old threshold will no longer be Bayes-optimal. Veto rule: block automatic screening until the model is recalibrated and the threshold is re-derived from current costs. This is the most operationally important veto because it is verifiable with a simple calibration table and a cost update, and it directly tests the paper’s central promise of calibrated risk and honest uncertainty.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
