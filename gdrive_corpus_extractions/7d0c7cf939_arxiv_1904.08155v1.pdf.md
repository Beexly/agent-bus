The source defines the scientific object as a mapping from a chessboard image to a probability-like saliency field that predicts where human chess players fixate. The authors explicitly frame chess attention as neither purely bottom-up nor purely top-down: “visual attention in Chess can be seen as a combination of these two approaches as salient areas may be defined by both visual pattern recognition (bottom-up analysis) and an understanding of the game (top-down prediction)” [PAGE 2]. That sentence is the causal root of the entire derivation: if attention depends on both image features and chess knowledge, then a model must learn local piece appearance, spatial relations, and task-relevant move intentions.

The first multi-hop derivation is therefore: (1) human gaze data produces empirical saliency maps; (2) those maps are too sparse and too few; (3) the authors augment or synthesize additional data; (4) the network is trained under several pretraining and fine-tuning regimes; (5) performance is evaluated with multiple saliency metrics; (6) the results show a tradeoff between correlation-style metrics and threshold-based discrimination metrics. This chain is supported by the source: the initial eye-tracking corpus had “11 configurations and the same amount of saliency maps” [PAGE 5], and the authors state that “the saliency maps are too sparse to let a neural network converge” [PAGE 5]. They respond by averaging player maps: “we decided to group each task into one saliency map, averaging all saliency maps from all players computed from eye tracking data” [PAGE 5]. They then apply transformations: sliding windows of 3x3, 4x4, and 5x5 squares, color inversion, and board orientation reversal. The final augmented set “contains 6600 examples” [PAGE 6]. A second corpus, GDS, is generated from Lichess PGN games: “more than 22 Million for March 2018” [PAGE 6], and from a subset the authors created “a bit more than one hundred and twenty thousand new images for training” [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit begins with the fact that the 6600 augmented examples are not 6600 independent human observations. They are transformations of 11 base configurations. Formally, the sample space expands, but the latent chess situations do not expand at the same rate. This matters because the paper’s training/evaluation protocol is fragile: “we cannot create a validation set without drastically reducing the size of the training set” and “we kept 9 configurations as training data and used the 2 lasts, the more complex and difficult ones, as test configurations” [PAGE 7]. That protocol protects against training on the same problem, but it creates high variance when only two test configurations remain. The table later reports standard deviations, e.g. V4 has “0.69±0.09” AUC-Judd and “0.74±0.10” AUC-Borji [PAGE 8], which is consistent with unstable evaluation.

The second gray-zone issue is metric conflict. The paper reports LCC, SIM, NSS, AUC-Judd, and AUC-Borji [PAGE 8]. These metrics do not measure the same property. LCC measures linear association between continuous maps; SIM measures normalized overlap; NSS evaluates normalized saliency at fixation points; AUC measures thresholded discrimination between fixated and non-fixated pixels. The source says V2 and V4 are “the best models looking globally at all metrics” [PAGE 8], but the table shows V2 has higher LCC and SIM while V4 has higher AUC-Judd and AUC-Borji. Therefore, any claim that one model is universally better is underdetermined unless the application prioritizes correlation, overlap, or pixel discrimination.

The third gray-zone issue is domain shift. CAT2000 pretraining improves scores: “pretraining our model on the CAT2000 dataset appears to give better results” [PAGE 8]. Yet CAT2000 consists of natural images, while chess boards are synthetic, grid-structured, and piece-label dependent. The source itself reports that CAT2000-pretrained V4 “was a bit struggling to generalize to new configurations, displaying some salient areas around the boundaries of the board” [PAGE 10]. This is a classic transfer-learning trap: natural-image features can improve optimization and low-level saliency priors, but they can also import inappropriate edge or texture expectations.

The fourth gray-zone issue is synthetic-label bias. GDS saliency maps are not measured human gaze; they are constructed from chess move metadata. The authors state three hypotheses: the player looks at the moving piece and its destination, the path of the moved piece has lower saliency, and checking moves increase saliency [PAGE 6]. They also admit biases: “the vast majority of them were openings”, “Pawns being the vast majority of them, they are often way more salient because they are moved more often”, and “The king being not very often moved nor the center of attention ... had a limited saliency” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, GDS is useful for teaching piece relations, but it cannot be treated as an unbiased estimate of human attention.

The final derivation is a decision-theoretic one. If the target is human-like average attention over these limited chess tasks, V2 or V4 are plausible depending on the metric. If the target is robust pixel-level discrimination, V4 is stronger. If the target is dense correlation with continuous human saliency, V2 is stronger. If the target is ecological generalization to arbitrary chess positions, the paper’s own limitations warn against overclaiming because the eye-tracking corpus is small, the test set is tiny, and the synthetic corpus has content bias.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let the input chessboard image be
\[
X \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}, \qquad X_{u,v,c} \in [0,1],
\]
where \(H\) and \(W\) are image height and width in pixels, and \(c\) indexes color channels. The board itself is an \(8 \times 8\) grid of squares, but the model operates on pixel coordinates unless explicitly cropped into subviews. The source uses sliding windows of “3x3, 4x4 and 5x5 squares” to create subviews [PAGE 5].

The model output is a saliency map
\[
F_\theta(X) \in [0,1]^{H \times W},
\]
where each pixel value represents predicted visual-attention probability or saliency intensity. The source states: “For each pixel, a probability between 0 (black) and 1 (white) is computed” [PAGE 2]. For metric computation, saliency maps are often normalized into probability distributions:
\[
\widehat{S}(p) = \frac{S(p)}{\sum_{q} S(q)}, \qquad \sum_p \widehat{S}(p)=1,
\]
with \(p\) denoting a pixel location. The normalized map is dimensionless. The unnormalized map is also treated as dimensionless saliency intensity, but not necessarily a normalized probability mass.

Ground-truth saliency from eye tracking can be formalized as a Gaussian-smoothed fixation-duration field. Let fixations be
\[
\mathcal{F} = \{(p_i, d_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_f},
\]
where \(p_i\) is a fixation location in pixels and \(d_i\) is fixation duration in seconds. A raw saliency field is
\[
G_{\text{raw}}(p) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_f} d_i \, K_\sigma(p-p_i),
\]
where \(K_\sigma\) is a Gaussian kernel with spatial scale \(\sigma\) measured in pixels. If \(K_\sigma\) is normalized as a 2D Gaussian, its units are pixel\(^{-2}\), while \(d_i\) has units of seconds; after normalization, the final map is dimensionless. The source says fixation data are “often transformed to continuous saliency maps by convolution with a Gaussian function where intensity indicates duration of fixation” [PAGE 2].

Breakdown regime for this operator: if \(\sigma \to 0\), the map approaches a set of delta-like spikes and becomes numerically sparse. If many pixels have zero or near-zero saliency, the variance of \(G\) becomes small, destabilizing correlation metrics. The source directly reports this failure mode: “the saliency maps are too sparse to let a neural network converge” [PAGE 5].

The Kullback-Leibler divergence used as a conceptual loss is
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(G \,\|\, F_\theta(X)) = \sum_p G(p) \log \frac{G(p)}{F_\theta(X;p)},
\]
with units of nats if natural logarithm is used, or bits if base-2 logarithm is used. The source notes that KL divergence “does not respect the triangle inequality: it is not symmetrical, it does not have an upper bound” [PAGE 4]. Singularity: if \(G(p) > 0\) and \(F_\theta(X;p) = 0\), the divergence is infinite.

The actual training loss is binary cross entropy:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}} =
-\frac{1}{|P|}
\sum_{p \in P}
\left[
G(p)\log F_\theta(X;p)
+
(1-G(p))\log(1-F_\theta(X;p))
\right].
\]
This is dimensionless when averaged over pixel set \(P\). The source states: “Binary cross entropy is used as a loss function in the experiments described below” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: if \(F_\theta(X;p)\) saturates at 0 or 1 while the label is opposite, the logarithm diverges or the gradient vanishes, producing numerical instability or stalled learning.

The linear correlation coefficient is
\[
\mathrm{LCC}(G,F) =
\frac{\sum_p (G(p)-\mu_G)(F(p)-\mu_F)}
{\sigma_G \sigma_F},
\]
where \(\mu_G,\mu_F\) are means and \(\sigma_G,\sigma_F\) are standard deviations. LCC is dimensionless and ranges from \(-1\) to \(+1\). The source says it “ranges between -1 and +1” and that symmetric computation means it “does not make the difference between false positives and false negatives” [PAGE 8]. Singularity: if \(\sigma_G = 0\) or \(\sigma_F = 0\), LCC is undefined.

The similarity metric is
\[
\mathrm{SIM}(G,F) = \sum_p \min(\widehat{G}(p), \widehat{F}(p)),
\]
where \(\widehat{G}\) and \(\widehat{F}\) are normalized saliency maps. SIM is dimensionless and ranges from 0 to 1. The source defines it as “the sum of minimums of two normalized saliency maps” with “a null value” meaning no overlap and “1” meaning identical maps [PAGE 8]. Singularity: if a map has zero total mass, normalization is undefined.

Normalized Scanpath Saliency can be written as
\[
\mathrm{NSS}(S,\mathcal{F}) =
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}
\sum_{p \in \mathcal{F}}
\frac{S(p)-\mu_S}{\sigma_S},
\]
with units of standard deviations relative to the saliency map distribution. The source says NSS “was introduced by Peters et al. [2005] as a correspondence measure between a normalized saliency map and a ground truth at fixation points” and that “a mean subtraction step is added to gain robustness against any kind of linear transformations” [PAGE 8]. It also states that a positive value indicates “correspondence above chance” and a negative value indicates “anti-correspondence” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: if \(\sigma_S = 0\) or if there are no fixation points, NSS is undefined.

The AUC operator treats saliency as a binary classifier by thresholding:
\[
\hat{y}_\tau(p) = \mathbf{1}\{F_\theta(X;p) \ge \tau\},
\]
where \(\tau \in [0,1]\). The ROC curve plots true positive rate against false positive rate as \(\tau\) varies. The area under this curve is
\[
\mathrm{AUC} = \int_0^1 \mathrm{TPR}(\mathrm{FPR}^{-1}(u))\,du,
\]
which is dimensionless and ranges from 0 to 1. The source explains that for saliency evaluation, “we consider the saliency map as a binary classifier, varying the threshold to transform probabilities to \{0,1\} values” [PAGE 8]. AUC-Judd and AUC-Borji differ in how false positives are sampled: AUC-Borji “computes the false positive rate as the ratio of values above a threshold sampled at random pixels” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: if all pixels are labeled positive or all negative, ROC and AUC are degenerate.

The data augmentation cardinality can be expressed as a set transformation. If \(T_k\) denotes an augmentation operator acting jointly on image and ground truth,
\[
(X',G') = T_k(X,G),
\]
then the augmented ETDS satisfies
\[
|\mathrm{ETDSa}| = |\mathrm{ETDS}| \times N_{\text{window}} \times N_{\text{color}} \times N_{\text{orientation}}.
\]
The source gives \(|\mathrm{ETDS}| = 11\) configurations [PAGE 5], window augmentation multiplying data by 150, color inversion by 2, and orientation change by 2, yielding “6600 examples” [PAGE 6]. However, these are transformed copies of a small base set, so the effective number of independent chess situations is much smaller than 6600.

The GDS construction is described qualitatively as highlighting the moving piece’s origin and destination, the traversed path with lower intensity, and checking relations [PAGE 6]. No calibrated numerical weights are provided, so the only defensible formal representation is set-based:
\[
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{GDS}} =
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{origin}}
\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{destination}}
\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{path}}
\cup
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{check}},
\]
with intensity ordering origin/destination greater than path, and check-related elements added when applicable. This is not a fitted probabilistic human-gaze model; it is a synthetic supervision operator. Breakdown: because openings dominate and pawns are frequent, the induced distribution over piece types and game phases is biased [PAGE 6].

Training hyperparameters include “2000 epochs with a learning rate of 0.01” [PAGE 7]. A generic update is
\[
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\theta_t),
\]
where \(\eta = 0.01\) is dimensionless. Breakdown can occur if learning rate is too large for the decoder, but the source only says this value was selected experimentally [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of this paper is a convergence of three traditions: attention neuroscience, chess expertise psychology, and deep saliency prediction. The source cites Posner and Petersen [1990] for the claim that visual attention is not a single brain area but a network of components: “visual attention is not the function of a single area of the brain but rather the result of a network of different components” [PAGE 2]. That lineage establishes attention as distributed and selective.

The chess-specific lineage is more important. Charness et al. [2001] found that experts fixate empty squares more and encode configurations rather than individual pieces; the source summarizes: “experts were encoding configurations rather than pieces and produced patterns of saccades” [PAGE 3]. Reingold and Charness [2005] showed experts use larger visual spans and “focus on relations between pieces rather than fixate a large number of individual pieces” [PAGE 3]. Sheridan and Reingold [2017] argued that chess expertise is related to rapid recognition of complex patterns, allowing experts to “detect interesting patterns by looking at their center, using peripheral vision to detect the configuration” [PAGE 3]. This lineage creates a retro-causal constraint on any model: a chessboard saliency model that only highlights individual pieces may be modeling novice-like attention, not expert-like attention.

The computer-vision lineage comes from saliency models such as Huang et al. [2015], Kruthiventi et al. [2015], Wang and Shen [2017], and datasets such as MIT300, CAT2000, and ImageNet-pretrained VGG16. The source says the encoder is “a VGG16 pretrained on ImageNet” and the decoder uses deconvolution layers to upsample feature maps [PAGE 4]. It also says three feature maps are decoded and combined through a “fusion layer” [PAGE 4]. This places the paper within the deep saliency tradition where multi-scale features and skip connections are used to recover spatial detail.

A multi-week trap in this genealogy is the false assumption that natural-image pretraining automatically transfers to chessboard attention. The paper finds a benefit: “deep features, pretrained on natural images, were found to be helpful in training visual attention prediction for chess” [PAGE 1]. But it also finds boundary artifacts: CAT2000-pretrained V4 shows “some salient areas around the boundaries of the board” [PAGE 10]. The historical trap is to treat ImageNet or CAT2000 features as neutral. They are not neutral; they carry priors about natural scenes, textures, and object boundaries.

A second multi-week trap is treating synthetic chess supervision as equivalent to human gaze. GDS is generated from PGN move sequences, not from eye tracking. The source admits the first hypothesis is that players look at the moving piece and destination, the second is that they follow the path, and the third is that checks are salient [PAGE 6]. These are plausible, but they are author hypotheses. The source also admits content bias: “the vast majority of them were openings” and “Pawns being the vast majority of them, they are often way more salient because they are moved more often” [PAGE 6]. The retro-causal danger is that a network trained on GDS may learn chess-move frequency rather than human fixation probability.

A third multi-week trap is the illusion of scale through augmentation. The augmented ETDS becomes 6600 examples, but the base corpus is only 11 configurations [PAGE 5-6]. If a researcher treats augmentation as independent evidence, they may overstate generalization. The correct retro-causal reading is: augmentation increases transform invariance, but not necessarily ecological validity. The source itself says the eye-tracking corpus is limited: “the available recordings of eye tracking data in chess context represent a huge amount of work and hours of data. Nevertheless, they contain few configurations” [PAGE 12].

A fourth historical trap is metric genealogical confusion. The paper inherits metrics from saliency research: LCC, SIM, NSS, AUC-Judd, AUC-Borji. Each metric has a different history and different failure modes. The source cites Bylinskii et al. [2016] and Le Meur and Baccino [2013] for metric interpretation [PAGE 7]. A multi-week trap is to treat the highest number in one metric as scientific victory. The paper’s own results resist that simplification: V2 is best by LCC, while V4 is best by AUC metrics [PAGE 8].

Thus, the retro-causal genealogy says: the paper is valid as a baseline, but not as a settled model of chess attention. Its lineage includes human expertise, sparse eye tracking, synthetic game data, natural-image pretraining, and metric pluralism. Each ancestor imposes a constraint. Human expertise warns against piece-only saliency. Sparse eye tracking warns against overconfident generalization. Synthetic GDS warns against move-frequency bias. Natural-image pretraining warns against boundary artifacts. Metric pluralism warns against single-number adjudication.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit asks how the model, the evaluator, and the human player infer each other’s attentional intentions under crisis, habit, and temporal pressure. In this source, the crisis is not a sports contest but an epistemic crisis: the model must predict where humans look in a sparse, task-dependent domain.

Crisis habit regression occurs when a learner falls back on familiar cues under uncertainty. In this paper, two habit regressions are visible. First, the GDS-pretrained network may regress to frequently moved pieces because “Pawns being the vast majority of them, they are often way more salient because they are moved more often” [PAGE 6]. Second, the CAT2000-pretrained network may regress to natural-image saliency habits, such as boundary or texture saliency, which the source observes as “salient areas around the boundaries of the board” [PAGE 10]. These are not merely errors; they are learned priors becoming dominant when the chess-specific signal is weak.

The counter-trap is to evaluate under conditions that expose the regression. The paper does this partially by testing on the “2 lasts, the more complex and difficult ones” [PAGE 7], and by qualitatively inspecting synthetic configurations where the correct attentional relation is known. For example, in a simple check configuration, the expected fixation pattern includes the two pieces and the relation between them [PAGE 9]. In another configuration, the expected salient areas include the king, rooks, queen, and checking knight [PAGE 9]. These qualitative probes act as counterfactual tests: if the model highlights only local pieces and misses relations, it has regressed to a shallow habit.

The clock is a formal part of attention because fixations have durations and scan paths have temporal order. The source says fixations are “characterized by the time spent on different parts of the scene” [PAGE 2]. A temporal saliency field can be written as
\[
A_T(p) = \int_0^T s(p,t)\,dt,
\]
where \(s(p,t)\) is instantaneous attentional weighting and \(T\) is task or recording duration. The source reports average recording time per participant as “13:35 minutes” with min, max, and standard deviation also given [PAGE 5]. The task set includes N-Check-Mate problems with increasing difficulty, from N=1 to N=6 [PAGE 5]. This means the clock matters: harder problems require longer search, and if the recording ends before solution, the scan path may truncate before the most relevant squares are fixated.

A recursive model of the evaluator’s mind must also consider expertise. The source states: “the saliency of individual chess pieces depends on the chess expertise of the player” [PAGE 3]. Experts “fixate more often on empty squares than intermediate players” and “display a much larger number of fixations on relevant pieces” [PAGE 3]. But Reingold and Charness are also cited for the finding that experts make fewer fixations and focus on relations [PAGE 3]. These statements are not contradictory once temporal scale and task are considered: experts may make fewer but more informative fixations, with larger visual spans and better peripheral encoding. A model trained on averaged maps across many expertise levels may blur this distinction. The source acknowledges this: “This specificity of visual attention of player depending on their skills is a difficulty to address in this research” [PAGE 3].

The counter-trap to expertise regression is to stratify by player skill or by configuration complexity. The paper suggests future separation into beginner, intermediate, and expert networks: “one could have different networks pretrained on the whole data, but adjusted on data from different player groups” [PAGE 12]. Until then, any single model should be interpreted as an average, not as a universal chess-attention operator.

The clock also appears in training. The systems are trained for “2000 epochs with a learning rate of 0.01” [PAGE 7]. If the optimization clock is too short or the learning rate poorly chosen, the decoder may fail to reconstruct fine spatial relations. If the evaluation clock is too short, the model may only learn coarse piece saliency rather than multi-step tactical relations. The source’s qualitative examples show this tension: V3 and V4 sometimes focus on the right pieces but miss the relation between them. For example, in a simple check configuration, “V3 is close to this expected result but with some salient areas on the left part of the board” and “V4 shows less saliency between the two pieces” [PAGE 9]. This is a clock-related failure: the model detects endpoints but not the temporal or spatial path connecting them.

The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is: the model is not merely predicting pixels; it is implicitly modeling a player’s search process. If the evaluator assumes the model should predict expert attention, the model may fail because it was trained on mixed expertise and synthetic move intentions. If the evaluator assumes the model predicts beginner-like attention, GDS-pretrained V3 may appear more plausible because it shows “smaller salient areas focused on pieces” [PAGE 12]. If the evaluator assumes robust generalization, the two test configurations and high variance warn against overconfidence. The correct stance is to treat the model as a conditional predictor: conditional on training corpus, metric, and player expertise distribution.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof. Let the chessboard image domain be \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}\). A valid saliency map must satisfy
\[
F_\theta(X;p) \in [0,1] \quad \forall p,
\]
because the source defines saliency as a pixel probability: “For each pixel, a probability between 0 (black) and 1 (white) is computed” [PAGE 2]. If a model outputs negative saliency, saliency greater than one, or undefined values outside the board mask, the output is physically invalid. If the board image is misoriented, the physical coordinate system is broken. The source uses orientation augmentation: “the last augmentation changes the orientation of the board to the point of view of the other player” [PAGE 6]. This is valid only if the orientation transform is consistently applied to image and saliency map. If training uses white perspective but evaluation uses black perspective without transformation, the physical mapping from squares to pixels is invalid and should be vetoed.

A second physical constraint is normalization. If saliency is treated as a probability distribution for SIM or certain AUC procedures, then
\[
\widehat{S}(p) \ge 0, \qquad \sum_p \widehat{S}(p)=1.
\]
If the sum is zero or numerically unstable, SIM and normalized metrics fail. The source’s SIM definition requires normalized maps: “the sum of minimums of two normalized saliency maps” [PAGE 8]. A model that produces all-zero saliency for an unseen board is physically and operationally invalid for those metrics.

Biomechanical veto proof. Human gaze is constrained by eye movements. The source says eye tracking records “the locations in a visual scene that receive fixation as well as the path between fixations (scan path)” [PAGE 2]. Fixations have durations, and saccades connect them. If a predicted saliency map places all mass on one pixel with no plausible scan-path continuity, or if it ignores the fact that players often fixate empty squares and relations among pieces, it may be biomechanically implausible for expert chess attention. The source states: “experts fixate more often on empty squares than intermediate players” and experts “focus on relations between pieces rather than fixate a large number of individual pieces” [PAGE 3].

Formally, let a scan path be a sequence
\[
\pi = (p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k),
\]
with fixation durations \(d_i \ge 0\). A plausible saliency map should place non-negligible mass in neighborhoods of the \(p_i\). If the model’s high-saliency regions are disjoint from all recorded fixation regions in a test configuration, the biomechanical correspondence is weak. This does not prove the model is wrong, because the model predicts average attention, but it triggers a veto against claims of human-like expert attention.

A biomechanical singularity occurs when the ground-truth fixation set is empty or extremely small. NSS becomes undefined if there are no fixation points or if the saliency map has zero variance. The source reports sparse maps as a convergence problem: “the saliency maps are too sparse to let a neural network converge” [PAGE 5]. This is both a statistical and biomechanical breakdown: the observable gaze evidence is too weak to constrain the model.

Weather veto proof. The source contains no weather variable, no outdoor environmental covariate, and no meteorological measurement. The data come from eye-tracking experiments and online PGN games [PAGE 5-6]. Therefore, any claim that weather, temperature, humidity, wind, or illumination outdoors influences the model’s predictions is unsupported by the source. Let \(W\) denote weather state. Since the source provides no observable mapping \(P(W \mid X)\) or \(P(S \mid W,X),\] any inference depending on \(W\) must be vetoed as unidentifiable. This is not a physical impossibility in the world; it is a source-identifiability failure. The source’s domain is controlled chess-task observation, not environmental meteorology.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: a result survives only if it satisfies image-domain validity, saliency-bound validity, biomechanical plausibility, and source-identifiability. Under this rule, the paper’s quantitative results are admissible as baseline evidence, but broad claims such as universal chess-attention prediction, expert-specific prediction without expertise labels, or environment-dependent prediction are vetoed. The admissible conclusion is narrow: the proposed architecture can learn useful chessboard saliency patterns from limited eye-tracking data, augmented eye-tracking data, and synthetic game data, with performance depending on pretraining corpus and evaluation metric [PAGE 1, PAGE 8].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR counterfactual veto 1: metric monoculture veto. Suppose the reward signal is only LCC. The verifiable counterfactual is: if only LCC is rewarded, the selected model should be V2, because V2 has LCC 0.66, while V4 has LCC 0.57 [PAGE 8]. However, V4 has stronger AUC performance: AUC-Judd 0.69 versus V2’s 0.61, and AUC-Borji 0.74 versus V2’s 0.70 [PAGE 8]. Prediction: an LCC-only selection procedure will prefer V2, but on a pixel-discrimination task V4 will classify fixated versus non-fixated pixels better. Verification: recompute model rankings using the full metric vector \((\mathrm{LCC}, \mathrm{SIM}, \mathrm{NSS}, \mathrm{AUC\text{-}Judd}, \mathrm{AUC\text{-}Borji})\). If LCC-only ranking conflicts with AUC ranking, the monoculture reward is vetoed. This veto is already supported by the source’s statement that “V2 and V4 are the best models looking globally at all metrics” [PAGE 8], which implies no single metric dominates.

RLVR counterfactual veto 2: sparse-evaluation overconfidence veto. Suppose the reward signal is based on the reported cross-validation table without accounting for the tiny base corpus. The source says “we cannot create a validation set without drastically reducing the size of the training set” and “we kept 9 configurations as training data and used the 2 lasts” [PAGE 7]. It also reports a “6-fold cross validation on the 11 configurations” in the table caption [PAGE 8]. The verifiable counterfactual is: if evaluation is treated as robust despite only 11 base configurations, confidence intervals will be underestimated and model selection will be unstable. Prediction: leave-one-configuration-out or repeated task-level splits will produce high variance, especially for NSS and AUC-Judd, which already show nonzero standard deviations in the table [PAGE 8]. Verification: if variance across folds or held-out hard configurations exceeds the difference between candidate models, the overconfidence claim is vetoed. This is especially relevant because the test configurations are described as “the more complex and difficult ones” [PAGE 7], meaning they are not representative of average positions.

RLVR counterfactual veto 3: synthetic-label bias veto. Suppose the reward signal treats GDS-generated saliency as if it were human attention. The source admits GDS biases: openings dominate, pawns are overrepresented, and the king is under-saliency because it is not often moved or checked [PAGE 6]. The verifiable counterfactual is: if GDS is treated as unbiased, a model trained on it should match human ETDS across piece types and game phases. Prediction: it will not. It will overpredict saliency around pawn moves and opening positions, and underpredict saliency around king-check events relative to human eye-tracking ground truth. Verification: stratify evaluation by moved piece type, game phase, and check status. If saliency mass on pawn-related squares exceeds human fixation mass after controlling for task difficulty, or if check-related human fixations are missed, the GDS-as-ground-truth assumption is vetoed. The source already provides a qualitative version of this prediction: GDS-trained networks show “smaller salient areas focused on pieces” and this “corresponds more to how beginner/intermediate players would look at the board” [PAGE 12].

These three vetoes are not betting lines; they are falsifiable evaluation constraints. They require exact measurements: metric-vector comparisons, task-level cross-validation variance, and stratified saliency mass by piece type, game phase, and check state. A model or reward that fails any one veto should be rejected for the corresponding claim. The safest source-supported conclusion is that the proposed architecture is a baseline, not a finished theory of chess attention: “The novelty of our work makes it difficult to compare to existing techniques, but current results and ideas could be used as a baseline for future work” [PAGE 12].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
