The paper’s central derivation begins with a known failure mode rather than a new feature detector. In a scene dominated by a plane, the usual two-view epipolar constraint loses rank or conditioning: the source states that “the standard approach of estimating camera poses through epipolar geometry is subject to a two-parameter ambiguity [9], resulting in a multifold solution space” and that “the estimation of the epipolar geometry becomes ill-posed and is prone to arbitrary determination by outlier correspondence” [PAGE 1]. Planar-SfM therefore changes the primitive from an essential-matrix edge to a homography edge. Given calibrated cameras, a homography \(H_{ij}\) for a plane \(\pi\) decomposes into \((R_{ij},c_{ij},n_{ij})\), where \(n_{ij}=R_i n_\pi\), and the relative pose \((R_{ij},c_{ij})\) is “invariant to \(\pi\)” [PAGE 4]. That invariance is the multi-hop hinge: one physical plane gives one relative-pose estimate, and multiple planes, or multiple segmentations of one dominant plane, give multiple independent estimates of the same relative pose.

The graph construction follows directly. For each image pair \((i,j)\), the method estimates \(k_{ij}\) homographies, producing a multigraph \(G=(V,E)\) whose nodes are cameras and whose multi-edges are plane-induced homographies [PAGE 3-4]. The next derivation is a reliability calculus. A similarity matrix \(S\) scores whether two incident multi-edges represent the same physical surface. It combines a normal-angle kernel \(\eta(n_a,n_b)=\exp(-\arccos^2(n_a^T n_b))\) with a probability \(P\) that the two homographies are non-erroneous and co-planar [PAGE 4]. That probability factors image-space overlap \(O\), the count of feature points that are inliers in both homographies, and per-homography confidence \(w\), which measures angular agreement between the homography-derived rotation \(R^q_{ij}\) and an auxiliary essential-matrix rotation \(R^e_{ij}\) [PAGE 4-5]. The graph is then pruned by a spectral embedding: solve for \(h\) minimizing \(\sum(\tilde h_{ij}-\tilde h_{lm})^2 S_{ij,lm}\) subject to a degree-normalized unit-norm constraint, whose optimum is the second-smallest eigenvector of \(I-D^{-1}S\) [PAGE 5]. Because \(h\) has arbitrary scale, the spanning tree is selected by minimizing the range of edge embeddings, i.e. the minimum-range spanning tree [PAGE 5, PAGE 11]. Pose recovery then consolidates multi-edge estimates by weighted projection onto \(SO(3)\) and \(S^2\), propagates orientations through the tree, expands to a bi-connected graph for positions, and solves a least-unsquared-deviations problem [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit is where the derivation becomes fragile. The confidence term \(w\) depends on an essential-matrix orientation, but the essential matrix is precisely the object that the paper says is ill-posed under dominant planarity [PAGE 1]. Thus the method’s “structural evidence” is partly circular in the single-plane regime: if \(R^e_{ij}\) is arbitrary, \(w\) may downweight genuinely correct homographies or promote wrong ones. The paper mitigates this with multi-edge normal consistency and pruning, and its ablation shows that both components matter enormously, but it does not prove that \(R^e_{ij}\) is reliable when the scene is one plane. Other singular regimes are equally important. If \(S\) has an isolated edge or a zero row sum, \(D^{-1}\) is undefined; if \(S\) is disconnected or has multiple zero eigenvalues, the second eigenvector is non-unique and the embedding range is not identified. If the baseline is near zero or the motion is nearly pure rotation, relative position and homography decomposition become ill-conditioned. Homography decomposition also has normal-sign and multi-solution ambiguities not resolved by the angular kernel alone. For positions, the paper explicitly notes that the tree “is inadequate” because no tree is bi-connected, and that parallel rigidity is “empirically found” equivalent in many cases to bi-connectivity, not a theorem [PAGE 6]. Finally, the ablation values are stark: disabling pruning gives AUC@1° of 3.303 versus 67.39 for the full method, and disabling confidence scores gives 0.808 [PAGE 8]. That is the correct place to audit the method: not at the elegance of the spectral step, but at the validity of \(S\), \(D\), and the auxiliary orientation used to define \(w\).

Units: rotations are dimensionless elements of \(SO(3)\); normals are unit vectors on \(S^2\); translations are homogeneous scene units up to global scale; angles are radians; probabilities, similarity scores, and inlier counts are dimensionless; AUC values are dimensionless fractions.

The core homography decomposition is
\[
H_{ij}\mapsto (R_{ij},c_{ij},n_{ij}),\qquad n_{ij}=R_i n_\pi .
\]
The source writes that “with \((R_{ij},c_{ij})\) being precisely the relative pose defined in Section 3.1 and is invariant to \(\pi\)” [PAGE 4]. Assumption: \(H_{ij}\) is induced by a genuine planar surface visible in both calibrated views. Breakdown: erroneous correspondences, non-planar structure, moving objects, calibration error, or near-zero baseline make the decomposition non-physical or ill-conditioned.

The similarity matrix is
\[
S_{ij,lm}=\sum_{q=1}^{k_{ij}}\sum_{q'=1}^{k_{lm}}P^{q,q'}_{ij,lm}\,\eta(n^q_{ij},n^{q'}_{lm}),
\]
with
\[
\eta(n_a,n_b)=\exp\!\left(-\arccos^2(n_a^T n_b)\right).
\]
The paper says \(\eta\) “measures the angular similarity between surface normals” and that \(P^{q,q'}_{ij,lm}\) is the probability that the homographies “are not erroneous and correspond to the same physical surface,” with \(\sum P=1\) [PAGE 4]. Units: \(n_a^T n_b\) is dimensionless; \(\arccos\) is radians; \(\eta\) and \(S\) are dimensionless.

The probability model is
\[
P^{q,q'}_{ij,lm}\propto O(H^q_{ij},H^{q'}_{lm})\,w^q_{ij}w^{q'}_{lm},
\]
where \(O\) “counts the number of feature points that are inliers in both homographies” and the weights \(w^q_{ij}\) provide “structural evidence for the validity of each homography by measuring its agreement with an independently estimated orientation” [PAGE 4-5]. The confidence is
\[
w^q_{ij}\propto \exp\!\left(-\epsilon^2(R^q_{ij},R^e_{ij})\right),
\]
with
\[
\epsilon(R_a,R_b)=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{trace}(R_a^T R_b)-1}{2}\right),
\]
the geodesic distance on \(SO(3)\) [PAGE 5]. Units: \(\epsilon\) is radians; \(w\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: \(R^e_{ij}\) is estimated from the essential matrix, which is exactly the planar-degenerate object described on [PAGE 1]; pure rotation or insufficient translation can make \(R^e_{ij}\) arbitrary.

The spectral embedding is
\[
h=\arg\min_{\tilde h\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}}
\sum_{ij,lm\in E}(\tilde h_{ij}-\tilde h_{lm})^2 S_{ij,lm}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\tilde h^T D\tilde h=1,
\]
where \(D\) is the diagonal matrix of row sums of \(S\) [PAGE 5]. The source states: “The optimal solution is given by the eigenvector associated with the second-smallest eigenvalue of \(I-D^{-1}S\)” and that the smallest eigenvector is “uninformative” [PAGE 5]. Units: \(h\) has arbitrary scale and is therefore not a physical length. Breakdown: \(D^{-1}\) is undefined if any row sum is zero; if \(S=0\) or the graph is disconnected, the second eigenvector is non-unique; if the second eigenvalue is repeated, the embedding is not identified.

The tree objective is
\[
T=\arg\min_{\tilde T\subseteq G}
\left\{
\max_{ij,lm\in E(\tilde T)} |h_{ij}-h_{lm}|
\right\},
\]
the minimum-range spanning tree [PAGE 5]. Algorithm 1 implements this by sorting edge weights and building minimum-spanning-trees on thresholded graphs, using the fact that “any minimum-spanning-trees is also a minimum-bottleneck-spanning-tree” [PAGE 11]. Breakdown: if \(G\) is disconnected, no spanning tree exists; if the embedding range is dominated by eigenvalue noise, the selected tree is unstable.

Pose consolidation is
\[
\hat R_{ij}=\Pi_{SO(3)}\!\left(\sum_q w^q_{ij}R^q_{ij}\right),
\qquad
\hat c_{ij}=\Pi_{S^2}\!\left(\sum_q w^q_{ij}c^q_{ij}\right),
\]
where \(\Pi\) denotes projection onto the respective manifold [PAGE 6]. For positions, the source transforms \(\hat c'_{ij}=\hat R_j^T\hat c_{ij}\propto c_i-c_j\) and recovers absolute positions by “least-unsquared-deviations” [PAGE 6]. Assumption: the expanded graph is bi-connected and therefore, in the paper’s empirical finding, parallel-rigid; the source explicitly says “no tree is bi-connected” and that the tree “is inadequate for determining the camera positions” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown: if the expanded graph remains non-bi-connected, or if relative translations are nearly parallel, position recovery is not well-posed.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is visible in the citations and in the paper’s own framing. Epipolar SfM descends from Hartley and Zisserman’s multiple-view geometry [PAGE 9], while the planar degeneracy problem is older: Pollefeys, Verbiest, and Van Gool are cited for “Surviving dominant planes in uncalibrated structure and motion recovery” [PAGE 10]. Homography decomposition itself is attributed to Ma, Kosecká, Soatto, and Sastry [PAGE 9], and the modern pipeline inheritance is COLMAP, PixSfM, VGGSfM, and DFSfM [PAGE 9-11]. The spectral step is retrocausally linked to Belkin and Niyogi’s Laplacian eigenmaps [PAGE 9], and the tree step to Camerini’s min-max spanning tree, Kruskal’s MST, and Prim’s algorithm [PAGE 9-10]. The position solve inherits Özyeşil, Singer, and Basri’s stable camera motion estimation by convex programming [PAGE 10]. The application lineage is sports broadcasting and architectural documentation, with the OTE basketball arena as the planar stress case and IMC Phototourism as the general outdoor case [PAGE 1, PAGE 6-7].

The multi-week traps are specific. First, an engineer may spend weeks trying to improve the essential matrix or RANSAC on a dominant plane, but the paper’s premise is that this is “ill-posed” and “prone to arbitrary determination by outlier correspondence” [PAGE 1]. Second, an engineer may treat a single homography as a single hypothesis and ignore the multi-edge graph; the paper’s contribution is precisely that each plane gives an independent relative-pose estimate [PAGE 1-2]. Third, an engineer may trust the confidence weight \(w\) without noticing that it is defined against an essential-matrix orientation [PAGE 5], which is exactly the degenerate estimate in the planar case. Fourth, an engineer may assume that a spanning tree is sufficient for positions; the paper states the opposite, because “no tree is bi-connected” [PAGE 6]. Fifth, an engineer may treat the bi-connectivity-to-parallel-rigidity relation as a theorem; the paper says it is “empirically found” in many cases [PAGE 6]. Sixth, an engineer may skip the ablation and assume pruning is cosmetic; the ablation shows that replacing the minimum-range spanning tree with a random spanning tree collapses AUC@1° from 67.39 to 3.303, and random confidence weights collapse it to 0.808 [PAGE 8]. Those are the multi-week traps: optimizing the wrong primitive, trusting the wrong auxiliary prior, and underestimating the graph-selection step.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the default mental model of SfM under planarity: when the scene is a court, a facade, or a wall, the operator or system regresses to “more features, more RANSAC, better epipolar estimation.” The paper’s own description of conventional pipelines supports that habit: sequential registration is “prone for drift and error accumulation,” and bundle adjustment is non-convex, so final accuracy depends on initial estimates [PAGE 2]. But the regression is maladaptive because the planar case is not a low-inlier version of the general case; it is a different geometry. The counter-trap is to re-encode the scene as a collection of planar constraints: build a pose-graph of homographies, decompose each homography, and use the invariance of relative pose to the plane [PAGE 4]. That counter-trap is not merely “use homographies”; it is “use many homographies and prune the graph by mutual consistency.” The recursive part is that the method models other edges as potential observations of the same physical surface through \(P\), and models itself through the auxiliary orientation \(R^e\). That self-model is incomplete: it does not explicitly model the unreliability of \(R^e\) under planarity. A stronger recursive theory of mind would represent the auxiliary essential-matrix estimate as a possibly degenerate informant, not as ground truth.

The clock dimension is the evaluation and operational cadence. The OTE dataset uses 46 calibrated cameras around a basketball court, and matching is restricted to “two nearest neighbors from each side” because the camera arrangement is ordered [PAGE 6]. That is a clock-like constraint: not all pairs are equal, and the graph topology encodes temporal or spatial adjacency. The ablation averages over 100 random seeds [PAGE 8], which is another clock: the result is not a single lucky tree but a distribution over random tree choices. The recursive lesson is that the system must anticipate its own failure at the graph-selection stage. If pruning is removed, the system no longer has a reliable “clock” for edge ordering; if confidence is randomized, it no longer has a reliable “clock” for edge weighting. The full method keeps both clocks synchronized: \(w\) orders edges by structural agreement, \(S\) orders them by normal and overlap agreement, and the minimum-range tree selects a consistent interval in the embedding. The crisis habit says “add more data”; the counter-trap says “change the primitive”; the clock says “the primitive is only useful if the edge-selection clock is stable.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical/geometric veto is justified by the calibrated-planar model itself. If the intrinsics are unknown or wrong, the decomposition \(H_{ij}\mapsto(R_{ij},c_{ij},n_{ij})\) no longer yields the stated invariant relative pose; the source assumes “calibrated” cameras with “known intrinsic matrices” [PAGE 3]. If a purported homography has no inlier support in both hypotheses, \(O=0\), so \(P=0\), so that multi-edge contributes nothing to \(S\). If \(S\) has a zero row sum, \(D^{-1}\) is undefined and the spectral embedding is vetoed. If the graph has no spanning tree, Eq. 6 has no admissible solution. If the expanded graph is not bi-connected, the paper’s position-recovery assumption fails because the tree “is inadequate for determining the camera positions” [PAGE 6]. These are not statistical preferences; they are admissibility conditions for the operators as written.

A biomechanical veto applies in the basketball-arena case because the planar model assumes a static physical surface. Athletes, officials, and moving equipment introduce correspondences that are not generated by the court plane. Such correspondences can inflate \(O\) while producing normals that disagree with the dominant plane; the angular kernel \(\eta\) then lowers \(S\), and the edge should be pruned. The veto is not “people are present”; it is “the inlier overlap and normal evidence are inconsistent with a single physical plane.” The source’s OTE result supports the need for such vetoes: the full method reaches AUC@5° of 95.55 versus 91.12 for GLOMAP, but the ablation shows that without pruning or confidence the result collapses [PAGE 7-8]. A biomechanical confound that is not vetoed would appear as a spurious edge with high overlap but inconsistent normal.

A weather/environment veto applies to the IMC Phototourism scenes, which are “captured under unconstrained conditions” [PAGE 6]. Illumination change, rain, snow, haze, or seasonal appearance change can reduce SuperPoint/SuperGlue match counts, lowering \(O\) and therefore \(P\). If the weather change is severe enough that only a few correspondences survive, the homography is underdetermined and the edge should be vetoed. The proof obligation is layered: physical calibration and planarity are necessary for Eq. 1; biomechanical motion must not dominate the inlier set; weather must not destroy the match overlap. The paper does not provide explicit thresholds for these vetoes, so any implementation must calibrate them rather than invent a fake physical constant.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Veto confidence scoring while retaining pruning. Counterfactual: assign random weights to homographies on OTE, as the source describes: “To disable confidence scoring we assigned random weights to homographies” [PAGE 7-8]. Prediction from Table 3: AUC@1° \(=0.808\), AUC@3° \(=3.636\), AUC@5° \(=6.263\), versus the full method’s \(67.39\), \(86.55\), \(95.55\) [PAGE 8]. This is verifiable by rerunning the OTE ablation over 100 random seeds and checking the three AUC values.

Scenario 2: Veto graph pruning while retaining confidence scores. Counterfactual: “we replaced the minimum range spanning tree with a random spanning tree” and averaged over 100 random seeds [PAGE 8]. Prediction from Table 3: AUC@1° \(=3.303\), AUC@3° \(=11.566\), AUC@5° \(=17.909\), versus \(67.39\), \(86.55\), \(95.55\) for the full method [PAGE 8]. This is verifiable by the same ablation protocol; the prediction is that confidence scores alone cannot compensate for an unreliable spanning tree.

Scenario 3: Veto both components. Counterfactual: random confidence weights plus random spanning tree. Prediction from Table 3: AUC@1° \(=0.434\), AUC@3° \(=2.646\), AUC@5° \(=4.910\) [PAGE 8]. The source’s summary is explicit: “The dramatic performance drop when either component is disabled highlights their effectiveness in filtering outliers and reinforcing reliable estimates” [PAGE 8]. This is verifiable as the bottom row of the OTE ablation and predicts near-chance pose recovery under the paper’s own metric. A further verifiable veto, if one wants a non-tabulated case, is to set \(S=0\) or disconnect the pose-graph: then \(D^{-1}\) is undefined or no spanning tree exists, so the method has no admissible output; that prediction follows from Eq. 5 and Eq. 6 [PAGE 5].
