Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

James’s 1890 passage ([PAGE 80]) sits at the confluence of three lineages that only fully formalized decades later. First, Gestalt psychology (Wertheimer 1912; Köhler; Koffka) inherits the “one state or nothing” anti-elementism directly: the whole is prior to its parts, and insight restructuring is sudden rather than accumulative. James’s “sudden” reinterpretation of “Pas de lieu Rhône que nous” as “paddle your own canoe” is a proto-phenomenology of the Gestalt switch, preceding the canonical figure-ground and reversal ambiguities by twenty years. Second, Bartlett’s (1932) reconstructive memory and Neisser’s (1967) cognitive psychology take up the top-down theme: perception is an active schema-driven construction, not a passive register of sensation. The “associative irradiations” phrase is James’s pre-computational name for what later became spreading activation in semantic networks (Collins & Loftus, 1975) and prediction-error minimization in predictive processing (Friston, 2010; Clark, 2013). Third, semantic satiation—the “entirely unnatural aspect” of a repeated isolated word ([PAGE 80])—was first isolated experimentally by Severance and Washburn (1907), meaning James predicted the effect seventeen years before its laboratory demonstration.

Multi-week trap: The source describes acute, minute-scale phenomena—momentary inhibition, sudden recognition, repetition over “long enough.” A multi-week trap arises if one extrapolates these acute dynamics to chronic timescales without testing consolidation. Habituation to a repeated word recovers after a delay; semantic traces reconsolidate; the binding \(I(A;M)\) can be strengthened or weakened over weeks of exposure. Therefore a finding that a word looks unnatural after fifty repetitions in one session does not imply that the word’s meaning is degraded after a week, nor that the “gazouillement des oiseaux” effect ([PAGE 80]) persists for a non-native listener after months of immersion. The clock of neural adaptation (seconds to minutes) is distinct from the clock of long-term potentiation and systems consolidation (days to weeks). Any model that collapses these timescales into a single decay constant commits a category error: it mistakes an acute satiation boundary for a chronic representational erosion.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical layer veto. The auditory periphery imposes an upper bound on what top-down meaning can recover. Cochlear frequency resolution is limited by basilar-membrane place coding and critical-band bandwidth (roughly 100–300 Hz at low frequencies, widening proportionally at high frequencies), and the articulation index / speech intelligibility index (ANSI S3.5) quantifies the fraction of the speech signal available to the listener across frequency bands. If the acoustic channel is below the critical SNR—because of masking noise, reverberation, or severe spectral degradation—then no amount of associative irradiation can reconstruct phonemic identity. James’s “mere echoes of acoustic sensation” ([PAGE 80]) are thus not merely a psychological option; they are a physical necessity whenever the channel capacity drops below the information rate required for speech. Veto rule: physical channel failure vetoes semantic recovery.

Biomechanical layer veto. Reading or uttering an isolated word repeatedly engages the phonological loop and articulatory motor programs. Overt or subvocal repetition induces motor adaptation and auditory-forward-model attenuation; repeated production of the same phoneme sequence reduces the precision of the efference copy and the match between predicted and reafferent sound. This biomechanical fatigue is a plausible contributor to the “entirely unnatural aspect” ([PAGE 80]): the word feels alien because the motor-auditory prediction loop no longer binds the visual form to a fluent articulatory gestalt. Veto rule: if articulatory rehearsal is physically prevented (e.g., by articulatory suppression), the satiation rate should decline even if visual repetition is maintained.

Weather/environmental veto. The “gazouillement des oiseaux” effect ([PAGE 80])—foreign speech heard as bird twitter—is modulated by outdoor propagation physics. Temperature inversions, wind gradients, humidity, and atmospheric absorption alter SNR and spectral balance over distance. A German utterance heard clearly at close range as speech may, under a temperature inversion or downwind propagation, arrive at the listener with altered high-frequency emphasis and reduced intelligibility, pushing the percept across the boundary into “bird twitter.” Veto rule: environmental propagation conditions veto the psychological conclusion that the foreignness alone causes the acoustic impression; the same utterance can be speech or twitter depending on the physical transmission path, independent of the listener’s linguistic knowledge.

Adjudication hierarchy. When layers conflict, the physical layer has veto priority: no perceptual model can assign semantic content to a signal that the ear never encoded. The biomechanical layer vetoes claims that satiation is purely “cognitive” when articulatory suppression alters the effect. The weather/environmental layer vetoes claims that cross-linguistic acoustic salience is purely learned when propagation physics can reproduce the “bird twitter” impression with native speech under adverse conditions. [PAGE 80] provides the phenomenology; these layers supply the boundary conditions under which the phenomenology holds or collapses.
