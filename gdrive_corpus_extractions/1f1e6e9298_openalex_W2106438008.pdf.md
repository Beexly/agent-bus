The first-order theory of mind is the forecaster's model of the future variable; the second-order layer is the forecaster's model of how users interpret confidence, and the third-order layer is the user's model of the forecaster's competence. Crisis habit regression operates as follows: under volatility or time pressure, the judge regresses to dominant cognitive habits—anchoring on the most recent observation, damping both upward and downward trends (damping down-trends more), widening confidence bounds on down-trends while reducing point estimates, and reading patterns into noise [PAGE 5, citing Lawrence & Makridakis 1989; O'Connor, Remus & Griggs 1997]. Group discussion amplifies this by focusing attention on success-promoting factors, producing optimistic, "bold" forecasts that users interpret as signals of expertise [PAGE 6, citing Brenner et al. 2005; PAGE 13–14, citing Yates et al. 1996; Keren 1997; Price & Stone 2004]. The provider thus faces a recursive incentive: users prefer extreme probabilities and narrow intervals as displays of confidence, which rewards overconfidence and overextremity even as empirical calibration deteriorates [PAGE 12–16].

The counter-trap consists of the remediation stack—task-properties feedback, decomposition into trend/seasonal/random or event-specific components, mechanical combination, advice-taking with source weights assessed rather than intuitively applied, Theil-style error correction, and restricted judgmental adjustment conditional on naïve-benchmark deficiency or genuine unmodelled domain knowledge [PAGE 16–19]. But each counter-measure contains its own regression trigger: decomposition can induce fatigue and reduce accuracy below holistic judgment [PAGE 17]; recording adjustment reasons still leaves 35% of adjustments unnecessary and accuracy-reducing [PAGE 19]; advice is egocentrically discounted unless sources are independent and weights are applied mechanically [PAGE 17]; and Theil correction assumes systematic, sustained bias, failing when bias is transient [PAGE 18]. The counter-trap therefore fails precisely when the crisis habit is non-systematic or when the judge misclassifies a statistical-model deficiency as a domain-knowledge opportunity.

The clock is the forecast-horizon and information-refresh structure: as \(h\) increases, the judgmental anchor model naturally places less weight on the last observation [PAGE 4]; rolling origins expose the discontinuity-detection lag [PAGE 5]; feedback timeliness and presentation modality determine whether corrective information is processed at all [PAGE 16]. The adjudication rule is: crisis habit regression dominates at short horizons with noisy, infrequent feedback; counter-traps dominate when feedback is task-properties based, combination partners are negatively correlated, and adjustments are screened against a naïve benchmark; the clock runs out when the series exhibits nonlinear trend, high-complexity seasonality, or unannounced discontinuity, because none of the counter-traps restores calibration without domain-specific retraining [PAGE 4–5].
