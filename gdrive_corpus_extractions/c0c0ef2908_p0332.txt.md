Stratum one comprises first-order and second-order drives; stratum two comprises chains of specific minor appetites and aversions, units of which are “aroused” either by “physiologically provided sequences of minor organic excitements” (appetites) or by “appropriately provided sequences of environmental stimuli” (aversions); stratum three comprises “certain innate skills or dexterities” that Tolman classes under instincts because they appear initially innate or are products of “relatively standardized environments.” The crucial move is the explicit denial that skills are appetites or aversions: “Such skills are not appetites or aversions in themselves. They do not involve demands or means-end-readinesses.” Instead they are “discrimination- and manipulation-dexterities at the service of the latter”—that is, at the service of the drives and chains.

A multi-hop derivation proceeds as follows. A controlling demand d ∈ D activates a means-end-readiness chain C = (u₁, …, uₙ), where each unit uₖ is an appetite or aversion. That chain selects an object O and recruits a skill Σ_k to act upon O. The skill extracts “discriminanda” and “manipulanda” from O. Tolman then asserts that skills are “innate readinesses for the true discriminanda- and manipulanda characters of immediately presented objects” and describes them as innate readinesses to “perceive” correctly “the innate commerce with possibilities of immediately presented stimulus-object—irrespective of whether or not such commerce with possibilities are at the moment being accepted as ‘good’ by some controlling demand and means-end-readiness.” The scare quotes around “perceive” are load-bearing: Tolman is not endorsing a phenomenological episode of consciousness; he is positing a functional, pre-valuation sensitivity to object-relations.

Gray-zone audit: (1) Recruitment interface. Tolman says skills are “at the service of” appetites and aversions, but he never formalizes the coupling operator between a drive-state d and a skill Σ. How does a demand “call” a flying skill versus an eating skill? The text leaves this as an unmodeled handshake. (2) Innate versus standardized-environment boundary. Tolman admits skills may be “the product of relatively standardized environments,” which blurs the nativist/empiricist line he seems to draw; “innate” here is a stability claim, not necessarily a genetic-essence claim. (3) Commerce with possibilities. This phrase anticipates Gibsonian affordances by decades, yet Tolman embeds it inside a behaviorist demand/means-end apparatus, creating an internal tension: if perception of possibilities is independent of whether they are accepted as “good,” then the drive chain cannot fully determine perceptual content, but the text offers no law for how the two layers reconcile when they conflict. (4) Citation opacity. The page ends with “In the two preceding chapters we have already described in See preceding footnote,” an incomplete cross-reference that severs this page from its evidential anchor; any system treating this page as self-contained inherits a boundary-condition singularity.

Breakdown regimes: when d = ∅ (no controlling demand), the skill remains available as a readiness but is not triggered; when O presents no true discriminanda for Σ, the skill output is null; when the physical environment vetoes execution, behavior fails despite intact d and Σ. The gray zone between “skill as capacity” and “skill as behavior” is exactly where Tolman’s argument is most vulnerable to reification: treating an innate readiness as if it were an executed act conflates the operator with its output.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s 1932 page sits at a hinge in the genealogy of purposive behaviorism. It precedes Tolman’s 1948 cognitive-map paper and his expectancy formulations, yet it already separates motivational “demand” from perceptual “commerce with possibilities,” a move that later reappears in Gibson’s 1979 affordance theory and in ecological psychology’s perception-action coupling. The European ethological tradition—Tinbergen’s innate releasing mechanisms and Lorenz’s fixed action patterns—also treats species-typical skills as discrimination capacities keyed to sign stimuli, but ethologists typically couple those mechanisms tightly to motivational “appetitive” and “consummatory” phases, whereas Tolman explicitly decouples the skill from the demand’s hedonic acceptance (“irrespective of whether or not … accepted as ‘good’”). This makes Tolman a precursor to model-based reinforcement learning, where a learned transition model (the skill/commerce map) is distinct from the reward function (the demand/valuation), yet it also exposes him to the Hullian drive-reduction critique that all behavior must be traceable to physiological need.

Multi-week traps: In any extended observation program, three retro-causal traps recur. First, the standardization trap: because Tolman allows skills to arise from “relatively standardized environments,” a researcher running weeks of trials may mistake environment-induced stability for genetic innateness, especially if the rearing conditions are held constant without explicit manipulation. Second, the representational reification trap: reading “innate commerce with possibilities” as an internal model or representation rather than a relational disposition leads to over-attributing cognitive architecture to what Tolman treats as a functional readiness. Third, the temporal collapse trap: treating the skill layer (millisecond-scale discrimination/manipulation) and the drive-chain layer (seconds-to-minutes appetitive seeking) as a single undifferentiated “motivation” obscures the very separation Tolman defends on [PAGE 306]. These traps are not historical curiosities; they recur whenever a multi-week behavioral dataset is mined for “purpose” without first resolving whether the observed regularity lives in D × C or in Σ.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When a human observer attributes purposive behavior to an animal, they run a recursive theory of mind: they simulate the animal’s drive chain, predict its means-end readiness, and then infer which skill it will deploy. Tolman’s layering creates a specific recursive crisis. Under crisis—novelty, obstruction, or drive conflict—the observer’s own habits regress. One regression mode is radical behaviorist collapse: the observer abandons the intermediate skill layer and treats the animal as a pure stimulus-response organism, denying that Σ ever operates independently of d. The counter-trap is cognitive inflation: the observer treats innate discrimination as if it were learned expectancy, ascribing a cognitive map to a creature merely deploying an innate flying or swimming dexterity.

The clock resolves this. Drive chains unfold on a motivational clock (hunger → search → ingestion across minutes), whereas skills operate on a perceptual-motor clock (visual discrimination and motor synergy across milliseconds). If the observer ignores the clock, they conflate the two and attribute deliberation to reflexive skill, or reflex to deliberated drive. Tolman’s claim that skills involve “no demands or means-end-readinesses” means that seeing a bird fly does not imply the bird “wants” to fly in that instant; the skill may be a readiness waiting for a demand that never arrives. Recursive closure occurs when the modeler, under their own crisis of interpretation, either denies all animal intentionality or inflates every motor pattern into purpose; Tolman’s three-stratum model is the escape hatch because it localizes purpose to D × C while relegating Σ to a demand-independent capacity layer.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Tolman’s skill layer is a capacity, not a guarantee. A physical veto layer Ψ_phys(O, env) ∈ {0, 1} can nullify behavior even when R(d, O) > 0 and Σ(O) is intact. Biomechanical veto: flying requires wing loading W/S and available muscle power P_max to exceed drag; in a headwind beyond the animal’s climb gradient, the innate flying skill produces motor output but no forward progress—physics vetoes the skill. Weather veto: precipitation or turbulence changes aerodynamic coefficients and acoustic transmission; a bird’s vocalizing skill may run, but the signal fails to propagate through high wind or dense foliage, so the social demand remains unmet. Swimming veto: Reynolds number Re = ρvL/μ; in cold, viscous fluid, thrust from an innate swimming stroke falls below drag, so displacement ≈ 0 despite intact drive and motor pattern. Walking veto: static friction coefficient μ_s between foot and substrate; on ice, μ_s drops below the threshold needed for center-of-mass propulsion, causing slip and null locomotion even though the walking skill and food drive are fully active.

These vetoes prove Tolman’s point on [PAGE 306]: skills are “discrimination- and manipulation-dexterities at the service of” demands, but they are permissive capacities embedded in a physical envelope. The demand layer cannot veto physics; physics vetoes the demand. Any model that treats Σ(O) as sufficient for goal-directed behavior fails the veto test: behavior = R(d, O) × Σ(O) × Ψ_phys(O, env), and if Ψ_phys = 0, the product is zero regardless of the first two terms. The breakdown regime is precisely when an experimenter manipulates d or O while holding env constant, implicitly assuming Ψ_phys = 1; changing env (e.g., adding wind, ice, or viscosity) falsifies that assumption without touching the animal’s psychology.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
