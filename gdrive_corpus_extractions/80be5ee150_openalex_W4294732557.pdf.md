The multi-hop derivation is: broadcast video is sampled at 30 fps and 1280×720 resolution [PAGE 3]; a detector proposes bounding boxes; a tracker computes affinities and links detections into trajectories; CLEAR MOT and IDF1 then score detection and identity persistence [PAGE 6]. The critical gray zone is that MOTA can look excellent while identity tracking remains mediocre. The paper states that “the MOTA score of all methods is above 90%” because MOTA is driven by false positives and false negatives, which “heavily rely on the performance of the player detector” [PAGE 7]. Yet the best IDF1 is only 62.9 and the best ID-switch count is 431 [PAGE 6]. Thus the correct inference is not “tracking is solved”; it is “detection is strong enough to inflate MOTA, while identity association under camera motion remains the bottleneck.” A second hop is the motion-model conclusion: SORT uses a Kalman filter with “simple IOU based re-id” [PAGE 3], and the paper reports SORT at IDF1 53.7 with 673 ID switches, versus MOT Neural Solver at IDF1 62.9 with 431 ID switches [PAGE 6]. The proposed explanation is that linear-motion assumptions break under hockey motion: “Sharp changes in player motion often leads to identity switches” [PAGE 8], whereas the graph-based MOT Neural Solver has “no such assumptions since it poses tracking as a graph edge classification problem” [PAGE 8]. A third hop is the data-quality audit: the dataset has 84 clips, 91,807 frames, and 773,545 bounding boxes, split at game level into 58 training, 13 validation, and 13 test clips [PAGE 4]. That game-level split reduces game-specific leakage, but the paper still reports one test split without confidence intervals, variance estimates, or per-game uncertainty. A fourth hop is the pan-identity switch estimator. The paper assumes “ground truth annotations are accurate and there are no missing annotations” [PAGE 8], then counts a pan switch when the timestamp gap exceeds δ: \((t_i-t_j)>\delta\) [PAGE 9]. This estimator is threshold-dependent; the paper itself varies δ between 40 and 80 frames [PAGE 9] and finds “90% at a threshold of δ = 40 frames” for video 9 [PAGE 6], while the all-video average is “65% of identity switches” at δ=40 [PAGE 7]. The gray-zone lesson is that “65%” is not a universal constant; it is an estimator conditioned on δ, annotation completeness, and ID-switch denominator. Fifth, the source names player-player and player-board occlusion plus “camera panning and zooming” as core difficulties [PAGE 1]. It also notes that same-team players are visually similar: “jersey color, helmet model, visor model, stick model, glove model, skate model, tape color etc unidentifiable from bounding boxes cropped from 720p broadcast clips” [PAGE 8]. Therefore appearance re-id cannot be treated as a complete identity oracle after a player exits the camera field of view. The audit’s conclusion is that the paper supports a narrow claim: on this broadcast NHL dataset, graph-based association outperforms linear-motion trackers on IDF1 and ID switches, and panning is the dominant reported identity-switch source. It does not support claims about biomechanical causality, weather effects, player intent, or perfect identity continuity. Those require variables and tests not present in the source schema.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s detection object is defined as \(o_i=\{x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i,I_i,t_i\}\), where \(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i\) are bounding-box coordinates and dimensions in pixels, \(I_i\) is the image-pixel content, and \(t_i\) is the timestamp/frame index [PAGE 3]. The tracking problem is described as finding trajectories \(T=\{T_1,T_2,\ldots,T_m\}\) that best explain the detection set \(O=\{o_1,\ldots,o_n\}\) [PAGE 3]. For the graph formulation, the source states \(G=(V,E)\), with \(V=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\) nodes for \(n\) player detections and edges connecting detection pairs so missed detections can be recovered [PAGE 3]. The source does not provide a closed-form affinity equation; it describes affinity calculation qualitatively as IOU-based or deep-network-based [PAGE 3].

The explicit metric equation is MOTA:
\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t\left(FN_t+FP_t+IDSW_t\right)}{\sum_t GT_t},
\]
where \(t\) is frame index, \(GT\) is the number of ground-truth objects, \(FN\) is false negatives, \(FP\) is false positives, and \(IDSW\) is identity switches [PAGE 7]. \(\mathrm{MOTA}\) is dimensionless and is reported as a percentage. Breakdown regime: if \(\sum_t GT_t=0\), the ratio is undefined; the source does not address empty-ground-truth frames. The paper also states that MOTA “highly depends on the performance of the detector” [PAGE 7], so MOTA is not a pure association metric.

The pan-identity switch condition is:
\[
(t_i-t_j)>\delta,
\]
where \(t_i,t_j\) are timestamps in frames and \(\delta\) is a threshold in frames [PAGE 9]. The count estimate is:
\[
\sum_{G}\mathbf{1}\!\left(t_i-t_j>\delta\right),
\]
where \(\mathbf{1}\) is an indicator function and the summation is over ground-truth trajectories [PAGE 9]. The reported proportion is:
\[
\frac{\sum_{G}\mathbf{1}\!\left(t_i-t_j>\delta\right)}{\mathrm{IDSWs}},
\]
which is dimensionless [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\mathrm{IDSWs}=0\), the proportion is undefined; if \(\delta\) is too small, ordinary annotation gaps are overcounted as pan switches; if \(\delta\) is too large, true re-entry events are undercounted. The source uses δ from 40 to 80 frames and reports “90% at a threshold of δ = 40 frames” for video 9 [PAGE 6] and “65% of identity switches” on average at δ=40 [PAGE 7].

Detection quality is reported as AP=70.2, AP50=95.9, and AP75=87.5 on test videos [PAGE 7]. These are dimensionless percentages. The source does not provide the AP integral formula. The only physical units reported are pixels, frames, seconds, and frames per second: 30 fps, 1280×720 pixels, average clip duration 36 seconds [PAGE 3]. No SI mechanical units, no force units, no weather variables, and no probability-density units are reported. The tables report IDF1, MOTA, ID switches, FP, and FN [PAGE 6]. The highest reported values are MOT Neural Solver: IDF1 62.9, MOTA 94.5, ID switches 431, FP 1653, FN 4394 [PAGE 6]. The source’s own interpretation is that “IDF1 is considered a better accuracy measure than Multi Object Tracking accuracy (MOTA) since it measures how consistently the identity of a tracked object is preserved” [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from handcrafted multi-target tracking to deep joint detection-and-tracking. The paper recalls Okuma et al., who combined a particle filter and mixture particle filter with an Adaboost detector; the source states that “particles merge and split in the process and leads to loss of identities” and that the method has no mechanism to prevent identity switches after occlusions [PAGE 1]. Cai et al. are described as improving on that by using bipartite matching instead of the mixture particle filter, though the source says that work “is not trained or tested on broadcast videos” and performs tracking in rink coordinates after manual homography [PAGE 2]. The modern genealogy in the paper is SORT, Deep SORT, Tracktor, FairMOT, and MOT Neural Solver: SORT is “Kalman filter with simple IOU based re-id”; Deep SORT is “Kalman filter with deep CNN based re-id”; Tracktor is a JDT algorithm with separate detection and re-id networks; FairMOT is a JDT algorithm with a combined detection and re-id network; MOT Neural Solver uses “graph message passing with edge classification” [PAGE 3]. The source also places hockey inside a broader sports-tracking lineage: basketball and soccer work is cited, including deep SORT for soccer tactical analysis and CenterTrack for soccer player position extraction [PAGE 2].

The retrocausal reading is that later graph-based association exposes the older linear-motion assumption as the weak link. SORT’s “linear motion model assumption and simple IOU score for re-identification” are explicitly blamed for its “least IDF1 score and the highest number of identity switches” [PAGE 7]. Deep SORT improves because it uses deep-network features for re-identification [PAGE 7]. The paper then argues that joint detection and re-identification with a multi-task loss performs better than separate networks, “evident by better performance of FairMOT compared to Tracktor” [PAGE 7]. But the paper also cautions that JDT methods “do not show any significant improvement over deep SORT” in ID switches [PAGE 7]. The final retrocausal step is the MOT Neural Solver, which reframes tracking as graph edge classification rather than a linear state-propagation problem [PAGE 3, PAGE 8].

Multi-week traps for a build or evaluation cycle are clear. Trap one: optimizing MOTA and declaring success. The source shows MOTA above 90% for all methods while IDF1 ranges from 53.7 to 62.9 [PAGE 6-7]. Trap two: using clip-level random splits. The paper explicitly says “the split is made at the game level” to prevent game-level bias, with training clips from 17 games, validation from 4, and test from 4 [PAGE 4]. Trap three: treating δ=40 as universal. The proportion is a function of δ, and the source varies δ from 40 to 80 [PAGE 9]. Trap four: assuming appearance re-id solves same-team ambiguity. The paper says same-team players are visually similar and distinguishing gear is unidentifiable from 720p crops [PAGE 8]. Trap five: confusing detector quality with tracker quality. The paper states that FP and FN “heavily rely on the performance of the player detector” [PAGE 7]. These traps can consume weeks if the evaluation dashboard rewards MOTA alone or if panning-induced re-entry is not isolated.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer here is the evaluator’s model of what a tracker “believes” when a player disappears. The source’s crisis is a camera-pan or occlusion event: a player leaves the broadcast field of view and may re-enter at another screen coordinate [PAGE 8]. The tracker’s habitual regression is to fall back to a linear motion prior and IOU appearance matching, which is exactly the SORT-style behavior the paper associates with “the least IDF1 score and the highest number of identity switches” [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is to over-trust deep appearance re-id, but the source undercuts that hope: same-team players share jersey color, helmet model, visor model, stick model, glove model, skate model, and tape color, all “unidentifiable from bounding boxes cropped from 720p broadcast clips” [PAGE 8]. The clock is the timestamp gap \(t_i-t_j\) and the threshold δ [PAGE 9]. The tracker has no explicit model of player intention, only temporal continuity and learned edge classification. Therefore the correct recursive adjudication is: when the clock gap is large and the player has left the camera field of view, identity continuity is underdetermined; a confident ID assignment is a prior-driven guess, not evidence. The source’s pan-switch estimator formalizes this by assuming complete annotations and counting long gaps as pan events [PAGE 8-9]. A human analyst can use game context, but the tracking model cannot recover lost identity from the bounding-box crop alone. The counter-trap is to lower δ until every gap looks like a pan switch, or raise δ until panning disappears from the error budget. The disciplined clock policy is to report the full δ curve and not a single number. The source itself shows video 9 at “90% at a threshold of δ = 40 frames” [PAGE 6] and an all-video average of “65%” at the same threshold [PAGE 7]. That difference is a theory-of-mind warning: the evaluator’s belief about “the main error source” depends on the clock threshold chosen.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the source explicitly names “significant player-player and player-board occlusion, camera panning and zooming in hockey broadcast video” [PAGE 1]. If a player is outside the camera field of view, the bounding-box evidence set \(o_i\) no longer contains that player’s pixels, and identity must be inferred from temporal and appearance priors. Because same-team visual features are reported as unidentifiable in 720p crops [PAGE 8], any claim of guaranteed identity continuity through a camera-pan exit is vetoed. The physical camera boundary is a hard discontinuity, not a soft appearance problem.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the paper describes hockey motion as “fast-paced and non-linear” [PAGE 1], and states that “Sharp changes in player motion often leads to identity switches” [PAGE 8]. A linear Kalman/IOU tracker therefore has a biomechanical mismatch with the sport. The empirical veto is direct: SORT obtains IDF1 53.7 and 673 ID switches, while MOT Neural Solver obtains IDF1 62.9 and 431 ID switches [PAGE 6]. Any claim that a linear motion model is sufficient for broadcast hockey identity tracking is vetoed by those reported results. Any claim that graph message passing eliminates biomechanical failure is also too strong; the paper still reports 431 ID switches for the best model [PAGE 6]. The defensible claim is comparative, not absolute.

Weather-layer veto: the source contains no weather variable, no rink temperature, no ice-quality measurement, no humidity, no lighting covariate, and no arena-environmental feature. The detection schema is \(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i,I_i,t_i\) [PAGE 3], and the dataset description reports only broadcast NHL clips, frame rate, resolution, clip duration, and annotations [PAGE 3-4]. Therefore any weather-linked causal claim about tracking accuracy is unidentifiable from this source and must be vetoed. The correct proof is a schema-exclusion argument: if \(W(t)\) is absent from the input and annotation tables, then no \(\partial \mathrm{IDF1}/\partial W\) or \(\partial \mathrm{IDSW}/\partial W\) can be estimated without an external join. The source supports an indoor broadcast-video error analysis, not a weather analysis.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
