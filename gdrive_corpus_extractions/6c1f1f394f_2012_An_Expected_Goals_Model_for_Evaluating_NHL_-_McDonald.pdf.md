<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s core multi-hop logic is: hockey goals are scarce and noisy, so raw goals are a weak evaluative target; shot-based proxies such as Fenwick and Corsi improve prediction of future goal differential; therefore one can build a supervised model that predicts goals from a wider box-score set and interpret the fitted value as “expected goals.” The first hop is empirical: “a team’s current shot differential, Fenwick differential, and Corsi differential are each better than a team’s current goal differential at predicting goal differential when a half season’s worth of data is used [1]” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is model selection: the paper considers goals, shots, missed shots, blocked shots, Fenwick, Corsi, zone starts, turnovers, faceoffs, hits, hits against, and shooting percentage, then retains “goals, shots, hits, hits against, and faceoffs” [PAGE 3]. The third hop is validation: train on 2007-08 through 2009-10, validate on 2010-11, and cross-validate; compare correlation and mean squared error against goals, shots, Fenwick, Corsi, and Corsi-with-hits. The fourth hop is player evaluation: use expected goals per 60 as the dependent variable in an adjusted plus-minus model with ridge regression, so that player contributions are estimated “independent of the strength of his teammates, opponents, and the zone in which his shifts begin” [PAGE 2].

The gray-zone audit matters because the paper is predictive, not causal. The retained hit variables have a counterintuitive sign: “The coefficients of hits and hits against are negative and positive, respectively. This means that low hits, and high hits against, are good predictors of goals” [PAGE 5]. The paper immediately blocks a moralized reading: “We remark that these results do not necessarily indicate that hits are bad, or that players should stop finishing their checks” [PAGE 5]. The proposed mechanism is possession and temporary numerical disadvantage: “after a hit, the play can momentarily be 4-on-4 instead of 5-on-5” [PAGE 5]. A second gray zone is faceoff-event count. The paper admits: “A statistic like total faceoffs may have come up as significant in our model by chance, but may not be significant if the data were split randomly and the models were fit several times” [PAGE 6]. A third gray zone is collinearity in adjusted plus-minus: “Daniel Sedin is on the ice with his twin brother Henrik 93% of the time, and their adjusted plus-minus estimates have very large error bounds as a result” [PAGE 2]. Ridge regression is the paper’s response, but the paper does not report a full coefficient table, an exact λ value, or a full design matrix, so the audit cannot recover a unique causal decomposition from the text alone. The model is also explicitly bounded: “We focus strictly on team offense, and we restrict our attention to 5-on-5 situations in which both goalies are on the ice” [PAGE 2]. That excludes special teams, goalie-quality adjustment, and goals-against modeling. The paper’s own future-work section notes that goals-against work has “one added complication that goalies are involved” [PAGE 6]. Thus the correct inference is conditional and predictive: within even-strength 5-on-5 team offense, the expected-goals construction lowers out-of-sample MSE and raises correlation relative to the listed baselines; it is not a license to treat hits or faceoffs as causal levers, nor to extrapolate to penalties, power plays, or goalie evaluation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The central operator is a linear conditional-mean model. Let \(Y_t\) be goals for per 60 minutes of even-strength ice time for team-season split \(t\), and let \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^p\) contain the retained predictors. The paper states: “Unless otherwise specified, all statistics throughout this paper are given as ‘per 60 minutes’ rate statistics during even strength situations” [PAGE 2]. The fitted expected-goals operator is
\[
\hat{Y}(x)=E[Y\mid X=x]=x^{\top}\hat{\beta},
\]
with \(Y\) and \(\hat{Y}\) in goals per 60 even-strength minutes. This is a conditional expectation, not a probability; the paper does not specify a likelihood for the probability of at least one goal in a fixed interval, so no probability unit should be attached to \(\hat{Y}\).

Ordinary least squares solves
\[
\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{OLS}}
=\arg\min_{\beta}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(Y_t-x_t^{\top}\beta\right)^2
=(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}Y,
\]
when \(X^{\top}X\) is invertible. Ridge regression solves
\[
\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{ridge}}
=\arg\min_{\beta}\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(Y_t-x_t^{\top}\beta\right)^2
+\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2\right\}
=(X^{\top}X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^{\top}Y,
\]
with \(\lambda\) a nonnegative regularization parameter. The paper says “We also use ridge regression, as first used by Joe Sill in basketball [12], to estimate each player’s adjusted plus-minus” [PAGE 2]. If predictors are standardized, \(\lambda\) is dimensionless; if predictors remain in native rate units, \(\lambda\) carries inverse-squared predictor units. The paper does not report standardization details, so the exact dimensional interpretation of its \(\lambda\) is not recoverable from the text.

The OLS hat operator is \(H=X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}\); the ridge smoother is \(H_\lambda=X(X^{\top}X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^{\top}\). In eigendirections of \(X^{\top}X\) with eigenvalues \(\sigma_i^2\), ridge shrinks by \(\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\), and the effective degrees of freedom are \(\operatorname{tr}(H_\lambda)=\sum_i \sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\). Singularity regimes are exact: if \(\lambda=0\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(X)\) is less than \(p\), or if predictors are exactly collinear, \(X^{\top}X\) is singular and OLS is undefined; near-collinearity inflates variance even when the inverse exists. The paper’s motivating example is the Sedin co-occurrence: “Daniel Sedin is on the ice with his twin brother Henrik 93% of the time, and their adjusted plus-minus estimates have very large error bounds as a result” [PAGE 2]. With \(\lambda\) greater than zero, the inverse exists for nonnegative \(\lambda\), but breakdown shifts to bias: as \(\lambda \to 0^{+}\), ridge approaches unstable OLS; as \(\lambda \to \infty\), \(\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{ridge}} \to 0\) and expected goals collapse toward the intercept, producing underfitting. A further breakdown is distribution shift: the paper avoids first-half-to-second-half prediction because “Since midseason trades and injures can have an impact on a team’s performance, we did not use statistics from the first half of the season to predict goals in the second half” [PAGE 3]. Instead, “we split the season into odd and even games, and used statistics from odd games to predict goals in even games” [PAGE 3]. MSE is in \(({\rm goals}/60)^2\); correlation is dimensionless; player adjusted plus-minus coefficients are in goals per 60 per 0/1 on-ice indicator, conditional on the model’s teammate, opponent, and zone controls.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

The paper sits in a lineage that runs from shot-count hockey analytics to expected-goals player evaluation. The references cite JLikens on “Shots, Fenwick and Corsi” and Ferrari, Desjardins, and Macdonald on possession and adjusted plus-minus [PAGE 6]. The historical logic is retrocausal in the sense that later expected-goals models look back to this paper’s move: replace the noisy goal outcome with a model-based expected-goal estimate, then use that estimate as a dependent variable in an adjusted plus-minus framework. The paper’s own abstract states that Fenwick and Corsi “have been shown to be better than goals as a predictor of future goals” [PAGE 1]. That claim is the bridge from descriptive shot metrics to predictive modeling. The second lineage is basketball adjusted plus-minus: the paper cites Rosenbaum, Lewin, Witus, Ilardi and Barzilai, and Sill [PAGE 7]. The third lineage is Macdonald’s prior NHL adjusted plus-minus work [10], [11], [5], which the paper extends by replacing goals per 60 with expected goals per 60 [PAGE 2].

The multi-week trap in this genealogy is that the model’s apparent superiority can be inflated by leakage, outlier handling, or multiple testing. The paper’s design is deliberately temporal: training data from 2007-08, 2008-09, and 2009-10; validation on 2010-11; and 10-fold cross-validation [PAGE 3]. It also removes four outliers for the final model: “We note that for forming our final model, we removed four outliers, Ottawa and Carolina from 2007-08 and Minnesota and New York Islanders from 2010-11” [PAGE 3]. The paper reports that “We also fit the model without removing outliers, and the results for correlation and mean squared error are similar” [PAGE 3]. That is a robustness check against an outlier-driven result. A second multi-week trap is faceoff significance: the paper warns that “total faceoffs may have come up as significant in our model by chance” [PAGE 6] and proposes repeated random 41-game splits as a future test [PAGE 6]. A third trap is treating a retained variable as a causal mechanism. The paper’s hit discussion is explicitly predictive: low hits and high hits against are “good predictors of goals” [PAGE 5], not proof that hitting causes losses. A fourth trap is applying a 5-on-5 even-strength offense model to special teams or goals against without revalidation; the paper restricts the scope and notes that goalies complicate goals-against work [PAGE 2][PAGE 6].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is about how analysts reason under scarcity. The “crisis habit regression” is the familiar fallback to raw goals or raw plus-minus when the sample is small. The paper opens by naming the crisis: “the relatively low scoring rates compared to sports like basketball” [PAGE 1]. Under that pressure, an analyst may overtrust a single season of goals or a raw on-ice goal differential. The paper’s counter-position is that goals are scarce and random, while shots, Fenwick, and Corsi are less scarce and better predictors of future goal differential [PAGE 1]. The expected-goals model is the counter-trap to the crisis habit: it does not discard goals, but it uses goals as a target while borrowing information from shots, hits, hits against, and faceoffs [PAGE 3]. The recursive part is that player evaluation depends on teammates, opponents, and zone starts; adjusted plus-minus tries to separate a player’s contribution from those dependencies [PAGE 2]. The Sedin example shows why this recursion is fragile: co-occurrence creates collinearity, and collinearity creates large error bounds [PAGE 2].

The counter-trap is overinterpreting the model’s own significant coefficients. Hits are significant, but the paper refuses the moralized inference: “these results do not necessarily indicate that hits are bad, or that players should stop finishing their checks” [PAGE 5]. Faceoff-event count is significant, but the paper refuses the certainty trap: it “may have come up as significant in our model by chance” [PAGE 6]. The clock is the validation discipline. The paper uses odd/even game splits rather than first-half/second-half because midseason trades and injuries can change team performance [PAGE 3]. It trains on three seasons and validates on a fourth, then cross-validates [PAGE 3]. The clock also appears in future work: repeated random 41-game splits, smaller game chunks, and checks on whether goals remain a strong indicator when chunks shrink [PAGE 6]. A well-calibrated analyst should therefore hold three clocks simultaneously: the historical clock of pre-2012 shot-metric lineage, the within-season clock of odd/even splits, and the out-of-sample clock of 2010-11 validation. The recursive lesson is that expected goals are useful precisely because they are not a single number with a single causal story; they are a model-based estimate whose interpretation depends on the model’s scope, the collinearity structure, and the validation regime.

The physical-layer veto is scope. The paper restricts the model to “5-on-5 situations in which both goalies are on the ice” [PAGE 2] and focuses “strictly on team offense” [PAGE 2]. Therefore any proposed use for power plays, penalty kills, 4-on-4, empty-net, or goalie-quality-adjusted goals against is vetoed unless separately validated. The paper itself flags special-teams and goals-against extensions as future work, noting that “one added complication that goalies are involved” [PAGE 6]. The biomechanical-layer veto is the hit mechanism. A hit is not simply a “physicality” variable; the paper argues that “after a hit, the play can momentarily be 4-on-4 instead of 5-on-5” [PAGE 5]. That is a biomechanical and game-state mechanism: the hitter and the player hit can be temporarily removed from the play, and on special teams the temporary numerical state can change from 5-on-4 to 4-on-3 [PAGE 5]. The veto is against any claim that the negative hit coefficient prescribes fewer hits. The paper explicitly says the results “do not necessarily indicate that hits are bad” [PAGE 5]. The weather/environmental-layer veto is absence of evidence. The paper’s predictor list includes goals, shots, missed shots, blocked shots, Fenwick, Corsi, zone starts, turnovers, faceoffs, hits, hits against, and shooting percentage [PAGE 2–3]; it does not include weather, travel, rest, ice quality, or arena conditions. Any explanation that invokes weather or ice conditions is outside the model and should be vetoed as unsupported by the source.

The adjudication rule is therefore: a claim survives only if it respects the 5-on-5 even-strength offense scope, treats hits as a possession/game-state proxy rather than a causal instruction, and does not import unmodeled environmental variables. Within those bounds, the paper’s predictive claims are admissible: expected-goals OLS and ridge models have higher correlation and lower MSE than goals, shots, Fenwick, Corsi, and Corsi-with-hits in the reported validation and cross-validation figures [PAGE 3]. The player tables are also admissible as descriptive rankings conditional on the model, but not as unconditional causal rankings. The paper notes that Sidney Crosby is the best offensive player at even strength according to both \(E^{\mathrm{off}}_{EV}\) and \(G^{\mathrm{off}}_{EV}\) [PAGE 4], while also explaining that Nathan Horton’s strong goal-based ranking is consistent with teammate strength: “the strength of Horton’s teammates (2.43 goals per 60 minutes) is fairly low, and when Horton is on the ice, his team scores 3.20 goals per 60 minutes” [PAGE 5]. That is exactly the kind of conditional, context-aware reading the cross-layer veto should preserve.
