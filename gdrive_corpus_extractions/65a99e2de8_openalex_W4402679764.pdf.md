This is a methodological audit of Hegarty and Whelan’s “Comparing Two Methods for Testing the Efficiency of Sports Betting Markets.” I treat the paper as an econometric source, not as wagering guidance, and I avoid any betting-line terminology. The core derivation is straightforward but easy to misread. For event \(i\) with \(K\) mutually exclusive outcomes \(j\), let \(Y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) be the outcome indicator and \(P_{ij}\) the true probability. Let \(D_{ij}\ge 1\) be the decimal payout multiple, so a unit stake returns \(D_{ij}\) inclusive of stake if the bet wins and zero otherwise. Under strong-form efficiency, the bookmaker’s own probability estimate \(P^B_{ij}\) satisfies \(D_{ij}P^B_{ij}=\mu_i<1\) for every outcome in the same event, where \(\mu_i\) is the common expected payout per unit stake. Summing \(P^B_{ij}=\mu_i/D_{ij}\) over \(j\) gives \(\mu_i=1/S_i\) with \(S_i=\sum_j D_{ij}^{-1}\). Hence \(P^B_{ij}=D_{ij}^{-1}/S_i=P^N_{ij}\), the normalized probability. This is the paper’s central identity: under strong-form efficiency, normalized probabilities equal the bookmaker’s probability estimates, while inverse decimal prices equal \(S_iP^N_{ij}\), a multiple greater than one of those probabilities whenever \(S_i>1\) [PAGE 7]. The paper states that “normalized probabilities are unbiased estimates of the true probabilities” under its null [PAGE 3], and that strong-form efficiency implies “all bets on the same event placed at the same time should have the same expected rate of return” [PAGE 5].

The inverse-price regression is biased because it imposes an additive-margin approximation on a multiplicative-margin object. If the true model is \(Y_{ij}=\alpha+\beta P_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij}\), and one substitutes \(D_{ij}^{-1}=S_iP_{ij}\) under the idealized null \(P^N=P\), then
\[
Y_{ij}=\alpha+\beta D_{ij}^{-1}+\beta(1-S_i)P_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij}.
\]
Since \(S_i>1\), the composite omitted term is negatively correlated with \(D_{ij}^{-1}\), so the estimated coefficient on inverse decimal price is biased downward. This is exactly the direction that masks a favorite-longshot pattern, because that pattern requires \(\beta>1\). The paper’s Table 1 shows the normalized-probability test rejecting efficiency for soccer with \(\hat\gamma=0.075^{***}\) and for tennis with \(\hat\gamma=0.066^{***}\), while the inverse-price test gives \(\hat\gamma=0.009\) for soccer and \(0.0166^{**}\) for tennis [PAGE 12]. The simulation evidence is even sharper: under an efficient data-generating process, the inverse-price median \(t\)-statistic is \(-11.811\) for soccer and \(-10.99\) for tennis, falsely indicating a reverse pattern; under favorite-longshot bias, the normalized method rejects strongly while the inverse method’s median \(t\)-statistic is only \(0.52\) for soccer and \(1.78\) for tennis [PAGE 14].

The gray-zone audit matters. First, the paper assumes bookmaker estimates are unbiased, but unbiasedness is not exactness. If \(P^B=P+\psi\) with forecast noise \(\psi\), then a regression of \(Y\) on \(P^N=P^B\) suffers classical measurement-error attenuation: the population slope is \(\mathrm{Var}(P)/[\mathrm{Var}(P)+\mathrm{Var}(\psi)]<1\), so the test of \(\beta=1\) can over-reject even under efficiency. The paper’s simulations set the normalized probabilities as the model probabilities and construct prices as \(D=\mu/P\), effectively setting \(\psi=0\); they therefore isolate the overround bias but do not test sensitivity to bookmaker forecast noise [PAGE 13]. Second, there is an apparent sign inconsistency on [PAGE 6]: if \(P^B=P+\psi\) and \(Y=P+\nu\), then \(Y=P^B+(\nu-\psi)\), not \(P^B+(\nu+\psi)\) as printed. The main bias result survives if \(\psi\) is read with the appropriate sign, but the exact printed decomposition is not algebraically consistent. Third, the linear probability model can predict outside \([0,1]\), and the weighted least squares weights \(P(1-P)\) vanish at the boundary [PAGE 8]. Fourth, clustering is at the match level, not at tournament, date, or weather-event level, so cross-match correlation from shared conditions could inflate rejection rates. Fifth, the simulations use observed normalized probabilities as “truth” and calibrate the alternative by deciles of those same probabilities; this is realistic but not model-free truth, so the simulation is a calibration exercise rather than an independent proof. Sixth, overround heterogeneity across leagues and tournament stages is documented by the paper itself [PAGE 6], and because the inverse-price bias magnitude is tied to \(S_i-1\), pooled estimates can mix regimes with different bias magnitudes.

The paper’s lineage runs from Fama’s 1970 efficient-markets framework through Thaler and Ziemba’s 1988 strong and weak forms, then to Pope and Peel’s 1989 additive-margin regression, and to the Mincer-Zarnowitz 1969 forecast-evaluation regression. The retrocausal trap is that the older additive-margin specification became a habit because it produced a simple coefficient test, even though the modern fixed-price market implies a multiplicative margin. The paper’s contribution is to reconnect the testing equation to the price-formation identity: \(D_{ij}P^B_{ij}=\mu_i\), hence \(P^N_{ij}=P^B_{ij}\), hence the normalized-probability regression is the correctly specified test under the null [PAGE 7]. The alternative lineage—Forrest and Simmons, Strumbelj and Sikonja, and Hegarty and Whelan—already used normalized probabilities, but the paper makes the identification argument explicit.

Multi-week traps persist. The first is treating inverse decimal price as if it were a probability; because \(D_{ij}^{-1}=S_iP^N_{ij}\), this silently encodes the overround as part of the regressor and biases the test away from detecting a favorite-longshot pattern. The second is conflating strong and weak efficiency: the paper finds strong-form failure but states that “this bias does not allow bettors to earn profits so this pattern does not provide evidence for a failure of weak-form market efficiency” [PAGE 18]. A third trap is using maximum rather than average decimal prices; the paper argues average prices better reflect returns and that maximum prices can be loss leaders with stake restrictions and account-limiting risk [PAGE 9]. A fourth trap is pooling heterogeneous margins: the paper notes lower margins for the English Premier League than for other top leagues, lower margins for Grand Slam tennis and later tournament stages [PAGE 6]. Because the inverse-price bias depends on \(S_i-1\), pooling different margin regimes can produce unstable coefficients across weeks, leagues, and tournament stages. A fifth trap is simulation circularity: the paper’s simulations use observed normalized probabilities as model probabilities and calibrate the alternative by deciles of those same probabilities [PAGE 13], so the exercise is a realistic calibration, not an independent truth benchmark. A sixth trap is clustering only at match level when tournament-day or weather-day shocks could correlate errors across matches. The paper’s own summary of the betting literature quotes that “most inefﬁciencies leading to proﬁtable opportunities to bettors are short-lived and do not occur persistently over time or systematically across leagues” [PAGE 5], which is a warning against overreading any single-week anomaly.

That is the crisis habit regression. The counter-trap is to normalize the inverse prices first, then run the Mincer-Zarnowitz regression \(Y=\alpha+\beta P^N+\epsilon\) and test \(\alpha=0,\beta=1\), or equivalently test \(\gamma=0\) in \(Y-P^N=\alpha+\gamma P^N+\epsilon\). The clock is the compression imposed by closing prices, tournament stages, and publication schedules: the analyst wants a quick verdict, and the inverse-price test can produce a misleading “no rejection” for soccer, as Table 1 shows with an \(F\)-test of 1.85 [PAGE 12]. In recursive theory-of-mind terms, the bookmaker knows the margin; the bettor sees inverse prices; the researcher sees both but may not model the bookmaker’s normalization. If the researcher assumes the bookmaker’s margin is additive, the researcher misattributes the multiplicative overround to probability and under-detects the favorite-longshot pattern. The paper’s simulation evidence predicts this: under an efficient market, the inverse-price method nearly always rejects the true null with large negative median \(t\)-statistics; under favorite-longshot bias, it fails to reject most of the time, rejecting only 13 percent of the time for soccer and 47 percent for tennis [PAGE 14]. The counter-trap prediction is that the normalized method will reject in the biased DGP and will not systematically reject in the efficient DGP, which is what the paper reports: “the test based on normalized probabilities correctly rejects the null hypothesis of market efﬁciency in all simulations” [PAGE 14]. The clock also interacts with margin heterogeneity: later tournament stages have lower margins, so a quick pooled test can mix high-margin and low-margin weeks and obscure the bias magnitude.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no physical, biomechanical, or weather layer. The datasets are prices and outcomes only: 84,230 soccer matches and 55,988 tennis matches [PAGE 9]. There are no force, fatigue, velocity, injury, surface, temperature, wind, or precipitation variables. Therefore a physical, biomechanical, or weather veto cannot be proved from this source; it can only be conjectured as an omitted variable. The correct adjudication is that a public physical or weather shock that is already incorporated into the bookmaker’s probability estimate does not veto the efficiency test, because it is part of \(P^B\). A physical or weather shock that affects outcomes but is not incorporated into prices is not a confound; it is evidence of inefficiency. The only cross-layer route that can veto the paper’s inference is if such shocks create correlated errors across matches within tournament days or weather days, while the paper clusters only at match level [PAGE 12]. In that case the point estimates remain valid but standard errors are understated. A second route is if physical uncertainty makes bookmaker estimates noisy, producing measurement-error attenuation in \(P^N\); that is a statistical veto, not a biomechanical one. Thus the physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof is negative: no such layer is identified in the source, and the defensible veto is econometric—overround singularity, measurement error, boundary weights, and cross-match clustering.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reward counterfactual veto scenarios follow. Each is a simulation or audit that can be checked by a fixed metric, with predictions stated before running.

1. Zero-overround counterfactual. Set \(S_i=1\) for every event, so \(\mu_i=1\), \(D_{ij}^{-1}=P^B_{ij}\), and inverse decimal price equals normalized probability. Prediction: the mechanical bias term \(1-S_i\) is zero; the inverse-price median \(t\)-statistic under efficiency should move from the paper’s \(-11.811\) for soccer and \(-10.99\) for tennis toward zero, matching the normalized-probability median \(t\)-statistic. If the inverse-price method still rejects the true efficient null after \(S_i=1\), the cause is not overround but another specification error. This is a direct veto of any claim that the inverse-price method is intrinsically valid.

2. Bookmaker-estimate noise injection. Generate \(P^B=P+\psi\) with \(\psi\) mean-zero and independent of \(P\), set \(D=\mu/P^B\), and generate outcomes from \(P\). Prediction: the normalized-probability slope is attenuated to \(\mathrm{Var}(P)/[\mathrm{Var}(P)+\mathrm{Var}(\psi)]<1\), so \(\hat\gamma<0\) and the test of \(\beta=1\) over-rejects under efficiency as \(\mathrm{Var}(\psi)\) grows. The inverse-price slope remains downward biased because of \(S_i>1\). This scenario is verifiable by measuring rejection rates at the five percent level across 10,000 replications and by comparing the estimated slope to the attenuation formula. It vetoes the strong claim that normalized probabilities always provide an exact test when bookmaker estimates are merely unbiased rather than exact.

3. Cross-match correlation via tournament-day or weather-day shocks. Keep prices efficient but add a common shock to outcomes within tournament dates or venues. Prediction: match-level clustering understates standard errors and the normalized-probability test’s false rejection rate exceeds five percent; two-way clustering by match and date, or a wild cluster bootstrap, restores approximately five percent size. If the physical or weather layer is to act as a veto, this is its verifiable signature: inflated rejection rates without corresponding point-estimate bias. If no rejection-rate inflation appears, the cross-layer veto fails.
