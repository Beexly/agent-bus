Historical and scientific lineage plus multi-week traps.

The immediate genealogy on [PAGE 350] runs from Blodgett (1929) to Tolman (1932), with Peterson’s mental-maze studies (1920, 1922, 1929) appended as a human analogue. Blodgett’s latent-learning design deliberately removed the goal-box food during a pre-training phase so that path length carried no differential value; Tolman extracted the lesson that “acquisition” can outrun “appearance.” This positioned Tolman against Hullian drive-reduction orthodoxy, in which learning was theoretically synonymous with reinforcement-driven habit formation. If Hull’s equation made habit strength a monotonic function of reinforced drive reduction, then flat performance under non-reward implied flat learning; Tolman’s counterexample falsified that identity by decoupling representation from performance.

The multi-week trap in this lineage is methodological: behaviorists evaluated learning solely through the performance curve during reinforced trials. Because latent acquisition leaves no performance trace until reward is introduced, any experimental window that ends before reward introduction records a false negative—zero learning slope despite nonzero information gain. Extending the trap to multi-week horizons, if the latent map decays over weeks without rehearsal, then a long non-reward period followed by delayed reward could produce a false negative at test even though acquisition occurred early. Tolman’s [PAGE 350] passage does not address decay across weeks, so the multi-week retention boundary is an unexamined parameter in the original lineage.

Later descendants include cognitive-map theory, successor representations in reinforcement learning, and model-based RL (e.g., Dyna-style planning), all of which treat latent state-value learning as separable from action policy. The modern analogue of the multi-week trap is an evaluation protocol that measures only policy rollout regret and never probes the learned model through counterfactual state queries. A system can possess an accurate transition model (high I(Maze; b)) yet behave suboptimally because the policy-improvement step has not been triggered—exactly Tolman’s release discontinuity on [PAGE 350].

The counter-trap is the inverse error: assuming that because performance changed immediately upon reward, all learning must have occurred in that instant. Tolman blocks this by showing the reversal is too fast for trial-and-error stamping-in, forcing recognition of a pre-existing latent structure. Retrocausally, the later performance event reveals an earlier acquisition event that left no behavioral fossil during its formation.

Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. When an organism or agent is placed under stress, fatigue, or high cognitive load, the recursive theory of mind predicts regression from the latent short-path policy to the habitually traversed long path, even if the subject “knows” the short path. In Blodgett’s terms, the non-reward period establishes a long-path habit through sheer repetition (“continued to take the long path with almost … as great a frequency as the short path” [PAGE 350]). If a crisis condition is imposed—time pressure, exhaustion, or an unexpected maze perturbation—the agent may revert to the over-practiced long path despite possessing the latent short-path representation. The regression is not loss of knowledge; it is policy capture by the stronger habitual frequency distribution. Units: habit strength can be indexed by path-choice entropy H(π) and by the KL divergence D_KL(π_crisis || π_latent), which should increase under stress.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the expectation that immediate, near-perfect performance after reward introduction proves trial-and-error learning occurred during the rewarded phase only. Tolman explicitly rejects this: the “very next trial” near-100% shift ([PAGE 350]) cannot be explained by gradual reinforcement of the short path during non-reward because the short path was never differentially reinforced. The counter-trap is therefore to treat discontinuity as evidence of prior acquisition rather than as evidence of one-trial brilliance. A recursive theory of mind must model the experimenter’s own inference: the observer sees sudden performance and must avoid attributing all causation to the most recent differential effect.

Clock. The clock variable is the trial index T* at which r_t switches from 0 to 1. In Tolman’s design, T* is the moment food enters the goal box ([PAGE 350]). The behavioral signature is a step function in V_t at T*: V_t ≈ 0 for t ≤ T*, V_{T*+1} ≈ 1.0. If the clock is manipulated—delaying reward introduction by weeks, or introducing reward intermittently—the step should remain locked to the first post-reward probe if the latent representation is stable, or should smear/decay if the representation degrades. The clock also governs extinction: if reward is removed again after performance has peaked, does performance regress to the pre-reward long-path baseline, revealing that the habit layer was never overwritten, only superseded by the latent goal-directed layer? That regression timing is a clock-dependent test of whether acquisition truly persisted independent of performance.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof.

Physical layer veto. The maze itself is a physical constraint. If the short path is blocked by a barrier, narrowed below the subject’s body width, or rendered traversable only by a motor action the subject cannot perform (e.g., climbing when the subject is a rat that has been lamed), then π_t(p_short) = 0 regardless of b_t(p_short). This is a structural veto: knowledge is present but expression is physically prohibited. Tolman’s [PAGE 350] long-path/short-path maze assumes both paths are physically traversable; violating that assumption voids the performance inference without voiding the acquisition claim.

Biomechanical layer veto. Running speed, paw fatigue, and turning radius impose biomechanical costs that are distinct from reward value. Even when food makes the short path “better” in nutritional terms, a painful surface, an incline, or a required tight turn can make the long path energetically preferable. The differential effect r_t = 1 is then contaminated by a biomechanical differential c_t (cost), so the net path value is r_t − c_t. If c_t exceeds the food value, the subject rationally persists on the long path. This veto does not falsify latent learning; it falsifies a one-dimensional reward model. Units: metabolic cost in joules per meter, or normalized effort units; reward in caloric or utility units. The breakdown regime is c_t ≥ r_t.

Weather/environment layer veto. Tolman’s experiments are conducted in a controlled laboratory, so weather is technically extraneous. However, as a generalization layer, ambient temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure can alter rodent activity levels and maze-running vigor. If temperature falls below the thermoneutral zone, the subject may reduce overall locomotion, flattening π_t differences not because knowledge is absent but because motor output is globally suppressed. The veto condition is a global performance suppression factor s_env ∈ [0,1] multiplying all path velocities; when s_env → 0, V_t → 0 even if b_t encodes the short path. This is a nondiscriminatory veto: it affects short and long paths equally, preserving the equality of frequencies while masking latent knowledge.

Adjudication rule. A veto is sustained only if it operates differentially on the performance layer without affecting the acquisition layer, or if it suppresses performance globally. If the short path remains physically open, biomechanically affordable, and environmentally accessible, yet the subject still fails to switch after reward introduction, then the failure cannot be attributed to these vetoes; it must be assigned to an acquisition or representation breakdown (I(Maze; b_t) ≈ 0). Conversely, if the subject switches immediately when the veto is removed, latent acquisition is confirmed retroactively.
