**1. The Pavlovian Empirical Base (Pre-1927):**
Before Hull's equations, there was Ivan Pavlov's empirical observation. Pavlov noted that introducing a novel sound while a dog awaited food caused a temporary cessation of salivation. He termed this "external inhibition." Crucially, Pavlov treated this as a physiological reflex of the central nervous system—an inherent protective mechanism against overwhelming stimulation. It was descriptive, phenomenological, and lacked predictive mathematical power.

**2. The Gestalt Configuration Challenge (1920s-30s):**
Almost contemporaneously, Gestalt psychologists (Köhler, Wertheimer) argued against elemental summation. They posited that a change in any part of a field alters the whole (configural theory). For a Gestalt theorist, presenting $(s + S_8)$ does not produce "stimulus s minus interference"; it produces a *completely new object* $(s+S_8)$. Hull’s Corollary IV is a brilliant synthesis attempting to bridge these views. By treating $S_8$ as an "alien" element that reduces the efficiency of $s$, Hull acknowledges the disruption of the original "whole" without conceding to the idea that learning resets to zero. It’s a compromise: the habit persists, but its execution is degraded by the structural violation.

**3. The Thorndikian Echo:**
Edward Thorndike’s law of effect emphasized that practice strengthens connections. However, he also recognized that "change of situation" could disrupt performance. Hull provides the mechanistic "why": it is not merely the change of situation, but the specific *afferent interference* caused by the novel elements within that changed situation.

**4. The Multi-Week Trap (The Contextual Fade):**
This brings us to a critical historical trap often observed in experimental protocols spanning weeks. If an experimenter attempts to replicate external inhibition over a long duration (e.g., presenting the "new experimenter" daily for three weeks), Corollary IV fails to hold indefinitely.

**5. Tolman’s Cognitive Shadow:**
Later, Edward Tolman would critique Hull’s purely mechanistic view, suggesting the animal forms a *cognitive map*. From Tolman’s perspective, the dog in Pavlov’s quote isn't just suffering afferent interference; it is confused because its internal hypothesis ("My trusted experimenter is giving me food") is violated. Hull's genealogy successfully explains the *mechanics* of the drop in performance, but it deliberately bypasses the cognitive *meaning* assigned to the external inhibition, creating a tension that would define mid-20th-century psychology.
