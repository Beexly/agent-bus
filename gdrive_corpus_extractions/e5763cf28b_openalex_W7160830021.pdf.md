The source is a sports-engineering validation paper, not a prediction market or betting artifact. The core multi-hop derivation runs from camera geometry to tracking covariance to event-label stability. First hop: broadcast feed geometry determines the ball’s pixel footprint. The paper states that the Tactical feed is “a wide-view angle of the pitch” and is designed to “maintain 20 outfield players in shot at any one time, and as a result, the ball is made up of fewer pixels” [PAGE 2]. By contrast, the PGM feed “tend[s] to switch between different camera angles to zoom in on key events … keeping the ball more prominently in the camera frame at a higher pixel resolution” [PAGE 2]. Camera 1 is “a slightly tighter field of view compared to the Tactical feed” and “makes up the majority of the PGM feed, except it does not have any graphical overlay … replays, or cut to different camera angles” [PAGE 2]. Thus the first causal chain is: tighter view and higher pixel density improve ball localization, but production switching introduces replay and dead-ball artifacts.

Second hop: localization error propagates into possession-zone logic. The auto-eventing algorithm defines possession by proximity: “Possession occurs when the ball status is live and is determined based on the proximity of the ball to a player within a predefined distance pz” [PAGE 4]. If the ball position estimate has RMSE of metres, then possession-zone assignment becomes unstable whenever the ball error is comparable to the possession radius. Table 1 reports overall ball-position RMSE ranging from 3.5 m to 16.2 m across provider-feed configurations [PAGE 5]. The paper’s own summary states that “the overall position accuracy of the ball (RMSE = 3.5 to 16.2 m) remains far below the thresholds required for officiating (e.g., offside detection), or detailed tactical analysis” [PAGE 7]. This is the gray zone: set pieces anchored to fixed pitch locations can still be detected because their spatial anchors are coarse, while passes, shots, and saves depend on fine ball-player relations and ball height.

Third hop: event detection is not a pure function of ball RMSE. It also depends on player tracking, ball live/dead status, occlusion, replay artifacts, and event definitions. The paper reports that “broadcast-derived auto-events, particularly from Camera 1, have the potential to reach the accuracy of multi-camera optical tracking systems for certain events” [PAGE 1], and that “the best-case performance for most events examined in this study either exceeded, matched, or fell within 0.05 F1-score of the multi-camera system performance” [PAGE 1]. But the same results show that “shots and saves remained clear areas for improvement” [PAGE 4], with shots at best F1 = 0.24 versus 0.48 for the multi-camera benchmark [PAGE 7], and saves “not detected in any instance by the broadcast-derived auto-events” although the multi-camera system reached F1 = 0.67 [PAGE 7]. Therefore any claim that broadcast auto-eventing is globally equivalent to optical tracking is a gray-zone overreach: equivalence is event-dependent and feed-dependent.

Fourth hop: the validation design itself introduces dependency. Provider 1 supplied its own ball possession and status, while Providers 2 and 3 used ball status from the multi-camera system [PAGE 4]. The discussion admits that “Provider 1 may be conservatively estimated relative to Providers 2 and 3, which benefit from ‘borrowing’ multi-camera–derived ball live/dead status information” [PAGE 8]. This means provider comparisons are not symmetric. A naive ranking would conflate model quality with status provenance. The correct audit separates ball localization, player localization, ball status source, and event taxonomy.

Fifth hop: temporal matching creates a tolerance-based gray zone. Events are matched when “auto-events of identical type occurred within 3 s of the DCU event” [PAGE 4]. A 3 s window is reasonable for broadcast latency and human tagging, but it can absorb early or late live/dead transitions. The paper notes that “when the ball status switched to live earlier than in reality, this resulted in the wrong set piece being detected due to an incorrect sequence of events prior” [PAGE 8]. Thus the event clock and status clock must be audited separately from ball position accuracy. The deepest audit conclusion is: broadcast tracking is viable for coarse set-piece and possession-transition analytics, but not for officiating-grade spatial decisions or fine-grained shot/save attribution under the present single-match evidence.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
All operators below are definitional measurement or decision operators. Units are SI metres and seconds unless stated.

1. Position-error vector and agreement. For observation \(i\), let \(\hat{\mathbf r}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) be the provider-estimated ball position and \(\mathbf r_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) the multi-camera reference position, in metres. Define
\[
\mathbf e_i = \hat{\mathbf r}_i - \mathbf r_i , \qquad [\mathbf e_i]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
The mean bias is
\[
\mathbf b = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf e_i , \qquad [\mathbf b]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
The 95% limits of agreement are
\[
\mathrm{LOA} = \mathbf b \pm 1.96\,\mathbf s_e ,
\]
where \(\mathbf s_e\) is the sample standard deviation of the error component, in metres. The paper states that “mean bias ± 95% limits of agreement (LOA) were extracted” and “The root mean square error (RMSE) was separately calculated for position to quantify absolute error” [PAGE 3]. Table 1 reports overall ball-position values such as Provider 1 PGM RMSE = 3.5 m, Camera 1 = 6.3 m, Tactical = 11.6 m; Provider 2 = 11.3, 11.2, 9.1 m; Provider 3 = 5.5, 6.6, 16.2 m [PAGE 5]. Assumption: spatial and temporal alignment are applied before error calculation, as described by phase shifting and 360-degree rotation with X–Y translation [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if alignment is wrong, bias and RMSE absorb registration error and no longer estimate tracking error alone.

2. RMSE. For \(N\) paired samples,
\[
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathbf e_i\|_2^2} , \qquad [\mathrm{RMSE}]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
This is the operator behind the reported “overall position accuracy of the ball (RMSE = 3.5 to 16.2 m)” [PAGE 7]. Singularity: \(\mathrm{RMSE}\) diverges if any unphysical outlier is unbounded; robustly, a single gross mislocalization can dominate because the operator squares error.

3. Event-detection F1. Let \(TP\), \(FP\), and \(FN\) be matched true positives, false positives, and false negatives for one event class. Then
\[
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
F_1=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}.
\]
These are dimensionless. The paper states that “The F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was computed for each event type” [PAGE 4]. Matching is defined by
\[
M_{jk}=1 \quad \text{if type}_j=\text{type}_k \ \text{and}\ |t_j-t_k|\le 3\,\mathrm{s},
\]
otherwise \(M_{jk}=0\). The paper states matches were established “when auto-events of identical type occurred within 3 s of the DCU event” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: if the event stream is shifted by more than 3 s, recall collapses even when the event is visually correct; if replay artifacts create duplicate events, precision collapses.

4. Possession-zone operator. For ball position \(\mathbf r_b(t)\) and player centre-of-mass \(\mathbf r_i(t)\), both in metres, define
\[
P_i(t)=1 \iff \text{status}(t)=\text{live} \ \text{and}\ \|\mathbf r_b(t)-\mathbf r_i(t)\|_2 \le p_z ,
\]
with \(p_z\) in metres. This formalizes the paper’s definition: “Possession occurs when the ball status is live and is determined based on the proximity of the ball to a player within a predefined distance pz” [PAGE 4]. Singularities: \(p_z \to 0\) makes possession undefined or always zero; \(p_z \to \infty\) makes possession always one for any visible player; \(\|\mathbf e_b\| \gg p_z\) makes the indicator noise-dominated.

5. Set-piece trigger. A corner-kick indicator can be written as
\[
C(t)=1 \iff \text{status}(t^-)=\text{dead},\ \text{status}(t)=\text{live},\ \exists i: P_i(t)=1,\ \min_{c\in\mathcal C}\|\mathbf r_b(t)-\mathbf r_c\|_2 \le 3\,\mathrm{m},
\]
where \(\mathcal C\) is the set of corner locations, in metres. This mirrors the source: “a corner kick is detected when the ball status switches from ‘dead’ to ‘live’ and a player and the ball share a possession zone, and both are positioned no further than 3 m away from a corner of the pitch” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: if ball status switches early, \(C(t)\) fires on the wrong preceding sequence; if ball error exceeds several metres near the corner, the 3 m pattern test fails or misclassifies.

6. Shot/save trigger logic. The source states: “Shots are defined as attacking players losing possession immediately followed by a goal, corner kick, goal kick, or goalkeeper’s save” [PAGE 4]. In indicator form, let \(A(t)\) be attacking possession and \(E(t)\) be a subsequent terminal event in \(\{\)goal, corner, goal kick, save\(\}\). A shot candidate is
\[
S(t)=1 \iff A(t^-)=1,\ A(t)=0,\ \exists \tau \in (t,t+\Delta]: E(\tau)=1.
\]
Breakdown: intent is not observable from tracking alone, so deflected balls and passes are confused with shots; the paper reports shots at best F1 = 0.24 versus 0.48 for the multi-camera benchmark [PAGE 7].

7. Camera pixel-footprint operator. For a ball of physical diameter \(D_b\) in metres, camera focal length \(f_{px}\) in pixels, and ball depth \(Z_b\) in metres,
\[
n_{px}(t)=\frac{f_{px}D_b}{Z_b(t)} , \qquad [n_{px}]=\mathrm{pixels}.
\]
Visibility requires \(n_{px}(t)\ge n_{\min}\). The source supports the direction of this operator: the Tactical wide view leaves “the ball … made up of fewer pixels” [PAGE 2], while PGM zoom keeps the ball at “a higher pixel resolution” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown/singularity: as \(Z_b\to\infty\), \(n_{px}\to0\); if \(f_{px}\to0\) or \(n_{\min}\) is too high, detection is impossible. Occlusion is not captured by \(n_{px}\) alone, so the full veto must include an occlusion indicator \(O(t)\in\{0,1\}\).

8. Homography covariance and degenerate geometry. Let \(\mathbf u(t)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) be image coordinates in pixels and \(\mathbf H\) the pitch-to-image or image-to-pitch homography. Then
\[
\Sigma_x = J_H \Sigma_u J_H^{\top},
\]
where \(J_H\) is the Jacobian of the inverse mapping and \(\Sigma_u\) is image-measurement covariance in pixels\(^2\). Singularity: \(\det J_H=0\), camera centre lying in the pitch plane, or ill-conditioned \(J_H\) makes \(\Sigma_x\) unbounded. This is the formal reason why errors grow at frame extremities; the paper reports that “The Tactical feed exhibits more widespread errors, particularly in the attacking areas, which are located at the extremities of the camera frame” [PAGE 4].

Exact breakdown regimes: (a) \(\mathrm{RMSE}_{ball}\ge\) officiating threshold, which the paper places far above the observed 3.5–16.2 m range [PAGE 7]; (b) \(p_z\) comparable to ball RMSE; (c) \(n_{px}<n_{\min}\) or occlusion \(O=1\); (d) missing ball height \(z_b\), which the source says “impaired the detection of goalkeeper actions such as saves and claims” [PAGE 8]; (e) replay/graphics contamination in PGM, which the source identifies as the reason PGM was “weakest, which was largely due to broadcast replay artifacts” [PAGE 4]; (f) borrowed live/dead status for Providers 2 and 3 [PAGE 8]; (g) single-match estimation, because “The evaluation was restricted to a single match” [PAGE 8].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a progression from multi-camera optical EPTS to broadcast-derived tracking to rules-based auto-eventing. The paper records that optical tracking was “the fastest-growing category of Electronic Performance and Tracking Systems (EPTS) assessed by Fédération Internationale de Football Association (FIFA), increasing from fewer than 5 validated systems in 2018 to more than 40 by 2025” [PAGE 1]. It also states that multi-camera systems report “positional errors around 0.15 m and speed errors around 0.15 m·s−1” [PAGE 1]. Broadcast tracking was “formally recognized by FIFA as an EPTS category in 2021,” with “11 in 2025” certified systems [PAGE 1]. The prior player-tracking study established that “player position accuracy below 0.5 m can be achieved over a 90 min match from three commercially available broadcast systems” [PAGE 2]. The present paper extends that lineage by adding ball tracking and event detection, using a rules-based algorithm from Vidal-Codina et al. [PAGE 4]. Earlier action-spotting work framed event detection as deep-learning multiclass classification [PAGE 2], whereas this paper uses deterministic football-logic triggers.

Retrocausal traps across multi-week analysis cycles:
Trap 1: “PGM looks sharp, therefore PGM is best.” The paper confirms PGM often gives the best ball-position accuracy, e.g. Provider 1 PGM RMSE = 3.5 m [PAGE 5], but PGM is worst for auto-events because production switching and replays corrupt status. The source says systems using Camera 1 “consistently yielded the most accurate auto-events, followed by Tactical, with PGM weakest, which was largely due to broadcast replay artifacts” [PAGE 4]. It also explains that “PGM … struggled to detect any goals due to TV replays during the subsequent kickoff” [PAGE 7].
Trap 2: conflating player-centric and ball-centric feeds. Tactical is better for player tracking but worse for ball pixels [PAGE 2][PAGE 7]. The paper says “Tactical views yielded more accurate player tracking” and suggests combining Camera 1 and Tactical [PAGE 8].
Trap 3: treating provider comparisons as like-for-like. Providers 2 and 3 “benefit from ‘borrowing’ multi-camera–derived ball live/dead status information” [PAGE 8]. A multi-week ranking that ignores this will overstate those providers’ independent capability.
Trap 4: best-case F1 overfitting. The paper emphasizes “best F1-scores across feed–provider–trial configurations” [PAGE 6] and admits “performance varied highly across the three providers” [PAGE 8]. Best-case reporting is useful for capability bounds but dangerous for expected performance.
Trap 5: event-taxonomy myopia. The study evaluated nine events, while “full match analysis typically requires more than 100 distinct events” [PAGE 8]. A pipeline validated on kickoffs and corners may fail on the broader event ontology.
Trap 6: hidden temporal tolerance. The 3 s event-matching window [PAGE 4] can mask clock drift and status errors. If live/dead status switches early, “the wrong set piece being detected” [PAGE 8].
Trap 7: subjective event definitions. The paper notes “the absence of universally standardized event definitions which introduces a degree of subjectivity into the benchmarking process” [PAGE 8]. Shots are especially affected by intent subjectivity [PAGE 7].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under live-analysis pressure, an operator’s habitual model is “use the televised PGM view because it is visually clearest.” The paper shows that this habit regresses badly for auto-eventing: PGM is weakest for many events because of replay artifacts [PAGE 4], and “PGM, however, struggled to detect any goals due to TV replays during the subsequent kickoff” [PAGE 7]. The recursive model must represent three different observers: the broadcast director, who cuts to replays; the provider model, which sees a possibly non-live feed; and the auto-event algorithm, which assumes a coherent live/dead sequence. When these models are conflated, the operator attributes a detection failure to the algorithm rather than to the feed’s production logic.

Counter-trap: the opposite habit is “broadcast ball RMSE is metres, so reject all broadcast event data.” That counter-trap ignores the paper’s event-specific finding: for set pieces, “Kickoffs Auto-eventing achieved perfect detection (F1 = 1.00)” [PAGE 6], and Camera 1 goal kicks “reached as high as F1 = 0.97, exceeding the multi-camera’s 0.94” [PAGE 7]. The correct counter-trap is hybrid, uncertainty-aware use: Camera 1 or PGM for ball-centric event triggers, Tactical for player structure, and explicit uncertainty flags for shots and saves.

Clock model: the system clock is not one clock. There is the 25 Hz sampling clock [PAGE 2], the broadcast production clock with replays and cuts [PAGE 2][PAGE 4], the live/dead status clock [PAGE 4], and the human DCU tagging clock with a 3 s match window [PAGE 4]. The paper’s alignment procedure uses phase shifting to minimize RMSE [PAGE 3]. A recursive theory of mind must maintain separate latency distributions for each clock. If the status clock is early, a set piece is assigned to the wrong prior sequence [PAGE 8]. If the event clock exceeds 3 s, recall is penalized even when the event is visually correct [PAGE 4]. The operational rule is: never collapse status provenance into position provenance; never treat a 3 s match as proof of causal timing; and never let a single feed’s visual clarity override its production-artifact risk.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the paper’s ball RMSE range of 3.5–16.2 m [PAGE 5] vetoes any claim of officiating-grade spatial precision. The source explicitly states the accuracy “remains far below the thresholds required for officiating (e.g., offside detection)” [PAGE 7]. The physical layer also vetoes claims based on pixel-starved wide views: the Tactical feed is designed to keep players in shot, so “the ball is made up of fewer pixels” [PAGE 2]. If the ball’s projected pixel footprint falls below the detector’s minimum, the event must be marked invalid rather than interpolated as fact. Occlusion is a second physical veto: “tracking quality typically deteriorated during occlusions in congested areas like the penalty box” [PAGE 8]. A possession or goal event inside the box under occlusion is not equivalent to a clean-line-of-sight event.

Biomechanical veto: the algorithm uses “x, y coordinates of players’ center of mass” [PAGE 4], not full pose. Therefore events that depend on limb configuration, goalkeeper hand position, or ball contact geometry are not directly observable. The paper concedes that “the lack of ball height data impaired the detection of goalkeeper actions such as saves and claims” [PAGE 8]. Any save, claim, or deflection claim that requires ball height or body-part contact is vetoed by the current input representation. The source also identifies future work “towards three-dimensional pose estimations from monocular video” [PAGE 8], confirming that the present model is centre-of-mass limited.

Weather/environment veto: the paper reports a single 2022 World Cup match and does not test rain, snow, fog, low sun, shadow, or variable stadium lighting. Because the paper’s validation is restricted to one match [PAGE 8], any generalization to adverse weather is unsupported. The formal veto is: if contrast or visibility degrades the image-measurement covariance \(\Sigma_u\) enough that ball-position RMSE exceeds the event’s tolerance, the event is vetoed. The source’s own error map shows spatial nonstationarity, with “more widespread errors, particularly in the attacking areas, which are located at the extremities of the camera frame” [PAGE 4]. Weather and lighting would likely amplify this nonstationarity, so a weather-robust claim requires separate validation.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: permit broadcast auto-events for coarse set-piece registration, possession-transition zones, and ball-in-play timing when the event anchor is metres-scale and the feed is Camera 1 or Tactical with clean status. Veto fine-grained shot location, offside, save/claim attribution, and any event requiring ball height or sub-metre precision. The paper’s own conclusion supports this split: broadcast data “has the potential to produce auto-events with comparable accuracy to those derived from multi-camera systems; however, performance was found to be highly sensitive to the type of camera feed and quality of the underlying tracking data” [PAGE 9].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — PGM replay-artifact ablation. Intervention: mask non-live production frames, replay cuts, and graphical overlays from the PGM feed, then re-run the same auto-eventing pipeline. Prediction: PGM kickoff detection improves from the reported “all providers detected only 1 or 2 kickoffs out of 4” [PAGE 6] toward the Camera 1/Tactical perfect F1 = 1.00, and PGM goal detection improves from the reported struggle “due to TV replays during the subsequent kickoff” [PAGE 7]. Verifiable metric: paired F1 change on kickoffs, goals, and corners with replay frames removed versus retained. Veto condition: if F1 does not improve, the PGM weakness is not primarily a replay-artifact effect.

Scenario 2 — Ball-status provenance ablation. Intervention: remove borrowed multi-camera live/dead status from Providers 2 and 3 and replace it with provider-native or broadcast-estimated status. Prediction: Provider 2 and Provider 3 event F1 drops, especially for set pieces and goals, because the paper states they “benefit from ‘borrowing’ multi-camera–derived ball live/dead status information” [PAGE 8]. Provider 1 should remain relatively stable because it “supplied their own ball possession and status information” [PAGE 4]. Verifiable metric: provider-by-provider F1 delta under native-status versus borrowed-status conditions. Veto condition: if no material drop occurs, status provenance is not a confound.

Scenario 3 — Event-taxonomy and multi-match stress test. Intervention: expand from the nine reported events to more than 100 event classes and from one match to a pre-registered multi-match sample. Prediction: mean F1 falls and variance rises relative to best-case single-match F1, because the paper warns that “The evaluation was restricted to a single match” and “full match analysis typically requires more than 100 distinct events” [PAGE 8]. Shots and saves should remain the weakest classes, consistent with best shot F1 = 0.24 versus 0.48 [PAGE 7] and saves “not detected in any instance” while the benchmark reached F1 = 0.67 [PAGE 7]. Verifiable metric: pre-registered mean, median, and worst-case F1 across matches and event classes. Veto condition: if mean F1 remains within the claimed 0.05 band of the optical benchmark across the expanded taxonomy, the single-match overreach concern is falsified.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
