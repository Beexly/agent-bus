The source is a semi-supervised Precise Event Spotting paper, not a betting document, so the audit must center on representation geometry, temporal tolerance, label efficiency, and physical acquisition limits. The first multi-hop step is task definition. PES predicts frame-level event class and location from untrimmed sports video, and a prediction is correct only if its timestamp lies inside a tolerance window of δ frames and its class is correct, with δ typically 1 to 3 frames [PAGE 2]. The datasets are recorded at 25 or 30 FPS [PAGE 6], so δ=1 is a physical clock of about 40 ms at 25 FPS and 33.3 ms at 30 FPS. That clock is the real target: the model must resolve event boundaries at sub-40 ms granularity while operating on resized video where small objects may occupy only a few pixels [PAGE 5].

The second hop is representation geometry. The authors argue that PES is not coarse action recognition because it must distinguish visually similar adjacent frames [PAGE 1]. They state: “Precise Event Spotting (PES) requires distinguishing visually similar yet semantically distinct adjacent frames, making it fundamentally different from image classification and coarse action recognition” [PAGE 1]. Their diagnosis is that DINO-style projection-head invariance over-smooths neighboring frames. They write: “Through systematic analysis (Figure 4), we observe that DINO-style projection alignment over-smooths representations across neighboring frames, reducing boundary sensitivity that is critical for PES” [PAGE 1]. The empirical signature is inter-frame cosine similarity: “DINO features remain near-constant (≥0.999) across almost all ground-truth event boundaries, confirming that projection-level invariance suppresses temporal discriminability. In contrast, TFD features exhibit sharp similarity drops at boundaries — reducing to as low as 0.003 at one boundary compared to 0.999 for DINO — while maintaining a non-boundary average of 0.936” [PAGE 5]. This is the strongest mechanistic evidence in the paper: TFD is not merely a different loss; it is a different geometry of temporal separability.

The third hop is the operator chain. TGS inserts multi-scale depthwise 1D convolutions and full element-wise sigmoid gates at the end of ViT encoder blocks, with kernel sizes k_d=2d+1 for d in {1,3,5}, then shifts past and future channel groups by ±d frames and fuses scales with softmax weights [PAGE 4]. TMA computes frame-difference motion maps, scores non-overlapping spatial patches by the average of their top 2% highest-motion pixels, preserves high-motion regions, and adds temporal masking by dropping short segments [PAGE 4]. TFD aligns teacher and student backbone features, not projection-head outputs, with stop-gradient on the teacher and a supervised warm-up plus cosine ramp-up [PAGE 5, PAGE 6]. The overall objective is supervised cross-entropy plus a ramped temporal alignment loss [PAGE 6].

The fourth hop is evidence audit. Task and metric. Input clip \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times H\times W\times 3}\), labels per frame, and a prediction is correct if its timestamp is within \(\delta\) frames of ground truth and its class is correct, with \(\delta\) typically 1 to 3 frames [PAGE 2]. The primary metric is \(AP@\delta\), with \(mAP\) averaged over event classes; \(\delta=1\) is emphasized, and \(\delta\in\{0,2\}\) is supplementary [PAGE 6]. Units: \(\delta\) is frames; at 25 Hz, \(\delta=1\) corresponds to \(1/25=0.040\) s; at 30 Hz, \(1/30=0.0333\) s [PAGE 6]. \(AP\) and \(mAP\) are probability-like scores in \([0,1]\) or percent.

TGS operator. Let token features be \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times N\times C}\), where \(B\) is batch, \(T\) is temporal length in frames, \(N\) is tokens per frame, and \(C\) is channels [PAGE 4]. After layer normalization and ReLU, reshape to \(\tilde X\in\mathbb{R}^{(B\cdot N)\times C\times T}\). For \(d\in\{1,3,5\}\), \(k_d=2d+1\), and
\[
G^{(d)}=\sigma\!\left(\mathrm{MLP}\!\left(\mathrm{Conv1D}^{(d)}(\tilde X)\right)\right),
\tag{1}
\]
with \(G^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{(B\cdot N)\times C\times T}\) [PAGE 4]. The kernel size \(k_d\) and shift \(d\) are in frames; \(\sigma\) is sigmoid and produces dimensionless gates. The input is split as
\[
X=[X_{\mathrm{past}},X_{\mathrm{future}},X_{\mathrm{static}}],
\tag{2}
\]
and gates are partitioned similarly:
\[
G^{(d)}=[G^{(d)}_{\mathrm{past}},G^{(d)}_{\mathrm{future}},G^{(d)}_{\mathrm{static}}].
\tag{3}
\]
Modulation is
\[
\tilde X^{(d)}_{*}=G^{(d)}_{*}\odot X_{*},\quad *\in\{\mathrm{past},\mathrm{future},\mathrm{static}\},
\tag{4}
\]
where \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication; gates are dimensionless. Shifting is
\[
Y^{(d)}=\mathrm{Concat}\!\left(
\mathrm{Shift}_{-d}(\tilde X^{(d)}_{\mathrm{past}}),
\mathrm{Shift}_{+d}(\tilde X^{(d)}_{\mathrm{future}}),
\tilde X^{(d)}_{\mathrm{static}}
\right),
\tag{5}
\]
with shifts measured in frames [PAGE 4]. Scale fusion uses
\[
Y=\sum_{d\in\{1,3,5\}}\alpha_dY^{(d)},\qquad
\alpha_d=\frac{\exp(w_d)}{\sum_{j\in\{1,3,5\}}\exp(w_j)},
\tag{6}
\]
so \(\alpha_d\) is dimensionless and sums to one [PAGE 4]. The block output is
\[
Z=X+\mathrm{Proj}(Y).
\tag{7}
\]
TGS assumes temporal exchange is useful and that boundary padding is available. Breakdown regimes: if \(T\le d\), the shifted past or future group has no valid content; if \(T<2d+1\), the depthwise convolution is dominated by padding; for \(d=5\), \(k_d=11\) frames, so very short clips \(T=50\) or less are boundary-dominated [PAGE 4, PAGE 7]. Softmax weights are undefined only in pathological numerical cases; in practice they are stable, but they do not guarantee temporal causality because both past and future shifts are used.

TMA operator. The paper computes a motion map by frame differences between adjacent frames, divides it into non-overlapping spatial patches, scores each patch by averaging its top 2% highest-motion pixels, ranks patches, and preserves high-motion regions while down-weighting background [PAGE 4]. A formal version consistent with that description is
\[
M_t(u,v)=\left\|I_t(u,v)-I_{t-1}(u,v)\right\|,
\]
where \(M_t\) has intensity units per frame if \(I_t\) is not normalized, or is dimensionless if normalized. For patch \(P_{ij}\) with \(N_p\) pixels,
\[
s_{ij}(t)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{(u,v)\in \mathrm{TopK}(M_t\cap P_{ij})}M_t(u,v),
\quad k=\left\lceil 0.02N_p\right\rceil,
\]
with the ceiling convention stated here because the paper gives “top 2%” without specifying rounding [PAGE 4]. For a \(16\times16\) grid, \(N_p=256\), so \(k\approx 5\) or \(6\) pixels depending on rounding. Breakdown: if \(k=0\) for tiny patches, the score is undefined; if the moving object occupies fewer than about \(k\) pixels inside a patch, it can be missed; the first frame has no previous frame, so \(M_1\) requires padding or omission; global camera motion or illumination change can dominate the top percentile, causing TMA to select background rather than the ball or contact region [PAGE 4, PAGE 8]. The paper itself notes camera-motion confounding on Figure Skating [PAGE 8].

TFD operator. Teacher and student backbone features are
\[
F_t=\mathcal{T}(X_w),\qquad F_s=\mathcal{S}(X_s),\qquad
F_t,F_s\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times D},
\tag{8}
\]
where \(X_w\) is weakly augmented, \(X_s\) strongly augmented, \(\mathcal{T}\) is the EMA teacher, and \(\mathcal{S}\) is the student [PAGE 5]. Features are \(\ell_2\)-normalized along channels and the teacher branch uses stop-gradient. The temporal alignment loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}
=
\frac{1}{B|\Omega|}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{t\in\Omega}
\left(
1-\cos\!\left(F^{s}_{b,t},\mathrm{sg}(F^{t}_{b,t})\right)
\right),
\tag{9}
\]
where \(\Omega=\{1,\ldots,T\}\) is the temporal index set and \(\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)\) is stop-gradient [PAGE 6]. The cosine term is dimensionless; \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}\) is a mean cosine distance in \([0,2]\). The supervised loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}=\mathrm{CE}(\hat y,y),
\tag{10}
\]
and the overall objective is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TFD}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}+\lambda(t)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}.
\tag{11}
\]
The weight \(\lambda(t)\) is dimensionless; it is introduced after a supervised warm-up, and “the model is first trained using only Lsup for 5 epochs. The distillation term is then activated and its weight is gradually increased from 0 to 1 over the next 10 epochs, after which it remains fixed at 1 for the rest of training” [PAGE 5, PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\|F\|_2=0\), cosine distance requires an epsilon or is undefined; if strong augmentation removes the ball or contact region, the student is trained to match a teacher representation that cannot be inferred; if the warm-up is too short or the ramp too fast, the unlabeled loss can suppress subtle motion cues, which is exactly the failure mode the authors describe [PAGE 1, PAGE 5]. EMA decay is dimensionless: base 0.9995 and fast 0.99 [PAGE 6].

Complexity and units. Table 3 reports parameters in millions, GFLOPs, and FPS; GFLOPs use \(1\ \mathrm{MAC}=2\ \mathrm{FLOPs}\), and FPS is measured on a single 100-frame clip after warm-up [PAGE 8]. ViT-S has 23.45M parameters, 644.30 GFLOPs, and 1649 FPS; ViT-S+TGS has 28.89M, 1000.15 GFLOPs, and 867 FPS [PAGE 8]. These are reported values, not normalized per frame; the FPS unit is frames per second. The reported GFLOPs are per inference unit as measured by the authors, so cross-family CNN-versus-ViT comparisons are not directly fair, as the paper acknowledges [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is visible in three branches. The PES branch begins with Hong et al.’s E2E-Spot, which integrated GSM into a RegNetY backbone and is described as “a foundational model in this domain” [PAGE 2]. It then moves through T-DEED for finer temporal perception, UGLF for vision-language fusion, ASTRM for spatial-temporal injection plus contrastive learning, and ASTRA for audio-visual spotting [PAGE 2]. The efficiency branch comes from TSM, GSM, and GSF, which let 2D backbones model temporal information at roughly 40–45 GFLOPs, versus transformer PES methods that often exceed 600 GFLOPs [PAGE 2]. The representation-learning branch comes from DINO, DINOv2, and DINOv3, then Mean Teacher, FixMatch, SVFormer, VideoMAE, and video SSL variants [PAGE 2, PAGE 3, PAGE 9]. TFD is retrocausally assembled from these: the ViT and self-distillation scaffold from DINO, the EMA teacher from Mean Teacher, the shift operator from TSM/GSM/GSF, and the PES metric from E2E-Spot and successors.

The multi-week traps are now clear. Trap one is generic SSL transfer: DINO’s projection invariance is excellent for image-level semantics but can erase boundary cues; the paper’s Figure 4 is a direct rebuttal [PAGE 1, PAGE 5]. Trap two is auxiliary-modality escalation: Liu et al. use pose, ball tracking, and court detection, but that limits scalability; the authors deliberately stay RGB-only [PAGE 3]. Trap three is compute confound: TGS adds 5.44M parameters and 355.85 GFLOPs while cutting FPS from 1649 to 867, so any gain must be separated from raw capacity [PAGE 8]. Trap four is benchmark overfitting: clip length is dataset-dependent; Tennis degrades away from \(L=100\), while FSPerf improves at \(L=75\), \(125\), and \(200\) [PAGE 7]. Trap five is low-label overclaim: the 80% match to 100% supervision holds on two of four datasets, not all [PAGE 1]. Trap six is annotation ambiguity: event boundaries can be inconsistent, especially in amateur video with lower frame rates [PAGE 1]. A multi-week research program should therefore pre-register camera-motion strata, resolution strata, and compute-matched controls before celebrating a low-label gain.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is easy to name: when labels are scarce, teams regress to (a) generic DINO-style invariance, (b) auxiliary modalities such as pose and ball tracking, (c) bigger clip lengths, or (d) fully supervised baselines with more capacity. The paper’s counter-trap is task-specific: TGS injects temporal exchange, TFD aligns backbone features rather than projection heads, TMA emphasizes motion-salient regions, and a supervised warm-up prevents unlabeled distillation from treating small balls as noise [PAGE 1, PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. The clock is explicit: 5 epochs of supervised warm-up, 10 epochs of cosine ramp-up to \(\lambda=1\), base EMA 0.9995, fast EMA 0.99, default clip length 100 frames, and strict \(\delta=1\) evaluation [PAGE 5, PAGE 6]. This is a recursive teacher–student system: the teacher is an EMA of the student, so the teacher’s belief about a weakly augmented clip is recursively tethered to the student’s history; the student sees a strongly augmented clip and must infer the teacher’s representation; stop-gradient prevents the teacher from chasing the student [PAGE 5, PAGE 6].

Theory-of-mind reading: the authors anticipate that a reviewer will say “DINO is a strong SSL baseline, so your gain is just architecture or compute.” They preempt with Figure 4’s cosine-similarity evidence and Figure 5’s attention maps, and they report TGS-only, TFD-only, and combined ablations [PAGE 7, PAGE 8]. A reviewer may still say the DINO baseline is a controlled adaptation, not an exact reproduction; the authors concede this by calling the SSL/USL results “controlled adaptations to PES rather than exact reproductions” [PAGE 6]. A practitioner may believe 80% labels equal 100% labels; the paper’s own evidence limits that to two of four datasets [PAGE 1]. The recursive failure mode is strong augmentation that removes the very cue the teacher “knows”: if TMA crops or masks the ball, the student learns an invariant that is wrong for PES. The counter-clock is the warm-up and ramp: first learn event-aware features under supervision, then let unlabeled distillation refine them.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. PES correctness is tied to \(\delta\) frames, and \(\delta=1\) is 40 ms at 25 FPS or 33.3 ms at 30 FPS [PAGE 2, PAGE 6]. If the event is faster than the sampling interval, or if motion blur spans the boundary, no representation-level intervention can recover the exact frame. The paper’s own low-resolution warning is decisive: “sports videos are typically resized to relatively low resolutions, where small but critical objects such as tennis or table tennis balls may occupy only a few pixels” [PAGE 5]. With a \(16\times16\) TMA grid and top 2% scoring, the effective top-k is about 5–6 pixels per patch; a ball smaller than that can be treated as background [PAGE 4]. This is a physical veto on the motion-map operator, not a refutation of TFD in high-resolution video.

Biomechanical veto. Tennis and table tennis depend on racket–ball contact, wrist snap, and ball flight; figure skating depends on blade–ice contact, jump rotation, and landing; diving depends on board interaction and body rotation. The paper’s motion map uses adjacent-frame differences, so it selects the largest local change. If athlete motion is larger than ball motion, TMA can select the player rather than the ball. The authors themselves report that on Figure Skating “camera motion often co-occurs with athlete motion, making motion cues less distinctive” [PAGE 8]. That is a biomechanical–cinematographic veto: when global camera motion and athlete motion are entangled, the top-percentile patch score is not identifiable as event-relevant motion.

Weather and illumination veto. The source does not test rain, snow, glare, or stadium lighting changes. If deployment video has global illumination change, wet-surface reflection, or snow, the frame-difference map can be dominated by weather rather than the event. Under that condition, TMA’s top 2% patch selection is confounded; TFD may still learn something, but the claimed motion-aware mechanism is not identified. The proof is a non-identifiability proof: if the observed motion energy is dominated by a nuisance process \(N\) rather than event process \(E\), then the patch-ranking operator selects \(N\), and any mAP gain cannot be attributed to \(E\)-aware distillation. The paper’s evidence supports TMA on static-camera Tennis and weakly on Figure Skating, not on outdoor weather extremes. Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: accept TFD/TGS as representation-level candidates; accept TMA only under the stated static-camera or object-dominant-motion regime; veto strong deployment claims under severe camera motion, sub-pixel objects, or weather-dominated frame differences [PAGE 4, PAGE 5, PAGE 8].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
