Galton’s model of natural inheritance can be formalized as a decaying geometric series combined with a conditional probability distribution over phenotypic states. Let $S$ denote the subject (the offspring) and $A_{k}$ denote an ancestor at generational distance $k$ (where $k=0$ is the parent, $k=1$ the grandparent, etc.).

**1. The Operator of Ancestral Decay**
The contribution of a specific trait $D$ from an ancestor $A_k$ to the subject $S$ is governed by two successive halving operations: one for the transmission from $A_k$ to the immediate parent of $S$, and one for the transmission from the parent to $S$.
Let $w_k$ be the share of the peculiarity transmitted by an ancestor at distance $k$. As stated in the text [PAGE 150]: "the shares of their several peculiarities that will on the average be transmitted by F, G1, G2, &c., are 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, &c."
Formally:
$$ w_k = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{k+1} $$
The total hereditary mass contributed by all ancestors converges to unity:
$$ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} N_k w_k = 1 $$
where $N_k = 2^{k+1}$ is the number of ancestors at distance $k$. This confirms the conservation of hereditary potential in the model.

**2. Phenotypic Allocation and Residue Distribution**
For categorical traits like eye color, the raw contribution $w_k$ may result in a fractional chance that does not map cleanly to a single phenotype. Let $P(L)$ and $P(Dk)$ be the probabilities of Light and Dark eye colors in the general population. The text provides the ratio $61.2 : 26.1$ [PAGE 150].
If a parental combination yields a "residue" $R$ (e.g., $R = 0.40$) that must be allocated between Light and Dark, Galton uses the population proportion $\pi_L = \frac{61.2}{61.2 + 26.1} \approx 0.701$.
The allocation operator $\mathcal{A}$ distributes $R$ as:
$$ \mathcal{A}(R, L) = R \cdot \pi_L = 0.40 \times 0.701 \approx 0.28 $$
$$ \mathcal{A}(R, Dk) = R \cdot (1 - \pi_L) = 0.40 \times 0.299 \approx 0.12 $$
This matches the text's allotment of "0.28 out of the above residue of 0.40 to the heritage of light, and 0.12 to the heritage of dark" [PAGE 150].

**3. Hazel Eye Transition Matrix**
For intermediate phenotypes like hazel eyes, Galton applies a fixed deterministic split:
$$ P(L | \text{Hazel}) = \frac{2}{3}, \quad P(Dk | \text{Hazel}) = \frac{1}{3} $$
Thus, if a parent contributes a total chance $C = 0.30$, the split is:
$$ \text{Light} = 0.30 \times \frac{2}{3} = 0.20 $$
$$ \text{Dark} = 0.30 \times \frac{1}{3} = 0.10 $$

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Galton’s 1889 formulation sits at the nexus of Victorian eugenics and the dawn of biometry. Historically, it represents a desperate attempt to quantify heredity before the rediscovery of Mendel’s laws in 1900. The "retrocausal" aspect lies in how modern quantitative genetics looks back at this work: Galton’s "fractional chances" were not errors but proto-genomic predictions of polygenic risk scores. He was unknowingly modeling the additive effects of thousands of loci, where the "residue" corresponds to the background genetic noise of the population.

The multi-week trap embedded in this lineage is the "blending inheritance" fallacy. For decades, scientists rejected Mendel because his particulate model seemed incompatible with the continuous variation Galton observed. Galton’s mathematical elegance provided a false sense of mechanistic understanding, trapping geneticists in a statistical cul-de-sac until Fisher reconciled Mendelian particles with Gaussian distributions in 1918. The "hazelnut" intermediate category was a pragmatic patch for the lack of allelic resolution, a workaround that persisted in early 20th-century breeding programs until molecular markers allowed direct tracking of dominant/recessive alleles. The historical lesson is that statistical regularities (regression to the mean) can exist without the assumed causal mechanism (blending); Galton captured the former perfectly while being blind to the latter.
