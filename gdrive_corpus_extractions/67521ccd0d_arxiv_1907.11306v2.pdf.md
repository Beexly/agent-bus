The paper’s multi-hop argument begins with a rejection of the standard independence assumption. The first hop is detector ontology: detections are not deterministic truths but random-set measurements with false negatives and false positives. The abstract states that “undetected objects and false detections will persist in certain conditions” [PAGE 1], so the tracker must model persistence, not merely instantaneous error. The second hop is the standard MOT factorization: the measurement model in Eq. (9) assumes each object generates at most one detection with probability \(r_D(x)\), that this probability is independent between objects and across time, and that false detections are Poisson clutter [PAGE 3]. The third hop is the violation: complex learned detectors produce errors that are “consistent functions of the sensor and environment” [PAGE 1], and the paper says directly, “The reality is that detections at nearby timesteps are highly correlated” [PAGE 4]. The fourth hop is the repair: false-negative persistence is encoded by a binary detectability state in Eq. (20), with updates in Eqs. (21)–(22); false-positive persistence is encoded by a genuine/false object mixture in Eqs. (23)–(26), with a survival model that makes false objects unlikely to move or persist across viewpoints [PAGE 4–5]. The fifth hop is implementation: PDFMHT is a hypothesis-oriented MHT over BEV vehicle rectangles, with a 3 m tile grid, occupancy grid, occlusion reasoning, and detector-score-based genuity [PAGE 6]. The sixth hop is empirical: Table II reports CLEAR MOTA at 0.3 BEV IoU. Point-RCNN gives 86.6, without detectability 86.4, without genuity 81.4, while AB3DMOT gives 82.5; VoxelJones gives 74.7, without detectability 73.4, without genuity 60.6, and subselected environment 72.4; MonoGRNet gives 42.6; MonoGRNet plus sparse lidar gives 46.5, without genuity 33.4, without detectability 45.7 [PAGE 8]. The dominant empirical signal is therefore genuity, not detectability, for the strong detector.

The gray-zone audit is where the derivation must be handled carefully. First, the source does not derive the data-association step; it explicitly avoids it because “its implementation is largely unaffected by the discussed subject” [PAGE 2], and later says approximations exist for association at high speed [PAGE 4]. Thus any claim that persistence modeling solves association complexity is outside the source. Second, the detectability result is weak on Point-RCNN: “The addition of a detectability feature is not shown to impact this tracker and detector” [PAGE 8]. It does help VoxelJones and the fused model, but the source does not provide a parameter sweep over Markov half-life. Third, the false-positive model assumes “most false positives are stationary objects” [PAGE 2] and that false detections are “unlikely to move and unlikely to persist when viewed from different angles” [PAGE 5]; this is a plausible traffic-scene prior, not a universal detector law. Fourth, the empirical base is the first 10 Kitti tracking scenes with 281 vehicles and 8307 annotations [PAGE 7], with overlap thresholds 0.3, 0.7, and 0.1 for one-second prediction [PAGE 7–8]. That is a narrow benchmark slice. Fifth, the paper reports no weather ablation, no rain/snow/fog variable, and no precipitation-conditioned detector failure analysis. The closest physical evidence is the Fig. 1 example where a distant leading vehicle is undetected for three timesteps, “likely due to the distance of the car as well as absorption of lidar scans by black objects” [PAGE 4]. Formal state and units. Let time be indexed by \(t\) in seconds or by discrete scan index \(k\in\mathbb{N}\). A tracked object state is \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\), containing BEV position in meters, orientation in radians, length and width in meters, speed in meters per second, and yaw rate in radians per second; the implementation uses “position, orientation, length and width, speed in the direction of orientation, and rate of angle change” [PAGE 6]. A detection is \(z\), with position, orientation, shape, and a confidence score that is “the expected probability that this is a genuine detection” [PAGE 6]. Probabilities \(r_D,r_S,p_{\text{new,gen}},P(x\text{ genuine})\) are dimensionless. Poisson intensities \(\kappa_U,\kappa_{\text{new}},\kappa_F\) have units of counts per timestep or per scan, i.e. \(\text{timestep}^{-1}\). Densities \(P_U,P_{\text{new}},P_F,P(z|x)\) have inverse measure units on their respective spaces. MOTA and IoU are dimensionless. VoxelJones uses 0.125 m binary voxels, anchors spaced every 0.5 m and \(\pi/8\) rad, then 0.125 m and \(\pi/16\) rad [PAGE 6]. The tracker uses 3 m square tiles [PAGE 6].

[PAGE 3], and the implemented component form is

Key assumptions are explicit. Detection failures are modeled as persistent latent states rather than independent draws [PAGE 4]. False positives are assumed mostly stationary and viewpoint-persistent only when the same static structure is seen from a similar angle [PAGE 2, PAGE 5]. Detection value errors are treated as white noise across time, objects, and features [PAGE 6]. The sensing vehicle’s motion is assumed known accurately [PAGE 6]. (14) and Eq. (16) are undefined when \(P_{i,j}(z_j)=0\) or \(\int_x P_U(x)r_D(x)P(z_j|x)=0\), i.e. when the detection has zero likelihood under every object hypothesis. Eq. (22) has a denominator \(1-P_{i,\emptyset}(x\text{ detectable})\int_x r_D(x)\); it diverges when this equals zero and becomes ill-conditioned near that surface. Eq. (26) is undefined when \(P(x^+\text{ exists})=0\). The association sum in Eq. (18)–Eq. (19) has combinatorial growth in mappings \(k\leftrightarrow i,j\), so real-time breakdown occurs as object and detection counts grow; the paper defers this to approximations [PAGE 4]. A detectability Markov chain with too-long half-life drags false objects; too-short half-life recovers the independent-error baseline. Sparse-lidar segmentation breaks when contiguous returns are separated by more than 0.4 m [PAGE 7]. Occupancy-grid mixing and tile resolution can drift if untracked-object motion is misrepresented [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Read backward from PDFMHT, the scientific lineage is Bayes filtering to random finite sets to PMBM/MHT, then to the persistence augmentation. The paper places itself in the probabilistic MOT line by writing the random-set distribution, the PMBM \(R(k\leftrightarrow i)\) term, and the statement that the tracker is convertible to “the standard multi-hypothesis tracker, the GLMB, the track-oriented Multi-Bernoulli filter, and others” [PAGE 3]. The detector lineage is separate: Viola-Jones boosted classifiers and attentional cascades [PAGE 2], deep CNNs for image and lidar detection [PAGE 2], lidar clustering and engineered features before learned detectors [PAGE 2], and Point-RCNN as the strong lidar CNN [PAGE 6]. VoxelJones is a deliberate retro-innovation: a “simple Viola-Jones-type object detector for vehicular lidar” with binary voxel features and gradient-boosted trees, designed for interpretability and CPU operation [PAGE 6]. The benchmark lineage is Kitti: the first public 3D detection benchmark was established on Kitti in 2017 [PAGE 2], and the tracker is tested on the first 10 tracking scenes [PAGE 7]. AB3DMOT enters as the comparison tracker with public code [PAGE 6].

The retrocausal lesson is that later failures expose earlier hidden commitments. The first multi-week trap is detector-only optimization: the introduction notes that “simple tracking techniques have sometimes been shown to outperform more complex trackers built around weaker detectors” [PAGE 1], and the paper later argues for “the value of an accurate tracker compared to solely an accurate detector” [PAGE 8]. A team that spends weeks tuning only detector average precision can miss the tracker-level gains from genuity. The second trap is the independence habit: if one assumes detector errors are independent, then three missed detections collapse existence probability; the paper’s worked example gives about 0.11 under independent failures versus 0.875 under a steady-state detectability of 0.95 with a one-timestep half-life [PAGE 4]. A multi-week experiment that evaluates only the strong detector may conclude detectability is useless, because Point-RCNN changes only from 86.6 to 86.4 without it [PAGE 8], while VoxelJones drops from 74.7 to 73.4 and the fused model from 46.5 to 45.7 [PAGE 8]. The third trap is tracklet imputation: non-probabilistic tracklets “do not inherently reason about the motion of currently-undetected objects” [PAGE 5]. The fourth trap is fusion without false-object memory: the paper reports that without genuity, lidar detections are often matched to camera detections when they more likely correspond to previously detected non-vehicle objects, and “this effect is extreme enough to make a fused tracker less accurate than a tracker using only the MonoGRNet detections” [PAGE 9]. The fifth trap is metric monoculture: the paper reports precision-recall, identity switches, and one-second prediction, not only MOTA [PAGE 7–8]. The sixth trap is overgeneralizing from Kitti’s 10 scenes and from the black-object anecdote; the source contains no weather ablation, so a multi-week weather-tuning campaign would be extrapolating beyond the proof surface.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive structure is a tracker that reasons about a detector’s failure mode while using that detector’s outputs to update its beliefs. Level 0 is the detector’s confidence score, described as “the expected probability that this is a genuine detection” [PAGE 6]. Level 1 is the tracker’s belief over object existence \(P(x\text{ exists})\), genuity \(P(x\text{ genuine})\), and detectability. Level 2 is the tracker’s belief about the detector process itself: whether a missed detection is an independent draw from \(1-r_D(x)\) or evidence that the object has entered a low-detectability state. The paper’s point is that this Level 2 belief must be carried forward, because “detections at nearby timesteps are highly correlated” [PAGE 4].

Crisis habit regression is the failure mode under clutter, occlusion, or low confidence: the standard model regresses to the independence habit of Eq. (9), treating every miss as a fresh random event. The paper’s numerical example makes the regression concrete: with \(R=0.999\) and independent failure likelihood \(1-P_D(x)=0.05\), three misses reduce existence to about 0.11, whereas a detectability model with steady-state 0.95 and one-timestep half-life gives 0.875 [PAGE 4]. In a crisis, that regression discards a real track and later treats its reappearance as a new object rather than an association. The counter-trap is state augmentation: detectability Eq. (20)–Eq. (22) and genuity Eq. (23)–Eq. (26) force the tracker to remember that failures persist. Genuity is the stronger counter-trap in the reported data: “Utilizing genuity in the tracker improves performance substantially” [PAGE 8], and VoxelJones falls from 74.7 to 60.6 without it [PAGE 8]. The clock is the temporal scale: the detectability Markov transition half-life, the survival probability \(r_S\), the one-second prediction horizon, and the tracklet window. The clock determines whether the counter-trap helps or becomes a ghost trap. If the half-life is too long, false detections persist as tracked objects and precision falls; if too short, the model collapses to independent failures. The paper’s one-timestep half-life is an illustrative setting, not a tuned universal constant [PAGE 4]. The source also gives a clocked prediction test: one-second-ahead precision-recall with overlap lowered to 0.1 [PAGE 7]. Thus the recursive theory of mind is bounded: the tracker should believe that the detector has persistent reliability states, but that belief must decay on a clock calibrated to object motion, survival, and viewpoint change.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is admissible only if a lower layer physically or dynamically invalidates an upper-layer inference. The physical veto is well supported. A missed detection does not imply object absence when lidar returns are absorbed or occluded: Fig. 1 shows a distant leading vehicle undetected for three timesteps, “likely due to the distance of the car as well as absorption of lidar scans by black objects” [PAGE 4]. The tracker also estimates occlusion per tile: “Line-of-sight sensor occlusion is determined for each tile based on the lidar returns” [PAGE 6]. False positives can be generated by static structure: Fig. 2’s false detections near the road are attributed to “a combination of objects such as a bicycle, signpost, and barrier poles” [PAGE 5]. Sparse lidar segmentation has a hard physical threshold: contiguous returns less than 0.4 m apart form a segment, and segments that are too small or wrongly shaped become inaccurate position measurements [PAGE 7]. Therefore the physical veto “no detection means no object” is invalid; the correct physical inference is conditional on detectability, occlusion, absorption, and sensor geometry.

The biomechanical veto is not admissible for this source’s vehicle targets. The state is a BEV rectangle flat along the ground with position, orientation, length, width, speed, and yaw rate; human gait, limb biomechanics, and pedestrian articulation are outside the model [PAGE 6]. The only dynamics-based genuity evidence is vehicle motion: low-confidence detections corresponding to a vehicle are quickly judged genuine because the object is “moving at over 6 m/s” [PAGE 6]. A biomechanical veto imported from pedestrian tracking would therefore be a category error. It could not adjudicate a car track without an explicit vehicle dynamics model, and the source’s vehicle model is kinematic, not biomechanical. The weather veto is unsupported by the source. The paper has no rain, snow, fog, or precipitation variable; no weather-conditioned ablation; and no weather sensor model. The closest physical mechanisms are lidar absorption and sight occlusion [PAGE 4, PAGE 6], but these are not weather labels. Therefore a weather-based veto cannot be proven from this document. If one is used, it must be labeled an external assumption and tested separately. The source-grounded veto adjudication is thus: physical sensor-geometry vetoes are admissible; vehicle-kinematic vetoes are admissible only through the stated speed/yaw/rectance model; biomechanical pedestrian vetoes and weather vetoes are outside the proof surface.
