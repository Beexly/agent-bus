The paper’s central derivation is a three-market consistency test. First, the draft-pick market is reconstructed from observed trades: if two bundles are exchanged, their market values are assumed equal, and the decay of pick value with draft order is fitted as a Weibull curve. The authors state the null as: “The null hypothesis of rational expectations and market efficiency implies that ratio of market values of picks will be equal (on average) to the ratio of surplus values produced” [PAGE 10]. The alternative prediction is directional: “the market value of draft picks will decline more steeply than the surplus value of players drafted with those picks” [PAGE 10]. Second, the compensation market is reconstructed from rookie contracts and salary-cap charges; the authors find that “The slope of this curve very closely approximates the draft-pick value curve estimated in the previous section” [PAGE 20], so early picks are expensive both in foregone picks and in salary. Third, player performance is mapped into a veteran labor-market shadow price using sixth-year compensation, then converted into surplus value: performance value minus compensation. The decisive result is that the first-round surplus slope is positive, not negative: “The estimate for Round1 is significantly positive (p<.01), with a slope of +$18,500 per draft pick” [PAGE 32]. That is a sign reversal relative to the market-value curve, and it is stronger than the authors’ original hypothesis.

Multi-hop, the chain is: trade equality implies a steep Weibull price curve with λ = .148 and β = .700 [PAGE 15]; compensation declines almost as steeply [PAGE 20]; performance declines with draft order but less steeply than compensation, with only Pro Bowl frequency roughly matching the compensation slope [PAGE 24]; surplus therefore rises through the first round. The paper’s stark summary is: “If picks are valued by the surplus they produce, then the first pick in the first round is the worst pick in the round, not the best!” [PAGE 37]. The gray-zone audit matters because the identification is not a randomized experiment. The sixth-year compensation equation is treated as an efficient veteran market, but it is still a labor-market model; cap charges are smoother but available for fewer years; Pro Bowl selection is partly a popularity contest; high draft picks may receive more playing time because of sunk-cost escalation; cohort weighting favors early career seasons; and Tom Brady is an explicit low-round outlier [PAGE 31]. The authors argue that several of these biases work against their hypothesis, not for it: if high picks get excessive playing time or Pro Bowl name recognition, measured performance for high picks is too favorable, making the true surplus curve even steeper. They also report that draft-pick value is not significant in the sixth-year compensation regression after performance categories are included [PAGE 28], which weakens the “unobserved high-pick quality” explanation. Modern external validity is a further gray zone: the paper’s compensation regime predates the 2011 rookie wage scale, so the exact surplus curve cannot be exported mechanically to later CBAs. The source supports a strong within-sample inefficiency claim, not a universal law of all drafts.

Let \(t\in\mathbb{N}_{\ge 1}\) be the overall draft-pick index, dimensionless. Let \(MV(t)\) be the market value of a pick relative to the first overall pick, in pick-value units, with \(MV(1)=1\). The source states: “We assume the value of a draft pick drops monotonically with the pick’s relative position and that it can be well described using a Weibull distribution” [PAGE 13]. The fitted relative-value operator is

\[
MV(t;\lambda,\beta)=\exp\!\left[-\lambda\,(t-1)^{\beta}\right],
\]

where \(\lambda>0\) is a dimensionless scale parameter and \(\beta>0\) is a dimensionless shape parameter; the source says “where λ and β are parameters to be estimated” [PAGE 14]. Units: \(MV\) is dimensionless pick-value units; \(t\) is a count; \(\lambda\) and \(\beta\) are dimensionless. The trade-equality operator for a bundle exchange is

\[
\sum_{a=1}^{m} MV(t_a^{H};\lambda,\beta)
=
\sum_{b=1}^{n} MV(t_b^{L};\lambda,\beta),
\]

where \(H\) denotes the team with the higher draft position trading down and \(L\) the team trading up; \(m\) and \(n\) are pick counts. Units: both sides are in pick-value units. Breakdown regimes: at \(t=1\), \(MV=1\); for \(0\lt\beta\lt1\), the derivative \(\partial MV/\partial t\) is unbounded as \(t\to1^{+}\), producing a vertical tangent at the top of the draft; for \(\beta=1\), the curve is exponential with constant proportional decay; for \(\beta>1\), the initial slope is zero and decay accelerates later. If \(\lambda=0\), all picks are equal; if \(\beta=0\) and \(t>1\), the expression collapses toward a step drop \(\exp(-\lambda)\); if \(t\lt1\), the operator is outside the defined domain. The fitted values are “λ =.148 (se=.03) and β =.700 (se=.033)” with “R2 =0.999” [PAGE 15]; therefore the estimated operator has a steep initial drop and convex decay.

Future picks add a time-discount operator:

\[
MV(t,n;\lambda,\beta,\rho)
=
\exp\!\left[-\lambda\,(t-1)^{\beta}\right]\exp[-\rho n],
\]

where \(n\) is years in the future and \(\rho\) is the annual discount rate in \(\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\). The source reports: “The estimated discount rate, ρ, is a staggering 173.8% (se=.141) per year” [PAGE 15]. Units: \(n\) in years, \(\rho\) in \(\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\), and the annual discount factor is \(e^{-\rho}=e^{-1.738}\approx0.176\). Breakdown: \(\rho\to0\) removes time discounting; \(\rho\to\infty\) sends all future picks to zero; finite positive \(\rho\) preserves the rule-of-thumb pattern that “a pick in this year’s round n is equivalent to a pick in next year’s round n-1” [PAGE 17].

where \(Cat_{k,i,5}\) is the player’s relative frequency in performance category \(k\) over his first five years, \(\mathbf{I}_i\) is a position-indicator vector, and compensation is left-censored at the league minimum, here \(\log(300000)\) [PAGE 28]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is an explicit chain of judgment-and-decision biases. It begins with Kahneman and Tversky’s non-regressive prediction result: intuitive forecasts are “more extreme and more varied than is justified by the evidence on which they are based,” so NFL teams should regress player forecasts toward base rates but instead over-extrapolate from scouting evidence [PAGE 5]. Overconfidence enters next: people believe their knowledge is more precise than it is, and confidence can rise more than accuracy as information accumulates; the paper cites Oskamp’s clinical-file study and notes that teams track players from freshman year and intensify testing before the draft [PAGE 7]. The winner’s curse then enters through competitive bidding: the winning bidder is likely to be the one who most overestimated common uncertain value, and “increasing the number of bidders results in more aggressive bidding” [PAGE 8]. Harrison and March’s “expectation inflation” adds the single-decision-maker analogue: the more alternatives examined, the better the chosen one looks ex ante and the more likely disappointment ex post [PAGE 8]. False consensus completes the set: teams overestimate how much other teams covet the same player, so they overestimate the need to trade up [PAGE 9]. The paper’s own historical marker is the 2004 Eli Manning example: the Giants paid a steep price to move from the fourth pick to the first, while the alternative was to move down and take Ben Roethlisberger, and the paper notes that “Manning was four picks more expensive than Roethlisberger” [PAGE 3].

The retrocausal genealogy also contains a counter-lineage: Bronars (2004) assumes the draft-pick market is rational and places the error on the player-compensation side, while Massey and Thaler use player performance to adjudicate which side is wrong [PAGE 10, footnote 1]. De Bondt and Thaler’s mean-reversion work is invoked as the antecedent for expecting the strongest regression effects at extreme values [PAGE 10, footnote 2]. The NBA sunk-cost literature, especially Camerer and Weber and Staw and Hoang, supplies the playing-time bias warning: high draft order can predict playing time beyond performance, so measured high-pick performance may be too favorable [PAGE 24]. The historical lesson is not “markets always fail” but “consensus price curves can encode a shared bias.” The paper notes that the value curve has become widely used and that “This historical consensus may lend the considerable power of inertia and precedent to the over-valuation we suggest has psychological roots” [PAGE 16].

Multi-week traps in this literature are predictable. Week 1 trap: fit a flexible curve and treat a high \(R^2\) as proof of rationality; the paper itself reports \(R^2=0.999\), but a tight fit measures internal trading consistency, not fundamental value [PAGE 15]. Week 2 trap: confuse market price with surplus; the entire paper exists because those two objects diverge. Week 3 trap: use Pro Bowl appearances as a clean value metric; the paper warns that Pro Bowl selection is partly a popularity contest and high picks have name recognition [PAGE 24]. Week 4 trap: export the 1988-2004 compensation regime to the post-2011 rookie wage scale without re-estimating the surplus curve. Week 5 trap: overread the lowess peak at pick 43 as a precise optimum; the authors themselves caution that the curve is “meant to summarize the results simply” and that they do not have great confidence in its precise shape [PAGE 31]. The robust retrocausal conclusion is narrower: within this sample and institutional regime, the draft-pick market’s steep price curve is not validated by the steepness of player surplus.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive game has three layers. First, crisis habit regression: under high stakes, a short draft clock, and an information flood, teams regress to the habit of fine discrimination. The paper describes the mechanism directly: “the more information teams acquire about players, the more overconfident they will feel about their ability to make fine distinctions” [PAGE 7]. The draft clock amplifies this: the Giants had “15 minutes in which they had to make their selection” and faced a huge upgrade price versus a move-down alternative [PAGE 3]. Under that clock, the salient comparison is “our target versus the next player,” not “expected surplus versus market price.” Second, recursive false consensus: Team A believes Team B covets the same player, so Team A trades up; Team B, holding the same belief about Team A, also trades up or demands a premium; the shared belief inflates the value of the right to choose. The paper’s summary is that false consensus “will increase the value placed on the right to choose” [PAGE 9]. Third, the counter-trap: a team that recognizes the recursion can precommit to a surplus board, refuse the auction frame, and trade down or wait. The paper’s own counterfactual is explicit: the first pick is “really not a benefit at all, unless the team trades it away” [PAGE 38]. The counter-trap is not merely “be patient”; it is to price the pick by expected surplus, not by the conventional value curve.

The clock has two meanings. The literal clock is the selection timer; the economic clock is the discount rate. The estimated \(\rho=1.738\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}\) [PAGE 15] means a future pick is discounted to about 17.6% of an equivalent current-year pick after one year. In recursive terms, the clock converts impatience into a strategic choice: a team that fears another team will take “its” player pays a premium for now; a team that fears its own future regret pays the same premium. The counter-trap is to recognize that the other team’s clock is also running, so waiting has option value. The paper’s “loser’s curse” is the equilibrium outcome of this recursion: “The first pick in the draft is the loser’s curse” [PAGE 38]. The team that loses the season and earns the first pick is handed the most overvalued asset, unless it can find a buyer who shares the same recursive belief. The counter-trap thus requires a credible outside option: trade down at the conventional price, or select and accept lower surplus. The paper’s limits-to-arbitrage argument explains why the recursion persists: “since there is no way to sell the first draft pick short,” smart teams cannot actively correct the price [PAGE 38]. The recursive theory of mind therefore predicts persistence, not rapid correction: each team’s best response conditional on the shared belief is to participate, even if the collective outcome is inefficient.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-biomechanical-weather layer is not directly measured in the source. The paper mentions that players are tested on “speed, strength, agility, intelligence, etc.” [PAGE 7], but those are scouting signals, not modeled biomechanical state variables. There is no weather variable, no injury-severity model, no load-management model, and no explicit physical-durability equation in the paper. Therefore a cross-layer veto cannot be proven from the source; it can only be stated as a conditional confound. For a physical or biomechanical layer to veto the surplus conclusion, it would need to satisfy three conditions: (1) the omitted variable must be correlated with draft order; (2) it must affect performance value beyond the paper’s five performance categories; and (3) it must be favorable enough to high draft picks to eliminate or reverse the positive first-round surplus slope. The paper’s own checks make that veto difficult to sustain. First, draft-pick value is not significant in the sixth-year compensation model after performance categories are included, which argues against a large unobserved high-pick quality premium [PAGE 28]. Second, the authors report that their cap-charge bias likely works against their hypothesis because high picks receive more signing bonus and therefore may have underestimated costs [PAGE 30]. Third, they examine “ball handlers” versus non-ballhandlers and find a significantly positive first-round surplus slope for non-ballhandlers, weakening the star-appeal or fan-interest explanation [PAGE 36]. Weather is even weaker as a veto: the paper’s outcome is a labor-market surplus, not a single-game performance, and weather would have to systematically favor late-round picks across many seasons and positions to reverse the sign. Biomechanical injury risk is the strongest conditional challenge, because durability is a real omitted performance dimension. But it would need to be strongly draft-order-correlated in the direction of high-pick fragility. The source provides no such evidence. Adjudication: physical, biomechanical, and weather vetoes are not sustained on the source record; they remain conditional, testable confounds, not proven rebuttals.
