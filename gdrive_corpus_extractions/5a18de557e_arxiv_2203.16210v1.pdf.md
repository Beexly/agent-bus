<THINK_TRACE>
The paper is a bi-level learning system wrapped around a min-cost flow tracker. The multi-hop derivation is: detections \(D=\{d_i\}\) define a directed flow graph; a trajectory set \(T\) is scored by a Bayesian posterior \(P(T|D)\propto P(D|T)P(T)\); taking negative logarithms turns trajectory probability into additive edge and node costs; disjoint-path and flow-conservation constraints turn the combinatorial search into a constrained integer linear program \(x^*=\arg\min_{x\in X} c(f;w)^T x\) subject to \(Ax=b\) and \(Gx\le h\), with \(x\in\{0,1\}^n\) [PAGE 3 of 11]. The paper states that because the constraint matrices are uni-modular, “the solution to such an ILP problem can be optimally obtained by solving its relaxed version” [PAGE 1 of 11]. The learned object is the cost vector \(c(f;w)\), where \(f\) is an edge-feature representation and \(w\) is an MLP parameterization.

The learning problem is not ordinary supervised learning on edges. The upper-level loss \(L(\hat x,x^{gt})\) depends on the lower-level optimizer’s output. Thus the gradient chain is \(dL/dw=(dL/d\hat x)(d\hat x/dc)(dc/dw)\) [PAGE 4 of 11]. The hard term is \(d\hat x/dc\). The paper proposes to differentiate through the optimal KKT conditions of a convex problem using the implicit function theorem. The gray-zone is exactly here: for a purely linear objective \(f(x)=c^T x\), the Hessian \(\nabla_x^2 f(x)\) is zero, so the KKT linear system is singular and \(d\hat x/dc\) is not usable. The paper says this explicitly: “\(\nabla_x^2 f(x)\) would become 0 due to the linear objective. As a result, the left-hand side of Eq. 5 would become singular” [PAGE 4 of 11]. The fix is two-part: add Tikhonov damping \(f(x)=c^T x+\gamma\|x\|_2^2\), and relax \(x\in\{0,1\}^n\) to \(x\in[0,1]\). This converts the lower-level LP into a strictly convex quadratic program, because \(Q\succ 0\), and “the quadratic objective is strictly convex” [PAGE 4 of 11]. The new KKT matrix uses \(Q\) in place of the zero Hessian, and the Jacobian is obtained by solving Eq. 7 during the forward pass before back-propagating.

The gray-zone audit has several layers. First, the integrality argument is inherited from total unimodularity; if the actual constraint matrix or graph construction violates that structure, the LP relaxation can produce fractional solutions, and the claimed equivalence to the ILP is not automatic. Second, the differentiable-QP surrogate is not the same problem as the original LP: the damping \(\gamma\) changes the objective, and the box relaxation changes the feasible set. The paper sets \(\gamma=0.1\) and says the framework is “not very sensitive” to small \(\gamma\), but this is a validation claim, not a proof [PAGE 4 of 11]. Third, KKT differentiation requires a nondegenerate active set and strict complementarity; if inequality constraints are exactly active or dual variables are degenerate, the matrix block \(\mathrm{diag}(Gx-h)\) can be singular even with \(Q\succ 0\). Fourth, the transition cost uses \(-\log p_{ij}\), so probabilities near 0 or 1 create unbounded or vanishing costs; this is a numerical breakdown regime, not merely a modeling preference. Fifth, the tracker’s “global” claim is bounded by the batch clock: training uses \(T=15\) frames, inference uses 50–150-frame batches, and only \(\Delta=5\) frame gaps are allowed in the first-stage graph [PAGE 5 of 11]. Long-term occlusion handling is pushed into a second network-flow round over tracklets, and SOT is experimental but “not applied” in the final implementation because improvements are “marginal” [PAGE 7 of 11]. Therefore the strongest evidence is not that the method solves arbitrary global MOT; it is that a differentiable QP surrogate for a batch-level MCF improves edge-association error and some tracking metrics on MOT16/17/20 under specific detections and preprocessing.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source gives a Bayesian tracking objective and then converts it into a min-cost flow program. The posterior is
\[
P(T|D)=\frac{P(D|T)P(T)}{P(D)}.
\]
The paper says this “maximizes the posterior probability of data association given input detections” [PAGE 3 of 11]. Assuming independent tracks and conditionally independent detections,
\[
T^*=\arg\max_T\; p(T)\prod_i p(d_i|T)
=\arg\max_T\; \prod_k p(T_k)\prod_i p(d_i|T).
\]
The track prior is first-order Markovian:
\[
p(T_k)=p_{\mathrm{en}}(d_1)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}p_{\mathrm{tran}}(d_{i+1}|d_i)\right)p_{\mathrm{ex}}(d_l).
\]
This is stated on [PAGE 3 of 11], where the paper defines entry, exit, and transition terms.

The lower-level problem is
\[
x^*=\arg\min_{x\in X}\; c(f;w)^T x
\quad \text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\; Gx\le h,
\]
with \(x\in\{0,1\}^n\), \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times n}\), \(b\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}\), \(G\in\mathbb{R}^{2m\times n}\), and \(h\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}\) [PAGE 3 of 11]. Units: \(x\) is dimensionless binary; \(A,b,G,h\) are dimensionless incidence and conservation terms; \(c(f;w)\) is a cost per edge. If costs are negative log probabilities, they are in nats. However, the detection cost is \(c_i^{\mathrm{det}}=-s_i\), where \(s_i\) is a detector confidence, so that term is not guaranteed to be a calibrated negative log-likelihood in nats. The paper says “\(c_i^{\mathrm{det}}\) is defined as \(-s_i\)” [PAGE 5 of 11]. Entry and exit costs are learned scalars set to 1 after cross-validation, and transition cost is \(c_{ij}^{\mathrm{tran}}=-\log p_{ij}\), with \(p_{ij}=\mathrm{MLP}(e_{ij};w^*)\) [PAGE 5 of 11]. Thus transition costs are in nats only if \(p_{ij}\) is a calibrated probability.

The Lagrangian is
\[
\mathcal L(x,\lambda,\nu)=f(x)+\lambda^T(Gx-h)+\nu^T(Ax-b),
\]
where \(\lambda\ge 0\) and \(\nu\) are dual variables [PAGE 4 of 11]. The original KKT differentiation equation is
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
\nabla_x^2 f(x) & G^T & A^T\\
\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)G & \mathrm{diag}(Gx-h) & 0\\
A & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
dx/dc\\
d\lambda/dc\\
d\nu/dc
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-d\nabla_x\mathcal L/dc\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The exact singularity regime is stated: for a linear objective, \(\nabla_x^2 f(x)=0\), so the matrix is singular and \(dx/dc\) is trivial [PAGE 4 of 11]. The repair is
\[
f(x)=c^T x+\gamma\|x\|_2^2,
\qquad x\in[0,1],
\]
which turns the lower level into
\[
\hat x=\arg\min_{x\in X}\;\frac12 x^TQx+c^Tx
\quad \text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\; Gx\le h,
\]
with \(Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and \(Q\succ 0\) [PAGE 4 of 11]. The differentiable KKT system is then
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
Q & G^T & A^T\\
\mathrm{diag}(\lambda)G & \mathrm{diag}(Gx-h) & 0\\
A & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
dx/dc\\
d\lambda/dc\\
d\nu/dc
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-d\nabla_x\mathcal L/dc\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The lower-level loss is
\[
L(\hat x,x^{gt})=\|\hat x-x^{gt}\|_2^2,
\]
which is dimensionless squared error over association variables [PAGE 4 of 11]. The paper notes that “other loss functions between two binary vector, e.g., hamming loss, can also be used” [PAGE 4 of 11]. The full gradient is
\[
\frac{dL}{dw}=\frac{dL}{d\hat x}\frac{d\hat x}{dc}\frac{dc}{dw}.
\]
The edge feature vector is
\[
e_{ij}=\left(
\frac{2(x_j-x_i)}{h_i+h_j},
\frac{2(y_j-y_i)}{h_i+h_j},
\log\frac{h_i}{h_j},
\log\frac{w_i}{w_j},
\phi(d_i)^T\phi(d_j),
\mathrm{GIoU}(d_i,d_j)
\right),
\]
where \(\phi\) is a ReID feature extractor [PAGE 5 of 11]. These terms are dimensionless: normalized displacement, log size ratios, cosine similarity, and GIoU. The temporal and spatial units enter through pixel coordinates and frame indices; velocity in the second stage is therefore in pixels per frame if computed from positions and frame gaps.

The metrics are
\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t(FP_t+FN_t+IDS_t)}{\sum_t GT_t},
\qquad
\mathrm{IDF1}=\frac{2\mathrm{IDTP}}{2\mathrm{IDTP}+\mathrm{IDFP}+\mathrm{IDFN}},
\]
both dimensionless [PAGE 6 of 11]. The paper reports MOTA, IDF1, MT, ML, FP, FN, IDS, and FRAG.

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
- If \(\gamma\to 0\), the QP Hessian tends to the singular LP case; the KKT Jacobian is ill-conditioned or singular.
- If \(\gamma\) is too large, the QP solution is biased away from the LP/ILP objective, so the learned cost may optimize the surrogate rather than the intended flow problem.
- If \(p_{ij}\to 0\), \(-\log p_{ij}\to\infty\); if \(p_{ij}\to 1\), the transition cost vanishes. Numerically clipped probabilities are required, though the paper does not state a clip value.
- If strict complementarity fails or \(\mathrm{diag}(Gx-h)\) is singular, Eq. 7 can be singular even with \(Q\succ 0\).
- The QP is solved during training with relaxed \(x\in[0,1]\), while inference solves an ILP with Gurobi [PAGE 5 of 11]. This train/inference mismatch is controlled empirically, not by the derivation.
- The first-stage graph only connects adjacent-frame nodes and allows a maximum of “\(\Delta=5\) frames gap” [PAGE 5 of 11]; longer occlusions are delegated to the second-round tracklet flow.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is a retroactive reinterpretation of network-flow MOT as differentiable optimization. Earlier network-flow tracking, including Zhang et al.’s global data association and Berclaz et al.’s k-shortest-path formulation, treated the cost as a design object or a locally trained matching score; the tracker solved a graph problem after the cost was fixed. This paper moves the causal arrow backward: the downstream global flow solution is used to train the upstream cost. That is the “retrocausal” move. The paper explicitly frames prior cost learning as temporally local: previous studies “only consider a limited temporal context during training, e.g., two or three frames” [PAGE 1 of 11]. It also cites the Deep Network Flow work of Schulter et al. as a prior attempt to learn a global objective, but criticizes its log-barrier and change-of-basis heuristics and says its tracking results are inferior [PAGE 1 of 11]. The modern technical ancestors are OptNet-style differentiable optimization, Barratt’s differentiability of convex optimization solutions, and deep declarative networks [PAGE 9 of 11 bibliography references 1, 3, 22]. The paper is downstream of MPNTrack and GCNNMatch because it compares against learned message-passing and Sinkhorn-style differentiable matching [PAGE 2 of 11, PAGE 7 of 11]. It is also downstream of LifT and ApLift, which push lifted connections and higher-order MOT at heavier computational cost [PAGE 7 of 11, PAGE 8 of 11]. The retrocausal genealogy is therefore: hand-designed flow costs → locally trained edge classifiers → deep network flow with heuristics → differentiable convex optimization layers → bi-level MCF with implicit KKT differentiation.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. A first trap is assuming that total unimodularity transfers from the intended flow graph to every experimental graph. The paper’s integrality claim is stated for the ILP/LP pair [PAGE 1 of 11], but any added higher-order edge, non-adjacent connection, or altered constraint can break the argument. A second trap is spending weeks implementing the KKT backward pass before checking conditioning and active-set degeneracy; the paper itself identifies the zero-Hessian singularity and requires damping [PAGE 4 of 11]. A third trap is tuning \(\gamma\) without a pre-registered sensitivity sweep; the paper says it is not “very sensitive” for small \(\gamma\) and sets \(0.1\), but a project can overfit that one value [PAGE 4 of 11]. A fourth trap is comparing against a BCE baseline without matching features, detections, solver, and second-stage settings; the paper’s comparison is fair in features and second-round MCF [PAGE 6 of 11, PAGE 7 of 11], but a reproduction can accidentally change all four. A fifth trap is attributing gains to end-to-end learning when detection preprocessing or second-stage occlusion handling supplies them. The paper preprocesses detections with [5] because “network-flow based methods are very sensitive to false positives” [PAGE 5 of 11], and its ablation shows the second-round MCF raises MOT16 validation IDF1 from 43.5 to 55.1 and lowers IDS from 134 to 73 [PAGE 6 of 11]. A sixth trap is overextending the SOT post-processing claim: the paper tests it but does “not apply SOT” in the final benchmark implementation because improvements are “marginal” [PAGE 7 of 11]. A seventh trap is ignoring the clock: training uses 15-frame chunks, inference uses 50–150-frame batches, and the method permits only a 5-frame first-stage gap [PAGE 5 of 11]. A project that trains on short clips and evaluates on long occlusions can mistake a horizon mismatch for a learning failure. Finally, MOT20 crowded scenes punish pruning and memory assumptions; the paper contrasts its polynomial-time flow with LifT’s NP-hard lifted formulation and reports 1–5 minutes versus “26.6 mins per sequence” for LifT [PAGE 8 of 11]. That speed advantage is real but bounded by solver, batch size, and graph density.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer for this tracker has three regimes: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. Crisis habit regression is what the system or its designer does under pressure. In crowded scenes, missed detections, or long occlusion, the easiest fallback is local habit: use detector confidence \(c_i^{\mathrm{det}}=-s_i\), match adjacent frames, use the ReID and GIoU edge feature, or revert to a BCE-trained local classifier. The paper names exactly this prior habit: earlier methods “only consider a limited temporal context during training” [PAGE 1 of 11], and online methods “make non-reversible decisions” [PAGE 2 of 11]. Under crisis, the tracker can regress to those local decisions even though the inference graph is global. The second regime is the counter-trap: the global MCF cost is trained to avoid locally attractive but globally inconsistent links. Instead of optimizing each edge as an independent binary label, the upper-level loss sees the whole association vector and back-propagates through the QP solution. That is the intended counter-trap to local BCE. The evidence is in Table 1 and Table 3: L2 end-to-end learning lowers MSE Edge from 0.017 for BCE to 0.006, and raises MOT17 validation MOTA from 49.33 to 51.54 [PAGE 6 of 11]. But the counter-trap has its own trap: the model can overfit the training horizon \(T=15\) and the 50–150-frame inference batches rather than learning an invariant global cost [PAGE 5 of 11]. The third regime is the clock. The clock is not wall-clock time alone; it is the mismatch between training chunk length, inference batch length, allowed frame gap \(\Delta=5\), and the second-stage long-term occlusion window. The paper handles long-term occlusion with a second network-flow tracker over tracklets [PAGE 6 of 11], and it contrasts LifT’s 50-frame lifted connections with its own polynomial-time formulation [PAGE 8 of 11]. A recursive model of the evaluator must anticipate that boundary effects at batch edges and tracklet stitching can produce identity switches even when the per-batch cost is good.

At a higher recursive level, the designer models the benchmark’s future behavior, the tracker models the target’s future motion, and the evaluator models the tracker’s habits. The paper’s second-stage motion constraint uses average velocity and \(\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}\) to reject “physically implausible connections” [PAGE 6 of 11]. That is a model of the pedestrian’s future physical reachability. The SOT post-processing models the tracker’s own failure mode: if a track disappears before the last frame, SOT is initialized at the last position, and it is terminated when color-histogram similarity falls below \(\tau\) [PAGE 6 of 11]. That is a recursive self-monitoring habit, but the paper ultimately declines it in final results because gains are marginal [PAGE 7 of 11]. The safest reading is: the counter-trap works at the first-stage cost level; the clock limits its global claim; and crisis habit regression reappears whenever detections are sparse, appearances change, or batch boundaries hide long occlusions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto adjudication must separate hard constraints from soft features. The physical layer has one explicit hard-ish veto in the second stage: for temporally non-overlapping tracklets, the paper estimates average velocity and says “\(\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}\) is used to reject physically implausible connections” [PAGE 6 of 11]. Formally, for tracklets \(i\) and \(j\) with mean positions \(\bar p_i,\bar p_j\) and temporal gap \(\Delta t_{ij}\), one can write
\[
v_{ij}=\frac{\|\bar p_j-\bar p_i\|}{\Delta t_{ij}},
\]
and veto the link if \(v_{ij}>\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}\) or if the implied size change is outside a plausible range. The paper does not give the threshold or the exact functional form, so this is a conditional veto, not a fully specified proof. At the first stage, physical plausibility is softer: the edge feature includes normalized displacement and log height and width ratios [PAGE 5 of 11]. These are penalties, not absolute vetoes. The intuition stated in the paper is that “under a small time gap, the pedestrians cannot move far away, and the size of the pedestrian should not change much” [PAGE 5 of 11]. That is a physical prior, but it is learned into the MLP cost rather than enforced as a hard constraint.

The biomechanical layer is weaker. The paper uses a person ReID network and GIoU, and it uses color-histogram similarity to terminate SOT [PAGE 5 of 11, PAGE 6 of 11]. There is no explicit pose, gait, limb-joint, or biomechanical reachability model. Therefore a biomechanical veto can only be inferred indirectly: if appearance and geometry are inconsistent, the learned transition cost should rise. There is no proof that a biomechanically possible but appearance-altered person is preserved. The weather layer is also a proxy, not a meteorological model. Rain, glare, night, or illumination change can break the ReID embedding and color histogram. The paper’s SOT veto is “whenever their similarity of color histogram fall below a threshold \(\tau\), the tracker is terminated to avoid tracking false positives or drift to background” [PAGE 6 of 11]. That is an appearance-layer veto that can trigger under weather-induced change, but it can also falsely terminate a true target. The detection layer is another veto source: the paper preprocesses detections because “network-flow based methods are very sensitive to false positives” [PAGE 5 of 11]. Thus the adjudication hierarchy is: detection-quality veto first, physical \(\tau_{\mathrm{dist}}\) veto second, learned geometry and ReID penalties third, and no hard weather or biomechanical invariant. A claim that the method “proves” weather robustness or biomechanical validity is not supported by the source. The defensible claim is narrower: the system has a second-stage physical reachability filter and appearance-based termination, and its first-stage cost encodes normalized displacement and size ratios as soft evidence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes are tests whose reward is a verifiable metric computed from held-out data, not a narrative. Three scenarios follow.

1. Global-loss versus local-loss veto. Train the same two-layer MLP, with the same edge features, detections, solver, and second-stage settings, under two objectives: local BCE on ground-truth edges versus the proposed L2 loss on the QP association output. Evaluate on MOT16 validation and MOT17 validation with pre-registered MOTA, IDF1, and MSE Edge. Prediction from the source: L2 should beat BCE on MSE Edge and tracking. Table 1 reports MSE Edge 0.006 for L2 versus 0.017 for BCE, and Table 3 reports MOT17 validation MOTA 51.54 versus 49.33 and IDF1 60.15 versus 58.91 [PAGE 6 of 11]. Veto rule: if L2 fails to improve MSE Edge and MOTA/IDF1 beyond a pre-registered confidence interval, or if BCE wins, the claim that global back-propagation is causally useful is vetoed.

2. Implicit-gradient necessity veto. Keep the forward QP solver exactly as in Eq. 6, but replace the KKT-derived Jacobian \(d\hat x/dc\) from Eq. 7 with a zero gradient or a random perturbation of matched scale during training. Then compare validation MSE Edge, MOTA, and IDF1 against the true implicit gradient. Prediction: zero or random gradients should fail to reduce MSE Edge below the BCE baseline and should degrade MOTA. A second verifiable check is a finite-difference gradient test on nondegenerate active sets: perturb \(c\), resolve the QP, and compare finite-difference \(d\hat x/dc\) to the Eq. 7 solution. Veto rule: if the zero-gradient model matches the implicit-gradient model, the differentiability claim is unnecessary; if the finite-difference relative error is large on nondegenerate cases, the KKT implementation is suspect. This test is especially important because the paper’s derivation requires \(Q\succ 0\) and a nonsingular KKT matrix [PAGE 4 of 11].

3. Second-stage occlusion veto. Disable the second-round network-flow tracker over tracklets and keep only the first-stage MCF, with all else fixed. Evaluate on MOT16 validation. Prediction from Table 2: removing the second stage should drop MOTA from 42.9 to 38.9, drop IDF1 from 55.1 to 43.5, and raise IDS from 73 to 134 [PAGE 6 of 11]. Veto rule: if the second stage produces no measurable IDF1 or IDS improvement, the long-term occlusion claim is vetoed. A subsidiary SOT check is also verifiable: add the SOT post-processing and compare final MOTA/IDF1. The paper says SOT is not used in final results because improvements are “marginal” [PAGE 7 of 11]. If SOT produces a large, reproducible gain, that marginality claim is vetoed; if it produces a large loss, the SOT termination threshold is mis-specified.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
