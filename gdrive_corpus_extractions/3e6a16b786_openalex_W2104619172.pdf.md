The source is a variational computer-vision paper, not a forecasting or wagering document. Its central object is a pass region, explicitly defined as a region with high pass-success probability: “A pass region represents a region in which the pass has a high probability of succeeding.” [PAGE 1]. The model does not directly observe that probability. Instead, it constructs a three-dimensional energy volume from player positions and asks an Active grid to settle into low-energy regions. The first multi-hop step is the paper’s own motivation: “In a soccer match, players discover pass regions based on previous and current player positions.” [PAGE 1]. Therefore, a single-frame estimate is incomplete because it omits the temporal geometry that players actually use. The second step is the Active grid definition: “Active grid is a rectilinear grid structure model based on minimizing energy to extract a target object from three-dimensional data.” [PAGE 1]. Thus, the method is an energy-minimization segmentation over a spatiotemporal lattice, not a causal model of player intention.

The derivation chain is: obtain per-frame player positions; build two scalar fields, At for a pass to an attacking player and St for a pass into space; stack those fields over time into VA and VS; place a cylindrical Active grid centered on the ball carrier; minimize internal plus image energy; then count lattice-point density in each pitch cell. The paper states the stacking operation directly: “The proposed method generates the three-dimensional data VA and VS by connecting At or St with the temporal dimension.” [PAGE 3]. The grid is anchored by the ball carrier: “The center of Active grid in each frame is the position of the player with the ball.” [PAGE 4]. The pass-region geometry assumption is radial: “In soccer matches, since the player with the ball passes on a radial course, pass regions become radial shapes whose center is the position of the player with the ball.” [PAGE 4]. That assumption is powerful but also a gray zone: it biases the estimator toward radial pass courses centered on the carrier, even though real passes can be lateral, backward, lofted, or bent.

Why should multiple frames improve precision? The paper’s argument is neighbor coupling: “since each lattice point of Active grid depends on its neighboring lattice points.” [PAGE 2]. In a single-frame method, a false local minimum caused by one bad player detection appears independently in that frame. In the spatiotemporal grid, a lattice point is tied to neighbors in x, y, and time, so a one-frame artifact must be consistent with adjacent frames to survive. This is a regularizing argument, not a proof of correctness. The experimental result is consistent with regularization: Precision improves while Recall is unchanged. Table 1 reports Recall 0.85 for both methods and Precision 0.41 versus 0.27; Table 2 reports Recall 0.89 for both and Precision 0.50 versus 0.40 [PAGE 6]. The paper summarizes: “the proposed method realized an improvement of Precision.” [PAGE 6]. It also says “the proposed method estimates pass regions in which the direction of player movement is considered.” [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit must be strict. First, the gain is precision-specific, not a uniform accuracy gain. The model still produces many false-positive regions: Precision 0.41 or 0.50 means a substantial fraction of estimated regions do not contain the actual pass. Second, the experiments used manual player positions: “in order to exclude the influence of inaccurate player positions, we manually provided player positions for this experiment.” [PAGE 5]. This isolates the grid algorithm but weakens deployment claims. Third, the camera condition is restricted: “we used pass scenes from actual soccer programs in which there were no panning or zooming shots.” [PAGE 5]. Fourth, out-of-frame receivers are treated as failures in Table 1 and excluded in Table 2 [PAGE 6]. Fifth, the “probability” p(x,y)t in Eq. Sixth, the fixed velocity parameters vh and vb are assumptions, not measured biomechanics. Seventh, the paper does not compute tactical importance; it states that this remains future work [PAGE 6]. The correct inference is therefore narrow: the 3D Active grid improves precision on the paper’s 147-scene, manually positioned, non-panning, non-zooming sample. It is not evidence of general tactical prediction, and it is not a finished autonomous system.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper defines a variational cost, not a thermodynamic energy. The source calls it energy, but no joule units are assigned. If α, β, and ω are treated as tuning parameters that normalize the terms, the total cost is dimensionless. The primary operator is:

\[
E^{*}_{\text{grid}} = E^{*}_{\text{internal}} + E^{*}_{\text{image}}
\]

\[
E^{*}_{\text{internal}} = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \int_0^1 E_{\text{int}}(v(p,q,r))\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}r
\]

\[
E^{*}_{\text{image}} = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \int_0^1 E_{\text{img}}(v(p,q,r))\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}r
\]

These are from [PAGE 2]. The lattice parameters satisfy \(0 \le p \le 1\), \(0 \le q \le 1\), and \(0 \le r \le 1\). The source states: “Each point v(p, q, r) is connected to 6 neighboring points” [PAGE 2]. The spacing parameters are \(l = 1/(X-1)\), \(m = 1/(Y-1)\), and \(n = 1/(Z-1)\), where \(X,Y,Z\) are lattice-point counts along the axes. The internal energy is:

\[
E_{\text{int}}(v(p,q,r)) = \alpha E^{*}_{\text{int}} + \beta E^{**}_{\text{int}}
\]

\[
E^{*}_{\text{int}} = |v_p|^2 + |v_q|^2 + |v_r|^2
\]

\[
E^{**}_{\text{int}} = |v_{pp}|^2 + 2|v_{pq}|^2 + |v_{qq}|^2 + 2|v_{pr}|^2 + |v_{rr}|^2 + 2|v_{qr}|^2
\]

Here subscripts denote partial derivatives. The first term is a stretching or length penalty; the second is a curvature or smoothness penalty. The image term is:

\[
E_{\text{img}}(v(p,q,r)) = \omega I(p,q,r)
\]

The paper says: “Eimg is a contraction force, which draws Active grid toward a salient region in the three-dimensional data.” [PAGE 2]. In the soccer adaptation, \(I\) is generated from player positions. For a pass to an attacking player, the radius for player \(P_i\) in frame \(t\) is:

\[
r(P_i)_t = d_{t1}\frac{v_h}{v_b}
\]

The source explains: “where dt1 is the distance between the ball carrier and player Pi, vh is the velocity of the receiver and vb is the velocity of the ball.” [PAGE 4]. For a pass into space, the degree of space is:

\[
s(x,y)_t = \lambda d_{t2}
\]

where \(d_{t2}\) is the distance between the nearest defensive player and the point \((x,y)\). The probability-like score is:

\[
p'(x,y)_t = \gamma(d_{td} - d_{ta}) + 0.5
\]

\[
p(x,y)_t =
\begin{cases}
p'(x,y)_t, & 0 \le p' \le 1 \\
1, & p' > 1 \\
0, & p' < 0
\end{cases}
\]

The space-field intensity is:

\[
I(x,y)_t = I_{\max} - p(x,y)_t s(x,y)_t
\]

These equations are from [PAGE 4]. The evaluation metrics are:

\[
\text{Precision} = \frac{\text{Number of correctly estimated regions}}{\text{Number of all estimated regions}}
\]

\[
\text{Recall} = \frac{\text{Number of correctly estimated regions}}{\text{Number of passes}}
\]

These are from [PAGE 5]. The experimental settings are: “The number of Active grid lattice points is 52 × 36 × 3, with parameters λ = 15, γ = 0.2, ω = 5.0, vh = 24[km/h], vb = 30[km/h], and Imax = 255.” [PAGE 5].

Units and interpretation. The parameters \(p,q,r\) are dimensionless. The lattice coordinates \(v\) are not assigned a single SI unit by the paper because the third axis is temporal and the first two are pitch or image coordinates. If x and y are calibrated pitch coordinates, they are in metres; if they are image coordinates, they are in pixels. The temporal axis is a frame-connection axis, not necessarily seconds. The distance quantities \(d_{t1}\), \(d_{t2}\), \(d_{ta}\), and \(d_{td}\) are in the same spatial unit as the pitch coordinates. The velocities \(v_h\) and \(v_b\) are in km/h in the reported experiment, so they must be converted consistently if distances are in metres. The ratio \(v_h/v_b\) is dimensionless, so \(r(P_i)_t\) inherits the unit of \(d_{t1}\). The quantity \(p\) is bounded in the interval from 0 to 1, but it is not a normalized probability mass and no bits or nats are defined. It is a clipped score. The intensity \(I\) is on an 8-bit-like scale with \(I_{\max}=255\), so \(I\) is in intensity levels. Because \(p\) is dimensionless, \(s\) must be in intensity levels for Eq. (10) to be dimensionally coherent; the source does not assign \(\lambda\) an explicit unit, so \(\lambda\) is an empirical intensity-per-distance constant. Similarly, \(\gamma\) is an empirical inverse-distance constant for the linear score, and \(\omega\) converts intensity into variational cost.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. If \(X=1\), \(Y=1\), or \(Z=1\), then \(l\), \(m\), or \(n\) divides by zero. The lattice spacing is undefined and the finite-difference derivatives degenerate.
2. If \(v_b=0\) in Eq. (6), then \(r(P_i)_t = d_{t1}v_h/v_b\) is undefined or unbounded. Small \(v_b\) produces an arbitrarily large radius.
3. If no defensive player is detected, \(d_{t2}\) and \(d_{td}\) are undefined, so \(s\) and \(p'\) are undefined.
4. If no attacking player is detected, \(d_{ta}\) is undefined, so \(p'\) is undefined.
5. If the ball carrier is not detected, the cylindrical grid center is undefined and the radial prior cannot be anchored.
6. If \(\gamma\) is large, \(p'\) saturates at 0 or 1 almost everywhere by Eq. (9), destroying contrast. If \(\gamma=0\), \(p'=0.5\) everywhere.
7. If \(p s > I_{\max}\), Eq. (10) gives negative \(I\). The source does not clamp \(I\), so the image energy can become negative and unbounded from below if \(s\) grows without limit.
8. If \(\omega\) has the wrong sign, the image term repels rather than contracts.
9. If the temporal window is too short, the method approaches the single-frame predecessor. If it is too long or frame alignment is poor, temporal smoothing suppresses real changes in pass geometry.
10. If camera panning or zooming occurs, pitch-coordinate mapping is invalid; the paper explicitly used scenes “in which there were no panning or zooming shots.” [PAGE 5].
11. If many player detections are inaccurate, the distance fields are corrupted. The paper claims robustness to some inaccurate positions but did not deploy automatic positions in the reported experiment.
12. If the pass region touches the outer lattice surface, the paper’s fixed outer lattice points can prevent full convergence to the boundary.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicit in its own citations. The predecessor is the single-frame Active Net method of Takahashi, Kon, and Haseyama, reference 12 on [PAGE 6], described in the text as the previous method that estimates pass regions “from player positions within a single frame” [PAGE 1]. The 3D generalization comes from Doi, Fujiwara, Matsuda, and Kameda, reference 13 on [PAGE 6], which the paper cites for Active grid. The historical sequence is therefore: 2D active-region estimation with Active Net; single-frame pass-region estimation; then spatiotemporal Active grid over multiple frames. The paper’s novelty is not the invention of energy minimization or grid deformation. It is the specific coupling of broadcast soccer player geometry to a 3D lattice whose temporal axis encodes previous and current positions.

This genealogy creates several multi-week traps for a research program. Trap one is mistaking the precision improvement for a general accuracy improvement. The paper’s own tables show unchanged Recall and improved Precision: 0.85/0.85 and 0.41/0.27 in Table 1; 0.89/0.89 and 0.50/0.40 in Table 2 [PAGE 6]. A multi-week build that optimizes only Precision can drift toward conservative or narrow regions and lose coverage. Trap two is forgetting the manual-position condition: “in order to exclude the influence of inaccurate player positions, we manually provided player positions for this experiment.” [PAGE 5]. Automatic detection errors are not the same as the small yellow-point perturbations shown in the qualitative examples. Trap three is forgetting the camera restriction: “we used pass scenes from actual soccer programs in which there were no panning or zooming shots.” [PAGE 5]. A deployed broadcast system with camera motion needs a separate calibration and tracking layer. Trap four is treating the radial cylindrical grid as a soccer universal. The source assumes “the player with the ball passes on a radial course” [PAGE 4], which is a modeling prior, not a law. Trap five is over-reading the qualitative claim that the method shows “various pass courses” [PAGE 6] without a tactical-importance metric; the conclusion explicitly says tactical importance “must be calculated in future works” [PAGE 6]. Trap six is dataset leakage or scene reuse: the experiments are drawn from 147 scenes and compare methods on the same scenes, so the improvement should be replicated on held-out matches and automatic tracking before being treated as a general result.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer here is shallow and geometric. The model does not represent beliefs, intentions, deception, or opponent counterfactuals. It represents reachability through distances: \(d_{ta}\) is the distance from a target point to the nearest attacking player, and \(d_{td}\) is the distance to the nearest defensive player [PAGE 4]. The score \(p'(x,y)_t = \gamma(d_{td}-d_{ta})+0.5\) is a proxy for “attacker can arrive before defender.” That is a first-order theory of movement, not a recursive theory of mind. It assumes that the nearest attacker and nearest defender are the relevant agents and that arrival time is monotone in distance. It does not model a defender’s anticipation, a receiver’s decoy run, or a carrier’s feint.

Crisis habit regression appears when the evidence is noisy. Under bad player positions, the Active grid can regress to a habitual local minimum: dense lattice points near the ball carrier or near an attacking cluster, because the image term contracts toward low \(I\) and the internal term smooths the rest. The paper’s single-frame predecessor is the extreme habit regression: it estimates a region from one frame and cannot distinguish a persistent pass lane from a one-frame detection artifact. The multi-frame grid is the counter-trap: temporal neighbors force a candidate region to be consistent across frames. The source states that “each lattice point of Active grid depends on its neighboring lattice points” [PAGE 2]. That is the mechanism that blocks a one-frame false positive from dominating, but it also creates a new trap: over-smoothing. If the true pass lane changes quickly because a defender steps across or a receiver changes direction, the temporal smoothness term can delay the grid and preserve an obsolete region.

The clock is the temporal axis \(r\) and the frame window. The paper connects per-frame fields \(A_t\) and \(S_t\) with the temporal dimension [PAGE 3], and the grid has \(Z=3\) lattice points in the reported experiment [PAGE 5]. That is a very short clock. It provides enough temporal context to suppress independent single-frame noise, but it is not a long-horizon tactical memory. A longer clock would improve stability but increase latency and risk mixing different passing phases. A shorter clock would reduce latency but collapse toward the previous method. The correct counter-trap is therefore not “always use more frames.” It is “use enough temporal context to make the region stable, while explicitly detecting phase changes such as a carrier turn, a defender interception step, or a new ball trajectory.” The model has no explicit phase-change detector. It only has smoothness and a fixed window. That is the recursive-theory gap: the system can regularize over time, but it cannot reason about why the relevant agents’ intentions have changed.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is strong because the paper’s experiments exclude camera motion. The source states: “we used pass scenes from actual soccer programs in which there were no panning or zooming shots.” [PAGE 5]. The equations use pitch coordinates and distances; if the camera pans or zooms, the mapping from image coordinates to pitch coordinates is time-varying. A fixed grid in pitch coordinates would then be fed inconsistent geometry. The veto is therefore: if camera motion is present and no separate calibration-and-tracking layer is supplied, the pass-region output is not valid under the paper’s assumptions. The paper does not prove invariance to panning or zooming.

The biomechanical layer veto is also formal. Equation (6) fixes receiver speed and ball speed as constants: “vh is the velocity of the receiver and vb is the velocity of the ball.” [PAGE 4]. The reported values are \(v_h=24\) km/h and \(v_b=30\) km/h [PAGE 5]. The radius sensitivity is:

\[
\frac{\partial r(P_i)_t}{\partial v_h} = \frac{d_{t1}}{v_b} > 0
\]

\[
\frac{\partial r(P_i)_t}{\partial v_b} = -\frac{d_{t1}v_h}{v_b^2} < 0
\]

Thus, if the true receiver speed is lower than the assumed \(v_h\), the model overestimates the reachable radius. If the true ball speed is lower than the assumed \(v_b\), the model underestimates the reachable radius because the ball takes longer to arrive. Acceleration, fatigue, turning cost, surface traction, and player orientation are absent. The paper’s fixed velocities are an assumption that can be vetoed by any biomechanical measurement showing a different effective speed in the relevant phase. This is not a toy ratio; it is the source’s own radius formula, and its partial derivatives show the direction of bias.

The weather layer must be vetoed as unsupported. No equation in the source contains wind, rain, temperature, humidity, or surface friction. Weather can change effective ball speed, effective receiver acceleration, and traction. Because \(v_b\) and \(v_h\) are fixed constants in Eq. (6), any weather-induced change in those effective speeds becomes model bias rather than a modeled effect. The source cannot adjudicate a weather claim. A weather-modified deployment would need either measured ball speed and player speed or an explicit uncertainty model over \(v_h\) and \(v_b\). Without that, the correct adjudication is “no weather validity claim.”

The cross-layer veto rule is therefore: accept the paper’s pass-region estimate only when the camera is stable, the ball carrier is detected, at least one attacking and one defensive player are available for the relevant distance fields, the pitch calibration is consistent, and the fixed speed assumptions are plausible for the play phase. If \(v_b=0\), Eq. (6) is singular. If no defender is present, \(d_{t2}\) and \(d_{td}\) are undefined. If no attacker is present, \(d_{ta}\) is undefined. If the receiver is out of frame, the paper treats the case as a failure in Table 1 and excludes it in Table 2 [PAGE 6]. These are not minor edge cases; they are boundary conditions that determine whether the output is defined at all.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
