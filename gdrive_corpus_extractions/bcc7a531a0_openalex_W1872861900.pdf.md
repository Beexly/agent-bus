<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock, modeled on the mathematical operator rather than any wagering context.

Treat the solution operator as a recursive agent operating on the space C(X,Y) of continuous maps. Under crisis—namely, when r is not Fréchet differentiable—the agent does not halt; it regresses to its contingent habit, replacing the single-valued derivative Dr(z) by a set-valued contingent derivative with nonempty values ([PAGE 5]). This regression is the default mode: Lipschitz maps are generically non-differentiable but contingently differentiable, so the agent’s “crisis response” is to widen the solution concept from single-valued maps r to set-valued maps R. The paper’s central move is to prove that even this widened search converges to a well-behaved largest contingent solution R_λ ([PAGE 9, Theorem 1.1]).

The counter-trap is the comparison/localization layer. The localization property (Theorem 3.1, [PAGE 19]) bounds any bounded single-valued contingent solution r inside the set-valued map R_λ; the maximum principle (Lemma 3.2, [PAGE 19–20]) and Theorems 3.3–3.4 ([PAGE 21–22]) act as counter-traps that catch any rogue solution M attempting to deviate from r, forcing M to remain within a computable neighborhood determined by sup ||G(z,r(z)) − G(z,y)||. These theorems are anti-fragility devices: they do not assume uniqueness a priori; they bound the damage when uniqueness is absent.

The clock is the exponential weight e^{−λt}. On [PAGE 12], the estimate ||z(t)|| ≤ (||z|| + 1)e^{at} shows that the raw trajectory grows at rate a; the clock e^{−λt} must run faster than a for the integral defining R_λ to converge ([PAGE 12–13]). On [PAGE 14–15], the clock is extended from finite intervals [0,T] to [0,2T] to [0,∞) by concatenating Filippov solutions, which is only valid if the contraction per cycle exceeds the growth per cycle. If the clock is too slow (λ too small), the weighted integral diverges and the recursive fixed-point map H on [PAGE 17] ejects candidates from the compact ball B_k. Thus the recursion H(s) ↦ r has a fixed point exactly when the clock outpaces the crisis habit regression, and the counter-trap comparison theorems are meaningful only inside that ticking window.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof across three layers, with exact veto conditions.

Layer 1 — Physical / hyperbolic PDE layer. The equation is first-order hyperbolic only if A is coercive: ⟨Az,z⟩ ≥ A‖z‖² with A > 0 ([PAGE 5]). The semigroup e^{−At} is dissipative precisely under this condition. Veto: if A ≤ 0 (non-coercive or anti-damping operator), the weight e^{−λt} cannot dominate the natural growth of the system, the resolvent integral diverges, and no bounded contingent solution is guaranteed. Physical-layer veto is absolute and precedes all analysis.

Layer 2 — Biomechanical / feedback-control layer. The paper motivates the inclusion through feedback controls r: K → Y regulating solutions of ż(t) = f(z(t),u(t)), u(t) ∈ U(z(t)), with the growth constraint ‖u′(t) − A u(t)‖ ≤ c φ(z(t),u(t)) ([PAGE 6–7]). This is a biomechanical regulation condition: the control must not outpace the dissipation A. Veto: if U is not closed, f lacks linear growth, or φ grows super-linearly in ‖z‖ + ‖u‖, no absolutely continuous feedback control satisfying the constraint exists, and the inclusion has no single-valued solution.

Layer 3 — Weather / disturbance layer. G: X ⇉ Y represents disturbances or set-valued dynamics and must be Lipschitz with nonempty convex compact values ([PAGE 16]). Convexity is used for the projection argument in the Filippov proof ([PAGE 14]), and compactness ensures measurable selections. Veto: if G is non-convex, unbounded, or discontinuous, the measurable selection z₂(t) as a projection onto Q(z₂(t)) fails ([PAGE 14]), Kakutani’s convexity requirement on H fails ([PAGE 17–18]), and the comparison estimates in Theorems 3.3–3.4 acquire an uncontrolled additive term.

Adjudication: the existence claim of Theorem 2.1 is valid iff all three layers pass simultaneously: A coercive, φ at most linear, G Lipschitz/convex/compact, and λ > max(c, 4v‖f‖_A‖A‖‖G‖_A). Violation of any single layer voids the theorem; there is no partial credit. In particular, the “weather” disturbance G cannot compensate for a “physical” failure of coercivity, nor can a “biomechanical” feedback constraint rescue a non-convex disturbance map.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-style verification (RLVR) veto scenarios, each with a formal condition and a falsifiable prediction.

Veto Scenario 1 — Clock insufficiency. Condition: set λ := a exactly (equality with the linear growth rate of Γ on [PAGE 9]) instead of λ > a. Verifiable veto: the integral ∫₀^∞ e^{−At}z(t) dt in the definition of R_λ ([PAGE 9, eq. (5)]) fails to converge absolutely because ‖z(t)‖ can grow like e^{at}. Prediction: any numerical discretization on [0,T] will exhibit ‖z(T)‖/e^{aT} bounded away from zero, so the weighted partial sums do not Cauchy-converge as T → ∞; the algorithm must return “no solution” rather than a spurious fixed point.

Veto Scenario 2 — Non-convex disturbance. Condition: replace G by a non-convex set-valued map, e.g., Y = ℝ and G(z,y) := {−1, +1} independent of z,y. Verifiable veto: the Filippov selection argument on [PAGE 14] requires projecting z₁(t) onto the closed convex set Q(z₂(t)); with Q non-convex, the projection is set-valued and non-unique, and the measurable selection property collapses. Prediction: the fixed-point map H loses convex values ([PAGE 17–18]), so Kakutani’s theorem is inapplicable; a Monte-Carlo or subgradient search over H(s) will oscillate between distinct branches and fail to settle on any fixed point within tolerance ε > 0.

Veto Scenario 3 — Broken invariance domain. Condition: remove the assumption that Graph(M) is an invariance domain of (z,y) ↦ f(z,y) × (Ay + G(z,y)) required by Theorem 3.5 ([PAGE 23]), while keeping all other assumptions of Theorem 2.1. Verifiable veto: uniqueness M(x) = {r(x)} no longer follows; the comparison estimate of Theorem 3.4 permits a strictly positive gap sup_{(z,y)∈Graph(M)} ‖G(z,r(z)) − G(z,y)‖_B > 0. Prediction: two distinct bounded Lipschitz solutions r₁ ≠ r₂ exist on the same domain X, and a verification oracle querying both solutions will observe ‖r₁(z) − r₂(z)‖ > δ for some z ∈ X and δ > 0, falsifying the uniqueness claim.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
