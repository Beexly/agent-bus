First, let's unpack the core argument about interchangeability. Knight writes: "The variation in interchangeability in different uses introduces a special complication which has caused confusion." This refers to the classic factor-proportions debate in distribution theory—how the relative scarcity of factors determines their prices. The "former variety" is interchangeability in producing a specific physical good (e.g., using a lathe vs. a milling machine to make a gear). But what matters for market valuation is not whether two inputs can substitute in a given technological process, but whether they can substitute in generating an equivalent monetary return—that is, a certain amount of value. In other words, competitive markets do not require technical substitutability across all inputs; they only require that firms can reallocate resources toward those combinations that yield the highest net revenue per unit of investment. If one industry uses more labor-intensive methods and another more capital-intensive, effective substitution occurs through the relative growth or decay of entire industries as capital and labor flow in response to profit signals. This insight aligns with the Austrian capital-theoretic tradition (Wieser) and anticipates later general equilibrium models where factor mobility is mediated by price adjustments rather than direct engineering equivalence.

Knight explicitly connects this to Wieser’s critique of the productivity theory of distribution. Wieser rejected the idea that distribution follows from varying proportions of factors in a single production function; instead, he argued that distribution results from the existence of multiple production techniques (different combinations of factors) operating simultaneously, each with its own fixed proportions. Taylor, whom Knight cites, shows these two formulations are mathematically equivalent: you can model the same economic reality either as a continuum of substitution within a single technology or as discrete shifts between technologies. The key is that both types of proportion variation occur together in actual markets. This equivalence dissolves many spurious debates in early 20th-century economics about marginal productivity vs. surplus theories.

Now, the second cluster: the distinction between labor and property services. Knight systematically dismantles four commonly assumed grounds of separation. The first two are presented here: (1) Activity vs. passivity, and (2) preference in the agency itself. The activity-passivity dichotomy suggests that labor is "active" (humans exert effort) while capital goods are "passive" (machines sit idle until operated). Knight counters by pointing to enterprise organization: labor is directed by its employer, not its owner. A free worker sells his labor-time and follows managerial commands just like a piece of equipment does. Thus, there is "no sharp difference between a free laborer and a horse... who would, of course, be property." Even a slave, legally property, is still directed by an employer. Therefore, the active/passive distinction collapses when we consider the institutional reality of wage employment: both labor and capital become factors subject to hierarchical direction once they enter the firm. The worker may have bodily autonomy outside the workplace, but inside, he functions as an instrument of coordination, much like a tool. The "preference in the agency itself" likely refers to psychological motives—perhaps the idea that labor supplies come from agents with subjective preferences (disutility of work), whereas property services are devoid of such volition. But if we examine market behavior, both types of factors respond to price signals via ownership decisions. Landlords choose to rent or hold; workers choose to work or leisure. The underlying mechanism of supply elasticity is analogous, even if the sources differ.

4. All productive services are priced at rental rates: wage $w$ for labor, user cost of capital $r$ for property services.
5. Prices adjust so that expected profitability across industries is equalized; deviations trigger entry/exit (relative growth/decay).

**Value-Creation Operator**:
Define the "value created" by a factor bundle as its contribution to net revenue before entrepreneurial profit: 
$$V(\ell,k) = p \cdot F(\ell,k) - w\ell - rk,$$
where $p$ is output price. Under perfect competition, $V=0$ in equilibrium due to free entry. However, during transitions, $V > 0$ indicates pure profit opportunities.

**Interchangeability Condition**:
Two input bundles $(\ell_1,k_1)$ and $(\ell_2,k_2)$ are economically interchangeable if they generate identical value streams:
$$F_1(\ell_1,k_1)/p_1 = F_2(\ell_2,k_2)/p_2 \quad \text{and} \quad w\ell_1 + rk_1 = w\ell_2 + rk_2.$$
This is weaker than technological substitutability ($MP_\ell/\ell = MP_k/k$ etc.).

**Factor Price Equalization via Inter-Industry Reallocation**:
Suppose initially Industry A uses high labor-capital ratio, B low. If demand for A’s product rises, $p_A$ increases → excess profits → capital and labor flow from B to A → relative decay of B, growth of A — until new equilibrium with adjusted factor prices. Mathematically:
Let $Z_A$ and $Z_B$ denote zero-profit conditions:
$$p_AF_A(\ell_A,k_A) = w\ell_A + rk_A + \Pi_A^{entrepreneurial},$$
with similar for B. In static partial equilibrium, $\Pi^E=0$; in dynamic adjustment, sign($\Pi^E$) dictates directional flow.

System solves:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&L = \ell_A + \ell_B \\
&K = k_A + k_B \\
&w = p_A \frac{\partial F_A}{\partial \ell_A} = p_B \frac{\partial F_B}{\partial \ell_B} \\
&r = p_A \frac{\partial F_A}{\partial k_A} = p_B \frac{\partial F_B}{\partial k_B}.
\end{aligned}
$$

If solutions fail to exist uniquely (multiple equilibria), uncertainty prevails—this is the domain of Knightian profit.
