At level zero, the athlete models the self as a static object: selected or not-selected, talented or untalented. Because page 7 notes TID often rests on “the persistent assumption that elite athletic potential can be identified reliably at an early age” [PAGE 7], an early-deselected late-maturer internalises non-selection as a fixed ability deficit rather than a maturational timing mismatch. At level one, the coach models the athlete as current output $O(\tau)$. Under selection crisis—finite academy slots, performance pressure, short job horizons—the coach regresses to habit: the visible, immediately measurable physiological traits (size, speed, strength) that page 3 shows are confounded with maturation [PAGE 3]. This is crisis habit regression: under uncertainty, the decision-maker weights the most legible signal most heavily, even when the review identifies it as the least valid for long-term potential.

At level two, the federation or parent models the coach as an objective talent evaluator, failing to see that the coach’s mental model is itself recursively biased by relative age [PAGE 4] and socioeconomic access [PAGE 4–5]. The counter-trap is that the “holistic, inclusive, context-sensitive” model recommended on pages 8–9 [PAGE 8–9] becomes a credentialing barrier: only athletes with access to sports psychologists, GPS wearables, and well-resourced academies can display “psychological readiness,” “decision-making,” and “coachability” in forms legible to selectors. Psychological attributes are modifiable [PAGE 4], so a single early assessment captures a state, not a trait; yet systems that treat psychometrics as constants will deselect late-developing athletes whose resilience emerges only after environmental support improves.

The clock operates on two mismatched timescales. The selection calendar runs on annual age-grouping [PAGE 4], while the maturational clock runs on biological tempo, with peak height velocity occurring at different chronological ages [PAGE 3]. Late maturers may exit the system before their maturational clock aligns with the selection window, especially when page 7 warns that “failure to reassess athletes over time may lead to premature deselection of later-maturing athletes” (Till & Baker, 2020) [PAGE 3]. The recursive failure is that every layer models the other as more stable and more meritocratic than it is, while the review correctly describes talent as “dynamic, developmental, and influenced by the interplay between athlete and environment” [PAGE 8].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: any protocol selecting on a single physical test before peak height velocity is vetoed. Because $O(\tau)=h(M(\tau),\theta)+\epsilon$ with maturation gradient dominating talent gradient [PAGE 3], the measurement identifies $M$, not $\theta$. A passed protocol must stratify by maturity status or use maturity offset, and must repeat testing over time. Page 7: “It is also possible that early selection can benefit more biologically advanced athletes, at the expense of those who mature later” [PAGE 7].

Biomechanical and injury layer veto: early specialisation raises overtraining, injury, burnout, and dropout risk [PAGE 3, 7 citing DiSanti & Erickson, 2019]. High-volume, single-sport loading in pre-adolescence is vetoed unless balanced with multisport exposure and load monitoring.

Environmental and infrastructure layer veto: page 8 states the biopsychosocial model “needs to be flexible to local conditions, especially in sub-Saharan Africa where access to technology, trained staff, finance, and infrastructure is highly variable” [PAGE 8]. Where connectivity $R\approx0$ and trained-personnel density $\rho\approx0$, AI/GPS-dependent selection is vetoed on equity grounds; page 4 confirms athletes in underrated areas are excluded by “absence of an information platform and digital presence” [PAGE 4]. Decentralised, school-integrated identification (Kelly, 2018; Dunn, 2023; cited on page 8) is the non-vetoed alternative.

Measurement and cultural layer veto: psychometric instruments without cross-cultural validity and local norming are vetoed [PAGE 4]. Coaches’ interpretation of personality, motivation, and readiness must be validated in the specific cultural setting, or the attribute scores are incommensurable across athletes.

Adjudication rule: a TID protocol is admitted only if (1) selection is stratified by biological maturation rather than chronological age alone, (2) reassessment occurs at $\Delta t>0$ intervals (e.g., semi-annual or annual) to capture $\dot{\theta}$, (3) selection does not require digital presence or GPS infrastructure, and (4) psychological/cognitive instruments demonstrate measurement invariance in the target population. Violation of any condition triggers a hard veto at that layer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: A national federation responds to the review’s anti-early-specialisation stance by eliminating all objective physical testing before age 14, relying instead on coach nomination (citing the critique of single-point physical screening on page 3 and early-specialisation harms on page 7). Verifiable prediction: within 18 months, selection cohorts become narrower, not wider, because coaches substitute subjective “eye-test” judgments for the removed objective screen; early-maturing athletes remain overrepresented due to size/speed salience, while late-maturing, less visibly advanced athletes are quietly deselected. The relative-age skew documented by Cobley et al. (2009) and page 4 persists or intensifies.

Scenario 2: An AI scouting platform is deployed only in regions with 4G coverage and existing club databases, on the grounds that data-driven identification improves objectivity (page 4–5). Verifiable prediction: the platform’s reported AUC on its training population is high, but recall for rural and low-income athletes approaches zero because those athletes lack digital presence (page 4). Under national deployment, the model suffers catastrophic distribution shift: false negatives for structurally excluded athletes rise, and the apparent “objectivity” of the algorithm merely replicates the input missingness.

Scenario 3: A sports academy gates progression on a single psychometric battery measuring “motivation, self-regulation, resilience and coachability” (page 3), without longitudinal reassessment. Verifiable prediction: the false-negative rate increases over a 24-month horizon because psychological attributes are modifiable and context-bound (page 4); athletes who score low initially due to environmental stress or lack of organised-sport experience are deselected, despite the review’s assertion that resilience and learning potential must be evaluated “over time” (Ford et al., 2013, cited page 4). Longitudinal re-testing would recover a substantial fraction of these athletes; a one-time gate will not.
