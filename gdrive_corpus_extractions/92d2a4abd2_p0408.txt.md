If a proposed experimental timeline predicts habit formation when reaction onset precedes receptor stimulation, the physical layer vetoes it absolutely: \(C_t = 0\) and no \(s_H\) increment occurs.

Biomechanical layer veto: Reaction amplitude \(A\) is bounded by the force-velocity and fatigue properties of the effector musculature. The glossary defines \(A\) as “amplitude, magnitude, or intensity of a reaction” [PAGE 403] but gives no ceiling. Any model predicting \(A\) beyond the maximal isometric twitch or tetanic force of the relevant muscle group is biomechanically vetoed. Because \(A = \mathtt{h's\grave{E}R} - i\) [PAGE 403] contains an undefined OCR term, the equation cannot supply a biomechanical upper bound; it must be supplemented by muscle physiology or it is formally silent.

Environmental / “weather” layer veto: Drive \(D\) is calculated from objective conditions \(C_p\) [PAGE 403]. If the environment lacks the drive-producing condition—e.g., no food deprivation when hunger is the primary drive, no thermal challenge when thermoregulatory drive is operative—then \(C_p = \emptyset\) and, by Hull’s definition, \(D = 0\). Any prediction of motivated action under \(C_p = \emptyset\) is vetoed at the environmental layer regardless of habit strength \(s_H\) or incentive constants \(B, c\) [PAGE 403]. In practical terms, ambient temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure (“weather” in the broad environmental sense) alter \(C_p\) and therefore \(D\); a model holding \(D\) constant across a seasonal shift is environmentally falsified.

Sensory veto: \(d\) counts j.n.d.’s between \(\breve{S}\) and \(S\) [PAGE 403]. Weber’s law implies a finite j.n.d. count for any given modality. If the stimulus difference falls below threshold, \(d = 0\) and the exponential generalization term collapses; if the difference exceeds the dynamic range of the receptor, \(d\) saturates. Any model treating \(d\) as an unbounded linear variable is vetoed by the sensory layer.

Scenario 1 — OCR symbol substitution in reaction amplitude. Verifiable condition: the original print of Hull’s glossary shows \(A = s_{ER} - i_R\), whereas the OCR text reads \(A = \mathtt{h's\grave{E}R} - i\) [PAGE 403]. Veto: if a downstream model uses the OCR term \(\mathtt{h's\grave{E}}\) as a habit multiplier, it injects an unverified variable \(h\) into reaction amplitude. Prediction: for trials where the empirical habit term \(s_{ER} \approx 0\), the OCR-based model predicts \(A > 0\) (spurious habit-driven reaction), while the corrected equation predicts \(A = -i_R\) (pure inhibition). The veto is falsifiable by comparing model output against recorded reaction amplitudes on zero-habit catch trials.

Scenario 2 — Drive-symbol conflation under multiple concurrent drives. Verifiable condition: an experiment runs two independent primary drives simultaneously (e.g., hunger and thirst) such that \(D_{\mathrm{dom}}\) and \(D_{\mathrm{joint}}\) are both nonzero [PAGE 403]. Veto: if the model uses a single scalar \(D\) for both dominant and joint drive, it cannot represent the possibility that the non-dominant joint drive suppresses the dominant response. Prediction: behavior will be allocated to the response with the highest \(D_{\mathrm{dom}}\)-weighted habit only under single-drive conditions; under dual-drive conditions, choice proportions will shift according to \(D_{\mathrm{joint}}\), producing a violation of the single-scalar model’s predicted choice curve. The veto scenario is verifiable by manipulating the relative strength of the second drive and observing choice reallocation unexplained by \(D_{\mathrm{dom}}\) alone.

Scenario 3 — Exponential base mismatch in multi-week retention. Verifiable condition: Laboratory X fits Hull’s habit-decay curve using \(e = 2.7183\), while Laboratory Y uses Hull’s permitted convenience value \(e = 10\) [PAGE 403] on the same raw acquisition data. Veto: retention predictions after \(N\) weeks diverge by the scaling factor \(10^{kN} / (2.7183)^{kN} = (10/e)^{kN} \approx 2.3026^{kN}\) in the exponent, i.e., the models are not merely reparameterized; they generate different qualitative curves. Prediction: at \(N = 4\) weeks with \(k = 0.2\), the \(e = 10\) model predicts retention \(\approx 10^{-0.8} \approx 0.16\), while the \(e = 2.7183\) model predicts \(\approx e^{-0.8} \approx 0.45\). The veto is verifiable by running a fourth-week retention test: the model whose prediction falls outside the 95% confidence interval of observed responding is empirically falsified, and the other is provisionally retained pending further base-consistency checks.
