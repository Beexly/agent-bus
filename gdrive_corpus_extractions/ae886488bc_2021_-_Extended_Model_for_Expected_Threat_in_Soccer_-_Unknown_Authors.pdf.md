The source’s decision model is not a full game-theoretic recursive theory of mind. It has “Shoot or Move” [PAGE 5], then “Shoot,” “Move,” “Turnover” [PAGE 7], then “Shoot,” “Short or Long move,” “Short or Long turnover” [PAGE 8], with “Probabilities depend on location” [PAGE 5], [PAGE 7], [PAGE 8]. Therefore the transition probabilities \(P_M\), \(P_{TO}\), \(P_{MS}\), \(P_{ML}\), \(P_{TOS}\), and \(P_{TOL}\) are empirical aggregates, not best responses to a defender who anticipates the ball carrier’s value function. This creates a recursive-theory-of-mind gap. In crisis habit regression, a player under pressure defaults to a habitual short move or early shot; the model may overvalue that habit because the estimated probabilities are averaged over all match states. In a counter-trap, the opponent baits the short move by allowing an apparently safe cell while increasing \(T_S\) and redirecting \(P_{TOS}\) toward a dangerous counterattack location; the model can represent this only if the turnover matrices are estimated conditionally, and the deck’s “Expected cost of turnovers” title [PAGE 16] suggests that cost is part of the intended decomposition, but the supplied text gives no fitted values. In clock pressure, the five-event horizon [PAGE 3] and the “Time horizon up to the next shot” [PAGE 18] can diverge from match-clock urgency; a team trailing late may shoot earlier, changing \(S_i\) and \(xG_i\). A recursive model would make the opponent’s policy a function of the attacker’s value function, so \(P_{TO}\) becomes endogenous. The source does not do this. The correct conclusion is therefore conditional: xT is a single-agent Markov value under estimated average transitions, not a fixed point of mutual anticipation. The recursive counter-trap test is to ask whether changing the opponent’s response to high-xT cells changes the transition matrices; if it does, the linear equation is a reduced-form approximation, not a strategic equilibrium.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source provides no weather, physical, or biomechanical layer. It explicitly uses “Only ball event data” and “No locations for other players” [PAGE 3]. The model is a grid-cell Markov recursion with \(xG\) from “a suitable expected goals (xG) model” and event probabilities “calculated from real-life event data” [PAGE 4]. Therefore any cross-layer claim must be vetoed unless it demonstrates conditional invariance. Formally, let \(W\) be weather state and \(B\) a biomechanical/physical state. The deck estimates marginal cell probabilities such as \(\hat P(G=1\mid i)\) and \(\hat P_M(i,\cdot)\), not necessarily \(P(G=1\mid i,W,B)\) or \(P_M(i,\cdot\mid W,B)\). By the law of total probability,
\[
P(G=1\mid i)=\int P(G=1\mid i,W,B)\,dF(W,B\mid i).
\]
If \(F(W,B\mid i)\) differs across cells or changes between training and deployment, the marginal used by xT can be non-transportable. A windy match can change shot conversion and pass execution; a fatigued player can change short-move and long-move completion; wet turf can change turnover probabilities. The model’s negative turnover terms make the error non-monotone: a higher marginal \(xG_i\) can coexist with lower conditional \(xG_i\) under wind, and a higher \(P_M\) can coexist with higher \(P_{TO}\) under pressure. The source itself flags the missing player-location state: “Include player locations into xT models’ state space?” [PAGE 18]. The cross-layer veto proof is therefore: if a proposed claim uses xT to assert a physical, biomechanical, or weather-dependent causal effect, it must show either that \(P(\text{event}\mid i,W,B)=P(\text{event}\mid i)\) over the relevant range, or that \(W\) and \(B\) are included in the state and transition estimation. The deck does neither. The only admissible use of xT is conditional on the estimated event model and its unstated grid resolution; cross-layer extrapolation is vetoed.
