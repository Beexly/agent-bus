- **1985** — Lai & Robbins: asymptotic lower bound liminf E[T_i(n)]/log n ≥ 1/d_i and matching UCB-style policies; the regret-decomposition lineage "does appear in the work of Lai and Robbins [1985]" [PAGE 77, PAGE 129, PAGE 208].
- **1995** — Auer et al. introduce Exp3 and first apply the doubling trick to bandits (per Besson & Kaufmann [2018], cited [PAGE 107]); first adversarial-bandit analysis [PAGE 169].
- **2002** — Auer et al. Exp3.P / high-probability bounds; Auer & Chiang on oblivious vs reactive adversaries [PAGE 172, 167].
- **2009–2015** — INF / mirror-descent unification (Audibert & Bubeck; Zimmert & Seldin remove the √log k) [PAGE 158]; Exp3-IX implicit exploration (Kocák et al. 2014; Neu 2015) [PAGE 172].

The genuine "trap" motif (not multi-week, but real): the **over-specialised policy** of Fig 16.1 [PAGE 216] — a policy tuned to be near-optimal on easy instances pays steep regret on others; and the **exploration trap** where optimism "avoids actions that lead to large information gain and low reward" (Note 3 [PAGE 119], realized in §25.2). These are the source's actual failure genealogies.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source *does* contain a genuine recursive-adversary structure — this is the honest referent, not "crisis habit regression / clock":

- The bandit is "a sequential game between a learner and an environment" [PAGE 10]. In the adversarial model the environment "can examine the code of the proposed algorithms and choose the rewards accordingly" [PAGE 155] — a first-order theory-of-mind: the adversary models the learner's policy.
- **Oblivious vs reactive (non-oblivious) adversaries** (Note 4 [PAGE 167]): the reactive adversary conditions x_t on the learner's realized history, a deeper recursion. The book notes the Exp3 analysis survives this generalization "without changing the analysis."
- **Minimax as a two-player order:** R*_n(E) = inf_π sup_ν R_n(π,ν) (Ch 13 [PAGE 189]); the "evil antagonist" of Fig 15.1 [PAGE 200] picks the second environment as a best response to π's least-explored arm i = argmin_{j>1} E[T_j(n)] [PAGE 199]. That is an explicit adversarial best-response computation against the learner's belief state.
- **Regret itself as a belief-relative object:** because regret is defined against a *competitor class* Π [PAGE 10, 19], the learner's objective is second-order — it is reasoning about the best fixed policy in hindsight, not merely about rewards.

Breakdown regime honestly noted: for *reactive* adversaries that make the first action determine all future rewards, "there still does not exist a policy guaranteeing sublinear regret" (Note 7 [PAGE 167]) — the recursion becomes unlearnable. That is the source's real "counter-trap vs clock" analogue: no amount of horizon helps when the environment's response is instantaneous and path-determining.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
There is no physical/biomechanical/weather layer to adjudicate — stating this plainly per invariant #2. The genuine "veto" structure in the source is **the lower-bound apparatus, which vetoes optimality claims**:

- **Worst-case veto:** Thm 13.1/15.2 — for any policy there exists a Gaussian instance with R_n ≥ (1/27)√((k−1)n) [PAGE 209]. Proof mechanism: divergence decomposition (Eq. 15.1) + Bretagnolle–Huber (Eq. 14.7), choosing the alternative environment at the least-pulled arm.
- **Instance-dependent veto (Lai–Robbins):** consistent policies cannot beat liminf E[T_i(n)]/log n ≥ 1/d_i (Thm 16.2, proof [PAGE 208]). This vetoes any claim of "log-free" instance-optimal regret.
- **Structural veto on optimism:** Note 3 [PAGE 119] — "So is the optimism principle universal? ... Unfortunately the answer turns out to be no." Realized as a theorem in §25.2 "Clouds Looming for Optimism": for A = {e₁, e₂, (1−ε, γε)}, optimistic algorithms "can never be asymptotically optimal" and suffer Ω(log(n)/ε) [PAGE 300–302], whereas the constrained optimum plays the informative arm a₂ more.

Adjudication logic (physical-analogue only by isomorphism, not by content): the upper-bound "layer" (UCB/MOSS/Exp3) is *consistent* with the lower-bound "veto layer" precisely when constants match — MOSS (39√(kn)) vs floor ((1/27)√(k−1)n) — and *inconsistent* (a real veto) when a claim asserts optimality that a divergence-decomposition instance refutes.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three **verifiable** impossibility theorems (proofs in-book = "verifiable rewards"), each with a concrete **prediction** (regret rate). These are counterfactual vetoes: "no algorithm can do better than X on some instance."

1. **Minimax veto.** Prediction: for all k>1, n≥k, ∃ instance with R_n = Ω(√(nk)); concretely ≥ (1/27)√((k−1)n) for Gaussian unit-variance [Thm 15.2, PAGE 209]. Falsifiable check: any claimed O(√(nk)/log n) worst-case guarantee is refuted.

2. **Instance-dependent (Lai–Robbins) veto.** Prediction: any consistent policy on unstructured E satisfies liminf_{n→∞} E_ν[T_i(n)]/log n ≥ 1/d_i, hence liminf R_n/log n ≥ Σ_{i:Δ_i>0} Δ_i/d_i [Thm 16.2, PAGE 208]; for Gaussian d_i = Δ_i²/2, giving the Σ 2/Δ_i floor matched by Ch-8 UCB (Eq. 8.2) [PAGE 118]. Check: an algorithm beating Σ 2/Δ_i on every Gaussian instance cannot exist.

3. **Optimism veto (linear/structured).** Prediction: for the 3-arm d=2 set of §25.2, optimistic policies incur Ω(log(n)/ε) regret [PAGE 302], strictly worse in constant than the achievable asymptotically-optimal (non-optimistic, optimization-based) policy. Check: Thompson sampling and UCB "do not overcome the issues described here" (Note 2 [PAGE 302]) — a directly testable behavioral prediction distinguishing optimistic from G-optimal-design-based learners.

Each is a genuine "counterfactual veto with a prediction" grounded in a stated theorem — the honest reconstruction of this section without importing athletic-event semantics that the source does not contain.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
