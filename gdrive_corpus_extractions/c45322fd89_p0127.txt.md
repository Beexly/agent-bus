<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The fair catch descends directly from rugby union’s “mark,” imported into American football in the 1870s–1880s as a way to allow a receiver to secure a high kick without immediate tackling. The 1915 two-step limit reflects American football’s progressive contraction of rugby’s open-field running after a catch; by 1915 the sport had already moved toward scrimmage dominance, and the fair catch was being hedged by penalties (the 5-yard-back scrimmage) rather than rewarded with a free kick [PAGE 1].

The punt-out is an atavistic rugby survival: in rugby, a ball touched down in-goal led to a punt-out from the goal line, giving the defending territory a chance to kick back into open play. American football retained the punt-out as a distinct restart well into the early twentieth century, even as the scrimmage line and down-and-distance system erased most rugby continuity [PAGE 1]. Section 13’s treatment—failure of the first fair-catch attempt on a punt-out automatically triggers a kick-off—shows the rule book treating the punt-out as a fragile, probationary mechanism rather than a stable phase of play.

The try-at-goal is the direct genealogical ancestor of the modern extra point and field goal. In 1915, the ball could be placed on the ground and kicked after a touchdown, with Section 11 imposing a strict one-touch discipline [PAGE 1]. The post-touchdown kick-off regardless of success (Section 12) preserves the rugby principle that a scoring exchange resets field position through a restart kick [PAGE 1].

Touchback and safety originate in rugby’s distinction between a defending team grounding the ball in its own in-goal (touchdown/touchback) and an offensive team being held or downed in its own end (safety/touch-in-goal). Section 14’s assignment of a scrimmage from the 20-yard line to the team that made the touchback or safety is an American innovation diverging from rugby’s free-kick conventions; it anticipates the modern touchback spot but embeds it in a scrimmage rather than a kick-off [PAGE 1].

Multi-week trap: across a season, returners learn that a fair catch near their own goal line risks the Section 10 penalty if they take a third step under pressure. Rational returners may therefore elect to let the ball bounce, converting a potential fair catch into a live-ball scramble. Conversely, defensive coaches can train rushers to feign an oncoming tackle, inducing the returner to take extra steps out of habit regression (see Recursive Theory of Mind). The incomplete “suspended by” clause in Rule XV also sets a multi-week strategic trap: if officials habitually suspend play for fading light or weather near the end of games, trailing teams can manufacture stoppages after fair catches to protect a lead, while leading teams can exploit the lack of a play clock to run time off [PAGE 1].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression operates at the individual returner level. A player conditioned by years of open-field running will, under defensive pressure, take a third step after securing a fair catch rather than securing the ball and downing it immediately. That regression triggers Section 10, converting a potentially advantageous fair catch into a scrimmage five yards back with only first down and ten to gain [PAGE 1]. The returner’s mental model—“I must run to avoid being hit”—collides with the rule’s discrete gait count, producing a self-inflicted field-position penalty.

The counter-trap is defensive. A rushing unit can simulate an aggressive pursuit without actually committing to a tackle, exploiting the returner’s crisis reflex. If the returner bites and takes more than two steps, the defense has forced a scrimmage at a disadvantageous spot without needing to make a play [PAGE 1]. The offense, anticipating this, may train returners to execute a “freeze-catch”: secure the ball, remain stationary, and signal clearly, thereby denying the defense the psychological trigger. This is a second-order theory-of-mind loop: defense models returner as panicky runner; returner models defense as feigner; returner freezes; defense must then decide whether to honor the fair catch or risk a roughing/illegal contact penalty.

The clock layer is Rule XV. Time is taken out while the ball is brought out, after touchbacks/safeties, after fair catches, during penalties, after out-of-bounds plays, and upon suspension [PAGE 1]. Because no play clock appears on [PAGE 1], the duration of each stoppage is an umpire variable. A leading team can recursively use fair catches and time-outs to consume game clock, while a trailing team must model the umpire’s tolerance for delay. The recursive depth is threefold: (1) returner decides whether to take steps; (2) defense decides whether to feign rush to induce steps; (3) both teams model the clock-management externality of Rule XV stoppages near the end of the half or game. The clock is not merely a neutral timer; it is a strategic resource whose consumption is governed by the very restart rules on [PAGE 1].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: Section 11 assumes exactly one permissible ground touch at a try-at-goal [PAGE 1]. The physical validity of that assumption depends on the coefficient of restitution \(\epsilon\) between ball and field. On frozen or hardpan ground, \(\epsilon\) is high; a placement kick may strike the ground, rebound upward, and strike again before the kicker contacts it, violating the single-touch regime even if the kicker acted correctly. Veto condition: if field hardness \(H > H_{\text{crit}}\) such that \(N_{\text{bounces}} \ge 2\) with probability \(P > 0.5\), the rule cannot be fairly enforced and the restart defaults to kick-off under Section 12, but with a contested spot [PAGE 1]. On muddy ground, \(\epsilon \to 0\); the ball may plug and not bounce at all, making the “once” condition trivially satisfied but the kick itself physically impaired.

Biomechanical layer: Section 10’s “more than two steps” presumes a discrete, countable gait [PAGE 1]. Average human step length \(\ell \approx 0.75\,\mathrm{m}\), so two steps span \(\approx 1.5\,\mathrm{m} \approx 1.64\,\mathrm{yd}\). A sprinting returner’s stride may blur the boundary between step and bound; the umpire must make a sub-second visual count under load. Veto condition near the sideline: if \(x_{\text{catch}} < 5\,\mathrm{yd}\) from the touchline, then \(x_{\text{SC}} = x_{\text{catch}} - 5\,\mathrm{yd}\) places the scrimmage out of bounds, which is physically impossible. The rule lacks a clamp or sideline adjustment, creating a geometric singularity [PAGE 1].

Weather layer: precipitation, fog, or darkness reduces visibility of field marks. Section 14 requires placement “anywhere on its own 20-yard line” [PAGE 1]. If the 20-yard line is obscured by mud, snow, or paint wear, the scrimmage spot cannot be verified. Rule XV permits suspension when play is “suspended by” [cause cut off] [PAGE 1]; if ambient illumination \(L < L_{\text{min}}\) for line judging, officials must veto scrimmage placement and either default to a kick-off or suspend play. Wind also alters the kick-off trajectory after Sections 11–13, but because the rule fixes only the mode (kick-off) and not the tee or wind allowance, weather introduces unmodeled variance into the restart [PAGE 1].

Veto proof summary: a rule is vetoed when (a) the physical state space violates the rule’s counting assumption (\(N_{\text{steps}}\) or \(N_{\text{touches}}\)), (b) the geometric target lies outside the playable field (\(x_{\text{SC}} < 0\) or beyond sideline), or (c) environmental observability falls below the threshold required to mark the spot (\(L < L_{\text{min}}\) or field marking obscured). In all three cases, [PAGE 1] provides no fallback clause, so the referee/umpire must invoke extra-rule discretion.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
