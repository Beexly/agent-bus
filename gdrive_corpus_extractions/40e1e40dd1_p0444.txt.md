Prediction: skin-conductance response (SCR) and withdrawal latency do not differ significantly from baseline null trials, because \(w_\iota \iota\) suppresses the reaction below behavioral threshold. RLVR veto: if SCR exceeds baseline by more than \(2\sigma_{\mathrm{baseline}}\) or withdrawal occurs within \(500\,\mathrm{ms}\), the inhibition model is falsified and the impulse is not fully suppressed. Scenario 2 — Thermal-veto removal. A subject who habitually does not clean because ablution water is cold (\(T < T_{\mathrm{crit}}\)) is provided heated water (\(T \geq T_{\mathrm{crit}}\)) with the basin at arm's length (\(\tau_{\mathrm{getup}} \ll \tau_{\max}\)). Prediction: cleaning latency decreases by a factor of at least 2 and cleaning probability rises from near 0 to above 0.8 within one session. RLVR veto: if latency and probability remain unchanged after the thermal and biomechanical obstacles are removed, then the veto model is false and the obstacle was not physical but semantic. Scenario 3 — Habitual-checking obsolescence. An impulse is habitually checked for \(N = 28\) days, then the subject is re-exposed to \(X=1\) in a warm, low-effort context. Prediction under James's decay model: spontaneous-recovery amplitude is attenuated by \(\exp(-\lambda \cdot 28\,\text{days})\) relative to a naive baseline; recovery is partial and context-dependent. RLVR veto: if full naive-level recovery occurs immediately upon re-exposure, then the "obsolescence" claim is falsified and the impulse was inhibited, not decayed; the correct model is inhibition with context renewal, not habit extinction.
