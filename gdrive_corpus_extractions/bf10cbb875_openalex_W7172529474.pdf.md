Third, within-session adaptation: 30 forehand and 30 backhand strokes [PAGE 4] are blocked, not randomized, so later strokes may improve with warm-up or fatigue. Fourth, player-level clustering: repeated strokes are not independent, so week-to-week apparent effects can be dominated by a few players. Fifth, construct drift: if the training goal changes from “hit near apex” to “hit with safe margin,” the same \(D\) can improve while match performance worsens. The paper’s limitations acknowledge only small sample, controlled indoor single-camera setup, and missing 3D kinematics [PAGE 11]; they do not provide longitudinal invariance evidence. The historical lineage supports plausibility, not measurement invariance.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three regimes matter. Crisis habit regression: under pressure, players often revert to habitual contact height and timing rather than executing a coached “optimal” apex. The paper’s blocked, non-pressure protocol cannot observe this. Its claim that top players show “superior timing and spatial consistency” [PAGE 5] may reflect stable technique in a low-pressure task, not crisis resilience. Counter-trap: coaching every player toward the apex may be counterproductive if contact height is intentionally adapted to incoming ball speed, spin, grip, or defensive intent. The paper labels apex contact as “optimal vertical position” [PAGE 5], but it does not measure point outcomes, error rates, or ball speed and spin; the limitations explicitly note that variables such as “ball speed, spin, and psychological factors” were not explored [PAGE 11]. A counter-trap prediction is that forcing apex contact could reduce safety margin or increase injury risk for some players even while raising the overlap ratio. Clock: the camera clock is 30 fps [PAGE 4], and the session clock is a single block of strokes. At 30 fps, contact timing is sampled coarsely; within-session learning and fatigue are confounded with stroke order. The recursive theory-of-mind error is attributing the experimenter’s definition of “error” to the player’s intention. A player may deliberately contact lower to create topspin or to handle a low ball; the vision system sees “deviation,” not intent. The gender interaction—minimal among top players but pronounced among average players, especially backhand [PAGE 6]—is therefore not automatically an ability difference. It may reflect coaching history, strategy, anthropometry, or task interpretation. The defensible interpretation is conditional: in this indoor sample, top-level players’ detected ball positions were closer to the detected apex; the mental-state and pressure mechanisms remain untested.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The measurement is a 2D projection of a 3D trajectory. Let true ball position be \(X(t)\in\mathbb{R}^3\) and camera projection be \(\pi:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^2\). The observed contact height is \(z_c=s(y_g-y_c)\), but \(y_c\) conflates true height, depth, and perspective. The paper admits that “absolute distance accuracy was not achievable with a single-camera setup” [PAGE 5] and that depth of the contact point was not captured [PAGE 11]. At 30 fps [PAGE 4], if the ball’s vertical speed is \(v_z\), the per-frame height uncertainty is approximately \(v_z/f_s\). For \(v_z=5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(f_s=30\ \mathrm{Hz}\), \(u_z\approx 0.167\ \mathrm{m}\). That is comparable to several reported median contact-height differences, such as 1.45 m versus 1.25 m or 1.00 m [PAGE 6]. If \(u_z\) is not small relative to the between-group difference, the physical layer vetoes a precise mechanism claim. Occlusion is a second physical veto: the “contact” is the last visible maximum-X point before interaction [PAGE 5], so racket or body occlusion can truncate the trajectory. The backhand average-women value of 0% overlap [PAGE 8] is especially vulnerable to a truncation artifact.

Biomechanical veto. Contact-point height is not a pure timing variable. It depends on grip, stance, racket orientation, body rotation, incoming ball depth, and swing intent. The paper did not capture “body rotation or depth of the contact point” [PAGE 11]. Therefore \(D\) cannot be interpreted as anticipatory timing alone. The biomechanical layer vetoes any claim that skill group differences in \(D\) are caused by timing precision unless racket and body kinematics are measured. The paper’s discussion invokes anticipatory control [PAGE 11], but the design supports at most an association between skill group and a 2D ball-apex proxy.

Weather and environment veto. The data were collected “under controlled indoor conditions using a single-camera setup” [PAGE 11]. No outdoor lighting, wind, rain, court-surface, or temperature variable was measured. HSV filtering is illumination-sensitive [PAGE 3], and KNN background subtraction can adapt to local lighting but is not validated across weather states. The model has no weather term, so any weather-generalization claim is underidentified. The veto is logical: if the target claim is “skill dominates contact-point precision in tennis” without an indoor qualifier, the design lacks the environmental degrees of freedom to support it. The paper can support a narrower claim: in this indoor sample, with this camera and this proxy, top-level players had higher overlap ratios and smaller detected vertical deviations than average-level players. It cannot adjudicate outdoor or match-play weather effects.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: True-contact reannotation veto. Verifier: annotate true racket-ball collision at 240 fps or with a racket-mounted inertial sensor, then compare the paper’s max-X contact proxy to the true contact. Prediction: the proxy error will be largest for average-level women’s backhand, where the reported overlap is 0% [PAGE 8] and the contact-height IQR is broad [PAGE 6], because occlusion and missed detections are most likely there. Veto condition: if mean absolute error of \(D\) exceeds 0.05 m or if the skill-group rank order flips, the paper’s contact-point metric is not a valid timing measure. If the rank order survives but the effect size falls below partial \(\eta^2=0.14\), the mechanism claim is vetoed.

Scenario 2: Calibration and homography veto. Verifier: add a checkerboard or multi-camera calibration and recompute \(s\) and \(D\) with a full homography or 3D reconstruction. Prediction: the sideline-average scale [PAGE 5, PAGE 8] will overstate between-group separation because perspective and lens distortion are not uniform across the court. Veto condition: if the skill main effect drops below partial \(\eta^2=0.14\) or the skill-by-gender interaction becomes non-significant after calibration, the original ANOVA result is not robust to the physical measurement layer. A weaker but still important prediction is that top-level players will remain ranked higher while the effect size shrinks, indicating real association but inflated precision.
