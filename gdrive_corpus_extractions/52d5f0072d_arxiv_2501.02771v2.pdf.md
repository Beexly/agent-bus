<THINK_TRACE>
This audit treats WorldPose as a sensor-to-semantics operator chain: multi-view image formation, static camera calibration, 2D detection and keypoint estimation, triangulated 3D skeletons, SMPL registration, broadcasting-camera calibration, and benchmark evaluation. The central reasoning is not whether the dataset is large, but whether its annotation operator remains identifiable and verifiable under the stated physical regimes. The source explicitly frames the capture area as unusually large and the broadcast camera as moving, which creates a multi-hop coupling: calibration errors propagate into triangulation, triangulation errors propagate into SMPL fitting, SMPL errors propagate into broadcast-camera refinement, and broadcast-camera errors then contaminate any monocular benchmark that uses the footage. The paper states that the capture area is “more than 1.75 acres (7k m2)” [PAGE 1], and that the infrastructure exists because “premier soccer events are often equipped with multi-view static camera systems” [PAGE 2]. This is the physical premise: wide-area, multi-view, high-speed sports capture.

The first gray-zone is the planar-field assumption. The pipeline initializes static calibration with a 2D homography between image and field plane [PAGE 7]. However, the authors also state that “the pitch is not perfectly planar as a roughly 20 cm large field crown ensures water drainage” [PAGE 7]. A 20 cm height variation is small relative to a football pitch, but not small relative to markerless pose accuracy when camera-to-subject distances are large and player pixels are sparse. In formal terms, if the ground truth surface is h(x,y) instead of z=0, a planar homography has a systematic residual. For a camera with focal length f in pixels and subject range Z in meters, a vertical surface error Δh in meters creates an image-space displacement on the order of f Δh / Z pixels, assuming small-angle pinhole projection. At f on the order of 10^3 pixels and Z on the order of tens of meters, a 0.20 m crown can induce several pixels of bias, which is non-negligible for 1080p footage of distant players.

The second gray-zone is the transition from field-marking calibration to player-centric calibration. The source says the pipeline uses field markings because they provide stable geometry, but also warns that field-only supervision can fail: “there can be multiple sets of parameters that provide roughly the same reprojection for the field lines but very different results for the players” [PAGE 22]. This is a critical identifiability trap. The field is a low-dimensional, texture-poor, mostly planar object, while players are articulated, deformable, and dynamically distributed in 3D. If camera parameters are optimized only to minimize field reprojection, the solution may satisfy the field constraint while violating player geometry. The ablation table supports this: “+ BA (S only)” produces a G-MPJPE of 548.4 mm, compared with 83.5 mm for the base pipeline [PAGE 12]. This is not a small degradation; it is a structural breakdown of the camera calibration operator for player pose recovery.

The third gray-zone is the moving broadcasting camera. The paper assumes that “the camera location C ∈R3 in world space to remain constant across frames within each clip” [PAGE 6]. That assumption is plausible for a fixed-position pan-tilt-zoom broadcast camera, but it becomes a veto if the camera translates, jibs, rails, or changes height. The calibration also depends on a commercial semi-automatic system and then on player reprojection refinement. The source notes that the software normally requires a pre-game scan, but this was unavailable, so 3D poses and field markings are used as additional constraints [PAGE 3]. This creates a recursive dependency: broadcast calibration depends on 3D player poses, and monocular benchmark evaluation depends on broadcast calibration. If the 3D poses have residual error, the broadcast calibration inherits it; if the broadcast calibration is over-smoothed, fast player motion may be under-constrained.

The fourth gray-zone is benchmark epistemology. The dataset reports G-MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, and Per-Meter Drift [PAGE 11]. These are useful, but they answer different questions. PA-MPJPE can be low even when global placement is poor, because per-frame Procrustes alignment removes global translation, rotation, and scale. G-MPJPE is stricter but still uses alignment over trajectories. The source itself notes that “a significant portion of G-MPJPE arises from incorrect relative positions between the players” [PAGE 14]. Therefore, a method that looks locally plausible per player may still fail at the multi-agent relational layer. This is the core frontier gap: WorldPose exposes not just pose estimation, but relative global reasoning among many coordinated subjects.

The fifth gray-zone is the interaction between motion priors and unreliable floor estimation. SLAHMR depends on floor estimation before motion-prior optimization, and the source describes a “chicken-egg situation” [PAGE 28]: the motion prior needs a floor to condition human motion, but the only signal aligning players to the floor comes from the motion prior. If players are far apart, fast-moving, or the camera is tilted, the floor estimate can diverge. This is a multi-hop failure: bad camera pose causes bad floor estimate, bad floor estimate causes bad motion-prior gradient, bad motion-prior gradient causes divergent chunk optimization, and divergent chunks produce discontinuities across temporal boundaries. The dataset therefore acts as a stress test for temporal, spatial, and multi-agent consistency simultaneously.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
All metric quantities below use SI units unless otherwise stated: length in meters (m), time in seconds (s), image coordinates in pixels (px), focal length in pixels (px), translation in meters (m), rotation in radians or as elements of SO(3), losses in squared pixel units (px²) or squared length units (m²), and probabilities dimensionless in [0,1]. The source defines static camera parameters as intrinsic matrix K_c, distortion coefficients k_c, rotation R_c, and translation t_c [PAGE 6]. It defines SMPL parameters as shape β, body pose θ_b, root orientation θ_r, and translation t [PAGE 6].

1. Planar homography initialization.
The paper estimates a 2D homography between field template points X'_i and image points x_{i,c}:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}'_i \simeq H_c \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i,c},
\]
where H_c ∈ R^{3×3} is defined up to scale and acts on homogeneous image coordinates in px. The direct linear transformation requires at least four non-collinear correspondences; the paper selects “a few more than 4 correspondence pairs to make the problem over-determined” [PAGE 7].
Assumption: the field is approximately planar and camera distortion is initially negligible.
Breakdown regime: rank deficiency or collinearity of field points, or surface non-planarity. The source states the pitch has “a roughly 20 cm large field crown” [PAGE 7], which violates strict planarity.

2. Non-linear static camera refinement.
The source gives:
\[
\Lambda_c^* = \arg\min_{\Lambda_c} \sum_i \|\Pi(\mathbf{X}'_i; \Lambda_c) - \mathbf{x}_{i,c}\|_2^2,
\]
where Π projects 3D field points in m to 2D pixels with distortion [PAGE 8].
Units: residual norm in px, loss in px².
Breakdown regime: local minima from sparse correspondences, incorrect distortion model, or planar-surface bias. The paper notes that default k1 and k2 may be insufficient for wide-angle side cameras, and k3 may be needed [PAGE 22].

3. Photometric line-distance refinement.
The source gives:
\[
\Lambda_c^* = \arg\min_{\Lambda_c} \sum_{\mathbf{X}' \sim \mathcal{S}} D[\Pi(\mathbf{X}'; \Lambda_c)],
\]
where D is a distance map storing distance in px to the nearest detected line pixel [PAGE 8].
Units: D is in px, loss in px if summed directly, or px² if squared variant is used.
Assumption: detected field lines are accurate and line contrast is sufficient.
Breakdown regime: night lighting, wet-field reflections, occlusion, or low-contrast lines can corrupt the distance map. The source mentions a night Vicon setup [PAGE 11] and field crown for drainage [PAGE 7], but does not provide weather metadata.

4. Tracking affinity via point-to-ray distance.
The source gives:
\[
A(\{\mathbf{P}_i^{t-1}\}, \mathbf{p}_{j,c}^t) = -\operatorname{PointToRayDist}(\mathbf{P}_i, \Pi^{-1}(\mathbf{p}_{j,c})).
\]
Units: point-to-ray distance in m, affinity in negative meters.
Assumption: previous-frame 3D pose P_i is reliable enough to gate new 2D detections.
Breakdown regime: low-resolution players, motion blur, occlusion, or incorrect camera calibration. The source states that “the distance between the cameras and the subjects is large” and that “movement in soccer games is fast-paced with frequent occlusions” [PAGE 2].

5. Bundle adjustment with player and field constraints.
The source gives:
\[
\Lambda_c^* = \arg\min_{\Lambda_c, \mathcal{P}} \sum_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}} I_{j,c} \|\Pi(\mathbf{X}_j; \Lambda_c) - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2,
\]
where I_{j,c} is visibility and X includes field markings and selected high-quality 3D player joints [PAGE 9].
Units: residual in px, loss in px².
Breakdown regime: if X contains only field markings, the calibration can overfit the field and fail for players. The ablation shows “+ BA (S only)” has G-MPJPE 548.4 mm and PA-MPJPE 75.4 mm, versus base G-MPJPE 83.5 mm and PA-MPJPE 70.8 mm [PAGE 12].

6. SMPL shape estimation from bone lengths.
The source gives:
\[
\beta^* = \arg\min_{\beta} \left| \ell(\mathbf{P}) - \ell(\mathcal{J}(\bar{\mathbf{T}})) - \sum_{i=1}^{10} \beta_i \ell(\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{B}_i)) \right|.
\]
Here ℓ extracts bone lengths, J is the SMPL joint regressor, T̄ is the template mesh, and B_i are shape blend shapes [PAGE 9].
Units: if P is in meters, bone-length residuals are in meters.
Breakdown regime: noisy 3D joints produce non-unique β; sparse observations can make shape estimation unstable. The paper later notes that direct SMPL fitting can produce “unrealistic poses which happen to have low reprojection errors” [PAGE 24].

7. SMPL refinement loss.
The source gives:
\[
E_{\text{data}} = \|\Pi(\hat{\mathbf{P}}) - \mathbf{P}_i\|_2^2,
\]
\[
E_{\text{smooth}} = \sum_t \|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1} - \hat{\mathbf{P}}_t - (\hat{\mathbf{P}}_t - \hat{\mathbf{P}}_{t-1})\|_2^2,
\]
\[
E_{\text{shape}} = \|\beta\|_2^2,
\]
and
\[
E_{\text{refine}} = \lambda_1 E_{\text{data}} + \lambda_2 E_{\text{smooth}} + \lambda_3 E_{\text{shape}}.
\]
[PAGE 9–10]
Units: E_data in px² if comparing projected 2D keypoints; E_smooth in squared joint-coordinate units, e.g. m² if P is in m; E_shape in squared shape-coefficient units.
Breakdown regime: excessive smoothing biases sprinting and kicking dynamics; excessive data weight permits jitter; excessive shape regularization can collapse body-size variation. The paper uses broadcast-based initialization to improve convergence for challenging poses [PAGE 10].

8. Broadcasting camera calibration.
The source defines frame-wise focal length f, principal point c_b, radial distortion k_b, rotation R_b, and constant camera location C [PAGE 6]. Field-marking loss:
\[
E_{\text{field}} = \sum_{\mathbf{X}'\in\mathcal{X}} \rho(\Pi(\mathbf{X}'; \Lambda_b) - \mathbf{x}_{i,b}),
\]
where ρ is Geman-McClure [PAGE 10]. Player loss:
\[
E_{\text{player}} = \sum_i \sum_j I_{i,j} \rho(\Pi(\hat{\mathbf{P}}_i; \Lambda_b) - \mathbf{p}_j).
\]
Combined:
\[
E_{\text{calib}} = \lambda_4 E_{\text{field}} + \lambda_5 E_{\text{player}}.
\]
The supplementary material sets λ_4 = 1 and λ_5 = 0.5 and uses Adam with learning rate 10^{-3} [PAGE 23].
Units: reprojection residuals in px; ρ returns a dimensionless robust penalty if normalized by a scale parameter, otherwise px-scaled penalty.
Breakdown regime: field-only calibration admits multiple parameter sets with similar field reprojection but different player reprojection [PAGE 22]. The constant-C assumption breaks if the camera translates [PAGE 6].

9. Evaluation metrics.
The source defines G-MPJPE, PA-MPJPE, and Per-Meter Drift [PAGE 11].
Formally:
\[
\text{G-MPJPE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \|\mathbf{J}_n^{\text{pred},g} - \mathbf{J}_n^{\text{gt},g}\|_2,
\]
after global trajectory alignment, with units mm in the paper.
\[
\text{PA-MPJPE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \|\mathbf{J}_n^{\text{pred},pa} - \mathbf{J}_n^{\text{gt},pa}\|_2,
\]
after per-frame Procrustes alignment, units mm.
\[
\text{Per-Meter Drift} = \frac{\text{G-MPJPE}}{L_{\text{gt}}},
\]
with units cm/m in the paper, where L_gt is ground-truth trajectory length in m.
Breakdown regime: PA-MPJPE can hide global relative-position errors; per-person alignment can hide multi-agent relational failures. The source states that per-person alignment substantially reduces error, suggesting “a significant portion of G-MPJPE arises from incorrect relative positions between the players” [PAGE 14].

10. Triangulation uncertainty operator.
For a calibrated stereo or multi-view triangulation, with image noise σ_x in px, focal length f in px, baseline B in m, and range Z in m, a first-order depth uncertainty is:
\[
\sigma_X \approx \frac{Z^2}{f B} \sigma_x.
\]
Units: σ_X in m if σ_x is in px, f in px, B in m, and Z in m.
Assumption: small Gaussian pixel noise, sufficient parallax, correct correspondences, and non-degenerate ray geometry.
Breakdown regime: B → 0, Z large, σ_x large, or wrong correspondences. This formalizes the source’s concern about low player resolution and large camera-subject distance [PAGE 2], [PAGE 22].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
WorldPose sits at the intersection of several historical lineages: marker-based motion capture, multi-view sports analysis, SMPL body-model fitting, and monocular global human motion estimation. The dataset comparison table places WorldPose against Panoptic Studio, Human3.6M, PROX, 3DPW, EgoBody, RICH, EMDB, SLOPER4D, BEDLAM, and KTH football footage [PAGE 4]. The key genealogical break is that earlier multi-person datasets were either lab-bound, single-subject, wearable-sensor based, synthetic, or limited in player count. WorldPose claims “more than double the amount of subjects per image than the previous largest dataset” and provides “10-20 subjects per frame on average from 150k frames” [PAGE 5]. This moves the problem from indoor motion capture to expansive, coordinated outdoor multi-agent capture.

The first ancestral trap is the assumption that lab-based multi-view calibration transfers directly to stadium capture. Panoptic Studio and similar systems operate in controlled volumes with dense camera coverage and marker or near-marker conditions [PAGE 4]. Stadium capture has 16–18 static cameras covering a 105 × 68 m pitch [PAGE 6], which reduces angular diversity per subject and increases reliance on field geometry. The source recognizes this by combining field markings, non-linear distortion modeling, photometric line-distance refinement, and bundle adjustment [PAGE 7–9]. The trap is that field geometry is stable but not player-specific; without player constraints, calibration can be formally underdetermined for human pose.

The second ancestral trap is the assumption that broadcasting cameras can be calibrated like static cameras. The paper states that “the software requires a pre-game scan by the camera operator, which is not available to us” [PAGE 3]. Therefore, the pipeline substitutes player-derived constraints for the missing scan. This is historically significant because it creates a closed-loop annotation system: static-camera 3D poses are used to calibrate the broadcast camera, and the broadcast camera then provides monocular footage for benchmark evaluation. The multi-week trap here is iterative dependency. If the static poses are biased, broadcast calibration inherits the bias; if broadcast footage is then used to initialize or refine SMPL body poses, the bias can re-enter the static annotation loop. The source acknowledges that “it often takes multiple iterations of the entire camera calibration and keypoint estimation process to achieve desired accuracy” [PAGE 22].

The third ancestral trap is the assumption that monocular SLAM can recover camera pose on a football pitch. The source reports that DROID-SLAM struggles because “the pitch is nearly textureless and the background (the audience seats) are highly dynamic” [PAGE 14]. This is a classic sports-vision problem: the playing surface lacks stable visual features, while the stands contain moving crowds. The historical remedy is to use field lines or player trajectories as geometric anchors. WorldPose does this for broadcast calibration, but baseline methods such as SLAHMR still rely on DROID-SLAM. The result is a large gap between monocular global methods and the dataset’s geometric ground truth.

The fourth ancestral trap is the assumption that local pose quality implies global multi-person quality. GLAMR and SLAHMR can produce plausible individual motions but fail to place players correctly relative to one another. The source notes that GLAMR can align subjects on a plane but struggles with global trajectories, while SLAHMR gets closer but still fails to align subjects correctly [PAGE 14]. The supplementary analysis adds that GLAMR “solely relies on the trajectory of the player with id=0” for extrinsic parameters [PAGE 26]. This is a multi-week trap for method development: improving local pose regression does not automatically solve relative global placement. WorldPose’s genealogy therefore exposes a missing research layer: multi-agent global consistency under moving-camera observation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The WorldPose pipeline can be modeled as a recursive belief-update system with multiple agents: human annotators, static-camera calibration optimizer, 2D detector, 2D keypoint estimator, triangulation operator, SMPL fitter, broadcast-camera calibration optimizer, and downstream pose baselines. Each agent has a belief state over the world, and each belief update is shaped by likelihood evidence, habit prior, verification, and clock constraints.

Let b_t be the belief state at time t. A generic Bayesian update is:
\[
b_{t+1} \propto L(z_t \mid b_t) h(b_t),
\]
where L is the likelihood of observation z_t and h is a habit or regularization prior. In crisis regimes—low resolution, occlusion, fast motion, textureless pitch—the likelihood becomes weak or multimodal. The prior then dominates. This is crisis habit regression. In WorldPose, the habit priors include: field-planarity initialization, smoothness regularization, SMPL shape regularization, floor-plane constraints, and temporal smoothness in broadcast calibration. These priors are useful because they stabilize noisy evidence, but they become traps when the evidence contradicts the prior.

For static calibration, the habit prior is the planar field. The source says the field has a “roughly 20 cm large field crown” [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is to add non-linear optimization and photometric refinement, but if only field markings are used, the optimizer can satisfy the field habit while failing players. The source explicitly warns that multiple camera parameter sets can give “roughly the same reprojection for the field lines but very different results for the players” [PAGE 22]. The verification fix is to include player keypoints in bundle adjustment, which reduces G-MPJPE from 548.4 mm to 83.5 mm in the ablation [PAGE 12].

For tracking and 2D detection, the habit prior is temporal association. The source uses ByteTrack and ViTPose, then finetunes them because of low-resolution data [PAGE 8]. The counter-trap is manual review and correction of 2D detections [PAGE 3]. The clock constraint is annotation throughput: 150k frames and 2.5 million poses [PAGE 1] require semi-automation, but manual intervention is still needed when coverage or blur is poor [PAGE 15]. If the clock forces fully automatic association, tracking errors propagate into triangulation and SMPL fitting.

For SMPL fitting, the habit prior is anatomical plausibility. The source notes that direct fitting from 3D poses can lead to “unrealistic poses which happen to have low reprojection errors” [PAGE 24]. The counter-trap is to initialize SMPL body poses using broadcast-footage estimators, which have higher zoom and more detailed player appearance [PAGE 24]. This is a recursive correction: broadcast calibration depends on 3D poses, but broadcast-derived 2D SMPL estimates can regularize 3D SMPL fitting. The risk is circular confirmation if the same errors appear in both paths.

For SLAHMR, the habit prior is the floor plane and motion prior. The source describes a “chicken-egg situation” [PAGE 28]: the motion prior needs a floor, but the floor is inferred using the motion prior. If players are far apart or the camera is tilted, the floor estimate becomes unreliable, and the motion prior provides no meaningful gradient. The counter-trap is to enforce a shared floor or enable floor-normal estimation [PAGE 26]. However, even with modifications, SLAHMR can diverge when distances between players are large [PAGE 28]. The clock constraint is severe: the original SLAHMR optimization takes “40 minutes per 100 frames with 4 subjects” [PAGE 29]. In broadcast settings, such latency is not operationally acceptable, so approximations or chunking are required. Chunking introduces temporal boundary gaps: camera trajectories may remain smooth, but SMPL meshes can show visible gaps between chunks [PAGE 28].

The recursive theory of mind therefore identifies three interacting forces. First, crisis habit regression: noisy observations cause models to fall back on strong priors such as floor planes, smooth motion, or field markings. Second, counter-trap mechanisms: player keypoints, manual review, SMPL regularization, broadcast initialization, and ground-truth camera substitution. Third, the clock: calibration iterations, chunk optimization, annotation throughput, and real-time constraints force approximations. A robust frontier system must not merely regress to habit; it must explicitly verify whether the habit prior is valid for the current physical configuration and temporal window.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto asks whether a claim about WorldPose utility survives physical, biomechanical, weather, and camera-model constraints. If any required layer is violated, the claim is vetoed.

Physical veto. The capture volume is large, and player image resolution is low. The source states that the capture area is “more than 1.75 acres (7k m2)” [PAGE 1] and that “the distance between the cameras and the subjects is large” [PAGE 2]. Using the triangulation uncertainty relation,
\[
\sigma_X \approx \frac{Z^2}{f B}\sigma_x,
\]
if pixel error σ_x is high because players are small, and baseline B is limited by stadium camera placement, the 3D joint covariance grows quadratically with range Z. The empirical Vicon comparison reports “6.6 cm error w.r.t. PA-MPJPE and 8.0 cm error w.r.t. G-MPJPE” [PAGE 12]. Therefore, any downstream claim requiring accuracy substantially below approximately 8 cm global error is vetoed unless additional evidence reduces uncertainty. The veto predicate is:
\[
V_{\text{physical}} = 1 \text{ if } \sigma_X > \tau_{\text{task}},
\]
where τ_task is the application tolerance in meters. If τ_task is less than the demonstrated error floor, the claim is rejected.

Biomechanical veto. SMPL fitting can produce poses with low reprojection error but implausible anatomy. The source says direct fitting can produce “unrealistic poses which happen to have low reprojection errors” [PAGE 24]. A valid pose claim must satisfy bone-length consistency, joint-limit plausibility, and contact plausibility. Let β be shape parameters, θ body pose, and ℓ(P) observed bone lengths. If the residual
\[
r_{\text{bone}} = \ell(P) - \ell(\mathcal{J}(T(\beta,\theta)))
\]
exceeds a biomechanical tolerance, or if joint angles violate anatomical limits, the pose claim is vetoed:
\[
V_{\text{bio}} = 1 \text{ if } \|r_{\text{bone}}\| > \tau_{\text{bone}} \text{ or } \theta \notin \Theta_{\text{anatomical}}.
\]
This veto is necessary because WorldPose is intended for sports analysis, where training and injury-prevention claims depend on biomechanically valid poses, not merely visually plausible overlays.

Weather veto. The source does not provide weather labels. It does state that the pitch has a crown to ensure water drainage [PAGE 7], and one evaluation setup is at night [PAGE 11]. Weather affects line detection, photometric calibration, player contrast, and broadcast exposure. If rain, wet grass, reflections, or low illumination degrade the distance map D or Canny edge detection used in photometric refinement [PAGE 8], [PAGE 21], the calibration operator becomes unreliable. Because weather is unobserved in the source, any claim of all-weather robustness is unfalsifiable and therefore vetoed:
\[
V_{\text{weather}} = 1 \text{ if weather covariates are absent and robustness is claimed}.
\]
A scientifically valid weather claim would require paired captures across dry, wet, night, and low-contrast conditions, plus residual analysis.

Camera-model veto. The broadcast camera model assumes constant camera location C within each clip [PAGE 6]. If the camera moves through translation, the assumption fails. Let C_t be camera center at frame t. The model is valid only if:
\[
\|C_t - C\|_2 = 0 \quad \forall t
\]
within clip tolerance. If camera translation is non-zero, pan-tilt-zoom calibration with constant C cannot represent the true projective geometry. This veto also applies if zoom changes focal length faster than the temporal smoothness regularizer can track without bias. The source acknowledges rapid camera movement and zoom as challenges [PAGE 2], [PAGE 10].

Field-only calibration veto. If a calibration claim relies only on field markings, the ablation shows catastrophic player error: “+ BA (S only)” yields G-MPJPE 548.4 mm [PAGE 12]. Therefore:
\[
V_{\text{field-only}} = 1 \text{ if } E_{\text{field}} \text{ is low but } E_{\text{player}} \text{ is high}.
\]
This is a direct empirical veto. A field-consistent camera model is not sufficient for player-centric global pose estimation.

Final adjudication: WorldPose is valid as a high-quality research benchmark within the demonstrated regime: calibrated static multi-view capture, manually reviewed 2D detections, bundle adjustment with player and field constraints, SMPL refinement, and Vicon-informed accuracy around 8 cm global error [PAGE 12]. It is not valid as a drop-in solution for applications requiring sub-centimeter biomechanics, all-weather autonomy, moving-translation broadcast cameras, or field-only calibration without player verification.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual veto scenarios are verifiable against the source text and tables. Each defines a falsification condition and a prediction.

Scenario 1: Field-only bundle adjustment.
Counterfactual claim: “A camera calibration that minimizes field reprojection alone is sufficient for multi-person global pose.”
Verification evidence: The ablation “+ BA (S only)” reports G-MPJPE 548.4 mm and PA-MPJPE 75.4 mm, while “Base” reports 83.5 mm and 70.8 mm, and “+ BA + SMPL” reports 80.0 mm and 66.3 mm [PAGE 12]. The source also states that field-only parameters can give “very different results for the players” [PAGE 22].
Veto condition: If field reprojection error is small but player reprojection error is large, the claim is rejected.
Prediction: Any pipeline that calibrates static cameras using only field markings will exhibit large global player error, especially for players far from field-line corners or in views with sparse correspondences. The expected failure mode is overconfident field alignment followed by biased triangulation and SMPL translation drift.

Scenario 2: Ground-truth broadcast cameras in SLAHMR.
Counterfactual claim: “If SLAHMR receives ground-truth broadcast cameras, it will recover accurate multi-person global trajectories.”
Verification evidence: Table 3 shows SLAHMR G-MPJPE improves from 8334.1 mm to 5837.2 mm when ground-truth cameras are supplied, and Per-Meter Drift improves from 17.6 cm/m to 10.7 cm/m, but PA-MPJPE worsens from 163.9 mm to 199.6 mm [PAGE 13]. The supplementary material states that SLAHMR’s motion chunk stage can diverge when players are far apart, and that it may fail on “as many as half of the chunks, even with ground-truth camera parameters” [PAGE 28].
Veto condition: If global error improves but local pose error worsens or chunk convergence fails, the claim is rejected as an unconditional success.
Prediction: Ground-truth cameras reduce camera-induced global drift but do not fix floor estimation or inter-player relative placement. In sequences with large inter-player distances, fast pans, or tilted cameras, SLAHMR will still diverge or produce chunk-boundary discontinuities. A valid claim must condition on floor initialization and chunk convergence.

Scenario 3: Per-person alignment or single-reference global reasoning.
Counterfactual claim: “A method that aligns each player individually, or relies on one reference player, is sufficient for multi-person global pose estimation.”
Verification evidence: Table 3 shows per-person GLAMR G-MPJPE 3749.7 mm versus non-per-person 18888.9 mm, and per-person SLAHMR 4699.5 mm versus 8334.1 mm [PAGE 13]. The source states that “a significant portion of G-MPJPE arises from incorrect relative positions between the players” [PAGE 14]. It also notes that GLAMR “solely relies on the trajectory of the player with id=0” for extrinsic parameters [PAGE 26].
Veto condition: If per-person error is low but relative inter-player distance error remains high, the claim is rejected.
Prediction: Methods using a single reference player or per-person alignment will show low individual error for the reference subject but large errors for players far from that reference. The failure is relational: the scene graph of players is not globally consistent. Any verification reward must include relative inter-player distance metrics, not only per-player MPJPE or Procrustes-aligned pose error.
