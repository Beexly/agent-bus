The source can be audited as a scale-anchoring problem in monocular biomechanical measurement. MuyBridge does not attempt to regress whole-body center-of-mass directly from pixels. Instead, it separates the problem into three coupled estimation layers: image-space anatomical localization, relative depth measurement, and metric range anchoring. The first layer is a compact Halpe-26 pose network [PAGE 3]. The second layer is a distilled, affine-invariant monocular depth field [PAGE 3]. The third layer is an analytic fusion stage that converts relative observations into metric segment endpoints using anthropometry, ground contact, ballistic motion, and a constant-velocity range model [PAGE 2], [PAGE 4]. The central multi-hop derivation is therefore: keypoints define viewing rays; depth samples provide a relative range signal; anthropometric segment lengths provide a size-based range; physical cues provide metric anchors; the fused range places the rays into metric camera coordinates; the metric joint positions feed the de Leva segmental center-of-mass equations [PAGE 2].

A first gray zone appears at the definition of the camera ray. Equation (3) states \(d_{f,j} \propto K^{-1}[u_{f,j}, v_{f,j}, 1]^\top\) [PAGE 3]. The proportionality is essential. Unless the ray is normalized or otherwise consistently scaled, the scalar multiplying the ray has ambiguous units. The paper’s later range estimates assume that the ray geometry has been reduced to a consistent scale so that \(p_j = t d_j\) has metric range \(t\) in meters [PAGE 2]. If the implementation normalizes \(d\) to unit length, then \(t\) is Euclidean camera depth in meters. If it does not, then any scale error in \(K\) or in the normalization convention propagates multiplicatively into all metric joint positions. This is not a minor implementation detail: it determines whether the denominator in the anthropometric range equation is dimensionless or carries inverse length units.

A second gray zone is the affine-invariant depth field. The paper states: “The predicted depth is affine-invariant” [PAGE 3]. This means the network output is not metric. It can encode relative changes in athlete range, but it cannot by itself resolve the unknown multiplicative scale and offset between normalized depth and metric range. Equation (5) addresses this by fitting \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) to metric anchors: \((\alpha,\beta) = \arg\min_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{f\in A} w_f(\alpha \tilde z_f + \beta - t_f^{\text{anchor}})^2\) [PAGE 4]. The identifiability condition is strict. If the anchor set \(A\) contains only one distinct value of \(\tilde z_f\), then \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are not jointly identifiable. If the anchors have nearly constant \(\tilde z_f\), the least-squares problem becomes ill-conditioned. If the anchor range values are biased by a bad ground-plane calibration, then the learned depth scale is biased even if the depth field itself is locally smooth.

A third gray zone is the anthropometric size cue. Equation (4) gives \(t_{f,k}^{\text{size}} = L_k / \|d_{f,i} - d_{f,j}\|\) [PAGE 4]. This equation is exact only under strong assumptions: the segment endpoints are visible, the segment length \(L_k\) is known, the endpoints lie at approximately the same camera depth, and foreshortening is negligible. The paper explicitly acknowledges that “foreshortening biases individual estimates upward” [PAGE 4]. The mitigation is to aggregate visible segment estimates with a fixed low quantile [PAGE 4]. This is robust against many overestimates, but it creates a different failure mode: if most visible segments are severely foreshortened or occluded, the low quantile may still be too large, or if the remaining estimates are noisy, the low quantile can become unstable. In running, all 26 keypoints reduce median error to 187 mm, while hip-only sampling increases running error to 344 mm [PAGE 8]. This shows that segment-based scale recovery depends on distributed anatomical evidence.

A fourth gray zone is the transition from range to full metric pose. The paper says the fused athlete range \(\hat t_f\) places the predicted keypoint rays in metric camera coordinates, and “the hip-center ray defines a vertical reconstruction plane at this range” [PAGE 4]. This is a geometric simplification. It assumes the athlete can be approximated by a plane consistent with the hip ray and the estimated range. That plane is then intersected with each keypoint ray to obtain metric joint positions. The assumption is reasonable for many gait and throwing poses, but it breaks when the body has large out-of-plane depth variation, such as an extended arm or leg moving toward or away from the camera. In that case, forcing all joints onto one vertical plane can trade range error for articulation error.

A fifth gray zone is temporal. The pose network runs at 63 FPS, while depth updates at 2.86 Hz [PAGE 1], [PAGE 5]. The fusion uses the most recent depth field at pose rate [PAGE 5]. The depth period is approximately \(1/2.86 \approx 0.35\) s. During that interval, a translating athlete can move substantially through camera depth. At 8 m/s, the athlete can move about 2.8 m between depth updates. The paper’s asynchronous schedule is therefore valid only if the depth signal is sufficiently slowly varying or if the constant-velocity range model can bridge the gap. This is most dangerous in javelin and figure skating, where sustained translation and airborne motion make range recovery dominant [PAGE 6], [PAGE 7].

The overall gray-zone audit is therefore not that the equations are invalid. They are internally coherent. The gray zone is the set of assumptions required for each equation to be observable. The system is strongest when many anatomical endpoints are visible, when ground contact is present, when range is moderate, and when the depth field is refreshed often enough relative to motion. It weakens when range is large, when flight removes contact, when keypoints are occluded, when foreshortening dominates, when calibration drifts, and when the depth branch is stale. This is exactly what the ablations show: removing depth-derived range increases running error from 187 to 392 mm, while removing keypoint-depth entirely increases it to 387 mm [PAGE 8]. The method is not merely a pose-lifting method; it is a constrained scale estimator whose accuracy is governed by the weakest observable anchor.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal operators in the source are segmental mass integration, camera-ray back-projection, affine-invariant depth sampling, anthropometric range inversion, physical anchor fitting, metric fusion, and center-of-mass error accounting. Each has explicit units and breakdown regimes.

1. Segmental center of mass.

For segment \(s\), with proximal endpoint \(p_s^{\text{prox}}\) and distal endpoint \(p_s^{\text{dist}}\), the segment center of mass is

\[
c_s = p_s^{\text{prox}} + \rho_s \left(p_s^{\text{dist}} - p_s^{\text{prox}}\right)
\]

[PAGE 2].

Units: \(p_s^{\text{prox}}, p_s^{\text{dist}}, c_s\) are in meters. \(\rho_s\) is dimensionless and lies between 0 and 1 for a longitudinal point along the segment. The source states that \(\rho_s\) specifies “the longitudinal position of the center of mass along segment s” [PAGE 2].

Whole-body center of mass is

\[
C = \frac{\sum_s m_s c_s}{\sum_s m_s}
\]

[PAGE 2].

Units: \(m_s\) are dimensionless mass fractions; \(C\) is in meters. The paper uses “sex-specific segment mass fractions and longitudinal CoM positions” [PAGE 2].

Breakdown regime: if a segment endpoint is missing, \(c_s\) is undefined. If the wrong sex-specific parameter set is selected, the error is not merely geometric but anthropometric. If hand mass is folded into forearms, then forearm endpoint and mass assignments must be consistent [PAGE 4]. If segment endpoints are placed on a single reconstructed plane, out-of-plane segment depth becomes a modeled error rather than an observed quantity.

2. Camera ray formation.

For keypoint \((u_{f,j}, v_{f,j})\), the viewing ray is

\[
d_{f,j} \propto K^{-1}[u_{f,j}, v_{f,j}, 1]^\top
\]

[PAGE 3].

Units: \(u_{f,j}\) and \(v_{f,j}\) are pixels; \(K^{-1}\) maps pixel coordinates to camera-coordinate directions. The ray \(d_{f,j}\) is defined only up to scale unless normalized. If normalized to unit length, \(d_{f,j}\) is dimensionless and \(p_j = t d_j\) has \(t\) in meters.

Breakdown regime: if \(K\) is inaccurate, the ray direction is wrong. If lens distortion is unmodeled, the ray error grows toward image periphery. If the keypoint confidence \(w_j\) is low, the ray may not correspond to the intended anatomical landmark.

3. Sparse keypoint-depth sampling.

The depth branch predicts a dense normalized depth field \(Z(u,v)\) [PAGE 3]. At each accepted keypoint,

\[
z_{f,j} = Z_f(u_{f,j}, v_{f,j})
\]

[PAGE 3].

The robust aggregate is

\[
\tilde z_f = \operatorname{median}_{j \in V_f} z_{f,j}
\]

[PAGE 3].

Units: \(z_{f,j}\) and \(\tilde z_f\) are normalized depth values, not meters. They carry no metric scale because the depth is affine-invariant [PAGE 3]. The paper states that \(\tilde z_f\) “captures changes in athlete range but carries no metric scale” [PAGE 3].

Breakdown regime: if fewer than half of the valid samples agree with the athlete, the median can be dominated by background. If the athlete is partially occluded, the retained set \(V_f\) may be biased toward visible body parts. If depth is temporally stale, the aggregate may reflect a previous range.

4. Anthropometric size-based range.

For a visible segment \(k\) with known metric length \(L_k\), endpoint rays \(d_{f,i}\) and \(d_{f,j}\), the size-based range estimate is

\[
t_{f,k}^{\text{size}} = \frac{L_k}{\|d_{f,i} - d_{f,j}\|}
\]

[PAGE 4].

Units: if \(d\) is normalized, the denominator is dimensionless and \(t_{f,k}^{\text{size}}\) is in meters. \(L_k\) is a stature-scaled de Leva segment length or a measured length [PAGE 3], [PAGE 4].

The paper says: “Because foreshortening biases individual estimates upward, range estimates across visible segments are aggregated using a fixed low quantile” [PAGE 4].

Breakdown regime: if the projected segment length tends to zero, the denominator tends to zero and the range estimate diverges. If the segment is foreshortened, the denominator is too small and the range is overestimated. If the segment endpoints are at different depths, the single scalar \(t\) is only an approximation. The low-quantile aggregation assumes that at least some visible segments are not severely foreshortened.

5. Physical anchors: ground contact and ballistic motion.

Ground contact yields range through ray–ground intersection [PAGE 4]. If the ground plane is \(n^\top x = h\), with unit normal \(n\) and height \(h\) in meters, and a contact ray is \(p = t d\), then

\[
t_{\text{contact}} = \frac{h}{n^\top d}
\]

provided \(n^\top d \neq 0\).

Units: \(h\) in meters, \(n^\top d\) dimensionless if \(d\) is normalized. Breakdown: if the ray is nearly parallel to the ground plane, \(n^\top d \to 0\) and the estimate becomes unstable. If the foot is not actually on the calibrated ground plane, the anchor is biased.

Ballistic motion yields range by relating image-space acceleration to gravity [PAGE 4]. Under a small-angle, stationary-camera, approximately constant-range approximation, if \(f\) is focal length in pixels, \(a_{\text{img}}\) is image-space acceleration in pixels per second squared, and \(g_\perp\) is the projection of gravitational acceleration onto the image plane in meters per second squared, then

\[
t_{\text{ballistic}} \approx \frac{f \, \|g_\perp\|}{\|a_{\text{img}}\|}
\]

Units: \(f\) in pixels, \(a_{\text{img}}\) in pixels/s\(^2\), \(g_\perp\) in m/s\(^2\), giving \(t_{\text{ballistic}}\) in meters.

Breakdown: if the athlete is not airborne, there is no ballistic cue. If frame rate is too low, acceleration estimation is noisy. If range changes rapidly during flight, the constant-range approximation fails. If camera gravity orientation is unknown, \(g_\perp\) is misspecified.

6. Affine-depth anchoring.

For frames \(A\) where a metric anchor \(t_f^{\text{anchor}}\) is available, the depth-to-metric mapping is fitted by

\[
(\alpha,\beta) = \arg\min_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{f\in A} w_f \left(\alpha \tilde z_f + \beta - t_f^{\text{anchor}}\right)^2
\]

[PAGE 4].

Units: \(\tilde z_f\) is normalized depth; \(t_f^{\text{anchor}}\) is meters; \(\alpha\) has units of meters per normalized-depth unit; \(\beta\) has units of meters. If \(w_f\) is inverse variance, its units are m\(^{-2}\), making each weighted term dimensionless; if \(w_f\) is dimensionless, the objective has units of m\(^2\).

The resulting depth-derived range is

\[
t_f^{\text{depth}} = \alpha \tilde z_f + \beta
\]

[PAGE 4].

Breakdown: if \(A\) has too few frames or too little variation in \(\tilde z_f\), \(\alpha\) is poorly constrained. If the anchor is biased, the entire depth-derived range is biased. If \(\alpha\) is negative or nonphysical, the affine model has been fitted to inconsistent cues.

7. Constant-velocity range fusion.

The paper states that “the anthropometric, physical, and depth-derived measurements are fused with a constant-velocity range model to obtain the final athlete range \(\hat t_f\)” [PAGE 4]. A minimal formal representation is a state \(x_f = [t_f, \dot t_f]^\top\), with state transition

\[
x_f = 
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & \Delta t \\
0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
x_{f-1} + w_f
\]

where \(t_f\) is in meters, \(\dot t_f\) is in meters per second, \(\Delta t\) is in seconds, and \(w_f\) is process noise. Measurements from size, contact, ballistic, and depth cues enter through observation models. In probabilistic units, the posterior density \(p(t_f \mid \text{data})\) has units of m\(^{-1}\); log-likelihoods may be expressed in nats or bits.

Breakdown: constant velocity fails under acceleration. Javelin run-up and figure-skating takeoff/landing are not constant-range events. If the depth update interval is long, the prior must bridge a large temporal gap.

8. Metric plane reconstruction.

The fused range \(\hat t_f\) is used to place the hip-center ray in metric coordinates, and a vertical reconstruction plane is defined [PAGE 4]. If the plane has unit normal \(n_\Pi\) and offset \(d_\Pi\) in meters, then a keypoint ray \(d_{f,j}\) intersects the plane at

\[
t_{f,j}^{\text{plane}} = \frac{d_\Pi}{n_\Pi^\top d_{f,j}}
\]

provided \(n_\Pi^\top d_{f,j} \neq 0\). The metric point is then \( \hat p_{f,j} = t_{f,j}^{\text{plane}} d_{f,j}\).

Units: \(d_\Pi\) in meters; \(n_\Pi^\top d_{f,j}\) dimensionless if \(d_{f,j}\) is normalized; \(t_{f,j}^{\text{plane}}\) in meters.

Breakdown: if a keypoint ray is parallel to the plane, the denominator approaches zero. If the hip ray is wrong, the plane is wrong. If the athlete’s true joints have substantial out-of-plane depth, the plane approximation introduces articulation error even if the range is correct.

9. Error metric.

The paper defines range absolute-relative error as

\[
\text{AbsRel} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_t \frac{|\hat Z_t - Z_t|}{Z_t}
\]

[PAGE 5].

Units: dimensionless if \(\hat Z_t\) and \(Z_t\) are in the same length unit; commonly reported as a percentage. Breakdown: if \(Z_t\) is very small, relative error becomes unstable, although athlete-to-camera range is normally positive and bounded away from zero.

10. Error propagation and range dominance.

The source reports that larger 3D errors are concentrated along camera depth: “The larger 3D errors are concentrated along camera depth” [PAGE 6]. For a keypoint at angular offset \(\theta\) from the camera axis, metric lateral displacement is approximately \(X \approx t \tan\theta\). A range perturbation \(\delta t\) gives \(\delta X \approx \tan\theta \, \delta t\). A small angular perturbation \(\delta\theta\) gives \(\delta X \approx t \sec^2\theta \, \delta\theta\). At large \(t\), range error and angular error both amplify, but range recovery becomes dominant when the anthropometric projected length is small. This is consistent with the skating result: mean athlete range is 10.1 m and 3D CoM error is 707 mm [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of MuyBridge runs through three historically separate traditions: classical biomechanical body-segment modeling, computer-vision human pose estimation, and monocular depth estimation. The biomechanical tradition supplies the target quantity. The source explicitly uses “the segmental anthropometric model of Zatsiorsky and Seluyanov, as re-expressed by de Leva” [PAGE 2]. This lineage begins with cadaver-based and population-based body-segment parameter estimation and continues through marker-based motion capture, force plates, and inertial sensing. The paper states that conventional measurement “commonly requires metric body-segment positions together with segment lengths, mass fractions, and segment-specific centers of mass” [PAGE 1]. The historical trap is to assume that because CoM is a mechanical quantity, it can be measured without anthropometric priors. MuyBridge instead encodes those priors directly through \(\rho_s\) and \(m_s\).

The second lineage is pose estimation. The system uses an RTMPose-style compact pose branch with an RTMDet person detector, CSPNeXt backbone, gated attention, and SimCC regression head [PAGE 3]. This places MuyBridge in the modern efficient-vision lineage where 2D keypoints are treated as reliable front-end observations. The historical trap is to over-trust keypoint accuracy. The paper avoids some of this by using confidence and depth-consistency filtering [PAGE 3], [PAGE 8], but the system still depends on Halpe-26 keypoints being correctly localized. The ablation shows that broad keypoint coverage matters: all 26 keypoints provide the strongest range correlation in every regime [PAGE 8]. A multi-week deployment trap is that lighting changes, clothing changes, and camera viewpoint changes can degrade keypoint quality even if the network was evaluated on COCO-WholeBody and HICO-DET [PAGE 3].

The third lineage is monocular depth. MuyBridge uses a Marigold-based latent depth model reduced to a single-step UNet through latent consistency distillation [PAGE 3]. It is trained on synthetic RGB-D pairs from Hypersim and Virtual KITTI 2 [PAGE 3]. This lineage gives MuyBridge relative scene geometry but not metric scale. The paper is explicit: “The predicted depth is affine-invariant” [PAGE 3]. The historical trap is to assume that a strong depth network solves metric scale. It does not. Scale must be recovered from body dimensions, ground contact, ballistic gravity, or external calibration.

A deeper lineage is the biomechanically grounded vision tradition. The paper cites BioPose, HSMR, IPMAN, OpenCap, and visual-hull CoM estimation [PAGE 2]. These works show that anatomical and physical constraints improve human-motion reconstruction, but they often rely on heavy 3D body models, optimization, or multi-view capture [PAGE 2]. MuyBridge’s distinct contribution is not a new learned CoM regressor; it is an analytic deployment path. It uses “image-space pose, subject-specific anthropometry, sparse monocular depth, and geometric range constraints without task-specific 3D human-pose or CoM supervision” [PAGE 2].

Multi-week traps arise when the system is moved from benchmark evaluation to repeated field use. First, the one-time scene calibration can drift. The paper says a one-time scene calibration provides the ground plane and landmark offsets [PAGE 5]. If the camera is moved, the ground plane changes, or the landmark offsets are no longer valid, the physical anchors become biased. Second, the anthropometric prior is population-based. Subject stature and sex are specified once before recording [PAGE 5], but individual limb proportions, body composition, and segment mass distribution may differ. Third, asynchronous depth updates can become stale during translation. The depth network runs at 2.86 Hz, while pose runs at 63 FPS [PAGE 5]. Over multiple weeks of high-speed athletic motion, this can become the dominant temporal trap. Fourth, quantization can subtly degrade depth structure. The paper notes that post-training quantization degrades the UNet’s predicted depth structure, which is why quantization-aware training is used for the UNet [PAGE 4]. A long-term deployment trap is that quantization error may interact with lighting or weather, producing failures not captured by the reported AbsRel tables [PAGE 5].

The scientific lineage also includes the benchmark. AthletePose3D contains running, track and field, and figure skating [PAGE 5]. The paper evaluates across cyclic, rotational, and ballistic motion [PAGE 2]. This is historically important because CoM estimation in sport often assumes laboratory marker capture. MuyBridge asks whether a phone can recover the same biomechanical quantity under less controlled conditions. The multi-week trap is to confuse benchmark median error with field robustness. The paper itself acknowledges that absolute localization is limited by camera-to-athlete range, especially under sustained translation and long-range airborne motion [PAGE 8]. That limitation is not a bug; it is the physical consequence of monocular scale recovery.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem in MuyBridge is not about human intention; it is about how the system’s internal estimators model each other under stress. The pose branch, depth branch, anthropometric size estimator, physical anchor estimator, and constant-velocity fusion layer each hold an implicit belief about the athlete’s metric range. The final estimate \(\hat t_f\) is a negotiated belief. Under normal conditions, these beliefs agree. Under crisis conditions, they can diverge, and the system’s fallback behavior becomes the dominant error source.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency of the system to keep using the most recently trusted cue even when that cue has become invalid. The most dangerous habit is continued reliance on a stale depth field. The paper says fusion is performed at pose rate using the most recent depth field [PAGE 5]. That is efficient, but under rapid translation it means the depth belief may lag the pose belief. The depth update period is approximately 0.35 s. If the athlete translates at 8 m/s, the depth field can be spatially outdated by nearly 3 m. The pose branch sees current articulation, but the depth branch supplies an older range. The fusion layer may smooth this discrepancy through the constant-velocity model, but if acceleration is high, the habit becomes a failure mode.

A second crisis habit is ground-contact overconfidence. The physical range cue from ground contact is strong when the foot is truly on the calibrated ground plane. In running, this can stabilize metric scale. In figure skating flight, however, ground contact disappears. The paper reports contact error of 3.6–5.6% of athlete range compared with 7.0–8.7% during flight [PAGE 7]. If the system continues to weight a ground-contact belief during flight, it will underestimate or overestimate range depending on how the anchor was last updated. The counter-trap is to explicitly gate ground-contact cues by contact classification and to switch to ballistic or anthropometric cues during flight.

A third crisis habit is depth-median overconfidence. The sparse depth aggregate uses a median over accepted keypoints [PAGE 3]. The median is robust when most samples belong to the athlete. In profile views or occluded throwing poses, the accepted set may be small or biased. The paper notes that perpendicular orientations can increase overlap and occlusion of distal joints, reducing landmarks available for anatomical range estimation and depth sampling [PAGE 7]. If the system habitually treats the median as a whole-body range estimate, it may ignore that the visible samples represent only a subset of the body.

Counter-trap behavior requires the system to recognize when the dominant cue is invalid. The paper already contains partial counter-traps: pose confidence and local depth consistency are used jointly to reject observations inconsistent with the athlete [PAGE 3]; foreshortening is mitigated by low-quantile aggregation [PAGE 4]; anthropometric range can provide metric range from the first usable frame [PAGE 5]. A stronger counter-trap would be a cue-validity monitor that estimates the probability that each range cue is currently observable. In probability terms, the fusion should use posterior weights \(p(\text{cue valid} \mid \text{data})\), not fixed assumptions. If ground contact is absent, the ground-contact likelihood should collapse. If depth is stale, the depth likelihood should broaden.

The clock is the temporal adjudicator. The pose clock is fast: 63 FPS [PAGE 5]. The depth clock is slow: 2.86 Hz [PAGE 5]. The fusion clock must reconcile them. If the athlete’s range changes faster than the depth clock, the system must either trust the constant-velocity model more, use anthropometric size cues more heavily, or trigger a depth refresh. In running, the paper reports median depth error of 166 mm despite the asynchronous schedule [PAGE 6]. In skating, depth error is 672 mm at a mean range of 10.1 m [PAGE 6]. This is not merely a network-accuracy problem; it is a clock mismatch amplified by range.

The recursive failure mode is therefore a three-body problem: pose belief, depth belief, and physical-anchor belief. The pose belief is temporally sharp but lacks metric scale. The depth belief has relative range but is temporally slow and affine-invariant. The physical-anchor belief has metric validity but is conditional on contact, calibration, and gravity observability. MuyBridge is successful when these three beliefs cohere. It degrades when one belief becomes stale, biased, or unavailable. The correct theoretical interpretation is not that the network is wrong; it is that the system’s internal theory of observability must be as dynamic as the athlete.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather layers provide hard veto conditions. A veto is not a matter of taste; it is a regime in which the governing equations lose observability or the error propagation becomes unbounded relative to the task requirement.

Physical veto: affine depth cannot determine metric scale. If the depth field is affine-invariant, then an exact transformation \(\tilde z' = a \tilde z + b\) leaves the relative depth observations consistent with the same underlying scene up to scale and offset. Without an external anchor, the metric range is unidentifiable. The source acknowledges this by estimating \((\alpha,\beta)\) from physical anchors [PAGE 4]. Therefore, any condition that removes all anchors vetoes metric reconstruction. Examples include flight without a reliable ballistic acceleration estimate, an uncalibrated ground plane, and a scene with no contact event. In such a regime, the posterior over range remains broad, and any point estimate is arbitrary.

Physical veto: range error grows quadratically with distance for fixed angular error. For an anthropometric segment of true length \(L\), the projected angular length is approximately \(s \approx L/t\) when the segment is perpendicular to the viewing ray. Inverting gives \(t \approx L/s\). A small angular error \(\delta s\) produces

\[
\delta t \approx \frac{L}{s^2}\delta s \approx \frac{t^2}{L}\delta s
\]

Thus, at long range, a fixed pixel or angular error becomes a large metric error. This explains why skating at a mean range of 10.1 m has 707 mm 3D CoM error while running at 4.4 m has 187 mm [PAGE 6]. The veto condition is explicit: when \(t^2/L\) becomes large relative to the allowed CoM error, monocular metric recovery fails.

Biomechanical veto: CoM requires segment endpoints, and segment endpoints require anatomical visibility. Equation (1) cannot be evaluated if \(p_s^{\text{prox}}\) or \(p_s^{\text{dist}}\) is missing. The system uses Halpe-26 keypoints [PAGE 3], but occlusion, self-overlap, and profile orientation can remove distal landmarks. The paper observes that perpendicular orientations can increase overlap and occlusion, reducing available landmarks [PAGE 7]. In that regime, the anthropometric size cue loses segments, the depth median loses valid samples, and the plane reconstruction becomes more sensitive to the remaining joints. A biomechanical veto occurs when the visible keypoint set is too small to constrain all segment endpoints needed for whole-body CoM. The running ablation supports this: hip-only sampling increases running error from 187 to 344 mm [PAGE 8]. The system needs distributed anatomical evidence.

Biomechanical veto: flight removes the ground-contact anchor. The paper separates contact and flight phases and finds that flight error is 1.5–2.3 times contact error across six jump types [PAGE 7]. This is a direct biomechanical veto. During flight, the only physical metric anchor is ballistic motion. If the airborne phase is short, blurred, or viewed from a poor angle, the ballistic anchor may be weaker than the constant-velocity prior. In that case, the range estimate is governed by extrapolation rather than observation. The paper’s skating error confirms that flight is a major degradation regime [PAGE 6], [PAGE 7].

Biomechanical veto: population anthropometry can be structurally biased. The model uses stature-scaled, sex-specific de Leva parameters [PAGE 1], [PAGE 5]. If the athlete’s true segment lengths or mass fractions differ substantially from the population prior, the CoM will be biased even if all keypoints are perfectly localized. The paper acknowledges that the anthropometric reconstruction uses stature-scaled, sex-specific population segment proportions [PAGE 8]. This is not a failure of the vision system; it is a failure of the biomechanical prior. A subject-adaptive anthropometry layer would be required to remove this veto.

Weather veto: adverse weather can break the appearance assumptions of both pose and depth networks. The paper does not provide a dedicated weather experiment, but the physical argument is decisive. The pose network is trained on COCO-WholeBody and HICO-DET [PAGE 3]. The depth network is trained on Hypersim and Virtual KITTI 2 [PAGE 3]. Rain, snow, ice glare, low sun, fog, and wet clothing can shift the input distribution. In probabilistic terms, if training distribution is \(P\) and test distribution is \(Q\), the expected risk under \(Q\) is

\[
R_Q(f) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim Q}[\ell(f(x), y)]
\]

If \(Q\) places mass on appearance modes absent from \(P\), the risk can increase without bound relative to the validation risk under \(P\). In formal terms, if \(Q\) is not absolutely continuous with respect to \(P\), there is no finite density ratio to correct the error. Thus, severe weather can veto the system by making the depth field unreliable, the keypoints unstable, or the ground plane unobservable.

Weather veto: ice and snow change the ground-contact cue. The ground-contact anchor assumes a calibrated ground plane and a visible contact event. On ice, reflective glare can corrupt depth samples; on snow, the foot may sink or the contact point may be visually ambiguous. If the ground plane calibration was performed under different conditions, the ray–ground intersection is biased. This is a geometric veto, not a learned-model issue. The equation \(t_{\text{contact}} = h/(n^\top d)\) is only valid when \(h\) and \(n\) correspond to the current physical surface.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: the system should not output a metric CoM estimate when the posterior over range is dominated by unobservable cues. The paper’s best performance occurs when multiple anchors cohere: pose, depth, anthropometry, and physical cues [PAGE 8]. The worst performance occurs when the range anchor is weak: long-range skating, javelin translation, profile occlusion, and flight [PAGE 6], [PAGE 7]. Therefore, the physical and biomechanical vetoes are not peripheral; they define the operational envelope of the method.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards style veto scenarios. Each scenario defines a test, a predicted quantitative outcome, and a veto condition. The reward is negative metric CoM error or negative range AbsRel. A candidate claim is vetoed if the measured reward worsens beyond the stated threshold when the counterfactual condition is introduced.

Scenario 1: Stale-depth clock veto.

Test: Take AthletePose3D running sequences and replay them with the depth branch delayed by one full depth update relative to the pose branch. The nominal depth update latency is 349.9 ms, corresponding to 2.86 Hz [PAGE 5]. The pose network runs at 63 FPS [PAGE 5]. Compare three conditions: synchronous depth, one-update stale depth, and two-update stale depth.

Prediction: The one-update stale depth condition should increase running depth error above the reported median depth component of 166 mm [PAGE 6]. The two-update stale depth condition should increase it further, because the athlete translates through camera depth during the stale interval. At 8 m/s, one depth interval corresponds to roughly 2.8 m of translation, which is large relative to the reported running range of 4.4 m [PAGE 6].

Verifiable veto: If the depth error does not increase monotonically with stale-depth delay, or if the increase is smaller than the expected translation-induced range change, then the claim that the asynchronous schedule is safe under rapid translation is vetoed. In reward terms, let \(R = -\text{Depth MAE}\). The veto condition is \(R_{\text{stale}} > R_{\text{sync}}\) by a margin larger than measurement noise.

Scenario 2: Flight-phase ballistic-anchor veto.

Test: In figure skating, separate contact and flight frames using the reference phase labels. Disable the ground-contact cue during flight frames and retain only ballistic, anthropometric, and depth-derived cues. Compare against the full MuyBridge formulation.

Prediction: The paper reports contact error of 3.6–5.6% of athlete range and flight error of 7.0–8.7% [PAGE 7]. Removing the ground-contact cue during flight should not improve flight error, because flight already lacks contact. However, if the system was implicitly relying on the last contact anchor during early flight, disabling it should increase early-flight error. The ablation also shows that removing the ground-contact cue increases error across motions [PAGE 8]. The expected direction is therefore an increase in skating flight error, especially near takeoff and landing transitions.

Verifiable veto: If disabling the ground-contact cue during flight reduces error, then the reported flight degradation may be misattributed. The veto condition is a negative change in flight-phase reward, \(R_{\text{no-ground, flight}} < R_{\text{full, flight}}\), beyond confidence bounds. The prediction is verifiable using the phase-separated error protocol in Figure 6 [PAGE 7].

Scenario 3: Keypoint-coverage anthropometric veto.

Test: Restrict depth sampling and segment-length range estimation to progressively smaller keypoint subsets: all 26 keypoints, 12 limb keypoints, 6 torso keypoints, and hip only. Re-evaluate running, track and field, and skating.

Prediction: The paper reports that all 26 keypoints give the strongest range correlation in every regime and reduce running error from 341–344 mm to 187 mm [PAGE 8]. Track and field is relatively insensitive, remaining between 185 and 187 mm across sampling sets [PAGE 8]. Skating is more complex: torso-only sampling achieves 633 mm, lower than the full 707 mm, despite lower range correlation [PAGE 8]. Therefore, the verifiable prediction is regime-dependent. In running, reducing keypoint coverage must worsen error substantially. In track and field, the change should be small. In skating, reduced coverage may not worsen error monotonically because torso samples may be more stable than distal limb samples during flight.

Verifiable veto: If reducing keypoint coverage in running does not increase error, then the claim that broad anatomical coverage is necessary for size-based range recovery is vetoed. If torso-only sampling in skating does not produce the reported 633 mm behavior under the same aggregation protocol, then the claim that correlation alone does not determine CoM accuracy is vetoed. The reward is negative 3D CoM MAE; the veto condition is failure to reproduce the direction and approximate magnitude of the ablation table [PAGE 8].

These three vetoes are verifiable because they rely on reported metrics, reported ablation directions, and physically measurable temporal or anatomical perturbations. They do not require betting assumptions. They require only reproducible counterfactual tests and quantitative thresholds.
