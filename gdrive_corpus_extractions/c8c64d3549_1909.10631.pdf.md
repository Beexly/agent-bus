The multi-hop derivation runs as follows. Hop 1: football data are observational. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Define the decision \(D_i\in\{0,1\}\), where \(D_i=1\) means “go for it” and \(D_i=0\) means field goal or punt. Define \(U_i\) as the precise yards needed for a first down, with unit yd. Define \(Y_i\) as an outcome: either a conversion indicator (dimensionless) or win probability added (a dimensionless probability difference). Wins-added per team-year has unit wins·team\(^{-1}\)·yr\(^{-1}\). The play-by-play coarsening operator is
\[
Z_i=\mathcal{B}(U_i),\qquad
\mathcal{B}(u)=1\ \text{for}\ u\in(0,2)\ \text{yd},\qquad
\mathcal{B}(u)=2\ \text{for}\ u\in[2,3)\ \text{yd},
\]
which reflects the source’s statement that “both 4th-and-0.1 inches and 4th-and-71.9 inches are listed as 4th-and-1’s” [PAGE 4]. The causal contrast is
\[
\Delta=\mathbb{E}[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)],
\]
where \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\) are potential outcomes under go-for-it and not-go-for-it. Identification requires conditional ignorability
\[
\{Y_i(1),Y_i(0)\}\perp D_i\mid (X_i,U_i),
\]
with \(X_i\) the observed play-by-play covariates. Omitting \(U_i\) breaks that condition. A minimal linear confounding model is
\[
Y_i=\alpha+\tau D_i+\beta U_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad
D_i=\gamma+\delta U_i+\nu_i.
\]
Omitting \(U_i\) gives
\[
\operatorname{plim}\hat{\tau}_{\text{omit }U}
=\tau+\beta\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(D_i,U_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(D_i)}.
\]
Here \(\beta\) has units probability per yd; \(\delta\) has units 1/yd; the bias term has units probability. Since conversion falls with distance (\(\beta\) negative) and longer distance lowers the go-for-it rate (\(\delta\) negative), the covariance is negative and the omitted-distance bias is positive: the naive benefit is too large. This is the formal version of the source’s conclusion that “past findings supporting an aggressive fourth down strategy may have been overstated” [PAGE 1].

The matching operator is
\[
\Delta_U(x)=\mathbb{E}[U_i\mid D_i=1,X_i=x]-\mathbb{E}[U_i\mid D_i=0,X_i=x],
\]
with unit yd. The source’s “squeezing the balloon” result is the regime \(\Delta_U(x)>\Delta_U\) for most \(x\), where \(\Delta_U=0.28\) yd is the aggregate gap [PAGE 7]. The GAM operators used in Figure 2 are
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(D_i=1\mid U_i=u)=f_D(u),\qquad
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(Y_i=1\mid D_i=1,U_i=u)=f_Y(u),
\]
with \(u\) in yd and probabilities dimensionless. The paper notes that although the GAMs allow nonlinearity, “each curve looks somewhat linear” [PAGE 7]. Measurement error is
\[
U_{\text{obs}}=U+\eta,\qquad |\eta|\leq 12\ \text{in}=0.333\ \text{yd},
\]
using the stated “+/- 12 inches” accuracy [PAGE 12]. Classical error gives attenuation
\[
\lambda=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(U)}{\operatorname{Var}(U)+\operatorname{Var}(\eta)},\qquad
\hat{\beta}_{\text{obs}}\to \lambda\beta.
\]
Breakdown regimes are exact: if \(\operatorname{Var}(D_i)=0\), no decision contrast exists; if propensity overlap fails, \(e(X)=\Pr(D=1\mid X)\) approaches 0 or 1 and matching estimates become unstable; if measurement-error variance dominates true variance, \(\lambda\to 0\) and the precise-distance effect collapses; if \(\eta\) is correlated with \(D\), the bias direction is unidentified; and if \(U\) is perfectly collinear with \(D\), no within-distance comparison is possible. The source therefore supports attenuation, not a claim that all confounding has been removed.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive structure has three agents: the coach, the opposing coordinator, and the analyst. The coach observes precise distance, clock, score, timeouts, and defensive alignment; the defense observes formation and motion and reacts; the coach may audible in response. The source describes this recursion directly: “Substitutions by one team lead to changes in personnel by the other. Formations by the defense lead to audibles by the offense. Motion from the slot back spurs a new defensive coverage scheme” [PAGE 2]. The analyst, by contrast, often observes only the play-by-play row and the integer distance bucket. If the analyst omits precise distance, the coach’s decision looks more conservative than it is, and the analyst’s model of the coach’s model becomes systematically wrong. The crisis-habit regression is the tendency to explain high-leverage fourth-down behavior as a coarse conservative habit. But the source shows continuous sensitivity: 70% go-for-it at inches versus 30% in long 4th-and-1, and 79% versus 55% conversion [PAGE 6]. That is not a binary habit; it is a graded response to an unobserved continuous variable. The counter-trap is overcorrection: after learning that past estimates were biased upward, an analyst may replace one coarse policy with another coarse policy (“always go”) rather than conditioning on precise distance. The clock is the temporal layer that makes the trap dangerous. Time, score, timeouts, and pre-play win probability are among the 17 play-by-play variables in the Yam and Lopez model [PAGE 7], but the clock does not measure precise distance. In crisis states, the clock can dominate attention while the distance confounder remains invisible. A correct recursive theory of mind therefore models the coach as responding to \(U\), the defense as responding to formation and motion, and the analyst as responding to a coarsened \(Z=\mathcal{B}(U)\). The analyst should not infer irrationality from a decision that is rational given information the analyst lacks. The source’s lesson is not that coaches are always right; it is that “there’s almost always more to the story” [PAGE 9].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
