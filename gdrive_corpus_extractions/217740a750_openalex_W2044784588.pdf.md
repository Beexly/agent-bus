The source’s formal operators can be stated with explicit units and assumptions. Let \(G_n\) be the gradient at equal-distance sub-section \(n\). The paper’s modified gradient, quoted verbatim, is: “Modified gradient =  (0.5 x g n + 0.25 x gn-1 +  0.125 x gn-2 …+ 0.003906 x g n-7 ) where g = gradient and n = current sub-section.” [PAGE 15]. In formal notation:
\[
M_n=\sum_{k=0}^{7}2^{-(k+1)}G_{n-k}
=0.5G_n+0.25G_{n-1}+0.125G_{n-2}+\cdots+0.00390625G_{n-7}.
\]
Here \(G_n\) is dimensionless if expressed as rise/run, or percent if expressed as percent grade; \(M_n\) carries the same gradient unit. The weights sum to \(1-2^{-8}=0.99609375\). There is no singularity in this finite sum. Breakdown regimes: \(n-k\) outside the measured course, early sub-sections lacking seven predecessors, fewer than eight equal-distance sub-sections, missing gradient survey data, or mixing percent and fraction units. If \(G\) is entered as percent rather than fraction, every regression coefficient \(B\) must be divided by 100 for dimensional consistency.

The group raw-gradient regression is:
\[
v_n=\beta_0+\beta_G G_n+\beta_L L_n+\varepsilon_n,
\]
with \(v_n\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(G_n\) as gradient fraction, \(L_n\) the lap index, and \(\varepsilon_n\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Table 2 gives \(\beta_0=3.948\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\beta_G=-8.265\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) per unit gradient fraction, \(\beta_L=-0.103\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) per lap, adjusted \(R^2=0.825\), and SEE \(=0.239\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 32]. The modified-gradient group regression is:
\[
v_n=\beta_0'+\beta_M M_n+\beta_L' L_n+\varepsilon_n',
\]
with \(\beta_0'=3.979\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\beta_M=-9.743\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) per unit modified gradient fraction, \(\beta_L'=-0.114\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) per lap, adjusted \(R^2=0.891\), and SEE \(=0.189\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 32]. The abstract’s “89% of group level speed was predicted using a modified gradient factor” [PAGE 3] is this adjusted \(R^2\) statement. The paper also reports individual-level adjusted \(R^2\) values in the 0.651–0.733 range when adding physiological predictors such as VO2max, VT, vVO2max, or vVT [PAGE 32].

Stride length is derived from speed and stride frequency. With \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(f_s\) in strides/min, and \(L_s\) in metres:
\[
L_s=\frac{60v}{f_s}.
\]
The paper says: “Direct interpolation from GPS speed data was then used to derive average stride lengths based on speed and stride frequency.” [PAGE 11]. This equation has an exact singularity at \(f_s=0\), i.e. no detected steps, and it breaks down when accelerometer step detection misses peaks or when “stride” and “step” definitions are inconsistent. The relative VT workload is:
\[
\rho_{\mathrm{VT}}=100\frac{\dot V O_2}{\dot V O_{2,\mathrm{VT}}},
\]
where \(\dot V O_2\) and \(\dot V O_{2,\mathrm{VT}}\) are in \(\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}\) or \(\mathrm{mL\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\), and \(\rho_{\mathrm{VT}}\) is percent. This ratio is undefined if the ventilatory-threshold denominator is zero or unmeasured. Table 1 reports \(\rho_{\mathrm{VT}}\) as \(100.4\pm11.9\%\) uphill, \(89.3\pm13.8\%\) level, and \(78.9\pm11.3\%\) downhill [PAGE 31]. That is \(\Delta v/\Delta G\approx0.082\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) per 1% grade, with the prior study at \(0.034\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) per 1% grade. There are no information-theoretic bits or calibrated probabilities reported in the source; adjusted \(R^2\) is dimensionless variance fraction, and SEE is in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Breakdown regimes for the regressions include extrapolation beyond the observed course gradients, confounding by individual pacing strategy, small group \(N=8\), and the interpolation of drink-stop sub-sections: the paper states that some data were “replaced with estimates through subject-by-subject linear interpolation” [PAGE 9].

The source’s own cross-sectional design supports only acute spontaneous pacing under one field protocol. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive-reader model of the runner must distinguish three regimes. First, crisis habit regression: under fatigue, the runner may fall back on a stable cadence and a previously learned stride-length habit rather than continuously optimizing. The source reports that stride frequency was “remarkably stable across all sections of the course” [PAGE 13], and that stride length carried most speed change [PAGE 14]. It also found two lap strategies: “four of the runners slowed monotonically  across the three laps” while the other four increased speed from lap 2 to lap 3 [PAGE 16]. A recursive model should predict that the monotonic-slowdown group is more likely to regress to a conservative habit when internal effort signals rise, whereas the accelerating group is either better at reserving early effort or more willing to tolerate later discomfort.

Second, the counter-trap: the runner forms a second-order model of their future self (“if I go harder uphill, I will pay on the level”) and may overcorrect. The paper’s correlation supports this: “The r of -0.775 suggests that those runners who minimised fluctuations in their oxygen consumption across the gradients achieved this by varying their speed more (i.e., by running slower on uphills and faster on downhills).” [PAGE 16]. That is a counter-trap because the runner who tries to keep pace too even across gradients may incur larger VO2 fluctuations, whereas the runner who varies pace more may stabilize metabolism. The paper proposes: “those runners who varied their pace more as a function of gradient showed smaller changes in oxygen consumption” [PAGE 24]. But the counter-trap has a second edge on downhills: the runner may over-speed to recover time and expose themselves to impact, or under-speed for recovery and lose time. The source notes that some runners “may have attempted to attenuate the shock of running downhill as an injury prevention mechanism.” [PAGE 21].

Third, the clock: because “No watches were worn by participants and no feedback was given so as to prevent any form of external” [PAGE 9] “pacing.” [PAGE 10], the runner must use an internal clock and effort heuristic. The lap effect shows this internal clock is imperfect: Lap 1 was faster than Laps 2 or 3 by 55 seconds and 51 seconds respectively [PAGE 12–13]. The recursive theory of mind here is not mind-reading others; it is the runner’s model of their own future physiological state, the experimenter’s model of spontaneous pacing, and the analyst’s model of whether a speed change is recovery, strategy, or biomechanical protection. The study supports a hierarchical control account: macro-lap pacing and micro-gradient pacing can diverge, as “those who adopted a conservative strategy with respect to laps … did not necessarily do so over hills” [PAGE 24]. The clock is therefore not a single timer but a multi-level internal estimate: lap-level time, section-level gradient expectation, and threshold-level effort.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer is gradient and gravity. The paper states that “changes in gradient pose a special challenge as they involve the largest changes in energy expenditure” [PAGE 5]. Course geometry is explicit: level 765 m, uphill 820 m, level 770 m, downhill 820 m [PAGE 8], with uphill sub-section gradients 6.3%, 9.3%, 11.2%, 6.8%, 11.7%, 10.7%, 1.5%, and 7.8% [PAGE 8]. The speed response is large: 23% slower uphill and 13.8% faster downhill [PAGE 3]. The metabolic veto is supported uphill because VO2 averaged \(100.4\%\) of individual VT uphill and lap 1 exceeded VT at \(105.2\pm13.1\%\), after which runners reduced speed [PAGE 14][PAGE 31]. Thus the uphill adjudication is: physical gradient imposes work, and the physiological VT layer vetoes further sustainable speed. The level-after-uphill lag of \(78.4\pm7.0\ \mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 3] is consistent with recovery from that metabolic cost.

The biomechanical layer vetoes the naive “run as fast as VO2 allows” rule downhill. Downhill VO2 was only \(78.9\%\) of VT [PAGE 31], so metabolism was not the limiting factor: “downhill speeds were not limited by physiological cost as, as oxygen consumption was substantially less than VT” [PAGE 20]. Instead, impact and shock attenuation become plausible vetoes. The source cites increases of 54% normal force and 73% shear force when running at \(3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) on a \(-9\%\) grade versus level [PAGE 22]. It also states: “Shock attenuation has been shown to be altered primarily with changes in stride length rather than frequency” [PAGE 22]. This makes stride length the likely control variable for downhill protection. The adjudication is therefore not “VO2 veto everywhere”; it is “VO2 veto uphill, biomechanical veto downhill, and stride length as the shared actuator.”

The weather and surface layer is controlled but not fully adjudicated. Trials were held “between 6-7 am to avoid large variations in temperature.” [PAGE 9], so gross heat effects were minimized. Surface differed, however: “The initial level section utilised a compacted dirt road which was free of loose gravel while the other sections consisted of bitumen roads and concrete footpaths.” [PAGE 8]. The authors acknowledge that surface conditions may matter “if the surface conditions impede gait.” [PAGE 18]. Thus weather is a nuisance-control success but not a measured causal layer; no wind, humidity, solar load, or friction coefficient is reported. Cross-layer proof is strongest for uphill metabolic veto and downhill biomechanical veto; weather is a controlled background variable, not a proven speed determinant. The appropriate veto hierarchy is physical gradient first, physiological VT second on uphill, biomechanical impact second on downhill, and weather/surface as an uncontrolled but time-controlled context.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
