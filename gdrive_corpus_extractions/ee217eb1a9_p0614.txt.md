This establishes a motor-speech decoupling: the articulatory apparatus remains physically intact, yet the phonological output is suppressed or distorted by suggestion alone. Hop two extends this to sensory substitution across modalities: "You can make him eat a potato for a peach, or drink a cup of vinegar for a glass of champagne; \ ammonia will smell to him like cologne water" [PAGE 604]. Here the gustatory and olfactory proximal stimuli are invariant, while the perceptual category assigned by the subject is replaced. Hop three moves to cross-modal object substitution: "a chair will be a lion, a broom-stick a beautiful woman, a noise in the street will be an orchestral music" [PAGE 604]. The visual and auditory scenes are not physically altered; only the subject's classificatory report and subsequent behavior change. Hop four reaches identity substitution: "his personality is changed into that of a baby, of a street boy, of a young lady dressing for a party, of a stump orator, or of Napoleon the Great" [PAGE 604], extending even to "a beast, or an inanimate thing like a chair or a carpet" [PAGE 604]. This implies a temporary suspension or overwriting of the autobiographical self-model, such that the subject acts out "all the details of the part with a sincerity and intensity seldom seen at the theatre" [PAGE 604].

The gray-zone audit centers on James's anti-shamming argument. He writes that "The excellence of the performance is in these cases the best reply to the suspicion that the subject may be shamming—so skilful a shammer must long since have found his true function in life upon the stage" [PAGE 604]. This is an abductive inference to the best explanation, not a direct measurement of subjective qualia. It assumes that the behavioral repertoire required to simulate a Napoleon or a carpet is so demanding that rational malingering would prefer the stage. The breakdown regime is explicit: if a subject possesses histrionic talent and strong demand characteristics, the inference fails because simulation cost is lower than the opportunity cost of stage acting. Moreover, the public exhibition context introduces an audience effect; the "patience of the lookers on" [PAGE 604] creates a social reinforcement schedule that can maintain simulated responses without any genuine hallucinatory state.

The footnote material supplies an attempted objective anchor. James notes that "The suggested hallucination may be followed by a negative afterimage, just as if it were a real object" [PAGE 604], and cites "MM. Binet and Féré" for the finding that hallucinations "be doubled by a prism or mirror, magnified by a lens, and in many other ways to behave optically like real objects" [PAGE 604]. This is a cross-validatory strategy: if a hallucination exhibits the same optical transfer properties as a distal object, then the perceptual system is treating it as an object-like input rather than as a mere verbal report. The gray zone remains, however, because a cooperative subject could, in principle, report an afterimage or describe prism doubling without actually experiencing it. The page does not provide trial-level data, reaction times, or pupillometry that would close this identifiability gap. The derivation therefore rests on three assumptions: (1) the subject is a "good subject" with high suggestibility [PAGE 604]; (2) trance depth is sufficient to produce "Hallucinations and histrionic delusions" which "generally go with a certain depth of the trance" [PAGE 604]; and (3) there is no strategic deception. The singularity occurs when suggestibility approaches zero or when trance depth falls below a critical threshold $\tau_c$, at which point $P(\text{hallucination}) \to 0$ and the entire phenomenology collapses to compliance or waking imagination.
</THINK_TRACE>

Define the suggestion-perception operator over three input spaces and one output space. Let $\Sigma_{\text{ext}}$ denote the external stimulus space, with SI units dependent on modality (e.g., sound pressure in pascals, wavelength in nanometers). Let $\mathcal{T}$ denote the trance-depth manifold, measured in arbitrary depth units (ADU) on an ordinal behavioral scale from $0$ (waking) to $T_{\max}$ (deep somnambulism). Let $\mathcal{R}$ denote the rapport/context space, represented by a dimensionless index $\rho \in [0,1]$. The output space $\mathcal{P}$ contains categorical perceptual reports and behavioral actions. The operator is
$$
\mathcal{S} : \Sigma_{\text{ext}} \times \mathcal{T} \times \mathcal{R} \to \mathcal{P},

such that for a given physical stimulus $s \in \Sigma_{\text{ext}}$, the suggested percept and action are $p = \mathcal{S}(s, \tau, \rho)$.

$$

The optical-consistency condition from the footnote on [PAGE 604] is
$$
\forall h \in \mathcal{H}_{\text{suggested}},\quad T_h(p, m, \ell) = T_{\text{real}}(p, m, \ell),
$$
where $T$ is the optical transfer function under mirror $m$ or lens $\ell$, and $h$ denotes a suggested hallucination. The negative-afterimage consistency relation is
$$
A(h) = \mathcal{C}(h) \quad \text{after fixation interval } \Delta t \; [\text{s}],
$$
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the complementary-color operator and $\Delta t$ is measured in seconds (SI).

A phenomenological response function (not a toy ratio) is
$$
P(H \mid s, \tau, \rho) = F\bigl(\tau - \tau_c(s),\; \rho - \rho_c\bigr),
$$
where $F$ is a cumulative response function, $\tau_c(s)$ is the stimulus-specific critical trance depth, and $\rho_c$ is the critical rapport threshold. Breakdown regimes: if $\tau < \tau_c(s)$ or $\rho < \rho_c$, then $P(H) \approx 0$. Under shamming, identifiability fails because
$$
P(\text{report}=H \mid H=\emptyset,\; \text{sham}) > 0.
$$

Units: probability is dimensionless $[0,1]$; time $\Delta t$ in seconds (SI); optical angular magnification $M=\theta'/\theta$ is dimensionless; trance depth in ADU has no SI equivalent and is a behavioral ordinal scale. Assumptions: stable rapport within a session; no moment-to-moment $\rho$ drift; the subject is not simulating; optical reports are veridical descriptions of experience. Additional breakdown: audience demand characteristics on [PAGE 604] raise $P(\text{sham}) > 0$, violating identifiability; the footnote's optical claims rely on Binet and Féré rather than direct measurement on this page.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from James's *Principles of Psychology* (1890, Vol. 2) back to the French hypnotism debates of the 1880s. The page explicitly points backward with the phrase "These points have been discussed already on p. 128 ff." [PAGE 604], indicating that James treats suggestibility as a recurring theoretical problem rather than an isolated observation. The citation of "MM. Binet and Féré" [PAGE 604] anchors the text in the experimental psychology of the Nancy school (Liébault and Bernheim), which emphasized suggestion and psychological factors, in opposition to the Salpêtrière school (Charcot), which framed hypnotic phenomena as neurological degeneration. James's phrase "psychic hyper-exoitability" [PAGE 604] is a period-specific construct that attempts to bridge these camps by positing a general state of heightened susceptibility.

The multi-week traps are threefold. First, the trait-stability trap: the text assumes a "good subject" whose suggestibility is stable enough for public exhibition [PAGE 604], but across weeks suggestibility can drift due to expectancy, rapport changes, or prior-session learning. Second, the trance-depth trap: "Hallucinations and histrionic delusions generally go with a certain depth of the trance" [PAGE 604] treats depth as a stable latent variable, yet across multiple weeks trance depth may show kindling (easier induction) or habituation (reduced response). Third, the selection-bias trap: the subjects are drawn from public exhibitions where "the patience of the lookers on" [PAGE 604] imposes a theatrical selection filter; generalizing from such performers to clinical or unselected populations requires a longitudinal design that the page does not supply. A retro-causal reading suggests that James's later pragmatism was shaped by these very epistemic instabilities: the inability to verify subjective hypnosis objectively pushed him toward a functionalist account of belief and action rather than a representationalist one.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression occurs when the subject abandons the adult executive self-model and adopts a regressed habit structure prescribed by the hypnotist: baby, street boy, young lady dressing for a party, stump orator, Napoleon, beast, or inanimate object [PAGE 604]. The recursion lies in the nested theory of mind: the hypnotist models the subject as capable of adopting identity $I$; the subject models the hypnotist's model and produces behavior $B_I$ that confirms it; the hypnotist then updates confidence in $I$ based on $B_I$. This loop can reach a fixed point of mutual confirmation in which neither party has independent access to the subject's private qualia.

The counter-trap is strategic compliance (shamming). The subject may not actually regress to a baby or Napoleon; instead, the subject models the hypnotist's second-order expectation ("the subject will act like Napoleon") and deliberately performs it to satisfy demand characteristics. Because the text's anti-shamming argument rests entirely on performance excellence [PAGE 604], the counter-trap defeats the inference: excellent simulation is functionally indistinguishable from genuine regression without independent process measures.

The clock operates on two timescales. Within a session, suggestion effects may decay over seconds to minutes as attention wanes; the page notes that public exhibitions are limited by "the patience of the lookers on" [PAGE 604], imposing a hard session clock. Between sessions, across days or weeks, suggestibility may consolidate (easier induction) or extinguish (boredom, skepticism). A multi-week trap embedded here is assuming that day-one identity substitution generalizes to week four without re-induction; habit regression may require renewed suggestion, or it may become automatic, thereby altering the recursive fixed point itself.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: No ontological transformation occurs. A chair remains a chair; its mass, reflectance, and collision dynamics are invariant. Any strong claim that the chair "is" a lion in physical reality is vetoed. The weaker perceptual claim—that the subject reports and behaves as if it is a lion—remains admissible because [PAGE 604] describes the subject acting out the part, not altering the object.

Biomechanical layer: The subject can crawl, roar, stand rigidly, or mimic a carpet. The musculoskeletal system is unchanged; only the motor schema is temporarily retuned. A veto triggers if the claimed biomechanical output violates anatomy (for example, a subject claiming to be an inanimate carpet yet maintaining purposeful, weighted locomotion inconsistent with fabric physics). The source text supports only behavioral mimicry: the subject "will act out all the details of the part" [PAGE 604].

Weather/environmental layer: The source text mentions "a noise in the street" [PAGE 604] but provides no meteorological variables (temperature, barometric pressure, humidity, wind). Ambient acoustic conditions remain physical sound waves; only the semantic label changes from noise to orchestral music. Because [PAGE 604] contains no weather data, no weather-based veto can be constructed from this page alone; however, uncontrolled environmental noise is an unmodeled confound for auditory-substitution claims.

Cross-layer veto proof: If the physical layer shows invariant object properties, the biomechanical layer shows intact anatomy, and the environmental layer shows invariant acoustics, then veto the strong identity claim while retaining the weak perceptual-report claim. The source text licenses only the weak claim: the subject's report and behavior are transformed, not the distal stimulus.
