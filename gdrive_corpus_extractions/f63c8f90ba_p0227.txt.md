Over multiple weeks, if the distractor remains novel or variable, the suppression may persist rather than habituate; if the distractor becomes familiar, the orienting component of external inhibition may decay and performance may recover. The critical variable is whether the afferent input from the distractor continues to modify the impulse patterning of the original conditioned stimulus [PAGE 227].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under afferent load or contextual perturbation, the organism does not express the full 70-hab habit strength; it regresses online to an effective strength near 40 habs because each stimulus’s afferent impulses depress the other [PAGE 227]. This is not forgetting or extinction; it is an interactional gain suppression that is reversible if the interacting stimulus is removed.

Counter-trap: The experimenter’s model may assume that presenting two conditioned stimuli simultaneously yields additive or super-additive response strength. The text refutes this: physiological summation of 70 + 70 yields 91 habs (not 140), and after mutual afferent interaction the joint output is 64 habs—less than either individual component [PAGE 227]. The counter-trap is believing that compounding cues compounds behavioral control. The recursive theory of mind enters because the experimenter attributes a stable habit parameter to the organism, observes a divergence between predicted (91 habs) and actual (64 habs) output, and must then revise the model of the organism’s “mind” (its habit architecture) to include an interaction operator.

Clock: External inhibition is immediate upon stimulus substitution: the four added figures produce an abrupt drop from >95% to 90% correct [PAGE 227]. The recovery clock operates across sessions. If training trials are spaced such that the distractor remains salient at each session, the 90% plateau may persist for weeks. If the distractor becomes familiar through repeated exposure, the orienting/afferent disturbance may attenuate and effective habit strength may rebound. The clock therefore tracks the reconsolidation interval between sessions and the novelty decay of the interfering afferent pattern.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A response is expressed only if all layers pass; any single layer’s failure vetoes the response, regardless of habit strength.

Physical layer (afferent/neural): Added figures or concurrent stimuli alter afferent impulse patterning [PAGE 227]. If sensory transduction or neural volley strength falls below the threshold required to drive the central habit, the response is vetoed. The text explicitly invokes reaction evocation power sinking “below the reaction threshold” [PAGE 227]. This is a hard physical veto: no central habit strength can compensate for absent afferent drive.

Biomechanical layer (effect plant): The rat’s response is a jump [PAGE 227]. Even if central effective habit strength remains at 64 habs, the musculoskeletal system must execute the jump. Fatigue, injury, or biomechanical constraint on takeoff kinetics constitutes an independent veto. A 64-hab central command cannot produce a jump if the plant cannot generate the required force-time profile.

Weather/environmental layer: “Weather” is construed here as ambient environmental context (chamber illumination, auditory background, air currents) that contributes to the afferent field. Hull notes that external inhibition operates “on a very large scale in all the higher organisms” and is experienced as “distraction of the attention” [PAGE 227]. A sudden environmental change—analogous to a weather shift—acts as an external inhibitor if it modifies afferent impulses sufficiently to depress effective habit strength below threshold.

Adjudication rule (logical AND gate): Response R occurs iff (1) afferent input passes the sensory transduction floor, (2) H_eff > θ_thresh after all inhibitory interactions, and (3) the biomechanical plant can execute R. Habit strength in habs cannot veto-proof against a failure in any one layer. If H_eff = 64 habs but θ_thresh = 70 habs due to environmental distraction, the response is suppressed [PAGE 227].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each specifies a setup, a counterfactual manipulation, a verifiable measurement, a prediction derived from [PAGE 227], and a veto condition that would falsify the Hullian operator.

Scenario 1 — Lashley distractor replication.
Setup: Train rats on triangle-versus-cross jump discrimination to >95% correct [PAGE 227]. Counterfactual: Substitute cards with four small figures added to both stimuli [PAGE 227]. Measurement: Session-by-session percent correct. Prediction: Correct rate drops to approximately 90% on the first post-substitution session [PAGE 227]. Veto condition: If correct rate remains at baseline (>95%) across ten sessions with the added figures, the external-inhibition operator ΔH_ext is falsified for this stimulus set because the afferent impulses were not sufficiently modified to weaken effective habit strength [PAGE 227].

Scenario 2 — Simultaneous dual-stimulus competition.
Setup: Calibrate S₁ and S₂ separately so each evokes R at 70 habs [PAGE 227]. Counterfactual: Present S₁ and S₂ simultaneously. Measurement: Response amplitude (e.g., jump force or latency) for single versus compound stimuli. Prediction: Compound response is equivalent to 64 habs, strictly less than the 70-hab single-stimulus response [PAGE 227]. Veto condition: If the compound response equals or exceeds the single-stimulus response (≥70 habs), or if it matches the 91-hab physiological summation with no interaction depression, the afferent-interaction mechanism and the sub-individual response claim are falsified [PAGE 227].

Scenario 3 — Threshold crossing under strong distraction.
Setup: Establish R at 70 habs and determine the reaction threshold θ_thresh via an independent stimulus-intensity series [PAGE 227]. Counterfactual: Introduce a high-intensity distractor sufficient to drive effective habit strength toward zero. Measurement: Response probability as a function of distractor intensity. Prediction: Once H_eff < θ_thresh, response probability drops to zero; above threshold, partial suppression occurs [PAGE 227]. Veto condition: If full-amplitude response persists despite distractor intensity known to suppress afferent transmission below threshold, the reaction-threshold singularity and the complete-neutralization claim are invalid [PAGE 227].
