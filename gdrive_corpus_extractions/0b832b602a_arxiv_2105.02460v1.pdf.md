The paper’s inference chain is multi-hop and lossy at every hop. First, a 640×480 visual-camera image is reduced to 80×60; the source says “an 80x60 resolution image is generated from the original image” [PAGE 2]. This is an 8× downsampling in each spatial axis, so high-frequency iris-sclera edge information is discarded before the iris is found. Second, an isodata threshold produces a segmented image; the eyebrow and eye are selected as “two largest regions” and “the eye area is the region below the eyebrow” [PAGE 2]. That rule assumes the eyebrow is visible, correctly segmented, and above the eye. Third, a horizontal scanning window of height equal to the eye region and width “0.15 times the eye region width” finds the iris by maximum grey-scale sum [PAGE 2]. This assumes the iris is the darkest or brightest compact region under the current threshold; it is not a semantic iris detector. Fourth, sample points are found by zigzag scanning at the iris-sclera border [PAGE 2], then fitted by a double-circle procedure. The geometric objective is Eq. (1), but the implemented fit is algebraic Eq. (2) because “we choose algebraic fit because of its high performance” [PAGE 2–3]. Algebraic fitting is fast but not identical to geometric distance minimization; it can be biased by noise and can produce invalid radius estimates. The double fit removes points “far from the center” as noise [PAGE 3], but that can also remove valid boundary points when occlusion is severe, making the next fit rank-deficient. Fifth, eye-corner detection uses the variance projection function Eq. (4)–(5) and a Sobel eyelid edge [PAGE 3]. This assumes a high-contrast corner and a detectable eyelid; spectacles, hair, shadow, or make-up can violate it. That is a geometric similarity assumption, not a measured per-user calibration. Seventh, a two-point linear calibration inserts k in Eq. This reduces systematic bias only if the calibration geometry is representative and the head/eye pose is stable.

**Source operators.** The pipeline operators are: (i) spatial downsample \(640\times480 \to 80\times60\) [PAGE 2]; (ii) isodata threshold and connected-region segmentation [PAGE 2]; (iii) eyebrow/eye region selection by “two largest regions” and eye-below-eyebrow rule [PAGE 2]; (iv) horizontal maximum-sum iris scan with window height \(h_{\text{eye}}\) and width \(0.15\,w_{\text{eye}}\) [PAGE 2]; (v) zigzag iris-sclera sampling [PAGE 2]; (vi) double circle fit [PAGE 2–3]; (vii) vertical variance projection function (VPF) plus Sobel eyelid edge for eye corner [PAGE 3]; (viii) eye-ball model and two-point linear calibration [PAGE 3–4].

**Circle fitting.** With sample points \((x_i,y_i)\), \(1\le i\le n\), the geometric objective is
\[
F(a,b,R)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{(x_i-a)^2+(y_i-b)^2}-R\right)^2
\tag{1}
\]
where \(a,b,R\) are in pixel units in the image coordinate system; \(F\) has units of \(\text{pixel}^2\). The algebraic fit replaces the geometric distance by
\[
F(a,b,R)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(z_i+Bx_i+Cy_i+D\right)^2,
\quad z_i=x_i^2+y_i^2,\quad B=-2a,\quad C=-2b,\quad D=a^2+b^2-R^2
\tag{2}
\]
with \(z_i,Bx_i,Cy_i,D\) in \(\text{pixel}^2\). Differentiating gives the moment system
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
M_{xx}&M_{xy}&M_x\\
M_{xy}&M_{yy}&M_y\\
M_x&M_y&n
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}B\\C\\D\end{bmatrix}
=
-\begin{bmatrix}M_{xz}\\M_{yz}\\M_z\end{bmatrix},
\tag{3}
\]
where \(M_{xx}=\sum x_i^2\), \(M_{xy}=\sum x_i y_i\), etc. [PAGE 3]. The paper solves this “by Cholesky decomposition” [PAGE 3]. **Breakdown:** Eq. (3) is singular if \(n<3\), if the sample points are collinear, or if the moment matrix is not positive definite; Cholesky then fails or yields an unstable center. After solving, \(R^2=a^2+b^2-D\); if \(R^2<0\), the algebraic circle has imaginary radius and must be rejected. The double fit removes “sample points which are far from the center” [PAGE 3], but if too many points are removed, \(n<3\) or rank deficiency recurs. The paper’s own failure mode is “insufficient number of sample points” when “a large part of the iris is hidden” [PAGE 5].

**Eye-corner VPF.** For intensity \(I(x,y)\) and vertical interval \([y_1,y_2]\),
\[
\sigma_v^2(x)=\frac{1}{y_2-y_1}\sum_{y_i=y_1}^{y_2}\left[I(x,y_i)-V_m(x)\right]^2
\tag{4}
\]
\[
V_m(x)=\frac{1}{y_2-y_1}\int_{y_1}^{y_2}I(x,y)\,dy
\tag{5}
\]
where \(I\) is grey-scale intensity (dimensionless in the paper; not specified as photometric units), \(y\) is pixel coordinate, and \(\sigma_v^2\) has intensity-squared units [PAGE 3]. **Breakdown:** \(y_2=y_1\) makes Eqs. (4)–(5) undefined; a uniform vertical intensity gives \(\sigma_v^2(x)=0\) and no corner peak; if the Sobel eyelid edge and VPF vertical position do not intersect, the corner is undefined. The paper uses the derivative of VPF and “a Sobel edge detector ... to detect the eyelid” [PAGE 3].

**Eye-ball gaze mapping.** The source’s OCR of Eqs. (6)–(9) is bracket-corrupted, but the geometry-consistent reconstruction is a similarity projection with factor \((r_{\text{ball}}+d)/r_{\text{ball}}\):
\[
g_1=\frac{r_{\text{ball}}+d}{r_{\text{ball}}}\,\Delta_1,\qquad
g_2=\frac{r_{\text{ball}}+d}{r_{\text{ball}}}\,\Delta_2
\tag{6–7}
\]
\[
g=\frac{r_{\text{ball}}+d}{r_{\text{ball}}}\left(\Delta_2-\Delta_1+\Delta_{\text{ref}}\right)
\tag{8}
\]
\[
g=k\,\frac{r_{\text{ball}}+d}{r_{\text{ball}}}\left(\Delta_2-\Delta_1+\Delta_{\text{ref}}\right)
\tag{9}
\]
Here \(r_{\text{ball}}\) is “12mm to 13mm” [PAGE 3], \(d\) is distance from eyeball surface to screen plane, \(g,\Delta_i,\Delta_{\text{ref}}\) are screen/projection displacements, and \(k\) is a dimensionless linear calibration factor. The paper states “If \(g\) is the distance between two screen points” [PAGE 4] and “\(d\) is assumed given or can be intentionally set to fixed value” [PAGE 3]. **Breakdown:** \(r_{\text{ball}}=0\) is singular; unknown or changing \(d\) biases the similarity factor; if the two calibration points coincide, \(k\) is underdetermined; if \(\Delta_{\text{ref}}\) is contaminated by eyelid/occlusion, head-motion compensation injects error.

**Angular conversion and units.** The source’s own conversion is “50mm on the screen equals a view angle of 4.40 degrees” at 650 mm [PAGE 4–5]. The formal relation is
\[
\theta(s,d)=2\arctan\!\left(\frac{s}{2d}\right)\ \text{rad},
\qquad
\theta_{\deg}=\frac{180}{\pi}\,\theta(s,d),
\]
with \(s\) in mm and \(d\) in mm. For small angles, \(\theta\approx s/d\) rad. This yields the reported averages: \(28\) mm at 650 mm \(\approx 2.47^\circ\) (reported “2.50 degrees”), \(35\) mm \(\approx 3.08^\circ\) (reported “3.07 degrees”), and \(21\) mm \(\approx 1.85^\circ\) [PAGE 5]. No probability distribution, entropy, or bit/nat uncertainty is reported; the calibration is deterministic least squares. The only information-rate statement is the computational one: camera sampling \(30\) fps, algorithm repeated \(10\) times per frame, “maximum sampling rate ... 300Hz” [PAGE 5]; this is not 300 independent frames/s, because repeated deterministic computation on one frame adds no new camera information.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits in a lineage that runs from physiological eye-movement recording to visual-camera game interaction. The introduction lists “electro-oculography (EOG), the photoelectric method, and video-oculography (VOG)” [PAGE 1] and criticizes EOG/photoelectric for “unreliably vertical eye gaze measurements, low sensitivity and limited bandwidth” [PAGE 1]. It then positions VOG as attractive because of “high accuracy, insignificant artifacts and wide applicability” [PAGE 1], while rejecting near-infrared illumination because “the use of infra-red light source may result in potential eye hazards such as eye dryness, lens burning, and retina thermal injury” [PAGE 1]. That safety argument is the retrocausal hinge: later visual-camera work can be read as an attempt to escape IR hazards, but it inherits visible-light problems—ambient illumination, shadows, specular reflections, and lower iris-sclera contrast. The game lineage is also explicit: Sony EyeToy in 2003, Nintendo Wiimote in 2005, Microsoft Kinect, then Jonsson’s eye-vs-mouse comparison, Smith and Graham’s immersion study, and Kenny et al.’s first-person-shooter eye-data study [PAGE 1]. The paper’s claim that gaze is “a fast and natural input method” [PAGE 1] is therefore genealogically dependent on prior game-HCI findings, not independently established here.

The algorithmic genealogy is equally layered. The eye-corner detector uses the “variance projection function (VPF)” from Feng and Yuen [PAGE 3, PAGE 6]; the threshold uses the isodata method of Ridler and Calvard [PAGE 2, PAGE 6]; the circle fit is tied to Phung et al. [PAGE 6]; the eye-ball model and marker-free head-displacement idea are compared with Kim and Ramakrishna [PAGE 1, PAGE 6] and Matsumoto and Zelinsky’s stereo-camera iris-contour method [PAGE 1, PAGE 6]. The paper’s novelty is not any single operator but the composition: visual camera + double-circle iris center + VPF eye corner + eye-ball model + two-point calibration, with no head-mounted attachment. The source explicitly rejects the marker approach: “any additional attachments is resulted in user discomfort and should be avoided” [PAGE 1]. That rejection is a design genealogy: it forbids the very reference markers that would make head-pose estimation easier.

Multi-week traps follow from this lineage. First, calibration drift: Eq. (9) uses a linear factor \(k\), but the paper does not model changes in posture, eyeglass position, hair, or camera focus over weeks. Second, distance drift: \(d\) is “assumed given” [PAGE 3], and automatic distance determination is future work [PAGE 5]; a fixed \(d\) becomes a systematic angular bias whenever the user leans in or out. Third, illumination drift: the isodata threshold is dynamic, but the paper reports no outdoor or weather trial; a threshold that adapts indoors may adapt to the wrong region under sunlight or glare. Fourth, occlusion drift: hair covering the eyebrow causes “wrong detection of eye area” [PAGE 5], and a large hidden iris causes “insufficient number of sample points” [PAGE 5]. These are not one-time calibration errors; they are recurring environmental/biomechanical states. Fifth, evaluation genealogy: nine subjects and seven points support a prototype claim, not a longitudinal usability claim. The later game “Hammer Heads” and the survey machine with one-point calibration [PAGE 5] retroactively justify the low-calibration design, but they also show that the application constrains the calibration burden rather than the other way around.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system’s interaction loop is recursive: the player sees a cursor/monster response, infers whether the machine detected the gaze, and adjusts; the machine infers the player’s intent from iris center and eye corner. Under crisis—repeated missed detections—the likely habit regression is low-level motor behavior: head movement, leaning, blinking, squinting, or re-fixating the screen center. The paper gives evidence for this class of failure: “players’ hair covered the eyebrow result in wrong detection of eye area” and “players had to try again” [PAGE 5]. A head-motion habit is especially dangerous because the eye-corner reference \(\Delta_{\text{ref}}\) is supposed to offset head displacement [PAGE 3–4], but that compensation is geometric and assumes the corner is detected. If the user responds to failure by moving the head more, the compensation term and the iris projection both change, so the user’s “fix” can amplify the error.

The counter-trap is the designer’s response: add calibration points, add markers, add IR illumination, or constrain the head. The paper explicitly rejects attachments because they cause “user discomfort” [PAGE 1] and rejects IR because of “potential eye hazards” [PAGE 1]. Yet the paper also adds “one more calibration point” to determine \(k\) [PAGE 4]. That is a trade: more calibration reduces systematic error but increases setup burden and creates a new failure point if the user’s calibration pose differs from play pose. The survey machine reduces calibration to “one point located at the center of screen” [PAGE 5], showing that the counter-trap can be relaxed when the task has only two answer options. The recursive design lesson is that calibration complexity must match task entropy: a two-button task can tolerate one point, while a continuous game needs more, but neither fixes hidden-iris failure.

The clock layer is explicit and asymmetric. The camera samples at 30 fps [PAGE 4], the algorithm can be repeated ten times per frame for a “300Hz” computational rate [PAGE 5], but human gaze and game events do not become 300 Hz independent observations. The survey machine starts when the user “gazes at questioner for 3 seconds” [PAGE 5], a dwell-time clock; the game uses sound-guided calibration and shooting events [PAGE 4–5]. Under time pressure, users may shorten dwell, make larger saccades, or move the head, which stresses the eye-ball model and the corner detector. A counter-trap would be to speed up the game clock or add more calibration prompts; that may increase throughput but also increase the probability of occlusion and missegmentation. The source does not measure stress or cognitive load, so this section is a design-level counterfactual, not a claimed psychological measurement.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical/optical veto.** The paper chooses a visual camera partly to avoid IR hazards: “the use of infra-red light source may result in potential eye hazards such as eye dryness, lens burning, and retina thermal injury” [PAGE 1]. That is a valid physical-safety veto against IR-based claims for this design, but it does not prove visible-light robustness. Visible-light iris-sclera contrast depends on illumination, shadow, and specular reflection; the only photometric adaptation described is dynamic isodata thresholding and recalculation after iris-region refinement [PAGE 2]. There is no reported photometric calibration, no lux measurement, and no spectral measurement. Therefore any claim that the system is illumination-invariant is vetoed by absence of evidence. The same applies to eyeglasses: three subjects wore them [PAGE 4], but no separate eyeglass error table is given, so a claim of eyeglass-invariance is not source-supported.

**Biomechanical veto.** The vertical error is larger than horizontal, and the paper explains: “This result matches the fact that eyelids cover upper and lower parts of our eyes” [PAGE 5]. This is a biomechanical veto on vertical accuracy: the eyelid occludes the iris-sclera border, reducing the sample set for Eq. (3). The explicit failure mode is “a large part of the iris is hidden which resulted in insufficient number of sample points” [PAGE 5]. Formally, if the visible sample set has \(n<3\) or the moment matrix in Eq. (3) has rank \(<3\), the circle center is not identifiable; if the double-fit removes too many outliers, the same singularity recurs. Hair is a second biomechanical/segmentation veto: “players’ hair covered the eyebrow result in wrong detection of eye area” [PAGE 5]. Because the eye area is defined as the region below the eyebrow [PAGE 2], hair that merges with or covers the eyebrow corrupts the region-selection rule before iris detection even starts. A claim of robust performance for users with heavy bangs, deep-set eyes, or ptosis is vetoed.

**Weather/environment veto.** The source contains no outdoor trial, no weather variable, no rain/fog/direct-sun measurement, and no temperature/humidity record. The evaluation is a lab setup with an additional display, sound player, DSP board, and camera [PAGE 4–5]. Therefore no weather-conditioned accuracy claim can be verified. This is a proof of absence of evidence: if a claim says “works in sunlight” or “unaffected by weather,” the 6-page source provides no supporting variable. The nearest source statement is that Matsumoto’s stereo method “works well even in daylight conditions” [PAGE 1], but that is cited prior work, not this system’s test. The correct adjudication is a veto on weather generalization, not a claim that the system fails in all weather.

**Geometry/calibration veto.** Eq. (8)–(9) require \(d\), \(r_{\text{ball}}\), and \(\Delta_{\text{ref}}\). The paper says \(d\) “is assumed given or can be intentionally set to fixed value” [PAGE 3] and calls automatic distance determination future work [PAGE 5]. If \(d\) changes while the calibration uses a fixed value, the angular error scales approximately as \(\theta\approx s/d\); the same millimetre residual subtends a larger angle at shorter distance. A claim of distance-invariant accuracy is therefore vetoed. The two-point calibration can absorb a scalar \(k\), but it cannot absorb a depth-dependent nonlinearity or a head-pose change that violates the eye-corner reference assumption.

**Scenario 1 — Iris-occlusion rank veto.** Counterfactual: synthetically occlude the iris so that the zigzag sampler returns either \(n<3\) visible iris-sclera points or a collinear set, making the Eq. (3) moment matrix rank-deficient. Verifiable reward: the detector must output a finite center \((a,b)\) and positive \(R^2\); the check fails if Cholesky fails, if rank \(<3\), or if the double fit leaves \(n<3\). Source prediction: the paper says “a large part of the iris is hidden which resulted in insufficient number of sample points” and that “it is difficult to detect iris centers in these situations” [PAGE 5]. Prediction: the system should fail or return a high-variance center; any accuracy claim under this occlusion is vetoed.

**Scenario 2 — Hair/eyebrow segmentation veto.** Counterfactual: overlay hair across the eyebrow so that the two largest segmented regions no longer correspond to eyebrow-above-eye. Verifiable reward: correct eye-region mask and iris center within the calibrated tolerance. Source prediction: “players’ hair covered the eyebrow result in wrong detection of eye area which was not focused by our system” [PAGE 5]. Because the eye area is selected as the region below the eyebrow [PAGE 2], the wrong mask propagates to the iris scan and then to the circle fit. Prediction: the detector selects the wrong region or fails; the game demonstration should require retry, exactly as the source reports.

**Scenario 3 — Distance/calibration drift veto.** Counterfactual: calibrate at \(d=650\) mm as in the paper’s example [PAGE 4–5], then test at \(d=500\) mm and \(d=800\) mm without recalibration. Verifiable reward: measured angular error versus the geometric prediction \(\theta(s,d)=2\arctan(s/(2d))\). Using the paper’s horizontal average residual \(s=28\) mm, the predicted angle is about \(3.21^\circ\) at 500 mm and \(2.00^\circ\) at 800 mm, versus about \(2.47^\circ\) at 650 mm. Using the vertical average \(s=35\) mm gives about \(4.01^\circ\), \(3.08^\circ\), and \(2.51^\circ\), respectively. Source prediction: because \(d\) is “assumed given” [PAGE 3] and automatic distance measurement is future work [PAGE 5], the fixed-\(d\) model should show distance-dependent bias. Prediction: the reward should drop when actual distance departs from calibration distance; a claim of distance-invariant accuracy is vetoed.
