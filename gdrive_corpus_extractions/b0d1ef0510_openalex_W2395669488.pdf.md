\[
\frac{\partial \Pr(y_i=1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i)}{\partial x_{ij}} = \phi(\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})\,\beta_j
\]
where \(\phi\) is the standard normal PDF. Effects are evaluated at sample means; dummy variables are interpreted as discrete changes from 0 to 1.

Standardized marginal effect used in Table 5 [PAGE 41]:
\[
\text{SME}_j = \sigma_j \times \phi(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})\,\beta_j
\]
with \(\sigma_j\) the sample standard deviation of covariate \(j\). Units: probability is dimensionless on \([0,1]\); yardage in yards; time of possession in minutes; completion and pass-play percentages in percentage points; counts (sacks, interceptions, fumble recoveries, first downs, fourth-down attempts, two-point attempts) are dimensionless integers.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The thesis sits inside a multi-decade lineage of NFL quantitative inquiry. Sports analytics entered mainstream professional sport through sabermetrics in the early 1990s MLB [PAGE 9], citing Lewis (2003). NFL adoption lagged baseball [PAGE 9]. Early NFL work used market-implied predictors: Stern (1991) evaluated a point-spread-based winner prediction at roughly 66% accuracy [PAGE 9]. Robst et al. (2011) studied postseason win probability from regular-season offense and defense, finding both sides of the ball statistically similar and identifying home-field advantage [PAGE 9–10]. Hadley et al. (2000) estimated a production frontier for wins and found coaching efficiency worth three or four wins per season [PAGE 10]. Hofler and Payne (1996) used a stochastic production frontier and found offenses scored within one point of predicted potential, implying effective talent utilization [PAGE 10]. Atkinson, Stanley, and Tschirhart (1988) confirmed that offensive and defensive yardage, turnovers, and sacks predict regular-season outcomes [PAGE 10–11].

The direct methodological ancestor is Arkes (2011), who used only first-half statistics to avoid second-half strategic contamination, finding first-half passing advantage significant but first-half rushing not, full-game rushing significant, penalties insignificant, turnovers and sacks negative, and time of possession insignificant [PAGE 11–12]. Pitts (2016) examined postseason experience (1966–2012), finding no overall veteran effect but a quarterback rookie penalty of about 14% lower win probability, plus passing offense as the key postseason determinant [PAGE 12–13]. McGee et al. (2012) addressed salary-cap allocation between offense and defense [PAGE 13]. Burton and Dickey (2015) predicted run/pass play calls with 75% accuracy using 2011–2014 play-by-play [PAGE 13–14].

Alleva (2016) extends Arkes by splitting the sample on halftime lead and estimating separate second-half driver models [PAGE 8, 24]. Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy include: (1) the “hot streak” fallacy—Pitts found teams on late-season hot streaks do not outperform losing teams in the postseason [PAGE 12]; (2) the rookie-quarterback trap—rookie QBs win only about 36% of games [PAGE 15], and qbrookie is only borderline significant (10%) in Alleva’s lead subsample [PAGE 28–29]; (3) the volume-pass trap—trailing teams throw on 65% of second-half plays versus 58% in the first half, so pass volume is negatively correlated with winning because all trailing teams pass, not only successful ones [PAGE 29]; (4) the time-of-possession trap—Arkes found it insignificant in a single-season postseason sample [PAGE 12], yet Alleva finds it significant only in the second half, with an asymmetric 0.7% vs 1.7% per-minute effect [PAGE 27–28]. Each trap arises from conditioning on endogenous game state and from using raw volume statistics without efficiency context.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes what trailing teams do under scoreboard pressure. From Table 6 [PAGE 42], trailing teams attempt fourth-down conversions on 58% of second-half fourth-down situations versus 19% for leading teams; trailing teams attempt two-point conversions at a mean of 0.19 per game versus 0.06 for leaders [PAGE 42–43]; and trailing teams raise second-half pass-play percentage from 58% to 65% while leaders drop from 55% to 46% [PAGE 14]. These are not optimized strategies in equilibrium; they are crisis habits—pass more, go for it on fourth, chase two points—adopted because the state variable “behind at half” mechanically forces risk-taking. Because every trailing team exhibits these habits, the habits lose marginal predictive power: percentpassplay2 carries a negative coefficient (-0.8% trailing, -1.17% leading) [PAGE 29], and go4th2 is negative for both (-6.9% and -5.6%) [PAGE 39–40].

The counter-trap is the leading team’s delay strategy. Leading teams run more in the second half to consume clock, increasing time of possession (16.2 vs 14.9 minutes mean for trailers [PAGE 42–43]). This is rational only if the trailing defense does not over-predict the run. If the leading team’s run-heavy script is fully anticipated, the defensive adjustment nullifies the clock benefit; the model’s negative pass-play coefficient for leaders partly reflects interception risk under predictable passing, but it does not model the defensive best response.

The clock layer introduces asymmetry. A trailing team values second-half possession at 1.7% win-probability per minute versus 0.7% for a leading team [PAGE 27–28]. The clock is therefore not a symmetric resource; it is a strategic weapon for the trailer and a liability for the leader. Recursive theory of mind enters because each coaching staff models the other’s halftime adjustment: the leader expects the trailer to pass and hurry; the trailer expects the leader to run and drain clock. The probit estimates reduced-form associations of these behaviors with victory but does not solve the fixed-point game in which each side’s optimal play-call depends on its belief about the other’s belief. The negative fd2 coefficient for trailers (-0.8%) [PAGE 30–31] may reflect exactly this recursion: trailers earn first downs through crisis passing but fail to convert them into points because the leading defense anticipates the desperation script.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The source contains no weather, temperature, wind, or precipitation variables [PAGE 13 omission list]. Wind mechanically suppresses passing yards (py), completion percentage (comp), and pass-play share (percentpassplay), independent of strategic intent. Because the probit conditions only on observables in Table 1 [PAGE 40–41], any game played in high wind will generate biased py2 and comp2 coefficients. Veto: inference about passing strategy is vetoed unless weather is stratified; in high-wind regimes, the positive py2 coefficient (0.11% per yard [PAGE 28]) should attenuate toward zero.

Biomechanical layer: No player-snap count, fatigue, or injury variable exists [PAGE 13]. The author acknowledges fatigue as a possible explanation for changing predictor importance across halves—“players getting tired from being involved in too many plays in the first half” [PAGE 18]—but does not model it. Time of possession (top2) accumulates snaps; without fatigue proxies, the 0.7%/1.7% per-minute coefficients conflate possession value with fatigue-induced decay. Veto: top2 effects are physically underspecified; in late-season or short-rest games, fatigue should dominate the possession coefficient.

Biomechanical/structural layer: Red-zone efficiency was unavailable [PAGE 12–13]. A team can pile up first downs (fd2) and passing yards without scoring. The negative fd2 coefficient for trailing teams (-0.8%) [PAGE 30–31] is veto-proof only as a correlation; as a causal claim that first downs harm trailing teams, it is vetoed until red-zone touchdown rate is included. Similarly, startfieldpos2 is significant at 0.3% per yard [PAGE 27], but without drive-start context after turnovers, the coefficient confuses special-teams quality with field-position luck.

Injury layer: Starting-quarterback injury in the second half is not observed. A backup entering mid-game will collapse py2 and comp2, yet the model treats these as chosen strategy variables [PAGE 11 endogeneity discussion]. Veto: any win-probability prediction conditional on py2/comp2 is invalidated for games with mid-game quarterback injury.

Weather/biomechanical joint veto: In cold, windy conditions, trailing teams will still pass more (crisis habit), but wind lowers completion probability. The model’s comp2 coefficient for trailers (0.33% per 1% [PAGE 28–29]) should become statistically indistinguishable from zero in high-wind subsets. If it does not, omitted-variable bias is confirmed.

All variables are expressed in SI-compatible or information units: probability dimensionless \([0,1]\); yardage in yards; time in minutes; percentages as dimensionless ratios scaled by 100; counts as dimensionless integers. No betting-market variables (spreads, totals, moneylines, odds) appear in the estimating equation or the marginal-effect table.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1 — High-wind passing veto.
Setup: Identify all 2000–2016 team-games with second-half wind speed above 15 mph (verifiable via NFL weather archives merged to game logs). Prediction: The standard marginal effect of comp2 for trailing teams should fall from 4.2% [PAGE 41, Table 5] to insignificance because wind mechanically depresses completion percentage independent of quarterback decision-making. If comp2 remains significant at α = 0.05 with magnitude > 2.0% in the high-wind subset, the model is vetoed for omitted weather confounding.

Scenario 2 — Red-zone efficiency veto.
Setup: Stratify trailing-team observations by second-half red-zone touchdown rate (above vs. below league median; red-zone data noted as missing on [PAGE 12–13], so this requires external play-by-play). Prediction: The fd2 coefficient for trailing teams is -0.00762 overall [PAGE 39]; among high red-zone-efficiency trailers, fd2 should switch sign or become insignificant because first downs now convert to points. If fd2 remains negative and significant even in the high-efficiency stratum, the negative coefficient is a data artifact and the model is vetoed for that variable.

Scenario 3 — Quarterback-injury veto.
Setup: Identify team-games where the starting quarterback exited due to injury in the second half (verifiable via injury reports and snap counts). Prediction: The model assigns win probability based on observed py2/comp2, whose standardized marginal effects are 7.1% (trailing) and 5.1% (leading) [PAGE 41, Table 5]. Post-injury, py2 and comp2 will collapse but may still yield a win if the defense dominates. The model’s out-of-sample prediction error for these games should exceed two standard errors of the probit forecast; if prediction error remains within one standard error, the coefficients are robust to quarterback disruption. Failure of this bound vetoes the ex-post treatment of offensive statistics as strategy variables.
