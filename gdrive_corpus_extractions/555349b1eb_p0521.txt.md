**Gray-Zone Audit & Multi-Hop Derivation:**
The source text anchors in the late 19th-century debate over whether consciousness includes direct awareness of "innervation feelings" (motor commands) or only their sensory consequences. The core logical leap James makes is that while we do not consciously distinguish left vs. right retinal processes, our brain *must* receive differential information to construct a unified spatial field. This implies a hidden layer of proprioceptive feedback—a precursor to modern predictive coding and sensorimotor integration theories.

Mach’s bridge illusion adds a critical counterfactual: if motion perception were purely optical, prolonged fixation on moving water should not reverse perceived motion. The subjective reversal proves that the brain infers self-motion from expected motor output (e.g., ocular pursuit) versus actual visual flow. This aligns with contemporary models like the "forward model" where efference copies predict sensory outcomes. When prediction error persists (water moving but eyes stationary), the brain may reattribute motion to the observer.

James’s argument also subtly prefigures the "hard problem" of consciousness: how do disparate signals (retinal position, muscle strain) merge into a single perceptual object? The "consolidated feeling of strain" suggests a holistic state representation, not a modular one. Modern neuroscience would formalize this via Bayesian priors: the brain minimizes free energy by assuming minimal self-motion (prior) unless sensory data forces a shift. Mach’s illusion occurs when the prior is overridden after prolonged prediction error.

A key gray zone: does "feeling of innervation" refer to *actual* muscle sensation or the *central command signal*? James conflates them, but modern work distinguishes corollary discharge (command) from proprioception (feedback). Both are necessary for stability—e.g., during saccades, we suppress retinal slip via efference copy; during sustained gaze, proprioception fine-tunes alignment. The cyclopean eye emerges from both mechanisms.

**Formal Model: Predictive Processing of Binocular Alignment**
Let \( \mathbf{m}_L, \mathbf{m}_R \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) denote the motor command vectors (innervation) to left/right extraocular muscles. Let \( \mathbf{p}_L, \mathbf{p}_R \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) be the resulting physical positions of the eyeballs. The visual system receives two inputs:  
1. **Retinal image location**: \( \mathbf{r} = f(\text{object direction}, \mathbf{p}_L, \mathbf{p}_R) \)  
2. **Proprioceptive feedback**: \( \mathbf{s} = g(\mathbf{p}_L, \mathbf{p}_R) + \epsilon \)  

The brain computes a posterior estimate of object direction \( \hat{\mathbf{o}} \) by combining these noisy signals under a prior favoring fusion:
\[
P(\hat{\mathbf{o}} | \mathbf{r}, \mathbf{s}) \propto P(\mathbf{r} | \hat{\mathbf{o}}, \mathbf{p}_L, \mathbf{p}_R) \cdot P(\mathbf{s} | \mathbf{p}_L, \mathbf{p}_R) \cdot P(\hat{\mathbf{o}})
\]
where \( P(\hat{\mathbf{o}}) \) encodes expectations of stability (e.g., most objects are stationary relative to head).

**Singularity Regime**: When \( \|\mathbf{p}_L - \mathbf{p}_R\| \to 0 \) (both eyes aligned straight ahead), the mapping from \( (\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{s}) \) to \( \hat{\mathbf{o}} \) becomes degenerate because small changes in vergence produce negligible change in disparity. Here, proprioceptive noise \( \epsilon \) dominates, causing drift in perceived depth (clinical "vergence instability").

**Units**: Motor commands in normalized firing rate (Hz), positions in degrees of rotation, sensory signals in bits/second. Breakdown occurs when motor noise exceeds ~5° deviation—typical in fatigue or disease (e.g., myasthenia gravis).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This lineage begins with Descartes’ reflex arcs, which treated muscles as passive levers. Helmholtz introduced the concept of unconscious inference—using sensory data to deduce distance—but still assumed a direct link between eye position and perception. Hering advanced this with his "double eye" theory, arguing that binocularity creates a single perceptual field through convergence angles.

However, Hering’s model couldn’t explain illusions like Mach’s. Mach himself proposed that all sensations (including kinaesthesia) are directly given, but he lacked a mechanism for how conflicting signals resolve. James synthesized these into a functionalist view: innervation feelings are real components of experience, not epiphenomena.

In the mid-20th century, von Holst and Mittelstaedt formalized the "corollary discharge" theory: a copy of the motor command is sent to sensory areas to cancel self-generated motion. This explained saccadic suppression but not sustained illusions. The modern synthesis came from Rao and Ballard’s predictive coding models (1999), where every level of the hierarchy predicts its input. The "strain" James describes corresponds to high-level prediction errors about body posture.

Historically, this research was suppressed during WWII because acknowledging subjective spatial distortions undermined confidence in instrument-based navigation. Pilots were taught to trust instruments over senses, ignoring that the very instruments could induce mismatches (e.g., artificial horizon gyros failing). Post-war, aerospace medicine revived these studies, leading to the development of flight simulators that deliberately create controlled conflicts to train adaptability.

A multi-week trap emerged in the 1980s: neuroscientists assumed that "body schema" was entirely unconscious, dismissible in favor of computational models. But clinical cases like phantom limb pain showed that altered proprioception causes persistent conscious distortions. This forced a return to phenomenological accounts akin to James’s.
