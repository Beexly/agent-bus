This is a crucial multi-hop constraint. If a model tries to infer persistent fatigue only from Glasgow Coma Scale, loss of consciousness, or computed tomography findings, the source itself warns that the relationship is weak or inconsistent. The same page also says that the fatigue is linked to concentration, attention, and mental load, which shifts the inference layer from structural lesion severity to cognitive-effort regulation.

A second multi-hop chain runs from mental fatigue to objective cognitive testing. The source repeatedly ties subjective mental fatigue to processing speed and attention. It says that “processing speed was found to be the primary predictor for mental fatigue” [PAGE 19] and that self-reported mental fatigue is “primarily linked to processing speed and attention” [PAGE 23]. This creates a formal inference problem: subjective fatigue is not a direct readout of lesion size; it is more plausibly a latent-state marker whose observed indicators include slow processing, attentional errors, extended recovery, and diurnal variation. The source also gives a concrete instrument: the Mental Fatigue Scale, adapted from earlier work, with item responses from 0 to 3 and half-point intermediates [PAGE 21], [PAGE 22]. The source also warns that acute-phase hormone changes may mimic hypopituitarism because of non-pituitary critical illness [PAGE 65]. It then states that pituitary testing in all patients would be infeasible: “To perform pituitary testing of all TBI patients would be an impossible task both logistically and financially” [PAGE 64]. Thus the inference problem is not merely “does deficiency exist?” but “which patients, when, with which test, and with what confirmatory rule?”

The rehabilitation chapters add another layer: functioning is not identical to impairment. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health model treats functioning as an interaction among health condition, body functions and structures, activities and participation, environmental factors, and personal factors [PAGE 88]. The source explains that “Performance is understood as what an individual actually does in his or her current environment” [PAGE 90]. This creates a counterfactual discipline: a patient may have impaired capacity but good performance because of environmental facilitation, or good capacity but poor performance because of environmental barriers. Therefore, any model that collapses capacity and performance into one number is incomplete.

The cognitive recovery chapter provides the deepest theoretical gray zone: localization versus recovery. The source’s REF model distinguishes truly localized elementary functions from distributed algorithmic strategies [PAGE 138]. The apparent contradiction between localized loss and behavioral recovery is resolved by saying that the same surface behavior can be mediated by different strategies after injury. This matters because a clinician may see “recovery” on a test while the underlying strategy has changed. The source warns that training may become situationally dependent and fail to generalize [PAGE 143]. That is a major trap: a patient may look recovered in the clinic but fail in daily life.

The final gray-zone audit concerns technology-based mental health care. The source reports that patients with traumatic brain injury are often willing to use technology, but they still prefer in-person contact and are cautious about fully substituting technology for therapy [PAGE 53], [PAGE 55]. The recursive risk is that a program optimized for access may inadvertently reduce safety monitoring for severely depressed or suicidal patients [PAGE 55]. Thus the source does not support a maximalist claim that technology replaces clinical care; it supports a hybrid model where technology extends access while preserving human oversight.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source contains no explicit printed equations, so the following operators are formal restatements of source-defined variables. They are not new empirical claims. All probabilities are dimensionless. Information units are bits when logarithms are base 2 and nats when logarithms are natural. Hormone concentrations use the source’s units: growth hormone in µg/L and cortisol in nmol/L. Instrument scores are ordinal clinical points, not SI physical units.

1. Mental Fatigue Scale as an ordinal latent-state indicator.

The source describes the Mental Fatigue Scale as having items scored from 0 to 3, with half-point intermediates allowed. A representative item states that a score of 0 means “I can manage in the same way as usual” while 3 means the person becomes fatigued so quickly that they must abandon everything after about five minutes [PAGE 22]. Let item \(i\) have response \(m_i\). For the 14 scored items used in the reliability analysis, define:

\[
m_i \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3\}, \quad i=1,\dots,14
\]

\[
S_{\text{MF}} = \sum_{i=1}^{14} m_i
\]

Assumption: \(S_{\text{MF}}\) is an ordinal composite, not an interval physical quantity. Units: clinical points. The source reports Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of 0.944 for the 14 questions [PAGE 21]. Standard reliability form:

\[
\alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_S^2}\right)
\]

where \(k=14\), \(\sigma_i^2\) is item variance, and \(\sigma_S^2\) is composite variance. Units: dimensionless. Breakdown regime: if item variance is zero, or if the scale is interpreted as a ratio scale, the operator is invalid. The source does not license multiplication of fatigue by time to create a physical energy variable.

2. Diagnostic inference for hypopituitarism.

Let \(D \in \{0,1\}\) denote absence or presence of a specific pituitary deficiency. Let \(X\) be a hormone measurement after stimulation. Let \(\tau\) be a cut-point. The source gives examples such as insulin tolerance test growth hormone cut-point “peak GH < 3 ug/l” [PAGE 69], and BMI-specific GHRH-arginine cut-points such as “BMI<25; peakGH < 11 ug/l” [PAGE 69]. A formal classifier is:

\[
\hat{D} = \mathbf{1}(X < \tau)
\]

for deficiency when lower stimulated values indicate failure. Units of \(X\): µg/L for growth hormone or nmol/L for cortisol, depending on axis. The source notes that cortisol deficiency may be considered when basal morning cortisol is low or when dynamic testing fails, with cut-points varying by assay [PAGE 63], [PAGE 64]. Bayesian interpretation:

\[
P(D \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid D)P(D)}{P(X)}
\]

where \(P(D)\) is pre-test probability. The source emphasizes that symptoms are vague and that diagnostic decisions rely heavily on pre-test probability [PAGE 62]. It also says cut-points are arbitrary and grey-zones exist [PAGE 62].

Positive and negative likelihood ratios may be defined formally:

\[
LR^{+} = \frac{\text{sensitivity}}{1-\text{specificity}}
\]

\[
LR^{-} = \frac{1-\text{sensitivity}}{\text{specificity}}
\]

Assumptions: sensitivity and specificity are defined relative to a chosen reference standard. Breakdown regimes: (i) specificity equals 1, making \(LR^{+}\) diverge; (ii) sensitivity equals 0, making \(LR^{-}\) undefined for a positive test; (iii) pre-test probability equals 0 or 1, making Bayesian updating degenerate; (iv) assay-specific cut-points are ignored, causing false classification. The source explicitly warns that diagnostic stringency affects reported prevalence [PAGE 55] and that acute illness can confound hormone measurements [PAGE 65].

3. ICF functioning as a vector of qualified categories.

The source states that the ICF contains 1,424 categories and that functioning is described through body functions, body structures, activities and participation, and contextual factors [PAGE 87], [PAGE 88]. For a patient profile, let category \(c\) have a qualifier \(q_c\). For body functions, activities, participation, and environmental impact, the generic scale is:

\[
q_c \in \{0,1,2,3,4,8,9\}
\]

where 0 means no problem, 1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe, 4 complete, 8 not specified, and 9 not applicable [PAGE 91]. For environmental factors, the source uses a plus sign for facilitator and a dot for barrier:

\[
q_e^{(env)} = s_e \cdot |q_e|, \quad s_e \in \{+1,-1\}
\]

where \(s_e=+1\) indicates facilitator and \(s_e=-1\) indicates barrier [PAGE 90]. Assumption: these are ordinal qualifiers, not additive interval scores. A scalar sum is not formally justified by the source unless a purpose-specific instrument has been validated. Breakdown regimes: (i) use of 8 or 9 in place of a substantive score prevents inference about severity; (ii) conflating capacity and performance obscures environmental effects; (iii) treating ordinal qualifiers as ratio variables creates false precision.

4. Rehabilitation outcome change operators for community integration and life satisfaction.

The source uses the Community Integration Questionnaire and the Satisfaction with Life Scale in a severe traumatic brain injury rehabilitation cohort [PAGE 116], [PAGE 117]. The Community Integration Questionnaire is described as having home integration, social integration, and productivity domains [PAGE 116]. The Satisfaction with Life Scale uses five items with a 7-point response format and a score range from 5 to 35 [PAGE 106]. Define change from baseline:

\[
\Delta \text{CIQ}_t = \text{CIQ}_t - \text{CIQ}_0
\]

\[
\Delta \text{SWLS}_t = \text{SWLS}_t - \text{SWLS}_0
\]

Units: instrument points. The source reports that aggregate community integration improved significantly from baseline to 90-day follow-up and to one-year follow-up, while life satisfaction decreased significantly from baseline to 90-day follow-up before a non-significant gain by one year [PAGE 120]. A formal null hypothesis for a nonparametric paired analysis is:

\[
H_0: \text{median}(\Delta Y)=0
\]

where \(Y\) is CIQ or SWLS. Breakdown regimes: (i) small sample size; (ii) ordinal scale; (iii) ceiling or floor effects; (iv) self-awareness changes causing apparent worsening despite functional gain [PAGE 123].

5. REF model operator for cognitive recovery.

The source’s REF model distinguishes elementary functions, algorithmic strategies, and surface phenomena [PAGE 138]. Let \(E_j\) denote an elementary function, localized to a subregion. Let \(A_k\) denote an algorithmic strategy composed of interacting elementary functions. Let \(B\) denote a surface behavior or cognitive performance. A simplified formalization is:

\[
B = A_k(\{E_j\}_{j \in J_k})
\]

where \(J_k\) is the set of elementary functions required by strategy \(A_k\). If injury removes set \(L\) of elementary functions, then any strategy whose support intersects \(L\) is disrupted:

\[
A_k \text{ disrupted if } J_k \cap L \neq \varnothing
\]

Post-traumatic functional recovery occurs if there exists a spared or newly formed strategy \(A_{k'}\) such that:

\[
J_{k'} \cap L = \varnothing
\]

and

\[
B' \approx B_{\text{pre}}
\]

where \(B'\) is post-injury performance and \(B_{\text{pre}}\) is pre-injury performance. Assumption: surface equivalence does not imply identical neural mechanism. The source states that elementary functions are “Truly localized” [PAGE 138] and that novel algorithmic strategies may be created when existing strategies fail [PAGE 140]. Breakdown regimes: (i) no spared strategy exists; (ii) all relevant strategies depend on lost elementary functions; (iii) training feedback is absent or non-generalizable; (iv) apparent recovery is due only to penumbra resolution rather than strategy reorganization [PAGE 134].

6. Prevalence uncertainty and information entropy.

The source reports chronic anterior pituitary hormone deficiency prevalence after traumatic brain injury ranging from 1% to 83% depending on study design and diagnostic stringency [PAGE 55]. Let \(p\) be the true prevalence. The Bernoulli entropy is:

\[
H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)
\]

Units: bits. At \(p=0.5\), entropy is maximal at 1 bit. At \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), entropy is 0 bits. Breakdown regime: using entropy to compare studies requires that the same case definition and diagnostic threshold are being modeled; otherwise entropy differences reflect methodological heterogeneity rather than biological uncertainty.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the source can be reconstructed as four interacting traditions: post-concussion and mental fatigue research, neuroendocrine pituitary injury research, functioning classification and rehabilitation outcomes, and technology-assisted mental health care. Each tradition contains historical traps that persist in current inference.

The mental fatigue lineage begins with the observation that mild traumatic brain injury often appears benign in the acute phase but can produce persistent subjective exhaustion. The source notes that patients may recover within days to weeks, but a significant minority develop persistent mental fatigue [PAGE 15]. It also says that “Mental fatigue is common and it occurs after both a mild TBI as well as moderate to severe TBI” [PAGE 16]. Historically, this creates a trap: because acute motor or language deficits may be absent, fatigue is mistaken for laziness, apathy, or depression. The source explicitly warns that mental fatigue “can easily be mistaken for laziness or unwillingness” and that patients often have high motivation but missing energy [PAGE 17]. The scientific lineage therefore moves from lesion severity toward effort regulation, processing speed, and network compensation. The source cites the “coping hypothesis,” in which the brain must work harder to compensate for impaired attention or processing speed [PAGE 19]. This lineage is not a single lesion model; it is a control-system model.

A multi-week trap in this lineage is the mismatch between short-term functional appearance and long-term sustainable capacity. The source says that many people are eager to return to previous work levels, but this can create unsustainable activity levels [PAGE 16]. It recommends avoiding prolonged attempts to increase mental endurance because “this does not work” [PAGE 24]. The trap is recursive: the patient tries to prove recovery by pushing through fatigue, which increases fatigue, which then appears to confirm failure or poor motivation. The source also notes a 24-hour variation, with morning often best and afternoon/evening worse [PAGE 21]. This means that assessment timing matters. A patient tested in the morning may appear more capable than the same patient tested after sustained cognitive load.

The hypopituitarism lineage begins with autopsy observations of pituitary necrosis after fatal head injury and later expands to magnetic resonance imaging evidence of pituitary enlargement, hemorrhage, infarction, stalk abnormalities, and long-term volume loss [PAGE 61], [PAGE 62]. The source says newer studies indicate higher-than-expected frequency of chronic anterior pituitary hormone deficits [PAGE 61]. The historical trap is overdiagnosis from acute-phase hormone alterations. The source explains that acute critical illness can produce hormone patterns mimicking hypopituitarism [PAGE 65]. It also says acute-phase screening is not currently supported by evidence of clinical benefit from replacement therapy, except for adrenal insufficiency when clinically suspected [PAGE 74]. The multi-week trap is therefore the difference between transient critical-illness endocrine changes and persistent post-traumatic deficiency. The source recommends neuroendocrine evaluation no earlier than one year post trauma unless the clinical picture indicates otherwise [PAGE 64]. This creates a temporal gate: testing too early may produce false positives; testing too late may miss treatable deficiency.

The rehabilitation and functioning lineage comes from the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health. The source frames the ICF as a common language for rehabilitation and as a bridge between disease classification and lived functioning [PAGE 87]. It emphasizes that functioning is not a direct consequence of a health condition but the result of interactions with environmental and personal factors [PAGE 88]. The historical trap is to treat body impairment, activity limitation, and participation restriction as the same variable. The ICF model prevents this by separating body functions and structures, activities, participation, and contextual factors. Another trap is to assume that improvement in a clinical test automatically means improvement in daily life. The source’s case linking exercise shows that medical records often contain insufficient information about domestic life, community life, and environmental factors [PAGE 95], [PAGE 96]. This is a documentation trap: the patient may be improving in a hospital but still failing at home because the relevant environmental barriers were never measured.

The cognitive recovery lineage comes from the tension between functional localization and post-traumatic recovery. The source says that regional specialization is well documented, but recovery after lesion is also well documented [PAGE 134], [PAGE 135]. The REF model resolves this by distinguishing elementary functions from algorithmic strategies. The historical trap is to infer that because a behavior returns, the original neural circuit has returned. The source says recreation of lost basic circuitry is unlikely because mature brain tissue contains factors that prevent developmental-like network reformation [PAGE 136], [PAGE 137]. The multi-week trap is to interpret behavioral recovery as circuit restoration. In reality, recovery may involve new strategies, altered connectivity, or compensatory networks. This has clinical consequences: training must be designed for generalization, not merely for task-specific success [PAGE 143].

The technology lineage begins with internet-based and telephone-based mental health interventions for depression, anxiety, and caregiver support. The source reviews feasibility and efficacy studies and reports that technology can reach patients who might otherwise lack access [PAGE 39], [PAGE 40]. It also reports a survey in which many patients with mild to moderate traumatic brain injury expressed willingness to use technological solutions, but preferred in-person counselor contact when available [PAGE 53]. The historical trap is technological maximalism: assuming that because a platform is accessible, it is clinically sufficient. The source warns that severely depressed or suicidal patients require monitoring that may be lacking in online resources [PAGE 55]. The multi-week trap is engagement decay: initial novelty may produce short-term use, but cognitive fatigue, memory problems, and concentration difficulties may reduce sustained adherence unless programs are tailored [PAGE 49].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis must model three agents: the patient, the clinician, and the patient’s social environment. Each agent forms beliefs about the others, and those beliefs can become self-reinforcing. In brain injury rehabilitation, the most dangerous loops occur when the patient’s internal state is inferred incorrectly from surface behavior.

The first loop involves mental fatigue. The patient may believe that returning to full activity proves competence. The clinician may initially believe that apparent competence indicates recovery. The family or workplace may believe that the patient should resume prior responsibilities because no visible injury remains. The source says that mental fatigue is often not recognized immediately because attention focuses on apparent motor or language impairment [PAGE 15]. It also says that patients may be “competent and can be well-educated” but still have insufficient energy [PAGE 16]. The crisis habit regression is therefore “push through and prove normalcy.” This habit temporarily satisfies social expectations but increases mental exhaustion. The source explicitly warns against taking on more activities over longer periods to improve mental endurance: “this does not work” [PAGE 24]. The counter-trap is energy conservation, scheduled rest, reduced simultaneous demands, and acceptance that activity level must be profoundly reduced after injury [PAGE 24]. The clock matters because fatigue fluctuates over the day and recovery may take years. The source says some individuals need several years to find the right balance between rest and activity [PAGE 24].

The second loop involves hypopituitarism. The patient may present with nonspecific symptoms: fatigue, low mood, reduced libido, amenorrhea, hyponatremia, or poor recovery. The clinician may attribute these symptoms to the brain injury itself, to depression, or to deconditioning. The source says symptoms of hypopituitarism are often vague and unspecific [PAGE 62]. The crisis habit regression is to attribute all post-injury symptoms to the primary neurological event and delay endocrine evaluation. The counter-trap is to recognize high-risk patterns: severe injury, computed tomography abnormalities, increased intracranial pressure, diffuse axonal injury, basal skull fractures, or clinical suspicion of adrenal insufficiency [PAGE 76]. The source emphasizes that undiagnosed hypoadrenalism can be life-threatening and that patients may present with subtle signs yet still be endangered [PAGE 66]. The clock is nontrivial: acute-phase testing may be confounded, but chronic deficiency may require assessment after the acute stressor resolves. The source recommends evaluation no earlier than one year unless clinical status demands earlier action [PAGE 64]. This creates a recursive scheduling problem: the clinician must decide whether symptoms are transient critical-illness physiology, permanent deficiency, or de novo deficiency emerging later.

The third loop involves rehabilitation awareness. The source reports that community integration can improve while life satisfaction initially decreases. In the studied cohort, aggregate community integration improved from baseline to 90-day follow-up and one-year follow-up, but life satisfaction decreased significantly from baseline to 90-day follow-up before returning toward baseline at one year [PAGE 120]. The patient may believe that functional gains should automatically produce emotional satisfaction. The clinician may believe that improved independence will improve mood. The family may believe that return to activity should restore happiness. The source suggests that increased awareness of deficits may reduce early life satisfaction even as function improves [PAGE 123]. The crisis habit regression is to interpret early dissatisfaction as treatment failure and abandon rehabilitation. The counter-trap is to anticipate an awareness dip: as patients recognize the magnitude of their deficits, satisfaction may fall before adaptation and social participation improve. The clock matters because the source finds that social integration and life satisfaction become related at one-year follow-up [PAGE 120]. This suggests that the recursive loop may require months of feedback, role re-entry, and identity adjustment before subjective satisfaction catches up with objective participation.

The fourth loop involves technology-mediated care. The patient may believe that an app or website can replace therapy because it is private and convenient. The clinician may believe that technology improves access but does not replace risk assessment. The source says patients are receptive to technology, with a large proportion willing to use computer-based aids, but many still prefer in-person counselor contact [PAGE 53]. The crisis habit regression is to use technology only during crisis and then disengage. The counter-trap is to design hybrid care: technology for psychoeducation, monitoring, and booster sessions; human contact for risk assessment, therapeutic alliance, and complex decision-making. The source warns that online resources may lack monitoring for severely depressed or suicidal individuals [PAGE 55]. The clock matters because technology adherence may be undermined by cognitive fatigue, memory problems, and concentration difficulties [PAGE 49]. A recursive design must therefore include reminders, modular content, low cognitive load, and clear escalation pathways.

The deepest recursive risk is that the patient’s self-model becomes organized around either denial or collapse. Denial says: “I am fully recovered, so I will resume everything.” Collapse says: “I am broken, so I cannot do anything.” Both are unstable. The source supports a third model: “I have a changed energy budget; I will allocate it deliberately.” This is not a motivational slogan. It is a control policy. The patient must model their own fatigue, the clinician must model the patient’s capacity, and the environment must model the patient’s need for accommodation. When these models align, rehabilitation becomes a recursive optimization process rather than a series of pass/fail trials.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto adjudication must determine whether a proposed causal claim survives physical, biomechanical, clinical, and environmental constraints present in the source. The source provides several explicit veto conditions.

First, veto any claim that persistent mental fatigue is primarily determined by acute injury severity. The source states directly: “The degree of the fatigue appears to have no relation to the severity of the trauma or time since injury” [PAGE 16]. It also says the extent of fatigue seems unrelated to the primary brain area affected [PAGE 16]. Therefore, if a model predicts long-term mental fatigue solely from Glasgow Coma Scale, lesion volume, or injury severity, the source vetoes that model. The admissible layer is instead cognitive-effort regulation: attention, processing speed, mental load, recovery time, and diurnal variation [PAGE 17], [PAGE 19], [PAGE 21].

Second, veto any claim that acute endocrine testing can be used as a universal screening rule immediately after traumatic brain injury. The source says acute hormone alterations are common but may reflect critical illness rather than permanent hypopituitarism [PAGE 65]. It also says no evidence supports acute-phase anterior pituitary screening for growth hormone, thyroid, or gonadal axes because replacement benefit is not established at that stage [PAGE 74]. The physical-layer veto is therefore: acute hormonal abnormality does not equal chronic deficiency. The only urgent exception is suspected adrenal insufficiency, which should be treated on clinical suspicion because it can be life-threatening [PAGE 74]. The source reinforces this with a case description: patients initially misdiagnosed as having inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion actually had severe hypoadrenalism and required glucocorticoid replacement [PAGE 66].

Third, veto any deterministic biomechanical claim that left-handedness alone predicts traumatic brain injury or poor outcome. The source discusses left-handedness as a possible risk marker but also states: “However, there are also studies which show no elevated injury risk” [PAGE 30]. It presents mixed evidence on handedness, accidents, fractures, carotid asymmetry, and longevity. The biomechanical layer is therefore probabilistic and inconsistent, not deterministic. A valid model may include handedness only as a weak covariate, not as a gatekeeper.

Fourth, veto any claim that improved performance on a clinical cognitive test automatically equals improved community participation. The source’s REF model says training may produce situationally dependent recovery that fails to generalize to everyday life [PAGE 143]. The rehabilitation outcome data also show that community integration and life satisfaction can diverge: community integration improved significantly while life satisfaction initially decreased [PAGE 120]. Therefore, a model that uses only clinic-based test improvement as the outcome is incomplete. It must include participation, environmental support, and subjective satisfaction.

Fifth, veto any weather-based or ambient-condition causal claim unless the source provides a measured environmental variable. The source mentions natural environments as potentially restful: “Going for walks in natural environments as well as doing calm and relaxing activities can provide mental rest” [PAGE 24]. It does not provide weather measurements, temperature effects, barometric pressure effects, or meteorological modifiers. Therefore, if a proposed model includes weather as a causal variable for brain injury rehabilitation outcomes, the source contains no operator to identify it. The veto is not that weather is impossible in reality; the veto is that the supplied source does not support a formal weather variable. In a source-grounded model, weather must be excluded or marked as unmeasured.

Sixth, veto any claim that technology-based mental health treatment can fully replace clinician monitoring for high-risk patients. The source says patients are interested in technology-based approaches but warns that online resources may lack monitoring for severely depressed or suicidal individuals [PAGE 55]. The physical-layer constraint is not biomechanical but clinical: risk detection requires interaction, escalation, and responsibility. Therefore, a technology-only pathway for high-risk patients is vetoed. The admissible form is hybrid: technology as adjunct, with human oversight for safety-critical decisions.

The overall adjudication is that the strongest source-supported models are probabilistic, multi-layer, and context-sensitive. They must not collapse fatigue into lesion severity, endocrine deficiency into acute hormone values, recovery into clinic-task success, or technology access into clinical safety.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards style veto scenarios. Each scenario states a counterfactual intervention, the source-based veto, and a prediction that can be checked against the source’s variables.

Scenario 1: “Push-through endurance training” for persistent mental fatigue.

Counterfactual intervention: A patient with persistent mental fatigue after traumatic brain injury is instructed to resume full cognitive workload and gradually increase duration despite exhaustion, on the theory that mental endurance can be trained.

Source-based veto: The source explicitly rejects this strategy. It says: “It is recommended to avoid taking on more activities over longer periods in an attempt to improve mental endurance as this does not work” [PAGE 24]. It also says that when mental activity limits are pushed, fatigue increases and performance decreases [PAGE 24]. Therefore, the counterfactual is vetoed.

Verifiable prediction: If the push-through policy is applied, the patient’s Mental Fatigue Scale score will increase or remain high, recovery time after cognitive activity will lengthen, and errors during sustained attention will increase. The source supports this because fatigue is linked to concentration, attention, mental load, and prolonged recovery [PAGE 17], [PAGE 23]. A verifiable reward signal would be: reduced cognitive errors and lower fatigue scores under a policy of activity pacing, scheduled rest, and reduced simultaneous tasks. If the push-through policy produces worse performance and longer recovery, the veto is confirmed.

Scenario 2: “Fluid restriction only” for hyponatremia after traumatic brain injury.

Counterfactual intervention: A patient with severe traumatic brain injury develops hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, and hypotension. The clinical model assumes inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion and treats with fluid restriction alone, without considering secondary adrenal insufficiency.

Source-based veto: The source describes cases initially misdiagnosed as inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion but later found to have severe hypoadrenalism. It says all three patients had extremely low baseline cortisol and undetectable adrenocorticotropin, and the condition improved with glucocorticoid replacement [PAGE 66]. It also warns that patients may have subtle signs yet still be life-threatened by undiagnosed hypoadrenalism [PAGE 66]. Therefore, the counterfactual is vetoed.

Verifiable prediction: If fluid restriction alone is used in the presence of secondary adrenal insufficiency, hyponatremia will not correct reliably, and hypotension or hypoglycemia may persist. If glucocorticoid replacement is added, sodium and hemodynamic stability should improve. The source’s case evidence supports this prediction: “The condition ameliorated in all upon glucocorticoid replacement” [PAGE 66]. A verifiable reward signal is normalization of sodium and clinical stabilization after glucocorticoid administration. If fluid restriction alone corrects the condition without endocrine treatment, the veto would be weakened, but the source’s case evidence indicates that this is not the safest inference.

Scenario 3: “Technology-only mental health program” for high-risk depressed patients after traumatic brain injury.

Counterfactual intervention: A health system replaces clinician-delivered mental health care with an internet or handheld program for patients with traumatic brain injury, assuming that access, privacy, and cost-effectiveness are sufficient.

Source-based veto: The source acknowledges that technology-based approaches can be useful and that many patients are willing to use them [PAGE 53]. However, it explicitly warns that safety considerations for severely depressed or suicidal patients must be recognized because online resources may lack monitoring, while a healthcare provider can identify risk factors and intervene [PAGE 55]. It also says patients generally prefer technology as an aid rather than a complete replacement for traditional care [PAGE 55]. Therefore, a technology-only model for high-risk patients is vetoed.

Verifiable prediction: In a technology-only model, high-risk patients will have lower detection of suicidal ideation, lower escalation to emergency care, and higher risk of adverse events than in a hybrid model. The source supports this because it highlights the lack of monitoring in online resources and the importance of face-to-face resources when needed [PAGE 55]. A verifiable reward signal would be: high adherence and symptom improvement in low-risk patients using technology as an adjunct, plus documented safety escalation for high-risk patients. If a technology-only program shows no safety monitoring and adverse events rise among severely depressed patients, the veto is confirmed.

These three vetoes are source-grounded and empirically testable. They illustrate the central lesson of the volume: brain injury rehabilitation requires models that respect temporal dynamics, diagnostic uncertainty, environmental context, and the difference between surface behavior and underlying mechanism.
