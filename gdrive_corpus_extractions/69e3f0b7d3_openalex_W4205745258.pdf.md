Let the video be \(V_t\), a sequence of frames indexed by \(t\). The paper’s preprocessing stage resizes frames to “480x720 resolutions for smoother computations” [PAGE 4], computes illumination via \(I-I_b\) where \(I_b\) is the luminous background, and uses histogram equalization because “sports action will be recorded at different angles and different lighting conditions” [PAGE 4]. Formally, with \(\mathcal R_{480\times720}\) the resize operator and \(\mathcal H\) the histogram-equalization operator:
\[
F_k=\mathcal R_{480\times720}(V_t),\qquad I_t=I-I_b,\qquad \tilde F_k=\mathcal H(F_k).
\]
Units: pixel dimensions are pixels; \(I_t\) is in digital counts (DN) or normalized intensity, dimensionless if scaled to \([0,1]\). Assumption: the foreground/background separation is meaningful and brightness changes are monotone. Breakdown: resizing below the ball/limb scale destroys evidence; histogram equalization is global and can amplify sensor noise, erase specular ice/snow highlights, or distort shadow cues. The paper explicitly warns that HAR is difficult because of “varying camera viewpoint, lack of depth information, occlusion, overlapping objects, shadowed region” [PAGE 2]; preprocessing cannot recover information that was never sampled.

The wavelet transform is described as decomposing “the temporal signal into a summation of the time-domain basis function of various frequencies” [PAGE 5]. Using the standard \(L^2\)-normalized form consistent with the visible notation:
\[
\psi_{a,b}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a|}}\psi\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right),
\qquad
W_\psi f(a,b)=\int_{\mathbb R} f(t)\,\psi^*_{a,b}(t)\,dt .
\]
Here \(a\) is the scale parameter and \(b\) is the dilation/translation parameter; the source states “a and b as scale and dilation parameter respectively” [PAGE 5]. Units: if \(t\) is seconds, \(b\) is seconds and \(a\) is dimensionless; with normalized frame index, both are dimensionless. The coefficient \(W_\psi f\) carries the units of \(f\) times \(\sqrt{\text{time}}\) under this normalization, or digital-count units if time is normalized. Breakdown: \(a=0\) is undefined; very small \(a\) underflows and amplifies noise; boundary effects corrupt clip edges; 20 fps sampling [PAGE 10] cannot capture fast whole-body motion beyond its Nyquist limit; motion blur creates scale-dependent aliasing. The paper claims “it is totally reversible and the original signal can be reconstructed from its transformation form” [PAGE 5], but reversibility holds only for a complete basis and correct boundary handling, not for a truncated best-basis selection.

For the discrete form, a standard dyadic wavelet coefficient is:
\[
d_{j,k}=\int_{\mathbb R} f(t)\,2^{-j/2}\psi^*\!\left(2^{-j}t-k\right)dt,
\]
with \(j\) the decomposition level and \(k\) the translation index. The paper states “The discrete form of wavelet coefficients is derived as” [PAGE 5] but the printed equation is not reliably legible in the supplied text; the above is the standard dyadic reconstruction, not a verbatim source formula. For 2-D wavelet packets, the paper says “the decomposition is carried out in both scaling and dilating coefficients” and “we consider 2-D wavelet packet decomposition” [PAGE 6]. A separable 2-D packet basis can be written as:
\[
\Psi_{j,p,q}(x,y)=\psi_{j,p}(x)\,\psi_{j,q}(y),
\]
with sub-band coefficient arrays obtained by inner products against \(\Psi_{j,p,q}\). Units: coefficient arrays are in intensity or intensity-squared if energy is computed; with normalized images they are dimensionless. The paper says “the sub-bands engaged in the quad-tree splitting follow a separable product of 1D wavelet packet subspace” [PAGE 6]. Best-basis selection is described qualitatively: “sub-bands are selected when the information cost is higher than the cumulative sum of consecutive next level sub-bands” [PAGE 6]. No cost functional is printed; if Shannon entropy were used, its units would be bits, but the source does not specify this. Breakdown: deeper packet trees overfit small clip lengths; cost comparison without a penalty can select noise-dominated sub-bands; the method’s “tradeoff with computational complexity” [PAGE 7] is acknowledged but not quantified.

For feature selection, the paper’s regularized-LDA operator is consistent with the visible statement that the regularization parameter is “a small positive constant which is added to all the diagonal elements of within-class scatters matrix” [PAGE 8]. Define the between-class and within-class scatter matrices:
\[
S_b=\sum_{c=1}^{p} n_c(\mu_c-\mu)(\mu_c-\mu)^T,
\qquad
S_w=\sum_{c=1}^{p}\sum_{i:y_i=c}(x_i-\mu_c)(x_i-\mu_c)^T,
\]
where \(x_i\in\mathbb R^{d}\) is a wavelet-packet feature vector, \(n_c\) is the number of samples in class \(c\), \(\mu_c\) is the class mean, and \(\mu\) is the global mean. Entries of \(S_b\) and \(S_w\) have units of squared feature units; if features are normalized, they are dimensionless. The regularized eigenproblem consistent with the source’s description is:
\[
(S_w+\alpha I)^{-1}S_b\,w=\lambda w,
\qquad \alpha>0,
\]
where \(\alpha\) carries squared feature units unless features are normalized, and \(\lambda\) is dimensionless. The paper writes an eigenvalue decomposition of the form “= EDbwET” [PAGE 7, PAGE 8] with “E” as eigenvector and “Dbw” as diagonal matrix. Strictly, \((S_w+\alpha I)^{-1}S_b\) is not symmetric in general, so an orthogonal \(E\) and diagonal \(E^T\) decomposition is not guaranteed; the symmetric generalized problem \(S_b w=\lambda (S_w+\alpha I)w\) is the safer formulation. Class imbalance also biases \(S_b\) and \(S_w\), and a linear projection cannot represent non-linear class boundaries even though the abstract cites “non-linearity among the features” [PAGE 2].

A_{ij}=P(q_{t+1}=j\mid q_t=i),\qquad
B_i(o_t)=P(o_t\mid q_t=i),
\]
with row-stochastic constraints \(\sum_i\pi_i=1\), \(\sum_j A_{ij}=1\), and \(\sum_{o}B_i(o)=1\). Units: all probabilities are dimensionless in \([0,1]\). Prediction: confident errors on A01–A06, where right/left hand shots above waist, knee, and ground level differ by wrist and arm trajectory rather than by gross body shape [PAGE 5].

The counter-trap is the opposite overcorrection. To suppress inter-class confusion, the system may over-regularize, over-weight the rule set, or overfit the discriminant to the training venues. The paper’s rule set uses deterministic factors “σ (OF, MO, Ppose, Einfo)” [PAGE 9] and group-specific constraints such as “interaction (P, OF), dynamic (MO), Ppose (AC)” [PAGE 9]. A counter-trap occurs when those rules encode dataset-specific regularities rather than sport invariants. It yields high in-domain accuracy and brittle out-of-domain behavior. The counter-trap is distinguishable from crisis regression by ablation: if removing the rule set preserves accuracy, the rules are not the cause; if removing them destroys accuracy only on rare classes, the rules are memorizing those classes.

The clock is the temporal alignment problem. The paper uses “20fps” and “3-5secs” clips [PAGE 10] and an HMM with transition matrix \(A\) [PAGE 8]. The HMM’s implicit duration model is a clock: state transitions encode expected action timing. If the action starts before the clip, ends after it, is replayed in slow motion, or is captured at a different frame rate, the clock desynchronizes. A clock error is not the same as a counter-trap: if shuffling frame order destroys accuracy but duration changes do not, the model is using temporal order; if duration changes destroy accuracy while shuffled order does not, the model is using static scene cues. The paper’s own sequence-based rationale [PAGE 3] makes clock alignment a first-class assumption, not a detail. The recursive ToM lesson is that the organizer’s clock — broadcast delay, review window, officiating deadline — can truncate the sequence before the HMM has observed the discriminating transition.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a proof obligation: if a lower layer destroys the information needed to distinguish two labels, no upper-layer classifier can recover it. Let \(x_{1:t}\) be the observed video evidence after preprocessing, \(y\in\{a,b\}\) the action label, and \(\mathcal L\) a layer (physical, biomechanical, or weather). If the layer makes the observation distributions identical,
\[
p(x_{1:t}\mid y=a,\mathcal L)=p(x_{1:t}\mid y=b,\mathcal L),
\]
then for equal priors the Bayes error is at least \(1/2\):
\[
\inf_{\hat y}\Pr(\hat y\ne y\mid \mathcal L)\ge \frac{1}{2}.
\]
For unequal priors, the identical-distribution Bayes error is \(1-\max\{\pi_a,\pi_b\}\). This is a probability statement, dimensionless. It is the formal core of the veto: DN-LDA, wavelet packets, and HMM cannot separate classes whose evidence has been collapsed by occlusion, resolution, viewpoint, biomechanical ambiguity, or weather.

Physical veto. The paper lists “varying camera viewpoint, lack of depth information, occlusion, overlapping objects, shadowed region” as core HAR difficulties [PAGE 2]. Frames are resized to “480x720” [PAGE 4] and clips are “20fps” and “3-5secs” [PAGE 10]. If a right-hand shot above the waist versus above the knee is distinguished by wrist/elbow position that is occluded by another player, the pixel evidence is insufficient. Histogram equalization addresses brightness, not occlusion: the paper says it is used for “the brightness issues” [PAGE 4]. Physical veto condition: after resize and equalization, the two actions map to the same wavelet-packet support and the same HMM emission distribution. Prediction: high-confidence errors on A01–A06 and A09–A10 when the implement or arm is hidden.

Biomechanical veto. The paper states the core difficulty is “massive variations in human look, posture and body size inside identical category” [PAGE 2] and distinguishes joint-orientation versus pairwise-position approaches [PAGE 2]. If two athletes produce different joint angles but the same projected silhouette, or the same joint angles at different body sizes, the chosen features may be invariant to the wrong variable. The paper’s own similar-action examples — “Hockey - Hit” versus “Hockey - Push” [PAGE 5], and “Swimming Freestyle,” “Swimming Butterfly,” and “Swimming Breakstroke” [PAGE 5] — are biomechanically close. A linear discriminant can collapse them if the between-class scatter is dominated by venue or body-size variance. Biomechanical veto condition: within-class posture variance exceeds between-class action variance in the selected feature subspace, or the linear projection is rank-deficient. Prediction: DN/DR-LDA improves average accuracy but leaves confusion concentrated on the closest kinematic pairs.

Weather and surface veto. The paper’s background-scaling rationale explicitly includes “the floor where the action takes place” to distinguish “field hockey vs. ice hockey; tennis vs. cricket” [PAGE 4]. That is a surface cue. Weather changes surface appearance: rain darkens field hockey turf, snow and ice create specular reflectance, sun and shadow create high-contrast regions that histogram equalization may amplify. If weather alters the surface statistics more than the action alters the body statistics, the classifier can learn the surface instead of the action. Weather veto condition: the same action on wet versus dry turf, or indoor versus outdoor ice, produces larger feature displacement than the action-class difference. Prediction: cross-weather evaluation will expose reliance on background/surface features, especially for Group 3 events such as skating, ice hockey, and water polo [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. The veto is not a claim that the pipeline is worthless; it is a proof that the paper’s 97.4% cannot be interpreted as weather-invariant or occlusion-invariant without the missing cross-layer tests.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
