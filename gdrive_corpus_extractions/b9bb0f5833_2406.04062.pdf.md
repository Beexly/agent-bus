<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The theoretical lineage begins with Kelly (1956), who introduced expected-log-wealth maximization as the asymptotically optimal growth criterion, later applied to sports betting and portfolio management by Thorp (2008; 1975) and Rotando & Thorp (1992). Busseti et al. (2016) criticized full Kelly as overly aggressive, motivating fractional and distributionally robust variants, which the paper notes can be accommodated by replacing the wealth distribution with a joint wealth-fraction distribution (page 3).

On the market-making side, Hanson (2007) proposed the logarithmic market scoring rule (LMSR) as an automated market maker for prediction markets, linking proper scoring rules (Brier, 1950; Savage, 1971; Gneiting & Raftery, 2007) to online optimization. Chen & Pennock (2007), Chen et al. (2008), and Abernethy et al. (2013) generalized LMSR and connected it to online convex optimization. Frongillo et al. (2012) showed prediction-market dynamics implicitly minimize KL divergence between the average belief and the market price, a purely information-elicitation objective with zero maximal profit for the market maker.

Levitt (2004) empirically identified better prediction ability and systematic exploitation of bettor biases as the two main drivers of bookmaker profit. Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2006) framed prediction markets as belief aggregators. The present paper formalizes Levitt’s insights and proves that profit and information elicitation are fundamentally incompatible: when the bookmaker matches the crowd’s mean belief, profit collapses to zero (page 4).

The online-learning machinery draws on Robbins & Monro (1951) stochastic approximation, Pemantle (1990) on avoiding unstable equilibria, and Mertikopoulos et al. (2020) on almost-sure convergence in non-convex settings. Algorithm 1 is a stochastic-approximation root-finder; Algorithm 2 is a Follow-the-Leader method (Hazan et al., 2016) specialized to the concave fair-price case.

Multi-week traps: The theory assumes i.i.d. bettor beliefs (page 6). Over a multi-week horizon, team news, injuries, roster changes, and market sentiment induce non-stationarity in f(p). If the belief distribution drifts, the running average in Algorithm 2 and the stochastic approximation in Algorithm 1 track a moving optimum; the O(√T) regret bounds assume a fixed comparator and become meaningless in wall-clock time when the effective sample size stalls. A second trap is local-maxima capture: Appendix F (page 17) shows that with multiple profit maximizers, poor initialization can lock the bookmaker into a “bad” equilibrium with regret growing comparably to the naive risk-balancing heuristic. A third trap is the clipping boundary τ in Algorithm 2: if the true average belief migrates toward 0 or 1 over weeks, the clipped estimate saturates and the algorithm cannot recover the optimal price without manual re-centering.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under market stress—such as a surge in sharp bettor activity or a sudden news shock—the bookmaker may abandon the online SA/FTL pricing rule and revert to the risk-balancing heuristic described in Appendix F.I (page 17), which equalizes total dollars wagered on each side. The paper’s simulations show risk balancing incurs regret more than an order of magnitude larger than Algorithm 1 and that regret keeps increasing on a log scale (Fig. 2, page 8). This regression is a crisis habit: it feels safe because it limits directional exposure, but it forfeits the exploitable deviation between g and E[p_t] that Eq. (4) identifies as the source of profit.

Counter-trap: The bookmaker’s price updates (Eqs. 10–11, page 6) are observable to subsequent bettors. If bettors anticipate that placing a large stake on A will push a_{t+1} upward, they may strategically time or size their bets to manipulate the belief estimator \hat b p_t. The SA convergence proof (Lemma K.2, page 23) requires E[\hat b p_t | F_t]=E[p_t | p_t≥a_t], i.e., zero conditional noise. Strategic bettor adaptation breaks this martingale property, turning the noise process M_{t+1} into a biased, state-dependent signal. The algorithm would then converge to a fixed point of the bettors’ counter-strategy rather than a local maximizer of u_t. This is a counter-trap: the very transparency required for online learning creates an incentive for bettors to game the learner.

Clock: The step-size schedule η_t=γ/(t+m) imposes an intrinsic clock. Early bettors (small t) produce large price moves; late bettors produce diminishing updates. If bettor arrival slows—seasonal lulls, off-peak days—the effective T in the regret bound O(√T) grows slowly in calendar time, so the algorithm remains far from its asymptotic neighborhood for extended periods. Conversely, a sudden burst of volume compresses many updates into a short interval, risking overshoot if γ is not rescaled. The clock is therefore not neutral: it couples the learning rate to the arrival process, and any mismatch between bettor inter-arrival time and the assumed t-index invalidates the O(√T log T) high-probability guarantee of Corollary 5.2 (page 7).
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The model assumes pre-determined binary outcomes (page 1 footnote 1), such as a home team winning. Physical disruptions—weather postponement, venue change, referee intervention, or mid-game rule alterations—can shift the true outcome probability after prices are posted. The bookmaker’s belief g is fixed at pricing time; if an exogenous physical event makes Pr[A | new information] fall outside the imprecise belief interval [g^−,g^+] of Appendix C (page 15), the non-negative profit lower bound fails and the algorithm’s price updates no longer target a coherent optimum.

Biomechanical / liquidity layer veto: Kelly stake sizing assumes the bettor can cover the wager. Theorem 4.3 explicitly requires v_t≤W_t so that \hat b p_t∈[0,1] (page 6). In practice, wallet limits, credit constraints, or platform-imposed max bet caps can prevent a bettor from placing the Kelly-optimal stake. If v_t is truncated below the Kelly level, the affine mapping from stake to belief in Eq. (8) is distorted; \hat b p_t becomes a biased, censored estimator, and the SA gradient noise loses its zero-mean property.

Weather veto proof: Suppose a binary outdoor event has bookmaker belief g=0.6 before a weather report. If heavy rain arrives and shifts the true conditional probability to Pr[A | rain]=0.3, the fair-price condition a+b=1 no longer anchors to a stable information target. Under the imprecise-belief lemma (page 15), the bookmaker could set a≥g^+ and b≥1−g^− to maintain non-negative estimated profit, but only if the true probability remains within [g^−,g^+]. A weather-driven jump outside that interval vetoes the profit guarantee entirely; the model contains no exogenous shock process to absorb it.

Adjudication: The model itself imposes hard vetoes. The domain excludes a+b<1 because it creates arbitrage (page 4, footnote 3). Boundary points a=1 or b=1 yield zero profit (Lemma 3.2 proof, page 20) and are ruled out as maximizers. The support condition supp(f)=[0,1] in Theorem 4.3 vetoes belief distributions concentrated on strict subsets of (0,1), such as when all bettors are clustered near 0.5 with no mass in the tails—precisely the case where polarization-driven profit (Prop. 3.6, page 5) vanishes.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning veto scenarios with counterfactual predictions:

Veto 1 — Non-stationary belief drift.
Scenario: The bettor belief distribution f_t(p) is not i.i.d. but undergoes concept drift, e.g., the mean E[p_t] shifts by Δ per week due to public information releases.
Verifiable condition: Compute the 1-Wasserstein distance W_1(f_t,f_{t−1}) over a sliding window; if W_1>ε for ε>0 persistently, the i.i.d. assumption of Section 4 is falsified.
Prediction: The SA regret will cease to be O(√T). The algorithm converges to a stale local maximizer of the historical distribution, incurring linear regret relative to the time-varying optimum u_t(a_t^⋆,b_t^⋆). Empirically, the cumulative regret curve will transition from concave (√T growth) to linear growth once drift dominates the learning rate γ/(t+m).

Veto 2 — Bettor strategic adaptation to price updates.
Scenario: Bettors observe the update rule Eqs. (10)–(11) and deliberately place oversized bets on the side whose price they wish to move, thereby biasing \hat b p_t.
Verifiable condition: Test whether Cov(v_t, sign(a_{t+1}−a_t))≠0 conditional on the bet side. A significant correlation indicates bettors are manipulating the belief estimator.
Prediction: The martingale condition E[M_{t+1}|F_t]=0 (Lemma K.1–K.2, pages 22–23) fails. Algorithm 1 will either diverge or converge to a fixed point that is not a critical point of u_t. The profit gap u_t(a^⋆,b^⋆)−u_t(a_t,b_t) will remain O(1) rather than decaying as O(T^{−1/2}).

Veto 3 — Fair-odds clipping boundary saturation.
Scenario: In Algorithm 2, the true average belief E[p_t]=0.1 while the clipping interval is [τ,1−τ] with τ=0.2 (page 7).
Verifiable condition: Monitor the fraction of clipped estimates \hat b p_t that hit the lower bound τ; if this fraction approaches 1, the cumulative average \bar p_t is trapped at τ.
Prediction: The price a_t converges to ψ(τ) rather than ψ(E[p_t]). By Theorem 5.1’s local Lipschitz argument (page 8), the profit gap u_t(a^⋆,b^⋆)−u_t(a_t,b_t) converges to a positive constant determined by |ψ(τ)−ψ(E[p_t])|, violating the O(T^{−1/2}\sqrt{\log(1/δ)}) convergence rate. A bookmaker observing persistent clipping should reject Algorithm 2 and switch to the unfair-odds SA method or re-specify τ.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
