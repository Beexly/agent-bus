Vanilla temperature scaling learns a single positive scalar \(\tau\) by minimizing NLL on a labeled held-out set, but the paper’s central obstacle is that new LLM tasks often have no labeled data [PAGE 3]. The proposed move is to replace the task-specific scalar with a task-specific latent variable \(\tau_k\), place a prior on it, and amortize inference through a recognition network \(\psi_\theta\) that sees only prompt features \(\phi(x)\), not labels. This is why the method can estimate temperature for an unseen unlabeled task: the test-time estimator is \(\tau^*=\frac{1}{N^*}\sum_{n=1}^{N^*}\psi_{\theta^*}(\phi(x_n^*))\) [PAGE 5].

The variational derivation has three linked steps. First, form a lower bound with an expectation over \(q(\tau_k;\theta)\) minus a KL term [PAGE 4]. Second, choose \(q\) as a product of Gaussians with fixed variance \(\epsilon\) and means \(\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n^k))\), which collapses to a Gaussian with mean \(\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n^k))\) and variance \(\epsilon/N_k\) [PAGE 4]. Third, for small \(\epsilon\), approximate that Gaussian by a point mass, yielding the trainable objective with a Gamma prior and a regularizer \(\lambda_{\rm reg}\) [PAGE 4]. The “evidence accumulation” is therefore not a toy ratio; it is the exact Gaussian product identity plus a small-variance approximation, and the paper explicitly notes the point-mass approximation and the Gamma prior [PAGE 4].

with \(\hat Y=\arg\max p(Y\mid X;\mathcal M)\) and \(\hat P=\max p(Y\mid X;\mathcal M)\) [PAGE 3]. The empirical ECE is
\[
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{N}
\left|\mathrm{acc}(B_m)-\mathrm{conf}(B_m)\right|,
\]
where \(B_m\) are confidence bins; the paper uses \(M=10\) bins in evaluation [PAGE 6]. Temperature scaling is
\[
p(y_n\mid x_n;\tau,\mathcal M)=
\frac{\exp\!\left(w_{y_n}^{\top}\phi(x_n;W)/\tau\right)}
{\sum_{v'=1}^{V}\exp\!\left(w_{v'}^{\top}\phi(x_n;W)/\tau\right)},
\]
with \(\tau\in\mathbb R_+\) and \(\hat\tau=\arg\min_{\tau\in\mathbb R_+}-\sum_{n=1}^{N^*}\log p(y_n\mid x_n;\tau,\mathcal M)\) [PAGE 3]. Because \(\tau\) only divides logits, it “does not change the class with the maximum probability and preserves accuracy” [PAGE 3].

\[
\mathcal L(\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb E_{q(\tau_k;\theta)}
\left[\log p(D_k\mid \tau_k;\mathcal M)\right]
-\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q(\tau_k;\theta)\,\|\,p(\tau_k\mid\nu_0)\right),

where \(q(\tau_k;\theta)\propto\prod_{n=1}^{N_k}\mathcal N(\tau_k\mid\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n^k)),\epsilon)\), which equals
\[
\mathcal N\!\left(\tau_k\;\middle|\;
\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n=1}^{N_k}\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n^k)),\;
\frac{\epsilon}{N_k}\right)
\]
[PAGE 4]. The paper states: “We choose a product of Gaussian PDF for the variational approximation. Each corresponding Gaussian variable has a fixed variance ϵ, and a mean parameterized by a recognition network” [PAGE 4]. For small \(\epsilon\), the paper approximates \(q\) by a point mass and optimizes
\[
\tilde{\mathcal L}(\theta)=
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log p\!\left(D_k\;\middle|\;
\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n=1}^{N_k}\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n^k));\mathcal M\right)
-\lambda_{\rm reg}\log p\!\left(
\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n=1}^{N_k}\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n^k))\;\middle|\;\nu_0\right),
\]
with a Gamma prior \(p(\tau_k\mid\nu_0)=\mathrm{Gamma}(\tau_k\mid\alpha_0=1.25,\beta_0=4)\) [PAGE 4]. The test-time estimator is
\[
\tau^*=\frac{1}{N^*}\sum_{n=1}^{N^*}\psi_{\theta^*}(\phi(x_n^*)).
\]
[PAGE 5]. Lemma 4.1 assumes \(\sup_{x\in X}\psi_\theta(\phi(x))\le C_\theta\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n))]\le V_\theta\), plus \(L\)-Lipschitz calibration error \(\mathrm{CE}(\tau)\), and gives
\[
\mathbb E\left|\frac{1}{N^*}\sum_{n=1}^{N^*}\psi_\theta(\phi(x_n))
-\mathbb E_{x\sim P^*}\psi_\theta(\phi(x))\right|
\le
\frac{4}{3}C_\theta\frac{\log N^*}{N^*}
+(2V_\theta)^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{\log N^*}{N^*}},
\]
with probability at least \(1-2/N^{*2}\), and the CE error is bounded by \(L\) times the same right-hand side [PAGE 5].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from classical forecast verification to modern post-hoc neural calibration. Brier’s 1950 proper scoring rule and Dawid’s 1982 “well-calibrated Bayesian” framing establish the probabilistic-calibration vocabulary; Platt et al. 1999 and Guo et al. 2017 make temperature and parametric scaling standard post-hoc tools; the paper explicitly builds on Guo et al. and temperature scaling [PAGE 1–PAGE 3]. The variational branch comes from Dayan et al.’s Helmholtz machine and Bouchacourt et al.’s evidence accumulation, which the paper cites for the product-of-Gaussians aggregation [PAGE 4]. The multi-task temperature branch is distinguished from Joy et al. 2023, which predicts per-instance temperatures without multi-dataset learning, and Yu et al. 2022, which learns dataset-specific temperatures then fits a linear regression [PAGE 2]. THERMOMETER instead jointly learns a nonlinear auxiliary model across tasks by maximizing a lower bound [PAGE 2]. The elicitation branch—Tian et al. 2023 and Xiong et al. 2023—is orthogonal and prompt-based; the paper notes that it can be combined with temperature scaling and that it is not the same as statistical calibration [PAGE 3].

The multi-week traps are empirical and structural. Trap one is the alignment regression: “common interventions such as instruction tuning often result in poorly calibrated LLMs” [PAGE 1], so a model that looks accurate can still be badly overconfident. Trap two is labeled-test dependence: vanilla TS needs a labeled held-out set, which is often unavailable for new tasks [PAGE 3]. Trap three is transfer overreach: the paper demonstrates transfer across LLaMA-2-Chat scales and across MMLU and BIG-bench [PAGE 2, PAGE 8], but transfer is an empirical regularity, not an identity; a new task family can break it. Trap four is free-form label construction: the self-check prompt labels a response correct when ROUGE-L precision exceeds zero [PAGE 19], which is a loose criterion and can create false positives under verbose generations. Trap five is regularization instability: \(\lambda_{\rm reg}=0\) was numerically unstable [PAGE 17]. Trap six is sample-wise overfitting: sample-wise temperature scaling is “sub-optimal when compared to aggregation across data samples” [PAGE 9]. A multi-week evaluation should therefore track ECE, NLL, Brier, and TL-ECE across tasks, not just average accuracy, and should hold out both tasks and benchmarks.

The recursive layer has three agents: the base LLM, the auxiliary THERMOMETER, and the evaluator. The base LLM’s “crisis habit” is fluent overconfidence after instruction tuning [PAGE 1]; THERMOMETER does not retrain that habit, it rescales the logits so greedy decoding is preserved [PAGE 3]. That is a counter-habit: it treats the model’s confidence as a measurable object rather than as a policy to be retrained. The counter-trap is the obvious alternative—multiple forward passes, prompt elicitation, ensembles, or retraining. The paper rules out retraining as prohibitively expensive and shows that elicitation can fail outright on FLAN-T5-XL: “by providing the crafted prompts, FLAN-T5-XL often fails to generate the corresponding verbalized confidence” [PAGE 14]. The clock is two clocks at once: inference runtime, where THERMOMETER is “only ∼0.5% slower than the uncalibrated LLM” [PAGE 2], and evidence accumulation, where \(\tau^*\) converges as \(N^*\) grows and the lemma gives a \(1/N^*\)-type concentration bound [PAGE 5]. The nested theory-of-mind point is that the LLM is asked to judge its own answer in the free-form QA setup, and THERMOMETER then calibrates that self-judgment; the paper reports self-check accuracy from 70.5% to 88.7% across six MRQA development sets [PAGE 19]. That is not perfect introspection, so the evaluator must model both the LLM’s self-check error and the auxiliary model’s temperature error. The practical counter-trap is to demand per-sample introspection; the paper finds dataset-level aggregation better than sample-wise temperature [PAGE 9]. The correct recursive stance is therefore: model the dataset’s temperature, not each sample’s private certainty, and keep the base model’s first-order predictions unchanged.
