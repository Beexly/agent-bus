Recalibration over weeks would erode the very error the theory seeks to explain.
- Single-muscle generalization: The external rectus palsy affects abduction only. Extending the conclusion to “space-theories” generally [PAGE 236] ignores conjugate gaze, head movements, binocular disparity, and cervical proprioception, all of which contribute to spatial localization independently of any abducens corollary discharge.
- Effort-illusion confound: “Something in the effort” [PAGE 236] may be a central sense of effort or motor-attentional signal rather than a discrete “feeling of innervation.” Conflating effort magnitude with positional extent is a category error that a weeks-long adaptation paradigm would help disentangle.
- Methodological trap: The case is hypothetical (“Suppose a man…”) [PAGE 236]. Without longitudinal data on pointing bias before, during, and after paralysis, one cannot estimate the adaptation time constant or verify that the 10-degree error decays as predicted by \Delta G = -\kappa e u.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Theory of mind in this scenario is nested: the patient models the external object’s location; the patient models his own eye’s position; the theorist (Wundt or James) models the patient’s inner feeling. Each layer is vulnerable to misattribution.

Crisis habit regression: Under acute partial palsy, the patient regresses to the pre-morbid sensorimotor habit. The learned mapping u \mapsto \theta was calibrated under gain G_0. After the lesion, the same motor command produces less rotation, but the patient’s phenomenal readout still consults the old internal model \hat{\theta} = G_0 u. The crisis (lesion) therefore triggers a behavioral regression: high effort is interpreted as large displacement, producing the rightward pointing overshoot [PAGE 236]. This is not evidence of a new sense; it is the persistence of an outdated habit under changed plant dynamics.

Counter-trap: Wundt’s inference to a “feeling of innervation” is itself a counter-trap. It takes a systematic prediction error (command exceeds consequence) and reifies it as a dedicated afferent-like entity “opposed to every afferent sensation whatever” [PAGE 236]. James’s counter to this trap is to deny the entity and explain the datum by forward-model mismatch plus residual afferent cues. The counter-trap is avoided only by refusing to grant phenomenal confusion ontological status as a separate sensory channel.

Clock: Recalibration runs on a sensorimotor clock, not an instantaneous reset. Each pointing attempt generates e_i = s_i - \hat{\theta}_i; the update \Delta G_i = -\kappa e_i u_i accumulates over trials. Convergence requires N \gg 1/(\kappa \bar{u}^2) repetitions, which in biological systems spans days to weeks. During that window the patient’s judgment remains biased; after convergence, the “feeling of innervation” explanation loses its empirical target because the pointing error has vanished without postulating any special sense. The recursion closes when the theorist models the patient as modeling his own eye—if the patient’s model is updated correctly, the theorist’s posited “feeling” becomes redundant.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof with adjudication.

Physical layer: The lesion is in the external rectus (lateral rectus), an extraocular muscle innervated by the abducens nerve (CN VI). The physical veto is that the palsy alters plant output, not the existence of a sensory channel. A veto condition would be: if pointing error persists after experimentally equating retinal input (e.g., by stabilizing the image or using the seeing eye), then the error cannot be attributed to retinal afference alone. The source supports that the error is effort-linked [PAGE 236], consistent with an efferent or central estimate rather than a purely afferent eyeball sensation.

Biomechanical layer: The eye rotates in the orbit about a center roughly 11 mm radius; torque \tau = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} with \mathbf{F} in newtons. Half-paralysis reduces maximal lateral rectus force, but the orbit is nonlinear: passive elasticity of the medial rectus and orbital tissues creates angle-dependent resistance, so G_1 is not constant and saturates near mechanical limits. Any veto that assumes linear gain G_1 = constant across all angles is invalidated near \theta_{max}. The text’s 20 vs 30 degree example [PAGE 236] is a small-angle regime where linearization is approximately valid, but extrapolation to extreme gaze is a breakdown regime.

Neural layer: Abducens motor neurons encode command in spike rate (tens to hundreds of Hz). There is no known dedicated afferent tract from abducens motor neurons to somatosensory cortex that could carry a literal “feeling of the outgoing nervous current” [PACE 236]. The signal must arrive via corollary discharge through thalamocortical relays or be inferred from effort. This vetoes a naïve interpretation of innervation feeling as a direct afferent sensation of motor outflow.

Weather layer: The vignette is an indoor clinical oculomotor task (waking, pointing, eye closure) [PAGE 236]. Weather variables—temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, illumination changes from weather—have no plausible pathway to alter abducens drive or extraocular mechanics on the timescale of the described pointing movement. Weather is therefore a null/inactive veto for this scenario. If the task were moved outdoors, wind-induced retinal slip or changes in illuminance could perturb afferent calibration, but that is outside the source’s described conditions.

Adjudication: The physical lesion is real; the biomechanical plant is nonlinear and saturating; the neural substrate does not support a dedicated afferent “feeling of innervation”; and the behavioral error is fully accounted for by forward-model gain mismatch with recalibration on a trial-by-trial clock. James’s verdict—that the feeling is a fictitious entity—is upheld at every layer except the phenomenal report of effort, which remains real but is mislocated in ontology by Wundt’s inference [PAGE 236].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards / counterfactual veto) scenarios, each with a falsifying prediction.

Veto Scenario 1 — Selective sensory afferent block with intact motor output.
Design: Temporarily anesthetize or block sensory afferents from the extraocular muscles (e.g., ophthalmic division input related to ocular proprioception) while preserving motor command to the abducens nerve in a partially paralyzed lateral rectus. Measure pointing bias toward a rightward target before and after block.
Veto condition: If the pointing error (overshoot to the right) disappears or is abolished when afferent feedback is removed but the motor command remains high, then the error was not driven by an independent “feeling of innervation”; it depended on afferent comparison.
Prediction: Under the corollary-discharge/forward-model account, the error should persist after afferent block because the outdated internal model \hat{\theta} = G_0 u still converts high command into overestimated extent, though recalibration would be impossible without the error signal e(t) = s(t) - \hat{\theta}(t). Verifiable via psychophysical pointing bias and video-oculography of actual eye position.

Veto Scenario 2 — Intact command, zero movement (conjugate/brainstem gaze palsy).
Design: Use a subject with an abducens nucleus or axonal lesion that prevents lateral rectus contraction despite intact descending motor command u (e.g., verified by electrophysiology or fMRI of premotor oculomotor areas). Ask the subject to attempt rightward gaze and report perceived eye displacement.
Veto condition: If the subject reports large rightward eye rotation in the absence of any actual rotation (verified by scleral search coil or video-oculography, \theta_a = 0), then a pure efferent “feeling of innervation” would be supported.
Prediction: The subject should report little or no perceived rotation because there is no retinal displacement, no proprioceptive stretch, and no sensory prediction error to validate the command. This vetoes the claim that the outgoing current alone specifies extent [PAGE 236].

Veto Scenario 3 — Artificial gain manipulation (prism or video-oculography loop).
Design: Insert a closed-loop video-oculography system that scales the relationship between motor command and visual feedback by gain K \neq 1 (e.g., the displayed target moves only half as far as the eye). Measure pointing bias over days of adaptation.
Veto condition: If the pointing bias remains locked to the raw magnitude of motor command u and does not shift toward the new visual gain K after adaptation, then a fixed “feeling of innervation” is supported over a recalibrated forward model.
Prediction: The bias should track the recalibrated internal model \hat{\theta} = G_K u after adaptation, shifting systematically with K and trial number. The error curve should follow \Delta\theta_n \approx \Delta\theta_0 (1 - \kappa \bar{u}^2)^n over n trials. Failure of bias to adapt with visual gain falsifies the fixed-innervation hypothesis and supports James’s fictitious-entity position.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
