The development of Weil topology represents a confluence of algebra, analysis, and topology in the mid-20th century. Its historical lineage can be traced through several key figures and concepts:

1.  **André Weil (1940)**: The seminal work is Weil's *L'intégration dans les groupes topologiques et ses applications*. Weil sought to generalize the Haar measure to all locally compact groups. He realized that the topological structure could be recovered from the measure-algebraic structure alone, provided one works with the Baire $\sigma$-algebra rather than the Borel $\sigma$-algebra. This was a profound shift: topology was no longer primitive but emergent from measure. The class $\mathcal{N}$ described above is Weil's innovation—using the continuity of translation in $L^1$ (specifically, $\lim_{x \to e} \| \tau_x f - f \|_1 = 0$) to define neighborhoods.

2.  **Haar Measure (1933)**: Before Weil, Haar established the existence of invariant measures on compact and later locally compact groups, but his proofs relied heavily on the pre-existing topology. The problem of uniqueness and the relationship between different invariant measures was partially solved, but the converse—recovering topology from measure—was open.

3.  **Stone's Representation (1937)**: Marshall Stone's work on Boolean algebras and the representation of commutative C*-algebras (Gelfand-Naimark precursors) provided tools to associate a topological space (the Stone-Čech compactification or spectrum) to an algebraic/measurable structure. While not directly about groups, it influenced the mindset that algebraic/combinatorial data could encode topology.

4.  **Halmos's Synthesis (1950)**: Halmos's *Measure Theory* serves as a pedagogical and systematic consolidation. His presentation of Weil topology (Section 62) makes Weil's somewhat technical arguments more accessible. By emphasizing the "measurable group" starting point and using elementary measure theory constructs like symmetric difference and finite unions of rectangles, Halmos demystifies the process. His choice to begin with integration by parts hints at the analytic payoff: once the topology is established, classical analysis becomes available.
