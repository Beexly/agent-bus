The scientific lineage traces from Hoffman’s applied physiology of American football ([PAGE 1], ref 1) and Wellman et al.’s GPS quantification of competitive game demands ([PAGE 1], ref 3), which established that linemen cover less aggregate distance than non-linemen. Flatt et al. (2020) then documented the counter-intuitive finding that BIG players exhibited the lowest cardiac-autonomic recovery despite the lowest external load ([PAGE 2], ref 4). The present study ([PAGE 1]–[PAGE 7]) extends that lineage by substituting internal HR-derived TRIMP for GPS-derived PlayerLoad, thereby closing the measurement gap that Impellizzeri, Marcora, and Coutts identified as central to the internal–external load distinction ([PAGE 2], ref 6). The heat-illness lineage runs through Anderson et al.’s 2025 exertional heatstroke paradigm shift and Boden et al.’s 2020 documentation of nontraumatic exertional fatalities, both cited on [PAGE 1] (refs 10–11): 97% of EHS deaths from 1998–2018 occurred in linemen, and 100% occurred during supervised practice.

Multi-week traps are embedded in the design. The 10-week in-season window excludes preseason heat acclimatization, when EHS risk is historically highest, and excludes the postseason, so the observed HRV–TRIMP relationship may not generalize across the full annual calendar. Sampling only Tuesday (GD-4) and Wednesday (GD-3) ([PAGE 2]) aliases the microcycle: Monday is an off-day and Saturday is game-day, neither monitored. Consequently, the pre-practice supine HRV snapshot captures neither the nadir of post-game autonomic suppression nor the recovery supercompensation that may occur later in the week. The study is therefore a cross-sectional slice of a dynamic, recursive process: internal load accumulates across the week, ANS recovery lags that accumulation by 24–72 hours, and the Tuesday–Wednesday observation window cannot disentangle acute session load from cumulative season fatigue. A multi-week trap arises if practitioners interpret this 10-week slice as a stable trait of position group rather than a phase-specific state within a longer season-long trajectory.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Practitioner theory of mind regarding linemen follows a crisis-habit regression loop. Because external load (GPS distance, PlayerLoad) is lowest for BIG players ([PAGE 1], [PAGE 2]), coaches and sport-science staff may habitually regress to the belief that linemen are “low-stress” athletes during periods of organizational crisis—such as a heat-illness scare or a losing streak—redirecting monitoring resources toward skill-position players who accumulate more running distance. This regression is precisely the trap the paper identifies: internal load reveals that BIG players sustain the highest cardiovascular strain ([PAGE 5]) yet demonstrate the lowest ANS recovery ([PAGE 6]), so the habitual external-load mental model is inverted under physiological reality.

The counter-trap is metric capture: once HRV and TRIMP are institutionalized as readiness gatekeepers, the measure becomes the target. Coaches may manipulate practice structure to depress TRIMP or elevate rMSSD without improving actual heat tolerance or cardiovascular resilience, producing Goodhart’s-law dynamics where the dashboard improves while the heat-stroke habitus (high mass, high metabolic heat, low surface-area-to-volume ratio) remains unaddressed. Because caffeine and stimulant use were uncontrolled ([PAGE 3]), acute pharmacological modulation of HRV could further decouple the metric from true autonomic state, making the counter-trap harder to detect.

The clock layer introduces recursive misalignment. Data were collected 60–90 minutes pre-practice ([PAGE 3]), a circadian phase that does not equal overnight recovery or game-day acute stress. Saturday game-load, Sunday light practice, and Monday off-day are unobserved, so the “clock” being measured is a Tuesday–Wednesday microcycle fragment, not the full weekly recovery oscillator. If a staff treats this pre-practice HRV clock as the authoritative season-long readiness signal, they will systematically mis-time recovery interventions, under-loading players who are actually recovered and over-loading linemen who are already in a high-TRIMP, low-HRV danger state. The recursion closes when the monitoring system itself reshapes practice behavior, which then alters the very HRV signal the system was designed to read.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto (mass–thermal layer): BIG players have mean mass \(M = 126.5 \pm 11.3\ \text{kg}\) and body fat \(30.7 \pm 3.5\%\) ([PAGE 4]), producing a surface-area-to-volume ratio that scales approximately as \(M^{-1/3}\). High metabolic heat generation with reduced convective and evaporative dissipation capacity constitutes a physical veto against GPS-only load models: external distance and accelerometer counts cannot capture the isometric, contact-heavy, thermoregulatory load borne by linemen. Any safety protocol that omits HR-based internal load for this subgroup is physically vetoed.

Biomechanical veto (locomotion–contraction layer): PlayerLoad™ integrates running, jumping, changes of direction, and body contacts ([PAGE 2]), but linemen’s work is dominated by short-duration, high-force static engagements against opponents. Accelerometer-based external load under-weights static muscular tension and intramuscular pressure, so PlayerLoad may report low values while myocardial afterload and core-temperature rise remain high. This is a biomechanical veto: low external-load readings do not veto high internal physiological strain.

Weather veto (environment–safety layer): The paper speculates on EHS risk in “a warming world” ([PAGE 6], ref 22) but records no wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), no ambient humidity, and no core temperature ([PAGE 6]). Formal veto rule: if core temperature \(T_c(t)\) and environmental heat stress \(WBGT\) are unmeasured, the causal chain \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{TRIMP}} \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{metabolic heat} \uparrow \Rightarrow T_c \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{EHS}\) is vetoed at the third link. The inference that linemen face elevated practice EHS risk remains epidemiologically supported ([PAGE 2], refs 10–11) but causally unverified within this dataset.

Statistical veto (sample-size layer): COMBO \(n=3\) places the Kruskal-Wallis post-hoc contrasts involving COMBO outside the reliable asymptotic regime. Any claim that COMBO differs from BIG or SKILL on TRIMP, HRV, or RHR is statistically vetoed pending exact permutation testing or Bayesian hierarchical modeling. Similarly, the BIG RHR \(CV \approx 74.8\%\) vetoes the use of \(\mu \pm SD\) as an individual readiness threshold without robust outlier screening.

Adjudication outcome: The only non-vetoed conclusion is that BIG players exhibit higher aggregate TRIMP and lower pre-practice HRV/RHR than SKILL players during Tuesday–Wednesday midseason practices, with the caveat that the effect is observed in a small, unequal, proprietary-AU dataset lacking core-temperature validation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-via-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios:

Scenario 1 — Substitution of GPS PlayerLoad for HR-derived TRIMP in linemen load monitoring.
Veto condition: If the monitoring system replaces chest-strap TRIMP with GPS/accelerometer PlayerLoad as the sole internal-load proxy for BIG players, the intervention is vetoed because linemen generate high cardiovascular strain with low locomotive displacement ([PAGE 1], [PAGE 2]). Verifiable prediction: aggregate PlayerLoad for BIG will remain numerically lower than for SKILL (e.g., 389.8±47.0 AU vs. 523.9±76.9 AU from prior work cited on [PAGE 2]), while TRIMP remains higher (135.0±61.0 AU vs. 101.0±53.1 AU; [PAGE 5]). The internal–external dissociation (Kruskal-Wallis H(2)=23.25, p<0.001) will persist, and EHS-risk alerts derived solely from PlayerLoad will produce false negatives for linemen.

Scenario 2 — Inference on COMBO group differences using the reported Kruskal-Wallis asymptotic p-values.
Veto condition: If a counterfactual policy assigns recovery resources based on COMBO-vs-BIG or COMBO-vs-SKILL significance claims, the inference is vetoed because COMBO n=3 invalidates the chi-square approximation for H. Verifiable prediction: an exact permutation test on the TRIMP and HRV data will yield COMBO pairwise p-values > 0.05, and the observed moderate RHR effect size for BIG vs. COMBO (ES = 0.89; [PAGE 6]) will collapse toward the null or become non-replicable under resampling. Any resource allocation predicated on COMBO statistical separation is therefore unverifiable.

Scenario 3 — Real-time readiness intervention triggered by single pre-practice supine rMSSD without caffeine/stimulant control.
Veto condition: If an automated system flags individual linemen as “unrecovered” based on one 3-minute supine rMSSD reading below a population threshold, the intervention is vetoed by measurement nonstationarity and unconfounded stimulant use ([PAGE 3]). Verifiable prediction: given BIG RHR SD = 48.6 bpm on mean 65.0 bpm (CV ≈ 74.8%; [PAGE 6]), the false-alarm rate for individual readiness flags will exceed 50% under Gaussian assumptions, and repeated measures will show high within-subject variance unrelated to training load. The system will regress to the mean rather than track true autonomic recovery, and interventions triggered by it will not improve subsequent TRIMP or HRV outcomes.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
