<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a random-effects meta-analysis, not a single experiment. The inferential chain is: (1) define honesty as a binary outcome in Sender-Receiver game treatments; (2) estimate a study-level gender coefficient by logit regression; (3) pool study-level coefficients by inverse-variance random-effects meta-analysis; (4) test whether the pooled gender coefficient is moderated by lie type; and (5) interpret the sign and magnitude under social-preference and deontological accounts. The sign convention is crucial: positive pooled coefficients mean females are more honest, equivalently males are more likely to lie. The abstract states the headline result exactly: “males are significantly more likely than females to tell black lies (N=4,173); males are significantly more likely than females to tell altruistic white (N=2,940); and results are inconclusive in the case of Pareto white lies (N=1,615).” [PAGE 1]. The overall pooled coefficient is positive: “females are more honest than males (eﬀect size = 0.271, 95% CI = [0.172,0.370], Z = 5.37, p &lt; 0.001)” [PAGE 3]. Black-lie and altruistic-white-lie subsets are also positive, while Pareto white lies are only marginal: “males are almost signiﬁcantly more dishonest than females, when I do not control for age and level of education (eﬀect size = 0.222, 95% CI = [−0.012, 0.457], Z = 1.86, p = 0.063)” [PAGE 7]. The gray-zone audit is that the meta-analysis estimates logit-scale coefficients, so the reported “eﬀect size” is not a raw percentage-point difference; the raw percentages are auxiliary. For black lies: “On average, 36% of males versus 44% of females are honest” [PAGE 5]. For altruistic white lies: “On average, 76% of males versus 83% of females acts honestly” [PAGE 6]. For Pareto white lies: “On average, 26% of males versus 29% of females acts honestly” [PAGE 7]. Thus the pooled logit coefficients and the raw percentages point in the same direction, but the former are adjusted and study-weighted. Another gray zone is dependence: 36 of 65 treatments come from the author’s group, and the Pareto subset is entirely from that group: “I analyze N = 1,615 distinct observations, in 8 experimental conditions, all by my research group” [PAGE 7]. The source controls for research group in a meta-regression and reports “the eﬀect of lie type is robust (p = 0.035)” [PAGE 5], but a group-control fixed effect is not the same as a multilevel model with group random intercepts. The moderation evidence is also near conventional thresholds: lie-type p = 0.049 before group control, p = 0.035 after; Pareto-versus-altruistic pairwise p = .045/.032; black-versus-altruistic is described as highly significant. The Pareto subset has limited power: the source reports “Power analysis shows that to detect the overall eﬀect with power 0.9 at a 5% level one needs a sample of size N=2,103” [PAGE 9], while observed Pareto N is 1,615. The source also handles a logit singularity: “Logit regression applied to single studies has the limitation that, if the dependent variable can be perfectly predicted, it returns no coeﬃcient” and “This happens rarely in these dataset (3 studies over 65). When this happens, I do a correction by adding one data point by hand in such a way to maintain the sign of the eﬀect” [PAGE 3]. That correction is inference-preserving by construction, so it is a legitimate sensitivity target. Finally, there are no spiteful-lie observations: the total includes “0 in spiteful lies conditions” [PAGE 3], so no gender estimate exists for that cell. The meta-analysis therefore supports a directional claim for black and altruistic white lies, a weaker and inconclusive claim for Pareto white lies, and no claim for spiteful lies.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(H_i\in\{0,1\}\) be the honesty indicator for subject \(i\), and let \(G_i\in\{0,1\}\) be the source’s gender indicator, with the coding convention such that a positive coefficient means greater female honesty. The study-level operator is a logit regression:
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(H_i=1\mid G_i,X_i)=\alpha+\beta_G G_i+\gamma^\top X_i,
\qquad
\operatorname{logit}(p)=\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right).
\]
Here \(\beta_G\) is in natural-logit units per unit change in \(G_i\); its standard error has the same units. \(X_i\) includes age and, when known, education. The source reports pooled age and education coefficients on the logit scale: “age: coeﬀ= 0.005, z = 2.40, p = 0.016; education: coeﬀ= −0.081, z = −3.90, p&lt;.001” [PAGE 3]. The random-effects pooling operator is:
\[
\hat\theta=\frac{\sum_i w_i\hat\theta_i}{\sum_i w_i},
\qquad
w_i=\frac{1}{\tau^2+v_i},
\]
where \(\hat\theta_i\) is a study-level logit coefficient, \(v_i\) its sampling variance in logit-squared units, and \(\tau^2\) the between-study variance in logit-squared units. The source states the overall pooled result as “eﬀect size = 0.271, 95% CI = [0.172,0.370], Z = 5.37, p &lt; 0.001” [PAGE 3]. Heterogeneity is quantified by \(I^2\), a percentage of variability not explained by sampling error, and by \(\tau^2\); the source reports “no evidence of heterogeneity across studies in the true size of this eﬀect (without control: p = 0.553; with control: p = 0.631)” [PAGE 3]. The lie-type moderator operator is a meta-regression:
\[
\hat\theta_i=\beta_0+\beta_A A_i+\beta_B B_i+\beta_R^\top R_i+\epsilon_i,
\]
where \(A_i\) and \(B_i\) are indicator variables for altruistic white lies and black lies, with Pareto white lies as baseline in the source’s pairwise analysis, and \(R_i\) are research-group indicators. The source reports “The mean eﬀect sizes for the three lie types are .19 for black lies, .47 for altruistic white lies, and .22 for Pareto white lies” [PAGE 5]. Subset estimates are black-lie “eﬀect size = 0.186, 95% CI = [0.053,0.319], Z = 2.73, p = 0.006” [PAGE 5]; altruistic-white-lie “eﬀect size = 0.469, 95% CI = [0.256,0.681], Z = 4.33, p &lt; 0.001” [PAGE 6]; and Pareto “eﬀect size = 0.222, 95% CI = [−0.012, 0.457], Z = 1.86, p = 0.063” without controls [PAGE 7]. The source’s power statements are dimensionless design quantities: overall \(N=3,091\) [PAGE 5], altruistic white lies \(N=728\) [PAGE 6], and Pareto white lies \(N=2,103\) [PAGE 9], all for power 0.9 at a 5% significance level. Exact breakdown regimes are: (i) perfect prediction in a study-level logit yields no coefficient; the source performs a sign-preserving one-observation correction in 3 of 65 studies [PAGE 3]; (ii) zero spiteful-lie observations produce no estimate [PAGE 3]; (iii) sparse Pareto cells and a small number of Pareto studies weaken the moderation inference; (iv) meta-regression coefficients can be unstable when research-group indicators are included with few studies per group; and (v) Egger and Begg tests do not prove absence of publication bias, they only fail to reject it: “Egger’s test: without control: t = −0.99, p = 0.328; with control: t = −0.67, p = 0.507; Begg’s test: without control: z = −0.62, p = 0.533; with control: z = −0.44, p = 0.659” [PAGE 5].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage is clear: Gneezy (2005) made consequences central to deception; Dreber and Johannesson (2008) supplied the early black-lie gender claim; Erat and Gneezy (2012) introduced the white-lie taxonomy and reported opposite-direction white-lie results; Cappelen et al. (2013) and Biziou-van-Pol et al. (2015) failed to replicate parts of that pattern; and Capraro’s 2018 meta-analysis reweights the whole lineage. The source explicitly records the mixed ancestry: “An early paper by Dreber and Johannesson (2008) found that males lie more than females, at least in the domain of black lies” but “This result was successfully replicated in some studies ... but not in others” [PAGE 1]. It then records the reversal problem: Erat and Gneezy found “males lie more than females in the context of Pareto white lies” but “females lie more than males in the context of altruistic white lies,” while Cappelen et al. found no Pareto difference and Biziou-van-Pol et al. found “the opposite, that males tell more altruistic white lies than females” [PAGE 1]. The meta-analysis is therefore a retrospective adjudicator of earlier local findings. Its most striking retrocausal move is the treatment of Erat and Gneezy (2012): after estimating the coefficient from reported results, the source says “This suggests that the original ﬁnding by Erat and Gneezy (2012) might have been a false positive” [PAGE 9]. That is not a mere literature summary; it reinterprets an influential prior as a possible sampling artifact. The long-horizon traps are multi-week and multi-stage. First, the data collection spanned a first draft and a revision: the author announced the plan in April 2016, posted a first draft on SSRN on March 11, 2017, left it online for almost one year, and revised in January 2018 [PAGE 3]. This creates a temporal window in which inclusion decisions, unpublished data, and referee comments can interact. Second, file-drawer control is attempted but not guaranteed: “To minimize ﬁle-drawer eﬀects, I included in the meta-analysis also unpublished studies” [PAGE 3]. That reduces but does not eliminate selection. Third, the author’s own group contributes 36 of 65 treatments, so a multi-week revision process could unintentionally overweight internally generated conditions. Fourth, the source estimates rather than obtains raw data from two key contrary studies: Erat and Gneezy supplied no exact data, and Cappelen et al. did not respond, so the source uses reported percentages [PAGE 9]. Fifth, the final attempted data call produced no additional data: “However, I did not receive any more data this time” [PAGE 3]. The genealogical conclusion is that the meta-analysis is best read as a cumulative correction to a fragile early literature, not as a final arbitration. The robust retrocausal claim is narrow: “males are more likely than females to tell black lies, and males are more likely than females to tell altruistic white lies (at least when lying is socially eﬃcient)” [PAGE 9].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The Sender-Receiver game is a recursive mentalizing task. Player 1 knows the payoff allocations and sends a message about private information; Player 2 may guess the truth. In the standard game, a sender can tell the truth for strategic reasons if the sender expects the receiver to disbelieve the message. The source names this counter-trap explicitly: “sophisticated deception (i.e., Player 1 telling the truth because he or she expects that Player 2 will not believe him or her, Sutter 2009)” [PAGE 2]. This is a second-order belief problem: the sender’s action depends on the sender’s model of the receiver’s model of the sender. A simple threshold representation is:
\[
L_i^*=1
\iff
u_i(L=1)-u_i(L=0)>c_i,
\]
where \(L_i^*\) is the lie decision, \(u_i\) is utility in experimental currency units, and \(c_i\) is an intrinsic lying cost in the same units. In the no-choice variant used to suppress sophisticated deception, the receiver’s action is deterministic: “Player 2 has no active choice: whether participants are paid according to Option A or Option B depends only on whether Player 1 decides to lie or to tell the truth” [PAGE 2]. That collapses the recursive belief chain. The sender no longer benefits from being disbelieved, so truth-telling cannot be a counter-trap. The meta-analysis therefore estimates a cleaner social-preference or moral-cost contrast, not a full strategic-mentalizing contrast. “Crisis habit regression” would mean that under cognitive load, time pressure, or crisis, behavior falls back to a habitual default — either habitual honesty or habitual self-interest — rather than running the full recursive model. The source does not manipulate crisis, load, or time pressure in the meta-analysis, so it cannot adjudicate habit regression. The “clock” layer appears only in cited adjacent work: “The morning morality eﬀect: The inﬂuence of time of day on unethical behavior” [PAGE 11] and “The Dark Triad of personality and unethical behavior at diﬀerent times of day” [PAGE 11]. Those are time-of-day effects, not time-pressure effects, and neither is a moderator in the meta-regression. The recursive-theory conclusion is therefore conditional: the pooled gender contrast is interpretable as a lie-cost or social-preference contrast only to the extent that the no-choice variant and the included conditions suppress sophisticated deception. If some included studies retained an active receiver, the estimated gender coefficient could mix preference with recursive belief. The source’s own robustness checks do not separate those channels.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical, biomechanical, or weather veto would require measured physical variables, biomechanical constraints, or meteorological covariates that can plausibly override the social-cognitive mechanism. This source contains none. It has gender, honesty, lie type, age, education, research group, and study identifiers. The only temporal layer is age and education controls [PAGE 3] plus cited time-of-day literature [PAGE 11]. There is no ambient temperature, humidity, barometric pressure, sleep, circadian phase, physiological load, motor constraint, or biomechanical measurement in the model. Therefore: physical veto = not applicable; biomechanical veto = not applicable; weather veto = not adjudicable. The correct cross-layer statement is not that such layers are absent in reality, but that this meta-analysis cannot test them. If a weather or biomechanical variable were added and absorbed the gender coefficient, the source’s social-preference interpretation would be vetoed; if it did not, the social-cognitive interpretation would survive that particular challenge. The source provides no such test. Its nearest “clock” evidence is external citation, not internal moderator evidence: “The morning morality eﬀect: The inﬂuence of time of day on unethical behavior” [PAGE 11] and “The Dark Triad of personality and unethical behavior at diﬀerent times of day” [PAGE 11]. Those citations indicate that time-of-day effects exist in adjacent paradigms, but the meta-analysis does not estimate them. A cross-layer veto proof is therefore unavailable; the only defensible adjudication is a scope restriction: the reported gender effects are social-cognitive and payoff-structural, not physical or meteorological.
