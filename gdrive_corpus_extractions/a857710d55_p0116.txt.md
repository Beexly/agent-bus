x = x₃: tangent passes through origin, ξ(x₃) = 0;
x > x₃: tangent cuts positive Y axis, η(x) > 0 ⇒ MP(x) < AP(x).

Concavity (decreasing increments) condition [PAGE 1]:
f″(x) < 0 for x ∈ (x₃, x₄).

Knight’s non-a-priori extension [PAGE 1]:
It is reasonable but not demonstrably necessary that f″(x) < 0 hold on the whole interval (x₃, x₄).

Indeterminacy condition (dotted-line case) [PAGE 1]:
∃ (a,b) ⊂ (x₃,x₄) such that f″(x) changes sign or f′ is non-monotonic ⇒ competitive situation indeterminate on (a,b).

Zero-output boundary:
∃ x₀ > x₄ such that f(x₀) = 0; operation for x > x₀ yields no physical output.

Assumptions: fixed proportions of other factors; variable agent obtainable at a uniform price; fixed factors divisible; size of individual establishments nearly a matter of indifference [PAGE 1, footnote].

Breakdown regimes: (i) indivisible capital prevents discarding “a portion of the other factors” [PAGE 1]; (ii) local convexity on (3,4) voids the diminishing-returns graphic condition; (iii) exogenous shocks (weather, input-quality shifts) move x₃ and x₄, invalidating a fixed input plan. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The geometry on [PAGE 1] sits inside a long lineage of marginal productivity theory. The idea that “diminishing returns begin” at a tangency to a line through the origin echoes Turgot’s agricultural diminishing-returns logic and was formalized by Marshall and Wicksell in the late nineteenth century. The condition MP = AP at x₃ is the static factor-proportion analogue of the neoclassical optimal-input rule; under homogeneous functions it connects to Euler’s theorem and the adding-up problem of distribution. Knight’s footnote—that establishment size must be “nearly a matter of indifference” [PAGE 1]—is precisely the assumption later targeted by Sraffa’s 1926 critique of the Marshallian representative firm, where indivisibilities and external economies destroy the presumption that a unique, scalable optimum exists.

The “dotted line” interval between points (3) and (4) [PAGE 1] anticipates modern non-convex production sets. If f″ fails to remain negative over that interval, the competitive input choice is indeterminate, which in general-equilibrium terms admits multiple equilibria or sunspot-driven coordination failures. A multi-week empirical trap is to estimate a single concave production function from weekly input-output observations and then treat x₃ as a permanent target ratio. If the true technology has a local convex pocket inside (3,4), the estimated “optimum” will shift as input prices change, and firms may repeatedly cycle around the indeterminate zone rather than settle at a stable proportion. Another trap is to treat the tangency point as a long-run equilibrium when Knight’s argument is explicitly short-run: other factors are fixed, establishment size is assumed indifferent, and the variable agent is priced uniformly. Relax any one of these, and the genealogical link from point (3) to a competitive equilibrium breaks down.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under volatile input prices, a firm’s managerial habit regresses to the factor combination that seemed stable before the crisis. If that habitual combination lies below x₃, the firm operates where AP is still rising but fixed factors are underutilized—precisely the region Knight calls unintelligent even if the variable agent is free [PAGE 1]. The firm’s belief is “we survived last crisis with this mix,” but the recursive flaw is that it ignores the possibility of discarding fixed factors to raise output.

Counter-trap: observing that MP is still positive beyond x₄, a manager may extrapolate “more variable input means more output” and push x toward the X-axis intersection x₀ where f(x₀) = 0 [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is convex: the firm mistakes the declining portion of the total product curve for a still-productive region because the tangent looks flat locally, thereby overshooting the maximum point (4) and entering congestion.

Clock: the economically relevant clock is the input–output price ratio, p_x/p_y, which determines how far along the curve the firm should move. The capital-adjustment clock, by contrast, is slow because fixed factors cannot be discarded continuously [PAGE 1, footnote]. When the price clock moves faster than the adjustment clock, the firm is stranded in Knight’s indeterminate interval (3,4) [PAGE 1], where competitive outcomes are not pinned down by concavity alone. Multi-agent recursion enters because each firm’s input choice affects market output and thus p_x; if all firms assume concavity where the dotted-line case obtains [PAGE 1], their mutual best-response functions intersect multiple times, producing recurring coordination failures rather than a unique steady state.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: Knight’s claim that below x₃ output can be raised by “discarding a portion of the other factors” [PAGE 1] presupposes continuously divisible fixed capital. In plants with lumpy machinery—single boilers, indivisible presses, minimum-scale reactors—no portion can be discarded. This is a hard physical veto: the x < x₃ region is not merely suboptimal but physically inaccessible to smooth contraction, so the firm must either idle the variable agent or shut down rather than operate at Knight’s irrational low-x points.

Biomechanical/operational layer: human-machine systems exhibit fatigue and congestion. As x rises past x₄, MP turns negative and the curve approaches the X-axis intersection [PAGE 1]. A biomechanical veto triggers when operator error rates or machine jam probabilities rise nonlinearly, effectively shifting f(x) downward and moving x₄ leftward. A staffing plan calibrated to normal conditions becomes irrational under overload because total output collapses toward zero.

Weather veto: f(x; 𝜃) depends on an environmental parameter 𝜃 (temperature, humidity, precipitation). Adverse weather rotates the total product curve downward and leftward, reducing both x₃ and x₄ [PAGE 1 geometry]. A contract fixing x at the normal-weather tangency may place the firm either below the new x₃ (wasted fixed factors) or beyond the new x₄ (negative marginal returns).

Veto adjudication hierarchy: the establishment-size indifference assumption [PAGE 1, footnote] is the meta-veto. If it fails, the physical veto is activated automatically, and Knight’s graphic condition—that tangency to a line through the origin defines the start of diminishing returns—ceases to be a usable decision rule for that industry.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Indivisible capital veto. Counterfactual: a firm faces a free variable agent x and considers cutting employment below x₃. Veto: the fixed factor is one indivisible machine that cannot be partially discarded [PAGE 1 footnote]. Verifiable prediction: employment will bunch at the minimum operating scale rather than continuously contracting toward x₃; output per worker will not rise after layoffs because the machine remains fully deployed, falsifying Knight’s “discard fixed factors” mechanism.

Scenario 2 — Concavity failure on (3,4). Counterfactual: two identical plants face identical input and output prices. Veto: the true production function has a local convex pocket inside Knight’s (3,4) interval, so f″ is not negative throughout [PAGE 1]. Verifiable prediction: the two plants select different, stable factor proportions within (3,4) and maintain them over multiple weeks; aggregate industry input ratios display excess dispersion relative to price changes, confirming competitive indeterminacy in that interval.

Scenario 3 — Weather-shift veto on x₃. Counterfactual: a processing plant operates under a labor contract negotiated at normal weather θ₀. Veto: an adverse weather shock θ₁ shifts the total product curve leftward, lowering x₃ and x₄ [PAGE 1]. Verifiable prediction: within one week of the shock, output per labor-hour falls by more than the proportional weather effect; plants with flexible input clauses renegotiate labor downward, while plants with fixed contracts operate beyond the new x₄ and register declining total output despite unchanged labor input.
