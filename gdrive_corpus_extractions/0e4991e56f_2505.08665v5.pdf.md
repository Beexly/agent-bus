The paper’s core classification operator is a map from synchronized views to a four-class probability simplex:
\[
f_\theta:\mathbb{R}^{V\times T\times H\times W\times 3}\to \Delta^{3},\qquad
A=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left\{\arg\max_{c\in\{0,1,2,3\}}p_\theta(c\mid x_i)=y_i\right\},
\]
where \(A\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless and is reported as \(100A\%\) accuracy. The assumption is i.i.d. sampling from the official held-out validation split, with no multi-crop testing; the paper states “we do not apply multi-crop testing” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: \(N=0\) makes \(A\) undefined; severe class imbalance makes accuracy dominated by majority classes; label noise creates an irreducible Bayes ceiling. Cross-entropy, if used for optimization, is
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=0}^{3}y_{i,c}\ln p_\theta(c\mid x_i),
\]
with units of nats per sample. It is not a percentage and cannot be compared directly with accuracy.

CrossViewFusion receives view-specific features \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times V\times d}\), where the paper states “B is batch size, V is number of views, and d = 768” [PAGE 3]. Per-view layer normalization is applied first, then multi-head cross-attention:
\[
X_{\mathrm{norm}}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}(X),\qquad
Q=X_{\mathrm{norm}}W_Q,\quad K=X_{\mathrm{norm}}W_K,\quad V_{\mathrm{val}}=X_{\mathrm{norm}}W_V,
\]
\[
X_{\mathrm{attn}}
=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right)V_{\mathrm{val}},
\qquad
d_h=\frac{d}{H}=\frac{768}{16}=48,
\]
because Table 2 fixes “attention heads = 16” [PAGE 6]. The attention score matrix has shape \(B\times H\times V\times V\), so complexity is \(O(BV^2d_hH)=O(BV^2d)\). With \(V\le5\), this is small, but the singularity regimes matter: \(V=0\) is undefined; \(H=0\) is undefined; and if the softmax logits become very large, attention saturates toward one-hot weights and gradients vanish. View aggregation is mean pooling:
\[
h=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}X_{\mathrm{attn}}[:,v,:].
\]
If views are redundant or anti-correlated, mean pooling can cancel signal. If \(V=0\), the operation is undefined. The feed-forward and gating operators are
\[
h_{\mathrm{proj1}}=\mathrm{Linear}_1(h),\qquad
h_{\mathrm{hidden}}=\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\mathrm{GELU}(h_{\mathrm{proj1}}))),
\]
\[
g=\sigma(\mathrm{Linear}_{\mathrm{gate}}(h_{\mathrm{hidden}})),\qquad
h_{\mathrm{gated}}=g\odot h_{\mathrm{hidden}},
\]
where \(g\in(0,1)^d\). The logistic derivative is \(\partial g/\partial z=g(1-g)\). If \(|z|\gg1\), then \(g\to0\) or \(g\to1\) and \(\partial g/\partial z\to0\); this is gate saturation. If \(g\approx0\) everywhere, features are suppressed; if \(g\approx1\) everywhere, the gate is effectively inactive. The paper describes the gate as a “dynamic filter to selectively amplify informative features while suppressing irrelevant ones” [PAGE 4].

The final projection and adaptive self-calibration are
\[
h_{\mathrm{proj2}}=\mathrm{Linear}_2(h_{\mathrm{gated}}),\qquad
h_{\mathrm{norm}}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}(h_{\mathrm{proj2}}),
\]
\[
h_{\mathrm{centered}}=h_{\mathrm{norm}}-\mathrm{mean}(h_{\mathrm{norm}},\dim=-1),\qquad
h_{\mathrm{scaled}}=\frac{h_{\mathrm{centered}}}{\mathrm{std}(h_{\mathrm{norm}},\dim=-1)+\epsilon},
\]
\[
h_{\mathrm{final}}=h_{\mathrm{scaled}}\odot\sigma_{\mathrm{learn}}+\mu_{\mathrm{learn}}.
\]
Here \(\sigma_{\mathrm{learn}}\) is an affine scale parameter, not the logistic sigmoid. The paper says “we learn per-feature affine parameters to reshape feature distributions in a data-driven manner” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\mathrm{std}(h_{\mathrm{norm}})\to0\), the denominator is dominated by \(\epsilon\), so the normalized signal collapses toward zero or amplifies numerical noise; if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{learn}}=0\), the corresponding feature is killed; if \(\sigma_{\mathrm{learn}}\) is negative, the feature polarity flips. The mean and standard deviation are computed over the feature dimension \(\dim=-1\), not over the batch, so a single sample with nearly constant features can be poorly calibrated.

LoRA adaptation is
\[
W=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA,
\qquad
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\quad B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},
\]
with trainable parameter count per adapted matrix \(r(d_{\mathrm{in}}+d_{\mathrm{out}})\) parameters. \]
If \(F=1\), interpolation is degenerate; if \(F=0\), undefined; if the action event duration is shorter than \(\Delta t\), aliasing occurs. The paper admits “Fixed frame sampling may miss critical temporal cues in variable activities” [PAGE 7].

A second trap is the “up to 16.4%” wording. The paper writes “by 16.4% (from 40.8% to 47.5%)” [PAGE 1]; the absolute gain is 6.7 percentage points, and \(47.5/40.8-1=0.1642\) is relative. The same is true for “14% (from 40.6% to 46.3%)” [PAGE 1]: relative 14.0%, absolute 5.7 percentage points. A third trap is the class prior. The paper warns that the “proficiency label distribution is notably imbalanced, skewed toward intermediate and late experts” [PAGE 5], yet overall accuracy is the headline metric. A fourth trap is per-domain selection: Basketball 77.88% and Cooking 60.53% are prominent [PAGE 1][PAGE 6], while Dancing 13.68% is below the majority baseline 51.61% [PAGE 6]. A fifth trap is the scaling confound: frames, LoRA rank, alpha, hidden width, and learning rate all change with view count [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. A sixth trap is temporal resolution: fixed frame sampling is acknowledged as a limitation [PAGE 7]. These traps persist over multi-week research cycles because each looks like a small implementation detail until it reverses the claimed causal story.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind here means that the model is not merely predicting a physical action; it is predicting an evaluator’s latent judgment of expertise. Let \(y\) be the latent proficiency level, \(z\) the human label, and \(x\) the multi-view observation. The learned distribution \(p_\theta(y\mid x)\) is trained against \(z\), but \(z\) is itself generated by an evaluator’s criteria, including task norms, stylistic expectations, and label noise. If those criteria are weakly observable, the model’s implicit “theory” of the evaluator collapses to visual salience rather than skill. That is the crisis-habit regression: under class imbalance and subjective domains, the model regresses to habits that are statistically convenient rather than diagnostically valid. The paper’s warning about imbalance toward intermediate and late experts [PAGE 5] is exactly the condition under which prior collapse can masquerade as skill recognition. Dancing is the clearest counterexample: Ego+Exos accuracy 13.68% is far below the majority baseline 51.61% and below the Ego-only 20.51% [PAGE 6]. The model is not simply predicting the majority; it is failing to represent the evaluator’s subjective criterion.

The counter-trap is the assumption that more views are monotonically better. Bouldering shows Exos-only 33.52% beating Ego+Exos 31.87% [PAGE 6], and Dancing shows Ego+Exos 13.68% below Ego-only 20.51% [PAGE 6]. Cross-view attention and mean pooling can amplify redundant or conflicting cues, and the gate may suppress or pass the wrong view. The clock is the temporal budget: as views increase, the authors reduce frames from 32 to 16 [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. The model’s “clock” is therefore not constant across configurations. A multi-view gain can be a temporal-resolution loss, a fusion gain, or a LoRA-capacity effect. A rigorous recursive-ToM evaluation would report view-dropout, gate entropy, and fixed-token-budget ablations; otherwise the model’s apparent perspective-taking is confounded with its sampling clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer vetoes architectural claims when the signal is not observable. The paper uses “center-cropped to 224 × 224” video [PAGE 4], one egocentric Aria view and up to four static GoPro exocentric views [PAGE 4]. If a relevant hand-object interaction subtends too few pixels, if occlusion removes the action, or if synchronization error exceeds the action’s characteristic time, then no fusion operator can create the missing information. Formally, if the observed mutual information satisfies
\[
I(Y;X_{\mathrm{obs}})\le I(Y;X_{\mathrm{ideal}})-\delta
\]
in nats, and the Bayes accuracy falls below the label-noise ceiling,
\[
A^{*}\le 1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{label}},
\]
then the physical layer vetoes any claim that a better fusion module alone can recover the lost skill signal. The paper’s own limitation that fixed sampling “may miss critical temporal cues” [PAGE 7] is a partial admission of this veto.

The biomechanical layer vetoes semantic claims when kinematic distributions overlap across expertise levels. Let \(q(t)\) denote joint kinematics or full-body motion. If
\[
p(q\mid y=c)\approx p(q\mid y=c')
\]
for adjacent expertise levels \(c\ne c'\), then the Bayes classifier cannot separate them reliably regardless of attention depth. The paper states that SkillFormer “lacks semantic understanding of skill components, limiting interpretability” [PAGE 7]. That is a direct admission that the model is not a biomechanical evaluator; it is a learned visual correlate. Therefore a claim that SkillFormer “understands” proficiency is vetoed by the biomechanical layer unless pose, force, or expert-annotated skill components are added.

The weather/environment layer vetoes generalization claims when deployment conditions are outside the training distribution. The supplied pages mention “123 real-world environments” [PAGE 4], but they do not report weather strata, illuminance, wind, or precipitation. If training distribution is \(P_S\) and deployment is \(P_T\), and
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_T\parallel P_S)>\tau
\]
in nats with no adaptation, then benchmark accuracy does not transfer. Weather variables would need SI units such as illuminance in lux, wind speed in m/s, and precipitation in mm/h. Because the paper provides no weather-conditioned evaluation, any weather-robustness claim is vetoed. The adjudication order is physical observability first, biomechanical identifiability second, model architecture third, and weather/OOD transfer last. The reported EgoExo4D numbers satisfy none of the cross-layer veto proofs for open-world deployment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
