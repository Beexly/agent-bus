<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s estimand is not the observed survivor curve. Let \(A\) be age in years, \(Y\) be the arcsine-transformed OPS (dimensionless), and \(S=1\) when a player-season is observed under the paper’s rule of at least 100 plate appearances; let \(S=0\) when it is treated as missing. The target is the counterfactual mean \(\mu(a)=\mathbb{E}[Y(a)]\) under the question: “What would the aging curve look like if players competed in every season from a fixed range of age?” [PAGE 2]. The complete-case curve estimates \(\mu_{CC}(a)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid A=a,S=1]\), not \(\mu(a)\). The selection-bias identity is
\[
\mu_{CC}(a)-\mu(a)=\{\mathbb{E}[Y\mid A=a,S=1]-\mathbb{E}[Y\mid A=a,S=0]\}\,\Pr(S=0\mid A=a).
\]
The second conditional mean is unobserved. If the missing player-seasons would have had lower \(Y\), the complete-case curve is biased upward; this is the paper’s warning that “only players that are good enough to remain are observed” and that this “very likely results in an overestimation in the construction of aging curves” [PAGE 1]. But the direction is not universal: the literature review notes that Judge (2020a) “concludes that survivorship bias leads to an underestimation, not overestimation, of the aging curves” [PAGE 3]. The paper’s simulation resolves the sign for its own dropout mechanisms. Random retirement of 25% at age 30 is MCAR and nearly overlaps the original curve, with “MAE = 7.42 × 10−4”; dropout based on a 4-year OPS average below 0.55 gives “MAE = 0.031 for (1)”; early-career OPS average below 0.55 gives “MAE = 0.019 for (2)” [PAGE 5]. Thus the missingness mechanism, not the word “survival” by itself, drives the bias.

The repair is multiple imputation. The paper’s logic is standard Rubin: create \(m\) completed datasets, analyze each, and pool with within- and between-imputation variance [PAGE 4]. The key gray zone is the imputation model. The paper uses “multiple imputation by a two-level normal linear mixed model with heterogeneous within-group variance” [PAGE 4], operationalized as the 2l.norm method in mice [PAGE 5]. Yet the discussion admits: “In our current imputation model, age is the only predictor for estimating performance” [PAGE 8]. That admission matters because dropout cases (1) and (2) are called MAR “since players get removed due to low previously observed performance” [PAGE 5]. MAR is conditional on the observed variables that drive missingness; if prior OPS drives dropout but only age enters the imputation model, the MAR condition is not fully operationalized. The simulation still reports a combined-curve “MAE of 0.0039” for dropout case (2) [PAGE 6], so age carries substantial information in that simulated design, but that is not a general proof that age-only imputation is sufficient for real MLB data. The real-data result is that “the combined imputed curve is lower than the curve obtained when ignoring the missing data” and “the aging effect is overestimated without considering the unobserved player-seasons that are observable” [PAGE 7]. That is the paper’s central empirical claim: complete-case aging curves overstate performance at older ages relative to the imputed curve. The audit conclusion is therefore: the paper demonstrates a useful MI correction under its simulation, but the causal and real-data interpretation is conditional on MAR, correct multilevel specification, convergence, and no omitted dropout predictors.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core simulation model is a player-level random-intercept cubic age model:
\[
Y_{pq}=(\beta_0+b_{0p})+\beta_1 X_q+\beta_2 X_q^2+\beta_3 X_q^3+\epsilon_{pq},
\]
\[
b_{0p}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2),\qquad \epsilon_{pq}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2).
\]
Here \(Y_{pq}\) is transformed OPS for player \(p\) at age/season \(q\), \(X_q\) is age in years, \(\beta\) are fixed effects, \(b_{0p}\) is a player random intercept, \(\tau^2\) is the between-player variance, and \(\sigma^2\) is the observation-level variance [PAGE 5]. The response is dimensionless transformed OPS; variances are in squared transformed-OPS units; age is in years. The paper scales OPS and applies “an arcsine transformation” [PAGE 3], restricts careers to “age 21 to 39,” and treats a season as missing if plate appearances fall below 100 [PAGE 3].

Rubin’s combining rules are given as follows. For parameter \(Q\) with \(m\) estimates \(\hat Q_i\) and estimated sampling variances \(\hat U_i\),
\[
\bar Q=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat Q_i,
\]
\[
T=\bar U+\left(1+\frac{1}{m}\right)B,
\]
\[
\bar U=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat U_i,
\]
\[
B=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat Q_i-\bar Q)^2.
\]
Inference uses
\[
\frac{Q-\bar Q}{\sqrt{T}}\sim t_\nu,
\]
with
\[
\nu=(m-1)\left(1+\frac{1}{r}\right)^2,\qquad
r=\left(1+\frac{1}{m}\right)\frac{B}{\bar U}.
\]
The Wald interval is
\[
\bar Q\pm t_{\nu,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{T}.
\]
These are exactly the paper’s displayed equations [PAGE 4]. Units: \(\bar Q\) is in transformed-OPS units; \(\bar U\) and \(B\) are in squared transformed-OPS units; \(T\) is in squared transformed-OPS units; \(r\) and \(\nu\) are dimensionless; the interval is in transformed-OPS units. The assumptions are MAR, a correctly specified imputation model, valid multilevel normal linear mixed structure with heterogeneous within-group variance [PAGE 4], adequate convergence of MICE [PAGE 6], and \(m\) large enough for stable between-imputation variance. The paper uses “m = 5 imputations with each made up of 30 iterations” [PAGE 6].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are exact and important. If \(\tau^2\to 0\), the player random intercept collapses and the model becomes effectively single-level; the 2l.norm structure loses its player-level variance component. If \(\sigma^2\to 0\), the model becomes deterministic conditional on age and random effect, so imputation uncertainty is understated. If \(\bar U\to 0\), \(r\) is undefined because of division by zero; if \(B\to 0\), then \(r\to 0\) and the \(t_\nu\) limit approaches normality, but the displayed formula for \(\nu\) is undefined at exactly \(r=0\). If \(m=1\), \(B\) is undefined and single imputation cannot represent between-imputation variance. If a player has only one observed season, the within-player variance for heterogeneous within-group variance is not estimable. If all player-seasons at an age are missing, there is no direct support and imputation is extrapolation. If missingness is MNAR—defined as “a link between the missingness and the unobserved values” [PAGE 4]—the MI model is invalid unless the MNAR mechanism is modeled. If the imputation model includes only age while dropout depends on prior OPS, the MAR condition is not fully guaranteed; the paper itself flags that “age is the only predictor” [PAGE 8]. The simulation dropout definitions are: 4-year OPS average below 0.55; OPS average from ages 21 to 25 below 0.55; and 25% random retirement at age 30 [PAGE 5]. The first two are classified MAR and the third MCAR [PAGE 5]. The application restricts to players debuting no sooner than 1985, yielding 2323 players [PAGE 6].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from parametric curve-fitting to missing-data correction. Morris (1983) used parametric empirical Bayes and shrinkage to obtain a parabolic Ty Cobb batting-average trajectory; Albert (1992) proposed a quadratic random-effects log-linear model for home-run rates; Berry et al. (1999) used a nonparametric age effect; Albert (1999) questioned common peak-age and growth/decline assumptions and preferred a second-degree polynomial; Albert (2002) proposed a Bayesian exchangeable model for hitting performance; Fair (2008) and Bradbury (2009) used fixed-effects regression with quadratic age forms [PAGE 2]. Parallel sports lineages include Moore (1975) on track and field exponential aging, Page et al. (2013) on Gaussian-process NBA curves, Vaci et al. (2019) on Bayesian latent-variable NBA aging, Kovalchik (2014) on tennis change points, and Wakim & Jin (2014) on functional data analysis [PAGE 2]. Peak-age findings in the paper’s review are 27 to 30 from Schulz et al. (1994), 28 from Fair (2008), about 29 from Bradbury (2009), and 25 to 27 in soccer from Dendir (2016) [PAGE 2].

The survival-bias lineage is the paper’s direct ancestor: Schulz et al. (1994) and Schell (2005) note selection bias because better players have longer careers; Schall & Smith (2000) model survival probabilities with a logit model; Lichtman (2009) corrects for survival bias and reports overestimation; Judge (2020a) reports underestimation instead; Brander et al. (2014) predict unobserved NHL players; and Schuckers et al. (2021) use regression and imputation for NHL aging curves [PAGE 3]. The paper’s advance is explicitly multiple imputation rather than a single imputed dataset: “This could be improved by applying multiple imputation rather than considering only one imputed dataset” [PAGE 3]. The recursive genealogy matters because the same word “aging curve” has meant a descriptive survivor average, a peak-age parameter, a random-effect trajectory, and a counterfactual full-career mean. Multi-week traps follow from that ambiguity. Week-one trap: compare peak ages across studies without matching outcome, population, era, and missingness rule. Week-two trap: assume survivorship bias has one sign; the paper’s own review contains both overestimation and underestimation claims [PAGE 3]. Week-three trap: treat complete-case curves as biological aging rather than selected performance. Week-four trap: use single imputation and then treat filled values as observed; the paper warns that single imputation “essentially assumes that the filled-in values correctly estimate the true values of the missing observations” [PAGE 3]. Week-five trap: pool imputations without checking MICE convergence or between-imputation variance. Week-six trap: overinterpret a cubic age term outside the observed age support or without dropout predictors. The paper’s own limitations—age-only imputation and no additional offensive covariates—are the correct guardrails against overreading [PAGE 8].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the analyst’s fallback to the visible survivor curve. Under time pressure, the habitual move is to fit a quadratic or cubic to observed player-seasons, report a peak age, and ignore that the sample is produced by team and player decisions. The paper’s introduction describes the recursive social process: teams “may elect to assign their young prospects to their minor/development league affiliates,” later call them up, and “a player might head for retirement because they cannot perform at a level like they used to” [PAGE 1]. That is a theory-of-mind loop: the team models the player’s future, the player models his own remaining career, and the analyst models both while seeing only the survival outcome. The missingness is therefore not a neutral statistical accident; it is a decision clock. The counter-trap is equally dangerous: knowing that survival bias exists, an analyst may mechanically lower the curve, or may over-impute a mean career for every missing player. The paper’s counter-trap is multiple imputation with uncertainty, not a deterministic correction. But even MI has a recursive trap: the combined curve can be treated as the truth rather than as a model-dependent estimate under MAR, a multilevel normal specification, and an age-only predictor [PAGE 8]. The clock is both career age and computation time. Career age is fixed to 21–39 [PAGE 3], age is measured on June 30 with birth-month adjustment [PAGE 3], dropout timing differs by early career, middle career, and retirement [PAGE 1], and the imputation itself runs 5 chains for 30 iterations [PAGE 6]. A recursive audit should ask whether the analyst’s clock matches the player’s clock: a season missed at age 24 due to minor-league assignment is not the same missing-data event as a season missed at age 36 due to decline. The paper’s dropout cases encode exactly that distinction: early-career OPS average below 0.55, 4-year OPS average below 0.55, and random retirement at 30 [PAGE 5]. The correct counter-trap is to model the dropout clock explicitly, or at least to report that the age-only imputation cannot distinguish these clocks. The final recursive check is media/team feedback: an aging curve that says “past prime” can shape playing time, which then shapes the next observed curve. The paper cannot identify that feedback from Lahman season tables alone; it can only estimate a model-based counterfactual under stated assumptions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The paper’s age coefficient is a statistical age-performance association, not a biomechanical or meteorological causal effect. The cross-layer veto is formal. Let \(C\) collect omitted physical, biomechanical, and weather/park variables. Multiple imputation is valid under conditional exchangeability of the missing response and survival given the observed variables used in the imputation model. In symbols, the needed condition is \(Y_{\text{mis}}\perp S\mid (A,Y_{\text{obs}},C_{\text{obs}})\). If \(S\) depends on \(C\) and \(Y\) depends on \(C\), but \(C\) is omitted, then the imputation model is misspecified and the selection bias identity remains nonzero. For a linear simplification \(Y=\alpha A+\gamma C+\epsilon\), regression on \(A\) alone yields omitted-variable bias proportional to \(\gamma\,\mathrm{Cov}(A,C\mid S=1)/\mathrm{Var}(A\mid S=1)\), plus the selection term from conditioning on \(S=1\). This is a veto proof against causal claims, not against the paper’s descriptive MI estimate: the estimate can be useful for prediction while still failing a biomechanical causal interpretation.

Physical layer: the paper explicitly names injury as a cause of nonappearance: “Injury is unavoidable in sports, and this could cost a player at least one year of their playing time” [PAGE 1]. If injury risk rises with age and injury lowers OPS, then injury is a omitted common cause of both missingness and performance. An age-only imputation cannot recover that missing season unless injury is ignorable or included. Biomechanical layer: the paper contains no swing mechanics, bat speed, launch angle, sprint speed, or joint-load variables in the described pipeline; the data sources are the Lahman Batting and People tables [PAGE 3]. Any claim that the estimated curve is a biomechanical aging law is therefore vetoed by design. Weather layer: the described pipeline contains no weather, park, or game-condition covariates; OPS is scaled and arcsine-transformed [PAGE 3], but no weather adjustment is described. If weather or park affects OPS and also affects roster survival, the age curve is a composite. The paper’s own limitation that “age is the only predictor for estimating performance” [PAGE 8] is the evidential admission. Adjudication: the MI aging curve survives as a model-based counterfactual under MAR and the stated multilevel model; it is vetoed for claims that age itself mechanically causes decline, that injury is ignorable, or that weather/park effects are absent. The correct cross-layer proof would require adding \(C\) to the imputation model or demonstrating that \(C\) is ignorable for both \(Y\) and \(S\); neither is shown in the source.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
