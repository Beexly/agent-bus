<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal operator definition. Let an image frame be \(I_i \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}\), with pixel coordinates in px. A pose estimator is a parameterized map
\[
f_\theta: I_i \mapsto \hat{K}_i=\{(\hat X_{k,i},\hat Y_{k,i})\}_{k=1}^{K},
\]
where \(K\) is the number of annotated landmarks per frame. In this source, the landmark set is left/right ankle, left/right heel, and left/right big toe, so the relevant \(K\) is six per frame. The coordinates are 2D image-plane coordinates, not 3D anatomical joint centers; the source uses “three main points of the athlete’s feet including left/right ankle, heel, and big toe” [PAGE 5]. The estimator is trained with “10,000 training iterations with batch size 2” and “Adam” [PAGE 8]. The source does not report an explicit loss equation, so no loss formula should be invented.

RMSE operator. The source states: “We calculated the RMSE between the location of the predicted point and the reference point in pixels for each body part across all frames in the test dataset” [PAGE 9]. Formally, for body part \(k\),
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_k=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[(\hat X_{k,i}-X_{k,i})^2+(\hat Y_{k,i}-Y_{k,i})^2\right]},
\]
with units of px when the coordinates are in px. If a pixel-to-world scale \(s_k\) in m·px\(^{-1}\) were available, the SI version would be \(\mathrm{RMSE}_{k,\mathrm{m}}=s_k\mathrm{RMSE}_{k,\mathrm{px}}\); the paper does not provide a single global scale, only an interpolation mapping that “produced approximately 3mm accurate locations” in the measured central plane [PAGE 6]. Assumptions: correct manual ground truth, stable camera geometry, and negligible out-of-plane motion. Breakdown regimes: \(N=0\) makes RMSE undefined; annotation error, occlusion, foot deformation, or perspective drift makes the squared residual non-identifiable as a pure model error; large out-of-plane motion invalidates the central-plane mapping.

PCK operator. The source defines PCK as a proportion of correctly localized keypoints: “measures the proportion of correctly localized keypoints within a certain threshold distance from the ground truth keypoints” [PAGE 9]. The printed aggregation is
\[
\mathrm{PCK}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{PCK}_i}{M}\times 100,
\]
where the source says \(M\) is “the total number of keypoints evaluated” and \(\mathrm{PCK}_i\) is “the PCK value for a body part in frame \(i\)” [PAGE 9]. There is an index ambiguity in the printed notation: the sum runs over frames \(i\), but the denominator is described as total keypoints \(M\). The safest interpretation is that PCK is a percentage in \([0,100]\), or a proportion in \([0,1]\) before multiplication by 100. The threshold condition is printed as
\[
\mathrm{PCK}_i@0.2=\mathbb{1}\left(|\hat X_i-X_i|+|\hat Y_i-Y_i|\lt 0.2d\right),
\]
where “PCK@0.2 refers to the Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK) calculated with the threshold \(T\) set at 20% of a maximum allowable distance \(d\)” [PAGE 9]. This uses an \(L_1\) condition, whereas RMSE uses an \(L_2\) squared residual; the two metrics therefore have different error geometry. Units are dimensionless only if coordinate differences and \(d\) share the same unit. The source’s \(d\) values are dimensionally ambiguous: “we use \(d=6.8\) for the heel which corresponds to approx. 8cm in this dataset. For the big toe and the ankle, we also set \(d\) to 4.0 and 5.4 which represent 4.5 and 7cm in the dataset, respectively” [PAGE 10]. Because \(d\) is not explicitly given a unit, PCK comparability across studies is limited. Breakdown regimes: \(M=0\) makes PCK undefined; \(d\to 0\) drives PCK toward 0 for any nonzero error; \(d\to\infty\) drives PCK toward 100; changing \(d\) per body part changes model ranking; a pixel-space \(d\) cannot be interpreted as an anatomical tolerance without calibration.

Temporal operators. With frame rate \(f_s\) in Hz, the frame interval is
\[
\Delta t=\frac{1}{f_s}.
\]
At \(f_s=120\) fps, \(\Delta t=8.33\) ms. The source’s two-frame contact-time bound gives
\[
\epsilon_t^{\max}\approx 2\Delta t=\frac{2}{f_s}.
\]
The paper states: “with 500 fps this leads to 4 ms maximum error” [PAGE 14]. At 120 fps, the same two-frame bound is about 16.7 ms, not 4 ms. Breakdown regime: if the true contact duration \(\tau_c\lt 2/f_s\), first-touch and last-touch are not temporally identifiable from frame counts alone; the measurement is vetoed. The spatial sampling gap is
\[
\Delta x=v\Delta t=\frac{v}{f_s}.
\]
The source gives the motivating case: “a 20m/s forward movement of an ankle is captured by 120 fps video resulting a 17cm movement between frames” [PAGE 4]. This is consistent with \(20/120=0.167\) m. Breakdown regime: when \(\Delta x\) is comparable to or larger than the foot-landmark scale, interpolation or higher frame rate is required; otherwise contact-phase keypoints are aliased.

Augmentation operators reported by the source are crop, rotation, and contrast with parameters in Table 2: Tensorpack crop size \((400,400)\), crop ratio 0.4, rotation 5 degrees, rotation ratio 0.4, contrast \([0.5,2.0]\); Imgaug crop size \((200,200)\), crop ratio 0.4, contrast \([0.4,1.6]\) [PAGE 9]. These are transformations on training images; they do not create new physical ground truth. The source’s augmentation conclusion is partially contradicted by its own Table 3: DLCRNet Imgaug average RMSE 7.05 is worse than DLCRNet Tensorpack 6.84 and DLCRNet without augmentation 6.58 [PAGE 11], despite the text’s “Imgaug consistently outperforms Tensorpack across all models” [PAGE 10]. This is a formal counterexample to the universal wording, not a reason to invent a new ratio.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is a layered retrocausal chain: gait analysis and spring-mass running models [PAGE 3, refs. 25–26] → video-based foot-speed and frame-interpolation concerns [PAGE 4, refs. 29–30] → multi-scale CNN pose estimation [PAGE 4, refs. 19, 31–34] → DeepPose, Stacked Hourglass, ResNet, MobileNet, YOLO, DeepLabCut, and OpenPose [PAGE 2–7, refs. 9, 11, 21–22, 35–37]. The current YOLOv8l result is therefore not an isolated finding; it inherits the benchmark logic of PCK/RMSE from pose estimation, the backbone logic of ResNet/MobileNet from image recognition, and the field logic of hurdle clearance from biomechanics. The paper also explicitly positions itself against its own prior OpenPose work: “Our previous work [8] stands as a pioneering effort” [PAGE 2]. That retrocausal framing explains why the authors emphasize a low-cost, in-the-field pipeline rather than a laboratory motion-capture standard.

Multi-week trap 1: single-session environmental overfit. The dataset was gathered in June 2018 in Jyväskylä, Finland, with “one female athlete (100m hurdles PB 12.81 s)” and “The weather was half cloudy, so the lighting conditions changed only a little during the video shoot” [PAGE 5]. A model trained on that session may learn the athlete’s shoe appearance, lane color, shadow pattern, and camera angle. Across weeks, lighting, footwear, lane surface, and camera placement change; the 3 mm interpolation mapping [PAGE 6] is not a portable calibration.

Multi-week trap 2: athlete technique drift. The paper aims to track “step progression in the time domain” [PAGE 1] and “development trend of an athlete” [PAGE 16]. But if the athlete’s technique improves, the keypoint distribution shifts. A model validated on old technique may silently degrade; the paper’s single-athlete limitation [PAGE 16] means there is no between-athlete variance estimate to bound this drift.

Multi-week trap 3: frame-level cross-validation leakage. There are “8 videos that totally generated 344 images” [PAGE 5], and the paper uses 8-fold cross-validation [PAGE 8]. If folds are assigned by frame rather than by video/run, adjacent frames from the same stride can appear in both training and validation. That would inflate validation and obscure true multi-week generalization. The source does not fully specify fold grouping, so this remains a reproducibility trap.

Multi-week trap 4: version and toolbox drift. DeepLabCut “version 2.2.2” [PAGE 8] and YOLOv8 variants [PAGE 8, Table 1] are fixed in the paper, but later YOLO releases and DeepLabCut updates change architectures, pretrained weights, and augmentation defaults. Reusing the paper’s numbers as a baseline after a toolbox upgrade is not a like-for-like comparison.

Multi-week trap 5: metric-threshold drift. PCK depends on \(d\), and the source chooses \(d=6.8, 4.0, 5.4\) with approximate centimeter correspondences [PAGE 10]. If a later study changes \(d\) or uses a different normalization, the “best” model can change even with identical predictions. The paper’s PCK values are therefore genealogically tied to its expert-chosen thresholds, not to a universal anatomical tolerance.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under time pressure, a coach or analyst may regress to the simplest available heuristic: trust the model’s toe point, use the lane line as a contact trigger, and skip manual verification. The paper warns against exactly this: “using the lane line as the threshold for finding the contact points is not adequate as such due to the inaccuracy of the toe point predictions” [PAGE 14]. The recursive trap is that the model’s confident-looking keypoint becomes a social license for the coach to stop looking. The paper’s own workflow resists this by treating pose estimation as a narrowing tool, not an oracle.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the semi-automatic search window: “by enlarging the range of frames of being searched a few frames before and after the predicted point surpasses the lane line is enough to cover the range for first and last touch” [PAGE 14]. This is a theory-of-mind correction: the model’s output is a proposal, and the human retains adjudication over first/last touch. A second counter-trap is video-level or run-level validation; a third is reporting per-landmark PCK rather than a single headline. These counter-traps preserve the coach’s uncertainty instead of collapsing it into a false precision.

Clock. The clock is the frame rate. At 120 fps, \(\Delta t=8.33\) ms and the source’s two-frame contact bound is about 16.7 ms; at 500 fps, the paper’s stated bound is 4 ms [PAGE 14]. The recursive effect is that slower clocks make contact events look discrete when they are actually deformed and rolling [PAGE 3]. A coach who sees a single “contact frame” may attribute technique changes to the athlete that are actually sampling artifacts. The correct mental model is an interval-valued contact event, not a point event.

Recursive athlete-coach-model loop. The athlete adapts to coaching; the coach adapts to model plots; the model is retrained on new videos. If the coach over-trusts PCK 82.9% for the right heel [PAGE 12], the athlete may receive feedback on a measurement that is least reliable exactly when the heel is occluded or deforming. The paper’s qualitative figure is presented as validation [PAGE 15], but qualitative alignment does not calibrate the model’s uncertainty. The theory-of-mind discipline is to treat YOLO as an instrument with known failure modes, not as a coach with intent. The model has no belief about the hurdle; the coach must hold the belief about the model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The physical sampling layer vetoes any claim of millisecond contact precision from 120 fps. Let \(\tau_c\) be true contact duration and \(f_s\) the frame rate. If
\[
\tau_c \lt \frac{2}{f_s},
\]
then first-touch and last-touch are not identifiable to the source’s two-frame bound. At \(f_s=120\) Hz, the bound is \(2/120=16.7\) ms. The paper’s “4 ms maximum error” is explicitly tied to 500 fps [PAGE 14], not to the 120 fps data used in the main experiment. Therefore a claim such as “this pipeline measures contact time to 4 ms” is vetoed for the 120 fps dataset. A second physical veto is spatial aliasing: at \(v=20\) m/s and 120 fps, \(\Delta x=0.167\) m [PAGE 4], so the ankle moves about 17 cm between frames. Any method that treats adjacent frames as continuous without interpolation is physically under-sampled.

Biomechanical veto. The biomechanical layer vetoes point-event contact definitions. The source states that “the contact event deform the feet and shoe while a holonomic rolling movement at the big toe makes the actual period of contact hard to define” [PAGE 3]. If foot/shoe deformation displacement \(u_f\) is comparable to the annotated landmark scale, the keypoint is not a fixed anatomical point. Then “first touch” and “last touch” are not well-posed without a contact model that includes shoe compliance and toe rolling. The paper’s own annotation scheme—ankle, heel, big toe on the shoe/foot—cannot by itself resolve this. A claim of exact contact time from a single toe coordinate is therefore biomechanically vetoed unless accompanied by a deformation model or force-plate validation.

Weather/environment veto. The weather layer vetoes external validity beyond the source’s half-cloudy, controlled session. The paper reports “The weather was half cloudy, so the lighting conditions changed only a little during the video shoot” [PAGE 5] and requires a “rather homogeneous background and suitable sportswear” [PAGE 3–4]. Rain, glare, hard shadows, spectators, different lane colors, or non-standard clothing are outside the validated distribution. A model trained under those conditions may still produce coordinates, but the coordinates are not covered by the reported PCK. The veto is not that the model is useless; it is that the paper’s quantitative guarantee does not transfer.

Adjudication order. The cross-layer veto order should be: physical sampling veto > biomechanical definition veto > weather/environment veto > model-metric preference. A high PCK cannot override a frame-rate alias; a low RMSE cannot override a deformed contact definition; a favorable YOLOv8l comparison cannot override a single-athlete, single-session external-validity limit. The paper’s strongest defensible claim is therefore: under a controlled field setup, with one athlete and manually annotated foot landmarks, YOLOv8l gives useful 2D keypoint localization and a semi-automatic contact-search aid, not a validated general biomechanical measurement system.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
