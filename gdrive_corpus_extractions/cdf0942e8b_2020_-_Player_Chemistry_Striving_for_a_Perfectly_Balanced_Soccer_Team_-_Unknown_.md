The paper’s core inference chain is multi-hop and must be audited at each hop. Hop 1 is the latent-variable assumption: the authors cannot observe “chemistry” directly, so they state that “Since we cannot observe the mutual chemistry between two players directly, we adopt the assumption that two players who have high mutual chemistry perform better than players who have low mutual chemistry, all other things being equal” [PAGE 3]. This is not an identity; it is a measurement assumption. Hop 2 is the action-value substrate: the authors “build on top of the VAEP framework for valuing individual on-the-ball actions of players” [PAGE 3]. VAEP assigns each action a probability-unit reward for changing the team’s likelihood of scoring and conceding. Hop 3 is the interaction definition: “an interaction, which are two consecutive actions where each of both players performed one action each” [PAGE 3], and “We obtain the VAEP rating for an interaction by summing the VAEP ratings for the two constituting actions” [PAGE 4]. Thus JOI is not a generic pair-passing count; it is a sum of consecutive-action VAEP values involving both players. Hop 4 is normalization: JOI90 divides by shared minutes, so the metric is undefined when two players never share the pitch. Hop 5 is defensive attribution: the authors assume “when an opponent underperforms their expected offensive impact, then the pairs of players that are responsible for defending this opponent likely have done well” [PAGE 5], and “multiple pairs of players can be responsible for defending the same opponent” [PAGE 5]. That is a credit-allocation model, not direct observation of defending. Hop 6 is prediction: two CatBoost regressors map pair features to JOI90 and JDI90, trained only on pairs that “played at least 700 minutes together” [PAGE 9]. Hop 7 is optimization: the Team Builder selects eleven players by maximizing mutual predicted chemistry under position-count constraints [PAGE 10-11]. The gray-zone audit is that each hop introduces identifiability risk. JOI is relatively well anchored in observed on-ball actions, but it inherits VAEP’s horizon and model error. JDI is much weaker because the source admits “we do not know the exact spatial locations of the players at the time of each action” and uses “default positions” to compute responsibility [PAGE 6]. The predictive models are trained on survivorship-selected pairs with at least 700 shared minutes; therefore predictions for new signings or low-minute pairs are extrapolations. The Team Builder’s objective is quadratic in binary selection variables, even though the paper calls it mixed-integer programming, so exact solution requires linearization or pair-selection variables. The case studies are illustrative, not causal: Özil’s decline is temporally coincident with Sánchez’s departure [PAGE 15], Real Madrid’s “extra matches” projections are counterfactual model outputs [PAGE 19], and Ziyech’s shortlist depends on external SciSports models for minutes, development, and affordability [PAGE 21]. Finally, the paper’s strongest practical warning is the Andy Carroll example: “The then-22-year-old Carroll is a tall target man thriving from crosses, but Liverpool did not play that way and also did not have the appropriate players in their squad to accommodate his preferred playing style” [PAGE 1]. That is a cross-layer veto against over-trusting chemistry scores without tactical, physical, and environmental context.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Notation and units. Let \(a_i\) be the \(i\)-th on-ball action in a match, with \(p(a_i)\) the acting player. VAEP assigns \(V(a_i)\in\mathbb{R}\) in probability units: the change in the team’s goal-scoring probability minus the change in conceding probability over the framework’s action horizon. The source describes the five action types as “pass, cross, dribble, take-on, or shot” [PAGE 4]. Minutes are in minutes; JOI90 and JDI90 are probability units per 90 minutes, i.e. normalized probability-impact rates.

Interaction and JOI. The source defines the \(j\)-th interaction between players \(p\) and \(q\) in match \(m\) as a two-action subsequence:
\[
I^{j}_{m}(p,q)=\left(a_i,a_{i+1}\right),\qquad p(a_i)=p,\ q(a_{i+1})=q,
\]
or the reverse ordering. The source states: “We obtain the VAEP rating for an interaction by summing the VAEP ratings for the two constituting actions” [PAGE 4]. Thus
\[
V\!\left(I^{j}_{m}(p,q)\right)=V(a_i)+V(a_{i+1}).
\]
For a match,
\[
JOI_{m}(p,q)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}V\!\left(I^{j}_{m}(p,q)\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{l}V\!\left(I^{j}_{m}(q,p)\right),
\]
where \(k\) counts interactions initiated by \(p\) and finished by \(q\), and \(l\) counts the reverse. For a season,
\[
JOI90(p,q)=90\,\frac{\sum_{m}JOI_{m}(p,q)}{\sum_{m}MINS_{m}(p,q)}.
\]
The source says \(MINS_{m}(p,q)\) is “the number of minutes that players \(p\) and \(q\) spent together on the pitch in match \(m\)” [PAGE 4]. Exact singularity: if \(\sum_m MINS_m(p,q)=0\), JOI90 is undefined; if shared minutes are very small, JOI90 is unstable and should not be ranked as if it were a stable rate. The paper separately constructs predictive models only for pairs with “at least 700 minutes together” [PAGE 9], so raw JOI90 rankings below that support are not equivalent to model-supported estimates.

JDI. The defensive metric is an attribution model. The source states: “We determine the actual offensive impact for a player in a match by summing the VAEP ratings for the player's passes, crosses, dribbles, take-ons, and shots in that match” [PAGE 6]. For opponent \(o\),
\[
OI_m(o)=\sum_{a\in A_m(o)}V(a).
\]
The expected offensive impact is built from earlier same-season, same-competition matches: “We determine the expected offensive impact for a player in a match by computing the player's average offensive impact in the matches in the same season of the same competition that were played earlier” [PAGE 6]. The source’s typeset formula is incomplete in the OCR, so the following is a prose-consistent reconstruction, not a claim of source-exact typography:
\[
\widehat{OI}_m(o)=\frac{\sum_{m'\in \mathcal{E}_m(o)}OI_{m'}(o)}{\sum_{m'\in \mathcal{E}_m(o)}MINS_{m'}(o)}\,MINS_m(o)
\]
for players with at least 700 earlier minutes; for fewer than 700 minutes, the paper uses a Bayesian shrinkage: “we apply a Bayesian approach to obtain more robust estimations of the expected offensive impact for players who have played fewer than 700 minutes” and “the weights are linearly proportional to the share of the required 700 minutes the player has played” [PAGE 6]. A formal shrinkage form consistent with that prose is
\[
\widetilde{OI}_m(o)=w_o\widehat{OI}_m(o)+(1-w_o)\pi_{\mathrm{pos}(o)}MINS_m(o),\qquad
w_o=\min\!\left(1,\frac{\sum_{m'}MINS_{m'}(o)}{700}\right),
\]
where \(\pi_{\mathrm{pos}(o)}\) is the position-specific prior offensive impact rate. The source does not print this exact algebra in the OCR text, so it should be treated as a transparent reconstruction.

Responsibility share. The source uses a 5-by-5 grid and says: “The responsibility share is inversely proportional to the Euclidean distance in the grid. The lower the distance is, the higher the responsibility share is” [PAGE 6]. Let \((r_p,c_p)\) and \((r_o,c_o)\) be grid coordinates. Then
\[
d(p,o)=\sqrt{(r_p-r_o)^2+(c_p-c_o)^2}.
\]
A normalized individual share would be
\[
RESP_m(p,o)=\frac{d(p,o)^{-1}}{\sum_{p'}d(p',o)^{-1}},
\]
but the source only states inverse proportionality, so the raw scale is not fully specified in the OCR. The pair share is the average:
\[
RESP_m(p,q,o)=\frac{RESP_m(p,o)+RESP_m(q,o)}{2},
\]
which matches the source’s pair-share formula fragment [PAGE 7]. Exact singularity: \(d(p,o)=0\) makes inverse distance undefined unless smoothed; the paper does not state a smoothing rule in the provided text. JDI is then a minute-normalized weighted difference:
\[
JDI90(p,q)=90\,\frac{\sum_m\sum_o RESP_m(p,q,o)\left(\widetilde{OI}_m(o)-OI_m(o)\right)}{\sum_m MINS_m(p,q)}.
\]
The OCR omits the exact typeset JDI formula, so the minute-normalization placement is reconstructed from the prose: JDI is “weighted by the responsibility shares and accounted for the number of minutes spent together on the pitch” [PAGE 8]. Exact breakdown: zero shared minutes again makes JDI90 undefined; responsibility weights are proxy-based; if actual marking assignments differ from default-position distance, JDI is biased.

Prediction and Team Builder. The feature vector includes age, position line, height, weight, nationality, region, subregion, mother tongue, Physical Performance Indicators, and 22 Player Role scores [PAGE 9]. CatBoost models predict JOI90 and JDI90; the offensive model uses “500 trees with a maximum depth of 7,” the defensive model “1000 trees with a maximum depth of 5” [PAGE 9]. Test RMSE: JOI baseline 0.05448 versus model 0.04464; JDI baseline 0.89075 versus model 0.88906 [PAGE 9]. The defensive improvement is small in absolute terms, so JDI prediction should carry a wide uncertainty veto. The Team Builder solves, in source prose, “a mixed-integer programming problem where the goal is to select precisely eleven players who maximize the sum of their mutual offensive and defensive chemistry” [PAGE 10]. Formally:
\[
\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{|P|}}\sum_{p\in P}\sum_{q\in P,\ q\ne p}
\left[\alpha\widehat{JOI90}(p,q)+(1-\alpha)\widehat{JDI90}(p,q)\right]x_px_q
\]
subject to
\[
\sum_{p\in P}x_p=11,\quad
\sum_{p\in P}GKP_px_p=1,\quad
3\le\sum_{p\in P}DEF_px_p\le5,\quad
3\le\sum_{p\in P}MID_px_p\le5,\quad
1\le\sum_{p\in P}FWD_px_p\le3.
\]
The source gives these position constraints explicitly [PAGE 11]. Exact breakdown: \(x_px_q\) is quadratic; the printed formulation is not a linear MIP unless the authors linearize with \(z_{pq}=x_px_q\) or equivalent pair variables. Also, self-pairs \(p=q\) and missing pair predictions must be defined; otherwise the objective sum is ill-posed.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is retrocausal in the sense that its “chemistry” concept is assembled from older action-value and team-assembly traditions. The offensive metric descends from basketball EPV and soccer VAEP: the source says VAEP “was inspired by the EPV framework for basketball” [PAGE 3], and the reference list includes Cervone et al.’s POINTWISE and Decroos et al.’s VAEP [PAGE 24]. The chemistry framing descends from basketball synergy and lineup-complementarity work: the references include Maymin, Maymin, and Shen’s “NBA Chemistry,” Kuehn’s complementary-skill-set work, and Goldberg’s lineup evaluation [PAGE 24]. The Team Builder descends from combinatorial team-selection work; the authors say their approach is “Inspired by the approach taken in [1]” [PAGE 10]. The data lineage is Wyscout event data converted to SPADL, with SciSports SciSkill, Potential, Player Roles, and Physical Performance Indicators layered on top [PAGE 2-3]. This genealogy matters because each ancestor imports assumptions: EPV/VAEP import an action-horizon and probability model; basketball chemistry imports lineup-synergy intuitions from a different sport; SciSports role scores import proprietary role taxonomies; the optimizer imports a static knapsack-like selection logic.

The multi-week traps are temporal. Trap 1 is the 700-minute Bayesian threshold: early-season expectations are prior-dominated, so a player’s first weeks can produce misleadingly stable-looking chemistry values [PAGE 6]. Trap 2 is the 50-match learning curve: the source says “the number of matches that two players had played together ahead of the season has a considerable impact on the predictions” and “Beyond the mark of 50 matches together (i.e., slightly more than a season in most competitions), this effect is much weaker” [PAGE 10]. A scout who ignores the first 50 matches will understate developing partnerships; a scout who extrapolates beyond 50 will overstate the marginal return of more shared minutes. Trap 3 is the Özil-Sánchez temporal confound: the paper observes that “the average JOI90 for the five best-ranked Arsenal player pairs has rapidly declined for both Özil and Arsenal since Alexis Sánchez' departure in 2018” [PAGE 15], but this is a single-club time series with roster, tactical, and age confounds. Trap 4 is the Real Madrid counterfactual: “Vinícius Júnior is expected to overtake Bale around the 20-extra-matches mark, whereas Rodrygo is expected to do the same around the 40-extra-matches mark” [PAGE 19]. That is a model-based projection, not an observed longitudinal result; the paper itself advises giving both players “enough playing time” to improve chemistry [PAGE 19-20], which creates a self-fulfilling feedback loop if selection is based on the projection. Trap 5 is the Ziyech shortlist: the five clubs are selected using external SciSports models for minutes, development, and affordability, then chemistry is evaluated within that shortlist [PAGE 21]. That is a conditional ranking, not a global transfer recommendation. Trap 6 is league-strength and sample-size heterogeneity: the JOI90 top ten includes players from the Belarusian Premier League, Ukrainian second tier, German fourth tier, NWSL, MLS, and elite European leagues [PAGE 11-12], so per-90 chemistry values are not automatically comparable across competition strength without a calibration layer.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The decision ecology has three agents: the manager selecting a lineup, the scout evaluating a signing, and the player evaluating a destination. Crisis habit regression appears when uncertainty is high and the agent reverts to the familiar individual-quality heuristic. The paper explicitly says: “Although this example illustrates the importance of chemistry among players, recruitment departments at soccer clubs still largely ignore this aspect in their evaluation of potential signings” [PAGE 1]. That is the baseline habit: scouts watch a limited number of matches and judge individual quality; they do not model pair interactions. The counter-trap is overcorrection: an agent who learns about chemistry may treat the predicted JOI90/JDI90 as a causal, context-free ranking. But the paper’s own feature analysis says cultural features “exhibit only limited predictive power” and that “it is more important that two players communicate well with their feet and that they have complementary playing styles” [PAGE 10]. The counter-trap is therefore to optimize chemistry while ignoring role complementarity, tactical style, physical load, and availability. The clock is the temporal dimension: chemistry is not static. Early-season Bayesian priors dominate under 700 minutes [PAGE 6]; shared-match history matters heavily before 50 matches and weakly after [PAGE 10]; Özil’s chemistry declines after Sánchez leaves [PAGE 15]; Bale starts ahead of Rodrygo and Vinícius but is projected to be overtaken at roughly 20 and 40 extra matches respectively [PAGE 19]. Recursive theory of mind adds a feedback loop: the manager believes the scout will use chemistry predictions; the scout believes the manager wants maximum-chemistry lineups; the player believes clubs will value chemistry fit. If the club then gives a young player more minutes to realize the predicted chemistry improvement, the observed future chemistry is partly caused by the selection decision, not by an exogenous “fit.” The safe interpretation is counterfactual discipline: use chemistry as a conditional screen, not as a deterministic assignment rule. The manager should ask “given our style and the opponent, which pair interactions are likely to be available?” The scout should ask “does the predicted chemistry survive a change in role, league, and playing time?” The player should ask “is the club’s chemistry model measuring teammates who will actually play, or a shortlist artifact?” The clock warning is strongest for the Real Madrid use case: a current ranking at matchday zero is not the same as a projected ranking after 20 or 40 matches [PAGE 19].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The paper’s event-data layer cannot directly adjudicate physical, biomechanical, or weather causality. It uses “Wyscout match event data” converted to SPADL [PAGE 2], and for defense it explicitly uses default positions because “we do not know the exact spatial locations of the players at the time of each action” [PAGE 6]. That admission is the formal basis for a cross-layer veto: if actual marking assignments \(A_m(p,o)\) differ from the proxy responsibility \(RESP_m(p,q,o)\), then the JDI estimate is biased by
\[
\Delta JDI_m=\sum_o\left[A_m(p,q,o)-RESP_m(p,q,o)\right]\left(\widetilde{OI}_m(o)-OI_m(o)\right).
\]
The source itself acknowledges a related omission: the Liverpool figure omits Mané-Robertson and Salah-Alexander-Arnold links because “these pairs of players have not been responsible for defending the same opponent for long enough” [PAGE 15]. That is evidence that the defensive pairing is assignment-dependent, not merely position-dependent. A physical/biomechanical veto applies when a player’s value depends on a skill that the event layer underrepresents. The Andy Carroll case is the canonical proof: “The then-22-year-old Carroll is a tall target man thriving from crosses, but Liverpool did not play that way and also did not have the appropriate players in their squad to accommodate his preferred playing style” [PAGE 1]. A chemistry score that ignores crossing volume, aerial-duel geography, and team style would wrongly rank Carroll. The paper does include Physical Performance Indicators such as “ground duel strength, air duel strength, speed, and work rate” [PAGE 3], but those are player-level features, not a full biomechanical simulation of contested space. Weather is not in the provided dataset at all; therefore any claim that chemistry predictions transfer across rain, wind, heat, altitude, or pitch condition is an out-of-scope extrapolation. The correct adjudication is veto, not adjustment: if the match environment changes the action mix enough that VAEP interaction frequencies and defensive assignments shift, the model’s output should be withheld or flagged, not silently rescaled. For Team Builder, a physical veto also applies when the optimizer selects eleven players who maximize pair chemistry but cannot satisfy minutes load, injury recovery, or tactical pressing constraints; the source’s constraints are only goalkeeper, defender, midfielder, and forward counts [PAGE 11].
