The source is a mechanistic tauopathy study, not a sports event, so the audit must build a causal chain from molecule to tissue and then define where the chain is evidential versus speculative. The highest-confidence causal skeleton is: pathological tau appears and aggregates; extracellular tau is secreted; glial cells accumulate lipid droplets; mitochondrial ATP falls; neuronal GLUT3 falls; acylcarnitines rise, consistent with a shift toward fatty-acid oxidation. The paper states this frame explicitly: “Accumulation of tau leads to neuroinflammation and neuronal cell death in tauopathies, including Alzheimer’s disease” [PAGE 1], and “Increased secretion of pathological tau protein in vivo and in vitro induced upregulated production of phospholipids and sphingolipids and accumulation of lipid droplets in microglia” [PAGE 1]. The temporal ordering is the key multi-hop constraint. At 4 and 6 months, the authors report no high-molecular-weight sarkosyl-insoluble aggregates: “There were no high molecular weight aggregates in sarkosyl-insoluble tau in pons (Fig. 1C) and medulla (Fig. 1D) from 4- and 6-month-old Tg rats” [PAGE 8]. Yet they also report early lipid changes: “significant changes have been found in the early stages of tauopathy before the formation of high-molecular-weight tau aggregates and neurofibrillary pathology” [PAGE 1], and later, “We found significantly increased amounts of LPC and LPE in 6-months old animals before the development of high-molecular-weight tau aggregates” [PAGE 17]. This is not merely a correlation claim; the authors use it to argue that lipids may be causal contributors: “Our results indicate that lipids not only accumulate as a result of aberrant metabolic processes caused by pathological tau protein but may also contribute to aberrant protein aggregation and progression of neurofibrillary pathology” [PAGE 2]. The gray-zone audit must separate three evidential strata. First, the strongest within-source experimental evidence is the in vitro tau-to-microglia lipid-droplet result: BV2 microglia treated with truncated tau at 500 nM for 24 h show increased BODIPY signal, with “CN: 23.91 ± 5.62; Tau: 34.41 ± 8.60; p = 0.0013; n = 7” [PAGE 18]. Second, the LDM ATP result connects lipid droplets to bioenergetics: “Luciferase luminescence ATP detection assay was performed, and we found decreased ATP activity in LD accumulating mitochondria (Fig. 6I, CN: 60,346 ± 1310; Tau: 52,267 ± 1032; p = 0.0029; n = 5)” [PAGE 18]. Third, the in vivo human extension is suggestive but limited: human PSP and CBD tissue showed lipid droplet accumulation in microglia, but the source does not establish disease specificity or a quantitative dose-response across tau burden. The paper’s own limitations are explicit: “the analysis conducted does not extend to the investigation of cell-specific effects” [PAGE 21], and “the animal model used, SHR-24, predominantly exhibits pathology in the brainstem, with a minor presence of NFTs in the frontal cortex. Consequently, some findings may reflect region-specific effects” [PAGE 21]. The biofluid timing creates a further trap. CSF total-tau rises early and then falls late: “Decreasing levels of total tau in the CSF after 12 months correlated with the appearance of high molecular tau aggregates in brain tissue and the upregulation of soluble hyperphosphorylated tau forms” [PAGE 8]. A naive reading would call that “improvement”; the source explicitly rejects that interpretation by linking the decline to aggregation and reduced release. The same pattern is reported for NFL: “NFL might aggregate in brain tissue, hence its release to biofluids decreases with progressing damage” [PAGE 8]. Finally, a source-governance oddity must be flagged: the data-availability statement says “No datasets were generated or analysed during the current study” [PAGE 22], which is plainly inconsistent with the reported LC-MS, ELISA, Western blot, and imaging data. That does not invalidate the science, but it lowers reproducibility confidence and requires independent replication before any high-stakes inference.

\dot{T}_e(t)=S_T(t)-k_{\mathrm{clr}}T_e(t)-k_{\mathrm{int}}M(t)T_e(t).
\]
Assumptions: well-mixed compartment; constant clearance; no active transport saturation. Breakdown regime: if \(k_{\mathrm{clr}}\) approaches zero or if \(S_T\) is unmeasured, \(T_e\) is unidentifiable. The paper’s CSF tau decline after 12 months shows that CSF tau is not a monotonic proxy for tissue tau: “Decreasing levels of total tau in the CSF after 12 months correlated with the appearance of high molecular tau aggregates in brain tissue” [PAGE 8].

2. Glial lipid-droplet induction operator \(O_2\): let \(L(t)\) be BODIPY-positive lipid-droplet burden in cells field\(^{-1}\), \(\alpha\) an induction coefficient, \(\beta\) a turnover rate in month\(^{-1}\), and \(\Theta(t-t_6)\) a step function beginning at the 6-month early-lipid window. Then
\[
\dot{L}(t)=\alpha T_e(t)\Theta(t-t_6)-\beta L(t),\qquad L(t)\le L_{\max}.
\]
Assumptions: linear dose-response at low \(T_e\); no saturation except at \(L_{\max}\). Breakdown regime: at high extracellular tau, BODIPY fluorescence saturates and \(L(t)\) becomes ordinal, not quantitative. The source’s in vitro dose is 500 nM for 24 h and cannot be equated to interstitial concentration [PAGE 18].

3. Mitochondrial ATP suppression operator \(O_3\): let \(A_{\mathrm{LDM}}(t)\) be relative luminescence in arbitrary units (a.u.) from lipid-droplet-associated mitochondria, and \(L(t)\) the droplet burden. A first-order suppression model is
\[
A_{\mathrm{LDM}}(t)=A_0-\delta\int_{0}^{t}L(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
with \(\delta\) in a.u. per cell field\(^{-1}\) month\(^{-1}\). Assumptions: ATP luminescence is proportional to mitochondrial ATP content; LDM isolation is pure. Breakdown regime: if LDM fractions contain non-droplet mitochondria or if luciferase is ATP-limited, \(\delta\) is not physically interpretable. The source reports “decreased ATP activity in LD accumulating mitochondria” with values “CN: 60,346 ± 1310; Tau: 52,267 ± 1032; p = 0.0029; n = 5” [PAGE 18], and in vivo “a significant decrease in ATP levels already at 8 months of age (Fig. 7E, p = 0.016, n = 4)” [PAGE 15].

4. Neuronal glucose-transport operator \(O_4\): let \(G(t)\) be GLUT3 mean fluorescence intensity in NeuN-positive neurons in a.u., \(G_0\) the age-matched control level, and \(I_{\mathrm{agg}}(t)\) the sarkosyl-insoluble tau signal in semi-quantitative a.u. Then
\[
G(t)=G_0-\gamma I_{\mathrm{agg}}(t),
\]
with \(\gamma\) in a.u. per a.u. Assumptions: immunofluorescence intensity tracks transporter abundance and not epitope masking. Breakdown regime: if neuronal loss or section thickness varies, \(G(t)\) is confounded. The source reports 8-month values “CN: 25.07 ± 1.43, Tg: 20.91 ± 0.54; p = 0.035” and 10-month values “CN: 28.37 ± 1.03, Tg: 22.36 ± 1.67; p = 0.022” [PAGE 20], and argues that the change is “most likely specific to neurofibrillary pathology rather than being caused by neuronal loss” [PAGE 15].

5. Acylcarnitine shift operator \(O_5\): let \(C_{\mathrm{AC}}(t)\) be acylcarnitine median intensity in a.u.; a linear glucose-deprivation proxy is
\[
C_{\mathrm{AC}}(t)=C_0+\eta\left(G_0-G(t)\right),
\]
with \(\eta\) in a.u. intensity\(^{-1}\). Assumptions: acylcarnitines reflect fatty-acid oxidation flux; no dietary or diurnal confound. Breakdown regime: if carnitine shuttle capacity saturates or if amino-acid catabolism dominates, the model fails. The paper’s interpretation is explicit: “This is indicative of a metabolic shift from glucose to fatty acid oxidation for energy production” [PAGE 15].

6. Biofluid washout operator \(O_6\): let \(B(t)\) be CSF or plasma biomarker concentration in pg mL\(^{-1}\). A two-process model is
\[
B(t)=B_{\mathrm{release}}(t)-B_{\mathrm{retention}}(t),
\]
where retention includes aggregation and tissue binding. Assumptions: release and retention are separable. Breakdown regime: if aggregation sequesters analyte before release, peripheral biomarkers invert. The source’s own warning is that “NFL might aggregate in brain tissue, hence its release to biofluids decreases with progressing damage” [PAGE 8]. No weather, biomechanical load, or environmental covariate is present in the source; any such term would be an external addition, not a source operator.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from tau aggregation as a neuropathological hallmark to lipid dyshomeostasis as a modifying process. The paper opens with tauopathies as “a heterogeneous group of progressive neurodegenerative disorders characterized by severe cognitive impairment, memory loss, and aberrant behavioral patterns” [PAGE 2], and then states, “Growing evidence suggests critical involvement of lipid dyshomeostasis in pathogenesis of tauopathies” [PAGE 2]. The retocausal chain is therefore not “lipid first” or “tau first” in a simple sense; it is staged. Early tau secretion and phosphorylation precede high-molecular-weight aggregates, and early lysophospholipid changes are already present at 6 months, before the 8-month co-assembly of endogenous tau with truncated tau. The source says that in 8-month-old animals, “endogenous tau co-assembled with truncated tau and became a part of the sarkosyl-insoluble complexes” [PAGE 8]. Later, the pons lags the medulla: “high-molecular tau aggregates (90 kDa) were mostly absent in pons, only appearing around 12 months of age” [PAGE 11]. That regional delay is a multi-week trap: averaging pons and medulla would blur the staging. Another trap is treating the 4-to-14-month design as a continuous within-animal trajectory when the methods describe separate age groups of transgenic and control rats at 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 months [PAGE 3]. A third trap is reading the 12-to-14-month CSF tau decline as disease remission, when the authors link it to aggregation: “Decreasing levels of total tau in the CSF after 12 months correlated with the appearance of high molecular tau aggregates in brain tissue and the upregulation of soluble hyperphosphorylated tau forms” [PAGE 8]. The genealogical lesson is that lipid markers may be early amplifiers or consequences depending on stage. The paper itself draws the bidirectional conclusion: “Our data showed a strong connection between the transition of tau into a sarkosyl insoluble fraction and changes in brain metabolism” [PAGE 11]. The historical scientific ancestry also includes the shift from bulk lipid measurement to ontology enrichment and targeted LC-MS/MS, which allows class-level claims about glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and lipid droplets. But the lineage is not a completed causal genealogy; the source’s limitation statement leaves cell-specific and region-specific attribution unresolved [PAGE 21].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the single-cause reflex: “lipid accumulation is bad, therefore remove lipids.” That habit is tempting because the paper reports elevated LPC, LPE, PC, PG, SM, and lipid droplets in tauopathy. But the authors do not endorse a simple lipotoxicity model. They note that lipid droplets may be protective: “LDs have been previously proposed to serve as a lipid buffering system to prevent acylcarnitine accumulation and lipotoxicity in mitochondria [57]” [PAGE 17]. A crude anti-lipid intervention could therefore worsen mitochondrial stress by removing a buffering compartment. The counter-trap is equally dangerous: “lipids are only a consequence, so ignore them.” The source resists that too, stating that lipids “may also contribute to aberrant protein aggregation and progression of neurofibrillary pathology” [PAGE 2]. The recursive theory-of-mind problem is that the experimenter, the clinician, and the modeler each see a different layer. The experimenter sees BODIPY and ATP; the clinician sees CSF tau and NFL; the modeler sees a staged causal graph. The clock matters because the correct intervention window differs by layer. At 6 months, the dominant measurable event is early lipid change before high-molecular-weight aggregates [PAGE 17]. At 8 months, endogenous tau co-assembles into sarkosyl-insoluble complexes, GLUT3 falls, and LDM ATP falls [PAGE 8; PAGE 15; PAGE 20]. At 10 to 14 months, lipidosis and inflammatory markers dominate, and CSF tau falls rather than rises [PAGE 8]. The paper’s own epistemic verbs are cautious: “we propose,” “could,” “suggest,” and “may.” For example, “Based on the data, we propose that an increase in extracellular tau, particularly in the proline-rich domain in the media, induces the formation of LDs in rat microglia” [PAGE 14]. A recursive reader should not upgrade “propose” to “prove.” The counter-trap is over-trusting the in vitro 24-to-96-hour clock as if it were the 4-to-14-month in vivo clock. The source’s cell model uses 24, 48, 72, and 96 h induction and 24 h tau treatment [PAGE 4; PAGE 18], while the animal model spans months [PAGE 3]. The safe conclusion is stage-dependent: early extracellular tau and lysoPL changes are candidate amplifiers; late lipid droplets and mitochondrial ATP loss are candidate maintenance mechanisms; neither is established as a sole cause.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
No weather layer exists in this source. There is no meteorological variable, no ambient temperature, no humidity, and no outdoor exposure model. Any weather-based inference must be vetoed as unsourced. No organismal biomechanical load layer exists either. The only biophysical layer is membrane lipid order, measured with pyrenedecanoic acid: “A shift to the liquid phase, indicating decreased membrane lipid order, was observed in BV2 cells treated with truncated tau protein” [PAGE 14]. That is a cellular membrane-fluid material property, not a tissue-level mechanical load. Cross-layer adjudication therefore proceeds from molecule to organelle to cell to tissue to biofluid. Molecular layer: tau phosphorylation and sarkosyl insolubility increase with age, with no high-molecular-weight aggregates at 4 and 6 months [PAGE 8]. Organelle layer: lipid droplets associate with mitochondria and LDM ATP is lower in transgenic animals, with “a significant decrease in ATP levels already at 8 months of age (Fig. 7E, p = 0.016, n = 4)” [PAGE 15]. Cellular layer: microglia accumulate BODIPY-positive droplets after tau exposure, and membrane order shifts [PAGE 18; PAGE 14]. Tissue layer: the brainstem, especially medulla, is more affected than pons, and the source warns that “some findings may reflect region-specific effects” [PAGE 21]. Biofluid layer: CSF tracks brain lipid changes better than plasma; the paper reports that “CNS lipidosis was not reflected in plasma with only minor changes in several GPLs” [PAGE 12]. Human layer: PSP and CBD tissue show lipid droplet accumulation, but the sample context is postmortem and not a controlled time series. The veto rule is this: no higher-layer biomarker may override the lower-layer evidence unless the source provides a calibration between layers. Here it does not. CSF tau is not a monotonic tissue-tau meter because it falls late while insoluble aggregates rise [PAGE 8]. Plasma is explicitly weaker than CSF for CNS lipidosis [PAGE 12]. Therefore, a claim that plasma lipids diagnose brain tau burden is vetoed. Likewise, a claim that lipid droplets alone cause ATP loss is vetoed by the correlational LDM data; the source says “We conclude that the accumulation of LDM negatively affects cell bioenergetics” [PAGE 15], which is a causal hypothesis, not an isolated perturbation proof. The correct cross-layer claim is conditional: in the SHR-24 brainstem, tau pathology co-segregates with glial lipid droplets, LDM ATP reduction, GLUT3 decrease, and acylcarnitine increase, with the strongest temporal anchor at 8 months.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Counterfactual 1: extracellular tau neutralization. If the source’s claim that extracellular tau induces glial lipid droplets is causal, then neutralizing or knocking down secreted truncated tau in the co-culture should reduce BODIPY-positive microglia and preserve LDM ATP. Prediction: BODIPY mean fluorescence and BODIPY-positive cell count should fall toward control; ATP luminescence should rise toward control. Source basis: tau treatment increased BODIPY-positive cells to “CN: 23.91 ± 5.62; Tau: 34.41 ± 8.60; p = 0.0013; n = 7” [PAGE 18], and LDM ATP fell to “CN: 60,346 ± 1310; Tau: 52,267 ± 1032; p = 0.0029; n = 5” [PAGE 18]. Veto condition: if tau neutralization lowers extracellular tau but BODIPY and ATP remain unchanged, then the droplet-ATP link is not driven by the secreted tau species specified by the paper.

Counterfactual 2: early lysophospholipid inhibition. If early LPC and LPE elevation at 6 months is an amplifier rather than a bystander, then inhibiting phospholipase-mediated lysophospholipid production before 8 months should delay high-molecular-weight sarkosyl-insoluble tau and reduce later lipid-droplet burden. Prediction: at 8 months, sarkosyl-insoluble high-molecular-weight tau should be lower than untreated transgenic controls, and LPC/LPE class intensities should normalize. Source basis: the paper reports early lipid changes before aggregates [PAGE 1], and “significantly increased amounts of LPC and LPE in 6-months old animals before the development of high-molecular-weight tau aggregates” [PAGE 17]. Veto condition: if LPC/LPE are normalized but sarkosyl-insoluble tau and downstream lipid droplets are unchanged, then lysoPL elevation is a consequence or epiphenomenon, not a causal amplifier.

Counterfactual 3: GLUT3 rescue and fatty-acid oxidation reversal. If the GLUT3-to-acylcarnitine axis is causal, then restoring neuronal glucose uptake before 8 months should reduce long-chain and hydroxylated acylcarnitines and reduce lipid-droplet accumulation. Prediction: GLUT3 mean fluorescence in NeuN-positive neurons should rise toward control, and acylcarnitine median intensities should fall, especially the hydroxylated species that remain elevated. Source basis: GLUT3 is reduced at 8 and 10 months [PAGE 20], and the authors state, “This is indicative of a metabolic shift from glucose to fatty acid oxidation for energy production” [PAGE 15]. Veto condition: if GLUT3 is rescued but acylcarnitines and lipid droplets remain elevated, then the metabolic shift is not downstream of GLUT3 alone and the source’s linear \(O_4 \to O_5\) chain fails. Each counterfactual has a binary, verifiable endpoint and a pre-stated failure condition; no probability estimate is inferred from the source because the paper reports no interventional rescue experiment.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
