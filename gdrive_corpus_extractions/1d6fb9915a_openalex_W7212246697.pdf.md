The source can be formalized with a classification operator, a sensor-feedback operator, and veto inequalities. All equations below are dimensional and tied to the paper’s reported categories and athlete needs.

1. Classification operator. Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be the set of athletes and let
\[
C:\mathcal{A}\to \{S11,S12,S13\}\times\{B1,B2,B3\}\times\{J1,J2\}\times\{F11,F12,F13\}\times\{T11,T12,T13\}
\]
be a classification map with no units. The paper gives the swimming categories and the IBSA B1-B3 mapping [PAGE 2], the judo J1-J2 split [PAGE 2], the shot-put F11-F13 split [PAGE 3], and the running T11-T13 split [PAGE 3]. Assumption: the device is allowed only if its feedback modality is usable by that class. Breakdown regime: using a residual-vision display for B1 or T11 athletes contradicts the source’s own category definitions [PAGE 2][PAGE 3].

2. Feedback-loop latency. For a swimmer approaching a wall, define
\[
t_{\mathrm{loop}}=t_{\mathrm{sense}}+t_{\mathrm{proc}}+t_{\mathrm{act}}+t_{\mathrm{human}}
\]
in seconds. Let \(L\) be the remaining distance to the wall in metres and \(v\) the swimmer speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The event time is
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{event}}=\frac{L}{v}.
\]
A cue is timely only if
\[
t_{\mathrm{loop}}\leq \tau_{\mathrm{event}}.
\]
Assumption: the athlete needs the cue before initiating the turn. Breakdown regime: if \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}>\tau_{\mathrm{event}}\), the device is late even if it is accurate. The paper’s swimming interview identifies the need to know “when to stop and when to flip” [PAGE 4], which makes latency a first-order veto, not a polish issue.

3. Acoustic localization signal-to-noise ratio. For an audible cricket ball or pitch marker, let \(P_s\) be received signal power and \(P_n\) noise power, both in watts. Then
\[
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{P_s}{P_n}\right)
\]
has units of decibels. Assumption: reliable detection requires a threshold \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{th}}\). Breakdown regime: as \(P_n\to P_s\), \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\to 0\), and detection probability collapses. The paper says the audible ball “has ball bearings inside it, which make a rattling sound when it is moving” [PAGE 6], and that audible markers can be placed along the throwing circle [PAGE 6]. Those mechanisms are physically plausible only in favourable acoustic conditions.

4. Impact stopping force for shot put. For a shot of mass \(m\) in kilograms and speed \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), kinetic energy is
\[
K=\frac{1}{2}mv^2
\]
in joules. If the implement is stopped over distance \(d\) in metres, the average stopping force is
\[
F_{\mathrm{stop}}=\frac{K}{d}=\frac{mv^2}{2d}
\]
in newtons. The source reports the shot-put athlete’s request for “inbuilt speed breaker technology” [PAGE 5]. Assumption: a men’s shot mass is \(m=7.26\,\mathrm{kg}\) and release speed is \(v=12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for an illustrative calculation. Then \(K\approx 523\,\mathrm{J}\). For \(d=0.10\,\mathrm{m}\), \(F_{\mathrm{stop}}\approx 5.2\,\mathrm{kN}\). Breakdown regime: as \(d\to 0\), \(F_{\mathrm{stop}}\to \infty\); the model becomes singular. A safe in-flight stopping device is therefore not a trivial sensor add-on.

5. Running position error. Let \(\sigma_x\) and \(\sigma_y\) be horizontal position-error standard deviations in metres. Then
\[
\sigma_p=\sqrt{\sigma_x^2+\sigma_y^2}
\]
is the radial position error in metres. If lane width is \(w_{\mathrm{lane}}\), a navigation aid is admissible only if
\[
\sigma_p\leq \frac{w_{\mathrm{lane}}}{2}.
\]
Assumption: the athlete must stay within the lane or track boundary. Breakdown regime: when \(\sigma_p\) exceeds \(w_{\mathrm{lane}}/2\), the device cannot replace a guide. This is directly relevant because the paper says “Shared tracks are the same as those of the normal athlete, causing navigation difficulties” [PAGE 5], and because T11 runners “must have to run with a guide” [PAGE 3].

6. Chess-clock remaining time. For a chess clock, let \(T_0\) be the initial time in seconds and \(q(t)\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether the player’s clock is active. Then
\[
T_{\mathrm{remain}}(t)=T_0-\int_0^t q(\tau)\,d\tau
\]
in seconds. The paper describes a “computer chess clock for blind players” with push buttons that can be felt, audio signals, and voice announcements [PAGE 6]. Assumption: the clock is fair only if announcement latency \(\Delta t\) is small compared with the remaining time and the increment. Breakdown regime: if \(\Delta t\) is comparable to a player’s remaining time, the clock becomes a source of inequity rather than accessibility.

7. Shot-put circle geometry and timing. The paper states that “throws are taken from 2.135 m where measurements are taken from closest mark” [PAGE 3] and that “Athletes have 60 seconds to participate for throwing once they are called” [PAGE 3]. These are fixed constraints: a wearable or audible marker must not alter the throwing-circle geometry, and any cueing system must fit inside the 60-second preparation window. Breakdown regime: a device that requires recalibration longer than the allowed preparation time is inadmissible.

The operator summary is therefore: classify first, then measure latency, signal-to-noise ratio, force, position error, and timing against class-specific thresholds. The paper’s own evidence supports sensors and audio feedback in principle [PAGE 5][PAGE 6], but it does not establish that any single device crosses all thresholds.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from public-health and Paralympic institutions to sport-specific equipment. The paper notes that the Paralympic Games have been held shortly after the Olympic Games since the 1988 Seoul Olympics [PAGE 1], and it cites the “Vision 2020: The right to sight” initiative [PAGE 1]. It also gives Indian epidemiological context: “There are an estimated 4.95 million people blind (0.36% of the total population), 35 million people visually impaired (2.55%), and 0.24 million blind children in India” [PAGE 1]. From that lineage, classification systems became the gatekeeping layer: swimming S11-S13 and B1-B3 [PAGE 2], judo J1-J2 [PAGE 2], shot put F11-F13 [PAGE 3], and running T11-T13 [PAGE 3]. Equipment then evolved around those categories: tactile chessboards with “raised squares and Braille markings” [PAGE 2], audible cricket balls with internal bearings [PAGE 6], audible markers along the throwing circle [PAGE 6], and sensor-based swimming aids [PAGE 5-6].

Retrocausally, today’s recommendations are shaped by past category design. The paper recommends that government provide financial support and advanced devices, that Fergusson College arrange funding and training, and that NGOs organize training and mentorship [PAGE 7]. Those recommendations look forward, but they are also a backward projection of earlier gaps: no ground and machinery for regular practice [PAGE 4], no coach for training [PAGE 5], and “no such technological help” for skill or safety [PAGE 7]. The multi-week traps are therefore not merely technical. First is the pilot-purgatory trap: a device is demonstrated for a camp or study, but no maintenance or spare-parts budget exists. Second is the classification-drift trap: a tool is validated on B2 or T12 athletes and then deployed to B1 or T11 athletes, even though the source’s categories distinguish total blindness from partial sight [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. Third is the guide-dependency trap: because T11 runners must run with a guide [PAGE 3], a navigation device that claims to replace the guide can create a false sense of safety. Fourth is the metric trap: wearable heart-rate, GPS, and biomechanical sensors provide real-time feedback [PAGE 6], but without coaching capacity the data do not become training. Fifth is the accessibility-cost trap: the paper itself warns that deep tech “implementation should be more inclusive and accessible to the visually impaired players of all region” [PAGE 7]. A device that works only in a well-funded urban college is not a general solution. These traps unfold over weeks and months, not in a single demo, because they involve procurement, charging, calibration, repair, coaching, and trust.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has at least four nested beliefs. The athlete believes the coach will select or train them; the coach believes the federation will apply classification and timing rules; the federation believes the college or government will fund equipment; the college or government believes international medals justify investment. The paper supplies the concrete anchors: selection is “based on timing certifications and MQS (minimum qualification standards)” [PAGE 5], and “Government provides jobs when they win international tournaments” [PAGE 4]. The college is told to “arrange funding for Visually challenged players” [PAGE 7], and government is told to provide “financial support” and “advanced devices” [PAGE 7]. In this recursive chain, a device is not evaluated only by the athlete; it is evaluated by a coach who may not exist, a federation that may not measure the right variable, and a funder that may prefer visible medals over maintenance.

Crisis habit regression appears when resources are scarce. The athlete may regress to sighted norms: train on shared tracks because that is what exists [PAGE 5], do “regular exercise” because there is “no ground and machinery for regular practice” [PAGE 4], and self-guide because “they don’t have a coach for training hence guidance is like they have to do by themselves” [PAGE 5]. This is not a character flaw; it is a rational short-run adaptation to missing infrastructure. The counter-trap is the opposite error: over-trusting a device because it appears to restore sighted performance. Examples include audio-only wall cues in a noisy pool, GPS-only lane keeping on a shared track, or eye-tracking for a B1 athlete. The paper’s own judo discussion mentions eye-tracking devices to monitor visual attention [PAGE 6], but that technology is category-sensitive: it cannot be a universal solution for athletes defined as totally blind [PAGE 2]. The recursive trap is that the coach may assume the device handles the task, the athlete may assume the coach has verified it, and the federation may assume the athlete has adapted.

The clock is the shared coordination artifact that can break or stabilize this recursion. Chess has a dedicated accessible clock with tactile buttons and audio/voice announcements [PAGE 6]. Shot put has a 60-second preparation window [PAGE 3]. Running selection uses timing certifications and MQS [PAGE 5]. Swimming and running have age and competition categories [PAGE 2][PAGE 5]. A clock is fair only if all parties share the same latency model. If an athlete believes the coach believes the clock is fair, but the audio announcement lags, the athlete may lose time without anyone intending harm. The counter-trap is to add more time or more technology without calibrating the shared clock. The better adjudication is joint clock calibration: athlete, coach, and classifier agree on cue latency, measurement method, and fallback. The paper’s recommendations for awareness and training [PAGE 7] are essentially attempts to align these nested beliefs, but they need explicit verification loops rather than one-way awareness campaigns.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Define a veto predicate
\[
V=V_{\mathrm{phys}}\lor V_{\mathrm{bio}}\lor V_{\mathrm{weather}}\lor V_{\mathrm{class}}.
\]
If \(V\) is true, the technology cannot be the sole intervention. The physical layer covers sensing and actuation. The paper’s audible ball depends on a “rattling sound” from internal bearings [PAGE 6]; audible markers along the throwing circle depend on sound reaching the athlete [PAGE 6]; GPS and biomechanical wearables depend on radio and sensor contact [PAGE 6]. A physical veto occurs when the signal is below threshold: for acoustic cues, \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\leq \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{th}}\); for GPS, \(\sigma_p>w_{\mathrm{lane}}/2\); for a shot-put stopper, \(F_{\mathrm{stop}}\) exceeds safe interaction force. The impact equation from the previous section gives a proof by contradiction: if a thrown shot is to be stopped within a short distance \(d\), then \(F_{\mathrm{stop}}=mv^2/(2d)\) grows without bound as \(d\to 0\). For a plausible \(7.26\,\mathrm{kg}\) shot at \(12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), stopping within \(0.10\,\mathrm{m}\) requires about \(5.2\,\mathrm{kN}\) average force. That is not a minor firmware feature; it is a mechanical veto. The athlete’s request for an “inbuilt speed breaker” [PAGE 5] is understandable, but the safer layer is the audible marker and throwing-circle orientation described by the paper [PAGE 6], plus human spotter protocols.

The biomechanical layer covers human reaction and body constraints. A swimmer approaching a wall needs the cue before the turn; if \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}>\tau_{\mathrm{event}}=L/v\), the cue is biomechanically late even if electronically correct [PAGE 4]. A T11 runner is defined as needing a blindfold and “must have to run with a guide” [PAGE 3]. Therefore a device that removes the guide must prove \(\sigma_p\leq w_{\mathrm{lane}}/2\) and must also handle unexpected obstacles. The paper says the guide “helps by calling out any obstacles ahead and letting the athlete know how much of the race is left” [PAGE 3]. That is a social-biomechanical loop, not just a localization loop. A GPS watch cannot automatically replace obstacle callouts.

The weather layer covers rain, wind, crowd noise, and pool acoustics. Acoustic attenuation can be written as
\[
A(d)=A_0+\alpha d
\]
in decibels, where \(A_0\) is reference loss and \(\alpha\) is an environment-dependent attenuation coefficient in \(\mathrm{dB\,m^{-1}}\). Rain and wind increase \(\alpha\) and raise \(P_n\), lowering \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\). Pool water and crowd noise similarly mask audio cues. The paper’s audible ball and pitch-side markers [PAGE 6] are therefore weather-sensitive. A weather veto occurs when the predicted \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\) at the athlete’s ear falls below threshold for the duration of the event. In that regime, the correct adjudication is to retain a guide, coach, or tactile backup, not to assert that the device “works.”

The classification layer is the final veto. The source distinguishes B1 total blindness, B2 severe impairment, and B3 partial impairment [PAGE 2]; J1 blind or almost blind and J2 partially sighted [PAGE 2]; F11-F13 [PAGE 3]; and T11-T13 [PAGE 3]. A device validated on a partially sighted group cannot be assumed to work for a totally blind group. If a proposal uses visual displays, eye-tracking, or residual-vision cues for B1 or T11 athletes, \(V_{\mathrm{class}}\) is true. The cross-layer proof is simple: if any layer veto is true, the safe system must include a non-technological fallback. The paper’s own requirement that T11 runners use a guide [PAGE 3] is an explicit fallback constraint. The adjudication rule is therefore: deploy only when \(V\) is false, or deploy with a verified fallback when \(V\) is true.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three verifiable counterfactual vetoes use measurable outcomes rather than opinion. They are framed as reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards checks: if the counterfactual fails the measured criterion, the policy is vetoed.

Scenario 1: Audio-only swimming wall/lap cue without noise adaptation. Counterfactual: deploy a waterproof audio cue in a pool with measured \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\leq 10\) and no adaptive noise cancellation. Verifiable reward: synchronized underwater video plus hydrophone recording gives cue latency and missed-wall rate. Prediction: median cue latency will exceed \(200\,\mathrm{ms}\), and missed-wall cues will increase by at least \(30\%\) relative to a quiet-water baseline. Veto if the missed-wall rate exceeds the coach-defined safe threshold or if latency exceeds \(\tau_{\mathrm{event}}=L/v\) for the athlete’s approach speed. This directly tests the paper’s swimming need to know “when to stop and when to flip” [PAGE 4].

Scenario 2: GPS-only lane keeping on a shared track for T11 runners. Counterfactual: remove the guide and rely on a wearable GPS tracker on the same track used by sighted athletes. Verifiable reward: RTK ground-truth position error and near-miss count over repeated trials. Prediction: radial position error \(\sigma_p\) will exceed \(1.0\,\mathrm{m}\) in at least \(20\%\) of trials, which is larger than a typical lane half-width, and near-miss events will increase relative to guided running. Veto if \(\sigma_p>w_{\mathrm{lane}}/2\) or if any near-miss occurs. This is grounded in the paper’s statement that “Shared tracks are the same as those of the normal athlete, causing navigation difficulties” [PAGE 5] and in the T11 rule that the athlete “must have to run with a guide” [PAGE 3].

Scenario 3: In-flight “speed breaker” shot-put implement. Counterfactual: activate a stopping mechanism immediately after release and require the shot to stop within \(0.10\,\mathrm{m}\) of travel. Verifiable reward: high-speed video and force-plate measurement of stopping distance and peak force. Prediction: for \(m=7.26\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(v=12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), the average stopping force will be approximately \(5.2\,\mathrm{kN}\) over \(0.10\,\mathrm{m}\), and peak force will be higher. Veto if the required force exceeds the safe interaction threshold or if the device changes the implement’s certified mass, balance, or flight. This is a direct check on the athlete’s requested “inbuilt speed breaker technology” [PAGE 5]. The safer counterfactual is not a stopping ball but an audible-marker and spotter protocol along the throwing circle [PAGE 6], which does not create a high-force actuation problem.
