**O1. Accuracy hierarchy and differential-error gate.**

\]
\[
E_{\mathrm{SUT}}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k^{\mathrm{SUT}}-\mathbf{x}_k\right\|_2^2\right)^{1/2}\ \mathrm{m}.
\]
The report’s design heuristic is \(E_{\mathrm{SUT}}/E_{\mathrm{GT}}\ge 10\), derived from [PAGE 4]: “Typically the ground truth system measurements should be an order of magnitude greater than those obtained by the system being evaluated.” Assumption: independent errors with correlation \(\rho\in[-1,1]\). \[
E_{\Delta}^{2}=E_{\mathrm{GT}}^{2}+E_{\mathrm{SUT}}^{2}-2\rho E_{\mathrm{GT}}E_{\mathrm{SUT}}\ \mathrm{m}^2.
\]
Breakdown: if \(\rho\to 1\), \(E_{\Delta}\to |E_{\mathrm{SUT}}-E_{\mathrm{GT}}|\), so shared calibration bias can make a 10:1 nominal ratio meaningless. If \(E_{\mathrm{GT}}\gtrsim E_{\mathrm{SUT}}/10\), the gate is not met. If \(E_{\mathrm{GT}}\) is claimed to be zero, suspect identity of GT and SUT sensors or overfitting rather than physical truth.

**O2. Six-degree-of-freedom weighted pose error.**

Assumption: the object trajectory is band-limited below \(f_{\max}\). Breakdown: if \(f_s<2f_{\max}\), aliasing occurs and two distinct trajectories can map to the same sample sequence. Under schedule pressure, the crisis habit regression is predictable: the experimenter returns to the most familiar, visually inspectable GT modality—usually optical fiducials or a mocap rig—because it is easy to explain in a status meeting. The source gives the exact reason this regression is dangerous. Optical systems “require line of sight” and are affected by occlusion and fluorescent/sunlight interference [PAGE 11]. Visual fiducials are “highly visible so they can give systems under test unwanted information” [PAGE 16]. If the SUT is optical, a visible tag turns the evaluation into a partially supervised task: the SUT may lock onto the GT marker rather than the object. The experimenter’s belief—“the GT is independent of the SUT”—is false, and the recursive loop is: experimenter designs visible GT, SUT senses GT, SUT performance is inflated, experimenter updates belief that the algorithm is better than it is. The counter-trap is to use a GT channel that is invisible or non-cooperative to the SUT: embedded radio-frequency tags, UWB, or inertial sensors. The report notes that radio systems “do not require line of sight and can be embedded in the tracking object, and can be invisible to optical tracking systems” [PAGE 16]. But the counter-trap has its own cost: UWB accuracy is “0.5 m” [PAGE 16], and RFID active accuracy is “1.5 m” [PAGE 16]. Invisible GT may be too coarse for pose certification, so the correct recursive move is hybrid: optical metrology where LOS is guaranteed, radio identity where optical fails, and inertial continuity with drift correction. The experimenter must model which channel the SUT can see and run an ablation with the GT marker removed; if SUT performance collapses, the GT leaked.

The clock is the second recursive pressure point. Crisis habit regression treats timestamps as a software afterthought and assumes that two logs can be aligned post hoc. [PAGE 4] explicitly warns of “synchronization, latency issues, and time drift between the ground truth system and the system under test.” The counter-trap is a hardware trigger or a shared clock; the report notes that HumanEva synchronization was done “by software in [28] and by a hardware trigger in [29]” [PAGE 9]. A hardware trigger removes the unknown offset but not drift; the experimenter must still measure \(\tau(t)\). The recursive ToM here is that the SUT and GT are not observing the same event if the clock is wrong. A 20 ms offset at 1 m/s is a 20 mm spatial disagreement, which can exceed the entire GT error budget. The “clock” is therefore not a metadata field; it is a physical layer in the error budget. The correct counter-trap is to treat synchronization as a measured quantity with its own uncertainty, not as a post-processing convenience.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source supports a hard veto logic: if a physical-layer predicate is false, the GT modality is inadmissible for that test regardless of its reported accuracy. First, optical line-of-sight veto. [PAGE 11] states that optical systems “require line of sight to an object (hence are affected by occlusion)” and are “impacted by interference of light signals from fluorescent lights and sunlight.” If the test volume has intermittent occlusion, the occlusion singularity in O5 applies: \(E_{\mathrm{NLOS}}\to\infty\) makes the expected GT error diverge. In a cluttered bin-picking scene, a visible fiducial may be blocked by parts or by the human body; the GT is then not a noisy measurement but a missing measurement. Veto unless \(P_{\mathrm{LOS}}\) is measured and the NLOS frames are recovered by an independent channel.

Second, illumination and weather veto. The report’s optical interference clause includes sunlight [PAGE 11]. For a controlled indoor factory cell with no solar path and no fluorescent flicker on the camera, weather is not a first-order veto. But if the cell has skylights, open loading docks, or direct solar irradiance, optical GT is vetoed unless spectral filtering and exposure control are validated. UWB is described as usable “outdoor and indoor” [PAGE 16], and inertial systems can be used “both indoors and outdoors, and in any light condition” [PAGE 19], but neither is a pose-accurate substitute for sub-centimetre optical metrology. Weather proof therefore means: no solar or precipitation path into the calibrated volume; otherwise the optical modality is conditionally inadmissible.

Third, material veto. RFID is not material-agnostic: the report says tags must be selected considering “part composition (metal parts will reflect some RFID frequencies)” [PAGE 16]. Magnetic mocap is “susceptible to magnetic and electrical interference from metal objects in the scene” [PAGE 20]. If the manufacturing cell contains steel parts, conveyors, or motors, magnetic GT is vetoed unless the field distortion is mapped and compensated. Radio GT may still identify metal parts, but its localization accuracy can degrade; identity truth and pose truth must be separated.

Fourth, dynamic-speed veto. The iGPS conflict on [PAGE 12] is a direct warning: static 0.2 mm accuracy does not imply dynamic 0.2 mm accuracy, and the reported dynamic errors range from 3–4 mm at 1 m/s in one study to <0.3 mm at 3 m/s in another. The veto condition is empirical: if the object’s speed and acceleration exceed the validated dynamic envelope of the GT system, the GT is inadmissible until a path-specific dynamic calibration is performed.

Fifth, biomechanical veto for human mocap. Passive optical markers produce “three degrees of freedom for each marker, and rotational information must be inferred from the relative orientation of three or more markers” [PAGE 17]. If the body occludes fewer than three markers on a segment, orientation is underdetermined. Markerless systems require “strong lighting, no occlusion, and a room with simple white walls” [PAGE 19], so they are vetoed in a general cluttered factory. Mechanical exoskeletons are “not very accurate and not comfortable to wear because of the heavy exoskeleton” [PAGE 20]. Inertial systems drift and require correction [PAGE 19]. The biomechanical adjudication is therefore segment-specific: use passive markers where at least three are visible, inertial where markers are occluded, and hardware sync to align them; otherwise the articulated GT is not valid.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
