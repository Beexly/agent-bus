<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s experiments stand at the confluence of several intellectual currents converging in early 20th-century psychology. Historically, they react against Watsonian behaviorism’s strict S-R contiguity doctrine, which held that learning required immediate reinforcement pairing. By demonstrating that unrewarded exposure still supported later rapid improvement, Tolman introduced intentionality (“purposive behavior”) back into scientific discourse—not as mystical will, but as goal-directedness inferable from observable patterns.

Scientifically, his work inherits from Köhler’s insight problems in apes and Hull’s habit-strength calculus, yet diverges sharply. Where Hull emphasized drive reduction as sole motivator, Tolman posited intermediate variables like expectations and meanings. His notion of “cognitive maps” presaged modern neuroscience concepts of place cells and grid cells discovered decades later in hippocampal recordings. Indeed, O’Keefe & Nadel’s *The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map* (1978) explicitly credits Tolman.

There’s also a retrocausal element: Tolman designed these mazes anticipating future discoveries about neural representation. He imagined brains constructing hypotheses about space—akin to scientists forming theories—and testing them via movement. In this sense, the animal behaves like a mini-researcher, gathering evidence silently until decisive confirmation arrives. That metaphor shaped entire fields including connectionism and artificial intelligence planning algorithms.

Moreover, consider the multi-week trap inherent in such paradigms: researchers often interpret single-session improvements as proof of latent learning, failing to account for gradual fatigue recovery or circadian rhythms influencing performance. Tolman avoided this by controlling feeding schedules rigorously and using separate housing cages—ensuring that the one-hour delay wasn’t confounded by physical exhaustion or sleep cycles. Still, modern replications sometimes overlook these controls, misattributing spontaneous remission of stress-induced suppression to cognitive breakthroughs.

Furthermore, the assumption noted in the footnote—that food is universally more demanded than confinement—is historically contingent. Later studies show that under certain conditions (overcrowding, predator scent), even hungry animals avoid baited locations. This reveals a deeper genealogical thread: the tension between biological imperatives and experiential learning about safety. Tolman himself acknowledged this complexity elsewhere, noting that “meaning” derives from whole situations, not isolated stimuli.

Thus, his legacy lies not just in specific results but in framing questions appropriately: How do organisms represent their world? What constitutes a ‘goal’? And crucially, when does representation guide action versus remaining dormant? These remain central challenges in computational psychiatry, robotics, and AI alignment today.
