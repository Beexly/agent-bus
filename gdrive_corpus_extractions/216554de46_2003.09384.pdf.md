\[

\]
\[
R_{YA}^{t}=R_{YA}^{t-1}+e_A\lambda k,\qquad
R_{YH}^{t}=R_{YH}^{t-1}+\gamma\bigl(R_{YA}^{t}-R_{YA}^{t-1}\bigr).
\]
Units: ratings and errors are goals; \(\lambda\) and \(\gamma\) are dimensionless learning rates; \(k\) is a dimensionless multiplier. The source sets \(k=3\) when both teams had previously played fewer than 38 matches, otherwise \(k=1\) [PAGE 6]. The error terms are:
\[
e_H=\Delta o_H-\Delta p_H,\qquad e_A=\Delta o_A-\Delta p_A,
\]
\[
\Delta o_H=G_{oH}-G_{oA},\qquad \Delta o_A=G_{oA}-G_{oH},
\]
\[
\Delta p_H=G_{pH}-G_{pA},\qquad \Delta p_A=G_{pA}-G_{pH}.
\]

\]
in goals [PAGE 7–8]. \(RD\) is the only observable node; all other nodes are latent [PAGE 8]. The \(RDL\) parent has 23 discrete states [PAGE 8]. The conditional density of \(RD\) given \(RDL\) is a Gaussian mixture:

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific genealogy runs from Bayesian networks to football rating systems to probabilistic scoring rules. The source states: “A BN is a type of a probabilistic model introduced by Pearl (Pearl, 1985) that consists of nodes and arcs” [PAGE 2]. The pi-rating lineage is from Constantinou and Fenton (2013), and the source says “The pi-rating is a football rating system that determines team ability based on the relative discrepancies in scores between adversaries” [PAGE 5]. The scoring-rule lineage is from Constantinou and Fenton (2012), with RPS described as a multinomial ordinal extension of the Brier score [PAGE 14]. The football-modeling lineage includes Dixon and Coles (1997), Rue and Salvesen (2000), Crowder et al. (2002), Goddard (2005), Hvattum and Arntzen (2010), Constantinou and Fenton (2013), and hybrid BN/rating models [PAGE 1, PAGE 26–28]. The source’s novelty is not a new Bayesian primitive; it is the coupling of a modified pi-rating to a Beta-Binomial BN whose graph is fixed by the temporal order possession -> shots -> shots on target -> goals [PAGE 6–7].

The retrocausal element is in validation. The ratings are strictly temporal: a prediction at \(t\) uses ratings at \(t-1\) [PAGE 11]. But the BN is “a machine learning model independent of time and is validated using leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV)” [PAGE 11]. The source justifies this by invariance: “it does not matter whether the data comes from past or future” because the conditional relationships are assumed invariant for the average EPL team [PAGE 11]. This is a retrocausal validation design: future matches help estimate the probability of past matches, under an invariance assumption. The source supports the assumption with consistent accuracy across 13 seasons, including seasons not used for BN training [PAGE 11], and with the claim that “the goal difference, remains essentially unchanged over time” [PAGE 16]. The trap is multi-week and multi-season: if the invariant relationship shifts, the LOOCV estimate becomes optimistic, and the model’s apparent stability is an artifact of pooling future data.

Multi-week traps identified by the source include: (i) single-season threshold optimization, because “the optimal threshold \(\theta\) varies dramatically between seasons” [PAGE 18]; (ii) insufficient samples per rating-difference state, because the 23-state discretization requires more than 50 points per interval [PAGE 8]; (iii) the integer-only optimization of \(k\), which the source flags as a limitation [PAGE 6]; (iv) the use of only one settlement offset per match, which the source says is a possible limitation [PAGE 22]; (v) data consistency problems in the first five seasons of the settlement data [PAGE 22]; (vi) the minimum five-season data requirement for well-informed priors [PAGE 16]; and (vii) the oscillatory component of the rating-difference-to-goal-difference relation, which suggests that goal data alone may not fully explain team ability [PAGE 24]. The source’s own genealogy therefore contains both a strength and a warning: the rating-BN combination is historically grounded, but its temporal validation is conditional on a stationary goal-generation process.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression is the analyst’s tendency to convert a short-horizon success into a fixed rule. In this source, the dangerous habit is selecting the decision threshold that maximizes one season’s outcome and treating it as stable. The source explicitly says that “the optimal threshold \(\theta\) varies dramatically between seasons” [PAGE 18] and that single-season optimization should be discouraged [PAGE 25]. A second regression is to overfit the 23-state \(RDL\) discretization by creating intervals with too few samples; the source requires more than 50 data points per interval [PAGE 8]. A third regression is to ignore the 38-match rating convergence horizon and treat early-season ratings as equally reliable [PAGE 6]. A fourth is to treat the LOOCV estimate as a strict forward-looking estimate even though the BN pools future data [PAGE 11]. A fifth is to interpret the linear rating-difference-to-goal-difference relation as exact, despite the source’s observation that “the linear relationship is oscillatory” [PAGE 24].

The counter-trap is a pre-registered decision architecture. Before seeing a season’s results, the analyst should fix the rating update rule, the \(RDL\) partition, the minimum sample per interval, the minimum number of seasons, the metric (RPS and Brier score), and the evaluation horizon. The source’s own counter-traps are: use at least five seasons of league data [PAGE 16]; keep more than 50 samples per \(RDL\) state [PAGE 8]; restrict optimization to matches where both teams have at least 38 prior matches [PAGE 12]; report average RPS 0.195 and Brier score 0.248 as baseline anchors [PAGE 15]; and distinguish between maximizing a single-season objective and maximizing the aggregate objective. The source notes that optimizing for maximum profit per season guarantees aggregate profit maximization, but optimizing for maximum ROI per season does not guarantee aggregate ROI maximization [PAGE 18–19]. This is a counter-trap against local optimization.

The clock is the multi-scale time structure. The micro-clock is 90 playable minutes [PAGE 9]. The meso-clock is 38 matches for rating convergence [PAGE 6]. The macro-clock is the 13-season evaluation window [PAGE 15] and the 27-season rating-training window [PAGE 1]. The discretization clock is the 23-state \(RDL\) partition [PAGE 8]. The modeler must recursively represent three selves: the past self who chose the model, the present self who evaluates it, and the future self who will face a new season with a different optimal threshold. The source’s warning is that the future self cannot reliably know the optimal threshold before the season starts [PAGE 18]. The recursive counter-trap is to optimize the meta-rule, not the season-specific threshold: choose a static rule across seasons, report its aggregate metrics, and audit whether any claimed advantage survives a change in the \(RDL\) partition, a forward-only validation, and the fixed-90-minute assumption.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer imposes hard constraints. Possession is a fraction: \(P\in[0,1]\), and away possession is \(1-P\) [PAGE 9]. Possession minutes are \(P\times90\) and \((1-P)\times90\), under the assumption of 90 playable minutes [PAGE 9]. Shots, shots on target, and goals are nonnegative counts. The source’s Beta parameterization for \(p(G)\) sets \(\beta\) equal to shots on target minus goals scored [PAGE 9]; therefore the likelihood is undefined if goals exceed shots on target. The source’s \(p(ST)\) parameterization sets \(\beta\) equal to shots minus shots on target; therefore the likelihood is undefined if shots on target exceed shots. The source’s \(p(SM)\) parameterization sets \(\beta\) equal to minutes minus shots; therefore \(p(SM)\) is not a probability if shots exceed minutes. These are exact physical vetoes, not statistical preferences.

The biomechanical layer is not explicitly modeled. The BN has no nodes for player load, sprint count, injury status, recovery time, or mechanical efficiency. The only biomechanical proxy is the separation of home and away ratings to capture home advantage and away disadvantage [PAGE 5]. Therefore, any claim that a biomechanical variable “explains” the source’s predictions is non-identifiable from the source alone. The source does acknowledge a related environmental shock: COVID-19 seasons with fewer or no fans, and it says the model “can be easily adjusted” by assigning away ratings to both teams if home advantage is irrelevant [PAGE 26]. That is a cross-layer adjustment, but it is not a biomechanical model.

The weather layer is also absent. The source has no weather, pitch, temperature, wind, or precipitation node. Weather can veto the model only through the playable-minute assumption: if a match has \(M\neq90\) playable minutes, the correct Binomial trial count should be \(P\times M\), not \(P\times90\). The source explicitly assumes “a match lasts 90 playable minutes” [PAGE 9]. Extreme weather can also alter \(p(SM)\), \(p(ST)\), and \(p(G)\), but the source provides no conditional variables for those effects. Therefore, a weather-adjusted claim cannot be validated without adding new nodes and re-estimating the conditional distributions.

The cross-layer veto proof is therefore conditional: the model is physically admissible only when \(a>0\), \(\beta>0\), \(P\in[0,1]\), \(p(SM),p(ST),p(G)\in[0,1]\), shots on target \(\le\) shots, goals \(\le\) shots on target, and playable minutes \(=90\). If any condition fails, the model’s exact likelihood is undefined or must be replaced by a smoothed or continuous extension. The source’s empirical evidence is limited to the rating-difference and possession-shots-shots-on-target-goals chain [PAGE 6–9]; it does not provide physical, biomechanical, or weather variables. Any cross-layer assertion beyond that chain is a vetoable extrapolation.
