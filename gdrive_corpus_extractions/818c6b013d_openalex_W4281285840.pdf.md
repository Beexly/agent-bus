<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Using recursive theory of mind as an audit lens, not as a claim in the paper, the GraphNet is a shallow recursive interaction model: each node’s hidden state is updated through message passing with all other nodes, so an agent’s imputed state depends on a recursive summary of other agents’ temporal contexts [PAGE 3]. The source motivates role-invariance because “the role of any individual player may change multiple times throughout a given trajectory sequence” and models “should be invariant to permutations of player orders within each team” [PAGE 2]. That is a theory-of-mind-adjacent design choice: predict behavior conditioned on game context rather than fixed role labels. However, the model does not explicitly represent beliefs, intentions, counterfactual opponent models, or strategic recursion. It learns a Gaussian latent state per agent per timestep and shares information through graph edges [PAGE 3, PAGE 10]. Therefore it can capture correlations in movement but cannot, by itself, prove that it has learned a recursive opponent model.

Crisis habit regression. Under prolonged occlusion, the model can regress toward learned habitual formation or average motion because the prior has no direct observation of the missing agent. The source warns that interpolation methods “can particularly suffer from a decrease in accuracy in situations where off-screen players behave defensively or offensively, exhibit sudden movements, or are off-screen for extended periods of time” [PAGE 4]. The Graph Imputer is designed to reduce this through bidirectionality and interaction context, but the failure mode remains: if the missing agent’s true behavior is a tactical deviation from the training distribution, the prior can produce a plausible but habitual trajectory. The reported \(L_2(\mathrm{Min.})\) over six samples can hide this by selecting a lucky sample; the mean loss is the safer habit-regression indicator [PAGE 5].

Counter-trap. The bidirectional design is a counter-trap for causal deployment: it uses future observations to correct past imputations. The source is explicit that this is “bidirectional use of temporal information at test-time” [PAGE 8]. That is valid for post-hoc analytics such as pitch control, but invalid for live decision-making where future frames do not yet exist. A user who treats the imputed trajectory as an online forecast has fallen into the counter-trap: the model’s apparent accuracy partly comes from information that would be unavailable at the claimed decision time. The paper’s pitch-control use case is post-hoc, which is consistent, but real-time use would require a unidirectional variant and a separate evaluation.

Clock. The temporal clock is mismatched across layers. Raw tracking is 25 frames per second; training sequences are 240 frames, or 9.6 s, downsampled to 6.25 frames per second [PAGE 4]. The simulated camera yields consecutive in-frame duration of \(4.94\mathrm{s}\pm3.49\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 11]. The fusion weight depends on \(\vec{\tau}_t^i\) and \(\overleftarrow{\tau}_t^i\), the number of timesteps to the nearest future and past observations [PAGE 10]. If occlusion exceeds the typical in-frame duration, the nearest-observation clock can be far away in both directions, and the prior dominates. This is the precise regime where habit regression and pitch-control error should be stress-tested. The 60 by 40 pitch discretization also imposes a spatial clock: errors are averaged over coarse cells, so local errors near goalkeepers can be diluted [PAGE 6, PAGE 11]. <CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The source’s equations do not contain a hard physical constraint operator. There is no acceleration bound, turning-radius constraint, fatigue state, collision term, or surface-friction term in Eqs. (1) through (15) [PAGE 9, PAGE 10, PAGE 11]. Physical plausibility is described as an ideal learned property: “Well-performing models will, ideally, learn the key behavioral characteristics of player interactions and physics (e.g., velocities, constraints on acceleration, player turning radii, etc.) given the available positional information to make realistic predictions” [PAGE 5]. The word “ideally” is decisive: the model is not proved to enforce those constraints. A predicted trajectory can be physically implausible and still have low \(L_2\) if it matches the missing ground-truth positions. The downstream pitch-control model does use a “physics-based motion model, incorporating factors such as ball time-of-flight and player time-to-intercept” [PAGE 11], but the authors use “a slightly simplified variant” with constant reference ball speed and 75th-percentile player and ball speeds [PAGE 11]. Therefore physical veto proof is conditional: pitch control can reject some impossible arrivals through its own motion model, but the Graph Imputer’s raw trajectory is not physically vetoed.

Biomechanical layer. The source contains no joint angles, gait phase, muscle state, injury status, fatigue, or player-specific biomechanical parameters. It tracks “all on-pitch players and the ball” at 25 frames per second [PAGE 4] and models positions in \(d=2\) [PAGE 2]. Any biomechanical inference from these outputs is unsupported by the source. A biomechanical veto can therefore only be adjudicated externally: if a predicted displacement requires a speed or turn outside a separately validated biomechanical envelope, the trajectory should be rejected, but the paper does not supply that envelope. The same applies to player-specific top speed; the source uses a common 75th-percentile reference rather than individual physiological profiles [PAGE 11].

Weather layer. The source text contains no weather variables, no rain, wind, temperature, humidity, lighting, or surface-condition state across pages 1 through 13. The camera model is geometric: it tracks the ball and masks players by intersecting the camera view cone with the pitch plane [PAGE 4]. Weather could affect vision, ball flight, traction, and player speed, but none of those mechanisms appear in the model or evaluation. Therefore any claim that the Graph Imputer is robust to weather is vetoed as unsupported. A weather-conditioned evaluation would require new input channels and a new camera/visibility model; the current paper cannot adjudicate it.

Cross-layer adjudication. The only environment layer explicitly modeled is occlusion through the simulated camera. The source reports average in-frame players and consecutive in-frame duration [PAGE 11], and it demonstrates downstream pitch-control errors under partial observability [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. Physical, biomechanical, and weather layers are outside the formal model. Thus a strict veto hierarchy is: first reject unsupported weather and biomechanical claims; second treat physical realism as a learned prior, not a guarantee; third accept occlusion claims only within the simulated camera distribution. The paper’s strongest defensible claim is that, under its simulated ball-tracking camera and its dataset split, the Graph Imputer lowers \(L_2\) and pitch-control MAE relative to the tested baselines. It is not proof of universal physical, biomechanical, or weather robustness.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
