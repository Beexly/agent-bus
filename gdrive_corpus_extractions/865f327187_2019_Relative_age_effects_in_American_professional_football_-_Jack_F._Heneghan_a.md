The multi-hop derivation begins with a null model for birth-month counts. Let \(O_m\) be observed professional-football births in calendar month \(m\), and let \(B_m\) be CDC births in month \(m\). The null probability is \(\hat p_m = B_m / \sum_{m'} B_{m'}\), a dimensionless probability, and the expected count is \(E_m = N \hat p_m\), measured in players. With \(N=18{,}898\), the paper reports \(\chi^2 \approx 66.3\), significant at \(p &lt; 0.001\) [PAGE 189]. That rejection is the first hop: professional football births are not distributed like the CDC null. The second hop is directional: January has the largest percentage excess, about 14 percent, and February the second largest, about 10 percent [PAGE 189]. The third hop converts counts into per-year affected players, about six excess January-through-March players per year, with fewer than ten missing summer births per year [PAGE 189]. A robustness check on players born on or after 1990 yields about 8.4 excess January births and 5.9 excess February births per year [PAGE 190]. The fourth hop checks school cutoffs: among 3,147 players in fall-cutoff states, Table 4 shows a January spike and a November spike, with \(\chi^2 \approx 21\), \(p=0.034\) [PAGE 192–193]. The fifth hop checks position: skill players show strong evidence, combination players weaker evidence, line players do not reach conventional significance, but the global 12-by-3 test does not reject independence, \(p \approx 0.46\) [PAGE 195]. The sixth hop checks draft selection bias: among 2,513 draftees from 2008–2017, the coefficient on “Days born after January 1” is negative for Av, \( -0.005^{**}\), negative but insignificant for Av per season, and negative and marginally significant for games, \( -0.011^{*}\) [PAGE 197]. The paper concludes this is not the positive coefficient pattern expected under NHL-style selection bias [PAGE 198].

The gray-zone audit is the important part. First, the paper itself warns that the percentage differences are not monotonic: “The percentage differences in Table 1 are not monotonically-decreasing across months, and most notably there are positive differences in November and December” [PAGE 189]. A simple January-to-December calendar effect cannot explain that shape. Second, the paper admits an identification limit: “Because school years and the start of football seasons are roughly coincidental, we cannot distinguish between fall-winter births that are valuable for a player on account of a school cutoff versus those that are valuable due to the timing of football” [PAGE 192]. Third, the position analysis is explicitly weaker than a clean interaction test: the separate tests are “suggestive evidence,” but the 12-by-3 table does not reject, \(p \approx 0.46\) [PAGE 195]. Fourth, the cutoff sample is unbalanced: fall-cutoff players number 3,147, winter-cutoff players only 418, and the winter test is null, \(p=0.39\) [PAGE 193]. Fifth, the null distribution assumes CDC monthly birth rates can be extended backward to 1940; the paper checks 1968–1996 and still rejects, \(\chi^2 33.7\), \(p \approx 0.0004\), but that check does not eliminate cohort-specific or socioeconomic composition effects [PAGE 189]. Sixth, the draft regression tests selection bias conditional on being drafted and on recorded performance; it cannot rule out selection earlier in the pipeline. The paper’s own summary is that rejection of the birth-month null “does not necessarily identify what sort of an age effect, if any, is at work” [PAGE 200]. Thus the defensible inference is layered: a robust birth-month distortion exists; school cutoff and sport-season mechanisms are plausible and partly supported; position-specific claims are not established by the global test; and draft selection bias is not supported in the direction found in NHL studies.

The source’s core operators are count comparisons, chi-square goodness-of-fit statistics, percentage deviations, and regression coefficients. For month \(m\), let \(O_m\) be observed player births, a count in players; let \(B_m\) be CDC monthly births, a count; let \(N=\sum_m O_m\); and let
\[
\hat p_m=\frac{B_m}{\sum_{m'}B_{m'}},\qquad E_m=N\hat p_m,
\]
where \(\hat p_m\) is dimensionless and \(E_m\) is in players. The percentage deviation is
\[
\Delta_m=100\frac{O_m-E_m}{E_m}\quad (\%),
\]
and the goodness-of-fit statistic is
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{m=1}^{12}\frac{(O_m-E_m)^2}{E_m},
\]
which is dimensionless with \(df=11\) under the null that monthly birth probabilities equal the CDC probabilities. The paper reports \(\chi^2\approx 66.3\), \(p &lt; 0.001\), for the 18,898-player table [PAGE 189]. For quarters \(q\), the same operator is applied to \(O_q=\sum_{m\in q}O_m\) and \(E_q=N\sum_{m\in q}\hat p_m\), with \(df=3\); the paper reports \(\chi^2\approx 28.3\), \(p &lt; 0.001\) [PAGE 190]. For the position-by-month table, the independence statistic is
\[
\chi^2_{\text{ind}}=\sum_{m=1}^{12}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{(O_{mj}-E_{mj})^2}{E_{mj}},
\]
with \(df=(12-1)(3-1)=22\); the paper reports \(p\approx 0.46\) [PAGE 195].

The draft-performance model is a regression of player success on relative age and normalized draft position:
\[
Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_d D_i+f(P_i)+\alpha_{\text{pos}(i)}+\gamma_{\text{year}(i)}+\varepsilon_i.
\]
Here \(Y_i\) is approximate value in Av points for the first column, Av per season in Av points per season for the middle column, and games played in games for the censored column; \(D_i\) is days born after January 1, in days; \(P_i\) is normalized draft pick, dimensionless; \(f(\cdot)\) is a natural spline with five degrees of freedom; \(\alpha_{\text{pos}}\) and \(\gamma_{\text{year}}\) are fixed effects; and \(\varepsilon_i\) is an error term. The reported coefficient on \(D_i\) is \(-0.005^{**}\) for Av, \(-0.001\) for Av per season, and \(-0.011^{*}\) for games [PAGE 197]. The games model is described as censored from the left at zero and from the right at 16 games per season; a latent-index representation is
\[
G_i^*=\beta_0+\beta_d D_i+f(P_i)+\alpha_{\text{pos}(i)}+\gamma_{\text{year}(i)}+\varepsilon_i,
\]
\[
G_i=
\begin{cases}
0, & G_i^*\le 0,\\
G_i^*, & 0<G_i^*<16T_i,\\
16T_i, & G_i^*\ge 16T_i,
\end{cases}
\]
where \(G_i\) is observed games and \(T_i\) is the maximum number of seasons available since drafting. The paper does not enumerate every distributional assumption of the censored model, so the formalization should be read as the standard latent-index form consistent with the reported specification.

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are explicit. The chi-square test breaks down when expected counts are too small; the usual Cochran-type rule is \(E_m&lt;5\), and in the limit \(E_m=0\) the statistic is singular. The main monthly table has large expected counts, but the winter-cutoff subset has only 418 players, so power is limited and the null \(p=0.39\) is not strong evidence of absence [PAGE 193]. The null distribution also breaks if CDC month probabilities are not transportable across cohorts; the paper’s 1968–1996 check rejects with \(\chi^2 33.7\), \(p\approx0.0004\), which supports robustness but does not prove cohort-invariance [PAGE 189]. The regression breaks down under rank deficiency—for example, if position or draft-year indicators are collinear with the spline basis or if a fixed-effect cell has no variation—and under selection on unobservables. The draft-bias test is especially sensitive to conditioning: it asks whether relatively younger players outperform their draft positions, not whether they were selected earlier in youth football.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a retrocausal chain in which later designs reinterpret earlier nulls. The older literature on relative age in hockey—Barnsley and Thompson (1988), Addona and Yates (2010)—established the January-heavy birth pattern and gave the template for football. Deaner, Lowen, and Cobley (2013) then introduced the selection-bias mechanism: evaluators may mistakenly give fewer opportunities to relatively younger players than their latent talent warrants. The source quotes this definition directly: “By selection bias, we mean that evaluators (e.g. teachers, coaches) mistakenly grant fewer opportunities (e.g. instruction, access to elite group or team) to relatively younger individuals than is warranted by their latent ability or talent” [PAGE 186]. That concept retroactively turns earlier null football findings into possible power or design failures rather than evidence of no effect.

The immediate football lineage is mixed. MacDonald et al. (2009) studied 2,144 United States-born NFL players on 2004 rosters and found no relative age effect, hypothesizing that youth football’s age-and-weight placement may blur the effect [PAGE 186–187]. Stanaway and Hines (1995), Steingröver et al. (2016), and studies of the Canadian Football League and Japan’s X-League also reported nulls [PAGE 187]. Against that, Beyer et al. (2016) found an excess of December–February births among 2,546 draftees, Böheim and Lackner (2012) found evidence among 1,673 draftees, and Glamser and Marciani (1992) offered suggestive college evidence [PAGE 187]. Heneghan and Herron’s contribution is to enlarge the sample to 18,898 players born 1940 or later and to add school cutoff data from Colasanti (2007), thereby making the earlier nulls look underpowered or specification-limited [PAGE 188, 191]. The paper’s own multi-week trap is visible in its window analysis: a 1974–1985 birth window yields 4,278 players and \(p\approx0.04\), while a narrower 2002–2006 window yields 3,570 players and \(p\approx0.07\) [PAGE 191]. A single-season or short-window design can therefore flip between marginal significance and null without any change in the underlying phenomenon.

The retrocausal lesson is that “no effect” in football was never a stable scientific object. It was a function of sample size, position pooling, school-cutoff heterogeneity, and the age at which players are drafted. The NFL’s three-years-out-of-high-school rule gives evaluators more time to observe late maturers, which the paper argues should weaken relative age effects compared with the NHL’s 18-year-old draft [PAGE 187–188]. Later work that ignores that institutional clock can mistakenly compare football and hockey as if they shared the same developmental window. The multi-week trap is thus not merely statistical; it is institutional. Pooling across decades hides changes in youth football, school cutoffs, and draft rules. Restricting to one roster year hides power. Treating January as the only test hides the November and September signals that the paper itself reports [PAGE 189, 193]. The genealogy therefore runs from hockey birth-month counts to football nulls to large-sample rejection to school-cutoff and position refinements, with each later step retroactively changing what the earlier nulls mean.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that evaluators, players, and families are modeling one another’s models. A coach or scout sees an early-born player who is bigger or faster at age 14 and must infer whether the advantage is maturation, selection, or latent skill. If the evaluator’s crisis habit is to regress to the most salient cue—relative age—then the evaluator over-rewards the January-born player and under-rewards the December-born player. That is the selection-bias channel. The paper’s own definition captures the recursive error: evaluators “mistakenly grant fewer opportunities” to relatively younger individuals [PAGE 186]. The player and family, in turn, may anticipate the evaluator’s heuristic and make strategic choices about school district, reclassification, or early specialization. The paper notes that “one’s school district, and potentially one’s position, are not negligible factors in determining success,” though it also says the aggregate returns to strategic district choice are microscopic given the huge denominator of youth players [PAGE 200].

The counter-trap is overcorrection. If evaluators become aware of relative age effects, they may begin to prefer late-born players as “undervalued,” producing a relative age reversal or a new bias. The literature the paper cites includes exactly this possibility: Gibbs, Jarvis, and Dufur (2012) argue for a relative age reversal among elite hockey players, and McCarthy, Collins, and Court (2016) argue for reversal in rugby and cricket [PAGE 185]. The recursive danger is that the correction itself becomes a crisis habit. A scout who knows the January effect may over-weight a December birthday and mistake “young for his class” for “high ceiling.” The paper’s NFL draft evidence is relevant here: the negative coefficient on days born after January 1 means that, holding draft position constant, relatively younger players did not outperform their draft slots in the direction expected under NHL-style bias [PAGE 197–198]. That does not prove evaluators are unbiased; it only fails to find the specific bias signature.

The clock is the institutional and developmental timer that constrains all of this. School cutoff dates assign children to cohorts; the football season runs through fall and winter; the NFL draft requires players to be at least three years out of high school [PAGE 187]. These clocks are not synchronized. A September-born player may be young for a fall-cutoff school cohort but old relative to the January-born player in the same grade; a November birth may benefit from the football season even if it is late in the calendar year. The paper admits that school years and football seasons are “roughly coincidental,” so the two clocks cannot be separated cleanly [PAGE 192]. Recursive theory of mind therefore has three layers: the evaluator’s belief about maturity, the player’s belief about the evaluator’s belief, and the institutional clock that determines who is compared with whom. The paper’s strongest conclusion is not that one layer dominates, but that the observed birth-month distortion is consistent with maturation and selection, while the draft-bias signature is absent [PAGE 198–199].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer is the strongest prima facie mechanism. The maturation hypothesis says relative age differences drive differences in size, strength, and coordination [PAGE 186]. In football, line positions often require players who “often weigh at least 300 pounds,” while skill positions require throwing, catching, running, and kicking [PAGE 194]. If the effect were purely physical maturation, line positions should be at least as vulnerable as skill positions, perhaps more so, because weight and strength mature later. The data do not show that: skill positions are significant at \(p &lt; 0.001\), combination positions at \(p &lt; 0.05\), and line positions at \(p=0.13\); the global 12-by-3 interaction is \(p\approx0.46\) [PAGE 195]. So the biomechanical layer does not veto the existence of a relative age effect, but it vetoes a strong claim that the effect is concentrated in weight-dependent line play. If anything, the pattern is opposite to the simple weight hypothesis, though the global test prevents a firm position conclusion.

The biomechanical layer also complicates practice. The paper argues that line techniques are contact-intensive and cannot be practiced repeatedly like throwing or catching, and that line success depends on strength and weight that “do not fully develop in players until they are grown” [PAGE 194]. That is a plausible mechanism for delayed maturation effects, but it is not a clean identification proof. A physical mechanism predicts a January-heavy distribution; a skill-learning mechanism predicts early birth advantages in throwing and catching; a selection mechanism predicts evaluator bias. All three can coexist. The paper’s own language is conditional: the position results are “suggestive evidence” and the interaction test is null [PAGE 195]. Thus the biomechanical veto is partial: it blocks an overstrong weight-only story, not the general relative age finding.

The weather and season layer is the most serious confound. Football is a fall-winter sport, so being born in late fall or early winter may be beneficial for development independent of school cutoff. The paper states this directly: “we cannot distinguish between fall-winter births that are valuable for a player on account of a school cutoff versus those that are valuable due to the timing of football” [PAGE 192]. The fall-cutoff table shows a January spike and a November spike, with November at 15 percent above expected and January at 14 percent above expected [PAGE 193]. That is consistent with both a school-cutoff effect and a sport-season effect. The winter-cutoff subsample has only 418 players and a null result, \(p=0.39\), so it cannot adjudicate the weather/season hypothesis [PAGE 193]. The cross-layer veto proof therefore runs as follows: a physical maturation story is supported but not isolated; a biomechanical position story is not established by the global test; a weather/season story is plausible and cannot be separated from school cutoff with these data. None of these layers vetoes the existence of a birth-month distortion, but each vetoes a monocausal interpretation. The paper’s own conclusion is appropriately layered: relative age effects exist, school timing matters, position evidence is suggestive, and draft selection bias is not found [PAGE 198–199].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Veto scenario 1: school-cutoff discontinuity. Take students born in a narrow window around a state’s fall cutoff, for example late August versus early September under a September 1 cutoff. If the school-cutoff mechanism is real, the child born just after the cutoff is old for the grade and should have a higher probability of reaching professional football than the child born just before the cutoff, holding calendar month and family background as constant as possible. A verifiable RLVR reward can check a discontinuity estimate \(\Delta_{\text{cutoff}}=P(\text{pro football}\mid \text{just after cutoff})-P(\text{pro football}\mid \text{just before cutoff})\). Prediction: \(\Delta_{\text{cutoff}}&gt;0\) in fall-cutoff states if the school mechanism is causal; near zero if only calendar-year effects matter. The source supports the logic that cutoff states create a school-based age effect [PAGE 187, 192], but its aggregate design cannot directly estimate this discontinuity.

Veto scenario 2: position-by-month interaction. If skill positions are more vulnerable than line positions, then the 12-by-3 table of month by position group should reject independence, and the skill-minus-line excess should be positive and stable across cohorts. A verifiable reward can check the interaction statistic and the sign of the skill-line contrast. Prediction: the interaction test should have \(p &lt; 0.05\) and the skill excess should exceed the line excess. The source reports separate skill, combination, and line tests, but the global interaction test gives \(p\approx0.46\) [PAGE 195]. Under this veto, the position-specific claim is not verified as an interaction; it remains exploratory.

Veto scenario 3: draft-selection-bias sign test. If NFL evaluators underweight relatively younger players in the way Deaner et al. describe for the NHL, then holding normalized draft position constant, relatively younger players—those born later in the calendar year—should outperform older players. A verifiable reward can check the sign and significance of \(\beta_d\) in the Av, Av-per-season, and games models. Prediction under selection bias: \(\beta_d&gt;0\) and significant. The source reports \(\beta_d=-0.005^{**}\) for Av, \(-0.001\) for Av per season, and \(-0.011^{*}\) for games [PAGE 197]. The paper concludes that the expected positive pattern is absent and that “we see negative estimates, one of which is statistically significant” [PAGE 198]. Under this veto, the NHL-style selection-bias hypothesis is rejected for the NFL draft sample. These three scenarios are verifiable because each has a pre-specified sign or significance threshold, a defined sample, and a stated prediction that can be checked against the reported statistics.
