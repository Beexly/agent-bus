<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression. When a model operates under high uncertainty—occlusion, rare classes, or degraded tracking—it regresses to high-prior habits. In FOOTPASS, Drive and Pass dominate the annotation distribution; under stress, a sequence decoder will over-predict these frequent classes and under-predict Tackle, Block, and Header. This is visible in the reproduced X3D-L+DST baseline, where Tackle achieves F1 = 0.000 and Header only 0.287 [PAGE 6-7]. The regression is recursive: missed rare events reduce gradient signal for rare classes, which further suppresses their representation in subsequent decoding steps, reinforcing the habit of predicting common, low-entropy actions. The confidence threshold τ = 0.15 [PAGE 14] does not prevent this; it merely filters low-scoring outputs after the habit has already shaped the autoregressive beam.

Counter-trap. ME-DST installs three counter-traps against crisis habit regression. First, role embeddings r_n provide explicit identity priors that decouple tactical responsibility from short-term motion similarity, preventing the model from attributing a rare event (e.g., a Header) to the wrong player simply because their trajectory resembles a Pass [PAGE 6-7]. Second, factorized temporal–spatial attention preserves within-player evolution before cross-player exchange, so fleeting ball contacts are not averaged away by global flattening [PAGE 3, 13]. Third, removing the global temporal refinement layer is itself a counter-trap: it blocks the tendency to over-smooth temporal localization with redundant long-range attention, keeping event boundaries sharp within the ±12-frame tolerance [PAGE 10]. The net effect is a shift of the decision boundary toward rare, structurally complex classes: Cross (+0.216), Header (+0.211), Block (+0.173), and even the previously extinct Tackle (+0.044) [PAGE 6-7].

Clock. The architecture is bounded by several hard temporal constraints. Inference partitions each match half into consecutive 750-frame windows (~30 s at 25 fps), with zero-padding only on the final incomplete window [PAGE 14]. Events are matched within ±12 frames (±0.48 s), a clock tolerance finer than many biomechanical contact events [PAGE 4-5]. The autoregressive decoder halts after at most 25 events per window or upon EOS [PAGE 13], imposing an event-rate ceiling. Visual inputs are 50-frame clips (~2 s) processed by the TAAD branches [PAGE 4, 12], meaning the sequence model receives already-compressed perceptual snapshots rather than raw video. The interaction of these clocks creates a context horizon: roughly 30 seconds of rolling memory, a 0.48-second localization aperture, and a 25-event output buffer. If a match phase contains more than 25 salient ball actions in a window, or if a substitution invalidates role-slot identity mid-window, the clock constraints bind and the model must discard or misattribute events.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto layers with cross-layer adjudication.

Physical veto (tracking and visibility). The entity tensor relies on synchronized player tracking organized into 26 role slots [PAGE 3, 11]. Five raw tracking attributes include a binary visibility indicator [PAGE 12]. Under occlusion—ball-hidden events, player pile-ups, or camera occlusion—the visibility flag drops, and velocity/acceleration features become undefined or stale. The paper reports that ME-DST improves recall on ball-hidden events from 0.391 to 0.480, but this remains far below live-play recall (0.794) [PAGE 7], confirming a physical veto: when tracking is missing, tactical descriptors cannot be computed, and the encoder must rely solely on visual logits that are themselves degraded under occlusion.

Biomechanical veto (speed, acceleration, contact). Tactical features f0 (speed) and f3 (acceleration magnitude) are computed from consecutive visible observations [PAGE 12-13]. Rapid biomechanical events—tackles, blocks, shots—occur on sub-second timescales that may fall inside or at the edge of the ±12-frame (0.48 s) tolerance [PAGE 4-5]. If a tackle or block occurs during a tracking gap, f1 (teammate distance), f2 (opponent distance), and f7 (pressure index) are corrupted, and the model lacks the geometric context needed to distinguish a Tackle from a Drive. This explains why Tackle remains the most difficult class (F1 = 0.044) despite overall gains [PAGE 6-7, 10].

Weather veto. The source contains no weather modality (rain, wind, lighting, pitch condition). Broadcast video quality and ball roll physics under adverse weather are unmodeled; visual logits from X3D-L and Swin3D-S are the only weather-sensitive pathway, and they are frozen during sequence training [PAGE 12, 14]. Because the model cannot condition on weather state, adverse conditions that alter ball trajectory or player motion constitute an unmodeled veto layer.

Cross-layer adjudication. Vetoes propagate across layers: (1) Perception-to-sequence: errors in TAAD visual logits, player detection, or ROIAlign pooling cannot be corrected by the denoising decoder because the branches are trained separately [PAGE 11]. (2) Tracking-to-identity: role-slot indices are converted to jersey numbers using shirt-number observations interpolated across missing frames; this procedure is explicitly unreliable near substitutions, tactical reassignment, or long invisible stretches [PAGE 11]. A veto at the identity layer corrupts the four-dimensional match criterion (frame, team, jersey, class), converting otherwise correct temporal detections into false positives. (3) Feature-to-architecture: eight-dimensional tactical features hurt flat DST but help multi-entity encoding [PAGE 5-6], so the veto boundary is not the feature set alone but the feature–encoder interface. Adjudication rule: a prediction is accepted only if visibility, role-slot integrity, and visual confidence jointly pass thresholds; otherwise the event is vetoed or assigned low confidence s_k < 0.15 and discarded at evaluation [PAGE 14].
