<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the ordinal reinforcement clock.

Crisis habit regression: under high drive or crisis conditions, an organism tends to emit the response with the greatest accumulated habit strength rather than a newly acquired, weaker alternative. In Hull's own algebra, behavior potential is a multiplicative function of habit strength, drive, and incentive; when drive is elevated during crisis, the term with the largest H_N dominates. Because H_N approaches M asymptotically and early increments are large while later increments shrink [PAGE 114], a deeply overtrained response (large N) sits near M and will outcompete a novel response (small N, low H_N) under stress. This is habit regression: performance collapses to the historically strongest habit, not necessarily the most adaptive one.

Counter-trap: the error of reading small ΔH_N near asymptote as "weak habit" or "poor learning." The equation explicitly states that increments decrease as the physiological limit is approached [PAGE 114]. Small late increments are the expected signature of a strong habit near its ceiling, not evidence of weakening. A counter-trap intervention would mistakenly increase reinforcement magnitude or switch schedules in response to small increments, thereby disturbing a stable asymptote and potentially inducing extinction or frustration-induced variability.

The clock: N is an ordinal reinforcement counter, not a wall-clock variable. The model makes no prediction about how many calendar minutes or days elapse between reinforcements. A subject receiving 10 reinforcements in one hour and another receiving 10 reinforcements over one week have identical N and therefore identical predicted H_N under this formalism, despite vastly different temporal contexts. Treating "week" as the unit of N conflates reinforcement density with time and produces false predictions about retention, fatigue, and circadian modulation. Any applied use must log actual reinforcement deliveries; calendar duration is not a sufficient statistic for N.

Formal veto condition: if an intervening physical perturbation causes H_N < H_{N-1} or a step decrease in habit strength, the acquisition-only model is vetoed and an extinction/recovery layer must be added.
