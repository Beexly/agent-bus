### 1. Abstract minimization operator — anchor: [PAGE 2] "Existence and uniqueness results for minimization problems"

Let $U$ be a reflexive Banach space, $K \subseteq U$ nonempty, closed, convex, and $J: U \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}$.

$$
\text{(P)}\qquad \inf_{u \in K} J(u).
$$

**Existence (Weierstrass–Tonelli in reflexive setting):** if $K$ is bounded (or $J$ is coercive: $J(u) \to +\infty$ as $\|u\|_U \to \infty$) and $J$ is *weakly sequentially lower semicontinuous*, then (P) attains a minimizer. **Uniqueness:** if $J$ is *strictly convex* on $K$, the minimizer is unique. Anchor for convexity: [PAGE 2] "Convex functionals".

**Units:** $J$ is a *cost functional*; in the control setting below, $J$ carries units of **[cost]** and every term inside $J$ must be dimensionally homogeneous (checked explicitly in §3).

**Breakdown regime:** reflexivity fails (e.g., $U = L^1$ or $U = C^0$ with sup-norm) ⇒ minimizing sequences can fail to converge weakly ⇒ **infimum not attained**. This is not a technicality: the dam free-boundary problem on [PAGE 2] is precisely where one must choose the weak space ($H^1$ rather than $C^1$) to retain attainment. Additionally, strict convexity fails whenever the cost is *linear in the control* (bang-bang control) ⇒ uniqueness is lost and extremals form a set.

### 2. Projection and variational inequality operator — anchor: [PAGE 2] "Projection Operators in Hubert Spaces", "The general form of Elliptic Variational Inequalities"

For $K$ closed convex in a Hilbert space $H$ with inner product $(\cdot,\cdot)_H$:

$$
P_K(u) \;=\; \arg\min_{v \in K} \|u - v\|_H ,
\qquad
(u - P_K u,\; v - P_K u)_H \le 0 \quad \forall v \in K .
$$

The variational inequality: find $u \in K$ with

$$
\big(A(u) - f,\; v - u\big) \;\ge\; 0 \qquad \forall v \in K .
$$

**Equivalence to complementarity:** if $K = \{v : v \ge \psi\}$ (obstacle $\psi$), then $A(u) - f \ge 0$, $u - \psi \ge 0$, $(A(u)-f)(u-\psi) = 0$ a.e. — the **obstacle/Signorini form** used for the semi-permeable dam boundary on [PAGE 2].

**Units (dam instantiation):** with hydraulic head $h$ in $\mathrm{m}$ and hydraulic conductivity $K_h$ in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, Darcy flux $q = -K_h \nabla h$ has units $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; the operator $A(u) = -\nabla\cdot(K_h \nabla h)$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so the duality pairing $(A(u),v)$ with $v$ in $\mathrm{m}$ yields $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ — a flux, as required. **Dimensional check passes.**

**Breakdown regime:** (i) $A$ merely *hemicontinuous + monotone* but not coercive on $K$ ⇒ VI may be solvable only in a *coset* (the dam problem's non-uniqueness up to an additive constant on the outflow face). (ii) $K$ not convex (e.g., a non-convex admissible set of plate thicknesses) ⇒ VI formulation invalid; one must revert to non-smooth minimization. (iii) When $K$ degenerates to $\{0\}$ — the **vanishing obstacle regime** $\psi \to 0$ — the active set changes discontinuously and the solution map $f \mapsto u$ is only Lipschitz, not differentiable; Newton-type methods then lose local quadratic convergence.

### 3. Optimal control / indirect (adjoint) operator — anchor: [PAGE 3] "INDIRECT METHODS FOR OPTIMAL CONTROL PROBLEMS", "The elimination of the State"

State $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ (or a Banach space), control $u \in U_{\mathrm{ad}} \subseteq L^\infty(0,T; \mathbb{R}^{m})$:

$$
\dot{x}(t) = f\big(x(t), u(t), t\big), \quad x(0) = x_0,
\qquad
J(u) = \int_0^T L(x,u,t)\,dt + \varphi\big(x(T)\big).
$$

Hamiltonian and costate (Pontryagin form):

$$
H(x,u,p,t) = L(x,u,t) + p^{\top} f(x,u,t),
\qquad
\dot{p}(t) = -\,\partial_x H, \quad p(T) = \nabla \varphi\big(x(T)\big),
$$

with stationarity $\partial_u H = 0$ on $\operatorname{int} U_{\mathrm{ad}}$, or the variational inequality $\partial_u H \cdot (v-u) \ge 0$ on the boundary of $U_{\mathrm{ad}}$.

**Units:** $[L] = \text{cost}\cdot\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $[\varphi] = \text{cost}$, $[p] = \text{cost}$ per unit state (the costate is a *shadow price* — dimensionless-cost per state-unit). The product $p^\top f$ therefore has units $\text{cost}\cdot\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, matching $L$. **Dimensional check passes.** This is the "elimination of the State" made explicit: the reduced gradient is $\nabla J(u) = \partial_u H$.

**Breakdown regime:** (i) $f$ or $L$ non-smooth in $x$ ⇒ costate ODE has no classical solution; must pass to Filippov/contingent formulation. (ii) Control enters linearly and $U_{\mathrm{ad}}$ is a box ⇒ $\partial_u H = 0$ has no interior solution; the extremal is bang-bang and the "gradient" is a switching function, so smooth optimization diverges. (iii) **Stefan case** [PAGE 3]: the state map is not classically differentiable w.r.t. the free-boundary position, so the naive adjoint is *wrong*; one needs the enthalpy (weak) formulation to move the derivative inside the time derivative.

### 4. Stefan free-boundary energy balance — anchor: [PAGE 3] "The one-phase Stefan problem", "The two-phase Stefan Problem", "An optimal control problem related to the inverse Stefan problem"

Enthalpy form (weak, distributional):

$$
\partial_t H(\theta) - \nabla\cdot\big(k(\theta)\,\nabla \theta\big) = 0,
\qquad
H(\theta) = \rho c\,\theta + \rho L\,\chi_{\{\theta > \theta_m\}},
$$

with $\theta$ temperature $[\mathrm{K}]$, $k$ thermal conductivity $[\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}]$, $\rho$ density $[\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}]$, $c$ specific heat $[\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}]$, $L$ latent heat $[\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}]$, $H$ volumetric enthalpy $[\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}]$.

Two-phase Stefan condition at the interface $s(t)$ with normal $\mathbf{n}$ from solid to liquid:

$$
\rho L\,\dot{s}(t) \;=\; k_l\,\frac{\partial \theta_l}{\partial \mathbf{n}} - k_s\,\frac{\partial \theta_s}{\partial \mathbf{n}} .
$$

**Dimensional check:** LHS $[\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}][\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}][\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}] = \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}s^{-1}}$ (energy flux). RHS $[\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}][\mathrm{K\,m^{-1}}] = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}s^{-1}}$. **Consistent.**

**Singularity / breakdown regimes (exact):**
1. **Vanishing phase:** $s(t) \to 0$ — the liquid phase disappears; the latent-heat term degenerates and the classical two-phase formulation loses meaning. This is a genuine singularity, not a numerical artifact.
2. **Nucleation / onset:** $s(0) = 0$ with supercooled liquid — the problem is *not* well-posed without a Gibbs–Thomson / surface-tension regularization; mushy-zone models replace the sharp interface.
3. **Jump in $k$ at $\theta = \theta_m$:** the enthalpy is discontinuous in $\theta$ (a Heaviside), so the *inverse map* $\theta(H)$ is set-valued at $H = \rho L$. Any solver that differentiates $\theta$ w.r.t. $H$ pointwise at the interface will produce $\pm\infty$. This is the precise reason the "Algorithm ALG-R" [PAGE 3] must be a *regularized* scheme.
4. **Ill-posedness of the inverse problem** [PAGE 3]: recovering boundary data from interior temperature is exponentially ill-conditioned in the number of modes; the book's remedy — recasting as optimal control — trades ill-posedness for a *Tikhonov-type* bias, whose weight is a modelling choice.

### 5. Fourth-order plate operator — anchor: [PAGE 3] "Decomposition of fourth order elliptic equations", "The clamped plate problem", "Optimization of plates", "A Fourth-Order Variational Inequality"

Clamped Kirchhoff plate:

$$
D\,\Delta^2 w = f \ \text{ in }\Omega,
\qquad w = \partial_{\mathbf{n}} w = 0 \ \text{ on }\partial\Omega,
$$

with deflection $w\ [\mathrm{m}]$, load $f\ [\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}]$, flexural rigidity $D = \dfrac{E t^3}{12(1-\nu^2)}\ [\mathrm{N\,m}]$ for thickness $t\ [\mathrm{m}]$, Young's modulus $E\ [\mathrm{Pa}]$, Poisson ratio $\nu$ (dimensionless).

**Dimensional check:** $[D][\Delta^2 w] = \mathrm{N\,m}\cdot\mathrm{m^{-3}} = \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$. **Consistent.**

**Breakdown regime:** the Kirchhoff hypothesis requires $\|\nabla w\| \ll 1$ and $t \ll \operatorname{diam}(\Omega)$. When the optimization ("Optimization of plates") drives $t \to 0$ in regions of low moment, the *thin-plate asymptotics itself fails* (membrane/shear deformation dominates) and the computed optimum is an artifact of an invalid model — a structural, not numerical, veto (see §CROSS_LAYER).

### 6. Quadratic program, KKT, interior point, Krylov — anchor: [PAGE 3] "The quadratic programming problem", "Interior-point methods for the Solution of problem (QP)", "Krylov subspace algorithms", "Preconditioning for MINRES and SYMMLQ", "Preconditioning for the KKT system"

$$
\text{(QP)}\quad \min_{x} \tfrac12 x^{\top} Q x + c^{\top} x
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad A x = b,\ \ x \ge 0,
$$

KKT system (block form, symmetric indefinite):

$$
\begin{pmatrix} Q & A^{\top} \\ A & 0 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \lambda \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} -c \\ b \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Why MINRES/SYMMLQ and not CG:** the KKT matrix above is symmetric *indefinite* whenever $A$ has full row rank and $Q \succeq 0$ with a nontrivial nullspace on the constraint directions. CG requires positive definiteness; MINRES and SYMMLQ are the admissible Krylov methods. **This inference follows directly from the two algorithm names on [PAGE 3].**

**Interior point:** the barrier subproblem replaces $x \ge 0$ by $-\mu \sum_i \ln x_i$; the central path $x(\mu)$ satisfies $Qx + c - A^{\top}\lambda - \mu X^{-1}\mathbf{1} = 0$. As $\mu \downarrow 0$:

$$
\kappa\big(\text{KKT}(\mu)\big) \;=\; \Theta(\mu^{-1}) \quad\text{(generic nondegenerate case, standard scaling).}
$$

**Krylov convergence bound (SPD case, for reference and for the preconditioned reduced system):**

$$
\|e_k\|_A \;\le\; 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^{k}\|e_0\|_A .
$$

**Consequence, and this is the operational content:** combining the two displays, the iteration count to reach fixed relative accuracy scales as $k = \Theta(\sqrt{\kappa}) = \Theta(\mu^{-1/2})$. So **interior-point accuracy and Krylov cost are locked together**; this is the quantitative form of the commutation defect flagged in `<THINK_TRACE>`.

**Breakdown regimes:** (i) $\mu \to 0$ with no rescaling ⇒ catastrophic cancellation in $X^{-1}\mathbf{1}$, loss of digits $\sim -\log_{10}\mu$. (ii) Rank-deficient $A$ (redundant constraints on a discretized free boundary) ⇒ KKT is singular; MINRES stagnates on the inconsistent component. (iii) Preconditioner built from a *different* discretization than the operator ⇒ the preconditioned Krylov subspace no longer contains the true residual direction; convergence degrades to no-preconditioning or worse.

### 7. HJB and Markov-chain approximation — anchor: [PAGE 4] "The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations", "The Markov chain approximation", "Approximation in Policy Space", "Approximation in Value Space"

$$
-\partial_t V(x,t) = \min_{u \in U}\Big\{ L(x,u,t) + \nabla_x V(x,t)\cdot f(x,u,t)\Big\},
\qquad V(x,T) = \varphi(x).
$$

**Units:** $[V] = [\varphi] = \text{cost}$; $[\partial_t V] = \text{cost}\cdot\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; $[L] = \text{cost}\cdot\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; $[\nabla_x V \cdot f] = \text{cost}\cdot\mathrm{m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = \text{cost}\cdot\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. **Consistent.**

**Breakdown / singularity regimes:** $V$ is generically only Lipschitz, not $C^1$; classical solutions fail and one requires **viscosity solutions**. Sharp failure: when two controls $u_1,u_2$ achieve the same minimum ("flat minimum"), the *argmin* is discontinuous in $x$ even though $V$ is Lipschitz — **value convergence does not imply policy convergence**. The Markov-chain approximation [PAGE 4] is *locally consistent* by construction, so this failure is invisible to a local-consistency check and only appears in the policy.

### 8. Epidemic control — anchor: [PAGE 3] "A CONTROL PROBLEM FOR A CLASS OF EPIDEMICS" (reconstruction only; no compartmental structure is named on the page)

Generic normalized form:

$$
\dot{S} = -\beta(t)\,\frac{S I}{N}, \quad
\dot{I} = \beta(t)\,\frac{S I}{N} - \gamma I, \quad
\dot{R} = \gamma I,
\qquad
R_0 = \frac{\beta}{\gamma}\ \ (\text{dimensionless}),
$$

with $\beta,\gamma \in [\mathrm{s^{-1}}]$ and $N$ total population (dimensionless fraction basis). Control $u(t)\in[0,u_{\max}]$ modulating $\beta(t) = \beta_0(1-u(t))$, cost $J = \int_0^T \big[\,c_I I(t) + c_u u(t)^2\,\big]dt$ with $[c_I] = \text{cost}$, $[c_u] = \text{cost}$.

**Breakdown regime:** the mass-action term $SI/N$ presumes homogeneous mixing; when $I \to 0$ the *relative* error in the gradient explodes because $\partial J/\partial u \propto I$, so **the adjoint signal vanishes exactly when the epidemic is controlled** — the classical "vanishing-gradient" trap in epidemic optimal control.

## <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>Historical/scientific lineage & multi-week traps</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

*Note: dates and attributions below are external lineage, **not** page-sourced. The drive pages supply only the section titles.*

**Lineage chain, reverse-engineered from the Contents ordering.**

The Contents on [PAGE 2] begins with functional analysis, not with control. That ordering is itself a *genealogical statement*: the book descends from the **direct methods of the calculus of variations** (Euler–Lagrange; Hilbert's program of minimizing functionals over function spaces), which reached its modern form in the mid-20th century through convex analysis and monotone operator theory. The section "Convex functionals" [PAGE 2] is the descendant of that program: convexity is what converts *stationarity* into *global optimality*.

The next ancestral line is the **variational inequality**, introduced to unify obstacle problems, unilateral contact, and free boundaries. The dam seepage example on [PAGE 2] ("The seepage flow of water through a homogeneous rectangular dam") belongs to the classical *phreatic-line* tradition in groundwater hydrology, whose modern treatment recasts it as an obstacle problem via a Baiocchi-type transformation. **Retrocausal consequence:** the ordering on [PAGE 2] — fluid-mechanics example *first*, general VI *second*, finite elements *third*, optimization methods *fourth* — is the pedagogical fossil of the historical order in which the field actually resolved the problem: physics motivated the inequality, the inequality demanded a weak formulation, the weak formulation demanded finite elements, and only then did optimization become tractable.

The **Stefan problem** [PAGE 3] descends from 19th-century heat-conduction-with-phase-change; its interface condition is a *flux balance*, and its modern treatment is the enthalpy/weak formulation. The *inverse* Stefan problem [PAGE 3] is the descendant of the continuous-casting and cryosurgery inverse-heat-conduction literature: the ill-posedness is structural (analytic continuation of boundary data), and the historical resolution — **replace inversion with optimization** — is exactly the book's move.

The **indirect method** [PAGE 3] traces to the Pontryagin maximum principle lineage; the **stochastic branch** [PAGE 4] traces to Bellman's dynamic programming and, for the discrete approximation, to the Kushner–Dupuis locally-consistent Markov-chain approximation. The **direct branch** [PAGE 3] traces to the post-1984 interior-point revolution and to the Krylov lineage (Hestenes–Stiefel for CG; Paige–Saunders for MINRES/SYMMLQ). The book's *decision* to put indirect methods (Chapter 4) *before* direct methods (Chapter 7) is retrocausally informative: the authorial judgment is that **the adjoint is the concept, the QP is the implementation**.

**Multi-week traps (the ones that take weeks, not hours, to detect).**

1. **The discretization-identity trap.** [PAGE 2] "The internal approximation of Elliptic Variational Inequalities" vs. [PAGE 3] "The quadratic programming problem." A VI discretized internally (conforming on $K_h \subset K$) and the same VI discretized by penalization are *different problems*; a code that mixes the two — assembling the VI mass/stiffness internally but applying an exterior penalty — solves neither. Symptom: convergence under mesh refinement to a limit that violates the discrete maximum principle.
2. **The free-boundary-mesh trap.** [PAGE 3] Stefan chapters. A fixed mesh that is *too coarse relative to the front speed* produces a front that lags by $\Theta(\Delta t)$ and is numerically *stable* — so it looks converged. Weeks are lost before one realizes the front position, not the temperature field, is the quantity of interest.
3. **The preconditioner-mismatch trap.** [PAGE 3] "Preconditioning for MINRES and SYMMLQ" + "Preconditioning for the KKT system." A preconditioner derived from an unconstrained reduction is *not* a valid preconditioner for the saddle-point system unless it respects the $(1,1)$-block/$(2,2)$-block structure; the failure mode is *slow*, not catastrophic, so it survives code review.
4. **The regularization-weight trap.** [PAGE 3] inverse Stefan + [PAGE 2] "The penalization method." Choosing $\alpha$ (Tikhonov) or $\varepsilon$ (penalty) by "it looks smooth" produces an answer that is a property of $\alpha$, not of the data. Weeks are lost re-running with different $\alpha$ before realizing no $\alpha$ is identifiable from the given data.
5. **The flat-minimum trap.** [PAGE 4] "Approximation in Policy Space" vs. "Approximation in Value Space." Value iteration converges; the greedy policy does not. Detected only by comparing policies across refinement levels, which nobody does by default.

## <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

**Framing.** In this drive there is no adversary in the game-theoretic sense; the "opponent" is the *numerical and physical environment*, and the "clock" is the **compute-and-time budget** expressed as the convergence-time constraint $T_{\text{wall}}$ in the Krylov/KKT loop. I model the designer's reasoning as a recursion over *what the designer believes the environment will do to the algorithm*.

**Level 0 — Crisis habit regression.** Under clock pressure, the designer reverts to the most rehearsed habit in the book: **penalize and run gradient descent** ([PAGE 2] "The penalization method" + "The steepest descent method"). This habit is *evolutionarily selected* because it always returns a number. Its regression signature is diagnostic: the penalty parameter $\varepsilon$ gets tightened reactively whenever the constraint violation is "too big," with no measurement of $\kappa$. Predicted observable: $\varepsilon$ decreases by a fixed factor every time the run "fails," i.e., $\log \varepsilon$ is a linear function of iteration-of-the-human-loop. This is the crisis tell.

**Level 1 — Counter-trap (first-order theory of the environment).** The designer models the environment's response: "if I tighten $\varepsilon$, the environment (the KKT conditioning) will push back with $\kappa \sim \varepsilon^{-1}$." The counter-move is to *stop tuning $\varepsilon$ and start tuning the formulation*: switch to "The Lagrange multipliers method" [PAGE 2] or the KKT/MINRES route [PAGE 3]. This is a genuine level-1 belief: the designer holds a model of the algorithm's *response* to the designer's action.

**Level 2 — Counter-counter-trap (the environment models the designer).** The environment's "belief" is encoded in the *discretization*: a mesh generated by an error estimator that was tuned to the *previous* solution will coarsen exactly where the new solution's front is moving. So the environment "anticipates" the designer's refinement strategy. The level-2 counter is to **decouple the estimator from the current iterate** (use a fixed reference estimator, or a hierarchical/embedded pair), i.e., to make the design *non-exploitable*. Formal statement: the mesh sequence $\{\mathcal{T}_k\}$ must not be a function of the iterate $u_k$ alone, else the pair $(u_k,\mathcal{T}_k)$ can converge in a self-consistent loop that is not a discretization of the continuous problem.

**Level 3 — Terminal level: the recursion terminates at physics.** The recursion cannot go deeper because there is a level that does not model anything — the **cross-layer veto** (next section). A solution that violates a maximum principle or an energy balance is *void*, regardless of the level of strategic sophistication. So the recursion has a **fixed point at the conservation law**, and a *finite depth*. This is the theoretical payoff: **recursive theory-of-mind in numerical optimal control is well-founded precisely because it is bounded by physics.** In a purely strategic setting the recursion is unbounded (infinitely many levels of "he thinks that I think..."); here it is cut off at depth 3 by the conservation layer.

**The clock, treated explicitly.** The wall-clock budget imposes a hard constraint $k \le k_{\max}$ on iterations. Combining with the interior-point/Krylov coupling $\kappa = \Theta(\mu^{-1})$ and $k = \Theta(\kappa^{1/2})$:

$$
k_{\max} \;\gtrsim\; C\,\mu^{-1/2} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mu \;\gtrsim\; \left(\frac{C}{k_{\max}}\right)^{2}.
$$

**Operational reading:** under a fixed clock, there is a *floor* on achievable barrier parameter, hence a *floor* on achievable constraint satisfaction. A designer who ignores this will attribute the residual constraint violation to a bug and will spend the remaining clock debugging. **The correct counter-trap is to declare the floor up front** and to report the optimality/feasibility pair, not a single "solution."

## <CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

A **veto** is an assertion that a computed optimum is *inadmissible* because it contradicts a lower (more fundamental) layer, independent of the value of the cost functional. Three layers are adjudicated here, each anchored to a section title in the drive.

### Veto A — Continuum/thermodynamic layer: the Stefan energy balance (biomechanical/thermal)

**Anchor:** [PAGE 3] "The two-phase Stefan Problem", "3.1 The two-phase Stefan Problem".

**Claim (veto):** any computed pair $(\theta, s)$ that violates
$$
\rho L\,\dot{s}(t) \;=\; k_l\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\theta_l - k_s\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\theta_s
$$
or that produces $\dot{s} > 0$ at a point where the liquid-side temperature is below $\theta_m$ is **void**.

**Proof sketch (entropy/monotonicity).** The Stefan problem is the Euler–Lagrange condition of a convex energy functional in the enthalpy variable; consequently the solution satisfies a comparison principle: if $\theta_1 \le \theta_2$ on the parabolic boundary, then $\theta_1 \le \theta_2$ in the domain. A computed front that advances into sub-melting-point material requires, by the interface balance, a positive liquid-side normal derivative at a point where $\theta_l < \theta_m$ — contradicting the comparison principle. Hence the computed state is not the discretization of any admissible solution; the numerical scheme has, at that point, lost monotonicity. **The optimum built on it is inadmissible at the physical layer, before any question of optimality is asked.** This is the exact analogue of a biomechanical "yield" veto: the model's constitutive inequality has been breached.

### Veto B — Elliptic maximum-principle layer (the "weather"/boundary-data layer, i.e., the dam)

**Anchor:** [PAGE 2] "The seepage flow of water through a homogeneous rectangular dam", "An Example from Fluid Mechanics problems in media with semi-permeable boundaries".

**Claim (veto):** with reservoir head $h_1$ upstream and tailwater $h_0$ downstream ($h_1 > h_0$), any computed piezometric head satisfying
$$
\max_{\Omega} h > h_1 \quad\text{or}\quad \min_{\Omega} h < h_0
$$
is **void**.

**Proof.** The seepage problem in the saturated region is $-\nabla\cdot(K_h \nabla h) = 0$ with $K_h > 0$; the operator is uniformly elliptic there. The weak maximum principle gives $\inf_{\partial\Omega} h \le h(x) \le \sup_{\partial\Omega} h$ for all $x \in \Omega$. Since the Dirichlet data are exactly $h_0, h_1$ on the wetted faces, the bound follows. Any overshoot is therefore *not* a physical solution but a discrete artifact (typically: the free surface was allowed to rise above the upstream level, or the semi-permeable condition was enforced with a sign error in the variational inequality). **This is the "weather veto" in its exact form: the forcing (boundary data) bounds the response, and no optimizer may exceed its own forcing.** An optimizer that "improves" the cost by raising $h$ above $h_1$ has optimized a constraint-violating point.

### Veto C — Structural layer: thin-plate validity (biomechanical analogue)

**Anchor:** [PAGE 3] "The clamped plate problem", "Optimization of plates", "A Fourth-Order Variational Inequality".

**Claim (veto):** an optimal thickness distribution $t^\star(x)$ is inadmissible if either (i) the Kirchhoff small-deflection hypothesis is violated, $\|\nabla w\| \not\ll 1$, or (ii) the von Mises stress exceeds the material yield stress $\sigma_Y$:
$$
\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}}\big(w(x), t^\star(x)\big) > \sigma_Y .
$$

**Proof.** The Kirchhoff functional is derived under the kinematic ansatz $u_\alpha = -z\,\partial_\alpha w$ and the neglect of transverse shear; these are *assumptions*, and the variational problem solved by "Optimization of plates" is the Euler–Lagrange equation of a functional that is only valid *within* the assumption's range. Outside it, the functional no longer represents the structure, so its minimizer is a minimizer of the wrong object. Separately, $\sigma_{\mathrm{vM}} \le \sigma_Y$ is a *state constraint*; if the optimization formulation omits it (as a purely geometric thickness optimization would), the mathematical optimum is routinely at $t \to 0$ in low-moment regions — physically, a hole. **Veto: the mathematical optimum is not a feasible structure.** The correct adjudication is that the state constraint must be added *before* the optimization, not checked after.

### Adjudication rule (the meta-rule)

Layers are ranked: **conservation/thermodynamics (A) > elliptic bounds/forcing (B) > constitutive validity (C) > optimality.** A solution that passes a higher layer and fails a lower one is rejected; a solution that passes all three but has a poor optimality gap is *accepted with a reported gap*. **Optimality never vetoes physics; physics always vetoes optimality.**

## <RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>3 verifiable RLVR veto scenarios + predictions</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

Each scenario is stated as a *machine-checkable* prediction: a specific functional form, a specific sign or slope, and a pass/fail criterion. "RLVR" here means the reward is computed by an automatic verifier on the artifact (log file, residual history, front trace), not by human judgment.

### Veto 1 — Penalization "convergence" claim vs. conditioning scaling

**Anchor:** [PAGE 2] "The penalization method"; [PAGE 3] "Krylov subspace algorithms", "Convergence properties".

**Claim under test:** "The penalty method converges, therefore the penalized system is a valid computational route for the dam VI."

**Verifiable prediction.** Let $\varepsilon$ be the penalty parameter and let $N_{\mathrm{it}}(\varepsilon)$ be the Krylov iteration count to reach a *fixed* relative residual tolerance on a fixed mesh. Then, for the standard exterior penalty applied to an obstacle-type VI:
$$
N_{\mathrm{it}}(\varepsilon) \;=\; \Theta\!\left(\varepsilon^{-1/2}\right)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d \log N_{\mathrm{it}}}{d \log(1/\varepsilon)} \;\approx\; \tfrac12 .
$$

**Verifier procedure:** run the solver at $\varepsilon \in \{10^{0},10^{-1},\dots,10^{-6}\}$ on a single fixed mesh; fit a line to $\big(\log(1/\varepsilon), \log N_{\mathrm{it}}\big)$; report the slope $\hat{s}$.

**Pass/fail:** $\hat{s} \in [0.4, 0.6]$ ⇒ the implementation is *consistent with* the theoretical coupling, and the "convergence" claim is at least not contradicted. $\hat{s} \approx 0$ ⇒ **VETO**: the reported iterations are being truncated by an iteration cap or a reused initial guess, and the "convergence" is an artifact of the stopping rule, not of the method. $\hat{s} > 1$ ⇒ **VETO**: the penalty is being applied inconsistently (e.g., different $\varepsilon$ in different blocks), producing super-physical ill-conditioning.

**Why verifiable:** it is a regression slope on a log–log plot from a log file. No human judgment.

### Veto 2 — Stefan front-position error order and monotonicity

**Anchor:** [PAGE 3] "2.3 The numerical realization of the Algorithm ALG-R", "3.3 A numerical algorithm".

**Claim under test:** "The enthalpy scheme resolves the two-phase front to first order in $\Delta t$."

**Verifiable prediction (two parts).**
(i) *Order:* against a manufactured solution with known front $s_{\mathrm{ex}}(t)$, the computed front error satisfies
$$
\big|s_h(T) - s_{\mathrm{ex}}(T)\big| \;=\; \Theta(\Delta t^{\,p}),
\qquad
p \approx \tfrac12 \ \text{for enthalpy/fixed-grid methods},\quad p \approx 1 \ \text{for front-tracking}.
$$
(ii) *Monotonicity:* the discrete enthalpy sequence must satisfy $H^{n+1} \ge H^{n}$ pointwise whenever the boundary data are non-decreasing in time; equivalently, no node may *decrease* in temperature while receiving heat.

**Verifier procedure:** (i) Richardson-style triple-refinement in $\Delta t$ at fixed $\Delta x$; estimate $p$ by least squares on $\log(\text{error})$ vs $\log \Delta t$. (ii) Scan the node-wise enthalpy trace for any negative increment under monotone forcing.

**Pass/fail:** $p \ge 0.45$ and zero monotonicity violations ⇒ pass. **$p \approx 1$ claimed but measured $\hat{p} \approx 0.5$** ⇒ **VETO** of the order claim (the code is an enthalpy method wearing a front-tracking label). Any monotonicity violation ⇒ **VETO** of the solution itself under Veto A of the previous section (thermodynamic layer), irrespective of $p$.

**Why verifiable:** $p$ is a fitted exponent; monotonicity is a sign check on an array.

### Veto 3 — Value-function convergence does not certify policy convergence (HJB / Markov chain)

**Anchor:** [PAGE 4] "The Markov chain approximation", "5.1 Approximation in Policy Space", "5.2 Approximation in Value Space".

**Claim under test:** "The Markov-chain approximation converges, so the computed optimal policy is the optimal policy."

**Verifiable prediction.** Construct a two-action MDP whose continuous limit has a *flat minimum*: $L(x,u_1) - L(x,u_2) = \delta$ independent of $x$. Then:
$$
\big|V_h(x) - V(x)\big| \;=\; O(h),
\qquad\text{but}\qquad
\mathbb{P}\big[\pi_h(x) \ne \pi(x)\big] \;\not\to\; 0 \ \text{as } h \to 0 \ \text{when } \delta = O(h).
$$
Equivalently: the *value* error is first order, but the *policy* mismatch probability stays bounded away from zero along the sequence $\delta_k = c\,h_k$.

**Verifier procedure:** for $h \in \{2^{-3},\dots,2^{-10}\}$, set $\delta = h$; run value iteration and policy extraction; record (a) $\sup_x |V_h - V|/h$ and (b) the empirical policy-mismatch fraction. Predict (a) bounded, (b) bounded away from 0.

**Pass/fail:** (a) bounded *and* (b) $\to 0$ ⇒ the problem is not in the flat-minimum regime; the policy claim survives. (a) bounded *and* (b) bounded away from 0 ⇒ **VETO** of the policy claim: value convergence has been used to certify a policy that is not certified. The correct report is the *suboptimality bound* $V_{\pi_h} - V \le 2\sup|V_h - V|$, which is $O(h)$ — a genuine, verifiable guarantee, and strictly weaker than policy equality.

**Why verifiable:** both (a) and (b) are computed numbers from a scripted experiment; the prediction is a specific (bounded, non-vanishing) signature.
