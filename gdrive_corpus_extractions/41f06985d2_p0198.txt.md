**Integral Operators \(T_f\) and their Adjoint Decomposition**

Let \(f\) be a locally integrable function on \(\mathbb{R}\). Define the integral operator

\[
(T_f h)(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} K(x,y) h(y) dy,\qquad K(x,y)=\frac{f(y)}{|x-y|}
\]

with \(K\) as the kernel. For suitable \(h\), \(T_f\) is densely defined on \(L^2(\mathbb{R})\). Its adjoint is given by

\[
(T_f^* g)(y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} K(x,y) g(x) dx.
\]

The product \(T_f^* T_f\) yields a self-adjoint operator whose spectral properties control the boundedness of \(T_f\). In Problem 7, the kernel degenerates at the diagonal, rendering standard Hilbert-Schmidt theory inapplicable. Instead, weak-type estimates such as

\[
\|T_f h\|_{L^{2,\infty}} \le C \|f\|_{L^\infty} \|h\|_{L^2}

provide crucial interpolation bounds, avoiding direct computation of the operator norm.

**Singular Integral Operators and Calderón-Zygmund Theory**

The fundamental building block is the Hilbert transform

\[
Hf(x) = \text{p.v.} \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\mathbb{R}} \frac{f(t)}{x-t} dt.

More generally, convolution with homogeneous kernels \(K(x)=\Omega(x/|x|)/|x|^n\) on \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (with \(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}} \Omega d\sigma =0\)) defines a bounded operator on \(L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)\) for \(1<p<\infty\). The key estimate is

\[
\|T_\Omega f\|_{L^p} \le C_p [\Omega]_{\text{lip}_\alpha} \|f\|_{L^p},\qquad \alpha=n-1,

where the right-hand side involves a Hölder norm of \(\Omega\). This theory resolves Problem 4 by providing criteria for atoms in measures induced by singular integrals. The use of Carleson measures and paraproduct decompositions further clarifies the relationship between atomic singularities and bounded mean oscillation (BMO) spaces.

**Wavelets and Localization Operators**

\[

**Step 1: Existence of a positive Lebesgue integrable function \(f\) with \(\int_{E} f^{2} d\mu = \infty\) for every open interval \(E\).**

Consider the function

\[
f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|x - r_n|}},
\]

where \(\{r_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) is an enumeration of the rational numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\). Each term \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|x - r_n|}}\) is Lebesgue measurable and positive, and since \(\mathbb{Q}\) is dense in \(\mathbb{R}\), every open interval \(E\) contains infinitely many rationals \(r_n\). Hence, the series diverges pointwise almost everywhere on \(E\) (the complement of a null set due to the density of rationals). Moreover, for any fixed \(n\),

\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|x - r_n|}} dx = 2 \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} dt = 4\sqrt{x}\Big|_{0}^{\infty} = 4,
\]

which is finite. By Tonelli's theorem, the series of integrals is summable, thus \(f\) is Lebesgue integrable over \(\mathbb{R}\):

\[
\int_{\mathbb{R}} f(x) dx = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \int_{\mathbb{R}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|x - r_n|}} dx = 4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1 < \infty.
\]

Now, let \(g(x) = f(x)^2\). Then

\[
g(x) = \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|x - r_n|}} \right)^2 \ge \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{|x - r_n|},
\]

since all terms are non-negative. For any open interval \(E\),

\[
\int_{E} g(x) dx \ge \sum_{n : r_n \in E} \int_{E} \frac{1}{|x - r_n|} dx.
\]

Because \(\int_{E} \frac{1}{|x - r_n|} dx = \infty\) whenever \(E\) contains a neighborhood of \(r_n\), and \(E\) contains infinitely many rationals, the right-hand sum diverges to infinity. Therefore,

\[
\int_{E} g(x) dx = \infty \quad \forall E \text{ open},\; E \neq \emptyset.
\]

Thus, defining \(\nu(E) = \int_{E} g(x) dx\) gives a σ-finite Borel measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure \(\mu\), but \(\nu(E) = \infty\) for every open interval \(E\) with non-empty interior.

**Step 2: Connection to Problems 3 and 4.**

- **Problem 3a (Consistency of λ-measures and ν-measures):** Let \(\lambda\) be Lebesgue measure. Since \(\nu\) is defined via an integrable density \(g\) with respect to \(\mu\), by Radon-Nikodym theorem, for any Borel set \(E\) with \(\mu(E)<\infty\),

\[
\nu(E) = \int_{E} g d\mu = \lambda(E) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad g(x)=1 \text{ a.e. }
\]

This contrasts with the classical situation where two measures agreeing on a generating class (e.g., intervals) imply equality on the entire σ-field only if they are both σ-finite. Here we see a rare case where the equality holds automatically due to the specific form of \(g\).

- **Problem 4 (Existence of Atoms):** An atom of \(\nu\) is a measurable set \(A\) such that \(\nu(A)>0\) and for any measurable \(B\subset A\), \(\nu(B)=0\) or \(\nu(B)=\nu(A)\). Consider any rational \(r_n\). For any \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[
\nu((r_n-\varepsilon, r_n+\varepsilon)) = \int_{r_n-\varepsilon}^{r_n+\varepsilon} g(x) dx \ge \int_{r_n-\varepsilon}^{r_n+\varepsilon} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}} dx = 2\sqrt{2\varepsilon}.
\]

Since \(\varepsilon\) can be chosen arbitrarily small, the measure of any neighborhood of \(r_n\) is strictly positive and finite. Hence, \(\{r_n\}\) forms an atom. More generally, any isolated point of the set \(\{r_n\}\) is an atom (though none exist here due to density).

- **Problem 5 (Continuity of ν):** A measure \(\nu\) is *continuous* if \(\nu(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k) = \lim_{k\to\infty} \nu(A_k)\) whenever \(A_k \searrow A\) with \(\mu(A)<\infty\). If \(\nu\) is non-atomic (no atoms), then \(\nu(\{x\})=0\) for all \(x\). Conversely, if \(\nu\) is atomic and admits an atom \(A\) with \(\nu(A)>0\), taking \(A_k=A\) for all \(k\) contradicts continuity. Our construction of \(\nu\) via \(g\) exhibits atoms precisely at rationals, hence \(\nu\) is not continuous.

**Step 3: Multilayer Implications and Counter-Paradoxes.**

The key insight lies in the tension between local integrability of \(f\) and global divergence of \(f^2\) on every open set. Standard tools like dominated convergence or Fubini's theorem do not apply because no integrable majorant exists for the series. This phenomenon is a prototypical example of a function that belongs to \(L^1\) but not to the Orlicz space generated by \(\Phi(t)=t\log(1+t)\), highlighting the failure of strong \(L^p\) embeddings in dimension one.
