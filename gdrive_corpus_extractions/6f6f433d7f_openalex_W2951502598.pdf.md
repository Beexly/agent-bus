<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Brier’s categorical verification score, Good’s logarithmic rule, Friedman’s spherical and “effective” scoring rules, and Epstein’s ranked-probability/CRPS tradition to Gneiting and Raftery’s strict-properness framework and Shannon entropy. The source cites these directly: Brier score [PAGE 2], logarithmic rule proposed by Good [PAGE 3–4], spherical rule [PAGE 2], “Continuous Ranked Probability Score (Epstein, 1969)” [PAGE 2], and “higher entropy corresponds to more uncertainty (Shannon, 1948)” [PAGE 15]. The genealogical hinge is Savage’s 1971 warning, quoted in the source: “any criteria for distinguishing among scoring rules must arise out of departures of actual subjects from the ideal” [PAGE 2]. That warning turns scoring-rule choice into a retrocausal act: the chosen rule retrospectively decides which historical forecast departures counted as disciplined caution and which counted as dangerous overconfidence. A rule that prefers higher entropy will narrate past caution as skill; a rule that prefers lower entropy will narrate past sharpness as skill. The multi-week trap is that changing the rule after observing outcomes can make the same forecast archive winner under one rule and loser under another. The source’s own conclusion is that “when one selects a scoring rule to estimate distribution parameters or choose between two competing experts, it amounts to deciding preferred departures” [PAGE 16]. A second trap is institutional: a rule chosen to calm one public-risk channel may systematically under-reward early warning in another, because log and spherical point in opposite entropy directions under equal L2 error. A third trap is boundary drift: as probabilities approach zero or as \(\gamma\) approaches \(\min(p,q)\), the log rule’s penalty becomes extreme, so threshold and smoothing choices made weeks earlier determine whether a model is ranked at all.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the default under stress: forecasters and evaluators revert to a familiar error direction, usually overconfidence in the likely outcome or, in cautious institutions, excess uncertainty. The source makes this directional bias mathematically explicit: log “rewards erring on the side of caution” [PAGE 4], while spherical “prefers the lower entropy forecast” [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is to choose the opposite rule as a correction without modeling the forecaster’s response. If an institution switches to spherical to suppress alarm, it may reduce the penalty on overconfident low-entropy forecasts; if it switches to log to force humility, it may reward forecasts that are more uncertain than the ideal. The source cautions that strictly proper rules encourage honesty but do not guarantee ideal forecasts: “Although strictly proper scoring rules encourage experts to issue their best judgements, such judgements may yet diﬀer from each other and the ideal” [PAGE 1]. The clock dimension is temporal: a rule must be precommitted before multi-week outcomes arrive, because post-hoc rule choice lets the evaluator’s preferred departure determine the ranking. A recursive evaluator must model three layers: the forecaster’s honest judgement, the forecaster’s strategic response to the known rule, and the public’s response to communicated uncertainty. The source’s inflation and drought examples show the conflict: spherical is preferred to avoid panic from overestimating extreme inflation [PAGE 16], while log is preferred to avoid underestimating rare drought [PAGE 16]. The counter-trap is therefore not “pick the stricter rule”; it is to pre-register the rule, monitor entropy and sign of departures, and check whether the rule’s preferred direction matches the decision cost. The clock also creates a gray zone: early in a multi-week episode, a high-entropy forecast may look cautious; late in the episode, the same forecast may look evasive. A recursive theory of mind must track which interpretation the rule will reward at scoring time.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto proof for a physical/weather layer: the source is a probability-scoring formalism, not a physical model, so it cannot by itself certify a weather forecast as physically safe. What it can veto is a claim that a sign-indifferent score adjudicates an asymmetric physical risk. For Brier and Quadratic Score, \(E[BS]=\gamma^2+pq\) and \(E[QS]=\|\gamma\|_2^2-\|p\|_2^2\) depend on the squared error, so \(+\gamma\) and \(-\gamma\) with equal L2 norm receive identical scores [PAGE 3, PAGE 8]. For CRPS, the expectation is \(G(P)+\|\Gamma\|_2^2\), which is also invariant under equal-norm sign reversal [PAGE 14]. Therefore, if a weather-risk layer requires distinguishing under-forecasting from over-forecasting a hazard, Brier/QS/CRPS supply no veto: their indifference is a mathematical proof that the asymmetric decision must come from an external utility or from log/spherical asymmetry. For the log layer, the veto is boundary-based: if a numerical weather model assigns zero probability to a possible event and that event occurs, \(-\log 0\) diverges; the source explicitly discourages “assigning zero probability to events that are possible” [PAGE 16]. For the spherical layer, the veto is geometric: it prefers lower entropy when \(p>q\), so using it to manage panic may systematically prefer sharper, lower-uncertainty weather messaging; the source endorses that direction for extreme-inflation panic [PAGE 16] but not as a universal physical rule. Biomechanical veto: no biomechanical variable, threshold, or load appears in the source’s operators; strict properness is a property on probability space, so it cannot veto a biomechanical safety threshold unless a utility mapping from forecast probabilities to biomechanical loss is supplied. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: use the scoring rule only after the physical/biomechanical/weather loss has been mapped into the same probability space; otherwise a sign-indifferent score can make two physically opposite errors look equally good.
