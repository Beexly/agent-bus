The source text from Tolman (1932) describes the transition from an undifferentiated stimulus space to a differentiated cognitive map. Initially, all alley-openings are responded to "as alike," functioning as ambiguous sign objects ($O$ and $O_i$) that could signify either food/leading-on-ness or non-food/blind-ends. Learning is the process of developing a differentiation between the characters (directions) of these signs and their correlated significates (outcomes) and distances. The key insight is that the rat does not merely associate a specific stimulus with a response (S-R), but rather builds a "sign-gestalt-expectation" where specific stimuli come to release expectations of specific significates ($O_2$). This implies a representational state that encodes topological relationships (distances, blind-ends vs. through-paths).

To formalize this, we must construct a model where the agent's internal state represents probability distributions over possible outcomes given a sensory input. The "sign object" corresponds to a local sensory cue $S_t$. The "significate" corresponds to the potential reward or continuation value $R_{t+k}$ or the nature of the future state $X_{t+k}$. The "learning" process is the minimization of prediction error or the maximization of mutual information between signs and significates.

We can model this using a predictive coding framework or a reinforcement learning model with a world model. Let $\theta$ parameterize the agent's policy and value function. The goal is to maximize expected cumulative reward, but Tolman emphasizes the *cognitive* aspect: the formation of expectations. We can define a "cognitive map" as a function $M: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \Delta(\mathcal{O})$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of sensory states (alley openings), and $\Delta(\mathcal{O})$ is a probability distribution over outcome types (food, blind-end, leading-on). Initially, $M(S)$ is uniform or highly entropic for all $S$. Learning reduces this entropy, making $M(S)$ sharply peaked for each $S$.

The differentiation between "characters, i.e., directions, of two sign objects" suggests that the agent learns to discriminate between similar stimuli based on their distal consequences. This is a form of feature learning where the features are defined by their predictive value. The "correlated significates and distances" indicates that the learned representation includes metric information (distance to goal) and qualitative information (type of goal).

A critical gray zone is the nature of the "sign-gestalt-expectation." Is it a symbolic representation or a sub-symbolic pattern? Tolman's language ("gestalt") suggests a holistic, configural representation. In modern terms, this could be a distributed neural representation that captures the joint statistics of signs and significates. The transition from "all alley-openings were responded to as alike" to "some... more specifically signs of food" is a shift from a high-entropy, uninformative policy to a low-entropy, informative one.

Another gray zone is the role of motivation. Tolman mentions "expectation picture," which implies a forward-looking, goal-directed process. The agent is not just reacting to stimuli but anticipating outcomes. This requires a model of the environment that can simulate future states. The "blind-end" is an object that terminates exploration, while the "true path opening" leads to further possibilities. The agent learns to distinguish these based on the long-term payoff.

Finally, we must consider the timescale of learning. Tolman describes "after learning," implying a gradual process. This suggests a credit assignment problem: how does the agent attribute the discovery of a blind-end to the specific sign object that led to it? This is analogous to the temporal difference learning problem in RL.

In summary, the derivation involves moving from a behavioral description (differentiation of responses) to a computational description (minimization of prediction error, maximization of mutual information). The challenge is to formalize the "gestalt" aspect, which may require non-Markovian or hierarchical models.

We define a stochastic process where an agent interacts with an environment containing a set of locations $\mathcal{L}$ (alley openings). Each location $l \in \mathcal{L}$ is associated with a sensory observation $s_l \in \mathcal{S}$ and a true outcome type $y_l \in \{ \text{food}, \text{blind-end}, \text{leading-on} \}$. The agent maintains a belief state $b_l \in \Delta(\mathcal{Y})$, representing its current expectation about the outcome at $l$.

**Assumptions:**
1.  The true outcome types are fixed but initially unknown to the agent.
2.  The agent's actions (entering an alley) reveal the true outcome type after a delay (travel time).
3.  The agent aims to minimize the expected travel distance to a food outcome while avoiding blind-ends.

**Formal Equations:**

1.  **Initial Undifferentiated State:** At time $t=0$, the belief state for all locations is uniform:
    $$ b_l^{(0)} = \left( \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3} \right) \quad \forall l \in \mathcal{L}. $$
    This corresponds to the "all alley-openings were responded to as alike" condition.

2.  **Learning Dynamics:** Learning occurs via experience. Upon entering location $l$ and observing outcome $y_l$, the agent updates its belief using Bayes' rule or a gradient-based method. For simplicity, consider a Bayesian update with a Dirichlet prior. Let $\alpha_y$ be the prior count for outcome type $y$. After $n$ visits to $l$ with observed counts $c_{l,y}$, the posterior is:
    $$ b_l^{(n)} \sim \text{Dirichlet}(\alpha_{\text{food}} + c_{l,\text{food}}, \alpha_{\text{blind}} + c_{l,\text{blind}}, \alpha_{\text{lead}} + c_{l,\text{lead}}). $$
    The expected value $E[b_l^{(n)}]$ converges to the true empirical distribution as $n \to \infty$.

3.  **Differentiation Metric:** The degree of differentiation can be measured by the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the initial uniform distribution and the learned distribution:
    $$ D_{\text{KL}}(b_l^{(n)} \| b^{(0)}) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} b_{l,y}^{(n)} \log \frac{b_{l,y}^{(n)}}{1/3}. $$
    Learning is complete when $D_{\text{KL}}$ is large for all $l$, indicating sharp differentiation.

4.  **Sign-Gestalt-Expectation as a Policy:** The agent's policy $\pi(a|s)$ is derived from its beliefs. A simple greedy policy selects the action that maximizes the expected immediate reward (food) minus a cost proportional to the estimated distance to the goal. If $d(l)$ is the shortest path distance from $l$ to any food location (which can also be learned), then:
    $$ \pi(\text{enter } l | s) \propto \exp\left( \beta \cdot (b_{l,\text{food}}^{(n)} - \lambda \cdot d(l)) \right), $$
    where $\beta$ is an inverse temperature parameter and $\lambda$ weights distance.

5.  **Singularity/Breakdown Regime:** The model breaks down if the environment becomes non-stationary (outcome types change randomly) or if the sensory observations are insufficient to distinguish locations (e.g., all $s_l$ are identical). In such cases, the belief state cannot converge, and the agent remains in a state of perpetual uncertainty, akin to the initial "undifferentiated" state. Another breakdown occurs if the "gestalt" property is lost—i.e., if the representation fails to capture the holistic relationship between signs and significates, reducing to a set of independent S-R associations.

**Units:** Belief states $b_{l,y}$ are dimensionless probabilities. Distance $d(l)$ is in meters (or arbitrary spatial units). Time is discrete steps or continuous seconds. The KL divergence is in nats or bits.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman's work emerges from the Gestalt psychology tradition, which reacted against elementalism (Watson, early behaviorism) and associationism. Gestaltists emphasized that perception and cognition are organized wholes, not sums of parts. Tolman imported this into behaviorism, creating "purposive behaviorism." His "cognitive map" was a radical departure from S-R theories because it posited an internal representational state that mediates between stimulus and response.

Historically, this lineage connects to Kant's notion of schemata and later to Piaget's schemas. In neuroscience, it prefigures the discovery of place cells and grid cells (O'Keefe, Moser), which provide a neural substrate for cognitive maps. Tolman's emphasis on "expectancy" also anticipates predictive processing frameworks (Friston, Clark), where the brain is a prediction machine minimizing surprise.

A multi-week trap arises from misinterpreting "purpose" as requiring consciousness or intentionality. Tolman used "purpose" technically to mean goal-directedness, not necessarily conscious intent. Another trap is conflating his "cognitive map" with a literal cartographic representation; it is an abstract, functional representation. Finally, a trap is assuming his work is obsolete due to connectionism; rather, it provides the conceptual foundation for understanding what connectionist networks learn (world models, policies).

The genealogy also includes a tension between "latent learning" (learning without immediate reinforcement) and traditional conditioning. Tolman demonstrated that rats explore mazes even without food rewards, forming cognitive maps that are only expressed when a goal is introduced. This challenges purely reinforcement-driven models and supports the idea of intrinsic motivation or curiosity-driven learning.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From a theory of mind perspective, we can analyze the rat's behavior in three stages: crisis, habit, and counter-trap.

**Crisis Habit Regression:** When first placed in the maze, the rat is in a "crisis" state—it has no map and must rely on innate habits (thigmotaxis, turning preferences). This leads to inefficient, random exploration. The habit of responding similarly to all alley openings ("responded to as alike") is maladaptive because it treats blind ends and goal paths identically, wasting energy.

**Counter-Trap Development:** Through exploration, the rat encounters negative outcomes (blind ends) and positive ones (food). It begins to form differentiated expectations. This is the "counter-trap": instead of blindly trying every alley, it uses the learned map to avoid previously encountered blind ends and prefer paths that led to food or further exploration. The "sign-gestalt-expectation" acts as a mental trap that captures the regularities of the environment, allowing for efficient navigation.

**Clock Mechanism:** The "clock" is the timing of learning. Tolman's experiments often involved a latent learning phase followed by a test phase. The clock ticks as the rat accumulates experiences. The transition from crisis to counter-trap is not instantaneous; it requires sufficient sampling to overcome the initial entropy. The "differentiation" is gradual, with some alleys becoming distinguished earlier than others based on visitation frequency and outcome salience.

This recursive loop—crisis leading to exploration, exploration leading to map formation, map formation leading to efficient goal-directed behavior—mirrors the development of more sophisticated agents. A failure in this loop (e.g., inability to form stable expectations) would result in perpetual crisis, akin to certain neuropsychiatric conditions.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even with a perfect cognitive map, performance can be vetoed by factors at other layers.

**Physical/Biomechanical Veto:** The rat's motor system may fail to execute the intended turn due to muscle fatigue, injury, or drug effects. Similarly, sensory systems (vision, whiskers) may be impaired, preventing accurate identification of the "sign objects" (alley openings). In such cases, the cognitive map is intact but cannot be utilized effectively.

**Weather/Environmental Veto:** External conditions can disrupt the mapping process. For example, if the maze is rearranged between trials (spatial rotation), the previously learned sign-significate correlations may no longer hold, causing disorientation. Changes in lighting or odor cues can also interfere with the recognition of sign objects. This is a veto on the stability of the learned associations.

**Proof of Independence:** Tolman designed experiments to dissociate these layers. For instance, he showed that rats trained in a maze could transfer their knowledge to a rotated version of the same maze, suggesting they had learned a spatial map relative to distal cues, not just a sequence of turns. This demonstrates that the cognitive map (representational layer) is somewhat independent of the specific motor routines (motor layer) used during training. However, if the distal cues themselves are altered, the map may need updating, showing dependence on the perceptual layer.

Thus, the "expectation picture" is a higher-order abstraction that can be disrupted by failures at lower levels, but it also provides robustness by decoupling goals from specific actions.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

We consider three verifiable counterfactual scenarios that would veto the standard learning trajectory described by Tolman.

1.  **Scenario: Non-Stationary Outcome Types.** Suppose the experimenter changes the reward schedule so that a previously reliable food location now yields a blind end, and vice versa. **Prediction:** The rat's learned map becomes inaccurate, and its performance deteriorates. It may persistently choose the former food path (a "habit") despite no reward, demonstrating that the cognitive map is not instantly updated. This vetoes the assumption of a static environment and highlights the need for online learning or metaplasticity.

2.  **Scenario: Sensory Deprivation During Exploration.** If the rat is prevented from using vision or olfaction during the initial exploration phase (e.g., in darkness or with scent masking), it cannot form a richly detailed cognitive map. **Prediction:** Even after sensory restoration, the rat's navigation will be less efficient and more reliant on idiothetic cues (self-motion) than allocentric cues. This vetoes the assumption that all sensory modalities contribute equally to map formation and shows modality-specific contributions.

3.  **Scenario: Pharmacological Disruption of Memory Consolidation.** Administration of a drug that impairs hippocampal function (e.g., scopolamine) after maze exposure but before retention testing. **Prediction:** The rat will show poor retention of the cognitive map, performing as if it had not learned, even though it explored extensively. This vetoes the idea that mere exposure is sufficient for learning; consolidation is a necessary step that can be independently disrupted.

These counterfactuals demonstrate that the formation of a "sign-gestalt-expectation" is contingent on environmental stability, intact sensory systems, and normal neurobiological processes. They highlight the fragility of the cognitive map and the multiple points of potential failure.
