<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The central operator is the planar projective map
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{H}\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\qquad
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=(u,v,1)^\top,\quad
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}=(X,Y,1)^\top,\quad
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},
\]
with \(\mathbf{H}\) defined only up to a nonzero scale \(\lambda\), so it has 8 degrees of freedom. Here \(X,Y\) are court-plane coordinates in metres and \(u,v\) are image coordinates in pixels. The assumption is that all used points lie on the FIBA ground plane; the paper makes this explicit by saying the challenge error is “estimated only on the ground plane” and that this “transforms the full calibration problem to a homography estimation” [PAGE 1]. The singularity/breakdown regime is \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})<8\) in the DLT system, including fewer than four non-collinear correspondences, duplicate points, or all points effectively collinear in the image/court configuration. A singular \(\mathbf{H}\) with \(\det(\mathbf{H})\approx 0\) also breaks inversion and the subsequent calibration step.

For each court-image correspondence \((X_i,Y_i)\leftrightarrow(u_i,v_i)\), DLT uses the two rows
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
X_i & Y_i & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -u_iX_i & -u_iY_i & -u_i\\
0 & 0 & 0 & X_i & Y_i & 1 & -v_iX_i & -v_iY_i & -v_i
\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}=0,
\]
where \(\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^9\) is the vectorized \(\mathbf{H}\). The units are mixed by construction: \(X_i,Y_i\) in metres, \(u_i,v_i\) in pixels, and \(\mathbf{h}\) carries the scale normalization. The exact singularity is rank deficiency of the stacked DLT matrix; the practical gray-zone is ill-conditioning when the keypoints are heavily clustered, which is why the paper introduces perspective-aware sampling [PAGE 3].

RANSAC is the robust estimator wrapped around DLT. The paper states: “a RANSAC algorithm [13] with DLT [15] estimates a robust homography between the court space and the image space by excluding outliers” [PAGE 2], and gives “The RANSAC re-projection threshold is set to 35 pixels” [PAGE 4]. A formal inlier test is
\[
r_i^2=
\left(u_i-\frac{h_{11}X_i+h_{12}Y_i+h_{13}}{h_{31}X_i+h_{32}Y_i+h_{33}}\right)^2+
\left(v_i-\frac{h_{21}X_i+h_{22}Y_i+h_{23}}{h_{31}X_i+h_{32}Y_i+h_{33}}\right)^2
\le \tau^2,
\]
with \(\tau=35\) px. The RANSAC iteration count for sample size \(s=4\), inlier probability \(w\), and confidence \(p\) is
\[
N_{\text{iter}}\ge \frac{\log(1-p)}{\log(1-w^4)},
\]
dimensionless. Breakdown occurs when the inlier fraction is too low, when the four-point sample is degenerate, or when the residual threshold is dominated by heatmap localization error.

The heatmap training objective is a weighted pixelwise cross-entropy:
\[
p_{x,k}=\frac{\exp(z_{x,k})}{\sum_{j=1}^{K+3}\exp(z_{x,j})},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}
=
-\sum_x\sum_{k=1}^{K+3}\alpha_k\,p_{x,k}\log(p_{x,k}).
\]
Here \(x\) is a heatmap pixel, \(k\) indexes classes, \(K=91\) court keypoints [PAGE 4], plus two basket heatmaps and one background class, giving \(K+3=94\) classes. The probabilities satisfy \(p_{x,k}\in[0,1]\) and \(\sum_k p_{x,k}=1\). If the logarithm is natural, \(\mathcal{L}\) is in nats per summed pixel-class; with base-2 it is in bits. The paper sets “\(\alpha_k=1000\) for the keypoint classes while \(\alpha_k=1\) for the background class” [PAGE 3] and uses a ground-truth disk “with a radius of 10 pixels” [PAGE 3]. The class weights are dimensionless. The breakdown regime is over-weighting: if \(\alpha_k\) is too large, the model can become overconfident on rare keypoint pixels and underfit the background; if the disk radius is too large relative to the 1/4-resolution heatmap, localization blurs. The paper also says the two basket heatmaps “cannot be used to estimate the homography but this supplementary task helps the network to localize correctly the field keypoints” [PAGE 3].

Perspective-aware sampling is governed by
\[
w_i=w_0+i r,\qquad i=0,\dots,N-2,
\]
where \(w_i\) is the \(i\)-th court-width spacing starting from the camera side, in metres. Summing the \(N-1\) intervals gives
\[
W=\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}w_i
=(N-1)w_0+\frac{(N-1)(N-2)}{2}r,
\]
and solving for the common difference yields
\[
r=\frac{2}{N-2}\left(\frac{W}{N-1}-w_0\right).
\]
The paper reports \(W\) as the real-world court width and experimentally chooses “\(w_0=1.75\)m” [PAGE 3]. The exact singularity is \(N=2\), where the denominator \(N-2\) vanishes; the paper’s phrase “for \(𝑁≥2\)” [PAGE 3] is therefore algebraically incomplete for the \(r\)-formula. The monotonicity condition is \(r>0\), i.e.
\[
0<w_0<\frac{W}{N-1}.
\]
If \(w_0\ge W/(N-1)\), then \(r\le 0\), and the intended “more spread near the camera” sampling breaks down.

The degeneracy/fallback operator is also explicit. The paper says: “In the case KaliCalib outputs a degenerated estimation or no homography, the plausible average homography of the training set is returned” [PAGE 4]. The test is: “re-projecting the frame points [240, 270] and [720, 270] in the court space and checking that their distance is below a threshold of 1800 cm” [PAGE 4]. Formally, with \(\mathbf{x}_1=(240,270,1)^\top\), \(\mathbf{x}_2=(720,270,1)^\top\), and \(\pi_{\text{court}}\) the normalization from homogeneous to court-plane coordinates,
\[
d=\left\|\pi_{\text{court}}(\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_1)-
\pi_{\text{court}}(\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_2)\right\|_2
<1800\ \text{cm}.
\]
The threshold is in centimetres; the singularity is that \(\mathbf{H}^{-1}\) is undefined when \(\det(\mathbf{H})=0\). The fallback is a prior, not a measurement.

The evaluation metric is defined as
\[
E=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{6}
\left\|
\mathbf{X}^{\text{est}}_j-
\mathbf{X}^{\text{gt}}_j
\right\|_2^2
},
\]
where the six frame points are “left, center and right at the middle on bottom part of the frame” [PAGE 4]. \(E\) is in centimetres. The paper calls this “MSE” in Table 1 [PAGE 5], but the definition contains a square root and the reported column is “MSE (cm)” [PAGE 5]. Dimensionally, a true MSE would be in \(\mathrm{cm}^2\); the reported quantity is a root-mean-square frame error in cm. This is the exact place where a reader should not silently convert the “4.7” factor into a squared-error factor.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
KaliCalib’s lineage is a sequence of sports-field registration strategies: line detection with Hough transforms and RANSAC-like fitting, line-intersection matching, segmentation plus structured models, template matching with siamese features, and keypoint heatmap regression. The paper summarizes that older work used “a Hough transform to extract tennis court lines” and “a RANSAC-like algorithm to detect the court lines” [PAGE 2], while later work used segmentation networks and Markov random fields [PAGE 2]. The keypoint branch is the one KaliCalib inherits: the paper says “KaliCalib is built upon the approach of Nie et al. [21] without the homography refinement” [PAGE 2]. This genealogy matters because the method’s novelty is not the homography solver itself; it is the combination of perspective-aware keypoint sampling, basket auxiliary heatmaps, and heavy data augmentation around an existing keypoint-to-homography pipeline.

The retrocausal pressure is visible in the challenge design. Because the future metric was ground-plane calibration error, the authors chose a ground-plane homography rather than a full 3D calibration model: “As, in the challenge, the calibration error is estimated only on the ground plane, we decided to estimate the calibration parameters with only points from this plane” [PAGE 1]. In other words, the evaluation target retro-causally selected the representation. That is legitimate for the benchmark, but it creates a deployment trap: a model optimized for a six-point ground-plane frame error may not recover the full camera pose needed for 3D player reconstruction, augmented reality, or off-ground objects. The paper’s own future work hints at this boundary: “we would like to test this approach on other datasets and study new network architectures to better capture the field features” [PAGE 5].

The multi-week data trap is the small basketball corpus. The paper says “the organizers released the first public dataset for basketball composed of 728 images” [PAGE 2], and the challenge dataset is “480 images for the training, 164 for the validation, 84 for the testing and 84 for the challenge” [PAGE 4]. The training set “does not contain a wide variety of arenas,” making “it therefore hard to generalize to various court color designs” [PAGE 4]. A naive multi-week workflow would overfit arena color and line appearance, then mistake test-set gains for cross-arena robustness. KaliCalib’s augmentation stack—random flips and ColorJitter—is a direct response to that trap [PAGE 4]. But the trap is only partially closed: the paper’s hard examples include “Multi-sports courts or arena with different configuration” with “many lines that can hinders the basketball court localization” [PAGE 4]. A second multi-week trap is the fallback average homography: it can make aggregate error look stable while individual hard images are not actually registered. A third trap is treating the ablation percentages as additive; they are relative to different ablated baselines, not independent contributions to a single variance budget.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer has three agents: the challenge organizer who defines the six-point ground-plane error, the participant who optimizes that error, and the downstream user who needs a reliable court registration system. The organizer’s metric shapes the participant’s representation; the participant’s fallback and augmentation choices shape what the downstream user can trust. KaliCalib’s “crisis habit regression” is the average training-set homography returned when the model “outputs a degenerated estimation or no homography” [PAGE 4]. That is a habit: under crisis, the system reverts to a prior rather than reporting uncertainty. The counter-trap is perspective-aware sampling, which compensates for the RANSAC bias toward far-court keypoint clusters: “the RANSAC will more likely estimate the homography with keypoints from this area despite less pixels were available” [PAGE 3]. The clock is the 9 ms inference time [PAGE 5], which makes the system attractive for broadcast but does not resolve the crisis cases.

The recursive theory-of-mind question is whether the model “knows” when it does not know. The paper’s degeneracy check is a coarse self-model: it tests whether two frame points collapse in court space below 1800 cm [PAGE 4]. That is not a calibrated uncertainty estimate; it is a binary veto. If the veto fires, the system substitutes an average homography. If the veto does not fire but the visible court area is small, or the image contains multi-sport lines, the system may still be wrong. The paper acknowledges such hard examples [PAGE 4]. A counter-trap would be to train an explicit uncertainty head or to require a minimum inlier count and spatial spread before accepting a homography. KaliCalib does not report that; it reports a fallback. The clock pressure also creates a tradeoff: 9 ms per image encourages real-time use, but a robust system might need iterative refinement, which the authors deliberately omitted relative to Nie et al. [PAGE 2]. The recursive audit therefore separates three claims: fast inference (supported by 9 ms), benchmark accuracy (supported by 126.61 cm test frame error [PAGE 5]), and trustworthy abstention (only partially supported by the 1800 cm degeneracy test and average-homography fallback).

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is planar identifiability. KaliCalib assumes a FIBA court plane of “28m by 15m” [PAGE 4]. If the visible court area is too small, or if the detected points are effectively collinear, the DLT system is rank-deficient and no unique homography exists. The paper’s own hard examples include images where “the visible area of the court is sometimes small” [PAGE 4]. That is a physical veto: without sufficient spatial support on the plane, the output is not a measurement. A formal veto predicate is \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A})<8\), or \(\det(\mathbf{H})\approx 0\), or fewer than four non-collinear inliers after RANSAC.

The sensor/physical-occlusion veto is line visibility. The paper states: “During the game, some of these features can be occluded by the players” and “When the camera moves quickly to follow the action, the field lines can also be blurry, which hinders their detection” [PAGE 1]. In biomechanical terms, player bodies act as dynamic occluders; the model has no biomechanical player model and does not reconstruct limbs or poses. The only relevant biomechanical fact is that players can remove court-line evidence. If occlusion plus blur removes the keypoint heatmap support, the system should abstain. KaliCalib’s fallback is the only explicit abstention mechanism, and it is triggered by the 1800 cm court-space distance test [PAGE 4], not by a direct occlusion mask.

The environmental/weather layer is mostly outside the paper’s scope because basketball is indoor and broadcast-based. There is no weather model, no rain/snow/sun-glare operator, and no explicit lighting physics. The closest proxy is ColorJitter: brightness 0.7, contrast 0.5, saturation 0.5, hue 0.5 [PAGE 4]. That augmentation can improve robustness to arena color design, but it does not prove robustness to severe specular highlights, shadow boundaries, or outdoor multi-sport court markings. The paper’s challenge hard cases include “Multi-sports courts or arena with different configuration” with “many lines” [PAGE 4]. A weather/environmental veto for deployment would be: if line contrast falls below the heatmap’s learned signal-to-noise regime, or if non-basketball lines dominate the keypoint response, reject the homography rather than returning the average prior.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: physical geometry can veto via rank deficiency; sensor/occlusion can veto via missing or blurred keypoints; environmental appearance can veto via line confusion; biomechanical occlusion can veto via player masking. KaliCalib’s only explicit veto is the degeneracy check, and its only fallback is the average training homography [PAGE 4]. That is a weak-layer proof: the system can be fast and accurate on the benchmark while still lacking a physically grounded abstention certificate for the hard cases.
