<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: After a demand shock, middlemen tend to regress to a simple heuristic—cut the price when sales slow, raise it when sales accelerate. This is the first-order theory of mind: "If my sales are low, others must be finding my price too high." Knight's description matches this: price is changed "as the rate of sales indicates it to be too high or too low" [PAGE 88]. The regression is crisis-driven because under uncertainty, the salience of recent sales overwhelms the latent estimate of p*.

Counter-trap: A second-order middleman anticipates that rivals will panic-cut on slow sales. Rather than following, the sophisticated middleman holds price or even accumulates inventory, expecting rivals' cuts to clear competing stock and restore demand for his own offering at the old price. This counter-trap destabilizes the simple update rule: if all middlemen believe others will overreact, none moves first, and p* remains undiscovered. If all believe others will hold, all cut simultaneously, overshooting p* downward.

Clock: The adjustment interval τ between sales observations and price changes is the critical timescale. Stability requires τ < T_shock, where T_shock is the characteristic duration of demand fluctuations. If τ exceeds T_shock, the middleman is always fighting the last observation, producing oscillations around p*. If τ is too short relative to sales noise, the update reacts to random fluctuations, generating churn rather than convergence. The recursive depth d—how many levels of "I think that he thinks that I will cut"—determines whether the system settles at p* or enters a limit cycle of mutual misestimation. On [PAGE 88], Knight provides no bound on d or τ, leaving the stability of the middleman price process an open gray zone.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
