Conversely, when the drive state is identical across both extinction phases, the animal fully contacts the non-reinforcement contingency under the same internal cue, allowing extinction inhibition to consolidate and reducing resistance. This account treats \(S_D\) not merely as a scalar multiplier of response magnitude but as a stimulus whose intensity gradient supports discrimination learning.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage traces from Hull’s (1943) deductive behaviorism, in which drive \(D\) serves as both an energetic determinant of reaction potential and a putative stimulus whose intensity can enter generalization gradients [PAGE 235]. Hull’s framework builds on Pavlovian stimulus generalization and on the discrimination-gradient work of Spence and others, in which responding declines as a function of perceptual distance from the training stimulus. Extending that gradient to the interoceptive domain—treating hunger intensity itself as a dimension along which stimuli are similar or dissimilar—represents a theoretical leap from exteroceptive tones and lights to visceral drive states.

Heathers and Arakelian (4), cited on [PAGE 235], occupy an intermediate historical node: their incidental finding anticipates modern phenomena of renewal, resurgence, and reinstatement in extinction learning. In contemporary terms, if extinction is conducted under drive state \(D_1\) and the animal is later tested under \(D_2\), a mismatch can occasion renewal of the extinguished response because the extinction context (including the interoceptive occasion-setter) is no longer present. The present design compresses that logic into two extinction phases separated by two days [PAGE 235], creating a multi-week trap in applied settings: an animal or patient extinguished under one motivational or affective state (e.g., low anxiety, satiety) may relapse when re-exposed under a different state (e.g., high hunger, stress), not because the original habit was stronger, but because the drive stimulus failed to generalize across the extinction–test boundary.

The multi-week trap operates as follows. Over weeks of intermittent training and testing, nominal drive states drift with circadian rhythms, feeding schedules, and environmental temperature. A protocol that labels sessions “strong” or “weak” based on pre-session deprivation may misclassify the actual internal state at the moment of responding. Consequently, an apparent “strong–strong” match may be a physiological mismatch, while a “weak–strong” mismatch may accidentally align. Repeated exposure to such mismatches can produce a spurious partial-reinforcement-like effect: resistance to extinction appears inflated not because of habit strength \(H\), but because the animal repeatedly encounters novel drive occasions that disrupt extinction inhibition. Hull’s own caution on [PAGE 235]—that the evidence is incidental and inconclusive—is therefore methodologically prescient for longitudinal designs in which drive state is treated as a static factor rather than a time-varying internal covariate.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under conditions of high drive or acute stress, an organism regresses to the habit hierarchy’s dominant response. If the dominant response was trained under strong hunger \(D_s\), then testing under strong hunger should theoretically evoke that habit most vigorously. However, extinction under strong hunger may have installed an extinction inhibition specific to the \(D_s\) occasion-setter. A recursive theory of mind must therefore model the animal’s expectation: “When I am hungry and the bar does not pay off, I stop pressing.” If the second extinction occurs under the same drive, the prediction error is consistent and extinction consolidates; if the drive shifts, the animal faces a novel interoceptive context in which the old extinction rule may not apply, so responding resurges.

Counter-trap: the experimental design tempts the interpreter to assume that matching drive across extinction sessions strengthens retention of extinction (because “same stimulus” should generalize). The data point in the opposite direction: same drive produced less resistance to extinction [PAGE 235]. The trap is treating \(D\) as a pure scalar gain in \(sE_r = H \cdot D \cdot K \cdot V\) while ignoring its role as a discriminative stimulus \(S_D\). When \(S_D\) is identical across acquisition and extinction, the animal can attribute non-reinforcement specifically to that drive occasion, suppressing the response more efficiently. When \(S_D\) changes, attribution is blurred, and the response persists out of stimulus-control uncertainty. The counter-trap is to predict that drive *mismatch* increases apparent resistance—not because the habit is stronger, but because the extinction cue is absent.

Clock: the two-day interval between extinction phases [PAGE 235] introduces a temporal operator \(\Delta t = 48\,\text{h}\). During this interval, homeostatic variables (blood glucose, gastric emptiness, circadian phase) drift, so the internal state at retest is \(D(t + \Delta t) \neq D(t)\) even under identical deprivation protocols. A recursive model must include a state-transition equation \(D_{t+1} = f(D_t, \text{intake}, \text{circadian phase}, \text{ambient temperature})\). If the clock is ignored, the experimenter misattributes variance to the categorical strong/strong manipulation that actually belongs to continuous state drift. This is especially severe for the “weak” condition, where low drive places the organism near the satiation singularity \(D \approx 0\), at which small physiological changes produce large proportional shifts in \(S_D\) and hence in behavioral output.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Under weak hunger, response vigor and force may decline; if the press force falls below the apparatus threshold, responses are mechanically unrecorded even if the animal attempts them. This creates a floor effect that mimics extinction. Any conclusion about resistance to extinction must therefore veto sessions in which mean press force \(F_{\text{press}} < F_{\text{threshold}}\) or in which \(F_{\text{press}}\) covaries with drive condition.

A biomechanical veto requires direct measurement of competing responses (water licking, food chewing) during extinction; if their rates differ across drive groups, the bar-press difference is not attributable to drive-stimulus generalization alone.

Weather/environmental layer: the source is a controlled laboratory preparation, so ambient weather is not a registered variable on [PAGE 235]. However, if the finding is extrapolated to field or outdoor animal work, ambient temperature \(T_a\) enters the energy-balance equation and shifts hunger drive independently of food deprivation: cold exposure increases caloric demand, elevating \(D\) even under “weak” privation. A weather veto would require holding \(T_a\) within the thermoneutral zone or including \(T_a\) as a covariate; otherwise the strong/weak manipulation is confounded by thermoregulatory drive.

Veto adjudication rule: the claim that primary stimulus-intensity generalization applies to \(S_D\) is provisionally supported only if (a) press force remains above apparatus threshold across all four groups, (b) competing consummatory responses are equated or statistically partialled out, and (c) ambient temperature is controlled or modeled. On the evidence printed on [PAGE 235], none of these physical-layer controls is demonstrated, so the result remains in the gray zone.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning verifiable-reward (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a falsifiable prediction:

Veto 1 — Physiological drive matching instead of nominal deprivation. Replicate the \(2 \times 2\) design, but assign animals to “strong” and “weak” drive based on a continuous physiological readout (e.g., blood-glucose concentration or running-wheel activity) immediately before each extinction session, rather than on pre-session food-deprivation hours. Prediction: if the original effect is an artifact of noisy deprivation categories, the resistance difference between same-drive and different-drive groups will shrink to \(\Delta R \approx 0\) with \(p > 0.5\); if the effect reflects a genuine \(S_D\) generalization gradient, the effect will persist with the same sign when groups are matched on measured drive intensity.

Veto 2 — Contextual mismatch control. Hold drive constant across both extinction phases but change the exteroceptive context (apparatus, room, odor) between the first and second extinction for half the subjects. Prediction: if context mismatch, rather than drive mismatch, drives the increased resistance, then context-switched animals will show equal or greater resistance than drive-mismatched animals, and the original 4–6 percent drive effect will become nonsignificant (\(p > 0.2\)) once context is included as a factor. This veto isolates whether the interoceptive drive stimulus or the exteroceptive context is the dominant occasion-setter for extinction retention.

Veto 3 — Within-subject equated extinction magnitude. Replace the between-groups “equal extent” extinction with a within-subject, counterbalanced design in which each animal receives two extinction episodes matched on cumulative response rate (not experimenter-judged “equal extent”), with drive state orthogonally manipulated. Prediction: if the original same-drive disadvantage was caused by differential extinction doses across weak/strong conditions, equating cumulative responding will eliminate or reverse the sign of \(\partial R / \partial \mathbb{I}(\text{same drive})\); the same-drive group should then show equal or greater resistance than the different-drive group, contradicting the original direction reported on [PAGE 235].
