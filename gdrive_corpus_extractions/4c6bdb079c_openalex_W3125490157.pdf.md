(3) The pull function confidence intervals are large and unreliable below 4% short rates because of sparse observations (page 27), so claims about nonlinear mean reversion in low-rate regimes are fragile. (4) The GARCH(1,1) exercise uses the 5-year yield, which was not part of the calibration moments (page 23), so the volatility match is an out-of-sample validation rather than a calibration target.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under stress, the representative agent’s inner minimization over h∈H selects a worst-case drift h∗ that distorts the state dynamics away from the reference belief. Equation (13) on page 12 shows h∗ is a nonlinear feedback control depending on Ξ′V_Y and σ′; in a crisis, as state variables move, V_Y shifts and h∗ re-optimizes, generating a time-varying ambiguity premium. Because the agent has logarithmic utility, the standard intertemporal hedging motive against changes in the opportunity set vanishes under the reference belief (page 15), but ambiguity aversion restores a non-myopic hedge against future changes in the worst-case opportunity set, linked one-to-one to realizations of h∗. This is a crisis habit: the agent continuously regresses to the most pessimistic admissible model within the relative-entropy ball.

Counter-trap: The closed-form two-factor solution under Assumption 1 (page 17–19) assumes ambiguity loads only on the h₃ shock and yields a constant ambiguity price λ_A = √(2η)(0,0,1)′ (equation 25, page 18). In a genuine crisis, ambiguity would likely not remain confined to one shock; the state-dependence of λ_A in the general multi-factor model (page 14) could produce sign changes in excess returns and nonlinear regime shifts that the constant-premium closed form misses. Treating the calibrated η = 0.0136 as structural across all regimes is a trap because the small-η approximation and the rectangular prior set may not hold under extreme model discontinuity.

Clock: The infinite-horizon recursive utility with discount rate δ imposes a fixed temporal metric on the agent’s worst-case contemplation. The value function separates into a wealth term log(δx)/δ and a state-dependent term V(y) (equation 11, page 12), meaning the clock of discounting is independent of the ambiguity state. The elasticity of intertemporal substitution is one due to log felicity (page 29), which detaches the equity premium from the IES and permits the calibrated 6.8% annual equity premium (Table IV, page 35). However, this clock assumes time-separable preferences and a constant δ; if agents revisit their ambiguity set at crisis-driven frequencies faster than the model’s continuous-time recursion, the recursive separation fails.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The production technology dQ/Q = α(Y)dt + σ(Y)dZ (equation 1, page 9) is an abstract output-rate process, not a biomechanical or meteorological system. There are no variables representing temperature, precipitation, wind, or physiological load; σ(Y) is financial volatility of the technology return, not a weather-driven physical diffusion. Any attempt to import biomechanical constraints (e.g., muscle fatigue, joint torque limits) or weather shocks (e.g., storm fronts affecting σ) is outside the model’s domain and is vetoed. The only admissible exogenous distortion of the physical production process is the contaminating drift h satisfying h′h ≤ 2η (equation 4, page 10), which enters through the Girsanov kernel and changes the drift of Z under P^h.

Biomechanical layer: Portfolio weights π = [ω, v]′ (equation 7, page 11) are financial allocations, not human movement variables. There is no biomechanical state, no force–fatigue relationship, and no motion-capture observable. A veto is issued on any mapping of V_Y or h∗ to biomechanical effort; the gradient V_Y is the marginal utility of the representative agent with respect to financial state variables Y, not a musculoskeletal signal.

Weather layer: No meteorological filtration exists in (Ω, ℱ, P). The Brownian motion Z generates financial and technological uncertainty only. Weather-based factor models cannot be nested into the ambiguity set H without redefining the state vector Y and the volatility matrix Ξ, which would violate the specified Markovian structure and the relative-entropy bound.

Adjudication: Because the source contains zero physical, biomechanical, or weather variables, the cross-layer veto is trivially satisfied by domain exclusion. All uncertainty must be represented within H via h satisfying equation (4). Introducing weather or biomechanics would require changing the probability space, the filtration, and the entropy bound, thereby voiding the HJB equation (14) and the pricing PDE (20).
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Out-of-sample recalibration beyond small-η: If the model is recalibrated on post-2000 Treasury data and the implied ambiguity parameter exceeds η = 0.0136, the first-order expansion V_Y = V_0Y + O(√η) (equation 29, page 21) and the yield-curve approximation R(t,T) ≈ R₀(t,T) − √(2η)P₁/[(T−t)P₀] (equation 32, page 22) accumulate non-negligible o(√η) error. Verifiable prediction: for maturities T − t > 5 years, the relative error between the exact numerical solution of PDEs (14) and (20) and the first-order approximation exceeds 10%, invalidating the calibration shortcut used in Table I (page 30). Veto: reject the first-order approximation for any recalibrated η ≥ 0.02.

Scenario 2 — Vanishing volatility regime Y₂ ↓ 0: Under Assumption 1, the risk premium on Y₂ is negative and decreasing in Y₂, while the ambiguity premium remains positive and large even as Y₂ → 0 (page 18). Verifiable prediction: in a low-volatility regime, the model generates negative bond excess returns for moderate Y₂ as Y₁ increases (as illustrated in Figure III, page 37), whereas a completely affine model (η = 0) cannot produce negative excess returns when the priced risk factor is zero. Veto: if empirical 5-year excess returns remain strictly positive during a sustained low-volatility episode, the ambiguity-dominance mechanism is falsified for that episode.

Scenario 3 — Non-rectangular ambiguity set: If the admissible drift set H is time-varying or state-dependent in a way that violates the rectangularity condition of Epstein and Schneider (2003) (page 7), dynamic consistency fails. Verifiable prediction: the saddle-point property in Sion’s theorem (Appendix A, page 26) breaks down, so h∗ in equation (13) (page 12) is no longer optimal, the HJB equation (14) (page 13) ceases to hold, and the closed-form bond price (26) (page 19) is invalid. Veto: any ambiguity specification that imposes a terminal constraint on h rather than the instantaneous bound h′h ≤ 2η voids the recursive multiple-prior solution and requires re-derivation of equilibrium prices.
