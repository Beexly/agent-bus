<THINK_TRACE>
The source is not a betting artifact; it is a multi-agent imitation-learning paper whose central claim is that a per-trajectory latent “strategy representation” can be learned without player identification and then used to filter sub-optimal demonstrations. The derivation path is: Markov-game/POMDP formalism [PAGE 2] → per-trajectory strategy representation \(l\) held constant across a trajectory → Partially-trainable-conditioned Variational Recurrent Neural Network (P-VRNN) trained by a variational lower bound [PAGE 3–4] → two indicators, Randomness Indicator (RI) and Exploited Level (EL) [PAGE 4–5] → percentile filtering of the offline dataset → any plug-in IL algorithm [PAGE 5]. The paper explicitly says: “STRIL is a plug-in method that can be integrated into existing IL algorithms.” [PAGE 1]. That plug-in claim is the highest-level operator: STRIL does not replace BC or IQ-Learn or ILEED; it changes the empirical trajectory distribution entering the IL loss.

The first multi-hop gray zone is identification. STRIL claims “without player identification” [PAGE 1, PAGE 4], but its representation is optimized per trajectory. If the same player’s style is consistent across many trajectories, clusters may still align with player ID; Figure 3a is literally labeled “Player ID” [PAGE 6]. That is not necessarily leakage, but it means “no player ID” does not imply “no player-style cluster.” A stronger leakage risk is observation conditioning: the paper says “To disentangle the strategy of the opponent player from the strategy representation, we consider the observation as a conditional variable.” [PAGE 3]. That is an architectural attempt, not a formal guarantee. If observations contain opponent identity, betting history, or opponent action traces, \(l\) may encode opponent information. This is a gray-zone audit point because the paper’s own motivation says “the strategy of the player changes according to their opponent within each episode” [PAGE 3], yet it assumes “the strategy of a player is consistent within a single trajectory.” [PAGE 3]. Those two statements are compatible only if “strategy” means a trajectory-level policy conditioned on opponent observations, not a fixed open-loop action sequence.

The second multi-hop gray zone is the RI. RI is defined from reconstruction entropy [PAGE 4]. The paper invokes “the hypothesis of Beliaev et al. (2022), a strategy with more randomness is considered worse.” [PAGE 4]. But the same paper notes that in Rock-Paper-Scissors “a uniformly random strategy constitutes a Nash equilibrium.” [PAGE 2]. Therefore high entropy can be optimal in some zero-sum games. RI is not a universal skill metric; it is a dataset- and game-dependent proxy. The paper itself later admits the breakdown: “the estimation fails when the demonstrators of offline trajectories only adopt deterministic but poor strategies.” [PAGE 13]. This is crucial: low entropy can mean habitual, predictable, exploitable behavior, not skill. In Two-player Pong, “the strategy of Player 5 has the lowest entropy. However, this player is heavily exploited by Players 6, 7, and 8” [PAGE 13]. That is a direct counterexample to a naive entropy-as-skill reading.

The third gray zone is EL. EL is a conditional expectation of negative reward, conditioned on \(r(\pi,\pi(\tau))\le 0\) [PAGE 4–5]. Higher EL means more negative reward in the exploited region, i.e., more exploitable/worse in the paper’s sign convention. The paper’s toy model proves a proportionality \(EL(\tau_k)=\frac{1}{n+1}E(\pi(\tau_k))\) under strong assumptions: two-player zero-sum symmetric game, competent players, each player exploitable by at most one pure strategy, uniform simplex distribution [PAGE 10]. This is not a general identity. The paper states Proposition 0.2: if \(\pi(\tau)\) is \(\epsilon_1\)-Nash and nearby representations imply nearby strategy distributions with constant \(\alpha\), then \(EL_\delta(\tau)<\epsilon_1+\alpha\delta M\) [PAGE 5, PAGE 11]. That is an upper-bound regime, not an exact estimator. The empirical claim “EL can be precisely estimated even with limited reward data” [PAGE 1] depends on reward-anchor coverage. The limitation section says: “the estimation fails when the sampled trajectories with rewards are biased, covering a small area of representation space or providing biased choices of demonstrators.” [PAGE 13]. So EL is not unsupervised; it is semi-supervised with a coverage assumption.

The fourth gray zone is filtering. The filter is \( \tilde\Gamma_p=\{\tau\in\Gamma\mid I(\tau)<I_p\}\) [PAGE 5], with \(p\) the chosen percentile. The paper notes: “As the value of p closer to 0, more data is filtered out; conversely, setting p to 1 filters none of the data, reverting STRIL to the original IL algorithm.” [PAGE 5]. The sensitivity analysis observes “a drop in performance from p = 0.1 to p = 0.05 is commonly observed” [PAGE 7]. Thus aggressive filtering creates a data-starvation regime. The optimal \(p\) is not invariant; the paper says RI’s optimal results occur at \(p=0.4\) or \(p=0.1\) in different game scenarios [PAGE 7]. A deployable STRIL must therefore treat \(p\) as a dataset-dependent hyperparameter, not a universal constant.

The deepest audit point is that STRIL measures exploitability against the demonstrator set, not against the true best response. The evaluation metric is \(WS(I,\pi_i)=\min_{j\in I} r_i(\pi_j,\pi_i)\) [PAGE 5]. This is worst-case over the finite demonstrator set \(I\), not a Nash exploitability computation. EL tries to approximate the missing best response through representation-space neighborhoods, but that approximation is only as good as the local reward coverage. Therefore the correct interpretation is: STRIL filters trajectories that are locally exploitable by the observed demonstrator ecology. It does not certify global Nash robustness. This distinction is essential for avoiding overclaiming.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s formal objects are game-theoretic and information-theoretic. No SI mechanical units appear: rewards are game points/chips; probabilities are dimensionless; entropy is in nats if \(\log\) is natural and bits if \(\log_2\); representation coordinates and distances are dimensionless network-space quantities unless a metric with physical units is imposed.

1. Markov-game trajectory reward and expected reward [PAGE 2]:
\[
\hat r_i(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T} r_t^i,\qquad
r_i(\pi_{-i},\pi_i)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim(\pi_{-i},\pi_i)}[\hat r_i(\tau)].
\]
Here \(\hat r_i\) has reward units; \(T\) is the discrete time horizon. Assumption: partially observable Markov game with per-agent action/observation spaces; a “null” action exists when an agent need not act [PAGE 2].

2. Best response and exploitability [PAGE 2–3]:
\[
BR(\pi_{-i})=\arg\max_{\pi'_i} r_i(\pi_{-i},\pi'_i),
\]
\[
E(\pi)=\sum_{i\in P}\left(r_i(\pi_{-i},BR(\pi_{-i}))-r_i(\pi_{-i},\pi_i)\right).
\]
In zero-sum symmetric cases:
\[
E(\pi_i)=-r_i(BR(\pi_i),\pi_i)=\sum_{j\in P,j\ne i} r_j(BR(\pi_i),\pi_i).
\]
A strategy is \(\epsilon\)-Nash if \(E(\pi_i)\le\epsilon\). Units: \(E\), \(\epsilon\), \(r_i\), and \(M\) are reward units. Breakdown: this formula is defined for the zero-sum symmetric setting; non-zero-sum or asymmetric games require a different exploitability definition.

3. P-VRNN generative model [PAGE 3, Eq. (1)]:
\[
p(a_{\le T},z_{\le T}\mid o_{\le T},l)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
p(a_t\mid z_{\le t},a_{<t},o_{\le t},l)\,
p(z_t\mid a_{<t},z_{<t},o_{\le t},l).
\]
The prior is Gaussian:
\[
z_t\mid h_{t-1},o_t,l\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{\mathrm{pri},t},\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{pri},t})\right),
\]
\[
[\mu_{\mathrm{pri},t},\sigma_{\mathrm{pri},t}]
=\phi_{\mathrm{pri}}(h_{t-1},o_t,l).
\]
The decoder is categorical:
\[
a_t\mid h_{t-1},z_t,o_t,l\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\mu_{\mathrm{dec},t}),\qquad
\mu_{\mathrm{dec},t}=\phi_{\mathrm{dec}}(h_{t-1},z_t,o_t,l).
\]
Recurrence:
\[
h_t=\phi_{\mathrm{rec}}(h_{t-1},a_t,z_t,o_t,l).
\]
Units: \(z_t\) is a dimensionless latent vector; \(\mu_{\mathrm{pri},t}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{pri},t}\) are in latent-coordinate units; \(\mu_{\mathrm{dec},t}\) is a probability vector over \(|\mathcal{A}|\) actions. Assumption: diagonal Gaussian prior/posterior and categorical action likelihood. Breakdown: if \(l\) is not held constant per trajectory, the “strategy representation” interpretation collapses; if observations leak opponent identity, \(l\) may represent opponent context rather than player strategy.

4. Inference model [PAGE 3–4, Eq. (5)–(6)]:
\[
q(z_{\le T}\mid a_{\le T},o_{\le T},l)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T}
q(z_t\mid a_{\le t},z_{<t},o_{\le t},l),
\]
\[
z_t\mid h_{t-1},a_t,o_t,l\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{\mathrm{enc},t},\operatorname{diag}((\sigma_{\mathrm{enc},t})^2)\right),
\]
\[
[\mu_{\mathrm{enc},t},\sigma_{\mathrm{enc},t}]
=\phi_{\mathrm{enc}}(h_{t-1},a_t,o_t,l).
\]

5. P-VRNN loss [PAGE 4, Eq. (7)–(9)]:
\[
\mathcal{L}
=
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z_{\le T}\mid a_{\le T},o_{\le T},l)}
\left[
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Recon},t}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Reg},t}\right)
\right],
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Recon},t}
=
-\log p_\theta(a_t\mid z_{\le t},a_{<t},o_{\le t},l),
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Reg},t}
=
\mathrm{KL}\!\left(
q_\phi(z_t\mid a_{\le t},z_{<t},o_{\le t},l)
\,\big\|\,
p_\theta(z_t\mid a_{<t},z_{<t},o_{\le t},l)
\right).
\]
Units: if \(\log\) is natural, both losses are nats per step; if base-2, bits per step. Assumption: the ELBO is a valid bound under the specified factorization. Breakdown: a KL near zero does not guarantee meaningful strategy representation; it may indicate prior/posterior collapse. The paper notes the regularization loss is optimized “to a very small value close to 0” while reconstruction loss remains larger because “players usually do not act deterministically.” [PAGE 4].

6. Randomness Indicator [PAGE 4, Eq. (10)–(11)]:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(a_t\mid z_{\le t},a_{<t},o_{\le t},l)}
[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Recon},t}]
=
H\!\left(p_\theta(a_t\mid z_{\le t},a_{<t},o_{\le t},l)\right),
\]
\[
RI(\tau)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}
H\!\left(p_\theta(a_t\mid z_{\le t},a_{<t},o_{\le t},l)\right).
\]
Units: nats or bits depending on log base. Assumption: “a strategy with more randomness is considered worse” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: this assumption fails when randomness is optimal, e.g., the paper’s own RPS example where “a uniformly random strategy constitutes a Nash equilibrium” [PAGE 2]. It also fails for “deterministic but poor strategies” [PAGE 13], where entropy is low but skill is low.

7. Exploited Level [PAGE 4–5, Eq. (12)–(14)]:
\[
EL(\tau)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[-r(\pi,\pi(\tau))\mid r(\pi,\pi(\tau))\le 0\right]
=
\frac{\int_{\pi\in\Pi}(-r(\pi,\pi(\tau)))_+\,d\pi}
{\int_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{1}_{r(\pi,\pi(\tau))\le 0}\,d\pi},
\]
\[
EL_\delta(\tau)
=
\frac{
\sum_{d(f(\tau),f(\tau'))<\delta}
(-r(\hat\pi,\pi(\tau')))_+
}{
\sum_{d(f(\tau),f(\tau'))<\delta}
\mathbb{1}_{r(\hat\pi,\pi(\tau'))\le 0}
}.
\]
Units: \(EL\) and \(EL_\delta\) are reward units; \(d\) is a metric on representation space; \(\delta\) has the same units as \(d\); \(f\) is the representation map. Assumption: nearby representations imply nearby strategies, formalized via Lipschitz/smoothness. The paper states \(\lim_{\delta\to0^+}EL_\delta(\tau)=EL(\tau)\) [PAGE 5]. Breakdown/singularity: if no neighbor satisfies \(r(\hat\pi,\pi(\tau'))\le0\), the denominator is zero and \(EL_\delta\) is undefined; if \(\delta\to0^+\) with an empty neighborhood, the estimator becomes \(0/0\). If \(\delta\) is too large, heterogeneous strategies are averaged and EL loses discriminative power.

8. EL estimator training and filtered IL [PAGE 5, Eq. (15)]:
\[
\min \sum_{\hat r_i\ge0}\left\|\mathcal{L}(l_i)-\hat r_i\right\|^2,
\]
\[
\mathcal{L}_p(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[
\mathbb{1}_{I(\tau)<I_p}\,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IL}}(\pi,\tau)
\right].
\]
Units: \(\mathcal{L}(l_i)\) and \(\hat r_i\) are reward units; \(I_p\) is the \(p\)-percentile threshold in indicator units. Breakdown: as \(p\to0\), the retained dataset vanishes and the empirical loss becomes undefined or high-variance; at \(p=1\), no filtering occurs and STRIL reverts to the original IL algorithm [PAGE 5].

9. Evaluation metric [PAGE 5, Eq. (16)]:
\[
WS(I,\pi_i)=\min_{j\in I} r_i(\pi_j,\pi_i).
\]
Units: reward units. Assumption: \(I\) is the demonstrator set; this is worst-case over observed demonstrators, not true best-response exploitability. Breakdown: if \(I\) does not cover the relevant strategy space, \(WS\) underestimates exploitability.

10. Dataset scale and representation dimensions [PAGE 12]:
Two-player Pong: \(|o|=8\), \(|a|=2\), \(N_{\mathrm{dem}}=8\), \(|\Gamma|=64K\), \(T=500\). Limit Texas Hold’em: \(|o|=72\), \(|a|=5\), \(N_{\mathrm{dem}}=7\), \(|\Gamma|=49K\), \(T=100\). Connect Four: \(|o|=84\), \(|a|=7\), \(N_{\mathrm{dem}}=7\), \(|\Gamma|=49K\), \(T=200\). The implementation uses \(z_{\dim}=8\), \(h_{\dim}=32\), \(r_{\dim}=32\), \(l_{\dim}=8\) for Pong, and \(z_{\dim}=2\), \(l_{\dim}=2\) for the other environments [PAGE 12]. These are dimensionless network dimensions, not physical units.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from POMDPs and Markov games through variational recurrent models and imitation learning. Littman’s Markov-game framing [PAGE 2] supplies the multi-agent state/action/reward object; Kaelbling, Littman, and Cassandra’s POMDP framing [PAGE 2] supplies partial observability. The P-VRNN is explicitly “similar to the original VRNN” [PAGE 3], citing Chung et al. 2015, which itself inherits the VAE/ELBO lineage: a latent variable is trained by reconstruction plus KL regularization. STRIL’s novelty is not the VRNN; it is making one latent condition \(l\) trainable per trajectory while all network parameters are shared. That is a retrocausal move: instead of identifying the demonstrator, the method makes the trajectory itself the identity-bearing object. The paper states: “The strategy representation l, which is a trainable variable, is randomly initialized for each trajectory τ.” [PAGE 4]. The representation is then “optimized together with the parameters” but “individually optimized for each trajectory.” [PAGE 4].

The imitation-learning lineage is equally important. Behavior cloning from Pomerleau 1988 is the base plug-in; ILEED from Beliaev et al. 2022 is the closest prior because it also estimates expertise, but STRIL rejects ILEED’s assumption that a uniformly random policy is necessarily worse. The paper says ILEED’s assumption “cannot be satisfied even in simple games like RPS, where a uniformly random strategy constitutes a Nash equilibrium.” [PAGE 2]. Play-LMP’s VAE latent-plan approach is rejected because it may “leak opponent information from the observations and disturb the evaluation of the demonstrator.” [PAGE 2]. Grover et al. 2018 is rejected because it uses “agent identification during training.” [PAGE 2]. TRAIL is acknowledged as sample-efficient IL via latent action space, but it is not the same filtering mechanism [PAGE 2]. Franzmeyer et al. 2024 is acknowledged as a similar filtering framework but is rejected because “their method assumes a cooperative setting and requires reward information.” [PAGE 2]. This genealogy matters because it shows STRIL is a synthesis: VRNN latent modeling + per-trajectory conditioning + zero-sum exploitability filtering.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are numerous. First trap: treating “no player ID” as “no player information.” The paper’s Figure 3a shows clusters by player ID [PAGE 6], and the observation-conditioning trick is an architectural attempt at disentanglement, not a proof. A team could spend weeks believing it removed player identity while \(l\) still encodes opponent or player style. Second trap: treating entropy as skill. RPS is the explicit counterexample; Pong Player 5 is the empirical counterexample [PAGE 13]. A team could tune RI and get apparent gains on one dataset while failing on another. Third trap: treating EL as fully unsupervised. The paper says “only 5% of the dataset is reward-labeled for EL estimation” [PAGE 5], and the EL estimator loss uses \(\hat r_i\ge0\) terms [PAGE 5]. Fourth trap: filtering too aggressively. The paper observes a drop from \(p=0.1\) to \(p=0.05\) [PAGE 7]; a team optimizing percentile on a small validation set may over-filter into data starvation. Fifth trap: evaluating only average reward rather than worst-case. The paper deliberately uses WS because zero-sum performance is about not being exploited [PAGE 5]; average reward can hide a brittle policy. Sixth trap: assuming the demonstrator ecology covers the best response. EL is local in representation space; if reward-labeled anchors are biased, EL fails [PAGE 13]. Seventh trap: assuming strategy consistency within a trajectory across long horizons; Pong uses \(T=500\), LHE \(T=100\), Connect Four \(T=200\) [PAGE 12]. If opponents adapt within a long episode, the constant-\(l\) assumption is strained.

The historical lesson is that STRIL is not a new game-theoretic solution concept. It is a representation-learning filter layered on top of existing IL. Its retrocausal “genealogy” is: POMDP/Markov game → VAE/VRNN → per-trajectory latent conditioning → entropy proxy → local reward-neighborhood exploitability proxy → percentile filter. Each arrow introduces an assumption that can break. The paper’s own limitations section is the cleanest audit: “The limitations of our work emerge when the offline datasets have undesirable properties for specific indicators.” [PAGE 13].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind reading of STRIL is not explicit in the paper, but it is forced by the multi-agent setting. A player’s observed action is a function of its own policy, its observation, and its opponent’s policy. The paper acknowledges this when it says “the strategy of the player changes according to their opponent within each episode.” [PAGE 3]. P-VRNN tries to factor this by conditioning on \(o_t\) and a trajectory-constant \(l\), so that \(l\) captures the player’s strategy while \(o_t\) carries opponent-dependent context. That is a level-1 ToM move: model the opponent as part of the observation stream. It is not a full level-2 recursive belief model, because there is no explicit belief over the opponent’s belief. Exploitability \(E(\pi_i)\) is a worst-case counter-strategy object, not a recursive belief hierarchy.

Crisis habit regression appears when a player under exploitation falls back to a predictable, low-entropy habit. The paper gives a concrete case: in Pong, “the strategy of Player 5 has the lowest entropy. However, this player is heavily exploited by Players 6, 7, and 8” [PAGE 13]. This is exactly the habit-regression failure mode: low RI looks “stable” or “expert-like” under a naive randomness heuristic, but the behavior is deterministic and exploitable. RI would rank this player as low-randomness, potentially favorable, while cross-evaluation shows it is weak. The counter-trap is the opponent’s best response: if Player 5 always repeats a habit, Players 6–8 can exploit it. EL is designed to detect this via negative-reward neighborhoods, but only if reward-labeled anchors cover the relevant representation region. If the anchors are biased, the counter-trap is invisible [PAGE 13].

The clock dimension is both game horizon and filtering percentile. The horizon \(T\) is 500 for Pong, 100 for LHE, and 200 for Connect Four [PAGE 12]. A strategy that is consistent over \(T=100\) may not be consistent over \(T=500\); the paper’s assumption “the strategy of a player is consistent within a single trajectory” [PAGE 3] is a modeling choice, not a law. The filtering clock is \(p\): at \(p=1\) there is no filtering; at \(p\to0\) the dataset vanishes. The paper reports “a drop in performance from p = 0.1 to p = 0.05 is commonly observed” [PAGE 7]. Thus the recursive-ToM policy must distinguish three clocks: the opponent-adaptation clock inside an episode, the trajectory-level strategy clock, and the dataset-filtering clock. If these are conflated, a team may filter out the very trajectories that contain adaptive counter-play, or retain habitual regressions because they look low-entropy.

The counter-trap audit is therefore: does the representation neighborhood used by EL actually contain the opponent’s best response? If not, EL underestimates exploitability. The paper’s Proposition 0.1 shows that using a single mixed strategy can underestimate the average exploitability of the mixture: \(\int \tau(\pi)E(\pi)d\pi \ge E(\int \pi\tau(\pi)d\pi)\) [PAGE 10]. That is a formal warning against collapsing a distribution of strategies into one representative strategy. STRIL uses a local mean to stabilize the estimate, but the bias remains if the neighborhood is too wide or too sparse. The recursive-ToM conclusion is: STRIL is a statistical proxy for “how badly can the observed ecology punish this habit?” It is not a full recursive opponent model, and it should not be interpreted as one.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is absolute for real physical, biomechanical, and weather claims. The source studies simulated games: Two-player Pong, Limit Texas Hold’em, and Connect Four [PAGE 5]. The Pong description says “The observation of players includes ball and paddle positions across two consecutive time steps and potential actions include moving up or down.” [PAGE 11]. That is a discrete simulated kinematic game; it contains no mass, friction, restitution, force, torque, velocity in SI units, or human joint data. Limit Texas Hold’em uses a “72-element vector” of cards and betting history [PAGE 11]; Connect Four uses an “84-element vector” of cell occupancy [PAGE 12]. Neither contains weather variables. The dataset table lists observation sizes \(8,72,84\), action sizes \(2,5,7\), demonstrator counts \(8,7,7\), trajectory counts \(64K,49K,49K\), and horizons \(500,100,200\) [PAGE 12]. None of these are physical or meteorological measurements.

Proof of veto by variable absence: a cross-layer physical claim would require a mapping from source state/action/reward into physical state variables with SI units. The source’s state space is game state; its action space is discrete game actions; its reward is win/loss or chip difference [PAGE 5]. There is no force variable \(F\) in newtons, no torque \(\tau\) in newton-metres, no mass \(m\) in kilograms, no weather state such as temperature, wind, or precipitation. Therefore any statement of the form “STRIL proves a biomechanical effect” or “STRIL predicts weather-sensitive performance” is undefined relative to the source. The only legitimate physical statement is the narrow one: in simulated Pong, paddle position and ball position evolve under game rules, and the action set is up/down [PAGE 11]. That is not a biomechanical or weather model.

Biomechanical veto: the paper has no human subjects, no motion capture, no electromyography, no joint angles, no muscle fatigue, and no physiological measurements. The “players” are policies/checkpoints, not bodies. The observation vector in Pong is ball/paddle position, not proprioception. The LHE and Connect Four observations are symbolic game states. Thus any biomechanical transfer is out-of-distribution and inadmissible.

Weather veto: no weather state, no environmental stochasticity beyond game randomness, no outdoor setting. The paper’s stochasticity is policy stochasticity and game randomness, not weather. Therefore any weather-layer claim is unsupported.

The only cross-layer bridge that survives is abstract game-theoretic: reward, exploitability, strategy representation, and filtering. That bridge is valid inside the source’s zero-sum game setting, with the caveats already noted: EL is a local proxy, RI is not universal, and filtering has a data-starvation regime. If a downstream user attempts to project STRIL onto physical sport, robotics, or weather-sensitive operations, the correct adjudication is veto unless new measurements and new state variables are supplied. The source itself does not claim such transfer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto scenario 1: Remove all reward labels and claim EL is still estimable.** The paper says “EL can be precisely estimated even with limited reward data” [PAGE 1] and uses “only 5% of the dataset” as reward-labeled for EL estimation [PAGE 5]. The EL estimator is trained by minimizing \(\sum_{\hat r_i\ge0}\|\mathcal{L}(l_i)-\hat r_i\|^2\) [PAGE 5]. Counterfactual: set the reward-labeled subset to zero and attempt to train \(\mathcal{L}\) exactly as specified. Prediction: the supervised EL objective has no terms; the estimator cannot be identified from the source objective alone. If an implementation substitutes pseudo-rewards or unsupervised proxies, it is no longer the paper’s EL. Veto condition: any claim that “EL is fully unsupervised” is vetoed; the correct claim is “RI is fully unsupervised; EL is semi-supervised and requires reward anchors.” Check: inspect the training loss and the 5% reward-label statement.

**Veto scenario 2: Permute player-ID labels and retrain.** STRIL claims to work “without player identification” [PAGE 1] and says the representation derivation is “without needing player identification” [PAGE 4]. Counterfactual: consistently permute player-ID labels in the dataset (or remove them entirely) and rerun P-VRNN training and filtering. Prediction: if the no-identification claim is true, the learned \(l\) geometry and the filtered WS should be invariant to ID permutation up to seed noise. If WS changes significantly, or if clusters track the permuted IDs, then hidden ID leakage or player-specific confounds are present. Veto condition: the claim “without player identification” is vetoed if performance depends on ID labels. Check: compare WS and representation clustering across ID-permuted runs. This is verifiable because the paper’s Figure 3a already labels clusters by Player ID [PAGE 6], making the confound testable.

**Veto scenario 3: Deterministic-poor demonstrator stress test for RI.** The paper’s limitation says “the estimation fails when the demonstrators of offline trajectories only adopt deterministic but poor strategies.” [PAGE 13]. Counterfactual: construct a dataset with a deterministic poor demonstrator and a deterministic competent demonstrator. Both have near-zero action entropy, so RI should give them similar low randomness scores. Prediction: RI will fail to rank the poor demonstrator below the competent one; filtering by RI will not reliably improve WS and may degrade it. The Pong evidence already predicts this: “the strategy of Player 5 has the lowest entropy. However, this player is heavily exploited by Players 6, 7, and 8” [PAGE 13]. Veto condition: any claim that RI is a general skill indicator is vetoed. Check: measure RI rank correlation with cross-evaluation WS on the deterministic-poor vs deterministic-competent contrast; if correlation is near zero or negative, the veto fires. A secondary check is the paper’s own \(p\)-sensitivity: “a drop in performance from p = 0.1 to p = 0.05 is commonly observed” [PAGE 7], so aggressive RI filtering should be tested against data starvation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
