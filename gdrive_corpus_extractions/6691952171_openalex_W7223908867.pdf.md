<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s inferential object is not an observed competition age. It is the latent functional \(\tau_i=\arg\max_t Y_i(t)\), where \(Y_i(t)\) is an athlete-specific latent performance trajectory. The observed data are noisy, irregular competition records \(y_{ij}=Y_i(t_{ij})+\varepsilon_{ij}\). The paper states the distinction directly: “the age of the best recorded performance may differ from the latent APP of scientific interest” [PAGE 2]. The first multi-hop step is therefore functional definition: peak age is a maximizer of an unobserved smooth curve. The second step is likelihood construction: the observed-data likelihood must integrate out both athlete-specific trajectory parameters \(\theta_i\) and latent peak ages \(\tau_i\), as in the observed-data likelihood \(L^{(o)}(\Theta\mid D)\) [PAGE 8]. The third step is regression: the AFT submodel \(\log(\tau_i)=\beta_0+\beta^\top x_i+e_i\) maps athlete covariates to log peak age [PAGE 6]. The fourth step is uncertainty propagation: the Bayesian joint posterior keeps \(\tau_i\), \(\theta_i\), and \(\Theta\) in one inferential system, so first-stage trajectory uncertainty is not discarded before the AFT regression.

The gray-zone audit is where the model can fail despite correct algebra. In the symmetric quadratic model, \(\mu(t;\alpha,\rho,\tau)=\alpha-\rho(t-\tau)^2\), \(\rho>0\) [PAGE 6]. The score with respect to \(\tau\) is \(\partial\mu/\partial\tau=2\rho(t-\tau)\), so the expected Fisher information for \(\tau\) is \(J(\tau)=4\rho^2\sigma_y^{-2}\sum_j(t_j-\tau)^2\), in units of \(\mathrm{year}^{-2}\). Thus \(\tau\) is weakly identified when \(\rho\to0\), when all \(t_j\) are close to \(\tau\), or when the design has little age variation. In the asymmetric model, \(\mu(t)=\alpha-\rho_L(\tau-t)^2\) for \(t\le\tau\) and \(\alpha-\rho_R(t-\tau)^2\) for \(t>\tau\) [PAGE 7]; the information becomes \(J(\tau)=4\sigma_y^{-2}\{\rho_L^2\sum_{t_j\le\tau}(\tau-t_j)^2+\rho_R^2\sum_{t_j>\tau}(t_j-\tau)^2\}\). If all observations lie on one side or the trajectory is nearly flat, the peak is extrapolated rather than sharply identified.

The paper’s own simulation makes this gray zone concrete by varying whether the true peak lies inside the observed window with probability approximately 90%, 70%, or 50% [PAGE 16]. Under the 50% setting, the naive pseudo-peak method attenuates \(\beta_2\) to \(0.01390\) while the proposed model gives \(0.05325\) against a true value of \(0.05\) [PAGE 17]. This is not a small numerical difference; it is the signature of generated-response and boundary bias. The audit must also include selection: the data are all-time rankings, not full careers, so “observed records primarily consist of high-level performances and may not capture every competition in an athlete’s career” [PAGE 3]. The model can reconstruct a latent curve only within the information supplied by that selected record window. It cannot by itself recover injury history, retirement, weather, event transitions, or unobserved training load. The paper concedes this: “Data-related limitations include the lack of explicit information on injury, retirement, event transitions, competition-level changes, and competing career endpoints” [PAGE 19]. A rigorous reading therefore treats the posterior for \(\tau_i\) as conditional on the observed ranking-selected record process, not as a universal biological peak.

The core source operator is the argmax operator
\[
\tau_i=\arg\max_t Y_i(t),
\]
where \(t\) is age in years and \(Y_i(t)\) is the latent performance score in World Athletics points. The observed score model is
\[
y_{ij}=\mu(t_{ij};\theta_i,\tau_i)+\delta^\top z_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ij},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_y^2),
\]
as stated in Eq. (1) [PAGE 5]. Here \(y_{ij}\) and \(\alpha_i\) are in points; \(\sigma_y\) is in points; \(t_{ij}\) and \(\tau_i\) are in years; \(\rho\), \(\rho_L\), and \(\rho_R\) are in points per year squared; \(\delta\) has units of points per unit of \(z_{ij}\). The symmetric trajectory is
\[
\mu(t;\alpha,\rho,\tau)=\alpha-\rho(t-\tau)^2,\qquad \rho>0,
\]
Eq. (4) [PAGE 6]. The asymmetric trajectory is
\[
\mu(t;\alpha,\rho_L,\rho_R,\tau)=
\begin{cases}
\alpha-\rho_L(\tau-t)^2, & t\le \tau,\\
\alpha-\rho_R(t-\tau)^2, & t>\tau,
\end{cases}
\]
Eq. (5) [PAGE 7]. The paper notes that this piecewise curve “remains continuous and differentiable at the peak age \(\tau\)” while allowing different pre-peak and post-peak curvature [PAGE 7].

The hierarchical random-effects operator is
\[
\theta_i\mid x_i\sim F_\theta(\cdot\mid x_i;\psi_\theta),
\]
Eq. (2) [PAGE 5]. In the normal special case,
\[
\theta_i=\theta_0+Bx_i+b_i,\qquad b_i\sim \mathrm{MVN}(0,\Sigma_\theta).
\]
The latent peak-age operator is the lognormal AFT model
\[
\log(\tau_i)=\beta_0+\beta^\top x_i+e_i,\qquad e_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\tau^2),
\]
Eq. (3) [PAGE 6]. Here \(\log\tau_i\), \(\beta_0\), and \(e_i\) are on the log-age scale; \(\sigma_\tau\) is a log-age standard deviation; \(\beta\) is a log-age shift per unit covariate. The conditional likelihood is
\[
f_y(y_i\mid\theta_i,\tau_i,z_i;\delta,\sigma_y)
=(2\pi\sigma_y^2)^{-n_i/2}
\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_y^2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}
\left(y_{ij}-\mu(t_{ij};\theta_i,\tau_i)-\delta^\top z_{ij}\right)^2
\right\},
\]
Eq. (6) [PAGE 7]. The latent densities are
\[
f_\theta(\theta_i\mid x_i;\psi_\theta)
=\phi_{\mathrm{MVN}}(\theta_i;\theta_0+Bx_i,\Sigma_\theta),
\]
and
\[
f_\tau(\tau_i\mid x_i;\beta_0,\beta,\sigma_\tau)
=\frac{1}{\tau_i\sigma_\tau\sqrt{2\pi}}
\exp\left\{
-\frac{(\log\tau_i-\beta_0-\beta^\top x_i)^2}{2\sigma_\tau^2}
\right\},
\]
Eqs. (7) and (8) [PAGE 7]. The complete-data likelihood is
\[
L_i^{(c)}(\Theta\mid y_i,\theta_i,\tau_i,x_i,z_i)
=f_y(y_i\mid\theta_i,\tau_i,z_i;\delta,\sigma_y)
f_\theta(\theta_i\mid x_i;\psi_\theta)
f_\tau(\tau_i\mid x_i;\beta_0,\beta,\sigma_\tau),
\]
Eq. (9) [PAGE 8]. The observed-data likelihood integrates out the latent variables:
\[
L^{(o)}(\Theta\mid D)
=\prod_{i=1}^{I}\left[
\int_{S_\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}
f_y(y_i\mid\theta_i,\tau_i,z_i)
f_\theta(\theta_i\mid x_i)
f_\tau(\tau_i\mid x_i)
\,d\tau_i\,d\theta_i
\right],
\]
Eq. (10) [PAGE 8]. The joint posterior is
\[
\pi\left(\Theta,\{\theta_i,\tau_i\}_{i=1}^{I}\mid D\right)
\propto
\left[\prod_{i=1}^{I}L_i^{(c)}(\Theta\mid y_i,\theta_i,\tau_i,x_i,z_i)\right]\pi(\Theta),
\]
Eq. (11) [PAGE 9].

The application-specific model in Eq. (12) writes
\[
\alpha_i=\alpha_0+\eta^{(\alpha)}_{\mathrm{sport}[i]}+u_i^{(\alpha)},
\]
\[
\log\rho_{Li}=\log\rho_{L0}+\eta^{(L)}_{\mathrm{sport}[i]}+u_i^{(L)},
\qquad
\log\rho_{Ri}=\log\rho_{R0}+\eta^{(R)}_{\mathrm{sport}[i]}+u_i^{(R)},
\]
and

\]
with \(u_i^{(\alpha)}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\alpha^2)\), \(u_i^{(L)}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_L^2)\), \(u_i^{(R)}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_R^2)\), and \(e_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\tau^2)\) [PAGE 10]. These are probability distributions on parameters, not observed frequencies.

The observed-data likelihood is “not a standard survival likelihood for directly censored or truncated event times”; incomplete windows affect inference through the longitudinal component \(f_y\), and uncertainty about \(\tau_i\) is propagated by integrating over latent trajectory parameters and latent peak age [PAGE 8]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is not simply “sports peak-age regression.” It is a convergence of four older lines. First, survival and event-time analysis developed methods for right-, left-, and interval-censored event times, with regression extensions for censored outcomes; the paper cites Turnbull, Finkelstein, Tian and Cai, and Zhang and Sun [PAGE 22]. Second, joint longitudinal-survival modeling linked longitudinal biomarkers to externally observed clinical events such as death, disease progression, or injury; the paper cites Wulfsohn and Tsiatis, Wu et al., Brilleman et al., and Wang and Zhong [PAGE 22]. Third, two-stage generated-regression theory warned that plugging first-stage estimates into a second-stage regression can understate uncertainty; the paper cites “Estimation and inference in two-step econometric models” [PAGE 21]. Fourth, sports-science peak-age studies used pseudo-peaks, best-performance ages, or fitted-curve summaries, often with cross-sectional or two-stage designs; the paper cites Allen and Hopkins, Huebner and Perperoglou, Haugen et al., Solberg et al., Griffin et al., Romann et al., and Spyropoulou et al. [PAGE 19 through PAGE 22].

The retrocausal genealogy matters because the paper’s key move is to reverse the usual direction of evidence. Standard joint models begin with an externally observed event time and a longitudinal biomarker. Here the “event” is not externally observed at all; it is a functional of the latent curve. The paper is explicit: APP “is not an externally occurring event time observed through a censoring mechanism. It is a latent functional, \(\tau_i=\arg\max_t Y_i(t)\), of an unobserved performance trajectory” [PAGE 3]. This reverses the usual survival-model logic: censoring-like incompleteness does not arise from a missing event indicator but from a missing or partial trajectory. The model therefore borrows the integration machinery of joint modeling while redefining the target as a curve-derived latent feature.

Multi-week and multi-season traps follow from that lineage. A short-horizon analyst sees a sparse record and substitutes the observed best age for \(\tau_i\). The paper warns that “heuristic treatments, such as excluding athletes with few records or declaring the last observed age to be the peak, may introduce additional bias” [PAGE 2]. A two-stage analyst may fit individual curves and then regress the fitted peak ages as if observed. That looks more sophisticated, but it still treats first-stage estimates as responses and can ignore first-stage uncertainty; the paper cites Murphy and Topel for this generated-regression problem [PAGE 21]. A longer-horizon trap is selection: all-time ranking data include only performances meeting ranking criteria, so the observed window is not a random sample of the athlete’s career [PAGE 3]. Another multi-season trap is event transition: athletes in multiple selected events were excluded, and the paper says those athletes “raise important questions about event-specific trajectories, within-athlete dependence, and event transitions, which we leave to future work” [PAGE 4]. Thus the genealogy warns against treating a single-event, ranking-selected longitudinal record as a complete career biography. The model’s Bayesian hierarchy corrects some of this by borrowing information across athletes, but it cannot reverse the historical selection process that generated the database.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how the analyst, the athlete, and the selection system reason about the peak. The athlete’s competition schedule is not exogenous: athletes and coaches may enter competitions when they believe performance is high, may avoid competitions when injured, and may retire before a latent peak would have occurred. The analyst observes a selected record and must infer the latent peak. A “crisis habit regression” occurs when the analyst, facing sparse or boundary-limited records, falls back to the observed best age or the last observed age. The paper directly identifies this habit as a bias source [PAGE 2]. The counter-trap is to respond with a two-stage procedure that appears to use the full trajectory but then treats the first-stage peak estimate as an observed outcome. The paper’s simulation shows that Naive 2 reduces the boundary sensitivity relative to Naive 1, but still treats first-stage peak-age estimates as observed responses, so uncertainty is not propagated [PAGE 17]. The clock is the observation-window informativeness: the simulation sets true peak coverage at approximately 90%, 70%, and 50% [PAGE 16]. As the clock runs down, the naive methods translate information loss into attenuation; the proposed model translates it into wider posterior uncertainty. The paper states this contrast: “as peak-age information weakens, pseudo-peak and two-stage analyses tend to attenuate regression effects while reporting standard errors that change little or even decrease” [PAGE 18].

The recursive problem is that each agent’s behavior changes the information available to the next agent. If selectors preferentially retain athletes who peak early, the observed record window truncates late peak ages. If athletes with late maturation are underrepresented in all-time rankings, the AFT covariate effects are conditional on selection. The model’s sport-by-sex interactions are an attempt to absorb some of this structure, but they are not a causal identification strategy. The paper reports, for example, that female athletes tend to show slightly later peak ages in most sport categories, and it cautions that this “contrasts with the common expectation that female athletes peak earlier due to earlier biological maturation” [PAGE 13]. The authors propose career dynamics, selection, participation pathways, and competitive longevity as possible explanations [PAGE 13]. That is a theory-of-mind statement about unobserved selection behavior, not a direct physiological estimate. A counter-trap would be to over-interpret the sex coefficient as biological maturation without modeling participation and retirement. The correct recursive stance is to treat the posterior peak age as a belief conditional on the observed competitive record, the athlete’s event category, and the selection process, and to widen uncertainty when the clock—the observed age window—does not contain the peak.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto has three parts: physical, biomechanical, and weather. The physical veto is that a performance score is a monotone transform of time or distance and is bounded by human physiology. The model enforces positivity of curvature through log-scale parameterization, which is physically sensible: \(\rho_L>0\) and \(\rho_R>0\) imply improvement before the peak and decline after it. But positivity alone does not guarantee a physiologically valid extrapolation. If the observed window ends before the true peak, the model can place \(\tau_i\) beyond the last observation; that estimate is a model-based extrapolation, not a measured physical event. The paper acknowledges that the posterior for \(\tau_i\) is useful “including cases in which the true peak may lie outside the observed age range” [PAGE 9], but the price is dependence on the trajectory family and prior.

The biomechanical veto is event-specific. The paper finds that “sprinting events have the largest rising-side curvature and among the smallest declining-side curvature estimates” [PAGE 15], while throwing events have “the largest declining-side curvature among the sport categories” [PAGE 15]. These patterns are biologically plausible: explosive events rise quickly and decline gradually, while throws may have sharper post-peak decline. But the model uses a single asymmetric quadratic form per athlete; it cannot represent multi-phase development, late-career technique changes, or injury-induced discontinuities. The paper concedes that “quadratic trajectories are interpretable but may not capture multi-phase or multi-peak career patterns” [PAGE 19]. Therefore any biomechanical claim requiring multi-phase structure is vetoed by the trajectory family. The model can compare sport-category curvature summaries, but it cannot prove a specific neuromuscular mechanism.

The weather veto is the clearest. The general model includes competition-level covariates \(z_{ij}\) such as wind speed, and Eq. (1) contains \(\delta^\top z_{ij}\) [PAGE 5]. However, the application states: “Competition-level covariates \(z_{ij}\) are not included in this application, since no such variables are consistently available across all events considered here” [PAGE 10]. Therefore the fitted application cannot adjudicate wind, temperature, altitude, or precipitation effects. If weather is correlated with age—for example, if athletes compete in more favorable conditions at certain career stages—the omitted term biases both the trajectory and the AFT coefficients. A formal veto proof is simple: suppose the true data-generating process is \(y_{ij}=\mu(t_{ij};\theta_i,\tau_i)+\delta^\top z_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ij}\), but the fitted model sets \(\delta=0\). If \(z_{ij}\) is correlated with \(t_{ij}\) and with the covariates in \(x_i\), the pseudo-true \(\tau_i\) and \(\beta\) minimize a Kullback-Leibler divergence that absorbs the omitted term; they need not equal the causal parameters. The source provides no weather variables, so no weather-adjusted peak-age claim is licensed. The same logic applies to injury, retirement, and training load: the paper lists their absence as a limitation [PAGE 19]. These are cross-layer variables that can veto a physiological interpretation even when the MCMC converges.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
