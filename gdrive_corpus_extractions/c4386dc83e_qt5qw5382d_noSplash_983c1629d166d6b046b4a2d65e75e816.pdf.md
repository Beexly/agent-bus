The source describes a measurement-to-inference chain: raw frame-level tracking coordinates are transformed into lineman-level play aggregates, those aggregates are joined to salary, and then salary is predicted under a salary-cap-driven front-office decision problem. The thesis states: “The purpose of this study is to evaluate the performance of oﬀensive linemen in the National Football League by using play-by-play tracking data to predict a player’s annual salary” [PAGE 3]. The central statistical object is therefore not a single play but a lineman-play observation. The algorithm first filters to passing plays, then to frames where an offensive lineman has an identified blocked defender, and then constructs per-play summaries. The source reports 8,106 retained plays after omitting rare plays without blocked players [PAGE 29], and the final modeling table contains 39,579 lineman-play rows [PAGE 38]. After excluding players below a snap threshold, the source says the data set was “reduced our number of linemen to 234” [PAGE 38].

The derivation has five layers. First, coordinates are defined on an NFL field: “the range of x-coordinates is 0 to 120 yards” and “the range of y-coordinates is 0 to 53.3 yards” [PAGE 20]. Second, frame-to-frame Euclidean distances are computed. Third, pairwise distances are computed between lineman and defender, defender and quarterback, and lineman and quarterback. Fourth, these frame-level distances are summarized by maximum, mean, median, or minimum. Fifth, the summarized features are used in regression or ensemble models to predict salary.

A major gray-zone audit issue is the difference between the printed equations and the textual description of vectors. The source says it creates an “ochanges” vector, but the printed equation for ochanges is written as a sum over frames [PAGE 30]. The same ambiguity appears for xchanges, disttrav, opoldist, defqbdist, defqbclose, and olqbdist: the equations display summation notation, while the surrounding prose describes per-frame vectors whose maximum, mean, median, or minimum are extracted. If the printed equations were implemented literally as scalar sums, the later max/mean/median operations would be ill-defined. The mathematically coherent interpretation is therefore a two-step operator: first compute a frame-indexed vector, then compute a summary statistic. This distinction matters because a scalar cumulative orientation-change sum is not equivalent to a per-frame orientation-change distribution.

Another gray-zone issue is angular wraparound. Orientation is measured in degrees, but the source equation uses absolute differences without explicitly correcting for the 359-to-0 degree boundary [PAGE 30]. A formal implementation should use a wrapped angular difference. Otherwise, a small rotation near the boundary can appear as a near-360-degree jump. A similar issue arises for “distance closed” by the defender. The source defines a proportion where “the defender covered 45.5 percent of the initial distance to get to the quarterback” [PAGE 35]. This proportion is dimensionless, but it is undefined if the initial defender-quarterback distance is zero or negative. It can also become negative if the defender moves away from the quarterback. These are breakdown regimes that should be explicitly adjudicated.

The modeling layer also contains a diagnostic shift. The unweighted stepwise regression produced heteroskedastic residuals: “the residuals tend to increase in magnitude as the ﬁtted values increase” [PAGE 48]. The weighted stepwise model did not materially fix this. The Box-Cox transformed response model improved residual behavior and yielded “a root mean square error (RMSE) of 4.7” on the test set [PAGE 52]. Random forests then improved performance, with “the 75-tree model with the best RMSE” [PAGE 55]. The source reports test RMSE values of 4.42, 4.36, 4.39, 4.43, and 4.45 for 50, 75, 100, 125, and 150 trees respectively [PAGE 55].

The gray-zone audit must also account for salary skew and injury confounding. Salary is divided by 1 million for modeling: “dividing it by 1 million” [PAGE 39]. The response is strongly right-skewed, so a log transform is appropriate. However, salary is not a pure contemporaneous performance signal. It contains contract age, draft capital, injury history, and market timing. The source illustrates this with Ronnie Stanley: “his predicted salary of just 1.41 million dollars was dwarfed by his actual 23.75 million dollar salary” [PAGE 53]. The source attributes the discrepancy to injury. This is not merely an outlier; it is a structural missing-variable problem. If injury status is omitted, the model learns a biased mapping from tracking features to salary.

The final inference is therefore bounded. The thesis shows that tracking-derived distance, acceleration, snap count, and beaten-rate features carry measurable association with salary, but the predictive mapping is unstable under injury, contract timing, and small-sample noise. The strongest verified result is not that tracking fully explains salary, but that a 75-tree random forest reduces test RMSE relative to transformed regression within this limited 8-week window.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive inference problem has three agents: the evaluator, the front office, and the player-career state. The evaluator observes tracking features \(X\), such as distance maintained from the quarterback, acceleration, and beaten rate. The evaluator wants to infer salary \(Y\). The front office wants to infer future value under a salary cap. The player-career state includes injury, age, contract status, and role. If the evaluator omits the player-career state, the model treats contract salary as if it were a direct function of current blocking features.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the model leans on stable, easily observed variables such as snap count. The source notes that “the number of snaps a player participated in” had a strong positive correlation with salary [PAGE 39]. This is rational but hazardous: snaps are partly assigned by coaches, and injured or declining veterans may lose snaps before salary adjusts. The model may regress toward the habit of rewarding availability, then mistake availability for blocking quality.

The counter-trap is the elite-contract anomaly. A player such as Trent Williams or Ronnie Stanley may have salary that is not synchronized with the current 8-week tracking sample. The source explicitly identifies Stanley’s large residual and explains it by injury [PAGE 53]. This is a counter-trap because a purely performance-tracking model will predict low salary for a high-paid injured player, while a purely salary-tracking model will assume the tracking features should already encode the injury. The correct mental model must include a latent injury state.

The clock is the temporal constraint. The source uses only Weeks 1 through 8 [PAGE 18]. This creates a recursive timing problem: early-season salary may reflect prior-year reputation, while early-season tracking may reflect current health or role. A front office reasoning under the clock knows that the observed sample is short and that the player’s true state may be partially hidden. The counter-trap is therefore not merely to add more variables, but to model the timing mismatch between contract information and tracking information.

A formal recursive representation is  
\[
P(Y_i\mid X_i, Z_i)
\]
where \(X_i\) is tracking-derived blocking evidence and \(Z_i\) is contract/injury/role context. If \(Z_i\) is omitted, the learned conditional becomes  
\[
P(Y_i\mid X_i)
=
\int P(Y_i\mid X_i, Z_i)\,P(Z_i\mid X_i)\,dZ_i,
\]
which can be biased when \(Z_i\) is correlated with both \(X_i\) and \(Y_i\). Injury is exactly such a confounder: it reduces snaps and tracking quality while salary remains contractually sticky.

The physical layer veto concerns coordinate validity, frame continuity, and biomechanical plausibility. The source defines field bounds: “the range of x-coordinates is 0 to 120 yards” and “the range of y-coordinates is 0 to 53.3 yards” [PAGE 20]. Any row with coordinates outside these bounds, non-finite coordinates, or missing player identity should be vetoed before distance operators are applied. The distance equations require finite coordinate pairs; if either coordinate is missing, Euclidean distance is undefined.

The biomechanical layer veto concerns orientation and acceleration. Orientation is reported in degrees [PAGE 30]. If angular differences are computed without wrap correction, a boundary crossing can create a false extreme. Acceleration is reported in yards per second squared, and the source says the distribution is “centered around 2.6 yards per second squared” [PAGE 44]. Extreme acceleration values should be checked against sensor noise, but the source does not provide an explicit physical upper bound. Therefore, the veto should be conditional: reject non-finite acceleration, flag values far outside the empirical distribution, and do not silently clip without documentation.

The weather layer veto cannot be adjudicated from the source because the source does not provide weather variables. It lists tracking fields as “coordinates, speed, acceleration, and orientation” [PAGE 20], but not temperature, wind, precipitation, or surface type. Therefore, a weather veto is not verifiable from this corpus. The correct adjudication is: weather may be a latent confounder, but the source provides no operator to include or reject it. This is an evidence gap, not proof that weather is irrelevant.

The salary-cap layer veto is algebraic. The source says the cap “is calculated by taking a proportion of league revenues from the previous year and dividing that figure by 32” [PAGE 16]. The final cross-layer proof is conditional: a tracking-derived salary model is admissible only if (1) coordinates are finite and within field bounds, (2) frame pairs are valid, (3) angular differences are wrap-corrected, (4) distance-closed denominators are positive, and (5) salary is positive before log or Box-Cox transformation.
