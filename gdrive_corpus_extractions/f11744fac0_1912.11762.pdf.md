Let the prediction problem be defined over a dataset of matches D = { (x^(i), y^(i)) }_{i=1}^{N}, where x^(i) ∈ ℝ^d is the d-dimensional feature vector for match i, y^(i) ∈ {1, …, K} is the discrete class label, N ∈ ℕ is the match count (dimensionless count of matches), d ∈ ℕ is the feature count (dimensionless count of predictors), and K ∈ ℕ is the number of outcome classes.

Accuracy operator:
A(𝓜 ; D) = (1/N) Σ_{i=1}^{N} I[ 𝓜(x^(i)) = y^(i) ] ∈ [0,1] , dimensionless probability, often reported as percent A × 100. For binary classification K = 2: A = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN), where TP, TN, FP, FN are counts [PAGE 6]. The review notes that three-class formulations are not directly comparable to two-class formulations because accuracy becomes harder to achieve as K increases [PAGE 6].

Ranked Probability Score (used in the 2017 Soccer Prediction Challenge rather than accuracy) [PAGE 25]:
RPS = Σ_{k=1}^{K} ( CDF_k( p̂ ) − CDF_k(y) )^2 , where p̂ is the predicted probability vector over ordered categories and y is the realized category. Units: squared probability (dimensionless²); lower is better.

Temporal cross-validation constraint (order-preserved folds) [PAGE 38]:
Let t_i ∈ ℝ be the match timestamp in seasons or rounds. A valid fold requires max_{i ∈ train} t_i < min_{j ∈ val} t_j. Violation of this constraint constitutes look-ahead leakage.

Breakdown / singularity regimes:
1. Small-sample singularity: N < 10d or N < 500 without regularization leads to variance-dominated error and unstable feature rankings [PAGE 30–31, Figures 5–7; PAGE 8 Purucker N=90].
2. Multi-class degradation: K = 3 (win/draw/loss) strictly reduces achievable A relative to K = 2 for fixed model capacity because the Bayes error increases with the number of classes [PAGE 6, PAGE 36].
3. Temporal leakage singularity: random k-fold CV on time-ordered match data invalidates the i.i.d. assumption and inflates A by an amount proportional to the strength of autocorrelation in team form [PAGE 38].

O’Donoghue & Williams (2004) shifted to Rugby Union’s 2003 World Cup, N = 48, d = 3, comparing human experts (mean 40.7/48 correct) against computer models; the simulation model achieved 93%, demonstrating that a small, lopsided tournament can yield exceptionally high predictability [PAGE 10, Table 4].

The 2010s mark the transition to algorithmic diversification. Loeﬀelholz et al. (2009) used NBA data (N = 650, d = 4) with fused ANN architectures and reported 74.3%, superior to USA Today experts [PAGE 12]. Delen et al. (2012) showed CART (d = 28, N = 244) reaching 86% in NCAA Bowl American Football, superior to SVM and ANN [PAGE 8, Table 1; PAGE 16]. Shi et al. (2013) identified an empirical accuracy ceiling of 74% for NCAAB using ANN, C4.5, RIPPER, and Random Forest, attributing the limit to feature-engineering headroom rather than algorithm choice [PAGE 18–19]. Weissbock & Inkpen (2014) introduced a cascading meta-classifier for NHL (N = 708, d = 6) reaching 60.25%, while explicitly noting that models trained beyond one or two seasons degrade because of player turnover [PAGE 10, Table 3; PAGE 21].

The late 2010s pivot to ensembles and big data: Gu et al. (2019) applied boosting, bagging, AdaBoost, and RobustBoost to NHL data (N = 1,230, d = 19) achieving 91.8% [PAGE 10, Table 3], the highest ice-hockey result in the review. Simultaneously, the Open International Soccer Database (N = 216,743 matches across 52 leagues) enabled Hubáček et al. (2019b) to reach only 52.4% with XGBoost (d = 66) [PAGE 17, Table 5; PAGE 25], Constantinou (2019) to reach 51.5% with a Hybrid Bayesian Network [PAGE 17, Table 5; PAGE 26], and Berrar et al. (2019) to reach 51.9% with kNN on rating features [PAGE 17, Table 5; PAGE 26–27]. The lineage thus moves from ANN-centric small-sample studies to ensemble-heavy moderate-sample studies, and finally to large-N soccer studies where accuracy plateaus despite massive N, implicating feature quality and sport inherent stochasticity as binding constraints.

Multi-week traps recur throughout this genealogy. Purucker’s reliance on weeks 11–16 and direct week-ahead forecasting [PAGE 8] creates a narrow temporal window that does not generalize to full-season dynamics. Kahn’s split (weeks 1–13 train, weeks 14–15 test) [PAGE 9] is temporally valid but short-horizon. Weissbock & Inkpen’s warning about player changes invalidating models beyond one or two seasons [PAGE 21] is a explicit multi-week trap: roster turnover induces non-stationarity, so a model trained on Season t has degraded validity at Season t+2 unless retrained. O’Donoghue et al. (2016) documented exactly this for Rugby: the 2015 World Cup was less predictable than prior tournaments because the gap between high- and low-ranked nations narrowed, increasing upsets [PAGE 35]. Each trap reveals that temporal validity, not raw algorithmic power, is the genealogical bottleneck.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of practitioners, when predictive accuracy collapses, to retreat to low-complexity priors. The review lists baseline comparisons: expert predictions published in newspapers or online, a majority-class rule (typically always predicting home victory because of the home-advantage phenomenon), and random selection [PAGE 37–38]. When a soccer model produces 51–55% three-class accuracy [PAGE 17, Table 5], the crisis habit is to abandon the ML model and defer to expert consensus or the home-win prior, which are themselves weakly informative under competitive parity.

The counter-trap is the feature-engineering arms race without selection. Figure 10 in the review shows nearly 90% of surveyed papers cite engineering richer features as a primary future direction [PAGE 39–40], yet only 10% cite improving feature selection [PAGE 40]. Domingos (2012) is cited for the proposition that richer features matter more than algorithm sophistication [PAGE 38], but the review also warns that overfitting is amplified when the feature pool is large relative to dataset size [PAGE 39]. Thus the counter-trap: analysts add features (ratings, recency, pre-game report sentiment [PAGE 21]) without pruning, increasing d while N remains modest, which drives variance upward and accuracy downward or flat. This is observable in soccer, where N = 216,743 but d = 66 still yields only 52.4% [PAGE 17, Table 5]; more features without selection do not break the plateau.

The clock refers to temporal validity decay. Weissbock & Inkpen state it is difficult to predict matches with models trained beyond one or two seasons due to player changes [PAGE 21]. O’Donoghue et al. (2016) show the 2015 Rugby World Cup was less predictable than earlier tournaments because competitive depth increased [PAGE 35]. The clock is therefore the rate of roster turnover, coaching changes, injury cycles, and convergence of team quality. A model’s accuracy A(t) is a decreasing function of Δt = t_test − t_train unless retraining occurs on a rolling season-by-season or round-by-round basis [PAGE 38–39]. The recursive theory of mind for this domain is: (1) crisis triggers regression to majority/expert priors; (2) the counter-trap of unchecked feature inflation inflates overfitting; (3) the clock of non-stationarity forces periodic retraining, making any static accuracy figure a time-localized snapshot rather than an enduring property.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: Low-scoring invasion sports exhibit higher outcome stochasticity per scoring event. In soccer, a goal increments the score by exactly one and no other increment is possible from a single scoring play [PAGE 35]; ice hockey is similar but marginally higher scoring [PAGE 35]. This generates a Poisson-like scoring process where a single fortuitous event can overturn a quality differential, raising the irreducible error floor. Basketball and American Football, by contrast, permit multiple point increments per scoring action (one, two, three points in basketball; varied scores in American Football), creating more score permutations but also richer signal per match [PAGE 35–36]. Veto: any model claiming high accuracy in low-scoring sports without explicitly modeling scoring-event stochasticity is physically under-specified.

Biomechanical / physiological layer: Aggregate team statistics omit player availability, injury status, fatigue, motivation, and individual quality. Reed & O’Donoghue (2005) explicitly state that motivational and injury variables were not included [PAGE 11]; Joseph et al. (2006) propose adding player-quality nodes (e.g., international-level players) to Bayesian networks [PAGE 11]; Danisik et al. (2018) incorporate player-level attributes from video-game data (139 features total) yet still achieve only 52.5% three-class soccer accuracy [PAGE 17, Table 5; PAGE 26]. Veto: if the biomechanical layer (player health, fatigue, selection) is unobserved, out-of-sample distribution shifts caused by injury waves or key-player absence invalidate the model regardless of in-sample accuracy.

Weather / venue layer: Travel distance, home advantage, and venue are documented predictors. Reed & O’Donoghue (2005) include distance travelled and match venue among seven common predictors for soccer and rugby [PAGE 10–11]. O’Donoghue & Williams (2004) use distance travelled as a proxy for home advantage in the Rugby World Cup [PAGE 10]. David et al. (2011) incorporate home-away differentials to capture home advantage [PAGE 16]. Veto: outdoor team sports are subject to weather and travel fatigue; models lacking weather and venue covariates are structurally incomplete and will fail under extreme weather or long-haul travel conditions not represented in training data.

Veto proof composition: A model may pass statistical accuracy benchmarks (e.g., 86% in American Football [PAGE 8]) yet fail in production if any of these layers shifts. The veto condition is: if P(injury | training) ≈ 0 but P(injury | test) > 0, or if weather/venue distributions differ between train and test, then the conditional independence assumption y ⟂ layer | x breaks, and accuracy is no longer bounded by the in-sample estimate. Robust deployment requires explicit monitoring of physical, biomechanical, and weather covariates as distribution-shift detectors.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

(2013) NHL data (N = 517 matches, d = 11) without enforcing order-preserved folds [PAGE 20; PAGE 38]. Veto: The reported ANN accuracy of 59% [PAGE 10, Table 3] is invalidated because future matches leak into training folds. Prediction: Under a strict season-ordered split (train on earlier matches, validate on later matches), accuracy collapses by at least four percentage points to ≤ 55% because the look-ahead information regarding team form and injuries is removed.

Scenario 2 — Feature-inflation saturation veto (verifiable): Condition: The Open International Soccer Database (N = 216,743 matches, 52 leagues) is modeled with XGBoost using d = 66 features and no dimensionality reduction or explicit feature-selection step beyond embedded tree splitting [PAGE 17, Table 5; PAGE 25]. Veto: The reported test accuracy of 52.4% [PAGE 25] sits at a saturation ceiling; further increases in d without selection will not improve generalization. Prediction: Accuracy remains bounded within [51%, 53%] unless exogenous player-level, injury, or tactical features are introduced; simply adding more aggregate match statistics yields diminishing returns because per-feature signal-to-noise in low-scoring soccer is low [PAGE 35].

Scenario 3 — Cross-sport transfer veto (verifiable): Condition: A model trained on NBA box-score features (invasion sport, quarter-based, multi-point scoring increments) is transferred zero-shot to ODI cricket (striking/fielding, innings-dependent, single toss outcome) [PAGE 20, Table 6; PAGE 36]. Veto: Direct transfer is invalid because cricket’s scoring architecture, match duration, and class structure differ fundamentally; Pathak & Wadhwa (2016) required cricket-specific features (toss, home/away, day/night, batting order) to achieve 61.7% balanced accuracy [PAGE 20–21]. Prediction: Zero-shot NBA-to-cricket transfer accuracy is ≤ 50% for binary outcomes (chance level) or ≤ 33% for three-class outcomes; after re-engineering cricket-specific features, balanced accuracy near 61.7% is achievable but not portable from basketball without structural modification.
