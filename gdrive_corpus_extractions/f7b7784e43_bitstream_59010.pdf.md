The source supplies categorical and temporal operators rather than numerical equations. We can formalize them without inventing empirical values.

1. External-force event set.  
The CDE definition lists possible external-force events: “The head being struck by an object; The head striking an object; The brain undergoing an acceleration/deceleration movement without direct external trauma to the head; A foreign body penetrating the brain; Forces generated from events such as a blast or explosion” [PAGE 33]. Let \(F\) be the set of qualifying external-force events. Units: event classes, not SI quantities. If \(F = \varnothing\), the mTBI predicate is not satisfied.

2. mTBI predicate from ACRM criteria.  
The ACRM definition states that mild traumatic brain injury is “a traumatically induced physiological disruption of brain function” and that severity “does not exceed” loss of consciousness “of approximately 30 min or less,” initial Glasgow Coma Scale score “13–15” after 30 minutes, and post-traumatic amnesia “not greater than 24 hr” [PAGE 38]. A formal predicate is:
\[
I_{\mathrm{mTBI}}(x,t_0)=1
\]
iff there exists \(f \in F\) acting on person \(x\) at event time \(t_0\), and at least one of the following holds:
\[
t_{\mathrm{LOC}} \le 30\,\mathrm{min},
\]
\[
13 \le G \le 15,
\]
\[
t_{\mathrm{PTA}} \le 24\,\mathrm{h},
\]
or another alteration in mental state or focal neurologic deficit.  
Units: \(t_{\mathrm{LOC}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{PTA}}\) are durations; \(G\) is the Glasgow Coma Scale, an ordinal clinical score ranging from 3 to 15, not an SI quantity.

3. WHO variant.  
The WHO task force defines MTBI as “an acute brain injury resulting from mechanical energy to the head from external physical forces” and includes “loss of consciousness for 30 minutes or less, post-traumatic amnesia for less than 24 hours,” and “Glasgow Coma Scale score of 13–15 after 30 minutes post-injury” [PAGE 39]. The WHO formulation uses strict inequality for post-traumatic amnesia:
\[
t_{\mathrm{PTA}} < 24\,\mathrm{h}.
\]
Breakdown regime: ACRM allows \(t_{\mathrm{PTA}} \le 24\,\mathrm{h}\), whereas WHO uses \(t_{\mathrm{PTA}} < 24\,\mathrm{h}\). At exactly 24 hours, the definitions may diverge.

4. CSG sport-concussion operator.  
The Concussion in Sport Group states that concussion “is defined as a complex pathophysiological process affecting the brain, induced by biomechanical forces” [PAGE 38]. It may be caused by “a direct blow to the head, face, neck or elsewhere on the body with an ‘‘impulsive” force transmitted to the head” [PAGE 38]. It also states that acute symptoms “largely reflect a functional disturbance rather than a structural injury and, as such, no abnormality is seen on standard structural neuroimaging studies” [PAGE 38]. A compact operator is:
\[
C_{\mathrm{CSG}} = \Phi(F_{\mathrm{biomech}}, S_{\mathrm{clinical}}, N_{\mathrm{imaging}})
\]
where \(F_{\mathrm{biomech}}\) is a biomechanical force event, \(S_{\mathrm{clinical}}\) is a graded set of clinical signs and symptoms, and \(N_{\mathrm{imaging}}\) is normal on standard structural neuroimaging. Units: categorical variables; no numerical magnitude is supplied in the source.

5. Severity veto thresholds.  
From ACRM and WHO, the mTBI window is bounded by:
\[
t_{\mathrm{LOC}} \le 30\,\mathrm{min}, \qquad 13 \le G \le 15, \qquad t_{\mathrm{PTA}} \le 24\,\mathrm{h}
\]
for ACRM, and by:
\[
t_{\mathrm{LOC}} \le 30\,\mathrm{min}, \qquad 13 \le G \le 15, \qquad t_{\mathrm{PTA}} < 24\,\mathrm{h}
\]
for WHO [PAGE 38–39]. If any threshold is exceeded, the case moves toward moderate or severe TBI classification. Breakdown regime: the boundary is not a natural kind but a convention; small measurement errors around 30 minutes, GCS 13, or 24 hours can change classification.

6. Energy-deficit balance.  
The source says concussion has been described as an “energy deficit injury” and quotes the claim that “at a cellular level, the brain is in a state of crisis immediately following concussion because of an imbalance in energy demand and available resources” [PAGE 41]. Let \(E_{\mathrm{avail}}(t)\) be available metabolic resources, and let demand be:
\[
E_{\mathrm{demand}}(t)=E_{\mathrm{cognition}}(t)+E_{\mathrm{repair}}(t)+E_{\mathrm{sensory}}(t).
\]
A crisis condition is:
\[
E_{\mathrm{avail}}(t) < E_{\mathrm{demand}}(t).
\]
Units: energy or metabolic resource units, for example joules or ATP-equivalent flux, but the source does not provide numerical values, so the equation is qualitative. Breakdown regime: if symptoms persist after \(E_{\mathrm{avail}}(t) \ge E_{\mathrm{demand}}(t)\), the simple energy-deficit model becomes underdetermined and additional mechanisms, such as network dysfunction, autonomic dysregulation, or affective processing changes, must be considered.

7. Genre-representation operator.  
The thesis states, “Each genre approaches brain injury through a different lens” [PAGE 15]. Let \(R_i\) be the representation produced by genre \(i\):
\[
R_i = \Phi_i(E, P, L)
\]
where \(E\) is the injury event, \(P\) is patient perspective, and \(L\) is language/form. For medical literature, \(\Phi_{\mathrm{med}}\) suppresses \(P\): the source says medical literature “does not include patient voices or perspectives” [PAGE 27]. For life writing, \(\Phi_{\mathrm{life}}\) centers \(P\), but market constraints select certain narratives [PAGE 22]. For fiction, \(\Phi_{\mathrm{fic}}\) may use brain injury as “narrative prosthesis” [PAGE 24]. For poetry, \(\Phi_{\mathrm{poem}}\) permits fragmentation, repetition, found text, and hybrid medical-personal language [PAGE 31]. Units: representational content, not measurable SI units.

8. Uncertainty operator.  
The source says, “Brain injury creates a feeling of uncertainty, especially as timelines for recovery and extent of recovery are difficult to estimate” [PAGE 46]. If \(S\) is the patient’s true symptom state and \(M\) is the clinician’s model of that state, one may symbolically write residual uncertainty as:
\[
U = H(S \mid M)
\]
in bits, where \(H\) is conditional uncertainty. The source does not provide probabilities, so no numerical entropy can be calculated. Breakdown regime: if \(M\) is built from a definition that excludes atypical presentations, \(U\) increases because the model cannot assign high likelihood to the patient’s actual symptom configuration.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage in the source moves from ancient observation to modern definitional instability. The earliest cited record is the Edwin Smith Papyrus, “an ancient Egyptian record of surgical cases dating from approximately 1700 BCE and believed to be copied from an older manuscript dated to approximately 3000–2500 BCE” [PAGE 13]. It contains “27 case descriptions relating to head and brain injury” and includes anatomical descriptions and trauma treatments [PAGE 13]. Hippocrates is described as using a probe to determine skull fracture and recommending trepanation if no fracture is present to relieve brain pressure [PAGE 13]. Al Rhazes, around 900 CE, “first differentiated concussion-type injuries from other types of brain injury” by distinguishing open and closed head injuries while allowing physiological damage without a lesion [PAGE 13]. Berengario da Carpi later articulated the idea that the soft brain impacts the hard skull [PAGE 14]. John Bell, in the eighteenth century, emphasized indirect brain injury and concussion, writing that “the brain is hurt … and very often, so close is the connection and sympathy of all the external and internal parts, it is hurt by the very slightest injury of scalp or bone” [PAGE 14].

The lineage then forks into a recurring trap: concussion is repeatedly pulled between structural and functional models. The source says, “Debate then began over whether there was a structural, physiological component to concussion, or whether it was a strictly functional, transient injury” [PAGE 14–15]. This fork returns in modern definitions. The CSG says acute symptoms “largely reflect a functional disturbance rather than a structural injury” and that “no abnormality is seen on standard structural neuroimaging studies” [PAGE 38]. Yet the source also notes that modern imaging may reveal pathology and that biomarkers may develop [PAGE 33]. The retro-causal trap is that historical debates do not disappear; they reappear inside contemporary diagnostic categories.

A second lineage is the sport-history trap. The source notes that “A recent study based on the population of the province of Ontario, Canada found the prevalence of concussion at 1.2% of the population annually” [PAGE 9]. It also states that “75% of all concussions are the result of ‘falls, being struck by or against an object, and motor-vehicle crashes’” [PAGE 10]. This creates a multi-week trap: because sport concussion research is prominent, clinicians, media, and patients may overgeneralize sport-return-to-play frameworks to non-sport populations. The author warns that “being able to identify the specificity of that research prevents overgeneralizing results to populations that may need different forms of care” [PAGE 31]. The trap is temporal and institutional: over weeks or months, a sport-derived definition can become the default public template, erasing elderly fall patients, domestic-violence patients, and motor-vehicle patients.

A third lineage is the repetitive-trauma lineage. In 1928, H. S. Martland described “punch drunk syndrome” in boxers and documented neurological symptoms and microscopic cerebral haemorrhages [PAGE 15]. Although his microhaemorrhage theory was later shown incorrect, his work “pioneer the understanding of repetitive head trauma as the direct cause of neurological symptoms” [PAGE 15]. This lineage feeds modern concerns about chronic traumatic encephalopathy and athletic head trauma. The multi-week trap here is cumulative exposure: a season, a tournament circuit, or repeated impacts can create a pattern that is invisible in a single clinical snapshot. The source’s creative section reflects this through repeated injury episodes and the fear that “it will happen as it has happened before: I hit my head again & it’s all over” [PAGE 223].

A fourth retro-causal trap is narrative lineage. Medical literature seeks standardization, life writing seeks voice, fiction uses tropes, and poetry hybridizes. The source argues that these genres co-produce cultural understanding. If a culture inherits a “knock on the head” comic trope, then even clinicians may absorb minimization. The author observes that “doctors and researchers are consuming the same popular media narratives as everybody else” [PAGE 21]. The retro-causal trap is that cultural narratives precede clinical encounters and shape what counts as credible injury. A patient who says “brain injury” may receive more seriousness than a patient who says “concussion,” because “the language chosen in diagnosis has been shown to influence perceptions regarding the seriousness of injury” [PAGE 21].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source reveals a recursive patient-clinician loop. The patient anticipates the clinician’s interpretive frame and tries to perform symptoms or story in a way that will unlock care. The opening passage says, “The doctors a test I need to pass. They test and test and me. If I get it right they will help. Someone help. If I get it right I will be well” [PAGE 7]. This is not merely a request for treatment; it is a theory-of-mind loop: the patient models the clinician’s model of the patient. The patient believes that correct articulation will produce correct recognition. The recursive trap is that if the clinician’s model is built around typical or mild transient injury, the patient’s atypical symptoms may be interpreted as exaggeration, anxiety, or noncompliance.

Crisis habit regression appears when the patient’s stress state activates older identity habits. The author’s pre-injury identity was athletic, disciplined, and high-performing. After injury, she describes the urge to overextend: “Maintaining balance remains elusive, however, as I ignore the urge to overdo, to extend my ambition past sustainability and into injurious modes” [PAGE 57]. Under crisis, the habit system regresses to the former athlete-self: train harder, push through, prove competence, ignore symptoms. This regression is understandable because the old self is the source of meaning, social belonging, and identity. But in concussion, the old habit can become a counter-therapeutic loop because symptom exacerbation may follow exertion, sensory load, or insufficient recovery.

The counter-trap is the construction of paced routines. The source gives concrete examples: “I keep a calendar where I give myself a sticker on days when I get writing done and a checkmark on days when I exercise” [PAGE 66]. This is a counter-trap because it replaces the binary of cure versus failure with a manageable metric of consistency. The author also uses breathing exercises and scored breath in poems such as “learning to breathe” [PAGE 187] and “voiced breathing exercise – practice daily” [PAGE 196]. These are not decorative; they are regulatory operators. The recursive counter-trap asks: instead of trying to pass the clinician’s test by performing wellness, the patient builds a daily practice that stabilizes capacity.

The clock is both external and internal. Externally, medical systems, workplaces, sports leagues, and social expectations impose timelines. The source says, “Brain injury creates a feeling of uncertainty, especially as timelines for recovery and extent of recovery are difficult to estimate” [PAGE 46]. Internally, the patient’s clock is disrupted by fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain, and cognitive fog. The author describes sleep as both compulsory and unrefreshing: “sleep an irresistible compulsion that brings no rest” [PAGE 249]. The clock trap is that the patient may be judged against a linear recovery clock even though the condition is wave-like. The source explicitly states, “Brain injury recovery is not linear, it is a wave” [PAGE 66]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore model three agents: the patient, the clinician, and the institutional clock. If the institutional clock demands rapid return to work or sport, the patient may regress to concealment. The source describes concealment as a protective response: “Reciting rote medical information in response to a question allows me to feel like my old self. My intact knowledge helps me to conceal my deficits—an innate, protective response of injured creatures” [PAGE 65].

The deepest recursive risk is that the patient begins to monitor herself through the clinician’s expected narrative. If the expected narrative is restitution, the patient may hide chaos. If the expected narrative is inspiration, the patient may hide ongoing suffering. If the expected narrative is sport tragedy, the patient may be forced into a spectacle of damage. The source resists all three by using poetry as a hybrid counter-narrative. The final poem, “Sheepish Vigil,” ends with restraint rather than triumph: “I’ll write a poem about it. / Later. Alone” [PAGE 246]. This ending preserves ambiguity. It does not force the patient to resolve into either cured hero or broken victim. The counter-trap is therefore not simply positive thinking; it is the creation of a representational space where uncertainty can be held without being immediately disciplined by a clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof separates three layers: physical/biomechanical injury reality, symptom-expression layer, and environmental modulation layer. A valid adjudication must not allow one layer to veto another incorrectly.

Physical/biomechanical veto proof.  
Let \(I\) be the proposition that the patient sustained mTBI. The source’s minimal definition requires an external force: “Mild traumatic brain injury is an alteration or disruption of brain function caused by an external force” [PAGE 29]. Therefore:
\[
I \Rightarrow F \neq \varnothing.
\]
If there is no qualifying external-force event \(F\), the mTBI claim is vetoed at the physical layer. The external-force set includes direct head impact, indirect impulsive force, acceleration/deceleration, blast, or other defined force [PAGE 33]. The CSG adds that concussion may be caused by “a direct blow to the head, face, neck or elsewhere on the body with an ‘‘impulsive” force transmitted to the head” [PAGE 38]. Thus, a purely psychological or stress-only event without biomechanical force does not satisfy the source’s mTBI predicate, though it may still produce distress or other clinical conditions.

Severity veto proof.  
The ACRM and WHO definitions impose upper bounds for mildness. If:
\[
t_{\mathrm{LOC}} > 30\,\mathrm{min},
\]
or:
\[
G < 13
\]
after 30 minutes, or:
\[
t_{\mathrm{PTA}} > 24\,\mathrm{h},
\]
then the mTBI classification is vetoed and the case enters moderate or severe TBI territory [PAGE 38–39]. The WHO also excludes MTBI if manifestations are due to drugs, alcohol, medications, other injuries, or penetrating craniocerebral injury [PAGE 39]. This is a differential-diagnosis veto: if the clinical signs are better explained by another cause, the mTBI label is not robust.

Neuroimaging veto and anti-veto.  
The CSG states that acute symptoms “largely reflect a functional disturbance rather than a structural injury and, as such, no abnormality is seen on standard structural neuroimaging studies” [PAGE 38]. This creates an important anti-veto: normal structural imaging does not veto concussion. In fact, the absence of structural abnormality is consistent with the definition. Conversely, if structural pathology is present and clinically decisive, the case may move beyond concussion into a different diagnostic class. The CDE definition allows “other evidence of brain pathology” and notes that modern imaging techniques such as diffusion tensor MRI may increase sensitivity [PAGE 33]. Thus, imaging operates as a boundary tool, not as a simple yes/no proof.

Weather veto proof.  
The source mentions environmental triggers: “Stress, screentime, workload, a change in routine, a late night, barometric pressure, socializing, shopping, or going to an event all might spark a multiday headache” [PAGE 58]. Let \(W\) be weather or barometric pressure. The source supports:
\[
W \rightarrow S_{\mathrm{headache}}
\]
but does not support:
\[
W \rightarrow F
\]
unless weather causes a fall, collision, or other external-force event. Therefore, weather can veto a deterministic claim that every headache is directly caused by ongoing structural injury, because weather may modulate symptom expression. But weather cannot veto the existence of mTBI once the initial force event and clinical criteria are established. Units: barometric pressure would be measured in hectopascals or pascals, but the source gives no threshold, so no numerical weather veto can be asserted.

Biomechanical-weather interaction veto.  
If a patient slips on ice and hits their head, then weather becomes part of the causal chain because it contributes to the external-force event. In that case:
\[
W \rightarrow F \rightarrow I.
\]
Here weather is not merely a symptom trigger; it is upstream in the injury mechanism. The source’s own injury history includes roller derby impacts, not weather, but the general force taxonomy includes falls and being struck by objects [PAGE 10, PAGE 33]. Therefore, the adjudication rule is: weather vetoes only symptom-timing claims unless it can be shown to have produced a qualifying force event.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards style veto scenarios can be derived directly from the source. Each veto is a test: if a counterfactual claim contradicts the source text, the claim is rejected, and a prediction follows.

Veto Scenario 1: “Concussion always resolves rapidly and completely.”  
This claim is vetoed by the CSG definition itself: “Resolution of the clinical and cognitive symptoms typically follows a sequential course. However, it is important to note that in some cases symptoms may be prolonged” [PAGE 38]. The source also records the author’s ongoing symptoms years after injury: “With each year that passes, I feel a little more embarrassed and a little more reluctant to talk about the ongoing symptoms I experience related to that concussion” [PAGE 8]. Prediction: If a corpus contains patient narratives, it will include cases where symptoms persist beyond expected acute windows, and where patients feel pressure to appear recovered. The source confirms this with the statement that “there is likely no ‘fix’ for my brain” [PAGE 8].

Veto Scenario 2: “Loss of consciousness is required for concussion diagnosis.”  
This is vetoed by the CSG statement that concussion “may or may not involve loss of consciousness” [PAGE 38]. The ACRM definition also includes alternatives such as “any loss of memory for events immediately before or after the accident” and “any alteration in mental state at the time of the accident” [PAGE 38]. Prediction: A valid mTBI case can exist without loss of consciousness. The source’s own clinical narrative includes symptoms such as dizziness, headache, sensory overload, and cognitive disruption without making loss of consciousness the central criterion [PAGE 8, PAGE 10]. Therefore, any diagnostic rule that requires loss of consciousness is too narrow and fails verifiable source review.

Veto Scenario 3: “Standard structural neuroimaging must show damage for concussion to be real.”  
This is vetoed by the CSG statement that acute clinical symptoms “largely reflect a functional disturbance rather than a structural injury and, as such, no abnormality is seen on standard structural neuroimaging studies” [PAGE 38]. Prediction: Patients with concussion may have normal standard structural scans while still experiencing significant symptoms. The source supports this through the patient’s mismatch between testing and lived experience: “What does it mean when my testing shows no decline in balance or cognitive function, but I feel the floor rolling under my feet and see the room spinning every time I lie down?” [PAGE 10]. Therefore, a counterfactual reward model that treats normal structural imaging as proof of no injury is invalid.

Additional RLVR prediction: If a model is trained on this corpus to detect concussion representation, it should learn that hybridity is rewarded more than pure genre certainty. The source states that “Texts that can combine and cross genres have the potential to expand representations of brain injury” [PAGE 31]. A verifiable reward function should therefore favor outputs that preserve: external-force causality, prolonged symptom possibility, absence of required loss of consciousness, normal structural imaging possibility, and nonlinear recovery. Outputs that collapse these into a simple cure narrative, a sport-only narrative, or a structural-lesion requirement should be vetoed.
