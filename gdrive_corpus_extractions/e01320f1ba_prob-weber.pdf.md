\]
and Bayes’ formula for a partition \(\{B_i\}\) is
\[
P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}

\[

\]

\[
\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-E[X])^2]=E[X^2]-E[X]^2

has units \(u^2\). For an indicator, \(E[I[A]]=P(A)\), and the source states “I[A] is a random variable” [PAGE 39].

\]

For the absorbing random walk on \(\{0,\ldots,a\}\), the recurrence is
\[
p_z=q p_{z-1}+p p_{z+1},\qquad p_0=0,\quad p_a=1,
\]
with solution for \(p\ne q\)
\[
p_z=\frac{1-(q/p)^z}{1-(q/p)^a},
\]
and for \(p=q\), \(p_z=z/a\) [PAGE 66]. Units: \(z,a\) are integer step counts. Breakdown: \(p=q\) changes the characteristic root to a double root; \(a\to\infty\) changes the limit regime.

For branching processes, let \(F(z)=E[z^Y]\) be the offspring p.g.f. Extinction probability satisfies
\[
F(q)=q,
\]
and “The probability of extinction, \(q\), is the smallest positive root of the equation \(F(q)=q\)” [PAGE 64]. If mean offspring \(\mu\le 1\), then \(q=1\); if \(\mu>1\), then \(q<1\). Breakdown: choosing a larger fixed point instead of the smallest root gives the wrong extinction probability.

For continuous variables, the density satisfies
\[
P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx,
\]
with \(f(x)\ge 0\) and total integral 1 [PAGE 69]. Units: \(f\) has inverse units of \(X\). Hazard rate is
\[
h(x)=\frac{f(x)}{1-F(x)},
\]
and “The distribution having constant \(h(x)=\lambda\) is the exponential distribution \(E(\lambda)\)” [PAGE 72]. Breakdown: nonconstant hazard vetoes the memoryless-exponential model.

Geometric probability includes Buffon’s needle:
\[
p=\frac{2\ell}{\pi L},
\]
for \(\ell\le L\), with \(X\sim U[0,L]\) and \(\Theta\sim U[0,\pi)\) [PAGE 80]. Units: \(\ell\) and \(L\) are lengths, so \(p\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: \(\ell>L\), nonuniform angle or position, or nonparallel lines.

The Chernoff bound is
\[
P(S_n>na)\le e^{-nI(a)},\qquad
I(a)=\max_{\theta>0}\{\theta a-\log m(\theta)\},
\]
where \(m(\theta)=E[e^{\theta X_1}]\) [PAGE 102]. Here \(\theta\) has inverse units of \(X\), and the exponent is dimensionless. Breakdown: \(m(\theta)\) not finite near zero; then the bound may not exist.

Value at risk is defined by a tail probability: the source says “The VaRα at α = 0.995 is the value of x such that \(P(S_K\ge x)=0.005\)” [PAGE 59]. Units: monetary units of aggregate loss. Breakdown: misspecified claim-count or severity distribution; discrete FFT truncation can also distort ultra-tail quantiles.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Renaissance game analysis to modern measure theory and random-matrix theory. The source begins with “Problem of points considered by Pascal, Fermat 1654” [PAGE 9], then moves to Laplace’s classical quotient of favourable to possible outcomes [PAGE 10], then to Kolmogorov’s axioms [PAGE 21]. The Bernoulli/binomial/Poisson/geometric family is developed as a discrete-distribution toolkit [PAGE 27–28], and Bayes’ formula is presented with a screening-test base-rate reversal [PAGE 30–31]. The Yule-Simpson effect is explicitly dated: “this phenomenon was actually first recorded by Udny Yule (a fellow of St John’s College) in 1903” [PAGE 32]. Information entropy enters via a “surprise function” with \(S(1)=0\), monotonicity, continuity, and additivity for independent events [PAGE 48]. Chernoff bounds and large deviations enter as the asymptotic theory of rare events [PAGE 101–102]. Wigner’s semicircle law appears as a random-matrix analogue of the CLT: “This result is Wigner’s semicircle law” [PAGE 104].

The multi-week traps are historical repeats. Week 1 trap: treating “probability” as one thing, when the source lists classical, frequentist, and subjective senses [PAGE 8]. Week 2 trap: counting without specifying with/without replacement and with/without ordering; the source’s “twelvefold way” discussion makes the sample space explicit [PAGE 15–16]. Week 3 trap: using Stirling-type asymptotics outside the \(n\to\infty\) regime; the source gives both weak and strong forms and an improved bound [PAGE 18–20]. Week 4 trap: assuming independence from pairwise independence; the dice examples show pairwise independence without mutual independence [PAGE 27]. Week 5 trap: base-rate neglect in Bayes; the screening example is the canonical antidote [PAGE 31]. Week 6 trap: Simpson aggregation reversal [PAGE 32]. Week 7 trap: treating conditional entropy as pointwise non-increasing; the source says reduction is only “on average” [PAGE 60]. Week 8 trap: selecting any extinction fixed point rather than the smallest positive root [PAGE 64]. Week 9 trap: applying CLT to heavy-tailed or mean-undefined variables; Cauchy is the counterexample [PAGE 88], [PAGE 97]. Week 10 trap: assuming a “random chord” has a unique answer; Bertrand’s three mechanisms give \(1/3\), \(1/2\), and \(1/4\) [PAGE 79]. These traps are retrocausal because later formalisms expose earlier informal assumptions; the fix is not a new formula but a declaration of the sample space and conditioning protocol.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis, the habit regression is to fall back to classical equiprobability: assume equally likely outcomes, independent trials, and a single pooled rate. That habit is visible in base-rate neglect: a positive screening test feels diagnostic, but the source calculates only about 9% posterior disease probability [PAGE 31]. The counter-trap is to force a partition and a likelihood: write \(P(D\mid +)\) from \(P(+\mid D)\), \(P(+\mid D^c)\), and \(P(D)\), then check whether the agent’s belief update is coherent. The recursive theory-of-mind move is to model the modeler’s model: does the agent believe that another agent will use the pooled table, the stratified table, or the mechanism-specific random-chord protocol? Simpson’s paradox is precisely a failure of recursive aggregation: each subgroup can have one direction while the pooled table has the other [PAGE 32]. Bertrand’s paradox is a failure of mechanism inference: three different “random” protocols produce three different answers [PAGE 79]. The counter-trap is to ask which physical sampling procedure generated the chord, not to average the three answers.

The clock dimension is the horizon over which the process is observed. The exponential and geometric distributions are memoryless: after waiting, the residual distribution is unchanged [PAGE 71]. That clock property is a strong structural claim; if the hazard rate is nonconstant, the clock is not memoryless. The inspection paradox adds a length-biased clock: a randomly arriving observer is more likely to land in a long interval, so the observed waiting time exceeds the naive half-mean [PAGE 76]. Conditional entropy has a clock-like averaging trap: knowledge of \(Y\) reduces entropy on average, but a particular \(Y=b_j\) can increase surprise [PAGE 60]. In recursive terms, the agent must distinguish “average over my possible observations” from “the observation I actually received.” The counter-trap is to compute the posterior for the actual conditioning event, not the average posterior. The clock also matters for extinction and ruin: finite-horizon probabilities are not equal to infinite-horizon limits; the source defines extinction as an increasing union and takes a limit [PAGE 63–64]. A crisis habit that reads an \(n\)-step simulation as an infinite-horizon theorem is a clock error, not a probability error.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A mathematical probability model can be vetoed by physical, biomechanical, or weather layers even when its algebra is correct. The proof pattern is premise-checking. If a weather process has serial dependence, the independent-trials premise is false; the source’s random-walk and Bernoulli constructions assume independent increments [PAGE 58], and the WLLN remark explicitly says independence cannot be relaxed to identical copies without change [PAGE 50]. If interarrival times have nonconstant hazard, the exponential memoryless model is vetoed; the source states that constant hazard characterizes the exponential [PAGE 72]. The bus-waiting inspection paradox is the empirical signature: a random arrival is biased toward long gaps [PAGE 76]. Thus a weather-layer veto is: estimate the hazard or autocorrelation; if it is not constant or not independent, reject the exponential/independent model.

A biomechanical veto appears in the normal-height example. The source compares UK and Netherlands male heights and shows that a small mean shift can produce a much larger conditional advantage among selected tails [PAGE 82–83]. The lesson is stated exactly: “if members of a population A are only slightly better in some activity than members of a population B, then members of A may nonetheless appear much more talented than members of B when one focuses upon the sub-populations of exceptional performers” [PAGE 83]. The veto is: if selection is threshold-based and the distribution is not normal, the normal-tail calculation must be re-estimated; if the selection mechanism changes, the conditional comparison changes. A physical-layer veto appears in Buffon’s needle: the formula \(p=2\ell/(\pi L)\) requires \(\ell\le L\), uniform angle, and uniform offset [PAGE 80]. If the needle is flexible, the source’s “Buffon’s noodle” argument changes the interpretation but preserves the expected-crossing result [PAGE 100]; if the floor lines are not parallel, the formula is invalid. A further cross-layer surprise is stochastic bin packing: Mary’s heaviest-item policy and John’s sequential-fit policy have the same unused-capacity distribution, “So, surprisingly, \(Z_J =_{st} Z_M\)” [PAGE 84]. Intuition is not a proof; the stochastic-order equality is the adjudicator.

The hardest veto is moment existence. Markov and Chebyshev bounds require finite first or second moments [PAGE 49]; the CLT requires finite variance [PAGE 97]; the Cauchy distribution has no mean [PAGE 88]. Therefore a heavy-tailed physical process—earthquake loss, network traffic, financial severity—can veto a normal approximation even if the histogram looks bell-shaped in the central region. The source’s VaR discussion depends on the aggregate-loss distribution and its tail [PAGE 59]; if the severity model is beta, Poisson, or heavy-tailed, the tail is a model output, not a universal law. Cross-layer adjudication must therefore ask: which layer supplies the moment? If no layer supplies finite variance, the CLT layer is vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Simpson aggregation reversal. Verifiable setup: use the source’s admissions table, where overall admission appears higher for one school type but the sex-specific tables reverse the comparison [PAGE 32]. Prediction: a model that reports only the pooled contrast will predict one sign; a stratified counterfactual will predict the opposite sign within each stratum. RLVR veto: reject the answer if it asserts a unique causal direction without declaring the aggregation level. The check is mechanical: compare pooled and stratified conditional probabilities; if they disagree, the correct output must flag Simpson’s paradox rather than average the signs.

Scenario 2: Bertrand mechanism non-identifiability. Verifiable setup: the prompt says only “a random chord.” The source gives three protocols with answers \(1/3\), \(1/2\), and \(1/4\) [PAGE 79]. Prediction: if the protocol is “endpoints uniform on circumference,” the answer is \(1/3\); if “midpoint uniform in disk,” the answer is \(1/4\); if “perpendicular offset uniform on radius,” the answer is \(1/2\). RLVR veto: reject any single numeric answer that does not specify the sampling measure. A second verifier can instantiate each protocol and check the empirical frequency; the three frequencies should separate as above.

Scenario 3: CLT versus Cauchy heavy tail. Verifiable setup: draw i.i.d. Cauchy variables. The source says the Cauchy distribution has no mean [PAGE 88] and that if \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are i.i.d. Cauchy then \(\frac1n(X_1+\cdots+X_n)\) is also Cauchy [PAGE 88]. The CLT theorem explicitly assumes finite variance [PAGE 97]. Prediction: the standardized sample mean will not converge to \(N(0,1)\); its tails remain heavy and scale-invariant. RLVR veto: reject any answer that applies the normal approximation without a finite-variance check. A simulation-based verifier can compare empirical tail quantiles against normal quantiles; persistent heavy tails veto the CLT claim.

A fourth possible check, if a third is needed for branching processes: use \(F(q)=q\) with mean offspring \(\mu\le 1\) versus \(\mu>1\). Prediction: \(\mu\le 1\) gives extinction probability 1; \(\mu>1\) gives \(q<1\) and the correct root is the smallest positive one [PAGE 64]. RLVR veto: reject any answer that selects a larger fixed point or reports \(q=1\) for a supercritical process. This is fully verifiable by solving the fixed-point equation and checking the root ordering.
