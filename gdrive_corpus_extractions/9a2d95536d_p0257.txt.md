First, the derivation: If a variable $X$ is log-normally distributed (which arises from the Central Limit Theorem applied to products of independent positive factors), its probability density function is $f(x) = \frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(\ln x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$. The "law of Frequency of Error" based on the arithmetic mean assumes a symmetric distribution around the mean $\mu_{arith}$. For small deviations, this is approximately true because the Taylor expansion of the log-normal near its peak is nearly Gaussian. However, for large deviations (e.g., giants or massive capital accumulation), the arithmetic mean's requirement of balancing positive and negative deviations leads to the absurdity of negative heights or populations—a physical impossibility for strictly positive quantities.

The geometric mean, defined as $\mu_{geom} = \exp(\mathbb{E}[\ln X])$, emerges naturally as the maximum likelihood estimator for the central tendency of a log-normal distribution. It respects the multiplicative nature of growth: if a nation's size doubles, its capacity for further growth scales proportionally. This is the Gompertz-Makeham law of mortality or the Pareto principle in economics—phenomena governed by scale invariance, not additive noise.

Galton's reference to Donald Macalister’s 1879 memoir is crucial; Macalister derived the exact form of the “Law of Error” based on the geometric mean, showing it follows a different functional form than the Gaussian bell curve. This was a precursor to modern information geometry, where the Fisher information metric distinguishes between exponential families (like the Gaussian) and other natural parameterizations.

**Formal LaTeX Definitions & Assumptions:**

1. **Arithmetic Mean Error Model (Gaussian):**
   Let $X$ be a random variable representing a measurable quantity (e.g., height). The standard normal error model assumes:
   \[
   X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)
   \]
   with probability density function (PDF):
   \[
   f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}, \quad x \in (-\infty, \infty).
   \]
   *Assumption:* Errors are additive, symmetric, and unbounded.  
   *Breakdown Regime:* When the coefficient of variation $\text{CV} = \sigma/\mu > 0.5$, significant probability mass lies below zero, violating positivity constraints for physical quantities like stature or population. Specifically, if $\mu + 3\sigma < 0$ is impossible, then $P(X < 0) \approx \Phi(-\mu/\sigma)$ becomes non-negligible.

2. **Geometric Mean Model (Log-Normal):**
   Define $Y = \ln X$, where $X > 0$. Assume $Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)$. Then $X$ follows a log-normal distribution with PDF:
   \[
   g(x) = \frac{1}{x\sigma_Y\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(\ln x - \mu_Y)^2}{2\sigma_Y^2}}, \quad x > 0.
   \]
   The **geometric mean** is $\mu_G = \exp(\mu_Y)$, and the **arithmetic mean** is $\mu_A = \exp(\mu_Y + \sigma_Y^2/2)$.  
   *Key Identity:* $\mu_A \geq \mu_G$, with equality iff $\sigma_Y = 0$.  
   *Breakdown Regime:* None for positivity; however, if $\sigma_Y$ is large, the mode ($\exp(\mu_Y - \sigma_Y^2)$) diverges from both means, creating interpretational ambiguity about “central tendency.”

3. **Macalister’s Law of Error (from Proc. Royal Soc., 1879):**
   Though not explicitly quoted here, Macalister derived the asymptotic form of the sampling distribution of the geometric mean. For a sample of size $n$ from a log-normal parent, the geometric mean estimator $\hat{\mu}_G$ converges normally to $\mu_G$ with variance $\sigma_Y^2/n$, whereas the arithmetic mean estimator has higher variance due to skewness.

   - Capital: Units of currency [$]. Proportional growth implies multiplicative updates → log-return is additive → justifies log-normal.

We now adjudicate whether any cross-layer vetoes invalidate the core argument.

**Physical/Biomechanical Layer:** Human stature is bounded below by zero (embryonic minimum ~0 meters) and above by biomechanical constraints (bone strength scales with cross-sectional area ∝ L², weight ∝ L³; hence maximum feasible height ~2.5–3 m given current physiology). A Gaussian model with σ=10 cm and μ=170 cm assigns negligible probability beyond ±3σ (~140–200 cm), so for routine anthropometry, the Gaussian is acceptable. Here, a mixture of log-normals or truncated Gaussians would be more appropriate.

**Meteorological/Environmental Layer:** Consider precipitation amounts. Daily rainfall is strictly non-negative and highly skewed (many zeros, few extreme events). Using arithmetic mean and standard deviation to define “normal” ranges leads to nonsensical negative lower bounds. Climate models increasingly use gamma or Weibull distributions precisely because they respect positivity and skewness—validating Galton’s intuition.

**Statistical Modeling Veto Proof:** Suppose one fits a linear mixed-effects model to longitudinal height data across ages, assuming Gaussian residuals. For children undergoing puberty, growth spurts introduce transient skewness. Residual diagnostics would show funnel-shaped plots (increasing variance with fitted value), violating homoscedasticity. Correct specification requires either transforming response (√h or ln h) or using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS). Thus, the Gaussian veto fails when dynamics involve state-dependent variability.

**Conclusion on Vetoes:** No absolute veto exists against using arithmetic means in limited contexts, but strong conditional vetoes apply whenever:
1. Data support strict positivity (e.g., concentrations, durations).
2. Variance increases systematically with mean (heteroscedasticity).
3. Extreme values dominate inference (risk assessment, insurance).
In these domains, geometric mean principles provide superior operational validity.
