The source uses feet for court distance, seconds for time, counts for player-number metrics, dimensionless probabilities, and points per possession for net gain. No information-theoretic quantity such as entropy or mutual information is reported in the source; if such a quantity were added, its unit would be bits or nats, but it must not be attached retroactively to the paper’s metrics.

Let the basket be at coordinates \(b=(x_b,y_b)\). For player \(i\) at time \(t\), define radial distance
\[
r_i(t)=\sqrt{(x_i(t)-x_b)^2+(y_i(t)-y_b)^2}\quad [\mathrm{ft}].
\]
Let \(t_{\mathrm{rel}}\) be shot release and \(t_{\mathrm{reb}}\) be rebound securement. The source defines reaction time as the shot-to-rebound interval:
\[
T_r=t_{\mathrm{reb}}-t_{\mathrm{rel}}\quad [\mathrm{s}],
\]
with reported mean \(2.22\,\mathrm{s}\) and standard deviation \(0.65\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 2]. Assumption: the tracking frame rate is sufficient to resolve the displacement; the source states \(\{x,y\}\) player positions and \(\{x,y,z\}\) ball coordinates at “25 frames per second” [PAGE 2].

The four movement counts are:
\[
PI=\#\{i: r_i(t_{\mathrm{rel}})\le 8\,\mathrm{ft}\},
\]
\[
PO=\#\{i: r_i(t_{\mathrm{rel}})\gt 33\,\mathrm{ft}\},
\]
\[
NPC=\#\{i: 8\,\mathrm{ft}\lt r_i(t_{\mathrm{rel}})\le 33\,\mathrm{ft},\; r_i(t_{\mathrm{reb}})-r_i(t_{\mathrm{rel}})\le -5\,\mathrm{ft}\},
\]
\[
NPR=\#\{i: 8\,\mathrm{ft}\lt r_i(t_{\mathrm{rel}})\le 33\,\mathrm{ft},\; r_i(t_{\mathrm{reb}})-r_i(t_{\mathrm{rel}})\ge +5\,\mathrm{ft}\}.

The source describes these as “the number of players positioned 8 feet from the basket when the shot is released,” “more than 33 feet from the basket,” neutral players who “move at least 5 feet toward the basket,” and neutral players who “move at least 5 feet away from the basket” [PAGE 2]. Assumption: the extracted “8 feet” phrase means at or inside 8 ft; the paper’s subsequent CI/RI logic requires that reading. The counts are dimensionless. The summary statistics are
\[
CI=PI+NPC,\qquad RI=PO+NPR,
\]
also dimensionless [PAGE 2]. The source removes rare \(CI=4\) cases and retains combinations with at least 100 examples [PAGE 2].

The paper’s Eq. 1 is a probability-ratio statistic, not a wagering quantity. For possession-length bin \(b\), upper-RI quartile \(U\), and lower-RI quartile \(L\),
\[
\mathcal{R}_{b}=
\frac{P_{U,b}(+1)\,P_{L,b}(0)}
{P_{L,b}(+1)\,P_{U,b}(0)}.
\]
The outcome is binary: \(0\) for a made shot or attempted free throw, \(+1\) otherwise, with \(+1\) good for the team that just missed [PAGE 3]. \(\mathcal{R}_b\) is dimensionless. Assumption: the quartile split is applied within the possession-length bin or consistently across bins; the source says possessions were grouped into upper and lower RI quartiles and then binned by possession length [PAGE 3-4]. Singularity: \(\mathcal{R}_b\) is undefined if \(P_{L,b}(+1)=0\) or \(P_{U,b}(0)=0\); finite-sample estimates require smoothing or exclusion. Breakdown: if the bins are sparse, the ratio is unstable; if possession outcomes are correlated within games, the apparent decline across bins is not independent evidence.

\[
G=P(\mathrm{OR})\,\mu_{+}-P(\mathrm{DR})\,\mu_{-},
\]
where \(P(\mathrm{OR})\) is offensive-rebound probability, \(\mu_{+}\) is average points scored after an offensive rebound, \(P(\mathrm{DR})\) is defensive-rebound probability, and \(\mu_{-}\) is average points allowed on the next possession after a defensive rebound; \(G\) has units of points per possession [PAGE 4]. Those are conditional aggregate estimates, not guaranteed causal effects. \]
because “We found that using the 75th percentile of a player’s shot distance distribution works well” [PAGE 5]. Within the first five seconds of the possession, let

\[
d_j(t_j)=\min_{k\in \mathrm{def}}\|p_j(t_j)-p_k(t_j)\|\quad [\mathrm{ft}].
\]
Then

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicitly strategic and quantitative. It opens with a military contrast: Patton’s “When in doubt, attack” against Wellington’s “know when to retreat, and to dare to do it” [PAGE 1]. That is not decoration; it frames the retrocausal problem. Analysts often observe a missed shot, then attribute the result to crash or retreat after the fact. The paper tries to move from outcome-based storytelling to tracking-based operators. Its immediate scientific ancestor is Maheswaran et al., “Deconstructing the Rebound with Optical Tracking Data,” cited as reference [1] and presented at the MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference in March 2012 [PAGE 7]. The data source is STATS SportVU [PAGE 7], with 233 games from the 2011-2012 season including playoffs [PAGE 2]. The genealogy is therefore: optical tracking makes player movement observable; movement counts become CI and RI; CI and RI become correlates of offensive rebounding and net gain; MDET becomes a defensive-transition correlate; the policy conclusion is that offensive rebounding “seems to trump the gain a team gets with a head start on getting back” [PAGE 6].

The multi-week traps are where retrocausal misuse becomes costly. Trap 1, the early-win trap: a team sees the aggregate Table 1 contrast and increases crashing for two weeks, observes more offensive rebounds, and concludes success. But offensive-rebound rate is not net gain; the source’s own Table 1 says “In all cases, the net gain is negative, i.e., missed shots are bad” [PAGE 4]. More rebounds can coexist with worse transition defense if MDET worsens. Trap 2, the adaptation trap: opponents observe a high-CI tendency and prepare early leak-outs; after several weeks the same crash rate produces worse MDET and worse net gain. The source’s Figure 11 already says MDET rises with RI and falls with CI [PAGE 6], so a crash-heavy policy has a transition cost that an opponent can exploit. Trap 3, the personnel trap: the paper admits it does “not account for the personnel on a team” and that “some teams may not have two good rebounders” [PAGE 4]. A multi-week policy built on league-average net gain can fail if the team’s rebounders are below average. Trap 4, the threshold-drift trap: MDET depends on a player-specific 75th-percentile threat range; after weeks of scouting, opponents may force different shot-distance distributions, shifting \(\rho_j\) and making historical MDET comparisons invalid. Trap 5, the coverage trap: SportVU was not installed in all arenas [PAGE 3], so a team’s early-season sample may be arena-selected; multi-week trends can be tracking-coverage artifacts. Trap 6, the transience trap: the retreat benefit is short-possession-specific, and MDET’s effect is absent after 15 s [PAGE 5]. A team that generalizes “retreat more” to all possessions will over-allocate to a transient mechanism. The correct genealogy is thus not “crash beats retreat” or “retreat beats crash”; it is a conditional, time-bounded, personnel-dependent tradeoff.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind appears in the paper as a strategic interaction between offensive crash decisions and defensive retreat decisions. At level 0, an offensive player reacts only to the ball: shoot, then chase the rebound. At level 1, the player models the defender’s likely retreat and chooses crash if the expected offensive-rebound probability is high. At level 2, the defender models the crasher’s chase and leaks out early; the offensive player then models that leak-out and assigns one teammate to retreat. At level 3, the coach models the opponent’s model: show crash to bait the leak-out, then use a counter-trap by sending a late retreater into the passing lane. The source does not formalize these levels, but its operators are the observable residue: CI is the offense’s commitment, RI is the offense’s early defensive preparation, and MDET is the defense’s success at neutralizing the first threat. The paper’s warning that “Players far away from the basket may not even consider crashing since the probability of getting the rebound is so low” [PAGE 2] is a level-1 belief about rebound probability. The paper’s finding that “Simply retreating to the defensive end of the court is not sufficient” [PAGE 5] is a level-2 correction: the defender must not merely run back; the defender must neutralize the early threat, which is MDET.

Crisis habit regression is the failure mode under fatigue, late-game pressure, or repeated exposure. A team that has practiced a Patton-like crash habit will regress to that habit when the clock is short and cognitive bandwidth is low; a team that has practiced Wellington-like retreat will regress to retreat. The paper’s short-possession result makes this concrete: the retreat benefit is greatest on short possessions, and the probability-ratio statistic declines as possession length grows [PAGE 3-4]. Under crisis, the relevant clock is not a vague “late game” variable; it is the observed shot-to-rebound interval with mean \(2.22\,\mathrm{s}\), the first-five-second MDET window, and the 15 s possession cutoff [PAGE 2, 5]. In the first two seconds after a miss, a player has almost no time to update a recursive model; habit wins. That is why the source’s operators are useful as habit diagnostics: high CI and low RI indicate a crash-default; high RI and low CI indicate a retreat-default.

The counter-trap is the strategic response to a predictable habit. If an opponent knows a team crashes three players, the opponent can release a guard early and force the crashers to run back into a lower-MDET defensive shape. If an opponent knows a team retreats early, the opponent can send a second-chance crasher and win the offensive rebound. The recursive equilibrium is mixed: some possessions crash, some retreat, and the mix depends on shot location, personnel, and clock. The paper’s Figure 11 is the tradeoff signature: MDET “tends to increase with an increase in RI and decrease with an increase in CI” [PAGE 6]. That means a crash-heavy counter-trap lowers MDET quality, while a retreat-heavy counter-trap lowers offensive-rebound probability. The clock bounds the recursion: short possessions reward early retreat; long possessions dilute the retreat benefit; first-five-second threat neutralization is the defensive test. A team that ignores the clock will overfit a habit; a team that ignores the counter-trap will be predictable; a team that ignores the personnel layer will misprice the habit for its actual rebounders.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical veto proof. The source’s movement classifier requires a radial displacement of at least \(5\,\mathrm{ft}\) over the shot-to-rebound interval. With the reported mean \(T_r=2.22\,\mathrm{s}\), the minimum average radial speed consistent with classification is
\[
\bar v_r=\frac{5\,\mathrm{ft}}{2.22\,\mathrm{s}}\approx 2.25\,\mathrm{ft/s}\approx 0.686\,\mathrm{m/s}.
\]
This is a kinematic feasibility check, not a claim that players move only at that speed; it is the lower bound for crossing the 5 ft threshold within the mean interval. If \(T_r\) is much shorter, the required average radial speed rises as \(5/T_r\); as \(T_r\to 0\), the classification threshold becomes physically impossible or dominated by tracking noise. If \(T_r\) is long, slow drift can be misclassified as an intentional crash or retreat. Biomechanically, the paper does not measure player height, reach, vertical jump, acceleration, fatigue, or position, so it cannot prove that “effort” rather than proximity, size, or assignment caused a rebound. The source itself says the association “does not show that crashing the boards causes offensive rebounds” [PAGE 2]. A physical-layer veto therefore applies to any strong causal claim that CI “creates” rebounds without controlling for shot location, defensive alignment, and rebounder skill.

The MDET layer has a similar physical check. Threat range is player-specific at the 75th percentile of shot distance, so a center and a 3-point shooter have different \(\rho_j\) [PAGE 5]. That is physically sensible, but it means MDET is not a pure distance measure; it is a distance-to-defender normalized by a player-specific shot profile. If a player’s shot-distance distribution shifts, MDET changes without any defensive improvement. Also, MDET is a maximum over early threats, so one blown assignment can dominate the score; a low mean MDET does not prove every defender neutralized every threat. The source’s mean difference of \(9.05\,\mathrm{ft}\) versus \(9.63\,\mathrm{ft}\) [PAGE 6] is compatible with a small number of high-leverage breakdowns.

Weather-layer veto. The source contains no weather variable. The games are indoor NBA games, and the paper does not report arena temperature, humidity, travel, or atmospheric conditions. Any explanation that invokes weather to explain CI, RI, MDET, or net gain is therefore vetoed as unsupported by the source. Arena and tracking-coverage differences are present in the data—SportVU was not installed in all arenas [PAGE 3]—but those are instrumentation and selection issues, not weather. Cross-layer adjudication therefore proceeds in this order: first, reject any claim not represented by the source operators; second, reject any causal claim that ignores personnel, defensive positioning, and game situation, all of which the paper lists as unconsidered [PAGE 6]; third, retain only claims that survive kinematic feasibility and out-of-sample validation. Under that rule, the defensible claim is conditional: in the observed 2011-2012 SportVU sample, crash/retreat movement and early-threat neutralization are associated with offensive rebounding, short-possession defense, and net gain, but the physical and biomechanical mechanisms are not isolated.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
