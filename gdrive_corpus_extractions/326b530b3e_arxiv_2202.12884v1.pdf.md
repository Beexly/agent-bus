This source is not a contest or performance-event corpus; it is an arXiv software-engineering paper about automated bug detection in 3D video games. The reasoning chain must therefore be built from rendering, collision, observation, labeling, and anomaly-detection operators rather than from any external event model. The first multi-hop step is the paper’s definition of automated bug detection as two problems: “automated game exploration and bug identification” [PAGE 1]. The second step is the platform: World of Bugs uses a 3D first-person Unity environment, an exploration agent, a main camera observation, a bug-mask camera, and a vector sensor [PAGE 4]. The third step is the learning target: the model sees only the rendered screen, while the bug mask supplies pixel-level labels: “The bug mask acts as a label for the agents observation and is instrumental in enabling machine learning to be applied to the bug identification problem” [PAGE 4]. The fourth step is the anomaly model: a convolutional autoencoder is trained on normal observations, reconstructs inputs, and scores anomalies by reconstruction error. The paper states: “We use Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) [23] with a small MSE penalty term as the objective function, and squared L2 norm as the anomaly score” [PAGE 6]. The fifth step is evaluation: observations are labeled positive when the mask contains more than a threshold τ of colored pixels, and precision, recall, PR-AUC, and ROC-AUC are reported [PAGE 6, PAGE 10]. The sixth step is the gray-zone audit: does this prove general perceptual-bug detection? No. The paper itself says the reconstruction approach “was able to identify many of the bugs” but “was less good at identifying some of the more subtle bugs” [PAGE 6]. The hardest gray zone is geometry clipping: if the player is fully enclosed inside an object, the observation may look normal because the back-side geometry is not rendered; the model then sees a normal background while the mask still labels the frame as bugged [PAGE 6]. That is a direct mismatch between visual evidence, mask label, and model belief. A second gray zone is the threshold τ: increasing τ removes small mask regions and can change precision-recall behavior, especially for subtle bugs such as Z-clipping; Table I shows Z-clipping PR-AUC falling from 0.319 at τ=0 to 0.072 at τ=400 [PAGE 10]. A third gray zone is temporality: screen tearing, Z-fighting flicker, and the onset of geometry clipping are not fully represented by one frame; the paper notes that “A model that learns from sequences of observations may perform better in this case” [PAGE 6]. A fourth gray zone is the unsupervised premise: in real development “it is unlikely that one has access to a bugless version of the game,” so the paper treats its single-normal-version experiment as a proof of concept [PAGE 5]. The audit conclusion is therefore narrow but strong: the paper demonstrates that a simple reconstruction model can identify some visual bugs from screen pixels, especially missing textures, boundary holes, camera clipping, geometry corruption, and black screen; it does not demonstrate general perceptual-bug detection, audio-bug detection, behavioral-bug detection, or robustness to fully occluded/enclosed geometry. \[
x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 84\times 84},
\]
with the source’s exact dimension statement: “All observations are images are of dimension \(3 \times 84 \times 84\)” [PAGE 5]. The paper does not specify pixel normalization; if normalized to \([0,1]\), pixel intensity is dimensionless, while if 8-bit intensities are used, MSE and squared-L2 have units of intensity squared. The total scalar pixel count is
\[
N = 3\cdot 84\cdot 84 = 21{,}168.
\]
The bug mask is a pixel map \(M_t\), and the source defines the label rule as: “an observation is labelled as a containing a bug if the bug mask contains more than \(\tau\) coloured pixels” [PAGE 6]. Formally, with \(n_M(x_t)\) the number of colored mask pixels,
\[
n_M(x_t)=\sum_{i=1}^{84}\sum_{j=1}^{84}\mathbf{1}\{M_t(i,j)\neq 0\},
\qquad
y_\tau(x_t)=\mathbf{1}\{n_M(x_t)>\tau\}.
\]
The threshold \(\tau\) is measured in colored pixels and takes values \(0,10,50,100,200,400\) in Table I [PAGE 10].

The autoencoder is
\[
\hat{x}_t = D_\phi(E_\theta(x_t)),
\]
where \(E_\theta\) is the encoder and \(D_\phi\) the decoder. The paper’s training objective is a weighted SSIM plus MSE combination: “We used a combination of SSIM and MSE weighted with 0.9 and 0.1 respectively as the objective function” [PAGE 9]. A formal statement is
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)
=
0.9\left[1-\mathrm{SSIM}(x_t,\hat{x}_t)\right]
+
0.1\,\frac{1}{N}\left\|x_t-\hat{x}_t\right\|_2^2 .
\]
SSIM is dimensionless; MSE has intensity-squared units; the \(0.9/0.1\) weights are dimensionless. The anomaly score is the squared L2 norm:
\[
s(x_t)=\left\|x_t-\hat{x}_t\right\|_2^2,
\]
which has intensity-squared units. The source confirms: “The scoring function used to rank observations is squared L2 between the observation and reconstruction” [PAGE 9].

The evaluation operators are
\[
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}},
\qquad
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},
\]
with precision and recall dimensionless in \([0,1]\). PR-AUC and ROC-AUC are also dimensionless ranking areas. The paper emphasizes precision because a developer report should not force sifting through many false positives [PAGE 6].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are explicit. First, the reconstruction method assumes normal training data: “The reconstruction approach only works if the auto-encoder rarely (or never) encounters anomalous examples during training, the training is in this sense semi-supervised” [PAGE 6]. If bugs contaminate the normal set, the score’s normal/abnormal separation is invalid. Second, SSIM alone is unstable: “Although SSIM produces better reconstructions we found it was subject to some instability which was reduced by introducing a small MSE penalty term” [PAGE 9]. The singularity regime is therefore the MSE-weight-zero limit, or the SSIM denominator/local-variance degeneracy that the MSE term stabilizes. Third, using MSE or BCE alone is worse: the paper found “SSIM + MSE penalty were visibly more convincing and the resulting models performed better than those trained with Mean Squared Error (MSE) or with Binary Cross Entropy loss (BCE) or SSIM alone” [PAGE 6]. Fourth, precision is undefined when \(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}=0\); PR-AUC is ill-conditioned when positives are extremely rare. This matters at high \(\tau\): Z-clipping PR-AUC is \(0.319\) at \(\tau=0\) but \(0.072\) at \(\tau=400\), while its ROC-AUC is \(0.812\) and \(0.864\) respectively [PAGE 10]. Fifth, if \(\tau\) exceeds the maximum possible mask count, all labels become negative and AUC is undefined. Sixth, a single-frame score cannot represent temporal artifacts; the paper explicitly suggests sequence learning for geometry clipping [PAGE 6]. The reported experiment used “5 epochs on the full 300k normal training examples and tested on the full test dataset (500k) examples” [PAGE 9], with model size “Total trainable parameters = 2,225,379” [PAGE 11].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind here means tracking nested beliefs: the renderer produces ground truth; the exploration agent has no explicit goal and only wanders; the autoencoder has a learned belief about “normal”; the developer has a belief about intended behavior; the evaluator has the bug mask. The paper says the environment “does not have a defined goal for the agent” and that omitting a goal simplifies exploration [PAGE 5]. That is a belief mismatch: the agent does not know what the developer intends, and the autoencoder does not know what the mask knows.

Crisis habit regression: the autoencoder’s habit is to reconstruct normal scenes. A crisis occurs when a bug appears but the model reconstructs a plausible normal scene anyway. The clearest case is geometry clipping: “the model was not able to identify cases where it was fully enclosed in the geometry of the object. The poorer performance could be because the back-side of the object geometry is not rendered” [PAGE 6]. The model’s habit regresses to “normal background” precisely when the player is inside geometry. The same risk exists for subtle texture corruption or Z-clipping, where the visual deviation may be small relative to normal variation.

Counter-trap: the natural response is to lower the anomaly threshold so subtle bugs are caught. That is a counter-trap because it increases false positives, and the paper’s report design explicitly cares about developer workload: a model should have high true-positive rate and low false-positive rate, and precision is highlighted [PAGE 6]. Lowering the threshold may rescue a few subtle positives while flooding the report with normal frames. The Z-clipping PR-AUC collapse at high \(\tau\) is the mirror image: raising \(\tau\) can remove small positives and make precision-recall behavior worse even when ROC-AUC looks stable [PAGE 10].

Clock: the missing variable is time. Screen tearing is a refresh/frame-rate mismatch [PAGE 9]; Z-fighting flickers as the view shifts [PAGE 9]; geometry-clipping onset is a boundary-crossing event. The paper notes: “A model that learns from sequences of observations may perform better in this case” [PAGE 6]. A single-frame model has no clock, so it cannot distinguish “object disappeared because the player is inside it” from “object is simply not in view.” The recursive correction is to give the model a temporal belief state and to give the developer a report ordered by both anomaly score and temporal onset, not just isolated frames.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
