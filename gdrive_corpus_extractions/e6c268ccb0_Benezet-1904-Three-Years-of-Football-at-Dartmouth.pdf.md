P_d(L)=1-e^{-L/L_c}\quad\text{(dimensionless)},
\]
where \(L\) is illuminance in lux and \(L_c\) is a critical scale. Breakdown regime: \(L\ll L_c\), so \(P_d\to0\) and officiating/player perception dominates. The 1902 Harvard account describes “intense darkness,” a referee lighting a match, and a lantern piloting the teams [PAGE 100–101]; the source claims the half was “six minutes too long” [PAGE 95]. This is a formal perception veto, not a skill term.

Weight is a physical parameter. If \(\bar W_D,\bar W_O\) are mean line weights in pounds, then in SI
\[
\Delta W=0.453592\,(\bar W_D-\bar W_O)\quad\text{(kg)}.
\]
The 1901 Harvard account says Dartmouth’s line averaged “19 pounds per man lighter than Harvard’s” [PAGE 39]. The 1903 Harvard account says the total weights were “identical, 2,039 pounds for each team” [PAGE 205]. Thus the same institution moves from a negative \(\Delta W\) regime to a near-zero \(\Delta W\) regime; the source treats this as a necessary condition for the 1903 result.

Penalty expectation is
\[
E[L_p]=\sum_k k\,\Pr(P=k)\quad\text{(yards)},
\]
with \(L_p^{\rm SI}=0.9144E[L_p]\) m. The source repeatedly notes penalties and disputed off-side calls: in the 1902 Harvard game, Dartmouth is set back 20 yards on a holding call and Witham takes a 20-yard retreat [PAGE 195–196]; in the 1903 Williams game, Dartmouth is “set back 20 yards for holding, and 10 more for off-side play” [PAGE 156]. These are observable penalty operators.

The most important breakdown regime is data inconsistency. The Bowdoin 1901 summary gives 35–6 [PAGE 30], while the telegram gives 35–0 [PAGE 31]. Any equation using \(S_D,S_O\) as exact measurements must carry an error term \(\epsilon\) with nonzero variance when the source disagrees with itself. A second breakdown is sample-size: single-game rushing totals such as “Dartmouth rushed the ball 225 yards, Princeton 64” [PAGE 186] are descriptive, not estimates of a stable mean. A third is rule-regime nonstationarity: 1901, 1902, and 1903 are not the same game because of the changing-goals rule and the seven-men-in-line rule [PAGE 11].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage begins with reconstruction. In 1901, Dartmouth “began a new era” with McCornack’s return and Bowler’s training [PAGE 13]. The source says the alumni and students were “firmly resolved that to achieve victory no stone should be left unturned” [PAGE 13]. That is an institutional operator: it changes recruiting, training, and tolerance for injury. The 1901 season ends with a 22–0 Brown win [PAGE 60], and the source frames this as the moment Dartmouth acquires “a taste of football glory” [PAGE 66]. That expectation then feeds the 1902 season. In 1902, the Athletic Council bars Lindsay and Turner from athletics [PAGE 67–68], a hard veto that prevents Dartmouth from fielding the heavyweight line it had just acquired. This is a multi-week trap: the roster improves in raw mass, but eligibility removes the mass from the field, so the coaching staff must compensate with speed and team play.

The 1903 genealogy is then a delayed consequence. Folsom returns as head coach [PAGE 139], and the source says the “new epoch, the ‘big four’ feeling in Dartmouth, dated from November 15th, 1902” [PAGE 140]. That is a retrocausal marker: the 1903 confidence is dated to the 1902 Harvard game, not to the 1903 roster. The 1903 schedule is “one of the hardest that the college ever attempted” [PAGE 140], including Williams, Princeton, Wesleyan, Amherst, Harvard, and Brown. The Princeton trip is “450 mile journey each way” and is “the fight which was feared most of all” [PAGE 141]. That travel load is a multi-week trap because it interacts with injury and classwork.

The clearest multi-week trap is overwork before Williams. The source says Dartmouth was “a bit ‘stale’” and that “Fearing the Williams game on account of Dartmouth’s 1901 experience ‘Mac’ had overworked them in preparation for this year’s battle” [PAGE 83]. The result is the Amherst loss, 12–6 [PAGE 86]. This is not a one-week surprise; it is a training-load error whose effect appears the following week. The source notes that “several men began to lose weight” and that Vaughan began the game at 156 pounds [PAGE 83]. That is measurable: a weight-loss threshold crossed before the contest. A second trap is look-ahead substitution. Before Amherst, Witham starts substitute guards and a substitute tackle and leaves Vaughan on the sidelines because “It was the Harvard game which was in mind” [PAGE 176]. The source admits the risk: “it would not do to show our full strength beforehand” [PAGE 176]. That is a recursive planning trap: the coach optimizes for a future opponent and thereby increases the probability of a present loss.

A third trap is schedule delay. The 1901 Brown game is postponed from November 23 to November 28, and the source says “Every day of delay meant five per cent added to the strength of the Brown team” [PAGE 43]. Whether or not the five-percent figure is exact, the operator is clear: delay favors the team with more recoverable injuries and more practice time. Brown then holds Dartmouth to a 12–6 win [PAGE 121], much closer than the 1901 22–0 result. The source’s own counterfactual is that Brown was “coming” fast [PAGE 43].

The scientific lineage is therefore not “momentum” but reliability and survival analysis. The team is a redundant system: when one lineman fails, the Markov chain changes; when several fail, the system crosses a threshold. The 1903 Princeton game is the proof: Dartmouth outrushes Princeton 225 to 64 [PAGE 186], but the injury cascade at right tackle and the loss of Bullock, Glaze, Dillon, and Knibbs [PAGE 160] dominate. The 1903 Harvard and Brown games are the counter-proof: when the roster is restored and the training load is timed correctly, the same system produces 11–0 and 62–0 [PAGE 205–206, 231–232]. The genealogy is thus: 1901 training rebuild → 1901 Brown win → 1902 expectation and eligibility veto → 1902 Harvard near-miss → 1903 Folsom and heavy line → 1903 Princeton injury veto → 1903 Harvard/Brown peak. The multi-week traps are overwork, look-ahead substitution, travel, injury concealment, and weather.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears whenever the source describes a team under stress reverting to individual tricks rather than system play. In the 1901 Wesleyan game, Thompson “having snatched the ball from Knibbs’ hands, emerged from the crowd and with a clear field tore down towards the goal” [PAGE 27]. In the 1901 Harvard game, Barnard “picked the ball out of Place’s hands after the latter had called ‘Down’” [PAGE 39]. In the 1903 Brown game, Russ “steals the ball” and runs, but the referee brings it back [PAGE 118]. These are counter-trap failures: the opponent models the rule edge and tries to exploit it, but the officials’ perception is the final veto. The source’s repeated complaints about these plays show that the Dartmouth model expects clean officiating; when the officials do not share that model, the expected transition matrix is wrong.

Counter-traps are the positive cases. McCornack hides plays before the 1901 Brown game because he is “assured that the Providence men had sent their coaches up to ‘get a line’ on Dartmouth’s play,” so “he would not permit the use of any of our best formations” [PAGE 40]. That is recursive theory of mind: Dartmouth models Brown modeling Dartmouth. In 1903, Witham uses the 20-yard retreat against Harvard: when Dartmouth is penalized, he moves the ball back to keep possession and get a new first down [PAGE 195–196]. The source says the east stand “rocks with applause as the importance of this bit of strategy dawns upon the rooters” [PAGE 195]. In the 1903 Brown game, Witham calls a quick on-side kick from the left side line; the ball strikes the goal post and Dartmouth recovers [PAGE 222]. These are counter-traps that exploit the opponent’s expectation of a conventional punt or conventional down.

The clock is the third recursive layer. In the 1902 Harvard game, the source says the second half was “six minutes too long” [PAGE 95] and describes officials “scouring the field in search of the ball” in darkness [PAGE 100–101]. The Dartmouth model says the game should have ended with a 6–5 lead; the Harvard model says the game continued and produced two late touchdowns. This is not a skill disagreement; it is a disagreement over the clock state. In the 1903 Brown game, the source says Brown’s late surge was due in part to Dartmouth fatigue and that “very few of the men had slept the night before” [PAGE 232–233]. The clock and fatigue interact: a late-game surge by Brown is more likely when Dartmouth’s hazard rate is elevated and the remaining time is sufficient. The recursive model must therefore include the officials’ clock, the players’ fatigue, and the coaches’ expectations about both.

The crisis-habit regression is most visible in the 1903 Brown game’s late sequence. Brown, already far behind, “began to hustle their heavy opponents back up the field in quick order” [PAGE 230]. The source says the plays were aimed at Glaze, Turner, and Clough [PAGE 230], i.e., at the injured or tired points. That is adaptive opponent modeling: Brown reads the Dartmouth fatigue map and targets it. Dartmouth’s counter is substitution: Bullock takes Lillard’s place, Gage takes Clough’s place, and the line repulses two plays for a loss [PAGE 231]. The recursive loop is: Brown models Dartmouth fatigue → Dartmouth substitutes → Brown’s expected gain falls. The final whistle ends the game before Brown can convert the late advantage [PAGE 231].

The source’s own recursive bias is also visible. It repeatedly predicts that Dartmouth “should” have scored more, that officials “should” have called differently, and that the clock “should” have ended earlier [PAGE 95, 186]. That is a theory-of-mind failure in the opposite direction: the chronicler models the game as a fair test of skill, while the actual game includes officials, weather, and darkness as first-class agents.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical veto proof is strongest in the 1903 Princeton game. The source says Dartmouth “outrushed the Tigers, 119 yards to 28” in the first half [PAGE 165] and later gives a full-game figure of 225 to 64 [PAGE 186]. Yet Princeton wins 17–0. The reason is not mysterious: Bullock is knocked flat and disabled for the season, Glaze is stunned, Dillon is blinded, and Knibbs is “badly used up” [PAGE 160]. Turner’s right arm is “absolutely useless” [PAGE 160], and the Princeton account identifies right tackle as “the only apparent weakness in the Dartmouth line” [PAGE 162]. This is a biomechanical veto: the injury removes a specific node from the line graph, and the opponent routes its gains through that node. The source even says Princeton scored two touchdowns on Turner’s right side [PAGE 160]. The veto condition is an inequality: if the injury-induced drop in effective line strength exceeds a threshold \(W_c\), the offensive rush advantage cannot convert into points. The source’s own data show the threshold was crossed.

A second physical veto is weight. In 1901, Dartmouth’s line averaged “19 pounds per man lighter than Harvard’s” [PAGE 39], and Harvard wins 27–12. In 1903, the total weights are “identical, 2,039 pounds for each team” [PAGE 205], and Dartmouth wins 11–0. The source explicitly says that in 1901 “This great discrepancy in weight was the main cause of Dartmouth’s loss” [PAGE 39]. That is a cross-layer veto claim: when the mass difference is large, skill and speed cannot fully compensate. The 1903 case is the control: when mass is equal, the faster and better-coached team wins. The formal condition is \(\Delta W < -W_c\) for a weight veto; if \(\Delta W\approx0\), the veto is inactive.

A third veto is weather and field friction. The 1903 Williams game is played in “a steady downpour of rain” and the field becomes “one great bog” [PAGE 153]. Dartmouth rushes for 435 yards but fumbles five times and is penalized repeatedly; the source says “On a dry day the score would have been 36 to 0, at least” [PAGE 159]. The veto is not that Dartmouth was weaker; it is that the ball-handling probability and footing probability fell below the threshold needed to convert yards into points. The 1903 Brown game is played at “18 degrees” with the ground “frozen to an adamantine hardness” [PAGE 216]. That is a different friction regime: hard ground increases injury and reduces secure footing, but Dartmouth’s heavier line and straight-ahead rushing are less affected than Brown’s lighter, edge-oriented attack.

A fourth veto is visibility and officiating. The 1902 Harvard game is played into “intense darkness”; the referee lights a match and a lantern is used to locate the side line [PAGE 100–101]. The source says the half was “six minutes too long” [PAGE 95]. The formal condition is \(L\ll L_c\): when illuminance is below the critical scale, \(P_d\to0\), and the game outcome is dominated by the officials’ ability to see the ball and the clock. The 1903 Harvard game is played in clear November sun [PAGE 183], so the visibility veto is inactive and the better team wins.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: physical veto > informational advantage > play-calling entropy. The Princeton game proves that a team can outrush an opponent and still lose when the physical veto is active. The Harvard 1903 game proves that when weight is equal and visibility is good, the better-coached, faster team wins. The Brown 1903 game proves that when the physical and training systems are aligned, the score can become extreme. The exact breakdown regimes are: injury cascade at a key line position; weight deficit beyond \(W_c\); visibility below \(L_c\); field friction below \(\mu_c\); and officiating uncertainty that makes the clock or ball state non-measurable.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

**Counterfactual 1: Restore Dartmouth’s injured/absent players for the 1903 Princeton game.** The source says Dartmouth outrushed Princeton 225 to 64 [PAGE 186], but lost because Bullock, Glaze, Dillon, and Knibbs were injured and Turner’s arm was useless [PAGE 160]. It also says Princeton’s gains went through right tackle [PAGE 162]. Prediction: if Bullock, Lindsay, Patteson, Clough, and Foster are available and Turner and Vaughan are healthy, Princeton’s right-tackle gains fall below the level needed to sustain drives, and Dartmouth’s rush advantage converts into at least one score. Verifiable test: compare the Princeton chart’s right-tackle gains [PAGE 162, 186] with the 1903 Harvard and Brown charts, where Dartmouth’s line is intact [PAGE 212–215, 235–236]. If the healthy-line Dartmouth rush/point conversion exceeds the Princeton-game conversion, the counterfactual veto is confirmed.

**Counterfactual 2: End the 1902 Harvard second half at the correct time.** The source says “those of us who saw this game will never be convinced that the second half was not six minutes too long” [PAGE 95], and it describes the final Harvard touchdowns occurring in darkness [PAGE 100–101]. Prediction: if the half ends when Dartmouth’s timekeepers believe it should, the final score is Dartmouth 6, Harvard 5, not Harvard 16, Dartmouth 6. Verifiable test: reconstruct the scoring sequence from [PAGE 97–101] and compare with the source’s own claim that the score “would have stood 6 to 5 in our favor” [PAGE 95]. If the last two Harvard scores occur after the disputed interval, the clock veto is confirmed.

It also says Brown’s late surge was aimed at Glaze, Turner, and Clough [PAGE 230]. Prediction: with normal sleep, Dartmouth’s late-game injury/fatigue hazard is lower, Brown’s final 19-yard sequence fails earlier, and the 62–0 score is preserved without the late Brown advance.
