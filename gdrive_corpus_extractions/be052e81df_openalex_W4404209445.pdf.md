<THINK_TRACE>
The source paper is not a match-preview artifact in the usual sense; it is a measurement-to-forecasting study about whether extreme training or match GPS-derived efforts can predict extreme match performance metrics for soccer players. The deepest inferential chain begins with the observation that the authors deliberately restrict themselves to sparse-sample, explainable models because the number of train-then-play pairs is extremely limited. They state: “the number of individual training samples is equal to the number of matches played, that is, very small w.r.t. contemporary models’ requirements” [PAGE 3]. This constraint drives every downstream modeling decision: linear regression with positive slopes, piecewise linear extensions, persistence-weighted weekly aggregation, standard scaling for group models, and exploratory GPS threshold grids rather than high-capacity neural architectures.

A first derivation is the transformation from raw observation to model input. The raw sample is defined as \(s = (t, t + \tau, A, q)\) [PAGE 4], where \(t\) is time, \(\tau\) is the interval duration, \(A\) contains categorical metadata, and \(q\) contains measurements. In the Apex Pro workflow, \(q\) becomes a rich vector of aggregated soccer load metrics; in the raw GPS workflow, \(q\) collapses to instantaneous velocity. The modeling target is formed by applying an aggregation operator \(M\) to match-day samples, while the predictor is formed by applying a history operator \(T\) to training samples preceding match day \(d\): \(x_d = T(s : d - H \le t < d)\), \(y_d = M(s : t = d)\) [PAGE 6]. This is the structural heart of the paper. It means the model is not predicting a latent psychological state; it is predicting an engineered physical metric, such as distance in a high-intensity zone, metabolic time in a high zone, or high metabolic load distance.

A second derivation is the role of persistence. The authors weight prior weeks by \(\alpha^k\) over a four-week macrocycle, with \(k=1,\ldots,4\) [PAGE 6]. The persistence factor controls how much recent training dominates older training. If \(\alpha\) is near zero, only the most recent relevant week matters; if \(\alpha\) is near one, older weeks retain weight. The paper finds that models are generally stable across \(\alpha\), but player-specific exceptions exist. For player A3, stronger discounting of older data improves error from \(3.1\%\) at \(\alpha=0.9\) to \(2.1\%\) at \(\alpha=0.1\) [PAGE 8, PAGE 10]. This is a crucial gray-zone insight: optimal memory length is not universal. It depends on the athlete’s adaptation, fatigue decay, and training consistency.

A third derivation concerns extreme value logic. The authors invoke Extreme Value Theory as motivation, noting that extreme speed, strength, and endurance often decide competitive advantage. They write: “EVT was chosen over conventional prediction methods because it allows for the analysis of extreme values, which are crucial in the context of sports performance” [PAGE 2]. However, they do not fit a full generalized extreme value distribution. Instead, they use linear or piecewise linear models because the sample size is too small. This is an important gray zone: the paper is philosophically extreme-value oriented but operationally ridge-safe. It seeks maximal efforts, but avoids heavy-tailed parametric estimation because the data cannot support it.

A fourth derivation is the GPS interval method. The authors define high-effort intervals as consecutive samples above a velocity threshold \(v_{\min}\) lasting at least \(t_{\min}\): \(S(t_{\min}, v_{\min}, d, H)\) [PAGE 7]. The input can be the count of such intervals, the duration percentile, or the velocity percentile. They state: “GPS-Cnt applied over tmin × vmin provides, in fact, bivariate complementary cumulative distribution function (ccdf) for intervals over period [d - H, d) for a given player” [PAGE 7]. This is a compact way to summarize sprint-like exposure without assuming a single fixed sprint threshold. The paper’s best GPS-based result for player A3 uses \(H=14\), \(v_{\min}=5.75\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(t_{\min}=10\ \mathrm{s}\), with \(R^2=0.77\) for HML Distance [PAGE 11]. Yet the authors also warn that distribution tails are fragile: “far parts of the distribution tails contain only a couple of intervals, and hence are risky to base any reliable model upon” [PAGE 7]. This is a major gray-zone boundary: the farther one pushes into rare maximal efforts, the fewer observations support the inference.

A fifth derivation concerns the distinction between correlation and prediction. The GPS models show that shorter histories often align better with match metrics: “Observed values of R2 score show that the shorter training history H is considered, the better alignment of GPS-based intervals with match metrics is obtained” [PAGE 10]. But \(R^2\) and MAPE can disagree. The paper explicitly notes incoherence between model assessment metrics for GPS models [PAGE 10]. This means a model can explain variance well in-sample or correlationally while still failing as a forward prediction. The practical consequence is that extreme-effort models must be treated as descriptive unless test-set error remains small.

A sixth derivation concerns individual versus group modeling. Individual models are preferred because player-specific scaling and idiosyncratic relationships dominate. The authors warn that group models can mislead: “We have encountered situations (not shown in the paper) where the relationships in unique models contradict those that reveal joint modeling for the group, perfectly illustrating Simpson’s paradox” [PAGE 13]. This is a deep audit point: a group model may appear useful because it pools data, but it can encode between-player confounding rather than within-player temporal dynamics. The paper’s strongest caution is therefore: “The resulting models are, therefore, descriptive rather than predictive” [PAGE 14]. That sentence should govern any downstream use. The models are useful for coaching diagnosis, drill selection, and workload interpretation, not as autonomous forecasts.

The final gray-zone audit concerns missing latent variables. The paper recognizes that match performance is shaped by unobservable factors: opponent strength, tactics, randomness, weather, player physical condition, and detailed match context. It says future work should extend the database with “weather conditions, physical condition of players, or detailed match statistics” [PAGE 14]. Since the current models omit these layers, they are structurally incomplete. A high predicted extreme value can be vetoed by a biomechanical, environmental, or health-related constraint that the model never sees. Thus the correct epistemic status is conditional: the models are valid inside the observed training history and measurement system, but they become unreliable when latent physical or environmental constraints dominate.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are explicit and should be treated as measurement transformations rather than metaphorical indices.

1. Sample tuple.  
The basic observation is defined as  
\[
s = (t, t + \tau, A, q)
\]
[PAGE 4].  
Here \(t\) is time in seconds, \(\tau\) is the sampling interval in seconds, \(A\) is a categorical attribute set, and \(q\) is the measurement vector. In the Apex Pro case, \(q\) contains aggregated load metrics. In the raw GPS case, the sample simplifies to  
\[
s = (t, v)
\]
where \(v\) is instantaneous velocity in \(\mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\tau = 0.1\ \mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 6]. The paper also states: “Position and instantaneous acceleration are sampled with 10 Hz and 100 Hz frequency, respectively, by the biometric vests” [PAGE 4]. Thus time resolution is \(0.1\ \mathrm{s}\), velocity has units \(\mathrm{m/s}\), and acceleration validation uses \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\).

2. Linear predictive model.  
The core model is  
\[
\hat{y} = a_0 + a^{T}x
\]
[PAGE 5], with constraints  
\[
a \ge 0, \quad x \ge 0.
\]
Here \(\hat{y}\) is the predicted match performance metric, \(a_0\) is the intercept, \(a\) is the coefficient vector, and \(x\) is the nonnegative input vector. The nonnegativity constraint is intentional: the authors write that they “base our analyses on plain or moderately evolved linear regression models with positive slope coefficients” [PAGE 5]. The units of \(\hat{y}\) depend on the selected metric. Examples include distance in meters, time in seconds, or per-distance normalized quantities. The coefficient units are therefore output-units divided by input-units.

3. Match-day aggregation.  
The target construction is  
\[
x_d = T(s : d - H \le t < d),
\]
\[
y_d = M(s : t = d)
\]
[PAGE 6].  
\(T\) transforms training history into an input vector; \(M\) extracts and aggregates the match-day output. \(H\) is the history window in days. In the GPS experiments, \(H\) is tested at 14, 21, and 28 days [PAGE 10]. In the Apex aggregation, the default macrocycle persistence window is four weeks [PAGE 6].

4. Persistence-weighted aggregation.  
For Apex inputs, the weighted weekly aggregation is  
\[
x_i^d = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{4} \alpha^k q_n}{\sum_{k=1}^{4} \alpha^k}
\]
[PAGE 6].  
\(\alpha\) is dimensionless and controls decay of older weeks. \(q_n\) is the selected metric in a sample with fixed attributes. The denominator normalizes missing weeks and avoids bias from unequal exposure. Breakdown regime: if all relevant historical activities are absent and no substitute historical value exists, the operator is undefined. The paper handles this by saying: “If a given player has not completed any activity selected by (A, qn) in the four weeks preceding the match, the most recent historical value is taken as substitute” [PAGE 6]. This substitution is a pragmatic assumption, not a physical law.

5. Standard scaling for group models.  
For player \(j\), sample \(i\),  
\[
x'_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j}{\sigma_j},
\]
\[
y'_{ij} = \frac{y_{ij} - \bar{y}_j}{\varsigma_j}
\]
[PAGE 6].  
Here \(\bar{x}_j\) and \(\bar{y}_j\) are player-specific means, while \(\sigma_j\) and \(\varsigma_j\) are player-specific standard deviations. The transformed variables are dimensionless. Breakdown regime: if a player has zero variance in a feature or target, \(\sigma_j = 0\) or \(\varsigma_j = 0\), and the scaling is undefined. The paper uses this scaling to allow group models to pool players, but then evaluates errors back in original player spaces [PAGE 6].

6. Feature redundancy removal.  
The authors reduce feature redundancy using a threshold: “It was decided to find pairs of features that correlate with each other at a level above the threshold. In our case it was 0.99. The Mutual Information method was used for this purpose” [PAGE 6]. If correlation or mutual information is used as a dimensionless dependence measure, the threshold \(0.99\) is near-perfect redundancy. In information-theoretic form, mutual information can be written as  
\[
I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}.
\]
If the logarithm is base 2, units are bits. If natural logarithm is used, units are nats. The source does not specify the log base, so the safest statement is that the dependence measure is dimensionless up to the chosen logarithmic unit.

7. GPS high-effort interval operator.  
The source defines high-effort intervals as  
\[
S(t_{\min}, v_{\min}, d, H) = \{S_i\},
\]
where  
\[
S_i = \{(t,v) : d - H \le t < d,\ v \ge v_{\min}\},
\]
and  
\[
\max_{S_i} t - \min_{S_i} t \ge t_{\min}
\]
[PAGE 7].  
Here \(v_{\min}\) has units \(\mathrm{m/s}\), \(t_{\min}\) has units \(\mathrm{s}\), and \(H\) has units days. The count model is  
\[
x_d = \|S(t_{\min}, v_{\min}, d, H)\|
\]
[PAGE 7].  
The output of this operator is a dimensionless count. The paper notes that this grid of counts is a bivariate complementary cumulative distribution function over the history window [PAGE 7].

8. Probabilistic GPS variants.  
Two additional operators are defined:  
- GPS-Time: value of \(t_{\min}\) corresponding to the \(p\)-th percentile of samples for a given \(v_{\min}\).  
- GPS-Vel: value of \(v_{\min}\) corresponding to the \(p\)-th percentile of samples for a given \(t_{\min}\)  
[PAGE 7].  
The percentile \(p\) is dimensionless. The resulting input units depend on whether the model uses duration in seconds or velocity in \(\mathrm{m/s}\).

9. Cross-matched GPS output.  
For match-day cross-checking,  
\[
y_d = \|S(t'_{\min}, v'_{\min}, d+1, 1)\|
\]
[PAGE 7].  
This counts intervals on the next day using possibly different thresholds \((t'_{\min}, v'_{\min})\). The method is called GPS-X. The paper finds a systematic shift: “extreme training intervals correlate well with match intervals less intense by 0.7 m/s and by 3 s” [PAGE 12]. This is a major operational result: training extremes may not map one-to-one to match extremes; the match expression can be slightly lower in intensity and duration.

10. Error metrics.  
The paper uses \(R^2\) for training correlation and MAPE for predictive quality [PAGE 8]. A standard definition consistent with the source’s use is  
\[
\mathrm{MAPE} = 100 \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|y - \hat{y}|}{|y|}\right],
\]
with units of percent. \(R^2\) is dimensionless:  
\[
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar{y})^2}.
\]
The source reports the best Apex individual model with “a relative error of 2.3%” [PAGE 1]. It also reports that some models reach MAPE as small as \(0.8\%\), but warns that flat regression lines can make such errors practically useless [PAGE 8]. This is a key breakdown regime: low numerical error without meaningful slope is not a valid performance model.

11. Saturation and piecewise linear breakdown.  
The piecewise linear model fits two line segments. The paper reports: “intense training (x1 ⪆20) does not result in consistently improved match performance” [PAGE 9]. This is a saturation regime. Formally, if the marginal derivative  
\[
\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial x_1}
\]
becomes near zero beyond a threshold, additional training input no longer yields proportional match output. The model’s second segment being almost flat indicates that the linear positive-slope assumption breaks down at high loads.

12. Extreme-value lineage without full EVT fitting.  
The paper situates itself in extreme value modeling: “The idea of extreme value modelling has been present in environmental sciences for decades, where models of events of extraordinary magnitude were based on parametric tuning of one of three heavy-tailed distributions: Frechét, Weibull or Gumbel” [PAGE 3]. However, because of sparse match samples, the authors do not estimate those distributions. Instead, they use simple linear or piecewise linear relationships. This is a formal breakdown regime: the theoretical object is extreme value prediction, but the empirical estimator is a low-capacity regression.

13. GNSS measurement assumptions.  
The raw GPS fields include latitude, longitude, height, timestamp, speed, course, HDOP, VDOP, number of visible satellites, and satellite ID [PAGE 3]. The authors validate acceleration by comparing reported acceleration with velocity difference quotients and find “almost perfect linear correlation” with a typical cutoff near \(6\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) [PAGE 4]. They also state that they found no apparent impact of instantaneous satellite count in that validation [PAGE 4]. Nevertheless, the paper acknowledges that GPS data are noisy and may need filtering such as Kalman filtration [PAGE 4]. Therefore, a measurement breakdown regime exists if HDOP or VDOP indicates poor geometry, if satellite visibility is insufficient, or if the velocity field is corrupted by signal loss.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of this paper runs from environmental extreme value theory into sports physiology, then into GPS microtechnology, and finally into practical soccer monitoring. The authors explicitly trace extreme modeling to environmental sciences: “The idea of extreme value modelling has been present in environmental sciences for decades, where models of events of extraordinary magnitude were based on parametric tuning of one of three heavy-tailed distributions: Frechét, Weibull or Gumbel” [PAGE 3]. This lineage matters because extreme environmental events are rare, high-impact, and often modeled by tail distributions. The paper borrows the conceptual importance of extremes but not the full statistical machinery, because match samples are too few.

A second lineage is sports physiology. The paper cites Extreme Value Theory being applied to maximal oxygen uptake: “This is confirmed by the work where EVT has proven to be an effective tool for predicting extreme values in sports physiology based on extreme VO2max values” [PAGE 2]. This is important because VO2max is a physiological capacity metric, whereas the present paper predicts GPS-derived output metrics. The causal distance is greater: VO2max is a lab capacity estimate, while match GPS output is an expression constrained by tactics, opponent, pacing, fatigue, and environment. The paper therefore inherits the aspiration of extreme physiology but must downgrade its certainty.

A third lineage is meteorology and air quality. The authors mention Russell et al. applying extreme modeling to ozone concentration by identifying factors such as temperature, sunshine, and humidity [PAGE 3]. They also mention Russell extending the approach to drafting football players [PAGE 3]. This genealogy explains why the paper is comfortable with the idea that extreme outcomes can be predicted from combinations of preceding conditions. But it also reveals a missing layer: ozone modeling included environmental covariates. The soccer paper’s current models largely omit weather, pitch, and health covariates, except as future work.

A fourth lineage is sports analytics. The paper reviews basketball, baseball, hockey, graph neural networks, transformer-based action prediction, and reinforcement learning [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. It acknowledges that such methods can model complex interactions but then rejects them for this dataset because they require competencies and data volumes not available. The chosen method is therefore deliberately ancestral: linear regression, piecewise linear regression, simple aggregation, and threshold counting. This is a retro-causal design choice: the future ambition is advanced prediction, but the historical constraint is sparse data.

The multi-week traps are central. The first trap is the four-week macrocycle. The authors assume a four-week persistence window because it is “the usual macrocycle duration” [PAGE 6]. This assumption may be valid for a stable training block, but it can fail during congested schedules, international breaks, injury return, or tactical periodization changes. If the true adaptation window is shorter or longer, the persistence factor \(\alpha\) will misweight the relevant causal history.

The second trap is memory decay. The model uses \(\alpha^k\) weighting, but the paper finds that the best \(\alpha\) is not universal. Player A3 improves when older data are discounted, while other players show the reverse [PAGE 10]. This suggests that fatigue decay, motivation, and training history interact differently across athletes. A multi-week trap occurs when a coach assumes that more history is always better, when in fact older data may encode a previous fitness state that no longer applies.

The third trap is the match-to-match carryover. The paper finds that a match metric can predict a later match metric: “the model based on metrics from the last match also effectively predict extreme motor performances occurring in the game” [PAGE 1]. For one player, the input was “Accelerations Total Time Per Distance in Zone 6” and the output was “Distance in Zone 6” [PAGE 1]. This is scientifically interesting, but operationally dangerous if overinterpreted. A previous match may reflect a deterministic readiness state, but it may also reflect opponent-specific tactics or an anomalous game script. The authors note that such match-based models are of little practical utility for systemic planning because match performance is not directly controllable [PAGE 10].

The fourth trap is GPS history length. The GPS experiments compare 14, 21, and 28 days. The paper finds better correlation with shorter history, but this does not automatically mean better prediction [PAGE 10]. A shorter window may capture recent neuromuscular readiness, but it may also overfit to a temporary spike. A longer window may capture chronic capacity, but it may include stale fatigue. The correct interpretation is regime-dependent: short windows are better for acute readiness, while longer windows may be better for chronic load, but only if the athlete’s state is stationary.

The fifth trap is extreme value rarity. The paper warns that far distribution tails contain few intervals and are risky [PAGE 7]. This is a classic multi-week trap: the most extreme efforts are rare, so models trained on them have high variance. A single anomalous sprint can dominate a percentile estimate. The result is that extreme forecasting can become unstable precisely when it is most desired. The paper’s caution is therefore not incidental; it is structural.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how coaches, players, and analysts interpret extreme performance signals. The paper’s models are not merely statistical objects; they become part of a social feedback loop. If a coach sees that a player produced an extreme value in training or the previous match, the coach may infer readiness, fatigue resistance, or tactical opportunity. The player may infer that the coach expects repeated high output. This expectation can alter behavior: the player may push harder, hide fatigue, or alter pacing. The model then becomes an input to the very phenomenon it tries to predict.

A useful formal frame is to distinguish habit prior, crisis signal, counter-trap, and clock. Let \(q_t\) be a training or match metric at time \(t\). A persistence-weighted expectation is  
\[
\mu_t = \sum_{k=1}^{4} w_k q_{t-k},
\]
where  
\[
w_k = \frac{\alpha^k}{\sum_{j=1}^{4} \alpha^j}.
\]
This is the model’s internal “habit memory.” It has the units of the selected metric. If \(q_t\) is velocity in \(\mathrm{m/s}\), \(\mu_t\) is also in \(\mathrm{m/s}\). If \(q_t\) is distance in meters, \(\mu_t\) is in meters.

Crisis habit regression occurs when a high-effort match or a dramatic training spike causes the coach and player to overattribute a new stable state. In simple terms, the system mistakes a crisis-driven burst for a new baseline. The paper’s persistence factor is a partial defense against this: if \(\alpha\) is low, older extreme values decay quickly, reducing the risk that a single crisis effort dominates the forecast. However, if \(\alpha\) is high, the model may retain the crisis value too long. The paper’s player-specific \(\alpha\) results show that this is not a universal solution [PAGE 8, PAGE 10].

Counter-trap means designing the model and the coaching decision to avoid being captured by a misleading extreme signal. The piecewise linear model is one counter-trap. The paper reports that after a high input threshold, “intense training (x1 ⪆20) does not result in consistently improved match performance” [PAGE 9]. This is a saturation counter-trap: it tells the coach that more high-intensity training may not linearly increase match output. Another counter-trap is the GPS-X shift: training intervals correlate with match intervals that are “less intense by 0.7 m/s and by 3 s” [PAGE 12]. This tells the coach that match expression may be attenuated relative to training expression, so the model should not assume exact transfer.

Clock refers to the temporal window in which the signal is valid. The paper tests 14, 21, and 28 days for GPS models [PAGE 10]. It also says: “However, logic suggests that the last seven days are good predictors (in this aspect, further research is needed)” [PAGE 15]. The clock layer is therefore ambiguous. The empirical GPS results favor shorter histories for alignment, but the practical conclusion points to the last week as especially relevant. This creates a recursive tension: the model can see four weeks, but the coach may need to act on the last seven days. If the coach overweights old data, the model may predict stale capacity. If the coach overweights the last seven days, the model may become reactive to temporary spikes.

A deeper recursive trap is Simpson’s paradox. The authors state: “We have encountered situations (not shown in the paper) where the relationships in unique models contradict those that reveal joint modeling for the group, perfectly illustrating Simpson’s paradox” [PAGE 13]. In theory-of-mind terms, this means a coach may observe a group trend that is opposite to the individual trend. If the coach applies the group model to an individual, the player may be misclassified. The player’s actual response may then surprise the coach, causing a crisis interpretation: “The model failed.” But the real failure is the mismatch between group-level inference and individual-level dynamics.

The recursive loop also includes measurement trust. The player may trust GPS because it is objective, but the paper acknowledges that GPS can be noisy and may require filtering [PAGE 4]. The coach may trust the model because it is simple and explainable, but the authors themselves say the models are “descriptive rather than predictive” [PAGE 14]. This creates a counter-trap: the very simplicity that makes the model usable also encourages overconfidence. The correct recursive stance is to use the model as a diagnostic clock, not as an oracle. It should answer: “Which recent training patterns are associated with extreme match output?” not “What will the player certainly do next?”

The final theory-of-mind layer is tactical decision latency. If the model is used before a match, the coach can adjust training load, rest, or lineup expectations. If used during a match, the model would need real-time inference and a much faster clock. The source is not designed for live in-play forecasting. Its clock is daily or weekly. Therefore, any live use would require a new temporal operator, new validation, and new physical constraints. Without that, the model’s recursive influence remains pre-match and post-match, not in-play.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a latent physical, biomechanical, weather, or measurement constraint invalidates a model prediction even if the statistical fit is acceptable. The source paper’s models are built from historical training and match GPS metrics, but they do not fully observe the physical state that determines whether an extreme output can be realized.

Let the modeled output be \(\hat{y}\), with units determined by the selected metric. Let the realized output be \(y\). A minimal formal decomposition is  
\[
y = f(x) + b(C) + \varepsilon,
\]
where \(f(x)\) is the learned historical relationship, \(C\) is a vector of latent constraints, \(b(C)\) is the constraint-induced shift in output units, and \(\varepsilon\) is residual noise. The model predicts \(\hat{y} \approx f(x)\). A veto is valid when \(b(C)\) is sufficiently negative that \(y\) cannot approach \(\hat{y}\). The veto is not a statistical artifact; it is a physical unattainability condition.

Biomechanical veto.  
The paper selects extreme metrics such as accelerations, decelerations, high metabolic load distance, and high-speed running. These require neuromuscular capacity, tendon resilience, and recovery state. If a player has accumulated microtrauma, reduced eccentric strength, or impaired deceleration mechanics, the realized high-zone output can fall below the model’s expectation. The authors acknowledge the importance of injury prevention and fatigue management: extreme efforts can expose players to temporary fatigue or injury risk [PAGE 2]. They also note that training effort can have a negative impact on competition performance: “non-linear and negative impact of the training effort on performance in competition is an established fact” [PAGE 5]. This supports a biomechanical veto: if recent load exceeds the player’s absorptive capacity, additional high-intensity training may reduce rather than increase match output.

Formally, if \(x\) is high but recovery \(R\) is low, the effective output can be written as  
\[
y = g(x, R) + \varepsilon,
\]
with  
\[
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} > 0 \quad \text{when } R \text{ is sufficient},
\]
but  
\[
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} \le 0 \quad \text{when } R \text{ is insufficient}.
\]
The model assumes the first regime. The veto applies when the second regime dominates. This is exactly the saturation warning in the piecewise model: after a high threshold, more input does not produce more output [PAGE 9].

Weather veto.  
The paper explicitly identifies weather as a missing variable for future precision: “Extending the database with additional variables, such as weather conditions, physical condition of players, or detailed match statistics, can significantly increase the precision and reliability of predictions” [PAGE 14]. Weather affects high-intensity output through heat strain, humidity, wind, wet pitch friction, and ball dynamics. If temperature is measured in degrees Celsius, humidity as a fraction, and wind in \(\mathrm{m/s}\), these variables can alter metabolic cost and sprint mechanics. A model trained on temperate, dry conditions may overpredict high-zone distance in hot or humid conditions. A veto is valid when the environmental state lies outside the training distribution.

A formal weather veto can be expressed as a distribution shift:  
\[
P_{\text{match}}(y \mid x) \ne P_{\text{train}}(y \mid x).
\]
If the shift is caused by weather, the historical conditional relationship is no longer transportable. The paper’s models do not include weather covariates, so they cannot distinguish a true performance decline from an environment-induced decline. This is not a minor caveat; it is a structural veto.

Measurement veto.  
The raw GPS fields include HDOP, VDOP, visible satellites, and satellite ID [PAGE 3]. The authors validate acceleration against velocity-derived acceleration and find strong agreement, but they also acknowledge that GPS is noisy and may require Kalman filtration [PAGE 4]. If HDOP or VDOP indicates poor satellite geometry, or if signal loss produces incomplete paths, the input \(x\) may be corrupted. In that case, the model may predict from an artifact rather than from true effort. A measurement veto is valid when the measurement error variance becomes comparable to or larger than the biological signal variance.

Let \(\sigma^2_{\text{GNSS}}\) be GNSS measurement variance and \(\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}\) be biological performance variance. A simple admissibility condition is  
\[
\sigma^2_{\text{GNSS}} \ll \sigma^2_{\text{bio}}.
\]
If this fails, the observed extreme value may be a sensor artifact. The paper’s validation reduces this risk but does not eliminate it, especially for rare maximal efforts where few samples exist [PAGE 7].

Adjudication rule.  
The correct cross-layer adjudication is hierarchical. First, verify measurement validity. If GNSS quality is poor, veto the observation. Second, verify physical availability. If injury, illness, or excessive fatigue is present, veto the expected output. Third, verify environmental transportability. If weather or pitch conditions differ materially from training history, veto the numerical prediction. Only after these vetoes are cleared should the statistical model be used diagnostically. The source supports this conservative stance because it concludes: “The resulting models are, therefore, descriptive rather than predictive” [PAGE 14].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means counterfactual verification against observable outcomes before allowing the model to influence training or selection decisions. The verification reward can be defined from the paper’s error metric:  
\[
r = -\mathrm{MAPE} = -100 \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{|y - \hat{y}|}{|y|}\right].
\]
The unit is negative percentage error; higher reward means lower error. A counterfactual veto rejects a model’s influence if a plausible intervention changes the latent state and the observed outcome falsifies the model’s implied expectation.

Veto Scenario 1: GPS-Cnt extreme interval model under environmental stress.  
The paper reports that the best GPS-Cnt model for player A3 uses \(H=14\), \(v_{\min}=5.75\ \mathrm{m/s}\), and \(t_{\min}=10\ \mathrm{s}\), with \(R^2=0.77\) for HML Distance [PAGE 11]. The model implies that recent training intervals above \(5.75\ \mathrm{m/s}\) lasting at least \(10\ \mathrm{s}\) are associated with high match HML Distance. Counterfactual veto: if the match occurs under heat, humidity, wet pitch, or congested fixture conditions that reduce high-speed output, the realized HML Distance should fall below the model’s expectation. Verifiable prediction: compare observed match HML Distance to \(\hat{y}\) using the same test protocol. If the observed value is lower than \(\hat{y}\) by more than the model’s GPS-Cnt MAPE tolerance reported for \(H=14\) [PAGE 10], the environmental veto is supported. The model should then be downgraded from predictive to descriptive. This veto is especially important because the paper itself identifies weather as a missing variable [PAGE 14].

Veto Scenario 2: APX individual model under acute fatigue or health perturbation.  
The best individual Apex model predicts Metabolic Time Zone 5 and 6 Per Distance from Decelarations Distance in Zone 5 and 6 Per Distance for player A1, with MAPE \(2.3\%\) at \(\alpha=0.5\) [PAGE 8]. Counterfactual veto: if player A1 enters the match with acute illness, sleep loss, muscle soreness, or a congested schedule, the historical relationship may fail. Verifiable prediction: if the player’s match output falls below \(\hat{y}\) by a margin exceeding the model’s \(2.3\%\) test error, and if independent health or load monitoring confirms the perturbation, the veto is accepted. The paper’s own caution supports this: models are descriptive rather than predictive [PAGE 14], and training effort can negatively affect competition performance when nonlinear fatigue is present [PAGE 5]. The model should not be used to push additional high-intensity work if the player’s physiological state is outside the historical manifold.

Veto Scenario 3: APX group model applied to a player with incompatible distribution.  
The paper reports group-model MAPE values for players A1–A4 under APX-Grp. For A1, values are low, around \(2.3\%\) at \(\alpha=0.5\). For A2, the reported MAPE is \(17.5\%\) at several \(\alpha\) values, and for A4 it is above \(11\%\) [PAGE 8]. This shows that group models can fail when player-specific scaling or behavior differs. Counterfactual veto: if a new player or an atypical player is assigned to a positional group model, the group slope may not represent that player’s within-player dynamics. Verifiable prediction: the new player’s test MAPE will exceed the individual-model baseline and may approach or exceed the high group-model errors seen for A2. The paper explicitly warns of Simpson’s paradox: group relationships can contradict individual relationships [PAGE 13]. Therefore, the group model should be vetoed whenever individual data are sufficient to test the player-specific relationship.

Additional RLVR audit: match-lag model veto.  
The paper finds that match metrics can predict later match metrics, with one example achieving \(R^2=0.84\) for player A2 [PAGE 10]. However, the authors say such models have little practical utility because match physical performance is not subject to systemic control [PAGE 10]. Counterfactual veto: if the previous match was against a weak opponent, in a game state that required little defensive running, or with an early substitution, the next match’s expected output may not follow the same relationship. Verifiable prediction: if the next match has a different opponent strength or tactical role, the realized output will diverge from the match-lag prediction. The model should be used only as a descriptive marker of deterministic persistence, not as a planning target.

Final RLVR decision rule.  
A model should influence training only if it survives three counterfactual checks: measurement validity, physical availability, and contextual transportability. If any check fails, the model’s output should be treated as a hypothesis, not a prescription. This aligns with the source’s own conclusion: the models are useful for identifying which training patterns contribute to extreme match performance, but they are not yet reliable autonomous forecasts [PAGE 14].
