<THINK_TRACE>
The drive source is a computer-vision dataset and baseline paper, not an event-pricing document, so the reasoning must stay at the level of measurement validity, tracking failure modes, and verifiable metric behavior. The core multi-hop chain is: fixed-camera 3x3 video enters a detector; detections become tracklets; tracklets are converted to court coordinates by homography; non-player candidates are vetoed by court-geometry rules; fragmented tracklets are merged by an ID-switch heuristic; jersey number and color histogram resolve identity on the Outdoor subset; pose models estimate 10 keypoints; and the resulting claims are scored by TI-HOTA, TI-DetA, TI-AssA, PDJ, and AUC. A gray-zone audit shows why the paper’s headline numbers cannot be read as “end-to-end game-state reconstruction”: the authors explicitly say “court localization is performed manually and others can be automatically done” [PAGE 5], and the Track-ID task is “a simplified version of the game state reconstruction (GSR) task [64], excluding field detection to target fixed-camera scenarios” [PAGE 2]. The Indoor subset has field information, the Outdoor subset has field information, but the Drone subset does not: “slight movements occurred due to wind and other environmental influences, and thus ground truth field information was not annotated” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, any inference that treats Drone TI-HOTA as a field-registration score is overreach; the paper says “the Drone dataset is used exclusively for tracking” [PAGE 4]. The second gray zone is identification. Indoor players lack bib numbers, so the baseline infers team and initial position from opening-frame geometry; Outdoor players have bib numbers, so the baseline uses tracklet-level jersey-number recognition plus color histograms. This means the two subsets do not share the same identification operator, and their TI-HOTA values are not directly comparable as if they were the same task. The third gray zone is occlusion. The paper reports “the maximum number of detections required in each frame is determined to be 6” [PAGE 5], but occlusion can make hidden and foreground boxes nearly identical; the authors warn that “tracking methods based on Tracking by Detection, such as CAMELTrack, may actually increase mis-tracking during occlusion when using detectors with excessively high detection power” [PAGE 8]. That is a counter-trap: better detection recall can worsen association if high-IoU hidden-player boxes create ambiguous matches. The fourth gray zone is time. Indoor clips are short, Outdoor clips are longer, and an ID exchange poisons identity for the remainder: “if ID exchange between players occurs in tracking (see Figure 3), the attributes will no longer be correct from then on, and the match between the prediction and the ground truth will fail for a large part of the video clip” [PAGE 7]. Thus the Indoor-versus-Outdoor gap is partly a clock effect, not only a scene-difficulty effect. The fifth gray zone is pose. The pose models are evaluated without fine-tuning, on only 10 selected keypoints mapped from 17 COCO keypoints; the paper reports that “elbows and wrists in the upper limbs demonstrate weaker performance, probably due to occlusion and motion blur” [PAGE 8]. The Indoor subset has the lowest pose accuracy, attributed to lower resolution: “The lower performance observed in the Indoor dataset can be primarily attributed to its lower image resolution (1280 × 720) compared to the Outdoor and Drone datasets (3820 × 2160)” [PAGE 8]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines the Track-ID detection object at frame \(t\) as a player detection \(d_i^t\) with \(i\in\{1,2,\ldots,6\}\), and the task is to predict localization plus identification [PAGE 5]. For Indoor,
\[
d_i^t=\{\underbrace{\mathrm{court\_x},\mathrm{court\_y}}_{\mathrm{localization}},\underbrace{\mathrm{team},\mathrm{initial\_position}}_{\mathrm{identification}}\},
\]
where \(\mathrm{team}\in\{\mathrm{offense},\mathrm{defense}\}\) and \(\mathrm{initial\_position}\in\{\mathrm{top},\mathrm{left},\mathrm{right}\}\) [PAGE 5]. For Outdoor,
\[
d_i^t=\{\underbrace{\mathrm{court\_x},\mathrm{court\_y}}_{\mathrm{localization}},\underbrace{\mathrm{team},\mathrm{jersey\_number}}_{\mathrm{identification}}\},
\]
with jersey number replacing initial position [PAGE 5]. Court coordinates are in metres after homography; frame index \(t\) is an integer count; video duration is in seconds. The homography operator is
\[
(x,y,1)^{\top}\sim H\,(u,v,1)^{\top},
\]
where \((u,v)\) are image pixels, \((x,y)\) are court metres, and \(H\) is estimated from manually annotated court keypoints [PAGE 6]. The breakdown regime is standard: if the keypoint set is rank-deficient or collinear, \(H\) is singular and court coordinates are undefined.

The paper’s Track-ID metric is “TI-HOTA,” stated as equivalent to GS-HOTA but with manual/automatic court-localization and identification differences [PAGE 5]. The exact equations are:
\[
\mathrm{TI\!-\!HOTA}
=\frac{1}{19}\sum_{\alpha\in\{0.05,0.10,\ldots,0.95\}}
\sqrt{\mathrm{TI\!-\!DetA}_{\alpha}\times \mathrm{TI\!-\!AssA}_{\alpha}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{TI\!-\!DetA}_{\alpha}
=\frac{|TP_{\alpha}|}{|TP_{\alpha}|+|FP_{\alpha}|+|FN_{\alpha}|},
\]
\[
\mathrm{TI\!-\!AssA}_{\alpha}
=\frac{1}{|TP_{\alpha}|}\sum_{c\in TP_{\alpha}}A_{\alpha}(c),
\]
\[
A_{\alpha}(c)=
\frac{|TPA_{\alpha}(c)|}
{|TPA_{\alpha}(c)|+|FPA_{\alpha}(c)|+|FNA_{\alpha}(c)|}.
\]
These are dimensionless fractions in \([0,1]\), reported in percent [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. The similarity gate is
\[
\mathrm{Sim}_{\mathrm{TI\!-\!HOTA}}(P,G)
=\mathrm{LocSim}(P,G)\times \mathrm{IdSim}(P,G),
\]
\[
\mathrm{LocSim}(P,G)
=\exp\!\left(\ln(0.05)\frac{\lVert P-G\rVert_2^2}{\tau^2}\right),
\]
\[
\mathrm{IdSim}(P,G)=
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if all attributes match},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Here \(P,G\) are predicted and ground-truth court positions in metres, and \(\tau\) is a distance tolerance in metres; the experiments use \(\tau=1\) [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. Exact breakdown regimes: if \(P=G\), \(\mathrm{LocSim}=1\); if \(\lVert P-G\rVert_2=\tau\), \(\mathrm{LocSim}=0.05\); if \(\lVert P-G\rVert_2\to\infty\), \(\mathrm{LocSim}\to0\); if \(\tau\to0\) with \(P\neq G\), \(\mathrm{LocSim}\to0\). The multiplicative \(\mathrm{IdSim}\) is an exact veto: one wrong team or jersey attribute makes the whole similarity zero even when localization is perfect. TI-AssA is undefined when \(|TP_{\alpha}|=0\), which is a real high-threshold sparsity regime.

The ID-switch merger in Algorithm 1 uses frame-count cost, not a toy ratio: for a new tracklet ID and an original ID,
\[
\mathrm{overlap}=|\mathrm{framesOrig}\cap\mathrm{framesNew}|,
\]
\[
\mathrm{missing}=F_{\max}-|\mathrm{framesOrig}\cup\mathrm{framesNew}|,
\]
\[
\mathrm{cost}=\mathrm{overlap}+\mathrm{missing},
\]
and the best original ID is the one with minimum cost, subject to \(\mathrm{overlap}\geq T_{\mathrm{overlap}}\) [PAGE 6]. Units are frames; \(T_{\mathrm{overlap}}\) is set to 10 frames for Indoor and 50 frames for Outdoor [PAGE 6]. The pose metric is PDJ: “a keypoint is deemed correctly detected if the normalized distance between the predicted and ground truth keypoints is less than 0.5” [PAGE 7]. Formally, with \(K=10\) annotated keypoints,
\[
\mathrm{PDJ}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\mathbf{1}\!\left(
\frac{\lVert \hat{k}_k-k_k\rVert_2}{L_{\mathrm{torso}}}<0.5
\right),
\]
where \(L_{\mathrm{torso}}\) is the Euclidean distance between the shoulder midpoint and the hip midpoint [PAGE 7]. PDJ is dimensionless; AUC is the area under the PDJ curve as the threshold varies from 0 to 0.5 in 0.01 steps, with maximum 0.5 [PAGE 8]. PDJ is undefined if \(L_{\mathrm{torso}}=0\), which can occur under degenerate boxes or severe occlusion.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is tracking-by-detection plus game-state reconstruction plus top-down pose estimation. The paper places modern MOT in a line from SORT and BoT-SORT, which “employ Kalman filters and data association techniques,” through DeepSORT and Deep-EIoU appearance features, to CAMELTrack, which “incorporates an association method based on a learnable module rather than the heuristic associations” [PAGE 3]. It also places GSR in the SoccerNet lineage: the Track-ID task is a simplified GSR variant excluding field detection [PAGE 2], and TI-HOTA is “essentially equivalent to GS-HOTA” but renamed for the task difference [PAGE 5]. The metric genealogy runs through HOTA: the paper states that GS-HOTA improves the HOTA similarity score for matching predictions and ground truth [PAGE 5]. The pose genealogy runs from DeepPose, which “pioneered this approach,” to HRNet, which preserves high-resolution representations, to RTMPose and SwinPose, which use transformer-based architectures [PAGE 3]. The dataset genealogy is a gap-filling move: existing datasets are dominated by soccer and 5-on-5 basketball, while TrackID3x3 is described as “the first publicly available comprehensive dataset specifically designed for multi-player tracking, player identification, and pose estimation in 3x3 basketball scenarios” [PAGE 1]. The retrocausal element is visible in Algorithm 1: a tracklet ID that appears midway is relabeled to an earlier original ID, and “all rows where tracklet_id = newID” are relabeled to “tracklet_id = bestID” [PAGE 6]. The future tracklet thus rewrites the identity of later rows based on overlap and missing-frame cost. Multi-week traps follow from this lineage: annotation drift because roughly half the Outdoor and Drone boxes were manually annotated and the rest were tracking outputs manually corrected [PAGE 4]; ID-switch propagation because a single exchange makes attributes wrong “from then on” [PAGE 7]; detector-power counter-trap because excessive detection power can create high-IoU hidden/foreground pairs [PAGE 8]; and domain shift across Indoor, Outdoor, and Drone, where wind prevents Drone field annotation and only tracking is evaluated [PAGE 4].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three nested beliefs: the player’s belief about role and spacing, the tracker’s belief about which detection belongs to which identity, and the evaluator’s belief about whether the metric actually measures identification. Crisis habit regression appears when ambiguity rises: the baseline falls back to rule-based court exclusion, tracklet continuity, ID-switch merging, linear interpolation, end-point extrapolation, and a six-detection cap [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. These are habits, not learned re-identification; the paper admits the merger “requires all players to be constantly visible on camera” [PAGE 6]. The counter-trap is that the obvious fix—stronger detection—can worsen identity: “tracking methods based on Tracking by Detection, such as CAMELTrack, may actually increase mis-tracking during occlusion when using detectors with excessively high detection power” [PAGE 8]. The baseline deliberately reduces detection power, but the trap remains because lower power can miss players. The clock is the third layer: Indoor clips average \(8.98\pm3.24\) s and Outdoor clips \(40.11\pm34.23\) s [PAGE 7]; a 3x3 game is limited to 5 minutes without timeouts [PAGE 4]. After an ID exchange, the evaluator’s identification belief is wrong for the remaining frames, so longer clips expose the failure more severely. The paper states the Indoor advantage is explained “to some extent by the shorter video length and the easier identification of attributes” [PAGE 7]. Thus the recursive failure mode is: short clocks hide identity regressions; long clocks amplify them; high detection power creates the occlusion ambiguity that triggers regression; and heuristic habits restore continuity only under the assumption that all players remain visible.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
