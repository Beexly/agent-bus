This audit reconstructs the paper’s HTA mechanism as a causal online assignment problem, then stress-tests where its statistical correction can legitimately fire and where it must regress to distance-only behavior. The first hop is the association cost. For detection \(i\) at frame \(t\), with normalized appearance feature \(\mathbf f_t^i \in \mathbb R^\ell\), and track \(j\) with historical features \(T_t^j=\{\mathbf f_s^j\}\), the paper defines \(d_t^{i,j}=F(\mathbf f_t^i,T_t^j)\). The baseline is a current-frame appearance distance, for example minimum cosine distance. The paper states that “most existing association methods only consider the appearance feature distances calculated at the current frame and ignore the historical appearance feature distance records” [PAGE 2]. This is the first gray zone: current-frame costs are low-variance but memoryless; historical costs are higher-variance but can disambiguate close competitors.

The second hop is the ambiguity example. The paper’s Fig. 1 shows a detection whose raw distance to Track 1 is smaller than to Track 2, but whose historical likelihood under Track 2 is higher. The caption states: “The distance-based assignment will wrongly assign detection i to Track 1” [PAGE 3], and the likelihood comparison is “the likelihood of di,2 t belonging to the distance distribution of Track 2 is higher than that of di,1 t belonging to the distance distribution of Track 1” [PAGE 3]. That is the core motivation: a distance-only Hungarian solve is a point estimate; the historical distribution supplies a likelihood ratio over tracks. The correction is not guaranteed, because the historical distribution itself is contaminated by occlusions and inaccurate detections.

The third hop is the distributional transform. The paper models cosine or squared Euclidean distance between unit-norm features as chi-square with \(\ell\) degrees of freedom, then says “we take the fourth root of the chi-square random variable to approximately transform the chi-square distribution to a normal distribution” [PAGE 3]. This is a modeling compromise: the fourth-root transform makes IGMM fitting more plausible, but it is not an exact normalizing transform for all \(\ell\) and all tails. The gray-zone risk is a tail mismatch: rare large distances can be either true outliers or legitimate appearance changes, and the transform changes their effective influence.

The fourth hop is incremental estimation. The IGMM creates a component when no component exists or when the Mahalanobis update criterion is not met, updates existing components by posterior-weighted sufficient statistics, and removes a component when “\(v_{k,n} \gt v_{\min}\)” and “\(N_{k,n} \lt N_{\min}\)” [PAGE 4]. The paper fixes “\(K_{\max}=5\)” and “\(v_{\min}=5\) and \(N_{\min}=3\)” [PAGE 4]. This is a bounded-memory robust estimator, not a full Bayesian nonparametric model. The gray zone is that component pruning can remove a true mode if that mode is rarely revisited, especially after a long occlusion or a pose change.

The fifth hop is outlier suppression and cost fusion. The paper sorts components by mean distance and selects the first \(M\) components covering at least \(\Upsilon=0.8\) of the mixture; this treats at most \(1-\Upsilon\) of historical data as outliers. It then forms the hybrid cost in Eq. (8), with “a large weighting factor \(\lambda=0.9\)” [PAGE 5] because “the probability-based term is not as discriminative as the distance-based term because the IGMM is only the statistical summary of the historical distances” [PAGE 5]. HTA is only estimated when the track is long enough: “we set a minimum track length \(L=15\) to ensure that an IGMM will be estimated only for the tracks with a length no less than \(L\)” [PAGE 4]. Below \(L\), HTA deliberately regresses to the distance-only habit. That is an explicit clock gate, not a bug.

The sixth hop is the assignment. The cost matrix is solved by Hungarian assignment under a permissible maximum distance; the paper reports \(d_{\max}=0.2\) in the experiments [PAGE 6]. The gray-zone audit must distinguish three regimes: (a) all costs above \(d_{\max}\), where no admissible assignment exists and the detector should start or retain a tentative track; (b) ambiguous costs below \(d_{\max}\), where the IGMM likelihood can change the argmin; and (c) distribution-shift costs, where the IGMM is stale and the likelihood term can be actively misleading. The paper’s own results show this non-monotonicity: “HTA may give rise to more frequent ID switching” [PAGE 7], while also correcting persistent errors because “the integration of the statistical information of historical records gives a chance to quickly correct the wrong ID assignment at frame 285” [PAGE 7]. Therefore the correct interpretation is not “HTA dominates every tracker on every identity metric”; it is “HTA improves identification F1 on average at a small speed cost, with a possible ID-switch trade-off.”

The final audit point is benchmark and speed evidence. HTA reports MOT15 IDF1 \(67.7\%\), MOT16 \(62.7\%\), and MOT17 \(62.5\%\) in Table 1 [PAGE 6], and the authors conclude that HTA “achieves better or comparable performance than many other state-of-the-art online trackers in terms of the balance between tracking speed and quality” [PAGE 8]. But HTA is “about \(1\sim3\) FPS slower” than CMS and EMA [PAGE 7]. The paper also avoids benchmark leakage by stating “we do not train on the training sets of the MOT benchmarks nor train on any dataset” that overlaps [PAGE 6]. That is a strong generality claim, but the evidence is still benchmark-bound. The gray-zone conclusion: HTA is a statistically motivated disambiguation layer for online MOT, valid when the track history is long enough, the mixture is not overwhelmed by outliers, and the appearance model’s distance distribution is approximately stationary after the fourth-root transform. Outside those conditions, it must be vetoed or fall back to motion and current-frame distance.

\[
d_t^{i,j}=\min_{s}\left(1-\frac{\mathbf f_t^i\cdot \mathbf f_s^j}{\|\mathbf f_t^i\|_2\|\mathbf f_s^j\|_2}\right)\in[0,2].
\]
The paper notes that cosine distance and squared Euclidean distance are equivalent for unit-norm vectors [PAGE 3]. If \(d\) is interpreted as a squared Euclidean distance after normalization, it is also dimensionless in normalized feature units. The historical record for track \(j\) is \(D=\{d_n\}\), where \(d_n\) is the fourth root of the distance between the track and a detection assigned to it. The transformed variable \(x_n=d_n^{1/4}\) is also dimensionless because \(d_n\) is dimensionless; the Gaussian density \(p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)\) therefore has inverse-\(x\) units, but in normalized feature coordinates it is reported as a dimensionless statistical density.

The IGMM is
\[
p(d_n\mid\Theta_n)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_n}\pi_{k,n}\,p(d_n\mid\mu_{k,n},\sigma^2_{k,n}),
\]
with
\[
p(d_n\mid\mu_{k,n},\sigma^2_{k,n})
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2_{k,n}}}
\exp\left(-\frac{(d_n-\mu_{k,n})^2}{2\sigma^2_{k,n}}\right),
\]
where \(\pi_{k,n}\) is dimensionless, \(\sum_{k=1}^{K_n}\pi_{k,n}=1\), and \(\mu_{k,n}\), \(\sigma^2_{k,n}\) are in fourth-root distance units and squared fourth-root distance units, respectively. The posterior responsibility is
\[
p(z_k=1\mid d_n)=
\frac{\pi_{k,n}p(d_n\mid\mu_{k,n},\sigma^2_{k,n})}

\[
v_{k,n+1}=1,\quad N_{k,n+1}=1,\quad \mu_{k,n+1}=d_n,\quad
K_{n+1}\leftarrow K_n+1,\quad
\sigma^2_{k,n+1}=\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ini}},\quad
\pi_{k,n+1}=\frac{1}{\sum_{m=1}^{K_{n+1}}N_{m,n+1}},
\]
with “\(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ini}}\)” user-specified [PAGE 4]. The update rule is
\[
v_{k,n+1}=v_{k,n}+1,\quad
N_{k,n+1}=N_{k,n}+p(z_k=1\mid d_n),
\]
\[
\mu_{k,n+1}=\mu_{k,n}+\xi_{k,n}(d_n-\mu_{k,n}),
\]
\[
\sigma^2_{k,n+1}
=\sigma^2_{k,n}

-\omega^2_{k,n}(d_n-\mu_{k,n})^2,

\[
\pi_{k,n+1}=\frac{N_{k,n+1}}{\sum_{m=1}^{K_n}N_{m,n+1}},
\quad
\xi_{k,n}=\frac{p(z_k=1\mid d_n)}{N_{k,n+1}}.
\]
The paper fixes “\(K_{\max}=5\)” and “\(v_{\min}=5\) and \(N_{\min}=3\)” [PAGE 4]. A component is removed when \(v_{k,n}\gt v_{\min}\) and \(N_{k,n}\lt N_{\min}\). The selected outlier-free mixture order is
\[
M=\arg\min_m\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_{k,n}\gt \Upsilon\right),
\]
with \(\Upsilon=0.8\) in the experiments. The hybrid cost is
\[
C_t=\lambda d_t+(1-\lambda)

{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\pi_{k,n}},
\]
where \(C_t\) is dimensionless, \(\lambda\in[0,1]\), and the paper uses \(\lambda=0.9\) [PAGE 5]. The assignment is
\[
A_t=\arg\min_{A\in\mathcal A_t}\sum_{(i,j)\in A}C_t^{i,j},
\]
subject to the permissible distance gate. The paper reports \(d_{\max}=0.2\) [PAGE 6], but the excerpt does not fully specify whether this gate is applied to the raw \(d_t\) term or the fused \(C_t\); that is a reproducibility gray zone.

Breakdown and singularity regimes. First, \(\sigma^2_{k,n}\to 0\) makes the Gaussian density in Eq. (2) singular at \(x=\mu_{k,n}\); the implementation’s \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ini}}\) and update equations regularize this, but exact zero variance is undefined. Second, \(K_n=0\) is handled only by the creation procedure; a numerical implementation that evaluates Eq. (3) before the first component exists has a zero-denominator failure. Third, if the mixture weights underflow or a component is removed at \(K_{\max}\), the denominator of Eq. Fourth, the fourth-root transform is an approximation, not an exact normalizer; heavy tails or small \(\ell\) can produce residual non-normality and bias the IGMM. Fifth, \(L=15\) creates a hard regime boundary: below \(L\), HTA is distance-only; above \(L\), the statistical term suddenly enters. Sixth, \(\lambda=1\) removes the probability term entirely, while \(\lambda=0\) removes the distance term; the paper reports peak performance at \(\lambda=0.9\), so both endpoints are degenerate special cases. Seventh, \(d_{\max}=0.2\) is a hard rejection gate; if every candidate exceeds it, the assignment problem has no admissible match and the tracker must create or defer tracks. Eighth, \(K_{\max}=5\), \(v_{\min}=5\), \(N_{\min}=3\), and \(\Upsilon=0.8\) define a robust but non-universal model: a true mode that is rarely revisited can be pruned, and a persistent outlier that survives pruning can dominate a low-weight true mode. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a sequence of increasingly expensive memory operators. SORT used only IoU between Kalman-predicted boxes and current detections; the paper says SORT “only uses the Intersection over Union (IoU) distance” and “shows poor identification performance in scenes with moderate occlusions” [PAGE 2]. DeepSORT added a CNN appearance model and cascade matching; the paper describes “the cascade matching strategy (CMS)” as giving higher priority to recently seen tracks [PAGE 2]. EMA then introduced a smoothed feature by exponential moving average over temporally adjacent detections [PAGE 2], and HTA adds a distribution over historical appearance distances rather than a single smoothed vector. The lineage is therefore: motion-only gating, appearance-only matching, temporal smoothing, and finally distributional memory.

The distributional memory has its own genealogy. HTA explicitly borrows the background-modeling analogy: “if we consider the historical appearance distances of a track as pixel ‘values’ appearing at a ‘track location’ over time, modeling the historical distances of a track is equivalent to modeling the background pixel values at a pixel location” [PAGE 3]. That analogy descends from Stauffer and Grimson’s adaptive background mixture model and Pinto and Engel’s fast incremental Gaussian mixture model. The chi-square-to-normal transform descends from Hawkins and Wixley’s note on transforming chi-squared variables to normality [PAGE 3]. The assignment layer descends from the Hungarian algorithm applied to a cost matrix. The evaluation layer descends from MOT15, MOT16, and MOT17 metrics: IDF1, MOTA, MOTP, MT, ML, Frag, FP, FN, and IDS [PAGE 5]. HTA is thus a hybrid of classical robust statistics, online background modeling, deep appearance embedding, and combinatorial assignment.

The longitudinal traps are where the genealogy matters. The first trap is benchmark leakage. The paper explicitly avoids training on MOT training sets and states “we do not train on the training sets of the MOT benchmarks nor train on any dataset” that overlaps [PAGE 6], arguing that this “prevents the detector from being biased to the MOT benchmarks” [PAGE 6]. That is a generality claim, but it also means HTA’s numbers are not directly comparable to trackers that train on MOT benchmarks. The paper itself notes that JDE-1088 and FairMOT share feature maps and are faster, but both are trained on MOT training sets; it also reports “a large performance gap” for FairMOT on out-of-domain YouTube videos [PAGE 7]. A multi-week evaluation that mixes in-domain and out-of-domain sequences would therefore be more informative than a single benchmark table.

The second trap is the speed-accuracy frontier. HTA is not free: it is “about \(1\sim3\) FPS slower” than CMS and EMA [PAGE 7], and many high-accuracy trackers with private detectors process fewer than 8 FPS [PAGE 7]. A longitudinal deployment test must therefore measure not only IDF1 but also latency variance and worst-case frame time, because the IGMM update is per track and per frame.

The third trap is identity-switch non-monotonicity. HTA improves IDF1, but “HTA may give rise to more frequent ID switching” [PAGE 7]. The qualitative example is instructive: EMA has fewer switches but a wrong ID at frame 432 persists to the end, whereas HTA can correct a wrong ID at frame 285 [PAGE 7]. A multi-week audit should therefore track IDS, Frag, and IDF1 jointly; optimizing IDF1 alone can hide a higher switch rate, while optimizing IDS alone can hide persistent identity corruption.

The fourth trap is parameter drift. The paper tunes \(L=15\) and \(\lambda=0.9\) on MOT15 training sets and reports that performance “improves until the minimum track length \(L\) reaches 15 and trends downward as \(L\) continues to increase” [PAGE 7]. That is a local optimum, not a universal constant. A different detector, frame rate, or crowd density changes the historical-distance distribution and therefore the optimal \(L\), \(\lambda\), \(\Upsilon\), and component-pruning thresholds.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the tracker as an agent that maintains a recursive belief about identity: “track \(j\) is the same target as detection \(i\)” is not a single boolean but a posterior over the track’s own historical residual distribution. In that framing, three failure modes dominate.

Crisis habit regression. Under occlusion, missed detection, or appearance change, the baseline habit is “smallest current distance wins.” HTA is designed to override that habit only when the track has at least \(L=15\) observations and a reliable IGMM. The paper is explicit that “for the tracks with a length less than \(L\), we only use the appearance distance as the association cost” [PAGE 4]. That is a deliberate regression to habit under crisis. The audit question is whether the regression is triggered by the right signal. A short track after a long occlusion may have many historical distances but few recent ones; a long track after a scene cut may have a stale IGMM. The paper’s component-pruning rules are the only defense against stale memory, and they are not a scene-change detector.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is the opposite error: the tracker becomes overconfident in its own historical distribution. If a target changes pose, illumination, or clothing, or if occlusion injects a burst of high-distance outliers, the IGMM can encode the wrong mode. The paper tries to prevent this by sorting components by mean and keeping the first \(M\) components covering \(\Upsilon=0.8\) of the mixture, treating at most 20% as outliers. But if the true appearance change persists, it is not an outlier; it is a new mode, and a high \(\Upsilon\) may admit it while a low \(\Upsilon\) may suppress it. The paper acknowledges that a high \(\Upsilon\) “could results in a multi-modal IGMM, which allows for modeling more dynamic distributions, e.g. caused by scenes where tracked targets change pose constantly” [PAGE 4]. The counter-trap is therefore parameter-dependent: the same historical memory that corrects ambiguity can entrench a wrong identity.

Clock. The clock is the online causal constraint. HTA cannot wait for future frames; it must decide at frame \(t\) with information up to \(t\). The \(L=15\) gate is a clock gate, and the FPS budget is a real-time clock. The paper reports that HTA is “about \(1\sim3\) FPS slower” than CMS and EMA [PAGE 7], so the statistical correction consumes time exactly when the system may be under load. A recursive theory-of-mind audit should therefore ask whether the tracker’s belief revision is worth the latency: if the IGMM only changes the assignment in a small fraction of ambiguous frames, the average IDF1 gain may be real but the worst-case deadline risk may be unacceptable. The paper’s own results support a nuanced conclusion: HTA improves identification F1 consistently across MOT15, MOT16, and MOT17 [PAGE 6], but it does not dominate every identity metric and it pays a speed cost. The correct mental model is a bounded-rationality tracker: it uses historical statistics as a prior, but it must veto that prior when the clock, the track length, or the outlier regime makes the prior unreliable.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is an admissibility test that sits above the HTA cost. Let \(\mathcal A_t\) be all candidate detection-to-track pairs. Define three gates: physical visibility, biomechanical motion, and weather or image-quality reliability. A pair is admissible only if all three gates pass; otherwise its cost is set to infinity. This is conservative: it can increase false negatives or fragmentations, but it cannot create a false positive association from a pair that violates a necessary physical condition. The paper supplies direct evidence for the physical gate: outliers in tracks are “typically caused by occlusions or inaccurate detections” [PAGE 4]. The paper also uses a detector score threshold, with 0.3 for MOT16 and MOT17 and 0.7 for MOT15 due to higher false positives [PAGE 6]. That threshold is a physical/detection-quality veto: a detection below threshold should not be allowed to drive an identity decision merely because its appearance distance is small.

The biomechanical gate uses the Kalman motion model that DeepSORT already contains. The paper describes the tracker as having “a motion estimation model based on Kalman filtering” [PAGE 5]. For a detection measurement \(\mathbf z_i\) and track state \(\mathbf x_j\), define the Mahalanobis motion residual
\[
g_{ij}=(\mathbf z_i-\mathbf H\mathbf x_j)^{\top}\mathbf S_j^{-1}(\mathbf z_i-\mathbf H\mathbf x_j),
\]
where \(\mathbf z_i\) and \(\mathbf H\mathbf x_j\) are in pixels, \(\mathbf S_j\) is in pixels squared, and \(g_{ij}\) is dimensionless. A sound biomechanical veto rejects the pair when \(g_{ij}\gt \chi^2_{m,1-\alpha}\), where \(m\) is the measurement dimension and \(\alpha\) is the allowed false-veto rate. The paper does not report this threshold in the excerpt, so the audit treats it as an external calibration parameter, not as a claimed result. The important point is structural: if a detection is physically incompatible with the track’s motion, no historical appearance likelihood should override the veto. Otherwise HTA can produce a high-confidence identity swap across two pedestrians who cross paths.

The weather or image-quality gate is outside the paper’s validated evidence, so it must be labeled external. Appearance models are sensitive to rain, snow, glare, motion blur, and low illumination; these degrade the feature vector and inflate the historical distance distribution. A weather veto can be defined by an image-quality statistic \(Q_t\), such as contrast-to-noise ratio or blur kernel energy, with a threshold \(Q_{\min}\). If \(Q_t\lt Q_{\min}\), appearance-based costs are down-weighted or disabled, and the tracker falls back to motion and recent detections. The proof obligation is not that HTA fails in bad weather; it is that HTA’s likelihood term is only valid when the appearance-distance distribution is approximately stationary. Weather breaks that stationarity assumption. Therefore the weather gate is a necessary-condition veto, not a performance tweak.

Adjudication rule: when HTA’s fused cost prefers pair \((i,j)\) but any cross-layer gate vetoes \((i,j)\), the veto wins. If all candidates for detection \(i\) are vetoed, the tracker should create a new tentative track or leave the detection unassigned. This prevents the most damaging error mode, a confident identity swap under occlusion. The cost of the veto is over-fragmentation: an overly strict motion gate can reject a correct re-identification after a long occlusion, and an overly strict weather gate can discard appearance information when it is still useful. The veto is therefore sound only if its thresholds are calibrated on a held-out set that includes occlusion, crossing, and low-quality imagery. The paper’s own qualitative example shows why this matters: HTA can correct a wrong ID, but it can also “give rise to more frequent ID switching” [PAGE 7]. A cross-layer veto is the mechanism that keeps the statistical correction from becoming a statistical overreach.
