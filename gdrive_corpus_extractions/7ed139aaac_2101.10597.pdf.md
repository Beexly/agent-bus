<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s derivation chain is multi-hop and must be audited at each hop. First, observed goals are treated as counts: for match \(m\), \(Y_{ijm}\) and \(Y_{jim}\) are the goals scored by teams \(i\) and \(j\). The source models their joint distribution with a bivariate Poisson law whose means are \(\lambda_{ijm}\) and \(\lambda_{jim}\) and whose interaction is \(\lambda_C\) [PAGE 5]. Second, the means are linked log-linearly to team strengths and a home effect: \(\log(\lambda_{ijm})=\beta_0+(r_i-r_j)+h\,I(\text{team }i\text{ home})\) [PAGE 5]. Third, the strengths are identified only up to an additive constant, so the paper imposes \(\sum_{i=1}^n r_i=0\) [PAGE 5]. Fourth, the likelihood is maximized over played matches, producing current-season strength estimates. Fifth, the fitted parameters generate 100,000 simulations of the remaining fixtures, and league rules are applied to each simulated end-of-season table [PAGE 5–6]. Sixth, the simulation output is a rank-probability matrix \(p_{t,r}\), not a single deterministic table. Seventh, model quality is scored at match level by RPS and at tournament level by TRPS [PAGE 8–9]. Eighth, decision rules—champion threshold \(C\), expected-rank ordering, and compensation transfers—are layered on top [PAGE 16–17].

The gray-zone audit begins with the earliest matchdays. The paper is explicit that the PFSC is “relatively bad in the beginning of the season” [PAGE 10] because it uses only current-season matches. Its convergence is empirical: “in most cases the PFSC converges towards the performance of the benchmark methods after around 10 match days, and in all but one of fifteen league-seasons it has caught up after 25 match days” [PAGE 10]. The exception is the 2017–2018 Italian Serie A, where “the PFSC leads to worse predictions than the benchmarks throughout” [PAGE 10]. This is not a cosmetic caveat: it is a breakdown regime for any decision rule that treats PFSC as universally superior. A second gray zone is nonstationarity. The model assumes constant strengths and a constant home effect over the fitted window, but the paper deliberately avoids evaluating resumed 2019–2020 competitions because changed conditions “could influence team performances, by lowering the effect of the home advantage” [PAGE 3–4]. A third gray zone is the set of remaining fixtures. The simulation is only as valid as the encoded fixture list and league rules; if matches are cancelled, moved to neutral venues, or if tie-breakers are omitted, the rank probabilities are functionals of the wrong event space. A fourth gray zone is benchmark construction. The plus-minus model simulates starting elevens with a 10% injury/inadmissibility chance and then generates goals coarsely—losing teams 0, drawing teams 1, winning teams randomly 1 to 3 [PAGE 8]. That may be adequate for win/draw/loss probabilities but weak when goal difference drives ranking. A fifth gray zone is metric behavior near season end: “When nearing the end of the season, the RPS is calculated over few matches. Therefore, the mean RPS behaves erratically and sometimes increases sharply” [PAGE 10]. A sixth gray zone is decision-theoretic: expected rank is a compression of the full probability matrix, and the paper itself shows that Rennes and Lille are differently advantaged by a deterministic expected-rank assignment [PAGE 16–17]. Thus the audit conclusion is that PFSC is a probabilistic counterfactual engine, not a fairness oracle; it must be phase-gated by matchday count, conditioned on the actual remaining schedule, and paired with compensation when a deterministic table is forced.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core source operator is the bivariate Poisson goal model. For \(x,y\in\mathbb{N}_0\) goals, the paper states the joint probability mass function as
\[
P(Y_{ijm}=x,Y_{jim}=y)=
\frac{\lambda_{ijm}^{x}\lambda_{jim}^{y}}{x!\,y!}
e^{-(\lambda_{ijm}+\lambda_{jim}+\lambda_C)}
\sum_{k=0}^{\min(x,y)}
\binom{x}{k}\binom{y}{k}k!
\left(\frac{\lambda_C}{\lambda_{ijm}\lambda_{jim}}\right)^k,
\]
with \(\lambda_{ijm},\lambda_{jim}\ge 0\) expected goals per match and \(\lambda_C\ge 0\) the covariance/interaction parameter [PAGE 5]. Units: \(x,y\) are goal counts; \(\lambda_{ijm},\lambda_{jim}\) are expected goals per match; \(\lambda_C\) is in goals squared because it is the covariance of the two goal counts; the probability is dimensionless. The source says: “\(\lambda_C \ge 0\) is a covariance parameter representing the interaction between both teams. This parameter is kept constant over all matches” [PAGE 5]. The means satisfy
\[
\log(\lambda_{ijm})=\beta_0+(r_i-r_j)+h\cdot I(\text{team }i\text{ playing at home}),
\]
where \(\beta_0\) is a real intercept, \(r_i,r_j\) are real-valued team strengths on a log scale, \(h\) is the real home-effect parameter, and \(I(\cdot)\) is an indicator [PAGE 5]. Units: \(\beta_0,h,r_i\) are dimensionless log-scale quantities; \(e^{\beta_0}\) is expected goals for equal teams at a neutral venue. The identification constraint is
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i=0,
\]
as stated: “The strengths \(r_1,\ldots,r_n\) can take both positive and negative real values and are subject to the identification constraint \(\sum_{i=1}^n r_i=0\)” [PAGE 5]. Over \(M\) matches, the likelihood is
\[
L=\prod_{m=1}^{M}P(Y_{ijm}=y_{ijm},Y_{jim}=y_{jim}),
\]
where \(y_{ijm},y_{jim}\) are observed goals [PAGE 5]. The likelihood is dimensionless. Estimation is by numerical maximum likelihood; the fitted parameters are then used to simulate the remaining matches 100,000 times and to average rank indicators into \(p_{t,r}\) [PAGE 5–6].

The first benchmark uses weighted maximum likelihood with time decay:
\[
L=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\left[P(Y_{ijm}=y_{ijm},Y_{jim}=y_{jim})\right]^{w_{\text{time},m}},
\qquad
w_{\text{time},m}(x_m)=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x_m/\text{Half period}},
\]
where \(x_m\) is days back and Half period is set to 390 days [PAGE 6]. Units: \(x_m\) and Half period are days; \(w_{\text{time},m}\) is dimensionless. The source notes that this “gives more importance to recent matches” and that the model “uses two years of past matches to estimate the team strengths” [PAGE 6]. The second benchmark is plus-minus player rating, defined by minimizing
\[
\sum_{s\in S}\left(\sum_{j\in P_h(s)}\beta_j-\sum_{j\in P_a(s)}\beta_j-g(s)\right)^2,
\]
where \(S\) is the set of constant-player segments, \(P_h(s)\) and \(P_a(s)\) are the home and away players on the pitch, \(g(s)\) is the home-perspective goal difference in the segment, and \(\beta_j\) is player \(j\)’s rating [PAGE 7]. Units: \(g(s)\) is goals; \(\beta_j\) is goals per standardized segment after duration scaling. The source says the principle is “to find ratings such that the sum of the player ratings of the home team minus the sum of the player ratings of the away team is as close as possible to the observed goal difference” [PAGE 7]. The plus-minus model then uses an ordered logit regression for home win/draw/away win, simulates starting elevens with a 10% injury/inadmissibility chance, and generates winning-goal counts from 1 to 3 [PAGE 8].

The match-level scoring operator is RPS:
\[
\mathrm{RPS}=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}(o_j-x_j)\right)^2,
\]
where \(R\) is the number of ordered outcomes, \(o_j\in\{0,1\}\) is the empirical probability, and \(x_j\) is the forecast probability; RPS is dimensionless and lower is better [PAGE 8]. For \(R=3\), the paper simplifies it to
\[
\mathrm{RPS}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(o_1-x_1)^2+(o_3-x_3)^2\right],
\]
so a home win predicted as a draw is penalized less than a home win predicted as an away win [PAGE 9]. The tournament-level operator is TRPS:
\[
\mathrm{TRPS}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R-1}(O_{rt}-X_{rt})^2,
\]
where \(T\) is the number of teams, \(R\) the number of possible ranks, \(O_{rt}\) is the empirical cumulative indicator that team \(t\) reaches at least rank \(r\), and \(X_{rt}\) is the predicted cumulative probability [PAGE 9]. TRPS is dimensionless; perfect prediction gives 0. The expected-rank compression is
\[
E[\rho_t]=\sum_{r=1}^{R} r\,p_{t,r},
\]
and the compensation transfer for a team assigned deterministic rank \(\hat r_t\) is
\[
\Delta_t=P_{\hat r_t}-\sum_{r=1}^{R}p_{t,r}P_r,
\]
where \(P_r\) is the monetary profit associated with rank \(r\) [PAGE 16–17]. For Marseille the source gives
\[
\Delta_{\text{Marseille}}
=P_2-(0.77P_2+0.18P_3+0.05P_4)
=0.18(P_2-P_3)+0.05(P_2-P_4)
\]
[PAGE 16]. Units: \(P_r\) is money, so \(\Delta_t\) is money.

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are explicit. The model assumes independent matches, constant \(\lambda_C\), constant home effect \(h\), and constant team strengths over the fitted current season [PAGE 5–6]. If \(\lambda_C=0\), the model reduces to independent Poisson and is non-singular; if \(\lambda_C>0\) but \(\lambda_{ijm}\lambda_{jim}=0\), the term \(\lambda_C/(\lambda_{ijm}\lambda_{jim})\) is singular and the PMF must be evaluated by a limiting argument or the independent model. Without \(\sum_i r_i=0\), the likelihood is invariant under \(r_i\mapsto r_i+c\), producing a flat ridge and a singular Hessian in the all-ones direction. If the played-match graph is disconnected or \(M\) is too small, strength differences are weakly identified and PFSC variance explodes; the paper itself says a season stopped after “less than a third of the games played would certainly be declared void” [PAGE 18]. Nonstationarity—especially the COVID-era change that could lower home advantage—is a breakdown regime for stale \(h\) [PAGE 3–4]. Finally, if league tie-break rules or fixture sets are not encoded exactly, rank probabilities are correct for the wrong event space even when goal simulation is correct [PAGE 5–6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s own genealogy reaches back to the “problem of the points”: if a pre-determined series is interrupted at a score such as 5–2, how should the stake be divided? The source identifies Antoine Gombaud, the Chevalier de Méré, as posing this around 1654 to Pascal and Fermat, whose letters “established the basis of our modern probability theory” [PAGE 18]. That is the retrocausal root: a stopped season is not merely an incomplete table; it is a counterfactual completion problem. The modern lineage runs from Maher’s 1982 Poisson goal model to Karlis and Ntzoufras’s bivariate Poisson formulation, then to Ley et al. (2019) for current-strength maximum-likelihood rankings, Pantuso and Hvattum (2020) for plus-minus player ratings, and Ekstrøm et al. (2020) for TRPS [PAGE 4–9, 19–20]. The COVID-19 branch includes Guyon’s Elo-based approach, Lambers and Spieksma’s eigenvector approach, Csató’s generalized row sum and fairness criteria, and Gorgi et al.’s expected-points model [PAGE 3]. The cricket analogue is Duckworth-Lewis-Stern for interrupted one-day matches [PAGE 3]. The historical lesson is that the problem is old, but the data-rich probabilistic solution is new.

The multi-week traps are where the genealogy becomes operational. Trap one: the early-season trap. Before roughly 10 matchdays, PFSC is “relatively bad” because it discards prior-season information [PAGE 10]. Any rule that applies PFSC uniformly from matchday 1 will overreact to small-sample strength estimates. Trap two: the mid-season convergence trap. PFSC catches the benchmarks after about 10 matchdays and “in all but one of fifteen league-seasons it has caught up after 25 match days” [PAGE 10]. A decision-maker who switches to PFSC too late leaves fairness gains on the table; one who switches too early buys noise. Trap three: the single-league exception. Serie A 2017–2018 is a persistent outlier where PFSC is worse throughout [PAGE 10]. This warns against universal model supremacy claims and supports ensemble or benchmark checks. Trap four: the end-of-season RPS volatility trap. Near the end, few matches remain, so a single upset can sharply increase mean RPS; all three methods follow each other closely because the remaining matches are hard for everyone [PAGE 10]. Trap five: the resumed-season comparability trap. The paper avoids evaluating 2019–2020 resumed competitions because ghost games and other changes could lower home advantage [PAGE 3–4]. Trap six: the unequal-games trap. Ligue 1 used points per match because teams had played unequal numbers of games [PAGE 2]; that is a habit-based compression that ignores remaining fixture difficulty. The PFSC’s advantage is precisely that it models the remaining schedule and team strength rather than only the current points ratio [PAGE 5–6, 13–15].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when decision-makers under time pressure retreat to the most legible object: the current table, points per match, or “the ranking from the moment the competition got interrupted” [PAGE 2]. The paper records that Ligue 1 and the Jupiler Pro League declared champions and relegation on that basis, and that the Jupiler Pro League later had to nullify relegation [PAGE 2]. That is habit regression because it treats an unfinished season as if the stopped table were a completed measurement. The recursive problem is that clubs know the rule before the crisis and can anticipate which schedule asymmetries will be rewarded; the league then faces appeals framed by perceived fairness, as with Lyon’s frustration [PAGE 15]. The counter-trap is the opposite failure: treating the PFSC probability matrix as an oracle. A 100% champion probability for PSG is not the same kind of statement as a deterministic title, and a 77% second-place probability for Marseille still leaves 23% mass elsewhere [PAGE 15]. The paper’s own expected-rank table shows that Rennes at 3.55 and Lille at 3.62 are separated by a small expected-rank gap, yet a deterministic assignment gives Rennes rank 3 and Lille rank 4 [PAGE 16–17]. The counter-trap is to ignore that compression loss. The clock is the matchday and remaining-fixture clock. The paper’s empirical convergence after about 10 matchdays, and catch-up by 25 in fourteen of fifteen league-seasons, defines a phase boundary [PAGE 10]. The clock also governs the champion threshold: the source recommends that “a team can only be declared champion if it has more than \(C\%\) chance to win the league according to the PFSC,” with \(C\) possibly 80 [PAGE 16]. A recursively sophisticated decision-maker therefore uses different rules at different clocks: void or prior-informed ranking very early, probabilistic PFSC with benchmark checks in mid-season, and PFSC plus compensation late. The theory of mind must also anticipate that clubs will compare deterministic outcomes to probabilistic entitlements; the compensation fund is the mechanism for making that comparison explicit rather than leaving it to appeal [PAGE 16–17].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer can veto the PFSC whenever it changes the goal-generating means or the home effect relative to the fitted window. Formally, let \(Z_{\text{phys}}\) be a physical state—fatigue, injury burden, illness, travel, or crowd absence—that shifts \(\lambda_{ijm}\) or \(h\). The simulated rank probability is \(\hat p_{t,r}=F(\hat\lambda,\hat h;\text{fixtures})\), while the target is \(p_{t,r}=F(\lambda(Z),\ h(Z);\text{fixtures})\). If \(Z\) is omitted and team-specific, \(\hat p_{t,r}\neq p_{t,r}\) in general, and the bias can reverse expected-rank order. The paper itself flags the COVID crowd condition because it “could influence team performances, by lowering the effect of the home advantage” [PAGE 3–4]. Veto proof: estimate \(h\) pre-break and post-break; if the shift exceeds a pre-registered confidence bound, stale-\(h\) simulations are inadmissible until re-estimated or neutralized. The biomechanical layer—red cards, substitutions, player availability, and starting-eleven composition—is partially internalized by the plus-minus benchmark but not by PFSC, which uses only match results [PAGE 5–7]. If a team’s remaining availability is systematically worse than its played-season average, PFSC overrates it. Veto proof: compare PFSC expected rank with a plus-minus or availability-conditioned expected rank; if the divergence exceeds a threshold and the availability shock is independently verified, PFSC cannot be the sole adjudicator. The weather layer is an omitted-variable and event-space veto. The paper has no weather covariate; if weather changes scoring rates, postpones matches, or forces neutral venues, then either \(\lambda\) or the fixture set is wrong. Veto proof: if the actual remaining fixture list differs from the simulated one, the rank distribution is computed over the wrong schedule; if neutral venues replace home venues, \(h\) must be reset. Across all three layers, the adjudication rule is: a layer may veto only with a measurable shift or a schedule mutation, not with anecdote. If the layer is measurable and material, the correct response is conditioning, re-estimation, or rescheduling; if it is not measurable, the PFSC output should be reported with sensitivity bounds rather than treated as decisive.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: early-stop phase veto. Counterfactual: stop each of the five major leagues in 2016–2017, 2017–2018, and 2018–2019 before matchday 10, then score PFSC against the current-strength and plus-minus benchmarks with RPS and TRPS. Verifiable prediction: PFSC mean RPS and mean TRPS will be worse than both benchmarks in the majority of the fifteen league-seasons, matching the paper’s statement that PFSC is “relatively bad in the beginning of the season” [PAGE 10]. Decision rule: if the stop occurs before the convergence clock, PFSC cannot be the sole basis; the season should be voided or a prior-informed benchmark should be used.

Veto scenario 2: home-effect shift veto. Counterfactual: estimate \(h\) on pre-break matches, then simulate remaining matches under a true \(h'<h\) (for example, crowd absence or neutral venues) while keeping the stale \(\hat h\). Verifiable prediction: simulated home-win probability is biased upward, and teams with more remaining home fixtures gain expected-rank mass relative to the \(h'\) simulation; the bias is monotone in \(h-\hat h\). The paper’s warning that changed conditions could lower home advantage supplies the mechanism [PAGE 3–4]. Decision rule: if the estimated shift in \(h\) exceeds its confidence bound, the stale-\(h\) PFSC is vetoed until \(h\) is re-estimated or set to the neutral-venue value.

Veto scenario 3: fixture-set mutation veto. Counterfactual: compare PFSC rank probabilities under the original remaining fixture list versus an actual rescheduled list with postponed matches, neutral venues, or unequal home/away counts. Verifiable prediction: TRPS against the realized final table will be worse under the original-list simulation than under the actual-list simulation whenever the mutation is material; expected ranks of teams with altered home/away balance will move in the direction of the altered schedule. The paper’s simulation applies league rules to each simulated end-of-season table [PAGE 5–6], so a wrong fixture set is a wrong event space. Decision rule: if the fixture set or venue assignment changes, PFSC must be rerun on the actual list; otherwise the output is vetoed as a valid fairness calculation.
