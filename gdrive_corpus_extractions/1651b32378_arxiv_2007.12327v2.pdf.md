The intellectual lineage traces to Shapley's foundational stochastic games (1953) [PAGE 13, Ref. 31], which establish Nash equilibrium in stationary strategies for finite zero-sum stochastic games with nonzero stopping probability; the paper extends this to constant-sum games and notes the result carries over [PAGE 13, Appendix]. Boutilier, Dean, and Hanks (1999) contribute decision-theoretic planning structural assumptions [PAGE 5, Ref. 5], cited for the deterministic-action minimax property. Littman's value-function reinforcement learning in Markov games (2001) [PAGE 5, Ref. 17] and Hu & Wellman's Q-learning frameworks (1998, 2003) [PAGE 2, Refs. 11, 12] provide the multi-agent RL backdrop, though those assume perfect information, unlike the imperfect-information APT-DIFT setting [PAGE 8]. Direct precursors include Moothedath et al.'s earlier stochastic game approach to DIFT (arXiv:2006.12327) [PAGE 2, Ref. 21], min-cut DIFT trap placement [PAGE 2, Ref. 22], multi-attacker DIFT games [PAGE 2, Ref. 29], and conditional-branching DIFT game theory [PAGE 13, Ref. 30]. The conference precursor [19] used input-convex neural networks (ICNN) with alternating optimization to approximate equilibria under convex payoff restrictions; this paper relaxes convexity and employs a general supervised network with hierarchical policy iteration [PAGE 2, Ref. 19].

Multi-week operational traps emerge from this lineage. First, the IFG is rebuilt from system logs via RAIN, which incurs performance overhead and depends on log granularity [PAGE 3]. If the defender recomputes the IFG and re-runs value iteration weekly, the APT's dwell time—nation-state engagements can last days, as in the three-day DARPA red-team engagement used here [PAGE 10]—may exceed the recomputation cadence, leaving the defender with a stale topological ordering and hierarchical levels. Second, node versioning [10] acyclifies cyclic IFGs by expanding state space; repeated weekly versioning as the IFG grows can blow up |S| and violate the T ≤ N+4 termination bound in practice, even if the asymptotic bound holds. Third, FN(·) and FP(·) are empirically estimated and attack-dependent [PAGE 4]; an APT that observes the defender's retraining schedule can shift its exploit chain during the window when transition probabilities are being re-estimated, exploiting the incomplete-information gap before HSL's policy iteration converges. Fourth, the HSL neural network is trained on randomly generated strategy pairs [PAGE 9]; if the APT's actual policy drifts outside the training distribution over weeks, the universal approximation bound |f − g| < ξ no longer applies to the encountered region of X, and the predicted Q-values feed into LP Problem 6.10 with unbounded equilibrium error.

Crisis habit regression: When the HSL pipeline fails—due to NN approximation error, cyclic IFG non-termination, or unavailable FP/FN estimates—the defender regresses to a crisis habit of static trap placement or signature-based detection, which the paper explicitly notes cannot detect customized APTs [PAGE 3]. This regression is sub-optimal because static traps ignore the dynamic information-flow state and the minimax structure; the adversary, modeling the defender as regressed to a fixed policy, shifts from worst-case minimization to best-response exploitation.

Counter-trap: The APT's recursive theory of mind assumes the defender is solving for p_D^\star via value iteration or HSL policy iteration. If the APT can observe trap placement outcomes (detected vs. undetected flows), it can infer the defender's mixed strategy p_D(s) over time and solve its own best response max_{p_A} min_{p_D} U_A, effectively inverting Eq. (13). Because the game is constant-sum, the APT's optimal counter-strategy p_A^\star minimizes U_D, forcing the defender into lower detection probability. The counter-trap is therefore not a one-shot trap relocation but a continuous path-selection adaptation: the APT chooses transitions in the IFG to avoid nodes where the defender's predicted trap probability is high, leveraging the imperfect-information structure where DIFT cannot condition on the attack path [PAGE 4].

Clock: The game clock is the termination horizon T ≤ N + 4 steps under Assumption 6.6 [PAGE 7], but real APT operations unfold over days with multiple stages (initial compromise, reconnaissance, foothold establishment, exfiltration) [PAGE 3, 10]. The paper's single-stage abstraction (m = 1) compresses this; the multi-stage IFG G_m duplicates the graph per stage and connects destination sets D_j to D_{j+1} [PAGE 9]. The recursive clock mismatch is that the stochastic game's step clock (system-call transitions) is orders of magnitude faster than the APT's operational clock (days), yet the value iteration clock k = 1, 2, ... must converge before the defender can deploy p_D^\star. In the nation-state experiment, convergence required k = 32 iterations with δ = 10^{-7} [PAGE 11]; if each iteration requires re-probing the live IFG, the wall-clock latency may exceed the APT's stage-transition time, rendering the computed equilibrium stale by deployment. The HSL algorithm attempts to amortize this cost via the neural network, but its training episodes (100) and sample budget (10^5) introduce an offline clock that competes with the online detection requirement.
