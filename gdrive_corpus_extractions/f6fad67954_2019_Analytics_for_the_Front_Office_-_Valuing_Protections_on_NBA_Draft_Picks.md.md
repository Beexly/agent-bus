\]
Units: dimensionless conditional probability. Data: historical NBA standings, 1980–2017. Assumptions: year-over-year rank transition is stationary and Markovian across the protection horizon. Breakdown: post-2019 lottery reform, tanking-incentive shifts, or structural parity changes invalidate the historical matrix.

3) Monotonicity filtering algorithm (Page 10, Eq. 5):
\[
\text{if } WS_{CP,DP+1} > WS_{CP,DP} \text{ then }
WS_{CP,DP} \leftarrow \frac{WS_{CP,DP} + WS_{CP,DP+1}}{2}
\]
Units: win shares over five years (WS/5yrs), dimensionless basketball-contribution units. Assumptions: historical outliers must be smoothed so that a worse pick never yields more win shares than a better pick at any cumulative probability. Breakdown: the averaging reallocates value from worse picks to better picks, biasing valuations upward (Page 11) and erasing genuine historical anomalies where late picks outperform early picks.

4) Monte Carlo assembly (Page 11): draw finishing position from Module 1; draw lottery pick position; test membership in protection set \( \mathcal{P} \); if protected, draw Year \( y+1 \) rank from conditional transition; repeat until conveyance or horizon expiry; output empirical distribution of WS/5yrs. Units: WS/5yrs. Breakdown: 10,000 trials (Page 24) provide Monte Carlo standard error but cannot resolve structural misspecification in Modules 1–2.

5) Wang-transform pricing (Page 12, Eq. 7):
\[
\text{Price}(x) = E_{V^*} = \sum_x x \, F^*(x)
\]
where \( F^* \) is the distorted density and \( \gamma \) is the market price of risk (Sharpe ratio). Units: price denominated in WS/5yrs. Assumptions: \( \gamma \) captures risk-seeking (\(\gamma < 0\)) versus risk-aversion (\(\gamma > 0\)) per asset class. Calibration: pick-only trades imply \( \gamma = 0.413 \) and \( 0.436 \); rounded average \( 0.42 \) (Page 13). Asset-class ratios (Page 13, Table 1): pick 1: \(-0.77\); 2–3: \(-0.39\); 4–5: \(-0.19\); 6–10: \(-0.01\); 11–18: \(0.24\); 19–24: \(0.44\); 25–30: \(0.54\). Breakdown: only two observable pick-only trades anchor \( \gamma \); extrapolation to seven classes is assumption-heavy and sensitive to the chosen reference trades.

6) Omitted variables (Page 22): no time-value discounting; no rookie-salary cost. Example: a top-10 protected pick that conveys outside the top 10 carries a starting salary capped near \$3.4M, whereas a top-1 conveyance the following year can reach roughly \$8.1M (footnote 14, Page 22). Breakdown: for multi-year protections, ignoring discounting and salary scale makes Year-2–6 win shares appear equivalent to Year-1–5 win shares, overvaluing deferred conveys for high-salary slots.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s intellectual lineage begins with the 1984 Dallas–Indiana trade, the first NBA transaction to formally use a pick protection (Page 1), establishing draft picks as alienable, conditionally conveyable assets. The theoretical core imports financial-option logic: a protected pick is “much like financial option contracts” whose payoff is contingent on the uncertain future value of an underlying draft-position asset (Page 2). This motivates the use of the Wang transform (Wang, 2002; Page 12), a distortion-based pricing method from insurance and financial-risk mathematics, rather than a simple expected-value calculation.

The Elo simulation follows Silver and Fischer-Baum (2015; Page 7), adapting five-thirty-eight-style team-rating mechanics to forecast standing position. Player-value measurement draws on 36 years of Basketball-Reference data (Page 9), with win shares over five years chosen to align with rookie-contract retention windows. Asset-class segmentation uses K-means clustering (Lloyd, 1982; Page 13) to partition the 30 pick positions into seven risk-preference classes: {1}, {2–3}, {4–5}, {6–10}, {11–18}, {19–24}, {25–30}.

Multi-week traps. The model’s temporal architecture contains at least three layered traps. First, the trade-deadline-to-lottery interval creates a window in which Elo inputs are static but actual roster health, minutes distribution, and trade activity evolve; the simulation cannot condition on in-season personnel shocks. Second, the conditional rollover matrix assumes that a team finishing at rank \( r_1 \) in Year \( y \) transitions to Year \( y+1 \) according to 1980–2017 base rates (Page 8). For a team protected in Year 1, this multi-week look-ahead effectively bets that post-season trajectory regresses toward historical means, ignoring coaching changes, free-agency signings, or lottery-odds rule changes. Third, the seven-year pick-trading limit and the prohibition on consecutive first-round pick trades (Page 3) impose a hard clock on asset liquidity: a protection scheme extending across multiple years must resolve before the asset’s tradable window closes, yet the model does not price the optionality of waiting versus conveying now.

Historically, protections cluster on psychological anchors—5, 10, 14 (Page 2)—which the paper reads as evidence of regular mis-valuation. The recursive implication is that market behavior lags the model: front offices continue to use round-number protections while the valuation system suggests that optimal protection boundaries should be continuous functions of Elo, conditional-transition probabilities, and class-specific \( \gamma \).
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Level-0 reasoning: a front office values a pick at its expected WS/5yrs conditional on its current standing and Elo. Level-1 reasoning: the office anticipates that counterparties anchor on top-5, top-10, and top-14 protections (Page 2) and structures schemes to exploit those anchors—for example, offering a top-14 protection that is economically nearer to a top-10 because of rollover dynamics. Level-2 reasoning: the counterparty recognizes the anchor exploitation and demands a risk premium calibrated to the tiered asset class (Table 1, Page 13), transforming the negotiation into a contest over \( \gamma \) rather than over raw pick position.

Crisis habit regression. Under trade-deadline time pressure, GMs regress to familiar protection numerology (top-5, top-10) rather than computing the protection-distorted distribution \( P(d \mid \mathcal{P}) \). The paper’s results show this is costly: for poor teams, moving from unprotected to top-1 protected can shift nearly 10 WS of value (Page 16), while for middle teams such as the Knicks, an unprotected pick is worth approximately the same as a top-8 protected pick, with the minimum at top-1 protection (Page 16). A counter-trap is to offer a protection level that appears concessionary—e.g., top-14—while the conditional rollover probability makes it less costly than a top-10 protection because protected Year-1 outcomes defer to an unprotected Year-2 draw where bottom teams have elevated chances of landing pick #1 (Page 15).

The clock operates at three scales: (1) the in-season trade deadline and CBA timing rules (Page 3) constrain when protections can be negotiated; (2) the seven-year pick-trading horizon (Page 3) caps the maximum maturity of the option; and (3) the five-year win-shares valuation window (Page 9) truncates player contribution at the rookie-contract boundary, ignoring Year 6+ value. A recursive theory-of-mind failure occurs when Team A prices Team B’s pick using Team A’s own \( \gamma \) while Team B is actually in a different asset class with a different risk preference, producing a clearing failure in a low-liquidity barter market.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The valuation model is a conditional statement: it holds only if no exogenous veto fires across physical, biomechanical, or regulatory layers.

Physical layer (injury/availability): Module 2’s win-shares distribution assumes historical draftee health trajectories are stationary (Page 9). A veto fires if a projected draftee suffers a career-altering injury in Year 1, collapsing realized WS/5yrs to near zero. The model cannot condition on pre-draft medical information or in-season load-management decisions.

Biomechanical/development layer: WS/5yrs presumes roughly linear accumulation over the rookie-contract window (Page 9). A veto fires if a player’s development stalls, plateaus, or accelerates nonlinearly, invalidating the five-year cumulative metric as a proxy for trade value.

Schedule/weather layer: Elo inputs assume the remaining schedule is simulated without weather or travel-fatigue modifiers (Page 7). Extreme back-to-back congestion, arena unavailability, or travel disruptions can bias game-win probabilities and therefore shift the finishing-position distribution used in Module 1.

Regulatory layer: CBA deadlines, the prohibition on consecutive first-round pick trades, and the seven-year trading limit (Page 3) veto certain protection structures outright. A multi-year scheme that attempts to convey a pick outside these windows is unenforceable regardless of model price.

Adjudication rule: the model’s output price is admissible only when all veto layers are quiescent. If any layer fires—injury, coaching change, CBA restriction, or salary-scale revision—the computed WS/5yrs price must be overridden by a re-simulation or a manual adjustment. The paper explicitly acknowledges the salary omission (Page 22) and the absence of time discounting (Page 22), confirming these are known veto boundaries rather than modeled features.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1: Star-player season-ending injury before the trade deadline. A team modeled as a middle seed suffers an injury that collapses its Elo trajectory, shifting the simulated finishing-position distribution toward the bottom. The existing protection scheme—priced assuming playoff contention—now misprices the rollover probability. Veto condition: injury-adjusted Elo moves the team more than 5 standings ranks below the pre-injury simulation median. Prediction: the model overvalues the pick’s protection because the actual chance of a top-4 conveyance rises above the simulated probability; a re-simulation with injury-adjusted Elo would reduce the tiered risk-adjusted price by at least 3–5 WS/5yrs for a top-10 protected asset.

Scenario 2: Mid-season coaching change altering team strength. Module 1 assumes static roster strength updated only by game results with \( k = 16 \) (Page 7). A coaching upgrade improves team performance beyond the Elo projection, pushing the team out of the protected range more often than simulated. Veto condition: actual post-change winning percentage exceeds the Elo-implied expectation by more than 0.100 over the remaining schedule. Prediction: for a team such as the Knicks (Page 16), the top-1 protected minimum value is overestimated; the counterfactual veto predicts a lower price because the unprotected conveyance probability increases and the risk-seeking premium for low picks (asset class 1, \( \gamma = -0.77 \)) is not realized.

Scenario 3: CBA rookie-salary scale revision increasing the cost of top-5 picks. The model values picks solely in WS/5yrs and omits salary (Page 22). If the rookie scale raises the starting salary for a top-5 pick from roughly \$3.4M to roughly \$6M while top-10–30 salaries remain capped lower, the net value of a top-5 protected conveyance falls relative to a top-10 protection. Veto condition: salary differential exceeds the marginal WS gain from improving pick position by 5 slots. Prediction: the model’s WS/5yrs price remains unchanged, but the veto overrides it; a cost-benefit re-analysis would show a top-10 protection dominating a top-5 protection for teams near the luxury line, contradicting the model’s raw-value recommendation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
