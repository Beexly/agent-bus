Counter-trap: The limited-audience scenario overturns a naive null that only full stadiums matter. With up to 25% capacity (except the Super Bowl [PAGE 5]), \hat{\pi}=54.92% and mean D=1.336 points, with confidence intervals overlapping the merged historical sample [PAGE 6, 11]. This implies a nonlinear psychological threshold: sparse, scattered spectators suffice to reactivate the "being watched" self-consciousness and referee social-pressure effects. The counter-trap is that crowd support is not a continuous dose–response but a switch-like trigger activated well below full occupancy.

Clock: The effect operates on an immediate, within-season clock rather than requiring multi-week habit formation. The 2020 no-audience and limited-audience games occur in the same season, so recovery under limited attendance demonstrates rapid psychological recalibration once any crowd is present. However, the paper provides no week-indexed decay or buildup curve, so the precise time constant of the effect—how quickly home advantage erodes without fans or re-emerges with them—remains unobserved and is a boundary of the inference.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The paper records game outcomes and scores but not stadium weather, surface, or dome status. Outdoor NFL games in late autumn and winter are exposed to temperature, wind, and precipitation, all of which shift scoring D=H-A independently of crowd presence. If 2020 featured systematically different weather patterns at home venues relative to 1970–2019, the suppressed no-audience D could be partially weather-confounded. No weather covariate is included, so this physical-layer veto remains open.

Biomechanical/travel layer: The authors explicitly state travel effects do not disappear without spectators [PAGE 1–2], nor do familiarity and territoriality [PAGE 1]. These non-crowd causes should preserve some home advantage even in empty stadiums. The observed no-audience \hat{\pi}=46.53% lies below 50%, suggesting crowd removal overrode these advantages, yet the 95% CI [41.57%,54.67%] and 99% CI [35.80%,57.26%] [PAGE 10] both cover 50%. Therefore the data do not statistically veto the persistence of travel/familiarity/territoriality effects; they remain within uncertainty bounds and cannot be isolated from the crowd effect.

Weather veto proof: There is no weather variable in the dataset or model [PAGES 3–5], so no formal weather veto can be executed from this source alone. A systematic 2020 weather anomaly—e.g., more inclement conditions at home stadiums—would produce exactly the observed pattern (lower home scoring, lower D) without invoking crowd psychology. Because the paper cannot condition on weather, the claim that "all other potential causes are irrelevant" [PAGE 2] is not veto-proof against meteorological confounding.

Cross-layer adjudication: The strongest identifying restriction is the within-2020 contrast between no-audience and limited-audience games, which share the same season, schedule protocols, and weather regime. Since limited attendance restores historical home performance while zero attendance does not, and since travel/familiarity/territoriality are constant across the two subsets, the differential effect is plausibly attributed to crowd presence. Nevertheless, the absence of weather and granular travel controls means a fully veto-proof causal chain is not established from this document alone.

Veto condition: if 2021 shows \hat{\pi} persistently below 50% or D mean significantly negative despite full crowds, the crowd-absence mechanism is falsified, implicating a season-specific confounder rather than spectators.

Veto Scenario 2 — Venue-type stratification within 2020 no-audience games. Split the 144 no-audience games by stadium environment (fully enclosed domes vs. open-air venues) and by travel distance (same-time-zone vs. cross-country trips). Prediction under the pure crowd hypothesis: suppression of home performance is homogeneous across venue and travel strata because the removed variable (crowd) is common to all. Under the travel/weather confound hypothesis: dome and short-travel home teams show significantly higher \hat{\pi} and D than open-air, long-travel home teams. Veto condition: a statistically significant stratum-by-stratum difference (two-sample Z or Welch T rejecting equality at 95%) falsifies the claim that crowd effects are the sole operative factor [PAGE 2].

Using play-by-play or officiating records, compute the home-minus-away penalty/flag differential for 2020 no-audience games, 2020 limited-audience games, and 1970–2019 historical games. Prediction under the referee-social-pressure mechanism [5–8]: the home-favoring differential collapses toward zero in no-audience games and recovers to historical levels in limited-audience games, mirroring the win-proportion recovery [PAGE 6–8].
