Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

The document sits inside the Walter Camp–era All-America tradition but represents a deliberate Western counter-institution. Spalding's Official Foot Ball Guide was the canonical annual record of the 1914 college football season; page 29 [PAGE 1] gathers ballots from sporting editors across the Midwest and South rather than the Eastern establishment that had dominated early All-America picks. The lineage runs: Camp's personal selections (1889s–1920s) → newspaper sporting-editor All-America lists → wire-service and consensus compilations (here E. B. Moss, Associated Press) → official guide codification. The selectors named on [PAGE 1]—Howard Pearson (Detroit Journal), Diek Jemison (Atlanta Constitution), Frank G. Menke (International News Service), Walter H. Eckersall (Chicago Tribune), Lambert G. Sullivan (Chicago Daily News)—were the nodes of a regional information network that transmitted player reputations via game reports, not via standardized statistics. There are no rushing yards, tackles, or weights in [PAGE 1]; only names, schools, and positions.

Scientific lineage relevant to modern analysis: this is an early expert-aggregation problem, structurally similar to Delphi panels or jury theorems, except the voters are correlated by geography and by access to the same regional games. The "Consensus of Opinion" compiled by Moss is an antecedent of modern ensemble methods, but it lacks independence assumptions; it is more accurately described as a positional mode over correlated regional ballots.

Multi-week traps embedded in treating [PAGE 1] as static ground truth:

Trap 1: Retrospective canonization. Because the Moss consensus [PAGE 1] prints Cherry, Graves, Halligan, Buck, Routh, Chapman, Des Jardien, Clark, Maulbetsch, Pogue, Solon, a later researcher may treat these eleven as the "true" 1914 All-Americans and project that status backward or forward across weeks of a season. But the consensus was produced after the season from ballots whose internal dates are unknown; a player's selection in week 10 tells nothing about week 3 form, injuries, or coaching changes.

Trap 2: OCR drift across reprints. The strings "Olark," "Bolon," "Solen," "Aggles," "Ihinois" [PAGE 1] are artifacts of one OCR pipeline. If a different reprint or OCR pass were used, player identity clusters would split or merge, changing f(x) and hence H, D_eff, and C*. Treating any single OCR layer as immutable is a trap.

Trap 3: All-Conference conflation. Leonard Adams and Sidney Casner [PAGE 1] head their lists "ALL-CONFERENCE SELECTIONS," yet they are printed immediately after the All-American section. A researcher pooling all twelve lists into one consensus pool imports conference-eligible players (Huntington Chicago, Blocker Purdue, Barron Iowa, Baston Minnesota, Hamilton Minnesota) into an All-American inference set, violating the eligibility boundary.

Trap 5: Regional homogeneity bias. All players on [PAGE 1] come from Midwestern/Western schools (Michigan, Illinois, Chicago, Nebraska, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Ohio State, Purdue, Notre Dame, Iowa, plus small colleges). There is no East Coast representation. A multi-week analysis that assumes national talent parity would be mis-calibrated; the source is a regional census, not a national probability sample.
