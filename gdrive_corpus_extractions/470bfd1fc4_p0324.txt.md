Historical/scientific lineage and multi-week traps.

The passage belongs to the Hullian behaviorist tradition of the 1940s, in which behavior is treated as a quantifiable, law-governed system amenable to hypothetico-deductive testing. Its statistical apparatus descends from the error theory of Gauss and Laplace, the Central Limit Theorem, and Galtonian/Queteletian social physics: the idea that central tendencies emerge from the cancellation of numerous small, independent errors. Hull (1943) transposes this physical-science template onto organismic behavior, arguing that the "backward condition" of social relative to physical science stems not from theoretical poverty per se but from the universality of behavioral oscillation ([PAGE 317]).

Multi-week trap one: stationarity mirage. A researcher collecting behavior over multiple weeks assumes that the central tendency estimated in week 1 generalizes to week 4. Hull's principle implies that even under nominally constant conditions, trial-by-trial oscillation persists; across weeks, circadian, seasonal, and experiential drift violate ceteris paribus. A mean computed from 64 observations in week 1 is not transportable to week 4 unless N is effectively infinite within each epoch and conditions are frozen.

Multi-week trap two: N-inflation illusion. Pooling data across weeks to reach large N (e.g., 4096 observations) does not eliminate blur if the generating process itself drifts. The 1/\sqrt{N} reduction applies to random distortion around a fixed \mu, not to systematic drift of \mu across sessions. Thus massing data without within-week stationarity yields a precise estimate of a moving target—a high-precision wrong mean.

Multi-week trap three: habit-family consolidation vs. momentary oscillation. Hull's broader theory posits habit hierarchies that consolidate over weeks of reinforcement. The oscillation principle describes short-run molar variability, whereas the habit system describes long-run convergence. Conflating the two timescales produces the error of treating a week-4 stable habit curve as if it were exempt from trial-level oscillation, or conversely treating trial noise as evidence against habit formation.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock.

Crisis habit regression: Under pressure or crisis conditions, an organism's behavior regresses to previously dominant habit-family responses rather than executing the optimally computed response. In Hullian terms, reaction intensity is mediated by receptor-effector pathways subject to spontaneous nerve-cell variance ([PAGE 317]); under stress, gain on those pathways shifts, and the sample of reactions drawn during the crisis is not drawn from the same population as baseline trials. The mean under crisis conditions estimates a different \mu, so applying the baseline 1/\sqrt{N} correction is invalid.

Counter-trap: The observer falls into a mirror error by assuming that the subject's choices reveal stable preferences or strategies. Because molar behavior is inherently variable and specifically unpredictable under seemingly constant conditions ([PAGE 317], SUMMARY), a finite sequence of choices cannot be inverted to recover a unique underlying utility or rule. The counter-trap is to overfit a model to a short behavioral run, mistaking oscillation for signal.

Clock: The decisive variable is the timescale separation between the oscillation clock (trial-by-trial nerve-cell fluctuation, receptor-effector latency) and the central-tendency clock (session-level aggregation over N trials). If the observation window is shorter than the drift timescale of \mu, the 1/\sqrt{N} law governs. If the window spans multiple drift regimes (e.g., multiple weeks), the mean is aliased by non-stationarity and the error no longer vanishes as N^{-1/2}. Any behavioral inference must specify which clock is running.
