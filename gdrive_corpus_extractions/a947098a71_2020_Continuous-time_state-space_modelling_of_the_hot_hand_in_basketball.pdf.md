The paper explicitly motivates the need for continuous time by noting that intervals can range from seconds to many minutes: “For intervals between attempts that are fairly short (such as a few seconds), a player will most likely be able to retain his form from the last shot. On the other hand, if several minutes elapse before a player takes his next shot, it becomes less likely that he is able to retain his form from the last attempt.” [PAGE 3 of 17]. Thus the first multi-hop step is: raw hit/miss sequences are not exchangeable in time; the sampling clock carries information about latent persistence.

The second step is the latent-state formulation. The observation model is Bernoulli with success probability \(\pi_{t_k}\), and the logit of that probability is additively decomposed into a latent player-form state \(S_{t_k}\), a player-specific intercept \(\beta_{0,p}\), and covariates for home, score difference, last 30 seconds of a quarter, second free throw, and third free throw [PAGE 7 of 17]. This is important because the paper’s definition of the hot hand is serial correlation in success probabilities, not merely serial correlation in realized outcomes: the observed attempts are conditionally independent given the latent states, while dependence is induced by the hidden state process [PAGE 7 of 17]. The third step is the continuous-time state law: the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dS_t=\theta(\mu-S_t)dt+\sigma dB_t\), with \(\mu=0\), so that positive states mean above-average form and negative states mean below-average form [PAGE 7 of 17]. The fourth step is the transition law over an observed gap \(\Delta_\tau=t_\tau-t_{\tau-1}\): \(S_\tau\mid S_{\tau-1}=s\sim N(e^{-\theta\Delta_\tau}s,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1-e^{-2\theta\Delta_\tau}))\) [PAGE 9 of 17]. The fifth step is likelihood evaluation by discretizing the continuous state space; the paper defines the state range as \([-2,2]\) and divides it into 100 intervals, then applies the forward algorithm [PAGE 9 of 17]. The sixth step is interpretation: smaller \(\theta\) means slower reversion and stronger serial dependence; the paper states, “The smaller θ, the longer it takes for the OU process to return to its mean and hence the higher the serial correlation.” [PAGE 7 of 17].

The gray-zone audit matters because the reported evidence is statistically favored but substantively small. The reported estimates are \(\hat\theta=0.042\) with 95% CI \([0.016;0.109]\) and \(\hat\sigma=0.101\) with 95% CI \([0.055;0.185]\) [PAGE 10 of 17]. The implied mean-reversion half-life is \(\ln 2/\hat\theta\approx16.5\) minutes, so within a 48-minute game the latent state can persist, but the stationary state variance is \(\hat\sigma^2/(2\hat\theta)\approx0.121\) logit\(^2\), giving a stationary standard deviation near 0.348 on the logit scale. The paper itself describes the stationary distribution as \(N(0,0.348^2)\) and says it indicates “a rather small range of the state process” [PAGE 10 of 17]. The information-criterion evidence is \(\Delta AIC=61.20\) and \(\Delta BIC=41.97\) in favor of the state-space model [PAGE 10 of 17], but with 110,513 observations the per-observation likelihood gain is roughly \(3\times10^{-4}\) nats if two additional state parameters are counted, so the model wins but the practical separation is tiny. The paper’s own conclusion is guarded: “Although we provide evidence for the existence of a hot hand, the magnitude of the hot hand eﬀect is rather small as the underlying success probabilities are only elevated by a few percentage points” [PAGE 14 of 17]. Gray zones include the lack of a universal definition, the arbitrary mapping from \(\theta\) to “hot hand exists,” the discretization range \([-2,2]\), the conditioning on players with at least 2,000 free throws and games with at least four attempts, the clustering of free throws at nearly equal times, and the large but composition-sensitive third-free-throw coefficient. These are not side notes; they are the boundaries within which the paper’s claim is valid.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The observation operator is a Bernoulli logit model. For observation \(\tau=1,\dots,T\),
\[
Y_{t_\tau}\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi_{t_\tau}),\qquad
\operatorname{logit}(\pi_{t_\tau})
=S_{t_\tau}+\beta_{0,p}+\beta_1\,\mathrm{home}
+\beta_2\,\mathrm{scorediff}
+\beta_3\,\mathrm{last30}
+\beta_4\,\mathrm{ft2}
+\beta_5\,\mathrm{ft3}.
\]
This is Equation (1) on [PAGE 7 of 17]. Here \(\pi_{t_\tau}\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless probability; \(S_{t_\tau}\) is dimensionless on the natural-logit scale; the dummy covariates \(\mathrm{home},\mathrm{last30},\mathrm{ft2},\mathrm{ft3}\in\{0,1\}\) are unitless; \(\mathrm{scorediff}\) is in points; and \(\beta_2\) is therefore in logit per point, while the other \(\beta_j\) are in logit per unit change of a dummy. The player-specific intercept \(\beta_{0,p}\) is dimensionless logit.

The latent-state operator is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck stochastic differential equation,
\[
dS_t=\theta(\mu-S_t)\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t,\qquad S_0=s_0,
\]
with \(\theta>0\), \(\mu\in\mathbb{R}\), and \(\sigma>0\) [PAGE 7 of 17]. The paper sets \(\mu=0\) so that the state fluctuates around average form given covariates [PAGE 7 of 17]. If \(t\) is measured in minutes, then \(\theta\) has units \(\mathrm{min}^{-1}\), and under the standard Brownian-motion convention \(\sigma\) has units \(\mathrm{min}^{-1/2}\) so that \(S_t\) remains dimensionless logit. The transition kernel over \(\Delta_\tau=t_\tau-t_{\tau-1}\) is
\[
S_\tau\mid S_{\tau-1}=s
\sim
N\!\left(
e^{-\theta\Delta_\tau}s,\;
\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\left(1-e^{-2\theta\Delta_\tau}\right)
\right),
\]
which is Equation (4) on [PAGE 9 of 17]. The stationary distribution used at the start of a game is
\[
S_1\sim N\!\left(0,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\right),
\]
as stated on [PAGE 9 of 17]. The likelihood for one throwing sequence is the integrated product
\[
L_T=\int\cdots\int
\Pr(s_1)\Pr(y_1\mid s_1)
\prod_{\tau=2}^{T}\Pr(s_\tau\mid s_{\tau-1})\Pr(y_\tau\mid s_\tau)
\,ds_T\cdots ds_1,
\]
which is Equation (3) on [PAGE 9 of 17]. Because this integral is intractable, the paper approximates it by discretizing the state space over \([-2,2]\) into 100 intervals and using the forward algorithm [PAGE 9 of 17]. The decoding operator is the Viterbi maximization
\[
(s_1^*,\dots,s_T^*)=\operatorname*{argmax}_{s_1,\dots,s_T}\Pr(s_1,\dots,s_T\mid y_1,\dots,y_T),
\]
as given on [PAGE 12 of 17]. The model-selection comparison uses AIC and BIC; the paper reports \(\Delta AIC=61.20\) and \(\Delta BIC=41.97\) favoring the state-space model over the no-state benchmark [PAGE 10 of 17].

Key assumptions are: conditional independence of free-throw outcomes given the latent states; a stationary, continuous-valued, Gaussian OU state; a common drift \(\theta\) and diffusion \(\sigma\) across players, with player heterogeneity absorbed by \(\beta_{0,p}\); additive covariate effects on the logit scale; and approximate likelihood evaluation by finite discretization. The reported empirical anchors are \(\hat\theta=0.042\) \([0.016;0.109]\), \(\hat\sigma=0.101\) \([0.055;0.185]\), and a stationary distribution described as \(N(0,0.348^2)\) [PAGE 10 of 17]. The paper also reports 110,513 free throws from 44 players, 15,075 throwing sequences, and a median of 6 free throws per game with minimum 4 and maximum 39 [PAGE 4 of 17].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are exact consequences of the equations. As \(\theta\to0^+\), mean-reversion time \(1/\theta\to\infty\) and stationary variance \(\sigma^2/(2\theta)\to\infty\); the process approaches a nonstationary random walk, the “average form” anchor becomes meaningless, and the OU model’s stationary-distribution assumption fails. As \(\theta\to\infty\) with \(\sigma\) fixed, \(e^{-\theta\Delta}\to0\) and \(\sigma^2/(2\theta)\to0\); the state collapses toward zero and the hot-hand state becomes unidentifiable. As \(\sigma\to0\), the state is deterministic at zero and there is no latent form variation. As \(\Delta_\tau\to0\), the transition variance goes to zero and the state is carried over exactly; if all gaps were zero, only the between-cluster gaps would identify \(\theta\). The finite state range \([-2,2]\) can truncate tails if the stationary standard deviation grows too large. Finally, the paper notes that “there is no universally accepted definition of the hot hand” and that “a further quantiﬁcation of the magnitude of the hot hand eﬀect is not possible” [PAGE 14 of 17], so the mapping from a positive \(\theta\) to a substantive hot-hand magnitude is not a clean threshold.

</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a retrocausal correction of the original hot-hand debate. Gilovich et al. (1985) found no evidence and treated the hot hand as a cognitive illusion, a result the paper says was “much to the disapproval of many athletes and fans” [PAGE 2 of 17]. Bar-Eli et al. (2006) reviewed 24 studies, 11 in favor of the phenomenon [PAGE 2 of 17]. Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) then showed that the original Gilovich et al. study “suﬀers from a selection bias,” and when that bias is accounted for, “their results do reveal a hot hand eﬀect” [PAGE 2 of 17]. The present paper inherits a second correction: earlier work often used discrete-time models that “require the data to follow a regular sampling scheme and thus, cannot (directly) be applied to irregularly sampled data” [PAGE 3 of 17]. The OU state-space model is the retrocausal bridge: it reinterprets past streak data as noisy observations of a continuously evolving latent form, and it makes the time gap between attempts a first-class variable rather than an ignored nuisance.

The multi-week traps are selection, aggregation, and clock confounding. A raw “hot” or “cold” week can be created by conditioning on streaks after observing them; the paper’s own simulation and decoding show decoded states ranging only from \(-0.42\) to \(0.46\), which is a narrow band [PAGE 12 of 17]. Aggregating across players hides heterogeneity: empirical free-throw proportions range from 0.451 to 0.906, and player intercepts range from \(-0.311\) to \(2.192\) on the logit scale [PAGE 5 of 17] [PAGE 11 of 17]. Clock confounding is central: free throws arrive in clusters of two or three, often at the same recorded time, followed by long gaps; the paper’s LeBron James examples show repeated times such as 11.01, 11.01, 23.48, 23.48 [PAGE 6 of 17]. If an analyst treats all consecutive attempts as equally spaced, short within-cluster gaps and long between-cluster gaps are averaged together, and the apparent persistence can be misestimated. Another trap is composition: the third free throw has a much higher success rate, 0.883 versus 0.769 for the first, but the paper cautions that this is “probably caused by the fact that three free throws in a row are only awarded if a player is fouled while shooting a three-point ﬁeld goal, which, in turn, is more often attempted by players who regularly perform well at free throws” [PAGE 12 of 17]. Thus a multi-week “hot free-throw shooter” narrative can be an artifact of who gets three-shot trips, which game states produce them, and how many attempts are included.

</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer has three competing mental models: crisis habit regression, counter-trap overadjustment, and clock-sensitive state tracking. Crisis habit regression is the OU interpretation: under pressure or after a miss, a player tends to return toward his average form, with reversion speed \(\theta\). The model’s small \(\hat\theta=0.042\ \mathrm{min}^{-1}\) implies a half-life near 16.5 minutes, so a player can carry form across short clusters but not indefinitely across long gaps. The player’s own belief can feed back into this: if he believes he is “hot,” he may slow his routine or overfocus; if he believes he is “cold,” he may rush. The paper’s free-throw setting is unusually clean because there is no defender, but it is not belief-free.

The counter-trap is the opposite error: coaches, players, and analysts overread a short streak and change behavior. In free throws, the direct defensive response is absent, but substitution patterns, who is allowed to shoot technical free throws, foul-drawing style, and late-game possession choices can change the sample. The paper includes a last-30-seconds dummy to capture pressure, but its estimate is \(\hat\beta_3=0.003\) with 95% CI \([-0.051;0.058]\), which includes zero [PAGE 10 of 17]. The home coefficient is also small and its interval includes zero, while score difference is positive and small with an interval excluding zero [PAGE 10 of 17]. So the recursive danger is not that the clock directly proves a hot hand; it is that agents condition on the clock and on streaks when deciding whom to observe.

The clock model is the correct counter-trap: the relevant question is not “did he make the last three?” but “how much time elapsed, and what state could persist?” The paper’s example calculation makes this explicit: after a decoded state of \(-0.092\) and a 1.65-minute gap, the next state has conditional mean \(-0.086\) and variance \(0.016\); after a 12.22-minute gap, the next state has mean \(-0.050\) and variance \(0.078\) [PAGE 13 of 17]. Longer gaps increase variance and weaken retention, so a “hot” label should decay with the clock. A recursive theory of mind therefore predicts that short-gap clusters should show more state persistence than long-gap pairs, that late-clock pressure should be a weak moderator at best, and that any strong “clutch” narrative must be separated from selection into who shoots late.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical veto is an identifiability gate. Suppose the true logit is not just \(S_t+X_t\beta\) but also contains an unmodeled biomechanical drift \(B_t\), for example fatigue-related changes in release angle, elbow alignment, leg drive, or injury compensation. If \(B_t\) is slowly varying within a game and has autocorrelation on the same timescale as \(1/\theta\), then \(B_t\) can masquerade as the OU state \(S_t\). The estimated stationary state variance is about \(0.121\) logit\(^2\), with standard deviation about \(0.348\) logit. A conservative veto rule is: if an independently measured biomechanical shift has standard deviation comparable to or larger than 0.348 logit and persists over tens of minutes, the hot-hand interpretation is not identified by this model alone. The paper controls some contextual layers—home, score difference, last 30 seconds, and free-throw position—but it does not include minutes played, rest days, injury status, shooting mechanics, or player fatigue [PAGE 5 of 17]. Free throws are standardized and involve no defender, which weakens direct opponent interaction, but it does not eliminate biomechanical drift.

The weather veto is essentially not triggered. The data are NBA games played indoors between October 2012 and June 2019 [PAGE 4 of 17]. The paper does not model weather, altitude, travel, or arena HVAC, and there is no source evidence that outdoor weather directly enters the free-throw process. A weather-veto proof therefore reduces to a scope condition: if the sport were outdoor or if arena conditions changed ball flight materially, the stationarity and common-parameter assumptions would need an environmental covariate; in this indoor NBA setting, the weather layer is not a credible competing explanation for the latent persistence. The physical-layer veto is more serious: an injury or fatigue regime change would violate the constant \(\theta,\sigma\) and stable \(\beta_{0,p}\) assumptions. If a player’s form shifts permanently within the sample, the OU model would average across regimes and could bias \(\theta\) downward. Thus the model’s causal claim survives only if the physical layer is either stable or absorbed by covariates and player intercepts. The paper’s own narrow decoded-state range and small effect size are consistent with a weak latent process, not with a large unmodeled biomechanical wave.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: time-interval permutation negative control. Keep each game’s outcome sequence and covariates fixed, but randomly permute the observed gaps \(\Delta_\tau\) within that game, then refit the continuous-time state-space model and compare held-out log score and AIC against the true-clock model. Verifiable prediction: the true-clock model should retain a positive advantage over the no-state benchmark, while the permuted-clock model should lose most of the time-aware advantage. Because the reported full-sample advantage is \(\Delta AIC=61.20\) over 110,513 throws [PAGE 10 of 17], the per-observation log-score gain is roughly \(3\times10^{-4}\) nats; a reasonable prediction is that the true-clock model beats the permuted-clock model by a small but positive margin, with the permuted model’s \(\Delta AIC\) falling below half of 61.20. Veto condition: if the permuted-clock model matches or beats the true-clock model on held-out log score and AIC, the time-aware OU interpretation is vetoed.

Veto scenario 2: lag-decay calibration of decoded states. Use the Viterbi or posterior-mean state sequences, bin pairs by elapsed time, and estimate the correlation between states at lag \(\Delta\). Under the fitted OU process, the theoretical autocorrelation is \(\exp(-\theta\Delta)\). With \(\hat\theta=0.042\ \mathrm{min}^{-1}\), the predicted correlations are approximately 0.996 at 0.1 minutes, 0.939 at 1.5 minutes, 0.533 at 15 minutes, and 0.284 at 30 minutes. Verifiable prediction: empirical state correlation should decline monotonically with \(\Delta\) and should fall near the OU curve, with the 15-minute bin roughly between 0.25 and 0.75. Veto condition: if the empirical correlation is flat, increases with lag, or remains above 0.75 at 15 minutes, the fitted state process is not behaving like the claimed OU process; if it collapses to near zero at very short lags where the model predicts near-perfect persistence, the decoding or model specification is vetoed.

Veto scenario 3: set-position stratification and composition control. Refit the model separately on first free throws only, and on second or third free throws only, keeping player intercepts. The full model already includes \(\mathrm{ft2}\) and \(\mathrm{ft3}\), whose estimates are \(\hat\beta_4=0.223\) \([0.192;0.254]\) and \(\hat\beta_5=0.421\) \([0.279;0.563]\) [PAGE 10 of 17], and the paper warns that the third-throw effect is likely composition-driven [PAGE 12 of 17]. Verifiable prediction: if the latent hot-hand process is real rather than a set-position artifact, the first-throw-only fit should still produce a positive \(\theta\), though with wider uncertainty, and the second/third-throw fit should not be the sole source of the full-sample signal. Veto condition: if \(\theta\) collapses to zero or becomes negative in the first-throw stratum while the full-sample \(\theta\) is positive, and if the second/third stratum explains the entire effect, then the apparent hot hand is a composition and selection artifact rather than serial dependence in success probabilities.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
