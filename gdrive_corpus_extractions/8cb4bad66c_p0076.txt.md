The lineage begins with Abraham de Moivre's discovery of the Normal Curve in the context of binomial probabilities, followed by Carl Friedrich Gauss and Pierre-Simon Laplace, who rigorously established the law of error in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. For mathematicians and astronomers, the Normal Law was a triumphant method to isolate "truth" (planetary orbits) from "noise" (observational mistakes). They sought to eliminate error to find certainty.

Francis Galton, however, inverted this objective entirely. Working in the shadow of Charles Darwin, Galton was not trying to eliminate the variability he observed in pea seeds and human stature; rather, the variability *was* the object of study. He needed a language to describe how parental traits were transmitted to offspring without strict uniformity. As Galton explicitly notes, "mathematicians laboured at the law of Error for one set of purposes, and we are entering into the fruits of their labours for another" [PAGE 57]. He borrowed the elegant mathematical machinery of Gauss—the PDF, the standard deviation, and the median deviation (Probable Error)—but stripped it of its epistemic baggage regarding "mistakes." 

A multi-week trap—or rather, a multi-century trap—in this genealogy is the persistence of the term "Probable Error." Because it retained the word "Error," generations of early biometricians and statisticians struggled to internalize that biological variance is a feature, not a bug. Furthermore, by mapping biological inheritance onto the Normal Curve, early biometry inadvertently created a trap for Mendelian genetics. If inheritance follows the continuous, bell-shaped distribution of blended averages (as modeled by the Normal Law), how could discrete, particulate alleles exist without being instantly washed out? The conflict between the continuous variability described by Galton's Normal Curve and the discrete jumps of Mendel remained a massive blind spot until the advent of quantitative genetics in the early 20th century, which eventually reconciled polygenic traits with Mendelian laws using the very variance equations Galton pioneered.
