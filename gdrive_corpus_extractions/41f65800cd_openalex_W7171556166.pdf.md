The source is not a match-prediction product; it is a prospective evaluation protocol whose central object is a timestamped forecast record tied to an unresolved event. The first derivation hop is target definition: for match \(i\), the realized class is \(Y_i \in \mathcal{C}=\{H,D,A\}\), where the paper defines the outcome “after 90 min plus stoppage time” and explicitly excludes “extra time and penalty shootouts” [PAGE 3]. This matters because a knockout match can be drawn after 90 minutes while one team advances; the paper separates 90-minute probabilities \(p_i=(p_{i,H},p_{i,D},p_{i,A})\in\Delta^2\) from advancement probabilities \(a_{i,H}+a_{i,A}=1\) [PAGE 3]. Any audit that mixes regulation and advancement is evaluating a different random variable.

The second hop is scoring. The primary metric is the unscaled multiclass Brier score \(BS_i=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(p_{i,c}-y_{i,c})^2\), with log loss \(LL_i=-\log p_{i,Y_i}\) [PAGE 6]. The Brier score is unitless and bounded: zero is perfect, two is worst, and a uniform forecast gives \(2/3\) [PAGE 6]. Log loss is in nats under natural log and has a singularity at \(p_{i,Y_i}=0\). This is a real breakdown regime, not a rhetorical one: a model that assigns zero probability to the realized class receives infinite log loss, while Brier remains finite. The paper’s modal accuracy rule also matters: if \(k\) outcomes share the maximum probability, “each receives credit \(1/k\)” [PAGE 6].

The third hop is design. The factorial set is \(Z=\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{H}\), \(z=(m,a,p,h)\in Z\) [PAGE 5]. The World Cup main panel is \(104\times 7\times 3\times 2\times 2=8,736\) match forecasts, with each model contributing 1,248 forecasts [PAGE 6]. This balanced panel permits within-match paired contrasts: the same match, same prompt information, same horizon, and same response schema are held fixed while one factor changes. The paper’s information-access contrast is the clearest example: the closed-book mean Brier falls from 0.535 to 0.512 open-book, a paired closed-minus-open difference of 0.0228, 95% CI [0.0044,0.0403], Holm-adjusted \(p=0.045\) [PAGE 6]. But the treatment is assigned access, not observed search. The paper reports that “not all LLMs with enabling web search eventually use it” and that search is observed in only 84.5% of open-book forecasts, with model-specific rates from 48.4% to 100% [PAGE 7]. Thus the 0.0228 estimate is an intent-to-treat contrast, not a pure search-use effect. A gray-zone audit must not relabel it as “search caused 0.0228 Brier improvement.”

The fourth hop is horizon. The paper reports that “The mean Brier score decreases by 0.0054 from stage opening to T–24h and by 0.0021 from T–24h to T–2h” [PAGE 7]. So recency helps only slightly, and the clock does not rescue weak forecasts. Closed-book forecasts “change little across the same horizons” [PAGE 7]. This is a multi-hop finding: web access is a more important source of variation than model identity, but forecast timing is not a large lever.

The fifth hop is model diversity. The seven models produce “highly correlated H/D/A probabilities, with a mean pairwise correlation of 0.943” and mean Jensen–Shannon divergence “only 0.0044” [PAGE 6]. The equal-weight ensemble improves on the average member by only 0.0047 Brier [PAGE 6]. Under Brier loss, that gain is the nonnegative disagreement term \( \frac{1}{M}\sum_m \lVert p_m-\bar p\rVert_2^2 \), so low disagreement mechanically yields low ensemble gain [PAGE 16]. The paper concludes: “The leaderboard shows no clear winner” [PAGE 6]. None of the 21 paired model comparisons remains significant after Holm correction; the smallest adjusted \(p=0.055\) for GPT versus Mistral [PAGE 6].

The sixth hop is tournament coherence. The 12 group-winner questions have 91.1% correct final selections, but all 28 configurations select Portugal for Group K, which Colombia wins [PAGE 21]. The semifinalist question has exactly 10 of 28 exact four-team recoveries and a mean of 3.29 correct teams [PAGE 21]. More importantly, the marginal semifinal probabilities should sum to four, but “several forecasts violate this requirement” [PAGE 21], and the sum audit is reported without retrospective normalization [PAGE 22]. That is a coherence veto: final-set recovery can be scored even when the marginal probability vector is not admissible.

The seventh hop is calibration and confidence. The paper’s calibration average shows mean draw probability 24.7% versus an observed draw rate of 27.9%, a predicted-minus-observed difference of \(-3.2\) percentage points, 95% CI [\(-12.5,4.9\)], \(p=0.461\) [PAGE 20]. Confidence is not a probability of correctness: “Confidence is the model’s overall self assessment and is not treated as a probability that the selected outcome is correct” [PAGE 4]. Within-condition confidence ranking does associate with accuracy: Brier falls from 0.620 to 0.423 and modal accuracy rises from 49.8% to 73.1% from lowest to highest group [PAGE 8]. That is a diagnostic association, not calibration.

The gray-zone audit therefore has several hard failure modes: temporal leakage if a forecast is recorded after kickoff or after outcome resolution; simplex failure if probabilities do not sum to one beyond tolerance; log-loss singularity if the realized class receives zero probability; tournament marginal failure if semifinal probabilities do not sum to four; endpoint drift if the canonical model version changes; and physical/weather event changes that alter the outcome random variable. The source’s own barrier is explicit: “Official outcomes enter the benchmark only after the corresponding forecasts have been stored” and “This step acts as a barrier that prevents known outcomes from affecting the recorded forecasts” [PAGE 4]. Any audit that ignores that barrier is not auditing this benchmark.

Match target and simplex. For match \(i\), \(Y_i\in\mathcal{C}=\{H,D,A\}\), and the model reports \(p_i=(p_{i,H},p_{i,D},p_{i,A})\in\Delta^2\), where \(\Delta^2=\{p:p_{i,c}\ge 0,\ \sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}p_{i,c}=1\}\) [PAGE 3]. Probabilities are dimensionless. For knockout matches, advancement probabilities satisfy \(a_{i,H}+a_{i,A}=1\) [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if the forecast is evaluated against a target that includes extra time or penalties, the random variable is misdefined.

Scoring. The unscaled multiclass Brier score is
\[
BS_i=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(p_{i,c}-y_{i,c}\right)^2,
\]
unitless, \(BS_i\in[0,2]\), with uniform forecast value \(2/3\) [PAGE 6]. Log loss is
\[
LL_i=-\log p_{i,Y_i},
\]
in nats under natural log [PAGE 6]. Singularity: \(LL_i\to\infty\) as \(p_{i,Y_i}\to0^+\); exact \(p_{i,Y_i}=0\) is an infinite-loss breakdown. Brier remains finite at that point.

Accuracy and graded scoreline. Modal H/D/A accuracy gives credit \(1/k\) when \(k\) outcomes share the maximum probability [PAGE 6]. Exact-score accuracy is the indicator that the reported scoreline equals the realized 90-minute score [PAGE 6]. The Scoring System is five points for exact score, two for correct goal difference, one for correct H/D/A tendency, and zero otherwise [PAGE 6]; units are points per match.

Factorial design. The configuration space is
\[
Z=\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{H},\qquad z=(m,a,p,h)\in Z,
\]
where \(\mathcal{M}\), \(\mathcal{A}\), \(\mathcal{P}\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) are model versions, information access, prompting strategy, and forecast horizon [PAGE 5]. The main panel is \(104\times7\times3\times2\times2=8,736\) match forecasts [PAGE 6]. Breakdown: if a factor is changed mid-run without recording the canonical endpoint, the within-match pairing is confounded.

Forecast lead time. Let \(t_{\mathrm{kick}}\) be scheduled kickoff and \(t_{\mathrm{req}}\) the actual request time. The realized lead time is \(L_i=t_{\mathrm{kick}}-t_{\mathrm{req}}\), in hours [PAGE 4]. The scheduler checks every 15 minutes [PAGE 4]. Temporal veto: if \(L_i\le0\), the forecast is at or after kickoff and cannot be a pre-event forecast. The source stores both scheduled and actual request timestamps [PAGE 4].

Tournament questions. For a one-team question \(q\), the model reports \(\pi_q\) over candidate set \(\mathcal{T}_q\), with \(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_q}\pi_{q,j}=1\) and one final selection [PAGE 4]. For a fixed-\(k_q\) question, the model reports marginals \(\rho_{q,j}\in[0,1]\) with
\[
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_q}\rho_{q,j}=k_q,
\]
and exactly \(k_q\) final selections [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: the paper reports that “several forecasts violate this requirement” for the semifinalist sum [PAGE 21]. The required sum is four [PAGE 22]. Such violations make marginal probability scoring inadmissible unless explicitly audited.

Calibration audit. For bins \(b\), a standard reliability audit uses
\[
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}\left|\bar p_b-\bar y_b\right|,
\]
unitless, where \(\bar p_b\) is mean predicted probability and \(\bar y_b\) is observed frequency. The source reports reliability curves and wide intervals; mean draw probability is 24.7% versus observed 27.9%, difference \(-3.2\) percentage points, 95% CI [\(-12.5,4.9\)], \(p=0.461\) [PAGE 20]. Breakdown: ECE is diagnostic, not a proper scoring rule; do not substitute it for Brier or log loss.

Forecast diversity. For two probability vectors \(P,Q\), Jensen–Shannon divergence is
\[
\mathrm{JSD}(P\Vert Q)=\tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert M)+\tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\Vert M),\quad M=\tfrac12(P+Q),
\]
in nats. The source reports mean pairwise JSD 0.0044 and mean pairwise correlation 0.943 [PAGE 6]; the appendix gives mean pairwise JSD 0.00442 nats and range 0.00162 to 0.00834 nats [PAGE 16]. Breakdown: correlation and JSD are similarity diagnostics, not accuracy metrics.

Ensemble decomposition. For \(M\) forecasters with mean \(\bar p=(1/M)\sum_m p_m\),
\[
BS(\bar p,y)=\frac1M\sum_m BS(p_m,y)-\frac1M\sum_m\lVert p_m-\bar p\rVert_2^2.
\]
The second term is nonnegative disagreement. The paper reports ensemble improvement of 0.0047 Brier over the average member [PAGE 6] and identifies this as the disagreement term under Brier loss [PAGE 16]. Units are Brier-score units squared? The Brier score itself is unitless; the squared Euclidean term is also unitless.

The primary T–24h closed-minus-open Brier contrast is \(0.0228\), 95% CI [\(0.0044,0.0403\)], Holm-adjusted \(p=0.045\), corresponding to a 4.3% reduction from the closed-book mean [PAGE 6]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from proper scoring and parametric soccer models to live LLM benchmarks. Brier’s 1950 “Verification of Forecasts Expressed in Terms of Probability” is the scoring ancestor [PAGE 9]. Gneiting and Raftery’s 2007 work on strictly proper scoring rules supplies the decision-theoretic justification for evaluating full probability vectors rather than only the modal pick [PAGE 9]. Soccer-specific ancestors include Maher’s 1982 Poisson model [PAGE 9] and Dixon and Coles’s 1997 model of association-football scores and market inefficiencies [PAGE 9]. Modern tournament forecasting also uses regularized Poisson and hybrid random-forest approaches [PAGE 9]. The benchmark inherits these traditions but deliberately does not provide curated domain features; it asks whether general-purpose LLMs can retrieve and synthesize evidence under uncertainty [PAGE 4].

The LLM-forecasting lineage is explicitly retrocausal in its problem statement. Static benchmarks are “typically static and retrospective” and can reward memorization or benchmark familiarity rather than prospective forecasting [PAGE 2]. The paper notes that “asking a model to forecast from a historical date does not ensure that it ignores ex ante knowledge acquired after that date” [PAGE 1]. Autocast and Autocast++ reconstruct historical information using time-indexed news and retrieval, but such reconstruction “cannot fully prevent information leakage from post-forecast knowledge already encoded in the model” [PAGE 3]. ForecastBench, Prophet Arena, and TS-Arena move toward live unresolved events, but the paper argues that “continuous prospective evaluation remains difficult because existing benchmarks are constrained by the limited availability of standardized unresolved events” [PAGE 2]. LLM-SoccerArena’s contribution is a recurring, standardized stream of soccer events with an outcome barrier [PAGE 4].

Multi-week traps follow directly from this genealogy. First, benchmark aging: a live benchmark can become contaminated if forecasts, outcomes, or rationales leak into later model training. The paper’s answer is prospective registration and an outcome barrier [PAGE 4], but the audit must still check timestamps. Second, model-endpoint drift: the paper records configured and canonical frozen versions, yet public-weight verification is “unverified” for every exact deployed endpoint [PAGE 12]. A multi-week run that silently changes the endpoint is a confounded longitudinal study. Third, operational shocks: Claude Fable 5 has partial coverage because “data collection was temporarily paused due to U.S. sanctions” [PAGE 12]. That is a real missingness mechanism, not random noise. Fourth, stage-sample instability: the paper warns that “Cumulative Brier score and modal accuracy are volatile early and stabilize as matches accumulate” [PAGE 15], and late-stage estimates have very small \(n\). Fifth, search-tool drift: observed search rates vary by model, and a model may retrieve “archival or otherwise weakly relevant sources (e.g., arxiv.org)” [PAGE 8]. A multi-week leaderboard can therefore move because the retrieval environment changed, not because forecasting improved. Sixth, annotation drift: rationale labels use a frozen annotator and cached raw responses because temperature zero does not guarantee identical regeneration [PAGE 22]. Any re-annotation after weeks is a new measurement instrument.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory-of-mind here is not a claim that the models have beliefs; it is an audit of nested prediction. Order-0 is a team-strength prior. Order-1 is a model predicting opponent behavior from form, injuries, and tactics. Order-2 is a model predicting what other forecasters, the evaluator, or the public consensus will expect. The paper’s diversity results show order-2 convergence: mean pairwise correlation is 0.943 and mean Jensen–Shannon divergence is only 0.0044 [PAGE 6]. The models are not producing independent forecasts; they are producing largely overlapping signals from the same public information. The equal-weight ensemble improves by only 0.0047 Brier [PAGE 6], exactly because the disagreement term is small [PAGE 16]. In recursive terms, the models are modeling the same consensus and collapsing toward it.

Crisis habit regression appears when uncertainty is high. Under closed-book conditions, the model has no fresh evidence and falls back on generic football priors: modal scorelines, favorite bias, and clichéd rationales. The paper’s evidence-category analysis shows that open-book rationales mention recent form 68.0 percentage points more often and injuries or lineups 53.0 percentage points more often, while generic unsupported claims occur 18.1 percentage points less often [PAGE 7]. That is the habit-regression signature: without retrieval, the model produces more unsupported assertions. The prompting experiment adds a representational split: probabilistic prompting produces 3.98 percentage points more score-implied draws and 4.05 points more exact 1–1 predictions, while the mean reported draw probability is 0.21 percentage points lower [PAGE 7]. The scoreline mode and the probability mode disagree. That is not necessarily invalid, because one exact scoreline can be most likely even when another aggregate outcome has the highest total probability [PAGE 19], but it is a crisis habit: the model’s narrative and its distribution are not the same object.

The counter-trap is overcorrection to retrieved evidence. Open-book access helps on average, but the model can anchor on recent form, injury news, or weakly relevant sources. The paper notes that models sometimes retrieve “archival or otherwise weakly relevant sources (e.g., arxiv.org)” [PAGE 8]. A recursive forecaster that treats a retrieved snippet as decisive can become more confident without becoming better calibrated. The paper’s confidence result is the warning: higher relative confidence is associated with lower Brier and higher accuracy, but confidence “is not a calibrated probability of correctness” [PAGE 4]. The counter-trap is to mistake fluency and retrieval volume for evidential weight.

The clock is the forecast horizon. Stage opening to T–24h improves Brier by 0.0054; T–24h to T–2h improves by only 0.0021 [PAGE 7]. Closed-book forecasts change little across horizons [PAGE 7]. So the clock does not create a crisis-resolving information shock. A model that is poorly calibrated at stage opening is likely still poorly calibrated two hours before kickoff. The recursive lesson is that information access matters more than last-minute timing, but only if the model actually uses search and integrates it. The paper’s own phrase is direct: “not all LLMs with enabling web search eventually use it” [PAGE 7]. The theory-of-mind trap is assuming that a model with a search tool is a model that has searched, and that a model that has searched is a model that has weighed the evidence correctly.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer vetoes must be adjudicated before any Brier or log-loss comparison. The benchmark’s registered event contains a unique event identifier, teams, stage, scheduled time, available venue information, and the rule used to resolve the outcome [PAGE 4]. The prompt itself contains only basic event information; the paper states: “We deliberately do not provide curated historical results” [PAGE 4]. Therefore physical, biomechanical, and weather variables are not controlled inputs in the primary factorial design. They can enter only through model priors or retrieved evidence. That is the key identification point.

Let \(C_i=(R_i,D_i,W_i,I_i,V_i)\) denote rest days (days), travel distance (km), weather state \(W_i\) (WBGT in °C, precipitation in mm/h, wind in m/s), injury/suspension set \(I_i\), and venue/pitch state \(V_i\). The benchmark prompt does not include \(C_i\) as a curated block [PAGE 4]. If \(C_i\) is not in the prompt and not in the frozen search trace before \(t_{\mathrm{req}}\), then the forecast \(p_i=f(X_i)\) is not identified as a function of \(C_i\). Any claim that weather or biomechanics caused a Brier change is therefore unsupported. If \(C_i\) is present in the trace, it is an evidence mention, not a manipulated treatment; the paper warns that rationales “do not reveal private model reasoning or establish which information causally influenced a forecast” [PAGE 8].

Weather veto proof. Suppose a weather event after the forecast changes the venue, postpones the match, abandons the match, or changes the official outcome-resolution rule. The benchmark requires one official 90-minute outcome for every completed match [PAGE 11]. If the event identity or resolution rule changes, the realized \(Y_i\) is not the same random variable that the forecast targeted. Scoring \(p_i\) against a different \(Y_i\) is not a proper scoring-rule evaluation. Therefore the forecast is censored from the primary panel. The same veto applies if a match is replayed or if the official 90-minute result is unavailable. No imputation is permitted; the paper’s main analysis uses the balanced panel “without imputation” [PAGE 11].

Biomechanical veto proof. Injury, suspension, and lineup changes are information arrivals, not controlled physical treatments. The paper reports that open-book rationales mention injuries or lineups 53.0 percentage points more often than closed-book rationales [PAGE 7], but that is a text-prevalence result. It does not show that the injury information changed the probability vector. If a biomechanical claim is made, the audit must require a timestamped trace before \(t_{\mathrm{req}}\) and a recorded prompt or search snippet. If the claim relies on post-forecast information, the forecast is retrospectively contaminated. If the claim relies on a model’s internal prior, it is not a cross-layer physical effect at all.

Physical-layer veto proof. The prompt’s input \(X_i\) is deliberately simple: competition, edition, stage, UTC kickoff, teams, venue, and knockout status [PAGE 4]. Because \(X_i\) does not contain rest, travel, weather, or injury covariates, two matches with identical \(X_i\) but different \(C_i\) receive the same registered prompt. Any difference in forecast quality between those matches cannot be assigned to \(C_i\) without an observed retrieval trace. This is an identification veto, not a judgment about soccer. The source’s own limitation is compatible: the rationales are “generated text” and do not establish causal information use [PAGE 8].

Exact breakdown regimes for cross-layer adjudication are: \(t_{\mathrm{req}}\ge t_{\mathrm{kick}}\); event identity or venue change after registration; outcome-resolution rule change; no official 90-minute outcome; physical covariate absent from prompt and trace; search-assigned but no observed search; canonical endpoint hash change; and probability-simplex violation or tournament-marginal violation. In any of these regimes, the primary Brier and log-loss comparison must be vetoed or explicitly downgraded to a sensitivity analysis. The paper’s own prospective barrier is the anchor: “Official outcomes enter the benchmark only after the corresponding forecasts have been stored” [PAGE 4].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
