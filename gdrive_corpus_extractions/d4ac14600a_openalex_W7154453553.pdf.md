<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are trajectory differencing, EMA smoothing, e-ball hypergraph construction, hypergraph convolution, SSM state transition, HyperSSM fusion, and a two-term trajectory loss. Units are as follows: box positions are in pixels; velocities are in pixels per frame; embeddings \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\) are dimensionless learned representation units; \(\theta\) is a threshold in the same norm units as the embedding distance; HOTA, IDF1, MOTA, AssA and DetA are percentages; parameter counts are millions; speed is frames per second. The source says “a distance threshold \(\theta\) is used to construct an \(e\)-ball around each motion feature, which will serve as a hyperedge” [PAGE 4].

Trajectory and EMA operator [PAGE 4]:
\[
v_t=p_t-p_{t-1},\quad t=2,\ldots,L,
\]
\[
\hat v=\sum_{t=1}^{L}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{L-t}v_t.
\]
Here \(p_t=(x,y,w,h)\) is the box at frame \(t\), \(v_t\in\mathbb{R}^4\) is the frame-to-frame displacement, \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) is dimensionless decay, and \(L\) is the sliding-window length. The source says “we apply an exponential moving average (EMA) weighting scheme” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regimes: \(\alpha=0\) annihilates all motion evidence; \(\alpha=1\) collapses to the last displacement only, with \(0^0\) ambiguity at \(t=L\); large \(\alpha\) increases noise sensitivity; small \(\alpha\) over-smooths nonlinear motion.

Hyperedge construction [PAGE 4]:
\[
ball(v,e)=\{u\in V\mid \|x_u-x_v\|_d&lt;\theta\},\quad
\mathcal{E}=\{ball(v,e)\mid v\in V\}.
\]
The incidence matrix is \(H\in\{0,1\}^{|V|\times|E|}\), with \(H_{v,e}=1\) if vertex \(v\) belongs to hyperedge \(e\). The source states that “a hypergraph allows a single hyperedge to connect multiple nodes, thereby capturing complex and high-order relationships” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: \(\theta\to 0\) produces near-empty or self-only hyperedges and destroys collaboration; \(\theta\to\infty\) produces a single giant hyperedge and identity blending. The source’s empirical tail is explicit: at \(\theta=0.5\), HOTA is 47.7; at \(\theta=0.8\), HOTA is 65.5; at \(\theta=0.98\), HOTA is 64.7 [PAGE 8]. The source’s explanation is “a smaller threshold introduces excessive associations, while a larger threshold results in too few connections” [PAGE 8].

Hypergraph convolution, matrix form [PAGE 4]:
\[
\operatorname{HConv}(X)=\sigma\left(D_v^{-1}HWD_e^{-1}H^{T}X_v\Theta\right).
\]
Here \(X_v\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d}\) is the vertex feature matrix, \(W\) is a diagonal edge-weight matrix, \(D_v\) and \(D_e\) are diagonal vertex-degree and edge-degree matrices, \(H^{T}\) is the incidence transpose, \(\Theta\) is a learnable parameter matrix, and \(\sigma\) is a nonlinear activation. The source defines \(H\) as “the \(|v|\times|e|\) incidence matrix of the hypergraph” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: if a vertex is isolated, its vertex degree is zero and \(D_v^{-1}\) is singular; if a hyperedge is empty, its edge degree is zero and \(D_e^{-1}\) is singular; if \(W\) has zero diagonal entries, the corresponding edge contribution vanishes. A residual path avoids total loss only for vertices that retain a self connection.

Gather and scatter form [PAGE 4]:
\[
x_e=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_v(e)|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{N}_v(e)}x_v\Theta,
\]
\[
x'_v=x_v+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_e(v)|}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{N}_e(v)}x_e.
\]
Here \(\mathcal{N}_v(e)\) is the set of vertices in edge \(e\), and \(\mathcal{N}_e(v)\) is the set of edges incident to vertex \(v\). Breakdown regimes: empty \(\mathcal{N}_v(e)\) makes \(x_e\) undefined; empty \(\mathcal{N}_e(v)\) leaves \(x'_v=x_v\), so an isolated vertex receives no collaboration. The source’s Figure 1 describes “Gathering” and “Scattering” as the two-stage collaborative mechanism [PAGE 1].

SSM transition [PAGE 3]:
\[
h_t=Ah_{t-1}+Bx_t,\quad y_t=Ch_t+Dx_t.
\]
Here \(h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{N_h}\) is the hidden state, \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{N_h\times N_h}\) is the transition matrix, \(B\) maps input to state, \(C\) maps state to output, and \(D\) is the skip term. The source says \(N\) denotes “the dimension of the hidden state” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: if the spectral radius satisfies \(\rho(A)>1\), the recurrence can diverge; if \(\rho(A)&lt;1\), long-range memory decays; if \(B=0\), input is ignored; if \(C=0\), the state has no output path. Discretization by zero-order hold or Euler is assumed, as the source notes that \(A\) and \(B\) are “typically discretized from continuous parameters using zero-order hold (ZOH) or Euler discretization rules” [PAGE 3].

HyperSSM fusion [PAGE 5]:
\[
h_t=Ah_{t-1}+B\cdot\operatorname{HConv}(X_t),
\]
\[
Y_t=X_t+Ch_t+\operatorname{HConv}(X_t).
\]
The source states “The operation can be formalized as” this pair, and that the design removes “the redundant \(D\) parameter” while adding a residual connection [PAGE 4–5]. Breakdown regimes: the residual \(X_t+\operatorname{HConv}(X_t)\) requires \(\operatorname{HConv}(X_t)\) to preserve the feature dimension of \(X_t\); otherwise the addition is ill-defined. If \(\operatorname{HConv}\) collapses to an identity because all hyperedges are singletons, HyperSSM reduces to an SSM plus residual. If hyperedges over-merge, the same residual can propagate consensus bias into every vertex.

Training loss [PAGE 5]:
\[
\mathcal{L}=\lambda_1\sum_t \mathcal{L}_{L1}(P_t,P_{gt}^t)+\lambda_2\sum_t \mathcal{L}_{GIoU}(P_t,P_{gt}^t).
\]
Here \(P_t\) is predicted box state, \(P_{gt}^t\) is ground truth, \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) are dimensionless weights, and \(L\) is the sliding-window length. The source says it uses “the smooth L1 loss and generalized-IoU” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: \(\lambda_1=0\) removes coordinate supervision; \(\lambda_2=0\) removes overlap supervision; degenerate boxes with zero area make IoU-based terms numerically delicate; an empty or truncated window makes the sum incomplete.

The source does not provide formal equations for HOTA, IDF1, MOTA, AssA or DetA; it reports them as percentages in Tables 1–4 [PAGE 6–7]. The source also does not include any operator for physical contact, biomechanics, weather, illumination, or detector uncertainty. The input is explicitly trajectory-based: “trajectory segments—rather than images—are used for training” [PAGE 6], and “The input consists of position and motion data derived from 5-frame trajectory segments” [PAGE 6]. Therefore any physical or weather veto must be external to the stated architecture.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal genealogy runs from constant-velocity Kalman filtering through SORT-family trackers, then to learnable motion predictors, then to SSM-based predictors, then to hypergraph-collaborative SSM. The source positions itself against both query-based and motion-based ancestors. Query-based methods regress object positions with persistent queries, while motion-based methods explicitly estimate displacement and associate detections. The source says motion-based methods “decouple detection and association” [PAGE 2] and that Kalman filters “assume constant-velocity linear motion” [PAGE 3]. That lineage explains the paper’s central contrast: Kalman is stable in linear MOT17/MOT20, but “deteriorate sharply in nonlinear cases such as dancing or sports” [PAGE 3]. The source’s own ablation confirms the ancestry: “Kalman series excels in MOT17 due to its constant velocity assumption” [PAGE 7], while HyperSSM “delivers substantially larger improvements in non-linear patterns” [PAGE 7].

The graph-learning branch is also explicit. The source notes that prior graph MOT methods “model objects across frames as nodes, while edges represent potential associations between detections” [PAGE 3], but criticizes them because they “still track each object separately without considering relationships among different targets” [PAGE 3]. Hypergraph ancestry enters through hypergraph neural computation: the source cites hypergraph construction and convolution, and defines incidence-matrix propagation through \(D_v^{-1}HWD_e^{-1}H^{T}\) [PAGE 3–4]. The SSM branch enters through S4-style recurrences and the claim that SSMs “have demonstrated strong capability in modeling long-range dependencies within sequential data” [PAGE 3]. HyperSSM’s novelty is therefore a splice: hypergraph spatial consensus inside an SSM temporal recurrence.

The multi-week trap is confusing ancestry with proof. First trap: treating the private-protocol leaderboard numbers in Tables 1–4 as clean module evidence. They are not; they include detector, association, initialization, and termination choices. The cleaner evidence is Table 5, where “The detection and association strategies are fixed across all methods” [PAGE 7]. Second trap: training-set mixing. The source says “we merge the MOT17 and MOT20 training sets to train our model” [PAGE 6], while MOT17 ablation uses train-half and val-half [PAGE 6]. A reader who compares the wrong rows will overstate or understate transfer. Third trap: hyperparameter retrofitting. The \(\theta\) sweep is sharp: 0.5 gives 47.7 HOTA and 0.8 gives 65.5 [PAGE 8]. A multi-week replication that tunes \(\theta\) on the test benchmark is not a replication; it is leakage. Fourth trap: efficiency protocol. The source warns that the efficiency results “serve as a reference for comparing model sizes and do not adhere to the same evaluation standards as other results” [PAGE 8]. Fifth trap: DanceTrack tie. HyperSSM and HybridSORT both report 65.7 HOTA in Table 3 [PAGE 7], so the claim must shift to IDF1, AssA, or nonlinear robustness rather than a unique HOTA win. Sixth trap: overreading “collaborative” as causal occlusion recovery; the source’s occlusion evidence is qualitative visualization in Figure 5 plus benchmark gains [PAGE 8], not a controlled occlusion intervention.

The historical lesson is that HyperSSM is a hybrid descendant, not a replacement for physics. Its Kalman ancestor supplies the linear-motion prior; its SSM ancestor supplies long-range temporal recurrence; its hypergraph ancestor supplies high-order spatial consensus. The paper’s strongest scientific contribution is the ablation showing that the hybrid beats both Kalman and plain SSM under fixed detection and association [PAGE 7]. The weakest inference is that the hybrid is universally superior; the source itself reports “comparable gains” on MOT17 and a large gain on DanceTrack [PAGE 7], which is exactly what a nonlinear-motion genealogy predicts.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when the tracker loses visual evidence. The habitual fallback is per-object constant velocity or an independent SSM. The source names the failure: “When a target becomes heavily occluded, motion estimation tends to fail, resulting in trajectory fragmentation or identity switches” [PAGE 2]. The paper’s counter-habit is to let correlated objects constrain one another: “Joint reasoning inherently stabilizes motion estimation” and “Cross-object motion transfer enhances occlusion resilience” [PAGE 2]. But the regression is not eliminated; it is displaced. If the hyperedge is empty, the gather-scatter operator leaves the vertex unchanged, so the model silently reverts to per-object prediction. If the SSM transition dominates, the model silently reverts to temporal extrapolation. If the detector is missing for longer than the 5-frame window, cross-object transfer has no clock to run on.

The counter-trap is over-collaboration. A tracker that has learned “peers help” can over-trust peers with similar velocity but different identity. The source’s \(\theta\) ablation is the signature: a smaller threshold introduces “excessive associations,” and a larger threshold gives “too few connections” [PAGE 8]. The best value is \(\theta=0.8\) on MOT17 [PAGE 8]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the model must infer not only another object’s motion but whether that object is a valid collaborator. In a crowd, two pedestrians moving in the same direction may be independent; in DanceTrack, dancers may share velocity while carrying different identities; in SportsMOT, athletes may converge and diverge under nonlinear acceleration. The model’s “mind” of the group is only as good as the hyperedge’s identity hypothesis.

The clock is the sliding window and the layer depth. The source trains on 5-frame trajectory segments [PAGE 6] and reports that “performance peaks at a segment length of 5 frames” [PAGE 7]. The hidden state “acts as a messenger, transmitting information between cascaded layers” [PAGE 5], and the paper selects 8 HyperSSM layers because gains diminish beyond that [PAGE 7]. The recursive clock has three horizons: the frame-to-frame velocity horizon, the 5-frame segment horizon, and the hidden-state horizon across 8 layers. Occlusion shorter than the segment horizon can be bridged; occlusion longer than it must be vetoed or suspended. A counter-trap is to let the hidden state “remember” through an occlusion beyond the evidence horizon; that produces confident but unsupported identity continuity. The correct recursive stance is: collaborate while the clock is valid, fall back when the clock expires, and never let the group prior override a strong detection contradiction.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The source’s loss is only smooth L1 and GIoU [PAGE 5], and its state transition is learned through \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) [PAGE 3–5]. There is no explicit constraint that a predicted box displacement remain below a maximum speed or that acceleration remain below a physical bound. Therefore a physical veto must be external. A formal adjudication is: let \(c_t=(x_t+w_t/2,y_t+h_t/2)\) be the box center in pixels, and let \(u_t=c_t-c_{t-1}\) be center velocity in pixels per frame. Veto the HyperSSM prediction if \(\|u_{t+1}\|_2>v_{\max}\Delta t\) or \(\|u_{t+1}-u_t\|_2>a_{\max}\Delta t^2\), where \(v_{\max}\) and \(a_{\max}\) are class-specific external bounds. The source cannot supply those bounds because it models boxes, not bodies. The source’s own acknowledgment that Kalman is strong in linear MOT17 and weak in nonlinear DanceTrack [PAGE 7] means the physical veto should route linear, low-acceleration cases to the simpler constant-velocity prior, and only route nonlinear cases to HyperSSM when the evidence supports it.

Biomechanical veto. The source’s state is \(p_t=(x,y,w,h)\) and its training input is “position and motion data” [PAGE 6]; no pose, joint, contact, or body-orientation variables appear in the equations. A biomechanical veto therefore cannot be computed from the paper’s operators. For pedestrians, dancers, and athletes, high acceleration may be legitimate or illegitimate depending on pose and contact. A practical adjudication is: if pose or keypoint evidence is unavailable, do not certify biomechanical plausibility; instead apply class-specific acceleration envelopes and suspend the track when the envelope is exceeded. If pose evidence is available, veto when predicted box motion implies a joint-angle or support-foot transition outside the class envelope. The source’s qualitative claims about “motion instability (C1)” and “occlusion robustness (C2)” [PAGE 8] are not biomechanical proofs; they are trajectory-smoothness and identity-continuity claims.

Weather veto. The source contains no weather variable. The equations depend on \(X_t\), \(H\), \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), and \(\Theta\); the loss depends on predicted and ground-truth boxes [PAGE 3–5]. Training uses trajectory segments rather than images [PAGE 6]. Therefore, absent detector input, the model is invariant to weather: changing rain, snow, glare, or fog without changing detections cannot change the motion prediction. That is a proof of absence, not a proof of robustness. A weather veto must be triggered by external evidence: detector confidence collapse, association cost explosion, or image-quality degradation. When weather veto is active, cross-object transfer is especially dangerous because the hyperedge may group objects only because all detections have drifted in the same camera-induced way. The correct adjudication is to suspend collaborative inference and fall back to a conservative prior until detections recover.

Cross-layer adjudication rule. The source’s architecture has no internal layer that can distinguish “consensus because the group is physically coherent” from “consensus because the group is similarly wrong.” Therefore the veto hierarchy must be: detection evidence first, physical envelope second, biomechanical envelope third, weather/illumination fourth, and HyperSSM consensus last. The paper’s fixed-detection ablation [PAGE 7] supports HyperSSM as a motion module, not as an evidence source. When a higher layer vetoes, HyperSSM must not be allowed to override it.
