The methodology then decomposes the task into a multi-hop pipeline: camera acquisition, segmentation, YOLOv8 bounding-box prediction, class selection, 3D point-cloud construction, SLAM/localization, costmap generation, global/local path planning, parallel-axis alignment with \(\Delta\phi \to 0\), IR-ray tracing inside a \(0.15\) m approach radius, four-step insertion, charging, and GSM/GPRS notification. The authors explicitly describe the near-field sequence: “As the ACR approaches the charging port within a specified range (0.15m), it initiates an infrared (IR) ray tracing process” [PAGE 7 of 24], and later state that the approach position is “located within a 0.15-meter radius around the charging port,” with velocity reduction to “15% of its maximum joint speed” and then “2% of its maximum” [PAGE 12 of 24]. This is not a single classifier claim; it is a cyber-physical control stack, so every detection error becomes a kinematic, force, and safety error.

The second hop is the formal vision layer. YOLOv8 predicts bounding boxes, class probabilities, and objectness scores; NMS filters boxes; ROI extraction isolates the port; Hough-circle feature points are matched by the Kuhn–Munkres algorithm using an inverse-distance weight, \(C_{ij}=k/\mathrm{dist}(P_i-P_j)\) [PAGE 9 of 24]. The homography step then maps planar points across views, with the paper stating that “a homography matrix, possessing 8 degrees of freedom, can be computed using four distinct points” [PAGE 10 of 24]. The third hop is the kinematic layer: the ACR is modeled as a 6-DOF arm with DH parameters and a Jacobian \(J=[J_a\;J_b]\), where the linear block entries are \(J_{a_n}=k_n(U_{Grip}-U_n)\) [PAGE 14 of 24]. The fourth hop is the outcome layer: the reported model has precision \(0.927\), recall \(0.947\), mAP50 \(0.930\), and mAP50-95 \(0.545\) [PAGE 15 of 24], while the conclusion says “a commendable accuracy rate of 93%” [PAGE 19 of 24]. The fifth hop is the deployment layer: a 12 V lead battery is used in the experiment, and the paper says “The charging process involved a lead battery with a capacity of 12V and 1.3A” [PAGE 15 of 24], which is dimensionally ambiguous because volts and amperes are not capacity; that is an audit flag, not a minor typo, because SOC estimation depends on capacity \(Q_N\).

The gray-zone audit is therefore not whether YOLOv8 works on the curated seven-class dataset. It is whether the full chain preserves validity when the detector is uncertain, the homography is degenerate, the IR channel is saturated, the cable is stiff, or the user is absent. Several source inconsistencies must be carried forward. First, the aggregate recall is \(0.947\) in the results narrative and Table 3 [PAGE 15 of 24; PAGE 16 of 24], but the SOTA table gives YOLOv8 recall \(0.898\) [PAGE 18 of 24]. Second, CHAdeMO recall is only \(0.750\) and Tesla(S) recall is \(0.882\) [PAGE 16 of 24], so class-specific risk is not summarized by the aggregate. Third, the simulation is reported as “Gazebo simulation software within the ROS 2 Humbel framework” [PAGE 18 of 24], with no contact-dynamics validation against a real EV port. Fourth, the homography derivation assumes “an absence of lens distortion” [PAGE 9 of 24], while real cameras have radial/tangential distortion. Fifth, the KM weight has a singularity at zero feature distance; the paper writes the inverse distance without a regularization term [PAGE 9 of 24]. Sixth, the IR alignment is a proximity logic, not a calibrated optical-power budget: direct sunlight, rain, fog, dust, and retroreflection are not quantified. The exact breakdown regimes are therefore: denominator \(h_7x_1+h_8y_1+h_9\to 0\) in the homography; \(\mathrm{dist}(P_i,P_j)\to 0\) in KM matching; \(\mathrm{rank}(J)<6\) or \(\det(JJ^T)\to 0\) in the arm Jacobian; \(p(\hat c)\to\tau\) or NMS IoU near threshold in detection; \(I_{rx}\to I_{th}\) or \(I_{bg}\to I_{sat}\) in IR alignment; and \(\delta_\perp + L\sin\theta \to c/2\) in insertion clearance. These are the real veto boundaries. The source text supports the pipeline but does not prove robustness across them.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s operators are best represented as a staged stochastic-control map rather than a single detector. Let \(I\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}\) be an RGB image with pixel dimensions \(H,W\) in pixels. YOLOv8 computes
\[
\hat{Y}=f_\theta(I)=\{(\mathbf{b}_i,\hat{c}_i,p_i)\}_{i=1}^{N},\qquad 
\mathbf{b}_i=(x_i,y_i,w_i,h_i)\in\mathbb{R}^4\ \text{pixels},\quad p_i\in[0,1],
\]
where \(\hat{c}_i\) is the class label and \(p_i\) is a dimensionless confidence. NMS retains boxes according to an IoU threshold \(\tau_{\mathrm{IoU}}\in[0,1]\). The paper states that “Non-maximum suppression (NMS) retains the most confident bounding boxes” [PAGE 9 of 24] and that YOLOv8 predicts “bounding box coordinates, class probabilities, and objectness score” [PAGE 2 of 24]. Assumption: the pre-trained YOLOv8 model and configuration files are correctly loaded. Breakdown regime: \(p_i<\tau_p\), heavy occlusion, class imbalance, or \(IoU\) near \(\tau_{\mathrm{IoU}}\); then the wrong gun class can be selected.

The reported detection metrics are
\[
P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad 
F1=\frac{2PR}{P+R},
\]
with \(TP,FP,FN\) dimensionless counts. The mean average precision is
\[
\mathrm{mAP@}\tau=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\int_0^1 P_c(R_c)\,dR_c,
\]
and mAP50-95 averages this over IoU thresholds \(0.50,0.55,\ldots,0.95\). The source reports “precision (P) at 0.927 and a recall (R) of 0.947, resulting in a high mean Average Precision (mAP50) score of 0.930 and a mAP50-95 of 0.545” [PAGE 15 of 24]. Units are dimensionless. Breakdown regime: high mAP50 with low mAP50-95 means localization is weak even when class presence is detected; for a peg-in-hole task, localization error is not cosmetic.

The KM feature-matching operator is written by the authors as
\[
C_{ij}=\frac{k}{\mathrm{dist}(P_i-P_j)},
\]
where \(P=(a_n,b_n,r_n)\) is a central coordinate and \(k\) is a parameter [PAGE 9 of 24]. To make \(C_{ij}\) dimensionless, \(k\) must carry the same unit as \(\mathrm{dist}\), e.g. metres if \(P\) is in metres; otherwise \(C_{ij}\) has inverse-length units. The paper does not state the unit convention. A safe regularized form is
\[
C_{ij}^{(\epsilon)}=\frac{k}{\|P_i-P_j\|_2+\epsilon},\qquad \epsilon>0,
\]
which prevents the exact singularity at \(P_i=P_j\). Breakdown regime: \(\|P_i-P_j\|\to0\) gives unbounded weight; outliers, repeated texture, or one-to-many correspondences can force a false assignment. The paper’s phrase “employing the inverse of distance as the algorithm’s weight” [PAGE 9 of 24] does not provide an outlier model or a rejection threshold.

The homography operator is
\[
\lambda
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\ y_1\\ 1
\end{bmatrix}
=
H
\begin{bmatrix}
x_2\\ y_2\\ 1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
H=
\begin{bmatrix}
h_1&h_2&h_3\\
h_4&h_5&h_6\\
h_7&h_8&h_9
\end{bmatrix},
\]
with \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\), \(\lambda\neq0\), and \(h_9\) normalized to 1 by “multiplying it with a non-zero factor” [PAGE 10 of 24]. The paper correctly notes that \(H\) “contains 8 DOF” and “can be computed using four distinct points” [PAGE 10 of 24]. Assumptions: planar target, no lens distortion, and non-collinear point correspondences. Breakdown regime: the denominator
\[
h_7x_1+h_8y_1+h_9\to0
\]
makes the projected coordinates diverge; four nearly collinear points make the linear system ill-conditioned; noisy point matches propagate into rotation and translation. The paper’s own assumption of “an absence of lens distortion” [PAGE 9 of 24] is a clear boundary condition, not a validated property of the physical camera.

The 6-DOF arm uses DH transforms
\[
T_i^{i-1}=
\begin{bmatrix}
R_i^{i-1}& \mathbf{t}_i^{i-1}\\
\mathbf{0}^T&1
\end{bmatrix}
\in SE(3),
\qquad
T_6^0=\prod_{i=1}^{6}T_i^{i-1},
\]
with revolute angles in radians and link lengths in metres; the paper gives the transforms explicitly in equations (8)–(13) [PAGE 13 of 24]. The Jacobian is
\[
J=[J_a\;J_b],\qquad J_{a_n}=k_n(U_{Grip}-U_n),
\]
where \(k_n\) is the 3-element joint axis, \(U_{Grip}\) is the end-effector position relative to the base, and \(U_n\) is the joint position relative to the base [PAGE 14 of 24]. For revolute joints, the linear block has units of metres per radian and the angular block is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: \(\mathrm{rank}(J)<6\), \(\det(JJ^T)\to0\), or the smallest singular value \(\sigma_{\min}(J)<\epsilon_J\). At such a singularity, inverse-kinematics commands can demand unbounded joint rates even for a small Cartesian correction.

For the IR near-field alignment, a physically meaningful operator is
\[
I_{rx}(d)=I_0e^{-\beta d},\qquad d\ \text{in m},\quad \beta\ \text{in m}^{-1},\quad I_{rx}\ \text{in W m}^{-2},
\]
with detection requiring \(I_{rx}\ge I_{th}\) and \(\mathrm{SNR}_{IR}\ge\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\). The paper’s near-field trigger is a “0.15-meter radius” [PAGE 12 of 24], and its velocity schedule is “15%” then “2%” of maximum joint speed [PAGE 12 of 24]. Breakdown regimes: \(I_0e^{-\beta d}<I_{th}\) (attenuation), \(I_{bg}>I_{sat}\) (saturation from sunlight or reflective metal), and multipath/retroreflection causing a false lock. The source does not report a radiant-power budget, receiver saturation point, or weather attenuation coefficient, so the IR claim is bounded to the tested indoor-like condition.

For SOC notification, the paper states: “Upon reaching a SOC of 100%, denoting the completion of the charging process, an automatic notification or message is dispatched to the user” [PAGE 14 of 24]. A formal coulomb-counting model is
\[
SOC(t)=SOC(t_0)+\frac{1}{Q_N}\int_{t_0}^{t}I(\tau)\,d\tau,
\]
where \(SOC\in[0,1]\), \(I\) is current in A, \(Q_N\) is nominal capacity in A·s (or Ah with consistent time units), and \(t\) is in s. Breakdown regimes: \(Q_N\) unknown or misreported, current-sensor bias, temperature-dependent capacity, voltage-plateau ambiguity, and the source’s own dimensional inconsistency: “a capacity of 12V and 1.3A” [PAGE 15 of 24] cannot be a capacity without an ampere-hour or joule specification. Thus the 100% notification is only as valid as the SOC estimator, and the paper does not provide an uncertainty bound.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is visible in the paper’s own justification. The authors write that “Historically, computer vision relied on manual feature engineering techniques like HOG and SIFT. However, the advent of CNNs and YOLOv8 signifies a shift towards more data-driven, adaptive solutions” [PAGE 2 of 24]. That lineage moves from handcrafted descriptors and Hough geometry to learned detectors, then to the YOLO family: YOLOv4, YOLOv5, YOLOv6, YOLOv7, and YOLOv8 are compared in the SOTA table [PAGE 18 of 24]. The robotics lineage is equally explicit: ROS is described as “a robust open-source framework developed collaboratively by Willow Garage and Stanford University” [PAGE 4 of 24], with packages for SLAM, navigation, MoveIt, tf/tf2, and Gazebo [PAGE 6 of 24]. The charging-standard lineage is bounded by IEC62196-3 and SAEJ1772/IEC62196-2 [PAGE 2 of 24]. AViTRoN is therefore a convergence of three genealogies: learned perception, ROS middleware, and EV connector standardization.

The retrocausal audit asks which future failures are already encoded in these design choices. The first multi-week trap is calibration drift. The homography derivation assumes a planar target and no lens distortion [PAGE 9 of 24], but a camera mounted on a vibrating robot will accumulate extrinsic drift over weeks; the result is a slowly growing angular error that is invisible in a single-frame mAP score. The second trap is dataset drift. The experiment uses “over 2000 images” from Roboflow, annotated into seven classes: Type1, Type2, CCS1, CCS2, CHAdeMO, GB/T, and TESLA Model(S) [PAGE 14 of 24]. A new connector variant or a dirty port not represented in that set can be confidently misclassified. The third trap is augmentation illusion. The authors apply cropping, shear \(\pm15^\circ\), hue \(-25^\circ\) to \(+25^\circ\), brightness \(-25\%\) to \(+25\%\), blur up to 2.5 pixels, noise up to 5% of pixels, and mosaic [PAGE 14 of 24]. These are image-space perturbations, not physical weather: rain droplets, road salt, ice, and direct solar loading change both optics and mechanics. The fourth trap is software and middleware drift. The simulation is reported under “ROS 2 Humbel” [PAGE 18 of 24], an apparent typo for Humble; dependency changes in ROS 2, MoveIt, OMPL, PCL, or the YOLO runtime can alter timing and message compatibility over weeks. The fifth trap is mechanical wear: repeated insertion changes clearance, chamfer geometry, and connector spring force. The paper mentions “unwieldy cables and low-light conditions” [PAGE 8 of 24] but does not model cable stiffness, connector wear, or insertion-force evolution. These are multi-week traps because each is small per trial and cumulative across trials.

The retrocausal genealogy therefore inverts the usual performance narrative. The 93% conclusion [PAGE 19 of 24] is a point-in-time result on a curated dataset, but the future failure surface is determined by the assumptions made earlier: planar homography, distortion-free optics, inverse-distance matching, clean IR channel, fixed class set, and a 12 V lead-battery test [PAGE 15 of 24]. The lineage is not wrong; it is narrow. The correct historical reading is that AViTRoN inherits the speed and flexibility of learned detection and ROS modularity, but it also inherits the fragility of unmodeled calibration, weather, and mechanical contact.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns three agents: the vehicle user, the ACR, and the charging operator/maintainer. Each maintains a belief about the others. The user believes the robot can find the port and notify at completion; the robot believes the user will return when notified; the operator believes the reported 93% accuracy applies to all classes and conditions. The source gives the notification anchor: “Upon reaching a SOC of 100%, denoting the completion of the charging process, an automatic notification or message is dispatched to the user” [PAGE 14 of 24]. That sentence creates a clock: the user’s return time is keyed to the SOC message. If the SOC estimate is early, the user arrives before charge completion; if late, the bay is occupied. If the message never arrives because GSM/GPRS fails, the user’s trust collapses.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under a charging fault, a user who has previously plugged manually may regress to manual operation rather than wait for the robot. Let \(T_w\) be wait time in seconds and \(b_t\in[0,1]\) be the user’s belief that the robot is reliable. A simple Bayesian trust update after a success \(s_t\) is
\[
b_{t+1}=
\frac{b_t\,\ell(s_t\mid R)}
{b_t\,\ell(s_t\mid R)+(1-b_t)\,\ell(s_t\mid \neg R)},
\]
where \(\ell(\cdot\mid R)\) and \(\ell(\cdot\mid\neg R)\) are likelihoods, both dimensionless. A crisis-regression trigger can be modeled as
\[
p_{\mathrm{manual}}(T_w)=1-\exp(-\lambda T_w),\qquad \lambda>0\ \text{s}^{-1},
\]
so manual override probability rises with waiting time. The system must detect this regression and hand off safely, not merely continue retrying.

The counter-trap is automation complacency. Because the paper reports high aggregate precision and recall [PAGE 15 of 24], the operator may ignore class-specific weaknesses: CHAdeMO recall \(0.750\), Tesla(S) recall \(0.882\), and CHAdeMO mAP50-95 \(0.419\) [PAGE 16 of 24]. The recursive belief “the robot is 93% accurate” is not the same as “the robot is safe for this port, this cable, this weather, and this user.” The counter-trap is that a confident detector can suppress the operator’s vigilance. The correct policy is to expose posterior entropy, class identity, and alignment residuals to the operator, and to veto insertion when \(p(\hat c)<\tau_p\) or when the IR/force residuals exceed limits.

The clock is the third element. The user’s mental clock is set by the SMS; the robot’s clock is set by the charging controller; the operator’s clock is set by maintenance intervals. If these clocks diverge, the system appears reliable in the lab but fails in the field. The recursive fix is a shared state model: the robot should publish not only “charging complete” but also confidence in SOC, port class, alignment, and insertion force. The user should receive an explicit “ready” or “fault/handoff” state, not an ambiguous completion event. The operator should see weekly drift statistics, not only a single mAP score. Without that shared model, the system’s own success message becomes the source of the next failure.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto must be stated as a proof obligation, not as a narrative. Let \(c=r_h-r_p\) be the radial clearance in metres between socket radius \(r_h\) and gun radius \(r_p\); let \(\delta_\perp\) be lateral misalignment in metres; let \(\theta\) be angular misalignment in radians; and let \(L_g\) be the inserted gun length in metres. A conservative geometric insertion condition is
\[
\delta_\perp+L_g|\sin\theta|\le \frac{c}{2}.
\]
If the left side exceeds \(c/2\), the connector contacts the socket face before full insertion. The paper’s near-field sequence reduces speed to 15% and then 2% of maximum [PAGE 12 of 24], but speed reduction does not increase clearance. The physical veto is
\[
V_{\mathrm{phys}}=
\mathbb{1}\!\left[
\delta_\perp+L_g|\sin\theta|>\frac{c}{2}
\;\lor\;
F_{\mathrm{insert}}>F_{\mathrm{allow}}
\;\lor\;
\sigma_{\mathrm{contact}}>\sigma_{\mathrm{allow}}
\right],
\]
where \(F_{\mathrm{insert}}\) and \(F_{\mathrm{allow}}\) are in newtons and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{contact}},\sigma_{\mathrm{allow}}\) are in pascals. A simple contact model is
\[
F_{\mathrm{insert}}=F_{\mathrm{friction}}+k_s\left(\delta_\perp-\frac{c}{2}\right)_+,
\]
with \(k_s\) in N m\(^{-1}\). The source provides no force sensor or insertion-force threshold; it provides only the 0.15 m IR trigger [PAGE 12 of 24]. Therefore the physical veto is not proven by the paper.

The biomechanical veto concerns cable stiffness, port height, and end-effector loading. Let \(T_{\mathrm{cable}}\) be cable tension in newtons, \(L_c\) the moment arm in metres, and \(M_{\mathrm{joint,allow}}\) the allowable joint moment in N·m. Then
\[
V_{\mathrm{bio}}=
\mathbb{1}\!\left[
T_{\mathrm{cable}}>T_{\mathrm{payload}}
\;\lor\;
T_{\mathrm{cable}}L_c>M_{\mathrm{joint,allow}}
\;\lor\;
h_{\mathrm{port}}\notin[h_{\min},h_{\max}]
\right].
\]
The paper acknowledges “unwieldy cables and low-light conditions” [PAGE 8 of 24], but it does not quantify cable tension or port-height variation. For a low port, the arm may need a posture near a kinematic singularity; for a stiff cable, the cable may generate a lateral force that the position-controlled arm cannot reject.

The weather veto is the strongest missing proof. The IR channel is modeled as a proximity aid, not a calibrated optical link. Let \(I_0\) be emitted irradiance in W m\(^{-2}\), \(\beta\) the extinction coefficient in m\(^{-1}\), \(d\) the range in m, and \(I_{th}\) the receiver threshold. Then
\[
V_{\mathrm{wx}}=
\mathbb{1}\!\left[
I_0e^{-\beta d}<I_{th}
\;\lor\;
I_{\mathrm{bg}}>I_{\mathrm{sat}}
\;\lor\;
V_{\mathrm{vis}}<V_{\min}
\;\lor\;
\beta_{\mathrm{rain}}d>\ln\!\left(\frac{I_0}{I_{th}}\right)
\right],
\]
with \(I_{\mathrm{bg}},I_{\mathrm{sat}},V_{\mathrm{vis}}\) in W m\(^{-2}\) or visibility metres as appropriate. Direct sunlight, wet retroreflective surfaces, fog, snow, and dust can either attenuate the signal or saturate the receiver. The paper’s four-step strategy uses IR “to ensure exact positioning” [PAGE 12 of 24], but it reports no outdoor weather trial, no solar-loading measurement, and no rain/fog attenuation. The experimental validation uses a 12 V lead battery [PAGE 15 of 24], not a full EV in weather. Therefore the weather veto is unresolved: a single \(I_{\mathrm{bg}}>I_{\mathrm{sat}}\) event can produce a false alignment lock even when the geometric clearance condition is satisfied.

Adjudication: autonomy is permitted only when \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}=0\), \(V_{\mathrm{bio}}=0\), and \(V_{\mathrm{wx}}=0\). If any veto is true, the correct action is not a faster insertion; it is re-scan, slow compliant search, or human handoff. The source supports the nominal indoor-like path but not the cross-layer proof.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Direct-sun IR saturation. Counterfactual: keep the same port, gun, and 0.15 m approach, but add a solar-like near-infrared background \(I_{\mathrm{bg}}\). Verifier: \(I_{\mathrm{rx}}+I_{\mathrm{bg}}>I_{\mathrm{sat}}\) or \(\mathrm{SNR}_{IR}<\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\). The source’s IR alignment is triggered inside “a 0.15-meter radius” [PAGE 12 of 24], but no saturation limit is given. Prediction: the IR receiver can lock onto background/reflection rather than the transmitter, so the four-step sequence proceeds to insertion with a false alignment; the measurable veto outcome is \(\delta_\perp+L_g|\sin\theta|>c/2\) at contact. Correct policy: veto insertion and re-scan with modulated IR or visual servoing. This is verifiable because the optical power and SNR are measurable before contact.

Scenario 2 — Low-posterior class confusion. Counterfactual: present a CHAdeMO or Tesla(S) port under occlusion or partial dirt. Verifier: \(p(\hat c)<\tau_p\) or class entropy \(H(Y\mid I)>\ H_{\max}\). The paper reports CHAdeMO recall \(0.750\) and Tesla(S) recall \(0.882\) [PAGE 16 of 24], so these classes are the natural stress cases. Prediction: YOLOv8 may select the wrong gun or accept a low-confidence box; the measurable veto outcome is a gun-port mismatch before insertion. Correct policy: reject the class, capture additional images, and require a second independent view. This is verifiable by comparing the predicted class posterior and the physical connector standard.

Scenario 3 — Homography/pose degeneracy. Counterfactual: keep the same scene but make the matched feature points nearly collinear or make the denominator \(h_7x_1+h_8y_1+h_9\) approach zero. Verifier: condition number \(\kappa(H)>\kappa_{\max}\) or \(|h_7x_1+h_8y_1+h_9|<\epsilon_H\). The paper’s homography has 8 DOF and requires four distinct points [PAGE 10 of 24], but it does not provide a conditioning test. Prediction: the decomposed rotation/translation is unstable, so the arm receives a plausible but wrong pose; the measurable veto outcome is a large Cartesian residual after the first slow move. Correct policy: veto the pose, reacquire non-collinear points, or fall back to the IR/force-guided search. This is verifiable from the homography conditioning number and the post-move residual.

In all three cases, the RLVR-style verifier is external to the learned policy: optical SNR, class posterior/entropy, and homography conditioning are measurable signals. The prediction is not a claim of inevitable failure; it is a counterfactual test that the current source does not report. If those verifiers were logged, AViTRoN could convert a silent failure into a safe veto. Without them, the 93% aggregate result [PAGE 19 of 24] cannot adjudicate the physically critical near-field step.
