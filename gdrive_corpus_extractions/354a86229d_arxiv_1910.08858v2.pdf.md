The source’s argument is a multi-hop inference chain, not a single claim. First, Ramesh et al. state the high-level proposition: “Inefficient markets allow investors to consistently outperform the market.” [PAGE 1] Second, they operationalize “outperformance” through a non-parametric historical-frequency estimator of a binary win event conditional on a public market score variable; they describe it as “our Bayesian formula for a team’s likelihood to win a game” [PAGE 4] and state “This formula is the engine of our algorithm.” [PAGE 4] Third, they convert estimated win probability and observed payout into expected value using the exact source equation “Expected Value = [P(Winning) ∗Payout] −P(Losing)” [PAGE 5]. Fourth, they apply two filters—an epsilon band around 0.5 and an expected-value threshold—to decide whether to act. Fifth, they evaluate return on investment and use bootstrap intervals, with the claim that “Returns always increase at each step” [PAGE 8] because the thresholds are chosen optimally. Sixth, they use Bonferroni correction for five sports and conclude: “we reject the null hypothesis that the optimal returns from this strategy are equivalent to zero for every sport” [PAGE 11].

The gray-zone audit must separate identification from optimization. The estimator \(H_N(x)\) is an empirical proportion from historical events. It is identified only where the historical support for \(x\) is adequate and where the current event is exchangeable with the historical sample. The source itself notes that “The optimal Epsilon value varies significantly from sport to sport” [PAGE 6], which means the threshold search is a high-dimensional researcher choice across sports. The bootstrap in Algorithm 6 resamples events with replacement and re-optimizes epsilon and expected-value thresholds, but it does not necessarily resample the entire data-acquisition process: archive reconstruction, venue inclusion, timestamp alignment, and sport inclusion remain fixed. That is a gray zone because the confidence interval is conditional on those upstream choices. The source also excludes certain years: “The sample of games we have excludes the early 2000’s, 2008, and 2018” [PAGE 9], and it does not use compounding or Kelly sizing: “we do not utilize Kelly’s criterion” [PAGE 9]. Those are defensible design choices, but they are not neutral with respect to the economic comparison.

A second gray zone is dependence. Events within a season share participants, information shocks, scheduling, and market sentiment. The bootstrap treats events as exchangeable; if temporal or clustered dependence exists, percentile and high-density intervals understate variance. The source reports that “All ROI and Epsilon Value intervals for all sports in each panel do not contain zero” [PAGE 18], but that statement is conditional on the resampling unit and on the two-model family. If one treats Simple and Weighted as two searched model families across five sports, the family-wise error control becomes more delicate than a per-model Bonferroni correction.

A third gray zone is singularity and support. The non-parametric estimator is not defined meaningfully when the historical count \(N_x\) at a conditioning value \(x\) is zero or very small. The source acknowledges sparse historical support in some sports, especially WNBA [PAGE 9]. Near the tail, the empirical proportion has high variance and can be driven by a handful of events; the expected-value filter can then select exactly those noisy tail cells. The return-on-investment denominator also has a singularity: if the threshold \(\tau\) is high enough that \(N=0\), then \(\mathrm{ROI}=100\sum_i W_i/N\) is undefined. The algorithm’s decision boundary is not the same as a calibrated probability guarantee.

A fourth gray zone is the “last update” clock. The source prefers the most recent market update because “it was closest to game time and thus contained the most recent information” [PAGE 5]. That helps staleness, but it also creates a timestamp-audit requirement: if archived revisions are recorded after the decision time, the evaluation leaks future information. The correct test is event-time alignment, not merely recency. Thus the deep conclusion is conditional: the paper demonstrates a reproducible historical anomaly under its estimator and filter, but the stronger claim of persistent exploitable inefficiency requires nested selection, dependence-aware resampling, support constraints, and clock audits.

\text{Expected Value} = [P(\text{Winning}) * \text{Payout}] - P(\text{Losing})
\]
[PAGE 5]. With a normalized unit stake, let \(R\) be the net payout per unit stake and \(p=P(Y=1)\). Then
\[
\mathrm{EV}=pR-(1-p).
\]
Units: \(\mathrm{EV}\) is currency per unit stake; with unit stake normalized to one, it is dimensionless currency units per unit stake. Assumption: the payout is executable at the decision time and the event settles without unmodeled cancellation. Breakdown regime: if \(R\) is stale, non-executable, or capacity-limited, the computed \(\mathrm{EV}\) is not a realizable expected value. The two probability aggregators are:
\[
\hat p_{\text{simple}}=\frac{1}{|\sigma|}\sum_{s\in\sigma}\pi_s,
\]
\[
\hat p_{\text{weighted}}=\frac{\sum_{s\in\sigma}\pi_s\phi_s}{\sum_{s\in\sigma}\phi_s},
\]
where \(\sigma\) is the set of unique conditioning values, \(\pi_s\) is the historical win proportion at \(s\), and \(\phi_s\) is the frequency of \(s\). Units: probabilities dimensionless; \(\phi_s\) integer counts. Assumptions: Simple weights each unique value equally; Weighted weights by observed frequency. Breakdown: the Weighted denominator is zero when no observations exist in \(\sigma\), and Simple can over-weight rare unique values with tiny \(N_s\).

p\in(0.5-\varepsilon,\;0.5+\varepsilon),

\[
\mathrm{EV}>\tau.
\]
Units: \(\varepsilon\) and \(\tau\) are dimensionless; \(\mathrm{EV}\) is currency per unit stake. The source states “The optimal Epsilon value varies significantly from sport to sport” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: if \(\varepsilon\) is selected on the same sample used for reporting without nested selection, the reported optimum is optimistic; if \(\tau\) is raised until \(N\) is very small, the return estimate has high variance and the denominator singularity dominates.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer treats the market as an adversarial inference environment. A first-order model assumes the public quote is a fixed input and that historical frequencies are stable. A second-order model assumes other participants anticipate the first-order rule: if the rule is public, quotes can be shifted so that the selected cells become unfavorable. The source’s own “last update” preference—because the last update “was closest to game time and thus contained the most recent information” [PAGE 5]—is a clock-aware move, but it is still public-information timing. It reduces staleness; it does not create private information.

Crisis habit regression is the failure mode in which, under stress or sparse data, the model regresses to a simple habit: trust the raw historical proportion, widen the epsilon band, or lower the threshold to keep acting. That habit is dangerous precisely where \(N_x\) is small and where the source admits sparse historical support in some sports [PAGE 9]. The counter-trap is the adversarial response: if the model tightens \(\tau\) to avoid noise, the counterparty can create apparent positive expected value in cells the model has learned to trust. The defense is not a stronger threshold; it is support diagnostics, private randomization of decision boundaries, and out-of-sample calibration. The clock is the event-time constraint: the decision must use only information in \(\mathcal{F}_t\), the information set at decision time \(t\). Any revision timestamped after \(t\) is a leak, even if it is “more accurate.” Thus crisis habit regression says “act because the threshold says act”; counter-trap says “the threshold is being mined”; clock says “you may not know what you think you know until the timestamp audit is complete.” The correct recursive posture is to abstain when support, calibration, or clock alignment fails, rather than to let the threshold absorb all three uncertainties.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is formalized by transportability. Let \(Z\) be a physical, biomechanical, or weather vector: wind speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), precipitation rate in \(\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\), temperature in \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\), ground reaction force in \(\mathrm{N}\), load rate in \(\mathrm{N\,s^{-1}}\), sprint speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and asymmetry index dimensionless. Let \(P_T\) be the training distribution and \(P_C\) the current event distribution. The source model estimates \(\hat p_T(x)=H_N(x)\). The current probability is
\[
P_C(Y=1\mid X=x)=\int P_C(Y=1\mid X=x,Z=z)\,dP_C(z\mid x).
\]
If \(P_C(z\mid x)=P_T(z\mid x)\) and \(P_C(Y=1\mid X=x,Z=z)=P_T(Y=1\mid X=x,Z=z)\) over the overlap, then the historical proportion transports. Otherwise it does not. A sufficient veto condition is
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_C(Z\mid X=x)\,\|\,P_T(Z\mid X=x)\right)\gt \kappa,
\]
with \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) in nats and \(\kappa\) a pre-registered tolerance. A second veto is support failure: if the current \(z\) lies outside the historical support, no non-parametric average identifies the counterfactual.

The proof is direct. By the law of total probability, if the conditional outcome \(P(Y=1\mid X=x,Z=z)\) is non-constant in \(z\), then a change in the distribution of \(z\) given \(x\) changes the integral even when \(x\) is unchanged. Therefore \(H_N(x)\) is biased for \(P_C(Y=1\mid X=x)\). Physical examples: high wind can alter trajectory and timing; precipitation can alter surface traction; an injury can change ground reaction force asymmetry and acceleration capacity; a cancelled event makes \(Y\) deterministic and outside the model’s stochastic support. In those regimes the market-aggregate signal is not a substitute for the physical layer. The source’s recency preference [PAGE 5] helps only if the physical information is actually incorporated before the decision clock; otherwise recency is cosmetic. The veto is therefore: if \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\gt\kappa\), if \(z\notin\mathrm{supp}(Z_T\mid x)\), or if a hard physical constraint makes the outcome deterministic, the model must abstain rather than override the physical layer.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
