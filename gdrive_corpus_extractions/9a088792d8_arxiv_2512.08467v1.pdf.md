The genealogy therefore reverses the usual narrative: SAM is not the persistence mechanism; it is only a high-quality initializer and occasional re-segmentation prompt, while identity persistence remains dependent on an appearance model that the paper itself shows is insufficient after full visibility loss.

Multi-week research traps visible in the source:

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system has a nested belief structure. At the outer level, the human believes that a click plus team label creates a persistent player identity. At the SAM level, the model believes that a point prompt yields three plausible masks with scores [PAGE 4]. At the CSRT level, the tracker believes that a local correlation template remains valid across frames and emits confidence \(c_i^t\) [PAGE 4]. At the recovery level, the module believes that constant-velocity prediction plus a SAM prompt near the predicted point will recover the player, and that an HSV jersey histogram with similarity threshold 0.6 is sufficient to confirm identity [PAGE 4-5]. At the evaluator level, the paper believes that TSR, bounding-box stability, ORR, and persistence measure robustness [PAGE 5]. The recursive failure is that each inner belief can be locally valid while the outer identity belief is false. CSRT can maintain high area-ratio stability while tracking the wrong player or while the target is absent; the paper’s 0.9891-0.9905 stability scores coexist with a TSR of 8.66% in the long-term case [PAGE 7]. The recovery module can successfully re-initialize a mask that passes the 0.6 similarity test but belongs to a teammate with a similar jersey.

Crisis habit regression appears when occlusion pressure rises. The system regresses to its habitual mechanism: CSRT local template tracking. The paper states that “17 out of 19 occlusions did not necessitate recovery” [PAGE 6], meaning CSRT often rides through partial overlap without invoking the recovery module. That is a strength in light and moderate occlusion, but it is also a habit: when the target is fully lost, the same mechanism has no memory. The paper concludes that CSRT lacks “the inability to re-establish correspondence after the target leaves the camera frame for extended periods” [PAGE 7], and reports “failed re-acquisition” [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is to add more SAM re-segmentation or more appearance matching without changing the identity model. If the appearance distribution is not injective across players—same team, similar kit, referee—then increasing the number of SAM prompts only increases the chance of confidently re-initializing on the wrong person. The correct counter-trap is a memory or re-identification module with a calibrated open-set rejection rule, not merely a lower similarity threshold.

The clock is the third recursive constraint. The system runs at 7.6-7.7 FPS [PAGE 1], while live broadcast implies 25 FPS; the paper admits “not achieving the 25 FPS real-time threshold for live broadcast” [PAGE 6]. Recovery is triggered only after \(L=10\) lost frames [PAGE 4], and the long-term event lasts approximately 15 frames [PAGE 7]. Thus the clock has three scales: 40 ms per frame for live broadcast, 10 frames for recovery latency, and 15 frames for the observed off-screen absence. The recursive theory-of-mind conclusion is that the evaluator must model the tracker’s belief as partially observable and non-monotonic: confidence below \(\tau_c=0.3\) [PAGE 5] is not merely “low certainty”; it is an identity-belief invalidation. Once invalidated, SAM segmentation is an observation, not an identity proof.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: identity persistence requires visible pixels or a memory mechanism. If the player leaves the camera frame, the visible area \(A_i^{\mathrm{vis}}(t)\) tends to zero; then the jersey mask \(m_i^{\mathrm{jersey}}\) is empty, the HSV histogram normalization in Eq. 6 is undefined, and CSRT has no template to update. The source records exactly this failure: “single off-screen event lasting approximately 15 frames” [PAGE 7] and “failed re-acquisition” [PAGE 7]. Any claim of long-term identity continuity without an explicit memory or re-identification bank is therefore vetoed by the paper’s own 8.66% long-term TSR [PAGE 7]. Crowd physics adds a second veto: in the heavy-occlusion penalty-box scenario, the paper reports 19 occlusion events and one identity switch [PAGE 6]. The system’s own attempt-conditioned recovery is 1 success out of 2 attempts, while event-conditioned recovery is 1 out of 19 [PAGE 6]. A claim of robust crowded-scene identity must therefore specify that most events are handled by CSRT persistence, not by the recovery module.

Biomechanical veto: the appearance model assumes the jersey is in “top 60% of the mask” [PAGE 4]. That assumption breaks under arm occlusion, ball occlusion, player overlap, turning, jumping, and jersey deformation. A player’s silhouette can remain trackable while the upper-body HSV histogram is corrupted by grass, skin, another kit, or shadow. The source does not report gait, pose, or limb-motion features; it reports only HSV hue and saturation histograms [PAGE 4]. Therefore any claim that the system tracks players through biomechanically complex contact is unsupported. The correct veto condition is that the appearance vector must remain separable from all other players: for all \(j\neq i\), \(\mathrm{sim}(a_i,a_j)\lt\tau_{\mathrm{sim}}\). The paper does not report such a separation test, and same-team uniforms make the condition non-trivial.

Weather and illumination veto: HSV hue and saturation are sensitive to white balance, stadium floodlights, shadow, rain, and kit reflectance. The paper does not report weather conditions, illumination protocols, or cross-stadium tests; its dataset is 36 clips from the DFL Bundesliga Data Shootout at 1080p and 25 FPS [PAGE 5]. Therefore an all-weather or all-stadium claim is vetoed by absence of evidence. The physical-layer proof is simple: if illumination changes the hue histogram \(H_i^{\mathrm{hue}}\) by more than the inter-player separation, then the 0.6 similarity threshold no longer identifies the same player. The paper’s own long-term failure and single identity switch [PAGE 6-7] are sufficient to veto a strong “robust in all football conditions” claim. The defensible claim is narrower: stable visible-segment tracking in light to moderate occlusion, with post-match rather than live-broadcast latency [PAGE 6].
