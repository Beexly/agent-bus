The following equations are formal reconstructions of operators named in the source. They are not claimed to be verbatim from the paper. Units are stated explicitly; normalized deep-learning tensors are dimensionless unless otherwise noted.

1. Multi-sensor observation model. Let \(m=1,\dots,M\) index sensor channels. A measured signal is
\[
\mathbf{y}_m(t)=\mathbf{s}_m(t)+\mathbf{n}_m(t)+\mathbf{a}_m(t)+\mathbf{d}_m(t),
\]
where \(\mathbf{s}_m\) is the physiological or biomechanical signal of interest, \(\mathbf{n}_m\) is stochastic noise, \(\mathbf{a}_m\) is motion artifact, and \(\mathbf{d}_m\) is drift or calibration error. Channel units depend on the sensor: accelerometer \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), gyroscope \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), magnetometer \(\mu\mathrm{T}\), EMG \(\mu\mathrm{V}\), ECG \(\mathrm{mV}\), heart rate \(\mathrm{beats\,min^{-1}}\), pressure \(\mathrm{Pa}\), temperature \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Assumption: additive contamination with finite variance over the analysis window. Breakdown regime: when \(\|\mathbf{a}_m\|_2\) or \(\|\mathbf{d}_m\|_2\) is comparable to \(\|\mathbf{s}_m\|_2\) inside the physiological band, or when the sensor saturates, clips, or loses contact. [PAGE 7] states: “Motion artefacts, especially in surface EMG or heart rate measurements, introduce noise that distorts the signal.” [PAGE 8] states: “Raw signals acquired from motion, physiological, and environmental sensors often contain noise, irregularities, and inconsistencies that hinder effective interpretation.”

2. Sampling and aliasing. For a signal with maximum relevant frequency \(f_{\max}\), the Nyquist condition is
\[
f_s>2f_{\max},\qquad \Delta t=\frac{1}{f_s}\ \mathrm{s}.
\]
Assumption: the signal is band-limited below \(f_{\max}\) before sampling. Breakdown regime: if \(f_s\le 2f_{\max}\), high-frequency movement components alias into lower frequencies and are misclassified as physiological or biomechanical events. [PAGE 7] states: “Sensors must operate sufficiently high sampling frequency to capture fast-paced activities such as sprinting, jumping, or explosive directional changes.”

3. Filtering. A linear filter with impulse response \(h(t)\) produces
\[
y_f(t)=(h*y)(t),\qquad H(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}h(t)e^{-i2\pi ft}\,dt.
\]
For a low-pass filter, \(H(f)\approx 1\) for \(|f|\le f_c\) and \(H(f)\approx 0\) for \(|f|>f_c\). Units: \(h\) has units of \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) if \(y_f\) and \(y\) share units; \(H(f)\) is dimensionless. Assumption: the noise and signal spectra are sufficiently separable. Breakdown regime: if signal and artifact overlap in frequency, linear filtering cannot separate them; if \(f_c\) is too low, physiological content is removed; if too high, artifact passes through. [PAGE 8] says: “Filters such as low-pass, high-pass, and band-pass are applied depending on the frequency characteristics of the noise.”

4. Normalization. Min-max normalization is
\[
x'=\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}},
\]
which is dimensionless if \(x\), \(x_{\min}\), and \(x_{\max}\) share units. Z-score normalization is
\[
z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},
\]
which is dimensionless if \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) share the units of \(x\). Assumptions: min-max requires finite bounds; z-score requires a stable mean and nonzero standard deviation. Exact singularities: min-max is undefined when \(x_{\max}=x_{\min}\); z-score is undefined when \(\sigma=0\). Breakdown regime: a constant or near-constant signal causes division by zero or amplification of noise; nonstationary calibration causes the training \(\mu,\sigma\) to mismatch deployment data. [PAGE 8] states: “Min-max normalization is widely applied, converting raw signal values into a bounded range between 0 and 1, which preserves the signal’s shape while removing unit-based disparities.” [PAGE 9] states: “Z-score normalization is another popular technique, especially in machine learning applications. It transforms the signal into a distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.”

5. Windowing. For discrete signal \(x[n]\), a window is
\[
x_w[n]=x[n+wH],\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1,
\]
where \(N\) is window length in samples and \(H\) is hop size in samples. The window duration is \(T_w=N/f_s\) seconds; the nominal frequency resolution is \(\Delta f=f_s/N\) hertz; the time-frequency product obeys the Gabor bound \(\Delta t\,\Delta f\ge 1/(4\pi)\) for a Gaussian window. Assumptions: the signal is approximately stationary within \(T_w\). Breakdown regime: short windows lose frequency resolution; long windows violate stationarity during rapid transitions; \(N=0\) is undefined. [PAGE 9] states: “A 100 Hz gyroscope signal may need to be downsampled or interpolated to match a 50 Hz EMG signal for combined feature extraction.”

6. Fourier transform and power spectral density. The discrete Fourier transform is
\[
X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-i2\pi kn/N},\qquad k=0,\dots,N-1.
\]
A one-sided power spectral density estimate is
\[
\hat{S}_{xx}[k]=\frac{|X[k]|^2}{f_s N},
\]
with units of \(x^2\,\mathrm{Hz^{-1}}\). Assumption: stationarity within the window. Breakdown regime: nonstationary athletic transitions, spectral leakage, and finite-record bias; \(N=0\) is undefined. [PAGE 10] states: “The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is the most common method, which decomposes a time-series signal into its frequency components.” [PAGE 10] also states: “Although FFT assumes signal stationarity, this assumption may not always hold in athletic environments, where dynamic movements and transitions between activities are frequent.”

7. Short-time Fourier transform and wavelet transform. The STFT is
\[
X_{\mathrm{STFT}}(m,k)=\sum_{n}x[n]w[n-mH]e^{-i2\pi kn/N},
\]
where \(w\) is a window and \(m\) indexes time frames. The continuous wavelet transform is
\[
W_\psi(a,b)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|a|}}\int x(t)\psi^*\!\left(\frac{t-b}{a}\right)dt,
\]
where \(a\) is a dimensionless scale, \(b\) is time in seconds, and \(\psi\) is the mother wavelet. Assumptions: the chosen window or mother wavelet matches the signal morphology. Breakdown regime: fixed STFT windows trade time and frequency resolution; wavelet choice can create edge effects and scale-dependent bias; if \(a=0\), the transform is undefined. [PAGE 10] states: “Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) and Wavelet Transform (WT) are widely used techniques that provide both time and frequency resolution.”

8. Convolutional operator. For layer \(l\),
\[
z_i^{(l)}=\phi\!\left(b_i^{(l)}+\sum_j w_{ij}^{(l)}*z_j^{(l-1)}\right),
\]
where \(*\) is convolution, \(\phi\) is a nonlinearity such as ReLU, and tensors are dimensionless after normalization. Assumption: local translation invariance is meaningful for the sensor axis. Breakdown regime: distribution shift across athletes or sensor placements, overfitting under small labeled data, and saturation or dead-unit behavior. [PAGE 12] states: “Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are designed to capture spatial dependencies and are highly effective in identifying patterns within time-series data and multi-dimensional sensor inputs.”

9. LSTM recurrence. With input \(x_t\), hidden state \(h_t\), and cell state \(c_t\),
\[
f_t=\sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f),\quad
i_t=\sigma(W_i[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_i),
\]
\[
\tilde c_t=\tanh(W_c[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_c),\quad
c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t,
\]
\[
o_t=\sigma(W_o[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_o),\quad
h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t).
\]
Units are dimensionless after normalization. Assumption: temporal dependence is captured by the hidden state. Breakdown regime: vanishing or exploding gradients, numerical underflow, excessive sequence length, and nonstationary drift; \(T=0\) sequences are undefined. [PAGE 14] states: “Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are the most widely used RNN variant for sports signal processing due to their ability to model long-range dependencies and resist gradient-related issues.”

10. GRU recurrence. With update gate \(z_t\) and reset gate \(r_t\),
\[
z_t=\sigma(W_z[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_z),\quad
r_t=\sigma(W_r[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_r),
\]
\[
\tilde h_t=\tanh(W_h[r_t\odot h_{t-1},x_t]+b_h),\quad
h_t=(1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1}+z_t\odot\tilde h_t.
\]
Assumption: a lighter gated memory is sufficient for the task. Breakdown regime: gate saturation, long-range dependency loss, and non-IID sensor noise. [PAGE 14] states: “GRUs combine the forget and input gates into a single update gate and use a reset gate to manage memory content.”

11. Attention. For query \(q\), keys \(k_t\), and values \(v_t\),
\[
\alpha_t=\frac{\exp(q^\top k_t/\sqrt{d})}{\sum_{s}\exp(q^\top k_s/\sqrt{d})},\qquad
c=\sum_t\alpha_t v_t,
\]
where \(d\) is the key dimension and all normalized quantities are dimensionless. Assumption: attention weights reflect task-relevant evidence. Breakdown regime: softmax saturation, entropy collapse, \(d=0\) undefined, and attention weights misread as causal explanations. [PAGE 14] states: “Attention layers assign weights to each time step, guiding the model’s focus toward areas of interest.”

12. Multimodal fusion. A late-fusion probability model is
\[
p(y\mid x_1,\dots,x_M)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m\,p_m(y\mid x_m),\qquad
w_m\ge 0,\quad \sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m=1,
\]
with probability units. Assumption: each modality provides calibrated evidence. Breakdown regime: a missing or corrupted modality, uncalibrated weights, or a modality that is informative in training but invalid in deployment; if all modalities are invalid, the fused probability is invalid. [PAGE 15] states: “Hybrid deep learning frameworks combine multiple neural network architectures to exploit the strengths of each and address the limitations inherent in using a single model type.”

13. Federated averaging. For \(K\) clients with \(n_k\) samples,
\[
w^{t+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_k}{n}w_k^{t+1},\qquad
n=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_k.
\]
Weights are dimensionless. Assumption: secure aggregation and tolerable client heterogeneity. Exact singularity: \(K=0\) or \(n=0\) is undefined. Breakdown regime: non-IID client drift, straggler effects, update inversion, and excessive differential-privacy budget \(\epsilon\) (dimensionless). [PAGE 27] states: “Federated learning circumvents this issue by keeping raw data on local devices and transferring only model updates—such as gradients or parameters—to a central server.”

14. Real-time loop and power. A feedback loop has
\[
T_{\mathrm{loop}}=T_{\mathrm{acq}}+T_{\mathrm{tx}}+T_{\mathrm{pre}}+T_{\mathrm{inf}}+T_{\mathrm{fb}}
\]
in seconds, and average power is
\[
P_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{E}{T},\qquad
T_b=\frac{E_b}{P_{\mathrm{avg}}},
\]
with energy in joules, power in watts, and battery life \(T_b\) in seconds. Breakdown regime: \(T_{\mathrm{loop}}\) exceeds the task’s permissible feedback delay, jitter exceeds the alignment tolerance, or \(P_{\mathrm{avg}}>E_b/T_{\mathrm{required}}\). [PAGE 20] states: “Wearable sensors must be lightweight, durable, and ergonomically shaped to remain secure and comfortable during dynamic activities.” [PAGE 21] states: “Low-power wireless communication protocols such as Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), Zigbee, or ANT+ transmit real-time data from wearables to mobile devices or cloud gateways.”

15. Information measures. For discrete class labels,
\[
H(Y)=-\sum_y p(y)\log_2 p(y)\ \mathrm{bits},\qquad
I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X).
\]
Assumption: probabilities are calibrated and estimated from representative data. Breakdown regime: zero probabilities are handled by \(0\log 0=0\); differential entropy can be negative; finite-sample mutual-information estimates are biased. A sensor channel with \(I(X;Y)\approx 0\) cannot justify a decision even if its raw signal looks dramatic. This is the formal check against “toy” feature importance.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s architecture story has a retrocausal genealogy: current claims about athlete monitoring inherit assumptions from signal processing, statistics, control theory, and machine learning. Fourier analysis and the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem underwrite the FFT, PSD, STFT, and wavelet operators discussed on [PAGE 10]. Kalman and complementary filtering underwrite IMU orientation tracking on [PAGE 8]. Convolutional networks inherit translation-invariance assumptions from image processing and are adapted to one-dimensional sensor sequences on [PAGE 12]. Recurrent networks inherit state-space and sequence-modeling assumptions; LSTM/GRU gating is presented on [PAGE 14]. Attention and transformer-style models inherit context-weighting assumptions, while federated learning inherits distributed optimization and privacy engineering on [PAGE 27]. The modern athlete-monitoring narrative is therefore not a single invention; it is a layered stack of older mathematical commitments.

The first multi-week trap is calibration drift. A model trained on week 1 baseline data may misclassify week 6 adaptation as fatigue or injury risk because sensor bias, strap tension, skin contact, and hardware wear have changed. The source acknowledges this: [PAGE 7] says, “In real-time settings, constant recalibration is impractical, so adaptive filtering and drift compensation algorithms must be employed to ensure data accuracy.” A retrocausal audit must therefore ask whether the baseline is refreshed, whether drift is estimated, and whether the model can distinguish physiological adaptation from instrument change.

The second multi-week trap is battery and adherence. A device that works for one session may fail over a training block because duty cycling, wireless retransmission, and on-device inference drain energy. [PAGE 21] discusses power-efficient design, sleep modes, and adaptive sampling, but the paper gives no energy budget. A multi-week deployment must therefore pre-register uptime, charging cadence, and missing-data tolerance.

The third trap is data drift and model drift. [PAGE 24] says, “Data scarcity remains one of the most persistent challenges in developing robust deep-learning models for athlete signal processing.” A model trained on one team, sport, or sensor placement may not generalize to another. Over weeks, athletes change fitness, injury status, and movement strategy; the model’s decision boundary may become stale. The source’s discussion of transfer learning, data augmentation, few-shot learning, and domain adaptation on [PAGE 24]–[PAGE 25] is the correct lineage, but it is not a validated multi-week solution.

The fourth trap is longitudinal privacy. [PAGE 25] notes that continuous monitoring “leads to the accumulation of longitudinal datasets that capture trends in fatigue, recovery, and performance decline over time.” A one-week consent may not cover a season-long model that later infers injury risk or psychological stress. [PAGE 26] says, “Informed consent is a foundational principle in ethical data collection, yet it becomes increasingly complex in real-time athlete monitoring contexts.” The retrocausal lesson is that later uses can retroactively change the meaning of earlier data; consent and governance must be dynamic.

The fifth trap is feedback dependency. If an athlete learns to satisfy the model rather than the underlying biomechanical goal, the data-generating process changes. [PAGE 19] discusses immediate versus delayed feedback, and [PAGE 26] warns about performance anxiety and reduced autonomy under pervasive monitoring. A multi-week system must therefore audit for model-facing behavior, not just model accuracy.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit distinguishes three agents: the athlete’s self-model, the coach’s model of the athlete, and the model’s model of both. Under crisis, fatigue, or competition stress, athletes may regress to overlearned habits. The source notes that fatigue monitoring uses EMG, joint kinematics, and HRV over time: [PAGE 17] says, “Fatigue monitoring using deep learning approaches typically involves modelling patterns in EMG signals, joint kinematics, and heart rate variability (HRV) across time.” A regression to habit can reduce movement variability or increase compensatory asymmetry; either pattern can be labeled “fatigue” or “injury risk” without the model knowing whether the change is protective, adaptive, or technical. The recursive problem is that the athlete may also know the system is watching and may alter behavior to avoid an alert.

The counter-trap is feedback-induced behavior change. An adaptive feedback system can close the gap between data and action, but it can also create a new target. [PAGE 19] states: “Feedback timing also plays a critical role—immediate feedback supports real-time correction, while delayed feedback facilitates post-session reflection and analysis.” If immediate feedback is delivered during a high-intensity movement, the athlete may consciously correct to satisfy the cue, producing a gait or posture that is less natural and possibly less safe. If delayed feedback is used, the correction may arrive after the injury-relevant window. The model must therefore represent its own uncertainty and the athlete’s likely response, not just classify the current signal.

The clock dimension is decisive. There is a biomechanical clock for tissue load, a physiological clock for recovery, a psychological clock for stress, and a competition clock for decision timing. The same probability of fatigue has different consequences at different clocks. A fatigue alert during a training session may justify load reduction; a fatigue alert during competition may be ignored or may create anxiety. [PAGE 18] says, “Injury monitoring integrates fatigue and stress analysis outputs and biomechanical assessments to detect early warning signs of potential physical damage.” That integration must be time-aware: a risk score is not an action; it is an input to a clock-specific adjudication. The recursive theory-of-mind rule is that no model output should be interpreted as the athlete’s ground truth when the athlete’s behavior is itself conditioned on the model’s feedback.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication requires a fail-safe logic. Let \(V_{\mathrm{sensor}}\) be a sensor-validity flag, \(V_{\mathrm{env}}\) an environmental-validity flag, \(V_{\mathrm{biomech}}\) a biomechanical-safety flag, and \(V_{\mathrm{privacy}}\) a governance flag. A deployment decision \(a\) from model evidence \(x\) is admissible only if
\[
\mathrm{Admit}(a\mid x)=1 \iff
V_{\mathrm{sensor}}=0 \land V_{\mathrm{env}}=0 \land V_{\mathrm{biomech}}=0 \land V_{\mathrm{privacy}}=0 \land P(y\mid x)\ge \tau \land a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{safe}},
\]
where \(\tau\) is a pre-registered probability threshold and \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{safe}}\) is the set of actions allowed by clinical and biomechanical constraints. If any veto flag is unknown, the default is veto, not permission.

The physical and sensor veto is justified because a corrupted observation cannot support a valid inference. [PAGE 7] states: “GPS accuracy may degrade under heavy foliage or inside buildings.” [PAGE 7] also notes that optical heart-rate sensing can be affected by ambient light or skin pigmentation. Therefore, if the environmental-validity flag is false, the model must abstain or downgrade confidence. The biomechanical veto is independent of the model’s probability. [PAGE 3] discusses pressure-sensitive insoles and force plates quantifying impact forces and distribution; [PAGE 18] discusses joint angles, impact forces, acceleration spikes, and motion asymmetry. If a measured impact force, joint angle, or asymmetry violates a clinically validated safety envelope, no “push through” recommendation is admissible even if the classifier is confident. The weather veto is a special case of environmental validity: rain, heat, humidity, electromagnetic interference, and poor GPS geometry can invalidate both the signal and the action. The source’s discussion of environmental variability on [PAGE 7] supports this rule.

The proof is not an empirical theorem; it is an authority ordering. The model estimates latent state from observations. Physical safety constraints and sensor validity are prior conditions on whether the observation is admissible. Therefore, if \(V_{\mathrm{sensor}}=1\), \(V_{\mathrm{env}}=1\), or \(V_{\mathrm{biomech}}=1\), the model’s posterior cannot override the veto. The correct output is “abstain, recalibrate, retest, or refer,” not a training prescription.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Aliasing veto. A sprint-impact signal contains a true 60 Hz component, but the IMU is sampled at \(f_s=100\ \mathrm{Hz}\). By the Nyquist condition, 60 Hz aliases to \(|60-100|=40\ \mathrm{Hz}\). If a model interprets the 40 Hz energy as a physiological or fatigue signature, the inference is invalid. Counterfactual: apply an anti-alias low-pass filter with cutoff below 50 Hz before sampling or before spectral analysis. Verifiable prediction: the injected 60 Hz peak should disappear or move to the correct band after anti-alias filtering, and the fatigue alert should be suppressed. Verifier: inject a known sinusoid, compute the FFT, and confirm the alias location. Cite [PAGE 7] for the sampling requirement.

Scenario 2: Drift and calibration veto. A gyroscope develops a multi-week bias. The model’s gait-asymmetry score crosses a risk threshold, but the score is an artifact of orientation drift rather than a biomechanical change. Counterfactual: recalibrate the sensor and re-run with Kalman or complementary filtering. Verifiable prediction: after bias correction, the asymmetry score should return toward the athlete’s baseline within a pre-registered equivalence margin; if it does not, the alert remains. Verifier: compare pre- and post-calibration sessions with held-out calibration data. Cite [PAGE 7] for drift compensation and [PAGE 8] for Kalman filtering.

Scenario 3: Federated privacy veto. A federated model update is trained on a small cohort. Even though raw data remain local, the update may permit membership inference or attribute inference. Counterfactual: apply secure aggregation and differential privacy with a pre-registered privacy budget \(\epsilon\), then re-run the attack. Verifiable prediction: membership-inference success should fall to near chance under the pre-registered attack; if it remains above the pre-registered threshold, deployment is vetoed. Verifier: run a membership-inference attack on the update and report attack AUC or advantage. Cite [PAGE 27] for federated learning and model-update transfer. These three vetoes are counterfactual because each removes a specific invalidity condition and predicts a specific, checkable change in the decision.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
