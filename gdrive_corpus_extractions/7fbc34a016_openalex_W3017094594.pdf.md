The source is a computer-vision paper, so the audit target is a learned video operator and its claims, not any external forecasting artifact. The first multi-hop step is the paper’s time-base construction. The input is described as “a set of L frames in an L-fps video,” and the task is to “predict whether those frames depict 1 second of the object’s motion in the world (normal speed), or more than 1 second” [PAGE 1]. With L frames at L fps, the normal-speed interval is L/L = 1 s. If the same L frames are temporally subsampled by a factor f, they span f seconds of source content; the binary label is therefore a proxy for f ≈ 1 versus f ≈ 2, not a metric measurement of object velocity. The paper makes the proxy explicit: “we train SpeedNet to discriminate between videos played at normal speed and videos played at twice (2×) their original speed” [PAGE 3]. The training sampler chooses a skip factor f between 1 and 1.2 for normal speed and between 1.7 and 2.2 for sped-up clips, with skip probability 1 − 1/f [PAGE 3]. Thus the label is a sampled playback-rate class, and the model output is a probability of normal speed, not a physical speed in m/s.

The second hop is the architecture. A T × N × N input segment is passed through a fully convolutional base network and produces features of dimension T × N/32 × N/32 × 1024 [PAGE 3]. Spatial global max pooling and temporal global average pooling collapse this to a 1024D vector, which is mapped to logits by a 1 × 1 convolution W [PAGE 4]. The model is trained with binary cross entropy [PAGE 4]. This means the operator’s native output is p_normal ∈ [0,1], a dimensionless probability. A high p_normal means the model thinks the clip is closer to the 1× training class; a low p_normal means closer to the 2× class. It is not a calibrated speedometer. The paper itself rejects perfect accuracy as a goal: “we do not expect (or desire for) our model to reach perfect accuracy” [PAGE 3]. This is crucial because several visually ambiguous cases—static scenes, slow walking sped up to fast walking—can make the binary proxy disagree with naturalness [PAGE 3].

The third hop is the adaptive-speedup operator. The paper generates v_0 through v_k by subsampling an input video by an exponential factor X^i, using X = 1.25 and k = 10 [PAGE 4]. Each version is fed to SpeedNet in a sliding-window fashion, and “Pvi(t) represents the (softmax) probability of normal speed” [PAGE 4]. The prediction curves are interpolated, binarized with a threshold ρ selected from {0.1, . . . , 0.9}, multiplied by their speedup factors, and combined by taking the maximum at each timestep [PAGE 4]. The resulting V(t) is then smoothed into a speedup curve S(t) by minimizing a speed, rate, and smoothness objective [PAGE 4]. The appendix gives the exact terms: E_speed uses both (S(t) − R_min)^2 and (S(t) − R_o)^2 weighted by the normalized speediness score; E_rate constrains the mean speedup to R_o; E_smooth penalizes first differences of S [PAGE 10]. This is a constrained perceptual optimization, not a reconstruction of physical kinematics.

The fourth hop is the evidence audit. The paper’s ablation is the key gray-zone check. Without temporal augmentations, spatial augmentations, and same-batch training, SpeedNet reaches 96.9% on Kinetics but only 57.4% on NFS; with all three, Kinetics falls to 75.6% while NFS rises to 73.6% [PAGE 5]. The authors interpret the high in-domain number as shortcut-driven: “When training SpeedNet without (1), (2) and (3), SpeedNet relies on learned ‘shortcuts’–artificial cues that are present in Kinetics, which lead to a misleading high test accuracy” [PAGE 5]. The fifth hop is the breakdown audit. The source states that “the correlation between speediness and motion magnitude does pose a formidable challenge to our method in cases of extreme camera or object motion” [PAGE 2], and that SpeedNet “does tend to fail in scenarios which contain either extreme camera motion or very large object motion” [PAGE 5-6]. The hypothesized cause is training-set coverage: “our training set does not contain enough normal-speed videos with such large frame-to-frame displacements” [PAGE 6]. Therefore any claim of metric robustness in extreme motion requires a recalibration or stress test not present in the paper. The same logic applies to frame-rate clocks: Kinetics is 25 fps, while NFS is 240 fps and is tested after 10× or 20× uniform speedup to effective 24 fps or 12 fps [PAGE 5]. A final gray-zone issue is the observer model: the user study with 30 participants and five videos shows preference for adaptive speedup, but it does not establish a universal perceptual threshold [PAGE 6-7]. Formal operators and units from the paper:

1. Time-base and sampling operator. Let the source frame rate be f_0 in frames per second, and let the sampled frame interval be Δt = 1/f_0 seconds. The paper’s binary task uses L frames from an L-fps video; normal-speed input is intended to represent L/L = 1 s of world motion, while sped-up input represents more than 1 s [PAGE 1]. In training, a skip factor f_s is sampled as f_s ∈ [1, 1.2] for normal speed and f_s ∈ [1.7, 2.2] for sped-up clips, with skip probability p_skip = 1 − 1/f_s [PAGE 3]. The approximate playback factor is therefore f_s, dimensionless. The binary target can be written as y = 1 for f_s ≈ 1 and y = 0 for f_s ≈ 2, but this is a proxy class, not a physical velocity.

2. Classification operator. Let x ∈ R^{T × N × N} be a video segment, where T is frames, N is pixels, and the channels are implicit. Let Φ(x) be the fully convolutional base network. The paper states the output dimension is T × N/32 × N/32 × 1024 [PAGE 3]. Define spatial global max pooling and temporal global average pooling:
z = Avg_{t=1}^{T} Max_{u,v} Φ(x)_{t,u,v,:} ∈ R^{1024}.
The final logits are produced by a 1 × 1 convolution W, and the normal-speed probability is
p_normal = softmax(W z)_{normal} ∈ [0,1].
The training loss is binary cross entropy:
L_BCE = −[ y log p_normal + (1 − y) log(1 − p_normal) ].
Units: p_normal is dimensionless probability; L_BCE is dimensionless information/nats if natural log is used. The source explicitly says “Our model is trained using a binary cross entropy loss” [PAGE 4].

3. Adaptive-speedup operator. The paper generates sped-up videos v_0, . . . , v_k by subsampling v by an exponential factor {X^i}_{i=0}^{k}, with X = 1.25 and k = 10 [PAGE 4]. For each v_i, a sliding-window prediction curve P_{v_i}(t) is produced, where P_{v_i}(t) is the softmax probability of normal speed [PAGE 4]. The curves are linearly interpolated to the longest curve’s temporal length, binarized with threshold ρ ∈ {0.1, . . . , 0.9}, multiplied by their factors X^i, and combined by
V(t) = max_i [ X^i · 1{ P_{v_i}^{ρ}(t) is not sped-up } ].
Units: X^i and V(t) are dimensionless speedup factors; t is a frame index or interpolated time coordinate. The paper then solves
arg min_S E_speed(S,V) + β E_rate(S,R_o) + α E_smooth(S′),
where S(t) is the smooth speedup curve, R_o is the user-requested target rate, and S′ is the first derivative [PAGE 4]. The appendix gives:
E_speed(S,V) = Σ_t (1 − \hat{V}(t))(S(t) − R_min)^2 + γ Σ_t \hat{V}(t)(S(t) − R_o)^2,
E_rate(S,R_o) = ((1/T) Σ_t S(t) − R_o)^2,
E_smooth(S′) = Σ_t (S(t) − S(t+1))^2 [PAGE 10].
Units: S, R_o, R_min, \hat{V}, β, α, and γ are dimensionless; the squared terms are dimensionless. The source says the optimization “has a closed-form solution” as a linear system, though TensorFlow was used in practice [PAGE 10].

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
- Static-scene ambiguity: the paper states, “when nothing is moving in the scene, a video played at 2× will still show no motion” [PAGE 3]. Here p_normal is not identifiable from motion evidence; the output is prior-dependent.
- Natural-fast ambiguity: “slow walking sped up to fast walking can still look natural” [PAGE 3]. The binary proxy can therefore mislabel natural fast motion as sped-up.
- Optical-flow scale confound: mean flow magnitude is correlated with object distance from the camera [PAGE 5]. A simple projection relation is u ≈ (f_cam · v)/Z, where u is image velocity in pixels/s, f_cam is focal length in pixels, v is object velocity in m/s perpendicular to the optical axis, and Z is depth in m. Multiple (v, Z) pairs produce the same u, so flow magnitude alone cannot identify v. The paper’s mean-flow baseline reaches only 55.8% on Kinetics and 55.0% on NFS [PAGE 5].
- Shortcut regime: without augmentations and same-batch training, Kinetics accuracy is 96.9% but NFS accuracy is 57.4%; with them, 75.6% and 73.6% [PAGE 5]. The paper calls the high in-domain result “a misleading high test accuracy” [PAGE 5]. Thus p_normal is not a semantic estimate when compression, aliasing, resizing, or codec cues correlate with the temporal subsampling label.
- Extreme-motion regime: the paper says SpeedNet “does tend to fail in scenarios which contain either extreme camera motion or very large object motion” [PAGE 5-6], and the training set lacks enough normal-speed videos with large frame-to-frame displacements [PAGE 6]. In this regime, large displacement is biased toward the sped-up class.
- Threshold and smoothness regime: V(t) is piecewise constant after thresholding, while S(t) is smoothed by E_smooth. If the target R_o is incompatible with the segments marked as not-sped-up, the optimizer must trade rate error against smoothness and speed error; the source does not guarantee a feasible S(t) that both meets R_o exactly and preserves naturalness for every clip.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs through video time remapping and self-supervised video representation learning. Earlier time-remapping work includes Bennett and McMillan’s computational time-lapse, which computes non-uniform sampling to satisfy visual objectives [PAGE 2]; Zhou et al. use space-time saliency and frame importance [PAGE 2]; Petrovic et al. perform query-based adaptive speedup [PAGE 2]; and several egocentric fast-forwarding papers select frames to preserve gist or reduce jitter [PAGE 2-3]. SpeedNet differs by learning a motion prior from a binary normal-versus-sped-up task rather than selecting frames only by saliency or reconstruction error. The self-supervised lineage is broader: cycle consistency [PAGE 3], shuffled-frame order [PAGE 3], space-time cubic puzzles [PAGE 3], future prediction [PAGE 3], optical flow prediction [PAGE 3], and colorization [PAGE 3]. The closest prior task is arrow-of-time classification, which discriminates between −1 and +1 playback, whereas SpeedNet discriminates among positive speeds [PAGE 3]. Concurrent work by Epstein et al. uses intrinsic speed to predict unintentional action [PAGE 3].

Retrocausally, this explains why the paper’s strongest claims are not “we measured speed” but “we learned a space-time representation that supports action recognition and retrieval.” The representation is validated by fine-tuning on UCF101 and HMDB51; SpeedNet with S3D-G reaches 81.1% and 48.8%, beating other self-supervised Kinetics pre-training methods in the table [PAGE 6]. The paper notes that DynamoNet, pre-trained on YouTube-8M, is 10× larger than Kinetics and reaches 88.1% and 59.9% [PAGE 6], so cross-paper comparisons are not controlled. It also notes that strong random-init S3D-G baselines partly use 64 frames during training [PAGE 6]. Retrieval results are competitive but not dominant: the Recall-at-topK table shows the method is “only slightly inferior to [40]” [PAGE 8].

Multi-week traps in this lineage are therefore: first, treating high in-domain binary accuracy as semantic understanding, when the paper explicitly warns that shortcuts can produce 96.9% on Kinetics and 57.4% on NFS [PAGE 5]; second, treating flow magnitude as a speed proxy, when the paper’s flow baseline is near chance at 55.8% and 55.0% [PAGE 5]; third, comparing self-supervised methods without matching architecture, frame count, and pre-training corpus size [PAGE 6]; and fourth, treating adaptive speedup preference as a universal perceptual law. The user study used 30 participants and five videos, with adaptive preference ranging from 59.3% to 81.5% across videos [PAGE 6-7]. That is evidence of a useful perceptual prior, not a general theory of all viewers or all motion domains.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper’s observer model is implicit rather than recursive. It begins from the premise that “A human observer can often easily notice if an object’s motion is sped up or slowed down” [PAGE 1], and it cites speed-discrimination development through adolescence [PAGE 2]. SpeedNet is trained to imitate a basic human distinction: normal versus faster-than-normal. Adaptive speedup then assumes that p_normal is a proxy for what a viewer will notice as unnatural. The user study tests that proxy by asking participants to choose between adaptive and uniform speedup [PAGE 6-7]. This is a first-order model of another observer’s preference, not a recursive theory of mind with nested beliefs about what the observer believes the model sees.

Crisis habit regression: under distribution shift, the model can regress to habitual low-level cues. The paper’s ablation is the clearest evidence: without augmentations and same-batch training, Kinetics accuracy is 96.9% but NFS is 57.4% [PAGE 5]. The high Kinetics result is described as “misleading” because the network uses “learned ‘shortcuts’–artificial cues” [PAGE 5]. In a crisis regime—new codec, new frame rate, new camera motion—the habitual cue may dominate the semantic motion prior.

Counter-trap: the paper’s countermeasure is to accept lower in-domain accuracy in exchange for cross-domain consistency. Same-batch training, spatial resizing between 64 and 336 pixels, and temporal sampling ranges 1×-1.2× versus 1.7×-2.2× are designed to reduce reliance on artificial cues [PAGE 3]. The result is Kinetics 75.6% and NFS 73.6%, a 2% gap [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap is not to maximize binary accuracy but to make p_normal track motion semantics rather than compression artifacts. The paper also accepts ambiguity: “we do not expect (or desire for) our model to reach perfect accuracy” [PAGE 3].

Clock: the observer and the model share a temporal clock problem. Kinetics is 25 fps; NFS is 240 fps and is tested after 10× or 20× uniform speedup to effective 24 fps or 12 fps [PAGE 5]. The source uses this mismatch to test robustness, but it does not make the model clock-invariant. Adaptive speedup adds another clock: P_{v_i}(t) is a per-frame curve, interpolated to the longest curve, thresholded, and then smoothed into S(t) [PAGE 4]. If the frame clock or interpolation changes, the threshold crossings and the resulting V(t) can change. Thus the recursive observer model is bounded by three clocks: the source capture clock, the temporal-subsampling clock, and the adaptive-playback clock. The paper’s smoothness term E_smooth counteracts abrupt speed changes [PAGE 10], but it does not eliminate clock sensitivity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Permissible claim: within the training distribution, SpeedNet outputs p_normal ∈ [0,1] that ranks a clip as closer to 1× or 2× sampled motion, and that score can drive adaptive playback. The paper supports this with Kinetics and NFS tests, ablation, and adaptive-speedup user preference [PAGE 5-7]. Any claim beyond that requires cross-layer calibration.

Physical veto: SpeedNet does not output velocity in m/s or acceleration in m/s². The source explicitly says “Speediness ≠ motion magnitude” [PAGE 2], and it shows that motion magnitude varies with distance from the camera even at normal speed [PAGE 2]. The mean-flow baseline is limited by “its correlation with the distance of the object from the camera” [PAGE 5]. The formal projection relation u ≈ (f_cam · v)/Z shows that image motion u in pixels/s conflates object velocity v in m/s, focal length f_cam in pixels, and depth Z in m. Without camera intrinsics, depth, and frame timestamps, p_normal cannot be inverted to v. The paper also says the model fails under “extreme camera motion or very large object motion” [PAGE 5-6]. Therefore any physical-velocity or physical-acceleration claim is vetoed unless an external calibration layer is supplied.

Biomechanical veto: the paper contains no joint angles, ground reaction forces, torques, power, energy, anthropometry, or load variables. It uses human-action videos and says solving the task requires “high-level reasoning and understanding of the way different objects move in the world” [PAGE 1], but that is a motion prior, not a biomechanical model. The paper also notes that “slow walking sped up to fast walking can still look natural” [PAGE 3], which means the learned category does not map cleanly to effort or force. A claim that SpeedNet measures biomechanical intensity, fatigue, injury risk, or force output is therefore not identifiable from the source and is vetoed. The only biomechanical statement allowed is weaker: human observers can notice unnatural speed in some actions [PAGE 1-2], and the model approximates that perceptual judgment under its training distribution.

Weather veto: the source pages do not define weather covariates, rainfall, wind, snow, temperature, lighting, or surface friction, and they do not report weather-stratified tests. Kinetics and NFS are natural video corpora [PAGE 4-5], but the paper does not analyze weather as a causal layer. Therefore any weather-specific claim—for example that SpeedNet distinguishes speed changes caused by wind or slippery ground—is outside the source’s evidence and is vetoed pending a weather-labeled stress test. Weather could alter motion and therefore alter p_normal through the motion prior, but the source provides no measurement to adjudicate that effect.

Aggregate veto proof: suppose a downstream claim requires a physical quantity q, where q is velocity, force, or a weather effect. The source provides only p_normal and a dimensionless speedup curve S(t). There is no stated invertible map from p_normal to q, and the paper’s own confound evidence shows multiple physical configurations can produce similar p_normal. Therefore q is not identifiable from the source. The veto is not a rejection of SpeedNet’s usefulness; it is a restriction of its admissible interpretation to relative, distribution-dependent, perceptual speediness and adaptive playback.
