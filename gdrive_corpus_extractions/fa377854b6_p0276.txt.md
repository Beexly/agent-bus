The prevailing economic assumption of his era—and still common today—is that agents possess an innate aversion to this uncertainty, demanding a premium (higher prices or profits) to engage in speculative operations. Knight challenges this by arguing that if uncertainty were truly abhorred to the extent of suppressing production below what equivalent fixed costs would dictate, it would imply a systemic failure of entrepreneurial judgment.

Furthermore, we must consider the evolutionary pressure on firms. The "tendency of profit to zero" mentioned by Knight becomes crucial here; if uncertainty premiums were consistently realized, profits would persist, contradicting the long-run equilibrium prediction. Thus, the persistence of profit implies that uncertainty is not a drag on productivity but the very engine of entrepreneurial reward.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage of this argument traces back to Adam Smith’s invisible hand, which assumed perfect knowledge of local conditions, and David Ricardo’s focus on comparative advantage based on known resource endowments. Alfred Marshall introduced the concept of long-period supply prices, implicitly assuming that costs could be aggregated without regard to future unknowns. It was Francis Ysidro Edgeworth who began probing the statistical nature of averages, hinting at the limitations of applying frequency-based statistics to unique economic events.

Knight’s breakthrough in *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* (1921) was to formalize why profits exist despite competition driving prices toward costs. Previous theories struggled to explain persistent abnormal profits. Knight identified that standard risk could be insured away (becoming a fixed cost), leaving only uninsurable uncertainty as the source of residual claims. This shifted the genealogical focus from material scarcity to epistemic limits.

However, a multi-week trap exists for modern analysts reading Knight through the lens of later behavioral economics. There is a temptation to retroactively project concepts like "loss aversion" or "prospect theory" onto Knight’s "abhorrence of uncertainty." This is a trap because Knight explicitly argues against the idea that uncertainty acts as a friction that stifles production. He views the entrepreneur's response to uncertainty as rational and dynamic, driven by confidence and judgment, not paralyzed by irrational fear. Recognizing this prevents misinterpreting his skepticism as an endorsement of excessive caution; rather, he posits that successful capitalism relies on individuals who are uniquely equipped—or evolutionarily selected—to navigate uncertainty without requiring the safety net of predictable returns.
