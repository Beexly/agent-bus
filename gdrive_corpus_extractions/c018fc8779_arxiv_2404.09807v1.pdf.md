The source defines the camera projection operator abstractly. Let \( \mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) be a 3D world point with SI unit meters, and let \( \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^2 \) be its image location in pixels. The projection function is
\[
\mathbf{x} = \pi_{\Theta}(\mathbf{X}),
\]
where \( \Theta \) denotes camera parameters. The source requires only that \( \pi_{\Theta} \) be defined and return an image location for a known 3D point [PAGE 5]. If homogeneous coordinates are used, \( \tilde{\mathbf{x}} \simeq K [R \mid \mathbf{t}] \tilde{\mathbf{X}} \), followed by dehomogenization \( \mathbf{x} = (u/w, v/w) \) when \( \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (u,v,w)^\top \). Breakdown: \( w = 0 \) is a projective singularity, and the projected point may fall outside the physical image, in which case the source notes that reprojection error “may be undefined when the projection of the 3D object does not land in the image” [PAGE 5].

A homography is the plane-restricted special case:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}} \simeq H \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\pi}, \qquad H \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}, \quad \operatorname{rank}(H)=3,
\]
where \( \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\pi} \) lies on the field plane. The unit of \( \mathbf{x} \) is pixels. Breakdown: the operator is undefined for off-plane points because, as the source says, “This mapping is not defined outside these two planes” [PAGE 2]. This is the central singularity of homography-only benchmarking.

For the thresholded metric, let \( L \) be an ordered 3D polyline representing a field element. Its projection \( \pi(L) \) is a 2D polyline. The polyline defines line segments \( S \). For an annotated image point \( \mathbf{x}_i \) and a projected segment \( S_{ab} \) with endpoints \( \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2 \), define the scalar projection parameter
\[
z = \frac{(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{a}) \cdot (\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})}{\|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|_2^2},
\]
where \( \mathbf{c} \) is the Euclidean projection of \( \mathbf{x}_i \) onto the infinite line through \( \mathbf{a} \) and \( \mathbf{b} \). The point-to-segment distance is
\[
d(\mathbf{x}_i, S_{ab}) =
\begin{cases}
\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{a}\|_2, & z \le 0, \\
\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c}\|_2, & 0 < z < 1, \\
\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{b}\|_2, & z \ge 1.
\end{cases}
\]
All quantities in this equation are in pixels. Breakdown: if \( \|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\|_2 = 0 \), the segment degenerates to a point and the equation must be replaced by \( d(\mathbf{x}_i,S_{aa}) = \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{a}\|_2 \).

The correctness condition for a field element is
\[
\min_{S \in \pi(L)} d(\mathbf{x}_i, S) < \tau, \qquad \forall \mathbf{x}_i \in L,
\]
where \( \tau \) is in pixels [PAGE 5]. The source uses \( \tau = 5 \) pixels for JaC5 and \( \tau = 2 \) pixels for JaC2 when more precise discrimination is needed [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: \( \tau = 0 \) demands exact pixel coincidence; \( \tau \) too large admits gross misprojection; sparse annotations weaken constraints; dense annotations increase strictness but may amplify annotation noise.

The calibration score is the Jaccard index:
\[
\mathrm{JaC}_{\tau} = \frac{TP_{\tau}}{TP_{\tau} + FN + FP}.
\]
This quantity is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). \( TP_{\tau} \) is the number of field elements whose projected polylines satisfy the threshold for all annotated points. \( FP \) includes hallucinated elements and wrongly detected elements whose reprojection exceeds \( \tau \). \( FN \) is the number of annotated field elements that are missing or fail the threshold [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: if \( TP_{\tau} + FN + FP = 0 \), the metric is undefined because no annotated element is visible. If \( TP_{\tau}=0 \) and the denominator is positive, \( \mathrm{JaC}_{\tau}=0 \). If \( FN=FP=0 \) and \( TP_{\tau}>0 \), \( \mathrm{JaC}_{\tau}=1 \).

For probability-unit auditing, define the Bernoulli detection variable
\[
Y_i = \mathbf{1}\left[d(\mathbf{x}_i, \pi(L_i)) < \tau\right],
\]
with success probability
\[
p_i = \Pr(Y_i = 1) \in [0,1].
\]
The Shannon information of that binary detection outcome is
\[
H(Y_i) = -p_i \log_2 p_i - (1-p_i)\log_2(1-p_i) \quad \text{bits}.
\]
Assumption: \( p_i \) is a marginal detection probability induced by calibration error, annotation noise, visibility, and image formation. Independence is not required for the marginal interpretation, but independence or an explicit dependence model is required for variance or confidence statements. Breakdown: \( p_i \) is undefined if no annotated evidence exists; \( H(Y_i)=0 \) at \( p_i=0 \) or \( p_i=1 \).

The source also discusses reprojection error. A normalized form consistent with the source is
\[
e_{\mathrm{rep}}^{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\|\mathbf{x}_i^{\mathrm{gt}} - \mathbf{x}_i^{\mathrm{est}}\|_2}{H_{\mathrm{img}}},
\]
where \( H_{\mathrm{img}} \) is image height in pixels, so \( e_{\mathrm{rep}}^{\mathrm{norm}} \) is dimensionless. If not normalized, the numerator average has units of pixels. The source says the distance is “normalized by the image height” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: \( N=0 \) is undefined; \( H_{\mathrm{img}}=0 \) is invalid; projected points outside the image may still exist algebraically but may be operationally undefined [PAGE 5].

For homography-based field-plane error, a formal projection-error operator is
\[
e_{\mathrm{proj}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{j=1}^{M} \left\|H_{\mathrm{gt}}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_j - H_{\mathrm{est}}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_j\right\|_2,
\]
with \( e_{\mathrm{proj}} \) in meters when the inverted homographies map image points to field-plane coordinates in meters. The source describes the projection error as “the average distance in meters between the projection of pixels sampled in the field image, using the inversion of both the estimated homography and the ground-truth homography” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: \( H_{\mathrm{gt}} \) or \( H_{\mathrm{est}} \) must be invertible; the formula is valid only for field-plane points and cannot certify off-plane calibration.

The source’s core assumptions are therefore: (1) known 3D field geometry is accurate; (2) human annotations are acceptable ground truth in the absence of physical camera ground truth; (3) a good calibration should yield minimal reprojection error [PAGE 4]; (4) \( \tau \) must reflect annotation thickness and application tolerance; and (5) enough well-distributed field elements must be visible to avoid overfitting [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retro-causal genealogy of this benchmark shift is a story of future requirements invalidating past evaluation habits. Early sports calibration research inherited a convenient shortcut: sports fields are known, visible, and roughly planar, so homography estimation became the default estimand. The source records that many datasets provide homographies rather than full camera parameters [PAGE 3]. This annotation choice was practical. It made leaderboards comparable, code pipelines simpler, and visual overlays intuitive. However, once high-precision applications such as offside assessment, 3D ball trajectory reconstruction, player localization, and multi-view reconstruction became the target, the homography-only genealogy became a bottleneck. The future requirement retro-causalized the past: a benchmark that once seemed sufficient became insufficient because it could not certify off-plane 3D accuracy.

The first multi-week trap is dataset inertia. The source observes that World Cup 14 remains popular despite limitations [PAGE 3]. Once a dataset becomes the common reference, researchers optimize against its annotation type. If the annotation type is homography, the implicit reward encourages methods that align field markings under a plane-only model. This can improve measured alignment while failing to model radial distortion, lens imperfections, or off-plane geometry. The source later confirms this concern by showing that homography ground truth can fail to reproduce field markings when compared with richer camera models [PAGE 6].

The second trap is metric inertia. Intersection-over-union metrics such as IoUwhole and IoUpart are visually meaningful, but they inherit the limits of homography annotations [PAGE 3]. The source notes that these metrics rely on annotated homographies, “which are a limited and simplified interpretation of the observable field markings in the image” [PAGE 4]. A multi-week research loop can therefore reinforce itself: better IoU on the same homographies is treated as better calibration, even though the metric cannot observe non-planar structure. This creates a false convergence: many papers appear to improve, but the evaluation manifold remains too narrow.

The third trap is annotation correction without annotation replacement. CARWC improves World Cup 14 annotations and provides more precise homographies [PAGE 4]. The source acknowledges this as a “welcome enhancement in terms of precision” [PAGE 6]. Yet the source also finds that “the homography models fail to reproduce the images of the field markings” [PAGE 6]. This is a crucial retro-causal insight: improving the precision of a limited representation does not remove the representational limit. A more accurate plane mapping is still not a full camera model. The trap is to assume that better annotations of the wrong object class solve the calibration problem.

The fourth trap is camera-model premature closure. The source notes that top-tier sports events may involve many cameras of different types: wide-angle, fish-eye, super slow-motion, PTZ, static, and moving [PAGE 4]. Choosing one restrictive camera model at the outset can bias both method design and evaluation. The source argues that the protocol should allow any camera model and can help identify which model is best suited to the data [PAGE 4]. Retro-causally, this means that future production constraints require evaluation before model selection, not after.

The fifth trap is the illusion of visual sufficiency. The source shows that small differences in reprojected wireframes can hide large 3D disagreements; some field parts are seen over 2.5 meters apart by two models [PAGE 8]. A multi-week development cycle that relies only on visual overlay inspection can therefore normalize dangerous error. The genealogy moves from convenient homography overlays to model-agnostic reprojection of known 3D structure, but the trap persists whenever visual plausibility replaces measurable off-plane verification.

The crisis is that homography-based leaderboards no longer certify the accuracy needed for 3D applications. The agents then choose among habit regression, counter-trap adoption, or clock-driven compromise.

Habit regression is the first response. Under uncertainty, method developers often return to familiar metrics because they are comparable, reproducible, and publishable. If the community has years of IoU results on World Cup 14, a new method can claim progress by improving that score, even if the score is blind to off-plane error. The source shows why this is tempting: World Cup 14 is still “the most popular dataset for benchmarking” despite limitations [PAGE 3]. The habit loop is: familiar annotation → familiar metric → familiar leaderboard → incremental claims. This loop reduces immediate research risk but preserves the original blind spot.

The second response is the counter-trap. The source itself proposes a counter-trap: semantic 3D annotations plus JaC. This breaks the homography habit by evaluating projection of known 3D objects [PAGE 1]. However, any metric can become a new target for optimization. If JaC becomes the only reported number, developers may tune thresholds, select favorable images, exploit annotation density, or overfit to visible field classes. The source warns that with few field elements, “camera models may become overparameterized (and be overfitted)” [PAGE 7]. It also warns that “the uniqueness of the results is not guaranteed” because symmetry can produce multiple parameterizations with similar reprojection [PAGE 8]. Thus, the counter-trap is not automatically safe. It replaces plane-only blindness with threshold-conditioned proxy blindness unless physical checks are added.

The third response is clock pressure. Production systems need camera models quickly. Broadcast operations cannot wait for a complete physical ground-truth acquisition campaign. The source acknowledges that acquiring real ground truth for camera parameters “requires new datasets and much greater means to equip the production cameras with sensors” [PAGE 8]. Under clock pressure, a team may accept the best available proxy, such as JaC5, and deploy a radial-distortion model because it scores better. This can be rational: Table 3 shows pinhole plus one radial coefficient achieving JaC5 = 92.5, above CARWC homography at 79.1 [PAGE 6]. But the clock also creates a governance risk: if the proxy is accepted without off-plane validation, the system may pass benchmark review while failing operational accuracy.

A probabilistic belief-update view clarifies the recursion. Let \( B_t \) be the community’s posterior probability that model-agnostic 3D benchmarking is required. Evidence includes the source’s finding that homographies fail to reproduce field markings while richer camera models improve JaC and reduce reprojection error [PAGE 6]. As evidence accumulates, \( B_t \) should increase. But if publication incentives reward small improvements on legacy IoU, the community may assign too much weight to legacy evidence and update too slowly. The theory-of-mind failure is not ignorance of the new protocol; it is mispricing the cost of switching metrics.

The stable resolution requires a layered belief policy. First, treat JaC as a necessary screening metric, not a final physical truth. Second, require threshold sensitivity analysis: report JaC2 and JaC5, as the source does [PAGE 7]. Third, require off-plane probes: goal posts, ball trajectories, athlete localization, or surveyed 3D points. Fourth, require model-class comparison: homography, pinhole, pinhole plus radial distortion, and higher-order distortion models when justified. Fifth, require uniqueness audits for symmetric field configurations. Under this policy, the counter-trap becomes a diagnostic instrument rather than a new vanity metric.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto layer proves that no purely image-plane leaderboard can certify off-plane operational accuracy. Suppose two camera models, model A and model B, are both evaluated on field-plane annotations. Let \( \mathbf{p} \) be an image location corresponding to an off-plane or field-adjacent physical point. Let \( \mathbf{X}_A(\mathbf{p}) \) and \( \mathbf{X}_B(\mathbf{p}) \) be the backprojected 3D locations under the two models. If
\[
\left\|\mathbf{X}_A(\mathbf{p}) - \mathbf{X}_B(\mathbf{p})\right\|_2 > 0.5 \ \mathrm{m},
\]
then at least one model violates the 0.5 m accuracy condition identified by the source as “the minimal accuracy standard demanded by the FIFA for Offside Technologies” [PAGE 8]. This is a veto proof: a benchmark that cannot observe the disagreement cannot certify compliance. The source demonstrates a related physical disagreement, stating that “some parts of the sports field are seen over 2.5 meters apart by these two camera models” [PAGE 8]. That magnitude is five times the 0.5 m threshold, so the veto is not marginal.

The biomechanical veto layer addresses moving humans and balls. The source notes that semantic annotations could support calibration even when a 3D point belongs to a moving object, but only if the system can triangulate a 3D point and know its projection [PAGE 5]. This introduces a biomechanical constraint. Let \( \sigma_{\mathbf{X},\mathrm{bio}} \) be the 3D uncertainty of a moving anatomical or ball point caused by pose estimation, segmentation, motion blur, or temporal interpolation. Let \( J = \partial \pi / \partial \mathbf{X} \) be the local projection Jacobian with units pixels per meter. If
\[
\sigma_{\mathbf{X},\mathrm{bio}} > \frac{\tau}{\|J\|_2},
\]
then biomechanical uncertainty dominates the calibration threshold, and the moving point cannot serve as reliable calibration evidence. Breakdown: if \( \|J\|_2 \) is small, the inequality becomes easier to violate; if the point lies near a degenerate projection geometry, the Jacobian may be poorly conditioned. This veto prevents a counter-trap in which dynamic objects are used as calibration evidence without a motion or uncertainty model.

The weather veto layer addresses visibility and image formation. Let \( w \) denote weather or environmental state, such as rain, fog, glare, water droplets, snow, or low illumination. Let \( p_i(w) \) be the probability that annotated field element \( i \) is visible and measurable under state \( w \). The expected true-positive count is bounded by
\[
\mathbb{E}[TP_{\tau} \mid w] \le \sum_i p_i(w).
\]
If \( p_i(w) = 0 \) for enough elements, the metric loses evidence even if the camera model is physically correct. Conversely, if weather changes apparent line thickness or local refraction, the effective threshold may need adjustment. The source’s threshold logic assumes that field markings are visible enough for human annotation and that reprojection can be compared within \( \tau \) pixels [PAGE 5]. Weather violates that assumption by adding stochastic observation noise. Therefore, a high JaC under clear conditions does not automatically transfer to adverse conditions.

The cross-layer adjudication result is a hierarchy of vetoes. First, if off-plane geometric disagreement exceeds the application tolerance, plane-only metrics are vetoed. Second, if moving-object uncertainty exceeds the projected calibration tolerance, dynamic keypoints are vetoed as calibration evidence. Third, if environmental visibility collapses, JaC loses statistical power and must be supplemented by uncertainty-aware imaging models. The source’s protocol survives these vetoes only when combined with explicit physical checks: enough well-distributed elements [PAGE 7], model-class comparison [PAGE 6], threshold sensitivity [PAGE 6], and, ideally, independent 3D ground truth [PAGE 8].

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reward or validation driven by verifiable evidence. The following three counterfactual vetoes show where a calibration evaluation pipeline must reject a conclusion despite apparently favorable benchmark behavior.

Veto 1: Plane-only reward vetoes true 3D improvement.
Counterfactual: A method is evaluated only by IoU on World Cup 14 homographies. The method improves IoU by forcing stronger alignment to a plane-only ground truth, but it fails to model radial distortion or off-plane geometry. The source shows that homography ground truth can be inadequate: “the homography models fail to reproduce the images of the field markings” [PAGE 6]. Prediction: Under ProCC-style semantic annotation and JaC5, the plane-only method will underperform a pinhole-plus-radial model on images with visible off-plane elements or off-axis field markings. Verifiable test: compare JaC5 and off-plane backprojection error on goal posts, field corners, and off-field points. If the plane-only method has lower JaC5 or larger off-plane disagreement, the veto is confirmed. The source’s Table 3 is consistent with this: pinhole plus one radial coefficient reaches JaC5 = 92.5, while CARWC homography reaches 79.1 [PAGE 6].

Veto 2: Threshold misspecification vetoes an otherwise valid calibration.
Counterfactual: A camera model is physically good, but the evaluation uses an excessively strict threshold without accounting for annotation thickness, human labeling noise, or lens distortion. The source defines correctness as all annotated points lying within \( \tau \) pixels of the projected polyline [PAGE 5]. If \( \tau \) is too small, small annotation jitter can turn a correct calibration into false negatives. Prediction: JaC will drop disproportionately as \( \tau \) decreases from 5 pixels to 2 pixels, and method rankings may become unstable. The source already uses two thresholds, JaC5 and JaC2, to distinguish precision levels [PAGE 6]. Verifiable test: sweep \( \tau \) over a plausible range, such as 1 to 10 pixels, and measure rank correlation. If rankings collapse at strict thresholds while visual overlays remain plausible, the veto is confirmed. This veto prevents a naive RLVR reward from treating threshold artifacts as physical truth.
