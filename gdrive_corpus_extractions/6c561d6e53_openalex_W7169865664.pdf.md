Definitions and assumptions. Let \(m\) index systems, \(n=1,\dots,N\) index matches, and \(k\in\{H,D,A\}\) index home win, draw, and away win. The central assumption is the pre-event lock: predictions and evidence are saved before kickoff, and no post-deadline source may enter [PAGE 3, PAGE 14]. Units: probabilities are dimensionless; scores are goal counts; layer and composite scores are dimensionless display points. No SI physical units are defined by the benchmark.

Standard Brier score, used for probabilistic result calibration [PAGE 4]:
\[
B_m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in\{H,D,A\}}\left(\hat p_{mnk}-y_{nk}\right)^2 .
\]
Units are probability-squared. Standard convention is lower-is-better. The paper also says “All metrics are defined so that a larger value is better” [PAGE 4], so a display inversion or transformation is required; the excerpt does not specify it. Breakdown: \(B_m\) is undefined only if \(N=0\); it is not a proper scoring rule if probabilities are not normalized, which is why the parser checks normalization [PAGE 14].

Result accuracy and exact-score accuracy [PAGE 4]:
\[
A_m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\left[\arg\max_k \hat p_{mnk}=\arg\max_k y_{nk}\right],
\qquad
E_m=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\left[\hat s_{mn}=s_n\right].
\]
Both are dimensionless proportions in \([0,1]\), often displayed as percentages. Breakdown: \(A_m\) is undefined if \(N=0\); ties in \(\arg\max\) need a fixed rule. \(E_m\) has a low base rate and treats every miss equally, which is why the paper adds a scoreline score [PAGE 4].

Raw scoreline score for a non-exact prediction [PAGE 13, Eq. 1]:
\[
S_{\rm score}=45I_r+25\left[1-\frac{e_d}{5}\right]_+ +20\left[1-\frac{e_t}{6}\right]_+ +10\left[1-\frac{e_{\rm team}}{8}\right]_+,

where \([x]_+=\max(x,0)\), \(I_r\in\{0,1\}\) marks a correct result class, \(e_d\) is goal-difference error, \(e_t\) is sum-goal error, and \(e_{\rm team}\) is team-wise goal error. Breakdown: each bracketed term saturates at zero when its error exceeds the cap, so large misses cannot produce negative values; exact predictions use the separate 100 endpoint. If the error definitions are changed, the 45/25/20/10 weights no longer correspond to the stated allocation.

{\sigma(6)-\sigma(-14)},
\qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]
Units are display points in \([0,100]\) approximately. The denominator is positive, so there is no finite singularity. Breakdown: \(\bar S_{\rm score}\) is undefined if no eligible scoreline task exists; extrapolation far outside the calibrated range is monotone but not evidence of interval-scale meaning. The paper says the calibration preserves order and the 0/100 endpoints and is not fitted to the evaluated systems [PAGE 13].

That defense works for confirmed absences, not for unobserved data.

C(R)=100\frac{\sigma\left(\frac{R-50}{5}\right)-\sigma(-10)}
{\sigma(10)-\sigma(-10)},

\]
Units are display points. The raw composite \(R\) is the fixed weighted mean of layers with weights 0.40, 0.20, 0.15, 0.15, 0.10 [PAGE 4]:

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s measurement lineage is explicit. The Brier score comes from 1950 verification theory and is used here to separate calibration from bare result accuracy [PAGE 4]. Kendall’s \(\tau\) is used for group-order agreement; nDCG is used for ranked player lists, citing Järvelin and Kekäläinen [PAGE 5]; Hungarian assignment is used to match predicted events to real events, citing Kuhn [PAGE 5]; sMAPE is used for count statistics, citing Hyndman and Koehler [PAGE 5]. The benchmark genealogy also runs through dynamic forecasting benchmarks: RealTime QA and LiveBench for freshness, ForecastQA and Autocast for historical cutoffs, and ForecastBench for pre-outcome collection [PAGE 2]. Sports understanding lineage runs through SoccerNet-v2, MatchTime, UniSoccer, Sports-QA, and SPORTU, which mostly evaluate completed events [PAGE 2]. WorldCupArena’s novelty is the combination of pre-event lock, broad output schema, and post-match truth across five layers.

The retrocausal risk is that later records can make earlier forecasts look more skilled than they were. The protocol blocks this in three places. First, the prediction deadline is T−24h, and post-deadline information is excluded [PAGE 3]. Second, the competition forecast is a single pre-tournament artifact: group order, bracket, finalist, and champion claims are “elicited and frozen before the opening match,” and the program never asks the model to revise the bracket after an outcome is known [PAGE 14]. Third, truth acquisition is independent and normalized before scoring; the record must satisfy basic score, event, and statistic consistency checks [PAGE 4]. This creates a multi-week trap sequence. In the pre-tournament week, the model must commit to a bracket. In the final 24 hours before each match, it may use squad and availability news but not confirmed lineups or later reports. During the match, in-play checkpoints record observed minute, score, events, and available statistics [PAGE 3]. After the match, truth enters only for grading. After the tournament, champion Spain is known, so any new “prediction” that uses that fact is retrospective contamination, not prospective skill.

A second multi-week trap is bracket derivation. The program may derive later fixtures from the model’s own group forecast, but it “never asks the model to revise the bracket after an outcome is known” [PAGE 14]. This is important because a model could accidentally name the finalists through a wrong bracket and still receive champion credit. The T5 formula distinguishes full-route credit from champion-only credit through the \(0.7A+0.3M\) term [PAGE 13]. The paper reports that four systems selected Spain as champion, and only the two Claude configurations also predicted the Spain–Argentina final [PAGE 6]. That distinction is exactly the kind of retrocausal audit the protocol is designed to preserve.

A third trap is baseline timing. The market and human-fan baselines are descriptive because their timestamps and fixture intersections differ from the S1 T−24h horizon [PAGE 5]. A later baseline can incorporate lineup news that S1 did not have; an earlier baseline can miss injury news. Therefore a small difference between the best system and a baseline is not a clean skill difference. The paper itself says the best system shows “only small gains” in result and exact-score accuracy against those baselines [PAGE 1], which is appropriately modest.

A fourth trap is search contamination. S2 agents search until the deadline, and the run manifest records provider, model identifier, tool configuration, query and response timestamps, retrieved URLs, tool-call count, latency, token counts, and the complete response [PAGE 14]. This is a good audit trail, but it does not equalize search budgets across providers. The paper explicitly does not claim “that S2 has a common search budget or that its difference from S1 is a retrieval-only effect” [PAGE 14]. Thus the historical lineage from retrieval-augmented agents to this benchmark is not a causal lineage. The correct next experiment is a randomized or budget-matched retrieval intervention, not a comparison of complete products.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive-theory-of-mind dimension here is not about a model inferring a single opponent’s private belief. It is about a model inferring the evaluator’s information clock, the crowd’s prior, and its own future update rule. Three failure modes organize the evidence.

Crisis habit regression. Under high uncertainty, systems regress to the modal favorite and the modal scoreline. The consensus-failure table shows this directly: for Spain–Cape Verde, “all systems selected Spain by 3–0 or 4–0, but the match ended 0–0” [PAGE 9]; for France–England in the third-place playoff, the unanimous favorite pick was reversed in a ten-goal match [PAGE 9]. The paper also reports that “All 13 systems selected France, Spain, Brazil, and Argentina as semifinalists, missing England” [PAGE 6]. This is not independent reasoning; it is a shared prior. A model that recursively models other forecasters may converge on the crowd’s answer because it expects the crowd to be right, or because its training distribution rewards the modal narrative. The benchmark measures the output, not the internal belief, so it cannot distinguish genuine confidence from herd-following. But the pattern is consistent with crisis habit regression: when the match is difficult, the model falls back to the favorite and a conventional score.

Counter-trap. The obvious corrective is to add search, but the paper shows that the corrective can become a trap. “On shared fixtures, the no-search version scores higher for all three paired systems” [PAGE 6], yet “the comparison is not a causal retrieval ablation” [PAGE 6]. A model that searches may retrieve the same consensus, may retrieve outdated or low-quality reports, or may overfit to a single preview. The counter-trap is assuming that more evidence is automatically better evidence. Recursive theory of mind matters because an agent that models the evaluator may search for “what will sound well-sourced” rather than “what will improve calibration.” The S2 manifest records URLs and timestamps [PAGE 14], so this is auditable, but the paper does not show that S2 agents used independent sources. The safest interpretation is descriptive: search did not reliably improve match prediction in this tournament, and the causal effect remains unidentified.

Clock. The clock is the strongest discipline in the paper. The forecast is locked at T−24h [PAGE 3]; in-play checkpoints record observed minute, score, events, and statistics [PAGE 3]; after a goal, accuracy rises, but the paper says “These gains measure adaptation to new match state, not anticipation of the goal” [PAGE 8]. A recursive model must represent three different information sets: what it knew at T−24h, what it knows at minute \(t\), and what the evaluator will know after the final whistle. Confusing these sets produces false skill. For example, a model that updates its final-score prediction after a goal is doing legitimate state-conditioned inference. A model that claims it “knew” the goal would happen is making a retrocausal claim that the checkpoint design can veto. The benchmark’s best defense is to compare consecutive checkpoints around the score change and to require that pre-goal predictions be evaluated before the event. The paper’s goal-pair analysis is exactly that kind of clock-aware test [PAGE 8].

The recursive lesson is therefore: do not reward a model for modeling the crowd, and do not reward it for modeling the evaluator’s hindsight. Reward it for making a calibrated forecast under a fixed information set, then updating only when the clock advances. The paper’s five-layer schema helps because a model cannot hide a bad result prediction behind a good rationale; T2–T4 expose whether the predicted players, events, and statistics are coherent with the predicted score. But the schema does not prove that the model’s internal theory of mind is accurate. It only proves that the output is temporally and structurally auditable.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether a lower-layer physical, biomechanical, or weather fact can invalidate an upper-layer forecast. The source supports a narrow version of this veto and not a broad causal one.

First, the allowed physical layer is match-state, not sensor physics. In-play records give “the current minute, score, events, and available match statistics” to models [PAGE 3]. That is a physical/observational state layer in the sense of observable match facts. It can veto a forecast that contradicts the current score or event record: a model cannot predict a 2–0 final score at minute 80 when the observed score is already 3–0 without an explicit correction. The parser also checks “result–score consistency” [PAGE 14], and the normalized truth record must satisfy “basic score, event, and statistic consistency checks” [PAGE 4]. These are real cross-layer constraints.

Second, there is no biomechanical layer in the contract. The S1 input is a JSON object with a fixture header and five evidence blocks covering squad and availability information, recent results, team and player statistics, pre-lock news headlines, and pre-lock market information [PAGE 14]. Injury or availability status may appear in news, but there is no force plate, GPS load, gait, sprint biomechanics, soft-tissue measurement, or fatigue sensor. Therefore any claim of the form “team A lost because of a biomechanical collapse” is not adjudicable from this source. The correct cross-layer veto is: if a causal claim requires biomechanical data that the benchmark does not contain, the claim is outside the evidence contract and cannot be used to explain a forecast error.

Third, there is no weather layer. The fixture header includes venue and kickoff time [PAGE 14], but not temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, pitch condition, or altitude. Weather may be mentioned in pre-lock news headlines, but that is text evidence, not a measured weather variable. A claim that a specific upset was caused by weather is therefore a cross-layer overreach. The source cannot prove or disprove it. The veto is not “weather is irrelevant”; the veto is “weather is not a measured layer in this benchmark, so it cannot be invoked as a verified explanation.”

Fourth, T4 statistics are perceptual/aggregate outputs, not physical measurements. Predicted possession, shots, corners, passing, fouls, and saves [PAGE 4] are count or percentage targets scored by sMAPE [PAGE 5]. A model can predict 60% possession and be close without any biomechanical model. Therefore T4 cannot serve as a physical causal layer. It can only serve as a consistency layer: if a model predicts a 4–0 win but also predicts zero shots on target, the forecast is internally implausible. The paper’s consistency checks are basic, not a full causal graph [PAGE 4].

The resulting adjudication rule is: a cross-layer veto is valid only when the lower layer is actually observed in the benchmark contract. Observed score, events, lineups, and available statistics can veto contradictory upper-layer claims. Weather and biomechanics cannot veto anything because they are not measured. If a model uses post-deadline weather or injury reports, the temporal veto applies first: “A prediction is excluded if it contains information published after the deadline or reveals the final result” [PAGE 3]. That is the strongest cross-layer proof in the paper. It is a proof by contract, not a physical proof.

Scenario 1: Post-deadline leakage veto. For every saved prediction \(i\), require \(t_i\le t_{\rm kickoff}-24h\) and require that every cited URL or document timestamp be no later than the prediction deadline. The source states: “A prediction is excluded if it contains information published after the deadline or reveals the final result” [PAGE 3]. Verifiable prediction: if a timestamp audit is run over the released evidence snapshots and URLs, any prediction that used a post-deadline source will be removable, and its apparent skill will shrink or disappear. If a system’s high score depends on a source published after the deadline, the score is vetoed rather than explained as forecasting skill. This scenario is RLVR-checkable because timestamps and URLs are objective.

Scenario 2: Search causal-effect veto. Randomize or budget-match S1 and S2 within the same fixture set, holding provider, prompt, output schema, and scoring fixed. Estimate the search contrast
\[
\Delta_{\rm search}=\mathbb E\left[Y\mid S2\right]-\mathbb E\left[Y\mid S1\right]
\]
on the pre-specified composite and on T1. The paper warns that “Because these are complete products, the comparison is not a causal retrieval ablation” [PAGE 6] and that S2 does not have “a common search budget” [PAGE 14]. Verifiable prediction: a budget-matched randomized contrast will not reproduce a reliable positive search effect on match prediction; if it does, the effect should survive provider controls and source-quality controls. If the effect vanishes under those controls, the claim “search improves forecasting” is vetoed. The existing descriptive pattern that “the no-search version scores higher for all three paired systems” [PAGE 6] is not enough to establish the opposite causal claim either.

Scenario 3: In-play anticipation veto. For every consecutive checkpoint pair with an observed score change, compare pre-goal and post-goal Brier scores and result accuracy on the same pair. The paper reports that after an observed score change, result accuracy rises to 77.4–78.9% and Brier improves by 8.4–12.1 points, but says “These gains measure adaptation to new match state, not anticipation of the goal” [PAGE 8]. Verifiable prediction: if a model truly anticipates the goal, its pre-goal checkpoint should already show lower Brier and higher accuracy than a matched no-goal control; if the gain appears only after the event, the anticipation claim is vetoed. A model that merely updates well after the goal receives adaptation credit, not precognition credit. This is verifiable from the stored checkpoint timestamps, observed minutes, and score states [PAGE 3, PAGE 8].
