The extensive-form LP has constraints \(Az=b\), \(T^l z+W y^l=h^l\), \(z,y^l\ge 0\), and objective \(cz+\sum_l p_l q^l y^l\). Page 1 states: [PAGE 1] “NOT FOR QUOTATION WITHOUT PERMISSION OF THE AUTHOR.” Short verbatim excerpts below are used only for verification.

Core recourse operator. Let \(z\in\mathbb{R}_+^{n_1}\) be the first-stage decision. Let \(\xi^l=(q^l,h^l,T^l)\) for \(l=1,\ldots,L\), with \(p_l=\Pr[\xi(\omega)=\xi^l]\). The source defines the recourse value on [PAGE 4]:
\[
Q(z,\xi^l)=\inf\left\{q^l y\ \middle|\ Wy=h^l-T^l z,\ y\in\mathbb{R}_+^{n_2}\right\}.
\]
The stochastic objective is
\[
\mathcal{Q}(z)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l Q(z,\xi^l)
=\mathbb{E}\left[Q(z,\xi(\omega))\right],
\]
and the problem is
\[
\min_{z}\ cz+\mathcal{Q}(z)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Az=b,\ z\in\mathbb{R}_+^{n_1}.
\]
[PAGE 3] supports the setting: “We study the use of large scale linear programming techniques for solving (linear) recourse problems”. [PAGE 4] supports the fixed-recourse restriction: “Because W is nonstochastic we refer to this problem as a model with $zed recourse.” The tuple definition is also on [PAGE 4]: “= ( q ' . h l . T ' ) = (q: ,...,q n2;hi . . . hm2;t:l , , t i n l , . , t m ~ n l” in OCR form; mathematically the tuple is \((q^l,h^l,T^l)\). The matrix dimensions are \(z\in\mathbb{R}^{n_1}\), \(y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_2}\), \(b\in\mathbb{R}^{m_1}\), \(h^l\in\mathbb{R}^{m_2}\), \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{m_2\times n_2}\), and \(T^l\in\mathbb{R}^{m_2\times n_1}\).

Extensive-form operator. Substituting the scenario recourse problems yields
\[
\min\ cz+\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l q^l y^l
\]
subject to
\[
Az=b,\qquad T^l z+W y^l=h^l,\qquad z\ge 0,\qquad y^l\ge 0,
\quad l=1,\ldots,L.
\]
[PAGE 7] states the size: “This is a linear program with m, + m2.L constraints, an d n l + n2.L variables.” The same page says the structure must be exploited: “it is necessary to exploit the properties of this highly structured large scale linear program.” [PAGE 8] identifies the form: “dual block angular structure with the important additional feature that all the matrices, except for A, along the block diagonal are the same.”

Dual operator. The dual form on [PAGE 9] is
\[
\max\ ub+\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l \pi^l h^l
\]
subject to
\[
uA+\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l \pi^l T^l\le c,
\qquad
\pi^l W\le q^l,\quad l=1,\ldots,L.
\]
Here \(u\) and \(\pi^l\) are unrestricted dual variables. [PAGE 8] notes that Dantzig and Madansky, 1961, suggested a dual solution procedure. The dual block structure is the basis for decomposition.

Basis-factorization operator. [PAGE 13] imposes the rank condition: “We assume that W is of full row rank”. It also assumes \(A\) full row rank, “(possibly 0 when there are no constraints of that type).” A basic feasible solution includes at least \(n_2\) variables of each subsystem
\[
\pi^l W+s^l=q^l,\qquad s^l\ge 0,\quad l=1,\ldots,L.
\]
The basis block is split into an invertible \(n_2\times n_2\) part plus slack columns. [PAGE 16] gives the compact-inverse reduction: “The system (2.6) involves nl equations in nl variables and the L systems (2.5) are of order nz.” Thus the work is not an inverse of order \(n_1+n_2L\); it is \(L\) inverses of order \(n_2\) plus one inverse of order \(n_1\). [PAGE 17] further says it suffices to know the inverse of \(W_{(L)}\), an \(m_2\times m_2\) matrix, relying on sparse updates. The breakdown regime is exact: if \(W\) is not full row rank, or if the chosen \(W_{(l)}\) is singular, the compact basis inverse does not exist. If \(A\) is not full row rank, redundant first-stage constraints must be removed or transformed.

Decomposition master operator. [PAGE 17] says: “The decomposition principle, as used to solve the linear program (1.3), generates the master problem from the equations”. The simplified bounded case assumes no recession directions other than zero: [PAGE 17] “i.e. there are no directions of recession other than 0”. The master problem on [PAGE 18] uses extreme points \(\eta_k\) generated by subproblem iterations and weights \(h_k\ge 0\), with
\[
uA+\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}h_k\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l\eta^{lk}T^l\right)\le c,
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}h_k=1,
\]
maximizing
\[
ub+\sum_{k=1}^{\nu}h_k\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l\eta^{lk}h^l\right).
\]
The master basis is order \(n_1\); each subproblem basis is order \(n_2\). If scenario polyhedra are unbounded, recession directions must be added; otherwise the master is incomplete.

L-shaped primal operator. [PAGE 20] describes the L-shaped algorithm as “a cutting hyperplane algorithm (outer linearization)” and “a partial decomposition method.” The master problem in Step 1 on [PAGE 22] is
\[
\min\ cz+\vartheta
\]
subject to
\[
Az=b,\qquad D_k z\ge d_k,\quad k=1,\ldots,r,
\qquad E_k z+\vartheta\ge e_k,\quad k=1,\ldots,s,
\qquad z\ge 0.
\]
The feasibility subproblem in Step 2 on [PAGE 22] is
\[
\min\ e v^+ + e v^-
\]
subject to
\[
Wy+v^+-v^-=h^l-T^l z^\nu,\qquad
y\ge 0,\quad v^+\ge 0,\quad v^-\ge 0,
\]
where \(e=(1,1,\ldots,1)\). If the optimal value \(v^l\gt 0\), a feasibility cut is generated. The cost subproblem in Step 3 on [PAGE 23] is
\[
\min\ q^l y
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Wy=h^l-T^l z^\nu,\quad y\ge 0.
\]
With optimal multipliers \(\pi^{l\nu}\), the optimality cut uses
\[
w^\nu=\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l\pi^{l\nu}\left(h^l-T^l z^\nu\right)
=e_\nu-E_\nu z^\nu.
\]
[PAGE 23] states the stopping rule: “If gV;r wV, we stop; zV is the optimal solution.” The convergence argument on [PAGE 21] rests on finite bases of \(W\) and finite weighted averages of the scenario data; nondegeneracy is the stated qualification. Degeneracy can produce duplicate cuts, cycling, or a need for anti-cycling safeguards.

Complete-recourse and relatively-complete-recourse operators. [PAGE 24] says: “Step 2 can be skipped altogether if the stochastic program is with complete recourse”. Formally, complete recourse means
\[
\operatorname{pos}(W)=\mathbb{R}^{m_2},
\]
so \(Wy=h^l-T^l z,\ y\ge 0\), is feasible for every right-hand side. [PAGE 24] also defines relatively complete recourse: for every \(z\) satisfying \(Az=b,\ z\ge 0\), and every \(l\), the system
\[
Wy=h^l-T^l z,\qquad y\ge 0
\]
is feasible. This condition is weaker and harder to verify; without it, Step 2 is not automatically skippable. The breakdown regime is a false complete-recourse assumption: if \(\operatorname{pos}(W)\neq\mathbb{R}^{m_2}\), some scenario may require a feasibility cut, and omitting Step 2 can return an infeasible first-stage decision.

Nonstochastic-\(T\) reduction. When \(T^l=T\) for all \(l\), [PAGE 29] reformulates Step 1:
\[
\min\ cz+\vartheta
\]
subject to
\[
Az=b,\qquad Tz-x=0,
\qquad F_k x\ge f_k,\quad k=1,\ldots,r,
\qquad G_k x+\vartheta\ge g_k,\quad k=1,\ldots,s.
\]
The nonlinear variable is handled in dimension \(m_2\), not \(n_1\). [PAGE 30] interprets this as searching for \(x^*\), “the certainty equivalent.” Then the original problem can be solved by
\[
\min\ cz
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Az=b,\qquad Tz=x^*,\qquad z\ge 0.
\]
The exact breakdown regime: if \(T^l\) varies with \(l\), the substitution \(x=Tz\) is invalid and the certainty-equivalent reduction cannot be used.

Units and information audit. The source is an abstract LP and does not assign SI base units. A consistent unit convention is: \(p_l\) is dimensionless probability; \(q^l_j\) has objective-unit per \(y_j\); \(c_i\) has objective-unit per \(z_i\); \(W_{ij}\) maps \(y_j\) units into constraint-row units; \(T^l_{ij}\) maps \(z_j\) units into constraint-row units. The finite-support distribution has entropy
\[
H(p)=-\sum_{l=1}^{L}p_l\ln p_l\ \text{nats},
\qquad
0\le H(p)\le \ln L,
\]
with \(0\ln 0=0\). This is an information audit, not a source equation. If \(p_l\lt 0\) or \(\sum_l p_l\neq 1\), the expectation operator and entropy are undefined. If \(L\) is finite but enormous, exact extensive-form memory scales like \(O(L(m_2+n_2))\), which is the practical singularity regime even when the mathematical LP remains finite.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is explicit in the working paper. The dual decomposition proposal is attributed to Dantzig and Madansky, 1961, on [PAGE 8] and [PAGE 13]. The primal L-shaped method is attributed to Van Slyke and Wets, 1969, on [PAGE 20]. The substitution that produces the staircase structure is attributed to Wets, 1966, on [PAGE 10]. Basis factorization is attributed to Strazicky, 1980, with later analysis by Kall, 1979, Wets, 1983, and Birge in Chapter 12, on [PAGE 13]. The equivalence of effort between basis factorization and dual decomposition is noted on [PAGE 18], and the preference for the primal version is argued on [PAGE 19]. The dynamic-systems analogy is tied to Perold and Dantzig, 1979, Fourer, 1984, and Saunders, 1983, on [PAGE 11] and [PAGE 12]. The Nazareth reformulation appears on [PAGE 29], and the certainty-equivalent interpretation on [PAGE 30]. Approximation and statistical-analysis references include Birge and Wets, 1984, Dupacova and Wets, 1984, and Salinetti, 1983, on [PAGE 3] and [PAGE 5].

The retrocausal genealogy matters because later readers often collapse the distinction between three operators that historically diverged: dual decomposition, compact basis factorization, and primal cutting-plane L-shaped iteration. The first is a Dantzig–Madansky dual master; the second is a Strazicky–Kall–Wets–Birge basis-factorization scheme; the third is the Van Slyke–Wets primal cut generator. They are mathematically close but operationally different. [PAGE 18] says they “require the same computational effort,” while [PAGE 19] says the primal version offers “organizational flexibility.” That is not a contradiction; it is a statement about equivalent algebra versus software control. A modern implementation that treats all three as the same algorithm will miss the primal feasibility advantage: the L-shaped method always carries a feasible first-stage \(z\), which permits early stopping, warm starts, and scenario subproblem reuse.

The historical analogy to dynamical systems is also a trap. [PAGE 12] warns: “dynarnical systems have coefficients (data) that are I-parameter depen- dent (time) whereas we can view the coefficients of stochastic problems as being multi-parameter dependent.” The staircase matrix may look like a discrete-time control problem, but the scenario index is not time. Scenario data can be reordered, split, duplicated, or generated by a distribution; time cannot be reordered without changing the system. Multi-week trap one: a team sees the staircase and imports time-series state-space methods, then misinterprets \(l\) as a time step and applies causal filtering. Multi-week trap two: a team sees the block angular form and builds a dense extensive-form LP, then discovers memory failure only after the scenario count grows. Multi-week trap three: a team assumes the dual basis factorization is automatically cheaper than decomposition, ignoring the organizational overhead and the need for sparse updates. Multi-week trap four: a team treats the certainty-equivalent \(x^*\) as a forecast rather than a decision variable; [PAGE 30] uses “the certainty equivalent” in the optimization sense, not as a predictive point estimate. Multi-week trap five: a team assumes complete recourse without proving \(\operatorname{pos}(W)=\mathbb{R}^{m_2}\), then suppresses Step 2 and returns an infeasible \(z\). Multi-week trap six: a team uses a coarse discretization with very small \(L\) and reports a stable solution without a sequential refinement scheme; [PAGE 5] explicitly allows \(L\) “less than 20 or 30” in limited-statistical-information cases, but that is a modeling approximation, not a guarantee of distributional robustness. Multi-week trap seven: a team confuses the finite-support empirical distribution with the true continuous distribution; [PAGE 5] says the empirical distribution is used “the idea being of submitting its solution to statistical analysis,” which requires a separate statistical layer.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory-of-mind here is the solver’s model of the decision-maker’s model of uncertainty. The first-stage decision \(z\) is chosen before the scenario is revealed; the second-stage \(y^l\) is a best response to the realized \(h^l-T^l z\). The L-shaped master is therefore a recursive anticipation operator: Step 1 proposes \(z^\nu\), Step 2 checks whether each scenario’s recourse problem can respond feasibly, and Step 3 checks whether the master’s linear approximation has correctly anticipated the expected recourse cost. The master is not “wrong” in a generic sense; it is under-informed until the subproblems reveal a missing cut. [PAGE 21] frames this as outer linearization, and [PAGE 21] says the Step 3 row is “a subgradient” of the recourse function. The recursive loop stops when the master’s estimate matches the subproblem-generated value, as in [PAGE 23]: “If gV;r wV, we stop; zV is the optimal solution.”

Crisis habit regression. Under stress, a planner regresses to a deterministic certainty-equivalent habit: replace the random scenario by a single average or base case, solve \(Az=b,\ Tz=x^*,\ \min cz\), and call the result optimal. [PAGE 30] does describe the search for \(x^*\) as “the certainty equivalent,” but that is valid only inside the nonstochastic-\(T\) reformulation and only as an algorithmic device. The regression error is to treat \(x^*\) as a sufficient statistic for the whole distribution. In a crisis, this habit discards the feasibility cuts and optimality cuts that encode scenario-specific responses. The counter-trap is over-conservatism: generate every possible feasibility cut, assume the worst scenario, or impose complete recourse when it is not proved. That avoids infeasibility but can be massively inefficient and can distort the first-stage decision. The correct middle is the cut clock: generate only cuts that are violated by the current master, and stop on the verified gap.

Counter-trap. A second failure mode is to treat the dual multipliers or the scenario probabilities as beliefs to be recursively updated by narrative. The source does not provide a Bayesian belief-update operator. The probabilities \(p_l\) are fixed discrete weights in the expectation. The multipliers \(\pi^{l\nu}\) are LP duals, not posterior beliefs. If a user changes \(p_l\) after seeing a cut, the objective changes and the old cuts may no longer be valid for the new distribution. The counter-trap is therefore “narrative updating”: adding cuts because the story feels riskier, not because a subproblem produced a violated inequality. The verifier is arithmetic: a cut must be generated by a solved subproblem and must separate the current master point.

Clock. The algorithmic clock is the triple \((v,r,s)\) in [PAGE 22]: iteration count, number of feasibility cuts, number of optimality cuts. The master’s \(\vartheta\) is a lower bound in the minimization form; the subproblem aggregate is an upper-bound estimate when the first-stage \(z^\nu\) is feasible. The gap closes monotonically under exact solves and nondegeneracy. The clock is not wall-clock time; it is the finite basis enumeration clock. [PAGE 21] grounds convergence in finitely many bases of \(W\) and finitely many weighted averages of scenario data. If the solver cycles or repeats cuts, the right response is anti-cycling, perturbation, or basis repair, not a new story about the scenario. The recursive mind should ask: is the current master infeasible because of a missing feasibility cut, or is it feasible but underestimating cost because of a missing optimality cut? Step 2 answers the first; Step 3 answers the second. Mixing them is a category error.

The theory-of-mind trap is also present in the scenario-generation layer. [PAGE 5] describes sequential approximation schemes and the use of empirical distributions; [PAGE 20] notes that primal methods extend naturally to arbitrary distribution functions and successive refinement. A planner who treats a finite \(L\) as the world rather than as a discretization will overfit the sample. A planner who treats the world as continuous but refuses to refine the discretization will underfit. The recursive discipline is: solve the finite-support problem exactly, audit the optimal \(z\) against refinement, and only then claim distributional robustness. The clock for that outer loop is separate from the cut clock; confusing them is another multi-week trap.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source is a mathematical programming layer, not a physical, biomechanical, or meteorological model. Therefore cross-layer claims are admissible only if they can be expressed through the source primitives \(q^l,h^l,T^l,W,p_l,z,y^l\). The tuple on [PAGE 4] defines the model’s entire data surface. [PAGE 4] also states the fixed-recourse assumption: “Because W is nonstochastic we refer to this problem as a model with $zed recourse.” If a claimed physical force, biomechanical load, or weather event cannot be mapped into a scenario-dependent \(q^l\), \(h^l\), or \(T^l\), it is outside the model. If it can be mapped but violates the rank, boundedness, probability, or nondegeneracy conditions, it is vetoed by the mathematical layer.

Physical veto proof. Suppose a user asserts that an external physical effect changes the decision. For admissibility, there must exist a measurable mapping from the physical state to a scenario index \(l\) and data \((q^l,h^l,T^l)\), with units consistent with the LP. The source provides no SI base dimensions and no physical laws; it provides only linear constraints and a linear objective. Therefore the default verdict is veto: the source cannot derive a physical force law. The only exception is a data injection that preserves the LP form. If the injection changes \(W\), the fixed-recourse assumption fails; [PAGE 4]’s “W is nonstochastic” is no longer true, and the special decomposition shortcuts tied to identical block-diagonal matrices on [PAGE 8] no longer hold in the same form. If the injection changes \(T^l\), the certainty-equivalent reduction on [PAGE 29] and [PAGE 30] is invalid. If it changes \(h^l\), then feasibility and Step 2 cuts must be regenerated. If it changes \(q^l\), then the optimality cuts and objective change. A physical claim that does none of these is not a model input.

Biomechanical veto proof. A biomechanical layer would normally introduce nonlinear constitutive laws, fatigue, contact, or kinematic constraints. The source has only linear equalities and inequalities. A biomechanical variable can enter as an LP variable only if it is already a linear decision variable in \(y^l\) or \(z\), with a linear cost in \(q^l\) or \(c\) and linear constraints in \(W\) or \(T^l\). There is no source operator for force-fatigue interaction, no tensor, no differential equation, and no nonlinear material law. Therefore any claim that the model “accounts for” biomechanics beyond a linear constraint block is vetoed. The admitted version is narrow: biomechanical quantities may parameterize \(h^l\), \(T^l\), or \(q^l\), and then the algorithm solves the resulting linear recourse problem. The rank condition on [PAGE 13] still applies: “We assume that W is of full row rank.” A biomechanical constraint block that makes \(W\) rank-deficient or makes the recourse polyhedron unbounded triggers the singularity and recession regimes.

Weather veto proof. Weather can enter only as a finite scenario set or as a distribution approximated by one. [PAGE 5] describes discretization of an absolutely continuous probability measure and coarser discretization of a large finite set. A weather claim that is not discretized into \(L\) scenarios with probabilities \(p_l\) has no operator in the source. If it is discretized, the model treats it as exogenous data \(h^l,T^l,q^l\), not as a feedback system. The source has no weather dynamics, no forecast operator, and no time causality. [PAGE 12] distinguishes one-parameter time dependence from multi-parameter stochastic dependence; weather time cannot be silently identified with the scenario index. Therefore a claim that “weather causes the algorithm to adapt” is vetoed unless adaptation is explicitly implemented as an outer scenario-refinement loop, which is not the L-shaped inner cut loop. The inner loop’s clock is \((v,r,s)\); the outer weather-refinement clock is a separate modeling decision.

The cross-layer verdict is thus a two-stage test. First, closure test: can the claim be written as a change to \(q^l,h^l,T^l,W,p_l\) or to the LP constraints? If no, veto. Second, validity test: does the changed model still satisfy \(p_l\ge 0\), \(\sum_l p_l=1\), full row rank of \(W\), full row rank of \(A\), boundedness or explicit recession directions, and nondegeneracy for finite-cut convergence? If no, veto or repair. This is a proof by model closure, not a physical proof. The source cannot adjudicate a real biomechanical or weather mechanism; it can only adjudicate whether that mechanism has been encoded as a well-posed linear recourse program.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR is interpreted here as reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: each counterfactual has an executable verifier and a falsifiable prediction. No wagering constructs are used.

Scenario 1: Rank-deficient \(W\). Intervention: perturb \(W\) so that \(\operatorname{rank}(W)\lt m_2\), while keeping all other data fixed. Verifier: compute numerical rank of \(W\) and test whether every scenario recourse problem \(Wy=h^l-T^l z,\ y\ge 0\), is feasible for the candidate \(z\). Prediction: the compact basis-factorization algorithm will fail to form an invertible \(n_2\times n_2\) basis block, or it will report infeasibility for at least one scenario; if the implementation assumes complete recourse, it will return a \(z\) that fails a Step 2 feasibility check. Falsification: if the algorithm returns a verified feasible \(z\) for all scenarios without adding a feasibility cut or deleting redundant rows, then the rank-deficiency intervention did not actually reach the model data. Veto rule: reject any run that asserts [PAGE 13] “We assume that W is of full row rank” without a numerical rank certificate. This is directly tied to [PAGE 13] and to the basis structure on [PAGE 15].

Scenario 2: Duplicate a scenario with split probability. Intervention: replace one scenario \((q^l,h^l,T^l,p_l)\) by two identical scenarios with probabilities \(p_l/2\) and \(p_l/2\). Verifier: compare the optimal extensive-form objective values and the optimal first-stage \(z\) before and after duplication. Prediction: the optimal value is invariant up to solver tolerance because
\[
p_l Q(z,\xi^l)=\frac{p_l}{2}Q(z,\xi^l)+\frac{p_l}{2}Q(z,\xi^l),
\]
and the feasible region in \(z\) is unchanged. The L-shaped algorithm may generate a different cut sequence or duplicate multipliers, but the final verified objective and \(z\) should match within tolerance. Falsification: if the optimal value changes by more than numerical tolerance, then either the probability split was not exact, the solver tolerance is too loose, or the implementation is indexing probabilities incorrectly. Veto rule: reject any claim that scenario duplication creates new uncertainty. The source’s expectation operator on [PAGE 3] and [PAGE 4] is linear in \(p_l\), so duplication without probability split is a different model, while duplication with split probability is the same model.

Scenario 3: Stochastic \(T^l\) under a certainty-equivalent shortcut. Intervention: set \(T^l\) to differ across scenarios, then run the Nazareth reformulation on [PAGE 29] as if \(T^l=T\) and \(x=Tz\). Verifier: check whether \(T^l z-x=0\) holds for all \(l\) at the returned \(z\). Prediction: the shortcut will either declare infeasibility for some scenario or return a \(z\) for which \(T^l z\neq x\) for at least one \(l\); the resulting objective will not equal the true expected recourse objective. Falsification: if the shortcut returns a \(z\) satisfying all \(T^l z=x\) and matching the full recourse objective, then the intervention failed to make \(T^l\) scenario-dependent. Veto rule: reject any use of the certainty-equivalent reduction when \(T^l\) varies, because [PAGE 29] introduces the reformulation under “When the matrix T is nonstochastic,” and [PAGE 30] calls \(x^*\) “the certainty equivalent.” The correct fallback is the general L-shaped algorithm with scenario-specific \(T^l\) in the feasibility and optimality cuts.

A fourth auxiliary verifier can be attached to the cut clock. Intervention: at a master solution \(z^\nu\), solve all Step 3 subproblems exactly and compute the aggregate \(\sum_l p_l q^l y^{l\nu}\). Verifier: compare \(\vartheta^\nu\) with the aggregate. Prediction: if \(\vartheta^\nu\) is strictly below the aggregate beyond tolerance, at least one optimality cut is missing; if a Step 2 subproblem has positive artificial-variable cost, at least one feasibility cut is missing. Veto rule: no termination claim is accepted unless the [PAGE 23] stopping condition is numerically certified. This makes the RLVR reward depend on verified cut separation, not on narrative confidence.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
