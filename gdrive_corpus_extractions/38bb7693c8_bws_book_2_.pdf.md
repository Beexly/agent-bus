<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source repeatedly models the reader’s inference state. [PAGE 199] says, “The whole point of a proof is to convince someone else of a truth that you have already ﬁgured out,” so a proof is a recursive communication act: the author predicts what the reader can infer from each line, then adds definitions or intermediate steps until the intended inference is forced. [PAGE 112] demands that “we should be as explicit and rigorous as possible,” and [PAGE 310] warns against leaving it to the reader to intuit whether a variable is arbitrary, fixed, or merely existentially asserted. The author’s theory of mind therefore includes three reader states: the reader who can reconstruct the base case, the reader who can reconstruct the inductive step, and the reader who will silently assume the converse unless warned.

Crisis habit regression occurs under time pressure: the student reverts to “it works for small cases,” skips the base case, or writes \(P(k)\Rightarrow P(k+1)\) without defining \(P(n)\). The counter-trap is to overcorrect by checking every domino individually; [PAGE 127] explains that induction does not require reaching the “top” of an infinite ladder, only that every rung is reached by the two-step rule. The clock is the external pressure that makes the student prefer a finite check to a quantified proof. The correct counter-trap is to slow down at the logical joints: define \(P(n)\), prove \(P(1)\), state the inductive hypothesis, and derive \(P(k+1)\). For strong induction, the recursive theory of mind must also track which previous instances are needed; [PAGE 349] shows a case where the inductive step uses all previous instances, and [PAGE 352] shows a Fibonacci case requiring two base cases. The author’s voice is a “clock” against the reader’s regression: it repeatedly says what must be checked and why.
