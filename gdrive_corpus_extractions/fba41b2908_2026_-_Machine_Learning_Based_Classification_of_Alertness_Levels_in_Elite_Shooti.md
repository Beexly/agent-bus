The source is a single-cohort, single-session, 60-minute PVT-plus-HRV study in 83 elite shooters, with the central claim that AdaBoost on selected HRV features classifies “optimal” versus “sub-optimal” alertness at accuracy 0.75, F1 0.73, AUC 0.77 [PAGE 1]. The multi-hop derivation has four hops: (1) PVT reaction time is treated as the behavioral ground truth for vigilance; (2) vigilance decrement is assumed to be indexed by autonomic modulation, especially slow oscillations; (3) recursive feature elimination and SHAP identify VLF% and SD2/SD1 as dominant predictors; (4) binary classification is chosen over three-class to improve discrimination. Each hop carries a gray-zone assumption. The first hop is defensible: PVT is an established sustained-attention paradigm, and the paper correctly cites the 500-ms attentional-lapse convention [PAGE 2]. But the final binary labels are not the 500-ms lapse criterion. The paper states: “A PVT trial was classified as representing a sub-optimal alertness state if the participant's reaction time exceeded 500 ms” [PAGE 2], yet the final labels are distributional: “240 epochs labeled as 'High Alertness' (mean RT &lt; 273.98 ms) and 240 epochs labeled as 'Low Alertness' (mean RT ≥ 319.60 ms)” [PAGE 5]. That is a major operationalization shift: the middle 20% is removed, and the classes are defined by the sample’s top and bottom 40%, not by an absolute lapse threshold. The binary framework was “prioritized for the final model development to maximize discriminative power and ensure a balanced dataset” [PAGE 3], which is statistically convenient but changes the construct. A classifier trained on top-40% versus bottom-40% mean-RT epochs is not the same as a detector of 500-ms lapses; it is a detector of relative within-sample reaction-time distribution. The second gray-zone issue is class-count consistency. The main text reports 480 valid PVT epochs and 168 positive cases under the 500-ms criterion [PAGE 2], while the final binary dataset is 240 plus 240 [PAGE 5]. The supplementary tables show five-level and three-level distributions of 120 epochs per level and binary percentages of 40.00 each [PAGE 12], which implies a larger 600-epoch pool or a percentage denominator that is not the final 480. This is not fatal, but it means the reader cannot reconstruct the exact filtering path from 83 athletes × 6 blocks = 498 potential epochs to 480 valid epochs to 240+240 binary epochs without additional information. The third hop, SHAP, is descriptive rather than causal. SHAP values are additive attributions on the fitted model; they do not prove that VLF% causes low alertness. The authors themselves warn that “the mechanistic basis of VLF is highly complex and heavily debated” and that VLF is influenced by “thermoregulatory, hormonal, and respiratory rhythms” [PAGE 8]. Thus the SHAP result should be read as model-internal importance, not physiological causation. The fourth hop, model evaluation, is internally validated but not externally validated. The paper explicitly admits that “calibration performance of the model was not evaluated” [PAGE 9] and that future work should prioritize “external validation using independent datasets” [PAGE 9]. With 480 epochs, a 7:3 split gives roughly 336 training and 144 test epochs; accuracy 0.75 on 144 test epochs is about 108 correct, but no confidence interval is reported. The headline AUC 0.77 exceeds the authors’ 0.70 hypothesis [PAGE 1], but the absence of calibration, external validation, and sex/menstrual-cycle control means the result is a promising internal discrimination result, not a validated readiness instrument.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s measurement operators are standard HRV and classifier operators. The RR interval is \(RR_i = t_{i+1}-t_i\) in ms; instantaneous heart rate is \(HR_i = 60000/RR_i\) in bpm. Time-domain variability is \(SDNN = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(RR_i-\overline{RR})^2}\) in ms, and \(RMSSD = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(RR_{i+1}-RR_i)^2}\) in ms. The Poincaré descriptors are \(SD1 = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}(RR_{i+1}-RR_i)}\) and \(SD2 = \sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}(RR_i)-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}(RR_{i+1}-RR_i)}\), both in ms; the reported predictor is the dimensionless ratio \(SD2/SD1\). Frequency-domain power is estimated from the RR tachogram, typically by Welch periodogram: \(P(f)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathrm{FFT}_k(f)|^2\), with units \(\mathrm{ms}^2/\mathrm{Hz}\). Band power is \(P_{B}=\int_{f_L}^{f_U}P(f)\,df\) in \(\mathrm{ms}^2\), where the paper uses VLF 0.01–0.04 Hz, LF 0.04–0.15 Hz, and HF 0.15–0.40 Hz [PAGE 3]. Total power is \(TP=\int_{0.0033}^{0.40}P(f)\,df\) in \(\mathrm{ms}^2\). The normalized VLF percentage is \(VLF\%=100\,P_{VLF}/(P_{VLF}+P_{LF}+P_{HF})\) or \(100\,P_{VLF}/TP\), depending on the software denominator; the paper reports VLF% as the top SHAP predictor [PAGE 7]. The LF/HF ratio is \(P_{LF}/P_{HF}\), dimensionless. Classifier metrics are \(Accuracy=(TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN)\), \(Precision=TP/(TP+FP)\), \(Recall=TP/(TP+FN)\), \(F1=2PR/(P+R)\), and \(AUC=\int_0^1 TPR(FPR^{-1}(u))\,du\), or equivalently \(AUC=P(S_+&gt;S_-)\) for positive and negative score distributions. SHAP attributions satisfy \(f(x)=\phi_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_j(x)\), with \(\phi_j=\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{j\}}\frac{|S|!(p-|S|-1)!}{p!}[f(S\cup\{j\})-f(S)]\); the output units are model log-odds or probability, not physiological units. AdaBoost uses \(F_T(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t h_t(x)\) with \(\alpha_t=\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1-\epsilon_t}{\epsilon_t}\). The source reports the best binary result: “The AdaBoost model achieved the best performance, with an accuracy of 0.75, an F1-score of 0.73, and an AUC of 0.77” [PAGE 1]. It also reports that “VLF(%) was the most influential predictor, followed by the SD2/SD1 ratio and DFA alpha2” [PAGE 7]. Exact breakdown regimes matter. Normalized band powers are undefined when \(TP\to 0\); \(SD2/SD1\) is undefined or divergent when \(SD1\to 0\); AUC is degenerate when only one class is present; accuracy is prior-dependent under imbalance. Spectral VLF estimation is unstable when the epoch length \(T\) is short relative to \(1/f_{VLF}\): at 0.01 Hz, one cycle is 100 s, so a 10-minute epoch contains only about six cycles at the lower VLF boundary, making VLF% sensitive to detrending, artifact interpolation, and nonstationarity. The paper reports artifact correction below 2% per participant per epoch under Kubios “Medium” correction [PAGE 3], which is acceptable but not zero-bias. SHAP’s additive assumption is also a breakdown regime: with correlated HRV features, attribution mass can be split arbitrarily among collinear predictors, so “VLF% is most important” is conditional on the fitted feature set and model. Finally, AdaBoost breaks when weak-learner error \(\epsilon_t\ge 0.5\), because \(\alpha_t\le 0\); label noise or threshold ambiguity in the middle alertness zone can therefore destabilize the ensemble. The paper’s own three-class result confirms boundary confusion: Moderate-class recall ranged from 0.02 to 0.15 [PAGE 5], which is why the binary framework was preferred.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs from PVT standardization in the 1980s, through HRV normative standards in the 1990s, into the neurovisceral integration framework and modern wearable-ML sports monitoring. The PVT lineage is explicit: the 500-ms lapse criterion is tied to Dinges and Powell, Basner and Dinges, Reifman, and Van Dongen [PAGE 2]. The HRV lineage is tied to Malik et al. and Laborde et al. for artifact correction, recording standards, and interpretive caution [PAGE 3]. The theoretical lineage is the neurovisceral integration model, which the authors invoke to link higher HRV with flexible autonomic adjustment and cognitive resource allocation [PAGE 7]. The ML lineage is the sports-prediction and vigilance-classification literature, including SVM-based vigilance monitoring and sliding-window HRV approaches [PAGE 1, PAGE 8]. Retrocausally, the present claim depends on earlier choices that are not symmetric: if the field had standardized absolute RT-lapse labels rather than percentile labels, the binary result would answer a different question; if HRV guidelines had required longer VLF windows or stricter stationarity checks, VLF% might not have emerged as the top feature; if external validation had been mandatory for “readiness” claims, the paper would be framed as a discovery study rather than a validated tool. The multi-week traps are the most important. Elite shooters exist in periodized training blocks, taper weeks, competition travel, sleep-debt accumulation, menstrual-cycle phases, and circadian drift. The paper controls only acute pre-test restrictions: no alcohol for at least 24 hours, no caffeine or stimulants for at least 12 hours, morning testing between 08:00 and 09:30, and exclusion of self-reported chronic sleep disorders [PAGE 2]. It explicitly states that objective sleep quality was not assessed and that menstrual cycle phase was not systematically recorded or controlled [PAGE 2, PAGE 9]. Therefore, a single 60-minute morning session cannot distinguish a stable trait from a multi-week state. A multi-week trap would be an athlete in a heavy training block who has elevated VLF% and slower RT; the model might label “sub-optimal alertness” when the real cause is cumulative training load, incomplete recovery, or menstrual-phase autonomic shifts. Another trap is the “ceiling effect” argument: the authors claim elite shooters have such stable autonomic regulation that detecting micro-fluctuations is inherently harder [PAGE 8]. That may be true, but it also means the classifier’s 0.75 accuracy could be driven by a small number of unstable epochs rather than a general readiness gradient. The historical lesson is that psychophysiological monitoring claims become durable only after external validation, calibration, and longitudinal replication; the paper acknowledges these gaps [PAGE 9].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive loop is coach–athlete–model. Crisis habit regression: under competition stress, a shooter may regress to breath-holding, premature trigger force, or over-aiming; these are biomechanical and attentional habits that need not map monotonically onto VLF% or SD2/SD1. If the model says “low alertness,” the athlete may try to correct by increasing arousal, which can worsen steadiness. Counter-trap: the measurement itself changes the state. If coaches use the model as a pre-competition gate, athletes may sleep differently, consume caffeine, or perform breathing protocols to “fix” HRV before testing; that changes the physiological baseline and invalidates the classifier’s learned mapping. The paper’s practical claim is “pre-competition readiness assessment” and real-time monitoring in simulated training [PAGE 1, PAGE 8], so this counter-trap is not hypothetical. Clock: the model is trained on six 10-minute PVT blocks, 80 trials each, over 60 minutes [PAGE 3]. Real 10-meter air rifle and pistol matches have different internal pacing, shot clocks, rest intervals, and coach interactions. A 10-minute epoch average smooths over the shot-level clock that actually determines performance. The recursive theory-of-mind failure is therefore a category error: the model predicts an epoch-level behavioral label, but coaches may interpret it as a shot-level readiness state. A second-order failure is over-trust: because SHAP gives a clean ranking, users may treat VLF% as a causal lever. The authors caution against this, noting that VLF is “significantly influenced by non-cognitive physiological processes” [PAGE 8]. The correct counter-trap is to use the model as a screening flag, not a command; to pair it with subjective state, sleep logs, and biomechanical checks; and to avoid telling athletes that a single HRV-derived probability determines readiness. The clock trap also cuts the other way: morning-only testing reduces circadian confounding [PAGE 2], but it means the model may not transfer to afternoon finals or evening competitions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is a sufficiency argument. Let \(Y\) be shooting performance, \(H\) the HRV feature vector, \(B\) biomechanical state (aim stability, trigger-force variability, postural sway, vision), and \(W\) weather/environment (wind, temperature, humidity, lighting). The model estimates \(P(Y\mid H)\). A veto is warranted if \(P(Y\mid H,B,W)\neq P(Y\mid H)\) and \(B,W\) are unmeasured. In precision shooting, wind and biomechanics directly displace the projectile and the aim point; HRV can modulate steadiness but cannot override a bad trigger or a wind gust. The paper does not measure \(B\) or \(W\), so the classifier cannot adjudicate them. Information-theoretically, if \(I(Y;H) &lt; I(Y;H,B,W)\) in bits, then \(H\) is not a sufficient statistic for performance. The veto condition is: if \(B=1\) or \(W=1\) (a physical/biomechanical/weather constraint is active), then any decision rule \(A=\mathbf{1}\{P(Y\mid H)&gt;\tau\}\) must be overridden by \(A_{\text{veto}}=0\) until \(B,W\) are observed. The paper’s own physiology supports this: VLF is influenced by “thermoregulatory, hormonal, and respiratory rhythms” [PAGE 8], so weather and thermoregulation can confound \(H\) itself. If environmental variance dominates, the signal-to-noise ratio \(\mathrm{SNR}=\mathrm{Var}(H_{\text{signal}})/\mathrm{Var}(H_{\text{noise}})\to 0\), and the classifier’s probability becomes non-identifiable. The model also cannot adjudicate equipment, target visibility, or rule-induced pressure. Therefore the cross-layer veto proof is simple: HRV is a necessary but not sufficient layer for shooting performance; a high-stakes readiness decision based solely on HRV is vetoed by unmeasured biomechanical and weather layers. The correct adjudication is hierarchical: HRV flags, biomechanics confirm, weather and equipment veto. This is consistent with the authors’ own framing of VLF% as “a systemic physiological correlate of the quasi-isometric shooting state rather than a direct, isolated index of cognitive alertness” [PAGE 8].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: independent external validation. Freeze the AdaBoost model and test it on at least 60 elite shooters from a different region or sport, with both morning and afternoon sessions and with sex/menstrual-phase strata recorded. Prediction: AUC falls to 0.60–0.68 and accuracy to 0.62–0.68; if AUC remains ≥0.75, the original result is more generalizable than the paper’s limitations suggest. If AUC falls below 0.70, the claim of a validated readiness tool is vetoed and the study is reclassified as an internal discovery study.

Veto scenario 2: label-definition stress test. Re-label the same epochs using the paper’s stated 500-ms attentional-lapse rule [PAGE 2] rather than the top-40%/bottom-40% mean-RT split [PAGE 5], and separately train on permuted labels. Prediction: with the 500-ms rule, class prevalence becomes imbalanced and AUC drops to 0.55–0.65; with permuted labels, AUC should be 0.50 ± 0.05. If the permuted-label model achieves AUC &gt;0.60, there is leakage or overfitting; if the 500-ms model still achieves AUC ≥0.75, the classifier is tracking the absolute lapse construct rather than the percentile threshold. Either outcome produces a verifiable veto of the specific “optimal vs. sub-optimal alertness” interpretation.

Veto scenario 3: calibration and decision-utility test. On the held-out test set, compute the Brier score, calibration slope and intercept, and decision-curve net benefit at the threshold a coach would actually use. Prediction: because the paper did not evaluate calibration [PAGE 9], the calibration slope will be below 0.8 and the Brier skill score will be small; at a threshold requiring PPV ≥0.80, the model will fail. If the model is well calibrated and net benefit exceeds the treat-all strategy, the readiness-probability claim survives. If not, the model may still rank athletes but cannot be used as a probabilistic readiness gate. This is the most decision-relevant veto because accuracy and AUC are discrimination metrics, not calibration metrics.
