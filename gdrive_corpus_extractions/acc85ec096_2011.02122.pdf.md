Multi-hop audit: high accuracy at late J does not prove probabilistic correctness; it may reflect the near-determinism of the target-run gap near the end of a chase.

The source does not print the LSTM gate equations, so the following is a standard formal reconstruction of the architecture the paper describes, not a verbatim quotation from the paper. Let \(T=300\) be the nominal ball index horizon in an ODI innings, as described on [PAGE 3]: “Ideally, there must be 300 balls in one inning of the ODI.” Let \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d_t}\) be the input feature vector at ball \(t\), containing continuous counts and one-hot categorical entries. The paper says continuous data is “used as it is” and categorical data is one-hot encoded [PAGE 3]. Because \(x_t\) mixes counts of runs, wicket indicators, ball indices, and one-hot identity entries, its components are not dimensionally homogeneous; the network weights absorb scale, but formal unit consistency requires normalization or explicit scaling, which the paper does not report.

The paper’s Input Transformation block is represented as:
\[
u_t=\phi(W_{\mathrm{in}}x_t+b_{\mathrm{in}}),
\]
where \(u_t\) is a higher-dimensional dimensionless activation, \(W_{\mathrm{in}}\) is a weight matrix, \(b_{\mathrm{in}}\) is a bias vector, and \(\phi\) is a nonlinearity. The LSTM block then uses the standard gated recurrence:
\[
i_t=\sigma(W_i u_t+U_i h_{t-1}+b_i),\quad
f_t=\sigma(W_f u_t+U_f h_{t-1}+b_f),
\]
\[
o_t=\sigma(W_o u_t+U_o h_{t-1}+b_o),\quad
\tilde c_t=\tanh(W_c u_t+U_c h_{t-1}+b_c),
\]
\[
c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t,\quad
h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t),
\]
where \(i_t,f_t,o_t\) are dimensionless gate activations, \(c_t\) is the dimensionless cell state, \(h_t\) is the dimensionless hidden state, and \(\odot\) is elementwise multiplication. [PAGE 2] motivates this choice: LSTMs are “the most widely used RNNs because of their selective gating and direct gradient transfer properties.” The paper does not give tensor dimensions, initialization, dropout, regularization, or optimization hyperparameters.

The Output Transformation block and softmax are described on [PAGE 3]: “The softmax of this output layer gives the win probabilities of the match.” Formally:
\[
z_t=W_{\mathrm{out}}h_t+b_{\mathrm{out}},\qquad
p_t=\frac{\exp(z_{t,1})}{\exp(z_{t,0})+\exp(z_{t,1})},
\]
where \(p_t\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless and is interpreted as the probability of the batting team winning at ball \(t\), consistent with [PAGE 1]: “The win probability refers to the probability of the batting team winning the game at that instance.” If the two logits are swapped, the complement \(1-p_t\) is the other team’s probability; the paper’s sign convention is not fully specified, which is a label-definition gray zone.

The training loss is Binary Cross Entropy, as stated on [PAGE 3]: “The final two node output is compared with the ground truth of win or lose by the Binary Cross Entropy Loss.” For \(N\) ball-level training records and label \(y_t\in\{0,1\}\):
\[
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\left[y_t\log p_t+(1-y_t)\log(1-p_t)\right].
\]
With natural logarithms, \(\mathcal{L}\) has units of nats per sample; with base-2 logarithms, it has units of bits per sample. The reported evaluation, however, is accuracy:
\[
\mathrm{Acc}(J)=\frac{1}{N_J}\sum_{t:\,t\le J}\mathbf{1}\{\hat y_t=y_t\},
\]
reported in percent, where \(\hat y_t=\mathbf{1}\{p_t\ge \tau\}\) for an unspecified threshold \(\tau\). [PAGE 4] Table II and Table V report percent accuracy at selected \(J\), e.g. 95.7% at \(J=300\), 75.1% at \(J=250\), 66.4% at \(J=200\). These are classification accuracies, not calibrated probabilities. A proper scoring audit would add Brier score
\[
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_t(p_t-y_t)^2
\]
and log-loss, but the source does not report either.

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. First, softmax is singular only when the denominator is zero; for finite logits it is not, but numerical overflow occurs for large logit gaps, requiring log-sum-exp stabilization. Second, BCE becomes infinite if \(p_t=0\) with \(y_t=1\) or \(p_t=1\) with \(y_t=0\); clipping is required. Third, the fixed 300-ball vector construction [PAGE 3] breaks when an innings is shortened by weather, when extras are shifted to the previous ball, or when the actual ball count differs from 300. Fourth, one-hot encoding with rare-category removal [PAGE 3] breaks for unseen teams or players: the encoding becomes all-zero or uninformative. Fifth, Model D’s cumulative-sum input loses ball-level resolution; [PAGE 4] Table II reports Model D at only 60.8% at \(J=300\), far below Model B’s 95.7%, showing that cumulative aggregation can destroy useful temporal information. Sixth, the paper’s own late-match ceiling is not 100% because of abandoned matches, rain, Duckworth-Lewis, and ties [PAGE 5]. Those are not numerical singularities but distributional breakdowns: the label is not generated by the same ordinary win/lose rule the model is fitting.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is explicitly traced in its literature survey [PAGE 1-2]. It begins with classical classifiers: KNN, Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, and Random Forest. It then cites cricket-specific work: Shah et al. used logistic regression on ODI data and achieved “81%” accuracy [PAGE 2]; Daniel et al. used Decision Tree and XGBoost on IPL matches and reached “94.8%” [PAGE 2]; Jhanwar et al. trained KNN and logistic regression on ODIs and predicted “71%” [PAGE 2]; Gagana and Paramesha compared Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and Random Forest on IPL and reached “around 90%” [PAGE 2]; Rabindra et al. used Random Forest and Multiple Logistic Regression; Mayank et al. introduced a Most Valuable Player notion and used Genetic Algorithms; Shimona et al. used data mining and reached “97%” on a balanced dataset [PAGE 2]. The closest ancestor is Jhawar et al. [14], which trained separate models for each over and achieved “75.68% of average accuracy across the overs” [PAGE 2]. The source’s claimed novelty is a single LSTM model that predicts after every ball rather than requiring one model per over.

The neural lineage is also explicit: neural networks are described as cascaded logistic regressions, RNNs as temporal connections, and LSTMs as the gated RNN of Hochreiter and Schmidhuber [17], with Graves et al. [16] as a handwriting-recognition application [PAGE 2]. Retrocaustically, the paper sits at the intersection of two older lines: statistical cricket outcome classification, which usually predicts before the match from match-level statistics, and sequence modeling, which treats ball-by-ball data as a time series. Its advance is not a new mathematical operator; it is the application of a standard LSTM to a ball-indexed sequence and the empirical comparison of four input/output variants.

The multi-week research traps are substantial. Trap one is comparing accuracy across incompatible tasks: pre-match IPL classification at 94.8% or 97% is not the same as in-match ODI chase prediction at 98.4%, because the prediction time, label base rate, and information set differ. Trap two is treating the target score as if it were available throughout the match; [PAGE 4] says “we append the target score from the first innings of the match to every input of the network,” which is legitimate only for second-innings chase modeling. Trap three is ignoring match-level dependence: 1597 matches with up to 300 balls each create hundreds of thousands of correlated ball records, and the paper does not report a grouped train/test split by match. If ball records from the same match appear in both train and test, early-J accuracy can be inflated by memorized match context. Trap four is the fixed-300 preprocessing: shifting no-ball extras to the previous ball [PAGE 3] may be reasonable for a constant-length vector but can misalign the exact event time. Trap five is label ambiguity from rain, Duckworth-Lewis, and ties [PAGE 5]. Trap six is overreading accuracy as probability quality; the source reports no calibration, no reliability curve, no log-loss, and no baseline comparison at the same J values except its own variants. These traps can consume weeks of replication effort if not isolated before model comparison.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model is intended for in-game decision support: [PAGE 1] says it can be used “in the recommendation system to the manager or the captain of the team about the critical in-game changes required to win the match,” and [PAGE 5] says the numbers “can be helpful in designing a recommendation system to the team manager or the team captain in making critical decisions during the game.” That creates a recursive theory-of-mind problem. The model predicts the batting team’s win probability from historical observational data. The captain or manager then acts on that prediction. The opponent reacts to the changed strategy. The resulting match trajectory is no longer drawn from the same observational policy that generated the training data. A model trained on “what happened” is being used for “what should we do,” which is a counterfactual or interventional question, not a purely predictive one. The paper does not report off-policy evaluation, action-conditioned modeling, or any causal identification of “critical in-game changes.”

Crisis habit regression is the first human failure mode. Under late-game pressure, a captain may revert to habitual bowling changes, defensive field placements, or senior-player deference rather than integrating a probability estimate. The model’s output can be ignored precisely when it is most decision-relevant. The second failure mode is the counter-trap: because the model reports high late-J accuracy, the captain may over-trust it and become complacent. But [PAGE 5] itself says late accuracy is “very straightforward and intuitive” because “as we reach the end of the game it is very easy to predict the winning team by simply looking at the target score and the current runs.” A high number near the end is not evidence of special insight; it is evidence of a nearly deterministic clock. Over-trusting that number early, when accuracy is 62.7% at 1 over and 72.4% at 5 overs [PAGE 5], is a separate error.

The clock is the third issue. The ball index \(J\) is both a feature and a deadline. The value of information can be formalized by the conditional entropy
\[
H(Y\mid F_t)=-\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}}P(y\mid F_t)\log_2 P(y\mid F_t)
\]
in bits, and the mutual information \(I(Y;F_t)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid F_t)\). As \(t\) approaches the end of the chase, \(H(Y\mid F_t)\) collapses because the target-run gap and remaining balls dominate. That collapse is real, but it is not the same as model skill. A useful decision-support system must distinguish “the clock has made the result obvious” from “the model has learned nontrivial early-game structure.” The paper’s own table shows the distinction: 82.7% at 10 overs, 87.3% at 20 overs, 88.0% at 30 overs, 92.1% at 40 overs, and 98.4% at 50 overs [PAGE 5]. The jump near the end is consistent with clock-driven determinism.

Recursive theory of mind also requires the model to represent opponent adaptation. If the batting team knows the model expects a certain scoring rate, it may change risk tolerance; if the bowling team knows the model, it may change matchups. The LSTM has no explicit opponent-policy variable. It sees player identities and outcomes, but it does not see the strategic reaction function. For a manager, the correct use is therefore not “obey the probability” but “use the probability as one input, check calibration, check whether the match state is inside the training support, and check whether weather, injury, or match-shortening has changed the decision problem.” The paper’s recommendation-system framing is plausible, but its evidence supports a descriptive ball-by-ball classifier more strongly than a causal decision engine.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is clear from the feature table. [PAGE 2-3] Table I lists ball attributes: innings number, over and ball, batting team, batsman, non-striker, bowler, runs-off-bat, extras, and wicket. The match-level list on [PAGE 3] includes team, gender, season, date, series, match number, venue, city, toss winner, toss decision, player of the match, umpire, TV umpire, match referee, winning team, and runs scored by the winning team. There is no ball-tracking trajectory, no pitch moisture, no temperature, no wind, no rainfall time series, no player load, no EMG, no force-plate, no joint kinematics, and no injury state. The abstract’s mention of “advanced sensors” [PAGE 1] is a general motivation, not a description of the model’s input tensor. Therefore any claim that the LSTM captures biomechanical fatigue, injury risk, or weather-driven ball movement is not supported by the source.

The weather-layer veto is the strongest and is admitted by the paper. [PAGE 5] states: “One reason for this is the abandoned matches. This can be due to unforeseen situations like the rains which resulted in deciding the winning team by some other algorithm like Duckworth Lewis Method rather than the general rules of the game. Other reasons could be the match ties in which case the win is ambiguous.” Formally, let \(F_t\) be the sigma-algebra generated by the model’s observed ball features up to ball \(t\), and let \(W\) be a weather or match-shortening state not in \(F_t\). If \(W\) affects the outcome \(Y\), then
\[
P(Y\mid F_t)=\sum_W P(Y\mid F_t,W)P(W\mid F_t),
\]
and an estimator that omits \(W\) is generally biased unless \(Y\perp W\mid F_t\). Rain, revised targets, reduced overs, and Duckworth-Lewis outcomes violate that conditional independence. A prediction made after a rain interruption is therefore vetoed until the target and overs are recomputed under the applicable rule and the model is retrained or recalibrated on that regime. The same veto applies to abandoned matches and ties: a binary “win or lose” softmax cannot represent “no result” or “tie” without an additional label class or a filtering rule.

The biomechanical-layer veto is also decisive for player-state claims. A fast bowler’s injury, a batsman’s cramp, or a fielder’s fatigue can change the next-ball distribution without appearing in Table I. The model can only update after the observed outcome; it cannot anticipate a biomechanical event from the provided features. If a team uses the model for “critical in-game changes,” it must not interpret a probability shift as a physical diagnosis. The paper’s model is a sequence classifier over event outcomes and identities, not a physiological or mechanical simulator.

The pre-match layer is partially present but weaker than the paper’s prose suggests. [PAGE 4] says pre-match classification experiments use “match level statistics like the venue, weather, pitch, etc.” But the dataset list on [PAGE 3] does not include weather or pitch, and Table III reports AdaBoost at 47.2% test accuracy and LGBM at 61.3% [PAGE 4]. That is far below the in-match accuracies. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: venue and toss information can enter as coarse categorical context; actual weather and pitch state are not demonstrated in the feature table; biomechanical state is absent; and Duckworth-Lewis/rain/tie states are explicit label-veto regimes. Any report that ignores these distinctions overclaims the model’s physical validity.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: match-level split leakage. Counterfactual: rebuild the train/test split so that all ball records from a given match lie entirely in one partition. Verifiable prediction: if the original split was random at the ball-record level, then grouped-by-match splitting will reduce early-J test accuracy at \(J=6\) and \(J=30\) relative to the reported 62.7% and 72.4% [PAGE 5], while late-J accuracy at \(J=300\) will remain high because the target-run gap dominates. The reward check is simple: compute \(\Delta\mathrm{Acc}(J)=\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{grouped}}(J)-\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{reported}}(J)\); a significantly negative \(\Delta\) at small \(J\) with a near-zero \(\Delta\) at large \(J\) verifies the leakage/inflation veto. The source does not state its split protocol, so this is a necessary replication check, not an accusation.

Veto scenario 2: target-score availability and first-innings use. Counterfactual: remove the first-innings target score from every input, or restrict the model to first-innings states where the target is not yet known, and retrain under the same architecture. Verifiable prediction: the large gain attributed to target score on [PAGE 4] will disappear. The paper reports baseline \(66.4\%\) at \(J=200\), \(75.1\%\) at \(J=250\), and \(95.7\%\) at \(J=300\), rising to \(87.1\%\), \(90.1\%\), and \(98.0\%\) after target-score augmentation. Therefore, withholding the target should move \(J=200\) accuracy back toward the baseline range and should substantially reduce \(J=250\) accuracy; the exact drop is measurable and falsifiable. This veto prevents anachronistic use of a second-innings feature in a first-innings or pre-innings claim.

Veto scenario 3: weather, Duckworth-Lewis, and tie label audit. Counterfactual: classify each match as ordinary win/lose, Duckworth-Lewis result, abandoned/no-result, or tie; then evaluate the model separately and also evaluate a filtered ordinary-only subset. Verifiable prediction: the reported endpoint accuracy of 98.4% [PAGE 5] is below 100% precisely because the full label set contains non-ordinary outcomes; the ordinary-only subset should have higher endpoint accuracy, while the full-set calibration and log-loss should be worse than the ordinary-only metrics. The paper itself supplies the mechanism: “the model fails to capture this trivial result” because of “abandoned matches,” “rains,” “Duckworth Lewis Method,” and “match ties” [PAGE 5]. A verifiable RLVR reward can be defined as \(+1\) if the ordinary-only subset raises endpoint accuracy and lowers log-loss relative to the mixed set, and \(0\) otherwise. If the reward does not fire, the label-contamination explanation is falsified and the residual errors require a different account.

These three counterfactuals are independent: the first tests match-level dependence, the second tests information availability, and the third tests label validity under weather and match-shortening rules. Together they vet the paper’s headline claim that the LSTM can “predict the outcome of the match at any instance of the match” [PAGE 5] by checking whether the reported accuracy is skill, leakage, anachronistic features, or label contamination.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
