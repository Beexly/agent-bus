Let \(X\) be the mid-parent stature, \(Y\) the offspring stature. Define:
\[
\bar{X} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i, \quad \bar{Y} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} Y_i
\]
with \(Q\) representing the probable error or quartile deviation: \(Q_X = 1.7\) inches. The regression coefficient \(b\) is derived from the covariance and variance:
\[
b = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\text{Var}(X)} = r \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}
\]
where \(r\) is the Pearson correlation coefficient. Units are squared inches (\(\text{in}^2\)) for variances and covariance, making \(b\) dimensionless.

Assumptions: Linear relationship, homoscedasticity, normally distributed errors. Breakdown occurs if relationships are nonlinear or variances differ across subgroups.

From [PAGE 109], the distribution among sons of equally tall mid-parents provides the data for computing these moments. The exclusion criteria for Tables 12 and 13 introduce a truncation in the sample space: let \(F\) be family size; we condition on \(F < 6\). Thus, expectations become conditional:
\[
E[Y|X, F<6] = a + b X
\]
which biases the estimate if \(F\) is correlated with \(X\) or \(Y\). The "transmutation" for daughters implies a scaling factor \(k\) such that \(Y_{daughter}^{*} = k Y_{daughter}\), aiming for \(\sigma_{Y^{*}} = \sigma_{Y_{son}}\).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
This work is the progenitor of quantitative genetics and the concept of heritability. It directly led to Karl Pearson's development of correlation and regression, formalizing the mathematical tools used today in genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Historically, it provided empirical evidence against Lamarckian inheritance, showing that acquired characteristics do not persist across generations as strongly as genetic ones.

A multi-week trap for modern analysts is to apply these 19th-century methods uncritically to contemporary big data without accounting for population stratification or the impact of widespread nutrition and healthcare on phenotypic expression. Another trap is the "ecological fallacy"—inferring individual-level heritability from aggregate familial data, which can conflate genetic and environmental variance components.

Moreover, the initial focus on elite families (like the R.F.F.—likely Royal Family records) introduces a socio-economic bias that may not generalize. Modern replication must address these historical artifacts by using diverse, randomly sampled cohorts and controlling for environmental covariates.
