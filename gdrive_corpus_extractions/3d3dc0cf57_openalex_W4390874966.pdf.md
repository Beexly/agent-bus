<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s schedulable-task operators are ILP-style binary variables \(s_{j,k,t}\) and \(p_{j,k,t}\), where \(j\) indexes tasks, \(k\) indexes processors, and \(t\) indexes discrete time. The paper states: “In the message triggered task model, each task j will only be executed once and must also be executed once” [PAGE 4 of 20]. The execution-once constraint is given as:

\[
\sum_{k=0}^{proNum-1}\sum_{t=0}^{timeLimit} s_{j,k,t}=1,\qquad \forall j\in Node .
\]

The start/execution relation is printed as:

\[
s_{j,k,t}\, job\_wcet[j]\le \sum_{te=t}^{t+job\_wcet[j]-1} p_{j,k,te},
\qquad \forall j\in Node,\ \forall k\in Pro,
\]

and

\[
p_{j,k,t}=\sum_{te=t-job\_wcet[j]+1}^{t}s_{j,k,te},
\qquad \forall j\in Node,\ \forall k\in Pro.
\]

These are dimensionless Boolean constraints except for the time index and \(job\_wcet[j]\), which must be in a common time unit. The paper does not state whether that unit is milliseconds, microseconds, or abstract ticks. The prose says “the start time of the task must be within the time period [t-job_WCET [j], t]” [PAGE 4 of 20], but the equations alone do not prove schedulability; they encode start/execution bookkeeping.

The cache-allocation operator is a binary vector \(X_i^n\). The paper states: “Since each task can only have one cache unit allocation result, only one element in the vector can be 1, and all other elements can be 0” [PAGE 6 of 20]. The constraint is printed as:

\[
0\le \sum_{j=1}^{k} X_j^i \le 1 .
\]

The task’s cache-dependent worst-case execution time is:

\[
E_i=\sum X_i W_i
= X_i^1 W_{t_i}(1)+X_i^2 W_{t_i}(2)+\cdots+X_i^k W_{t_i}(k),
\]

with \(W_{t_i}(c)\) in time units and \(c\) the number of cache units. The cache-capacity constraint is:

\[
\sum_{\text{all tasks}}\left[X_i^1\ \cdots\ X_i^k\right]
\begin{bmatrix}1\\ \vdots\\ k\end{bmatrix}
\le k,
\]

equivalently \( \sum_{i\in tasks}\sum_{c=1}^{k} c\,X_i^c\le k \). The paper reports, in its own table, “as the number of cache units allocated to the task increases, the worst-case execution time of the task decreases” [PAGE 5 of 20], with values from 478.92 at 0 cache units down to 264.05 at 4 cache units [PAGE 5 of 20]. The unit of those numbers is not printed. That missing unit is a material gray zone because WCET safety cannot be audited without it.

Deadline feasibility is expressed with a task-preemption tree. For a leaf node, \(ET_i=r_i+E_i\le d_i\); for a non-leaf node, the printed expression adds the execution times of posterity nodes [PAGE 6 of 20]:

\[
ET_i=
\begin{cases}
r_i+E_i\le d_i, & \text{leaf node},\\
r_i+E_i+\sum E_{\text{posterity node}}\le d_i, & \text{other node}.
\end{cases}
\]

This is a feasibility predicate, not a computed deadline value. The breakdown regime is direct: if \(r_i+E_i+\sum E_{\text{posterity node}}>d_i\), the constraint is infeasible. If the summation double-counts shared descendants or misses migration and context-switch costs, the predicate can accept a schedule that misses in practice.

\[
P_{act}=P_{dyn}+P_{sta}+P_{on},
\]

\[
P_{dyn}=C_e V_{dd}^2 f,
\]

\[
P_{sta}=V_{dd}I_{subn}+|V_{bs}|I_j,
\]

\[
I_{subn}=K_3 e^{K_4 V_{dd}} e^{K_5 V_{bs}}.
\]

The source writes “Isubn = K3eK4VdieK5Vbs” on [PAGE 7 of 20]; the likely intended variable is \(V_{dd}\), but the OCR text says “Vdie.” The frequency-voltage relation is:

f=\frac{(V_{dd}-V_{th})^{\gamma}}{L_d K_6},

and the threshold voltage is:

\[
V_{th}=V_{th1}-K_1V_{dd}-K_2V_{bs}.
\]

Units, if the physical model is taken literally, are watts for \(P\), farads for \(C_e\), volts for \(V_{dd}\), \(V_{th}\), and \(V_{bs}\), hertz for \(f\), and amperes for \(I_{subn}\) and \(I_j\). The paper does not give numerical values, calibration range, or SPICE parameter provenance. Breakdown regimes include \(L_d K_6=0\), \(V_{dd}\le V_{th}\) for real positive \(f\) when \(\gamma\) is non-integer or when positive frequency is required, and exponential overflow in \(I_{subn}\) outside the fitted voltage range. A negative or zero frequency makes \(P_{dyn}\le 0\), which is physically invalid for an active processor.

The linearized makespan constraint is:

obj\ge end(j)=start(j)+job\_wcet[j],\qquad \min(obj),\qquad \forall j\in Node,

as stated on [PAGE 8 of 20]. This is a standard epigraph reformulation only if \(end(j)\) is affine in the decision variables and the underlying \(start(j)\) is correctly constrained. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Consider three agents: the athlete \(A\), the coach \(C\), and the system \(S\). The athlete holds a belief about whether \(S\) sees the correct motor pattern; the coach holds a belief about whether \(S\) measures readiness; the system holds an implicit model of the athlete as a task or a tracked object. The paper’s own language supports this triad: it claims “real-time monitoring and analysis of Student activism” and “personalized teaching and precise guidance” [PAGE 3 of 20], and it describes a 3D model that “can rotate 360 degrees while the athlete model is in motion” [PAGE 13 of 20]. But the recursive belief structure is never modeled. The system does not represent the athlete’s confidence, the coach’s trust, or the athlete’s model of the system’s reliability.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under time pressure, an athlete under load tends to regress to overlearned motor habits, and a coach under match pressure tends to regress to verbal correction and video review. The system’s response is a 3D animation and text/video modules [PAGE 12 of 20], not a detector of habit regression. If the athlete’s technique degrades, the system may still display the canonical model; the coach may interpret the canonical model as evidence that the athlete has received correction. That is a recursive false consensus: \(A\) believes \(S\) is correcting, \(C\) believes \(A\) is correcting, and \(S\) has no state variable for either belief.

The counter-trap is automation bias. If \(S\) outputs “the optimal tactics and decisions” [PAGE 2 of 20], the coach may over-trust the output because it is labeled AI. A counter-trap design would require uncertainty display, coach override, and a no-recommendation state when confidence is low. The paper does not provide a confidence estimate for the volleyball decision layer. It provides timing regressions and a scheduling model, not a calibrated tactical policy. The recursive danger is that the athlete begins to optimize for the system’s visible feedback rather than for the game, and the coach begins to optimize for the system’s dashboard rather than for the athlete’s retention.

The clock is the third failure mode. Real-time assistance has a human decision horizon \(\tau_{\text{human}}\). If end-to-end latency \(L\) satisfies \(L>\tau_{\text{human}}\), the tactical recommendation is stale, even if the scheduler met its own deadline. The paper claims “real-time tracking” [PAGE 2 of 20] and reports a worst-case environment of “1 task migration and 2 context switches” [PAGE 9 of 20], but it does not report end-to-end camera-to-screen latency, network latency, or human response time. The recursive effect is that a missed clock changes trust: the athlete stops using the system, the coach stops trusting the dashboard, and the system’s future data becomes biased toward only the situations in which it was fast enough.

A defensible recursive design would make three states explicit: athlete skill state, coach trust state, and system confidence state. It would suppress tactical prompts when \(L>\tau_{\text{human}}\), show “no recommendation” when confidence is below a pre-registered threshold, and log override events so that habit regression and automation bias can be measured. The source does not do this; its recursive theory of mind is implicit and unsupported.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
