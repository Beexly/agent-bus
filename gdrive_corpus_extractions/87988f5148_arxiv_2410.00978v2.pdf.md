<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s causal architecture is a simultaneous-equation peer-effects model applied to match-level binary toxicity labels. The first move is to define \(Y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) as whether player \(j\) used toxic speech in match \(i\), with player-specific intercept \(\alpha_j\) and teammate outcome \(Y_{ik}\). The structural equation is stated verbatim as “\(Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta \times Y_{ik} + \varepsilon_{ij}\)” [PAGE 2]. The estimand \(\beta\) is the change in player \(j\)’s probability of toxic speech induced by teammate \(k\)’s toxic speech, holding other factors constant [PAGE 2]. The immediate gray-zone is that \(Y_{ik}\) is not exogenous: the paper writes the mirror equation “\(Y_{ik} = \alpha_k + \beta \times Y_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ik}\)” [PAGE 3], then substitutes the first into the second and solves for \(Y_{ik}\). The algebra is multi-hop: \(Y_{ik}=\alpha_k+\beta(\alpha_j+\beta Y_{ik}+\varepsilon_{ij})+\varepsilon_{ik}\), hence \((1-\beta^2)Y_{ik}=\alpha_k+\beta(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})+\varepsilon_{ik}\), hence \(Y_{ik}=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^2}(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})+\frac{1}{1-\beta^2}(\alpha_k+\varepsilon_{ik})\) [PAGE 3]. That result is the formal reflection problem: \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) enters \(Y_{ik}\), so OLS on the structural equation captures both \(j\)’s effect on \(k\) and \(k\)’s “reflection” on \(j\). The paper states the consequence explicitly: “OLS estimates do not only capture the effect of the teammate’s toxic speech on a player’s probability of engaging in such behavior but also its ‘reflection’” [PAGE 3].

The second hop is identification. The paper instruments \(Y_{ik}\) with \(\bar{Y}_{k,-j}\), the probability that player \(k\) engages in toxic speech in matches where \(k\) is not paired with \(j\): “\(Y_{ik} = \delta_j + \gamma \times \bar{Y}_{k,-j} + u_{ik}\)” [PAGE 3]. This is a leave-one-out or spatial instrument. Relevance is empirically checkable through the first-stage \(F\) statistic; the paper’s rule is that “the F statistic against the null hypothesis that the instrument is irrelevant in the first-stage regressions should have a value greater than ten” [PAGE 4]. The reported first-stage \(F\) values are 1937.6, 497.6, 6855.3, and 1485.4 across the four samples [PAGE 4], so weak-instrument breakdown is not the live threat in the reported estimates. Exclusion is not testable: the paper must assume independence of the relevant error terms, and it states the sufficient condition as “\(\varepsilon_{ij} \perp\!\!\!\perp \varepsilon_{\ell k}\) for all matches \(i, \ell\) in which players \(j\) and \(k\) are paired” [PAGE 4]. This is the main gray zone because players can form parties, share habits, or react to the same match events; the paper acknowledges both same-match error correlation and cross-match party-selection correlation [PAGE 3].

The third hop is interpretation. The IV estimand is a local average treatment effect for compliers: “our identification strategy yields an estimate of the local average treatment effect (LATE) for ‘compliers’” [PAGE 4]. Compliers are players whose teammates were toxic because those teammates were exogenously more likely to be toxic in other matches. This excludes, for example, players who intentionally provoke teammates. Thus the estimate is not automatically an average treatment effect for all players. The fourth hop is generalization to Quads: the RHS becomes the share of teammates toxic, “\(Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta \times \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} Y_{ik}}{K} + \varepsilon_{ij}\)” [PAGE 4], so \(\beta\) is the effect of all teammates being toxic, and the single-teammate effect in Quads is one-third of that coefficient per the footnote [PAGE 5]. The fifth hop is estimation: player fixed effects are absorbed by demeaning dependent, independent, and instrumental variables at the individual level [PAGE 5], with restrictions requiring at least one other-match observation for the teammate and at least two matches for the player [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit must separate three quantities: OLS association, 2SLS LATE, and ITT exposure. The paper reports Duos 2SLS 0.0737 versus OLS 0.0375; algorithmic Duos 0.0291 versus 0.0185; Quads 0.0650 versus 0.0596; all modes 0.0757 versus 0.0420 [PAGE 6]. The paper expresses effects as multiples of average toxicity: 29.6, 28.6, 26.1, and 30.3 times the average probability [PAGE 6]. That multiple is a dimensionless scaling of the probability-point estimate, not a risk ratio and not a betting quantity. The strongest internal audit is exposure: the paper admits it does not observe whether players heard the speech, and “if a player’s audio chat is disabled, they cannot hear their teammates’ toxic speech. In this case, it cannot plausibly affect their behavior” [PAGE 6]. Therefore the estimates are ITT, not direct-exposure effects: “our estimates can be interpreted as reflecting the effect of the ‘intent to treat,’ where the treatment is exposure to toxicity, rather than the effect of the treatment itself” [PAGE 6]. That makes the reported effects conservative under the stated monotonicity logic, but it also means the causal story is about teammate behavior, not verified auditory reception.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses probability-scale linear probability models. \(Y_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) is a binary match-level indicator; \(\alpha_j\) is a player-specific intercept in probability units; \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) is an error in probability units; \(\beta\) is a probability-point change per unit change in the teammate variable. In Duos the teammate variable is binary; in Quads it is the teammate share in \([0,1]\). No SI physical units such as meters, kilograms, or seconds are reported. The source reports counts of matches, players, and observations; \(F\) statistics are dimensionless; standard errors are in probability units. If one encodes the binary label information-theoretically, the natural auxiliary unit is bits via \(H_2(p)=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)\); for example, \(p=0.00264\) in Duos corresponds to about \(0.0264\) bits, but the paper itself reports probabilities and percentage points, not bits.

The core structural equation is “\(Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta \times Y_{ik} + \varepsilon_{ij}\)” [PAGE 2]. The simultaneity system is “\(Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta \times Y_{ik} + \varepsilon_{ij}\)” and “\(Y_{ik} = \alpha_k + \beta \times Y_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ik}\)” [PAGE 3]. Solving gives “\(Y_{ik}=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^2}\times(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})+\frac{1}{1-\beta^2}\times(\alpha_k+\varepsilon_{ik})\)” [PAGE 3]. The singularity is exact at \(\beta^2=1\), i.e. \(\beta=\pm 1\): the reduced form denominator vanishes and the simultaneous system has no unique reduced-form representation. For \(|\beta|>1\), the linear multiplier explodes and the linear probability model becomes dynamically unstable. The reported 2SLS estimates are 0.0291 to 0.0757 [PAGE 6], so the estimates are far from that singularity, but the singularity is a formal breakdown regime of the reflection derivation.

The IV first stage is “\(Y_{ik} = \delta_j + \gamma \times \bar{Y}_{k,-j} + u_{ik}\)” [PAGE 3], where \(\bar{Y}_{k,-j}\) is the probability that player \(k\) is toxic in matches not paired with \(j\). Relevance is accepted because the \(F\) statistics are 1937.6, 497.6, 6855.3, and 1485.4 [PAGE 4], all above the paper’s rule-of-thumb threshold of ten. Breakdown regime: if \(F\) falls to or below ten, or if \(\gamma\) is zero, the instrument is weak and 2SLS is unreliable. Exclusion requires independence: the paper states the sufficient condition “\(\varepsilon_{ij} \perp\!\!\!\perp \varepsilon_{\ell k}\) for all matches \(i, \ell\) in which players \(j\) and \(k\) are paired” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if party formation, shared match events, or unmeasured team chemistry correlate \(\bar{Y}_{k,-j}\) with \(\varepsilon_{ij}\), the exclusion restriction fails and the 2SLS estimate loses causal interpretation. The paper itself flags “misspecification and self-selection” and writes “corr(\(\varepsilon_{ij}, \varepsilon_{ik}\)) \(\neq 0\)” for same-team errors and “corr(\(\varepsilon_{ij}, \varepsilon_{\ell k}\)) \(\neq 0\) for all matches \(i, \ell\) in which players \(j\) and \(k\) are paired” for party selection [PAGE 3].

The generalized Quads equation is “\(Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta \times \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} Y_{ik}}{K} + \varepsilon_{ij}\)” [PAGE 4]. Here \(\beta\) is the effect of all teammates being toxic; the single-teammate Quads effect is \(\beta/K\), and the footnote says the single-teammate effect “can be computed by multiplying the coefficient by the portion of teammates it represents, that is, by one-third” [PAGE 5]. Fixed effects are absorbed by demeaning dependent, independent, and instrumental variables at the individual level [PAGE 5]. The LATE interpretation is explicit: the estimate is for “compliers” [PAGE 4], so heterogeneity can break the mapping from LATE to population average treatment effect.

Reported estimates are: Duos OLS 0.0375, 2SLS 0.0737 with SE 0.0211 and multiple 29.6; algorithmic Duos OLS 0.0185, 2SLS 0.0291 with SE 0.0118 and multiple 28.6; Quads OLS 0.0596, 2SLS 0.0650 with SE 0.0091 and multiple 26.1; all modes OLS 0.0420, 2SLS 0.0757 with SE 0.0088 and multiple 30.3 [PAGE 6]. Average toxic-speech probabilities are 0.264%, 0.102%, 0.249%, and 0.249% respectively [PAGE 3]. The abstract summarizes the range as “26.1 to 30.3 times the average player’s likelihood” [PAGE 1], and the introduction gives the absolute range as “2.91 to 7.57 percentage points (pp.)” [PAGE 2].

Breakdown regimes beyond the singularity are: weak instrument (\(F\) at or below ten); exclusion violation from correlated errors or party selection; LATE heterogeneity; binary linear-probability boundary distortion if fitted probabilities leave \([0,1]\); exposure failure when audio chat is disabled; and external-validity failure because the data are from “September 1 and October 31, 2023” [PAGE 2], North America, English-first ToxMod rollout, and Call of Duty: Warzone. The paper’s own exposure caveat is decisive for interpretation: “if a player’s audio chat is disabled, they cannot hear their teammates’ toxic speech. In this case, it cannot plausibly affect their behavior” [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is econometric peer effects plus social-psychological conformity. The paper explicitly frames peer effects through conformity to social norms and cites the peer-effects literature [18]–[24] and conformity work [28] [PAGE 1]. It also cites prior correlational toxicity-contagion work [30], [31] [PAGE 1], and homophily as an alternative mechanism [40] [PAGE 5]. The econometric genealogy is visible in the reflection problem, the leave-one-out instrument, and the LATE framework: the paper cites spatial or “leave-one-out” instruments used in empirical industrial organization [36], [37] [PAGE 3], LATE/compliers [38] [PAGE 4], and fixed-effect demeaning [39] [PAGE 5]. This lineage matters because the paper is not inventing a new behavioral theory; it is importing an identification technology from demand/supply and program-evaluation econometrics into digital toxicity.

The retrocausal genealogy is specifically non-local in time. The instrument \(\bar{Y}_{k,-j}\) is computed from “all matches in which they are not paired with player \(j\)” [PAGE 3]. The paper does not say whether those leave-one-out matches precede or follow the focal match. If the instrument includes future matches, the treatment definition is forward-looking: a player’s current teammate toxicity is instrumented by behavior that may occur later in calendar time. That is not automatically invalid, but it creates a temporal-exclusion audit: if ToxMod behavior drifts across weeks, a future-match instrument can encode post-treatment adaptation rather than pre-treatment propensity. The source’s observation window is “September 1 and October 31, 2023” [PAGE 2], immediately after the “August 30, 2023” North America rollout [PAGE 2], so the genealogy is post-deployment and moderation-aware.

Multi-week traps are therefore real. First, moderation adaptation: players may learn to avoid detected speech after the rollout, changing both \(Y_{ik}\) and \(\bar{Y}_{k,-j}\) over weeks. Second, party formation: players can endogenously choose teammates, which the paper flags as self-selection [PAGE 3] and addresses partly with algorithmically matched pairs [PAGE 6]. Third, matchmaking and game updates can change team composition and interaction frequency over the two-month window. Fourth, classifier drift in ToxMod can change the binary label across weeks. Fifth, repeated-play learning can create dyadic habits that look like peer contagion but are stable relationship effects. The paper’s defense is that algorithmic-pair estimates preserve the relative magnitude: “While the absolute magnitude of peer effects in algorithmically matched pairs is lower compared to that of all Duos, these effects’ magnitude relative to the average probability ... is the same” and “the magnitude of estimated peer effects is unaltered by homophily” [PAGE 6]. That is a useful retrocausal firewall, but it does not eliminate week-level moderation drift because the paper does not report week-by-week stability.

The historical lineage also explains why the “viral” metaphor is a measured contagion claim, not a biological one. The paper’s contagion is behavioral: teammate toxic speech changes the probability of player toxic speech. The older peer-effects tradition supplies the reflection problem; the conformity tradition supplies the mechanism; the moderation-technology genealogy supplies the measurement layer. The resulting claim is conditional on ToxMod’s binary detection, the 2023 rollout, and the Warzone team structure. It is not a universal claim about all online games, all languages, or all forms of toxicity. The paper itself limits future work to “how the spread of toxic speech to a player’s teammates varies across different contexts” [PAGE 6].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading must start from the paper’s simultaneity, not from a single-agent habit story. Player \(j\)’s behavior depends on teammate \(k\)’s behavior, and \(k\)’s behavior depends on \(j\)’s; the structural system is mutually determined [PAGE 3]. In belief terms, let \(\mu_{j\to k}(t)=\Pr_j(Y_{ik}=1\mid \mathcal{I}_{ij}(t))\in[0,1]\) be \(j\)’s subjective probability that \(k\) will be toxic, given \(j\)’s information at time \(t\). A natural interpretive extension—not estimated by the paper—is \(\Pr(Y_{ij}=1\mid \mathcal{I}_{ij}(t))=\Phi(\alpha_j+\beta\mu_{j\to k}(t)+\delta C_i(t)+\eta H_j(t))\), where \(C_i(t)\) is the match clock or shrinking-zone pressure and \(H_j(t)\) is habit strength. The paper estimates a linear probability version with realized \(Y_{ik}\), not latent beliefs, so this extension is a theory-of-mind map rather than a source coefficient.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under acute match stress—shrinking playable area, elimination risk, lost loot, or teammate failure—a player may regress to previously learned toxic scripts. The fixed effect \(\alpha_j\) absorbs time-invariant propensity [PAGE 2], but it does not absorb within-match stress. If stress raises both \(j\)’s and \(k\)’s toxicity, then \(\mathrm{corr}(\varepsilon_{ij},\varepsilon_{ik})\neq 0\), exactly the same-match error-correlation problem the paper flags [PAGE 3]. The IV can survive only if the leave-one-out instrument is uncorrelated with that stress shock. Thus crisis habit regression is not a refutation by itself; it is a named exclusion threat that requires time-stamped stress covariates to adjudicate.

Counter-trap is the second failure mode. A player who anticipates a toxic teammate may preemptively mute voice chat, avoid the teammate, retaliate, report, or disengage. This is recursive: \(j\)’s belief about \(k\)’s type changes \(j\)’s exposure and response. The paper’s LATE explicitly excludes some strategic actors: compliers are players whose teammates were toxic because those teammates were exogenously more likely to be toxic elsewhere, and the definition “excludes players who intentionally use toxic language to provoke reactions from their teammates” [PAGE 4]. Counter-trap behavior also creates noncompliance: \(Y_{ik}\) may be 1 in the ToxMod data while \(j\) never hears it. The paper concedes this: “if a player’s audio chat is disabled, they cannot hear their teammates’ toxic speech. In this case, it cannot plausibly affect their behavior” [PAGE 6]. Therefore the estimated effect is an intent-to-treat effect on teammate behavior, not a direct exposure effect. If counter-trapping is widespread, the ITT estimate should attenuate among players who mute or disable audio; the paper cannot test that because it lacks exposure data.

The clock is the third failure mode. The binary match-level outcome aggregates events within a match, but toxicity has temporal order: \(k\) may be toxic before \(j\), after \(j\), or simultaneously. The structural model is written as a current-match simultaneous system [PAGE 3], not a time-ordered contagion process. A match clock or shrinking-zone clock can synchronize interaction and create common shocks. At the multi-week scale, the clock is the Sep 1–Oct 31 window [PAGE 2] and the post-rollout adaptation period. The paper’s Quads result is instructive: “the presence of more teammates diminishes the influence of a single teammate engaging in toxic speech” [PAGE 5], consistent with a dilution or attention-clock mechanism in which each teammate receives less interaction time. But the paper does not estimate event order, so the recursive theory remains an interpretation of the aggregate coefficient, not a measured temporal mediation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof is negative and boundary-setting. The paper’s data-generating process is digital: proprietary Call of Duty: Warzone match data, ToxMod voice-chat detection, team membership, and match outcomes [PAGE 2]. The paper reports no physical biomechanical variables, no weather variables, no physiological measures, no hardware latency, no input-device data, and no server-load data. Therefore no physical, biomechanical, or weather layer is measured or controlled in the source. A cross-layer variable \(V\) can veto the causal claim only if it is measured, varies within player or match, predicts both teammate toxic speech and player toxic speech conditional on \(\alpha_j\), and is correlated with the leave-one-out instrument. None of those conditions can be verified from [PAGE 1–7] because \(V\) is absent.

The one source-grounded physical/perceptual veto is audio audibility. The paper says: “if a player’s audio chat is disabled, they cannot hear their teammates’ toxic speech. In this case, it cannot plausibly affect their behavior” [PAGE 6]. This is a real cross-layer gate from the digital voice signal to the player’s auditory perception. It does not refute the paper’s estimate; it changes the estimand. The paper explicitly interprets the result as “the effect of the ‘intent to treat,’ where the treatment is exposure to toxicity, rather than the effect of the treatment itself” [PAGE 6]. Under that interpretation, the ITT effect is conservative if exposure is a prerequisite for the behavioral effect. A direct-exposure effect should be at least as large as the ITT effect under monotonicity, but the source does not measure exposure.

Biomechanical veto: no biomechanical variable appears. One could hypothesize that fatigue, motor stress, or device ergonomics raise toxic speech, but such a variable would need to correlate with the teammate’s other-match toxicity history to threaten exclusion. That is an external omitted-variable challenge, not an internal finding. Weather veto: no weather variable appears. Weather could affect latency, mood, or session length, but again it is unmeasured and would need to survive the same exclusion audit. Physical-layer veto: no physical venue, crowd, or exertion variable appears. The paper’s setting is online, so physical venue is not part of the measured interaction layer.

The adjudication is therefore: physical, biomechanical, and weather explanations are outside the identified estimand and cannot be used as post hoc refutations without measured tests. The internal veto that does bind is exposure/audibility, and the paper already narrows its claim to ITT. The source’s strongest cross-layer proof is thus a proof of scope: the causal claim is about teammate toxic speech in the digital match network, not about all sensory exposure, not about physiological stress, and not about weather-driven mood. Any stronger cross-layer claim would require data the source does not contain.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
