Audit mode: source-grounded derivation. The source is a forecast-verification paper, not a wagering document, so the reasoning must be built from the stochastic-process operators that the paper actually supplies: adapted forecasts, conditional ideal forecasts, proper scoring rules, divergences, martingale quadratic variation, energy scores, Wasserstein distances, and RKHS/MMD kernels. The key chain is as follows. First, Model 1 defines the observable process \(Y_n=f(U_n)\), a sub-filtration \(\mathcal F_n\subset \sigma(U_k;k\le n)\), and a \(\mathcal P(\mathcal Y)\)-valued forecast \(F_n\) adapted to \(\mathcal F_n\) [PAGE 4]. The lead-time convention is explicit: at time \(n\), the forecaster issues \(F_n\) for \(Y_{n+T}\), using only \(\mathcal F_n\) [PAGE 5]. The ideal forecast is the conditional law \(F^*_{n,T}=\mathcal L(Y_{n+T}\mid \mathcal F_n)\) [PAGE 5]. Thus the object being scored is not a marginal climatology and not a point prediction; it is a stochastic process of predictive distributions adapted to information. This immediately rules out any audit that compares a forecast against an outcome while ignoring the information set or the lead time.

Second, a proper scoring rule \(S(F,y)\) has the property that \(S(G,G)\le S(F,G)\) for all \(F,G\) in the score domain, and the associated divergence is \(\operatorname{div}_S(F,G)=S(F,G)-S(G,G)\ge0\) [PAGE 9]. The single-step conditional result is stated as Proposition 1: almost surely,
\[
\mathbb E[S(F,Y)-S(F^*,Y)\mid \mathcal F_0]
=\operatorname{div}_S(F,F^*)\ge0,
\]
with equality for strictly proper scores only when \(F=F^*\) a.s. [PAGE 11]. That proposition is the local engine; the global theorem is obtained by converting the local nonnegative conditional expectation into a long-run temporal average.

Third, Theorem 1 is the multi-hop result. Define
\[
\Delta_i=S(F_i,Y_{i+T})-S(F^*_{i,T},Y_{i+T}).
\]
Because \(\Delta_i\) is \(\mathcal F_{i+T}\)-measurable while \(\mathbb E[\Delta_i\mid\mathcal F_i]=\operatorname{div}_S(F_i,F^*_{i,T})\ge0\), the proof uses the telescoping decomposition
\[
\Delta_i-\mathbb E[\Delta_i\mid\mathcal F_i]
=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\left(
\mathbb E[\Delta_i\mid\mathcal F_{i+T+1-k}]
-\mathbb E[\Delta_i\mid\mathcal F_{i+T-k}]
\right).
\]
The summands define martingale increments \(\delta_i^k\), and the associated martingales \(M_n^k=\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_i^k\) have quadratic variation
\[
\langle M^k\rangle_n
=\sum_{i=1}^n
\mathbb E[(\delta_i^k)^2\mid \mathcal F_{i+T-k}].
\]
Condition (6) assumes this quadratic variation is \(O(n)\) a.s. [PAGE 11]. Then the strong law of large numbers for square-integrable martingales gives \(M_n^k/n\to0\) a.s., so
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\Delta_i
=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{div}_S(F_i,F^*_{i,T})+o_{\mathrm{a.s.}}(1).
\]
Therefore \(\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_i\Delta_i\ge0\), and the long-run score gap tends to zero iff the long-run average divergence tends to zero [PAGE 11]. The paper explicitly notes that this proof “does not assume any stationarity condition” [PAGE 12]. That is a crucial gray-zone audit point: stationarity is not the default license for averaging; the martingale quadratic-variation condition is.

Fourth, the energy-score/Wasserstein bridge is not a loose analogy. Theorem 2 states, under \(\alpha\in(0,2)\), \(\beta=2\), finite moments and uniform integrability, that
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{div}_S(F_i,G_i)\to0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n W_1(F_i,G_i)\to0
\]
[PAGE 13]. The divergence representation uses characteristic functions,
\[
\operatorname{div}_S(F,G)
=\frac1{C(d,\alpha)}
\int_{\mathbb R^d}
\frac{|\hat F(t)-\hat G(t)|^2}{\|t\|_2^{d+\alpha}}\,dt,
\]
so there is an explicit singularity at \(t=0\) [PAGE 15]. Near the origin, finite first moments make \(|\hat F-\hat G|^2\) vanish at least quadratically, giving radial integrability \(\int t^{1-\alpha}\,dt\) near zero; hence \(\alpha<2\) is the exact parameter threshold used by Theorem 2. At \(\alpha=2\), and a fortiori beyond it, this particular Fourier-integrability and \(W_1\)-equivalence argument is not the asserted regime. The kernel-score section extends the same logic: for a conditionally negative definite kernel \(\rho\), the kernel score is proper when \(\rho\) is continuous, and its divergence equals twice the squared MMD of the associated positive definite kernel [PAGE 23]. The CRPS example uses \(\rho(x,y)=|x-y|\), \(k(x,y)=|x|+|y|-|x-y|\), and the MMD domain is larger than the CRPS domain [PAGE 24]. Thus domain mismatch, strict propriety, finite moments, lead time, and quadratic variation are the principal breakdown gates. The rest of this audit applies those gates to historical lineage, recursive-agent traps, cross-layer vetoes, and RLVR-style counterfactual checks.

The source defines the dynamic forecast as a sequence of predictive distributions adapted to a filtration. The exact Model 1 statement is: “a sub-filtration \((\mathcal F_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}\) of the natural filtration \((\mathcal G_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}\) associated with \((U_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}\), i.e. \(\mathcal F_n\subset\mathcal G_n=\sigma(U_k;k\le n)\)” and “a sequence \((F_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}\) of \(\mathcal P(\mathcal Y)\)-valued random variables adapted to \((\mathcal F_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}\)” [PAGE 4]. The lead-time meaning is: “the forecaster produces at time \(n\) a forecast \(F_n\) for the future value \(Y_{n+T}\) and this predictive distribution \(F_n\) is built in view of the limited information encoded in \(\mathcal F_n\) only” [PAGE 5]. The ideal forecast is
\[
F^*_{n,T}=\mathcal L(Y_{n+T}\mid \mathcal F_n)\quad\text{a.s.},\qquad n\in\mathbb N,
\]
as defined on [PAGE 5].

A scoring rule is “a measurable real valued function \(S:\mathcal L\times\mathcal Y\to\mathbb R\)” [PAGE 9]. Properness is defined by
\[
S(G,G)\le S(F,G)\qquad\forall F,G\in\mathcal L,
\]
and strict properness means equality iff \(F=G\) [PAGE 9]. The divergence is
\[
\operatorname{div}_S(F,G)=S(F,G)-S(G,G)\ge0.
\]
The score \(S(F,y)\) has units inherited from the scoring rule: for CRPS with \(Y\) measured in metres or degrees Celsius, the score has units of \(Y\); for an energy kernel
\[
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\|x-y\|_\beta^\alpha
\]
[PAGE 10], the energy score
\[
S_{\rho_{\alpha,\beta}}(F,y)
=\int_{\mathbb R^d}\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)\,dF(x)
-\frac12\int_{\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d}
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(x,x')\,F(dx)F(dx')
\]
has units \([Y]^\alpha\). Divergence has the same units as the score. The Wasserstein distance \(W_1\) has units \([Y]\). The martingale quadratic variation \(\langle M^k\rangle_n\) has units of score squared, so condition (6), \(\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E[(\delta_i^k)^2\mid\mathcal F_{i+T-k}]=O(n)\), is a statement about bounded mean-square growth per observation, not a dimensionless cosmetic assumption [PAGE 11].

Theorem 1 states:
\[
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\left(S(F_i,Y_{i+T})-S(F^*_{i,T},Y_{i+T})\right)\ge0
\quad\text{a.s.},
\]
and
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\left(S(F_i,Y_{i+T})-S(F^*_{i,T},Y_{i+T})\right)=0
\quad\text{a.s.}
\]
iff
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{div}_S(F_i,F^*_{i,T})\to0
\quad\text{a.s.}
\]
[PAGE 11]. The paper says “The proof of Theorem 1 is based on the strong law of large numbers for square integrable martingales and does not assume any stationarity condition” [PAGE 12]. A practical sufficient condition for the energy score with \(\alpha\le1\) and \(1\le\beta\) is uniform boundedness of first moments: \(\sup_i m(F_i)+m(F^*_{i,T})<\infty\) a.s., where \(m(F)\) is the first moment [PAGE 12].

The energy-score/Wasserstein result is exact only in its stated regime. Theorem 2 requires \(\alpha\in(0,2)\), \(\beta=2\), membership in \(\mathcal P_{\max(1,\alpha)}(\mathbb R^d)\), and uniform integrability [PAGE 13]. Under those hypotheses,
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{div}_S(F_i,G_i)\to0
\iff
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n W_1(F_i,G_i)\to0.
\]
The divergence representation is
\[
\operatorname{div}_S(F,G)
=\frac1{C(d,\alpha)}
\int_{\mathbb R^d}
\frac{|\hat F(t)-\hat G(t)|^2}{\|t\|_2^{d+\alpha}}\,dt,
\qquad C(d,\alpha)>0,
\]
where \(\hat F(t)=\int e^{it\cdot x}dF(x)\) [PAGE 15]. The singularity at \(t=0\) is explicit. For \(\alpha<2\), finite first moments make the integral locally integrable; at and beyond the \(\alpha=2\) threshold the displayed \(W_1\)-equivalence argument is not the theorem’s asserted regime. Uniform integrability is also not cosmetic: it prevents mass from escaping to infinity, which is exactly how an average divergence could shrink while average \(W_1\) does not. The paper states the equivalence “assuming condition (6) together with uniform integrability” [PAGE 13].

Energy-score propriety has exact parameter regimes from [PAGE 10]: “\(d=1\) and \(\alpha\in(0,2]\)”; “\(d\ge2\), \(\beta\in(0,2]\) and \(\alpha\in(0,\beta]\)”; “\(d=2\), \(\beta\in(2,+\infty]\) and \(\alpha\in(0,2]\).” Outside these regions, the energy score is not guaranteed proper; then the entire Theorem 1 discrimination logic is not licensed. Kernel scores require a conditionally negative definite kernel: symmetric, \(\rho(y,y)=0\), and
\[
\sum_{1\le i,j\le n}a_i a_j\rho(y_i,y_j)\le0
\quad\text{whenever}\quad \sum_{i=1}^n a_i=0
\]
[PAGE 22]. The associated score is
\[
S_\rho(F,y)
=\int_{\mathcal Y}\rho(x,y)\,dF(x)
-\frac12\int_{\mathcal Y\times\mathcal Y}
\rho(x,x')\,F(dx)F(dx').
\]
The paper states: “The scoring rule \(S_\rho\) is proper as soon as the kernel \(\rho\) is continuous” [PAGE 23], but strict propriety is not automatic; compactness and characteristic-kernel conditions are discussed via Steinwart and Ziegel. For the positive definite kernel
\[
k(x,y)=\rho(y_0,x)+\rho(y_0,y)-\rho(x,y),
\]
the divergence identity is
\[
\operatorname{div}_{S_\rho}(F,G)=2d_k^2(F,G)
\]
[PAGE 23]. For CRPS, \(\rho(x,y)=|x-y|\), \(k(x,y)=|x|+|y|-|x-y|\), and because \(k(x,x)=2|x|\), the MMD domain is \(\mathcal P_{1/2}(\mathbb R)\), while CRPS is defined on \(\mathcal P_1(\mathbb R)\) [PAGE 24]. This is an exact domain-breakdown regime: a claim transferred from CRPS to MMD or back without checking \(\mathcal P_1\) versus \(\mathcal P_{1/2}\) is unsupported.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a sequence of increasingly weak assumptions about information and time. The prequential and proper-scoring tradition is represented by Dawid’s prequential approach and by Gneiting and Raftery’s strict proper scoring rules; the source cites both and builds on the calibration literature of Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery, and on verification under serial dependence by Bröcker and Ben Bouallègue [PAGE 2, PAGE 17-18]. A crucial intermediate step is Holzmann and Eulert’s result on the role of the information set, cited for Proposition 1 and for the measurability convention [PAGE 9, PAGE 11]. Strähl and Ziegel’s cross-calibration framework is then used to interpret public forecasts from multiple experts [PAGE 12]. The present paper’s distinctive move is to replace a stationary or ergodic default by Model 1, which only requires adaptedness to a filtration and a lead time, and then to recover long-run discrimination through martingale stabilization [PAGE 4, PAGE 12]. The stationary ergodic corollary is retained but explicitly separated as Model 2 [PAGE 13].

A second lineage is the energy-score and kernel-score line. The energy kernel \(\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\|x-y\|_\beta^\alpha\) and the energy score are tied to Schoenberg, Koldobsky and Zastavnyi for propriety regimes [PAGE 10]. The Fourier representation of energy-score divergence is attributed to Szekely and is connected to Modeste and Dombry’s translation-invariant MMD work [PAGE 15]. The RKHS/MMD connection is grounded in Berg et al., Sejdinovic et al., Steinwart and Ziegel, Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan, Smola et al., and Steinwart and Christmann [PAGE 22-23]. The CRPS lineage runs through Epstein, Hersbach, Bröcker, and Taillardat et al. [PAGE 9, PAGE 17-19].

The multi-week traps are therefore historical rather than mysterious. Trap one: treating stationarity as the universal accounting convention. The source explicitly constructs a Model 1 process,
\[
U_{n+1}=t_n+s_n+\alpha U_n+\varepsilon_{n+1},
\]
where \(s_n\) is periodic seasonal variability and \(t_n\) is a trend that “can represent a global warming in the context of climate change,” and states that it “fulfills Model 1’s general assumptions but not Model 2’s” [PAGE 8]. A multi-week evaluation that assumes ergodicity while the truth has trend plus seasonality is a category error. Trap two: averaging a score without checking the martingale quadratic-variation condition. Theorem 1 permits temporal averaging under condition (6), not under a vague “large sample” intuition [PAGE 11-12]. Trap three: confusing properness with strict propriety. Properness gives a nonnegative divergence; strict propriety is what makes zero divergence identify the ideal forecast [PAGE 9, PAGE 13]. Trap four: confusing score divergence with Wasserstein closeness outside the energy-score regime. Theorem 2 requires \(\alpha\in(0,2)\), \(\beta=2\), finite moments, and uniform integrability [PAGE 13]. Trap five: cross-calibration mistakes. Public forecasts can generate a public filtration that outperforms individual experts, but private information is not automatically in the public filtration; the source’s Example 9 makes this exact [PAGE 12]. Trap six: domain leakage between CRPS and MMD, because CRPS is defined on \(\mathcal P_1(\mathbb R)\) while the associated MMD is defined on \(\mathcal P_{1/2}(\mathbb R)\) [PAGE 24]. These traps can persist for many evaluation cycles because each individual average can look stable while the wrong invariant—stationarity, moment boundedness, strict propriety, or domain membership—is silently false.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive-agent reading has three levels. Level 0 is the raw environment \(U_n\) and observable \(Y_n=f(U_n)\). Level 1 is the forecaster, who models the information filtration \(\mathcal F_n\) and emits \(F_n\). Level 2 is the evaluator, who models the forecaster’s information and scores \(F_n\) against \(Y_{n+T}\). Level 3 is the adversarial or regime-shifting environment, which may exploit the evaluator’s assumptions about stationarity, lead time, or score propriety. The source’s Example 8 formalizes Level 2 with nested filtrations \(\mathcal F_n^2\subset\mathcal F_n^1\): the better-informed expert has the lower long-run average score [PAGE 12]. Example 9 formalizes a public-information recursion: private forecasts \(F_i^j\) generate a public filtration
\[
\mathcal F_n=\sigma(F_i^j;i\le n,1\le j\le J),
\]
and the public ideal forecast can outperform each expert [PAGE 12]. This is a theory-of-mind result: the evaluator can infer information from the forecasts themselves, not only from the forecasters’ stated private data.

Crisis habit regression occurs when, under regime stress, an agent reverts to a previously successful stationary habit: marginal climatology, fixed-order autoregression, or a fixed calibration map. In source terms, this is the temptation to use Model 2 and the ergodic corollary even when the generating process is Model 1 with trend or seasonality. Example 4 is the formal counterexample: periodic \(s_n\) and trend \(t_n\) satisfy Model 1 but not Model 2 [PAGE 8]. The habit forecasts may look acceptable for several weeks because the trend is slow or the season is locally stable. But Theorem 1’s average divergence term does not vanish if the forecast conditions on the wrong filtration; the long-run score gap remains nonnegative and can become strictly positive. The counter-trap is an environment or adversary that rewards the habit temporarily: a public forecast can appear cross-calibrated because the public filtration contains enough information to mimic the private expert for a while [PAGE 12]. The evaluator’s recursive model of the forecaster must therefore include the forecast’s information source, not just the forecast’s numerical output.

The clock is the lead time \(T\). The source’s entire multi-step proof is a \(T\)-fold telescoping over \(\mathcal F_{i+T+1-k}\) [PAGE 11]. This means that a forecast issued at \(i\) is not scored until \(i+T\), and the martingale stabilization is over overlapping delayed outcomes. If \(T\) is large relative to the evaluation window, the average score gap may not have settled even if the forecast is good. If the quadratic-variation condition (6) fails, the martingale noise term need not vanish at rate \(n\), so the running average can drift or fluctuate rather than converge. The recursive evaluator must therefore treat the clock as a formal variable: lead-time mismatch, overlapping outcomes, and quadratic-variation growth are all observable traps. A counter-trap is to choose \(T\) after seeing outcomes or to compare forecasts with different \(T\) under one average. The source defines the ideal forecast relative to a fixed \(T\) [PAGE 5]; changing \(T\) changes the conditional law and therefore changes the score target. The correct habit is not “average and rank”; it is “state \(\mathcal F_n\), state \(T\), verify score propriety, verify moment and quadratic-variation conditions, then average.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer claim is admissible only if it passes four veto layers. Statistical veto: the forecast must be adapted to the claimed filtration and the target must be \(Y_{n+T}\), not \(Y_n\) or a marginal proxy; otherwise the ideal forecast \(F^*_{n,T}=\mathcal L(Y_{n+T}\mid\mathcal F_n)\) is not the object being scored [PAGE 5]. Scoring veto: \(S\) must be proper on the relevant domain; strict propriety is required if equality of scores is to identify the ideal forecast [PAGE 9]. Convergence veto: Theorem 1 requires condition (6), the quadratic-variation bound \(\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E[(\delta_i^k)^2\mid\mathcal F_{i+T-k}]=O(n)\) a.s. [PAGE 11]. If condition (6) fails, the martingale term need not vanish at rate \(n\), and the long-run score decomposition is not licensed. If the claim uses stationarity, the ergodic Corollary 1 additionally requires Model 2 and ergodicity [PAGE 13]. Domain veto: energy-score propriety is restricted to the parameter regions on [PAGE 10], and the energy-score/\(W_1\) equivalence is restricted to \(\alpha\in(0,2)\), \(\beta=2\), finite moments, and uniform integrability [PAGE 13]. Kernel-score properness requires continuity of \(\rho\), and strict propriety is not automatic [PAGE 23]. CRPS and its associated MMD live on different moment spaces, \(\mathcal P_1(\mathbb R)\) versus \(\mathcal P_{1/2}(\mathbb R)\) [PAGE 24].

Physical veto proof. The observable space \(\mathcal Y\) is a Polish space, and the scoring rule must be measurable and finite on the forecast domain [PAGE 4, PAGE 9]. If a physical variable has a distribution outside the score’s moment domain—for example a heavy-tailed quantity with no finite first moment when CRPS or first-moment energy score is used—then the score integral may fail to be well-defined. The paper’s sufficient condition for condition (6) in the energy-score case is uniform boundedness of first moments [PAGE 12]; if that bound is absent, the theorem’s hypothesis is unverified. A physical claim such as “this forecast is better” is therefore vetoed until the moment condition is checked. A second physical veto is support mismatch: if \(Y\) is constrained to a compact or nonnegative set but the forecast places mass outside the admissible support, calibration and score comparisons can be dominated by impossible mass. The source’s kernel-score discussion notes that compactness is a common sufficient condition for strict propriety via Steinwart and Ziegel [PAGE 23]. A physical model that ignores support constraints can appear sharp while assigning probability to impossible states.

Biomechanical veto proof. The source’s Example 2 gives the exact structure: the quantity of interest is \(Y_n=U_n^{(1)}\), but the available observation is \(X_n=U_n^{(2)}=U_n^{(1)}+\delta_n\) with measurement noise \(\delta_n\) [PAGE 8]. If the forecaster treats \(X_n\) as if it were \(Y_n\), the conditional law being estimated is \(\mathcal L(Y_{n+T}\mid \mathcal F_n)\) with \(\mathcal F_n=\sigma(X_k;k\le n)\), not \(\mathcal L(Y_{n+T}\mid \sigma(Y_k;k\le n))\). The noisy-information ideal forecast is necessarily wider than the noiseless-information ideal forecast. A biomechanical or health forecast that ignores measurement error will be overconfident; its average divergence from the true ideal forecast remains positive even if its scores look stable. This is a formal veto: target alignment and filtration alignment must be checked before any score comparison.

Weather veto proof. The source’s meteorological framing is explicit: \(U\) may represent atmospheric states, \(Y_n\) may be temperature, and \(\mathcal F_n\) may include temperature, pressure and precipitation records up to time \(n\) [PAGE 5]. The same source then gives the anti-stationarity example with periodic seasonal \(s_n\) and trend \(t_n\), stating that it “fulfills Model 1’s general assumptions but not Model 2’s” [PAGE 8]. Therefore a weather-evaluation claim that uses the stationary ergodic corollary without testing for trend and seasonality is vetoed. The correct weather-layer adjudication is: if \(s_n\) is periodic and \(t_n\) is a trend, then Model 1 applies; Theorem 1 may apply if condition (6) holds; the ergodic Corollary 1 does not apply. This is not a philosophical caution; it is a direct consequence of the source’s own example. A second weather veto is lead-time mismatch: a forecast for \(Y_{n+T}\) cannot be evaluated as if it were a nowcast \(Y_n\), because the ideal conditional law changes with \(T\) [PAGE 5]. A third weather veto is score-domain mismatch: precipitation is nonnegative and often heavy-tailed; a first-moment score may be undefined or the uniform-integrability hypothesis may fail. The veto is therefore layered: physical support, finite moments, proper score, correct filtration, correct lead time, and quadratic-variation control.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: nonstationary seasonal-plus-trend process evaluated by a stationary ergodic average. Let the environment follow the source’s Example 4,
\[
U_{n+1}=t_n+s_n+\alpha U_n+\varepsilon_{n+1},
\]
with periodic \(s_n\) and nonconstant trend \(t_n\), while the evaluator assumes Model 2 and applies Corollary 1 [PAGE 8, PAGE 13]. Verifiable prediction: the long-run average score gap
\[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\left(S(F_i,Y_{i+T})-S(F^*_{i,T},Y_{i+T})\right)
\]
will not converge to zero if \(F_i\) omits the seasonal and trend components, while the average divergence term will remain positive. A direct test is to compute the running average divergence and the running average \(W_1(F_i,F^*_{i,T})\); under the energy-score regime, both should fail to vanish together [PAGE 13]. Veto rule: reject the stationarity claim if a seasonal/trend model improves the held-out average score and if the residual trend is statistically persistent. The counterfactual prediction is that a correctly specified Model 1 forecast with trend and seasonality will have a lower long-run average score than the stationary habit forecast.

Scenario 2: energy score outside its proper or equivalence regime. Set \(d=1\) and \(\alpha=2.5\), or set \(\alpha\in(0,2)\) but use a distribution sequence with unbounded first moment so uniform integrability fails. The source’s propriety list permits \(d=1,\alpha\in(0,2]\) [PAGE 10], and Theorem 2’s \(W_1\) equivalence requires \(\alpha\in(0,2)\), finite moments, and uniform integrability [PAGE 13]. Verifiable prediction: outside the proper regime, the empirical energy score can rank a non-ideal forecast above the ideal forecast; outside the equivalence regime, average divergence can tend to zero while average \(W_1\) does not, or the score integral may be undefined. A direct test is to simulate a known ideal forecast, compute both \(\operatorname{div}_S\) and \(W_1\), and check whether
\[
\frac1n\sum_i\operatorname{div}_S(F_i,G_i)\to0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac1n\sum_i W_1(F_i,G_i)\to0
\]
agree within Monte Carlo error. Veto rule: reject any reward or ranking claim that uses an energy score outside the stated parameter region or that asserts \(W_1\) control without checking uniform integrability.

Scenario 3: measurement-error target mismatch. Use Example 2: target \(Y_n=U_n^{(1)}\), observation \(X_n=U_n^{(2)}=U_n^{(1)}+\delta_n\), and a forecast that conditions on \(X\)-history as if it were \(Y\)-history [PAGE 8]. Verifiable prediction: the forecast’s predictive variance will be too small relative to the true conditional law \(\mathcal L(Y_{n+T}\mid\sigma(X_k;k\le n))\); its average divergence from the noisy-information ideal forecast will be positive; and its average score gap will not vanish. A direct test is to compare three forecasts: the true noisy-information ideal, the overconfident forecast that ignores \(\delta_n\), and a forecast that correctly inflates variance. Veto rule: reject the claim if the forecast is scored against \(Y\) but its filtration contains only noisy \(X\) and it does not model the measurement-error conditional law. A fourth, closely related RLVR check is condition (6): if the martingale quadratic variation \(\langle M^k\rangle_n\) grows faster than \(O(n)\), then the running score gap need not stabilize; the verifiable prediction is that the running average’s variance will fail to shrink at the expected rate and that the martingale term \(M_n^k/n\) will not vanish.
