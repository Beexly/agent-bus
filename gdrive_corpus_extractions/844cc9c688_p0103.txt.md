**1. Lineage and Historical Context**
Egbert (1943) directly adopted Tolman’s forward-pointing expectation framework to analyze human maze behavior, establishing a direct genealogical link from animal cognition to complex human learning systems. This lineage demonstrates how foundational cognitive theories were built on the principle that expectations are inherently temporal projections, a concept central to understanding how agents navigate uncertain environments.

**2. Multi-Week Traps in Behavioral Analysis**
In applied settings, such as multi-week behavioral interventions or training programs, a common trap is assuming that an agent's behavior at Week 3 is driven solely by stimuli present at Week 3. Tolman’s model warns against this: an action at any given time *t* is governed by expectations formed at *t* - Δ*t*, which may have originated from stimuli encountered weeks prior. This creates a "memory lag" where the apparent trigger for a behavior is misidentified, leading to flawed intervention strategies. For example, a subject's hesitation in a new environment might stem from a failed verification of a spatial expectation established days earlier, not from the current novelty.

**3. Connection to Modern Cognitive Science**
The "forward-pointing" nature of expectations prefigures modern predictive processing frameworks, where the brain is seen as a prediction machine constantly generating top-down models of imminent sensory input. Tolman’s "cognitive map" can be interpreted as a sophisticated internal model whose predictions must be verified through ongoing interaction with the environment, a process now understood as minimizing prediction error.

*   **Setup:** A rodent is trained in a linear alley where a side door (*S₁*) opens at *t=5s*. It learns the expectation "here an opening". At test, the door is removed but a visual cue (*S₂*) identical to the pre-opening cue is presented.

*   **Veto:** The rat approaches the wall where the door was, sniffs, finds no crevice (fails verification), and aborts the action. The RLVR veto occurs because the environmental support (the actual opening) is absent, nullifying the expectation despite the triggering stimulus being present. **Prediction:** The rat will exhibit orienting responses toward the empty space before retreating, a behavior inconsistent with simple S-R chaining.

**Scenario 2: The Delayed Verification Trap (Temporal Dissociation Veto)**
*   **Setup:** In a two-stage task, Stimulus A signals that Reward B will be available only if Action X is performed within a 30-second window after A.
*   **Counterfactual:** A reflexive model suggests Action X should be cued by the immediate availability of B. But under Tolman, A releases an expectation "Reward B is contingent on future Action X".
*   **Veto:** If the subject delays Action X beyond 30 seconds, the expectation is not falsified until it attempts to consume B and finds it gone. The veto is the failure of verification at the goal stage, which retroactively penalizes the initial expectation-release system. **Prediction:** Performance will degrade not because the delay itself is punishing, but because the delayed action fails to satisfy the temporally-bound expectation set by A, demonstrating the dissociation between stimulus release and later verification.

**Scenario 3: The Means-Object Illusion (Qualitative Veto)**
*   **Setup:** A puzzle box has a lever (*L*) that looks solid but is actually spring-loaded to retract upon touch. The subject expects "here a stable pullable object".

*   **Veto:** Upon contact, the lever retracts. The immediate tactile commerce reveals the object is not "stable," violating the expectation about its manipulandum properties. This qualitative mismatch vetoes the ongoing motor program. **Prediction:** The subject will quickly inhibit further attempts to pull the specific lever, but may still approach other levers in the apparatus, showing discrimination based on violated means-object expectations rather than global shock avoidance.

For a means-object like "an opening," E is a preparatory motor set ("ready to enter"). V is the physical act of entering. The "failure to verify" isn't just a missed reward; it's a structural contradiction between the anticipated affordance and the encountered reality.

Next, consider the phrase "qualitatively other than." The stimulus complex (e.g., distal cues like light patterns on a wall) is of a different ontological category than the proximal encounter (the tactile sensation of a smooth surface vs. a concave one). This necessitates a representational bridge—the cognitive map—which holds the relational information needed to translate S into a predicted V. Without this bridge, S could not reliably point forward to a specific V.

Furthermore, the "pre-setting" function highlights the adaptive value: behavior is organized in advance of need. An animal doesn't wait to feel a wall to decide not to push; the memory/perception of "wall" pre-sets inhibition. This pre-setting is what Tolman calls "purposive"—behavior aimed at a future state, guided by anticipatory structures.

Finally, the dissociation applies equally to goal-objects. A food cue (S) releases hunger-expectation (E). The later eating (V) verifies it. The difference is teleonomic: the goal-object's verification fulfills a drive, while the means-object's verification enables effective navigation. Both, however, rely on the same forward-pointing architecture, proving that even low-level discrimination is cognitively mediated, not merely reflexive.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
We formalize Tolman's descriptive logic into a deterministic operator framework. Let:
- \( \mathcal{S} \) be the stimulus complex at time \( t_0 \).
- \( \mathcal{E}(\mathcal{S}) \) be the expectation released by \( \mathcal{S} \), a cognitive state mapping to an anticipated encounter \( \mathcal{V}_{\text{pred}} \).
- \( \mathcal{V} \) be the actual environmental encounter/verification at time \( t_1 > t_0 \).
- \( \Delta T = t_1 - t_0 > 0 \) be the mandatory temporal separation.

**Core Operator: Temporal Projection**
\[
\boxed{\mathcal{P}: \mathcal{S}(t_0) \xrightarrow{\text{releases}} \mathcal{E}(t_0^+) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathcal{E} \implies \mathcal{V}_{\text{pred}}(t_0 + \Delta T)}
\]
This projects the stimulus forward in time to a predicted verification event.

**Verification Condition Function:**
Define \( \chi(\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{V}) \) as an indicator function over the cognitive state and encounter:
\[
\chi(\mathcal{E}, \mathcal{V}) = 
\begin{cases} 
1 & \text{if } \mathcal{V} \text{ confirms } \mathcal{V}_{\text{pred}} \\
0 & \text{if } \mathcal{V} \text{ disconfirms } \mathcal{V}_{\text{pred}}
\end{cases}
\]
Failure (\( \chi = 0 \)) constitutes a "failed verification," updating the cognitive map.

**Units and Dimensionality:**
- Time \( t \): SI unit seconds (s).
- \( \Delta T \): Seconds (s), representing the minimal latency between cue and verification.
- \( \mathcal{E} \): Dimensionless cognitive state variable, but its content carries semantic units derived from the environmental regularities it encodes (e.g., "opening" implies a vector field of permissible locomotion).

**Exact Breakdown/Singularity Regimes:**
1.  **Zero-Latency Singularity (\( \Delta T \to 0 \)):** If verification occurred simultaneously with stimulation, the forward-pointing property collapses. Behavior becomes purely reactive, indistinguishable from Pavlovian reflex arcs. Tolman’s entire theory breaks down, reverting to S-R psychology.
2.  **Perfect Prediction Limit (\( \chi \equiv 1 \)):** If all verifications succeed, the cognitive map becomes static and infallible. Learning ceases, as there are no prediction errors to drive map updates. This represents a degenerate case of a perfectly adapted organism in a perfectly predictable environment.
3.  **Total Mismatch Catastrophe (\( \chi \equiv 0 \)):** Persistent failed verifications indicate a fundamentally invalid cognitive map. The system enters a state of chronic frustration, potentially leading to stereotypic behavior or complete withdrawal, as the forward-pointing projections are consistently useless.

The source text [PAGE 1] explicitly supports the non-zero \( \Delta T \) requirement: "the actual encounter... is a temporally separate and later event" and "a moment of stimulation is prior in time to... a direct encounter." This is the foundational postulate of the model.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis conditions—such as sudden environmental change or reward omission—an agent's recursive theory of mind regarding its own decision-making shifts dramatically, cycling through three phases aligned with Tolman’s framework:

**1. Crisis Habit Regression (Phase 1):**
When faced with a failed verification (\( \chi = 0 \)), the agent first defaults to **over-trusting past expectations**. It assumes the cognitive map is correct and the failure is due to noise or temporary anomaly. Behavior exhibits increased persistence of the old action pattern (e.g., repeatedly checking the empty food location), reflecting a deep-seated belief in the stability of learned expectancies.

**2. Counter-Trap Activation (Phase 2):**
After repeated failures, the agent hypothesizes that the **environment itself is deceptive or adversarial**. It shifts from trust in the map to suspicion of the verification process. The agent begins testing the reliability of cues and the consistency of outcomes, engaging in exploratory behaviors designed to stress-test the cognitive map. This is the "counter-trap" phase, where the agent tries to outsmart perceived tricks.

**3. Recalibration Clock (Phase 3):**
Once deception is ruled out, the agent accepts the map’s invalidity and initiates **map recalibration**. The forward-pointing expectations are systematically updated based on new S-V pairings. The "clock" here is the time required to rebuild a reliable predictive structure from scratch. Behavior shows rapid, often erratic, trial-and-error as new expectations are formed and tested.

This recursive cycle—from blind faith in expectations, through paranoid testing, to humble reconstruction—reveals the dynamic interplay between an agent's model of the world and its metacognitive assessment of that model's reliability. It explains why extinction is not simple unlearning but a complex process of epistemic revision.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
