<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The concept of quantifying motivation on a centigrade scale (the "mote") finds its historical lineage in the late 19th-century psychophysics of Fechner and Weber, which sought to bridge physical stimuli and mental sensations. Hull extends this from sensory continua to motivational drives, influenced heavily by Thorndike’s law of effect and Watson’s behaviorist rejection of introspective constructs. By defining $D$ mathematically, Hull anchors behaviorism in a neo-Pavlovian framework where internal states are just latent variables waiting for operational definition.

However, a multi-week trap emerges from this genealogy. Early experiments by Beach (cited indirectly via phenomenon 1) on gonadectomized animals demonstrated that hormones are necessary but not sufficient for complex sexual behaviors; they prime the readiness (drive) but do not specify the exact motor patterns. Hull’s formalism risks reifying "drive" as a fluid that flows through neural pipes, potentially obscuring the distributed, emergent nature of behavior. The historical trap is assuming that because we can measure hormone levels (physical cause) and observe behavior (effect), the intermediate construct $D$ must be a linear, scalar quantity. Modern neuroendocrinology reveals that hormones alter receptor densities, gene expression, and synaptic plasticity over days/weeks, creating a slow-moving background field rather than an instantaneous "boost" in $D$. Thus, the immediate "restoration" described by Hull might be a lagged effect of prior priming, challenging the strict temporal locality implied by a simple $D(t)$ variable. Furthermore, the cross-sex behavior anomaly hints at epigenetic or organizational effects of early hormone exposure that permanently wire the brain—a factor completely outside the scope of Hull’s proximal $D$ calculus, representing a generational shift from functional to developmental causality.
