**Adolphe Quetelet and the “Average Man”**  
Quetelet (1796–1874) introduced the idea that social and biological phenomena could be described by normal curves. His concept of *l’homme moyen* posited that individuals deviate from a central tendency due to chance and heredity. Galton explicitly adopted this statistical framework, but applied it to familial resemblance rather than aggregate populations. The notion that a trait’s distribution is stable and bell‑shaped became a cornerstone of Galton’s approach.

Prior to tackling disease, Galton studied human height, discovering that the correlation between parent and child stature was approximately 0.5, leading to the regression coefficient of ⅔ when considering both parents. This empirical finding, published in *Hereditary Talent and Character* (1885), was generalized to other traits, including disease susceptibility. The leap from physical dimensions to complex pathologies like tuberculosis involved an implicit assumption of quantitative continuity—that susceptibility is a measurable quantity subject to the same laws as stature.

**Late 19th‑Century Heredity Theories**  
Contemporaneous debates centered on blending inheritance versus particulate (Mendelian) genetics. Galton rejected Mendel’s discrete factors, favoring a continuous “gemmule” theory. His regression model was designed to explain why offspring of extreme parents do not remain extreme, countering the fear of perpetual degeneration. However, the rediscovery of Mendel in 1900 would later show that simple additive models fail for many traits, especially those with major genes.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even if Galton’s statistical operators were internally consistent, they must pass scrutiny from multiple layers of reality. Several cross‑layer factors provide decisive vetoes on his purely hereditarian model of consumption.

**Physical / Biomechanical Layer**  
Tuberculosis is an infectious disease caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. Transmission requires aerosolized bacilli, inhalation, and establishment of infection within alveolar macrophages. Individual susceptibility is influenced by immune function, lung architecture, nutritional status, and co‑morbidities (e.g., diabetes). Galton’s model reduces this complexity to a single scalar “consumptivity” with a fixed threshold. This is a massive oversimplification that cannot capture the multi‑stage process of infection, latency, and reactivation. The existence of latent TB infection (LTBI) in up to a quarter of the global population, with only 5–10% progressing to active disease, demonstrates that the threshold is not fixed but dynamically regulated by host and environmental factors. Therefore, any model that treats disease manifestation as a static exceedance of a heritable trait is fundamentally flawed.

**Environmental / Socio‑economic Layer**  
Historical records unequivocally show that TB incidence correlates strongly with overcrowding, poor ventilation, malnutrition, and occupational exposures (e.g., silica dust). The dramatic decline in TB mortality in Europe and North America from the 1850s to the 1950s occurred long before the advent of effective chemotherapy (streptomycin in 1944). This decline is attributed to improved living standards, nutrition, and public health measures. Galton’s data, collected in the 1880s, reflect a snapshot during a period of high endemicity, but his model cannot account for secular trends. If “consumptivity” were primarily heritable and stable, we would expect disease rates to remain constant across generations absent deliberate breeding programs—which they did not. Thus, environmental factors constitute a powerful veto on hereditarian explanations.

**Information / Network Layer**  
Disease spread follows network dynamics determined by contact patterns, mobility, and bacterial virulence. Even with identical genetic susceptibility, an individual’s risk depends on the number and intensity of exposures. Galton’s model assumes independence among individuals and ignores transmission chains. Modern epidemiology uses reproductive number \(R_0\) and household secondary attack rates to quantify these effects. Ignoring them leads to spurious attributions of clustering to genetics when in fact it may be due to shared exposure.

**Proof of Veto**  
Consider a thought experiment: take two genetically identical groups (e.g., monozygotic twins) and place one in a well‑ventilated, well‑nourished environment and the other in a crowded, malnourished setting. Empirical evidence shows dramatically different TB incidence. Since Galton’s model predicts identical outcomes for identical “consumptivity”, the model fails this test. Hence, the cross‑layer veto is conclusive: environmental and infectious factors dominate hereditary ones in determining observed disease patterns, at least in the short term.

Therefore, while Galton’s statistical machinery is intellectually intriguing, it cannot stand as a complete explanation for the heredity of consumption. Any modern application must incorporate multi‑level data and explicitly model gene‑environment interactions.
