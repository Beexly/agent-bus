<THINK_TRACE>
The source text is a 1920 coaching manual, not a statistical model, but it contains a surprisingly clean engineering logic: build the body, secure the ball, encode intention into signals, synchronize bodies in time, and only then add tactical complexity. Walter Camp presents the team as a sociotechnical system. The first causal layer is somatic: sleep, food, cleanliness, and progressive loading determine whether the player can later execute skills. [PAGE 18] says, “First—Cut out smoking,” and [PAGE 19] warns that playing football directly after eating can produce apparent heart trouble. The second layer is ball security. [PAGE 16] states that a poorly taught handler becomes “a fumbler,” and [PAGE 28] emphasizes possession because “the side that has possession makes the attack.” This creates a multi-hop derivation: early handling practice reduces fumble probability, which increases expected possession, which increases the number of structured offensive attempts available to the team.

The third layer is information. Camp’s signal system is not merely a list of plays; it is a communication protocol. [PAGE 58] says, “Signals are the force which binds the team and knits it into a coherent, smooth, efficient machine.” But he immediately warns against complexity: [PAGE 58] “The worst signals are those that take so much study or that are so complicated that the players get mixed and are helpless.” The formal implication is that signal entropy must be matched to team competence. If the signal has too many alternatives, the probability of assignment error rises. Camp also embeds deception inside simplicity: the blind number can hide the starting moment, as in [PAGE 62], where the quarterback calls “62, 24, i” and the center snaps “without waiting for the I.” The opponent is kept in a state of temporal uncertainty, while the offense reduces its own uncertainty through rehearsal.

The fourth layer is temporal mechanics. Camp repeatedly treats football as synchronized motion. [PAGE 71] states that linemen, interferers, and runners “must all hit the same spot with their dynamic effort, else all their effort is wasted.” This is a coordination equation: a hole is useful only if its opening time intersects the runner’s arrival time. [PAGE 72] explains both failure modes: “If the hole is opened too soon, it is generally closed when the runner arrives,” and “If a hole is opened too late, the runner goes up against a dead wall.” The gray zone is therefore not “effort” versus “laziness,” but timing. A player can be powerful and still useless if his force arrives outside the temporal window.

The fifth layer is ballistic and contact mechanics. Camp’s kicking instruction is essentially a targeting problem. [PAGE 46] says the punter should “hit the ball squarely with his instep upon the point of the ball,” and [PAGE 68] insists, “The kicker must always strike for speed, speed, speed.” The place-kick and punt become projectile problems constrained by impulse, launch angle, contact point, wind, and ball condition. [PAGE 87] notes that “A wet, soggy ball must be cocked up more than a dry one,” while [PAGE 88] says a new ball “travels low and fast.” These are not vague remarks; they identify boundary conditions for the kicking operator.

The gray-zone audit must also confront internal tensions. Camp says, “Go at the early practice easily” [PAGE 31], yet also says, “Practice fast and hard” while avoiding exhaustion [PAGE 32]. The resolution is dose control: high technical quality, short duration, and avoidance of fatigue-induced slowness. He warns that practicing “to a point where, through weariness, the work becomes slow” creates “habits of slowness” [PAGE 32]. Another tension is between simplicity and unpredictability. The signal system must be simple enough for the team but opaque enough to the opponent; Camp solves this by placing deception in the timing operator rather than in semantic complexity [PAGE 62]. A third tension is between spirit and staleness. [PAGE 179] praises team spirit as something that can overcome technical inferiority, but [PAGE 184] warns that “There is an invisible line in conditioning,” beyond which the player becomes stale. Spirit is therefore not infinite motivation; it is a state-dependent resource that must be protected by rest, sleep, and appropriate practice load [PAGE 185].

A final audit point is evidentiary. The text provides qualitative laws, not modern measurements. Still, its repeated cause-effect claims can be formalized as testable operators: possession security, signal entropy, temporal gating, projectile range, impulse transfer, wet-surface friction, joint torque, and cumulative training load. The proper method is not to invent a fake universal ratio, but to state each operator with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes. Where the source says a condition is “almost impossible” [PAGE 104], that language marks a veto regime, not a motivational problem.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Signal-information operator.  
Camp’s signal system can be treated as a discrete communication channel. Let a signal be a tuple
\[
S=(b,c,h,k),
\]
where \(b\) is the blind/start cue, \(c\) is the carrier identifier, \(h\) is the hole identifier, and \(k\) is an optional modifier. The uncertainty of the signal set is
\[
H(S)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i \quad \text{bits per signal}.
\]
If there are \(N\) equally likely signals, \(H=\log_2 N\) bits. The source warning is direct: “The worst signals are those that take so much study or that are so complicated that the players get mixed and are helpless” [PAGE 58]. Assumption: under fixed training time, assignment error increases with signal entropy and with lack of repetition. Breakdown regime: when \(H(S)\) exceeds the team’s trained processing capacity, the team exhibits misaligned starts, missed holes, or false starts. Camp’s countermeasure is walk-through rehearsal: “At this stage of the practice the candidates should walk through the plays” [PAGE 64].

2. Temporal-gating operator for hole opening.  
Let
\[
t_{\text{arr}}=t_{\text{snap}}+t_{\text{handoff}}+\frac{d_{\text{run}}}{v_{\text{run}}}
\]
be the runner’s arrival time at the intended point, and let
\[
t_{\text{open}}=t_{\text{snap}}+t_{\text{block}}
\]
be the time at which the hole becomes usable. Define the timing error
\[
\Delta t=t_{\text{open}}-t_{\text{arr}}.
\]
Units: seconds. An effective hole requires
\[
0<\Delta t<\tau_{\text{recover}},
\]
where \(\tau_{\text{recover}}\) is the time required for the displaced defender to recover. If \(\Delta t\leq 0\), the runner meets a closed wall; if \(\Delta t\geq \tau_{\text{recover}}\), the hole has already closed. This formalizes [PAGE 72]: “If the hole is opened too soon, it is generally closed when the runner arrives,” and “If a hole is opened too late, the runner goes up against a dead wall.” [PAGE 71] adds that all actors “must all hit the same spot with their dynamic effort.”

3. Projectile operator for place kicking.  
Assume a place kick is a point-mass projectile launched from height \(h_0\) with speed \(v_0\) and angle \(\theta\). Ignoring drag and wind,
\[
x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\,t,
\]
\[
y(t)=h_0+v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2.
\]
To clear a horizontal bar at distance \(d\) and height \(b\),
\[
b=h_0+d\tan\theta-\frac{g d^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}.
\]
Units: meters for \(d,h_0,b\); meters per second for \(v_0\); radians for \(\theta\); \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}\). Camp’s instruction that the kicker “hit the ball squarely with his instep upon the point of the ball” [PAGE 46] corresponds to reducing off-center impulse and unwanted spin. Breakdown regimes: wind, drag, wet ball, new ball, and angled kicks. [PAGE 87] says a wet ball must be “cocked up more,” and [PAGE 88] says a new ball travels “low and fast.” With wind, the relevant drag force is
\[
\mathbf{F}_d=\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d A\,\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}\|\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}},
\quad
\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}=\mathbf{v}_{\text{ball}}-\mathbf{v}_{\text{wind}},
\]
with units newtons. The simple projectile equation breaks down when crosswind, wet mass distribution, or aerodynamic asymmetry dominates.

4. Impulse and angular-control operator.  
The kick or throw is an impulse event:
\[
\mathbf{J}=\int_{t_c}^{t_c+\Delta t_c}\mathbf{F}(t)\,dt
=m(\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{v}^{-}),
\]
where \(\mathbf{J}\) is impulse in newton-seconds, \(m\) is ball mass in kilograms, and \(\mathbf{v}^{-},\mathbf{v}^{+}\) are pre- and post-contact velocities in meters per second. If the impulse vector does not pass through the ball’s center of mass, angular impulse appears:
\[
\Delta\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{J},
\]
where \(\mathbf{r}\) is the contact offset in meters and \(\Delta\mathbf{L}\) is angular impulse in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\). This explains why Camp insists on a target and square contact [PAGE 46]. Breakdown: off-center contact creates wobble, slice, or unpredictable spiral; wet contact reduces effective friction and control [PAGE 104].

5. Wet-surface grip operator.  
For catching or securing a wet ball, the maximum tangential force before slip is
\[
F_{t,\max}=\mu_w N,
\]
where \(\mu_w\) is the wet coefficient of friction, \(N\) is normal force in newtons, and \(F_{t,\max}\) is in newtons. The required tangential force during a catch may be approximated by
\[
F_{t,\text{req}}\approx \frac{m\Delta v}{\Delta t_{\text{catch}}},
\]
with \(m\) in kilograms, \(\Delta v\) in meters per second, and \(\Delta t_{\text{catch}}\) in seconds. If
\[
F_{t,\text{req}}>\mu_w N,
\]
the ball slips or is fumbled. Camp’s source condition is severe: “the forward pass is difficult, in fact, almost impossible, when playing on a wet field or while rain is falling. If the ball is slimy and wet, it is almost impossible to control it in the throwing and the catching” [PAGE 104]. Breakdown regime: rain lowers \(\mu_w\) and increases failure probability.

6. Knee-torque injury operator.  
A knee twist injury can be represented by external knee torque
\[
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{knee}}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_{\text{ground}},
\]
where \(\mathbf{r}\) is the moment arm from the knee joint to the foot-ground contact in meters, and \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ground}}\) is ground reaction force in newtons; \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{knee}}\) is in newton-meters. Injury risk rises when torsional or valgus torque exceeds ligament tolerance. Camp gives the clinical rule: “There is a point where home treatment of injuries should cease, and that point comes when the knee is injured” [PAGE 42]. He adds that a knee twist “may or may not displace the semilunar cartilage” and that “the ligaments around the joints are stretched and do not recover their normal elasticity” [PAGE 43]. Breakdown regime: any twist with swelling or effusion vetoes continued participation.

7. Training-load and staleness operator.  
Mechanical work accumulated in practice can be expressed as
\[
W=\int \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,dt,
\]
with units joules. Recovery depends on sleep, nutrition, and rest days. Camp’s qualitative condition is that staleness appears when accumulated load exceeds recovery capacity: “There is an invisible line in conditioning. On this side of it a man is fit—on the other side, even the least bit on the other side, he is stale” [PAGE 184]. Symptoms include sluggishness, loss of pep, easy tiring, scrappiness, listlessness, bad sleep, and worry [PAGE 184][PAGE 185]. Breakdown regime: when staleness appears, practice must be reduced even if a game is near [PAGE 185].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retro-causal genealogy of this text runs from postwar American sport to factory teams, school teams, and the standardization of coaching knowledge. Camp identifies himself as “SECRETARY FOOTBALL RULES COMMITTEE” [PAGE 7], giving the book institutional authority. The preface frames the problem as an organizational one: a factory team near a railroad has ten days of practice and then asks, “What ought a team to do during the second week of the season?” [PAGE 9]. The book’s answer is to become “the pen-and-ink coach of that team” [PAGE 9]. This is important because the manual is designed for teams without expert coaching, so its genealogy is not elite college football alone; it is the diffusion of coaching into industrial and school contexts [PAGE 10].

The first historical cause is the postwar expansion of football. Camp writes that the war gave football “a greater impetus” and that the game was played “by thousands of men in camps, cantonments and stations” [PAGE 16]. He also notes that “The new rules have made the game less strenuous in some respects, and have made it more interesting in others” [PAGE 16]. The result is a demand for instruction: “An enormous demand has arisen for instruction and coaching” [PAGE 17]. The book therefore emerges from a shortage of coaches and a surplus of new teams. Its retro-causal structure is that later competitive success depends on whether early-season teams can self-organize without expert supervision.

The second lineage is the sequence of weekly development. Camp builds the season as a staged protocol. Week one is foundation: diet, cleanliness, sleeping, light practice, ball handling, falling on the ball, and gentle kicking [PAGE 18][PAGE 25][PAGE 31]. Week two is candidate evaluation and position-specific demands: ends, tackles, guards, center, quarterback, backs, catching, captaincy [PAGE 37][PAGE 47][PAGE 50][PAGE 51][PAGE 55]. Week three introduces the first scrimmage and signals [PAGE 57][PAGE 58]. This staged structure is a multi-week trap detector. If a team skips fundamentals and rushes to scrimmage, the source predicts later failure: “Many of the minor injuries of football come in the first days of practice because of eager recklessness” [PAGE 31].

The third lineage is technical: ball security becomes character. Camp treats the fumbler as a systemic risk, not merely an individual error. [PAGE 16] says a poorly taught player “is very apt to be a fumbler,” and [PAGE 29] calls “fumbler” “the worst name that can be applied to anybody on a good team.” The retro-causal link is clear: a later lost game can be traced to an earlier failure to teach holding, squeezing, and falling on the ball [PAGE 30]. The same logic applies to kicking. Camp says a punter’s leg is “like a pitcher’s arm” and warns that early overkicking can “put a good kicker out of business or shorten the possibilities of his kicking for the whole season” [PAGE 32]. Thus a late-season kicking failure may be retro-caused by an early-season strain.

The fourth lineage is tactical evolution, especially the forward pass. Camp treats the forward pass as a newer weapon that changes defensive spacing: “the forward pass is no longer an experiment” [PAGE 97]. He argues that it must be “just as definitely planned as the end run or the play off tackle” [PAGE 98]. This marks a transition from brute-force football to a mixed attack in which running and passing constrain the defense. But the tactical gain depends on earlier mastery: “practice in forward passing should begin early in the season, the earlier the better” [PAGE 97]. The retro-causal trap is that a team which delays passing practice will later lack a weapon against heavier opponents [PAGE 104].

The final genealogy is moral and organizational. Camp links discipline, promptness, and harmony to competitive outcomes. Tardiness is not a small administrative issue: “It is bad for the discipline of the team,” and “When those who report on time have to sit around and wait for late comers, they speedily lose interest in the practice” [PAGE 34]. Quarrels are likewise systemic: “Quarrels among players have wrecked more than one team” [PAGE 60]. Spirit is presented as a decisive but fragile resource: “The team that has spirit can never be called beaten… until the final whistle blows” [PAGE 179]. Yet spirit can be destroyed by cliques, jealousy, and bad leadership [PAGE 182]. The multi-week trap is therefore not only physical overtraining; it is also social erosion. A team can be technically superior and still lose if its internal trust collapses.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The text contains an implicit recursive model of mind: each player must model teammates, opponents, and the evolving state of the game. Camp’s signal system is the clearest example. The offense knows the intended play, but the defense must infer it. The blind number is designed to manipulate the opponent’s belief about when the play starts. In [PAGE 62], the quarterback may call “62, 24, i,” where the final digit indicates whether the snap occurs on the first, second, or third repeated number. The result is described in [PAGE 63]: “Your opponents, if they do not discover your starting signal, will never know just when the play is to start. They will be kept worried and harassed all through the game.” This is a belief-manipulation operator: offense hides the start time, defense must guess, and hesitation creates delay.

The recursive layer deepens under crisis. When a team is losing or confused, players regress to habits. Camp identifies several regression modes. First is blame: after botched plays, “Players sometimes have a habit of blaming one another” [PAGE 74]. Second is temper: “The player who loses his temper is of absolutely no use to his team” [PAGE 84]. Third is lazy feinting: interferers on a blind thrust “put so little life and drive into their effort that experienced football men know at once that their advance is only a feint” [PAGE 143]. Fourth is the “big head” problem, where a star player’s ego fractures harmony [PAGE 112]. Fifth is the substitute habit, where players accept a secondary role and stop fighting for position [PAGE 190]. These are crisis regressions: under stress, the team’s shared model degrades into individual self-protection.

Camp’s counter-trap is to convert crisis into procedure. Walk-throughs are a belief-alignment device. When plays go badly, the correct response is not emotional escalation but slowed rehearsal: “Occasionally, if plays go limping through lack of coordination, it is well to pause, walk through the play, have each man impressed with what he is to do and when he is to do it” [PAGE 126]. Simple signals are another counter-trap because they reduce shared uncertainty [PAGE 58]. Captaincy is a third counter-trap: the captain must be “absolutely fair,” must “get things done,” and must be the person “to whom the team looks in the crisis” [PAGE 55]. After defeat, the captain must make the analysis impersonal: “the whole matter is impersonal” and the team must understand why it lost [PAGE 112].

The clock is the third control variable. There are at least three clocks in Camp’s system. The first is the practice clock. Camp limits early scrimmage: “Scrimmage work for the first few days should be very short, not lasting over four or five minutes” [PAGE 82]. He also limits kicking timing: “Timing, however, should not be done more than twice a week, for too much anxiety about the clock makes the kicker nervous and over-anxious” [PAGE 68]. The second is the developmental clock: the season moves from fundamentals to signals to scrimmage to strategy [PAGE 15][PAGE 57][PAGE 138]. The third is the game clock and down-and-distance clock. Camp’s zone strategy depends on field position, score, and time left [PAGE 171]. He advises that forward passes are generally best on second or third down [PAGE 168], and that in one’s own territory one should usually kick unless wind conditions require running [PAGE 168]. The recursive theory of mind therefore becomes: choose actions not only by current field position, but by what the opponent believes you will do, what your own team can execute under time pressure, and what the clock permits.

A further recursive element is substitution timing. Camp says that a player taken out must trust the higher judgment: “He sees only his own little sector of play; the man who ordered him out sees the whole field and the team as a whole” [PAGE 187]. This is a multi-agent inference problem: the individual’s local model may be wrong even when his subjective feeling is strong. The coach or captain must model fatigue, matchup, field position, and the opponent’s likely adjustment. Camp gives a practical rule: “let well enough alone when leading but… take chances when behind” [PAGE 188]. The recursive structure is not merely psychological; it is temporal. The team must anticipate its future state, not only its present emotion.

In sum, the source describes a loop: signal design shapes opponent belief; practice shapes team execution; fatigue shapes available behavior; captaincy shapes trust; clock pressure shapes risk. Crisis reveals which loops have become habits. If the loops are healthy, the team regresses to coordination. If the loops are damaged, the team regresses to blame, temper, lazy feints, and stale effort. Camp’s final imperative, “Play the game hard, play the game square” [PAGE 193], is therefore not a moral slogan only; it is a constraint on the recursive system. Hard play without square habits produces penalties, injuries, and loss of trust; square play without hard execution produces staleness and defeat.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a lower physical, biomechanical, or weather constraint invalidates a higher tactical or motivational intention. Camp’s text repeatedly establishes such vetoes. The first is the weather veto. In kicking, the deep snap is especially vulnerable. Camp writes that if a punter relies on extra distance behind the line, “if by chance a wet day comes for the game it is impossible to get the ball back this distance” [PAGE 44]. The reason is mechanical: “if the ball is slippery and heavy with wet the feat is impossible” [PAGE 44]. This is not a courage issue. If the center cannot deliver the ball cleanly to a deep punter on a wet field, the tactic is vetoed. The source’s own conclusion is severe: the kicker who has not learned to kick quickly “never can do the punting on a wet day” [PAGE 44].

The second weather veto is the forward pass. Camp states, “the forward pass is difficult, in fact, almost impossible, when playing on a wet field or while rain is falling. If the ball is slimy and wet, it is almost impossible to control it in the throwing and the catching” [PAGE 104]. In formal terms, the wet surface reduces the available friction coefficient. If the required tangential grip exceeds the available wet grip,
\[
F_{t,\text{req}}>\mu_w N,
\]
the pass or catch fails. The tactical layer cannot override this by demanding more focus. The correct adjudication is to shift to running plays, short secure handoffs, or kicking strategies that do not depend on fine aerial control. Camp also notes that a wet ball must be “cocked up more than a dry one” [PAGE 87], confirming that even place-kicking changes its boundary conditions.

The third weather-related veto is field condition. Camp advises quarterbacks to watch for dry spots on a sloppy field and to use them for runs [PAGE 153]. He also says that a field sloping downhill toward a sideline may make a downhill run preferable to a central plunge [PAGE 153]. These are not preferences; they are surface-traction constraints. If footing is unreliable, sharp cuts and sudden stops become hazardous. The veto condition is met when the available coefficient of friction is insufficient for the intended change of direction.

The fourth veto is biomechanical: the knee. Camp says, “An injured knee should be taken to a doctor at once, especially if there is any swelling or effusion” [PAGE 42]. He explains that a knee twist may displace the semilunar cartilage and that “the ligaments around the joints are stretched and do not recover their normal elasticity” [PAGE 43]. In torque terms, if
\[
\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{knee}}\|>\tau_{\text{fail}},
\]
the ligamentous structure is at risk. Continued participation is vetoed because the cost is not a momentary loss of yardage but long-term incapacity. This veto applies even if the player feels able to continue. The source treats the knee as a privileged site where home treatment ends and surgical judgment begins.

The fifth veto is kicking-load biomechanics. Camp warns that “many a good man has lamed himself by punting too hard and too long in the early part of the season” [PAGE 32]. He compares the punter’s leg to a pitcher’s arm and says it “should be worked in gradually” [PAGE 32]. The veto condition is early maximal kicking. If a kicker is forced to boot at maximum effort before tissue adaptation, the risk of strain rises. The tactical desire for immediate distance is overridden by the season-long availability constraint. A kicker who is injured in week one cannot provide field position in week ten.

The sixth veto is practice recklessness. Camp says, “Many of the minor injuries of football come in the first days of practice because of eager recklessness” [PAGE 31]. This is a load-management veto. If players perform “mighty stunts” before muscles are hardened, the injury risk invalidates the intended benefit. The correct adjudication is to reduce intensity, emphasize details, and delay maximal effort until tissue readiness is established.

The adjudication principle is therefore strict: if a physical constraint is violated, the higher-layer plan is not merely suboptimal; it is inadmissible. Camp’s language supports this. He does not say wet-field passing is merely harder; he says it is “almost impossible” [PAGE 104]. He does not say deep snapping in wet weather is risky; he says it is “impossible” [PAGE 44]. These are veto statements. In a formal system, they function as constraints that remove actions from the feasible set. The remaining feasible set consists of plays that preserve possession, protect the body, and respect the surface.

The final cross-layer judgment is that coaching intention must be subordinate to physical truth. Camp’s praise of spirit [PAGE 179] does not override his warnings about staleness [PAGE 184], injury [PAGE 42], or weather [PAGE 104]. Spirit can increase effort within the feasible set, but it cannot change the feasible set itself. A wet ball remains wet; a strained leg remains strained; a twisted knee remains compromised. The source’s deepest engineering lesson is that football strategy is bounded by the body and the environment. Tactical creativity is valuable only inside those bounds.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
For reinforcement-learning-style evaluation with verifiable rewards, define the reward vector as lexicographic:
1. Avoid terminal failure: no turnover, no penalty, no injury.
2. Preserve or improve possession and field position.
3. Execute the intended assignment within the temporal window.
A counterfactual veto is a predicted action that would reduce the verifiable reward below threshold. Camp supplies three especially clear veto scenarios.

Scenario 1: Deep punt formation on a wet field.  
Counterfactual: the team places the punter far behind the line to gain distance, but rain has made the ball slippery. Source basis: “if by chance a wet day comes for the game it is impossible to get the ball back this distance,” and “if the ball is slippery and heavy with wet the feat is impossible” [PAGE 44]. Prediction: the snap will be fumbled, bobbled, or delivered too slowly; the punt will be blocked or result in a turnover. Verifiable test: in wet practice, measure snap success rate from deep formation versus standard formation. If deep snap success collapses, veto the deep formation. The reward loss is not merely lost yards; it is loss of possession and possible defensive score.

Scenario 2: Complex signal package before walk-through mastery.  
Counterfactual: the team introduces a high-entropy signal system with many blind numbers, shifts, and motion keys before players can execute simple plays. Source basis: “The worst signals are those that take so much study or that are so complicated that the players get mixed and are helpless” [PAGE 58]. Camp also requires walk-throughs: “At this stage of the practice the candidates should walk through the plays” [PAGE 64]. Prediction: players will hit the wrong hole, start late, or false-start; interference will be misaligned; the runner will meet a closed wall. Verifiable test: count assignment errors during signal drills. If error rate rises with signal complexity before walk-through, veto the complexity increase. The correct action is to reduce \(H(S)\) and increase repetition until error rate falls.

Scenario 3: Premature cross-field pursuit by a tackle or guard.  
Counterfactual: a lineman sees a fake flow and crosses behind the center before confirming the play side. Source basis: the tackle must not be drawn across “until he is sure” [PAGE 127], and earlier Camp warns that a tackle must not cross “BUT NOT UNTIL HE IS ABSOLUTELY SURE IT IS GOING AROUND THE OTHER SIDE” [PAGE 79]. Prediction: the offense counter-runs into the vacated gap, producing a long gain. Verifiable test: film review or tracking data should show that when a defender crosses before play confirmation, the opponent’s cutback lane widens and yards-per-play increase. If confirmed, veto premature cross-field pursuit. The reward loss is a defensive breakdown: the runner reaches the secondary with an open field.

A fourth possible veto, implied by the source, is captaincy by popularity alone. Camp says, “Too often this office falls to the lot of a player who has popularity and nothing else,” and warns that popularity “will avail nothing once the season starts” [PAGE 55]. Counterfactual prediction: a popular but undisciplined captain will fail to enforce promptness, fairness, or crisis composure; the team will drift. Verifiable test: compare practice tardiness, quarrel frequency, and crisis response under different captains. If the popular captain produces worse discipline metrics, veto the selection. This is a social-system veto, but it is verifiable through observable team behavior.

These counterfactual vetoes share a common structure. Each identifies an action that appears attractive locally: more punt distance, more tactical variety, more aggressive pursuit, or more social harmony through popularity. Each also identifies a source-based failure mode: wet snap impossibility, signal confusion, cutback exposure, or leadership collapse. The RLVR-style evaluation should therefore not reward the appearance of sophistication. It should reward actions that survive the veto tests: dry-field or short-snap kicking when wet, low-entropy signals mastered by walk-through, disciplined pursuit that waits for confirmation, and captain selection based on force of character rather than popularity. Camp’s final advice remains the compact objective function: “Squeeze the ball, hit the line with all your power, and don’t foul” [PAGE 193]. That is a verifiable policy: secure possession, execute with synchronized force, and avoid penalty.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
