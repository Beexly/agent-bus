<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s central derivation is geometric, not statistical in the usual sense. The paper begins from an international soccer-field constraint: FIFA/IFAB allow a length range of 100 m to 110 m and a width range of 64 m to 75 m, producing a possible area difference of 1850 m² [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. The authors ask whether a single FIFA-recommended field template can serve as a generic calibration object. In camera-geometry terms, the world field is a 2D plane embedded in 3D space; the camera projection matrix \(\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times4}\) maps homogeneous world points to homogeneous image points, but when all world points lie on one plane the 3D-to-2D estimation is rank-deficient, so the problem is reduced to a 2D homography \(\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\). The paper explicitly notes that “if all 3D coordinates lie on a 2D plane, the resulting system of equations will lack sufficient linearly independent equations for a valid solution” [PAGE 3]. Thus the operative operator is not \(\mathbf{P}\) but \(\mathbf{H}\), estimated by the direct linear transform (DLT), which “minimises the reprojection error and does not necessarily guarantee an optimal solution” [PAGE 3].

The multi-hop derivation then proceeds through four links. First, a synthetic camera matrix \(P_0\) is used to project the FIFA template and four extreme true fields onto an image plane. Second, DLT estimates \(H_{\text{field}}\) from template world coordinates \(x_{\text{FIFA}}\) to the image coordinates of each extreme field. Third, accuracy is measured by several intersection-over-union (IoU) variants. Fourth, the experiment is expanded to every integer field length/width combination and many camera poses. The crucial gray-zone point is that a single homography can align the outer perimeter while misaligning fixed inner segments—penalty area, goal area, penalty arc, centre circle—because those inner segments do not scale with the outer field dimensions. The paper states that the standard metric \(IoU_{\text{part}}\) ranged from 93.5% to 97.5% with mean 95.3%, while the proposed \(IoU_{\text{seg}}\) ranged only from 82.9% to 85.4% with mean 83.9% [PAGE 7]. That is the audit hinge: the same homography can look excellent under perimeter-area overlap and still be poor for inner-line registration. The extended analysis also found that “some arbitrary camera perspectives limit the accuracy to less than 90%” [PAGE 1].

A deeper audit exposes the assumptions. The model is 2D-to-2D, synthetic, and uses all available template points even when some image points fall outside the visible frame [PAGE 12]. The reported accuracies are therefore not absolute real-broadcast maxima, and the paper itself calls this a limitation [PAGE 12]. The data are “synthetically created” [PAGE 13]. There is no real stadium, no lens distortion model, no rolling-shutter model, no weather degradation, no player occlusion, and no biomechanical model of player/ball motion. Thus the valid inference is narrow: under ideal planar geometry and known synthetic correspondences, a generic template can be fitted with high perimeter IoU but materially lower segment-level IoU. The invalid inference is that a real broadcast system is automatically safe. The paper’s own warning supports the narrower reading: “If the exact camera parameters are provided, but the exact field dimensions are unknown, the operator should not assume that the FIFA-standard template, xFIFA , can be used as the calibration object” [PAGE 12]. The gray zone is therefore a metric-selection and assumption-audit zone, not merely a numerical-error zone.

1. Perspective projection and homogeneous coordinates. The paper writes
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}' = \mathbf{P}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}, \qquad 
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times4},
\]
where \(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\) is a homogeneous world coordinate and \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}'\) is a homogeneous image coordinate [PAGE 1][PAGE 3]. Homogeneous image coordinates are written as \((ku,kv,k)\), \(k\in\mathbb{R}\) [PAGE 3]. The paper explains that \(\mathbf{P}\) has rank three and that omitting the depth column yields a \(3\times3\) homography:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}' = \mathbf{H}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad 
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3},
\]
where \(\mathbf{x}\) is a 2D homogeneous world coordinate [PAGE 3]. The paper’s notation also includes
\[
x'_{\text{field}} = H_{\text{field}}x_{\text{FIFA}}, 
\qquad \text{field}\in\{\text{Min},\text{Max},\text{MinMax},\text{MaxMin}\},
\]
as the mapping from the FIFA template to each extreme field configuration [PAGE 5].

2. DLT estimation. For point correspondences \(\mathbf{x}_i\leftrightarrow \mathbf{x}'_i\), the DLT forms a linear system in the entries of \(\mathbf{H}\). A standard homogeneous form is
\[
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}=0,\qquad 
\mathbf{h}=\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{H}),\qquad
\mathbf{h}=\arg\min_{\|\mathbf{h}\|=1}\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{h}\|_2^2,
\]
solved by singular value decomposition; the paper states that “singular value decomposition may then be used to determine the solution with the least squared reprojection error” [PAGE 3]. For a unique \(\mathbf{H}\) up to scale, at least four point correspondences in general position are required. Breakdown regimes: fewer than four correspondences; three or more collinear correspondences; repeated points; all visible points lying on one line; or a planar 3D configuration used to estimate \(\mathbf{P}\) rather than \(\mathbf{H}\). The paper explicitly identifies the planar \(\mathbf{P}\)-estimation degeneracy [PAGE 3].

3. IoU family. The generic area overlap is
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\in[0,1],
\]
dimensionless. The source distinguishes:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}
=
\mathrm{IoU}\big(\mathcal{V}_{\text{template}},\mathcal{V}_{\text{field}}\big)
\]
for the visible bird’s-eye view;
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{mask}}
=
\mathrm{IoU}\big(\mathcal{M}_{\text{template}},\mathcal{M}_{\text{field}}\big)
\]
for the visible camera-perspective outer-field mask; and
\[
\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{vis}}|}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{vis}}}
\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{mask}}(s),
\]
where \(\mathcal{S}_{\text{vis}}\) is the set of visible field segments. The paper defines the final metric as one “where the IoUmask for each visible field segment is computed individually and then averaged across all visible segments. This variation is referred to as the segmented IoU, or IoUseg” [PAGE 5]. The image planes are standardised to \(720\,\text{px}\times1280\,\text{px}\) [PAGE 5]. Line-based IoU is additionally parameterised by predicted line thickness \(t\in\{5,10,20,30,40\}\,\text{px}\), while true lines are 5 px thick [PAGE 5]; the paper notes that a 40 px line equals “5.6% of the overall image plane width” and is not a realistic field-line representation [PAGE 6].

4. Extreme fields and expanded search. The four extreme fields are \([100,64]\), \([110,75]\), \([100,75]\), and \([110,64]\) in metres [PAGE 4]. The expanded experiment uses “132 distinct combinations of field lengths and widths” and 100 randomly sampled camera-parameter sets over 10 pan values, giving “a total of 132,000 data points” [PAGE 6]. The candidate minimum expected calibration error can be formalised as
\[
\mathrm{MECE}(d)
=
\inf_{\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{adm}}}
\mathbb{E}_{c\sim p(c)}
\left[
1-\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}(\mathbf{H};c,d)
\right],
\]
where \(d\) is the field-dimension discrepancy and \(c\) is the camera-parameter vector; this is a conceptual formalisation, not a numerical equation supplied by the paper. The paper concludes that predicting MECE is redundant when both camera parameters and field discrepancy are known, because IoU can then be calculated directly [PAGE 10]. Its breakdown regime is exactly that: known camera parameters plus unknown or mismatched field dimensions invalidate the generic-template assumption [PAGE 12].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is traceable. Homayounfar et al. introduced a public benchmark for soccer camera calibration in 2017, and Cioppa et al. released SoccerNetV3 in 2022; both used templates of the same dimensions despite regulatory field-size variance [PAGE 2][PAGE 13]. Sharma et al. introduced \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}\) in 2018, and Chen and Little’s 2019 synthetic-data calibration work popularised parallel use of \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{whole}}\) and \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}\) [PAGE 3][PAGE 13]. Breytenbach and Grobler’s 2024 study was, according to the paper, “the first known study to validate a generic calibration object” [PAGE 2]. The present 2025 paper is therefore retrocausally shaped by an earlier deployment assumption: because broadcast analytics needed scalable, real-time calibration, the field template was made generic; only later did segment-level validation reveal that outer-perimeter IoU can hide inner-segment error.

The multi-week traps follow from that lineage. First, a template can be adopted once and then reused across many matches; if the true field is near an extreme, the error is not a one-frame glitch but a persistent bias in player tracking, augmented reality, and virtual refereeing. Second, \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}\) gives a comfortable high number, so weekly quality reports may never trigger review; the paper shows that “the mean IoUseg value does not fall within the third quartile of IoUpart” [PAGE 10]. Third, camera parameters and field dimensions can drift independently across fixtures and broadcast setups, so a calibration validated in one week may not transfer to the next. Fourth, the expanded experiment’s non-linear relationship between field discrepancy and accuracy means a simple linear correction or one-off calibration offset is not reliable across all camera poses [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. Fifth, the GUI and public repository are a mitigation, but only if operators actually check the true field dimensions and visible segments rather than defaulting to the template [PAGE 10][PAGE 12]. The retrocausal lesson is that the original scalability claim was true only under an implicit metric and geometry; once segment-level requirements enter, the old template-first habit becomes a hidden debt.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Consider three agents. Agent A is the live broadcast operator under clock pressure: the match is running, the calibration must be produced now, and the habitual action is to load the FIFA template. Agent B is the analyst who reads \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}\approx95\%\) and concludes that the calibration is safe. Agent C is the modeler who knows that inner segments are fixed while outer dimensions vary and who therefore asks for \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}\) and true field dimensions.

Crisis habit regression appears in Agent A: under time pressure, the generic template becomes the default because it is available and historically accepted. The paper’s warning directly counters that habit: when camera parameters are known but field dimensions are unknown, “the operator should not assume that the FIFA-standard template, xFIFA , can be used as the calibration object” [PAGE 12]. The counter-trap appears in Agent B: after learning that \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}\) can be misleading, the analyst may overcorrect and reject every generic-template calibration, even when the true field is close to the template and the paper says “popular soccer fields, however, tend to be designed similar to the recommended designs of FIFA” [PAGE 12]. The clock appears at the system level: real-time broadcast cannot wait for a full field survey, so the correct move is not blind trust or blanket rejection but a precomputed or quick check of visible segments and field dimensions.

Recursive theory of mind matters because each agent models the other’s model. Agent A assumes Agent B’s metric will certify the feed; Agent B assumes Agent A used the correct field; Agent C knows both assumptions can be false. A second-order trap is that Agent B may treat a high perimeter IoU as evidence about inner lines, even though the paper explicitly questions that assumption: standard methods “assume that the inner field markings, such as penalty or goal areas, will align correctly if the outer perimeter of the fields align” [PAGE 3]. A third-order counter-trap is to over-weight \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}\) without checking visibility: when few field lines are visible, the metric’s lower whisker drops sharply while \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}\) remains relatively high [PAGE 10]. The stable policy is clock-aware: use the GUI or a precomputed lookup when possible, require \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}\) for inner-segment-critical tasks, and escalate to field-dimension estimation when the camera parameters are known but the field is not.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The source’s geometry is valid only for a planar field and a perspective camera model. The homography reduction assumes the world points lie on a 2D plane [PAGE 3]. If the field is materially non-planar, if the camera has strong lens distortion, or if the visible points are insufficiently non-collinear, the DLT homography is not identifiable or is biased. The paper’s own limitation—estimating \(\mathbf{H}\) from all template points even when some image points are outside the visible frame—means visible-plane accuracy is not automatically the absolute maximum [PAGE 12]. Physical veto condition: reject the calibration when the visible correspondences cannot support a stable \(\mathbf{H}\), or when the camera model is not projective-homographic.

Biomechanical veto. The paper does not model players, ball trajectories, human occlusion, or surface interaction. It is a static field-geometry experiment with synthetic data [PAGE 13]. Therefore no biomechanical claim can be validated from this source. If player occlusion hides the penalty area or goal area, the segment-level IoU cannot be computed as though the segment were visible. Veto condition: reject inner-segment claims when the relevant segment is occluded or when player/ball motion changes the visible line set.

Weather veto. The paper does not model rain, fog, shadow, glare, snow, or line-contrast degradation. It uses synthetic projections and hardcoded field segments [PAGE 12][PAGE 13]. Weather can destroy line detection even when the homography is mathematically valid. Veto condition: reject any real-broadcast calibration claim that depends on line visibility under adverse weather unless an external detector-robustness study is supplied. The cross-layer proof is therefore asymmetric: the source proves a synthetic geometric result, not a real-world all-weather or player-loaded result. The correct adjudication is to accept the geometric and metric findings and veto any extension to biomechanical or weather layers that the paper did not test.

Scenario 1 — extreme maximum field with arbitrary camera. Counterfactual: keep the FIFA template but set the true field to the maximum extreme configuration and use the paper’s arbitrary camera. Verifiable prediction from Table 1: for \(H_{\text{Max}}\), \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}=97.47\%\) but \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}=83.20\%\) [PAGE 7]. RLVR veto: if the downstream task requires inner-segment alignment at a threshold above 90%, the generic template must be vetoed even though the standard metric exceeds 97%. The verifier can recompute both metrics from the projected template and true field.

Scenario 2 — known camera, unknown field, mixed extreme. Counterfactual: use \(H_{\text{MinMax}}\) with the true field at 100 m by 75 m while the camera parameters are known. Prediction: \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{part}}=93.68\%\) but \(\mathrm{IoU}_{\text{seg}}=82.90\%\) [PAGE 7]. The segment audit predicts that the outer segment can reach 90.7% while the goal area falls to 70.4% [PAGE 7]. RLVR veto: reject the template-first calibration when goal-area or penalty-area registration is safety-critical. The verifier checks the per-segment IoU values, not only the outer mask.

Scenario 3 — metric-artifact veto from line thickness. Counterfactual: use the \(H_{\text{MaxMin}}\) homography and the 40 px line-based metric. Prediction: line-based IoU reaches 99.75%, but at 5 px the same homography yields 31.38% [PAGE 7]. Because the paper states that 40 px is 5.6% of image width and “not a realistic representation of a line on a field” [PAGE 6], the RLVR veto is: do not reward a calibration policy for high line-based IoU when the line thickness is implausibly large. The verifier recomputes IoU at realistic line thickness and rejects the policy if the apparent gain disappears.
