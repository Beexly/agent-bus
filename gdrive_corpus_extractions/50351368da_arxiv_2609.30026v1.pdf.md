The paper derives a training-free hold-usage detector by chaining three non-obvious design decisions that each address a specific failure mode of prior wrist/ankle pose pipelines. First, the localization problem: COCO-style skeletons place hand keypoints at the wrist and foot keypoints at the ankle, which are physically offset from the actual contact points (fingertips and big-toe tips). Prior work compensates by inflating a region of interest (ROI) around the wrist or ankle, but on densely packed routes this inflated box overlaps multiple holds simultaneously, collapsing precision. The authors replace this with Sapiens-1B, a frozen foundation pose model predicting 308 whole-body keypoints, from which they retain only the true contact keypoints. Because the contact point *is* the keypoint, no inflated ROI is needed, and per-limb mutual exclusion assigns each limb to exactly its highest-scoring hold.

Second, the occlusion problem: hands are occluded in roughly half of all annotated frames, degrading wrist signals. The proposed fix is not an explicit occlusion model but a four-stage temporal aggregation pass applied to the per-frame proximity score sequence. The stages are (1) moving-average smoothing to suppress pose jitter spikes, (2) hysteresis with separate enter (0.4) and exit (0.15) thresholds to prevent boundary toggling, (3) gap bridging to merge intervals separated by few frames so brief occlusions do not split a grasp, and (4) dwell filtering at 20 frames to discard transient contacts. This linear-time pass preserves recall through occluded spans without semantic reasoning about *why* the limb disappeared.

Third, a counterintuitive minimalism result: two intuitively richer cues—dense foundation-feature change gating against an empty-wall clean plate and body-part segmentation from Sapiens—both degrade performance. The ablation shows body-part segmentation alone scores F1 1.1% because, at the whole-image scale Sapiens requires, hands and shoes are too small to segment reliably. The dense feature gate lowers F1 from 90.2% to 80.4% because it suppresses genuine contacts during which visual change is subtle. The gray-zone audit therefore localizes the dominant uncertainty to keypoint localization under occlusion and small scale, not to appearance or semantics.

Score = 1 inside the hold box, decaying linearly to 0 at distance \(\tau\). [PAGE 2]

Temporal Intersection over Union (Eq. 2, [PAGE 3]):
\[
\mathrm{tIoU} = \frac{|T_p \cap T_g|}{|T_p \cup T_g|},
\]

The current work adopts that dataset and metric but replaces the skeleton with Sapiens (ECCV 2024, [5]), a foundation pose model predicting 308 keypoints including fingers and toes absent from COCO-style skeletons. The climber localization uses a promptable segmenter (Segment Anything, ICCV 2023 [6]; SAM 2, ICLR 2025 [7]) with a person prompt, keeping the largest mask’s bounding box. Competing baselines are YOLOv8-pose ([17], 2023) and ViTPose-L (NeurIPS 2022, [16]), both wrist/ankle-anchored. The dense feature-change gate experiment was motivated by DINOv2-style self-supervised features (TMLR 2024, [19]) and clean-plate background subtraction, but the ablation shows it suppresses true contacts. Evaluation borrows temporal IoU from ActivityNet (CVPR 2015, [21]).

Multi-week traps. A multi-week deployment would expose several latent failure modes not visible in the held-out split. First, hyperparameter drift: τ = 20 px and dwell = 20 frames were tuned on p1; a new gym with different camera distance, wall angle, or hold size would require re-tuning, and the paper explicitly limits claims to a single vertical wall and viewpoint ([PAGE 6]). Second, temporal threshold inflation: performance degrades gracefully but measurably as tIoU tightens (F1 79.9% → 77.5% → 72.3% overall; hand F1 72.7% → 68.7% → 61.8%), so a scoring system demanding precise start/end alignment would see hand detection deteriorate faster than foot detection ([PAGE 4]). Third, occlusion stratification was previously highlighted as hardest but never measured ([PAGE 1]); this paper measures it (81.0% occluded recall) and finds occlusion—mostly hands—remains the hardest regime ([PAGE 5]). Fourth, published baseline numbers (ViTPose 86.6%) are not reproducible under same conditions because per-model margin tuning was not replicated ([PAGE 3], [PAGE 4]); any literature comparison that cites published numbers without same-protocol reproduction is confounded. Finally, the minimalism result is dataset-specific: appearance cues hurt here because keypoint localization is the dominant bottleneck, but in environments with severe motion blur or low resolution, appearance semantics might become necessary, meaning the “richer cues hurt” conclusion is not universal.
