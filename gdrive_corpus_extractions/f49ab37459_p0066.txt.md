$$ P(D | A, C) = \frac{P(C | D, A) \cdot P(D | A)}{P(C | A)} $$

$$ P(D | A, C) \propto P(C | D) \cdot P(D | A) $$
Here, $P(D | A)$ represents the **antecedent function**, mapping privation time to expected drive. $P(C | D)$ represents the **consequent function**, mapping drive intensity to expected energy expenditure.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The concept of the "intervening variable" did not emerge in a vacuum; it is the culmination of a centuries-long struggle to reconcile mechanistic physics with phenomenal experience. Its genealogy traces back to the rejection of vitalism and the subsequent need for psychological realism within a materialist framework.

**1. The Pre-Hull Lineage: From Ghosts to Variables**
In the 19th century, physiological psychology (e.g., Wundt, James) struggled with "mental states." They were treated as epiphenomena or vague qualia. Behaviorism, led by John B. Watson, attempted a radical surgery: excising the mental entirely. Watson’s 1913 manifesto demanded psychology be a purely objective branch of natural science, dealing only with stimulus (S) and response (R). However, the S-R model proved insufficient. It could not account for *latent learning* (Tolman), *frustration* (Dollard & Miller), or the obvious fact that the same stimulus elicits different responses based on internal state (e.g., a rat presses a lever harder if hungry).

**2. Hull’s Intervention: The Algebraic Bridge**
Clark L. Hull introduced the intervening variable in the 1930s and solidified it in *Principles of Behavior* (1943). His genius was adopting the mathematical syntax of physics. Just as temperature ($T$) is an intervening variable in thermodynamics—defined by the ideal gas law $PV=nRT$ but not directly observed as a single particle's property—Hull proposed Drive ($D$). This allowed behaviorists to retain objectivity while acknowledging internal complexity. It was a "retrocausal" move in the sense that it looked backward to explain why simple S-R bonds failed, yet projected forward toward cognitive maps and information processing.

**3. The Multi-Week Trap: The "Black Box" Fallacy**
The historical trap associated with intervening variables is the **"Black Box Fallacy."** Researchers often mistake the mathematical convenience of a variable for ontological reality. By defining $D$ solely through its correlations with $A$ and $C$, there is a risk of creating tautological explanations ("The rat ran fast because of high drive; we know drive was high because it ran fast"). This trap leads to circular reasoning if the "double determination" is not rigorously constrained by independent measures. For decades, psychologists fought over whether "drive" was a real physiological tension or merely a statistical artifact of varying reinforcement histories. This lineage shows that Hull's "X" was not just a placeholder but a battleground for the soul of scientific psychology.
