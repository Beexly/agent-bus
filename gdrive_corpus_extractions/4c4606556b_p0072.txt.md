<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of Knight's framework is a story of reaction and synthesis. Its direct ancestor is the **Marginalist Revolution** (Jevons, Menger, Walras) of the 1870s, which shifted economics from cost-of-production theories to subjective value and marginal analysis. This provided the tools to define "specific contribution" mathematically. However, the pre-marginalist **Classical Political Economy** (Smith, Ricardo, Marx) had already grappled with the distribution of output among capital, labor, and land—what Knight calls the "apportionment... of existing stocks."

The pivotal retrocausal link is **Alfred Marshall's synthesis** in *Principles of Economics* (1890). Marshall introduced the concepts of supply and demand as interacting forces determining price, and the notion of "representative firm" and "quasi-rent," which implicitly acknowledged time and adjustment processes. Knight’s two-stage model is a more explicit temporal and organizational unpacking of Marshall’s partial equilibrium analysis.

**Multi-Week Traps and Historical Context:** The "week-long trap" referenced in the prompt is metaphorical. In the context of economic history, it refers to periods where the neoclassical model, taken literally, led to policy failures because it ignored the very uncertainties and institutional rigidities Knight was highlighting. The **Great Depression (1929-1939)** served as a massive historical refutation of the idea that free competitive relations automatically and efficiently impute correct rewards. Widespread unemployment meant labor's "reward" was zero, despite its clear productive contribution. This crisis validated Keynes's insights and later inspired **Austrian School** critiques (Hayek, Mises) that emphasized the *process* of discovery and the *knowledge problem*—echoing Knight's focus on uncertainty and entrepreneurship. The modern **Behavioral Economics** critique (Kahneman, Tversky) adds another layer: the assumption that agents correctly interpret prices and make consistent choices based on them is psychologically flawed, especially under ambiguity (related to Knightian uncertainty). Thus, Knight's 1921 text sits at a nexus, both culminating classical/marginalist thought and planting the seeds for its most powerful 20th-century critiques.
