<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s neural operator is the artificial neuron. The source states, “The specific expression of neurons is shown in Equation (1)” [PAGE 4]:
\[
\sigma_{w,b}(X)=\sigma\!\left(W^{\top}x\right)
=\sigma\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n} w_i x_i+b\right).
\]
Here \(x_i\) is the \(i\)-th input feature, \(w_i\) is its weight, \(b\) is the bias or offset, and \(\sigma(\cdot)\) is a nonlinear activation. The paper does not assign SI units to these quantities. Operationally, if \(x_i\) is pixel intensity, its unit is digital counts; if \(x_i\) is an optical-flow component, its unit is pixels per frame. Weights then carry reciprocal units if the preactivation is to be dimensionless; the paper does not report this normalization. The bias carries the same unit as the weighted sum. The output of a saturated activation is dimensionless.

The activation functions are given as Equations (2)–(5) [PAGE 4]–[PAGE 5]:
\[
\tanh(x)=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}},\qquad
\operatorname{sgn}(x)=
\begin{cases}
1, & x\ge 0,\\
0, & x<0,
\end{cases}
\]
\[
\operatorname{sigmoid}(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}},\qquad
\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x).
\]
The source notes that the sigmoid’s output range is between 0 and 1, and that “When the input value of the S-shaped growth curve function has an extreme value, the gradient of the function approaches 0, which will affect the back propagation, so it is not suitable for DL” [PAGE 5]. In formal terms, \(\frac{d}{dx}\operatorname{sigmoid}(x)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(x)(1-\operatorname{sigmoid}(x))\to 0\) as \(x\to\pm\infty\). The hyperbolic tangent has derivative \(1-\tanh^2(x)\), also vanishing under saturation. The step function is discontinuous at \(x=0\) and has zero derivative almost everywhere, so it is not useful for gradient-based training. The ReLU is non-differentiable exactly at \(x=0\) and has zero derivative for \(x<0\), producing “dead-unit” regimes. The paper states that ReLU “has the characteristics of fast derivative convergence, so it is widely used to study the problem of gradient disappearance” [PAGE 5].

The feedforward network is expressed through layer activations, Equations (6)–(11) [PAGE 5]:
\[
a^{(2)}_1=f\!\left(H^{(1)}_{11}m_1+H^{(1)}_{12}m_2+H^{(1)}_{13}m_3+c^{(1)}_1\right),
\]
with analogous expressions for \(a^{(2)}_2\) and \(a^{(2)}_3\), and
\[
q_{H,c}(m)=a^{(3)}_1=f\!\left(H^{(2)}_{11}a^{(2)}_1+H^{(2)}_{12}a^{(2)}_2+H^{(2)}_{13}a^{(2)}_3+c^{(2)}_1\right).
\]
The compact forms are
\[
v^{l}=H^{(l-1)}f\!\left(v^{(1-l)}\right)+c^{(l-1)},\qquad
a^{l}=f\!\left(v^{(l)}\right),
\]
where \(m\) is input, \(H\) is a weight parameter, \(c\) is bias, \(q\) is the final hidden-layer output, and \(f\) is the activation function [PAGE 5]. The squared-error loss is
\[
P(H,c;m,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|q_{H,c}(m)-y\right\|^2,
\]
and the backpropagated residual is
\[
\beta^{(l)}_i=
\left(\sum_{j=0}^{s_{l+1}}H^{(l)}_{ji}\beta^{(l+1)}_i\right)
f'\!\left(v^{(l)}_i\right),
\]
where \(s\) is the number of nodes in all layers [PAGE 5]. Parameter updates are
\[
H^{(l)}_{ji}\leftarrow H^{(l)}_{ji}
-\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial H^{(l)}_{ji}}P(H,c),\qquad
c^{(l)}_j\leftarrow c^{(l)}_j
-\alpha\frac{\partial}{\partial c^{(l)}_j}P(H,c),
\]
with learning rate \(\alpha\) [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes are explicit: if \(\alpha\) is too large, the iteration can diverge; if \(\alpha\) is too small, convergence is slow; if the product of layer derivatives is small, gradients vanish; if activations saturate, learning stalls; if ReLU units receive negative preactivations indefinitely, they die.

For video action recognition, the paper uses a dual-stream CNN and a time-segmented variant. The source states: “The time-segmented CNN is used to segment the entire video and sparsely sample short segments from it as the network input” [PAGE 11]. The spatial stream takes RGB frames and the temporal stream takes stacked optical-flow images. The optical-flow equation is given as Eq. (18):
\[
D^{\top}D\,\vec w=D^{\top}(-c),
\]
where \(T\) is time, \(c\) is a constant term, \(D\) is a coefficient matrix, and \(\vec w=dy/dt\), with \(y\) the ordinate position [PAGE 8]. The Lucas–Kanade solution appears as Eq. (19) [PAGE 8]:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}u\\ w\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sum_i F_x(X_i)^2 & \sum_i F_x(X_i)F_y(X_i)\\
\sum_i F_x(X_i)F_y(X_i) & \sum_i F_y(X_i)^2
\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}
-\sum_i F_x(X_i)F_t(X_i)\\
-\sum_i F_y(X_i)F_t(X_i)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
with \(u=dx/dt\), \(F\) brightness, \(x\) abscissa, and \(y\) ordinate [PAGE 9]. If \(x,y\) are in pixels and \(t\) is in frames, then \(u,w\) have units of pixels per frame; \(F_x,F_y\) have units of intensity counts per pixel; \(F_t\) has units of intensity counts per frame. The structure matrix is singular when the image patch has only one gradient direction, which is the aperture problem, or when texture is absent. The brightness-constancy assumption also fails at occlusions, specular highlights, motion blur, and illumination changes. The paper itself notes that the Hörm–Schunck smoothness assumption “may have a large error in the edge area of the image or the presence of occluded areas” [PAGE 8].

For field-line detection, the improved model increases convolutional layers “from 15 to 34” and constructs “4 residual blocks” [PAGE 15]. A residual block uses a skip connection so that input features are added to output features; the source states that “a convolutional layer with a stride of 2 is used to replace the pooling layer for downsampling” [PAGE 15]. The evaluation metrics are average pixel precision \(P\) and mean intersection over union \(Q\), given as Equations (21) and (22) [PAGE 16]:
\[
P=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}
\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{ii}}{\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{ij}},
\]
\[
Q=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^{k}
\frac{P_{ii}}
{\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{ij}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{ji}-P_{ii}},
\]
where \(k+1\) is the number of classes including background, \(P_{ii}\) is correctly predicted, \(P_{ij}\) is class \(i\) predicted as class \(j\), and \(P_{ji}\) is class \(j\) predicted as class \(i\) [PAGE 16]. These quantities are dimensionless fractions and are reported in percent. The MIoU denominator is zero when a class has neither ground-truth nor predicted pixels, so \(Q\) is undefined for empty classes; pixel precision can be dominated by the background class, so it can rise even when thin field lines are poorly segmented. Equation (21) as printed also has a repeated \(P_{ii}\) summation that is likely a notational artifact; any replication must audit whether the intended numerator is \(P_{ii}\) or \(\sum_j P_{ii}\), because the literal form changes the metric.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The relevant recursive model has five actors: the athlete, the coach, the camera/classifier, the scoring system, and the evaluator. Each represents the others’ intentions under incomplete information. The athlete infers what the coach rewards; the coach infers whether the athlete’s movement is technically correct; the classifier infers the action label from RGB and optical flow; the scoring system infers fouls and deductions; the evaluator infers whether the reported percentages transfer to real performance. The paper’s scoring design sharpens the recursion: “the scoring system mainly deducts in accordance with the foul action of the players” [PAGE 11]. That creates a counter-trap. If athletes learn which movements are detected as fouls or which actions produce high recognition scores, they can optimize for the camera-visible, classifier-legible habit rather than for match-effective skill. The system then measures its own incentive effect and mistakes compliance for competence.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under fatigue, high opposition, or occlusion, an athlete reverts to a previously grooved motor habit. The paper’s own example is the starting action: it has the lowest reported recognition accuracy, 89.8%, and the authors explain that “when the 10-person group set up starts, the overlap rate of the athletes in the video is relatively high, so the system identification is more difficult” [PAGE 17]. In that regime the classifier’s error and the athlete’s habitual movement are confounded. A coach who sees a low score may correct the athlete, but the low score may be an occlusion artifact rather than a motor deficit. The counter-trap is to train the athlete to avoid overlap, which may reduce classifier error while degrading competitive starting behavior. The clock is the mismatch between the one-week measurement window [PAGE 19]–[PAGE 20] and the slower adaptation of jumping strength, which the paper admits is delayed [PAGE 20]. The recursive error is that the coach believes the system measures strength, the athlete believes the system measures technique, and the evaluator believes both are measuring match performance. A defensible interpretation is narrower: the system measures action-class recognizability under the recorded camera and lighting protocol, not latent force or game transfer.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is decisive for any claim that the system measures strength in mechanical units. The input is monocular RGB video, with the network input set to \(224\times224\) [PAGE 10] and the dataset at \(320\times240\), 25 frames per second [PAGE 13]. No force plate, joint-torque sensor, electromyography, or marker-based kinematics is reported. The training exercises are specific: “In groups of three, tie sandbags on your legs to fight for the top ball” for jumping power; “After finishing the forward roll, jumping the low hurdle and drilling the high hurdle, sprint and shoot” for kicking strength; “In groups of 10 people, after hearing the whistle, they began to sprint 100 meters” for starting force; and a two-person solid-ball return for leg strength [PAGE 13]. These are real biomechanical tasks, but the paper’s output is a video-recognition score. A video classifier cannot, by itself, recover ground reaction force in newtons, joint angular velocity in radians per second, or muscle activation. The physical veto is therefore: the paper supports action-label recognition and relative scoring within its protocol; it does not support newton-level strength claims, injury-risk claims, or neuromuscular adaptation claims. The biomechanical veto applies with special force to the one-week protocol: jumping adaptation is stated to require a long time [PAGE 20], and the reported 25.7% maximum upgrade [PAGE 17] is an immediate score change, not a durable force adaptation.

The weather-layer veto is also unresolved. The paper tests “three lighting conditions … lighting, sufficient natural light, and insufficient natural light” [PAGE 16], and reports that under insufficient natural light the improved model reaches 93.7% pixel accuracy while the color-segmentation model is 45% [PAGE 20]. That is evidence about illumination, not about rain, snow, fog, mud, wet turf, wind-blown debris, or changing shadows. The natural-light notice [PAGE 4] makes illumination a first-order concern, but it does not cover precipitation or surface water. The robot branch also depends on line contrast and camera exposure, so a wet or muddy field can defeat the residual FCN even if the indoor lighting test passes. The cross-layer veto verdict is: extrapolation to all outdoor weather is not supported; the paper supports a narrow natural-light robustness claim and a broad “color segmentation is fragile under lighting change” claim, not an all-weather field-line guarantee. The same veto applies to using the system as a general football-robot localizer, because the authors explicitly did not study positioning [PAGE 20].
