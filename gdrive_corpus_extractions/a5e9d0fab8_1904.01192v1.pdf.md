The next hop is numerical. The paper states that the maximum stable step is “inversely proportional to the square root of Young’s modulus divided by the mass density [40]” [PAGE 11]. Brain tissue stiffness is described as about eight orders of magnitude lower than steel [PAGE 11], so the stable step is comparatively long. That inference depends on element size, wave speed, mass scaling, and contact stiffness; it is not a universal permission to use large steps. The Total Lagrangian formulation then precomputes derivatives relative to the undeformed configuration [PAGE 10], which is why the same architecture supports both finite element TLED and meshless MTLED.

A third hop is material-model selection. The paper says that when loading is imposed as boundary motion, the displacement field “depends very weakly on the mechanical properties of the continuum [23, 24, 26]” [PAGE 8]. That justifies a Neo-Hookean model for displacement-only registration [PAGE 9]. It does not justify using Neo-Hookean stresses for tool reaction forces. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source does not print a complete display-equation set for the governing PDEs. It states the class of equations and the numerical operators. The following formalizations are therefore standard continuum-mechanics expressions for the operators named in the source; direct source claims are quoted with page markers. Units are SI unless noted.

1. Finite-deformation Lagrangian balance. The source states: “We consider the brain deformation is described by the equations of continuum mechanics (balance of mass, linear momentum, angular momentum and energy), written in a Lagrangian framework” [PAGE 9]. A minimal formal statement is
\[
\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\nabla_0\mathbf u,\qquad J=\det\mathbf F,\qquad
\rho_0\ddot{\mathbf u}=\nabla_0\cdot\mathbf P+\rho_0\mathbf b,\qquad
\mathbf P=\frac{\partial W}{\partial \mathbf F}.
\]
Here \(\mathbf u\) is displacement in m, \(\mathbf F\) is dimensionless, \(J\) is dimensionless, \(\rho_0\) is initial density in kg m\(^{-3}\), \(\mathbf P\) is first Piola-Kirchhoff stress in Pa, \(\mathbf b\) is body force per mass in N kg\(^{-1}\), and \(W\) is strain-energy density in J m\(^{-3}\) = Pa. Assumptions: Lagrangian reference configuration, sufficient smoothness, and a constitutive law \(W\). Breakdown regimes: \(J\le 0\) indicates element inversion or nonphysical compression; exact incompressibility \(J=1\) makes the pressure indeterminate unless a mixed or penalty formulation is used; contact and impact make the boundary term non-smooth; explicit stepping requires the stability condition below.

2. Neo-Hookean model. The source says “we use a Neo-Hookean material model [13, 36-38], the simplest model suitable for modeling large deformation behavior” [PAGE 9]. A compressible form is
\[
W_{\mathrm{NH}}=\frac{\mu}{2}(I_1-3)-\mu\ln J+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\ln J)^2,
\qquad I_1=\operatorname{tr}\mathbf C,
\]
with \(\mu,\lambda\) in Pa and \(I_1\) dimensionless. For incompressible tissue the volumetric terms are replaced by a constraint and a pressure multiplier. Assumptions: isotropy, moderate strain-rate insensitivity, and parameters not necessarily patient-specific. Breakdown regimes: very high strain rate, strong anisotropy at small sample scales, near-incompressibility with low-order tetrahedra causing volumetric locking, and reaction-force prediction without a more realistic model. The source notes that “The commonly applied material models assume the brain tissue to be incompressible and isotropic” [PAGE 9], but also reports small-scale anisotropic measurements and defends isotropy at about 1 cm scale [PAGE 9].

3. Ogden hyperviscoelastic model. The source recommends “an Ogden-based hyperviscoelastic constitutive model, capable of capturing the highly nonlinear behavior of the brain tissue [39]” [PAGE 9]. A hyperelastic Ogden form is
\[
W_{\mathrm{Og}}=
\sum_{p=1}^{N}\frac{2\mu_p}{\alpha_p^2}
\left(\lambda_1^{\alpha_p}+\lambda_2^{\alpha_p}+\lambda_3^{\alpha_p}-3\right),
\]
with \(\mu_p\) in Pa, \(\alpha_p\) dimensionless, and principal stretches \(\lambda_i\) dimensionless. Hyperviscoelasticity adds internal variables and dissipation. Assumptions: parameters must be identified from appropriate tests; the model is intended especially near the tool where reaction forces matter. Breakdown regimes: non-identifiability from displacement-only data, extrapolation outside tested strain and strain rate, and use of Ogden stresses without resolving the high-gradient contact zone. The source’s recommended workflow is displacement with Neo-Hookean, then resubstitution of deformation gradients onto Ogden near the tool [PAGE 9].

4. Dirichlet weak-dependence operator. For prescribed boundary displacement \(\mathbf u|_{\Gamma_D}=\mathbf g\), write
\[
\mathbf u=\mathcal A(\mathbf C;\mathbf g),
\]
where \(\mathbf C\) is the constitutive law. The source claims that “When loading is prescribed as imposed motion on the boundary, the computed deformation field within the domain depends very weakly on the mechanical properties of the continuum [23, 24, 26]” [PAGE 8]. A formal sensitivity statement is that the Fréchet derivative \(D_{\mathbf C}\mathcal A\) is small in the relevant displacement norm, but the source gives no numerical bound. Assumptions: Dirichlet-dominated loading, sufficient boundary coverage, and a stable discretization. Breakdown regimes: traction or Neumann loading, contact-dominated motion, severe volumetric locking, nearly unconstrained rigid modes, and reaction-force or stress prediction. The source itself separates “Reliable computation of displacements” from “Reliable computation of reaction forces” in its table [PAGE 4].

5. Explicit stability. The source says “the maximum time step allowed for stability is inversely proportional to the square root of Young’s modulus divided by the mass density [40]” [PAGE 11]. Restoring the length scale gives
\[
\Delta t\le \Delta t_{\mathrm{cr}}\approx \frac{2}{\omega_{\max}},
\qquad c_d=\sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}},
\qquad \omega_{\max}\sim \frac{c_d}{L},
\qquad \Delta t_{\mathrm{cr}}\sim L\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{E}}.
\]
Here \(E\) is Pa, \(\rho\) is kg m\(^{-3}\), \(L\) is m, \(c_d\) is m s\(^{-1}\), \(\omega_{\max}\) is s\(^{-1}\), and \(\Delta t\) is s. Assumptions: linearized stability estimate, lumped mass, and no severe mass scaling. Breakdown regimes: very small elements, distorted elements, stiff contact, high wave speeds, and nonlinear material stiffening. The source also notes that implicit methods can require “three orders of magnitude” more operations per step [PAGE 10], which is why explicit integration is favored for the time-critical path.

6. Dynamic relaxation. For steady-state registration the source uses “an explicit iterative method which relies on the introduction of an artificial mass dependent damping term in the equation of motion, to attenuate the oscillations in the transient response, resulting in convergence towards the steady state solution [59, 60]” [PAGE 11]. A representative form is
\[
\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf u}+c\mathbf M\dot{\mathbf u}
=\mathbf f^{\mathrm{ext}}-\mathbf f^{\mathrm{int}}(\mathbf u),
\]
with \(\mathbf M\) mass matrix in kg, \(\mathbf u\) in m, \(c\) damping in s\(^{-1}\), and forces in N. Assumptions: artificial damping is chosen to accelerate convergence without changing the steady state. Breakdown regimes: nonlinear problems where optimal damping changes during the run; the source responds with adaptive parameter estimation [PAGE 11]. Convergence is not guaranteed for every contact or material history.

7. Brain-skull contact projection. The source states: “The contact algorithm works by detecting the nodes on the brain surface (also called the slave surface) which have penetrated the skull surface (master surface) and displacing such slave nodes to the closest point on the master surface” [PAGE 15]. Formally,
\[
\mathbf x_s \leftarrow \Pi_{\Gamma_m}(\mathbf x_s)
=\arg\min_{\mathbf y\in\Gamma_m}\|\mathbf x_s-\mathbf y\|_2,
\]
with positions in m. Assumptions: rigid skull, frictionless finite sliding, separation allowed, and a sufficiently smooth master surface. Breakdown regimes: nonconvex skull geometry with nonunique closest points, tunneling at high speed, adhesion or friction that the model omits, and contact stiffness that forces a smaller explicit step. The source explicitly chose “frictionless contact which allows separation” [PAGE 8].

8. Image warping transform. The source defines a forward transform \(T\) from source to target and states “the backward transform XS = T-1(XT) needs to be computed” [PAGE 15]. A cubic B-spline approximation is
\[
T(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\mathbf i\in\mathcal I}\mathbf c_{\mathbf i}\,
\beta^3\!\left(\frac{\mathbf x}{h}-\mathbf i\right),
\qquad
\mathbf x_S=T^{-1}(\mathbf x_T),
\]
with \(\mathbf x\) in m, control coefficients \(\mathbf c_{\mathbf i}\) in m, and lattice spacing \(h\) in m. Assumptions: the deformation is smooth enough for a B-spline approximation and the transform is invertible on the image domain. Breakdown regimes: \(\det(\nabla T)=0\) or negative Jacobian, folding, large localized deformation near the craniotomy, insufficient control-lattice resolution, and interpolation error at edges. The source notes that a low-resolution image can still contain \(256\times256\times128=8,388,608\) voxels [PAGE 16], so voxel-wise inverse evaluation is a real computational load.

9. Hausdorff validation metric. The source uses edge-based Hausdorff distance and a 1.7 mm success threshold [PAGE 22]. Formally,
\[
H(A,B)=\max\left\{
\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}\|a-b\|,
\sup_{b\in B}\inf_{a\in A}\|a-b\|
\right\},
\]
with distances in m. The empirical percentile is \(F_D(d)=\Pr(D\le d)\), dimensionless. Assumptions: edge extraction is meaningful and image resolution bounds achievable alignment. Breakdown regimes: edge detection failure, repeated edges, partial field of view, and interpretation of a threshold as anatomical truth. The source says “we considered any edge pair having an HD value less than 1.7 mm to be successfully registered” [PAGE 22].

10. Timing and information units. The source gives \(T_{\mathrm{compute}}\) less than 40 s for intraoperative registration [PAGE 2], a table range of about 40–90 s [PAGE 4], and \(f_{\mathrm{haptic}}\ge 500\) Hz [PAGE 3]. For an image with \(N_v=8,388,608\) voxels and \(K\) intensity levels, a simple information-capacity expression is
\[
I=N_v\log_2 K\ \mathrm{bits},
\]
with \(N_v\) in voxels and \(K\) dimensionless. Assumptions: independent voxel symbols and fixed intensity alphabet. Breakdown regimes: the actual image is spatially correlated, so this is not a compression bound; and timing claims depend on hardware, implementation, model size, and whether image warping is included. The source reports a 100,000-degree-of-freedom nonlinear meshless calculation at 19.2 s on an Intel Core i7 930 [PAGE 23] and a GPU solution “less than four seconds on commonly available hardware” [PAGE 19].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s present-tense recommendations can be traced backward through a numerical lineage. The Total Lagrangian explicit dynamics idea descends from explicit finite-element practice and from the decision to refer all variables to the undeformed configuration [PAGE 10]. That choice retroactively explains why the later clinical clock is survivable: spatial derivatives and hourglass-control parameters can be precomputed before the intraoperative phase [PAGE 10, PAGE 13]. The hourglass-control lineage is Flanagan and Belytschko’s uniform-strain hexahedron with orthogonal hourglass control [PAGE 12], which the authors extended to the Total Lagrangian formulation [PAGE 13]. The tetrahedral lineage is the averaged nodal pressure element of Bonet and Burton, adopted to avoid volumetric locking in nearly incompressible brain tissue [PAGE 13]. The meshless lineage is Element-Free Galerkin with moving least-squares shape functions [PAGE 13-14], plus the authors’ modified MLS and EBCIEM treatment of essential boundary conditions [PAGE 14]. The steady-state lineage is dynamic relaxation with artificial mass-dependent damping [PAGE 11]. The image-warping lineage is multi-level B-spline scattered-data interpolation [PAGE 16]. The validation lineage is edge-based Hausdorff distance descending from Huttenlocher et al. [PAGE 20, PAGE 22].

This genealogy exposes multi-week traps. First, automatic hexahedral meshing for pathological anatomy is described as “still an unsolved problem” [PAGE 12]; a team that spends weeks trying to force high-quality hexahedral meshes on tumors may be solving the wrong bottleneck. The source’s escape is mixed meshes or meshless clouds of points [PAGE 12-14]. Second, linear tetrahedra in incompressible tissue exhibit volumetric locking [PAGE 13]; a team that ignores ANP or a mixed formulation can produce plausible but artificially stiff results. Third, meshless methods do not remove all hard problems: MLS shape functions are non-interpolating, so essential boundary conditions require a special imposition method [PAGE 14], and EFG integration is not exact because shape functions are rational [PAGE 14]. Fourth, image warping is not a postprocessing afterthought; the forward transform must be inverted for target voxels, and a naive finite-element interpolation was “too slow for intraoperative image registration” [PAGE 16]. Fifth, validation by a single metric can mislead: the 1.7 mm Hausdorff threshold is tied to 0.86 mm in-plane resolution and about 1 mm manual surgical accuracy [PAGE 22], not to a universal clinical truth. These are multi-week traps because each looks like an implementation detail but changes the validity of the entire pipeline.

The correct adjudication is not that weather is impossible in some other system, but that it is absent from this model and cannot be used to override its boundary conditions.
