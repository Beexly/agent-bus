<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Hull's compound-stimulus analysis runs from Pavlov's conditioned reflex through Sherringtonian integration to Hull's own deductive behaviorism. Pavlov noted that a stimulus acting continuously without change tends to induce external inhibition or habituation, reducing its effectiveness as a conditioned stimulus—a phenomenon later formalized as sensory adaptation. Kappauf and Schlosburg ([PAGE 166], summarized [PAGE 207]) supplied the experimental pivot: unchanging receptor stimulation loses capacity to acquire habit loadings. Hull abstracts this into a static-dynamic asymmetry that privileges changing aggregates (a flashing light, a varying odor concentration) over constant ones (a steady tone, unvarying pressure).

The multi-week trap embedded here is temporal misalignment between acquisition and expression. Habit strengths acquired under dynamic contingencies may dominate early training, but if the environment later stabilizes into a static context (constant illumination, unvarying background noise, fixed restraining-band pressure), the originally potent channels lose acquisition potency while previously weak static channels may come to control behavior through residual strength or through extinction of the dynamic competitors. Over weeks, an organism can regress to habit hierarchies built under entirely different stimulus statistics, producing spontaneous recovery or renewal effects that Hull's moment-of-reinforcement sheaf (△sHz) does not predict because it lacks a memory trace for prior \tau^{\text{cont}}_i histories. The theory therefore traps the experimenter who assumes that the current compound is the only relevant compound: it ignores the retrocausal influence of prior static exposure on present acquisition capacity.

Weather or ambient conditions that produce constant E_i (steady temperature, unvarying humidity, constant background illumination) drive receptors toward adaptation steady states, increasing \tau^{\text{cont}}_i and invoking the Kappauf-Schlosburg veto ([PAGE 166], [PAGE 207]).

If the band pressure varies with the animal's movement (dynamic), \dot{E}_i \neq 0 and acquisition proceeds. A biomechanical veto occurs whenever the restraint is so static that proprioceptive feedback flatlines; the organism then cannot acquire a habit to the restraint itself as a conditioned stimulus.

Weather/environmental layer: Constant environmental stimuli (e.g., steady wind direction, unchanging barometric pressure) are static aggregates under Hull's definition and are "considerably less potent" ([PAGE 207]). Changes in weather (gusts, light shifts from cloud cover) are dynamic and acquire habit strength preferentially. The veto proof: if the physical receptor cannot transduce a change (\dot{E}_i = 0 due to saturation, adaptation, or inadequate energy flux), then no habit-strength increment is possible at that channel for that reinforcement, no matter how rich the compound or how potent the reinforcer.
