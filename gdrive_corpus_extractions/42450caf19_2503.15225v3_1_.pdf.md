<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage: The paper’s IMS construct descends from Slowiński et al. (2016) [17], which formalized the individual motor signature as a stable trait in velocity profiles within the mirror-game paradigm [15,16]. Earlier, coordination dynamics supplied the low-dimensional oscillator language: Haken–Kelso–Bunz (1985) [18] for bimanual phase transitions, Kelso’s Dynamic Patterns (1997) [12], and Schöner & Kelso (1988) [19] for dynamic pattern generation. The human dynamic clamp and virtual partner interaction (Kelso et al. 2009, 2014 [22,23]; Dumas et al. 2014 [23]) coupled humans to model-based partners using hand-designed oscillators. Virtual players for the mirror game (Zhai et al. 2016, 2017 [24,25]) combined prerecorded trajectories with feedback control but required reference motion signals. Deep sequence modeling entered via Graves (2013) [30] and mixture-density outputs (Bishop 1994 [31]); LSTMs trace to Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997) [28]. Computer-animation generative architectures (normalizing flows, diffusion [35–37]) operate on high-dimensional pose corpora and broad style categories, mismatching the few-minutes-per-individual regime targeted here [PAGE 1–2].

Multi-week traps: The source explicitly notes human motion fluctuates with attention, fatigue, and engagement [PAGE 1], yet all data are 30-second solo trials [PAGE 4]. This is a longitudinal blind spot: in rehabilitation or sports training over weeks, an individual’s IMS may drift due to fatigue accumulation, learning, or injury compensation. The model is calibrated per participant on a static empirical sample and has no mechanism to track slow nonstationarity. A multi-week deployment would face signature drift invalidating the per-instance weights selected by L_gen; re-calibration frequency and drift-detection thresholds are unspecified. Additionally, the dataset contains only five participants, so population-level variability across age, pathology, or skill level is uncharacterized, creating a trap if the architecture is ported to clinical cohorts without re-validation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under stress, fatigue, or crisis, human motor behavior regresses to stereotyped attractors rather than preserving the baseline IMS captured in spontaneous oscillatory trials [PAGE 1 notes variability with attention and fatigue]. A model trained on preferred-frequency finger oscillation [PAGE 4] encodes the subject’s baseline regime, not crisis-mode regressions. If a collaborative robot or avatar relies on this model to anticipate a partner, it will mispredict during high-stakes or fatigued states because the conditional density p(v_t | history) was never trained on crisis attractors.

Counter-trap: The recursive theory-of-mind loop is Agent models Human IMS → Human models Agent IMS → mutual adaptation. The paper’s generative model is open-loop: it emits position signals but accepts no exogenous coupling input [PAGE 9 conclusion: “extend the model to respond to external stimuli” is future work]. In a closed mirror-game or joint-action setting, an artificial agent emitting a reproduced IMS can entrain the human partner; the human then adapts to the machine, violating the stationarity assumption that the human is the fixed source of the signature. This is a counter-trap where the model’s success in reproducing IMS becomes the vector for destabilizing the very behavior it was designed to emulate.

Clock: Real-time closed-loop operation requires inference and rollout at T_s = 0.01 s (100 Hz) with a buffer of l = 400 samples [PAGE 4]. If LSTM inference plus sampling-filter-integration latency exceeds 10 ms per step, phase lag accumulates through the integration p̂(t) = p̂(t−1) + T_s v̂_f(t), breaking interpersonal coordination in tasks sensitive to millisecond synchrony. The model as specified has no latency budget or clock-sync mechanism; therefore, it is currently a generative building block, not an online interactive cognitive architecture [PAGE 1, 9].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto proof: The pipeline is sensor → signal processing → feature extraction → generative model → IMS validation. A veto at any layer invalidates downstream claims.

Physical veto: Data were captured with Leap Motion infrared sensors on the Chronos platform in a controlled lab [PAGE 4]. The sensor’s perception area and IR modality impose a hard physical envelope; operation outside controlled lighting, with occlusions, or beyond the sensor range invalidates position p(t) at the source. Nyquist at 100 Hz is 50 Hz; finger oscillation near or above this limit aliases the velocity signal before any modeling occurs.

Biomechanical veto: The amplitude envelopes A_p^±(t) switch on strict velocity sign conditions (v(t) ≤ 0 with v(t−1) > 0, etc.) [PAGE 3]. At exact zero crossings or numerical noise around v = 0, the envelope update is ill-conditioned, causing jitter in Ā_p^± and thus in δ_a [PAGE 8]. The first-order low-pass filter β = 0.6 is asserted to guarantee a cutoff at least ten times typical human movement frequency (~3 Hz) [PAGE 4]; however, the same page states the Butterworth cutoff was “twice the typical one associated with natural human movement (≈3 Hz),” an internal ambiguity (6 Hz vs. ≥30 Hz) that must be resolved, otherwise the generated motion is over-smoothed relative to the true biomechanical bandwidth and IMS is lost in the high-frequency tail.

Weather veto: Not directly applicable; indoor laboratory setting with no environmental exposure [PAGE 4]. However, temperature-dependent IR sensor drift or humidity-induced reflectivity changes are unmodeled physical vetoes if deployed outside the Chronos lab setup.

Adjudication outcome: No layer currently fails for the reported 5-participant, 1D, solo dataset, but the pipeline lacks a veto-execution specification (e.g., automatic trial rejection when velocity exceeds ±30 cm/s or when zero-crossing density exceeds a threshold). Until such veto rules are formalized, the model’s generalization claims are conditional on the physical and biomechanical envelope remaining inside the training distribution.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions:

Veto Scenario 1 — Velocity support overflow (boundary clipping).
Counterfactual: A new participant oscillates with peak velocity exceeding 30 cm/s, outside the fixed histogram V = [−30,30] cm/s used for all velocity profiles [PAGE 4].
Verification protocol: Record a participant instructed to move “as fast as possible”; compute the empirical velocity histogram; measure mass in the boundary bins at −30 and +30 cm/s.
Prediction: If boundary-bin mass > 0, the true support exceeds the model’s representation. The EMD δ_em is computed on clipped profiles, so generated velocity cannot reproduce out-of-range samples; fidelity H2 fails (corrected p > 0.05) for that participant, and the generated signal’s velocity profile saturates at the bin edge. The amplitude distance δ_a may remain deceptively small because mean amplitudes depend on peak positions, not velocity tails, creating a false-negative in amplitude space.

Veto Scenario 2 — Autoregressive position drift under transient seeds.
Counterfactual: The generative model is seeded with a 4-s window containing a movement onset or offset (non-stationary transient) not represented in the stationary oscillatory training distribution [PAGE 4–5].
Verification protocol: Seed the model with a transient segment; roll out 60 s autoregressively; compute position drift Δp(t) = p̂(t) − p̂(0) and the mean velocity bias b = (1/T) Σ_t (μ̂(t) − v̂_f(t)).
Prediction: Under any persistent bias b ≠ 0 [cm/s], drift grows as Δp(T) ≈ b·T [cm], unbounded in T. The generated signal’s velocity profile shifts away from the target’s, collapsing the block-diagonal structure in the EMD matrix [PAGE 6–7, Figure 7]; H3/H4 specificity fails because the model’s signature migrates toward a drift-dominated attractor.

Veto Scenario 3 — Cross-subject specificity collapse for overlapping IMS.
Counterfactual: Two participants have overlapping IMS in at least one descriptor space; the source notes participants 1 and 3 overlap in velocity-profile space but not amplitude space, and vice versa for 1 and 4 [PAGE 4].
Verification protocol: Select the overlapping pair (e.g., P1 vs. P3 in velocity space); train GM_1 on P1; compute δ_em^avg(P_1, G_3) and compare to δ_em^avg(P_1, G_1) [PAGE 6–7, H4].
Prediction: If the overlap is substantial, δ_em^avg(P_1, G_3) ≈ δ_em^avg(P_1, G_1), violating H4’s strict inequality and invalidating specificity R2. The model would attribute the other subject’s signature to the wrong target. This is directly testable from the off-diagonal blocks of the EMD matrix and the covariance ellipses [PAGE 7–8, Figure 7–8].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
