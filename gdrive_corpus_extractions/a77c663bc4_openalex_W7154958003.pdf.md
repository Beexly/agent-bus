1. Benchmark cardinality and unit assumptions  
Let \(N_v\) be the number of videos and \(N_q\) the number of QA pairs. The paper reports \(N_v = 925\) videos and \(N_q = 6{,}475\) QA pairs across 11 sports [PAGE 1], [PAGE 4]. If every video receives the fixed seven-question template, then  
\[
q_v = \frac{N_q}{N_v} = \frac{6475}{925} = 7
\]
where \(q_v\) has units of QA pairs per video. This is exact under the paper’s stated template. Breakdown regime: if some videos are excluded from one or more question types, then \(q_v\) is no longer constant and per-question denominators must be used instead of a global average.

2. Accuracy operator  
For model \(m\), let \(\hat{y}_{m,i}\) be the predicted option letter and \(y_i\) the ground-truth letter. The reported accuracy is  
\[
\hat{a}_m = \frac{1}{N_q}\sum_{i=1}^{N_q} \mathbf{1}\left[\hat{y}_{m,i} = y_i\right]
\]
with dimensionless units, interpretable as an empirical probability of exact match. The paper states that accuracy is computed by direct comparison without an external evaluator [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: if the model emits free text rather than a single option letter, exact-match accuracy becomes undefined unless a normalization operator is added.

3. Chance accuracy under the paper’s option structure  
Q1 is a 3-choice question, while Q2–Q7 are 4-choice questions [PAGE 5]. Under uniform random guessing,  
\[
a_{\text{rand}} = \frac{1}{7}\left(\frac{1}{3} + 6 \cdot \frac{1}{4}\right)
= \frac{11}{42}
\approx 0.2619
\]
This is dimensionless. The paper’s blind text-only test reports accuracy less than 30%, which is close to this baseline [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if Q1 is omitted, the baseline becomes \(1/4\); if option priors are non-uniform, the baseline must be recomputed using the empirical prior distribution.

4. Information-theoretic option entropy  
If Q1 is uniform over three options and Q2–Q7 are uniform over four options, then  
\[
H_{\text{Q1}} = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits}
\]
\[
H_{\text{Q2--Q7}} = \log_2 4 = 2.000 \text{ bits}
\]
\[
\bar{H} = \frac{1}{7}\left(\log_2 3 + 6\log_2 4\right)
\approx 1.941 \text{ bits per video}
\]
The paper says the correct-answer options follow a near-uniform distribution, reducing positional bias [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: if the correct answer is concentrated in one letter, entropy falls below the uniform bound and positional shortcuts become more informative.

5. Cluster correlation and effective sample count  
Because seven questions are attached to each video, QA correctness may be correlated within a video. Let \(\rho\) be the intra-video correlation of correctness. A standard design-effect approximation gives  
\[
\text{DEFF} = 1 + (q_v - 1)\rho
\]
\[
N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{N_v q_v}{\text{DEFF}}
\]
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{a}) \approx \frac{a(1-a)}{N_{\text{eff}}}
\]
where \(a\) is dimensionless accuracy and \(N_{\text{eff}}\) is an effective QA count. Breakdown regimes: if \(\rho = 0\), then \(N_{\text{eff}} = 6475\); if \(\rho \to 1\), then \(N_{\text{eff}} \to 925\), meaning the benchmark behaves more like 925 video-level decisions than 6,475 independent QA decisions.

6. Audio gain operator  
Let \(a_{\text{frames}}\) be accuracy using sampled frames only and \(a_{\text{audio}}\) be accuracy using the original video file with audio. Then  
\[
\Delta a_{\text{audio}} = a_{\text{audio}} - a_{\text{frames}}
\]
For Gemini-3-Flash, the paper reports 60.55% to 76.54%, so  
\[
\Delta a_{\text{audio}} \approx 0.1599
\]
For Qwen3-Omni, the paper reports 48.97% to 65.65%, so  
\[
\Delta a_{\text{audio}} \approx 0.1668
\]
The paper states that adding audio substantially improves performance for both models [PAGE 7]. Units are probability points, dimensionless. Breakdown regime: if audio contains commentator narration that directly names the foul, the gain may reflect semantic leakage rather than pure refereeing evidence. The paper itself warns that broadcast videos may contain auxiliary cues such as commentator descriptions [PAGE 8].

7. Negative-sample over-call operator  
For normal clips without a violation, define the false-positive rate for foul existence as  
\[
\text{FPR}_{\text{Q1}} = P(\hat{y} = \text{foul} \mid y = \text{no foul})
\]
The paper reports misidentification rates under neutral and suggestive settings [PAGE 8]. For example, Gemini-3-Flash has 37.7% misidentification on Q1 under neutral wording, while Qwen3-VL-8B has 12.7% on Q1 but 60.4% on foul classification under suggestive wording [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regime: if the negative clip set does not contain a valid “no” option, the conditional probability is undefined; if all clips are forced to contain a foul, over-calling cannot be estimated.

8. Rule-augmented retrieval effect  
Let \(a_{\text{ZS}}\) be Zero-Shot accuracy, \(a_{\text{Plain}}\) be Plain-RAG accuracy, and \(a_{\text{Hybrid}}\) be Hybrid-RAG accuracy on rule-intensive questions Q2–Q5. The paper reports average accuracies of 55.91%, 53.92%, and 50.98%, respectively [PAGE 13]. Thus  
\[
\Delta a_{\text{Plain}} = 0.5392 - 0.5591 = -0.0199
\]
\[
\Delta a_{\text{Hybrid}} = 0.5098 - 0.5591 = -0.0493
\]
The paper states that external rule retrieval does not consistently improve end-to-end sports officiating performance [PAGE 13]. Breakdown regime: if the visual premise is correct and the retrieved rule clause is exact, retrieval may help; if the visual premise is wrong, retrieval can amplify the wrong premise.

9. Temporal sampling capture operator  
Let \(\delta\) be the duration of the decisive officiating interval in seconds and \(\Delta t\) be the frame sampling interval in seconds. Under a simplifying assumption that the decisive interval is fully inside the clip and the sampling phase is uniformly random,  
\[
P_{\text{hit}} = \min\left(1, \frac{\delta}{\Delta t}\right)
\]
where \(P_{\text{hit}}\) is dimensionless. For models using 1 frame per second, \(\Delta t = 1\,\text{s}\) [PAGE 11]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\delta \to 0\), then \(P_{\text{hit}} \to 0\); if \(\delta \geq \Delta t\), then \(P_{\text{hit}} \to 1\) provided the clip fully contains the interval. If the clip boundary cuts the decisive interval, \(P_{\text{hit}} = 0\) regardless of model capability.

10. Rule-grounded decision operator  
A rule-grounded decision requires a joint evidence condition:  
\[
P(y \mid x_{\text{visual}}, x_{\text{audio}}, r_{\text{rule}})
\]
where \(x_{\text{visual}}\) is visual evidence, \(x_{\text{audio}}\) is acoustic evidence, and \(r_{\text{rule}}\) is sport-specific rule knowledge. The paper shows that audio improves performance [PAGE 7], but also that models possess only partial refereeing knowledge [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regime: if any evidence channel is missing or corrupted, the model must either abstain or fall back to a prior. The negative-sample results show that the prior can become an over-calling bias [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of RefereeBench runs from perception-first sports video analysis to rule-grounded officiating. Early benchmarks emphasized action spotting and fine-grained recognition, such as SoccerNet and SoccerNet-v2 [PAGE 3]. The paper then notes that refereeing-oriented resources were historically limited to soccer, including VARS and X-VARS [PAGE 3]. Real-world systems such as Video Assistant Referee and Semi-Automated Offside Technology are cited as practical evidence that multi-view visual evidence and spatial tracking can support human officials [PAGE 3]. RefereeBench expands the lineage to 11 sports and seven officiating-oriented question types [PAGE 2], [PAGE 4].

The genealogical traps are multi-week because they are structural rather than incidental. First, soccer-centric bias can make a model appear capable when it has merely memorized soccer-like event patterns. Second, broadcast production cues can create shortcuts: commentator descriptions, referee gestures, replay graphics, or audio emphasis may correlate with the correct answer without forcing true rule application. The paper explicitly warns that broadcast videos may contain auxiliary cues such as commentator descriptions or referee gestures, which may introduce shortcuts that do not reflect true decision-making ability [PAGE 8]. Third, language-only shortcuts are a trap because multiple-choice questions can sometimes be answered from textual plausibility. The authors screen for this by presenting textual questions alone to Gemini-3-Flash and revising items that can be solved without visual context [PAGE 4]. Fourth, temporal annotation drift is a trap because a model may detect an incident but misplace the decisive moment. The paper introduces temporal grounding into automated sports refereeing and argues that precise localization is needed to anchor decisions to the specific incident rather than spurious contextual correlations [PAGE 4]. Fifth, retrieval augmentation can become a trap if rule text is injected without verifying the visual premise. The RAG experiment shows that external rule retrieval does not consistently improve end-to-end sports officiating performance [PAGE 13]. Sixth, calibration drift is a trap because models may learn that sports video contains aggressive contact and therefore over-call fouls. The paper reports that current MLLMs struggle to distinguish legal sports actions from true fouls and tend to over-officiate, especially under suggestive wording [PAGE 8].

The retro-causal audit therefore says that future systems must not only be trained on more videos; they must be trained to resist inherited shortcuts from broadcast sports media, soccer-specific priors, textual plausibility, and over-calling habits.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind audit asks what the model believes the annotator believes, and whether that belief collapses under crisis-like uncertainty. The annotator is a certified referee who models the player’s action, the rulebook, and the match context. The model is asked to imitate that referee. The first recursion is: model predicts foul because the question frame says “referee.” The second recursion is: model predicts foul because it expects the benchmark to contain a foul. The third recursion is: model predicts a more severe penalty because it expects that serious incidents attract stronger sanctions. The negative-sample evidence shows that this recursive expectation can become a habit regression: models over-call fouls on normal clips. The paper reports high misidentification rates under neutral and suggestive settings, concluding that current MLLMs struggle to distinguish legal sports actions from true fouls and tend to over-officiate [PAGE 8].

The counter-trap is to make uncertainty explicit. A calibrated system should estimate \(P(\text{foul} \mid \text{evidence})\) and abstain when the evidence is below a decision threshold. In the benchmark’s multiple-choice setting, this can be approximated by adding “no foul,” “no penalty,” or “not sure” options, but the paper already ensures valid negative options for negative-sample evaluation [PAGE 8]. A stronger counter-trap is to require the model to state the visual premise before applying a rule: contact, body position, ball or object state, player intent, and time interval. If the visual premise is absent, rule retrieval should not be allowed to dominate. This is consistent with the paper’s finding that retrieved rule text can bias the model toward over-interpreting the scene when visual evidence is ambiguous [PAGE 13].

The clock is the temporal dimension. A referee decision is not merely “something happened”; it is “this happened at this decisive moment.” The paper argues that temporal grounding provides exact visual evidence for human review and anchors decisions to the specific incident rather than spurious contextual correlations [PAGE 4]. Yet grounding is weak: Q7 scores are around or below 50% for strong closed-source models and lower for open-source models [PAGE 6]. The recursive trap is that a model may use coarse event context as a proxy for time. For example, if a clip contains a later celebration, the model may anchor to the celebration rather than the contact. The countermeasure is event-triggered sampling, audio-visual synchronization, or explicit temporal verification. The audio result supports multimodal evidence but also warns that commentary can act as a semantic clock that substitutes for true visual timing [PAGE 7], [PAGE 8].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof treats a refereeing decision as a chain of necessary conditions. Let \(E\) be the observed incident evidence, \(C\) be the physical contact event, \(R\) be the applicable sport-specific rule, \(T\) be the temporal interval, and \(Y\) be the final officiating label. A valid contact-based foul requires that the visual or multimodal evidence support \(C = 1\) and that \(R\) classify the contact as illegal. If \(P(C = 1 \mid E) = 0\), then any rule requiring contact is invalid, so  
\[
P(Y = \text{contact foul} \mid E, R) = 0
\]
This is a physical-biomechanical veto at the evidence layer. RefereeBench does not measure force, joint torque, or biomechanical injury risk; therefore any claim that contact exceeded a physical threshold is outside the measured variables. The safe adjudication rule is epistemic: if the clip does not establish contact or the manner of contact, the model should not assert a contact foul.

The second veto is temporal. If the decisive interval \(T\) is not observed, then even a correct rule cannot be anchored. From the sampling model,  
\[
P_{\text{hit}} = \min\left(1, \frac{\delta}{\Delta t}\right)
\]
If \(\delta\) is the decisive interval and \(\Delta t\) is the frame interval, then \(P_{\text{hit}} \to 0\) as \(\delta \to 0\). The paper states that precise localization ensures that the model’s decision is strictly anchored to the specific incident rather than spurious contextual correlations [PAGE 4]. If the model receives frames that miss the decisive contact, its grounding score must fail, regardless of its language reasoning.

The third veto is weather or visibility degradation. The paper does not provide explicit weather labels, so weather must be treated as an external breakdown regime. If visibility \(V_t = 0\) because of rain, spray, ice glare, water surface distortion, occlusion, or broadcast artifact, the visual likelihood collapses:  
\[
P(E_t \mid Y) \to \text{degenerate}
\]
Audio may rescue some cases, and the paper shows that adding audio substantially improves performance for Gemini-3-Flash and Qwen3-Omni [PAGE 7]. However, audio is not a universal substitute. If the acoustic track contains commentary but not the physical sound of contact, whistle, or referee signal, the audio cue may be semantically suggestive rather than evidentially decisive. The negative-sample analysis shows that models over-interpret legal physical contact and hallucinate specific but non-existent events [PAGE 8].

The fourth veto is rule-layer incompleteness. The paper finds that current MLLMs possess non-trivial but incomplete refereeing knowledge [PAGE 8]. If \(R\) is missing or misapplied, the final label is invalid even when \(E\) and \(T\) are correct. Therefore the adjudication chain is:  
\[
\text{Valid decision} \iff E \text{ is present} \land T \text{ is localized} \land R \text{ is correctly applied} \land \text{calibration is not over-calling}
\]
If any layer fails, the decision should be vetoed or abstained. The benchmark results support this proof: existence and perception are relatively stronger, while reasoning and grounding are weaker [PAGE 1], [PAGE 6]. The model can see an incident but cannot reliably bind it to rule, time, and calibrated judgment.

Veto 1: Negative-clip over-call veto  
Verifiable evidence: The paper evaluates negative samples, defined as normal sports clips without an actual violation, and reports misidentification rates under neutral and suggestive settings [PAGE 8]. Gemini-3-Flash reaches 37.7% misidentification on Q1 under neutral wording, 63.6% on foul classification under neutral wording, and 76.8% under suggestive foul wording. Qwen3-VL-8B reaches 60.4% on foul classification under suggestive wording and 74.9% on penalty classification under suggestive wording [PAGE 8]. Prediction: if a reinforcement-learning reward is trained without explicit penalties for false positives on normal clips, the model will retain or increase over-calling. If the reward includes negative-clip abstention and penalizes suggestive bias, Q1, Q2, and Q4 false-positive rates should decrease. A verifiable success condition is a lower misidentification rate on held-out negative clips without reducing existence accuracy on true incidents.

Veto 2: Temporal sampling veto  
Verifiable evidence: The benchmark includes temporal grounding as a core dimension, and the paper states that temporal awareness is crucial because precise localization provides exact visual evidence and anchors decisions to the specific incident [PAGE 4]. Yet Q7 grounding remains weak across strong models, with Gemini-3-Flash at 46.8%, Gemini-3-Pro at 48.8%, Doubao-Seed-1.8 at 50.1%, and Qwen3-VL at 39.0% [PAGE 6]. The appendix reports that some models use 1 frame per second sampling [PAGE 11]. Prediction: if the decisive interval is shorter than the sampling interval, temporal grounding cannot be reliable. Event-triggered sampling, higher frame rate around candidate incidents, or explicit temporal proposal modules should improve Q7. A verifiable failure condition is that increasing frame count without temporal supervision leaves grounding near chance or near incident-detection accuracy rather than precise interval accuracy.

Veto 3: Rule-retrieval visual-premise veto  
Verifiable evidence: The paper tests rule-augmented inference with Plain-RAG and Hybrid-RAG on rule-intensive questions Q2–Q5. Average accuracy is 55.91% for Zero-Shot, 53.92% for Plain-RAG, and 50.98% for Hybrid-RAG [PAGE 13]. The authors state that external rule retrieval does not consistently improve end-to-end sports officiating performance [PAGE 13]. Prediction: rule retrieval helps only when the visual premise is correct. If the model misperceives the contact, applies the retrieved rule to the wrong event, or allows ambiguous visuals to be forced into retrieved legal categories, retrieval should reduce accuracy. A verifiable test is to stratify questions by whether the model’s visual premise is correct. In the correct-premise subset, rule retrieval may improve classification or reasoning. In the incorrect-premise subset, rule retrieval should not improve accuracy and may worsen it. A successful counter-trap system should therefore retrieve rules only after a verified visual premise and should abstain when the premise is uncertain.
