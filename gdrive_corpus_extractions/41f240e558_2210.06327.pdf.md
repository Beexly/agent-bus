<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(y_i\) be the observed goals scored by a team in match \(i\), with units of goals per match, and let \(\hat y_i\) be the predicted goals for that team. The source explicitly treats home and away goals as two independent models [PAGE 2], so the formal prediction system is
\[
\hat y_{H,i}=f_H(\mathbf{x}_{H,i};\theta_H),\qquad
\hat y_{A,i}=f_A(\mathbf{x}_{A,i};\theta_A),
\]
with \(\hat y_{H,i},\hat y_{A,i}\) in goals per match and \(\mathbf{x}\) the 52-feature schema [PAGE 3]. The combined predicted scoreline is \((\hat y_{H,i},\hat y_{A,i})\), and the paper states, “The ﬁnal score is the combination of these predictions resulting in the ﬁnal scoreline” [PAGE 2].

The source uses MAE, RMSE, and \(R^2\) as fitness metrics [PAGE 2]. Their standard definitions are
\[
\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|y_i-\hat y_i\right|,
\]
with units of goals per match;
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\hat y_i\right)^2},
\]
also in goals per match; and
\[
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\hat y_i\right)^2}
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_i-\bar y\right)^2},
\]
which is dimensionless. The exact singularity regimes are important. MAE and RMSE are undefined for \(n=0\); RMSE is more sensitive to large errors because of the square. \(R^2\) is undefined when the denominator \(\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2=0\), i.e., when all observed goals are identical. Out-of-sample \(R^2\) may be negative; the paper reports negative values such as \(-0.23\) for Home Win and \(-1.06\) for Recency in table 1 [PAGE 4], which is a legitimate breakdown flag, not an error. The paper’s best home Lineup Stats SVR reports MAE 0.89, RMSE 1.16, and \(R^2=0.17\) [PAGE 4]; the best away Team Stats SVR reports MAE 0.87, RMSE 1.14, and \(R^2=0.20\) [PAGE 4].

For standings, the source uses Kendall \(\tau\) rank correlation [PAGE 2]:
\[
\tau=\frac{n_c-n_d}{\sqrt{(n_0-n_1)(n_0-n_2)}},
\]
where \(n_c\) is concordant pairs, \(n_d\) discordant pairs, \(n_0=n(n-1)/2\), \(n_1\) counts ties in the first ranking, and \(n_2\) counts ties in the second ranking. \(\tau\) is dimensionless and lies in \([-1,1]\). It breaks down when the denominator is zero, e.g., \(n<2\) or all ranks tied. The paper reports low full-table correlations, e.g., Players \(\tau=0.232\) and Team Stats \(\tau=0.053\) [PAGE 5], so the rank signal is weak even when other outcomes look strong.

Feature importance uses chi-squared dependence [PAGE 4]:
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{k}\frac{(O_k-E_k)^2}{E_k},
\]
where \(O_k\) is an observed count and \(E_k\) is the expected count under independence. \(\chi^2\) is a dimensionless test statistic, not a probability and not in bits. It is invalid when \(E_k=0\), and unstable for sparse or constant features; that is a key breakdown regime for the 52-feature player aggregation. The paper’s result is that “with a keeper’s clean sheets (not conceding a goal in a match) chi2 score being higher than the next four features combined” [PAGE 5].

The historical Poisson baseline is also formalized in the source lineage: Maher used “an independent Poisson model to describe football scores” [PAGE 1]. For goals \(X\in\mathbb N_0\),
\[
\Pr(X=x)=\frac{\lambda^x e^{-\lambda}}{x!},
\]
with \(\lambda\) in goals per match and probability dimensionless in \([0,1]\). This breaks down under overdispersion, underdispersion, negative dependence between home and away goals, or \(\lambda=0\) degeneracy. The paper does not rely on Poisson for its main learners, but it inherits the same independence assumption problem: home and away goal processes are modeled separately [PAGE 2], so shared match state is not jointly identified.

The paper’s heuristic models have simple formal forms. For Home Win, \(\hat y_H=1,\hat y_A=0\) for every fixture [PAGE 2]. For Recency, \(\hat y_{H,i}=g_{H,i-1}\) and \(\hat y_{A,i}=g_{A,i-1}\), with \(g\) the previous match goals [PAGE 2]. For the Players model, each player indicator \(x_j\in\{-1,0,1\}\) encodes home, away, or neither [PAGE 2]. That encoding creates a high-dimensional, collinear design; the “Players LR” row with “<< 1” [PAGE 4] is therefore a breakdown regime rather than evidence of a superior model.

The machine-learning operators are also explicit enough to audit. KNN regression averages the \(k\) nearest training outcomes:
\[
\hat y(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in N_k(\mathbf{x})}y_j,
\]
in goals per match; it breaks down when distance scaling is dominated by irrelevant features or when \(k\) is too large/small. Random forest regression averages bagged trees:
\[
\hat y(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_b(\mathbf{x}),
\]
in goals per match; it breaks down under correlated trees and small \(B\). SVR uses an \(\epsilon\)-insensitive loss,
\[
L_\epsilon(y,\hat y)=\max(0,|y-\hat y|-\epsilon),
\]
with \(\epsilon\) in goals per match; the paper describes SVR as finding “the hyperplane that intersects the largest number of points in a 52 dimensional space” [PAGE 3]. Its breakdown regimes are kernel-scale misspecification, \(\epsilon\) mis-tuning, and high-dimensional collinearity.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from Maher’s 1982 independent Poisson model [PAGE 1] through classification work by Hucaljuk and Rakipović, who reported 68% and 65% accuracy on Champions League matches [PAGE 1], and through Herbinet’s 2018 regression work with RMSE 1.153 and MAE 0.861 [PAGE 1]. The source also cites Lindberg and Söderberg’s LSTM player-performance work with averaged MSE 8.63 across position groups [PAGE 1], Horvat and Job’s review of 20+ models [PAGE 1], and team-rating/player-rating work by Arntzen and Hvattum [PAGE 8]. This genealogy matters because the current paper’s novelty is not “ML for football” but the specific attempt to inject individual starting-player sets into a scoreline regression. The paper says the closest prior work is Herbinet’s, and the main difference is “our paper investigates the importance of individual player lineup statistics rather than global measurements for an entire team” [PAGE 1].

The retrocausal trap is that later evaluation choices can make earlier hypotheses look confirmed or refuted depending on aggregation. The paper aggregates “the individual players listed on the lineup of a match” into “the average of each position groups stats” up to that point in the season [PAGE 3]. That design is a rolling, multi-week state variable. Early-season estimates are noisy because the denominator is small; mid-season estimates are contaminated by managerial changes, injuries, transfer-window arrivals, and fixture congestion; late-season estimates may encode schedule strength. The paper does include current-season and previous-season data [PAGE 2–3], but it does not model the multi-week decay of player form or the exact availability clock. A second trap is the chronological split: 680 matches, with the last 100 as test [PAGE 2]. That is a defensible time-series split, but it gives only one realized future. A model that looks strong on one 100-match block may be learning a season-specific defensive regime rather than a stable law. A third trap is the goalkeeper finding. If clean sheets are a team-defense proxy [PAGE 5], then the “goalkeeper importance” result is retrocausally consistent with old defensive-organisation arguments, but it does not prove that signing a keeper is the causal lever. The paper itself cites José Mourinho’s 2004/05 Chelsea conceding only 15 goals and notes that Petr Čech was protected by John Terry and Claude Makélélé [PAGE 5]. That is exactly the multi-week, multi-layer confound: keeper quality, back-line quality, midfield screening, and tactical shell reinforce one another over a season.

The broader historical lesson is that football score models repeatedly rediscover the same hierarchy: exact score prediction is hard, outcome prediction is easier, and full-table ranking is harder still. The paper reports 50% top-four accuracy for most models and 100% relegation accuracy for Lineup Stats and Team Stats [PAGE 6], but also weak full-table \(\tau\) values [PAGE 5]. That is not a contradiction; it is the signature of a model that can separate extremes while failing on the middle of the table. The multi-week trap is to overfit the extreme classes and then mistake that for a general ranking law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer has three competing hypotheses about what the modeler believes the manager, player, and evaluator know. First, crisis habit regression: under pressure, managers may revert to familiar defensive shells, familiar starting elevens, or familiar substitution patterns. The model does not measure stress, fatigue, or tactical habit directly; it sees only aggregated position stats [PAGE 3]. So a “lineup effect” may actually be a “crisis habit” effect: the same players appear because the manager is risk-averse, not because those players independently cause goals. Second, the counter-trap: the paper’s own intuition was that attacker goals would dominate, but the chi-squared ranking placed goalkeeper clean sheets and goals against at the top [PAGE 5]. The modeler’s theory of mind about “star attackers win matches” is contradicted by the model’s feature ranking. But the counter-trap is that the model may be reading the opponent’s defensive organization through the keeper, not reading the keeper’s individual skill. Third, the clock: the model updates week by week using “stats … up to that point in the season” [PAGE 3]. That is a recursive belief-updating clock, but it is not a causal clock. A player who is about to return from injury, a manager who is about to be replaced, or a team entering a congested fixture block is not represented. The recursive agent model therefore must distinguish “what the model knows now” from “what the manager knows now” and “what the evaluator knows after the season.” The paper’s chronological test split [PAGE 2] is the right clock discipline, but the feature construction still allows within-season information to be smoothed in ways that may not match real decision timing. The safest interpretation is that lineup presence is a social-capital signal, as the paper says the Players model uses “encoded names” and “social capital rather than their objective productive indicators” [PAGE 2], while the Lineup Stats model uses objective production. The two are not the same theory of mind: one models reputation, the other models measured contribution. The paper’s weak and inconsistent lineup gains suggest reputation and measured contribution are both incomplete proxies for the manager’s actual tactical intent.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto is not discharged by the source. The 52-feature schema contains 13 defender stats, 14 midfielder stats, 13 attacker stats, and 12 opponent stats, with 5 goalkeeper and 7 defender stats from the opponent [PAGE 3]. There is no weather variable, no pitch condition, no travel load, no injury status, no biomechanical sprint load, no referee variable, and no tracking-derived spacing variable. Therefore any claim that goalkeeper clean sheets are the “most impactful stat” [PAGE 7] is a predictive claim inside this feature set, not a proof that keeper skill physically dominates. The paper itself gives the cross-layer caveat: “Goalkeeper’s clean sheets and goals against are uniquely positioned to reﬂect the team’s defensive eﬀorts due to the nature of how other stats are distributed” [PAGE 5]. That is an explicit admission that the keeper feature is a compressed team-defense signal. Weather can alter shot quality, pass completion, and goalkeeper handling; biomechanical fatigue can alter pressing intensity and defensive spacing; travel and fixture congestion can alter recovery. None are in the model. The cross-layer veto therefore remains open: if a physical-layer variable changes the goal process, the model’s feature importance can shift without any change in keeper skill. The correct adjudication is to accept the source’s internal predictive ranking but reject external causal generalization until weather, load, injury, and tactical-state variables are added or held fixed. The paper’s own small test block of 100 matches [PAGE 2] also makes cross-layer confounding harder to detect, because one unusual weather regime or one injury cluster can dominate a short evaluation window.
