<THINK_TRACE>
The provided text from Tolman (1932) presents a foundational debate between Hedonic Reinforcement Theory (represented by Troland) and Cognitivist Expectancy Theory (Tolman's own view). Troland posits that behavior is driven by the direct stimulation of beneceptive (pleasant) or nociceptive (painful) receptors, which enhance or depress neural conductance. In this model, motivation is a downstream consequence of these sensory inputs: "To get to pleasant... sensations... are for him the ultimate motivations of behavior." This implies a reactive loop where the organism is a passive recipient of hedonic signals that then retroactively reinforce past actions.

Conversely, Tolman argues for a proactive, cognitive architecture. He asserts that pleasantness is not a raw sensory input but an "expression of cognitive expectations already functioning." The feeling arises from a "total sign gestalt expectation" that current activity will lead to a "good significate" (a meaningful, goal-directed outcome). Unpleasantness similarly reflects an expectation of a "bad significate." Thus, affect is a readout of an internal predictive model, not its driver.

This distinction is critical for understanding modern decision-making frameworks. If Troland were correct, we would expect a strong correlation between immediate sensory pleasure and behavioral persistence, even in the absence of long-term goals. However, Tolman’s view suggests that organisms can persist in activities that are immediately unpleasant (e.g., studying, training) if they are coupled with a strong positive expectancy. This aligns with contemporary theories of "delayed gratification" and "goal-gradient effects," where the anticipation of reward modulates effort allocation independent of immediate sensation.

The "retroflex action" described by Troland—where beneception/nociception alters the conductance of *then or just previously conducting* neural processes—is a form of Hebbian-like plasticity. However, Tolman’s framework subsumes this mechanism within a larger symbolic process. The "sign gestalts" (cognitive maps of cues-outcomes) determine which neural pathways are active during a given context. Therefore, the modulation of conductance is not random but is gated by the active expectancy. This provides a mechanistic basis for how learning occurs: successful predictions strengthen the specific sign-gestalt pathways, while failures weaken them, without requiring a global hedonic signal as the sole teacher.

The historical lineage here is pivotal. Early 20th-century psychology was dominated by S-R (stimulus-response) models that struggled to explain complex, latent learning. Tolman’s introduction of intervening variables (expectancies, purposes) was a paradigm shift. His critique of Troland is not merely semantic; it repositions the locus of control from peripheral receptors to central cognitive structures. This has profound implications for AI and neuroscience: if motivation is fundamentally about maintaining coherent world-models rather than maximizing momentary pleasure, then intelligent systems must prioritize prediction error minimization over raw reward accumulation. The "gray zone" lies in integrating both views: perhaps beneceptive signals act as a meta-heuristic that validates or invalidates higher-order expectancies, creating a multi-layered control system where sensory feedback tunes cognitive maps.

**Operator 1: Hedonic Retroactive Plasticity (Troland's Model)**

\[
\Delta G(N(t)) = 
\begin{cases} 
+\alpha & \text{if } B=1 \\
-\beta & \text{if } D=1 \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
where \( \alpha, \beta > 0 \) are scaling factors. This operator describes a non-specific, global modulation of currently active circuits based on peripheral affective state. **Units**: Siemens (S) for conductance. **Breakdown Regime**: This equation fails when the same stimulus produces opposite valences depending on context (e.g., spicy food), as it lacks a mediating variable for meaning.

**Operator 2: Expectancy-Modulated Affect (Tolman's Model)**
Define \( E(S \rightarrow O) \) as the strength of a sign-gestalt expectation linking stimulus context \( S \) to outcome \( O \). The expected utility \( U_{exp} \) is a function of this expectation: \( U_{exp}(S) = f(E(S \rightarrow O)) \). Affective state \( A \) is derived from this utility:
\[
A(S) = k \cdot \left( U_{exp}(S) - U_{baseline} \right)
\]
where \( k \) is a sensitivity constant. Here, \( A > 0 \) corresponds to pleasantness, and \( A < 0 \) to unpleasantness. Crucially, \( A \) is a *consequence* of \( E \), not its cause. **Units**: Arbitrary cognitive units for \( E \); utils for \( U \); subjective magnitude for \( A \). **Singularity**: Occurs when \( E(S \rightarrow O) \) becomes infinitely confident (\( \lim_{E \to \infty} A \to \pm \infty \)), representing delusional certainty or catastrophic anxiety.

**Formal Contrast**:
In Troland's model, the gradient of learning \( \nabla L \) is proportional to \( \Delta G \). In Tolman's model, learning updates the expectation itself: \( \Delta E \propto \delta \), where \( \delta \) is the prediction error between expected and actual outcome. The two models diverge in their treatment of "latent learning"—learning that occurs without immediate reinforcement. Tolman's equation allows \( \Delta E \neq 0 \) even when \( A = 0 \) (no affective feedback), whereas Troland's requires \( \Delta G \neq 0 \) (some hedonic signal) for any lasting change.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of this debate traces back to the fundamental problem of explaining "purpose" in biological systems. Pre-Tolman, dominant behaviorist doctrines (Watson, Thorndike) reduced purpose to conditioned reflex chains, struggling with phenomena like insight learning and maze variations. Edward Tolman’s doctoral work under behaviorists led him to propose "intervening variables"—unobservables like expectancies and purposes—that mediated S-R relationships. His confrontation with Troland (a physiologist-psychologist) represents a clash between two reductionist paths: one reducing purpose to physiology (receptor states), the other reducing physiology to information processing (symbolic maps).

Historically, Troland’s view is a descendant of James-Lange theory and early hedonic theories (Bain, Spencer), which saw emotion as awareness of bodily changes. The "beneceptive/nociceptive" dichotomy echoes von Frey’s receptor theory. Tolman’s rebuttal draws on Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Wertheimer), emphasizing holistic perception ("sign gestalts") over elemental sensations. It also prefigures later cognitivist revolutions (Neisser, Anderson) by treating the mind as a symbolic processor.

A **multi-week trap** exists in interpreting this history linearly. Modern affective neuroscience often revisits somatic marker hypotheses (Damasio), seemingly reviving Troland-like ideas. However, Damasio’s markers are themselves learned associations—a fusion of Tolman’s cognitions with Troland’s physiology. The trap is seeing this as a victory for hedonics, when in fact it demonstrates that "raw" sensations are always filtered through cognitive schemas. Thus, the true lineage shows a dialectical synthesis: cognition shapes physiological response, which in turn feeds back to update cognition. The enduring tension is between top-down (expectancy) and bottom-up (sensory) drivers of behavior, a tension unresolved even in contemporary reinforcement learning debates (reward shaping vs. intrinsic motivation).
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

Thus, the organism defaults to whichever level (heuristic pleasure or cognitive plan) offers faster convergence given current uncertainty.
