The source defines a camera-calibration operator by the need to relate image pixels to court/world coordinates: “The relationships between the pixel coordinates in each image and the world(court) coordinates must be understood in order to perform measurements based on the acquired images” [PAGE 2]. A standard formalization consistent with that statement is the pinhole projection

\[
s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}
=
K\begin{bmatrix}R&\mathbf{t}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\\1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
K=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_x & \gamma & c_x\\
0 & f_y & c_y\\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\]

where \(u,v,c_x,c_y,f_x,f_y\) are in pixels, \(X,Y,Z\) and \(\mathbf{t}\) are in metres, \(R\) is dimensionless, \(s\) is a homogeneous scale scalar, and \(\gamma\) is the skew term. For a planar playing field, the standard planar homography is

\[
s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}
=
H\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}.
\]

The source does not print \(H\), but it does state that strict field landmarks simplify calibration: “Sports have strict rules that exactly define the locations and sizes of key landmarks on the playing field, simplifying the process [2]” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown regimes for this operator are exact: if \(\det(H)=0\), the mapping is not invertible; if calibration landmarks are collinear or too few, \(H\) is unidentifiable; if the field is not planar or the camera moves after calibration, the homography residual becomes systematic; if lens distortion is not radial-only, the pinhole/homography model is misspecified.

The source’s explicit equations are the fisheye radial transformation [PAGE 5]:

\[
r_{\mathrm{Defish}}
=
f\tan\!\left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}}{f}\right),
\tag{1}
\]

\[
r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}
=
f\arctan\!\left(\frac{r_{\mathrm{Defish}}}{f}\right),
\tag{2}
\]

where \(r_{\mathrm{Defish}}\) is “the distance to the center of the corrected (Defish) image,” \(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}\) is “the distance to the center of the distorted(Fisheye) image,” and \(f\) is “the focal length(in pixels)” [PAGE 5]. Units: \(r_{\mathrm{Defish}},r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}},f\) are pixels; \(r/f\) is dimensionless radians. The assumption is exact and quoted: “This transformation is carried out under the assumption that the lens is spherical and that the distortion is radial but not tangential [5]” [PAGE 5]. Equation (1) has a principal-branch singularity when \(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}/f\to \pi/2+k\pi\); physically this is the edge of the model’s valid angular domain. Equation (2) is asymptotically bounded: finite \(r_{\mathrm{Defish}}\) maps to \(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}\to f\pi/2\) as \(r_{\mathrm{Defish}}\to\infty\), so the inverse cannot represent an incidence angle beyond the principal half-space without branch handling.

The reverse-mapping equations are [PAGE 5]:

\[
X_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}
=
(X-X_{\mathrm{center}})
\frac{r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}}{r_{\mathrm{Defish}}}
+
X'_{\mathrm{Center}},
\tag{3}
\]

\[
Y_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}
=
(Y-Y_{\mathrm{center}})
\frac{r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}}{r_{\mathrm{Defish}}}
+
Y'_{\mathrm{Center}},
\tag{4}
\]

\[
r_{\mathrm{Defish}}
=
\sqrt{(X-X_{\mathrm{center}})^2+(y-y_{\mathrm{center}})^2}.
\tag{5}
\]

Here all coordinates and radii are in pixels. The clear singularity is \(r_{\mathrm{Defish}}=0\) at the image center, where Eqs. (3)–(4) are undefined by direct division; the center must be assigned by continuity \(X_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}=X'_{\mathrm{Center}}\), \(Y_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}=Y'_{\mathrm{Center}}\). A second breakdown is the one-time look-up-table assumption: the source says “look-up tables are used, and each camera’s mapping is only calculated once” [PAGE 5]. If the camera zooms, focuses, vibrates, or is remounted, the mapping is stale and the residual is not zero-mean. The source also notes “Interpolation is necessary because the restored image is larger than the distorted one” and that “Reverse mapping can avoid interpolation by copying a few of the original, deformed image’s pixels into the corrected image” [PAGE 5]. This creates a discretization error bound in pixels, not a continuous exact inverse.

For tracking, the source gives qualitative operators rather than equations: background subtraction “categorize[s] each pixel in the current scene as either foreground or background” [PAGE 3]; template matching “is centred on an image point throughout the template matching procedure, and the matched pixels are tallied” [PAGE 3]; head/shoulder tracking is used “because it is simpler and more visible [5]” [PAGE 3]; and multi-athlete assignment uses “Voronoi partitioning [6]” [PAGE 3]. The closed-world assumptions are explicitly listed: “(1) There are two fixed cameras that provide a full field view; (2) The field is bounded; and(3) Two players cannot occupy the same position at the same time-step [5]” [PAGE 3]. These assumptions are the breakdown surface: occlusion, camera motion, unbounded or crowded scenes, and same-pixel overlap each violate a premise.

The temporal operator is constrained by the Hawk-Eye description: “up to 10 cameras placed around the field to record live images at up to 340 frames per second” and “once it is struck, the rules of physics can be used to forecast its motion” [PAGE 3]. A minimal physical association condition is

\[
\frac{v}{f_s} \le \Delta_{\max},
\]

where \(v\) is target speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(f_s\) is frame rate in \(\mathrm{Hz}=\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), and \(\Delta_{\max}\) is the association gate in metres. If the inequality is violated and no motion prior is used, frame-to-frame identity is ambiguous. This is a physical veto, not a substitute for the source’s empirical system.

The broadcast/modeling operator has its own geometric breakdown. The source states: “The players’ planar nature becomes clear when the viewing direction shifts by over 15 degrees” [PAGE 4]. That is a quoted angular threshold for billboard artifacts. It also states: “It is also difficult to distinguish between two players when their positions overlap” [PAGE 4]. Thus a single-camera billboard model is valid only in a gray zone: small virtual-camera angular deviation, no severe overlap, and stable player segmentation. The paper’s proposed fix is “Add one or more cameras, make a ‘2.5D’ model, and require seamless merging of several billboard images” [PAGE 4]. The breakdown is then multi-camera registration and seam consistency, not merely detection.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is visible in its own citations and examples. It traces an early fixed-camera, simple-background approach: “The snooker analysis tool takes advantage of the relatively non threatening characteristics of the game. Specifically, when the ball is separated from a simple green background, the camera is usually fixed. This is an early example [10]” [PAGE 5]. That 1984 TELETRACK reference is the ancestor of later virtual graphics. The genealogy then moves to 3D virtual production [7], the BBC Piero sports graphics system [8], and the Ericsson Piero system [9], with the billboard strategy “initially applied in 2004” [PAGE 4]. In parallel, Hawk-Eye is described as “one of the first multi-camera computer vision systems that was commercially available in 2001,” first for cricket and later tennis [PAGE 3]. The retrocausal lesson is that later requirements—multi-camera 3D, automatic replay, long-term athlete tracking—retroactively expose assumptions that were acceptable in the earlier fixed-camera, simple-background regime. A fixed snooker camera with a green background can tolerate a one-time calibration; a multi-week outdoor tennis or football deployment cannot.

The multi-week traps are therefore calibration and environment traps. The source says the earliest systems measured “the camera’s pan and tilt on a fixed mount, as well as the zoom and focus settings on the lens, using mechanical sensors” and that “These lens encoders’ ‘raw’ outputs will have an impact on the calibration information for the lens” [PAGE 2]. It also says computer vision “can eliminate the need for extra lenses or mounts” [PAGE 2], but that does not eliminate drift. A multi-week deployment must re-estimate the homography and distortion look-up table after vibration, thermal expansion, lens servicing, or camera bump. The paper’s own “look-up tables are used, and each camera’s mapping is only calculated once” [PAGE 5] is efficient for a broadcast fixture but fragile as a long-horizon invariant. The second multi-week trap is occlusion statistics: a tracker tuned on a sparse early-season scene may fail when player density rises; the paper’s closed-world assumption that “Two players cannot occupy the same position at the same time-step [5]” [PAGE 3] is exactly the assumption that crowded play violates. The third is background drift: background subtraction [PAGE 3] assumes a stable background model, while weather, lighting, shadows, and camera auto-exposure change the pixel distribution. The source does not test these, so the genealogy must be read as a warning: the historical success of fixed-camera systems does not transfer automatically to multi-week, multi-venue, weather-exposed operation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer asks what each human stakeholder expects the vision system to know, and what the system expects the human to tolerate. The referee expects a geometric answer: where is the player or ball relative to a court landmark? The coach expects a biomechanical answer: what movement occurred at a key moment? The broadcaster expects a visual answer: can a virtual camera show a viewpoint that the real camera cannot? The spectator expects continuity: no unexplained identity swaps, no frozen replay, no delayed rhythm. The source states that “The rhythm of some fast-paced sports will be disrupted by the repeated use of computer vision for replay, which will also have an impact on player performance and spectator sentiment” [PAGE 5]. That is a clock-layer veto: even a correct geometric answer can be socially or athletically wrong if its latency breaks the event clock.

Crisis habit regression appears when the system encounters occlusion or ambiguity. The source notes that commercial systems range “from manually selecting parts of athletes in calibrated camera images to automated techniques [3][4]” [PAGE 3]. Under stress, the pipeline regresses to the habit it knows: template matching on head and shoulders, manual selection, or a single-camera billboard. The paper says “The head and shoulders are typically tracked in part when tracking an athlete because it is simpler and more visible [5]” [PAGE 3]. That is a reasonable fallback, but it is also a regression: head/shoulder visibility is not the same as full-body pose, and it fails when the head is occluded or when two players’ shoulders overlap. The counter-trap is to add cameras and build a “2.5D” model [PAGE 4], but that can create a new habit: trusting multi-camera fusion without checking registration. If the cameras disagree, the system may produce a smooth but geometrically false virtual view. The clock layer is the third mode: repeated replay and re-analysis consume time, and the source explicitly warns about rhythm disruption [PAGE 5]. A recursive design must therefore decide when to answer, when to abstain, and when to hand off to a human. The source’s question “How can the target be tracked when it is obscured?” [PAGE 5] is not only technical; it is a theory-of-mind question about whether the referee or spectator will accept an inferred position when the evidence is missing. The correct counter-trap is to expose uncertainty and timestamped provenance—camera, calibration version, occlusion state, and look-up-table version—so the human can calibrate trust rather than anthropomorphize the tracker.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/optical veto is proved by the source’s own distortion model. Equation (1), \(r_{\mathrm{Defish}}=f\tan(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}/f)\), is valid only on a principal branch. As \(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}/f\to\pi/2\), \(\tan\to\infty\); the corrected radius diverges. Equation (2), \(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}=f\arctan(r_{\mathrm{Defish}}/f)\), is bounded by \(f\pi/2\) for finite corrected radius. Therefore any claim that the inverse mapping can recover arbitrarily large incidence angles without branch management is false. The source’s assumption is explicit: “the lens is spherical and that the distortion is radial but not tangential [5]” [PAGE 5]. If tangential distortion or camera tilt is present, the residual is systematic and the veto condition is: measured calibration residual exceeds the pixel tolerance required by the sport’s landmark geometry. The one-time look-up table [PAGE 5] is also vetoed by any camera zoom, focus change, vibration, or remount, because the mapping is stale.

The biomechanical/geometric veto is proved by the billboard and overlap statements. The source says “The players’ planar nature becomes clear when the viewing direction shifts by over 15 degrees” [PAGE 4]. Thus a single-camera billboard virtual view has a quoted angular validity threshold. Beyond that threshold, the model is no longer a faithful 3D reconstruction; it is a planar approximation. The source also says “It is also difficult to distinguish between two players when their positions overlap” [PAGE 4]. In pixel space, if two players’ foreground masks overlap, the assignment problem is ambiguous unless additional cameras, motion priors, or identity features resolve it. The closed-world condition “Two players cannot occupy the same position at the same time-step [5]” [PAGE 3] is therefore a veto trigger, not a neutral assumption: when two players project to the same image region, the tracker must not pretend the condition holds. A formal adjudication is: if no camera has an unoccluded ray to the player centroid and no temporal identity prior is available, the position estimate must be marked “occluded/inferred,” not “measured.”

The temporal/biomechanical veto uses the frame-rate and ball-motion evidence. The source describes “up to 340 frames per second” and “short shutter times” for Hawk-Eye [PAGE 3], and says physics can forecast motion “once it is struck” [PAGE 3]. The veto inequality is \(v/f_s\le\Delta_{\max}\). If the per-frame displacement exceeds the association gate, a tracker without prediction cannot maintain identity. This is especially relevant to fast ball sports and to limb motion in training, where the source says marker-based motion capture is “impracticable” in lower-level training and competition [PAGE 4]. The cross-layer conclusion is that high frame rate is necessary but not sufficient; shutter time, multi-camera geometry, and motion priors must all be validated together.

The weather/environment veto is an inference from the source’s background-subtraction operator: “Background detection uses background subtraction to categorize each pixel in the current scene as either foreground or background” [PAGE 3]. This assumes a stable background distribution. Rain, snow, shadows, lighting changes, and camera auto-exposure can violate that assumption. The source also notes that “some remote locations lack the resources and technology necessary to employ computer vision” [PAGE 5]. The veto proof is operational: if the background model’s false-foreground rate on known non-player regions exceeds the tolerance, or if the homography residual grows after wind/vibration, the system must abstain or recalibrate. The paper does not provide weather experiments, so this is a cross-layer audit, not a sourced empirical result. The safe adjudication is to require per-venue calibration checks and per-session validation before trusting automated measurements.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: the counterfactual must be checkable from logs, not judged by vibes. Three veto scenarios follow.

1. Occlusion identity counterfactual. Intervention: take a calibrated multi-player sequence and synthetically occlude player \(A\) behind player \(B\) for \(K\) frames, leaving only partial head/shoulder evidence. Verifiable prediction: a template-matching or head/shoulder tracker [PAGE 3] will either lose \(A\) or swap identity when \(A\) and \(B\) overlap, because the source states “It is also difficult to distinguish between two players when their positions overlap” [PAGE 4]. Veto if identity-switch count exceeds zero within the occlusion window or if the tracker reports a measured position without an “occluded/inferred” flag. The correct counterfactual is to force the system to abstain or use a multi-camera/motion-prior estimate, then score the abstention against ground truth.

2. Fisheye-boundary and calibration-drift counterfactual. Intervention: inject a synthetic radial displacement near the principal-branch boundary of Eq. (1), and separately perturb the camera mount after the look-up table was computed. Verifiable prediction: Eq. (1) will produce a large corrected radius as \(r_{\mathrm{Fisheye}}/f\to\pi/2\), and the one-time look-up table [PAGE 5] will no longer match the new geometry. Veto if the reprojection residual on known court landmarks exceeds the pre-registered pixel tolerance, or if any pixel is mapped outside the valid principal branch without an explicit branch/abstention flag. This tests the source’s assumption that “the lens is spherical and that the distortion is radial but not tangential [5]” [PAGE 5].

3. Weather/background and clock counterfactual. Intervention: replay a fixed-camera sequence with synthetic rain, snow, or lighting flicker, and separately introduce a replay latency that exceeds the event clock. Verifiable prediction: background subtraction [PAGE 3] will misclassify non-player pixels as foreground, increasing false detections; and the source’s warning that “The rhythm of some fast-paced sports will be disrupted by the repeated use of computer vision for replay” [PAGE 5] predicts a human-factor veto even if the geometry is correct. Veto if false-foreground rate on masked non-player regions exceeds tolerance, if homography residual grows after the weather perturbation, or if replay latency exceeds the sport-specific clock budget. The counterfactual should reward correct abstention and timestamped provenance, not merely confident tracking.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
