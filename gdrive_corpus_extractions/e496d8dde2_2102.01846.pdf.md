The source is not a causal model of football; it is a resampling engine. The core operator is stated plainly: “The simulations are generated by sampling play-by-play data from previous football seasons” [PAGE 1]. That means the first multi-hop derivation is from strategy \(s\) to a historical play pool \(\mathcal{P}_{s,d,g,\ell}\), where \(d\) is down, \(g\) is yards to go, and \(\ell\) is yardline. The package samples “according to a simple random sample with replacement from our population of interest (NFL play-by-play data) to produce unbiased and representative results” [PAGE 3]. The estimator is therefore a Monte Carlo average over resampled drives, not an intervention. Any claim that “always go for it” causes \(2.28\) points per drive is an estimate of the resampling distribution under the observed historical response, not of a counterfactual where opposing defenses change their behavior.

The second hop is the drive simulator. The paper says `sample drives()` will “simulate a series of plays by one team (a drive) following some speciﬁc strategy versus another team employing a ‘normal’ strategy” [PAGE 4], and that repeated drives identify “expected points per drive and proportion of drives resulting in a score” [PAGE 4]. Thus the reported Table 1 quantities are plug-in Monte Carlo functionals: \(\hat{\mu}_{s}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}^{(s)}\), with \(S_{i}\) in points per drive and \(n=10000\) [PAGE 6]. The confidence intervals are Monte Carlo intervals for that resampling estimand. They do not include model uncertainty about opponent adaptation, weather, clock, or player health.

The third hop is the fourth-down decision logic. The paper gives the expected-points decomposition as “the product of the probability of securing a ﬁrst down and the point value of a ﬁrst down at the speciﬁc yardline added to the product of the probability of not securing a fourth down and the point value of the other team taking possession at the given yardline” [PAGE 5]. This is a valid finite-state expected-value identity if the first-down probability, the resulting field-position value, and the opponent-possession value are all conditioned on the same context. The gray-zone issue is that the source estimates those quantities empirically and then compares five sub-strategies, including “always go,” “never go,” and “expected points” [PAGE 6]. The comparison is descriptive, not a causal ranking. The authors themselves caution: “Here, we caution the reader that this is by no means a causal investigation of fourth down strategies” [PAGE 9].

The fourth hop is the apparent paradox in Table 1. “Never go” has the largest probability of any score because it combines \(33\%\) field goals and \(15\%\) touchdowns, while “Always Go” has \(0\%\) field goals and \(33\%\) touchdowns [PAGE 7]. Yet “Always Go” has the largest mean score, \(2.28\) points, versus \(2.03\) for “Never go” [PAGE 7]. The resolution is objective mismatch: a coach may maximize probability of any score, while the simulation table reports mean points. The source states that “the fourth down sub-strategy with the largest average points per simulated drive is always go for it (average of 2.28 points)” [PAGE 7], but it also states that “the never go for it strategy oﬀers the largest probability for scoring on a single drive with the majority of the scores coming from ﬁeld goals” [PAGE 6]. That is a multi-hop tradeoff, not a contradiction.

The fifth hop is sparsity and window expansion. The source warns that “it may be there are few or no plays where a team had the ball on 3rd down, on the 47th yardline, with 15 yards to go for a ﬁrst down” [PAGE 3], and that “In such cases we widen our sampling range” [PAGE 4]. This is a singular regime: when the exact cell is empty, the sampling operator is undefined unless a neighborhood window is imposed. The window changes the estimand from \(E[S\mid d,g,\ell]\) to a weighted mixture over neighboring \(g,\ell\). The package’s validity therefore depends on the window being small enough that the mixture is scientifically acceptable, and large enough that the cell is nonempty. That is not a toy issue; it is the central gray zone for rare down-distance-yardline states.

Finally, the pass-proportion analysis adds a second estimand. The source defines \(p\) as “the probability a given oﬀensive play on ﬁrst, second, or third down is a pass” [PAGE 10]. Let the historical play corpus be \(\mathcal{D}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\), where each play \(x_i\) has down \(d_i\in\{1,2,3,4\}\), yards to go \(g_i\in\mathbb{N}\) in yards, yardline \(\ell_i\in\{1,\dots,99\}\) in yards from own goal, and play type \(a_i\in\{\text{pass},\text{run},\text{kick}\}\). The source says each year contains “approximately 48,000 plays of data” [PAGE 3]. A strategy \(s\) defines an admissible subset \(\mathcal{P}_{s,d,g,\ell}\subseteq\mathcal{D}\). The basic sampling operator is

\[
X_t^{(s)} \sim \mathrm{Unif}\!\left(\mathcal{P}_{s,d_t,g_t,\ell_t}\right),
\qquad t=1,2,\dots,
\]

with replacement, matching “a simple random sample with replacement” [PAGE 3]. Units: \(X_t\) is a play record; \(d_t\) is dimensionless down index; \(g_t\) and \(\ell_t\) are yards.

If \(\mathcal{P}_{s,d,g,\ell}=\varnothing\), the source expands to a neighborhood. Define

\[
\mathcal{P}_{s,d,g,\ell}^{(W_\ell,W_g)}
=
\left\{x_i\in\mathcal{D}: d_i=d,\; |\ell_i-\ell|\le W_\ell,\; |g_i-g|\le W_g\right\},
\]

where \(W_\ell\) and \(W_g\) are window widths in yards. The source describes this as widening “our sampling range to include plays from yardlines close the to the yardline of interest or with one less yard to go for a ﬁrst down” [PAGE 4]. The breakdown regime is exact: if the exact cell is empty and no window is used, the sampler is undefined; if the window is too wide, the estimator no longer targets the stated \((d,g,\ell)\) state.

For a drive simulation, let \(O_i\in\{\text{no score},\text{FG},\text{TD},\text{turnover}\}\) and let

\[
S_i = 3\,\mathbf{1}\{O_i=\text{FG}\} + 7\,\mathbf{1}\{O_i=\text{TD}\},
\]

in points per drive, following the source’s convention that “a touchdown always results in 7 points” [PAGE 6]. The Monte Carlo estimator is

\[
\hat{\mu}_s=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_i^{(s)},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_s=\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}_s^2}{n}},
\qquad
\mathrm{CI}_{95\%}=\hat{\mu}_s\pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_s,
\]

with \(n=10000\) drives in the source’s fourth-down application [PAGE 6]. Units: \(\hat{\mu}_s\) is points per drive; \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_s\) is points per drive; the interval is points per drive. The source reports, for example, “Always Go 0% 33% 2.28 (2.22, 2.35)” [PAGE 7]. The table caption says “out of 1000” [PAGE 7] while the text says \(10000\) [PAGE 6]; that internal inconsistency is a verifiable audit point because it changes the reported Monte Carlo precision by a factor of \(\sqrt{10}\) if one treats the caption literally.

The expected-points fourth-down operator is

\[
\mathrm{EP}_{\text{go}}(g,\ell)
=
P(\text{first down}\mid g,\ell)\,V(\ell')
+
\left[1-P(\text{first down}\mid g,\ell)\right]\,V_{\text{opp}}(\ell),
\]

where \(P(\cdot)\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless, \(V(\ell')\) and \(V_{\text{opp}}(\ell)\) are point values at the resulting field positions, and \(\ell'\) is the post-conversion yardline. This is the formal version of the source’s statement that expected points are “the product of the probability of securing a ﬁrst down and the point value of a ﬁrst down at the speciﬁc yardline added to the product of the probability of not securing a fourth down and the point value of the other team taking possession at the given yardline” [PAGE 5]. The kick branch is

\[
\mathrm{EP}_{\text{FG}}
=
P(\text{make FG}\mid \ell)\,3
+
\left[1-P(\text{make FG}\mid \ell)\right]\,V_{\text{opp}}(\ell_{\text{miss}}),
\]

and the punt branch is \(\mathrm{EP}_{\text{punt}}=E[V_{\text{opp}}(\ell_{\text{punt}})]\). The decision rule is \(\arg\max\{\mathrm{EP}_{\text{go}},\mathrm{EP}_{\text{FG}},\mathrm{EP}_{\text{punt}}\}\). The source says it uses “the expected points estimated from the nflscrapR R package” and then “solve[s] for the expected value of going for it, punting it, and kicking a ﬁeld goal” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: if \(P(\text{first down}\mid g,\ell)\) is estimated from a sparse cell, the expectation is unstable; if the opponent’s post-possession value is not conditioned on clock and score, the comparison is a marginal rather than contextual decision.

For the run/pass operator, let \(p\in[0,1]\) be the probability that a first-, second-, or third-down play is a pass. The source defines \(p\) exactly as “the probability a given oﬀensive play on ﬁrst, second, or third down is a pass” [PAGE 10]. The sampled play type satisfies

\[
\Pr(a_t=\text{pass}\mid d_t\le 3)=p,
\qquad
\Pr(a_t=\text{run}\mid d_t\le 3)=1-p.
\]

On fourth down, the source follows “an empirical procedure” and samples “from all fourth down plays at the given yardline (or within a neighborhood of the yardline) and distance to go” [PAGE 10]. The estimand is \(E[S\mid p]\), but the observed contrast is not causal because \(p\) is varied without conditioning on quarterback quality, opponent response, clock, or weather. The source reports that “There is a clear trend showing more touchdowns are scored as the proportion of plays that are passes increases” [PAGE 10], while also stating that “we are not saying that passing more will necessarily lead to more scores, particularly if the team has a sub-standard quarterback” [PAGE 11].

Assumptions and exact breakdowns:
1. Exchangeability: historical plays in \(\mathcal{P}_{s,d,g,\ell}\) are representative of the target context. Break: rule changes, roster turnover, weather, or opponent adaptation shift the conditional distribution.
2. Replacement sampling: plays are independent draws. Break: drives are serially dependent and game state updates within a drive.
3. “Normal” opponent: the opposing team is sampled from all plays “without a speciﬁc strategy in mind” [PAGE 4]. Break: if the opponent best-responds to \(s\), the simulated payoff is not the counterfactual payoff.
4. Truncation: fourth-down analyses keep only plays “before the ﬁnal 2 minutes of each half” and “where one team is within 28 points of the other” [PAGE 6]. Break: the estimates are not identified for two-minute drills or extreme score states.
5. Threshold search: the “go for it if yardage is smaller than \(Y\)” strategy scans \(Y=1,\dots,10\) [PAGE 8–9]. Break: selecting \(Y=5\) after inspecting Figures 2.1–2.3 is a selection problem; the source only says “a value of 5 yards may be the best option” [PAGE 9], not that it is a pre-registered optimum.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is explicit. Romer (2006) begins the modern fourth-down discussion by estimating expected point values from 1998, 1999, and 2000 play-by-play data [PAGE 5]. The New York Times Fourth Down Bot updated that logic in 2013, and Yam and Lopez (2019) used the bot’s data in a causal analysis, estimating “approximately 0.4 more wins per year” under more aggressive fourth-down behavior [PAGE 5]. The pass/run lineage runs through Kovash and Levitt (2009), who “found NFL teams did not pass as much as they should” [PAGE 2], and Hermsmeyer (2018), who similarly argued teams should pass more [PAGE 2]. The simulation lineage runs through Lopez (2019), whose blog post used “a simulation-based approach to investigate a potential overtime rule change in the NFL” [PAGE 3], and into NFLSimulatoR itself, which generalizes that one-off into an R package [PAGE 3].

The retrocausal feedback is important: later models can be folded back into the earlier simulation frame. The paper says a “new model-based fourth down decision maker was introduced by Ben Baldwin” and that “Such a strategy could be integrated and tested within the simulation based framework we created” [PAGE 14]. That is a retroactive re-use of the same resampling operator for a newer decision rule. The multi-week trap is that this lineage is not stationary. The package currently downloads data “from 2009 - 2019” [PAGE 3], but the fourth-down application uses “play-by-play data from both 2018 and 2019” [PAGE 6], and the authors say the package “can be re-run each season as more data becomes available” [PAGE 6]. A conclusion calibrated on 2018–2019 may drift as rule enforcement, offensive philosophy, quarterback supply, and defensive tactics change.

A second multi-week trap is software genealogy. The paper says NFLSimulatoR is “in its infancy with the current release being v0.3.1” [PAGE 14], and it depends on nflscrapR or nflfastR data access [PAGE 3]. API changes, column renaming, and package-version drift can silently alter the play pool. A third trap is inferential lineage: the fourth-down result “always go for it” has the highest mean points, but “never go for it” has the highest probability of any score [PAGE 6–7]. Later readers may collapse those two objectives into one “best strategy” claim. The authors block that move by cautioning that this is “by no means a causal investigation” [PAGE 9]. A fourth trap is the Table 1 caption-text mismatch: “out of 1000” [PAGE 7] versus \(10000\) drives in the text [PAGE 6]. That is a small but verifiable reproducibility hazard. A fifth trap is the playoff-team proxy: the source uses “playoﬀ appearance as a proxy for quality” [PAGE 11], then corrects with passer-rating terciles, showing that “teams in the upper tercile of passing ratings score more touchdowns as the proportion of passing plays increases than teams in the other two groups” [PAGE 11]. The genealogy therefore moves from “pass more” to “pass more if the quarterback quality supports it,” which is a conditional, not universal, conclusion.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that the coach is not choosing against a fixed distribution; the coach is choosing against an opponent who is modeling the coach. The source states the historical habit: “Historically, NFL coaches tend to not go for it on fourth down unless time is running out and/or the only possible way to win the game involves increasing the risk of a turnover for the potential beneﬁt of a ﬁrst down” [PAGE 5]. That is crisis habit regression: under high leverage, the coach reverts to the conservative punt or field goal because the immediate failure is socially legible, even when the expected-points model favors aggression. The source’s own summary is that “NFL coaches tend to be too conservative in their fourth-down calls” [PAGE 2].

The counter-trap is that an aggressive policy changes the opponent’s beliefs. If a team becomes known for “always go for it,” the defense can prepare for fourth-down conversion attempts, alter coverage, and allocate practice time differently. The package’s “normal” opponent is sampled from all plays “without a speciﬁc strategy in mind” [PAGE 4], so the simulated payoff is \(E[S\mid s,r_0]\), where \(r_0\) is the historical response, not \(E[S\mid s,r(s)]\), where \(r(s)\) is the opponent’s best response. The formal veto condition is \(r(s)=r_0\). If \(r(s)\neq r_0\), the simulation overstates or understates the strategy’s value depending on whether the opponent’s response reduces or increases the strategy’s efficiency. The paper’s proposed extension to “randomly selecting and setting various parameters for a strategy” [PAGE 13] is useful for exploration but does not by itself solve the counter-trap, because random strategy selection is not the same as equilibrium response.

The clock is the third recursive layer. The source explicitly says “a future study might include a time-in-game parameter, \(\tau\), in order to sample from plays within a window around \(\tau\)” [PAGE 13]. It also notes that “teams that are winning in the fourth quarter might elect to rush more often in order to ‘shorten the game’ and losing teams might pass more” [PAGE 11–13]. This means the optimal \(p\) and the optimal fourth-down rule are functions of \(\tau\), score differential, and the opponent’s clock incentives. The current fourth-down application removes the final two minutes of each half [PAGE 6], so it explicitly does not identify the two-minute-drill regime where clock pressure is highest. A coach who uses the simulation to justify aggression in the final two minutes is therefore extrapolating outside the source’s support.

A useful formal representation is a decision utility

\[
U(s;\tau,\Delta)
=
\lambda\,P(\text{score}\mid s;\tau,\Delta)
+
(1-\lambda)\,E[S\mid s;\tau,\Delta]
-
\rho\,R(s;\tau,\Delta),
\]

where \(P(\text{score})\) is dimensionless, \(E[S]\) is points per drive, \(\lambda\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless objective weight, \(\rho\ge0\) is a dimensionless regret-aversion weight, and \(R\) is a normalized regret penalty. This is not a source equation; it is a formal way to show why the “never go” versus “always go” comparison is unstable. If \(\lambda\) is high, “never go” can look best because it has the largest probability of any score [PAGE 6]. If \(\lambda\) is low, “always go” can look best because it has the highest mean points [PAGE 7]. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the coach’s \(\lambda\) and \(\rho\) are themselves shaped by owner patience, media criticism, and opponent expectations. The simulation reports the payoff table, but it does not model the coach’s utility or the opponent’s belief update.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is straightforward: the source’s data layer does not contain the physical, biomechanical, or weather variables needed to transport the simulation to a target context. The paper’s own future-work list includes “time-in-game, current score, weather, location” as additional simulated in-game characteristics [PAGE 13]. That is an admission that the current operator samples from a marginal play distribution, not from a context-conditioned distribution. Formally, let \(C\) be a context vector containing wind speed in meters per second, precipitation rate, temperature, field surface, player load, injury status, and score differential. The simulation estimates

\[
\hat{E}[S\mid s]
=
\sum_{C}\hat{E}[S\mid s,C]\,\hat{P}_{\mathcal{D}}(C),
\]

but a target query asks for \(\hat{E}[S\mid s,C_0]\). If \(P_{\mathcal{D}}(C_0)\approx0\), or if \(C\) is not recorded, the target query is not identified. This is the exact cross-layer breakdown.

Physical veto: a pass-heavy strategy increases the number of dropbacks, which changes sack and quarterback-hit exposure. The source does not model pass-rush pressure, injury risk, or cumulative contact. It does mention the quarterback-quality confound: “particularly if the team has a sub-standard quarterback” [PAGE 11]. That is a performance confound, but it is also a physical proxy: a sub-standard quarterback under pressure is more likely to take sacks and throw interceptions. The simulation’s \(p\)-scan therefore cannot be transported to a team with a weak offensive line without an external biomechanical or pressure model.

Weather veto: wind, rain, snow, and cold alter ball flight, grip, and route timing. The source’s play-by-play corpus is not conditioned on weather in the reported analyses, and the authors list weather only as a possible extension [PAGE 13]. A high-wind or heavy-rain game can reverse the sign of the pass-proportion effect: the historical average says more passing increases touchdowns [PAGE 10], but in a low-grip environment the same \(p\) may increase incompletions, sacks, and turnovers. The veto condition is \(C_0\notin\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{D}_C)\) or \(P_{\mathcal{D}}(C_0)\) too small to estimate. In that regime, the simulation’s confidence interval is a false precision because it captures only Monte Carlo noise, not weather transport error.

Biomechanical veto: fourth-down conversion attempts are contact events with non-negligible injury and fatigue consequences. The source assumes that “the impact of, and mental anxiety among, players due to it being fourth down is negligible” [PAGE 5–6]. That assumption may be defensible for professional players, but it is not a biomechanical proof. If a team’s conversion success depends on offensive-line push, running-back health, or quarterback mobility, the historical play pool must be conditioned on those states. The source does not provide player-tracking load, injury reports, or surface friction. Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: the simulation can rank strategies inside its historical, healthy, weather-marginal support; it cannot veto a coach’s physical/medical judgment outside that support. The correct output is a conditional estimate plus an explicit unsupported-regime flag, not a universal “always go” mandate.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
