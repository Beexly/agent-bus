The source is a statistical-methods paper, not a causal risk-factor discovery paper. Its own framing is decisive: “The present study aimed at providing an appropriate statistical modelling strategy for sports injury data in football, which holds some challenges, rather than providing evidence about lower-limb injuries’ risk factors.” [PAGE 14]. That sentence must govern every downstream inference. The model chain is: injury is a recurrent time-to-event outcome measured in exposure minutes; the Cox partial likelihood estimates log-hazard coefficients; regularized Cox methods perform variable selection under small-sample and high-covariate conditions; selected variables are then inserted into a shared frailty Cox model; the frailty term absorbs unobserved player-level heterogeneity and within-player recurrence dependence; gap-time risk intervals restart after recovery; predictive performance is assessed by Brier score and integrated Brier score with inverse probability of censoring weighting and bootstrap .632+ correction. The real case is a female football team with 22 players in the 2017-2018 season; 28 variables were preselected from 200 measured screening variables; 12 players suffered lower-limb injuries from 19 injuries; 7 players were injured once, 4 twice, and 1 four times; 45 percent never injured [PAGE 3]. That is a very small event count relative to the covariate space, which is exactly why the paper turns to penalization and simulation.

The first multi-hop audit point is sample-size fragility. The paper states that “predictive performance greatly improves as more player observations are available” [PAGE 1], and the simulation results show that “differences between frailty models diminish when the sample size is increased” [PAGE 12]. The clinical temptation is to read the selected variables as stable player-level risk markers. The statistical reading is weaker: with 22 players and 19 injuries, variable selection is unstable, the frailty variance is weakly identified, and the Brier-score curve is estimated with internal bootstrap correction because external validation data are absent [PAGE 7]. The second audit point is recurrent-event alignment. The paper resets the time origin at mid-season after new covariates are collected [PAGE 4], and the gap-time approach creates a new risk interval every time a player has fully recovered and starts training [PAGE 5]. Any analysis that ignores this resetting will merge pre-injury and post-recovery clocks and produce biased risk sets. The third audit point is the two-step procedure. The paper explicitly warns: “the techniques used in the first step do not consider the correlation between groups of observations, and note that this could consequently lead to a flawed selection” [PAGE 15]. It also says: “A preferable choice would be to jointly perform both steps, i.e. selecting the important variables and fitting the model” [PAGE 15]. Thus, the selected variable sets are screening hypotheses, not final causal structures. The fourth audit point is predictive vs etiologic interpretation. The paper reports that BeSS “leads to the sparsest model, it estimates 2 out of 28 to be non-zero” [PAGE 10], and that “ASLR lumbar strength LSI” and “Drop jump vertical propulsion LSI” are “the only variables selected by all considered methods” [PAGE 10]. That is evidence of selection consistency, not proof of biomechanical causation. The fifth audit point is the absence of weather or environmental variables. The 28 variables are anthropometrics and biomechanical functional tests assessing bilateral strength asymmetries [PAGE 3]; no weather, surface, temperature, rainfall, or travel variable appears in the source. Any weather claim is therefore an extrapolation beyond the source operators and must be vetoed unless new data are supplied. The sixth audit point is model-evaluation geometry. The Brier score is a squared probability error; the IBS integrates it over a time interval; the paper reports medians in [0,1000] and [0,3500] minute windows because “the prediction error depends on the time interval considered” [PAGE 12]. Early-time IBS values are similar across models, while later-time IBS differences are more apparent. A model that looks acceptable at 1000 minutes may fail at 3500 minutes. The gray-zone conclusion is that the paper supports a disciplined recurrent-frailty workflow, not individualized guarantees or causal certainty.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The time scale is exposure time in minutes. The paper defines “a player’s exposure time in minutes until the player gets injured” as a non-negative random variable \(T\) [PAGE 4]. I keep the source’s minute as the coherent time unit rather than converting to seconds. The Cox hazard operator is
\[
\lambda_i(t\mid x_i)=\lambda_0(t)\exp(x_i\beta),\qquad i=1,\dots,N,
\]
where \(\lambda_0(t)\) is a nonparametric baseline hazard in \(\mathrm{min}^{-1}\), \(x_i\beta\) is dimensionless if covariates are standardized, and \(\beta\) is a vector of log-hazard coefficients [PAGE 4]. If covariates are not standardized, the units of \(\beta\) are inverse covariate units and the penalty scale must compensate; the source does not state a standardization rule, which is a reproducibility gray zone.

The partial likelihood is
\[
pL(\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}
\left(
\frac{\exp(x_i\beta)}
{\sum_{l\in R(Y_i)}\exp(x_l\beta)}
\right)^{\delta_i},
\]
where \(R(Y_i)=\{l:Y_l\ge y_i\}\) is the risk set and \(\delta_i\) is the censoring indicator [PAGE 4]. The partial log-likelihood \(pl(\beta)\) is dimensionless; under natural log its information unit is nats. The paper’s regularized objectives are [PAGE 4]:
\[
\arg\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p} pl(\beta)-\lambda\|\beta\|_0
\quad \text{(Best Subset Selection)},
\]
\[
\arg\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p} pl(\beta)-\lambda\|\beta\|_1
\quad \text{(Lasso)},
\]
\[
\arg\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p} pl(\beta)-\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2
\quad \text{(Ridge)},
\]
\[
\arg\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p} pl(\beta)-\lambda\left((1-\alpha)\|\beta\|_2^2+\alpha\|\beta\|_1\right)
\quad \text{(Elastic Net)},
\]
\[
\arg\max_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^p} pl(\beta)-\lambda\sum_{g=1}^{G}\|\beta_g\|_2
\quad \text{(Group Lasso)}.
\]
The tuning parameter satisfies \(\lambda\ge 0\); Elastic Net uses \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), and the paper fixes \(\alpha=0.5\) [PAGE 5]. The penalty terms are dimensionless when \(\beta\) is dimensionless. The breakdown regimes are exact: \(\lambda=0\) gives no regularization; \(\lambda\to\infty\) drives all coefficients toward zero and yields the null model; if \(p\) is large relative to events, cross-validation can select noise; with 75 percent censoring and few injuries, a fold can contain no events, making the partial likelihood and tuning unstable; complete separation or collinearity can make unpenalized estimates nonfinite.

The shared frailty hazard is
\[
\lambda_{jk}(t\mid\alpha_k,x_{jk})
=\alpha_k\lambda_0(t)\exp(x_{jk}\beta)
=\lambda_0(t)\exp(x_{jk}\beta+z_{jk}b_k),
\]
with \(j=1,\dots,n_k\), \(k=1,\dots,K\), \(b_k=\ln(\alpha_k)\), and \(z_{jk}=1\) when observation \(j\) belongs to player \(k\) and 0 otherwise [PAGE 6]. Here \(\alpha_k\) is a dimensionless multiplicative frailty, \(b_k\) is dimensionless, and \(\lambda_0(t)\) remains in \(\mathrm{min}^{-1}\). The paper assumes a log-normal frailty, i.e. Gaussian \(b_k=\ln(\alpha_k)\) [PAGE 6]. The marginal survival function is
\[
S(t\mid x)
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S(t\mid b,x)g(b)\,db
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S_0(t)^{\exp(X\beta+Zb)}g(b)\,db,
\]
where \(g\) is the Gaussian density [PAGE 6]. This integral is generally not closed-form, so numerical integration or Laplace approximation is required. Breakdown occurs when the frailty variance approaches zero: the model collapses to ordinary Cox and the frailty parameter becomes weakly identified. At the opposite boundary, a very large frailty variance makes numerical integration unstable. The paper also notes that frailty estimation “is likely to undergo convergence problems when the number of parameters to be estimated considerably increases” [PAGE 2].

Predictive performance uses the Brier score:
\[
BS(t\mid \hat S(t\mid x))
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(0-\hat S(t\mid x_i))^2}{\hat G(t_i)}, & t_i\le t,\ \delta_i=1,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{(1-\hat S(t\mid x_i))^2}{\hat G(t)}, & t_i>t,\\[6pt]
0, & t_i\le t,\ \delta_i=0,
\end{cases}
\]
where \(G(t)=P(C<t)\) is the censoring distribution and IPCW adjusts for censoring [PAGE 6]. The BS is a squared-probability error, dimensionless, with smaller values better. The integrated Brier score is
\[
IBS(BS(t),\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^\tau BS(u,\hat S)\,du,
\]
with \(\tau=t_{\max}\) or \(0<\tau<t_{\max}\) [PAGE 7]. The IBS is dimensionless when \(\tau\) is in minutes. Exact singularity regimes: \(\tau=0\) makes the IBS undefined; \(\hat G(t)=0\) or near zero makes IPCW weights infinite or explosive; if \(\tau\) extends beyond the follow-up where the censoring survival estimate reaches zero, the IBS is not estimable. The paper’s use of bootstrap .632+ is an internal correction because external validation data are lacking [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is clear. The Cox proportional hazards model comes from Cox’s 1972 regression model for life-tables and 1975 partial likelihood [PAGE 4; PAGE 16 references]. Penalized selection descends from Ridge regression, Lasso, Elastic Net, Group Lasso, and likelihood-based boosting; the paper cites Hoerl and Kennard, Tibshirani, Zou and Hastie, Yuan and Lin, and Bühlmann and Hothorn [PAGE 4; PAGE 16 references]. Best subset selection is represented by the BeSS package and Wen et al. [PAGE 4; PAGE 19 reference]. Frailty models descend from McGilchrist and Aisbett, Hougaard, and Ripatti and Palmgren [PAGE 5; PAGE 18 references]. Recurrent-event gap-time modeling descends from Kelly and Lim and from Ullah, Gabbett, and Finch [PAGE 5; PAGE 18 references]. Sports-injury time-to-event analysis is framed by Nielsen et al. and by the critical screening review of Bahr, whose title states that screening tests to predict injury “do not work—and probably never will” [PAGE 16 reference list]. That lineage matters retrocausally: later regularization and frailty choices reinterpret earlier screening variables as candidate predictors, but the earlier screening literature already warns that a single preseason test has weak predictive validity. The paper’s own result that BeSS selects only 2 of 28 variables [PAGE 10] is consistent with that caution, not with a triumphant screening panel.

The multi-week traps are structural. First, screening is measured only three times, and “the information recorded on the third series of screening tests was not used, since the players’ follow-up ended at that moment” [PAGE 3]. Thus, a variable measured in the first test can be treated as fixed for many weeks until mid-season, even though injury susceptibility and training load change. Second, the time origin is reset at mid-season after new covariates are collected [PAGE 4]. This is statistically sensible but creates a discontinuity: pre-reset and post-reset intervals are not on one uninterrupted clock. Third, the gap-time approach restarts the risk interval after full recovery [PAGE 5]. A player who is injured, rehabilitates, and returns contributes multiple intervals; if the analyst instead uses calendar time from season start, the risk sets and recurrence dependence are misspecified. Fourth, the paper’s simulation fixes 75 percent censoring and \(T_{\max}=4000\), with 100 simulated data sets and 500 knots [PAGE 9]. Those choices stress-test small event counts, but they also mean that “multi-week” behavior is represented only through the generated hazard and interval structure, not through measured weather, surface, travel, or training-load time series. Fifth, the real-data analysis lacks external validation; the .632+ bootstrap is internal [PAGE 7]. The retrocausal trap is to let a later sparse model retroactively validate an earlier screening protocol. The correct genealogy is reversed: the screening protocol supplies candidate covariates; the recurrent-frailty model tests whether a small subset survives; the result is a hypothesis-generating workflow, not a validated prevention program.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when the medical staff or analyst faces a small number of injuries and reaches for the most recent abnormal screening value, the most familiar asymmetry variable, or a dense model that appears to “use all the data.” The paper’s own warning is the counter-trap: “as statisticians it is important to convey to medical service in a professional sports team that despite obtaining a large number of screening tests, the small sample size of individuals and lower-limb injury events limit the usefulness for predicting the risk of injury” [PAGE 14]. The crisis habit is to over-personalize: “this player’s asymmetry is high, therefore this player is at high risk now.” The counter-trap is to model the player as a recurrent process with unobserved frailty, to prefer sparse and interpretable selection when the sample is small, and to report population-averaged predictions for new players rather than pretending to know an individual’s latent frailty. The paper states that the marginal survival approach “includes predictions for new players which have not been part of the data used to fit the model,” whereas the conditional approach “do not allow estimating predictions of new players –in this case player-specific predictions– since their frailties are unknown” [PAGE 6]. That is the epistemic boundary.

The clock is the third agent in the recursive loop. The modeler’s clock is exposure minutes; the clinician’s clock is the training week and return-to-play decision; the player’s clock is recovery and re-injury. The paper synchronizes them with the gap-time formulation: “a new risk interval is set every time the player has totally recovered from a sustained injury and starts to train” [PAGE 5]. If the clinician thinks in calendar weeks while the model thinks in cumulative exposure minutes, the risk set is wrong. If the analyst resets at mid-season but the coach does not, the covariate update and the training plan diverge. If the player returns before full recovery, the gap-time assumption is violated. The counter-trap is therefore not only statistical; it is operational. Use sparse selection to avoid overfitting, use frailty to absorb unobserved susceptibility, use the marginal survival curve for new-player communication, and explicitly log the clock reset. The paper’s conclusion reinforces this: “a major key to increase predictive performance of the models and to obtain more accurate predictions of players’ injuries lies in the size of available data” [PAGE 16]. The recursive theory of mind is that the modeler must model the clinician’s decision rule, the clinician must model the player’s recovery state, and both must respect the same exposure clock; otherwise the model becomes a crisis habit dressed as precision.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
