1. KCF tracking operator. KCF is a kernelized ridge-regression tracker. In standard form,
\[
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(K+\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{y},
\]
with FFT-domain solution
\[
\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{y}}}{\hat{\mathbf{k}}^{xx}+\lambda}.
\]
Here \(\mathbf{y}\) is a dimensionless regression target, \(K\) is a kernel matrix in feature-inner-product units, \(\lambda\) has the same units as \(K\), and \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\) is dimensionless. Assumptions: circulant shifts, periodic boundary, fixed search window, stationary kernel, and target appearance that is locally learnable. Breakdown regimes: severe occlusion, scale change, out-of-plane rotation, illumination change, target leaving the window, and boundary artifacts. The source’s use is justified by speed and adaptivity: “KCF は学習データの収集に高速フーリエ変換（FFT）を用いており，他の逐次学習型追跡手法21）22）に比べて高速な学習が可能である．” [PAGE 3]. The source also reports per-frame update: “逐次学習型の追跡手法を導入し，ストーンをフレーム毎に再学習した．” [PAGE 3].

2. Planar homography operator for grounded objects. For a point on the plane \(Z=0\),
\[
s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}
=
H\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\1\end{bmatrix},
\]
where \(u,v\) are pixel coordinates, \(X,Y\) are meters, \(s\) is a dimensionless scale factor, and \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\). The inverse mapping is
\[
\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\1\end{bmatrix}
\propto H^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Assumptions: calibrated fixed camera, planar surface, negligible object thickness, and no lens distortion beyond calibration. \]
Here \(\lambda\) is in meters and \(F\) is an implicit surface with meter-consistent units. =
\arg\min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}
\sum_{c=1}^{4}
\left\|
\left(I-\mathbf{d}_c\mathbf{d}_c^{\top}\right)
\left(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{C}_c\right)

\]
The residual has units of \(\mathrm{m}^2\), and \(\mathbf{X}^{*}\) has units of \(\mathrm{m}\). <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive operator model has three competing loops: crisis habit regression, counter-trap inhibition, and the broadcast clock. Crisis habit regression is the tendency, under occlusion or appearance change, to fall back to a pre-trained detector or a stale appearance template. The source explicitly criticizes this habit: conventional pre-trained tracking generally does not update the classifier, so it cannot fully handle appearance change [PAGE 3]. Counter-trap inhibition is the opposite failure: updating too aggressively so that the brush, a player’s shadow, or another high-contrast object becomes part of the target model. The Bayesian form in the previous section shows why: when the likelihood is flat or multimodal, the posterior should be carried by the motion prior or by another camera, not by the newest appearance patch. The clock is the program-production deadline. The source says sports programs require analysis during or immediately after the attempt and that high speed and high accuracy must both be satisfied [PAGE 2]. KCF’s FFT learning is chosen partly for this clock [PAGE 3].

A recursive theory of mind is needed because the operator must model three observers simultaneously: the athlete whose body occludes the target, the broadcast editor who needs a CG overlay now, and the viewer who needs the overlay to be semantically faithful. The curling case models sweeping as a biomechanical occluder; the golf case models weather and shadow as appearance perturbations; the beach-volleyball case models the opposing team and the wind as physical context. The source’s beach-volleyball environment statement is: “ビーチバレーは屋外で行われるため，屋内のバレーボール競技の試合環境に“光”，“砂”，“風”の影響が加わる．” [PAGE 3]. The paper adds: “特に浜風によるボール軌道の変化が大きく勝負を分ける．” [PAGE 4]. The operator’s correct mental model is therefore not “track the ball” but “track the ball while deciding whether the current evidence is target, occluder, shadow, or weather-induced appearance change.”

The counter-trap policy is a gated update: accept a new appearance model only when the detection is consistent with motion, scale, color-area prefiltering, and, where available, another camera. The source uses color and area prefiltering before KCF for curling [PAGE 3], and uses four cameras so that one camera’s shadow occlusion can be overridden by another camera’s coordinates: “特定のカメラでボールが選手の影に隠れるシーンもあったが，他のカメラ映像から取得した画像座標を利用し，ボールの3次元位置を全フレームで算出できた．” [PAGE 5]. That is the counter-trap: do not let a single-view crisis rewrite the target identity.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer has veto power over every algorithmic claim. In curling, the planar homography is valid only under the zero-height approximation. The source states that the stone is grounded and its thickness can be ignored, then projects 2D image coordinates to the real court plane [PAGE 2]. If the stone is lifted, or if the ice surface is not the calibrated plane, the operator must be vetoed and replaced by a 3D ray or stereo method. In golf, the green has “アンジュレーション” and the paper uses a laser range sensor to build a 3D model, then intersects the camera-to-ball ray with that model [PAGE 3]. A flat-plane homography is therefore vetoed by the physical surface. In beach volleyball, the ball is airborne, so the zero-height approximation is explicitly impossible [PAGE 4]; stereo triangulation is required. This is a clean cross-layer proof: the object’s height state determines the measurement operator.

The biomechanical layer vetoes purely appearance-based tracking during occlusion. Sweeping puts brushes in front of the stone; players’ bodies and shadows put occluders in front of the ball. The source reports that sweeping causes frequent occlusion and that a single classifier is insufficient [PAGE 3]. The paper’s response is per-frame relearning and, for beach volleyball, multi-camera redundancy [PAGE 3–5]. The veto is: if the occluder shares color, area, or motion with the target, a single-view appearance update is not sufficient evidence. The system must use motion continuity, prefiltering, or another view.

The weather layer vetoes static appearance models. Outdoor golf has weather and shadow changes; beach volleyball has “光”，“砂”，“風” [PAGE 3]. Illumination changes alter image features, and wind changes the ball trajectory itself. The source’s golf and beach-volleyball systems both use sequential learning to handle appearance change [PAGE 3–5]. But weather also vetoes the claim that a trajectory is purely a tracking artifact: wind can physically bend the ball, so a visually plausible CG path must be checked against the physical scene. The source uses 3D CG to show wind-induced ball changes [PAGE 5].

The clock layer vetoes unbounded computation. The broadcast requirement is analysis during or immediately after the attempt [PAGE 2]. KCF is selected for FFT-based speed [PAGE 3]. A global bundle adjustment over all frames may be accurate but late; it is vetoed by the program clock unless run offline. The correct adjudication is therefore: physical layer first, biomechanical occlusion second, weather third, clock fourth. If the physical layer is violated, no amount of tracking accuracy can repair the 3D coordinate. If the clock is violated, the correct coordinate arrives after the broadcast window and is operationally invalid.

\]
in meters. Veto condition: if \(\delta\) exceeds the allowed overlay error, the homography operator is invalid. Verifiable prediction: reconstruction error grows monotonically with \(Z\) and with shallower \(\theta\), and the homography fails even when 2D tracking is perfect. This directly tests the source’s zero-height approximation for curling [PAGE 2] and its rejection of that approximation for beach volleyball [PAGE 4].
