The paper contains no SI mechanical units. Its units are baseball-statistical units: runs per team-season, earned runs per nine innings, percentages on a 0–100 scale, counts such as double plays, and dimensionless variance proportions \(R^2\). \[
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X^\top\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^\top \mathbf y,

R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}},

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage begins with Bill James and the word itself: “The word sabermetrics comes from an acronym of the Society of American Baseball Research and represents an analysis of the game of baseball using detailed performance data, rather than qualitative methods based on such numbers as a player’s height and weight, ‘look on his face,’ and relatively simple statistics such as batting average” [PAGE 1]. The paper’s historical hinge is James’s claim that “the purpose of a team’s offense is not to compile a high batting average, but to score more runs!” [PAGE 1]. From that hinge come OBP, SLG, OPS, and then wOBA; the paper says wOBA weights OBP and SLG and that “on-base percentage should get more weight than slugging percentage” [PAGE 3]. The Moneyball branch adds Beane’s critique of BA, the rejection of stolen-base volume without caught-stealing cost, and the claim that “you can’t steal first base” [PAGE 3]. The defensive branch runs through FRAA, UZR, and DRS: the paper says UZR uses “play-by-play data recorded by Baseball Info Solutions” and accounts for “temperature and ballpark played in” [PAGE 4]. The pitching branch runs through WHIP, K/9, FIP, and xFIP; FIP is “created using only a pitcher’s strikeouts, walks given up, home runs allowed, and innings pitched, plus a constant” [PAGE 4], and the paper notes “the extremely small influence pitchers have on BABIP” [PAGE 4]. Later DRS is described as “how many more or fewer successful plays a defensive player will make than league average” [PAGE 8].

Retrocausal traps. First, later constructs are projected backward as if they were stable across the whole window. UZR was developed in 2002, and the paper admits it “is constantly being updated, so data from the first several years in the study may not have been exactly the same measure” [PAGE 7]. That is a measurement-clock trap: a 2002 UZR value is not the same object as a 2010 UZR value. Second, wOBA and WHIP are treated as endpoints rather than genealogical intermediates; wOBA compresses OBP and power, while WHIP compresses hits and walks, so their high \(R^2\) values can hide the omitted components that later metrics separate. Third, the Moneyball narrative can create a selection illusion: the paper notes that proprietary team regressions exist and are not public [PAGE 5], so public-model success is not the same as front-office decision success. Fourth, multi-week and multi-season traps arise from sample windows: 300 team-years and 270 team-years are large enough to produce high in-sample \(R^2\), but not large enough to stabilize stepwise selection across eras, parks, weather, or metric revisions. Fifth, Goodhart pressure is built into the genealogy: once a metric is used to value players, player behavior and roster selection change the distribution that generated the coefficient. The paper’s own “partially confirmed” conclusion [PAGE 2] is therefore historically honest: the sabermetric lineage is powerful, but not a completed causal genealogy.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A lower-layer statistical coefficient can be vetoed by physical, biomechanical, or weather mechanisms when the coefficient is asked to do causal work that the measurement layer cannot support. Formally, suppose the true outcome is
\[
Y=\beta_0+\beta_jX_j+\gamma W+\varepsilon,
\]
where \(W\) is an omitted physical/weather factor. The fitted coefficient obeys
\[
\tilde\beta_j=\beta_j+\gamma\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_j,W)}{\operatorname{Var}(X_j)}.
\]
If \(W\) affects both \(X_j\) and \(Y\), the coefficient is biased even if its \(p\)-value is small. Veto proof requires a mechanism and a measurement gap, not merely suspicion.

The source supplies the measurement gap. UZR is said to “take into account data such as temperature and ballpark played in” [PAGE 4], but the paper also says FieldFX would “use several other factors (including humidity, precipitation, and length of the grass) that are not included at all in UZR” [PAGE 8]. Therefore any claim that UZR or Fielding% fully captures the physical environment is vetoed: humidity, precipitation, and grass length are explicitly outside the UZR layer. Fielding% is even weaker because it ignores range; the paper’s own inherited argument is that “errors are the same as hits as far as the defense is concerned” [PAGE 3], and a perfect fielding percentage with poor range can be worse than occasional errors with superior range [PAGE 3]. Biomechanically, range, arm strength, double-play turn speed, and route efficiency are not in Fielding%; a coefficient on Fielding% cannot be read as a biomechanical range effect.

The pitching layer has its own veto. The paper says pitchers have “extremely small influence on BABIP” [PAGE 4], so an ERA model that credits WHIP or FIP as pure pitching skill is physically overclaiming. Weather and park also affect HR/9 and batted-ball travel; if those factors are not stratified, the HR/9 coefficient is a mixture of pitcher skill, park, temperature, wind, and humidity. The double-play coefficient is physically conditional on base-runner state: a double play can occur only when at least one runner is on base [PAGE 7]. Thus a model that assigns a stable marginal effect to double plays without conditioning on base state is vetoed by the state-dependent mechanism. The correct adjudication is: use the regression as a descriptive in-sample compression, but veto causal interpretations that contradict the physical/measurement layers identified by the source itself.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
