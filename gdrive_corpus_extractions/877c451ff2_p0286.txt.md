(1) P(T_e = 1 | E_f = 1, daring = 1) > P(T_e = 1 | E_f = 1, daring = 0).

Let D ∈ {0,1} indicate whether the runner allows a tag (D = 1) or forces a throw (D = 0). With runners on first and second and a ball between third and shortstop [PAGE 1], the double-play veto is:
(2) If fielder seeks double play, then D ≠ 1; i.e., the runner must not allow the fielder to touch him with the ball. Allowing a touch yields P(out) = 1 locally; forcing a throw yields P(out) = 1 − P(T_e = 1).

Expected utility of forcing a throw:
(3) U_force = P(T_e = 1)·ΔU_advance − [1 − P(T_e = 1)]·C_out,
where C_out is the dimensionless utility loss of an out and ΔU_advance is the gain from reaching the next base. Advance is rational iff U_force > U_stay.

Pitcher irregularity from frequent throws to bases [PAGE 1]:
(4) σ_release = f(N_throws), with ∂σ_release/∂N_throws > 0, where σ_release is release-point standard deviation in meters [m] and N_throws is a dimensionless count.

Fly-ball tag-up feasibility from second to third [PAGE 1]:
(5) Advance is feasible iff t_h > t_r + t_a + t_throw(3rd) + ε_margin,
where t_h is ball hang time [s], t_r is runner reaction plus return-to-bag time [s] (≈0.15–0.25 s if toe is on bag; longer if off bag), t_a is runner advance time [s], and t_throw(3rd) = d_{1→3}/v_t + t_transfer [s]. With toe on bag, t_r ≈ t_reaction only.

Runner–fielder timing inequality:
(6) v_r·t_h > d_lead + d_advance for successful tag-up; otherwise P(out) → 1.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The Camp 1900 passage [PAGE 1] sits in the "scientific baseball" instructional lineage, predating modern sabermetrics but encoding the same deep structure: base-running value is manufactured by manipulating defensive decisions under uncertainty. The conditional error-propagation principle from [PAGE 1] anticipates contemporary defensive metrics (e.g., DRS, UZR) by treating fielder error state as a persistent Markov variable: an error on play n elevates the hazard of an error on play n+1 under baserunner pressure. The tag-up reasoning prefigures run-expectancy matrices (RE24) and the stolen-base break-even calculation, where advancing is justified only when expected runs gained exceed expected runs lost from an out. The pitcher-throw-to-bases insight maps directly onto modern pace-of-play interventions: pitch clocks and pickoff limits exist partly because excessive throws to bases degrade pitcher rhythm and mechanics, exactly the "irregular pitching" described in [PAGE 1].

Multi-week traps: Across a series or week, fielders who commit an early error tend toward regression in subsequent error rates, yet baserunners may overapply the "daring chance" rule after observing a single error—a reverse hot-hand fallacy. Pitchers subjected to repeated throw-overs across multiple games accumulate mechanical fatigue, but baserunners who steal purely on throw-over frequency ignore catcher pop-time and pitcher time-to-plate, causing systematic overaggression. Conversely, runners who become excessively conservative after one failed advance exhibit availability bias, forgoing high-probability tag-ups. The source's prescription to keep the toe "just touching the bag" [PAGE 1] functions as a commitment device against both traps: it preserves the option to advance while capping exposure to a pickoff or a throw behind the runner.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: A fielder who has just dropped or fumbled a ball [PAGE 1] regresses toward a low-skill, consciously monitored motor program under pressure, disrupting the automatic timing of the throw (choking). The baserunner models this recursively: I run daringly because I believe the fielder believes I will run daringly, thereby increasing his throwing-error probability. The fielder may anticipate this and hold the ball, inducing the runner to break early (a counter-trap), then throw to the advancing base. The runner's toe-on-bag stance [PAGE 1] is a partial solution: it commits to a legal tag-up position while preserving a minimal lead, bounding the downside of the counter-trap.

Clock layer: Baseball has no game clock, but a tempo clock operates via inter-pitch intervals, catcher throw-downs, and pickoff attempts. Frequent throws to bases consume this temporal budget and degrade release regularity, favoring the batsman and troubling the catcher [PAGE 1]. The runner's decision is therefore nested: runner models fielder models pitcher models catcher, with physical clock variables (hang time, throw time, reaction time) bounding each recursion. If any layer's time budget is exceeded, the recursion collapses into an out.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
An advance is permitted only if C = C_phys ∧ C_bio ∧ C_weather ∧ C_info; any FALSE returns VETO.

C_phys (physics): t_throw + t_receive < t_run. With t_throw = d_t/v_t [s], d_t from first to third ≈ 38–40 m [m], and v_t for an infield throw ≈ 25–30 m/s [m/s]. If t_throw ≥ t_run, VETO.

C_bio (biomechanics): On the foul fly over first [PAGE 1], the first baseman runs away from the plate with his back turned toward third, requiring deceleration, plant, pivot, and throw. Reorientation time Δt_reo [s] is added to t_throw. Footing (grass vs. dirt) alters first-step acceleration [m/s²]. If Δt_reo + t_throw exceeds the runner's lead window, VETO. A throw off the back foot while moving away from target lowers shoulder–elbow angular velocity [rad/s] and increases throw variance σ_t [m].

C_weather (environment): Wind drift on a high foul fly [m/s lateral] shifts the catch point and degrades hang-time predictability; twilight or stadium lighting reduces fielder-tracking contrast; temperature affects ball grip and coefficient of restitution. If wind carries the ball into the crowd or creates an unpredictable hop, C_weather = FALSE.

C_info (information): The runner sees the catch immediately; the fielder must secure, locate the target, and throw. If mutual information I(fielder_state; runner_action) is low—e.g., the fielder is already positioned to throw—VETO. This information asymmetry is the core mechanism in [PAGE 1] when the first baseman's back is turned to third.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
