<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The selections on [PAGE 51] descend from the Walter Camp All-America tradition institutionalized in the 1880s–1890s and annually reprinted in Spalding's Official Foot Ball Guide. Regional all-state and all-scholastic teams were the downstream distributive layer: college athletic directors, sporting editors, and local coaches converted subjective season-long observation into published rosters. In 1915, football operated under single-platoon rules with minimal protective equipment (leather helmets, no face masks), so every listed player was implicitly a two-way athlete; offensive half-backs and full-backs also handled defensive backfield duties and kicking.

Multi-week traps embedded in this lineage: (1) recency bias, because selectors convened after the season and weighted late-season performance over early-season form; (2) prestige cascade, whereby Dartmouth, Williams, Brown, and Whitewater received outsized representation because selector networks overlapped with those programs, as seen on [PAGE 51] where Dartmouth supplies Telfer, McAuliffe, Spears, Ghee, and Whitney; (3) geographic echo-chamber capture, because Newark Sunday Call produced four of the twelve teams, over-weighting Rahway, Rutherford, and Stevens School relative to the rest of New Jersey. The genealogical trap for a modern reader is assuming that an all-eleven label measures intrinsic athletic merit; historically it measures selector access, institutional visibility, and end-of-season narrative salience. A player omitted from [PAGE 51] may have been superior but played for a school outside the selector's newspaper circulation or coaching acquaintance, producing a false-negative selection error that persists in the historical record.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Level 0: the athlete performs on the field. Level 1: the selector observes the athlete and records a name. Level 2: the selector anticipates what other selectors will choose, producing convergence on "name" schools such as Dartmouth and Whitewater because those picks are socially defensible. Level 3: the selector anticipates Level 2 convergence and may counter-select obscure players from Platteville, Hendrix, Henderson-Brown, or River Falls to differentiate the roster or to signal local knowledge.

Crisis habit regression: when source data is degraded—as on [PAGE 51] with OCR-corrupted names ("Mitchelì," "Jehne Hopkins," "Tusculumi," "quarter-hack") and missing institutions ("Hoeltzel," "Bruce Jackson")—selectors and later analysts regress to familiar elite names rather than risking unverifiable obscure picks. The counter-trap is the reverse overcorrection: assuming every OCR-corrupted or institution-less name is a hidden gem. Proper recursive updating weights the institutional repetition signal (Whitewater five times, Dartmouth five times, Newark Sunday Call four teams) as evidence of selector access and newspaper circulation, not necessarily as evidence of player superiority.

Clock structure: selections on [PAGE 51] were published post-season, so early-season injuries, weather-interrupted games, and September form decay from memory by selection time. Memory decay is approximately exponential in week-lag; a dominant October performance carries less selection weight than a November reputation. Recursive theory of mind therefore implies that the roster distribution reflects not who played best across the full season, but who was most legible to the selector network at the moment of publication.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Weather/geography layer: New England games occurred in late-autumn cold; Arkansas high-school games in heat and humidity; East Tennessee teams traveled mountainous terrain.
