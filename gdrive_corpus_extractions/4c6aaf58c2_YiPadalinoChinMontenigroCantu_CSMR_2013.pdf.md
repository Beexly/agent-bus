<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage in the source runs from Martland’s 1928 “punch drunk” description of boxers to Millspaugh’s 1937 “dementia pugilistica,” then to the 1960s use of “chronic traumatic encephalopathy” for neurologic deterioration after repetitive traumatic brain injury [PAGE 1–PAGE 2]. The modern histopathological lineage begins with Omalu et al. and the 2005 report in a former NFL player, followed by Boston University’s Center for the Study of CTE registry with “more than 500 affected former athletes” [PAGE 1, PAGE 3]. This genealogy is retrocausal in the precise sense that later postmortem tau and TDP-43 findings are projected backward onto earlier blows. The source warns against a simple backward arithmetic: “CTE is not a result of the accumulation of prior injuries but rather progressive neuronal dysfunction and death” [PAGE 2], and “it may arise in patients who had no prior symptomatology” [PAGE 2]. Therefore a later diagnosis does not license the inference that every earlier concussion was a CTE step.

Multi-week traps appear at the acute-chronic boundary. The source reports MRS findings in concussed athletes: “Concussed athletes experienced acute neurometabolic impairment in prefrontal and motor cortices, and while there was some recovery in the chronic phase, a level of impairment remained in the motor cortices” [PAGE 3]. A multi-week observer may see symptom resolution and wrongly infer full biological resolution; the source’s chronic-phase residual impairment blocks that inference. A second multi-week trap is PCS-to-CTE overlap: the source says CTE “may arise in individuals who had complete resolution of PCS symptoms” and that “some cases involve overlap, more specifically subsiding PCS symptoms with progressively worsening CTE symptoms” [PAGE 2]. A third trap is return-to-play timing: SCAT2 is validated for assessment, but “return-to-play decisions are made based on these findings” [PAGE 4], not on a CTE biomarker. A fourth trap is the helmet genealogy: protective equipment may reduce severe traumatic brain injury and fatal impact-related injury, but “there is no evidence that helmets actually reduce the incidence of concussion” [PAGE 3], and protective gear “may actually lead to more aggressive play” [PAGE 3]. The historical lesson is not that prevention is useless; it is that visible equipment is not the same as validated incidence reduction. The source also notes boxing career length fell “down from 19 to 5 years” and matches “from an average of 336 to 13” [PAGE 3], which is a structural exposure-reduction mechanism, not a helmet mechanism. Genealogically, the strongest source-faithful claim is that repetitive head trauma is a constant while the exact mechanism remains unresolved: “repetitive head trauma is a constant” [PAGE 1], yet the mechanism “has yet to be elucidated” [PAGE 2].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the tendency, under uncertainty, to substitute a visible ritual for a causal model. In this source, the ritual is helmet mandate or sideline SCAT2 use; the causal model requires exposure history, latency, and clinical trajectory. The source directly weakens the helmet ritual: “there is no evidence that helmets actually reduce the incidence of concussion” [PAGE 3]. It also weakens impact-magnitude ritual: “Broglio et al. (7) found no significant correlation between impact and post-concussive outcome” [PAGE 3]. The recursive agent should therefore ask: is the claim about severe traumatic brain injury, about concussion incidence, or about CTE? These are different layers. A helmet may reduce fatal impact-related injury while not reducing concussion incidence, and neither result establishes a CTE prevention effect.

The counter-trap is overcorrection into fatalism: “all contact sport causes CTE” or “a single hit guarantees CTE.” The source supports a single-TBI possibility: “even a single TBI could lead to both clinical and histopathological findings consistent with CTE” [PAGE 2]. But possibility is not inevitability, and the same source says CTE may arise without prior symptomatology and may present years after exposure [PAGE 2]. The counter-trap also appears in APOE reasoning: genetic testing “does not have a clear role in determining risk of developing CTE” [PAGE 3], and the Boston University work “has not confirmed the genetic APOE link thus far” [PAGE 2]. A recursive theory of mind must model the media’s selection bias: famous athlete deaths raise awareness, but the source’s registry is not a population cohort. The clock operator is the antidote to both habits. The clock tracks time since exposure, midlife presentation, latency, and acute-versus-chronic recovery. The source’s clock statements are explicit: patients “usually present in midlife and several years after end of exposure” [PAGE 2]; chronic-phase MRS shows residual motor-cortex impairment [PAGE 3]; and ERP “is not able to predict those who will eventually develop CTE before it occurs” [PAGE 4]. Thus the correct recursive stance is neither “helmet solves it” nor “CTE is destiny,” but a time-indexed, layer-specific uncertainty statement: exposure reduction lowers dose, clinical assessment manages current injury, and definitive CTE adjudication remains postmortem until validated in vivo markers exist.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

A physical dose claim may describe exposure, but it cannot alone establish CTE.

Biomechanical-layer veto proof. A claim that helmets reduce concussion incidence is vetoed as unsupported: “there is no evidence that helmets actually reduce the incidence of concussion” [PAGE 3]. A claim that protective gear reduces severe traumatic brain injury or fatal impact-related injury is allowed only if separated from concussion incidence, because the source says severe traumatic brain injury and fatal impact-related injuries “may be reduced with the protective gear” [PAGE 3]. A claim that protective gear cannot increase risk is also vetoed, because “the use of protective gear may actually lead to more aggressive play and may result in an increased incidence of concussion” [PAGE 3].

Clinical-layer veto proof. A claim that SCAT2 predicts future CTE is vetoed. SCAT2 is “currently in wide use and is a validated assessment tool” [PAGE 3], and return-to-play decisions use it [PAGE 4], but ERP and neuropsychological testing “is not able to predict those who will eventually develop CTE before it occurs” [PAGE 4]. A claim that symptom resolution equals biological resolution is vetoed by the chronic-phase MRS residual impairment [PAGE 3].

Histopathological-layer veto proof. A definitive CTE claim in a living person is vetoed unless a validated in vivo marker exists, because “the definitive diagnosis of CTE relies on a post-mortem examination” [PAGE 1] and “there is no readily available imaging modality or biochemical marker” that accurately and reproducibly detects early concussion or CTE development [PAGE 4]. Weather-layer veto proof. The supplied source contains no meteorological variable, no weather exposure metric, and no weather-CTE analysis. Any weather-based causal claim is therefore inadmissible under source-only adjudication. The correct output is a veto, not a speculative mechanism. Cross-layer adjudication succeeds only when exposure, clinical trajectory, and where definitive histopathology align; a weather-only, helmet-only, impact-only, or SCAT2-only claim fails the predicate established in the source operators.
