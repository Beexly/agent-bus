The source artifact is a human-computer interaction paper about Surveyor, an exploration assistance system for blind and low vision players. The central claim is not that Surveyor provides more raw navigation data than prior tools, but that it changes the epistemic structure of play: it lets the player maintain an internal model of where discovery remains possible. The paper states that Surveyor is “an exploration assistance tool designed to foster a sense of discovery in BLV players” [PAGE 1]. This is important because the artifact is not merely a map replacement; it is an attempt to preserve uncertainty as a designed resource. The authors contrast this with prior accessibility patterns where “the presentation of worlds is reduced to simplistic lists and grids and players are guided directly from landmark to landmark” [PAGE 2]. That contrast gives the paper its main hypothesis: direct guidance can reduce discovery, while exploratory tracking can restore agency.

The formal reconstruction begins by separating four layers: physical input, perceptual rendering, spatial-state tracking, and player decision-making. The physical input layer is a standard game controller. The perceptual rendering layer is spatialized audio, including menu announcements, landmark announcements, and looping beacons. The spatial-state layer is an internal matrix representation of cells marked explored, obstruction, or unknown. The decision layer is the player’s choice to walk, scan with the right stick, query menus, or activate beacons. The paper explicitly describes the scanning behavior: “When the player tilts the right stick in any direction, the game will announce the name of the first item that lies in that direction within their current line-of-sight” [PAGE 5]. It also describes the matrix: “Cells traversed by the player or covered during a sweep of the right stick are labeled as ‘explored.’” [PAGE 5]. These two mechanisms are coupled: scanning changes both what is announced and what the system marks as seen.

A gray-zone audit reveals several ambiguities. First, the matrix is internal and hidden: “this matrix is solely used as Surveyor’s underlying data structure and is not exposed to the user” [PAGE 5]. Therefore, the player experiences only an abstraction of the matrix, mediated by audio. Second, “explored” has two sources: physical traversal and right-stick coverage. This means exploration is not purely locomotion; it is also attentional scanning. Third, unknown regions are defined relationally, as cells beyond obstructions or corners. The paper calls the boundary between seen and unseen the “cusp of the unknown” [PAGE 5]. Fourth, the menu does not reveal the full geometry; it announces patches with distance and direction, for example “Unexplored patch, 5 meters to your 3 o’clock.” [PAGE 6]. This creates a compressed representation: many distinct spatial configurations can yield the same audio phrase.

The evaluation adds another layer of interpretation. Nine participants completed levels with Surveyor, a simple audio menu, and a shockwave utility [PAGE 8]. The authors counterbalanced tool order using a Latin square to reduce order effects [PAGE 8]. The outcome metrics include completion time, perceived fun, restrictiveness, path traces, and counts of optional console discoveries. Surveyor produced longer completion times, with a reported median of 1003 seconds [PAGE 11], but it also produced strong agency reports: “Surveyor granted participants a strong sense of agency.” [PAGE 9]. The audio menu was faster but could feel “sterile” and less surprising [PAGE 12]. The shockwave was fastest but produced little exploration; “With the shockwave, however, participants activated a median of zero consoles.” [PAGE 12]. This means speed and discovery can dissociate. The paper’s key finding is that discovery is not merely a function of reaching an objective; it depends on the player’s ability to model the unobserved world and act upon that model.

The most important theoretical risk is treating the system as a simple navigation aid. If the reward function only measures completion time, Surveyor may appear inefficient. If the reward function only measures agency, the system may underweight learnability. The paper itself acknowledges this tension: some participants found Surveyor cumbersome because it “forces you to feel out the rest of the room” [PAGE 10]. Thus, the formal analysis must preserve both the informational benefit of exploration and the possible cognitive burden of choice. The correct unit of analysis is not merely distance traveled, but reduction in uncertainty, player-controlled attention, and the preservation of surprise.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source describes a spatial assistance system rather than a physical dynamical system. Nevertheless, the described behavior can be formalized with explicit operators, assumptions, units, and breakdown regimes. The equations below are derived from the paper’s reported mechanics and evaluation metrics.

1. Spatial state operator

Let a room or local area be represented as a bounded planar region:
\[
A \subset \mathbb{R}^2.
\]
Partition \(A\) into discrete cells:
\[
C = \{c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_N\}.
\]
At time \(t\), each cell has a state:
\[
s_t(c_i) \in \{U, E, O\},
\]
where \(U\) denotes unknown, \(E\) denotes explored, and \(O\) denotes obstruction. The paper states: “Cells traversed by the player or covered during a sweep of the right stick are labeled as ‘explored.’” [PAGE 5]. It also states: “If the scanning utility encounters an obstruction, such as a wall or large object, cells corresponding to the obstruction will be marked as ‘obstructions.’” [PAGE 5]. Areas outside these marked cells “remain unknown to Surveyor and, consequently, the player” [PAGE 5].

The explored set evolves as:
\[
E_t = E_{t-1} \cup \operatorname{Trav}(p_{0:t}) \cup \operatorname{Scan}(p_t, \Theta_t),
\]
where \(p_t\) is the player position at time \(t\), \(\operatorname{Trav}(p_{0:t})\) is the set of cells traversed by movement, and \(\operatorname{Scan}(p_t, \Theta_t)\) is the set of cells covered by right-stick scanning directions \(\Theta_t\). The unknown set is:
\[
U_t = C \setminus (E_t \cup O_t).
\]
Units: cells are dimensionless spatial indices; position \(p_t\) is in meters; time \(t\) is in seconds.

Assumption: the world can be represented as a two-dimensional or top-down projected grid. This is consistent with the paper’s description of matrix-based rooms and patches [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: the operator fails when verticality, elevation, or non-planar geometry carries task-relevant meaning, because a planar cell state cannot distinguish stacked spaces.

2. Directional scanning operator

The right-stick scan is a directional visibility operator. Let \(\theta \in [-\pi, \pi)\) be a stick direction and let
\[
u(\theta) = (\cos \theta, \sin \theta).
\]
The ray from player position \(p_t\) is:
\[
\rho(p_t, \theta) = \{p_t + r u(\theta) : r \ge 0\}.
\]
The paper says the game announces “the name of the first item that lies in that direction within their current line-of-sight” [PAGE 5]. Let \(I\) be the set of items, with item locations \(l_i \in \mathbb{R}^2\). A simplified visible-item operator is:
\[
V_t(\theta) = \{i \in I : \operatorname{dist}(p_t, l_i) \le r_i,\ \angle(l_i - p_t, u(\theta)) \le \alpha_i,\ \rho(p_t, \theta) \cap O_t = \emptyset\}.
\]
If multiple items satisfy the condition, the system selects the nearest eligible item. Units: \(r_i\) is meters; \(\alpha_i\) is radians; direction is relative to the player’s heading.

Breakdown regimes:
- If no item satisfies the condition, the scan yields no announcement.
- If an obstruction blocks the ray, the item is not visible.
- If the player is inside an obstruction or facing a non-geometric object class, the operator is undefined.
- If the matrix is coarse, multiple real-world objects may collapse into one cell and become indistinguishable.

3. Patch boundary operator

The system highlights unexplored patches. A patch boundary can be defined as unknown cells adjacent to explored cells:
\[
B_t = \{c_i \in U_t : \exists c_j \in E_t,\ \operatorname{adj}(c_i, c_j)\}.
\]
The paper states that Surveyor conveys “information about the boundaries separating ‘explored’ cells and unexplored patches” and that these boundaries represent the “cusp of the unknown” [PAGE 5].

For each patch \(b \in B_t\), define a representative point \(g(b)\). The distance announcement is:
\[
d_t(b) = \Vert p_t - g(b) \Vert_2,
\]
with units in meters. The relative direction is:
\[
\phi_t(b) = \operatorname{atan2}(g_y(b) - p_{t,y},\ g_x(b) - p_{t,x}),
\]
with units in radians. Clock-face mapping may be written as:
\[
h_t(b) = \frac{\phi_t(b) \bmod 2\pi}{2\pi} \cdot 12,
\]
where \(h_t(b)\) is in clock hours. The paper gives the example: “Unexplored patch, 5 meters to your 3 o’clock.” [PAGE 6]. It also states: “Each patch is announced via spatialized sound emanating from the direction of the patch.” [PAGE 6].

An area is fully explored when:
\[
B_t = \emptyset.
\]
This matches the paper’s condition that an area is fully explored when no unexplored patches remain [PAGE 5].

Breakdown regimes:
- If multiple patches share similar distance and direction, the audio representation becomes ambiguous.
- If a patch centroid lies inside an obstruction, \(g(b)\) may be non-representative.
- If spatial audio rendering is degraded, direction estimation fails.
- If patch size is not announced, players may underestimate the effort required to explore it. The paper notes that unexplored patches were presented uniformly and that this could make choice overwhelming [PAGE 10].

4. Information-state and surprise operator

Surveyor operates under what the authors call a “zero-initial-knowledge principle”: “We designed Surveyor to operate on a zero-initial-knowledge principle, where neither the tool nor the player possesses information about what lies within unexplored areas until they are ‘seen.’” [PAGE 13].

If unknown cells are treated with a uniform prior, the entropy of the unknown state is:
\[
H_t = -\sum_{c_i \in U_t} \frac{1}{|U_t|} \log_2 \left(\frac{1}{|U_t|}\right)
= \log_2 |U_t|,
\]
with units in bits. If a scan converts \(k\) previously unknown cells into explored cells, the uncertainty reduction is:
\[
\Delta H_t = \log_2 |U_t| - \log_2 |U_{t+1}|.
\]
If no new cells are revealed, \(\Delta H_t = 0\). If \(U_t = \emptyset\), then \(H_t\) is conventionally treated as zero because no unknown state remains.

Assumption: all unknown cells are initially equiprobable. Breakdown regime: this entropy measure treats area as uncertainty but does not encode semantic value. A patch containing a key card and a patch containing only decorative crates may have equal entropy reduction but unequal player value.

5. Beacon detectability operator

The paper states that menu items can trigger “a looping audio beacon that will guide them toward the specified element” [PAGE 6]. For a beacon at distance \(r\), received sound level may be modeled as:
\[
L_{\text{rx}}(r) = L_0 - 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{r}{r_0}\right) - \alpha r,
\]
where \(L_0\) is reference level in decibels, \(r_0\) is reference distance in meters, and \(\alpha\) is absorption loss in decibels per meter. Detection occurs if:
\[
L_{\text{rx}}(r) - L_{\text{noise}} \ge D,
\]
where \(L_{\text{noise}}\) is ambient noise level in decibels and \(D\) is the required detection margin in decibels.

Breakdown regime: if hearing sensitivity is reduced or ambient noise is high, the inequality fails and the beacon becomes unusable. The paper acknowledges this limitation: “However, there may be tweaks we can make for users with more severe hearing impairments or for users with physical impediments that may prevent effective use of a standard game controller.” [PAGE 14].

6. Study metric operators

For participant \(j\) and tool \(k\), define:
\[
T_{j,k} \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}
\]
as level completion time in seconds. The paper reports that Surveyor had “the highest completion times (with a median of 1003 seconds)” [PAGE 11]. Define optional discovery count:
\[
C_{j,k} \in \{0,1,2,3\},
\]
where each level contained three optional log-entry consoles. The paper reports: “With the shockwave, however, participants activated a median of zero consoles.” [PAGE 12].

Define subjective restrictiveness:
\[
R_{j,k} \in \{1,2,3,4,5\},
\]
where lower values indicate less restrictiveness. The paper states that Surveyor “had the lowest median perceived restrictiveness out of all three tools with a median of 1” [PAGE 10]. Define fun:
\[
F_{j,k} \in \{1,2,3,4,5\}.
\]
The paper reports that Surveyor and the simple audio menu “received identical distributions of scores” for fun [PAGE 11].

These metrics must not be collapsed into a single scalar reward without stating assumptions. Completion time measures efficiency; console count measures optional discovery; restrictiveness measures perceived agency; fun measures affective evaluation. A formal multi-objective evaluation would keep these dimensions separate unless a documented weighting scheme is supplied.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s conceptual lineage runs through three historical currents: game-design theory, audio-game accessibility, and spatial-cognition assistance for blind users. In game-design theory, the authors rely on the MDA framework and the “8 Kinds of Fun,” where discovery is described as seeing games as “uncharted territory” to be explored and uncovered by players [PAGE 3]. This gives Surveyor its normative target: discovery is not a decorative feature but a core aesthetic category. The retro-causal implication is that if future designers optimize only for task completion, they regress to a narrower definition of accessibility and eliminate the very aesthetic that made the game meaningful.

The second lineage is audio games. The paper observes that many blind-accessible games represent worlds as “basic lists and grids” and that movement is “heavily guided” [PAGE 3]. It cites titles such as Terraformers, A Hero’s Call, and ShadowRine as examples of menu- or grid-based representation [PAGE 3]. This lineage explains why the authors include the simple audio menu as a status quo condition. The simple menu is not a strawman; it is a historically important accessibility pattern. However, the paper argues that such simplification can produce inequivalent experiences: the player can reach objects, but may not experience the world as a space to be discovered. The retro-causal trap is to treat accessibility as equivalent to simplification. If designers repeat that move, they recreate railroading.

The third lineage is mainstream accessibility features, especially The Last of Us enhanced listen mode. The paper notes that this mode “often spoils the locations of hidden objects and enemies” and that traversing the environment can be “a highly automated process” [PAGE 3]. The shockwave utility in the study emulates this pattern. It is fast, but it reduces environmental understanding. Participants activated optional consoles at a median of zero with the shockwave [PAGE 12], showing that the golden-path mode suppresses discovery. The historical lesson is that high-fidelity sensory pulses are not sufficient for discovery if they do not preserve the player’s need to interpret, search, and choose.

A fourth lineage comes from physical-world exploration. The paper discusses NavCog3, Microsoft Soundscape, and Seeing AI as tools that announce nearby points of interest or allow users to place beacons [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. It also cites work arguing that navigation systems should support cognitive-map formation rather than merely turn-by-turn compliance [PAGE 3]. This lineage matters because Surveyor’s patch-tracking resembles a cognitive-map scaffold: it does not tell the player every object location, but it marks the frontier of the unknown. The multi-week trap here is to import turn-by-turn logic into games. If a game tool behaves like a GPS route, it may reduce the player’s role from explorer to passenger.

The retro-causal genealogy also includes NavStick, the authors’ prior direction-scanning system. Surveyor adopts right-stick scanning but adds matrix-based patch tracking and menus because prior work indicated that scanning alone gives immediate surroundings but not a broader overview [PAGE 3]. The design is therefore a response to a known limitation: local looking is insufficient for global discovery. The recursive lesson is that each accessibility generation can solve a local problem while creating a new global problem. Surveyor solves the “look around” problem but introduces the problem of uniform patch presentation, where players may feel forced to explore even when they do not want to [PAGE 10]. Future work must therefore avoid treating Surveyor as a terminal solution. The lineage predicts that the next generation will need hints, reveal options, adaptive difficulty, and multimodal redundancy.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind in this paper involves three agents: the player modeling the system, the system modeling the player, and the designer modeling both models. The player asks: “What does Surveyor know about where I have not looked?” The system answers through patch announcements, landmark menus, and spatial audio. The player then asks: “Should I explore this patch now, or use a beacon to return later?” The designer must anticipate both the player’s uncertainty and the player’s emotional response to uncertainty.

A central crisis habit is the use of beacons when lost. The formative interview participant P1 says beacons are useful “to stop the player from getting lost or confused when trying to find something,” but warns: “But you can’t make it too easy on them, right? Otherwise the challenge is gone and they don’t want to play it anymore.” [PAGE 4]. This is a recursive trap: the more effective the beacon, the more likely it is to erode the challenge that motivated the beacon’s use in the first place. Surveyor’s design response is to make beacons available but not mandatory. The paper states: “Players, however, are not required to use beacons for navigation.” [PAGE 7]. This preserves player agency while still offering recovery from disorientation.

A second crisis pattern is choice overload. Surveyor gives players multiple tools: right-stick scanning, patch menus, landmark menus, area menus, and beacons. This supports design goal four: providing multiple approaches for understanding and navigating the environment [PAGE 5][PAGE 7]. However, P2 reports: “With Surveyor, it’s like, you spin the stick around, but you’re not getting everything — you’re only getting [what’s around you], and it just forces you to feel out the rest of the room.” [PAGE 10]. P6 similarly found the uniform patch presentation to require random choice and “hope for the best” [PAGE 10]. The recursive failure mode is that a tool intended to increase agency can become a burden if the player cannot estimate the value of unobserved regions.

The counter-trap is to “solve” choice overload by revealing everything. That would convert Surveyor into a simple list. Some participants liked that simplicity. P5 says the simple menu had “something to be said for just the simplicity of a single menu” [PAGE 10]. But P5 also says the simple menu felt “very, very sterile and very soulless” because it removed surprise [PAGE 12]. This is the second recursive trap: too little information produces sterility; too much information produces railroading. The desirable regime is calibrated uncertainty: enough information to act, but not enough to eliminate discovery.

The clock is another recursive variable. Surveyor produced longer completion times, with a median of 1003 seconds, compared with 585 seconds for the audio menu and 355 seconds for the shockwave [PAGE 11]. If the clock is treated as the sole measure of quality, Surveyor looks inefficient. But participants reported agency, confidence, and freedom. P8 says Surveyor gives confidence “to be dropped at a random place” and formulate a “game plan” [PAGE 9]. P9 says the value was that “there were a lot of ways to see the level” [PAGE 10]. P5 says Surveyor was closest to the sighted experience because the player can look in a direction, see what is there, and decide where to go [PAGE 10]. The recursive insight is that time is not merely cost; in discovery-oriented play, time can be evidence of engagement. However, if time becomes excessive without perceived progress, the player may model the system as coercive rather than exploratory.

The deepest recursive adjustment is for the designer to model the player’s model of discovery. The player must believe that unexplored patches are meaningful, not arbitrary. The paper proposes hints as a future mechanism: unexplored area size, nearby cues, or other supplementary information can “nudge players in a given direction” [PAGE 13]. This is a theory-of-mind upgrade: the system should not only mark the unknown, but also communicate the probable value of investigating the unknown. The counter-trap is that hints can become spoilers. The recursive design objective is therefore to preserve uncertainty about object identity while reducing uncertainty about exploration value.

The cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether physical, biomechanical, or weather constraints invalidate the paper’s claims. The evaluated artifact is a virtual game system, so weather does not directly alter the in-game state unless the game explicitly simulates weather. The paper does not describe weather as a variable in the testbed. Therefore, for the reported study, weather is not a direct veto layer.

However, the system has a physical interface layer. The paper states that Surveyor was implemented in Unity and that all interactions use a standard game controller [PAGE 5]. The required controls include the left thumbstick for movement, the right thumbstick for scanning, the upper face button for menus, and the right bumper for beacons [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. This creates a biomechanical veto condition. If a player cannot operate the controller, or cannot perceive the spatial audio, the system’s discovery mechanism is blocked. The paper acknowledges this: “However, there may be tweaks we can make for users with more severe hearing impairments or for users with physical impediments that may prevent effective use of a standard game controller.” [PAGE 14].

The physical veto can be formalized as follows. Let \(H\) be the player’s auditory detection capability and let \(M\) be the motor capability required for controller use. Let \(S\) be the system’s audio spatialization fidelity. The system succeeds only if:
\[
H \ge H_{\min}, \quad M \ge M_{\min}, \quad S \ge S_{\min}.
\]
If any inequality fails, the veto is active:
\[
V_{\text{physical}} = 1
\]
if controller use is impossible or audio localization is insufficient. In the reported study, two participants reported mild-to-moderate hearing loss in one ear, but the paper states that their hearing issues were not an impediment in the main study [PAGE 8]. This means the veto was not triggered for those participants under the study conditions. It does not prove that the veto will never trigger for other users.

The biomechanical veto also includes cognitive-motor interaction. The right-stick scanning mechanism requires continuous wrist or thumb control. The menu system requires button selection and auditory working memory. If a player has a motor tremor, limited range of motion, or severe fatigue, the scanning action may become burdensome. The paper does not measure fatigue, but the longer completion times with Surveyor suggest that the tool can demand sustained interaction [PAGE 11]. Therefore, a biomechanical veto is plausible for users who cannot sustain repeated right-stick inputs.

The weather veto becomes relevant only if Surveyor-like assistance is moved into physical environments. The paper speculates that physical-world exploration assistance could help blind users explore campuses, museums, parks, and urban areas [PAGE 14]. In that setting, weather becomes a real constraint because spatial audio beacons depend on auditory detectability. Let ambient noise be \(L_{\text{noise}}\), beacon level at distance \(r\) be \(L_{\text{rx}}(r)\), and detection margin be \(D\). Detection requires:
\[
L_{\text{rx}}(r) - L_{\text{noise}} \ge D.
\]
Rain, wind, traffic, crowd noise, and reverberation can increase \(L_{\text{noise}}\) or distort spatial cues. If the inequality fails, the beacon is not usable. In physical deployment, weather therefore can veto audio-only exploration assistance. The paper’s in-game findings do not test this regime, so the physical-world extension remains conditional.

A second weather-adjacent veto is environmental masking. Even without rain or wind, a noisy indoor environment can mask spatialized audio. The paper relies on spatialized sound for patch direction and landmark direction [PAGE 6]. If the environment is acoustically reverberant or noisy, the player may not localize the sound source. This is not a weather veto in the strict sense, but it is an environmental acoustic veto. The formal condition is the same: if signal-to-noise ratio falls below threshold, the perceptual operator fails.

The adjudication result is layered. For the reported virtual study, no weather veto applies because the game environment is software-defined and weather is not part of the testbed. For the reported virtual study, physical and biomechanical vetoes are possible but not observed as blocking in the sample. For future physical-world adaptation, weather and ambient noise become first-class veto layers. Therefore, the paper’s discovery claims are valid within the evaluated virtual context, but they do not automatically transfer to outdoor physical navigation without additional sensing, redundancy, and noise-robust feedback.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR-style veto scenarios use verifiable game telemetry, questionnaire scores, or formal detection conditions. RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: the reward is not a vague preference score alone, but a measurable behavioral or signal-detection outcome. Each scenario identifies a condition under which the exploration-assistance hypothesis should be vetoed, rejected, or revised.

Veto Scenario 1: Uniform patch presentation causes unverifiable exploration value.

The paper reports that unexplored patches were presented uniformly, and some participants found this cumbersome. P2 says Surveyor “forces you to feel out the rest of the room” [PAGE 10]. P6 reported randomly selecting a patch and hoping for the best [PAGE 10]. If patch selection is effectively random with respect to valuable objectives, then Surveyor’s discovery assistance is not reliably guiding exploration; it is only increasing labor.

Verifiable metric: path logs from the cloud backend, patch entry frequency, time spent in each patch, and console discovery count. Define patch-selection entropy:
\[
H_{\text{patch}} = -\sum_{b \in B_t} q_t(b) \log_2 q_t(b),
\]
where \(q_t(b)\) is the probability that the player selects patch \(b\). If \(H_{\text{patch}}\) is close to \(\log_2 |B_t|\) and optional console discovery does not exceed the simple menu baseline, the reward signal for patch guidance is weak.

Prediction: adding contextual hints, such as patch size, nearby cue presence, or estimated semantic value, will reduce unnecessary patch revisits and increase the probability that optional consoles are discovered. The paper already suggests this direction: hints can “nudge players in a given direction” while preserving discovery [PAGE 13]. A verifiable test would compare two versions: uniform patches versus hinted patches. The hinted version should produce higher console discovery counts without collapsing surprise, measured by post-level fun and agency scores.

Veto Scenario 2: Audio beacon failure invalidates spatial guidance.

Surveyor relies on spatialized audio for direction and distance announcements [PAGE 6]. If a player cannot localize the beacon, the system cannot guide them. The formal detection condition is:
\[
L_{\text{rx}}(r) - L_{\text{noise}} \ge D.
\]
If this fails, the beacon is not functionally available.

Verifiable metric: localization error in meters or clock-face sectors, beacon activation success rate, and time to target after beacon activation. A veto is triggered if localization error exceeds a predefined threshold, for example greater than one room-cell width or greater than 45 degrees of directional error.

Prediction: participants with more severe hearing loss, or participants tested in noisy environments, will show higher localization error and longer target acquisition times. The paper acknowledges this risk: “there may be tweaks we can make for users with more severe hearing impairments” [PAGE 14]. A verifiable remedy would be multimodal redundancy, such as haptic directional pulses or controller vibration patterns. The test would compare audio-only beacons against audio-plus-haptic beacons. If haptic redundancy significantly reduces localization error and improves restrictiveness scores, the original audio-only implementation is vetoed as insufficiently general.

Veto Scenario 3: Golden-path shockwave eliminates discovery reward.

The shockwave condition produced the fastest completion times but the lowest discovery behavior. The paper reports: “With the shockwave, however, participants activated a median of zero consoles.” [PAGE 12]. This is a verifiable veto against the claim that fast object localization is equivalent to rich exploration. If the system orients the player along a prescribed path and filters out non-objective items, optional discovery collapses.

Verifiable metric: console activation count, decorative-object engagement count, path entropy, and post-level immersion ratings. In the source study, Surveyor and the simple menu produced a median of three console activations, while the shockwave produced a median of zero [PAGE 12]. A reward model that values discovery should assign low reward to shockwave-only conditions unless the player explicitly requests a speed-run mode.

Prediction: if decorative objects are made optionally audible in the shockwave mode, and if the golden-path orientation is disabled by default, console activation will rise above zero and immersion ratings will improve. Conversely, if the shockwave remains objective-only and auto-oriented, participants will continue to report low environmental understanding. The paper supports this prediction with P1’s statement that the shockwave provided “not enough information” to feel present in the world [PAGE 12] and P2’s statement that the system prevented console discovery and made them lose interest [PAGE 12].

These three vetoes are not speculative. They are grounded in the paper’s reported mechanisms and participant statements. They also produce falsifiable predictions. If hinted patches do not improve discovery efficiency, the hint hypothesis is weakened. If haptic redundancy does not improve beacon localization for users with hearing constraints, the multimodal remedy is insufficient. If optional decorative feedback in the shockwave does not increase console discovery, the golden-path design remains vetoed for discovery-oriented play.
