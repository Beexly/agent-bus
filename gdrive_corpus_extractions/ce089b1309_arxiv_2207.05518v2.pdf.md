<THINK_TRACE>
The source paper’s central thesis is that multi-object tracking should not be reduced to a single point estimate per object, such as a bounding box or a center point, but should instead be represented as a pixel-wise distribution that can be propagated, predicted, and associated across frames. The paper states this directly: “P3AFormer propagates pixel-wise features guided by flow information to pass messages between frames” [PAGE 1] and “During inference, a pixel-wise association procedure is proposed to recover object connections through frames based on the pixel-wise prediction” [PAGE 1]. The derivation chain is therefore: two consecutive frames enter a backbone; a pixel decoder lifts backbone features into multi-scale pixel-wise feature maps; a flow-guided warping operation aligns previous-frame pixel features to the current frame; an object decoder with masked attention produces object queries; those queries are decoded into center heatmaps and size maps; a pixel-wise Hungarian matching loss trains the model; and at inference a Kalman-filtered track forecast is matched to predicted heatmaps using a pixel-wise association cost.

The gray-zone audit begins with the representation itself. A pixel-wise distribution is not merely an image of activations; it is an object-centric evidence field whose peak should coincide with the object center. The paper says the shape of the pixel feature map is “HPl × WPl × d” [PAGE 6], which means the representation has spatial axes and a feature-channel axis. In the paper’s final models, the hidden feature dimension is “d = 128” [PAGE 10]. This matters because the dot products that generate center and size heatmaps operate in a 128-dimensional feature space, not in raw RGB space. The formal consequence is that a pixel’s evidence for object i depends on alignment between the pixel feature vector and an object-specific center embedding. If that alignment is noisy, the heatmap peak can split, drift, or vanish. The paper’s remedy is masked attention: “The mask matrix Ml is initialized as an all-zero matrix, and it is determined by the center heatmaps at the previous level” [PAGE 6]. This creates a recurrent spatial prior: previous-level center heatmaps restrict current-level attention to plausible local regions.

A second gray zone is the temporal operator. The flow-guided propagation equation is not a generic temporal filter; it is a dense geometric alignment step. The paper defines a flow network Φ and a bilinear warping function W, producing a warped previous-frame feature map [PAGE 5-6]. The formal implication is that the model’s temporal context is only as good as the flow field’s pixel-level accuracy. In crowded MOT scenes, flow can fail at occlusion boundaries, motion blur, scale change, and low-texture regions. The paper acknowledges that pixel-wise information helps “overcome occlusion based on low-level clues” [PAGE 1], but low-level clues can also be corrupted by motion blur or appearance change. The ablation table shows that pixel-wise propagation contributes to detection quality: the model without all pixel-wise techniques has lower mAP, MOTA, and IDF1 than the full model [PAGE 13]. However, the exact cosine-similarity formula for the propagation weight is said to be in supplementary material, not in the main text [PAGE 6], which creates an auditability gap.

A third gray zone is the training objective. The paper uses Hungarian matching with a pixel-wise cost: “P3AFormer leverages the bipartite Hungarian matching [4,82] to match N predicted objects to K ground-true objects” [PAGE 7]. The cost contains a classification term and a heatmap L1 term [PAGE 7]. This means the model is not trained only to detect boxes; it is trained to make the entire predicted heatmap agree with a ground-truth Gaussian heatmap. The Gaussian heatmap construction is described as having a center at the object center and a radius proportional to object size [PAGE 7]. That design couples detection and association: a good heatmap is simultaneously a detection score map and an association template. The risk is that heatmap calibration becomes critical. If predicted heatmap peaks are overconfident but spatially biased, the Hungarian matcher may select the wrong object, especially in dense crowds.

A fourth gray zone is inference. The paper describes an online, frame-by-frame procedure: tracks are forecasted by a Kalman filter, matched by Hungarian algorithm using pixel-wise cost, initialized if confident, and killed after a dead period [PAGE 7-8]. The exact thresholds are given: “The thresholds in the pixel-wise association are ηm = 0.65 and ηs = 0.80 on all benchmarks” [PAGE 11]. The dead-track horizon is “nk = 30 frames” [PAGE 11]. These are not merely implementation details; they define the tracker’s temporal memory. A track surviving 30 frames without detection is a strong persistence prior. In high-frame-rate video, 30 frames may be short; in low-frame-rate video, it may be long. The paper does not provide a frame-rate-normalized rule, so the temporal unit remains frame-indexed rather than seconds-normalized.

The final gray zone is leaderboard comparability. The reported result is “81.2% in terms of MOTA on the MOT17 benchmark” and “78.1% IDF1” [PAGE 1], obtained under “private detection” [PAGE 9]. The table also distinguishes “P3AFormer” from “P3AFormer (+W&B)” [PAGE 9], where “W&B” means “whistles and bells” [PAGE 9]. This means the headline number includes training augmentations, mixed datasets, learnable queries, and related techniques, not only the core pixel-wise architecture. The ablation shows that some training techniques have nontrivial effects, but not all effects are monotone: for example, removing bounding-box postprocessing in Table 5 yields higher MOTA but lower IDF1 than the full model [PAGE 13]. That non-monotonicity warns against treating each component as independently additive. The overall audit conclusion is that the paper’s pixel-wise thesis is internally coherent and empirically supported on its selected benchmarks, but its exact reproducibility depends on supplementary details, flow-domain adaptation, heatmap calibration, and threshold sensitivity.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s formal operators can be separated into five layers: pixel feature propagation, masked attention, heatmap decoding, pixel-wise matching loss, and track association.

1. Pixel-wise feature propagation

The source gives the warped previous-frame feature as:

\[
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t-1)\rightarrow(t)}
=
\mathcal{W}\left(
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t-1)},
\Phi\left(\mathcal{I}^{(t-1)}, \mathcal{I}^{(t)}\right)
\right)
\]

[PAGE 6, Eq. (1)].

Units and assumptions:
- \(\mathcal{I}^{(t)}\) is an input image frame, indexed by frame number \(t\). The frame index has unit “frame”; if converted to physical time, \(t \Delta t\) has unit seconds, where \(\Delta t\) is seconds per frame.
- \(\Phi\) outputs an optical-flow field. Its natural unit is pixels per frame, or displacement vectors in the image plane.
- \(\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t-1)}\) is the previous-frame pixel feature map at decoding level \(l\). The paper states its shape is “HPl × WPl × d” [PAGE 6]. Thus each spatial location has a \(d\)-dimensional feature vector.
- \(\mathcal{W}\) is bilinear warping. It samples the previous feature map at coordinates displaced by the flow field.

The fused feature is:

\[
\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{l}^{(t)}
=
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t)}
+
w^{(t-1)\rightarrow(t)}
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t-1)\rightarrow(t)}
\]

[PAGE 6, Eq. (2)].

The weight \(w^{(t-1)\rightarrow(t)}\) is described as “pixel-wise cosine similarity” [PAGE 6]. A formal probabilistic fusion version can be written as:

\[
w_{h,w}
=
\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\left(
\frac{
\left\langle
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t)}[h,w,:],
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t-1)\rightarrow(t)}[h,w,:]
\right\rangle
}{
\left\|
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t)}[h,w,:]
\right\|_2
\left\|
\mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t-1)\rightarrow(t)}[h,w,:]
\right\|_2
}
\right)
\]

The clipping to \([0,1]\) is an explicit assumption for stable feature fusion; the main text does not provide the exact supplementary formula, but cosine similarity is inherently dimensionless and lies in \([-1,1]\). If negative similarities are allowed without clipping, the fusion can subtract evidence rather than merely discount it.

Breakdown regimes for propagation:
- If flow displacement exceeds the reliable matching range of the feature map, the warped feature samples the wrong semantic neighborhood. In pixel units, failure becomes likely when flow error approaches or exceeds the object’s spatial support in feature-map pixels.
- If the feature vector norm is zero or near zero, cosine similarity is undefined or numerically unstable.
- If occlusion creates one-sided flow evidence, the warped previous feature may contain background or disappearing-object evidence. The model can resist this only if the current-frame feature dominates or if the similarity weight suppresses the bad warped evidence.

2. Masked attention in the object decoder

The paper’s masked attention is based on standard attention with an additive mask. A formal version is:

\[
\mathbf{X}_{l}
=
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(
\mathbf{M}_{l-1}
+
\mathbf{Q}_{l}\mathbf{K}_{l}^{\mathsf{T}}
\right)
\mathbf{V}_{l}
+
\mathbf{X}_{l-1}
\]

where:
- \(\mathbf{X}_{l}\) is the hidden query feature at layer \(l\).
- \(\mathbf{Q}_{l}\) is the query matrix.
- \(\mathbf{K}_{l}\) and \(\mathbf{V}_{l}\) are key and value matrices derived from the fused pixel feature \(\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{l}\).
- \(\mathbf{M}_{l-1}\) is the additive attention mask from the previous decoding level.

The paper says the mask matrix is initialized as all-zero and determined by previous-level center heatmaps [PAGE 6]. The mask update is:

\[
\mathbf{M}_{l-1}(i,x,y)
=
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } c_{l-1}[i,x,y] > 0.5, \\
-\infty, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

[PAGE 7, Eq. (5)].

Units and interpretation:
- Attention logits are dimensionless.
- The mask values are either zero or a prohibitory negative infinity.
- The threshold \(0.5\) is a probability-like cutoff on the sigmoid center heatmap.

Breakdown regimes:
- If no pixel for object \(i\) has \(c_{l-1}[i,x,y] > 0.5\), then the entire row of allowed keys is masked. In exact arithmetic this gives an undefined softmax; in finite precision it can produce NaNs or an effectively zero attention output.
- If the heatmap is over-smoothed, multiple objects may share the same high-probability region, causing the mask to fail to localize attention.
- The hard threshold is non-differentiable at exactly \(0.5\); the paper uses strict inequality, so equality falls into the masked branch.

3. Center and size heatmap decoding

The paper computes center heatmaps and size maps by dot products between pixel features and object embeddings:

\[
c_{l}[i,h,w]
=
\operatorname{sigmoid}
\left(
\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{l}[h,w,:]
\cdot
\mathcal{E}^{ctr}_{l}[i,:]
\right)
\]

\[
s_{l}[i,h,w,j]
=
\operatorname{sigmoid}
\left(
\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{l}[h,w,:]
\cdot
\mathcal{E}^{sz}_{l}[i,:,j]
\right)
\]

[PAGE 7, Eq. (4)].

Units and assumptions:
- \(\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{l}[h,w,:]\) is a \(d\)-dimensional feature vector.
- \(\mathcal{E}^{ctr}_{l}[i,:]\) is an object-specific center embedding.
- \(\mathcal{E}^{sz}_{l}[i,:,j]\) is an object-specific size embedding for spatial dimension \(j\), where \(j\) indexes the two bounding-box size dimensions.
- The dot product is dimensionless if the feature and embedding spaces are trained jointly; the sigmoid output is a probability-like scalar in \([0,1]\).

Breakdown regimes:
- If feature and embedding norms grow large, sigmoid saturation can make gradients vanish.
- If the center embedding is not discriminative, the heatmap peak may shift to background or to a neighboring object.
- The peak value is used as a confidence score: “the score τk.score (the peak value in the center heatmap)” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, calibration errors directly affect track initiation.

4. Pixel-wise training cost

The pixel-wise cost is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{pixel-wise}}
=
\sum_{l}\sum_{i}
\left(
-\log \hat{p}_{l}^{i}(c_{l}^{i})
+
\mathbf{1}_{c_{l}^{i}\neq\varnothing}
\left|
\hat{h}_{l}^{i} - h_{l}^{i}
\right|
\right)
\]

[PAGE 7, Eq. (6)].

Units:
- If \(\log\) is natural logarithm, the classification term is measured in nats. If base-2 logarithm were used, the unit would be bits. The paper does not specify base, but the conventional deep-learning convention is natural log, so nats is the audit-safe unit.
- The heatmap L1 term is dimensionless if heatmaps are normalized to comparable ranges. The paper describes ground-truth heatmaps generated by a Gaussian function [PAGE 7], but the exact normalization is not fully specified in the provided text.
- The indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{c_{l}^{i}\neq\varnothing}\) disables the heatmap term for the “no object” class.

Breakdown regimes:
- The term \(-\log \hat{p}_{l}^{i}(c_{l}^{i})\) diverges if predicted probability for the true class approaches zero. Training therefore requires numerical epsilon clipping or equivalent stabilization, even though the paper does not state the exact epsilon.
- If predicted and ground-truth heatmaps have different scales, the L1 term can dominate or vanish depending on normalization.
- If \(K > N\), where \(K\) is the number of ground-truth objects and \(N\) is the number of queries, not all ground truths can be uniquely matched. The paper sets \(N=100\) queries [PAGE 11], which is large but not infinite in extremely crowded scenes.

5. Inference association

At inference, the association cost is described as the L1 distance between a track’s forecast heatmap and an object’s predicted heatmap [PAGE 8]. A matching is accepted if the cost is below \(\eta_m\):

\[
\text{accept match}
\iff
\mathcal{C}_{\text{assoc}}
<
\eta_m
\]

with \(\eta_m = 0.65\) [PAGE 11].

A new track is initialized if:

\[
\tau_{k'}.\text{score} > \eta_s
\]

with \(\eta_s = 0.80\) [PAGE 8, PAGE 11].

Dead tracks are removed after \(n_k\) unmatched frames, with \(n_k=30\) [PAGE 11].

Units:
- \(\mathcal{C}_{\text{assoc}}\) is dimensionless if heatmaps are normalized.
- \(\eta_m\) and \(\eta_s\) are dimensionless thresholds.
- \(n_k\) has unit frames.

Breakdown regimes:
- If true matching costs concentrate near \(\eta_m=0.65\), small calibration shifts cause either fragmentation or false links.
- If a newborn object’s peak score is below \(0.80\), it will not be initialized, causing persistent false negatives.
- If occlusion lasts longer than 30 frames, the track is killed; if reappearance occurs after that, a new ID is likely assigned.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s intellectual genealogy runs through three historical streams: transformer detection, pixel-wise dense prediction, and tracking-by-detection association.

The first stream is transformer-based object detection. The paper cites DETR as the seminal end-to-end detection framework [PAGE 4, PAGE 15]. DETR introduced learnable object queries and bipartite matching, and P3AFormer inherits this structure: “The object decoder follows the standard transformer [4,56], which transforms N learnable positional embeddings” [PAGE 6]. However, P3AFormer changes the decoder’s role. Instead of only producing box predictions, the decoder produces embeddings that interact with a full-image pixel representation to generate center and size heatmaps [PAGE 6-7]. This is a direct descendant of mask-based transformer architectures such as MaskFormer and Mask2Former, which the paper cites for meta-architecture and masked attention [PAGE 2, PAGE 4, PAGE 6].

The second stream is dense prediction. The paper cites DenseFusion, pixel-wise voting networks, DPT, and pyramid vision transformer as evidence that pixel-wise prediction can preserve low-level detail [PAGE 2, PAGE 4]. It states: “pixel-wise prediction preserves more low-confident details, which can improve tracking robustness” [PAGE 2]. The historical trap here is to assume that dense prediction is automatically better for tracking. Dense prediction helps only if the dense representation is object-centric and temporally aligned. P3AFormer’s contribution is to make dense prediction object-centric through query embeddings and masked attention, rather than treating every pixel as an independent classifier output.

The third stream is tracking-by-detection. The paper reviews DeepSORT, CenterTrack, graph-based methods, and joint detection-tracking approaches [PAGE 3]. CenterTrack is especially relevant because P3AFormer uses center heatmaps and size maps, but differs by making the association cost pixel-wise rather than point-wise or box-wise. The paper states: “Different from tracking objects via bounding boxes [75,51,1] or as points [68,80], we propose to track objects as pixel-wise distributions” [PAGE 4]. This is the key genealogical break: bounding-box tracking assumes a compact rectangular object evidence region; point tracking assumes a single high-confidence center; pixel-wise tracking assumes a distributed evidence field that can survive partial occlusion.

The paper also inherits the flow-guided feature aggregation lineage. It cites Zhu et al.’s dense feature propagation for video object detection [PAGE 4] and uses FlowNet for flow estimation [PAGE 5, PAGE 10]. The historical trap is domain shift: FlowNet is trained on Flying Chairs [PAGE 10], while MOT17 and MOT20 contain crowded pedestrian scenes with different motion statistics, camera motion, and occlusion patterns. The paper uses the “simple version of FlowNet” pre-trained on Flying Chairs [PAGE 10], which suggests that flow quality may be a limiting factor in dense crowds.

A multi-week trap in this lineage is the false equivalence between detection quality and association quality. The paper’s tables separate mAP, MOTA, and IDF1. In Table 5, removing bounding-box processing yields “48.3 mAP, 79.1 MOTA, 74.8 IDF1” while the full model has “48.3 mAP, 78.4 MOTA, 76.0 IDF1” [PAGE 13]. This shows that a component can improve one metric while slightly worsening another. A researcher who optimizes only MOTA may adopt postprocessing that harms identity preservation. Conversely, a researcher who optimizes only IDF1 may miss detection gains. The paper’s multi-metric reporting is therefore not decorative; it encodes the multi-week trap of single-metric optimization.

Another genealogical trap is the “whistles and bells” problem. The headline MOT17 result is 81.2 MOTA, but the table distinguishes “P3AFormer” from “P3AFormer (+W&B)” [PAGE 9]. The improvement from 69.2 MOTA to 81.2 MOTA is not solely architectural; it includes strong augmentation, mixed datasets, learnable queries, and other training techniques [PAGE 3, PAGE 13-14]. The historical lesson is that transformer MOT papers often conflate architecture with recipe. P3AFormer is transparent enough to show both versions, but the causal attribution of the final leaderboard number requires the ablation tables, not the headline alone.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper can be interpreted as a recursive theory-of-mind loop across three temporal scales: pixel-level evidence, object-level query reasoning, and track-level persistence.

At the pixel level, the model’s “belief” about an object is not a single coordinate but a distribution over image locations. The center heatmap is the belief map. Its peak is the most probable center, but the surrounding mass preserves uncertainty. This is important under occlusion because the model can retain evidence from visible parts of an object even when the center is temporarily ambiguous. The paper argues that pixel-wise information helps overcome occlusion based on low-level clues [PAGE 1]. In recursive terms, the model does not commit to a hard object location too early; it maintains a spatial probability field until downstream association resolves the track.

At the object-query level, the model reasons about each object through masked attention. The mask is determined by the previous level’s center heatmaps [PAGE 6]. This creates a recursive expectation: the model expects each object to remain near its previous high-probability region. The mask prevents the query from attending globally to background noise, but it also creates a habit: if the previous heatmap is wrong, the next level may be trapped in a local region. This is the crisis-habit regression risk. In a crowded scene, if one object’s heatmap leaks into a neighboring object’s region, masked attention may reinforce the wrong localization. The paper’s counter-trap is multi-scale feature maps and pixel-wise prediction, which preserve more detail and allow recovery if the local evidence is sufficiently discriminative [PAGE 2, PAGE 13].

At the track level, the tracker uses a Kalman-filter forecast and a Hungarian match. The forecast is not merely geometric; the heatmap is translated along with the forecast movement via bilinear warping [PAGE 8]. This means the track’s belief is a moving distribution, not a moving point. The association cost is the L1 distance between forecast and predicted heatmaps [PAGE 8]. The recursive question is: does the predicted distribution support the forecasted track, or does it support a different object? The threshold \(\eta_m=0.65\) defines the acceptance boundary [PAGE 11]. If the predicted distribution is sharp and aligned, matching is easy. If it is broad or split, the Hungarian matcher may choose the wrong pairing or reject the match.

The clock variable is the dead-track horizon. The paper says: “The lost tracks are deleted if they do not appear after nk = 30 frames” [PAGE 11]. This is a temporal belief-decay mechanism. A track is allowed to survive 30 frames without direct evidence. In occlusion, this is beneficial because the object may reappear. In crowded scenes, this is risky because a dead track may steal a detection from a newborn object if the forecast remains plausible. The counter-trap is the combination of track death and newborn confidence threshold: a newborn must have peak score greater than \(\eta_s=0.80\) [PAGE 8, PAGE 11]. Thus, the system prefers mature tracks over uncertain newborns. This is rational under persistence bias, but it can suppress recovery of small or heavily occluded objects if their initial heatmap peak is low.

The recursive crisis scenario is as follows. During occlusion, the pixel distribution loses peak confidence. If the score falls below \(0.80\) before an existing track can cover it, the object is not initialized. If an existing track exists, the Kalman forecast and 30-frame memory may bridge the gap. If the forecast drifts, the association cost may exceed \(0.65\), causing the track to die. If the object reappears after 30 frames, it receives a new ID. The paper’s design tries to counter this by preserving low-confidence predictions [PAGE 3] and by using pixel-wise distributions rather than hard boxes [PAGE 1]. However, the inference thresholds reintroduce a hard decision boundary. The recursive tension is therefore: dense evidence versus thresholded commitment. The paper’s empirical claim is that the balance improves IDF1 and reduces ID switches, as shown by the reported 78.1 IDF1 on MOT17 and 1893 IDSW in the full model [PAGE 9].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto succeeds only if a lower-layer physical, biomechanical, or environmental variable invalidates the paper’s empirical or formal claim. The paper’s claim is scoped to specific benchmarks: MOT17, MOT20, and KITTI [PAGE 1, PAGE 9-11]. Therefore, a physical veto must show that the benchmark measurement itself is invalid, or that the model’s core operator fails in a way that negates the reported improvement.

1. Physical veto: optical-flow warping failure

The propagation operator assumes that previous-frame features can be aligned to the current frame using flow \(\Phi\) and bilinear warping \(\mathcal{W}\) [PAGE 6]. A physical veto can be constructed as follows. Let the true object displacement in feature-map pixels between frames be \(\Delta \mathbf{x}\). Let the estimated flow be \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}\). Define flow error:

\[
\mathbf{e}_{\text{flow}}
=
\hat{\mathbf{u}} - \Delta \mathbf{x}
\]

with units pixels per frame. If the object center’s spatial support in the feature map has radius \(r\) pixels, then a sufficient local failure condition is:

\[
\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{flow}}\|_2 > r
\]

Under this condition, the warped previous-frame evidence may no longer overlap the current object support. The center heatmap can lose its peak or shift to a neighboring object. This is a real physical breakdown regime, especially at occlusion boundaries, fast motion, and motion blur.

However, this veto is local, not global. The paper’s benchmark results are aggregate metrics over many sequences. A local flow failure does not automatically falsify the claim that pixel-wise propagation improves average MOTA or IDF1. It only shows that the operator has a conditional failure domain. The paper’s own framing supports this: pixel-wise information helps under occlusion [PAGE 1], but it does not guarantee correctness under arbitrary flow error.

2. Biomechanical veto: non-rigid human motion and crowd deformation

The main benchmarks are pedestrian-centric: MOT17 is focused on multiple persons tracking in crowded scenes [PAGE 9], and MOT20 consists of crowded scenes [PAGE 9]. Human motion is non-rigid, articulated, and often partially occluded. A biomechanical veto would claim that pixel-wise center heatmaps fail because human bodies deform, split, or merge visually.

Formally, let the object silhouette be \(S_t\), a set of pixels at frame \(t\). If the silhouette changes such that the centroid of visible evidence moves away from the physical center, then the center heatmap peak may become biased. Let \(\mu_t\) be the centroid of visible object pixels and \(c_t\) be the physical or annotated center. If:

\[
\|\mu_t - c_t\|_2 > \rho
\]

where \(\rho\) is the heatmap tolerance radius, the predicted center may be systematically offset. This can increase association cost and cause ID switches.

But this veto does not overturn the paper’s result unless it shows that P3AFormer is worse than point-based or box-based methods under the same biomechanical stress. The paper claims the opposite: pixel-wise distributions “can robustly track the small objects under occlusion” [PAGE 2] and reduce ID switches compared with TransCenter [PAGE 2]. The ablation also shows that pixel-wise association improves a CNN tracker: Tractor with propagation, prediction, and association improves from 61.9 MOTA and 326 IDSW to 73.1 MOTA and 208 IDSW [PAGE 13]. Therefore, the biomechanical veto is not sufficient to invalidate the claim; it only identifies a stress regime where center heatmaps may need shape-aware or part-aware extensions.

3. Weather or environmental veto

The source text does not annotate weather conditions for MOT17, MOT20, or KITTI in the provided pages. A weather veto would require showing that rain, fog, snow, low illumination, or sensor noise changes the pixel evidence so severely that the reported metrics are not meaningful. Without weather labels, the veto cannot be adjudicated from the source alone. The paper does not claim weather robustness. It claims robustness to occlusion and small objects [PAGE 1-2]. Therefore, a weather veto is out of scope unless additional metadata proves that the benchmark sequences are dominated by weather conditions not represented in the reported evaluation.

A formal environmental veto can still be stated. Let \(A\) be atmospheric attenuation and \(N\) be sensor noise. If the effective signal-to-noise ratio:

\[
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{eff}}
=
\frac{\|I_{\text{clean}}\|_2}{\|A + N\|_2}
\]

falls below the model’s operational threshold, pixel features may collapse. But the paper provides no weather-stratified evidence, so this remains a conditional failure regime, not a proven veto.

Final adjudication: no blanket physical, biomechanical, or weather veto overturns the paper’s benchmark-scoped claims. The valid vetoes are conditional: flow error larger than object support can break propagation; severe silhouette deformation can bias center heatmaps; unmodeled environmental degradation can collapse pixel evidence. These conditions identify breakdown regimes but do not falsify the paper’s reported average improvements on MOT17, MOT20, and KITTI.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
In a reinforcement-learning-with-verifiable-rewards setting, the paper’s claims can be treated as policies whose rewards are measurable metrics: mAP, MOTA, IDF1, MT, ML, FP, FN, and IDSW. A veto scenario is a counterfactual intervention that, if verified, would falsify a causal claim. Below are three verifiable veto scenarios with predictions derived from the source.

1. Masked-attention veto

Claim: masked attention improves detection and association.

Source evidence: the paper states that mask attention “is beneficial to detection (0.3 mAP) and association (1.3% IDF1)” [PAGE 14]. Table 5 shows the full model at 48.3 mAP, 78.4 MOTA, and 76.0 IDF1, while the model without mask attention has 48.0 mAP, 71.4 MOTA, and 74.7 IDF1 [PAGE 13].

Counterfactual intervention: remove the mask update in Eq. (5), so attention is unconstrained by previous-level center heatmaps.

Prediction:
- mAP should decrease by approximately 0.3 points.
- IDF1 should decrease by approximately 1.3 points.
- MOTA may decrease more substantially if localization degrades, but the exact MOTA change can be confounded by other components.

Veto condition: if an ablation with identical training and inference settings shows no IDF1 drop, or an IDF1 increase, after removing mask attention, the causal claim that masked attention improves association is vetoed.

Formal reward check:

\[
\Delta \text{IDF1}_{\text{mask}}
=
\text{IDF1}_{\text{with mask}}
-
\text{IDF1}_{\text{without mask}}
\]

Expected:

\[
\Delta \text{IDF1}_{\text{mask}} \approx +1.3
\]

If the verified counterfactual yields \(\Delta \text{IDF1}_{\text{mask}} \leq 0\), the veto succeeds.

2. Pixel-wise association generalization veto

Claim: pixel-wise techniques generalize beyond the transformer architecture to a tracking-by-detection tracker.

Source evidence: Table 6 shows Tractor baseline at 61.9 MOTA, 64.7 IDF1, and 326 IDSW. Tractor with propagation, prediction, and association improves to 73.1 MOTA, 72.3 IDF1, and 208 IDSW [PAGE 13]. The paper says: “It is clear that tracking objects as pixel-wise distributions also improves CNN-based frameworks” [PAGE 14].

Counterfactual intervention: apply only pixel-wise propagation to Tractor without prediction and association, or apply prediction without changing the association procedure.

Prediction:
- If propagation alone is insufficient, the large IDSW reduction should not occur until the dense association scheme is added.
- The full pixel-wise stack should reduce IDSW by roughly 118 events relative to baseline Tractor.
- MOTA should increase by roughly 11.2 points relative to baseline Tractor.

Veto condition: if Tractor with propagation, prediction, and dense association fails to improve MOTA or IDSW over Tractor baseline under the same training and evaluation protocol, the generalization claim is vetoed.

Formal reward check:

\[
\Delta \text{IDSW}_{\text{assoc}}
=
\text{IDSW}_{\text{Tractor}}
-
\text{IDSW}_{\text{Tractor+Pro+Pre+Ass}}
\]

Expected:

\[
\Delta \text{IDSW}_{\text{assoc}} \approx 326 - 208 = 118
\]

If the verified counterfactual gives \(\Delta \text{IDSW}_{\text{assoc}} \leq 0\), the association-generalization claim is vetoed.

3. Flow-propagation necessity veto

Claim: pixel-wise propagation contributes to detection and tracking quality by transferring temporal context between frames.

Source evidence: Table 4 compares incomplete systems. The model with prediction only has 42.1 mAP, 72.8 MOTA, and 74.5 IDF1. The full model with propagation, prediction, and association has 48.3 mAP, 78.4 MOTA, and 76.0 IDF1 [PAGE 13]. The paper also states that removing pixel-wise propagation or prediction worsens detection mAP [PAGE 13].

Counterfactual intervention: remove the flow-guided propagation term in Eq. (2), replacing the fused feature with the current-frame feature only:

\[
\bar{\mathbf{P}}_{l}^{(t)} = \mathbf{P}_{l}^{(t)}
\]

Prediction:
- Detection mAP should decrease relative to the full model.
- MOTA should decrease if temporal context is important for small objects and occlusion recovery.
- The degradation should be larger in crowded or occluded sequences than in sparse sequences, because propagation is intended to aggregate temporal evidence.

Veto condition: if removing propagation leaves mAP, MOTA, and IDF1 statistically unchanged under matched training and evaluation, the necessity claim is vetoed.

Formal reward check:

\[
\Delta \text{mAP}_{\text{prop}}
=
\text{mAP}_{\text{full}}
-
\text{mAP}_{\text{without propagation}}
\]

Expected from the table pattern:

\[
