F_I=\frac{MVC_{T0}-MVC_{T1}}{MVC_{T0}}\times 100\%,
\]
with \(MVC\) in N and \(F_I\) in %. Assumption: T0 is a stable, pre-fatigue baseline and T1 is immediately post-fatigue. Singularity: \(MVC_{T0}=0\) makes \(F_I\) undefined. Breakdown regime: baseline injury, pain inhibition, or invalid dynamometry makes the denominator non-representative. The source reports declines of 25.3%, 30.4%, and 35.0% by population [PAGE 355].

2. Recovery ratio relative to baseline:
\[
R_X(t)=\frac{X_{CC}(t)}{X_{CC}(T0)}\times 100\%,
\]
where \(X\) is MVC (N), stiffness (N/m), perfusion (PU), or PPT (N/cm² or kPa, after conversion). Assumption: the same unit and same measurement site are used at T0 and \(t\). Singularity: \(X_{CC}(T0)=0\) or a near-zero baseline makes \(R_X\) unstable. For stiffness, \(R_X>100\%\) means residual stiffness above baseline; the source reports 118.0% and 132.1% at T4 under CC in some groups [PAGE 355].

3. Between-condition contrast:
\[
D_X(t)=X_{CC}(t)-X_{Sham}(t),
\]
in the outcome’s unit. This is the direct paired difference. Assumption: crossover periods are exchangeable after the 14-day washout [PAGE 351]. Breakdown regime: carryover, period effects, or unequal sequence effects contaminate \(D_X(t)\).

4. Paired standardized effect size:
\[
d_z=\frac{\bar D}{s_D},
\]
unitless, where \(\bar D\) is the mean within-participant CC–Sham difference and \(s_D\) is the standard deviation of those paired differences. Singularity: \(s_D=0\) makes \(d_z\) infinite. Breakdown regime: non-normal paired differences, outliers, or pairing errors. The source reports \(d_z=0.76, 0.97, 1.02\) for MVC at T2–T4 [PAGE 355].

5. Mixed ANOVA model:
\[
Y_{ijk}=\mu+P_i+C_j+T_k+(P C)_{ij}+(P T)_{ik}+(C T)_{jk}+(P C T)_{ijk}+\epsilon_{ijk},
\]
where \(Y\) is the continuous outcome in its unit, \(P\) is Population, \(C\) is Condition, and \(T\) is Time. Assumptions: approximately normal residuals, homogeneity of variance, independence of subjects, and sphericity for repeated factors. The source states that “when sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied” [PAGE 355]. Breakdown regime: severe non-sphericity, missing data, or unequal subgroup variances; Supplementary Table S8 shows sphericity was violated for all listed repeated effects and GG corrections were applied [PAGE 370].

6. Ordinal aligned-rank model for TQR:
\[
Y^*_{ijk}=\operatorname{rank}(Y_{ijk})\ \text{after alignment},
\]
followed by the same Population × Condition × Time factorial structure. Assumption: ordinal scores are not treated as interval-scaled. Breakdown regime: excessive ties, empty ordinal cells, or violated alignment assumptions. The source reports TQR via “aligned rank-transform factorial ANOVA” [PAGE 355].

7. Ordinal logistic model for Borg CR10:
\[
\operatorname{logit}\left[P(Y\le c)\right]=\theta_c-\left(\beta_P+\beta_C+\beta_T+\beta_{PC}+\beta_{PT}+\beta_{CT}+\beta_{PCT}\right),
\]
with cumulative odds ratios (OR) unitless. Assumption: proportional odds. Breakdown regime: proportional-odds violation, sparse ordinal categories, or ceiling/floor effects. The source reports ORs for population effects, e.g. older adults versus MMA athletes \(OR=19.44\) [PAGE 359].

8. Rank-based paired contrast for ordinal outcomes:
\[
r_{rb}=1-\frac{2U}{n_1 n_2}\quad\text{or the matched rank-biserial equivalent},
\]
unitless, with \(U\) the Mann–Whitney/Wilcoxon rank statistic. Assumption: ordinal comparability and paired structure. Breakdown regime: many ties, zero variance, or non-exchangeable pairs. The source states that effect sizes are “matched rank-biserial correlations for paired ordinal contrasts” [PAGE 355].

9. Variance-proportion effect sizes:
\[
\eta_p^2=\frac{SS_{\text{effect}}}{SS_{\text{effect}}+SS_{\text{error}}},\qquad
\eta^2=\frac{SS_{\text{between}}}{SS_{\text{total}}},\qquad
\epsilon^2=\frac{H}{(n^2-1)/(n+1)}
\]
for Kruskal–Wallis-type ordinal comparisons. These are unitless proportions in \([0,1]\). Assumption: the corresponding ANOVA or rank model is valid. Breakdown regime: small samples, unequal subgroup sizes, or ordinal models with sparse cells. The source reports \(\eta_p^2=.771\) for population on MVC [PAGE 355] and \(\eta_p^2=.879\) for population on perfusion [PAGE 358].

Dose operators: the active set-point was 3 °C with nominal high compression of 75 mmHg for 10 min twice daily over 3 days [PAGE 352], and sham was 15 °C and 15 mmHg [PAGE 352]. These are device set-points, not measured tissue temperatures or measured cuff pressures; therefore the formal dose variable \(D_{\text{tissue}}\) is unobserved. The paper states, “we cannot quantify the actual mechanothermal dose delivered at the tissue level” [PAGE 361]. Any equation using a tissue-level dose would have an unmeasured latent variable and is therefore not verifiable from this source.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from historical cold therapy and compression to modern pneumatic cryo-compression, then to the Nordic Hamstring Exercise as a standardized eccentric fatigue model. The paper’s own citation base reaches back to “Cold for centuries: a brief history of cryotherapies” [PAGE 361], and the Introduction frames cooling as reducing tissue temperature and modulating blood flow and inflammatory responses [PAGE 350]. The compression lineage is separate: intermittent pneumatic compression is linked to lactate clearance, perfusion, and swelling/stiffness reduction, with the paper citing Chleboun and colleagues on eccentric exercise-induced swelling, stiffness, and strength loss [PAGE 360]. The hamstring lineage is also distinct: the NHE is adopted because it has “causal evidence for reducing hamstring injury incidence in randomized controlled trials” [PAGE 353]. The present trial fuses these lineages into a 3-day, twice-daily, 10-min protocol with a 14-day crossover washout [PAGE 351].

Multi-week traps are numerous. First, the 14-day washout is intended “to minimize carryover effects” [PAGE 351], but that is an assumption, not a measurement. If a recovery modality has effects lasting longer than 14 days, the second period is contaminated. Second, the intervention is only 3 days, yet the title and abstract use “Acute Impact,” and the longest follow-up is 48 h [PAGE 350–351]. A claim about multi-week adaptation, chronic recovery, or injury prevention is not supported. Third, the paper warns that “repeated cold-water immersion can blunt anabolic signaling and satellite-cell responses” [PAGE 351], citing Roberts et al. This is a critical counter-lineage: acute recovery benefits can coexist with attenuated long-term hypertrophy or strength adaptation if cold is used chronically around resistance training. Fourth, older adults show delayed onset: the CC advantage in older adults became significant at T3 rather than T2 [PAGE 355], and at 48 h MVC under CC remained only 69.1% of baseline versus 46.2% under Sham [PAGE 355]. A clinician who extrapolates the athlete T2 response to older adults will mistime the expected benefit. Fifth, the paper itself limits generalization: “Results may not generalize to injured/high-risk patients or other devices/doses” [PAGE 363]. Sixth, the trial did not measure skin or intramuscular temperature or real-time cuff pressure [PAGE 361], so the historical “3 °C/75 mmHg” label is a device genealogy, not a demonstrated tissue dose. The correct historical reading is therefore: cryo-compression has a plausible acute recovery lineage, but this trial does not establish a chronic-adaptation benefit, a uniform age-independent response, or a precise thermal-mechanical mechanism.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem is three-layered: participant expectation, therapist knowledge, and assessor blindness. The participant experiences a strong sensory contrast between active 3 °C/75 mmHg and sham 15 °C/15 mmHg [PAGE 352], so the participant may infer allocation. The paper admits, “no formal blinding assessment was performed. Therefore, the success of participant blinding could not be verified and should be considered uncertain” [PAGE 354]. The therapist delivering the intervention was necessarily unblinded; only the assessors, data-entry personnel, and statisticians were blinded [PAGE 354]. This creates a recursive risk: the therapist’s behavior, encouragement, and wrap application could subtly differ by condition; the participant’s subjective TQR and Borg ratings could be shaped by expected benefit; and the blinded assessor records those ratings without knowing allocation. The paper itself notes that “subjective benefits may be influenced by training background and recovery expectations” [PAGE 360]. Therefore, TQR should be treated as a perceptual outcome, not as independent proof of tissue recovery.

Crisis habit regression appears when a clinician under time pressure regresses to the simple habit “cold + compression = faster recovery” and then overextends it: using T2 as if it were 48-h recovery, claiming complete restoration, or applying the athlete timeline to older adults. The counter-trap is that T2 is explicitly an immediate post-session response: “T2 was assessed immediately after the first intervention session and therefore does not represent recovery in isolation” [PAGE 359]. A second counter-trap is the incomplete-recovery signal: at 48 h MVC remained below baseline in all populations [PAGE 355], and stiffness remained above baseline under both conditions [PAGE 357]. A third counter-trap is population timing: older adults separated at T3, not T2 [PAGE 355]. The clock is therefore not a single 48-h endpoint; it is a T0–T4 sequence with a 14-day washout, six sessions over three days, and a 48-h horizon. The correct recursive inference is: the participant may know more than the assessor about condition; the therapist knows allocation but should not influence assessment; the assessor is blind but cannot verify participant blindness; and the analyst must not convert subjective recovery or acute T2 physiology into a claim of complete multi-week recovery.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the trial reports device set-points of 3 °C and nominal 75 mmHg active versus 15 °C and 15 mmHg sham [PAGE 352], but it did not measure skin temperature, intramuscular temperature, or real-time cuff pressure. The authors state, “we did not directly measure individual skin or intramuscular temperatures, nor did we log real-time cuff pressures at the limb. As a result, we cannot quantify the actual mechanothermal dose delivered at the tissue level” [PAGE 361]. Therefore, any claim that a specific tissue temperature or intramuscular pressure was achieved is vetoed. Biomechanical-layer veto: stiffness was measured with MyotonPRO, but “myotonometry ... does not capture deeper muscle or tendon adaptations, and we did not employ shear-wave elastography or MRI” [PAGE 361]. The trial also did not directly quantify edema, interstitial fluid shifts, fascial mechanics, or intramuscular hemodynamics [PAGE 360]. Therefore, any claim that the mechanism is proven edema reduction, fascial unwinding, or intramuscular blood-flow restoration is vetoed. Functional-layer veto: MVC was measured with handheld dynamometry in healthy participants; the paper states results “may not generalize to injured/high-risk patients” [PAGE 363]. Therefore, any claim of injury-prevention efficacy from this acute recovery trial is vetoed. Weather/environmental-layer veto: the only environmental control reported is “room temperature maintained between 20 - 22 °C and relative humidity kept stable across centers” [PAGE 351]. There is no outdoor weather, altitude, barometric pressure, or seasonal exposure variable. Therefore, any claim that weather, altitude, or ambient temperature moderated the CC effect is vetoed. Measurement-layer veto: PPT is described in kPa in Methods [PAGE 354] but reported in N/cm² in Supplementary Table S4 [PAGE 367]; this unit mismatch must be resolved before any exact PPT effect-size claim. Numerical-layer veto: the narrative MVC T4 difference of +95.4 N [PAGE 355] conflicts with Supplementary Table S1’s 87.88 N [PAGE 364], and the narrative stiffness differences of -21.7, -9.0, and -26.3 N/m [PAGE 357] conflict with Supplementary Table S2’s -57.44, -38.45, and -61.95 N/m [PAGE 365]. Therefore, any claim that quotes a single precise point estimate without naming the source table is vetoed as under-specified. Safety-layer veto: “No adverse events were reported” [PAGE 350] supports tolerability in this healthy cohort, but it does not prove safety in patients with vascular disease, cold intolerance, or sensory neuropathy. Any generalized safety claim is vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR Scenario 1 — “Complete recovery by 48 h.” Veto trigger: any output claiming MVC or stiffness returned to baseline at T4. Verifiable prediction if the claim were true: T4 MVC under CC should be 100% of baseline in all three populations, and T4 stiffness should be 100% of baseline under CC. Observed verifiable values: MVC at T4 under CC was 96.1% in MMA athletes, 76.3% in young adults, and 69.1% in older adults [PAGE 355]; stiffness at T4 under CC remained 18.0% above baseline in MMA athletes and young adults and 32.1% above baseline in older adults [PAGE 357]. The paper also states, “full mechanical normalization was not achieved by 48 h under either condition” [PAGE 357]. RLVR reward: 0 for the complete-recovery claim; positive reward only for “accelerated or partial recovery.”

RLVR Scenario 2 — “Participant blinding was verified.” Veto trigger: any output claiming successful participant blinding. Verifiable prediction if the claim were true: a formal blinding assessment or blinding index would be reported, and condition discrimination would be absent or quantified. Observed verifiable text: “no formal blinding assessment was performed. Therefore, the success of participant blinding could not be verified and should be considered uncertain” [PAGE 354]. RLVR reward: 0 for “verified blinding”; positive reward only for “blinding was attempted but unverified.”

RLVR Scenario 3 — “The tissue received 3 °C and 75 mmHg.” Veto trigger: any output claiming measured tissue temperature or measured cuff pressure. Verifiable prediction if the claim were true: the trial would report skin temperature, intramuscular temperature, and real-time cuff-pressure logs. Observed verifiable text: “we did not directly measure individual skin or intramuscular temperatures, nor did we log real-time cuff pressures at the limb” [PAGE 361]. RLVR reward: 0 for tissue-dose claims; positive reward only for “device set-points were 3 °C and nominal 75 mmHg, with unmeasured tissue dose.”
