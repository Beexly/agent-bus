<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

Hull’s 1943 system is a terminus in the deductive-behaviorist lineage that runs from Thorndike’s Law of Effect through Pavlovian excitation gradients and Tolman’s expectancy variables. The threshold-oscillation hypothesis itself draws on psychophysical tradition: the ogive or sigmoid learning curve descends from Fechnerian and Thurstonian cumulative-normal thinking, where difficulty and sensitivity determine whether a plotted performance curve shows an initial lag. The source explicitly invokes Thurstone’s finding that sigmoid forms appear only for difficult material [PAGE 334], positioning Hull’s corollary as a theoretical explanation for a psychometric regularity. Generalization of excitation, the mechanism that lifts sE_R(0) from zero to 7 wats, is Pavlovian in origin: a stimulus similar to the reinforced stimulus evokes a fraction of the original excitatory potential, so the organism does not begin learning from a null state.

Multi-week trap one: misattribution of the lag phase. In a multi-week protocol, if generalization gives a high initial sE_R, week-one performance may be strong with no visible acceleration. A modeler expecting a classic ogive may infer a “fast learner” and underestimate the amount of naive habit strength that must still be built. By week three the curve may have reached its asymptote; any subsequent flattening or decline is then misread as fatigue, satiation, or extinction when it is mathematically just the ceiling produced by a high starting point and rapid k.

Multi-week trap two: confounding k with motivational drive. The source treats “rate of learning” as the fractional increment k [PAGE 334]. In applied multi-week settings, an apparently rapid curve may reflect high sE_gen rather than high k. If the reinforcer is later devalued, the effective k drops, and the curve may retroactively reveal an ogive that was hidden by generalization. A model that credits all early speed to k will misestimate parameters and mis-time intervention.

Multi-week trap three: extinction of the generalization component. sE_gen is not necessarily permanent. Over weeks, as the specific reinforced contingencies dominate, the contribution of generalized excitation may decay while true habit strength sH continues to accumulate slowly. The observed curve can therefore show an early pseudo-plateau followed by a late, slow rise—a pattern that violates a single-phase sigmoid fit and tempts the analyst to posit two unrelated learning processes when one decomposition (high initial sE_gen plus slow k) suffices.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and the trial clock.

Recursive theory of mind here concerns the nested modeling loop: the observer builds a model of the learner’s habit structure, the learner’s behavior updates that model, and the observer’s revised model changes the reinforcement contingencies delivered to the learner. Under the Hullian formalism, p_t is an aggregate probability; at the individual level the reaction is all-or-none. A crisis—emotional perturbation, drive shift, or unexpected nonreinforcement—can induce habit regression by temporarily raising the effective threshold Θ or lowering sE_R. The learner’s p_t collapses even though sH may be unchanged, because the threshold-oscillation operator places fewer trials above threshold.

Crisis habit regression. When crisis strikes early in training (low t, low sH), the margin between sE_R and the population threshold is thin; a small upward shock to Θ pushes a large fraction of the individual all-or-none units below threshold, causing a steep drop in observed p_t. Recovery is slow because sH itself is still small. When crisis strikes late (high t, high sH), the same shock produces only a shallow dip because sE_R exceeds Θ for most of the population with margin to spare.

Counter-trap. The observer, attributing the post-crisis drop to “forgetting” or “failure of the current habit,” may reintroduce primary reinforcement at an intensity calibrated for a naive learner. This re-immerses the organism in a high-generalization context, artificially re-inflating sE_R(0) for the next phase. The counter-trap is that the observer treats a performance regression as a learning deficit and, by doing so, resets the initial conditions in a way that masks the true habit strength. The model then fits a new high starting point and concludes the learner is “rapid,” when in fact the original sH was never accurately assessed.

Clock. The trial index t functions as the clock of the system. Crisis impact is a function of t: Δp_crisis(t) ≈ Pr[ sE_R(t) − Δ_crisis ≥ Θ ] − p_t, where Δ_crisis grows as the trial approaches an expected reinforcement omission. Because sE_R(t) is monotonically increasing under normal acquisition, the same crisis magnitude has time-varying effect. The recursive loop closes when the observer’s belief that “the learner is in crisis” alters the schedule (e.g., easier trials, more reinforcement), which changes sE_R via generalization, which then confirms the observer’s crisis attribution. This self-fulfilling recursion is invisible if one analyzes p_t alone without modeling the observer’s contingent interventions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto layers.

Any predicted probability-of-reaction curve from the Hullian operators must pass through three veto layers before it can be claimed as observable.

Physical layer. Neural reaction potential has a finite dynamic range. Let sE_max^phys be the ceiling imposed by maximum firing rates and metabolic limits, measured in wats. The physical veto is

p_t^{phys} = Pr[ min( sE_R(t), sE_max^phys ) ≥ Θ ].

If a model projects sE_R(t) → ∞ and p_t → 1, the physical layer vetoes that projection whenever sE_max^phys < θ_{95}, the 95th percentile of the threshold distribution. Breakdown regime: when even maximal physiological potential cannot exceed the upper tail of F_Θ, perfect performance is structurally impossible regardless of learning history.

Biomechanical layer. The observable response is executed by musculature with refractory periods and force-velocity constraints. Let r_max be the maximum sustainable response frequency per unit time. Even if p_t^{model} = 1, the measured reaction probability per unit time cannot exceed biomechanical throughput:

p_t^{obs} = p_t^{model} · I( r_t ≤ r_max ).

Breakdown regime: when the required response rate for expressing the learned habit exceeds r_max, the curve plateaus at a value below the habit-strength asymptote, producing a false ceiling that looks like learning termination but is actually motor saturation.

Weather/environmental layer. Ambient conditions (temperature, noise, humidity) alter sensory and motor thresholds, widening F_Θ. Let σ_Θ(t) denote the time-varying threshold standard deviation induced by environmental variance. The environmental veto is

p_t^{env} = 1 − F_Θ( sE_R(t); σ_Θ(t) ).

Breakdown regime: when σ_Θ(t) becomes comparable to or larger than sE_R(t), the threshold distribution swamps the signal and p_t collapses toward chance-level noise, erasing the learning curve shape entirely.

Adjudication rule. The admissible observed curve is bounded by

p_t^{obs} ≤ min( p_t^{phys}, p_t^{bio}, p_t^{env} ).

If empirical data fall below the Hullian prediction, the veto hierarchy identifies the layer: a low early p_t despite high sE_R(0) and rapid k first implicates the biomechanical layer (response cannot be emitted fast enough), then the environmental layer (threshold variance too high), and only finally the central learning parameters. This prevents misreading a motor or environmental ceiling as an absence of positive acceleration in the habit curve.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario A — High initial potential with rapid increment. Conditions: sE_R(0) = 7 wats, k = 1/10 [PAGE 334]. Model prediction (Corollary IV): no initial positive acceleration; p_t is convex or approximately linear from trial 1, with the maximum slope at or near the outset. Verifiable veto: if trial-by-trial reaction probability shows statistically significant initial positive acceleration (d²p_t/dt² > 0 over the first five trials), then either sE_R(0) was not appreciably above zero or generalization did not contribute as assumed. The model is vetoed; the all-or-none threshold hypothesis or the independence of generalization from habit must be revised.

Scenario B — Difficult material and the sigmoid. Conditions: material classified as difficult per Thurstone’s criterion cited on [PAGE 334], implying sE_R(0) ≈ 0 and relatively slow effective k. Model prediction: sigmoid/ogival curve with an initial lag, visible positive acceleration, and later deceleration. Verifiable veto: if difficult material nevertheless yields an immediate growth-type curve with no initial acceleration, then the difficult/easy distinction is not mediated by initial sE_R and k as posited; Thurstone’s condition (10) is insufficient to determine curve shape. The sigmoid prediction is falsified for that population.

Scenario C — Nominal rate versus effective increment. Conditions: a treatment labeled “rapid” (nominal k = 1/10) but with reinforcement magnitude devalued so the effective fractional increment drops toward 1/20. Model prediction: because k is the operative control parameter for the disappearance of the ogive [PAGE 334], reducing effective k should restore the initial period of positive acceleration even though the schedule is nominally rapid. Verifiable veto: if the curve remains non-ogival despite effective k ≈ 1/20, then k is not the sole determinant; an additional variable (e.g., drive D or incentive magnitude) must enter the learning operator. Prediction for the vetoed world: manipulating reward magnitude while holding schedule constant transforms growth-type curves back into ogival curves.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
