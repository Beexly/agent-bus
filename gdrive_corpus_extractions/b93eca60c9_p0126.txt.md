Tolman's 1932 *Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men* sits at the pivot between Thorndikian connectionism and the cognitive revolution. Tolman counters this with purposive behaviorism: the rat acts toward an expected goal on the basis of an internal cognitive representation rather than a stamped-in habit. The detention-chamber result on [PAGE 108]—that a rat eventually prefers the shorter-detention route—supports an expectancy model: the rat represents the future cost of each path and selects accordingly.

A veto proof specifies conditions under which Tolman's force-resistance prediction must fail or be reinterpreted. Three layers are considered.

**Physical layer.** For an inclined path of angle \(\theta\) and length \(L\), the gravitational work difference relative to the horizontal path is
\[
\Delta W_g = m g L \sin\theta.
\]
If \(\theta\) is sufficiently small that \(\Delta W_g\) falls below the rat's work-detection threshold \(\Delta W_{\min}\), the physical layer vetoes any force-based preference. Moreover, if the incline is equipped with a low-friction surface such that the coefficient of kinetic friction \(\mu_k\) approaches zero, the additional resistive work vanishes, and the two paths are mechanically degenerate despite differing geometry. Under these physical conditions, Tolman's prediction [PAGE 108] that rats prefer the horizontal route is vetoed by the absence of a work differential.

**Biomechanical layer.** Rat locomotion involves limb-swing dynamics, center-of-mass oscillation, and tendon elasticity. The metabolic cost of incline locomotion is not equal to the mechanical work \(m g \Delta h\) because of muscle efficiency \(\eta\) (typically \(0.2\text{--}0.3\)) and the cost of force production. If the vertical displacement \(\Delta h\) is small—say, less than \(5\text{--}10\,\mathrm{cm}\)—the incremental metabolic cost is below the just-noticeable difference in energy expenditure, estimated at roughly \(1\text{--}2\%\) of basal metabolic rate per trial. The biomechanical layer thus vetoes force discrimination for shallow inclines, even when a physical work difference exists in principle.

**Weather/environmental layer.** Although Tolman's maze is presumably indoors, ambient temperature \(T_a\) alters thermoregulatory load. If the room temperature deviates from the rat's thermoneutral zone (~\(28\text{--}30\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)) by more than \(5\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), the cost of maintaining body temperature dwarfs the incremental cost of climbing an incline. A thermal veto occurs when
\[
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{thermo}} \gg \frac{\Delta W_g}{\eta\, \Delta t},
\]
so that path choice is governed by thermal comfort rather than mechanical work. Humidity and air flow can similarly introduce confounds by altering evaporative cooling and odor transport.

**Veto adjudication rule.** The force-resistance hypothesis is operationally supported only when (i) \(L_1 = L_2\), (ii) \(\tau_1 \approx \tau_2\) within the second-scale temporal resolution, (iii) \(|\Delta W| > \Delta W_{\min}\), (iv) ambient conditions are held within the thermoneutral zone, and (v) the animal is not in a crisis habit-regression state. Since Tolman explicitly states [PAGE 108] that no such experiment has been carried out, the force-resistance operator remains an unverified theoretical construct pending a design that passes all five veto layers.

Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit.

The source passage ([PAGE 108]) establishes an empirical asymmetry that Tolman treats as a boundary condition for rat cognition. In the detention-chamber paradigm, a rat is held for four minutes on one side and one minute on the other; after sufficient exposure it "would sooner or later come to take the left side only." Tolman generalizes this to the claim that rats can learn "to take the shorter of two alternative routes which differ in temporal Iengths only." Crucially, he immediately qualifies this result as "very gross—not nearly refined enough to explain the results for the spatial differences reported above." The inferential hop is this: when time differences are manipulated directly and massively (minutes versus minutes), rats eventually discriminate them. But when the same time differences arise incidentally from spatial path geometry, they are "a matter of mere seconds," and the rats' performance on those spatial tasks cannot be explained by this coarse temporal channel. This dissociates a slow, explicit temporal valuation system from a faster, spatially tuned path-selection system.

The second hop enters the "Force resisting Distances" section. Tolman explicitly states that "no actual experiment has, so far as we know, been carried out to demonstrate the reality or non reality of such force resisting preferences." He then offers a theoretical belief: given two paths equal in spatial length and temporal duration, but differing in physical work—e.g., one horizontal, one inclined—rats should eventually prefer the path involving "less forte-resistance." He imagines a maze (Figure 34) with paths P1 and P2 from starting box S to food chambers F1 and F2, one horizontal, one inclined, and predicts eventual preference for the lower-work route. He adds a second case where the inclined path slopes downward, though the OCR rendering makes the exact directional prediction ambiguous. The gray zone here is substantial: Tolman moves from a demonstrated fact (gross temporal discrimination) to an unverified belief (force/work discrimination) without an experimental bridge, and the provided text cuts off mid-sentence at "the horzontal path would probably involve," leaving the biomechanical rationale incomplete.

A third hop connects this to modern formalisms. If path value is a weighted sum of spatial length L, temporal cost τ, and mechanical work W, then the rat's choice operator is something like D = argmin_i(αL_i + βτ_i + γW_i), where α, β, and γ are sensory-motor weighting coefficients. Tolman's data suggest β is active at the minute scale but blind at the second scale for pure temporal contrasts, while spatial behavior somehow exploits sub-second temporal structure. The force term γ remains entirely speculative in 1932. A multi-hop audit must flag three breakdown regimes: (1) temporal singularity, where |Δτ| falls below the minute-scale just-noticeable difference and choice collapses to chance; (2) force degeneracy, where L and τ are matched but |ΔW| is below the work-detection threshold; and (3) motivational saturation, where hunger or stress overwhelms the cost-minimization operator. The absence of any citation to an actual force-resistance experiment means that every downstream inference about "work minimization" in rats is, at this point in the text, a theoretical extrapolation rather than an empirical operator.

Textual instability is itself a research trap. The OCR renders P1 and P2 interchangeably, and the downward-incline prediction is garbled, so secondary literature may harden a directionality Tolman did not firmly establish. Any modern replication must specify the incline angle, the coefficient of friction, and the exact work differential before the hypothesis can be tested.

Let the set of admissible maze paths be
\[
\Gamma = \{\gamma : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^3 \mid \gamma(0)=S,\ \gamma(1)=F_i,\ i\in\{1,2\}\}.
\]

Define three path-cost functionals:

1. Spatial length:
\[
L[\gamma] = \int_0^1 \left\|\dot{\gamma}(s)\right\|\, ds \quad [\mathrm{m}].
\]

2. Temporal cost (travel plus detention):
\[
\tau[\gamma] = \int_0^1 t(s)\, ds \quad [\mathrm{s}],
\]
where \(t(s)\) includes running time plus any imposed detention time.

3. Force-resistance work:
\[
W[\gamma] = \int_0^1 \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{resist}}(\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s)) \cdot \dot{\gamma}(s)\, ds \quad [\mathrm{J}],
\]
with the gravitational component
\[
W_g = m g \Delta h \quad [\mathrm{J}],
\]
where \(m\) is rat mass \([\mathrm{kg}]\), \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and \(\Delta h\) is vertical displacement \([\mathrm{m}]\).

Tolman's choice operator is hypothesized to be
\[
D(\gamma_1,\gamma_2) = \arg\min_{i\in\{1,2\}} \Bigl\{ \alpha L_i + \beta \tau_i + \gamma W_i + \varepsilon_i \Bigr\},
\]
with \(\alpha\) in cost-per-meter, \(\beta\) dimensionless relative to temporal cost, and \(\gamma\) in cost-per-joule. A softmax decision rule gives
\[
P(i) = \frac{\exp[-\lambda(\alpha L_i + \beta \tau_i + \gamma W_i)]}{\sum_{j=1}^2 \exp[-\lambda(\alpha L_j + \beta \tau_j + \gamma W_j)]},
\]
where \(\lambda\) is inverse temperature.

* **Temporal singularity:** From [PAGE 108], "the time differences discriminated in the experiment just cited were a matter of minutes," whereas spatially differentiated paths involve "mere seconds." * **Force degeneracy:** Tolman stipulates L1 = L2 and temporal equality, but predicts preference only if one path involves more "work," i.e. \(W_1 \neq W_2\). If \(|W_1-W_2| < \Delta W_{\min}\) (the just-noticeable work difference), the force term vanishes.
* **Motivational saturation:** Under high food deprivation, drive \(h\) may dominate all cost terms, rendering \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma \to 0\) behaviorally.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage.

Tolman's 1932 *Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men* sits at the pivot between Thorndikian connectionism and the cognitive revolution. Thorndike's Law of Effect posits that responses followed by satisfaction are "stamped in," a trial-and-error mechanism that requires repeated reinforcement to shape behavior. Tolman counters this with purposive behaviorism: the rat acts toward an expected goal on the basis of an internal cognitive representation rather than a stamped-in habit. The detention-chamber result on [PAGE 108]—that a rat eventually prefers the shorter-detention route—supports an expectancy model: the rat represents the future cost of each path and selects accordingly.

The force-resistance section extends this lineage into what we would now call metabolic or energetic optimality. Although Tolman explicitly notes [PAGE 108] that no experiment has demonstrated force-resistance preferences, his belief anticipates later work in locomotor energetics (e.g., the minimization of cost of transport) and modern model-based reinforcement learning, where an agent computes the expected cumulative cost of trajectories before acting. The shift from Hull's drive-reduction habit strength to Tolman's cognitive expectancy is a retro-causal move: later theories (Tolman's own 1948 cognitive-map paper, O'Keefe and Nadel's place cells, Friston's free-energy principle) reinterpret the 1932 maze as a testbed for internal models of spatial, temporal, and mechanical cost.

The "multi-week trap" is that longitudinal maze training over weeks can convert an initially expectancy-driven choice into an automatic habit, thereby masking the very discrimination Tolman describes. If a rat is run daily for three weeks in a force-resistance maze, a null preference in week four may not mean the rat cannot detect work differences; it may mean overtraining has regressed behavior to a rigid side-turning habit that no longer integrates the cost functional \( \alpha L + \beta\tau + \gamma W \). A second trap is the detention chamber itself: repeated four-minute detentions may function as aversive punishment, so the rat avoids the "right" side not because it discriminates four minutes from one minute, but because it has developed a conditioned aversion to the chamber. Tolman's "sooner or later" formulation [PAGE 108] is therefore vulnerable to a confound between temporal discrimination and punishment learning, a trap that only a counterbalanced, within-subject design can escape.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

What does the rat "know" when it chooses the left side after differential detention? Under Tolman's purposive framework, the rat maintains an internal model of the maze in which each path carries an expected temporal cost. It is not merely emitting a response; it is navigating an expectancy space. The recursive theory of mind enters when we consider who models whom. The experimenter models the rat as a temporal-discriminating agent; the rat, in turn, may model the experimenter's contingency (detention on one side) and adjust its path choice. If the experimenter misattributes the cause—reading "temporal discrimination" when the rat is actually responding to differential handling, olfactory residue, or a conditioned aversion to detention—the inference loop is corrupted at the second order.

Three conceptual elements structure this recursion:

**Crisis habit regression.** Under stress—long detention, high hunger, or repeated error—the rat may regress from flexible, model-based choice to a stereotyped habit (e.g., always turning left). This regression violates the optimality prediction \(D = \arg\min(\alpha L + \beta\tau + \gamma W)\) because the habit term dominates the cost functional. In crisis conditions, the rat's behavior ceases to reflect the integrated path cost and reflects instead a regressed motor program.

**Counter-trap.** The experimental apparatus itself can manufacture the preference it purports to measure. If the detention chamber on the right has a different smell, texture, or temperature than the left chamber, the rat's eventual preference for the left side may be a sensory preference rather than a temporal one. The counter-trap is to equate all non-temporal dimensions of the chambers; otherwise, the measured preference is an artifact of the detention hardware, not a property of the rat's internal clock.

**Clock.** Tolman's result depends on the rat's ability to represent duration on a minute scale ([PAGE 108]: "four minutes ... and only one minute"). Yet spatial choices involve "mere seconds," implying either a single clock with state-dependent resolution or multiple timing systems. If the rat's internal clock is accurate to minutes but blind to seconds, then any spatial path preference that tracks second-scale differences must be mediated by a different mechanism—perhaps optic flow, proprioceptive effort, or an integrated spatial map—rather than by the same temporal valuation system used in the detention task.

The recursive loop closes on theory choice: if we assume the rat is a cognitive agent minimizing an internal cost functional, we predict systematic path preferences under matched spatial and temporal conditions. If we assume the rat is a habit-driven organism, we predict regression to side bias under stress. The correct theory of mind determines whether a null result falsifies the force-resistance hypothesis or merely reveals a habit-regression regime.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

A veto proof specifies conditions under which Tolman's force-resistance prediction must fail or be reinterpreted. Three layers are considered.

**Physical layer.** For an inclined path of angle \(\theta\) and length \(L\), the gravitational work difference relative to the horizontal path is
\[
\Delta W_g = m g L \sin\theta.
\]
If \(\theta\) is sufficiently small that \(\Delta W_g\) falls below the rat's work-detection threshold \(\Delta W_{\min}\), the physical layer vetoes any force-based preference. Moreover, if the incline is equipped with a low-friction surface such that the coefficient of kinetic friction \(\mu_k\) approaches zero, the additional resistive work vanishes, and the two paths are mechanically degenerate despite differing geometry. Under these physical conditions, Tolman's prediction [PAGE 108] that rats prefer the horizontal route is vetoed by the absence of a work differential.

**Biomechanical layer.** Rat locomotion involves limb-swing dynamics, center-of-mass oscillation, and tendon elasticity. The metabolic cost of incline locomotion is not equal to the mechanical work \(m g \Delta h\) because of muscle efficiency \(\eta\) (typically \(0.2\text{--}0.3\)) and the cost of force production. If the vertical displacement \(\Delta h\) is small—say, less than \(5\text{--}10\,\mathrm{cm}\)—the incremental metabolic cost is below the just-noticeable difference in energy expenditure, estimated at roughly \(1\text{--}2\%\) of basal metabolic rate per trial. The biomechanical layer thus vetoes force discrimination for shallow inclines, even when a physical work difference exists in principle.

**Weather/environmental layer.** Although Tolman's maze is presumably indoors, ambient temperature \(T_a\) alters thermoregulatory load. If the room temperature deviates from the rat's thermoneutral zone (~\(28\text{--}30\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)) by more than \(5\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), the cost of maintaining body temperature dwarfs the incremental cost of climbing an incline. A thermal veto occurs when
\[
\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{thermo}} \gg \frac{\Delta W_g}{\eta\, \Delta t},
\]
so that path choice is governed by thermal comfort rather than mechanical work. Humidity and air flow can similarly introduce confounds by altering evaporative cooling and odor transport.

**Veto adjudication rule.** The force-resistance hypothesis is operationally supported only when (i) \(L_1 = L_2\), (ii) \(\tau_1 \approx \tau_2\) within the second-scale temporal resolution, (iii) \(|\Delta W| > \Delta W_{\min}\), (iv) ambient conditions are held within the thermoneutral zone, and (v) the animal is not in a crisis habit-regression state. Since Tolman explicitly states [PAGE 108] that no such experiment has been carried out, the force-resistance operator remains an unverified theoretical construct pending a design that passes all five veto layers.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
