A third hop is the critical causal gap: the review states that “the critical assumption that an enhanced DT ability transfers to creative on-ﬁeld actions has yet to be tested” [PAGE 1], and later that “the critical assumption that improving a player’s DT ability transfers to creative on-field actions remains untested so far” [PAGE 14]. A fourth hop is the alternative proposal: creativity is relational and product-centered, judged by experts within a social and cultural frame, and may arise from a rich sensorimotor skill repertoire rather than a separate DT ability. The source says “no product is creative as such; creativity is rather ascribed to the product, relative to its social context, by experts” [PAGE 2], and it recommends shifting from improving DT to “increasing his or her actual options in speciﬁc game tasks” [PAGE 15]. The final practical implication is stark: “Creativity, in turn, would not be the ability to be trained, but the result of situational skill training” [PAGE 15].

The gray-zone audit matters because the source is strong on construct mapping and weak on direct causal tests. It proves heterogeneity of conceptualization, the dominance of DT, the existence of trainability evidence for DT, and the absence of a direct DT-to-on-field transfer test. It does not prove that DT training fails; it proves that the transfer assumption is untested in the reviewed corpus. It does not prove that sensorimotor training succeeds; it proposes a functional-relational hypothesis and says empirical work is “currently underway” [PAGE 15]. It does not supply weather, biomechanical, or physiological measurements, so any cross-layer veto must be labeled as a logical or definitional constraint, not an empirical result from this paper. It also has author concentration: “Memmert and colleagues have contributed a substantial number of studies to the topic of interest (19 of the 38 studies reviewed; 17 of the 31 empirical studies)” [PAGE 10]. That does not invalidate the findings, but it raises the need for independent replication. The expert-rating pipeline also has a gray zone: game-test ratings require trained experts measured against a “golden standard,” while video tasks use Likert originality ratings and z-transformed composites [PAGE 10][PAGE 11]. Those ratings are not physics; they are socially situated judgments. The review itself notes that creativity is “local with respect to time and place” [PAGE 2]. Therefore, any strong inference must separate three claims: DT can be improved; DT improvement transfers to field creativity; creative field behavior is best explained by skill repertoire plus relational judgment. The source supports the first, leaves the second untested, and argues for the third as an alternative. That is the correct adjudication.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source supplies several formalizable operators. I distinguish extracted definitions from formal reconstructions, and I mark units and breakdown regimes.

1. PRISMA inclusion operator. The record counts are:
\[
N_{\text{identified}}=1823,\quad N_{\text{after dedup}}=1042,\quad N_{\text{title excluded}}=775,\quad N_{\text{final included}}=38.
\]
Units: records or articles. The final set is “31 empirical studies and seven theoretical papers” [PAGE 4]. Assumption: duplicate removal and screening are reproducible. Breakdown regime: duplicate-detection error, language or database coverage bias, or misclassification of theoretical versus empirical papers. The source reports six databases and a last search date of June 17th, 2019 [PAGE 3].

2. Four Ps classification operator. Each study is mapped to person, process, product, or press, and then to manifestation context and task [PAGE 4]. This is a categorical operator, not a continuous score. Breakdown regime: studies that mix approaches or lack a clear theoretical link require “reading between the lines,” which the authors explicitly acknowledge [PAGE 3]. A study assigned to “person” because it measures DT may still use a product rating or a process manipulation; the category is dominant emphasis, not exclusive ontology.

3. DT composite for video-based tasks. For player \(i\), scene or task \(j\), and component \(k\in\{F,X,O\}\):
\[
x_{i,j,F}=|R_{i,j}|,
\]
where \(R_{i,j}\) is the set of generated responses; units: count of responses.
\[
x_{i,j,X}=|C(R_{i,j})|,
\]
where \(C(R_{i,j})\) is the set of response classes such as shot, cross, or short pass; units: count of distinct classes.
\[
x_{i,j,O}=\frac{1}{|R_{i,j}|}\sum_{r\in R_{i,j}}\ell_r,\quad \ell_r\in\{1,2,3,4,5\},
\]
where \(\ell_r\) is the expert Likert originality rating; units: Likert points. The source describes scoring fluency as number of options, flexibility by grouping responses into classes, and originality by expert Likert ratings [PAGE 11]. The composite is:
\[
z_{i,j,k}=\frac{x_{i,j,k}-\bar{x}_{j,k}}{s_{j,k}},\quad s_{j,k}>0,
\]
\[
DT_{i,j}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k\in\{F,X,O\}} z_{i,j,k},
\]
and, across \(J\) tasks or scenes,
\[
DT_i=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}DT_{i,j}.
\]
Units: \(z_{i,j,k}\) and \(DT_i\) are dimensionless. The source states that “the three components (fluency, flexibility and originality) are first analyzed separately and subsequently averaged after z-transformation to obtain the player’s overall DT score” [PAGE 11]. Assumptions: common task battery, nonzero between-subject variance, comparable scenes, reliable expert originality ratings, and equal weighting of the three components. Singularity and breakdown regimes: if \(s_{j,k}=0\), the z-score is undefined; if scenes differ in difficulty, averaging without equating is invalid; if originality ratings are unreliable, the composite inherits that error; if fluency, flexibility, and originality are not exchangeable indicators, the equal-weight sum is a measurement convention rather than a latent variable. The source itself warns that DT tests do not equal creativity: “There are misunderstandings, the most notable that tests of DT measure creativity, which they do not” [PAGE 2].

4. Game-test expert rating operator. The source describes expert ratings of originality and flexibility in standardized game tasks, with three experts rating independently and ratings averaged; technical skill evaluation is to be avoided [PAGE 10]. A formal reconstruction is:
\[
C_i=\frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{i,t}^{(e)},\quad E=3,
\]
where \(c_{i,t}^{(e)}\) is an ordinal creativity rating for player \(i\), task \(t\), expert \(e\); units: rating scale points, dimensionless after standardization. Breakdown regime: low inter-rater reliability, expert drift across weeks, task-specific expertise mismatch, or contamination by technical execution despite the instruction to avoid it.

5. Dynamic overlap operator for exploratory behavior. The source reports actions defined on 51 categories for every 1 s interval, a 51-component binary vector per time window, principal component analysis, and “average dynamic overlap qd(t)” as a measure of exploratory behavior, with stationary value \(q_{\text{stat}}\) compared across conditions [PAGE 12]. A formal reconstruction is:
\[
b(t)\in\{0,1\}^{51},\quad t=1,\dots,T,
\]
\[
q_d(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\operatorname{sim}(b(\tau),b(\tau+t))\right],\quad q_d(t)\in[0,1],
\]
\[
q_{\text{stat}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}q_d(t),
\]
where \(\operatorname{sim}\) is a categorical similarity function and \(q_d(t)\) is dimensionless. Units: \(t\) is seconds; \(q_d\) and \(q_{\text{stat}}\) are dimensionless proportions. The source states that if players repeat the same action over observation time, the value is close to 1; if players explore all possible combinations, it is close to 0 [PAGE 12]. Assumptions: stable 51-category coding, stationarity for \(q_{\text{stat}}\), and sufficient observation length. Singularity and breakdown regimes: nonstationary behavior makes the limit undefined; category-coding drift across weeks makes \(b(t)\) non-comparable; 1 s binning can merge distinct actions or split continuous actions; \(q_{\text{stat}}\) near 0 or 1 saturates and loses discriminative resolution.

6. Creative-product indicator. Under the product-oriented view, an action is creative only if it is original relative to a reference set and functional for task success. A formal indicator is:
\[
C(a)=\mathbf{1}\left[O(a)\ge \theta_O \land F(a)=1\right],
\]
where \(O(a)\) is an originality score, \(\theta_O\) is a pre-specified threshold, and \(F(a)\in\{0,1\}\) is functional success. Units: binary indicator; \(O(a)\) in the local rating scale. The source says creative actions are “original (relative to the individual or group) and functional (i.e., they support task success)” [PAGE 13]. It also reports that goals with at least one action rated 8+ were defined as “highly creative” [PAGE 13]. Breakdown regime: threshold arbitrariness; same action judged differently across contexts; original but nonfunctional actions fail the definition; functional but statistically common actions are not original. This is exactly why the source says creativity is ascribed “relative to its social context, by experts” [PAGE 2].

7. Transfer hypothesis operator. The dominant DT account implies:
\[
H_{DT}:\quad \frac{\partial P(C=1)}{\partial DT_i}>0,
\]
where \(P(C=1)\) is the probability that an action or performance is judged creative. Units: probability per DT z-score. The alternative functional-relational account implies:
\[
H_{S}:\quad \frac{\partial P(C=1)}{\partial |\mathcal{A}_i(E)|}>0,
\]
where \(\mathcal{A}_i(E)\) is the set of functional action options available to player \(i\) in environment \(E\). Units: probability per available functional option. The source explicitly frames the unresolved test: “the critical assumption that an enhanced DT ability transfers to creative on-ﬁeld actions has yet to be tested” [PAGE 1]. Breakdown regime: if DT and skill repertoire are collinear, the partial derivatives are not separately identifiable; if expert ratings are context-dependent, \(P(C=1)\) is not a stable outcome; if the intervention does not faithfully manipulate DT or skill, the derivative is not causal.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from Guilford’s 1950 APA address, which the review identifies as the formal start of scientific creativity research and the origin of the psychometric push toward individual-difference creativity tests, through Guilford’s 1967 work and the Torrance Test of Creative Thinking [PAGE 2]. Rhodes’s 1961 four Ps model then supplied the classification scheme used by the review—person, process, product, press [PAGE 2][PAGE 4]. Sternberg and Lubart’s investment model and domain-specificity debates enter because the dominant sport-science approach treats tactical creativity as a moderately domain-specific DT ability inspired by Sternberg and Lubart’s integrative model [PAGE 10]. Westmeyer’s relational concept then provides the counter-lineage: the product is primary, and creativity is attributed by experts relative to social context [PAGE 2]. Csikszentmihalyi’s systems perspective and Gardner’s communal-judgment view reinforce that shift [PAGE 2]. Later, ecological-dynamics and functional approaches—Hristovski et al., Orth et al., Hossner et al.—reinterpret creative action as emergent from performer-environment-task constraints rather than from a prior idea in the head [PAGE 12][PAGE 13][PAGE 15]. The source’s own synthesis is retrocausal in the precise sense that it rereads the earlier DT corpus through a functional-relational lens: earlier studies remain valid as DT studies, but they do not establish DT as the cause of field creativity.

The multi-week traps follow from that genealogy. First, the construct-label trap: over a season, a program can claim to train “creativity” while actually training fluency on video tasks, movement variability in small-sided games, or coach-rated risk-taking. The review warns that “training programmes that claim to improve players’ ‘creativity’ in an undiﬀerentiated manner can be expectedly misleading” [PAGE 15]. Second, the transfer trap: a six-month attention-broadening program improved children’s DT [PAGE 5], but the review says the transfer from improved DT to creative on-field action remains untested [PAGE 1][PAGE 14]. Third, the longitudinal play-practice trap: one retrospective study reported that highly creative offensive professional players spent more time in unstructured play and sport-specific training [PAGE 7], while a prospective study found “No correlations ... between the amount of deliberate football play and players’ creative skill ratings at the age of 15 (academy), 17 (young professional), 20 (adult professional)” [PAGE 7]. That is a direct multi-week, multi-year inconsistency. Fourth, the expert-rating drift trap: if judges recalibrate across weeks or tournaments, an apparent change in creativity may be a change in the judge, not the player. Fifth, the intervention-fidelity trap: differential-learning and constraint-manipulation programs can improve CBATS components [PAGE 8], but if the outcome is a count of non-standard attempts rather than functional novelty, the program may reward mere deviation. The correct historical reading is therefore not “DT was wrong” but “DT was one historically rooted psychometric operator applied to a multi-level phenomenon; later functional and relational operators test a different causal pathway.”
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is where the review’s relational account becomes behaviorally sharp. In game situations, a player is not only solving a physical task; the player is modeling an opponent who is modeling the player. A creative action, in the source’s functional-relational sense, is an action that remains functional while falling outside the opponent’s predicted set. The review links creativity to “being less predictable to the opponent in game situations” [PAGE 2]. That is a recursive prediction problem: the player’s advantage depends on the opponent’s belief about the player’s likely action. If the player’s repertoire is narrow, the opponent’s model can be accurate even if the player’s DT score is high, because the player cannot execute the alternatives he or she can name.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under time pressure, fatigue, or score pressure, action selection tends to collapse toward well-trained attractors. The source’s ecological-dynamics section describes exploration as “hopping between attractors on diﬀerent hierarchical levels” and recommends “relaxing key constraints or suppressing habitual actions” to enhance exploratory behavior [PAGE 9][PAGE 12]. In a crisis, the opposite happens: constraints tighten, habitual actions dominate, and the observable repertoire shrinks. A player may still generate ideas on a video task, but the in-game clock does not allow a 45 s ideation window. The video task explicitly freezes the final frame and asks participants to name options within a given time frame, for example 45 s [PAGE 10]. That is not the same clock as a live pressing defender.

Counter-trap is the second failure mode. An opponent can deliberately offer a bait action that triggers the player’s habit, then close the counter-space. If the player’s only functional solutions are the habitual ones, the opponent’s recursive model wins. The source’s alternative hypothesis is that a refined sensorimotor repertoire allows “a variety of functional task-solutions” and thus makes the player harder to predict [PAGE 1][PAGE 15]. The counter-trap therefore tests whether the player can execute a second or third functional option, not merely whether the player can list one. This is why the review’s critique of the DT approach is not a critique of ideation in general; it is a critique of ideation decoupled from motor skill. The source quotes the dominant view that “Unlike motor competencies, it is possible to train tactical creativity independently of the movement techniques” [PAGE 10]. The recursive counter-trap predicts that this decoupling will fail precisely when the opponent forces a live action under constraint.

Clock is the third failure mode. The source describes “in-the-moment creativity” as enacted in the course of a game situation and emphasizes that skills liberate imagination, where imagination is the capacity to conceive possible courses of action that “must be felt to be possible” [PAGE 11]. The clock compresses the recursive loop: perceive, model opponent, simulate options, select, execute. DT video tasks may measure the simulation stage but not the execution stage. A player with high DT and low skill repertoire can lose at the execution node; a player with high skill repertoire can win by executing a solution the opponent did not model. The recursive prediction is therefore: under high time pressure, skill-repertoire richness should predict expert-rated creative actions better than DT, because the clock eliminates the long deliberative ideation window that DT tests reward.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether physical, biomechanical, or weather constraints can overrule a purely cognitive or DT-based explanation. The source does not contain weather measurements, force-plate data, joint-torque data, or surface-condition data. Therefore no empirical weather veto can be claimed from this paper. What the source does support is a definitional and logical veto: a creative action must be functional, and functionality is constrained by the performer-environment system. The source states that creative actions “emerge in the act rather than before ... [and] are as much a product of individual constraints as they are of the task and environment constraints” [PAGE 13]. It also recommends manipulating “task-relevant constraints” so players must adapt and explore alternative functional ways to reach the task goal [PAGE 15]. That is the adjudication layer.

Formally, let \(B_i\) be player \(i\)’s biomechanical capacity set, \(S_i\) the sensorimotor skill repertoire, and \(E\) the physical environment including surface, weather, and space. Let
\[
\mathcal{A}_i(E)=\{a:\ a \text{ is executable and functional given } B_i,S_i,E\}.
\]
If \(a\notin\mathcal{A}_i(E)\), then \(P(a\mid DT_i)=0\): a high DT score cannot cause an action that the body cannot execute or that the environment makes nonfunctional. This is not a toy ratio; it is a necessary-condition argument. The source’s product criterion requires functionality: creative actions are “original (relative to the individual or group) and functional (i.e., they support task success)” [PAGE 13]. Weather enters through functionality: if reduced traction or wind makes a action fail the task goal, then \(F(a)=0\), so the action fails the source’s creative-product criterion even if it is original. Biomechanics enters through executability: if range of motion, torque production, or balance limits \(a\), then the action is not in the affordance set. The DT account can generate the idea, but it cannot veto the body or the surface.

The proof is therefore layered. Physical and biomechanical veto: if \(B_i\) or \(S_i\) excludes \(a\), then no DT intervention alone can make \(a\) functional without also changing the body or skill. Weather veto: if \(E\) changes the affordance set such that \(a\) is no longer functional, then \(a\) cannot be creative under the product definition, regardless of ideation. The source supports the general constraint logic but not the specific weather claim. The correct label is “cross-layer veto by necessary condition,” not “empirically demonstrated weather effect.” This matters because the review’s alternative hypothesis is precisely that sensorimotor skill, not DT, is the proximal constraint on functional novelty. The source says the practical goal should shift toward “increasing his or her actual options in speciﬁc game tasks” [PAGE 15]. Actual options are cross-layer objects: they depend on perception, action capability, surface, and opponent. A DT-only account is underdetermined at that layer; a functional-relational account is not.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I propose three pre-registerable, verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each has a clear intervention, a measurable outcome, and a falsification predicate. These are not betting claims; they are experimental decision rules.

Scenario 1: DT-transfer veto. Randomize youth invasion-game players to a DT-training arm versus a constraint-led sensorimotor skill arm versus an active control. Primary outcomes: (a) the video-based DT composite \(DT_i\) as z-transformed fluency, flexibility, and originality; (b) expert-rated creative actions in live game-test or small-sided games, using the source’s product criterion of originality plus functionality [PAGE 10][PAGE 11][PAGE 13]. Prediction from the dominant DT account: the DT arm improves \(DT_i\) and also improves expert-rated creative actions relative to control. Prediction from the functional-relational alternative: the skill arm improves functional action variety and expert-rated creativity, while the DT arm improves \(DT_i\) without a corresponding field-creativity gain. Veto predicate: if the DT arm shows a pre-registered improvement in \(DT_i\) but the field-creativity effect is not distinguishable from zero, with a confidence interval excluding the minimal practically important effect, then the strong DT-transfer hypothesis is vetoed. The source supports the need for this test: “the critical assumption that an enhanced DT ability transfers to creative on-ﬁeld actions has yet to be tested” [PAGE 1].

Scenario 2: Exploratory-behavior sufficiency veto. Manipulate numerical disadvantage in small-sided games, following the source’s report that a numerical disadvantage led to more exploratory behavior and that the authors measured \(q_d(t)\) and \(q_{\text{stat}}\) [PAGE 12]. Outcomes: \(q_{\text{stat}}\), CBATS-style counts of standard, non-standard, successful, and unsuccessful actions, and independent expert ratings of functional novelty. Prediction from the exploratory-behavior account: disadvantage lowers \(q_{\text{stat}}\), indicating more exploration, and increases novel functional solutions. Veto predicate: if \(q_{\text{stat}}\) shifts as predicted but independent expert ratings of functional novelty do not increase, or if the only increase is in nonfunctional attempts, then exploratory behavior is not sufficient for creative product. This veto is important because the source distinguishes “attempts” from successful creative components in the CBATS logic [PAGE 11], and because the product definition requires functionality, not mere deviation [PAGE 13].

Scenario 3: Relational-judgment invariance veto. Present the same video-recorded actions to independent expert panels drawn from different competitive or cultural contexts. Outcomes: inter-panel agreement, context-dependent rating shifts, and the predictive weight of \(DT_i\) versus functional success. Prediction from the relational account: ratings should vary with panel context and should covary more strongly with functional success than with \(DT_i\), because creativity is “ascribed to the product, relative to its social context, by experts” [PAGE 2] and because evaluation is “local with respect to time and place” [PAGE 2]. Veto predicate: if ratings are invariant across panels and contexts and are strongly predicted by \(DT_i\) alone, then the strong relational-context claim is weakened. Conversely, if ratings shift by panel while \(DT_i\) remains fixed, the DT-as-creativity equation is vetoed as a measurement claim.

Across all three scenarios, the verifiable reward should be tied to pre-registered outcomes, not post hoc narrative. The source’s own conclusion supplies the decisive prediction: “Creativity, in turn, would not be the ability to be trained, but the result of situational skill training” [PAGE 15]. If that prediction is correct, then the strongest counterfactual veto is Scenario 1: DT improves, field creativity does not, and skill-based functional variety does. If Scenario 1 fails and DT transfer is demonstrated, the source’s alternative hypothesis must be revised. If Scenario 2 fails, exploration alone is insufficient. If Scenario 3 fails, relational judgment is weaker than the source claims. These are verifiable tests, not rhetorical positions.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
