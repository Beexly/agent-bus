The participation statistics are the measurable consequence: high-school girls were 7.5% of sport competitors in 1971–1972, 28% in 1976–1977, and 42.9% in 2018–2019 [PAGE 4-5]; college women were about 15% in the early 1970s and 46.9% in 2017–2018 [PAGE 4-5]; Tokyo 2020 is described as “the first ever gender-balanced games in history, with 49% of the athletes being women” [PAGE 5].

The second hop is the empirical residual. The source does not treat Title IX as eliminating all sex-linked performance differences. It reports that after rapid improvement in the 1970s and 1980s, elite sex differences “have remained relatively stable at approximately 8–12%” [PAGE 8]; that women’s running times have reached a plateau and “The remaining sex gaps in performance appear biological in origin” [PAGE 8]; and that “the gap in running performances between men and women is unlikely to narrow naturally” [PAGE 8]. It cites Thibault et al.’s event-specific gaps: 5.5% for 800m freestyle swimming, 36.8% for weightlifting, 10.7% mean for running, 17.5% for jumps, 8.9% for swimming races, 7.0% for speed skating, and 8.7% for cycling [PAGE 8]. The physiological mechanism is not a single chromosome but a developmental and endocrine cascade: adult males on average have larger body size, more muscle mass and strength, greater tendon force and stiffness, larger bone structure, lower body fat, stronger cardiovascular function, higher blood hemoglobin, and greater anaerobic and aerobic energy delivery [PAGE 7]; testosterone has dose-response effects on muscle mass and strength, bone size and density, and circulating hemoglobin [PAGE 7]. The source states that the evidence “makes it highly likely that the sex difference in circulating testosterone of adults explains most, if not all, the sex differences in sporting performance” [PAGE 7].

The third hop is the definitional rupture. Renée Richards’ case shows the conflict between a Barr-body/chromosome test and a multi-criterion definition based on surgery, hormones, external genitalia, social presentation, and psychological identity [PAGE 10-11]. The court “did not strike down the use of the Barr test, per se, but contended it should not be the sole defining criterion” [PAGE 11]. Caster Semenya’s DSD case produces the same rupture more sharply: she is described as “both female and not-female, male and not-male, depending on the definitional criteria and context” [PAGE 16]. At the policy layer, the IOC moved from 2004 requirements—surgery, legal recognition, hormone therapy, and eligibility no sooner than two years after gonadectomy [PAGE 12]—to the 2015 rule requiring a fixed female declaration for at least four years and serum testosterone below 10 nmol/L for at least 12 months before first competition [PAGE 13]. The IAAF DSD rule uses a lower threshold: below 5 nmol/L for at least six months [PAGE 16]. The NCAA requires one calendar year of testosterone suppression for a trans female before competition on a women’s team [PAGE 16]. High-school policy varies across five categories, from biological-essentialist birth-certificate rules to pure self-identification to no explicit policy [PAGE 17]. The gray-zone audit is therefore not “which definition is true”; it is “which authority, purpose, and context make a definition operative.” The source’s own answer is contextual: “which attribute is definitive depends on the purpose of the defining authority and context” [PAGE 16]. The remaining uncertainty is empirical and regulatory: the source cites an “urgent need” for properly designed intervention studies on transition, hormone therapy, trainability, and performance [PAGE 13-14]; it notes that transwomen retained a “9% faster mean run speed” after one year of suppression [PAGE 14]; that “the biological advantage enjoyed by transgender women is only minimally reduced when testosterone is suppressed” [PAGE 14]; and that strength “may be well preserved in transwomen during the first 3 years of hormone therapy” [PAGE 14]. This is the exact gray zone: threshold rules are administrable, but they are not metaphysical tests of womanhood, and they are not perfectly predictive of performance in every sport.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source supplies several explicit policy operators. They should be formalized with units, assumptions, and failure regimes rather than treated as universal definitions.

1. IOC 2015 eligibility operator for male-to-female transgender athletes. Let \(i\) be an athlete, \(t\) the competition date, \(g_i\) the declared gender identity for sporting purposes, \(\tau_i(t)\) the elapsed duration of that declaration, and \(T_i(t')\) the serum testosterone concentration in \(\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}\). A source-faithful Boolean is
\[
E_{\mathrm{IOC2015}}(i,t)=1
\iff
g_i=\mathrm{F},\;
\tau_i(t)\ge 4\ \mathrm{yr},\;
T_i(t')&lt;10\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}
\ \forall t'\in[t-12\ \mathrm{mo},t].
\]
The source states that the declaration “cannot be changed, for sporting purposes, for a minimum of four years” and that the athlete must show that serum testosterone “has been below 10 nmol/L for at least 12 months prior to her first competition” [PAGE 13]. Units: serum testosterone in \(\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}\); duration in months or years. Assumptions: the measurement is valid, the athlete is in the male-to-female category, and the policy authority is the IOC 2015 framework. Boundary regime: at \(T_i=10\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}\) the rule is discontinuous; exactly 12 months is a boundary condition. Breakdown regime: if the measurement method is an immunoassay rather than LC-MS, the source itself warns that conventional testosterone immunoassays are “particularly unreliable with respect to measuring low levels of testosterone” [PAGE 23]. The source also reports that 95% of males have 7.7 to 29.4 nmol/L and 95% of females have 0 to 1.7 nmol/L [PAGE 13], so a 10 nmol/L threshold is a buffer, not a clean sex separator.

2. IAAF DSD eligibility operator. For an athlete with a 46 XY Difference in Sex Development, the source describes a rule requiring legal recognition as female or as having a DSD, plus a blood testosterone level below 5 nmol/L for at least six months before competition and maintenance of that level [PAGE 16]. A compact predicate is
\[
E_{\mathrm{IAAF}}(i,t)=1
\iff
\bigl(\ell_i=\mathrm{F}\lor \mathrm{DSD}_i\bigr)
\land
T_i(t')&lt;5\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}
\ \forall t'\in[t-6\ \mathrm{mo},t].
\]
Units are again \(\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}\). Assumptions: the athlete falls under the IAAF DSD regulations; the threshold is measured under the relevant protocol. Boundary regime: \(T_i=5\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}\) is the discontinuity. Breakdown regime: this operator applies to DSD, not to all transgender women; conflating the IAAF DSD rule with the IOC transgender rule produces a category error. The source quotes the IAAF’s stated aim: “The IAAF respects the dignity of all individuals, including individuals with DSDs” and seeks “conditions on such participation only to the extent necessary to ensure fair and meaningful competition” [PAGE 16].

3. NCAA eligibility operator for a trans female student-athlete. The source states that a trans female “may not compete on a women’s team without changing it to a mixed team status until completing one calendar year of testosterone suppression treatment” [PAGE 16]. Thus
\[
E_{\mathrm{NCAA}}^{\mathrm{MTF}}(i,t)=1
\iff
\mathrm{suppression\ duration}_i(t)\ge 1\ \mathrm{calendar\ year}.
\]
Assumptions: the athlete is a trans female under NCAA policy, and the team remains a women’s team. Boundary regime: exactly one calendar year is the switching point. Breakdown regime: this is not the same as the IOC’s 12-month rule combined with the 10 nmol/L ceiling, nor the IAAF’s six-month DSD rule; the source’s policy patchwork means the same athlete can be eligible under one authority and ineligible under another [PAGE 16-17].

4. Performance-gap operator. For an event \(e\), let \(\bar P_{e,\mathrm{F}}\) and \(\bar P_{e,\mathrm{M}}\) be mean elite performance in a higher-is-better metric, such as speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The source’s reported gender gap can be represented as
\[
G_e
=
100\left(1-\frac{\bar P_{e,\mathrm{F}}}{\bar P_{e,\mathrm{M}}}\right)\%.
\]
This is not an invented causal law; it is a bookkeeping operator for the source’s reported percentages. The source reports \(G_e=5.5\%\) for 800m freestyle swimming and \(G_e=36.8\%\) for weightlifting, with a mean of 10.7% for running, 17.5% for jumps, 8.9% for swimming races, 7.0% for speed skating, and 8.7% for cycling [PAGE 8]. Assumptions: same event, comparable elite sampling, higher-is-better metric, and similar rules and equipment. Boundary regime: \(G_e=0\) means no measured gap under that metric. Breakdown regime: if the metric is lower-is-better, such as time, the sign must be inverted or the metric transformed; if females outperform males, as in open-water “ultra-distance” swimming [PAGE 8], then \(G_e&lt;0\) and any monotonic “male advantage” claim is false. The source also reports larger gaps in upper-body-strength events, including an 18% jump gap, 20% between fastest recorded tennis serves, and gaps exceeding 50% for fastest baseball pitches and field-hockey drag flicks [PAGE 8].

5. Participation-share operator. Let \(N_{\mathrm{F}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{M}}\) be female and male athlete counts. The source’s participation share is
\[
S_{\mathrm{F}}
=
100\frac{N_{\mathrm{F}}}{N_{\mathrm{F}}+N_{\mathrm{M}}}\%.
\]
The source reports \(S_{\mathrm{F}}\approx 7.5\%\) for high-school girls in 1971–1972, \(42.9\%\) in 2018–2019, and \(46.9\%\) for NCAA athletes in 2017–2018 [PAGE 4-5]. Assumptions: the denominator is the reported athlete count for the same level and year. Breakdown regime: the share does not measure fairness in head-to-head competition, media coverage, funding, or elite opportunity; it only measures numerical participation.

The cross-source breakdown is therefore not a single equation failure but an authority failure: the same athlete can satisfy \(E_{\mathrm{IOC2015}}\) while failing \(E_{\mathrm{IAAF}}\), or satisfy a self-identification state rule while failing a birth-certificate state rule [PAGE 17]. The source’s strongest formal conclusion is that eligibility is a predicate parameterized by authority, purpose, sport, event, age, and duration—not a context-free property.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from ancient and medieval contexts in which women’s sport could be part of mating rituals and a successful female competitor could be seen as a desirable mate [PAGE 1], through 19th-century medical energy conservatism, in which Clarke warned that “Force must be allowed to flow thither in an ample stream, and not diverted to the brain by the school, or to the arms by the factory, or to the feet by dancing” [PAGE 2]. That lineage is retrocausal in the precise sense that later regulatory categories inherit older assumptions: the chromosome test, the nude parade, the hormone threshold, and the self-identification rule are not isolated technical fixes; they are successive attempts to answer a question that was never merely biological—who counts as a woman for a particular institutional purpose. The source traces that succession explicitly: 1946 IAAF medical certificates, 1948 British and IOC follow-on rules, 1966 Barr testing, 1968–1998 IOC genetic testing, post-1999 individualized suspicion, the 2004 Stockholm Consensus, the 2015 IOC testosterone rule, the 2018–2019 IAAF DSD rule, the NCAA 2011 handbook, and the 2021 state legislative wave [PAGE 11-17]. The scientific lineage is equally layered: the 1992 Nature letter “wrongly” predicted that women would match men in the marathon by 1998 and in all running events within the first half of the 21st century [PAGE 6]; later work found plateaus and stable gaps [PAGE 8]; Thibault et al. quantified event-specific gaps [PAGE 8]; Handelsman, Hirschberg, and Bermon argued for a lower 5.0 nmol/L threshold using mass spectrometry [PAGE 14]; Hilton and Lundberg argued that the biological advantage of trans women is “only minimally reduced when testosterone is suppressed” [PAGE 14]; Roberts et al. found a residual 9% faster mean run speed after one year [PAGE 14]; Harper et al. found strength may be well preserved for three years [PAGE 14]. The multi-week traps are long-horizon policy traps, not short-term rhetorical traps. First, the threshold-ratchet trap: lowering the threshold appears more fair to cisgender women with high androgen levels, but it may exclude DSD athletes; raising it appears more inclusive, but it may preserve a competitive advantage. The source captures this with the IOC’s 10 nmol/L buffer and the IAAF’s 5 nmol/L DSD rule [PAGE 13, PAGE 16]. Second, the duration-illusion trap: 12 months, one calendar year, six months, four years, and two years after gonadectomy are administrable clocks, but the source’s evidence says suppression does not fully erase pubertal bone, muscle, hemoglobin, or strength advantages [PAGE 12-14]. Third, the binary-test trap: Barr bodies, chromosomes, genitalia, surgery, hormones, and self-identification each fail as a sole criterion, as Richards and Semenya show [PAGE 10-11, PAGE 15-16]. Fourth, the patchwork trap: the same high-school athlete can be eligible in California, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, Rhode Island, Vermont, or Washington under self-identification, but ineligible in Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, New Mexico, South Dakota, Tennessee, or Texas under biological-essentialist birth-certificate rules [PAGE 17]. Fifth, the measurement trap: policy thresholds are only as stable as the assay, and the source’s note on LC-MS versus immunoassay makes that explicit [PAGE 23]. These traps persist across news cycles because each solves one layer while creating another.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis, institutions exhibit habit regression: when faced with a definitional rupture, they reach for the most administrable prior—genital inspection, chromosome test, surgery requirement, or a single hormone cutoff—because those are legible to regulators even when they are poor proxies for fairness. The source shows this regression in the 2004 IOC’s surgical, legal, and hormonal requirements [PAGE 12] and in the earlier “nude parade” and Barr-test regimes [PAGE 11]. The counter-trap is the mirror image: an inclusion-first actor may treat self-identification as the only legitimate criterion and thereby ignore the source’s evidence that male puberty and testosterone exposure can create persistent performance advantages [PAGE 7, PAGE 14]. The source’s Women’s Sports Policy Working Group is explicitly trying to escape both traps: it rejects “the effort to exclude trans girls and trans women from girls’ and women’s sport” and also rejects “the effort to disadvantage females by forcing them to compete against some trans athletes with male sex-linked physical advantages,” concluding “There is a middle way” [PAGE 18]. The clock is the mediating variable. There is a developmental clock: before puberty, female swimmers under 10 outperform males, and top running speed is “almost identical in ten-year-old boys and girls” [PAGE 7]; by 14, the throwing gap “becomes a chasm,” and by 18 the average boy can throw three times as far as the average girl [PAGE 7]. There is a regulatory clock: four years for a fixed declaration, 12 months below 10 nmol/L, six months below 5 nmol/L for DSD, one calendar year of NCAA suppression, and two years after gonadectomy in the 2004 IOC rule [PAGE 12-16]. There is also a political clock: the source notes that as of May 2021, legislators in more than 30 states had introduced over 100 bills to limit transgender rights, more than half about sports participation [PAGE 17]. Recursive theory of mind therefore predicts that regulators will model the athlete’s privacy and dignity, athletes will model the regulator’s credibility, cisgender competitors will model the fairness of head-to-head competition, and politicians will model the salience of the issue to sports fans. The source gives evidence for each: the Richards court weighed medical evidence against “unfounded fears and misconceptions” [PAGE 11]; the Soule complaint alleges “emotional distress, anxiety, and even depression and nausea on an ongoing basis, race after race” [PAGE 19]; the Goldberg report suggests participation may “even increase alongside inclusive policies” [PAGE 20]; and Flores and O’Neill found that self-identified sports fans are generally opposed to transgender athletes competing, especially if they believe in traditional gender roles [PAGE 22]. The non-trap policy is not a single clock but a layered one: age, level of play, sport-specific biomechanics, duration of hormone exposure, and institutional purpose must be modeled together.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is admissible only if the source supplies an observable mechanism and a link to the competitive outcome. Formally, let \(\mathrm{Admit}(V_k)=1\) only when the source contains evidence \(O_k\) and a stated or clearly implied relation \(\mathrm{Link}(O_k,Y)\) to performance or eligibility. Under that rule:

Physical veto: admissible and conditionally decisive. The source documents average adult male advantages in body size, muscle mass, grip strength, tendon force and stiffness, bone structure, body-fat percentage, cardiovascular function, blood hemoglobin, and anaerobic and aerobic energy delivery [PAGE 7]. It also documents androgen-sensitive pathways: testosterone increases muscle mass and strength, bone size and density, and circulating hemoglobin [PAGE 7]. In events with large upper-body-strength demands, the measured gap is especially large: 36.8% in weightlifting and more than 50% in fastest baseball pitches and field-hockey drag flicks [PAGE 8]. This veto defeats any claim that self-identification alone can guarantee fair head-to-head competition in every elite event.

Biomechanical veto: admissible but event-specific and bidirectional. The source reports that females have “superior hydrodynamic properties” in longer swimming events and have outperformed males in open-water “ultra-distance” swimming [PAGE 8]. It also reports that male bone structure confers advantages that “cannot be negated by sex reassignment surgery or hormone therapy” [PAGE 21]. Therefore the biomechanical layer is not a one-way veto for males: it vetoes universal rules in both directions. A rule that assumes a fixed male advantage across all events is falsified by ultra-distance swimming; a rule that assumes no advantage is falsified by weightlifting and throwing events.

Weather veto: not admissible on this source. The source contains no meteorological data, no temperature, wind, altitude, humidity, or venue-condition measurements, and no weather-performance model. Under \(\mathrm{Admit}(V_k)\), the weather observable \(O_w\) is absent, so the weather veto cannot be validated. Any weather-based claim would require evidence outside the 23 pages and cannot be used to override the source’s eligibility operators.

Regulatory veto: admissible and decisive for definitional rupture. The source shows that the same athlete can be female under a birth certificate, male under chromosomal criteria, female under social duration, and male under a testosterone threshold [PAGE 15-16]. It also shows a five-category high-school patchwork, so a given athlete would “count” as female in some states but not others [PAGE 17]. This is the strongest cross-layer veto against any claim of a single national or global definition: the regulatory layer itself produces contradictory labels.

The adjudication is therefore: physical and biomechanical vetoes are real but conditional on sport, event, age, and metric; the weather veto is a null layer; the regulatory veto is decisive for the definitional dispute because it changes the eligibility predicate without changing the athlete.
