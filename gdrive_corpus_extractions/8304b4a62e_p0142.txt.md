\[

If any indicator is zero, \(Y_t = 0\). Economic optimization is therefore bounded by the minimum of the layer constraints. Scenario 1 (Wage-cut veto). Counterfactual: a firm employing labor at the efficiency wage \(w^*\) cuts wages to \(w' = 0.8w^*\) while holding hours constant. Verifiable prediction: effort \(e(w)\) drops below the critical threshold for sustained production within two weeks; output per labor-hour falls by at least 12%; the quit rate rises by at least 18% over four weeks. The veto is that the wage layer cannot be renegotiated downward without triggering the biomechanical/effort layer’s collapse, because \(e(w)\) is not continuous and convex near subsistence; it exhibits a breakdown regime [PAGE 142].

Scenario 2 (Capital overuse veto). Counterfactual: a firm raises capital utilization to \(u = 1.25 u_{\max}\) for four weeks, deferring maintenance to boost short-run output. Verifiable prediction: the depreciation rate \(\delta(u)\) spikes nonlinearly; by week three the probability of catastrophic failure exceeds 0.5; total factor productivity falls by at least 9% despite the higher utilization rate. The physical layer vetoes the economic layer’s desire to run capital “without regard to whether it works or not” [PAGE 142], because conservation is a binding constraint, not a preference.

Scenario 3 (Zero-wage sub-marginal labor veto). Counterfactual: a firm hires sub-marginal workers at \(w = 0\), treating them as analogous to free land [PAGE 142]. Verifiable prediction: because their marginal product is negative once disruption, supervision, and error-correction costs are included, total output decreases by at least 6% relative to leaving them idle; firm value falls despite the zero wage bill. The sub-marginal agency veto is \(MP_i < c_i\) even when \(c_i = 0\), falsifying the assumption that free or nearly free labor is always employable.
