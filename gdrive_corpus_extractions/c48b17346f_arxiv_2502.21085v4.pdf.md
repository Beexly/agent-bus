<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the primary information channel—shuttlecock trajectory—is degraded. Under occlusion, tracker dropout, or hit-frame misalignment, the model cannot execute CrossTrans MHCA validly because K and V derive from trajectory latents [PAGE 4–5]. The network then regresses to pose-dominant heuristics resembling TemPose-V (pose-only), which the paper shows struggles with fine-grained stroke distinctions such as top smash vs. top wrist smash and bottom smash vs. bottom wrist smash [PAGE 11, Fig. B]. This regression is “crisis” behavior: the model falls back to the coarser “hitting” action representation that broader SAR models use, exactly the failure mode BST was designed to avoid [PAGE 1].

The counter-trap is adversarial at the perceptual level. Players may deliberately perform deceptive movements to confuse opponents; the paper asserts trajectory contains no misleading information [PAGE 2]. However, if the trajectory tracker is itself deceived—by motion blur, occlusion by the racket or player body, or reflectance from indoor lighting—the CrossTrans similarity mechanism may align pose queries with corrupted trajectory keys and values, effectively amplifying the deception rather than rejecting it. The Clean Gate (CG) module attempts to filter trajectory noise using player–shuttlecock interaction strength [PAGE 5–6], but it cannot recover a trajectory that was never observed. Thus the counter-trap is a double bind: deception that fools the tracker forces the model to choose between a corrupted trajectory signal and a pose-only regression, both of which favor incorrect stroke-type logits.

The clock is the temporal constraint governing clip construction. The target hit frame must remain near the clip center to preserve the fixed-width strategy’s ideal characteristic [PAGE 4]; the actual time distance from target frame to clip boundaries is capped at 1.5 seconds [PAGE 14]; sequence length is fixed at 100 frames for ShuttleSet under the proposed strategy [PAGE 15]; and rally intervals satisfy h^{(i+1)}_1 - h^{(i)}_{n(i)} > h^{(k)}_{j+1} - h^{(k)}_j for rally separation [PAGE 13]. If the match pace accelerates such that inter-stroke intervals shrink below the clip radius, stage overlap increases and the three-stage information structure collapses; if pace slows, the clip pads with irrelevant frames. The clock therefore imposes a hard real-time bound on valid inference: outside the [t-\epsilon, t+\epsilon] temporal envelope around the hit frame, the model’s conditional probability P(stroke-type | pose, trajectory, position) is no longer estimable with the claimed accuracy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical veto. Badminton has the fastest ball speeds among all sports [PAGE 1]. At elite velocities, shuttlecock displacement between consecutive 30 or 100-frame clips may exceed the tracker’s receptive field, causing TrackNetV3 to drop the object. A missing shuttlecock trajectory nullifies SP input; per Table 1 vs. Table 2, removing SP collapses accuracy from BST-CG-AP 0.8254 to TemPose-PF levels near 0.7942 on ShuttleSet merged [PAGE 6]. Physical veto condition: if instantaneous shuttlecock speed exceeds the frame-rate-limited displacement threshold of the tracker, the trajectory input is vetoed and model accuracy degrades to pose-only SAR baselines (accuracy <= 0.7758, macro-F1 <= 0.7352) [PAGE 6, Table 1].

Biomechanical veto. The 3D pose estimator exhibits systematic facing-direction error: when forearms point forward, the model assumes the face points forward, misrepresenting the player’s actual orientation relative to the opponent [PAGE 12–13, Fig. C]. Table E quantifies this on ShuttleSet 35 classes: BST-CG-AP accuracy drops from 0.7695 (2D) to 0.7525 (3D), macro-F1 from 0.7043 to 0.6760, Acc-2 from 0.9267 to 0.9181 [PAGE 12]. Biomechanical veto condition: if HPE facing error > ~15 degrees or 3D joint depth error places the racket-hand joint behind the torso plane, strokes whose discrimination depends on orientation (net shot vs. defensive return drive, class ID 1 vs. 13 [PAGE 15, Table G]) are vetoed to chance-level confusion.

Environmental/weather veto. Broadcast videos introduce variable lighting and court-perspective shifts. In TenniSet, camera perspective shifts slightly left or right during a rally, requiring per-frame court detection rather than a single per-match calibration [PAGE 15]. If fewer than two people are detected inside court lines, pose and trajectory information are zeroed for that frame [PAGE 4, 14–15]. Spectators and referees in badminton clips further contaminate multi-person pose estimation unless court lines filter irrelevant poses [PAGE 4]. Veto condition: if court detection fails on the calibration frame and manual corner marking is unavailable, or if <2 players are on court for >10% of clip frames, the position input PP is invalidated and the model loses the position-fusion gain (e.g., TemPose-PF vs. TemPose-V improvement vanishes) [PAGE 6–7].

Adjudication hierarchy. A valid prediction requires all three layers to pass: (1) physical: tracker returns non-null trajectory within 1.5 s of hit frame [PAGE 14]; (2) biomechanical: 2D pose preferred over 3D unless facing error is below threshold [PAGE 12–13]; (3) environmental: court lines detected and >=2 players on court [PAGE 4, 14–15]. Failure at any layer forces fallback to BST-0 or TemPose-V baselines, removing the 0.03–0.04 accuracy margin that BST-CG-AP holds over TemPose-TF [PAGE 6, Table 1].
