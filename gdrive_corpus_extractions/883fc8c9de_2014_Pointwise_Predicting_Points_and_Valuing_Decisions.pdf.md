The source’s core operator is a conditional expectation over terminal possession points, not a terminal box-score tally. The paper states that “EPV is a conditional expectation” and that it is “the expected number of points the offense will score” given the spatial configuration at time \(t\) during the possession [PAGE 2]. This is the first multi-hop step: a possession is represented as a stochastic process with state \(X_t\), action \(A_t\), and terminal reward \(Y\in\{0,2,3\}\) under the paper’s half-court, no-foul simplification, so \(EPV(t)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X_t]\). The second step is the macro/microtransition decomposition. The authors write: “We call the former actions macrotransitions and the latter actions microtransitions” [PAGE 3]. Macrotransition probabilities are estimated with competing risks and spatial covariates; the value after a macrotransition is modeled with a coarsened court-space Markov chain; microtransition components rely on “basic mathematical assumptions of local space-time smoothness” [PAGE 3]. The third step is the Markov assumption: “We assume that the decision the ballhandler makes depends only on the current spatial configuration of the possession” [PAGE 3]. That assumption makes the decomposition estimable, but it is also the main gray zone: the observed spatial state is an equilibrium object containing defender anticipation, play-call history, fatigue, clock, and score context. If any of those hidden variables affect \(A_t\) conditional on \(X_t\), then \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid X_t)\) is a behavioral equilibrium probability, not an intervention probability. The paper’s own admission is decisive: “our model loses efficiency in estimating EPV by regarding players’ decisions as Markovian when their actions in fact execute a carefully designed sequence” [PAGE 6]. So the model is best treated as a real-time descriptive valuation engine, not a fully causal counterfactual engine.

A second gray-zone audit concerns attribution. EPV changes are not automatically additive player credit. The authors warn that “the sum of EPV changes that a player makes is zero by definition” [PAGE 4], which is why they define EPV-added over replacement rather than aggregate raw increments. EPVA compares a player’s realized EPV path to a league-average replacement player in the same situations; the paper reports “Chris Paul had the highest EPVA at +3.48 per game” and “Ricky Rubio had the lowest EPVA during 2012-13 at -3.33 points per game” [PAGE 5]. Those numbers are model-dependent, sampling-dependent, and baseline-dependent. The 2012-13 data cover only 13 arenas, and the paper says “Because player-tracking cameras were only installed in half of the league’s arenas in 2012-13” [PAGE 6]. The appendix quantifies the consequence: “Chris Paul (ranked 1st) and LeBron James (ranked 23rd, not shown) were only observed in away games, for 11 and 17 games, respectively” and “Paul shot 54% compared to his season average 48%, while James shot for 50% in our data compares to his season average 57%” [PAGE 9]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines EPV as a conditional expectation. Let \(t\) be possession time in seconds, \(X_t\) the full spatial state of players, referees, and ball at time \(t\), and \(Y\) the points scored by the offense by the end of the possession. Then
\[
\mathrm{EPV}(t)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X_t],\qquad [\mathrm{EPV}]=\text{points}.
\]
This matches the paper’s statement that EPV is “the expected number of points the offense will score” [PAGE 2]. The terminal reward is simplified because “we focus on half-court possessions and ignore fouls” [PAGE 3]; thus free throws and full-court transition are outside the stated scope.

For a small time window \(\Delta t\), let \(M=\{\text{pass},\text{shot},\text{turnover}\}\) be the macrotransition set and let \(R_t\) be the event that no macrotransition occurs in \((t,t+\Delta t]\). A discrete decomposition consistent with the paper’s description is
\[
\mathrm{EPV}(t)=\sum_{a\in M}\mathbb{P}(A_t=a\mid X_t)\,V_a(t;\Delta t)
+\mathbb{P}(R_t\mid X_t)\,\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{EPV}(t+\Delta t)\mid X_t,R_t\big],
\]
where \(V_a(t;\Delta t)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X_t,A_t=a]\) has units of points and each conditional probability is dimensionless. The source says the model conditions “on the ballhandler’s next action during a small time window” [PAGE 3]. A continuous-time competing-risk form, matching the paper’s use of “competing risks, a statistical framework for modeling the occurrence of discrete events in continuous time” [PAGE 3], is
\[
\mathrm{EPV}(t)=\int_t^{t+\Delta t} S(u-t\mid X_t)\sum_{a\in M}\lambda_a(u\mid X_t)V_a(u\mid X_t)\,du
+S(\Delta t\mid X_t)\,\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{EPV}(t+\Delta t)\mid X_t,R_t\big],
\]
with hazard \(\lambda_a\) in \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\), survival \(S\) dimensionless, and value \(V_a\) in points. The paper’s implementation uses situational covariates and “spatially-smoothed random effects that capture individual players’ tendencies” [PAGE 3]; a generic competing-risk representation is
\[
\lambda_a(t\mid X_t)=\lambda_{0a}(t)\exp\{f_a(X_t)+u_{i,a}\},
\]
where \(u_{i,a}\) is a player-specific spatial random effect and \(f_a\) is a learned spatial function. The coarsened court-space value component is a homogeneous Markov chain with row-stochastic transition matrix \(P_{jk}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t+\delta}=k\mid X_t=j)\), dimensionless [PAGE 3].

The replacement-based value metric is EPVA:
\[
\mathrm{EPVA}_i=\sum_{k=1}^{N_i}\left[\mathrm{EPV}_i(T_k)-\mathrm{EPV}_{\bar i}(t_k)\right],
\qquad [\mathrm{EPVA}]=\text{points per game},
\]
where \(T_k\) is the end of the \(k\)-th touch and \(t_k\) its start; the paper reports EPVA “per game” [PAGE 5]. Shot satisfaction is
\[
\mathrm{SS}_{i,j}=\mathrm{EPV}_i(t_j\mid \text{shot})-\mathrm{EPV}_i(t_j\mid \text{pass}),
\qquad [\mathrm{SS}]=\text{points per shot attempt},
\]
with the paper’s example “Carmelo Anthony … averages +0.053” [PAGE 6]. The zero-sum attribution warning is formalized by the residual/martingale property of conditional expectations: the source states “the sum of EPV changes that a player makes is zero by definition” [PAGE 4]. This is not a reason to sum raw increments as player value; it is the reason the replacement baseline appears.

Assumptions and exact breakdown regimes:
1. Markov decision assumption: \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid X_t,H_t)=\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid X_t)\). Breakdown if \(I(A_t;H_t\mid X_t)\gt 0\) bits, where \(H_t\) is history, play call, fatigue, or pressure state. The paper concedes efficiency loss when actions “execute a carefully designed sequence” [PAGE 6].
2. Endogenous defender state: if defender position \(D_t\subset X_t\) is a best response to expected \(A_t\), then \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid X_t)\) is equilibrium behavior, not \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid do(X_t))\). Breakdown when defender anticipation and recovery speed are omitted.
3. Sampling non-exchangeability: 2012-13 coverage was limited to 13 arenas [PAGE 7], and some players were observed only away [PAGE 9]. Breakdown when missingness depends on player, arena, or shooting form.
4. Omitted fouls and half-court restriction: \(Y\) excludes free throws and full-court sequences by construction [PAGE 3].
5. Rare-event overfitting: the model must be robust to “the Dwight Howard corner 3” [PAGE 3]; breakdown when a macrotransition has insufficient support.
6. Physical infeasibility: if \(\mathbb{P}(A_t=a\mid X_t)\gt 0\) for an action outside the kinematically feasible set, the EPV term integrates impossible mass.
7. Unobserved environment: the source data contain player/referee coordinates and ball coordinates [PAGE 7], not weather, humidity, or biomechanical load; environmental claims are not identifiable from this source alone.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is explicitly backward-looking from terminal box-score events to earlier decisions. It begins with the chess analogy: “Basketball is a game of decisions; at any moment, a player can change the character of a possession by choosing to pass, dribble, or shoot” [PAGE 1]. The retrocausal move is to value the move before checkmate, not only the checkmate. The authors compare EPV to microeconomic utility and to a stock ticker: EPV is “a weighted average of the values of the ballcarrier’s decisions and the probability of making each decision” [PAGE 2]. The scientific genealogy then runs through Markov models in baseball and football [PAGE 3], competing-risk survival analysis from Cox and Prentice et al. [PAGE 3], spatial statistics and spatially smoothed random effects [PAGE 3], and replacement baselines from baseball’s WAR [PAGE 5 footnote]. The computational genealogy runs through INLA and stochastic partial differential equations [PAGE 3], with a reported fit cost of “several hours … using hundreds of processors and over 4 terabytes of memory” [PAGE 3].

The multi-week trap is that a retrocausal metric can look more stable than its inputs. EPVA is computed over a season, but the 2012-13 source is arena-selective: “data were only available from 13 arenas” [PAGE 7], and Paul and James were observed only away [PAGE 9]. A weekly or monthly ranking can therefore move because of schedule and camera coverage, not because the player’s decision policy changed. A second trap is nonstationarity: player tendencies, lineups, and opponent schemes change across weeks, while the model’s spatially smoothed random effects may pool across those regimes. A third trap is narrative retrocausation: once Chris Paul is labeled the top EPVA player at +3.48 [PAGE 5], analysts may read earlier passes as causally valuable even if the model’s counterfactual pass value is poorly identified. The paper itself warns that the ranking contains “some anomalies” because of sampling bias [PAGE 9]. The correct genealogy treats EPV as a model-based historical reconstruction, not a discovered natural constant.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
EPV’s decision model is not a recursive game model. The paper assumes “the decision the ballhandler makes depends only on the current spatial configuration of the possession” [PAGE 3]. That is a level-0 policy estimated from observed states. In real basketball, the ballhandler is modeling the defender’s beliefs, and the defender is modeling the ballhandler’s next action. Three failure modes matter.

First, crisis habit regression. Under high pressure, a player may revert from a flexible policy \(\pi_O(A_t\mid X_t,\text{context})\) to a habit policy \(h_i(A_t\mid X_t)\). The model’s player-specific random effects capture average tendency, but not state-dependent collapse. If pressure \(P_t\) is latent, then the estimated \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid X_t)\) is a marginal over \(P_t\), and EPVA can misattribute a pressure-induced habit to a stable player trait. Prediction: in high-leverage or late-clock possessions, calibration residuals and EPV variance should increase relative to neutral possessions.

Second, the counter-trap. A defender can bait a pass by leaving a lane visually open, then closing it after the passer commits. The observed spatial state \(X_t\) then includes a defender position \(D_t\) that is chosen in anticipation of \(A_t\). Formally, the model estimates \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid X_t)\), but the causal quantity for evaluating the pass is \(\mathbb{P}(A_t\mid do(X_t))\) or, better, a value that integrates the defender’s response kernel \(D_{t+\delta}\sim\pi_D(\cdot\mid X_t,A_t)\). If defender recovery speed and anticipation are omitted, EPV overvalues bait passes. Prediction: adding defender recovery kinematics should lower EPV for passes through “open” lanes that are actually baited.

Third, the clock. The paper’s Spurs example is “Down by 2 with less than 10 seconds left” [PAGE 4]. EPV is expected points by possession end, not win probability. Near period end, the clock and score differential change the utility of a 2-point shot, a 3-point shot, a foul, and a pass. The model’s half-court, no-foul simplification [PAGE 3] makes clock-state decision analysis incomplete. Prediction: EPV-optimal actions and win-probability-optimal actions will diverge most in the final seconds and in asymmetric-score states. A recursive theory of mind would add hidden beliefs, defender response, clock, and score to the state; without them, EPV is a descriptive equilibrium valuation, not a full strategic counterfactual.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is physical and biomechanical before it is statistical. Let \(F(X_t)\subseteq M\) be the set of macrotransitions that are physically feasible at time \(t\) given ball position, player reach, maximum ball speed, defender distance, reaction time, and release envelope. A pass is feasible only if the ball can traverse the lane before a defender can intercept it; a shot is feasible only if the release can occur outside the defender’s contest envelope; a drive is feasible only if acceleration and bounded court space permit it. If the estimated model assigns \(\hat{\mathbb{P}}(A_t=a\mid X_t)\gt 0\) for \(a\notin F(X_t)\), then the EPV sum includes positive mass on an impossible event. Since the true conditional expectation has \(\mathbb{P}(A_t=a\mid X_t)=0\) for physically impossible \(a\), the estimator violates the probability axioms at the option level. That is the veto: do not interpret that option’s EPV as a causal value; either set its probability to zero or mark the EPV as extrapolated.

Biomechanically, the source data provide coordinates but not force, torque, fatigue, or joint load [PAGE 7]. Therefore fatigue-adjusted EPV claims are not identifiable from this paper alone. A biomechanical veto should be triggered whenever a claimed EPV difference depends on unobserved capacity: for example, treating a contested shot as “open” because the defender’s hand coordinate is outside a naive radius, while ignoring jump timing, release height, and body orientation. The corrective is a release-envelope model with units of meters and seconds, not a fake scalar index.

Weather is an even sharper scope veto. The SportVU data described in the appendix are “X- and Y- coordinates for each of the ten players and three referees on the court, in addition to X-, Y-, and Z-coordinates for the ball” [PAGE 7]. There are no temperature, humidity, wind, or air-pressure variables. For an indoor NBA game, weather is not identifiable from the source; any weather-based EPV adjustment is outside the evidence. For an outdoor sport, weather would have to enter \(X_t\) explicitly and be tested for calibration. Thus the weather veto is not a claim that weather never matters; it is a claim that this source cannot adjudicate it. The admissible cross-layer proof is conditional: given the observed coordinate state and a stated kinematic/biomechanical model, infeasible actions receive zero probability; without such a model, EPV remains a descriptive estimate and cannot veto physical reality.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
