Multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit. The source’s decision chain is: a probabilistic classifier produces \(\hat Y,\hat P\); a decision rule compares \(\hat P\) against a bookmaker-implied probability; the bankroll update depends on whether the realized outcome matches the chosen side and on the stake; and the season-level metric is return on investment (ROI). The paper’s central claim is that because the decision rule uses probabilities rather than only labels, calibration—not accuracy—should drive model selection. The formal bridge is perfect calibration, \(P(\hat Y=Y\mid \hat P=p)=p\), which is a conditional-frequency statement over the joint distribution [PAGE 6]. Accuracy, by contrast, is only the proportion of correctly classified data [PAGE 4]. Thus a model can be highly accurate yet systematically distant from true probabilities, and a model can be well-calibrated yet not maximize argmax correctness. The paper operationalizes calibration with classwise expected calibration error (classwise-ECE), using 20 bins and a non-empty-bin constraint [PAGE 6]. The predictive pipeline has two branches: one maximizes accuracy; the other minimizes classwise-ECE. Feature selection, hyperparameter optimization, and final model selection are all performed separately along each branch [PAGE 6–10]. The empirical comparison then uses a single simulation season, 2018/2019, with data splits: 2014/15–2015/16 initial training, 2016/17 validation, 2017/18 test, and 2014/15–2017/18 final training [PAGE 11]. The reported average ROI is \(+34.69\%\) for calibration-driven systems versus \(-35.17\%\) for accuracy-driven systems, with best cases \(+36.93\%\) versus \(+5.56\%\) [PAGE 3, PAGE 13]. 

Gray-zone audit. First, the final calibration-driven and accuracy-driven models are both SVM models [PAGE 12]. Therefore the empirical contrast is not “SVM versus another algorithm”; it is a contrast between two SVM instances selected under different feature subsets and hyperparameters, with different objectives. That is a confound: the ROI gap cannot be attributed solely to the calibration metric because feature selection and HPO differ by branch. Second, classwise-ECE is gameable: a predictor that outputs the overall class distribution for all instances can obtain low calibration error, which is why the authors impose the 80% non-empty-bin constraint [PAGE 6]. But the authors themselves call this constraint “somewhat arbitrary” [PAGE 15]. Third, the ROI experiment uses one season, and the paper states: “The most obvious is the use of a single season for the betting experiments” [PAGE 15]. Fourth, under the fractional Kelly rule, identical bet selections in the second half do not imply identical bankroll paths; the paper notes that bankroll size, predicted probability, and bookmaker-implied probability all affect returns [PAGE 12]. That makes ROI path-dependent and high-variance. Fifth, the model is pre-game and batch-processed; in-game events such as early injury of a star player are not modeled [PAGE 5]. That is a physical-layer omitted-variable problem: marginal calibration can hold while conditional calibration given injury state fails. Singularity/breakdown regimes: empirical calibration is undefined when a probability bin has no samples; classwise-ECE terms vanish for empty bins, but the 80% constraint exists precisely to prevent degenerate concentration; the Kelly fraction has a denominator singular at \(b=0\), where \(b\) is net return per unit stake; and ROI is undefined at zero initial bankroll. These are not decorative caveats—they define where the paper’s evidence is strongest and where it is fragile.

1. Perfect calibration. For classifier \(f(X)=(\hat Y,\hat P)\),
\[
\Pr(\hat Y=Y\mid \hat P=p)=p,\qquad \forall p\in[0,1].
\]
This is stated on [PAGE 6]. The unit is probability, \(p\in[0,1]\). Assumption: the conditional frequency is evaluated over the joint distribution of test instances; in finite samples it is approximated by binning. Breakdown: if no test instances have \(\hat P=p\), the conditional probability is empirically undefined; with finite bins, the equality is replaced by an average bin error. The source states: “Perfect calibration occurs if” [PAGE 6], followed by the equation above.

2. Classwise expected calibration error. With \(K\) classes, \(M\) bins, dataset size \(n\), bin \(B_{j,i}\), actual rate \(y_i(B_{j,i})\), and average predicted probability \(\bar p_i(B_{j,i})\),
\[
\mathrm{classwise\text{-}ECE}
=
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{M}
\frac{|B_{j,i}|}{n}
\left|
y_i(B_{j,i})-\bar p_i(B_{j,i})
\right|.
\]
This is given on [PAGE 6]. Unit: probability error, bounded in \([0,1]\). Assumption: equal-width bins over \([0,1]\); the paper uses 20 bins [PAGE 6]. Breakdown: empty bins contribute zero; if predictions collapse into a few bins, the metric can be made artificially small. The paper imposes: “we require at least 80% of the bins (that the predicted probabilities are grouped into) to be non-empty” [PAGE 6]. If fewer than 80% are non-empty, classwise-ECE is set to 1, the maximum error [PAGE 10]. The paper calls the constraint “somewhat arbitrary” [PAGE 15].

\[
\mathrm{Acc}
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\mathbf{1}\{\hat Y_i=Y_i\}.
\]
Unit: dimensionless proportion. Assumption: label prediction is the object of interest. Breakdown: accuracy ignores the distance between \(\hat P\) and the true probability. The paper’s core argument is that this distance matters for probabilistic decision-making [PAGE 3–4].

4. Kelly fraction. The paper gives
\[
k=\frac{pb-q}{b},\qquad q=1-p,
\]
where \(k\) is the proportion of bankroll to allocate, \(p\) is the model’s predicted probability, \(b\) is potential net winnings per unit stake, and \(q=1-p\) [PAGE 5]. The source states: “\(k\) is the proportion of the bettor’s bankroll to wager on the given outcome” [PAGE 5]. Units: \(k\) is dimensionless; \(p,q\in[0,1]\); \(b\) is dimensionless net return per unit stake. The paper uses the eighth-Kelly, \(k_{1/8}=k/8\), as a conservative variant [PAGE 11]. Breakdown/singularity: \(b=0\) makes the expression singular; near \(b=0\) the fraction can explode; if \(pb-q\le 0\), the Kelly fraction is non-positive and no positive stake is indicated. The paper warns: “Kelly betting only works with a well-calibrated model” [PAGE 2], and notes that full-Kelly can lead to “almost sure ruin” [PAGE 5].

5. Return on investment. The paper measures profitability by percentage change in bankroll:
\[
\mathrm{ROI}
=
\frac{W_{\mathrm{final}}-W_0}{W_0}\times 100\%.
\]
Unit: percent. Assumption: initial bankroll \(W_0>0\), here \$10,000 [PAGE 11]. Breakdown: ROI is path-dependent and single-season variance can dominate; the paper reports average ROI \(+34.69\%\) versus \(-35.17\%\) and best-case \(+36.93\%\) versus \(+5.56\%\) [PAGE 3, PAGE 13].

Empirical operators. On the test set, the best calibration-driven model was SVM with classwise-ECE \(3.23\%\); the best accuracy-driven model was SVM with accuracy \(66.55\%\) [PAGE 12]. The paper reports the calibration-driven system as betting on \(87.55\%\) of games and winning \(38.8\%\) of bets, while the accuracy-driven system bet on \(89.89\%\) and won \(38.46\%\) [PAGE 13]. These are dimensionless proportions. The source’s summary is direct: “using calibration, rather than accuracy, as the basis for model selection leads to greater returns” [PAGE 3].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy runs from information-theoretic bankroll growth to probabilistic calibration. The Kelly criterion is traced in the paper to analysis of “long-distance telephone signal noise” and later applications across domains [PAGE 5]. The source also cites Thorp, Rotando, Barnett, Dotan, and Hsieh–Barmish for bankroll growth and fractional Kelly limitations [PAGE 5]. The calibration branch descends from modern probabilistic classifier evaluation: Guo et al. on neural-network calibration, Kull et al. on classwise and Dirichlet calibration, and Nixon et al. on the limitation that original ECE focuses only on the most likely class [PAGE 6]. The paper’s novelty is to connect these traditions: model selection for a probabilistic decision problem should minimize calibration error rather than maximize label accuracy [PAGE 3, PAGE 15]. 

The multi-week traps are temporal and structural. First, single-season evaluation is an overfitting trap: a system that profits over one season may not replicate over another, and the authors explicitly flag this [PAGE 15]. Second, chronological splitting is essential in sports prediction; the paper uses past seasons for training and later seasons for validation/test/simulation [PAGE 11]. Shuffled cross-validation would leak future information and is not used. Third, covariate shift is a multi-week trap: the paper uses two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests at the 1% level to drop features whose distributions differ between validation and prior training data [PAGE 9]. A fixed weekly or seasonal model can silently drift as roster composition, tactics, and rule enforcement change. Fourth, HPO randomness is a trap: the paper runs Bayesian optimization over several random seeds before selecting hyperparameters [PAGE 10]. Fifth, feature-selection instability is a trap: subset B for calibration and subset C for accuracy differ, so the “same” SVM family can behave differently because it sees different features [PAGE 11]. Sixth, the classwise-ECE non-empty-bin constraint is a historical patch for a known metric weakness, not a law of nature; the authors concede it is arbitrary [PAGE 15]. The retrocausal lesson is that the paper’s result is a strong existence proof, not a universal law: calibration-oriented selection produced profitable systems in the reported setting, but the causal ancestry includes data split, feature subset, HPO seed, staking rule, and season-specific variance.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under uncertainty, analysts regress to the familiar metric: accuracy. The paper names this habit directly: much prior work evaluates models on accuracy, and accuracy is “a suitable model evaluation metric for the sports outcome prediction problem” but “not necessarily true for the sports betting problem” [PAGE 3–4]. The recursive trap is that a stakeholder sees a high accuracy number and infers decision quality, while the downstream decision uses probability distance. The paper’s counter-hypothesis is that calibration is more appropriate because the system compares predicted probabilities to bookmaker-implied probabilities [PAGE 3]. 

Counter-trap. A naive response to “use calibration” is to game the calibration metric. Classwise-ECE can be reduced by predicting the overall class distribution for all instances, which is why the authors impose the 80% non-empty-bin constraint [PAGE 6]. That constraint is itself a patch, and the authors call it “somewhat arbitrary” [PAGE 15]. Recursive theory-of-mind therefore requires modeling three agents: the bettor, who wants ROI; the modeler, who wants a low calibration error; and the bookmaker, who also generates predictions and can use classwise-ECE for risk management [PAGE 16]. The bettor thinks “low ECE means good decisions”; the modeler may be tempted to optimize the metric rather than the decision; the bookmaker may exploit overconfident probability outputs. The paper observes that the accuracy-driven model identified more value opportunities, had a lower success rate, and appeared more overconfident [PAGE 15]. That is the counter-trap in behavioral form: a metric can create an illusion of rigor. 

Clock. The correct temporal discipline is chronological: train on past seasons, validate on a later season, test on the next, simulate on the final season [PAGE 11]. The paper’s clock is a single season, which the authors criticize [PAGE 15]. A recursive agent should therefore treat the reported ROI as a clock-stamped observation, not an invariant. The system should be re-evaluated across multiple seasons, with drift detection and recalibration, before the claim “calibration dominates accuracy” is treated as a stable policy. The paper’s own phrase is that model calibration is “more important than accuracy” for sports betting [PAGE 2], but the recursive audit says: important under the paper’s clock, data split, feature set, and staking rule—not automatically under all clocks.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof. The paper is explicitly pre-game and batch-processed: it focuses on pre-game prediction and states that “generating predictions for pre-game betting can be done using batch processing” [PAGE 5]. The model therefore cannot ingest in-game or late-breaking physical state. The source warns: “Events such as the early injury of a star player can have a significant influence on the outcome of a game” [PAGE 5]. Formally, calibration is a marginal statement:
\[
\Pr(Y=1\mid \hat P=p)=p.
\]
A physical state variable \(S\)—injury, minutes restriction, back-to-back fatigue, travel, illness, or roster turnover—can alter the outcome conditional:
\[
\Pr(Y=1\mid \hat P=p,S=s)\neq \Pr(Y=1\mid \hat P=p).
\]
If \(S\) is omitted from \(X\), the model can remain marginally calibrated over the training distribution while failing conditionally in the simulation season. That is a cross-layer veto: no amount of classwise-ECE minimization on stale features can encode a star player’s early exit. Biomechanically, box-score averages and prior-season winning percentage do not represent minute load, player-level injury risk, or matchup-specific physical constraints; the paper’s feature set is team-level and historical [PAGE 6–9]. Weather is a weaker direct veto for indoor basketball, but it can still affect travel, scheduling, and recovery, which are not in the feature set. The proof of veto is structural, not empirical: the model class lacks the variables; the data pipeline is batch; the calibration condition is unconditional on physical state. Therefore a physical-layer shock can invalidate the probability output even if classwise-ECE on the test set was \(3.23\%\) [PAGE 12]. The correct adjudication is to veto probability-sensitive decisions when a known physical state change is outside the training support, or to require real-time re-estimation before allocation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios follow. Each is designed so a reward signal can accept or reject the paper’s policy claim without relying on rhetorical authority.

1. Base-rate degeneracy veto. Counterfactual: remove the 80% non-empty-bin constraint and allow a predictor to output the overall class distribution for every game. Prediction: classwise-ECE will fall below \(1\%\), but accuracy will remain near the base rate and ROI under the eighth-Kelly rule will be negative or near zero. Verifiable test: train the degenerate predictor, compute classwise-ECE, accuracy, and season ROI on the same split. Veto condition: if the calibration branch selects the degenerate model because its ECE is lowest, then “calibration-based selection” is not a valid decision policy without the non-empty-bin guard. This directly tests the paper’s acknowledged metric weakness [PAGE 6] and the arbitrary 80% constraint [PAGE 15].

2. Multi-season replication veto. Counterfactual: rerun the exact pipeline on additional seasons after 2018/2019 using the same chronological train/validation/test/simulation logic. Prediction: the calibration-minus-accuracy ROI gap will shrink substantially from the reported \(+34.69\%\) versus \(-35.17\%\) average [PAGE 3], and at least one replication season will show a sign reversal. Verifiable test: report mean, median, standard deviation, and sign count of ROI differences across seasons. Veto condition: if the calibration branch does not beat the accuracy branch on average out of sample, the universal claim “calibration is more important than accuracy” is rejected for this decision setting; the paper’s own single-season caveat supports this test [PAGE 15].

3. Covariate-shift/injury-shock veto. Counterfactual: inject a verifiable physical shock into the simulation season—e.g., remove a star player for a known interval or impose a roster-turnover distribution shift—while keeping the model frozen. Prediction: classwise-ECE will rise above the reported \(4.46\%\) calibration-branch level [PAGE 13], and ROI will fall below the accuracy-branch comparator. Verifiable test: compute pre-shock and post-shock ECE, accuracy, and ROI; also run the paper’s KS-based feature-drop procedure [PAGE 9] and compare frozen versus recalibrated models. Veto condition: if the frozen calibration-selected model loses its ROI advantage after a physical shock that is absent from the feature set, then the policy is vetoed by omitted-variable failure, not by a failure of the calibration concept itself. This is the cleanest RLVR test because the shock is externally logged and the prediction is falsifiable: calibration error should increase, and decision quality should degrade, exactly where physical state is missing.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
