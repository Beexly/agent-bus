<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The patent’s lineage sits at the intersection of three historical threads: (1) inertial measurement in team sports, (2) workload-injury modeling, and (3) ice-hockey-specific groin and hip pathology. Catapult’s commercial trajectory (founded mid-2000s) popularized GPS/IMU player logging, but this US 10,372,975 B2 filing (priority Aug. 10, 2015 [PAGE 5]) narrows the modality to torso accelerometers and gyroscopes specifically for ice hockey, citing prior art in musculoskeletal imaging diagnosis [PAGE 5], ACL diagnosis [PAGE 5], foot-pressure sensing [PAGE 5], and helmet concussion sensing [PAGE 5]. Notably, none of the cited prior art addresses groin load or slap-shot torque, positioning the torso-IMU stride and slap-shot pattern as the novel claim.

Scientifically, the load–injury genealogy runs from early training-load monitoring through Banister’s impulse–response model and into the acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) literature. ACWR, however, is a toy ratio when stripped of its statistical context: dividing a rolling acute load by a chronic rolling average produces a dimensionless index whose confidence interval is rarely reported, whose temporal alignment with injury onset is assumed rather than proven, and whose denominator approaches zero during detraining or return-to-play periods. The present patent avoids explicitly naming ACWR but inherits its structural vulnerability: aggregating groin load and slap-shot counts over a sliding window without reporting the window length, decay constant, or confidence bounds produces the same class of fragility. A multi-week trap emerges when a trainer sees elevated groin load in week 1, rests the player in week 2, observes no injury in week 3, and attributes the non-injury to the intervention—while the counterfactual (player might not have been injured anyway) is unobserved.

The hockey-specific trap is position confounding. Defensemen take more slap shots [PAGE 5] and also absorb more body-check contact and log more even-strength minutes than forwards. A multi-week observational dataset will therefore attribute hip displacement and groin strain to slap-shot volume when the true driver may be total collision exposure or ice-time dose. Retroactive attribution—looking back from an injury and assigning causality to last week’s slap-shot count—is particularly hazardous because groin strains have delayed onset (24–72 h post-overload) and hip displacement is structural rather than acute soft-tissue [PAGE 5]. Without a prospective cohort that separates defensemen by slap-shot quartile while matching for ice time and contact count, the causal arrow from slap shots to injury cannot be identified. The patent’s own admission—“No one knows the cause of this” regarding hip displacement [PAGE 5]—is an honest boundary condition that the probability model does not respect if it treats slap shots as a validated covariate.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: when a team loses a defenseman to groin or hip injury, the coaching staff typically increases ice time and slap-shot responsibilities for the remaining defensemen to compensate [PAGE 5]. This regresses the system toward the crisis habit—higher rotational volume and lateral loading on the very athletes already flagged as high-risk. The sensor system detects rising G_load and N_slap on the surviving defensemen, but if the availability algorithm only rests the most-loaded individual, it displaces load onto the next most-loaded player rather than reducing team-level mechanical stress. The recursive loop is: injury \rightarrow increased load on substitutes \rightarrow secondary injury \rightarrow further roster compression.

Counter-trap: a sophisticated opponent or internal actor can game the availability signal. If a star defenseman knows that exceeding a groin-load threshold triggers a mandatory rest, he may modify stride mechanics (e.g., shorter strides, reduced lateral edge engagement) to suppress the torso-IMU signature while maintaining game output, or may under-report symptoms to remain available. Conversely, a coach may rest a player strategically before a high-stakes matchup not because of injury risk but to manage rotations, producing a false positive in the injury-prediction ledger. The counter-trap is therefore adversarial: the measurement system changes the behavior it measures, invalidating the stationarity assumption of p(inj | β).

Clock: the slap shot’s four-stage sequence unfolds in roughly one second or less [PAGE 5], while the hockey stride cycle is approximately 0.8–1.2 s. If the IMU sampling clock is not synchronized to a common timebase across players, or if clock drift exceeds a small fraction of the event duration (e.g., >50 ms), the fused side-accelerometer-plus-gyroscope template match for strides and the four-stage matched filter for slap shots suffer temporal aliasing. Battery-powered data loggers also exhibit clock drift with temperature; on-ice sessions in cold rinks can exacerbate crystal-oscillator drift. Breakdown regime: when inter-sensor clock skew δ_t satisfies δ_t > 0.1 \, T_{\mathrm{event}} (T_{\mathrm{event}} \approx 1 \mathrm{s}), the pattern match Λ(τ) peaks at an incorrect lag and both stride and slap-shot counts are systematically underestimated. The availability decision then rests on a corrupted denominator.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto (sensor-to-anatomy gap): the torso IMU measures rigid-body kinematics of the trunk [PAGE 5], but groin injury occurs at the adductor origin/insertion near the pubis and hip joint. There is no disclosed transduction from trunk a_y or ω_z to adductor muscle-tendon force. Veto condition: if no concurrent surface EMG, dynamometry, or instrumented-skate ground-reaction data validates the groin-load proxy, the physical layer vetoes any automated availability restriction based on G_load alone.

Biomechanical veto (kinetic chain invisibility): slap-shot torque is generated through stick flex (spring storage), wrist roll, weight shift, and blade-puck contact [PAGE 5]. A torso sensor captures trunk rotation and lateral acceleration but not stick deformation x(t), stick stiffness k, or puck exit velocity. Veto condition: if N_slap is used as a hip-torque surrogate without high-speed video or instrumented-stick validation of U(t) = ½kx², the biomechanical layer vetoes causal attribution of hip displacement to slap-shot volume [PAGE 5].

Weather veto (environmental non-stationarity): ice temperature alters friction, skate edge grip, and vibration transmission through the boots to the torso [PAGE 5]. Colder, harder ice increases impact transients and may inflate |a_y| without increasing muscular effort; warmer, softer ice increases stride resistance and may elevate metabolic load while reducing peak acceleration. MEMS IMU bias and scale-factor errors also drift with temperature. Veto condition: if rink temperature deviates by more than the sensor’s compensated operating range (commonly ±10 °C) from calibration conditions, or if ice-resurfacing state is unrecorded, the weather layer vetoes longitudinal comparison of G_load across games or practices.

Cross-layer adjudication rule: an availability decision is permitted only if (i) stride/slap-shot detections pass clock-sync validation (δ_t < 0.1 s), (ii) G_load exceeds a minimum-duration, minimum-stride-count threshold (G_L + G_R > \epsilon_{\min}), (iii) temperature and ice-state are logged within sensor compensation bounds, and (iv) an orthogonal observer (video or coach) confirms the detected event class. If any layer fails, the system defaults to manual coach assessment rather than algorithmic rest. This prevents the recursive crisis-habit loop from being automated on a corrupted signal.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios, each with a falsifiable counterfactual prediction:

Veto Scenario 1 — False-positive rest from threshold overreach.
Condition: A defenseman’s G_load crosses the alert threshold during a single high-intensity practice, triggering automated rest for the next game, yet the player completes the game without injury and shows no decline in stride efficiency (strides per course unchanged). Verifiable measurement: compare G_load in the 48 h pre-game against game-time skate telemetry and post-game adductor soreness scale. Counterfactual prediction: if the threshold were raised by one standard deviation of within-player G_load variance, the player would not have been rested and injury incidence over a 4-week microcycle would remain statistically indistinguishable (hazard ratio ≈ 1.0, 95% CI including 1.0). Veto: automated rest decisions require sustained elevation (G_load above threshold for ≥2 consecutive sessions), not single-session spikes.

Veto Scenario 2 — Injury-type conflation (groin vs. hip displacement).
Condition: The model flags high N_slap as predictive of “groin injury” [PAGE 5], but longitudinal tracking shows slap-shot volume correlates with hip displacement (structural) while groin pulls (soft tissue) [PAGE 5] track with total lateral skating load instead. Verifiable measurement: fit separate Cox models for groin strain and hip displacement with N_slap and G_load as covariates. Counterfactual prediction: the hazard ratio for groin strain conditional on N_slap alone converges to ≈1.0 after adjusting for G_load and ice time, whereas hip displacement retains N_slap sensitivity. Veto: the probability output must be injury-specific (p_groin ≠ p_hip); a single “injury probability” score conflating soft-tissue groin pulls with structural hip displacement is vetoed.

Veto Scenario 3 — Conservation of load under availability management.
Condition: Team A rests Player X due to high groin load; Player Y (substitute) absorbs X’s minutes and slap shots and sustains a groin strain in week 2. Team-level injury incidence is unchanged despite individual load reduction. Verifiable measurement: aggregate team-level G_load and injury count over a 6-week block with and without the availability algorithm active, controlling for games played. Counterfactual prediction: if the algorithm reduces individual peak G_load by ≥20% but team-level groin-injury incidence does not decline (rate ratio ≈ 1.0), the intervention merely redistributes load rather than reducing it. Veto: availability decisions must be paired with a replacement-load cap (e.g., substitute’s projected G_load ≤ rested player’s pre-alert baseline) or the system is vetoed as load-shifting theater.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
