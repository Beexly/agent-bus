<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s central derivation is a chain from a naive baseline to a complex ceiling to a simple, real-time decision aid. First, the class prior is estimated from the dataset: “Overall, 58.9% of plays were passing plays” [PAGE 3 of 9]. Thus a constant “pass” classifier has expected accuracy 0.589 in dimensionless probability units, and any model below that is worse than the prior-only rule. Second, the complex-model ceiling is established across CART, KNN, random forest, and neural network, with hyperparameters tuned by “repeated 10-fold cross validation over 15 iterations” [PAGE 3 of 9]. The neural network reaches 75.3% accuracy and 10.6% false negative rate [PAGE 4 of 9]. The lift over the 58.9% baseline is therefore 16.4 percentage points, not a free-standing “skill” number; it is conditional on the features, the four-season sample, and the pass/rush label convention. That convention matters: “intended passing plays that reverted into QB scrambles were still classified as passing plays” [PAGE 2 of 9]. Label ambiguity is therefore a gray-zone source, because some scrambles are physically run-like while being labeled pass.

Third, the interpretable model is selected under two constraints: real-time implementability and interpretability. The paper states: “we limited the variables that the simple model could utilize to static variables that can be easily observable at any point in time” [PAGE 4 of 9]. The chosen tree uses “current down, yards to go for first down, and point differential” and has “three variables and 10 splits” [PAGE 4 of 9]. Its accuracy is 65.3%, which is “86% of the accuracy generated by our neural network model” [PAGE 4 of 9]. Multi-hop arithmetic gives 65.3/75.3 = 0.867, so the paper’s 86% is a ratio of accuracies, not an \(R^2\), not explained variance, and not a causal effect. The secondary tree adds offensive formation and reaches 72.3%, “which captures 96% of the predictive power of our neural network model” [PAGE 5 of 9]; again 72.3/75.3 = 0.960, a relative accuracy ratio. Team-specific trees range from 64.7% to 82.5%, with average 73.0% [PAGE 6 of 9]. The random-label robustness check gives average 71.5% and min–max 70.3%–72.8% [PAGE 6 of 9], so the observed Dallas 82.5% and Buffalo 64.7% lie outside the simulated band. This supports real between-team variation, but it does not prove a causal coaching mechanism; the paper says only that it “found an association” between coaching changes and poorer prediction accuracy [PAGE 6 of 9].

The gray-zone audit must distinguish cross-validation from deployment. The 75.3% neural-network figure is a cross-validated result, while the 65.3% tree is chosen for memorability. The paper explicitly says the model “does not replace a coach’s knowledge but should be used to support decision making” [PAGE 4 of 9]. Therefore the operational claim is not “the tree knows the play”; it is “the tree supplies a prior or pre-snap read.” The clock constraint is real: “the offense has a maximum of 40 seconds” and “within the first 25 seconds, the defensive coordinator can communicate, via a one-way radio, to the middle linebacker” [PAGE 4 of 9]. The secondary model can be used “up until the time of the snap” [PAGE 5 of 9]. Breakdown regimes include leaf confidence near 0.5, undefined false-negative rates when a subset has zero actual passes, class imbalance below 58.9%, missing physical variables such as injuries and weather, team-specific sparse leaves, and strategic adaptation by the offense if the defense begins using the model. The source itself flags weather, injuries, exact personnel, and media comments as future data [PAGE 7 of 9], so the simple model’s conditional probabilities are marginal over those hidden layers.

The paper’s information-theoretic ceiling of prior uncertainty can be written as \(H_2(\pi_P)=-\pi_P\log_2\pi_P-(1-\pi_P)\log_2(1-\pi_P)\), in bits; at \(\pi_P=0.589\), \(H_2 \approx 0.976\) bits. This is an auxiliary unpredictability measure, not a model result from the paper. For a classifier, \(\mathrm{Accuracy}=(TP+TN)/(TP+TN+FP+FN)\), \(\mathrm{FNR}=FN/(FN+TP)\), and \(\mathrm{FPR}=FP/(FP+TN)\), all dimensionless. The paper reports the neural network at 75.3% accuracy and 10.6% FNR [PAGE 4 of 9]. Its relative capture arithmetic is \(\mathrm{Capture}_{tree}=\mathrm{Acc}_{tree}/\mathrm{Acc}_{NN}=0.653/0.753=0.867\), rounded to “86%” [PAGE 4 of 9], and \(\mathrm{Capture}_{secondary}=0.723/0.753=0.960\), rounded to “96%” [PAGE 5 of 9]. These are accuracy ratios, not explained variance.

The source operators are: CART classification trees, \(k\)-nearest neighbors, random forests, and neural networks, with hyperparameters tuned by “repeated 10-fold cross validation over 15 iterations” [PAGE 3 of 9]. The selected simple tree is a CART model with three variables and 10 splits [PAGE 4 of 9]. For a terminal leaf \(l\), the paper uses “the proportion of the majority class in each terminal leaf node as a measure of how strong each prediction is” [PAGE 4 of 9]. Formally, leaf confidence is \(c_l=\max(\hat p_l,1-\hat p_l)\), dimensionless. A prediction is actionable only if \(c_l\) is materially above 0.5. The false-negative definition is important because the paper argues: “we believe the false negative rate is the more important metric to consider” [PAGE 4 of 9]. That means the cost asymmetry is \(C_{FN}>C_{FP}\) in coaching terms: a defense expecting pass is less damaged by a run than a run-expecting defense is by a pass [PAGE 4 of 9].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are exact: (i) \(\mathrm{FNR}\) is undefined if \(FN+TP=0\), i.e., an evaluation subset contains no actual passes; (ii) \(\mathrm{FPR}\) is undefined if \(FP+TN=0\); (iii) accuracy below 0.589 is worse than the constant-pass baseline; (iv) a leaf with \(\hat p_l \approx 0.5\) has \(c_l \approx 0.5\), so the tree has no directional advantage; (v) team-specific or pair-specific subsets can produce sparse leaves and unstable splits, and the paper warns that “as more and more tailoring is desired, the available data to train such a model becomes sparser” [PAGE 7 of 9]; (vi) the simple model omits weather, injuries, and exact personnel, which the paper lists as future data [PAGE 7 of 9]. The observed model ranking is CART 73.3%, KNN 71.3%, random forest 74.7%, neural network 75.3% [PAGE 4 of 9]; the random-forest–neural-network gap is “only 0.6%” [PAGE 9 of 9]. The team-specific range is 64.7% to 82.5%, average 73.0% [PAGE 6 of 9]; the random-label simulation average is 71.5%, min–max 70.3%–72.8% [PAGE 6 of 9]. Units: accuracy and FNR are percentages or dimensionless proportions; clock variables are seconds; entropy would be bits; no physical SI measurements are provided in the source beyond time.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is explicit. It situates itself against prior NFL play-prediction work: Lee, Chen, and Lakshman achieved 75.9% with gradient boosting and random forests using 2011–12 to 2014–15 data, while Burton and Dickey achieved 75.0% with quarter-specific and game-state logistic regressions using 2011–12 to 2014–15 data [PAGE 2 of 9]. The authors therefore position their 75.3% neural network as “competitive with the state-of-the-art” [PAGE 1 of 9]. The older lineage includes Alamar’s sports-analytics program [PAGE 1 of 9], Alamar and Gould on linemen and passing completion [PAGE 2 of 9], Mulholland and Jensen on tight-end draft success [PAGE 2 of 9], Dhar on wide-receiver draft success [PAGE 2 of 9], and Berri and Simmons on quarterback draft value [PAGE 2 of 9]. The cross-sport lineage includes NCAA tournament prediction, NBA game prediction, European hockey, and NHL playoff prediction [PAGE 2 of 9]. Thus the paper is retrocausally connected to a broader movement: first predict outcomes, then predict in-game actions, then make the prediction implementable.

The multi-week trap is nonstationarity. The dataset covers “four NFL regular seasons, from 2013-2014 to 2016-2017” with “1034 games and 130,344 pass/rush plays” [PAGE 2 of 9]. But team behavior changes through coaching turnover, roster turnover, injuries, and strategic adjustment. The paper notes an association between more coaching changes and poorer prediction accuracy [PAGE 6 of 9], and future work proposes “training the model sequentially over time as the season progresses to reduce noise associated with player/staff turnover in the offseason” [PAGE 7 of 9]. A second trap is data leakage through ratings: “Madden 14 is based on data from the 2012–13 season and we use these ratings to predict plays for the 2013–14 season” [PAGE 3 of 9]. That is a deliberate prior-season feature, but it also means the ratings lag current injuries and role changes. A third trap is pair-specific tailoring: a Jets defensive coordinator might want a Patriots model trained only on Patriots–Jets games, but “the available data to train such a model becomes sparser” [PAGE 7 of 9]. The historical lineage therefore warns against treating one cross-validated accuracy as a permanent law of offensive behavior.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under the 40-second play clock and the first-25-second radio window [PAGE 4 of 9], a coach or linebacker is likely to regress to a memorized heuristic rather than run a black-box model. The paper deliberately designs for this: the tree “can be easily memorized and implemented in an actual game” [PAGE 1 of 9], and it “does not replace a coach’s knowledge but should be used to support decision making” [PAGE 4 of 9]. The recursive risk is that the simple tree becomes the habit. If the defense treats “third down means pass” as a fixed rule, the offense can counter-trap with a rush. The paper’s exploratory result shows why this trap is plausible: “the proportion of passing plays on 3rd down is approximately 79.3%” [PAGE 3 of 9], but that is a historical marginal, not an equilibrium response to a defense that has memorized the tree.

Counter-trap: the model is trained on observed play calls, not on a game-theoretic best response. If defensive coordinators use the tree, offensive coordinators can shift their conditional distribution; the historical leaf probabilities then become stale. The paper’s team-specific models partially capture style, with Dallas at 82.5% and Buffalo at 64.7% [PAGE 6 of 9], but they do not model “the offense knows that the defense knows.” The recursive theory-of-mind layer is therefore a veto on overconfident interpretation: the tree predicts a pass/rush tendency, not a guaranteed call. The secondary formation model reaches 72.3% and can be used “up until the time of the snap” [PAGE 5 of 9], which improves the clock fit but still does not solve strategic adaptation.

Clock: the implementation constraint is exact. The offense has “a maximum of 40 seconds” and the defensive coordinator can communicate “within the first 25 seconds” [PAGE 4 of 9]. A complex neural network may be accurate but not sideline-implementable under NFL technology restrictions [PAGE 1 of 9]. The simple tree uses only static variables [PAGE 4 of 9], while the secondary tree adds formation and is intended for “pre-snap reads” [PAGE 5 of 9]. The recursive balance is: use the simple tree early in the clock, use the formation-augmented tree late, and preserve coach override authority. The crisis habit is not the tree itself; it is forgetting that the tree is a prior.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather layers are not measured in the simple model. The simple model’s static variables are “the quarter, down, minute, yards to go for first down, previous yards gained and the point differential” [PAGE 4 of 9]. The paper’s future-work list explicitly names “the exact players on the field, injuries, weather, even whether players/coaches have made media comments in the days leading up to a game” [PAGE 7 of 9]. Therefore the simple tree has no direct causal coverage of weather or current injury state. Formally, let \(X\) be the simple-model features and \(W\) be hidden physical state. The tree estimates \(\Pr(Y=P\mid X)\), which marginalizes over \(W\): \(\Pr(Y=P\mid X)=\sum_w \Pr(Y=P\mid X,W=w)\Pr(W=w\mid X)\). If for a physically relevant \(w\), \(\Pr(Y=P\mid X,W=w)\) falls below 0.5 while the marginal is above 0.5, the tree’s “pass” call is physically vetoed. This is a proof of non-identification, not a claim that weather always flips the call.

Biomechanical veto: a quarterback or receiver injury can reduce passing viability, and Madden ratings are prior-season aggregates [PAGE 3 of 9]; they do not update to current injury status. Weather veto: wind, precipitation, or field condition can alter pass/run selection, but weather is absent from both the simple and secondary feature lists [PAGE 4 of 9; PAGE 5 of 9]. Personnel veto: the secondary model adds offensive formation, not exact personnel, and the paper proposes a future alternative using “the exact personnel on the field” [PAGE 7 of 9]. Adjudication: the model is valid as a descriptive prior under its measured features, but it cannot veto a coach’s physical read. The correct hierarchy is physical evidence first, model prior second. The source itself supports this by calling the model a decision aid and not a replacement for coach knowledge [PAGE 4 of 9].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto test 1: team-label permutation. The paper already performed a robustness check by randomly assigning each play to a team and rebuilding 32 trees 50 times; the simulated average was 71.5% with min–max 70.3%–72.8% [PAGE 6 of 9]. Verifiable prediction: true team-specific accuracies should show wider dispersion than the simulated band, with Dallas 82.5% above 72.8% and Buffalo 64.7% below 70.3% [PAGE 6 of 9]. Veto rule: if a faithful reanalysis with identical preprocessing places the true team distribution inside the simulated band, reject the claim that between-team predictability is real rather than noise.

Veto test 2: formation ablation. The base tree reaches 65.3% [PAGE 4 of 9], while the formation-augmented secondary tree reaches 72.3% [PAGE 5 of 9]. Verifiable prediction: removing the formation variable from the secondary tree should reduce holdout accuracy toward the base-tree range, with a material drop rather than a negligible one. Veto rule: if formation removal leaves accuracy essentially unchanged, then the claimed 96% capture is not attributable to formation and the secondary-model interpretation is vetoed.

Veto test 3: temporal holdout. The paper reports cross-validated neural-network accuracy of 75.3% [PAGE 4 of 9], but deployment is forward-in-time. Verifiable prediction: training on the first three seasons and testing on the final season should produce lower accuracy than the cross-validated figure, especially for teams with coaching changes, because the paper associates coaching changes with poorer prediction accuracy [PAGE 6 of 9] and proposes sequential updating [PAGE 7 of 9]. Veto rule: if a strict season-forward holdout matches or exceeds the cross-validated 75.3% with no deterioration, the nonstationarity concern is weakened; if it falls materially, the static four-season model cannot be treated as a stable real-time predictor.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
