<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
First-order theory of mind: agent X experiences e because object o arouses reflex acts R_X that are forthwith felt [PAGE 454]. Second-order theory of mind: agent Y observes X's expression and attempts to infer e. James makes this inference non-trivial, even impossible without auxiliary data, because "there is no objective meaning" to a typical expression of anger or fear [PAGE 454]. Y must therefore recursively model X's association history A_X and vascular reactivity to interpret the observed behavior; Y cannot read e directly from the face or posture.

Crisis habit regression. Under acute stress, X may regress to habitual expressive patterns—grief sitting bowed down, or running restlessly [PAGE 454]. James notes these are not fixed reflexes; the same cause produces different vascular results in different men [PAGE 454]. Thus crisis does not guarantee a return to a universal prototype; it returns the individual to their own idiosyncratic attractor, which may be opposite to the cultural stereotype.

Counter-trap. The observer Y falls into a counter-trap when assuming a counter-stereotype—e.g., that fright must pale the victim rather than drive blood to the head [PAGE 454]. Y then misattributes internal state because Y's model of X's vaso-motor map M(I, A_X) is calibrated to Y's own association history, not X's.

Clock. The impulse travels "on its way through the brain to the vaso-motor centre" [PAGE 454], introducing a finite neurophysiological latency Δt between object encounter and full vascular expression. If Y's inference of X's emotion operates on a faster social clock than Δt, Y predicts the expression before it is fully formed, producing a temporal mismatch error: Y judges the emotion at t while the bodily signature arrives at t + Δt. Recursive theory of mind in this domain is therefore not a static matrix-to-mind readout but a dynamic, history-dependent prediction over a delayed hemodynamic and vascular clock.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto. The Jamesian genesis clause requires reflex acts to be "forthwith felt" [PAGE 454]. If afferent somatic feedback is physically interrupted—by spinal transection, peripheral nerve block, or visceral deafferentation—the felt term is nullified. Under a strong Jamesian reading, the corresponding emotion should be abolished or qualitatively degraded. Persistence of normal emotional report despite intact cortical appraisal but blocked afference vetoes the claim that bodily feeling is constitutive of the emotion.

Biomechanical layer veto. Vascular responses are bounded by smooth-muscle tone, autonomic innervation density, and cardiac output. James observes that fright can drive blood to the head or produce pallor, depending on individual blood-vessel reactivity [PAGE 454]. Any claimed expression requiring instantaneous, whole-body exsanguination or vasodilation beyond Poiseuille-limited flow is biomechanically impossible and vetoes the observational report as artifact.

Weather/environmental layer veto. James's equation M(I, A_i) on [PAGE 454] contains no ambient temperature term. Ambient thermal load shifts baseline vaso-motor tone: in hypothermia, fear-induced cutaneous flushing may be suppressed despite intact afferent pathways and intact association history. Adjudication. The model survives only in the regime where afferent feedback is intact, vascular responses lie within physiological conductance bounds, and ambient thermal load is held constant. The source is a single OCR page from William James, Principles of Psychology, Vol. 2, printed page 454 [PAGE 454]. James conducts a phase transition in the science of emotion: he moves from descriptive classification to genetic inquiry. The first hop is explicit in the text: "We step from a superficial to a deep order of inquiry. Classification and description are the lowest stage of science. They sink into the background the moment questions of genesis are formulated" [PAGE 454]. The second hop is the constitutive claim: "Now the moment the genesis of an emotion is accounted for, as the arousal by an object of a lot of reflex acts which are forthwith felt" [PAGE 454]. Here the object does not merely cause an emotion; it arouses reflex acts, and the feeling of those acts is the emotion.

The third hop concerns reflex variability. James states, "For there is nothing sacramental or eternally fixed in reflex action. Any sort of reflex effect is possible, and reflexes actually vary indefinitely, as we know" [PAGE 454]. He then gives concrete counter-examples to any fixed expression catalogue: "We have all seen men dumb, instead of talkative, with joy; we have seen fright drive the blood into the head of its victim, instead of making him pale; we have seen grief run restlessly about lamenting, instead of sitting bowed down and mute ; etc., etc." [PAGE 454]. The mechanism for this variance is twofold: "one and the same cause can work differently on different men's blood-vessels (since these do not always react alike), whilst moreover the impulse on its way through the brain to the vaso-motor centre is differently influenced by different earlier impressions in the form of recollections or associations of ideas" [PAGE 454].

The fourth hop is the anti-essentialist conclusion: "any classification of the emotions is seen to be as true and as'natural' as any other, if it only serves some purpose" [PAGE 454]. Consequently, "What is the'real'or'typical expression of anger, or fear ?" has "no objective meaning at all" [PAGE 454]. The replacement question is historical and mechanical: how did any given expression come to exist, as "a real question of physiological mechanics on the one hand, and of history on the other" [PAGE 454]. James closes the page by noting the experimental difficulty and promising later discussion of attempts to answer the question [PAGE 454].

Gray-zone audit. The text never operationalizes "a lot of reflex acts"; there is no cardinality, no threshold, and no information measure specifying when a collection of felt reflexes becomes an emotion rather than a diffuse bodily state. The boundary condition between emotion and non-emotion is therefore undefined. The split between "physiological mechanics" and "history" [PAGE 454] leaves the interaction term unspecified: how exactly do "recollections or associations of ideas" weight the impulse to the vaso-motor centre? This is the precise gray zone later colonized by appraisal and constructionist theories. Finally, the admission of "DIFFICULTY OF TESTING THE THEORY EXPERIMENTALLY" [PAGE 454] marks a breakdown regime: when afferent bodily feedback cannot be isolated from concurrent cognitive appraisal, the genesis map cannot be identified by bodily manipulation alone. The theory thus requires intact somatic feedback, bounded vascular mechanics, and controlled association history to remain valid; outside that regime it is underdetermined.
