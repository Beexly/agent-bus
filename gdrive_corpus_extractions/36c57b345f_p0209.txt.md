The defense must decide pre-snap whether the left-side blocking is the real attack or a screen for the right-edge sweep. Clockwise, 1905 football had no play clock and a less segmented game clock than modern football, but the play is still slow-developing: the Left Tackle must release, arc, receive, and accelerate before defensive containment arrives [PAGE 195]. In a clock-protective situation (holding a lead), a stuffed sweep consumes downs and time without yardage; in a trailing situation, the long development risks a third-down-and-long or a defensive stop that returns possession with little clock run off. The play therefore favors a neutral or slightly clock-consuming game state, not a desperate two-minute analog [PAGE 195].

Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof for the play [PAGE 195]. A veto at any layer is sufficient to cancel the play.

Weather layer (explicit source veto). [PAGE 195] states: "This play should not be attempted on a wet field." Because no quantitative threshold is provided, the veto is categorical: any observed field wetness W=1 triggers a hard veto. Wet grass reduces the coefficient of friction between ball and hands, between cleats and turf, and between blocker and defender, raising slip probability at the handoff and during the Left Tackle's curved release [PAGE 195].

Physical layer. The Left Tackle, mass m \approx 90–110 kg, must traverse an arc from his initial line position to the Quarter Back and then to the edge hole before the defensive end contains. If the straight-line distance from release point to edge hole is d \approx 5 m and the defensive edge arrival window is \Delta t \approx 2.5 s, the required average acceleration magnitude is a = 2d / \Delta t^2 \approx 1.6 m/s^2, excluding the arc detour. Any increase in release latency t_rel raises required acceleration approximately as 1/(\Delta t - t_rel)^2, quickly exceeding a lineman's practical acceleration reserve. If the required acceleration is not physically attainable for that player, the play is vetoed on physical grounds independent of weather [PAGE 195].

Biomechanical layer. The Quarter Back-to-Left Tackle handoff occurs while both are moving; relative speed \|\mathbf{v}_{LT} - \mathbf{v}_{QB}\| must remain below a maximum handoff speed v_{handoff,max} to avoid a muff. The text requires the Quarter Back to hand the ball and then "assist the runner on the left side, keeping him on his feet" [PAGE 195], implying the Quarter Back remains in contact after the exchange. If the arc forces a high crossing angle, the handoff becomes a lateral toss rather than a secure mesh, and the biomechanical failure mode is fumble. In addition, 1905 equipment (minimal padding, soft leather helmets) raises injury risk at the edge where the runner meets unblocked or partially blocked defenders [PAGE 195].
