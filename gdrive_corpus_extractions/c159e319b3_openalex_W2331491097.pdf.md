On a case violating those conditions, the analytic candidate will fail the viscosity test even if it satisfies the PDE on smooth patches, exactly as the source warns [PAGE 24].

**Scenario 2: Static-Nash convergence veto.** Counterfactual: a model asserts that every acceptable trajectory must converge to the unique static Nash equilibrium. Use the appendix’s “almost antagonistic” case with \(A=C_1\) and \(B=C_3\), where the bimatrix game has one Nash equilibrium \(NE=(2/3,1/3)\) [PAGE 38]. Veto trigger: simulate the positive feedback controls from the same initial positions used in Figures 11–13 and measure the Euclidean distance to \(NE\) versus the distance to the intersection of switch lines \(SW1,SW2\). Prediction: the acceptable trajectory will not converge to \(NE\); it will tend to the switch-line intersection, and the long-term functionals \(J_1,J_2\) computed on that trajectory will be larger than those computed on a trajectory that converges to \(NE\). This prediction is directly supported by the source’s statement that the acceptable trajectories “don’t converge to the static Nash equilibrium NE ... but tend to the point of intersection of switch lines SW1, SW2” and that the long-term payoffs are “better” [PAGE 38]. A verifier can therefore assign a binary veto to any claim of static-Nash convergence in that case.

**Scenario 3: Contraction-horizon veto.** Counterfactual: a numerical solver uses a time step \(h\ge1/\lambda\), or uses an iteration count \(m\) that does not satisfy \((1-\lambda h)^m\le h^{1/2}\), yet claims the theorem’s \(O(h^{1/2})\) accuracy. Veto trigger: check the two explicit hypotheses. The source requires \(h\in(0,1/\lambda)\) and states that the contraction coefficients of \(\Pi^*,\Pi_*\) are \((1-\lambda h)\) [PAGE 27–28]; the convergence theorem requires \(m\) satisfying \((1-\lambda h)^m\le h^{1/2}\) [PAGE 29]. Prediction: if \(h\ge1/\lambda\), the contraction factor is not in \((0,1)\), so the successive-approximation argument loses its contraction guarantee; if \(m\) is too small, the truncation error bound is not met and the observed error may stagnate or oscillate under grid refinement. A verifier can therefore veto the numerical claim on the basis of hypothesis violation alone, without needing an external empirical dataset. This is a purely source-internal, verifiable counterfactual.

**Scenario 4: Short-term replicator-constraint veto.** Counterfactual: a model replaces the long-term control with the myopic argmax of \(\partial g_1/\partial x\) and ignores the state-dependent constraints (9.5)–(9.10). Veto trigger: check whether the resulting control satisfies the sign-dependent constraints. If \(\partial g_1/\partial x=C_Ay-\alpha_1\ge0\), the source requires \(x\le\phi_1(x,y)\le u\le\phi_2(x,y)\le1\); if the sign is negative, it requires \(0\le\phi_3(x,y)\le u\le\phi_4(x,y)\le x\) [PAGE 35]. With the replicator choice \(\phi_1=\phi_4=x+x(x-1)(C_Ay-\alpha_1)\), the constraint is not the unconstrained myopic argmax unless the sign and boundary conditions align [PAGE 34]. Prediction: a myopic unconstrained simulation will produce a control history that violates the source’s constraints at some grid nodes, while the constrained replicator simulation will satisfy them by construction. A verifier can assign a binary veto to the unconstrained myopic claim whenever a constraint violation is detected. This is also a formal test of the paper’s “alliance” of long- and short-term interests, not an external physical claim.
