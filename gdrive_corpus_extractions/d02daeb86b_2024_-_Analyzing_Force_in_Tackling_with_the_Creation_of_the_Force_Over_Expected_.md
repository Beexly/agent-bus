<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind layer treats the tackle as a two-agent belief-updating problem. The tackler forms a belief about the ball carrier’s future path; the ball carrier forms a belief about the tackler’s pursuit angle; both update under time pressure. The source’s variables are proxies for this recursion: DIOVDT_T measures “how well a tackler ‘squared up’ his movement during the tackle” [PAGE 3]; tackleAngle measures where the ball carrier was tackled relative to the direction he faced [PAGE 3]; ballReceivedAngle measures whether the tackler was moving toward or away from the ball carrier’s movement at the moment the ball was received [PAGE 3]; and distFromEachOther measures separation in yards [PAGE 3].

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under high unpredictability, a defender may regress to a habitual pursuit angle rather than a controlled squared-up approach. The source’s counterintuitive DIOVDT_T result is relevant: “greater differences were associated with a higher expected force” [PAGE 5], and the paper suggests this may occur because “tacklers square up their momentum more frequently when tackling from behind” [PAGE 5]. In theory-of-mind terms, the defender’s habit is not simply “bad”; it is conditional on the ball carrier’s likely path and the defender’s information state. Along the sideline, the source says defenders face “greater unpredictability because these tackle attempts are likely to be reactive to the ball carrier’s movement” [PAGE 8]. Reactive tackling is a crisis-habit regime: the defender’s belief about the cutback is uncertain, so the approach may be less squared-up and less force-productive.

The counter-trap is the second failure mode. A ball carrier can accelerate away from the tackler, making \(\phi\) near zero and subtracting from \(F_{\rm gen}\); this is the source’s negative-component regime [PAGES 1-2]. But the ball carrier can also cut back toward the tackler, making \(\phi\) near \(\pi\) and producing an outlier where actual force is much higher than expected [PAGES 4-5]. A counter-trap model predicts that high ball-carrier acceleration is not uniformly “more force”; its sign depends on the angular relation. The source confirms the statistical shadow of this: ball-carrier mass and acceleration have “a negative correlation with expected force” [PAGE 5], because high acceleration often means movement away from the tackle direction.

The clock is the third failure mode. Let \(d\) be separation and \(\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_T\) the relative velocity. A closing time-to-contact proxy is \(t_c=d/\|\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_T\|\) when the denominator is nonzero. As \(t_c\to 0\), the defender has less time for belief correction, so habitual pursuit dominates. If \(\|\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_T\|=0\), \(t_c\) is singular: the players are not closing, or the relative velocity is tangential. The source’s TacklerAcc_BR and ballReceivedAngle variables are early-clock signals: a tackler already accelerating toward the ball carrier at the moment of reception is more likely to make a proactive tackle [PAGE 3]. Thus the recursive model is: early information shapes belief; belief shapes approach angle; approach angle shapes projected force; force residual shapes evaluation. The source’s positional result that defensive backs underperform while strong safeties do better [PAGE 7] is consistent with a position-dependent clock and information regime, not with a single scalar “tackling force” skill.
