The source’s inference chain is explicit: raw Strava streams are converted into training-volume, terrain-normalized, best-effort, race-pace-specific, workout, heart-rate-efficiency, and demographic features; those features are materialized with Spark and regressed with XGBoost; the model is evaluated by RMSE in minutes and then interpreted through XGBoost gain and three case studies. The first multi-hop step is terrain normalization. The paper defines gradient-adjusted distance as “the effective distance of a course taking into account the elevation changes” [PAGE 3], then converts that into course difficulty by dividing course GAD by marathon distance, with equation “𝐶𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑠𝑒 𝐷𝑖𝑓𝑓𝑖𝑐𝑢𝑙𝑡𝑦= 𝐶𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑠𝑒 𝐺𝐴𝐷/𝑀𝑎𝑟𝑎𝑡ℎ𝑜𝑛 𝐷𝑖𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑛𝑐𝑒 (26.22 𝑚𝑖𝑙𝑒𝑠)(1)” [PAGE 6]. That lets the model compare a training run, a race course, or a best-effort segment on a flat-equivalent basis. The second step is fitness extraction without requiring a prior race: for fixed times and distances, the paper finds the best sub-stream and builds a “best-effort curve” [PAGE 4-5]. The third step is race-pace specificity: the model measures continuous and cumulative distance around goal marathon pace, plus 90% and 95% variants [PAGE 7]. The fourth step is aerobic efficiency: heartbeats per mile uses a fixed heartbeat budget derived from lactate-threshold heart rate over one hour [PAGE 7-8]. The fifth step is supervised learning: XGBoost is chosen because it runs distributed and gives quantitative feature importance [PAGE 8], with RMSE as the optimization target because it is in “the same intuitive units as the prediction variable - in this case, estimated finish time in minutes” [PAGE 9].

The reported test-set figures are strong: 62,933 test races, average finish 3:43:56, mean error 0:03, RMSE 8:46, MAPE 2.7%, R² 0.93 [PAGE 10]. The gray-zone audit must separate population accuracy from individual decision validity. A mean error of three seconds does not imply a three-second individual error; RMSE 8:46 means substantial right-tail residuals. The paper itself notes that “Means and standard deviations, however, are not the language that athletes speak” [PAGE 11], and quotes Outside Magazine’s critique that a 7.7% error for a three-hour marathoner is almost fourteen minutes [PAGE 11]. The paper’s claim is that its tighter band bridges that utility gap, but it does not report prediction intervals, calibration curves, or athlete-level uncertainty. That is a gray zone. Another gray zone is data splitting: “We also split our data into a training, validation, and test set and used GridSearchCV to tune the model to avoid overfitting” [PAGE 9]. The text does not specify whether the split is athlete-level or activity-level. If repeat athletes appear in both train and test, the reported RMSE can be optimistic because athlete-specific physiology, routes, and device behavior leak across splits. A third gray zone is heart-rate privacy: “Due to privacy regulations, we are unable to process all athletes’ heart rate data for research purposes without first obtaining consent” [PAGE 8]. The HBPM feature is therefore demonstrated on a small consented sample, not the full 356,618 marathons. A fourth gray zone is the GAD operator: the paper cites an improved GAP model [PAGE 3, reference 8] but does not reproduce the grade-cost function, so the exact transformation is not auditable from the paper alone. A fifth gray zone is course difficulty: the paper explicitly says the difficulty “does not encompass where in the course the difficulty may arise” and “does not encompass the weather” [PAGE 6]. A sixth gray zone is case-study causality: the n=3 case studies are illustrative, not identified causal estimates; Varun’s improvement co-occurs with more active days, more workouts, and weight change [PAGE 13-14], so the paper’s attribution is a plausible decomposition, not an isolated treatment effect. Finally, the table on PAGE 12 contains “-1” entries for “Total Workouts” for Nikhil’s races, indicating missing-value handling that is not fully described. These gray zones do not refute the model; they define the conditions under which its predictions should be trusted, audited, or vetoed.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source gives one explicit named equation. Course difficulty is:
\[
C_{\text{course}}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{GAD}}}{D_{\mathrm{mar}}},\qquad D_{\mathrm{mar}}=26.22\ \mathrm{mi},
\]
reproduced as “𝐶𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑠𝑒 𝐷𝑖𝑓𝑓𝑖𝑐𝑢𝑙𝑡𝑦= 𝐶𝑜𝑢𝑟𝑠𝑒 𝐺𝐴𝐷/𝑀𝑎𝑟𝑎𝑡ℎ𝑜𝑛 𝐷𝑖𝑠𝑡𝑎𝑛𝑐𝑒 (26.22 𝑚𝑖𝑙𝑒𝑠)(1)” [PAGE 6]. The source interprets GAD as “the effective distance of a course taking into account the elevation changes” [PAGE 3]. A formal audit notation, not a source-specified formula, is:
\[
D_{\mathrm{GAD}}=\int_{0}^{D}\phi\!\left(g(s)\right)\,ds,
\]
where \(s\) is course arc length in meters, \(g(s)=dz/ds\) is dimensionless grade, and \(\phi(g)\) is a dimensionless local cost multiplier with \(\phi(0)=1\). The paper does not publish \(\phi\); it cites an external GAP model [PAGE 3, ref. 8]. The course-time scaling implied by the table is:
\[
T_{\text{course}}\approx C_{\text{course}}\,T_{\text{flat}},
\]
with \(T\) in minutes. For a 3:00 flat marathoner, the table gives Boston GAD 26.446 mi, difficulty 1.0087, equivalent 3:01:33; Berlin GAD 26.285 mi, difficulty 1.0025, equivalent 3:00:27 [PAGE 6]. Units are miles for GAD and dimensionless for difficulty. The linear scaling assumption breaks down at extreme grades, steep downhills where the cost curve is non-monotonic, and hill placement, because the paper warns that Boston’s hills are “backloaded (from miles 16-21)” and “much worse than coming at miles 1-6” [PAGE 6]. Singularities occur if \(\phi(g)\) is unbounded or if \(D_{\mathrm{mar}}\to 0\); operationally, the breakdown regime is any course whose fatigue-weighted grade cost is not captured by an additive integral.

Heartbeats per mile is defined by example rather than a single displayed equation. The source states: “At 6: 00/mile, HBPM= 1080 beats/mile” for an LT of 180 beats/minute and “1 hour @ LT= 180 beats/minute*60 minutes= 10800 heartbeats” [PAGE 8]. Formal notation:
\[
B_i=H_i\cdot 60\ \mathrm{min},\qquad
\mathrm{HBPM}_i(a,b)=\frac{\int_a^b H_i(t)\,dt}{D_i(a,b)},
\]
where \(H_i(t)\) is beats/minute, \(B_i\) is heartbeats, \(D_i\) is miles, and HBPM is beats/mile. The paper then finds “every subsection of an athlete’s runs that encompass this number of heartbeats” and takes the longest distance covered [PAGE 8]. Thus:
\[
D_i^*(B_i)=\max_{[a,b]:\int_a^b H_i(t)dt\le B_i}D_i(a,b).
\]
Breakdown regimes: optical-heart-rate latency, cardiac drift, heat/anxiety-driven heart rate, incorrect LT estimation, and missing consent data, since the paper says the sample was limited by privacy regulations [PAGE 8].

Best-effort curves are operators over activity streams. For fixed distance \(d\), the model finds the minimum moving time over all sub-streams:
\[
T_{\min}(d)=\min_{[a,b]:D(a,b)=d}\left(t_b-t_a\right),\qquad P(d)=T_{\min}(d)/d,
\]
with \(P\) in minutes/mile; for fixed time \(\tau\), it finds maximum distance and \(P(\tau)=D_{\max}(\tau)/\tau\). The source says it can “find the best sub-stream (or section of the run) of various fixed distances ... and times” [PAGE 4]. Singularities occur as \(d\to 0\), where GPS and pace noise dominate, and in sparse training histories where no sub-stream exists near the requested duration or distance.

XGBoost is the supervised operator. The source says “We chose to use a gradient-boosted framework called XGBoost” [PAGE 8] and optimized RMSE [PAGE 9]. Definitions:
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat t_i-t_i\right)^2}\ \mathrm{min},
\quad
\mathrm{MAPE}=\frac{100\%}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|\hat t_i-t_i|}{t_i},
\quad
R^2=1-\frac{SS_{\mathrm{res}}}{SS_{\mathrm{tot}}}.
\]
RMSE is minutes, MAPE is percent, and \(R^2\) is dimensionless. The test-set values are “62,933 ... 3:43:56 ... 0:03 ... 8:46 ... 2.7% ... 0.93” [PAGE 10]. XGBoost gain is a relative split-quality score, not an information-theoretic bit count:
\[
\mathrm{Gain}=\frac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}-\frac{(G_L+G_R)^2}{H_L+H_R+\lambda}\right]-\gamma,
\]
where \(G\) and \(H\) are first- and second-order gradient sums and \(\lambda,\gamma\) are regularization parameters. The paper uses “gain” qualitatively: “The best predictors are prior best efforts, notably those closest to most athlete’s lactate thresholds (7 miles, 1 hour) rather than strictly the longest ones (half marathon)” and “Specificity (time spent around, but not faster, than goal pace) beats volume (long runs and weekly mileage)” [PAGE 11]. No probability calibration or information-unit interpretation is reported, so gain must not be treated as mutual information in bits.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs from Cold War periodization and Bannister’s four-minute mile through Nike’s Breaking2 project to modern Strava-scale marathon modeling [PAGE 1]. It invokes Pheidippides and the Battle of Marathon [PAGE 1], then grounds endurance running in evolutionary biology via Bramble and Lieberman [PAGE 1, ref. 4]. The physiological lineage is visible in its training principles: glycogen stores sustaining “90 to 120 minutes” without nourishment [PAGE 2], lactate threshold as “the speed or heart rate that an athlete can hold for 1 hour all-out” [PAGE 3], running economy as the energy cost at a given pace [PAGE 3], and the marathon cycle as “around three to four months” [PAGE 2]. The computational lineage is Spark, XGBoost, GridSearchCV, and gradient-boosted feature gain [PAGE 8-9]. The modern comparison lineage includes Riegel and Runner’s World, FiveThirtyEight, Vickers/Vertosick, Altini/Amft, and Smyth/Muniz-Pumares [PAGE 10, refs. 5, 10, 16-18].

The retrocausal trap is multi-week attribution. The paper tries 3-, 6-, and 12-month windows: “The two longer time frames provided better results but were not themselves that distinguishable. We thus went with a 6-month window” [PAGE 3]. This choice is pragmatic, but it means the model’s “training cycle” is an operational window, not a clean physiological cause. The paper also says “Physiological gains are accumulated over an entire lifetime of miles run” [PAGE 2], which creates a long-horizon confound: a six-month feature vector is a recent slice of a much longer adaptation history. In the case studies, Varun’s 13-minute improvement is attributed to consistency and workouts, with volume not markedly different [PAGE 13-14]; Dave’s Santa Rosa preparation appears better than Boston 2019 even though times are similar, which the paper suggests “probably highlights how people learn to race the marathon and their bodies adapt to it over time (Consistency)” [PAGE 15]. Those are retrocausal readings: the outcome is known, then the prior training is narrated as cause. The multi-week traps are injury and burnout from longer cycles, incomplete adaptation from shorter cycles, taper and race-day variability, and the fact that consistency, workouts, and weight can change together. A causal claim would require holding the other training layers fixed or using repeated-measures designs across many athletes; the paper’s three case studies are hypothesis-generating, not causal identification.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is athlete-model-athlete feedback. The model predicts an athlete’s time, the athlete interprets the prediction as a goal or verdict, changes training, and then races under a new psychological frame. The paper sees this in the round-number clustering: “A series of distinctly clustered bands emerge from the scatter plot at round boundaries - 3:00, 3:30, 4:00, etc.” [PAGE 11]. That is a clock effect: athletes pace and select goals around culturally salient boundaries, so the model is learning both physiology and goal psychology. The Boston qualifying cutoff is the starkest clock: Dave ran “2:58:39” but the cutoff was “2:58:21” [PAGE 14], so a qualifying-time target became a hard institutional clock. The recursive trap is that a prediction can become a self-fulfilling pacing target or a self-limiting identity.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode: when an athlete is close to a goal, they regress to the habit they trust, even if the model says the marginal gain is elsewhere. Varun “perhaps even over-indexed” on workouts compared with the average marathoner of his speed, and that developed speed carried into the longer distance [PAGE 14]. But for a top-100 Boston goal, the paper says his “lack of high mileage and long run distance and frequency is glaring” and recommends building sustained mileage [PAGE 14]. The counter-trap is to avoid overcorrecting into the model’s top feature. Dave is told to focus “perhaps less on accumulating time on feet” and instead spend miles at faster speeds, while Nikhil is told to increase mileage “across the board” [PAGE 15]. The correct recursion is not “maximize the most important feature”; it is “close the specific gap relative to the goal population while preserving the athlete’s existing strengths.” The clock then re-enters: a 2:55 goal is not just a physiological target; it is a buffer against Boston’s cutoff uncertainty. The model’s prescription must therefore be goal-aware, not merely time-minimizing, and it must avoid turning a population feature ranking into a universal training ideology.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical veto is established by the paper’s own scope limits. Course difficulty is computed from GAD, but the paper says it “does not encompass where in the course the difficulty may arise” and “does not encompass the weather, which is notoriously fickle in Boston in April compared to Berlin in September” [PAGE 6]. For a 3:00 marathoner, the table’s course-only Boston-Berlin difference is 3:01:33 versus 3:00:27, or 66 seconds [PAGE 6]. A hot or windy Boston day can exceed that 66-second course correction, so a course-only prediction cannot adjudicate the actual result. The model also omits gear and weather as inputs, with the paper listing them as future work: “we could incorporate data about gear ... as well as weather - both of these are already available in the Strava dataset” [PAGE 15]. Biomechanical and nutritional layers are not modeled either. The paper’s weight discussion is a veto against naive “race weight” optimization: “race weight is a complicated concept - generally speaking, lighter weight means lowered energy expenditure required, but it is more important to stay biomechanically, nutritionally, and psychologically healthy and not let weight jeopardize those far more important characteristics” [PAGE 14]. Therefore, if a prediction is used to prescribe training, the cross-layer veto is: any intervention that changes injury risk, gait mechanics, footwear, heat tolerance, glycogen availability, or illness status can dominate a small GAD or feature-gain difference. The model’s RMSE of 8:46 [PAGE 10] is the scale against which such vetoes should be judged; a weather or biomechanical perturbation larger than that scale makes the training-feature prescription non-identifiable for that athlete.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto 1: weather-residual veto. Train the same XGBoost pipeline with and without race-day weather covariates, holding the athlete-level split fixed. Prediction: without weather, residuals will be positively biased on hot or windy races after course-GAD correction, and the bias will be largest where the course-only correction is small relative to weather stress. Veto condition: if adding weather does not reduce held-out RMSE below 8:46 [PAGE 10] or if residuals show no weather association, the weather-veto hypothesis is not supported. This is verifiable because race date, location, and weather are external labels, and the paper already says weather is available in Strava [PAGE 15].

Veto 2: HBPM-consent veto. On the consented heart-rate subset, compare XGBoost with HBPM against XGBoost without HBPM using athlete-grouped cross-validation. Prediction: HBPM will improve RMSE on the consented subset because the paper reports that it “showed a lot of promise as a feature in improving the model” [PAGE 8]. Veto condition: if the HBPM gain is smaller than the cross-validation standard error or if the consented subset’s prediction errors are not improved, the claim that HBPM is a generalizable feature is vetoed for the full population. This is verifiable because the source explicitly limits HBPM to a small consented sample due to privacy regulations [PAGE 8].

Veto 3: athlete-leakage veto. Re-run the reported test evaluation with an athlete-level split rather than an unspecified activity-level split, since the paper only says it “split our data into a training, validation, and test set” [PAGE 9]. Prediction: athlete-grouped RMSE will be higher than 8:46, especially for repeat marathoners, because athlete-specific physiology and device behavior can leak across activity-level splits. Veto condition: if athlete-grouped RMSE is statistically indistinguishable from the reported 8:46 and the mean error remains near 0:03 [PAGE 10], the leakage concern is not supported. This is verifiable from Strava athlete identifiers and repeated race participation, and it directly tests whether the reported precision is a population generalization or an athlete-memorization effect.
