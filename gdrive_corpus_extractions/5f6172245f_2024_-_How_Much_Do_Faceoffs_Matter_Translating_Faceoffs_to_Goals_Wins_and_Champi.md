The paper’s causal chain is not “faceoff win causes goal.” The source explicitly guards against that: “there is no automatic relationship between faceoffs and goal scoring” [PAGE 1]. The derivation instead runs through possession, zone time, and a cutoff rule. A faceoff is a discrete reset: “Occurring around 60 times per game, hockey faceoffs uniquely start without any team possessing the puck but invariably end with one team in control” [PAGE 1]. The first multi-hop step is therefore to identify what follows a win that would not have followed a loss. The paper’s estimator is incremental: compare the offense generated after an offensive-zone win with the offense generated after an offensive-zone loss. It uses zone changes as the attribution boundary: “We use zone changes—transitions of the puck between the offensive, neutral, and defensive partitions of the rink—as a barometer for faceoff attributability, considering any incremental offense generated by the faceoff-winning team before a zone change occurs” [PAGE 3]. This prevents the absurd extreme where every later goal is credited to the nearest faceoff, while avoiding the opposite extreme where faceoffs and scoring are treated as disjoint.

The second step is the definition of the two objectives. “Net expected goals: the cumulative expected goal differential gained through offensive zone faceoff wins and forfeited through faceoff losses” [PAGE 4]. “Incremental zone time: a goal-scoring-agnostic measure of the expected gain in offensive zone time achieved via a faceoff win relative to a loss” [PAGE 4]. The third step is the defensive-zone sign inversion: “for the defensive team, the net expected goals and incremental zone time are each considered to be the negative of that credited to the offensive team” [PAGE 4]. This means a defensive-zone win is credited by how much opponent expected offense and zone time are suppressed relative to a loss. Neutral-zone faceoffs are omitted because the expected-goal support is weak and neutral-zone possession is largely transitional: “Accordingly, we omit neutral zone faceoffs from consideration” [PAGE 4].

The fourth step is a modeling fix for sparsity. The source reports that net expected goals is sparse because “many non-neutral-zone faceoffs quickly resulting in a zone change (or play stoppage) that prevents any faceoff-attributable expected goal generation” [PAGE 6], and regression models suffered “too much bias and far too little variance” [PAGE 7]. The paper therefore trains an incremental-zone-time model first and translates it into net expected goals using a constant league-average conversion rate. The source says “IZT is measured in seconds per faceoff, and NXG is measured in expected goals per faceoff” [PAGE 7], and “we intentionally choose to instead use a constant league-average rate for expected goals accrued per second of incremental faceoff-attributable offensive zone time” [PAGE 7]. The fifth step is seasonal aggregation: Table 2 multiplies per-faceoff NXG by 82 games and by an assumed number of extra faceoff wins per game. The source gives Kopitar as “an additional 1.82 goals in offense over the course of a full season beyond his existing projected output from Table 1 if he started winning one more faceoff per game” [PAGE 9]. That is consistent with \(0.022\) goals per faceoff times 82 games, up to rounding.

The gray-zone audit is where the claim must be weakened. First, the estimand is conditional on zone-change attribution; if the tracking of zone changes is noisy, the cutoff is noisy. Second, the constant conversion rate is a deliberate abstraction, not a claim that every player converts zone time identically. Third, Table 2 assumes linear benefit: “the reflected numbers assume a linear benefit for each incremental non-neutral-zone faceoff win” [PAGE 10]. Fourth, the source warns that “professional NHL centers are almost certainly maximizing their faceoff win percentages already” [PAGE 10], so the counterfactual of adding wins is constrained. Fifth, the leaderboard is out-of-sample relative to training but built on the All Three Zones subset, not full seasons. Sixth, the paper’s strongest conclusion is modest: “elite faceoff performers can eclipse over five goals per season in projected effect” [PAGE 12]. The correct interpretation is therefore a conditional, model-based goal-value estimate, not a deterministic faceoff-to-championship law.

Let \(f\) index a faceoff. Let \(z_f \in \{O,D,N\}\) denote offensive, defensive, or neutral zone. Neutral-zone faceoffs are excluded, so the modeled set is \(z_f \in \{O,D\}\). Let \(W_f^{O} \in \{0,1\}\) denote whether the team in the offensive zone wins the faceoff, with \(W_f^{O}=1\) for a win and \(W_f^{O}=0\) for a loss. Let \(G_f\) be the expected goals for the offensive-zone team in the post-faceoff sequence until the next zone change or stoppage, and let \(T_f\) be the corresponding offensive-zone time in seconds. All expectations are conditional on the faceoff location and covariates.

For an offensive-zone faceoff, the paper’s net-expected-goals estimand can be written as
\[
NXG_O(f)=E[G_f\mid z_f=O,W_f^{O}=1]-E[G_f\mid z_f=O,W_f^{O}=0],
\]
with units goals per faceoff. The incremental-zone-time estimand is
\[
IZT_O(f)=E[T_f\mid z_f=O,W_f^{O}=1]-E[T_f\mid z_f=O,W_f^{O}=0],
\]
with units seconds per faceoff. These equations match the source’s definitions: “Net expected goals: the cumulative expected goal differential gained through offensive zone faceoff wins and forfeited through faceoff losses” [PAGE 4], and “Incremental zone time: a goal-scoring-agnostic measure of the expected gain in offensive zone time achieved via a faceoff win relative to a loss” [PAGE 4].

For a defensive-zone faceoff, let \(W_f^{D}=1\) denote a win by the defensively positioned team. The paper’s sign convention is “for the defensive team, the net expected goals and incremental zone time are each considered to be the negative of that credited to the offensive team” [PAGE 4]. Formally,
\[
NXG_D(f)=-\left(E[G_f\mid z_f=D,W_f^{D}=1]-E[G_f\mid z_f=D,W_f^{D}=0]\right),
\]
\[
IZT_D(f)=-\left(E[T_f\mid z_f=D,W_f^{D}=1]-E[T_f\mid z_f=D,W_f^{D}=0]\right).
\]
The sign is positive when a defensive-zone win suppresses opponent expected goals or zone time relative to a defensive-zone loss. This is why the source notes that defensive values can be “frequently positive, indicating credit to the defensive center” [PAGE 4].

The empirical estimator for an offensive-zone stratum is
\[
\widehat{NXG}_O=
\frac{1}{N_W}\sum_{f:W_f^{O}=1}G_f-
\frac{1}{N_L}\sum_{f:W_f^{O}=0}G_f,
\]
where \(N_W\) and \(N_L\) are faceoff counts. Units remain goals per faceoff. \(N_W\) and \(N_L\) are the information-bearing sample sizes; if either approaches zero, the conditional means are undefined or unstable. The source addresses this with “regression to the mean for projections based on small quantities of faceoffs” [PAGE 7]. The variance units are goals squared per faceoff squared.

The translation from zone time to expected goals is
\[
NXG(f)=\lambda\,IZT(f),
\]
where \(\lambda\) is the league-average expected-goals rate per second of incremental faceoff-attributable offensive-zone time, with units goals per second. The source’s wording is exact: “we intentionally choose to instead use a constant league-average rate for expected goals accrued per second of incremental faceoff-attributable offensive zone time” [PAGE 7]. From Table 1, Kopitar’s rounded values are \(IZT=5.03\) seconds per faceoff and \(NXG=0.022\) goals per faceoff [PAGE 8], implying \(\lambda \approx 0.022/5.03 \approx 4.37\times 10^{-3}\) goals per second for that rounded display.

The seasonal increment equation used in Table 2 is
\[
\Delta G_i(m)=82\,m\,NXG_i,
\]
where \(m\) is the assumed number of additional non-neutral-zone faceoff wins per game and \(\Delta G_i(m)\) is in goals per 82-game season. The source gives the Kopitar example: “an additional 1.82 goals in offense over the course of a full season beyond his existing projected output from Table 1 if he started winning one more faceoff per game” [PAGE 9]. Table 2’s linearity is an assumption, not a derived law: “the reflected numbers assume a linear benefit for each incremental non-neutral-zone faceoff win” [PAGE 10].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit. First, \(z_f=N\) is outside the model: “we omit neutral zone faceoffs from consideration” [PAGE 4]. Second, as the probability of immediate zone change approaches one, the NXG support collapses toward zero; the source describes “many non-neutral-zone faceoffs quickly resulting in a zone change (or play stoppage) that prevents any faceoff-attributable expected goal generation” [PAGE 6]. Third, if \(N_W\) or \(N_L\) is near zero, the per-faceoff conditional means become unstable; this is the small-sample singularity. Fourth, if the true conversion rate \(\lambda_i\) varies by player or line, the constant-\(\lambda\) model has bias \((\bar\lambda-\lambda_i)IZT_i\) in goals per faceoff. Fifth, if the defensive sign convention is not antisymmetric between teams, the defensive credit equation fails. Sixth, if marginal faceoff wins produce diminishing or nonlinear returns, Table 2 overstates the seasonal effect. Seventh, the source’s data are not full seasons: the training data come from the All Three Zones subset [PAGE 5]. The model is therefore a conditional projection, not an unbounded causal operator.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a reaction to the raw-faceoff-percentage skepticism literature. The source says several articles [1], [2], [3] “basically arguing that they are not important because faceoff win percentage in its raw form is fairly uncorrelated with goal-scoring metrics or game win percentage” [PAGE 1]. The retrocausal move is to reinterpret that null result: raw faceoff percentage is a coarse summary that mixes locations, deployment, linemates, and post-faceoff sequences. The 2019 Sloan paper by Czuzoj-Shulman et al is the mechanical ancestor: it focuses on “how directionality, clean wins, and player handedness play a significant role in creating value” [PAGE 1]. The current paper does not reject that; it shifts the level of analysis from how a faceoff is won to how much goal value a center’s faceoff performance implies in a given deployment. The Schuckers et al paper is the quantitative ancestor: it suggests that a center improving from “winning 50% of their faceoffs to winning 60% of them gains just over 12 goals per season which is equivalent to two additional wins” [PAGE 2]. The current paper relaxes Schuckers’ assumption that “after 20 seconds the impact of an individual event in the NHL is noise with the exception of penalties” [PAGE 2] and replaces it with zone-change attribution. It also moves from center-by-center modeling to line-by-line modeling, explicitly to account for linemates and opposition.

The data genealogy matters. Evolving Hockey supplies play-by-play [PAGE 4], but it omits zone changes. Corey Sznajder’s All Three Zones project supplies hand-tracked zone entries and exits [PAGE 5]. The paper’s R linkage between these datasets is itself a source of value because it creates play-by-play data with zone changes as events [PAGE 5]. Hockey Reference supplies the box-score table context [PAGE 10-11]. This lineage explains why the paper’s conclusion is not a reversal of prior skepticism but a conditional refinement: faceoffs are important only through a measurable incremental-possession channel, and only for a small subset of players.

The multi-week traps are genealogical and observational. Trap one is the raw-correlation trap: a hot faceoff week or month can look predictive while location and usage are omitted. Trap two is the small-sample specialist trap: a center with a short run of high faceoff wins can appear elite, but the source implements “regression to the mean for projections based on small quantities of faceoffs” [PAGE 7]. Trap three is the deployment-endogeneity trap: coaches change usage after observing faceoff performance, so later faceoff wins are not independent of earlier outcomes. Trap four is the linemate-attribution trap: a center’s post-faceoff offense may reflect linemates, which is why the paper uses line-level features [PAGE 2]. Trap five is the special-teams extrapolation trap: the power-play discussion is explicitly framed as a “worthy topic of future research” [PAGE 11], not a settled result. Trap six is the linear-extrapolation trap: Table 2’s +1 through +6 columns are arithmetic under linearity, not evidence that coaches can realize six extra wins per game. Trap seven is the box-score integration trap: Table 3 adds NXG alongside goals, points, and plus-minus, but the source warns that faceoff performance is “likely distributed across nearly every statistic in the box score since nearly all depend on puck possession” [PAGE 10]. These traps persist over multiple weeks because they are structural, not because a single game is unlucky.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has three agents: the center, the coach, and the general manager. The center knows the coach is watching faceoff outcomes and may infer that a lost draw in a high-leverage moment will reduce trust. The coach knows the GM may value faceoff specialists and may overdeploy them to signal competence. The GM knows the coach may overvalue raw faceoff percentage and may therefore misprice personnel. The paper’s model inserts a counter-belief: value is not the raw win percentage but the incremental expected goals and zone time attributable before a zone change. That counter-belief is the counter-trap.

Crisis habit regression appears when pressure collapses the decision rule back to a salient habit: “win the draw, get possession, keep the puck.” The source’s opening example is exactly this narrative: a game-winning overtime goal off an assist from the victorious faceoff taker, with four goals within 11 seconds of a faceoff victory [PAGE 1]. The source immediately qualifies it with “there is no automatic relationship between faceoffs and goal scoring” [PAGE 1]. The recursive danger is that players and coaches remember the salient win and forget the denominator: many faceoffs produce no attributable expected goal because a zone change or stoppage occurs quickly [PAGE 6]. Crisis habit regression therefore predicts overuse of veteran faceoff specialists in late-game or overtime situations even when the model’s incremental-goal estimate is small.

The counter-trap is model-guided deployment. The source’s actionable note is that “one way to in effect achieve an incremental increase in faceoffs won is for coaches to either play standout faceoff performers more often or specifically substitute them in more frequently during pre-faceoff line changes after stoppages in play” [PAGE 10]. That is a recursive move: the coach uses the model to decide when the faceoff specialist’s incremental value exceeds the cost in offensive quality. The clock matters because the attribution window is not a fixed 20 seconds; it is the time until the next zone change. The source also suggests that “faceoffs could yield a modern reconstruction of power plays” [PAGE 10] and raises the future question whether “faceoff specialists—even faceoff specialists who are less offensively gifted than traditional power play participants—were given power play time” [PAGE 11]. The clock is therefore not just game time; it is the time-to-zone-change and the special-teams clock.

The recursive equilibrium is fragile. If the GM believes the coach will overuse faceoff specialists, the GM may avoid acquiring them. If the coach believes the GM undervalues faceoffs, the coach may hide usage. If the center believes a single loss is career-relevant, the center may optimize win percentage at the expense of post-faceoff positioning. The paper’s evidence supports a narrow counter-trap: acquire or deploy the small minority of centers whose projected NXG is large, but do not infer that every faceoff win has equal value. The source’s conclusion is calibrated: “elite faceoff performers can eclipse over five goals per season in projected effect” [PAGE 12], not that faceoffs dominate championships.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical layer can veto the model if faceoff mechanics change the quality of the post-faceoff sequence in a way not captured by the covariates. The source acknowledges that the prior literature examined “how directionality, clean wins, and player handedness play a significant role in creating value” [PAGE 1]. The current model abstracts from those mechanics and focuses on incremental goals and zone time. That abstraction is valid only if the mechanical variation is either absorbed by prior-year player features or small relative to the zone-change signal. If a player’s handedness, stick position, reaction time, grip strength, or faceoff stance changes the probability of a clean win and the probability of retaining the zone after the win, then the constant conversion rate \(\lambda\) is not structural. A biomechanical veto occurs when the sign or magnitude of \(NXG\) is unstable across mechanical strata, for example strong-side versus weak-side draws or clean versus contested wins.

The physical fatigue and injury layer is also a veto candidate. The model uses prior-year performance statistics [PAGE 6], but a player returning from injury or under a heavy minutes load may have a different faceoff win probability and a different post-faceoff conversion rate. If fatigue changes both \(W_f^{O}\) and \(G_f\), the estimated \(NXG\) is biased by an omitted state variable. A formal veto condition is: let \(B_f\) be the unmeasured biomechanical, fatigue, or ice-state vector. The model estimates \(E[NXG\mid X]\). If \(B_f\) affects both the faceoff outcome and the post-faceoff expected goals, then \(E[NXG\mid X]\) is not equal to \(E[NXG\mid X,B]\). The cross-layer veto holds when this omitted-variable bias changes the ranking of centers or flips the sign of a defensive-zone credit.

The weather and ice layer is an indoor microclimate veto. Hockey is played indoors, but ice temperature, humidity, arena airflow, and puck bounce affect faceoff control and zone entries. The source’s data linkage depends on hand-tracked zone changes [PAGE 5], which are vulnerable to rink-specific tracking and ice-quality variation. If ice quality changes the probability of an immediate zone change, the attribution window changes. The source already notes that many faceoffs produce “a zone change (or play stoppage) that prevents any faceoff-attributable expected goal generation” [PAGE 6]. A weather or ice veto occurs when the distribution of the attribution window shifts systematically across arenas or games, so that the league-average \(\lambda\) is not exchangeable across environments.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore not a rejection of the paper; it is a scope condition. The paper’s result is strongest when the physical and ice layers are approximately stationary, when the zone-change tracking is unbiased, and when the center’s role is stable. The result is weakest when mechanical variation, injury state, or ice microclimate is large relative to the incremental-goal effect. The source itself supplies the guardrail: “there is no automatic relationship between faceoffs and goal scoring” [PAGE 1]. A cross-layer veto is proven when a stratified holdout shows that the model’s NXG ordering is not stable across biomechanical or ice-state strata after controlling for deployment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Immediate-zone-change placebo veto. Counterfactual: take only faceoffs where the next zone change or stoppage occurs almost immediately, so the attribution window is near zero. The paper’s logic implies that the faceoff-attributable expected goal and zone-time increments in this stratum should collapse toward zero. Prediction: on a pre-registered holdout, the empirical \(NXG\) for the immediate-zone-change stratum is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and the model’s predicted \(NXG\) is also near zero. If the model assigns large positive \(NXG\) to faceoffs with no measurable attribution window, the attributability operator is invalid; the veto is triggered. Verifiable by recomputing the model with the zone-change cutoff and comparing it to a fixed-time placebo window.

2. Neutral-zone falsification veto. Counterfactual: score neutral-zone faceoffs as if they were model inputs, even though the paper excludes them. The source says “we omit neutral zone faceoffs from consideration” [PAGE 4] because neutral-zone shots and possession are weak expected-goal channels. Prediction: neutral-zone faceoffs should produce near-zero and unstable NXG estimates; they should not enter the top decile of faceoff value. If a model trained on neutral-zone faceoffs produces large out-of-sample NXG or if neutral-zone NXG correlates strongly with offensive-zone NXG, the model is leaking location information or overfitting. Verifiable by a neutral-zone holdout and a ranking-stability test against the non-neutral-zone model.

3. Linear-usage stress-test veto. Counterfactual: use pre-faceoff substitutions or usage changes to add one non-neutral-zone faceoff win per game for a defined set of centers, then compare the observed seasonal goal change to the Table 2 prediction \(\Delta G_i(1)=82\cdot 1\cdot NXG_i\). Prediction: for small \(m\) and stable deployment, the observed goal delta should lie within the model’s uncertainty interval; if the observed delta is systematically smaller as \(m\) increases, the linear-benefit assumption is false. The source itself warns that “professional NHL centers are almost certainly maximizing their faceoff win percentages already” [PAGE 10] and that Table 2 assumes a linear benefit [PAGE 10]. A verifiable veto occurs if the observed marginal goal return declines with \(m\) while the model predicts constant marginal return. This would not invalidate the per-faceoff sign; it would invalidate the +1 through +6 seasonal extrapolations.
