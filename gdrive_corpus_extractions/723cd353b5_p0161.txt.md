    \]
    where \( I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} \). Breakdown occurs when \( \Delta I \to 0 \) due to saturating channel capacity or when stimulus similarity causes \( p(x|s_1) \approx p(x|s_2) \), making differentiation impossible.

    \[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = D_{\text{KL}}\left( P(\mathbf{S}_{1:T}) \,\|\, \prod_{t=1}^T P(S_t) \right) \quad [\text{nats}]
    \]
    where \( \mathbf{S}_{1:T} \) is the sequence of states along the learned path. A low \( \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} \) indicates strong temporal/structural integration. Singularity arises when the path becomes a rigid, overfitted sequence where \( P(\mathbf{S}_{1:T}) \) collapses to a delta function, losing flexibility.

3.  **Temporary Goal-Characters for Means-Objects [PAGE 1]**: This is a dynamic value assignment. For a means-state \( m \), its temporary goal-character \( V(m) \) is the discounted expected return from that state under the learned policy \( \pi \):
    \[
    V(m) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi} \left[ \sum_{k=0}^\infty \gamma^k R(s_{t+k}) \mid s_t = m \right] \quad [\text{utility units}]
    \]
    Originally \( V(m) = 0 \); learning induces \( V(m) > 0 \). Breakdown: if \( \gamma \to 1 \) and rewards are distant, \( V(m) \) may become unstable or dominated by discounting artifacts.

4.  **Reformation of General Sign-Gestalt-Readinesses [PAGE 1]**: This is a change in the learning algorithm's hyperparameters or prior. Formally, let the posterior over world models be \( P(\theta | \mathcal{D}) \). After maze experience, the "general readiness" shifts to a new prior \( P'(\theta) \) optimized for the class of maze-like tasks. This can be captured by the update:
    \[
    P'(\theta) \propto P(\theta) \exp\left( \lambda \cdot \nabla_\theta \log P(\mathcal{D}_{\text{maze}} | \theta) \right)
    \]
    where \( \lambda \) controls the strength of transfer. Exact singularity: if \( \lambda \) is too large, the prior becomes overly specialized, failing on out-of-distribution tasks (negative transfer).

5.  **Reduction to "Fixations" [PAGE 1]**: Fixation corresponds to convergence to a point attractor in behavior space. The dynamics near a fixation point \( x^* \) are governed by a stable fixed point of the policy gradient flow. The "rank" reduction is the loss of exploratory variance:
    \[
    \sigma^2_{\text{post}} \ll \sigma^2_{\text{pre}} \quad [\text{(action units)}^2]

    where \( \sigma^2 \) is the variance of actions at a given state. Breakdown regime: excessive fixation leads to brittleness; small perturbations \( \delta \) can cause complete failure if \( \|\delta\| > \epsilon_{\text{basin}} \), the radius of the basin of attraction.

6.  **Delayed Reaction Learning [PAGE 1]**: Requires holding an aroused expectation \( h_t \) (a memory trace) over delay \( \Delta t \). The fidelity of retention decays with time due to noise. Modelled by a differential equation for the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR):
    \[
    \frac{d}{dt} \text{SNR}(t) = -\alpha \cdot \text{SNR}(t) + \beta \cdot \text{noise\_power}, \quad \text{SNR}(0) = \text{SNR}_0
    \]
    where \( \alpha \) is the decay rate [\(\text{s}^{-1}\)]. The "condensed sign" is a compressed representation \( z = f(h_t) \) that sustains higher SNR. Singularity: when \( \Delta t > 1/\alpha \), SNR drops below detection threshold, and delayed reaction fails regardless of initial arousal.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cognitive architecture posited by Tolman exists within a multi-layered system where higher-order intentions can be overridden—or vetoed—by lower-level constraints. A robust adjudication protocol must consider three domains:

1.  **Biomechanical/Physical Veto**: An animal may form a perfect sign-gestalt-expectation to turn left at a junction, but a simultaneous muscle cramp or leg injury physically prevents execution. The intention is formed but cannot be enacted. More subtly, the geometry of the body itself imposes a veto: a rat cannot turn sharply enough in a narrow alley to follow its planned path; the plan must be adapted to kinematic constraints. Here, the "veto" is not a conscious decision but a hard physical limitation that forces a rapid replanning cycle.

2.  **Neurochemical State Veto**: Tolman assumes a consistent "readiness," but neurochemistry dynamically modulates it. Consider a rat under high metabolic stress (low glucose). Its dopamine levels drop, reducing the salience of distal goals and weakening the "temporary goal-characters" of means-objects. The integrated map remains intact, but the motivational drive to traverse it is suppressed. The animal may ignore the goal chamber entirely to seek food elsewhere. This is a state-dependent veto where homeostatic priorities override learned purposive behavior.

3.  **Sensory Noise/Weather Veto**: In a naturalistic setting, environmental conditions can corrupt the very signs upon which the gestalt depends. Imagine a rainstorm washing away scent trails used as "condensed signs" for a delayed reaction task. Or fog reducing visual acuity so that bifurcation points are no longer differentiable. The sensory input fails to trigger the appropriate arousal of the stored expectation. The cognitive system holds a valid map, but the interface between world and map is degraded. The behavior becomes aimless wandering because the triggering mechanism has been vetoed by poor signal quality.

Proof of Veto Operation: To demonstrate that a veto occurred, one must show dissociation between cognitive state and behavioral output. For instance, after recovery from neuromuscular blockade (physical veto lifted), the animal should immediately resume correct performance without relearning. Similarly, if deprived animals are satiated (neurochemical veto lifted), goal-directed behavior should return rapidly. These recoveries confirm that the cognitive map was preserved despite being temporarily inaccessible for action.

This multi-layered veto system ensures that learning is not a brittle program but a flexible, context-sensitive process capable of negotiating conflicts between intention, embodiment, and environment.
