The source is a 2022 narrative review and mixed-methods commentary, not a preregistered experimental trial. Its central chain is: high-resolution video and cameras capture an athlete; computer-vision and AI models infer body landmarks; a skeletal or biomechanical model converts landmarks into kinematics; coaches and analysts convert kinematics into training, tactical, officiating, and injury-prevention decisions; and organizations convert those decisions into claimed performance gains. Each hop is an operator with assumptions. The paper states that it “performs methodological research by analyzing published studies, examples from practical applications, cond, acting system comparison methods, and conventional systems” [PAGE 1]. That sentence signals synthesis, not controlled measurement. The results table reports NBA and FIFA percentage changes—“Player Speed Improvement 12.5” and “10.8”; “Injury Reduction 18.3” and “16.7”; “Decision Accuracy Increase 22.1” and “25.4”; “Training Efficiency Increase 22.1” and “28.9” [PAGE 7]—but the paper gives no sample sizes, confidence intervals, injury definitions, exposure denominators, pre-periods, or statistical tests. Therefore the table cannot be treated as a causal effect estimate. It is a reported summary whose denominator and estimand remain undefined.

A second multi-hop problem is construct validity. “Player speed improvement” could mean sprint time, peak velocity, acceleration, or a composite. “Decision accuracy” could mean coach decisions, referee decisions, or model classification. “Training efficiency” is not operationally defined. The source says evaluation centers on “accuracy, reliability, scalability, and usability” [PAGE 7], which are legitimate dimensions, but the reported percentages do not map cleanly onto those four constructs. The paper also claims that “The NBA has adopted markerless motion analytics as their main sports technology” [PAGE 6] and that “FIFA implemented this state-of-the-art system to deliver more precise quantitative data about player acceleration combined with joint angle measurements and gait pattern analytics” [PAGE 7]. Those are institutional-adoption claims; they are not the same as independent scientific validation. The NBA and FIFA examples are case descriptions, not randomized comparisons.

A third gray zone is the source’s own admission of environmental fragility. It says: “Current tracking algorithms experience several performance-limiting problems that stem from unclear lighting as well as blocking between athletes and dissimilarities in their body shapes” [PAGE 6]. It further states: “Systems operating outside under changeable weather combined with diversified lighting conditions and uneven ground surfaces will need continuous calibration because these elements affect system performance” [PAGE 6]. These admissions directly limit generalization from arena or laboratory settings to outdoor, contact, and occluded competitive environments. A system that works in a calibrated basketball arena cannot automatically be assumed to work in rain, fog, glare, crowded scrums, or multi-athlete occlusion. The paper also notes “computational needs and security concerns about collected data” [PAGE 2]. Thus the claim that markerless analytics is universally superior is an overreach unless each deployment passes physical, optical, biomechanical, latency, and privacy vetoes.

The fourth gray zone is biomechanical causality. Markerless systems estimate landmarks and kinematics; they do not directly measure ground reaction force, muscle force, tendon load, or tissue stress. The paper mentions sensor fusion with “force plates, proximity sensors, and vision-based tracking methods” [PAGE 5], which implicitly acknowledges that vision alone is incomplete for force estimation. Yet the results claim “Injury Reduction 18.3” and “16.7” [PAGE 7] without describing the injury model, external load measurement, or control condition. An injury-reduction claim requires exposure-adjusted incidence, case definitions, and a counterfactual. Without those, the correct reading is “markerless analytics may support injury-prevention workflows,” not “markerless analytics reduces injuries by a fixed percentage.” The cross-layer veto logic below makes this explicit.

Finally, the paper’s real-time claim is underspecified. It says the system provides “immediate data acquisition” and “instant access” [PAGE 6], but no end-to-end latency budget is given. Real-time is a clock property: capture plus inference plus transmission plus display plus human interpretation must fit inside the decision window. If the window is shorter than the pipeline latency, the system is not real-time for that decision, even if it is real-time for post-hoc review. The formal quantities in the next section separate these layers so that the source’s claims can be audited rather than accepted as a single blended assertion.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The PDF contains no formal equations of its own; the following are standard formalizations of the operators named or implied by the source. Units are stated explicitly, and each equation has an assumption and a breakdown regime.

1. Camera projection operator. The source says markerless systems “process video captured by one or several cameras directly to find the specific key body points, also known as ‘landmarks’ on athletes” [PAGE 4]. For camera \(c\), joint \(j\), and frame \(i\):
\[
\mathbf{x}_{cij}=\pi\!\left(\mathbf{K}_c[\mathbf{R}_c|\mathbf{t}_c]\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right)+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{cij},
\]
where \(\mathbf{X}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is in metres, \(\mathbf{x}_{cij}\in\mathbb{R}^2\) is in pixels, \(\mathbf{K}_c\) is in pixels, \(\mathbf{R}_c\) is dimensionless, \(\mathbf{t}_c\) is in metres, and \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{cij}\) is pixel error. Assumptions: calibrated intrinsics and extrinsics, rigid camera rig, synchronized capture. Breakdown regimes: \(Z_{ij}\to 0\) near the optical plane; rank deficiency in the camera geometry; rolling-shutter skew; calibration drift; unsynchronized cameras. A single camera cannot recover absolute metric depth for arbitrary poses without priors or known scale.

2. Pose-estimation operator. The source states that “CNNs demonstrate exceptional ability in visual information processing” and that “RNNs analyze time-series data” [PAGE 5]. A generic estimator is
\[
\hat{\mathbf{Y}}_i=f_\theta(\mathbf{I}_i),\qquad \hat{\mathbf{Y}}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times3},
\]
with loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MPJPE}}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{ij}-\mathbf{y}_{ij}\right\|_2,
\]
reported in millimetres when coordinates are in millimetres. Assumptions: labelled training data, camera domain similar to deployment, sufficiently high keypoint confidence, and identifiable anatomy. Breakdown regimes: occlusion, self-occlusion, loose clothing, motion blur, unusual body shape, contact between athletes, domain shift from indoor arena to outdoor field, and label noise in ground truth. The source itself warns of “blocking between athletes and dissimilarities in their body shapes” [PAGE 6].

3. Kinematic operator. Once landmarks are tracked, velocity and acceleration are
\[
\mathbf{v}_j(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}_j}{dt},\qquad
\mathbf{a}_j(t)=\frac{d^2\mathbf{r}_j}{dt^2},
\]
in metres per second and metres per second squared. Joint angle is
\[
\theta_{jk}(t)=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}_{jk}(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}_{jk}(t)}
{\left\|\mathbf{u}_{jk}(t)\right\|\left\|\mathbf{v}_{jk}(t)\right\|}\right),
\]
in radians, with angular velocity \(\omega_{jk}=d\theta_{jk}/dt\) in radians per second. Assumptions: landmarks correspond to anatomical joint centres, frame rate is high enough, and soft-tissue artefact is negligible. Breakdown regimes: temporal aliasing when frame rate is too low; jitter amplification under double differentiation; landmark swaps; missing frames; and occlusion-induced interpolation. Motion blur can be expressed as \(B_{\mathrm{px}}=v\tau_{\exp}/p\), where \(v\) is m/s, \(\tau_{\exp}\) is exposure time in seconds, and \(p\) is metres per pixel; when \(B_{\mathrm{px}}\) exceeds the detector tolerance, the landmark error grows sharply.

4. Inverse-dynamics operator. The source notes sensor fusion with “force plates, proximity sensors, and vision-based tracking methods” [PAGE 5]. The standard rigid-body relation is
\[
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}
+\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}
+\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}},
\]
where \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) is in newton-metres, \(\mathbf{q}\) is in radians, \(\mathbf{M}\) is in kilogram-metre squared, and \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}\) is in newtons. Assumptions: segment masses and inertias are known, external ground reaction forces are measured or modelled, and contact constraints are specified. Breakdown regimes: markerless vision alone does not identify \(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}\); unmeasured contact forces make torque ambiguous; \(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\) can become singular or ill-conditioned near kinematic singularities; and injury-risk claims are not identifiable without tissue-load models. Therefore an “Injury Reduction 18.3” claim [PAGE 7] is not a direct output of markerless kinematics.

5. Sensor-fusion and synchronization operator. With \(M\) sensors, fused state uncertainty can be represented by covariance \(\mathbf{P}\). A simple fusion update is
\[
\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{P}_m^{-1},
\]
with \(\mathbf{P}\) in squared state units. Assumptions: independent, unbiased sensors and known time stamps. Breakdown regimes: correlated errors, unknown latency, clock drift, and time-sync error \(\Delta t\). For acceleration \(a\) and sync error \(\Delta t\), the induced velocity error is approximately \(\Delta v\approx a\Delta t\), in metres per second when \(a\) is m/s\(^2\) and \(\Delta t\) is seconds.

6. Evaluation and statistical operator. The source names “accuracy, reliability, scalability, and usability” [PAGE 7]. Accuracy can be measured by
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i-\mathbf{x}_i\right\|_2^2},
\]
in millimetres. Decision accuracy is
\[
\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},
\]
dimensionless. Injury incidence should be
\[
I=\frac{n_{\mathrm{inj}}}{N_{\mathrm{athletes}}T_{\mathrm{exposure}}},
\]
in injuries per athlete-hour or athlete-season, with a percentage change
\[
\Delta I\%=100\frac{I_0-I_1}{I_0}.
\]
Assumptions: explicit injury case definition, complete exposure capture, and a control or counterfactual. The paper’s table does not provide those inputs, so its percentages are descriptive claims, not audited incidence estimates.

7. Information, compute, and latency operator. Let \(J\) be keypoints, \(f_s\) frames per second, and \(b\) bits per coordinate. A raw data-rate bound is
\[
R_b=f_sJb,
\]
in bits per second, with volume \(V=R_bT\) in bits. End-to-end latency is
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{end}}=\tau_{\mathrm{capture}}+\tau_{\mathrm{inference}}
+\tau_{\mathrm{transmit}}+\tau_{\mathrm{display}}+\tau_{\mathrm{human}},
\]
in seconds. Assumptions: independent coordinate coding, fixed network throughput, and fixed model runtime. Breakdown regimes: battery or thermal throttling, network congestion, queued inference, and human review time. The source’s “real-time” language [PAGE 6] is valid only when \(\tau_{\mathrm{end}}\) is below the relevant decision window.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs from manual photography to high-speed video, marker-based optoelectronics, wearable inertial units, computer-vision tracking, deep-learning pose estimation, and finally markerless institutional deployment. The source itself says: “Researchers first introduced basic photography methods during the pioneering steps of this field for manually analyzing movement sequences” [PAGE 3], and “High-speed cameras and specialized software enabled researchers and coaches to evaluate detailed aspects of an athlete’s gait motions, joint angles, and velocity measurements” [PAGE 3]. It then describes traditional tracking as requiring “specialized hardware equipment such as markers and wearable sensors” [PAGE 4]. The markerless turn is presented as a response to calibration burden, cost, and movement interference: “The search for less burdensome motion capture solutions with improved scalability and user-intuitive characteristics by practitioners led to the development of new markerless technological systems” [PAGE 4].

The retrocausal genealogy is the story the paper tells backwards from 2022 adoption to 2015 experimentation to 2018 computer-vision feasibility to 2020 AI pose estimation. The paper claims 2015 marker-based systems “owned the industry landscape,” 2018 brought early markerless tracking through computer vision and deep learning, 2020 brought “major advancements in artificial intelligence pose estimation technology,” and 2022 brought FIFA World Cup and NBA deployment [PAGE 9]. That periodization is a narrative reconstruction, not a causal identification strategy. It makes the present look inevitable. A more defensible genealogy would distinguish four separate causal claims: technical feasibility, measurement validity, institutional adoption, and outcome improvement. The source frequently collapses them. For example, “The NBA has adopted markerless motion analytics as their main sports technology” [PAGE 6] establishes adoption, not that the technology caused a measured speed or injury effect. “FIFA implemented this state-of-the-art system” [PAGE 7] establishes a deployment, not a controlled trial. The claim that “The technology will revolutionize sports performance training worldwide because of its maturing capabilities” [PAGE 5] is a projection from adoption and capability, not an estimated causal effect.

The multi-week traps are therefore traps of inference rather than traps of arithmetic. Trap one: treating a review as an empirical study. The paper’s own phrase “methodological research by analyzing published studies” [PAGE 1] should trigger a lower evidence tier than a prospective trial. Trap two: treating Table 1’s “Player Speed Improvement 12.5” and “Injury Reduction 18.3” [PAGE 7] as effect sizes without denominators, confidence intervals, or case definitions. Trap three: assuming that NBA and FIFA adoption proves generalizability to youth, amateur, outdoor, contact, and low-budget settings. Trap four: ignoring the paper’s own warning that “Current tracking algorithms experience several performance-limiting problems” under unclear lighting, blocking, and body-shape differences [PAGE 6]. Trap five: assuming markerless means sensor-free. The paper explicitly mentions fusion with “force plates, proximity sensors, and vision-based tracking methods” [PAGE 5]. Trap six: assuming single-camera 3D is metric. Trap seven: assuming that model accuracy on a curated dataset transfers to live competition with occlusion and weather. Trap eight: treating “decision accuracy” as referee accuracy when it may mean coach decision support or model classification. Trap nine: ignoring privacy and data governance; the paper itself flags “data security and privacy challenges because biometric data has become an attractive asset” [PAGE 11]. A multi-week evaluation should therefore audit data provenance, calibration logs, occlusion rates, latency logs, and outcome definitions before accepting any headline percentage.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression pathway is: an injury crisis, a disputed officiating call, or a losing streak creates pressure for immediate certainty; staff revert to familiar marker-based laboratories, manual video review, or coach intuition because those methods are socially legible even when they are slower. The source names this resistance directly: “Traditional methods that coaches and support staff use resist major changes, which promise more efficient insights even when these changes would lead to better results” [PAGE 6]. That is a theory-of-mind statement about institutions: coaches do not merely evaluate accuracy; they evaluate whether a new system threatens status, workflow, and accountability. Under crisis, the familiar habit wins unless the new system has trusted interfaces and “authentic reports proving system benefits in practical applications” [PAGE 6].

The counter-trap is overcorrection: after a crisis, an organization adopts an AI dashboard as an oracle and treats its landmark estimates as ground truth. Recursive theory of mind then runs through at least four levels. Level 1: the athlete believes that if the camera sees the body, the measurement is objective. Level 2: the coach believes the athlete may alter movement when aware of tracking, so the coach wants “invisible” measurement. Level 3: the analyst believes the coach will trust a clean dashboard, so the analyst suppresses uncertainty and occlusion flags. Level 4: the organization believes stakeholders will accept adoption because NBA or FIFA has adopted it, so the organization skips local validation. The source’s claim that markerless systems provide “immediate data acquisition” and “instant access” [PAGE 6] can intensify this counter-trap because immediacy feels like validity. The recursive failure is not that anyone is irrational; it is that each layer assumes the layer below has already validated the measurement.

The clock is the third pathway. A coach under time pressure does not have the luxury of a long calibration debate. If the decision window is seconds, the relevant question is \(\tau_{\mathrm{end}}\) versus \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\). If end-to-end latency exceeds the window, the system is only a post-hoc review tool, however accurate it is. The source’s real-time claims therefore need a clock audit: capture rate, inference time, network transmission, display, and human interpretation. The correct protocol is not “trust the dashboard” or “reject the dashboard,” but “state the decision, state the latency budget, and veto the system for that decision if the budget is exceeded.” Crisis habit regression predicts fallback to manual methods; the counter-trap predicts blind adoption; the clock predicts that neither habit is a substitute for a latency-and-validity test.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and optical veto. If keypoint confidence \(c_j(t)\) falls below a prespecified threshold \(c_{\min}\), or if the fraction of occluded frames exceeds \(f_{\mathrm{occ}}\), then the pose stream is vetoed for that joint and downstream metrics are not valid. The source supports this veto: “Current tracking algorithms experience several performance-limiting problems that stem from unclear lighting as well as blocking between athletes and dissimilarities in their body shapes” [PAGE 6]. The same source admits that “objects obstructing vision, subpar illumination, and unanticipated player interactions degrade measuring precision” [PAGE 11]. Therefore any claim of uniform accuracy across contact sports is vetoed unless occlusion-stratified error is reported.

Weather and calibration veto. Outdoor rain, fog, glare, and uneven ground alter image contrast and camera geometry. The source states: “Systems operating outside under changeable weather combined with diversified lighting conditions and uneven ground surfaces will need continuous calibration because these elements affect system performance” [PAGE 6]. Formal veto: if \(\mathrm{SNR}(t)\) falls below \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\), or if calibration residual \(r_{\mathrm{cal}}\) exceeds \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{cal}}\), the tracking stream is invalid. The burden is on the deployer to show that calibration is maintained through the weather envelope, not merely that the system worked in a dome or arena.

Biomechanical veto. Markerless vision estimates kinematics, not muscle force, tendon load, or ground reaction force. The paper’s mention of “force plates, proximity sensors, and vision-based tracking methods” [PAGE 5] is evidence that force measurement is a separate layer. Therefore an injury-reduction claim is vetoed unless the study reports exposure-adjusted injury incidence, a control condition, and a specified mechanism linking kinematics to tissue load. Table 1’s “Injury Reduction 18.3” and “16.7” [PAGE 7] cannot survive this veto as causal estimates because the paper provides no incidence denominator, no injury case definition, and no counterfactual. A biomechanical veto is not a rejection of the technology; it is a rejection of an unidentifiable causal claim.

Clock and real-time veto. If \(\tau_{\mathrm{end}}>\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\), the system cannot be used for that in-game decision. The source’s “real-time” and “instant access” language [PAGE 6] is a capability claim, not a measured latency claim. A real-time veto requires a timestamped pipeline log. Without it, the correct classification is “near-real-time or post-hoc,” not “real-time.”

Privacy and governance veto. The source warns of “data security and privacy challenges because biometric data has become an attractive asset” [PAGE 11]. A deployment without consent, retention limits, access control, and a data-protection impact assessment is vetoed at the governance layer, regardless of model accuracy. The paper recommends collaboration with technology firms to “implement data protection solutions that will protect athletes’ privacy during biometric operations” [PAGE 12]. That recommendation is itself an admission that privacy is not solved by the pose estimator.

The cross-layer proof is therefore a chain of necessary conditions: valid projection, valid pose, valid kinematics, valid dynamics or explicit non-causal use, valid latency, and valid governance. Failure at any layer vetoes the downstream claim. The source’s own admissions about occlusion, weather, calibration, compute, and privacy supply the premises for that proof.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a deployment policy receives a binary or bounded reward only when a preregistered, externally checkable condition passes. The three scenarios below are counterfactual tests that can falsify the source’s strong claims.

Scenario 1: Occlusion counterfactual. Preregister a test set with 20–40 percent of frames containing lower-limb occlusion, athlete-athlete overlap, or clothing-induced landmark loss. Ground truth is independent marker-based or manually adjudicated 3D pose. Reward \(r_1=1\) only if MPJPE stays below a prespecified threshold, for example 50 mm, and keypoint confidence is calibrated. Prediction: the source’s uniform-accuracy implication fails under occlusion, because the paper itself warns of “blocking between athletes” [PAGE 6]. A verified failure vetoes any claim that the same model is equally valid in open play and in a controlled drill.

Scenario 2: Weather and calibration counterfactual. Preregister outdoor sessions in rain, fog, low sun, and uneven ground, with periodic calibration checks. Reward \(r_2=1\) only if \(\mathrm{SNR}(t)\ge \mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\) and calibration residual \(r_{\mathrm{cal}}\le\epsilon_{\mathrm{cal}}\) for the full session, and only if landmark error remains within the same tolerance as the indoor baseline. Prediction: the source’s outdoor generalization claim fails or requires continuous recalibration, because it states that “changeable weather combined with diversified lighting conditions and uneven ground surfaces will need continuous calibration” [PAGE 6]. A verified failure vetoes “deploy anywhere” language.

Scenario 3: Causal injury and performance counterfactual. Preregister a controlled comparison with intervention and control groups, fixed exposure windows, explicit injury case definitions, and outcomes reported as injuries per 1000 athlete-hours plus sprint or acceleration metrics. Reward \(r_3=1\) only if the intervention shows a statistically and practically significant improvement over control with confidence intervals excluding no effect, after adjusting for exposure and baseline risk. Prediction: the table’s “Injury Reduction 18.3” and “16.7” [PAGE 7] will not replicate as causal effects without denominators and controls; the likely result is a smaller or null effect, or an effect conditional on coaching adherence. A verified null or inconclusive result vetoes the paper’s strongest causal interpretation while preserving the weaker claim that markerless analytics can support workflow and measurement.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
