Source-scope: arXiv:2004.10142v1 [cs.SI], 21 Apr 2020, four pages. The paper asks two linked questions: “How to measure a user’s presidential candidate preference accurately when the social media posts or public opinions of a user are not known” and what behavioral patterns NBA and NFL fans show toward presidential candidates [PAGE 1]. The derivation is multi-hop rather than a single correlation. First, the authors collect three follower-ID sets: candidate followers, team followers, and senator followers. Second, they analyze raw candidate-following ratios by sport and state, finding that “the majority of the users are following Trump” among NBA and NFL followers who also follow candidates [PAGE 2]. Third, they note a contradiction: raw Trump following is high, but “the Republican senators’ following ratio is much lower than expected among the users with strong political interest” [PAGE 3]. Fourth, they propose “a new metric, Congressional Devotedness Score, to model candidate preferences more accurately” [PAGE 1]. Fifth, they convert user-level CDS into Congressional Devotedness Ratio (CDR), “simply the weight of a candidate with respect to the sum of CDS of all candidates” [PAGE 4]. Sixth, they compare sport-level, state-level, and team-level CDRs. The result is a correction: at sport-level, “Trump’s CDRs are the lowest while the CDRs for Biden are the highest” [PAGE 4], and “NBA also has a lower Trump CDR compared to NFL” [PAGE 4].

The gray-zone audit matters. The paper does not measure votes, donations, turnout, or textual sentiment; it measures follower graphs. The senator-following filter is a proxy for “strong interest in politics” [PAGE 3], not a validated measure of ideology. The CDS weights party following by \(\alpha_i/(\alpha_i+\beta_i)\) or \(\beta_i/(\alpha_i+\beta_i)\), where \(\alpha_i\) and \(\beta_i\) are counts of Democrat and Republican senators followed [PAGE 3]. That weighting saturates at 0 or 1 for single-party followers and gives 0.5 to balanced followers; it does not distinguish a user who follows one senator from one who follows twenty. The Devotedness Score splits candidate support equally among followed candidates, so a user following Trump and Biden receives \(1/2\) for each [PAGE 3]. The paper restricts CDS to users who “follow at least one senator and one candidate” [PAGE 4], which removes the zero-denominator cases but also changes the population. There is an internal data-count inconsistency: page 2 says “15 million users following the 14 selected NFL teams,” but the same page later says “12 million unique users for 15 selected NFL teams” [PAGE 2]. Table I lists 15 NFL teams. This does not invalidate the CDR rankings, but it is a reproducibility flag. There is also a proxy ambiguity: the paper counts “53 Republicans, 45 Democrats, and 2 Independents who both caucus with Democrats” [PAGE 2], yet Eq. (1) defines only Democrat and Republican weights; the treatment of the two Independents is unspecified. Finally, the paper itself warns that “a proportion of Trump followers are fake and random followers” [PAGE 3], so raw follower counts are not a clean preference signal. The strongest admissible claim is therefore descriptive and proxy-based: among senator-following team followers in April 2020, CDR ordering differs from raw following ratios. The paper does not establish causal effects of sport fandom on politics, nor political effects on athletic performance.

The retrocausal trap is the use of later political events to validate an earlier snapshot. The data are from April 2020, but the paper says “Biden’s CDRs are generally higher than those of Sanders,” and calls this “consistent with the Super Tuesday outcome, where Biden won 10 out of 14 states” [PAGE 4]. Super Tuesday occurred on 3 March 2020, before the April data collection. That is retrodiction, not prospective prediction: the April follower graph already contains the post-Super-Tuesday state of the campaign. A second temporal trap is Sanders’s later dropout: the paper says the dropout occurred “after our study has been conducted” [PAGE 2], so the CDR does not measure general-election behavior or post-dropout follower migration. A third trap is incumbent status and follower inflation: Trump’s raw follower count is “76.9 million” [PAGE 2], but the paper warns that incumbent status and fake/random followers can inflate it [PAGE 3]. A fourth trap is platform churn: follower IDs are volatile under API changes, account suspensions, and bot purges; the April 2020 graph is not a stable political variable. A fifth trap is geographic lineage: the paper explains Rockets-versus-Spurs differences by fan-base geography, quoting that “the followers of Rockets are mainly scattered around Houston and Eastern Texas whereas the followers of Spurs are mainly located around San Antonio and in Southern Texas” [PAGE 4]. That explanation is plausible but not causal; it is a retrodicted overlay with past election results, not a panel design. Multi-week traps therefore include: treating April CDR as a forecast of November; treating Super Tuesday consistency as validation; ignoring Sanders’s dropout; ignoring incumbent and fake-follower inflation; and treating team geography as an exogenous cause rather than a correlate of demographics, migration, and media markets.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. In an election-season “crisis” frame, users may regress to habitual identity signals: following a party’s senators and a preferred candidate becomes a low-cost expression of self. The paper’s design assumes this: senator-following is used as an indicator of “strong interest in politics” [PAGE 3], and candidate-following is treated as support. The recursive model is: user \(i\) models the political field as party + candidate; the analyst models user \(i\) as a party-weighted candidate follower; the team is modeled as an aggregate of users; the state is modeled as an aggregate of teams. The paper’s sport-level finding that “a large proportion of NBA and NFL fans with strong political interests lean towards Democrats” [PAGE 4] is a statement about that layered model, not about individual votes.

Counter-trap. The obvious counter-trap is overcorrection. Raw following shows Trump dominant, so the analyst may infer that NBA and NFL fans are pro-Trump. The paper counters by filtering to senator followers and computing CDS. But a second counter-trap appears: the analyst may then treat CDS as revealed preference and conclude that fans are pro-Biden because “Trump’s CDRs are the lowest while the CDRs for Biden are the highest” [PAGE 4]. That overreads the proxy. CDS measures party-weighted candidate-following among users who follow at least one senator and one candidate; it excludes casual followers, swing voters, and users who follow Trump but no senators. The paper itself says candidate-following alone “does not exclude the swing voters and casual followers” [PAGE 1]. A third counter-trap is team-level essentialism: treating “Rockets fans” or “Spurs fans” as homogeneous. The paper warns that same-state teams differ because of geography/demographics [PAGE 4], so the agent model must include location, not just team logo.

Clock. The clock is April 2020: Biden is consolidating, Sanders has not yet dropped out in the data window, and Trump is the incumbent. The paper’s own clock statement is: “Bernie Sanders has dropped out of the race after our study has been conducted, but it does not affect the users’ behaviors in the time period that our paper primarily focuses on” [PAGE 2]. That is a bounded clock claim. Any recursive theory-of-mind inference must be time-indexed: \( \text{CDR}_j(t) \) for \(t\approx\) April 2020, not \(t=\) November 2020. The clock also affects the analyst’s model of the candidate: Trump’s incumbent status changes follower acquisition, Biden’s consolidation changes attention, and Sanders’s exit changes follower retention. The safest recursive reading is bounded: fans use follows to signal identity; the analyst uses senator follows to infer political engagement; the metric is a snapshot of that signaling, not a fixed preference.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
