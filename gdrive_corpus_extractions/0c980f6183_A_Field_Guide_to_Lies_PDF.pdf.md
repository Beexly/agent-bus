Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with predictions. RLVR denotes verifiable counterfactual validation layers.

Scenario 1 — Truncated-axis magnitude claim.
Veto rule: If a graph’s y-axis domain is $[y_{\min}, y_{\max}]$ with $y_{\min} > 0$ and the truncation is not disclosed, reject all trend-magnitude inferences. Counterfactual: re-render the same data on a zero-baseline axis $[0, y_{\max}]$.
Prediction: The perceived effect size contracts by factor $R = \frac{y_{\max}}{y_{\max} - y_{\min}}$; if the re-rendered difference falls below the decision threshold, the original policy recommendation is reversed. This is directly grounded in [PAGE 15]: “A truncated y-axis, which does not start at zero, can make minor differences appear more significant than they are.”

Scenario 2 — Base-rate neglect alarm.
Veto rule: If a report presents $P(E \mid H)$ (e.g., a test’s sensitivity) without the prior $P(H)$ (base rate), reject the posterior alarm $P(H \mid E)$. Counterfactual: compute the Bayesian posterior using the true base rate.
Prediction: Even with high $P(E \mid H)$, a low $P(H)$ yields $P(H \mid E) < 0.5$; the recommended intervention (e.g., treatment or regulatory action) is predicted to be unwarranted. Grounded in [PAGE 12]: “the base rate fallacy, emphasizing the importance of considering the underlying probabilities rather than getting swayed by striking statistical occurrences that lack contextual grounding.”

Scenario 3 — Correlation-as-causation policy.
Veto rule: If $\rho_{XY} \neq 0$ but there is no temporal precedence, no identified mechanism, and no control for confounders $Z$, reject the causal policy “intervene on $X$ to change $Y$.” Counterfactual: run a controlled intervention or natural experiment holding $Z$ constant.
Prediction: The intervention on $X$ produces no statistically detectable change in $Y$, falsifying the causal claim. Grounded in [PAGE 11-12]: correlation does not imply causation, and readers must “look deeper into the potential factors influencing the data.”
