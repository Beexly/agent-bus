<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Pitchers whose pre-2024 behavior already tracked the ABS target—right-handed and side-arm pitchers who had been raising pitches since 2021–2023—exhibit little acceleration because they are regressing toward an existing habit, not responding to the machine [PAGE 25, Table A11]. Counter-trap: The league’s choice of ABS geometry is the actual treatment. Scaling the zone with registered height lifts the top for tall batters (to ~110 cm for the tallest group vs ~102–105 cm under human absolute-height anchoring) [PAGE 19]. Defining the strike as “some part of the ball touching the zone” rather than the whole ball inside adds two percentage points of agreement; plane choice (middle alone vs middle and back) matters little [PAGE 11, Table A1]. Widening the plate by 2 cm per side adds two percentage points [PAGE 11]. These are design parameters with distributional consequences: they shift strikes toward tall batters at the top and equalize called-strike rates across handedness matchups at the edges [PAGE 20]. A league adopting a challenge system (as MLB does since 2026) still chooses the geometry that defines what can be overturned [PAGE 30].

Clock: Adjustment is modeled as partial adjustment with convex costs, λ governing speed toward the target; any λ including near-one is consistent with the implications [PAGE 7]. Empirically, the response is concentrated in the first ABS season (2024), close to zero in 2025, and slightly negative in 2026—overshooting followed by correction [PAGE 24]. Batters face a real-time decision clock: swing decisions occur in a fraction of a second, and the gain scales with individual exposure E, which H3 implies is small (<1 extra strike per 23 PA) [PAGE 7, 20]. Pitchers set plans ex ante applying to every batter, so their gain scales with the league-wide zone shift [PAGE 7]. Recursive theory of mind: pitchers anticipate that batters will not re-optimize swing behavior (because batters’ own exposure is tiny and decision costs are real-time), so pitchers reallocate toward high locations without fearing batter counter-adjustment; batters observe pitchers throwing higher but do not adjust swings because the payoff to any single batter is below the fixed re-optimization cost [PAGE 7, 30]. The observed asymmetry—pitchers adapt, batters do not—is the equilibrium outcome of this cost structure.
