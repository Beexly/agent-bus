The reasoning chain must therefore be multi-hop. First, the physical object must be represented by balance laws: electrical circuit balance, electromagnetic induction, force/torque production, and rotational mechanics [PAGE 19–20]. Second, those laws must be reduced to a tractable state-space model. The source explicitly warns that physical equations with different dimensions cannot be freely summed: “the second equation has the dimension rad/s, and the third – A/s” [PAGE 26]. A major gray-zone issue is the meaning of “energy-efficient.” The source states: “Electrical energy savings can be guaranteed by using energy-efficient laws of optimal control for electromechanical systems” [PAGE 8]. However, much of the mathematical apparatus is normalized and control-effort oriented. It then argues that high-order sliding modes can eliminate overvoltages and improve energy performance by limiting supply voltage at intermediate levels [PAGE 8]. The most important formal subtlety is the transition from ideal sliding to real sliding. <CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof: an ideal sliding-mode controller cannot be accepted as an energy-efficient hardware design if it requires zero boundary-layer thickness and infinite switching frequency. The source states that real sliding becomes ideal only as the switching-imperfection parameter \(\Delta\) approaches zero, while amplitude and frequency approach their limiting behavior [PAGE 163]. Formally, if \(\Delta\to 0\), then:
\[
A(\Delta)\to 0,
\]
and the estimated switching frequency satisfies:
\[
f \le \frac{\|f\|}{2\pi A(\Delta)}.
\]
If \(A(\Delta)\to 0\), the upper bound diverges. In a semiconductor converter, each switching event dissipates a nonzero energy \(E_{sw}\). Therefore, the switching-loss power is:
\[
P_{sw} = E_{sw} f.
\]
If \(f\to\infty\), then \(P_{sw}\to\infty\), violating any finite thermal limit. This is a hard physical veto against literal implementation of ideal sliding.

A second physical veto comes from discontinuous control and parasitic inductance. The source explicitly warns that discontinuous controls cause overvoltages on converter switches [PAGE 8]. In a real circuit, parasitic inductance \(L_p\) produces voltage spikes:
\[
v_{\text{spike}} = L_p \frac{di}{dt}.
\]
If the controller commands abrupt current changes, \(di/dt\) can become large enough that:
\[
v_{\text{bus}} + v_{\text{spike}} \gt V_{\text{switch,max}}.
\]
This destroys the switch or forces protective shutdown. Therefore, a relay or discontinuous controller is vetoed unless the implementation includes snubbers, controlled gate drivers, finite switching frequency, or a boundary layer that limits \(di/dt\).

A third veto comes from the symmetry principle’s unlimited-gain limit. The source says that ideal inertialess reproduction would require “an energy source of unlimited power, which is physically impossible” [PAGE 116]. Therefore, any design that relies on exact pole-zero cancellation of unstable or lightly damped modes is vetoed. The modified symmetry principle’s saturation and integral action are not optional refinements; they are physical necessities.

Biomechanical veto: if the electromechanical system interacts with a human operator, wearable actuator, exoskeleton, or manual override, the controller must respect human reaction time and force limits. Human neuromuscular bandwidth is finite, typically far below high-frequency sliding dynamics. If the control law demands correction faster than the human can perceive or tolerate, the system may induce instability, discomfort, or injury. Therefore, a high-order sliding controller that is mathematically stable on a bench may be vetoed in a human-in-the-loop setting unless the sliding frequency and force ripple are reduced below biomechanical safety thresholds.

Weather/environmental veto: outdoor or industrial electromechanical systems experience temperature, humidity, and load variation. Winding resistance varies with temperature:
\[
R(T) = R_0\left[1+\kappa(T-T_0)\right].
\]
The source includes heat conduction and heat balance equations for windings [PAGE 23], indicating that thermal state is part of the plant. If the controller is synthesized at nominal temperature but deployed across a wide temperature range, the electrical time constant:
\[
T_e = \frac{L}{R(T)}
\]
and torque production can drift. A controller whose stability depends on exact parameter values is vetoed unless robust or adaptive margins are proven.

Mechanical veto: if the plant is multi-mass, a one-mass controller is unsafe. The source includes two-mass equations with elasticity and damping [PAGE 21–22]. For an undamped two-mass system, the natural frequency is:
\[
\omega_n = \sqrt{c\left(\frac{1}{J_1}+\frac{1}{J_2}\right)}.
\]
If feedback excites this mode, torsional oscillations can damage shafts, couplings, or gearboxes. Therefore, a controller is vetoed if it ignores the first significant elastic mode.

The cross-layer adjudication rule is therefore: accept only if all of the following hold:
\[
V_{\text{peak}} \le V_{\text{max}},
\]
\[
I_{\text{rms}} \le I_{\text{max}},
\]
\[
T_{\text{junction}} \le T_{\text{max}},
\]
\[
f_{\text{sw}} \le f_{\text{max}},
\]
\[
\text{phase margin and gain margin remain positive under parameter drift,}
\]
and the mechanical mode is either damped or not excited. If any condition fails, the design is vetoed regardless of formal optimality in the abstract functional.

The following three RLVR-style counterfactual vetoes are verifiable because they specify observable measurements, failure thresholds, and model-based predictions.

1. Counterfactual: replace the proposed nonlinear/high-order controller with a pure relay controller to save development time.  
Prediction: the closed-loop system will exhibit finite-amplitude, finite-frequency sliding with switch voltage spikes. The source warns that discontinuous controls cause “significant overvoltages on the power switches of the converter during switching” [PAGE 8].  
Verification: measure switch drain-source voltage \(V_{DS}\), current derivative \(di/dt\), switching frequency \(f_{sw}\), and case temperature.  
Veto condition: if:
\[
\max(V_{DS}) \gt V_{DS,\text{max}},
\]
or if EMI exceeds the applicable standard, or if thermal rise exceeds the converter’s rated limit, the relay fallback is vetoed.  
Formal expectation: the relay controller may stabilize the origin in a Filippov sense [PAGE 153–154], but it is not acceptable as an energy-efficient hardware solution if switching stress exceeds device ratings.

2. Counterfactual: set the irrational activation exponent \(\alpha\) very close to zero without regularization.  
Prediction: the control law approaches relay-like behavior, but the derivative of the activation function becomes highly singular near the origin. The source notes that irrational activation functions require careful treatment because their derivatives are discontinuous and may lead to fractional-order sliding interpretations [PAGE 184–185].  
Verification: inject realistic sensor noise into \(\eta\), then measure the variance of the control signal, the variance of the switching function derivative, and the steady-state jitter of the output.  
Veto condition: if:
\[
\operatorname{Var}(U) \to \infty
\]
as \(\eta\to 0\), or if the actuator saturates due to noise amplification, the design is vetoed.  
Formal prediction: for:
\[
\Phi(\eta,\alpha)=|\eta|^{\alpha}\operatorname{sign}(\eta),
\]
the derivative contains a term proportional to:
\[
|\eta|^{\alpha-1}.
\]
If \(\alpha \lt 1\), this term becomes unbounded near \(\eta=0\). Therefore, a controller using direct differentiation of \(\Phi\) without filtering or boundary-layer approximation is expected to fail under noise.

3. Counterfactual: synthesize the controller using a one-mass model while the physical drive is a two-mass elastic system.  
Prediction: the controller will miss a torsional mode and may excite it. The source includes the two-mass equations with elasticity and dissipation [PAGE 21–22]. For the undamped approximation, the natural frequency is:
\[
\omega_n = \sqrt{c\left(\frac{1}{J_1}+\frac{1}{J_2}\right)}.
\]
Verification: perform a frequency-response test on the mechanical drivetrain, measure speed oscillation at \(\omega_n\), and compare the closed-loop phase margin with and without the elastic model.  
Veto condition: if the measured oscillation amplitude at \(\omega_n\) exceeds the mechanical tolerance, or if the phase margin collapses below the required robustness threshold, the one-mass-based controller is vetoed.  
Formal expectation: the neglected mode appears as an unmodeled pole pair. If the controller’s gain at \(\omega_n\) is high, the closed loop can become unstable even if the reduced-order model is stable.

These vetoes are not betting conditions; they are falsifiable engineering tests. They translate the monograph’s formal operators into measurable hardware outcomes: voltage stress, switching frequency, control variance, noise sensitivity, thermal rise, and torsional vibration. A controller passes only if it satisfies the physical inequalities above and preserves the formal stability conditions of the source: controllability or stabilizability [PAGE 32–43], valid relative degree for feedback linearization [PAGE 70], sliding existence and hitting conditions [PAGE 151–152], and Hurwitz positivity for the variable-gain characteristic equation [PAGE 243–247].
