The quadratic cost hop is \(c_{ij}=\sum_t\sum_{d_i}\sum_{d_j} x_{id_it} q_{d_id_j} x_{jd_jt}\), written in matrix form as \(c_{ij}=\mathrm{vec}(X_i)^T Q_{ij}\mathrm{vec}(X_j)\) [PAGE 4]. Stacking the frames gives \(z=[\mathrm{vec}(X_1)^T,\ldots,\mathrm{vec}(X_F)^T]^T\) and \(c=z^T Q z\) [PAGE 4]. The QUBO hop uses \(Q'=Q+\lambda G^T G\) and \(b'=-2\lambda G^T b\) [PAGE 4], then substitutes \(z=\frac12(s+1)\) to obtain spin variables with \(b'^T=2(b^T+1^T Q)\) [PAGE 5]. The hardware hop is the Ising Hamiltonian \(\hat H_{\mathrm{ising}}=\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j+\sum_i h_i\sigma_i\), with \(\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}\) [PAGE 3]. The remaining hops are experimental: annealing time \(T\) and repeated measurements [PAGE 3], optimized multipliers \(\lambda_i=\lambda_b+\lambda'_i+\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\) [PAGE 5], and post-processing by overlap matching [PAGE 13].

The gray-zone audit is where the paper must be read carefully. The paper says “In this work we present the ﬁrst quantum computing approach to MOT” [PAGE 2], but it does not demonstrate a general quantum speedup. Its AQC claim is bounded by the hardware: “The system contains at least 5000 qubits and 35,000 couplers implemented as superconducting qubits [9] and Josephson-junctions [26] respectively” [PAGE 6], but “Every qubit of the D-wave Advantage is connected to 15 other qubits” [PAGE 6], and “The actual parameters can vary due to defective qubits and couplers” [PAGE 6]. The paper’s real quantum experiment is small: 3 detections over 4 frames with noise levels \(\sigma\in\{0.0,0.1,0.2\}\) [PAGE 7], and a PETS09-S2L1 excerpt with “three tracks that contain two occlusions” between frames 121 to 155 [PAGE 8]. The reported solution probabilities are tiny: “When optimally tuned for σ = 0, our method returns the best solution in 4.8% of the measurements, compared to 3.5% when using a fixed multiplier” [PAGE 7], and “even without an additional offset λoff = 0, the best solution is returned in 0.8% of the measurements” [PAGE 7]. Thus, a measurement is not a proof of optimality; it is a sample from a noisy energy distribution. The “solution probability” is a Monte Carlo statistic, not a correctness certificate.

The spectral-gap audit is also central. The source states: “The condition for a sufficiently slow evolution depends mostly on two factors, the temperature of the environment and the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian, i.e. the difference between lowest and second-lowest energy level or eigenvalue” [PAGE 3]. Therefore, if the gap shrinks, the required annealing time grows, and noise excites higher energy states. The paper observes exactly this: “Solution probabilities are lower compared to simulated annealing and high energy solutions are returned more often. This can be explained by the high noise of current AQCs” [PAGE 7]. The Lagrangian audit is another gray zone: the penalty must be large enough to enforce constraints but small enough to preserve conditioning. The paper warns: “with a high λ the conditioning of the corresponding Hamiltonian in the AQC gets worse” [PAGE 5]. The heuristic \(\lambda_i=\lambda_b+\lambda'_i+\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\) is empirically motivated, not a theorem [PAGE 5]. The classical-solver audit is similarly bounded: “As binary quadratic problems are very hard to solve with classical approaches, it is not possible to find an optimum solution for segments that contain a high number of tracks” [PAGE 7], so “we terminate the optimization after 900 s on a single segment and use the best solution found” [PAGE 8]. The Hessian regularization is the one place with a formal invariance proof: adding diagonal blocks \(E_{ii}\) changes the per-frame block cost by exactly \(e(T-1)\), under the binary and equality constraints [PAGE 6; PAGE 13]. That proof does not imply that the QUBO is easy; it only preserves the minimizer for classical branch-and-bound relaxations. The correct reading is therefore: a new formulation, competitive classical results on MOT15, and a small-scale AQC feasibility demonstration, not a scalable quantum advantage claim.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal objects and units. Let \(D\) be detections including one dummy, \(T\) tracks including one dummy, and \(F\) frames. Let \(X_f\in\{0,1\}^{D\times T}\) be the binary assignment matrix for frame \(f\); each entry \(x_{dt}\) is a bit. The equality constraints are
\[
\sum_{d=1}^{D} x_{dt}=1 \quad \forall t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\},
\]
and
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{T} x_{dt}=1 \quad \forall d\in\{1,\ldots,D-1\},
\]
with the dummy row \(D\) and dummy column \(T\) exempted [PAGE 4]. The pairwise frame cost is
\[
c_{ij}=\sum_{t}\sum_{d_i}\sum_{d_j}x_{id_it}q_{d_id_j}x_{jd_jt}
=\mathrm{vec}(X_i)^T Q_{ij}\mathrm{vec}(X_j),
\]
and the stacked vector \(z=[\mathrm{vec}(X_1)^T,\ldots,\mathrm{vec}(X_F)^T]^T\) gives \(c=z^T Q z\) [PAGE 4]. The QUBO form is
\[
\arg\min_z z^T Q z+b^T z,
\]
with
\[
Q'=Q+\lambda G^T G,\qquad b'=-2\lambda G^T b,
\]
for equality constraints \(Gz=d\) [PAGE 4]. Substituting \(z=\frac12(s+1)\) with \(s\in\{-1,+1\}\) gives the spin form
\[
\arg\min_s s^T Q s+b'^T s,\qquad b'^T=2(b^T+1^T Q).
\]
The Ising Hamiltonian is
\[
\hat H_{\mathrm{ising}}=\sum_{i,j}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j+\sum_i h_i\sigma_i,
\]
with \(\sigma_i\in\{-1,+1\}\), \(J_{ij}\) the coupling, and \(h_i\) the external field [PAGE 3]. In physical units, the Schr\u00f6dinger equation is
\[
i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle,
\]
where \(\hbar\) has units \(\mathrm{J\cdot s}\), \(\hat H\) has units \(\mathrm{J}\), and \(t\) has units \(\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 3]. The adiabatic interpolation is
\[
\hat H(t)=\left(1-\frac{t}{T}\right)\hat H_B+\frac{t}{T}\hat H_P,
\]
with annealing time \(T\) in seconds [PAGE 3]. The source uses \(1600\,\mu\mathrm{s}=1.6\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}\) annealing time [PAGE 6]. Solution probability \(p\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless; in information units, a measurement with success probability \(p\) has surprisal \(-\log_2 p\) bits. Qubit count is an integer count, and the source’s reported energy axis \(E\) is in solver-normalized Ising/QUBO units rather than a stated joule value [PAGE 7; PAGE 8].

Penalty heuristic. The paper uses a quadratic equality penalty
\[
\lambda\|Gz-d\|_2^2
\]
and notes that only positive \(\lambda\) need be considered [PAGE 5]. The optimized multiplier is
\[
\lambda_i=\lambda_b+\lambda'_i+\lambda_{\mathrm{off}},
\]
with
\[
a_i(z_\lambda)=\frac{2\left(z_G^T Q z_\lambda-\min\left(z_G^T\circ Q z_\lambda\right)\right)}{v_i^2},
\]
\[
z_G^T=(G_i\circ z_\lambda^T),\qquad v_i(z_\lambda)=G_i z_\lambda-d_i,
\]
and
\[
\lambda'_i(z_\lambda)=-a_i(z_\lambda)-\lambda_b+\epsilon,
\]
where \(\circ\) is the Hadamard product and \(\epsilon\) is a small positive constant to enforce constraint satisfaction [PAGE 5]. Assumptions: binary variables, equality-only constraints, positive penalty weights, and a sufficiently expressive qubit graph. Breakdown regimes: first, \(\lambda\) too small violates constraints; second, \(\lambda\) too large worsens Hamiltonian conditioning, and the source explicitly warns that “with a high λ the conditioning of the corresponding Hamiltonian in the AQC gets worse” [PAGE 5]. Third, as the spectral gap \(\Delta_{\min}\to 0\), adiabatic evolution requires \(T\) to grow; a standard sufficient adiabatic scaling is \(T\gg\hbar/\Delta_{\min}^2\), and thermal excitation is suppressed only when \(k_B T_{\mathrm{env}}\ll\Delta_{\min}\). The source frames this physically: the required slowness depends on “the temperature of the environment and the spectral gap” [PAGE 3]. Fourth, if \(Q\) is not positive definite, the continuous relaxation is not convex; the paper states that branch-and-bound requires “a positive-deﬁnite cost matrix Q, i.e. a matrix with only positive eigenvalues, which is not fulﬁlled for the given cost matrix in most cases” [PAGE 6]. Hessian regularization adds diagonal entries
\[
e_{idt}=
\begin{cases}
e, & d\in\{1,\ldots,D\},\ t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\},\\
0, & t=T,
\end{cases}
\]
so that \(c_{ii}=\mathrm{vec}(X_i)^T(Q_{ii}+E_{ii})\mathrm{vec}(X_i)=e(T-1)\) under the constraints [PAGE 6; PAGE 13]. Fifth, hardware breakdown: limited connectivity forces chains, and “The actual parameters can vary due to defective qubits and couplers” [PAGE 6]. Sixth, noise breakdown: high noise returns high-energy samples, as “Solution probabilities are lower compared to simulated annealing and high energy solutions are returned more often” [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is unusually explicit. The Ising model enters through \(\hat H_{\mathrm{ising}}\) [PAGE 3], with the historical Ising reference [31] and the quantum-annealing reference [33] in the bibliography [PAGE 3; PAGE 10]. Adiabatic quantum computing is framed by the Born–Fock and Kato adiabatic theorems and by Farhi et al. [PAGE 3; PAGE 9–10]. The quantum-computing formulation is then linked to QUBO, which the paper calls “NP-hard and known to be very challenging for classical solver” [PAGE 3]. The MOT lineage is tracking-by-detection, network-flow formulations, lifted disjoint paths, and AP-lift: the source cites Li et al. for global data association via network flows [PAGE 2; PAGE 11], Hornakova et al. for lifted disjoint paths and AP-lift [PAGE 2; PAGE 4; PAGE 10], and Braso and Leal-Taixe for a learned neural solver [PAGE 2; PAGE 9]. The quantum computer-vision lineage is Feld et al. for geometry compression, Benkner et al. for permutation-matrix graph matching, and Birdal et al. for quantum permutation synchronization [PAGE 2]. The paper’s retrocausal move is to reinterpret the earlier MOT assignment object as an Ising spin system: the assignment matrix \(X_f\) becomes a block of \(z\), the pairwise similarity \(q_{d_id_j}\) becomes a coupling in \(Q\), and the equality constraints become penalty terms \(G^T G\) [PAGE 4]. In that sense, the older classical MOT formulation is re-read as a special case of a QUBO whose ground state is the assignment.

The multi-week trap is not a single equation but a research habit: tuning one global \(\lambda\) as if it were a universal constant. The paper shows that a fixed penalty has a narrow operating range: “With increasing noise level, the solution probability for the best value of λ reduces considerably” [PAGE 7], and “Optimization of the Lagrangian multipliers is initialized with a base value of λb = 0.5. The probability of ﬁnding the right solution is increased and stays high over a large range of λoff compared to only using a single λ” [PAGE 7]. The trap is to spend many experimental cycles scanning \(\lambda\) without modeling per-constraint penalties. The counter-trap is the heuristic \(\lambda_i=\lambda_b+\lambda'_i+\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\), which merges multiple near-optimal measurements by \(\lambda'_i=\max_j\lambda'_i(z_j)\) [PAGE 5]. A second multi-week trap is to confuse classical solver timeout with formulation failure. The paper reports that on high-track segments it terminates after “900 s on a single segment and use the best solution found” [PAGE 8], and that two high-density test sequences “account for only 27.3% of the total detections, but for 58.7% of the ID switches” [PAGE 14]. A third trap is to over-read small AQC successes. The real D-Wave experiment is a small PETS09 excerpt with 3 tracks and 5-frame segments [PAGE 8], and the source’s own conclusion says current limitations stem from formulation and experimental-stage hardware [PAGE 8]. The correct genealogy is therefore: Ising and adiabatic theorems enable a QUBO mapping; MOT’s assignment matrices provide a linear-qubit encoding; the trap is hyperparameter and scale overgeneralization; the counter-trap is per-constraint tuning plus post-processing and regularization.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when the modeler treats the AQC as a deterministic optimizer: choose one \(\lambda\), run annealing, accept the best measured sample. The paper’s fixed-\(\lambda\) experiments are exactly that habit, and the result is fragile: “With increasing noise level, the solution probability for the best value of λ reduces considerably” [PAGE 7]. A second regression is to assume the classical relaxation is convex; the paper states that branch-and-bound needs “a positive-deﬁnite cost matrix Q” and that this “is not fulﬁlled for the given cost matrix in most cases” [PAGE 6]. A third regression is to assume that a low measured energy equals the ground state. The source’s energy histograms show bands, and the correct solution appears at a specific energy level; the spectral gap is the distance to the next band [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is to model the solver’s output distribution, not just its argmin. The optimized-Lagrangian loop does this: it solves, observes violations \(v_i(z_\lambda)=G_i z_\lambda-d_i\), estimates \(a_i(z_\lambda)\), sets \(\lambda'_i(z_\lambda)=-a_i(z_\lambda)-\lambda_b+\epsilon\), and merges near-optimal measurements [PAGE 5]. The Hessian regularization is another counter-trap: it adds \(E\) without changing the minimizer, so the classical solver’s convexity requirement is met without corrupting the assignment objective [PAGE 6; PAGE 13]. Post-processing is a third counter-trap: overlapping subproblems are matched by a linear sum problem that maximizes shared detections [PAGE 13].

The clock is the annealing and measurement budget. The source uses “an annealing time of 1600 µs and an additional delay between measurements to reduce the inter-sample correlation” [PAGE 6], and “a single measurement refers to the combination of an annealing cycle and the subsequent measurement” [PAGE 6]. The clock constrains theory of mind: the modeler cannot ask for an arbitrarily precise ground-state estimate; it must allocate measurements. The paper uses 4096 measurements per \(\lambda\) in simulation [PAGE 7], 4000 measurements per setting on D-Wave for the synthetic task [PAGE 7], and 500 measurements per segment for the PETS09 excerpt [PAGE 8]. The recursive loop is: modeler updates \(\lambda\) from observed violations; solver returns a noisy energy distribution; modeler infers whether the gap is too small or the penalty too high; modeler adds \(\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\) or regularizes \(Q\); clock limits how many such updates are observable. The occlusions in subproblems 5 and 10 are a clean example: “These are harder to solve problems, as multiple solutions with small differences in their energy exist and thus, they have a lower solution probability” [PAGE 8]. The correct recursive stance is not “the solver failed”; it is “the solver’s energy landscape is nearly degenerate, so the measurement clock and penalty schedule must be treated as part of the inference.”

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The source’s physical substrate is superconducting qubits and Josephson-junction couplers: “The system contains at least 5000 qubits and 35,000 couplers implemented as superconducting qubits [9] and Josephson-junctions [26] respectively” [PAGE 6]. The connectivity veto is explicit: “Every qubit of the D-wave Advantage is connected to 15 other qubits” [PAGE 6], so a dense QUBO must be embedded with chains. The defect veto is explicit: “The actual parameters can vary due to defective qubits and couplers” [PAGE 6]. The temporal veto is explicit: “All experiments are performed using an annealing time of 1600 µs and an additional delay between measurements to reduce the inter-sample correlation” [PAGE 6]. The noise veto is explicit: “Solution probabilities are lower compared to simulated annealing and high energy solutions are returned more often. This can be explained by the high noise of current AQCs” [PAGE 7]. Therefore, any claim that ignores qubit count, connectivity, defect drift, annealing time, or noise is physically vetoed by the source itself.

Biomechanical-layer veto. There are no biomechanical state variables, joint torques, gait variables, muscle fatigue, or human-motion dynamics in the provided pages. MOT15 detections are boxes and appearance features, not biomechanical measurements; the paper’s cost uses intersection-over-union and DG-Net appearance features [PAGE 5], and the dataset variables are density, tracks, boxes, and FPS [PAGE 14]. Therefore, any biomechanical causal claim attached to this source is out-of-scope and should be vetoed rather than inferred.

Weather-layer veto. There are no meteorological variables, weather covariates, temperature in degrees Celsius, wind, precipitation, or visibility measurements in the source. The only “temperature” mention is the physical environment temperature in the adiabatic condition: “the temperature of the environment and the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian” [PAGE 3]. That is a quantum-hardware noise variable, not weather. Therefore, any weather-layer claim is unsupported by the source and should be vetoed. The cross-layer adjudication is thus asymmetric: the physical layer has direct textual proof and a real veto; the biomechanical and weather layers have no operators in the source and cannot be used to explain the reported MOT or AQC results.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: fixed versus optimized Lagrangian multiplier. Verifiable protocol: run the same synthetic sequence and measurement budget with a fixed \(\lambda\) and with \(\lambda_i=\lambda_b+\lambda'_i+\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\); compare the best-solution probability \(p\). Prediction from the source: optimized multipliers should improve the best probability and broaden the useful \(\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\) plateau. The paper reports that “When optimally tuned for σ = 0, our method returns the best solution in 4.8% of the measurements, compared to 3.5% when using a fixed multiplier” [PAGE 7], and that even at \(\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}=0\) the best solution appears in “0.8% of the measurements” [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if the optimized-multiplier run does not exceed the fixed-multiplier run at matched measurement count and matched noise, the claimed optimization advantage is falsified for that instance.

Scenario 2: noise-induced spectral-gap collapse. Verifiable protocol: sweep noise \(\sigma\) at fixed annealing time \(T=1600\,\mu\mathrm{s}\), estimate \(p(\sigma)\), and inspect the energy histogram gap between the correct-solution band and the next band. Prediction: increasing \(\sigma\) should reduce \(p\) and shrink the effective gap; high-energy samples should become more frequent. The source states that “With increasing noise level, the solution probability for the best value of λ reduces considerably” [PAGE 7], and that the spectral gap is “the difference between lowest and second-lowest energy level or eigenvalue” [PAGE 3]. Veto condition: if \(p\) rises with increasing \(\sigma\) or the energy-band separation widens under the same \(T\), the spectral-gap explanation is vetoed for that data.

Scenario 3: Hessian-regularization invariance. Verifiable protocol: solve the binary problem with and without the diagonal \(E\) block, check the objective difference and the argmin. Prediction: under the equality constraints, adding \(E\) should change the per-frame block objective by exactly \(e(T-1)\) and should not change the minimizer. The source defines \(e_{idt}=e\) for \(d\in\{1,\ldots,D\}\), \(t\in\{1,\ldots,T-1\}\), and \(e_{idt}=0\) for \(t=T\), then proves \(c_{ii}=e(T-1)\) [PAGE 6; PAGE 13]. Veto condition: if the argmin changes, or if the objective shift is not \(e(T-1)\) under the stated constraints, the regularization proof is invalid for that implementation. A secondary check is that the dummy-track entries remain zero; otherwise the constant-cost argument fails because the dummy track can receive multiple detections [PAGE 6].
