The source is a six-page cross-sectional study of 20 male Brazilian American-football players, divided after exclusions into an offensive group (OG, n = 10) and a defensive group (DG, n = 10). The paper’s stated aim is “To evaluate the levels of strength, agility and the maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) between the offensive and defensive teams of football players” [PAGE 1]. The first multi-hop inference is therefore not “offense is fitter than defense” in any general sense; it is narrower: in this single sample, under the specific field/lab protocol used, the OG’s mean shuttle-run time was lower than the DG’s, and the paper reports that “The independent Student t-test showed that the OG was significantly more agile than the DG (p &lt; 0.05)” [PAGE 1]. The actual table gives the agility contrast as “9.84 ± 0.42” versus “10.26 ± 0.30” seconds, p = 0.020, d = 1.40 [PAGE 3]. Because agility is measured as time, lower is better; the sign convention in the correlation is negative for that reason.

The second hop is from test performance to physical capacity. The paper uses three different constructs: strength via a Smedley dynamometer for back and lower limbs, agility via a 9.14 m shuttle run with wooden blocks, and aerobic capacity via the 12-minute Cooper test. These are not interchangeable latent variables. The strength result is “162.50 ± 53.56” kg versus “168.50 ± 50.38” kg, p = 0.799, d = 0.12 [PAGE 3], which is a negligible-to-weak contrast and not statistically significant. The distance result is “2.47 ± 0.13” km versus “2.46 ± 0.24” km, p = 0.925, d = 0.04, and the estimated VO2max is “43.85± 2.95” versus “43.66 ± 5.26” ml/kg/min, p = 0.925, d = 0.04 [PAGE 3]. Thus, the only statistically flagged group contrast is shuttle-run time. The paper itself states that “The other study variables did not show any statistically significant differences” [PAGE 3]. A defensible reading is therefore: in this sample, OG was faster on the shuttle run; there is no reported evidence for a group difference in dynamometer strength or Cooper-test-derived VO2max.

The third hop is from group contrast to causal position effect. The design is observational and cross-sectional: “A comparative and cross-sectional study was performed” [PAGE 2]. Position is not randomized; athletes self-select or are selected into offensive or defensive roles, and the groups differ in age (OG 25.50 ± 6.15 years; DG 22.50 ± 5.48 years) [PAGE 3]. The paper reports that “The players from both groups regularly train three times per week. Each training session lasts 90 minutes” [PAGE 2], but it does not report position-specific training load, prior athletic history, sleep, nutrition, menstrual status, injury history beyond exclusion, or test order. The discussion’s body-mass explanation is a gray zone: it says “This may be associated with the lower body mass of the offensive team, as lighter players tend to be more agile [5]” [PAGE 4], but Table 1 gives OG body mass 81.17 ± 22.51 kg and DG 82.34 ± 16.56 kg [PAGE 3]. The OG mean is only about 1.17 kg lower, with very large dispersion; a simple standardized mean difference from those summary statistics is approximately −0.06, i.e., negligible. That does not prove body mass is irrelevant, but it means the paper’s own summary table does not strongly support the “lighter OG” mechanism.

The fourth hop is statistical fragility. With n = 10 per group, df = 18, and multiple outcomes, the agility p = 0.020 is not robust to many hidden multiplicities or protocol perturbations. The paper says “The study adopted p &lt; 0.05 for statistical significance” [PAGE 3] but does not report correction for the four group comparisons. The effect size rule is stated as “&lt;0.2: weak; 0.2–0.79: moderate; &gt;0.8: strong” [PAGE 3], and the paper emphasizes “The strong effect size (d) found in agility demonstrates the magnitude of this variable in the OG” [PAGE 3]. That is fair as a descriptive effect-size statement, but it is not a substitute for a well-powered randomized or repeated-measures design.

The fifth hop is correlation. The OG correlation between agility time and VO2max is r = −0.834, p = 0.003, r² = 0.70; the DG correlation is r = −0.677, p = 0.031, r² = 0.46 [PAGE 1; PAGE 4]. The negative sign means shorter shuttle time associates with higher estimated VO2max. The paper states that “There was no correlation between the variables muscle strength and body fat percentage” [PAGE 3]. But with n = 10, a Pearson r is highly sensitive to outliers, bivariate normality, range restriction, and the fact that VO2max is itself estimated from the Cooper distance rather than measured by gas exchange. The paper acknowledges a limitation: “This study has a limitation due to the methodological differences that may exist in evaluating the strength, agility and aerobic capacity” [PAGE 4]. The gray-zone audit therefore separates three claims: (a) OG had a faster shuttle-run mean in this sample; (b) the OG correlation between shuttle time and estimated VO2max was strong; (c) the paper does not establish a universal positional law, a causal mechanism, or a stable multi-week training effect.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The central source equation is the Cooper-test regression: “VO2max(kg · min)–1 = (22.351 × distance) – 11.288” [PAGE 2]. The paper’s printed unit is dimensionally incomplete; Table 2 labels the outcome “VO2max [ml/kg/min]” [PAGE 3], so the intended quantity is oxygen uptake per unit body mass per minute. A unit-consistent reconstruction is

\[
\dot{V}\mathrm{O}_{2,\max}\,[\mathrm{ml\,O_2\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}]
= 22.351\,[\mathrm{ml\,O_2\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}\,km^{-1}}]\cdot d\,[\mathrm{km}]
- 11.288\,[\mathrm{ml\,O_2\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}],
\]

where \(d\) is the 12-minute distance in km. The equation is affine, not singular; it has no mathematical pole. Its breakdown regimes are domain-based: at \(d=11.288/22.351\approx0.505\) km the estimate is zero, and below that it becomes negative and nonphysical. Extrapolation far outside the observed 2.46–2.47 km range is also invalid because this is an empirical field-test regression, not a metabolic law.

The shuttle-run operator is a time measurement, not an equation: the athlete runs between lines 9.14 m apart, picks up blocks measuring 5 cm × 5 cm × 10 cm, and returns them behind the starting line [PAGE 2]. The reported outcome is time in seconds; smaller values indicate better performance. The paper states: “The test was repeated twice, with an interval of two minutes between them, using the best result” [PAGE 2]. Formally, if \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) are the two trials in seconds, the analyzed score is \(T_{\mathrm{best}}=\min(T_1,T_2)\). This “best-of-two” operator reduces random error but can also select the luckier trial; it does not remove learning, pacing, or fatigue effects.

The strength operator is a Smedley dynamometer reading reported in kilograms. The paper says the participant “applied the highest possible force in the knee extension movement” [PAGE 2], and the test was “repeated twice with a 1-minute interval, and the best result was taken for the analysis” [PAGE 2]. Because the table labels strength as “[kg]” [PAGE 3], the numerical value is effectively a kilogram-force reading; in SI force units, \(F_{\mathrm{N}}=F_{\mathrm{kg}}\times9.80665\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The breakdown regime is biomechanical: trunk position, knee angle, chain length, support-bar placement, motivation, and whether the action is truly knee extension or a whole-body pull can all change the reading.

The group statistics are standard. The independent-samples \(t\) statistic is

\[
t=\frac{\bar X_{\mathrm{OG}}-\bar X_{\mathrm{DG}}}
{s_p\sqrt{1/n_{\mathrm{OG}}+1/n_{\mathrm{DG}}}},
\quad df=n_{\mathrm{OG}}+n_{\mathrm{DG}}-2=18,
\]

with \(s_p\) the pooled standard deviation in the outcome’s units. The standardized effect size is

\[
d=\frac{\bar X_{\mathrm{OG}}-\bar X_{\mathrm{DG}}}{s_p},
\]

which is unitless. The Pearson correlation is

\[
r=\frac{\sum_i (A_i-\bar A)(V_i-\bar V)}
{\sqrt{\sum_i (A_i-\bar A)^2\sum_i (V_i-\bar V)^2}},
\]

where \(A_i\) is agility time in seconds and \(V_i\) is estimated VO2max in ml kg⁻¹ min⁻¹; the units cancel. The significance transform is

\[
t_r=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}},\quad df=n-2=8.
\]

The reported values are r = −0.834, p = 0.003, r² = 0.70 for OG and r = −0.677, p = 0.031, r² = 0.46 for DG [PAGE 1; PAGE 4]. These are internally plausible for n = 10: the OG r is very strong and the DG r is moderate. The assumptions are independence, approximate bivariate normality, linearity, and no severe outliers; with n = 10, those assumptions are fragile. The paper’s own effect-size interpretation is “&lt;0.2: weak; 0.2–0.79: moderate; &gt;0.8: strong” [PAGE 3]. The Table 2 contrasts are: strength 162.50 ± 53.56 vs 168.50 ± 50.38, p = 0.799, d = 0.12; agility 9.84 ± 0.42 vs 10.26 ± 0.30, p = 0.020, d = 1.40; distance 2.47 ± 0.13 vs 2.46 ± 0.24, p = 0.925, d = 0.04; VO2max 43.85± 2.95 vs 43.66 ± 5.26, p = 0.925, d = 0.04 [PAGE 3]. The source also states: “The independent Student t-test showed that the OG was significantly more agile than the DG (p &lt; 0.05)” [PAGE 1] and “The other study variables did not show any statistically significant differences” [PAGE 3].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The tests in this paper have a long lineage that predates American-football positional analysis. The Cooper 12-minute test descends from mid-20th-century field fitness testing and is cited here as Cooper K.H. (1968), “A means of assessing maximal oxygen uptake. Correlation between field and treadmill testing” [PAGE 5]. The shuttle run is cited through Johnson B.L. and J.K. Nelson (1979), “Practical measurements for evaluation in Physical Education” [PAGE 5]. The skinfold protocol is Jackson and Pollock (1978), “Generalized equations for predicting body density of men” [PAGE 5]. Anthropometry follows the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry standards, cited as Marfell-Jones et al. (2006) [PAGE 5]. Agility as a construct is framed by Sheppard and Young (2006), “Agility literature review: classifications, training and testing” [PAGE 6]. These are not neutral instruments: they were built for physical-education, military, clinical, and sport-science purposes, then retrofitted onto a positional contrast between offense and defense.

That retrocausal genealogy matters because the paper’s grouping variable is modern and role-specific, while the tests are general. The paper says “American football is a collective sport practiced in different countries, including Brazil” and describes offensive and defensive player lists [PAGE 1]. But the tests do not encode route-running, blocking, tackling, coverage, or line play. A shuttle run measures change-of-direction time under a specific block-transfer protocol; a Smedley dynamometer measures a whole-body extension pull; the Cooper test estimates aerobic capacity from 12-minute distance. None is a direct positional task. The paper itself notes that “few studies have investigated these variables in football athletes and their relations with the positions of these players in a game” [PAGE 2]. That is an admission of thin lineage, not proof of a strong positional law.

The multi-week trap is the confusion of a cross-section with a training trajectory. The design measures once, after a diagnostic evaluation; the paper says “The players from both groups regularly train three times per week. Each training session lasts 90 minutes” [PAGE 2]. But regular training is not the same as matched training. If offensive and defensive players have different weekly exposure to sprinting, change-of-direction drills, collisions, or recovery, then a single snapshot cannot tell whether the agility difference is innate, selected, acquired, or acute. A multi-week repeated-measures design would be needed to separate selection from adaptation. The “best of two” rules for shuttle and strength [PAGE 2] also create a small retest-learning trap: the second trial may improve because of familiarization, not because of a stable capacity. The paper’s recommendation for “specific studies for each position of offense and defense athletes” [PAGE 5] is therefore appropriate; the present design is a starting point, not a positional genealogy.

A second trap is the use of the Cooper equation as if it were a direct metabolic measurement. The paper cites Cooper 1968 [PAGE 5] and uses the field regression, but it does not report gas exchange, ventilatory threshold, or heart-rate data. The resulting VO2max is an estimate whose error is correlated with pacing, running economy, motivation, and environmental conditions. The historical lineage from laboratory ergometry to field regression is a compression of information, not a lossless transfer. That is why the paper’s strongest correlation—OG agility with estimated VO2max—should be read as a within-test association, not as proof that agility training raises aerobic capacity.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The protocol creates a recursive evaluator–athlete loop. The athlete knows they are being timed; the evaluator knows the athlete knows; and the paper does not report instruction, encouragement, blinding, or pacing feedback. In the Cooper test, “The volunteer should run or walk for 12 minutes without interruption, aiming to maintain constant speed during the test” [PAGE 2]. That instruction asks for pacing, but the paper reports only total distance, not split times or perceived exertion. Under a fixed clock, an athlete who overpaces early can enter a crisis-habit regression: form degrades, cadence becomes jagged, and the athlete falls back on habitual movement rather than an optimal pacing strategy. The counter-trap is the opposite error—conserving too much early and finishing with unused capacity. Without split data, the observed distance conflates aerobic capacity with pacing model and self-regulation.

In the shuttle run, the crisis habit is turn mechanics. The athlete must approach a line, decelerate, pick up a block, turn, and accelerate; under fatigue, the athlete may revert to a dominant-leg turn, a wider arc, or a rushed pickup. The counter-trap is over-correction: trying to shave time by braking later or grabbing the block hastily, which can increase slip risk or produce a slower net time. The paper reports “The test was repeated twice, with an interval of two minutes between them, using the best result” [PAGE 2]. That two-minute clock is short relative to full recovery; the second trial may be a fatigue test as much as a skill test. The “best result” rule also creates a strategic model: the athlete may treat trial one as a rehearsal and trial two as the real attempt, or vice versa. The paper does not report whether the trials were counterbalanced or whether the athlete knew which trial would be scored.

The strength test has a similar recursive structure. The participant stands on the dynamometer platform with knees flexed and trunk fully extended; the assessor aligns the head and trunk, adjusts the chain, and places the support bar near the knees [PAGE 2]. The athlete’s model of “maximum effort” depends on the assessor’s cues, the perceived safety of the posture, and the fear of injury. The test is “repeated twice with a 1-minute interval, and the best result was taken for the analysis” [PAGE 2]. A one-minute clock is very short for maximal force recovery; the second trial can be potentiated or fatigued depending on the athlete. The counter-trap is biting off more than the posture allows: a maximal pull in a compromised trunk position may feel powerful but be mechanically inefficient.

The “clock” is therefore not a neutral detail. The 12-minute Cooper clock, the 2-minute shuttle rest, and the 1-minute strength rest are all part of the treatment. They determine whether the measured score reflects capacity, pacing, learning, or fatigue. The paper does not report test order across the three assessments, environmental conditions, or randomization. That leaves the recursive loop open: the athlete’s impression management and the evaluator’s expectancy are uncontrolled. The defensible interpretation is not “offense has better aerobic capacity” but “under this unblinded, fixed-clock, best-of-two protocol, the OG shuttle mean was faster and the OG agility–VO2max correlation was strong.” The cognitive layer is a confounder, not a decoration.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

The cross-layer adjudication asks whether physical, biomechanical, or weather layers could veto the paper’s group claims. A formal decomposition for any outcome \(Y\) is

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{obs}}
= \Delta_{\mathrm{position}}
+ \beta_{\mathrm{age}}\Delta_{\mathrm{age}}
+ \beta_{\mathrm{mass}}\Delta_{\mathrm{mass}}
+ \beta_{\mathrm{biomech}}\Delta_{\mathrm{biomech}}
+ \beta_{\mathrm{weather}}\Delta_{\mathrm{weather}}
+ \beta_{\mathrm{order}}\Delta_{\mathrm{order}}
+ \varepsilon,
\]

with \(\Delta\) in the outcome’s units: seconds for agility, kg for dynamometer strength, and ml kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ for estimated VO2max. If any uncontrolled term is large enough that \(|\beta_E\Delta_E|\ge|\Delta_{\mathrm{obs}}|\), the positional attribution is vetoed as unidentified. This is not a fake ratio; it is a causal-budget condition.

Physical layer: age and body composition are reported but not controlled in the group comparison. OG age is 25.50 ± 6.15 years; DG age is 22.50 ± 5.48 years [PAGE 3]. OG body fat is 9.99 ± 4.27%; DG body fat is 11.82 ± 4.14% [PAGE 3]. The paper says “The values of body fat percentage of the current study did not show significant differences” [PAGE 3], but it does not present a between-group body-fat test; the Table 1 p-values are labeled “p-value (SW)” and are Shapiro–Wilk normality checks, not group contrasts. That is a key gray-zone audit point. The observed agility difference is 0.42 s. If age, body fat, or body mass each contribute a fraction of that 0.42 s, the position effect is not cleanly identified. The body-mass discussion is especially weak: the paper says “This may be associated with the lower body mass of the offensive team, as lighter players tend to be more agile [5]” [PAGE 4], but the OG and DG body-mass means are close (81.17 vs 82.34 kg) with large SDs [PAGE 3]. The mechanism is plausible in general, but not established in this sample.

Biomechanical layer: the strength test is posture-dependent. The participant stands with knees flexed and trunk fully extended; the assessor aligns the head with the trunk, adjusts the chain, and places the support bar near the knees [PAGE 2]. A few centimeters of chain length or support-bar placement can change the contribution of the back versus the lower limbs. The reported unit is kg, not N, and the action is described as “knee extension” but performed in a whole-body dynamometer frame [PAGE 2]. That is enough to veto any claim that the strength numbers isolate quadriceps strength. The agility test has its own biomechanical veto: 9.14 m between lines, 5 cm × 5 cm × 10 cm blocks, and a turn-and-pickup action [PAGE 2]. Footwear, surface friction, turning leg, and block placement can alter time by more than the observed 0.42 s group difference. The paper does not report these controls.

Weather layer: the Cooper test is conducted “on an athletics track” [PAGE 2], but the paper reports no temperature, humidity, wind, solar load, or track-surface condition. Cabo Frio is a coastal municipality; heat and wind are plausible. For estimated VO2max, the observed group difference is only 43.85 − 43.66 = 0.19 ml kg⁻¹ min⁻¹ [PAGE 3], which is smaller than the reported SDs and smaller than plausible weather effects on a 12-minute run. Therefore the VO2max group contrast is especially vulnerable: a small headwind, heat load, or pacing difference could easily produce or erase 0.19 units. For agility, wet or dusty track conditions could alter shuttle times. Because these covariates are absent, the correct adjudication is “not adjudicable from the source,” not “proven.” The only weather-proof claim is that the tests were performed under whatever conditions prevailed on that day; the positional inference remains provisional.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
