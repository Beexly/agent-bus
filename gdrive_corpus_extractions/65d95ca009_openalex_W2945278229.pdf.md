If physical conditioning diverges between two teams, the turnover-generation mechanism assumed by the defensive counterattacking component breaks down because tackling success becomes a function of anaerobic capacity rather than tactical design.

Biomechanical layer. Hand-pass success was 97.6% for winners versus 96.9% for losers, while kick-pass success was 78.7% versus 78.2% [PAGE 12, Table 4]. The biomechanical cost of long passing under pressure is higher than that of hand passing, and kick passes are more vulnerable to environmental interference. Because counterattacks identified by Bradley and O’Donoghue (2011) required ≥5 passes and penetration into the 21 m zone [PAGE 3–4], any layer that suppresses passing accuracy—especially kicking—raises the failure rate of counterattacking transitions.

Weather and environmental layer. Games were scheduled between 13:00 and 21:00 h [PAGE 7], but the study records no temperature, precipitation, or wind data. Wind materially affects kick-pass and dead-ball execution; rain affects ball handling and tackling. Because the logistic and discriminant models are conditioned only on 2014–15 technical-tactical indicators, they lack a weather covariate. A veto is triggered if match-day conditions deviate from the sample distribution such that kick-pass success falls below the historical 78.7% baseline or dead-ball free-kick retention (94.4–95.5%) deteriorates [PAGE 12].

Veto adjudication. Any claimed decision-relevant edge from the defensive counterattacking component is void if (1) physical fatigue differs materially between opponents, (2) biomechanical passing constraints are altered by weather, or (3) match context (extra time, replays, extreme score margin) places the fixture outside the univariate population from which the PCA was derived [PAGE 7, 18]. Because Elo ratings were balanced [PAGE 11], there is no skill-stratification buffer to absorb these cross-layer shocks.
