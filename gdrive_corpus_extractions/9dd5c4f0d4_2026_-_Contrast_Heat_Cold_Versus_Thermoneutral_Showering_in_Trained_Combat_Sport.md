<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

The study’s intellectual genealogy proceeds through several distinct layers. At the physiological level, it inherits from Wilcock et al. (2006) on the physiological response to water immersion, which established that immersion confers hydrostatic pressure, greater body-surface contact, and different heat-transfer characteristics than showering. Torres-Ronda and Schelling i del Alcázar (2014) formalized that immersion and showering are not physiologically equivalent: showering delivers primarily a surface thermal stimulus without hydrostatic-loading or whole-limb fluid-shift effects. The present trial explicitly restricts its claims to showering and does not generalize immersion mechanisms such as central blood-volume shifts to its protocol.

Shadgan et al. (2015, 2018) investigated contrast baths and intramuscular hemodynamics/oxygenation via near-infrared spectroscopy, showing that vascular effects are compartment- and protocol-dependent. The current study measures cutaneous PORH at the great toe with laser Doppler flowmetry, which Fabregate-Fuente et al. (2019) caution should not be interpreted as skeletal-muscle perfusion, intramuscular oxygenation, or brachial endothelial function. Thus the lineage supports measuring local cutaneous reactivity but explicitly vetoes systemic vascular claims.

At the evidence-synthesis level, Bieuzen et al. (2013) and Higgins et al. (2017) provided meta-analytic support for cold-water and contrast-water interventions, albeit with heterogeneous quality and protocol dependence. Higgins et al. (2017) is especially relevant to the multi-week trap: most recovery research has not tested whether repeated exposure produces coordinated, persistent adaptation across microvascular, autonomic, and endocrine systems, nor whether effects persist after cessation. The present trial was designed to close exactly that gap, and its wash-out result indicates the effect does not persist.

In the autonomic domain, Aubert et al. (2003), Esco and Flatt (2014), Malik et al. (1996), and Laborde et al. (2024) establish RMSSD/lnRMSSD as sensitive vagally mediated indices responsive to training load and recovery. Laborde et al. (2024) supports the plausibility of small-to-moderate HRV increases after post-exercise recovery techniques. The observed lnRMSSD ΔΔ of 0.123 log units sits within that plausible range but is bounded by the absence of acute mediator measures (catecholamines, baroreflex, skin/core temperature), which the Discussion explicitly acknowledges.

In the perceptual domain, Kenttä and Hassmén (1998) introduced TQR as a 20-point recovery appraisal. Broatch et al. (2014) demonstrated that cold-water immersion benefits are not greater than placebo, warning that TQR gains in non-blinded hydrotherapy trials may reflect expectancy and contextual factors. The present trial’s lack of participant blinding places it squarely in this interpretive bind.

Multi-week traps:
Trap 1: Assuming a 4-week repeated exposure induces durable physiological adaptation. Higgins et al. (2017) note that many recovery modalities modulate acute homeostatic state rather than inducing training-like adaptation. The T2 reversion to baseline (HR ΔΔ 0.01 bpm, lnRMSSD ΔΔ −0.002, TQR ΔΔ 0.12, all ns) confirms this is a short-term state modulation, not a durable set-point shift.
Trap 2: Generalizing immersion hydrostatic effects to shower surface thermal exposure. Torres-Ronda (2014) and Shadgan (2018) deny this equivalence; the study correctly limits inference to shower-specific stimuli.
Trap 3: Interpreting within-group PORH improvement over time as an intervention effect when both groups improved similarly (Lenasi & Štrucl, 2010; Roustit et al., 2010a). This is a time/training confound, not a contrast-shower effect.
Trap 4: Assuming a two-week wash-out is sufficient for slower vascular remodeling. The paper acknowledges this may be insufficient relative to low-dose hydrotherapy versus cardiovascular-adaptation protocols.
Trap 5: Expecting morning salivary cortisol to capture brief post-exercise thermal manipulation. Cevada et al. (2014) and Díaz et al. (2013) distinguish basal circadian cortisol from acute reactivity; the null RRR result is consistent with circadian dominance masking small effects.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

First-order model (coach/athlete): contrast heat–cold showering is a familiar recovery ritual that “resets” the body after hard grappling, clinch, and striking sessions. Under competition or dense training stress, athletes regress to this habit because it offers perceived control and sensory contrast. This is crisis habit regression: when fatigue mounts, the behavioral repertoire collapses to a trusted thermal routine.

Second-order model (athlete interiority): because participants are unblinded and the comparator is an active thermoneutral shower, both arms receive contextual care. Broatch et al. (2014) showed that a meaningful portion of hydrotherapy benefit is expectancy-driven. The TQR improvement (ΔΔ 0.61 points, g 0.80) may therefore reflect perceived restoration, altered affective state, and reduced pain perception rather than a purely physiological recovery signal. When the shower protocol stops at T2, the expectancy contingency is withdrawn and TQR reverts (ΔΔ 0.12 points, p = 0.622), consistent with habit regression to the pre-intervention appraisal set point.

Third-order model (researcher measurement): the study instruments autonomic (resting HR, lnRMSSD, RMSSD, SDNN), endocrine (morning salivary cortisol), perceptual (TQR), and microvascular (PORH) layers. It finds coherent short-term shifts in autonomic and perceptual indices but null effects in cortisol and cutaneous PORH. This partial dissociation means the athlete’s subjective “feeling recovered” is not coupled to a measurable HPA-axis or cutaneous vascular adaptation at the group level.

Fourth-order model (meta-cognition of the trial): the null endocrine and microvascular results reveal a model mismatch between the popular claim of “integrated recovery” and the measured physiology. The recursive loop is: intervention delivered → athlete expects benefit → reports higher TQR → resting autonomic indices shift transiently → systemic endocrine and cutaneous vascular markers remain flat → wash-out removes the ritual → all proximal effects decay. The “clock” is the external 4-week + 2-week design boundary: effects are phase-locked to the intervention calendar, not to endogenous circadian, seasonal, or training-periodization rhythms. Morning cortisol, tightly governed by circadian timing, correctly refuses to be entrained by a 10-minute post-training shower.

Counter-trap: if a practitioner treats the active thermoneutral comparator as inert, they overestimate the absolute value of contrast showers. The study estimates only relative advantage over an equivalent-duration warm shower, which is itself a recovery behavior. The counter-trap is therefore to mistake a relative behavioral effect for a physiological adaptation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, weather, and physiological veto rules.

Veto 1 — Physical layer (hydrostatic equivalence): Immersion provides hydrostatic pressure, whole-body surface contact, and different tissue-cooling characteristics; showering does not (Torres-Ronda & Schelling i del Alcázar, 2014; Shadgan et al., 2018). VERDICT: Any mechanism invoking central blood-volume shifts, hydrostatic fluid mobilization, or whole-limb immersion cooling is vetoed for this protocol. The stimulus is a surface thermal contrast only (38–40 °C warm, 13–15 °C cold, whole-body trunk/limbs as uniformly as feasible).

Veto 2 — Biomechanical / performance layer: The trial collected no direct performance outcomes, neuromuscular tests, or sport-specific readiness measures. Trybulski et al. (2025) reported acute forearm-mechanics shifts after contrast immersion in MMA athletes (5–60 minutes post-treatment), but those are short-term, immersion-based, and biomechanically specific to grip. VERDICT: Claims of performance transfer, improved clinch/grip readiness, or durable neuromuscular adaptation are vetoed. The study’s conclusion explicitly notes no performance conclusion can be made.

Veto 3 — Weather / environmental layer: Physiological testing occurred at ambient 21–23 °C and relative humidity 40–60%, which reduces environmental noise. However, skin temperature was not directly monitored during PORH, and PORH indices are sensitive to ambient temperature, site conditions, and psychophysiological state (Roustit et al., 2010a). VERDICT: Microvascular results are valid only within the standardized clinical room; extrapolation to hot/cold training venues, outdoor fight environments, or uncontrolled sauna/ice-bath contexts is vetoed.

Veto 4 — Microvascular compartment layer: PORH was assessed via laser Doppler at the pulp of the great toe. Fabregate-Fuente et al. (2019) and Shadgan et al. (2018) emphasize that cutaneous flux is not muscle oxygenation or brachial flow-mediated dilation. VERDICT: Null PORH findings veto only cutaneous microvascular reactivity, not possible effects in skeletal muscle, deep vasculature, or endothelial function under different thermal doses.

Veto 5 — Endocrine layer: Morning salivary cortisol reflects basal circadian/endocrine state under standardized sampling, not acute stress reactivity (Díaz et al., 2013; Cevada et al., 2014). It is relatively insensitive to modest post-exercise thermal manipulation. VERDICT: The null cortisol result (RRR 1.05, p = 0.132; RRR 1.00, p = 0.962) vetoes HPA-axis adaptation claims but does not rule out acute cortisol reactivity under a different sampling paradigm.

Veto 6 — Blinding / expectancy layer: Participants were not blinded; assessors were. No sham or expectancy-control condition existed. VERDICT: TQR and possibly resting HRV effects are partially attributable to expectancy, preference, and contextual factors (Broatch et al., 2014). Causal attribution of perceptual recovery to physiology alone is vetoed.

Veto 7 — Comparator layer: Thermoneutral showering (32–34 °C) is an active comparator, not no-treatment. VERDICT: The estimand is relative contrast-versus-thermoneutral efficacy. Absolute efficacy versus no recovery routine is vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios (RLVR-style falsification tests) with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Wash-out duration veto (tests the “not maintained” claim).
Veto condition: If a replication extends the wash-out from 2 weeks to 4 weeks and autonomic/perceptual effects re-emerge or diverge from baseline at T3, then the current 2-week window was a breakdown regime too short to judge persistence.
Falsifiable prediction: At T3 (4-week post-intervention), resting HR ΔΔ remains within ±0.5 bpm of zero, lnRMSSD ΔΔ within ±0.02 log units of zero, and TQR ΔΔ within ±0.2 points of zero (all p > 0.10). Observation of |ΔΔ| exceeding these bounds at T3 would veto the conclusion that effects are strictly transient.
Verifiable measurement: Same mixed-effects model, Group×Time T3–T0 contrast, per-protocol and ITT.

Scenario 2 — Blinding / expectancy veto (tests the perceptual and autonomic attribution).
Veto condition: If a participant-blinded, sham-controlled trial (e.g., concealed thermostatic device delivering thermoneutral water to both groups with only the contrast group receiving actual thermal switching) finds TQR and HRV between-group effects collapse toward null, then the current effects are expectancy-mediated rather than physiological.
Falsifiable prediction: In the blinded sham arm, contrast versus thermoneutral TQR ΔΔ ≈ 0.0–0.1 points (g < 0.20, p > 0.20) and lnRMSSD ΔΔ ≈ 0.00–0.02 log units (p > 0.10), while the unblinded original trial’s ΔΔ values stand as above.
Verifiable measurement: TQR 6–20 scale; Polar H10/Kubios lnRMSSD; pre-registered Group×Time contrast with expectancy questionnaire as covariate.

Scenario 3 — Vascular compartment veto (tests the PORH null result’s scope).
Veto condition: If a trial adds intramuscular near-infrared spectroscopy (muscle StO₂ recovery rate) and brachial artery flow-mediated dilation alongside cutaneous toe PORH and finds significant contrast-shower effects in muscle or conduit vasculature while cutaneous PORH remains null, then the current PORH null is a measurement-site veto, not evidence of absent vascular physiology.
Falsifiable prediction: Contrast group shows >5% relative improvement in muscle StO₂ reoxygenation slope or FMD % change versus control (p < 0.05), while cutaneous PORH Peak ΔΔ remains ≈ 0 PU (95% CI spanning zero, p > 0.50).
Verifiable measurement: NIRS muscle oxygenation kinetics and FMD percentage with standardized occlusion, compared against the present PORH table (Peak ΔΔ 0.36 PU, p = 0.651; RF ΔΔ 0.15 PU, p = 0.365).
