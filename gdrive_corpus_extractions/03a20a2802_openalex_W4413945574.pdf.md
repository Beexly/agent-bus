The paper states, “In this paper, we present PTZ-Calib, a robust two-stage PTZ camera calibration method” [PAGE 1]. The offline stage calibrates reference frames in a local frame; the online stage calibrates arbitrary new views by matching against calibrated references. This separation is important because the heavy estimation burden is moved offline, while online operation is constrained to relocalization and reduced optimization.

The first multi-hop derivation begins with the pinhole map. A world point is transformed into the camera frame by equation (1), projected to normalized coordinates by equation (2), distorted by equation (3), and mapped to pixels by equation (4) [PAGE 2]. The paper explicitly simplifies the intrinsic model: “We assume square pixels, a principle point at the image center and a zero skew factor” [PAGE 2]. These assumptions reduce the intrinsic vector to focal length, distortion coefficients, and fixed principal point. The physical PTZ constraint is stronger: “the camera primarily experiences rotation-only motions” [PAGE 2], and the method assumes “without offset between the camera’s projection center and its rotation center” [PAGE 2]. Therefore, the extrinsic state per view is a rotation, not a full pose with translation. This is the central observability assumption. If it holds, the camera center is fixed and ray directions are the stable scene representation. If it fails, the entire ray-landmark construction becomes biased.

The second derivation hop is the conversion from pixels to ray landmarks. Given an undistorted image point, the source computes a ray by inverse intrinsics and inverse rotation, then normalizes: equation (5) [PAGE 3]. The paper defines a ray landmark as “a normalized three-dimensional vector” [PAGE 2]. Because translation is absent, ordinary triangulation of 3D points is not directly valid. The source acknowledges this: “images captured by the PTZ camera do not involve translation, traditional triangulation methods cannot directly determine the positions of 3D landmarks” [PAGE 4]. The workaround is to treat each track as a ray and estimate the ray by combining observations across views. This is not full structure-from-motion; it is a directional bundle adjustment over rotations and intrinsics.

The third hop is incremental bundle adjustment. Image registration solves a robust reprojection problem for one new frame, equation (6), and global bundle adjustment solves the joint problem over all registered frames and rays, equation (7) [PAGE 3, PAGE 4]. The initialization rule is critical: “The initial focal length can be empirically determined by f = 1.2∗max(W, H)” [PAGE 3], with zero initial distortion and rotation initialized from homographies. This is a gray zone: the heuristic works if the lens and image format are typical, but it can fail for extreme wide-angle lenses, severe distortion, or unusual crops. The robust loss mitigates outliers, but it does not remove nonconvexity. Poor initialization, insufficient overlap, or degenerate view geometry can create local minima.

The fourth hop is georeferencing. The paper says, “PTZ-IBA only provides the pose and camera intrinsic parameters within a local coordinate system” [PAGE 4]. To obtain absolute geographic alignment, it adds sparse 2D-3D correspondences: “we need additional 2D-3D correspondences to align the PTZ camera with geographic coordinate systems like UTM” [PAGE 4]. Equation (8) adds a second residual block for annotated 3D points and estimates a geographic-to-local transform. This is where absolute translation becomes observable. Without that block, the local solution remains gauge-free in world position and scale-like geographic reference, even though camera rotation and intrinsics may be internally consistent.

The gray-zone audit exposes several boundary conditions. First, the model assumes fixed principal point, square pixels, zero skew, and rotation-only motion. Any mechanical offset, vibration, lens decentering, or principal-point drift violates the model. Second, the feature front end uses SuperPoint and SuperGlue for robustness, then RANSAC with homography filtering. The source reports, “The RANSAC geometric verification threshold is set to 4 pixels, with a minimum number of matching pairs set at 40” [PAGE 5]. These are hard operational gates. If the inlier count falls below the minimum or the residual distribution exceeds the threshold, registration becomes underconstrained. Third, the synthetic evaluation adds “zero-mean Gaussian noise of 3.0 pixels” [PAGE 5], which is useful but optimistic relative to fog, rain, night illumination, motion blur, or reflective field lines. Fourth, the paper notes that SIFT slightly outperformed SuperPoint and SuperGlue in some synthetic tests but is “sensitive to illumination and weather changes” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, the pipeline is robust only when the appearance gap is within the matcher’s tolerance. The overall audit conclusion is that PTZ-Calib is formally coherent under rotation-only, sufficient overlap, accurate initialization, and adequate correspondences, but its breakdown regimes are exactly where those assumptions fail.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s core imaging operator maps a 3D world point to pixels through a chain of homogeneous transforms, normalization, distortion, and intrinsic scaling.

1. World-to-camera transform.

\[
X' = R[I \mid -C]X
\]

This is equation (1) [PAGE 2]. Here \(X = [X,Y,Z,1]^T\) is a homogeneous world point, \(C\) is the camera projection center, and \(R\) is the camera rotation in the world frame. Units: if \(X\) is in meters, then \(C\) is in meters; \(R\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: if the PTZ rotation center and projection center do not coincide, the model acquires an effective view-dependent translation term. The paper’s assumption is explicit: “without offset between the camera’s projection center and its rotation center” [PAGE 2]. If that assumption is false, equation (1) cannot be reduced to pure rotation, and ray landmarks become view-dependent.

2. Normalized projection.

\[
x' = [x', y', 1]^T = [X'/Z', Y'/Z', 1]^T
\]

This is equation (2) [PAGE 2]. The normalized coordinates are dimensionless. Breakdown regime: \(Z' = 0\) is singular; \(Z' < 0\) places the point behind the camera; small positive \(Z'\) creates large numerical sensitivity. The projection operator is therefore ill-conditioned near the camera plane or horizon.

3. Distortion model.

\[
x'' = x'(1 + k_1r^2 + k_2r^4) + 2p_1x'y' + p_2(r^2 + 2x'^2)
\]

\[
y'' = y'(1 + k_1r^2 + k_2r^4) + p_1(r^2 + 2y'^2) + 2p_2x'y'
\]

These are equation (3) [PAGE 2], with \(r^2 = x'^2 + y'^2\). Units: \(x', y'\) are dimensionless normalized coordinates; \(k_1, k_2, p_1, p_2\) are distortion parameters in the normalized-coordinate polynomial. Breakdown: high distortion can make the mapping non-invertible or multivalued; wide-angle lenses may require higher-order terms, and the source model does not include them. The method assumes that this low-order model is sufficient for the target PTZ lenses.

4. Intrinsic pixel mapping.

\[
x = Kx'' = 
\begin{bmatrix}
f & 0 & c_x \\
0 & f & c_y \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x'' \\
y'' \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

This is equation (4) [PAGE 2]. Units: \(f, c_x, c_y\) are in pixels; \(x'', y''\) are dimensionless; the output pixel coordinates are in pixels. The source assumes “square pixels, a principle point at the image center and a zero skew factor” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown: if the principal point moves with zoom, if pixels are non-square, if skew is nonzero, or if the center is not known, the simplified \(K\) is biased.

5. Ray landmark backprojection.

\[
r' = R^{-1}K^{-1}x, \qquad r = \frac{r'}{\|r'\|}
\]

This is equation (5) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(r\) is a dimensionless unit vector. The paper defines the ray landmark as “a normalized three-dimensional vector” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown: if \(K\) is singular or if \(f\) is estimated as zero or nonphysical, the inverse is invalid. For finite valid \(K\), \(r'\) is nonzero because the homogeneous image vector has a nonzero third coordinate, but numerical normalization becomes unstable if \(r'\) is very small after scaling or if the rotation estimate is poor.

6. Single-frame registration objective.

\[
P_i^* = \arg\min_{P_i} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \rho\left(\|\pi(P_i, r_k) - x_{ik}\|^2\right)
\]

This is equation (6) [PAGE 3]. \(P_i\) contains the view parameters: rotation, focal length, and distortion coefficients. The residual is in pixels. The function \(\pi\) is the full projection operator from equations (2) through (4). The robust loss \(\rho\) reduces outlier influence. Breakdown: if the number of reliable tracks \(m\) is too small, or if all tracks lie in a degenerate configuration, the Jacobian loses rank. Initialization is critical: the source uses “f = 1.2∗max(W, H)” [PAGE 3], zero distortion, and homography-derived rotations. If initialization is far from the optimum, the nonconvex objective may converge to an incorrect local minimum.

7. Ray averaging and rays triangulation.

The source states that traditional triangulation fails under pure rotation, and then estimates ray positions by averaging ray coordinates across frames [PAGE 4]. A formal version is:

\[
\bar{r}_k = \frac{1}{|I_k|}\sum_{i \in I_k} r_{ik}, \qquad \hat{r}_k = \frac{\bar{r}_k}{\|\bar{r}_k\|}
\]

where \(I_k\) is the set of views observing ray landmark \(k\). Units: dimensionless unit vectors. Breakdown: if pose estimates are inconsistent, the averaged ray is biased; if rays are nearly opposite or ill-conditioned, normalization becomes unstable. The method does not recover absolute 3D positions from rotation-only observations.

8. Global bundle adjustment.

\[
P^*, r^* = \arg\min_{P,r} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m} \rho\left(\|\pi(P_i, r_k) - x_{ik}\|^2\right)
\]

This is equation (7) [PAGE 4]. Here \(n\) is the number of registered images and \(m\) is the number of rays. Residual units are pixels. Breakdown: global bundle adjustment is computationally expensive, so the source performs it periodically and finally after all images are registered [PAGE 4]. If drift is already severe, or if the graph of matches is disconnected, the joint optimization can converge to an inconsistent solution. If the Jacobian rank is below the parameter dimension, the information matrix is singular and some directions are unobservable.

9. Georeferencing objective.

\[
P^*, r^*, T_l^{w*} = \arg\min_{P,r,T_l^w}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m_1} \rho\left(\|\pi(P_i, r_k) - x_{ik}\|^2\right)
+
\sum_{i \in S}\sum_{j=1}^{m_2} \rho\left(\|\pi(P_i, T_l^w X_j) - x_{ij}\|^2\right)
\]

This is equation (8) [PAGE 4]. \(T_l^w \in SE(3)\) maps geographic coordinates into the local camera calibration frame. \(X_j\) are annotated 3D points, and \(S\) is the set of annotated images. Units: translation in \(T_l^w\) is in meters if the geographic coordinates are in meters; rotation is dimensionless; image residuals are in pixels. The source uses only a small number of annotated images, “typically 2 to 4” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: if the 2D-3D annotations are wrong, if the 3D model is misaligned, or if the annotated images do not constrain the full transform, georeferencing becomes biased or rank-deficient.

10. Probabilistic information and covariance.

For formal uncertainty analysis, linearize the residual vector \(e(\theta)\) around the estimate, where \(\theta\) stacks rotations, intrinsics, rays, and optionally \(T_l^w\):

\[
e(\theta + \delta\theta) \approx e(\theta) + J(\theta)\delta\theta
\]

If pixel noise is zero-mean Gaussian with covariance \(\Sigma_e\), the Fisher information matrix is:

\[
\mathcal{I}(\theta) = J(\theta)^T \Sigma_e^{-1} J(\theta)
\]

The approximate covariance is:

\[
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\theta}) \approx \mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1}
\]

Units: if \(\Sigma_e\) is in pixels squared, then image-information units are inverse pixels squared; parameter covariance units follow the parameter units, such as radians squared for rotation parameters, pixel squared for focal length, or meter squared for translation. The differential entropy of the estimate, under a Gaussian approximation, is:

\[
H(\hat{\theta}) = \frac{1}{2}\log_2\left((2\pi e)^d \det \mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\theta})\right)
\]

with units of bits when using base-2 logarithms. Breakdown: if \(\mathcal{I}(\theta)\) is singular, the estimate has infinite variance in at least one direction; this occurs when geometry does not constrain a parameter, such as absolute geographic translation before georeferencing or focal length under degenerate zoom/rotation coverage.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage of PTZ-Calib is a convergence of classical multiple-view geometry, sports field registration, PTZ self-calibration, learned feature matching, and modern bundle adjustment. The paper cites homography and sports field registration methods, PTZ-SLAM, self-calibration of stationary cameras, dynamic PTZ calibration, and modern learned calibration approaches [PAGE 1, PAGE 2, PAGE 7]. Its formal roots are the pinhole camera model, homography decomposition, and bundle adjustment. Its practical roots are broadcast sports registration, surveillance, traffic monitoring, and geographic overlay.

A key scientific ancestor is the homography-based sports field registration tradition. The source explains that sports field registration is “traditionally treated as a homography estimation problem” [PAGE 2]. However, homography assumes planarity or a known fixed structure. The paper argues that this “restricts the use in complex 3D scenes” [PAGE 1]. PTZ-Calib therefore replaces pure homography with ray landmarks and camera parameters. This is a retro-causal move: the older homography regime remains useful for planar fields, but the new method treats the same problem as calibration, enabling broader 3D and geographic applications.

Another ancestor is PTZ self-calibration under rotation-only motion. The source cites methods that assume known initial poses, rotation-only motion, no distortion, or simple illumination changes [PAGE 1, PAGE 2]. PTZ-Calib tries to reduce those assumptions by estimating focal length, distortion, and rotation from reference images, while still retaining the physically important rotation-only condition. The lineage is therefore not a rejection of prior constraints but a selective relaxation: remove unnecessary assumptions, retain the mechanical constraint that makes the problem identifiable.

A multi-week trap in this lineage is the belief that a homography alone is sufficient. In a planar stadium, homography can work. In an urban 3D scene, or when objects lie above and below the field plane, homography becomes an approximation. Another trap is assuming that zoom scale and focal length have a fixed simple relation. The source criticizes prior work that assumes “a consistent relationship between zoom scale and focal length” [PAGE 2]. Real lenses can vary due to temperature, wear, calibration drift, or manufacturing tolerance. A third trap is ignoring distortion. The paper emphasizes wide-angle PTZ lenses and estimates radial and tangential distortion coefficients [PAGE 2]. If distortion is ignored, straight field lines become curved and reprojection residuals become biased.

A fourth historical trap is the availability of synthetic benchmarks. The paper creates Blender scenes and adds 3-pixel Gaussian noise [PAGE 5]. This provides ground truth but can create an optimistic prior. Real weather, illumination, shadows, rain, and crowd occlusion produce heavier tails than Gaussian pixel noise. A fifth trap is feature matcher selection. The ablation shows SIFT slightly better in some synthetic settings, but “sensitive to illumination and weather changes” [PAGE 6]. SuperPoint and SuperGlue are more robust but not infallible. The multi-week operational lesson is that calibration is not only geometry; it is also appearance invariance, mechanical repeatability, and geographic annotation quality.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive operational question is how the system behaves when crisis conditions pressure it to regress to simpler habits. A common regression is to fall back to hardware-reported pan, tilt, zoom, or fixed intrinsics when matching fails. Another regression is to treat the scene as planar and solve a homography because it is faster. A third regression is to trust the most recent online frame and avoid expensive global re-optimization. These habits are understandable under latency pressure, but they can conceal drift.

PTZ-Calib contains explicit counter-traps. The first counter-trap is the offline reference construction. The source selects reference frames, builds tracks, and performs incremental bundle adjustment before online deployment [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. This prevents the online stage from solving a fully unconstrained calibration problem from scratch. The second counter-trap is next-best-view selection: “we pick the frame that has the most neighborhood matches among the neighbors of the already registered frames” [PAGE 3]. This reduces the chance of registering a poorly connected frame. The third counter-trap is frustum-overlap matching in the online stage. The source matches the current image against calibrated references after estimating an initial pose, then uses overlap to reduce search and improve robustness [PAGE 4]. This is a memory-based counter-trap: the system does not treat each new frame as isolated.

The clock dimension is explicit. The source reports offline time for 30 images as 29.80 seconds and online time as 0.68 seconds per image, with the note that “most time is spent on feature extraction and matching” [PAGE 6]. If the deployment clock requires lower latency, the natural counter-trap is to reduce reference frames or cache features. However, reducing references can weaken overlap and increase online drift. If the deployment clock requires high accuracy, the counter-trap is to trigger periodic global bundle adjustment or re-run offline calibration when residuals rise.

A crisis model can be stated formally. Let \(N_{\text{inlier}}\) be the number of inlier correspondences after RANSAC, and let \(\tilde{e}\) be the median reprojection residual in pixels. The source’s operational gate is “a minimum number of matching pairs set at 40” and a “RANSAC geometric verification threshold ... set to 4 pixels” [PAGE 5]. If \(N_{\text{inlier}}\) is below 40, or if \(\tilde{e}\) is near or above the threshold, the registration problem is fragile. In that regime, the correct counter-trap is not blind acceptance but fallback: search more references, wait for appearance improvement, use temporal continuity, or flag the frame for offline repair. The theory of mind here is simple: the system must predict its own failure mode before it commits to a parameter estimate. If it regresses to homography or hardware priors during crisis, it may produce a plausible image overlay while losing metric validity.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which the calibration pipeline should not be trusted, regardless of numerical convergence. The first veto is physical: violation of the rotation-only assumption. The source assumes “the camera primarily experiences rotation-only motions” and “without offset between the camera’s projection center and its rotation center” [PAGE 2]. If the mechanical rotation center and optical projection center differ by a vector \(\delta\) in meters, then a scene point at depth \(Z\) meters produces an approximate lateral image displacement on the order of:

\[
\Delta x_{\text{pix}} \approx \frac{f \delta}{Z}
\]

where \(f\) is focal length in pixels. If \(\Delta x_{\text{pix}}\) exceeds the matching or robust-loss tolerance, the ray-landmark assumption is violated. For the source’s operational threshold of 4 pixels [PAGE 5], the veto condition is:

\[
\frac{f \delta}{Z} \gtrsim 4
\]

This is a physical breakdown proof: the fixed-center ray model cannot absorb a view-dependent translation caused by rotation-center offset.

The second veto is mechanical or control-layer jitter. If pan or tilt angular error is \(\delta\theta\) radians, the induced image displacement is approximately:

\[
\Delta x_{\text{pix}} \approx f \delta\theta
\]

If this exceeds the RANSAC threshold or destabilizes track consistency, registration should be vetoed. The source’s 4-pixel threshold [PAGE 5] gives a practical upper bound on tolerable angular jitter for a given focal length. This is especially important at high zoom, where \(f\) is larger and the same angular error produces more pixels.

The third veto is weather or appearance-layer failure. The paper uses SuperPoint and SuperGlue for robustness to “illumination, weather, and seasonal changes” [PAGE 3], but it also notes that SIFT is “sensitive to illumination and weather changes” [PAGE 6]. If visibility, contrast, rain, fog, night lighting, or occlusion reduce the inlier count below the required minimum, the matching layer vetoes the geometric layer. The source’s threshold is “a minimum number of matching pairs set at 40” [PAGE 5]. Therefore, if the inlier count is below 40, or if the residual distribution is too heavy-tailed relative to the 4-pixel gate, the estimate should not be accepted as metric.

A fourth veto is georeferencing inconsistency. The local solution is explicitly local: “PTZ-IBA only provides the pose and camera intrinsic parameters within a local coordinate system” [PAGE 4]. Absolute geographic alignment requires 2D-3D correspondences and the transform \(T_l^w\) [PAGE 4]. If annotated points are wrong, if the 3D model is misregistered, or if the annotated images do not constrain the transform, the georeferenced output must be vetoed even if local reprojection error is low. In that case, the system may be locally accurate but globally false.

The adjudication rule is therefore layered: physical rotation-center validity, mechanical angular repeatability, appearance-layer correspondence sufficiency, and geographic annotation consistency. A failure in any layer vetoes the final metric claim, even if the optimizer reports convergence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios test whether PTZ-Calib’s claims hold beyond the reported datasets.

1. Remove georeferencing after offline calibration.

Verifiable intervention: disable the 2D-3D annotation block in equation (8) and retain only ray-landmark bundle adjustment. Prediction: local relative calibration may remain stable, but absolute geographic alignment becomes unobservable. The source states that local calibration must be aligned using extra 3D information because “PTZ-IBA only provides the pose and camera intrinsic parameters within a local coordinate system” [PAGE 4]. Verifiable metrics: BEV overlay error in geographic coordinates should remain undefined or arbitrary; local reprojection error and ray consistency can stay low; absolute translation error cannot be meaningfully measured against geographic ground truth. Veto condition: if the system reports a geographic pose without 2D-3D constraints, the output should be rejected as gauge-inconsistent.

2. Introduce a nonzero rotation-center offset.

Verifiable intervention: mount the PTZ camera so that the optical projection center is displaced from the mechanical pan-tilt center by a known offset \(\delta\). Prediction: the ray-landmark consistency degrades as \(f\delta/Z\) grows. For a scene point at depth \(Z\), the induced pixel shift is approximately \(f\delta/Z\). Verifiable metrics: median reprojection residual, APErot, APEtrans relative to a full-pose ground truth, and ray averaging variance should increase monotonically with \(\delta\). The source’s 4-pixel RANSAC threshold provides a practical veto: if the induced shift approaches or exceeds 4 pixels, matching and registration become unreliable [PAGE 5]. Veto condition: if the model still enforces fixed projection center despite measurable parallax, the calibration is physically misspecified.

3. Stress the appearance layer with illumination, weather, or low-texture scenes.

Verifiable intervention: deploy at night, in rain, fog, snow, glare, or on a low-texture field, while preserving the same geometry. Prediction: inlier count drops; RANSAC residual distribution becomes heavy-tailed; online registration may fail or rely excessively on temporal continuity. The source uses SuperPoint and SuperGlue for robustness [PAGE 3], but the ablation shows that even strong matchers can be surpassed by SIFT under clean synthetic conditions while SIFT remains vulnerable to appearance change [PAGE 6]. Verifiable metrics: number of matches before and after RANSAC, inlier count relative to the required minimum of 40, median reprojection residual relative to the 4-pixel threshold, online latency, and drift over time. Veto condition: if inlier count is below 40 or the residual median approaches the threshold, the frame should be marked unverified and excluded from metric claims.

The counterfactual predictions are testable with logs. For scenario 1, log the presence or absence of the georeferencing residual block and measure geographic overlay error. For scenario 2, log the known mechanical offset and regress error against \(f\delta/Z\). For scenario 3, log matcher statistics, inlier count, residual distribution, and failure rate across weather or illumination conditions. The source’s own thresholds make these vetoes verifiable: 4-pixel geometric verification and 40 minimum matching pairs [PAGE 5]. If any scenario causes those gates to fail while the system still reports high-confidence calibration, the output should be vetoed as numerically converged but operationally invalid.
