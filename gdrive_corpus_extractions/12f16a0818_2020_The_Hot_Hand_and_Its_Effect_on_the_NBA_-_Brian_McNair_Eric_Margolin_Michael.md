<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a retrocausal chain of reinterpretation. Gilovich, Vallone, and Tversky (1985) framed the hot hand as a “widely shared cognitive illusion” rooted in belief in the law of small numbers [PAGE 1]. That framing made streak perception a psychological error rather than a dynamical property of shooting. Larkey et al. (1989) challenged the scope of that rejection; Tversky and Gilovich (1989) replied that the critics misunderstood the argument [PAGE 2]. The current paper inherits this dispute and reframes it with modern data and modern multiple-testing discipline.

The next retrocausal layer is the Miller and Sanjurjo correction. The paper says: “Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) showed that the statistic used to estimate hot hand behavior ... is biased, and correcting for this bias in controlled shooting experiments ... suggests plausibility in the hot hand after all” [PAGE 1]. This is a retroactive reinterpretation: a null result from the 1980s can be partially reopened if the estimator was biased. The paper then distinguishes controlled environments from field play. Free throws and three-point contests are “a nice balance between a controlled shooting experiment and a competitive environment” [PAGE 2], but the live game introduces defensive pressure and shot selection. The paper’s own conclusion is careful: “GVT’s outright rejection of the hot hand is based on limited information and can thus only reject the hot hand in an equally limited capacity” [PAGE 2].

Multi-week analysis traps compound this genealogy. Week-level trap 1 is the law-of-small-numbers explanation: it can explain away streaks without modeling shot difficulty. Week-level trap 2 is estimator bias: conditional-probability estimators from finite sequences can be biased even when the underlying process is independent. Week-level trap 3 is selection by significance: scanning 443 players or 480 free-throw shooters at the 5% level produces false discoveries unless FDR is controlled. Week-level trap 4 is post-treatment conditioning: if a make changes defender distance, dribbles, or shot distance, then comparing later shot outcomes by prior make/miss is not a clean test of a stable hot-hand state. Week-level trap 5 is power: the simulation shows that large \(\delta\) values are needed to detect the effect with these procedures [PAGE 11]. A multi-week research program that ignores these traps will oscillate between “hot hand exists” and “hot hand is a fallacy” depending on which estimator, which subset, and which multiplicity correction is chosen.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is the most useful way to read the behavioral results. First-order belief: the shooter interprets a make as evidence of being “hot,” which may alter dribble rhythm, shot timing, or shot selection. The dribble result is the cleanest first-order signature: “40 (14.2% ...) of these players had significant differences between their average number of dribbles, with six ... taking more dribbles after a miss and 34 ... taking more dribbles after a make” [PAGE 7]. This is not necessarily “heat-check” risk-taking; it is a habit/rhythm regression. The paper finds mixed distance and frequency effects, but dribbles show a repeated motor pattern: more dribbles after a make for a minority of players.

Second-order belief: the defender models the shooter’s confidence and adjusts pressure. The defender-distance result gives “34 (12.1% ...) had significant differences in their mean defender distance. Seven ... had defenders closer after a miss and 27 ... had defenders closer after a make” [PAGE 8]. That is a counter-trap pattern: defenders tighten after a make, which can suppress the observed make probability even if the shooter’s underlying state is better. The paper’s global statement is that “defenders do not appear to play significantly ‘tighter’ or ‘looser’ defense on an opponent depending on the result of the previous shot” [PAGE 8], but the player-level exceptions are exactly where the counter-trap could mask a hot-hand signal.

Third-order belief: the coach models the defender’s model and the game clock. The “icing” strategy is a clock intervention: a timeout between free throws, or a long halftime break, is intended to interrupt rhythm. The paper says halftime is “fifteen minutes long, and is the longest pause from play throughout the entire game” [PAGE 9]. The test finds “no strong evidence that shooting performance changes significantly after the halftime break” [PAGE 10]. In recursive terms, the coach’s belief that the clock can cool a shooter is not supported by the halftime proxy, though the paper explicitly limits this: “this finding does not necessarily apply to instances of ‘icing’ a shooter, such as in crucial free throw shooting situations” [PAGE 12].

The three-way adjudication is therefore: crisis habit regression predicts that under uncertainty players revert to practiced motor routines, so dribble counts should move before shot distance or shot frequency; counter-trap predicts that defenders should tighten after a make, so raw \(p_H-p_M\) should be biased downward; clock predicts that long breaks should decorrelate shooting states, but the halftime table is mostly null. The evidence favors a weak version of habit regression and a weak version of counter-trap, not a strong hot-hand state. The recursive model is a masking model: belief changes behavior, behavior changes shot difficulty, and the observed conditional probability is an equilibrium object rather than a pure ability parameter.
