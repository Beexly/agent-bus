The paper’s central claim is a layered one: first, the channel average non-fading duration can be used as a link-stability operator; second, the same channel state information can support preemptive handoff; third, paths that fade can be retained as dormant rather than discarded; and fourth, these mechanisms should reduce route-discovery frequency and improve delay, delivery ratio, and overhead. The reasoning chain is therefore not a single protocol tweak but a cross-layer argument from physical fading statistics to routing-table semantics to network-level performance.

The first derivation hop is physical. The authors define the average non-fading duration, ANFD, as the average time the received envelope remains above a threshold, and the average fading duration, AFD, as the average time below that threshold. They state that “The average non-fading duration (ANFD) is affected by both the physical propagation environment (e.g., obstacles such as trees and buildings) and the node velocities” [PAGE 4]. This makes the ANFD a composite operator: it depends on path loss, threshold, Doppler shift, relative velocity, and propagation environment. The paper then argues that “The ANFD is inversely proportional to link length, d, and node velocities vT and vR” [PAGE 4]. That is the physical basis for using ANFD as a stability metric: slower, closer nodes produce longer expected non-fading intervals, while fast or distant nodes produce shorter intervals.

The second hop is statistical. The paper treats link lifetime as an exponential random variable, and path lifetime as the minimum of link lifetimes. The text states: “We therefore assume that the lifetime for link ℓ has PDF, fZℓ(t) = λℓe−λℓt” [PAGE 8]. Then it defines path lifetime as the minimum over links: “For a path composed of L fading links, path lifetime can be represented by Zp = min[Z1, · · · , ZL]” [PAGE 8]. This converts physical channel stability into a tractable reliability model. For i.i.d. links, the path lifetime becomes exponentially distributed with rate Lλ, so a path with more hops has shorter expected lifetime. This is an important gray zone: the model assumes link lifetimes are independent and exponentially distributed, but real fading can be correlated through shared scatterers, common mobility patterns, or non-Rayleigh shadowing.

The third hop is system-level. In AOMDV, once a path fails it is discarded. In CA-AOMDV, a faded path is marked dormant and can return after its expected fade interval. The paper therefore derives a live-die-live-again system lifetime. It says: “with CA-AOMDV, a new route discovery process is only required when all Np paths are simultaneously in a fade” [PAGE 8]. This is the key architectural advantage. However, the advantage depends on two assumptions: first, that the predicted fade interval is accurate enough to set a useful dormant timer; second, that alternative paths do not all fade together. If those assumptions fail, the theoretical lifetime gain collapses.

The fourth hop is prediction. The protocol uses LMMSE prediction to trigger handoff before a fade becomes fatal. The paper warns: “The LMMSE prediction algorithm performs quite poorly if not matched to the current channel conditions” [PAGE 6]. It also states: “Therefore, the prediction length should not be too long” [PAGE 6]. This introduces a timing gray zone. If the prediction horizon is too short, the handoff occurs too late; if it is too long, prediction error grows and false handoffs may occur. The protocol mitigates this by predicting at t0 + ψ and t0 + 2ψ, and by using a threshold with margin, but the fundamental constraint remains: prediction must be faster than the channel coherence time and slower than the handoff execution time.

The fifth hop is overhead and delivery. The paper derives routing-control overhead and packet delivery ratio. It claims that CA-AOMDV reduces route-discovery frequency because it reuses paths. The text says: “the routing overhead for AOMDV is greater than that for CA-AOMDV as long as the signal threshold is not too high, at which point they are about equal” [PAGE 10]. This is a bounded claim, not an unconditional one. The advantage depends on the fading threshold, path count, hop count, traffic load, and MAC contention. The gray-zone audit therefore must separate regimes: low-to-moderate fading thresholds, moderate mobility, reasonably accurate channel prediction, and limited path correlation are favorable. High mobility, high traffic, strong correlation, or inaccurate Doppler estimation are unfavorable.

The deepest gray zone is the interaction between prediction clock and fade clock. The handoff decision depends on a discrete-time prediction interval Δt, the HREQ propagation time, and the actual fade onset. If the prediction clock is misaligned with the channel coherence clock, the protocol may hand off after the link has already broken or may unnecessarily dormancy a healthy path. This is not captured by the nominal lifetime equations unless the prediction error is explicitly modeled. The paper acknowledges high-variability limits, stating: “In conditions of high channel variability, there is little sense in even attempting channel prediction and other performance improvement methodologies will need to be utilized” [PAGE 3]. That is the correct physical boundary: CA-AOMDV is a predictable-channel protocol, not a universal solution.

The source provides several formal operators. I list them with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Channel threshold and Doppler parameters.

The paper defines the fading threshold ratio as

\[
\rho = \frac{R_{th}}{R_{rms}},
\]

where \(R_{th}\) is the receive threshold and \(R_{rms}\) is the root-mean-square received signal amplitude. The parameter \(\rho\) is dimensionless. The transmitter Doppler frequency is

\[
f_T = \frac{f_0 v_T}{c},
\]

with units \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), where \(f_0\) is carrier frequency in \(\mathrm{Hz}\), \(v_T\) is transmitter speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(c\) is electromagnetic propagation speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The receiver-to-transmitter speed ratio is

\[
\mu = \frac{v_R}{v_T},
\]

which is dimensionless. The receiver Doppler term is analogously \(f_R = f_0 v_R / c\), also in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

Assumption: mobile-to-mobile flat fading, known positions and velocities, and a common threshold across links. Breakdown: if node speed estimation is noisy, if \(v_T\) or \(v_R\) is not observable, or if the channel is not narrowband flat fading, the operator loses validity.

2. Average non-fading duration.

The paper prints the ANFD operator as

\[
\bar{\vartheta}
=
\frac{1}{\rho f_T}
\sqrt{2\pi(1+\mu^2)}
=
\frac{c\sqrt{G_0}}
{R_{th} d^{\alpha/2} f_0}
\sqrt{2\pi(v_T^2+v_R^2)}.
\]

The intended unit is seconds. The first form is the compact Doppler-threshold form. The second form introduces large-scale path loss: \(G_0\) is proportional to transmitted signal power, \(d\) is link distance in meters, and \(\alpha\) is the propagation loss coefficient, typically between 2 and 4. The source says: “For transmission over a distance, d, in the presence of flat fading, the received signal power is exponentially distributed with mean G0d−α” [PAGE 4].

The paper states the qualitative behavior directly: “the value of the ANFD is high for low transmission threshold (low ρ), and decreases with an increase of µ or ρ” [PAGE 4]. It also says: “increased node mobility (captured by vR and vT) would cause a corresponding decrease in the ANFD” [PAGE 4].

Breakdown regime: as \(\rho \to 0\), the threshold becomes vanishingly small and the model predicts extremely long non-fading durations; in the exact zero-threshold limit, the notion of a fade becomes singular. As \(\rho\) becomes large, non-fading durations shrink and the protocol’s stability advantage weakens. As \(v_T\) or \(v_R\) grows, \(f_T\) and \(\mu\) increase, reducing \(\bar{\vartheta}\). If \(d\) exceeds the valid communication range or the propagation exponent changes, the path-loss mapping fails.

3. Average fading duration.

The source prints the AFD operator as

\[
\bar{\chi}
=
\frac{e^{\rho^2}-1}{\rho f_T}
\sqrt{2\pi(1+\mu^2)}.
\]

The intended unit is seconds. This operator is used to determine how long a path should remain dormant. The paper says: “The AFD metric is used in CA-AOMDV to determine for how long a faded link will be unavailable and is recorded in the route cache” [PAGE 5].

Assumption: the fade duration distribution is well approximated by the level-crossing and Rayleigh fading structure implied by the mobile-to-mobile model. Breakdown: non-Rayleigh fading, shadowing, obstruction changes, or abrupt mobility changes make the dormant timer incorrect. If the AFD estimate is too short, the path is revived while still unusable. If too long, a recoverable path is wasted.

- \(N_p\) small: fewer alternatives, so simultaneous fade probability dominates.
- Correlated paths: independence assumption fails, and CA-AOMDV reuse benefit collapses.
- Heavy traffic: \(q\) falls, and control overhead may erase delay benefits.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The protocol sits inside a clear historical lineage. AODV supplies on-demand route discovery and route error handling. The paper says: “AODV is a single-path, on-demand routing protocol” [PAGE 3]. AOMDV extends this with multiple loop-free, link-disjoint paths. The paper states: “The key distinguishing feature of AOMDV over AODV is that it provides multiple paths to nd” [PAGE 3]. CA-AOMDV then adds two channel-aware mechanisms: ANFD-based route discovery and AFD-based dormant-path reuse.

The scientific lineage runs through wireless fading theory. The paper relies on mobile-to-mobile channel statistics and Rayleigh fading. It says: “In MANETs, potentially all nodes are in motion, so it is appropriate to use the mobile-to-mobile channel model” [PAGE 4]. It also says: “the signal envelope of a mobile-to-mobile signal has a Rayleigh distribution” [PAGE 9]. This places the protocol within classical level-crossing theory, Doppler analysis, and LMMSE prediction.

The multi-week traps in this genealogy are subtle. First, one may incorrectly treat ANFD as a pure mobility metric, ignoring path loss and threshold effects. The paper explicitly includes distance and propagation loss through \(d\) and \(\alpha\) [PAGE 4]. Second, one may treat AOMDV’s path redundancy as independent redundancy. The paper assumes independence but admits: “While this is not strictly the case, here we are seeking general trends” [PAGE 8]. That admission is critical. Third, one may treat handoff as a local repair mechanism, ignoring the timing of HREQ propagation and prediction length. The paper defines the handoff clock through \(\psi = \mathrm{round}(T_j^i/\Delta t)\) [PAGE 7], showing that route maintenance is not instantaneous.

Another trap is the assumption that node position and velocity are known. The paper says: “In this paper we assume that all nodes know their positions and velocities” [PAGE 4]. If this assumption fails, the ANFD metric becomes an estimate built on localization error, and the theoretical route-selection advantage weakens. A final trap is to compare protocols only at one mobility or one traffic load. The paper itself shows that the advantage varies with mobility, path lifetime, packet rate, and threshold. It says: “At low mobilities, path characteristics vary less quickly and the advantages of handoff in CA-AOMDV are less” [PAGE 13]. This prevents a simplistic conclusion that CA-AOMDV always dominates.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The protocol contains multiple nested decision agents: source node, intermediate nodes, upstream nodes, destination, and the channel itself. A recursive model must ask how each agent’s belief about the channel affects its action, and how those actions alter future beliefs.

The source node believes that a path is usable until either a route error arrives or a handoff updates the active path. In AOMDV, the source’s crisis habit is reactive: wait for failure, then initiate discovery. In CA-AOMDV, the intended counter-habit is anticipatory: predict fade, propagate HREQ, and switch before failure. The paper says: “we pre-empt a failure by using channel prediction on path links, allowing a handover to one of the remaining selected paths” [PAGE 5].

The intermediate node has a local belief table of signal strengths. It predicts the next samples using LMMSE. If its belief says the downstream link will fall below threshold, it emits a HREQ. But this belief can be wrong. If the node overestimates Doppler or underestimates coherence time, it may generate a false handoff. The counter-trap is the dual prediction check at \(t_0+\psi\) and \(t_0+2\psi\) [PAGE 7]. This reduces single-sample error but does not eliminate correlated prediction failure.

The handoff table introduces a second recursive layer. Nodes avoid duplicate HREQs by checking source, sequence number, destination, and expiration timeout. The paper says: “Whenever a node receives a HREQ targeting a particular ns, it checks its handoff table for an entry relating to that ns” [PAGE 7]. This is a habit-suppression mechanism. Without it, repeated fade predictions could flood the network with redundant handoff requests.

The crisis clock is the dominant hidden variable. The handoff decision depends on three clocks: the prediction clock \(\Delta t\), the HREQ propagation clock \(T_j^i\), and the physical fade clock governed by \(f_T\) and \(\rho\). If the physical fade clock is faster than the control clock, the agent’s belief arrives too late. The paper’s prediction length rule is a partial solution: “the prediction length should not be too long” [PAGE 6]. But the correct timing condition is stronger: the handoff must complete before the predicted threshold crossing becomes actual. If not, the protocol regresses to AOMDV-like crisis behavior, where packets are lost and route discovery is triggered after failure.

The recursive failure mode is therefore not merely inaccurate prediction. It is a belief-action loop in which nodes dormancy paths based on estimated AFD, then revive them based on timers, while the true channel may have already changed. The handoff dormant time is set to the maximum AFD over faded links in the path. The paper says: “The handoff dormant time field in the routing table for CA-AOMDV is the amount of time for which the path should be made dormant due to channel fading” [PAGE 6]. If the AFD estimate is wrong, the node’s belief becomes stale, and the path may be revived too early or kept dormant too long.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is valid when a lower-layer physical constraint makes the higher-layer claim impossible under stated assumptions. The paper itself acknowledges one such veto: “In conditions of high channel variability, there is little sense in even attempting channel prediction and other performance improvement methodologies will need to be utilized” [PAGE 3]. I formalize three veto proofs.

Physical Doppler veto.

The LMMSE predictor assumes the channel is approximately constant over the prediction step. The prediction horizon is measured in discrete intervals \(\Delta t\). The channel coherence scale is inversely related to Doppler frequency, especially \(f_T\) and \(f_R\). If

\[
\Delta t \gtrsim \frac{1}{2\pi f_T}
\]

or if the handoff propagation time satisfies

\[
T_j^i \gtrsim \frac{1}{2\pi f_T},
\]

then the predicted state no longer represents the channel when the handoff completes. In that regime, the handoff decision is based on expired channel belief. The veto proof is direct: if the true channel changes faster than the protocol can observe, predict, and switch, then preemptive handoff cannot guarantee improved delivery. The protocol may still reduce some discovery overhead, but it cannot reliably avoid packet loss caused by fast fading.

Distance and threshold veto.

The ANFD operator decreases with distance and threshold. The paper states: “an increased link distance (via d) would cause a decrease in ANFD due to a greater path-loss influence” [PAGE 4]. If links are near the communication edge, the non-fading duration becomes short, and the path selection metric may favor paths that are still physically fragile. A veto occurs when

\[
\bar{\vartheta}_{h} < \Phi_d
\]

for the selected path links. If the expected non-fading duration is shorter than route-discovery or handoff delay, then selecting the path based on ANFD does not produce usable connectivity. In that regime, the higher-layer routing metric is dominated by the physical layer’s inability to sustain transmission.

Correlation veto.

The theoretical lifetime formulas assume independent path failures. The paper says: “we assume that the lifetimes of the links of the multiple cached paths are independent” [PAGE 8]. It also admits this is not strictly true. If alternative paths share a common bottleneck, common scatterer, or correlated mobility, their fade events become synchronized. Then the CA-AOMDV downtime expression

\[
F_{Y_C}(t)
=
1 -
\left[
1 -
\left(1-e^{-\gamma t}\right)^L
\right]^{N_p}
\]

overstates the probability that at least one path remains usable. In the fully correlated limit, the multipath system behaves like a single path. The veto proof is that if

\[
P_{\mathrm{simultaneous\_fade}}
\approx
P_{\mathrm{single\_path\_fade}},
\]

then the live-die-live mechanism provides little additional lifetime, and CA-AOMDV’s theoretical advantage vanishes.

Weather and obstruction veto.

The paper notes that ANFD is affected by “obstacles such as trees and buildings” [PAGE 4]. Weather-related attenuation, foliage loss, rain, or moving obstructions can change the propagation exponent \(\alpha\), the RMS power, and the threshold ratio \(\rho\) faster than the routing table updates. If the environment violates the assumed exponential power distribution with mean \(G_0 d^{-\alpha}\), the ANFD and AFD operators are no longer calibrated. The veto condition is:

\[
P_{\mathrm{measured}}(R < R_{th})
\neq
1-e^{-\rho^2}
\]

by a margin larger than the protocol’s threshold safety margin. When that happens, dormant timers and handoff triggers become unreliable.

Biomechanical and motion veto.

For wearable or handheld nodes, human body shadowing, orientation changes, and gait-induced motion can create nonstationary fading not captured by simple velocity ratios. If node motion is not smooth relative to the channel model, the estimated \(v_T\) and \(v_R\) may not represent the actual Doppler spectrum. The veto is again a timing mismatch: if the motion-induced channel variation rate exceeds the prediction update rate, the higher-layer routing decisions are invalidated by lower-layer body-shadowing dynamics.

The cross-layer conclusion is narrow but rigorous: CA-AOMDV is physically valid when the channel is predictable, path failures are not perfectly correlated, link distances are not too close to the communication edge, and the control-plane clock is faster than the fade clock. Outside those conditions, the protocol’s theoretical advantages are vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable RLVR-style veto scenarios. Each scenario specifies an observable condition, a measurable prediction, and a falsification test.

Veto Scenario 1: Coherence-time collapse.

Condition: node speed or carrier frequency is high enough that the channel coherence interval is shorter than the handoff clock. A verifiable threshold is

\[
\Delta t > \frac{1}{2\pi f_T}
\]

or

\[
T_j^i > \frac{1}{2\pi f_T}.
\]

Prediction: CA-AOMDV will not improve packet delivery ratio over AOMDV. More specifically, if the condition holds, the measured CA-AOMDV packet delivery ratio should be less than or approximately equal to AOMDV, and the normalized routing overhead should not decrease by the predicted margin. The prediction can be tested by varying node speed while holding topology and traffic fixed. If CA-AOMDV still outperforms AOMDV under this condition, then the veto is falsified, indicating that prediction robustness or threshold margin is stronger than the simple coherence-time bound suggests.

Veto Scenario 2: Path-correlation collapse.

Condition: alternative paths are not effectively independent. This can be measured by cross-correlating fade indicators on the links of different paths. If the correlation coefficient of fade indicators exceeds a high value, for example near one, the independence assumption fails. A verifiable implementation is to compute the empirical simultaneous-fade probability and compare it to the theoretical independent-path expression

\[
P_{\mathrm{simultaneous\_fade}}
=
\left(1-e^{-L\rho^2}\right)^{N_p}.
\]

Prediction: the measured CA-AOMDV system lifetime ratio over AOMDV will approach one. The theory predicts that CA-AOMDV reuses paths only when not all paths are down. If paths fade together, reuse fails. If the measured lifetime ratio is significantly above one under strong correlation, the veto is falsified, implying either hidden path diversity or effective dormancy recovery not captured by the correlation test.

Veto Scenario 3: Control-overhead and contention collapse.

Condition: traffic load and neighbor density are high enough that the handoff control cost plus MAC contention dominates. The verifiable condition uses the delivery ratio equations. If

\[
\Phi_C \ge \Phi_A
\]

or if

\[
\Upsilon_C > \Upsilon_A,
\]

then CA-AOMDV cannot have a delivery or overhead advantage. The prediction is that at high packet rate and high node density, CA-AOMDV will show lower packet delivery ratio and higher normalized control overhead than AOMDV. This can be tested by increasing packet rate while fixing mobility and topology. If CA-AOMDV still improves delivery and reduces overhead at the saturation point, the veto is falsified, indicating that handoff savings exceed HREQ and contention costs beyond the model’s approximation.

These three veto scenarios are verifiable because they rely on measurable quantities: coherence time, fade correlation, packet delivery ratio, control overhead, and handoff delay. They also align with the paper’s own boundary statements. The paper says: “At high mobilities channel and path characteristics change rapidly, again mitigating handoff scheme advantages, and increasing signal strength prediction efﬁcacy” [PAGE 13]. That is an empirical acknowledgment that the protocol’s advantage is not universal. The RLVR counter-trap is to treat that acknowledgment as a tunable design boundary rather than a failure. The correct counterfactual test is to identify the regime where the physical channel or network contention veto fires, then verify whether CA-AOMDV degrades to AOMDV or worse.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
