<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under time pressure, crisis, or high-stakes information needs (breaking news, medical emergencies, outage response), users regress to a satisficing heuristic—clicking the first plausible result rather than scanning deeply. In the model terms above, this makes $E(p)$ sharply top-heavy during crises, inflating clicks at $p=1$ regardless of $R(d,q)$. The ranking engine may interpret this inflation as relevance and reinforce the top result, creating a self-fulfilling loop.

Counter-trap: the system's correction mechanism can itself become the trap. If the model over-attributes crisis-time top-position clicks to presentation bias and aggressively demotes position-1 results, it may suppress genuinely relevant content that users needed immediately. The counter-trap is therefore a double-bind: under-correct and you amplify position bias; over-correct and you demote relevance during moments when users have least patience for deep search.

Clock: the prior model $\hat{\pi}(\mathbf{x})$ has a temporal half-life determined by log freshness and retraining frequency. If logs are aggregated over weeks but deployed in real time, there is a clock skew between the distribution that generated the prior and the distribution of live queries. During a crisis, the skew is maximal because the live intent distribution diverges sharply from the historical aggregate. The recursive interaction is: crisis habit regression shifts the user distribution; the clock-stale prior mislabels the shifted clicks; the counter-trap over-corrects or under-corrects; and the ranking output degrades precisely when user tolerance for error is lowest. A robust system must therefore condition the prior on a crisis/exigence detector or shorten the retraining clock during high-variance periods.
