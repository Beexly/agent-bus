The source is a VA Evidence-based Synthesis Program systematic review: O’Neil et al., “Complications of Mild Traumatic Brain Injury in Veterans and Military Personnel: A Systematic Review,” 52 pages, January 2013 [PAGE 1 of 52]. Its target is not a single clinical trial but a systematically assembled observational literature: 31 primary studies in U.S. Veterans or active-duty U.S. military personnel [PAGE 11 of 52]. The key multi-hop derivation is: first, define mTBI strictly using VA/DoD criteria; second, exclude studies whose case definitions do not match; third, abstract heterogeneous outcomes; fourth, grade the body of evidence; fifth, interpret symptom reports against non-mTBI comparators and against civilian recovery benchmarks. The report states that “to have sustained a TBI one must have experienced an event (e.g., motor vehicle crash, fall) which resulted in a structural injury to the brain or a physiological disruption of brain function (e.g., alteration of consciousness,5 loss of consciousness [LOC], or post-traumatic amnesia6)” and that “Severity of residual symptoms reported or observed should not be used to classify TBI severity” [PAGE 10 of 52]. This is a crucial anti-circularity operator: symptom burden cannot redefine injury severity. The only permitted definitional variation was positive imaging: “the only variation from this definition in included studies relates to positive imaging results” [PAGE 10 of 52], and studies were included “regardless of whether they reported or included participants with positive imaging results as long as the rest of their mTBI inclusion criteria were consistent with the VA/DoD criteria” [PAGE 11 of 52].

The next hop is quality. The review states: “all of the included studies were rated as having high risk of bias” [PAGE 11 of 52], and “We did not combine the studies in a quantitative manner via meta-analysis because of the heterogeneity of outcomes and study characteristics” [PAGE 12 of 52]. Therefore, the strongest admissible conclusion is not an effect size but a low-strength qualitative synthesis. The report’s central result is: “In general, we found that, though cognitive, physical, and mental health symptoms were frequently reported by Veterans/military members following mTBI, there was little evidence that symptoms were more common in those with mTBI than those without mTBI” [PAGE 23 of 52]. Later it repeats that “there was little evidence that symptoms were more commonly reported by study participants with mTBI than similar participants without mTBI” [PAGE 42 of 52]. That is a comparison claim, not a claim that no one has symptoms.

The gray-zone audit must separate four layers. First, objective cognitive performance: most mean scores were within normal limits, but early deficits were observed at 72 hours and 5 days and not at 10 days in the ANAM studies [PAGE 26 of 52, PAGE 27 of 52, PAGE 28 of 52]. Second, self-report versus objective performance: the review warns that disability evaluation context affected effort and motivation: “Veterans tested in the context of a disability evaluation scored worse on every measure of effort and motivation administered, and five out of the seven outcomes reported were statistically significant” [PAGE 29 of 52]. Third, comorbidity: PTSD, depression, anxiety, and Axis I disorders are common and overlap with post-concussive symptoms; the report notes “Rates of Axis I disorders ranged from 50-78% in two studies” and “the rate of PTSD was 45%” [PAGE 34 of 52]. Fourth, time since injury is frequently missing, and the report explicitly flags “inadequate reporting of and accounting for time since injury” and retrospective recall limits [PAGE 46 of 52]. Thus, any causal claim that mTBI alone produces chronic symptoms is a gray-zone overreach. The source’s own conclusion is that “many symptoms that patients ascribe to mTBI may be related to comorbid mental or physical health concerns, or to other factors such as readjustment to civilian life following deployment or injury beliefs and perceptions” [PAGE 45 of 52]. The correct adjudication is: early objective deficits are plausible and time-limited; late subjective complaints require competing explanations and stronger designs.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Case-definition operator.** Let \(I_{\mathrm{mTBI},i}\in\{0,1\}\) be the included case indicator for participant \(i\). The source requires an injury event plus physiological disruption, with severity classification not based on residual symptoms [PAGE 10 of 52]. A formal inclusion rule is:
\[
I_{\mathrm{mTBI},i}=1 \iff E_i=1 \;\land\; \bigl(\mathrm{AOC}_i=1 \lor \mathrm{LOC}_i=1 \lor \mathrm{PTA}_i=1\bigr)\;\land\; D_i=D_{\mathrm{VA/DoD}},
\]
where \(E_i\) is an injurious event, AOC is alteration of consciousness, LOC is loss of consciousness, PTA is post-traumatic amnesia, and \(D_i\) is the study’s mTBI definition. Units: \(I\) is dimensionless; LOC and PTA are durations in minutes/hours; AOC is a binary indicator. Assumption: the study applies a VA/DoD-consistent definition. Breakdown regime: if \(D_i\neq D_{\mathrm{VA/DoD}}\), the case is excluded; if imaging is positive but otherwise VA/DoD-consistent, the source allowed inclusion, so imaging cannot be used as a clean severity separator [PAGE 10 of 52, PAGE 11 of 52]. The source states: “Severity of residual symptoms reported or observed should not be used to classify TBI severity” [PAGE 10 of 52].

**Prevalence operator.** For a reported outcome with \(n\) cases among \(N\) participants,
\[
\hat p=\frac{n}{N},\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{N},
\]
with \(\hat p\) dimensionless probability and variance in probability-squared units. Assumption: the sample is representative and the outcome is measured without differential misclassification. Breakdown regime: \(N=0\) makes \(\hat p\) undefined; small \(N\) gives wide variance; selection into headache, compensation, or clinic samples makes \(\hat p\) non-generalizable. Source examples include “prevalence of neurology referrals for headache in an mTBI population was 33.3%” [PAGE 32 of 52], “the rate of PTSD was 45%” [PAGE 34 of 52], “approximately 20% of Veterans with mTBI experience unemployment” [PAGE 38 of 52], sleep disturbance “13%” to “23%” [PAGE 40 of 52], and current counseling “4-6%” with current mental health medication “4-5%” [PAGE 41 of 52]. These are not interchangeable population prevalences because sampling frames differ.

**Information-uncertainty operator.** For a binary outcome with probability \(p\),
\[
H(p)=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p),
\]
in bits. This quantifies diagnostic uncertainty, not clinical severity. Example: \(p=0.45\) gives \(H\approx 0.993\) bits. Assumption: the probability estimate is calibrated. Breakdown regime: \(p=0\) or \(p=1\) gives \(H=0\), but that is certainty about a badly measured outcome, not validity. The source’s low-strength grading means the probabilities are not calibrated to a stable target population.

**Standardized mean difference operator.** If means \(\bar X_1,\bar X_0\) and pooled standard deviation \(s_p\) are available,
\[
d=\frac{\bar X_1-\bar X_0}{s_p},\qquad s_p=\sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_0-1)s_0^2}{n_1+n_0-2}},
\]
with \(d\) dimensionless and \(s_p\) in test-score units. The review did not pool because of heterogeneity: “We did not combine the studies in a quantitative manner via meta-analysis because of the heterogeneity of outcomes and study characteristics” [PAGE 12 of 52]. Assumptions: comparable constructs, comparable tests, and independent groups. Breakdown regime: \(s_p=0\) or near-zero variance makes \(d\) unstable; different neuropsychological tests and differing time-since-injury windows violate comparability. The source reports that “all of the mean scores fell within normal limits for memory functioning; none of the studies reported proportions of patients who obtained impaired scores” [PAGE 26 of 52]; that is a mean-level statement, not an individual-impairment prevalence.

**GRADE confidence operator.** The source uses GRADE categories High, Moderate, Low, Very Low [PAGE 12 of 52]. Formally, the body-of-evidence confidence is bounded by the weakest critical domain:
\[
C_{\mathrm{body}}=\min\{C_{\mathrm{risk\ of\ bias}},C_{\mathrm{inconsistency}},C_{\mathrm{indirectness}},C_{\mathrm{imprecision}}\}.
\]
Here \(C_{\mathrm{body}}\) is ordinal, not a physical unit. The source states all included studies were high risk of bias [PAGE 11 of 52], so the ceiling is Low. Breakdown regime: if risk of bias is high and time since injury is missing, no statistical pooling can rescue causal inference; it can only produce a more precise estimate of a biased quantity.

**Time-window operator.** Let \(t\) be time since injury in days. The source’s early window is 72 hours, 5 days, and 10 days for ANAM cognitive findings [PAGE 26 of 52, PAGE 27 of 52, PAGE 28 of 52]; vestibular findings extend to 1-4 weeks [PAGE 32 of 52, PAGE 33 of 52]; civilian recovery is “within 2-3 months” [PAGE 43 of 52]. A simple adjudication indicator is:
\[
W(t)=
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{early}, & t\le 10\ \mathrm{days},\\
\mathrm{intermediate}, & 10\ \mathrm{days}<t\le 3\ \mathrm{months},\\
\mathrm{late}, & t>3\ \mathrm{months}.
\end{cases}
\]
Assumptions: \(t\) is measured from the index injury. Breakdown regime: if \(t\) is missing or multiple injuries occur, \(W(t)\) is undefined or misclassified; the source flags “inadequate reporting of and accounting for time since injury” [PAGE 46 of 52].

**Cost operator.** If cost data existed, total utilization cost would be
\[
C_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j n_j,
\]
with \(c_j\) in currency per service and \(n_j\) a count. The source reports “no studies reported costs associated with mTBI” [PAGE 40 of 52], so \(C_{\mathrm{total}}\) is unidentified, not zero.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The report’s diagnostic lineage runs through the VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline for Management of Concussion/Mild Traumatic Brain Injury, ACRM 1993 criteria, Kay et al. 1993 criteria, and the WHO Collaborating Centre for Neurotrauma Prevention, Management and Rehabilitation Task Force search strategy [PAGE 7 of 52, PAGE 10 of 52]. The search strategy was “adapted” from the WHO Task Force and expanded with military, VA, and Veteran terms [PAGE 7 of 52]. Inclusion required a VA/DoD-consistent mTBI definition, and the report excluded otherwise labeled mTBI studies that used differing definitions [PAGE 10 of 52]. This creates a retrocausal filter: later studies that call a population “mTBI” but use looser criteria are not allowed to redefine the evidence base. The report notes that “the only variation from this definition in included studies relates to positive imaging results” [PAGE 10 of 52]. Therefore, historical inconsistencies in mTBI definitions are not noise to be averaged away; they are a reason the review is narrow.

The civilian lineage is used as a benchmark, not as a substitute. The report states that “objective cognitive deficits associated with mTBI resolve within 2-3 months” in a civilian systematic review [PAGE 43 of 52], and that “litigation or evaluation for compensation was a risk factor for worse cognitive test performance” [PAGE 43 of 52]. It also notes that “subjective cognitive complaints may linger for years for some individuals who experience mTBI” [PAGE 43 of 52]. This is the central multi-week trap: objective and subjective recovery clocks diverge. A study that measures symptoms at one late cross-section cannot tell whether the symptom is a persistent injury effect, a compensation-context effect, a PTSD/depression effect, or recall bias.

The report also places modern imaging and biomarker claims in lineage. It says CTE “can only be identified by direct tissue examination” and that “limited data currently exists in support of this relationship” for blast-exposed individuals [PAGE 44 of 52]. For DTI, it says advanced imaging is “more sensitive to white matter injury than conventional MRI and CT” but that the literature is mostly cross-sectional with small samples and method variability [PAGE 44 of 52, PAGE 45 of 52]. The retrocausal lesson is that later biomarker or imaging findings may reinterpret earlier symptom reports, but they do not retroactively validate causal attribution in the 2013 observational literature. The multi-week traps are explicit: ANAM deficits at 72 hours and 5 days but not 10 days [PAGE 26 of 52, PAGE 27 of 52, PAGE 28 of 52]; vestibular differences at 1 week but not 4 weeks on DVAT [PAGE 32 of 52, PAGE 33 of 52]; civilian resolution within 2-3 months [PAGE 43 of 52]; and self-reported complaints potentially lasting years [PAGE 43 of 52]. Any claim spanning these windows must state which clock it is using.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression in this source is the tendency, under uncertainty, to collapse a heterogeneous post-deployment symptom picture into a single mTBI explanation. The evidence resists that collapse: symptoms are “frequently reported” but “there was little evidence that symptoms were more common in those with mTBI than those without mTBI” [PAGE 23 of 52]. The mental-health layer is too large to ignore: “Rates of Axis I disorders ranged from 50-78% in two studies” and “the rate of PTSD was 45%” [PAGE 34 of 52]. A recursive model must represent both patient beliefs and assessor expectations. The report notes that symptoms may relate to “injury beliefs and perceptions” and readjustment [PAGE 45 of 52], and that “Assessment for mTBI is often associated with potential financial compensation, which in turn has been commonly associated with worse outcomes” [PAGE 46 of 52]. Thus, the patient’s attribution (“my symptoms are from mTBI”) and the assessor’s attribution (“this is post-concussive”) can reinforce each other unless blinded objective measurement and comorbidity adjustment break the loop.

The counter-trap is overcorrection: concluding that mTBI has no acute effects. That is false to the source. Early objective deficits are reported: processing speed and memory deficits at 72 hours and 5 days, with non-significant differences at 10 days [PAGE 26 of 52, PAGE 27 of 52, PAGE 28 of 52]. Vestibular symptoms were significantly worse at 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks on the Dizziness Handicap Inventory, and DVAT differences were present at 1 week but not 4 weeks [PAGE 32 of 52, PAGE 33 of 52]. The correct counter-trap is not “nothing happened”; it is “early measurable effects can occur, but persistence and uniqueness require stronger evidence.”

The clock is the adjudicator. The source’s own conclusion is that “most objective results ... are not significantly different from control participants, and deficits that are present shortly following injury most often resolve within days to months” [PAGE 48 of 52]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore ask three time-indexed questions: at \(t\le 10\) days, are there objective deficits beyond expectation? At \(10\) days to \(3\) months, have deficits resolved? Beyond \(3\) months, are complaints explained by PTSD, depression, sleep disruption, pain, compensation context, or recall bias? The source cannot answer the late question causally because of “inadequate reporting of and accounting for time since injury” and retrospective recall [PAGE 46 of 52]. The clinical habit should be: treat early deficits as real but provisional; treat late subjective complaints as real to the patient but multiply attributable until proven otherwise.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical layer.** The source reports headache, pain, vestibular, vision, hearing, nausea/appetite, and neurological outcomes, but the evidence is mostly self-report and often single-item NSI data [PAGE 30 of 52, PAGE 31 of 52, PAGE 32 of 52, PAGE 33 of 52, PAGE 34 of 52]. It states: “Low strength evidence suggests that self-reported physical symptoms are associated with mTBI” and “only two studies included a comparison group of participants without mTBI” [PAGE 30 of 52]. A physical-layer veto applies to any claim that mTBI uniquely causes chronic physical symptoms: the comparator base is too thin. Headache referral prevalence was “33.3%” in one mTBI sample [PAGE 32 of 52], but that sample was selected for headache care, so it cannot be generalized.

**Biomechanical layer.** Blast versus non-blast is not a clean veto. The source reports mixed findings: some blast associations with PTSD and symptoms, but non-significant blast differences for several cognitive and physical outcomes [PAGE 32 of 52, PAGE 36 of 52]. LOC, AOC, and PTA appear as risk factors for some outcomes, including PTSD, depression, anxiety, and neurological findings [PAGE 33 of 52, PAGE 36 of 52, PAGE 37 of 52]. Yet the same literature reports “additional injury” as a protective factor for some NSI items, including headaches and vestibular symptoms [PAGE 32 of 52, PAGE 33 of 52, PAGE 34 of 52]. That counterintuitive result is a sampling and measurement warning, not a biological law: it likely reflects cohort composition, injury severity, or response style. A biomechanical veto therefore blocks simple mechanism claims such as “blast always causes worse chronic outcomes” and also blocks “additional injury always worsens symptoms.”

**Mental-health layer.** This layer has strong veto power over causal attribution. PTSD, depression, anxiety, and Axis I disorders are highly prevalent and correlate with self-reported cognitive, physical, and sleep outcomes [PAGE 34 of 52, PAGE 36 of 52, PAGE 37 of 52, PAGE 40 of 52]. The report explicitly says symptoms “may be related to comorbid mental or physical health concerns” [PAGE 45 of 52]. Therefore, any model that assigns all post-deployment symptoms to mTBI without adjusting for PTSD/depression is vetoed by the source’s own comorbidity data.

**Functional and service layer.** Unemployment was approximately “20%” in one mTBI sample [PAGE 38 of 52]; sleep disturbance prevalence was “13%” to “23%” [PAGE 40 of 52]; service utilization showed few significant differences, and “no studies reported costs associated with mTBI” [PAGE 40 of 52]. These outcomes do not support a strong unique-disability causal claim, but they do support a burden-of-symptoms claim.

**Weather layer.** The source contains no weather variable, no meteorological exposure, and no environmental adjudication. The weather veto is therefore unpopulated: it cannot be used to support or refute any mTBI outcome. Any claim that weather modifies mTBI complications would be an external hypothesis, not a source-grounded inference. The cross-layer verdict is: physical and biomechanical layers are too heterogeneous for unique causation; the mental-health layer is a major confounder; the functional/service layer shows burden but not unique attribution; the weather layer is empty.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Scenario 1: Persistent objective cognitive deficit beyond 3 months.** A verifiable counterfactual is a prospective, blinded, matched cohort that measures objective cognitive performance at \(t>3\) months, adjusts for PTSD and depression, and reports a pre-registered standardized mean difference \(d\ge 0.2\) with a confidence interval excluding zero. If observed, it would veto the source’s conclusion that “most objective results ... are not significantly different from control participants, and deficits that are present shortly following injury most often resolve within days to months” [PAGE 48 of 52]. Prediction: the current low-strength conclusion would be revised to “persistent deficits in a subgroup,” and the late-window claim would become design-dependent rather than universal. If the counterfactual is not observed, the source conclusion survives verification.

**Scenario 2: Compensation-context effect disappears under blinding or exclusion.** The source reports that “Veterans tested in the context of a disability evaluation scored worse on every measure of effort and motivation administered, and five out of the seven outcomes reported were statistically significant” [PAGE 29 of 52]. A verifiable counterfactual would randomize or balance compensation context, blind assessors to compensation status, and repeat effort and processing-speed measures. If the effect estimates for effort and processing speed attenuate to the null when compensation context is removed, the causal interpretation that disability evaluation drives worse performance is supported. If the effect persists after blinding and exclusion, the source’s compensation-context explanation is vetoed and an alternative mechanism must be sought. Prediction: effort/motivation differences should be largest in unblinded compensation contexts and smallest in blinded research contexts.

**Scenario 3: PTSD/depression adjustment eliminates the mTBI symptom association.** The source states that many symptoms “may be related to comorbid mental or physical health concerns” [PAGE 45 of 52]. A verifiable counterfactual is a pre-registered model comparing self-reported physical and mental-health outcomes in mTBI versus non-mTBI participants before and after adjustment for PTSD, depression, anxiety, sleep, and pain. If the mTBI coefficient attenuates toward zero while PTSD/depression coefficients remain significant, the unique-mTBI causal claim is vetoed and the comorbidity explanation is supported. If the mTBI coefficient remains material after adjustment, the source’s caution is itself challenged. Prediction: self-reported outcomes should show stronger attenuation than objective cognitive outcomes, because the source repeatedly notes self-report and single-item measurement limitations [PAGE 30 of 52, PAGE 45 of 52].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
