The source is a benchmark-construction and evaluation paper, not a market document; no market-price quantities are used. The multi-hop derivation is: (i) COLMAP reconstructs landmark scenes; (ii) Progressive-X+ finds dominant 3D planes; (iii) each plane induces a homography in every image pair where visible; (iv) DLT re-estimation tests whether the plane-induced homography is recoverable from its own GT inliers; (v) HEB evaluates robust estimators and deep correspondence filters; (vi) SIFT orientation/scale/position uncertainties are measured against the GT homographies. The first geometric hop is exact: the paper states, “The planar homography is a projective mapping between images of co-planar 3D points” [PAGE 1], and “The homography induced by a plane is unique up to a scale and has eight degrees-of-freedom (DoF)” [PAGE 1]. The plane-induced normalized homography is \(H=R-(t n^\top)/d\) [PAGE 4], with \(R\in SO(3)\), translation \(t\) in the reconstruction’s length unit, unit normal \(n\), and plane intercept \(d\). Assumptions fail exactly at \(d=0\) (plane through the camera center, formula undefined) and at \(\det H=1-n^\top R^\top t/d=0\) (rank-2 homography; no unique DLT solution). If \(t=0\), then \(H=R\), so translation is unobservable; if \(d\to\infty\), then \(H\to R\), the plane-at-infinity rotation-only case.

The second hop is the benchmark filter. A homography is rejected if it cannot be recovered from its assigned GT inliers without camera parameters; the source says “Homographies with fewer than 10 inliers are rejected” [PAGE 4] and “We reject homography H if either ϵR′ > 3◦or ϵt′ > 3◦” [PAGE 4]. This is a strong gray-zone audit: the dataset is intentionally difficult, because “approximately the 80% of the homographies in the dataset have at most 0.1 inlier ratio” [PAGE 5], and “In 30% of the cases, the homographies have fewer than 20 inliers” [PAGE 5]. Under the standard RANSAC sample-complexity model, with minimal point sample size \(s=4\), inlier probability \(w\), and confidence \(p\), the required iterations are \(N=\lceil \ln(1-p)/\ln(1-w^s)\rceil\). For \(p=0.95\), \(w=0.1\) gives roughly \(2.9957/0.000100\approx 29{,}956\) iterations; \(w=0.01\) gives roughly \(2.9957/10^{-8}\approx 2.996\times 10^8\) iterations. Thus low-inlier regimes are not merely “hard”; they are computationally inadmissible without prefiltering or higher-order correspondence constraints.

The third hop is metric adjudication. The paper’s main metric is the average of rotation and translation mAA; it also reports absolute translation error \(\epsilon^{abs}_{t'}=|t-t'|_2\) [PAGE 5], GT-correspondence reprojection error \(\epsilon_{repr}=|x-H(x')|_2\) [PAGE 5], inlier counts, and runtime. The gray zone is that these metrics can disagree: the paper explicitly reports that “The mAA of the re-proj. error is also in agreement with the mAA of the pose error ... with some exceptions” [PAGE 5], and that “the method ranking is significantly affected by the metric” for rotation-only mAA [PAGE 6]. Absolute scale is also not identifiable from \(H\) alone: “Since the scale can not be recovered from an essential matrix or homography [32], we assign the GT absolute scale to the estimated translation t′” [PAGE 5]. Therefore any claim of metric superiority must specify whether it uses scale-assigned translation, angular translation, reprojection, or inlier count; otherwise the claim is underdetermined.

The fourth hop is detector uncertainty. The paper measures OpenCV SIFT orientation STD \(\hat\sigma_{\Delta\alpha_i}=11.8^\circ\), scale log-ratio STD \(\hat\sigma_{\rho_i}=0.51\), and positional uncertainty \(\hat\sigma_x\approx 0.67\) pixels [PAGE 8]. These are lower-layer noise scales. A claimed improvement below those scales is not credible unless it is measured on the same GT-correspondence protocol and with uncertainty propagated. The gray-zone audit is therefore: benchmark difficulty, metric choice, scale identifiability, implementation confounds, and detector noise all interact. The paper’s own warning is procedural: “one should not not make more than one or two evaluation runs on the test set” [PAGE 5], because repeated test tuning converts benchmark progress into test-set memorization.

A final multi-hop trap is deep prefiltering. The source evaluated pretrained models that were not fine-tuned for homography estimation: “We emphasize that we neither trained, nor fine-tuned them for the homography estimation task, so their performance is sub-optimal” [PAGE 7]. The top result is “achieved by combining VSAC [35] with OANet [75]” [PAGE 8], but OANet uses SNN ratio side information and the measured gain must be weighed against “at least 5-10 ms per image pair for the fastest methods” [PAGE 8]. Thus the correct audit is conditional: compute accuracy under a fixed iteration budget, then compute accuracy under a wall-clock budget with prefilter time included. The gray zone is where algorithmic ranking flips because the clock changes.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(\mathbf{x}=(x,y,1)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{x}'=(x',y',1)^\top\) be homogeneous image points in pixels. A homography is \( \mathbf{x}' \sim H\mathbf{x}\), \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}\), \(H\sim\lambda H\) for nonzero scalar \(\lambda\). It has 8 DoF and is dimensionless up to scale [PAGE 1]. The plane-induced normalized form is
\[
H = R - \frac{t n^\top}{d},
\]
where \(R\in SO(3)\) is dimensionless, \(t\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is in reconstruction length units, \(n\in\mathbb{R}^3\) is a unit normal, and \(d\) is the plane intercept in the same length unit as \(t\) [PAGE 4]. Assumptions: normalized camera coordinates, a single 3D plane, and consistent relative pose. Exact breakdown regimes: \(d=0\) makes the formula undefined (plane through the camera center); \(\det H = 1 - n^\top R^\top t/d = 0\) makes \(H\) rank 2; \(t=0\) yields \(H=R\) and no translation information; \(d\to\infty\) yields the rotation-only plane-at-infinity limit.

Relative pose in the source is \(R=R_2R_1^\top\) and \(t=t_2-R_2R_1^\top t_1\) [PAGE 4]. The rotation error is
\[
\epsilon_{R'} = \frac{180}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}(R'R^\top)-1}{2}\right) \quad [\text{degrees}],
\]
and the angular translation error is
\[
\epsilon_{t'} = \frac{180}{\pi}\arccos\!\left(\frac{t^\top t'}{|t|\,|t'|}\right) \quad [\text{degrees}],
\]
both from [PAGE 4, Eqs. (1)–(2)]. The absolute translation error is
\[
\epsilon^{abs}_{t'} = |t-t'|_2 \quad [\text{meters}],
\]
and the GT-correspondence reprojection error is
\[
\epsilon_{repr} = |x-H(x')|_2 \quad [\text{pixels}],
\]
from [PAGE 5, Eqs. (3)–(4)]. The source rejects homographies with fewer than 10 inliers and rejects if either \(\epsilon_{R'}>3^\circ\) or \(\epsilon_{t'}>3^\circ\) [PAGE 4]. These thresholds are hard admissibility gates, not soft scores.

For robust estimation, the standard RANSAC iteration count is
\[
N = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(1-p)}{\ln(1-w^s)} \right\rceil \quad [\text{iterations}],
\]
where \(p\) is confidence probability, \(w\) is inlier probability, \(s\) is minimal sample size, and \(\ln\) is in nats. For homography DLT, \(s=4\) under the standard point-correspondence model. The source’s empirical distribution makes this nontrivial: “approximately the 80% of the homographies in the dataset have at most 0.1 inlier ratio” [PAGE 5]. At \(w=0.1\), \(p=0.95\), \(s=4\), \(N\approx 29{,}956\) iterations; at \(w=0.01\), \(N\approx 2.996\times 10^8\) iterations. This is the formal basis for the paper’s prefiltering claims.

For affine/local decomposition, the source defines condition number \(\operatorname{cond}(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\), dimensionless, and states no shears if \(\operatorname{cond}(A)=1\); it also defines shear magnitude
\[
d_s^2 = |[p_3,p_4]|^2 = p_3^2+p_4^2 = 0
\]
for no shears, with \(d_s^2\approx \operatorname{cond}(A)\) for not-too-large shears [PAGE 13, Eqs. (26)–(27)]. The paper restricts some SIFT analysis to \(\operatorname{cond}(\tilde A_i)<1.5\) [PAGE 8]. The scale ratio is
\[
\tilde r_i = \sqrt{|\tilde A_i|},
\]
dimensionless, from [PAGE 11, Eq. (6)], and the weighted log-ratio is \(\rho_i=\log(\Delta r_i)/\tilde r_i\), dimensionless, from [PAGE 11]. The symmetric positional residual is
\[
\epsilon_{x_i} =
\sqrt{\frac{|x'_i-\tilde H(x_i)|_2^2 + |x_i-\tilde H^{-1}(x'_i)|_2^2}{8}}
\quad [\text{pixels}],
\]
from [PAGE 11, Eq. (5)]. The measured OpenCV SIFT uncertainties are \(\hat\sigma_{\Delta\alpha_i}=11.8^\circ\), \(\hat\sigma_{\rho_i}=0.51\), and \(\hat\sigma_x\approx0.67\) pixels [PAGE 8]. The manual absolute scale has standard deviation approximately \(16\) cm [PAGE 4].

The source’s covariance analysis gives algebraic and ML covariance matrices:
\[
\Sigma^{(\mathrm{ALG})}_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}
=(A^\top A)^+A^\top B^\top \Sigma_{ll}BA(A^\top A)^+,
\]
\[
\Sigma^{(\mathrm{ML})}_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}
=(A^\top(B^\top\Sigma_{ll}B)^{-1}A)^+,
\]
from [PAGE 14, Eqs. (33) and (36)]. Units follow the parameter vector \(\theta=\operatorname{vec}H\) and the observation covariance \(\Sigma_{ll}\); the pseudoinverse is mandatory because \(H\) has redundant scale and the point covariance is rank 2. The loss criteria are
\[
l_{mean}=\sqrt{\operatorname{trace}(\Sigma_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}\tilde\Sigma^{-1}_{\hat\theta\hat\theta})/8},
\quad
l_{max}=\sqrt{\max\lambda(\Sigma_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}\tilde\Sigma^{-1}_{\hat\theta\hat\theta})},
\]
dimensionless, from [PAGE 14, Eqs. (42)–(43)]. Breakdown occurs when \(A^\top A\) is rank-deficient, when the plane intercept \(d\) approaches zero, when \(H\) becomes singular, when the correspondence set is collinear/degenerate, or when the inlier probability is so small that \(N\) exceeds the budget.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is projective geometry and DLT (Hartley–Zisserman [32]), robust consensus (Fischler–Bolles RANSAC [25]), locally optimized RANSAC [21], PROSAC [19], graph-cut RANSAC [7], MAGSAC/MAGSAC++ [9,8], VSAC [35], and affine-correspondence GC-RANSAC [10]. The feature lineage is SIFT [45,46] and RootSIFT [2]; the data lineage is 1DSfM [69] and COLMAP [59]. The retrocausal move of HEB is to make later methods reveal hidden dependencies on earlier evaluation practice. For example, the source reports “The top accuracy is achieved by combining VSAC [35] with OANet [75]” [PAGE 8], but also that “PROSAC – a well known, but often ignored sampling scheme accelerates RANSAC by an order of magnitude” [PAGE 8]. Thus the “new” result is partly a rediscovery of sampling geometry, not a purely architectural breakthrough.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are: (1) tuning on test data. The source explicitly warns against more than “one or two evaluation runs on the test set” [PAGE 5] and fixes hyperparameters on the training split [PAGE 5]. A team that ignores this can spend weeks optimizing a test-set artifact. (2) The SNN threshold trap. “For most methods, the best SNN threshold is 0.6, which is stricter than the widely used 0.8” [PAGE 7]; using the habitual 0.8 can under-filter outliers in low-inlier HEB pairs. (3) The implementation trap. The paper defines a method by name and library because C++ OpenCV, Python scikit-image, kornia, and author implementations differ by orders of magnitude in runtime [PAGE 6]. (4) The deep-prefilter domain trap. Pretrained models were trained for epipolar geometry, not homography: “We emphasize that we neither trained, nor fine-tuned them for the homography estimation task, so their performance is sub-optimal” [PAGE 7]. (5) The metric trap: rotation-only mAA and pose mAA can rank methods differently [PAGE 6]. (6) The affine-approximation trap: affine GC-RANSAC approximates affine correspondences from SIFT orientation and scale [PAGE 6, PAGE 10], so its advantage is bounded by SIFT orientation/scale noise. (7) The clock trap: GPU prefiltering costs “at least 5-10 ms per image pair for the fastest methods” [PAGE 8], which can erase a nominal accuracy gain in a real-time setting. These are multi-week traps because each requires re-running a full protocol, not a single code change.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the tendency, under HEB’s low-inlier stress, to regress to familiar defaults: Lowe ratio 0.8, unvalidated RANSAC iteration counts, or inlier-count worship. The source counters this with measured protocol: optimal SNN is often 0.6, not 0.8 [PAGE 7], and the ranking is metric-dependent [PAGE 6]. The recursive model is: the evaluator expects the method designer to tune; the designer expects the evaluator to use a hidden threshold; therefore the only stable policy is to pre-register hyperparameters on the training split and report a time-accuracy curve. The source states the protocol: “one should not not make more than one or two evaluation runs on the test set” [PAGE 5].

Counter-trap reasoning: a sophisticated designer might try to exploit the deep-prefilter result by assuming OANet/CLNet superiority transfers to homography. The counter-trap is that those models were not fine-tuned for homography and were trained on an epipolar task [PAGE 7]; their apparent advantage may be a side-information or domain effect, not a homography-specific one. The source’s own counterfactual is to include prefiltering time: with the clock included, “It is at least 5-10 ms per image pair for the fastest methods” [PAGE 8]. Thus a claim that ignores the clock is not a stable belief.

Clock reasoning: at very short budgets, the source reports “The RHO algorithm is still the leader in top-speed part, outperformed by VSAC-PROSAC with increasing time budget” [PAGE 7]. A recursive agent should therefore maintain two beliefs simultaneously: a low-budget belief (cheap RANSAC/RHO) and a high-budget belief (VSAC-PROSAC/OANet). The clock is not a nuisance parameter; it is a layer that can veto an otherwise accurate method. The correct recursive policy is: fix the test protocol, fix the training-tuned hyperparameters, then vary iterations from 10 to 10,000 and report both without-prefilter-time and with-prefilter-time curves [PAGE 5, PAGE 7].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-geometry veto: a single homography is admissible only for co-planar 3D points; the source defines it as “a projective mapping between images of co-planar 3D points” [PAGE 1]. If multiple planes are present, the benchmark keeps only one estimatable homography per test case and removes other planes’ inliers [PAGE 4]. If \(d=0\) or \(\det H=0\), the DLT system is rank-deficient and the estimate is vetoed. If absolute scale is required, it is not identifiable from \(H\) alone, so the source assigns GT scale to the estimate [PAGE 5]; any claim of metric translation accuracy without that assignment is vetoed.

Biomechanical/detector veto: human scale annotation has standard deviation approximately 16 cm [PAGE 4]. OpenCV SIFT orientation STD is 11.8°, scale log STD is 0.51, and position STD is approximately 0.67 pixels [PAGE 8]. These are lower-layer noise floors. A claimed sub-degree orientation improvement or sub-0.1-pixel positional improvement is vetoed unless it is measured against the same GT homography and uncertainty propagation. The source itself says the positional STD is “a factor two larger, than expected, which might result from accepting small outliers” [PAGE 8]; that is a direct warning against over-reading SIFT localization.

Weather/photometric veto: HEB includes significant illumination changes [PAGE 1], and the source notes that the benchmark contains “significant viewpoint and illumination changes” [PAGE 1]. If illumination or viewpoint change destroys available correspondences, no robust estimator can recover the model; the lower layer vetoes the upper-layer accuracy claim. This is formalized by RANSAC sample complexity: when \(w\) is small, \(w^s\) is tiny and \(N\) explodes. For \(w=0.1,s=4,p=0.95\), \(N\approx29{,}956\); for \(w=0.01\), \(N\approx2.996\times10^8\). The source’s observed distribution, with about 80% of homographies at or below 0.1 inlier ratio [PAGE 5], is exactly the regime where prefiltering or higher-order constraints are required. Veto rule: if a method’s claimed gain disappears when the prefilter time is included or when the SNN threshold is fixed at the source-optimal 0.6 rather than an unvalidated 0.8 [PAGE 7], the claim is not adjudicated as robust.

1. Inlier-ablation / correspondence-shuffle veto. Scenario: take HEB test pairs, randomly permute the correspondence order or ablate the highest-scoring correspondences, then rerun the same estimator with the same fixed hyperparameters. Prediction: mAA at \((10^\circ,5\text{ m})\) collapses toward the low-inlier baseline; any method that retains high mAA under shuffled labels is evidence of leakage or non-geometric scoring. Verification is direct because HEB supplies GT homographies and per-pair metrics [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. This veto tests whether the method actually uses geometry rather than dataset-specific ordering.

2. Clock counterfactual with and without prefilter time. Scenario: fix max iterations at 10 and at 10,000, and compute two curves: estimator-only time and estimator-plus-prefilter time. Prediction: at 10 iterations, low-cost RHO/RANSAC variants lead; at 10,000 iterations, VSAC-PROSAC/OANet leads. With deep-prefilter time included, the apparent gain shrinks because the fastest filters cost “at least 5-10 ms per image pair” [PAGE 8]. Verification: rerun the source’s time-accuracy protocol [PAGE 5, PAGE 7] and check whether the ranking flips at the same iteration and wall-clock thresholds.

Scenario: perturb SIFT orientations by the measured STD \(11.8^\circ\) and scales by the measured log STD \(0.51\) [PAGE 8], then rerun affine GC-RANSAC and VSAC+OANet with identical budgets. Prediction: affine GC-RANSAC’s advantage decreases because its affine correspondences are approximated from SIFT orientation/scale [PAGE 6, PAGE 10]; VSAC+OANet is less sensitive to that particular affine approximation but still bounded by SNN side information and prefilter time. Verification: compare mAA at \((10^\circ,5\text{ m})\) before and after perturbation; a statistically significant drop in affine GC-RANSAC relative to VSAC+OANet confirms the veto. If the advantage survives the measured detector noise, the claim is verifiable rather than ritual.
