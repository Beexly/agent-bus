<THINK_TRACE>
This is a GSE/GSI-style gray-zone audit of the supplied calibration paper. The multi-hop derivation is: (1) the paper needs camera calibration to map a player’s image position to world coordinates, and it states that “camera calibration is required to recognize the player’s position from a video” [PAGE 1]; (2) without a calibration object, it uses a priori field geometry; (3) field extraction is thresholded in HSV by Eq. (1) [PAGE 2]; (4) LSD lines are merged, selected by length, and filtered by a white-line-rate test; (5) intersections are formed from selected lines, then matched to a field template; (6) a homography is estimated from four or more intersection pairs; (7) candidate homographies are scored by Eq. (4); (8) intersections are refined in a local patch and the homography is recalculated. The central causal claim is that flexible intersection selection increases the number of usable pairs, while refinement reduces the displacement between detected and real intersections. That matters because the homography has eight unknowns after normalization: “When two vectors are normalized by z and Z, the number of unknown parameters of the homography matrix is eight. Therefore, the matrix can be estimated by four intersection pairs between an input image coordinate and a field template coordinate” [PAGE 2]. If line detection fails, the chain breaks immediately: “When the line detection fails, a homography matrix is not calculated due to the lack of the number of intersections” [PAGE 2].

The gray-zone audit must separate mean performance from tail performance. Table 3 reports Yao et al. at 2/33 high-IoU images, Chen et al. at 25/33 with mIoU 0.973, and the proposed method at 26/33 with mIoU 0.975 [PAGE 6]. That is a small mean gain but a large qualitative difference against the older Yao pipeline. More importantly, Table 4 reports that intersection refinement changes high-IoU images from 18/33 to 26/33 while mIoU remains 0.974 in both cases [PAGE 7]. Thus the refinement is not primarily a mean-IoU optimizer; it is a tail-risk reducer. The paper itself says, “Although mIoU performance is the same, the number of high IoU images is increased by IR” [PAGE 7]. This is exactly a gray-zone result: an evaluator who watches only mIoU would conclude that refinement does nothing, while an evaluator who watches the count of high-IoU images sees a major robustness gain. The paper also acknowledges subjective/objective divergence: when IoU is higher than 0.9, it is difficult to identify subjective difference in the image-to-template projection, so the template-to-image projection is needed to see small displacement [PAGE 7]. That is a key audit point because a high aggregate score can hide visible displacement.

Another gray zone is parameter transfer. The paper uses common parameters for 33 images, but warns that parameters “need to be adjusted by the user for each soccer video, because, for example, the color of the field is different for each stadium and country” [PAGE 6]. Thus the method is automatic in the sense of avoiding a calibration object, but not fully parameter-free in deployment. The method also assumes the target video contains the penalty area, because it needs more than four points and the penalty area supplies the intersection structure. The paper says the penalty area is assumed to be contained in the target video [PAGE 2], and the experiments use 33 test images that contain the penalty area [PAGE 6]. If the penalty area is absent or the visible line structure is too weak, the method can be vetoed before homography estimation. The paper also admits failure from overdetection: “some input images are evaluated as being less than 0.7 of IoU because the real line extraction extracts lines other than real lines” [PAGE 9]. Therefore the correct reading is not “automatic calibration always works,” but “automatic calibration works when the physical field-line evidence survives extraction and when the projective geometry is nondegenerate.”

The refinement chain is also physically constrained. The paper extracts a 127 by 127 pixel patch centered on an intersection, applies bilateral filtering, binarization, median filtering, dilation, thinning, and Gaussian filtering, then runs LSD on the patch [PAGE 5]. It notes that “The LSD detects the line along a boundary between the white line and field, not the center of the white line” [PAGE 5]. Therefore two boundary lines are merged and a center line is computed. If the patch misses the real intersection, if the line fragments are wrongly merged, or if lighting and occlusion destroy the white-line boundary, the refinement can be neutral or harmful. That is a singularity regime at the local-patch layer. The global singularity regime is projective: four point correspondences must be in general position; if intersections are collinear, duplicated, or too few, the homography is underdetermined or ill-conditioned. The paper reduces combinatorics by sorting: “By using sorted Vi, the number of combination patterns is reduced from M! × P CM to P CM” [PAGE 4], and side information further halves the template search. But sorting cannot repair missing evidence; it only prunes a search space that already contains a valid correspondence set.

The final audit judgment is that the paper is credible on its stated domain: penalty-area soccer images with visible field lines and a known template, where the goal is short recalibration after camera disturbance. It is not a proof of general sports calibration. Its strongest evidence is the high-IoU count increase from refinement and the comparison against Yao, not the tiny mIoU edge over Chen. Its weakest evidence is runtime: the paper says runtime evaluation is omitted and that the method is slower than Yao due to many intersection pairs, with future GPU work [PAGE 7]. The clock is therefore a real constraint, not a cosmetic one. The paper’s own conclusion frames the remaining problem as line extraction and GPU implementation [PAGE 9]. That is the correct boundary of the claim.

The source defines a field mask over HSV components. Equation (1) is given as:

\[
mask_{i,j} =
\begin{cases}
1, & I^{H,S,V}_{i,j} \in [\sigma^{H,S,V}_{\min}, \sigma^{H,S,V}_{\max}] \\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

This is on [PAGE 2]. The symbols \(i,j\) are pixel indices; \(I^{H,S,V}\) is the HSV color triplet; \(\sigma_{\min}\) and \(\sigma_{\max}\) are the lower and upper HSV thresholds. The output is a binary mask, so the natural unit is a pixel label, not a physical unit. The paper says the continuous region of “1” with the maximum number of pixels is defined as the field area [PAGE 2]. Assumption: the field color is sufficiently separable from non-field regions. Breakdown: if stadium color, lighting, shadow, or weather shifts the HSV distribution outside the thresholds, the mask selects the wrong region. The paper reports the field-extraction parameters in Table 1 as H 30 to 90, S 70 to 255, V 5 to 255 [PAGE 6]. Those are native HSV channel values, not SI units.

The homography relation is:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
x \\ y \\ z
\end{bmatrix}
\sim
\begin{bmatrix}
h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\
h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\
h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33}
\end{bmatrix}
\cdot
\begin{bmatrix}
X \\ Y \\ Z
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

This is Eq. (2) on [PAGE 2]. The vectors are homogeneous coordinates: \((X,Y,Z)\) is the world or template coordinate and \((x,y,z)\) is the image coordinate. The symbol \(\sim\) means equality up to scale. The paper states that after normalization by \(z\) and \(Z\), the homography has eight unknown parameters, so four intersection pairs are sufficient [PAGE 2]. The entries of \(H\) are dimensionless up to the global scale absorbed by normalization; if the world template is metric, the template coordinates carry a metre scale, but the projective mapping itself is scale-normalized. Assumptions: at least four non-collinear correspondences, no three collinear in either point set, and correspondences that actually refer to the same template intersections. Singularity and breakdown regimes: fewer than four pairs gives an underdetermined system; collinear or duplicate intersections produce a rank-deficient or ill-conditioned configuration; large intersection displacement produces a biased \(H\); a singular \(H\) cannot be inverted for image-to-template projection. The paper’s failure statement is explicit: “When the line detection fails, a homography matrix is not calculated due to the lack of the number of intersections” [PAGE 2].

The white-line-rate operator is:

\[
\text{WhiteLineRate}
=
\frac{\text{Number of pixels on the white line}}
{\text{Number of all pixels}}.
\]

This is Eq. (3) on [PAGE 3]. It is dimensionless and bounded in principle between 0 and 1. The paper says, “When the selected line of the white line rate is less than 80%, this line is removed from SL” [PAGE 3]. Assumption: a valid field line should have most of its pixels on white markings. Breakdown: if the line passes through white advertising boards, player kits, goal nets, or other white objects, the rate can be high for a non-field line; if shadow or blur reduces whiteness, a real field line can be removed. The denominator is zero if the candidate line has no sampled pixels, making the rate undefined.

The homography evaluation function is:

\[
E =
\frac{
C(\mathrm{AND}(W^0,W^n))
-
\alpha C(\mathrm{XOR}(W^0,W^n))
}{
C(W^0)
}.
\]

This is Eq. (4) on [PAGE 4]. \(W^0\) is the white-line image from the input field, \(W^n\) is the binary image generated by the \(n\)-th homography, \(C(\cdot)\) counts non-zero pixels, and \(\alpha\) is a weighting factor. The output is dimensionless because it is a ratio of pixel counts. Assumption: the projected template should overlap the observed white lines without painting the whole image white. Breakdown: if \(C(W^0)=0\), the denominator is zero; if \(W^n\) is all white, the AND term can be large, which is why the XOR penalty exists; if non-field white pixels dominate \(W^0\), the evaluator can reward a wrong homography. The excerpt does not state the numerical value of \(\alpha\), so no numerical claim about that weight should be made from the supplied pages.

The IoU metric is:

\[
\mathrm{IoU}
=
\frac{
C(\mathrm{AND}(I_{GT},I_{pred}))
}{
C(\mathrm{OR}(I_{GT},I_{pred}))
}.
\]

This is Eq. (5) on [PAGE 6]. \(I_{GT}\) is the projection using the ground-truth inverse homography, and \(I_{pred}\) is the projection using the estimated inverse homography. IoU is dimensionless and in the interval from 0 to 1 when the denominator is non-zero. Assumption: the ground-truth homography and the estimated homography are compared on the same image domain. Breakdown: if both masks are empty, the denominator is zero and IoU is undefined; if the evaluation image is dominated by the template-to-image or image-to-template convention, a high IoU can still hide local displacement. The paper defines high-IoU images by “When IoU is greater than 0.95, we define it as a high IoU image” [PAGE 6]. It also warns that “When IoU is higher than 0.9, it is difficult to identify the subjective difference in I2T by the comparison of real field lines and the field template” [PAGE 7], so T2I inspection is required for small displacement.

The paper reports no Shannon entropy, probability mass, or information-theoretic unit. Therefore no bit or nat units are assigned. The only empirical proportions are dimensionless: for example, Table 3 reports high-IoU counts of 2/33, 25/33, and 26/33, with mIoU 0.973 and 0.975 [PAGE 6]. Table 4 reports 18/33 without intersection refinement and 26/33 with refinement, both at mIoU 0.974 [PAGE 7]. The patch size is explicitly “127 × 127 pixels” [PAGE 5], and the selected-line and intersection counts are “sixteen and four, respectively” [PAGE 6]. Angular thresholds are reported in native algorithm units: \(T_{\theta1}=0.05\), \(T_{\rho1}=15\), \(T_{\theta2}=0.26\), \(T_{\rho2}=10\), and \(\tau=5^\circ\) [PAGE 6]. The excerpt does not label the \(\rho\) thresholds as metres, so they should be treated as detector-native distance units rather than SI lengths.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from object-based calibration, exemplified by Zhang’s flexible calibration technique, to a priori information-based sports calibration, where the field itself is the calibration target. The paper places object-based calibration in contrast with a priori methods: “The a priori information-based method can calibrate cameras without the calibration object” [PAGE 1], but also notes that its accuracy is inferior because feature extraction is difficult [PAGE 1]. The immediate ancestor is Yao et al., which classifies detected lines into horizontal and vertical directions and finds intersections from their combinations [PAGE 2]. That design is simple but brittle: the number of intersection candidates is limited by the horizontal-vertical pairing, and intersection displacement directly perturbs the homography. The paper’s retrocausal move is to loosen the selection: use more lines, match intersections to the template, and then refine the local intersection position.

The lineage also includes Homayounfar et al.’s vanishing-point approach and Chen et al.’s learning-based method. The paper notes that Homayounfar’s method is inferior because “the homography matrix is estimated by two vanishing points” and small displacement has a significant effect [PAGE 2]. Chen’s method is strong but requires training data, and the experiments use 209 training images [PAGE 6]. The proposed method is positioned as a non-learning alternative that is competitive with Chen: Table 3 gives 26/33 high-IoU images and mIoU 0.975 for the proposed method versus 25/33 and 0.973 for Chen [PAGE 6]. The historical lesson is that the field-line approach did not disappear when deep learning arrived; it was re-engineered around better correspondence selection and local geometric refinement.

The multi-week trap is deployment drift. A team could spend weeks tuning HSV thresholds and line parameters on one stadium, then transfer them to another country and fail because “the color of the field is different for each stadium and country” [PAGE 6]. A second trap is optimizing the wrong metric. If the team watches only mIoU, it may remove intersection refinement and conclude that nothing changed, even though the high-IoU count drops from 26/33 to 18/33 [PAGE 7]. A third trap is mistaking the method’s domain for all sports scenes. The method assumes the penalty area is present [PAGE 2] and uses side information to distinguish left and right half-field views [PAGE 4]. Without those constraints, the correspondence search is not the same problem. A fourth trap is runtime: the paper omits running-time evaluation and says the method is slower than Yao because of the large number of intersection pairs, with GPU implementation left as future work [PAGE 7]. That is a clock trap: an offline accuracy gain can be irrelevant for live free-viewpoint synthesis unless the parallelizable parts are actually accelerated.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the operator’s default under time pressure: revert to a calibration object, revert to Yao-style horizontal/vertical intersection pairing, or manually tune Hough parameters per image. The paper’s counter-trap is to keep the no-object constraint but change the correspondence policy: flexible intersection selection increases candidate pairs, and intersection refinement reduces displacement before the final homography is accepted. The operator’s recursive model of the evaluator matters because the paper’s own metrics can disagree. A mean-IoU evaluator sees 0.974 with and without refinement and may skip the refinement stage; a high-IoU-count evaluator sees 18/33 versus 26/33 and keeps it [PAGE 7]. A template-to-image evaluator sees small displacement that an image-to-template IoU above 0.9 can hide [PAGE 7]. Thus the correct counter-trap is not “add more computation”; it is “add the right verification layer”: high-IoU count, T2I inspection, and a check that the penalty area and side information assumptions are satisfied.

The clock is the third pole. The proposed method is computationally heavier than Yao’s method because it evaluates many intersection pairs, and the paper explicitly says runtime evaluation is omitted and GPU implementation is future work [PAGE 7]. In a crisis, the operator may therefore regress to the faster but less accurate method. The recursive theory of mind should predict that regression: if the system is judged by live latency alone, the refinement stage will be dropped even when it is the part that rescues low-IoU images. The counter-trap is to separate offline calibration from online tracking: use the slower flexible matching and refinement when recalibrating after a camera disturbance, then use the fixed homography for live player projection. The paper’s target use case supports this: re-calibration is needed after an accidental touch of the camera position and settings, and short running time is desirable [PAGE 1]. The clock veto is real, but it applies to the calibration phase, not necessarily to every video frame after calibration.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer vetoes are explicit in the paper. First, camera disturbance: the motivation is that re-calibration is required “due to an accidental touch of the camera position and settings” [PAGE 1]. If the camera pose changes after calibration, the homography is invalid regardless of how good the intersection matching was. Second, field-color veto: the HSV mask Eq. (1) and the Table 1 thresholds are stadium-dependent, and the paper warns that parameters need adjustment for each video because field color differs by stadium and country [PAGE 6]. If lighting, weather, or shadow pushes the field outside the threshold, line extraction loses evidence. Third, white-object veto: the paper shows overdetection from white pixels other than field lines and states that this causes calibration failure [PAGE 7], with some images below 0.7 IoU because non-field lines are extracted [PAGE 9]. Fourth, geometry veto: the method assumes the penalty area is present [PAGE 2] and needs at least four valid intersection pairs. If the visible field structure is too small, occluded, or outside the template, the homography is underdetermined. Fifth, local-patch veto: the refinement patch is 127 by 127 pixels [PAGE 5]; if the patch does not contain the real intersection or the white-line boundary is destroyed, the refinement cannot recover the true position.

The biomechanical layer is not modeled in the paper. The closest biomechanical surrogate is the player-position error budget: the paper says the required accuracy is two or three pixels in the input image, and if the actual and virtual cameras have the same distance from the player’s position, the generated free-viewpoint video has less than two or three pixels’ displacement of the player’s position, which is negligible in the video representation [PAGE 7]. That is the veto proof for downstream use: if calibration displacement exceeds the pixel budget, player projection and free-viewpoint synthesis are not physically trustworthy even if the aggregate IoU looks acceptable. Weather is not simulated here, but it acts through the same physical channel as lighting and field appearance: it changes the HSV distribution and the white-line contrast, which vetoes Eq. (1) and Eq. (3) before Eq. (2) is reached. Therefore the cross-layer adjudication is: no field evidence, no line evidence, no intersection evidence, no homography. The paper’s own failures are consistent with this chain.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: ablate intersection refinement while keeping line selection and point matching fixed. The paper’s Table 4 is the verifiable counterfactual: without refinement, 18/33 high-IoU images and mIoU 0.974; with refinement, 26/33 high-IoU images and mIoU 0.974 [PAGE 7]. Prediction: the mean metric is nearly unchanged, but the high-IoU count increases by 8 images. Veto condition: if the reward is the high-IoU count or tail robustness, the no-refinement variant must be rejected. This is verifiable by rerunning the same 33 images and counting IoU greater than 0.95.

Scenario 2: replace flexible intersection selection with the conventional horizontal-vertical intersection classification associated with Yao et al. The paper reports Yao et al. at 2/33 high-IoU images, while the proposed method is 26/33 with mIoU 0.975 [PAGE 6]. Prediction: the conventional selection will collapse on the same penalty-area test set because the number of available intersection pairs is too small and because Yao’s method needs parameter adjustment for each input image [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if the deployment requires the high-IoU tail, the conventional-selection variant is vetoed. This is verifiable by the same high-IoU count and by checking whether four or more valid pairs survive for each image.

Scenario 3: remove the penalty-area assumption or feed an image with insufficient visible intersections. The paper states the penalty area is assumed to be contained in the target video [PAGE 2], and the experiments use 33 images containing the penalty area because the method needs more than four points [PAGE 6]. Prediction: the proposed and Yao-style methods fail when fewer than four intersection pairs are available, whereas Chen’s learning-based method can still operate because it does not have the same four-point requirement [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: if the target domain lacks a penalty area or reliable field-line intersections, the method is vetoed before accuracy comparison. This is verifiable by constructing a no-penalty-area subset and checking for homography failure or IoU below the 0.95 high-IoU threshold.

A fourth verifiable stress case, closely related to the third, is non-field white-pixel overdetection. The paper states that some input images are evaluated below 0.7 IoU “because the real line extraction extracts lines other than real lines” [PAGE 9]. Prediction: adding advertising boards, white kits, or goal-net pixels to the field image will increase false line detections, corrupt Eq. (3) and Eq. (4), and reduce high-IoU count. Veto condition: if the physical scene contains dominant white non-field structures, the current line-extraction operator must be replaced or masked before the homography is trusted. All three primary vetoes are grounded in the paper’s own reported metrics and failure statements, so they are verifiable rather than speculative.
