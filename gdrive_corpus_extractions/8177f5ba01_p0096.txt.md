Hull describes B.F. Skinner's experiment where a rat is first conditioned to associate an auditory click (secondary reinforcing stimulus, $S_{sr}$) with food delivery ($R_0$). The rat spontaneously presses a bar, which triggers the click but no food. When the environment transitions to Phase 2 (empty magazine), the conditional probability $P(R_0 | \text{Click})$ drops to zero, but the internal model of the rat has not yet updated. The prediction error (or surprise), defined as the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the expected distribution and the observed outcome, drives learning. However, before the model fully collapses, the occurrence of the click still generates a transient positive scalar signal—a pseudo-reward—because it momentarily activates the entrenched neural pathways associated with high-value outcomes.

Crucially, the mechanism implies a temporal binding window. Any motor primitive executed within a specific backward-looking integration kernel preceding the click receives a gradient update proportional to this pseudo-reward. In Hull’s framework, this is driven by the interaction between Drive reduction expectations and Cue potency. If $S_{sr}$ occurs at $t^*$, the weight update $\Delta \theta$ for action $M_{t^* - \delta}$ depends on the product of the secondary drive $sD_r$ and the habit strength $sH_r$. The gray zone lies in the transition regime where the system is neither purely driven by primary reinforcement nor entirely governed by the extinction of the secondary cue. It is a metastable state. If the click were presented randomly without the bar press, the rat would simply exhibit searching behavior (as per Phase 1 training). $$ sE_r = D_b \times V_{sr} \times K_{eff} $$
Where $D_b$ is the primary physiological drive (e.g., hunger, measured in units of caloric deficit or molarity of glucose deviation), $V_{sr}$ is the association strength of the secondary stimulus (dimensionless, $[0,1]$), and $K_{eff}$ is an efficiency constant. **2. Temporal Binding and Habit Strength Update ($\Delta _sH_R$):**

$$ \Delta _sH_R = C \cdot (sE_r)^n \cdot f(t_{bind}) $$
Here, $_sH_R$ is the acquired neural connection strength (measured in electrochemical synaptic efficacy units, e.g., nmol Ca$^{2+}$/m$^2$ membrane area). $C$ is a capacity constant. The exponent $n$ ($0 < n \le 1$) accounts for diminishing returns on drive magnitude. 
The term $f(t_{bind})$ represents a Gaussian decay kernel modeling the temporal window of association:
$$ f(t_{bind}) = \exp\left(-\frac{(t_{click} - t_{press})^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) $$
where $\sigma$ dictates the temporal resolution of causal attribution (typically tens to hundreds of milliseconds in rodents).

**3. Information-Theoretic Breakdown Regime:**
Secondary reinforcement relies on the preservation of mutual information between the stimulus and the ultimate reward. We define the informational validity of the secondary stimulus as:
$$ I(S_{sr}; R_0) = H(S_{sr}) - H(S_{sr} | R_0) $$
During Phase 2 of the experiment described in [PAGE 1], $P(R_0 | S_{sr}) \to 0$. Consequently, the mutual information decays exponentially toward zero. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The concept of secondary reinforcement detailed on [PAGE 1] does not emerge in isolation; it is the culmination of a retrocausal lineage stretching from late 19th-century neurophysiology to mid-20th-century cognitive revolution. 

**Scientific Lineage:**
1. **Pavlovian Foundations (Late 1800s - Early 1900s):** Ivan Pavlov demonstrated that a neutral stimulus (bell) could evoke a conditioned response (salivation) when paired with an unconditioned stimulus (meat powder). However, Pavlov primarily focused on the elicitation of reflexive responses, not instrumental actions. The question posed by Hull—"whether the reaction involved... must necessarily be the same"—is a direct departure from strict Pavlovian contiguity.
2. **Thorndike's Law of Effect (1898):** Edward Thorndike introduced the idea that behaviors followed by satisfying states of affairs become more likely. While lacking the explicit secondary stimulus variable, it established the ground rules for operant modification.
3. **Skinner’s Operant Chamber (1930s-40s):** As cited by Hull in [PAGE 1] ("Skinner (9, p. 82)"), B.F. Skinner operationalized the isolation of behavior. The invention of the "Skinner box" allowed for the precise timing of stimuli (like the magazine click) and responses (bar pressing). This technological advancement permitted the mathematical formalization of behavior that Hull was attempting.
4. **Hullian Deductive Systems (1943):** Clark L. Hull sought to synthesize these empirical streams into a rigid system of postulates and definitions. The analysis of secondary reinforcement on Page 87 of his *Principles of Behavior* serves as a bridge, explicitly acknowledging that environmental cues acquire motivational properties independent of the original primary drive mechanisms.

**Multi-Week Traps & Historical Blind Spots:**
A critical trap in this genealogical line is the "behaviorist blind spot" regarding cognitive maps. For decades, researchers interpreted Hull's and Skinner's findings strictly through mechanistic stimulus-response (S-R) bonds. The multi-week trap here was the assumption that the click merely triggered a localized spinal or subcortical reflex arc that mechanically "glued" itself to the preceding muscle contraction. It took Tolman's purposive behaviorism (late 1940s) and later Dopamine Reward Prediction Error theories (Schultz et al., 1997) to reveal that the click actually updates a global cognitive map of value. The historical trap was believing the mechanism was local and mechanical, whereas modern neuroscience shows it is holistic, involving widespread neuromodulatory cascades (dopamine/acetylcholine) that tag diverse neural ensembles active during the window $f(t_{bind})$.

While Skinner boxes are climate-controlled, in naturalistic settings, environmental physics can veto reinforcement loops. Imagine the experiment conducted in extreme cold with high humidity. Condensation forms on the horizontal brass bar.

Furthermore, extreme low temperatures increase the viscosity of synovial fluid in the rat's joints, slowing down the rapid "standing up" movement required to interact with the bar. The temporal window $f(t_{bind})$ is violated not because the rat lacks motivation, but because the physical body cannot execute the kinematics fast enough to register as a valid precursor to the auditory stimulus.
