The second inference chain concerns the rest-to-activity transition. The guideline recommends “an initial 24-48 hour period of rest with limited physical and cognitive activity” [PAGE 6]. Thus the guideline is not a single linear protocol; it is a multi-domain clock with different subsystem timers.

The fourth inference chain concerns medical clearance. The source states that full-contact sport or high-risk activity return is conditional: the child must have returned to school full-time without concussion-related accommodations, have a normal neurological and cervical spine examination, be asymptomatic at rest or back to pre-injury baseline, tolerate physical and non-contact activity without symptoms, and not be using substances that mask symptoms [PAGE 31]. This is a conjunctive gate, not a weighted score. If any criterion fails, the veto remains. The guideline also notes: “Medical clearance is not required to return to school” [PAGE 13]. This distinction is crucial: school return is prioritized, while full-contact return is gated by medical clearance and completion of a return-to-sport protocol.

The fifth inference chain concerns telemedicine and biomarkers. Telemedicine is allowed only under constrained conditions. The source says in-person assessment is ideal, and virtual care may be considered when a prior medical assessment has been performed, access is limited, and there is no history of abnormal diagnostic imaging [PAGE 36]. It explicitly lists virtual examination limits: motor, sensory and vestibular functioning; palpation and provocative testing of the cervical spine; visual acuity and deep tendon reflexes; fundoscopic and otoscopic examinations “cannot be performed virtually” [PAGE 37]. Biomarkers are similarly constrained: advanced neuroimaging biomarkers “are not yet ready for clinical implementation/management” [PAGE 63], and serologic biomarkers are not clinically indicated because “there is no validated ‘concussion blood test’” [PAGE 64]. These statements create epistemic vetoes: virtual care cannot replace necessary physical examination, and biomarkers cannot replace clinical assessment.

The gray-zone audit must also address baseline testing. The guideline says baseline testing “is not recommended or required for concussion diagnosis or management following an injury” [PAGE 33]. It adds: “The current evidence does not support an added benefit of baseline testing” [PAGE 33]. This is a trap because baseline testing can create false reassurance, invalid comparisons, or unnecessary restriction. Another trap is medication overuse: over-the-counter analgesics should be limited to “less than 15 days a month” and “around-the-clock” dosing avoided [PAGE 6]. A third trap is premature return to contact: “returning to full-contact sport or high-risk activities before the child/adolescent has recovered increases the risk of delayed recovery and for sustaining another more severe concussion or more serious injury” [PAGE 22]. A fourth trap is prolonged school absence: the source warns against letting the child “settle into the habit” of missing school [PAGE 60]. The final gray zone is environmental: weather is not encoded in the guideline, so any weather veto must be treated as an external physical hazard layer rather than a source-derived operator.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let injury time be \(t = 0\). Time is measured in hours \(h\) or days \(d\). Let \(A(t)\) denote activity intensity, normalized to the interval \([0,1]\), where \(0\) is complete rest and \(1\) is maximal pre-injury cognitive or physical exertion. Let \(B(t)\) denote symptom burden, expressed in ordinal clinical units such as symptom count, severity rating, or standardized inventory score. Let \(p\) denote a probability, dimensionless, with \(p \in [0,1]\). Let \(I\) denote information in bits when using base-2 logarithms.

1. Acute rest operator.
The guideline specifies an initial rest window of 24 to 48 hours: “Recommend an initial 24-48 hour period of rest with limited physical and cognitive activity” [PAGE 6]. A formal rest constraint is:
\[
T_{\text{rest}} \in [24,48]\ \text{h}
\]
\[
\int_{0}^{T_{\text{rest}}} A(t)\,dt \le A_{\text{rest}}\,T_{\text{rest}}
\]
where \(A_{\text{rest}}\) is a low normalized activity ceiling. Assumption: \(A(t)\) is measurable or clinically approximable, and the patient is not in a red-flag state. Breakdown regime: if any red flag is present, the equation is inadmissible because emergency assessment supersedes activity dosing [PAGE 16].

2. Prolonged-rest hazard operator.
The source explicitly rejects extended rest: “Prolonged rest beyond the first 24-48 hours after a concussion is not recommended and may cause more harm than good” [PAGE 18]. Define an over-rest hazard:
\[
H_{\text{rest}}(t) =
\begin{cases}
0, & t \le 48\ \text{h} \\
1, & t > 48\ \text{h and activity remains restricted without clinical rationale}
\end{cases}
\]
This is a logical hazard operator, not a calibrated risk score. Breakdown regime: if prolonged rest is medically justified by red flags, structural injury, cervical spine injury, or severe symptom instability, the operator is overridden by the emergency or structural-injury layer [PAGE 16], [PAGE 20].

3. Graduated activity constraint.
The guideline recommends gradual activity “at a level that does not result in recurrence or exacerbation of symptoms” [PAGE 22]. Let \(\Delta B\) be the change in symptom burden over a monitoring interval \(\Delta t\):
\[
\Delta B = B(t+\Delta t)-B(t)
\]
The clinical tolerance constraint is:
\[
\Delta B \le \varepsilon
\]
where \(\varepsilon\) is a patient-specific allowable symptom fluctuation. Units: \(B\) is in ordinal symptom units; \(\varepsilon\) is in the same ordinal units. Assumption: symptoms are measured consistently across intervals. Breakdown regime: if \(\Delta B > \varepsilon\) repeatedly, the activity prescription must be reduced. If red flags emerge, the pathway exits to emergency assessment [PAGE 16].

4. Prolonged-symptom referral operator.
The guideline uses 4 weeks as the prolonged-symptom boundary: “Refer to specialized care with an interdisciplinary concussion team if post-concussion symptoms do not gradually resolve by 4 weeks” [PAGE 29]. Let \(I_d(t)\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether domain \(d\) is symptomatic at time \(t\). Domain examples include headache, sleep, mental health, cognition, vision, vestibular, oculomotor, fatigue, and school difficulty [PAGE 4]. Define unresolved domain persistence:
\[
P_d = \mathbf{1}\left[ \exists\, t \ge 28\ \text{d} : I_d(t)=1 \right]
\]
If \(P_d=1\) for any clinically meaningful domain, then referral to specialized interdisciplinary care is indicated. Assumption: symptom domain is correctly classified and not wholly explained by an alternative diagnosis. Breakdown regime: if a red flag, focal neurological deficit, or worrisome imaging finding is present, urgent MRI or structural evaluation may be required rather than routine 4-week referral [PAGE 28].

5. Risk-score operator for prolonged symptoms.
The guideline recommends using a clinical risk score to predict prolonged symptoms and links to the 5P score [PAGE 19]. A formal probability model is:
\[
p_{\text{persistent}} = \Pr(\text{prolonged symptoms} \mid z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_k)
\]
A conventional logistic form is:
\[
p_{\text{persistent}} =
\frac{1}{1+\exp\left[-\left(\beta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta_i z_i\right)\right]}
\]
where \(p_{\text{persistent}}\) is dimensionless, \(z_i\) are modifier variables, and \(\beta_i\) are calibrated coefficients. The source lists modifiers including age, female sex, duration of recovery from previous concussion, high pre-injury symptom burden, high symptom burden at presentation, vestibular or oculomotor dysfunction, migraine history, learning or behavioural difficulties, mental health history, and family socioeconomic status or education [PAGE 19], [PAGE 20]. Assumption: the score is calibrated for the relevant pediatric population and all modifiers are measured. Breakdown regime: the score is not valid if red flags dominate, if structural injury is suspected, if data are missing, if the patient is outside the guideline age scope, or if the score is applied outside its derivation context. The guideline itself says validated tools “are meant to assist but not replace clinical judgment” in the context of imaging decision rules [PAGE 20].

6. Diagnostic information-gain operator.
Follow-up assessments can be interpreted as information-acquisition actions. Let \(Y\) be the latent clinical state, such as recovery trajectory or structural injury presence, and let \(H\) be the history or examination evidence. The expected information gain is:
\[
I(Y;H) =
\sum_{h}\sum_{y}
p(h)\,p(y\mid h)
\log_2\left(
\frac{p(y\mid h)}{p(y)}
\right)
\]
Units: bits. Assumption: probabilities are defined over clinically meaningful states. Breakdown regime: if red flags are present, the optimal action is not information gain but immediate emergency evaluation [PAGE 16]. If probabilities cannot be estimated because of missing data or poor measurement, the information value is undefined rather than zero.

7. Emergency red-flag veto.
Let \(V_{\text{red}}\) be a binary veto operator:
\[
V_{\text{red}} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if any red flag is present} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
Red flags include severe or increasing headache, neck pain or tenderness, double vision, weakness or numbness, seizure, loss of consciousness, deteriorating conscious state, repeated vomiting, agitation or combativeness, and slurred speech [PAGE 16]. If \(V_{\text{red}}=1\), then:
\[
V_{\text{red}}=1 \implies \text{emergency medical assessment required}
\]
and routine graduated return-to-activity is suspended until emergency evaluation is completed.

8. Structural-injury imaging veto.
The guideline states: “Consider CT of the brain or cervical spine only in patients whom, after a medical assessment, a structural intracranial or cervical spine injury is suspected; do not conduct routine neuroimaging for the purpose of diagnosing concussion” [PAGE 5]. Let \(V_{\text{CT}}\) be a binary imaging-veto operator:
\[
V_{\text{CT}} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if clinical decision rule or clinical judgment indicates possible structural injury} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
Decision tools include PECARN, CATCH2, and minor-head-trauma algorithms [PAGE 20]. Assumption: the tool is applied within its validated population. Breakdown regime: clinical judgment overrides tool omission when structural injury is suspected; the tools “are meant to assist but not replace clinical judgment” [PAGE 20].

9. Contact-activity veto and clearance gate.
The guideline says patients should avoid “activities associated with a risk of contact, fall, or collisions such as high speed and/or contact activities and full-contact sport” during recovery [PAGE 6]. Let \(A_{\text{contact}}\) denote an activity class with contact, fall, or collision risk. Define:
\[
V_{\text{contact}}(a) =
\begin{cases}
1, & a \in A_{\text{contact}} \\
0, & a \notin A_{\text{contact}}
\end{cases}
\]
Medical clearance is a conjunctive gate. Let \(C_{\text{clear}}\) be 1 only if all criteria are met: full return to school without concussion-related accommodations, normal neurological and cervical spine examination, asymptomatic at rest or back to pre-injury baseline, tolerance of physical and non-contact activity without symptoms, and no masking substances [PAGE 31]. Then:
\[
\text{Full-contact permitted} \iff
C_{\text{clear}}=1
\ \land\
V_{\text{red}}=0
\]
This is a logical permission, not a probabilistic prediction. Breakdown regime: if any clearance criterion fails, full-contact permission remains false.

10. Telemedicine physical-examination veto.
Let \(V_{\text{tele}}\) be a binary veto indicating that a required physical examination component cannot be adequately assessed virtually. The source says certain aspects “cannot be performed virtually,” including assessment of motor, sensory and vestibular functioning; palpation and provocative testing of the cervical spine; testing of visual acuity and deep tendon reflexes; as well as fundoscopic and otoscopic examinations [PAGE 37]. Formal rule:
\[
V_{\text{tele}}=1
\implies
\text{in-person assessment required if clinically necessary}
\]
Breakdown regime: virtual assessment is invalid when visual quality or environment prevents safe examination. The source notes that “the visual quality of in-person videoconferencing and environmental factors” may limit examination components [PAGE 147].

11. Environmental and weather veto layer.
The source does not provide a weather variable, weather threshold, or meteorological unit. Therefore, under source-only inference:
\[
V_{\text{weather}} = \text{undefined}
\]
If an external weather hazard model is introduced, one could write:
\[
V_{\text{weather}} =
\begin{cases}
1, & W \ge W^{*} \\
0, & W < W^{*}
\end{cases}
\]
where \(W\) is a dimensionless environmental hazard probability and \(W^{*}\) is an externally validated threshold. However, \(W\) and \(W^{*}\) are not supplied by the guideline. The only environmental factors explicitly recognized by the source are examination-room factors for virtual care, such as lighting and room configuration [PAGE 147]. Thus, any weather veto must be marked as an external assumption, not a guideline operator.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The guideline’s lineage is not a single algorithm but a convergence of recognition tools, risk scores, imaging decision rules, return-to-learn strategies, and virtual-care adaptations. The document is a living guideline from the Ontario Neurotrauma Foundation, last updated March 1, 2021 [PAGE 1]. Its structure reflects a shift from isolated concussion diagnosis toward a multi-domain management ecosystem. The recognition layer descends from community tools such as the Concussion Recognition Tool 5 and sport tools such as SCAT5 and Child SCAT5 [PAGE 15], [PAGE 17], [PAGE 20]. The risk-stratification layer descends from clinical risk scores such as the 5P score, which is linked as a score calculator for predicting persistent post-concussive problems [PAGE 19]. The imaging layer descends from pediatric head-trauma decision rules such as PECARN and CATCH2 [PAGE 20]. The return-to-school layer descends from educational accommodation models and school-based concussion management teams [PAGE 60], [PAGE 61]. The telemedicine layer descends from rural-access, teleneurology, telepsychiatry, and pandemic-era virtual-care expansion [PAGE 35].

A retro-causal reading shows why certain temporal traps persist. The 24-48 hour rest window is historically corrective. Older concussion culture often prescribed prolonged dark-room rest or extended withdrawal from school and sport. The guideline reverses that trap by stating that prolonged rest “may cause more harm than good” [PAGE 18]. However, the same document preserves an initial rest period to avoid acute symptom exacerbation [PAGE 6]. The multi-week trap is therefore not a contradiction but a transition problem: clinicians must convert brief rest into graded activity before deconditioning and avoidance become entrenched.

Another lineage is the 4-week prolonged-symptom boundary. The source says most children recover within 1-4 weeks, but some have symptoms at one month and beyond [PAGE 18]. This creates a referral trap if clinicians wait too long. The guideline counters with early referral when modifiers are present [PAGE 30] and with domain-specific escalation for headache, sleep, mental health, cognition, vision, vestibular function, fatigue, and school difficulties [PAGE 44], [PAGE 47], [PAGE 49], [PAGE 51], [PAGE 53], [PAGE 58], [PAGE 61]. A third lineage is baseline-testing skepticism. The guideline says baseline testing is not recommended or required and that current evidence does not support added benefit [PAGE 33]. This is historically important because baseline testing can create a false sense of precision. The guideline instead relies on post-injury assessment, clinical history, symptom burden, modifiers, and individualized follow-up.

The sleep subsystem has its own genealogy. Sleep disturbance is treated as both a symptom and a recovery modifier. The guideline says sleep disturbances after concussion can be a risk factor for prolonged recovery [PAGE 46]. It then layers interventions: sleep hygiene and non-pharmacological strategies [PAGE 46], cognitive behavioural therapy and supplements after 4 weeks [PAGE 47], sleep-specialist referral after 6 weeks [PAGE 48], and short-term medication if sleep has not improved after 6 weeks [PAGE 48]. This nested clock reflects the scientific lineage of concussion recovery as a multi-system process rather than a single neurological event.

The telemedicine lineage is also corrective. It attempts to preserve access while acknowledging physical-examination limits. The source says preliminary research supports timely access for carefully selected patients in underserved regions [PAGE 35]. But it also says virtual care cannot perform motor, sensory, vestibular, cervical palpation, visual acuity, reflex, fundoscopic, or otoscopic assessment [PAGE 37]. The multi-week trap here is substitution: treating a virtual encounter as equivalent to an in-person assessment when red flags or objective deficits are present. The guideline blocks that trap by requiring in-person care when virtual examination is insufficient [PAGE 39].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The guideline implicitly models several interacting minds: the child or adolescent, the parent or caregiver, the coach or teacher, the clinician, and the school or sport organization. Each agent has beliefs about risk, recovery, and permission. The central crisis is not only physiological; it is a recursive belief problem. The child may believe that any symptom means damage. The parent may believe that rest is protective. The coach may believe that return to play is a test of toughness. The clinician must align these beliefs with the guideline’s staged risk gates.

The habit-regression trap occurs when early protective behavior becomes a persistent habit. After injury, the child rests, misses school, avoids screens, avoids peers, and avoids physical activity. Initially, this is consistent with the 24-48 hour rest recommendation [PAGE 6]. But if rest persists beyond 48 hours without a graduated plan, the system can enter a regression loop: reduced activity worsens mood, sleep, fatigue, and school tolerance, which then appear as ongoing concussion symptoms. The guideline counters this with early low-risk activity: “Activities that pose no/low risk of sustaining a concussion should be resumed even if mild residual symptoms are present” [PAGE 22]. It also advises maintaining social networks beyond the brief initial rest period [PAGE 23]. The counter-trap is therefore not “push through symptoms”; it is “resume low-risk, sub-threshold activity before avoidance becomes entrenched.”

The counter-trap must itself be audited. If the child, parent, or coach interprets “activity as tolerated” as permission to return to contact sport, the recursive belief system becomes unsafe. The guideline separates low-risk activity from high-risk activity. It says patients should avoid “activities associated with a risk of contact, fall, or collisions” during recovery [PAGE 6]. It also requires full return to school without accommodations before full-contact clearance [PAGE 31]. Thus the recursive model must distinguish two belief objects: “activity is allowed” and “contact is allowed.” The first can be true while the second remains false.

The clock is a third agent in the recursive system. The guideline uses multiple clocks: 24-48 hours for initial rest, 1-2 weeks for medical follow-up, 4 weeks for prolonged-symptom referral, 6 weeks for persistent sleep disturbance, and 15 days per month for analgesic limitation [PAGE 6], [PAGE 7], [PAGE 29], [PAGE 48]. These clocks prevent two opposite errors. The first error is premature escalation to full-contact activity. The second error is indefinite waiting. The guideline’s theory of mind is therefore temporal: beliefs must be updated at specific checkpoints, not only when crisis occurs.

A recursive belief audit can be stated as follows. If the child believes symptoms are dangerous, the clinician should provide education and graded activity. If the parent believes rest is protective, the clinician should explain that prolonged rest may cause harm [PAGE 18]. If the coach believes return to play is safe because symptoms are mild, the clinician should invoke the clearance gate [PAGE 31]. If the teacher believes school absence is necessary, the clinician should recommend a stepwise return-to-school plan with accommodations [PAGE 60]. If the patient believes a virtual visit is sufficient, the clinician should invoke the virtual-examination limits [PAGE 37]. The guideline is therefore not merely a medical protocol; it is a model for correcting recursive misunderstandings under uncertainty.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The guideline supports a layered veto architecture. Layer 1 is the emergency red-flag layer. Layer 2 is the structural-injury imaging layer. Layer 3 is the cervical-spine and neurological examination layer. Layer 4 is the graduated-activity layer. Layer 5 is the return-to-school layer. Layer 6 is the medical-clearance layer for full-contact or high-risk activity. Layer 7 is the telemedicine adequacy layer. Layer 8 is the external environmental or weather layer, which is not explicitly encoded in the source.

Layer 1 proof.
If any red flag is present, emergency assessment is required: “Recommend an emergency medical assessment for a child/adolescent with any of the ‘red flag’ symptoms” [PAGE 16]. The red-flag list includes severe or increasing headache, neck pain or tenderness, double vision, weakness or numbness, seizure, loss of consciousness, deteriorating conscious state, repeated vomiting, agitation or combativeness, and slurred speech [PAGE 16]. Formal adjudication:
\[
V_{\text{red}}=1
\implies
\text{emergency assessment}
\]
This veto supersedes all lower layers. No graduated activity, telemedicine plan, or return-to-school strategy can be safely finalized while an unevaluated red flag remains.

Layer 2 proof.
Structural injury is addressed through selective imaging. The guideline says CT should be considered only when structural intracranial or cervical spine injury is suspected, and routine neuroimaging should not be used to diagnose concussion [PAGE 5]. It also says most children with acute head injury do not need diagnostic imaging [PAGE 20]. Formal adjudication:
\[
V_{\text{CT}}=1
\implies
\text{urgent structural evaluation}
\]
\[
V_{\text{CT}}=0
\land
V_{\text{red}}=0
\implies
\text{routine concussion pathway may proceed}
\]
Breakdown regime: if clinical suspicion is high despite a negative decision rule, clinical judgment requires escalation. The source says decision tools “are meant to assist but not replace clinical judgment” [PAGE 20].

Layer 3 proof.
The cervical spine is a biomechanical veto layer. The initial assessment includes “A cervical spine examination (palpation, range of motion, provocative cervical spine tests)” [PAGE 20]. The guideline also advises imaging when symptoms suggest structural cervical spine injury, including severe neck pain, tenderness, radiculopathy, or myelopathy [PAGE 21]. Formal adjudication:
\[
V_{\text{cervical}}=1
\implies
\text{avoid contact activity and pursue cervical evaluation}
\]
This is biomechanically necessary because cervical pathology can mimic or complicate concussion and may be worsened by contact or collision.

Layer 4 proof.
Graduated activity is permitted only below the symptom-exacerbation threshold and only for low-risk activities. The guideline says low to moderate activity should begin 24-48 hours after concussion “at a level that does not result in recurrence or exacerbation of symptoms” [PAGE 22]. It also says to avoid activities with risk of contact, fall, or collisions [PAGE 6]. Formal adjudication:
\[
V_{\text{contact}}(a)=1
\implies
a\ \text{is vetoed during recovery}
\]
unless the medical-clearance gate is satisfied.

Layer 5 proof.
Return-to-school is prioritized before full-contact return. The guideline says return-to-school and return-to-sport strategies can be performed simultaneously, but the child should return to school full-time at full academic load before returning to full-contact sport or high-risk activities [PAGE 62]. It also says medical clearance is not required to return to school [PAGE 13]. Formal adjudication:
\[
\text{School return allowed} \not\Rightarrow \text{Full-contact return allowed}
\]
This prevents a common category error: treating educational reintegration as equivalent to sport clearance.

Layer 6 proof.
Full-contact clearance is a conjunctive gate. The criteria are: successful return to all school activities without concussion-related accommodations, normal neurological and cervical spine examination, asymptomatic at rest or back to pre-injury baseline, tolerance of physical and non-contact activity without symptoms, and no masking substances [PAGE 31]. Formal adjudication:
\[
C_{\text{clear}}=1
\iff
\bigwedge_{j=1}^{5} c_j=1
\]
where \(c_j\) are the five criteria. If any \(c_j=0\), then \(C_{\text{clear}}=0\). If \(C_{\text{clear}}=0\), full-contact activity remains vetoed.

Layer 7 proof.
Telemedicine cannot override physical-examination necessity. The source says virtual physical examination should assess mental status, speech, cranial nerves, coordination, balance, gait, cognition, oculomotor functioning, and cervical spine where possible [PAGE 37]. But it also says motor, sensory and vestibular functioning; cervical palpation and provocative testing; visual acuity and deep tendon reflexes; fundoscopic and otoscopic examinations cannot be performed virtually [PAGE 37]. Formal adjudication:
\[
V_{\text{tele}}=1
\land
\text{clinical need for missing examination}
\implies
\text{in-person assessment required}
\]
This layer prevents virtual care from masking physical deficits.

Layer 8 proof: weather and environment.
The source does not define a weather veto. It does recognize environmental limitations for virtual examination: “Due to conditions such as the visual quality of in-person videoconferencing and environmental factors” some virtual tests may not be performable [PAGE 147]. Therefore, an environment veto can be defined for virtual examination adequacy:
\[
V_{\text{env}}=1
\implies
\text{virtual examination invalid}
\]
if lighting, room size, camera quality, or configuration prevents safe assessment [PAGE 147]. A weather veto, however, cannot be derived from the source. If weather is introduced as an external hazard, the correct formal status is:
\[
V_{\text{weather}} = \text{undefined under source-only inference}
\]
Any assertion of a weather veto must cite an external hazard model and cannot be represented as a guideline operator. This is the rigorous cross-layer adjudication: physical red flags and structural injury dominate; cervical biomechanics restrict contact; school return does not imply sport clearance; telemedicine cannot replace required physical examination; weather remains an external, unproven layer unless separately validated.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Red-flag counterfactual.
Counterfactual condition: A child has suspected concussion and reports repeated vomiting, worsening headache, slurred speech, or deteriorating conscious state.
RLVR veto: The system must veto continued sport, continued school without medical review, and any virtual-only management pathway.
Verifiable prediction: Emergency medical assessment is required. The guideline says: “Recommend an emergency medical assessment for a child/adolescent with any of the ‘red flag’ symptoms” [PAGE 16]. If structural intracranial or cervical spine injury is suspected after assessment, CT of the brain or cervical spine should be considered; routine neuroimaging is not for diagnosing concussion [PAGE 5]. Verification: observe whether emergency assessment is triggered and whether return-to-activity is suspended until red flags are evaluated.

Scenario 2: Four-week prolonged-symptom counterfactual.
Counterfactual condition: A child has persistent post-concussion symptoms at day 29 without gradual resolution.
RLVR veto: The system must veto indefinite primary-care-only management without specialty consideration.
Verifiable prediction: Referral to an interdisciplinary concussion team is indicated. The guideline says: “Refer to specialized care with an interdisciplinary concussion team if post-concussion symptoms do not gradually resolve by 4 weeks” [PAGE 29]. Domain-specific escalation follows: headache referral after more than 4 weeks [PAGE 44], sleep management after 4 weeks and sleep-specialist referral after 6 weeks [PAGE 47], [PAGE 48], cognitive difficulties beyond 4 weeks [PAGE 52], vestibular dysfunction beyond 4 weeks [PAGE 57], and fatigue beyond 4 weeks [PAGE 59]. Verification: observe whether referral occurs, whether domain-specific assessment is performed, and whether early referral is used if recovery modifiers are present [PAGE 30].

Scenario 3: Baseline-testing or virtual-clearance counterfactual.
Counterfactual condition: A coach or parent demands baseline testing before a post-injury decision, or requests virtual clearance to full-contact sport without an adequate in-person examination.
RLVR veto: The system must veto the claim that baseline testing is required, and it must veto virtual clearance if necessary physical examination components are missing.
Verifiable prediction: Baseline testing is not recommended or required: “Baseline testing on children/adolescents using concussion assessment tools or tests (or any combination of tests/tools) is not recommended or required for concussion diagnosis or management following an injury” [PAGE 33]. The source also says: “The current evidence does not support an added benefit of baseline testing” [PAGE 33]. For virtual care, the source says certain physical examination components “cannot be performed virtually” [PAGE 37]. Therefore, if red flags, cervical spine concerns, visual deficits, motor or sensory deficits, or need for graded aerobic exercise testing exist, in-person assessment is required [PAGE 37], [PAGE 39]. Verification: observe whether baseline testing is imposed as mandatory, whether clearance is issued virtually despite missing examination components, and whether the guideline’s conjunctive clearance criteria are documented [PAGE 31].
