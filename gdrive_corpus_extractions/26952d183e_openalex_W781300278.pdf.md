<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source is legal-economic rather than mathematical, but its claims can be formalized without inventing pseudo-empirical ratios. The basic monopoly operator is:

\[
\max_{Q}\ \Pi(Q)=P(Q)Q-C(Q), \qquad \text{FOC: } P(Q)+Q\frac{dP}{dQ}=C'(Q).
\]

Here \(P(Q)\) is inverse demand in dollars per unit, \(Q\) is output in units such as tickets, broadcast viewers, or franchises, and \(C(Q)\) is cost in dollars. With downward-sloping demand, \(dP/dQ<0\), so monopoly output is below the competitive level where \(P=C'(Q)\). This formalizes the source’s statement that monopolies produce “higher prices, lower output, and a transfer of wealth from consumers to the producer/monopolist” [PAGE 4].

Consumer surplus can be written as:

\[
CS=\int_{0}^{Q} \left[P_d(q)-P(q)\right]dq,
\]

where \(P_d(q)\) is the inverse demand curve and \(P(q)\) is the price actually paid. Units are dollars per period. The source defines the concept directly: “Economists refer to the difference between the maximum amount consumers would pay and the actual price as consumer surplus” [PAGE 54]. Breakdown regime: if demand is perfectly elastic, or if competition forces price to marginal cost, the surplus loss collapses toward zero.

For stadium subsidies, define public facility cost \(K\) in dollars, day-of-game marginal operating cost \(MC_t\), and negotiated rent \(L_t\). The source predicts:

\[
L_t \approx MC_t, \qquad S_t = K - L_t,
\]

where \(S_t\) is the implicit subsidy in dollars. This follows the quoted principle that a monopoly lessee extracts concessions “until the rent paid to the lessor covers only the direct (i.e., marginal) costs of operating the facility on the day of the game” [PAGE 8]. Assumptions: the city has weak bargaining power, the league restricts franchise entry, and the facility is publicly financed. Breakdown regime: if a rival league can credibly supply a replacement tenant, \(L_t\) rises toward the facility’s opportunity cost, reducing \(S_t\). The source says precisely: “The existence of competing sports leagues would significantly reduce subsidies” [PAGE 14].

For expansion restriction, let \(N\) be the number of franchises and \(\pi_i(N)\) owner \(i\)’s profit. An owner approves expansion only if:

\[
\pi_i(N+1)\geq \pi_i(N).
\]

If national broadcast revenue \(B\) is divided equally, each owner receives \(B/N\). Expansion changes the payoff by approximately:

\[
\Delta\pi_i = \Delta G_i + B\left(\frac{1}{N+1}-\frac{1}{N}\right)-\Delta C_i,
\]

where \(\Delta G_i\) is local gate change and \(\Delta C_i\) is added cost. Units are dollars per season. The source states the structural reason for resistance: “the competition among cities for teams will push the rents to essentially nothing” [PAGE 16]. Breakdown regime: if \(B\) is small relative to local gains, or if a new franchise increases league-wide demand enough to raise \(B\), expansion may become privately profitable even for a monopolist.

For player allocation, define player \(p\)’s marginal revenue product as:

\[
MRP_{i,p}=\frac{\partial R_i}{\partial p},
\]

in dollars per player-year. The source quotes the definition: “a team values a player for his marginal revenue product, defined as ‘the amount of money that adding a particular player would contribute annually to the net revenues of a team’” [PAGE 47]. Under monopsony, wage \(w\) is:

\[
w = MRP \cdot \frac{\varepsilon_s}{1+\varepsilon_s},
\]

where \(\varepsilon_s\) is the elasticity of player labor supply, dimensionless. If \(\varepsilon_s\to 0\), wage suppression is severe; if \(\varepsilon_s\to\infty\), wage approaches \(MRP\). This formalizes the claim that monopoly leagues “allow league owners artificially to hold down player salaries” [PAGE 26]. Breakdown regime: competing leagues raise \(\varepsilon_s\) because players can receive outside bids.

Competitive balance can be expressed through outcome uncertainty. Let \(p_o\) be the probability of outcome \(o\). Outcome entropy is:

\[
H=-\sum_o p_o \log_2 p_o,
\]

with units bits. Higher \(H\) means less predictable outcomes. The source recognizes this value: “Uncertainty of outcome is a necessary feature of competitive team sports” [PAGE 29]. A complementary dispersion metric is:

\[
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(s_i-\bar{s})^2},
\]

where \(s_i\) is a team success measure, such as wins, final-four appearances, or division titles. Units are wins, appearances, or titles. The source says: “The smaller the standard deviation, the greater the degree of competitive balance” [PAGE 34]. Breakdown regime: \(\sigma\) is sensitive to league size, playoff format, and sample length.

Natural monopoly requires cost subadditivity:

\[
C(Q_1+Q_2)\leq C(Q_1)+C(Q_2).
\]

If this inequality fails, multiple firms can produce at lower aggregate cost. The source uses the classic definition: competition is undesirable only when “one firm of efficient size can produce all or more than the market can take . . . and can continually expand its capacity at less cost than that of a new firm entering the business” [PAGE 75]. With low fixed cost \(F\), average cost is:

\[
AC(Q)=\frac{F}{Q}+c,
\]

where \(c\) is marginal cost. If \(F\) is small, multiple leagues are feasible. The source cites the low fixed-cost estimate: “one economist estimated the annual fixed cost for a sports franchise at less than $100,000” [PAGE 76].

The source’s free-agent regression can be written as:

\[
y_i = \beta_0+\beta_1 Pop_i+\beta_2 Temp_i+\varepsilon_i,
\]

where \(y_i\) is net free agents signed over the 1976–1985 period, \(Pop_i\) is population in millions, and \(Temp_i\) is April temperature in degrees. The source states the test: “This statistical analysis seeks to test the proposition that absent player restraints free agents will flock to the top teams, the largest cities, with the warmest temperatures” [PAGE 118]. Football’s genealogy differs because football remained subject to antitrust law, but Congress granted a merger exemption. The merger transformed a two-league rivalry into a single incumbent with enhanced power over players, cities, and broadcasters. The source later notes that the merger’s supporters claimed it was necessary for a championship game and competitive balance, but the article argues those goals could be achieved through inter-league agreements rather than monopoly.

A second genealogical layer is the evolution of player restraints. Baseball’s reserve clause bound players to one club; football’s Rozelle Rule required compensation when a player changed teams. The source describes the reserve clause as allocating “the exclusive rights to the services of each player to one team” [PAGE 26]. The Rozelle Rule permitted movement only with significant compensation, and the article notes that it “virtually eliminated any free agency in football” [PAGE 27]. The Messersmith arbitration disrupted baseball’s reserve system and opened a period of veteran free agency. The source uses that period to test whether free agency harms competitive balance and finds that more teams contended after the reserve clause’s effective abolition [PAGE 33].

A third genealogical layer is franchise relocation and stadium subsidy. The source records repeated cycles in which cities grant concessions to retain teams: New Orleans, Philadelphia, Los Angeles, Arlington, Oakland, and others are discussed. The structural mechanism is scarcity. With few franchises, cities compete against one another for mobile teams. The source quotes Senator Gorton’s formulation: teams threaten to move “simply because the artificial shortage of teams creates a great bidding war” [PAGE 10]. This creates a multi-week and multi-year trap: cities facing immediate relocation risk capitulate to demands even when long-run welfare would favor refusal.

A fourth genealogical layer is regulatory capture. The source argues that regulation is not a workable cure because the regulated group is small, concentrated, and highly motivated, while the public is diffuse. It states that regulators “soon may become ‘captured’ by the very owners that they supposedly are regulating” [PAGE 62]. The deepest multi-week trap is the fallacy of treating incumbent stability as proof of natural monopoly. The source rejects this: “Neither football nor baseball is a natural monopoly” [PAGE 74]. Historical single-league dominance is explained instead by mergers, predatory entry deterrence, and legal exemptions. The source’s genealogical claim is that if the legal environment had permitted rival leagues, the observed market structure would likely have been different.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The article requires a recursive model of beliefs because each actor’s behavior depends on beliefs about the other actors’ future behavior. Cities believe that losing a franchise is politically catastrophic, so they bid against one another. Owners believe that cities will capitulate, so they threaten relocation. Leagues believe that cities will blame the league if a team leaves, but also believe that owners will not support rules that materially reduce franchise value. Courts believe that sports leagues require cooperation, but also recognize that leagues are not single entities. Congress believes that sports are politically salient, but also responds to concentrated owner lobbying. The recursive equilibrium is therefore not a simple competitive market; it is a bargaining equilibrium sustained by scarcity and legal insulation.

The first habit regression is city behavior under crisis. When a team threatens to leave, the city’s short-term political clock favors settlement. The source notes that owners often refuse long-term leases because the threat of relocation is credible: when Oakland asked for a long-term lease, the owner “absolutely refused” [PAGE 11]. Even if a city obtains a lease, breach may be met only with damages, and injunctive relief is uncertain. The recursive belief is: if I refuse, the team leaves; if the team leaves, voters blame me; therefore I pay. The counter-trap is credible rival demand. If a rival league can replace the franchise, the city’s belief changes: the threat is no longer “team or nothing,” but “which league offers better terms.” The source states: “The existence of competing sports leagues would significantly reduce subsidies” [PAGE 14].

The second habit regression is owner behavior inside a monopoly league. Owners support restraints because they internalize league-wide price effects but not consumer harm. The source explains that monopoly leagues “grant fewer franchises than would exist in a competitive market” [PAGE 4]. Owners also resist expansion because each owner receives a smaller share of broadcast revenue. The recursive belief is: if I support expansion, my national revenue share falls; if my share falls, franchise value falls; therefore I block expansion. The counter-trap is inter-league competition for markets. If a rival league can enter a city, the incumbent league must expand or risk losing the market. The source argues that rival leagues “would have a greater incentive to expand to available markets” [PAGE 24].

The third habit regression is league self-regulation. League officials claim that the league protects cities, players, and competitive balance. The source is skeptical. It notes that leagues may occasionally block relocation, but not because they reliably protect taxpayers. For example, the NFL attempted to bar the Raiders’ move, but the article argues that the league’s motive was territorial protection and bargaining leverage, not public welfare. The source states: “There is no reason to expect that franchise owners routinely will interfere with their joint-venturers’ efforts to make more money at the taxpayers’ expense” [PAGE 13]. The counter-trap is antitrust enforcement plus divestiture: if owners cannot collude on franchise location, expansion, and player allocation, league self-regulation becomes less dangerous.

The fourth habit regression is judicial behavior. Courts facing monopoly leagues must choose between deference and regulation. Deference preserves monopoly harm; regulation forces courts into business judgments they are ill-suited to make. The source says judicial regulation is “costly and time-consuming” and that courts are poorly equipped to determine franchise number, relocation, or optimal player allocation [PAGE 73]. The counter-trap is to change the market structure so courts only need to police collusion among rival leagues. The source’s proposed legal rule is narrow: prohibit inter-league agreements on franchise number, player allocation, and broadcast rights except for genuinely joint products such as championship games. This reduces the recursive burden on courts because the market, not the judge, corrects most inefficiencies.

The second veto condition is talent dilution. If expansion reduces player quality enough to reduce demand, then adding franchises harms consumers. The source acknowledges that expansion reduces average quality somewhat, but argues that the welfare gain in new cities offsets the loss. It states that “overall attendance increased in 1962, 1969, and 1977 after each Major League Baseball expansion” [PAGE 23]. It also states that “overall attendance at NFL games increased after each expansion” [PAGE 24]. For hockey, it notes that when the NHL “expanded overnight from six to twelve teams in 1967, overall attendance at NHL games continued to increase” [PAGE 24]. Formally, if \(A_t\) is attendance and \(E_t\) is an expansion event, the veto condition is \(\Delta A_t<0\) after expansion. The source’s historical evidence reports \(\Delta A_t>0\), so the quality-dilution veto is not supported.

The third veto condition is biomechanical talent supply. If the population of athletes capable of major-league performance were too small, expansion would force teams to use players so inferior that the product collapses. The source assumes a broad talent distribution: “Assuming that the ability to play baseball is distributed normally among the population, a slight decrease in the level of quality of players will permit a significantly larger number of men to play on the major league level” [PAGE 23]. This is an assumption, not a measured biomechanical law. It could be falsified by evidence that minor increases in roster expansion cause nonlinear injury rates, performance collapse, or fan rejection. But within the source’s domain, the biomechanical veto is not triggered.

The fourth veto condition is weather. The source tests temperature as a predictor of free-agent movement. It finds that temperature has only a weak and statistically insignificant effect: “a city that is eight degrees warmer than another city will sign only one more free agent over a ten-year period” [PAGE 42]. In the appendix regression, the temperature coefficient is \(0.168379\) with \(t=1.078237\), and the model’s \(R^2=0.13607032\) [PAGE 119]. Thus weather does not veto the claim that player allocation is driven primarily by contract rules and team valuation, not climate.

The cross-layer veto proof is therefore: let \(V\) be a veto indicator. \(V=1\) if any of the following holds: (i) \(N_{avail}<2N_{min}\); (ii) expansion causes persistent attendance decline; (iii) talent supply is so inelastic that expansion causes quality collapse; (iv) weather dominates player movement. The source provides evidence against each condition. Therefore, within the source’s evidentiary regime, \(V=0\). If future data show injury-driven quality collapse or climate-driven market failure, the veto would need to be revisited.
