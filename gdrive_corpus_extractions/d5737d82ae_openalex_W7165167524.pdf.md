\mathbf{y}_k=\mathbf{z}_k-\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k-1},\quad

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The tracker’s “mind” is recursive in a concrete sense: its estimate at frame \(k\) depends on its prediction from frame \(k-1\), its prediction depends on its previous update, and its update depends on whether the detector is trusted through the measurement covariance and gating. In crisis—occlusion, motion blur, low confidence, or score-overlay corruption—the system regresses to habits. The first habit is constant-velocity prediction: when no detection arrives, the state is propagated by \(\mathbf{F}\) and the covariance grows. The second habit is velocity damping, which contains drift but also biases the predicted position toward the last stable direction. The third habit is track retention: “Unmatched existing tracks are retained across frames rather than immediately deleted to survive short occlusions” [PAGE 5]. The fourth habit is adaptive process noise: the innovation drives \(Q_k=\alpha_k Q_0\), with hard-coded multipliers 3.0 on direction reversals and 1.2 otherwise [PAGE 4]. The fifth habit is gating with a gain boost capped at 8.0 so that “the filter [does not] fully discard its prediction on a single anomalous detection” [PAGE 4]. These habits are exactly what produce the 24-frame occlusion survival result [PAGE 9], but they also create the counter-trap: a stale track can become overconfident and reattach to a wrong detection. The paper’s caps are anti-counter-trap devices: missed-frames penalty capped at 0.5, age reward capped at 0.2, confidence floor 0.3 [PAGE 5], and confirmed-track acceptance threshold growing to 3.0 at 12 or more lost frames [PAGE 5]. The clock is the decisive boundary. With 25 fps, one frame is 40 ms. A five-frame speed window is 0.2 s. The EMA homography time constant is about 6.67 frames, or 0.267 s. The reported occlusion horizon is 24 frames, or 0.96 s; the prose says 25 frames, or 1.00 s [PAGE 9]. A 30-second clip is 750 frames [PAGE 8]. Therefore the system’s “memory” is about one second of missing evidence, not minutes. Recursive theory-of-mind failure occurs when the tracker’s model of the player and its model of the detector disagree. If the detector is correct but the gate is too tight, the track is lost; if the detector is wrong but the gate is too loose, the track persists with a false identity. The paper’s own failure cases make this concrete: “partial occlusion caused by on-screen score overlays” and “motion blur” during rapid directional changes [PAGE 7]. In those moments, the detector-confidence channel and the motion-prediction channel conflict. The system’s habit is to trust motion longer than detection. That is rational for short occlusions and dangerous for long ones. The counter-trap is a feedback loop: a retained track increases its age reward and hit count, which can make it more likely to be accepted later, even if its predicted position has drifted. The clock breaks the loop only when the maximum loss streak is exceeded. A robust deployment would therefore make the loss-streak clock explicit, log per-frame innovation and gate decisions, and separate “identity continuity” from “positional accuracy.” The paper reports both players visible only 72.0% for the proposed system versus 92.8% for DeepSORT [PAGE 9], which is consistent with a system that sometimes sacrifices simultaneous visibility to preserve one dominant identity. That is a theory-of-mind trade-off, not a free improvement.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto proof begins with the court polygon. The homography maps image points to a plane whose doubles width is 10.97 m and baseline-to-baseline length is 23.77 m; singles width is 8.23 m, each doubles alley is 1.37 m, baseline-to-service-line is 6.4 m, and half-singles width is 4.115 m [PAGE 6]. Let \(\mathbf{p}_k=(X_k,Y_k)\) be the mapped player position in metres. A hard physical veto is \(\mathbf{p}_k\notin \mathcal{C}\), where \(\mathcal{C}\) is the court polygon plus a stated tolerance. A second veto is a mapped displacement that is geometrically impossible within the court over the estimator window. The maximum straight-line distance between any two points in the doubles rectangle is \(\sqrt{10.97^2+23.77^2}=26.18\) m; over the 0.2 s speed window, a displacement of 26.18 m would imply 130.9 m s\(^{-1}\), which is not human tennis motion. More usefully, the paper’s own base-noise discussion says position entries match “the 15–25 px displacement of players moving at 3–5 m s\(^{-1}\) at broadcast resolution” [PAGE 4]. Any mapped speed estimate that repeatedly exceeds the plausible human sprint regime without a corresponding detection discontinuity should be flagged as a homography or association failure, not accepted as an athletic measurement. Biomechanical veto proof is about the bounding box. The measurement covariance assigns the largest variance to aspect ratio, 6.0, “due to racket occlusion, arm extension, and motion blur” [PAGE 4]. Therefore a bounding-box aspect-ratio jump is not automatically an identity change; it is a biomechanical state change. The system’s cost function includes aspect-ratio consistency \(a_r\) [PAGE 5], and the gate can reject a legitimate detection if the player extends the racket or stretches. A biomechanical veto should fire when the observed height ratio \(s_r\) and aspect ratio \(a_r\) are inconsistent with the predicted player pose beyond the cost threshold, but the paper does not give a labeled pose model. That is a limitation: the tracker models boxes, not joints, so it cannot distinguish a lunge from an identity swap purely from geometry. Weather veto proof is the weakest layer. The dataset spans clay, hard, grass, camera angles, and lighting [PAGE 3], but the paper reports no rain, wind, shadow, or wet-surface stratification. Homography depends on court keypoints; if weather or lighting causes keypoint occlusion, the RANSAC inlier set can fall below four points, making DLT degenerate, or the reprojection error can exceed the 8-pixel inlier threshold [PAGE 6]. In that regime the metric speed and heatmap are not physically valid. A weather veto should therefore be: if keypoint inliers are fewer than four, or if the final inlier reprojection error exceeds 8 px, then court-space statistics are vetoed for that frame. A second weather/biomechanical veto is surface-dependent traction: clay sliding and grass slip change acceleration, but the constant-velocity model with process-noise expansion does not encode surface friction. The paper’s claim is therefore limited to image-space tracking with planar court mapping under the tested broadcast conditions, not to all-weather biomechanics. The authors themselves say the primary failure is “partial occlusion caused by on-screen score overlays” and that motion blur reduces confidence during rapid directional changes [PAGE 7]; those are physical/optical vetoes, not model “noise.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
