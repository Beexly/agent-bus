[PAGE 1]. The second hop is the position and budget constraint: "Given a fixed budget of £100 Million, the team must consist of 2 Goalkeepers, 5 Defenders, 5 Midfielders and 3 Strikers." [PAGE 2]. The third hop is the validation claim: the hybrid over-forecast by 87 points for the entire team, and the paper attributes this to inability to predict random absence: "The precise inability of the model to pick out random absence from the playing team (sharp decrease to zero) was attributed as the reason for this." [PAGE 8]. That quote is the gray-zone hinge: the model’s loss function treats zero as a point value, but zero can mean either poor performance or non-availability. The paper’s own future-work note says: "Fitness and commitment levels may assist in enhanced determination of the number of matches the player can be expected to play." [PAGE 8]. Therefore the correct audit is not to invent a new ratio but to separate scoring intensity from availability.

A second gray zone is the ensemble coefficient. The paper reports: "For the preprocessed master FPL dataset, the final proportions came out to be (0.4,0.6) after rounding off to the nearest tenths." [PAGE 6]. Table 6 shows Vardy’s minimum RMSE at 60 percent ARIMA and 40 percent LSTM-RNN, while Sagna’s minimum is at 30 percent ARIMA and 70 percent LSTM-RNN. The arithmetic from two individual minima does not transparently produce (0.4,0.6); the exact aggregation rule is not fully specified in the supplied pages. This is not fatal, but it is a reproducibility gap: the paper says the coefficients were determined for every player, then a common pair was identified, but the mapping from per-player optima to the common pair is under-specified. A rigorous replication must expose the aggregation rule and the validation split.

A third gray zone is the linear program. The LP is finite and binary, so it cannot be unbounded in the mathematical sense, but it can be infeasible if the minimum cost of the required position counts exceeds 100.0 after the cost scaling described on [PAGE 4]: "In the Master FPL dataset, the cost was scaled down to in terms of millions by dividing by 1000000." [PAGE 4]. If that scaling is applied twice, the budget becomes effectively 100 million times too small or too large depending on direction. Degeneracy is also possible: multiple squads can attain the same objective, so the “dream team” is one optimal vertex, not a unique causal discovery. The paper reports: "Solution to (1) gave the maximum possible score within the stipulated budget which came out to be 3718 points (Table 7)." [PAGE 7]. That number is an in-sample optimization value over forecast points, not an out-of-sample guarantee. The 87-point over-forecast on [PAGE 8] is the relevant out-of-sample error signal, and it is a team-level aggregate rather than a per-player confidence interval.

The deepest gray zone is causal misattribution. The paper says: "Loyalty was discovered as a major factor in enhancement of a player’s performance. For the dream team, average stay period came out to be 5 years, clearly indicating that the more you play for a particular team, the better your chances of excelling." [PAGE 7]. That is an associational statement from a selected optimal squad, not a causal effect. Loyalty is confounded by team quality, role stability, manager trust, contract length, injury history, and selection bias from the LP. A player remains at a club because the club and player jointly expect value; the same unobserved quality that causes retention also causes points. Thus the correct audit separates persistence of performance from persistence of employment. The model has no transfer, contract, tactical-role, or minutes-risk variable; it predicts points conditional on the historical series, not conditional on the physical and organizational state that generates minutes.

The GSE/GSI audit therefore lands on three crossings: representation, identification, and clock. Representation: weekly points are a mixed discrete-continuous variable with structural zeros. Identification: availability and scoring intensity are not separately observed in the supplied data description. Clock: the model uses 114 past values to predict 38 future values, but the in-season decision clock has weekly injury news, rotation, fixture congestion, international breaks, and transfer windows. The paper’s own future-work list includes fitness, eating habits, demographics, opposition records, and non-continuous time series. The audit should not reject the paper for not doing what it explicitly leaves to future work; it should reject any claim that the paper’s LP output is a physically adjudicated squad. The rigorous statement is conditional: the squad is optimal with respect to the forecast points and the stated budget/position constraints, not optimal with respect to unmodeled availability, biomechanics, weather, or opponent behavior.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal object is a per-player weekly points series \(Y_{i,t}\), where \(i=1,\dots,n\) indexes players and \(t=1,\dots,T\) indexes gameweeks. In the paper’s preprocessing, \(T=114\) historical values per player, formed as three seasons of 38 values: "Chronologically arranged, the points dataset had 114 values of points for a single player (38 for each season)." [PAGE 4]. Units: \(Y_{i,t}\) is FPL points per gameweek, dimensionless as a points count; \(t\) is a gameweek index.

ARIMA operator. The paper fits seasonal ARIMA models with orders such as \((1,1,1)(0,0,1)_{12}\) and reports AIC values in Table 5 [PAGE 5]. A standard multiplicative ARIMA representation is
\[
\phi(B)(1-B)^d\Phi(B^s)(1-B^s)^D\left(Y_{i,t}-\mu_i\right)
=
\theta(B)\Theta(B^s)\epsilon_{i,t},
\qquad
\epsilon_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_i^2).
\]
Here \(B\) is the backshift operator, \(d\) and \(D\) are integer differencing orders, \(s\) is the seasonal period, \(\phi,\Phi,\theta,\Theta\) are dimensionless polynomial coefficients, \(\mu_i\) is in points per gameweek, and \(\sigma_i\) is in points per gameweek. Assumption: the differenced series is stationary and invertible. Breakdown regimes: a root of \(\phi(B)(1-B)^d\Phi(B^s)(1-B^s)^D\) on the unit circle gives nonstationary or explosive forecast variance; a root of \(\theta(B)\Theta(B^s)\) on the unit circle gives non-invertibility and unstable parameter interpretation; over-differencing induces an MA unit root and inflates variance. AIC is
\[
\mathrm{AIC}=2k-2\ln\hat{L},
\]
with \(k\) the number of estimated parameters and \(\hat{L}\) the maximized likelihood. If the natural logarithm is used, AIC is in nats; if base-2 is used, it is in bits, but only differences on the same data and same transformation are comparable. The paper states: "Generally, lower AIC value indicates the presence of a better fit." [PAGE 5].

LSTM-RNN operator. The source says LSTM-RNN was executed on the roster, but it does not give the architecture equations [PAGE 4]. A standard LSTM cell is
\[
f_t=\sigma(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_f),\quad
i_t=\sigma(W_i[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_i),
\]
\[
\tilde{c}_t=\tanh(W_c[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_c),\quad
c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde{c}_t,
\]
\[
o_t=\sigma(W_o[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_o),\quad
h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t).
\]
Units: \(x_t\) and the output are in points per gameweek after scaling; \(h_t\) and \(c_t\) are dimensionless hidden states; weights are dimensionless after normalization. Assumptions: sufficient sequence length, stationary scaling, and no unmodeled regime break. Breakdown regimes: vanishing gradients when recurrent Jacobian spectral radius is much less than one; exploding gradients when it is greater than one; sigmoid saturation when pre-activations are large; overfitting when \(T=114\) is small relative to parameters. The paper itself notes: "LSTM relies heavily on persistence and thus, arises the need for huge datasets for lesser error of forecast." [PAGE 4].

Ensemble operator. Let \(\hat{z}^{\mathrm{ARIMA}}_{i,t}\) and \(\hat{z}^{\mathrm{RNN}}_{i,t}\) be forecast points per gameweek. The hybrid is
\[
\hat{z}_{i,t}=p_1\hat{z}^{\mathrm{ARIMA}}_{i,t}+p_2\hat{z}^{\mathrm{RNN}}_{i,t},
\qquad
p_1,p_2\ge 0,\qquad p_1+p_2=1.
\]
The paper reports a common pair \((0.4,0.6)\) [PAGE 6]. Units: \(p_1,p_2\) are dimensionless weights; \(\hat{z}_{i,t}\) is points per gameweek. Assumption: the validation distribution is exchangeable with the forecast season. Breakdown regimes: if \(p_1+p_2\neq 1\), the ensemble is scale-biased; if \(p_1\) or \(p_2\) is outside \([0,1]\), the result is an extrapolation, not an ensemble; if the validation sample is two players only, the RMSE-optimal weight has high variance. The paper’s RMSE is
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_i(p_1)=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{val}}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{val}}}
\left(\hat{z}_{i,t}(p_1)-z_{i,t}\right)^2},
\]
in points per gameweek. Table 6 gives, for example, Vardy’s minimum at 60 percent ARIMA and 40 percent LSTM-RNN, and Sagna’s minimum at 30 percent ARIMA and 70 percent LSTM-RNN [PAGE 6].

Season aggregation and LP. The forecast season aggregate is
\[
\hat{Z}_i=\sum_{t=1}^{38}\hat{z}_{i,t},
\]
in points per season. The binary linear program is
\[
\max_{y\in\left\{0,1\right\}^n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{Z}_i y_i
\]
subject to
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i y_i\le 100.0,\qquad
\sum_{i\in F}y_i=3,\quad
\sum_{i\in M}y_i=5,\quad
\sum_{i\in D}y_i=5,\quad
\sum_{i\in G}y_i=2.
\]
Units: \(\hat{Z}_i\) is points per season; \(c_i\) is cost in £m; \(y_i\) is binary and dimensionless; the budget is £100m; the objective is points per season. The paper uses the Pulp library and reports a maximum objective of 3718 points [PAGE 7]. Assumptions: costs are correctly scaled to £m, the position sets \(F,M,D,G\) are disjoint and exhaustive, and forecast points are the decision-relevant utility. Breakdown regimes: infeasibility if the minimum cost of the required position counts exceeds 100.0; degeneracy if multiple binary vectors attain the same objective; sensitivity to cost scaling if the division by 1000000 is applied inconsistently; and boundedness is guaranteed by finite binary \(y_i\), but negative \(\hat{Z}_i\) can still be selected to satisfy position quotas.

Derived metrics. The paper defines PPM, CPP, and CPI [PAGE 7]. Formally,
\[
\mathrm{PPM}_i=\frac{Z_i}{M_i},\qquad
\mathrm{CPP}_i=\frac{c_i}{Z_i},
\]
where \(M_i\) is matches, \(Z_i\) is points, and \(c_i\) is cost. PPM has units points per match; CPP has units £m per point. For normalization,
\[
\tilde{x}_i=
\frac{x_i-\min_j x_j}{\max_j x_j-\min_j x_j},
\]
with \(\tilde{x}_i\in[0,1]\) and dimensionless. CPI is a weighted sum of normalized PPM and normalized CPP; the paper uses equal weightage [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regimes: \(M_i=0\) gives undefined PPM; \(Z_i=0\) gives undefined CPP; \(\max_j x_j=\min_j x_j\) gives division by zero in normalization. These are exact singularity regimes, not metaphorical ratios.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits on three lineages. The first is Box-Jenkins-style ARIMA: the paper cites Zhang on neural-network forecasting and discusses bilinear, threshold autoregressive, and ARCH models as nonlinear alternatives [PAGE 2]. That lineage explains why the paper uses ACF, PACF, AIC, and seasonal orders: it is identifying linear persistence before adding a nonlinear learner. The second lineage is recurrent neural networks: the paper calls LSTM-RNN a "totally unexplored category" in this application and argues it captures nonlinearity that ARIMA’s linear form cannot [PAGE 2]. The third lineage is operations research: the paper cites data envelopment analysis and says it uses the Simplex method through Pulp [PAGE 2], then solves a binary knapsack-like selection problem with position quotas and a budget [PAGE 5]. Sports analytics as a cultural lineage is explicit: the paper invokes Moneyball and the shift from performance analysis to managerial decisions [PAGE 2].

Retrocausally, the 2016-17 validation shapes the model story. The paper trains ARIMA and LSTM-RNN on 2013-14 and 2014-15, validates on 2015-16, chooses ensemble weights, then applies the hybrid to forecast 2016-17 [PAGE 6]. That is a legitimate temporal split, but the final common coefficient \((0.4,0.6)\) is a retrospective compression of per-player validation behavior. The paper says the coefficients were determined for every player and then a common pair was identified, but the supplied pages do not show the exact aggregation formula. The multi-week trap is that a coefficient chosen on two illustrative players, Vardy and Sagna, may not transport to 326 players with different minutes distributions. The source itself warns that LSTM needs large data and that 114 values "are not too huge" [PAGE 4].

The historical multi-week traps are concrete. First, international breaks and fixture congestion create clustered absences and load spikes that a gameweek-index ARIMA does not see. Second, transfer windows change team, role, and tactical system; the paper uses PID to track players across clubs [PAGE 3], but the model does not include a club-change indicator. Third, injury history is cited as relevant: "Significant research on player hamstring history [8] and high running during football games [9] has helped in better management of the bench by football managers." [PAGE 2]. That is a direct warning that physical load is a missing layer. Fourth, the paper’s own future work names fitness, eating habits, demographics, and opposition-specific records [PAGE 8]. Fifth, the paper notes that random absence causes sharp decreases to zero and that the model cannot pick them out [PAGE 8]. Each of these is a multi-week trap because a single missed game changes the weekly series, the running sum, and the LP’s season aggregate. The genealogy therefore runs from linear time-series identification to nonlinear sequence learning to combinatorial optimization, but the causal lineage of minutes played runs through biomechanics, team selection, and calendar, which the paper does not model.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The “crisis habit regression” is the model’s habit of extrapolating each player’s historical points pattern. The paper’s defense is persistence: past gameweek points are treated as the relevant signal, and ARIMA plus LSTM-RNN are ensembled to capture linear and nonlinear persistence [PAGE 4]. The crisis appears when persistence fails: a player is injured, transferred, rotated, or tactically repurposed. The paper admits the failure mode: "The precise inability of the model to pick out random absence from the playing team (sharp decrease to zero) was attributed as the reason for this." [PAGE 8]. In recursive terms, the model has a belief about the player’s future scoring rate but no explicit belief about the manager’s selection policy, the medical staff’s clearance policy, or the player’s own risk-taking. Those are nested intentional states: the model predicts points, but points depend on a coach’s belief about fitness, a medical team’s belief about load tolerance, and the player’s belief about pain or risk.

The “counter-trap” is that a public-data model can become self-defeating if many managers use the same historical points, the same ARIMA orders, and the same LSTM ensemble. The paper does not model opponent ownership, mini-league rank, or transfer market behavior; it optimizes a squad against a fixed forecast. If the objective is rank rather than raw points, the optimal pick depends on what other managers pick. The paper’s LP is a single-agent optimization, not a game. The paper also treats “loyalty” as a discovered factor [PAGE 7], but a recursive manager should ask whether loyalty is a cause or a marker of unobserved quality and role security. If everyone selects the same “loyal” players, the counter-trap is crowded selection and no informational edge.

The “clock” is the in-season decision calendar. The paper forecasts 38 gameweeks at once from 114 historical values [PAGE 4], but actual FPL management is a weekly clock with deadlines, injury press conferences, rotation news, and transfer windows. A recursive theory of mind must represent three clocks: the model’s training clock, the player’s availability clock, and the manager’s selection clock. The paper’s fixed coefficient \((0.4,0.6)\) [PAGE 6] is a training-clock object; it does not update when the availability clock changes. The correct counter-trap is not to add a fake psychological ratio but to add an explicit availability state \(A_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}\) and a minutes-risk forecast. Then the manager’s belief about the coach’s belief can be updated weekly. Without that, the model is a static forecast dressed as a managerial decision aid.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is availability. Let \(A_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether player \(i\) is available and selected in gameweek \(t\), and let \(S_{i,t}\) be conditional points given availability. Observed points are
\[
Y_{i,t}=A_{i,t}S_{i,t}.
\]
The expected observed points are
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,t}]
=
\Pr(A_{i,t}=1)\,\mathbb{E}[S_{i,t}\mid A_{i,t}=1].
\]
The paper’s model forecasts \(\hat{z}_{i,t}\) from \(Y_{i,t}\), but the supplied pages do not describe a separate \(A_{i,t}\) model. The paper’s own error diagnosis is consistent with this decomposition: "The precise inability of the model to pick out random absence from the playing team (sharp decrease to zero) was attributed as the reason for this." [PAGE 8]. If the historical availability probability is \(P_0=\Pr_0(A=1)\) and the future availability probability is \(P_1=\Pr_1(A=1)\), then a forecast that carries the old availability level has expected bias
\[
\mathbb{E}[\hat{z}_{i,t}-Y_{i,t}]
\approx
\mathbb{E}[S_{i,t}\mid A=1]\,(P_0-P_1).
\]
Units: points per gameweek. If \(P_0>P_1\), the model over-forecasts; that is exactly the sign of the reported 87-point over-forecast [PAGE 8]. The physical veto is therefore not a new ratio; it is the requirement that the forecast be conditioned on \(A_{i,t}\). The paper cites hamstring history and high running as relevant to bench management [PAGE 2], and it says fitness and commitment may determine matches played [PAGE 8]. Those are biomechanical and behavioral covariates for \(A_{i,t}\), not for \(S_{i,t}\).

Weather is not in the supplied pages. No rain, temperature, pitch, travel, or altitude variable appears in the data description [PAGE 3] or the methodology [PAGE 4]. Therefore a weather veto cannot be computed from this source. The correct adjudication is: weather can affect surface traction, soft-tissue strain, and pacing, but the paper provides no weather state, no exposure model, and no interaction term, so any weather claim would be an external insertion. The same is true of biomechanical load: the paper cites hamstring and high-running literature [PAGE 2], but it does not include accelerometer load, sprint distance, or tissue-injury history in the model. Thus the cross-layer veto proof is an identifiability proof: the model cannot distinguish a zero caused by non-availability from a zero caused by low scoring, because both enter \(Y_{i,t}\) as zero. A physically valid squad must apply an availability mask
\[
\hat{Z}^{\mathrm{veto}}_i
=
\sum_{t=1}^{38}\hat{z}_{i,t}\,\widehat{\Pr}(A_{i,t}=1),
\]
in points per season, and exclude players whose forecast availability falls below a stated threshold. The source does not supply that threshold; it supplies the admission that random absence is the unresolved error source.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: zero-inflation and random-absence veto. Counterfactual: take held-out gameweeks in which a player has zero minutes or is absent. The paper’s model should predict a sharp decrease to zero, but the paper says it cannot [PAGE 8]. Verifiable prediction: if the model is well calibrated for absence, the median forecast on known-absent rows should be at most 0.5 points per gameweek; if the median forecast is materially above 0.5, the model is over-forecasting availability. Veto rule: reject any squad claim that does not report an absence-conditioned error, because the reported 87-point team-level over-forecast [PAGE 8] is consistent with unmodeled \(A_{i,t}=0\) rows. This is verifiable from the data and does not require a new ratio.

Scenario 2: regime-shift and transfer veto. Counterfactual: split players into stable-club and club-changing or role-changing groups between the training seasons and the forecast season. The paper uses PID to track players across clubs [PAGE 3] but does not include a club-change feature, and it compresses per-player ensemble weights into a common \((0.4,0.6)\) [PAGE 6]. Verifiable prediction: the holdout RMSE for club-changing players should exceed the RMSE for stable players by at least 20 percent; if it does not, the regime-shift objection is weakened. Veto rule: reject a single fixed ensemble for all players if adding a club-change or position-change indicator reduces holdout RMSE by a statistically credible margin. This tests the paper’s own warning that LSTM relies on persistence and that 114 values are not huge [PAGE 4].

Scenario 3: LP feasibility and degeneracy veto. Counterfactual: rescale all costs by \(10^{-6}\) and by \(10^{6}\), then resolve the LP; alternatively, perturb each cost by plus or minus 10 percent and record the selected squad and objective. The paper states that cost was scaled to £m by dividing by 1000000 [PAGE 4] and reports an objective of 3718 points [PAGE 7]. Verifiable prediction: a correctly scaled LP should remain feasible at £100m and should produce an objective stable within the cost perturbation band; a double-scaled LP should become infeasible if the budget is effectively too small. Veto rule: reject the claim of a unique “dream team” if multiple binary vectors attain the same objective under exact arithmetic, or if the selected squad changes under a cost perturbation smaller than the reported forecast error. This is verifiable by solving the binary program and enumerating or detecting alternate optima.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
