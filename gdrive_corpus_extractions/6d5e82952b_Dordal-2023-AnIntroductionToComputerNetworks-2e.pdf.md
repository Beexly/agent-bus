<THINK_TRACE>
I treat the drive source as a layered-systems object, not as a bag of acronyms. The source itself insists on a four-layer model in which applications hand data to TCP, TCP calls IP, and IP calls the LAN layer; the LAN layer is further split into physical and logical parts [PAGE 25]. The first multi-hop consequence is that any higher-layer claim must be audited downward: a TCP window claim is meaningless unless the bandwidth-delay product, queue capacity, loss regime, and link-layer timing are compatible. The source gives the exact physical delay decomposition: for a 1500-byte packet on 100 Mbps Ethernet, bandwidth delay is 120 µs, while for 1,000 m of Ethernet cable at about 230 m/µs, propagation delay is about 4.3 µs [PAGE 173]. That asymmetry is the first gray-zone audit: within a LAN, bandwidth delay often dominates, but as networks get larger or faster, propagation delay becomes dominant because it does not shrink with added link rate [PAGE 173]. Any model that treats “latency” as a single scalar invites a category error.

The second audit is the collision domain. The source’s classic Ethernet analysis is not decorative: collision detectability requires a full round-trip interval to be visible to the sender, and the delay budget is expressed in bits, with 2500 m of coax costing 110 bits, transceiver cables 25 bits, transceivers 40 bits, repeaters 25 bits, and encoders 20 bits, totaling 220 bits with a fuller accounting of 232 bits [PAGE 65]. This is why 10-Mbps Ethernet throughput can be analyzed as alternating packet and contention intervals, and why the source states that “if more than one station is waiting for line to clear, then when the line does clear a collision is certain” [PAGE 69]. The source’s own example gives \(1500/(640+1500)=70\%\) of capacity as a reasonable upper limit for shared-media Ethernet [PAGE 69]. But the breakdown regime is explicit: Gigabit Ethernet is largely full-duplex, collisions “simply do not occur,” and the original slot-time logic becomes largely irrelevant [PAGE 73]. A model that carries 10-Mbps half-duplex collision logic into modern switched Ethernet is therefore vetoed by the physical layer.

The third audit is wireless. The source states that radio collision detection is not feasible because the remote signal may be “as little as 1/1,000,000 of the transmitted signal (-60 dB)” at the transmitter [PAGE 105]. That one sentence forces link-layer ACKs, SIFS/IFS timing, and rate scaling. The numeric timing is concrete: slot time 20 µs, IFS 50 µs, SIFS 10 µs for 802.11b/g in 2.4 GHz [PAGE 113]. The source adds that long-distance Wi-Fi at 304 km has a propagation RTT around 2 ms, and “links of such length require some tweaking of the protocol, to address link-layer ACKs” [PAGE 113]. Thus any transport claim that assumes a 1-ms RTT without accounting for link-layer ACK timeout is in a breakdown regime.

The fourth audit is transport. The source defines the bandwidth-delay product as “the bandwidth ˆ RTT product represents the amount of data that can be sent before the first response is received” [PAGE 197]. It then analyzes the congestion knee: below \(W=R\cdot RTT_{\text{noLoad}}\), throughput is proportional to window size and delay is constant; above it, throughput is constant at the bottleneck rate, delay rises, and queue utilization rises [PAGE 205]. The source’s own “power” definition is “throughput/RTT” [PAGE 205]. TCP Reno is then modeled as additive increase and multiplicative decrease: it “slowly increases the window size if no losses occur,” and “rapidly decreases the window size otherwise” [PAGE 457]. The gray zone is that Reno treats loss as congestion, which the source explicitly says fails for high-bandwidth and wireless paths [PAGE 477]. The synchronized-loss hypothesis is also an assumption, not a theorem; the source says it is “mostly a convenience for reasoning about TCP” and “should not be taken as literal fact” [PAGE 497]. That is a direct warning against overfitting.

The fifth audit is policy and routing. Datagram forwarding is stateless, and the source quotes RFC 1122: “gateways are designed to be stateless, forwarding each IP datagram independently of other datagrams” [PAGE 29]. Yet exterior routing is not a pure shortest-path problem: BGP is “the mechanism by which neighboring (that is, directly connected) ISPs share routing information” [PAGE 345], and the source’s BGP instability example states flatly, “Example 2: No stable state exists” [PAGE 365]. Therefore a technical metric alone cannot predict interdomain paths. This is the deepest gray zone: a model can be correct at the IP-layer equations and still wrong at the policy layer.

The final audit is observability. DNS TTLs, TCP state machines, traceroute, netstat, and Wireshark are all fallible instruments [PAGE 49], [PAGE 245]. DNS is “distributed” and “hierarchical” [PAGE 241], and its cache behavior can lock users out for a week if lookup-failure TTLs are too long [PAGE 245]. TIMEWAIT can exhaust ports: with 4,000 client ports and a 120-s TIMEWAIT, exhaustion occurs at only 33 connections/s [PAGE 433]. Thus a prediction must name the measurement and the breakdown regime. The source supports that rigor; it does not support magical single-variable explanations.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The following operators are source-grounded. Units are stated explicitly; where the source gives a qualitative statement rather than an equation, I do not invent a ratio.

1. Bandwidth delay and propagation delay. For link rate \(R\) in bits/s, packet length \(L\) in bits, distance \(d\) in m, and propagation speed \(v\) in m/s,
\[
T_{\text{bw}}=\frac{L}{R},\qquad T_{\text{prop}}=\frac{d}{v}.
\]
Assumption: serialization plus propagation; no queue. The source computes \(12000\ \text{bits}/(100\ \text{bits}/\mu s)=120\ \mu s\) for a 1500-byte packet on 100 Mbps Ethernet, and \(1000\ \text{m}/(230\ \text{m}/\mu s)\approx 4.3\ \mu s\) [PAGE 173]. Breakdown: queueing, store-and-forward, fragmentation, or non-negligible serialization at multiple hops.

2. Store-and-forward end-to-end delay. For \(n\) links,
\[
T_{\text{e2e}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{L}{R_i}+\frac{d_i}{v_i}+Q_i(t)\right),
\]
where \(Q_i(t)\) is queue delay in seconds. Assumption: no loss, no reordering, known path. The source notes that “store-and-forward delay” is an additional bandwidth delay for the second link [PAGE 173]. Breakdown: queue is full, route changes, or packets fragment.

3. Bandwidth-delay product and window knee. Let \(R\) be bottleneck rate in bits/s, \(RTT\) in s, and \(W\) the window in bits. Then
\[
BDP=R\cdot RTT,\qquad W_{\text{knee}}=R\cdot RTT_{\text{noLoad}}.
\]
The source states that the bandwidth ˆ RTT product is “the amount of data that can be sent before the first response is received” [PAGE 197]. Below the knee, throughput is proportional to \(W\); above it, throughput is constant and delay grows [PAGE 205]. Breakdown: \(RTT\) includes the connection’s own queue; then \(W=R\cdot RTT_{\text{actual}}\) can be satisfied by queue growth, not by more transit capacity.

4. TCP Reno additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease. Per RTT,
\[
cwnd_{k+1}=cwnd_k+1\quad\text{(no loss)},\qquad
cwnd_{k+1}=\frac{cwnd_k}{2}\quad\text{(loss event)}.
\]
Assumption: congestion-avoidance regime, loss treated as congestion, no SACK/limited-transmit complications. The source states TCP “slowly increases the window size if no losses occur” and “rapidly decreases the window size otherwise” [PAGE 457]. Breakdown: non-congestive loss, high-bandwidth paths, wireless, and synchronized losses. The source explicitly flags “every loss is treated as evidence of congestion” as an assumption that fails for high-bandwidth TCP and wireless [PAGE 477].

5. Reno loss-rate scaling. The source’s exercise material uses
\[
cwnd_{\text{mean}}=\frac{C}{\sqrt{p}},
\]
with \(p\) the packet-loss probability (dimensionless) and \(C\) a constant [PAGE 525]. Assumption: random independent loss, long-lived Reno flow. Breakdown: deterministic loss, burst loss, AQM, non-Reno transport, or \(p\) so small that \(cwnd_{\text{mean}}\) exceeds the path’s physical/queue ceiling.

6. Token-bucket bound. For a compliant flow with rate \(r\) in bits/s, bucket size \(B_{\max}\) in bits, and cumulative bits sent \(bits(t)\),
\[
bits(t)\leq rt+B_{\max}.
\]
The source states exactly “bits(t) <= rt + Bmax” and proves it via the “red line lemma” [PAGE 605]. The router queue bound is \(B_{\max}\) plus at most one packet [PAGE 605]. Assumption: full bucket at \(t=0\), compliance, outbound rate at least \(r\). Breakdown: bucket not initially full, noncompliant traffic, variable rate, or policing rather than shaping.

7. RED drop probability. For average queue size \(q_{\text{avg}}\), minimum threshold \(q_{\min}\), maximum threshold \(q_{\max}\), maximum drop probability \(p_{\max}\), and packet count \(count\) since last drop,
\[
p_{\text{base}}=p_{\max}\frac{q_{\text{avg}}-q_{\min}}{q_{\max}-q_{\min}},
\qquad
p_{\text{actual}}=\frac{p_{\text{base}}}{1-count\cdot p_{\text{base}}}.
\]
The source gives these formulas and notes \(p_{\max}=0.02\) as proposed in [FJ93] [PAGE 517]. Units: queue sizes in bytes or packets; probabilities dimensionless. Breakdown: parameters are “decidedly ad hoc,” and a single \(p_{\max}\) cannot fit both small and large bandwidth-delay products [PAGE 517].

8. Error-detection collision probability. For an \(N\)-bit check code and random corruption,
\[
\Pr[\text{undetected error}]\approx 2^{-N}.
\]
The source states, “The probability that two random N-bit codes will match is 1/2N” [PAGE 181]. It then combines a 16-bit code with a 1-in-100,000 corruption rate to get about one undetected corrupted packet in \(6\times 10^9\) [PAGE 181]. Breakdown: non-random errors, adversarial corruption, or transposition patterns that a sum-based checksum does not catch [PAGE 181].

9. Wi-Fi timing veto. For 802.11b/g in 2.4 GHz, the source gives slot time \(20\ \mu s\), IFS \(50\ \mu s\), SIFS \(10\ \mu s\) [PAGE 113]. At 11 Mbps, one IFS is about 70 bytes; at 54 Mbps, almost 340 bytes [PAGE 113]. Breakdown: when propagation RTT exceeds the ACK/IFS budget, link-layer ACKs time out. The source’s long-distance example says a 304 km link has RTT around 2 ms and must address link-layer ACKs [PAGE 113].

10. Ethernet collision/slot budget. The source’s delay budget is in bit-times: coax 2500 m = 110 bits, transceiver cables 500 m = 25 bits, transceivers = 40 bits, repeaters = 25 bits, encoders = 20 bits, totaling 220 bits and 232 in a fuller accounting [PAGE 65]. A bit at 10 Mbps lasts 0.1 µs; at 0.77c the signal travels about 23 m [PAGE 65]. Breakdown: full-duplex switched Ethernet removes collision constraints [PAGE 73].

11. BGP policy preference. BGP is not reducible to a scalar cost. The source describes AS-path preference and gives an example with no stable state: “Example 2: No stable state exists” [PAGE 365]. Assumption for convergence: policy preferences do not form a cycle. Breakdown: cyclic preferences, route oscillation, or import/export filtering that reverses preferred paths.

12. DNS cache lifetime. If a resolver caches a negative answer for \(T_{\text{neg}}\) seconds, clients can be locked out for that interval. The source warns that a one-week lookup-failure TTL could make a site unavailable for a week after a temporary deletion [PAGE 245]. Assumption: resolver obeys TTL. Breakdown: misconfigured TTL, cache poisoning, or resolver ignoring negative TTL.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The source’s lineage is a sequence of constraints that later designs reinterpret. Baran’s 1962 packet-switching concern about node failure is cited as the reason datagram forwarding is robust: stateless gateways and independent forwarding allow redundant paths [PAGE 29], [PAGE 153]. The 1982 DIX Ethernet standard is the source of the 10-Mbps delay budget and collision logic [PAGE 65]. Berkeley Unix 4.1BSD in 1981, 4.2BSD in 1983, 4.3BSD in 1986, 4.3BSD-Tahoe in 1988, 4.3BSD-Reno in 1990, and 4.4BSD in 1994 become de facto TCP/IP reference implementations; TCP Tahoe and TCP Reno took their names from those releases [PAGE 53]. RFC 791 in 1981 introduced IPv4 and the class A/B/C split; RFC 988 added class D multicast in 1986 [PAGE 37]. Subnets were proposed in RFC 917 and became official with RFC 950 [PAGE 225]. CIDR then moved the network/host division leftward and introduced longest-match routing [PAGE 333]. IPv6’s 1993 IPng process, 1994 SIPP adoption, 1995 specifications, and 1998 64/64 split are all in the source [PAGE 265]. Later, QUIC uses UDP and a Connection ID to survive NAT rebinding and middlebox filtering [PAGE 449].

Retrocausal reading: later constraints explain earlier design choices. Full-duplex Gigabit Ethernet makes the original minimum packet size and slot-time logic largely vestigial [PAGE 73]. Wi-Fi inherits Ethernet’s collision concerns but cannot detect collisions, so it adds link-layer ACKs [PAGE 105], [PAGE 113]. NAT made IPv4 peer-to-peer and VoIP harder, which retroactively explains IPv6’s globally exposed addresses and the persistence of NAT traversal [PAGE 221], [PAGE 285]. BGP’s policy messiness retroactively explains why interior routing protocols assume common management and uniform link costs [PAGE 345]. RED’s ad hoc parameters retroactively explain CoDel’s parameter-free design [PAGE 517].

Multi-week traps are temporal, not merely conceptual. DNS TTLs can be days by RFC 1035 recommendation, though modern TTLs are often 300 s [PAGE 245], [PAGE 249]. A one-week negative TTL can produce a week-long outage [PAGE 245]. TIMEWAIT holds ports for an interval that can exhaust a small port range at 33 connections/s [PAGE 433]. BGP table growth from about 400,000 prefixes in 2012 to about 800,000 in 2019 is a multi-year scaling trap [PAGE 41]. IPv6 adoption has been slow for decades; the source notes that “IPv6 is now twenty years old, and yet usage as of 2015 remains quite modest” [PAGE 265]. Bufferbloat is a multi-week operational trap because large queues look harmless in bulk-transfer tests but destroy interactive latency [PAGE 513]. The retrocausal lesson is to date every assumption: what was true for 10-Mbps half-duplex coax is not true for full-duplex switched fiber; what was true for classful IPv4 is not true for CIDR; what was true for wired loss is not true for radio.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In protocol terms, recursive theory of mind is the sender’s model of the receiver’s model of the sender. TCP is explicitly a state machine because the operating system “must respond immediately to packet and program events, without indefinite waiting” [PAGE 421]. The sender infers loss from duplicate ACKs; the receiver infers the sender’s next expected sequence number; both infer each other’s ISN and window. The source’s three-way handshake exists partly to detect duplicate SYNs and to confirm both ISNs [PAGE 433]. The crisis-habit regression is the fallback to slow start after timeout or idle: TCP Reno sets \(cwnd=1\) and re-enters slow start after a coarse timeout, and the source’s fast-retransmit section notes that the pipeline drains at the end of the next RTT [PAGE 465]. That regression is rational only if loss means congestion. Under wireless loss or high-bandwidth random loss, it becomes a habit that misreads the environment [PAGE 477]. Counter-traps are adversarial or erroneous signals that induce the wrong regression: spoofed DNS replies with a guessed 16-bit ID [PAGE 253], ARP spoofing [PAGE 277], ICMP “administratively prohibited” or blackholing [PAGE 261], BGP route hijacks or cyclic preferences [PAGE 365], and SYN flooding [PAGE 409]. A naive sender that treats every failure as congestion can be driven into slow start by an attacker or a middlebox.

The clock is the only trustworthy arbiter, but even clocks have regimes. TCP’s original timeout was \(TimeOut=2\cdot EstRTT\), later replaced by Jacobson/Karels variability estimation [PAGE 441]. Karn/Partridge says: on retransmission, double the timeout and stop sampling RTT until the ambiguity clears [PAGE 441]. DNS TTLs are clocks [PAGE 245]. Wi-Fi beacons default to 100 ms [PAGE 121]. TIMEWAIT is a clock that prevents old duplicates but can exhaust ports [PAGE 433]. The recursive discipline is: model the other side’s state, but ground the model in a verifiable clock and a verifiable packet event. If the clock says the ACK cannot have returned, do not infer congestion from the absence. If the resolver TTL says the record is stale, do not infer that the destination is down. If a BGP peer’s AS-path contains your own AS, reject the route rather than trust the preference. The counter-trap is to distinguish “the other side is malicious” from “the other side is slow” from “the other side is lossy.” The source’s own caution about synchronized losses is a theory-of-mind warning: two connections sharing a bottleneck may not actually lose packets at the same instant; the hypothesis is a convenience, not a literal fact [PAGE 497].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer vetoes are decisive in this source. Ethernet’s collision budget is not a software parameter; it is a function of cable length, repeater count, transceiver delay, and encoder delay, measured in bit-times [PAGE 65]. When the physical layer changes to full-duplex switched links, the old collision constraints are vetoed: collisions “simply do not occur” and the network diameter is no longer a concern [PAGE 73]. Radio vetoes collision detection because the remote signal can be 1/1,000,000 of the local signal (-60 dB), and hidden nodes make direct collision detection impossible [PAGE 105]. The response is not a stronger TCP retransmission; it is link-layer ACKs, SIFS/IFS timing, and rate scaling [PAGE 113], [PAGE 117]. Long-distance Wi-Fi is a clean proof: a 304 km link has about 2 ms RTT, and the source says such links “require some tweaking of the protocol, to address link-layer ACKs” [PAGE 113]. If the propagation RTT exceeds the ACK timer, no transport-layer optimism can recover the link.

Weather and obstruction vetoes appear qualitatively in fixed wireless. The source states that lower frequencies “tend to be better at ‘seeing’ through trees and other obstructions,” and that line-of-sight requirements become stricter as frequency increases [PAGE 141]. It does not provide a quantitative rain-fade equation, so I do not invent one. The admissible claim is conditional: if foliage, walls, or distance push the link beyond its budget, the higher layers see loss and delay; TCP Reno may then misinterpret that loss as congestion [PAGE 477]. The correct cross-layer response is link-layer adaptation, fragmentation, rate scaling, or a different path, not merely a larger cwnd.

Biomechanical vetoes are not represented in this source. The closest operational layer is human/administrative: network management includes “staffing the help desk” and FCAPS fault/configuration/performance/security categories [PAGE 645]. That is not biomechanics, and treating it as a physical veto would be a category error. The source also gives received-power benchmarks in dBm: Polaris about -97 dBm, Venus about -73 dBm, GPS about -127 dBm [PAGE 117]. These are useful because they force absolute units, not arbitrary “signal strength” scores. The proof standard is therefore: any claimed cross-layer effect must name the physical quantity (bits/s, seconds, meters, dBm, bit-error rate), the measurement point, and the regime where the higher-layer assumption fails. Ethernet’s \(10^{-10}\) target BER and packet error rate below 1 in \(10^6\) for 1000BASE-T [PAGE 73] are examples of a quantitative physical veto; Wi-Fi’s lack of collision detection is another [PAGE 105].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
