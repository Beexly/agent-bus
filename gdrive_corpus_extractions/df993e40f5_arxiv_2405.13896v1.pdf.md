The source is arXiv:2405.13896v1, Koshkina and Elder, “A General Framework for Jersey Number Recognition in Sports Video.” The first multi-hop move is to identify the task correctly: the paper explicitly frames jersey-number recognition as “a variant of scene text recognition” [PAGE 1], not as a generic player classifier. That reframing drives the entire architecture. The image-level pipeline is a three-stage conditional system: a binary legibility classifier decides whether a player crop is worth reading; a body-pose detector locates the torso; and PARSeq performs scene text recognition on the torso crop. The tracklet-level pipeline adds a Re-ID-based main-subject filter, weak pseudo-labeling for legibility, and a consolidation stage that turns many per-image string predictions into one tracklet label.

The second move is to audit the headline numbers against their conditioning. The abstract says the system achieves “91.4% accuracy for hockey images and 87.4% for soccer tracklets” [PAGE 1]. But the hockey image-level 91.4% is not a full-frame, all-player accuracy. The paper states that evaluation considers “only legible images” [PAGE 7-8]. Table 1 reports 7,787 legible images, 132,983 total images, and 4,250 jersey-number-labelled images [PAGE 4]. The legibility distribution is extremely skewed: “Our hockey legibility dataset is highly imbalanced with only 5% of images labelled as legible” [PAGE 4]. Thus the 91.4% figure is conditional on a small, curated, legible subset. It is a valid recognition metric for that subset, but it cannot be silently promoted to an unconditional full-video recognition rate.

The third move is to check the tracklet claim. Soccer tracklet accuracy is 87.45% on the SoccerNet test set, but only 79.31% on the challenge set [PAGE 8]. That gap is a domain-shift warning. The average SoccerNet tracklet length is 482 frames [PAGE 4], so there is ample temporal evidence in principle, but the challenge partition shows that evidence aggregation does not erase distribution shift. The paper also notes a dataset flaw: “it includes tracklets for a soccer ball with the label of jersey number ”1”” [PAGE 4]. That is a verifiable annotation artifact and a reminder that tracklet metrics inherit label noise.

The fourth move is to inspect the probabilistic consolidation equation. Equation (1) is written as

The correct operational reading is to treat it as a score, then take an argmax, and normalize only if a probability is required. This is a gray-zone mathematical mismatch, not a fatal flaw, because the downstream decision is argmax and the paper also applies temperature scaling to recalibrate confidences [PAGE 7]. But the mismatch matters for any attempt to interpret the output as a calibrated posterior.

This is a counter-trap: fine-tuning on the target sport is not automatically the best transfer strategy if the source sport is harder and more diverse. This is the key failure mode: partial visibility induces a systematic 2-digit to 1-digit regression. Any claim of robust tracklet recognition must survive that confusion matrix.

The paper is explicit that “motion blur, body pose variations, projective distortions, occlusions, and folds in the jersey material causing complex distortions all conspire to make reliable recognition difficult” [PAGE 1]. If the number is not visible, no image-level recognizer can recover it except by prior. If the number is partially visible, the STR model may read a prefix. If the camera geometry changes from wide-field university hockey to zoomed broadcast NHL, the crop statistics change. The paper explicitly notes that “the camera geometries are very different” [PAGE 3]. These are the defensible cross-layer vetoes. Weather is not measured in the source, so any weather veto would be speculative and must be refused.

The legibility classifier is trained on pseudo-labels produced by running a hockey-trained classifier on SoccerNet tracklet images. At inference, “a tracklet is deemed legible if it contains one or more images classified as legible” [PAGE 6]. This is a recursive system: the model’s own legibility decisions shape the training set for the next legibility model and shape which images the STR model sees. That recursion is useful, but it creates compounding-error risk. The paper mitigates it with main-subject filtering and confidence-weighted consolidation, but the challenge-set drop from 87.45% to 79.31% shows that the loop is not fully closed.

The source operators are: a binary legibility classifier, a pose-based torso localizer, a scene text recognizer, a Re-ID main-subject filter, and a prediction consolidator. I formalize each with its units and breakdown regime.

Legibility operator. Let \(I_n\) be image \(n\), and let \(L_n\in\{0,1\}\) be the legibility label. The classifier is a ResNet34 pre-trained on ImageNet and fine-tuned on the hockey legibility dataset [PAGE 3-4]. Its output is
\[
\hat p(L_n=1\mid I_n)=\sigma(f_\theta(I_n)),\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
\]
which is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). The decision is \(\hat L_n=1[\hat p(L_n=1\mid I_n)\ge \tau]\). Training uses binary cross-entropy,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\left[
L_n\log \hat p_n+(1-L_n)\log(1-\hat p_n)
\right],
\]
in nats when natural logs are used. The paper trains for 20 epochs with starting learning rate 0.001 and momentum 0.9, using Sharpness-Aware Minimization with SGD [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: because “only 5% of images labelled as legible” [PAGE 4], a trivial all-illegible predictor can achieve high accuracy; the reported 94.5% accuracy with F1 71.7% [PAGE 7] must be read as an imbalanced metric, not as balanced discrimination.

Pose localization operator. ViTPose trained on MS COCO extracts shoulder and hip keypoints; the torso rectangle is padded by 5 pixels on the left, right, and bottom [PAGE 4]. Coordinates are in pixels. The operator is a deterministic crop \(C_n=\mathrm{Crop}(I_n;\mathbf{k}_n)\), where \(\mathbf{k}_n\) are keypoints. Breakdown regime: pose failure or occlusion corrupts the crop; if the torso is not detected, the crop is undefined, and the downstream STR operator receives no valid input.

Scene text recognition operator. PARSeq models the conditional string probability
\[
p_\phi(y_{1:m}\mid I_n)
=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p_\phi(y_i\mid y_{<i},I_n),
\]
where the label length is limited to 2 and the alphabet is 10 digits plus an end-of-string token, so the output dimension is 11 [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. The paper fine-tunes for 25 epochs with default PARSeq training settings [PAGE 5]. Temperature scaling is applied to recalibrate confidences [PAGE 7]:
\[
q_i(T)=\frac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_{k=1}^{11}\exp(z_k/T)},\qquad T>0.
\]
\(T\) is dimensionless. Breakdown regimes: \(T\to 0\) makes the distribution one-hot and overconfident; \(T\to\infty\) makes it uniform and uninformative; if the true jersey number has more than two digits, the label-length constraint makes recognition impossible by construction.

Consolidation equation. The paper’s Equation (1) is, verbatim,
\[
p(d_j=k\mid \{I_n\})
=\sum_{n\in N_l}
\left(
\log p(I_n\mid d_j=k)+\log p(d_j=k)
\right)
\tag{1}
\]
[PAGE 7]. As written, the left side is a probability and the right side is a sum of log-likelihoods. The mathematically clean reading is to define the score
\[
S_j(k)=\sum_{n\in N_l}
\left(
\log p(I_n\mid d_j=k)+\log p(d_j=k)
\right),
\]
then predict
\[
\hat k_j=\arg\max_{k\in\{0,\dots,9\}}S_j(k).
\]
If a normalized posterior is required, use
\[
\hat q_j(k)=\frac{\exp(S_j(k))}{\sum_{k'=0}^{9}\exp(S_j(k'))}.
\]
Units are nats for natural logs and bits for base-2 logs. Breakdown regimes: if \(N_l=\emptyset\), the empty sum gives zero score for every digit, so the prediction is prior-only; if any likelihood is zero, \(\log 0\) is singular and smoothing is required; if the prior is misspecified, the argmax inherits that bias.

Re-ID main-subject filter. Let \(z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d\) be the Centroid-ReID feature for image \(n\), trained on Market1501 [PAGE 6]. The paper fits an isotropic Gaussian and excludes outliers more than \(N\) standard deviations from the mean, repeated \(K\) times. Formally,
\[
\mu=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{n\in T}z_n,\qquad
\sigma^2=\frac{1}{d|T|}\sum_{n\in T}\|z_n-\mu\|_2^2,
\]
and the exclusion rule is
\[
\|z_n-\mu\|_2>N\sigma,\qquad N=3.5,\quad K=3.
\]
\(N\) is dimensionless; \(z_n\) and \(\sigma\) are in feature-norm units. The paper states: “We found the optimal parameters to be K = 3, N = 3.5” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regimes: if \(\sigma=0\), the threshold is undefined; if \(N\) is too small, all frames are removed; if \(N\) is too large, no distractors are removed.

Heuristic consolidation. The paper describes a confidence-weighted majority vote with a confidence-sum threshold and down-weighting of one-digit votes when some frames predict two digits [PAGE 7]. I write the operational form as
\[
\hat y=\arg\max_{y}\sum_{n\in N_l}c_{n,y}w(y),
\]
subject to \(\sum_{n\in N_l}c_n\ge \tau_c\), where \(c_{n,y}\) is the confidence for label \(y\) and \(w(y)\) is a down-weight for one-digit labels. This is a reconstruction, because the paper does not print the exact weight values. Breakdown regimes: empty \(N_l\), threshold too high, or one-digit down-weighting that overcorrects two-digit errors.

Metrics. Accuracy is
\[
\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}1[\hat y_m=y_m],
\]
dimensionless; F1 is \(2PR/(P+R)\), also dimensionless. The paper reports 91.4% hockey image accuracy [PAGE 7], 87.45% SoccerNet test tracklet accuracy, and 79.31% challenge tracklet accuracy [PAGE 8]. Those are conditional metrics: hockey image accuracy is on legible images; tracklet accuracy is on tracklets with defined labels.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from general scene text recognition to sports-specific jersey recognition. The paper says jersey-number recognition “can be viewed as a variant of scene text recognition” [PAGE 1]. Earlier jersey-number work mostly trained specialized classifiers from scratch or with pose-guided detectors; the paper cites Gerke et al., Li et al., Liu and Bhanu, Vats et al., and Balaji et al. [PAGE 2]. The retrocausal move is to take a later, general STR model—PARSeq, trained on “SynthText, COCO-Text, and TextOCR” [PAGE 5]—and retrofit it to an earlier task. This reverses the usual genealogy: instead of building a sports-specific recognizer and hoping it generalizes, the paper inherits a text-in-the-wild recognizer and fine-tunes it on a small sports dataset. That is why the method can predict numbers absent from training: the token-processing STR model is not restricted to a closed label set [PAGE 4-5].

The dataset lineage is equally important. The paper notes that progress “has been slowed by the lack of large public datasets” [PAGE 2], and it positions its hockey dataset against the 2023 SoccerNet release [PAGE 2]. The hockey dataset combines university games and McGill NHL tracking data, with different camera geometries [PAGE 3]. The paper reports 54 unique jersey numbers in the hockey training set and 25 in the test set, with “Two numbers in the test set do not appear in the training set” [PAGE 4]. For SoccerNet, there are 55 unique jersey numbers across training and test, and “the test set contains 10 numbers that do not appear in the training partition” [PAGE 4]. These are open-set conditions, not closed-set classification.

Multi-week traps. First, the proprietary-data trap: prior methods used large labelled datasets that “have been proprietary and not released publicly” [PAGE 1], so cross-paper comparisons are confounded. Second, the metric-conditioning trap: 91.4% is computed on legible images, while only 5% of hockey images are legible [PAGE 4, PAGE 7-8]. Third, the cross-sport transfer trap: hockey fine-tuning transfers to soccer at 83.90%, but soccer fine-tuning transfers to hockey at only 65.84% [PAGE 7]. Fourth, the annotation-artifact trap: SoccerNet includes “tracklets for a soccer ball with the label of jersey number ”1”” [PAGE 4]. Fifth, the digit-prior trap: two-digit numbers are roughly twice as frequent as one-digit numbers, but occlusion causes 48% of true two-digit numbers to be predicted as one-digit [PAGE 6-7]. These traps are multi-week because they only surface after reproducing splits, retraining the classifier, and running the ablations.

The paper’s own genealogy claim is modest: it says the pipeline “can also benefit from advances in STR methods” [PAGE 5]. That is the retrocausal hinge. If STR improves, the jersey pipeline improves without redesign. But the same hinge creates dependence: if the STR model’s language prior is wrong for jersey numbers, the pipeline inherits that prior. The paper mitigates this with fine-tuning and temperature scaling, but it does not eliminate it.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
I treat “theory of mind” here as a computational analogy, not a psychological claim. The relevant agents are the annotator, the legibility classifier, the STR model, and the evaluator. The recursive structure is explicit: on SoccerNet, the paper derives “weak pseudo-labels from the tracklet-level annotations” [PAGE 6], then trains a legibility classifier on those pseudo-labels, then uses that classifier to decide which images the STR model sees. The model is therefore modeling the annotator’s notion of legibility through its own prior outputs.

Crisis habit regression. Under occlusion and partial visibility, the STR model regresses to a learned habit: it predicts a one-digit number when the true number is two digits. Table 3 shows that for true two-digit numbers, 48% are predicted as one-digit, while only 40% are predicted correctly as two-digit [PAGE 6]. The paper explains the mechanism: “only one digit may be visible even when the jersey number consists of two digits” [PAGE 7]. This is a crisis habit regression because the model’s prior over string length dominates the visual evidence under stress. The heuristic consolidator tries to counter this by down-weighting one-digit votes [PAGE 7], but the confusion matrix shows the habit is strong.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is fine-tuning on one sport and then overcommitting to that sport’s statistics. Hockey fine-tuning gives 91.40% on hockey and 83.90% on soccer; soccer fine-tuning gives 87.45% on soccer but collapses to 65.84% on hockey [PAGE 7]. The paper’s interpretation is that hockey is the more complex dataset, so training on hockey transfers better to soccer than the reverse [PAGE 8]. The recursive lesson is that the model’s “belief” about what a jersey number looks like is shaped by the source sport. If the evaluator assumes symmetry, the counter-trap is invisible. If the evaluator checks both directions, the asymmetry is exposed.

Clock. The clock is the tracklet temporal aggregation. The average SoccerNet tracklet is 482 frames [PAGE 4], so the system has many opportunities to observe a legible frame. The heuristic consolidator uses a confidence-weighted vote over legible frames, with a confidence threshold and a two-digit bias [PAGE 7]. The paper reports that the heuristic method is best: 87.45% with full filtering, versus 84.56% with no main-subject filtering [PAGE 8]. The clock is not a neutral timer; it is a weighting scheme. If the clock is too short, there are too few legible frames. If the clock is too long, distractor frames and occlusions accumulate. The Re-ID filter is the mechanism that keeps the clock from being polluted by other players.

Recursive failure mode. The legibility classifier is trained on pseudo-labels that are themselves derived from tracklet labels. If the classifier is overconfident, it passes bad crops to STR. If STR is overconfident, the consolidator overweights bad predictions. The paper’s temperature scaling [PAGE 7] and confidence threshold [PAGE 7] are attempts to break this recursion. The challenge-set drop to 79.31% [PAGE 8] shows the recursion is not fully broken under distribution shift.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The source documents the physical veto directly: “motion blur, body pose variations, projective distortions, occlusions, and folds in the jersey material causing complex distortions all conspire to make reliable recognition difficult” [PAGE 1]. It also states that “the jersey number is typically only clearly visible on a minority of frames” [PAGE 1]. Therefore, any claim of unconditional image-level recognition is vetoed by visibility. The correct conditional statement is: given a legible torso crop, the recognizer can achieve high accuracy; given an illegible crop, the recognizer is prior-only. The paper’s own evaluation respects this: it evaluates “considering only legible images” [PAGE 7-8].

Biomechanical layer. The relevant biomechanics are pose variation and material deformation. The paper uses shoulder and hip keypoints to crop the torso, padded by 5 pixels [PAGE 4]. If the player’s pose hides the number, or if the jersey folds, the crop contains distorted glyphs. The confusion matrix is the proof: true two-digit numbers are predicted as one-digit 48% of the time [PAGE 6]. That is not a random error; it is a biomechanical-optical truncation. The veto condition is: if the visible glyph set is a proper prefix of the true string, the STR model can only recover the suffix by prior, not by evidence.

Geometric layer. The paper compares university wide-field hockey with broadcast NHL and says “the camera geometries are very different” [PAGE 3]. It also says the university images are “typically of lower resolution and jersey numbers are harder to decipher” [PAGE 4]. The geometric veto is scale and projection: a wide-field camera reduces the number of pixels on the jersey, and a broadcast camera introduces zoom changes. The model’s cross-sport table [PAGE 7] is the empirical signature of this veto.

Weather layer. The source does not report weather variables, field moisture, wind, or illumination spectra. There is no weather ablation and no meteorological measurement. Therefore a weather veto is not admissible from this source. The only defensible statement is: weather is an unmeasured confound that could affect outdoor soccer, but the paper provides no evidence to quantify it. I refuse to invent a weather equation or a weather coefficient.

Veto proof. Let \(V_n\in\{0,1\}\) be visibility, \(L_n\in\{0,1\}\) legibility, and \(Y_n\) the true jersey string. The pipeline only observes \(I_n\). If \(V_n=0\), then \(I_n\) contains no glyph evidence, so
\[
p_\phi(Y_n\mid I_n)=p_\phi(Y_n),
\]
the prior-only regime. If \(V_n=1\) but the visible glyphs are a prefix, the likelihood is maximized by a prefix string, which is why two-digit numbers collapse to one-digit. The paper’s heuristic threshold marks a tracklet illegible when the summed confidence is below threshold [PAGE 7], which is the correct operational response to the veto. The tracklet-level clock can override the image-level veto only if at least one frame has \(V_n=1\) and \(L_n=1\). That is the precise sense in which temporal aggregation is a cross-layer repair: it does not make the invisible visible; it waits for visibility.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
