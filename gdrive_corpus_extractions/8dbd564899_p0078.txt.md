<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of this argument reaches back to military command theory and early 20th-century industrial efficiency movements (Taylorism). In 1920, football was being "scientized"; the playbook was becoming a proprietary technology. Coaches like Amos Alonzo Stagg were building encyclopedic systems. Roper’s stance is a reaction against this burgeoning complexity. He is implicitly citing the genealogy of *failure*: every great collapse contains a moment where the most intricate plan was abandoned for a fundamental drill.
The "multi-week trap" is pedagogical. If a team spends weeks practicing "136-B" but rarely runs it, they create a neural pathway associated with that signal that is weaker than the pathways for their core four plays. When pressure mounts (the game), Hebbian plasticity favors the stronger connections. The "trick formation" with the center on his elbow is an outlier event—so rare in practice that it never attains the status of an automatic response. It exists only as a declarative memory ("I know this play exists"), not a procedural one ("My body knows how to run this").
Historically, this insight precedes modern concepts like "System 1 vs. System 2" thinking (Kahneman) by decades. Roper identifies that high-stakes performance requires System 1 (fast, intuitive, automatic) processing. Complex signals force a switch to System 2 (slow, analytical), which is impossible under the temporal constraints of a football snap. Thus, the genealogy of *success* he proposes is rooted in the repetitive, almost monastic drilling of a minimal kernel until it becomes reflexive—a philosophy that would echo in later minimalist systems like the Wishbone offense or certain aspects of basketball’s motion offense.
