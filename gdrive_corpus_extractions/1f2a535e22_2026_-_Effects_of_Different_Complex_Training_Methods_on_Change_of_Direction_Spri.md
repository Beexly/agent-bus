The first multi-hop audit is definitional. The paper’s stated question is not “which complex-training method is universally better?” but whether exercise order changes adaptation when volume and intensity are matched. The abstract already fixes the boundary: “After adjustment for baseline performance, no consistent between-group differences were observed for jumping performance during CMJ, CMJ-A, SJ, or DJ, nor for sprinting or change-of-direction performance (all p ≥ 0.05). A significant between-group effect favoring DCT was observed only for CMJ-A peak velocity (p = 0.015) and early-phase isometric force production at 100 ms during the IMTP (p = 0.011)” [PAGE 1]. That is a narrow, task-specific claim, not a global superiority claim. The same page concludes that “Exercise sequencing appears to act as a fine-tuning variable that may influence specific neuromuscular qualities, rather than producing broad performance advantages across athletic tasks” [PAGE 1]. Any downstream interpretation that turns DCT into a universal force-first prescription is therefore already over-reading the source.

The second hop is design. The study is “A nonrandomized, single-blind (referring to participants’ blinding), parallel-group intervention” [PAGE 3], and the authors state: “Because group allocation was determined by team membership rather than randomization, the design carries an inherent limitation for causal inference” [PAGE 3]. The final sample is 20, with ACT n=8 and DCT n=12 [PAGE 3]. That imbalance matters because adjusted group contrasts with one covariate and an interaction term leave very few error degrees of freedom; with n=20, a model with intercept, group, baseline covariate, and interaction has df_error=16, and a simpler ANCOVA has df_error=17. Small-n ANCOVA is fragile when slopes are heterogeneous, when baselines differ, or when multiple outcomes are tested. The baseline body-mass difference is also not trivial for relative force: ACT 88±10 kg vs DCT 75±7 kg [PAGE 3]. Because IMTP is reported as N/kg body mass, the scaling denominator is part of the outcome operator, not a cosmetic normalization.

The third hop is the statistical gray zone. The authors specify a fallback: “If the homogeneity of regression slopes assumption was violated, the affected variable was analyzed using delta scores (post–pre), and between-group differences were tested with independent-samples t-tests” [PAGE 5]. For CMJ peak velocity, they report “the significant group × covariate interaction (F = 5.691, p = 0.030, η²p = 0.262) indicated a violation of the homogeneity of regression slopes assumption” [PAGE 6], and the delta t-test was “t(18) = -2.044, p = 0.056), although the effect size was large (d = 0.93)” [PAGE 6]. Thus CMJPV should not be presented as a clean ANCOVA-confirmed group effect; the fallback test is borderline and non-significant at α=0.05. For CMJ-A peak velocity, the paper reports “significant difference for CMJ-APV (adjusted mean difference = 0.12 m/s, 95% CI: -0.02 to 0.26 m/s; t(18) = –2.676, p = 0.015, d = 1.22), both favoring DCT” [PAGE 7]. That is stronger, but the 95% CI still touches zero, so the evidence is fragile. For IMTPF100, they report “ANCOVA indicated a significant group effect for postintervention IMTPF100 (F = 8.347, p = 0.011, η²p = 0.343)” [PAGE 7], but also “The group × covariate interaction was significant for IMTPF100 (F = 11.466, p = 0.004, η²p = 0.417), indicating different pre–post relationships between groups” [PAGE 7]. A significant interaction directly undermines the constant-effect ANCOVA interpretation. The supplementary table compounds the problem: “IMTP force at 100 ms [N/kg b.m.] | 1.34 | -1.47 to 4.15 | 0.011* | 0.343” [PAGE 13] has a 95% CI that crosses zero, while “5–10–5 time [s] | -0.011 | -0.053 to 0.05 | 0.022* | 0.287” [PAGE 13] also crosses zero, and “CODAT [s] | 0.043 | 0.01 to 0.08 | 0.159 | 0.120” [PAGE 13] excludes zero despite p=0.159. These are internal inconsistencies that must be flagged, not smoothed over.

The fourth hop is the sprint/COD contradiction. The results text says: “ANCOVA indicated no significant group effects for postintervention performance in the 5–10–5 test (F = 6.426, p = 0.022, η²p = 0.287), 5 m sprints (F = 4.503, p = 0.050, η²p = 0.220), 10 m sprints (F = 0.146, p = 0.708, η²p = 0.009), or CODAT (F = 2.184, p = 0.159, η²p = 0.120)” [PAGE 8]. Calling p=0.022 and p=0.050 “no significant group effects” is verbally inconsistent with α=0.05. The discussion later says: “Although statistically significant group effects were observed for the 5–10–5 test and 5 m sprint time, these effects were not accompanied by corresponding improvements in other sprint or COD tests” [PAGE 9]. The most defensible reading is not “no effects anywhere,” but “fragile, task-specific, uncorrected signals that do not survive a coherent family-wise or mechanistic interpretation.” With 23 rows in Supplementary Table S1 [PAGE 13], the probability of at least one false-positive under a global null with independent tests is \(1-(1-0.05)^{23}\approx0.693\); outcomes are correlated, so this is not exact, but it proves that uncorrected multiplicity is a real veto on strong claims.

The fifth hop is mechanism. The authors propose that DCT puts high-load resistance under lower accumulated fatigue, favoring “mechanical tension and early-phase force production” [PAGE 9], and that IMTPF100 is “predominantly influenced by neural factors, such as rapid motor-unit recruitment and initial discharge rate” [PAGE 9]. But they also admit: “given the lack of direct acute measurements, these interpretations remain speculative” [PAGE 9]. They did not measure EMG, motor-unit behavior, tendon stiffness, corticospinal excitability, or acute potentiation-fatigue. The conclusion therefore must be a program-level, non-mechanistic inference: DCT may be associated with small advantages in selected early-force and CMJ-A velocity metrics, while most jump, sprint, and COD outcomes are comparable. The strongest defensible claim is exactly the authors’ own practical statement: “Exercise sequencing should be considered a fine-tuning variable within CT programs, complementing appropriate load prescription, movement specificity, and overall training structure” [PAGE 10].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The primary statistical operator is an analysis of covariance with baseline as covariate and group as the adjusted factor:
\[
Y_{i,\mathrm{post}}=\alpha+\beta Y_{i,\mathrm{pre}}+\tau G_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad 
\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
\]
where \(G_i=0\) for ACT and \(G_i=1\) for DCT, \(Y\) is in the outcome unit (cm, m/s, s, or N/kg), \(\beta\) is the baseline coefficient, \(\tau\) is the adjusted DCT−ACT group effect in outcome units, and \(\varepsilon_i\) is residual error. The assumption is homogeneity of regression slopes: no \(G_i\times Y_{i,\mathrm{pre}}\) interaction. The source explicitly says that if this is violated, it uses delta scores and independent-samples t-tests [PAGE 5]. The breakdown regime is therefore exact: when the group×covariate interaction is significant, the constant-effect \(\tau\) is not identified as a single adjusted contrast. The paper reports violations for CMJPV (\(p=0.030\)) [PAGE 6], CMJ-A JH (\(p=0.033\)) and CMJ-A PV (\(p=0.012\)) [PAGE 7], and IMTPF100 (\(p=0.004\)) [PAGE 7]. In those regimes, ANCOVA-based group claims must be vetoed or downgraded; the delta t-test is the only pre-specified fallback.

The F operator is
\[
F=\frac{MS_{\mathrm{group}}}{MS_{\mathrm{error}}}
=\frac{SS_{\mathrm{group}}/df_{\mathrm{group}}}{SS_{\mathrm{error}}/df_{\mathrm{error}}},
\qquad df_{\mathrm{error}}=n-p,
\]
which is dimensionless. Partial eta squared is
\[
\eta_p^2=\frac{SS_{\mathrm{effect}}}{SS_{\mathrm{effect}}+SS_{\mathrm{error}}},
\]
also dimensionless and bounded in \([0,1)\). Cohen’s d for a contrast is
\[
d=\frac{\bar{D}_{\mathrm{DCT}}-\bar{D}_{\mathrm{ACT}}}{s_p},
\]
a standardized mean difference with no physical unit; for within-group pre-post changes, \(D_i=Y_{i,\mathrm{post}}-Y_{i,\mathrm{pre}}\). These equations are standard; they are not substitutes for the missing acute fatigue/potentiation measures.

Jump height from vertical impulse uses the impulse-momentum and free-flight relations. The source states: “The jumping height was determined from force impulse by using the following equation (Hojka et al., 2022):” [PAGE 4]. The formal chain is
\[
J_z=\int_{t_0}^{t_{\mathrm{to}}}\left[F_z(t)-m_b g\right]dt,
\qquad [J_z]=\mathrm{N\cdot s},
\]
\[
v_{\mathrm{to}}=\frac{J_z}{m_b},
\qquad [v_{\mathrm{to}}]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\]
\[
h=\frac{v_{\mathrm{to}}^2}{2g},
\qquad [h]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
Assumptions: vertical force only, negligible air resistance, constant body mass, correct force-plate zero, accurate onset and takeoff detection, and a rigid-body center-of-mass approximation. Breakdown regimes: arm swing in CMJ-A adds upper-limb momentum that a simple lower-body impulse model does not fully capture; countermovement depth changes the impulse window; low sampling rate or integration drift biases \(J_z\); and invalid trials such as landing off the plate destroy the operator. The source’s CMJ-A is therefore not just “CMJ plus arms”; it changes the mechanical model and the interpretation of peak velocity.

The reactive strength index operator is
\[
RSI=\frac{h}{t_c},
\qquad [RSI]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\]
where \(h\) is jump height in metres and \(t_c\) is contact time in seconds. The unit is m/s, not a dimensionless score. The singularity is exact: as \(t_c\to0^+\), \(RSI\to\infty\), and at \(t_c=0\) the expression is undefined. In practice, breakdown occurs when contact time is near the force plate’s temporal resolution, when landing strategy changes, or when jump height and contact time are measured from different events. The source reports DJ contact time in ms and RSI in m/s [PAGE 7], so the conversion \(t_c[\mathrm{s}]=t_c[\mathrm{ms}]/1000\) is mandatory before using the equation.

Relative isometric force is
\[
F_{\mathrm{rel}}(t)=\frac{F_z(t)}{m_b},
\qquad [F_{\mathrm{rel}}]=\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}},
\]
with the source recording “Peak force output … at 100 ms and 200 ms” [PAGE 5]. Assumptions include standardized hip and knee angles (\(125^\circ\)–\(145^\circ\) and \(155^\circ\)–\(165^\circ\), respectively) [PAGE 5], consistent onset detection, stable pre-tension, and a standard “pull as hard and fast as possible” instruction [PAGE 5]. Breakdown occurs when onset is manually or automatically mis-detected, when sampling rate is too low for a 100 ms window, when body-mass scaling is applied to a group with different body composition, or when the athlete pre-tensions differently between trials. This is why the ACT/DCT baseline body-mass difference (88±10 kg vs 75±7 kg) [PAGE 3] is a cross-layer confound for relative IMTP.

Finally, multiplicity is a probability operator. With \(k=23\) rows in Supplementary Table S1 [PAGE 13] and nominal \(\alpha=0.05\), the global-null family-wise error under independence is
\[
P(\text{at least one false positive})=1-(1-\alpha)^k
=1-(0.95)^{23}\approx0.693.
\]
This is a probability, not a physical unit. Because the outcomes are correlated, the exact value differs, but the operator proves that uncorrected “significant” rows cannot carry a strong mechanistic claim. The source’s own CI/p-value mismatches—IMTPF100 adjusted difference 1.34 with 95% CI −1.47 to 4.15 and p=0.011; 5–10–5 adjusted difference −0.011 with 95% CI −0.053 to 0.05 and p=0.022; CODAT adjusted difference 0.043 with 95% CI 0.01 to 0.08 and p=0.159 [PAGE 13]—are additional breakdown indicators.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is traceable: complex training pairs high-load resistance with plyometric action in one session; contrast training and French contrast are variants; ascending complex training (ACT) places plyometrics before resistance; descending complex training (DCT) places resistance before plyometrics. The source frames this directly: “ACT appears to be a velocity-oriented approach, whereas DCT is more force-oriented” [PAGE 2]. The historical comparison anchor is Kobal et al. (2017), which the paper says directly compared ACT and DCT in 27 elite young soccer players and found similar half-squat and CMJ improvements, with ACT but not DCT improving 10- and 20-m sprint, and neither improving the 505 agility test [PAGE 2]. The present study retrocausally extends that line to national-level male basketball players, off-season, twice weekly, with multidirectional eccentric-focused plyometrics and IMTP early-force outcomes.

The multi-week traps are visible in the genealogy. First is the “sequence determinism” trap: earlier literature sometimes implies that exercise order determines adaptation. The present data undercut that; the authors conclude that sequencing “acts as a secondary programming variable” [PAGE 10]. Second is the “matched volume solves all” trap: sets, reps, and load were matched, but “basketball practice load (e.g., duration, volume, and rate of perceived exertion) was not formally quantified, which is acknowledged as a limitation” [PAGE 3]. Third is the “single significant p validates mechanism” trap: the CMJ-A PV and IMTPF100 signals coexist with interaction violations and CI inconsistencies [PAGE 7, PAGE 13]. Fourth is the “off-season equals clean laboratory” trap: there was no non-training control group, and the authors state that “The nonrandomized allocation of participants introduces potential selection bias, and the relatively small final sample size (n = 20) limits statistical power and generalizability” [PAGE 10]. Fifth is the “angle-specific transfer” trap: the authors themselves note that “no intervention study in basketball has systematically evaluated pre-post changes across COD tasks covering a wide range of cutting angles (e.g., 45° to 180°)” [PAGE 2]. The 8-week, twice-weekly, 72-h-recovery design is enough to detect some chronic adaptations, but it cannot identify acute potentiation-fatigue kinetics or long-term retention. The genealogy therefore predicts replication failure for broad superiority claims and replication success only for narrow, pre-registered early-force or CMJ-A velocity endpoints.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression here is the coach’s or analyst’s habit of converting a fragile p-value into a program identity: “DCT is the force method” or “ACT is the basketball method.” The source’s own conclusion is the counter-habit: “Exercise sequencing did not result in generalized performance advantages across athletic tasks” [PAGE 10], and “DCT protocol was associated with greater improvements in early-phase isometric force production (IMTP at 100 ms) and CMJ-APV” [PAGE 10]. The recursive trap is that a coach who knows the hypothesis may bias exercise execution, encouragement, or scheduling; participants were blinded, but the design is only “single-blind (referring to participants’ blinding)” [PAGE 3], and allocation was made “in consultation with team coaches” [PAGE 3]. Thus the theory-of-mind layer includes coach expectation, athlete belief about which block is “the real stimulus,” and assessor expectation.

The counter-trap is overcorrection in the opposite direction: declaring ACT superior because it is “velocity-specific” or “basketball-specific.” The data do not support that either. The 5–10–5 and 5 m signals are inconsistent with the 10 m and CODAT results [PAGE 8], and the discussion itself says the significant 5–10–5 and 5 m effects “were not accompanied by corresponding improvements in other sprint or COD tests” [PAGE 9]. A disciplined theory of mind must hold both models simultaneously: DCT may be slightly better for early force and CMJ-A peak velocity, ACT may be equally viable for most jump/sprint/COD outcomes, and athlete preference/logistics may dominate.

The clock layer is off-season time. The intervention is 8 weeks, twice weekly, with a 72-h recovery interval [PAGE 3]; pre/post testing occurred 48–96 h around the sessions [PAGE 3]. That clock measures chronic adaptation, not the acute potentiation-fatigue window that would explain why order matters. The authors admit no direct acute measurements and call the mechanistic interpretation “speculative” [PAGE 9]. Therefore the correct recursive stance is: use DCT when early-force expression is the targeted quality and the athlete tolerates the resistance-first sequence; use ACT when velocity-first exposure or session logistics favor it; do not promise broad sprint/COD/jump superiority from sequence alone. The “clock” veto is that a 2×/week, 8-week off-season block cannot adjudicate in-season maintenance, taper, congestion, or acute post-activation performance.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: the study is in national-level male basketball players, age 17±1, with 6.5–7 years basketball experience and 3.5±1 years resistance-training experience [PAGE 3]. That population and off-season timing veto generalization to children, females, in-season congested schedules, or elite senior players. The absence of a non-training control group [PAGE 10] vetoes attributing all pre-post changes to ACT or DCT; regular basketball practice could contribute. The lack of quantified basketball practice load [PAGE 3] vetoes a clean “matched total load” claim. The small final n=20 with 8/12 allocation [PAGE 3] vetoes strong precision claims, especially for interaction models.

Biomechanical-layer veto: the COD tests are not a full angle spectrum. The CODAT uses 45°, 90°, 90°, and 45° turns over 5 m, 3 m, 3 m, 3 m, and 10 m segments [PAGE 5], while the 5–10–5 is a different cutting pattern. The paper itself says wide-angle COD relies on eccentric braking and maximal force, whereas small-angle COD relies more on fast SSC and lateral reactive force, and that no basketball intervention has systematically covered 45°–180° [PAGE 2]. Therefore a claim that DCT is superior for “change of direction” in general is vetoed by task specificity. The jump layer is similarly bounded: CMJ, CMJ-A, SJ, DJ-40, and DJ-60 are distinct mechanical tasks; the absence of between-group differences in SJ and DJ [PAGE 7] vetoes a global stretch-shortening-cycle claim. The IMTP angles and 100/200 ms windows are standardized, but the significant group×covariate interaction at IMTPF100 [PAGE 7] vetoes a constant adjusted effect.

Weather/environmental-layer veto: the assessments were conducted “on an indoor court” [PAGE 3], and the source reports no temperature, humidity, altitude, or venue-climate variable. Therefore any weather or environmental explanation for the ACT/DCT difference is unsupported. The correct adjudication is “weather veto: not measured, not identified, and not needed for the indoor court protocol.” If the study were transported outdoors, heat/humidity could alter fatigue and force output, but that is a new study, not an inference from this one.

The cross-layer proof is therefore a conjunction: physical population and control limitations + biomechanical task specificity + statistical interaction/multiplicity + missing environmental variables. Each layer independently blocks a broad superiority claim. Only a narrow claim survives: in this off-season, national-level male basketball sample, DCT was associated with selected early-force and CMJ-A peak-velocity advantages, while most performance outcomes were comparable. That is exactly the paper’s “fine-tuning variable” conclusion [PAGE 10].

Veto scenario 1 — Randomized equal-allocation replication with pre-registered interaction check. Design: multicenter randomized ACT vs DCT, 1:1 allocation, n≥40, matched sets/reps/load, blinded assessors, primary endpoints IMTPF100 and CMJ-A peak velocity, pre-registered test of group×baseline interaction. Prediction: if the DCT early-force advantage is real, the adjusted DCT−ACT difference in IMTPF100 should have a 95% CI excluding zero and the interaction should be non-significant; CMJ-A peak velocity should show a positive DCT−ACT difference with a 95% CI excluding zero. Veto rule: reject the DCT early-force claim if the 95% CI includes zero, if the interaction is significant, or if the effect disappears after multiplicity control. This directly tests the current weak points: the IMTPF100 CI crossing zero and the significant interaction [PAGE 7, PAGE 13].

Veto scenario 2 — Acute potentiation-fatigue mechanistic crossover. Design: within-athlete crossover, resistance-first vs plyometric-first, measure IMTPF100, CMJ peak velocity, and surface EMG at baseline, +4, +8, and +12 min after each conditioning block. Prediction: the chronic DCT advantage should be preceded by a greater early-force potentiation window (higher IMTPF100 or CMJPV) after resistance-first than after plyometric-first, without a larger fatigue decrement. Veto rule: if resistance-first produces only fatigue and no potentiation, or if the acute DCT−ACT difference is absent, then the proposed “lower accumulated fatigue on high-load exercise” mechanism is rejected, even if the chronic p-values were small.
