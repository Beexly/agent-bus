<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer asks whether a soft shell can reduce head acceleration. The impulse-momentum relation is
\[
\mathbf J=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F(t)\,dt=\Delta\mathbf p,
\]
and energy absorption is
\[
E_{\mathrm{abs}}=\int F\,dx.
\]
A compliant layer can lower peak force by extending duration or deformation distance, but only if it remains coupled, does not bottom out, and is loaded in the intended direction. On-field football impacts are often oblique, helmet-to-helmet, and variable; the GC can slide, compress, or dislodge. The source reports that GCs “tend to slide off the players helmets during high contact drills or scrimmages” [PAGE 12]. That is a physical treatment-fidelity problem. No physical law guarantees attenuation in this configuration. The physical layer therefore vetoes a strong claim that GCs must reduce PLA or PAA universally; it does not veto the paper’s narrower null result.

The biomechanical layer asks whether PLA and PAA are sufficient proxies for brain injury. The paper measured PLA, PAA, and impact count, but did not measure diffuse axonal multi-axis general evaluation or head acceleration response metric, which prior work reported [PAGE 11]. It also did not track concussions: “concussions were not tracked during this study” [PAGE 10], and “no concussion rates were tracked during this study as the performance period was too short” [PAGE 12]. The iMM threshold was 5 G, detection was approximately 95%, and video verification removed non-verified events [PAGE 7-8]. These are reasonable field-methods constraints, but they mean the study cannot adjudicate clinical concussion reduction. The biomechanical layer vetoes any inference from “no PLA/PAA difference” to “no concussion benefit” or “proven ineffective for brain injury.” It permits only the kinematic conclusion.

The weather layer is unmeasured. The source provides no temperature, humidity, precipitation, or heat-index data. Weather can affect foam stiffness, mouthguard compliance, hydration, fatigue, and practice intensity. Absence of weather control is a limitation, not evidence for or against GC efficacy. The weather layer therefore vetoes post hoc weather rescue claims and post hoc weather dismissal claims equally. The cross-layer adjudication is: the physical layer shows plausible mechanisms but also fidelity failures; the biomechanical layer shows proxy limits and missing clinical outcomes; the weather layer is silent. The only defensible verdict is a conditional negative: under the observed practice conditions, with imperfect cap fidelity and no behavioral or clinical control, no significant kinematic attenuation was detected. Strong causal claims in either direction are vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

\[
y_{ijk}=\mu+\tau_j+u_i+\epsilon_{ijk},
\qquad
u_i\sim N(0,\sigma_u^2),
\qquad
\epsilon_{ijk}\sim N(0,\sigma^2).
\]
The design effect is
\[
DE=1+(m-1)\rho,
\]
where \(m\) is the average number of impacts per player and \(\rho\) is the intraclass correlation. Prediction: if \(\rho\) is positive, the effective sample size falls, standard errors widen, and the full-sample p-values for PLA and PAA become larger than the reported 0.20 and 0.51 [PAGE 9]. The seven-player repeated-measures estimates remain directionally unchanged but with very wide intervals.
