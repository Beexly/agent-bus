"If a given individual has had little or no opportunity for normal experience, he is likely to become 'fixated' upon some perverted practice, a practice for which he has had the opportunity" [PAGE 308]. The strong original urge does not dissipate; it conducts a trial-and-error search over whatever practices are environmentally available. Fourth, he complicates pure learning with biological heterogeneity: "There are probably, in part, actual differences of biological make-up which predetermine some individuals to find more satiation in certain practices than in others" [PAGE 308]. This introduces a non-uniform payoff landscape over the readiness manifold. Fifth, sex is uniquely subordinated to exogenous motivational systems: "sex, to a far greater extent than food hunger, is subject to the control of all sorts of other appetites and aversions, to all sorts of contributing social goals and inhibitions" [PAGE 308]. Sixth, and most cognitively radical for 1932, Tolman asserts that sex-activities are learned "on a basis of purely verbal reports"; the adolescent "comes to his sex-life already provided with innumerable mores" and has "already made very definite selections as to what the 'good' ... sex activities are to be before he has actually tried any of them out" [PAGE 308]. Thus verbal instruction functions as a pre-experience selection operator that can rank behavioral candidates prior to reinforcement.

Gray-zone audit: (1) Tolman's term "perverted" is normatively loaded; the text never defines dysfunction except by deviation from statistically normal experience, conflating statistical rarity with functional impairment. (2) The boundary between selection and shaping is unstable: does early experience merely choose among pre-existing readinesses, or does it modify the readinesses themselves? Tolman gestures at both without resolving the tension. (3) Verbal report learning is under-specified: we are not told whether mores operate by creating conditioned emotional responses (shame, desire) or by constructing propositional expectations that then guide attention. (4) The "sign gestalt-readiness" concept lacks a formal metric for similarity among readinesses, making it impossible to state how far a fixated practice must deviate from the normal target before the system reclassifies it as a distinct attractor rather than a degraded approximation.

Recursive implication: The first actual sexual encounter is not a blank-slate test of innate preference but a disconfirmation or confirmation experiment against a verbally pre-loaded hypothesis. If the verbal mores predict satiation from practice A, but actual experience delivers quiescence from practice B, the system must either revise the mores, suppress B, or develop conflict. This creates a second-order learning problem: the organism must learn not only which practice yields quiescence but also how to reconcile that with socially transmitted expectations. The gray zone widens because Tolman provides no operator for resolving conflict between Q(actual experience) and V(verbal report) when they assign different maximizers.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \mathcal{R} = \{r_1, \dots, r_n\} be the finite set of initial sign gestalt-readinesses cited on [PAGE 308]. Let E denote early experience history and \mathcal{M} the set of verbal mores received before any sexual experience.

Selection operator:
\[
\Sigma_E : \mathcal{R} \to \mathcal{R}^*, \qquad \mathcal{R}^* \subseteq \mathcal{R},
\]
where \mathcal{R}^* is the adult behavioral repertoire. Tolman's claim is that \Sigma_E retains r_i iff r_i yields sexual quiescence under E.

Quiescence payoff:
\[
Q : \mathcal{R} \times \mathcal{E} \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0},
\]
units: dimensionless quiescence index. [PAGE 308] supports Q > 0 for retained practices; Q = 0 for pruned practices.

Fixation singularity:
If normal experience N = \emptyset and available set is \mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{R}, fixation occurs at
\[
p = \arg\max_{r \in \mathcal{A}} Q(r, E),
\]
provided Q(p,E) > 0. If Q(r,E) = 0 for all r \in \mathcal{A}, the system reaches a frustration breakdown: the original urge has no quiescence-producing outlet, and no stable adult selection emerges.

Verbal pre-selection:
\[
\mathcal{V} : \mathcal{M} \to 2^{\mathcal{R}},
\]
mapping mores to a pre-experience ranking. The adolescent's pre-behavioral selection is
\[
r^{(0)} = \arg\max_{r \in \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M})} \Phi(r),
\]
where \Phi is the socially encoded "goodness" or expected satiation. This occurs before E_sexual, as noted on [PAGE 308].

Joint selection rule (softmax over experience and verbal evidence):
\[
P(r_i \mid E, \mathcal{M}) =
\frac{
\exp\!\big(\beta Q(r_i,E) + \gamma \Psi(r_i,\mathcal{M})\big)
}{
\sum_{j=1}^{n} \exp\!\big(\beta Q(r_j,E) + \gamma \Psi(r_j,\mathcal{M})\big)
},
\]
with \beta, \gamma > 0 in units of inverse quiescence index; \Psi is the verbal-value function. Breakdown regime: as \gamma / \beta \to \infty, behavior is governed entirely by verbal mores and may become decoupled from physical quiescence (symbolic satiation without behavioral reinforcement).

Biological heterogeneity:
\[
Q(r,E) = Q_0(r) + \delta Q_{\text{bio}}(r) + \delta Q_{\text{exp}}(r,E),
\]
where \delta Q_{\text{bio}} encodes "actual differences of biological make-up" [PAGE 308]. Singularity: if \delta Q_{\text{bio}}(r) < -Q_0(r) for all socially permitted r, the practice is biomechanically or endocrinologically inaccessible regardless of learning.

Citations and verbatim material: all equations are grounded in [PAGE 308]; footnote 10 references Hunter (1920), Psychol Rev, 27, 247–269 [PAGE 308, fn 10].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman's page 308 sits at the intersection of three lineages. First, the footnote cites W. S. Hunter, "The modification of instinct from the standpoint of social psychology," Psychol Rev, 1920, 27, 247–269 [PAGE 308, fn 10], placing Tolman within the early behaviorist project of re-conceptualizing instinct as modifiable habit rather than fixed action pattern. Second, Tolman's purposive behaviorism treats the organism as selecting among goal-directed routes to satiation, a direct precursor to cognitive maps and expectancy-value models of the 1940s–1950s. Third, the fixation concept echoes Freudian psychoanalysis but is stripped of psychosexual stage theory; for Tolman, fixation is a learning-history artifact caused by absent normal experience, not an unavoidable developmental catastrophe.

Multi-week trap: The verbal mores described on [PAGE 308] are acquired over months and years of adolescence, well before the first sexual act. This creates a latency trap: the social-symbolic selection \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}) is computed on a slow developmental clock (weeks to years), while the sexual urge operates on a fast physiological clock (minutes to hours). If the slow clock pre-selects a practice that the fast clock cannot physically satisfy, or if the first actual experience produces quiescence from a different practice, the organism must resolve a cross-timescale mismatch. The trap recurs whenever the individual encounters a novel partner or social context: the pre-loaded verbal schema must be re-evaluated against new reinforcement, potentially generating weeks of hesitation, avoidance, or conflict before a new stable selection is consolidated.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression operates as follows. Under stress, novelty, or intoxication, the social-inhibitory layer that maintains adult sex-behavior within the bounds of \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}) is weakened. The organism regresses to the earliest available readiness that yielded quiescence, which in a deprived history is the fixated practice p identified on [PAGE 308]. This is not a return to infancy but a collapse of the counter-trap.

The counter-trap is recursive theory of mind: the adolescent models how significant others will evaluate each possible practice. Because the mores are verbal and pre-experiential [PAGE 308], the individual forms second-order desires—desires about which desires ought to be satisfied. When the first actual experience contradicts the verbal schema (e.g., quiescence arises from a practice the mores label "bad"), the subject must simultaneously update the object-level selection and the meta-level self-model. This produces a recursive loop: shame inhibits the effective practice, which increases urge, which heightens sensitivity to social evaluation, which deepens shame.

Clock layer: There are two clocks. Clock A is the developmental acquisition of mores (slow, cumulative, verbal). Clock B is pubertal urgency (fast, hormonal, nonverbal). When Clock B outruns Clock A, the subject acts with an incomplete or contradictory social model; when Clock A dominates Clock B, behavior may be suppressed indefinitely despite biological readiness. The recursive theory-of-mind failure occurs when the subject cannot distinguish whether a given urge is genuinely their own selection \Sigma_E or an internalized verbal command \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}) masquerading as desire.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
I adjudicate behavior through a four-layer veto stack. Each layer is a partial function \mathcal{V}_k : \mathcal{R} \rightharpoonup \mathcal{R}; if \mathcal{V}_k(r) = \emptyset, practice r is excluded.

Layer 1—Biological/endocrinological veto: [PAGE 308] acknowledges "actual differences of biological make-up which predetermine some individuals to find more satiation in certain practices than in others." If the hormonal or neurological substrate does not support a practice, Q(r,E) collapses to zero regardless of learning. \mathcal{V}_{\text{bio}} is a hard prerequisite.

Layer 2—Biomechanical veto: anatomical and physical constraints determine which practices can mechanically produce sexual quiescence. This is implicit in the trial-and-error search on [PAGE 308]: only practices that are physically executable can be "tried out" and found to "lead to some degree of sexual quiescence."

Layer 3—Environmental/social weather veto: the social climate—availability of partners, privacy, cultural prohibition, taboo—acts as a weather system that gates access to the available set \mathcal{A}. If \mathcal{A} is empty because the environment offers no opportunity, fixation cannot settle on any practice; the urge remains unquiesced. This layer is non-meteorological but functions as environmental contingency.

Layer 4—Verbal/mores veto: \mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}) can suppress a practice even when Layers 1–3 permit it. Because mores are pre-experiential [PAGE 308], this veto operates before any behavioral data exist.

Composition:
\[
\mathcal{R}_{\text{allowed}} = \mathcal{V}_{\text{verbal}} \circ \mathcal{V}_{\text{weather}} \circ \mathcal{V}_{\text{biomech}} \circ \mathcal{V}_{\text{bio}} (\mathcal{R}).
\]
Breakdown: if \mathcal{V}_{\text{bio}} and \mathcal{V}_{\text{biomech}} return r but \mathcal{V}_{\text{verbal}} returns \emptyset, the result is not elimination of urge but substitution or neurotic conflict, because the original strong urge described on [PAGE 308] continues to search for any available outlet.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto Scenario 1. Counterfactual: An adolescent receives verbal mores \mathcal{M} that strongly prohibit normal experience N, yet N is physically and socially available. Verification: compare pre-experience self-report with post-experience behavior. Prediction: The verbal veto raises the selection threshold for N but does not eliminate it; because the original urge is strong [PAGE 308], N will still be attempted, but quiescence will be accompanied by elevated shame, producing an approach-avoidance oscillation rather than clean selection.

Veto Scenario 2. Counterfactual: Normal experience is entirely absent (\mathcal{A} excludes N), and biological make-up strongly predisposes the individual to a single available practice P that verbal mores prohibit. Verification: observe whether fixation develops on P despite prohibition. Prediction: Yes. Tolman's mechanism [PAGE 308] states that with little or no normal opportunity, the individual becomes fixated on "some perverted practice, a practice for which he has had the opportunity." The verbal veto cannot create quiescence where none exists; it can only suppress expression, driving the practice underground or into fantasy.

Veto Scenario 3. Counterfactual: Verbal pre-selection ranks practice A as the sole satiation-producing activity (\mathcal{V}(\mathcal{M}) = \{A\}), but the first actual experience yields Q(A, E_actual) \approx 0 while an unranked practice B yields Q(B, E_actual) > 0. Verification: measure self-reported mores before and after first experience, and track practice choice over subsequent trials. Prediction: Rapid re-ranking. Direct reinforcement overrides the verbal pre-selection within one to a few trials; the selection operator \Sigma_E supersedes \mathcal{V}. Failure to re-rank indicates that the verbal layer has achieved pathological dominance, decoupling behavior from quiescence entirely.
