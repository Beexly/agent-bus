   Verbatim basis [PAGE 15]: “deleting any items that (1) have already been purchased or rated by the user, (2) have a negative score, or (3) do not fall within the designated product group… or category.” Strictly, C_j = 0 survives because only negative scores are deleted.

7. Stochastic cart insertion (step 192 [PAGE 15]):
   For c ∈ Cart(u), pos(c) ~ Uniform{1,…,M}, M = 15 (history path). Verbatim: “inserted at randomly-selected locations within the top M (e.g., 15) positions.”

8. Output (step 194 [PAGE 15]):
   Return Top_M(𝓕 ⊕ Π_rand(Cart items)) to Web server 32.

Shopping-cart path [PAGE 5] (Section VI, FIG. 7):
   Threshold invocation: |Cart_{popular}(u)| > 1.
   Step 282: retrieve similar-items lists from table 60 for each popular cart item.
   Step 286: merge lists summing CI values: C_j^{cart} = Σ CI_{i→j}.
   Step 288: sort descending.
   Step 290: veto j if j ∈ Cart(u) ∨ j ∈ Purchased(u) ∨ j ∈ Rated(u).

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
   • τ(Δt) undefined on (0, 6) months; any interpolation is an unmodeled invention.
   • b_{u,i} undefined for items with no rating and no purchase (cold start).
   • w_{u,i} = 0 is unreachable under stated integer ratings, contradicting the text’s “weights of zero and below” claim.
   • CI value ranges and distributions are not specified in the excerpt; if CI can be negative, sign propagation flips.
   • Exact C_j = 0 is measure-zero under continuous CI but constructible under discrete CI, creating a filter edge case where zero-scored items survive and may occupy top-M slots if positive items are scarce.
   • Web-server latency and client rendering at step 194 [PAGE 15] are unbounded, introducing a network veto outside the recommendation algebra.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The patent US 6,266,649 B1 sits in the lineage of item-based collaborative filtering, most directly the Amazon item-to-item system described by Linden, Smith, and York (IEEE Internet Computing, 2003) and the foundational item-item CF work of Sarwar et al. (WWW 2001). Its architectural break from user-user CF is computational: instead of computing similarities among users online, the system precomputes a similar-items table 60 offline and retrieves per-item lists at query time [PAGE 15]. This shifts the online burden from O(|U|) to O(|I_{history}|), making the system scalable to large catalogs but freezing the similarity geometry until the offline table is rebuilt.

Retrocausally, the system is a closed temporal loop: past ratings and purchases determine b_{u,i} and τ(Δt), which determine present recommendations; user acceptance of those recommendations generates future purchases and ratings; and the explicit feedback mechanism—“Indicating ownership of an item causes the item to be added to the user's purchase history listing” [PAGE 15]—feeds the present output back into the historical state that conditions the next round. The recency kernel privileges order_date = now with τ=2, creating a sharp present-past boundary: an item purchased one minute ago receives the maximum multiplier, while an item purchased six months and one day ago collapses to τ=1. This temporal asymmetry means the recommendation state is not time-translation invariant; a user’s history is reweighted every time the clock crosses the six-month threshold.

Multi-week traps arise from this structure. First, the six-month cliff: because τ is specified only at Δt = 0 and Δt ≥ 6, implementations that threshold at exactly 180 days will produce discontinuous weight halving for items near that boundary, causing week-over-week recommendation instability for anything purchased in the 175–185 day window. Second, category stasis: step 190 filters by a “designated product group or category” [PAGE 15], but the patent provides no temporal decay on old ratings outside the purchase-recency kernel, so a user’s category preference from three months ago weights equally with one from yesterday unless a purchase intervenes. Third, cart ephemerality versus history durability: the shopping-cart service [PAGE 5] acts on contents that persist for minutes to days, whereas the item-to-item service acts on years of purchase history. A single session of cart-stuffing can trigger M=5 cart-based recommendations that override longer-term signals, injecting short-timescale noise into a long-timescale model. Finally, the ownership-feedback loop creates a retrocausal filter bubble: accepting a recommendation and marking it as owned expands the purchase history, which increases b_{u,i}=5 weights for similar items, making future recommendations increasingly self-referential.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: when the user history is sparse—new user, new item, or thin category—the system lacks sufficient b_{u,i} signal. In the shopping-cart variant [PAGE 5], invocation is automatic once the cart contains more than a threshold number (e.g., 1) of popular items, and recommendations are generated “exclusively on the current contents of the shopping cart.” This is a habit regression to a popularity prior: instead of personalized collaborative filtering, the system falls back to cart-triggered, short-window association. The “popular item” gate acts as a low-signal bypass, regressing from individualized taste to aggregate cart co-occurrence.

Counter-trap: the user possesses an explicit model of the system through the “refine your recommendations” link [PAGE 15], which allows rating or indicating ownership of recommended items. This is a counter-trap against system drift: the user can reshape the very history that conditions future weights. Adversarially, a user could add items to the cart purely to induce recommendations of complements (cart stuffing), or rate decoy items negatively to inject w_{u,i} < 0 and suppress competitors through composite-score cancellation in step 186 [PAGE 15]. Because negative scores are summed in the merge step, a small number of negative-weight items can veto high-CI neighbors.

Clock: the system operates on multiple asynchronous clocks. The history-based path runs at session request time with M=15 [PAGE 15]; the cart path runs at cart-display time with M=5 [PAGE 5]; and step 192 inserts cart items at random positions within the top M, introducing temporal jitter so that identical histories produce different visible rankings on reload [PAGE 15]. The top-M truncation imposes an attentional deadline: items below rank M are pruned before the user ever sees them. The system’s theory of mind assumes cart contents reflect current purchase intent; the user’s theory of mind assumes recommendations reflect stable preference. These models diverge most sharply at the clock boundary where cart-driven recency (τ=2 if order_date=now) collides with history-driven valence, meaning the system may recommend cart complements the user never intended to buy, simply because the cart clock overrode the history clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The recommendation stack can be decomposed into veto layers, each capable of suppressing an item independent of its intrinsic similarity:

Data layer (similar-items table 60 [PAGE 15], CI values [PAGE 5]): an item pair absent from the table receives no edge; CI = 0 yields s_{i→j} = 0 regardless of weight.

Compute layer (weight and score [PAGE 15]): if w_{u,i} < 0, then s_{i→j} < 0 for any CI > 0, injecting a negative contribution into C_j. Because merging sums scores, a negative edge can veto a target item even when other edges are positive, provided the composite drops below zero.

Filter layer (steps 190 and 290 [PAGE 15], [PAGE 5]): hard logical vetoes remove j if (a) already purchased/rated, (b) C_j < 0, or (c) outside the designated category/product group; in the cart path, j ∈ Cart(u) is also deleted. C_j = 0 survives step 190 but ranks below all positive items.

Network/presentation layer (Web server 32, FIG. 6 [PAGE 15]): even if an item survives all algebraic vetoes, it must be rendered in the top M of the web page and selected by the user. Server latency, page-load failure, and “more recommendations” pagination are probabilistic vetoes outside the formal algebra.

Biomechanical layer: the user must dwell on the page, parse five listed items [PAGE 15], and click a link. Reading time, motor control, and device form factor veto items that are technically recommended but never perceived.

Weather/exogenous layer: the patent mentions capturing search terms as a possible content-based filter [PAGE 5], but contains no weather variable. Real-world demand shifts (e.g., regional weather affecting category demand) are unmodeled; they pass through as noise rather than being explicitly vetoed or admitted.

Adjudication order: the filter layer is the final deterministic gate before output; network and biomechanical layers are stochastic vetoes that act after step 194. No layer in the provided text accounts for weather, so weather-driven preference shifts are neither weighted nor filtered, representing an exogenous blind spot in the cross-layer stack.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) veto scenarios, each with a concrete prediction:

Scenario 1 — Negative-weight composite veto. Construct user u with two history items: A purchased now with rating 5 (b=5, τ=2, w=10) similar to target T with CI=0.08, giving s_{A→T}=0.8; and B rated “1,” not purchased (b=-3, τ=1, w=-3) similar to T with CI=0.30, giving s_{B→T}=-0.9. The merge yields C_T = -0.1, which step 190 deletes as negative. Verification: inject this history into the recommendation engine and query for T. Prediction: T is absent from the returned top-M list despite possessing a strong positive edge (0.8) because the composite veto threshold C_j ≥ 0 is violated. A null result (T appears) would falsify the strict negative-score deletion rule on [PAGE 15].

Scenario 2 — Six-month recency cliff veto. Purchase item X at Δt = 179 days versus Δt = 181 days, with X similar to neighbors Y and Z at fixed CI values. If the implementation thresholds τ at 180 days, the weight on all edges out of X halves discontinuously (τ drops from >1 toward 1 at the boundary), halving C_Y and C_Z. Verification: run the recommendation generator with system clock set to 179 and 181 days post-purchase, holding all else constant, and measure the score ratio C_Y(179)/C_Y(181). Prediction: a discontinuous drop consistent with the endpoint-only specification of τ on [PAGE 15]; a smooth decay curve would indicate an unlisted interpolation law and thus a deviation from the disclosed source text.

Scenario 3 — Zero-score survival versus random-insertion veto. Engineer two similar-items lists for target T such that their weighted scores sum to exactly C_T = 0 (e.g., +0.5 from one list, -0.5 from another). Step 190 deletes only negative scores, so T survives and ranks at the bottom of the positive field. Run 100 trials with no cart items and M=15. Prediction: T appears in the top 15 only if fewer than 15 items have C_j > 0; its appearance rate is bounded by (15 - N_{positive})/15. Now add one cart item subject to step 192 random insertion in the top 15; prediction: T’s appearance rate drops further because Π_rand can displace it even when it would otherwise rank within M. A deterministic appearance of T at a fixed rank would falsify the random-insertion clause on [PAGE 15].
