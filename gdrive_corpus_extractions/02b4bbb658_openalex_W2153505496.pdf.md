<THINK_TRACE>
The source’s estimand is not a generic “sports violence” effect. It is a count of recorded domestic-violence incidents in Strathclyde, observed for 30 police subdivisions across 3200 days from 1 January 2003 to 5 October 2011, with the day defined from 12noon to 11.59am the following day so that early-morning incidents attach to the prior match day [PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. The paper’s first methodological move is to note that because the football regressors vary over time but not across subdivisions, a regression on the Strathclyde aggregate is equivalent to a fixed-effects panel model at subdivision level [PAGE 10]. That equivalence is important: the headline Old Firm coefficient is not a neighborhood-level causal effect; it is an aggregate time-series/count effect that survives because the treatment is time-assigned. The second move is to decompose football exposure into (1) existence of a match, (2) context of the match, and (3) what happens during the match [PAGE 5]. This decomposition is the multi-hop spine: Old Firm existence is one treatment; non-Old Firm existence is another; context includes home/away, television, importance, traditional rival, and referee activity; outcome includes win/draw/loss and outcome relative to pre-match expectations. The paper’s abstract already states the main result: “Old Firm matches, where Glasgow rivals Celtic and Rangers play, are associated with large increases in domestic violence (regardless of the timing or the outcome of the match)” [PAGE 2]. It also states the negative result: “Non-Old Firm matches tend to have little impact on domestic violence” and “we find little evidence for the importance of reference points” [PAGE 2].

The count model is a Poisson log-link model. If \(y_t\) is the number of incidents per day in Strathclyde, then \(y_t\mid X_t\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_t)\), with \(\lambda_t\) in incidents/day and
\[
\log \lambda_t=\beta_0+\sum_k \beta_k D_{kt},
\]
where each \(D_{kt}\in\{0,1\}\) is a dummy. The \(\beta_k\) are dimensionless log-rate changes; \(\exp(\beta_k)\) is a multiplicative rate ratio. The paper treats the coefficients as approximate percentage changes. In model (A2), the Old Firm coefficient is \(0.359\), and the paper says “the average level of domestic violence in Strathclyde increases by some 36% on days when there is an Old Firm match” [PAGE 17]. The Christmas/New Year coefficient is about \(0.33\), i.e. a 33% increase [PAGE 16]. The rough equality of these magnitudes is the core empirical comparison: an Old Firm fixture is about as large a count shock as the Christmas holiday period. The reference-point test is then a second-layer model: match outcomes are classified by pre-match implied probability \(q\). The source says: “if the pre-match winning probability is at least as large as 70% (q ≥0.7) then the team is expected to win” [PAGE 15]. The paper’s table categories are expected-win, tight, and expected-loss, crossed with win/draw/loss. The key identification problem is that both Celtic and Rangers are strong, so neither is ever classed as expected to lose; and all Old Firm matches are predicted tight: “the match is invariably predicted to be close” [PAGE 10]. Therefore the data cannot identify an “upset win” in the Card–Dahl sense, and Old Firm matches cannot generate an “unexpected” result under the \(q\)-classification. That is a structural gray zone, not a statistical nuisance.

The gray-zone audit has several layers. First, the data are over-dispersed: “the mean is 65.4 whilst the variance is 485.5” [PAGE 15]. Poisson standard errors are therefore suspect, so the paper reports robust standard errors and also reports negative-binomial estimates that are “virtually the same” [PAGE 15; PAGE 29–30]. Second, the “important” category is deliberately restrictive: top-half split with points gap no larger than 10, yielding very few observations; in model (B2) two parameters are omitted because there are no observations in those cells [PAGE 21–22]. Third, the extended-importance model (B3) changes the conclusion, and the one surviving “upset” effect is tied to a single match: “The average number of domestic violence incidents midweek in 2008 was 61.5 but following Rangers’ defeat on 22nd May 2008 there were 77 reported domestic violence incidents” [PAGE 23]. Fourth, television and importance are entangled: the television effect in model (A3) disappears after controlling for importance in model (A4), so a naive “do not schedule both teams on the same day” recommendation is not supported [PAGE 17–18]. Fifth, the mixed-effects model finds little coefficient variation across subdivisions, but the source warns that “the average police subdivision contains about 75,000 inhabitants and this degree of spatial resolution may be too coarse to pick up effects associated with neighbourhoods of a particular sectarian hue” [PAGE 26]. Sixth, the paper controls year, day of week, and Christmas/New Year [PAGE 16], but the pages do not report weather, alcohol, policing, or reporting-intensity controls. So the Old Firm effect is robust to the paper’s controls, but a strict causal veto is not established.

The reference-point result is therefore best read as a context-dependent veto on Card–Dahl. In American football, upset losses were associated with about a 10% increase in domestic violence; in Glasgow, the dominant effect is the fixture itself, not the disappointment relative to expectation. The paper’s own hypothesis is that “the angle of this kink is reduced by other salient features of the league” [PAGE 27]. That is a multi-hop causal claim: the Old Firm rivalry is so emotionally salient that the gain–loss kink around expected result is flattened or swamped. The evidence is consistent with that, but not decisive, because the reference-point cells are sparse and because outcome and fixture are not fully separable for Old Firm matches.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(t=1,\dots,3200\) index days and \(i=1,\dots,30\) index Strathclyde Police subdivisions. Let \(y_{it}\in\mathbb{N}_0\) be recorded domestic-violence incidents per subdivision-day. The aggregate count is \(y_t=\sum_i y_{it}\). Units: \(y_t\) is incidents/day; \(\lambda_t\) is the Poisson mean in incidents/day; \(\beta\) coefficients are dimensionless log-rate changes; \(\exp(\beta)\) is a rate multiplier.

The source’s mixed-effects Poisson specification is stated verbatim as:
\[
\Pr(y_{it}=y\mid \alpha_i)=\frac{\exp(-\lambda_{it})\lambda_{it}^{y}}{y!},\qquad y=0,1,\dots
\]
and “The mean is given by: \(\lambda_{it}=\exp(Z_t\beta+W_t\alpha_i)\)” [PAGE 25]. Here \(Z_t\) are regressors with common coefficients \(\beta\), \(W_t\) are regressors with subdivision-varying coefficients \(\alpha_i\), and \(\alpha_i\) is multivariate normal with diagonal covariance. The mixed-effects model is fragile when \(\dim(\alpha_i)\) is large; the paper therefore lets only `oldfirm close`, `oldfirm rlose`, `cfavlose`, and `rfavlose` vary [PAGE 25]. That is a real identifiability breakdown regime: if too many random coefficients are included, the covariance matrix and coefficient means become imprecisely estimated.

The aggregate count model can be written compactly as:
\[
y_t\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_t),\qquad 
\log\lambda_t=\beta_0+\beta_{\text{conly}}C_t+\beta_{\text{ronly}}R_t+\beta_{\text{candr}\neg}B_t+\beta_{\text{old}}O_t+\gamma'X_t+\delta'Z_t,
\]
where \(C_t,R_t,B_t,O_t\in\{0,1\}\) identify only-Celtic, only-Rangers, both-but-not-Old-Firm, and Old Firm days; \(X_t\) contains context/salience/outcome dummies; \(Z_t\) contains year, day-of-week, and Christmas/New Year controls. The source says: “In all of the regressions reported in this paper we control for year (2003 is the omitted dummy variable), day of the week (Saturday is the omitted variable) and the Christmas and New Year holiday period” [PAGE 16]. The paper’s Table 2 defines the match-day indicators; Table 3 defines outcome relative to pre-match expectation [PAGE 11–12][PAGE 15–16].

Reference-dependent utility is not directly estimated, but the paper’s theoretical object can be formalized without inventing fake ratios. Let \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) be a normalized match outcome and \(r=\mathbb{E}[x\mid \mathcal{F}_t]\) the rationally expected reference point. Let \(u(x)\) be consumption utility and \(\mu(x-r)\) gain–loss utility:
\[
U(x\mid r)=u(x)+\eta\,\mu(x-r),\qquad
\mu(x-r)=
\begin{cases}
(x-r), & x\ge r,\\
\lambda\,(x-r), & x<r,
\end{cases}
\]
with \(\lambda>1\) and \(\eta\ge0\) dimensionless. Units: \(x,r\) in normalized outcome units; \(u,\mu,U\) in utility units; \(\lambda,\eta\) dimensionless. This is the Kahneman–Tversky / Köszegi–Rabin lineage cited by the paper [PAGE 4]. The paper’s test is reduced-form: if \(\eta\) is large and \(\lambda>1\), then \(x<r\) should raise domestic-violence counts relative to \(x\ge r\). The source finds this only in a narrow set of end-of-season “important” games, not generally [PAGE 20–23].

The salience/outcome operators are: `cfavwin`, `cfavdraw`, `cfavlose`, `ctightwin`, `ctightdraw`, `ctightlose` and the Rangers analogues; `oldfirm draw`, `oldfirm close`, `oldfirm rlose`; `cimp`, `rimp`, `oldfirm imp`; `cvstr`, `rvstr`; `ctv`, `rtv`; `chome`, `rhome`; `cref`, `rref`, `oldfirm ref`; and `sal` interactions for important/traditional-rival/referee-active matches [PAGE 12–16][PAGE 21]. The expected-win threshold is \(q\ge0.7\); the tight interval should be read as \(0.25<q<0.7\), although the source text prints “\(0.7<q<0.25\)” [PAGE 15]. That is a typographical singularity: taken literally, the interval is empty, so the corrected interval is the only coherent reading.

Breakdown regimes: (i) Poisson equidispersion fails because variance/mean \(\approx 485.5/65.4\approx7.42\); robust and negative-binomial checks are required [PAGE 15][PAGE 29–30]. (ii) Sparse cells: important-game estimates can be omitted because no observations fall into the category [PAGE 21–22]. (iii) Perfect or near-perfect collinearity: Old Firm matches are all in Glasgow, all televised, and all between the same two clubs; context effects are entangled, as shown by the disappearance of the television effect after importance controls [PAGE 17–18]. (iv) Measurement breakdown: the \(q\)-classification cannot generate upset wins because neither club is ever expected to lose, and Old Firm matches are always tight [PAGE 10][PAGE 15]. (v) Spatial aggregation: subdivision-level random effects may be too coarse to detect sectarian neighborhoods [PAGE 26]. (vi) Omitted-layer breakdown: no weather, alcohol, or policing-intensity controls are reported on these pages, so residual confounding cannot be formally excluded [PAGE 16].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from prospect theory and loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky 1979) through the endowment effect and reference-dependent choice, then to Köszegi and Rabin’s rationally expected reference point, and then to Card and Dahl’s American-football test [PAGE 4]. Card and Dahl found that unexpected losses were associated with roughly a 10% increase in domestic violence, while expected losses and unexpected wins were not; the paper summarizes that result and contrasts it with Glasgow [PAGE 3–4]. Priks’s Swedish hooliganism study used league position as a reference point and found that unfulfilled expectations increased object-throwing [PAGE 5]. Crawford and Meng’s taxi-driver study showed that loss aversion around an earnings target depends on whether the target has already been reached [PAGE 5]. Finkel’s impelling/inhibiting distinction is cited as the psychological-mechanism background [PAGE 3]. The Glasgow lineage adds the Old Firm’s city derby, sectarian divide, and political identity: Celtic identified with Catholicism and Irish nationalism, Rangers with Protestantism and Ulster unionism [PAGE 6–7]. The 2011 Joint Action Group and the 2012 Rangers insolvency/administration/third-division relocation are historical shocks that turn the Old Firm into a natural experiment [PAGE 3][PAGE 7][PAGE 27].

The multi-week traps are temporal, not merely statistical. First, there is a general upward trend in recorded incidents over 2003–2011, plausibly due to reporting/awareness changes rather than incidence [PAGE 8]. Second, weekends and Christmas/New Year are strong: the Xmas/NY coefficient is about 0.33, and the paper explicitly controls for it [PAGE 9][PAGE 16]. Third, the SPL season structure creates a post-split importance window and an end-of-season title-race window; the restrictive “important” definition yields very few matches, while the extended definition changes the reference-point conclusion [PAGE 13–14][PAGE 22–23]. Fourth, the 2008 UEFA Cup final and the 22 May 2008 Rangers defeat at Aberdeen are a single-match temporal cluster that can drive an apparently significant coefficient [PAGE 23]. Fifth, the post-2012 absence of Rangers creates a multi-year natural experiment: the paper predicts “a major fall in domestic violence incidents in Glasgow due to the absence of the Old Firm fixtures” [PAGE 7]. A retrocausal genealogy therefore warns against treating any one season, one holiday, or one end-of-season match as a stable reference-point effect.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper is explicit that it is “agnostic about the psychological mechanism through which football causes domestic violence” [PAGE 3]. A recursive theory-of-mind reading therefore should be framed as interpretation, not as the paper’s tested mechanism. First-order: a fan forms an expectation \(r\) about the team’s result and evaluates \(x-r\); loss aversion predicts a stronger negative cue when \(x<r\). Second-order: the fan models rival fans’ expectations and the sectarian meaning of the fixture; the Old Firm is not just a match but an identity contest [PAGE 6–7]. Third-order: household members may be co-fans, rival fans, or non-fans, so the same match can produce asymmetric emotional cues within the same home. The paper’s aggregate design cannot observe these recursive beliefs; it observes only counts.

Three competing temporal-psychological models fit the evidence differently. The “crisis habit regression” model says the Old Firm fixture itself is the cue: the entrenched rivalry script raises violence regardless of result. This matches the abstract’s “regardless of the timing or the outcome of the match” [PAGE 2] and the positive coefficients for Old Firm draw/close/rlose: about 39%, 41%, and 29% in model (A6) [PAGE 19]. The “counter-trap” model is Card–Dahl’s reference-point prediction: upset non-wins should spike violence. In Glasgow this appears only in tightly defined important end-of-season games: Celtic expected-win draw about +37%, Rangers expected-win draw about +24%, Rangers tight loss about +16%, while important wins show no significant effect [PAGE 21–23]. The “clock” model is methodological and behavioral: the noon-to-noon day construction attaches early-morning incidents to the previous day’s match [PAGE 8]; Christmas/New Year and weekends are separate clocks [PAGE 9][PAGE 16]; and end-of-season matches are a seasonal clock [PAGE 13–14]. The crisis-habit model wins on broad Old Firm effects; the counter-trap model wins only in a narrow clock window; the clock model explains why naive daily aggregation can mislead.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical layer does not veto the Old Firm result because the outcome is a count of recorded incidents, not an athletic-performance measure. The relevant physical layer is geographic and scheduling: all Old Firm matches are in Glasgow at Celtic Park or Ibrox [PAGE 12], and the paper controls home/away for non-Old Firm matches [PAGE 12–13]. The biomechanical layer is non-operative: there is no measured biomechanical mediator in the source, so no biomechanical mechanism can be invoked to explain away the count effect. The weather layer is the real veto risk. The paper controls year, day of week, and Christmas/New Year [PAGE 16], but it does not report weather, alcohol, or policing-intensity controls. Therefore a strict weather-veto proof is impossible from these pages. What can be adjudicated is this: the Old Firm coefficient is not a Christmas artifact because the Old Firm coefficient and the Xmas/NY coefficient are separately estimated and both are large [PAGE 16–17]; it is not a simple weekend artifact because day-of-week is controlled [PAGE 16]; and it is not a simple home/away artifact because home indicators are included [PAGE 12–13]. But weather and alcohol remain unmeasured common causes. The correct cross-layer verdict is “not vetoed by physical/biomechanical controls; weather-veto unresolved.” The paper’s own policy framing mentions alcohol misuse as a JAG target [PAGE 3], which reinforces that the unmeasured alcohol layer is a live confounder, not a refuted one.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Each scenario below has a binary verifier and a pre-specified veto rule; “RLVR” here means reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards, i.e. the prediction is scored by an observable coefficient sign, significance threshold, and confidence interval rather than by narrative fit.

1. Natural-experiment veto: Rangers’ post-2012 absence. Verifier: re-estimate the count model on post-2012 seasons with the same year/day/Xmas controls and compare former Old Firm match dates with matched non-Old Firm dates. Prediction: the Old Firm coefficient should disappear; former Old Firm dates should show a substantial fall relative to pre-2012 Old Firm days, consistent with the paper’s prediction of “a major fall in domestic violence incidents in Glasgow due to the absence of the Old Firm fixtures” [PAGE 7]. Veto: if the same date-specific spike persists without an Old Firm fixture, the fixture effect is not causal or is confounded by date-specific factors.

2. Salience/upset non-win veto. Verifier: pre-register the restrictive importance definition (top-half split, points gap ≤10) and the \(q\)-categories, then test the `sal` interaction coefficients in Table 5. Prediction: in important games, expected-win draws and tight losses should show positive coefficients (roughly +37% Celtic expected-win draw, +24% Rangers expected-win draw, +16% Rangers tight loss), while important wins should be insignificant; non-important games should be null or negative [PAGE 21–23]. Veto: if important wins also spike, or if non-important upset losses show the same positive coefficients, the reference-point interpretation fails.
