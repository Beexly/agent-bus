<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s causal chain is multi-hop. First, a Steam user i has a binary adoption outcome \(y_{i,t}\) for Super Meat Boy in week t. The key regressor is whether i’s first-degree friend j adopted in the same week. Because simultaneity can run both ways, the paper invokes Manski’s reflection problem and uses an IV: second-degree friend k’s adoption in week \(t-1\) instruments for j’s adoption in week t. The logic is: k’s past purchase can influence j’s current purchase, but i does not observe k’s decision, so k’s lagged purchase should not directly affect i. The paper reports a first-stage Anderson-Rubin statistic of 341.17, which is presented as strong evidence that the lagged second-degree adoption is relevant [PAGE 16]. The IV second-stage estimate is a 1.7 percentage-point increase in i’s adoption probability when j adopts in the same week, while OLS gives 7.3 percentage points [PAGE 16]. The gap between OLS and IV is the central diagnostic: OLS is likely contaminated by reflection, homophily, common shocks, or correlated unobservables, and the IV is meant to isolate a local average treatment effect for compliers [PAGE 16]. The heterogeneity step interacts peer adoption with key-player status and old-friend status. The paper reports old-friend coefficients around 0.11 and key-player coefficients around 0.05, both smaller than the aggregate effect around 0.13, with the reference group being more recent non-key friends; hence the paper concludes that the strongest peer effects are actually from more recent friends [PAGE 18]. The final hop is from adoption to experienced utility, measured by log playtime in a cross-section. Players whose purchase was not influenced by any peer show higher playtime: approximately 45 percent higher for SMB and 56 percent higher for NV [PAGE 20]. The paper interprets this as partial support for taste projection: users overestimate preference alignment with peers, buy, then engage less than users who were not peer-influenced [PAGE 20 and PAGE 21].

The gray-zone audit is where the paper’s causal claims need pressure-testing. First, the purchase date is not directly observed; it is proxied by the timestamp of the first achievement [PAGE 9]. The paper validates this with median time to first achievement of 73 minutes among one-achievement SMB players [PAGE 33], which supports same-week approximation, but it is still a proxy. If a user buys and waits days or weeks before playing, the treatment timing is misclassified. Second, gifting accounts for about 3.5 percent of SMB ownership [PAGE 9], so some “purchases” are gifts and not necessarily peer-induced adoptions. Third, the network is incomplete: private-private friendship links are unobservable [PAGE 32], and the constructed network includes 42.2 million nodes and 122.2 million links, with 22.1 million nodes having at least one link and 20.1 million assumed to have no friends [PAGE 10]. If a private edge makes a supposed second-degree friend k actually a first-degree friend of i, the exclusion restriction fails because i could observe k’s lagged purchase. Fourth, the IV identifies only a LATE for the rare subset whose friend’s adoption is shifted by a second-degree friend’s lagged adoption [PAGE 16]. Fifth, the playtime analysis is cross-sectional, and the paper itself notes that playtime data “is only available in the cross-section and measures the total playtime for each game at the time of the initial database scrape” [PAGE 20]. That means the paper cannot directly observe an “initial surge” followed by decline; it observes total accumulated playtime. The taste-projection interpretation is therefore plausible but not uniquely identified. Finally, the paper acknowledges alternative mechanisms such as game-specific mechanics, skill mismatch, novelty decay, and shifting preferences [PAGE 21]. These are not minor caveats; they are competing explanations for the playtime result. The adoption IV is stronger than the playtime claim because the adoption design has panel fixed effects, a temporal IV, and a first-stage diagnostic, while the playtime design is cross-sectional and relies on peer-type categories that may correlate with unobserved skill, taste, and game difficulty.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s core operator is a linear-in-means peer-effects model with individual and week fixed effects. In the paper’s notation, the baseline specification is:

\[
y_{i,t}=\alpha_i+\beta\sum_{j}g_{ij,t}y_{j,t}+W_t+\epsilon_{i,t}
\]

Here \(y_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}\) is a binary adoption indicator for player i in week t, so the outcome is in probability units; \(\beta\) is the endogenous peer-effect coefficient in probability points per unit of peer adoption; \(g_{ij,t}\) is the network adjacency weight; \(\alpha_i\) is an individual fixed effect; \(W_t\) is a vector of week fixed effects; and \(\epsilon_{i,t}\) is the error. The paper states: “A statistically significant and positive β indicates that the purchase decision of the player’s friends increases the purchase likelihood of player i, otherwise known as an endogenous peer effect” [PAGE 13]. Standard errors are clustered at the player level [PAGE 13]. The paper also states that week fixed effects “absorb these global, time specific shocks to individual purchase decisions” [PAGE 12].

The first-stage IV specification is:

\[
y_{j,t}=\alpha_i+\delta'\sum_{k}g_{jk,t}y_{k,t-1}+W_t+u_{i,t}
\]

where \(y_{k,t-1}\) is second-degree friend k’s adoption in the previous week. The paper’s validity argument is: “The purchasing decision of k in the previous week cannot have a direct impact on i’s purchasing decision, as i does not observe k’s purchasing decisions, which satisfies the exclusion restriction” [PAGE 14]. The relevance condition is that the instrument is correlated with \(y_{j,t}\), and the exclusion restriction is that it is uncorrelated with \(\epsilon_{i,t}\) [PAGE 14]. The paper reports a first-stage Anderson-Rubin statistic of 341.17 and a second-stage IV estimate of 1.7 percent [PAGE 16]. It also reports OLS at 7.3 percent and the reduced form at 0.8 percent [PAGE 16]. These are probability-point quantities, not SI units. The paper explicitly notes that the IV estimates “only allows us to estimate a Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)” [PAGE 16].

The heterogeneity specification is:

\[
y_{i,t}=\alpha_i+\beta_1\sum_{j}g_{ij,t}y_{j,t}KP_j+\beta_2\sum_{j}g_{ij,t}y_{j,t}OF_j+W_t+\epsilon_{i,t}
\]

where \(KP_j\) is a key-player indicator and \(OF_j\) is an old-friend indicator. The reference group is “new (more recent) friends who are not key players in player i’s steam network” [PAGE 18]. The paper reports coefficients around 0.11 for old friends and 0.05 for key players, with an aggregate effect around 0.13 [PAGE 18]. The key-player variable is defined using Katz centrality at or above the 99th percentile four weeks before SMB release, and the old-friend variable is defined as friendship age of at least one year at four weeks before release [PAGE 10].

The playtime specification is:

\[
PT_i=\mu+\gamma_1(Purchase\times KP)_i+\gamma_2(Purchase\times OF)_i+X_i+u_i
\]

where \(PT_i\) is the log of hours played. The unit is log-hours, so coefficients are approximately proportional changes in hours for small coefficients and exact proportional changes via exponentiation. The paper reports that users without peer purchases show “approximately 45% higher playtime in the case of SMB and 56% higher playtime in the case of NV” [PAGE 20]. The controls \(X_i\) include number of games owned, number of groups, age of the Steam account, number of friends, and game ownership indicators [PAGE 20].

For completeness, if one treats the binary adoption outcome information-theoretically, its entropy is

\[
H(y_{i,t})=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)
\]

in bits, where \(p=P(y_{i,t}=1)\). The paper does not estimate entropy or mutual information; it estimates linear probability effects. The formal network multiplier for the linear-in-means system is

\[
\mathbf{y}_t=(\mathbf{I}-\beta\mathbf{G}_t)^{-1}(\alpha+W_t+\epsilon_t)
\]

The multiplier exists only when \(\mathbf{I}-\beta\mathbf{G}_t\) is invertible. The exact singularity regime is

\[
\det(\mathbf{I}-\beta\mathbf{G}_t)=0
\]

equivalently, \(1/\beta\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{G}_t\). For a symmetric or row-normalized network, the stable regime requires \(|\beta|\rho(\mathbf{G}_t)\) below 1, where \(\rho(\cdot)\) is the spectral radius. As \(|\beta|\rho(\mathbf{G}_t)\) approaches 1, the reduced-form multiplier diverges; in finite samples this appears as extreme collinearity, weak identification, and explosive peer-effect estimates. The paper does not estimate this structural multiplier directly, but the singularity is the formal breakdown regime for any linear-in-means interpretation.

Other breakdown regimes are empirical. Weak instruments occur when the first stage is near zero; here the reported Anderson-Rubin statistic of 341.17 is presented as strong [PAGE 16], but a lower statistic would push IV toward OLS bias. Exclusion failure occurs if i can observe k’s lagged purchase or if k is secretly a first-degree friend through an unobserved private edge [PAGE 32]. Measurement failure occurs if first achievement is not in the same week as purchase [PAGE 9 and PAGE 33]. LATE failure occurs when the effect for compliers does not generalize to the broader population [PAGE 16]. Playtime confounding occurs if skill, game difficulty, novelty decay, or local shocks correlate with peer type [PAGE 21]. These are the exact regimes in which the paper’s operators cease to support causal claims.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs through three genealogies. The first is the peer-effects identification genealogy: Manski’s reflection problem, Bramoullé, Djebbari, and Fortin’s network IV using second-degree friends, and Bramoullé, Djebbari, and Fortin’s later survey of network panels [PAGE 14 and PAGE 15]. The paper’s twist is temporal: instead of using contemporaneous second-degree friend characteristics, it uses second-degree friend adoption from the previous week. That is a panel-specific instrument. The paper claims it is “only applicable where individual-level panel network data is available” [PAGE 14]. The second genealogy is experience goods and social learning: word-of-mouth from friends, Campbell et al. on search and communication, and the taste-projection literature from Ross et al.’s false-consensus effect through Orhun and Urminsky to Gagnon-Bartsch and Rosato [PAGE 5 and PAGE 6]. The third genealogy is digital-trace economics: O’Neill et al.’s Steam dataset, Gandhi et al.’s League of Legends belief-and-engagement study, and the broader use of platform data to measure revealed preferences [PAGE 7 and PAGE 8].

The retrocausal trap is that the paper’s interpretation can look more retrospective than the data allow. The adoption result is a contemporary-week peer effect, but the IV is a one-week lag. If peer influence decays over multiple weeks, the IV estimates only the one-week-lagged first-stage channel; it does not identify a cumulative multi-week peer effect. The paper’s old-friend and key-player variables are measured four weeks before SMB release to preserve exogeneity [PAGE 10], but the one-year old-friend threshold and the 99th-percentile Katz threshold are analyst choices. A different threshold could change the heterogeneity result. The historical release pattern is another multi-week trap: SMB purchases spike around release, and the paper notes peaks between weeks 160 and 180 likely due to the MAC OS release [PAGE 11]. Week fixed effects absorb global release shocks, but they do not absorb peer-specific interactions with release timing. The paper’s playtime variable is even more retrocausal: it is a single cross-section measured at the initial database scrape [PAGE 20]. The paper interprets lower total playtime as evidence of disappointed projected enjoyment, but total playtime is the integral of all engagement up to the scrape. A user could have a high initial surge and a long slow decline, or a low initial surge and a long plateau; total playtime alone cannot distinguish those trajectories. The paper acknowledges this by calling the taste-projection connection “not a direct test” [PAGE 20]. Thus the historical lineage supports the adoption IV more strongly than the playtime story. The multi-week traps are timing misclassification from first achievements, release and platform shocks, threshold-dependent friend categories, and the inability to observe playtime trajectories.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under uncertainty about an experience good, the user recurses into other minds. The user asks: what did j buy, what does j know about my tastes, and what does j’s purchase imply about quality? Old friends are theorized to have a richer model of i’s preferences, so their purchases should be more diagnostic. Key players have centrality but not necessarily taste alignment. The paper’s result that old-friend effects are larger than key-player effects is consistent with a recursive model in which old friends have better preference models [PAGE 18]. But the paper also reports that the reference group of recent non-key friends has the strongest aggregate effect, which complicates a simple “old friends know you best” story [PAGE 18]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore allow for novelty and social proximity: recent friends may provide fresher, more salient signals even if they have less historical knowledge.

Counter-trap: the obvious counter-trap is to over-read the playtime result as proof of taste projection. The paper itself lists alternatives: game-specific mechanics, online community effects, skill mismatch, novelty decay, and shifting preferences [PAGE 21]. If key-player-influenced users are less skilled or more susceptible to difficulty spikes, their lower playtime could reflect skill mismatch rather than projected enjoyment. The paper says “these other network effects should work against our results” [PAGE 21], but that is an argument, not a test. A strict counter-trap would require controls for skill, achievement completion, game difficulty, and in-game social interaction. Without those, the recursive mentalizing story is plausible but not identified. The paper’s own conclusion calls for “experimental studies or the incorporation of richer data on player skill and in-game social interactions” [PAGE 23].

Clock: the timing structure matters. The adoption model runs weekly from September 2008 to October 2013, with 269 weeks [PAGE 2]. The old-friend and key-player statuses are frozen four weeks before SMB release [PAGE 10]. The IV uses \(t-1\) second-degree adoption. The playtime outcome is measured later, at the 2014 scrape [PAGE 20]. The recursive belief that drives purchase is formed before play; the playtime outcome accumulates after play. The paper’s “initial surge” language [PAGE 21] is a trajectory claim, but the clock of the data only gives total playtime. A proper recursive theory of mind would separate the purchase-time belief from the post-purchase belief update. The paper cannot observe that separation. Thus the clock veto is serious for the taste-projection mechanism, while the adoption result remains a one-week peer-effect estimate.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: there is no physical-performance outcome in this study. The dependent variables are binary game adoption and log playtime. Physical constraints enter only through platform hardware and download requirements. The paper notes SMB has “relatively small disk space requirements” and short download time [PAGE 9], so download duration is unlikely to be a binding physical veto. A physical veto would require evidence that hardware or bandwidth determined adoption in the same weeks as peer purchases; the paper does not measure that, but the outcome is not a biomechanical performance variable, so the physical layer does not overturn the adoption IV.

Biomechanical layer: the closest biomechanical proxy is player skill and game difficulty. The paper’s playtime discussion explicitly raises “a mismatch between their skill level and the game’s demands” [PAGE 21]. That is a real biomechanical and cognitive-performance confound for playtime. If key-player-influenced users are worse at SMB or NV, their lower playtime may reflect skill mismatch, not taste projection. The paper does not control for achievement completion, death counts, or difficulty progression. Therefore a strict biomechanical veto blocks a strong causal interpretation of the playtime result. It does not block the adoption result, because adoption is a purchase decision, not a performance outcome.

Weather layer: the paper has no weather variables. Global week fixed effects absorb common seasonal shocks [PAGE 12], and the paper argues that Steam ads, releases, and bundles are globally distributed, so local shocks are less likely to align with purchase timing [PAGE 16]. That protects the adoption IV from global weather seasonality but not from local weather shocks. Local weather could affect leisure time and playtime, and the playtime regression is cross-sectional without week fixed effects. A weather veto therefore applies to the playtime causal claim unless the model includes local weather or week-of-year controls. It does not directly veto the adoption IV because the adoption panel includes week fixed effects and individual fixed effects, though local weather interacting with peer timing remains an omitted variable.

Platform and data layer: this is the strongest veto layer. The network is incomplete because private-private friendships are unobservable [PAGE 32]. The friend limit is 250 by default, extendable through Steam level or Facebook linking [PAGE 7 and PAGE 32]. Purchase dates are proxied by first achievements [PAGE 9]. Gifting is about 3.5 percent of SMB ownership [PAGE 9]. Public profiles are 93.6 percent of users [PAGE 10]. These are not physical or weather vetoes, but they are cross-layer data vetoes. The exclusion restriction depends on i not observing k; an unobserved private edge could make k a first-degree friend. The adoption IV survives only if those private-edge cases are rare or if pruning them does not change the estimate. The playtime claim survives only if peer-type categories are not proxies for unobserved skill, game difficulty, or local shocks. Adjudication: adoption IV survives the physical and weather layer conditionally; playtime taste-projection claim does not survive a strict biomechanical or weather-layer audit without additional controls.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
