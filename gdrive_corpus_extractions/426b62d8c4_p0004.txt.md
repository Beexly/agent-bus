5. Non-iid or non-stationary p_i: the almost-sure limit and part (vi) are not valid without an explicit filtering or regret model.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of [PAGE 1] traces to Kelly (1956), "A New Interpretation of Information Rate," which proposed maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth as the growth-optimal portfolio criterion. Thorp's article "The Kelly Criterion and the Stock Market," of which [PAGE 1] is a page, transposes this from the original horse-race / binomial setting into continuous security markets. Deeper roots include Bernoulli's logarithmic resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox, Kolmogorov's strong law of large numbers (which supplies the almost-sure convergence of (1/n)\log X_n to G(f)), Breiman's formulation of growth-optimal portfolios, and Shannon's information-theoretic analogy between gambling growth rate and channel capacity. Markowitz mean-variance analysis enters the genealogy as a local, finite-horizon approximation to log-utility, whereas Theorem 1 on [PAGE 1] asserts global almost-sure asymptotic optimality under the binomial model.

Multi-week traps embedded in this lineage: (a) Rolling-window estimation of p_i over one- to four-week samples induces regime-drift bias; if the estimated \hat p_i crosses below 1/2, the Kelly fraction f_i^* = p_i - q changes sign, causing whipsaw between long and short exposure that the static theorem does not cover. (b) Transaction costs, bid-ask impact, and execution slippage shift the effective fraction f_{\text{eff}} = f^* + \delta f; because the outcome at f_e is discontinuous—growth on one side, ruin or oscillation on the other—small slippage can push an apparently safe Kelly bet into the ruin regime of part (ii). (c) The asymptotic qualifiers in parts (iv) and (v) create a psychological trap: over a multi-week horizon, non-Kelly paths can temporarily outperform, inducing practitioners to abandon logarithmic utility precisely before the infinite-horizon guarantee activates. (d) Time-varying probabilities in part (vi) require trial-by-trial knowledge of p_i; in practice p_i is a latent regime variable, and using a stale estimate violates the theorem's assumptions while preserving the appearance of Kelly discipline.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: When drawdowns accumulate, human operators regress from the abstract logarithmic utility of Theorem 1 toward linear expectation maximization or fixed-fraction heuristics. Because [PAGE 1] part (ii) states that any f with G(f)<0 yields almost-sure ruin, this regression is not merely suboptimal; it is existentially terminal for the capital process. The operator effectively implements an f above f_e while believing they remain in the growth interval of part (i).

Counter-trap: An adversarial or market environment that can manipulate the operator's estimate of p_i induces a second-order belief collapse. If the operator believes the environment is stationary with p_i>1/2 but the true data-generating process has shifted to p_i\le 1/2, the prescribed f_i^*=p_i-q becomes non-positive while the operator continues betting a positive fraction from the stale model. The recursive theory of mind thus fails at the model-selection layer: the operator thinks they are best-responding to a binomial environment, but the environment has changed the payoff structure, and the Kelly prescription now maximizes the speed of ruin.

Clock mismatch: Theorems (iv) and (v) on [PAGE 1] are limits as n\to\infty or as the preassigned goal grows without bound. A crisis operates on a finite clock—hours, days, or weeks—during which the almost-sure statements have no binding force. A manager can be asymptotically optimal in the mathematical limit yet experience total drawdown before the clock expires. The "finite number of terms" before X exceeds any fixed bound M [PAGE 1] may be longer than the survival horizon of the fund or the trader's risk tolerance, creating a recursive trap where the optimal infinite-horizon policy is infeasible on the finite crisis clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto layers and formal veto conditions:

1. Physical capital layer (solvency boundary): The state space of X is [0,\infty), with X=0 an absorbing ruin boundary. If \Pr(1+fU\le 0)>0, then \log(1+fU)=-\infty on a positive-probability set, so G(f)=-\infty and the next step can drive X to zero with positive probability. Veto condition: \exists u\in\text{supp}(U) such that 1+fu\le 0 \implies automatic veto; Kelly is physically inadmissible.

2. Biomechanical / execution layer: Human or algorithmic implementation incurs latency and slippage, so the executed fraction f_{\text{exec}} = f^* + \epsilon_f differs from the ideal f^*=p_i-q. Because the qualitative outcome changes discontinuously at f_e where G(f_e)=0, the veto condition is f_{\text{exec}}>f_e \implies G(f)<0 \implies \lim X_n=0\ \text{a.s.} [PAGE 1, part (ii)]. If f_{\text{exec}}=f_c exactly, then \limsup X_n=\infty and \liminf X_n=0\ \text{a.s.} [PAGE 1, part (iii)], an oscillatory divergence that no risk budget can absorb. Cognitive load under stress increases \epsilon_f, making this layer the most frequent veto trigger.

3. Weather / regime layer: The binomial assumption and fixed p_i are stationarity assumptions. A regime shift (volatility shock, structural break, liquidity crisis) that renders p_i non-stationary invalidates the strong-law convergence \frac{1}{n}\log X_n\to G(f). Veto condition: estimated p_i from regime A is applied in regime B where p_i^{(B)}\le 1/2 or the return law is no longer binomial \implies the assumptions of part (vi) fail and the Kelly fraction has no guaranteed meaning.

Veto proof: The strategy is admissible iff (a) p_i\in(1/2,1) is known or consistently estimated; (b) f^* is implemented without execution error crossing f_e; (c) \text{supp}(U) satisfies 1+f^*U>0 a.s.; and (d) the investment horizon is sufficiently long for the asymptotic results of [PAGE 1] parts (iv) and (v) to dominate finite-sample noise. Violation of any layer triggers a hard veto; there is no partial Kelly because the almost-sure results are discontinuous at the critical threshold.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios, each with a checkable condition, veto action, and counterfactual prediction derived from [PAGE 1].

Scenario 1 — Probability-estimate collapse veto.  
Verifiable condition: Rolling estimate \hat p_i^{(t)}\le 1/2 over the current window while the implemented fraction remains f_t=\hat p_i-\hat q>0 carried from the previous window.  
Veto: Set f_t=0 and halt new exposure until \hat p_i>1/2 is re-established.  
Prediction if not vetoed: Because f_t>0 while p_i\le 1/2, the effective G(f_t)<0 (or the Kelly formula is undefined), so by [PAGE 1] part (ii), \lim_{n\to\infty}X_n=0\ \text{a.s.}; capital decays approximately as X_n\approx X_0 e^{nG(f_t)} with G(f_t)<0.

Scenario 2 — Execution-overshoot veto.  
Verifiable condition: Audited order fills show f_{\text{exec}}>f_e, where f_e is the unique root of G(f_e)=0, detected by comparing target Kelly notional to executed notional.  
Veto: Reduce size to f\le f_e or flat the position.  
Prediction if not vetoed: For f_{\text{exec}}>f_e, [PAGE 1] part (ii) gives almost-sure ruin \lim X_n=0; if f_{\text{exec}}=f_c exactly, part (iii) gives \limsup X_n=\infty and \liminf X_n=0\ \text{a.s.}, i.e., oscillatory divergence with no stable capital floor.

Scenario 3 — Return-support violation veto.  
Verifiable condition: An observed return u_k satisfies u_k\le -1/f^*, so that 1+f^*u_k\le 0.  
Veto: Reject the bet; do not evaluate E\log(1+fU).  
Prediction if not vetoed: \log X_{k+1}=\log X_k+\log(1+f^*u_k)=-\infty with positive probability; G(f^*)=-\infty and the Kelly criterion breaks down completely, because the expected logarithm is no longer finite and ruin can occur in a single trial.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
