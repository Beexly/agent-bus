4. The variational Euler equation is Lemma 4.6 [PAGE 16]:
\[
\partial_0s+L_0s=\partial_0s-\frac1p\frac{d}{dx}[p\partial_1s]+\frac1p\frac{d^2}{dx^2}[p\partial_2s]=c_p,
\]
with \(c_p=\int(\partial_0s)p\). This is the Euler equation of the calculus of variations in the paper’s log-derivative variables [PAGE 17]. It forces any local proper score of order 2 to be a fixed point of the tangent construction [PAGE 17].

5. A \(z_2\)-independent kernel is constructed by Proposition 4.8 [PAGE 17]:
\[
K=s-\left(z_1+\frac{d}{dx}\right)V,\qquad
V=\int_0^{z_1}\partial_2s(x,z_0,t,z_2)\,dt.
\]
The reduction principle [PAGE 20] then forces \(\partial_{22}^2s=0\) on the relevant arguments, so the kernel can be taken independent of \(z_2\).

6. Linearity in \(z_0\) follows. Proposition 4.12 [PAGE 21] concludes that \(K\) has the form
\[
K(x,z_0,z_1)=c z_0+K_0(x,z_1),
\]
and \(s\) has the form
\[
s(x,y_0,y_1,y_2)=c y_0+(1-y_1\partial_1-\partial_{x1}^2-y_2\partial_{11}^2)K_0(x,y_1).
\]
This is the same representation as Theorem 3.2 on [PAGE 6–7]. Proposition 3.3 [PAGE 7] supplies the practical sufficient condition: if \(K\in\mathcal R_1\), \(c\le 0\), and \(y_1\mapsto K_0(x,y_1)\) is concave for every \(x\), then \(\Phi_K\) is concave, hence the score is proper. Strict concavity gives strict propriety under the stated class restrictions.

The gray-zone audit is where most errors occur. First, the density class \(\mathcal P\) is load-bearing. Definition 2.3 on [PAGE 4–5] requires strict positivity on \(\mathbb R\), four continuous derivatives, polynomial decay of derivatives, and a polynomial-growth condition for logarithmic derivatives. If one relaxes strict positivity, strict propriety can fail. Remark 3.9 on [PAGE 9–10] gives Huber’s counterexample with densities \(p_\alpha\) that vanish at \(x=0\). The paper states: “the logarithmic derivatives, \(p'_\alpha(x)/p_\alpha(x)\), do not depend on \(\alpha\), the Fisher information of \(p_\alpha\) does not depend on \(\alpha\) either” [PAGE 10]. Consequently “the Fisher information distance does not distinguish the densities \(p_\alpha\), that is, \(d_{FI}(p_\alpha,p_\beta)=0\) for \(\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)\), and the Hyvärinen score (2) fails to be strictly proper relative to the augmented class \(\mathcal P_1\)” [PAGE 10]. This is the main singularity/breakdown regime: a convex class with a common zero destroys strictness even though the kernel may look strictly concave.

Second, boundary terms in integration by parts must vanish. The proof of Lemma 4.2 relies on the \(P3\) and \(P4\) decay conditions and on the polynomial growth of \(K\) and its partials [PAGE 13–14]. If the density is too heavy-tailed or the score grows too quickly, the tangent construction can become non-integrable or the boundary terms can survive. The paper’s discussion on [PAGE 26–27] says the choice of \(\mathcal S=\mathcal R_2\) and \(\mathcal D=\mathcal P\) “appears to be usefully general and achieving a reasonable balance,” and that one could shift the balance for heavier-tailed densities by adapting the polynomial growth order.

Third, exact order 1 is a trap. Proposition 5.1 on [PAGE 22–23] proves that any local score \(s\in\mathcal R_1\) proper relative to \(\mathcal P\) is of the form \(s=c z_0+k(x)\) with \(c\le 0\). The paper also notes that Parry, Dawid and Lauritzen “show that there are no key local score functions of odd order” [PAGE 22]. Thus any claimed “exact order 1 derivative score” is not a genuinely new local proper score; it reduces to the logarithmic score plus an \(x\)-only term.

Fourth, unit consistency matters. If \(x\) is a physical variable such as temperature in kelvin, then \(q(x)\) has units \(\mathrm K^{-1}\), \(z_1=(\ln q)'\) has units \(\mathrm K^{-1}\), and \(z_2=(\ln q)''\) has units \(\mathrm K^{-2}\). The Hyvärinen score \(z_1^2+2z_2\) therefore has units \(\mathrm K^{-2}\). The logarithmic score \(-\ln q(x)\) is in nats up to an additive constant depending on the measurement unit; score differences are dimensionless. The log cosh score in (32) on [PAGE 24] must be applied after nondimensionalizing \(z_1\) in a physical setting, because \(\cosh\) and \(\tanh\) require dimensionless arguments. Properness inequalities themselves are dimensionless because both sides are expected losses in the same units.

The weather example is a cross-check, not a proof of all applications. On [PAGE 25–26], BMA and EMOS density forecasts for surface temperature over the North American Pacific Northwest are evaluated on 23,691 forecast cases. Table 1 on [PAGE 26] reports mean logarithmic score, Hyvärinen score, log cosh score, quadratic score, and spherical score. The paper states: “Under all scoring rules, the EMOS technique shows a slightly lower (i.e., better) mean score than the BMA method” [PAGE 26]. It also reports that the smoothed unprocessed ensemble has very large scores, “reaching 21.4 for the logarithmic score, \(1.14\times 10^4\) for the Hyvärinen score” [PAGE 26], which demonstrates the benefit of statistical postprocessing. The paper cautions on [PAGE 27] that “locality and sensitivity to distance appear to be mutually exclusive properties,” and that local and nonlocal proper scoring rules “generally yield comparable results” in the meteorological example. So the source supports local proper scoring for weather density forecasts, but it does not license a universal claim that local scores dominate nonlocal scores.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
All equations below are from the source or are immediate algebraic restatements of source equations. No invented ratios or toy formulas are used.

Properness. On [PAGE 3], Definition 2.1: \(S\) is proper relative to \(\mathcal M\) if
\[
S(P,P)\le S(P,Q)\quad\text{for all }P,Q\in\mathcal M,
\]
where
\[
S(P,Q)=\int S(\omega,Q)\,dP(\omega).
\]
Strict propriety requires equality iff \(Q=P\). The paper says on [PAGE 1]: “A scoring rule is proper if it encourages truthful reporting.” The loss is negatively oriented: the forecaster wishes to minimize \(S(x,Q)\) [PAGE 1].

Locality of order \(k\). Definition 2.2 on [PAGE 4]: \(S\) is local of order \(k\) if there is a scoring function \(s:\mathbb R^{2+k}\to\mathbb R\) such that
\[
S(x,q)=s\bigl(x,\ln q(x),(\ln q)'(x),\ldots,(\ln q)^{(k)}(x)\bigr)
\]
for every \(q\in\mathcal M\) and \(x\in\mathbb R\). The paper explains that this depends on the predictive density only through its value and derivatives up to order \(k\) at the realizing event [PAGE 1, PAGE 4].

Density class \(\mathcal P\). Definition 2.3 on [PAGE 4–5] requires:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(P1)\quad p(x)\gt 0 \text{ on } \mathbb R;\\
&(P2)\quad p \text{ admits four continuous derivatives on } \mathbb R;\\
&(P3)\quad \lim_{x\to\pm\infty}|x|^m p^{(j)}(x)=0
\quad\text{for every }m\gt 0,\ j=0,1,\ldots,4;\\
&(P4)\quad \exists\,a=a(p)\gt 0 \text{ such that }
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}|x|^{-a}\frac{p^{(j)}(x)}{p(x)}=0
\quad(j=1,\ldots,4).
\end{aligned}
\]
The paper states: “The class \(\mathcal P\) is quite broad and includes many well-known densities, such as all normal and logistic densities, the corresponding skew variants … and finite mixtures of these densities” [PAGE 5]. Lemma 2.4 and Corollary 2.5 [PAGE 5] establish convexity: “The class \(\mathcal P\) is convex.” Convexity is required for the supergradient characterization of proper scoring rules.

Kernel functional. For \(K\in\mathcal R_k\), equation (3) on [PAGE 6]:
\[
\Phi_K(p)=\int_{\mathbb R}K\bigl(x,\ln p(x),(\ln p)'(x),\ldots,(\ln p)^{(k)}(x)\bigr)p(x)\,dx.
\]
The class \(\mathcal R_k\) in Definition 3.1 [PAGE 6] requires continuous partial derivatives up to order \(2k\) and polynomial growth: for \(W=K\) or any partial derivative up to order \(2k\),
\[
|W(x,y_0,\ldots,y_k)|\le C\{(1+|x|)(1+|y_0|)\cdots(1+|y_k|)\}^r
\]
for finite positive \(C,r\).

Main representation: Theorem 3.2 on [PAGE 6–7]. Part (a): if
\[
K(x,y_0,y_1)=c y_0+K_0(x,y_1)
\]
with \(K\in\mathcal R_1\) and \(\Phi_K\) concave, then
\[
s(x,y_0,y_1,y_2)=c y_0+(1-y_1\partial_1-\partial_{x1}^2-y_2\partial_{11}^2)K_0(x,y_1)
\]
represents a local score of order 2 proper relative to \(\mathcal P\). Here \(\partial_1=\partial/\partial y_1\), \(\partial_{x1}^2=\partial^2/(\partial x\,\partial y_1)\), and \(\partial_{11}^2=\partial^2/\partial y_1^2\). Part (b) gives the converse: any local proper score of order 2 relative to \(\mathcal P\) admits this form with a concave \(\Phi_K\). Part (c) upgrades concave to strictly concave and proper to strictly proper.

Concavity criterion: Proposition 3.3 [PAGE 7]. If \(K\) has the form above, \(K\in\mathcal R_1\), \(c\le 0\), and \(y_1\mapsto K_0(x,y_1)\) is concave for every \(x\), then \(\Phi_K\) is concave. Strict concavity of the map gives strict concavity of the functional under the class restrictions.

Canonical examples. On [PAGE 2], the logarithmic score is
\[
S(x,Q)=-\ln q(x).
\]
The Hyvärinen score is
\[
S(x,Q)=2\frac{q''(x)}{q(x)}-\left(\frac{q'(x)}{q(x)}\right)^2
=((\ln q)'(x))^2+2(\ln q)''(x).
\]
The paper says on [PAGE 2]: “The Hyvärinen score is also proper.” The kernel \(K=-y_0\) yields the logarithmic score; the kernel \(K=-y_1^2\) yields the Hyvärinen score [PAGE 7]. The associated functional \(-\int (p'/p)^2 p\) is minus the Fisher information, and
\[
d_{FI}(p,q)=\int\left(\frac{p'}{p}-\frac{q'}{q}\right)^2 p
\]
is the Fisher information distance [PAGE 7].

Tangent construction. Lemma 4.2 [PAGE 12] gives the gradient for \(K\in\mathcal R_2\):
\[
G=K+\partial_0K-\frac1p\frac{d}{dx}[p\partial_1K]+\frac1p\frac{d^2}{dx^2}[p\partial_2K].
\]
The compact operator form on [PAGE 14] is
\[
L_0K=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(-1)^j\frac1p\frac{d^j}{dx^j}[p\partial_jK],
\qquad G=K+\partial_0K+L_0K.
\]
If \(\Phi_K\) is concave, Proposition 4.5 [PAGE 15] gives a proper score
\[
s=K+L_0K+\partial_0K-\int(\partial_0K)p.
\]

Euler equation. Lemma 4.6 [PAGE 16]:
\[
\partial_0s+L_0s
=\partial_0s-\frac1p\frac{d}{dx}[p\partial_1s]+\frac1p\frac{d^2}{dx^2}[p\partial_2s]
=c_p,
\]
where
\[
c_p=\int(\partial_0s)p.
\]
The paper identifies this as “essentially … the Euler equation of the calculus of variations” [PAGE 17].

Kernel reconstruction. Proposition 4.8 [PAGE 17]:
\[
K=s-\left(z_1+\frac{d}{dx}\right)V,
\qquad
V=\int_0^{z_1}\partial_2s(x,z_0,t,z_2)\,dt.
\]
Then \(K\in\mathcal R_1\), \(\Phi_K=\Phi_s\), and \(K\) does not depend on \(z_2\). The reduction principle is Lemma 4.11 [PAGE 20].

Linearity in \(z_0\). Proposition 4.12 [PAGE 21] gives
\[
K(x,z_0,z_1)=c z_0+K_0(x,z_1)
\]
and
\[
s=c z_0+K_0-z_1\partial_1K_0-\partial_{x1}^2K_0-z_2\partial_{11}^2K_0.
\]
This is the same as the Theorem 3.2 representation with \(c y_0\) and the differential operator applied to \(K_0\).

Order 1 exclusion. Proposition 5.1 [PAGE 22–23]: any local score \(s\in\mathcal R_1\) that is proper relative to \(\mathcal P\) is of the form
\[
s=c z_0+k(x),
\qquad c\le 0.
\]
The paper states: “there are no key local score functions of odd order” [PAGE 22].

Robust log cosh example. Equation (32) on [PAGE 24]:
\[
s=-\ln\cosh z_1+z_1\tanh z_1+z_2(1-\tanh^2 z_1).
\]
The paper notes that as \(|y|\to\infty\),
\[
y\tanh y-\ln\cosh y\to\ln 2,
\qquad
1-\tanh^2 y\sim 4\exp(-2|y|),
\]
so the factor of \(z_2\) tends to zero exponentially. It states: “Of course, the log cosh score (32) is strictly proper relative to \(\mathcal P\), since \(K\) is strictly concave” [PAGE 24].

Units and breakdown. For a physical variable \(x\) in SI units, \(q(x)\) has inverse-\(x\) units; \(z_1\) has \(x^{-1}\) units; \(z_2\) has \(x^{-2}\) units. Thus the Hyvärinen score has \(x^{-2}\) units; the logarithmic score is in nats up to an additive unit constant; log-score differences are dimensionless. The log cosh score must be nondimensionalized before applying \(\cosh\) and \(\tanh\). Breakdown regimes are: failure of \(P1\) strict positivity, which breaks strict propriety of the Hyvärinen score via the Huber common-zero example [PAGE 9–10]; failure of \(P3\)–\(P4\) or \(\mathcal R_k\) growth, which can invalidate boundary-term vanishing in the integration by parts [PAGE 13–14]; non-convex density classes, which obstruct the supergradient characterization; odd-order exact local scores, which do not exist beyond the logarithmic-plus-\(x\)-only form [PAGE 22–23]; and bounded or atomic supports, which require boundary conditions not covered by the \(\mathbb R\)-Lebesgue formulation, although the paper says the finite-boundary case “appears to be tractable similarly” [PAGE 27].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit in the paper. On [PAGE 3], the authors trace proper scoring rules to Brier (1950) and Good (1952), with the term “proper” coined by Winkler and Murphy (1968). They credit Bernardo (1979) for the local logarithmic score and the infinitesimal-neighborhood interpretation, quoting Bernardo: “when assessing the worthiness of a scientist’s final conclusions, only the probability he attaches to a small interval containing the true value should be taken into account” [PAGE 3]. The modern characterization through concave functionals and supergradients is attributed to Hendrickson and Buehler (1971) and Gneiting and Raftery (2007) [PAGE 10, PAGE 11]. The Hyvärinen score comes from Hyvärinen (2005), and the propriety result from Dawid and Lauritzen (2005) [PAGE 2, PAGE 7]. The key comparison is with Parry, Dawid and Lauritzen (2012), whose Euler–Lagrange and operator-algebra approach characterizes key local score functions of any order and establishes nonexistence for odd orders [PAGE 2, PAGE 8, PAGE 22]. The paper acknowledges priority on the compact representation \(s=K-(z_1+d/dx)\partial_1K\) in Remark 3.4 [PAGE 8].

The retrocausal trap is that later notation can make earlier constraints look optional. For example, the kernel form \(K=c y_0+K_0(x,y_1)\) looks like a harmless affine decomposition, but the strict-propriety claim depends on the density class. The Huber counterexample on [PAGE 9–10] is a multi-week trap in applied work: a team may adopt Hyvärinen scoring because its kernel is strictly concave in \(y_1\), then add a forecast family with a common zero at a boundary point. The kernel remains strictly concave, but the functional is not strictly concave on the enlarged class, and \(d_{FI}(p_\alpha,p_\beta)=0\) for distinct forecast densities. The paper states the lesson directly: “strict concavity of the function \(K_0(x,y_1)\) of Proposition 3.3 in \(y_1\) does not imply strict concavity of the associated functional, unless we restrict the class of densities under consideration” [PAGE 10]. This is the canonical “convex-class drift” trap.

A second multi-week trap is temporal leakage in the weather comparison. The data example uses the five-member University of Washington Mesoscale Ensemble over the North American Pacific Northwest at a 48-hour horizon [PAGE 25]. The predictive models are fitted on trailing training periods of length 25 days for BMA and 40 days for EMOS, “as recommended and described in the aforementioned papers” [PAGE 25]. Verification is over 24 April to 30 June 2000, “the largest period common to those used by Raftery et al. (2005) and Gneiting et al. (2005)” [PAGE 25]. A later analyst who compares BMA and EMOS without preserving those training windows or the common verification period can manufacture a ranking change that is not a scoring-rule effect. The paper’s Table 1 [PAGE 26] shows EMOS only slightly better than BMA under every scoring rule: for example, logarithmic score 2.486 versus 2.502, Hyvärinen \(-0.118\) versus \(-0.113\), log cosh \(-0.0595\) versus \(-0.0572\). The large effect is postprocessing versus the raw smoothed ensemble, not BMA versus EMOS. That is the historical lesson: do not retroactively convert a small model-class difference into a universal preference.

A third trap is score shopping. Because proper scoring rules are negatively oriented losses, a forecaster has no incentive to misreport if the rule and class are fixed before seeing outcomes. But if the evaluator switches between local and nonlocal scores, or between the log score and Hyvärinen score, after inspecting results, the truth-telling guarantee no longer applies to the selection procedure. The paper’s discussion on [PAGE 27] says locality and sensitivity to distance “appear to be mutually exclusive properties” and that local and nonlocal rules “generally yield comparable results.” The safe genealogy is: fix the scoring rule, fix the density class, fix the training/verification clock, then compare. The unsafe genealogy is: fit many forecast families, choose the rule that ranks the preferred family best, and then cite properness as if the rule had been precommitted.

A fourth trap is heavy-tail adaptation. The class \(\mathcal P\) requires polynomial decay of derivatives and polynomial growth of log derivatives [PAGE 4–5]. The discussion on [PAGE 26–27] says the balance could be shifted “in favor of more heavy-tailed densities, by adapting the polynomial growth order in \(\mathcal S\).” A team that imports the theorem but uses a density outside \(\mathcal P\) is not covered. The retrocausal error is to read the theorem’s “proper relative to \(\mathcal P\)” as “proper relative to any reasonable forecast class.” It is not. The class is part of the theorem statement, not a decoration.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Recursive theory of mind here is the forecaster–evaluator loop. The forecaster models the evaluator’s scoring rule; the evaluator models that the forecaster may adapt to the rule. A proper scoring rule collapses that recursion to truth-telling: if the forecaster’s belief is \(P\), then \(S(P,P)\le S(P,Q)\) for all \(Q\), so there is no expected gain from quoting \(Q\neq P\) [PAGE 1, PAGE 3]. The paper’s “crisis habit regression” analogue is a forecaster or evaluation team reverting under time pressure to the logarithmic score alone because it is familiar and order zero. The logarithmic score is proper and is “the most prominent example” [PAGE 2], but it is not the only order-2-compatible choice. In a crisis, a team may ignore derivative geometry, use only \(-\ln q(x)\), and then claim it has evaluated a local proper score of order 2. The paper’s data example shows why the habit is detectable: the raw smoothed ensemble has mean logarithmic score 21.4, whereas postprocessed BMA and EMOS have mean logarithmic scores 2.502 and 2.486 [PAGE 26]. A regression to raw ensemble evaluation is visible in the score scale.

The “counter-trap” is the evaluator who tries to counter potential gaming by changing the score class after outcomes are known. This is mathematically self-defeating. If the evaluator enlarges the density class to include a convex family with a common zero, the Hyvärinen score may cease to be strictly proper even though the kernel is strictly concave in \(y_1\) [PAGE 9–10]. The forecaster who understands this can exploit the class change: distinct forecast densities \(p_\alpha\) and \(p_\beta\) have the same logarithmic derivative and therefore zero Fisher information distance, so the score cannot distinguish them. The counter-trap is not that the forecaster lies; it is that the evaluator’s enlarged class makes truth-telling and certain false reports score-equivalent. The defense is to precommit to a class satisfying \(P1\)–\(P4\), or to a subclass \(\mathcal P_0\subset\mathcal P\) satisfying the additional completeness and richness properties described in Remark 3.8 [PAGE 9]. The recursive belief “the forecaster will game the rule” should lead to stricter class verification, not to ad hoc rule changes.

The “clock” is temporal discipline. The weather example fixes a 48-hour prediction horizon, trailing training windows of 25 days for BMA and 40 days for EMOS, and a common verification period from 24 April to 30 June 2000 [PAGE 25]. The clock prevents leakage and prevents the forecaster from knowing future outcomes when choosing the predictive distribution. It also prevents the evaluator from choosing the scoring rule after seeing which rule favors which model. In recursive terms, the forecaster’s model of the evaluator includes the clock; the evaluator’s model of the forecaster includes that the forecaster knows the clock. Properness gives the forecaster an incentive to report the belief formed at the clock time, not a belief updated with future information. The paper’s order-2 theory adds a further layer: the score depends on derivatives at the realizing event, so the forecast density must be smooth and strictly positive at that event [PAGE 4]. If the clock is broken and the density is updated after the event, the derivative information at the event is no longer a forecast object. Thus the recursive equilibrium is: fixed rule, fixed class, fixed clock, truthful report. Any deviation from one of these is a crisis habit, a counter-trap, or a clock violation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto proof. The mathematical derivation is on \(\mathbb R\) with Lebesgue measure and a strictly positive smooth density [PAGE 3–5]. A physical variable must therefore be represented as a real-valued quantity with a density in the chosen unit. For surface temperature measured in kelvin, \(q(x)\) has units \(\mathrm K^{-1}\), \(z_1=(\ln q)'\) has units \(\mathrm K^{-1}\), \(z_2=(\ln q)''\) has units \(\mathrm K^{-2}\), and the Hyvärinen score has units \(\mathrm K^{-2}\). The logarithmic score is in nats up to an additive unit constant; score differences are dimensionless. If a proposed application uses a bounded physical variable, an atomic forecast, or a density with a zero, the \(\mathcal P\) assumptions are not met. The paper explicitly notes that the bounded-support case \(\Omega=(0,\infty)\) “appears to be tractable similarly” with suitable boundary conditions at \(x=0\) [PAGE 27], but that is a separate theorem, not the \(\mathbb R\) theorem. Veto: do not apply the order-2 representation to a bounded or atomic physical variable without proving the boundary conditions and the density-class properties.

Biomechanical-layer veto proof. The source contains no biomechanical model, no joint or muscle variable, and no clinical or motor outcome. It is a paper about probabilistic forecasts of a real-valued quantity, with a weather data example [PAGE 24–26]. Therefore any biomechanical extension is outside the evidence. To permit a biomechanical application, one would need to verify the same \(P1\)–\(P4\) density conditions, convexity of the forecast class, absence of a common zero, integrability of the kernel and its derivatives, and unit consistency for the biomechanical variable. None of that is in the source. The correct adjudication is a veto: the mathematical representation can be transported only after a separate density-class proof. The existence of a local proper score of order 2 does not imply that an arbitrary biomechanical signal has a smooth strictly positive Lebesgue density with polynomial-decay derivatives.

Weather-layer veto proof. The weather layer is directly supported. The paper uses BMA and EMOS to postprocess a five-member ensemble for surface temperature over the North American Pacific Northwest, with 23,691 forecast cases, at a 48-hour horizon [PAGE 25]. Table 1 [PAGE 26] reports mean scores under logarithmic, Hyvärinen, log cosh, quadratic, and spherical rules. The paper states: “Under all scoring rules, the EMOS technique shows a slightly lower (i.e., better) mean score than the BMA method” [PAGE 26]. The numerical entries include BMA logarithmic 2.502, EMOS logarithmic 2.486; BMA Hyvärinen \(-0.113\), EMOS Hyvärinen \(-0.118\); BMA log cosh \(-0.0572\), EMOS log cosh \(-0.0595\); BMA quadratic \(-0.101\), EMOS quadratic \(-0.103\); BMA spherical \(-0.319\), EMOS spherical \(-0.321\) [PAGE 26]. The smoothed unprocessed ensemble is far worse: “reaching 21.4 for the logarithmic score, \(1.14\times 10^4\) for the Hyvärinen score, 0.230 for the log cosh score, 0.194 for the quadratic score, and \(-0.217\) for the spherical score” [PAGE 26]. This is the weather veto proof: a claim that local proper scoring rules are inapplicable to postprocessed weather ensembles is rejected by the paper’s demonstration. However, a claim that local scores are universally superior to nonlocal scores is also vetoed. The paper says on [PAGE 27] that “locality and sensitivity to distance appear to be mutually exclusive properties” and that local and nonlocal proper scoring rules “generally yield comparable results” in the meteorological example. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: weather use is supported for order-2 local scoring under the stated density class; biomechanical use is unsupported; physical use requires unit and support verification; and no layer supports post hoc score shopping.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR veto scenario 1: strict-propriety reward on a class with a common zero. Suppose a reward verifier uses the Hyvärinen score to reward truthful density forecasts and augments the density class with the Huber family
\[
p_\alpha(x)=
\begin{cases}
\alpha g(x), & x\ge 0,\\
(1-\alpha)g(-x), & x\lt 0,
\end{cases}
\qquad
g(x)=x^5e^{-x}/\Gamma(6),\quad x\ge 0,
\]
with \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) [PAGE 9]. Veto condition: the class violates \(P1\) because \(p_\alpha(0)=0\). Formal prediction: the logarithmic derivatives do not depend on \(\alpha\), so
\[
\frac{p_\alpha'(x)}{p_\alpha(x)}=\frac{p_\beta'(x)}{p_\beta(x)}
\]
for all \(x\neq 0\), and therefore
\[
d_{FI}(p_\alpha,p_\beta)
=\int\left(\frac{p_\alpha'}{p_\alpha}-\frac{p_\beta'}{p_\beta}\right)^2p_\alpha\,dx
=0
\]
for \(\alpha\neq\beta\). The paper states exactly that “the Fisher information distance does not distinguish the densities \(p_\alpha\), that is, \(d_{FI}(p_\alpha,p_\beta)=0\) for \(\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)\), and the Hyvärinen score (2) fails to be strictly proper relative to the augmented class \(\mathcal P_1\)” [PAGE 10]. Predicted verifier outcome: a strict-propriety reward test must fail; the reward is flat between distinct densities. Verifier action: veto the class augmentation unless \(P1\) strict positivity is restored.

RLVR veto scenario 2: exact order-1 local derivative score claim. Suppose a model proposes a reward
\[
s(x,z_0,z_1)=z_1g(x,z_0)+h(x,z_0)
\]
and claims it is a genuine local proper score of exact order 1, with nontrivial \(z_1\) dependence. Veto condition: Proposition 5.1 [PAGE 22–23] proves that any \(s\in\mathcal R_1\) proper relative to \(\mathcal P\) must be \(s=c z_0+k(x)\) with \(c\le 0\). Formal prediction: substituting \(s=z_1g+h\) into the reduced Euler equation forces \(g=0\), leaving only \(c z_0+k(x)\). The paper states: “there are no key local score functions of odd order” [PAGE 22]. Predicted verifier outcome: any nonzero \(g\)-term fails the Euler-equation residual test; the exact-order-1 claim is rejected. Verifier action: require the verifier to check the Euler residual and to collapse the candidate to \(c z_0+k(x)\), or reject it.

RLVR veto scenario 3: score evaluation outside the polynomial-growth and decay class. Suppose a reward verifier applies the tangent construction or the Theorem 3.2 representation to a density \(p\) that is not in \(\mathcal P\), for example a heavy-tailed density whose derivatives do not satisfy
\[
