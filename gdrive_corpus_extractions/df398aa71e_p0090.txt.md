**Operator 1: Experience-Based Expectation Update**
The core postulate from the source [PAGE 71] is that a change in goal-object quality induces a behavioral change only after the new reward has been experienced one or more times, thereby inducing a new cognitive expectation. We model this as a sequential Bayesian updating process. Let \(E_t = P(G_t | D_{1:t})\) be the cognitive expectation at trial \(t\), where \(D_{1:t}\) is the history of experiences up to \(t\). Upon experiencing a new reward \(r_t\) in state \(s_t\) after action \(a_t\), the expectation updates according to Bayes' rule:
\[
P(G_t | r_t, D_{1:t-1}) \propto P(r_t | G_t) P(G_t | D_{1:t-1}).
\]
In a simplified binary case (good vs. poor reward), let \(h_t\) be the hidden state indicating reward type (\(h_t=1\) for good, \(0\) for poor). The expectation is the posterior probability \(p_t = P(h_t=1 | D_{1:t})\). If the reward generation is stationary but unknown, each observation updates \(p_t\) via a sufficient statistic. For instance, if rewards are i.i.d. Bernoulli with unknown parameter \(\theta\), using a Beta prior \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\), the posterior becomes \(\text{Beta}(\alpha + \sum_{i=1}^t r_i, \beta + t - \sum_{i=1}^t r_i)\). The mean of this posterior serves as the cognitive expectation for the probability of a good outcome.

**Operator 2: Demand Strength Modulation**
The text states different goal-types are demanded with different strengths given the same physiological drive. Let \(D_{ph}\) represent the scalar intensity of the physiological drive (e.g., thirst level). The effective motivational force \(M(s,a)\) for pursuing a specific goal \(g\) is not simply a function of \(D_{ph}\) but is gated by the cognitive expectation \(E[g]\):
\[
M(s,a; g) = f(D_{ph}) \cdot V(E[g]),
\]
where \(V(\cdot)\) is a valuation function mapping expectation to anticipated utility. A common choice is linear: \(V(p) = p \cdot U_{good} + (1-p) \cdot U_{poor}\), where \(U\) are intrinsic utilities. Thus, even with high \(D_{ph}\), if \(E[good] \approx 0\), \(M\) will be low, leading to reduced responding. This captures the "getting in their licks" phenomenon: the expectation mediates the translation of drive into directed behavior.

**Units and Breakdown Regimes:**
- Cognitive Expectation \(E[\cdot]\): Dimensionless probability mass or rate (events per unit time), bounded in \([0,1]\).
- Physiological Drive \(D_{ph}\): Measured in SI units of energy deficit (Joules) or hormonal concentration (mol/L), but often normalized to a dimensionless scale \([0,1]\) in models.
- Valuation \(V\): In utility units (utils) or expected reduction in drive (Joules saved).
- **Singularity/Breakdown:** The Bayesian update breaks down if the likelihood \(P(r_t|G_t)\) is zero for the observed reward (model misspecification), causing infinite divergence. Also, if the environment changes non-stationarily faster than the learning rate (experience frequency), the expectation cannot converge, leading to persistent prediction errors and erratic behavior. The assumption of independent trials fails if there are temporal correlations in reward availability.

This formalism precisely reflects the textual claim that behavioral adaptation to new reward contingencies requires experiential confirmation to reshape internal cognitive expectations.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s argument emerges from the late 1920s/early 1930s cognitive shift against strict Watsonian behaviorism. While Watson and Skinner emphasized external stimuli and responses, Tolman, along with Köhler and other Gestalt psychologists, argued for intentional systems. The lineage traces back to Kant’s notion of categories structuring experience and Helmholtz’s unconscious inference, but Tolman operationalized these via maze experiments with rats. Historically, his work provided a retrocausal link: future goals (expected rewards) causally influence present behavior by shaping cognitive maps. This challenged the prevailing unidirectional causality (stimulus → response).

Multi-week traps arise from misinterpreting "cognitive expectation" as mere habit strength. Early critics might have reduced it to associative bonds strengthened by reinforcement. However, Tolman demonstrated through latent learning (rats learning mazes without food reward) that cognition exists independently of performance, implying expectations are formed without immediate drive reduction. Another trap is equating expectation with classical conditioning (CS-US association); while related, Tolman’s concept is propositional and flexible—animals can transfer knowledge to novel situations, something simple CS-US bonds cannot explain. Over several weeks, an organism might maintain a stale expectation if the environment is stable, leading to perseveration when conditions change—a phenomenon akin to the "confirmation bias" where prior expectations filter incoming data. Understanding this genealogy clarifies that Tolman was arguing for a mentalistic ontology long before computational neuroscience provided tools to simulate such processes, making his insights remarkably prescient for modern AI and neurobiology.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis situations, the formation of cognitive expectations undergoes significant stress testing. Under high threat or severe deprivation (elevated \(D_{ph}\)), animals may exhibit regressive behaviors, relying on simpler, more rigid stimulus-response mappings rather than flexible expectations. This regression could be seen as a failure of higher-order processing due to resource constraints or emotional overload, forcing the system to fall back on older, evolutionarily conserved routines. For example, a thirsty rat in a novel, complex maze might initially ignore distal cues associated with water if its drive is overwhelming, instead showing undirected searching or thigmotaxis (wall-following). 

Counter-traps emerge when the environment exploits these expectations. Predators or competitive species might use deceptive cues that trigger strong but false expectations (e.g., visual mimicry of water sources). The organism must recursively monitor prediction errors—the discrepancy between expected and actual outcomes—to update its model. If the error signal is consistently high, it indicates a faulty expectation, prompting deeper cognitive restructuring. This recursive loop involves comparing the current expectation \(E_t\) with sensory input \(r_t\), generating an error term \(\delta_t = r_t - E_t\), which then adjusts the next expectation \(E_{t+1}\). 

The "clock" aspect refers to the temporal dynamics of this process. Tolerance for prediction errors may depend on recent history; after prolonged success, even small errors might trigger rapid re-evaluation, whereas during periods of frequent change, the system might become more tolerant of noise, slowing down updates to avoid overfitting to transient fluctuations. This adaptive time-scale management is critical for balancing stability and plasticity. In essence, the theory of mind here is not just about predicting others’ intentions but about recursively modeling one’s own predictive machinery and its reliability in a changing world.

Furthermore, homeostatic conflicts can create internal vetoes—if the animal is simultaneously hungry and thirsty, but only water is available, the expectation for food might suppress drinking behavior due to competing drives, illustrating how multiple layered systems adjudicate final action selection. Thus, the cognitive layer is subject to veto from both lower-level physical/biological systems and higher-level conflict resolution processes.
