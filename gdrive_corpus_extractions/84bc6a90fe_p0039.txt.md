Crisis habit regression: under the pressure of a record attempt, an athlete accustomed to even pacing may revert to an all-out start because the spectator imagination is captured by the “dash” [PAGE 16]. The athlete’s internal model of the timer is recursive: the runner knows the watch reads to the nearest fifth of a second [PAGE 16], so any improvement smaller than 0.2 s is unobservable; this knowledge can either suppress effort (why push for an invisible gain?) or inflate risk (if the gain is invisible, I must make it visibly large). Counter-trap: the coach imposes split-time discipline, forcing v_uniform for the half-mile and above [PAGE 16], thereby overriding the athlete’s first-person urge to “dash.” The clock itself is an observer in the theory-of-mind loop: the timer’s expectation of a record (the “enthusiast” mind [PAGE 16]) biases the split-second-hand reading, while the athlete anticipates that bias and adjusts release of effort accordingly. Spectator theory of mind is most distorted in walking: because walkers do not run, the audience fails to model their exertion as best-effort [PAGE 16], producing a false-belief state in which the spectator attributes low effort to a high-exertion gait constrained by rule rather than by choice.
