Breakdown regimes: (i) small N, where discreteness dominates and h is a non-negligible fraction of the full scale (for N = 8, h = 6.25 centesimal degrees); (ii) p arbitrarily close to 0 or 1, where relative error diverges because the distance to the boundary tends to zero while the absolute bias h remains fixed; (iii) ties or multiple observations per square, violating the one-piece-per-centre premise; (iv) non-uniform bin widths, invalidating cᵢ = i + ½. Exact singularity: at p = 0 or p = 1 the uncorrected empirical quantile is either undefined or set equal to the sample min/max, which is inconsistent for the true boundary quantile because the support of the discrete scheme does not include the boundary itself. As N → ∞, h → 0 and the correction vanishes; as p → {0,1}, the relative error → ∞ regardless of N.

Boundary grid on an 8-point scale (chess board) [PAGE 45]:
bⱼ = j°, j = 0,…,8; b₀ = 0°, b₈ = 8°.

Centre positions:
cᵢ = i + ½°, i = 0,…,7; c₀ = 0.5°, c₇ = 7.5°.

General N-grade scale mapped to centesimal [0,100]:
gᵢ = 100 · (i + ½) / N °, i = 0,…,N−1.
First centre: g₁ = 50/N °. Last centre: g_N = 100 − 50/N °.

Half-place width:
h_N = 50/N °.

Empirical plotting position (midpoint convention):
pᵢ = (i + ½) / N, so Q_N(pᵢ) = gᵢ.

Boundary bias if uncorrected:
Bias_lower ≈ −h_N for p near 0; Bias_upper ≈ +h_N for p near 1.

Mandatory correction zone (from Galton’s “always when values … very near … 0° or 100°” [PAGE 45]):
D_N = [0, h_N] ∪ [100 − h_N, 100].
Inside D_N the half-place cannot be disregarded. Outside D_N, and particularly between 5° and 95° [PAGE 45], the correction is negligible only when N is sufficiently large that h_N ≪ tolerance.

Breakdown / singularity predicates:
Veto if N ≤ 20 (comparatively few observations) and h_N is omitted.
Veto if p ∈ D_N and h_N is omitted.
Veto if the model assigns density to g = 0° or g = 100° as interior attainable values, violating the hard support of the scheme.
As N → ∞, h_N → 0 and the correction is asymptotically unnecessary; as p → 0 or 1, |Bias|/distance-to-boundary → ∞, a non-removable singularity of the uncorrected estimator.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. Galton’s 1889 *Natural Inheritance*, p. 45 [PAGE 45], sits between the error theory of Gauss (1809) and Laplace (1812), the social physics of Quetelet (1835, “average man” and the normal curve), and the later formalization of frequency curves by Pearson (1895, ogives, histograms, moments). Galton’s explicit centesimal grading and his half-place caution anticipate Sheppard’s corrections for grouping (1897–1898) and the continuity correction in the de Moivre–Laplace normal approximation to the binomial. The passage is a bridge from discrete enumeration—pieces on squares—to continuous distribution theory: the empirical CDF constructed from class centres rather than boundaries is the direct ancestor of the modern empirical quantile function and of plotting positions such as (i − α)/(N + 1 − 2α).

Multi-week traps. In any rolling weekly forecasting or re-binning operation, the half-place error does not average out; it is structural. If an analyst re-derives centesimal grades each week from a finite sample and plots minima at 0° and maxima at 100°, every weekly refresh injects the same boundary bias. Over a multi-week horizon, this produces spurious tail compression: extreme observations are pulled toward the centre, making week-over-week changes in the 1st and 99th percentiles appear smaller than they truly are. Conversely, applying an uncorrected continuous kernel smoother at the edges allows probability mass to leak beyond 0° or 100°, violating the bounded support of many biological and physical traits Galton studied. The trap is therefore recursive: ignoring the half-place in week t changes the starting distribution for week t + 1, compounding a false mean-reversion illusion in the tails.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under pressure, an analyst reverts to naïve integer binning: ranks 1 through N are plotted as centesimal grades 1°, 2°, …, 100°, implicitly treating the observed minimum as exactly 0° and the maximum as exactly 100°. This regresses to a boundary-absorbing model in which the discrete lattice is mistaken for a continuous support that touches its limits, contrary to Galton’s opening image that the pieces “will never actually touch them” [PAGE 45].

Counter-trap. The opposite failure is over-correction: applying the half-place shift to large-N central data where Galton permits disregard between 5° and 95° [PAGE 45], or assuming a smooth inverse CDF for inherently discrete, lumpy data (Likert scales, copy-number counts, coarse clinical grades). This injects false decimal precision beyond the instrument’s resolution and treats the empirical quantile as differentiable at points where the underlying distribution has atoms.

Clock interaction. The model refresh clock determines how quickly the half-place bias recurs. A fast clock with small N (e.g., weekly re-binning of N = 8–20 observations) keeps h_N large relative to the scale, so the boundary error is re-injected before any law-of-large-numbers attenuation can occur. A slow clock with large N reduces h_N but risks staleness if the distribution shifts between refreshes. The clock therefore sets the effective signal-to-noise ratio of the correction: fast + small = mandatory correction; slow + large = correction asymptotically optional but still mandatory within D_N.

Recursive theory of mind. Level 0: the data are points on squares. Level 1: I bin them into centesimal grades. Level 2: I assume you treat my grades as continuous probabilities. Level 3: I know you know the bins are discrete, so I apply the half-place correction. Level 4: I know you know I correct, so you expect boundary-respecting kernels; if I omit them, my quantile forecasts are systematically miscalibrated exactly where the user most cares—near 0° and 100°.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. Any measurement device imposes a minimum resolvable interval Δ. If Δ > h_N, the half-place is sub-resolution and cannot be estimated from the data. A model that reports percentile precision finer than Δ is vetoed because it claims information the physical layer cannot supply.

Biomechanical / biological layer. Galton’s domain is human inheritance (stature, etc.), where traits possess hard biological bounds (e.g., height cannot be negative or exceed species limits). Mapping to a centesimal 0°–100° scheme creates a soft percentile envelope, but the underlying variable remains bounded. A model that permits density beyond the hard biological ceiling or floor, or that treats 0° and 100° as interior points rather than unattainable limits of the observed class centres, is vetoed. The half-place correction respects that observed extrema are interior centres, not boundary absorbers.

Weather layer. If the same scheme is applied to meteorological percentile thresholds (e.g., temperature or precipitation extremes), physical singularities exist at absolute zero, saturation, and phase transitions. An uncorrected empirical quantile that sets the sample maximum equal to the 100th percentile collapses the upper tail and understates return periods for rare events; a corrected model places the last observable centre at 100 − h_N, preserving a finite gap to the unattainable boundary.

Formal veto predicate V:
V = 1 if any of the following hold:
(i) N ≤ 20 and h_N is omitted [PAGE 45: “comparatively few observations”];
(ii) p ∈ [0, h_N] ∪ [100 − h_N, 100] and h_N is omitted [PAGE 45: “always when values … very near … 0° or 100°”];
(iii) the model’s support includes g < 0° or g > 100° or assigns mass exactly at the boundaries as attainable.
Otherwise V = 0. Units: h_N and g in centesimal degrees °; N and p dimensionless.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
