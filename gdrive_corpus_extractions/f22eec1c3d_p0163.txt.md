To rigorously analyze Tolman's description of learning and delayed reactions in maze navigation, we must first deconstruct the qualitative terms "sign-gestalt," "good/bad significates," and "immanent expectations" into formal information-theoretic and geometric operators. The core of Tolman's argument is that learning is not simply a reinforcement of stimulus-response bonds but a cognitive restructuring of a topological map where specific cues (alley entrances) become reliable predictors (signs) of distal outcomes (food or non-food).

The initial state before learning is characterized by an epistemic symmetry: the agent assigns equal probability to entering either alley and holds a uniform distribution over potential outcomes ("good" vs. "bad"). This implies a maximum entropy state regarding the spatial configuration. Learning, therefore, corresponds to a reduction in this uncertainty, driven by the acquisition of conditional information. We can model this using a directed graph \( G = (V, E) \) where nodes represent decision points and edges represent alleys. The "refinement of sign-gestalts" is the process of updating the edge weights or transition probabilities based on observed rewards, effectively differentiating between paths leading to high-value states and those leading to low-value or terminal states.

A critical gray-zone emerges when considering the "delayed reaction." Tolman posits that expectations are immanent and persist even when action is postponed. In our formalization, this suggests a latent variable or a held representation in memory that remains stable over time intervals, resisting decay unless actively updated. This challenges simple Markovian assumptions where the current state fully encapsulates all necessary history; instead, it implies a non-Markovian feature where past expectations influence future choices independently of immediate sensory input during the delay.

Furthermore, the differentiation between "routes which actually lead to food" and "non-foods" requires a mechanism for error correction and predictive coding. If an expectation of a "good" significate is violated (e.g., a path previously associated with food is blocked), the system must rapidly re-weight the significance of the sign. This introduces a dynamic component to the sign-gestalt, making it a function of both current sensory data and historical success rates. The concrete maze picture (Figure 49 mentioned in text) serves as a bifurcation point analysis: at \( t_0 \), the agent faces two vectors \( \vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2 \). Pre-learning, \( P(\text{Food}|\vec{v}_1) \approx P(\text{Food}|\vec{v}_2) \). Post-learning, one vector becomes a strong indicator (\( P \to 1 \)) while the other becomes a strong negative indicator (\( P \to 0 \)).

However, a multi-hop derivation reveals a potential trap: assuming that "equal frequencies" of entry pre-learning necessarily implies zero knowledge. It might instead reflect exploratory behavior under uncertainty, governed by an exploration-exploitation trade-off rather than pure ignorance. Thus, the baseline model must account for an intrinsic noise term or exploration bonus that prevents immediate collapse into deterministic behavior until sufficient evidence accumulates. This nuance is crucial for defining the exact singularity regime where the transition from ambiguous signs to crisp categories occurs—likely related to the accumulation of mutual information exceeding a threshold variance. Finally, we must audit the assumption that "non-foods" and "non-explorable objects" form a homogeneous category. They likely possess distinct statistical signatures (e.g., texture, odor) that allow for finer-grained discrimination once attention is directed, suggesting the learning process involves expanding the dimensionality of the feature space, not just compressing probabilities.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
We define the **Sign-Gestalt Operator** \( \mathcal{S}: \mathcal{X} \to \Delta^{\mathcal{Y}} \), mapping a perceptual stimulus space \( \mathcal{X} \) (specifically, the visual/proprioceptive features of an alley entrance) to a probability simplex over outcome spaces \( \mathcal{Y} = \{ \text{Good}, \text{Bad} \} \). Here, "Good" encompasses food or lead-on-ableness, and "Bad" encompasses non-foods/dead-ends.

Let \( x_t \in \mathcal{X} \) be the observation at bifurcation point \( t \). The pre-learning state is defined by the uniform prior:
\[ \mathcal{S}_{\text{init}}(x_t) = \left[ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}, \dots, \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|} \right]^T \]
This reflects Tolman's assertion: *"the rat is ready to treat both the alternative alleys as alike... expect the possibilities of both 'good' and 'bad' significates... as a result of either of them"* [PAGE 145].

Learning updates \( \mathcal{S} \) via Bayesian inference or Reinforcement Learning value iteration. Let \( R(y) \) be the reward function where \( R(\text{Good}) > 0 \) and \( R(\text{Bad}) \leq 0 \). The refined sign-gestalt after \( N \) trials is:
\[ \mathcal{S}_{N}(x_t) \propto \exp\left( \beta \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}(x_{t_i}=x_t) \cdot V(s_{t_i+1}) \right) \]
where \( V \) is the value of the subsequent state and \( \beta \) is an inverse temperature parameter controlling stochasticity. As \( N \to \infty \) and assuming ergodic sampling, \( \mathcal{S}_{N}(x_t) \) converges to a delta function peaked at the true outcome class if the environment is stationary.

The **Delayed Reaction Constraint** introduces a temporal lag \( \tau \). The expectation \( E_t \) formed at \( t \) persists into \( t+\tau \). Formally, the policy \( \pi(a|x_{t+\tau}) \) depends on \( E_t \):
\[ \pi(a | x_{t+\tau}, E_t) = \text{softmax}\left( \alpha \cdot \langle \phi(x_{t+\tau}), \theta(E_t) \rangle \right) \]
Here, \( \phi \) encodes current context and \( \theta \) projects the held expectation. If \( E_t \) indicates a "good" significate was expected down path A, the agent biases toward re-entering path A upon release, even if \( x_{t+\tau} \) is neutral.

**Singularity/Breakdown Regime:** The model breaks down when the mutual information \( I(X; Y) \) approaches zero (pure noise) or when the delay \( \tau \) exceeds the memory capacity limit \( C_{\text{mem}} \), causing \( E_t \) to decay to the prior. Specifically, if the variance of the estimated value \( \text{Var}(\hat{V}) \) exceeds a critical threshold \( \sigma_c^2 \), the sign-gestalt loses discriminative power, reverting to exploratory random walks. This corresponds to the "gray zone" where neither "good" nor "bad" expectations dominate.

**Units:** Probabilities are dimensionless (SI unit 1). Rewards \( R \) are in utility units (e.g., kcal equivalent). Time \( \tau \) is in seconds.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s "purposive behaviorism" represents a pivotal shift from Thorndike’s connectionism (S-R bonds strengthened by satisfaction) to cognitive map theory. Historically, this lineage traces back to Kantian noumena/phenomena distinctions, filtered through Gestalt psychology’s emphasis on holistic perception (*Gestalt*) rather than elemental stimuli. Tolman introduced "sign-gestalt" to bridge the gap: the organism perceives signs (stimuli) that signify goals (mediational processes).

Scientifically, this prefigures modern Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Predictive Coding. Tolman’s "expectancies" are analogous to value functions \( Q(s,a) \) or predictive models \( P(s'|s,a) \). However, a multi-week trap exists in interpreting his work solely through contemporary RL lenses. Tolman explicitly rejected "drive reduction" as the sole motivator, emphasizing "purpose" without postulating internal mental states in a dualistic sense. Modern interpretations often retrocausally project neural network architectures onto his "cognitive maps," potentially obscuring his strict adherence to operational definitions observable via behavior (e.g., latency, choice frequency).

Another genealogical thread connects to cybernetics and Wiener’s feedback loops. Tolman’s delayed reaction tasks were among the first to demonstrate that animals could hold information online, challenging the then-dominant view that behavior was strictly reflexive. This laid groundwork for working memory research. A subtle trap lies in conflating Tolman’s "immanent expectations" with conscious foresight; he maintained these were unobservable intervening variables, deducible only from systematic behavioral deviations in novel configurations (means-end analyses). Thus, any formalization must preserve the opacity of the mediating process, focusing on input-output transformations rather than simulating subjective experience.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis habit regression scenarios (e.g., sudden blockage of a learned optimal path), the agent’s sophisticated sign-gestalt map may fail, triggering a fallback to lower-level, habitual S-R chains. From a Recursive Theory of Mind perspective, the agent monitors its own prediction errors. When the discrepancy between expected "good" significate and observed reality exceeds a tolerance threshold, confidence in the current cognitive map drops.

*   **Crisis Habit Regression:** High prediction error -> Suppression of model-based control -> Activation of model-free habitual system (basal ganglia dominance). The rat might repeatedly attempt the blocked alley (perseveration) before switching to random exploration. This is a maladaptive trap where the "sign" retains residual salience despite negative feedback.
*   **Counter-Trap Strategy:** To avoid regression, the system employs metacognitive tagging. Uncertain outcomes are flagged, promoting broader sampling (exploration) rather than commitment to a single path. This aligns with Tolman’s "latency shifts" where hesitation increases when cues are ambiguous.
*   **Clock Mechanism:** The timing of decisions is crucial. Under stress or urgency (shortened clock cycles), the integration window for refining sign-gestalts narrows, forcing reliance on cached heuristics (habits) rather than online computation. Conversely, extended delays (long clock) allow for consolidation of new associations but risk memory decay. The interplay defines whether the agent acts as a planner or a reflex machine.

Thus, the learning process is not monotonic improvement but a oscillation between exploiting known maps and exploring to update them, modulated by internal estimates of reliability and external time pressure.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical constraints impose hard vetoes on the abstract sign-gestalt refinement. Even if a path is cognitively marked as "good," execution may fail due to:
1.  **Biomechanical Limitations:** Turn radius, stride length, or muscle fatigue may prevent precise navigation of narrow alleys identified as optimal. For instance, an obese rat might physically jam in a corridor that a leaner counterpart navigates easily, effectively rendering the "sign" invalid for that specific agent morphology.
2.  **Sensory Noise/Veto:** Lighting conditions, olfactory contamination, or auditory distractions can corrupt the \( x_t \) input. If the signal-to-noise ratio drops below detection thresholds, the sign-gestalt operator cannot activate, defaulting to random search. This is a bottom-up veto overriding top-down expectation.
3.  **Weather/Environmental Drift:** In open-field mazes, wind direction can alter scent trails, decoupling the static map from dynamic reality. A route leading to food yesterday might now lead away due to shifted odor plumes. The system must incorporate environmental covariates into the state definition; failure to do so results in persistent "bad" outcomes despite correct map retrieval.

These factors prove that cognitive maps are not infallible blueprints but probabilistic guides subject to continuous validation against physical interaction. A robust model must include a "veto layer" that inhibits motor programs when proprioceptive feedback conflicts with predicted sensory consequences.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
