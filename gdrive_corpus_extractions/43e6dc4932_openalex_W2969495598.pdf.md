The source’s sleeper definition is a joint condition on draft cost and realized positional rank: “we define sleepers as players who reach within the top twenty within their role who are drafted in the fourth round or later” [PAGE 12]. That definition immediately creates a multi-hop dependency chain. Let \(S_i\) be the sleeper indicator for player \(i\). Then \(S_i\) depends on realized fantasy points \(y_i\), which depend on opportunity \(o_i=(\text{carries}_i,\text{targets}_i)\) and athletic/team covariates \(x_i\). The model estimates \(y_i\) from \(x_i\) and \(o_i\), but the sleeper decision is made before \(o_i\) is known. The paper concedes this exact identification gap: “this performance is heavily reliant upon the accuracy of estimates of athletes’ workloads” [PAGE 2]. Thus high in-sample explanatory power is not evidence of prospective sleeper detection unless opportunity is forecast or updated.

The audit’s central gray zone is opportunity leakage. The paper reports very strong regression fits with carries and targets included: “Our model returned a R2 value of 0.927 for forecasting fantasy points, and an MSE of 352.03” [PAGE 16]. But when carries and targets were omitted, the authors report that “Nearly all predictions were extremely far from actual performance to the point of no reasonable use case (with R2 values of 0.4)” [PAGE 15]. That is not a minor ablation; it changes the interpretation of the model from “athletic sleeper discovery” to “workload-conditional scoring projection.” The feature-importance discussion reinforces the same point: targets and carries dominate, with “targets, which are the specific ‘opportunity’ for running backs, have the highest importance” [PAGE 16]. The simulation section then fixes carries and targets at common levels, which is a useful stress test but not a prospective forecast.

A second gray zone is model selection versus sleeper selection. The ensemble weights are source-defined: for running backs, “82% lasso, and 18% XGBoost”; for wide receivers, “19% lasso, 41% random forest, 40% XGBoost” [PAGE 20]. The ensemble MSEs are close to the best individual models, but close MSE does not imply stable player rankings. A model can have low average squared error while misranking the tail players who matter for sleeper identification. The paper itself notes that some elite names appear near the bottom of the simulated list, and some older players remain near the top: “This is likely indicative that the models do not fully reduce performance for age correctly and more research may be needed” [PAGE 21]. That is a direct caution against treating the model as a completed sleeper detector.

A third gray zone is data generation. Missing seasonal statistics are replaced with zero, and combine metrics are imputed. The source says “we replaced null values for seasonal statistics with ‘0’ values to represent a player’s lack of activity” [PAGE 9]. That is defensible for inactivity, but it conflates “did not play” with “played and produced zero.” Imputation is also selective: missing combine data can arise from injury or choice, so the missingness mechanism is not ignorable. The random-forest imputation with 3-fold cross-validation is a reasonable engineering choice, but it cannot recover the counterfactual performance of an injured player who never completed the workout. Finally, team context is modeled partly through a “black box method” from Pro Football Focus [PAGE 9], and team identity was tried but “proved to have no clear positive effect” [PAGE 15]. The multi-hop conclusion is therefore conditional, not causal: the paper demonstrates that fantasy points are predictable when workload is supplied, but it does not demonstrate that athletic metrics alone identify undervalued players. The correct audit stance is to separate three claims: (1) workload-conditional point projection, which is supported; (2) prospective opportunity forecasting, which is explicitly left to future work [PAGE 20]; and (3) sleeper discovery, which remains suggestive but not statistically closed. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a sleeper as a player whose draft cost is late and whose realized positional rank is high. With \(D_i\) the draft round (dimensionless count) and \(R_i\) the within-role rank (dimensionless count), the paper’s working definition is
\[
S_i=\mathbf{1}\{D_i\ge 4\ \land\ R_i\le 20\},
\]
where \(S_i\in\{0,1\}\) is dimensionless. This is exactly the source’s definition: “we define sleepers as players who reach within the top twenty within their role who are drafted in the fourth round or later” [PAGE 12]. The assumption is league-size and position invariance; the paper notes this is “a working definition and is subject to position-specific variance” [PAGE 3].

The core regression operator is
\[
\hat y_i=f_\theta(x_i,o_i)+\epsilon_i,
\]
where \(y_i\) is fantasy points, \(\mathrm{FP}\); \(x_i\) is the athlete and team covariate vector; \(o_i=(\text{carries}_i,\text{targets}_i)\) is the opportunity vector in counts; and \(\epsilon_i\) is residual error in \(\mathrm{FP}\). The paper’s scoring convention is “1 point per 10 rushing or receiving yards and 6 points for a touchdown. Bonuses for long runs were not applied, and no point value was assigned to completed receptions” [PAGE 15]. The fit metric is
\[
\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2,
\]
with units \(\mathrm{FP}^2\), and
\[
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2},
\]
which is dimensionless. The paper reports, for example, an RB lasso \(R^2=0.927\) and \(\mathrm{MSE}=352.03\) [PAGE 16], and an XGBoost RB \(R^2=0.96\) and \(\mathrm{MSE}=397.85\) [PAGE 17]. These are workload-conditional diagnostics, not prospective sleeper diagnostics.

The ensemble operator is a convex combination:
\[
\hat y_i^{\mathrm{ens}}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}w_m\hat y_i^{(m)},\qquad w_m\ge 0,\quad \sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}w_m=1,
\]
with units \(\mathrm{FP}\). For running backs, \(w_{\mathrm{lasso}}=0.82\) and \(w_{\mathrm{XGB}}=0.18\); for wide receivers, \(w_{\mathrm{lasso}}=0.19\), \(w_{\mathrm{RF}}=0.41\), and \(w_{\mathrm{XGB}}=0.40\) [PAGE 20]. The reported ensemble MSEs are “347.17” for running backs and “246.73” for wide receivers [PAGE 20]. The conclusion page later states 347.18 and 246.74 [PAGE 22], a small textual inconsistency that should be treated as a reporting rounding or transcription discrepancy, not as a new model.

Opportunity sensitivity is the key derived operator. A first-order local expansion gives
\[
\Delta \hat y_i\approx \frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial c_i}\Delta c_i+\frac{\partial f_\theta}{\partial t_i}\Delta t_i,
\]
where \(c_i\) is carries and \(t_i\) is targets. The partial derivatives have units \(\mathrm{FP}\) per carry and \(\mathrm{FP}\) per target. The source’s ablation implies large derivatives: without carries and targets, \(R^2\) falls to “0.4” [PAGE 15]. The source’s simulation fixes opportunity levels to compare players, but the paper states that this is not a full opportunity forecast: “Forecasting playing opportunity was attempted, but this proved to be an area of required additional research” [PAGE 20].

The source gives one explicit athletic formula, the running-back Speed Score:
\[
\mathrm{SS}=\frac{200\,m}{t_{40}^{4}},
\]
where \(m\) is the source’s weight variable and \(t_{40}\) is the 40-yard dash time in seconds. The source describes it as “Speed Score (weight*200) / (40-timeˆ4). Formula developed by Bill Barnwell to predict running back success. Biased towards heavier running backs” [PAGE 8]. Dimensionally, the source expression has mass divided by time to the fourth power; in SI-converted variables it would be \(\mathrm{kg\,s^{-4}}\) with the empirical constant adjusted, so the numerical 200 should not be treated as dimensionless. Its logarithmic sensitivity is
\[
\frac{\partial \ln \mathrm{SS}}{\partial \ln t_{40}}=-4,
\]
so a small timing error is amplified fourfold in relative terms. This is why the formula is biomechanically fragile near small \(t_{40}\) values.

Imputation can be written as
\[
\hat x_{ij}=g_j(x_{i,-j}),
\]
where \(x_{ij}\) is the missing metric in its native unit and \(g_j\) is the random-forest imputation operator. The source says random forest was chosen after testing and used “3-fold cross validation” [PAGE 9]. The assumption is that missingness is conditionally recoverable from observed metrics. That assumption fails if injury or nonparticipation is correlated with the missing value itself.

If one converts the regression output into a sleeper probability \(p_i=P(S_i=1\mid X_i)\), the proper scoring rule is log loss
\[
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[S_i\log p_i+(1-S_i)\log(1-p_i)\right],
\]
measured in nats. The paper does not train this classifier, so this is an audit extension, not a reported result.

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. \(R^2\) is undefined when \(\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2=0\), that is, when the outcome is constant. Speed Score is singular as \(t_{40}\to 0\) and has derivative \(\partial \mathrm{SS}/\partial t_{40}=-800m/t_{40}^{5}\), which blows up as \(t_{40}\to 0\). Lasso with the reported alpha of 1.0 can set all coefficients to zero, collapsing to an intercept-only model. Ensemble weights are non-identifiable if base-model predictions are collinear. Opportunity simulation breaks down outside the convex hull of observed carries and targets, where linear models extrapolate without support and tree models become flat. Imputation breaks down under missing-not-at-random mechanisms. Weather is not represented in the source’s metric tables or data-preparation sections, so no weather coefficient or weather-veto proof can be estimated from these pages.

The paper’s sleeper model is historically downstream of a long fantasy-football lineage. The source traces the first league concept to 1962: “The initial concept of the first fantasy football league was created by three men in a Manhattan hotel in 1962” [PAGE 4]. That origin matters because the modern sleeper concept is not a natural kind; it is produced by draft formats, scoring conventions, and public information markets. The paper describes auction, snake, and linear drafts [PAGE 5], and it notes that snake and auction formats are seen as fairer while linear drafts disadvantage later pick order [PAGE 4]. These institutional forms determine what “undervalued” means. A player’s average draft position is not an exogenous talent signal; it is an equilibrium belief formed by other participants.

The scientific lineage runs through athletic testing, scouting, and team-context grading. The source uses combine and predraft metrics such as the 40-yard dash, 20-yard shuttle, 3-cone drill, bench press, broad jump, vertical jump, height, weight, arm length, and hand size [PAGE 7]. It also uses Bill Barnwell’s Speed Score, explicitly “Biased towards heavier running backs” [PAGE 8], and Frank DuPont’s Dominator Rating, with historical thresholds described as “35% level as elite; 20-35% as high potential; < 20% as point of concern” [PAGE 7]. Team context is imported through Pro Football Focus, which “has developed a black box method for scoring different components of the team” [PAGE 9]. The paper also cites prior work on the predictive value of the NFL scouting combine for running backs and wide receivers [PAGE 24]. This genealogy explains why the model mixes open athletic measurements with proprietary, opaque team grades.

The retrocausal trap is that the final model’s high \(R^2\) can make the earlier athletic lineage look more decisive than it is. The historical record contains many players who tested well and never received opportunity, and many who tested modestly but succeeded in the right role. The source’s own simulation finds some known stars near the top but also finds “Davante Adams and DeAndre Hopkins were noticeably near the bottom” [PAGE 21]. That is a retrocausal warning: a model fit to realized points can appear to validate athletic metrics when the actual driver was opportunity.

Multi-week traps are also visible in the paper’s design. The train/test split uses “2013 to 2016 data as training data, and 2017 and 2018 as test data” [PAGE 15]. That is a seasonal split, not a weekly one. Within a season, depth-chart changes, injuries, opponent context, and team pace can shift carries and targets over multiple weeks. The paper acknowledges that the model “must be combined with opportunity forecasting, or active management by the user throughout the NFL season to update predicted opportunities for relevant players” [PAGE 22]. A multi-week trap is therefore any workflow that freezes preseason opportunity estimates and then interprets later point changes as athletic emergence. The paper’s external benchmark also shows how volatile preseason projections are: for wide receivers, “our model had an R2 of .28 and an MSE of 2186” [PAGE 22]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that sleeper identification changes the beliefs that produce sleepers. A participant models other participants’ beliefs about undervalued players; those beliefs determine average draft position; draft position then changes the sleeper condition itself. In compact form, let \(\hat p_i\) be the model’s sleeper probability and \(D_i\) the player’s draft position. Public belief and model output form a feedback loop \(D_i=\mathcal{D}(\{\hat p_j\})\) and \(\hat p_i=\mathcal{P}(X_i,D_i)\), so the fixed point \(D_i^*=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}(X_i,D_i^*))\) is the object of interest. The paper’s definition is not stable under disclosure: if a model publicly flags a late-round player as a sleeper, that player’s draft position can rise, and the same player may no longer satisfy the late-draft condition [PAGE 12]. This is a reflexive validity problem, not a statistical nuisance.

Crisis habit regression appears when uncertainty is high. Under pressure, participants often regress to name-brand players, recent playoff performances, or average draft position as a social proof heuristic. The model can reinforce that habit if it reports a high \(R^2\) without disclosing that carries and targets were supplied. The source’s abstract already warns that performance is “heavily reliant upon the accuracy of estimates of athletes’ workloads” [PAGE 2], but a hurried user may anchor on the reported \(R^2=0.927\) [PAGE 16] and ignore the workload condition. That is a crisis habit: mistaking a conditional scoring model for an unconditional talent detector.

The counter-trap is overcorrection. A sophisticated user may react to the opportunity critique by over-weighting obscure combine metrics and ignoring role. The source does not support that overcorrection. It reports that without carries and targets, predictions were “extremely far from actual performance” [PAGE 15], and it reports that opportunity features dominate. The correct counter-trap is not “ignore opportunity” but “model opportunity explicitly and keep athletic metrics as a secondary, uncertainty-qualified layer.” The paper’s simulation does exactly this in a limited way by fixing carries and targets and comparing players under equal workload [PAGE 21]. That is a controlled thought experiment, not a prospective forecast.

The clock dimension is timing. The model is annual, but the relevant information arrives weekly. A recursive theory of mind must include a clock variable: at what week is the belief being updated, and what opportunity information is available at that time? The paper says the model must be paired with “active management by the user throughout the NFL season” [PAGE 22]. That means the user’s belief about a player should be a time-indexed posterior, not a single preseason rank. A participant who updates after depth-chart news, injury reports, and weekly target/carry changes is playing a different game from one who freezes the preseason model. The recursive trap is that the model’s high training fit can create false confidence in a static ranking, while the source’s own benchmark shows low out-of-sample explanatory power under preseason projections [PAGE 22].

The ethical recursion is also relevant: the paper warns that quantified selection can create disparate impact even without explicit protected variables, and it invokes the ACM Code’s fairness principle [PAGE 22]. In a fantasy setting the stakes are lower, but the modeling habit is the same: a quantified score can be treated as objective when it is actually a conditional estimate built from incomplete and partly proprietary inputs. The counter-trap is to publish the conditionality, the missingness, and the opportunity dependence alongside any sleeper ranking.

Weather veto. Across the provided 24 pages, no weather variable appears in the metric tables, data-preparation section, modeling section, or benchmark section. The source’s covariates are athletic metrics, body measurements, team grades, draft position, age, experience, targets, carries, and historical performance; weather is absent. Pro Football Focus provides team component scores through a “black box method” [PAGE 9], and the authors tried team identity as a variable but found “no clear positive effect” [PAGE 15]. The paper also warns that team schemes can change dramatically over six years [PAGE 15]. A context veto should trigger when a player changes teams, coaching staff, or role and the model has no updated opportunity forecast. The paper’s own conclusion is that the model “must be combined with opportunity forecasting” [PAGE 22]. Thus the physical, biomechanical, and context layers all converge on the same veto rule: if opportunity is not forecast or updated, the sleeper claim is not identified, regardless of the model’s training \(R^2\).

Counterfactual: take the reported running-back and wide-receiver models and replace carries and targets with a constant or with permuted values on the 2017-2018 holdout. Verifiable prediction: performance should collapse toward the source’s no-opportunity regime, where “Nearly all predictions were extremely far from actual performance to the point of no reasonable use case (with R2 values of 0.4)” [PAGE 15]. If the ablation collapses as predicted, then any sleeper claim without an opportunity forecast is vetoed. This is verifiable by a deterministic rerun with the same train/test split “2013 to 2016 data as training data, and 2017 and 2018 as test data” [PAGE 15].

Counterfactual: rerun the sleeper ranking twice, once with the source’s random-forest imputation and once with complete-case-only records for players missing combine metrics. Compare the top-20 sleeper sets \(A\) and \(B\) using the Jaccard overlap \(J(A,B)=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|\), which is dimensionless. Verifiable prediction: the overlap should be materially below 1, especially for players with injury-related or nonparticipation missingness, because the source says missing combine data can be caused by injury or choice [PAGE 9] and imputation was used to retain records [PAGE 9].
