The second hop is the algorithm. NSAR keeps an active set \(A_r\), initializes \(A_1=[K]\), and proceeds for \(K-1\) rounds. The source says: “The algorithm proceeds for K −1 rounds to deactivate the arms sequentially (one arm at each round) until a single arm is left.” [PAGE 3] At round \(r\), the active arms are sampled \(n_r-n_{r-1}\) times, where \(n_r\) is set by a nonlinear denominator \((K-r+1)^p\). Thus the algorithm is not merely “eliminate the worst arm”; it maintains the count of accepted top-M arms and can reject a high empirical gap that lies below the current top-M boundary.

The third hop is the proof architecture. The proof defines a good event \(E\) in which every empirical mean is within one quarter of the relevant gap \(\Delta^{\langle M\rangle}_{(K+1-r)}\). Hoeffding’s inequality gives a two-sided tail of order \(\exp(-2n_r( \Delta/4)^2)\). The union bound over \(K\) arms and \(K-1\) rounds produces \(2K^2\) and the maximum over \(r\) of the exponent. The scientific lineage is a compression of pure-exploration bandit history. The source places itself against cumulative-regret MAB, citing Lai and Robbins and Auer et al. The later theorem retrospectively explains why earlier linear schedules were a special case and why the nonlinear family can improve the exponent by an \(O(\log K)\) factor in the two regimes of Corollary 3 [PAGE 4].

The second trap is to compare NSAR to AT-LUCB as if both were fixed-budget algorithms; the source notes that AT-LUCB is “an anytime algorithm” and is included as a benchmark because it can also be used in the fixed-budget framework [PAGE 3]. The fourth trap is to tune \(p\) quantitatively from a small grid when the paper describes the choice as “more qualitative rather than quantitative” [PAGE 4]. The fifth trap is to ignore the fixed-budget correction \(T-K\) and the floor in \(n_r\), which matter in small-\(T\) experiments. The sixth trap is to extrapolate the Bernoulli simulation setups to arbitrary reward distributions; the theorem needs bounded independent rewards, and the experiments use Bernoulli rewards with \(K=50\) and \(M\in\{2,4\}\) [PAGE 5]. These are the main historical and experimental fault lines.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In this paper, the “theory of mind” object is not a human opponent but a nested modeling problem: the designer models the reward regime, the algorithm models the active arms through empirical gaps, and the experimenter models the algorithm’s parameter \(p\) through observed misidentification probability. The first failure mode is crisis habit regression. When the environment is ambiguous, the designer falls back to the familiar linear schedule \(p=1\), which “amounts to SAR” [PAGE 3], or to uniform allocation, which the source says is “outperformed by the other methods” in all setups [PAGE 5]. This is a safe habit because it is a known special case, but it is a regression because it discards the nonlinear family that the paper is testing. The recursive trap is that the fallback is justified by the belief “I do not know the regime,” while the paper’s own claim is that the choice of \(p\) is qualitative: \(p\in(0,1)\) when suboptimal arms are nearly alike, and \(p\in(1,2]\) when only a few arms are truly competitive [PAGE 4]. The habit becomes dangerous if it is applied after the empirical gap pattern already reveals which regime is likely.

The second failure mode is the counter-trap: over-inferring the regime and over-tuning \(p\). A designer who believes “there are few competitive arms” chooses \(p>1\), but the source warns that “larger values for p increase H⟨M⟩(p) and decrease Cp” [PAGE 4]. At finite \(K=50\), the increase in \(H^{\langle M\rangle}(p)\) can dominate the decrease in \(C_p\); the source says larger \(p\) is “not suitable” in those setups [PAGE 5]. The recursive theory-of-mind error is that the designer models the environment’s gap structure but does not model the algorithm’s finite-\(K\) distortion of \(H^{\langle M\rangle}(p)\). The counter-trap is therefore a second-order belief failure: the belief about the regime is not wrong, but the belief about the cost of acting on that belief is incomplete.

The third failure mode is clock failure. NSAR has a hard clock: \(K-1\) rounds, a fixed budget \(T\), and a schedule \(n_r\) that increases as the active set shrinks. A designer who evaluates the algorithm before the budget clock has elapsed, or who compares algorithms under different effective sample counts, will mistake schedule effects for regime effects. The source’s experiments use a fixed budget \(T=\lceil H_1^{\langle M\rangle}\rceil\) in each setup [PAGE 5], which is itself a clock normalization. The recursive correction is to hold the clock fixed, then vary \(p\), then compare against SAR and AT-LUCB. A counter-trap is to let the algorithm’s own accept/reject history update the designer’s belief about \(p\) during a run; NSAR does not adapt \(p\), and the source’s conclusion says that adaptive fine-tuning is a future direction [PAGE 5]. Thus the correct recursive stance is: model the regime before the run, hold the budget clock fixed during the run, and do not treat the algorithm’s intermediate empirical gaps as a license to change \(p\) without a new certified schedule.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source has no biomechanical, weather, or physical-layer variables. Its variable inventory is \(K\), \(M\), \(T\), \(r\), \(p\), \(C_p\), \(n_r\), \(\mu_i\), \(\Delta_i^{\langle M\rangle}\), empirical means, and probabilities. None of these carries SI biomechanical units such as newtons, joules, or fatigue; none carries weather state such as temperature, wind, or precipitation. The paper’s motivating example is communication channel allocation, where “Each channel feedback is noisy, and the number of trials (budget) is limited” [PAGE 1], but that example is still an abstract bandit with bounded rewards, not a physical simulation. Therefore a physical or biomechanical veto cannot be applied to the theorem as stated: there is no cross-layer variable to veto. The theorem’s only physical-style constraints are the bounded-support assumption on [0,1] and the independence/fixed-distribution assumption [PAGE 2].

The adjudication rule is conditional. If an external physical layer changes the reward means \(\mu_i\) during the run, the source’s fixed-distribution assumption is violated and Proposition 2 is not a valid guarantee; the problem becomes nonstationary bandit identification, which is outside the source. If a biomechanical fatigue variable changes the reward support outside [0,1], the Hoeffding support assumption is violated and the bound is vetoed. If weather changes the ordering \(\mu_1>\mu_2>\cdots>\mu_K\), then the strict-gap assumption and the \(H^{\langle M\rangle}(p)\) singularity analysis may break. But if the physical or weather effect is fully encoded as a fixed, bounded reward distribution per arm, then the cross-layer language is irrelevant: the theorem applies to the encoded bandit, and the physical interpretation has no independent veto power. The proof obligation is therefore not “does weather matter?” but “does weather preserve stationarity, boundedness, and the gap ordering?” If yes, no veto; if no, the source theorem is inapplicable. This is the cross-layer veto proof: every admissible external effect must be expressible as a change in \(\mu_i\), in the support, or in the stationarity assumption; otherwise it is outside the source’s formal domain.
