Hull (1943) cites Guthrie [PAGE 191] for the idea that all primary generalization might be built on a contiguity model: stimuli evoke responses because they share elements with the originally conditioned stimulus. Guthrie’s contiguity theory, in turn, descends from the associationist tradition and anticipates modern population-coding concepts in sensory neuroscience, where neighboring points on a sensory map (retinotopic, tonotopic, somatotopic) activate overlapping neural ensembles. Hull also invokes Thorndike [PAGE 191] for training transfer, linking stimulus generalization to the educational and comparative-psychology tradition of identical elements. Finally, the discussion of conditioned stimuli, trace reactions, and sensitization [PAGE 192] places Hull squarely in the Pavlovian classical-conditioning lineage, where the CS–US contiguity and trace interval determine whether generalization remains modality-specific or propagates across receptor systems.

Tracing forward, Hull’s "molecules" are an informal precursor to receptive-field overlap models and to information-theoretic treatments of perceptual discriminability (e.g., signal-detection theory and d′). The 75 per cent criterion prefigures modern psychophysical threshold methods, though without the formal likelihood-ratio machinery. The distinction between primary generalization (within modality, afferent overlap) and secondary generalization (across modalities, face/voice/footstep equivalence [PAGE 192]) anticipates the modern division between perceptual learning and semantic/conceptual generalization.

Multi-week traps arise directly from this architecture. Because generalization gradients are continuous over physical space, a stimulus similar—but not identical—to a conditioned stimulus will elicit a CR long after the original training if the organism re-encounters it weeks later. Extinction of the trained CS does not extinguish responses to generalizing stimuli unless those specific points on the continuum are also extinguished; this yields spontaneous recovery and renewal effects. Under conditions of stress or changed context, the organism may regress to the most strongly generalized habit because the habit hierarchy is organized by overlap strength rather than by explicit rule. Thus a behavior changed in week one can re-emerge in week five when a sufficiently similar stimulus reappears, producing a relapse trap that looks like a failure of will but is, in Hull’s terms, a predictable function of the generalization gradient and the residual afferent overlap of the provoking stimulus [PAGE 191].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From a recursive theory-of-mind perspective, the organism under crisis does not reason abstractly about which stimulus is "the same"; it regresses habitually to the response with the highest generalized excitatory potential. Hull’s model implies a habit-strength hierarchy ordered by afferent overlap: the closer a crisis stimulus is, on the physical continuum, to the originally reinforced stimulus, the stronger the competing tendency to emit the old response. Crisis habit regression is therefore the deployment of a high-overlap generalization gradient under load, when deliberative inhibition is weakened.

The counter-trap is that attempts to suppress the regressed habit via punishment or aversion can invoke the sensitization exception Hull explicitly notes [PAGE 192]. Sensitization raises general arousal and can produce responding across the continuum that is not graded by \(\Omega\), effectively flattening the generalization gradient and making the organism reactive to stimuli that should, under the primary model, be discriminable. In other words, the counter-measure (punishment) can convert a controlled, overlap-governed generalization into a non-associative, sensitization-driven generalization, undermining the very discrimination the trainer seeks to establish.

The clock enters through the trace-conditioning distinction. Hull limits primary generalization to within-modality contiguous stimuli except under "long" trace conditioned reactions [PAGE 192]. Temporal contiguity is therefore a gating variable: short-trace conditioning preserves sharp, modality-specific gradients, whereas long-trace conditioning permits broader or cross-modal generalization. Operationally, if the interval between CS and US exceeds the trace-decay constant, the affective or motivational state becomes dissociated from the specific sensory afferents and can attach to temporally or even modally remote cues. This creates a recursive loop: the organism anticipates the US not because the current stimulus overlaps the trained CS, but because the clock has allowed the US representation to free-float and re-attach to any available cue, producing superstition and generalized anxiety that persist across weeks.
