<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight’s *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* did not emerge in a vacuum; it was a calculated intervention in the late 19th/early 20th-century debate over economic surplus. The genealogy traces back to Karl Marx’s labor theory of value and John Bates Clark’s marginal productivity theory. Clark argued that in a state of "dynamic equilibrium," each factor of production receives its exact marginal product, eliminating any unexplained residual profit. Change was viewed merely as a sequential series of equilibria.

Knight identified the blind spot in this neoclassical lineage: the assumption of perfect foresight. By retrocausally analyzing the Industrial Revolution, Knight realized that the massive accumulation of capital could not be explained by predictable, incremental adjustments. The trap of the "Clark school" was assuming that because physical laws are deterministic, economic laws must be similarly tractable. They conflated physical friction with economic uncertainty.

Simultaneously, Knight engaged with the Austrian School (Menger, Mises) and Thorstein Veblen. Veblen emphasized the habitual vs. reflective nature of human action, foreshadowing behavioral economics. Knight synthesized these by positing that entrepreneurs operate in a realm of reflective judgment precisely because the future lacks a calculable frequency distribution. This historical realization created a multi-week (or rather, multi-decade) trap for later economists: the mid-20th century saw the rise of Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz) and the Black-Scholes model, which arrogantly assumed Knight’s "unmeasurable uncertainty" could be mathematically tamed into "measurable risk" via volatility surfaces. The 2008 crash was the violent historical reckoning with this suppression of Knight's core theorem.
