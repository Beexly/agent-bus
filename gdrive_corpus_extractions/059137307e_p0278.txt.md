Gibson and later expanded by Land and Mnatsakanian. However, James stops short of fully embracing the ecological approach. He remains focused on the internal construction of the image ("imaginary form") rather than the pickup of ambient optical arrays.

**The Multi-Week Trap:**
The trap lies in the seductive simplicity of the "extensive whole." For weeks or months, a student of psychology might operate under the assumption that space is fundamentally homogeneous and isotropic, constructed by adding up discrete parts. James’s warning about the "jungle of intricacy" serves as a deterrent to this reductionism. The trap is believing that once you have discriminated the parts, the whole is understood. In reality, the "relations" are primitive and irreducible. They are felt, not calculated. Modern cognitive science often falls into this trap by trying to simulate spatial cognition using pure Euclidean geometry engines, ignoring the messy, affective, and non-metric nature of lived space described by James. The "gray zone" is the boundary between the metric space of physics and the topological, qualitative space of phenomenology—a boundary James deliberately refused to cross by insisting on the "inexplicable" nature of the whole.
