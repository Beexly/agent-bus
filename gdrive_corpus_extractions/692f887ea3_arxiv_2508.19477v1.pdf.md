Audit trace, methodological rather than private cognition: the source tests concurrent validity of commercial computer-vision and AI player-tracking providers against TRACAB Gen 5 across three camera feeds, two resolutions, and detection state. The causal chain is provider model -> feed geometry -> resolution -> detection probability -> imputation/extrapolation -> position, speed, and aggregate-distance error. The central multi-hop derivation is the detection-mixture identity. If \(e_{X,k}\) is the signed error for position or speed, \(\mathrm{RMSE}_X^2=\frac1N\sum e_{X,k}^2\), and if frames partition into detected and undetected states with probabilities \(p_d\) and \(1-p_d\), then \(\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{all}}^2=p_d\mathrm{RMSE}_d^2+(1-p_d)\mathrm{RMSE}_u^2\) under the source’s raw-error definition. This is not a toy ratio; it follows from the definition of squared error and the partition. Using Table 1 and Table 2 [PAGE 22] [PAGE 23], Provider 1 at 1080p tactical has \(p_d=2{,}819{,}394/2{,}976{,}033=0.9474\), \(\mathrm{RMSE}_d=1.68\) m, \(\mathrm{RMSE}_u=12.23\) m, so predicted overall RMSE is \(\sqrt{0.9474(1.68^2)+0.0526(12.23^2)}=3.19\) m, close to the reported 3.25 m. Provider 2 at 1080p tactical has \(p_d=0.904\), \(\mathrm{RMSE}_d=0.44\) m, \(\mathrm{RMSE}_u=6.19\) m, giving \(\sqrt{0.904(0.44^2)+0.096(6.19^2)}=2.00\) m, close to 1.96 m. Provider 2 at 720p programme has \(p_d=0.364\), \(\mathrm{RMSE}_d=1.13\) m, \(\mathrm{RMSE}_u=11.56\) m, giving 9.27 m, close to 9.24 m. This proves that the headline “overall” position errors are largely a detection-mix phenomenon, not merely a provider-average defect. The paper states: “Player detection is key in maximising accuracy” [PAGE 14], and the tactical feed yields “89 to 96% detection” versus “36 to 64% detected” on other feeds [PAGE 15]. 

Gray-zone auditing: first, the source’s RMSE is absolute error, while bias and limits of agreement are separately modeled; a provider can have small bias but large RMSE, or suitable RMSE in detected frames but catastrophic undetected extrapolation. Second, the methods describe temporal phase shifting and spatial rotation/shift to minimize error before comparison [PAGE 6]; this is a legitimate alignment step, but it can absorb latency and calibration offsets, so it is not a pure independent validation of raw timing. Third, the aggregate-distance sentence is formally suspect: “Total distance was calculated for each player by multiplying their change in speed by change in time” [PAGE 6]. Dimensionally this gives metres, but physically distance must be the time integral of speed, \(D_i=\int v_i(t)dt\), or a sum \(D_i\approx\sum_k v_i(t_k)\Delta t_k\). If the prose is literal, aggregate-distance bias is not interpretable as speed-integration error; if the prose is imprecise, the paper should state the actual quadrature. Fourth, Table 2 shows Provider 3 at 720p tactical with detected RMSE 16.24 m, undetected 19.38 m, and overall 16.39 m [PAGE 23], meaning detection is necessary but not sufficient; the provider algorithm can fail even when detection is high. Fifth, video resolution is statistically significant but the authors conclude “there does not appear to be any practically meaningful changes between video resolution configurations” [PAGE 15]. Therefore procurement claims that 1080p alone fixes validity are not supported. Finally, the study is a single match in “prime conditions” [PAGE 16], so cross-stadium, weather, jersey-colour, and camera-operator generalization remains a gray zone.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal error operators. Let \(i\) index player, \(t\) index frame, \(P\) provider, \(F\in\{\mathrm{PGM},\mathrm{Cam1},\mathrm{Tactical}\}\), \(R\in\{720p,1080p\}\), and detection state \(S\in\{d,u\}\). Position error and speed error are
\[
e_{\mathrm{pos},i,t}^{(P,F,R,S)}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i,t}^{(P,F,R,S)}-\mathbf{x}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{TRACAB}},
\qquad [e_{\mathrm{pos}}]=\mathrm{m},
\]
\[
e_{\mathrm{spd},i,t}^{(P,F,R,S)}=\hat{v}_{i,t}^{(P,F,R,S)}-v_{i,t}^{\mathrm{TRACAB}},
\qquad [e_{\mathrm{spd}}]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
\]
The source defines root mean square error as
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_X=\sqrt{\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N e_{X,k}^2},
\qquad [\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{pos}}]=\mathrm{m},\quad [\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{spd}}]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\]
where \(N\) is “the number of observations in the raw data” [PAGE 7]. Mean bias and 95% limits of agreement are
\[
b_X=\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N e_{X,k},
\qquad \mathrm{LOA}_{95}=b_X\pm1.96\,s_{e_X},
\]
with units matching \(X\); the source states that “mean bias and the limits of agreement (LOA) were estimated from linear mixed effects (position and speed) and linear models (total distance)” [PAGE 7]. The mixed model is of the form
\[
e_{i,t}=\beta_0+\beta_1 X_{i,t}^{\mathrm{TRACAB}}+u_i+\varepsilon_{i,t},
\qquad u_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_u^2),\quad \varepsilon_{i,t}\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2),
\]
where \(u_i\) is the player random intercept; for distance the source uses linear models, then models RMSE and mean bias with provider, feed, and resolution in a three-way interaction and player ID as random intercept [PAGE 7]. The model does not repair missing detection; it estimates conditional mean error, so a low marginal bias can coexist with high conditional undetected error.

Detection operator. Define
\[
p_d^{(P,F,R)}=\frac{n_d}{n_d+n_u}\in[0,1],
\]
dimensionless. Table 1 gives detected counts such as Provider 1 tactical 1080p at 2,819,394 detected and 156,639 undetected [PAGE 22], while Table 2 gives detected and undetected RMSE [PAGE 23]. Under the source’s RMSE definition, the exact partition identity is
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{all}}^2=p_d\mathrm{RMSE}_d^2+(1-p_d)\mathrm{RMSE}_u^2.
\]
This identity has units \(\mathrm{m^2}\) for position and \(\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\) for speed. It breaks down as a causal summary if detection state is misclassified, if the provider’s “detected” metadata are not independent of the error model, or if the reported RMSEs are computed on different observation weights. The most important singularity is \(p_d\to0\): then \(\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{all}}\to\mathrm{RMSE}_u\), and the estimate becomes an extrapolation model, not an observation model. Conversely, \(p_d\to1\) does not guarantee validity if \(\mathrm{RMSE}_d\) is large, as seen in Provider 3’s 720p tactical detected RMSE of 16.24 m [PAGE 23].

Aggregate-distance operator. The correct formal operator is
\[
D_i=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}v_i(t)\,dt\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_i(t_k)\Delta t_k,
\qquad [D_i]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
The manuscript’s prose says: “Total distance was calculated for each player by multiplying their change in speed by change in time” [PAGE 6]. That sentence is dimensionally metres but is not the physical distance operator unless “change in speed” is a misprint for “speed.” This is a formal breakdown regime: if the implementation used \(\Delta v\,\Delta t\), then aggregate-distance bias cannot be interpreted as integration error; if it used \(v\Delta t\), then the prose is wrong. The source reports TRACAB average distance as “7997 ± 3297 m” [PAGE 10] and provider bias ranges from “-1745 m (−21.8%) to 1945 m (24.3%)” [PAGE 2]. Percentage error is
\[
\mathrm{PE}_i=100\frac{\hat D_i-D_i^{\mathrm{TRACAB}}}{D_i^{\mathrm{TRACAB}}}\,\%,
\]
dimensionless percent.

Alignment, filtering, and calibration operators. Temporal alignment minimizes RMSE over phase shift \(\tau\):
\[
\tau^*=\arg\min_\tau \mathrm{RMSE}\{\hat v_i(t),v_i^{\mathrm{TRACAB}}(t+\tau)\},
\qquad [\tau]=\mathrm{s}.
\]
Spatial alignment searches rotation \(\theta\) and translation \(\mathbf b\):
\[
(\theta^*,\mathbf b^*)=\arg\min_{\theta,\mathbf b}\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N\|\mathbf R(\theta)\hat{\mathbf x}_k+\mathbf b-\mathbf x_k\|^2,
\qquad [\theta]=^\circ,\quad [\mathbf b]=\mathrm{m},
\]
with the source using 0.1-degree increments [PAGE 6]. Speed is “filtered using a 4th order 1 Hz low-pass Butterworth filter” [PAGE 6]. The Butterworth magnitude is
\[
|H(f)|^2=\frac{1}{1+(f/f_c)^{2n}},
\qquad f_c=1\,\mathrm{Hz},\quad n=4,
\]
dimensionless gain; breakdown occurs when the biomechanical question depends on acceleration or deceleration content above approximately 1 Hz, because the filter attenuates it. Calibration breakdown occurs when homography is ill-conditioned, when the pitch model is wrong, or when the camera geometry changes; the paper notes that “the playing area must first be calibrated” and that homography techniques are common [PAGE 13]. Positional suitability is stated as “an accuracy of ~ 1m was considered suitable,” and speed suitability as “RMSE ~ 10% (~ 1 m·s-1) of the maximum speed deemed suitable” [PAGE 7]. Against those thresholds, the abstract range “Position RMSE ranged from 1.68 to 16.39 m, while speed RMSE ranged from 0.34 to 2.38 m·s⁻¹” [PAGE 2] means only certain provider-feed-state combinations pass, not the method as a whole. The best reported position result is Provider 2 at 720p tactical, “RMSE = 1.68 m; mean bias [LOA] = 0.68 m [-2.35 to 3.71 m]” [PAGE 8]; best speed is Provider 2 at 1080p tactical, “RMSE = 0.34 m.s-1; mean bias [LOA] = 0.02 m.s-1 [-0.63 to 0.67 m.s-1]” [PAGE 9]. The reference system itself is not errorless: TRACAB has “RMSE = 0.08 m” and “RMSE = 0.08 m.s-1” against 3D motion capture [PAGE 6], so all comparisons are relative to a reference with its own small error.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is not a single 2026 innovation; it is a retrocausal chain from notational analysis to optical tracking to validation science. The source traces optical tracking from notational analysis [5, 6] to semi-automated vision-based systems [7, 8] to automated computer-image-processing systems [9-11], noting that earlier systems “all require human intervention” and fixed camera infrastructure [PAGE 3]. References on [PAGE 19] include Jaques and Pavia’s 1974 Australian-rules movement analysis, Nettleton and Sandstrom’s 1963 skill and conditioning work, Barris and Button’s 2008 review, Duthie et al.’s 2003 video time-motion reliability study, Figueroa et al.’s 2006 soccer-player kinematic tracking, and Iwase and Saito’s 2003 and 2004 homography and multi-view tracking work. The modern validation anchor is Linke et al. 2020 on TRACAB’s football-specific validity [PAGE 19], which is why this study can use TRACAB as the high-definition reference. Broadcast-specific computer vision then enters through Hurault et al. 2020, Scott et al. 2022 SoccerTrack with fish-eye and drone video, Stein et al. 2017, Thinh et al. 2019, and earlier work by Xu et al. 2004, Mazzeo et al. 2008, and Naik and Hashmi 2021 [PAGE 19-20]. FIFA’s 2021 “Handbook of test methods assessment of broadcast tracking accuracy” is the standards ancestor [PAGE 20], and Crang et al. 2024 on GNSS sprinting validity is the comparison branch [PAGE 20]. Thus the paper is a convergence node: broadcast video, deep-learning detection, multi-object tracking, homography calibration, and mixed-effects agreement analysis.

Multi-week traps. Week 1 trap: vendor demonstration on a tactical feed with high detection is not evidence of programme-feed validity. Week 2 trap: best-case detected RMSE is not overall RMSE; Provider 2’s detected tactical RMSE of 0.44 m [PAGE 23] coexists with undetected RMSE of 6.19 m for 1080p tactical. Week 3 trap: resolution fetishism. The source says “there does not appear to be any practically meaningful changes between video resolution configurations” [PAGE 15], so buying 1080p without fixing detection is a false fix. Week 4 trap: aggregate-distance drift. Small same-direction speed errors accumulate; the paper explicitly notes that “even small errors in same direction capable of accumulating and magnifying the discrepancy in total distance” [PAGE 16]. Week 5 trap: prime-condition overgeneralization. The authors warn that “stadium design, camera angles and jersey colour may vary at other stadiums which could influence the validity of the software” [PAGE 16]. Week 6 trap: provider opacity. The exact calibration, filter, machine-learning model, and extrapolation method are undisclosed [PAGE 13], so cross-provider ranking is unstable across feeds and resolutions. The correct genealogy is therefore conditional: the method descends from validated optical tracking, but broadcast AI inherits new failure modes—occlusion, detection metadata, extrapolation, and calibration—that the older fixed-camera lineage did not have.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three epistemic postures govern deployment. First, crisis habit regression: under match-week pressure, practitioners revert to the programme feed because it is the televised product, visually familiar, and already available. That habit is exactly the feed with the lowest detection in Table 1: Provider 1 720p programme has 42% detected and 58% undetected [PAGE 22], and Provider 2 720p programme has 36% detected and 64% undetected [PAGE 22]. The paper’s own warning is that undetected frames drive error: “Player detection is key in maximising accuracy” [PAGE 14]. A practitioner who ignores the detected/undetected flag is not using the same measurement model that produced the validation statistics. The source distinguishes “detected” from “visible”: “detected’ or ‘undetected’ refers to whether the player was identified by the computer-vision software, while ‘visible’ indicates that the player was within the camera’s field of view” [PAGE 6]. That distinction is the counter to habit regression: a player can be visible but undetected, and the software may impute position and speed by AI. 

Second, the counter-trap: a provider or analyst cites the best-case cell, such as Provider 2’s 720p tactical position RMSE of 1.68 m [PAGE 8] or 1080p tactical speed RMSE of 0.34 m·s⁻¹ [PAGE 9], and treats it as the product’s validity. The recursive theory-of-mind counter is to ask what the provider knows that the consumer does not: exact model, filter, calibration, extrapolation, and detection threshold [PAGE 13]. The correct consumer response is not to average over providers but to condition on feed, resolution, detection state, and player. 

Third, the clock: temporal phase shift and spatial alignment are audited as measurement operators, not as nuisance parameters. The source aligns by “phase shifting the players’ speeds within the TRACAB data to establish the smallest RMSE” and rotates through 360 degrees in 0.1-degree increments [PAGE 6]. That clock can make speed agreement look better if latency is absorbed; it can also hide systematic lag. The counter-trap is to report phase shift and residual timing error alongside RMSE. The deployment rule is: if the use case is spatial analysis, require detected frames and tactical feed; if the use case is speed, allow broader feeds but annotate filter and phase shift; if the use case is aggregate distance, do not infer validity from pointwise RMSE because accumulation direction matters. The paper’s recommendation is consistent: use a tactical feed and either 720p or 1080p, because the tactical feed maximises visibility and detection [PAGE 15-17].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The tactical feed is defined as “a wide-view angle of the pitch, captured from a fixed camera positioned on the top tier of the grandstand, at the half-way line,” with the purpose “to maintain 20 outfield players in shot” [PAGE 4]. If a venue’s tactical camera is lower, narrower, or occluded, the physical precondition for the reported 89 to 96% detection fails. The source explicitly warns that “a stadium with a tactical camera that is at a lower elevation (e.g. narrower field of view) may introduce more undetected data points” [PAGE 16]. Physical veto proof: by the detection-mixture identity, a fall in \(p_d\) increases overall MSE even if detected and undetected conditional errors are unchanged. Quantitatively, Provider 2’s programme 720p case has \(p_d=0.36\), giving predicted overall position RMSE 9.27 m, whereas the tactical 1080p case has \(p_d=0.90\), giving 2.00 m; the observed values are 9.24 m and 1.96 m respectively [PAGE 22-23]. Therefore any claim that a non-tactical or low-elevation camera is “equivalent” is vetoed unless it demonstrates equal \(p_d\) and equal undetected RMSE. Occlusion is a separate physical veto: “Occlusions can occur where players are gathered closely (e.g., set pieces), misidentification may result when players are similar in appearance” [PAGE 3]. Set-piece spatial analysis should be vetoed when detection metadata are absent or when occlusion is known.

Biomechanical veto. The source filters speed with a fourth-order 1 Hz low-pass Butterworth filter [PAGE 6]. That is appropriate for summary speed and distance, but it is not appropriate for high-frequency acceleration, deceleration, change-of-direction, or injury-risk biomechanics. The filter’s gain falls with frequency; if a practitioner uses the tracked speed to infer rapid braking or asymmetric loading, the measurement layer has already removed much of the relevant signal. Biomechanical veto proof: the validation target is speed RMSE against TRACAB, not acceleration or force; the reference itself is an optical position system, and the paper does not validate ground reaction force, joint load, or muscle activation. Therefore any inference from these data to tissue load or injury mechanism is outside the evidential chain. A second biomechanical veto is player-specific morphology and gait: similar body shape or boot colour can cause misidentification [PAGE 3], so player identity must be audited before individual load conclusions.

Weather and environment veto. Weather is not a measured variable in this study. The authors state: “A limitation of this study was that the data was collected from a single match, stadium and broadcaster in what could be considered prime conditions” [PAGE 16]. There is no rain, fog, snow, wind, shadow, or solar-glare experiment. Therefore an all-weather validity claim is vetoed by absence of evidence, not by a negative result. The physical reason is straightforward: weather can reduce contrast, alter shadows, blur the ball and player edges, and change detection probability; the mixture identity then raises overall error. The same logic applies to jersey colour and broadcast graphics: the programme feed includes “graphics and replays” [PAGE 4], while camera 1 has “no graphical overlay” [PAGE 5]. If overlays, replays, or cut scenes lower detection, the effective \(p_d\) is not the tactical-feed \(p_d\). The cross-layer veto rule is: no spatial claim without detection metadata; no speed claim without filter disclosure; no aggregate-distance claim without quadrature disclosure; no all-weather or all-stadium claim without a multi-venue detection study.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
