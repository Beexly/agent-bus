<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The document’s lineage is the migration from ad hoc event selling to structured lead-to-event operations. Historically, small food and event businesses handled inquiries through text messages, paper notes, memory, and a single owner’s calendar. The proposal’s workflow chain is a compact version of customer-relationship management, lead-response management, and event-operations practice: capture the inquiry, timestamp it, classify it, draft a response, produce a shareable quote, track opens, schedule follow-ups, convert to an event sheet, confirm near the event, notify on the day, and close the loop with a thank-you and review request [PAGE 2]. The scientific lineage is not exotic; it is queueing, information capture, human-in-the-loop control, and food-service event planning. The formal center is the dependency graph: the future event sheet depends backward on the quote record, and the post-event review depends backward on the event actually occurring and on a photo being captured.

Retrocausal genealogy appears in three places. First, the post-event “thank-you with a photo from their table and a one-tap Google review link” [PAGE 2] retro-determines that a photo must be captured at the event and associated with the host record. Second, “Venues and planners who send you weddings are tracked and thanked” [PAGE 2] retro-determines that the quote intake must capture source attribution, not just the guest’s name. Third, “one screen a month shows quotes, bookings and where they came from” [PAGE 2] retro-determines a monthly reporting schema with quote, booking, and source fields. If those fields are not captured at intake, the later report cannot be produced. This is the proposal’s hidden architecture: the advertised front-end experience is simple, but the back-end value depends on disciplined data capture.

Multi-week traps are explicit and implicit. The explicit clock is day 3 and day 7 for quote follow-up [PAGE 2], two days out for headcount and arrival confirmation, the day of for “on our way,” after the event for thank-you/review, each season for past-host outreach, and monthly for the reporting screen [PAGE 2]. The longer trap is the “ninety days of care after” [PAGE 1] against “No monthly fee” [PAGE 2]. A buyer can reasonably hear “yours for good” as indefinite support, while the seller has written a 90-day care window. Another multi-week trap is launch slippage: the source says “Two rounds of changes before launch” [PAGE 1], but it does not state whether the 90-day care window begins at payment, at launch, or at final acceptance. If launch slips by several weeks, the care window’s start date becomes contractually ambiguous. A third trap is scope creep in “both photography services” [PAGE 2]: the phrase is named but not separately specified, so the bundle’s $150 difference [PAGE 2] cannot be used to infer a photography price or a photography deliverable count. The genealogy therefore runs from simple quote to structured record to event execution to seasonal relationship management; the trap is assuming that the later stages are automatic when their inputs were never contractually specified.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has at least five minds: the owner(s), Tricia and Stacey; the guest; the fiancé or planner who receives the forwarded quote; the venue/host; and Garrett, the seller. The owner’s model is “I need to reply fast and sound like myself.” The guest’s model is “I sent a request; I expect a quote with my date, venue, and table.” The planner’s model is “I need a forwardable quote page I can open and share.” The venue’s model is “I need a written arrival time and headcount.” Garrett’s model is “The site gets them to ask; the workflows get them booked” [PAGE 2]. These models are nested: the owner’s speed affects the guest’s trust; the guest’s forward affects the planner’s ability to approve; the planner’s approval affects the venue’s scheduling; the venue’s confirmation affects the owner’s day-of execution.

Crisis habit regression is the failure mode in which the owner, under event-day pressure, reverts to manual texting, memory, and ad hoc notes. The source tries to counter this with a human-in-the-loop design: “Every message is written with you, in your voice. You read it, you tap send. Nothing goes out without you” [PAGE 2]. That is a counter-trap because it preserves voice and control, but it also creates a single-point human latency: if the owner is cooking, driving, or hands-full, \(T_{\text{human}}\) rises and the “in seconds” intake advantage is not matched by send speed. The counter-trap is not full automation; it is assisted automation with an explicit send gate. The clock is the third element: the system’s memory is externalized into day 3, day 7, two days out, day of, after event, each season, and monthly [PAGE 2]. A recursive owner should ask: “What will my future self need to know at 7:00 a.m. on event day?” The answer is the event sheet’s “Timeline, map, headcount, dietary notes, and a prep list” [PAGE 2]. A recursive guest should ask: “What will my fiancé or planner need to approve?” The answer is “Their date, table, your photos and the estimate, easy to forward to a fiancé or planner” [PAGE 2]. A recursive planner should ask: “What will the venue need in writing?” The answer is the two-day confirmation of “headcount and arrival time” [PAGE 2]. The theory-of-mind failure is assuming that because the owner saw the request, the guest saw a reply; because the reply was drafted, the quote was sent; because the quote was sent, the planner opened it; because the planner opened it, the venue was informed. The source provides open tracking only for the quote page: “You see when it’s opened” [PAGE 2]. It does not provide open tracking for the draft, the day-3 nudge, the day-7 check, or the venue confirmation. Those are the recursive blind spots.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The source’s event sheet includes “dietary notes, and a prep list scaled to the guest count from your own menu” [PAGE 2], but it does not state a food-safety time-temperature control plan. A prep list is a quantity plan, not a safety proof. Let \(T_{\text{food}}\) be food temperature and \(T_{\text{safe}}\) the applicable safety threshold; let \(t_{\text{exposure}}\) be time outside control and \(t_{\text{safe}}\) the applicable limit. The physical veto is
\[
T_{\text{food}}>T_{\text{safe}}\quad\text{or}\quad t_{\text{exposure}}>t_{\text{safe}}
\;\Rightarrow\;\text{workflow cannot authorize service}.
\]
The exact numerical thresholds are jurisdiction- and product-dependent and are not given in the source, so they must be supplied by the food-safety authority, not invented here. The adjudication is clear: the workflow may schedule and document, but it cannot override a food-safety veto.

Biomechanical veto. The human send gate is “You read it, you tap send. Nothing goes out without you” [PAGE 2]. This requires visual attention, decision time, and manual input. If the owner’s hands are occupied, if the owner is driving, or if the owner is in a low-connectivity kitchen, \(T_{\text{human}}\) can become unbounded. The biomechanical veto is human availability: no automated send is permitted by the stated design. A second biomechanical issue is data entry at the event: headcount and arrival time must be “confirmed in writing” two days out [PAGE 2]; if the confirmer is unavailable, the written confirmation cannot be produced.

Weather veto. The event sheet includes “Timeline, map, headcount” [PAGE 2], and the two-day step confirms “headcount and arrival time” [PAGE 2]. Severe weather can invalidate the map, delay arrival, close roads, or force venue changes. Let \(W\) be a weather-disruption indicator. If \(W=1\) and the venue or route is unsafe, the timeline and arrival confirmation are stale. The workflow can notify, but it cannot make the physical route safe. The veto is
\[
W=1\;\Rightarrow\;\text{reconfirm venue, route, and arrival before treating the event sheet as final}.
\]
Connectivity veto. “It lands on your phone in seconds” [PAGE 2] presupposes network, power, and device availability. If any of those fail, \(T_{\text{ingest}}\) is not bounded by seconds. The source gives no offline fallback. Tax/legal veto. “Texas sales tax is added where it applies” [PAGE 1] presupposes a taxability determination. If the event crosses state lines or the service classification is unclear, the charged amount is indeterminate until tax counsel or the state revenue authority resolves it. Venue/planner veto. The quote page is “easy to forward to a fiancé or planner” [PAGE 2], but forwarding depends on link accessibility and the planner’s ability to open it. If the link is broken or permissioned incorrectly, the planner’s approval path is vetoed. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: automation can prepare, remind, and document; it cannot veto food safety, human availability, severe weather, network failure, tax ambiguity, or venue access. Those layers retain final authority.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
