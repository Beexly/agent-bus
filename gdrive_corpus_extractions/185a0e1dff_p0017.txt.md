<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. Daly's 1921 text descends from the Walter Camp–Amos Alonzo Stagg era of American football, when the game transitioned from rugby-like scrummaging to a structured, down-and-distance territorial sport. The explicit debts to Colonel Graves, Colonel Thompson, and Major Pritchard, U.S. A. [PAGE 1] embed the book in the post-WWI expansion of military physical training into civilian education. Army officers brought drill methodology, command discipline, and calisthenic systems into football coaching; thus Daly's "principles" likely include not only X's and O's but also organizational hierarchy.

Multi-week traps. A retrospective analyst reading [PAGE 1] might treat Daly's end-of-season concentration—"one or two important contests ... toward which practically the entire effort of the team is directed"—as a timeless periodization template. Trap 1: applying a two-game peaking model to a modern multi-week playoff tournament. In 1921 the season ended in one or two bowls or rivalry games; today a champion may navigate four weeks of elimination games. A coach who tapers for only two contests risks under-preparation in weeks three and four. Trap 2: assuming rule and equipment invariance. The forward pass, legalized in 1906, was still restricted in 1921; helmets were leather, substitutions limited. Daly's principles therefore operate under a different constraint manifold than modern football. Trap 3: institutional scale confusion. Because [PAGE 1] claims principles apply "equally" from college to high school, a coach might port a college scheme unchanged into a preparatory school. The multi-week trap is the compounding of this error across a season: early-season success against inferior competition masks physiological mismatch that emerges in late-season, high-intensity games.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under end-of-season pressure, coaches exhibit regression to foundational authority. Daly explicitly locates success in "the coaches and captains, upon whose knowledge of the game depends largely the success of their teams" [PAGE 1]. In a crisis—tied game, final minutes—the head coach reverts to the principles he trusts most, often those codified early in his career or in canonical texts such as Daly's.

Counter-trap. If opponent film study reveals that Coach A habitually regresses to Daly-era principle football under pressure (e.g., conservative running, punt-on-fourth-down, military-style discipline), Coach B sets a counter-trap: disguise blitzes against predictable line play, or use tempo to prevent the captain-coach dyad from making substitutions. The counter-trap exploits the predictability of the regression.

Clock recursion. The 1921 game clock operated without a play clock and with different stoppage rules; Daly's text presumes this temporal substrate. Modern recursive theory of mind must therefore model clock manipulation as a strategic layer unknown to Daly. Level 0: Coach A runs clock per Daly principle. Level 1: Coach B anticipates this and uses no-huddle to burn clock differently. Level 2: Coach A anticipates the no-huddle and reverts to an even older principle set. The clock thus becomes a recursive battleground: each layer of assumption about the opponent's assumption about clock management changes the optimal decision. Because [PAGE 1] says nothing about clock mechanics, any Daly-based clock strategy is vetoed by the modern rule set unless explicitly translated.

Biomechanical layer veto. Adolescent skeletons possess open epiphyseal plates. The veto is absolute where injury risk exceeds acceptable threshold.

Weather layer veto. [PAGE 1] is silent on environmental conditions. If precipitation rate P_{\text{rain}} exceeds the grip threshold of a 1921-era leather ball, ball-handling principles derived from dry-conditions assumptions fail. Because the source provides no weather contingency, weather veto supersedes textual principle.

Adjudication hierarchy. When layers conflict, the veto order is: biological maturity (physical/biomechanical) > environmental (weather) > textual principle. Daly's scale-invariance axiom is therefore not a theorem but a defeasible default, overridden first by the physical inability of smaller, immature athletes to execute college-scale momentum exchanges.
