<THINK_TRACE>
The source asks a narrow but consequential question: across several head-impact datasets, which rotational kinematic factors most strongly predict the KTH finite-element model’s 95th-percentile maximum principal strain, MPS95, and does that ranking change by impact type. The multi-hop derivation begins with the feature construction. On [PAGE 4], the paper defines factors by derivative order, component, and power, and Table 1 states: “ω(t); ω′(t); ω′′(t)” and “n = 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, f = ω, α, j”. On [PAGE 6], the authors state: “Collectively, 84 features were extracted from the kinematics in different derivative orders (3 factors), component and the magnitude (4 factors), and powers (7 factors).” That is the 3 × 4 × 7 feature universe. The paper then standardizes features per dataset because derivative orders have different units and scales [PAGE 6]. The outcome is MPS95 from the KTH model; on [PAGE 5], “a total of 3262 impacts from the 7 datasets described above were simulated using the KTH head model.” The statistical bridge is OLS regression of MPS95 on standardized kinematic features, followed by four interpretation lenses: zero-order correlation, structure coefficients, commonality analysis, and dominance analysis [PAGE 6]. The interpretive logic is not “which raw beta is biggest,” because the authors explicitly avoid interpreting coefficient values under collinearity [PAGE 18]. Instead, zero-order correlation asks how each factor relates to MPS95 alone; structure coefficients ask how each factor relates to the full model’s fitted MPS95 [PAGE 8]; commonality analysis decomposes explained variance into unique and shared portions using all-possible-subsets regression [PAGE 8]; dominance analysis averages incremental R^2 when a factor is added across subset models at each level [PAGE 9].

The result is a layered ranking. On [PAGE 10], “on all datasets but dataset MMA and NASCAR, the first-order features (angular acceleration) were the most predictive features while the second-order features (angular jerk) were the least predictive features,” with a CF2 exception where structure coefficients did not distinguish angular acceleration from angular velocity, p = 0.60. Magnitude was generally most predictive except NHTSA, where zero-order correlation favored z-axis but structure coefficients did not, p = 0.17 [PAGE 10]. First-power features were generally most predictive except NHTSA, where square-root features led [PAGE 14]. Commonality analysis further shows that shared information often dominates unique information; for powers, “the common information of all factors had the highest predictive power, which indicates that changing the power of features does not provide new information in the regression” [PAGE 15].

The gray-zone audit is where the inference must be throttled. First, these are predictive regressions, not causal biomechanical laws. Second, the dependent variable is an FE-model output, not a clinical injury label; the paper itself says “this study is a data-driven statistical analysis and the findings of this study may not be biomechanically important deemed by experts” [PAGE 18]. Third, the KTH model is simplified; on [PAGE 18], “it is simplified when compared to the state-of-the-art FEHMs.” Fourth, no consensus exists on the best strain metric [PAGE 19]. Fifth, commonality and dominance analyses require samples larger than features; thus “the datasets NFL and NHTSA were excluded in these two analyses” [PAGE 9]. Sixth, strong collinearity and variance inflation make raw coefficients unstable [PAGE 18], and negative commonality coefficients indicate suppressor effects [PAGE 13]. Seventh, high derivative orders are limited because numerical differentiation lowers signal-to-noise ratio [PAGE 18]. Eighth, powers above 6 were not tested, and the authors admit “the features we analyzed are limited in the power and the derivative order” [PAGE 18]. Finally, cross-dataset transfer is not guaranteed: the paper warns of “interchangeably using BIC designed for different types of head impacts” [PAGE 17]. The correct audit conclusion is therefore conditional: acceleration, magnitude, and first power are robust relative predictors in many datasets, but impact duration, dataset composition, sample size, collinearity, and FE-model assumptions define the valid domain.

The source’s formal operators are regression, variance decomposition, and subset-model dominance. The feature operator can be written as \(F_{d,c,p} = \left(\max_t \left|D^d \omega_c(t)\right|\right)^p\), where \(d \in \{0,1,2\}\) indexes angular velocity, angular acceleration, and angular jerk; \(c \in \{x,y,z,\mathrm{mag}\}\) indexes anatomical components; and \(p \in \{0.5,1,2,3,4,5,6\}\) indexes power. This formalization follows Table 1 on [PAGE 4] and the statement on [PAGE 6] that “All features were based on the absolute values, and therefore, both negative peaks and positive peaks were considered.” Units are: \(\omega\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), \(\alpha\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-2}}\), \(j\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-3}}\); after powering and standardization, the regression predictors are dimensionless. MPS95 is a strain fraction, dimensionless \(\mathrm{m\,m^{-1}}\).

The OLS model is \(Y_i = \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k X_{ik} + \epsilon_i\), where \(Y_i\) is MPS95, \(X_{ik}\) are standardized kinematic features, \(\beta_k\) are regression parameters, and \(\epsilon_i\) is model error [PAGE 6, PAGE 8]. The paper’s two-predictor exposition is \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_m m + \beta_n n + \epsilon\) [PAGE 8]. Assumptions include linearity in parameters and stable subset-model estimability; the paper does not claim causal identification, and it explicitly avoids interpreting coefficient magnitudes under collinearity [PAGE 18].

Commonality analysis uses \(U_m = R^2_{m,n} - R^2_n\), \(U_n = R^2_{m,n} - R^2_m\), \(C_{m,n} = R^2_n + R^2_m - R^2_{m,n}\), and \(T_m = R^2_m = U_m + C_{m,n}\) [PAGE 8]. All quantities are dimensionless variance fractions in \([0,1]\) when the model is well behaved. Negative \(C\) is possible and is interpreted as suppressor effect: “Negative commonality coefficients indicate that there is a suppressor effects between some factors” [PAGE 13].

Dominance analysis uses the level-1 averaged increment \(\bar{\Delta}^{l1}R^2_{o1} = \frac{1}{\binom{3}{1}-1}\left[(R^2_{o0,o1}-R^2_{o0}) + (R^2_{o2,o1}-R^2_{o2})\right]\) [PAGE 9]. Here \(R^2\) values are dimensionless explained-variance fractions; “level” denotes the number of predictors in a subset model [PAGE 9]. The paper limits power dominance to 1, 0.5, 2, 3 because “the higher powers showed lower predictive power according to zero-order correlation coefficients and structure coefficients” [PAGE 9].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit. If the design matrix \(X^{T}X\) is singular or near-singular, OLS coefficients are non-identifiable or unstable; the paper warns of “strong collinearity among the features we selected” and a “variation inflation effect” [PAGE 18]. If \(N \le K\), commonality and dominance decompositions are not valid; the paper states that “the datasets NFL and NHTSA were excluded in these two analyses” [PAGE 9]. If commonality coefficients are negative, additive interpretation breaks down into suppressor logic [PAGE 13]. If derivative order is high, numerical differentiation noise dominates; the paper limits analysis to velocity, acceleration, and jerk [PAGE 5-6]. If power exceeds 6, the paper assumes lower predictive value rather than proving it [PAGE 6]. If datasets are pooled without interaction terms, the cross-impact-type transfer assumption fails; the paper explicitly finds dataset-specific exceptions and warns about interchangeable BIC use [PAGE 17].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from rotational mechanics of head injury to modern finite-element and statistical interpretation. Holbourn’s rotational mechanics is cited as the reason linear acceleration contributes less to brain strain [PAGE 4, PAGE 21]. Versace’s severity index, BrIC, RIC, PRHIC, BRIC, RVCI, BAM, and Gabler’s single-degree-of-freedom and second-order models form the BIC genealogy [PAGE 20-22]. The present paper retrocausally re-reads that lineage: if angular acceleration, magnitude, and first power are generally most predictive, then criteria built on those factors gain support, while criteria built on angular velocity may be context-limited to short-duration impacts. The paper notes that BrIC, BRIC, and RVCI “may be more effective in predicting MPS95 on the short-duration head impacts, such as those experienced in MMA” [PAGE 17]. The older single-degree-of-freedom result already anticipated this: “for short-duration pulses, maximum brain deformation was primarily dependent on angular velocity magnitude. While for long-duration pulses, the angular acceleration magnitude stood out” [PAGE 3].

The multi-week traps are methodological and temporal. Trap one is pooling all sports into one universal BIC without stratification; the paper finds angular velocity most predictive on MMA and NASCAR, acceleration elsewhere, and square-root power on NHTSA [PAGE 10, PAGE 14]. Trap two is using commonality or dominance on small datasets; NFL and NHTSA were excluded because samples must exceed features [PAGE 9]. Trap three is interpreting raw OLS coefficients under collinearity; the paper warns that correlated predictors cause unstable coefficients and variance inflation [PAGE 18]. Trap four is over-reading high powers or high derivatives; the authors restrict powers above 6 and derivative orders above jerk, admitting “the features we analyzed are limited in the power and the derivative order” [PAGE 18]. Trap five is treating MPS95 as clinical injury; the KTH model is simplified and no consensus strain metric exists [PAGE 18-19]. A multi-week study design must therefore pre-register dataset-specific interactions, sample sizes, derivative-order noise controls, and external validation across impact durations.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is about how modelers, clinicians, and regulators reason under crisis. “Crisis habit regression” is the cognitive fallback to a single familiar criterion—peak angular velocity, peak angular acceleration, or one BIC—because it is fast and legible. The counter-trap is the opposite failure: overfitting to a dataset-specific exception, such as the NHTSA square-root result or the MMA velocity result, and then universalizing it. The clock is impact duration and sampling scale. The paper’s evidence says the clock matters: angular velocity is more predictive for short-duration MMA and NASCAR impacts, while angular acceleration dominates most other datasets [PAGE 10, PAGE 15-17]. The paper states directly: “For short-duration impacts, angular velocity tends to be more predictive of brain strain [5,24]” [PAGE 17]. The recursive theory-of-mind correction is that each agent must model the other’s likely overgeneralization: the modeler must not present a relative R^2 ranking as a clinical threshold; the clinician must not treat MPS95 as injury; the regulator must not transfer a BIC across sports without revalidation. The CF2 non-significance between acceleration and velocity, p = 0.60, and the NHTSA component non-significance, p = 0.17, are reminders that the clock is not perfectly resolved [PAGE 10]. The safe counter-trap is to retain multiple factors, report uncertainty, and validate by impact type rather than replacing one habit with another.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer adjudication must veto claims that jump from kinematics to clinical injury without intermediate evidence. Physical veto: the analysis is rotational because “linear acceleration has been proved to contribute less to brain strain [31]” [PAGE 4]. Therefore a claim that linear acceleration is the dominant predictor is not supported by this source’s feature set. Biomechanical veto: the KTH model is simplified relative to state-of-the-art FEHMs [PAGE 18], lacks some anatomical detail, and MPS95 is a model output, not a diagnosed injury. The paper also states “no consensus has been reached yet about the best metric to describe the tissue strains and discriminate the injury severity” [PAGE 19]. Thus clinical severity claims are vetoed unless externally validated against injury outcomes. Statistical veto: raw coefficient signs and magnitudes cannot be adjudicated causally under strong collinearity and variance inflation [PAGE 18]; only the variance-decomposition and dominance results are interpretable within the paper’s stated scope. Weather veto: the source reports datasets HM, CF1, CF2, MMA, NFL, NHTSA, and NASCAR [PAGE 4-5] but no weather, temperature, humidity, or environmental covariates. Any weather-based performance or injury claim is therefore outside the evidence and must be vetoed. The valid cross-layer statement is narrow: in these seven impact datasets, using the KTH FE model and MPS95, angular acceleration, magnitude, and first-power features are generally strong relative predictors, with documented exceptions by impact type.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
