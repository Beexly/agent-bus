The danger is that s_HR may remain high while s_Er is depressed by accumulated I_R, producing the dissociation Hull emphasizes—reactive inhibition opposes s_Ee, not s_HR [PAGE 297]. The agent “knows” the habit but cannot execute the CR because inhibitory potential swamps reaction potential at the conclusion of the last reinforcement.

Counter-trap: The opposite error is assuming that greater spacing is always better, ignoring Hull’s explicit “within limits” boundary [PAGE 297]. A recursive theory-of-mind trap occurs when the learner models the trainer as deliberately spacing trials to reduce inhibition, and the trainer models the learner as capable of tolerating arbitrarily long Δt. If both parties recurse on “more space = more potential” without representing Δt_max, they drift into the upper-bound regime where trace decay or context shift negates the spacing advantage. The counter-trap is therefore to treat Δt as a bounded control variable, not a monotonic lever.

Clock: The inter-reinforcement interval Δt functions as an external temporal contingency that the learner must represent recursively: “If the light appears, I expect reinforcement after Δt; if it arrives sooner, prediction error and reactive inhibition spike; if it arrives later, the prior trace may disintegrate.” Because Hull’s formulation ties ΔI_R to every response R whether reinforced or not [PAGE 297], the learner’s model of the clock includes nonreinforced responses as inhibition-generating events. A second-order theory-of-mind read emerges: the learner infers the trainer’s reinforcement rate ρ, and the trainer infers the learner’s invisible I_R(t) through response probability. Crisis moments collapse this recursion into a zero-delay model (ρ → ∞, Δt → 0), which the equations on [PAGE 297] identify as the saturation regime for I_R.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto adjudication proceeds hierarchically across physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers before the potential equation on [PAGE 297] is evaluated.

Physical layer: The CR is eyelid closure to a light, originally evoked by a shock below the eye [PAGE 297]. If the orbicularis oculi is in its neuromuscular refractory or twitch-relaxation period, or if repeated periorbital shock produces local tissue changes that preclude closure, then CR probability is zero regardless of s_Er. This is a hard physical veto: no amount of effective excitatory potential can drive a biologically unavailable effector. The equation for s_Er is not evaluated when the motor output channel is refractory.

Biomechanical layer: Each response consumes energy E [J], and ΔI_R = g(E) with dg/dE > 0 [PAGE 297]. If shock intensity is reduced so that E ≈ 0 (no detectable lid closure), then g(E) → 0 and no reactive-inhibition increment is generated. This veto removes the differential accumulation mechanism between massed and spaced conditions for that parameter regime. High-E responses produce larger ΔI_R and therefore faster saturation under massing.

Environmental/weather layer: The conditioned stimulus is a light; its visibility depends on ambient illumination, contrast, and ocular exposure conditions. Ambient temperature may alter the biochemical rate of spontaneous disintegration (τ), although no Q₁₀ or thermal coefficient is given on [PAGE 297] and must not be invented. If the light stimulus is not detected because of glare, dimness, or environmental occlusion, the conditioning contingency fails before any inhibitory computation occurs.

Adjudication order: Physical veto > Biomechanical veto > Environmental veto > Equation evaluation. If any veto fires, s_Er is undefined for that trial and the Corollary XXII prediction is not applicable. This prevents applying a formal potential equation to trials in which the CR cannot be emitted, the response energy is null, or the CS is imperceptible.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards / verifiable-condition) veto scenarios with formal predictions:

Scenario 1 — Motor-refractory veto (short Δt).  
Setup: Reduce the inter-reinforcement interval below the eyelid’s neuromuscular recovery interval (e.g., Δt < 1 s), far shorter than the 3.33 s used in the 18-per-minute condition on [PAGE 297].  
Veto: Physical refractory period prevents CR emission.  
Prediction: CR probability at conclusion of the last reinforcement drops to zero for the massed condition, independent of reinforcement count; s_Er cannot be expressed because the effector is unavailable.  
Verification: Record EMG from orbicularis oculi; confirm zero CR incidence despite reinforcement delivery.

Scenario 2 — Upper-bound spacing veto (long Δt).  
Setup: Set Δt to 24 h between reinforcements, testing the “within limits” clause of Corollary XXII [PAGE 297].  
Veto: The conditioned-stimulus trace or context representation decays beyond the effective window.  
Prediction: s_Er at conclusion of the last reinforcement is lower than at an intermediate Δt (e.g., 20 s); the monotonic spacing benefit reverses or vanishes.  
Verification: Compare acquisition and delayed retention across Δt = 20 s, 1 h, and 24 h groups; expect non-monotonic performance with the 24-h group not outperforming the 20-s group.

Scenario 3 — Zero-energy veto (E → 0).  
Setup: Use a periorbital shock below perceptual/motor threshold so that E ≈ 0 and no lid closure occurs [PAGE 297].  
Veto: g(E) → 0, so ΔI_R = 0 on every trial; reactive inhibition does not accumulate.  
Prediction: No difference between 3/min and 18/min groups because the inhibition-based spacing mechanism is disabled; learning curves converge or both remain at floor.  
Verification: Confirm absence of CR by video/EMG; compare group curves; null spacing effect falsifies the reactive-inhibition account for E ≈ 0 while leaving other learning mechanisms unaddressed.
