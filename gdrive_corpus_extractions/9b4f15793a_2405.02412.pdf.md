Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit. The source models a player-gameweek regression: for player \(p\) at gameweek \(t\), the target \(y_{t+1}\) is upcoming FPL points, and the input is a historical window of tabular FPL features plus upcoming match difficulty. The paper’s headline claim is that a custom 1D CNN is the highest-performing model in its comparison set, with holdout MSE \(5.83\) averaged over positions versus \(6.71\) for LightGBM and \(6.73\) for Ridge [PAGE 4]. The first derivation step is to note that MSE optimization estimates a conditional mean, not a full scoring distribution. Because FPL points are low-scoring, integer-valued, zero-inflated, and heavy-tailed, the conditional mean is dominated by the modal low-score region. That is exactly why the authors report that “The CNN model makes reasonable predictions, but is unsurprisingly unable to forecast outliers.” [PAGE 7]. A second step is to audit the comparison set position by position. The CNN is better for GK, DEF, and FWD, but the MID result is \(6.16\) versus Ridge \(6.08\) and LightGBM \(6.11\) [PAGE 4]. Thus the average improvement is real within this experiment but not universal. A third step is to audit the “previous best” comparison: the paper’s conclusion cites a 30% improvement over an LSTM, but the footnote says “Same data source, but model evaluated on different seasons.” [PAGE 9]. That makes the claim directionally plausible but not a clean like-for-like benchmark.

The gray-zone audit centers on split difficulty. The authors state that “we were unable to completely standardize the difficulty of the performance prediction task between train/val/test splits.” [PAGE 9]. They also report that “Validation set gets lucky split and we overfit to the validation data via early stopping.” [PAGE 8]. This is a serious evaluation caveat: early stopping selects a model using a validation split whose task may be easier than the test split. The CNN did not use cross-validation in the final architecture search, unlike the baseline grid searches, because of compute limits [PAGE 7-8]. Therefore the CNN’s holdout MSE is a noisy estimate whose uncertainty is not reported. The baseline CV table shows train and validation MSEs that are close for Ridge and LightGBM [PAGE 6], but the CNN table is a holdout result. The gray zone is not that the CNN is fake; it is that the reported margin may be inflated or deflated by split luck, and the source itself says future work should use “a cross-validation grid search experiment framework and more advanced stratification strategies” [PAGE 10].

Feature audit yields another multi-hop conclusion. The CNN’s best features were “only the previous w weeks of FPL points and upcoming match difficulty d” [PAGE 6]. The baseline models, by contrast, found minutes, difficulty gap, influence/creativity/threat, and recent points important [PAGE 5]. This is not contradictory: the CNN may learn temporal form from raw point sequences, but it also omits starting-XI risk and role information. The source explicitly dropped “Benched players with no minutes played” [PAGE 2], because otherwise “predicting these players to score 0 points becomes nearly trivial” [PAGE 2]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s core forecasting operator is a position-conditioned map from a window and fixture difficulty to a real-valued FPL-point forecast. Let \(q\in\{\mathrm{GK},\mathrm{DEF},\mathrm{MID},\mathrm{FWD}\}\), let \(X_i^{(q)}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{w_q\times f_q}\) be the retained feature window, let \(d_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}\) be upcoming match difficulty, and let \(y_i(t+1)\in\mathbb{R}\) be the realized FPL points. The model is
\[
\hat y_i^{(q)}(t+1)=f_{\theta_q}\!\left(X_i^{(q)}(t),d_i(t)\right),
\]
with \(\hat y\) and \(y\) measured in FPL points. The training loss is
\[
L_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\left(y_i-\hat y_i\right)^2,
\]
in units of \(\mathrm{points}^2\). Under squared loss, the population-optimal predictor is the conditional mean
\[
y^\star(X,d)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[y\mid X,d\right],
\]
and the irreducible error is the conditional variance \(\sigma_\epsilon^2(X,d)\) in \(\mathrm{points}^2\). The source’s data pipeline uses player-wise splits, “player-by-player to avoid data leakage” [PAGE 2], with roughly 60% train, 25% validation, and 15% test [PAGE 2]. The paper reports per-position holdout MSEs of \(5.08,5.87,6.16,6.22\) for the CNN and an average of \(5.83\) [PAGE 4]. These are FPL-point-squared units; taking square roots gives RMSE in FPL points, but the paper reports MSE directly.

Ridge regression solves
\[
\hat\beta=\arg\min_{\beta}\;\|y-Z\beta\|_2^2+\alpha\|\beta\|_2^2,
\qquad
\hat\beta=(Z^\top Z+\alpha I)^{-1}Z^\top y,
\]
where \(Z\) is the standardized feature matrix. The source applies standard scaling \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\) to CNN and Ridge inputs but not to LightGBM [PAGE 3]. The exact breakdown regimes are: if \(\sigma=0\) for a feature, scaling is singular; if \(\alpha=0\) and \(Z^\top Z\) is rank-deficient, the closed-form inverse is singular; if \(\alpha\to\infty\), all coefficients collapse toward zero and the model becomes a constant predictor. LightGBM fits additive trees to negative gradients:
\[
\Theta_m=\arg\min_{\Theta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-g_{im}-T(x_i;\Theta)\right)^2,
\qquad
f_M(x)=\eta\sum_{m=1}^{M}T(x;\Theta_m),
\]
with output in FPL points, gradient \(g\) in FPL points, and learning rate \(\eta\) dimensionless. The source’s selected configuration used only 50 trees, depth 3, L2 strength 10, and 7 leaves with at least 70 observations per leaf [PAGE 4]. This is a heavily regularized regime chosen because the dataset is small; breakdown occurs when depth or tree count grows faster than the effective sample size, producing overfitting despite low training error.

The CNN’s source cost function is
\[
J_{\mathrm{CNN}}
=
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}\left(y^{(i)}-\hat y^{(i)}\right)^2
+\lambda_1\left(\|C\|_1+\|W^{[1]}\|_1\right)
+\lambda_2\left(\|C\|_2^2+\|W^{[1]}\|_2^2\right),
\]
as stated on [PAGE 4], where \(C\) is the convolution weight tensor and \(W^{[1]}\) is the dense hidden-layer weight matrix. The first term is in \(\mathrm{points}^2\); with dimensionless weights, \(\lambda_1\) has units of \(\mathrm{points}^2\) per weight and \(\lambda_2\) has units of \(\mathrm{points}^2\) per weight squared. The L1 term is nondifferentiable at zero and is usually handled by subgradients or proximal steps. Exact breakdown regimes are: \(\lambda_1=\lambda_2=0\) allows overfitting; \(\lambda_1\to\infty\) or \(\lambda_2\to\infty\) drives weights to zero and yields a near-constant predictor; a mismatch between the flattened convolution output and the dense input dimension is an architectural singularity; and an unbounded outlier target can dominate the squared loss because the error grows quadratically in FPL points.

The ranking metric is the generalized Spearman correlation on [PAGE 8]:
\[
\rho_s=
\frac{S_{y\hat y}}{S_yS_{\hat y}}
=
\frac{
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left(R(y^{(i)})-\overline{R(y)}\right)
\left(R(\hat y^{(i)})-\overline{R(\hat y)}\right)
}{
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(R(y^{(i)})-\overline{R(y)}\right)^2
}
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(R(\hat y^{(i)})-\overline{R(\hat y)}\right)^2
}
},
\]
which is dimensionless and lies in \([-1,1]\). The exact singularity is \(S_y=0\) or \(S_{\hat y}=0\), i.e. all true or predicted ranks tied; the source correctly notes that generalized Spearman is needed because integer FPL points create many ties [PAGE 8]. The reported CNN rank correlations are \(0.70,0.57,0.58,0.62\) by position [PAGE 9], indicating monotone ranking signal even where MSE is not best, as in MID.

The transfer model uses a Longformer with 4096 tokens and a sigmoid output scaled to \([-5,24]\) FPL points [PAGE 4]. Formally, if \(z_i\) is the output logit, then
\[
\hat y_i = -5 + 29\,\sigma(z_i),
\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
\]
with \(\hat y_i\) in FPL points and \(\sigma(z)\in[0,1]\). The assumption is that the news corpus contains positive mutual information \(I(y;N\mid X,d)>0\), measured in nats if natural logarithms are used. If that mutual information is zero, the transfer branch can only add estimation variance and cannot reduce expected MSE below the tabular model. The sigmoid has gradient \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\), which vanishes for large \(|z|\); this is an exact saturation breakdown. The source also notes that standard attention scales quadratically, while Longformer uses sliding-window attention in linear time [PAGE 4], so the text encoder’s compute is linear in sequence length only under that architectural restriction. The transfer holdout average of \(9.10\) [PAGE 4] is consistent with a weak or absent text signal under these constraints.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs backward from the current CNN to several older modeling traditions. The first is match-outcome modeling with Poisson processes, Bayesian networks, and graph models in the mid-2010s [PAGE 2]. Those models treated team-level match results as the target; the present work moves down one granularity level to player-gameweek points. The second lineage is time-series sequence modeling: LSTMs were proposed for player performance because they model sequential data, while 1D CNNs were argued to be more flexible through learned filters [PAGE 2]. The present CNN is therefore a retrocausal descendant of both the older Poisson/Bayesian match models and the neural sequence models, but it changes the target and the split unit. The third lineage is text-based forecasting: Twitter text and sentiment had been used for soccer prediction, but the source claims no prior news-corpus evaluation for player performance [PAGE 2]. The Longformer transfer branch is the descendant of that text lineage, adapted to long documents with linear-time sliding-window attention [PAGE 4].

The multi-week traps are visible in the data design. Window length is position-specific: the optimal CNN used 6 weeks for GK, 9 for DEF, 3 for MID, and 9 for FWD [PAGE 6]. That is not a neutral hyperparameter. A long window can average away a player’s current role change; a short window can overreact to one outlier. The source’s own qualitative filter analysis finds that average normalized filter weights are “quite close to 0” and that “there is no consistent pattern in longitudinal feature importance” [PAGE 13]. This is a warning against reading too much into multi-week temporal structure. A second trap is the dropped zero-minute rows: “Benched players with no minutes played were dropped” [PAGE 2]. That is defensible for training a performance model, but it changes the deployment distribution; at prediction time, the system must know whether a player will appear. A third trap is the handling of rare events. The source notes that prior-week goals for forwards can have a negative coefficient, possibly because scoring is rare and is followed by low-scoring weeks [PAGE 5]. That is a multi-week mean-reversion trap: a hot streak is not a stable state, and a model that treats it as one will overpay for recent goals. A fourth trap is news recency. The transfer dataset used the three most recent articles per player and the first 512 words of each article [PAGE 3], but many articles were outdated or unrelated to the upcoming match [PAGE 8]. The genealogy therefore warns that adding a new data layer does not automatically add signal; the layer must be temporally aligned with the gameweek and structured enough to identify the player’s likely role.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper’s model is not explicitly recursive: it maps observed FPL statistics and fixture difficulty to points. But soccer is a multi-agent system in which player behavior depends on a manager’s instructions, an opponent’s countermeasures, and the player’s own beliefs about both. A recursive theory-of-mind layer would represent, at minimum, the player’s belief about the manager’s selection policy, the manager’s belief about the opponent’s shape, and the opponent’s belief about the player’s habitual action. In probability notation, one can write a policy \(A_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid B_t^{(k)})\), where \(B_t^{(k)}\) is a level-\(k\) belief state and \(A_t\) is an action. The point is not to invent a new ratio; it is to note that the current feature set is a projection of behavior, not a representation of beliefs.

The first failure mode is crisis habit regression. Under a deficit, a red card, or a late-game press, players and teams often regress to habitual patterns: longer passes, more crosses, more individual shots, or deeper defensive blocks. The current CNN sees only lagged FPL points and difficulty. It does not see score state, match minute, man-advantage, or tactical instruction. Therefore, if crisis changes the mapping from lagged points to next-week points, the CNN’s residuals should be state-dependent. The verifiable prediction is that a crisis indicator \(C_t\) built from score differential and match minute will interact with lagged performance in a held-out set. The second failure mode is the counter-trap: an opponent anticipates a predictable habit and adjusts. A forward who has scored in three straight weeks may be marked more tightly; a midfielder who always cuts inside may be shown outside. This creates nonstationarity in feature sign. The paper’s linear coefficient heatmap already shows that the sign of prior goals varies by position, especially for forwards [PAGE 5]. A counter-trap model would explain that as an equilibrium effect, not as a fixed coefficient. The third failure mode is the clock. There are two clocks: the within-match clock and the FPL deadline clock. The within-match clock determines minutes, fatigue, and the chance of bonus points; the deadline clock determines when news is available and when a decision must be made. The source uses weekly windows and upcoming difficulty, but its best CNN excluded minutes [PAGE 6], while the baseline models found minutes important [PAGE 5]. That is a clock mismatch: the CNN may rank players well on average but miss the appearance and minutes risk that a deadline decision requires.

The recursive-theory-of-mind prescription is therefore not to add a generic “psychology” feature. It is to add structured, verifiable state: starting-XI probability, expected minutes, score state, red-card state, opponent pressing intensity, and managerial tendency. News text alone did not supply this signal in the source: the transfer branch underperformed the tabular models [PAGE 4], and the authors note that articles were not evaluated for quality in detail [PAGE 8]. A text encoder could still help if it extracted structured entities such as “ruled out,” “expected to start,” or “playing through injury,” but that is a named-entity and relation-extraction problem, not a generic long-document regression problem. The counter-trap conclusion is that any feature that becomes public and predictable can be neutralized by opponents; the clock conclusion is that the model must be evaluated at the decision deadline, not only on retrospective gameweek windows.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto asks whether a physical, biomechanical, or weather state is sufficient to invalidate a statistical forecast even when the tabular model is confident. The source does not include such layers: its features are FPL tabular data plus engineered match difficulty [PAGE 2], and the transfer branch adds news text [PAGE 3], not physical load or weather. The veto proof is therefore conditional and support-based. Let \(A_i\in\{0,1\}\) be an appearance/availability indicator, let \(M_i\) be a biomechanical load vector with components such as distance covered in km, high-speed running in m, accelerations in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and sprint count, and let \(W_i\) be a weather vector with components such as ambient temperature in \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\), wind speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), precipitation in \(\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\), and pitch water in mm. The CNN forecast \(\hat y_i\) is admissible only if the realized state \(V_i=(A_i,M_i,W_i)\) lies in the training support \(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{train}}\). If \(A_i=0\), then the player does not appear and the realized FPL points from that appearance are zero; hence \(\mathbb{E}[y\mid A=0]=0\). Any nonzero forecast on that subset has positive squared error. Because the source dropped zero-minute players [PAGE 2], the model has no training rows with \(A=0\). That is a formal support mismatch, not a matter of tuning.

The weather veto is similar but more nuanced. A postponed or unplayed fixture is not represented by the difficulty scalar \(d_i\). If the match is not played, the points-generating process is absent, so the forecast must be replaced by an event-conditioned expectation or a zero outcome. If the match is played in severe wind or rain, the distribution of passes, shots, and clean-sheet outcomes shifts; the CNN has no weather feature to condition on. The proof is that \(d_i\) is a scalar summary of opponent strength, not a sufficient statistic for weather. A biomechanical veto applies when a player is injured, returning from injury, or carrying a load that changes expected minutes. The source’s best CNN used only points and difficulty [PAGE 6], so it cannot distinguish a fully fit 90-minute player from a player likely to be substituted after 55 minutes. The veto does not need an invented threshold; the threshold must be estimated from verified event data. What can be proved is the direction: when the event changes the support of \(y\), the unmodified model’s forecast is not identified by its training distribution, and a hard or soft veto is required for calibration.

A practical adjudication rule is to separate hard vetoes from soft recalibrations. Hard vetoes are events with deterministic or near-deterministic point consequences, such as no appearance, a postponed fixture, or a pre-match withdrawal. Soft vetoes are events that shift the conditional mean, such as a return from injury, congested fixtures, extreme heat, or a waterlogged pitch. The hard veto is a set-valued correction: if the event is verified, replace the model output with the event-conditioned outcome. The soft veto is a recalibration: estimate \(\mathbb{E}[y\mid X,d,V]\) on a subset with the cross-layer variable present, or use importance weighting. The source’s own limitation section supports this caution: it says the prediction task difficulty was not fully standardized across splits [PAGE 9], which means the model’s error is already sensitive to distribution shift. Adding physical and weather state would not be cosmetic; it would test whether the CNN’s apparent advantage survives when the support of the target changes.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a policy or post-hoc rule is rewarded only when an externally checkable event label and outcome agree. The three counterfactual vetoes below are verifiable from match sheets, fixture status, and event logs; each yields a falsifiable prediction about the source model.

Scenario 1: zero-minute appearance veto. Event \(A_i=0\) means the player recorded zero minutes. The verifier checks official minutes. The source dropped these rows: “Benched players with no minutes played were dropped.” [PAGE 2]. Prediction: on a held-out set of zero-minute rows, the unmodified CNN will usually output a positive FPL-point forecast because it never saw this regime; a veto that sets \(\hat y_i=0\) reduces row MSE from \(\hat y_i^2\) to \(0\). The overall MSE reduction is exactly
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{MSE}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i:A_i=0}\hat y_i^2,
\]
in \(\mathrm{points}^2\). This is verifiable by comparing the same rows before and after the veto. If the CNN already predicts near zero on those rows, the predicted reduction fails.

Scenario 2: unplayed-fixture veto. Event \(P_i=0\) means the fixture was not played or the player’s team had no fixture in that gameweek. The verifier checks official fixture status. Prediction: the difficulty scalar \(d_i\) cannot encode absence of a match, so the CNN will issue a nonzero forecast; a veto to the event-conditioned no-play outcome reduces error on that subset. The same \(\Delta_{\mathrm{MSE}}\) identity applies with \(A_i\) replaced by \(P_i\). A stronger prediction is that this veto has a small effect on overall MSE because unplayed fixtures are rare, but a large effect on the affected rows; that separates row-level calibration from aggregate MSE.

Scenario 3: in-match sent-off or early-injury veto. Event \(R_i=1\) means a red card or injury forced an early exit before the player could accumulate normal minutes. The verifier checks the event log and minutes. Prediction: the source model, trained on weekly aggregates without in-match state, will overpredict the conditional mean for these rows because it cannot represent the lost minutes and lost bonus opportunities. A hard zero is not always correct, since the player may have accumulated points before the event; the correct counterfactual is a soft veto \(\hat y_i^{\mathrm{veto}}=\mathbb{E}[y_i\mid X_i,d_i,R_i=1]\), estimated from verified event rows. The verifiable prediction is that this event-conditioned recalibration lowers MSE on the \(R_i=1\) subset relative to the unmodified CNN, while leaving the non-event subset unchanged. If the recalibrated model does not lower subset MSE, the recursive-theory-of-mind claim that in-match state matters is falsified for this dataset.
