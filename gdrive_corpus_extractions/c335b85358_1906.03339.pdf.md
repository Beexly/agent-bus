<THINK_TRACE>
The source describes a multi-hop pipeline that converts a rendered football image into a spatial probability model and finally into a scalar passer/defense evaluation. The first hop is geometric: an image from Next Gen Stats is not an orthographic top-down field but a trapezoidal rendering, so the authors estimate a planar homography to recover field coordinates [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7]. This matters because every downstream density, completion surface, and CPAE integral is defined over physical field coordinates, not raw pixel indices. If the homography is wrong, the entire coordinate layer is warped, and subsequent smoothing will transfer image distortion into supposed football reality. The second hop is photometric segmentation: pass endpoints are encoded by color, so HSV thresholding is used to isolate candidate pixels [PAGE 9]. This creates a binary mask whose connected components are not yet passes, because noise, overlapping marks, and same-colored field elements contaminate the mask. The third hop is clustering: K-means++ separates candidate marks into individual pass endpoints, while DBSCAN removes noise for touchdown images where the line of scrimmage and trajectories share similar colors [PAGE 10] [PAGE 13]. The fourth hop is statistical smoothing: kernel density estimation estimates where passes are targeted, and a generalized additive model estimates completion probability by field location [PAGE 19] [PAGE 20]. The fifth hop is hierarchical correction: individual quarterbacks and defenses are sparse, so a two-dimensional naive Bayes expression shrinks group-specific surfaces toward league-level surfaces [PAGE 22]. The final hop is scalar summarization: CPAE integrates the above-league completion surface against the group’s own target-location density [PAGE 30].

A key gray-zone audit is that the pipeline is not merely reconstructing “passes”; it is reconstructing a visible subset of an unobserved event process. The paper itself says the analysis is “strictly retrospective” and that “QBs tend to target open receivers, defenses tend to cover high-leverage areas of the field more closely, and target locations depend greatly on the game situation” [PAGE 19]. Those game-situation variables are absent from the extracted image dataset. Therefore, a high CPAE can reflect target selection, receiver quality, defensive coverage, game state, or genuine quarterback skill; the image-derived model cannot fully separate these latent factors. This is especially important because CPAE is a density-weighted integral: if a quarterback’s target distribution is concentrated in areas where league completion probability is already high, his CPAE can be inflated without any additional “skill” beyond shot selection. Formally, the estimator is:

\[
\mathrm{CPAE}_g = \int_X \int_Y \widehat{P}^{*}_{g,\mathrm{league}}(\mathrm{Complete}\mid x,y)\,\widehat{f}_g(x,y)\,dx\,dy
\]

with \(x\) and \(y\) measured in yards relative to the line of scrimmage and field center [PAGE 10] [PAGE 30]. The density \(\widehat{f}_g(x,y)\) has units of \(\mathrm{yd}^{-2}\), the completion surface is dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\), and the product integrated over \(\mathrm{yd}^2\) yields a dimensionless expected completion-percentage offset. If the density is not normalized over the analytic domain, the scalar is not a proper expectation. If the completion surface is numerically outside \([0,1]\), the integral is invalid. If the homography or pixel-to-yard mapping has a singular Jacobian, the density estimate is not a physical probability density.

A second gray-zone issue is missingness. The paper reports that only “2.8% of pass locations in 2017 and 2.7% of pass locations in 2018 had missing coordinates in the final output dataset” [PAGE 12]. That is small globally, but missingness is not necessarily random: the text notes that missing coordinates “appear to happen more often for certain home teams” [PAGE 13]. If missingness correlates with team, stadium rendering, or quarterback, then the naive Bayes shrinkage toward the league surface may incorrectly pull certain groups toward the prior simply because their observed evidence is incomplete. The paper also excludes 2016 because “approximately 65% of the pass charts are missing” and because “the missing charts are not uniformly distributed across teams” [PAGE 6]. This is a crucial multi-week trap: temporal comparisons that ignore chart availability can mistake data scarcity for performance change.

A third gray-zone issue is validation asymmetry. The authors validate completed non-touchdown passes against Big Data Bowl tracking data because “the NFL’s tracking data does not specify the point at which the ball hits the ground and is rendered incomplete” [PAGE 16]. This means the extraction is best verified where outcomes are unambiguous, but weaker where the method must infer incomplete-pass endpoints. The paper also says the greedy linkage produces right-skewed distance errors because “we likely make some linkage errors at the right-tail” [PAGE 18]. Thus, the reported median deviation of “1.7 yards” is a central tendency measure, not a bound on worst-case error [PAGE 25].

Finally, the CPAE comparison with the league’s proprietary metric is informative but not equivalent. The authors’ public-data CPAE correlates strongly with the NFL NGS CPAE, with “ρ2017 = 0.81, ρ2018 = 0.91” [PAGE 32]. However, the NFL version uses additional variables such as “the location of the receiver, the location of the defenders, the, distance from the sideline” [PAGE 32]. The public version therefore approximates a richer causal model with only spatial location and outcome. The stable correlation across seasons is moderate for the authors’ metric, “0.41” [PAGE 31], while the NFL metric appears to have a temporal shift not mirrored by the public metric [PAGE 33]. This suggests the public pipeline captures much of the spatial signal but not the full private tracking signal.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Pixel-to-field homography operator

The source states: “we calculate the homography matrix H” to map trapezoidal image coordinates \((x_t,y_t)\) to rectangular field coordinates \((x_r,y_r)\) [PAGE 7]. The formal equation is:

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
x_r \\
y_r \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
=
H
\begin{bmatrix}
x_t \\
y_t \\
1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

where \(H\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\) is defined up to scale. Units: \(x_t,y_t\) are pixel coordinates \(\mathrm{px}\), while \(x_r,y_r\) are field coordinates, later interpreted as yards relative to the line of scrimmage and field center [PAGE 10]. SI conversion: \(1\ \mathrm{yd}=0.9144\ \mathrm{m}\). Assumptions: the field is a plane, the chart projection is a pure projective transformation, and enough non-degenerate correspondences exist to estimate \(H\). Breakdown regimes: \(H\) is singular or ill-conditioned; correspondences are collinear or clustered; the field markings used for calibration are absent, cropped, or obscured. If \(H\) collapses area, the Jacobian determinant vanishes and no proper 2D density can be defined.

2. Color-thresholding operator

The source describes HSV thresholding: pixels within specified ranges are retained, others set to zero [PAGE 9]. A formal operator is:

\[
M_c(u,v)=\mathbf{1}\left[(H_c(u,v),S_c(u,v),V_c(u,v))\in R_c\right]
\]

where \(c\) denotes pass type, \(u,v\) are pixel coordinates, \(R_c\) is the threshold region, and \(M_c\) is a binary mask. Units: hue is angular in degrees, saturation and value are intensity-like coordinates. The paper’s table gives threshold intervals for complete, incomplete, touchdown, and interception masks [PAGE 9]. Assumptions: pass markers have stable chromatic signatures and field elements have been suppressed. Breakdown: overlapping colors, compression artifacts, anti-aliasing, and similar-colored trajectory lines produce false positives, explicitly noted for touchdown passes [PAGE 8] [PAGE 10].

3. K-means and K-means++ operators

K-means minimizes within-cluster variation:

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{C(i)=k}\left\|X_i-c_k\right\|^2
\]

where \(X_i\) are pixel or field observations, \(c_k\) are centers, and distances are Euclidean [PAGE 11]. The source says K-means “assumes the co-variance structure of the clusters is spherical” [PAGE 11]. K-means++ initializes centers with probability:

\[
p_i=\frac{D(X_i)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}D(X_j)^2}
\]

where \(D(X_i)\) is the distance from \(X_i\) to the nearest already chosen center [PAGE 11]. Units: if applied before homography, distances are \(\mathrm{px}\); if applied after mapping, they are \(\mathrm{yd}\). Breakdown: \(K>n\); empty clusters; duplicate points causing denominator zero in K-means++ initialization; non-spherical markers; local minima. The paper uses K-means++ for completions and interceptions and also for adjusted incompletions after iterative checking [PAGE 12].

4. DBSCAN noise-removal operator

DBSCAN uses an \(\epsilon\)-neighborhood and a minimum-points threshold \(\tau\):

\[
N_\epsilon(p)=\{q : \|p-q\|\leq \epsilon\}
\]

A cluster is retained if \(|N(p)|\geq \tau\), otherwise treated as noise [PAGE 14]. For touchdown images, the source says: “we use DBSCAN on these observations/pixels to find the ntd highest density clusters, with ε = 10 and τ = ntd” [PAGE 14]. Units: \(\epsilon\) is in pixel units unless converted through the homography Jacobian to yards. Breakdown: \(\epsilon\) too small yields all noise; \(\epsilon\) too large merges distinct passes; \(\tau\) too high discards valid clusters; \(\tau\) too low retains line/trajectory fragments. The source shows a case where DBSCAN initially detects four clusters but the true number of touchdowns is three [PAGE 15].

5. Kernel density estimation operator

The league pass-location distribution is estimated by two-dimensional KDE:

\[
\widehat{f}_h(x,y)=\frac{1}{n h_x h_y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_x\left(\frac{x-x_i}{h_x}\right)K_y\left(\frac{y-y_i}{h_y}\right)
\]

with bandwidths \(h_x,h_y\) and kernels \(K_x,K_y\) [PAGE 19]. The paper uses a bivariate normal kernel and Scott’s rule:

\[
\widehat{h}=1.06\times \min\left(\widehat{\sigma},\frac{\mathrm{IQR}}{1.34}\right)\times n^{-1/5}
\]

[page 19]. Units: \(x,y\) in yards; \(h_x,h_y\) in yards; \(\widehat{f}\) has units \(\mathrm{yd}^{-2}\) if normalized over the field domain. Assumptions: observations are independent samples from a smooth density, boundary effects are handled by bounding the field, and the chosen kernel adequately represents spatial smoothing. Breakdown: \(n=0\); \(\widehat{\sigma}=0\) and \(\mathrm{IQR}=0\), giving zero bandwidth; extreme boundary truncation; severe sparsity causing artificial mass near the boundary. The paper bounds the KDE to the field range “between 10 yards behind the line of scrimmage, 55 yards in front of the line of scrimmage, and between both sidelines” [PAGE 19].

6. GAM completion-surface operator

The completion model uses a logit link:

\[
\log\left(\frac{P(\mathrm{Complete})}{P(\mathrm{Incomplete})}\right)
=
c_0+f_x(x)+f_y(y)+f_{xy}(x,y)
\]

where \(x\) is downfield distance, \(y\) is lateral field position, and \(f_{xy}\) is a tensor-product interaction smoother [PAGE 21]. Units: logit is dimensionless; \(x,y\) are in yards. Assumptions: smooth functional dependence, adequate sample support, and no complete separation in local field regions. Breakdown: empirical completion probability equal to 0 or 1 makes the logit singular; tensor bases can become rank deficient if data are too sparse; overfitting can occur if smoothing is too weak; underfitting can occur if smoothing is too strong. The source explicitly says longer passes are harder, sideline targets differ from middle targets, and interaction terms are needed [PAGE 20].

7. Two-dimensional naive Bayes shrinkage operator

For group \(g\), the source defines:

\[
\widehat{P}^{*}_{G}(\mathrm{Complete}\mid x,y)
=
\frac{
N_g \widehat{f}_g(x,y)\widehat{P}_g(\mathrm{Complete}\mid x,y)
+
N_{\mathrm{Median}}\widehat{f}_{NFL}(x,y)\widehat{P}_{NFL}(\mathrm{Complete}\mid x,y)
}{
N_g \widehat{f}_g(x,y)
+
N_{\mathrm{Median}}\widehat{f}_{NFL}(x,y)
}
\]

[PAGE 22]. Here \(N_g\) is the number of passes for the group, \(N_{\mathrm{Median}}\) is the median pass count for the relevant group class, and densities are estimated over the field domain. Units: counts are dimensionless; densities are \(\mathrm{yd}^{-2}\); completion probabilities are dimensionless. Assumptions: the league surface is an appropriate prior; group-specific evidence accumulates proportionally to count and spatial density; the domain is common across groups. Breakdown: denominator equals zero; densities are zero in a region where completion probability is needed; \(N_{\mathrm{Median}}\) is chosen poorly; missing coordinates bias \(\widehat{f}_g\) or \(\widehat{P}_g\).

8. CPAE scalar operator

The scalar CPAE for group \(g\) is:

\[
\mathrm{CPAE}_g
=
\int_X\int_Y
\widehat{P}^{*}_{g,\mathrm{league}}(\mathrm{Complete}\mid x,y)
\widehat{f}_g(x,y)
\,dx\,dy
\]

[PAGE 30]. Units: dimensionless expected completion-percentage difference if \(\widehat{f}_g\) integrates to one over the domain. If \(\widehat{f}_g\) is a raw count density rather than a normalized density, CPAE scales with pass count and is not comparable across groups. Breakdown: integration domain excludes part of the target distribution; \(\widehat{f}_g\) is improper; \(\widehat{P}^{*}\) is outside \([0,1]\); missing data make the empirical density non-representative. The paper’s field range is “between 10 yards behind the line of scrimmage to 55 yards in front of the line of scrimmage” [PAGE 18], and the coordinate system has \(x=0\) at the line of scrimmage and \(y=0\) at the center of the field [PAGE 10].

9. Unit and information ledger

- Pixel coordinates: \(\mathrm{px}\), image size \(1200\times 1200\ \mathrm{px}\) [PAGE 6].  
- Field coordinates: \(\mathrm{yd}\), convert to SI by \(1\ \mathrm{yd}=0.9144\ \mathrm{m}\).  
- Tracking frequency: \(12.5\ \mathrm{Hz}=12.5\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}\) for NFL RFID data [PAGE 2].  
- Probability: dimensionless, \([0,1]\).  
- Logit: dimensionless.  
- Density: \(\mathrm{yd}^{-2}\).  
- Log density: \(\log(\mathrm{yd}^{-2})\), units depend on logarithm base; natural log gives nats, base-2 gives bits.  
- DBSCAN \(\epsilon\): pixel distance unless transformed to yards through the local homography Jacobian.  
- KDE bandwidth: yards.  
- CPAE: dimensionless expected completion difference; multiplied by 100 for percentage points.

10. Exact singularity conditions

- Homography: \(\det(H)=0\) or near-zero condition number.  
- KDE: \(n h_x h_y=0\).  
- Scott bandwidth: \(n=0\), \(\widehat{\sigma}=0\), or \(\mathrm{IQR}=0\).  
- GAM: local empirical completion probability at boundary; smoother basis rank deficiency.  
- Naive Bayes: \(N_g\widehat{f}_g+N_{\mathrm{Median}}\widehat{f}_{NFL}=0\).  
- CPAE: \(\int \widehat{f}_g \neq 1\) over the chosen domain.  
- K-means++: \(\sum_j D(X_j)^2=0\).  
- DBSCAN: \(|N_\epsilon(p)|<\tau\) for all true pass clusters, or \(\epsilon\) so large that all points merge into one cluster.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual genealogy of the source moves from league-wide optical/RFID tracking toward public image reconstruction. The paper situates itself in a broader sports-analytics lineage: MLB PITCHf/x, Statcast, NBA optical tracking at “25 times per second (25 Hz)”, NHL tracking plans, and NFL RFID tracking at “12.5 Hz” [PAGE 2]. The NFL’s Next Gen Stats platform provides visualizations but not raw tracking data publicly, creating the motivating scarcity that the authors address [PAGE 1] [PAGE 3].

A critical historical trap is the temporary public release of NFL tracking data through competitions. The source says the league released tracking data for punt plays and “all tracking data from the first six weeks of the 2017 regular season” for the Big Data Bowl [PAGE 3]. This release is both a validation opportunity and a temporal trap: any model calibrated only on that window may overfit a six-week regime and mistake it for season-long behavior. The authors explicitly avoid relying on it as a substitute for full-season public charts.

A second lineage is computer vision. The homography step follows projective geometry and image rectification principles, citing Szeliski [PAGE 7]. The color segmentation step follows HSV color-space methods, citing Koschan and Abidi [PAGE 9]. The clustering lineage follows MacQueen for K-means [PAGE 11], Arthur and Vassilvitskii for K-means++ [PAGE 11], and Ester et al. for DBSCAN [PAGE 13]. These methods are not football-specific, but the authors adapt them to the specific failure modes of pass charts: overlapping marks, color collision between touchdown markers and line-of-scrimmage graphics, and mismatch between JSON metadata counts and visible marks [PAGE 8].

A third lineage is statistical sports modeling. The paper references openWAR, nflscrapR, pitchRx, nhlscrapR, and related reproducible sports research projects [PAGE 4]. Methodologically, it combines KDE for spatial target distributions with GAMs for completion probability, then uses an empirical-Bayes-like shrinkage for small samples [PAGE 19] [PAGE 20] [PAGE 22]. This reflects a recurring trend in sports analytics: use dense spatial data to estimate local expectations, then regularize sparse player- or team-level estimates toward league baselines.

Multi-week traps are central. First, 2016 pass charts are largely missing: “approximately 65% of the pass charts are missing” and the missingness is not uniform across teams [PAGE 6]. Second, the final 2017–2018 dataset still has missing coordinates, with 2.8% and 2.7% missing respectively [PAGE 12], and those missing values appear more frequent for certain home teams [PAGE 13]. Third, out-of-bounds passes and spikes may be counted as attempts but not depicted, causing metadata-image mismatch [PAGE 12]. Fourth, the validation window is only six weeks of 2017, so the authors cannot directly compare all passes to tracking data [PAGE 16]. Fifth, the NFL’s proprietary CPAE uses variables absent from public charts, so the public metric can only approximate the private metric [PAGE 32].

The retro-causal lesson is that a public image dataset can reproduce much of a private tracking signal, but only within the manifold of visible outcomes and available metadata. The source’s strength is that it does not claim to recover the full tracking process; it claims to “partially close the gap” between private and public data [PAGE 1]. Its weakness is that downstream models may still be interpreted as if they recover decision quality, when they actually recover location-conditioned outcome tendencies under missingness and observational bias.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind layer is needed because football passing is not a passive point process; it is generated by strategic agents with beliefs about each other. The source acknowledges this indirectly: “QBs tend to target open receivers, defenses tend to cover high-leverage areas of the field more closely, and target locations depend greatly on the game situation” [PAGE 19]. However, the image-derived dataset lacks coverage geometry, receiver separation, defender positions, down, distance, score, and time. Therefore, any inference about quarterback skill or defensive quality must be interpreted as a reduced-form spatial outcome model, not a full strategic causal model.

Crisis habit regression refers to the tendency of agents to revert to familiar spatial habits under pressure, fatigue, or adverse game state. For example, a quarterback may default to short middle throws when pressured, or a defense may compress toward the middle when protecting a late lead. The source cannot directly test this because it lacks game-state covariates. The correct recursive interpretation is: if a QB’s target density becomes more concentrated in high-completion zones during adverse situations, CPAE may rise even if underlying decision quality does not. Conversely, if a defense forces opponents into low-efficiency areas, a simple completion surface may understate defensive quality if it does not condition on situation.

Counter-trap refers to strategic manipulation of the observable data-generating process. Teams and quarterbacks may deliberately shape their public chart tendencies to mislead opponents. For example, a quarterback may throw more often to the sideline in low-leverage situations to build a public tendency that differs from postseason behavior. A defense may allow short completions to bait aggressive play-calling while preserving deep safety help. The source’s CPAE cannot distinguish “good process” from “good outcome given a favorable target distribution” without additional causal variables. The public model is therefore vulnerable to a counter-trap: an agent can improve the metric by changing target selection rather than by improving play.

The clock dimension operates at three levels. First, the image clock: Next Gen Stats charts are game-level summaries, not play-by-play streams; the source notes that passes from next-gen-scraPy “do not contain play identifiers or timestamps” [PAGE 23]. Second, the season clock: CPAE stability across 2017 and 2018 is moderate, with correlation “0.41” [PAGE 31], suggesting that season-specific noise and roster changes matter. Third, the league-metric clock: the NFL NGS CPAE appears higher on average in 2018 than 2017, while the public CPAE does not show the same temporal trend [PAGE 33]. This divergence may reflect changes in private tracking inputs, league modeling choices, or changes in missing chart availability.

A robust recursive model would therefore include: (i) a strategic prior about target selection; (ii) a missing-data mechanism tied to team and season; (iii) a temporal drift term; and (iv) a counterfactual model of what completion probability would be under alternative target distributions. The source provides the spatial skeleton for such a model but not the full strategic state. The safest interpretation is that CPAE is a descriptive location-adjusted outcome summary, not a complete measure of decision quality. If future work links these coordinates to nflscrapR play-by-play, air yards, EPA, or win probability, the recursive loop can be closed more tightly [PAGE 34].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication tests whether image-derived conclusions survive physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints. The source provides several veto layers.

Physical field-boundary veto. The extracted coordinate must satisfy the field domain used by the model. The paper focuses on the range “between 10 yards behind the line of scrimmage to 55 yards in front of the line of scrimmage” [PAGE 18], with \(x=0\) at the line of scrimmage and \(y=0\) at field center [PAGE 10]. A valid pass endpoint should satisfy approximately:

\[
x\in[-10,55]\ \mathrm{yd}
\]

and

\[
|y|\leq y_{\mathrm{sideline}}
\]

where \(y_{\mathrm{sideline}}\) is half the field width. If a cluster center falls outside this domain, the extraction should be vetoed or corrected. This veto is provable because the source explicitly bounds the KDE to the field rectangle [PAGE 19].

Photometric-veto layer. The source identifies a specific physical rendering problem: for touchdown passes, “the color of the pass location is the same color as the line of scrimmage and pass trajectory path, rendering color thresholding used for the other types of passes ineffective” [PAGE 8]. This creates false positives that must be removed by DBSCAN. If DBSCAN retains line fragments or trajectory fragments, the physical interpretation of the cluster as a catch point is invalid. The paper’s example shows four DBSCAN clusters where the true number of touchdowns is three [PAGE 15]. The veto rule is: after noise removal, the number of retained high-density clusters must match the JSON count \(n_{td}\); otherwise the touchdown coordinate layer is not physically reliable.

Biomechanical-veto layer. The source notes that NFL RFID tracking chips are “placed in players’ shoulder pads” [PAGE 23]. Therefore, tracking data may mark player torso position rather than ball position, while chart extraction may mark catch or ball endpoint. The authors state that passes to the left or right show systematic angular differences across datasets, suggesting one source may track the ball while the other tracks the receiver [PAGE 25]. A biomechanical veto is: if the inferred endpoint is farther from a plausible catch envelope than can be explained by receiver reach, ball trajectory, or digitization error, reject the link. The source provides an empirical central deviation of “1.7 yards” between linked completed-pass coordinates [PAGE 25], but also warns that linkage distances are right-skewed because errors occur in the tail [PAGE 18]. Thus, 1.7 yards is not a universal tolerance; it is a median diagnostic.

Weather veto layer. The source does not provide weather variables. Therefore, any claim that the extracted CPAE surface isolates quarterback skill independent of wind, rain, temperature, or stadium surface is not supported by the provided model. The correct veto is epistemic: reject causal weather adjustment claims unless external weather data are joined. The model can still describe completion tendencies, but it cannot prove that weather did not confound those tendencies.

Procedural veto layer. The dataset has missingness. The paper reports that some charts are missing without explanation [PAGE 6], some games lack charts for either team [PAGE 15], and some pass coordinates are missing [PAGE 12]. If a quarterback’s target density is estimated from a non-random subset of charts, the CPAE integral may be biased. A formal veto is: if the missingness indicator is correlated with team, stadium, season, or quarterback, then unadjusted CPAE comparisons across groups are not admissible without a missing-data correction.

Statistical veto layer. The completion surface must remain a valid probability surface. The GAM logit becomes singular if local empirical completion is exactly 0 or 1, and the naive Bayes denominator becomes invalid if weighted densities vanish. A formal statistical veto is:

\[
\widehat{P}^{*}_{g,\mathrm{league}}(x,y)\notin[0,1]
\]

or

\[
N_g\widehat{f}_g(x,y)+N_{\mathrm{Median}}\widehat{f}_{NFL}(x,y)=0
\]

for any region contributing non-negligible mass to CPAE. In such cases, the scalar CPAE should be treated as numerically unstable rather than football-meaningful.

The final adjudication is that the source’s outputs are physically plausible when the homography, color segmentation, cluster count reconciliation, and probability-surface constraints are satisfied. They are not admissible as full causal evaluations of quarterback or defense quality when missingness, private tracking variables, and strategic target selection are ignored.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable RLVR-style veto scenarios: each is a counterfactual condition under which a downstream claim from the source should be rejected unless additional evidence is provided.

1. Missing-chart veto

Condition: A game, quarterback, or team lacks an NGS pass chart, or the chart lacks necessary metadata. The source says some charts “are missing from the NGS website without explanation” [PAGE 6] and that 2016 is excluded because “approximately 65% of the pass charts are missing” [PAGE 6]. It also reports that “33 are due to Next Gen Stats not providing pass charts for either team during a game” [PAGE 15].

Prediction: If one attempts to estimate a season-level CPAE for a quarterback with incomplete chart coverage, the estimated target density \(\widehat{f}_g(x,y)\) will be biased toward the observed subset. Teams with more missing charts will show more unstable CPAE and stronger shrinkage toward the league prior. The paper already shows team-level missing-coordinate patterns, with certain home teams more affected [PAGE 13].

RLVR veto: Reject any claim that a quarterback’s CPAE is comparable to another quarterback’s CPAE unless the number of available charts and missingness pattern are controlled or reported. A verifiable test is to regress CPAE on chart coverage; if coverage predicts CPAE, unadjusted comparisons are vetoed.

2. Incomplete-pass coordinate veto

Condition: The pass is incomplete, especially an out-of-bounds pass or spike, and the NGS chart either does not depict it or depicts it in a way that cannot be separated from noise. The source says “out-of-bounds passes and spikes are not presented in the charts despite being counted as pass attempts” [PAGE 12]. It also says the NFL tracking data “does not specify the point at which the ball hits the ground and is rendered incomplete” [PAGE 16].

Prediction: The metadata count of incompletions will exceed the number of visually identifiable incomplete-pass clusters. The authors’ iterative adjustment may reduce \(n_{\mathrm{inc}}\) to \(n_{\mathrm{inc\text{-}adj}}\), and unidentifiable rows will be populated with N/A values [PAGE 12]. Missing coordinates will be concentrated in charts with ambiguous rendering or teams with unusual graphics.

RLVR veto: Reject any completion-surface estimate that treats incomplete-pass locations as fully observed without accounting for missingness. A verifiable test is to compare the distribution of adjusted incomplete counts to raw metadata counts; if the discrepancy is non-random across teams, the completion surface for those teams is vetoed unless corrected.

3. Touchdown-color-collision veto

Condition: A touchdown pass marker shares color with the line of scrimmage or trajectory lines, causing color thresholding to produce noise. The source explicitly says this occurs for touchdown passes [PAGE 8] [PAGE 10]. It also shows DBSCAN initially detecting more clusters than the true number of touchdowns [PAGE 15].

Prediction: If \(\epsilon\) is too large or \(\tau\) is too low, DBSCAN will retain trajectory or line fragments as false touchdown clusters. If \(\epsilon\) is too small or \(\tau\) is too high, true touchdown markers may be discarded as noise. The final K-means++ step will then either split a single pass into multiple centers or omit a true touchdown location.

RLVR veto: Reject touchdown coordinate claims unless the final number of touchdown clusters exactly matches the JSON count \(n_{td}\) after noise removal. A verifiable test is to plot the retained DBSCAN clusters before and after selecting the \(n_{td}\) densest clusters, as in the source’s example [PAGE 15]. If the count mismatch persists after cleaning, the touchdown location layer is vetoed.

Overall RLVR adjudication: The source’s pipeline is credible when its geometric, photometric, clustering, and probability constraints are satisfied. It becomes vulnerable when missingness is non-random, when incomplete-pass endpoints are unobserved, or when touchdown color collisions produce false positives. The strongest counterfactually verifiable predictions are: chart coverage should predict CPAE stability; incomplete-pass metadata should exceed identifiable visual clusters; and touchdown DBSCAN cluster counts should collapse to the JSON count after noise removal. If these predictions fail in a new dataset, downstream CPAE or spatial-completion claims should be vetoed until the extraction pipeline is recalibrated.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
