1. PCA and Hotelling diagnostics. Let the standardized feature matrix be \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\), mean-centered and scaled. The PCA model is
\[
X = T P^{\top} + E,
\]
where \(T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times a}\) are scores, \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times a}\) loadings, \(E\) residual. The squared prediction error for row \(x_i\) is
\[
\mathrm{SPE}_i = \lVert x_i - \hat{x}_i \rVert_2^2,
\]
in squared standardized units, hence dimensionless if inputs are standardized. Hotelling’s statistic is
\[
T_i^2 = t_i^{\top} S_t^{-1} t_i,
\]
dimensionless. Assumptions: approximate linear subspace, stable covariance, representative sampling, and approximately elliptical score distribution for chart thresholds. Breakdown: \(S_t\) singular or rank-deficient; \(p \ge n\); collinear variables; nonstationary seasons; outliers that dominate \(P\). The TOC names “SPE of the PCA model with nine PCs for teams.” [PAGE 3] and “Hotelling’s T 2 chart of the PCA model with nine PCs for teams.” [PAGE 3], but no numerical thresholds are supplied.

2. PLS-DA. With response dummy matrix \(Y\), the latent-variable model is
\[
X = T P^{\top} + E,\qquad Y = T Q^{\top} + F,
\]
and the coefficient operator can be written \(B = W(P^{\top}W)^{-1}Q^{\top}\), with scores \(T = XW(P^{\top}W)^{-1}\) under standard PLS conventions. Predicted class probability is a monotone function of \(X B\), with a decision threshold. Units: dimensionless scores and probabilities; coefficients carry units of \(Y\) per unit \(X\). Assumptions: linear latent covariance between \(X\) and \(Y\), adequate sample size, stable class encoding, and no perfect separation. Breakdown: rank deficiency in \(P^{\top}W\), perfect class separation, severe imbalance, nonlinear class boundaries, and distribution shift. Page 4 cites “PLS-DA regression coeﬃcients with 95% jackknife conﬁdence intervals” [PAGE 4], which presupposes resampling stability; the TOC alone does not prove that stability.

3. Random Forest. For \(B\) trees,
\[
\hat{p}(y \mid x)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{p}_b(y \mid x),
\]
with probability units dimensionless. For regression, \(\hat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_b(x)\), in the units of the target, here goals or goal difference. Assumptions: exchangeable training and deployment distributions, representative sampling, and no temporal leakage. Breakdown: distribution shift, small \(n\), correlated features, class imbalance, and extrapolation beyond the training support. Page 5 cites “according to the RF, for predicting goal diﬀerence (Z)” [PAGE 5], but no importance values or out-of-bag errors are given.

4. Skellam regression. Let \(Z=Y_1-Y_2\) be the difference of two independent Poisson goal counts conditional on covariates, with means \(\lambda,\mu > 0\) in goals per match. Then
\[
\Pr(Z=k)=e^{-(\lambda+\mu)}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)^{k/2}I_{|k|}\!\left(2\sqrt{\lambda\mu}\right),
\]
for integer \(k\), where \(I_{\nu}\) is the modified Bessel function. The mean is \(\mathbb{E}[Z]=\lambda-\mu\) in goals, and the variance is \(\mathrm{Var}(Z)=\lambda+\mu\) in squared goals. A regression specification uses
\[
\log \lambda = x^{\top}\beta_\lambda,\qquad \log \mu = x^{\top}\beta_\mu,
\]
so \(\beta\) is dimensionless on the log scale. Assumptions: conditional independence of the two counts, Poisson variance-mean equality, correct log link, and no unmodeled overdispersion. Breakdown: \(\mu \to 0\) or \(\lambda \to 0\) makes the displayed power form numerically singular and requires the limiting Bessel form; \(\lambda=\mu=0\) is degenerate at \(Z=0\); overdispersion, zero inflation, correlated counts, or negative binomial structure invalidate the variance assumption. Page 2 names “Using the Skellam regression model in combination with the Random Forest algorithm to predict match results” [PAGE 2], but no fitted \(\lambda,\mu\), coefficients, or goodness-of-fit values appear on the supplied pages.

5. Classification metrics. With confusion counts \(TP,TN,FP,FN\),
\[
\mathrm{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},
\]
dimensionless in \([0,1]\). The Matthews correlation coefficient is
\[
\mathrm{MCC}=\frac{TP\cdot TN-FP\cdot FN}
{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}},
\]
dimensionless in \([-1,1]\). AUC is dimensionless in \([0,1]\). Assumptions: defined classes, stable prevalence for interpretation, and no leakage. Breakdown: MCC denominator is zero if any marginal count is zero; AUC is undefined if only one class is present; sensitivity and specificity can be gamed by imbalance. Page 6 explicitly cites “MCC values of the supervised learning models for unbalanced and balanced data.” [PAGE 6], which is the correct guard against imbalance-driven claims. Page 7 cites “Sensitivity, Specificity, and MCC (Means and 95% Centred Intervals)” [PAGE 7], but the intervals themselves are not supplied.

6. Regression and value models. Multiple linear regression is
\[
y = X\beta+\varepsilon,
\]
with \(y\) in target units, \(\beta\) in target units per feature unit, and \(\varepsilon\) in target units. Assumptions: linearity, independent and homoscedastic errors, no severe collinearity, and correct specification. Breakdown: rank deficiency of \(X^{\top}X\), outliers, omitted variables, and nonstationarity. Root mean squared error is
\[
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2},
\]
in target units. Page 7 cites “RMSE for all methods according to the three models (e).” [PAGE 7]. For Google Trends popularity, a minimal formal statement is
\[
M_t = f(G_{t-\ell},X_t)+\varepsilon_t,
\]
where \(G_t \in [0,100]\) is a dimensionless search index, \(M_t\) is market value in currency, and \(\ell\) is a lag in weeks. Assumptions: no reverse causality from market value to search volume and no season-boundary leakage. Breakdown: contemporaneous endogeneity, lag misspecification, and structural breaks across seasons.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is composite. PCA and Hotelling control charts descend from industrial multivariate quality control; they ask whether a team or player is an outlier in a stable feature space. PLS-DA descends from chemometrics and latent-variable discrimination; it asks whether a low-dimensional projection separates top from bottom teams while preserving covariance with the response. Random Forest descends from ensemble tree learning; it asks whether nonlinear variable importance survives resampling. The Skellam model descends from Poisson count-difference theory; it asks whether goal difference has a mean-variance structure consistent with two conditional Poisson counts. Google Trends popularity modeling descends from nowcasting and digital-trace econometrics; it asks whether search attention leads or merely follows market value. The TOC juxtaposes these lineages without unifying them: “Quality or chance? Application of machine learning and multivariate statistics techniques” [PAGE 1] is not the same claim as “Using the Skellam regression model in combination with the Random Forest algorithm” [PAGE 2].

Multi-week traps are especially visible in the popularity chapter. Page 2 names “Development of popularity indicators with Google Trends to measure popularity inﬂuence on the market value of players” [PAGE 2]. If weekly Google Trends is measured in the same week as market value, reverse causality is live: value changes can generate news and searches. If lagged by several weeks, the lag choice becomes a researcher degree of freedom. The 2019/2020 and 2020/2021 seasons cited on pages 5 and 7 create a second trap: COVID-era schedule disruption, empty stadiums, and transfer-market shocks can break the mapping from historical features to outcomes. The correlation-pruning rule named on page 7, “after discarding variables with a correlation higher than 0.7 in each league for both seasons” [PAGE 7], is a sensible lineage-aware guard, but it also means that variable importance is conditional on a pruning decision. A retrocausal audit therefore asks not only whether the model predicted, but whether the feature genealogy made the prediction inevitable through leakage, lag selection, or season-boundary contamination.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis, the analyst’s habitual regression is to reach for the highest-capacity model and the most significant variable list. The TOC supplies the temptation: RF importance [PAGE 5], PLS-DA coefficients with jackknife intervals [PAGE 4], MCC under balanced and unbalanced data [PAGE 6], and 100 replications with 75/25 splits [PAGE 7]. A recursive model of the analyst must assume that these labels can create false confidence: “100 replications” sounds robust even if the split is random rather than temporal; “95% jackknife confidence intervals” sounds inferential even if the resampling unit is the match rather than the season; “MCC” sounds imbalance-proof even if prevalence shifts across leagues. The counter-trap is precommitment: fix a temporal holdout, fix the lag grid for Google Trends before looking at market value, fix the correlation-pruning threshold, and report permutation nulls. The clock is the third agent: season time. The model must not see future matches, future market values, or future popularity to predict the present. Page 3’s “double cross validation” [PAGE 3] is the right clock-discipline operator only if the outer loop is temporal or group-blocked. If the outer loop is random, the clock is broken. The recursive counter-trap is therefore to model the analyst’s incentive to overfit, then make the evaluation protocol expensive to game: nested splits, blocked seasons, and held-out leagues. The correct habit under crisis is not a bigger forest; it is a smaller claim with a clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical, biomechanical, or weather layer can veto a statistical model only if it changes the conditional distribution of the outcome beyond the included features. Formally, let \(Y\) be the outcome, \(X\) the statistical features, and \(W\) the physical, biomechanical, or weather covariates. The no-veto condition is
\[
\mathbb{I}(Y;W \mid X)=0 \ \text{nats},
\]
or equivalently \(\Pr(Y \mid X,W)=\Pr(Y \mid X)\). The veto condition is \(\mathbb{I}(Y;W \mid X)>0\) under a valid conditional randomization test. A second veto is missingness not at random:
\[
\Pr(M_W=1 \mid Y,X) \ne \Pr(M_W=1 \mid X),
\]
where \(M_W\) is the missingness indicator for the physical layer. A third veto is nonstationarity: if the conditional law \(\Pr(Y \mid X,W)\) changes across seasons, pooled coefficients are not transportable.

The supplied pages contain only TOC evidence for physical layers: “Empirical studies using global positioning systems (GPS) data” [PAGE 6] and “Empirical studies on the causes of injury in elite football” [PAGE 6]. GPS-style variables would carry position in metres, velocity in metres per second, acceleration in metres per second squared, and load indices in arbitrary units; injury-causes variables would be categorical or time-to-event. None of those variables, units, or estimates are supplied. Weather is not listed in the visible pages; therefore a weather veto claim is red-zone and cannot be proven from this source. The adjudication is: physical and biomechanical layers are plausible and lineage-consistent, but unproven here; weather has no page evidence. Any cross-layer veto must show conditional information gain in nats, missingness dependence, or season-specific coefficient instability. Without that, the statistical model is not vetoed by physics; it is merely under-specified.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a claim is vetoed if a rerun with a pre-registered counterfactual produces a reward below a pre-registered minimal effect or a confidence interval that includes zero. Three scenarios are verifiable from the named methods.

Scenario 1: temporal leakage counterfactual. Replace the random 75/25 split named on page 7 with a season-blocked holdout and a nested double cross-validation as named on page 3. Reward: \(\mathrm{MCC}_{\text{blocked}}\) with a 95% interval. Prediction: if the original performance depended on future-season leakage, \(\mathrm{MCC}_{\text{blocked}}\) collapses toward zero and the interval includes zero; if it was genuine, the interval remains above the pre-registered minimal effect. Veto rule: accept the original claim only if the blocked interval excludes zero.

Scenario 2: popularity-lag counterfactual. Re-estimate the Google Trends and market-value relation with \(G_{t-\ell}\) for \(\ell \in \{0,1,2,4,8\}\) weeks and with future-blind market values. Reward: sign stability and significance of the popularity coefficient plus out-of-sample RMSE in currency. Prediction: if the apparent effect is reverse-causal or contemporaneous, the \(\ell=0\) coefficient loses significance when \(\ell \ge 4\), and RMSE does not improve over a no-popularity baseline. Veto rule: reject the causal popularity claim if the lagged coefficients are unstable or the RMSE improvement is not replicated.

Scenario 3: class-balance and correlation-pruning counterfactual. Re-run the RF and PLS-DA comparisons after balancing classes, using the page 6 warning “MCC values of the supervised learning models for unbalanced and balanced data.” [PAGE 6], and after the page 7 rule “after discarding variables with a correlation higher than 0.7 in each league for both seasons” [PAGE 7]. Reward: rank stability of the top model and overlap of MCC 95% intervals. Prediction: if the original ranking was an imbalance or collinearity artifact, the top model changes and the MCC intervals overlap across RF, PLS-DA, and SRM. Veto rule: reject the claim of a uniquely superior model if the rank order is not stable under both stress tests. These three counterfactuals are verifiable, model-agnostic, and do not require any additional unavailable source pages.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
