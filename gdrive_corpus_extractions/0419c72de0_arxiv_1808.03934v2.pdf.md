Bieniek et al. (2015) used a larger, non-selected brain bank but ascertained athletic participation from medical records, risking information bias and undercounting true participation [PAGE 2]. Savica et al. (2012) found no elevated neurodegenerative risk among high-school football players versus male classmates but could not control for family background, general health, or adolescent IQ [PAGE 2]. Montenigro et al. (2017) and Alosco et al. (2017) suggest thresholded dose-response relationships between cumulative head impacts and later-life depression, apathy, executive dysfunction, and cognitive impairment, and an association between age of first exposure and later neuropsychiatric outcomes [PAGE 20].

Multi-week traps. The causal clock runs from Wave I baseline (1994–95 school year, adolescence) to Wave IV outcome (2008, ages 24–32), a span of roughly thirteen to fourteen years [PAGE 4]. Over this interval, 680 subjects (140 football-intention, 540 controls) are missing the CES-D, about one-quarter of the eligible sample [PAGE 6, 20]. Attrition is therefore a first-order trap: if football participation influences mortality, incarceration, or disabling impairment, the observed Wave IV sample is no longer representative of the original exposed cohort. The authors mitigate this by showing football is not a significant predictor of missingness conditional on baseline covariates [PAGE 7], but they cannot rule out missing-not-at-random mechanisms. A second era trap separates the 1957 WLS cohort from the 1994–95 Add Health cohort: helmet technology and training changed, and perceived safety may have encouraged more dangerous styles of play characterized by frequent sub-concussive impacts [PAGE 1]. Thus the study is embedded in a moving historical exposure regime; a null result in 1957 does not imply a null result in the 1990s, and a result in the 1990s may not extrapolate to the 2020s.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of analysts, clinicians, parents, and policymakers to revert to salient prior heuristics when a crisis becomes salient. In this domain, the crisis is the cluster of CTE diagnoses, suicides among collegiate players in their early 20s, and physician calls to ban youth tackle football [PAGE 1–2]. Under that salience, the default habit regresses to “football is inherently dangerous,” which can produce overinterpretation of noise as harm; the opposing regression, powered by the WLS null findings, defaults to “football is safe,” which can produce underinterpretation of real effects. Both regressions bypass the actual estimand.

The counter-trap is the study’s layered design intended to catch confounding before it catches the conclusion. First, multiple control groups—all controls, sport-playing controls, non-sport controls, and sport versus non-sport—systematically vary the unmeasured confounders associated with athletic identity, fitness, and temperament [PAGE 13–14]. If football players look worse than all controls but identical to sport controls, the signal may be “sports participation” rather than “football.” Second, the ordered testing procedure enforces a decision tree: test football versus all controls at α = 0.05; only upon rejection proceed to subgroup tests; stop on the first non-rejection [PAGE 13–14]. This prevents post-hoc cherry-picking among control groups and caps family-wise error. Third, Γ-sensitivity analysis acts as a second counter-trap for unmeasured confounding, forcing the analyst to state how strong a hidden bias must be to nullify the result [PAGE 10]. Fourth, BART covariance adjustment with permutation inference guards against functional-form misspecification that could manufacture or conceal an effect [PAGE 10–11].

The clock is the longitudinal timeline itself: Wave I covariates measured in 1994–95, treatment status recorded in adolescence, and CES-D outcome measured in 2008 during emerging adulthood [PAGE 3–4]. The clock imposes a temporal ordering that the cross-sectional CTE literature lacks, but it is interrupted by attrition (missing Wave IV) and by the fact that the exposure is only an intention-to-play indicator rather than a measured dose of head impacts [PAGE 6, 20]. A recursive analyst must therefore ask: under crisis pressure, did the decision-maker regress to a prior habit? If yes, which counter-trap (multiple controls, ordered stopping, sensitivity Γ, BART adjustment) was designed to catch that regression, and did the clock (attrition, era change, non-compliance) break before the counter-trap could close? The protocol attempts to lock inference ex ante via a pre-analysis plan, but the gray zones identified above—especially non-compliance and outcome missingness—mean the clock may have stopped before the counter-trap could verify the causal claim.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and meteorological veto proof.

Physical layer veto. The dataset contains no direct measure of actual football participation or exposure to football-related head trauma [PAGE 6]. The treatment variable records whether a subject “participated in or intended to participate” in school football; non-compliers are included in the treated group, and no information exists on position played, age of first exposure, or cumulative impact count [PAGE 6, 20]. Consequently, any physical-layer veto claiming that the estimated τ is the effect of playing football is invalid: the study at best identifies the intention-to-treat effect of indicated participation. The authors themselves state this limits applicability and generalizability [PAGE 6, 20].

Biomechanical layer veto. The hypothesized injury mechanism is repetitive sub-concussive head impacts, not diagnosed concussion alone [PAGE 1]. Add Health records no accelerometer data, helmet-sensor loads, impact counts, or neuroimaging, so the biomechanical dose—cumulative head-impact exposure—is unmeasured [PAGE 1, 20]. Recent work suggests a thresholded dose-response relationship between cumulative impacts and later-life depression and cognitive impairment, and that age of first exposure moderates later impairment [PAGE 20]. Because these variables are absent, the study cannot test whether a threshold exists, whether position-specific exposure patterns matter, or whether helmets and training changes altered the impact distribution across cohorts. The veto stands: without measured head-impact dose, a null CES-D result cannot exclude a harmful biomechanical effect concentrated in high-exposure subgroups, and a significant result cannot be attributed to impacts versus correlated athletic or socioeconomic factors.

Weather layer veto. The Add Health baseline covariate list [PAGE 26–33] includes demographics, family background, school experience, general health, substance use, violence exposure, suicidality, protective factors, feelings, personality, expectations, and school-climate items, but it contains no meteorological variables (temperature, precipitation, seasonal timing, regional climate). Because the sample is national and longitudinally followed, geographic climate could confound both football participation (regional football culture, season length) and mental-health outcomes (seasonal affective patterns, regional economic conditions), but no such adjustment is specified. The weather veto is therefore not addressed in the protocol and remains an open backdoor path.

Adjudication rule. If any veto is triggered—unknown actual play status, unmeasured head-impact dose, or regional climate confounding—the causal claim “playing football causes early-adulthood depression” is not identified from these data alone. The strongest defensible claim is an association between indicated adolescent football intention and Wave IV CES-D, conditional on observed covariates and under the assumptions of no unmeasured confounding (bounded by Γ), ignorable attrition, and constant additive effect. Any conclusion beyond that is vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios and predictions.

Veto scenario 1 — Missing-not-at-random attrition. Verifiable condition: Link Add Health respondents to National Death Index or disability records and test whether Wave IV non-response is associated with baseline football intention after adjusting for all Appendix A covariates; the authors’ Table/test already shows football is not a significant predictor of missingness conditional on baseline covariates [PAGE 7], but that only rules out observed-covariate-mediated missingness. Counterfactual prediction: If football-intention subjects who develop early neurocognitive impairment are differentially lost to Wave IV, the observed CES-D mean among football players will be biased upward (healthier survivors) or downward (if only the most affected remain), and the null finding may be a selection artifact rather than evidence of no effect.

Veto scenario 2 — Non-compliance / intention-to-treat dilution. Verifiable condition: Cross-walk the in-school “intended to play football” indicator against actual rosters, injury logs, or later-wave sport participation to estimate the compliance rate within the treated group. Counterfactual prediction: Because some treated subjects never played, the intention-to-treat effect |τ_ITT| is attenuated toward zero relative to the per-protocol effect |τ_PP|; thus a statistically null CES-D result is compatible with a harmful per-protocol effect masked by non-compliance, and the study’s power to detect the biological effect is reduced accordingly.

Veto scenario 3 — Unmeasured-confounding fragility. Verifiable condition: Run Rosenbaum sensitivity analysis (sensitivitymv for continuous CES-D; Mantel–Haenszel sensitivity for binary secondary outcomes) to locate the Γ value at which the primary result loses significance at α = 0.05 [PAGE 10]. Counterfactual prediction: If the critical Γ is close to 1 (i.e., a modest hidden bias suffices to explain away the association), the causal conclusion is fragile and should not be used to infer safety or risk; if the critical Γ is large, the result is robust to unmeasured confounding, but only under the assumption that the hidden confounder operates through treatment-assignment odds rather than through outcome misspecification or selection.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
