<THINK_TRACE>
The source paper must be read as a tracking-by-detection pipeline whose central claim is not merely that deep features are useful, but that pose-conditioned convolutional features from VGG-19 can replace several classical context cues while preserving competitive identity assignment under severe single-camera constraints. The first derivation hop is therefore the detection-to-pose bridge. The system detects the court first so that candidates outside active play are filtered, then detects players with a pretrained bottom-up pose model, and only then constructs bounding boxes from the minima and maxima of detected keypoint coordinates [PAGE 2] [PAGE 3]. This ordering matters because every later feature operator inherits the pose estimator’s failure modes: missing keypoints, low-confidence limbs, merged skeletons, and occluded players. The paper explicitly states that the pose output is a 25 by 3 vector per player, containing screen-coordinate positions and confidence scores [PAGE 2]. That representation is the semantic skeleton on which all later comparison operators are conditioned.

The second derivation hop is feature construction. Three families are evaluated: geometric centroid distance, visual color-patch distance, and deep-learning semantic features extracted from convolutional layers [PAGE 1] [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4]. The geometric operator is simple and physically interpretable: it assumes short-time motion is small, so centroids in adjacent frames are close in pixel coordinates. The color operator assumes that corresponding anatomical parts have similar local intensity neighborhoods, but it is fragile because it depends on jersey color, lighting, skin tone, and court background leakage. The deep-learning operator is more subtle: the authors crop a square patch around each player using the bounding-box height, resize to 224 by 224, pass it through VGG-19, downscale pose keypoints to the relevant convolutional-layer resolution, sample only at body-part locations, and L2-normalize the resulting feature vectors [PAGE 4]. This converts a full-frame appearance descriptor into a part-conditioned semantic vector, reducing background contamination.

The third derivation hop is matching. The tracker constructs a cost matrix from weighted feature distances and then performs a custom assignment procedure over frames N, N-1, and N-2 [PAGE 2] [PAGE 5]. The use of two previous frames is crucial: it gives the system a one-frame memory tolerance, allowing recovery if a player is missed in one frame [PAGE 5]. This is not a trivial temporal smoothing step; it changes the assignment problem from pairwise matching into a short-horizon identity continuation problem. The repeated-label decision rules also reveal a conservative behavior: if one association cost is much smaller than competing associations, the tracker commits; if costs are similar, it uses the largest gap between first and second minima to disambiguate [PAGE 5]. These rules are where many gray-zone failures occur, especially when two teammates wear similar jerseys, overlap, or cross paths.

The gray-zone audit must focus on the regimes where the paper’s own operators become non-identifiable. First, the dataset is constrained to static offensive basketball motion and explicitly excludes fast break or transition plays because camera stabilization does not handle those situations [PAGE 6]. This is a major boundary condition. The reported results are therefore not evidence for unrestricted broadcast basketball tracking; they are evidence for a narrower regime with limited camera motion, a single European court, and mostly half-court play. Second, the visual color operator assumes Euclidean color proximity in small neighborhoods, but the court-detection step itself relies on HSV color assumptions, such as hue values between 120 and 150 degrees in the illustrated case [PAGE 2]. This is not a universal court model; it is a context-specific filter. Third, the deep-feature operator depends on downsampling. At the chosen fourth block output, the spatial resolution is 28 by 28 for a 224 by 224 input [PAGE 4]. A 3 by 3 output neighborhood corresponds to a 24 by 24 input neighborhood, which can include court pixels and degrade discriminability [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7]. The paper’s empirical choice of a 2 by 2 neighborhood is therefore not aesthetic; it is an attempt to limit background contamination.

A fourth gray zone is part-level reliability. The ablation shows that torso-related parts such as chest, shoulders, and hips are much more discriminative than eyes, ears, wrists, heels, and mid-feet [PAGE 7]. The explanation is biomechanical and resolution-based: players’ eyes occupy at most a 5 by 5 pixel region in the dataset, making them nearly indistinguishable [PAGE 7]. This implies that the tracker’s identity signal is dominated by jersey-torso geometry rather than fine facial identity. A fifth gray zone is metric interpretation. MOTA aggregates false positives, misses, and mismatches, so a system can have high MOTA while still producing occasional catastrophic identity switches during congestion [PAGE 6]. The paper’s best reported value is 0.6843 when combining geometric, visual, and deep-learning features with stabilization, but the incremental gain of adding visual features to geometry plus VGG is only 0.2%, which the authors say comes at computational expense and is not worth it [PAGE 7] [PAGE 8]. This is a gray-zone tradeoff: marginal metric gain versus robustness and compute cost.

The final audit is the interaction between camera stabilization and geometric features. Stabilization improves geometric matching in controlled sequences because it reduces apparent global motion, but it is a homography-based assumption that the dominant image motion can be modeled by a planar projective transform [PAGE 2]. If the camera pans quickly, zooms, or follows transition play, the homography may fail or become unstable. The paper explicitly notes that stabilization techniques do not handle fast break situations [PAGE 6]. Therefore, any claim that the stabilized system generalizes beyond the dataset must be rejected unless tested on unrestricted motion. The deepest conclusion is that the tracker succeeds by suppressing background and motion ambiguity through pose-conditioned semantics, but its success is conditional on court layout, camera behavior, player density, jersey contrast, and frame rate. Those conditions are the real operators behind the reported numbers.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The following operators are extracted from the source and formalized with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Pose representation. The pose estimator returns, for each detected player, a keypoint tensor
\[
P_i(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{25 \times 3},
\]
where the first two channels are pixel coordinates in the image plane and the third channel is a confidence score in the dimensionless interval \([0,1]\) [PAGE 2]. The set of usable parts is defined by a confidence threshold:
\[
S(t_1,t_2)=\{k : c_k(t_1)\ge 0.3 \text{ and } c_k(t_2)\ge 0.3\}.
\]
This threshold is explicitly used by the paper to discard low-confidence parts before feature extraction [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: if \(S(t_1,t_2)=\varnothing\), all part-conditioned costs become undefined. If only one or two low-information parts remain, identity assignment becomes unstable.

2. Bounding-box construction. From detected part coordinates, the box is formed by extrema:
\[
B_t = \big[\min_k x_k(t),\min_k y_k(t),\max_k x_k(t),\max_k y_k(t)\big].
\]
Units: pixels. Assumption: detected parts sufficiently cover the visible player extent. Breakdown: severe occlusion removes torso or hip keypoints, causing truncated boxes and unreliable geometric centroids.

3. Geometric centroid distance. The paper defines
\[
C_d(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}) =
\frac{1}{\sqrt{w^2+h^2}}
\left\|x_{B_{t_1}}-x_{B_{t_2}}\right\|_2,
\]
where \(w\) and \(h\) are frame width and height in pixels, so \(C_d\) is dimensionless after pixel-distance normalization [PAGE 3]. Assumption: adjacent-frame player motion is small relative to frame diagonal. Breakdown: if inter-player separation is comparable to inter-frame displacement, the cost differences collapse. If \(w=h=0\), the operator is undefined; in real video this does not occur, but invalid frame metadata can create it.

4. Visual color similarity. Let \(E\) be a \(3\times3\) pixel neighborhood and \(I_t(p)\) the intensity vector at pixel \(p\) in frame \(t\). The color cost is
\[
C_c(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}) =
\frac{1}{255 |S| |E|}
\sum_{k\in S}\sum_{y\in E}
\left\|I_{t_1}(p_{k,1}+y)-I_{t_2}(p_{k,2}+y)\right\|.
\]
Units: normalized intensity distance, approximately dimensionless after division by 255 [PAGE 3]. Assumption: corresponding body parts have similar local color under stable illumination. Breakdown: if \(S=\varnothing\), undefined; if jersey colors are similar across teams, or lighting changes, the operator becomes non-discriminative.

5. VGG feature extraction. The network input is a square image
\[
X \in \mathbb{R}^{224\times224\times3},
\]
constructed by taking a square patch around the player center with side equal to the bounding-box height, then resizing to 224 by 224 [PAGE 4]. The selected convolutional output is block 4, convolutional layer 2:
\[
F_{b4c2} \in \mathbb{R}^{28\times28\times512}.
\]
For each part \(k\), the local feature vector is
\[
f_{t,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{512},
\]
then L2-normalized:
\[
\hat f_{t,k} = \frac{f_{t,k}}{\|f_{t,k}\|_2+\epsilon}.
\]
Units: dimensionless. Assumption: the convolutional feature is spatially approximately aligned with the downscaled keypoint. Breakdown: if \(f_{t,k}=0\), normalization is undefined without \(\epsilon\); if the keypoint lies outside the crop, the sampled feature is invalid.

6. Softmax similarity. The paper uses a normalized exponential similarity between feature vectors [PAGE 4]:
\[
\operatorname{Sim}(\hat f_{t_1,k},\hat f_{t_2,k}) =
\frac{\exp(\langle \hat f_{t_1,k},\hat f_{t_2,k}\rangle)}
{\sum_j \exp(\langle \hat f_{t_1,k},\hat f_{t_2,k}^{(j)}\rangle)}.
\]
The inner product is dimensionless because vectors are L2-normalized. The output is a dimensionless probability-like score. If interpreted as log-score before normalization, the natural unit is nats; if base-2 logarithms were used, the unit would be bits. Breakdown: denominator can underflow or overflow in naive floating-point implementation if scores are not range-limited; however, with L2-normalized vectors the dot product is bounded between -1 and 1, reducing numerical risk.

7. Part-conditioned deep cost. The total deep cost is
\[
C_{DL}(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}) =
\frac{1}{|S|}
\sum_{k\in S}
\max_{y_{t_1}\in E, y_{t_2}\in E'}
\operatorname{Sim}(\hat f_{t_1,k},\hat f_{t_2,k}).
\]
Units: dimensionless. Assumption: the maximum over local neighborhoods tolerates small keypoint misalignment. Breakdown: if \(|S|=0\), undefined; if neighborhoods contain court background, the max may select a court-court similarity rather than player-player similarity, as the paper notes for 3 by 3 neighborhoods [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7].

8. Weighted matching cost. The combined cost is
\[
C(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}) =
\alpha C_{\text{feat}1}(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}) +
(1-\alpha) C_{\text{feat}2}(B_{t_1},B_{t_2}),
\]
with \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) [PAGE 5]. Here \(C_{\text{feat}1}\) can be \(C_d\), and \(C_{\text{feat}2}\) can be \(C_{DL}\) or \(C_c\). Units: dimensionless if both component costs are dimensionless. Breakdown: if component costs have different scales or non-comparable saturation behavior, the weighted sum becomes arbitrary. The paper avoids this partly by normalizing costs, but validation is still required.

9. MOTA. The tracking accuracy metric is
\[
\mathrm{MOTA} =
1 -
\frac{\sum_t (FP_t + MT_t + MMT_t)}
{\sum_t GT_t}.
\]
Units: dimensionless. It can be negative if errors exceed ground truth instances. Breakdown: undefined if \(\sum_t GT_t = 0\). High MOTA does not guarantee absence of severe identity switches; it aggregates misses, false positives, and mismatches [PAGE 6].

10. MOTP. The precision metric is
\[
\mathrm{MOTP} =
\frac{\sum_{i,t} \mathrm{IoU}_{i,t}}
{\sum_t c_t}.
\]
Units: dimensionless. Breakdown: undefined if there are no correct assignments, i.e. \(\sum_t c_t=0\). The paper reports that MOTP barely changes across feature combinations because detections are fixed: 0.6165 plus or minus 0.0218 [PAGE 6].

11. Detection metrics. The detection table reports precision, recall, and F1-score [PAGE 6]:
\[
\mathrm{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},
\quad
\mathrm{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},
\quad
F_1 = \frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}\,\mathrm{Recall}}
{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\]
Units: dimensionless. Breakdown: precision undefined if no detections; recall undefined if no ground-truth objects; F1 undefined if both precision and recall are zero or undefined.

12. Camera stabilization as homography. The stabilization step estimates a homography per frame [PAGE 2]. In homogeneous image coordinates,
\[
\tilde x'_t \sim H_t \tilde x_t,
\quad
H_t \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3},
\quad
\operatorname{rank}(H_t)=3.
\]
The projective map is dimensionless up to scale. Assumption: dominant background motion is planar and can be compensated. Breakdown: fast camera motion, large panning, zoom changes, or independently moving players dominating the estimation region can make \(H_t\) unreliable. The paper states that the included sequences have no fast break or transition plays because stabilization does not handle those situations [PAGE 6].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of this paper is a convergence of four older research streams: tracking-by-detection, human pose estimation, convolutional appearance features, and classical court-camera geometry. The paper explicitly frames its method as “tracking by detection,” where potential players are detected and then compared across frames through feature similarity and assignment [PAGE 1]. This lineage descends from multi-object tracking literature that combines detectors with data association, rather than relying on motion-only trackers or background subtraction. The historical trap is to assume that a strong detector alone solves tracking. The paper shows that detection and tracking are separate operators: OpenPose has high detection F1, 0.9470, but tracking MOTA remains much lower, indicating that association is the limiting layer [PAGE 6].

The pose-estimation lineage is equally important. The method relies on a pretrained bottom-up pose model that detects anatomical keypoints, builds limbs, and merges them into skeletons [PAGE 2]. This follows the OpenPose and Convolutional Pose Machines tradition cited in the paper [PAGE 8]. The historical trap here is to treat keypoints as perfect evidence. The source warns that some parts may not be detected and that low-confidence parts must be discarded [PAGE 2] [PAGE 5]. Thus the tracker is not using pure body pose; it is using a filtered, confidence-gated subset of pose evidence. Any retro-causal reading that credits “pose” for the result must specify that the effective signal is the high-confidence torso and limb subset.

The deep-feature lineage comes from VGG-19 and ImageNet initialization [PAGE 4]. The paper does not train a tracking-specific network; it repurposes a classification backbone as a semantic appearance encoder. The historical trap is to assume that deeper features are always better. The paper’s ablation refutes that simplistic view: the fourth block is best among the tested layers, and the fifth block performs slightly worse [PAGE 6]. This indicates a mid-level semantic sweet spot, not a monotonic depth benefit. The retro-causal lesson is that the paper’s success depends on an ImageNet-pretrained representation whose features are generic but sufficiently discriminative for jersey-torso regions.

The court-detection and stabilization lineage is more classical. The court is detected using line segments and a contrario filtering, with color filtering in HSV space [PAGE 2]. Stabilization uses homography estimation [PAGE 2]. The historical trap is to assume that court normalization makes the problem domain-invariant. The paper’s own dataset restriction exposes the weakness: the sequences involve static offensive basketball motion and exclude fast break or transition plays because stabilization does not handle them [PAGE 6]. Therefore, the genealogy of the result includes a hidden selection filter: the method is retroactively valid for half-court, single-camera, low-motion sequences on a familiar court, not for unrestricted broadcast basketball.

Multi-week traps follow from this genealogy. The first trap is feature monoculture: teams may overfit to VGG features and ignore geometry, even though the best non-stabilized result improves when geometry and VGG are combined, reaching 0.6529 MOTA versus 0.6369 for VGG alone [PAGE 7]. The second trap is stabilization overtrust: stabilization improves geometric-only performance from 0.5689 to 0.6506 in the reported sequence, but that gain is conditional on camera behavior [PAGE 7]. The third trap is part overengineering: adding low-value parts can reduce performance, as the clustering table shows that using 20 parts yields 0.6423, using 12 parts yields 0.6412, and using all 25 parts yields 0.6369 [PAGE 8]. The fourth trap is metric complacency: MOTA can look stable while identity switches occur in congestion, because the metric aggregates multiple error types [PAGE 6].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis treats the tracker as an agent that maintains beliefs about player identities and updates those beliefs through a short temporal assignment process. The tracker does not model human intentions directly; it models identity continuity through cost minima. The “mind” of the tracker is therefore an assignment policy: for each bounding box at frame \(t_N\), it stores the two minimum association costs among boxes in \(t_{N-1}\), resolves repeated labels, repeats the procedure with \(t_{N-2}\), and keeps the minimum final match [PAGE 5]. This creates a recursive expectation: the identity at \(t_N\) is inferred from the best continuation among two temporal hypotheses.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the system falls back to older, simpler cues under stress. The classical geometric operator assumes that players remain near their previous screen positions. In crisis situations such as congestion, occlusion, or rapid direction changes, this habit becomes a regression trap: the tracker may assign identities based on proximity even when appearance evidence would be more reliable. The color operator is another crisis habit: it assumes that jersey and skin patches are stable, but similar team uniforms and lighting changes can make color regression dangerous. The paper’s ablation shows that color alone is weaker than VGG features by about a 3% MOTA margin in the tested combinations, and that adding color to geometry plus VGG gives only a 0.2% improvement [PAGE 7]. Thus the system’s older habits are not worthless, but they must be downweighted.

The counter-trap is the pose-conditioned deep-feature policy. By extracting VGG features only near detected body parts and using a 2 by 2 neighborhood around downscaled keypoints, the tracker avoids some background contamination and focuses on torso-shirt boundaries [PAGE 5] [PAGE 7]. The 2-frame memory is also a counter-trap mechanism: if a player is missed at \(t_{N-1}\), the tracker can still associate the box at \(t_N\) with the box at \(t_{N-2}\). The paper states that this introduces “2-frame memory tolerance” so that players lost for one frame can be recovered in the following one [PAGE 5]. This is a recursive stabilization rule: the identity model refuses to terminate a track merely because one frame lacks evidence.

The clock is the temporal bandwidth of the system. The dataset is sampled at 4 frames per second from original 25 frames per second video [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6]. This choice reduces computation but also limits the maximum observable motion between processed frames. At 4 Hz, adjacent-frame displacement is larger than at 25 Hz, so geometric assumptions become more fragile. The clock also defines the memory window: two previous frames, not long-term re-identification. If a player is occluded for more than the supported short interval, the tracker may generate a new label, as the final step says that unassigned boxes receive new labels [PAGE 5]. Therefore, the tracker’s recursive belief state is short-lived and metric-driven, not a long-term tactical identity model.

The recursive adversarial layer is the repeated-label rule. When multiple boxes claim the same previous label, the tracker compares first and second minima. If one repeated association is considerably smaller, by the paper’s 10% rule, it is selected; if costs are similar, the box with the largest difference between first and second minima is chosen [PAGE 5]. This is a conservative decision rule, but it can fail when two players have nearly identical feature vectors and similar distances. In that regime, the tracker’s theory of mind is ambiguous: it cannot distinguish which player is the true continuation. The correct recursive countermeasure is not to trust a single feature family but to require agreement across pose-conditioned deep features, geometry, and temporal memory, while rejecting stabilization assumptions when camera motion violates the planar homography model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto layers are decisive because they define where the tracker’s mathematical operators lose identifiability.

Physical veto: pixel resolution and spatial downsampling. The VGG feature extraction pipeline resizes a player patch to 224 by 224, and the selected fourth-block output has spatial size 28 by 28 [PAGE 4]. This implies an approximate spatial stride of 8 input pixels per output cell. The paper notes that a 3 by 3 neighborhood at this layer is equivalent to a 24 by 24 real neighborhood in the image domain, which can include court pixels and produce non-meaningful court-court matches [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7]. Therefore, if a body part occupies fewer pixels than the effective receptive sampling cell, its feature becomes unreliable. The paper gives the example of eyes: players’ eyes in the dataset images do not embrace more than a 5x5 pixel region, so they are not discriminative [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if a part’s visible pixel area is below the effective spatial resolution needed for semantic variation, tracking based on that part should be vetoed. This is not a stylistic preference; it is an information-theoretic limit.

Biomechanical veto: torso dominance and limb ambiguity. The part-wise ablation shows three clusters. The most discriminative parts are chest, shoulders, and hips, with chest at 0.5349 MOTA and shoulders near 0.47 MOTA [PAGE 7]. These regions coincide with the jersey-skin boundary and the main shape of the torso. The middle cluster includes elbows, knees, and ankles, which help but are not sufficient alone. The lowest cluster includes eyes, ears, heels, wrists, and mid-feet, many below 0.13 MOTA [PAGE 7]. Biomechanically, this means the tracker relies on stable trunk orientation and jersey geometry, not fine identity. Veto condition: if players turn away, compress into a scrum, or have torso regions occluded, the dominant discriminative features disappear. In that regime, limb features may not compensate because they are less reliable and more variable.

Environmental veto: camera motion and court color assumptions. The court-detection step uses line segments, parallel-line tests, and HSV color filtering; in the example, pixels with hue between 120 and 150 degrees satisfy the filter [PAGE 2]. This is a contextual assumption, not a universal court model. If the court surroundings, lighting, or jersey palette differ substantially, the pre-processing may filter incorrectly. The camera-stabilization layer assumes that a homography can remove camera motion. The paper explicitly excludes fast break and transition plays because stabilization does not handle those situations [PAGE 6]. Veto condition: if camera motion is non-planar, rapid, or dominated by player motion rather than court background, the homography residual will contaminate geometric costs. In that regime, geometric-only or stabilized-geometric tracking should be vetoed.

Weather is largely absent because the domain is indoor basketball, but the analogous environmental layer is illumination and broadcast color response. If lighting shifts, the visual color operator can fail because it compares raw intensity neighborhoods [PAGE 3]. If shadows or court reflections alter the appearance of jerseys, the color cost becomes unreliable. The veto proof is straightforward: the color operator assumes approximate photometric invariance; if that assumption is violated, the operator’s cost differences are no longer evidence of identity. The paper’s empirical result supports this: visual features are outperformed by VGG features by about 3% in the tested combinations, and the full combination of geometry, VGG, and visual features improves MOTA by only 0.2%, which is not worth the computation expense [PAGE 7]. Therefore, the cross-layer adjudication is: use pose-conditioned deep features plus geometry as the primary stack, use color only when photometric conditions are verified, and disable stabilization when camera motion violates the homography regime.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each scenario defines a pass/fail test and a prediction grounded in the source.

1. Fast-break stabilization veto.
Hypothesis: The claim that camera stabilization improves tracking cannot be generalized to fast break or transition basketball.
Test: Construct a held-out sequence containing fast breaks, large pannings, or full-court transitions. Run the stabilized geometric tracker and the non-stabilized geometric tracker on the same detections.
Prediction: Stabilization will not improve and may worsen identity assignment. The source says the dataset excludes fast break and transition plays because “camera stabilization techniques do not handle these situations” [PAGE 6]. Formal test statistic:
\[
\Delta_{\text{stab}} =
\mathrm{MOTA}_{\text{stab,geo}} -
\mathrm{MOTA}_{\text{nonstab,geo}}.
\]
Veto if \(\Delta_{\text{stab}} \le 0\) on transition-heavy content. The expected failure mechanism is that homography residuals increase apparent displacement, causing geometric cost matrices to associate players with the wrong nearby boxes. This veto is verifiable because it requires only MOTA comparison on a new sequence and does not depend on subjective judgment.

2. Color-similarity jersey collapse veto.
Hypothesis: Visual color features are not robust when jersey color contrast is low or illumination changes.
Test: Select sequences where opposing teams have similar jersey colors or where broadcast lighting creates strong color casts. Compare geometric plus visual tracking against geometric plus VGG tracking.
Prediction: Geometric plus VGG will outperform geometric plus visual by a margin consistent with the paper’s statement that “DL features outperform color ones by a 3% margin” in the tested settings [PAGE 7]. Formal test:
\[
\Delta_{\text{color}} =
\mathrm{MOTA}_{\text{geo+VGG}} -
\mathrm{MOTA}_{\text{geo+visual}}.
\]
Veto the use of visual color as a primary feature if \(\Delta_{\text{color}} > 0\) and identity switches concentrate in same-color player groups. The source’s color operator assumes local intensity similarity across corresponding keypoints [PAGE 3], so similar jerseys directly violate the discriminative assumption. This scenario is verifiable through controlled jersey-color contrast and frame-wise mismatch logging.

3. Low-information part veto.
Hypothesis: Including parts that are too small or weakly resolved reduces tracking performance.
Test: Run the tracker with all 25 parts, then with the subset of parts whose individual MOTA exceeds 0.20, then with the subset exceeding 0.35. Use the same detector, same 2 by 2 neighborhood, and no geometric combination, matching the paper’s clustering setup.
Prediction: The 12-part subset will outperform the all-25-part set, and the 6-part subset will not exceed the 12-part subset by much. The source reports total MOTA values of 0.6423 for 20 parts, 0.6412 for 12 parts, 0.6369 for all 25 parts, and 0.6105 for 6 parts [PAGE 8]. Formal test:
\[
\Delta_{\text{parts}} =
\mathrm{MOTA}_{>0.20} -
\mathrm{MOTA}_{\text{all}}.
\]
Veto the inclusion of low-value parts if \(\Delta_{\text{parts}} > 0\). The expected mechanism is that eyes, ears, heels, wrists, and mid-feet contribute noisy or non-discriminative features, as the paper explicitly notes for eyes [PAGE 7]. This veto is verifiable by part ablation and does not require retraining.

These three vetoes together enforce a conservative deployment rule: do not rely on stabilization for unrestricted camera motion, do not rely on color when jersey or lighting contrast is weak, and do not include body parts whose pixel-level information is below the discriminative threshold established by the ablation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
