When confronted with a spatial mismatch (e.g., reaching for an object that appears farther than expected), the human mind does not passively update its belief; it engages in a recursive game of hypothesis testing. The process begins with a **crisis habit regression**: under stress or high uncertainty, the system reverts to a deeply ingrained sensorimotor routine (e.g., a child-like “reach-and-grasp” pattern that relies heavily on vision). This regression is a safety mechanism—it sacrifices efficiency for robustness.  

However, if the crisis persists, the mind shifts to a **counter-trap strategy**. Here, it actively seeks disconfirming evidence to challenge its current model. For instance, a person might deliberately vary the speed or trajectory of their reach to test whether the discrepancy is due to faulty calibration or external interference (e.g., a slippery surface). This phase involves meta-cognitive monitoring: the prefrontal cortex evaluates the cost-benefit ratio of maintaining the old schema versus adopting a new one.  

The final arbiter is the **clock**—a timing mechanism that governs the decision to commit to a new model. If repeated trials yield consistent prediction errors, the clock accelerates, triggering rapid plasticity (e.g., LTP in cerebellar circuits). Conversely, if errors are intermittent, the clock decelerates, preserving the status quo. This dynamic explains why some illusions (like the Müller-Lyer) resist conscious correction even with full knowledge—they exploit the clock’s tendency to prioritize stability. In essence, the mind balances between being fooled by transient noise and being trapped by outdated priors, using time as a currency to purchase confidence in its representations.

This selection bias favors the hand, the primary tool for exploration, establishing it as the epistemic center of bodily space.

A complex, multi-scale body model would be computationally prohibitive for real-time action. This compression introduces systematic biases, which are then managed by hierarchical attention mechanisms.

Yet, humans rarely notice these errors because the system constantly corrects itself via landmarks and visual feedback.
