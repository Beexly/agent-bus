<THINK_TRACE>
The source describes a two-link visual tracking pipeline rather than a single end-to-end model. Link one is detection: the paper states that “The method is divided into two parts: detection and tracking” [PAGE 1], and that “the original YOLOv3 backbone network Darknet-53 is replaced by the lightweight backbone network MobileNetV2 to extract the target features” [PAGE 1]. Link two is tracking: a Kalman filter predicts the next center and bounding-box size, then a hierarchical data-association stage matches detections to existing tracks by appearance similarity first and motion similarity second, with an anomaly-correction branch for partial occlusion [PAGE 1, PAGE 5, PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. The causal chain is therefore: image preprocessing and convolutional feature extraction produce class and bounding-box outputs; the box center becomes the measurement; the Kalman filter supplies a prior for the next frame; the association cost matrix decides which detection updates which track; the correction equation adjusts the box center when the box aspect ratio changes abruptly.

The multi-hop failure analysis must start at the detector. YOLOv3 uses three scales and no pooling or fully connected layer, with the feature map reduced to one thirty-second of the input after five stride-2 stages: the source says “Three feature maps of different scales are output, namely 13×13, 26×26 and 52×52” [PAGE 3] and “after 5 sampling times, the feature map is 1/32 of the original video image” [PAGE 3]. For broadcast basketball, a distant player can occupy fewer than 32 pixels in width, so the stride-32 branch may not resolve the target. MobileNetV2 reduces parameters through depthwise separable convolution and inverted residuals [PAGE 4], but that efficiency gain can trade against small-object feature fidelity. Thus a detection miss is not a rare edge case; it is structurally induced by scale, occlusion, and motion blur.

The second hop is the Kalman assumption. The source sets the previous state as position and velocity, “where ... and v represent position and velocity respectively” [PAGE 5], and predicts the next state with a kinematic coefficient matrix and external control/noise terms [PAGE 5]. That is a constant-velocity or near-constant-velocity prior. Basketball players do not obey constant velocity: they jump, stop, cut, screen, and collide. During a screen or crowd occlusion, the measurement disappears or becomes a box fragment, so the predictor coasts on a stale velocity. The longer the occlusion, the larger the covariance and the more the predicted box drifts.

The third hop is association. The paper says “The appearance feature similarity and motion feature similarity mentioned above are described by cosine similarity” [PAGE 5]. The first layer uses appearance with the motion weight set to zero, and the second layer uses motion when appearance is below a confidence threshold [PAGE 6]. The gray-zone is that two same-jersey players can have high appearance similarity while crossing in opposite directions; motion-only association can then swap identities if the velocity vectors are ambiguous. The cost matrix is built from a similarity function, with infinite cost below threshold [PAGE 6]. If every candidate pair falls below threshold, the matrix has no finite assignment, and the track either dies or spawns a new ID.

The fourth hop is the correction branch. The source says “The algorithm uses the characteristic that the target frame will not change suddenly during the tracking process to correct the frame and trajectory” [PAGE 7], and it infers occlusion side from the sign of the center shift: “If Δy is positive, the lower body of the target is considered to be occluded” [PAGE 7]. This is a strong physical assumption. A player raising both arms in celebration or contesting a shot changes the bounding-box height without occlusion. A jump changes the center in the y direction without lower-body occlusion. Therefore the correction branch is only valid under a narrow camera and posture regime.

The largest gray-zone is quantitative reproducibility. Table 1 reports center position error in pixels [PAGE 9], while Figure 4 reports “Error /cm” and states that occluded error is “0.15-0.45cm” and non-occluded error is “0.05-0.25cm” [PAGE 8]. Those units cannot be compared without a camera homography and lens calibration, neither of which is reported. The runtime is also decisive: “the average computation time of each frame is about 96ms” [PAGE 9], which is about 10.4 serial frame computations per second. That is not 30 fps or 60 fps real-time for a full broadcast pipeline. The paper’s “real-time performance” claim [PAGE 9] is therefore conditional on downsampling, frame skipping, or parallel hardware not described in the excerpt. Finally, the experiment uses one CBA game and 100 frames [PAGE 9], with thresholds selected “After the experiment” [PAGE 6], so there is no evidence of multi-week or multi-arena generalization. The result is a plausible short-clip demonstration, not a validated long-duration tracking system.

G_{i,j,n}=\sum_{k,l,m}K_{k,l,m,n}\,F_{i+k-1,\,j+l-1,\,m}.
\]
Here \(F\) is the input feature map with spatial size \(Z_F\times Z_F\) and \(M\) channels; \(K\) is the kernel with spatial size \(Z_K\times Z_K\), input channels \(M\), and output channels \(N\); \(G\) is the output with spatial size \(Z_C\times Z_C\) and \(N\) channels. The indices are dimensionless integers. The source’s Eq. (2) for standard convolution computation is OCR-damaged, but the standard reading is:
\[
\Omega_{\mathrm{std}}=Z_K Z_K Z_F Z_F M N.
\]
For depthwise convolution, Eq. (3) [PAGE 4] is:
\[
\hat{G}_{i,j,m}=\sum_{k,l}\hat{K}_{k,l,m}\,F_{i+k-1,\,j+l-1,\,m}.
\]
Depthwise plus pointwise convolution is given by Eq. (4) [PAGE 4] in the form:
\[
\hat{\Omega}=Z_K Z_K Z_F Z_F M+Z_F Z_F M N.
\]
The source says “the total calculation amount ... is reduced” [PAGE 4], but the OCR of the reduction factor is corrupted. Algebraically, the exact ratio is
\[
\frac{\hat{\Omega}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{std}}}
=
\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{Z_K^2},
\]
under the assumptions of unit stride, same spatial output size, and dense channel use. This ratio is not a toy heuristic; it follows from the printed convolution sums. Breakdown regimes: if \(N\to 0\) or \(Z_K=0\), the expression is invalid; if \(Z_K=1\), depthwise separation gives no spatial-kernel saving; if \(M\) or \(N\) is small, the efficiency gain is limited. If padding is absent, the spatial dimensions shrink and the printed same-size assumption fails.

Kalman prediction is Eq. (5) [PAGE 5]:
\[
\hat{x}_k^- = F_k\hat{x}_{k-1}+B_k u_k,
\]
\[
\Phi_k^- = F_k\Phi_{k-1}F_k^{T}+Q_k.
\]
The source writes the previous state as position and velocity, with “position and velocity respectively” [PAGE 5]. The detection output is \(Q_k\) with covariance \(R_k\) [PAGE 5]. The update is Eq. (6) [PAGE 5]:
\[
\hat{x}_k=\hat{x}_k^-+K_k\left(Q_k-H_k\hat{x}_k^-\right),
\]
\[
\Phi_k=\left(I-K_kH_k\right)\Phi_k^-,
\]
\[
K_k=\Phi_k^-H_k^{T}\left(H_k\Phi_k^-H_k^{T}+R_k\right)^{-1}.
\]
Units: positions are pixels, velocities are pixels per frame, covariance entries are pixels squared or mixed position-velocity units, and the gain is dimensionless only for the position block. The exact singularity is the inverse in the gain: if \(H_k\Phi_k^-H_k^{T}+R_k\) is singular or numerically near-singular, the gain diverges. This can occur with duplicate detections, unobservable velocity states, or a collapsed covariance. The source does not report regularization, so this is a real breakdown regime.

Appearance similarity is Eq. (7) [PAGE 5]. The OCR loses the operator, but the text states cosine similarity [PAGE 5], so the intended form is:
\[
E_{i,j}^{t}=
\frac{\left\langle f_i^{t},f_j^{t}\right\rangle}
{\left\|f_i^{t}\right\|_2\left\|f_j^{t}\right\|_2},
\]
where \(f_i^{t}\) and \(f_j^{t}\) are appearance embeddings. This is dimensionless in \([-1,1]\), usually clipped to \([0,1]\). Breakdown: if either embedding has zero norm, the denominator vanishes; an epsilon is required.

Motion similarity is Eq. (8) [PAGE 6]. The printed equation is OCR-corrupted. The paper says “the modified similarity is considered to represent the similarity of the target direction and speed” [PAGE 6], so the operator is a direction-plus-speed similarity, not a pure cosine. A conservative audit proxy is:
\[
M_{i,j}^{t}
=
\frac{\left\langle v_i^{t},v_j^{t}\right\rangle}
{\left\|v_i^{t}\right\|_2\left\|v_j^{t}\right\|_2}
\cdot
\left(1-\frac{\left\|v_i^{t}-v_j^{t}\right\|_2}
{\max\left(\left\|v_i^{t}\right\|_2,\left\|v_j^{t}\right\|_2,\epsilon\right)}\right).
\]
This proxy is labeled as an audit reconstruction, not as the exact source equation. Breakdown: stationary players give zero velocity norms and undefined direction; two parallel runners give high direction similarity even if their identities are semantically distinct.

The cost matrix is Eq. (9) [PAGE 6]:
\[
c_{i,j}^{t}=
\begin{cases}
-f_{i,j}^{t}, & f_{i,j}^{t}\ge \tau,\\
\infty, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(\tau\) is the confidence threshold. The affinity is Eq. (10) [PAGE 6]:
\[
f_{i,j}^{t}=w_1E_{i,j}^{t}+w_2M_{i,j}^{t}.
\]
The first layer sets appearance weight to 1 and motion weight to 0; the second layer is described as setting appearance to 0.4 and motion to 0.6 [PAGE 6]. The text has an OCR/typographical inconsistency between \(w_1\), \(w_2\), and \(\omega_2\), so the exact symbol naming should not be over-trusted. Breakdown: if all \(f_{i,j}^{t}<\tau\), every cost is infinite and no valid assignment exists.

The trajectory-correction equation is Eq. (11) [PAGE 7]. The OCR preserves the structure of a signed half-extent correction but not the exact sign assignment:
\[
U_j^{y}(T)=U_j^{y}(T-1)\pm\frac{\Delta h}{2},
\]
\[
U_j^{x}(T)=U_j^{x}(T-1)\pm\frac{\Delta w}{2}.
\]
The source justifies this by “the characteristic that the target frame will not change suddenly” [PAGE 7] and by body-side occlusion inference [PAGE 7]. Units are pixels. Breakdown: a jump, arm raise, or camera zoom changes \(\Delta h\) or \(\Delta w\) without occlusion; then the sign rule corrects in the wrong direction. The source does not report the sign-selection function, so this equation is not fully reproducible from the excerpt.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is clear. The paper descends from the two-stage R-CNN family and the one-stage YOLO family: the text contrasts two-stage proposal-then-classify detection with single-stage regression and says the single-stage design “greatly improves the detection speed” [PAGE 3]. YOLOv3 inherits Feature Pyramid Network multi-scale prediction, with “13×13, 26×26 and 52×52” feature maps [PAGE 3]. The lightweight branch descends from MobileNet-style depthwise separable convolution and MobileNetV2’s “linear bottlenecks and reverse residuals” [PAGE 4]. The tracking branch descends from Kalman-filter-based multi-object tracking, where a constant-velocity prior is paired with appearance or motion association; the paper’s hierarchical two-layer association is a SORT/DeepSORT-like response to occlusion. The anomaly-correction branch descends from box-geometry heuristics: when a bounding box changes aspect ratio, infer partial occlusion and move the center back toward the prior.

The retrocausal problem is that the paper’s reported thresholds are selected after seeing the experiment. The text says “After the experiment, the target appearance influence factor ... is set to 0.4, and the motion influence factor ... is set to 0.6” [PAGE 6]. That is a post hoc tuning loop: the evaluation criterion helps choose the weights that are then used to report the evaluation. Without a separate validation split, the reported improvement is not a clean out-of-sample estimate. The same retrocausal risk applies to the anomaly-correction threshold \(\mu\) and to the confidence threshold \(\tau\), neither of which is selected on an independent arena or season.

The multi-week traps are more severe than the short-clip results suggest. First, the experiment uses “a CBA game” and “100 frames” [PAGE 9], not a multi-week season. Identity drift, jersey changes, arena lighting changes, camera cuts, replay inserts, and broadcast overlays are not tested. Second, occlusion duration is not swept. A tracker that works for a 5-frame occlusion can fail for a 50-frame occlusion because the Kalman covariance grows and the appearance embedding goes stale. Third, the paper only tracks the same category of target; the conclusion explicitly says “the current research is still carried out on the same type of target” [PAGE 9]. Cross-category association, referee/player confusion, and ball/player confusion remain open. Fourth, the reported 96 ms per frame [PAGE 9] means a multi-week deployment would need frame dropping or hardware parallelization; otherwise the temporal prior is updated at a different cadence than the broadcast clock. Fifth, the pixel-versus-centimeter inconsistency [PAGE 8, PAGE 9] means any claimed physical accuracy cannot be audited across venues without a homography. The historical lineage therefore supports the architecture, but it does not support the strong long-duration accuracy claim.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In algorithmic terms, the tracker has a recursive belief about each target: it predicts the next center and box size, then updates that belief from the detector. The paper’s own recursion is the Kalman loop: the new estimate is fed into the next prediction and update equation [PAGE 5]. The recursive theory-of-mind question is what happens when the tracker’s belief about a player conflicts with the player’s actual intent. Basketball is an adversarial multi-agent scene: a screener intentionally occupies a defender’s line of sight, a cutter changes direction to lose a marker, and two same-jersey players can cross to confuse a broadcast camera. The tracker does not model the opponent’s intent; it only models kinematic continuity. That is the central mismatch.

Crisis habit regression appears when appearance confidence collapses. The first association layer is appearance-only with motion weight zero [PAGE 6]. Under occlusion, appearance similarity falls below the confidence threshold, so the system regresses to its habitual fallback: motion similarity. If two players run parallel, motion similarity is high even when the correct match is the other player. If they cross in opposite directions, motion similarity is low and the track may be dropped. The regression is therefore not a psychological metaphor; it is a mathematically forced switch from a high-dimensional appearance cue to a low-dimensional velocity cue exactly when the scene is most ambiguous.

The counter-trap is a deliberate occlusion pattern that exploits this switch. A screen creates a short appearance gap; the motion-only layer then associates the emerging detection with the wrong track if the velocities are similar. The correction branch can amplify the error because it assumes the box “will not change suddenly” [PAGE 7]. A player who jumps or raises an arm violates that assumption without any occlusion. The counter-trap prediction is that identity switches should cluster at screens, jumps, and same-jersey crossings, not at isolated single-player occlusions. That prediction is testable with ID-switch logs and box-aspect-ratio traces.

The clock is the third recursive constraint. The tracker’s belief is updated once per processed frame, but the paper reports “about 96ms” per frame [PAGE 9]. At 96 ms, a player moving at 5 m/s displaces about 0.48 m between updates. A one-step Kalman prediction is therefore a substantial extrapolation. If the broadcast is 30 fps, the frame interval is 33.3 ms; the pipeline is slower than the clock, so it must skip frames or lag. The recursive belief and the physical clock are then out of phase. The paper’s “real-time” claim [PAGE 9] is valid only for a clock slower than about 10.4 fps. Under a faster clock, the motion prior dominates and the association becomes more dependent on prediction than on measurement. This is the regime where crisis habit regression and clock lag reinforce each other: stale appearance, faster motion, and a motion-only fallback produce exactly the ID-switch pattern the paper does not measure.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The paper’s physical model is a monocular bounding box plus a constant-velocity prior. There is no depth sensor, no camera calibration, and no court homography. The source reports center error in pixels in Table 1 [PAGE 9] but also reports “0.15-0.45cm” in Figure 4 [PAGE 8]. A centimeter claim requires a pixel-to-meter mapping; without it, the physical unit is not defined. The veto is therefore partial: the 2D pixel-tracking claim survives as a computer-vision claim, but the centimeter-level physical-accuracy claim does not. The occlusion geometry is also view-dependent: a box can shrink because a player is occluded, or because the player turns, crouches, or moves away from the camera. The correction rule that maps positive \(\Delta y\) to lower-body occlusion [PAGE 7] is not physically identifiable from a monocular box alone.

Biomechanical veto. The paper infers body-side occlusion from bounding-box center shifts and height/width changes [PAGE 7]. That is not a biomechanical model. A jump changes the center in the y direction; an arm raise changes the box height; a defensive crouch changes both height and width; a player turning sideways changes the aspect ratio without occlusion. The source says “If Δy is positive, the lower body of the target is considered to be occluded” [PAGE 7], but a positive Δy is equally consistent with a jump, a camera tilt, or a detection-box regression error. The veto is therefore strong for any claim that the method identifies the occluded body part. It is weaker for the narrower claim that the box center is adjusted toward a smoother trajectory.

Weather veto. The source is a CBA indoor basketball dataset [PAGE 7, PAGE 9]; there is no weather variable, no outdoor venue, and no rain, wind, or sunlight metadata. Weather is therefore a neutral layer for this paper, not a supporting layer. If the method were extrapolated outdoors, weather would become a veto because rain droplets, wind-blown clothing, and changing sunlight would alter appearance embeddings and box stability. For the indoor CBA claim, the correct adjudication is “not applicable,” not “proven robust.” The paper also does not report arena lighting, shadow, or broadcast-overlay conditions, so even the indoor physical layer is under-specified. The cross-layer proof is thus: the architecture is plausible for short indoor clips, but the physical-unit, biomechanical, and weather-extrapolation claims are either unsupported or explicitly out of scope.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: same-jersey parallel crossing with occlusion. Intervention: take the reported CBA clip and insert a controlled crossing where two players in the same jersey color pass within one bounding-box width, with appearance similarity forced below the first-layer confidence threshold and motion vectors nearly parallel. Prediction: the first layer fails, the second layer uses motion, and the parallel velocity similarity produces an identity swap or a merged track. Veto condition: if the method records more than one identity switch per crossing, or if occlusion coverage falls below the reported 51.3% lower bound in Table 2 [PAGE 9], the paper’s occlusion-robustness claim is falsified for the tested regime. A verifiable log is the track-ID sequence before, during, and after the crossing, plus the cost-matrix finite-entry count.

Scenario 2: clock-rate counterfactual. Intervention: replay the same 100-frame sequence at 30 fps and at 60 fps without changing the model or hardware, and measure end-to-end latency per frame. Prediction: because the paper reports “about 96ms” per frame [PAGE 9], the serial pipeline cannot meet a 33.3 ms or 16.7 ms frame budget. The prediction is that frames are dropped or the tracker lags the broadcast clock. Veto condition: if the measured per-frame latency remains above 33.3 ms at 30 fps, the “real-time performance” claim [PAGE 9] is vetoed for conventional broadcast rates. This is verifiable with timestamps and frame-drop counts; it does not require a new dataset.

Scenario 3: pixel-to-centimeter calibration audit. Intervention: reconstruct the camera homography from court lines, convert the reported pixel center errors from Table 1 [PAGE 9] into centimeters, and compare with the Figure 4 “Error /cm” range [PAGE 8]. Prediction: without a reported homography, the centimeter numbers are not reproducible; with a homography, the converted error will differ by a scale factor that depends on player depth. Veto condition: if the reported 0.30 cm average error cannot be reproduced within the stated 0.15-0.45 cm band after calibration, or if the pixel error maps to a subpixel displacement that is smaller than the detector’s localization noise, the physical-accuracy claim is vetoed. This is verifiable from the image geometry and the reported tables, and it directly tests the unit consistency that the paper leaves unresolved.
