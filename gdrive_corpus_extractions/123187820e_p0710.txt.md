When the prefrontal, reflective layer is overwhelmed, the system regresses to the fastest available chain—muscular and sensorial reflexes distinguished by Lange at I. 92 [PAGE 700]—producing stereotyped, automatic responses rather than novel deliberation.

The counter-trap is the psychologist’s fallacy, which the index sends the reader to look up (“see Fallacy” [PAGE 700]). This is the error of the observer who, knowing the objective stimulus and the correct response, assumes the subject experiences the same intentional object. James blocks this recursion by insisting that reaction time “what it measures is not conscious thought, 90” [PAGE 700]. The clock measures a physical latency, not the phenomenal content; therefore, any theory of mind that equates the two doubles-counts the observer’s interpretation inside the subject’s mind.

The clock itself is a recursive object. Reaction time indexes the “specious present” (“Present, the present moment, I. 606 ff.” [PAGE 700]) against the laboratory millisecond. The mind uses its own duration to measure duration; the psychologist uses reaction time to infer mental operations that James explicitly denies are conscious. The recursion closes dangerously when the experimenter assumes that shortening T_dec under “expectant attention, 427 ff.” [PAGE 700] means the subject “knows” what is coming, when in fact the Bayesian prior P(o) may have shifted without any access to conscious expectation. Crisis habit regression, the psychologist’s fallacy counter-trap, and the reaction-time clock thus form a loop: stress strips the mind to reflex chains; the observer risks projecting conscious meaning onto those chains; and the clock records only the bodily latency, remaining silent about the phenomenal layer that the observer is most tempted to reconstruct.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three layers must be adjudicated before any cognitive claim derived from this index can stand: the physical/neural layer, the biomechanical layer, and the ambient/environmental layer (here treated as the “weather” layer, since no literal meteorology appears in the leaf, but “Practical interests” and “Prayer” index the broader context in which the organism is embedded).

Physical layer. “PFLU, on frog’s spinal cord, I. 9,134” [PAGE 700] references Pflüger’s work on spinal reflexes, establishing that the lowest layer is a reflex arc independent of cortex. Any model of perception or decision that omits spinal and brainstem transmission is physically under-specified.

Biomechanical layer. “Lange’s distinction between muscular and sensorial, 92” [PAGE 700] separates afferent sensory conduction from efferent motor execution. Let τ_syn be synaptic delay (~0.5–5 ms), τ_axon be axonal conduction time (distance / conduction velocity; for human peripheral motor axons roughly 50–70 m·s⁻¹, so τ_axon scales with limb length), and τ_muscle be electromechanical delay (~30–100 ms). The total reaction time must satisfy:
T_total ≥ τ_syn + τ_axon + τ_muscle.
This inequality is a hard biomechanical veto: no central decision process can make T_total shorter than the sum of peripheral transmission and contraction latencies. If a measured T violates this bound, the data are censored or the apparatus is faulty, not the subject “fast.”

Ambient/environmental layer. “Practical interests, their effects on discrimination, I. 515 ff.” [PAGE 700] and “Play, II. 427” [PAGE 700] index context as a modulator of perception. Although “weather” is not indexed, ambient thermal load, illumination, and noise act as environmental inputs that shift P(o) in the perception rule and alter R(t) in the habit equation. Extreme heat or cold changes conduction velocity and muscle contractility, tightening the biomechanical bound; high noise raises the effective threshold I₀ in the psycho-physic law.

Veto adjudication. When layers conflict, the most restrictive binding constraint wins. A central “attention” effect cannot veto the refractory limit of a motor neuron; a perceptual “probability” effect cannot veto the absolute threshold of a sensory receptor. The index entry “Reaction-time … what it measures is not conscious thought, 90” [PAGE 700] functions precisely as such a veto: it forbids inferring conscious content from a latency that is already bounded by spinal, axonal, and muscular physics.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios. Each names a scenario, a counterfactual intervention, the veto condition, and a falsifiable prediction.

Scenario 1: Peripheral conduction block vs. expectant attention.
Index basis: “Reaction-time … influenced by expectant attention, 427 ff.” [PAGE 700] and “Lange’s distinction between muscular and sensorial, 92” [PAGE 700]. Counterfactual: reversibly anesthetize the afferent sensory nerve or efferent motor nerve in the response limb while keeping the central expectant-attention manipulation intact. Veto condition: if peripheral transmission is blocked, T → ∞ (no response) regardless of attentional state. Prediction: the hazard function λ(t) for response emission drops to zero during the block; the claim that attention alone modulates T is falsified because the biomechanical inequality T_total ≥ τ_syn + τ_axon + τ_muscle is violated at the upper bound (τ_axon or τ_muscle becomes undefined/infinite). Verifiable via surface EMG and nerve-conduction latency measurements.

Scenario 2: Uniform prior vs. probability-determined perception.
Index basis: “Probability determines what object shall be perceived, II. 82, 104, 258, 260-3” [PAGE 700]. Counterfactual: present a set of N objects with physically identical stimulus energy but enforce a uniform prior P(o) = 1/N across trials, removing base-rate differences. Veto condition: if perception still shows a stable asymmetry in object choice despite equal priors and equal sensory evidence, James’s rule is falsified. Prediction: forced-choice frequency for each object should converge to 1/N within sampling error (binomial confidence interval); any systematic deviation above 1/N + 3σ indicates an uncontrolled prior or likelihood asymmetry, vetoing the simple probability-determination claim. Verifiable via a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) psychophysical task with trial counts sufficient for binomial power.

Scenario 3: Interference-induced habit decay vs. monotonic plasticity.
Index basis: “Plasticity, as basis of habit, defined, I. 105” [PAGE 700] and “concatenated habits are constituted by a chain of [reflex acts]” [PAGE 700]. Counterfactual: train a habit to asymptote H ≈ 1 using repetition rate R(t), then introduce a competing, interfering task for a matched duration without further practice of the original habit. Veto condition: if H does not decay after interference, the simple consolidation model dH/dt = α·R(t)·(1−H)−β·H with constant β is incomplete. Prediction: habit strength should follow a decay phase dH/dt = −γ·I(t)·H during interference, where I(t) is the interference rate (events·s⁻¹) and γ > 0, yielding H(t) = H₀·exp(−γ∫I·dt). Measurable via response automaticity (error rate and latency) on the original task after the interference interval. Verifiable within a single experimental session using a standard proactive/interference protocol.
