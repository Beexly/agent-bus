$$ I_t(\mathcal{S}; \mathcal{R}) = H_t(\mathcal{R}) - H_t(\mathcal{R} | \mathcal{S}) $$

$$ \delta_t = r_t - V_{t-1}(\mathcal{S}) $$

Following a Bayesian update (or Rescorla-Wagner rule), the new value of the secondary cue $V_t(\mathcal{S})$ decreases proportionally to the negative error:
$$ \Delta V_t(\mathcal{S}) = \alpha \cdot \delta_t $$

The historical lineage of this phenomenon traces back to the early 20th-century debate over behaviorism, deeply rooted in the work of Ivan Pavlov. Pavlov’s discovery of "higher-order conditioning" demonstrated that a neutral stimulus could acquire reinforcing properties merely by being paired with an already conditioned stimulus. However, as noted on [PAGE 92], Pavlov encountered severe limits when attempting to establish "third and fourth order conditioned reflexes." The further the chain of associations stretched from the original unconditioned stimulus (UCS)—the meat powder triggering salivation—the more fragile the response became.

Clark L. Hull extended this genealogical line by formalizing these observations into a deductive, mathematical system of drive reduction theory. Hull posited that all behavior is fundamentally motivated by biological drives (hunger, thirst), and secondary reinforcements are simply symbolic placeholders for the ultimate reduction of those drives. Edward Tolman and others (like Grindley, whose data is cited on [PAGE 92]) introduced cognitive maps, suggesting animals learn contingencies and expectancies rather than just blind S-R bonds. Grindley’s chickens did not simply freeze; they "gained in speed nearly as fast as did the rewarded ones" initially, indicating a robust cognitive map was formed, which subsequently collapsed under the weight of violated expectancy.

In modern neuroscience, this genealogy crystallizes into the dopamine hypothesis of incentive salience ("wanting" vs. "liking"). The visual cue (rice) triggers dopaminergic firing in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), driving locomotion. When the primary reward is consistently omitted, the dopaminergic response shifts from the reward itself to the earliest available cue, and then eventually extinguishes entirely if the cue never predicts reward again. The "multi-week trap" emerges when an individual attempts to rely exclusively on high-order conditioned stimuli (abstractions, charts, narratives) without periodically verifying them against ground-truth primary reinforcers (actual physiological well-being, deep human connection, tangible progress).
