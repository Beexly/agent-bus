<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The STAMP lineage runs from spatial point-process shot-chart models to areal Poisson GLMMs. The paper positions itself against log-Gaussian Cox process (LGCP) models: LGCPs model log-intensity over continuous space as a Gaussian process, often discretized [PAGE 2]. The source says that when marks such as shot type are introduced, “the continuous field can absorb variation that should instead be attributed to those categories” [PAGE 2]. That is the retrocausal hinge: earlier continuous-space models explained spatial variation with a smooth latent field; STAMP instead pre-partitions the court into areal categories and assigns categorical shot-type effects. The model is therefore a deliberate coarsening: it gives up within-region \((x,y)\) resolution to gain replication, interpretability, and INLA scalability. The paper cites prior shot-chart work including Reich et al. (2006), Miller et al. (2014), Jiao et al. (2021), and Wong-Toi et al. (2023) [PAGE 2–3, PAGE 21–24]. Its methodological ancestors are Poisson GLMMs, latent Gaussian models, INLA [PAGE 3], and PC priors [PAGE 16].

The historical trap is that each ancestor carries a different default interpretation. Continuous point-process models treat intensity as a spatial field; areal GLMMs treat region counts as exchangeable categories; INLA treats the latent field as Gaussian and the hyperparameters as low-dimensional; PC priors treat variance and correlation as penalized complexity. STAMP combines all four, so a claim that a region is “hot” can mean a fixed area effect, a team×area interaction, a shot-type slope, a side effect, or a season correlation. The paper’s factorial comparison of 16 structures is an attempt to disentangle these components [PAGE 5–6], but the components are not orthogonal in small samples. The full model wins by roughly \(3.2\times 10^3\) \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}\) over the baseline [PAGE 6], yet the no-team×side model is only two units worse and has much better LPML [PAGE 6]. That is a retrocausal warning: the “full” model is not uniquely selected by predictive evidence; it is selected by a combination of predictive score, interpretability, and prior stability.

Multi-week traps that would take repeated seasons to detect include: (a) nonstationarity of team style after roster turnover, which the equicorrelation prior may smooth away; (b) the shot-type taxonomy, which merges action labels manually and is “inevitably somewhat ad hoc” [PAGE 12–13]; (c) the possession parser, because the paper adopts a “shot-terminated definition of possession: a possession ends at each field goal attempt, and an offensive rebound begins a new possession” [PAGE 13]; (d) the distinction between regular-season fitting and post-season evaluation, where post-season is only 3.16% of shots [PAGE 17]; (e) the absence of outcome modeling, since the paper “focuses exclusively on shot frequency rather than shot outcomes or efficiency” [PAGE 12]; and (f) the absence of defender, play-type, and tracking context [PAGE 12]. A multi-week replication should therefore freeze the possession parser, pre-register the shot-type merge, and evaluate across at least three seasons before treating left-right bias as a stable team trait.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In decision terms, STAMP is a model of what a team habitually does, not a model of what a team will do after it knows it is being modeled. That distinction creates a recursive theory-of-mind problem. A coach sees a percentile map showing a hot region; an opponent sees the same map and adjusts defense; the next season’s shot chart is no longer generated by the same policy. The model’s season-specific team×side effects and season-correlated team×area effects partially absorb this drift [PAGE 14–15], but the likelihood has no strategic interaction term. The “crisis habit regression” hypothesis is that under post-season pressure teams revert to habitual shot types and areas. STAMP can test a weak version of this: if post-season shot charts are more predictable from regular-season team×area effects, then \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}_{\mathrm{MC,post}}\) should improve for models with team×area and shot-type slopes. The paper indeed finds the largest gains from team×area interactions and jump/step slopes [PAGE 6], but it does not test pressure, score margin, or game clock, so the behavioral mechanism is underidentified.

The counter-trap is to avoid interpreting Bayesian shrinkage as tactical regression. A team with a hot region in a small sample will be shrunk toward the league baseline by the hierarchical prior; that shrinkage can look like “regression to the mean” even if the team’s policy is unchanged. The correct counter-trap is to compare posterior predictive distributions and held-out season ranks, not raw percentile movement. The paper’s use of LPML alongside \(\widehat{\mathrm{elpd}}\) is a step in this direction [PAGE 18–19], but LPML is an in-sample fit criterion and can reward overparameterized models differently from elpd. The extended-slope table makes this vivid: some models achieve slightly better post-season elpd but LPML on the order of \(-5\times 10^5\), which the paper flags as overfitting [PAGE 9–10]. A recursive analyst should therefore treat the “best” post-season score as one vote, not a verdict.

The clock layer is discrete season, not game clock. STAMP’s temporal structure is \(s=1,\dots,S\) with equicorrelation for team×area and shot-type slopes, and season-specific team×side effects [PAGE 14–15]. It does not model within-game time, possession duration, shot-clock pressure, or late-game urgency. Thus “crisis” in the sense of late-game shot selection is outside the model. A counter-trap is to use the season clock correctly: fit on regular season, evaluate on post-season [PAGE 17], and avoid tuning on the post-season because that destroys the out-of-sample claim. The paper’s prior sensitivity analysis, with Kendall’s \(\tau\) above 0.97 across prior configurations [PAGE 7], supports robustness of the ranking, but it does not solve the strategic-feedback problem. The theory-of-mind conclusion is: STAMP is a useful diagnostic of revealed spatial habit, but it cannot certify a team’s “true” preference once opponents and the team itself react to the model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is straightforward to state formally. Let \(P\) denote physical environment variables (arena, travel, schedule, HVAC, weather), \(B\) denote biomechanical/player-state variables (handedness, fatigue, injury, posture, kinematics), and \(W\) denote weather variables. The STAMP linear predictor is
\[
\eta_{isadk}=f(E_{is},i,s,a,d,k;\theta).
\]
By construction,
\[
\frac{\partial \eta_{isadk}}{\partial P}=0,\qquad
\frac{\partial \eta_{isadk}}{\partial B}=0,\qquad
\frac{\partial \eta_{isadk}}{\partial W}=0,
\]
because \(P,B,W\) do not appear in Eq. (2) [PAGE 14]. Therefore any association between \(P,B,W\) and estimated random effects is a reduced-form residual association, not an identified causal effect. The model assumes conditional independence
\[
Y_{isadk}\perp (P,B,W)\mid (E_{is},i,s,a,d,k,\theta),
\]
but that assumption is not tested in the source. The paper’s own limitations state that the model “cannot resolve fine-grained variation in continuous shot locations within a region, nor does it explicitly account for defender positions, play type, or other contextual factors” [PAGE 12]. That is a direct biomechanical and contextual veto: left-right asymmetry \(z_{ids}\) is a composite of handedness, offensive system, defensive shading, camera standardization, and schedule, not a measured biomechanical quantity.

The weather veto is even stronger for indoor basketball. The source contains no weather, arena, travel, or HVAC variables; the data are play-by-play shot records and possession counts [PAGE 4, PAGE 13]. Direct weather exposure is physically screened by the indoor building envelope, and any indirect weather effect would have to act through travel, schedule, crowd, or arena conditions, none of which enter the likelihood. Thus a claim that STAMP estimates a weather effect is vetoed by specification, not merely by sample size. The physical-layer veto is also clear: \(E_{is}\) is a possession count, not an energy expenditure or physiological load. The model estimates attempts per possession, a dimensionless rate, not physical work in joules. A left-right bias in attempts per possession cannot be converted into a biomechanical force imbalance without external force-plate or tracking data.

The only defensible cross-layer claim is descriptive: after adjusting for possession exposure and partial pooling across teams, areas, sides, shot types, and seasons, STAMP summarizes relative attempt rates. If a team’s right-side multiplier is high, the model says “this team generated more right-side attempts per possession than the league baseline under the model’s coding,” not “this team has a rightward biomechanical bias” or “weather caused it.” The cross-layer veto therefore protects against overreach while preserving the model’s legitimate use as an exposure-adjusted, league-normalized shot-chart summary.
