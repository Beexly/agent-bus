The scientific lineage here is a reversal of a popular prior. The paper’s own reference lineage then connects blind gait and posture to older developmental work: congenitally blind children “take shorter strides, walke slower, and spend more time in the support phase of the gait than sighted children” [PAGE 4], citing the source’s references 18–23 [PAGE 7]. The modern layer is inertial motion capture and sports biomechanics: the source cites IMC validation and trunk-speed accuracy work [PAGE 7], and it uses a 17-IMU suit, 240 Hz sampling, and a 6 Hz zero-phase filter [PAGE 3]. The immediate experimental lineage is also visible in the design: the authors adapt a 5 m shuttle test [PAGE 2] and use a 60 s wall-pass task with an official blind football containing rings [PAGE 3]. Thus the paper sits at the intersection of blind gait development, auditory-spatial psychophysics, and wearable sports kinematics.

Multi-week traps are substantial. If one extrapolates these single-session differences to “after weeks of training, VI players will converge to SIG” or “blindfolded training will fix the gap,” the source does not adjudicate it. A multi-week study would need repeated measures, randomized order or counterbalancing, retention tests, and an equivalence margin in m/s and pass count. Without those, the correct genealogy is: cross-sectional kinematic difference → plausible compensatory adaptation → not a longitudinal training law.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three competing agent models fit the paper. The “crisis habit regression” model says that under visual loss the CNS reverts to developmentally older, cautious locomotion and posture: shorter strides, more stride cycles, greater arm movement, and forward trunk lean. The source supports this pattern: VI ran slower and used more strides than SIG, and VI showed greater arm speed and greater lumbar/thoracic trunk flexion during passing [PAGE 3–4]. The “counter-trap” model says sighted coaches or blindfolded novices may assume that sighted kinematics are the target and that simply removing vision reproduces VI performance. The data veto that assumption: BFO were the slowest and least effective passers, with “7 ± 1” passes versus VI “17 ± 7” [PAGE 4], and BFO’s faster foot contact was interpreted as “a lack of proper control” [PAGE 5]. The authors even suggest that “VI players may present the most effective adaptations to perform such motor tasks” [PAGE 6]. The counter-trap is therefore: copying a sighted template can be worse than using the long-term VI adaptation. The “clock” model says performance is organized by timing windows: 60 s of passing, 5 m shuttle distance, 240 Hz sampling, 500 ms turning windows, 250 ms pre/post-contact windows, and auditory cue timing from the experimenter’s whistle at the 5 m line [PAGE 2–3]. The player must infer when the whistle means “turn now,” when the ball’s rattle means “contact now,” and how much trunk lean improves auditory sampling before the pass.

A recursive theory of mind can be written as a partially observable decision process with belief \(b_t(s)\), as in the probability operator above. The player’s belief about ball state is updated from auditory observations; the coach’s belief about player state is updated from observed passes and speeds; the experimenter’s belief about familiarization is updated from whether the player “felt comfortable to perform running, passing and changing directions following auditory clues” [PAGE 2]. Crisis habit regression is the hypothesis that \(b_t\) collapses toward a prior over cautious gait when visual observation likelihoods vanish. Counter-trap is the hypothesis that the coach’s prior over “good movement” is wrong for the player’s observation model; the coach overweights sighted-like arm/trunk patterns. Clock is the hypothesis that the dominant latent variable is timing precision, not limb amplitude: the player’s turn and contact are gated by cue latency and the 250–500 ms analysis windows. The source cannot fully separate these because it does not measure cue-reaction time, coach expectations, or trial-by-trial learning. The safe adjudication is: habit regression is supported as a descriptive pattern; counter-trap is supported by BFO’s poor passing and faster contact speed; clock is supported only as a task constraint, not as a measured internal clock. Breakdown occurs if the auditory cue is masked, if the player’s prior is nonstationary across trials, or if the coach’s observation model is not identifiable from aggregate pass counts.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards: a claim is tested against a pre-registered counterfactual, and the reward is a dimensionless binary \(R\in\{0,1\}\), with \(R=1\) only if the prediction is confirmed under the stated analysis. Three verifiable veto scenarios follow.

1. Veto the claim “VI slowing is an immutable blindness effect.” Counterfactual: give the BFO group an extended, structured auditory-motor training block on the same 5 m shuttle and 60 s wall-pass tasks, with counterbalanced order and retention testing. Primary outcomes: \(S_{\max}\), \(\bar S_{\mathrm{turn}}\), total shuttle time, stride count, and pass count. Prediction under the immutable-blindness claim: BFO remains significantly below VI after training, with no equivalence. Veto condition: if trained BFO reaches VI within a pre-registered equivalence margin in m/s and pass count, the immutable claim is vetoed. The source already shows BFO is slower and less effective in a short protocol [PAGE 3–4], but it does not test multi-week training, so the longitudinal claim is not yet admissible.

2. Veto the claim “VI trunk flexion is specifically for maximizing auditory input.” Counterfactual: repeat the 60 s wall-pass task with the ball’s rattle masked by broadband noise or with altered spatial audio, while measuring L1/T12 and T9/T8 flexion in the same \(-250\) to \(250\) ms window [PAGE 3]. Prediction under the auditory-maximization claim: when the auditory cue is degraded, trunk flexion increases or pass count decreases relative to the unmasked condition. Veto condition: if trunk flexion and pass count are unchanged under masked audio, the auditory-maximization interpretation is vetoed. This is verifiable because the source already defines the exact trunk windows and segments [PAGE 3–4] and reports that lumbar/thoracic flexion was greater for VI [PAGE 4].

3. Veto the claim “VI arm-speed increase is postural stabilization.” Counterfactual: compare free-arm versus constrained-arm passing in VI players, measuring pass count, COM speed variability, and trunk flexion. Prediction under the stabilization claim: arm constraint reduces pass count or increases COM speed variability. Veto condition: if arm constraint produces no significant change in pass count or COM variability, the stabilization claim is vetoed. This is verifiable because the source reports VI’s “fastest ipsilateral … and contralateral arm speed” [PAGE 4] and discusses arm movements as important to “maintain and optimize postural control and reduce risks of falls” [PAGE 5]. A supplementary instrument veto is also possible: simultaneous optical motion capture versus IMC for trunk flexion; if the IMC bias exceeds a pre-registered angle threshold, the trunk-flexion claim is vetoed on measurement grounds, consistent with the authors’ protocol-specific caveat [PAGE 6–7]. In all three scenarios, the prediction must be fixed before data collection; otherwise the reward is not verifiable and the veto is invalid.
