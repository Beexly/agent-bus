1. Approximate Caratheodory operator.
Source quote: “the number of points k does not even depend on the dimension” [PAGE 10].
Formal statement:
\[
\left\|x-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_j\right\|_2 \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}.
\]
Assumptions: \(T\subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is contained in the unit Euclidean ball, \(x\in \operatorname{conv}(T)\), and \(x_j\in T\). Units: if coordinates have unit \(u\), then the norm and the right-hand side have unit \(u\); if \(T\) is normalized to the unit ball, the quantities are dimensionless. Breakdown regime: exact Caratheodory requires up to \(n+1\) points [PAGE 9]; the approximate version trades exactness for dimension-free cardinality, and the error cannot be made zero unless \(k\) is large or the geometry is degenerate.

2. Hoeffding inequality for Rademacher sums.
Source quote: “Let \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) be independent Rademacher random variables” [PAGE 36].
Formal statement:
\[
\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N} a_i X_i \ge t\right\}
\le
\exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\|a\|_2^2}\right),
\qquad t\ge 0.
\]
Assumptions: independence, Rademacher symmetry, fixed coefficients \(a_i\). Units: \(t\) has the same unit as \(\sum_i a_i X_i\); \(\|a\|_2\) carries the coefficient unit; the exponent is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: independence is essential; without it, the moment generating function need not factor, and the bound can fail. The two-sided version adds a factor 2 [PAGE 38].

3. Subgaussian norm.
Source quote: “A random variable \(X\) is called subgaussian” [PAGE 46].
Formal definition:
\[
\|X\|_{\psi_2}
=
\inf\left\{K>0:\mathbb{E}\exp\left(\frac{X^2}{K^2}\right)\le 2\right\}.
\]
Units: \(\|X\|_{\psi_2}\) has the same unit as \(X\). Information/probability meaning: the Orlicz-type norm measures the scale at which the tail is controlled by a Gaussian envelope. Breakdown regime: distributions with tails heavier than Gaussian, such as exponential or Poisson tails in the source’s examples, are not subgaussian [PAGE 47].

4. Subexponential Bernstein inequality.
Source quote: “Let \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) be independent, mean-zero, subexponential random variables” [PAGE 55].
Formal statement:
\[
\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i\right|\ge t\right\}
\le
2\exp\left[
-c\min\left(
\frac{t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|X_i\|_{\psi_1}^2},
\frac{t}{\max_i \|X_i\|_{\psi_1}}
\right)
\right].
\]
Assumptions: independence, mean zero, finite subexponential norm. Units: \(t\) has the unit of the sum; \(\|X_i\|_{\psi_1}\) has the unit of \(X_i\); the exponent is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: for subexponential variables the moment generating function is finite only near the origin [PAGE 52], so the inequality has two tails: Gaussian for small deviations and exponential for large deviations [PAGE 56].

5. Concentration of the Euclidean norm.
Source quote: “Let \(X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) be a random vector with independent, subgaussian coordinates” [PAGE 68].
Formal statement:
\[
\left|\|X\|_2-\sqrt{n}\right|
\]
has subgaussian norm bounded by \(C K^2\), where \(K=\max_i \|X_i\|_{\psi_2}\) [PAGE 68]. Equivalently, for all \(t\ge 0\),
\[
\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\|X\|_2-\sqrt{n}\right|\ge t\right\}
\le
2\exp\left(-\frac{c t^2}{K^4}\right).
\]
Assumptions: independent coordinates, subgaussian tails, unit second moments. Units: \(\|X\|_2\) and \(t\) have the coordinate unit; \(n\) is dimensionless if coordinates are normalized. Breakdown regime: coordinate independence is crucial; the source shows that the norm of a subgaussian vector need not concentrate without it [PAGE 103].

6. Operator norm on an epsilon-net.
Source quote: “Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) matrix and \(\varepsilon\in[0,1)\)” [PAGE 117].
Formal statement:
\[
\sup_{x\in \mathcal{N}}\|Ax\|_2
\le
\|A\|
\le
\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}
\sup_{x\in \mathcal{N}}\|Ax\|_2,
\]
where \(\mathcal{N}\) is an \(\varepsilon\)-net of the unit sphere \(S^{n-1}\). Units: \(\|A\|\) has the output-unit divided by input-unit; the net approximation is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: \(\varepsilon<1\) is required; if \(\varepsilon\ge 1\), the correction factor is undefined or vacuous. Covering numbers of the Euclidean ball are exponential in dimension [PAGE 121], so the method is powerful only when the net size is controlled.

7. Norm of a subgaussian random matrix.
Source quote: “Let \(A\) be an \(m\times n\) random matrix with independent, mean-zero, subgaussian entries” [PAGE 126].
Formal statement:
\[
\|A\|
\le
C K(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}+t)
\]
with probability at least \(1-2\exp(-t^2)\), where \(K=\max_{i,j}\|A_{ij}\|_{\psi_2}\) [PAGE 126]. Units: \(\|A\|\) is an operator norm; \(K\) has the entry unit; \(t\) is dimensionless if the entries are normalized. Breakdown regime: independence and subgaussian control are required; heavy-tailed entries require different tools. The lower bound shows the \(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}\) scale is typically sharp [PAGE 127].

8. Matrix Bernstein inequality.
Source quote: “Let \(X_1,\dots,X_N\) be independent, mean-zero, \(n\times n\) symmetric random matrices” [PAGE 161].
Formal statement:
\[
\mathbb{P}\left\{
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i\right\|\ge t
\right\}
\le
2n\exp\left(
-\frac{t^2/2}{\sigma^2+Kt/3}
\right),
\]
where \(\sigma^2=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}X_i^2\right\|\) and \(\|X_i\|\le K\) almost surely [PAGE 161]. Units: \(t\), \(\sigma\), and \(K\) have the matrix-entry unit; the dimension factor \(n\) is dimensionless. Breakdown regime: almost sure boundedness, or an equivalent tail control, is essential. The source later states that a boundedness assumption in covariance estimation “cannot be dropped” [PAGE 172].

9. Gaussian width.
Source quote: “The Gaussian width of a subset \(T\subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is defined as” [PAGE 217].
Formal definition:
\[
w(T)=\mathbb{E}\sup_{x\in T}\langle g,x\rangle,
\qquad g\sim N(0,I_n).
\]
Units: if \(x\) has unit \(u\), then \(w(T)\) has unit \(u\) when \(g\) is treated as dimensionless standard normal. Information/geometric meaning: Gaussian width measures the effective size of \(T\) in random directions. Breakdown regime: \(w(T)\) is finite if and only if \(T\) is bounded [PAGE 217].

10. Dudley integral inequality.
Source quote: “Let \((X_t)_{t\in T}\) be a mean-zero random process” [PAGE 230].
Formal statement:
\[
\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in T} X_t
\le
C K
\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{\log N(T,d,\varepsilon)}
\,d\varepsilon.
\]
Assumptions: subgaussian increments with metric \(d\) and constant \(K\). Units: if \(d\) has unit \(u\), then \(d\varepsilon\) has unit \(u\); \(K\) has process-unit per metric-unit, so the product has process-unit. Information units: \(\log N\) is measured in bits if base 2 is used, or nats if natural logarithm is used. Breakdown regime: the integral must be finite; if metric entropy grows too fast at small scales or the diameter is infinite, the bound becomes vacuous.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage in the source is explicit. The Appetizer attributes the empirical method to B. Maurey and notes that it was used by B. Carl to obtain covering-number bounds [PAGE 13]. This places the modern chaining and covering machinery inside a longer lineage: probabilistic constructions for deterministic geometry, then entropy bounds, then stochastic processes. The source also traces concentration inequalities to S. Bernstein, Chernoff, Hoeffding, and later subgaussian theory, stating that subgaussian distributions were introduced by J. P. Kahane [PAGE 57]. In the random-process chapter, the source attributes Slepian inequality to D. Slepian and Sudakov-Fernique to Sudakov and Fernique [PAGE 224].

The multi-week traps are structural, not accidental. The first trap is asymptotic substitution: a student sees the central limit theorem and assumes Gaussian tails for finite sums. The source blocks this by proving the Berry-Esseen error is too large to justify exponential tails [PAGE 35]. The second trap is exact dimension reduction: a student assumes Caratheodory’s exact \(n+1\) representation is necessary, while the approximate theorem shows dimension-free approximation is possible [PAGE 10]. The third trap is confusing covering and packing; the source proves their equivalence only up to scale changes [PAGE 119]. The fourth trap is applying matrix Bernstein without boundedness; the source later warns that the boundedness assumption cannot be dropped [PAGE 172]. The fifth trap is treating metric entropy as sufficient for all process bounds; Sudakov gives a lower bound, while Dudley gives an upper bound, and the gap is a central theme [PAGE 207] [PAGE 230].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In a crisis setting, an agent with limited time and high-dimensional data may regress to familiar but weak habits: use Chebyshev, use the sample mean, or invoke the central limit theorem. The source anticipates this regression. Chebyshev is general but “often too weak” [PAGE 33]. The sample mean has variance \(\sigma^2/N\), but without stronger assumptions its tails are not Gaussian [PAGE 40]. The counter-trap is the median-of-means estimator. The source proves that for any distribution with finite variance, there exists an estimator satisfying
\[
\mathbb{P}\left\{|\hat{\mu}-\mu|\ge \frac{t\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}
\le 2e^{-c t^2}
\]
for \(0\le t\le \sqrt{N}\) [PAGE 41]. This is a recursive correction: the agent recognizes that finite variance alone does not justify Gaussian tails, then constructs a robust estimator that recovers Gaussian confidence under weaker assumptions.

The clock is explicit: sample size \(N\), deviation scale \(t\), block count \(B\), and failure probability. The proof divides the sample into blocks, takes block means, and then takes the median [PAGE 41]. The source also notes a technical clock issue: “The number of blocks \(B\) must be an integer that divides \(N\)” [PAGE 42]. Thus the crisis policy is not merely “use a robust estimator”; it is “use a robust estimator with integer block arithmetic and a deviation range compatible with the clock.” In matrix and process settings, the same recursive pattern appears: replace weak union bounds with matrix Bernstein or chaining, but only when boundedness, independence, and metric entropy conditions are satisfied [PAGE 161] [PAGE 230].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Dependent-coordinate norm-concentration veto.
Counterfactual: assume that because \(X\) is subgaussian and isotropic, the norm concentration theorem holds without independent coordinates.
Source basis: the theorem requires “independent, subgaussian coordinates” [PAGE 68]. The source explicitly asks to show by example that the norm of a subgaussian vector need not concentrate without independence [PAGE 103].
Prediction: there exists an isotropic subgaussian random vector \(X\) such that
\[
\mathbb{P}\left\{\left|\|X\|_2-\sqrt{n}\right|\ge t\right\}
\]
does not obey a uniform subgaussian bound of the form \(2\exp(-c t^2/K^4)\). Verifiable prediction: any proof that drops coordinate independence must either produce a counterexample or prove a new dependence structure; the source’s exercise says the naive extension is false.

2. Heavy-tailed covariance-estimation veto.
Counterfactual: assume the sample covariance matrix concentrates for any distribution with finite covariance.
Source basis: the general covariance theorem assumes a boundedness condition, and the source states that “the boundedness assumption (5.21) cannot be dropped” [PAGE 172].
Prediction: if \(X\) is isotropic but equals zero with high probability, then with high probability the sample may contain only zeros, giving \(\Sigma_m=0\) while \(\Sigma\neq 0\). Therefore,
\[
\|\Sigma_m-\Sigma\|
\]
does not converge to zero at the claimed rate without additional tail or boundedness assumptions. Verifiable prediction: construct a distribution with finite covariance but sufficiently heavy upper-tail mass so that the operator-norm error remains large for any realistic sample size.

3. Central-limit-theorem tail-substitution veto.
Counterfactual: assume the binomial tail can be inferred directly from the central limit theorem with exponential accuracy.
Source basis: the source says the Berry-Esseen approximation error is of order \(1/\sqrt{N}\), which “ruins the desired exponential decay” [PAGE 35].
Prediction: for finite \(N\), the difference between the true binomial tail and the Gaussian tail can be as large as order \(N^{-1/2}\). Therefore any claim that the central limit theorem alone proves a bound like \(\exp(-N/8)\) is invalid. Verifiable prediction: numerical evaluation for moderate \(N\) will show a non-negligible discrepancy between the exact binomial tail and the Gaussian approximation, and the discrepancy decays only polynomially, not exponentially, under Berry-Esseen control.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
