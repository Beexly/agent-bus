<THINK_TRACE>
The drive source is Eager and Erickson’s Football Analytics with Python and R. The central operator is not “predict football” in a single model. It is a multi-hop audit chain: define an outcome, build an expectation from situational covariates, take the residual, then test whether that residual is stable enough to treat as signal. The authors state the general operator explicitly: “The process of building an expectation for an outcome—in the form of points, completion percentage, rushing yards, draft-pick outcome, and many more—and measuring players or teams via the residual (that is, the difference between the value expected by the model and the observed value) is a process that transcends football.” [PAGE 24 of 352]. That sentence is the spine of the audit.

First hop: data acquisition and grouping. The book uses nflfastR and nfl_data_py, filters to play types, groups by player ID and name, and sets minimum attempt thresholds. This is not neutral. A threshold such as 100 pass attempts or 50 carries changes the estimand from “all observed performance” to “performance among qualifiers.” The source’s own stability example uses 100 short-pass attempts and 30 long-pass attempts, producing Pearson correlations of 0.438 for short passes and 0.234 for long passes, and the text says “a quarterback’s performance on shorter passes is twice as stable as on longer passes” [PAGE 72 of 352]. The gray-zone audit is immediate: the long-pass bin is smaller, the 20-yard cutoff is arbitrary, and the exercise section explicitly asks the reader to “Find a cutoff that equally splits the data and perform the same analysis” [PAGE 75 of 352]. Thus the stability gap is not yet a clean causal claim about quarterback skill; it is a conditional description under a particular binning rule.

Second hop: expectation models. In the simple rushing model, the formula is rushing_yards ~ 1 + ydstogo, and the reported R-squared is about 0.0067 [PAGE 89 of 352]. The multiple regression adds down, distance, down-by-distance interaction, yardline_100, run location, and score differential; R-squared rises only to about 0.016 [PAGE 120 of 352]. This is a critical audit point: residualization can improve stability slightly without making the residual a pure player trait. The source reports that RYOE per carry is “slightly more stable year to year than yards per carry (because the correlation coefficient is larger)” [PAGE 97 of 352]. The difference in correlations is real but small: about 0.349 versus 0.323 in the Python output [PAGE 96 of 352]. A frontier audit must label this as weak incremental signal, not a decisive separation of running back skill from blocking, scheme, and defensive box count.

Third hop: binary outcomes. The completion model uses a generalized linear model with a binomial family and logit link. The source explains that “a link function converts (or links) probability (a value ranging from 0 to 1) to a value ranging from −∞ to ∞. The most common link function is the logit” [PAGE 140 of 352]. CPOE is then the observed completion minus predicted completion probability. The book reports CPOE stability of about 0.465 versus completion stability of about 0.445 [PAGE 152 of 352], then says “It looks like CPOE is slightly more stable than pass completion!” [PAGE 153 of 352]. Again the gain is small, and the source itself warns that context features can be quarterback-determined: some quarterbacks characteristically throw shorter passes, take more hits, or play in stable schemes [PAGE 153 of 352]. The audit must therefore distinguish descriptive stabilization from causal isolation.

Fourth hop: count outcomes. The Poisson model uses a log link and a Poisson family; the book defines the Poisson probability mass function and notes that the distribution models “the likelihood of a given number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space” [PAGE 168 of 352]. But the source also shows a visibly imperfect fit: “The Poisson distribution seems to slightly overestimate the likelihood of one touchdown pass, and as such, slightly underestimate the likelihood of zero, two, or more touchdown passes in a game” [PAGE 170 of 352]. This is a built-in veto condition. If the mean-variance restriction fails, Poisson predictive probabilities are not trustworthy without quasi-Poisson or negative-binomial repair.

Fifth hop: multivariate structure. PCA is used to collapse collinear combine features; the source defines it as taking features with collinearity and “mushing” them into smaller subsets that are linearly independent [PAGE 226 of 352]. Scaling is required because the raw axes have different units and magnitudes; the book states that scaling transforms a feature “usually to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1” [PAGE 243 of 352]. Then k-means partitions observations by nearest mean [PAGE 252 of 352]. The gray zones are sign indeterminacy, imputation, seed dependence, choice of K, and outlier sensitivity. The source itself says many combine records are incomplete [PAGE 229 of 352], so any cluster solution is conditional on the imputation model.

The overall audit conclusion is that the book supports a disciplined descriptive and predictive workflow, not automatic causal attribution. Crisis habit regression appears when an analyst overreads a one-season residual; the counter-trap is lagged stability plus multiple controls; the clock is the chosen season, week, or rolling window. Cross-layer vetoes must be applied before any player-level claim is treated as stable: omitted biomechanical state, injury, weather, and scheme variables can bias the residual, and a model with low explained variance cannot adjudicate those omitted layers by itself.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Notation: \(y_i\) is the observed outcome for play or player \(i\); \(X_i\) is a covariate row; \(\beta\) is a parameter vector; \(\hat{y}_i\) is the model expectation; \(r_i\) is the residual. Units are stated per model. Football distances are in yards; \(1\ \mathrm{yd}=0.9144\ \mathrm{m}\). Correlation is dimensionless. Logit and log-link quantities are in natural-log units, nats.

1. Expectation and residual operator. The book’s core operator is
\[
\hat{y}_i=f(X_i;\hat{\beta}),\qquad r_i=y_i-\hat{y}_i.
\]
The residual has the same units as \(y_i\). For rushing yards, \(r_i\) is in yards; for completion probability, \(r_i\) is a probability difference in \([0,1]\). The source calls this “the difference between the value expected by the model and the observed value” [PAGE 24 of 352]. Breakdown regime: if \(X_i\) omits a covariate \(Z_i\) that affects \(y_i\) and is correlated with \(X_i\), then \(E[r_i\mid X_i]\neq 0\) and the residual is not a player-skill estimate.

2. Pearson stability operator. Stability is measured by
\[
\rho=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}},
\]
where \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) are the same metric in adjacent seasons. \(\rho\) is dimensionless and lies in \([-1,1]\). The book defines stability as consistency over a predetermined time frame and uses Pearson’s correlation or its square [PAGE 42 of 352]. In the pass-depth example, \(\rho_{\text{short}}=0.438\) and \(\rho_{\text{long}}=0.234\) [PAGE 72 of 352]. Breakdown regimes: small \(n\), range restriction from qualification thresholds, missing pairs from inner joins, nonstationarity across rule changes, and outlier leverage.

3. Simple ordinary least squares. The rushing model is
\[
y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\mathrm{ydstogo}_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad
\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2).
\]
Here \(y_i\) is rushing yards; \(\beta_1\) has units yd per yd-to-go. The source gives the formula as rushing_yards ~ 1 + ydstogo [PAGE 87 of 352] and reports \(R^2\approx 0.0067\) [PAGE 89 of 352]. Assumptions: linear conditional mean, independent errors, homoscedastic variance, and approximately normal residuals for inference. Breakdown regimes: nonlinearity, heteroscedasticity, high-leverage plays, and rank deficiency if \(X'X\) is singular. With one continuous predictor plus intercept, singularity occurs only if ydstogo is constant.

4. Multiple ordinary least squares with interaction. The expanded model is
\[
\begin{aligned}
y_i={}&\beta_0+\beta_{\text{down},i}+\beta_{\text{yd}}\,\mathrm{ydstogo}_i
+\beta_{\text{int}}(\mathrm{down}_i\times \mathrm{ydstogo}_i)\\
&+\beta_{\text{y100}}\,\mathrm{yardline\_100}_i
+\beta_{\text{run}}\,\mathrm{run\_location}_i
+\beta_{\text{score}}\,\mathrm{score\_differential}_i+\varepsilon_i.
\end{aligned}
\]
The response is yards; the interaction coefficient is yd per yd-to-go within down. The source gives this formula and reports \(R^2\approx 0.016\) [PAGE 117 of 352 to PAGE 120 of 352]. Breakdown regimes: perfect collinearity among indicators, unstable interaction estimates under sparse cells, nonlinearity near the end zones, and outlier leverage. The source’s own residual diagnostics show non-normal tails and leverage concerns [PAGE 130 of 352 to PAGE 131 of 352].

5. Binomial GLM and CPOE. For completion, \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), and
\[
\Pr(y_i=1\mid X_i)=p_i=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\eta_i)},\qquad
\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\mathrm{air\_yards}_i+\cdots,
\]
with logit link \(\eta_i=\log[p_i/(1-p_i)]\) in nats. The source states that probability is bounded and that the logit converts it to the real-valued scale [PAGE 140 of 352]. CPOE is
\[
\mathrm{CPOE}_i=y_i-\hat{p}_i,
\]
a probability difference. Breakdown regimes: complete or quasi-complete separation, empty covariate cells, strong collinearity, and misspecified nonlinearity in air yards. The source’s CPOE stability result is \(\rho_{\text{CPOE}}\approx 0.465\) versus \(\rho_{\text{completion}}\approx 0.445\) [PAGE 152 of 352].

6. Poisson GLM. For touchdown counts,
\[
Y_i\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda_i),\qquad
\Pr(Y_i=y)=\frac{e^{-\lambda_i}\lambda_i^{y}}{y!},\qquad
\log \lambda_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\mathrm{pass\_td\_rate}_i+\beta_2\,\mathrm{score\_environment}_i.
\]
\(\lambda_i\) is a count per game. The source defines the Poisson mass function and fixed-interval interpretation [PAGE 168 of 352]. Breakdown regimes: overdispersion when \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i)\gt E[Y_i]\), excess zeros, dependence across plays or games, and nonstationarity. The source shows an empirical misfit in the one-versus-zero-and-two-or-more probabilities [PAGE 170 of 352].

7. PCA operator. After standardization,
\[
z_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j}{s_j},
\]
PCA finds orthonormal loadings \(v_k\) and scores
\[
a_{ik}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}z_{ij}v_{kj}.
\]
The variance explained by component \(k\) is \(\lambda_k/\sum_{j}\lambda_j\), dimensionless. The source defines PCA as collapsing collinear features into linearly independent subsets [PAGE 226 of 352] and states the mean-zero unit-variance scaling rule [PAGE 243 of 352]. Breakdown regimes: zero-variance columns, severe outliers, missingness and imputation error, sign indeterminacy, and interpreting rotated axes as physical traits without validation.

8. k-means operator. The objective is
\[
W(C)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i:c_i=k}\lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert_2^2,
\]
where \(\mu_k\) is the cluster centroid and the unit is squared Euclidean distance in the input or PC space. The source defines k-means as partitioning into \(k\) clusters by nearest mean [PAGE 252 of 352]. Breakdown regimes: local minima, seed dependence, empty or duplicate clusters, sensitivity to \(K\), non-spherical cluster shapes, and scaling mismatch.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage starts with expected points and residual thinking. The source traces modern football analytics to Virgil Carter’s 1971 expected-point work and later Brian Burke’s expected-points and win-probability formulations [PAGE 23 of 352 to PAGE 24 of 352]. It then credits Yurko, Ventura, and Horowitz’s nflscrapR, and later nflfastR and nfl_data_py, as the public infrastructure that made play-by-play residual models reproducible [PAGE 24 of 352 to PAGE 25 of 352]. The scientific genealogy is therefore not “one model”; it is a reproducibility chain: public data, expectation model, residual metric, stability test, and narrative caveat.

The baseball lineage matters because it supplied the stability habit. The book connects football stability analysis to baseball’s “three true outcomes” and SABR-era sabermetrics, arguing that stable production is the kind of production decision-makers should want to repeat [PAGE 25 of 352]. In football, that habit becomes: compute YPA, RYOE, CPOE, and draft value over expected, then ask whether the residual persists across seasons. The source’s own historical traps are explicit. It notes that 2016 is “largely an arbitrary choice” and lists rule and schedule breaks: 2002 expansion, 2011 collective bargaining change, 2015 extra-point move, 2020 COVID-19 and playoff expansion, and 2021 move from 16 to 17 regular-season games [PAGE 44 of 352]. These are multi-week and multi-season traps: a lagged correlation that crosses a rule boundary is not a clean stability estimate.

A second genealogy is the draft-value and combine-value lineage. The source connects the draft-pick value question to the Jimmy Johnson chart, to Cade Massey’s surplus-value critique, and to later public draft-curve work [PAGE 204 of 352 to PAGE 205 of 352]. The combine chapter then connects athleticism measurement to PCA and clustering, while acknowledging that tracking data may soon replace some on-field testing [PAGE 225 of 352 to PAGE 226 of 352]. The retrocausal warning is that models built on historical draft slots encode historical team behavior, historical positional valuation, and historical playing-time bias. A modern claim about “drafting skill” or “athletic type” must survive changes in scheme, rule enforcement, and data collection.

A third genealogy is the “signal versus noise” lineage. The source quotes Tukey’s warning that an approximate answer to the right question is better than an exact answer to the wrong question [PAGE 42 of 352], and it uses Nate Silver’s signal-and-noise framing to motivate stability analysis [PAGE 42 of 352]. That lineage produces the buy-low and sell-high vocabulary: a player who performs well in stable components but poorly in unstable ones may be undervalued; the reverse may be overvalued [PAGE 42 of 352]. The multi-week trap is that public narratives form faster than lagged validation. One deep-pass outlier season can become a story before the next season’s regression is observed. The source’s Alex Smith example is exactly this: a high deep-pass YPA season followed by disappearance from the leaderboard [PAGE 51 of 352 to PAGE 52 of 352]. The correct genealogical response is not to deny the observed season; it is to mark it as an unstable component until the next lagged correlation is estimated.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the tendency of analysts and audiences to fall back on narrative habits when uncertainty is high. In this source, the habit takes specific forms: overreading one deep-pass season, overreading one high-YPA backup season, overreading one draft class, or treating a low-R-squared residual as a pure player trait. The book supplies the counter-trap in its own method: define a testable hypothesis, quantify a stable versus unstable split, and check whether the result survives a second season. Tukey’s quote is the governing mental model: “Far better an approximate answer to the right question, which is often vague, than an exact answer to the wrong question, which can always be made precise” [PAGE 42 of 352]. The recursive layer is that the analyst must model the evaluator’s reaction to the residual, not only the residual itself. If the market, fan base, or front office overreacts to an unstable metric, the analyst’s model must include that selection pressure when deciding what a residual means.

The counter-trap has three moves. First, separate stable from unstable components before ranking. The source’s short-pass versus long-pass result is the template: short-pass YPA is more stable than long-pass YPA [PAGE 72 of 352]. Second, add context controls before calling a residual “skill.” The multiple-rushing model adds down, distance, field position, run location, and score differential [PAGE 117 of 352 to PAGE 120 of 352]. Third, label the result as conditional. CPOE is only “slightly more stable” than completion percentage [PAGE 153 of 352], and the source immediately notes that context features can be quarterback-determined [PAGE 153 of 352]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore represent three agents: the player, the evaluator, and the future evaluator who knows the lagged outcome. The analyst who ignores the third agent will mistake a public overreaction for a stable signal.

The clock is the time index. The source uses season-level aggregation for stability, week-level aggregation for within-season props, and lagged joins to create prior-season predictors [PAGE 64 of 352 to PAGE 65 of 352]. The recursive danger is clock mismatch: a week-level habit applied to a season-level question, or a season-level model applied to a single-game decision. The source’s later chapters introduce time-series and survival/time-to-event tools as advanced options [PAGE 241 of 352, PAGE 245 of 352]. The counter-trap is to state the clock explicitly: season \(n\) versus season \(n+1\), week \(w\) versus week \(w+k\), or game clock versus season clock. Without that statement, a “regression candidate” claim is underspecified.

A final recursive point: residuals can change behavior, and changed behavior changes future residuals. If a team uses CPOE or RYOE to acquire players, the acquired players’ future contexts change, so the metric’s future stability is not invariant. The source’s caution about residual metrics supports this: stripping context can leave some quarterback-driven context embedded in the expectation [PAGE 153 of 352]. The recursive audit therefore requires a feedback flag. Any player-acquisition or scheme decision based on a residual must be re-estimated after the decision, because the decision itself is a treatment.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is formal, not rhetorical. Suppose the analyst’s model is
\[
y=X\beta+\varepsilon,
\]
but the true data-generating process is
\[
y=X\beta+Z\gamma+\varepsilon,
\]
where \(Z\) contains omitted physical, biomechanical, injury, weather, or scheme variables. Then
\[
\hat{\beta}= (X'X)^{-1}X'y
= \beta + (X'X)^{-1}X'Z\gamma + (X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon.
\]
If \(X'Z\neq 0\) and \(\gamma\neq 0\), the estimated residual is contaminated. This is the omitted-variable veto. It applies even when the model’s \(R^2\) is statistically significant. The source’s rushing multiple regression has \(R^2\approx 0.016\) [PAGE 120 of 352], so the residual is a small signal embedded in large unexplained variance. A physical or weather layer that correlates with down, distance, or team cannot be adjudicated away by that regression.

Physical-layer veto. The combine data measure height, weight, 40-yard dash, vertical jump, bench press, broad jump, three-cone drill, and shuttle [PAGE 227 of 352 to PAGE 228 of 352]. These are not interchangeable. The source shows height-weight collinearity [PAGE 230 of 352], weight versus 40-yard dash structure [PAGE 231 of 352 to PAGE 233 of 352], 40-yard dash versus vertical jump [PAGE 233 of 352 to PAGE 235 of 352], and 40-yard dash versus three-cone drill [PAGE 236 of 352 to PAGE 237 of 352]. PCA then concentrates much variance in the first component, with loadings that mix size and speed [PAGE 244 of 352]. Veto rule: if a player’s physical profile is missing or imputed, the cluster or PC label cannot be treated as measured athleticism. The source notes that many combine records are incomplete [PAGE 229 of 352]. If the missingness is informative—for example, injured players not testing—then complete-case PCA and KNN imputation estimate different populations.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The 40-yard dash, vertical jump, broad jump, three-cone drill, and shuttle are pre-draft snapshots in seconds or inches. They do not encode current joint health, fatigue, technique change, or sport-specific coordination. The source itself says tracking data may give more accurate and timely athleticism estimates [PAGE 225 of 352 to PAGE 226 of 352]. Veto rule: if a claim is about current player ability, a static combine vector is insufficient when injury, age, or tracking data contradict it. The PCA sign indeterminacy is a second biomechanical veto: the source notes that PC signs can flip between software implementations [PAGE 243 of 352]. A signed PC loading is not a biological direction until the analyst fixes the sign convention.

Weather and venue veto. The source states that fuller models require adjustments for “weather and injuries” [PAGE 140 of 352]. But the public play-by-play examples do not jointly model game-time temperature, wind, precipitation, field surface, and travel. Formal veto: let \(W\) be a weather vector with units \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\). If \(W\) affects passing or rushing outcomes and \(W\) is correlated with schedule, home venue, or team style, then the CPOE or RYOE residual is not a clean player estimate. The correct action is either to include \(W\), stratify by \(W\), or downgrade the claim from causal to descriptive. The same logic applies to injury status and offensive-line continuity. The source’s own low-variance-explained models are evidence that the residual cannot absorb all omitted layers.

Adjudication rule: a player-level claim is vetoed for causal use if any of the following hold: the physical feature is more than a pre-specified missingness threshold imputed; the biomechanical snapshot is stale relative to the claim clock; the weather or venue layer is correlated with the model covariates and omitted; or the model matrix is rank deficient due to perfect collinearity. The claim may survive as a descriptive residual, but not as an isolated skill estimate.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Equal-count pass-depth split. Current source result: short-pass stability \(\rho\approx 0.438\), long-pass stability \(\rho\approx 0.234\), using the 20-yard cutoff and thresholds of 100 short attempts and 30 long attempts [PAGE 72 of 352]. Counterfactual: recompute the lagged Pearson correlation on nflfastR 2016–2022 after setting the long-short cutoff to the median air yards so the two bins have equal counts. Verifiable prediction: the absolute stability gap \(|\rho_{\text{short}}-\rho_{\text{long}}|\) falls below 0.10, and long-pass \(\rho\) rises above 0.234. If the equal-count split still produces a gap of 0.20 or more, the “deep passes are less stable” claim survives the sample-size veto. If the gap collapses, the original claim is vetoed as partly a bin-count artifact.

Scenario 2: Carry-threshold stability. Current source result at more than 50 carries: RYOE per carry \(\rho\approx 0.349\) versus yards per carry \(\rho\approx 0.323\), a difference of about 0.026 [PAGE 96 of 352 to PAGE 97 of 352]. Counterfactual: repeat the same lagged inner join with a threshold of more than 100 carries. Verifiable prediction: the stability advantage \(\Delta\rho=\rho_{\text{RYOE}}-\rho_{\text{YPC}}\) falls below 0.026 and may approach zero. If \(\Delta\rho\le 0\), the claim that this RYOE specification is meaningfully more stable than yards per carry is vetoed at the stricter threshold. If \(\Delta\rho\) remains above 0.026, the claim is strengthened. This is a direct test of whether the residual signal survives reduced sample and selection.

Scenario 3: Poisson dispersion veto. Current source diagnostic: the Poisson model overestimates one-touchdown probability and underestimates zero and two-or-more touchdown probabilities [PAGE 170 of 352]. Counterfactual: on the same quarterback-week data with at least 10 passes, compute the dispersion statistic
\[
\phi=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}{\operatorname{E}(Y)}
\]
for touchdown counts. Verifiable prediction: \(\phi\gt 1.1\), indicating overdispersion. If \(\phi\gt 1.1\), the Poisson model’s predictive intervals are vetoed for probability statements, and the analyst must use quasi-Poisson or negative binomial. If \(\phi\le 1.1\), the Poisson approximation is not vetoed by dispersion. A secondary check is calibration: if empirical 95 percent interval coverage is below 90 percent, the Poisson model is vetoed even if \(\phi\) is near 1. This scenario is fully reproducible from the source’s quarterback-week aggregation and is tied to an explicit source diagnostic [PAGE 170 of 352].
