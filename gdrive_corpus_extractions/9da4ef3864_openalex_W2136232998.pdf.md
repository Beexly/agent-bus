Within the GSE+GSI frame, the source is a self-contained mathematical kernel rather than an empirical study. Its central object is a forecast \(f\in[0,1]^n\) for a fixed vector of events \(E=(E_1,\ldots,E_n)\) over a sample space \(\Omega\). The first multi-hop derivation is: coherence is not an empirical accuracy property; it is membership in a convex hull. The paper states: “A forecast f is coherent just in case there is a probability measure µ over Ω such that for all i ⩽n, fi = µ(Ei).” [PAGE 4]. Proposition 1 then identifies coherence with \(f\in\operatorname{conv}(V)\), where \(V\) is the set of truth vectors \(C_E(\omega)\) [PAGE 6]: “A forecast f is coherent if and only if f ∈conv(V).” [PAGE 6]. This converts a probabilistic-consistency question into a convex-geometric one.

The second hop is scoring-rule structure. A proper scoring rule \(s:\{0,1\}\times[0,1]\to[0,\infty]\) satisfies the expected-score minimization condition: “ps(1, x) + (1 −p)s(0, x) is uniquely minimized at x = p” [PAGE 4]. Proposition 2 then defines \(\phi(x)=-x s(1,x)-(1-x)s(0,x)\) and proves the representation \(s(i,x)=-\phi(x)-\phi'(x)(i-x)\) for \(x\in(0,1)\) [PAGE 6]. Thus every proper scoring rule is encoded by a bounded, continuous, strictly convex \(\phi\) whose derivative may diverge only at the boundary. This is the load-bearing bridge: properness becomes convexity.

The third hop is the penalty decomposition. With \(\Phi(x)=\sum_i\phi(x_i)\) and Bregman divergence \(d_\Phi(y,x)=\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)-\nabla\Phi(x)\cdot(y-x)\), the paper derives [PAGE 7]:
\[
P_s(\omega,f)
=
d_\Phi(v_\omega,f)
+
\sum_{i=1}^n s(C_{E_i}(\omega), C_{E_i}(\omega)).
\]
That is, the realized penalty is a Bregman divergence from the truth vector \(v_\omega\) to the forecast \(f\), plus a truth-only term independent of \(f\). This is the exact operator that makes domination geometric.

The fourth hop is the incoherent case. If \(f\notin\operatorname{conv}(V)\), Proposition 3 gives a unique Bregman projection \(g=\pi_f\in\operatorname{conv}(V)\) such that
\[
d_\Phi(y,g)\le d_\Phi(y,f)-d_\Phi(g,f)\quad \forall y\in\operatorname{conv}(V),
\]
and hence for all truth vertices \(y\in V\). Since \(d_\Phi(g,f)>0\), the penalty of \(g\) is strictly lower for every \(\omega\). The paper states: “Since dΦ(g, f) > 0 this proves part (b) of Theorem 1.” [PAGE 8]. The fifth hop is the coherent case. If \(f\in\operatorname{conv}(V)\) and some distinct \(g\) weakly dominates \(f\), the divergence inequality extends by linearity from \(V\) to \(\operatorname{conv}(V)\), so \(d_\Phi(f,g)\le d_\Phi(f,f)=0\). Strict convexity forces \(g=f\). Hence Theorem 1: coherent forecasts are not weakly dominated by distinct rivals; incoherent forecasts are strongly dominated by some coherent forecast [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit is essential. First, coherence is not calibration and not truth-tracking. The source explicitly says scoring rules measure “the quality of a probability-estimate for a given event, with lower scores signifying probabilities that are closer to the event’s status” [PAGE 1], but Theorem 1 only concerns domination by rival forecasts under a proper rule. A coherent forecast can still be empirically poor. Second, properness is expected-score incentive compatibility, not ex-post dominance: the paper says “Our scoring rule thus encourages sincerity since your interest lies in announcing probabilities that conform to your beliefs.” [PAGE 2]. Third, the theorem is rule-relative. Change \(s\), and the penalty geometry changes. Fourth, the bounded case is cleaner: when \(s\) is unbounded, \(\phi'\) may diverge at \(0\) or \(1\), and the proof handles boundary faces by induction [PAGE 8]. Fifth, the generalization to non-unique minimization weakens strong dominance to weak dominance; the paper says “Strong dominance will not hold in general, as the example of s(i, x) ≡0 shows.” [PAGE 12]. Sixth, the discontinuous case is not settled: “Whether Theorem 1 holds for this generalized notion of a discontinuous scoring rule remains open.” [PAGE 13]. Seventh, Lindley’s transformation \(T\) is a historical warning: “The reliance on the transformation T, however, clouds the interpretation of Lindley’s theorem.” [PAGE 3]. Finally, there is no physical, biomechanical, or weather content in the source; the only “space” is an abstract sample space \(\Omega\), and the only “units” are dimensionless probabilities and rule-defined penalty units.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under high penalty or distributional stress, a forecaster may regress to improper habits such as announcing extreme \(0\) or \(1\) probabilities, anchoring on a single scenario, or switching to an absolute-deviation rule. The source warns that \(+\infty\) penalties can occur at \((0,1)\) or \((1,0)\) [PAGE 4], so extreme categorical announcements are exactly where the scoring geometry becomes singular. The proper-rule incentive is to announce the genuine belief \(p\): “Our scoring rule thus encourages sincerity since your interest lies in announcing probabilities that conform to your beliefs.” [PAGE 2]. Crisis regression therefore trades expected-score optimality for local psychological comfort.

Counter-trap: an incoherent forecast can be strongly dominated by a coherent projection [PAGE 5], but that counter-trap depends on the evaluator’s rule being proper and on the theorem’s assumptions. If the evaluator relaxes unique minimization, only weak dominance is guaranteed [PAGE 12]; if the evaluator uses a discontinuous rule, the extension is open [PAGE 13]. Recursive theory of mind matters because the forecaster’s best response depends on the evaluator’s scoring rule, and the evaluator’s inference depends on the forecaster’s reported probabilities. A proper rule aligns these levels: sincere reporting is the forecaster’s best response in expectation. An improper or ambiguous rule creates strategic hedging, and a discontinuous rule creates a gray zone where the forecaster cannot know whether the domination counter-trap is available.

Clock: event resolution and revision. Coherence is defined relative to the fixed event vector \(E\) and sample space \(\Omega\) [PAGE 4]. When new logical relations are introduced—inclusion, partition, or complementary events—the old forecast may become incoherent. The “clock” is therefore the revision clock: before resolution, the forecaster minimizes expected penalty; after resolution, the realized penalty is a Bregman divergence from the truth vector [PAGE 7]. A forecast that was coherent at time \(t_1\) can become incoherent at \(t_2\) if the event algebra changes. The recursive discipline is to re-check \(f\in\operatorname{conv}(V)\) whenever the event set changes, not merely when new data arrive. The counter-trap against crisis regression is to keep forecasts interior where possible, use the proper rule’s sincerity incentive, and avoid claiming strong dominance under relaxed or discontinuous rules.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
There is no physical, biomechanical, or weather veto proof in this source. The domain is an abstract nonempty sample space \(\Omega\), subsets called events, and probability measures over \(\Omega\) [PAGE 4]. Scoring rules are functions \(s:\{0,1\}\times[0,1]\to[0,\infty]\) [PAGE 4], and their role is to measure “the quality of a probability-estimate for a given event” [PAGE 1]. There are no state variables for force, fatigue, acceleration, joint torque, temperature, wind, precipitation, or any other physical quantity. There are no SI units; probabilities are dimensionless, and penalties are rule-defined score units. Therefore a claim that Theorem 1 supplies a physical, biomechanical, or weather veto is not derivable from the source.

The formal adjudication is this. A cross-layer veto \(V_{\text{phys}}\) would require a mechanistic mapping \(M:X_{\text{phys}}\to E\) from a physical state space to the event algebra, plus a measurement model and a scoring rule defined on those events. The source provides only \(\Omega\), \(E\), \(\mu\), and \(s\). It proves that incoherent forecasts are strongly dominated by coherent ones under proper scoring [PAGE 5], and that coherent forecasts are not weakly dominated by distinct rivals [PAGE 5]. It does not prove that a coherent forecast is physically feasible, biomechanically safe, or weather-consistent. Coherence is measure-theoretic consistency [PAGE 4], not physical realizability. Thus any downstream model output that says “proper scoring rule proves a weather veto” or “coherence implies biomechanical safety” must be vetoed as an unsupported cross-layer inference. The only legitimate cross-layer use of this source is as a scoring/coherence kernel: it can adjudicate whether a forecast is coherent relative to a stated event algebra and whether a rival dominates it under a stated proper rule. Physical, biomechanical, and weather vetoes require independent domain evidence and their own formal mapping; they cannot be imported from this paper.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
