The primary outcome is “self-rated health measured at age 65” [PAGE 1], dichotomized from the WLS question: “In general, would you say your health is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” [PAGE 7]. Secondary outcomes include pain limiting activities, ADL difficulty, maximum adult BMI, and cancer as a negative-control outcome [PAGE 7–8].

Second hop: audit sample construction. Page 4 gives the cleanest arithmetic: initial pool \(4{,}991\) men; exclude \(1{,}221\) without yearbook information; exclude \(245\) with yearbook but no sport information; exclude \(170\) for collision sports or “Other”; then “Following these initial exclusions, we are left with an pool of 3,355 subjects eligible for our study” [PAGE 4]. The same page states \(1{,}259\) football and \(2{,}096\) non-football eligible subjects. But the abstract says the study “compares 1,153 high school males who played varsity football with 2,751 male students who did not” [PAGE 1]. These are not the same cohort: \(1{,}153+2{,}751=3{,}904\), whereas \(1{,}259+2{,}096=3{,}355\). That is a 549-subject gray-zone discrepancy. A second arithmetic tension appears in missingness: the text says “We dropped the 334 football players and 573 controls missing the primary outcome” [PAGE 8], but Table 3 lists 176 sport controls plus 399 non-sport controls missing self-rated health, i.e. 575 controls, not 573. The aggregate 909 is consistent with 334+176+399=909, so the text’s “573” is likely a typo or version drift. This must be flagged before any verifier treats the abstract and methods as one fixed dataset.

Third hop: matching. The protocol estimates a propensity score, constructs a rank-based Mahalanobis distance matrix, adds a propensity-score caliper, and performs variable-ratio matching for \(K=2,\ldots,8\), selecting the \(K\) that uses the most subjects while achieving absolute standardized differences below 0.2 SD [PAGE 9; PAGE 12]. It then runs separate matches for football vs all controls, football vs sport controls, football vs non-sport controls, and sport controls vs non-sport controls [PAGE 10]. This is a deliberate counter-trap against unmeasured personality/fitness differences between athletes and non-athletes: if football effects are consistent across control definitions, the claim is stronger; if sport controls and non-sport controls differ, that difference must be adjudicated.

Fourth hop: missingness and attrition. Table 3 shows primary-outcome missingness of 909/3,355 = 27.09%, and pain missingness of 2,487/3,355 = 74.13% [PAGE 8]. This is not a minor nuisance: complete-case analysis can induce selection if missingness depends on football and on late-life health. The protocol plans an attrition analysis using baseline covariates [PAGE 8], but that only tests availability as a function of observed \(X_i\); it does not prove missingness is independent of unobserved \(Y_i\). The gray-zone audit therefore must treat the 2004 outcome sample as a survivor/responder sample, not as a random sample of the 1957 cohort.

Fifth hop: inference. The paper uses conditional logistic regression for binary outcomes and linear regression with matched-set indicators for maximum adult BMI [PAGE 10]. It uses ordered testing so that alternative control-group comparisons are tested only if a hypothesized effect falls outside the main comparison’s 95% confidence interval [PAGE 10]. It also reports a Rosenbaum sensitivity bound \(\Gamma\) at which a marginally significant result becomes insignificant [PAGE 12–13]. Larger \(\Gamma\) is stronger evidence against unmeasured confounding, but the protocol gives no numerical \(\Gamma\) because no outcome results are reported. Thus the correct conclusion is design-level: the protocol has a credible matching and sensitivity architecture, but the causal question remains open.

Gray-zone audit summary: (1) abstract vs methods sample counts conflict; (2) text vs table missing-control counts conflict by 2; (3) Table 1 sport counts are participation counts and can exceed unique controls because of multi-sport participation, so they should not be summed as unique sport controls; (4) pain missingness is extremely high; (5) no outcome estimates are present; (6) multiple outcomes and control groups require the ordered procedure; (7) cancer is a negative control, not a causal target; (8) weather and biomechanical load are not measured, so any weather-mediated or position-specific claim is underidentified. These are not minor editorial issues; they are the exact places where an RLVR verifier should demand page-anchored arithmetic rather than narrative fluency.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(i=1,\ldots,n\) index subjects, \(Z_i\in\{0,1\}\) football participation, \(Y_i\) the outcome, \(X_i\in\mathbb{R}^p\) pre-exposure covariates, and \(s(i)\) the matched set containing subject \(i\). The protocol’s target is a matched-set conditional effect, estimated after balancing \(X_i\) by propensity-score and rank-based Mahalanobis matching [PAGE 9].

Propensity score:
\[
e(X_i)=\Pr(Z_i=1\mid X_i),\qquad e(X_i)\in[0,1]\ \text{(dimensionless probability)}.
\]
Positivity requires \(0<e(X_i)<1\) for comparable subjects; if \(e(X_i)=0\) or \(1\), no overlap exists and matching must discard subjects or fail. The paper’s caliper restricts matched pairs to close propensity scores, while the rank-based Mahalanobis distance measures multivariate covariate separation [PAGE 9]:
\[
D_{ij}=\bigl(R(X_i)-R(X_j)\bigr)^{\top}\hat\Sigma_R^{-1}\bigl(R(X_i)-R(X_j)\bigr),
\]
where \(R(\cdot)\) denotes rank transformation and \(\hat\Sigma_R\) is the rank covariance. \(D_{ij}\) is dimensionless because it is computed on ranks. The caliper adds a constraint such as \(|e(X_i)-e(X_j)|\le c\), with \(c\) in probability units.

Balance is assessed by standardized differences:
\[
\Delta_k=\frac{\bar X_{T,k}-\bar X_{C,k}}{\sqrt{\bigl(s_{T,k}^2+s_{C,k}^2\bigr)/2}},
\]
where \(\Delta_k\) is in SD units and is dimensionless. The protocol states: “Standardized differences (Std. Diﬀ.) less than 0.2 in magnitude indicate adequate balance” [PAGE 12]. Table 4 shows, for example, standardized BMI moving from a before-matching difference of 0.416 to 0.083 after matching, and high-school size moving from \(-0.405\) to \(-0.013\) [PAGE 12]. Those are balance diagnostics, not outcome effects.

For binary outcomes, the conditional logistic model is:
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(Y_{ij}=1\mid Z_{ij},s_i)=\alpha_{s_i}+\beta Z_{ij},
\]
where \(\beta\) is on the natural-logit scale (dimensionless log-ratio units) and \(\exp(\beta)\) is a dimensionless multiplicative factor on the conditional logit scale. For maximum adult BMI, the protocol uses a linear model with matched-set indicators [PAGE 10]:
\[
Y_{ij}=\alpha_{s_i}+\beta Z_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij},\qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2),
\]
with \(Y_{ij}\) in kg/m\(^2\), \(\beta\) in kg/m\(^2\), and \(\sigma\) in kg/m\(^2\). The standardized effect is \(d=\beta/\sigma\), dimensionless. The protocol invokes Cohen/Sawilowsky thresholds: 0.01–0.2 SD “very small,” 0.2–0.5 SD “small,” 0.5–0.8 SD “medium,” 0.8–1.2 SD “large,” and over 1.2 SD “very large” [PAGE 10]. These are descriptive benchmarks, not probability statements.

The Rosenbaum sensitivity bound is a dimensionless treatment-assignment ratio. In probability form, for two subjects in the same matched set with treatment probabilities \(\pi_{s,1}\) and \(\pi_{s,0}\),
\[
\Gamma=\sup_s \frac{\pi_{s,1}(1-\pi_{s,0})}{\pi_{s,0}(1-\pi_{s,1})},\qquad \Gamma\ge 1.
\]
The protocol says it will report “the \(\Gamma\) at which the result is sensitive (i.e. the \(\Gamma\) at which the result becomes insigniﬁcant)” [PAGE 12], and “Larger values of \(\Gamma\) provide greater evidence for the study’s causal conclusions” [PAGE 13]. \(\Gamma=1\) corresponds to no hidden treatment-assignment bias; as \(\Gamma\) grows, a finding that remains significant is more robust. There is no reported numerical \(\Gamma\) in the source because this is a protocol, not a results paper.

The ordered testing rule is:
\[
\text{test } H_0:\tau=\tau_0 \text{ in an alternative control comparison only if }
\tau_0\notin CI_{0.95}^{\text{main}}.
\]
The paper states that it tests alternative control groups “only if \(\tau_0\) falls outside the 95% conﬁdence interval from the main comparison between football players and all controls” [PAGE 10]. This controls familywise error across the four comparisons, but it also means an alternative comparison may never be reached; unreached intervals are not multiplicity-adjusted.

Missingness operator: let \(M_i=1\) if subject \(i\) is missing the outcome. Complete-case analysis uses \(Y_i\) only when \(M_i=0\). The protocol reports 909/3,355 = 27.09% missing self-rated health and 2,487/3,355 = 74.13% missing pain [PAGE 8]. If \(\Pr(M_i=1\mid Z_i,Y_i,X_i)\) depends on \(Y_i\) even after \(X_i\), the complete-case estimand is not the target. The planned attrition model uses baseline covariates [PAGE 8], but it cannot verify outcome-dependent missingness.

Breakdown/singularity regimes:
- Positivity breakdown: no overlap in \(e(X)\) for some treated/control stratum; matching discards subjects or returns empty sets.
- Caliper breakdown: too tight produces few matches and poor precision; too loose produces imbalance above 0.2 SD.
- \(K\)-selection breakdown: if no \(K\in\{2,\ldots,8\}\) simultaneously preserves sample size and balance, the protocol’s selection rule is not decisive.
- Sparse matched-set breakdown: if a matched set has all outcomes equal, the conditional logistic contribution is degenerate and \(\beta\) is not identified.
- Ordered-testing breakdown: if the main interval contains every plausible \(\tau_0\), secondary comparisons are never reached; the design cannot resolve control-group heterogeneity.
- Sensitivity breakdown: if a result becomes insignificant at very small \(\Gamma\), the causal claim is fragile; if \(\Gamma\) is not reported, robustness is unverified.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study’s 1957 graduation cohort to the 2004 WLS interviews, when subjects were approximately age 65 [PAGE 3–4; PAGE 7]. That ~47-year gap is the central retrocausal problem: exposure is measured retrospectively from high-school yearbooks, while outcomes are measured decades later. The WLS is described as a “long-term study of a random sample of 10,317 men and women who graduated from Wisconsin high schools in 1957” [PAGE 3]. The protocol restricts to men and uses yearbook reviews performed between January 2005 and February 2011 to reconstruct sport participation [PAGE 4]. Thus the exposure variable is not a contemporaneous randomized assignment; it is a historical reconstruction.

The methodological genealogy is Rosenbaum-style observational causal inference: propensity scores, rank-based Mahalanobis matching, variable-ratio matching, matched-set conditional models, and \(\Gamma\)-sensitivity analysis [PAGE 9; PAGE 12–13]. The ordered-testing procedure is explicitly borrowed from Deshpande et al. (2017), which studied high-school football and later cognition/mental health [PAGE 10; PAGE 17]. This lineage matters because the protocol is designed to answer a public-health question while inheriting the assumptions of that matching literature: conditional exchangeability given observed covariates, stable treatment version, and adequate overlap.

Multi-week traps:
1. Historical treatment-version drift. Football in Wisconsin in the 1950s differs from modern football in equipment, rules, concussion recognition, and training. The protocol cites rule changes such as moving the kickoff line forward to reduce returns [PAGE 3], but the 1957 cohort predates modern concussion management. A claim about “football” simpliciter is therefore a claim about a historical version of football.
2. BMI era drift. The protocol adjusts for standardized BMI and uses maximum adult BMI as an outcome [PAGE 6–7]. But BMI norms, diet, smoking, and occupational physical activity changed substantially between 1957 and 2004. Maximum lifetime weight is chosen because “weight assessed in older age fails to separate subjects who were obese and then lost weight because of illness from subjects whose weight never changed” [PAGE 7]. That is a good outcome-construction defense, but it does not eliminate cohort-period confounding.
3. Attrition/survivorship. The 2004 sample is conditioned on survival and WLS response. The protocol plans an attrition analysis [PAGE 8], but a complete-case estimate can still be selected on late-life health. This is a retrocausal trap because the outcome sample is a filter on the future.
4. Multi-sport participation. Table 1 reports participation counts by sport, not unique persons; baseball 786, basketball 1,046, football 1,420, track 804, etc. [PAGE 5]. A person can appear in multiple sport rows. The protocol excludes hockey and wrestling because “these sports also involve frequent, violent collisions with other players or the ground and our goal is to isolate the effect of playing football specifically” [PAGE 4]. That is a physical-specificity defense, but it does not measure position, playing time, or collision dose within football.
5. Negative-control drift. Cancer is included because “there is no scientific basis for a connection between football and cancer” [PAGE 7]. If cancer shows an association, the match or the cohort is suspect. This is a useful counter-trap, but it is not a formal proof of no confounding.
6. Protocol-to-results drift. The abstract’s 1,153/2,751 counts conflict with the methods’ 1,259/2,096 counts [PAGE 1; PAGE 4]. A multi-week analysis pipeline that does not lock the analytic sample can silently switch cohorts. The pre-analysis plan mitigates this only if the final sample is audited against the protocol arithmetic.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: the default failure mode in observational football-health research is to compare raw groups, treat the association as causal, and then rationalize inconsistencies after seeing results. The protocol anticipates this by pre-specifying matching, ordered testing, multiple control pools, a negative control, and sensitivity analysis. The “crisis habit” would be to abandon those safeguards when a result is inconvenient—e.g., dropping the sport-control comparison, redefining the primary outcome, or reporting only the unadjusted difference. The source’s own structure is a counter-trap: it states four comparisons—football vs all controls, football vs sport controls, football vs non-sport controls, and sport controls vs non-sport controls—and says “We construct a separate match for each comparison” [PAGE 10]. A reviewer can therefore ask whether all four were reported, not just the most favorable one.

Counter-trap: the protocol uses cancer as a negative control because “there is no scientific basis for a connection between football and cancer” [PAGE 7]. It also uses the sport-control vs non-sport-control comparison to probe unmeasured personality/fitness differences: “It may be the case that these two subgroups differ along unmeasured dimensions related to personality or ﬁtness that may aﬀect our study outcomes” [PAGE 9–10]. This is recursive theory of mind in the design: the investigators imagine a skeptical reviewer who says “athletes differ from non-athletes in ways you did not measure,” and they build the comparison that can detect that difference. The counter-counter-trap is that the sport-control vs non-sport-control contrast may itself be underpowered or confounded; consistency across comparisons is reassuring but not decisive.

Clock: the clock is the 1957-to-2004 interval. The primary outcome is measured at age 65 [PAGE 1], and the WLS interviews in 2004 supply self-rated health, pain, ADL difficulty, and cancer [PAGE 7–8]. The clock creates three recursive pressures. First, subjects who die or stop responding before 2004 are not in the outcome sample; the protocol’s attrition analysis [PAGE 8] is a clock-aware defense, but it cannot recover missing outcomes. Second, the historical football exposure is not the same treatment as modern football; a reviewer in 2026 may over-read a 1957 cohort as if it were a modern safety study. Third, the multi-week analysis clock—time spent tuning \(K\), calipers, missingness strata, and control pools—creates researcher degrees of freedom. The pre-analysis plan is the counter-trap: it fixes the ordered testing and sensitivity reporting before results are known. The recursive failure would be to let the analysis clock run until the specification matches the desired narrative; the protocol’s defense is that the decision rules are stated in advance, including the \(\Gamma\)-reporting rule [PAGE 12–13].

In short: crisis habit = post hoc outcome switching; counter-trap = ordered testing plus negative control plus multiple control pools; clock = fixed 1957 exposure, 2004 outcome, and a long attrition window. The protocol is strongest where it pre-commits to reporting fragility, and weakest where the abstract/methods sample counts are inconsistent and where no outcome results yet exist.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto exists when a physical, biomechanical, or weather-layer variable \(L\) opens a backdoor path \(Z\leftarrow L\to Y\) conditional on the matched covariates \(X\), or when \(L\) changes the meaning of the treatment version. Formally, for the target \(\tau=E[Y(1)-Y(0)]\), matching supports \(\tau\) only under conditional exchangeability. A layer variable vetoes the claim if:
\[
Y(z)\not\perp Z\mid X,L \quad\text{and}\quad L\not\perp Z\mid X,
\]
or if the exposure \(Z\) is a mixture of distinct biomechanical treatments with different effects. The veto is not a generic “football is dangerous” claim; it is a specific failure of identification.

Physical layer: the source documents high injury burden—“over 440,000 injuries that require medical attention related to participation in high school football” [PAGE 2]—and notes that head injuries such as concussion represent 9–11% of injuries and are often unreported [PAGE 2]. It also cites early-life traumatic brain injury associations with disability pension and welfare benefits [PAGE 3]. These are plausible physical mechanisms, but they are mechanisms of the exposure, not necessarily confounders. The protocol adjusts for childhood health, asthma, pneumonia, bronchitis, family history, smoking exposure, BMI, and socioeconomic variables [PAGE 6]. After matching, Table 4 shows absolute standardized differences below 0.2 for the displayed variables [PAGE 12]. That is evidence against strong observed physical confounding, not proof against unmeasured injury history.

Biomechanical layer: the protocol excludes hockey and wrestling because they “also involve frequent, violent collisions with other players or the ground” and the goal is “to isolate the effect of playing football specifically” [PAGE 4]. This is a biomechanical specificity veto defense: it removes other collision sports that could contaminate the contrast. But the defense is incomplete because football itself is biomechanically heterogeneous by position, playing time, and era. The source does not report position, snap count, or collision dose. Therefore a claim about “football” is a claim about a bundled treatment; a position-specific or dose-response claim would be vetoed by the absence of biomechanical dose measurement. The matching balance on standardized BMI (0.416 before, 0.083 after) [PAGE 12] addresses one physical/biomechanical covariate, but not all load variables.

Weather layer: the source contains no weather variables, no outdoor temperature, no season duration, and no climate data. Wisconsin football is outdoor and seasonal, but the protocol does not measure weather exposure. A weather-layer veto would require evidence that weather affects both football participation and late-life health conditional on \(X\). The source provides none. The correct adjudication is therefore “unidentified, not proven”: weather cannot be used to affirm or deny the causal claim from this protocol alone. If a downstream analyst asserts a weather-mediated mechanism, the veto is valid because the source lacks the variables; if an analyst asserts no weather confounding, that is also unproven. The only defensible statement is that weather is outside the measured design.

Verdict: physical and biomechanical layers partially veto strong causal language. The protocol handles observed physical confounders and other collision sports, but it does not measure within-football biomechanical dose or injury history. The weather layer is a null/unmeasured layer; it neither supports nor refutes the primary self-rated health question. Any final claim should be phrased as a matched association in a 1957 Wisconsin male cohort, not as a universal football-safety verdict.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
