<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is direct: Sinkhorn’s 1964 matrix-scaling theorem and the Sinkhorn-Knopp iteration provide the alternating row and column normalization used in Algorithm 1 [PAGE 1]. The entropic optimal transport formulation is the discrete regularized problem \(P_\varepsilon\); in the modern optimal-transport literature it is the Cuturi entropic-regularization viewpoint, later systematized in Peyré-Cuturi style computational optimal transport. The source’s lecture context says “We know form Lecture 3” that the optimal solution has diagonal scaling form [PAGE 1], which is the Lagrange-multiplier derivation above. The retrocausal trap is that the algorithm looks like a simple normalization loop, so students often forget that the theorem requires a positive kernel and positive marginals. A second historical trap is the entropy sign: the source uses \(e(r)=r(\log r-1)\), while many texts write Shannon entropy with a minus sign, so a sign error reverses the large-\(\varepsilon\) limit from product coupling to concentration. A third multi-week trap is treating \(D_\phi,D_\psi\) as the answer rather than as scaling factors; the plan is the product \(\operatorname{diag}(D_\phi)K^\varepsilon\operatorname{diag}(D_\psi)\), as stated in [PAGE 1]. The \(\varepsilon\to0\) question is also a lineage trap: in finite-dimensional discrete OT, the limit is an unregularized OT plan, not a continuous Monge map unless one takes a further discretization limit. For quadratic cost on the line, the sorted matching is the correct reference; for non-Monge costs or multidimensional problems, the limiting plan may be nonunique or fractional. The truncated-Gaussian exercise in question 5 is a forward echo of the first practical class; its “smallest \(\varepsilon\)” is not a theoretical limit but a numerical continuation boundary determined by underflow and conditioning.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

A third regression is to label \(D_\phi\) and \(D_\psi\) as the marginal probabilities, even though the source says they are diagonal matrices and the plan is a three-factor product [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is to slow down and enforce the invariant: positive kernel, positive marginals, full-iteration reconstruction, both row and column checks, and a tolerance-based stop. The clock matters because the convergence rate degrades as \(\varepsilon\) decreases: \(\kappa(K^\varepsilon)\to1\) as \(\varepsilon\to0\), so the iteration budget must be increased or a stabilized log-domain solver used. A good recursive policy is: first verify small-\(\varepsilon\) stability on a tiny \(N\); then use \(\varepsilon\)-continuation from a large value down to the smallest stable value; then compare the barycentric projection with the previous class reference.
