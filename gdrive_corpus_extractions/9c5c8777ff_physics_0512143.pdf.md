1. Winning fraction. For team i in a season with G_i games, let W_i be wins and T_i be tied games treated as half-wins where applicable. The source defines "The winning fraction, the ratio of wins to total games" [PAGE 1]. A formal representation consistent with the text is
x_i = W_i / G_i, with x_i dimensionless, G_i a game count, W_i a game count.
In leagues with ties, the source's convention is that ties count as 1/2 of a victory for both teams [PAGE 2], so W_i can be a half-integer effective win count. The source reports tie fractions: "The fraction of ties in soccer, hockey, and football is 0.246, 0.144, and 0.016, respectively" [PAGE 1, Table I caption].

2. Empirical distribution. The source computes "F(x), the fraction of teams with a winning fraction of x or lower at the end of the season" [PAGE 1]. Formally, over a set of N team-seasons,
F(x) = (1/N) Σ_i 1[x_i ≤ x],
where F is dimensionless and 1[·] is an indicator.

3. Dispersion operator. The source defines "σ = sqrt(⟨x^2⟩ − ⟨x⟩^2), the standard deviation in winning fraction" [PAGE 1]. Thus
σ = sqrt(⟨x^2⟩ − ⟨x⟩^2), dimensionless.
Here ⟨·⟩ denotes "the average over all teams and all years using season-end standings" [PAGE 1]. This is a population-style average over the available team-seasons, not a Bayesian posterior.

4. Random-walk null. The source says that if "the outcome of a game is completely random, the total number of wins performs a simple random walk, and the standard deviation σ is inversely proportional to the square root of the number of games played" [PAGE 1]. Under the standard independent fair-game assumption with equal G games, W_i ~ Binomial(G, 1/2), so
Var(W_i/G) = (1/4G), hence σ_null ≈ 1/(2√G).
This is a derived null model, not a source-fitted law; the source's statement is the inverse-square-root scaling. Units: G is a count; σ_null is dimensionless.

5. Upset-probability operator. In the model, "with the 'upset probability' q < 1/2, the underdog wins, but otherwise, the favorite wins" [PAGE 1]. Thus q is a probability, 0 ≤ q < 1/2 in the model's intended range. Directly from game results, the source measures q "by counting the fraction of times that the team with the worse record on the game date actually won" [PAGE 2]. Formally, over eligible games m=1,...,M,
q_meas = (1/M) Σ_m 1[worse-record team wins game m],
with ties in soccer, hockey, and football counted as 1/2 of a victory for both teams [PAGE 2]. Games "between teams with no record (start of a season) or teams with equal records were disregarded" [PAGE 2]. q_meas is dimensionless.

6. Infinite-season relation. The source states: "In a hypothetical season with an infinite number of games, the winning fraction distribution is uniform in the range q < x < 1 −q and as a result, σ = (1/2 −q)/√3" [PAGE 2]. Therefore, under that ideal limit,
σ(q) = (1/2 − q)/√3, dimensionless,

7. Simulation-fit operator. The source runs "Monte Carlo simulations of these artificial sports leagues, with sport-specific number of games and a range of q values" and determines "the value of q that gives the best match between the distribution F(x) from the simulations to the actual sports statistics" [PAGE 2]. Schematically,
q_model = argmin_q D(F_sim(q; G_league), F_data),
where D is a distributional distance. The source does not name D in the excerpt, so D should not be over-specified. q_model is dimensionless.

Substituting finite-season σ into the inverse q=1/2−√3σ without correction is invalid in general.

- Label-inversion regime q > 1/2: the model says the underdog wins with probability q < 1/2; if q > 1/2, the labels "favorite" and "underdog" reverse in empirical content, so the formula is outside its stated parameterization.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND><![CDATA[
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

The recursive theory-of-mind structure is visible in the paper's rhetoric. The authors anticipate a reader's first inference from Figure 1 and Table I: wide σ means low parity, so NFL is least competitive. They state the inference and reject it: "Do these results imply that MLB games are the most competitive and NFL games the least? Not necessarily!" [PAGE 1]. That is a second-order move: the authors model the reader's model of the data, then model the reader's likely overcorrection.

Crisis habit regression. Under time pressure, the habitual move is to grab the most salient scalar. Here the salient scalar is σ, because it is visually and numerically presented before q. The habit is reinforced by Table I: NFL has the largest σ=0.210, MLB the smallest σ=0.084 [PAGE 1]. A crisis-mode analyst reverts to that ranking and says "NFL is least competitive." But the source has already supplied the countervailing mechanism: shorter seasons inflate σ, and "the shorter the season, the larger σ" [PAGE 1]. The regression is not a data error; it is an operator-selection error. The analyst uses the season-end dispersion operator where the game-level q operator is required.

Counter-trap. The opposite failure is to overcorrect: "season length explains everything, so no real competitiveness differences exist." That is also a trap. The authors do not stop at the season-length confound. They build a model with q, run Monte Carlo simulations, fit q_model to F(x), and also measure q directly from chronological game results [PAGE 1, PAGE 2]. They report stable orderings: FA q=0.452 and MLB q=0.441 are highest; NBA q=0.365 and NFL q=0.364 are lowest [PAGE 2]. The correct counter-trap is not "nothing can be inferred"; it is "infer only through the q operator and only within its assumptions." The source also warns that q_model is approximate, with visible gaps such as NBA 0.365 measured versus 0.316 fitted [PAGE 1]. So the counter-counter-trap is to avoid treating q_model as exact.

Clock. There are at least three clocks in the paper. The first is the season clock G_i: 14.0 games on average in NFL, 155.5 in MLB [PAGE 1]. The second is the historical clock: Figure 2 tracks cumulative σ and q by year, and the authors report that NFL and MLB are becoming more competitive while FA has displayed an opposite trend over 60 years [PAGE 2]. The third is the mathematical clock of the infinite-season limit, where σ=(1/2−q)/√3 [PAGE 2]. The crisis habit confuses these clocks. It applies an infinite-season formula to finite seasons, or it treats a century-long cumulative q as a timeless property. The recursive correction is to ask which clock the claim uses. If the claim is about a single season, use finite-G simulation or direct q. If the claim is about an era, use era-specific cumulative q. If the claim is about the model's limiting behavior, use the infinite-season formula and label it as hypothetical.

The theory-of-mind audit also reveals an epistemic asymmetry. The authors can measure q directly from game results, but they also want a bridge from standings data to q because standings are "more easily-accessible" [PAGE 2]. That bridge is useful, but it creates a temptation: once the bridge exists, analysts may forget it is a model. The source's own language is careful: the model "can be used to estimate the upset frequency from standings data" [PAGE 1]. Estimate, not identify. The recursive stance is therefore: model the naive reader (σ ranking), model the overcorrector (season length explains all), and then impose the clock-aware, operator-aware path through q and q_model.

The game-level measurement uses chronological standings and counts whether the worse-record team won "on the game date" [PAGE 2]. The source explicitly says: "Game location was ignored and so was the margin of victory" [PAGE 2]. It also says: "Our idealized model does not include the notion of innate team strength" [PAGE 2]. There is no measured variable for weather, temperature, altitude, playing surface, injury load, body mass, biomechanical force, travel distance, or physiological fatigue. Therefore any cross-layer causal claim that a league is less competitive because of weather or biomechanics is not identifiable from this source.

Formal veto. Let D be the source's data object: D = {league, year, games, wins, ties, chronological records}. Let W be a weather variable and B a biomechanical variable. The source operators q_meas and q_model are functions of D, not of W or B. Since W and B are not in D, no conditional contrast P(q | W=w) − P(q | W=w') is estimable. The same holds for B. A claim such as "NFL is less competitive because of cold-weather biomechanics" asserts a cross-layer path D ← W/B → q, but the source only licenses D → q. The cross-layer edge is vetoed unless new data are supplied.

The location clause strengthens the veto. Location is a proxy for venue, climate, and travel, yet the source says location was ignored [PAGE 2]. Thus weather is not conditioned, stratified, or adjusted. The margin-of-victory clause removes a possible intensity or physical-dominance signal [PAGE 2]. The no-innate-strength clause removes a stable talent or physical-quality parameter [PAGE 2]. The model's "favorite" is simply the team with the better current record; it is not a measured physical strength. The authors say the "spontaneous emergence of disparate-strength teams" is the mechanism, not an input [PAGE 2]. That is a statistical emergence claim, not a biomechanical one.

What survives the veto? Only a negative adjudication: the source can say that q differs across leagues and eras; it cannot say why in physical or meteorological terms.
