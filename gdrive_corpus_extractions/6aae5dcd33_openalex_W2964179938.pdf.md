<THINK_TRACE>
The source derives nationality and ethnicity inference from a single measurable primitive: names, but it refuses to treat names as isolated substrings. The first inferential jump is from surface orthography to latent social structure. The paper says: “We exploit the phenomena of homophily in communication patterns to learn name embeddings” [PAGE 1]. That sentence is the hinge of the entire architecture. Homophily is not used as a vague sociological flavor; it becomes a data-generating assumption: if account holder A communicates with account holder B, then A and B are more likely to share latent demographic attributes than a random pair. The paper invokes the classic principle: “birds of a feather ﬂock together” [PAGE 2]. The formal consequence is that contact lists can be converted into contexts for embedding learning. If names are words and contact lists are documents, then the context of a name is the set of frequent, recent contacts surrounding it. The source states the operational rule: “we weigh contacts by recency and frequency of communications” [PAGE 3]. It then constructs training contexts by “keeping top contacts of a sorted list” [PAGE 3]. This is crucial because the ordering of contacts inside a contact list is not treated as narrative sequence; instead, the sorted list is a way to select high-mass contexts.

The second inferential jump is from embeddings to Bayesian classification. The source uses a Naive Bayes model because names are decomposed into first and last parts. The core equation is: “P(N |vf ,vl) ∝P(vf |N)P(vl |N)P(N)” [PAGE 4]. Here N is nationality, vf is first name, vl is last name. The conditional independence assumption is explicit: the paper says it obtains the equation “under the assumption that vf and vl are conditionally independent given N” [PAGE 4]. This is a strong assumption, but it is made tractable by the hierarchy: coarse classes can absorb cross-national name overlap before fine leaf assignment. The source also emphasizes that first and last names jointly reduce errors: “Qiang Lee” and “John Lee” cannot be resolved by surname alone, because “Lee” is popular in both China and the UK [PAGE 4]. The joint posterior therefore encodes a disambiguating interaction between name parts.

The third inferential jump is from direct name counts to a multi-source likelihood stack. The paper defines four parameter regimes: training data, name embedding, prefix/suffix strings, and name characters. It explicitly orders them: “the increasing order of vocabulary size is Vtr , Vem, Vp/s, Vch, which is also the decreasing order of estimation confidence” [PAGE 4]. This is a graceful degradation ladder. For frequent names, direct counts dominate. For names absent from labeled training counts but present in embeddings, the model borrows strength from nearest neighbors. For rare names lacking embeddings, prefix/suffix evidence is used. For extremely rare names or non-Latin scripts, character-script priors are used. The Hangul example is explicit: a name written in “Hangul” is very likely Korean because most names in that script are Korean [PAGE 5]. This is not a claim about biological essence; it is a claim about corpus-level conditional distributions.

The fourth inferential jump is from Internet sample to world population. The paper is unusually careful here. It says: “We have PI (vi |N) = PW (vi |N)” by assuming that names of Internet population are random samples from corresponding countries [PAGE 5]. That assumption is fragile, and the authors know it. They provide a correction using sample ratios: Internet prevalence differs from world prevalence. The example is stark: “UK and South Africa have similar population (around 50M to 60M)” but the dataset has many more UK names than South African names [PAGE 5]. Without correction, the classifier would overrepresent high-Internet countries. The correction is not cosmetic; it changes the prior term in the Naive Bayes equation.

The gray-zone audit begins with construct validity. Nationality is not ethnicity, and neither is directly observed from a name. The paper acknowledges the difficulty: “manual annotation of nationality by names is, in fact, a very diﬃcult task” [PAGE 2]. It also acknowledges that privacy makes self-reporting hard [PAGE 1]. The classifier is therefore a proxy layer, not ground truth. A second gray zone is population coverage. The taxonomy covers “39 groups representing over 90% of the world population” [PAGE 1], but “over 90%” is not “all.” Low-Internet countries are weak. The paper admits limited performance for Central Asian and Maghreb nationalities because of scarce labels and contact lists [PAGE 8]. A third gray zone is temporal drift. Recency-weighted contacts imply that the embedding manifold is nonstationary. If communication norms change, or if Internet penetration changes, the mapping from name to posterior changes. A fourth gray zone is adversarial or strategic behavior. The social media section shows that follower populations can be anomalous: an Indonesian politician has “only 13% are Indonesian” followers, while “50% of the followers of British, Russian, or Indian nationality” [PAGE 9-10]. This is not merely a classification error; it is a signal that names can be used to audit possible artificial amplification. The model is not asked to prove fraud, but to expose a distributional anomaly that warrants further investigation.

The final reasoning chain is therefore: homophily creates contexts; contexts create embeddings; embeddings plus counts create likelihoods; likelihoods plus priors create posteriors; posteriors are evaluated by F1 and applied to population diagnostics. The source’s own headline metric is explicit: “our F1 score (0.795) is substantial beter than our closest competitor Ethnea (0.580)” [PAGE 1]. But the deeper contribution is not the raw score. It is the separation between name surface, embedding geometry, hierarchical taxonomy, and population correction. Those layers allow the system to remain useful when some layers are sparse, noisy, or biased.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source can be formalized as a probabilistic information system with five operator layers: homophily context construction, embedding geometry, Naive Bayes likelihood estimation, hierarchical decoding, and population-prior correction. All probabilities are dimensionless quantities in the interval [0,1]. Counts are dimensionless counts of persons, names, or contact events. Information-theoretic diagnostics derived from these probabilities can be expressed in bits when using base-2 logarithms, or in nats when using natural logarithms.

1. Homophily context operator.
Let C_i be the contact list of account holder i. The source states that contacts are weighted by “recency and frequency of communications” [PAGE 3]. A formal weighted contact mass is:

\[
m_{ij} = \alpha r_{ij} + \beta f_{ij}
\]

where r_{ij} is a recency score for contact j, f_{ij} is a communication frequency, and \alpha,\beta are nonnegative weights. The source does not specify exact SI time units for recency, but if timestamps are measured in seconds, then r_{ij} can be normalized as a dimensionless decay score. The top-contact sentence construction is:

\[
S_i = \operatorname{TopK}\left( \{(v_j, m_{ij})\} \right)
\]

where v_j is a name token and K is a cutoff. The source says the method constructs a “sentence” by “keeping top contacts of a sorted list” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regime: if C_i is empty, or all m_{ij}=0, then S_i is undefined and the account contributes no context. If recency metadata is missing, the operator degenerates to frequency-only.

2. Embedding operator.
The learned embedding is a map:

\[
f_\theta : V \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{100}
\]

The source uses “100D name embeddings” projected to two dimensions for visualization [PAGE 3]. The vector norm is a real-valued magnitude, while cosine similarity is dimensionless:

\[
\cos(u,v) = \frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|\|v\|}
\]

The paper tests CBOW and SG embedding variants [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if a name v is outside the embedding vocabulary, f_\theta(v) is undefined. If ||u||=0 or ||v||=0, cosine similarity diverges. Rare names, transliterated names, and logographic forms are especially vulnerable. The source notes that substring methods are ineffective for logograms [PAGE 1], but embeddings can still fail if the name never appears in contact contexts.

3. Naive Bayes posterior operator.
The central classification equation is:

\[
P(N \mid v_f, v_l) \propto P(v_f \mid N) P(v_l \mid N) P(N)
\]

The source gives this as Eq. 1 and cites it explicitly: “P(N |vf ,vl) ∝P(vf |N)P(vl |N)P(N)” [PAGE 4]. Units: P(N) is a prior probability over nationalities; P(v_f|N) and P(v_l|N) are likelihoods over name tokens. The proportionality becomes equality after normalization:

\[
P(N \mid v_f, v_l) = \frac{P(v_f \mid N) P(v_l \mid N) P(N)}{\sum_{N'} P(v_f \mid N') P(v_l \mid N') P(N')}
\]

The denominator is dimensionless and positive if at least one class has nonzero posterior mass. Breakdown regime: if P(N)=0 for the true class, that class cannot be recovered. If P(v_f|N)=0 or P(v_l|N)=0 for all plausible N, the posterior collapses. The hierarchical classifier uses a small constant σ for unseen parts, described as “a small constant” smoothing value [PAGE 5].

4. Direct training-count likelihood.
For names in training vocabulary V_tr, the source defines:

\[
P_{tr}(v_i \mid N) = \frac{C(v_i,N)}{C(N)}
\]

where C(v_i,N) is the count of name part v_i with nationality N, and C(N) is the aggregate count over name parts for N. Units: C(v_i,N) and C(N) are counts of name occurrences or labeled persons, depending on the dataset construction. The source says each name part in V_tr has more than 5 occurrences in training data to ensure high confidence [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if C(N)=0, the conditional probability is undefined. If C(v_i,N) is small, the estimate has high variance. The paper’s use of more than 5 occurrences is a minimum-support condition.

5. Embedding-derived likelihood.
For names in V_em, the source estimates a posterior from nearest neighbors. The printed equation is:

\[
P_{em}(N \mid v_i) = \frac{1}{|kNN(v_i)|} \prod_{v_j \in kNN(v_i)} P_{tr}(N \mid v_j)
\]

The text says the method first estimates posterior using neighbors’ posteriors and then applies Bayes rule [PAGE 4-5]. The Bayes conversion is:

\[
P_{em}(v_i \mid N) = \frac{P_{em}(N \mid v_i) P(v_i)}{P(N)}
\]

Units: P(v_i) is a marginal probability of name token v_i; P(N) is class prior. Breakdown regimes: (a) kNN(v_i) empty, which makes the divisor zero; (b) one or more neighbor posteriors equal zero, which can annihilate a product if the product form is used literally; (c) P(N)=0, which makes the Bayes conversion singular. A numerically coherent implementation may replace the literal product with an average or geometric mean, because the source text describes averaging over nearest neighbors [PAGE 4]. This is an important gray-zone: the printed operator and the prose description must be reconciled.

6. Prefix/suffix likelihood.
For names in V_{p/s}, the source uses prefix/suffix strings of length 3 to 5:

\[
P_{p/s}(v_i \mid N) = \frac{1}{|PS(v_i)|} \prod_{v_j \in PS(v_i)} P_{tr}(v_j \mid N)
\]

The surrounding text says the method averages likelihoods of name parts sharing the same prefix/suffix [PAGE 5]. Again, the product form as printed is fragile. If any factor is zero, the result vanishes. If PS(v_i) is empty, the estimator diverges. A stable version would use arithmetic mean in probability space or sum of log-likelihoods with smoothing. The breakdown regime is especially relevant for very short names or names with ambiguous affixes.

7. Character-script likelihood.
For names in V_ch, the source uses names written in the same language characters:

\[
P_{ch}(v_i \mid N) = \frac{1}{|CH(v_i)|} \prod_{v_j \in CH(v_i)} P_{tr}(v_j \mid N)
\]

The example is Hangul: if a name is written in Hangul, it is likely Korean because most Hangul names in training data are Korean [PAGE 5]. Units remain probabilities. Breakdown regime: CH(v_i) empty if no training names share the script; script collision if multiple nationalities use the same script; Unicode normalization failure if combining characters are not canonicalized.

8. Internet-to-world prior correction.
The source distinguishes Internet probabilities P_I from world probabilities P_W. It states: “We have PI (vi |N) = PW (vi |N)” by assuming Internet names are random samples from corresponding countries [PAGE 5]. The prior relation is:

\[
P_I(N) = P_W(N) \frac{s_N}{S}
\]

where s_N is the sample ratio for nationality N and S is the overall sample ratio. Units: both s_N and S are dimensionless ratios of observed Internet names to population names. Breakdown regimes: if s_N=0 for an underrepresented country, its prior collapses to zero; if S=0, the correction diverges. If Internet sampling is nonrandom, the assumption P_I(v_i|N)=P_W(v_i|N) fails, and the correction only partially repairs bias.

9. Hierarchical decoding operator.
Algorithm 1 defines a top-down decoder. At node T, the classifier chooses child class N_i maximizing posterior:

\[
T^{*} = \arg\max_{N_i \in \operatorname{children}(T)} P(N_i \mid v_f, v_l)
\]

The source says classification starts from the root and proceeds “until it meets a leaf class” [PAGE 5]. The taxonomy has 39 leaf nationalities [PAGE 1,2]. Breakdown regime: if the taxonomy is mis-specified, a name may be forced into an incorrect branch before reaching the correct leaf. Greedy top-down decoding is locally optimal but not necessarily globally optimal over all root-to-leaf paths unless posteriors are calibrated across levels.

10. Evaluation operator: F1.
The paper reports F1 scores. For a class c, precision and recall can be written as:

\[
\mathrm{Precision}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c}
\]

\[
\mathrm{Recall}_c = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c}
\]

\[
F1_c = \frac{2 \mathrm{Precision}_c \mathrm{Recall}_c}{\mathrm{Precision}_c + \mathrm{Recall}_c}
\]

All are dimensionless. Weighted average F1 is:

\[
F1_{\mathrm{weighted}} = \sum_c w_c F1_c
\]

where w_c is count weight. The source reports weighted average F1 of 0.806 on the 39-leaf taxonomy [PAGE 8] and 0.795 on a 13-class evaluation [PAGE 1,2]. Breakdown regime: if TP_c + FP_c = 0 or TP_c + FN_c = 0, F1 is undefined or convention-dependent. Rare classes can be unstable unless smoothed or aggregated.

11. Information diagnostic.
A derived uncertainty measure is conditional entropy:

\[
H(N \mid V) = -\sum_{n,v} P(n,v) \log_2 P(n \mid v)
\]

Units: bits. If the model is deterministic, H(N|V)=0 bits. If the posterior is uniform over 39 leaf nationalities, H = \log_2 39 \approx 5.285 bits. This diagnostic is not printed in the source but follows directly from the probabilistic objects defined there. It is useful for identifying gray-zone names where the posterior is too flat for reliable leaf assignment.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retro-causal genealogy of this source is not a simple line from “names” to “nationality.” It is a layered inheritance from census categories, sociological homophily, word embedding theory, and prior name-classification systems. The earliest layer is biomedical and sociological demand. The paper says nationality and ethnicity are used as proxies for cultural and historical experience, and that name-based classification has been used to reflect genetic differences and public health disparity [PAGE 1]. This creates a retro-causal pressure: the desired downstream use, such as health disparity research, shapes the classifier’s categories and evaluation metrics. If the classifier is built to support population studies, then coarse census-compatible classes become attractive even when they obscure intra-group diversity.

The second layer is the substring tradition. The source says prior methods “use name substrings as features” and are constrained by “small and artiﬁcial training sets, such as celebrity names from Wikipedia” [PAGE 1]. This is a historical trap. Wikipedia-derived celebrity names are not demographically representative. They overrepresent public figures, transliterated elite names, and globally visible naming styles. The retro-causal trap is that a system trained on such data may look accurate on similar benchmark names while failing on everyday names from low-visibility populations. The paper’s response is to move from substrings to embeddings, but the genealogy shows that benchmark choice can silently encode old biases.

The third layer is word embedding theory. The source references word embedding and distributed representation work [PAGE 10], including neural probabilistic language models, word vectors, GloVe, DeepWalk, and exponential family embeddings. The key retro-causal inheritance is the distributional hypothesis: entities that appear in similar contexts have similar latent representations. The paper adapts this from words to names. But names are not words in narrative sentences; their context is a contact list. This adaptation introduces a new lineage: social network homophily. The source explicitly cites homophily in social life [PAGE 2]. The genealogical consequence is that the embedding space inherits not only linguistic similarity but also social segregation. That is powerful for classification, but dangerous if the embedding space encodes historical segregation or unequal Internet access.

The fourth layer is prior name-ethnicity classification. The source compares against HMM-based methods, Ethnea, and EthnicSeer [PAGE 2,7,8]. Those systems are part of the lineage because they established taxonomies, substring features, and evaluation datasets. The paper’s improvement is not merely algorithmic; it is representational. It uses contact-list embeddings as a new feature source. However, the retro-causal lesson is that any evaluation against prior systems is partly an evaluation of shared assumptions. The source notes that EthnicSeer performs better on Wikipedia because it is trained on the same dataset, calling this “an unfair comparison” [PAGE 8]. That admission is a methodological trap: benchmark familiarity can inflate performance.

The fifth layer is census taxonomy. The paper uses U.S. Census categories for ethnicity: White, Black, API, Hispanic, AIAN, and 2PRACE [PAGE 1,8]. For nationality, it constructs a taxonomy based on Cultural, Ethnic, and Linguistic similarities [PAGE 5]. The retro-causal issue is that census categories are historically situated. They are not natural kinds. They encode administrative needs, migration histories, and racial formation. When NamePrisme reduces nationality classes to six ethnic groups, it inherits the census ontology. This can be useful for U.S.-centric social analysis, but it can misclassify people whose identities do not align with U.S. administrative categories.

The sixth layer is population correction. The source’s Internet-to-world correction is a retro-causal admission that the data-generating process is not neutral. The paper says: “Internet services (Email and Twiter) has varying popularity in diﬀerent countries” [PAGE 5]. This creates a multi-week trap: if the classifier is deployed months after training, changes in platform adoption, migration, or naming fashion can alter the likelihood ratios. The correction equation is not a one-time fix; it requires updated country population counts and updated sample ratios.

The deepest multi-week trap is strategic behavior. The social media section shows that names can reveal artificial follower populations. The Indonesian politician example is not just an anomaly; it is a retro-causal warning that once a classifier becomes public, actors may learn how to evade or manipulate it. If fake followers use British, Russian, or Indian names to appear organic, the classifier’s signal becomes an object of strategic optimization. The genealogy therefore ends with a governance lesson: name embeddings are not neutral measurement instruments. They are historical, social, and adversarial artifacts.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind problem here is not a single agent classifying a name. It is a stack of agents modeling each other. Layer 0 is the name token. Layer 1 is the classifier estimating P(N|v_f,v_l). Layer 2 is the analyst interpreting the classifier output. Layer 3 is the social media user or strategic actor who knows analysts may infer nationality from names. Layer 4 is the platform or public that reacts to inferred ethnic composition. The source becomes a crisis instrument because each layer can regress to a simpler habit when uncertainty is high.

Crisis habit regression occurs when social pressure favors coarse categories. In political analysis, there is a temptation to collapse 39 nationalities into a few ethnic blocks. The source supports coarse reduction when needed: NamePrisme reduces nationality taxonomy from 39 leaf nodes to 6 ethnic groups and incorporates census-based ground truth [PAGE 2]. This is useful, but in crisis it can become a habit: the analyst wants a clear narrative, so the classifier is asked to provide a simple White/Black/API/Hispanic split. The recursive risk is that the audience believes the coarse label reveals group essence, while the classifier’s posterior may be weak or conflicting. The source’s own election analysis illustrates the temptation: it reports that “whites are substantially overrepresented among Trump’s followers, while Clinton and Obama have disproportionately more followers among minorities” [PAGE 9]. That is a probabilistic description of follower composition, not a claim about individual intent. In crisis, however, such a description can be socially weaponized if treated as a deterministic identity marker.

The counter-trap is to preserve uncertainty and hierarchy. The source’s hierarchical classifier is designed to avoid premature leaf commitment: it begins at the root and descends until a leaf class is reached [PAGE 5]. This is a recursive safeguard. It allows the system to say “European” when leaf-level evidence is weak, rather than forcing “German” or “French” with false precision. A counter-trap analyst would also use abstention. If H(N|V) is high, or if the name is OOV, the correct output is not a forced leaf but a distribution, a coarse class, or an explicit unknown. The source already provides the vocabulary for this: training data, embeddings, prefix/suffix, and character scripts form a confidence ladder [PAGE 4-5]. The recursive counter-trap is to move down that ladder only as far as the evidence permits.

The clock layer is temporal. The source weights contacts by recency [PAGE 3], and it acknowledges that Internet service popularity varies across countries [PAGE 5]. This means the model has a clock. A posterior estimated from old contact data may be stale. A world-prior correction based on old population counts may be stale. A follower analysis from 2017 may not describe the same population in a later election cycle. The clock also affects adversarial response: if bad actors learn that certain names evade detection, the classifier’s posterior must be recalibrated. Recursive theory of mind includes the analyst modeling the adversary’s model of the analyst. If the adversary knows the classifier uses embeddings and first/last-name posteriors, they may choose names that straddle multiple nationalities. The source’s own mixture-name discussion anticipates this: immigration and cross-nationality marriage create names that are difficult even for humans [PAGE 4].

The anomaly case is the clearest recursive loop. The source says an Indonesian politician’s followers are “only 13% are Indonesian” and that “50% of the followers of British, Russian, or Indian nationality” [PAGE 9-10]. The classifier observes names. The analyst observes classifier output. The strategic actor may have selected names to avoid suspicion. The public observes the anomaly and asks whether the following is artificial. The recursive danger is to treat the name-based anomaly as proof of bot behavior. It is not proof. It is a probabilistic trigger for deeper audit. The correct recursive stance is: the classifier estimates name-derived nationality distributions; the analyst must separate name evidence from account behavior, language, location, and network structure. The source’s own limitation is that it does not fully model bot intent; it models name distributions.

A formal belief hierarchy can be written without toy mechanics. Let A_0 be the observed name pair. Let A_1 be the classifier belief P(N|A_0). Let A_2 be the analyst’s belief about how A_1 will be interpreted. Let A_3 be a strategic actor’s belief about how A_1 and A_2 affect visibility or credibility. The recursive problem is that A_3 may alter A_0 over time. If strategic actors know that names influence inferred nationality, they may choose names that maximize desired inference. The classifier then becomes part of the environment it measures. The source’s defense is not perfect robustness; it is transparency about the inference chain, hierarchical abstention, and population correction. Those mechanisms reduce, but do not eliminate, recursive manipulation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The requested physical, biomechanical, and weather veto must be adjudicated against the actual causal layer of the source. The source is not a biomechanical model of name production, nor is it a meteorological model of social behavior. It is an information-theoretic and probabilistic classification system over name tokens and communication graphs. Therefore, a physical, biomechanical, or weather variable can veto the source only if it changes the observable distribution P(v_f,v_l|N), changes the sampling process, or invalidates the contact-context assumption. If it does not enter through one of those channels, it cannot directly veto the model.

Let C denote a physical, biomechanical, or weather covariate. Let V denote the observed name evidence, V=(v_f,v_l). Let N denote nationality. The source model estimates P(N|V). A direct physical veto would require:

\[
P(N \mid V, C) \neq P(N \mid V)
\]

and C must be observed or reliably inferable. If C is not observed, the veto cannot be operationalized inside the source. The source does not include biomechanical signing pressure, handwriting fatigue, weather exposure, or environmental temperature. It uses email and Twitter name records, not physical performance data. Therefore, no direct biomechanical or weather veto is available from the source.

There are two admissible indirect channels.

First, script production channel. The source discusses writing systems and names, and notes that substring methods are ineffective for logograms [PAGE 1]. It also gives the Hangul example [PAGE 5]. A biomechanical or physical constraint could matter if the writing system is tied to motor production, such as handwriting, but the source operates on typed email and Twitter names. Even if handwriting biomechanics influenced spelling in the offline world, the observed data are already normalized digital strings. The veto would require evidence that digital name strings systematically encode the physical production context. The source provides no such evidence. Therefore, the biomechanical veto is non-admissible unless an external dataset links name strings to physical writing conditions.

Second, sampling channel. Weather or physical environment can affect Internet access, device use, or platform adoption. If so, the effect enters through the Internet-to-world correction. The source says Internet services have varying popularity in different countries and that the model must adjust to real country populations [PAGE 5]. The correction uses sample ratios:

\[
P_I(N) = P_W(N) \frac{s_N}{S}
\]

If weather or physical conditions affect s_N, they are already absorbed into the sampling ratio at the country level. They do not directly change P(v|N) unless they alter which names appear in the contact data. The source assumes that Internet names are random samples from corresponding countries, but it also recognizes this assumption is imperfect. Therefore, the only valid physical or weather veto is an aggregate sampling veto: if C causes s_N to be nonrepresentative, then the prior correction is required. This is not a direct physical override of the classifier; it is a population-level adjustment.

The adjudication proof is therefore:

1. The source observes V and estimates P(N|V) through likelihoods and priors.
2. Physical, biomechanical, and weather variables are not observed in the source.
3. A direct veto requires C to alter P(N|V) and be measurable.
4. Since C is not measured, no direct veto can be proven from the source.
5. If C affects Internet sampling, its effect is mediated by s_N in the world-prior correction.
6. Therefore, the only admissible cross-layer veto is a sampling-bias veto, not a direct physical or weather veto.

This adjudication also protects against false anthropomorphism. Names are cultural artifacts, not biomechanical readouts. A weather variable cannot tell us that a name is “really” Korean or Nigerian. It can only tell us that some populations may be underrepresented in the data. The correct veto is therefore epistemic: do not overinterpret leaf predictions for populations with weak Internet coverage. The source itself supports this caution by admitting limited performance for regions with few Internet users [PAGE 8].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios for an RLVR-style evaluation, where a reward model must avoid rewarding false confidence, population bias, or strategic manipulation. Each scenario is grounded in the source and includes a prediction that can be tested on held-out data.

Scenario 1: Rare-script-only veto.
A name is written in Hangul or another script with no training-data presence, no embedding presence, and no valid prefix/suffix evidence. The source says that for names not in V_tr or V_em and without valid prefix/suffix, character-script evidence can be used, as with Hangul [PAGE 5]. The veto scenario is: the model should not output a high-confidence leaf nationality solely from script. If the reward gives high score for a confident leaf prediction in this regime, it is rewarding a fragile prior.

Prediction: For held-out rare-script names, a calibrated system should assign most posterior mass to the script-level class or abstain from leaf-level prediction. If forced to choose among 39 leaf classes, accuracy will be low and entropy high. The verifiable test is to compare leaf accuracy under three ablations: (a) full model, (b) no V_tr/V_em, (c) script-only. If script-only accuracy is high without calibration, the reward is unsafe. If script-only posterior is diffuse and the system abstains, the reward is safer. The expected source-grounded outcome is that script-only evidence supports coarse nationality, not reliable leaf nationality.

Scenario 2: Internet-population prior veto.
The source gives the example that the UK and South Africa have similar population sizes, but the dataset contains many more UK names than South African names [PAGE 5]. The veto scenario is: a classifier trained without the world-prior correction will overpredict high-Internet nationalities and underpredict low-Internet nationalities.

Prediction: On a balanced world-population test set, the uncorrected Internet-prior model will assign disproportionate posterior mass to countries with high email/Twitter penetration. The corrected model using the sample-ratio adjustment will reduce this bias. The verifiable test is to compare predictions with and without the correction on names sampled to match country populations rather than Internet sample frequencies. The source reports NamePrism and NamePrism_w variants, where NamePrism_w uses world population priors [PAGE 7]. The expected result is improved macro-balance and reduced overprediction for high-Internet classes, though not necessarily uniform F1 improvement for every class. If a reward gives equal credit for raw accuracy on an Internet-skewed test set, it will reward the biased prior. That reward should be vetoed.

Scenario 3: Anomalous follower-set veto.
The source reports an Indonesian politician whose followers are “only 13% are Indonesian” and where “50% of the followers of British, Russian, or Indian nationality” [PAGE 9-10]. The veto scenario is: a reward system should not treat name-derived nationality distribution alone as proof of fraudulent followers. It also should not ignore the anomaly. The correct output is an audit trigger, not a definitive label.

Prediction: On held-out follower sets, NamePrism will produce a nationality distribution that diverges sharply from the expected domestic distribution for the politician’s language and country. If the follower set is organic, the domestic class should dominate after filtering for irregular names and accounting for diaspora. If the follower set is artificial or manipulated, foreign-name classes will remain overrepresented even after controlling for diaspora and language. The verifiable test is to compare the name-derived distribution against independent signals such as location fields, language metadata, account creation timing, and interaction patterns. The source itself notes that the politician’s tweets are in Indonesian, which most followers cannot understand [PAGE 9]. That is an independent behavioral clue. The expected counterfactual is: if the anomalous foreign-name distribution persists after controlling for diaspora and account quality, the anomaly is likely non-organic; if it disappears, the initial signal was likely a sampling artifact. A reward that treats the name distribution as conclusive evidence should be vetoed; a reward that treats it as a flagged anomaly requiring multi-signal verification is admissible.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
