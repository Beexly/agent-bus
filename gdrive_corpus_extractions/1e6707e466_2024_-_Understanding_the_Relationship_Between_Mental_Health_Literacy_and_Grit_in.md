<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression: Under acute psychological stress, adolescent female athletes are theorized to regress to the “toughness and invulnerability” script described by Reardon and Factor (2010) and Lopez and Levy (2013) [PAGE 3]. In this regression, signs of emotional difficulty are minimized as weakness; mental health issues are reframed as lack of toughness; and the “no pain, no gain” norm denies emotional problems, dissuading help-seeking [PAGE 3]. The present data show athletes score higher on Grit (p = .003) and Commitment (p = .001) than nonathletes [PAGE 6], which is consistent with a crisis habit profile: high perseverance paired with low help-seeking inclination. Because MHL did not correlate with Grit or Commitment, the regression model predicts that high-grit athletes possess the behavioral stamina to endure distress without activating the MHL help-seeking pathway. Their crisis response is to push through, not to parse symptoms or locate resources.

Counter-trap: The counter-trap is that high Challenge and Commitment scores may generate a false sense of self-sufficiency that masks inadequate MHL. An athlete who believes “challenges bring out the best in me” [PAGE 5] may interpret anxiety, depression, or suicidal ideation as a personal performance barrier to be overcome by effort rather than a condition requiring literacy-guided intervention. Bird et al. (2021) found mental toughness unassociated with public/self-stigma but associated with lower stigma toward help-seeking [PAGE 9]; Hone’s null MHL–mental toughness correlations suggest the protective toughness effect does not extend to literacy. The counter-trap is therefore a double bind: the athlete is mentally tough enough to avoid help, but not literate enough to recognize when avoidance becomes dangerous.

Clock: The study’s clock is cross-sectional and static. Grit is framed by Duckworth as passion and perseverance for long-term goals, a characteristic that is “malleable” but develops over extended periods [PAGE 3]. MHL, by contrast, is knowledge-based and can be altered by intervention on a much shorter timescale (weeks to months). Because both are measured at one time point, the model cannot determine whether high grit precedes high MHL, whether MHL interventions would shift grit, or whether a third variable (e.g., athletic identity, coaching quality) drives both. The clock asymmetry means any policy derived from these null correlations—such as “coaches should teach mental health practices alongside grit” [PAGE 11]—commits a temporal category error: it assumes simultaneous malleability where the data show only concurrent independence.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
