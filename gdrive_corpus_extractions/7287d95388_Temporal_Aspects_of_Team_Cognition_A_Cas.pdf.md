The source is a qualitative case study, not a quantitative causal experiment. Its evidentiary spine is: five French male under-18 players, first 10 min of an official match, video plus self-confrontation interviews, course-of-action reconstruction, 251 collective units, four typical concerns, three sharedness forms, and two convergence modes. The central multi-hop inference is: if complete sharedness of typical concerns is rare, yet partial sharedness dominates and team effectiveness often remains satisfactory, then full all-player cognitive similarity is not a necessary condition for viable coordination in this observed period. That inference is supported by the abstract's claim that all-teammate sharing was “relatively rare” but that temporal analysis found “two kinds of convergence phenomena (simultaneous and progressive)” [PAGE 1], and by the result that “Eighty-seven percent of the collective units of activity were of this type” for partial sharedness [PAGE 10]. The paper then localizes coordination: “Most points of connection occurred at the local level (between two or three players)” [PAGE 14]. The mechanism is not mystical team mind; it is compatible expectations, situation-type recognition, team routines, and dyadic perception-adjustment chains.

The first gray-zone audit is arithmetic. The paper states “251 collective units were identified” [PAGE 9] and “We counted 15 instances of complete sharedness” [PAGE 10]. Equal weighting gives \(15/251=0.05976\), about 5.98 percent, not the reported 12 percent. Two non-shared moments give \(2/251=0.00797\), about 0.80 percent, close to the reported 1 percent. If 15 complete and 2 non-shared units are removed, partial is \(234/251=0.9323\), about 93.23 percent, not 87 percent. The reported percentages may involve duration weighting, a different denominator, rounding, or an unreported classification rule; the source does not reconcile them. Therefore the safe claim is “complete sharedness was relatively rare, with 15 counted instances,” not “exactly 12 percent of collective units.” This is a major source-internal inconsistency and must be flagged in any formalization.

The second gray-zone is definitional. Non-sharedness is described as the “entire set of typical concerns” being represented [PAGE 10]. With four categories and five players, that means four distinct typical concerns appear, with one category repeated. Complete sharedness means all five players have the same category. Partial sharedness is the residual. This classification is categorical and observer-coded, not a continuous latent variable. It depends on the initial agreement rates of 83 percent for hexadic signs and 87 percent for typical concerns, with disagreements resolved by discussion [PAGE 9]. The paper reports initial agreement and consensus, not a fully independent final reliability estimate.

The third gray-zone is temporal and causal. The study analyzed only the first 10 min of the five starting players [PAGE 4], and the conclusion explicitly calls for research over “entire match, cumulative number of games, training period of several weeks” [PAGE 17]. Thus no multi-week stability claim is licensed. The effectiveness link is also non-deterministic: the paper says convergence phenomena “seemed to lead to satisfactory team effectiveness” [PAGE 13], and it asks “whether team effectiveness follows the sharedness of cognitive contents or whether progressive effectiveness ... fosters a sharedness configuration” [PAGE 17]. That is an explicit causal-direction uncertainty.

The fourth gray-zone is access to cognition. Self-confrontation “does not reach the non-reportable unconscious processes that guide their activity” [PAGE 5]. Therefore any claim about unconscious shared representations, automatic neurocognitive coupling, or hidden mental models is outside the source. The paper studies reportable concerns, expectations, knowledge, meaningful elements, actions, and interpreted changes.

The fifth gray-zone is situational modulation. Non-sharedness occurred in two moments characterized by “a temporary weakening in the degree of confrontation between the two teams and a drop in the time pressure” [PAGE 10]; one was a ball-put-in-play sequence when “the clock was stopped” [PAGE 10]. This makes clock state and time pressure boundary conditions, not trivial covariates. Complete sharedness, by contrast, appears in convergence episodes, either through simultaneous recognition of a familiar situation-type or progressive contagion-like adoption. The derivation is therefore multi-level: individual concern coding → collective unit classification → temporal convergence pattern → qualitative effectiveness judgment → cautious local-sharing interpretation. Each arrow is fallible, and the final claim must remain local, observational, and time-bounded.

\[
\mathcal{U}=\{u_1,\dots,u_N\},\qquad N=|\mathcal{U}|=251
\]
where the unit is one synchronized collective unit of activity, a dimensionless count [PAGE 9]. The source reports individual unit counts of 139 for Chris, 135 for Théo, 96 for Pierre, 110 for Luc, and 87 for Noé [PAGE 7]. These are counts of discrete meaningful activity units, not seconds.

Let the four typical concern categories be
\[
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathrm{Routine},\mathrm{Exploit},\mathrm{Personal},\mathrm{Spirit}\},
\]
corresponding to “carry out a team routine,” “exploit opportunities offered by the game,” “personally perform well,” and “strengthen team spirit” [PAGE 7]. For player \(i\in\{1,\dots,5\}\) and collective unit \(u\in\mathcal{U}\), assign
\[
c_i(u)\in\mathcal{C}.
\]
This assignment is a categorical label, not a scalar score. Define the number of distinct typical concerns represented at \(u\) by
\[
D(u)=\left|\{c_i(u):i=1,\dots,5\}\right|\in\{1,2,3,4\}.
\]
Under the paper's definitions, complete sharedness is
\[
\mathrm{CS}(u)=1 \iff D(u)=1,
\]
non-sharedness is
\[
\mathrm{NS}(u)=1 \iff D(u)=4,
\]
and partial sharedness is
\[
\mathrm{PS}(u)=1 \iff D(u)\in\{2,3\}.
\]
The assumption is that “the entire set of typical concerns was represented” means all four categories appear [PAGE 10]. A definitional edge case is that with five players and four categories, \(D(u)=4\) necessarily includes a repeated category; the paper still counts that as non-sharedness because the entire set is represented.

Let \(n_X=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbf{1}[X(u)=1]\) for \(X\in\{\mathrm{NS},\mathrm{PS},\mathrm{CS}\}\). The source reports two non-shared moments, 15 complete-sharedness instances, and percentages of 1 percent, 87 percent, and 12 percent [PAGE 10]. The count-derived proportions are
\[
\frac{n_{\mathrm{CS}}}{N}=\frac{15}{251}=0.05976\approx 5.98\%,
\]
\[
\frac{n_{\mathrm{NS}}}{N}=\frac{2}{251}=0.00797\approx 0.80\%.
\]
If those 17 units are removed from the 251, then
\[
\frac{N-n_{\mathrm{CS}}-n_{\mathrm{NS}}}{N}=\frac{234}{251}=0.9323\approx 93.23\%.

These do not equal the reported 12 percent and 87 percent. Therefore the source's percentages are either weighted by duration, based on a different denominator, rounded from an unreported classification, or internally inconsistent. A formal audit must use “15 counted complete-sharedness instances” as the hard count and treat 12 percent as a source-reported but unreconciled percentage. The exact breakdown regime is: denominator \(N=0\) is undefined; small counts \(n_{\mathrm{NS}}=2\) and \(n_{\mathrm{CS}}=15\) make percentages unstable; and the reported percentages should not be treated as exact probabilities.

A complementary information-theoretic audit operator, not reported by the authors, is the within-unit concern entropy. For each \(u\),
\[
p_c(u)=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathbf{1}[c_i(u)=c],

\[

Expectation compatibility can be represented as a Boolean relation. Let \(E_i(u)\) be the set of outcomes admissible under player \(i\)'s expectation at \(u\). A minimal compatibility model is
\[
\mathrm{Comp}_{ij}(u)=1 \iff E_i(u)\cap E_j(u)\neq\varnothing.
\]
The source states that expectations were compatible “when they were not mutually contradictory regarding the outcome of the unfolding situation” and that activities “could be carried out simultaneously” [PAGE 12]. This is a set-intersection assumption, not a measured continuous ratio. The breakdown regime is that empty intersection means incompatible expectations; non-empty intersection does not prove shared understanding, only non-contradiction.

G_t=(V,E_t),\qquad V=\{1,\dots,5\},
\]
where an edge \((i,j)\in E_t\) exists when players \(i\) and \(j\) have a compatible expectation or a local sharing link at time \(t\). The graph formalization is useful because complete sharedness would require a stronger all-vertex condition, not merely a connected graph. The breakdown regime is that the source does not provide edge weights or continuous coupling strengths; only qualitative local connections are documented.

\[

\]
with the added condition that the players recognize the same situation-type and associated team routine [PAGE 11]. The source reports four such instances [PAGE 11]. Progressive adoption is a time-ordered propagation:
\[
\exists (i_1,\dots,i_k),\ t_1,\dots,t_k:\ 
c_{i_1}(t_1)=\cdots=c_{i_k}(t_k)=c^*,
\]
with a perception-adjustment link from \(i_m\) to \(i_{m+1}\) at the relevant moment. The source reports 11 such instances and describes “a diffusion or contagion from one player to a proximal player” [PAGE 11] and “a succession of interpersonal adjustments” [PAGE 12]. The four plus eleven instances equal the 15 complete-sharedness instances, which again shows why the 12 percent figure cannot be directly derived from 15/251 without an additional weighting rule.

Coding reliability is reported as Bellack agreement rates of 83 percent for hexadic signs and 87 percent for typical concerns [PAGE 9]. Effectiveness is also qualitative: satisfactory effectiveness, relative dysfunction, and moments not describable in effectiveness terms [PAGE 9]. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper's lineage runs from ergonomics and team cognition in aviation, traffic regulation, and military organization into sport science [PAGE 2]. Classical team cognition treated sharedness as similarity and assumed that more shared knowledge, awareness, or concern would produce better coordination. The paper cites shared mental models, shared situation awareness, and the “flower” model associated with Eccles and Tenenbaum, in which the overlap of individual understandings is supposed to support viable team coordination [PAGES 14-15]. The course-of-action framework from Theureau supplies the method: video, stimulated recall, reconstruction of meaningful units, and six components labeled involvement, potential actuality, referential, representamen, unit of course of action, and interpretant [PAGES 3-6]. Prior basketball studies by Bourbousson and coworkers had already questioned full knowledge sharedness and full awareness sharedness [PAGE 2], and table tennis work by Poizat and coworkers had shown asymmetric and fragile information sharing [PAGE 3].

Retrocausally, the later “local sharing of understanding” and “constellation” language reframes the older flower model. The paper does not simply say sharedness is unimportant; it says complete all-player sharedness is an exceptional configuration, while local intersections and temporal chaining do much of the coordination work. The abstract's phrase “local sharing of understanding” [PAGE 1] and the discussion's “dynamic constellation of local sharing of understanding” [PAGE 16] are the retrocausal pivot: they reinterpret earlier similarity assumptions through a distributed, temporal, and locally chained lens.

The multi-week traps are explicit. First, the study is only the first 10 min of one official match with five starters [PAGE 4], so treating its percentages as stable across a season is a trap. The authors themselves call for investigation over “entire match, cumulative number of games, training period of several weeks” [PAGE 17]. Second, a training-design trap is to maximize complete sharedness at all times. The paper suggests that coaches distinguish the rare events when players need complete sharing, called situation-types, from the ordinary chaining of local coordinations [PAGE 17]. Third, a causal-direction trap is to assume sharedness precedes effectiveness. The conclusion asks whether effectiveness follows sharedness or whether progressive effectiveness fosters a sharedness configuration [PAGE 17]. Fourth, an aggregation trap is to treat the reported 12 percent as an exact count-derived proportion when 15/251 gives about 5.98 percent. Fifth, a generalization trap is to transfer findings from French male under-18 players at the highest national level to other ages, sexes, levels, or sports without new evidence. Sixth, a measurement trap is to treat qualitative effectiveness judgments as blind, continuous, or independent performance scores; the first author, a national-level coach, made the judgments, and the team coach confirmed them [PAGE 9]. These traps matter over multi-week training because a coach could over-train all-player shared awareness, under-train local coordination, or misread a single 10 min sample as a stable team trait.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is visible in the progressive convergence mode. One player perceives an element, interprets it, changes his concern, and acts; another player perceives that action, infers a compatible interpretation, changes his own concern, and acts; a third player perceives the second player's action and repeats the process. The source describes exactly this: “The perceptive judgment of this behavior by another player led this player to construct an interpretation of the situation that was compatible with that of his teammate” [PAGE 11]. That is second-order and higher-order modeling in ordinary game action: I infer what you expect from what I see you doing, and you infer what I expect from what you see me doing. The 4′05″ to 4′15″ episode is the clearest case: Chris's break creates an opening, Théo interprets it as Chris does, Pierre receives unexpectedly and looks for opportunity, Luc adjusts because the ball arrived outside the routine, and Noé adjusts when Luc looks at him [PAGE 12]. The chain ends with all five sharing “exploit opportunities offered in the game” [PAGE 12].

Crisis habit regression is the simultaneous mode. When players recognize a familiar situation-type, they do not have to negotiate every expectation from scratch. They share past knowledge of the configuration and the associated team routine, and each takes a predefined role. The source says that recognition was accompanied by expectations about a particular team routine and that the teammates acted “simultaneously within a framework of coordinated collective action” [PAGE 11]. In a fast transition after an opponent's basket, four players were focused on direct opponents while Luc was observing; after the basket, concerns shifted, the opponents were slow to defend, and all five recognized a fast-play situation and adopted the “transition” routine [PAGE 11]. This is adaptive habit regression: under time compression, the team falls back to a trained, shared routine. It is not necessarily pathological, but it is a failure mode when the situation-type is misrecognized or when the opponent's shape differs from the trained prototype.

The counter-trap is to assume that because complete sharedness is rare, local sharing is a deficiency to be corrected by forcing all five players into the same concern at every instant. The paper's counter-trap is the opposite: local sharing and dyadic adjustment can be sufficient for viable coordination, and the “chaining together of local coordinations” may be a more general and parsimonious form of team functioning [PAGE 16]. A second counter-trap is the opponent's slow defense: low confrontation and low time pressure can invite either complacency or exploitation. In the 5′03″ to 5′09″ episode, the opponents took their time going on defense, and the team recognized a situation for fast play [PAGE 11]. The trap is to read the opponent's slowness as absence of threat; the counter is to recognize the situation-type and use the team routine.

The clock is the boundary condition that modulates all three. Non-sharedness occurred when “the clock was stopped” or when the opposing point guard was slowly bringing the ball up and perceived threat was low [PAGE 10]. Those are low-time-pressure regimes in which team effectiveness could not even be described under the adopted criteria. In high-time-pressure regimes, simultaneous routine recognition and progressive contagion both appear. The source gives game periods such as 5′03″ to 5′09″ and 4′05″ to 4′15″ [PAGES 11-12], but it does not measure possession-clock duration or physiological arousal. The safe recursive model is therefore: clock state and time pressure alter the likelihood of non-shared, partial, or complete configurations; players recursively infer teammates' concerns from actions; and the team's coordination is carried by compatible expectations rather than by universal mental similarity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

A model that outputs “sharedness causes effectiveness” as a proven result fails the causal-direction check.
