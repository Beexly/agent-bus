<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under crisis or high-drive conditions, an organism tends to regress to earlier, lower-order habits in the Hullian hierarchy. Applied to the delay gradient, this means that when arousal is high, the effective time constant τ shortens further because the animal’s attention shifts from the delayed food outcome to immediate intra-chamber stimuli (bar pressing, sniffing, escape attempts). The result is a steeper effective decay than the ρ ∈ [1/16, 1/15] baseline measured under ordinary Wolfe conditions; the “critical minute” collapses toward a critical seconds-scale window under stress.

Counter-trap: A trainer observing poor learning with delayed rewards may conclude the animal “needs more patience” and lengthen the delay further, or switch to end-of-session jackpot rewards. This is the counter-trap: it assumes the habit trace accumulates across the delay, whereas the negative-growth function says the trace is spent within tens of seconds. The correct counter-intervention is a conditioned bridging stimulus (a clicker or tone) delivered immediately after the correct choice, which marks the response and preserves the contiguity bridge to delayed food, effectively resetting the clock.

Clock: The operative internal variable is a decay clock with τ ≈ 15 s and t₁/₂ ≈ 10 s. At d = 60 s, roughly six half-lives have elapsed, leaving ~1.6%–2.1% of the trace. Behaviorally, the organism is not “remembering” the correct choice after one minute; it is learning whatever occurs during the delay-chamber confinement. Therefore any recursive model of the animal’s “theory of mind” about the trainer must represent the trainer’s reward as temporally uncorrelated with the maze choice beyond ~60 s; the animal’s optimal inference is that food delivery is contingent on delay-chamber behavior, not on the T-maze unit.
