The counter-trap is the engineered moderate incongruence between the messenger (male NFL athletes) and the message (breast cancer prevention for women over 40) [PAGE 20]. Lee and Thorson (2008) found moderate mismatch generates higher purchase intention than perfect match when involvement is high, because consumers actively resolve the incongruence [PAGE 19]. Here, NFL players discussing personal loss of mothers and loved ones to breast cancer supplies the associative bridge that grants credibility despite indirect disease experience [PAGE 20]. High-identification fans therefore do not dismiss the campaign as mismatched; they cognitively work through it, increasing exposure and screening intention [PAGE 29].

The clock is the October campaign calendar versus the May–June survey clock. The seasonal urgency of "A Crucial Catch"—pink cleats, ribbon logos, stadium events—is a time-bound cue to action [PAGE 11, PAGE 24]. Measuring intentions seven to eight months later means the crisis salience has regressed to baseline; the observed r = .251 between identification and screening intention likely understates the contemporaneous campaign effect because the temporal cue has decayed [PAGE 26, PAGE 29]. A recursive loop thus operates: October primes identification-driven exposure; the off-season survey measures only the residual trace, filtered by recall and by a sample younger than the target demographic.

Physical layer: The campaign's physical artifacts—pink cleats, pink ribbon logos, cheerleader apparel, on-field survivor events [PAGE 11, PAGE 24]—impose no measurable biomechanical load on athletes and present no physical veto on message delivery; the veto strength is null.

Weather layer: Buffalo's outdoor Niagara Falls illumination and "Peace Bridge" event [PAGE 11, PAGE 15] are exposed to October weather, but the message is simultaneously broadcast on television, hosted on YouTube and NFL/team websites, and distributed through community events [PAGE 11]. Weather can suppress local outdoor attendance but cannot veto national mediated reach; veto strength is partial and attenuated.

Biomechanical/access layer: The ultimate behavior—scheduling and completing a mammogram—requires physical access to imaging infrastructure, transportation, time, and insurance coverage [PAGE 14]. The thesis acknowledges communication barriers and provider assumptions as drivers of racial/ethnic mortality disparities [PAGE 14] and notes the Buffalo Bills' collaboration with ECMC's Mobile Mammogram Coach to serve inner-city women with limited insurance [PAGE 14]. However, physical access, geographic distance, and facility capacity are never included as measured moderators in the survey [PAGE 25-27]. Therefore, while the HBM barrier construct is theoretically present [PAGE 12], its empirical operationalization is absent; any claim that campaign exposure converts into screening behavior is vetoed at the implementation layer where mammography access is unequal.

Adjudication verdict: Message-delivery veto = null (media infrastructure carries the campaign regardless of weather). Behavioral-conversion veto = active and unmeasured (physical/structural barriers to mammography are not modeled, so intention does not guarantee action, particularly for African American women who face higher mortality despite lower incidence [PAGE 14, PAGE 35]).
