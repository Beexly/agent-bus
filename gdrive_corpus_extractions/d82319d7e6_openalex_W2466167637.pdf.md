This is a source-grounded audit of Urizar et al. (2016), “A Hierarchical Bayesian Model for Crowd Emotions,” Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience 10:63. No wagering constructs are introduced.

The paper’s inference chain is multi-hop. First, raw individual trajectories are represented as coordinate estimates \(X=\{\hat x_{1t},\ldots,\hat x_{Nt}\}_{t=1}^{\tau_k}\), with \(\hat x_{it}\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in metres, and filtered by an Extended Kalman Filter [PAGE 4–5]. Second, a self-organizing map \(SOM_I\) learns a hexagonal zone topology \(S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_{m\times n}\}\), where each node is a “mutually exclusive zone” [PAGE 4]. Third, a trajectory is compressed into a “word” \(w_i=\{s_1,\ldots,s_q\}\), and words with similar origin and destination are grouped into a “vocabulary” \(V_l\), which is the paper’s operational definition of a behavior [PAGE 5]. Fourth, two conditional probability distributions estimate next state and transition time, and Bayes rule assigns an emotion label \(E\in\{\text{positive},\text{normal},\text{negative}\}\) [PAGE 5]. Fifth, the crowd is represented not by individual identities but by a density/count vector over zones, \(\hat X_C^t=\{\hat y_{1t},\ldots,\hat y_{m\times n,t}\}\), where \(\hat y_{kt}\) is “an estimated amount of individuals in zone \(s_k\in S\) at time \(t\)” [PAGE 5]. A second SOM, \(SOM_C\), clusters crowd configurations, and a first-order Markov process estimates crowd state transitions and transition times [PAGE 6]. Finally, the crowd’s emotion is set equal to the majority individual emotion [PAGE 8].

The reported headline numbers are “accuracy levels of 74% for individuals and 81% for the crowd” [PAGE 1]. The individual result is plausible only relative to the paper’s own label rule: behaviors with the fewest state transitions and shortest transition time are labeled positive; high-frequency behaviors are normal; high-transition-count and long-transition-time behaviors are negative [PAGE 7]. That rule is a proxy, not a measurement of emotion. The paper itself warns that “the relationship between emotions and behaviors is not straightforward as shown in appraisal theory” [PAGE 2]. Therefore the 74% is best interpreted as agreement with a context-specific heuristic label, not as ground-truth affective decoding.

The gray-zone audit must flag several issues. The experiments use a “realistic crowd simulator” based on social forces, and “the information of individual’s trajectories is provided directly from the crowd simulator, hence the steps for detection and tracking of people are omitted” [PAGE 7]. Thus the 74%/81% figures do not certify performance under real camera tracking, occlusion, identity switches, or sensor noise. The zone-resolution dependence is explicit: “The decision of how many zones to employ to describe trajectories has a direct impact on the reliability of our model to estimate the emotion of individuals; this happens because we describe the behavior of individuals by transition of zones, and with fewer zones there is a higher uncertainty of the motion of individuals” [PAGE 7]. The individual confusion matrix is not diagonal: for actual positive, 71% are estimated positive, 23% normal, 6% negative; for actual normal, 14% positive, 83% normal, 3% negative; for actual negative, 4% positive, 21% normal, 75% negative [PAGE 8]. The crowd result is even more delicate: “the emotional state of the crowd is not correlated to its behavior, instead is assigned to be the same as the experienced for the majority of individuals” [PAGE 8]. That is a majority-aggregation assumption, not a causal crowd-emotion model. The paper also notes that crowd state transitions “emerge as a cyclic process with people continuously joining and leaving the crowd” [PAGE 6], yet the implemented crowd model is a first-order Markov process; cycles, memory, and nonstationarity are therefore breakdown regimes. These points frame the formal operators below.

x_i^t=x_i^{t-1}+g_i^t,\qquad z_i^t=x_i^t+h_i^t,
\]
with \(x_i^t,z_i^t\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in metres; \(g_i^t,h_i^t\) are “independent, white, with Gaussian distribution” process and observation noise [PAGE 4–5]. Covariances have units \(\mathrm{m}^2\). A standard EKF update can be written as
\[
K_t=P_t^-H_t^\top\left(H_tP_t^-H_t^\top+R_t\right)^{-1},
\]
with \(K_t\) dimensionless under linearized \(H_t\), \(P_t^-\) and \(R_t\) in \(\mathrm{m}^2\). Breakdown/singularity: the innovation covariance \(H_tP_t^-H_t^\top+R_t\) is singular; \(R_t=0\) with rank-deficient \(P_t^-\); severe occlusion; non-Gaussian manoeuvres; identity switches. The paper does not provide a full EKF derivation, only the state/observation equations and the statement that “Applying an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) over the observation and state vectors we obtain an estimation \(\hat x_i^t\)” [PAGE 5].

**O2. \(SOM_I\) zone operator.**  
The source trains \(SOM_I\) on \(X\) to obtain nodes \(S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_{m\times n}\}\), each node a mutually exclusive zone [PAGE 4]. A standard Kohonen update consistent with the paper’s “Kohonen rule” [PAGE 7] is
\[
w_j(t+1)=w_j(t)+\eta(t)h_{j,c}(t)\left(x(t)-w_j(t)\right),
\]
where \(w_j\in\mathbb{R}^2\) is a node weight in metres, \(\eta(t)\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless learning rate, and \(h_{j,c}(t)\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless neighbourhood kernel. Breakdown: empty or dead nodes; too few zones, which the paper says creates “higher uncertainty of the motion of individuals” [PAGE 7]; too many zones, which fragments words and inflates sparsity; topology not aligned with physical barriers.

**O3. Word and vocabulary operators.**  
A trajectory is compressed as
\[
w_i=\{s_1,\ldots,s_q;\ s_j\in S,\ q\ge1\},
\]
and vocabularies \(V_l=\{w_1,\ldots,w_q\}\) group words with “similar origin and destination” [PAGE 5]. Units: zone index is dimensionless; a word is a finite sequence. Breakdown: no matching vocabulary at test time; ambiguous origin/destination; vocabulary collapse when distinct behaviors share endpoints; path deviations that preserve endpoints but change emotion.

**O4. Individual behavior CPDs.**  
The source defines two CPDs:
\[
\varphi^l_{s_\alpha,s_\beta,s_{b+1},w_{a:b}}
=p(s_\alpha,s_\beta,s_{b+1}\mid w_{a:b}),
\]
\[
\psi^l_{\Delta t,s_{b+1},s_b}
=p(\Delta t\mid s_{b+1},s_b),
\]
where \(s_\alpha\) is initial state, \(s_\beta\) final state, \(s_{b+1}\) predicted next state, and \(\Delta t\) transition time [PAGE 5]. Probabilities are dimensionless; \(\Delta t\) is in seconds. If \(\Delta t\) is modelled continuously, \(p(\Delta t\mid\cdot)\) is a density with units \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). Breakdown: zero-count transitions; sparse vocabularies; \(\Delta t\to0\) causing degenerate time densities; nonstationary crowd flow; label leakage through operator-selected thresholds.

**O5. Emotion Bayes rule.**  
The paper writes an unnormalized joint:
\[
p(E,w_{a:b+1},\Delta t)
=p(E)\,p(s_\alpha,s_\beta,s_{b+1}\mid w_{a:b})\,p(\Delta t\mid s_{b+1},s_b),
\]
and says it evaluates this “for each possible value of \(E\) in order to find the emotional state with the highest likelihood” [PAGE 5]. The normalized posterior is
\[
p(E\mid w_{a:b+1},\Delta t)
=\frac{p(E)p(s_\alpha,s_\beta,s_{b+1}\mid w_{a:b})p(\Delta t\mid s_{b+1},s_b)}
{\sum_{E'}p(E')p(s_\alpha,s_\beta,s_{b+1}\mid w_{a:b})p(\Delta t\mid s_{b+1},s_b)},
\]
with \(E\in\{\text{positive},\text{normal},\text{negative}\}\). Probabilities dimensionless. Breakdown: denominator zero or near-zero; uniform prior \(p(E)\) imposed by assumption; context shift changing the behavior-to-emotion map; no calibration of the three labels.

**O6. Crowd state operator.**  
The crowd state is
\[
\hat X_C^t=\{\hat y_{1t},\ldots,\hat y_{m\times n,t}\},
\]
where \(\hat y_{kt}\) is an estimated count in zone \(s_k\) at time \(t\) [PAGE 5]. Units: persons per zone, or persons/\(\mathrm{m}^2\) if normalized by zone area. Breakdown: changing \(N\) as people join/leave; count/density ambiguity; all-zero vector; zone-area bias; density estimator error propagating into \(SOM_C\).

**O7. Crowd Markov CPDs.**  
For \(SOM_C\) nodes \(C=\{c_1,\ldots,c_{p\times q}\}\), the source defines
\[
\Upsilon_{c_k,c_{k+1}}=p(c_{k+1}\mid c_k),
\]
\[
\Theta_{\Delta t,c_k}=p(\Delta t\mid c_{k+1},c_k),
\]
as a first-order Markov process [PAGE 6]. Probabilities dimensionless; continuous-time \(\Theta\) has units \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). Breakdown: the paper’s own recognition that crowd transitions are “a cyclic process” [PAGE 6] violates first-order Markov assumptions; long memory; periodic surges; nonstationary arrival rates; \(SOM_C\) clusters with no visual semantics, as the paper notes “unlike SOMI, a plot of SOMC does not provide a visual semantic” [PAGE 7].

**O8. Majority emotion operator.**  
The crowd emotion is effectively
\[
E_C=\arg\max_{E\in\{+,\mathrm{normal},-\}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{E_i=E\},
\]
with \(E_C\) dimensionless and \(N\) persons. Breakdown: ties; minority panic erased by majority; crowd emotion not actually behavior-correlated, as stated: “the emotional state of the crowd is not correlated to its behavior, instead is assigned to be the same as the experienced for the majority of individuals” [PAGE 8].

**O9. Reported accuracy and information measures.**  
Accuracy is
\[
\mathrm{Acc}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}\{\hat E_m=E_m\},
\]
dimensionless. Reported values are 74% for individuals and 81% for the crowd [PAGE 1]. Behavioral prediction success is about 76% for individuals [PAGE 7] and 81% average for the crowd, oscillating between 50% and 94% [PAGE 8]. A useful audit entropy is
\[
H(E\mid w_{a:b+1},\Delta t)
=-\sum_{E}p(E\mid\cdot)\log_2 p(E\mid\cdot),
\]
in bits. Breakdown: accuracy hides per-class error; high entropy should trigger abstention; low entropy can be false confidence under distribution shift.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs through crowd psychology, emotion theory, and computational perception. It cites Le Bon’s mental-unity conception of the crowd, Freud’s group psychology, and Allett’s discussion of crowd psychology and democratic elitism [PAGE 2, PAGE 9 references]. It also draws on Ekman’s universal facial expressions, Plutchik’s discrete emotions, Matsumoto’s legacy-of-universals argument, and Keltner and Ekman’s facial-expression work [PAGE 2, PAGE 9]. These sources support the paper’s premise that emotions are “discrete and measurable” [PAGE 2]. The paper then shifts from face/vocal channels to whole-body motion, citing de Gelder and related work that body expressions are “primary carriers of emotion and action information” [PAGE 2]. It also invokes Damasio’s somatic-marker hypothesis and appraisal theory to argue that emotion and behavior are linked but not one-to-one [PAGE 2]. The computational lineage is equally clear: Kohonen’s self-organizing map [PAGE 3–4], social-force pedestrian dynamics from Helbing and Molnar [PAGE 7], and the prior probabilistic crowd-emotion model of Baig et al. [PAGE 2]. The social-force layer is explicitly physical: “deriving its motion equations from Newtons law F = ma and accounting for its motivation as an attraction force pulling the individual toward its destination and repulsive forces from physical objects and other individuals in the environment” [PAGE 7].

This genealogy creates several multi-week traps. **Trap 1: reification of the crowd.** Le Bon’s mental-unity language is operationalized as a single entity with its own state and emotion. If deployed for weeks, the model can smooth over subgroup dynamics. The paper’s own majority rule is the weak point: “the emotional state of the crowd is not correlated to its behavior, instead is assigned to be the same as the experienced for the majority of individuals” [PAGE 8]. **Trap 2: inverse emotion inference.** The model infers emotion from behavior, but appraisal theory says the same event can provoke different emotions in different individuals [PAGE 2]. Over weeks, context drift can invert the behavior-emotion mapping. **Trap 3: simulation-to-field leakage.** The training and test sets are simulator-produced; detection and tracking are omitted [PAGE 7]. Real deployment introduces occlusion, identity switches, and sensor calibration drift. **Trap 4: operator labeling.** Behaviors are “labeled empirically by a human operator knowledgeable of the environment” [PAGE 3]. Operator knowledge is a hidden prior; in a new site, the labels may not transfer. **Trap 5: zone-resolution instability.** The paper warns that fewer zones increase uncertainty [PAGE 7]; changing camera geometry or zone count can silently change the emotion output. **Trap 6: cyclic crowd dynamics.** The paper observes cyclic transitions [PAGE 6], but the implemented Markov model is memoryless; long-period cycles can be mistaken for regime change. **Trap 7: majority erasure.** A persistent minority negative state can be invisible under majority aggregation. **Trap 8: time-as-emotion proxy.** Short transition time is labeled positive, but panic and evacuation can also compress transition time; the same proxy flips meaning under crisis. These are multi-week traps because they may not appear in a short simulated test but emerge as site-specific label drift, crowd composition change, and nonstationary arrival processes accumulate.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive issue is that the model estimates an entity’s emotion, the entity’s behavior may adapt to being observed, and the operator may adapt to the model’s alerts. The paper’s hierarchy has three observable channels: next-zone prediction, transition-time prediction, and crowd state prediction [PAGE 5–6]. In a recursive deployment, each channel can be gamed or misinterpreted.

**Crisis habit regression.** Under stress, individuals may revert to overlearned trajectories from the training vocabulary. The model’s CPD \(p(s_\alpha,s_\beta,s_{b+1}\mid w_{a:b})\) can remain high because the path looks familiar, while the transition-time CPD \(p(\Delta t\mid s_{b+1},s_b)\) shifts downward. If the fusion weights time weakly, the model outputs “normal” or even “positive” because the route matches a short-path vocabulary. The countermeasure is to monitor conditional entropy:
\[
H(E\mid w_{a:b+1},\Delta t)
=-\sum_E p(E\mid\cdot)\log_2 p(E\mid\cdot)
\]
in bits. If \(H\) rises while the route likelihood stays high, the correct action is abstention or reweighting toward the time channel, not a confident emotion label. Habit regression is a false-normal trap.

**Counter-trap.** A crowd or individual can act to satisfy the model’s expectations: take a familiar origin-destination word, avoid anomalous zones, or slow down to avoid a “negative” time label. This is recursive theory of mind because the observed agent models the observer. The paper’s “word” representation is vulnerable: words are grouped by similar origin and destination [PAGE 5], so an agent can preserve endpoints while changing the emotional content of the path. A verifiable counter-trap detector is to compare the observed trajectory against the physical admissible set and against the Markov transition likelihood. If \(p(c_{k+1}\mid c_k)\) is high but the path’s turning statistics or dwell times are anomalous, the model should not treat high likelihood as authenticity. High likelihood under a narrow vocabulary is not proof of normal emotion.

**Clock.** The clock channel is transition time. The paper uses \(\Delta t\) in both individual and crowd CPDs [PAGE 5–6]. A deadline, evacuation, or panic can compress \(\Delta t\), but the paper’s individual label rule treats “shortest transition time” as positive [PAGE 7]. That creates a sign ambiguity: fast can mean efficient or afraid. A simple standardized clock audit is
\[
Z_\tau=\frac{\Delta t-\mu_{\tau}}{\sigma_{\tau}},
\]
with \(\Delta t,\mu_\tau,\sigma_\tau\) in seconds and \(Z_\tau\) dimensionless. If \(|Z_\tau|>3\) relative to the training conditional, the time channel is out of distribution and should veto a confident emotion label. The recursive prediction is: under crisis, transition-time distributions shift first, path vocabularies shift second, and majority emotion shifts last. Therefore the model should prioritize the clock channel and physical vetoes before majority aggregation. The paper’s own statement that crowd emotion is majority-assigned [PAGE 8] means the recursive system can be stable and wrong: the majority can look normal while a localized minority is in crisis.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer vetoes are necessary because the paper’s emotion estimate is a probabilistic inference over behavior, not a physical measurement. The source’s simulator is explicitly force-based: individuals are “a particle subject to 2D forces” with motivation as attraction and repulsion, and motion derived from \(F=ma\) [PAGE 7]. That gives a physical admissibility layer that can veto impossible probabilistic outputs.

Let \(x_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) be position in metres, \(\dot x_i(t)\) velocity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\ddot x_i(t)\) acceleration in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and \(d_{\min}(s_b,s_{b+1})\) the shortest path distance between zone centroids or zone boundaries in metres. Define the weather-dependent speed bound \(v_{\max}(w)\), where \(w\) denotes weather state, in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and the friction coefficient \(\mu(w)\in[0,1]\), dimensionless. A physically admissible transition must satisfy
\[
\Delta \hat t \ge \frac{d_{\min}(s_b,s_{b+1})}{v_{\max}(w)}.
\]
Proof: if \(\Delta \hat t < d_{\min}/v_{\max}\), the required average speed would be \(d_{\min}/\Delta \hat t>v_{\max}\), contradicting the speed bound. This is an exact veto of the individual or crowd transition-time prediction. It is especially relevant when the model predicts a very short transition time and the Bayes rule then favors a positive label; the physical layer can veto that positive label without any emotion ground truth.

A second veto is acceleration/friction:
\[
\|\ddot x_i(t)\|_2 \le \mu(w)\,g,
\]
with \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). If the predicted motion requires \(\|\ddot x_i\|_2>\mu(w)g\), the trajectory is physically inadmissible under the assumed surface. For turning, the lateral bound is
\[
\frac{v_i^2}{R_i}\le \mu(w)g,
\]
where \(v_i\) is speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(R_i\) is turn radius in metres. This vetoes sharp high-speed turns that a probabilistic vocabulary might still assign to a familiar word. Weather enters through \(\mu(w)\): rain, ice, or wet flooring lowers \(\mu\), so the same predicted acceleration that is admissible in dry conditions becomes inadmissible in wet conditions. Weather also lowers effective visibility and speed, so \(v_{\max}(w)\) must be re-estimated; otherwise the model can label weather-slowed normal motion as negative because transition time increased.

A third veto is density/packing:
\[
\rho(x,t)\le \rho_{\max},
\]
with \(\rho\) in persons/\(\mathrm{m}^2\). If a zone state \(\hat y_{kt}\) implies a density above the packing limit, the crowd state is impossible. Related flow is
\[
q=\rho v,
\]
with \(q\) in persons/(\(\mathrm{m\,s}\)) and \(v\) in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); if the predicted flow exceeds the observed fundamental-diagram capacity, veto the state transition. The paper’s crowd state is count-based [PAGE 5], so the operator must divide by zone area before applying this check; otherwise count and density are conflated.

A fourth veto is biomechanical cadence:
\[
v_i=f_{s,i}\,\ell_{s,i},
\]
where \(f_{s,i}\) is step frequency in steps/\(\mathrm{s}\) and \(\ell_{s,i}\) is step length in metres. If the predicted speed exceeds the plausible cadence-length envelope for the inferred gait, veto. This is a biomechanical layer, not an emotion layer. Combined with physical obstacles, the admissible set is
\[
\mathcal A_i=\left\{x_i(\cdot):\ \|\dot x_i\|\le v_{\max}(w),\ \|\ddot x_i\|\le \mu(w)g,\ \rho\le\rho_{\max},\ \Delta t\ge \frac{d_{\min}}{v_{\max}(w)}\right\}.
\]
If the hierarchical Bayesian estimate falls outside \(\mathcal A_i\), the correct adjudication is veto, not reinterpretation. This proof is stronger than a probabilistic confidence threshold because it uses SI constraints that must hold regardless of the emotion label.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means a reinforcement-learning-style verifier with checkable rewards: a counterfactual intervention is run, and the model’s output is vetoed if a stated verification condition fails. Three scenarios follow.

**Veto 1: Zone-resolution counterfactual.**  
Intervention: retrain or re-map the same trajectories with \(SOM_I\) at \(5\times5\) instead of \(10\times10\), holding the emotion labels fixed. Verification condition: if the vocabulary assignment or next-zone prediction changes for a trajectory, veto the emotion label as resolution-unstable. The paper explicitly warns that “with fewer zones there is a higher uncertainty of the motion of individuals” [PAGE 7] and that zone count has “a direct impact on the reliability of our model to estimate the emotion of individuals” [PAGE 7]. Prediction: the \(5\times5\) model will produce more vocabulary collisions and lower individual accuracy than the reported 74% [PAGE 1]. Verifiable outcome: count label flips between the two maps; if flip rate exceeds the pre-registered threshold, the RLVR reward is zero for that label.

**Veto 2: Weather-adjusted transition-time veto.**  
Intervention: inject a weather layer that lowers \(v_{\max}(w)\) and \(\mu(w)\), then test whether the model still labels weather-slowed but otherwise normal motion as negative. Verification condition:
\[
\Delta \hat t \ge \frac{d_{\min}}{v_{\max}(w)}
\]
must hold; if not, veto the predicted transition. Prediction: without the veto, the model will overproduce negative labels because longer \(\Delta t\) mimics the paper’s “negative” label rule [PAGE 7]. With the veto, negative labels caused solely by weather are suppressed, and the remaining negative labels are those that also violate path or density constraints. Verifiable outcome: compare negative-label rate in dry versus wet synthetic runs with identical path vocabularies; the veto should remove the weather-induced delta.

**Veto 3: Minority-panic counterfactual.**  
Intervention: inject a localized negative event in one zone of the simulated train-station scenario while the majority remains on normal paths. Verification condition: compute crowd next-state entropy
\[
H(C_{k+1}\mid c_k)=-\sum_{c_{k+1}}p(c_{k+1}\mid c_k)\log_2 p(c_{k+1}\mid c_k)
\]
in bits. If \(H\) exceeds the baseline by a pre-registered margin and the minority negative fraction exceeds its baseline, veto the majority-derived positive crowd label. This directly tests the paper’s admission that crowd emotion “is not correlated to its behavior, instead is assigned to be the same as the experienced for the majority of individuals” [PAGE 8]. Prediction: the unmodified model will keep the crowd label positive because the majority remains positive; the veto will flag a localized crisis that majority aggregation hides. Verifiable outcome: detection latency of the injected minority panic, false-positive crowd-negative rate in control runs, and whether the veto improves minority-event recall without collapsing majority accuracy.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
