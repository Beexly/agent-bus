The scientific lineage of the ×G2.2 metric traces back to early association-football shot-quality work by Reep and Benjamin (1968), which established that shooting position and context govern scoring probability, and forward through modern Opta/StatsBomb expected-goals frameworks that condition on shot location, body part, and defensive pressure. The patent's use of spatial player tracking (goalkeeper, striker positions relative to the field) [PAGE 19] inherits from second-generation tracking systems (e.g., optical and wearable player-location data), while the SHAP explanation layer directly descends from Lundberg and Lee's (2017) additive feature-attribution theory, which guarantees local accuracy and consistency by distributing the difference between instance prediction and background expectation across input features. The multi-week trap embedded in this genealogy is threefold. First, archetype conversion rates are not stationarily stable across a season: "close range (central)" obs = 0.20 with n_shots = 577 [PAGE 19] may drift as teams adjust defensive lines, meaning a model trained on early-season matches mis-calibrates late-season fixtures. Second, goalkeeper positioning optimality is an adversarial equilibrium; as keepers learn a striker's tendencies, the save probability feature that once added +×G [PAGE 19] decays in predictive value within weeks. Third, goals_above_xG residuals such as Haaland +6.22345 and Salah -2.79740 [PAGE 19] exhibit strong mean-reversion over multiple gameweeks unless the model encodes persistent finisher skill rather than treating every shot within an archetype as exchangeable. A counter-trap lurks in assuming that over-performance is purely luck: if the training distribution over-samples elite finishers, the base value \phi_0 shifts upward for similar archetypes, causing systematic under-prediction for average shooters in those bins. The temporal clock therefore operates at two speeds: shot-level features (distance, clarity, pressure) decay within a single match as fatigue and desperation change defensive behavior, while archetype-level calibration drifts across a tournament cycle, invalidating the static Table 2 biases if applied beyond the specific competition named on page 19 [PAGE 19].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From a recursive theory-of-mind perspective, the ×G model faces a crisis-habit regression loop when observed outcomes diverge sharply from expected values. When a striker such as Haaland accumulates +6.22 goals above ×G [PAGE 19], analysts and betting-adjacent models habitually regress toward the mean, assuming finishing is a fixed skill and that the residual will vanish. However, the recursive trap is that the ×G model itself may be the source of the bias: if the archetype "close range (central)" systematically underestimates elite finishers because the training data pools all central close-range shots, then the model's conditional probability p(score | archetype) is misspecified for high-skill agents. The counter-trap is to overcorrect by attributing the entire residual to persistent striker skill, thereby inflating future ×G for similar players and creating a self-fulfilling overestimation loop. The clock moderates both: short-term (single-match), crisis habit regression dominates because sample sizes per archetype are small (e.g., n_shots = 96 for uncontested headers [PAGE 19]), so one or two goals move the observed rate by more than one standard error; medium-term (multi-week), the model must decide whether to update the archetype baseline upward for elite finishers or hold the population prior and wait for regression. A further recursion occurs at the goalkeeper layer: the SHAP attribution for low save probability [PAGE 19] assumes the keeper's position is exogenous, but in reality keepers adapt to the shooter's observed tendencies within a match, creating a feedback loop where the shooter's ×G rises precisely because the keeper is drawn out of position, only for the keeper to recover and suppress the next shot's probability. Thus the crisis habit is to treat ×G as a static property of the shot geometry, whereas the counter-trap recognizes ×G as a dynamic equilibrium between shooter decision, keeper positioning, and defensive pressure, all of which evolve on a match clock and a season clock simultaneously.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three independent veto layers can invalidate the ×G2.2 prediction even when the statistical metrics on page 19 appear favorable. The physical layer veto: ball flight is governed by drag and Magnus forces, so a shot from d ≈ 8 m or d ≈ 9 m [PAGE 19] cannot be assigned a fixed ×G without solving the trajectory equation m\,d\mathbf{v}/dt = -\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_d A\|\mathbf{v}\|\mathbf{v} - \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_L A(\mathbf{\omega}\times\mathbf{v}) + m\mathbf{g}, where \rho is air density, C_d is drag coefficient, C_L is lift coefficient, A is cross-sectional area, \mathbf{\omega} is spin vector, and g is gravitational acceleration (units: m, m/s, m/s², kg, N). If wind or spin places the trajectory outside the goalmouth intersection cone, ×G must be vetoed to zero regardless of what the learned model outputs; page 19 contains no such physics constraint, so this is an external veto. The biomechanical layer veto: volleys and first touches are coded as negative contributors [PAGE 19] because they reduce foot-ball contact time and control, but an elite technician with a biomechanical profile outside the training distribution (e.g., unusual approach angle, contact duration, or limb velocity) can override the archetypal penalty. If the player's measured joint kinematics fall beyond the 99th percentile of the training cohort, the archetype-level ×G should be vetoed. The weather/surface layer veto: although page 19 focuses on soccer, its tennis extension explicitly lists court surface as an input feature [PAGE 19]. Surface friction coefficient \mu (dimensionless) alters bounce height and horizontal speed; clay (\mu high, slow) versus grass (\mu low, fast) changes the conditional distribution of shot impact location and speed. Applying a surface-agnostic or hard-court-trained model to clay without retraining violates the support of the feature distribution and triggers a veto. Additionally, outdoor soccer matches under precipitation alter ball-surface friction and aerodynamic drag, which the page does not model; if measured rainfall or wind speed exceeds the calibration envelope, the ×G prediction is physically unverifiable and must be vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
