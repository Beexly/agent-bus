**1. The Stick Rotation Operator (Kinematic Unification)**

$$ \vec{r}_E(t) = (L \cos(\omega t), L \sin(\omega t)) $$
The retinal image is formed on a focal plane at distance $f$. Assuming small angles or a pinhole model, the lateral retinal coordinate $x_r$ and the perceived depth cue (mediated by convergence or parallax, abstracted as the projection along the optical axis) are mapped.
James states: "given amounts of the visual depth-feeling become signs of fixed amounts of the visual breadth-feeling" [PAGE 215]. We formalize this as a conditional probability mapping function $M$:
$$ M(d_v \rightarrow b_v) = P(B_v | D_v) $$
At the moment of eclipse ($\omega t = \pi/2$), the lateral retinal separation is $0$. However, the inferred depth difference $\Delta d_{inf}$ equals $L$. If $B_{max}$ is the retinal breadth when the stick was fully lateral, the learning rule integrates over time $T$:
$$ L_{inferred} = \int_0^T \alpha \cdot (\text{optic flow magnitude}) dt = B_{max}^{perceived} $$

**2. The Regiment Obliquity Singularity (Projective Collapse)**
Consider a regiment of width $W$ positioned along the x-axis from $-W/2$ to $W/2$ at initial distance $D_0$. The colonel moves along the z-axis to a position $(0, 0, 0)$ effectively (relative to the center man), looking down the x-axis.
For an end man at $x = W/2$, the distance from the colonel is $d = \sqrt{(W/2)^2 + y^2}$, but primarily perceived via the collapsed lateral spread.
Let $b_{retina}$ be the lateral extent on the retina. As the angle $\theta$ of the line of sight relative to the regiment's normal vector approaches $0$, the projected width scales as:
$$ b_{retina}(\theta) = b_{initial} \cdot \sin(\theta) $$
James asserts that when the line casts "hardly any lateral image," the judged distance of the end men is equated to "half the length of the regiment as it was originally seen" [PAGE 215].
Thus, the psychological depth estimator $Z_{psych}$ behaves as:
$$ Z_{psych} = \lim_{\theta \to 0} f(b_{retina}(\theta)) = \frac{W}{2} $$
Here, $W/2$ is derived from memory of $b_{initial}$. This represents a formal singularity in visual processing: as $\sin(\theta) \rightarrow 0$, the visual system experiences a breakdown in lateral metric scaling. To preserve object constancy, the cognitive operator redirects the lost dimensional magnitude entirely into the depth metric.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
William James's 1890 argument in *The Principles of Psychology* emerges from the intense late 19th-century debates between empiricist associationism and emerging structuralist psychophysics. Historically, this text is a direct, calculated intervention against George Berkeley's *New Theory of Vision* (1709). Berkeley argued that visual sensations are merely colors and lights, and that our perception of distance is a non-natural, learned "language" suggested by tactile and muscular sensations (proprioception) acquired through reaching and touching.

James agrees with the mechanism—that distance is a "result of suggestion" [PAGE 215]—but fundamentally alters the genealogical source of the data. By stating, "But visual experience alone is adequate to produce it, and this he erroneously denied" [PAGE 215], James initiates a retrocausal lineage that bypasses the somatic senses. He posits that the dynamic shifts in the retinal image (the shortening stick, the collapsing regiment) contain sufficient informational redundancy to bootstrap a 3D metric space purely optically.

This theoretical stance sets up a multi-week—or rather, century-long—trap for subsequent cognitive science. By isolating vision, James inadvertently fueled the Gestaltists' focus on innate organizational principles and later distracted computer vision researchers who spent decades trying to solve the "aperture problem" and structure-from-motion using only 2D pixel arrays, heavily relying on the exact kinematic unifications James described. However, modern embodied cognition and predictive processing theories suggest a trap: the brain does not actually wait for "visual experience alone." Multisensory priors (vestibular, proprioceptive) are fused almost instantaneously. The "week-long trap" in a contemporary context is the enduring illusion that a purely visual AI could develop a robust concept of physical depth solely through passive observation of rotating sticks, ignoring the necessity of active motor interaction with the environment to ground those visual variables in reality.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When analyzing the psychological dynamics of the colonel or the observer of the stick, we encounter a fascinating interplay between crisis habit regression and counter-trapping mechanisms.

**Crisis Habit Regression:** Under conditions of extreme sensory degradation—such as the regiment casting "hardly any lateral image" [PAGE 215]—the visual system faces a crisis. The standard heuristic of "retinal size correlates with physical size" fails catastrophically because the retinal size drops to near zero while the physical entity remains massive. In this crisis, the mind tends to regress to primitive Gestalt habits: it might perceive the regiment as a tiny, nearby vertical stack of individuals, completely failing to appreciate the true scale. This is a failure of the "unifying" principle mentioned by James.

**Counter-Trap vs. Clock:** To avoid this regression, the cognitive system employs a counter-trap: temporal persistence. The "clock" here is the continuous stream of visual history. Just before the lateral image collapsed, the mind recorded the maximal breadth. The observer actively compares the current impoverished state against the remembered rich state. The realization that "I am seeing the same regiment I saw a moment ago" forces a reinterpretation of the depth variable. The mind traps itself in a recursive loop: *If breadth is gone, and the object is constant, then the missing dimension must have been translated into depth.*
This recursive reasoning operates on a strict internal clock. If the transformation happens too quickly (e.g., a video played at high speed), the visual system cannot perform the necessary integration, and the counter-trap fails, resulting in a perception of a literally shrinking object rather than a receding one.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even if the visual geometry perfectly satisfies James's unification principle, the final perceptual output is subject to hard vetoes from physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers.

**Biomechanical Veto:** The "visual depth-feeling" awakened by the far end of the stick [PAGE 215] relies partially on binocular convergence. If the stick is extremely long (e.g., a pole extending 100 meters), the eyes will maintain a nearly parallel gaze (vergence angle $\approx 0$). Here, the biomechanical limit of extraocular muscles provides a veto. The visual cortex receives a flat convergence signal despite the changing retinal image. Consequently, the psychological mapping of depth to breadth breaks down at extreme ranges; we rely entirely on perspective and atmospheric cues rather than the kinematic unification James describes for intermediate distances.

**Physical/Weather Veto:** In the regiment scenario, the equating of depth to previous breadth assumes optimal visibility. Introduce heavy fog or smoke (attenuation of light, scattering). The visual clarity of the "far" end men drops drastically. The mind may veto the conclusion that they are merely "obscured by the line of sight" and instead interpret the lack of contrast as extreme distance, overriding the geometric prediction based on the initial dress parade width. The physical medium imposes a cap on the effective range of the learned visual rules.

**Neural Fatigue Veto:** If the stick rotates continuously for hours, neural adaptation to the optic flow patterns may occur. The sensitivity to the subtle changes in the "farther end" [PAGE 215] would diminish, blurring the precise moment of the eclipse, thereby smearing the sharp calibration point between depth and breadth.
