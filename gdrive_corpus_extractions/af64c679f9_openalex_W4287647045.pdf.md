The source is a statistical-methods paper whose central object is not a single forecast but a continuously updated probability curve. The derivation proceeds in three coupled hops. First, for each game \(i=1,\ldots,N\) and normalized intra-game time \(t\in[0,1]\), the paper treats the published home-team win probability \(\hat p_i^{\mathrm{ESPN}}(t)\) as an estimate of \(p_i(t)=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid \mathcal F_{i-1,t})\), where \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) is the home-team win indicator and \(\mathcal F_{i-1,t}\) is the information from previous games plus the current game up to time \(t\) [PAGE 7], [PAGE 31]. The paper states that “we develop new tools for measuring the quality of continuously updated probabilistic forecasts” [PAGE 1], and it separates evaluation into calibration and skill. Calibration is assessed by adaptive-bin reliability plots: at each \(t\), forecasts are ranked and split into \(M\) bins, the bin summary is the median \(\tilde p_j(t)\), and the observed frequency is \(\bar Y_j(t)\); Wilson intervals are used because late-game forecasts cluster near 0 and 1 [PAGE 11], [PAGE 12]. The paper explicitly says “we propose to use adaptive bins in constructing the calibration plots” [PAGE 11] and “we use these intervals in calibration plot construction below” [PAGE 12]. The surfaces \(U_{1-\alpha}(t,p)\) and \(L_{1-\alpha}(t,p)\) are interpolated from interval bounds, and calibration is judged by whether the reference plane \(f(t,p)=p\) lies between them [PAGE 12]. The summaries \(U^{\min}_{1-\alpha}(t)\) and \(L^{\max}_{1-\alpha}(t)\) compress the surfaces into time-indexed diagnostics.

Second, skill is measured through a loss function. The paper uses the Brier loss \(L(a,b)=(a-b)^2\); it notes that “Perhaps the most frequently used loss function is \(L(a,b)=(a-b)^2\) and defines the Brier score” [PAGE 16]. For two forecast methods \(A\) and \(B\), the pointwise average loss difference is \(\hat\Delta_N(t)=N^{-1}\sum_i[L(Y_i,\hat p_i^A(t))-L(Y_i,\hat p_i^B(t))]\) [PAGE 17]. A confidence interval is built from \(\hat\Delta_N(t)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}s_N(t)/\sqrt N\) [PAGE 18]. The nondegeneracy condition is essential: the theorem “only holds if the forecasts of the two methods to be compared do not coincide entirely” [PAGE 18]. The paper also replaces the unknown \(p_i(t)(1-p_i(t))\) by \(1/4\) to obtain a conservative interval [PAGE 18].

Third, the functional test aggregates over time. The null hypothesis is \(H_0:\|\Delta_N\|_2^2=0\) [PAGE 19]. The process \(Z_N(t)=\sqrt N\hat\Delta_N(t)\) is shown to converge weakly in \(L^2[0,1]\) to a Gaussian process, and \(\|Z_N\|_2^2\) converges to \(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_i\chi_i^2(1)\) [PAGE 20], [PAGE 33]. The eigenvalues are conservatively estimated from \(\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}(t,s)=N^{-1}\sum_i[\hat p_i^A(t)-\hat p_i^B(t)][\hat p_i^A(s)-\hat p_i^B(s)]\) [PAGE 20]. The paper describes “a simple way of conducting an approximate and conservative test of the hypothesis \(H_0\)” [PAGE 20] and uses \(D=10\) in the truncated weighted chi-square approximation [PAGE 20].

The gray-zone audit is where the method can fail. Assumption A.1 requires that forecasts use “only the information from all previous games as well as the current game up to time \(t\)” [PAGE 32]. If future information leaks into \(\hat p_i(t)\), the martingale-difference argument fails and the test is invalid. Assumption A.2 requires a stable limiting covariance kernel; nonstationary or adversarial forecast curves break the kernel convergence. Assumption A.3 requires tightness in \(L^2[0,1]\), which is a smoothness/continuity requirement; linear interpolation and moving-average smoothing help interpretation but do not prove the assumption. The paper itself flags the nested-model breakdown: “nested models may lead to a covariance kernel \(\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}\) that is approximately (and asymptotically) degenerate” [PAGE 29]. Small \(N\), near-deterministic late-game probabilities, and near-coincident forecasts also degrade the approximation. The calibration side has its own regimes: empty or sparse bins, extreme probabilities near 0 or 1, multiple simultaneous events averaged into one time point, and overtime games removed from the analysis [PAGE 5], [PAGE 12]. Thus the right reading is conditional: the tools are valid under no-leakage, stable-kernel, nondegenerate-loss-difference, and adequate-bin-occupancy conditions; they are not a universal causal engine.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s formal probability target is
\[
p_i(t)=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid \mathcal F_{i-1,t}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,\quad t\in[0,1],
\]
where \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\), \(t\) is the normalized proportion of the game completed, and \(\mathcal F_{i-1,t}\) is the information set up to time \(t\) in game \(i\) plus previous games [PAGE 7], [PAGE 31]. Units: \(Y_i\) is dimensionless indicator; \(t\) is dimensionless proportion in \([0,1]\); \(p_i(t)\) and \(\hat p_i(t)\) are dimensionless probabilities in \([0,1]\). The paper’s causal assumption is explicit: “only the information from all previous games as well as the current game up to time \(t\) are used to produce the forecasts” [PAGE 32]. A forecast curve that uses the final outcome \(Y_i\) or any post-\(t\) event violates this assumption and voids the martingale-difference proof.

For calibration, at a fixed \(t\), rank the forecasts as \(\hat p_{(1)}(t),\ldots,\hat p_{(N)}(t)\). With \(M\) adaptive bins, bin \(j\) contains ranks satisfying
\[
\lfloor N/M\rfloor(j-1)+1 \le i \lt \lfloor N/M\rfloor j
\]
up to the paper’s rank convention [PAGE 11]. The bin reference is
\[
\tilde p_j(t)=\operatorname{Median}\{\hat p_{(i)}(t): i\in \text{bin }j\},
\]
and the empirical event frequency is
\[
\bar Y_j(t)=\operatorname{Average}\{Y_i:\hat p_i(t)\in \mathrm{Bin}_j\}.
\]
The Wilson interval is
\[
\frac{n_j\bar Y_j(t)+\kappa^2/2}{n_j+\kappa^2}
\pm
\frac{\kappa n_j^{1/2}}{n_j+\kappa^2}
\left(\bar Y_j(t)(1-\bar Y_j(t))+\frac{\kappa^2}{4n_j}\right)^{1/2},
\]
with \(\kappa=z_{\alpha/(2M)}\) and Bonferroni correction over \(M\) bins [PAGE 11], [PAGE 12]. The upper and lower calibration surfaces \(U_{1-\alpha}(t,p)\) and \(L_{1-\alpha}(t,p)\) are interpolated from these bounds. The paper states calibration is judged by containment of \(f(t,p)=p\) between both surfaces [PAGE 12]. The summary diagnostics are
\[
U^{\min}_{1-\alpha}(t)=\min_{1\le j\le M}\{U_{1-\alpha}(t,\tilde p_j(t))-\tilde p_j(t)\},
\]
\[
L^{\max}_{1-\alpha}(t)=\max_{1\le j\le M}\{L_{1-\alpha}(t,\tilde p_j(t))-\tilde p_j(t)\}.
\]
If the reference plane is contained, \(U^{\min}_{1-\alpha}(t)\) should lie above zero and \(L^{\max}_{1-\alpha}(t)\) below zero [PAGE 13]. Units: all are probability units, dimensionless.

For skill, the Brier loss is
\[
L(a,b)=(a-b)^2,
\]
with \(a,b\in[0,1]\); units are squared probability units, dimensionless [PAGE 16]. The average loss and loss difference are
\[
\hat L_N(t)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N L(Y_i,\hat p_i(t)),
\]
\[
\hat\Delta_N(t)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\left[L(Y_i,\hat p_i^A(t))-L(Y_i,\hat p_i^B(t))\right].
\]
The paper defines
\[
s_N^2(t)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \delta_i^2(t)p_i(t)(1-p_i(t)),
\]
where
\[
\delta_i(t)=\left[L(1,\hat p_i^A(t))-L(0,\hat p_i^A(t))\right]-\left[L(1,\hat p_i^B(t))-L(0,\hat p_i^B(t))\right],
\]
and gives the pointwise interval
\[
\hat\Delta_N(t)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{s_N(t)}{\sqrt N}
\]
under the Lai et al. theorem [PAGE 17], [PAGE 18]. The nondegeneracy condition is that \(s_N^2(t)\) converges in probability to a positive constant; the paper warns the result is not valid for “two methods that produce equivalent, or almost equivalent, forecasts” [PAGE 18]. The conservative variance uses \(p_i(t)(1-p_i(t))\le 1/4\) [PAGE 18].

The functional null is
\[
H_0:\|\Delta_N\|_2^2=0,\qquad \|f\|_2^2=\int_0^1 f^2(t)\,dt,
\]
with \(Z_N(t)=\sqrt N\hat\Delta_N(t)\) [PAGE 19]. Under \(H_0\) and Assumptions A.1–A.3,
\[
\|Z_N\|_2^2 \xrightarrow{D}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_i\chi_i^2(1),
\]
where \(\chi_i^2(1)\) are independent central chi-square variables with one degree of freedom and \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge0\) [PAGE 20], [PAGE 33]. The conservative covariance estimate is
\[
\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}(t,s)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N[\hat p_i^A(t)-\hat p_i^B(t)][\hat p_i^A(s)-\hat p_i^B(s)],
\]
and its eigenvalues \(\hat\lambda_i\) satisfy
\[
\hat\lambda_i\hat\phi_i(t)=\int_0^1\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}(t,s)\hat\phi_i(s)\,ds,
\]
with \(\|\hat\phi_i\|_2=1\) [PAGE 20]. The approximate conservative \(p\)-value is \(\Pr(Q_D\ge\|Z_N\|_2^2)\) with \(Q_D=\sum_{i=1}^D\hat\lambda_i\chi_i^2(1)\), \(D=10\) in the paper [PAGE 20]. Units: \(Z_N(t)\) is dimensionless because \(\hat\Delta_N(t)\) is a squared-probability difference and \(\sqrt N\) is dimensionless; \(\|Z_N\|_2^2\) is dimensionless; \(\lambda_i\) and \(\chi_i^2(1)\) are dimensionless.

Breakdown and singularity regimes are specific. (1) \(s_N^2(t)\to0\) or \(\hat p_i^A(t)\approx\hat p_i^B(t)\) for many \(i\): the pointwise normal approximation degenerates; nested or near-nested models are the canonical failure [PAGE 18], [PAGE 29]. (2) \(\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}\) is rank-deficient or has many zero eigenvalues: the weighted chi-square approximation and eigenvalue truncation become unstable; the paper warns nested models can make it “approximately (and asymptotically) degenerate” [PAGE 29]. (3) \(p_i(t)\) near 0 or 1: the binomial variance \(p_i(t)(1-p_i(t))\) collapses; Wilson intervals are more robust than the normal approximation, but calibration bins can still be sparse or deterministic [PAGE 12]. (4) \(N\) small relative to \(M\) and \(D\): bin occupancy and eigenvalue estimation are noisy; the paper’s simulation shows competitive-model discrimination can require \(N\ge500\) [PAGE 24], [PAGE 26]. (5) \(t\to0\) or \(t\to1\): forecasts cluster and interpolation effects are largest; the paper discards forecasts outside \(0.005\) and \(0.995\) for calibration plots and analyzes those extremal cases separately [PAGE 12]. (6) Overtime removal and averaging of simultaneous events create a processed-data regime that differs from raw play-by-play [PAGE 5]. (7) Any leakage of post-\(t\) information violates Assumption A.1 and invalidates the martingale central limit theorem [PAGE 32]. (8) Nonstationarity or roughness violating Assumptions A.2–A.3 invalidates the \(L^2[0,1]\) weak convergence [PAGE 33]. These are the exact regimes where the formalism must be vetoed or reinterpreted rather than pushed through mechanically.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a fusion of forecast verification, functional data analysis, and martingale limit theory. The calibration/skill distinction descends from Murphy and Winkler’s forecast-verification framework, and the paper cites Murphy and Winkler (1987) and (1992) as the seminal evaluation split [PAGE 2]. The Brier score is traced to Brier (1950), and the calibration-versus-sharpness discussion is tied to Gneiting et al. (2007) and Gneiting and Katzfuss (2014) [PAGE 2], [PAGE 3], [PAGE 16]. The Wilson interval lineage is Wilson (1927) and Brown et al. (2001); the paper notes that the Wilson interval is “more robust to event frequencies close to zero and one” [PAGE 12]. The pointwise skill-difference inference is from Lai et al. (2011), and the functional aggregation relies on martingale central limit theory, Brown (1971), \(L^2[0,1]\) weak convergence, Bosq (2000), Mercer’s theorem, Karhunen–Loève representation, and Imhof (1961) for quadratic forms in normal variables [PAGE 17], [PAGE 20], [PAGE 33]. The sports-model lineage includes Stern (1994), Gabel and Redner (2012), and Chen and Fan (2018) for Brownian-motion or functional score-difference models, and Elo (1978) for team-strength ratings [PAGE 7], [PAGE 21], [PAGE 29]. The paper’s own simulation uses \(ScD_i(t)=tRS_i+W_i(t)\), with \(W_i(t)\) a standard Brownian motion and \(RS_i\sim a\,\mathrm{Unif}(-1,1)+c\), and defines the oracle probability
\[
p_i(t)=\Phi\left(\frac{ScD_i(t)+RS_i(1-t)}{\sqrt{1-t}}\right)
\]
[PAGE 21]. This is a phenomenological score-difference model, not a biomechanical or physiological model.

The retrodictive lesson is that each layer was imported for a reason: calibration plots because probabilities can be reliable but useless; loss differences because reliability alone does not reward sharpness; functional tests because pointwise significance can overstate aggregate evidence. The multi-week traps follow directly. First, treating each intra-game snapshot as an independent observation across weeks ignores within-game and within-team correlation; Assumption A.2 is precisely a stationarity/ergodicity stabilizer, not an independence guarantee [PAGE 32], [PAGE 33]. Second, repeated weekly re-analysis with the same games and multiple time points inflates false positives unless one accounts for the functional family or uses the aggregate test. Third, fixed-bin calibration across weeks fails when forecast distributions drift toward 0 and 1 late in games; adaptive bins are the paper’s answer [PAGE 11]. Fourth, comparing nested models across weeks with the Lai-type interval is a trap because the covariance kernel can degenerate [PAGE 29]. Fifth, the paper’s data processing—averaging multiple simultaneous events and removing overtime—creates a multi-week comparability issue: raw feeds and processed feeds are not the same estimand [PAGE 5]. Sixth, simulation evidence shows that competitive models may need \(N\ge500\) before the aggregate test has useful power, so a few weeks of games can be too small for strong claims [PAGE 24], [PAGE 26]. Seventh, the paper’s own conclusion cautions against overreading complexity: “ESPN’s sophisticated proprietary model, which ostensibly makes use of more nuanced information about the game status and more sophisticated models, did not significantly outperform a simple Logistic regression model” [PAGE 26]. That is the retrospective correction against assuming that a richer historical lineage or proprietary pipeline automatically yields higher skill.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that three agents reason about one another: the forecast producer chooses a continuously updated probability curve, the evaluator chooses calibration and skill diagnostics, and the forecast consumer chooses how much trust to place in the curve at each clock time \(t\). The paper’s evidence supports a time-varying theory of trust. Early in the game, relative team strength and home advantage dominate; the paper’s diagnostic discussion says relative team strength is most important early and becomes less important later as score difference becomes more informative [PAGE 9], [PAGE 10]. Late in the game, score difference dominates and forecasts cluster near 0 or 1. This is the “clock” layer: the same forecast method can be well-calibrated in aggregate but locally miscalibrated at particular \(t\), and the paper therefore builds surfaces and summaries over \(t\) rather than a single scalar [PAGE 12], [PAGE 13]. The moving-average smoother over 5% of game times is a recognition that raw curves fluctuate too much for stable visual inference [PAGE 13], [PAGE 18].

“Crisis habit regression” appears as the tendency to fall back to a low-information prior when the game is noisy. The HomeWP model sets \(\hat p_i(t)=0.593\) at all \(t\), the historical home-team win frequency; the paper reports it is well-calibrated “as expected” but obviously not sharp [PAGE 8], [PAGE 14]. That is the habit: when uncertainty is high, regress to the base rate. The counter-trap is the opposite error: assuming that a sophisticated proprietary model must beat a simple model. The paper’s comparison of ESPN with PgRSScD says the estimated Brier skill generally favored the simple logit model except in the last moments, and the difference was not statistically significant at the 5% level pointwise or in aggregate [PAGE 26], [PAGE 28]. Thus the recursive evaluator must avoid both traps: base-rate regression is calibrated but low-skill; complexity is not automatically high-skill. The correct move is to ask whether the added information improves the loss difference at the relevant clock region and whether the evidence survives the functional test.

The clock also changes the counterfactual reasoning of the consumer. If the consumer observes a late-game probability near 1, the relevant question is not whether the forecaster “knows basketball” but whether the forecast is calibrated at that extremal region; the paper’s Table 2 checks forecasts above 0.995 and below 0.005 and finds the empirical home-team win rates close to those thresholds [PAGE 14]. If the consumer observes a mid-game difference between competing methods, the relevant question is whether the pointwise interval excludes zero and whether the aggregate norm is significant [PAGE 18], [PAGE 19]. The recursive theory of mind therefore has three corrections: (1) do not infer global skill from one clock instant; (2) do not infer skill from calibration alone; (3) do not infer superiority from model complexity. The paper’s own conclusion is a disciplined counter-trap: ESPN beats naive models, but does not demonstrate superiority over a simple logistic model using score difference and relative strength [PAGE 4], [PAGE 26].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer claim must pass physical, biomechanical, and weather vetoes. The physical layer is the score-difference process: the paper’s simulation models \(ScD_i(t)=tRS_i+W_i(t)\), a Brownian motion with drift, and uses the oracle probability in [PAGE 21]. This is a stochastic phenomenological model of score evolution; it does not identify force, momentum, player load, or any mechanical quantity. Therefore any claim that a forecast difference is caused by a physical force or a biomechanical mechanism is vetoed unless additional measurements are supplied. The paper’s observed data analysis uses only a subset: “We consider here only a subset of these data that includes the real-time probabilistic forecasts provided by ESPN, as well as the evolution of the score throughout the game” [PAGE 5]. The data do not include player kinematics, ground reaction forces, muscle fatigue, or injury severity in the analyzed subset. Even though the paper acknowledges that ESPN’s initial forecasts may use injuries and in-game events [PAGE 2], the evaluation itself does not measure those mechanisms. Thus a biomechanical explanation for late-game calibration or skill differences is not identifiable from the presented evidence.

The weather layer is similarly vetoed. The application is indoor professional basketball; the paper contains no weather covariates, no arena temperature, no travel weather, and no outdoor exposure variable. Any extrapolation from these calibration surfaces to weather-driven sports or outdoor timing is outside the data-generating process. The paper’s scope is NBA game outcome prediction and its forecast curves [PAGE 1], [PAGE 2]. A weather claim would require a different dataset and a different causal graph; the present operators cannot adjudicate it.

There are additional cross-layer vetoes from data processing. Overtime is removed: the paper says overtime games are removed from the analysis and only regulation-time forecasts are considered [PAGE 5]. Multiple simultaneous events are averaged into one forecast at that instant [PAGE 5]. These choices are reasonable for comparability, but they mean the estimand is a processed regulation-time curve, not raw physical play-by-play. A claim about biomechanical fatigue in overtime, or about event-order-dependent microstructure, is vetoed by the processing itself. The calibration layer also vetoes overgeneralization: forecasts below 0.005 or above 0.995 are discarded from the calibration plot at a given \(t\) and analyzed separately [PAGE 12]. That is a deliberate extremal-regime separation, not evidence that the same binning logic works in all probability regions. Finally, the functional test’s assumptions veto claims of universal validity: Assumption A.1 forbids future information [PAGE 32], Assumption A.2 forbids arbitrary nonstationarity [PAGE 33], and Assumption A.3 requires \(L^2[0,1]\) tightness [PAGE 33]. A cross-layer claim that ignores these conditions is not adjudicable by the paper’s operators.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Future-information leakage counterfactual. Intervention: construct a counterfactual forecast \(\hat p_i^{\mathrm{leak}}(t)\) that uses \(Y_i\) or any post-\(t\) event, then compare it with a legitimate forecast using the paper’s pointwise and functional tests. Verifier: timestamp-audit the forecast construction and recompute \(\hat p_i(t)\) using only \(\mathcal F_{i-1,t}\), as required by Assumption A.1 [PAGE 32]. Prediction: the leaky forecast will appear artificially sharper and may show smaller Brier loss, but the martingale-difference condition \(E[X_{i,v}\mid\mathcal F_{i-1}]=0\) will fail; the reported \(p\)-values and confidence intervals will be invalid. A correct RLVR reward must therefore reject any policy that accesses post-\(t\) information, even if its apparent calibration improves.

2. Fixed-bin calibration counterfactual. Intervention: replace the paper’s adaptive rank bins with fixed deciles and recompute \(U^{\min}_{1-\alpha}(t)\) and \(L^{\max}_{1-\alpha}(t)\) across \(t\). Verifier: compare the two calibration summaries at late \(t\), where forecasts cluster near 0 and 1, and check whether bins contain forecasts below 0.005 or above 0.995; the paper discards those extremal forecasts for calibration plots and analyzes them separately [PAGE 12]. Prediction: fixed bins will produce more spurious crossings of the reference plane late in games because bin occupancy is unbalanced and the bin center is a poor summary; adaptive bins with Wilson intervals will be more stable [PAGE 11], [PAGE 12]. The verified reward should penalize a calibration diagnostic that changes its conclusion under a reasonable binning intervention.

3. Nested-model comparison counterfactual. Intervention: compare two nested generalized linear models, such as PgRSScD versus PgRS, using the pointwise intervals and the aggregate \(H_0\) test. Verifier: compute \(s_N^2(t)\), estimate \(\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}\), and inspect the eigenvalues \(\hat\lambda_j\); check whether \(s_N^2(t)\) is near zero or whether the kernel is approximately degenerate. The paper explicitly warns that nested models may lead to a covariance kernel \(\hat C_{\mathrm{cons}}\) that is “approximately (and asymptotically) degenerate” [PAGE 29], and the nondegeneracy condition requires forecasts not to coincide entirely [PAGE 18]. Prediction: the standard interval and weighted chi-square test will be unreliable or overly conservative in the nested case; a verified reward must veto a claim of significant skill difference unless a nested-model-specific procedure, such as the Clark–McCracken approach cited by the paper, is used [PAGE 29]. These three counterfactuals are externally checkable because they reduce to causal-mask audits, bin-occupancy checks, and eigenvalue/variance diagnostics rather than subjective interpretation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
