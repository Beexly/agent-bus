The first multi-hop reading is from the abstract’s claim that “social influences, relative advantages, and enjoyment impact the use of smartwatches during training and daily use by soccer players” [PAGE 1] to the model statement that “There are seven (7) hypotheses whose influence is a positive statement” [PAGE 2], then to the sample statement that “A total of 128 football players have filled out the questionnaire” [PAGE 3]. The model has six latent constructs: Attitude (A), Behavioral Intention (BI), Enjoyment (E), Relative Advantage (RA), Result Demonstrability (RD), and Social Influence (SI). The structural results in Table 5 [PAGE 4] show H1 RD→A = 0.131, p=0.157; H2 E→A = 0.214, p=0.041; H3 SI→A = 0.496, p=0.000; H4 RA→A = 0.182, p=0.033; H5 A→BI = 0.607, p=0.000; H6 RA→BI = 0.077, p=0.508; H7 SI→BI = 0.081, p=0.499. The authors summarize: “The four accepted hypotheses are H2, H3, H4, and H5, indicated by a p-value less than 0.05” [PAGE 4]. Thus the strongest direct path is attitude to behavioral intention, followed by social influence to attitude. The rejected direct paths RA→BI and SI→BI are important because they imply that, within this model, relative advantage and social influence are mediated through attitude rather than acting directly on intention. A simple arithmetic product of standardized paths gives approximate indirect associations E→A→BI ≈ 0.214×0.607 = 0.130, SI→A→BI ≈ 0.496×0.607 = 0.301, and RA→A→BI ≈ 0.182×0.607 = 0.110. These products are not tests of indirect effects because the paper does not report bootstrap confidence intervals for indirect paths; they are only consistency checks.

The second multi-hop reading concerns measurement. Table 2 reports outer loadings, and the authors state: “The outer loading value on the data has met the statistical requirements where the outer loading value is greater than 0.7” [PAGE 4]. Table 4 reports composite reliability and AVE values; for example, Attitude CR=0.947, AVE=0.783; BI CR=0.935, AVE=0.828; Enjoyment CR=0.910, AVE=0.670; RA CR=0.901, AVE=0.573; RD CR=0.915, AVE=0.682; SI CR=0.917, AVE=0.649 [PAGE 4]. Table 3’s diagonal values are the square roots of AVE, which match Fornell-Larcker values: sqrt(0.783)=0.885, sqrt(0.828)=0.910, sqrt(0.670)=0.818, sqrt(0.573)=0.757, sqrt(0.682)=0.826, sqrt(0.649)=0.806 [PAGE 4]. That is evidence of discriminant validity under the Fornell-Larcker criterion, but it is not evidence of causal direction.

The third multi-hop reading concerns explained variance. Table 6 reports Attitude R Square = 0.736 and Adjusted R Square = 0.728; BI R Square = 0.515 and Adjusted R Square = 0.503 [PAGE 5]. The text then says: “The test results show that the R-square of the attitude construct is 0.728” [PAGE 5] and “Likewise, for behavioral intention, an R-square of 0.503” [PAGE 5]. This is a gray-zone inconsistency: the text appears to be quoting the adjusted R² as if it were R². Mathematically, for n=128 and k=4 predictors for Attitude, adjusted R² = 1−(1−0.736)(127/123) ≈ 0.727; for BI with k=3 predictors, 1−(1−0.515)(127/124) ≈ 0.503. Thus the table values are internally coherent, but the prose mislabels adjusted R². This does not reverse the substantive conclusion, but it matters for precise reporting.

The fourth gray-zone audit is inferential. The paper uses causal language such as “impact” and “influenced by attitude” [PAGE 1; PAGE 4], but the design is a single-time questionnaire. The sample is 128 amateur and semi-professional players in East Java; “43% have used a smartwatch, and the rest plan to use it” [PAGE 3]. That means more than half of respondents are reporting intention or planning, not observed use. The outcome BI is intention, not logged training behavior. The paper cannot establish temporal precedence between attitude and use across weeks, cannot rule out reverse causation, and cannot rule out omitted variables such as team resources, coach mandates, price sensitivity, or device compatibility. Also, seven hypotheses are tested at p<0.05 without a stated multiplicity correction. Under a Bonferroni threshold α=0.05/7≈0.00714, H2 p=0.041 and H4 p=0.033 would not survive, while H3 p=0.000 and H5 p=0.000 would. That does not invalidate the model, but it is a precise fragility audit: the E→A and RA→A paths are the weakest accepted paths in the table.

Overall, the source supports a mediation-style reading: enjoyment, social influence, and relative advantage are associated with attitude; attitude is associated with intention; direct RA→BI and SI→BI paths are not supported. It does not support a claim that smartwatch use improves football performance, that the device is valid for heart-rate monitoring in match play, or that intention persists over a multi-week training block. Those are separate cross-layer claims requiring sensor validation and longitudinal behavioral data.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal PLS-SEM measurement model for indicator \(i\) on construct \(j\) can be written as
\[
x_{ij}=\lambda_{ij}\xi_j+\varepsilon_{ij},
\]
where \(x_{ij}\) is the observed Likert response (ordinal, 5-point scale), \(\xi_j\) is the latent construct score, \(\lambda_{ij}\) is the outer loading (dimensionless, standardized in the reported solution), and \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) is the measurement error. The source states that “Questionnaire items were measured using a 5-point Likert scale” [PAGE 3] and that “The outer loading value on the data has met the statistical requirements where the outer loading value is greater than 0.7” [PAGE 4]. Assumption: the reported loadings are standardized; if raw indicators are used, \(\lambda_{ij}\) inherits the units of \(x_{ij}/\xi_j\). Breakdown regime: if an indicator has zero variance, or if two indicators are exact duplicates, the indicator block has rank deficiency and the weights are not unique.

Composite reliability and average variance extracted are
\[
\mathrm{CR}_j=\frac{\left(\sum_i \hat\lambda_{ij}\right)^2}{\left(\sum_i \hat\lambda_{ij}\right)^2+\sum_i \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\varepsilon_{ij})},
\qquad
\mathrm{AVE}_j=\frac{\sum_i \hat\lambda_{ij}^2}{k_j},
\]
with \(k_j\) the number of indicators. Both are dimensionless. The reported Table 4 values are all above common thresholds: CR from 0.901 to 0.947 and AVE from 0.573 to 0.828 [PAGE 4]. The Fornell-Larcker condition is
\[
\sqrt{\mathrm{AVE}_j}>\max_{k\ne j}|r_{jk}|,
\]
where \(r_{jk}\) is the latent correlation. Table 3’s diagonal values match \(\sqrt{\mathrm{AVE}}\): 0.885, 0.910, 0.818, 0.757, 0.826, and 0.806 [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if \(\sqrt{\mathrm{AVE}_j}\le |r_{jk}|\), discriminant validity fails even when CR is high.

The structural model implied by Table 5 [PAGE 4] is
\[
A=\pi_{RD,A}RD+\pi_{E,A}E+\pi_{SI,A}SI+\pi_{RA,A}RA+\zeta_A,
\]
\[
BI=\pi_{A,BI}A+\pi_{RA,BI}RA+\pi_{SI,BI}SI+\zeta_{BI},
\]
where all \(\pi\) are standardized path coefficients (dimensionless), and \(\zeta\) are disturbance terms. The null hypotheses are \(H_0:\pi=0\) against positive one-sided alternatives as stated by “There are seven (7) hypotheses whose influence is a positive statement” [PAGE 2]. The reported estimates are: \(\hat\pi_{RD,A}=0.131\) (p=0.157), \(\hat\pi_{E,A}=0.214\) (p=0.041), \(\hat\pi_{SI,A}=0.496\) (p=0.000), \(\hat\pi_{RA,A}=0.182\) (p=0.033), \(\hat\pi_{A,BI}=0.607\) (p=0.000), \(\hat\pi_{RA,BI}=0.077\) (p=0.508), and \(\hat\pi_{SI,BI}=0.081\) (p=0.499). The paper concludes: “The four accepted hypotheses are H2, H3, H4, and H5, indicated by a p-value less than 0.05” [PAGE 4]. p-values are probabilities in \([0,1]\), not effect sizes.

Explained variance is
\[
R^2_A=1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\zeta_A)}{\mathrm{Var}(A)},\qquad
R^2_{BI}=1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\zeta_{BI})}{\mathrm{Var}(BI)}.
\]
Both are dimensionless proportions. Table 6 gives \(R^2_A=0.736\), adjusted \(R^2_A=0.728\), \(R^2_{BI}=0.515\), and adjusted \(R^2_{BI}=0.503\) [PAGE 5]. The adjustment formula is
\[
R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=1-(1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-k-1},
\]
with \(n=128\). For Attitude, \(k=4\), giving approximately 0.727; for BI, \(k=3\), giving approximately 0.503. This proves the prose’s “R-square … 0.728” and “R-square of 0.503” [PAGE 5] are adjusted values, not the table’s raw \(R^2\). Breakdown regime: if \(n\le k+1\), adjusted \(R^2\) is undefined or non-identifiable; if predictors are perfectly collinear, \(X^\top X\) is singular and path coefficients are not unique. Here the sample is 128 and the indicator count is 29, so the global rank condition is not immediately violated, but local collinearity or zero-variance items can still break estimation.

Indirect associations, under the simple mediation assumption and ignoring sampling uncertainty, are
\[
\widehat{\pi}_{E\to A\to BI}=\hat\pi_{E,A}\hat\pi_{A,BI}\approx 0.214(0.607)=0.130,
\]
\[
\widehat{\pi}_{SI\to A\to BI}=\hat\pi_{SI,A}\hat\pi_{A,BI}\approx 0.496(0.607)=0.301,
\]
\[
\widehat{\pi}_{RA\to A\to BI}=\hat\pi_{RA,A}\hat\pi_{A,BI}\approx 0.182(0.607)=0.110.
\]
These are arithmetic products of standardized coefficients; the paper does not report bootstrap intervals for indirect effects, so they are not verified indirect effects. The direct paths H6 and H7 are not supported (p=0.508 and p=0.499), which is consistent with full mediation through attitude in this model, but longitudinal and experimental data are needed to distinguish mediation from confounding.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is visible in the paper’s own citations. The model retrodicts earlier technology-acceptance constructs: Relative Advantage is explicitly mapped to UTAUT performance expectancy and TAM perceived usefulness. The authors state: “Relative advantage has a definition that is almost the same as the performance expectancy variable in UTAUT (Venkatesh et al. 2003) and the perceived usefulness variable in TAM (Davis et al.,1992)” [PAGE 4]. Enjoyment is treated as a hedonic factor, consistent with flow-oriented and enjoyment-based acceptance work cited in the paper; Social Influence is treated as a normative/status factor, with items such as “My manager/Coach influences my smartwatch usage, and it's important to influence my behavior toward using a smartwatch” [PAGE 3]. The item pool is drawn from Wu et al. (2016), listed in the references as “Exploring consumers’ intention to accept smartwatch” [PAGE 6]. Thus the paper is not a new behavioral theory; it is a domain transfer of TAM/UTAUT-style constructs to Indonesian football players.

The retrocausal trap is to read the 2023 cross-sectional result backward as proof that enjoyment, social influence, and relative advantage caused adoption over a training season. The design cannot support that temporal direction. The paper measures intention, and “43% have used a smartwatch, and the rest plan to use it” [PAGE 3]. The 57% planning group may have different attitudes, social pressures, and device access than the 43% user group; pooling them can create a retrocausal illusion in which current intention is treated as a proxy for future behavior. The brand distribution is also skewed: “The smartwatch brand that respondents most choose is Xiaomi” [PAGE 3], with 44 of 128 respondents choosing Xiaomi. That matters because sensor quality, app ecosystem, and social visibility are brand-dependent; a generic “smartwatch” construct may hide device-level heterogeneity.

Multi-week traps are therefore substantial. First, a one-time attitude measure cannot capture novelty decay: enjoyment may be high in early use and fall after repeated charging, syncing, and data-cleaning burdens. Second, social influence may be strongest in team selection or preseason training and weaker in isolated off-season work. Third, intention may not survive a multi-week protocol if coaches do not reinforce data use. The paper’s coach-use claim is practical but not longitudinal: “player fitness data can be directly observed by coaches through applications on smartphones” [PAGE 4]. That is a proposed workflow, not an observed multi-week adoption curve. Fourth, the outcome is intention, not recorded training sessions, so the model cannot distinguish players who intend to use a watch from players who actually wear it across repeated sessions. A genealogical reading therefore places the paper in the acceptance-intention tradition, not in the sensor-validation or behavioral-adherence tradition. The correct inference is associational and model-internal: within this sample, the attitude-mediated paths are supported; the multi-week causal claim remains outside the design.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The nested agents are the player, the coach, the team manager, and the smartwatch manufacturer. The player’s recursive model is: “If I wear the watch, the coach can see my fitness data; if the coach sees my data, my training or selection may change; therefore I should manage what the data show.” The coach’s recursive model is: “If the app shows a player’s heart-rate or distance data, I can make a substitution or adjust training.” The manager’s model is procurement and team investment; the paper notes that team investments and finances, including smartwatch procurement, are management responsibilities [PAGE 5]. The manufacturer’s model is feature design: enjoyment and social sharing are treated as adoption levers, with the paper recommending hedonic elements and social-media sharing [PAGE 4].

Crisis habit regression occurs when the player is fatigued, stressed, or competing for selection. Under those conditions, the player may revert to prior habits: not charging the device, forgetting to start the session, relying on coach observation, or removing the watch during contact drills. The model’s high A→BI path does not measure that regression because it has no repeated behavioral observations. The counter-trap is over-trust: the coach may treat app data as objective and ignore context such as sleep, illness, heat, or tactical role. The paper’s own example—using fitness data “to replace players during matches or to determine the appropriate portion of training” [PAGE 4]—shows why the counter-trap matters. If the player anticipates that the watch will drive substitution, the player may alter behavior to produce favorable data, which is a recursive response not captured by the SEM.

The clock is the missing variable. There is a session clock, a match clock, a weekly training clock, and a season clock. The paper measures attitudes at one time point and does not index behavior by minutes, sessions, or weeks. A player may have high intention before the season and low adherence after three weeks of travel and matches. A coach may use data acutely during a match but not longitudinally across a training block. The recursive theory-of-mind prediction is that social influence is not a static path coefficient; it is a time-varying expectation about how coaches and teammates will interpret wearable data. That is why the direct SI→BI path can be non-significant while SI→A is strong: social influence may shape evaluation and attitude without directly producing intention when the player lacks device access or a concrete use plan. The clock also creates a counter-trap in interpretation: a single-time attitude survey cannot distinguish “I like the idea” from “I will wear it in week four when the device is uncomfortable and the coach is not checking.” The correct mental-model audit is therefore to treat the reported paths as snapshots of appraisal, not as a time-integrated habit model.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is physical and measurement-theoretic. The paper justifies smartwatch relevance partly through heart rate: “an important point used by coaches to monitor players' fitness performance is the heart rate value” [PAGE 1]. But a survey of acceptance cannot establish that the watch’s heart-rate signal is valid during football. Let \(H_t\) be true heart rate in beats per minute and \(\hat H_t\) the watch estimate. A minimal error model is
\[
\hat H_t=H_t+b_{\mathrm{contact}}+m_{\mathrm{motion}}+w_{\mathrm{weather}}+q_{\mathrm{device}}+e_t,
\]
where all terms are in bpm. \(b_{\mathrm{contact}}\) is skin-contact offset from poor wrist fit; \(m_{\mathrm{motion}}\) is motion artifact from arm swing, ball impact, and wrist flexion; \(w_{\mathrm{weather}}\) is sweat, rain, heat, or cold-induced perfusion change; \(q_{\mathrm{device}}\) is quantization and algorithm error; \(e_t\) is random noise. The signal-to-noise condition for valid monitoring is
\[
\mathrm{Var}(H_{\mathrm{physiological\ signal}})>\mathrm{Var}(m_{\mathrm{motion}}+w_{\mathrm{weather}}+b_{\mathrm{contact}}+q_{\mathrm{device}}+e_t).
\]
If the inequality reverses, the observed watch value is dominated by artifact and cannot adjudicate training load. Breakdown regimes include: high-intensity intermittent football with rapid wrist acceleration; wet or sweaty skin reducing optical contact; cold conditions reducing peripheral perfusion; and contact drills in which the watch moves or is removed. Biomechanically, football is not a steady-state treadmill task; wrist-mounted optical sensing is vulnerable to motion and contact. Weather can veto the inference even if the player’s attitude is positive, because physical signal failure is independent of behavioral intention. The paper’s app workflow—data observed by coaches through smartphones [PAGE 4]—presupposes valid sensing and valid data transmission. If the sensing layer fails, a high A→BI coefficient cannot license player-substitution decisions. The correct adjudication is layered: acceptance is necessary but insufficient; sensor validity, wear compliance, and data interpretation must each pass before the coach-facing decision layer is entitled to use the data.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Longitudinal actual-use veto. Pre-register a four-week training log in which smartwatch-recorded sessions are counted per player. Metric: proportion of scheduled sessions with a valid recording, plus device wear time in minutes per session. Prediction from H5: players in the upper attitude tertile should show a higher proportion of recorded sessions than the lower tertile, after controlling for coach mandate and device ownership. Veto condition: if the attitude–use association is near zero, reverses, or disappears after coach fixed effects, then the survey’s A→BI path is not a valid proxy for multi-week behavior. This is verifiable because the log is timestamped and independent of self-reported intention.

2. Coach social-influence randomized veto. Randomize teams to a coach endorsement condition versus a neutral information condition for two weeks. Metric: change in attitude score and change in the proportion of players who record at least one training session. Prediction from H3 and the non-significant H7: the endorsement should move attitude and the indirect attitude-mediated pathway more than it moves direct use without attitude change. Veto condition: if the endorsement changes use but not attitude, or changes attitude but not use, the mediation story is misspecified. This test separates social influence on evaluation from social influence on behavior.

3. Sensor-validity veto during football-specific drills. Compare wrist smartwatch heart rate against a criterion ECG or chest strap during steady-state, high-intensity interval, and contact drills. Metrics: mean absolute error in bpm, root-mean-square error in bpm, and limits of agreement in bpm. Prediction: the device may perform acceptably in steady-state but degrade under high-intensity and contact conditions. Veto condition: if error exceeds a pre-registered athletic threshold, or if motion-artifact variance dominates the physiological signal, then the watch cannot be used for the coach-facing decisions described in the paper—regardless of how strong the attitude-to-intention path is. This is a verifiable cross-layer veto because it tests the physical measurement layer directly rather than inferring it from questionnaire acceptance.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
