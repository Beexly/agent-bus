<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision-maker in this source has a specific epistemic structure: at stage \(t\), the current disturbance \(v(t)\) is not observed before \(u(t)\) is chosen, but past disturbances are observable [PAGE 11]. The source’s rolling-horizon interpretation says that an entire optimal trajectory is found, but “only the first period control must be implemented before one allows for a changing environment” [PAGE 17]. This is the “clock” layer: finite horizon \(T\), backward recursion from \(J(T,x(T))\), and re-solving as time advances. The clock matters because the stochastic LP is not a static one-shot object; it encodes a sequence of information sets. The “crisis habit regression” layer is the temptation, under computational pressure, to collapse to \(J_4\): replace every \(v(t)\) by \(\bar v(t)\), solve one deterministic LP, and treat the first-period control as final. That regression is valid only under the common-basis conditions of Corollary 1 and Corollary 2, or under the \(T=1\) if-and-only-if condition of Corollary 3 [PAGE 19-23]. If any realization makes the basis infeasible, the mean-based habit is vetoed [PAGE 20, PAGE 23-24].

The “counter-trap” is the opposite overcorrection: build the full \(J_3\) scenario tree for every branch and every period, ignoring the exponential block growth [PAGE 14]. That counter-trap is intellectually safer but computationally self-defeating when \(T\) and \(k\) are large. The recursive theory of mind required here is to model the future self’s information and feasibility, not just the current LP. The current self must ask: will the future self, after observing future disturbances, be able to continue from the state induced by my current control? If the current control is feasible for all \(v\) in the support and the basis remains optimal, the deterministic shortcut is not a gamble; it is an equivalence. If not, the current self must preserve the stochastic characterization or add a feasibility safeguard. The source’s key sentence is that if one knows “that a given solution will not give infeasible results in the next periods, then one need only solve a deterministic problem” [PAGE 23]. The counter-trap is to mistake a mean-feasible solution for a support-feasible solution. The clock layer also distinguishes \(T=1\) from \(T>1\): the two-stage theorems do not automatically certify a long-horizon policy [PAGE 21-23]. A disciplined recursive solver therefore checks three things in order: support feasibility of the first-period control, common-basis optimality, and the horizon condition. If all three hold, use the deterministic shortcut; if any fails, retain the stochastic program.

If the state vector \(x(t)\) represented a biomechanical state and \(u(t)\) an actuator command, the source’s constraints \(G(t)x(t)+D(t)u(t)=f(t)\) and \(A(t)x(t)+B(t)u(t)+E(t)v(t)=x(t+1)\) would encode the admissible state/control envelope. The physical veto is then exactly the condition that some disturbance realization drives the basic state negative or violates the transition constraints. The source’s own example of infeasibility is explicit: “Different realizations of the stochastic elements may lead to this infeasibility” [PAGE 20], and “Problems, of course, arise if the first period controls do lead to future infeasibilities” [PAGE 23]. If \(v(t)\) represented weather, then weather is an exogenous additive disturbance only insofar as it enters through \(E(t)v(t)\); the source does not model weather affecting \(A(t),B(t),D(t),G(t)\), nor does it model random costs. A cross-layer adjudication must therefore veto any claim that the deterministic mean solution is certified when the physical layer supplies a disturbance outside the support, a nonconvex admissible set, a constraint coefficient that varies with weather, or a multiperiod coupling not covered by the common-basis theorem. The source’s proof obligations are clear: check feasibility for every \(v\), optimality for some \(v\), convexity where Theorem 2 is invoked, and the \(T=1\) restriction where Theorems 4-5 are invoked. Without those checks, physical or weather intuition cannot override the mathematical veto.

If the verifier finds one \(i\) with a negative basic variable, the deterministic shortcut is vetoed and the predicted control is not certified. This scenario is verifiable by direct basis inversion and reduced-cost arithmetic.

Construct a one-period problem with \(V=\{0,1\}\), equal probabilities, and a constraint that is feasible only when the control matches the realized \(v\). The mean is \(0.5\), which is not in \(V\). Verifier solves the mean LP and each support LP, then checks whether the mean-optimal control violates a transition constraint for \(v=0\) or \(v=1\). Prediction: the mean-based control is infeasible for at least one support point, Corollary 3’s “feasible for all \(v(0)\in V(0)\)” condition fails, and the deterministic shortcut is vetoed [PAGE 23-24].
