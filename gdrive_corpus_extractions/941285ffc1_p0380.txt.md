\[

\[

<development>
<p>The retroactive causality theory crystallized when Hull confronted the "omission paradox": animals could respond to the absence of expected stimuli, implying backward-in-time information flow from future states to present actions. To resolve this, he introduced the concept of a hypothetical "planned goal state" that retroactively modulated ongoing sensory processing, thus justifying real-time adjustments based on anticipated outcomes.</p>
</development>
<maturation>
<p>This evolutionary chain shows how precursor works provided fragmented pieces that Hull assembled into a cohesive framework. Subsequent generations built upon this foundation: Miller et al. (1960) formalized goal gradients; Simon (1961) introduced bounded rationality to decision-making; Minsky (1986) described society of mind as concurrent processors; meanwhile, psychology continued refining learning curve shapes and memory retrieval models. The idea of feedback predates engineering by centuries; ancient Greeks understood homeostasis through Hippocratic medicine, yet lacked formal quantification until the industrial revolution introduced steam engines and regulators.</p>
</<maturation>

<description>
We begin by recognizing that Hull's derivation implicitly averages across numerous presentations of the compound. On any single trial, the interaction may fluctuate due to noise sources like variability in decay rates or stochastic transitions between neural states. However, the Law of Large Numbers guarantees that the sample mean converges to the expected value, allowing us to work with deterministic equivalents.

<Stochastic Modeling of Decay Dynamics</step_number>
<description>
The stated decay of 20% corresponds to a reduction in amplitude; however, the underlying physical process is better modeled as exponential decay \(D(t)=e^{-\alpha t}\). The coefficient \(\alpha\) encapsulates absorption losses in air and viscous drag on the tympanic membrane. This introduces a random variable \(T\) representing the duration of the compound, assumed to follow an exponential distribution with mean \(\mu_T\); consequently, the actual power reduction becomes a realization of \(e^{-\alpha T}\).

The interaction term then becomes</p>
\[
k(s_C + s_E) \mapsto k(s_C + s_E)e^{-\alpha T}.
\]
<p>Its expectation is</p>
\[
\mathbb{E}[k(s_C + s_E)e^{-\alpha T}] = k(s_C + s_E) \mathbb{E}[e^{-\alpha T}].
\]
<p>For an exponential distribution, \(\mathbb{E}[e^{-\alpha T}] = \frac{1}{1+\alpha\mu_T}\). Typical values yield a modest adjustment factor near 0.8, consistent with the nominal 20% reduction quoted above, but accounting for trial-to-trial variation.</p>
</description>
<result>
<p>Under random trial lengths, the adjusted interaction strength is</p>
\[
\tilde{k}(s_C + s_E) = \frac{k(s_C + s_E)}{1+\alpha\mu_T},
\]
<p>which is smaller in magnitude than the deterministic counterpart, reflecting the additional uncertainty in timing.</p>
</result>
