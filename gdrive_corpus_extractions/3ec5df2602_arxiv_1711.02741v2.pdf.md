<THINK_TRACE>
The source establishes a multi-hop chain from raw video to tracking decisions. The first hop is the “tracking-by-detection” premise: objects are detected per frame and then linked across frames; the paper states, “The main challenge of online multi-object tracking is to reliably associate object trajectories with detections in each video frame based on their tracking history” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is representational: each trajectory carries an external memory of recent inputs and an internal recurrent memory. The paper says, “The RAN couples an external memory and an internal memory” and “The external memory explicitly stores previous inputs of each trajectory in a time window, while the internal memory learns to summarize long-term tracking history and associate detections by processing the external memory” [PAGE 1]. The third hop is generative: an autoregressive model predicts the next feature vector as a weighted sum of the previous \(K\) vectors plus Gaussian noise, with time-varying coefficients and variances. The paper says, “we utilize the autoregressive (AR) model which models the upcoming input xt as a weighted sum of the previous K input vectors plus a Gaussian noise” [PAGE 3]. The fourth hop is recurrent parameter estimation: a GRU hidden state produces the AR coefficients and standard deviations, so the model can change its estimation strategy as the sequence changes. The paper notes, “in our RAN model, we allow both variables to change over time” [PAGE 3]. The fifth hop is association: for each object and detection, the score is the product of an appearance likelihood and a motion likelihood under an independence assumption; the paper states, “we assume the independence of bounding boxes and appearance features” [PAGE 4]. The sixth hop is decision: bipartite matching selects associations above a threshold, initializes new trajectories from unassociated detections, and terminates trajectories lost for more than 20 steps. The seventh hop is memory update: associated detections update both memories; when lost, motion memory is updated with the predicted motion while appearance memory remains unchanged [PAGE 4]. The eighth hop is training: sampled detections with IOU greater than 0.5 with ground truth are used to maximize conditional likelihood, with a negative log-likelihood loss [PAGE 5].

A gray-zone audit shows several non-identical failure surfaces. First, the product of appearance and motion densities in Eq. (12) is not automatically a calibrated joint probability when the two modalities have different dimensions and different noise scales; it is a ranking score. Second, the diagonal Gaussian covariance assumes independent feature dimensions, which may be false for CNN embeddings. Third, the softmax on AR coefficients forces a convex combination of previous templates; this is stable but cannot extrapolate beyond the observed template span. Fourth, the exponential variance parameterization prevents negative standard deviations but can underflow to zero, producing a Gaussian singularity, or overflow, producing a flat likelihood. Fifth, the candidate-pair pruning by distance to the last box can veto a correct association if motion is large or the detector jumps. Sixth, the lost-target rule updates motion memory with the model’s own prediction, which can create self-confirming drift over the 20-step termination horizon. Seventh, the threshold is chosen by “an automatic random search” [PAGE 6], so threshold calibration is part of the model-selection surface. Eighth, the benchmark tables separate DPM and Faster-RCNN detections [PAGE 6], so comparisons that mix detector regimes are not apples-to-apples. The paper’s own ablation shows that the external-memory span improves MOTA and IDS until “converging after 9 steps” [PAGE 7], while longer spans are “harder to train since more GPU memory will be required” [PAGE 7]. Thus the RAN is a conditional generative tracker whose main strengths are memory and likelihood-based association, and whose main breakdowns are covariance degeneracy, independence mis-specification, lost-target drift, candidate pruning, and detector-induced feature noise.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The RAN operates on feature sequences \(x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N}\), with time index \(t\) in video frames. The paper does not assign SI units to the feature space. Appearance features are the fc8 layer of an Inception network and are “256-dimensional” [PAGE 5]; motion features are “a 4-dimensional vector” [PAGE 5]. Thus \(N_\phi=256\) and \(N_b=4\). The internal memories are “128-dimensional internal memories for appearance and 32-dimensional internal memories for motion” [PAGE 5]. The external-memory time span is \(K=10\): “The time spans of the external memories are both 10” [PAGE 5]. Appearance feature extraction “takes 0.45 second in average for each frame during test time” [PAGE 5]. Feature vectors are dimensionless CNN activations; motion coordinates are bounding-box coordinates in image or relative units, but the paper does not specify normalization. A continuous Gaussian density over \(\mathbb{R}^{N}\) has units of inverse feature-volume; when features are normalized dimensionless activations, the density is dimensionless per \(N\)-dimensional volume. The negative log-likelihood is additive in nats if natural logarithms are used; the paper writes \(\log\) without specifying base.

The core AR operator is
\[
x_t=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_k^t x_{t-k}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad
\varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_t),
\]
where \(\alpha^t=(\alpha_1^t,\ldots,\alpha_K^t)\) and \(\Sigma_t=\operatorname{diag}((\sigma_1^t)^2,\ldots,(\sigma_N^t)^2)\). The conditional model is
\[
p(x_t\mid x_{1:t-1})=\mathcal{N}(x_t\mid \mu_t,\Sigma_t),
\]
with
\[
\mu_t=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_k^t x_{t-k},\qquad
\Sigma_t=\operatorname{diag}((\sigma^t)^2).
\]
The paper says, “we allow both variables to change over time” [PAGE 3]. The parameters are produced by a GRU hidden state \(h_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\):
\[
(\hat\alpha^t,\hat\sigma^t)=f(h_{t-1}),
\]
\[
\alpha_k^t=\frac{\exp(\hat\alpha_k^t)}{\sum_{k'=1}^{K}\exp(\hat\alpha_{k'}^t)},\qquad
\sigma_j^t=\exp(\hat\sigma_j^t).
\]
The softmax “guarantee[s] that the parameters \(\alpha^t_k\) ... sum to one,” and the exponential “guarantees that the standard deviations are positive” [PAGE 3]. The GRU recurrence is
\[
z_t=g(W_zx_t+U_zh_{t-1}),\quad
r_t=g(W_rx_t+U_rh_{t-1}),
\]
\[
\tilde h_t=\tanh(Wx_t+U(r_t\odot h_{t-1})),\quad
h_t=(1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1}+z_t\odot\tilde h_t,
\]
where \(g\) is the logistic sigmoid and \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication [PAGE 4]. The hidden state is dimensionless internal memory; \(W_z,W_r,W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}\) and \(U_z,U_r,U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).

For data association, let detection \(i\) have appearance feature \(\phi_i^t\) and motion feature \(b_i^t\), and object \(l\) have history \(\phi_l^{1:t-1},b_l^{1:t-1}\). The score is
\[
s_{i|l}^t=p(\phi_i^t,b_i^t\mid \phi_l^{1:t-1},b_l^{1:t-1})
=p(\phi_i^t\mid \phi_l^{1:t-1})\,p(b_i^t\mid b_l^{1:t-1}),
\]
\[
s_{i|l}^t=\mathcal{N}(\phi^t\mid \mu_\phi^t,\Sigma_\phi^t)\,
\mathcal{N}(b^t\mid \mu_b^t,\Sigma_b^t).
\]
This factorization uses the paper’s explicit assumption: “we assume the independence of bounding boxes and appearance features” [PAGE 4]. Object \(l\) is associated to the detection with maximum score if that score exceeds a threshold; otherwise it is marked lost [PAGE 4]. The training loss is
\[
L=-\sum_l\sum_t \log p(\phi_l^{t+1},b_l^{t+1}\mid \phi_l^{1:t},b_l^{1:t}),
\]
which is a negative log-likelihood in nats under natural log [PAGE 5]. The paper trains with sampled boxes whose IOU with ground truth is larger than 0.5, “skip[s] the time steps when the ground truth object is invisible or lost,” and uses Adam with learning rate \(1\times10^{-3}\), \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.99\) [PAGE 5].

The exact singularity and breakdown regimes are these. If \(\sigma_j^t\to0^+\), the Gaussian density scales as \(1/\sigma_j^t\) in that dimension and diverges; if several dimensions collapse, the association score becomes numerically unbounded or non-finite. If \(\sigma_j^t\to\infty\), the density flattens and discriminative power vanishes. If the softmax saturates so that one \(\alpha_k^t\to1\), the predictor collapses to a single template and loses the averaging benefit under occlusion. If \(\alpha_k^t\) is nearly uniform, the predictor behaves like an average and lags fast motion. If the exponential argument \(\hat\sigma_j^t\) is very negative, underflow to zero triggers the singularity; if very positive, overflow or flat likelihood occurs. The product of two Gaussian densities in Eq. (12) can underflow in high dimension, so log-domain computation is required. The independence assumption is a gray-zone breakdown when appearance and motion residuals are correlated, because the product double-counts evidence and becomes overconfident. The candidate-pair pruning “only consider[s] detection bounding boxes within a distance to the last detected bounding box” [PAGE 4], so a large jump can veto the correct pair before scoring. The lost-target rule updates motion memory with the predicted mean while appearance memory remains unchanged [PAGE 4]; over repeated losses this can create self-confirming motion drift. The termination horizon is “more than tterminate = 20 time steps” [PAGE 4], so occlusions longer than the clock force termination. Finally, the threshold is found by “an automatic random search” [PAGE 6], and MOTA and IDS are the metrics that “most directly depict the quality of tracking and association” [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The RAN’s lineage is retrocausally legible as a synthesis of three older programs: autoregressive time-series modeling, recurrent memory, and tracking-by-detection. The paper explicitly places the autoregressive component in the NADE/DARN line: “The idea of introducing autoregressive structure into deep generative models first appeared in NADE [15] and DARN [9]” [PAGE 2]. It also places the architecture near memory networks: “Our RAN model can be seen as a variant of memory networks [30, 8, 36]” [PAGE 2]. The recurrent parameter estimator is a GRU [5], and the tracking framework inherits bipartite matching and online MOT practice from prior work [34, 6]. The paper’s contribution is therefore not a new detector or a new matching algorithm; it is a conditional generative memory that produces likelihoods for association. The paper states its scope: “we focus on learning a good feature representation of objects with RANs, which is used in a subsequent data association algorithm for online multi-object tracking” [PAGE 2].

The historical trap structure is multi-week because each layer has a plausible but incomplete replication path. A first trap is to treat MOTA alone as the target. The paper warns that MOTA and IDS are the two metrics that “most directly depict the quality of tracking and association” [PAGE 6]. A second trap is to mix detector regimes. The paper reports DPM detections and Faster-RCNN detections separately [PAGE 6], and it uses Faster-RCNN for validation analysis [PAGE 6]. A third trap is to skip the validation split discipline: the paper splits the 2DMOT2015 training set into “5 training videos and 6 validation videos as suggested by [34]” [PAGE 6]. A fourth trap is to ignore threshold search: “The association threshold for each trained model is chosen by an automatic random search” [PAGE 6]. A fifth trap is to extend the external-memory span without accounting for training cost; the paper reports that MOTA and IDS improve and then converge “after 9 steps” [PAGE 7], and that longer spans are “harder to train since more GPU memory will be required during training” [PAGE 7]. A sixth trap is to assume runtime scales with the memory span; the paper says “at test time the majority time is spent on forwarding the CNN, while the time span has minor influence on the runtime” [PAGE 7]. A seventh trap is to overlook the small-data overfitting risk; the paper motivates external memory partly because MOT datasets are “relatively small” and recurrent networks can overfit [PAGE 1]. These are not merely implementation notes; they are the genealogical constraints that determine whether a RAN reproduction is actually testing the paper’s claim. The paper’s benchmark success on “highly crowded and occluded scenes” [PAGE 1] is therefore retroactively explained by the combination of external templates, internal recurrent summarization, and likelihood-based association, not by any single component alone.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In the RAN, the tracker’s “mind” is the pair of memories \(B_t=(h_t,E_t)\), where \(E_t=\{x_{t-1},\ldots,x_{t-K}\}\) is the external template window and \(h_t\) is the GRU hidden state. This is not a full recursive theory of mind over other agents; it is a first-order belief state over the target’s next feature. The Gaussian variance \(\Sigma_t\) is the model’s self-estimate of uncertainty, and the AR weights \(\alpha_t\) are its self-estimate of which past observations matter. The qualitative analysis shows crisis habit regression: when occlusion is imminent and “the chosen detection box becomes noisy, the updated parameters lean more towards memorized features in previous time steps in the external memory” [PAGE 8]. When the object reappears, “the RAN chose to use a protective estimation strategy which is closer to averaging features in the external memory” [PAGE 8]. Later, “the updated parameters choose to trust more on the most recent frame when the appearance became more stable” [PAGE 8]. This is a habit-like regression to older templates under crisis: the model does not invent a new appearance model; it retreats to the averaging regime encoded by the softmax-weighted external memory.

The counter-trap is the lost-target motion recursion. The paper says, “When the target is lost, the ht b and Et b are updated with the predicted motion µt b, while the appearance counterparts remain the same” [PAGE 4]. In belief-state terms, the tracker updates its motion memory with its own prediction, not with an observation. That is a self-confirming loop: if the prediction is biased, the next prediction is conditioned on the biased memory, and the error can accumulate. Appearance memory is frozen, so re-identification after a long loss depends on stale templates. The 20-step termination rule is the clock that bounds this loop: “we terminate a trajectory when it has been lost for more than tterminate = 20 time steps” [PAGE 4]. The external-memory clock is \(K=10\): “The time spans of the external memories are both 10” [PAGE 5]. Thus the RAN’s recursive self-model has two clocks: the sliding window that decides how far back the AR operator can look, and the termination horizon that decides how long a lost belief can persist. A crisis longer than the window pushes the model toward averaging; a crisis longer than the termination horizon kills the trajectory. The theory-of-mind failure mode is not that the tracker lacks a hidden state, but that its hidden state can become a self-fulfilling prophecy when observations stop. The model has no explicit second-order belief about detector reliability, no explicit “I might be wrong because the detector is occluded” variable, and no counterfactual branching over future associations. It has only the Gaussian variance and the threshold. That is why the qualitative examples are important: they show the intended behavior is protective averaging under occlusion, but the same mechanism can become conservative or stale when the occlusion outlasts the memory window.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical, biomechanical, or weather veto cannot be directly adjudicated by this paper because the model’s state space contains only image-derived detections, appearance features, motion box features, and recurrent memories. The paper’s domain is “online multi-object tracking” from “video frames” and “detections” [PAGE 1]; it does not define a biomechanical state such as joint torque, ground reaction force, or gait phase, and it does not define weather variables such as rain rate, wind speed, or illumination spectra. Therefore a physical veto is undefined unless it is mediated by an explicit observation model. Formally, let the RAN likelihood be \(p(\phi,b\mid \text{history})\). A weather or biomechanical variable \(w\) can affect the tracker only through the detections and features, i.e. through \(p(\phi,b\mid \text{history},w)\). If \(w\) is not in the model and not measured, the claim \(w\) vetoes the RAN is not a valid cross-layer inference; it is an exogenous distribution shift. The only legitimate veto is empirical: if \(w\) changes the detector output or feature distribution, it will appear as false positives, false negatives, identity switches, or fragmentation. The paper’s evaluation already exposes those channels: it reports FP, FN, IDS, and Frag, and it states that “MOTA and IDS are the two metrics that most directly depict the quality of tracking and association” [PAGE 6]. A weather condition that degrades detection would therefore be adjudicated by a drop in those metrics, not by a first-principles physical contradiction of Eqs. (1)–(14). Likewise, biomechanical plausibility of pedestrian motion is not a constraint in the model: the motion feature is only a 4-dimensional bounding-box vector [PAGE 5], not a biomechanical trajectory. The paper’s own robustness claim is limited to “highly crowded and occluded scenes” [PAGE 1], not to weather or biomechanics. Thus the cross-layer veto proof is a scope proof: the RAN equations are functions of \(\phi\), \(b\), \(h\), and \(E\); any physical or weather veto must first be translated into a change in those variables. Absent that translation, the veto is invalid. If the translation is supplied, the veto is testable only as a distribution-shift experiment with the same metrics and the same detector regime; otherwise it conflates detector failure, feature shift, and association error.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
