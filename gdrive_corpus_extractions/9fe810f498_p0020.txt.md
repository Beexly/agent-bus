<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: under competitive pressure or fatigue, athletes and coaches tend to regress to previously successful motor schemas. In the Camp corpus, the explicit schema is “using the arms” to make an opening for a runner under the old rules (plate 137) [PAGE 20]. If a 1900 offensive line finds itself stalled near the goal line, the culturally available regression is to revert to arm-blocking techniques that were legal in the prior epoch but may be penalized under the current ruleset.

Counter-trap: a defensive coordinator who models the offense’s regression can set a counter-trap. By anticipating the illegal arm extension, the defense can draw the penalty (or, in the 1900 context, exploit the illegal block to force a turnover or loss of yardage). The offense’s attempt to solve a short-yardage crisis thus becomes the very mechanism of its own failure. This is first-order theory of mind: the defense models the offense’s habit.

Recursive depth: the offense, knowing the defense expects the old-rule arm block, may suppress the regression and instead use a legal shoulder block. The defense, anticipating that suppression, may over-pursue the ball carrier, creating a cutback lane. The offense then models that over-pursuit and inserts a delayed handoff. Each layer is a counter-model of the opponent’s model of the crisis habit.

Clock: the game clock provides the hard boundary that truncates this recursion. In 1900 football, clock management differed from modern codes (no play clock, different stoppage rules), but the finite duration of the contest means the infinite regress must resolve before the final whistle. The clock thus acts as an external veto on recursive theory of mind: at some point, a play must be snapped, and the unmodeled residual—weather, fatigue, officiating variance—determines the outcome. The Camp plates are static; they contain no clock variable, no down-and-distance state, and no time-remaining parameter. Therefore they cannot adjudicate which recursive level is optimal under time pressure.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: pole vault.  
The two plates specify \(h = 3.4798\,\text{m}\) and \(h = 3.2004\,\text{m}\) [PAGE 20]. A physical veto occurs if ambient temperature \(T\) alters the pole’s Young’s modulus \(E(T)\). Bamboo exhibits temperature-dependent stiffness; in cold conditions, \(E\) drops, reducing stored elastic energy \(E_{\text{pole}} = \frac{1}{2} k x^2\). If \(E_{\text{pole}}\) falls below the threshold required to elevate the vaulter’s center of mass to \(3.4798\,\text{m}\), the bar is dislodged. Veto condition: \(m g \, \Delta h > E_{\text{k,approach}} + E_{\text{pole}}(T) - E_{\text{loss}}\).

Biomechanical layer veto: shot put and blocking.  
The shot-put plates show “balancing,” “putting,” and a “left view” at pages 30–31 [PAGE 20]. A biomechanical veto is triggered if the thrower’s center of mass projects outside the base of support during the putting phase, inducing a rotational foul or balance loss. The formal condition for static stability is that the vertical projection of the COM lies within the convex hull of the foot contact patch: \(\vec{r}_{\text{COM}} \in \text{Conv}(\{\vec{r}_{\text{foot},i}\})\). The left-view plate suggests sagittal-plane analysis, but it provides no force-plate data, so the stability margin cannot be quantified from [PAGE 20].  
For football, the fair tackle (page 95) and tackle of a runner (page 129) [PAGE 20] imply contact mechanics governed by \(\mu_{\text{cleat-turf}}\). On natural grass (the surface implied by a 1900 field diagram at page 103 [PAGE 20]), rain reduces \(\mu\) below the threshold required for a secure wrap-up tackle: \(F_{\text{friction}} = \mu N < F_{\text{shear,required}}\).

Weather layer veto: field and sprint.  
The 100-yard dash start (page 21) [PAGE 20] assumes a dry, level track. Wet cinder or grass alters the coefficient of static friction at the starting position, changing the maximal horizontal impulse \(\int F_x\,dt\). The bicycle plate (page 33) [PAGE 20] is similarly weather-gated: pneumatic tires on a muddy track increase rolling resistance \(F_{\text{rr}} = C_{\text{rr}} N\), where \(C_{\text{rr}}\) is surface-dependent.

Veto proof: Because all plates are posed, monoscopic, and rule-epoch locked, they encode no dynamic weather variable, no opponent adaptive response, and no fatigue accumulation across four quarters. Therefore the illustrations cannot serve as unqualified predictors of live-game outcomes; they are boundary-condition diagrams valid only within their narrow physical, biomechanical, and historical regimes.
