<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Recursive theory of mind in this context is three-leveled. Level 0: the rat forms an expectancy about the maze, \( P(\tau \mid \sigma, h) \), treating the environment as an object with means-end structure. Level 1: the experimenter models the rat’s expectancy, designing probe trials (blocked paths, reversed goals) to infer whether \( C > 0 \). Level 2: the rat may model the experimenter as an intentional system that alters contingencies; if the rat expects the experimenter to change the maze, its sign-gestalt-readiness becomes conditional on an inferred experimenter policy \( \pi_E \), yielding \( P(\tau \mid \sigma, h, \pi_E) \). This recursion can regress indefinitely unless anchored by an operational criterion: immediate, above-chance path selection on the first trial after an unannounced contingency change.

Crisis habit regression. Under conditions of high error rate, unexpected non-reward, or time pressure, the animal may abandon the means-end field and revert to a lower-level Thorndikian S-R habit—effectively setting \( C \rightarrow 0 \) behaviorally even if the representation persists latently. This regression is not random; it is biased toward the most recently reinforced path, producing perseveration errors on reversal probes.

Counter-trap. The experimenter can set a counter-trap by anticipating regression: after the rat reverts to a habitual path, the experimenter blocks that path and introduces a novel shortcut that is only discoverable by means-end reasoning, not by habit. If the rat continues to attempt the blocked habitual path beyond an extinction threshold (e.g., > 10 consecutive errors), the counter-trap has closed: neither the old habit nor the means-end field is guiding behavior, suggesting a collapse into fixed action patterns or stress-induced rigidity.

Clock. The external clock—inter-trial interval, session length, light-dark cycle, feeding schedule—modulates all three layers. Short inter-trial intervals promote habit dominance (proceduralization); long intervals allow cognitive expectancy to guide choice but increase susceptibility to distraction and satiety. A “clock trap” occurs when performance is measured at a single circadian phase and generalized across the day; means-end-capacity is non-stationary with respect to time-of-day, hunger state, and prior trial history. The recursive model is broken only by varying the clock independently and showing that \( C \), measured in bits, remains invariant across phase shifts.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

A cognitive claim of means-end-capacity is valid only if every lower-layer veto is falsified or controlled. The physical layer includes maze geometry: wall height relative to rat center-of-mass, corridor width, floor texture, and lighting intensity in lux. If wall height is below the rat’s rearing threshold (~20–30 cm for a laboratory rat), the animal can climb or jump over walls, transforming a supposed detour into a direct traversal and invalidating the graph \( \mathcal{F} \). If olfactory cues from prior subjects linger, the sign stimulus \( \sigma_t \) is contaminated by exogenous scent trails, producing path selection that reflects chemotaxis rather than sign-gestalt expectancy. Lighting gradients can create preferential illumination zones that bias path choice independently of goal representation.

The biomechanical layer includes the rat’s vibrissa (whisker) sensory field, limb morphology, turning radius, and vestibular constraints. A “reverse end of one and the same detour” on [PAGE 180] presupposes bidirectional traversability; if the rat’s whisker field is asymmetrically lesioned or if one limb is impaired, the animal may fail to traverse a path not because it lacks expectancy \( P(\tau \mid \sigma) \), but because the motor plant cannot execute \( \tau \). Turning radius constraints impose a minimum feasible curvature \( \kappa_{\min} \) in m\(^{-1}\); graph edges with curvature \( \kappa > \kappa_{\min} \) are topologically present but biomechanically null. Such edges must be pruned from \( \mathcal{E} \) before computing \( C \), or the mutual information calculation is inflated by unrealizable paths.

The weather veto operates at the facility level. HVAC failure producing temperature shifts outside the rat’s thermoneutral zone (~26–30 °C) alters metabolic motivation \( w_g \) and can suppress exploration entirely. Humidity changes affect odor plume dispersion and thus the reliability of olfactory sign gestalts. Barometric pressure shifts, though subtle, can modify airborne cue transport in large maze rooms. Seasonal photoperiod changes shift circadian phase and thereby the “clock” discussed above. If any of these physical-layer variables co-vary with the experimental manipulation, the inference from behavior to means-end-capacity is vetoed.

Adjudication rule. Accept the cognitive interpretation only if: (1) maze geometry is verified against rat anthropometrics (no climbing shortcuts, all corridors passable); (2) olfactory and visual cues are randomized or counterbalanced across trials so that no exogenous sign predicts the goal; (3) biomechanical constraints are measured (turning radius, limb integrity) and graph edges are pruned accordingly; (4) facility temperature, humidity, and lighting are logged and shown not to correlate with path-selection latency or error rate. Vetoes are binary: one uncontrolled physical variable invalidates the \( C \)-estimate for that session.
