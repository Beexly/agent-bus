<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The mathematical lineage begins with Shapley’s 1951 formalization of transferable utility coalitional games [2], later axiomatized by Aumann and Shapley [2]. Myerson extended this to graph-constrained games in “Graphs and cooperation in games” (1977) [19] and “Conference structures and fair allocation rules” (1980) [22], proving the unique fair allocation rule on graphs coincides with Shapley values applied to the graph-restricted game. The modern XAI branch arrives via SHAP [6], Lundberg and Lee’s 2017 additive feature-attribution framework, which shows Shapley values are the unique per-feature linear coefficients satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency. The direct multi-agent RL antecedent is Heuillet et al. 2022 [7], which estimates agent policy importance via Monte-Carlo Shapley values in cooperative MARL but does not isolate individual attributes. Angelottia and Díaz-Rodríguez extend [7] by placing policies and attributes on equal footing as players in the coalitional game, generalizing the basketball-player analysis of Metulini and Gnecco [3], where attributes entered the characteristic function rather than serving as standalone players.

A multi-week trap emerges in the RL training curriculum described on page 10: the A2C model is trained in three sequential phases against enemies deploying No-Op, then Random, then Smart policies. This is a temporal curriculum trap: performance in Phase 1 (against a non-acting adversary) does not generalize to Phase 3, because the credit landscape shifts as the opponent’s policy becomes more sophisticated. The post-hoc Myerson/Shapley analysis captures the final deployed policy’s attribute dependencies, but it cannot unroll the counterfactual of what would have happened had training stopped after week one or week two. A second trap is the explanatory–causal conflation warned against in Remark 6 (page 8): a high Shapley value for MaxHealthPoints does not imply that increasing MaxHealthPoints will improve team performance; it only indicates that removing the attribute collapses coalition worth. A third trap is HKG misplacement (Remark 5, page 8): over-promoting a feature in the hierarchy falsely zeroes coalitions lacking it, while under-promoting merely sacrifices computational efficiency. The paper’s Arena results (Table 2) show MaxHealthPoints consistently dominant (values near 30–37), which is historically consistent with survival being the bottleneck in attrition games, but the temporal training trap means these values describe the end-state black box, not the learning trajectory that produced it.

The Arena game embeds recursive theory-of-mind structure through its handcrafted policies. On page 10, the Smart policy makes the Warrior and Mage target enemies by priority: 1) Priest, 2) Mage, 3) Warrior, while the Priest heals the living teammate with the lowest CurrentHealthPoints. Each agent must therefore model the enemy’s targeting function, which itself models the agent’s healing function. The Mage’s control action (Equation 12) removes an enemy’s next turn, which changes the enemy Priest’s healing opportunities, which changes the Warrior’s damage throughput, which changes the Mage’s own survival probability—a second-order belief loop.

The A2C RL policy exhibits deeper recursion. Page 10 states the A2C Warrior and Mage learn to attack/control the enemy Warrior first; upon the Warrior’s death, the Mage switches to controlling the enemy Priest while the Warrior attacks indiscriminately among remaining enemies. The Priest heals whichever teammate is taking damage. This is a counter-trap hierarchy: the opponent’s Mage attempts to control one’s Warrior (a first-order trap); if successful, one’s Mage re-targets the opponent’s Priest (a counter-trap); if the opponent’s Warrior dies, the recursion collapses to attrition between the two Priests and remaining damage dealers.

Crisis habit regression appears in the Priest’s fixed response: whenever a teammate takes damage, the Priest heals the lowest-health teammate. Under pressure, this habit does not adapt to whether the damage is survivable or whether the enemy Mage is about to cast control; it is a rigid regression to a precomputed healing rule. The clock is the match horizon \(T=1000\) rounds from page 9. Because the score \(S(\omega)=100\,r(\omega)/(T(\omega)+1)\) decays hyperbolically with duration, every agent faces an implicit deadline: the longer the match, the closer \(S\) approaches 100 regardless of outcome, eroding the marginal value of delayed victory. This clock converts the recursive targeting loop into a race condition: the counter-trap must resolve before \(T\) truncates the credit signal. No probabilistic odds or betting lines are involved; the only stochastic elements are action targeting uniformity under Random policy and ControlChance Bernoulli draws.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The Arena simulator contains no weather variable and no biomechanical layer beyond discrete health-point arithmetic; therefore no meteorological veto is applicable, and any claim of weather-dependent veto would be external to the source. The veto structure is instead defined by hard game-state constraints across three layers:

Physical layer (agent existence): Definition 5 and Equation 8 (page 7) make MaxHealthPoints a Static Necessary Attribute. If MaxHealthPoints \(=0\), the agent is dead and cannot act; by the replacement rule, setting this attribute to zero in a coalition is equivalent to removing the entire agent. This is a physical veto: all downstream attributes (AttackPower, HealingPower, ControlChance) and the agent policy are nullified because \(v(K\cup\{i\})=v(K)\) for the dead agent’s elements.

Action layer (biomechanical analogue): Equation 11 (page 10) bounds Warrior damage by AttackPower; if AttackPower \(=0\), \(\text{tarHP}_{t+1}=\text{tarHP}_t\), vetoing the Warrior’s offensive contribution. Equation 12 shows Mage control probability \(P\) scales linearly with ControlChance; if ControlChance \(=0\), \(P=0\) and the Mage cannot suppress enemy actions. Equation 13 bounds Priest healing by HealingPower and target MaxHealthPoints; if HealingPower \(=0\), no health transfer occurs.

Graph layer: Equation 7 (page 6) imposes coalition decomposition. If a coalition \(K\) omits a high-level necessary attribute that disconnects the HKG, \(v(K)\) decomposes into independent connected components \(\sigma\), and components containing a dead agent evaluate to zero.
