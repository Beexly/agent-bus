Francis Galton’s 1889 book *Natural Inheritance* was part of his campaign to establish heredity as a quantitative science, culminating in his discovery of regression toward the mean (first published in 1886 on sweet peas, then extended to human stature in 1887). The need for a male–female height correction factor arose because he often combined male and female data into a single analysis of hereditary transmission, treating sex as a nuisance variable rather than a biological variable of interest. The factor 1.08 was likely derived from earlier anthropometric surveys, possibly those of Karl Pearson or his own earlier work.

The lineage of such correction factors can be traced back to the French school of anthropology (Quatrefages, Topinard) and the British Army’s medical records, which showed a consistent average difference of about 5–6 inches between men and women. Galton’s insistence on using 1.08 specifically reflects his commitment to data-driven calibration rather than adopting a rounded number.

The mention of “R.F.F.” data likely refers to the “Royal Family of England” or some other pedigree dataset—Galton frequently used genealogical records of aristocratic families to study inheritance. The imprecision of these data (estimates, shoe issues) contrasts sharply with the “Special observations” which were probably taken under controlled conditions by trained observers, perhaps at the Anthropometric Laboratory he established at the Health Exhibition in 1884.

Historically, this page exemplifies a multi-week trap that many early statisticians fell into: the assumption that a simple linear correction can account for complex biological differences. Later research showed that male–female height ratios vary with nutrition, health, and socioeconomic status, so a constant factor may introduce bias if the populations are not homogeneous. From a retrocausal perspective, one could argue that Galton’s choice of 1.08 influenced subsequent anthropometric norms and even public perceptions of gender differences.
