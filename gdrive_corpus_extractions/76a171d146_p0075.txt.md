The lineage of page 66 runs through several historical strata. Hull’s neobehaviorism descends from Watson’s methodological behaviorism and Pavlovian classical conditioning, but diverges by treating drive D as an intervening variable rather than disposing of internal states altogether. The sUR notation evokes Sherrington’s reflex arc and the receptor–effector schema of early twentieth-century physiology, while the “hierarchy of responses” anticipates Lashley’s motor-equivalence and later cybernetic hierarchies. The two-equation demand for drive echoes the psychophysical tradition of Fechner and Weber (stimulus– sensation laws) but transposed into a motivational domain, and it prefigures the distinction Hull’s student Spence later made between drive (motivation) and habit (learning) in the Hull-Spence hypothesis.

A distinct retro-causal feature is Hull’s explicit warning against adaptive just-so reasoning: adaptation may suggest postulates but cannot validate them. This is a methodological reaction against functionalist and evolutionary psychology that would explain behavior by its survival value alone. The “multi-week trap” here is temporal and theoretical: a researcher operating over weeks may observe that food-deprived rats press levers more vigorously as deprivation accumulates, compress the entire relationship into one curve V_obs(t), and then reify “drive” as the latent cause without ever factoring it into f(t) and g(D, H). Over multi-week studies, this trap hardens because deprivation history confounds drive with habit strength H (lever pressing itself builds habit), with sensory-specific satiety, and with circadian rhythms. If H is not held constant or independently measured, the composite V_obs(t) is a moving target, and any fitted “drive” parameter absorbs all uncontrolled variance.

Another trap is cross-modal extrapolation. Hull notes the single empirical equation is easy to find for “food privation or the strength of an electric shock” [PAGE 66], but he assumes motivation D “as such” is commensurable across origins. Historically, this assumption was tested and often failed: hunger drive and shock-induced fear drive engage different neural and endocrine systems, and a common motivational unit may not exist without severe idealization. The multi-week trap is to run deprivation and shock experiments in parallel, fit one curve to each, declare the latent variable “drive” identical because both curves look similar, and then build a unified theory on a commensurability that was never demonstrated. Page 66’s postponement of the unit definition to p. 226 ff. marks this as a known open problem rather than a settled foundation.

The genealogy extends forward to reinforcement learning, where drive D maps loosely to reward prediction error or incentive salience, and habit H maps to cached model-free values. However, Hull’s requirement for two explicit equations is stricter than most modern model-free RL, which collapses motivation and value into a single scalar. Thus page 66 represents a historical fork: systematic behavior theory demanded explanatory decomposition, whereas later computational models often accepted empirical compression.

Physical layer veto. Postulate 3 requires an intact receptor–effector connection sUR as the conduit from combined S and D to response hierarchy [PAGE 66]. A physical veto occurs if the afferent receptor, the interneuronal pathway, or the efferent effector is structurally compromised: sUR = 0. In that regime, no amount of drive D or stimulation S can evoke the hierarchy; the postulate’s prediction of need termination is nullified by hardware failure. This is a hard veto independent of motivational state.

Biomechanical layer veto. Even with sUR = 1, S > 0, and D > 0, response vigor V = g(D, H) is bounded by biomechanical limits: muscle force-velocity relations, metabolic energy availability, and fatigue accumulation. If the required response to terminate need N exceeds maximum sustainable force or power output, the hierarchy cannot terminate the need despite satisfying Postulate 3’s probabilistic condition. The postulate as written on page 66 contains no biomechanical ceiling, so it overpredicts success in high-load need-termination tasks (e.g., a starving organism too weak to press a lever). The veto condition is V_required > V_max(D, H, biomechanical state).

Weather/environmental layer veto. “Combined stimulation (S)” [PAGE 66] assumes the stimulus is effectively transduced. Ambient conditions—temperature, humidity, background noise, lighting—can attenuate or mask S at the receptor surface, effectively reducing the delivered stimulation below the activation threshold of sUR. In extreme weather (e.g., hypothermic conditions), metabolic drive D may be reallocated to thermoregulation rather than the target need, so the same D no longer maps to the same response hierarchy. The veto is environmental: S_effective = η(weather) · S, with η → 0 under masking or noxious conditions, collapsing the left side of Postulate 3’s inequality regardless of nominal D.

Adjudication: These veto layers are not mentioned on page 66, but they define the exact breakdown regimes where Postulate 3 fails to hold as a universal molar principle. Hull’s own requirement that postulates yield deductions matching detailed phenomena [PAGE 66] forces the inclusion of such boundary conditions; otherwise the postulate stands on a priori adaptive plausibility alone, which Hull explicitly rejects. A veto-proof version of Postulate 3 would therefore be conditional: given intact sUR, S above transduction threshold, D above zero, biomechanical capacity sufficient, and environmental modulation within tolerable bounds, the evoked hierarchy is more likely to terminate need than random selection. Outside this domain, the postulate is silent or falsified.
