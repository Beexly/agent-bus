The source defines a supervised regression problem with data cases \(i=1,\dots,N\), feature vectors \(x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\), and targets \(y_i\in\mathbb{R}\). The target unit is a league-defined fantasy point, denoted \(\mathrm{fp}\). The paper does not use SI units for the target; the audit therefore labels fantasy points as \(\mathrm{fp}\) and treats features as either dimensionless after normalization or as counts and years where stated.

Support vector regression is introduced on [PAGE 2] as a linear regression model with an epsilon-insensitive loss. The source prints:
\[
f_{\mathrm{SVR}}(x)=\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}w_d x_d\right)+b=xw+b
\]
and
\[
w^*,b^*=\arg\min_{w,b}\frac{C}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V_\epsilon\left(y_i-(x_iw+b)\right)+\|w\|_2^2
\]
with
\[
V_\epsilon(r)=
\begin{cases}
0, & |r|\le \epsilon,\\
|r|-\epsilon, & |r|>\epsilon.
\end{cases}
\]
Units: \(y_i\) and \(\epsilon\) are in \(\mathrm{fp}\); \(V_\epsilon\) is in \(\mathrm{fp}\); \(C\) is dimensionless and is chosen from \((0,1]\) [PAGE 2]. If \(x\) is normalized to \([0,1]\), then \(w\) carries \(\mathrm{fp}\) units and \(\|w\|_2^2\) carries \(\mathrm{fp}^2\), while the data term carries \(\mathrm{fp}\). The printed objective is therefore dimensionally inconsistent unless \(y\) is scaled to dimensionless or a scale factor such as \(1/\sigma_y^2\) multiplies the norm term. Breakdown regimes: \(C\to0\) suppresses the data term; \(\epsilon\) larger than all residuals makes the loss zero and the fit underdetermined; an unnormalized radial-basis kernel makes \(\gamma\) scale-dependent; a singular kernel matrix can make the dual solution non-unique. The paper normalizes SVR features before fitting: “Before applying SVR, I scaled the features down to the interval [0, 1]” [PAGE 3].

The neural network is introduced on [PAGE 2]. The source gives a hidden-unit activation of sigmoid form, but the printed equation omits the input multiplication:
\[
h_k=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}w_{dk}x_d+b_{dk}\right)\right)}
\]
The printed source form is missing \(x_d\); the standard intended form is shown above. The output layer is linear:

\]
Units: \(h_k\) is dimensionless and lies in the open interval between 0 and 1; \(w^o_k\) is in \(\mathrm{fp}\) per activation unit; \(b^o\) is in \(\mathrm{fp}\). \]

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is the claim that physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can override a statistical QB projection. The paper’s feature set includes age, experience, previous-game stats, last-10-game averages, and opposing-defense stats [PAGE 3]. It does not include injury status, suspensions, weather, or biomechanical load; the paper lists these as future work [PAGE 5]. Formally, let \(X\) be the observed feature vector and \(U\) be an unobserved physical-biomechanical-weather state. The model estimates \(p(Y|X)\), but the causal target is
\[
p(Y|X)=\int p(Y|X,U)p(U|X)\,dU.
\]
If \(U\) is not ignorable, the model’s point estimate is a marginal over a mixture and can be badly biased for a specific week. Define veto indicators \(V_{\mathrm{phys}},V_{\mathrm{bio}},V_{\mathrm{weather}}\in\{0,1\}\) and \(V=\max(V_{\mathrm{phys}},V_{\mathrm{bio}},V_{\mathrm{weather}})\). A formal veto rule is
\[
p(Y|X,V)= (1-V)p_{\mathrm{model}}(Y|X)+V p_{\mathrm{veto}}(Y|X,V),
\]
with
\[
\mathrm{Var}(Y|X,V=1)\ge \mathrm{Var}(Y|X,V=0)+\Delta_{\mathrm{veto}},
\]
where \(\Delta_{\mathrm{veto}}>0\) is in \(\mathrm{fp}^2\). The inequality states that when a veto layer is active, the predictive distribution must widen or shift; a point estimate alone is not admissible.

Physical veto: availability and contact load. Let \(A_{\mathrm{avail}}\in\{0,1\}\) be availability, \(F_{\mathrm{hit}}\) be contact force in newtons, and \(\tau_F\) be a team-medical threshold in newtons. A minimal rule is
\[
V_{\mathrm{phys}}=1_{\{A_{\mathrm{avail}}=0\ \text{or}\ F_{\mathrm{hit}}>\tau_F\}}.
\]
Biomechanical veto: throwing load and joint torque. Let \(L_{\mathrm{bio}}\) be cumulative throwing load in joules or arbitrary load units, \(\tau_{\mathrm{joint}}\) be shoulder or elbow torque in \(\mathrm{N}\cdot\mathrm{m}\), and \(\tau_L,\tau_\tau\) be thresholds. Then
\[
V_{\mathrm{bio}}=1_{\{L_{\mathrm{bio}}>\tau_L\ \text{or}\ \tau_{\mathrm{joint}}>\tau_\tau\}}.
\]
Weather veto: wind, precipitation, and temperature. Let \(v_w\) be wind speed in \(\mathrm{m}/\mathrm{s}\), \(r\) be precipitation rate in \(\mathrm{mm}/\mathrm{h}\), and \(T\) be temperature in \(\mathrm{^\circ C}\). Then
\[
V_{\mathrm{weather}}=1_{\{v_w>\tau_w\ \text{or}\ r>\tau_r\ \text{or}\ T\le T_{\min}\ \text{or}\ T\ge T_{\max}\}}.
\]
The proof obligation is not that these thresholds are universal; it is that the model must expose them and must allow them to veto or widen the projection. The paper’s omission of these layers is material because QB scoring depends on passing volume, accuracy, sacks, and turnovers, all of which are physically mediated. A model that predicts from last-10-game averages alone cannot know that a QB is playing through a shoulder injury, that a starting left tackle is out, or that a stadium has sustained wind. The source itself acknowledges the gap by listing injuries and team changes as future features [PAGE 5]. Therefore the cross-layer veto is valid: physical, biomechanical, and weather states are not merely extra features; they are regime indicators that can invalidate the conditional model for a given week.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
