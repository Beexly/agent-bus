The observer’s own theory of mind creates a trap: seeing goal-directedness, the observer infers a purpose P, then uses P to explain the very goal-directedness that triggered the inference. Tolman attempts to avoid this by demanding that P be “objectively determined” [PAGE 422], but without a response-independent operationalization of P, the counter-trap remains—purpose becomes a just-so story fitted after the fact to the response.

Third, the clock. A purely mechanistic clock model assumes \(R = \Phi(S;\omega)\) with no P term at all: stimulus plus internal state determines response deterministically (apart from \(\epsilon\)). Tolman’s addition of P is meaningful only if it improves prediction beyond the clock. If a clock model without purpose fits the data equally well, P is an unneeded parameter and should be excised by parsimony. The recursive issue is that adding P invites the observer to model the organism as modeling the environment, which invites an infinite regress unless P is strictly behaviorally indexed [PAGE 422].

Recursive synthesis: crisis habit regression tests whether P is robust across stress; the counter-trap tests whether P is independently measurable; the clock tests whether P is necessary. Tolman’s framework survives only if all three tests are passed: purpose persists under crisis, is measurable without circularity, and adds predictive power beyond a clockwork S-R map.
