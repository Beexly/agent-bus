<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Galton’s invocation of “chance” predates Mendel’s rediscovery (1900) and precedes the formalization of probability theory by Kolmogorov (1933). His 1889 work thus constitutes a crucial node in the genealogy of statistical mechanics applied to biology. The lineage can be traced: Laplace’s probabilistic mechanics → Gauss’s error theory → Quetelet’s “average man” → Galton’s eugenics-inspired statistics → Fisher’s synthesis (1918) which merged Mendelian particulates with biometrical variances. Each step introduced more sophisticated operators to model inheritance.

A multi-week trap arises from misinterpreting “blending” as literal fluid mixing. Galton used the term metaphorically to describe how statistical aggregation smooths individual variations. However, later critics (e.g., Darwin’s hybridization experiments) mistakenly assumed physical blending, leading to the incorrect belief that variation would be rapidly diluted. Only with the recognition of particulate inheritance did the paradox resolve. The frontier insight is to re-evaluate Galton’s metaphor through the lens of information flow: the “blend” is not loss of substance but loss of distinguishable signal due to channel noise. This retrocausal perspective reveals that Galton intuitively grasped the concept of ergodicity—the equivalence of ensemble and temporal averages—long before it was formalized in physics. Understanding this historical nuance prevents repeating the fallacy of conflating statistical convergence with physical homogeneity.
