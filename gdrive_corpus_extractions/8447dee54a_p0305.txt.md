**1. Formal Probability Model of Threat Perception**

Let $S$ be the objective stimulus complex (e.g., "large and obstreperous object"). Let $I$ denote the event of potential *injury* and $F$ denote the event of potential *interference*. The organism's internal state is characterized by a temperament variable $\tau \in \mathcal{T}$, where $\mathcal{T}$ is a continuous space representing the spectrum from extreme timidity to extreme pugnacity.

We define two conditional probability distributions over the latent threats given the stimulus and temperament:
$$ P(I | S, \tau) \quad \text{and} \quad P(F | S, \tau) $$

According to Tolman [PAGE 281], the organism's "ultimate desideratum" is either preventing $I$ (if frightened) or preventing $F$ (if pugnacious). This can be modeled as a utility function $U(a, I, F)$ for action $a \in \{\text{flee}, \text{hide}, \text{strike}, \text{destroy}\}$.

For a frightened individual ($\tau = \tau_{timid}$):
$$ U_{timid}(a, I, F) = -\alpha \cdot \mathbb{1}_{I} - \beta \cdot \mathbb{1}_{action\_cost} $$
where $\alpha \gg 0$ is the aversion to injury, and $\beta$ is the cost of action. The optimal action $a^*$ maximizes $E[U_{timid}]$.

For a pugnacious individual ($\tau = \tau_{pugnacious}$):
$$ U_{pugnacious}(a, I, F) = -\gamma \cdot \mathbb{1}_{F} - \delta \cdot \mathbb{1}_{action\_cost} $$
where $\gamma \gg 0$ is the aversion to interference.

**2. Information-Theoretic Sensitivity Filter**

Tolman posits that the timid individual is "sensitive to only one of these threats," effectively filtering out information about the other. We model this as a variational inference process where the posterior over threats is approximated by a factorized distribution:
$$ Q(I, F | S, \tau) \approx Q_{I}(I | S, \tau) \cdot Q_{F}(F | S, \tau) $$

The sensitivity is quantified by the mutual information between the stimulus and each threat channel, modulated by $\tau$:
$$ \mathcal{I}_{I}(\tau) = I(S; I | \tau) = \sum_{s,i} P(s, i|\tau) \log \frac{P(i|s,\tau)}{P(i|\tau)} $$
$$ \mathcal{I}_{F}(\tau) = I(S; F | \tau) $$

For a highly timid $\tau$, $\mathcal{I}_{I}(\tau) \approx \mathcal{I}_{max}$ and $\mathcal{I}_{F}(\tau) \approx 0$. The "facts of possible and even probable interference" become "relatively non-operative" because the channel capacity for processing interference signals is suppressed.

**3. Breakdown Regime and Singularity**

The decision boundary is defined by the equality of expected utilities:
$$ E[U_{timid} | S, \tau] = E[U_{pugnacious} | S, \tau] $$

This yields a critical threshold $\tau^*$ where:
$$ \alpha \cdot P(I | S, \tau^*) \cdot C_{injury} = \gamma \cdot P(F | S, \tau^*) \cdot C_{interference} $$

The **singularity regime** occurs when $P(I | S, \tau)$ and $P(F | S, \tau)$ are both high and strongly correlated ($corr(I,F) \to 1$). In this case, any small perturbation in $\tau$ around $\tau^*$ leads to a discontinuous jump in the chosen action, as the derivative of the policy with respect to $\tau$ becomes infinite. This represents a catastrophic loss of behavioral flexibility.

*   **Temperament ($\tau$):** A dimensionless scalar derived from neurochemical concentrations (e.g., cortisol/testosterone ratio) normalized to a baseline population mean.

**Assumptions:**
1.  The stimulus $S$ is stationary and fully observable by an external observer, but internally processed through a noisy, biased channel dependent on $\tau$.
2.  The costs $C_{injury}$ and $C_{interference}$ are constant for a given species and context.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Tolman’s dual-threat model traces back to James’ (1890) distinction between fear and anger as reactions to obstruction vs. harm, but Tolman injects a radical probabilistic epistemology. Preceding behaviorists (Watson) treated "stimulus" as monolithic; Tolman’s genius was in positing that the same physical stimulus generates two distinct *propositions* ("will this hurt me?" vs. "will this stop me?") whose truth values are inferred, not directly perceived. This inherits from Peirce’s logic of abduction: the organism hypothesizes the most likely threat category given its prior "temperament" (a prior distribution over threat types).

The historical trap lies in the mid-20th century reduction of this model to simple "drive-reduction." Later cyberneticians (e.g., Miller) mapped "injury" to pain receptors and "interference" to goal-blocking sensors, treating them as separate input channels. This missed Tolman’s point that the *same sensory data* (e.g., visual looming) is interpreted differently based on a unified cognitive set. The retrocausal mechanism Tolman hints at—"past training"—is essentially Bayesian updating of the prior $P(\tau)$, where repeated exposure to one type of threat strengthens the corresponding inference pathway, making future interpretations of ambiguous stimuli more biased toward that threat.

A multi-week trap emerges in modern algorithmic trading or autonomous vehicle systems calibrated on historical data. If the system is trained primarily on scenarios where "interference" (market volatility, traffic congestion) correlates weakly with "injury" (system failure, accidents), it may develop a pugnacious-like policy that aggressively exploits interference without sufficient safety margins. When a black swan event causes high correlation between interference and injury (e.g., a flash crash that also triggers hardware faults), the system’s policy collapses because its implicit temperament filter has suppressed the injury-monitoring channel. The historical record shows such cascading failures in financial markets (2010 Flash Crash) and aviation (MCAS), where the system’s "personality" was inadvertently tuned to ignore one class of threats.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From a recursive theory of mind perspective, the organism does not merely react to $S$; it simulates the intentions of $S$ if $S$ is animate. The frightened individual attributes hostile intent to cause injury ($T_{inj}$); the pugnacious individual attributes competitive intent to interfere ($T_{int}$). This attribution is itself a generative model that predicts the other agent’s next move. In a crisis habit regression scenario, under severe stress, higher-order simulation breaks down, and the organism reverts to the most entrenched low-level association: either "threat = pain" (timid) or "threat = obstacle" (pugnacious). This regression is dangerous because it bypasses the nuance that many "obstreperous objects" are neutral or even cooperative.

The counter-trap involves recognizing that the other agent might have a mixed motive or no motive at all. A sophisticated agent would maintain a joint belief state $P(\text{intent}_S | S, \text{history})$ and update it using Bayes rule. However, doing so computationally is expensive. The "clock" here is the time pressure of the interaction: if a response is delayed, the cost of inaction (allowing interference or injury to occur) rises nonlinearly. Thus, the agent faces a trade-off between computational depth (better inference) and speed (lower immediate risk). The optimal strategy is to use fast, heuristic temperament-based responses in high-clock-pressure situations and engage deeper modeling when time permits. Failure to calibrate this trade-off leads to either paralysis-by-analysis (over-modeling) or rash action (under-modeling), both of which can be exploited by a more strategically aware opponent.

Physical and biomechanical constraints impose hard vetoes on the choice between fleeing and fighting, regardless of psychological predisposition. Consider an animal cornered with no escape route (topological constraint). Even if its $\tau$ is extremely timid and it perceives overwhelming $T_{inj}$, the kinematic feasibility of fleeing is zero. The "veto" from the motor cortex overrides the limbic urge to run, forcing a fight response despite the aversion to injury. Conversely, a predator with superior strength and speed may veto a fight response because the biomechanical cost of engaging (risk of self-injury) outweighs the benefit of removing interference.

Weather and environmental conditions add another veto layer. Heavy rain reduces traction, making high-speed flight ineffective and increasing the chance of falls (self-inflicted injury). Thus, even a timid animal might adopt a defensive posture (a hybrid of hiding and fighting) because pure flight is biomechanically suboptimal. The adjudication process can be modeled as a hierarchical optimization: first, filter actions by physical possibility (a binary mask $M(a, \text{environment})$); second, among feasible actions, select the one maximizing expected utility based on threat perception. If the mask excludes all flee/hide actions, the organism is forced into a fight-or-flight-equivalent response (freezing) that it might otherwise avoid. This explains why some normally docile animals become dangerously aggressive when trapped—their "timid" policy is physically blocked, leaving only the "pugnacious" branch viable, albeit at great personal risk.
