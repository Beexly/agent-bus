<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s core move is to replace temporal triangulation with metric scale estimation from a single calibrated image. The source states the task as “ball 3D localization from a single calibrated image” and explicitly rejects the common assumptions of ballistic motion: “we drop assumptions of ballistic trajectory, time consistency, and clean visibility” [PAGE 1]. That framing forces a two-stage decomposition: first detect the ball in image space, then estimate the ball diameter in pixels, then convert that pixel diameter into metric depth using camera intrinsics and the known physical ball diameter. The derivation therefore has three coupled layers: detector heatmap, CNN diameter regressor, and calibrated inverse projection.

The detector layer is BallSeg, described as “a segmentation approach” producing a heatmap Ψ, where “the top-k highest peaks are identified on the output heatmap Ψ to produce k ball candidates” [PAGE 2]. The second layer extracts image patches I around those candidates and applies a CNN Mθ that outputs both a diameter estimate \hat d and a ball-presence confidence \hat c [PAGE 2]. This is crucial: the network is not merely a diameter regressor; it also acts as a candidate verifier. The source says the classification output lets the system “recover some of the detections missed when the detector adopts a top-1 detection strategy” [PAGE 6]. Thus the system’s behavior is not a simple feed-forward map from image to depth; it is a recursive candidate-selection process where detector uncertainty and CNN verification interact.

The geometric derivation is where the single-image claim becomes rigorous. For a camera matrix K, pixel coordinates are lifted into camera-coordinate rays by K^{-1}. The source gives the center ray as b_c and two diametrically opposed edge rays as e_c^±, using vertical offsets b_y ± d/2 [PAGE 2]. The metric scale comes from the known real ball diameter φ and the projected vertical separation of the edge rays. Formally, if the rectified rays are expressed in camera coordinates and the vertical image axis corresponds to the relevant diameter direction, the depth scale λ satisfies φ = λ (e_{c,y}^+ - e_{c,y}^-), hence λ = φ / (e_{c,y}^+ - e_{c,y}^-). The world-space ball center is then c_o + λ R^T b_c, with c_o the camera position and R the world rotation. This is the operative meaning of the paper’s Eq. 7 [PAGE 2]. The gray zone is that Eq. 7 as printed is compact and potentially ambiguous: it uses R both for lens rectification and for world rotation, and it assumes that the vertical pixel offset b_y ± d/2 is sufficient to represent the ball’s projected diameter. If the ball is only partially visible, if the candidate center is biased, or if lens rectification leaves residual distortion, the denominator and numerator are both corrupted.

Error propagation is the central practical constraint. In a pinhole model, apparent diameter in pixels scales approximately as d_px ≈ f_px φ / Z, where f_px is focal length in pixels and Z is depth. Therefore Z ≈ f_px φ / d_px, and a small diameter error δd produces a relative depth error approximately δZ / Z ≈ -δd / d, under the small-ball, fixed-calibration approximation. This explains the paper’s observation that “a few pixels diameter error can translate in meters in the 3D space” [PAGE 3]. It also explains why the paper reports that a 1.6 pixel diameter error translates to less than two meters in projection error and about 10% relative distance error [PAGE 6]. The relationship is not universal; it depends on focal length, pixel density, distance to scene, and calibration quality [PAGE 5]. The model is therefore strongest when the ball is large enough in pixels and the camera geometry is favorable.

The gray-zone audit must also include detector and dataset effects. The paper evaluates on DeepSport, APIDIS, and its own high-quality ballistic-annotation set. DeepSport is preferred because it has “largest environment diversity” and includes occlusions, clutter, and motion blur [PAGE 3]. APIDIS is weaker because of synchronization issues, low contrast, and duplicated frames [PAGE 3]. The high-quality evaluation set is clean but limited to free-flight balls, so it cannot test occluded or held balls [PAGE 4]. This creates a validation paradox: the cleanest annotations are least representative of the hardest game situations. The paper acknowledges this by saying the evaluation set “is limited to samples where the ball moves freely, on ballistic trajectories and does not include occluded balls or balls held in player hands” [PAGE 4]. The method’s robustness claim therefore rests on comparing DeepSport and the clean set; similar performance across those sets is used to argue robustness to occlusion [PAGE 6]. That inference is plausible but not absolute, because both sets may share annotation biases.

A second gray zone is the role of ballistic motion. The paper uses a second-degree motion model to regularize ground-truth annotations: F_{p0, \dot p0}(t) = p0 + \dot p0 t - [0,0,g]^T t^2/2, with g = 9.81 m/s² [PAGE 4]. This model is not used for single-image inference, but it shapes the evaluation ground truth. If the motion model is biased by ignored friction or spin, the “ground truth” inherits that bias. The paper explicitly says the model ignores “friction, ball spin and other effects” [PAGE 4]. For free flight over short basketball trajectories, this is acceptable, but for long passes, spin-sensitive bounces, or aerodynamic effects, the fitted trajectory may not represent the true physical path. Thus the evaluation pipeline is strong for metric annotation, but not infallible.

A third gray zone is the loss architecture. The CNN is supervised by binary cross entropy for presence and Huber loss for diameter [PAGE 2]. The weighted loss L = α L_d + (1-α) L_c mixes a regression term with pixel-squared units and a classification term with information-theoretic units. The paper selects α = 0.5 empirically [PAGE 5]. This is not a physical invariant; it is a tuning choice. The model may therefore trade diameter precision for detection confidence in ways that are dataset-dependent. The source supports this by showing that the classification head improves detection quality at varying FP-rate, while the regression head reaches about 1.6 pixels MAE on the best conditions [PAGE 5][PAGE 6].

The final reasoning step is that the method’s validity is conditional on calibration, known ball diameter, visible ball boundary, and sufficiently large pixel footprint. If those conditions fail, the single-image depth estimate becomes unstable. The source itself provides the relevant boundary cases: smaller balls still work surprisingly well on DeepSport when scaled down [PAGE 7], but APIDIS produces a distribution shift attributed to poor contrast [PAGE 7]. Occluded or partially visible balls are explicitly part of the target setting, but only insofar as enough of the ball is visible to estimate a diameter [PAGE 1]. Therefore the gray-zone boundary is not “occlusion yes/no” but “sufficient visible metric evidence yes/no.”

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal operators in the source can be separated into four families: loss operators, detector/heatmap operators, camera-geometry operators, and trajectory-regularization operators. Each has explicit units and failure regimes.

1. Classification operator.
For patch I, the CNN outputs \hat c = M^c_\theta(I) ∈ [0,1], interpreted as probability that the patch contains a ball [PAGE 2]. With target c ∈ {0,1}, the binary cross-entropy loss is

\mathcal{L}_c(c,\hat c) = -c \log(\hat c) - (1-c)\log(1-\hat c).

If natural logarithm is used, units are nats per sample. The source’s printed Eq. 1 shows the same intent but appears typographically compressed [PAGE 2]. Breakdown regime: if \hat c saturates at 0 or 1, gradients vanish and the confidence head becomes overconfident; if patches are systematically mis-centered, the classifier learns detector artifacts rather than ball presence.

2. Diameter regression operator.
The CNN outputs \hat d = M^d_\theta(I) ∈ \mathbb{R}^+_0, in pixels [PAGE 2]. With target diameter d ∈ \mathbb{R}^+_0 and Huber threshold δ = 1.0 [PAGE 5], the Huber loss is

\mathcal{L}_d(d,\hat d) =
\begin{cases}
\frac{1}{2}(d-\hat d)^2, & |d-\hat d| \le \delta, \\
\delta\left(|d-\hat d| - \frac{1}{2}\delta\right), & |d-\hat d| > \delta.
\end{cases}

Units: if d is in pixels, the quadratic region has units px²; the linear region has units px·δ, with δ in px. The weighted objective is

\mathcal{L} = \alpha \mathcal{L}_d + (1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_c,

with \alpha = 0.5 selected empirically [PAGE 5]. The source notes that L_d was ignored for non-ball inputs [PAGE 2]. Breakdown regime: if δ is too small, the loss behaves almost like L1 and may be unstable for early training; if δ is too large, it behaves like L2 and over-penalizes large outliers. The mixed-unit objective is not physically normalized; it is an empirical training choice.

3. Ball detection heatmap operator.
BallSeg, based on ICNet, predicts a heatmap Ψ over image space. The detector extracts top-k peaks from Ψ to produce k candidates [PAGE 2]. The baseline Hough-circle transform is applied to Ψ over a spatial neighborhood around the top candidate. Pre-processing includes morphological opening with circular filter diameter ρ, adaptive thresholding with hysteresis thresholds τ_h and τ_l, and diameter search bounds constrained by physical scene limits [PAGE 2]. In the reported implementation, ρ = 37, τ_l = 10, τ_h = 20, and a 2 meter margin constrained the Hough diameter range [PAGE 5].

Formal Hough idea: for each edge point (x_i,y_i) and candidate radius r, vote in parameter space (x,y,r). The selected circle maximizes accumulated votes within the allowed radius interval. Units: x,y,r in pixels. Breakdown regime: if Ψ is low-contrast, blurred, or noisy, edge votes disperse; if the ball is partially occluded, the circular evidence is incomplete. The source observes that larger training data improves Hough performance because “ball is much better segmented” [PAGE 7], showing that HCT is downstream-limited by heatmap quality.

4. Camera matrix and inverse projection.
The camera matrix is

K =
\begin{bmatrix}
f \mu_x & \gamma & u_x \\
0 & f \mu_y & u_y \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.

Here f is focal length, μ_x and μ_y are pixel densities in x and y, γ is skew, and (u_x,u_y) is the principal point [PAGE 2]. For dimensional consistency, f μ_x and f μ_y must have units of pixels. If f is in meters and μ in pixels per meter, the product is pixels. Invertibility requires

\det(K) = f^2 \mu_x \mu_y \neq 0.

If μ_x or μ_y is zero, or f is zero, inverse projection is undefined.

Given pixel center (b_x,b_y) and estimated diameter \hat d, the rectified camera-coordinate rays are

\tilde b_c = K^{-1} \rho\!\begin{bmatrix} b_x \\ b_y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\tilde e_c^\pm = K^{-1} \rho\!\begin{bmatrix} b_x \\ b_y \pm \hat d/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},

where ρ denotes the rectification map for lens distortion. The source calls the rectification function R, but also uses R for world rotation; here I separate them to avoid ambiguity [PAGE 2].

Let the vertical ray separation be

\Delta e_y = \tilde e_{c,y}^+ - \tilde e_{c,y}^-.

Assuming the projected vertical diameter corresponds to the known ball diameter φ, the metric scale is

\lambda = \frac{\phi}{\Delta e_y}.

Units: φ in meters; \Delta e_y is dimensionless if the rays have been normalized to z=1, or ray-unit dependent if K^{-1} leaves arbitrary homogeneous scale. Because numerator and denominator use the same ray construction, the ratio yields a scale factor in meters per ray unit. The world-space center estimate is

\hat b_o = c_o + \lambda R_w^T \tilde b_c,

where c_o is camera position in world coordinates and R_w is the world rotation. This is the rigorous interpretation of the source’s Eq. 7 [PAGE 2].

Singularity and breakdown regimes:
- Δe_y → 0: the projected vertical diameter collapses. This occurs when the ball’s diameter direction is nearly parallel to the viewing ray, when the ball is viewed from an unusual angle, or when the image patch is too small/blurry to produce stable edge rays.
- \hat d → 0: the two edge rays collapse to the center ray, making scale undefined.
- \hat d negative or nonphysical: violates d ∈ \mathbb{R}^+_0.
- K ill-conditioned: poor calibration, extreme principal point error, or near-zero pixel density causes ray amplification.
- Residual distortion: if ρ does not fully rectify lens distortion, the pixel offset b_y ± \hat d/2 does not correspond to a true metric diameter.
- Unknown φ: if the real ball diameter is wrong, all depths scale linearly with φ. For example, using a volleyball diameter for a basketball would produce a proportional metric bias.

5. Metric error operators.
The paper defines diameter MAE as

\mathrm{MAE}_{px} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N |d_i - \hat d_i|,

with units pixels. The projection error is the Euclidean distance between true and estimated 3D positions, projected onto a horizontal plane, with units meters. The relative distance error is the ratio of projection error to camera distance, reported as a percentage [PAGE 5]. These metrics are not dimensionless physical constants; they depend on scene scale and calibration.

6. Ballistic annotation operator.
For ground-truth regularization, the source uses

F_{p_0,\dot p_0}(t) = p_0 + \dot p_0 t -
\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\g\end{bmatrix}\frac{t^2}{2},

with g = 9.81 m/s² [PAGE 4]. Given N annotated positions \hat p_i at times t_i, the parameters are fit by

(p_0,\dot p_0) = \arg\min_{p_0,\dot p_0} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\|F_{p_0,\dot p_0}(t_i) - \hat p_i\right\|^2.

Units: p_0 in meters, \dot p_0 in meters per second, t in seconds. The model assumes no friction, no spin, and no aerodynamic force [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: long flights, strong spin, air resistance, or bounce-dominated motion. This operator is used for annotation denoising, not for the single-image test-time estimator.

7. Detection-selection operator.
At test time, given k candidates I_i, the selected patch is

j = \arg\max_{i \in [1,k]} M^c_\theta(I_i).

This operator changes the effective detector operating point. The source shows that increasing k improves AuC and recovers balls missed by BallSeg-top1 [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: if false candidates have spuriously high \hat c, selection can amplify false positives; if the true ball is absent from top-k, no CNN verification can recover it.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits inside a long lineage of sports ball localization. Multi-view triangulation is the classical reliable route: the source says most existing solutions rely on multiple viewpoints to triangulate 2D ball positions and that such systems are “expensive and complex to install” compared with single-viewpoint setups [PAGE 1]. That lineage includes industrial line-calling and goal-line technologies [PAGE 1]. The retro-causal trap is that multi-view reliability creates an assumption that single-view methods must also use temporal consistency. The paper breaks that assumption by stating that it addresses “any game situation where the ball is (even partly) visible” without requiring multiple frames [PAGE 1].

A second lineage is ballistic trajectory fitting. The source notes that several single-view works rely on 3D ballistic trajectory fitting from 2D detections [PAGE 1]. This is historically natural because physics provides strong constraints: gravity is known, acceleration is approximately constant, and free flight is smooth. However, the paper points out a multi-week operational trap: in basketball, ballistic trajectories “only represent less than 10% of game time” [PAGE 1]. If a system only works for shots and long passes, it misses dribbling, held-ball play, deflections, passes, and scrambled sequences. The retro-causal lesson is that scientific elegance can bias evaluation toward rare free-flight events while ignoring the common event distribution.

A third lineage is classical image processing. The baseline uses Hough-circle transformation on the heatmap gradient magnitude [PAGE 2]. Hough methods historically detect circular objects by voting in parameter space. The paper’s innovation is not to discard this lineage but to subordinate it to learned heatmap segmentation and learned patch regression. The source shows that Hough can work well when the heatmap is good, and it improves dramatically when the detector is trained on more data [PAGE 7]. This creates a genealogical lesson: classical operators are not obsolete; they become more reliable when upstream representations improve.

A fourth lineage is dataset construction. The paper compares DeepSport, APIDIS, Soccer-ISSIA, and its own ballistic evaluation set [PAGE 3]. DeepSport is chosen because it has environmental diversity: different arenas, angles, ball colors, court colors, and lighting conditions [PAGE 3]. APIDIS is multi-view but weak due to synchronization and resampled annotation issues [PAGE 3]. ISSIA is synchronized but short and soccer-focused, with the ball often out of view [PAGE 3]. The retro-causal trap here is that multi-view datasets appear authoritative, but if synchronization is imperfect, their 3D pseudo-ground-truth can contain dozens of pixels of image-space discrepancy [PAGE 3]. The paper therefore constructs a cleaner single-view annotation pipeline.

A fifth lineage is annotation methodology. The paper compares two approaches: annotating ball diameter directly, versus annotating ball center plus vertical ground projection [PAGE 3]. The diameter approach seems natural because Eq. 7 uses diameter. But the paper finds that “the ball center+projection approach is significantly better for ball 3D annotation in single calibrated viewpoints” [PAGE 4]. The diameter approach tends to underestimate the true diameter [PAGE 4]. This is a retro-causal design trap: the model’s mathematical operator encourages one annotation style, but human annotation geometry favors another. The projection method constrains the annotation with court geometry and a vertical line, reducing ambiguity [PAGE 3].

A sixth lineage is network architecture. The CNN uses a VGG feature extractor followed by fully connected layers inspired by LeCun et al. [PAGE 2]. This places the paper in the lineage of ImageNet-pretrained backbones adapted to small regression tasks. The historical lesson is that transfer learning can make small-patch regression viable even when the target dataset is small. However, the paper also shows that larger training data still matters: both CNN and Hough improve when trained on the extended DeepSport set [PAGE 7].

The multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy are:
1. Assuming temporal continuity is necessary, which limits deployment to video sequences.
2. Assuming ballistic motion is sufficient, which limits coverage to a minority of game time.
3. Assuming multi-view datasets are automatically superior, despite synchronization errors.
4. Assuming diameter annotation is more direct, despite projection annotation being more accurate.
5. Assuming a small dataset can generalize across arenas, lighting, and ball scales without distribution shift.
6. Assuming a single-frame estimate needs no temporal filtering, while acknowledging that raw estimates could be improved later [PAGE 8].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model here is not about human opponents in a literal sense, but about nested expectations among detector, regressor, annotator, and deploying analyst. Each layer expects the previous layer to behave in a certain way, and failure occurs when those expectations diverge.

The first recursion is detector-to-CNN. BallSeg produces candidates and a heatmap. The CNN expects candidates to be roughly centered on the ball. The source says patches are extracted “centered on the candidates proposed by any conventional ball detector” [PAGE 2]. If the detector’s peak is biased by motion blur or occlusion, the CNN receives a patch where the ball is off-center or truncated. The CNN may still estimate a diameter, but with systematic error. The paper’s oracle-diagnostic helps reveal this: using true ball positions improves diameter estimation in some cases, showing that detector localization quality matters [PAGE 6]. The recursive trap is that a detector optimized for presence may not be optimized for metric centering.

The second recursion is CNN-to-detector. The CNN’s classification output is used to revise detection confidence. The source says the CNN can “increase the ball detection rate by filtering the candidates produced by the detector” [PAGE 1]. But this assumes the CNN’s confidence head has learned true ball appearance rather than detector-specific artifacts. If BallSeg produces candidates with characteristic shapes or heatmap biases, the CNN may learn those artifacts. In deployment with a different detector, the confidence head may transfer poorly. The source mitigates this by training with k=4 candidates and treating non-ball patches as negatives [PAGE 5], but the recursive dependency remains.

The third recursion is annotation-to-model. The high-quality evaluation set is generated by fitting ballistic trajectories to manually adjusted annotations [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. The annotator expects the trajectory model to regularize noise. The model expects annotations to be close enough to the true ball positions for least-squares fitting to be valid. If initial annotations are biased, the fitted trajectory can lock in the bias. The source recognizes this by manually inspecting trajectories and readjusting annotations until the trajectory matches the scene [PAGE 4]. This is a human-in-the-loop counter-trap, but it is labor-intensive and may not scale to many venues.

The fourth recursion is analyst-to-deployment. An analyst may see a 10% relative distance error and judge it acceptable for statistics, but insufficient for officiating. The source itself says the method may appear “a lousy approximation” but can be valuable for automated estimation in 3D play analysis [PAGE 7]. This is a theory-of-mind issue: the same numeric error has different operational meaning depending on the use case. For offside-like decisions, metric precision requirements are stricter than for statistical tracking. The paper does not claim official-line accuracy; it claims usefulness for single-view 3D reconstruction and analysis [PAGE 1][PAGE 7].

Crisis habit regression occurs when time pressure pushes the system back to familiar assumptions. In a live sports analytics pipeline, the easiest habit is to require multiple frames, ballistic flight, or manual annotation. The paper’s counter-trap is to allow single-image inference and to use confidence-based candidate filtering. However, under crisis conditions such as low light, occlusion, or small ball size, the model may still regress to conservative outputs: low confidence, suppressed detections, or large uncertainty. The source shows this indirectly: APIDIS produces a distribution shift and underestimation, likely due to poor contrast [PAGE 7]. In a crisis deployment, the safest behavior may be to abstain when \hat c is low or when \hat d is near the lower pixel bound.

The clock dimension has three timescales:
- Inference clock: single-image prediction avoids waiting for trajectories, but the paper does not provide timing guarantees for the full pipeline.
- Annotation clock: ballistic regularization requires multiple consecutive images and manual trajectory inspection, which is slower than single-image annotation.
- Deployment clock: collecting a dedicated dataset for a new arena or sport is slow. The source explicitly says that for industrial applications, “collecting a dedicated dataset is generally required” [PAGE 8].

The recursive counter-trap is therefore to combine single-image speed with explicit uncertainty. The CNN confidence output is useful, but it should be interpreted as a gate, not as a calibrated probability unless validated. The paper evaluates ROC/AuC rather than probability calibration [PAGE 6], so the confidence is best used for ranking candidates, not for literal Bayesian belief.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a higher physical or informational constraint makes the single-image estimate invalid, regardless of network performance. The source provides enough conditions to adjudicate several vetoes.

Physical veto 1: No calibration.
The method requires camera calibration. The source states that camera calibration is “mandatory for 3D localization” [PAGE 8]. If K is unknown, the inverse projection K^{-1} is undefined. Even approximate focal length is insufficient for metric depth because the scale depends on f μ_x and f μ_y [PAGE 2]. Verdict: hard veto.

Physical veto 2: Unknown or wrong real ball diameter.
The scale equation uses true diameter φ in meters. If φ is wrong, the estimated depth scales proportionally. Suppose φ_true = 0.24 m but φ_used = 0.22 m; then all depths are underestimated by about 8.3%. The source assumes knowledge of the real ball diameter and notes this is generally available for many applications [PAGE 8]. Verdict: hard veto if φ is unknown or ball type changes without updating φ.

Physical veto 3: Insufficient visible ball boundary.
The method estimates diameter from two edge rays. If the ball is too occluded, blurred, or too small, the edge rays become unstable. The paper targets situations where the ball is “even partly visible” [PAGE 1], but partial visibility must still preserve enough circular or diameter evidence. If only a small cap is visible, the CNN may classify presence but diameter regression becomes unreliable. Verdict: soft-to-hard veto depending on visible arc and patch resolution.

Biomechanical veto: Ballistic assumptions are not required for inference, but they affect evaluation.
The single-image method does not require free fall. It can work during dribbling or held-ball moments if the ball is visible. However, the high-quality evaluation set uses ballistic trajectories [PAGE 3]. Therefore, a biomechanical event such as a bounce, spin-dominated flight, or player-hand occlusion may not be represented in the cleanest evaluation set. This does not veto the method physically, but it vetoes claims that the evaluation set covers all biomechanical regimes. Verdict: evaluation veto, not inference veto.

Biomechanical veto 2: Ball deformation and spin.
A basketball is approximately spherical, but spin and deformation can alter apparent shape, especially under motion blur. The paper does not model deformation. If the apparent patch is elongated by motion blur, the diameter estimate may be biased. The source acknowledges motion blur as a challenge in basketball images [PAGE 3]. Verdict: conditional veto under severe blur; the model may still estimate an average diameter but with increased variance.

Weather/environmental veto.
The source focuses on indoor basketball, where lighting is controlled but often weak [PAGE 3]. It does not provide a weather model. For outdoor sports, precipitation, fog, rain streaks, or low sun can reduce contrast and corrupt the heatmap. The paper’s APIDIS discussion shows that poor contrast causes distribution shift and underestimation [PAGE 7]. A formal environmental attenuation model can be expressed as image contrast decreasing with path length, for example C(r) = C_0 e^{-β r}, where β is an extinction coefficient in m^{-1}. If C(r) falls below the detector and edge-estimation threshold, the method vetoes. Verdict: environmental veto when visibility or contrast falls below the empirical operating range.

Geometric veto: Denominator collapse.
The scale equation contains Δe_y = e_{c,y}^+ - e_{c,y}^-. If the projected diameter has near-zero vertical component, the denominator approaches zero. This can happen if the camera’s vertical axis is not aligned with the ball’s apparent diameter direction, if the ball is viewed from above/below in a way that compresses vertical separation, or if the patch is too small. In that regime, λ becomes numerically unstable. Verdict: hard numerical veto when |Δe_y| is below a calibrated epsilon.

Calibration veto: Residual distortion.
The rectification map R must undo lens distortion. If distortion remains, the pixel offset b_y ± \hat d/2 does not correspond to a straight metric diameter. Wide-angle sports cameras often have significant distortion. The paper assumes calibration and rectification but does not quantify residual distortion error. Verdict: veto if residual distortion is comparable to the diameter error budget. Since a 1.6 pixel diameter error can already translate to meters of depth error [PAGE 6], even small systematic distortion can matter.

Adjudication summary:
- The method is physically valid when K is known, φ is known, the ball is sufficiently visible, and Δe_y is nonzero.
- The method is invalid when calibration, ball size, visibility, or rectified geometry fail.
- The method is not invalidated by non-ballistic motion; that is an advantage over prior single-view methods.
- The method is conditionally weakened by occlusion, low contrast, small ball size, and motion blur, but these are not absolute vetoes unless the visible metric evidence disappears.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following counterfactual vetoes are verifiable from the source and can be used as sanity checks for any reproduction or deployment claim.

Veto 1: APIDIS low-contrast distribution shift.
Counterfactual: Train on DeepSport, evaluate on APIDIS without contrast adaptation or retraining.
Source evidence: APIDIS images are described as having “rather weak lighting setup, resulting in poorly contrasted images” [PAGE 3]. The paper also says the APIDIS dataset “suffers from low contrasted images and poor annotations” [PAGE 7]. In the small-ball experiment, the model “underestimates the diameter from the balls of the APIDIS dataset,” and the authors conjecture this is caused by poor contrast [PAGE 7].
Verifiable prediction: APIDIS diameter predictions will show a negative bias relative to DeepSport, with larger error dispersion and lower reliability of 3D projection. If a reproduced model shows symmetric APIDIS errors centered at zero with similar variance to DeepSport, the reproduction likely added contrast normalization, retraining, or annotation filtering not present in the original evaluation.
Veto condition: If APIDIS performance is claimed to be equivalent to DeepSport without addressing contrast, the claim is vetoed by the source.

Veto 2: Top-1 detection versus top-k plus CNN verification.
Counterfactual: Use only BallSeg top-1 detection and discard the CNN classification head.
Source evidence: The paper states that with k candidates, “the number of true positives is largely increased with k > 1” and that the CNN can “recover balls missed by BallSeg-top1 but who ranked in the topk with k ≥ 2” [PAGE 6]. Figure 5 reports that BallSeg-topk + CNN improves AuC compared with BallSeg-top1 [PAGE 6].
Verifiable prediction: If k is increased from 1 to larger values and the CNN classification head is used to select among candidates, true-positive rate at low false-positive rate should increase. If a system reports no detection improvement when adding CNN verification over top-k candidates, either the detector is already saturated, the CNN was not trained on candidate negatives, or the evaluation protocol is inconsistent.
Veto condition: A claim that the CNN only improves diameter regression but never detection is incomplete, because the source explicitly uses \hat c to improve detection [PAGE 1][PAGE 6].

Veto 3: Extended training set and Hough baseline gain.
Counterfactual: Train BallSeg and the CNN only on the original DeepSport set versus the extended DeepSport set.
Source evidence: The extended DeepSport dataset contains 1514 images from 49 scenes, compared with 314 images from 15 scenes [PAGE 4]. The paper reports that when training on the extended dataset, the Hough baseline experiences “a drop of more than 50% of the mean absolute diameter error on all datasets,” while the CNN also improves, reaching about one pixel error on the evaluation set [PAGE 7].
Verifiable prediction: Increasing training data should improve both heatmap quality and diameter estimation. The Hough baseline should improve more dramatically because it depends directly on segmentation quality, while the CNN should improve but retain an advantage due to learned regression. If a reproduction shows no Hough improvement with more data, the detector heatmap likely failed to become sharper or the evaluation set changed.
Veto condition: A claim that more training data only helps the CNN and not classical Hough is contradicted by the source [PAGE 7].

Additional verifiable veto: Oracle detections.
Counterfactual: Provide perfect ball locations instead of detector candidates.
Source evidence: Oracle + CNN achieves 1.9 px MAE on DeepSport and 1.5 px on the high-quality set, compared with BallSeg + CNN at 1.6 px on both sets [PAGE 6]. The paper interprets this as evidence that the CNN can estimate size even when detection is challenging and that the method is robust to occlusions [PAGE 6].
Verifiable prediction: Oracle detections should not dramatically outperform BallSeg detections if the detector is already producing usable patches. If oracle performance is much worse than detector performance, the evaluation patch extraction or training procedure is inconsistent.
Veto condition: A claim that detector error is the dominant source of diameter error is weakened by the source’s finding that BallSeg + CNN is close to oracle performance [PAGE 6].

Deployment-level veto: Missing calibration or unknown ball diameter.
Counterfactual: Remove K or replace φ with an incorrect value.
Source evidence: Calibration is mandatory [PAGE 8], and the method uses knowledge of the real ball diameter [PAGE 1][PAGE 2].
Verifiable prediction: Removing K makes metric projection undefined. Using the wrong φ scales all estimated depths by φ_used / φ_true. This is algebraically verifiable from the scale equation λ = φ / Δe_y.
Veto condition: Any claim of metric 3D localization without calibration or known ball size is invalid under the paper’s own formulation.

Operational veto: Clean evaluation set only.
Counterfactual: Report only the ballistic clean evaluation set and omit DeepSport occluded/cluttered results.
Source evidence: The clean set “does not include occluded balls or balls held in player hands” [PAGE 4], while DeepSport includes many such cases [PAGE 3]. The paper prefers DeepSport evaluation because it is more challenging and diverse [PAGE 4].
Verifiable prediction: A model evaluated only on the clean set will likely show lower MAE than on DeepSport, but this does not prove robustness to real game occlusion. The source itself uses the clean set to assess annotation quality, not as the primary robustness benchmark [PAGE 4][PAGE 5].
Veto condition: A robustness claim based solely on the clean set is vetoed by the paper’s own dataset design.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
