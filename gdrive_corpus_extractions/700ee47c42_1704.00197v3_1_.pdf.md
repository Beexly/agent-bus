<THINK_TRACE>
GSE/GSI audit treats iWinRNFL as a state-conditional probability operator, not as a causal game engine. The multi-hop derivation is: first, the paper defines the target as “the likelihood that a certain team will win a game given the current state of the game” [PAGE 1]; second, it compresses that state into a logistic log-odds surface over ten covariates plus interactions [PAGE 3]; third, it estimates coefficients on 295,844 snaps from 1,792 regular-season games [PAGE 2]; fourth, it validates calibration and proper scoring rather than only classification accuracy [PAGE 4-PAGE 5]; fifth, it compares against naive Bayes and a feedforward neural network to test whether nonlinear boundary effects are materially important [PAGE 4-PAGE 6]. The key formal object is therefore \(\Pr(H=1\mid x_t)=\sigma(w^\top x_t)\), where \(x_t\) is the observed game state and \(H\) is home-team win. The coefficient table [PAGE 4] is not a set of causal effects; it is a conditional association surface. The paper itself warns that the model is simple and interpretable, but its value depends on calibration, not on coefficient realism. The calibration claim is strong: the fitted calibration relation has \(R^2=0.998\), slope \(0.98\) with 95 percent interval \([0.97,1.01]\), and intercept \(0.008\) with interval \([-0.001,0.02]\) [PAGE 4]. The Brier score is \(0.158\), against a climatology reference of \(0.26\) [PAGE 5]. Those are the central empirical operators.

The gray-zone audit has several branches. First, the paper states that Table 1 uses “standardized independent variables” [PAGE 4], but the text interprets the interaction as \(1.72-0.65\cdot(\text{TimeLapsed})\) and says the rating effect is \(1.72\) at the very start when “Time Lapsed is 0” [PAGE 4]. If time is standardized, time zero is the sample mean, not kickoff; if time is raw, the table label is ambiguous. This matters because the reported decay and growth of rating and score effects are unit-dependent. Second, the paper says only that data were split “in a training and test set in a 70-30% proportion respectively” [PAGE 4]. It does not state that the split is by game or season. If plays from the same game appear in both train and test, the effective sample size is far below 295,844 because within-game states are highly correlated; calibration intervals and accuracy can then be optimistic. Third, the model’s boundary behavior is suspect: near the end of a half or game the true win probability should approach a deterministic \(0\) or \(1\), but a finite linear log-odds surface does not enforce that degeneracy. The paper acknowledges nonlinear boundary effects in Figure 1 [PAGE 2] and admits that “During the OT the model does not perform very accurately due to the sparsity of the relevant data” [PAGE 6]. Fourth, the model lacks weather, injury, personnel, fatigue, and biomechanical variables; those omitted layers can veto calibration in specific strata even if average calibration is good. Fifth, the model is potentially performative: the paper says such models “can provide the backbone for in-game decisions as well as, play call and personnel evaluations” [PAGE 1]. If coaches optimize against the probability, the data-generating process shifts and the coefficients are no longer stationary. The correct audit is therefore not “is 76.5 percent accuracy good?” but “under which state strata, temporal holdouts, and strategic perturbations does the probability operator remain calibrated?”

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The primary operator is the binary logistic map
\[
\Pr(H=1\mid x)=\frac{\exp(w^\top x)}{1+\exp(w^\top x)}=\sigma(w^\top x),
\]
where \(H\in\{0,1\}\) is the home-team win indicator, \(x\) is the game-state vector, and \(w\) is the coefficient vector [PAGE 2-PAGE 3]. The linear predictor \(w^\top x\) is in log-odds units and is dimensionless; each \(w_j\) has units of log-odds per unit of \(x_j\), or per one standard deviation if \(x_j\) is standardized. The covariates include possession team, score differential in points, home and away timeouts as counts, time elapsed in seconds, down as categorical indicators, field position in yards from own yard line, yards-to-go in yards, home ball-possession time in seconds, and rating differential in points [PAGE 3]. The paper states: “The last independent variable is representative of the strength difference between the two teams” [PAGE 3]. It also includes possession-by-down, possession-by-field-position, possession-by-yards-to-go, time-by-rating-differential, and time-by-score-differential interactions [PAGE 3]. The reported intercept is zero: “the intercept of the model is 0” [PAGE 4]. Table 1 reports standardized coefficients, including score differential \(1.41\), rating differential \(1.72\), time lapsed \(0.43\), home timeouts \(0.06\), away timeouts \(-0.06\), and the time-by-score interaction \(2.88\) [PAGE 4]. The text interprets the rating effect as “equal to 1.72 −0.65 · (TimeLapsed)” and the score effect as “equal to 1.41 + 2.88 · (TimeLapsed)” [PAGE 4]. Those formulas are log-odds shifts per unit or per standard deviation of the corresponding covariate; they are not probabilities. The calibration operator is the regression of observed binary outcome on predicted probability,
\[
y_b=a+b\hat\pi_b+\epsilon_b,
\]
with ideal \(a=0\) and \(b=1\). The paper reports \(R^2=0.998\), slope \(0.98\) with interval \([0.97,1.01]\), and intercept \(0.008\) with interval \([-0.001,0.02]\) [PAGE 4]. The proper scoring operator is the Brier score
\[
\beta=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\pi_i-y_i)^2,
\]
where \(\pi_i\in[0,1]\) and \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\), so \(\beta\) is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\) [PAGE 5]. The paper reports “iWinRNFL exhibits a Brier score β of 0.158” and “The Brier score for this reference model is βbase = 0.26” [PAGE 5].

The team-rating operator is a constrained least-squares fit over observed home margins:
\[
\min_{h,R}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\left(\mu_m-(h+R_{H_m}-R_{V_m})\right)^2
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\sum_{i=1}^{32}R_i=0,
\]
where \(\mu_m\) is the home win margin in points, \(h\) is the home-edge parameter in points, and \(R_i\) is the team rating in points [PAGE 7]. The preseason prior is fit by
\[
\min_{h,\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{32}\left(\lambda_i-\mathbb{E}[W_i]\right)^2
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\sum_{i=1}^{32}\rho_i=0,
\]
where \(\lambda_i\) is the preseason expected-win prior and \(\mathbb{E}[W_i]=\sum_{j=1}^{16}\Pr[i\succ j]\) [PAGE 7]. The blended rating is
\[
\tilde R_i(w)=\gamma\rho_i+(1-\gamma)R_i(w),
\qquad
\gamma=\max\left(1-\frac{w-1}{10},0\right),
\]
so the preseason weight declines linearly and vanishes after week 10 [PAGE 7]. The paper states that “for the first few weeks of the season the ratings obtained can be fairly noisy (and for week 1 there are no observations to begin with!)” [PAGE 7]. That is a genuine singularity regime: in week 1 the season-rating fit has no observations and is identified only by the prior and the zero-sum constraint.

Breakdown regimes are explicit and implicit. The explicit one is overtime: “During the OT the model does not perform very accurately due to the sparsity of the relevant data” [PAGE 6]. The implicit boundary regime is \(t\to T_{\text{end}}\): a finite logistic surface cannot force \(\Pr(H=1\mid x_t)\) to \(1\) for a leading home team or \(0\) for a trailing home team at the final whistle; it only shifts log-odds by finite coefficients. The paper’s own Figure 1 warns that linear hypotheses degrade “towards the end of the half/game” [PAGE 2]. The rare-tail regime is also under-identified: Brier score is dominated by mid-range probabilities, not by \(0.1\) percent tails, and the Super Bowl anecdote shows the tail is not a precision test [PAGE 1, PAGE 6]. Finally, the standardization ambiguity in Table 1 versus the text’s \(TimeLapsed=0\) interpretation is a unit-breakdown risk: if the coefficient is per standard deviation, the reported decay curve is not on the raw clock; if it is raw, the table label is misleading.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from normal-margin scoring models to expected-points and win-probability-added systems, then to ensemble and simulation approaches, and finally to iWinRNFL’s deliberately simple logistic surface. The paper names the ancestry directly: “The most influential work in the space, Win Probability Added, has been developed by [4]” [PAGE 2]. It also notes that “More recently, [6] provided a random forest model for in-game win probability” [PAGE 2], and that a simulation approach is “computational expensive” [PAGE 2]. The retrocausal motive is Super Bowl LI: the paper says some models assigned New England less than \(0.1\) percent late in the third quarter, and the comeback was treated as evidence that models can be overconfident against the trailing team [PAGE 1]. iWinRNFL assigns a minimum of \(2.1\) percent, which the paper presents as conservative rather than superior: “the above does not mean that iWinRNFL is ‘better’ than other win probability models” [PAGE 6]. The correct genealogy is therefore not “simple beats complex”; it is “a transparent, reproducible baseline exposes how much of the complex-model performance is already captured by a linear-in-log-odds state surface.” The paper’s stated objective is “to provide an open and fully reproducible win probability model for the NFL” [PAGE 2].

The multi-week traps are structural. First, the rating system is weekly and recursive: ratings for week \(w\) use results up to week \(w-1\), with a preseason prior that decays as \(\gamma=\max(1-(w-1)/10,0)\) [PAGE 7]. Early-season ratings are noisy, and week 1 has no season observations [PAGE 7]. A model trained across seasons can therefore absorb era-specific coefficient drift, especially in the time-by-score interaction. Second, the paper warns that “One crucial point is that similar types of models need to be re-evaluated frequently” and that “The game changes rapidly both due to changes in the rules but also because of changes in players skills or even due to analytics” [PAGE 6]. That is a direct nonstationarity trap: a score differential that was safe under one rule regime or offensive style may be less safe under another, which would change the interaction coefficient \(2.88\) [PAGE 4]. Third, the Super Bowl anecdote is a selection-bias trap: a single high-profile comeback is not a calibration test, and the paper itself flags “selection bias” as part of the discussion [PAGE 1]. Fourth, the 70-30 split is a temporal-generalization trap unless it is grouped by game or season; the source does not specify grouping [PAGE 4]. A proper genealogy audit must therefore separate three claims: in-sample fit, game-level calibration, and season-forward calibration. The paper strongly supports the first two in its reported setup; the third requires an explicit temporal holdout.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is where the state surface becomes strategically reflexive. The paper defines the model as “the likelihood that a certain team will win a game given the current state of the game” [PAGE 1], but it also says such models “can provide the backbone for in-game decisions as well as, play call and personnel evaluations” [PAGE 1]. Once coaches condition decisions on the probability, the probability is no longer an exogenous summary of a fixed process; it becomes a feedback signal. The model has no explicit game-theoretic equilibrium, no hidden play-call type, and no opponent belief state. It therefore cannot distinguish three crisis modes: habit regression, counter-trap adaptation, and clock-resource optimization.

Crisis habit regression is the population-average pattern: trailing teams pass more and conserve clock, leading teams run more and burn clock, and timeouts are used to stop the clock. The model captures some of this through possession time, timeouts, down, yards-to-go, and time elapsed [PAGE 3]. But it estimates one coefficient per timeout: home timeouts \(0.06\) and away timeouts \(-0.06\) [PAGE 4]. The paper calls this “the symmetric impact of the number of timeouts left for the home and visiting team” [PAGE 3]. A constant log-odds shift per timeout is a strong misspecification if the value of a timeout depends on time remaining, score differential, possession, and down. The true timeout value is a state-dependent option value, not a fixed additive term. Counter-trap adaptation is the next layer: if the trailing team expects an aggressive defense, it may call a screen or play-action; if the leading team expects a run, it may call a safe play-action pass. The logistic model lacks play type, formation, personnel, and pre-snap motion, so it cannot represent the recursive belief “they think we will do X, therefore we do Y.” Clock optimization is the third layer: the paper’s own nonlinearity warning in Figure 1 says a linear time-yard relation explains less variance “towards the end of the half/game” [PAGE 2]. That is exactly where clock strategy dominates. The model’s time-by-score interaction \(1.41+2.88\cdot(\text{TimeLapsed})\) [PAGE 4] is a population-average clock effect, not a team-specific or opponent-adjusted policy effect. A verifiable counter-trap audit would add team-specific random effects for timeouts, a timeout-by-time-remaining interaction, and play-type covariates; if those terms improve game-level calibration, the fixed-coefficient crisis model is vetoed. The paper’s own caveat that overtime is poorly modeled because of sparse data [PAGE 6] is a narrow instance of this broader recursive-clock failure.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical veto is an omitted-variable problem. Suppose the true log-odds is
\[
\eta=w^\top x+\theta^\top z+\psi^\top(x\otimes z),
\]
where \(z\) contains weather, surface, injury, fatigue, and biomechanical state, and \(x\) is the paper’s state vector [PAGE 3]. If \(z\) is correlated with \(x\), the fitted coefficient obeys
\[
\mathbb{E}[\hat w]=w+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Z\theta
\]
under linear projection, so the reported coefficients are biased even if the logistic link is correct. The paper’s feature list contains no wind speed, precipitation, temperature, altitude, turf, injury report, quarterback pressure, receiver separation, or kicking biomechanics [PAGE 3]. That omission is not automatically fatal, but it becomes a veto when the omitted layer changes the mapping from score, field position, and time to win probability. Wind affects deep passing and kicking; rain affects grip and fumble probability; cold affects ball handling; injuries affect the latent team strength that the rating differential is supposed to summarize. The paper’s rating differential is only a points-based strength summary [PAGE 3, PAGE 7], not a personnel or biomechanical state.

The adjudication proof is stratified calibration. If the model is calibrated overall but a high-wind late-game stratum has calibration slope \(b_{\text{wind}}\) below \(0.9\) or Brier score more than \(0.03\) above the overall \(0.158\) [PAGE 5], then the fixed-coefficient surface is not transportable across the physical layer. The same test applies to precipitation, extreme temperature, and overtime: the paper already concedes that “During the OT the model does not perform very accurately due to the sparsity of the relevant data” [PAGE 6]. A weather-augmented or biomechanics-augmented model would be required if the stratified verifier improves by a pre-specified margin, for example a Brier improvement greater than \(0.01\) in the affected stratum. If no such improvement appears, the physical veto is not proven. The formal point is that average calibration is a marginal property; the cross-layer veto is a conditional-calibration property. The model can pass the marginal test and fail the physical-stratum test. The correct proof obligation is therefore not “add weather and see if it helps on average,” but “show that the residual calibration slope is stable across physical strata, and that the time-by-score interaction \(2.88\) [PAGE 4] does not reverse under wind, precipitation, or injury stress.”

In an RLVR-style audit, the verifier returns a scalar reward from a proper scoring rule on held-out data. Let the reward be \(R(M)=-\beta(M)\), where \(\beta(M)=N^{-1}\sum_i(\pi_i-y_i)^2\) is the Brier score [PAGE 5]. A veto is a pre-registered failure of the verifier under a counterfactual perturbation. Three verifiable scenarios follow.

Scenario 1: game-level temporal holdout versus play-level random split. The source says only that data were split “in a training and test set in a 70-30% proportion respectively” [PAGE 4]. Counterfactual: hold out entire seasons or entire games rather than individual snaps. Prediction: if the original split leaked within-game state, the reported Brier \(0.158\) [PAGE 5] rises materially, and the calibration slope moves away from \(0.98\) [PAGE 4]. Veto criterion: \(\Delta\beta\) greater than \(0.02\), or held-out calibration slope outside \([0.95,1.05]\). If the game-level holdout preserves Brier near \(0.158\) and slope near \(1\), the model survives this veto.

Scenario 2: physical-stratum calibration. Counterfactual: stratify held-out plays by wind speed, precipitation, temperature, and overtime, and fit the same calibration regression in each stratum. Prediction: high-wind and overtime strata show overconfidence for passing-dependent teams, because the model has no weather or biomechanical covariates [PAGE 3] and admits poor overtime performance [PAGE 6]. Veto criterion: in any pre-specified stratum, calibration slope below \(0.90\) or Brier increase greater than \(0.03\) over the overall \(0.158\), with a weather-augmented model improving the verifier by at least \(0.01\). If the weather-augmented model does not improve the verifier, the physical veto is rejected.

Scenario 3: strategic policy shift and performative drift. Counterfactual: simulate or reweight 4th-down and timeout policies so that coaches optimize against the model, then re-estimate the time-by-score interaction. Prediction: the coefficient \(2.88\) [PAGE 4] is nonstationary under policy shift, because the model is used for “in-game decisions” [PAGE 1] and the paper warns that the game changes “due to analytics” [PAGE 6]. Veto criterion: the re-estimated time-by-score coefficient shifts by more than one standard error, or held-out Brier exceeds \(0.17\), or calibration intercept exceeds \(0.03\). If the coefficient and calibration remain stable under the policy perturbation, the performative veto is not proven. These three scenarios are verifiable because each has a fixed data split, a fixed scoring rule, and a pre-registered threshold.
