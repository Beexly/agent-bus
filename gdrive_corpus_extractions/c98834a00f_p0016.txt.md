The critical result: If $\{A_n\}_{n=1}^\infty$ is a countable family of countable sets, then $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$ is countable. This relies on the axiom of countable choice. From (3): The real line $(\mathbb{R}, d)$ with Euclidean metric $d(x,y)=|x-y|$. Key properties:  
- Density of ℚ: For any $x<y$ in $\mathbb{R}$, $\exists q \in \mathbb{Q}$ such that $x<q<y$.  
- Open sets: Every open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is a countable union of disjoint open intervals: $U = \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty I_k$, where $I_k = (a_k, b_k)$, possibly infinite endpoints. This uses separability (existence of countable dense subset ℚ).  
- Heine-Borel Theorem: A subset $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is compact iff it is closed and bounded. Compactness enables reduction of infinite covers to finite ones, crucial for proving regularity of Lebesgue measure.

**4. Function Space Operations**  
From (4): A function $f: D \to \mathbb{R}$ (or $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$). Sums: $(f+g)(x) = f(x)+g(x)$; products: $(fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)$; constant multiples: $(cf)(x)=c f(x)$; absolute value: $|f|(x) = |f(x)|$. These form a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ when restricted to bounded functions, but more generally, they generate algebras of measurable functions.

**5. Order Completeness & Limit Superior/Inferior**  
From (5): For $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, $\sup A = \min \{ u \in \mathbb{R} : \forall a \in A, a \le u \}$ (if it exists); $\inf A$ analogously. For a sequence $(x_n)$, define:  
$$\limsup_{n\to\infty} x_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left( \sup_{k \ge n} x_k \right), \quad \liminf_{n\to\infty} x_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left( \inf_{k \ge n} x_k \right).$$  
These always exist in $\overline{\mathbb{R}}$. For a sequence of functions $(f_n)$, $\limsup f_n$ is the function $x \mapsto \limsup_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)$. This is essential for Fatou’s Lemma: $\int \liminf f_n \le \liminf \int f_n$ for non-negative measurable $f_n$.

**6. Extended Real Arithmetic**  
From (6): Define $\overline{\mathbb{R}} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty, +\infty\}$. For $x \in \mathbb{R}$:  
- $x + (+\infty) = +\infty$, $x \cdot (+\infty) = +\infty$ if $x>0$, $-\infty$ if $x<0$, $0$ if $x=0$.  
- $+\infty + (+\infty) = +\infty$, $(-\infty) + (-\infty) = -\infty$.  
- $+\infty \cdot (+\infty) = +\infty$, $(-\infty) \cdot (+\infty) = -\infty$ (with sign rules).  
Undefined expressions: $+\infty - \infty$, $0 \cdot \pm\infty$, $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$. This partial algebra is necessary to state that the integral of a non-negative function is always defined (possibly $+\infty$), avoiding division by zero issues in measure definitions.

**Singularity/Breakdown Regimes:**

- Countable additivity extends to finite additivity, but not vice versa; Vitali sets demonstrate non-measurable sets under AC.

Henri Lebesgue, in his 1902 thesis, introduced his revolutionary integral by measuring the range of a function rather than its domain, requiring a robust theory of measurable sets. This depended critically on the concept of countable unions (prerequisite 2) and the completeness of the real numbers (prerequisite 3, via Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences). The Heine-Borel theorem, proven around 1880s-1890s, provided the compactness tool needed to approximate measurable sets by simpler ones (intervals, finite unions).

Emil Artin and others formalized algebraic structures (prerequisite 1), while the systematic treatment of sequences and limits (prerequisites 4-5) was solidified by Bolzano, Weierstrass, and Cantor. The inclusion of ±∞ (prerequisite 6) emerged from the study of improper integrals and series divergence; it was formalized in the context of projective geometry and later adopted by measure theorists to handle infinite measures and integrals without special cases.

A multi-week trap arises if one views these prerequisites as static historical artifacts. In truth, they were forged in response to specific failures: Riemann integration fails for highly discontinuous functions; content (Jordan measure) is not countably additive. Measure theory’s power comes from embracing countable additivity (hence countable sets), order completeness (suprema/infima), and topological finiteness (Heine-Borel). Ignoring this genealogy leads to misunderstanding why, for example, σ-algebras are closed under countable unions—not because it’s convenient, but because it’s necessary to contain all Borel sets and allow limit operations essential for convergence theorems.
