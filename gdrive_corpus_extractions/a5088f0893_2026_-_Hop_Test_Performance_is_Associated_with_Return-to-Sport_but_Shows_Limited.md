1. Limb symmetry operator. For participant \(i\), task \(t\), injured limb \(x^{\mathrm{inj}}_{i,t}\), contralateral limb \(x^{\mathrm{con}}_{i,t}\):
\[
\mathrm{LSI}_{i,t}=100\,\frac{x^{\mathrm{inj}}_{i,t}}{x^{\mathrm{con}}_{i,t}}\quad [\%].
\]
Assumption: the contralateral limb is a valid reference. The source defines it as “injured limb performance ÷ contralateral limb performance ×100%” [PAGE 2]. Breakdown regime: \(x^{\mathrm{con}}_{i,t}\to 0\) makes \(\mathrm{LSI}_{i,t}\to\infty\) and the ratio undefined; a failed or unrecorded contralateral trial is a structural missing-data singularity, not a clinical result.

2. Threshold operator. \(E_i=\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{LSI}_{i,t}\ge 90]\). The 90 is percent, not a probability. A pass is \(E_i=1\). This operator discards all information above 90% and all continuous distance information below 90%; it is a coarse sufficient-statistic assumption.

3. Logit contrast operator. For a study \(k\) with 2x2 counts \(a_k\) passed and event, \(b_k\) passed and no event, \(c_k\) failed and event, \(d_k\) failed and no event:
\[
y_k=\ln\left(\frac{a_k d_k}{b_k c_k}\right),\qquad
v_k=\frac{1}{a_k}+\frac{1}{b_k}+\frac{1}{c_k}+\frac{1}{d_k}.
\]
\(y_k\) is dimensionless; \(v_k\) is the squared standard error. The paper’s reported statistic is \(\mathrm{OR}_k=\exp(y_k)\). Breakdown regime: any zero cell forces a continuity correction or an undefined \(v_k\); sparse-event studies are exactly where the THD reinjury estimate is least stable.

\[
y_k=\mu+u_k+e_k,\qquad u_k\sim N(0,\tau^2),\qquad e_k\sim N(0,v_k),
\]
\[
\hat\mu=\frac{\sum_k w_k y_k}{\sum_k w_k},\qquad w_k=\frac{1}{v_k+\hat\tau^2}.
\]
\(\mu\) is a pooled logit contrast; \(\tau^2\) is between-study variance in squared logit units. Assumption: exchangeable studies after weighting. Crisis habit regression appears when stakes rise: a playoff, a scholarship, or a contract creates pressure to clear the athlete, and the clinician regresses to the single most familiar number, LSI \(\ge 90\%\). The source’s evidence supports the habit’s appeal but not its sufficiency: SLHD and TCHD are associated with RTS, but the effect sizes are modest, heterogeneity is high, and publication-bias adjustment weakens SLHD. The counter-trap is more subtle. A test that predicts RTS can be misused as a reinjury shield. SLHD and TCHD passes do not lower reinjury in the pooled estimates; THD pass is actually associated with a small increase in reinjury (\(\mathrm{OR}=1.05\), 95% CI 1.01 to 1.09) [PAGE 3]. Thus the counter-trap is: “He passed the hop test, so he is safe,” when the correct statement is “He passed a readiness marker, but the reinjury signal is null or possibly adverse.” The clock trap is timing. Early postoperative testing measures recovery; late testing measures a different state; follow-up duration moderates the SLHD-RTS association. A clinician who clears at 9 months on a 6-month test is using a clock mismatch. The mitigation is recursive honesty: disclose uncertainty to the athlete and coach, separate RTS probability from reinjury protection, and require biomechanical and psychological inputs before clearance. The clinician must model the coach’s incentive and the athlete’s fear, not just the hop distance.

The physical layer veto is supported by graft type, meniscal status, and strength. The paper reports that the SLHD-RTS association is stronger in studies with HT autograft \(>90\%\) (\(\mathrm{OR}=1.64\), 95% CI 1.13 to 2.39, \(I^2=82\%\)) than in \(\le 90\%\) HT studies (\(\mathrm{OR}=1.11\), 95% CI 1.01 to 1.21, \(I^2=74\%\)) [PAGE 3], but it warns this is a population effect, not a direct causal graft effect. The meniscus subgroup is positive only without meniscal injury (\(\mathrm{OR}=1.40\), 95% CI 1.15 to 1.70, \(I^2=83\%\)) and null with meniscal injury (\(\mathrm{OR}=1.14\), 95% CI 0.98 to 1.34, \(I^2=70\%\)) [PAGE 3]. Therefore a physical veto is justified when there is concomitant meniscal injury, incomplete graft healing, or a strength deficit: the hop test’s discriminative value is attenuated exactly in the meniscal-injury stratum. The paper’s risk-of-bias findings, including uncontrolled confounders in 24 studies [PAGE 3], reinforce that physical variables cannot be assumed away.

The biomechanical layer veto is the strongest. The source states that traditional hop outcomes “primarily quantify overall performance rather than movement quality during the landing phase” [PAGE 4] and that LSI “may overestimate functional recovery because the contralateral limb also exhibited persistent neuromuscular deficits” [PAGE 4]. The paper also notes that noncontact ACL injury occurs as rapidly as 17 to 50 milliseconds after ground contact, and aberrant patterns such as excessive knee valgus, greater asymmetry in knee extensor moments, and decreased knee flexion angles may elevate ACL loading [PAGE 4]. Therefore, if an athlete has LSI \(\ge 90\%\) but persists with high knee valgus, asymmetric extensor moments, or reduced knee flexion on landing, the biomechanical veto must override the hop pass. This is not a speculative add-on; it follows logically from the measurement equation: LSI is a ratio of distance or time, not a landing-kinematics operator. A distance ratio cannot represent a moment asymmetry or a valgus angle.

The weather/environmental layer is not directly evidenced in this drive. The paper provides no temperature, surface, precipitation, or weather variable, so a weather-specific veto cannot be proved from the source. What the source does provide is environmental complexity: laboratory or clinical hop tests are “typically performed in predictable, single-plane conditions” and do not incorporate “opponent interaction,” “unplanned movement,” and “divided attention” [PAGE 4]. Those are sport-environment operators, not meteorological ones. The adjudication is therefore \(V_i=V_{\mathrm{phys},i}\lor V_{\mathrm{biomech},i}\lor V_{\mathrm{env},i}\), where \(V_{\mathrm{env}}\) is admitted only for verified sport-context complexity or external weather/surface data; a weather veto without such data is rejected as unsupported. If any veto is true, no clearance despite \(E_i=1\). If all vetoes are false, LSI remains one input. This is the only adjudication consistent with the paper’s conclusion that hop tests should be “part of a broader, multifactorial RTS assessment framework” [PAGE 5].
