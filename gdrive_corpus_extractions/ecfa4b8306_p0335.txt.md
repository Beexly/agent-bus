<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression: under stress or anxiety, an organism may regress to an earlier level of the habit hierarchy, effectively reducing P_eff,n even after prior reinforcements have raised it. From the modeler’s perspective, this looks like a sudden loss of conditioned control, but in Hullian terms it is a temporary collapse of effective reaction potential below θ due to a shift in the central tendency or variance of the depressing field. The regression is “recursive” because the subject’s history of reinforcement determines the habit hierarchy, the current crisis determines which habit is expressed, and the expression (or failure to express) feeds back into the experimenter’s estimate of habit strength.

Counter-trap: the observer sees non-evocation after several reinforcements and infers “no learning has occurred” or “extinction has begun.” The counter-trap is that non-evocation may be entirely explained by D(t) momentarily exceeding the superthreshold margin \(P_{\text{eff},n} - \theta\). Because D(t) can neutralize up to 50 wats, a superthreshold potential of only 30 wats can be suppressed on a given trial. Concluding “no learning” from a single omission is a Type I error under Hull’s own oscillation principle.

Clock: the passage’s oscillation operates “from moment to moment,” but experiments are usually organized in blocks, sessions, and weeks. There is a confound between trial-number (learning) and clock time (circadian/ultradian fluctuation in μ_D or σ_D). A decline in evocation probability late in a session may reflect rising σ_D from fatigue rather than any failure of reinforcement to increment P_eff. Conversely, a Monday-to-Friday improvement may reflect reduced environmental noise rather than increased habit strength.

Recursive theory of mind: the experimenter constructs a mental model of the subject (“the subject expects food,” “the subject has learned the cue”) based on observed evocations. The subject’s expectancy, in turn, may modulate arousal and therefore D(t), altering future evocation probability. The observer then updates the mental model again. Because D(t) is Gaussian and only partially observable, the subject’s internal state is underdetermined by behavior; any attribution of “expectancy” to the subject is a second-order inference resting on a first-order stochastic gate that the observer cannot directly measure.

Scenario 1: Deterministic-threshold violation. Counterfactual setup: after the first reinforcement, the measured effective reaction potential exceeds threshold by exactly 30 wats (\(P_{\text{eff},1}-\theta = 30\) wats). With σ_D = 10 wats and μ_D unspecified, the Gaussian oscillation model predicts a non-degenerate probability of omission on any single trial; a deterministic model would predict evocation on every trial. Veto condition: if 100 test trials show zero omissions (100% evocation) under fixed P_eff and θ, the stochastic oscillation layer is vetoed; if omissions occur at a rate inconsistent with \(\Phi((30-\mu_D)/10)\) at p < 0.01, the parameterization is vetoed. Prediction: omission events will be distributed trial-to-trial with a variance matching a Bernoulli process whose probability is set by the Gaussian CDF; the latency of successful evocations will show a conditional distribution truncated by the depression variable.

Scenario 2: Slope-only determinism confound. Counterfactual setup: two groups are yoked to identical learning-curve slopes (\(s = \Delta P_{\text{eff}}/\Delta n\) equal within measurement error) but differ in arousal state such that σ_D = 10 wats in Group A and σ_D = 20 wats in Group B. Corollary I states N_evoke is a decreasing function of slope alone. Veto condition: if Group B requires significantly more reinforcements to reach first evocation than Group A despite equal s (e.g., >30% difference in mean N_evoke, p < 0.05), the slope-only claim is vetoed.
