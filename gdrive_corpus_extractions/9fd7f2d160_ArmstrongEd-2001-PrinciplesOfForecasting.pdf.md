No specific forecast target was supplied, so I audit the forecasting operators in the Armstrong handbook rather than inventing an event forecast. The source is a principles handbook: its claims are conditional prescriptions, not unconditional laws. The first multi-hop chain is: (1) identify the forecasting situation, (2) match method to condition, (3) require ex ante validation, (4) combine diverse valid forecasts when method uncertainty is high, and (5) evaluate with reliable error measures. For example, high uncertainty about a numerical quantity triggers decomposition [PAGE 127 of 862]; high volatility or outliers in analogous series triggers Bayesian pooling [PAGE 217 of 862]; uncertainty about which method is best triggers combining [PAGE 430 of 862]; and unreliability in judgment triggers mechanical processing, cue limitation, and justification [PAGE 106 of 862]. These are not independent tips; they form an evidence-gated pipeline from condition to operator to validation.

The gray-zone audit has several layers. First, ex post versus ex ante: the source repeatedly warns that fitting historical data is not the same as forecasting future data. This is why the handbook insists on out-of-sample or holdout tests and warns that “complexity improves the ability to fit historical data ... but often harms forecast accuracy” [PAGE 388 of 862]. Second, retrospective versus prospective evidence: role-playing evidence is promising, but many studies are retrospective, and “researchers may choose interesting situations that would be surprising to experts” [PAGE 34 of 862]. That creates selection and hindsight contamination. Third, publication bias: conjoint results are explicitly described as favoring “positive results” over negative results [PAGE 169 of 862]. Fourth, expert reliability: physicians, teachers, clinical psychologists, grain inspectors, and weather forecasters “have been shown to be less than perfectly reliable judges” [PAGE 100 of 862]. Thus expertise alone does not veto unreliability. Fifth, causal-variable forecastability: a causal variable is not useful merely because theory says it matters; if its forecast error is too large, including it can worsen forecasts [PAGE 322 of 862]. Sixth, the breakdown regimes are mathematical and structural: coefficient-of-variation blows up near zero means; Manski bounds can be so wide as to be operationally useless; pooling fails if analogous groups diverge; lens-model reliability caps accuracy; and combining can preserve zero or negative accuracy. The audit therefore does not ask “is this method sophisticated?” It asks “is the condition matched, is the evidence ex ante, is the causal input forecastable, and is the output within physical and logical feasibility?”
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The handbook’s most portable operators are formal enough to state with units and breakdown regimes.

1. Volatility gate for pooling. For a non-trending, non-seasonal series \(x_t\), the coefficient of variation is
\[
\mathrm{CV}(x)=\frac{s_x}{\bar{x}},\qquad [\mathrm{CV}]=1,
\]
where \(s_x\) is the sample standard deviation and \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean. For trend or seasonality, \(s_x\) is replaced by the estimated standard error of an appropriate time-series model. Assumptions: ratio-scale data, non-zero mean, no dominant trend or seasonality when using the simple form. Breakdown: \(\bar{x}\to 0\), zeros, trend, or seasonality; \(\mathrm{CV}\) then becomes unstable or misleading. The source states: “High values for this index indicate volatility and imprecise estimates about the trend line” [PAGE 211 of 862]. This gate supports Bayesian pooling when volatility is high, but pooling is vetoed if the equivalence group is not exchangeable: “If grouped series diverge during forecast periods, then pooled forecast models may yield worse forecasts than conventional, single-series models” [PAGE 214 of 862].

2. Bayesian shrinkage operator. For a local parameter estimate \(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{local}}\) and an analogous-group estimate \(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{group}}\),
\[
\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{pooled}}
=
\lambda\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{local}}
+
(1-\lambda)\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{group}},
\qquad
\lambda\in[0,1],
\]
with \([\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{pooled}}]=[\theta]\). Assumptions: the group is an equivalence group, errors are approximately conditionally independent in a hierarchical model, and \(\lambda\) reflects relative parameter uncertainty. Breakdown: non-comoving groups, structural breaks, outlier-driven variance estimates, or \(\lambda\) estimated from too few series. The handbook describes pooling as “borrowing strength from neighbors” and as useful for noisy series [PAGE 217 of 862].

3. Exponential smoothing as a level update. For observations \(x_t\),
\[
\ell_t=\alpha x_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1},
\qquad
0\le\alpha\le1,
\qquad
[\ell_t]=[x_t].
\]
The weights on older observations decay geometrically. Assumptions: stable level process; if trend exists, add a trend update; if seasonality exists, deseasonalize or add seasonal terms. Breakdown: \(\alpha=0\) yields no adaptation; \(\alpha=1\) reduces to a random-walk-like last-value rule; structural breaks, outliers, or large measurement errors make the smoothed level lag. The source notes that “the higher the factor, the heavier the weight” placed on recent data [PAGE 241 of 862].

4. Lens-model reliability cap. In the lens-model equation,
\[
r_{Y_sY_e}
=
G R_s R_e
+
C\sqrt{1-R_s^2}\sqrt{1-R_e^2},
\]
all terms are dimensionless correlations or correlation-like components. Here \(Y_s\) is the judgment, \(Y_e\) is the environmental criterion, \(G\) is the matching component, \(R_s\) and \(R_e\) are reliability and predictability terms, and \(C\) captures unmodeled matching. Assumptions: a linear cue model adequately captures the judgment policy and the sample is stable enough to estimate the components. Breakdown: nonlinear cue use, unknown cues, tiny samples, or poor model capture; then \(R_s\) is not a clean reliability estimate. The handbook states: “the square root of judgmental reliability places an upper bound on accuracy” [PAGE 112 of 862].

5. Intentions bounds. For binary intentions with intended proportion \(p\) and misclassification allowance \(w\),
\[
L=wp,
\qquad
U=p+(1-p)w,
\qquad
p,w\in[0,1],
\]
so \([L]=[U]=1\) as probabilities. With \(p=0.15\) and \(w=0.5\), the source gives “between 7.5 and 57.5 percent of respondents will actually purchase a car” [PAGE 58 of 862]. Assumptions: representative sample, binary intention scale, stable \(w\), no measurement-induced behavior change. Breakdown: \(w\) unknown, non-binary scales, new products, impulse purchases, socially desirable behavior, or recall telescoping. The source itself warns that the bound’s usefulness “is questionable since the range is so wide” [PAGE 58 of 862].

6. Combination operator. For \(k\) forecasts,
\[
\hat{y}_c=\sum_{i=1}^k w_i\hat{y}_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^k w_i=1,
\qquad
[\hat{y}_c]=[y].
\]
Equal weights \(w_i=1/k\) are a reasonable starting point; a trimmed mean is useful for five or more forecasts. Assumptions: component forecasts are valid and draw from different information sources. Breakdown: “If forecasts with zero accuracy are combined, the result will have zero accuracy. If forecasts with negative accuracy are combined, the results will have even greater negative accuracy” [PAGE 109 of 862]. Thus combining is not a universal accuracy machine; it is a variance-and-bias reduction operator conditional on valid, diverse components. The handbook recommends: “combine forecasts derived from methods that differ substantially and draw from different sources of information” [PAGE 430 of 862].

7. Causal-variable forecastability veto. For a stationary causal variable \(x\) and a forecast \(\hat{x}_{t+h}\), Ashley’s theorem implies that, under known coefficient assumptions,
\[
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{x}_{t+h})>\mathrm{Var}(x_t)
\;\Rightarrow\;
\text{including }\hat{x}_{t+h}\text{ worsens forecasts versus omitting it.}
\]
Units are squared units of \(x\). Assumptions: stationarity, known coefficient, and the stated loss. Breakdown: estimated coefficients, nonstationarity, structural breaks, or nonlinear relations. The source states: “if the mean squared error (MSE) of the forecast of the causal variable exceeds its variance, then including the forecasted causal variable will produce worse forecasts than omitting it” [PAGE 322 of 862].

8. Damped seasonal factors. For multiplicative seasonal factor \(f_{\mathrm{mult}}\) and damping parameter \(\phi\),
\[
f'_{\mathrm{mult}}=1+\phi(f_{\mathrm{mult}}-1),
\qquad
\phi\in[0,1],
\qquad
[f'_{\mathrm{mult}}]=1.
\]
Assumptions: ratio-scale data not near zero, stable-enough seasonal pattern, and meaningful uncertainty about the historical seasonal estimate. Breakdown: additive seasonality, observations near zero, long horizons, or unstable seasonal regimes. The source advises “drawing them in toward 1.0” when seasonal uncertainty is present [PAGE 238 of 862].

9. Decomposition gate. A practical decision rule from the source is: if the target is highly uncertain, if the upper quartile geometric mean “has seven or more digits,” then decomposition should be considered [PAGE 127 of 862]. The formal condition is a digit-and-spread test, not a fake ratio:
\[
D=1
\quad\text{when}\quad
\operatorname{Digits}(\tilde{\mu}_{0.75})\ge 7
\ \text{or uncertainty rating exceeds the midpoint},
\]
then compare interquartile ranges of global versus decomposed estimates. Assumptions: component estimates are more accurate or more confident than the global estimate. Breakdown: low-uncertainty tasks, poor component estimates, or no decomposition theory. The source reports that decomposition improved accuracy by “a factor of approximately 5.0” across almanac-type problems [PAGE 124 of 862], while low-uncertainty decomposition could be worse than global estimation [PAGE 130 of 862].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is a chain of adversarial checks. Laplace and Galton supply the early logic of combining independent evidence; the handbook notes that Laplace in 1818 claimed combining can yield a “probability law of error” that decreases more rapidly, and Galton’s composite portraits were “better looking than their components” because averaging removed idiosyncrasy [PAGE 430 of 862]. The RAND Delphi procedure formalized anonymous, iterative, controlled feedback for expert aggregation [PAGE 139 of 862]. The M-Competition and its replications made ex ante time-series comparison a public discipline; the handbook says the M2 and M3 replications were “consistent with those from the initial M-Competition” [PAGE 453 of 862]. Brunswick, Hammond, Tucker, and Stewart converted judgment into a lens-model decomposition with reliability and predictability components [PAGE 112 of 862]. Juster and Manski turned intentions into probability and bounds problems, with explicit attention to non-intenders and response bias [PAGE 49 of 862 and PAGE 58 of 862]. Dawes, Meehl, and bootstrapping research converted expert rules into regression-like models and repeatedly found that simple models can outperform unaided judgment [PAGE 184 of 862]. Role-playing has a military and legal genealogy, including German war planning in 1929 and commercial jury-trial use, but the handbook also warns that legal role-playing accuracy “has not been evaluated” [PAGE 34 of 862].

The multi-week traps are the long-horizon failure modes. Hindsight bias makes forecasters “recall their forecasts as closer to the outcome than they actually were” [PAGE 76 of 862]. Confirmation bias makes record search one-sided, so forecast records are used to confirm rather than falsify hypotheses [PAGE 76 of 862]. Self-fulfilling prophecy corrupts feedback because “a forecast may lead to an action that results in the forecast being met” [PAGE 79 of 862]. Retrospective role-playing and disguised historical cases can be contaminated by selecting surprising cases [PAGE 34 of 862]. Publication bias favors positive conjoint validation and can inflate apparent method quality [PAGE 169 of 862]. Complexity overfits history and harms ex ante accuracy [PAGE 388 of 862]. Data cleaning and measurement-error traps persist: the Solow “structural shift” example was traced to arithmetic mistakes, not structure [PAGE 235 of 862]. Over a multi-week or multi-quarter horizon, structural breaks, outlier contamination, and changing causal-variable forecastability accumulate; this is why the source repeatedly prefers simple robust models, out-of-sample tests, and combining under uncertainty.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive-theory-of-mind layer separates three failure patterns. First, crisis habit regression: under time pressure or high uncertainty, forecasters regress to unaided judgment, anchoring on the last observation and making insufficient adjustment. The source states that “people use anchor-and-adjust heuristics to make forecasts” and “usually make insufficient adjustment” [PAGE 85 of 862]. Crisis does not create reliability; it exposes it. The source lists physicians, teachers, clinical psychologists, grain inspectors, and weather forecasters as less than perfectly reliable judges [PAGE 100 of 862]. The practical countermeasure is not “try harder”; it is structure: limit cues, mechanize processing, require justification, and combine independent forecasts [PAGE 106 of 862].

Second, the counter-trap: the forecaster notices expert bias and simply reverses the expert consensus. That is unsupported unless there is evidence of negative accuracy or systematic bias. The source warns that combining forecasts with zero accuracy yields zero accuracy and combining negative-accuracy forecasts yields greater negative accuracy [PAGE 109 of 862]. A counter-trap also appears in recursive social prediction: people “often assume that others will respond as they themselves do” [PAGE 40 of 862], so an analyst may model the opponent as a mirror rather than as an independent agent. Role-playing can help because it simulates cycles of action and reaction, but it is contaminated if players know the outcome or if roles are unrealistic [PAGE 40 of 862]. Self-fulfilling prophecy adds another recursion: the forecast changes the behavior being forecast [PAGE 79 of 862].

Third, the clock: forecast horizon and feedback timing determine whether judgment can learn. Delphi is designed around the clock: anonymous rounds, controlled feedback, and stopping when responses stabilize; “Generally, three structured rounds is enough” [PAGE 154 of 862]. Combining gains increase with horizon because “uncertainty increases with the forecast horizon” [PAGE 439 of 862]. Forecast records and cognitive feedback can reduce hindsight and confirmation bias, but only if records are kept before outcomes are known [PAGE 76 of 862]. The correct recursive protocol is therefore: independent first-pass judgments, controlled disclosure of reasons, explicit time-stamped records, and a stability clock that stops iteration when the panel stops changing. This avoids both crisis regression to habit and the counter-trap of automatic inversion.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A forecast must pass feasibility before it passes accuracy. Physical veto: for non-negative quantities such as demand, sales, population, or counts, the feasible set is \(y_t\ge0\). For capacity-constrained systems, \(y_t\le K\). The source treats intermittent series as “a non-negative series that contains zeros” [PAGE 235 of 862], so negative demand forecasts are not merely inaccurate; they are category errors. Multiplicative seasonal factors are valid only for ratio-scaled data not near zero; otherwise additive factors or another model is required [PAGE 238 of 862]. If a forecast violates these bounds, the accuracy score is irrelevant; the forecast is vetoed.

Biomechanical veto: human-performance and personnel-selection forecasts must respect validated performance criteria and reliability limits. The handbook reports that interview validities were low, with a correlation “about .06” for individual predictions in one study [PAGE 193 of 862]. Therefore a forecast of job success that relies on unstructured interview impressions is vetoed unless it is supported by job-sample tests, structured criteria, or reliable performance feedback. The same logic applies to any biomechanical or behavioral claim: reliability is not optional, and “the square root of judgmental reliability places an upper bound on accuracy” [PAGE 112 of 862]. If reliability is low, high apparent accuracy is suspect.

Weather veto: weather inputs are admissible only if they are forecastable at the relevant horizon. Ashley’s theorem gives a sharp veto: if the mean squared error of the causal-variable forecast exceeds its variance, including that forecasted variable worsens forecasts [PAGE 322 of 862]. Thus a temperature, rainfall, or wind variable may be theoretically causal and still be vetoed operationally. The handbook also notes that weather forecasters are less than perfectly reliable judges [PAGE 100 of 862], that an expert system for hail forecasting had mixed performance relative to experts [PAGE 294 of 862], and that short-term weather forecasts may require “six or seven models” in combination [PAGE 433 of 862]. The cross-layer proof is therefore: define the feasible set \(\mathcal{F}\) from physical, biomechanical, and meteorological constraints; veto any \(\hat{y}\notin\mathcal{F}\); then apply the causal-forecastability test \(\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{x})\le\mathrm{Var}(x)\) before admitting a causal variable; then apply the reliability cap. Only forecasts surviving all three gates should be scored for accuracy.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Intentions-adjustment veto. Hold out a representative panel with binary purchase intentions, measure actual purchase at \(t+1\), and compare unadjusted intention share against historical-conversion-adjusted intentions. Prediction from the source: adjusted average absolute percent error is about 9.7 percent versus 17.2 percent unadjusted [PAGE 55 of 862]. RLVR rule: reward the adjusted method only if it beats unadjusted on the pre-registered holdout; if unadjusted wins by a meaningful margin, veto the adjustment principle for that population. This is verifiable because the outcome is observed after the forecast horizon.

Scenario 2: Decomposition veto. Pre-register high-uncertainty estimation tasks whose upper-quartile geometric mean has seven or more digits, then randomize estimators to global versus algorithmic decomposition. Prediction: decomposition reduces median error ratio substantially, with the source reporting an improvement “by a factor of approximately 5.0” across almanac-type problems [PAGE 124 of 862]. Counterfactual check: for a low-uncertainty problem, global estimation should not be worse than decomposition; the source reports that low-uncertainty decomposition was less accurate than global estimation [PAGE 130 of 862]. RLVR rule: reward decomposition only when the uncertainty gate is satisfied and component estimates are more accurate or more confident; veto if decomposition helps only by overfitting or fails the high-uncertainty holdout.

Scenario 3: Role-playing versus unaided-expert veto. Pre-register a large-change, two-party conflict with disguised historical or prospective outcomes, matched role descriptions, independent role-play sessions, and unaided expert forecasts. Prediction: role playing should beat unaided expert opinion; the source reports that “role playing was correct for 56 percent of 143 predictions, while unaided expert opinions were correct for 16 percent of 172 predictions” [PAGE 28 of 862]. RLVR rule: reward role-playing only if it wins under matched roles and large-change conditions; veto the claim if unaided experts beat role-playing on the same pre-registered cases. An additional combining veto can be attached: equal-weight combination of at least five diverse valid forecasts should reduce average error, with the source reporting average ex ante error reduction of about 12.5 percent in 30 comparisons [PAGE 430 of 862]; if the combined forecast is worse than the average component without evidence of negative accuracy or systematic common bias, the combination operator is vetoed for that data regime.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
