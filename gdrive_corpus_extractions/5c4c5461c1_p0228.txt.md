Crisis habit regression vs counter-trap vs clock.

The theory-of-mind structure is recursive because the producer, the insurer, and the consumer each hold beliefs about p and about each other's beliefs. Let \theta_i be agent i's estimate of the burst probability. Under measurable risk, all agents share a common prior or converge on the same p through pooling [PAGE 1]. Under Knightian uncertainty, \theta_{\text{producer}} \neq \theta_{\text{insurer}} necessarily, and there is no objective arbiter to collapse the disagreement.

Level-k reasoning: Level-0 agents act on habit, treating last period's observed loss rate as a fixed cost and passing it to the consumer [PAGE 1]. Level-1 agents model the producer's estimate of p. Level-2 agents model the producer's model of the insurer's premium-setting rule. Under time pressure, the maximum feasible recursion depth k_{\max} is bounded by the physical clock:
k_{\max}(t) = \max\{k \in \mathbb{N}_0 : t_{\text{remain}} \ge k \cdot \tau_{\text{step}}\},
where t_{\text{remain}} is the time before the bottling run or insurance binder must be finalized (seconds), and \tau_{\text{step}} is the cognitive operation cycle per level (seconds). As the clock approaches the deadline, k_{\max} \to 0 or 1.

Crisis habit regression. When a crisis hits—e.g., a sudden spike in bottle breakage—the decision-maker's available deliberation time collapses. The agent regresses to Level-0: the loss is again treated as a fixed cost based on the most recent frequency, ignoring parameter uncertainty and the possibility that p itself has shifted. This is a regression because the agent abandons the higher-order model ("the insurer thinks p is rising") for a simple heuristic ("last week's rate is this week's fixed cost").

Counter-trap. A sophisticated counterparty can exploit this regression. If the insurer knows the producer will regress to Level-0 under clock pressure, the insurer may introduce ambiguity silently (e.g., switching glass lots without notice, altering internal pressure specifications) so that the producer's fixed-cost pass-through is based on a stale p. The producer pays for what it believes is measurable risk but is actually unmeasurable uncertainty. The counter-trap is profitable precisely because Knight's distinction [PAGE 1] is not operationally salient to a Level-0 regressor.

Clock interaction. Knight notes that constancy requires "a sufficiently large number of cases ... in a sufficiently short period of time" [PAGE 1]. The bottling line imposes a physical clock v (bottles per second). If line speed increases, the time available to observe and classify each batch shrinks, \tau_{\text{obs}} = 1/v, reducing k_{\max}. The system therefore drives itself toward the regression regime exactly when volume is highest and the fixed-cost assumption is most economically consequential.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical/weather veto proof.

Three physical layers must be adjudicated before Knight's risk calculus can be deployed. If any layer returns a veto, the system defaults to a Knightian uncertainty protocol rather than a fixed-cost insurance premium.

Layer 1 — Physical (fracture mechanics). Bottle rupture probability p depends on temperature T (Kelvin) and internal gauge pressure P (Pascal). A Weibull-type failure model gives
p(T,P) = 1 - \exp\left[-\left(\frac{P}{P_0(T)}\right)^m\right],
where P_0(T) is the characteristic strength and m is the Weibull modulus (dimensionless). Because P_0 varies with T, \partial p / \partial T \neq 0 generally. The veto predicate is
V_{\text{phys}} = \mathbf{1}\left\{ \left| \frac{dp}{dT} \right| \Delta T_{\text{day}} > \eta_p \right\},
where \Delta T_{\text{day}} is the diurnal temperature swing (K) and \eta_p is the maximum tolerable drift in p (dimensionless). If V_{\text{phys}} = 1, the proportion is not "practically constant" [PAGE 1] and pooling over time is invalid.

Layer 2 — Biomechanical (human inspection). Human inspectors remove defective bottles before filling with detection probability q \in [0,1]. Fatigue degrades q over shift duration \tau (seconds) according to
q(\tau) = q_0 e^{-\lambda \tau}, \quad \lambda \text{ in s}^{-1}.
The effective observed burst rate entering the cost accounts is p_{\text{obs}} = p(1 - q(\tau)). The veto predicate is
V_{\text{bio}} = \mathbf{1}\{ \tau > \tau_{\text{crit}} \}, \qquad \tau_{\text{crit}} = \frac{1}{\lambda} \ln\left(\frac{q_0}{q_{\min}}\right),
where q_{\min} is the minimum acceptable detection probability. Beyond \tau_{\text{crit}}, the measured proportion is biased and cannot serve as the basis for a fixed cost.

Layer 3 — Weather (common-shock correlation). Ambient weather induces correlation \rho across bottles in the same production batch. The variance of aggregate loss is
\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n p(1-p)c^2 + n(n-1)\rho\, p(1-p)c^2.
The pooling benefit exists only if \rho < \rho_c = 1/n; at \rho = \rho_c the variance scales as O(n^2) rather than O(n), and the law of large numbers fails to suppress idiosyncratic risk. The veto predicate is
V_{\text{weather}} = \mathbf{1}\{ \hat{\rho} \ge \rho_c \}.
Weather measurements (ambient temperature in K, relative humidity dimensionless) enter through \hat{\rho} = \hat{\rho}(T_{\text{amb}}, H).

Adjudication rule. The risk model is admitted iff
V_{\text{phys}} \land V_{\text{bio}} \land V_{\text{weather}} = 0.
If any veto is active, the contingency cannot be converted to a fixed cost by the mechanism Knight describes [PAGE 1]; the decision falls under genuine uncertainty, requiring robust control, maxmin criteria, or non-pooling contractual arrangements. No betting lines, spreads, or odds are invoked; the analysis is strictly actuarial and physical.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning via verifiable rewards) counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable conditions and predictions.

Scenario 1 — Small-sample fixed-cost veto.
Counterfactual: A boutique champagne producer fills n = 40 bottles per day, well below the scale required for constancy [PAGE 1]. Historical rupture probability is estimated at p = 0.02. Can the producer treat daily breakage loss as a fixed cost?
Verifiable condition: Over m = 30 production days, record daily loss L_d. Compute sample variance s^2. The binomial bound predicts Var(L_d) = n p(1-p)c^2. Veto triggers if
s^2 > 4 \, n p(1-p)c^2,
i.e., observed dispersion exceeds the theoretical variance by a factor of 4, rejecting the fixed-cost concentration assumption at a conservative level.
Prediction: If vetoed, the producer's unit cost volatility exceeds the pooled industry benchmark by more than 100%, forcing either absorption of variable loss or withdrawal from small-batch production. The fixed-cost pass-through to consumers [PAGE 1] fails empirically.

Scenario 2 — Knightian ambiguity veto (unknown p).
Counterfactual: A new bottle supplier introduces a different glass formulation with no rupture history. The insurer cannot assign a numerical p; the event is contingent but its probability is not known [PAGE 1].
Verifiable condition: After m = 100 trial bottles, compute the Shannon entropy of the posterior over p under a Beta(1,1) prior:
H(p|data) = -\int_0^1 \pi(p|x) \ln \pi(p|x) \, dp \quad \text{(bits)}.
Veto triggers if H(p|data) > 0.9 bits (near the maximum of 1 bit for a Bernoulli parameter), indicating the data have not identified p.
Prediction: The insurer either declines coverage or quotes a premium exceeding expected loss by an ambiguity factor > 2. A market gap emerges: measurable risk is insurable; this ambiguity is not. This is the operational signature of Knight's distinction between known numerical probability and contingent inference [PAGE 1].

Scenario 3 — Weather-correlation veto.
Counterfactual: During a heat wave, ambient temperature T_{\text{amb}} exceeds 300 K, raising glass failure probability and inducing positive intra-pallet correlation \hat{\rho} across bottles.
Verifiable condition: Measure burst outcomes across n_{\text{pallet}} = 600 bottles per pallet over 20 pallets during the heat wave. Estimate the intraclass correlation \hat{\rho}. Veto triggers if
\hat{\rho} \ge \frac{1}{n_{\text{pallet}}} \approx 1.67 \times 10^{-3}.
Prediction: The aggregate loss distribution develops fat tails; the variance scales as O(n^2), invalidating the insurance pooling premise. The premium calculated under independence underprices risk by a factor proportional to 1 + (n_{\text{pallet}}-1)\hat{\rho}. The insurer either becomes insolvent under the pooled rate or raises rates discontinuously in the following week. This is a direct physical instantiation of the breakdown of "practically constant" proportion [PAGE 1] under common environmental shock.

<THINK_TRACE>
Deep multi-hop derivation & gray-zone audit.

Hop 1: Foundational conditional. Knight stipulates that when an outcome is merely contingent but its numerical probability is known, conduct can be ordered intelligently [PAGE 1]. This is the boundary between risk and genuine uncertainty: a known p in [0,1] permits an expected-loss calculus; an unknown or non-measurable p does not. The first logical hop is therefore the conversion of a contingent proposition into a decision-theoretic object via a probability measure.

Hop 2: The champagne-bottle example. Knight argues that bursting bottles do not introduce uncertainty into champagne production because a practically constant and known proportion bursts [PAGE 1]. Whether the proportion is large or small is immaterial; the loss becomes a fixed cost passed to the consumer like labor or materials [PAGE 1]. This is an application of the law of large numbers at the level of a single producer only if that producer handles a sufficiently large number of cases within a sufficiently short period to secure constancy [PAGE 1].

Hop 3: Pooling and insurance. When a single producer lacks scale, an organization spanning many producers can realize the same result [PAGE 1]. This is explicitly identified as the principle of insurance, illustrated by fire loss [PAGE 1]. The mechanism is the extension of the base across many independent exposures so that the contingency is converted into a fixed cost [PAGE 1]. The multi-hop implication is that risk is, operationally, that which can be socialized through pooling because its frequency stabilizes.

Hop 4: The breakdown condition. Knight immediately reverses direction: practical difficulties are enormously increased where the inference is contingent rather than positive [PAGE 1]. Establishing an association between predicates is difficult even when universal, and is never done with accuracy except in critical cases [PAGE 1]. The gray zone is the transition from "known numerical probability" to "contingent inference without known probability." Here the insurance mechanism loses its foundation because pooling cannot stabilize a parameter that is not identified or is non-stationary.

Gray-zone audit. The text implies an objective, stable proportion but does not specify the epistemic source of the "known numerical probability." Is it a long-run frequency, a subjective degree of belief, or a physical propensity? This under-specification is the exact locus later occupied by Keynes's "weight of evidence," Knight's own uncertainty, and modern ambiguity theory. A secondary gray zone is the qualifier "sufficiently short period of time": constancy requires stationarity over the observation window, but the text gives no stopping rule or convergence criterion. The singularity regime is therefore triadic: (i) n too small for the sample mean to concentrate; (ii) p unknown so no unique expected loss exists; (iii) dependence across units so that pooling does not reduce variance at the O(1/sqrt(n)) rate. Where any holds, the fixed-cost conversion collapses and conduct can no longer be ordered intelligently by the risk calculus alone. This audit confirms that Knight's insight is not merely definitional but operational: the boundary between risk and uncertainty is a boundary of computational tractability for the decision-maker, not merely a metaphysical distinction.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal LaTeX, units, page citations, and breakdown regimes.

Let the probability space be (\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}). Define the indicator random variable for bottle i bursting as
I_i = \mathbf{1}\{E_i\}, \qquad I_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p), \quad p \in [0,1],
where p is dimensionless and E_i is the event of rupture. The loss per bottle in currency units (USD) is
L_i = c \cdot I_i,
with c the fixed replacement cost per bottle (contents plus glass), measured in USD. The expected loss per bottle and its variance are
\mathbb{E}[L_i] = p c, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(L_i) = p(1-p)c^2.

For n bottles produced by a single firm, assuming independence and identical distribution (i.i.d.), the aggregate loss is S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n L_i, with
\mathbb{E}[S_n] = n p c, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n p(1-p)c^2.
The weak law of large numbers gives
\frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{p} p c \quad \text{as } n \to \infty,
with standard error
\sigma_{\bar{L}} = c \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.
Chebyshev's inequality provides the finite-sample concentration bound
\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{S_n}{n} - p c\right| \ge \epsilon\right) \le \frac{p(1-p)}{n \epsilon^2}.

When N producers are pooled into an insurance association, total loss S = \sum_{j=1}^N S_j and the actuarially fair premium per unit exposure is
\pi = \frac{\mathbb{E}[S]}{\text{exposure}} + \lambda,
where \lambda \ge 0 is a loading factor (dimensionless or USD per unit) covering administrative cost and residual risk. Knight's fixed-cost conversion corresponds to the limit where the variance term vanishes relative to the mean, so the loss is predictable and passed on to the consumer [PAGE 1].

Assumptions: (A1) p is known and stationary over the observation window t \in [0,T] (T in seconds); (A2) failures are mutually independent or weakly dependent with \rho_{ij} \to 0 as |i-j| \to \infty; (A3) c is constant; (A4) n is large enough that n^{-1/2} terms are negligible for the decision horizon. Units: p dimensionless; c in USD; n dimensionless count; \epsilon in USD per bottle; T in seconds.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. Small-sample singularity: if n < n_{\min} = p(1-p)/(\delta \epsilon^2) for confidence 1-\delta, the Chebyshev bound does not guarantee concentration; the fixed-cost assumption fails.
2. Parameter-identification singularity: if p \notin [0,1] is known but instead belongs to an identifiable set \mathcal{P} (Knightian uncertainty), then \mathbb{E}[L] is not unique; the maxmin criterion \min_{P\in\mathcal{P}} \mathbb{E}_P[L] applies, and standard pooling premiums are incoherent.
3. Dependence singularity: if a common shock induces pairwise correlation \rho > 0, then \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n p(1-p)c^2 + n(n-1)\rho p(1-p)c^2 = O(n^2), destroying the O(n) diversification gain. Pooling is vetoed when \rho \ge \rho_c = 1/n.
4. Non-stationarity singularity: if \dot{p} = dp/dt \neq 0 over the production period, the historical proportion does not predict the future proportion, violating Knight's "practically constant" condition [PAGE 1].

Verbatim source anchors [PAGE 1]: "given outcome is not certain, nor even extremely probable, but only contingent, but if the numerical probability of its occurrence is known, conduct in relation to the situation in question may be ordered intelligently"; "the bursting of bottles does not introduce an uncertainty or hazard into the business of producing champagne"; "The loss becomes a fixed cost in the industry and is passed on to the consumer"; "This, of course, is the principle of insurance"; "practical difficulties of ordering conduct intelligently are enormously increased where the inference is contingent instead of being positive."
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical/scientific lineage & multi-week traps.

The intellectual genealogy runs from the classical foundations of probability to Knight's 1921 partition and beyond. Pascal and Fermat (1654) initiated the expectation calculus; Huygens (1657) formalized expected value; Bernoulli (1713, Ars Conjectandi) proved the weak law of large numbers, giving mathematical substance to the idea that a constant proportion emerges from many trials. Laplace (1812) extended this to inverse probability, and Poisson (1837) modeled rare events. Von Mangoldt's champagne-bottle example, cited by Knight [PAGE 1], is an applied instance of Bernoulli's law in industrial production: a known rupture frequency becomes a fixed cost.

Knight's own contribution [PAGE 1] was to draw a sharp line between measurable risk (known numerical probability) and unmeasurable uncertainty (contingent inference without known probability). This was contemporaneous with Keynes's Treatise on Probability (1921), which emphasized the "weight of evidence" and the non-numeric nature of many probabilistic judgments. The line continued through von Mises's frequency theory (1919/1928), de Finetti's exchangeability and subjective probability (1937), Ramsey's measurement of degrees of belief (1931), and Savage's foundations (1954). The modern closure arrives with Arrow-Debreu state-contingent claims (1953/1959), Ellsberg's ambiguity paradox (1961), and Gilboa-Schmeidler maxmin expected utility (1989), all of which formalize what Knight described intuitively: when p is not known, the expected-maximization framework collapses.

Multi-week traps arise when a decision-maker operates this calculus over rolling weekly horizons. Trap A: stationarity across weeks. The estimator \hat{p}_t from week t is applied to week t+1 as if p_{t+1} = p_t; a regime shift (e.g., new glass supplier, changed bottling pressure) invalidates the fixed cost. Trap B: small weekly n. Even if annual n is large, weekly batches may be below n_{\min}; the manager overreacts to sampling noise, treating random fluctuation as a change in risk. Trap C: correlated weekly shocks. Seasonal temperature or humidity affects all bottles in a given week simultaneously, inducing \rho > 0 across units and violating the i.i.d. pooling assumption. Trap D: meta-uncertainty about whether the process itself is stable. Over multiple weeks, the decision-maker cannot distinguish a run of high losses from bad luck under stable p versus a genuine shift in p; this is precisely Knight's "contingent inference instead of being positive" [PAGE 1], and it compounds because each week's mispriced premium feeds into the next week's cost structure.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
