<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Theory of mind in this human-device loop operates across three nested levels. Level zero: the device models the user’s physiology (HR 75 bpm, movement 1622 counts/min, 5 wakes) and emits Recovery Score R = 25%. Level one: the user models the device’s model. Seeing 8:03h of sleep but only 25% recovery, the user may infer that the device is malfunctioning or that duration does not equal quality. If the user distrusts the model, the feedback loop breaks. Level two: the device models the user’s likely reaction to the model. The tip "try lying on your side rather than your back" assumes the user has positional agency and that supine sleep is driving the fragmentation. But [PAGE 22] provides no evidence the device actually measures sleep position; the tip may be an inferred recommendation based on population priors rather than individual positional data. That mismatch creates a theory-of-mind failure: the device believes it knows the user’s sleep mechanics better than it does.

Crisis habit regression: When an athlete sees R = 25%, the habitual crisis response is either "push through it" (training-load escalation under fatigue) or "shut down completely" (detraining). Both are maladaptive fixed points. The patent’s BOBO FEEDBACK attempts to perturb this loop by attributing the low score to modifiable behavior (sleep position, wake frequency) rather than immutable fitness.

Counter-trap: The counter-trap is that the user may optimize for the metric rather than the physiology. If Recovery Score is the only visible gate, the user may manipulate inputs—staying in bed longer to inflate T_sleep, or avoiding morning HR spikes to game RHR—without improving autonomic recovery. The recursive element is that each manipulation changes the input distribution, which shifts the device’s future predictions, which the user then adapts to again.

The clock: The hourly axis 12:00–7:00 imposes a circadian frame. Waking 5 times across these bins suggests sleep-maintenance fragmentation rather than sleep-onset latency. A clock-aware theory of mind would recognize that recovery is not a function of clock time per se but of circadian phase alignment; however [PAGE 22] provides no bedtime, wake time, or phase marker, so the device cannot distinguish early-morning wakes from middle-of-the-night wakes beyond the binned sparkline. The user’s mental model of "the night" is therefore coarser than the device’s timestamped data, and the advice to change sleep position may land at the wrong phase if the wakes are circadian rather than positional.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
