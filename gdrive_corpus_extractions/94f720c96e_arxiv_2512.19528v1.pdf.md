The paper constructs a three-layer inference problem. First, it observes agents, not the ball. The observed modalities are player trajectories in pitch coordinates, player types, and player-centric image crops [PAGE 1], [PAGE 4]. Second, it fuses those modalities into a shared latent tensor and appends two learned CLS tokens, one for ball trajectory inference and one for ball-state classification [PAGE 4]. Third, it passes the fused tensor through coarse and fine temporal/social attention encoders, then predicts ball coordinates, ball state, and ball possessor in parallel [PAGE 4], [PAGE 5]. The deep derivation is therefore not “track the ball,” but “infer the unobserved ball variable from the joint distribution of player motion, player semantics, and localized visual context.” This creates several gray-zone audits.

The first gray zone is label provenance. The paper explicitly says the setup assumes no ball information during inference: “our setup assumes no access to ball information during inference, and only uses player-related data as input” [PAGE 1]. Yet training still requires ball supervision. The source says, “Using possessor annotations, we interpolate the 2D trajectory of the ball” [PAGE 5]. That means the learned mapping is calibrated to a ball trajectory that was itself reconstructed from possession labels. If possession annotations are noisy, the ADE objective inherits that noise. The model is therefore not learning raw physical ball tracking; it is learning a possession-conditioned interpolation of ball motion.

The second gray zone is temporal scope. Each sequence contains 60 frames sampled at 6.25 Hz, corresponding to 9.6 seconds [PAGE 6]. That window is long enough to capture a pass, dribble, or short combination, but short enough to fail at sequence boundaries. The paper acknowledges this: “when a sequence begins or ends with the ball not in possession, our model struggles due to limited temporal context” [PAGE 7]. A more explicit limitation is also stated: “when a sequence ends with the ball traveling between two players, the model lacks sufficient temporal context to confidently determine its final location” [PAGE 8]. This is a structural singularity: at t = 1 or t = T, the model has fewer neighboring observations to constrain the ball state, so the conditional distribution over ball position becomes flatter and more error-prone.

The third gray zone is modality shortcutting. The authors argue that visual crops can become dominated by ball visibility: “features extracted from player image crops are often dominated by whether or not the ball is visible in the crop” [PAGE 5]. That creates a shortcut: the model may classify possession by detecting which crop contains the ball, rather than reasoning about player motion and social context. CropDrop is designed to break that habit by masking entire visual sequences for randomly selected players during pretraining [PAGE 5]. The masking is not random per-frame; the source says, “we empirically find that sequence-level masking of the visual modality alone leads to better performance across tasks” [PAGE 3]. It also says, “Random frame-wise masking proves ineffective due to the high redundancy across adjacent frames” [PAGE 3]. This is a key gray-zone insight: masking must match the statistical redundancy of the data and the real-world failure mode, namely occlusion or missing crops over continuous intervals.

The fourth gray zone is task interaction. Ball trajectory, ball state, and ball possessor are supervised jointly. The total objective is a weighted sum of ADE, state cross-entropy, and possessor cross-entropy [PAGE 5]. The source sets λ1 = 1, λ2 = 3, and λ3 = 3 [PAGE 5]. This weighting reveals a priority: classification tasks receive three times the nominal weight of trajectory error. That matters because a small ADE improvement may not dominate training if possession/state classification is harder or noisier. The shared CLS tokens allow global scene aggregation, while player tokens preserve local agent information [PAGE 4]. The model therefore must balance frame-level localization with social inference.

The fifth gray zone is dataset exclusion. The source excludes out-of-play sequences: “We exclude sequences with frames labeled as out of play, as they are not informative for modeling ball dynamics” [PAGE 5]. It explains that during those intervals, “the flow of the match is paused: players may be walking, repositioning, or even carrying the ball with their hands, and the coordinated team behavior such as formations, pressing structures, or ball-oriented reactions completely breaks down” [PAGE 5]. This is important because it defines the model’s domain: coordinated in-play soccer, not dead-ball situations. If deployment includes set pieces, goalkeeper distributions, or stoppages, the model is extrapolating beyond its validated regime.

The final gray-zone audit is the distinction between ADE and MaxErr. ADE is the mean L2 error across all frames, while MaxErr is the worst frame error [PAGE 5]. A model can have low ADE while still failing catastrophically on a single ambiguous pass. The paper reports both, which is necessary because ball inference is not only average precision; it also requires avoiding large hallucinated trajectories. The qualitative failure of competing methods “hallucinate four passes” shows why MaxErr and frame-level semantic checks matter [PAGE 8].

The paper’s formal operators are built from three supervised outputs: ball position, ball state, and ball possessor. Let t index frames, i index players, N be the number of players, T the number of frames, and d the embedding dimension. The structured inputs are player trajectories p_{i,t} = (x_{i,t}, y_{i,t}) and player type u_i, where type encodes offensive/defensive team identity [PAGE 4]. The visual input is a crop v_{i,t} around player i at time t [PAGE 3], [PAGE 4]. After modality-specific projection, the source says, “we first project each input into a shared latent space. We then concatenate the projected embeddings along the feature dimension for each player and frame” [PAGE 4]. A compact formalization is:

\[
z_{i,t} = \operatorname{MLP}_{\text{fuse}}
\left(
\left[
E_{\text{traj}}(x_{i,t}, y_{i,t}),
E_{\text{type}}(u_i),
E_{\text{crop}}(v_{i,t})
\right]
\right)
\]

\[
H_t =
\left[
z_{1,t}, \ldots, z_{N,t}, c^{\text{ball}}_t, c^{\text{state}}_t
\right]
\in \mathbb{R}^{(N+2)\times d}
\]

This matches the source: “This results in a tensor of shape T ×(N +2)×d” [PAGE 4]. The two appended CLS tokens “aggregate global information across both time and agents” [PAGE 4]. The encoder stack is described as follows: “Each encoder operates on entire T ×(N +2)×d sequence and is composed of two Temporal Set Attention Blocks (SABT) followed by a Social Set Attention Block (SABS)” [PAGE 4]. The temporal block attends within each agent across time; the social block attends across agents within each frame. Positional encoding is added before the encoders to retain chronological order [PAGE 4].

The ball-position head maps the ball CLS token to a 2D coordinate sequence. The source states that the ball CLS token “is passed through a stack of fully connected layers with non-linear activations to produce a T ×2 sequence representing the predicted (x, y) ball coordinates for each frame” [PAGE 4]. The loss is Average Displacement Error:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ball}}
=
\frac{1}{T}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\left\|
\hat{b}_t - b_t
\right\|_2
\]

This is Eq. (1) in the source [PAGE 4]. The units are meters because “Both metrics are represented in meters” [PAGE 5]. The predicted ball coordinate is \hat{b}_t = (\hat{x}_t, \hat{y}_t), and the ground-truth ball coordinate is b_t = (x_t, y_t). The L2 norm is Euclidean distance in pitch coordinates. The source confirms: “where ˆbt and bt are the predicted and ground truth ball positions in 2D pitch coordinates at time t, respectively” [PAGE 4].

The maximum error metric is:

\[
\operatorname{MaxErr}
=
\max_{t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}}
\left\|
\hat{b}_t - b_t
\right\|_2
\]

This is Eq. (5) in the source [PAGE 5]. It measures worst-case displacement in meters, not average displacement. It is therefore a singularity-sensitive metric: a single missed one-two pass can dominate the value even if most frames are accurate.

For ball-state classification, the state CLS token is passed through an MLP to produce logits over S discrete states. The source defines the cross-entropy loss as:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{state}}
=
-\frac{1}{T}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\sum_{s=1}^{S}
c_t^{s}
\log
\left(
\hat{c}_t^{s}
\right)
\]

This is Eq. (2) [PAGE 4]. Here \hat{c}_t^{s} is the predicted probability of state s at time t, and c_t^{s} is the ground-truth indicator. If the logarithm is natural, the unit is nats per frame; if base-2, bits per frame. The source does not specify the logarithm base, so the safest information-theoretic interpretation is “nats per frame under natural log” unless otherwise stated. The probability simplex constraint is implicit:

\[
\hat{c}_t^{s} \geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{s=1}^{S} \hat{c}_t^{s} = 1
\]

The dataset labels include pass, possession, uncontrolled, and out of play, but out-of-play sequences are excluded [PAGE 5]. Therefore the effective training distribution is concentrated on coordinated in-play states.

For possessor identification, the player tokens are used to produce logits over N players:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{poss}}
=
-\frac{1}{T}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
c_t^{n}
\log
\left(
\hat{c}_t^{n}
\right)
\]

This is Eq. (3) [PAGE 5]. The probability simplex constraint is:

\[
\hat{c}_t^{n} \geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{N} \hat{c}_t^{n} = 1
\]

The total objective is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
=
\lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{ball}}
+
\lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{state}}
+
\lambda_3 \mathcal{L}_{\text{poss}}
\]

This is Eq. (4) [PAGE 5]. The source uses λ1 = 1, λ2 = 3, and λ3 = 3 [PAGE 5]. The units of \lambda_i are not physical SI units; they are dimensionless task weights. However, because \mathcal{L}_{\text{ball}} is in meters and the classification losses are in nats or bits per frame, the weighting implicitly converts heterogeneous objectives into a scalar training signal. This is a breakdown-sensitive design choice: if ADE becomes numerically large relative to classification entropy, increasing λ1 may be necessary; conversely, if classification is unstable, λ2 and λ3 may dominate.

CropDrop is formalized as a masking operator over visual embeddings. Let M_t be the set of players selected for visual masking at sequence level. The source says, “we randomly select a percentage of players in each input sequence and mask their visual features across all frames” [PAGE 5]. Let m be the masking ratio, with the paper using m = 0.5 for all pretrainings [PAGE 5]. The masked visual embedding is:

\[
\tilde{v}_{i,t}
=
\begin{cases}
v_{i,t}, & i \notin M \\
\phi, & i \in M
\end{cases}
\]

where \phi is a fixed placeholder outside the valid visual feature range. The source states: “we replace the masked visual embeddings with fixed placeholder values that lie outside the valid range of the visual features” [PAGE 5]. This preserves input dimensionality while signaling missing information. The masking is applied only to the visual branch, not to trajectories or player types, because “masking only the visual modality proves beneficial” [PAGE 8].

Breakdown regimes are explicit. First, random frame-wise visual masking is ineffective because adjacent frames are highly redundant [PAGE 3], [PAGE 7]. Second, masking trajectories or both trajectories and crops degrades performance: “When masking only trajectories or both trajectories and visual crops performance degrades” [PAGE 8]. Third, boundary temporal context is insufficient: “the model lacks sufficient temporal context to confidently determine its final location” [PAGE 8]. Fourth, out-of-play sequences break the modeling assumption: “the coordinated team behavior such as formations, pressing structures, or ball-oriented reactions completely breaks down” [PAGE 5]. These are exact singularity or breakdown regimes in the source’s own terms.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage begins in trajectory understanding. It distinguishes trajectory forecasting and imputation from trajectory inference. Forecasting and imputation assume partial knowledge of the target agent’s history, while inference aims to reconstruct an unobserved agent’s trajectory from surrounding agents [PAGE 2]. This is crucial: the ball is not merely predicted forward from its own past; it is inferred from player dynamics. The source says, “trajectory inference [6, 25] aims to reconstruct the full trajectory of an unobserved agent, such as the ball, using only the observed dynamics of the surrounding players” [PAGE 2].

The immediate technical ancestor is TranSPORTmer, a unified transformer-based framework for multi-agent sports trajectory understanding [PAGE 2]. The current paper extends that line by adding visual player crops. It also contrasts with BallRadar, which uses a hierarchical pipeline: first predict possessor, then guide ball trajectory inference [PAGE 2]. The paper criticizes that sequential dependency because “this sequential dependency imposes a rigid pipeline that can propagate errors from one stage to the next” [PAGE 2]. The proposed model instead performs supervised parallel prediction, avoiding a rigid cascade.

Another lineage comes from masked pretraining. The source references BERT and CLS tokens from NLP, as well as masked image modeling and MultiMAE [PAGE 3]. The CLS token idea is directly used: “To support ball inference and ball state classification, we append two learned CLS tokens” [PAGE 4]. The masking lineage is also explicit: “Masking has become a widely adopted strategy for pre-training, particularly in NLP [13, 30, 51, 56, 59] and visual representation learning [2, 3, 15, 23, 47]” [PAGE 3]. The paper’s novelty is not masking itself, but task-aligned, sequence-level masking of only the visual modality. It says, “we empirically find that sequence-level masking of the visual modality alone leads to better performance across tasks” [PAGE 3].

A multi-week trap appears in dataset provenance. The paper evaluates on “283 full professional matches from a top European league during season 2022-2023” [PAGE 5]. Matches are disjoint across splits: “Matches are disjoint across splits to ensure generalization to unseen games” [PAGE 6]. That controls for match-level leakage, but it does not control for tactical drift over subsequent weeks or seasons. If deployed later, teams may change pressing structures, substitution patterns, camera behavior, or broadcast framing. The model’s learned social priors may then become stale. This is a retro-causal trap: a model that appears robust on 2022-2023 held-out matches may still fail in 2026 if the tactical distribution has shifted.

Another multi-week trap comes from camera and visibility assumptions. The dataset uses a tactical camera: “This camera captures a wide-angle view that follows the flow of the game, typically ensuring visibility of most outfield players, although the goalkeeper at the opposite side of the field is usually out of view” [PAGE 5]. If the camera operator’s behavior changes, or if broadcast coverage differs, the visual crop distribution changes. The model may then under-observe goalkeeper or off-ball defenders, weakening possession inference. The source acknowledges that prior visual event-detection methods “suffer when the ball is occluded or outside the camera frame, particularly in crowded scenes” [PAGE 2]. That is a camera-dependent failure mode.

A third lineage is dataset limitation. SoccerNet-GSR removed ball annotations because the pitch-plane assumption fails for airborne balls. The source says, “While this approximation is acceptable for players, it prevents accurate localization of the ball, which spends significant time in the air. As a result, ball annotations had to be removed in SoccerNet-GSR [44]” [PAGE 2]. The current paper avoids that specific dataset limitation by using proprietary possession annotations to interpolate ball trajectories [PAGE 5]. However, this creates a new genealogy: the learned ball trajectory is dependent on the annotation protocol used to interpolate from possession. If that protocol changes across weeks, the model’s regression target changes.

The final historical trap is the exclusion of out-of-play frames. The paper intentionally removes them because they are not informative for coordinated ball dynamics [PAGE 5]. This is scientifically reasonable for the stated tasks, but it creates a deployment trap: real matches include throw-ins, goal kicks, corners, free kicks, and stoppages. If the deployed system is expected to handle all frames, the model will encounter states outside its training manifold. The retro-causal lesson is that the paper’s strong results are conditional on a curated in-play distribution, not on the full stochastic match record.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model’s recursive theory of mind has three layers. Layer 0 is physical observation: player positions, team types, and image crops. Layer 1 is social inference: which player likely controls the ball given body orientation, proximity, and motion. Layer 2 is adversarial anticipation: the model must infer not only current possession but also likely pass completion, dribbling, or loss of control. Layer 3 is meta-level auditing: the evaluator must detect whether the model is using genuine cross-modal reasoning or a shortcut based on ball visibility in crops.

The crisis habit regression is clearly identified by the authors. They say crop features are “often dominated by whether or not the ball is visible in the crop” [PAGE 5]. In a crisis distribution—occlusions, crowded scenes, fast passes, or low-resolution footage—the model may regress to the easiest cue: if the ball is visible in a crop, assign possession to that player. This is a habit because it works in many frames but fails when the ball is occluded, outside the frame, or temporarily untracked. The source says visual-only event methods can suffer “when the ball is occluded or outside the camera frame” [PAGE 2]. That is precisely the crisis condition where the shortcut becomes unreliable.

The counter-trap is CropDrop. By masking whole visual sequences for selected players, the model cannot consistently rely on the most visually anomalous crop. The source says, “By selectively masking the visual features of random players, we prevent the model from depending exclusively on this shortcut” [PAGE 5]. The masking is sequence-level rather than frame-level because random frame masking is ineffective due to redundancy [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap therefore forces the model to use trajectories and player types when visual evidence is missing. This is a regularization strategy aligned with real failure modes: players may be occluded or out of view for several seconds [PAGE 7].

The clock is explicit. Sequences are 60 frames at 6.25 Hz, or 9.6 seconds [PAGE 6]. This temporal window defines the model’s theory-of-mind horizon. Within 9.6 seconds, the model can observe a short passing sequence, a dribble, or a defensive intervention. But at the beginning or end of the clip, the clock creates an epistemic boundary. The source says, “when a sequence begins or ends with the ball not in possession, our model struggles due to limited temporal context” [PAGE 7]. In recursive terms, the model cannot infer the hidden ball state if there is insufficient prior or subsequent evidence of player intentions. The clock is therefore not just a sampling parameter; it is an identifiability constraint.

A second clock-related crisis is the fast one-two pass. The paper notes that competing methods miss a one-two pass, or wall pass, while the proposed model can follow the ball during that fast combination [PAGE 8]. This shows that temporal resolution and social attention matter. If the clock is too coarse or the sequence too short, the model may miss the exchange. If the clock is long enough but the model lacks social reasoning, it may hallucinate passes. The recursive theory of mind must therefore model both agent-level motion and frame-level attention over a short but information-dense horizon.

The model also uses player type as a stable prior. The source says player type is not masked because “player team affiliation is consistently known across all frames” [PAGE 8]. This is a low-entropy but robust input. It does not tell the model where the ball is, but it helps differentiate offensive and defensive roles. In a recursive theory-of-mind setting, team type allows the model to infer likely intent: offensive players may be more likely to receive or progress the ball, while defensive players may be more likely to intercept or clear. The model does not explicitly represent opponent beliefs, but its social attention blocks allow tokens to exchange information across agents and CLS tokens [PAGE 4]. That is the architectural substrate for an implicit theory of mind.

The deepest trap is that the model may learn possession as a visual anomaly detector rather than a social dynamical predictor. CropDrop is the counter-trap, but it is not a complete cure. If visual crops are systematically missing for a player over a long interval, the model must infer possession from motion alone. If trajectories are noisy, that inference becomes unstable. The source notes that trajectory masking deprives the model of essential structural information, making learning unstable or ineffective [PAGE 8]. Therefore the recursive balance is delicate: visual masking improves generalization only when structured inputs remain reliable. If both visual and trajectory modalities degrade simultaneously, the model’s theory of mind collapses into guesswork.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto is straightforward. The ball is not directly observed at inference. The source says, “our setup assumes no access to ball information during inference, and only uses player-related data as input” [PAGE 1]. Therefore any claim that the model directly tracks the ball is false. The model infers ball position from player dynamics. This creates a physical identifiability veto: if player motion does not encode ball possession, the ball state cannot be recovered. The paper handles this by using social attention and multi-task supervision, but the inference remains indirect.

A second physical veto comes from the ball being airborne or outside the ground plane. The source criticizes SoccerNet-GSR because player-based pitch-plane derivation “prevents accurate localization of the ball, which spends significant time in the air” [PAGE 2]. The current paper avoids that specific dataset by interpolating ball trajectories from possession annotations [PAGE 5], but the underlying physical issue remains: a ball in flight may not be inferable from player positions alone if players have not yet reacted. The model’s ADE objective measures 2D pitch-coordinate error, so vertical ball motion is outside the output space. If the ball is lofted, the 2D projection may be ambiguous until players converge or the ball lands.

The visual veto is also explicit. Prior visual methods “suffer when the ball is occluded or outside the camera frame, particularly in crowded scenes” [PAGE 2]. The proposed model mitigates this by fusing tracking and visual information [PAGE 2]. However, visual evidence can still be misleading. The source says crop features are often dominated by whether the ball is visible [PAGE 5]. If a player is near the ball but not in possession, the crop may still contain ball pixels. Conversely, if the true possessor is occluded, the crop may lack ball evidence. Therefore a visual-only veto rule—“assign possession to the crop containing the ball”—fails under occlusion.

The biomechanical veto concerns body orientation and physical interaction. The paper argues that visual crops help detect subtleties such as body orientation or physical interactions, improving fine-grained ball-state classification [PAGE 3]. That is a valid biomechanical signal: a player shielding the ball, reaching with a foot, or controlling with the chest provides visual evidence of possession. But biomechanical evidence degrades under low resolution, motion blur, and occlusion. The source notes that direct ball tracking remains error-prone in monocular footage because of occlusions, limited resolution, and unpredictable motion [PAGE 1]. Therefore biomechanical cues are useful but not sufficient when the visual layer is degraded.

The weather veto is not explicitly modeled in the source. The provided text does not contain a weather operator. However, a formal exogenous veto can still be stated: if weather changes visual appearance, crop embeddings may shift, while player types and trajectories may remain available. The source supports the robustness of player type because “player team affiliation is consistently known across all frames” [PAGE 8]. It also supports sequence-level visual masking as a defense against missing visual information [PAGE 3], [PAGE 5]. Therefore, under a weather-induced visual distribution shift, the model should rely more heavily on structured inputs. If trajectories remain accurate and player types remain known, the model may still infer possession, but visual-dependent state classification may degrade. If weather also corrupts tracking, the veto becomes absolute for the current architecture because both structured and visual inputs are compromised.

A decisive breakdown veto comes from out-of-play frames. The source excludes them because “the coordinated team behavior such as formations, pressing structures, or ball-oriented reactions completely breaks down” [PAGE 5]. During out-of-play intervals, “players may be walking, repositioning, or even carrying the ball with their hands” [PAGE 5]. If the model is forced to predict possession in those intervals, the physical assumption of coordinated soccer is violated. A goalkeeper holding the ball, a player taking a throw-in, or a defender walking away from a dead ball does not provide the same social-dynamical signal as open play. This is a cross-layer veto: the model’s learned social operators are valid only for in-play coordination, not for dead-ball behavior.

The adjudication rule is therefore: accept the model’s prediction only when at least one reliable layer remains. If visual crops are missing, structured trajectories and player types can still support inference, especially if the model was pretrained with CropDrop. If trajectories are noisy, visual crops can help, but only if the ball or player-ball interaction is visible. If both visual and trajectory layers are degraded, the prediction should be rejected. If the frame belongs to out-of-play or non-coordinated behavior, the model’s domain assumption is violated and the prediction should be vetoed. This adjudication follows directly from the source’s own failure regimes: “when the ball is occluded or outside the camera frame” [PAGE 2], “limited temporal context” [PAGE 7], and “coordinated team behavior … completely breaks down” [PAGE 5].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Veto: Reject any claim that out-of-play frames can be treated as ordinary in-play frames. The source’s modeling assumption is explicitly limited to coordinated in-play behavior.
