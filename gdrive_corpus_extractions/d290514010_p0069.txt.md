<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis conditions—such as a bad snap, a penetrating interior rusher, or late-game scoreboard pressure—the kicker's habit regresses toward safety: holding the ball longer than 2 seconds to ensure clean contact rather than risking a blocked kick [PAGE 48]. This regression is first-order: the kicker prioritizes personal execution over system timing. The coverage unit, operating under a recursive model of the kicker, expects the release at or before t = 2 s. When the kicker regresses, the coverage unit's internal model generates a prediction error: the line men have already driven downfield based on an anticipated flight time that does not materialize, leaving them out of phase with the actual ball trajectory.

The counter-trap is executed by the receiving team. If the return coach recognizes the crisis-habit regression (the slow release), he can instruct the returner to delay his initial break, inviting the coverage line to overcommit downfield, then accelerate once the ball is actually airborne. Because the line men are "altering their direction according to any movement the catcher makes" [PAGE 48], a deliberate false movement by the catcher can induce the fan to spread incorrectly, creating a seam. The clock variable interacts nonlinearly: as the game clock winds down, the kicking team's urgency compresses τ_max, increasing the probability of a muffed snap or a rushed, low kick; conversely, the return team may use its own timeouts to freeze the holder-kicker rhythm, converting the 2-second rule from an asset into a liability. The recursive loop closes when the kicking team, anticipating the counter-trap, rushes the release even further, degrading spiral quality and increasing the chance that the kick fails the h_cov requirement altogether.
