The provided source is a 2017 LSU doctoral dissertation by Jeremy Joseph Foreman. Its central derivation is a three-step adaptation: first, Fredrickson, Hambrick, and Baumrin’s CEO-dismissal model is imported into the NFL head-coach setting; second, the under-tested “availability of qualified candidates” force is operationalized with actual coach pools rather than firm-size or industry-size proxies; third, the model is extended with executive deviance and then linked to promotion/demotion mechanisms through race, position centrality, and the Rooney Rule. The first move is explicit: Fredrickson et al. define dismissal as a situation in which “the CEO's departure is ad hoc (e.g., not part of a mandatory retirement policy) and against his or her will” [PAGE 20], and the dissertation repeatedly warns that “its ceteris paribus argument requires all variables to be included” [PAGE 11]. The second move is the key empirical bet: NFL head-coach successors are mostly current coordinators, and “Most NFL head coach successors are already employed by an NFL team as offensive and defensive coordinators” [PAGE 25]. Foreman constructs OCDCPOOL, PREVYRHC, and CURRYRHC and then states that “The three aforementioned independent variables (i.e., OCDCPOOL, PREVYRHC, and CURRYRHC) are tabulated to form a fourth variable, the entire NFL head coach candidate pool (CANDPOOL)” [PAGE 27]. The third move is the null: the candidate-pool coefficients do not behave as Fredrickson et al. predict; “The independent variables of interest OCDCPOOL, PREVYRHC, and CANDPOOL have negative coefficients and are statistically insignificant in each of the models” [PAGE 34]. This null is not merely a failed test; it is a challenge to proxy-based claims that candidate availability raises dismissal probability.

The next multi-hop branch is deviance. The dissertation argues that executive deviance is absent from Fredrickson et al. and should be a fifth socio-political force: “Executive deviance is a subset of elite deviance and encompasses executives own deviance but also the deviance of subordinates” [PAGE 34]. It then separates workplace deviance by executives, workplace deviance by employees, serious versus minor deviance, and off-duty deviance, and it interacts those measures with the 2007 Goodell personal-conduct-policy shift. The empirical pattern is non-monotonic: coaching fines increase dismissal risk; player fines before the policy shift reduce dismissal risk; player suspensions increase dismissal risk before the interaction but the interaction reverses sign; penalty yards become significant only after the policy shift; off-field legal incidents are insignificant. The dissertation reports that “a one standard deviation increase in coaching fines for a given season increases the risk of head coach dismissal by 43.3%” [PAGE 72], and that “suspended players increase the risk of head coach dismissal by 87.3% for a one standard deviation increase in player suspensions, but decrease head coach dismissal risk by 8.7% after the PCP modification” [PAGE 73]. That reversal is the gray-zone hinge: the same observable punishment can be read as accountability or as an excuse for poor on-field performance, depending on the decision-maker’s attribution model.

The third branch is promotion/demotion. The dissertation links dismissal to successor supply, then studies race, centrality, and the Rooney Rule from 1984-1985 through 2015-2016. It codes movement across four coaching levels, estimates ordered and binary logit models, and finds that Black coaches are disadvantaged in some lower-level transitions while central-position coaches are advantaged. The discussion states that “Black coaches are 2.2% less likely to be promoted and 0.7% more likely to be demoted than their non-Black counterparts during the sample period” [PAGE 107], and that “Central position coaches (i.e., quarterback and linebacker coaches) in the sample experienced a 7.4% increased promotion likelihood” [PAGE 108]. It also concludes that “the Rooney Rule was ineffective in increasing the ability of Black coaches in the NFL to be promoted” [PAGE 107]. The gray-zone audit must therefore separate three claims: (a) a candidate-pool null; (b) a deviance-policy interaction; and (c) a race/centrality promotion disparity. Each has different identification threats.

Several audit traps are visible. The candidate pool is constructed with threshold choices: top 20 percent of sustained team performance over the prior three years plus top 20 percent offense/defense, and former head coaches with winning records. These are defensible but post hoc; different thresholds could change the null. The policy interaction uses a single 2007 binary, so any concurrent NFL change—salary-cap growth, rule enforcement, media environment, roster rules—can masquerade as a PCP effect. The race variable is a visual classification: the dissertation says race “was verified from popular sources” and assumes limited ambiguity [PAGE 81-82], which introduces measurement error. Centrality is a binary QB/LB indicator, not a continuous network-centrality measure [PAGE 82], so the mechanism is occupational role, not social-network brokerage. The promotion models also have low pseudo-R² values, which the author acknowledges: the promotion models show “the difficulty of determining how promotion decisions are made by practitioners” [PAGE 104]. Finally, the provided source text ends at [PAGE 230], although the metadata says 245 pages; pages 231-245 are not available for verification. Any claim about appendices, permission letters, or vita content beyond [PAGE 230] is therefore outside the evidential boundary.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The dissertation uses three formal empirical operators: a binary logit for dismissal, a hazard model for time-to-dismissal, and ordered/binary logits for promotion-demotion. I state them with units, assumptions, and breakdown regimes.

1. Binary dismissal logit. The source gives the broad form on [PAGE 22]:
\[
D_{ij}^{*}=\beta_0+\beta_1(\text{INDVAR})_{ij}+\beta_2(\text{WINPCT})_{ij}+\cdots+\beta_{22}(\text{AGE})_{ij}+e_{ij},
\]
with observed \(D_{ij}=\mathbf{1}(D_{ij}^{*}>0)\). Equivalently,
\[
\Pr(D_{ij}=1\mid X_{ij},u_i)=\frac{\exp(\eta_{ij}+u_i)}{1+\exp(\eta_{ij}+u_i)},\qquad
\eta_{ij}=\beta_0+\beta_C C_{ij}+\beta_W W_{ij}+\beta_P P_{ij}+\beta_H H_{ij}+\beta_T T_{ij}+\beta_F F_{ij}+\cdots.
\]
Here \(D_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) is dimensionless; \(C_{ij}\) is a candidate-pool count in coaches; \(W_{ij}\) is winning percentage in \([0,1]\); fines are in 2015 U.S. dollars; suspensions are weeks; tenure and age are years; \(\beta\) is a logit coefficient per one-unit change in its covariate; \(u_i\) is a random team effect. The source states that “a logistic regression model is estimated” [PAGE 22] and that a Hausman test “indicated controlling for random effects was more appropriate than fixed effects” [PAGE 32]. The source also reports correlations not exceeding 0.8, so multicollinearity was deemed non-binding [PAGE 32].

The candidate-pool operator is:
\[
C^{\text{pool}}_{ij}=C^{\text{OC}}_{ij}+C^{\text{prev}}_{ij}+C^{\text{curr}}_{ij}.
\]
\(C^{\text{OC}}_{ij}\) counts coordinators meeting two conditions: team average winning percentage in the top 20 percent over the prior three years, and top 20 percent points scored or allowed in the observed season. \(C^{\text{prev}}_{ij}\) and \(C^{\text{curr}}_{ij}\) count qualified former head coaches dismissed in the previous or observed season and not rehired. The source states that coordinators are “available and qualified if they meet both of the following criteria” [PAGE 26] and that the three counts are “tabulated” into CANDPOOL [PAGE 27]. Units are coaches; the coefficients therefore represent the logit change per additional available coach.

Breakdown regimes for this logit are exact. If a covariate perfectly separates \(D=1\) from \(D=0\), the maximum-likelihood estimate diverges and the singularity is complete separation. The candidate-pool counts are small integers, so sparse cells can produce unstable standard errors even without full separation. If the random-effects assumption fails—specifically, if \(u_i\) is correlated with \(X_{ij}\)—the random-effects estimator is inconsistent. The source’s Hausman test is a local check, not a universal proof. The null candidate-pool result is therefore robust only within the specified NFL sample, threshold rules, and random-effects specification.

2. Hazard model. Chapter 3 estimates
\[
h_i(t)=h_0(t)\exp\{\beta_0+\beta_1 X_{it}+\beta_2 G_t+\beta_3 X_{it}G_t+\beta_4 Z_{it}+\theta_t\},
\]
where \(i\) indexes coaches, \(t\) indexes seasons, \(G_t\in\{0,1\}\) is the post-2007 policy indicator, \(\theta_t\) is a season fixed effect, and \(h_i(t)\) is the dismissal hazard per team-season. The source gives this equation on [PAGE 65]. A Weibull baseline is used:
\[
h_0(t)=\lambda\rho t^{\rho-1},\qquad \lambda>0,\ \rho>0,
\]
with \(t\) in seasons. The reported shape is \(\rho=3.020^{***}\) [PAGE 70]. The hazard ratio is \(\exp(\beta)\), dimensionless. The source reports that a one-standard-deviation coaching-fine increase raises dismissal risk by 43.3% [PAGE 72], and that a one-standard-deviation suspension increase raises risk by 87.3% before the policy interaction but lowers it by 8.7% after [PAGE 73].

Breakdown regimes: the Weibull model assumes a monotone baseline hazard; if the true hazard is non-monotone, the shape parameter is misspecified. The source selected Weibull after graphical checks against Cox-Snell residuals [PAGE 66], but that is not a proof against time-varying omitted confounders. The interaction \(X_{it}G_t\) is vulnerable to collinearity because \(G_t\) is a step function; the source reports correlations below 0.7 and VIFs below 6 [PAGE 66], but those diagnostics do not address the fact that the policy indicator is collinear with era. The fines variables are zero-inflated: the source reports that 95.6% of observations had no coaching fines [PAGE 57]. A zero-inflated covariate makes the hazard ratio sensitive to a few high-leverage observations; the maximum fine is $571,561 [PAGE 57], so the tail dominates the standard deviation. The suspension variable is similarly sparse: 65.3% of observations had no player suspensions [PAGE 57]. Any causal reading of the PCP interaction must therefore survive a sparse-tail sensitivity check.

3. Ordered and binary promotion/demotion logits. Chapter 4 uses
\[
L=\log\frac{P}{1-P}=\beta X_{it}
\]
on [PAGE 96], with \(P\) the probability of promotion or demotion and \(X_{it}\) indexed by coach \(i\) and season \(t\). For the ordinal outcome PRODEM, coded \(1\) for promotion, \(-1\) for demotion, and \(0\) otherwise [PAGE 90-91], the ordered logit is
\[
\log\frac{\Pr(Y_{it}\le k)}{1-\Pr(Y_{it}\le k)}=\alpha_k-\beta^{\top}X_{it},\qquad k\in\{-1,0,1\}.
\]
The source reports cut points Cut1 and Cut2 and coefficients in logit units [PAGE 100]. The individual-performance operator is standardized by season:
\[
z_{it}=\frac{p_{it}-\bar p_t}{s_t},
\]
so \(z_{it}\) is in season-specific standard-deviation units [PAGE 83-84]. Monetary fines are converted to real 2015 dollars using the CPI-U:
\[
x^{\text{real}}_{it}=x^{\text{nominal}}_{it}\cdot\frac{CPI_{2015}}{CPI_t},
\]
with units of 2015 U.S. dollars [PAGE 57, footnote 1].

Breakdown regimes: ordered logit assumes proportional slopes across cut points. The dissertation does not report a formal parallel-lines test, so if the race or centrality effects differ between promotion and demotion thresholds, the ordered model is misspecified and the binary models are the safer evidence. Multicollinearity diagnostics are local: correlations below 0.6 and VIFs below 8 [PAGE 96-97] do not rule out interaction collinearity between BLCK, CENT, and ROON. Race is coded as “appears to be Black” [PAGE 81-82], which introduces nondifferential and possibly differential misclassification; if misclassification correlates with position level, the BLCK coefficient is biased toward or away from zero depending on the error structure. Centrality is a binary indicator for quarterback or linebacker coaching [PAGE 82], not a continuous centrality measure; the estimate therefore identifies an occupational-role effect, not a network-brokerage effect. Finally, the promotion models have low pseudo-R² values [PAGE 100], so the coefficients should be read as conditional associations, not as a complete decision rule.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The dissertation’s lineage runs backward from 2017 to the 1950s and 1960s. The immediate ancestor is Fredrickson, Hambrick, and Baumrin’s 1988 model, which treats dismissal as a sociopolitical process conditioned by board expectations and attributions, board allegiances and values, incumbent power, and alternatives to the incumbent [PAGE 11, PAGE 21]. Foreman’s contribution is not to invent a new dismissal theory but to operationalize the fourth force—candidate availability—with actual candidate counts rather than firm-size or industry-size proxies. That move is a response to a long-standing measurement problem: the hiring process is secretive, and the source notes that “The hiring process of firms is secretive, further limiting the abilities of researchers to examine its effect on dismissals” [PAGE 22]. The proxy tradition runs through Dalton and Kesner’s firm-size logic [PAGE 13], Parrino’s industry-homogeneity logic [PAGE 13-14], and Crossland and Chen’s country-specific candidate-pool logic [PAGE 14]. Foreman’s NFL setting is chosen because it is a relatively closed labor market with observable coordinator and former-head-coach pools [PAGE 25].

The second lineage is the sport-as-laboratory tradition. The dissertation cites Grusky’s 1963 baseball study of position centrality and managerial recruitment [PAGE 75], Scully’s 1989 baseball manager analysis [PAGE 75], Singell’s 1991 centrality and race study [PAGE 75], Massengale and Farrington’s 1977 college-football playing-position study [PAGE 75-76], Anderson’s 1993 college-football centrality study [PAGE 76], and Finch et al.’s 2010 update [PAGE 76]. The race-and-Rooney branch runs through Madden’s 2004 “last hire, first fire” argument [PAGE 77], Fee, Hadlock, and Pierce’s 2006 promotion study [PAGE 77], Malone et al.’s 2008 critique [PAGE 77-78], Madden and Ruther’s 2010 Rooney Rule evaluation [PAGE 78], Solow, Solow, and Walker’s 2011 coordinator-pipeline study [PAGE 78], Braddock, Smith, and Dawkins’s 2012 centrality and race study [PAGE 79], and Rider et al.’s 2016 promotion-bias study [PAGE 79]. The dissertation positions itself as addressing contradictory results and the scarcity of studies that jointly model race, centrality, and the Rooney Rule over a long post-Rule window [PAGE 79-80].

The third lineage is the institutional-policy branch. The NFL Personal Conduct Policy was implemented in 2000 under Commissioner Tagliabue and modified in 2007 under Commissioner Goodell [PAGE 44-45]. The dissertation treats the 2007 modification as an exogenous emphasis shift that changes the attribution environment: “a modified PCP was adopted in April 2007” and the commissioner gained power to punish conduct deemed to harm the league brand [PAGE 45]. The Rooney Rule is the other institutional intervention: implemented in 2003 to require minority interviews for head-coaching vacancies [PAGE 73]. These two policies are the dissertation’s “clock” interventions: they change the decision-maker’s reference point, not merely the coach’s behavior.

Multi-week traps in this genealogy are substantial. First, the candidate-pool null may be a threshold artifact: the top-20-percent rules for team performance and offense/defense are defensible but arbitrary, and a different cutoff could restore a positive coefficient. Second, the PCP interaction is a single-era binary; any concurrent change in roster rules, injury reporting, media coverage, or enforcement intensity can load onto the same indicator. Third, the race results are level-dependent: the dissertation finds no evidence of racial disparities at the head-coach or coordinator/assistant-head-coach ranks [PAGE 104], but does find disadvantages for Black Level 3 coaches [PAGE 107]. That pattern is a trap for anyone who collapses all coaching levels into one “NFL race effect.” Fourth, centrality is measured as QB/LB position coaching, not as network centrality; the historical lineage from Grusky is about interaction-based centrality, but the operationalization is a role binary. Fifth, the promotion models have low explanatory power [PAGE 104], so the absence of a significant effect at one level is not proof of fairness; it may be measurement noise. Sixth, the source’s 245-page metadata does not match the provided 230-page text, so any retrocausal claim about the appendices or permission materials must be flagged as unverified.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The decision architecture is recursive: owners and boards form beliefs about general managers, general managers form beliefs about coaches, coaches form beliefs about how owners will attribute performance, and candidates form beliefs about which vacancies are real. The dissertation’s evidence supports three distinct mental-model regimes.

First, crisis habit regression. When organizational performance is poor, decision-makers regress to a low-dimensional performance heuristic and underweight candidate availability. The candidate-pool null is consistent with this: the pool variables are insignificant [PAGE 34], while winning percentage dominates. The source reports that moving from the worst to the best observed winning percentage “decreases the likelihood of CEO dismissal by 62%” [PAGE 29]. In recursive terms, the owner’s belief is not “Do we have a qualified replacement?” but “Did the coach win?” The coach anticipates this and manages the performance narrative; the board’s attribution model becomes a habit under crisis. The candidate pool is cognitively available but not decision-relevant.

Second, counter-trap behavior. The deviance results show that coaches can sometimes convert a negative signal into an excuse. Before the PCP modification, player fines reduce dismissal risk; after the modification, player fines increase dismissal risk, but suspensions reduce dismissal risk [PAGE 73]. The source interprets this as a possible excuse mechanism: “coaches were not able to use all their resources” [PAGE 73-74]. This is a recursive belief loop: the coach models the owner’s model of responsibility. If the owner believes suspensions are externally imposed and unavoidable, the coach can attribute poor performance to missing players. If the owner believes fines and penalty yards signal a lack of discipline, the coach is blamed. The counter-trap is therefore conditional on the owner’s attribution frame, not on the deviance itself. The same act can be a liability or a shield.

Third, the clock. Tenure, age, and first-year status are the temporal cues that reset the recursive model. The dissertation finds a positive tenure coefficient in Chapter 2 [PAGE 35], contrary to Fredrickson et al.’s expectation, and a strong positive first-year dismissal risk in Chapter 3: “a strong positive relationship that increases dismissal risk by 229.5%, ceteris paribus” [PAGE 75]. Age increases dismissal risk by 5.1% per year [PAGE 76]. These are clock effects: the owner’s patience decays, the coach’s perceived remaining horizon shortens, and the candidate pool’s outside options become more salient. The recursive model must therefore include time-varying beliefs: a first-year coach is not evaluated on the same attribution schedule as a tenured coach, and an older coach is not evaluated on the same expected improvement path as a younger coach.

The gray-zone is that these three regimes interact. A crisis can trigger habit regression, which makes the coach adopt a counter-trap excuse, which then resets the clock. The dissertation’s policy interaction is the clearest example: the 2007 PCP shift changes the owner’s attribution model, which changes the coach’s excuse strategy, which changes the dismissal hazard. The source’s own caution is relevant: “stakeholders’ attention is directed in certain ways that bound where they look, limit what they notice, bias their assessment, and constrain their willingness to act” [PAGE 35-36]. That is a theory-of-mind statement: the decision-maker’s attention is bounded, and the coach’s strategy is a response to those bounds.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is simple: a coefficient estimated at the organizational layer cannot be given a causal interpretation if physical, biomechanical, or weather layers are omitted and correlated with the regressor and the outcome. The dissertation’s models do not include injury severity, collision load, fatigue, travel, field surface, temperature, wind, precipitation, or stadium type. The hazard specification includes coaching fines, player fines, suspensions, penalty yards, off-field incidents, winning percentage, tenure, age, and policy era [PAGE 65, PAGE 70]. The promotion models include performance, experience, penalties, turnover, age, and season fixed effects [PAGE 96-100]. Weather is absent from both. Therefore, any claim that a coaching or deviance effect is causal is vetoed unless the omitted-layer correlation is shown to be zero.

Formal veto condition. Let \(D\) be the dismissal or promotion outcome, \(X\) the included regressor, and \(M\) an omitted physical/biomechanical/weather factor. The naive coefficient satisfies
\[
\beta_X^{\text{naive}}=\beta_X+\gamma\,\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,M\mid Z)}{\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)},
\]
where \(\gamma\) is the effect of \(M\) on the outcome and \(Z\) is the included control set. The units are logit or log-hazard per one unit of \(X\). The veto is binding when \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,M\mid Z)\neq 0\). If \(M\) is injury exposure and \(X\) is penalty yards, aggressive play can raise both penalties and injury risk, so \(\beta_{\text{PEN}}\) absorbs a biomechanical effect. If \(M\) is wind or precipitation and \(X\) is passing performance, the promotion coefficient for quarterback coaches absorbs weather variance. If \(M\) is collision load and \(X\) is suspension weeks, the “excuse” effect is confounded by roster quality.

Physical veto. Suspensions reduce player availability; the source measures suspension weeks but not the quality of the replaced player, the injury status of the roster, or the coach’s replacement options [PAGE 52]. The source’s own excuse interpretation—“coaches were not able to use all their resources” [PAGE 73-74]—is a physical-availability mechanism, not a pure deviance mechanism. Without injury and roster controls, the suspension coefficient cannot separate policy attribution from physical depletion.

Biomechanical veto. Penalty yards and player fines are behavioral proxies for on-field conduct, but they also correlate with collision intensity, fatigue, and injury exposure. The dissertation measures penalty yards [PAGE 51-52] and uses them in both dismissal and promotion models, but it does not measure biomechanical load. A coach whose team plays a more physical style may accumulate penalties and injuries simultaneously; the coefficient on penalties then conflates discipline with biomechanics. The veto is not repaired by statistical significance; it is repaired only by adding biomechanical covariates or by using an identification strategy that isolates exogenous rule enforcement.

Weather veto. The dissertation does not include weather or stadium controls. Outdoor NFL games are exposed to wind, rain, snow, and temperature, all of which affect points scored, points allowed, net passing, rushing, and special-teams performance. The individual-performance measures for quarterbacks, wide receivers, offensive line, defensive line, secondary, and special teams [PAGE 95] are weather-sensitive. If weather correlates with coaching style, home stadium, or season, the promotion/demotion coefficients are biased. The veto proof is the specification itself: no weather variable appears in the models on [PAGE 65] or [PAGE 96-100]. Therefore, cross-layer causal claims about coaching performance are rejected unless the analysis adds weather fixed effects, stadium controls, or a within-game identification design.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I propose three verifiable counterfactual checks. Each has a clear prediction and a veto condition.

1. Candidate-pool threshold audit. Re-estimate the dismissal logit with alternative candidate-pool definitions: top 10 percent and top 30 percent sustained team performance; top 10 percent and top 30 percent offense/defense; former head coaches with career winning percentage above 0.500 versus above 0.550; and a continuous pool-quality index rather than a count. Prediction: if the original null is real, the candidate-pool coefficient remains statistically indistinguishable from zero and its sign is unstable across thresholds. Veto: if the coefficient becomes positive and significant under a defensible threshold while the original model remains null, the original null is a specification artifact, not a substantive finding. The source’s own robustness checks mention alternative pools, but they do not report a full threshold surface [PAGE 36].

2. Policy-timing placebo audit. Replace the 2007 PCP indicator with placebo cutoffs at 1998, 2001, 2005, and 2010, and estimate the same deviance interactions. Prediction: the suspension interaction should be negative and significant only for the 2007 cutoff; the player-fine interaction should be positive and significant only for the 2007 cutoff; penalty-yard interaction should be positive and significant only for the 2007 cutoff. Veto: if placebo cutoffs produce the same pattern, the “PCP effect” is an era effect, not a policy effect. The source’s single \(G_t\) binary [PAGE 65] cannot distinguish policy from secular trends, so this check is necessary.

3. Race-and-centrality reclassification audit. Recode race with a blind two-coder procedure and recode centrality as a continuous position-interaction index rather than a QB/LB binary. Re-estimate the Level 3 promotion and demotion models. Prediction: the Black-coach disadvantage at Level 3 should persist in sign and approximate magnitude if it is structural; the central-position advantage should persist if it is occupational-role-based. Veto: if the Black coefficient loses significance under blind coding, the original result is consistent with measurement error; if the centrality coefficient loses significance under a continuous index, the QB/LB binary is not capturing the Grusky interaction mechanism. The source reports that Black coaches are “2.2% less likely to be promoted and 0.7% more likely to be demoted” [PAGE 107] and that central-position coaches have a 7.4% promotion advantage [PAGE 108]; these are the quantities the audit must reproduce or overturn. A further prediction: the Rooney Rule coefficient should remain insignificant for Black promotion if the policy is ineffective, as the source concludes [PAGE 107]; if the audit finds a positive interaction only under one coding, the policy conclusion is not robust.
