<THINK_TRACE>
The internal logic of this text is a layered triage of *time* against *physiology*. Three deep couplings run through all nine chapters and are worth tracing because they generate most of the "gray zones."

First coupling — **the reserve-exhaustion principle.** The text repeatedly asserts that a compensated physiological system will hide catastrophic injury until reserve collapses, and that collapse is then terminal. Head injury: "Hypovolemic shock does not result from an isolated head injury; look for another cause of the hypovolemia" and "hypotension caused by a head injury is usually a terminal event" [PAGE 31]. Abdomen: "the adult peritoneal cavity can hold up to 1.5 L of fluid before evidence of distention is apparent" [PAGE 54]. Pregnancy: a third-trimester casualty "may lose 30% to 35% of total blood volume before showing signs of hypovolemia" [PAGE 55]. These are three expressions of one rule: **compensation masks volume loss, and hypotension is a late, ominous sign, not an early one.** The gray zone is that a medic trained to react to hypotension will, in these three contexts, react too late. The doctrine's countermeasure is to treat *mechanism and trend*, not absolute pressure.

Second coupling — **secondary injury is time-delayed and iatrogenically triggerable.** TBI primary injury is instantaneous; the killer is the secondary cascade (edema, rising ICP) which "can take up to 36 hours to present" [PAGE 29]. This means the medic's job is trajectory detection: serial GCS, because "a GCS score that deteriorates by more than 2 points is a significant finding" [PAGE 21]. The gray zone: the *absolute* GCS is nearly useless (floor/ceiling effects, intubated verbal = "T"); the *derivative* dGCS/dt is the real signal.

Third coupling — **the intervention that treats one problem creates a worse one.** Hyperventilation lowers PaCO₂ → cerebral vasoconstriction → aims to reduce ICP but "can worsen the outcome of casualties with head injuries" [PAGE 31]. Morphine controls pain but "increase[s] intracranial pressure" and obscures pupils [PAGE 32]. Rewarming frostbitten extremities before the core "actually LOWER[s] the core temperature" via acidosis/hyperkalemia washout [PAGE 139]. Pelvic-binder over-tightening in "high-stress environments" harms the casualty beyond the ≤33 lb design limit [PAGE 62]. This is the recurring trap: the reflexive fix is the iatrogenic injury.

A genuine internal tension worth flagging: the analgesia section is not fully self-consistent. It says ketamine and fentanyl "could make moderate to severe TBI worse but can be used with mTBI" [PAGE 32], then a later note says ketamine "has fewer cognitive adverse side effects than morphine" and "far less likely" to raise ICP. These are hedged, not contradictory, but a medic reading only one note would draw a stronger conclusion than the text supports. That is a documentation gray zone, not a clinical one.

**1. USAISR Rule of Ten (burn resuscitation)** [PAGE 64]:
$$R_{\text{init}} = 10 \times \mathrm{TBSA}_{\%}\ \ [\mathrm{mL/h}]$$
with a weight-dependent additive term (adults):
$$\Delta(m)=\begin{cases}0 & 88\text{–}175\ \mathrm{lb}\ (40\text{–}80\ \mathrm{kg})\\ 100 & 176\text{–}200\ \mathrm{lb}\ (80\text{–}91\ \mathrm{kg})\\ 200 & 201\text{–}225\ \mathrm{lb}\ (91\text{–}102\ \mathrm{kg})\\ 300 & 226\text{–}250\ \mathrm{lb}\ (103\text{–}113\ \mathrm{kg})\end{cases}\ \mathrm{mL/h}$$
Worked instance [PAGE 64]: $30\times10=300\ \mathrm{mL/h}$ for a 77 kg male at 30% TBSA.
**Titration target** [PAGE 65]: urine output $30\text{–}50\ \mathrm{mL/h}$.
**Breakdown regime:** valid only for $40\le m\le113$ kg; it is an *initial estimate*, and $\mathrm{TBSA}_{\%}$ estimation error (Rule of Nines / Rule of Palm, palm ≈ 1%) dominates the total error budget. Beyond 113 kg the linear correction is off-label.

**2. Glasgow Coma Scale** [PAGE 20, Fig 22-6; PAGE 26, Table 23-1]:
$$\mathrm{GCS}=E+V+M,\quad E\in\{1..4\},\ V\in\{1..5\},\ M\in\{1..6\},\ \mathrm{GCS}\in[3,15]$$
Intubated: score $E+M$, append "T" (e.g., 7T) [PAGE 20]. Worked cases [PAGE 35]: $E4\,V4\,M6=14$; deterioration to $E3\,V3\,M5=11$.
**Signal operator** [PAGE 21]: deterioration $\Delta\mathrm{GCS}\le -2$ ⇒ high risk of active ICP process. The derivative, not the level, is the decision variable.

**3. MACE 2 cognitive composite** [PAGE 29]:
$$\mathrm{MACE2}_{\text{cog}}=\underbrace{S_5}_{0\text{–}5}+\underbrace{S_6}_{0\text{–}15}+\underbrace{S_{15}}_{0\text{–}5}+\underbrace{S_{16}}_{0\text{–}5},\quad \max=30$$
with $S_{15}=S_{15A}+S_{15B}$, $S_{15A}\in[0,4]$ (reverse digits), $S_{15B}\in\{0,1\}$ (months reversed) [PAGE 27–28].
**Thresholds:** nonconcussed mean $\ge 28/30$; "$\le 25$ is the best indicator for true cognitive impairment" absent baseline [PAGE 29]. **Gray zone** = the interval $[25,28)$: uninterpretable without pre-deployment baseline. VOMS near-point-of-convergence $\ge 5\ \mathrm{cm}$ = abnormal [PAGE 28]; VOR at $180\ \mathrm{bpm}$, $\pm20^\circ$, 10 reps [PAGE 30].

**4. Ventilation operator** [PAGE 31]:
$$V_T\approx 350\text{–}500\ \mathrm{mL};\quad \dot V_E=V_T\cdot f$$
$f$: adult 10/min, child 20/min, infant 25/min; suspected raised ICP ≈ 20/min. Adult baseline $\dot V_E\approx 500\times10=5.0\ \mathrm{L/min}$. Targets: $\mathrm{SpO_2}\ge90\%$; $\mathrm{SBP}\ge90\ \mathrm{mmHg}$. Pupillary asymmetry $>1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ = abnormal [PAGE 21/32]. Combined hypoxia+hypotension mortality ≈ 44% [PAGE 32].

**5. Altitude operators** [PAGE 155–156]:
HACE: onset $>8{,}000\ \mathrm{ft}$; descend $\ge3{,}300\ \mathrm{ft}$ ($\approx1{,}006\ \mathrm{m}$); $\mathrm{O_2}\ 1\text{–}2\ \mathrm{L/min}$ to $\mathrm{SpO_2}\ge90\%$.
HAPE: onset $10{,}000\text{–}12{,}000\ \mathrm{ft}$; descend $1{,}700\text{–}3{,}300\ \mathrm{ft}$; $\mathrm{O_2}\ 4\text{–}6\ \mathrm{L/min}$.
Graded ascent constraint: sleeping-altitude gain $\le1{,}000\ \mathrm{ft/day}$ over prior night [PAGE 156].

**6. Hypothermia bands** [PAGE 150]: mild $94\text{–}97^\circ\mathrm{F}$ (34.4–36.1 °C); moderate $86\text{–}93^\circ\mathrm{F}$ (30.0–33.9 °C); severe $\le85^\circ\mathrm{F}$ ($\le29.4^\circ\mathrm{C}$). Cooling ceiling: do not drop core $<102^\circ\mathrm{F}$ [PAGE 149]. Frostbite forms typically $<32^\circ\mathrm{F}$ (0 °C) [PAGE 138].

**7. Crush/rhabdomyolysis** [PAGE 58–59]: pre-extrication crystalloid bolus $2\ \mathrm{L}$; urine-output goal $100\text{–}200\ \mathrm{mL/h}$; serum $\mathrm{K^+}$ ≈ 5× normal; tourniquet before extrication if entrapment $>2\ \mathrm{h}$.

3. **Pressure/packing on open or depressed skull fracture** → VETO: "Do not apply pressure dressings to depressed or open skull fractures" and "Do not pack depressed or open skull fractures with a hemostatic agent" [PAGE 32]; a "donut" keeps pressure off [PAGE 32]. **VETO 1 — Reflexive hyperventilation of a head-injured casualty.**
Counterfactual: medic, seeing a deteriorating GCS, hyperventilates to "blow off" presumed ICP.
Source-grounded prediction: outcome worsens — "Hyperventilation can worsen the outcome of casualties with head injuries" [PAGE 31]. Correct action is normocapnic ventilation at ~20/min with $\mathrm{SpO_2}\ge90\%$ and urgent transport. **Veto holds.**

**VETO 2 — Packing hemostatic gauze into an open depressed skull fracture.**
Counterfactual: medic treats a bleeding open skull defect as an ordinary scalp wound and packs it / applies a pressure dressing.
Source-grounded prediction: iatrogenic harm — bone driven into brain, infection entry. Text: "Do not pack depressed or open skull fractures with a hemostatic agent"; use a donut and "only sufficient pressure to stop the flow of blood" [PAGE 32]. **Veto holds.**
