<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression in this source is the analyst’s fallback to a stable centroid, a scaling ratio, or an average pairwise difference when the observation process becomes partial. The thesis documents each regression. Centroid centering of broadcast frames produces diffuse clusters because the observed centroid is unstable under censoring [PAGE 60 of 102]. Average differences can collapse roles when players switch or are misidentified [PAGE 61 of 102]. The scaling estimator assumes MCAR and fails because on-camera and offscreen movement distributions differ; the residual plot is evidence that “players move faster when on camera” [PAGE 49 of 102]. These are not merely technical errors. They are habits of mind: when the clock is short and the data are missing, the analyst reaches for translation invariance, ratio scaling, or simple averaging because those operations are familiar and computationally cheap. The source’s counter-trap is equally clear: do not merely add complexity; add an operator that respects the censoring mechanism. ACD is that operator because it compares point configurations as densities, is permutation invariant, and allows different observed counts \(N_x\) and \(N_y\) [PAGE 64 of 102]. MEDP is the second counter-trap because it estimates role distributions by minimizing role overlap rather than by averaging away switching [PAGE 65 of 102]. The mixture model with weighted assignment is the third counter-trap because it lets partially observed observations contribute only through observed coordinates [PAGE 72 of 102].

The recursive theory-of-mind layer is the distinction between what the player does and what the observer can see. Let \(O_t=1\) indicate that a player is inside the camera window and \(O_t=0\) indicate censoring. The analyst’s target is \(P(\text{load}_t\mid O_t=0)\), but the naive scaling estimator substitutes a function of \(P(\text{load}_t\mid O_t=1)\) and elapsed time. That substitution is valid only under MCAR. The residual evidence rejects MCAR for load metrics [PAGE 49 of 102]. A second-order theory-of-mind error is assuming that the camera follows a random sample of player behavior. In fact, the camera window is centered on an interpolated camera track tied to event locations [PAGE 44 of 102], so observability is correlated with play location and action. A third-order error is assuming that player identity is stable across broadcast tracks. The thesis says accurate player identification from broadcast data is an open research problem and that a player re-entering the camera window has a nonzero probability of being assigned an incorrect ID [PAGE 61 of 102]. That is why permutation-aware operators are necessary. The counter-trap is not to assume the observer sees everything; it is to model the observer’s partial view explicitly. ACD and MEDP do that by treating frames as sets and roles as distributions rather than as fixed labeled vectors. The clock enters in three places. First, temporal aggregation: 120-second windows are used because shorter windows were noisier, but the effective-sample-size calculation gave a median of 36 seconds and a range of 13 to 205 seconds [PAGE 70 of 102]. Second, match structure: averages do not span periods or player substitutions because tactical changes are likely to accompany those events [PAGE 70 of 102]. Third, Markov temporal homogeneity: the transition-density method assumes the mechanism is temporally homogeneous, which the thesis identifies as a limitation [PAGE 33 of 102]. The recursive conclusion is that the model must maintain separate beliefs about on-camera behavior, offscreen behavior, role identity, and time regime. Collapsing any of those beliefs into one average is the crisis habit; preserving them with explicit missingness, permutation, and time-window operators is the counter-trap.

The weather-layer veto is simpler: the source contains no weather covariates, no temperature, wind, rain, or surface-condition variables. Therefore any weather-mediated claim is outside the likelihood support. The nearest cross-layer variables actually present are stadium-specific pitch dimensions and homography: soccer pitch dimensions are not uniform, so a unique homography is needed for every stadium [PAGE 39 of 102], and prior work found that data accuracy was impacted by player velocities and varying pitch sizes [PAGE 43 of 102]. That supports a cross-stadium generalization veto, not a weather claim.

The adjudication rule is layered. A formation or load result is admissible only if the observation layer, the physical layer, and the biomechanical layer all pass. Observation-layer pass: either the method uses ACD plus MEDP, or it demonstrates that centroid or average-difference alignment is stable under the observed censoring. The source’s own evidence favors ACD for broadcast data, because average differences with broadcast data collapse the 4-3-2-1 and 4-3-3 defensive formations into a single cluster, whereas ACD-aligned estimates assign most observations in each named formation to a separate cluster [PAGE 77 of 102]. Physical-layer pass: frame alignment scaling factors stay within \((0.9,1.1)\) [PAGE 68 of 102]; if the optimizer wants a scale factor outside that band, the alignment is rejected rather than allowed to collapse the configuration. Biomechanical-layer pass: raw acceleration spikes above the stated plausibility range are smoothed or flagged [PAGE 51 of 102]. Cross-layer weather pass: because no meteorological variables appear in the source, no weather conclusion can pass; the only admissible environmental variation is the stadium and pitch-size variation explicitly discussed [PAGE 39 of 102] and [PAGE 43 of 102]. If any layer fails, the downstream claim is vetoed even if the clustering or regression metric looks good. That is the correct order of adjudication: a low RMSPE cannot rescue a physically implausible acceleration scale, and a high silhouette width cannot rescue a centroid that is unstable under censoring.
