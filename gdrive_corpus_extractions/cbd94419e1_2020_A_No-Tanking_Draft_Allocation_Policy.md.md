<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s causal chain starts with a league-level redistribution problem: without intervention, franchise performance is path dependent, and well-performing teams can convert attendance and media revenue into better players, reinforcing dominance. The authors state that “there is significant path dependence in a sports franchise’s performance” and that leagues use draft allocation mechanisms favoring the lowest-ranked teams, which can create incentives “known as ‘tanking’” [PAGE 1]. The central multi-hop inference is that a draft rule based only on final rank cannot generally preserve effort incentives, because the final-rank mapping is known before the season and therefore creates a last-game deviation for teams already outside the primary prize contest. The paper formalizes a season as m games among n teams, with histories \vec h_t, win counts, final rank v^{(k)}(\vec h_m), primary prize \pi_V for the top k^* teams, and a single secondary draft prize \pi_D allocated by lottery probabilities y_i(\vec h_t) [PAGE 4]. Each team chooses effort e_{it}\in[0,1], where e_{it}=1 is full effort and e_{it}=0 is tanking [PAGE 5]. Win probabilities are modeled through a latent performance draw and standard normal CDF \Phi, with the crucial property \partial p_{ijt}/\partial e_{it}>0 [PAGE 5]. The team’s payoff compares the probability of winning the primary prize q_i(\vec h_t) with the probability of receiving the secondary prize y_i(\vec h_t). Differentiating the expected payoff with respect to own effort gives the No-Tanking Condition: full effort is optimal when the gain in primary-prize probability from winning, scaled by \pi_V, is at least the gain in secondary-prize probability from losing, scaled by \pi_D [PAGE 6]. In symbols, \pi_V[q_i(W)-q_i(L)]\geq \pi_D[y_i(L)-y_i(W)] is the condition; if it fails, the team weakly prefers lower effort. The first theorem is a negative result: among rules that depend only on final rank and are non-increasing in rank, the only rule satisfying NTC for every possible history is a uniform lottery over teams that do not receive the primary prize [PAGE 6]. The proof is a last-game deviation: take two teams tied at ranks b and b+1, both mathematically eliminated from the primary prize. Then q_i(W)=q_i(L)=0, but if y_{b+1}>y_b, losing raises the draft probability, violating NTC. Since b was arbitrary, all non-playoff ranks must have equal draft probability [PAGE 6]. This is the gray-zone hinge: the theorem does not say all weighted lotteries are bad in every realized season; it says every non-uniform final-rank lottery has at least one feasible history with a profitable tank. The paper’s positive proposal is R-NTD, the No Tanking Draft Allocation Rule, which sets y_i(\vec h_t) equal to the conditional probability that team i finishes last given the history up to a stopping time t^* [PAGE 8]. The stopping time is the smallest s\leq t at which continued adjustment would violate NTC at s+1; if no such s exists, t^*=t [PAGE 8]. Thus the rule uses early-season information while the primary-prize race is still uncertain, then freezes lottery probabilities before a team can gain from losing. The proof claims R-NTD satisfies NTC, dynamic consistency, fairness, and probability normalization, and that it maximizes the league objective conditional on history up to t^* [PAGE 9]. The optimality argument is conditional, not global: the league objective is maximized by setting y_i equal to the conditional probability of finishing last, making the objective a sum of squares [PAGE 9]. The authors explicitly do not claim global optimality; they say other incentive-compatible rules might improve the objective or prescribe smaller early probability changes [PAGE 9]. The gray-zone audit must therefore distinguish three claims: (i) final-rank-only non-uniform lotteries fail NTC somewhere; (ii) R-NTD is incentive-compatible by construction under the model; (iii) R-NTD is approximately optimal conditional on t^*. Claim (iii) is not a universal optimality theorem. Additional breakdown regimes are explicit: if \pi_D/\pi_V is close to 1, NTC binds very early and the rule cannot improve much on a uniform lottery; as \pi_D/\pi_V grows, the stopping time moves later, but even with a negligible secondary prize some histories still violate NTC if every game is counted [PAGE 12]. The NBA game tree has 2^m histories with m=1,230, making exhaustive backward induction infeasible [PAGE 7]. The empirical examples are also conditional: in 1987 the probabilities freeze after the 266th game, giving the last-ranked Clippers 28.5% versus a uniform 14%; in 1989 the freeze occurs at the 332nd game and the last-ranked Heat receive 45.5% [PAGE 13; PAGE 14]. These are not proofs that tanking vanishes in real leagues; they are proofs under the model that the allocation rule avoids an incentive to tank while still favoring the worst team more than a uniform lottery does.

The model defines a season as sequences of home and away teams, a history \vec h_t, and team win totals. The key allocation rule is
\[
y_i(\vec h_t)=\Pr\left(v^{(n)}(\vec h_m)=i\mid \vec h_{t^*(\vec h_t)}\right)
\]
under R-NTD, where v^{(n)}(\vec h_m) is the last-ranked team [PAGE 8]. The paper calls this mechanism “the No Tanking Draft Allocation Rule (R-NTD)” [PAGE 8]. The probability normalization is
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i(\vec h_t)=1,
\]
and the dynamic-consistency condition is the conditional-expectation identity used in the proof:
\[
y_i(\vec h_{t-1})
=p_{it}(\alpha)y_i(\vec h_{t-1}\cup W)
+(1-p_{it}(\alpha))y_i(\vec h_{t-1}\cup L).
\]
The paper states that “After probabilities are fixed, DC holds since” the win and loss probabilities sum to one [PAGE 9]. The team’s effort choice is e_{it}\in[0,1], with “A team exerting full effort in a game has e_it = 1, while a team that is tanking has e_it = 0” [PAGE 5]. Win probability is modeled with the standard normal CDF \Phi, so that
\[
p_{ijt}=\Pr(i\text{ beats }j\text{ in game }t)=\Phi(\cdot),
\]
with the maintained property
\[
\frac{\partial p_{ijt}}{\partial e_{it}}>0.
\]
The No-Tanking Condition can be written as
\[
\pi_V\left[q_i(\vec h_{t-1}\cup W)-q_i(\vec h_{t-1}\cup L)\right]
\geq
\pi_D\left[y_i(\vec h_{t-1}\cup L)-y_i(\vec h_{t-1}\cup W)\right],
\]
where q_i is the probability of winning the primary prize and y_i is the probability of receiving the secondary prize [PAGE 6]. Equivalently, with B=\pi_V/\pi_D,
\[
B\left[q_i(W)-q_i(L)\right]
\geq
y_i(L)-y_i(W).
\]
The league objective is to maximize the probability that the secondary prize goes to the worst team while maintaining NTC in every game [PAGE 7]. The paper’s Theorem 1 states that the only final-rank-only non-increasing policy satisfying NTC for every history is a uniform lottery over teams that do not receive \pi_V [PAGE 6]. The stopping time is defined as the smallest s\leq t such that NTC would be violated at s+1 if draft probabilities continued to be adjusted; otherwise t^*=t [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regimes are explicit. As \pi_D/\pi_V\to 1, NTC binds very early, so R-NTD takes into account only one or two games and does not improve meaningfully on a uniform lottery [PAGE 12]. As \pi_D/\pi_V\to\infty, NTC holds until the first team is mathematically eliminated, but even then some histories fail if every game is counted [PAGE 12]. The exhaustive game-tree solution is computationally infeasible: for an NBA season m=1,230 and the tree has size 2^m [PAGE 7]. The rule is not globally optimal; it is optimal conditional on the history up to t^* [PAGE 9]. Extensions with multiple secondary prizes require a modified NTC with a sum over prize values and another probability-normalization constraint; the paper says there is no clear-cut way to determine whether expected t^* shifts earlier or later [PAGE 17]. The model also assumes abilities are known or equal and abstracts from effort costs, salary caps, and complementary policies [PAGE 7; PAGE 17]. Empirical parameterizations are not universal: the 1987 freeze at game 266 and 1989 freeze at game 332 are season-specific estimates [PAGE 13; PAGE 14].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from rank-order tournament theory to dynamic contest design. Lazear and Rosen (1981) show that prizes based on rank rather than absolute output can incentivize effort; Rosen (1986) extends this to sequential elimination contests where incentives change as contestants progress. The paper positions itself against that lineage by noting that its setting has repeated effort choices without elimination: losing teams keep playing, and the league controls only a secondary prize whose value is taken as given [PAGE 2]. The empirical lineage includes Taylor and Trogdon (2002), cited for documented shirking behavior, and the policy lineage includes Gold (2010), who proposed allocating the top pick by wins after playoff elimination, and Lenten (2016) and Lenten et al. (2018), who proposed giving the top pick to the team eliminated first from playoff contention [PAGE 2]. The paper’s Theorem 1 implies those rules are not fully incentive compatible under its framework; Gold’s rule also does not target the worst team, so it misses the league objective [PAGE 2]. Dagaev and Sonin (2018) model a different incentive incompatibility in multiple sequential tournaments, where a worse seed can yield a better draw; the paper explicitly sets that aside to focus on draft-allocation misspecification [PAGE 2]. The historical policy lineage is given in Appendix A: 1966–1984 used a coin flip between conference losers; 1985–1989 used a uniform lottery; 1990–present used a weighted lottery modified in 1994, 2005, 2010, and 2019 [PAGE 16]. This matters because the 1985–1989 uniform-lottery period is used as a real-world benchmark: the paper says “From 1985 to 1989, the NBA had a uniform lottery system” and that under the model teams did not have an incentive to intentionally lose [PAGE 12]. The multi-week trap is the dynamic path-dependence trap: a team that loses several early games can raise its conditional probability of finishing last, and thus its draft probability, before the league freezes the rule. In the three-team simulation, Team 1’s losing streak raises its draft probability above 70%, but later, when Team 1 is sufficiently behind, continued adjustment would violate NTC, so probabilities freeze; Team 2’s late winning streak does not change its draft probability because it occurs after the freeze [PAGE 11]. The empirical multi-week traps are visible in Table 1 and Table 2: in 1987 the Clippers receive 28.5% but the Kings receive 12.0% and the Spurs 9.0%, so final rank and draft probability are not monotone because the probabilities were set conditional on the first 266 games [PAGE 13]. In 1989 the Heat receive 45.5%, but the 24th-ranked Hornets receive 7.5% while the 20th-ranked Kings receive 12.0% and the 19th-ranked Pacers receive 18.5%, again because the timing of losses relative to the freeze matters [PAGE 14]. The retrocausal lesson is that late-season outcomes can be causally downstream of an earlier information cutoff; the rule does not retroactively reprice losses that occur after t^*. That is the intended anti-tanking device, but it also means the final ranking can diverge from the allocation, which is a deliberate decoupling rather than a bug.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive reasoning has three layers. First, each team reasons about its own future schedule, its current win total, its probability q_i of making the playoffs, and the league’s probability update y_i. Second, each team reasons about other teams’ effort choices, because p_{ijt} depends on both teams’ effort and ability; the paper suppresses effort in the incentive-compatible calculation because it focuses on mechanisms where equilibrium effort is 1 [PAGE 5]. Third, the league reasons about the first game at which some team would prefer to tank, which is exactly the stopping time t^* [PAGE 8]. The “crisis habit regression” is the tanking habit that emerges once a team is out of the primary-prize race: under a final-rank-only weighted lottery, the team’s q_i(W) and q_i(L) are both zero, so the only remaining payoff difference is the draft probability, and losing becomes attractive [PAGE 6]. R-NTD counter-traps this by freezing the draft probabilities before that deviation becomes profitable; the proof says that if NTC were violated at time t, R-NTD would already have fixed probabilities at some s<t, making the right-hand side of the NTC inequality zero, while the left-hand side is weakly positive [PAGE 9]. The “clock” is the season clock and the information clock: the league updates y_i as game results arrive, but stops the clock at t^*, which can be early or late depending on the prize ratio and the realized history [PAGE 8; PAGE 12]. In the three-team simulation, the clock stops after Team 1’s second losing streak, when Team 1 is sufficiently behind that it no longer has a meaningful probability of winning the primary prize [PAGE 11]. In 1987 the clock stops after the 266th game; in 1989 after the 332nd game [PAGE 13; PAGE 14]. The recursive trap is that a team may anticipate the freeze and try to lose before it, but the freeze is defined precisely to prevent continued adjustment from rewarding that loss. The counter-trap is not perfect in a global sense: the paper admits that other incentive-compatible rules might change probabilities after t^* or prescribe smaller early changes [PAGE 9]. There is also a fairness counter-trap: because probabilities freeze, a team that performs badly after the freeze does not receive the same draft-probability increase as a team that performed badly before the freeze. That is why the 1989 table is not monotone in final rank [PAGE 14]. The recursive theory of mind therefore predicts that strategic teams will reason about the cutoff, not just the final rank; the league’s rule is designed to make the cutoff the binding strategic object. The model assumes that if a team is indifferent between effort and tanking, it exerts effort, because winning is preferred in the short term absent long-term incentives [PAGE 6]. This tie-breaking assumption matters: without it, the NTC boundary would be weaker. The paper also assumes abilities are known or equal for the baseline, and in practice says win probabilities can be replaced by sport-specific scoring models as long as the derivative with respect to effort remains positive [PAGE 17]. That is the recursive consistency condition: the league’s forecast model, the team’s beliefs about q_i, and the draft-probability update must all be generated from the same information set \vec h_t; otherwise the stopping time is misspecified.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no physical, biomechanical, or weather layer. It is an economic tournament model with probabilities, effort, prize values, and histories. Therefore a physical or weather veto cannot be adjudicated from the paper’s own evidence. The correct adjudication is conditional: if a physical or weather variable X_t changes win probability p_{ijt} but is omitted from the forecast model, then the estimated q_i and y_i used to compute t^* can be biased, and the NTC guarantee holds only conditional on the model’s probability forecasts. The paper explicitly allows replacement of the baseline win-probability model with “any kind of sport-specific scoring model that estimates win probabilities and team specific factors” [PAGE 17]. That extension does not require the omitted variable to be physical; it requires the derivative of win probability with respect to effort to remain positive [PAGE 17]. A physical/biomechanical veto would therefore be valid only if it breaks that derivative or breaks the conditional-expectation identity. For example, if fatigue or injury changes the mapping from effort to win probability in a way that makes \partial p_{ijt}/\partial e_{it}\leq 0, the NTC derivation fails. If weather or travel changes p_{ijt} but is known and included in \vec h_t, the model can absorb it as a state variable without changing the formal structure. If it is latent and correlated with losses, then observed late-season losses cannot be interpreted as e_{it}=0; they could be physical regression, injury, or schedule effects. The paper’s own cross-layer boundary is the single-season abstraction: it ignores salary caps, lump-sum transfers, fan preferences, and long-term revenue interactions [PAGE 7]. Those are league-layer variables, not biomechanical ones, but they impose the same kind of veto: the model’s objective is justified only if long-term expected league revenue increases as the rank of the receiving team decreases [PAGE 7]. The empirical veto is also explicit: the 1987 and 1989 probabilities are based on simulations of the remainder of the season to approximate q_i, and the cutoff is an estimate [PAGE 13]. If the forecast model is wrong, the cutoff is wrong, and the claimed NTC satisfaction is only as good as the forecast. The paper’s robustness claim is narrow: the NTC derivation depends only on the derivative of win probability with respect to effort being positive, which must be true for any model [PAGE 17]. It does not claim robustness to unmodeled physical states that make effort unobservable or that change the causal effect of effort. Thus the cross-layer veto proof is: no source-level physical/weather veto exists; any such veto must be introduced as an additional state variable or as a failure of \partial p/\partial e>0; absent that, the model’s incentive conclusion stands within its domain.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Uniform-lottery era as a negative control. The model predicts that a final-rank-only uniform lottery over non-playoff teams satisfies NTC, so teams in 1985–1989 should not have a systematic post-elimination incentive to lose. The paper states “From 1985 to 1989, the NBA had a uniform lottery system” and that under the model teams did not have an incentive to intentionally lose [PAGE 12]. A verifiable RLVR test would compare post-elimination win probabilities or effort proxies in 1985–1989 against the weighted-lottery era. Prediction: no positive tanking effect in the uniform era. If a significant post-elimination tanking effect appears under uniform allocation, the model’s tie-breaking or effort-cost assumptions are vetoed, because Theorem 1 says uniform final-rank allocation is NTC-compatible [PAGE 6]. Scenario 2: Recompute the 1987 freeze and allocation. The paper reports that “The draft probabilities in this season were not adjusted after the 266th game” and that the last-ranked Clippers receive 28.5% while the uniform lottery would give 14% [PAGE 13]. A verifiable RLVR test would reconstruct the 1987 game order, recompute the conditional last-place probabilities after game 266, and check whether the reported probabilities and NTC inequality are reproduced. Prediction: the recomputation yields the same cutoff and the same qualitative non-monotonicity, with the Kings above the Spurs because the probabilities were set conditional on the first 266 games [PAGE 13]. If the recomputation shows NTC violated at the reported cutoff, the empirical implementation is vetoed even if the theorem is correct. Scenario 3: High-value draft-prize stress test. The model predicts that when \pi_D/\pi_V is close to 1 or below, NTC binds very early and R-NTD degenerates toward a near-uniform lottery; when the draft prize is especially valuable, a near-uniform lottery may be the only NTC-compatible rule [PAGE 12]. A verifiable RLVR test would take a season with an unusually high expected draft value, estimate \pi_D/\pi_V, and check whether the rule’s implied cutoff is early and whether the allocation is close to uniform. Prediction: high draft value produces early t^* and small deviations from uniform; if instead the rule still assigns a large probability to the worst team while teams have a strong post-elimination incentive to lose, the implementation is vetoed. A fourth, optional stress test is the multiple-prize extension: the paper says a modified NTC must sum prize-value-weighted probability changes, and that with two secondary prizes incentive compatibility could bind earlier than in the benchmark [PAGE 17]. Prediction: adding multiple secondary prizes with decreasing value can shift t^* earlier, not necessarily later; a test that finds no shift under multiple prizes would veto the paper’s stated ambiguity.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
