An opposing coach who models this regression can set a counter-trap: if the offense is expected to revert to a slow placement or a conservative snap under pressure, the defense keys the line of scrimmage earlier, rushes the snap, or collapses the pocket before the quarterback can execute the first-receiver function [PAGE 99]. The scrimmage snap imposes a hard temporal boundary: once the ball is snapped back with the hand [PAGE 99], the play clock (or, in 1900, the informal pace of scrimmage) converts a static set-piece into a dynamic event with no recall. The center-rusher's placement allowed deliberation; the snap-back commits the offense to a single temporal vector. In recursive modeling, this clock pressure collapses the depth of feasible counter-trapping: there is insufficient time to run multiple levels of "he thinks that I think" before the ball is live, so the system defaults to the lowest-latency habit (regression to placement) under duress.
