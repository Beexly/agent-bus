<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis decision problem has at least four agents: the injured player, the medical staff, the manager, and the referee/crowd. Each models the others’ thresholds. The player anticipates that signaling symptoms may cost his place in the match; the manager anticipates that removal weakens the team; the medical staff anticipates blame for missing a concussion versus blame for removing a player unnecessarily; the crowd and broadcast audience reward continuity. In this recursive equilibrium, a brief on-pitch check is a low-cost compromise: it signals that “something was done” while preserving RTP. That is crisis habit regression. The observed pattern fits it: 139 of 183 PCEs returned after on-pitch assessment (76.0%) [PAGE 3], and 128 of 158 assessments were under 1 min (81.0%) [PAGE 1]. The habit is not irrational at the individual level; it is reinforced by the immediate social reward of keeping the player on and by ambiguity about whether a PCE is a concussion.

The counter-trap is that stricter removal can create concealment incentives. If players believe any visible sign leads to automatic removal, they may try to hide signs, avoid staying down for 5 s, or minimize symptoms; if staff believe removal is socially costly, they may perform a “brief assessment” that satisfies appearances without the 10-minute SCAT-5. The paper’s own guideline standard—“removed from play immediately and medically assessed using the SCAT-5 protocol, which takes a minimum of 10 min to complete” [PAGE 4]—sets a high bar that is easy to simulate superficially. The counter-trap is therefore not “assessment causes harm”; it is “unenforced assessment mandates can produce compliance theater and displace honest disclosure.” A well-designed policy must make removal credible, protected, and non-punitive for the player and staff, otherwise the recursive belief equilibrium remains: player expects staff to keep him on, staff expects manager to prefer RTP, and manager expects referee/crowd to tolerate brief stoppage.

The clock layer operates on two scales. The in-match clock creates final-minutes pressure; the paper recorded match time to search for an RTP relationship [PAGE 3] and notes that one removed player suffered the PCE “in the final 5 min of the match” [PAGE 3/4], but does not present a full time-by-RTP model. The season clock creates an implementation lag from November 2018 training to 2019 practice [PAGE 2], and the multi-season clock creates staff turnover and training decay. A recursive theory-of-mind reading predicts that clock pressure should interact with sign severity: visible, severe signs force removal, while ambiguous signs are resolved in favor of RTP. That is consistent with the source’s conditional removal pattern: 10 of 43 PCEs with at least one sign were removed (23.3%), while only 3 of 140 with zero signs were removed (2.1%) [PAGE 1; PAGE 4, Table 1]. The policy implication is that the gray zone—ambiguous, helmet-obscured, transient signs—is exactly where habit and clock pressure dominate, and where a mandatory off-pitch SCAT-5 pathway would have the largest effect.
