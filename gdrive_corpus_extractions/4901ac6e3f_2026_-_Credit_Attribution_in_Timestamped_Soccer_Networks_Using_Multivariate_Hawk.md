First, the paper converts a match into a continuous-time directed network: “The collection of passes during a soccer match is naturally modeled as a continuous-time network with nodes denoting players and timestamped directed edges denoting passes from one player to a teammate at a specific time during the match” [PAGE 1]. Second, rather than making each player a Hawkes dimension, the model makes each dimension a sender-receiver pair, because “each variable or dimension in the MHP corresponds not to a single player, unlike in [2, 17], but to a pair of players” [PAGE 5]. Third, the conditional intensity of each pair-dimension is a sum of a baseline rate and exponentially decaying excitation from all prior pair-events [PAGE 3]. Fourth, the fitted excitation matrix is converted into parent probabilities: “An MHP can be used to compute the probability that an event l is the parent of event k, often called the responsibility of event l for event k” [PAGE 4]. Fifth, for each shot the model examines “the 6 preceding passes” [PAGE 7], multiplies the shot’s xG by each pass’s responsibility, and sums over shots to obtain xGI [PAGE 8].

The multi-hop audit is where the gray zones appear. The word “responsibility” is not a counterfactual causal effect; it is a model-conditional parent probability under a single-parent branching assumption. The paper’s own wording is precise: “l = 0 denotes that the event occurred from the baseline rate and is not a child of a previous event” [PAGE 4]. Thus z is a normalized probability over the baseline and prior events, not an experimental attribution. This matters because the model cannot represent simultaneous multi-causation, defensive pressure, off-ball movement, or a pass that was intentionally played into a low-threat area. The denominator of z is the total intensity at the shot time; if the baseline is small and many recent passes have small α, the probability mass is spread thinly, and small numerical changes in α can reorder credit. The paper guards against zero α by smoothing: “we smooth the estimated α by taking a convex combination of the MLE and a scaled all-ones matrix” [PAGE 7], with ε = 0.3, so “the effective floor of the matrix ≈ 0.001” [PAGE 7]. That floor is a modeling prior, not observed influence. It intentionally prevents an unobserved pair from receiving exactly zero credit, but it also injects uniform credit into every shot window.

A second gray zone is the top-100 pair filter. The dataset had “roughly 500 sender-receiver pairs,” but the model keeps “only the 100 most frequent pairs” [PAGE 5]. Rare pairs and most substitute combinations are therefore out of vocabulary or floored. The smoothing makes the model robust to unseen pairs, but it does not recover their true excitation. A third gray zone is the fixed decay parameter: “we choose the decay parameter β = 0.2. With this choice, about 98% of the influence of a pass on a future pass is gone after 20 seconds” [PAGE 6]. The authors later admit “the manual selection of β” as a limitation [PAGE 11]. A fourth gray zone is likelihood selection: regularization was tested, but “the unregularized model achieving the lowest value” led to “the unregularized MHP model, which is the maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE)” [PAGE 6]. That is defensible on validation NLL, but unregularized α matrices can be unstable when pair-events are sparse. A fifth gray zone is the six-pass window: it is a truncation rule, not a probability that passes beyond six events have zero influence. A sixth is temporal bookkeeping: the second half has “600 seconds” added to avoid overlap with first-half stoppage time [PAGE 5], but simultaneous timestamps and tie-breaking are not fully specified. The model is therefore strongest as a descriptive, within-possession credit allocator and weaker as a causal or cross-week talent estimator.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines an exponential multivariate Hawkes process with conditional intensity
\[
\lambda_i(t\mid \mathcal H_t)=\mu_i+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{t_\ell\in T_j,\,t_\ell\lt t}\alpha_{ij}\beta e^{-\beta(t-t_\ell)}.
\]
Here \(\lambda_i(t\mid \mathcal H_t)\) is the instantaneous event rate for pair-dimension \(i\), with units of events per second; \(\mu_i\) is the baseline rate in events per second; \(\alpha_{ij}\) is a dimensionless normalized jump size; \(\beta\) is the exponential decay rate in inverse seconds; and \(d\) is the number of pair-dimensions. The paper states that “each variable i corresponds to a specific pair of players: a sender is who initiates the pass and a receiver ir” [PAGE 4], and that “αij measures the influence of a pass from js → jr on a future pass from is → ir” [PAGE 4]. The stability assumption is \(\rho(\alpha)\lt 1\), where \(\rho(\alpha)\) is the spectral radius of the excitation matrix; if \(\rho(\alpha)\ge 1\), the linear Hawkes process is non-stationary or explosive, and the fitted α cannot be interpreted as a stable branching matrix. A second breakdown regime is \(\mu_i=0\) with no prior events in the denominator of the responsibility equation; then the parent probability is undefined. A third is simultaneous timestamps, where the ordering \(t_\ell\lt t\) is ambiguous.

The responsibility of event \(l\) for event \(k\) is
\[
z_{kl}=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\alpha_{v(k)v(l)}\beta e^{-\beta(t_k-t_l)}}{\mu_{v(k)}+\sum_{t_{l'}\lt t_k}\alpha_{v(k)v(l')}\beta e^{-\beta(t_k-t_{l'})}}, & 0\lt l\lt k,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{\mu_{v(k)}}{\mu_{v(k)}+\sum_{t_{l'}\lt t_k}\alpha_{v(k)v(l')}\beta e^{-\beta(t_k-t_{l'})}}, & l=0.
\end{cases}
\]
The paper introduces this as “the probability that an event l is the parent of event k” [PAGE 4]. Each \(z_{kl}\) is dimensionless and, by construction, the probabilities over \(l=0\) and all prior \(l\lt k\) sum to one. Breakdown occurs when the denominator is zero or numerically tiny: if all \(\alpha_{v(k)v(l')}=0\) and \(\mu_{v(k)}=0\), the ratio is \(0/0\); if the denominator is near machine precision, the estimate is unstable. Large \(\beta\) makes only the most recent pass matter; small \(\beta\) makes the six-pass window a severe truncation.

Smoothing is
\[
\alpha=(1-\epsilon)\alpha^{\mathrm{MLE}}+\epsilon\frac{0.5}{M}\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top,\qquad \epsilon=0.3,
\]
where \(M\) is the number of sender-receiver pairs and \(\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\) is the all-ones matrix. The paper says “We choose ϵ = 0.3 so that the final αij value is 70% based on the MLE and 30% based on a small non-zero value 0.5/M” [PAGE 7]. The scaling \(0.5/M\) is dimensionless and is chosen so the largest eigenvalue of the scaled all-ones matrix is less than one, preserving MHP stability. The paper reports “the effective floor of the matrix ≈ 0.001” [PAGE 7]. If \(M=0\), the smoothing expression is undefined; if \(M\) is very large, the floor becomes negligible and the out-of-vocabulary protection weakens.

The xGI metric is
\[
\mathrm{xGI}_{l_s}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(xG)_k\,z_{kl},
\]
where \(m\) is the number of shots in a game, \((xG)_k\in[0,1]\) is the dimensionless expected-goal value of shot \(k\), \(z_{kl}\) is the dimensionless responsibility of pass \(l\) for shot \(k\), and \(l_s\) denotes the sender of pass \(l\). The paper defines xGI as a metric that “measures a player’s involvement in creating goal scoring opportunities based on their passes” [PAGE 1] and distributes “the expected goal (xG) value of a shot” among contributors [PAGE 8]. The main assumptions are linear additivity of credit across shots, fixed xG values independent of attribution, a six-pass lookback window [PAGE 7], and no explicit defensive, goalkeeping, or off-ball actions. Breakdown regimes include missing xG, zero xG, a pass outside the six-event window, a sender-receiver pair outside the top-100 filter, and any match state in which the fitted α is non-stationary across halves or weeks.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal genealogy runs from last-touch attribution to multi-touch attribution to continuous-time branching attribution. In online advertising, “the last ad that a user clicks on is often assigned all of the credit if the user then makes a purchase,” which the paper calls “last-touch attribution” and compares to “giving an assist to the player making the final pass before a goal” [PAGE 4]. Multi-touch attribution then distributes credit across prior events, just as xGI distributes shot xG across prior passes. In soccer analytics, the lineage moves from goals and assists to xG and xA [PAGE 3], then to xGChain, which gives equal credit to every player in a possession; the paper criticizes that because “a player who makes the first pass in a long passing sequence receives equal credit to the player who makes the last pass” [PAGE 3]. The Hawkes lineage supplies the missing time-decayed branching mechanism: “A Hawkes process [9] is a self-exciting TPP where the occurrence of an event may influence the occurrence of additional events shortly thereafter” [PAGE 3]. Prior MHP soccer models such as FeatHawkes and Baouan et al. use player dimensions and branching or descendant matrices [PAGE 4]; this paper’s shift to sender-receiver pair dimensions is the genealogical novelty. The MULCH model is cited as a possible grouping extension, but the authors note it “can only perform last-touch attribution” in its current form [PAGE 11].

The multi-week traps are nonstationarity traps. First, a player’s role can change across weeks because of injury, rotation, or tactical repositioning; the top-100 pair set is estimated from a season, but weekly xGI uses a fixed α. Second, opponents adapt: a high-α hub that works in weeks 1–5 may be denied in weeks 6–10, so observed credit and latent influence diverge. Third, fixture congestion changes execution quality; the model has no fatigue covariate. Fourth, weather seasonality changes ball roll, flight, and traction, but α is pooled across conditions. Fifth, score-state effects are unmodeled: trailing teams may pass more urgently and through different pairs. Sixth, data-vendor xG revisions can change the target variable even if the pass network is unchanged. These are not minor caveats; they are time-scale mismatches between a season-level α and weekly attribution.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer separates three mechanisms that can mimic credit. Crisis habit regression occurs when a team under pressure reverts to its most frequent, highest-α passing pair. The model will assign high responsibility to those passes because α was learned from repeated normal-state behavior, but the pass may be a forced safety valve rather than a chance-creating action. Counter-trap behavior occurs when an opponent deliberately leaves a low-α passing lane open, inviting a pass that the model treats as contributory because it precedes a shot, even though the defender’s intent was to make that pass harmless. Clock pressure occurs late in a match, when time remaining changes risk tolerance; the model’s fixed β and six-pass window do not condition on score, time, or urgency. The paper acknowledges that “the model does not assign responsibility solely based on the time gap between a pass and a shot” [PAGE 7], which is an advantage over a pure temporal-decay baseline, but the learned α still cannot distinguish “this pass created the shot” from “this pass was the habitually available pass before a low-quality shot.” A recursive opponent model would need to discount credit for passes that were baited, forced, or made under extreme clock pressure. Without that layer, xGI can overcredit central hubs in late-game states and undercredit players who make rare but decisive progressive passes.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather veto is a data-generating-process veto. The Hawkes likelihood treats an observed pass as the event that occurred; it does not model the probability that an intended pass was executed successfully. Let \(I_{ij}(t)\in\{0,1\}\) denote an intended pass between pair \(ij\), and \(O_{ij}(t)\in\{0,1\}\) the observed pass. Under independent execution noise with \(P(O=1\mid I=1)=1-q_{ij}\) and \(P(O=1\mid I=0)=0\), the observed excitation is attenuated:
\[
\mathbb E[\hat\alpha_{ij}\mid I_{ij}=1]=(1-q_{ij})\alpha_{ij}.
\]
Here \(q_{ij}\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless execution-error probability. Rain, wind, poor traction, fatigue, injury, and awkward body orientation all raise \(q_{ij}\), especially for long or fast passes. If \(q_{ij}\) differs systematically between long-ball hubs and short-pass hubs, the fitted α and the resulting xGI ranking are biased even if the Hawkes optimizer converges perfectly. The biomechanical veto is therefore not a soft caveat: it changes the likelihood’s missing-data mechanism. The physical veto is topological: a formation change or substitution changes the sender-receiver pair set and the effective \(M\), so a season-level α may not be identifiable for the new configuration. The weather veto is stratification: the source data are 2015/2016 La Liga [PAGE 5], but the model does not stratify by pitch condition or wind. The paper’s own admission of “manual selection of β” [PAGE 11] compounds the weather problem, because wet or windy conditions alter the effective time scale of pass influence. A valid adjudication must therefore veto xGI rankings when execution-noise bias or network-topology change is large enough to reorder players, even if the NLL is good.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
