This audit treats the supplied manuscript as a technical claim about long-term multi-object tracking in sports video, not as an isolated algorithmic trick. The central claim is explicit: “We introduce McByte++, a training-free tracking-by-detection framework that integrates lightweight mask propagation, conditional camera motion compensation, and online re-identification within a unified pipeline” [PAGE 1]. The manuscript positions McByte++ as an extension of McByte, with the stated empirical consequence that it obtains “up to +3.0 HOTA and +6.1 IDF1 improvements over the original McByte in the online setting, with further gains when combined with offline global association” [PAGE 1]. The first reasoning hop is therefore to reconstruct the causal chain from architecture to identity-preservation metrics: the system starts from ByteTrack-style detection association, then adds three conditional modules: mask guidance, camera motion compensation, and long-term re-identification. The source says McByte++ “adopts a selective integration strategy in which each cue is used only when it is expected to provide complementary information” [PAGE 5]. That phrase is the core gray-zone governor: it means the system is not a monolithic fusion network, but a set of gates controlling when a cue may alter the association cost.

The second hop is to formalize the short-term association layer. The source states that “The association problem is then formulated as a bipartite matching task and solved using the Hungarian algorithm” [PAGE 4]. In standard ByteTrack-style operation, the matching cost is based on bounding-box overlap, and the manuscript says the “IoU-based cost matrix is constructed as 1 −IoU” [PAGE 4]. The mask module does not replace this cost everywhere. It intervenes only under ambiguity or isolation. The source defines ambiguity as cases where a detection can plausibly match multiple tracklets and isolation as cases where a tracklet and detection are spatially distant despite potentially corresponding to the same object [PAGE 5]. The formal operators are the mask coverage ratio and mask fill ratio. The manuscript gives these as pixel-count ratios in [0,1] [PAGE 6]. The important gray-zone is that the fill ratio is subtracted from the IoU cost when gating conditions are satisfied. This can make a match cheaper even if the original IoU cost is high, but only when the mask is visible, sufficiently fills the detection box, and is sufficiently covered by the detection box. The source emphasizes that coverage is used “strictly as a gating criterion rather than as a cost modifier” [PAGE 6], while fill ratio modifies cost. That asymmetry matters: coverage prevents tiny mask leakage into a large box from causing false guidance, while fill ratio measures how much of the detection box is actually explained by the propagated mask.

The third hop is the long-term re-identification layer. The source says a key design choice is “to decouple re-identification from short-term association” [PAGE 7]. Instead of applying appearance similarity at every frame, McByte++ extracts OSNet features from reliable detections, normalizes them, aggregates them over the tracklet, stores terminated identities in memory, and compares new tracklets to stored identities using cosine similarity. The acceptance rule is not merely “nearest neighbor”; the manuscript requires a “mutual best-match criterion” [PAGE 7]. This is a conservative matching policy, because it reduces one-sided greedy reattachments. The selected default threshold is 0.8 [PAGE 8], and the ablation text explains why intermediate thresholds are preferred: “overly low thresholds (e.g., 0.6) introduce incorrect long-term associations” while “overly high thresholds (0.9) become too restrictive” [PAGE 10]. The source also admits residual risk: “very similar teammates may still occasionally remain challenging” [PAGE 7]. That admission is crucial for a gray-zone audit: appearance-based reconnection is not identity-ontologically certain; it is a probabilistic heuristic constrained by threshold, aggregation, and mutual best matching.

The fourth hop is to inspect the evidence structure. The ablation tables show that most of the identity-preserving gain comes from re-identification rather than from mask replacement alone. On SoccerNet-tracking 2022, the conditional CMC no-re-ID configuration with ds=4 reports HOTA 84.1 and IDF1 78.9, while online re-ID with threshold 0.8 reports HOTA 87.5 and IDF1 84.5 [PAGE 9]. The runtime cost is also explicit: FPS values are reported in frames per second, and the lightweight mask replacement produces large speed gains [PAGE 9]. For example, SoccerNet runtime moves from 1.04 FPS for McByte to 6.49 FPS with ds=2 and 10.51 FPS with ds=4 before re-ID [PAGE 9]. This means the paper’s efficiency claim is not merely qualitative; it is operationalized as frames per second under specified detector and mask components.

The final gray-zone audit concerns missing formalization. The manuscript does not provide a complete reliability predicate for conditional camera motion compensation, does not specify the exact temporal reconnection window, does not give the memory capacity bound, and does not print the full HOTA/IDF1 equations, instead relying on standard benchmark definitions [PAGE 9]. Let a frame at time \(t\) contain detections \(D_t=\{d_{t,j}\}_{j=1}^{M_t}\) and active tracklets \(T_{t-1}=\{\tau_{t-1,i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{t-1}}\). Each detection has bounding box \(b_{t,j}\), and each tracklet has a predicted bounding box \(b_{t|t-1,i}\). The source states that ByteTrack separates detections into high- and low-confidence groups and uses an IoU-based cost matrix [PAGE 4]. The formal IoU cost operator is

\[
C^{\mathrm{IoU}}_{ij}
=
1-
\frac{
\left|b_{t|t-1,i}\cap b_{t,j}\right|
}{
\left|b_{t|t-1,i}\cup b_{t,j}\right|
}.
\]

Units are dimensionless. The breakdown regime is \( |b_{t|t-1,i}\cup b_{t,j}|=0 \), which occurs if both boxes have zero area. The Hungarian assignment solves a minimum-cost bipartite matching over the cost matrix, subject to a rejection threshold for excessively costly matches [PAGE 4].

The mask-guidance layer introduces two pixel-count operators. Let \(S_{t,i}=\mathrm{mask}(\tau_i)\) be the temporally propagated mask associated with tracklet \(i\), and let \(b_{t,j}\) be detection box \(j\). The bounding-box coverage of the mask is defined in the source as

\[
m^{c}_{ij}
=
\frac{
\left|S_{t,i}\cap b_{t,j}\right|
}{
\left|S_{t,i}\right|
}.
\]

The mask fill ratio of the bounding box is

\[
m^{f}_{ij}
=
\frac{
\left|S_{t,i}\cap b_{t,j}\right|
}{
\left|b_{t,j}\right|
}.
\]

Both \(m^{c}_{ij}\) and \(m^{f}_{ij}\) are dimensionless ratios in \([0,1]\), with numerator and denominator expressed as pixel counts [PAGE 6]. The manuscript fixes the operating thresholds as “minimum mask fill ratio threshold is set to mf = 0.05, while the minimum mask coverage threshold is set to mc = 0.90” [PAGE 6]. The gating conditions are: mask visibility in the current frame, sufficient mask fill, and sufficient box coverage. If these hold, the association cost is modified as

\[
C_{ij}
=
\begin{cases}
C^{\mathrm{IoU}}_{ij}-m^{f}_{ij}, & \text{if gating conditions hold},\\
C^{\mathrm{IoU}}_{ij}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

This is the paper’s Eq. (3) [PAGE 6]. The formal breakdown regimes are: \( |S_{t,i}|=0 \), which makes \(m^{c}_{ij}\) undefined; \( |b_{t,j}|=0 \), which makes \(m^{f}_{ij}\) undefined; and mask drift, where \(S_{t,i}\) is visible and numerically plausible but spatially wrong. The source explicitly warns that masks may be inaccurate due to “drift, partial occlusions, or rapid motion” [PAGE 5], which is why the gating strategy is used instead of unconditional mask cost injection. A further gray-zone is that the modified cost can become negative when \(m^{f}_{ij}>C^{\mathrm{IoU}}_{ij}\). Negative costs are not inherently invalid for Hungarian matching, but they create a strong preference for the masked pairing. This is safe only if the mask is reliable; if not, the regulator fails locally.

The re-identification layer uses appearance descriptors. Let \(f_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) be an unnormalized feature extracted from a reliable detection. The source normalizes it as

\[
\widetilde{f}_t
=
\frac{f_t}{\|f_t\|_2}.
\]

This is Eq. (4) [PAGE 6]. The norm has units of the feature space; after normalization the descriptor is dimensionless and lies on the unit hypersphere. The singularity is \( \|f_t\|_2=0 \), in which case normalization is undefined. For a tracklet with normalized descriptors \(\{\widetilde{f}_1,\dots,\widetilde{f}_N\}\), the average descriptor is

\[
\bar{f}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\widetilde{f}_n,
\]

and the normalized tracklet descriptor is

\[
\widetilde{\bar{f}}
=
\frac{\bar{f}}{\|\bar{f}\|_2}.
\]

These correspond to the paper’s Eqs. (5) and (6) [PAGE 7]. The singularity is \(N=0\) or \( \|\bar{f}\|_2=0 \). Cosine similarity between a provisional new tracklet descriptor \( \widetilde{f}^{(p)} \) and a stored identity descriptor \( \widetilde{f}^{(m)} \) is

\[
\operatorname{sim}
\left(
\widetilde{f}^{(p)},
\widetilde{f}^{(m)}
\right)
=
\widetilde{f}^{(p)}
\cdot
\widetilde{f}^{(m)}.
\]

This is Eq. (7) [PAGE 7]. The value is dimensionless and lies in \([-1,1]\). The source selects a default similarity threshold of 0.8 [PAGE 8]. Acceptance also requires mutual best match: the new tracklet must choose the stored identity as its maximum-similarity candidate, and that stored identity must choose the same new tracklet as its maximum-similarity candidate among all new candidates [PAGE 7]. Formally, if \(S_{pm}=\widetilde{f}^{(p)}\cdot\widetilde{f}^{(m)}\), acceptance requires

\[
S_{pm}>\tau,
\qquad
\arg\max_{m'} S_{pm'}=m,
\qquad
\arg\max_{p'} S_{p'm}=p.
\]

The threshold \( \tau \) is not a probability. It is a cosine-similarity cutoff. If one wanted a probabilistic interpretation, calibration would be required, and information-theoretic units such as bits would appear only after defining a loss or entropy functional; the source does not perform that calibration.

The camera-motion-compensation layer is formalized in the source only partially. It states that global camera motion is modeled as an affine transformation on tracked object positions and estimated using ORB-based feature matching [PAGE 8]. A formal affine map on a bounding-box reference point \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^2\), measured in pixels, is

\[
x'_t = A x_t + u,
\]

where \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\) is a dimensionless linear map and \(u\in\mathbb{R}^2\) is a translation in pixels. The source says compensation is applied only when reliability criteria prevent degenerate transformations [PAGE 7]. A formal reliability predicate can be written as

\[
\mathcal{R}
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\text{ORB correspondences reliable}
\right]
\cdot
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\det(A)\neq 0
\right]
\cdot
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\|A\|_2 \leq \alpha_{\max}
\right]
\cdot
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\|u\|_2 \leq \beta_{\max}
\right].
\]

The exact thresholds \( \alpha_{\max} \) and \( \beta_{\max} \) are not specified in the source, which is a reproduction gray-zone. The breakdown regimes are: insufficient ORB matches, \( \det(A)\approx 0 \) causing collapse, excessive scaling causing box explosion, and unstable estimates under weak texture or motion blur. The source explicitly warns that inaccurate motion estimates can lead to “distorted bounding boxes, unstable Kalman predictions, and severe degradation of tracking performance” [PAGE 7]. Downscaling is also formalizable. If original pixel coordinates are \(x\) and the downscaling factor is \(s\), the motion estimation operates on \(q=x/s\). The source adopts \(s=4\) as the default because it gives a better trade-off than \(s=2\) or \(s=6\) [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. The breakdown regime is aliasing: if scene motion or texture detail is not preserved at the downscaled resolution, the estimated affine transform may be biased.

Runtime is reported as FPS. Formally,

\[
\mathrm{FPS}
=
\frac{N_{\text{frames}}}{\Delta t_{\text{wall}}},
\]

with SI unit \( \mathrm{s}^{-1} \), interpreted as frames per second. The source uses this unit in the ablation tables [PAGE 9]. The evaluation metrics are reported as scalar scores. The source states, “We report three standard MOT metrics: HOTA [49], IDF1 [50], and MOTA [51]” [PAGE 9]. The manuscript does not print the full metric equations; therefore any exact numerical comparison must rely on the benchmark implementations. In the present audit, HOTA, IDF1, and MOTA are treated as externally defined scalar functionals mapping tracker output to bounded performance scales, not as informal ratios invented here.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of McByte++ is best understood as a convergence of three historical streams: Kalman-filtered tracking-by-detection, mask-propagation video object segmentation, and appearance-based re-identification. The first stream begins with simple tracking-by-detection formulations and becomes formalized in ByteTrack and its variants. The source notes that ByteTrack and its extensions “demonstrate that carefully designed association strategies can achieve strong performance without end-to-end training,” while also observing that many such methods rely on tuning and primarily address short-term association [PAGE 2]. McByte++ inherits this lineage but tries to repair its long-term weakness. The predecessor McByte is described as “a training-free tracking-by-detection framework that integrates temporally propagated segmentation masks as an association cue” [PAGE 1]. McByte++ is then presented as “a substantial extension of McByte that advances training-free sports MOT toward long-term identity tracking, while simultaneously improving efficiency and runtime performance” [PAGE 2].

The second stream is mask propagation. The manuscript cites XMem and Cutie as temporally propagated mask models, noting that XMem introduced a memory-based framework for long-term mask propagation and Cutie improved object-background separation [PAGE 3]. The original McByte relied on a heavier mask propagation model, while McByte++ replaces it with EdgeTAM. The source states that EdgeTAM “significantly reduces computational overhead compared to earlier propagation approaches” [PAGE 5]. This genealogical move is important: the paper does not abandon masks, but demotes them from a dominant segmentation pipeline to a lightweight auxiliary cue. The historical trap here is the assumption that better segmentation automatically yields better tracking. The source contradicts that assumption by comparing with mask-heavy systems and arguing that “segmentation masks alone are insufficient for robust multi-object tracking in sports” [PAGE 13].

The third stream is re-identification. The manuscript situates itself against methods that use appearance embeddings at every association step. It says that in most prior approaches, “re-ID features are used at every association step to refine frame-level matching” [PAGE 3]. It then invokes prior analysis showing that re-ID is not universally beneficial: “re-ID often provides limited gains or even degrades performance when applied indiscriminately during short-term association” [PAGE 4]. This is the key genealogical rupture. McByte++ does not discard re-ID; it re-times it. The source says McByte++ “invokes re-identification only when new tracklets are initialized” [PAGE 2]. This is a conservative historical correction: appearance should not dominate every frame, but should be reserved for recovery after identity termination.

A multi-week research trap visible in the lineage is cue maximalism: adding every cue everywhere. The paper’s related-work section warns that heavy learned association models require large annotated data and are sensitive to domain shift [PAGE 1]. It also warns that offline graph methods can be computationally burdensome and post-processing methods add separate stages [PAGE 4]. McByte++ tries to avoid these traps by selective integration. Another trap is runtime blindness: a method may improve HOTA or IDF1 but become impractical for live analytics. The source explicitly frames computational complexity as a limitation for practical deployment [PAGE 1]. McByte++ answers this with lightweight mask propagation and optimized camera motion compensation, claiming that “Replacing heavy segmentation components and optimizing motion modeling yields up to an order-of-magnitude speed increase” [PAGE 1].

A further trap is threshold drift. The manuscript reports that similarity threshold 0.8 is selected after evaluating 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, and 0.9 [PAGE 8]. It then explains that lower thresholds merge visually similar players, while higher thresholds fail to reconnect players after appearance change [PAGE 10]. This is a multi-week trap because the correct threshold is not universal in appearance space; it depends on jersey similarity, camera resolution, pose variation, and occlusion severity. The paper’s answer is a single fixed threshold justified by cross-dataset stability. That is defensible as a training-free operating point, but it remains a heuristic compromise rather than a calibrated probability threshold. The historical lesson is that long-term identity preservation is not obtained by adding more appearance memory; it is obtained by delaying finalization, requiring mutual best match, and limiting re-ID to newly initialized tracklets [PAGE 7].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading of McByte++ treats the tracker as an agent that must infer latent player identities from noisy observations, while the players and camera behave as adversarial or semi-adversarial environmental processes. The tracker’s belief state is not merely a set of boxes; it includes predicted tracklets, propagated masks, terminated identity descriptors, and the most recent reliable camera-motion estimate. The source describes the short-term loop as tracklet boxes at frame \(t-1\) being propagated to frame \(t\) using a Kalman filter, then compensated by camera motion, then matched to detections [PAGE 3]. The long-term loop stores terminated identities and compares new tracklets against them using appearance similarity [PAGE 3]. The recursive problem is that the tracker must decide whether a newly appearing player is a new entity or a returning entity, while the appearance evidence may be degraded by occlusion, blur, or similar uniforms.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the system faces conditions that invalidate its usual cues. In sports, crises include substitutions, camera cuts, prolonged occlusions, rapid panning, motion blur, and crowded overlapping players. Under crisis, a naive tracker may regress to one of two habits. The first regression is motion-only habit: if appearance is unreliable, keep relying on Kalman prediction. The second is appearance-only habit: if geometry fails after a long gap, force a re-ID match. McByte++ tries to prevent both regressions by using gated cues. It says camera motion compensation is applied only when estimated global motion satisfies reliability criteria, preventing degenerate transformations [PAGE 7]. It says masks are integrated only under regulated conditions because propagated masks may be inaccurate due to drift, partial occlusions, or rapid motion [PAGE 5]. It says re-ID is reserved for reconnecting newly initialized tracklets with previously terminated identities, avoiding known failure modes under partial occlusion, motion blur, and crowded interactions [PAGE 6].

The counter-trap policy is therefore a conditional state machine. During short-term association, the tracker trusts motion, geometry, and mask cues only when local admissibility conditions hold. During long-term recovery, the tracker trusts appearance only when a new tracklet exists, when descriptors are aggregated, when cosine similarity exceeds 0.8, and when the match is mutually best [PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. This is a recursive safeguard against the tracker’s own overconfidence. If the system were to accept every high-similarity candidate, visually similar teammates would be merged. If it were to accept none, players re-entering after camera cuts would remain fragmented. The selected threshold is thus an equilibrium between false merge and false split. The source states that “A threshold of 0.8 consistently provides the best overall balance” [PAGE 10].

The clock is also part of the theory of mind. The datasets operate at 25 FPS [PAGE 8], so temporal reasoning is measured in frame indices and elapsed seconds. The source says that if no reconnection occurs within a short temporal window, the tracklet is confirmed as a new long-term identity, and that this delayed finalization avoids premature identity assignment [PAGE 7]. The exact window length is not specified, which means the clock is a hidden hyperparameter. In a recursive model, the tracker must balance two deadlines: wait too long, and a returning player is incorrectly instantiated as a new identity; finalize too early, and a temporary occlusion becomes a permanent identity split. The memory entry is removed after a valid match to prevent duplicate assignments [PAGE 7]. This is a conservative bookkeeping policy: one stored identity can be consumed by only one reconnection event, reducing cascading identity conflicts.

The gray-zone in this recursive frame is adversarial visual similarity. In team sports, players on the same team can share jersey color, shorts, socks, and body-scale distributions. The source admits that “very similar teammates may still occasionally remain challenging” [PAGE 7]. The tracker’s theory of mind must therefore model not only “where was this player?” but also “is this appearance evidence discriminative enough to override the prior that teammates look alike?” The paper’s answer is not a learned probabilistic identity model but a training-free policy: aggregate features, require mutual best match, and choose a threshold that empirically balances merge and split errors [PAGE 10]. This is robust in practice, but it remains sensitive to appearance distribution shift. If the re-ID backbone was not trained on the target sport’s visual domain, the cosine geometry may compress same-team players into nearby regions, increasing ambiguity. The system’s counter-trap is therefore not omniscience; it is conservative gating.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical and biomechanical veto proof for McByte++ must be constructed across four layers: detection, motion prediction, mask propagation, and appearance memory. Let \(v_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}\) denote visibility of player \(i\) at time \(t\). If \(v_{i,t}=0\), the player is outside the field of view, fully occluded, or not detected. In that regime, no detection box exists for data association, no mask can be reliably propagated to the player’s true location, and no fresh appearance crop can be extracted. The source acknowledges that sports players “regularly leave and re-enter the field of view due to camera framing, substitutions, or changes in play” [PAGE 1]. Therefore, any claim that short-term association alone can preserve identity across arbitrarily long absences is physically vetoed. Long-term recovery must rely on stored memory and reappearance evidence, not on continuous observation.

The motion-prediction layer has a biomechanical veto. ByteTrack-style Kalman prediction assumes that short-term motion is locally linear enough for state propagation. The source says tracklet states are propagated using a linear motion model implemented via a Kalman filter [PAGE 4]. In sports, abrupt accelerations, sudden stops, jumps, and collisions violate linear motion assumptions over longer horizons. If the true acceleration exceeds the filter’s process-noise tolerance, predicted boxes become biased. The camera-motion layer can mitigate global motion, but not non-linear player motion. The source states that camera motion compensation aligns track predictions with global frame-to-frame motion [PAGE 7]. A formal veto follows: if the residual motion after camera compensation exceeds the association gate, the IoU cost becomes too high, and short-term matching fails. This is exactly the “isolation” case described by the source: relevant IoU-based costs are too high to allow association despite potential identity correspondence [PAGE 5]. The mask cue may help, but only if the mask remains visible and overlaps the detection box according to the gating ratios.

The mask-propagation layer has a physical singularity. The coverage operator \(m^{c}_{ij}\) is undefined if the propagated mask has zero pixel cardinality, and the fill ratio \(m^{f}_{ij}\) is undefined if the detection box has zero area. More importantly, masks can fail under drift. The source says masks can be inaccurate due to “drift, partial occlusions, or rapid motion” [PAGE 5]. If a mask drifts onto another player, the gating conditions may still be numerically satisfied because the mask is visible and overlaps some box. The veto proof therefore requires an additional assumption: mask drift must be detected by the gating ratios or by the regulated usage policy. The paper’s defense is that mask guidance is applied only in ambiguity or isolation and only when all three conditions hold [PAGE 6]. This reduces but does not eliminate the failure mode. If two players overlap heavily and a mask drifts between them, the fill ratio may be high for the wrong detection, producing a false cost reduction.

The camera-motion layer has a weather and texture veto. Sports broadcast footage can include rain, glare, low light, motion blur, crowd occlusion, and homogeneous backgrounds such as sky, track, or court surface. The source explicitly states that unconditional camera compensation can be harmful when motion estimation is unreliable, “for instance in scenes with weak texture, heavy motion blur, or largely homogeneous backgrounds” [PAGE 7]. In those regimes, ORB feature correspondences may be sparse or wrong. The affine estimate may have near-zero determinant, excessive scale, or implausible translation. The conditional application policy vetoes compensation when reliability criteria fail. The source says this prevents “degenerate transformations” and avoids harmful updates [PAGE 7]. A formal veto condition is therefore:

\[
\text{Do not apply CMC if }
\mathcal{R}=0.
\]

The exact predicate \( \mathcal{R} \) is not fully specified, which is a reproduction risk. The downscaling factor adds another physical constraint. The source adopts \(ds=4\) because it balances accuracy and efficiency [PAGE 8]. But if \(ds\) is too large, small feature displacements are quantized too coarsely. The ablation shows that \(ds=6\) degrades identity metrics on both SoccerNet and SportsMOT relative to \(ds=4\) [PAGE 9]. This is an empirical veto against excessive spatial downscaling. The physical explanation is loss of effective angular resolution: camera pan or zoom may produce subpixel motion at the original resolution that becomes invisible or aliased after downscaling.

The appearance layer has a biomechanical and perceptual veto. Re-ID features are extracted from bounding-box crops. If the crop is too small, heavily occluded, or motion-blurred, the descriptor may not be identity-discriminative. The source warns that appearance embeddings from partially occluded, motion-blurred, or low-resolution subjects can be unreliable [PAGE 4]. If the feature norm is zero or the aggregated descriptor collapses, normalization is undefined. If same-team players occupy nearby regions in feature space, the mutual best-match criterion may fail because multiple candidates are nearly tied. The source’s own threshold study confirms this: threshold 0.6 causes incorrect long-term associations, while threshold 0.9 prevents correct reconnections [PAGE 10]. The cross-layer veto proof therefore concludes that long-term identity transfer is admissible only when: visibility returns, detection exists, mask gating is valid if used, camera motion is reliable if applied, feature norms are nonzero, similarity exceeds 0.8, and mutual best match is unique. If any of these conditions fail, the system should not assert identity continuity; it should either create a provisional identity or wait for additional evidence, consistent with the delayed finalization rule [PAGE 7].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three reinforcement-learning-style verification vetoes are constructed as counterfactual checks against claims that McByte++ uniformly benefits from added cues. Each veto is verifiable from the manuscript’s own ablations and stated design principles.

Scenario 1: unconditional low-threshold re-identification is vetoed. Counterfactual claim: if re-ID is helpful, then lowering the cosine similarity threshold should improve identity preservation by reconnecting more players. The source refutes this. With similarity threshold 0.6 on SoccerNet-tracking 2022, HOTA is 85.7 and IDF1 is 82.9; with threshold 0.8, HOTA rises to 87.5 and IDF1 to 84.5 [PAGE 9]. On SportsMOT, threshold 0.6 yields HOTA 78.9 and IDF1 83.2, while threshold 0.8 yields HOTA 79.9 and IDF1 83.6 [PAGE 9]. The manuscript explains that “overly low thresholds (e.g., 0.6) introduce incorrect long-term associations” by merging visually similar players [PAGE 10]. Prediction: any deployment that reduces the threshold below the selected 0.8 without recalibration should exhibit lower IDF1 and HOTA in team-sport footage with similar jerseys. This veto is verifiable because the threshold sweep is explicit and the direction of degradation is reported.

Scenario 2: aggressive spatial downscaling for camera motion estimation is vetoed. Counterfactual claim: increasing downscaling should always improve runtime without harming identity because computation is reduced. The source shows a non-monotonic trade-off. On SoccerNet-tracking 2022 with unconditional CMC, ds=4 yields HOTA 84.1 and IDF1 78.9, while ds=6 yields HOTA 83.4 and IDF1 78.0 [PAGE 9]. With conditional CMC, ds=4 yields HOTA 84.1 and IDF1 78.9, while ds=6 yields HOTA 83.1 and IDF1 77.7 [PAGE 9]. On SportsMOT, conditional ds=4 yields HOTA 75.8 and IDF1 76.0, while ds=6 yields HOTA 73.8 and IDF1 73.3 [PAGE 9]. The source states that “ds = 4 consistently provides the best trade-off between accuracy and efficiency” [PAGE 10]. Prediction: if a future configuration selects ds greater than 4 for the sake of runtime, HOTA and IDF1 should degrade unless the camera-motion estimation is independently improved. This veto is verifiable because the ablation table directly compares downscaling factors.

Scenario 3: offline global tracklet association is not a free substitute for online processing. Counterfactual claim: if offline post-processing improves identity metrics, it can replace online re-ID without considering runtime. The source shows that offline GTA-link improves identity metrics but reduces FPS. On SoccerNet-tracking 2022, online re-ID with threshold 0.8 achieves HOTA 87.5, IDF1 84.5, and 8.69 FPS, while GTA-link achieves HOTA 88.6, IDF1 87.2, and 7.46 FPS [PAGE 9]. On SportsMOT, online re-ID achieves HOTA 79.9, IDF1 83.6, and 14.57 FPS, while GTA-link achieves HOTA 81.5, IDF1 86.0, and 12.49 FPS [PAGE 9]. The source explicitly says, “Runtime increases (FPS decreases) compared to pure online processing due to the additional offline step” [PAGE 10]. Prediction: in low-latency live analytics, replacing online re-ID with offline GTA should be vetoed unless the latency budget can absorb the reduced FPS. In archival or tactical review, the veto is weaker because identity continuity may be prioritized over frame rate. This scenario is verifiable because both accuracy and runtime are reported for the same benchmark configurations.

A fourth meta-veto is also derivable: mask propagation should not be treated as a standalone tracking solution. The source compares McByte++ with mask-based systems and reports that McByte++ outperforms them on HOTA and IDF1 while using masks as auxiliary cues [PAGE 13]. It concludes that “segmentation masks alone are insufficient for robust multi-object tracking in sports” [PAGE 13]. The formal reason is that masks do not provide global tracklet management, especially under missed detections, long occlusions, and frequent identity interruptions [PAGE 3]. Therefore, any counterfactual policy that removes Hungarian tracklet management and relies only on mask propagation should be vetoed. The verifiable prediction is that pure mask-based systems will show lower identity consistency than a structured tracking-by-detection pipeline with regulated mask guidance, camera-motion compensation, and selective re-ID, especially when detections are noisy or identities disappear for extended periods.
