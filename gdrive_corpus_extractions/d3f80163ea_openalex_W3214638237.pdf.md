The study recruited 65 boys aged 12–13 who had played football at least three times weekly, divided them into an injured group (n=25) and a non-injured group (n=40), then administered the seven-movement FMS. The design therefore compares people who already have an injury history with people who do not. It does not observe FMS before injury, and it does not establish that a low FMS caused the injury. The title’s phrase “injury risk occurrence” and the conclusion’s “effective diagnostic tool to identify previous injuries” [PAGE 5 of 6] must be separated: the latter is a retrospective classification claim; the former is a prospective prediction claim.

The FMS operator is an ordinal sum. Each of seven tests is scored 0–3, with 0 assigned for pain, and the maximum is 21: “Each of the study participants performed these 7 exercises, assessed on a scale of 0 to 3 points, where 0 was given in the event of pain. The maximum number of points that could be obtained by the participant was 21” [PAGE 2 of 6]. The injured group had a lower composite score: control M=16.58, SD=2.04; injured M=14.20, SD=1.96; p<0.001, r=0.54 [PAGE 3 of 6]. The largest single-test differences were in the deep squat (M=2.45 vs 1.72; p<0.001, r=0.50) and the in-line lunge (M=2.55 vs 2.04; p=0.001, r=0.41) [PAGE 2–3 of 6]. The asymmetry count also differed: 0.95 vs 1.64; p=0.007, r=0.34 [PAGE 3 of 6].

The threshold analysis is where the gray zone becomes visible. The authors report that “the most significant correlation could be observed at the threshold of 14 points (χ2(1) = 18.66, p < 0.001, φ = 0.54) and 15 points (χ2(1) = 17.96, p < 0.001, φ = 0.53)” [PAGE 4 of 6]. They then choose 15 on sensitivity/specificity grounds: “The best results were obtained for the threshold of 15 points. Players who scored higher than 15 points were classified as not injured with the correctness of 78% and players who scored 15 or below were classified as injured with the correctness of 76%” [PAGE 4 of 6]. The conclusion states: “The threshold in the FMS test below which the risk of injury occurrence increases is 15” [PAGE 5 of 6]. That is an overreach for a retrospective design. The data support, at most, an association between prior injury and a lower FMS score at a particular threshold in this sample.

A useful audit is to reconstruct the implied 2×2 table at threshold 15 from the reported rounded sensitivity and specificity. With 25 injured and 40 non-injured, sensitivity 0.76 implies about 19 injured players at or below 15; specificity 0.78 implies about 31 non-injured players above 15. That gives TP≈19, FN≈6, TN≈31, FP≈9. The phi coefficient is then approximately 0.526, consistent with the reported 0.53. This reconstruction shows internal numerical coherence, but it also shows how small the cells are. Small cell counts make threshold selection unstable and make confidence intervals wide. The same issue appears at threshold 14: sensitivity 0.64 and specificity 0.88 imply about 16 injured at or below 14 and about 35 non-injured above 14, with phi again near 0.53–0.54. The choice between 14 and 15 is therefore not a clean discovery of a single natural cutoff; it is a compromise between maximizing phi, maximizing sensitivity, and maximizing specificity.

There are additional gray-zone inconsistencies. The source’s effect-size scale says “r < 0.5 being a medium relationship, and r < 0.5 being a strong relationship” [PAGE 2 of 6], which is almost certainly a typographical error for one branch being r > 0.5. The height effect size is given as r=0.015 in the text but r=0.15 in Table 1 [PAGE 2 of 6]. Test 5 is reported as p=0.036 with r=0.26, yet the authors say “based on effect size (r = 0.26), it can be assessed that the difference was insignificant” [PAGE 3 of 6]. Test 6 is called “borderline significant” at p=0.055, r=0.24 [PAGE 3 of 6]. These are not fatal to the central association, but they show that the paper uses “significant” in both statistical and effect-size senses, sometimes interchangeably. A careful reading must not convert “statistically associated in a small retrospective sample” into “clinically predictive.”

The strongest defensible reading is therefore layered. At the descriptive layer, prior injury is associated with lower FMS composite scores, especially deep squat and in-line lunge, and with more asymmetries. At the diagnostic layer, threshold 15 has the best balance of sensitivity and specificity in this sample. At the causal-prediction layer, the design does not support a claim that FMS identifies future injury risk. The source itself acknowledges the retrospective branch: “The second type of correlation ‘FMS test score – injury’ is retrospective. This approach is a result of the assumption that past injuries are the best indication of risk of another injury occurrence” [PAGE 5 of 6]. That is exactly the reverse-causal trap: a low FMS may be a consequence of prior injury, deconditioning, fear of reinjury, or altered movement, rather than a cause of the next injury.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s main measured operator is the FMS composite score. For athlete \(i\) and movement test \(j\in\{1,\ldots,7\}\), let \(X_{ij}\in\{0,1,2,3\}\) be the ordinal score, with 0 assigned for pain. Then
\[
S_i=\sum_{j=1}^{7}X_{ij},\qquad S_i\in\{0,1,\ldots,21\}\ \text{points}.
\]
The unit is ordinal FMS points, not an SI unit. The source states that the maximum is 21 and that 0 is given for pain [PAGE 2 of 6]. The asymmetry operator is a count, not a continuous physical quantity:
\[
A_i=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,|X_{ij,L}-X_{ij,R}|>0\,\right],
\]
where \(\mathcal{B}\) is the set of bilateral movement patterns and \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is the indicator function. The unit is number of asymmetries. The source reports group means of 0.95 vs 1.64 asymmetries [PAGE 3 of 6], but it does not fully specify the bilateral scoring rule in the excerpt.

For a threshold \(\tau\), the source’s classification rule is effectively
\[
\hat I_i(\tau)=\mathbf{1}[S_i\le \tau],
\]
where \(\hat I_i=1\) means “classified as injured.” The diagnostic operators are
\[
\mathrm{Sens}(\tau)=P(\hat I=1\mid I=1)=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},
\qquad
\mathrm{Spec}(\tau)=P(\hat I=0\mid I=0)=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},
\]
with \(I=1\) denoting prior injury and \(I=0\) denoting no prior injury. Both are dimensionless probabilities in \([0,1]\). The positive and negative predictive values, not reported by the source but formally relevant, are
\[
\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{NPV}=\frac{TN}{TN+FN},
\]
also dimensionless. The source reports sensitivity and specificity at thresholds 12–17 [PAGE 4 of 6]; at threshold 15 it reports sensitivity 0.76 and specificity 0.78. Under the rounding implied by 25 injured and 40 non-injured, the approximate table is \(TP\approx19\), \(FN\approx6\), \(TN\approx31\), \(FP\approx9\), giving \(\mathrm{Sens}\approx0.76\), \(\mathrm{Spec}\approx0.775\), and \(\mathrm{PPV}\approx19/28\approx0.679\), \(\mathrm{NPV}\approx31/37\approx0.838\). These are derived values, not source-reported values.

The chi-square and phi statistics are dimensionless. For a \(2\times2\) table with total \(N=65\),
\[
\chi^2=\sum_{\text{cells}}\frac{(O_k-E_k)^2}{E_k},
\qquad
\phi=\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N}},
\]
with \(df=(2-1)(2-1)=1\). The source reports \(\chi^2(1)=18.66\), \(p<0.001\), \(\phi=0.54\) at threshold 14, and \(\chi^2(1)=17.96\), \(p<0.001\), \(\phi=0.53\) at threshold 15 [PAGE 4 of 6]. For the Mann–Whitney comparisons, the reported \(r\) is an effect-size transform of the rank test; it is dimensionless and should not be interpreted as a Pearson correlation of interval data. The source reports \(r=0.54\) for total FMS, \(r=0.50\) for deep squat, \(r=0.41\) for in-line lunge, and \(r=0.34\) for asymmetry count [PAGE 3 of 6].

The threshold-selection operator is not a single equation in the paper, but it can be written as a two-objective problem:
\[
\tau^\star_{\phi}=\arg\max_{\tau\in\{12,\ldots,17\}}\phi(\tau),
\qquad
\tau^\star_{\mathrm{ROC}}=\arg\max_{\tau\in\{12,\ldots,17\}}\bigl[\mathrm{Sens}(\tau)+\mathrm{Spec}(\tau)\bigr].
\]
The source’s own numbers place \(\tau^\star_{\phi}=14\) by a narrow margin, while the best sensitivity-specificity balance is at \(\tau=15\). This is a formal source of ambiguity: the conclusion selects 15, but the strongest phi is at 14.

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are critical. Sensitivity is undefined when \(TP+FN=0\) (no injured cases); specificity is undefined when \(TN+FP=0\) (no non-injured cases); PPV is undefined when \(TP+FP=0\); NPV is undefined when \(TN+FN=0\). Phi is undefined if any marginal total is zero. The chi-square approximation breaks down when expected cell counts are small; the source does not report expected counts, and with \(N=65\) and 25 injured, some thresholds have sparse cells. The threshold search over 12–17 also creates a selection/overfitting regime: choosing the best threshold on the same sample that supplies the sensitivity and specificity inflates apparent diagnostic performance. The retrospective design creates a temporal breakdown regime: \(S_i\) is measured after \(I_i\), so \(\mathrm{Sens}\) and \(\mathrm{Spec}\) estimate classification of prior injury, not prospective prediction of future injury. Finally, the FMS is ordinal, but the paper reports means and standard deviations; that treats ordinal points as if they were interval measurements, which is a measurement-model assumption not justified by the source. The paper does state that testing occurred in a closed room “in order to exclude the influence of external factors” [PAGE 2 of 6], which controls immediate environment but not injury exposure, maturation, or recall bias.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The FMS lineage runs from Cook, Burton, and Hoogenboom’s pre-participation screening papers and the 2010 FMS text, cited by the source as references 4–6 [PAGE 6 of 6]. The threshold lineage is dominated by Kiesel et al., whom the source describes as the first researchers to present a “FMS test score – injury” correlation: “The first researchers who presented a study on the ‘FMS test score – injury’ correlation were Kiesel et al., who in the span of 4.5 months tested 46 American football players [14]” [PAGE 5 of 6]. That study produced the familiar 14-point reference, with the source noting Kiesel’s specificity 0.91 and sensitivity 0.54 [PAGE 5 of 6]. The present paper inherits that threshold genealogy but studies 12–13-year-old football players rather than adult American football players. The authors acknowledge the transfer problem: the variation “resulted from the differences between the control and the study groups, which differ not only in age (adults – children) but also in the type of sport (American football – football). This makes finding a common denominator for meaningful and objective comparisons extremely difficult” [PAGE 5 of 6].

The source also inherits the prospective-versus-retrospective split from the FMS literature. It cites McCunn et al. as finding sixteen prospective articles, eight with a significant “FMS score – injury” correlation and eight without [PAGE 4 of 6]. That genealogy matters because the present study is retrospective. Its “retrocausal” structure runs backward from injury status to FMS score. The paper’s own rationale is that “past injuries are the best indication of risk of another injury occurrence” [PAGE 5 of 6], which is a legitimate clinical heuristic but not a causal identification strategy. A prior injury can lower FMS through pain, guarding, lost training time, altered motor coordination, or fear-avoidance. The FMS score can therefore be a scar of injury rather than a predictor of injury.

The multi-week traps are built into the design. First, injury is defined by a four-week absence threshold: “An injury was defined in this case as an incident during a game or a training session which simultaneously meets 3 conditions: (1) medical assistance was needed, (2) which rendered the player unable to participate in the rest of the activity and (3) he was absent from any subsequent physical activity for at least 4 weeks” [PAGE 2 of 6]. That selects more severe injuries and excludes transient episodes. Second, the reliability window for repeated FMS assessment is 48–72 hours [PAGE 1 of 6], so the score is assumed stable across days but the study does not report the interval between the prior injury and the FMS test. Third, Kiesel’s prospective window was 4.5 months [PAGE 5 of 6], while this study is cross-sectional. Fourth, the broader literature cited includes an 8-season prospective study of young elite female soccer players and a 5-year longitudinal police study [PAGE 6 of 6], both of which highlight how injury risk accumulates over long exposure windows. The present study’s single-time-point retrospective window cannot resolve those multi-week and multi-season dynamics.

The threshold genealogy also has a trap: the 14-point threshold is repeatedly quoted as a reference, and the present study tests thresholds 12–17, finds 14 strongest by phi, then concludes 15 because of sensitivity/specificity. That is a classic threshold migration: a new sample produces a new “best” cut point, which then becomes a new habit. The source itself notes that three of the null prospective studies were “underpowered due to the low number of participants and scheme of collecting data of injury” [PAGE 4 of 6]. The same caution applies here: with 65 boys and 25 injured, the 15-point threshold is a sample-specific compromise, not a universal law.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive measurement problem is that injury status is not a single observer’s fact. The athlete, parent, clinician, and researcher each hold a different model of what happened. The source reports that “because of the young age of tested players, the questionnaires concerning past injuries and general health condition were filled in based on the medical documentation and consulted with parents” [PAGE 5 of 6]. That is a nested belief chain: the athlete may underreport because of fear of losing selection; the parent may reconstruct from memory; the medical record may be incomplete; the researcher classifies the event using the three-part injury definition. The FMS assessor’s knowledge of injury history is not reported as blinded. If the assessor knows a boy is in the injured group, expectancy and scoring drift become plausible, especially on ordinal 0–3 judgments. The source does report moderate-to-good inter-rater and intra-rater reliability among novice raters and a 48–72-hour repeat window [PAGE 1 of 6], but reliability does not eliminate unblinded expectancy bias.

Crisis habit regression appears in how the FMS is used after the fact. When a young player is injured, the clinical habit is to reach for a composite score and a threshold—historically 14, now 15 in this paper—and then prescribe corrective exercise. The source says corrective programmes “allow for improvement in a specific basic movement pattern” and that improved function “reduces the risk of injury during sport activity” [PAGE 1 of 6]. That is a plausible clinical habit, but the present data do not test whether the corrective programme prevents injury. The habit regresses to the composite score because it is simple and familiar, even though the deep squat and in-line lunge carry most of the between-group signal.

The counter-trap is overcorrecting against the 14-point tradition by selecting 15 on sensitivity/specificity. The source’s own results show why this is a trap: 14 has the highest phi, 15 has the best sensitivity-specificity balance, and 16 gives sensitivity 0.84 but specificity only 0.50 [PAGE 4 of 6]. Moving the threshold to 15 is not a neutral correction; it changes who is labelled at risk and it is optimized on the same 65 boys. A recursive theory-of-mind reading asks what the next clinician will believe: if the paper says “the threshold … is 15” [PAGE 5 of 6], the next practitioner may treat 15 as a validated universal cut point, even though the paper itself notes age and sport differences make comparisons difficult [PAGE 5 of 6]. That is how a sample-specific counter-trap becomes a new habit.

The clock is the decisive recursive check. The retrospective clock runs from injury history to FMS score; the prospective clock must run from baseline FMS to future injury. The source’s conclusion that FMS “is an effective diagnostic tool to identify previous injuries” [PAGE 5 of 6] is a backward-clock claim. The title’s “injury risk occurrence” is a forward-clock claim. The recursive error is to let the backward-clock evidence satisfy the forward-clock question. A blinded, pre-registered, prospective design would break the recursion: assessor does not know injury history, threshold is fixed before data collection, and injury surveillance follows the FMS. Without that, the clinician’s belief about the athlete, the athlete’s belief about selection, and the researcher’s threshold choice all remain entangled.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer is partially controlled but not fully adjudicated. The source reports no significant anthropometric differences between groups: age p=0.602, r=0.06; height p=0.224, r=0.015 in the text (r=0.15 in Table 1); weight p=0.388, r=0.11; BMI p=0.845, r=0.02 [PAGE 2 of 6]. This weakens a simple “bigger boys got injured” explanation, but it does not address biological maturation, pubertal stage, training load, playing position, or previous exposure. Height is in cm, weight in kg, BMI in kg/m², and age in years; those units are physical, but the study’s inference is not. A physical-layer veto would trigger if the groups differed materially in maturation or exposure; the reported anthropometrics do not show that, but the study did not measure those deeper physical variables.

The biomechanical layer is the FMS itself. The deep squat and in-line lunge differences are the strongest single-test signals: deep squat M=2.45 vs 1.72, r=0.50; in-line lunge M=2.55 vs 2.04, r=0.41 [PAGE 2–3 of 6]. These are ordinal movement-quality scores, not joint angles, ground reaction forces, moments, or muscle forces. The source provides no kinematic or kinetic units. It cannot say whether the difference is hip mobility, ankle dorsiflexion, trunk control, pain inhibition, or rater expectation. The “closed room” statement [PAGE 2 of 6] controls the testing environment, not the biomechanical mechanism. A biomechanical veto therefore blocks any claim that FMS measures a specific physical deficit causing injury; it does not block the weaker claim that prior injury is associated with lower movement quality.

The weather layer is absent. The FMS was performed indoors “in order to exclude the influence of external factors” [PAGE 2 of 6]. That is good internal standardization, but it means the study contains no temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, surface, or playing-condition variables. Football injury exposure occurs outdoors, often on natural or artificial grass, and weather can affect surface hardness, boot–surface traction, fatigue, and playing style. Because those variables are not measured, the study cannot adjudicate a weather-specific risk claim. The weather veto is not that weather is irrelevant; it is that this dataset cannot support any weather-generalized inference.

Adjudication: the physical, biomechanical, and weather layers do not veto the descriptive association between prior injury and lower FMS. They do veto the strong causal-prediction interpretation. To claim “injury risk occurrence,” the study would need temporal precedence, blinded assessment, prospective injury surveillance, adjustment for maturation and exposure, and external validation of the threshold. None of those are present. The correct cross-layer verdict is therefore: admissible as retrospective diagnostic association; inadmissible as a standalone prospective injury-risk screen.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means a verifier-grounded counterfactual check: a pre-registered rule that vetoes the paper’s strong claim if a specified counterfactual test fails. Three verifiable veto scenarios follow.

1. Temporal-order counterfactual. Re-run the study prospectively: baseline FMS before the season, blinded assessors, pre-registered threshold 15, and independent injury surveillance over a defined follow-up. Verifier: exact 2×2 counts at threshold 15 and a pre-registered effect-size criterion. Prediction: if the retrospective association is causal, prospective sensitivity should remain at least 0.70, specificity at least 0.70, and phi at least 0.40 with a 95% confidence interval excluding 0. If the association is mostly a scar of prior injury, the prospective effect should attenuate below phi=0.30 or the confidence interval should include 0. Veto rule: if the prospective effect is below the pre-registered threshold, the claim “FMS identifies injury risk occurrence” is vetoed; the retrospective claim “FMS identifies previous injuries” may survive.

2. Blinding and rater counterfactual. Repeat the FMS scoring with assessors blinded to injury history and with independent video review or two raters per athlete. Verifier: inter-rater agreement, plus blinded sensitivity/specificity at threshold 15. Prediction: the total FMS effect should shrink relative to the unblinded retrospective estimate; the deep squat and in-line lunge may retain a smaller association, while threshold 15 sensitivity/specificity should drop below the reported 0.76/0.78. If the blinded effect remains essentially unchanged, the association is more robust than the design suggests. Veto rule: if blinding materially changes the threshold classification, the paper’s diagnostic accuracy estimate is vetoed as expectancy-contaminated.

3. Exposure and maturation counterfactual. Match injured and non-injured boys on age, pubertal maturity, training hours per week, playing position, time since injury, and prior exposure, then re-estimate the FMS difference. Verifier: pre-specified adjusted model or matched analysis with exact covariate balance. Prediction: the reported total-FMS \(r=0.54\) and asymmetry \(r=0.34\) should attenuate, because injured boys may have had different exposure and recovery histories. If the adjusted effect remains above \(r=0.40\) for total FMS and above \(r=0.25\) for asymmetry, the association is not explained by those covariates. Veto rule: if adjustment removes the effect, the causal-risk interpretation is vetoed; if it persists, the association survives a stronger counterfactual test. In all three scenarios, the verifiable prediction is not a betting line or a clinical guarantee; it is a falsifiable effect-size and classification criterion that can confirm or veto the source’s strong conclusion.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
