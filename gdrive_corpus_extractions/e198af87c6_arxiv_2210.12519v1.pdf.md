The source is not a physical or biomechanical model of football; it is a regularized linear model with spline bases for complementary-unit statistics, plus unpenalized strength-of-schedule and homefield terms. The first multi-hop step is data construction. The paper converts game-by-game team data into two observations per game, “one representing plays produced when first team’s offensive side (and second team’s defensive side) was on the field” [PAGE 3]. Thus the response \(y_{ijl}\) is team \(i\)’s offensive performance against team \(j\)’s defense, while the complementary covariate \(x_{c,jil}\) is recorded when team \(i\)’s defense and team \(j\)’s offense are on the field. This indexing matters: a selected “defensive” feature is not a property of the same unit that produced the response; it is a property of the alternating unit. The paper’s motivating example is exactly this: “turnovers forced by your defense could lead to easier scoring chances for your offense, while your offense’s ability to control the clock may help your defense” [PAGE 2]. That is a plausible causal path, but it is not the only path. If your offense scores, the opponent receives the ball, giving your defense another opportunity to force a turnover. Therefore the observed association can run from response to covariate through possession alternation. The paper names the problem directly: “Reverse causality is the most likely answer” [PAGE 7]. The special-teams audit is especially clear: “68% of attempted returns followed after a kickoff,” and a kickoff is most likely to follow a complementary score, with “60% chance it was a 7-point touchdown, 18% chance - a 3-point field goal” [PAGE 8]. The return attempt is therefore downstream of scoring, not necessarily upstream.

The second multi-hop step is the objective. The model is additive in team offensive margin \(\alpha_i\), opponent defensive margin \(\beta_j\), homefield indicator \(\delta h_{ijl}\), and spline functions of complementary statistics. The penalty is applied only to the complementary spline coefficient groups; the homefield and schedule terms are unpenalized. The paper calls this “partially constrained group penalization” [PAGE 5]. The practical consequence is that variable selection can shrink a complementary feature entirely to zero while still forcing full adjustment for schedule and homefield. This is a ranking-adjustment operator, not an identification strategy for causal effects. A selected feature means “this group survived BIC or sparse cross-validation under a group penalty,” not “this feature causally changes the response.” The paper’s own predictive validation is sober: “SOS+HF and our method consistently show better average performances (higher AUROC, lower MAE values)” [PAGE 13], but the text also says all average metrics are within less than two standard deviations of one another. The strongest defensible claim is therefore modest: efficiency-based complementary adjustment is a reasonable descriptive adjustment and sometimes a small predictive improvement, not a proven causal mechanism.

The third step is the gray-zone audit. The data are game-level, not play-level; the paper lacks play sequencing, weather, biomechanical load, travel, injury status, and within-game fatigue. The appendix feature lists contain football box-score and efficiency variables, not physical or meteorological covariates [PAGE 16] [PAGE 17]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The main response model is an additive team-margin model with natural cubic spline bases for complementary statistics. With \(i,j=1,\ldots,n\), \(l=1,\ldots,L_{ij}\), response \(y_{ijl}\) in response units such as points per game, touchdowns per game, or yards per game, homefield indicator \(h_{ijl}\in\{-1,0,1\}\), team offensive margin \(\alpha_i\), opponent defensive margin \(\beta_j\), and spline basis functions \(N_k(\cdot)\), the structural mean is
\[
\mathbb{E}[y_{ijl}\mid h_{ijl},x_{1,jil},\ldots,x_{C,jil}]
=
\mu+\delta h_{ijl}+\alpha_i+\beta_j
+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\gamma_{c,k}N_k(x_{c,jil}).
\]
This follows the paper’s notation: \(y_{ijl}\) is team \(i\)’s performance against team \(j\), and \(x_{c,jil}\) is “the value of cth statistic that’s complementary to the \(y_{ijl}\),” obtained when the complementary units are on the field [PAGE 4]. The paper uses “natural cubic splines” with \(K=5\) knots at the 0.00-, 0.25-, 0.50-, 0.75-, and 1.00-quantiles, giving \(K-1=4\) basis functions per complementary statistic [PAGE 4]. The intercept basis is omitted because it is folded into \(\mu\). Units: \(y_{ijl}\), \(\mu\), \(\alpha_i\), \(\beta_j\), and \(\delta h_{ijl}\) are in response units; \(h_{ijl}\) is dimensionless; \(N_k(x_c)\) is dimensionless; \(\gamma_{c,k}\) is in response units per dimensionless basis function. For percentages such as possession percentage or completion percentage, \(x_c\) is dimensionless on \([0,1]\); for yards per play, \(x_c\) has units yards per play.

The partially constrained group penalization operator is
\[
\min_{\mu,\delta,\{\alpha_i\},\{\beta_j\},\{\gamma_c\}}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{L_{ij}}
\left(
y_{ijl}
-\left(
\mu+\delta h_{ijl}+\alpha_i+\beta_j
+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\gamma_{c,k}N_k(x_{c,jil})
\right)
\right)^2
+
\lambda\sum_{c=1}^{C}P_c(\|\gamma_c\|_2),
\]
where \(\gamma_c=(\gamma_{c,1},\ldots,\gamma_{c,K-1})\), \(\|\gamma_c\|_2=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\gamma_{c,k}^2\right)^{1/2}\), and \(P_c(\cdot)\) is the group penalty [PAGE 5]. The paper states that this “guarantees inclusion of homefield effect” and “strength of schedule adjustment effects” [PAGE 5]. The unpenalized terms are \(\delta\), \(\alpha_i\), and \(\beta_j\); the penalized terms are the complementary spline groups. For classic group LASSO, \(P_c(\|\gamma_c\|_2)=\|\gamma_c\|_2\). For adaptive group LASSO, the paper uses \(P_c(\|\gamma_c\|_2)=w_c\|\gamma_c\|_2\) with weights tied inversely to the least-squares group norm; the exact printed formula is garbled, so the defensible formal statement is \(w_c=1/\|\hat\gamma_c^{LS}\|_2^2\) with the breakdown caveat below. SCAD and MCP are non-convex group penalties with an additional tuning parameter selected by BIC [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6]. For unweighted group LASSO, \(\lambda\) has response units because the residual sum is in response-squared units and \(\|\gamma_c\|_2\) is in response units; for weighted penalties the numerical scale is penalty-dependent.

Tuning and selection are operators in their own right. The paper uses “20-fold cross-validation (CV)” and BIC to select candidate \(\lambda\) values [PAGE 6]. It selects the CV \(\lambda\) whose test error is within one standard deviation of the minimum while giving a sparser model, and it repeats CV “five times for each season, only counting features that got selected at least four out of five times” [PAGE 6]. The consistency metric weights the four main methods equally and, within each method, weights BIC and sparse CV equally. Formally, for statistic \(c\),
\[
s_c=\frac14\sum_{m=1}^{4}\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{2}
\mathbf{1}\{\text{statistic }c\text{ selected by method }m\text{ and tuning rule }t\}.
\]
The final adjustment fits natural cubic splines using only features consistently selected across methods, with complementary statistics centered at their means [PAGE 6]. Prediction evaluation uses logistic regression for binary game outcome and linear regression for score differential; predictors are offensive and defensive points-per-game margins plus the homefield indicator; metrics are AUROC, a dimensionless probability on \([0,1]\), and MAE in points [PAGE 13]. Training uses the full season, while testing trains on the first 9 weeks and predicts the remainder [PAGE 13]. BIC is an information criterion in natural-log units, conventionally nats.

Key assumptions: additive separability of team offensive and defensive margins; linearity in parameters after spline expansion; independent errors with finite variance; exogeneity \(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{ijl}\mid h_{ijl},x_{c,jil}]=0\); low within-game dependence, supported by the reported ICC magnitudes [PAGE 3]; sufficient schedule connectivity so that \(\alpha_i+\beta_j\) is identified; and no perfect collinearity after the paper’s filtering [PAGE 7]. The imputation of missing defensive statistics assumes a linear regression relationship between missing and observed categories [PAGE 3]. The efficiency-only strategy assumes that per-play and per-possession rates are less game-script-dependent than cumulative counts [PAGE 8].

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. If \(\lambda=0\), the penalty vanishes and the objective reduces to ordinary least squares; if the design is rank deficient because of duplicate quantile knots, perfect collinearity, or disconnected schedule components, the solution is non-unique. If \(\lambda\to\infty\), all \(\gamma_c\) are driven to zero and the model collapses to SOS+HF only. At \(\|\gamma_c\|_2=0\), the group penalty is non-differentiable at the origin, so subgradient or proximal methods are required. In adaptive group LASSO, if a least-squares group norm is zero or near zero, the weight \(w_c\) becomes infinite or numerically unstable. If many observations take the same value, quantile knots can coincide and the natural-spline basis loses rank. Low-denominator efficiency statistics have undefined or zero values and are therefore excluded [PAGE 9]. If \(x_c\) is endogenous, \(\mathbb{E}[x_c\epsilon]\neq0\), the causal interpretation breaks even if prediction remains usable; the paper calls reverse causality “a special case of endogeneity” [PAGE 8]. If ICC is materially above 0.10, the independence assumption and standard errors break. If missingness is not random, the 7% imputation can bias selection [PAGE 3].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from classical offense-defense rating models to modern regularized variable selection. The paper traces strength-of-schedule adjustment to Harville’s linear-model rating approach [PAGE 2], with the full reference to Harville’s 1977 work in the bibliography [PAGE 18]. It also cites Albert, Bennett, and Cochran’s sports-statistics anthology and related soccer goal-dependence models [PAGE 2] [PAGE 18]. The regularization lineage is standard: LASSO from Tibshirani, group LASSO from Yuan and Lin, adaptive group LASSO from Wang and Leng, SCAD from Fan and Li, MCP from Zhang, natural cubic splines from Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman, BIC, and cross-validation [PAGE 18]. The endogeneity lineage is econometric: instrumental variables, weak instruments, and reverse causality as a special case of endogeneity [PAGE 8] [PAGE 18]. The paper’s novelty is not a new causal identification theorem; it is the combination of natural splines, group penalties, partially constrained optimization, and sports-domain feature construction for complementary football.

The retrocausal genealogy is the important part. In this model, game-level cumulative statistics are not primitive causes; they are residues of prior game states. Pass incompletions can facilitate complementary scoring by stopping the clock, but they are also “a partial function of play selection by the team that’s behind in the score” [PAGE 7]. Rushing attempts reverse the logic: the leading team runs to keep the clock moving, while the trailing team does not [PAGE 8]. Special-teams returns are even more clearly downstream: “68% of attempted returns followed after a kickoff,” and kickoffs usually follow a score [PAGE 8]. The paper’s efficiency-only turn is therefore a partial remedy, not a cure. It replaces cumulative counts with per-play and per-possession rates, but those rates still depend on down, distance, score margin, and time remaining.

Multi-week traps appear in three places. First, season-long cumulative features accumulate game script across weeks; a feature selected on full-season data can encode late-game or blowout dynamics. Second, the predictive audit trains on the first 9 weeks and predicts the rest of the season [PAGE 13], so early-season schedules and early-season play-calling can leak into later predictions. Third, selection stability is measured across 2009-2019 with five CV repeats and a four-out-of-five threshold [PAGE 6]; that reduces randomness but does not remove structural reverse causality. A feature can be stable precisely because the same game-script mechanism recurs every season. The historical lesson is that offense-defense ratings solved schedule adjustment, regularized selection solved high-dimensional feature choice, and endogeneity remains the unresolved layer beneath both.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive strategic layer is the coach’s model of the opponent’s model of the game state. Let \(S_t=(\Delta_t,\tau_t)\) be score margin and time remaining at play \(t\). A play call is not exogenous: it is a policy \(a_t=\pi_i(S_t,\hat\pi_j(S_t))\), where \(\hat\pi_j\) is team \(i\)’s belief about team \(j\)’s response. The observed complementary statistic is then \(x_c=g(a_t,S_t)\), while the response \(y\) also depends on \(S_t\). This creates a feedback loop: \(x_c\) is a function of the same score and clock that help determine \(y\). The paper’s examples fit this exactly. A trailing team is “forced to throw the ball more often in order to gain yards quicker and catch up,” and an incompletion stops the clock [PAGE 7]. A leading team rushes to keep the clock running [PAGE 8]. The observed incompletion or rushing count is therefore a behavioral response to the crisis state, not an exogenous treatment.

“Crisis habit regression” is the collapse of play-calling to habitual responses under pressure: when the margin is large or the clock is short, teams regress to stereotyped policies—trailing teams pass, leading teams run. That habit regression strengthens the reverse-causal path because the covariate becomes a near-deterministic function of the response state. “Counter-trap” is the opponent’s recursive adjustment: if the defense expects the habitual pass, it changes coverage or pressure, which changes completion probability, sack probability, and clock stoppage. The observed \(x_c\) is then an equilibrium outcome of both teams’ beliefs, not a one-way cause. “Clock” is the shared state variable that makes the recursion dynamic: an incompletion stops the clock, a rush keeps it moving, and either changes the number of future possessions available to the complementary unit. The paper’s non-scoring turnover and yards-per-play findings are therefore best read as equilibrium signatures. The safe counter-trap is to condition on score margin, time remaining, possession number, and play sequence; the paper cannot do that with game-level data and therefore falls back to efficiency rates as a partial remedy [PAGE 8].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is straightforward: a statistical association in this paper cannot prove a physical, biomechanical, or meteorological mechanism because the relevant layers are absent from the feature set. The appendix lists football box-score and efficiency variables, not weather, travel, rest, injury, player load, acceleration, or force-plate data [PAGE 16] [PAGE 17]. Homefield is encoded only as an indicator \(h_{ijl}\) [PAGE 4]. If a true model contains a weather or biomechanical variable \(W\) with effect \(\theta\) on the response and correlation with the selected complementary covariate \(x_c\), then the omitted-variable estimate obeys
\[
\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{omit}}
=
\gamma
+
\theta\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x_c,W)}{\operatorname{Var}(x_c)}.
\]
Here \(\gamma\) is the response-unit effect of \(x_c\), \(\theta\) is in response units per \(W\)-unit, and the covariance ratio restores response units per \(x_c\)-unit. If \(\theta\neq0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_c,W)\neq0\), the estimated complementary effect is biased regardless of how stable the group penalty is. Weather examples with physical units include wind speed in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), precipitation in \(\mathrm{mm\,h^{-1}}\), and temperature in \(^\circ\mathrm{C}\); each can affect passing, ball security, and kicking. Biomechanical examples include cumulative player load, acceleration counts, and injury status; these can affect tackling, turnovers, and yards per play. Physical examples include rest days and travel distance; these can affect fatigue and execution. Because none are in the model, the cross-layer veto blocks a strong causal reading. The paper’s own caution supports this: the efficiency-only approach “could still suffer from endogeneity” [PAGE 14], and it shows “lack of improvement over pure strength-of-schedule and homefield factor (SOS+HF) adjustment for game outcome predictions” [PAGE 14]. The statistical layer can still produce a descriptive ranking adjustment; it cannot veto the possibility that weather, fatigue, or injury generated both the complementary statistic and the response.
