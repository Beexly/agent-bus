Let roles be \(r\in\mathcal R=\{\mathrm{QB},\mathrm{RB},\mathrm{WR},\mathrm{TE},\mathrm{K},\mathrm{DS}\}\), draft slot \(t=1,\dots,T\), league \(\ell\), and team \(i\). All probability and proportion quantities below are dimensionless in \([0,1]\); count quantities are integer counts; point quantities are points per season; Bayes factors are dimensionless evidence ratios. The source contains no SI physical units.

1. Conditional previous-pick influence. Let \(C_t\) be the role chosen at slot \(t\), with \(C_{t-1}\) from a different team, excluding serpentine same-team consecutive picks. Define
\[
\Delta_{r,t}=\hat P(C_t=r\mid C_{t-1}=r)-\hat P(C_t=r).
\]
A positive \(\Delta_{r,t}\) means the current role probability is elevated after the immediately preceding pick used the same role. The paper reports visual evidence that \(\Delta\) is near zero for RB, WR, and TE, but positive for early QB and for almost all K and DS selections: “In contrast, for quarterback selections in about the first 20 draft positions, and for almost all kicker and defense selections, the conditional probability is clearly greater” [PAGE 20]. Assumption: leagues and draft slots are poolable after conditioning; previous pick is observable; auto-picks and same-team snake adjacencies are excluded. Breakdown regimes: \(\hat P(C_{t-1}=r)\to 0\) makes the conditional undefined or unstable; small \(n_{r,t-1}\) produces heavy sampling error; nonstationarity across leagues/seasons breaks pooling; if previous pick changes availability rather than social influence, \(\Delta\) is not a pure herding operator.

2. Empirical winning proportion. For a team group \(g\) with \(N_g\) games, let \(y_j\in\{0,1\}\) indicate a win. Then
\[
\hat w_g=\frac{1}{N_g}\sum_{j=1}^{N_g}y_j.
\]
This is a dimensionless proportion. The paper compares handcuffed and non-handcuffed teams: “These teams won 51.04% of their games, compared to 50.56% for the teams that had no handcuﬀ pairs” [PAGE 17]. Assumption: games are exchangeable within group after conditioning; group membership is not confounded by draft position, injuries, or opponent composition. Breakdown regimes: \(N_g\) small; injury/suspension shocks; opponent composition and league heterogeneity; composition-performance reverse causality; binary win aggregation discards margin and opponent strength.

3. Role-specific point decay. Let \(\bar P_r(k)\) be the average season points for the \(k\)-th ranked player in role \(r\), averaged over the 2015–2020 seasons under PPR scoring. Define the discrete best-to-fifth loss
\[
\Delta_r^{(1,5)}=\bar P_r(1)-\bar P_r(5),
\]
in points per season. The source reports \(\Delta_{\mathrm{RB}}^{(1,5)}=119\), \(\Delta_{\mathrm{WR}}^{(1,5)}=77\), \(\Delta_{\mathrm{TE}}^{(1,5)}=138\), \(\Delta_{\mathrm{QB}}^{(1,5)}=30\), \(\Delta_{\mathrm{K}}^{(1,5)}=19\), and \(\Delta_{\mathrm{DS}}^{(1,5)}=10\) [PAGE 6]. Assumption: PPR scoring and role definitions are stable; rank ordering captures expected value. Breakdown regimes: scoring-system change; role reclassification; rank instability; average over seasons masks year-specific shocks.

4. Handcuff chance expectation. For running-back pair \((i,j)\), let \(L_{ij}\) be the set of leagues in which both were drafted and \(m_\ell\) the number of teams in league \(\ell\). Under random independent assignment conditional on both being drafted, the expected same-team count is
\[
E_{ij}=\sum_{\ell\in L_{ij}}\frac{1}{m_\ell}.
\]
This is an expected count. The paper explains that the chance count is “based on the number of teams in the leagues for which both players were drafted” [PAGE 17]. Assumption: conditional on both being drafted, team assignment is uniform and independent. Breakdown regimes: small \(m_\ell\); nonrandom assignment due to strategy; both players frequently drafted so the “backup” interpretation fails; dependence between co-selection and league size.

5. Bayes factor for same/different winning proportions. The reported hierarchical comparison uses
\[
BF_{10}=\frac{p(D\mid M_1)}{p(D\mid M_0)},
\]
a dimensionless evidence ratio, with a Gaussian effect-size prior on the mean difference with mean zero and standard deviation \(0.1\) [PAGE 17]. The paper reports \(BF=4.2\) in favor of sameness for handcuffed versus non-handcuffed teams [PAGE 17], and \(BF=4.7\), \(5.7\), and \(8.8\) in favor of sameness for QB, K, and DS copying versus non-copying [PAGE 20]. Assumption: stated prior is scientifically appropriate; hierarchical model captures league/team dependence. Breakdown regimes: prior sensitivity; zero or near-zero variance; non-normal or outlier-heavy outcomes; small group counts; BF in favor of a null is not proof of exact equality.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from classical draft game theory and sequential-game optimization to behavioral decision science. Early draft analysis used game theory for very simple two- or three-team settings; later optimization work used stochastic dynamic programming, mixed-integer programming, and knapsack analogies. The source notes the intractability problem: “[s]equential games conceptually generalize Markov decision processes and game theory, and consequently suﬀer the similar fate of general intractability as the number of participants and possible actions increase” [PAGE 3]. That lineage matters retrocausally because many optimization methods assume simple fixed opponent strategies rather than behavior estimated from data; the paper’s descriptive operators can therefore revise the opponent models built into earlier normative methods.

The behavioral lineage includes bandit problems, optimal stopping, planning/goal pursuit, theory of mind, auctions, negotiations, and game shows [PAGE 2–3]. The specific fantasy-sport behavioral predecessor is Smith et al. (2006), which used cognitive ethnography on basketball message boards but focused on post-draft roster decisions rather than the draft itself [PAGE 3]. The paper also connects to level-\(k\) reasoning and depth of forward prediction [PAGE 24], to groupthink as a possible explanation for homogeneous strategies [PAGE 23], and to the representativeness heuristic as a possible explanation for roster compositions that mirror active-roster requirements [PAGE 16]. The retrocausal lesson is that later descriptive data can reinterpret earlier “rational” draft advice: expert advice may be well adapted to role-decay regularities yet still produce homogeneous, non-optimal compositions because salience and social norms shape imitation.

Multi-week traps are several. First, treating one platform and one season as a stable law: the paper repeatedly calls for other seasons and platforms, and the draft-position result is explicitly not interpreted because it may be season-idiosyncratic [PAGE 24]. Second, treating “no evidence” as proof of no effect: the handcuff and copying comparisons use Bayes factors in favor of sameness, but those are model- and prior-dependent [PAGE 17, PAGE 20]. Third, treating handcuff rarity as proof the strategy is irrational: handcuffing is an injury-contingent hedge, and “Running backs often score the most fantasy points, but are often injured” [PAGE 16]; a one-season win proportion cannot fully evaluate a tail-risk hedge. Fifth, treating the first-pick disadvantage as real: the source says the first-drafting team performed relatively poorly, but this is exactly the kind of result that requires replication before interpretation [PAGE 24]. Sixth, treating conditional-pick imitation as pure bias: it may be rational updating to an observed run on a role, and the paper itself notes no detectable overall winning disadvantage from copying [PAGE 20].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Drafting is a recursive theory-of-mind task because each team must model what opponents will do with future picks, and opponents are modeling the same. The paper frames competition as requiring “predicting the future choices of opponents” and links this to meta-cognitive and theory-of-mind capabilities in an adversarial social setting [PAGE 2–3]. The conditional-pick operator is a low-depth ToM signature: a team observes the immediately preceding role and adjusts. The paper finds this for early QB and for almost all K and DS picks, but not for RB/WR/TE [PAGE 20]. A crisis-habit regression interpretation is that under uncertainty and time pressure, decision makers fall back on salient imitation or role-template habits rather than executing a deeper plan. The paper’s groupthink and representativeness discussions support this family of explanations: homogeneous compositions may reflect “a form of groupthink” [PAGE 23] or judging a roster “representative of a concept” [PAGE 16].

The counter-trap is the strategic response to predictable opponent habits. If opponents herd on early quarterbacks or late kickers/defenses, a team with deeper ToM can time picks to exploit their predictable runs. The paper explicitly suggests that “opponents are likely to herd on quarterbacks picked early in a draft, and kickers and defenses throughout a draft, potentially provides an opening for exploitation” [PAGE 25]. But the counter-trap is constrained by the clock: the serpentine order changes how many opponent picks intervene. “The first and last teams in the draft alternate between making consecutive picks and waiting for every other team to make two selections before they pick again. Teams in the middle of the draft order, in contrast, always wait for every other team to make one pick” [PAGE 24]. Thus the same opponent model has different value by draft slot: a middle team must forecast a full round of intervening picks, while an end team sometimes gets two consecutive picks and sometimes waits longer. The paper connects this to level-\(k\) depth: if people’s forward predictions are less than the maximum number of intervening opponent picks, draft-slot adaptation should be imperfect [PAGE 24]. The “clock” is therefore not a physical clock but the pick-timing structure that limits recursive planning. The plan-versus-crisis tension is captured by the paper’s quotation of von Moltke, “No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first encounter with the main enemy forces,” and Mike Tyson’s “Everyone has a plan until they get punched in the mouth” [PAGE 25]. The strongest inference is not that drafters lack ToM, but that their ToM is selectively engaged: deep enough to produce expert-like role timing, yet vulnerable to salient local imitation when a role run begins.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Prediction: if the effect is social imitation, the conditional spike should be attenuated or absent when the previous pick is hidden or algorithmic and should reappear when a human role choice is observable.
