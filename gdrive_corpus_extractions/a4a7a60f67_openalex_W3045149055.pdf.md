Second, cryo-EM classification can be affected by symmetry. The rS2d map is refined with imposed C3 symmetry [PAGE 8], which is reasonable for an all-down trimer but would suppress asymmetric up-state features if present at low abundance. Third, negative-stain glutaraldehyde cross-linking may stabilize or bias certain classes, although the cryo-EM data largely corroborate the NSEM assignments [PAGE 4]. Fourth, binding changes are epitope-specific: rS2d loses CR3022 binding, but that does not mean every antibody epitope is eliminated; it means the RBD-down lock occludes the CR3022-accessible RBD state [PAGE 5]. Fifth, u1S2q does not simply “stabilize up” as a rigid state; the down-state RBD and SD1 shift, while the up-state RBD is comparatively closer to the unmutated up structure [PAGE 5]. Thus the mutation appears to destabilize the down state and redistribute the ensemble rather than create a wholly new up conformation.

A further gray zone is the vaccine interpretation. The authors argue that u1S2q may expose receptor-binding-site epitopes, while rS2d may favor non-RBD responses [PAGE 8]. But immunogenicity depends on expression yield, trimer integrity, glycosylation, adjuvant, animal model, and viral challenge context. The article itself notes that “The low expression yields of rS2d are a potential hindrance to its utility” [PAGE 8]. Therefore the structural result is robust, but the immunological conclusion is a design hypothesis, not a proof of protection.

A final gray-zone issue is evolutionary extrapolation. The authors connect D614G, SD2–S2 contacts, and conformational landscapes [PAGE 7]. That link is plausible but must be treated carefully: D614G may affect fitness through spike density, cleavage, membrane fusion kinetics, or virion incorporation, not only through RBD up/down population. The measured rS2d and u1S2q structures provide a controlled physical baseline, but mapping that baseline to in vivo viral fitness requires separate infectious-cycle measurements.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

1. Domain centroid operator.  
Let a structural domain \(D\) contain \(N_D\) Cα atoms with coordinates \(\mathbf{r}_i\in\mathbb{R}^3\). The domain centroid is

\[
\mathbf{c}_D=\frac{1}{N_D}\sum_{i\in D}\mathbf{r}_i,
\qquad [\mathbf{c}_D]=\text{Å}.
\]

The source defines Cα centroids for NTD, RBD, SD1, SD2, NTD′, an S2 β-sheet motif, and the connector domain [PAGE 10]. Assumption: each selected domain segment behaves as a rigid body. Breakdown regime: if the segment is internally disordered, hinge-flexed, or poorly resolved in cryo-EM, the centroid becomes an average over multiple conformations and loses one-to-one structural meaning.

2. Inter-centroid vector operator.  
For centroids \(A\) and \(B\),

\[
\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}=\mathbf{c}_B-\mathbf{c}_A,
\qquad 
\|\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}\|=\sqrt{\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{A\to B}},
\qquad [\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}]=\text{Å}.
\]

The paper uses vectors connecting S1 domain centroids and S2 motif centroids [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if either endpoint is ambiguous because of low local resolution or alternate conformations, the vector magnitude and direction have increased uncertainty.

3. Interdomain angle operator.  
For three centroids \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\), the angle at \(B\) between \(\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{B\to C}\) is

\[
\theta_{ABC}
=
\arccos
\left(
\frac{\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{B\to C}}
{\|\mathbf{v}_{A\to B}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}_{B\to C}\|}
\right),
\qquad
[\theta_{ABC}]=\text{rad}\equiv\text{deg}.
\]

The paper reports angles such as θ1, θ2, θ3 and compares SARS-CoV-1, MERS, SARS-CoV-2, HKU1, OC43, and murine β-CoV spikes [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if either vector length approaches zero, the angle is numerically unstable; if the two vectors are nearly collinear, small coordinate errors produce large angular variance.

4. Signed dihedral operator.  
For four centroids \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), \(D\), define

\[
\mathbf{b}_1=\mathbf{c}_B-\mathbf{c}_A,\quad
\mathbf{b}_2=\mathbf{c}_C-\mathbf{c}_B,\quad
\mathbf{b}_3=\mathbf{c}_D-\mathbf{c}_C.
\]

Let

\[
\mathbf{n}_1=\mathbf{b}_1\times\mathbf{b}_2,
\qquad
\mathbf{n}_2=\mathbf{b}_2\times\mathbf{b}_3,
\qquad
\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{b}_2}{\|\mathbf{b}_2\|}.
\]

Then the signed dihedral is

\[
\phi_{ABCD}
=
\operatorname{atan2}
\left(
(\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2)\cdot\mathbf{u},
\ \mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2
\right),
\qquad
[\phi_{ABCD}]=\text{rad}\equiv\text{deg}.
\]

The source reports dihedrals such as ɸ1, ɸ2, ɸ3, and ɸ4, which report on up/down configurations and interdomain hinge motion [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if \(\mathbf{b}_1\), \(\mathbf{b}_2\), or \(\mathbf{b}_3\) are collinear, one or both normals vanish and the dihedral is undefined. This is a true geometric singularity, not merely numerical noise.

5. Principal component analysis operator.  
Let \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\) be the matrix of vector features for \(n\) structural states and \(p\) features. After centering and scaling,

\[
x_{ij}'=\frac{x_{ij}-\mu_j}{\sigma_j},
\]

with \(\mu_j\) the feature mean and \(\sigma_j\) the feature standard deviation. The covariance matrix is

\[
C=\frac{1}{n-1}(X')^{\mathsf{T}}X'.
\]

Principal components solve

\[
C\mathbf{w}_k=\lambda_k\mathbf{w}_k,
\qquad
\mathbf{z}_k=X'\mathbf{w}_k,
\]

where \(\lambda_k\) has units of variance of the scaled features and \(\mathbf{z}_k\) is the score vector. The source uses PCA to show that SARS-CoV-1, MERS, SARS-CoV-2, HKU1, OC43, and murine spikes occupy distinct regions of geometric space [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. Breakdown: PCA is linear; if the conformational manifold is strongly nonlinear, closed-loop, or sampled sparsely, the leading components can mix physically distinct states. Also, if \(n\) is small relative to \(p\), covariance estimation is ill-conditioned.

6. Conformational probability and information entropy.  
Let \(p_s\) be the probability of structural state \(s\), with

\[
p_s\geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_s p_s=1.
\]

For an up/down state classification, one may define

\[
p_{\uparrow}=\sum_{s\in\text{up}}p_s,
\qquad
p_{\downarrow}=1-p_{\uparrow}.
\]

The Shannon entropy of the state distribution is

\[
H(p)=-\sum_s p_s\ln p_s,
\qquad [H]=\text{nats},
\]

or, if logarithms are base 2,

\[
H_2(p)=-\sum_s p_s\log_2 p_s,
\qquad [H_2]=\text{bits}.
\]

The unmutated spike is described as resolving into roughly equal down and one-RBD-up classes [PAGE 4], while rS2d is found only in the down class and u1S2q shows increased up-state occupancy, including a 2-RBD-up class [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: entropy is only meaningful if the state classes are exhaustively defined and sampling is sufficient; rare states below particle-picking sensitivity are invisible.

7. Free-energy relation for equilibrium binding.  
For a simple bimolecular interaction,

\[
\mathrm{S}+\mathrm{L}\rightleftharpoons \mathrm{SL},
\]

with dissociation constant

\[
K_D=\frac{[\mathrm{S}][\mathrm{L}]}{[\mathrm{SL}]},
\qquad [K_D]=\mathrm{M}.
\]

The standard free energy is

\[
\Delta G^\circ
=
RT\ln\left(\frac{K_D}{c^\circ}\right),
\qquad
[\Delta G^\circ]=\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}},
\]

where \(R=8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), \(T\) is temperature in K, and \(c^\circ=1\ \mathrm{M}\). For CR3022 binding to the unmutated spike, the source reports “KD = 166.3 nM”, and for u1S2q it reports “KD = 175.8 nM” [PAGE 7]. At \(T=298\ \mathrm{K}\), these correspond approximately to \(\Delta G^\circ\approx -38.7\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) and \(-38.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\), respectively. Breakdown: this relation assumes 1:1 binding, equilibrium, negligible mass-transport limitation, and no avidity. The source explicitly fits to a 1:1 Langmuir model [PAGE 7], so those assumptions are part of the reported model.

8. Surface plasmon resonance kinetic operator.  
For response \(R(t)\), analyte concentration \(C\), association rate \(k_a\), and dissociation rate \(k_d\),

\[
\frac{dR}{dt}=k_a C\left(R_{\max}-R\right)-k_d R,
\qquad
[k_a]=\mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}},
\quad
[k_d]=\mathrm{s^{-1}}.
\]

At steady state,

\[
R_{\mathrm{eq}}
=
R_{\max}\frac{C}{K_D+C},
\qquad
K_D=\frac{k_d}{k_a}.
\]

The source reports for the unmutated spike “ka = 1.13 × 10^5 M–1 s–1” and “kd = 1.88 × 10–2 s–1”, and for u1S2q “ka = 1.12 × 10^5 M–1s–1” and “kd = 1.96 × 10–2 s–1” [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: if the surface has heterogeneous ligand density, rebinding, or conformational capture during injection, the fitted rate constants become effective parameters rather than microscopic constants.

9. Cryo-EM resolution criterion.  
For two independent half-maps with Fourier transforms \(F_1(\mathbf{q})\) and \(F_2(\mathbf{q})\), the Fourier shell correlation is

\[
\mathrm{FSC}(q)
=
\frac{
\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|=q}F_1(\mathbf{q})F_2^*(\mathbf{q})
}{
\sqrt{
\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|=q}|F_1(\mathbf{q})|^2
\sum_{|\mathbf{q}|=q}|F_2(\mathbf{q})|^2
}
},
\qquad
[q]=\mathrm{Å^{-1}}.
\]

The resolution is the spatial frequency where FSC crosses 0.143:

\[
d=\frac{1}{q_{0.143}},
\qquad [d]=\mathrm{Å}.
\]

The source lists map resolutions of 2.7 Å for rS2d, 3.2 Å for u1S2q all-down, 3.3 Å for u1S2q 1-up, and 3.6 Å for u1S2q 2-up, with FSC threshold 0.143 [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: FSC can be inflated by mask correlation, overfitting to noise, or preferred orientation; local resolution can vary strongly within a map. The source itself notes that local resolution varied widely for rS2d, with higher resolution in S2 and lower resolution in S1 [PAGE 4].

10. Structural deviation operator.  
For two aligned structures with matched atom coordinates \(\mathbf{r}_i^A\) and \(\mathbf{r}_i^B\), after optimal rotation \(R\) and translation \(\mathbf{t}\),

\[
\mathrm{RMSD}
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left\|
\mathbf{r}_i^A-
\left(R\mathbf{r}_i^B+\mathbf{t}\right)
\right\|^2
},
\qquad [\mathrm{RMSD}]=\mathrm{Å}.
\]

The rS2d reconstruction aligned to the unmutated down-state structure with “root-mean-square deviation (r.m.s.d.) of 0.5 Å” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: RMSD depends on atom selection and structural superposition; large local hinge motion can be hidden if most of the trimer is aligned.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

The scientific lineage begins with the concept of class I viral fusion proteins. Such proteins must protect conserved fusion machinery while remaining capable of large conformational transitions. The article states that “Class I fusion proteins, such as the CoV-2 S-protein, undergo large conformational changes during the fusion process and must, by necessity, be highly flexible and dynamic” [PAGE 1]. That lineage runs through influenza hemagglutinin, paramyxovirus F proteins, HIV-1 Env, and coronavirus spikes. The common theme is metastability: the prefusion state is not a single rigid object but a free-energy landscape with accessible transitions.

A second lineage is the structural-mathematics lineage. The use of centroids, interdomain vectors, angles, and dihedrals is a direct extension of molecular-geometry analysis used in protein structural bioinformatics. The paper implements this with VMD Tcl scripts and R-based PCA [PAGE 10]. The method assumes that large domains can be reduced to point centroids and that relative orientations capture functionally meaningful differences. This is powerful but genealogically constrained: it inherits the assumption that centroid motion dominates the relevant conformational change. For SARS-CoV-2 spike, that assumption is supported by the visible rigid-body character of NTD, RBD, SD1, SD2, and S2 motifs [PAGE 3].

A third lineage is HIV-1 Env immunogen design. The authors explicitly invoke HIV vaccine lessons: “lessons learned in the continued effort to produce a broadly protective HIV-1 vaccine have demonstrated the importance of a detailed understanding and control of fusion protein dynamics” [PAGE 7]. This lineage imports the idea that epitope masking, conformational selection, and trimer stabilization are central to vaccine design. It also imports a warning: stabilization can reduce exposure of desired neutralizing epitopes or alter immunodominance.

A fourth lineage is coronavirus spike engineering. The construct background includes proline substitutions at residues 986 and 987, furin-site substitution, trimerization foldon, protease site, TwinStrepTag, and HisTag [PAGE 10]. That engineering tradition prioritizes soluble, well-folded trimers. The present paper extends that tradition by moving from generic prefusion stabilization to state-specific control: all-RBD-down rS2d versus RBD-up-enriched u1S2q.

Multi-week traps in this genealogy are concrete. Trap one: treating “closed” and “open” as binary when the experimental observable is a particle classification mixture. Trap two: assuming that a disulfide-locked down state is universally better because it is stable. The source counters this by noting that receptor-binding-site responses are important in convalescent patients and that down-state locking would not elicit those responses [PAGE 8]. Trap three: assuming that up-state exposure directly increases neutralization. The source shows that CR3022 docking on u1S2q up states has clashes and that binding affinity is nearly unchanged [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. Thus the antibody must capture or induce a conformational adjustment, not merely recognize a static up RBD. Trap four: assuming that cryo-EM classes represent thermodynamic populations without correcting for particle-picking bias, preferred orientation, or symmetry. Trap five: projecting evolutionary conclusions too quickly. The D614G discussion is relevant, but it requires independent virological evidence beyond the engineered constructs [PAGE 7].

The historical lesson is that rational immunogen design is not merely “make it stable.” It is control of the probability distribution over functionally relevant conformations, while preserving fold integrity, expression, glycosylation, and antigenic authenticity.

</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

The phrase “recursive theory of mind” here means modeling the decision problem as an interaction among the virus, the immune system, the experimentalist, and the inference model itself. The virus does not “know” the immune response, but natural selection acts on variants whose spike conformational distribution changes epitope exposure. The immune system responds to the exposed distribution, not to the hidden average structure. The experimentalist designs mutants under time pressure. The inference model must avoid attributing a single state to a heterogeneous ensemble.

A minimal formal decision model is:

\[
d^\star
=
\arg\max_{d\in\mathcal{D}}
\left[
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim p(\theta\mid \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}})}
\left[
R(d,\theta)
\right]
-
C(d)
\right],
\]

where \(d\) is a design choice, \(\theta\) is the true conformational or immunogenic parameter, \(\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{obs}}\) is observed structural and binding data, \(R\) is scientific or vaccine value, and \(C(d)\) is expression, manufacturability, and risk cost. The expectation is over posterior uncertainty, not over a single point estimate.

Crisis habit regression occurs when pandemic time pressure pushes the experimentalist toward a familiar solution: a closed, stabilized trimer, because previous coronavirus and HIV work favored stable prefusion antigens. The habit is rational in many contexts but can be trapped if the scientific question is specifically about receptor-binding-site exposure. The source shows the counter-trap: u1S2q increases RBD-up accessibility and may enhance RBD-directed responses [PAGE 8]. A crisis habit regression would discard this possibility because it appears less stable.

Counter-trap occurs when the design goal becomes “maximally up” without checking antibody accessibility, trimer integrity, or fusion relevance. The source’s docking analysis provides a check: even when RBDs are up, CR3022 Fab clashes with NTD and adjacent RBD in both 1-up and 2-up states, so binding requires a further conformational change [PAGE 6]. Thus, simply increasing up-state population is not sufficient for every epitope. The correct model is recursive: the design changes the conformational ensemble; the antibody selects or induces a state; the immunogen must make that state accessible without destroying the trimer.

Clock pressure appears in three ways. First, data-generation clock: cryo-EM classification, model building, and validation take time, especially under pandemic conditions. Second, biological clock: viral evolution may change spike contacts while vaccine design proceeds. The source warns that β-coronaviruses can rapidly evade conformationally selective antibody responses, although such mutations may carry fitness penalties [PAGE 7]. Third, decision clock: public-health demands favor deployable candidates, but the paper shows that expression yield can limit utility, especially for rS2d [PAGE 8].

A recursive theory-of-mind-safe design policy is therefore:

1. Estimate the conformational distribution, not just the best-resolution class.  
2. Identify which epitopes are preferentially exposed in each class.  
3. Test binding to antibodies that read out specific states, such as CR3022 for occluded RBD-up epitopes [PAGE 5].  
4. Measure expression, thermostability, and oligomeric state before advancing.  
5. Treat structural stability and immunogenic exposure as separate objectives, not as one scalar score.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

The physical veto layer asks whether the engineered designs are physically possible under the observed conditions. The rS2d construct introduces Cys383 and Cys985 to form an interprotomer disulfide between one RBD and S2 near HR1. The source reports connecting density at the engineered disulfide and says this confirms disulfide formation [PAGE 4]. The reconstruction reaches 2.7 Å overall resolution, and alignment to the unmutated down-state structure gives an r.m.s.d. of 0.5 Å [PAGE 4]. Therefore, no gross physical veto is observed: the disulfide forms, the trimer remains folded, and the spike remains recognizable as a down-state trimer.

However, a physical veto would be triggered if any of the following occurred: no disulfide density at Cys383–Cys985; widespread misfolding; inability to purify trimer; loss of the C3-symmetric down-state map; or disappearance of S1 density due to unfolding. Those vetoes are not observed. The low expression yield of rS2d is a practical veto for manufacturability, not a structural impossibility [PAGE 8].

The biomechanical veto layer asks whether the mutation interferes with the mechanical transition required for fusion or with the hinge mechanics of the spike. The spike must undergo large conformational changes to expose fusion machinery [PAGE 1]. Locking the RBD down may prevent receptor engagement, as shown by reduced ACE-2 binding and absent CR3022 binding [PAGE 5]. That is a functional consequence, not a structural collapse. For u1S2q, the SD1 region shifts relative to S2, with reported θ1 and ɸ3 displacements of 3.7° and 2.4° in the down state, and additional NTD shifts of about 6.0° in θ3 and ɸ2 [PAGE 5]. These are measurable but moderate rigid-body displacements. The structures still classify into down, 1-up, and 2-up states [PAGE 6, PAGE 8]. Therefore, the biomechanical veto is not triggered at the level of fold integrity; the mutations alter hinge equilibria rather than destroy the mechanical module.

A biomechanical veto would be triggered if u1S2q caused: complete loss of trimerization; severe interprotomer clash; disappearance of the 2-up class; or inability to model the mutated loop into density. The paper reports models for all-down, 1-up, and 2-up states, with map resolutions of 3.2 Å, 3.3 Å, and 3.6 Å respectively [PAGE 8]. Thus the biomechanical evidence supports a modified but viable conformational landscape.

The weather layer is interpreted here as microenvironmental experimental perturbation: buffer, pH, temperature, grid preparation, humidity, beam dose, and sample adsorption. A weather veto would occur if environmental noise produced an apparent conformational shift larger than the biological signal. Formal condition:

\[
|\Delta X_{\mathrm{env}}|
>
\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{bio}}^2+\sigma_{\mathrm{meas}}^2},
\]

where \(\Delta X_{\mathrm{env}}\) is the shift attributable to environmental perturbation, \(\sigma_{\mathrm{bio}}\) is biological heterogeneity, and \(\sigma_{\mathrm{meas}}\) is measurement uncertainty. The source uses controlled buffers: nCoV buffer for thermal shift, HBS pH 7.4 for negative stain, and HBS-EP+ for SPR [PAGE 10]. Cryo samples are energy-filtered, dose-fractionated, motion-corrected, and classified in cryoSPARC [PAGE 10]. These controls reduce environmental artifact risk. However, negative-stain glutaraldehyde cross-linking and air–water interface adsorption can still bias particle distributions, so the cryo-EM corroboration is essential. Because the cryo-EM results match the NSEM trends—rS2d down-only and u1S2q up-enriched [PAGE 4]—the weather veto is not sufficient to overturn the main structural conclusion.

The final adjudication is therefore: no physical or biomechanical veto is proven; no weather-like environmental veto is sufficient to overturn the structural findings. The remaining risk is interpretive: the structural ensemble is robust, but vaccine value and viral fitness effects require orthogonal assays.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

1. Counterfactual veto: S383C/D985C fails to oxidize or form the intended interprotomer disulfide.  
Verifiable prediction: if the engineered cysteines remain reduced, rS2d should not be exclusively down-state. One should observe CR3022 binding, ACE-2 binding comparable to unmutated spike, and cryo-EM classes with one or more RBD-up protomers. The observed data falsify this counterfactual: the source reports connecting density at the engineered disulfide [PAGE 4], no measurable CR3022 binding [PAGE 5], and failure to find open-state particles after exhaustive classification [PAGE 4]. Reward-consistent verification would use non-reducing versus reducing SDS-PAGE or mass spectrometry to confirm disulfide formation, followed by cryo-EM and SPR.

2. Counterfactual veto: u1S2q mutations destabilize SD1 so severely that the spike no longer forms a trimer or cannot be classified into down/1-up/2-up states.  
Verifiable prediction: if SD1 is disrupted, size-exclusion chromatography would show aggregation or degradation, negative-stain particles would be heterogeneous or absent, thermal shift would show a major drop in inflection temperature, and cryo-EM would fail to produce coherent maps. The observed data falsify this counterfactual: u1S2q yields separable cryo-EM classes, including all-down, 1-up, and 2-up maps [PAGE 8], and the source reports modeled states with resolutions of 3.2 Å, 3.3 Å, and 3.6 Å [PAGE 8]. A stricter verification would add differential scanning calorimetry, SEC-MALS, and mass photometry to quantify oligomeric state and conformational distribution.

3. Counterfactual veto: RBD-up exposure is sufficient by itself to produce high-affinity CR3022 binding without additional RBD rotation.  
Verifiable prediction: if up-state RBDs present the CR3022 epitope without further rearrangement, docking should show no steric clash in 1-up and 2-up structures, and u1S2q should show a substantially stronger CR3022 affinity than the unmutated spike. The observed data falsify this counterfactual: docking shows clashes of the CR3022 constant domain with NTD and adjacent RBD in both 1-up and 2-up conformations [PAGE 6], and the affinities are nearly unchanged: “KD = 166.3 nM” for unmutated and “KD = 175.8 nM” for u1S2q [PAGE 7]. Reward-consistent verification would use epitope-specific Fabs or IgGs with known angle requirements, hydrogen–deuterium exchange mapping, and negative-stain or cryo-EM antibody-soaking experiments to determine whether binding selects a transient rotated state.

</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
