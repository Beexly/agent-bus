<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight’s 1921 analysis sits at a historical inflection point. Looking backward, the passage engages the classical political-economy critique of monopoly found in Adam Smith’s *Wealth of Nations*, where monopolies are condemned for restricting output and raising prices above their natural level. Knight accepts the Smithian welfare logic but shifts the normative focus from producer surplus to inventor incentive: the patent is a temporary monopoly deliberately created to solve a public-goods problem. Looking forward, the passage anticipates Kenneth Arrow’s 1962 formalization of the patent paradox—information is costly to produce but cheap to reproduce—and Martin Weitzman’s later work on recombinant growth. The “pioneer versus finisher” distinction prefigures the cumulative-innovation literature (Scotchmer, Green and Scotchmer), in which broad pioneer patents can block follow-on innovation unless licensing or compulsory-licensing rules are designed correctly.

The multi-week traps embedded in this lineage are temporal rather than weekly in the literal sense, but they operate on innovation-cycle timescales. Trap A: the pioneer lag. Basic research and exploration may require months or years before an idea is reducible to practice, yet patent clocks begin at filing. An improver can file a narrow improvement patent weeks after the pioneer’s disclosure, capturing the “finishing touch” reward Knight describes [PAGE 372], while the pioneer’s broader patent remains pending. Trap B: the evergreen cliff. As a patent term approaches expiration, the incumbent has strong incentives to file continuation or divisional applications, or to make trivial formulation changes, thereby resetting the effective exclusion clock and reproducing the “artificially high price” beyond the statutory term [PAGE 372]. Trap C: the disclosure paradox. Because the patent requires public disclosure in exchange for exclusivity, competitors can design around the claims immediately upon grant; in fast-moving fields, the 20-year term may be longer than the technology’s useful life, making the patent a barrier to diffusion rather than a stimulus to invention.

Knight’s final recommendation—replacing artificial monopoly with “some direct method of stimulating and rewarding research” [PAGE 372]—echoes in modern innovation policy debates over prizes, grants, and advance market commitments. The genealogical trap is that every proposed alternative reintroduces the same information problem Knight identifies: who decides which research is “deserving,” and how is the pioneer’s contribution separated from the finisher’s without a market price for pioneering?

When uncertainty spikes, economic agents regress to crisis habits that reproduce Knight’s structural pathologies. The counter-trap is that the very act of defensive patenting increases the artificial exclusion zone, raising \(p_m\) and reducing \(q^*\) for downstream users. Each firm, reasoning recursively about the others’ recursive reasoning, produces an escalating race to the patent office. The equilibrium is over-provision of legal exclusion and under-provision of actual pioneering exploration. The clock layer enters through the finite patent term \(T\): as the expiration date approaches, the incumbent’s theory of mind about post-expiration competition shifts abruptly. Pre-cliff, the firm prices at monopoly levels; post-cliff, it must anticipate immediate entry. This discontinuity creates end-gaming behavior—evergreening, strategic continuations, and regulatory lobbying—to push the effective cliff further into the future.

The recursive theory of mind also applies to the policymaker. If the administrator designing the “direct method” of research rewards [PAGE 372] believes that pioneers are systematically under-rewarded, she may tilt funding toward basic science. But if she anticipates that political actors will capture the allocation process, she may instead preserve the patent system as a decentralized, if crude, commitment device. The fixed point depends on second-order beliefs about institutional competence, which Knight assumes away by invoking “political intelligence” without modeling its formation [PAGE 372].

Biomechanical layer: The distinction between “pioneering and exploration” and the “detail or finishing touch” [PAGE 372] is not merely conceptual; it is constrained by human cognition and labor time. Weather layer: Knightian uncertainty functions as an exogenous weather system for the innovation process. A patent term \(T\) set optimally under one regime may be catastrophically long under another (e.g., if the invention is rapidly superseded) or catastrophically short (e.g., if the market takes decades to develop).
