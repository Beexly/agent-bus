The source lineage moves from gross neurotrauma to microstructure to function. Early layers are clinical and CT-based: the Marshall classification is used for injury severity [PAGE 37], and the Glasgow Outcome Scale-Extended is used for 6-month outcome [PAGE 41; PAGE 43]. The next layer is histopathological and biomechanical: DAI is a shear-strain injury from acceleration–deceleration, with primary and secondary axotomy [PAGE 11], and diagnosis is confirmed postmortem [PAGE 9]. The next layer is microstructural imaging: DTI quantifies FA, MD, RD, and AD, with TBSS and FSL processing [PAGE 26], and the systematic review summarizes 10 PCS studies with heterogeneous regions [PAGE 72; PAGE 75–76]. The next layer is molecular: AMPAR, NMDAR, and KAR peptides, S100B, GFAP, UCHL1, tau, and SNTF are candidate biomarkers [PAGE 57–59]. The next layer is therapeutic: tDCS, FES, anticonvulsants, and rehabilitation [PAGE 16; PAGE 65; PAGE 80].

A normal CT at day 0 is retrospectively reclassified as “normal-appearing white matter” when DTI at 1 year shows increased MD [PAGE 24; PAGE 28]. An early seizure-free week is retrospectively misread as anti-epileptogenic success when late seizures are the actual endpoint; the source distinguishes early PTS within 7 days from late PTS after 7 days [PAGE 80] and states that late PTS are not decreased by AED prophylaxis [PAGE 82]. The 3-month trap: PCS requires symptom persistence for 3 months or longer, so 6-week symptoms are not the same endpoint [PAGE 72]. The 6-month trap: DAI mortality and dependence are measured at 6 months, not at discharge [PAGE 41]. The 1-year trap: chronic DAI TMS required at least 1 year from trauma [PAGE 8; PAGE 10]. The 5-week trap: FES used 15 one-hour sessions over 5 weeks [PAGE 65; PAGE 67]. The 12-month and lifetime trap: soccer heading exposure is sometimes measured over the prior 12 months and sometimes estimated over a lifetime [PAGE 85–86]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when decision-makers under uncertainty default to the most available signal. The available signal is often CT: a normal scan is treated as “no injury,” despite the source’s repeated point that DAI and PCS can be invisible on routine imaging [PAGE 9; PAGE 24; PAGE 72]. Another habit is symptom resolution as clearance: the SCAT is widely used, but the source states it “should not be relied on for decisions regarding whether an athlete should be allowed to return to play” [PAGE 55]. Another habit is early seizure freedom as proof of anti-epileptogenesis, although the evidence supports only early PTS reduction [PAGE 80; PAGE 82]. Another habit is biomarker threshold as verdict: AMPAR, NMDAR, and KAR cut-offs are presented with sensitivities and specificities, but the source notes the need for 24-hour sampling and limited concussion studies [PAGE 57]. These habits are regressions because they collapse a multi-layer, time-indexed problem into one familiar test.

The counter-trap is overcorrection. If normal CT is falsely reassuring, the counter-trap is to treat every DTI abnormality as PCS. But the systematic review explicitly states no consensus on one DTI parameter in one region as a PCS biomarker [PAGE 72]. If early phenytoin works, the counter-trap is to continue prophylaxis indefinitely; the guideline recommendation is 7 days [PAGE 82]. If TCCS correlates with CT, the counter-trap is to replace CT; but 5–18% of patients have inadequate acoustic windows [PAGE 40]. If heading is associated with white-matter changes, the counter-trap is to ban all heading without dose-response validation; the source reports thresholds of 885–1550 headings per year for FA reduction and 1800 per year for cognitive impairment, but the data are preliminary and cross-sectional [PAGE 86]. If a biomarker is elevated, the counter-trap is to declare concussion; but the source describes return-to-play decisions using biomarker trajectories alongside ImPACT and imaging, not a single cutoff [PAGE 58–59].

The clock is the corrective. Recursive theory of mind matters because each actor models the others’ thresholds. An athlete may model the clinician as a clearance gate and underreport symptoms to return to play; the clinician may model the athlete as incentive-biased and over-rely on objective tests; the coach may model the clinician as conservative and seek another opinion; the family may model recovery as independence at home rather than cognitive capacity. The source’s outcome data show why this recursion is dangerous: at 6 months after DAI, 30.8% died, 45.1% had full recovery, and 88.2% of survivors were independent, but 11.8% remained dependent [PAGE 41; PAGE 44–45]. “Independent” is not the same as “cognitively restored.” The clock forces the clinician to ask: which endpoint, at which time, measured by which layer? The recursive trap is answering that question with the other person’s incentive rather than the source’s time window.
