Knight insists that “all these classes are anything but homogeneous” [PAGE 128]. He invokes Cairnes’s “non-competing groups” for labor [PAGE 128], and natural-agent dissimilarities: “wheat land vs. pineapple land, arable vs. grazing or timber, and all contrasted with mineral-bearing and the multitudinous kinds of the latter” [PAGE 128]. Capital is “peculiar,” with “fluidity” depending on “the length of time taken into view” [PAGE 128].

These hops converge on the breakdown of classical imputation. If factors are not homogeneous, then an equal price paid for two services does not imply an equal marginal contribution to utility-bearing output; the services are not substitutes across non-competing groups or across soil types. If factors are inseparable from their owners, their price cannot be observed independently of the person’s capacity. If capital’s fluidity is horizon-dependent, the category “capital” is not a fixed input but a function of the planning period τ. Knight is clearing conceptual space for his central thesis: profit arises not from routine imputation of homogeneous factors, but from entrepreneurial judgment under genuine uncertainty, because the factors of production are heterogeneous, specific, and only partially alienable.

Gray-zone audit. The phrase “equal utilities to all persons” presupposes interpersonally comparable cardinal utility, a strong assumption that modern welfare economics treats as problematic. “Equal price contributions” assumes continuous divisibility and convex substitution, which fail when land is specific to wheat versus pineapple or when labor is locked in non-competing groups. The text does not specify how to measure “fluidity” as a function of time, nor does it define the exact horizon at which capital shifts from illiquid to fluid. These are not mere imprecisions; they are singularities where the stable pricing condition ceases to be well-posed, forcing the analyst from competitive imputation into discretionary entrepreneurial calculation under uncertainty.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(F\) be the set of productive services and \(C\) the set of consumption goods. Let \(p_f \in \mathbb{R}_+\) be the price of service \(f\), in [currency / service-unit]. Let \(U_i : \mathbb{R}_+^{|C|} \to \mathbb{R}\) be the utility of person \(i\) over consumption bundles, in [utils]. Knight’s stability condition from [PAGE 128] is formalized as:

\[
\forall i,\; \forall f,g \in F:\quad p_f = p_g \;\Longrightarrow\; p_f \,\Delta q_{if} \;=\; p_g \,\Delta q_{ig}
\quad\text{whenever}\quad \Delta U_i(c^{(f)}) \;=\; \Delta U_i(c^{(g)}),
\]

where \(\Delta q_{if}\) is the quantity of service \(f\) imputed to person \(i\)’s consumption bundle. Equivalently, equal expenditures on services yield equal utility-weighted contributions to consumption.

Assumptions:
(A1) Divisibility: services and goods are infinitely divisible.
(A2) Interpersonally comparable utility: \(U_i\) and \(U_j\) are measurable on a common scale.
(A3) Competitive price-taking: no agent unilaterally sets \(p_f\).
(A4) Fixed taxonomy: the classification of \(f\) into land/labor/capital is invariant over the analysis window.

Units: \(p_f\) [currency/service-unit]; \(q\) [service-units]; \(U\) [utils]; \(\tau\) [time].

Singularity / breakdown regimes:
Regime S1 — Factor heterogeneity: If \(f \in\) “wheat land” and \(g \in\) “pineapple land,” then \(f\) and \(g\) are non-substitutes [PAGE 128]. The implication \(p_f = p_g \Rightarrow\) equal contribution fails because \(\partial c / \partial f\) and \(\partial c / \partial g\) operate on disjoint production frontiers. Singularity arises at the soil-specificity boundary.
Regime S2 — Personal inseparability: If service \(f\) is embodied in person \(i\) and non-alienable (“not separable from the persons of their owners” [PAGE 128]), then \(p_f\) does not exist independently of \(i\)’s capacity. The operator \(\partial c / \partial f\) is undefined because \(f\) cannot be transacted separately from \(i\).
Regime S3 — Horizon-dependent capital fluidity: Let \(\Phi(\tau) \in [0,1]\) denote capital fluidity over planning horizon \(\tau\). Knight states fluidity depends on “the length of time taken into view” [PAGE 128]. If \(\tau\) is misspecified, the capital stock \(K(\tau)\) is discontinuous at horizon thresholds, and the production function \(Y = F(L, \text{Land}, K(\tau))\) is not well-defined without an exogenous \(\tau\).

Breakdown occurs when any of S1–S3 holds: the equal-price condition no longer identifies a unique stable price vector, and competitive imputation collapses into entrepreneurial discretion under uncertainty.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical lineage. Page 128 sits inside Knight’s treatment of distribution and imputation, immediately preceding his theory of profit as the return to uncertainty-bearing. The genealogy runs: Ricardo’s principle of differential rent (land heterogeneity) \(\rightarrow\) Cairnes’s non-competing groups (labor-market segmentation) \(\rightarrow\) Böhm-Bawerk’s average period of production and time-averaged capital (capital as a time-structured fund) \(\rightarrow\) Marx’s capital as self-expanding value (fluidity across time) \(\rightarrow\) Knight’s page 128, which dissolves the fixed land/labor/capital trinity into heterogeneous, specific, and horizon-relative agents [PAGE 128].

Multi-week traps. Because land is crop-specific (“wheat land vs. pineapple land” [PAGE 128]), a farmer who misclassifies soil suitability commits to a production cycle lasting weeks or months before discovery. Because labor is divided into non-competing groups, a worker cannot shift from one licensed occupation to another within days; wage differentials persist across multi-week search windows. Because capital fluidity depends on horizon \(\tau\), a firm that finances long-specific assets with short-term liabilities faces a maturity mismatch whose resolution unfolds over weeks of refinancing or fire-sale adjustment. These are structural traps whose duration is set by biological growth cycles, contractual lock-in, and the time needed to reclassify capital across horizons.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under uncertainty, agents regress to the conventional taxonomy: they speak as if there were one “labor” market, one “land” rent, and one homogeneous “capital” stock. This habit reduces cognitive load but contradicts Knight’s own heterogeneity argument [PAGE 128]. The regressed agent treats capital fluidity as constant, ignoring that it varies with “the length of time taken into view” [PAGE 128].

Counter-trap. A second-order agent anticipates the regression and exploits it: when crisis forces others to liquidate “capital” as if it were homogeneous and fluid, the second-order agent buys specific capital at a discount, knowing that its true value depends on a longer \(\tau\) and on its physical specificity (e.g., timber vs. mineral-bearing land [PAGE 128]). The counter-trap is to model others as using \(\tau_{\text{short}}\) while the agent uses \(\tau_{\text{long}}\).

Clock. The planning horizon \(\tau\) is the recursive clock. As \(\tau\) contracts under stress, capital fluidity \(\Phi(\tau)\) falls toward zero for specific assets; as \(\tau\) expands, \(\Phi(\tau)\) rises. Agents who fail to update \(\tau\) recursively misprice long-specific capital during short-horizon panics and overcommit to short-term labor contracts during long-horizon booms. The stability condition from page 128 therefore holds only within a fixed \(\tau\) regime; crossing the horizon boundary triggers a regime change in imputation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A viable model must pass three layer-specific vetoes.

Physical layer (natural agents). Wheat land, pineapple land, arable, grazing, timber, and mineral-bearing land are physically non-fungible [PAGE 128]. Any imputation model that aggregates them into a single “land” factor is physically vetoed because the production-possibility frontier has kinks at soil-type boundaries; no continuous price can equate marginal products across non-convertible land classes.

Biomechanical layer (human services). Knight restricts material goods that are “not separable from the persons of their owners, to whose personal capacities the same restrictions must apply” [PAGE 128]. Labor is embodied; a service cannot be alienated from the person’s capacity. Any model that rents human capital as if it were a separable machine is biomechanically vetoed because the supply curve is kinked by personal non-transferability and by the non-competing-group boundaries described by Cairnes [PAGE 128].

Weather / ecological layer. Grazing land and timber are exposed to weather cycles, ecological succession, and pest cycles; mineral-bearing land is subject to geological depletion. These exogenous processes alter the flow of productive services independently of price. A model that treats natural-agent services as price-determined flows without an ecological state variable is vetoed by the weather layer.

Veto proof. The minimal admissible model must contain: (i) a factor-specificity index \(\sigma_f \in \{0,1\}\) distinguishing crop- or mineral-specific land from convertible land; (ii) an inseparability constraint \(\iota_p \in \{0,1\}\) for embodied human services; and (iii) a fluidity function \(\Phi(\tau)\) for capital. Omitting any component triggers a veto at the corresponding layer, and the stable pricing condition of page 128 cannot be restored by price adjustment alone [PAGE 128].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto Scenario 1 — Homogeneous land across crops. Condition: a valuation model assigns a single price \(p_L\) to all agricultural land in a county, ignoring “wheat land vs. pineapple land” [PAGE 128]. Counterfactual: impose crop-specific land prices indexed to soil and climate suitability. Prediction: observed cash rents will diverge systematically by crop-suitability class even within the same market region; the homogeneous-land model will exhibit residuals correlated with soil-quality indices. Verifiable by matching USDA soil surveys to county-level land-rent transactions over a multi-year panel.

Veto Scenario 2 — Homogeneous labor across licensed occupations. Condition: a wage model treats all workers with similar measured experience as one competitive pool, rejecting Cairnes’s non-competing groups [PAGE 128]. Counterfactual: segment labor by occupational licensing and credential barriers. Prediction: wage differentials between adjacent licensed and unlicensed roles will persist despite equal years of experience, and inter-group mobility will remain low over six-month windows. Verifiable via BLS Occupational Employment Statistics and state licensing records.

Veto Scenario 3 — Capital fluidity independent of horizon. Condition: a liquidity model assigns a single haircut to a class of capital assets regardless of the holder’s planning horizon \(\tau\), contradicting the statement that capital “fluidity” depends on “the length of time taken into view” [PAGE 128]. Counterfactual: impose horizon-dependent fluidity \(\Phi(\tau)\), with \(\Phi(\tau) \to 0\) as \(\tau \to 0\) for specific capital (timber stands, mineral rights, specialized plant). Prediction: in a sudden horizon contraction, prices of long-specific capital assets will fall disproportionately relative to short-maturity claims on identical cash flows, not because fundamentals changed but because \(\Phi(\tau)\) shifted. Verifiable via maturity-matched asset-price differentials during liquidity stress events.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
