Deep multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit:

The causal chain in this work begins with raw telemetry—moment-to-moment in-game action logs collected automatically and at scale [PAGE 1] [PAGE 2]. These logs are not used directly as behavioral evidence; they are first abstracted into a state space following Juul's convention [PAGE 4] [PAGE 5]: nodes encode game states (e.g., marker position, key pickup status in Wuzzit Trouble) and directed links encode actions (e.g., turning cog 3 clockwise twice) [PAGE 5]. The second hop is the construction of a pairwise state-difference operator d(s1, s2), defined as the smallest number of actions needed to transform s1 into s2 or vice versa, set to infinity when no transformation path exists [PAGE 6]. This operator is intentionally domain-independent, avoiding the need for expert-supplied dissimilarity functions that biased prior systems such as Playtracer [PAGE 3] [PAGE 5].

The third hop lifts state differences to sequence differences. Given two behavior sequences a = {s1...sn} and b = {q1...qm}, Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) computes D(n,m) through dynamic programming [PAGE 6]. DTW non-linearly warps sequences of unequal length to minimize cumulative state difference, treating insertion, deletion, and match operations symmetrically in its recursion [PAGE 6]. This produces a dissimilarity matrix over the population of play traces.

The fourth hop is geometric embedding: sequence-distance values are encoded as link lengths in a force-directed graph, so that similar sequences clump and dissimilar sequences separate [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6]. This is formally equivalent to metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) [PAGE 6], inheriting MDS's geometric interpretability while remaining driven by the behavioral data itself rather than by expert priors.

The fifth hop is synchronized highlighting: querying a sequence ID in the sequence view highlights the corresponding path through the state graph, and vice versa, distributing visual information across two views to avoid overloading the user's cognitive process [PAGE 2] [PAGE 7]. The final hop is hypothesis formation: researchers identify clusters (e.g., Cluster 1: direct key collection; Cluster 2: bonus-first strategy) and detect outliers such as SequenceID 3, where a player moves one step at a time rather than using larger cog rotations [PAGE 7].

Gray-zone audit: (1) d(s1,s2) is described as symmetric in intent but the worked example on [PAGE 6] explicitly handles irreversible one-way transformations by taking the feasible direction only; this yields a quasi-metric, not a true metric, and DTW's triangle-inequality assumptions may be silently violated. Historical and scientific lineage:

The Glyph system is not an isolated invention; it is a recombination of three established lineages. First, the state/action abstraction follows Juul's [13] treatment of game states, adapted here so that "a game state captures all current information associated with in-game entities" [PAGE 5]. Second, the node-link population graph descends directly from Playtracer [1], which used multidimensional scaling (MDS) to place nodes and encoded popularity via node size and terminal status via color [PAGE 3]. Playtracer's limitation—requiring a domain-specific dissimilarity function and failing to support scalable individual-trace comparison—is the precise gap Glyph targets [PAGE 2] [PAGE 3]. Third, the sequence-similarity engine imports Dynamic Time Warping from Berndt & Clifford [3], originally developed for speech recognition and time-series pattern discovery at KDD 1994 [PAGE 6] [PAGE 9]. Glyph substitutes the state-difference operator d(s1,s2) for simple symbol matching, generalizing DTW into a minimum edit distance over game states [PAGE 6]. The force-directed layout invokes Fruchterman & Reingold [9], whose O(n³) placement cost becomes Glyph's primary scaling ceiling [PAGE 8]. Finally, the cognitive-load motivation cites Lavie [16] on selective attention under load and Sarter et al. [25] on attentional effort, grounding the dual-view design in human factors rather than purely algorithmic concerns [PAGE 3] [PAGE 8].

Multi-week traps: The dataset spans one and a half months [PAGE 7], which captures short-term session behavior but not the multi-week crystallization of stable expertise; strategies observed in week one may regress or consolidate by week six, a temporal blind spot the cross-sectional segmentation cannot reveal. Usability testing was conducted in discrete expert sessions [PAGE 7], so interface fatigue, color-channel exhaustion, and working-memory decay across repeated weekly use are unobserved. The force-directed O(n³) breakdown is a latent deployment trap: it does not appear during small expert tests with hundreds of users [PAGE 5], but emerges catastrophically once the system scales to full telemetry populations of tens of thousands of nodes [PAGE 8]. Continuous-state games (e.g., analog-stick or mouse-aim puzzles) represent a domain trap: discretization into region landmarks is a workaround, but it erases fine-grained strategic distinctions below landmark resolution [PAGE 8].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock dynamics:

Crisis habit regression: When puzzle difficulty increases, players do not necessarily explore the full action space; they regress to low-cognitive-load habits. In the Wuzzit Trouble case study, SequenceID 3 exhibits exactly this regression: instead of using a larger cog rotation to reach the key efficiently, the player repeats "move counter-clockwise 1 step" six times before collecting [PAGE 7]. Glyph exposes this as an outlier path embedded within Cluster 1, allowing researchers to distinguish regression from strategic choice. The theory of mind here is recursive: the researcher models the player as modeling the game's mechanics under pressure, and the tool surfaces the moment when the player's internal model collapses into step-wise iteration.

Counter-trap: Designers embed intended "traps" in level structure—multiple cogs, multiple keys, bonus gems—that are meant to elicit diverse solution pathways [PAGE 4]. If telemetry collapses onto a single dominant sequence, the design's counter-trap has failed: players have solved the intended puzzle space by ignoring its degrees of freedom. Glyph's sequence graph detects this collapse directly: low inter-node distance and a single high-popularity rank-0 node indicate homogeneity [PAGE 5] [PAGE 7]. The researcher's theory of mind must therefore model the designer's intent (diversity) against the player's actual behavior (convergence), and the tool makes the mismatch visible.

Clock: Although Wuzzit Trouble is not explicitly timed, scoring rewards the smallest number of cog rotations [PAGE 4], imposing an efficiency clock on every move. Similarly, the analyst operates under a cognitive clock: comparing ten traces simultaneously "is not a trivial task" [PAGE 3], and the dual-view synchronized highlighting is designed to compress that comparison into immediate feedback [PAGE 2]. Under time pressure, both player and analyst are subject to the same attentional-load constraints described by Lavie [16] and Sarter et al. [25]; Glyph's design is essentially a prosthetic working memory that offloads cross-view integration onto the visualization rather than the human operator.
