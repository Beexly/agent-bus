Assumptions: causal, linear, no unmeasured confounding after fixed effects + IV. Fixed effects \(\alpha_j\) time-invariant.

Simultaneity:
\(Y_{ij}=\alpha_j+\beta Y_{ik}+\varepsilon_{ij}\)
\(Y_{ik}=\alpha_k+\beta Y_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ik}\)
Substitution:
\((1-\beta^2)Y_{ik}=\alpha_k+\beta(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})+\varepsilon_{ik}\)
so \(Y_{ik}=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^2}(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})+\frac{1}{1-\beta^2}(\alpha_k+\varepsilon_{ik})\).
Singularity: \(1-\beta^2=0\) at \(\beta=\pm 1\). If \(|\beta|\to1\), reduced form diverges; if \(|\beta|>1\), unstable mutual amplification. Also if \(\beta=0\), no reflection. This is formal. <THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s causal claim is narrow but consequential: exposure to toxic language in *Call of Duty: Modern Warfare III* is said to change two outcomes—time to enter the next match, measured in hours, and the probability that the exposed player uses toxic language in the current match, measured as a probability or percentage-point shift. The abstract states that “exposure to toxic language significantly affects player engagement and the probability that players use similar language” [PAGE 1]. The analysis is an instrumental-variables/2SLS design using ToxMod-detected toxicity, with fixed effects for players and leave-one-out prior-match toxicity as the instrument [PAGE 8, PAGE 11]. The central multi-hop derivation is: OLS is biased because exposure and outcome are jointly determined by unobserved match events, enduring player relationships, and mutual toxicity; therefore the authors instrument current exposure counts with other players’ prior toxicity propensities in matches not involving the focal player; the first stage is strong; the reduced form is interpreted as a local average treatment effect for compliers [PAGE 10, PAGE 11, PAGE 13].

The gray-zone audit begins with the reflection problem. The paper formally shows that if two players \(j\) and \(k\) mutually influence each other, then substituting one equation into the other makes \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) enter \(Y_{ik}\), so OLS captures both the teammate’s effect and its “reflection” [PAGE 10]. That derivation is formal and correct in its own illustrative setup, but the empirical model is linear in counts and interactions, and the binary outcome is modeled as a linear probability. That creates boundary problems: the same-party propagation estimate is reported as a “59.93 to 69.48 percentage point increase” [PAGE 19], while the paper also reports that “the observed incidence of toxic language is 0.083%” [PAGE 18]. A 60–69 percentage-point marginal effect from one same-party teammate is enormous relative to a 0.083% baseline and is a serious extrapolation warning. The table coefficient is \(0.6949\) with standard error \(0.0785\) [PAGE 23], so the estimate is precise in the model, but its substantive scale suggests either a highly local complier effect, severe same-party selection, or a linear-probability breakdown. That is a gray-zone veto candidate.

A second gray zone is conditioning on match outcome. The structural model includes interactions between exposure counts and whether the player’s team won or lost [PAGE 8]. But win/loss may be post-treatment: toxicity could affect team performance, and then conditioning on win/loss blocks or distorts part of the causal effect. The paper does not resolve this. A third gray zone is missing exposure data: “Data on which players were exposed is unavailable for 34.6% of the statements identified as containing toxic language” [PAGE 4], and the daily share “fluctuated arbitrarily between five and 73%” [PAGE 4–5]. The authors argue the missingness is probably random and show proportional preservation across contexts [PAGE 8], but that is supporting evidence, not a proof of missing-at-random. A fourth gray zone is time-to-next-match censoring: the observation window ends December 10, 2023 [PAGE 3–4], so long returns after the window are unobserved; the paper does not present a survival or censoring model. A fifth is textual/table inconsistency: the paper says same-party toxicity “does not significantly affect the time it takes to enter the next match” [PAGE 19], but the appendix reports Same Party \(12.255^{*}\) and Same Party × Win \(-18.705^{**}\) [PAGE 23], producing a negative point estimate of about \(-6.46\) hours after a win [PAGE 17]. These are not fatal alone, but they define the audit boundary: the estimates are credible only as LATEs under exclusion, linearity, missing-at-random exposure, and a well-defined win/loss conditioning strategy.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Structural operator.** The paper specifies
\[
y_{ij}=\alpha_j+\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot \boldsymbol{x}_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ij},
\]
where \(i\) indexes match, \(j\) indexes player, \(\alpha_j\) is a player-specific intercept/fixed effect, \(\boldsymbol{x}_{ij}\) contains the number of other players who used toxic language to which player \(j\) was exposed, and \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) is an error term; the vector also contains interactions with win/loss [PAGE 8]. For the engagement outcome \(y_{ij}^{(T)}\), units are hours until the next match; for the propagation outcome \(y_{ij}^{(P)}\), units are probability in \([0,1]\), reported in percentage points in figures [PAGE 16–17, PAGE 23]. The coefficient \(\beta\) therefore has units of hours per additional toxic-exposing player for engagement, and probability per additional toxic-exposing player for propagation.

**Simultaneity/reflection operator.** The illustrative two-player system is
\[
Y_{ij}=\alpha_j+\beta Y_{ik}+\varepsilon_{ij},\qquad
Y_{ik}=\alpha_k+\beta Y_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ik}.
\]
Substitution gives
\[
(1-\beta^2)Y_{ik}=\alpha_k+\beta(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})+\varepsilon_{ik},
\]
so
\[
Y_{ik}=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta^2}(\alpha_j+\varepsilon_{ij})
+\frac{1}{1-\beta^2}(\alpha_k+\varepsilon_{ik}).
\]
This is the paper’s formal demonstration that \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) enters \(Y_{ik}\) and creates correlation between the endogenous regressor and the structural error [PAGE 10]. The exact singularity regime is \(1-\beta^2=0\), i.e. \(\beta=\pm1\). As \(|\beta|\to1\), the reduced-form mapping diverges; for \(|\beta|>1\), mutual amplification is unstable. At \(\beta=0\), the reflection channel vanishes. This singularity applies to the illustrative binary simultaneity equation, not directly to the hours-per-count structural coefficient.

**Instrument operator.** The first stage is
\[
x_{ij}=\delta_j+\gamma\times
\sum_{k\in P_{i,-j}}\sum_{\ell\in M_{i,k,-j}}
\frac{x^{\star}_{\ell k}}{\#M_{i,k,-j}}
+u_{ij},
\]
where \(P_{i,-j}\) is the set of other players in match \(i\), \(M_{i,k,-j}\) is the set of prior matches in which player \(k\) but not player \(j\) participated, and \(x^{\star}_{\ell k}\in\{0,1\}\) indicates whether player \(k\) used toxic language in prior match \(\ell\) [PAGE 11]. The inner term is an empirical prior-match probability, dimensionless; the sum over \(k\) is an expected count of toxic-exposing others. Relevance is checked by first-stage \(F\) statistics; the paper states that “the \(F\) statistic ... should have a value greater than ten” and reports values from 365.2 to 3159.9 in one panel and 505.1 to 2051.4 in another [PAGE 11–12], with later appendix \(F\) values of 8,958.2 and 504.1, and 12,551.2 and 8,454.4 [PAGE 23]. Exclusion is not testable: “the validity of the exclusion restriction cannot be empirically tested” [PAGE 11]. The IV estimand is
\[
\beta_{\mathrm{IV}}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{ij},y_{ij})}{\operatorname{Cov}(Z_{ij},x_{ij})},
\]
interpreted under monotonicity and exclusion as a LATE for “compliers,” defined by the paper as players “who were exposed to toxicity because they interacted with other players who were more likely to use toxic language in previous matches with other players” [PAGE 13].

**Reported operators and magnitudes.** For opponents and teammates, time effects are 60.683 hours for opponent-loss, 16.182 for teammate-loss, with opponent-win total \(60.683-36.596=24.087\) hours and teammate-win total \(16.182+0.8545=17.0365\) hours [PAGE 23]. Propagation effects are 0.1382 for opponent-loss, 0.0854 for teammate-loss, opponent-win total \(0.1382-0.0832=0.0550\), teammate-win total \(0.0854-0.0074=0.0780\) [PAGE 23], i.e. 13.82, 8.54, 5.50, and 7.80 percentage points. For different-party and same-party teammates, time effects are 17.936 and 12.255 hours, with win totals 23.036 and \(-6.450\) hours; propagation effects are 0.0208 and 0.6949, with win totals 0.0163 and 0.5993 [PAGE 23], i.e. 2.08, 69.49, 1.63, and 59.93 percentage points. The paper summarizes the same-party propagation as a “59.93 to 69.48 percentage point increase” [PAGE 19] against an observed toxic-use incidence of “0.083%” [PAGE 18]. Breakdown regimes are therefore: weak instruments if \(F\le10\) (not observed here); exclusion failure if prior toxicity propensity correlates with unobserved player traits, skill, schedule, or social ties; missing-exposure MNAR; linear-probability boundary failure near \(P(y=1)\approx0.00083\); and post-treatment conditioning on win/loss. The priority ordering for engagement places “Toxic language from opponents when the player’s team loses” first, while propagation places “Toxic language from teammates in the same party when the player’s team loses” first [PAGE 20].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs backward through three scientific traditions and forward into several multi-week traps. First, the peer-effects lineage: the introduction cites Manski, Sacerdote, Graham, bullying, delinquency, and adolescent peer studies to justify the expectation that toxicity propagates [PAGE 2, PAGE 22]. The reflection problem is the classic Manski problem, and the paper explicitly formalizes mutual determination with a two-player system [PAGE 10]. Second, the IV lineage: the instrument is described as belonging “to the general class of spatial or ‘leave-one-out’ instruments pioneered in empirical industrial organization for demand and supply estimation (Hausman 1996; Nevo 2001)” [PAGE 11, PAGE 22]. That genealogy matters because leave-one-out instruments are powerful when the leave-one-out mean is plausibly exogenous, but fragile when it encodes stable group-level traits. Third, the platform-moderation lineage: Activision partnered with Modulate, whose ToxMod technology detects toxic language in real time; beta rollout began August 30, 2023, and global release coincided with *Modern Warfare III* on November 10, 2023 [PAGE 3]. The data window is the first month after launch, November 10 to December 10, 2023 [PAGE 3–4], with later March 4–April 12, 2024 validation data for a randomly drawn subset [PAGE 7–8].

The multi-week traps follow directly. Trap one is launch-window exceptionalism: the first month of a new title has atypical engagement, learning, server load, and moderation novelty; a one-month LATE may not generalize to steady-state play. Trap two is moderation adaptation: once players learn what ToxMod detects, language and exposure patterns change, and missing exposure data after November 17 complicates longitudinal comparison [PAGE 4–5]. Trap three is instrument aging: early in the window players have short prior-match histories, so the leave-one-out instrument is sparse and noisy; later it accumulates history, changing the first-stage composition over time. Trap four is cumulative exposure: the model estimates a marginal effect of additional toxic-exposing players in the current match, not the cumulative effect of repeated exposure across weeks. Trap five is party persistence: same-party relationships are chosen and durable, so same-party toxicity may reflect relationship history rather than a one-match exposure effect. The paper’s validation comparison to March–April 2024 is useful, but it is a proportional-preservation check, not a full multi-week causal replication [PAGE 8].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The behavioral mechanism is recursive: players form beliefs about others’ toxicity, about whether retaliation will be rewarded or punished, and about whether a teammate is a safe or unsafe audience. Three modes organize the interpretation. **Crisis habit regression** is the prepotent response under toxic stress: exposed players fall back on habitual retaliation or same-party banter, producing propagation. This fits the finding that same-party toxicity has the largest propagation estimate, 59.93–69.48 percentage points [PAGE 19], because same-party teammates share expectations and lower the social cost of toxic speech. **Counter-trap** is anticipatory avoidance: players may mute or disable voice chat, avoid parties, or provoke toxicity to elicit reactions. The paper explicitly limits interpretation to compliers and excludes players “who seek to alter their exposure to toxic language by intentionally disabling the audio chat to evade it or using toxic language to provoke reactions from other players” [PAGE 13]. That exclusion is not a nuisance; it is a theory-of-mind boundary: the treatment effect is defined away from strategic avoidance. **Clock** is the outcome definition itself: time to next match mixes session termination with return latency. The paper notes that a player who returns the same hour next day yields 24 hours, while immediate re-entry yields near zero [PAGE 9]. Thus a 60.68-hour opponent-loss effect [PAGE 16] is not simply “engagement loss”; it is a shift in the session-exit and return clock.

Recursive beliefs also explain the asymmetry across contexts. Opponent toxicity after a loss produces the largest engagement delay, 60.68 hours [PAGE 16], consistent with a status threat plus hostile out-group signal. Opponent toxicity after a win produces a smaller 24.09-hour delay, and teammate toxicity produces 16.18–17.04 hours [PAGE 16]. For propagation, opponent-loss is 13.81 p.p., teammate-loss 8.54 p.p., opponent-win 5.49 p.p., teammate-win 7.80 p.p. [PAGE 16]. The same-party propagation result is the recursive-toxicity extreme: mutual knowledge and low accountability make toxic speech a coordinated habit. The counter-trap caution is that players who anticipate toxicity may exit voice entirely, so the observed LATE may understate avoidance effects and overstate the effect among non-avoiders.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto question is whether physical, biomechanical, or weather layers can invalidate the social/software-layer causal claim. The paper provides no direct biometric, headset, network, geolocation, or meteorological controls. Therefore no strong “proof” can be asserted for those layers from the supplied text. The physical layer—headset audio, voice codec, platform party chat, network latency—can veto exposure measurement because “exposure data is unavailable for some ToxMod offenses” [PAGE 4], and the daily missing share ranges from 5% to 73% [PAGE 4–5]. If technical failures cluster around high-intensity matches or particular outcomes, the IV estimates could be biased. The paper’s proportional-preservation evidence in Figures 2 and 3 [PAGE 8] is a reasonable audit but not a formal missing-at-random test.

The biomechanical layer—speech production, fatigue, arousal, reaction time—could confound because toxicity requires vocalization and because fatigue may simultaneously shorten sessions and increase irritability. The paper does not measure these states; the player fixed effects absorb only time-invariant propensity [PAGE 8, PAGE 13–14]. The weather layer is likewise unmeasured; date-window variation could proxy for weather, holidays, school schedules, or server load. Large sample size does not prove weather randomness because the sample is a single one-month launch window and a later validation window.

The strongest cross-layer veto is not physical or meteorological but game-performance and measurement: win/loss is likely post-treatment. The model includes “interactions between the number of other players using toxic language in a match and whether player \(j\)’s team has won or lost the game” [PAGE 8]. If toxicity affects team performance, then conditioning on win/loss is a post-treatment stratifier and can distort the very effect being estimated. The second strongest veto is missing exposure data: 34.6% of toxic statements lack exposure data [PAGE 4]. The paper’s own caveat that missingness “will not introduce any bias” only if “randomly unavailable” [PAGE 4] is conditional, not proven. Adjudication: the physical/biomechanical/weather layers do not supply a positive alternative explanation for the full 60-hour engagement shifts, but they are not ruled out as confounders. The causal claim survives only at the social/software layer and only conditional on missing-at-random exposure, valid exclusion, and a defensible win/loss conditioning strategy.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto scenario 1—future-instrument placebo.** Construct a placebo instrument \(Z^{\text{future}}_{ij}\) from other players’ toxic language in matches after the focal match, again excluding matches shared with player \(j\). Under the paper’s exclusion logic, future toxicity cannot cause current outcomes except through stable player traits or shared social ties; therefore \(Z^{\text{future}}_{ij}\) should have no conditional predictive power for \(y_{ij}\) once current exposure and fixed effects are included. Prediction under the paper’s model: placebo coefficient near zero, and the true prior-match instrument remains strong. Prediction under exclusion failure: nonzero future-instrument coefficients, especially for same-party and opponent-loss contexts, because the instrument is picking up durable player reputation, skill, or party composition rather than exogenous exposure. A verifiable RLVR veto triggers if the future placebo predicts time-to-next-match or toxic-use probability at conventional significance.

**Veto scenario 2—randomized voice-audio mute.** Randomly assign a subset of players to have incoming voice chat muted for selected matches, with ToxMod still measuring outgoing toxicity and with exposure otherwise unchanged. Under the paper’s causal claim, reducing exposure should reduce the engagement delay and the propagation probability by magnitudes near the reported effects: roughly 16–61 hours for time and 5.5–13.8 percentage points for opponent/teammate propagation, with larger same-party propagation if the 59.93–69.48 p.p. estimate is causal [PAGE 16–19, PAGE 23]. Prediction under the paper’s model: muted players show shorter next-match delays after toxic matches and lower toxic-use probability. Prediction under failure: muted players show no meaningful change, or the same-party effect collapses, indicating that the observational estimates are driven by selection into parties, match quality, or linear-probability extrapolation. A verifiable veto triggers if the randomized mute effect is null or directionally opposite.

**Veto scenario 3—random party composition with matched toxicity histories.** Randomly assign players with matched prior toxicity propensities to same-party versus different-party teammate conditions, holding match outcome and opponent toxicity fixed. Under the paper’s causal claim, a same-party toxic teammate should increase the focal player’s toxic-use probability by approximately 60–69 percentage points relative to a different-party toxic teammate in the loss condition, and by approximately 60 percentage points in the win condition [PAGE 19, PAGE 23]. Prediction under the paper’s model: large same-party propagation difference and a smaller, possibly negative, same-party time effect. Prediction under failure: the same-party propagation advantage disappears or shrinks toward the 1.63–2.08 p.p. different-party estimates, showing that the observational same-party result is a selection or collusion artifact. A verifiable veto triggers if randomized same-party assignment does not reproduce a large propagation gap or if it reproduces the gap only among pre-existing parties.
