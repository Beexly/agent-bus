The 9-frame window places the central frame \(m\) with four neighbors on each side [PAGE 4], so the feature for frame \(m\) uses four future frames. That is acceptable for post hoc summarization, but the paper also claims the method is “computationally efficient for real-time sports video analysis” [PAGE 8]. In a live setting, a causal decision for frame \(m\) cannot use four future frames unless a buffer delay is accepted. The abstract says “The prior and later frames around these keyframes are appended to produce meaningful video skims” [PAGE 1], which is explicitly noncausal. The recursive clock trap is that the system’s real-time claim and its lookahead window are in tension. The author’s model of the viewer wants smooth skims with prior and later context; the reviewer’s model of the system wants bounded latency; the editor’s model wants no missed events. These goals conflict. A counter-trap is to report the exact frame duration, buffer size, and end-to-end latency; the source does not. A second counter-trap is to evaluate the classifier with a strictly causal window, then compare to the centered window. A third is to calibrate the SVM output so that high-confidence false positives during crowd noise can be gated by a second modality or by a human editor. The paper’s own conclusion says false positives “can be better tackled by analyzing the visual stream as well” [PAGE 8], which is the honest recursive update: the audio-only agent model is incomplete, and the next iteration must either accept multimodal cost or accept a bounded error regime.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto begins with a formal identifiability condition. Let \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) be the latent event label and \(X\) the audio observation. An audio-only classifier is universally sufficient only if the conditional entropy \(H(Y\mid X)=0\) over the target domain, or equivalently if the mutual information \(I(Y;X)=H(Y)\) in bits. The paper reports empirical accuracy, not conditional entropy, and its own false-positive admission shows \(H(Y\mid X)>0\) in at least one regime: “the system may sometimes misclassify non-exciting segments with high crowd noise or commentary cheering as key events” [PAGE 8]. Therefore the universal robustness claim is vetoed. The physical layer provides a simple counterexample: if a non-exciting crowd cheer and an exciting crowd roar produce the same 20-D histogram, no SVM can separate them. The feature map is not injective with respect to the label. Microphone automatic gain control, clipping, YouTube transcoding, and loudness normalization can map distinct physical events to similar amplitudes. The source does not report loudness normalization or codec stratification, although it uses YouTube videos [PAGE 6]. The biomechanical layer adds another veto: crowd noise is generated by many vocal tracts, hands, feet, and stadium reflections; “excitement” is a psychological label, not a biomechanical invariant. Commentator tone is also a professional performance shaped by broadcaster style, language, and editorial delay. The paper treats “audience reactions, commentator tone, and excitement levels” as acoustic traits [PAGE 5], but it does not provide a biomechanical or perceptual calibration between vocal effort and event importance. The weather layer is relevant because the paper explicitly targets “outdoor environments with massive background noise” [PAGE 4]. Rain, wind, open-air stadium geometry, and day-night temperature gradients alter the noise spectrum. The dataset includes “daylight matches and night games” [PAGE 6], but the paper does not stratify results by weather, humidity, wind, or stadium type. A cross-layer veto proof can be stated as a counterexample construction: choose two clips with equal TMAD-LTP descriptors but opposite labels, one being a non-exciting segment with loud spectator cheering and one being an exciting segment with similar cheering. If the descriptors are equal, the Bayes-optimal audio-only classifier cannot exceed chance on that pair. The paper’s own “camera focus on spectators” false positive [PAGE 8] is an existence proof of such a pair in practice. The veto is not that the method is useless; it is that the claim of robustness across “background noise, broadcasters, editing effects, variations in camera angle, field colors, illumination conditions, and shot types” [PAGE 3] is too broad for an audio-only feature. The correct adjudication is to scope the claim to the tested distribution and to add a visual or event-metadata veto for high-crowd non-events. A second formal veto applies to the SVM decision: if the feature distribution shifts such that the kernel matrix becomes ill-conditioned or the margin hyperplane falls outside the convex hull of test features, the classifier can produce high-confidence errors. The paper reports no calibration curve, no confusion matrix by tournament, and no out-of-distribution detector. Thus the cross-layer proof supports a bounded, empirically tested audio-only summary system, not a universal sports-event detector.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
