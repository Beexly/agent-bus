This summation implies that when both stimuli are presented together, their excitatory potentials summate to produce a stronger response than either alone—a foundational concept for understanding how compound stimuli drive behavior more robustly than isolated cues.

Generalization gradients then play a critical role: Hull posits that the potential for each component shrinks by 25% when considered individually within the compound context—$s_1E_R$ reduces to 30 wats and $s_2E_R$ to 45 wats [PAGE 360]. This reduction reflects the spread of excitation from the trained compound to similar but non-identical stimuli; the gradient effect ensures that while the compound itself remains highly effective, its constituent parts, when presented alone, elicit weaker responses due to the loss of synergistic summation. The gray zone emerges during differential reinforcement phases. When the compound is reinforced but individual components are not, experimental extinction ($I_k$) begins to form for the alone presentations. Simultaneously, conditioned inhibition ($sIr$) develops, which can generalize back onto the compound presentation. This creates a complex interplay: the excitatory potentials from summation compete with inhibitory potentials from extinction and generalization. The net outcome determines whether the subject exhibits true patterning—responding strongly to the compound but weakly to individuals—or falls into a trap where residual excitation from one component masks the inhibition from the other.

This illustrates the fragility of patterning and the need for precise control over generalization gradients to avoid confounding effects.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
All equations and principles are strictly adhered to Hull's formalisms as presented in *Principles of Behavior* (1943). The base unit of measurement is the **wat** (watt), representing electrochemical energy potential in the neural system [PAGE 360]. 

**Core Equations & Operators:**

1.  **Physiological Summation (Excitatory):** For a compound stimulus $s_r + s_s$, the combined excitatory potential driving reaction $R$ is the sum of individual potentials, adjusted for afferent interaction:
    $$s_{r+s}E_R = s_rE_R + s_sE_R - \text{interaction term}$$
    In the specific example on p. 360, with $s_1E_R = 40$ wats and $s_2E_R = 60$ wats, the resulting compound potential is given as 76 wats, indicating a sub-additive interaction (summation less than simple arithmetic sum):
    $$s_{1+2}E_R = 76 \text{ wats} \quad (\text{from } 40 + 60 = 100 \rightarrow 76)$$
    *[Citation: PAGE 360, "yields... sr+s ER of 76 wats"]*

2.  **Generalization Gradient Decay:** The excitatory potential of an individual component when tested alone after training with the compound is reduced due to generalization from the trained compound. Hull specifies a 25% shrinkage:
    $$s_iE_{R,\text{alone}} = s_iE_{R,\text{trained}} \times (1 - g)$$
    where $g = 0.25$. Thus:
    $$s_1E_{R,\text{alone}} = 40 \times 0.75 = 30 \text{ wats}$$
    $$s_2E_{R,\text{alone}} = 60 \times 0.75 = 45 \text{ wats}$$
    *[Citation: PAGE 360, "shrinks 25 per cent from a value of 40 to one of 30 and ... from 60 to 45"]*

3.  **Reaction Threshold Condition:** Reaction $R$ is evoked only if the net excitatory potential exceeds a fixed threshold $\theta$:
    $$\text{If } sE_R > \theta \Rightarrow R \text{ occurs}; \quad \text{Else } R \text{ is suppressed}.$$
    Here, $\theta = 10$ wats [PAGE 360].

4.  **Experimental Extinction Potential ($I_k$):** Generated by non-reinforcement. Its magnitude increases with each non-reinforced trial, opposing excitation. While not given a formula on this page, its generation is noted as a key complication in patterning *[Citation: PAGE 360, "generation of experimental extinction (Ik)"]*.

5.  **Conditioned Inhibition ($sIr$):** An inhibitory potential developed through associative learning, often accompanying extinction. It can generalize to related stimuli *[Citation: PAGE 360, "conditioned inhibition (sIr) and the generalization of a portion of this back upon the positive... phase"]*.

**Breakdown Regimes & Singularity Points:**
*   **Threshold Singularity:** At exactly 10 wats, the system is bistable; infinitesimal changes could tip it toward or away from response emission.
*   **Gradient Overlap Singularity:** If two stimuli' generalization gradients overlap significantly, their individual potentials may not shrink proportionally, violating the 25% assumption and distorting patterning ratios.
*   **Summation Breakdown:** If afferent interaction becomes strongly inhibitory rather than facilitatory, $s_{r+s}E_R$ could drop below the sum of parts, potentially even below $\theta$, causing failure to respond to the compound—the exact opposite of positive patterning.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Hull's derivation of positive patterning sits at the intersection of several historical currents in physiology and psychology. The concept of **physiological summation** directly inherits from Ewald Hering's work on sensory interactions and Ivan Pavlov's observations on temporal and spatial summation in conditioning. Pavlov's finding that two weak stimuli presented together could elicit a conditioned response where neither alone could provided the empirical bedrock for Hull's energetic summation postulate.

The notion of **generalization gradients** traces back to Robert Yerkes' early 20th-century work on wavelength discrimination in animals, later formalized by Garcia and others in the mid-20th century. However, Hull uniquely quantified it as a percentage "shrinkage" of excitatory potential, linking it directly to his energy metric (wats). This allowed him to predict precise changes in responding based on stimulus similarity.

**Conditioned inhibition**, crucial for explaining the suppression of responses to individual components in patterning paradigms, was pioneered by Pavlov but remained controversial until the 1930s–40s when researchers like Kamin and Spence began exploring its systematic properties. Hull integrated it as a learned potential ($sIr$) that could develop independently of excitation and generalize across stimuli, adding a layer of complexity to his purely excitatory early models.

The **multi-week trap** referenced in the prompt finds its lineage in the phenomenon of **"spontaneous recovery"** and **"reinstatement"** observed in extinction studies. Early experiments by Pavlov showed that extinguished responses could reappear after a delay, suggesting that extinction did not erase the original association but created a new inhibitory one. In patterning tasks, this means that after weeks of differential reinforcement, the inhibition built against S1-alone might spontaneously recover and interfere with S1's contribution to the compound, or renewed reinforcement of the compound could reactivate latent excitation for S1-alone, disrupting the clean separation required for patterning. This historical awareness warns against interpreting short-term patterning success as stable long-term learning.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When facing a complex patterning task with ambiguous cues, subjects do not simply compute stimulus energies—they engage in recursive hypothesis testing about reward contingencies. Consider a rat in a chamber where light+tone predicts food, but light-alone or tone-alone do not. Initially, driven by high excitatory potentials (40/60 wats), the rat responds vigorously to both components when alone. After repeated non-reinforcement, it must infer a higher-order rule: “only the combination matters.”

This requires a shift from **cue-based responding** (“if I see light, run”) to **relational reasoning** (“light AND tone together mean food”). Neurocomputational models suggest this involves prefrontal cortex circuits suppressing dominant cue-response associations in favor of conjunctive representations. A crisis habit regression occurs if stress or novelty reinstates the simpler, older strategy—e.g., under fear arousal, the rat might revert to responding to the most salient cue (say, tone) regardless of context, overriding the learned pattern.

A **counter-trap** could be designed by introducing a third stimulus (e.g., a click) that is always reinforced alone. This would force the subject to maintain both simple (click→food) and complex (light+tone→food) rules simultaneously, strengthening relational processing and making the system more robust to interference.

The **internal clock**—likely mediated by striatal beat-frequency oscillators—plays a subtle but vital role. If the interval between stimulus onset and reinforcement varies unpredictably, the subject cannot rely on timing cues to disambiguate patterns. Conversely, a consistent temporal structure allows the brain to use elapsed time as an additional feature, sharpening the distinction between compound and single-stimulus trials. Thus, patterning performance is not just a function of spatial/configural cue analysis but also of temporal precision.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even if all cognitive and energetic conditions for positive patterning are met, lower-level systems can veto expression.

**Biomechanical Veto:** The reaction $R$ in Hull's model is often a lever press or runway sprint. If the subject has muscular fatigue or injury—for instance, a sprained forelimb—the biomechanical cost of executing $R$ may exceed the expected reward value, even if $sE_R > \theta$. The animal may exhibit "response deprivation," refusing to perform $R$ despite strong motivational drive. This overrides Hull’s threshold logic because the motor program itself is compromised.

**Weather/Environmental Veto:** Temperature extremes can alter neuromuscular efficiency. In cold conditions, synaptic transmission slows, potentially reducing the rate of rise of $E_R$ and causing it to fail to reach threshold within the decision window. High humidity might increase static electricity in apparatuses, inadvertently reinforcing accidental contacts and creating noisy data that masks true patterning effects.

**Pharmacological Veto:** Administration of dopamine antagonists (e.g., haloperidol) selectively impairs the ability to learn configural rules like those required for patterning, while sparing elemental learning. Such drugs reduce the signal-to-noise ratio in prefrontal-striatal pathways, effectively lowering the "effective" excitatory potential below threshold for compound stimuli specifically, leading to a false negative in patterning tests.

These vetoes demonstrate that Hull’s elegant energy calculus operates atop a fragile biological substrate; any disruption at the physiological level can invalidate theoretical predictions, regardless of psychological preparedness.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

During differential reinforcement, $s_1$-alone may continue eliciting responses despite non-reinforcement because its potential stays above threshold, preventing clean patterning.
