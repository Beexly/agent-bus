The page situates Perin's gradient within a mid-century behaviorist lineage descending from Watson, through Warden and Haas (13), and forward to Wolfe (Figure 31) and Hamilton. Watson's null finding and Warden and Haas's replication [PAGE 140] established an early empirical boundary: delay of reinforcement, by itself, did not reliably produce a gradient. Hull's reconciliation imports the secondary reinforcement principle from Chapter VII (p. 97), which functions as a retrocausal bridge: a stimulus present before primary reinforcement can, after repeated pairings, exert control over behavior as if it had been reinforcing all along. This makes the gradient context-dependent rather than a universal law of primary reward.

Multi-week trap: In extended training over weeks, an animal in a food chamber accumulates a stable bank of secondary reinforcers (cues, context, experimenter presence). If the experimental protocol then shifts the delay to a separate compartment without re-establishing those cues, the secondary bank does not transfer immediately; the gradient can collapse abruptly across sessions, producing a late-phase extinction that looks like a failure of learning rather than a context change. Conversely, enriching a sterile compartment with secondary cues can resurrect a gradient even at long delays. The trap is to infer a fixed physiological "reinforcement gradient" from single-session curves while ignoring the multi-week accumulation and decay of secondary reinforcement traces.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock:

Crisis habit regression: As Δt approaches the extrapolated zero (34–44 seconds) [PAGE 140], subjects regress from instrumental bar pressing to the default posture described on [PAGE 140]: "the animals sat quietly by the food cup after the withdrawal of the bar, and simply waited for the food to be delivered." This is a habit regression from goal-directed operant behavior to passive consummatory waiting, triggered when the delay renders the contingency ineffective.

Counter-trap: The experimenter faces an interpretive counter-trap. Cessation of bar pressing in the 30-second group [PAGE 140] can be misread as "no learning occurred" or "the animal cannot learn at this delay," when the more parsimonious reading is that the response was extinguished by delay and the subject reverted to waiting. Interventions that merely increase reward magnitude without reducing delay or adding secondary cues will fail because the behavioral layer has already vetoed the operant.

Clock: The system behaves like a delay clock with a critical phase near 34–44 seconds. Small parameter perturbations around 30 seconds [PAGE 140] produce discontinuous changes—active responding versus quiescent waiting—that smooth curve-fitting to Figure 32 may conceal. The clock is not perfectly sharp because individual animals cease after "varying numbers of trials," indicating a distributional threshold rather than a single hard cutoff.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical / biomechanical / weather veto proof:

The behavioral output measured in Figure 32 is gated by multiple layers. The motivational layer computes a gradient G_eff(Δt); the motor layer must execute the bar press; the situational layer determines whether secondary reinforcement cues are present.

Motor veto: [PAGE 140] reports that animals "ceased to operate the bar" and "sat quietly." This is a motor-layer veto: regardless of any residual motivational gradient, no bar press is emitted, so no learning curve can be plotted. A null gradient cannot be distinguished from motor extinction on this page alone.

Biomechanical note: Bar pressing requires discrete limb deformation and contact force; if the animal is sitting quietly, the biomechanical plant is idle. No weather variables apply because the experiment is conducted in a controlled laboratory chamber; therefore no meteorological veto is possible.

Adjudication rule: A reported absence of gradient is admissible only if motor responding is confirmed across the full delay range. In the 30-second group, the motor veto invalidates inclusion in Perin's gradient [PAGE 140], so the apparent descending gradient is conditioned on a survivorship bias toward animals that did not regress to waiting.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios:

Veto 1 — Sub-critical delay in sterile compartment. Set Δt = 24 seconds (below the 34-second / 44-second extrapolated intercept [PAGE 140]) in a separate non-feeding compartment. Prediction: bar pressing continues and a measurable gradient exists. Veto condition: if animals still cease pressing and no gradient can be plotted, the claim that the 34–44 second intercept is the primary-delay horizon is falsified; another factor (e.g., compartment novelty) dominates.

Veto 2 — Secondary-cue enrichment at long delay. In the food chamber, pair an explicit light/tone with pellet delivery and impose Δt = 50 seconds (well beyond the intercept). Prediction: secondary reinforcement sustains a nonzero gradient because η > 0 in G_eff. Veto condition: if the gradient collapses to zero at 50 seconds despite explicit secondary cues, the secondary-reinforcement bridge proposed on [PAGE 140] cannot account for the Watson/Wolfe divergence.

Veto 3 — Response-contingent secondary bridge for the 30-second group. Run the 30-second group [PAGE 140] with an immediate secondary reinforcer (e.g., brief tone) contingent on each bar press, followed by the delayed 30-second pellet. Prediction: if secondary reinforcement bridges the gap, pressing persists and the animals can be included in the gradient plot. Veto condition: if animals still cease pressing after varying numbers of trials despite the secondary bridge, the attrition is attributable to primary-delay extinction or satiation independent of secondary cues, falsifying a purely context-based explanation for the 30-second group's exclusion.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
