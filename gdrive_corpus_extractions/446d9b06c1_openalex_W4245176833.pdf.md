Standard reconstruction, with assumptions and units:

1. Extended signal space. Let \(u:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R^m\) and \(y:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R^p\). For \(T\ge 0\), define the truncation \(u_T(t)=u(t)\) for \(t\le T\), and \(u_T(t)=0\) otherwise. Then
\[
L_{2e}^m=\left\{u:\mathbb R_+\to\mathbb R^m:\ \|u_T\|_2&lt;\infty\ \text{for all }T&gt;0\right\},
\]
\[
\|u_T\|_2=\left(\int_0^T\|u(t)\|_2^2\,dt\right)^{1/2}.
\]
If \(u\) has unit \([U]\), then \(\|u_T\|_2\) has unit \([U]\,s^{1/2}\). Assumption: signals are measurable and locally square-integrable. Breakdown: if the signal is not in \(L_{2e}\), the finite-horizon norm may be infinite and the gain inequality is not testable without a different signal class.

2. Input-output operator and finite L2 gain. Let
\[
\Sigma:L_{2e}^m\to L_{2e}^p
\]
be a causal input-output map. Finite L2 gain means there exist constants \(\gamma\ge 0\) and \(\beta\ge 0\) such that
\[
\|(\Sigma u)_T\|_2\le \gamma\|u_T\|_2+\beta
\quad\text{for all }u\in L_{2e}^m,\ T\ge 0.
\]
For zero initial state one often takes \(\beta=0\); otherwise \(\beta\) absorbs initial-condition or bias energy. Units: if input and output have the same unit, \(\gamma\) is dimensionless; if not, \([\gamma]=[Y]/[U]\). This is the operator summarized by the TOC entry “Lq-stability and Lq-gain; closed-loop stability” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: \(\gamma\) may be infinite for unbounded-memory or non-dissipative maps; a finite-horizon bound does not imply a uniform infinite-horizon bound unless \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) are independent of \(T\).

3. Dissipativity, storage, and supply rate. A supply rate is a function
\[
s:\mathbb R^m\times\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R,
\]
with units \([s]=[U][Y]\), i.e. power if \(u\) and \(y\) are effort and flow variables. A storage function is
\[
V:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R_+,
\]
with units \([V]=[U][Y]\,s\), i.e. energy. Dissipativity means
\[
V(x(T))-V(x(0))\le \int_0^T s(u(t),y(t))\,dt
\]
for all admissible trajectories and all \(T\ge 0\). If \(V\) is differentiable along trajectories, the differential form is
\[
\dot V(x(t))\le s(u(t),y(t))
\]
almost everywhere. This is the formal content behind “passivity, internal energy and supplied power” [PAGE 5] and the Chapter 3 entry “Dissipative systems” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: if \(V\) is not \(C^1\), the differential inequality must be replaced by a viscosity or semigroup formulation; if \(V(x(0))&gt;0\), the integrated inequality carries an initial-energy offset; if the supply-rate sign convention is reversed, the conclusion reverses.

4. Passivity as a supply-rate specialization. The passivity supply rate is
\[
s(u,y)=u^\top y,
\]
with units \([U][Y]\). Output-strict passivity may be written
\[
s(u,y)=u^\top y-\varepsilon\|y\|^2,
\quad \varepsilon\ge 0,
\]
or input-strict passivity with \(-\varepsilon\|u\|^2\). The TOC gives “The passivity theorem” [PAGE 8] and “Euler-Lagrange equations and passivity” [PAGE 8]. Assumption: the interconnection ports and power pairing are physically consistent. Breakdown: mere passivity of both subsystems is not, by itself, a strict contraction; the standard passivity theorem requires at least one strict passivity condition, plus well-posedness and usually detectability or observability for asymptotic claims. At \(\varepsilon=0\) for both subsystems, the strict margin vanishes and only marginal conclusions may remain.

5. L2-gain supply rate. A standard finite-gain supply rate is
\[
s_\gamma(u,y)=\gamma^2\|u\|^2-\|y\|^2,
\]
with \(\gamma\) dimensionless when \([U]=[Y]\), or \([\gamma]=[Y]/[U]\) otherwise. If there exists \(V\ge 0\) with
\[
V(x(T))-V(x(0))\le \int_0^T\left(\gamma^2\|u(t)\|^2-\|y(t)\|^2\right)dt,
\]
then
\[
\|y_T\|_2^2\le \gamma^2\|u_T\|_2^2+V(x(0)),
\]
which gives a biased finite-gain bound when \(V(x(0))\) is finite. This is the finite-gain property linked to passivity through the scattering transformation on [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: the implication is not an identity between passivity and finite gain; the scattering transformation changes the port variables and does not remove the need for a finite storage bound or a well-defined signal class.

6. Small-gain condition. For two stable subsystems with finite gains \(\gamma_1\) and \(\gamma_2\), the strict small-gain condition is
\[
\gamma_1\gamma_2\in[0,1).
\]
The product must be dimensionless. The TOC entry is “The small-gain theorem” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: at \(\gamma_1\gamma_2=1\) the strict contraction margin is zero; at \(\gamma_1\gamma_2&gt;1\) the standard small-gain conclusion does not follow. The theorem also requires compatible signal spaces, causality, and well-posed interconnection.

7. Hamilton-Jacobi inequality. For an affine state-space system
\[
\dot x=f(x)+g(x)u,\qquad y=h(x),
\]
with \(f(0)=0\) and \(h(0)=0\), a standard \(C^1\) storage candidate \(V\ge 0\), \(V(0)=0\), for L2-gain \(\gamma&gt;0\) satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi inequality
\[
\nabla V(x)^\top f(x)+\frac{1}{4\gamma^2}\|\nabla V(x)^\top g(x)\|^2+\|h(x)\|^2\le 0
\]
on an invariant domain \(\mathcal X\). Units must be made consistent by weighting: \(\nabla V^\top f\) has units of storage per time, and \(\|h\|^2\) must be weighted to the same energy-per-time scale. The preface calls this step “a main bottleneck” [PAGE 5] and says “in general explicit globally-defined solutions of these Hamilton-Jacobi inequalities are hard to obtain” [PAGE 5]. Chapter 7 is “devoted to checking (local) solvability of Hamilton-Jacobi inequalities and to the structure of their solution set” [PAGE 6].
