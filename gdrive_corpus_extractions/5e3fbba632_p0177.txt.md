This anticipates Tolman’s later cognitive-map theory (1948) and latent-learning experiments (Tolman & Honzik, 1930), in which rats develop usable representations without immediate reward. Retrocausal traps emerge when the experiment is extended across multi-week protocols. First, proactive interference: previously baited doors from prior days leave residual sign traces \(\sigma_{t-k}(d)\) that compete with the current \(\sigma_t(d)\), producing proactive decay not captured by a single-session \(\tau_c\). Second, experimenter expectancy and subtle cuing (Clever Hans effects): if the experimenter’s posture, breathing, or position correlates with baiting, the animal condenses the experimenter’s intention rather than the door’s spatial feature, inverting the causal direction of inference. Third, circadian and homeostatic drift: \(\tau_c\) and noise \(s(\tau)\) vary with time of day, hunger, and fatigue across weeks, so a fixed-delay criterion is not stationary. These multi-week traps mean that a null result at long delay may reflect procedural drift, not a cognitive capacity bound.
