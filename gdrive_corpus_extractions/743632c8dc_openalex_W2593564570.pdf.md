The source is a 2016 M.Sc. thesis that treats hockey win probability as a home-team probability \(p_i \in [0,1]\) estimated at event \(i\). The first estimator, WPS, is a hand-built rule system. The second, WPWL/WPL, is a data-mining classifier over labels. The third is a windowed stream-mining update. The core derivation is not a single model; it is a composition of score difference, elapsed time, shot difference, penalty difference, and miss difference.

First hop: score state. The thesis defines leadsize as \(LS_i = HS_i - VS_i\), where \(HS_i\) and \(VS_i\) are the home and visiting scores at event \(i\) [PAGE 27]. The equation is verbatim: “𝐿𝑆𝑖= 𝐻𝑆𝑖−𝑉𝑆𝑖” [PAGE 27]. Units are goals, an integer count difference. The base WPS map \(B(LS_i)\) is a lookup table: values from 1% at \(LS \le -4\) through 50% at \(LS=0\) to 99% at \(LS \ge 4\) [PAGE 39]. The thesis justifies the 55% initial value from historical home-team wins: “at the beginning of the game (at 0 seconds), the home team’s win probability is taken as 55%” [PAGE 28]. That is a prior, not a law; Table 1 on [PAGE 28] contains missing values, and the average shown there is 55.4% over the displayed seasons.

Second hop: time. The thesis assumes “the variable time improves the impact of variable leadsize. The less time remaining, the more influence of leadsize there is” [PAGE 33]. The WPS weight table is piecewise: \(w(t)=1\) for \(t \lt 1200\) s, \(1.1\) for \(1200 \le t \lt 2400\) s, \(1.2\) for \(2400 \le t \lt 3000\) s, and \(1.5\) for \(t \ge 3000\) s [PAGE 39]. But the prose on [PAGE 29] describes period 3 as \([2400,3600]\), while the table splits at 3000 s. That is a gray-zone inconsistency: the boundary at 3000 s is not derived from the period structure and creates a discontinuity inside period 3.

If \(H/V\) instead means the event team, then the processed-data column shown on [PAGE 37] does not obviously match that interpretation. This is a material audit item because a constant shift changes calibration.

WPS adds or subtracts probability-point-like increments: shot bins from \(-10\%\) to \(+10\%\), penalty bins from \(+10\%\) to \(-10\%\), miss bins from \(+5\%\) to \(-5\%\) [PAGE 39]. If these are absolute probability points, then \(p_i\) can leave \([0,1]\) unless clipped; the source does not state a clipping rule in the WPS steps. <RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from baseball win-probability work in the 1960s, through stochastic scoring models, into modern machine-learning classifiers and data-stream mining. The thesis cites Lindsey 1961 for baseball score progress and Stern 1994 for a Brownian-motion model of sports scores [PAGE 92]. It cites Buttrey, Washburn, and Price 2011 for NHL scoring rates, and Lock and Nettleton 2013 for random forests in NFL win probability [PAGE 92]. It also cites Hulten, Spencer, and Domingos 2001 for mining time-changing data streams [PAGE 97]. That lineage matters because the thesis combines three traditions: historical frequency tables, supervised classification, and incremental Hoeffding-style stream learning. The retrocausal effect is that later stream-mining methods are used to reinterpret earlier static tables: the 55% home prior and leadsize bins become initial labels for a windowed learner. But the thesis never resolves whether the table is a prior, a feature, or a label generator.

Multi-week traps are visible in the design. First, the WPL accuracy trap: a learner trained on labels generated by the same variables can reach near-perfect accuracy while learning nothing about outcomes. The thesis reports 99.99% for Random Tree on WPL [PAGE 69], but that is a consistency check of the hand formula, not a predictive validation. Second, the leakage trap: the test games 611, 727, and 757 are excluded from the 400,000-event training set [PAGE 54], but the WPS and WPL tables are summarized from season 2013-2014, which includes those games. The thesis says “we randomly choose three games’ data from season 2013-2014” and “in data mining model, the data for the other games ... in this season are used for training” [PAGE 37]. The exclusion is partial and not a clean temporal holdout. Third, the stationarity trap: the thesis assumes the data-generating distribution does not change over time in Hoeffding-tree learning [PAGE 51], but hockey teams change tactics with score and clock; that is concept drift, not stationarity. Fourth, the sparse-state trap: Table 2 has small counts at extreme leadsizes, and Table 3 has extreme penalty cells with 100% or 0% win rates [PAGE 30, PAGE 24]. Those are not stable probabilities. Fifth, the missing-season trap: the data cover “8 seasons of data for the years 2003 to 2014 (season 2005-2006, season 2006-2007, and season 2010-2011 were not available from that package)” [PAGE 24]. Any era effect, rule change, or schedule change is uncontrolled. Sixth, the regular-time-only trap: the thesis excludes overtime and shootout games, stating “we only use the NHL regular games’ data as the experiment data (and in which the game finished during the regular period)” [PAGE 14]. That censors tied late-game states and makes the model’s late-game behavior nonrepresentative. These are multi-week traps because a quick read sees high accuracy and many seasons; a slower audit sees circular labels, partial leakage, censoring, and nonstationarity.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem is not just “what is the probability?” but “what does each coach believe about the other coach’s response to the probability?” A coach trailing late can choose an aggressive forecheck, an extra attacker, or a structured possession game. A coach leading can choose a defensive shell, a counter-trap, or continued pressure. Each choice changes the event stream that the model treats as exogenous. The thesis’s WPS model multiplies leadsize by a time weight and assumes “the less time remaining, the more influence of leadsize there is” [PAGE 33]. That is a clock effect, but it is not a strategic equilibrium. If the trailing team anticipates the leading team’s habit regression—falling into a passive shell—it can counter-trap by baiting pressure and attacking the vacated space. If the leading team anticipates that counter-trap, it may instead keep possession and avoid low-percentage entries. The model has no term for these beliefs.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency under stress to revert to trained patterns: dump-and-chase, point shots, collapse-to-the-net, or star-player isolation. In the thesis’s data, those patterns appear only as aggregate shot, miss, and penalty differences. The model cannot distinguish a disciplined 1-2-2 forecheck from a desperate 2-1-2 press if both produce similar counts. Counter-trap play is the opposite: deliberately invite the opponent’s habitual response, then exploit the space behind it. That is a recursive move—coach A models coach B’s model of coach A. The clock is the third layer: as \(t_i\) increases, the value of a goal changes, and risk preferences change. The source’s piecewise time weights are a crude clock model, but they are monotone and one-sided; they do not represent the trailing team’s increasing incentive to take risks or the leading team’s increasing incentive to reduce variance. A recursive theory of mind would make the win probability depend on the joint policy \(\pi_H(a\mid s)\) and \(\pi_V(a\mid s)\), not only on the observed state \(s=(LS,t,S,P,M)\). The thesis estimates \(p(Y=1\mid s)\) from historical averages, which marginalizes over policies. That is valid only if the policy distribution is stable. In crisis states, it is not. Therefore the model is a descriptive average, not a counterfactual policy engine. It can say “historically, home teams with this leadsize and time won at this rate,” but it cannot say “if the coach changes the forecheck now, the probability changes by X.” That distinction is essential for any decision-support use.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a physical, biomechanical, or environmental variable changes the conditional probability in a way that the event-stream model cannot identify. Formally, let \(X_{\mathrm{obs}}=(LS,t,S,P,M)\) be the observed variables and let \(Z\) be a latent physical state: injury, fatigue, ice quality, goaltender condition, line-matchup, travel load, or equipment failure. The model estimates \(\hat p(X_{\mathrm{obs}})\), but the data-generating object is \(p(Y=1\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}},Z)\). If \(p(Y=1\mid X_{\mathrm{obs}},Z)\) varies with \(Z\), and \(Z\) is not in \(X_{\mathrm{obs}}\), then \(\hat p\) is not point-identified. This is a veto proof: no amount of event-count training can recover \(Z\) unless \(Z\) is measured or proxied.

Physical veto: injury and lineup changes alter the true state. A home team with a 1-goal lead in period 3 has a historical win rate of 86.6% in Table 2 [PAGE 30], but if its starting goaltender is injured or a top defenseman is out, the conditional probability is lower. The model has no injury variable. The thesis’s future-work section admits “our attributes of data are limited” and proposes “some data of the players and coaches” [PAGE 90]. That admission is the veto: the current model is conditional on a coarse event state, not on the physical roster state.

Biomechanical veto: fatigue changes skating acceleration, recovery, puck control, and decision latency. A team on the second night of a back-to-back, or after a long shift in the defensive zone, has different transition probabilities than a rested team. The source’s variables are shot, miss, penalty, leadsize, and time; they do not measure shift length, skating load, or impact. A biomechanical veto occurs when fatigue reverses the expected effect of a shot or penalty: a tired team may accumulate shots from low-danger areas while being less able to defend a counterattack. The model would count those shots as positive \(S_i\), but the true probability could fall. That is a sign-reversal regime.

Weather/environmental veto: NHL games are indoor, so direct weather is less relevant than travel, schedule density, and arena-specific ice conditions. Ice quality affects puck bounce, edge control, and injury risk. Altitude and travel can affect recovery. The thesis does not include travel distance, rest days, or arena. A weather/travel veto occurs when a team’s arrival time or schedule compression changes the latent \(Z\). The source’s historical 55% home prior [PAGE 28] averages over all such conditions; it cannot be transported to a specific game without adjustment.

The stream-mining layer does not solve the veto. The \(P_k\) monitor can detect concept drift [PAGE 75], but it detects distribution change in the label/feature relationship; it does not attribute the change to injury, fatigue, or tactics. A drift alarm is not a physical diagnosis. Therefore the correct adjudication is: the model is valid only as a conditional descriptive average over observed event states, and any claim of precise in-game probability must be vetoed when a known latent physical state is present and unmodeled. The veto is not that the model is useless; it is that its output is an average over hidden states, and the hidden-state variance can dominate the observed-state signal in crisis regimes.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
