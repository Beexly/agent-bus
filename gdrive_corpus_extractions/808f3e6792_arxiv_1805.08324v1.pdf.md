GSE/GSI audit: the paper’s central move is to define occlusion as an operation on a random set rather than as a deterministic visibility rule. The derivation has two branches. In the object-wise branch, the object set \(X\) is split into visible \(X_V\) and occluded \(X_U\) sets, and static object-wise occlusion modifies the detection probability from \(P_D(x)\) to \(P_D(x)P(v|x)\). In the measurement-wise branch, the visible measurement set \(Z_V\) is known, and the unseen measurements \(Z_U\) are marginalized; the only change to the multi-Bernoulli update is in the undetected-object term, replacing \(p_i(x)(1-P_D(x))\) with \(p_i(x)(1-P_D(x)+P_D(x)\int_z p(z|x)p(\bar v|z,Z_V))\). The first multi-hop step is the random-set occlusion factorization in (12)–(15). The second is the standard measurement model in (5)–(7). The third is the multi-Bernoulli posterior factorization in (8)–(11). The fourth is the measurement-wise marginalization in (21)–(22). The fifth is the separable-likelihood specialization in (23)–(24). Each step preserves tractability only under stated restrictions: restricted occlusion, static occlusion, or separable measurements. The gray-zone is not a numerical approximation but a model-class boundary: measurement-wise occlusion is not well posed for post-processed radar clusters or part-based vision models, while object-wise occlusion struggles when partially occluded objects still occlude others, as in Figure 4. Table I exposes a hard veto: outcome E is impossible under measurement-wise occlusion, so no posterior exists there, whereas object-wise and no-occlusion models assign posteriors. The paper also states that “For a highly accurate sensor, the outcomes for which object-wise and measurement-wise occlusion differ would rarely occur and the difference becomes trivial” [PAGE 2]. Thus the practical singularity is sensor-model mismatch, not arithmetic. Another breakdown is the association mixture: (8) is exact but the number of mixture components equals the number of possible associations, so the exact posterior can explode combinatorially. The implementation truncates to “a fixed array of 2048 normal components and an array of 72 object labels” [PAGE 7], which is a model-approximation layer, not a theorem. The final gray-zone is temporal: MOT17 video 4 shows measurement-wise occlusion improves MOTA and false negatives but increases identity switches relative to the approximate object-wise tracker, and the paper says “It is possible that violation of the restricted occlusion assumption, by the undetected stationary obstacles, significantly impacts measurement-wise tracker” [PAGE 7]. So the correct audit is conditional: measurement-wise occlusion is formally cleaner for fixed-angle line-of-sight sensors, but object-wise expected-value approximations may be more robust when the visibility process is violated by unknown occluders. The source contains no biomechanical or weather variables, so any cross-layer veto must be grounded in line-of-sight geometry, sensor type, and visibility assumptions, not invented physical ratios.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a random finite set of objects \(X=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\) and measurements \(Z=\{z_1,\dots,z_m\}\), with posterior \(P(X|Z)\). The single-object Bayes rule is
\[
p(x_i|z_j)=\frac{p(z_j|x_i)p(x_i)}{\int_{x_i}p(z_j|x_i)p(x_i)\,dx_i}.
\]
Assumptions: \(p(z_j|x_i)\) is a conditional density, \(p(x_i)\) is a prior density, and the denominator is positive. Units: probabilities are dimensionless; \(p(z_j|x_i)\) has inverse measurement-space units, for example \(\mathrm{m}^{-1}\) for range or \(\mathrm{pixel}^{-1}\) for image coordinates. Singularity: if \(\int p(z_j|x_i)p(x_i)dx_i=0\), the posterior is undefined; this is the standard zero-evidence denominator failure. [PAGE 3]

The disjoint-union notation is
\[
P(X)=\sum_{X_1\uplus X_2\uplus\cdots\uplus X_n=X}P(X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n),
\]
with \(X_1\uplus X_2=X\) meaning \(X_1\cup X_2=X\) and \(X_1\cap X_2=\varnothing\). The source says this notation “has not been widely adopted but offers several conveniences” [PAGE 3]. The multi-Bernoulli form is
\[
P(X)=\mathrm{MB}(X|\dots,r_i,p_i,\dots)
=\sum_{X_1\uplus\cdots\uplus X_{N_i}=X}\prod_{i=1}^{N_i}P(X_i),
\]
where
\[
P(X_i)=
\begin{cases}
r_i p_i(x), & X_i=\{x\},\\
1-r_i, & X_i=\varnothing,\\
0, & |X_i|>1.
\end{cases}
\]
Here \(r_i\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless existence probability and \(p_i(x)\) is a density in object-state units. The paper notes the multi-Bernoulli distribution is an example and that “other distributions have similar forms and reach similar posterior distributions” [PAGE 3].

The standard measurement model is
\[
P(Z|X)=\sum_{Z_1\uplus\cdots\uplus Z_n\uplus Z_F=Z}P(Z_F)\prod_{k=1}^{n}P(Z_k|x_k),
\]
with
\[
P(Z_k|x_k)=
\begin{cases}
P_D(x_k)p(z|x_k), & Z_k=\{z\},\\
1-P_D(x_k), & Z_k=\varnothing,\\
0, & |Z_k|>1,
\end{cases}
\]
and
\[
P(Z_F)=e^{-\kappa}\prod_{z\in Z_F}\kappa p_F(z).
\]
Here \(P_D(x_k)\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless detection probability, \(\kappa\) is the Poisson false-positive generation rate per measurement set or frame, with dimensions of expected count per scan, and \(p_F(z)\) has inverse measurement-space units. The paper calls this the “standard measurement model” [PAGE 4].

The joint multi-Bernoulli and standard-measurement factorization is given in (8)–(11). The update terms are
\[
p^{z}_{ij}(x)=p_i(x)P_D(x)p(z_j|x),
\]
\[
p^{0}_{i}(x)=p_i(x)(1-P_D(x)),
\]
\[
\lambda^{0}_{i}=1-r_i+r_i\int_x p^{0}_{i}(x)\,dx.
\]
The posterior is a mixture of multi-Bernoulli distributions whose component count equals the number of possible associations. Breakdown regimes: exact association explosion; division by \(\lambda^{0}_{i}\) when \(\lambda^{0}_{i}=0\); and truncation of low-probability components. The paper states that “in practice approximations of this form are used” [PAGE 4].

Occlusion is formalized by splitting \(X\) into visible and occluded sets:
\[
P(X_V,X_U|X)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dju}}P(v_1,\dots,v_n|x_1,\dots,x_n),
\]
where \(\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dju}}=1\) if \(X_U\uplus X_V=X\) and zero otherwise. Restricted occlusion is
\[
P(X_V,X_U|X)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dju}}P(V|X_V)\prod_{x\in X_U}P(\bar v|x,X_V),
\]
and static occlusion is
\[
P(X_V,X_U|X)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{dju}}\prod_{x\in X_V}P(v|x)\prod_{x\in X_U}P(\bar v|x).
\]
The paper warns that restricted occlusion “may not always be realistic” when partially occluded objects still occlude others, as in Figure 4 [PAGE 4].

For static object-wise occlusion, the modification is
\[
p^{z}_{ij}(x)=p_i(x)p(v|x)P_D(x)p(z_j|x),
\]
\[
p^{0}_{i}(x)=p_i(x)p(v|x)(1-P_D(x))+p_i(x)p(\bar v|x)
=p_i(x)(1-P_D(x)p(v|x)).
\]
The source states: “It is clear that incorporating static object-wise occlusion in a tracking model is equivalent to modifying the probability of detection to \(P_D(x)P(v|x)\)” [PAGE 5]. Units remain probabilities; no extra physical unit is introduced.

For measurement-wise occlusion, the object term becomes
\[
P(Z_k|x_k,Z_V)=
\begin{cases}
P_D(x_k)p(z|x_k), & Z_k=\{z\},\\
1-P_D(x_k)+P_D(x_k)\int_z p(z|x_k)p(\bar v|z,Z_V), & Z_k=\varnothing.
\end{cases}
\]
The source states: “This result is in fact obtained under restricted occlusion, regardless of the visibility model \(P(V|Z_V)\)” [PAGE 5]. The corresponding multi-Bernoulli change is
\[
p^{0}_{i}(x)=p_i(x)\left(1-P_D(x)+P_D(x)\int_z p(z|x)p(\bar v|z,Z_V)\right).
\]
Breakdown regimes: measurement-wise occlusion is not well posed for sensors whose measurements are clustered or part-based rather than fixed-angle samples; Table I outcome E is impossible under measurement-wise occlusion; and the continuous integral over \(z\) requires a valid conditional occlusion probability \(p(\bar v|z,Z_V)\in[0,1]\).

The separable-likelihood specialization is
\[
p(z|X)=\sum_k c_k(X)p(z|x_k)+c_F(X)p_f(z),
\]
with
\[
c_k(X)=P_D(x_k)\prod_{k'\neq k}(1-P_D(x_{k'})),\qquad
c_F(X)=\kappa\prod_k(1-P_D(x_k)).
\]
The deterministic restricted measurement-wise occlusion update is summarized by
\[
p(z,X)\propto \mathrm{MB}(X|\dots,r^{+}_{i}(z),p^{+}_{i}(z),\dots),
\]
with cases for \(\hat z\approx z\), \(\hat z<z\), and \(\hat z>z\). The paper says “Measurement-wise occlusion gives the properties desired by [7], [17], [24], without their constraints on the measurement likelihood” [PAGE 6]. Metrics in Table II are dimensionless for MOTA, MOTP, IDF1, mostly tracked, mostly lost, false positives, false negatives, identity switches, and cardinality ratio; GOSPA is in the chosen position/length distance units. In Table III, GOSPA is reported as Euclidean distance in position and length, so it inherits simulation length units. [PAGE 7, PAGE 9]

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is random finite set tracking: Mahler’s statistical multisource-multitarget information fusion [23], multi-Bernoulli and labeled multi-Bernoulli conjugacy [13], the Poisson multi-Bernoulli filter [14], the PMB mixture filter [12], and the separable likelihood tradition [16], [17]. The paper’s retrocausal claim is that measurement-wise occlusion “naturally derives a popular approximation for lidar tracking” [PAGE 1] and reproduces properties that prior lidar trackers obtained through handmade measurement likelihoods. In that lineage, Petrovskaya and Thrun [7], Scheel et al. [17], and Scheel et al. [24] designed occlusion-tolerant likelihoods for horizontally scanning lidar: measurements near the hypothesized vehicle were likely, slightly farther measurements were unlikely, and significantly closer measurements were given moderate uniform likelihood. Measurement-wise occlusion retroactively explains that pattern as marginalization over occluded measurements, rather than as a special-purpose likelihood. The same genealogy explains NMS in vision: the paper states that NMS “removes or merges overlapping detections” and “is an intentional implementation of measurement-wise occlusion” [PAGE 3]. Thus a later engineering habit, NMS, is genealogically a crude measurement-wise occlusion operator.

Multi-week traps are implementation traps that survive short tests but fail over repeated tracking cycles. Trap 1: treating NMS as preprocessing rather than as an occlusion model, so nearby distinct objects are deleted and later mislabeled. Trap 2: implementing static object-wise occlusion and then expecting restricted object-wise behavior; the paper explicitly distinguishes static, restricted, and general occlusion, and Figure 4 shows the restricted model can be inaccurate. Trap 3: exact association enumeration without truncation; the PMB posterior is a mixture over associations, and the MOT17 implementation keeps “the most likely 2048 components” and “the most likely 72 objects” while recycling the rest [PAGE 7]. Trap 4: using standard MOT scoring cutoffs that remove heavily occluded or unmoving individuals; the paper says its goal is to track temporarily occluded objects and therefore uses a looser intersection cutoff [PAGE 7]. Trap 5: ignoring unknown stationary occluders; the paper attributes possible measurement-wise degradation in video 4 to “undetected stationary obstacles” [PAGE 7]. These traps unfold over weeks because they appear as label drift, identity switches, or cardinality bias rather than as single-frame numeric errors.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
There is no literal human theory of mind in the source. The recursive modeling is the tracker’s belief over object existence, state, visibility, and labels: \(P(X|Z)\) with latent occlusion variables. “Crisis habit regression” maps to the baseline no-occlusion tracker and to expected-value object-wise approximations. Under model stress, such as unknown stationary obstacles in MOT17 video 4, the measurement-wise tracker can regress toward incorrect occlusion order, while the object-wise expected-value tracker behaves like a habit: it keeps object dependencies simple and is more stable in identity switches. The paper reports that “The approximate object-wise tracker outperforms the measurement-wise tracker, especially in identity switches” [PAGE 7]. The counter-trap is the opposite error: assuming object-wise occlusion is always safer because it is traditional. The paper’s four-square example shows object-wise and measurement-wise occlusion produce different posteriors, and Table I shows measurement-wise occlusion can make outcome E impossible. The counter-trap is therefore to use the wrong abstraction for the sensor: scanning lidar and NMS-like vision favor measurement-wise reasoning, while post-processed radar clusters and part-based vision favor object-wise reasoning.

The “clock” is temporal bookkeeping. In the visual tracker, each object has a fifth feature, occludability, and “in the prediction step, occludability is slowly mixed to its equilibrium value” [PAGE 6–7]. That is a clocked latent state: undetected objects gain posterior occludability, detected objects keep it unchanged, and labels are maintained by track merging. A recursive theory-of-mind audit should therefore ask three clock questions: how fast occludability mixes, how long labels survive occlusion, and when a missed detection is evidence of occlusion rather than absence. The paper’s implementation choices, 2048 components and 72 labels with recycling, are clock-bound approximations. If the clock is too fast, transient occlusion is overinterpreted; if too slow, a truly occluded object is deleted. The correct counter-trap is to separate three clocks: sensor frame time, occlusion-order time, and label-recycling time.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the source is a line-of-sight tracking paper. The physical layer is sensor geometry: fixed-angle scanning lasers, rotational lidar height, elevation or tilt, angular span, range, and overlap. The paper says measurement-wise occlusion is especially clear for sensors “whose sight is not parallel to the plane in which the objects move, such as rotational lidars placed on drones or the tops of vehicles” [PAGE 3]. It also says the probability of occlusion for each laser “will depend on the height of each object, as well as any elevation or sensor tilt,” whereas measurement-wise occlusion “can be reasoned about with only a measured range value” [PAGE 3]. Therefore a physical veto applies when the sensor does not provide a fixed measurement lattice or when occlusion depends on unmodeled 3D height and tilt. A second physical veto applies to radar: the paper says radar “requires a complex formulation of occlusion in the first place due to its reflective tendency” [PAGE 2]. A third physical veto applies to nonconvex human shapes in part-based vision: object-wise occlusion is better suited when the algorithm infers overall position from individual parts.

Biomechanical veto: the source contains no joint angles, muscle forces, gait dynamics, or human biomechanics. The visual application tracks “bounding boxes of each person” in image space, with horizontal and vertical location and size as features [PAGE 6]. Any claim requiring biomechanical load, fatigue, or articulated motion is outside the source and must be vetoed as unadjudicable. The only body-related cue is bounding-box overlap and a heuristic that measurement boxes can be occluded only by boxes whose bottom is lower [PAGE 6]. That is geometric ordering, not biomechanics.

Weather veto: the source contains no weather variables, no rain, fog, snow, wind, or illumination model. Any weather-layer conclusion is unsupported. The closest physical statement is about high-resolution sensors and line-of-sight blockage, not atmospheric scattering. Therefore weather claims are vetoed, not because weather is impossible in tracking, but because this source provides no operator, equation, parameter, or page citation for it. The valid cross-layer proof is limited to sensor type, line-of-sight geometry, object overlap, and visibility assumptions.
