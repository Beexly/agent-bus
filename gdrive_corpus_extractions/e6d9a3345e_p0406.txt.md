<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage begins with Spalding's observations in Macmillan's Magazine, Feb. 1878, pp. 287 and 289, as cited in the footnote on [PAGE 398]. James then synthesizes these into Principles of Psychology, Vol. 2 (1890), p. 398, framing attachment and fear as opposed instincts gated by developmental timing. This prefigures Konrad Lorenz's work on imprinting (1935), which formalized the innate releasing mechanism and the sensitive period in nidifugous birds, and Gilbert Gottlieb's later psychobiological emphasis on endogenous maturation and experiential canalization. It also anticipates Hubel and Wiesel's critical-period research in visual cortex (1960s), where sensory deprivation during a bounded postnatal window produces irreversible organizational change—structurally analogous to James's hooded chicks.

Multi-week traps. Trap 1: allometric overreach. Extrapolating the chick's 3–4 day window directly to mammalian socialization periods (e.g., dogs at 3–14 weeks, humans at months) without metabolic or neurodevelopmental scaling. Trap 2: experiential overreduction. Conflating James's "not the effect of experience" (about this specific visual exposure to man) with "not the effect of learning whatsoever"; prenatal auditory and olfactory experience may still shape the template James attributes to "the ear, prior to experience" [PAGE 398]. Trap 3: object-specificity overreach. Assuming the attachment object must be the biological mother; Spalding shows chicks follow ducks and humans equally, so the template is relational/motoric rather than species-specific [PAGE 398].

James's phrase "changes in their own organizations" functions as a proto-developmental-systems claim, but it lacks the circuit-level resolution of later work. The genealogical risk is assuming that because modern critical-period biology confirms endogenous gating, James's specific inference about the hooded chicks is thereby validated. The source only establishes covariation between age, hooding, and terror; it does not isolate the maturational variable.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three embedded levels of theory of mind operate in the passage. Level 0 is the chick's object model. Before template formation, any moving object triggers the following motor schema; the chick does not represent the human as a distinct intentional agent, but as a moving stimulus that releases the "instinct to follow" [PAGE 398]. After fear maturation, the same object triggers a predator-avoidance schema. Under sudden sensory influx at unhooding, the chick regresses to the default defensive habit—crisis habit regression—because no attachment schema has been consolidated to compete with the fear program.

Level 1 is the human observer's model. Spalding's unreflecting lookers-on committed an agency-detection error, imagining he possessed "some occult power over the creatures" [PAGE 398]. The counter-trap is to recognize that the handler's role was merely permissive: he "simply allowed them to follow me from the first" [PAGE 398]. The observer's error was attributing causal agency to a person who merely failed to block an endogenous behavioral release.

Level 2 is the researcher/theorist's model. James infers endogenous organization from behavior. The recursive risk is circularity: behavior is used to posit organization ("changes in their own organizations"), and organization is then used to explain the behavior. The clock is the endogenous maturation timer \tau(t) that closes the following window. A second-order counter-trap would be to assume James's functional psychology maps one-to-one onto modern reinforcement-learning or imprinting algorithms; he described behavioral regularities, not computational mechanisms, and his N=3 hooding observation cannot support a universal developmental clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. Chicks "beat against the window like a wild bird" upon unhooding [PAGE 398]. A day-old Gallus gallus domesticus has mass m \approx 30\text{--}40\ \mathrm{g}. Fluttering toward glass at v \approx 1\text{--}2\ \mathrm{m/s} yields kinetic energy K = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \approx 1.5\text{--}80\ \mathrm{mJ}. This is sufficient for beak or cranial trauma but not for window fracture, so the described behavior is a bounded-energy escape attempt, not sustained flight. This physical bound constrains the interpretation of "wild bird" metaphors: the chick's locomotor system is operational but naive.

Biomechanical layer. Hooding eliminates patterned visual input and alters proprioceptive and vestibular afference. Neonatal neck musculature is immature, so sustained cowering in a corner [PAGE 398] may reflect tonic immobility or motor exhaustion rather than purely emotional fear. Without electromyographic or kinematic data, the behavioral label "terror" remains a functional description, not a biomechanically verified affective state.

Weather/environment layer. The passage opens with an Adirondack wilderness example concerning cows and calves [PAGE 398], implicitly grounding the discussion in seasonal, thermal, and predation contexts. A cold, wet spring could independently elevate calf mortality and chick stress, confounding any field generalization from Spalding's controlled indoor hooding to naturalistic development.

Veto proof. The endogenous-organization conclusion is vetoed if (a) hooding itself elevates glucocorticoids enough to produce avoidance at any age, independent of t; or (b) auditory exposure during hooding was not actually zero (e.g., human voices in the room), which would undermine James's claim that "the ear, prior to experience, attaches them to the right object" [PAGE 398]. Without controlling for hooding stress and auditory contamination, the causal attribution to internal organizational change remains underdetermined.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
