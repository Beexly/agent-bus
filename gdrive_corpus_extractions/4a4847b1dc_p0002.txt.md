P(G = g | λ) = e^{−λ} λ^g / g!,   g ∈ ℕ₀,   λ > 0.
Units: λ in goals per match (dimensionless rate over 90 minutes).

(2) Log-linear home intensity:
log λ_{ij} = β₀ + att_i + def_j + h.

(3) Log-linear away intensity:
log μ_{ij} = β₀ + att_j + def_i.
Parameters att, def, h, β₀ in log(goals/match). h ∈ ℝ is the home-advantage log-shift.

(4) Identifiability constraints (remove two-dimensional nullspace):
Σ_{r=1}^T att_r = 0,   Σ_{r=1}^T def_r = 0.
Singularity regime: if constraints omitted, (att, def, β₀) is identified only up to transformations att_r → att_r + c, def_r → def_r − c and β₀ → β₀ + d, att_r → att_r − d/2, def_r → def_r − d/2; early-season sparse fixtures exacerbate rank deficiency.

(5) Exponential time weight:
w_m = exp(−η Δt_m),   Δt_m in weeks,   η ∈ [0, ∞) week⁻¹.
Breakdown regimes: η = 0 recovers a static homogeneous Poisson regression, invalid under managerial transfers or roster churn; η → ∞ uses only the most recent match, producing high variance and singular estimation when one match cannot identify 2T+1 parameters.

(6) Weighted maximum log-likelihood:
ℓ(θ;η) = Σ_{m=1}^M w_m { g^h_m log λ_m − λ_m − log Γ(g^h_m + 1) + g^a_m log μ_m − μ_m − log Γ(g^a_m + 1) }.
Numerical singularity as λ_m → 0⁺ with g^h_m > 0, since ∂ℓ/∂att_i ∝ w_m(g^h_m/λ_m − 1) diverges.

P(home win) = Σ_{g=0}^∞ Σ_{a=0}^{g−1} e^{−λ}λ^g/g! · e^{−μ}μ^a/a!;
P(draw) = Σ_{g=0}^∞ e^{−(λ+μ)} (λμ)^g / (g!)²;
P(away win) = Σ_{a=0}^∞ Σ_{g=0}^{a−1} e^{−λ}λ^g/g! · e^{−μ}μ^a/a!.
Units: probabilities on [0,1]. Sensitivity is maximal near λ ≈ μ.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The statistical lineage of the model reaches back to Siméon Denis Poisson’s 1837 formulation of the distribution of rare events, adapted here to the low-count domain of football scoring. The regression foundation descends from Galton and Pearson and was formalized as generalized linear models by Nelder and Wedderburn (1972), supplying the log link and Poisson variance function used throughout. The direct scientific ancestor is Maher (1982), “Modelling association football scores,” which introduced attack and defense parameters for home and away teams within a bivariate Poisson framework. Dixon and Coles [PAGE 1] extend that tradition by fitting English league and cup data from 1992 to 1995 and by examining market efficiency using the 1995–1996 season, positing that the advance fixation of prices around one week before kickoff [PAGE 1] creates a window in which estimated probabilities can be compared against market-implied benchmarks.

This genealogy carries several multi-week traps embedded in the temporal structure of football. First, the fixture-congestion trap: teams contesting midweek cup ties and weekend league matches accumulate fatigue, yet a static 1992–1995 average conflates fresh and fatigued squads, misestimating λ for the congested side. Second, the international-break trap: national-team call-ups remove key personnel for one to two weeks, so club-level att/def parameters estimated with a full squad become invalid immediately after the break, producing a transient structural break the exponential decay cannot track. Third, the managerial-merry-go-round trap: a mid-season appointment changes tactical parameters as a step function, whereas fixed η assumes smooth exponential drift, causing lagged miscalibration for roughly three to six weeks. Fourth, the regression-to-the-mean trap: a team on a extended winning streak has a temporarily inflated attack parameter; a time-weighted model with excessive recency weighting extrapolates the streak instead of regressing toward the long-run baseline, overstating λ for the next fixture. Fifth, the cup-versus-league trap: knockout ties are typically more conservative (lower scoring) than league fixtures, so pooling without competition-specific intercepts biases league-match intensities downward after a cup-heavy period. Each trap represents a failure of the stationarity assumption implicit in fitting one parameter vector over 1992–1995 [PAGE 1] and then deploying it into 1995–1996.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of observers and market participants to extrapolate a team’s current losing run indefinitely, treating the crisis state as the new baseline rather than a deviation from established strength. A time-weighted Poisson model inherits this risk: if η is set too high, recent losses dominate the likelihood and the model chases the crisis, overreacting to transitory form; if η is too low, the model underreacts and retains stale strength parameters. The statistical countermeasure is to anchor att/def to the multi-year 1992–1995 panel [PAGE 1] while allowing only gradual decay, thereby forcing recent form to regress toward long-run team quality.

The counter-trap operates at the strategic level. Because prices are fixed approximately one week before kickoff [PAGE 1], the market may over-adjust to the most recent match or crisis, creating a divergence between the market-implied break-even probability and the model’s estimated event probability derived from λ and μ via convolution. The counter-trap is to deploy capital in the direction of the regression-to-the-mean correction when the market has over-weighted recent crisis form. This is itself a second-order forecast: the modeler must anticipate that other participants are also running Poisson or Dixon–Coles-type intensity models, so any identified edge decays as adoption spreads. The documented positive return in the source [PAGE 1] is therefore not a permanent arbitrage but a transient inefficiency available while the market’s probability specification remains weaker than the statistical model’s.

The clock structure defines the recursion depth. Level 0 is the team’s true latent strength; Level 1 is the statistical model’s estimate of att/def from historical data; Level 2 is the market’s price-setting process fixed one week ahead [PAGE 1]; Level 3 is the modeler who exploits divergence between Level 1 and Level 2. If Level 2 participants converge on Level 1-style models, the divergence shrinks and the out-of-sample return collapses. Additionally, information arriving between price fixation and kickoff—injury news, weather shifts, lineup announcements—cannot be incorporated into prices but can update the model if parameters are refit, creating a clock arbitrage that favors the statistical modeler provided the estimation clock (1992–1995) and the match clock (1995–1996) remain synchronized in data-generating process.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The Dixon–Coles specification as presented on [PAGE 1] contains no regressors for physical venue properties, biomechanical player availability, or meteorological conditions. Consequently, exogenous vetoes outrank the statistical output whenever their trigger conditions bind.

Physical layer. Pitch dimensions, altitude, and travel distance alter effective scoring intensity but are absent from log λ_m and log μ_m. Veto rule: if an away fixture is played at altitude above 1,500 m or on a pitch outside standard league dimensions, apply a venue-specific attenuation to the away attack parameter or suppress the match from strategy deployment. Breakdown: the 1992–1995 English sample contains few or no such venues, so venue-specific parameters are unidentified and the veto defaults to league-average priors.

Biomechanical / personnel layer. The attack parameter att_i summarizes the expected scoring intensity of the team’s typical front line. If a primary striker responsible for a large share s of team goals is injured or suspended, the Poisson intensity for that team is no longer transportable. Veto rule: if a key attacker is unavailable, substitute att_i′ = att_i − δ where δ is derived from the player’s goal share; do not deploy raw att_i. Prediction under veto: conditional on the starter’s absence, observed home goals satisfy g^h < exp(log λ̂ − δ) with probability exceeding 0.80 under cross-validation, and mean absolute error of λ̂ increases by more than 40% relative to full-squad matches.

Weather layer. Precipitation and wind speed degrade passing accuracy and scoring efficiency, violating the Poisson equidispersion assumption Var(G) = E[G]. Veto rule: if precipitation exceeds 5 mm/h or wind exceeds 15 m/s, replace the Poisson variance with a quasi-Poisson dispersion φ > 1, widen predictive intervals accordingly, and reject exact-score point predictions. Breakdown: if weather covariates were not recorded in the 1992–1995 estimation set, φ cannot be estimated historically and must be borrowed from external meteorological-sports literature.

Veto adjudication hierarchy: physical venue veto overrides biomechanical veto overrides weather veto overrides the baseline Dixon–Coles model. If any veto fires, the model’s λ and μ output is suppressed and replaced by a conservative prior (league-average attack/defense) or the match is excluded entirely, preventing stale parameters from propagating through a structural break.
