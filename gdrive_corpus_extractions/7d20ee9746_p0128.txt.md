A second agent observing this knows that week t+1 performance will statistically regress toward the mean regardless of tactics. If the second agent intervenes, the first agent may attribute recovery to the intervention, reinforcing a maladaptive crisis habit. The counter-trap is the inverse: knowing that regression is inevitable, an observer may withhold intervention, assuming that the extreme will correct itself, thereby allowing a genuine environmental or biological deterioration to go untreated because it is misread as mere regression to the mean. The clock is the generational or measurement lag that separates the known deviation from the unknown deviation—parent to son, brother to brother, week t to week t+1. Because the regression coefficient β is less than one but non-zero, the clock does not stop the process; it only slows it. Recursion arises when Agent A models Agent B’s expectation of regression, and Agent B models Agent A’s expectation of B’s correction. If both agents believe the other will regress, neither takes corrective action, and the system remains at the extreme longer than the statistics would predict for a single decision-maker. The crisis-habit loop is therefore closed by the clock: each tick of the measurement interval offers a fresh opportunity to misattribute natural regression to agency, or to miss a real shift because of an over-reliance on Galton’s universal rule.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: stature is bounded by biomechanical allometry. Body mass scales approximately as L³ while load-bearing bone cross-sectional area scales as L², so compressive stress on the skeleton scales roughly as L. There is a hard physical upper bound beyond which skeletal columns buckle or fracture under the body’s own weight. If a known man lies near this bound (large positive D), the unknown kinsman cannot exceed it by proportional inheritance; the regression inequality D′ < D is reinforced by a structural ceiling, but the lower tail is not similarly bounded by mechanics alone.

Biomechanical veto: developmental stability imposes a lower bound. Severe malnutrition or disease during growth plates closure can produce stunting that lies far below the genetically expected mid-parental value. In such cases the unknown kinsman’s deviation D′ may be more extreme in the negative direction than the known man’s positive D, violating Galton’s universal rule because the environmental layer overrides the kinship operator.

Weather veto: Galton’s squadron assumes all statures are drawn from one still larger group with a single composition [PAGE 1]. Weather-driven food shocks create cohort-specific mean shifts: a known man who grew during an agrarian abundance may be tall (D > 0), while his unknown kinsman who grows during a multi-year drought or severe winter may be stunted by climate-sensitive harvest failure. The conditional expectation operator must then be written as

\[
\mathbb{E}[S_U \mid S_K, \mathcal{E}_K, \mathcal{E}_U] \;=\; \mu_S(\mathcal{E}_U) + \beta_{KU}\,(s_K - \mu_S(\mathcal{E}_K)),
\]

where \mathcal{E} denotes the environmental epoch. If \mu_S(\mathcal{E}_U) << \mu_S(\mathcal{E}_K), the environmental shift can dominate the shrinkage term, producing D′ with magnitude greater than D and opposite sign. Adjudication therefore proceeds by stratifying the squadron into homogeneous environmental layers before applying Galton’s random-sample axiom; pooling across weather regimes invalidates the “practically identical” schemes claim [PAGE 1].
