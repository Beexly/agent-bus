Tolman identifies this as Watson "dallied with two different notions of behavior."

To operationalize this duality as an RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards) failure mode, we must treat the subject not as a philosophical inconsistency but as a formal information-theoretic conflict between two distinct state-representation spaces and their corresponding reward functions. Let the high-level behavioral space be $\mathcal{S}_{high}$, representing the observable, task-oriented actions of the "whole man" (e.g., number of bricks laid $B$, fatigue time $T_f$). Let the low-level physiological space be $\mathcal{S}_{low}$, representing the states of smooth muscles, glands, and sympathetic nervous system activations ($\vec{s}_{phys}$). The core issue is the mapping $f: \mathcal{S}_{low} \to \mathcal{S}_{high}$ and its inverse $g: \mathcal{S}_{high} \to \mathcal{S}_{low}$.

Tolman's critique hinges on the logical impossibility of Watson's dual claims if one assumes a strict reductionist $f$ that is both surjective (all high-level behavior stems from low-level states) and injective (low-level states are uniquely determined by high-level observations). If $f$ were truly comprehensive, then knowing $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ alone would necessitate inferring $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ via $g$, making a student ignorant of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ inherently incapable of a "comprehensive" study. Conversely, if the student can succeed without $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ knowledge, then $f$ cannot be uniquely invertible; there exists an information loss where multiple distinct physiological configurations map to the same macroscopic behavioral output. This implies $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ possesses its own causal structure independent of, or at least non-functionally dependent on, the fine-grained details of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ within certain observational timescales.

We model this as a two-layered Markov Decision Process (MDP). The agent has access only to observations in $\mathcal{S}_{high}$. It seeks to maximize a reward $R_{high}$ aligned with the "commonsense answer to 'what is he doing and why,'" e.g., efficiency in bricklaying. However, an internal, hidden process governed by $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ generates a secondary reward signal $R_{low}$ related to homeostatic maintenance or emotional state (the "study of the emotions"). Watson’s early work emphasizes optimizing $R_{low}$ through stimulus-response bonds, while his later pronouncements emphasize optimizing $R_{high}$ for societal utility. The duality arises when the policy $\pi$ that optimizes $R_{high}$ does not necessarily optimize $R_{low}$, and the mapping from $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ trajectories to $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ trajectories is many-to-one. The student ignorant of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ can accurately predict $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ transitions (and thus $R_{high}$) because the dynamics in $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ exhibit Markovian properties over the relevant timescale, rendering the specific microstates of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ irrelevant—a phenomenon akin to coarse-graining in statistical mechanics.

This creates a gray zone: Is the "why" in "what is he doing and why" explained by teleonomic goals in $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ (purposive behavior) or by mechanistic causes in $\mathcal{S}_{low}$? Tolman’s resolution is that purposive explanations operate legitimately at the $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ level, and these are not eliminated by the existence of a deeper $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ layer, provided the layers are decoupled in their predictive power for their respective domains. The failure of Watson's position is an RLVR misalignment: attempting to use a reward function and state representation appropriate for one scale ($\mathcal{S}_{low}$) to explain phenomena optimized at another ($\mathcal{S}_{high}$), leading to contradictory prescriptions about what constitutes valid scientific inquiry.

**Operator Definition: Cross-Layer Information Fidelity Metric**
Let $\mathcal{X} = (\mathcal{S}_{low}, \mathcal{A}, P_{low})$ be the low-level MDP, with state space $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ representing physiological variables (smooth muscle tension, glandular secretion rates, sympathetic activation levels). Let $\mathcal{Y} = (\mathcal{S}_{high}, \mathcal{A}', P_{high})$ be the high-level MDP, with state space $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ representing task-oriented, observable behaviors (e.g., brick-laying rate, posture sequences). There exists a deterministic encoding map $E: \mathcal{S}_{low} \to \mathcal{S}_{high}$ which aggregates low-level states into high-level observables. We assume actions in $\mathcal{A}$ (micro-muscular adjustments) induce transitions in $\mathcal{S}_{low}$, which project via $E$ to apparent action-outcome pairs in $\mathcal{Y}$.

The central question is the predictability of high-level future states given only high-level current states, without explicit access to $\mathcal{S}_{low}$. This is quantified by the **Cross-Layer Mutual Information Deficit**:
\[
\Delta I = I(\mathcal{S}_{low}^{t+1}; \mathcal{S}_{low}^t | \mathcal{A}^t) - I(E(\mathcal{S}_{low}^{t+1}); E(\mathcal{S}_{low}^t) | \text{projected } \mathcal{A}'^t)
\]
where $I(X;Y|Z)$ is conditional mutual information in bits. The first term is the information flow in the low-level system. The second term is the information flow preserved in the coarse-grained, high-level description. By the data processing inequality, $\Delta I \geq 0$.

**Assumptions & Units:**
1.  **State Spaces:** $\mathcal{S}_{low} \subset \mathbb{R}^{n_{phys}}$, $\mathcal{S}_{high} \subset \mathbb{R}^{n_{task}}$, with $n_{phys} \gg n_{task}$. Units for elements of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ are physiological (e.g., Pascals, moles/sec); units for $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ are task-specific (e.g., bricks/hour, angular velocity of limbs).
2.  **Encoding Map $E$:** A continuous, many-to-one function. For instance, $E$ might compute the center-of-mass trajectory and joint angles averaged over a 1-second window, discarding phase information of individual muscle twitches.
3.  **Timescales:** The analysis holds for discrete time steps $\Delta t$ large enough to average out high-frequency physiological noise but small enough to resolve meaningful changes in task performance.
4.  **Reward Functions:** Two reward signals exist: $r_{low}(s_{low}, a) \in \mathbb{R}$, measuring immediate physiological cost/efficiency (e.g., metabolic expenditure), and $r_{high}(s_{high}, a') \in \mathbb{R}$, measuring task accomplishment (e.g., +1 per brick laid correctly). These are generally not aligned: $\arg\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}[\sum \gamma^t r_{low}] \neq \arg\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}[\sum \gamma^t r_{high}]$.

**Singularity/Breakdown Regimes:**
*   **Regime 1: Complete Decoupling ($\Delta I \approx 0$).** This occurs if $E$ is an almost sure sufficient statistic for predicting future $E(\mathcal{S}_{low})$. In this limit, knowledge of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ provides no additional predictive power for $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ beyond $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ itself. Here, a student ignorant of $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ can indeed write a fully comprehensive and accurate study of *behavioral* patterns in $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ (like bricklaying). This validates Watson's later, "whole man" position [PAGE 6].
*   **Regime 2: Strict Reductionism ($\Delta I > 0$ and maximal).** This occurs if $E$ is injective (one-to-one) or nearly so, meaning $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ retains almost all information about $\mathcal{S}_{low}$. Then, predicting $\mathcal{S}_{high}$ requires knowing $\mathcal{S}_{low}$, contradicting Watson's claim that a physiology-ignorant student could succeed. This aligns with Watson's earlier, reductive stance that behavior concerns "nothing but stimuli... and muscle contraction" [PAGE 6].
*   **Breakdown:** Tolman's argument targets the logical impossibility of *simultaneously* occupying Regime 1 and Regime 2. Watson’s error is treating behavior as existing in a regime where $\Delta I$ is context-dependent—small for some queries (daily routines) and large for others (emotional physiology)—without specifying the query domain. The theory breaks down when applied to questions spanning both scales, such as "What is the physiological basis of the emotion that causes this worker to lay fewer bricks?" Answering this requires crossing the $\Delta I$ barrier, invoking $\mathcal{S}_{low}$, which the high-level-only student lacks tools to do. Thus, the "comprehensive study of emotions" Watson mentions is impossible under a high-level-only ontology if emotions are defined by $\mathcal{S}_{low}$ states.

The genealogy of this duality traces back to the late 19th-century crisis in psychology, epitomized by the work of Wilhelm Wundt and William James. Wundt’s voluntarism sought to analyze conscious experience (*Völkerpsychologie*) into elemental sensations, while James’s radical empiricism treated "pure experience" as neither mental nor physical, a neutral monad that could play roles in both causal chains. This created a persistent "gray zone": How do subjective reports (introspective data, high-level) relate to neurophysiological processes (low-level)? Watson’s behaviorism was a direct reaction against this ambiguity, aiming to purge psychology of unobservables like "consciousness" and ground it solely in objective, measurable S-R bonds.

However, Watson’s solution inherited the very problem it sought to solve. By defining behavior strictly in terms of measurable muscle contractions and glandular secretions (Regime 2), he adopted a purely mechanistic, bottom-up causal narrative. Yet, his later pronouncements on studying the "whole man" performing useful labor (Regime 1) implicitly appealed to top-down, functional/causal explanations—precisely the kind of "purpose" his earlier doctrine rejected. This is the multi-week trap: An initially coherent program (reductionist behaviorism) designed to avoid the pitfalls of introspection gradually drifts, under practical necessity (applied industrial psychology, animal training), toward recognizing the explanatory adequacy of high-level constructs. The trap springs when the original reductionist axioms are invoked to dismiss critiques (e.g., from Gestalt psychologists or Tolman himself) that highlight the necessity of those same high-level constructs. Watson found himself caught between the *methodological purity* of Regime 2 and the *practical efficacy* of Regime 1, unable to articulate a consistent ontology that permitted both.

From an RLVR perspective, this is a classic case of reward hacking across scales. The initial reward $R_{low}$ (predicting S-R outcomes) was easily optimizable in lab settings with simple stimuli and responses. When the domain shifted to complex, real-world tasks (bricklaying), optimizing $R_{low}$ proved insufficient; the environment demanded optimization of $R_{high}$ (task completion). Watson attempted to maintain the old policy ($\pi_{low}$) while claiming credit for successes driven by an emergent, unmodeled policy ($\pi_{high}$) operating at the new scale. The retrocausal influence is that the later need for functional explanations (to understand bricklaying efficiency) reached back to redefine what constituted valid "behavioral" data, stretching the original definition until it snapped into contradiction.
