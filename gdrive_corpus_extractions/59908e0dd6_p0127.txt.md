<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of Galton's fraternal regression is deeply intertwined with the development of biometry and the rejection of blending inheritance. Before Galton, the prevailing view (e.g., Darwin's pangenesis) suggested that traits blended like fluids, which would cause variation to disappear exponentially. Galton's discovery that regression coefficients are less than 1 but constant across generations provided a mathematical mechanism for the preservation of variation: offspring regress toward the mean, but do not reach it, creating a stationary distribution.

The specific concept of "Fraternal Regression" emerges from Galton's work on correlations among relatives, detailed in his 1888 paper "Co-relations and Their Measurement" and expanded in *Natural Inheritance*. He used data on the heights of British men, their parents, and siblings. The historical context includes the debate with Francis Ysidro Edgeworth, who argued that heredity should follow the laws of probability and thus produce a binomial expansion of types, leading to increased variance. Galton countered with empirical data showing that the frequency curves remained stable, implying a regressive process.

A multi-week trap in the genealogical analysis lies in confusing Galton's "regression" with the modern genetic concept of "heritability". While related, they are distinct. Heritability \(h^2\) is the proportion of variance due to additive genetic effects. In our derived model with no shared environment, \(h^2 = 2 \beta\) (since \(\beta = \frac{1}{2} h^2\) for mid-parent regression). With \(\beta=2/3\), \(h^2 = 4/3 > 1\), which is impossible. This signals that Galton's empirical estimate of 2/3 for mid-parent regression was too high for a purely additive genetic model without shared environment, or that his measurement error inflated the apparent stability. Modern estimates for height heritability are around 0.8, giving \(\beta \approx 0.4\). Galton's value of 0.667 suggests he underestimated the role of environmental variation or overestimated the strength of resemblance. This discrepancy highlights a "retrocausal" insight: the parameters Galton estimated were constrained by the available data (mostly upper-class English families) and the limitations of 19th-century measurement, biasing the result toward stronger familial clustering.

The intellectual genealogy also includes Sir Karl Pearson, who later developed the product-moment correlation coefficient to formalize these relationships, and R.A. Fisher, who reconciled Mendelian genetics with biometric methods by showing that polygenic inheritance could produce continuous variation and regression. The trap for modern readers is to project Fisher's infinitesimal model back onto Galton's work; Galton was working phenomenologically, discovering the statistical regularity before understanding the biological mechanism. His "compromise" argument is a precursor to the idea of Bayesian updating with normal priors, anticipating the mathematical machinery of Gauss and Laplace applied to inheritance.

If parents experienced poor childhood conditions but adult offspring did not, the offspring might exceed the parental regression prediction. Conversely, if the known brother benefited from a unique favorable environment (e.g., better schooling with meals), his exceptional stature might not be reflected in his sibling raised under different circumstances, weakening the fraternal correlation. Galton's data, collected over years, might average out these fluctuations, but the veto remains: the regression coefficient is not a fixed biological constant but a snapshot of a dynamic population.

**Proof of Veto:** To prove a veto, we would need data showing that the regression coefficient varies systematically with environmental covariates (e.g., income, region, birth cohort).
