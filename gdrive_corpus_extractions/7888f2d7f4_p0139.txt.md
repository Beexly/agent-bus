The foundational Hullian habit-strength growth equation (continuous approximation) is:
\[
\frac{d\,{}_{s}H_{r}}{dN} = K \cdot f(D) \cdot (A_{max} - {}_{s}H_{r}),
\\text{(units: dimensionless habit strength per trial)},
\]
where \(N\) is trial number, \(K\) is a learning-rate constant (incorporating stimulus intensity dynamic and modality factors), \(f(D)\) is a monotonic increasing function of current visceral drive \(D\) (units: arbitrary drive index), and \(A_{max}\) is the asymptotic habit strength determined by the maximum possible effective drive reduction achievable with the given reinforcement magnitude.

The term \(A_{max}\) is posited to be proportional to the size (mass) of the reinforcing agent: if a one‑gram pellet yields \(A_{1}\), a six‑gram pellet yields \(A_{6} = 6A_{1}\) under the linear scaling assumption. The effective drive reduction at reinforcement, \(\Delta D\), is assumed to be a fraction of the pre‑reinforcement drive: \(\Delta D = c \cdot m \cdot D\), where \(m\) is the mass of food and \(c\) a conversion constant. Consequently, \(f(D) \propto \Delta D\).

In the described experiment:
- Phase 1 (trials 1–15): reinforcement = 1 g. Solution of the ODE gives \({}_{s}H_{r}(N) = A_{1} \left(1 - e^{-k_1 N}\right)\), with \(k_1 = K \cdot f(D_{1})\).
- At trial 16, reinforcement switches to 6 g. The new parameters become \(A_{6}\) and \(k_6 = K \cdot f(D_{6})\). Since \(D\) is momentarily unchanged, \(f(D_{6}) > f(D_{1})\) because \(\Delta D\) is larger for the same drive level when more food is available. Thus the instantaneous slope \(dH/dN\) jumps.
- Phase 2 solution (for \(N > 15\)): \({}_{s}H_{r}(N) = A_{6} - (A_{6} - H_{15}) e^{-k_6 (N-15)}\), where \(H_{15}\) is the habit strength at the end of phase 1.

Breakdown regime: When the amount of food exceeds the animal’s metabolic capacity to convert it into immediate drive reduction (e.g., stomach fullness limits), the linear relation \(\Delta D \propto m\) saturates, causing the predicted increase in both \(A_{max}\) and \(k\) to flatten. Moreover, if the secondary reinforcement (stimulus substitution) is not negligible, the simple growth function underestimates early acquisition because the conditioned stimulus alone begins to sustain responding even before primary drive reduction occurs.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of the “amount of reinforcement” variable traces back to Thorndike’s Law of Effect (1898), which asserted that satisfaction strengthens stimulus-response bonds, but Thorndike did not quantify magnitude. Köhler’s insight learning (early 1920s) implicitly treated reinforcement as sudden cognitive restructuring, but still lacked a continuous metric. Pavlovian conditioning introduced unconditioned stimulus (UCS) intensity as a determinant of conditioned response strength, yet remained qualitative. Hull was the first to embed reinforcement magnitude directly into a differential equation governing habit growth, using the principle of effectivity: learning rate is proportional to the degree of primary drive reduction achieved at reinforcement. This formalization anticipated later quantitative theories such as Rescorla-Wagner (1972), where the prediction error is multiplied by an associability parameter that can be scaled by US magnitude, and Sutton-Barto’s Temporal Difference learning, where reward size influences weight updates.

Historically, experiments that manipulated food reward size (e.g., varying pellets in lever-press tasks) were used to validate the Hullian hypothesis. In the multi-week trap of behavioral research, early studies often suffered from confounding variables: larger rewards might also be more palatable (quality confound) or presented with a different handling time, altering the effective inter-trial interval. Moreover, long-term adaptations like weight gain could systematically alter baseline drive levels across weeks, making cross-subject comparisons invalid unless drive was normalized. Hull’s prescription to control for secondary reinforcement and to use “suitable variations in quality” aimed to isolate pure magnitude effects, but subsequent researchers recognized that these controls were difficult to implement perfectly, leading to mixed empirical support and eventual refinement of the theory.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From the organism’s perspective, the sudden jump from 1 g to 6 g creates a mismatch between expectation and outcome. After fifteen trials, the animal has built a predictive model: “response leads to ~1 g”. On trial sixteen, receiving six times that amount triggers a heightened arousal state and likely a temporary suppression of operant behavior (a “surprise” pause). This is a crisis habit regression: the existing habit strength is momentarily ineffective because the sensory feedback does not match the stored association. However, the larger primary reinforcement also amplifies the salience of the stimulus-reward pairing, potentially accelerating future learning once the animal recalibrates.

If the experimenter interprets the initial dip as evidence against Hull’s curve, they might prematurely switch to a smaller reward or introduce a secondary cue, inadvertently creating a counter-trap. The true counter-trap is to maintain the six-gram schedule long enough for the new growth curve to emerge, while carefully controlling for any changes in secondary reinforcement (e.g., by ensuring the delivery apparatus noise remains constant). Time is the clock: within a few dozen trials, the heightened learning rate should overcome the initial hesitation and propel habit strength toward the higher asymptote, confirming the theory if the curve’s shape matches the predicted exponential approach.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/biomechanical veto: If the subject is a small rodent (e.g., mouse) with a stomach capacity of ~2 ml, a 6‑gram food bolus (density ~1 g/ml) would physically exceed gastric volume, causing discomfort, regurgitation, or even vomiting. In that case, the drive reduction function \(f(D)\) would not increase monotonically with mass; instead, it would peak and then decline sharply due to pain and nausea, producing a negative reinforcement effect. This biological constraint overrides the theoretical growth function entirely. Even for larger animals (rats, dogs), very large meals can induce post-prandial lethargy (the "spleen" effect), reducing the vigor of subsequent responses and thus lowering the observable rate of habit acquisition despite high reinforcement magnitude.

Weather/environmental veto: Temperature fluctuations affect metabolic rate and hunger. In a cold room, an animal’s drive for calories is elevated, so a 6‑gram reward might produce a greater proportional drive reduction than in a warm room, enhancing learning. Conversely, in heat, appetite is suppressed; the same reward might be less effective. Thus, the assumed equivalence of "six grams" across environmental conditions is invalid without strict climate control.

Operant chamber veto: If the reward dispenser delivers the six‑gram portion slowly (e.g., via a hopper that requires multiple lever presses to fully retrieve), the temporal extension of reinforcement dilutes its impact. The critical factor is the immediacy of drive reduction; a delayed or fractionated reward reduces the effective \(f(D)\) compared to a single rapid delivery, again breaking the simple growth assumption.
