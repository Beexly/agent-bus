By 1889 (*Natural Inheritance*), Galton had shifted from studying exceptional individuals to analyzing familial distributions of common traits like temperament. The 1:2:1 ratio he identifies here is not novel; it mirrors the Mendelian F2 generation segregation ratio (dominant:recessive:heterozygote, though Mendel's paper was published in 1866, it remained obscure until 1900). It is highly probable Galton arrived at the 1:2:1 expectation via independent derivation based on his theory of "ancestral contribution," anticipating Mendelian laws before they were widely known in England.

Historically, this passage represents a pivot from qualitative moral philosophy to quantitative biometrics. Prior to this, temperament was discussed in humoral pathology (e.g., Hippocrates, Burton) as fluid balances. Galton retroactively re-frames "good" and "bad" temper not as moral failings but as statistical variances around a familial mean. The "trap" of the multi-week or multi-year scale in this genealogy is the conflation of *social observation* with *genetic transmission*. Victorian compilers of family histories (often clergymen or aristocrats) were embedded in rigid class structures. The "neutral" category was not a biological vacuum but a repository for information deemed "undesirable" [PAGE 1]. Over generations, relying on such skewed archival data to build hereditary models creates a feedback loop: the model assumes symmetry, prompts observers to enforce symmetry, and produces data that validates the initial flawed assumption. This epistemological trap delayed the understanding of gene-environment interactions for decades.
