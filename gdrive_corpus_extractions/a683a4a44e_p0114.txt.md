<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage on [PAGE 114] runs from classical diminishing returns (Turgot, Malthus, Ricardo) through neoclassical variable-proportions theory to Frank Knight's own treatment. The footnote on [PAGE 114] explicitly cites "F. M. Taylor, Principles of Economics, chap. Iv, for a very thorough and sound non-mathematical discussion of the whole question of variable proportions and diminishing returns," while Knight immediately qualifies this by rejecting Taylor's "treatment of the economy of large-scale production" as "based on fallacy" [PAGE 114]. This situates the page inside a debate about whether returns depend on factor proportions (Knight's emphasis) or on absolute scale (Taylor's economies of large-scale production). The multi-week trap embedded in this genealogy is scale-proportion confusion: over a weeks-long expansion, a firm may add labor and capital together and observe falling output per unit. If the analyst attributes this to "diminishing scale" rather than to a shift in the proportion r away from the optimum described on [PAGE 114], the firm will make incorrect capacity commitments weeks later. Knight's law explicitly severs output per unit from absolute amount [PAGE 114], so a multi-week diagnostic that conflates the two commits a category error.

A second multi-week trap concerns reversibility. Knight asserts that "Since either agency may be the increasing and the other the decreasing one, the first and third stages are identical in meaning" [PAGE 114]. Over a multi-week horizon, however, increasing labor and then decreasing it back does not retrace the same output trajectory because of hiring and firing costs, skill attrition, and capital fixity. The firm that treats Stage I and Stage III as symmetric will underestimate the cost of reversing a proportion error. A third trap is boundary neglect: Premise 3 on [PAGE 114] holds that extreme variation yields zero product, encouraging planners to extrapolate smooth decline toward zero as an agency becomes scarce. In practice, near the boundary (e.g., extreme overtime with fixed capital), fatigue and congestion produce discontinuous drops rather than the graceful "continuously varied" decline assumed on [PAGE 114]. These traps are retrospective in the sense that they become visible only after the proportion has been mis-set for several weeks, at which point the firm must reconstruct whether it was operating in Stage II (positive but falling average product) or Stage III (negative marginal product).
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the tendency of decision-makers under stress to revert to fixed proportions of agencies because varying the mix feels like experimenting with scarce resources during a crisis. If a firm holds L/K constant while demand shifts, it drifts away from the interior maximum identified on [PAGE 114], potentially residing indefinitely in Stage II or Stage III without recognizing that average product per unit is falling. The counter-trap is the "more is better" regression: observing that MP_L > 0 (total product still rising) and concluding that adding more labor is beneficial, even when AP_L is already declining. Knight's Stage II [PAGE 114] precisely captures this zone—product per unit of the relatively increased agency decreases—so the decision-maker confuses a positive marginal product with an improving average product and pushes into Stage III, where "the product relative to either agency decreases" [PAGE 114].

The clock layer introduces two distinct time scales: the proportion-adjustment clock (how quickly L and K can physically be varied) and the environment clock (how quickly demand, prices, or weather shift the optimal r). If the environment clock ticks faster than the adjustment clock, the firm chases a moving optimum r*(t) and perpetually operates off the maximum described on [PAGE 114]. The recursive loop runs as follows: misperception of the current stage → incorrect change in agency proportion → increased uncertainty about where r* lies → regression to fixed habit as a crisis response → further deviation from the optimum → amplified losses. Knight's emphasis on uncertainty (rather than measurable risk) in his broader theory implies that r* is not simply unknown but unknowable with precision; therefore the firm's belief state about which stage it occupies must be updated recursively, and habit regression is not merely a bias but a self-reinforcing attractor under time pressure.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Any economic prescription derived from [PAGE 114] must pass three veto layers before it is admissible.

Physical veto: The production function Q = F(L,K) must respect conservation laws and thermodynamic bounds. Knight's Premise 3 on [PAGE 114]—that extreme variation yields zero product—is physically coherent for processes requiring both agencies (no land means no crop), but it is vetoed for fully automated production where capital alone yields Q > 0 at L → 0. If a production layer is fully automated, the boundary condition lim_{L→0} Q = 0 fails, and the alleged interior maximum near the labor-extreme may not exist.

Biomechanical veto: Labor is not an agency that can "be added in amounts varying continuously from zero to infinity" [PAGE 114] without destroying productivity. Human operators exhibit fatigue, minimum viable crew sizes, and discrete skill thresholds. Below a crew-size floor, Q collapses discontinuously rather than varying smoothly, violating Premise 1 on [PAGE 114] ("The proportions of agencies in a combination may be varied without destroying its productivity") and eliminating the putative first stage in which product per unit increases.

Weather veto: For land-intensive production, the agency K (land) is weather-contingent. Adding labor to land under drought, flood, or freeze shifts the production function itself exogenously; the same proportion r yields different Q across weather states. Knight's scope condition on [PAGE 114]—that the law relates to proportion variation "irrespective of the absolute amount of any factor"—presumes a fixed physical environment, which weather violates.

Adjudication rule: A recommendation derived from [PAGE 114] is admissible only if all three veto layers pass. If any veto triggers, the law of variable proportions is either inapplicable or must be restated as a state-contingent relation g(r; weather, crew_floor, automation_level), and predictions of stage membership or optimal proportion are void.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable predictions:

Scenario 1: Fixed-coefficient (Leontief) technology. Technology Q = min(L/ā, K/ḱ) with fixed input requirements ā and ḱ. Counterfactual: vary L continuously while holding K fixed. Verifiable veto: Premise 1 on [PAGE 114] fails because varying the proportion off the required ratio does not continuously change productivity; output is flat until the kink and then jumps. Prediction: Q(L) at fixed K is a step function with no smooth first stage and no interior maximum in r; the only critical point is the kink at r* = ā/ḱ. Falsification: if measured Q(L) shows a smooth rise-and-fall instead of a step, the Leontief veto does not apply.

Scenario 2: Automation floor violating Premise 3. Technology Q(K,L) = K^α + βL^γ with α, γ ∈ (0,1), K > 0 fixed. Counterfactual: withdraw labor toward L → 0. Verifiable veto: Premise 3 on [PAGE 114] ("extreme variation ... will yield a zero product") fails because Q → K^α > 0 as L → 0. Prediction: output asymptotes to a positive capital-only flow; there is no zero-product boundary and no third-stage collapse to zero at labor-extreme. Falsification: if observed Q tends to zero as labor is withdrawn, the automation veto is not triggered for that process.

Scenario 3: Irreversible capital invalidating reversibility. Counterfactual: over two weeks, increase K then decrease K back to the original level while adjusting labor flow optimally at each date. Verifiable veto: Knight's claim that "the first and third stages are identical in meaning" [PAGE 114] fails because capital is heterogeneous, sunk, and subject to time-to-build; the path cannot be retraced costlessly. Prediction: hysteresis—the output path on the way down lies below the path on the way up at identical K, so Stage I and Stage III are not observationally identical over the adjustment clock. Falsification: if the firm returns to exactly the same Q at the same K after reversal, the irreversibility veto does not apply.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
