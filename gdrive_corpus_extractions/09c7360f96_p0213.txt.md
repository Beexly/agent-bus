We formalize the inheritance process as a discrete-time dynamical system operating on information states. Let $G_t \in \mathcal{G}$ represent the genetic information vector at generation $t$, and $E_t \in \mathcal{E}$ represent the environmental circumstance vector. The phenotype $P_t$ is a function $F: \mathcal{G} \times \mathcal{E} \rightarrow \mathcal{P}$, where $\mathcal{P}$ is the phenotype space. The evolution of genetics follows a stochastic operator $\mathcal{T}_G$:
$$ G_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_G(G_t, G'_t) + \eta_t^G $$
where $G'_t$ is the genetic vector of the other parent, and $\eta_t^G$ models mutational noise. The environmental state evolves as:
$$ E_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_E(E_t, C_t) + \eta_t^E $$
where $C_t$ represents cross-generational cultural/niche construction factors, and $\eta_t^E$ is environmental noise.

The contribution from a specific ancestor $A$ at generation $k$ back to the present is given by the expected mutual information $I(A; P_0)$ between the ancestor's genetic state and the present phenotype. Under a simplified additive model with no linkage disequilibrium, the variance contribution decays geometrically:
$$ \text{Var}_{A}(P_0) = \frac{\sigma_g^2}{2^{2k}} $$
where $\sigma_g^2$ is the additive genetic variance in the base population, and $k$ is the number of meioses separating the ancestor from the descendant. This matches Galton's "one quarter from each parent" ($k=1$, factor $1/4$) and "one sixteenth from each grandparent" ($k=2$, factor $1/16$).

The total phenotypic variance $V_P$ is decomposed as:
$$ V_P = V_G + V_E + V_{G\times E} $$
where $V_G$ is the aggregate genetic variance, $V_E$ is the environmental variance, and $V_{G\times E}$ is the interaction variance. For full siblings (fraternals), they share on average 50% of their genes identical by descent, so their covariance is approximately $\frac{1}{2}V_G$. For half-siblings or cousins (co-fraternals), the shared fraction is lower (25% for half-sibs, 12.5% for first cousins), and their environmental correlation is also lower due to different households. Thus, the phenotypic correlation $\rho$ satisfies:
$$ \rho_{\text{fraternal}} \approx \frac{1}{2}h^2 + c^2 $$
$$ \rho_{\text{co-fraternal}} \approx \frac{1}{4}h^2 + c'^2 $$
where $h^2 = V_G/V_P$ is heritability, and $c^2, c'^2$ are the common environmental variances. Since $c'^2 < c^2$ and the genetic term is smaller, the overall variance between co-fraternals is larger.

**Units:** All variance terms are in squared units of the measured phenotype (e.g., cm² for height). Mutual information $I$ is measured in bits or nats. The decay factor $1/2^{2k}$ is dimensionless.

**Breakdown Regimes:**
1. **Selection Singularity:** When a trait is under strong directional selection, the geometric decay fails because alleles contributing to the trait increase in frequency non-randomly, preserving more information than expected. The effective number of ancestors contributing to the trait becomes smaller than the pedigree count.
2. **Epistatic Collapse:** If the phenotype depends critically on multi-locus interactions (epistasis), breaking up these combinations through recombination in intermediate generations can cause the trait to vanish abruptly, not smoothly. The information loss is super-exponential.
3. **Environmental Saturation:** If environmental variance $V_E$ dominates and is highly variable across families, it can swamp the genetic signal, making all familial correlations approach zero regardless of relatedness. The geometric law becomes unobservable.

**Citation:** Galton's summary [PAGE 213] states: "the average contributions... assign an average of one quarter from each parent, and one sixteenth from each grandparent." This is the empirical basis for the $1/2^{2k}$ decay model.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Galton’s work emerges from the post-Darwinian effort to quantify inheritance, directly challenging blending inheritance theories. His geometric scale was derived from studies of peas and human stature, attempting to provide a mathematical foundation for heredity that aligned with Darwin’s natural selection. Historically, this lineage leads to the rediscovery of Mendel, the development of biometrical genetics by Pearson and Weldon, and ultimately the synthesis of Mendelian genetics with population statistics in the early 20th century. The “dual causes” framework prefigures the modern quantitative genetics decomposition of variance.

Scientifically, the insight that resemblance diminishes with distance due to the greater frequency of mediocre deviations is a precursor to the concept of regression to the mean. Galton observed that tall parents have tall children, but the children are less extremely tall than the parents, regressing toward the population mean. This is explained by the fact that extreme phenotypes often result from a combination of favorable genes *and* favorable environment; the offspring inherit only half the genes and a new environment, so their phenotype is closer to average.

Multi-week traps arise in interpreting these laws. First, the **Determinism Trap**: assuming that the geometric contributions imply fixed quotas of genetic material, ignoring that alleles are sampled stochastically. Second, the **Environment Neglect Trap**: overlooking that the “circumstances” Galton mentions include both prenatal and postnatal environments, which can correlate with genetics (gene-environment correlation). Third, the **Linearity Trap**: applying the simple geometric decay to traits influenced by dominance or epistasis, where the relationship between relatedness and similarity is not smooth. These traps lead to erroneous predictions about the persistence of traits or the design of breeding programs.

The geometric inheritance model can be vetoed by physical and biomechanical constraints. This creates a lower bound on the phenotype, truncating the distribution. Weather and climate act as veto mechanisms by imposing hard constraints on survival; if an inherited trait makes an organism vulnerable to temperature extremes, selection will eliminate those genotypes before they can reproduce, altering the expected contribution patterns. A formal proof: let $T$ be a temperature-dependent fitness function. If the phenotype $P$ determines thermal tolerance, then the realized genetic contribution to future generations is weighted by $w(P) = \exp(-\beta (P - T_{opt})^2)$. Even if the expected genetic contribution from an ancestor is $1/16$, the *effective* contribution after selection may be zero if all descendants fall outside the viable range. Thus, the geometric law is conditional on the absence of such physical vetoes.
