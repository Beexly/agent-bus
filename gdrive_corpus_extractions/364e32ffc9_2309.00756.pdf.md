<THE THINK_TRACE>
Multi-hop derivation of what the estimator actually identifies.

Hop 1 — Forward object. Fix a fourth-down state σ and action a. The next-state value is a genuinely random variable, not a scalar: with probability p(s′|σ,a) it takes the value r(s′)+v̄^π(s′), where v̄^π is the infinite-horizon expected-points value of the league-average stationary policy. Because the play can only transition to finitely many immediate successor states, this random variable has finite discrete support ([PAGE 8], eq. 4.5). That discreteness is load-bearing for everything downstream.

Hop 2 — The risk operator. Instead of the mean E[V], the coach is assumed to maximize the τ-quantile Q_τ[V] (eq. 4.6, [PAGE 8]). On a finite discrete support the quantile is a step function of τ, so the optimal action a*(σ, q_τ) is piecewise-constant in τ: it only changes at the finitely many τ where the argmax over {GO, FGA, PUNT} switches. This is the seed of the whole identification problem.

Hop 3 — The inversion. The inverse problem (eq. 4.11, [PAGE 9]) flips argmax-over-actions into argmin-over-τ of the Hamming disagreement rate between observed and τ-optimal actions. Because the loss is a sum of indicators over a piecewise-constant map, the loss curve ℓ(τ) is itself piecewise constant with flat floors. The set of minimizers is therefore an interval (or union of intervals), not a point. The authors concede this explicitly: "there are often multiple τ's which satisfy (5.5) … because the τ-optimal policies … are constant for many subsets of [0,1]" ([PAGE 12]). Their "estimate" τ̂ is the median of a set — a convention, not a unique identified value.

Hop 4 — Structural degeneracy in the partition. Enlarging the model to L field regions (eq. 4.12, [PAGE 9]) does not add identifying power once L ≥ |A| = 3: "once the partition size is equal to (or greater than) the size of the action space (|A| = 3), a partitioning can be constructed where, within any given subset, each game state has the same majority observed action … (4.12) will be minimized, no matter how many additional subsets are created" ([PAGE 12]). That is a hard non-identifiability singularity: the loss surface has a flat global minimum manifold. They retreat to L = 2 and, since |S₄| gives 2⁹⁹ partitions, restrict to 344 contiguous threshold partitions; the 40-yardline and 50-yardline cuts are numerically indistinguishable (losses 0.1499938 vs 0.1500247, [PAGE 26]), and they pick the 50 for interpretability, not because the data prefer it.

Hop 5 — The gray-zone confound the paper cannot fully escape. The MDP uses league-average transition dynamics (identical-teams assumption, P_AA = P_BB, P_AB = P_BA, [PAGE 25]). Therefore a coach who punts because he privately believes his offense is weak, and a coach who punts because he is genuinely risk-averse, produce the same observed action and get mapped to the same low τ̂. Risk preference and private belief about team strength are not separately identified in this design; the paper acknowledges only that team-specific transitions were omitted for sparsity/computation ([PAGE 22]), not that this conflates the estimand. That is the deepest gray zone: "conservative risk preference" is partly a relabeling of "we modeled everyone as the league-average team."

Hop 6 — Benchmark relativity. "Risk tolerance" is defined relative to the win-probability-optimal "4th Down Bot" as translated into this quantile scale ([PAGE 14]). The τ scale is model-relative: τ̂ answers "which quantile of *this* MDP's next-state-value distribution rationalizes the observed action," so τ̂ values are not comparable across MDP specifications, and the normative claim "coaches are too conservative" inherits any bias in the win-probability benchmark (selection bias à la Daly-Grafstein 2023; understated uncertainty à la Brill et al. 2023, both flagged on [PAGE 16] footnote 10).

Actual units: reward/value in expected points (EP); probabilities dimensionless; τ dimensionless in [0,1]; loss dimensionless in [0,1].

Reward (eq. 3.3, [PAGE 6]), from team A's perspective:
r(s) = 6.95 for a TD (6 + league-average conversion value), 3 for a FG, −2 for a safety; team B's rewards are the negatives. Note r depends on state only because scoring states are absorbing ([PAGE 6], fn. 5) — a nonstandard MDP where reward is not action-dependent.

Transition kernels (eqs. 3.1–3.2, [PAGE 6]): p(s′|σ,a) for the fourth-down play, p(s′|s,π̄) for the remainder under the fixed stationary league-average policy.

Next-state value (eq. 4.3, [PAGE 7]) — the random variable being risk-parameterized:
V^{π̄}(σ,a) := r(S_{t+1}(σ,a)) + E_{π̄}[ Σ_{n=t+2}^{T} r(S_n) | S_{t+1}(σ,a) ].

Quantile objective (eq. 4.6, [PAGE 8]):
q^{π̄}_τ(σ,a) = Q_τ[V^{π̄}(σ,a)] = inf{ x ∈ ℝ : τ ≤ F_{V^{π̄}}(x | σ,a) }.

Deliberate modeling choice (eqs. 4.8 vs 4.9, [PAGE 8]): they reject Q_τ[Σ_{n≥t+1} r(S_n)] (quantile of the whole return) because it "would inherently assume that coaches operate according to the same quantile in all future game situations, which is not plausible." They quantile only the *immediate* next-state value and keep the future in expectation. This is the crucial structural assumption: risk is applied one period deep, not over the return.

Inverse program (eq. 4.11, [PAGE 9]) with Hamming loss (eq. 4.10, [PAGE 9]):
min_{τ∈[0,1]} (1/N) Σ_j 1( a_j ≠ a*_j(σ_j, q^{π̄}_τ) ); partitioned estimator (eq. 5.5, [PAGE 12]) over τ = (τ₁, τ₂), P₁ = opponent half, P₂ = own half.

Value-function closed form (eq. A.2, [PAGE 25]):
v^{π̄}_A = r_A ( I − P^{π̄}_{AA} + P^{π̄}_{AB} )^{−1}.
Breakdown regime: this requires (I − P_AA + P_AB) to be invertible, i.e. the substochastic league-average chain must be properly absorbing/recurrent. The steady-state approximation (eq. 5.3, [PAGE 10]) assumes "the steady state is reached quickly," which fails for atypical game states (huge deficit, end-of-game) — exactly where risk preferences are most interesting.

Empirical quantile (eq. 5.4, [PAGE 10]): q̂_τ(σ,a) = inf{x : τ ≤ F̂_{V^{π̄}}(x)}. Singularity: where GO is never observed, p(s′|σ,GO) has no empirical support, F̂ is degenerate, and the raw quantile "vary drastically … producing counter-intuitive go-for-it 'islands'" ([PAGE 10]). Fixed by a monotone-decreasing bivariate penalized-B-spline smoother (scam, k = 4 knots) plus third-down augmentation ([PAGE 11]) — so the reported maps are partly a product of the regularizer.

Performance link (eqs. 6.1–6.2, [PAGE 21]): points-gained regressed on τ̂, team Elo, and an own-half indicator. Table 1: β₁ = 0.769 (partial R² = 0.048, p < 0.001), full R² = 0.059, N = 622. Interpretation: "excessive risk aversion on fourth down is negatively associated with team performance" ([PAGE 21]) — but the signal-to-noise is explicitly low (every partial R² < 0.05).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Intellectual lineage (not "retro-causal" — the causation runs forward through revealed preference):

1. Carter & Machol (1971, 1978) and Romer (2006) established the expected-points / dynamic-programming benchmark and the "coaches are suboptimal" gap. 2. Chan, Fernandes & Puterman (2021) supplied the Markov value function v^{π̄} and the closed-form (A.2) this paper reuses wholesale. 3. Distributional RL (Bellemare, Dabney & Munos 2017) motivated treating the *distribution* of value, not just its mean, as the object of interest. 4. Quantile MDPs (Gilbert, Weng & Xu 2017; Li, Zhong & Brandeau 2022) supplied the forward theory for optimizing Q_τ. 5. Inverse optimization (Chan, Mahmood & Zhu 2023) and risk-inference precedents (Di Castro, Oren & Mannor 2019 "risk-shaping"; Li 2021 convex risk; Yu, Chen & Dong 2020 portfolio risk) are the methodological parents; the paper's stated novelty is being "the first to consider inverse optimization of quantile MDPs" ([PAGE 3]).

Recurring methodological traps (the "multi-week" errors this subfield keeps re-hitting): (i) treating raw play-by-play as if it were a policy evaluation, when the *decision to attempt* is itself selected (Daly-Grafstein 2023) — a trap the paper partially patches with third-down augmentation; (ii) integer-rounding confounding of "yards to go" (Lopez 2020, [PAGE 4] fn. 4) that manufactures spurious aggressiveness; (iii) omitting Q4 (Romer 2006; Daly-Grafstein 2023) — this paper shows that is only necessary in the low-WP stratum ([PAGE 17]), a genuine correction of prior practice.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Honest scope: the paper's agent model is one-level revealed preference, not a recursive belief hierarchy. There is no coach-modeling-opponent-modeling-coach equilibrium here; the MDP conditions the future on a fixed league-average policy π̄ and asks only which single τ rationalizes the observed action. Any deeper "theory of mind" is imposed, not estimated.

What the paper *does* legitimately recover about behavioral state:
- "Clock" effect (genuine): in the fourth quarter at win probability < 0.2, coaches are "clearly much more risk-tolerant … than in Q1-Q3," while at higher WP the quarter is irrelevant ([PAGE 17]). This is a real clock-dependent shift in the fitted τ.
- "Habit/update" effect (from cited work, not their own estimate): Roach & Owens (2024) find coaches are "more sensitive to failures than successes," producing suboptimal later decisions ([PAGE 4]). That is the closest thing to an asymmetric loss-aversion dynamic, but this paper does not model it endogenously — π̄ is stationary, so within-game belief updating is assumed away.
- Field-region split is the paper's strongest ToM-adjacent result: τ̂₁ > τ̂₂ (opponent half more risk-tolerant than own half) across nearly all WP bins ([PAGE 14]).

Why you can't run a "counter-trap" prediction off this: the fitted τ̂ is set-valued with wide intervals (e.g. τ̂₂ ∈ [0.02,0.36] ∪ [0.39,0.40] in Fig. C.2, [PAGE 27]), and inference is truncated to [0.2,0.8] because policies plateau at the extremes ([PAGE 14]). A point estimate precise enough to exploit a specific coach's next decision is not identified. What *is* robust across 200 bootstraps is the punt/field-goal boundary; the go/kick boundary is not ([PAGE 28]) — so the model can weakly predict the kicking choice, not the go decision.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Confounds that would veto a physical/causal reading of "risk preference":

- Omitted in-game state (veto, partial): score differential, time, and timeouts are excluded from the state space and proxied only by win probability ([PAGE 7], [PAGE 22]). Two states with identical WP but different score/time can demand opposite optimal risk, so a chunk of measured "conservatism" is really unmodeled game state.
- Belief vs. preference (veto, unresolved): as derived in THINK_TRACE Hop 5, identical-team dynamics make "pessimism about my offense" indistinguishable from "risk aversion." No physical veto is available inside the model; it would require team-specific transition probabilities, which the authors deliberately omit.
- Benchmark bias (veto on normativity): the "too conservative" verdict is relative to a WP bot that is itself subject to selection bias and understated uncertainty ([PAGE 16] fn. 10). If the benchmark is biased aggressive, the conservatism is overstated.
- Smoothing-induced structure (veto on fine maps): the monotone-decreasing spline constraint and third-down augmentation impose the contiguous regions the paper then "discovers" ([PAGE 11–12]). The go-islands are removed by assumption as much as by data.
- Not in the data at all: weather (wind/snow materially changes FG/punt value), injuries/personnel, kicker leg range. The paper never conditions on these, so any physical kicking-range story is outside the model's identification entirely.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
