<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight's 1921 formulation sits at the intersection of three historical lineages. First, the Cantillon-Say tradition defines the entrepreneur as the residual claimant who buys inputs at fixed prices and sells output at uncertain prices. Second, the Marshallian marginalist tradition treats profit as a return to coordinating ability rather than uncertainty-bearing per se. Knight's innovation was to separate uncertainty from measurable risk and to argue that profit is the reward for bearing non-insurable uncertainty, not for labor or management alone.

This argument retroactively shapes later property-rights and agency theory. Alchian and Demsetz's team-production analysis (1972) echoes Knight's insight that residual claimancy is needed to monitor inputs, while Jensen and Meckling (1976) formalize the agency costs that arise when ownership and control separate—precisely the delegation problem Knight sketches with the hired manager. Hart and Moore's incomplete-contracts framework (1986) and Grossman-Hart-Moore property-rights theory (1986) generalize Knight's guarantee problem: because not all contingencies can be specified ex ante, ownership of assets allocates control over unforeseen states. The modern trappings—limited liability, corporate bankruptcy, manager stock options, sovereign guarantees—are institutional patches applied to the singularity Knight identifies at \(W_e \to 0\).

Multi-week traps: Over a single week, a firm may operate under stable, measurable demand conditions, making Knightian uncertainty appear dormant. Over multiple weeks, however, unforeseen contingencies accumulate: a key supplier fails, a regulator changes a rule, a competitor introduces a substitute. The probability distribution over monthly outcomes is not the convolution of weekly stable distributions; regime shifts invalidate the measurable-risk assumption. The trap is to manage week-to-week as if uncertainty were insurable risk, thereby failing to maintain the exposed, transferable wealth position \(W_e\) that Knight shows is necessary to guarantee long-horizon contractual remuneration. Firms that optimize for weekly earnings smoothness often erode \(W_e\) through buybacks or leverage, leaving the guarantee operator \(G(W_e, C)\) undercapitalized exactly when a multi-week shock sequence materializes.
