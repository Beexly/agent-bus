Let an athlete \(i\) have a latent skill state vector at time \(t\):
\[
\mathbf{s}_i(t) = \left[s_{i,1}(t), s_{i,2}(t), \dots, s_{i,K}(t)\right],
\qquad s_{i,k}(t)\in[0,1],
\]
where each component \(s_{i,k}\) is a dimensionless mastery probability for a KPI-associated skill \(k\), such as posture, grip, arm mechanics, balance, contact preparation, or blocking fit. Time \(t\) has SI units of seconds \([\mathrm{s}]\). The source defines KPIs as “foundational actions or movements that identify reasons for success or failure” [PAGE 6], so each \(k\) is treated as an observable behavioral marker of an underlying competence.

A source-compatible skill update operator is:
\[
\mathbf{s}_i(t+\Delta t)
=
\mathbf{s}_i(t)
+
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\boldsymbol{\eta}\,
\mathbf{a}_i(\tau)
\odot
\left[\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{s}_i(\tau)\right]
\,d\tau
-
\int_t^{t+\Delta t}
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,
f_i(\tau)
\odot
\mathbf{s}_i(\tau)
\,d\tau .
\]
Here \(\Delta t\) is in seconds, \(\mathbf{a}_i(\tau)\in[0,1]^K\) is task engagement, \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) has units \([\mathrm{s}^{-1}]\), \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) has units \([\mathrm{s}^{-1}]\), and \(f_i(\tau)\in[0,1]\) is a dimensionless fatigue index. The first integral models skill acquisition with diminishing returns as mastery approaches \(1\); the second models temporary performance decay from fatigue. This is consistent with the guide’s requirement that athletes execute skills “without undue fatigue relative to the needs of the activity” [PAGE 19].

KPI observations are modeled as Bernoulli variables:
\[
Y_{i,k,n}\in\{0,1\},
\qquad
Y_{i,k,n}\sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{i,k,n}),
\]
where \(n\) indexes an observed trial. A logistic observation model is:
\[
p_{i,k,n}
=
\sigma\left(
\alpha_k + \beta_k s_{i,k}(t_n) + \gamma_k z_{i,n}
\right),
\]
with \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\), \(\alpha_k\) and \(\beta_k\) dimensionless coefficients, and \(z_{i,n}\) a standardized disturbance such as fatigue, surface quality, or emotional state. The information value of a KPI is:
\[
I(Y_{i,k};S_k)
=
H(Y_{i,k}) - H(Y_{i,k}\mid S_k),
\]
measured in bits. This formalizes the guide’s use of KPIs as diagnostic signals rather than merely descriptive labels [PAGE 6].

For passing, a simplified projectile model may be used for a released football:
\[
x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\,t,
\qquad
y(t)=y_0+v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac{1}{2}gt^2,
\]
with \(v_0\) in \([\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}]\), \(\theta\) in radians, \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and \(y_0\) the release height in meters. The flight time to a target height \(y_{\mathrm{target}}\) is:
\[
t_f
=
\frac{
v_0\sin\theta
+
\sqrt{
(v_0\sin\theta)^2 + 2g(y_0-y_{\mathrm{target}})
}
}{g}.
\]
The horizontal distance is:
\[
d = v_0\cos\theta\, t_f.
\]
This model is valid under the assumption of negligible aerodynamic lift and drag. It breaks down when spiral stabilization, wind, or ball deformation materially changes the trajectory. The guide’s stage-specific distance goals, such as “more than 20 yards” for Stage 4 place-kick directional control [PAGE 162], are expressed in yards; for SI conversion, \(1\ \mathrm{yard}=0.9144\ \mathrm{m}\).

\[
\mathbf{J}
=
\int_{t_{\mathrm{contact,0}}}^{t_{\mathrm{contact,1}}}
\mathbf{F}(t)\,dt
=
m_b\left(\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{release}}-\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{initial}}\right),
\]
with \(\mathbf{J}\) in newton-seconds \([\mathrm{N\,s}]\), \(\mathbf{F}\) in newtons \([\mathrm{N}]\), and velocity in \([\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}]\). This is a source-consistent physical abstraction of the progression steps “Pre-Pass Position,” “Posture,” “Arm Mechanics,” and “Finish” [PAGE 11-12].

For blocking and tackling, the relevant contact variable is horizontal impulse. Define effective mass \(m_{\mathrm{eff}}\) in kilograms, target velocity change \(\Delta v\) in \([\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}]\), coefficient of friction \(\mu\), and gravitational acceleration \(g\). The required impulse to displace or stop an opponent is approximated by:
\[
J_{\mathrm{req}}
=
m_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta v.
\]
The available propulsive impulse through the feet is:
\[
J_{\mathrm{avail}}
=
\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
\left[
F_{\mathrm{leg}}(t)
-
\mu m_{\mathrm{eff}}g
\right]dt,
\]
with \(F_{\mathrm{leg}}\) in newtons. A conservative contact gate is:
\[
G_{\mathrm{contact}}
=
\begin{cases}
1, & J_{\mathrm{avail}}\ge J_{\mathrm{req}} + J_{\mathrm{margin}},\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
This aligns with the C.U.F.F. blocking model, where force is generated by “the uncoiling of the hips into the arms and focused out through the densest part of the palm” [PAGE 101]. It also aligns with tackling KPIs such as “Cleats in the Ground” and “Leg Drive” [PAGE 167].

Fatigue may be represented by mechanical work:
\[
W_i
=
\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
\mathbf{F}_i(t)\cdot\mathbf{v}_i(t)\,dt,
\]
with \(W_i\) in joules \([\mathrm{J}]\). A dimensionless fatigue state can be defined as:
\[
f_i
=
1-\exp\left(-\frac{W_i}{W_{\mathrm{ref},i}}\right),
\]
where \(W_{\mathrm{ref},i}\) is an athlete-specific reference work capacity in joules. When \(W_i \ll W_{\mathrm{ref},i}\), fatigue is negligible; when \(W_i \gg W_{\mathrm{ref},i}\), fatigue approaches saturation and movement quality degrades. This formalizes the guide’s repeated concern with “biomechanically correct” execution under increasing tempo [PAGE 19, PAGE 62, PAGE 109, PAGE 181].

A full contact-readiness gate can be written as:
\[
G_{\mathrm{contact},i}
=
\prod_{r\in\mathcal{R}}
\mathbf{1}\left[p_{i,r}\ge \tau_r\right]
\cdot
\mathbf{1}\left[J_{\mathrm{avail},i}\ge J_{\mathrm{req},i}+J_{\mathrm{margin},i}\right],
\]
where \(\mathcal{R}\) is the set of relevant progression stages, such as track, prepare, connect, accelerate, and finish [PAGE 167]. This is a formal representation of the source’s instruction to cover basics before progressing to contact [PAGE 91]. Breakdown occurs when KPI observation noise exceeds true skill signal, when fatigue collapses movement quality, or when biomechanical capacity is below the contact demand.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage of this document is a codified long-term athlete development framework. The guide positions itself within a broader shift from early competitive scoring toward developmentally appropriate exposure. It explicitly says that Stage 1 has “no scoreboard nor is there formal competition” [PAGE 9], which retro-causally implies that later competitive complexity is not assumed to be intrinsically beneficial for early learners. The progression is therefore not merely a sequence of drills; it is a theory of when competition becomes informative rather than distorting.

A second lineage is motor-learning theory. The guide’s repeated use of “progression,” “KPI,” and “drills to support” [PAGE 6, PAGE 11] implies a hierarchical decomposition of complex skills into observable subskills. The developmental checklists function as gatekeeping criteria. For example, Stage 1 requires “throw underhand while stepping forward with the opposite foot” and “catch a ball with their hands, not trapping it or cradling against the body” [PAGE 10]. These are not abstract performance goals; they are control laws for future skill acquisition. If the early controller is absent, later throw mechanics become unstable.

A third lineage is contact safety pedagogy. Stage 3 marks the introduction of more formal contact, but only after preparatory competence: “Young athletes just starting out in tackle football must show they possess the required skills against air and soft-sided bags before progressing to controlled contact and ultimately full contact” [PAGE 91]. This creates a retro-causal constraint: the existence of full contact in later stages forces earlier stages to be evaluated as safety prerequisites, not merely as skill-building activities.

Multi-week traps emerge from the same lineage. The first trap is “early technicalization”: treating a child as a miniature adult and introducing advanced blocking fits, complex routes, or high-intensity contact before the foundational movement alphabet is stable. The guide counters this by emphasizing “movement ABCs – agility, balance, coordination and speed” [PAGE 47] before sport-specific complexity. The second trap is “maturity misclassification.” The guide warns that some children are “already beginning to enter puberty while others remain immature both physically and emotionally” [PAGE 47], and later that “the range of maturity levels are the most varied at this stage” [PAGE 161]. A coach who interprets physical size as global readiness may overexpose a late-maturing athlete to contact or overload. The third trap is “fatigue disguised as technique failure.” The guide’s repeated language about “undue fatigue” [PAGE 19, PAGE 62, PAGE 109, PAGE 181] implies that apparent technical breakdown may be an energy-system constraint, not a motor-learning failure. If a coach corrects technique while ignoring fatigue, the multi-week progression can invert: the athlete practices failure under duress, consolidating compensatory patterns.

The deepest genealogical point is that the guide treats development as non-linear. The same skill labels recur across stages, but their meaning changes. “Posture” in Stage 1 is a basic neutral throwing position [PAGE 11]; in Stage 2 it includes “Drop the Hips” [PAGE 49]; in Stage 3 it includes “Cleats in the Grass” [PAGE 93]; in Stage 4 it includes “Sink the Hips” and load transfer [PAGE 163]. This is a retro-causal warning: a coach cannot teach Stage 4 cues to a Stage 1 athlete merely by naming them. The cue is meaningful only when the underlying biomechanical and perceptual prerequisites exist.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source supports a recursive theory-of-mind model because coaching is not merely instruction; it is belief management. At Level 0, the athlete perceives a drill. At Level 1, the coach models the athlete’s perception: is the athlete confused, afraid, bored, or confident? At Level 2, the athlete models the coach’s expectation: does the coach think I am unsafe, weak, or ready? At Level 3, the coach models the athlete’s model of the coach, adjusting language and task difficulty to preserve agency.

The crisis state is overmatching. The guide states: “A less skilled player who is overmatched consistently will lose confidence, which often can lead to giving up the sport” [PAGE 91]. In recursive terms, the athlete’s belief updates from “I can learn this” to “I am being exposed as incompetent.” This can cause habit regression: under threat, the athlete abandons newly learned posture or footwork and reverts to older, safer, but less efficient movement patterns. For example, a receiver who should “run through the catch” [PAGE 96] may instead stop, cradle the ball, or turn away from contact because the nervous system prioritizes threat avoidance over technical execution.

The counter-trap is coach overcorrection. A coach observing regression may respond by drilling the failing skill more intensely, often with more contact, more speed, or more public correction. The coach’s Level-2 belief is: “If I increase repetition, the athlete will regain control.” The athlete’s Level-2 belief may become: “Every repetition is evidence that I am failing.” This is a recursive trap because the coach’s attempt to restore competence increases the athlete’s threat appraisal, which further degrades performance. The guide’s matchup warning is directly relevant: “A player with more refined skills who is allowed to exploit matchups may gain regular success but is not improving as rapidly as he or she would against someone of equal size, strength and talent” [PAGE 91]. The correct response is not maximum repetition but calibrated challenge.

The clock dimension is the season calendar. Coaches often operate under a hidden assumption: “We must be game-ready by week N.” This creates pressure to skip gates. The guide counters with stage-gated checklists and practice plans that allocate time to warm-up, skill builders, activity, hydration, team time, and cool down [PAGE 20]. The clock can be formalized as a deadline \(T_{\mathrm{season}}\). If the required skill completion time \(T_{\mathrm{req}}\) exceeds \(T_{\mathrm{season}}\), the rational response is to reduce scope, not to skip safety gates. Skipping gates may produce short-term compliance but increases variance in execution and reduces long-term retention.

The recursive equilibrium desired by the source is: athlete believes coach believes athlete can succeed; coach believes athlete can safely progress; athlete adopts challenge as information rather than judgment. In equations, let \(b_i(t)\in[0,1]\) be the athlete’s self-efficacy belief, \(c_i(t)\in[0,1]\) the coach’s belief in athlete readiness, and \(r_i(t)\in[0,1]\) the athlete’s perceived relational safety. A stable coaching equilibrium requires:
\[
b_i(t+1) \approx \phi\left(c_i(t), r_i(t), p_{\mathrm{success},i}(t)\right),
\]
where \(\phi\) is increasing in credible coach belief, relational safety, and calibrated success probability. If \(p_{\mathrm{success},i}(t)\) is too low, \(b_i\) decays. If \(p_{\mathrm{success},i}(t)\) is too high, challenge value decays. The guide’s Stage 3 matchup warning is therefore not merely motivational advice; it is a recursive control law for maintaining learning without inducing threat.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto occurs when a higher-level coaching objective is blocked by a lower-level physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraint. The source provides explicit physical/biomechanical gates for contact. It does not provide explicit weather parameters, so weather vetoes must be handled as external physical constraints rather than direct textual requirements.

First, the contact-preparation veto is source-entailed. Stage 3 states: “always begin by covering the basics before progressing to player-on-player contact” [PAGE 91]. Therefore, if an athlete cannot demonstrate basic preparation against air or bags, full contact is vetoed. In formal terms:
\[
G_{\mathrm{contact},i}=0
\quad\text{if}\quad
\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}}p_{i,r}<\tau_r.
\]
Here \(\mathcal{R}\) includes track, prepare, connect, accelerate, and finish [PAGE 167]. The veto is not optional because the source makes the sequence normative. If the athlete fails the checklist, the correct adjudication is regression to preparatory drills, not advancement.

Second, the biomechanical veto follows from the blocking and tackling mechanics. The guide requires force to be generated from the hips and transmitted through the hands: “Energy is transferred from the uncoiling of the hips into the arms” [PAGE 101]. If an athlete lacks hip extension, trunk stability, or foot-ground force capacity, then the required impulse cannot be produced safely:
\[
J_{\mathrm{avail},i} < J_{\mathrm{req},i}+J_{\mathrm{margin},i}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
G_{\mathrm{contact},i}=0.
\]
This is a physical veto because contact without sufficient impulse control increases the probability of poor fit, missed leverage, or uncontrolled collision. The source’s C.U.F.F. model is effectively a biomechanical safety sequence: coil creates posture, uncoil creates force, fit creates contact geometry, finish secures control [PAGE 100-102].

Third, the fatigue veto is also source-compatible. The guide requires “foundational movement skills in a biomechanically correct manner” even “as tempo increases” [PAGE 19]. If fatigue reduces movement quality, the correct adjudication is to stop or modify the drill. Using the fatigue index:
\[
f_i(t) \to 1
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{movement quality veto}.
\]
This is not a punitive veto; it is a load-management veto. The source’s repeated concern with “undue fatigue” supports this inference [PAGE 19, PAGE 62, PAGE 109, PAGE 181].

Fourth, weather is not specified in the provided pages. However, a physical weather veto can be adjudicated from mechanics. If surface friction \(\mu\) is too low, then:
\[
J_{\mathrm{avail}}
=
\int
\left[
F_{\mathrm{leg}}(t)-\mu m_{\mathrm{eff}}g
\right]dt
\]
may become insufficient for acceleration, deceleration, or stable contact. If visibility \(V\) is too low, then the visual tracking KPIs—“Locate,” “Focus,” and “Track” [PAGE 52, PAGE 96, PAGE 166]—lose reliability. Thus, an external weather veto is:
\[
G_{\mathrm{weather}}=
\mathbf{1}[\mu\ge\mu_{\min}]
\cdot
\mathbf{1}[V\ge V_{\min}].
\]
Because the corpus does not supply \(\mu_{\min}\) or \(V_{\min}\), this veto must be marked as exogenous. The source does not contradict it; it simply does not quantify it.

The final adjudication rule is therefore:
\[
G_{\mathrm{total}}
=
G_{\mathrm{contact}}
\cdot
G_{\mathrm{biomech}}
\cdot
G_{\mathrm{fatigue}}
\cdot
G_{\mathrm{weather}}.
\]
If any factor is zero, advancement to the higher-risk objective is vetoed. This preserves the source’s developmental logic while making explicit the physical constraints that the source states qualitatively rather than numerically.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement learning from verifiable outcomes. The verifiable outcomes are observable KPI compliance, movement quality, participation retention, and safety-gate status. The reward function is not score-based; it is developmentally constrained:
\[
R_i
=
\Delta \mathbf{s}_i
-
c_1\max(0, W_i-W_{\mathrm{ref},i})
-
c_2\,\mathbf{1}[G_{\mathrm{total}}=0]\cdot \text{RiskExposure}
-
c_3\,\mathbf{1}[p_{\mathrm{success},i}<p_{\min}],
\]
with \(\Delta\mathbf{s}_i\) dimensionless skill gain, \(W_i\) in joules, and \(p_{\mathrm{success},i}\) a probability.

Counterfactual veto 1: Contact advancement despite failing preparation.
If the model vetoes contact because \(G_{\mathrm{contact},i}=0\), but the coach proceeds anyway, the verifiable prediction is an increase in failed contact attempts, poorer fit positions, and regression in downstream KPIs. The observable metrics would be: lower probability of successful “initial grasp accuracy” or “initial shoulder accuracy” [PAGE 98], increased repetitions in “learning to fall” or breakdown drills [PAGE 112, PAGE 249], and higher coach-flagged technical breakdowns. If the veto is respected, the prediction is that the athlete’s contact KPIs improve monotonically over the next microcycle because the athlete is drilling against air or bags as required by the source [PAGE 91].

Counterfactual veto 2: Practice-load spike.
If weekly mechanical work \(W_i\) exceeds \(W_{\mathrm{ref},i}\), the model predicts degraded movement quality despite stable coaching. The verifiable signs are: loss of “biomechanically correct” posture under tempo [PAGE 19], increased landing faults, slower change-of-direction execution, and poorer throw follow-through. If the veto is applied and load is reduced, the prediction is recovery of movement quality within the next low-load session. This is verifiable through video review of landing mechanics, throwing finish, and deceleration posture. The source supports the constraint by requiring skills to be performed “without undue fatigue” [PAGE 19] and “in a biomechanically correct manner” [PAGE 62].

Counterfactual veto 3: Overmatched matchup.
If the estimated success probability \(p_{\mathrm{success},i}\) falls below a calibrated lower bound, the model predicts confidence loss and disengagement. The guide explicitly warns that “a less skilled player who is overmatched consistently will lose confidence” [PAGE 91]. Verifiable predictions include: reduced attempt rate, increased avoidance behaviors, poorer self-reported enjoyment, and regression to earlier movement patterns. If the veto is applied by adjusting matchup, drill distance, or contact intensity, the prediction is stabilization of attempt rate and gradual return of KPI compliance. This is not a motivational guess; it is a behavioral control prediction derived from the source’s own matchup warning.

These three vetoes are falsifiable. If an athlete bypasses the contact gate and does not show increased technical breakdown, the contact-gate model is too conservative. If load reduction does not restore movement quality, the fatigue model is mis-specified. If matchup adjustment does not improve engagement, the recursive confidence model is incomplete. The source does not demand blind advancement; it demands stage-appropriate progression, and the verifiable predictions follow directly from that requirement.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
