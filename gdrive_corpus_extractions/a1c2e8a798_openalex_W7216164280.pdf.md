The fourth hop is X–Y projection: GVHMR’s gravity-aligned coordinates are “relatively stable” [PAGE 3], but tactical analysis requires a top-down rink template. The paper projects mesh vertices for the left and right feet back to the broadcast frame, connects them, takes the midpoint as “a stable approximation of the player’s current position on the image plane” [PAGE 3], and maps it through an off-the-shelf homography matrix [PAGE 3].

The gray-zone audit is severe because the paper gives qualitative improvements but not full calibration. First, \(z_{\min}\) is only an “approximate” contact indicator [PAGE 3]; during skating strides, jumps, falls, board contact, or occlusion, the minimum mesh vertex may not be the true ground-contact point. A plane fitted to false contacts can be smooth yet physically wrong. Second, the RANSAC stage is under-specified: the source does not report the inlier residual threshold, minimum inlier count, regularization coefficient, or uncertainty. The kernel-weighted average is a smoother, not a physical contact solver; if a player genuinely leaves the ice, smoothing can impose a ground plane that the player is not touching. A scale offset is not identical to a ground-plane error; subtracting a fitted plane may remove drift while leaving a global scale bias. The contact tuples are introduced as follows: “For each contact frame \(t\), we sample foot-surface points and form tuples \((x, y, t, z)\), where \((x, y)\) is the horizontal root position, \(z\) the sampled height, and \(t\) the frame index” [PAGE 3]. The stated reason for \(t\) is that “Including \(t\) allows the model to capture slow ground-level variations caused by camera motion or drift” [PAGE 3].

Units and assumptions: if \(x,y,z\) share a common world/mesh unit \(u\), then \(a\) and \(b\) are dimensionless, \(c\) has units \(u\cdot\text{frame}^{-1}\), and \(d\) has units \(u\). If the mesh is metric, \(u\) may be meters, but the provided source does not explicitly certify metric scale; therefore the formal unit should be recorded as unspecified mesh/world units. The design matrix is \(A=[x,y,t,1]\) and the parameter vector is \(\mathbf{p}=[a,b,c,d]^\top\). The least-squares/RANSAC step requires \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=4\). Breakdown regimes: \(\operatorname{rank}(A)<4\) when there are fewer than four independent contact tuples, when all contacts occur at one frame \(t\), or when the \((x,y,t)\) points lie in a degenerate 3D configuration such as a plane or line. In those cases the plane is not identifiable and \(\hat z_t\) is arbitrary. The method also breaks when the “approximate foot–ground contact indicator” [PAGE 3] is false—airborne players, occluded skates, board contact, falls, or identity switches—because the fitted plane then models non-ground points. The source gives no RANSAC residual threshold, minimum inlier count, or regularization coefficient, so the fit’s statistical confidence is unverified.

2. Kernel-weighted temporal fusion. The paper defines
\[
z_t=\frac{\sum_t w_t \hat z_t}{\sum_t w_t},\qquad
w_t=\exp\!\left(-\frac{|t-t_c|}{\tau}\right),
\]
where “\(t_c\) is the window midpoint and \(\tau=L/2\)” [PAGE 3]. The local estimates \(\hat z_t\) come from the overlapping windows of sizes \(L\) and \(2L\) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(L\) and \(\tau\) are in frames; \(w_t\) is dimensionless; \(z_t\) and \(\hat z_t\) are in the same world/mesh units as \(z\). The normalized weights \(w_t/\sum w_t\) are dimensionless and form a discrete weighting over local estimates; they are not calibrated probabilities and carry no reported information units such as bits or nats.

Assumptions: the ground height changes slowly relative to \(\tau\), and the local plane estimates are approximately unbiased. Breakdown regimes: \(\tau=0\) makes the exponential undefined at \(t=t_c\); very small \(\tau\) makes the estimate noisy and nearly frame-local; very large \(\tau\) over-smooths genuine ground-height changes and introduces lag. If the window contains too few valid contacts, or if RANSAC produces outlier planes, the weighted average propagates those outliers. The method also assumes overlapping windows are comparable; the source does not report how local estimates are normalized across window sizes \(\{L,2L\}\) beyond the kernel average.

3. Root-height correction. The paper computes
\[
\Delta z_t = z_t - z_{\min},
\]
“yielding a smooth, ground-aligned vertical trajectory” [PAGE 3]. Units: \(\Delta z_t\), \(z_t\), and \(z_{\min}\) are in world/mesh units. Assumption: \(z_{\min}\) is a valid foot–ground contact measurement for the frame. Breakdown regimes: if the player is airborne, if the minimum vertex belongs to a non-foot body part, if the feet are occluded, or if the mesh scale is inconsistent, then \(\Delta z_t\) corrects toward a false ground and can suppress true vertical motion. The source’s own qualitative note that initial Z values “are not centered around zero, which we attribute to inconsistencies in scale across the training data” [PAGE 4] indicates that the correction is not a full metric calibration.

4. Homography projection. The paper uses
\[
[x,y,1]^\top = H\,[x',y',1]^\top,
\]
where “\((x',y')\) denotes the player’s position in the broadcast frame, \((x,y)\) denotes the corresponding position on the rink template, and \(H\) is the estimated \(3\times3\) homography matrix” [PAGE 3]. The image-plane position is obtained by connecting the left and right foot vertices and taking the midpoint: “By connecting these two foot points, we obtain a line segment whose midpoint provides a stable approximation of the player’s current position on the image plane” [PAGE 3]. The homography is “predicted by the off-the-shelf model [20]” [PAGE 3].

Units: \(x',y'\) are in broadcast-image pixels; \(x,y\) are in rink-template units; \(H\) is dimensionless up to a global scale. In expanded form the actual projected coordinates are
\[
x=\frac{h_{11}x'+h_{12}y'+h_{13}}{h_{31}x'+h_{32}y'+h_{33}},\qquad
y=\frac{h_{21}x'+h_{22}y'+h_{23}}{h_{31}x'+h_{32}y'+h_{33}}.
\]
Breakdown regimes: if \(h_{31}x'+h_{32}y'+h_{33}=0\), the point lies on the horizon line and the projection is undefined; if \(\det(H)=0\) or \(H\) is rank-deficient, the mapping collapses; if the foot midpoint is occluded, misidentified, or belongs to a player whose identity was switched by the tracker, the projected rink coordinate is wrong even when \(H\) is correct. The source reports that projected positions “accurately align with the template” [PAGE 4], but it does not report homography reprojection error, denominator margins, or per-frame uncertainty.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from optimization-based human mesh recovery to learned regression, then to video transformers and world-grounded human motion, then to hockey-specific tracking and rink registration. The paper traces HMR from parametric models such as SMPL [12] and SMPL-X [17] through optimization methods, then to the canonical HMR CNN regressor [8], then to ViT-backed video models such as HMR 2.0 [6], Multi-HMR [1], TRAM [26], CameraHMR [16], and world-grounded systems [21,22] [PAGE 2]. GVHMR [21] is the baseline because it “incorporates a gravity-based coordinate refinement module to enhance temporal stability” [PAGE 2]. The hockey layer comes from an off-the-shelf hockey-specific tracker [18] and rink registration [20] [PAGE 2–3]. Retro causally, the later refinement modules justify the earlier warnings: generic HMR was evaluated on “general-purpose datasets such as Human3.6M [7] and 3DPW [25], leaving their robustness in sports-specific contexts largely unexplored” [PAGE 2]. The paper’s own tracker replacement is therefore not a cosmetic engineering choice; it is a domain-shift correction that retroactively explains why the baseline’s downstream pose and mesh stages degrade.

Multi-week traps are deployment traps. First, short-clip calibration is not multi-game calibration: the paper evaluates clips from “a few seconds to approximately one minute” at “1280 × 720 at 30 fps” [PAGE 4], while multi-week deployment encounters different arenas, camera cuts, lighting, jersey colors, and rink markings. A homography estimated on one broadcast view can silently fail on another. Second, vertical drift is cumulative: the paper explicitly identifies “accumulated per-frame errors” [PAGE 2], so a method that looks stable over 120 frames may still accumulate bias over a full game if the plane model or scale prior is wrong. Third, tracker identity switches are a latent multi-week trap: a single swap can corrupt both \(z_{\min}\) and the foot-midpoint homography projection, and the source does not report identity-switch metrics. Fourth, the scale inconsistency note [PAGE 4] is a warning against interpreting “close to zero” as metric truth; a model can be temporally smooth and still have a global scale bias. The correct genealogical reading is that the paper adds geometric post-processing to a lineage that has strong general-video priors but weak sports-specific calibration.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer treats the pipeline as an agent with habits, counter-habits, and a clock. The crisis habit is regression to the generic tracker’s behavior under domain shift: the paper says the tracking module “has not been trained on ice hockey data and thus fails to produce reliable player tracks” [PAGE 2], and that downstream pose, feature extraction, and mesh reconstruction “also suffer in accuracy and temporal consistency” [PAGE 2]. That is a crisis habit because the model does what it learned on generic video rather than what hockey requires. The paper’s counter-trap is to replace the tracker with a hockey-specific off-the-shelf model [PAGE 2], but a second counter-trap appears if one believes tracker replacement alone solves vertical drift. It does not: the paper still needs a separate Z-axis refinement because per-frame errors “cause noticeable drift along the vertical (Z) axis” [PAGE 2]. A third counter-trap is over-smoothing: if the kernel clock \(\tau=L/2\) is too large, the model “believes” the ground is more stable than it is and suppresses real jumps, falls, or skating transitions.

The clock is explicit: windows of sizes \(\{L,2L\}\) with \(L=15\), local estimates \(\hat z_t\), and kernel weights with \(\tau=L/2\) [PAGE 3]. The model’s temporal belief is therefore a weighted memory of nearby ground-plane estimates. If the contact detector is wrong, the model’s belief is wrong in a smooth, confident way—the worst kind of failure for downstream tactical analysis. The recursive check is: does the model know when it does not know? The source does not report uncertainty or veto logic. It reports qualitative stabilization: Player 1’s upward trend and Player 3’s jitter are reduced, and trajectories remain “stable and consistently close to zero” [PAGE 4]. That is a behavioral claim, not a calibrated self-assessment. A counter-trap against false confidence is to require contact-set rank, homography denominator margin, and tracker identity consistency before accepting a refined frame.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: the refinement is admissible only if the fitted ground plane is supported by true foot–ground contacts. The paper’s contact proxy is \(z_{\min}\), an “approximate foot–ground contact indicator” [PAGE 3]. If the player is airborne, the minimum vertex is not a ground contact; if the player is occluded, the minimum vertex may belong to another object; if the mesh scale is inconsistent [PAGE 4], the plane’s intercept is biased. A physical veto should trigger when the plane fit has fewer than four independent contact tuples, when \(\operatorname{rank}([x,y,t,1])<4\), or when RANSAC inliers are insufficient. The source does not provide the inlier threshold, so the veto must be implemented as an external verifier rather than claimed from the paper.

Biomechanical veto: the foot midpoint is a geometric proxy, not a measured center of mass. The paper says the midpoint “provides a stable approximation of the player’s current position on the image plane” [PAGE 3]. That is reasonable for a skater in a neutral stance, but it can fail during a split stance, a crossover, a fall, a board collision, or a jump. A biomechanical veto should reject frames where left and right foot vertices are not both visible, where the foot midpoint moves faster than plausible skating kinematics, or where the root-height correction \(\Delta z_t\) would place the feet below the estimated ice plane. The paper does not report such a constraint, but it does claim “physically plausible foot–ground contact” [PAGE 2–3], so the constraint is part of the paper’s own stated standard.

Weather veto: ice hockey is indoor, so direct weather is not a modeled layer; the paper provides no weather variables and no weather calibration. The analogous environmental veto is broadcast and ice-surface optics: glare, lighting changes, camera pan/tilt/zoom, cuts, and rink-marking visibility. These affect the homography [20] and the foot-vertex detection. A weather or environment claim cannot veto the method on meteorological grounds, but an optical veto can: if the homography denominator is near zero or the rink registration is invalid, the X–Y projection must be vetoed. Cross-layer adjudication therefore yields a hierarchy: physical contact support first, biomechanical plausibility second, camera/homography validity third, and only then accept the refined mesh. The source’s qualitative claims—stable Z trajectories [PAGE 4] and accurate template alignment [PAGE 4]—are consistent with this hierarchy but do not replace the missing quantitative veto tests.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
I interpret RLVR here as verifier-grounded reward: a refinement is rewarded only when an external check can verify the geometric precondition. Three counterfactual veto scenarios are directly testable against the source’s operators.

Veto 1: degenerate contact-plane rank. Construct a synthetic or mined sequence where all sampled contact tuples come from one frame, or where the \((x,y,t)\) points are collinear/planar so that \(\operatorname{rank}([x,y,t,1])<4\). The paper’s plane \(z=ax+by+ct+d\) [PAGE 3] is then unidentifiable. Prediction: local \(\hat z_t\) estimates will have large variance or arbitrary values; the kernel average \(z_t\) [PAGE 3] will not reduce vertical error; post-refinement Z trajectory will not be closer to the estimated ground than the pre-refinement trajectory. Verifiable metrics: rank of the design matrix, condition number, and pre/post Z RMS. If rank \(<4\), the refinement must be vetoed rather than rewarded.

Veto 2: homography horizon crossing. For the projection \([x,y,1]^\top=H[x',y',1]^\top\) [PAGE 3], choose or detect a frame where the foot midpoint approaches the horizon line, \(h_{31}x'+h_{32}y'+h_{33}\to0\). The projected rink coordinate diverges. Prediction: the template trajectory will show a displacement far larger than a plausible skating displacement in one frame, and the reprojection error will spike even if the mesh itself is stable. Verifiable metrics: denominator magnitude, per-frame template displacement, and reprojection error. When the denominator margin is below a chosen threshold, the homography projection must be vetoed for that frame.

Veto 3: tracker identity switch or foot occlusion. The paper relies on a hockey-specific tracker [18] and on left/right foot vertices for the midpoint [PAGE 3]. If the tracker swaps identities or one foot is occluded, the midpoint can belong to the wrong player or the wrong body part. Prediction: the template trajectory will jump between two spatially separated tracks; \(\Delta z_t=z_t-z_{\min}\) [PAGE 3] will use a \(z_{\min}\) from the wrong player; and the post-refinement Z curves for the affected identities will become inconsistent with neighboring frames. Verifiable metrics: identity-switch count, foot-vertex visibility, frame-to-frame trajectory jump, and cross-player Z consistency. If identity consistency fails, the frame must be vetoed. These three vetoes are counterfactual because they predict exactly when the paper’s qualitative gains [PAGE 4] should disappear; they are verifiable because they reduce to rank, denominator margin, and identity/trajectory consistency checks rather than subjective visual judgment.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
