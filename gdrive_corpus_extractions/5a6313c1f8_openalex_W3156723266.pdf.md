<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source reports no single unified injury-risk equation. It reports a pipeline of operators: data splitting, preprocessing, resampling, model fitting, hyperparameter tuning, feature selection or dimensionality reduction, evaluation, and feature-importance extraction [PAGE 2] [PAGE 3] [PAGE 8]. Formal statements below are standard definitions consistent with the review’s reported quantities; they are not invented injury ratios.

1. Independence operator for train and test:
\[
D=D_{\mathrm{train}}\cup D_{\mathrm{test}},\qquad D_{\mathrm{train}}\cap D_{\mathrm{test}}=\varnothing .
\]
Units: sets of athlete records or athlete-time records. Assumption: records are independent at the intended deployment level. Breakdown: repeated observations per athlete violate this when the athlete appears in both sets; the review identifies this as a violation in four papers [PAGE 8].

2. Area under the ROC curve:
\[
\mathrm{AUC}=\Pr\left(S(Y=1)>S(Y=0)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Pr\left(S(Y=1)=S(Y=0)\right).
\]
Units: dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). Assumption: a continuous or ordinal score \(S\), with ties handled by the half-credit term. Breakdown: AUC is undefined when only one outcome class is present; it is prevalence-invariant for ranking but does not guarantee calibration; dependence between training and test records biases the estimate upward. The review reports AUC from 0.52 to 0.87 [PAGE 1] and “AUC values for predicting the outcome ranged between 0.64 and 0.87” in the higher-performing subset [PAGE 9].

3. Confusion-matrix metrics:
\[
\mathrm{Se}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{Sp}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},
\]
\[
\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
F_1=\frac{2\,\mathrm{PPV}\,\mathrm{Se}}{\mathrm{PPV}+\mathrm{Se}}.
\]
Units: dimensionless proportions in \([0,1]\). Assumptions: a fixed classification threshold and complete outcome ascertainment. Breakdown: each is undefined when its denominator is zero; \(F_1\) is undefined when both PPV and sensitivity are zero; PPV and F1 are prevalence-sensitive under injury/non-injury imbalance. The review reports accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and f1-score as primary measures [PAGE 9] [PAGE 12].

4. Brier score and logarithmic loss, which the review explicitly recommends for probabilistic exactness [PAGE 13]:
\[
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(p_i-y_i)^2,
\]
\[
\mathrm{LL}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\right].
\]
Units: Brier score is in squared probability units, dimensionless in \([0,1]\); log loss is in nats per observation. Assumptions: \(p_i\in(0,1)\) and \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\). Breakdown: log loss has an exact singularity at \(p_i=0\) with \(y_i=1\), or at \(p_i=1\) with \(y_i=0\); Brier score does not blow up but still penalizes miscalibration. Neither can repair leakage or non-independent observations.

5. SHAP attribution used in the reviewed Rommers et al. study [PAGE 9]:
\[
\phi_j=\sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{j\}}
\frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}
\left[v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\right].
\]
Units: model-output units, usually probability or log-odds depending on \(v\). Assumptions: a cooperative-game attribution over feature coalitions. Breakdown: correlated features make attributions non-identifiable; the value depends on the background dataset; it is not a causal effect. The review also reports frequency across trees, LASSO non-zero coefficients, and mean decrease in Gini [PAGE 9] [PAGE 12].

6. Quality operators:
\[
Q_{\mathrm{NOS}}\in\{0,\ldots,8\}
\]
is described as an ordinal score with “a maximum of 8 points” [PAGE 3], yet the GRADE rule uses “mean NOS Score was less than 6 out of 9” [PAGE 4]. This is an internal gray-zone inconsistency. The GRADE operation is ordinal downgrading from moderate certainty: imprecision when the analysed sample is below 300 participants, and risk of bias when the mean NOS score is below 6 [PAGE 4]. Units: evidence-certainty levels, not physical units. Breakdown: ordinal labels cannot be averaged as if they were continuous measurements.

7. Causal backdoor veto for an omitted layer:
\[
P(Y|do(X))=\sum_{z}P(Y|X,z)P(z),
\]
with bias
\[
B(x)=P_{\mathrm{obs}}(Y|X=x)-P_{\mathrm{do}}(Y|X=x).
\]
Units: probability. Assumption: \(Z\) satisfies the backdoor condition and is measured. Breakdown: if \(Z\) is unmeasured, \(P(Y|do(X))\) is not identified from observed associations; if \(|B(x)|\) exceeds a pre-specified clinical tolerance, the causal claim is vetoed. The review’s feature list lacks a weather layer entirely [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is explicitly recursive. The review’s background cites the dynamic etiology model of Meeuwisse et al. and the complex-systems approach of Bittencourt et al. [PAGE 14], and it states that “sports injuries are a consequence of complex interactions of multiple risk factors and inciting events making a comprehensive model necessary” [PAGE 1]. It further states that “predictive modelling should not only focus on the prediction of the occurrence of an injury itself but, moreover, it should try to identify injury risk at an individual level and implement interventions to mitigate the level of risk [28]” [PAGE 1]. That lineage is retrocausal in the sense that later ML reviews reinterpret earlier statistical risk-factor work as either precursor or contamination. Van Eetvelde et al. explicitly exclude studies “if the used approach was rather statistical than ML” [PAGE 3], and they criticize Claudino et al. because including Bayesian logistic regression and single decision trees means “studies which were performed before the era of AI/ML and including statistical methods like linear or logistic regression would need to be considered to get a complete overview of the topic. This would seriously dilute true ML approaches” [PAGE 13]. The multi-week traps are built into the dataset structure: Carey et al. use “Training load variables (+ Exponential Weighted Moving Average features and Acute Chronic Workload Ratio features)” with 13,867 player matches and training sessions [PAGE 5]; McCullagh and Whitfort use player weeks [PAGE 6]; Thornton et al. use player days [PAGE 7]; Rossi et al. use player training sessions [PAGE 6]. These are not independent rows. A model that ignores athlete identity and time ordering can learn within-player autocorrelation and then mistake it for injury prediction. The review reinforces this with previous injury and training-load findings: previous injury is reported twice as important, and “lower training load after previous injury might indicate a not fully recovered athlete and can hence be considered being a risk factor after previous injury [36]” [PAGE 9]. Thus the multi-week trap is not merely statistical; it is a clinical time-order trap in which recovery state, load history, and repeated observations are entangled.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when decision-makers face uncertain model output and revert to familiar screening rituals. The review notes that some results are “in opposition with several authors who found that screening tests were not successful in predicting sports injuries [40, 43]” [PAGE 9], yet it also reports strong sensitivity and specificity for a pre-season screening model [PAGE 9]. The recursive risk is that clinicians remember the favorable screening result and forget the failed-screening literature. Counter-trap appears when a high-risk flag triggers load reduction or modified training. If the model was trained on observational load, it cannot identify the effect of the intervention; worse, the review notes that lower training load after previous injury may mark an incompletely recovered athlete [PAGE 9]. A coach who reacts to a red flag by unloading an athlete may therefore create a new exposure pattern that the model never validated. The review’s recommendation to report probabilities rather than only high/low labels is directly relevant: “From a clinical point of view, it could be more informative to know the probability of injury instead of only the classification into a high or low risk profile” [PAGE 13]. Clock mismatch is the third trap. The review insists that test data must be “unseen data” [PAGE 2] and that “the test dataset has to be seen as future data” [PAGE 8]. But clinical staff, coaches, and athletes operate on different clocks: pre-season screening, in-season weekly monitoring, and return-to-play windows. A model validated by random cross-validation may be answering a different clock than the practitioner’s decision. The review’s claim that future models “might help coaches, physical trainers and medical practitioners in the decision-making process for injury prevention” [PAGE 9] is therefore conditional on communicating uncertainty, time horizon, and the modifiable-versus-non-modifiable status of predictors; otherwise the recursive loop becomes overtrust, overintervention, and regression to habit.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer is partially admissible but not proven. Training load, sleep, mood, stress, fatigue, and perceived performance appear in the reviewed predictor sets [PAGE 6], and training-load features include EWMA and ACWR [PAGE 5]. However, the review’s own leakage and independence findings weaken causal interpretation [PAGE 8]. The biomechanical layer is also partial: anthropometric measurements, motor coordination, physical fitness, neuromuscular control, asymmetry, knee valgus angle, and body size are represented [PAGE 6] [PAGE 12], but the background states that a comprehensive model must include “a description of body and joint biomechanics at the time of injury [4]” [PAGE 1]. The reviewed studies generally do not contain that injury-time joint biomechanics layer; they contain screening proxies. The weather layer has no admissible proof in this source: no weather, surface, temperature, or environmental variable appears in the predictor lists across Tables 1 and 2 [PAGE 5] [PAGE 6] [PAGE 7]. Therefore a weather veto cannot be adjudicated from this corpus. Using the backdoor veto, if \(Z_{\mathrm{weather}}\) is an unmeasured cause of both exposure and injury, the observed association is not the do-intervention probability, and any weather claim would require external data. Physical and biomechanical vetoes are likewise conditional: if the measured proxy is not time-aligned with the injury event, or if it is measured after the outcome, it cannot serve as a pre-injury veto. The review’s strongest empirical anchors are previous injury, higher training load, and higher body size in youth players, reported twice as important [PAGE 9]; these are admissible risk markers, not proof of a mechanistic veto. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: no weather proof, partial biomechanical proof, and only conditional physical-load proof after leakage-free, athlete-grouped, time-forward validation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto 1: leakage-free nested preprocessing. Verification rule: reproduce the reported high-performing models, fit imputation, discretization, and standardization only inside each training fold, and evaluate on untouched test folds. The review flags four papers with whole-dataset preprocessing or standardization [PAGE 8], including Ayala et al. with reported AUC 0.873 and López-Valenciano et al. with reported AUC 0.747 [PAGE 12]. Prediction: at least one of these two AUCs will fall by more than 0.03 under leakage-free nested validation, and the original claim that the model is ready for future-athlete prediction will be vetoed if the drop exceeds that pre-registered tolerance.

Veto 2: athlete-grouped validation for repeated observations. Verification rule: for studies with multiple observations per athlete—Carey, McCullagh and Whitfort, Rossi, and Thornton [PAGE 8]—split by athlete identity or by future time block, not by row. Prediction: McCullagh’s reported accuracy of 82.9% and Rossi’s reported AUC of 0.76 [PAGE 12] will not both survive; at least one will drop by at least 0.05, and the confidence interval will widen enough to include clinically uninformative performance. This veto is verifiable simply by comparing row-wise versus grouped cross-validation on the same data.

Veto 3: external calibration and probabilistic scoring. Verification rule: pre-register an independent prospective cohort with at least 300 athletes and enough injury events for stable estimation, and evaluate Brier score and log loss in addition to AUC, as the review recommends [PAGE 13]. Prediction: the high f1-score of 85% reported by Rommers et al. and the high AUC of 0.873 reported by Ayala et al. [PAGE 12] will not both replicate; at least one will fail to exceed an AUC of 0.70 in the external cohort, or its calibration will be rejected by a Brier score worse than a prevalence-only baseline. If either occurs, the original performance claim is vetoed as a transportable clinical predictor, leaving only the weaker conclusion that ML may help identify candidate risk factors and generate hypotheses.
