Hop 1 — Identify the primitive quantities Camp actually manipulates. (a) Momentum/energy of contact plays: the flying wedge "getting under headway" [PAGE 129 of 398] is explicitly a build-momentum-before-contact claim, i.e. $\vec P=\sum m_i\vec v_i$. (b) Ballistics of the kicked ball: place-kick, punt, drop-kick described geometrically [PAGE 313–315 of 398], including the "twenty degrees off perpendicular" drop [PAGE 315 of 398]. (c) A discrete resource problem: "advance five yards... in three scrimmages" (1882) [PAGE 61 of 398], later ten yards in three downs — a finite-horizon conversion problem. (d) A signaling channel: early game = 4 plays [PAGE 97 of 398]; by 1910 = "no fewer than sixty possible plays" [PAGE 99 of 398]. (e) An explicit expected-value instruction: "the theory of chances" and the "stake of a touch-down against opponents' stake of four or five yards" [PAGE 225 of 398]. (f) A possession-time ratio: open plays succeed "one out of three," close plays keep the ball "four times as long" [PAGE 116 of 398].

Hop 2 — Map each to a formal operator. Kicks → projectile range with a drag/wind correction. Wedge/tackle → impulse–momentum. Downs → optimal stopping under a turnover penalty. Signals → Shannon information $\log_2 N$. Deception → mutual information $I(X;Y)$. Possession → a renewal-reward ratio. Delayed pass → a timing-window inequality.

Hop 3 — Gray-zone audit. Camp's "$1/3$" and "$\times 4$" are coach's heuristics, not distributions; I treat them as point estimates and flag the variance they hide. The vacuum range law $R=v_0^2\sin2\theta/g$ is only leading-order: at 50-yard punts (≈45.7 m, $v_0\approx22$ m/s) quadratic drag removes 10–25% of range, so the closed form is a breakdown-prone idealization. The "four times as long" claim conflates *possession count* with *possession time* and ignores clock-stoppage. Camp's risk rule ("do not venture such plays within one's own 25-yard line," [PAGE 240 of 398]) is an expected-points argument he never wrote as an inequality — I supply it and label it as my formalization, not his.

Hop 4 — Multi-week trap detection (the commitment story). Camp narrates introducing "tackle-back" plays: the adviser predicts the squad will "first lose confidence, then ambition" and want to revert to drilled plays [PAGE 213 of 398]; by November "almost every man on the team had lost confidence" [PAGE 218 of 398]; the plays are held, then "the final score was 35 to 0" [PAGE 219 of 398]. This is a genuine optimal-stopping/commitment problem with an option value of abandonment that must be *suppressed* — a theory-of-mind regress where the coach models the players modeling the coach's resolve.

Hop 5 — Counter-factual veto construction. Each proposed "always works" strategy is checked against a conservation law or a reward constraint and rejected, with a text-verifiable prediction.

The analysis below is rigorous where the physics permits and explicitly heuristic where Camp's numbers are only estimates.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**1. Ballistics of the kick (place-kick, punt, drop-kick).** Leading-order range on level ground (SI units; $R$ in m, $v_0$ in m·s⁻¹, $g=9.81$ m·s⁻²):
$$R=\frac{v_0^{2}\sin(2\theta)}{g}.$$
Camp's "fifty-yard" punt (≈45.7 m) at $\theta=45^\circ$ requires $v_0=\sqrt{Rg}\approx 21.2$ m·s⁻¹ (≈76 km·h⁻¹). **Breakdown regime:** at these speeds aerodynamic drag is non-negligible; the vacuum formula overestimates range by ~10–25%. Full model with quadratic drag and wind $\vec w$:
$$m\frac{d\vec v}{dt}=-mg\,\hat{\jmath}-\tfrac12\rho C_d A\,\lVert\vec v-\vec w\rVert(\vec v-\vec w),$$
with $\rho\approx1.2$ kg·m⁻³. This formalizes Camp's wind doctrine: "A wind which blows diagonally across the field is by no means to be despised" [PAGE 251 of 398] — a tailwind component adds to $R$, a headwind subtracts. The drop-kick geometry he describes — the ball "leaning off the perpendicular some twenty degrees" [PAGE 315 of 398] — sets the contact point so the impulse vector aligns with the ball's long axis; mis-tilt by angle $\phi$ wastes a fraction $\sim(1-\cos\phi)$ of the impulse into spin.

**2. Momentum / impulse of contact plays.** The flying wedge's "getting under headway" [PAGE 129 of 398] is a momentum accumulator:
$$\vec P_{\text{wedge}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\vec v_i .$$
To arrest a runner of mass $m_1$, velocity $\vec v_1$, a tackler must deliver impulse
$$\vec J=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\vec F\,dt=\Delta\vec p=m_1(\vec v_f-\vec v_1).$$
A perfectly inelastic tackle (they fall together) gives post-impact velocity
$$v_f=\frac{m_1v_1+m_2v_2}{m_1+m_2}.$$
Camp's "getting under headway" raises $\lVert\vec P\rVert$ before contact, which is why the 1910 ban on interlocked interference and pushing "cuts down somewhat the concentrated weight in the force of an attack" [PAGE 302 of 398]: it caps $n$ in the sum, lowering $\lVert\vec P\rVert$ for a fixed per-man effort.

**3. The downs rule as an optimal-stopping / expected-value operator.** Model a series of downs as $N=3$ attempts to gain $D=10$ yards (the 1882 precursor was $D=5$ [PAGE 61 of 398]). Per-play expected yardage:
$$\mathbb E[Y]=p\,G_{+}-(1-p)\,G_{-},$$
where $p$ is conversion probability, $G_+$ the successful gain, $G_-$ the loss on failure. Camp supplies the point estimate $p\approx \tfrac13$ for open plays [PAGE 116 of 398]. Raising $D$ from 5 to 10 lowers $p$ and is his stated mechanism: "the players outgrew the five-yard rule... and are in a fair way to outgrow the ten-yard rule" [PAGE 93 of 398] — i.e. increasing $D$ shrinks the profitable region of pure mass play.

**4. The risk inequality Camp never wrote.** His "theory of chances" instruction [PAGE 225 of 398] and the "own 25-yard line" prohibition [PAGE 240 of 398] formalize as an expected-points veto. For a risky pass:
$$\mathbb E[\Delta\Pi]=p_c\,Y_{\text{gain}}-p_{\text{int}}\,\Pi_{\text{opp}},$$
where $p_c$ is completion probability, $p_{\text{int}}$ the interception probability, and $\Pi_{\text{opp}}$ the expected points the opponent scores off the turnover. Camp's rule = veto when $p_{\text{int}}\Pi_{\text{opp}}>p_cY_{\text{gain}}$; near one's own goal $\Pi_{\text{opp}}$ spikes (short field), which is why he forbids such plays "within one's own 25-yard line" [PAGE 240 of 398].

**5. Information / signal capacity.** With $N$ equally likely signals, information per call is
$$H=\log_2 N\ \text{bits}.$$
Early game, $N=4$ [PAGE 97 of 398] $\Rightarrow H=2.0$ bits. By 1910, $N=60$ [PAGE 99 of 398] $\Rightarrow H=\log_2 60\approx 5.91$ bits — a ~3× increase in the working-memory load per snap, exactly the load that produced the quarterback who reported, "I've forgotten the signals!" [PAGE 367 of 398].

**6. Deception as mutual information.** Let $X$ = actual play, $Y$ = pre-snap formation observed by the defense. Deception quality is measured by
$$I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y).$$
A perfectly masked play drives $I(X;Y)\to 0$ (the formation reveals nothing). Camp's masked/wedge/delayed-pass plays [PAGE 129, 234–236 of 398] are engineering $I(X;Y)\!\downarrow$.

**7. Possession as a renewal-reward ratio.** Camp's claim that open plays succeed "one out of three" while close plays hold the ball "four times as long" [PAGE 116 of 398] is
$$\frac{T_{\text{close}}}{T_{\text{open}}}\approx 4,\qquad p_{\text{open}}\approx\tfrac13,$$
so scoring *rate per unit clock* can favor mass even at lower per-play yield — the tension the whole 1910 code is engineered to resolve.

**8. Delayed-pass timing window.** For Veeder's "Twenty-three" play [PAGE 145 of 398], the ball's flight time is $t_{\text{flight}}=2v_0\sin\theta/g$, and success requires
$$t_{\text{snap}}+t_{\text{fake}}+t_{\text{flight}}\ \ge\ t_{\text{converge}},$$
the time defenders need to collapse on the passer. The fake kick/run inflates the left side until the inequality holds.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Camp gives an explicit lineage of the ball-carrying code: the Greek *Pheninda* (statue to Aristonico Caristo), Lacedaemonian *Episcyrus*, Roman *Harpastum*, Florentine *Calcio* (27 men; revived 1898 after "one hundred and sixty years of quiescence") [PAGE 39–40 of 398]; the English street game proscribed by Edward II (1314), Henry VI (1457), Henry VII (1491) and called "a bloody and murthering practice" by Stubbs (1583) [PAGE 41, 166 of 398]; Rugby Union codified 1871 [PAGE 42 of 398]; Harvard's Rugby importation from Canada (1875–76) [PAGE 42, 48 of 398]. The American *differentiation* is the true causal spine: the "scrummage" diverged from the Rugby "scrum" via *heeling out*, then the foot-snap, then the two-hand snap-back [PAGE 50 of 398] — each step an information- and control-theoretic upgrade (deterministic ball delivery replaces the random "ball popping out" Camp explicitly complains of [PAGE 362 of 398]).

The multi-week trap is structural, not incidental. Camp's "coaching reversal" parable — an 18-to-4 loss, then 12-to-6, then a 20-to-0 win across three Saturdays against the *same* opponent [PAGE 32, 37–38 of 398] — is his proof that "It is not a game of brute force, or speed... It is a problem to be solved by the means of eleven men working together" [PAGE 38 of 398]. The genealogical trap: every safety valve (five-yard rule, then ten-yard rule) is later defeated by coaches re-deriving mass play once the forward pass pulls the defensive half-back off the tackle [PAGE 79, 93–94 of 398]. Each fix seeds the next crisis — a genuine feedback loop the rule-makers kept chasing.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Camp is the first American coach to formalize *recursive belief under a clock*. Three coupled mechanisms:

(a) **Crisis habit regression.** Under pressure a squad reverts to drilled primitives. The adviser predicts the team will be "desirous of dropping the plays and going back to the simple ones upon which they have been drilled" [PAGE 213 of 398], and by November "almost every man on the team had lost confidence" [PAGE 218 of 398]. Formally: as the option value of the new plays decays toward zero mid-season, players' posterior belief $P(\text{plays work}\mid \text{early losses})$ collapses — a Bayesian regression to the trained prior.

(b) **Counter-trap (deception).** The offense exploits the defense's regression by driving mutual information $I(X;Y)\to 0$: the fake kick, criss-cross, and delayed pass [PAGE 229, 234–236 of 398] all induce the *defender* to commit on a false posterior. Camp's DeWitt/Chadwick anecdotes [PAGE 179–186 of 398] are worked examples: the defense "opened the line" precisely because it modeled the play wrong.

(c) **The clock as a fourth agent.** Possession time is weaponized: hold the ball "four times as long" [PAGE 116 of 398] and the opponent's scoring *opportunities per game* shrink. This is why the 1910 code inserts 3-minute intermissions and goal changes every 15 minutes [PAGE 152 of 398] — the rule-makers attacking the clock strategy directly.

The regress is three-deep: coach models players modeling the opponent modeling the coach's resolve — and the winning move is a *commitment device* ("burned their bridges," [PAGE 218 of 398]) that makes abandonment more costly than persistence. That is precisely why the payoff "35 to 0... the plays had 'made good'" [PAGE 219 of 398] validates the commitment only ex post.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A physical/biomechanical veto overrides any strategic claim that violates a conservation law or a hard environmental bound.

- **Momentum veto.** No amount of "brains" repeals $\vec J=\Delta\vec p$. Camp concedes the heavy, overgrown center-man has an irreducible role because "pounds are... an advantage in this middle position" [PAGE 302 of 398]. A strategy of pure finesse at the point of contact is vetoed by impulse balance; the 1910 anti-interlock rule only lowers $\lVert\vec P\rVert$ by capping $n$, it does not zero it.

- **Environmental veto (weather).** Camp's frozen-ground game, "thermometer below zero on a ground... like cobblestones" [PAGE 113 of 398], and Hare kicking into "a perfect hurricane of wind" where "nobody but Hare could even get the ball over Pennsylvania's own rush-line" [PAGE 345 of 398] are projectile-regime vetoes: when the headwind term $-\tfrac12\rho C_dA\lVert\vec v-\vec w\rVert(\vec v-\vec w)$ dominates, net $R\le 0$ and the kick cannot clear the line. The physics forbids what strategy requests.

- **Field-geometry veto.** The 100-yd-long, ~53-yd-wide grid with 10-yd end zones [PAGE 227, 229 of 398] bounds every play. A delayed pass demanding $t_{\text{snap}}+t_{\text{fake}}+t_{\text{flight}}\ge t_{\text{converge}}$ [PAGE 142–147 of 398] is vetoed if the geometry leaves too little vertical room for $t_{\text{flight}}$; this is exactly why Camp insists the fake be launched "anywhere within forty yards or so" [PAGE 146 of 398] of goal — the geometry licenses the timing window.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable veto scenarios, each checked against a physical constraint, each with a text-grounded prediction.

**Veto 1 — "The unstoppable mass play."** Claim: a perfectly interlocked wedge with maximal $\lVert\vec P\rVert$ always converts. *Verification:* under ten-yard/three-downs, $\mathbb E[Y]=pG_+-(1-p)G_-$ with $p\!\approx\!\tfrac13$ for over-committed mass, and the turnover penalty $-(1-p)G_-$ dominates when $D=10$. *Veto:* the reward function penalizes non-conversion (loss of possession). *Prediction (text-verified):* mass "became a thing of the past, not to be resurrected for some three years" [PAGE 135 of 398] until the forward pass re-exposed the tackle. Confirmed by Camp's own reversal narrative.

**Veto 2 — "The desperation long pass from your own goal line."** Claim: a low-percentage deep pass is worth the gamble deep in one's own territory. *Verification:* $\mathbb E[\Delta\Pi]=p_cY_{\text{gain}}-p_{\text{int}}\Pi_{\text{opp}}$; near one's own goal $\Pi_{\text{opp}}\to 6$ (opponent touchdown off the interception), so the term $p_{\text{int}}\Pi_{\text{opp}}$ exceeds $p_cY_{\text{gain}}$ for any realistic $p_{\text{int}}$. *Veto:* expected-points inequality fails. *Prediction (text-verified):* Camp's "cardinal rule... is not to venture such plays when within one's own 25-yard line, on account of the risk of the opponents securing a touch-down" [PAGE 240 of 398].

**Veto 3 — "The routine punt into a hurricane headwind."** Claim: a standard-effort punt clears the rush line in a gale. *Verification:* the drag/wind ODE gives net horizontal impulse $\propto (v_0 - w)$; when headwind $w\gtrsim v_0\cos\theta$, $R\le 0$. *Veto:* projectile breakdown. *Prediction (text-verified):* in the Penn–Cornell snowstorm "nobody but Hare could even get the ball over Pennsylvania's own rush-line... into Cornell territory when trying to kick against the blast," and "nor did Hare succeed... every time" [PAGE 345 of 398] — the veto holds unless "all of his muscular force" is applied "squarely on to the ball," i.e. maximizing $v_0$ to force $v_0\cos\theta>w$.

All three vetoes are falsifiable against Camp's documented outcomes and against the stated conservation/expected-value constraints; none survives the cross-layer physical check as an "always-works" strategy.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
