From an information-theoretic standpoint, each unreinforced action represents a predictive failure. When a response $R$ is evoked but not reinforced, the sensory feedback loop registers a delta between expected reward and null outcome. This delta triggers an inhibitory cascade to prevent further energy expenditure on futile actions. Thus, $I_r$ can be modeled as accumulating proportional to the Shannon entropy reduction required to update the motor policy: $\Delta I \propto -\log P(Reward|R)$. Since $P(Reward|R)$ approaches zero in extinction, the log-odds diverge, driving up the necessary inhibition to suppress the response.

However, this scaling is bound by metabolic constraints. If we treat the muscular system as a thermodynamic engine, the work $W$ directly correlates with ATP hydrolysis rates. The generation of metabolic byproducts (e.g., lactate, $H^+$ ions, inorganic phosphate) acts as a veto mechanism on the central pattern generators. Therefore, the "positively accelerated" nature of the function implies non-linear fatigue accumulation; small increases in $F'$ or $L$ result in disproportionate spikes in local tissue acidosis, which heavily amplifies the inhibitory drive beyond what pure mechanics would predict.

A gray-zone audit reveals a singularity when $W$ approaches zero (e.g., purely cognitive tasks or micro-movements like eye saccades). Here, classical mechanical work vanishes, yet habituation and extinction still occur. The equation breaks down because the primary driver shifts from peripheral mechanical fatigue to central synaptic depression (e.g., AMPA receptor endocytosis or presynaptic vesicle depletion). Furthermore, if the force $F'$ exceeds the organism's maximal voluntary contraction, the response fails entirely before the inhibitory potential can fully manifest as behavioral suppression, leading to a breakdown in the assumed causal chain between $W$ and $I_r$. Thus, the boundary of this model lies strictly within the submaximal, mechanically defined domain of operant responses where neuromuscular transduction remains linear enough to support empirical constants $c$ and $B$.

*   **Variables:**
    *   $I_r$: Net functioning inhibitory potential resulting from a sequence of reaction evocations.

*   **Breakdown Regime:** Valid only when $W > 0$. Approaches a singularity if $n \to \infty$ without asymptotic decay, violating physiological limits of neural exhaustion.

**3. Mean Net Increment of Inhibition per Reinforcement (Evocation):**
$$\Delta I_{mean} = \frac{I_r}{n} = \frac{c \cdot W^{(n+B)}}{n}$$
*   **Units:** PSU per evocation.
*   **Assumption:** For a given organism, $\Delta I_{mean}$ possesses a constant value for a fixed $W$, independent of the absolute number of trials once steady-state metabolic conditions are achieved.

**4. Information-Theoretic Boundary Condition:**
To bridge Hull's mechanical framing with modern information theory, we introduce the predictive failure metric:
$$W_{info} \propto - \sum p(x) \log_2 p(x)$$
where $p(x)$ is the probability of reinforcement. When reinforcement ceases (extinction), $p(x) \to 0$, maximizing the informational "work" required to rewrite the synaptic weights, acting as a lower-bound multiplier on the physical $W$ in the original equation.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of $I_r$ originates in Edward Thorndike’s Law of Effect and Ivan Pavlov’s investigations into internal inhibition. However, Clark Hull radicalized these concepts by attempting to ground behaviorism entirely in the rigor of classical physics and mathematics. By the early 1940s, Hull recognized that merely tracking response frequencies was insufficient; he needed a mechanistic variable that explained why an animal stops pressing a bar after repeated failures.

Historically, the immediate precursor to $I_r$ was the physiological concept of fatigue. Early 20th-century physiologists demonstrated that repeated muscle contractions yielded acidic byproducts that depressed nerve impulse transmission. Hull abstracted this biological fatigue into a purely psychological operator. He retroactively mapped the mechanical work of a rat pressing a lever onto the theoretical buildup of a counter-force to the Drive Reduction gradient.

This genealogical path established a multi-week trap for subsequent behavioral science: the assumption that all learning dynamics could be reduced to a static algebraic summation of forces. Throughout the 1950s and 60s, researchers spent decades refining constants like $c$ and $B$ across various species, completely blind to the emerging field of cognitive neuroscience. They were trapped in a "force-field" metaphor of the mind, unable to account for phenomena like spontaneous recovery or latent learning, which inherently violate the strict monotonic accumulation of inhibitory potential dictated by mechanical work. The failure of this genealogical framework eventually paved the way for Tolman’s purposive behaviorism and, ultimately, the Cognitive Revolution.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In an operant conditioning context, an organism facing repeated unreinforced presses does not simply act as a passive accumulator of $I_r$; it engages in a recursive assessment of its own failing strategy. As $W$ accumulates over successive trials, the organism enters a state of crisis habit regression. It attempts to amplify $F'$ or increase the velocity of $L$ to overcome the perceived environmental blockage—a maladaptive escalation that dramatically accelerates the $W^{(n+B)}$ curve.

However, the very act of escalating effort triggers a meta-cognitive counter-trap. The sensory mismatch—the sheer absence of the expected reward despite increased kinetic output—forces a higher-order evaluation of the task itself. The organism realizes the contingency has changed. The "clock" here is the subjective temporal perception of deprivation; as time passes without reward, the baseline expectation decays. 

Consequently, rather than continuously increasing effort, the subject abruptly halts, shifting from active response execution to passive waiting. The recursive realization ("pressing no longer yields food") overrides the brute-force mechanical accumulation of fatigue. The physical exhaustion of the specific muscle group (the little finger vs. the arm, as noted by Hull) becomes secondary to the top-down cognitive veto issued by the prefrontal cortex, fundamentally bypassing the $I_r$ equation through a shift in attentional focus.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The mathematical certainty of $I_r$ reaching a threshold to extinguish a behavior is perpetually subject to cross-layer vetoes originating from biomechanical anomalies and environmental variables.

**Biomechanical Veto:** Consider an organism executing a high-frequency, low-amplitude tremor (small $L$, moderate $F'$). Mechanically, $W$ is negligible. According to standard models, $I_r$ should remain low. However, at extreme high frequencies, calcium ion reuptake mechanisms in the sarcoplasmic reticulum fail. This localized biomechanical veto instantaneously paralyzes the muscle, producing a cessation of behavior that mimics profound $I_r$, completely independent of the learned psychological inhibition.

**Weather/Environmental Veto:** Temperature drastically alters the coefficient of friction and muscle viscosity. In a freezing environment, the force $F'$ required to move a lever increases significantly due to stiffened tendons and higher air resistance, artificially inflating $W$. Simultaneously, peripheral vasoconstriction reduces blood flow to the extremities, accelerating lactic acid buildup. The physical layer vetoes the psychological continuity; the animal stops responding not because it has calculated the futility of the action (high $I_r$), but because the peripheral nervous system actively blocks the motor command to prevent tissue damage (the gate control theory of pain). Thus, environmental stressors hijack the mechanical inputs of the equation, rendering the calculated $I_r$ invalid as a predictor of behavioral cessation.
