because \(P(R=k)=1/32\) and \(P(W)=1/2\). This is Equation (1) [PAGE 6].

\[

=\frac{1}{16}\sum_{k=1}^{12}\frac{32-k}{31}

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive belief structure has four levels. At level 0, a team experiences a Week One win or loss. At level 1, an analyst updates a belief about team strength from that result. At level 2, the analyst anticipates that other analysts will update from the same public result, creating a shared narrative of “momentum.” At level 3, the modeler must anticipate that this shared narrative will contaminate the data-generating story unless the model controls for the head start and the prior rank distribution. The crisis habit regression is the level-1 shortcut: after a single salient result, agents regress to a simple habit—“Week One winners are playoff teams”—and neglect the base rate \(12/32=0.375\) and the all-week comparison. The source’s own language captures the temptation: the Brandt statistic “seems to imply that winning in week one is more important than winning in other weeks” [PAGE 2]. The regression is not irrational in isolation; a win is genuinely informative. It becomes a crisis habit when the agent treats the Week One label as uniquely causal rather than as one draw from a 16-game sequence.

The counter-trap is to overcorrect by discarding Week One information entirely. The paper does not do that. It keeps two legitimate channels: the head start from an observed win, and the updated opinion from a Bayesian rank posterior. The counter-trap is instead to confuse “not unique” with “not informative.” Week One winners do have a higher conditional playoff probability than losers, but so do winners in every other week. The correct recursive response is to ask: after conditioning on the final number of wins and on the rank posterior, does the Week One label retain residual predictive power? The paper’s model says no unique residual remains beyond the head start and updated opinion. The clock matters because the season is a finite 16-game horizon. A Week One win leaves 15 remaining games; a Week 16 win leaves zero. The binomial construction \(N=1+\mathrm{Binomial}(15,P_k)\) is a clock model: it counts one observed success and 15 future trials [PAGE 8]. The threshold of about 9.63 wins, rounded to 10 [PAGE 9–10], is the clock’s target. The recursive prediction is that agents who focus on the label rather than the clock will overweight early games; agents who focus only on the clock may underweight the genuine Bayesian signal. The paper’s balanced position is the counter-trap: retain the update, remove the label’s special status, and evaluate against all weeks.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is straightforward: the source provides no physical, biomechanical, or weather measurements, so no strong causal claim about Week One can survive adjudication. The paper’s win probability is a function of rank difference only, \(P_k(j)=(j-k)/64+1/2\) [PAGE 7]. If a physical state vector \(X\)—injury load, fatigue, surface, temperature, wind, travel—affects the outcome, then the correct likelihood is \(P(W\mid R=k,j,X)\), not \(P_k(j)\). By total probability,
\[
P(W\mid R=k,j)=\sum_X P(W\mid R=k,j,X)P(X\mid R=k,j).
\]
The rank-only kernel is valid only if either \(X\) is irrelevant or \(X\) is independent of rank and week in the sampled data. The paper does not establish either condition. It explicitly lists “Home Advantage,” “Weather,” “Winning and losing streaks,” and “Recent trades” as future additions [PAGE 12]. That is an admission that the current model omits cross-layer variables. The veto proof is therefore an identifiability proof: any observed Week One conditional probability is a mixture over unmeasured physical states; without \(X\), the Week One coefficient cannot be separated from weather, schedule, injury, or home-field composition. If physical variance dominates rank variance, the kernel collapses toward \(P_k(j)\approx1/2\), the Bayesian update term vanishes, and \(P(R\le12\mid W)\) tends to the base rate \(12/32=0.375\). In that regime the model still has a head-start effect from the observed win, but the “updated opinion” channel is dead. If physical effects are nonrandom with respect to Week One, the historical estimate is biased rather than merely noisy. The correct adjudication is thus negative: the paper can veto a claim that Week One is uniquely important under its assumptions, but it cannot prove a physical mechanism, and it cannot rule out a physical confound that would make the null result assumption-dependent.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Third, the head-start removal veto. Compare the full conditional law \(N=1+\mathrm{Binomial}(15,P_k)\) with the counterfactual \(N'=\mathrm{Binomial}(16,P_k)\) under the same posterior \(P(R=k\mid W)\). Because \(1+\mathrm{Binomial}(15,p)\) first-order stochastically dominates \(\mathrm{Binomial}(16,p)\), the prediction is
\[
P(\text{playoff}\mid W)_{\text{no head start}}
<
P(\text{playoff}\mid W)_{\text{full}}.
\]
The full model’s simulation value is 0.5254258 [PAGE 11]; the no-head-start value should be lower, and the gap is a direct measure of the head-start effect. A further exact check is \(P(N'=0\mid W)>0\) while the full model has \(P(N=0\mid W)=0\), since a Week One winner cannot finish with zero wins. These three vetoes are verifiable by exact rational arithmetic and simulation, and they test the paper’s core distinction between label, update, and head start without invoking any physical or league-specific claim beyond the source’s stated assumptions.
