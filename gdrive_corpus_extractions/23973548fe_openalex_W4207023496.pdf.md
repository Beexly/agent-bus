Use notation:
Let \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref}}(t_k)=(x_{\mathrm{ref},k},y_{\mathrm{ref},k})^\top\) and \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv}}(t_k)\). Units m. Time \(t_k\) s.

Central difference:
\[
\mathbf v_k=\frac{\mathbf r_{k+1}-\mathbf r_{k-1}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k-1}},\quad [\mathbf v_k]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
\]
Assumption \(t_{k+1}-t_{k-1}\neq0\); breakdown if duplicate timestamps, missing frames, nonuniform sampling uncorrected.

Speed scalar: \(s_k=\|\mathbf v_k\|_2\). Units m/s.

Low-pass:
\[
|H(j\omega)|^2=\frac{1}{1+(\omega/\omega_c)^{2n}}, \quad n=4,\ \omega_c=2\pi(1\,\mathrm{Hz}).
\]
Units rad/s. Assumptions LTI, no zero-phase unless specified; breakdown at \(\omega_c\) attenuation, phase lag; if movement bandwidth >1 Hz, attenuation; not for acceleration.

Cross-correlation:
\[
\tau^\star=\arg\max_{\tau\in\mathbb R} \int_T \mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)\cdot \mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv}}(t+\tau)\,dt
\]
or normalized. Units s. Assumption high SNR, nonperiodic; breakdown periodic gaits, short windows, multiple peaks.

Rotation:
\[
\theta^\star=\arg\min_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)} \frac{1}{N}\sum_k \|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref},k}-R(\theta)\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv},k}\|_1
\]
then refine \(\theta^\star\pm2^\circ\) step \(0.01^\circ\). Units rad or deg; \(R(\theta)\) dimensionless. Assumption rigid planar frame misalignment; breakdown local minima, non-rigid distortion.

\[
d_k = \|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref},k}-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv},k}\|_2 \quad \text{(if position)}
\]

\[
\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{SD}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=1}^N (d_k-\bar d)^2}.
\]
Conventional RMSD:
\[
\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{RMS}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum d_k^2}.
\]
Units m or m/s. If mean bias \(\bar d\neq0\), \(\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{RMS}}^2 = \mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{SD}}^2+\bar d^2\) (approximately for sample vs population). This is exact for population; sample slight. This is formal, not toy. Need note paper calls sample SD. Good.

The source is a sports-engineering validation paper, not an inferential sports-prediction document. Its central multi-hop claim is: if a bespoke computer vision system, VisionKit, agrees strongly with a high-precision three-dimensional motion capture criterion inside a 30 × 30 m stadium test area, then VisionKit can serve as a non-commercial reference for validating other electronic performance and tracking systems across areas where three-dimensional motion capture cannot operate. The derivation chain has five links. First, construct a criterion: five retro-reflective markers, one on each shoulder and three on the pelvis, are tracked by 36 Vicon Vantage cameras at 100 Hz; the pelvis-marker midpoint approximates centre of mass [PAGE 3]. Second, project both systems into a common planar coordinate frame: VisionKit uses four stationary high-definition cameras, genlocked via a remote-control panel, and calibrates each camera to the capture area via known points from a rigid calibration rig [PAGE 4]. Third, synchronize and align: cross-correlation of position data estimates time lag, and VisionKit position data are rotated through 360 degrees, then refined by 0.01-degree increments over a ±2-degree window, to minimize mean absolute error [PAGE 4]. Fourth, differentiate and resample: both systems use a three-point finite central difference formula for horizontal speed; VisionKit raw detections are at 25 frames per second, up-sampled to 50 Hz and then down-sampled to 10 Hz, while three-dimensional motion capture is down-sampled to 10 Hz; both then receive a fourth-order 1-Hz low-pass Butterworth filter [PAGE 4]. Fifth, compute error: the paper reports RMSD for speed and MAE for position [PAGE 4]. The abstract states “The root mean square difference for speed was 0.04 m·s−1 and for position was 0.18 m” [PAGE 1], while the Results state “The root mean square deviation for speed was 0.04 m·s−1 and the mean absolute error for position was 0.15 m” [PAGE 4]. That is a source-internal gray zone: 0.18 m versus 0.15 m, and RMSD versus MAE, are not interchangeable. A second gray zone is sample description: the abstract says “Ten participants undertook a series of football-specific movement tasks” [PAGE 1], but the Method says “Participants (n = 60) were members of an elite youth football academy, attached to a professional football team, or active community-level footballers” [PAGE 2]. A third gray zone is metric definition: the paper defines RMSD as “the sample standard deviation of the differences between three-dimensional motion capture and VisionKit” [PAGE 4]. That is not the conventional root-mean-square difference unless the mean difference is zero; if a systematic bias exists, the conventional RMSD exceeds the sample standard deviation of differences. A fourth gray zone is sample imbalance: Table 1 lists 96,611 low-velocity samples but only 518 sprint samples [PAGE 5], so an overall speed error of 0.04 m·s−1 is dominated by low-speed walking and jogging unless per-band errors are separately reported. The text says “Adequate data were collected to determine the error within all velocity bands” [PAGE 5], but the narrative does not give per-band numerical RMSD or MAE; it only points to figures. A fifth gray zone is filtering: the 1-Hz low-pass is defensible for overall velocity banding, but it removes intra-step velocity fluctuation and cannot be used to infer acceleration accuracy. A sixth gray zone is alignment fitting: rotation and lag are optimized on the same data used to compute error; without a holdout split, the reported agreement may be slightly optimistic. The paper also reports “Data availability Nil” [PAGE 6], so independent recomputation is not possible from the source alone. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Notation: let \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref}}(t_k)=(x_{\mathrm{ref},k},y_{\mathrm{ref},k})^{\top}\) be the reference three-dimensional motion capture ground-plane position at time \(t_k\), and let \(\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv}}(t_k)\) be the VisionKit position. Units are metres for position and seconds for time. The paper states that five markers are used, with “The mid-position of the three pelvis markers was determined for each data frame to approximate the centre of mass of the player” [PAGE 3]. The Vicon volume is reconstructed from “36 Vicon Vantage cameras … with a sampling frequency of 100 Hz” [PAGE 3]. VisionKit “samples raw detections at 25 frames per second” [PAGE 4].

Speed operator. Both systems use a three-point finite central difference formula [PAGE 4]. For a uniformly sampled sequence,
\[
\mathbf v_k=\frac{\mathbf r_{k+1}-\mathbf r_{k-1}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k-1}},
\qquad [\mathbf v_k]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\]
and the scalar horizontal speed is
\[
s_k=\|\mathbf v_k\|_2,\qquad [s_k]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
\]
Assumption: \(t_{k+1}-t_{k-1}\neq0\), and the central difference is applied on a regular time base. Breakdown regimes: duplicate timestamps, missing frames, uncorrected non-uniform sampling, or sharp direction changes within one differencing window produce biased speed. The paper’s later resampling is explicit: “VisionKit data were then up-sampled to 50 Hz using linear interpolation and then down-sampled to 10 Hz” [PAGE 4]. Linear interpolation is not a physical measurement; it cannot recover lost high-frequency motion.

Low-pass operator. The paper applies “A fourth-order 1-Hz low-pass Butterworth filter” to position and speed for both systems [PAGE 4]. In standard continuous-time form,
\[
|H(j\omega)|^2=\frac{1}{1+\left(\omega/\omega_c\right)^{2n}},
\qquad n=4,\quad \omega_c=2\pi f_c,\quad f_c=1\,\mathrm{Hz},
\]
with \(\omega\) and \(\omega_c\) in \(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), and \(|H|\) dimensionless. Assumption: linear time-invariant filtering on a uniformly sampled series. The paper does not state that this final 1-Hz filter is zero-phase, so phase lag is a possible breakdown regime. At \(\omega=\omega_c\), the Butterworth magnitude is \(1/\sqrt{2}\) for any order; there is no singularity there, but the filter deliberately attenuates movement content above 1 Hz. If the scientific claim were about acceleration or rapid braking, this cutoff would be a veto; for overall velocity bands, it is consistent with the intended smoothing.

Temporal synchronization. The paper states: “Three-dimensional motion capture and VisionKit data were time synchronized using cross-correlation of position data” [PAGE 4]. A standard estimator is
\[
\tau^{\star}=\arg\max_{\tau\in\mathbb R}\int_T \mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)\cdot\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv}}(t+\tau)\,dt,
\qquad [\tau^{\star}]=\mathrm{s}.
\]
Assumption: the two signals share a dominant common trajectory and the correlation peak is unique over the search window. Breakdown regimes: periodic gait can produce multiple correlation peaks; short or nonstationary windows can select a side peak; occlusion gaps can flatten the correlation surface. The paper does not report the correlation-peak ambiguity or a confidence interval for \(\tau^{\star}\).

Planar rotation alignment. The paper rotates VisionKit “through 360 degrees to find the lowest mean absolute error for position,” then refines “2° either side by 0.01° increments” [PAGE 4]. Formally,
\[
\theta^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta\in[0,2\pi)}
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\left\|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref},k}-R(\theta)\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv},k}\right\|_1,
\]
where \(R(\theta)\) is a dimensionless planar rotation and \(\theta\) is in radians or degrees. Assumption: the frame mismatch is a rigid planar rotation, not a non-rigid lens or ground-plane distortion. Breakdown regimes: local minima, camera calibration drift, non-planar pitch crown, or a rotation that absorbs a systematic tracking bias. Because the rotation is fitted on the same data used for error evaluation, the reported MAE is an in-sample alignment error, not a held-out generalization error.

Error metrics. The paper says “Velocity and position data were then compared by root mean square deviation (RMSD): the sample standard deviation of the differences between three-dimensional motion capture and VisionKit” [PAGE 4]. Let \(d_k\) be the difference for speed or position at sample \(k\). The paper’s stated sample-standard-deviation form is
\[
\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{SD}}
=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(d_k-\bar d\right)^2},
\qquad [\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{SD}}]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\ \text{or}\ \mathrm{m}.
\]
The conventional root-mean-square difference is
\[
\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{RMS}}
=
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}d_k^2}.
\]
For population moments, \(\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{RMS}}^2=\mathrm{RMSD}_{\mathrm{SD}}^2+\bar d^2\); therefore a nonzero mean bias makes the conventional RMSD larger than the sample standard deviation of differences. This is the exact metric-definition breakdown regime. The position metric is stated as “the mean absolute error for position was 0.15 m” [PAGE 4], formally
\[
\mathrm{MAE}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\left\|\mathbf r_{\mathrm{ref},k}-\mathbf r_{\mathrm{cv},k}\right\|_2,
\qquad [\mathrm{MAE}]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
Assumption: Euclidean ground-plane error after frame alignment. Breakdown regimes: uncorrected rotation, anisotropic pixel-to-ground scaling, or non-planar ground.

Stabilization. The paper states: “Adequate data were collected to determine the error within all velocity bands, with stabilization of the error for position occurring after about 7 s, with the exception of the moderate speed band where stabilization occurred after 24 s (Table 1)” [PAGE 5]. A formal running-error check would define \(S_N\) as a running RMSD or MAE and declare stabilization when \(|S_N-S_{N-w}|<\epsilon\) over a window \(w\); the paper does not specify \(\epsilon\) or \(w\). Table 1 gives “Low 621 96,611; Moderate 2368 29,199; High 475 2944; Very high 679 1349; Sprint 488 518” [PAGE 5]. Those are sample counts, not necessarily seconds. The exact singularity/breakdown regime is nonstationary error: if the high-speed or sprint error is still drifting at the end of a short trial, the table’s “stability achieved” count can be met while the per-band error remains unstable. No information-theoretic or probability units are reported in the source; all reported quantities are SI kinematic quantities.

The paper’s lineage is a validation genealogy in which later standards reshape earlier measurement choices. Before the 2019 FIFA quality standard, the market assumption was independent evaluation, but the authors state that “in many cases electronic performance and tracking systems are not independently evaluated prior to entering the market” [PAGE 1]. The FIFA standard is described as a response: “The Fédération Internationale de Football Association (FIFA) introduced a quality standard for electronic performance and tracking systems in 2019, against which commercial tracking systems are tested” [PAGE 1]. Earlier validation often used timing gates or radar “in the absence of a true criterion measure” [PAGE 2]. The retrocausal shift is that three-dimensional motion capture became the gold standard by which commercial optical, LPS, and GNSS systems were judged; the paper says those systems “are considered the gold standard and have been reported to produce sub millimetre errors” [PAGE 2]. But the same gold standard cannot cover a full pitch because “it is not currently possible to use them on a full football pitch due to the need for many fragile cameras to be placed around the capture volume and a lack of suitable depth of field of those cameras” [PAGE 2]. Hence the hybrid inversion: use Vicon in a small area, then use a non-commercial computer vision system outside that area to test whether commercial systems are “trained on the capture space only, and thus potentially misrepresenting the true full-pitch accuracy” [PAGE 2]. The reference system is deliberately non-commercial: “the reference system for comparison should not be commercially available and thus be in commercial conflict with the electronic performance and tracking systems tested” [PAGE 2]. This is a scientific-governance lineage, not merely a sensor comparison. Multi-week traps follow from that lineage. Third, version drift in tracking software and filtering; the paper does not report software versions or a pre-registered analysis plan. Fourth, criterion leakage: if a commercial system is trained or tuned on the Vicon capture area, full-pitch performance can look better than it is; the hybrid design is explicitly meant to detect that [PAGE 2]. Fifth, statistical drift: the abstract and results use different position-error labels and values [PAGE 1, PAGE 4], so a multi-week evidence review that cites only the abstract can propagate an inconsistent number. The paper’s own positioning against prior work reinforces the trap: it notes “only two published studies have tested electronic performance and tracking systems in stadium environments using three-dimensional motion capture as the criterion measure” [PAGE 5], which means the field has a thin comparative base and strong incentive to over-interpret a single non-commercial validation.
