Historical and scientific lineage, plus multi-week traps.

The intellectual genealogy of this study begins with Sánchez-Medina and González-Badillo (2011), who established velocity loss as an objective marker of neuromuscular fatigue and a practical criterion for set termination [PAGE 1]. Weakley et al. (2020) then acutely compared 10%, 20%, and 30% velocity-loss thresholds during back squats, documenting how threshold height manipulates repetition number, mean velocity, and effort [PAGES 1, 8]. Jukic et al. (2023a) produced a systematic review and meta-analysis indicating that lower thresholds preserve velocity and favor jumping/sprinting, whereas higher thresholds increase repetition volume and may favor hypertrophy [PAGES 1, 8]. Pareja-Blanco et al. (2020) demonstrated that velocity loss in the bench press influences strength gains, neuromuscular adaptations, and hypertrophy [PAGE 8], and Pareja-Blanco et al. (2026) later showed that strength response depends jointly on relative intensity and velocity loss, with 25% VL producing the largest bench-press 1RM gains and 50% VL favoring hypertrophy [PAGE 8]. On the sex-specific front, Rissanen et al. (2022) compared 20% and 40% VL in men and women and found effect-size patterns in women favoring 40% VL for some strength outcomes despite no significant group-level differences [PAGE 2]. Most recently, Kambara et al. (2026) compared 10% and 20% VL in trained female basketball players and found no significant group × time interactions, though both groups improved 1RM and sprint performance [PAGE 2].

Lv et al. (2026) extends this lineage by (a) using a 10% vs. 30% contrast—a wider spread than Kambara’s 10% vs. 20%—and (b) testing trained female collegiate athletes across multiple sports rather than a single sport. The multi-week traps embedded in this design are significant. First, an 8-week intervention with only 16 total sessions may capture early neurological and practice adaptations but miss longer-term accommodation, detraining, or hypertrophy plateaus that emerge beyond two months [PAGE 8]. Second, because pre- and post-tests are separated by exactly 8 weeks plus familiarization, any menstrual-cycle phase clustering at baseline or post-test (uncontrolled per [PAGES 4, 10]) can alias as a training effect in a female cohort. Third, the familiarization week and repeated testing create a practice effect, especially on the Smith-machine squat 1RM, which doubles as both intervention exercise and outcome measure; this risks inflating the VL30 advantage simply because VL30 performed more repetitions of the tested movement [PAGE 8]. Fourth, training was scheduled Tuesdays and Fridays with at least 72 hours recovery [PAGE 5], a rigid clock that may not align with individual recovery curves or sport competition schedules, potentially inducing residual fatigue heterogeneity across the cohort. Fifth, the post-intervention assessment occurred within one week of the final session [PAGE 3], capturing acute post-training potentiation or residual fatigue rather than stable, retained adaptation.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Crisis habit regression: Under VL30, athletes are forced to continue squatting as bar velocity declines by 30%. The later repetitions in each set are slower, mechanically distinct, and potentially associated with altered motor-unit recruitment and technique degradation. Recursive theory of mind predicts that athletes under high fatigue regress to habitual, low-velocity motor patterns—“grinding” repetitions—that may strengthen the specific slow-velocity coordination of the Smith-machine squat but do not transfer optimally to high-velocity tasks. This is consistent with the observation that VL30 won on squat 1RM (a slow, maximal-force task) but lost on SLJ and T30 (rapid force-expression tasks).

Counter-trap: VL10 operates as a counter-trap against this habit regression. By terminating sets at 10% velocity loss, the prescription preserves MPV (0.60 vs. 0.55 m·s⁻¹) and enforces maximal intended velocity on every repetition [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap logic is that repeated exposure to high-velocity intent maintains or enhances rapid force-expression capacity, which explains the superior SLJ (ηp² = 0.166) and T30 (ηp² = 0.254) adaptations despite lower 1RM gains. However, this counter-trap is not universal: vertical jumps (SJ, CMJ) did not differentiate, suggesting that preserving repetition velocity does not confer a generalized advantage across all explosive domains, only those whose task constraints (horizontal projection, sprint acceleration) align with preserved bar velocity.

Clock: The experimental clock is highly constrained—two sessions per week, 72-hour inter-session recovery, testing at approximately the same time of day [PAGES 4-5]. This controls circadian and recovery confounds but compresses the adaptation window into 16 exposures. From a recursive theory-of-mind perspective, the athlete’s brain must decide, session by session, whether to allocate adaptation resources to maximal force capacity (favored by volume) or rapid force expression (favored by velocity preservation). The clock’s brevity means we observe only the early phase of this recursive negotiation; we cannot know whether the VL10 advantage in sprinting would persist, reverse, or attenuate over a 24-week macrocycle with periodized threshold manipulation [PAGE 10 discussion of periodization]. Moreover, because T30 was recorded as a single total time without split times [PAGE 7], the clock cannot resolve whether the VL10 advantage emerged during acceleration (0–10 m), transition (10–20 m), or maximal velocity (20–30 m), leaving the temporal locus of the counter-trap unverified.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

Physical veto (equipment and modality): The Smith-machine squat constrains the bar to a fixed vertical path. As noted in the text, anterior or posterior foot displacement relative to this fixed path alters hip and knee joint torques [PAGE 4, citing Biscarini et al., 2011]. Participants self-selected stance width but remained within standardized technical constraints, and foot position was maintained across sessions. This is a hard physical veto: strength gains on the Smith machine cannot be assumed to transfer to free-weight squat strength, athletic ground-based force production, or sport-specific tasks requiring multiplanar stabilization. The 1RM advantage for VL30 is therefore bounded to the Smith-machine context.

Biomechanical veto (task structure): The outcome battery contains three distinct jump/sprint tasks with different mechanical demands. SJ is predominantly concentric impulse from a static start; CMJ adds eccentric braking, countermovement strategy, and muscle-tendon interaction [PAGE 9, citing Van Hooren and Zolotarjova, 2017]. SLJ requires conversion of take-off velocity into horizontal displacement. T30 captures only total 30-m time with no split times [PAGE 7]. The study did not measure horizontal impulse, take-off angle, joint kinetics, rate of force development, or sprint mechanics [PAGES 7, 9]. Consequently, any claim that VL10 “improves sprinting” or “improves power” is vetoed at the biomechanical layer: we know only that total 30-m time improved more under VL10, not which phase or mechanism changed. Likewise, null findings for SJ and CMJ do not prove physiological equivalence; jump height alone cannot reveal differences in braking impulse, propulsion duration, or joint-level contributions [PAGE 8, citing Anicic et al., 2023].

Weather veto (environmental): Sprint testing occurred on an outdoor synthetic athletics track using Brower TCI infrared gates [PAGE 4]. The limitations section explicitly states that environmental conditions such as wind and temperature were not formally recorded or controlled and may have introduced additional measurement variability [PAGE 10]. Because T30 showed the strongest interaction (p = 0.006, ηp² = 0.254), unmeasured tailwind/headwind or temperature variation between pre- and post-testing days constitutes a measurable veto threat: part of the VL10 sprint advantage could be environmental rather than training-induced. The same-time-of-day control [PAGE 4] does not mitigate day-to-day weather variance.

Biological veto (endocrine and recovery): Menstrual-cycle phase and hormonal contraceptive use were recorded but not experimentally controlled [PAGE 4], and sleep and energy availability were not systematically controlled [PAGE 10]. In trained women, these variables modulate neuromuscular fatigue, recovery, and strength adaptation [PAGE 2, citing Landen et al., 2023]. Their absence from experimental control is a biological veto on sex-specific generalization: the observed VL threshold effects may be partly confounded by cycle phase or contraceptive status, particularly given the small sample (n = 14 per group).
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with falsifiable predictions.

Veto Scenario 1 — Volume-equated counterfactual.
Condition: Repeat the protocol but equate repetition volume between groups (e.g., VL30 performs only as many repetitions as VL10 per set, or VL10 performs additional sets to match VL30’s total volume), while maintaining the same 10% vs. 30% velocity-loss termination rules for the repetitions that are performed.
Rationale: Prior literature reports similar maximal-strength gains across low and high velocity-loss thresholds when repetition volume is closely matched [PAGE 1, citing Myrholt et al., 2023; Andersen et al., 2024].
Verifiable prediction: The group × time interaction for Smith-machine squat 1RM becomes non-significant (p > 0.05) with ηp² reduced below 0.06 (small), and Hedges’ g for 1RM shifts toward zero (e.g., |g| < 0.20). If the interaction remains significant under volume matching, the nominal VL threshold itself—not accumulated repetitions—is the primary driver.

Veto Scenario 2 — Free-weight modality counterfactual.
Condition: Replace the Smith-machine squat 1RM test with a free-weight back-squat 1RM test while keeping the Smith-machine squat as the training exercise.
Rationale: The fixed bar path of the Smith machine creates practice-specificity and reduces stabilizer demand [PAGE 4]; transfer to free-weight strength is imperfect [PAGE 8, citing Stone et al., 2024].
Verifiable prediction: The VL30 advantage in squat 1RM attenuates substantially (interaction ηp² < 0.20, Hedges’ g moving toward the trivial zone |g| < 0.20), while VL10’s advantages in SLJ and T30 remain significant because those field tests are modality-independent. If VL30 retains a large free-weight 1RM advantage, then neural/structural transfer from Smith-machine volume is strong rather than practice-bound.

Veto Scenario 3 — Sprint split-resolution counterfactual.
Condition: Instrument the 30-m sprint with 10-m splits (0–10 m, 10–20 m, 20–30 m) and a force-velocity profiling apparatus, rather than recording only total 30-m time [PAGE 7 limitation].
Rationale: Total time masks the phase at which adaptation occurred; VL10’s preserved velocity may benefit acceleration more than maximal velocity.
Verifiable prediction: The group × time interaction is significant for the 0–10 m split (acceleration phase; p < 0.05, ηp² ≥ 0.14) but non-significant for the 20–30 m split (maximal velocity phase; p > 0.05). If both splits show significant interactions of similar magnitude, the VL10 sprint benefit is not phase-specific and the “velocity preservation aids sprinting” claim requires a different mechanical explanation.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
