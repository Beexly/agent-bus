<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the spammer as an adaptive agent with belief state \(b_t\) about defender technology and a profit function \(\Pi_t = R_t - C_t\), where \(R_t\) is revenue and \(C_t\) is operational cost. The baseline observation is that \(p_{\text{sale}} \approx 8 \times 10^{-8}\), so \(\Pi_t > 0\) only at massive \(N_t\) and minimal \(C_t\) per message [PAGE 10–11].

Crisis habit regression occurs when the defender removes the push-based low-cost channel. Spammers historically regress to high-volume push via botnets that ignore pull extensions, falling back to legacy SMTP where the RMTA must accept all messages [PAGE 12–13]. Under General Delivery, this regression is constrained because legacy SMTP is only accepted if the RMTA permits it; in STRICT mode, non-GDEL connections are rejected with 553 [PAGE 77]. A rational spammer therefore faces a choice: operate a pull-compliant SMTP service on each zombie (increasing \(C_t\) substantially due to uptime, static IP, and inbound connectivity requirements), or abandon the botnet channel.

The counter-trap is the spammer's attempt to satisfy General Delivery preconditions. The thesis identifies two counter-trap vectors: (1) brute-forcing unique identifiers by guessing \(\sigma\) [PAGE 70–71], mitigated by adding random salt and sufficient length [PAGE 70]; and (2) publishing SPF records for zombie hosts to pass RMTA validation [PAGE 67]. The second counter-trap is economically self-defeating at scale: an SPF record listing hundreds of thousands of dynamic IPs exceeds the 1300-byte DNS TXT guideline, marking the domain as suspect [PAGE 67], and dynamic ADSL addresses invalidate ip4 mechanisms over short timescales [PAGE 67].

The clock layer comprises temporal constraints that advantage the defender. \(T_{\text{avail}} = 48\) h bounds how long a zombie must host a retrievable message [PAGE 68]. Botnet maintenance cycles of "a couple of hours every day" [PAGE 59] collide with the requirement to keep an SMTP service active for retrieval during those same windows. RFC 5321 retry intervals of 30 minutes over 4–5 days [PAGE 68] exceed \(T_{\text{avail}}\), so even a persistent RMTA may retrieve nothing if the SMTA deletes after 48 hours [PAGE 68]. The notification-to-retrieval delay, even if only minutes, creates a perception window in which end users may resend messages, generating duplicate traffic and potential false-positive spam classification [PAGE 60].

Recursive closure: each defender hardening (SPF + GDEL) raises \(C_t\) for the spammer; because \(R_t\) scales linearly with volume while \(p_{\text{sale}}\) is fixed and tiny, modest increases in \(C_t\) drive \(\Pi_t\) negative. The spammer's optimal response is either to consolidate zombies under static, SPF-validated mail relays (reducing botnet size and resilience) or to exit. The system is therefore stable only if SPF adoption is near-universal; partial adoption leaves a push-based fallback that preserves \(\Pi_t > 0\) [PAGE 66–68].

Physical/network-layer vetoes are conditions under which General Delivery cannot operate regardless of software correctness.

Veto 1 — Inbound connectivity absence. Consumer ISPs frequently block inbound TCP/25 or assign dynamic addresses via DHCP/PPPoE [PAGE 22, 69]. A zombie behind symmetric NAT or CGNAT cannot accept the RMTA's RETR connection because the protocol "does not define how the connection is being established" and places responsibility on the sender to ensure reachability [PAGE 68]. If the RMTA cannot reach the SMTA within \(T_{\text{timeout}} = 300\) s, the message is not retrieved; after \(T_{\text{avail}} = 48\) h it is deleted [PAGE 68]. Veto condition: \(\text{reachable}(\text{SMTA}) = \text{false} \implies \text{throughput}_{\text{zombie}} = 0\) messages/day under General Delivery.

Veto 2 — DNS TXT size singularity. SPF validation requires the RMTA to resolve a TXT record for the domain embedded in the UID [PAGE 66]. The official suggestion is 1300 bytes to fit one Ethernet packet [PAGE 67]. If \(|\text{SPF record}| > 1300\) B, the response may truncate or be flagged as anomalous; a botnet attempting to authorize hundreds of thousands of hosts via ip4/ip6 mechanisms breaches this bound [PAGE 67]. Veto condition: \(\text{SPF}_{\text{size}} > 1300\text{ B} \implies \text{validation} \in \{\text{fail, suspect}\}\).

Veto 3 — Notification ingestion load under indirect DDoS. An attacker can send millions of forged GDEL notifications to legitimate RMTAs, pretending to be a target MTA [PAGE 71–72]. Without SPF, each RMTA may queue a RETR attempt, generating a distributed denial of service against the purported sender [PAGE 61–62]. With SPF enforced at ingestion, notifications from IPs absent from the domain's SPF record are dropped before retrieval state is allocated [PAGE 66]. Veto condition: \(\text{SPF}_{\text{enforced}} = \text{true} \implies \text{amplification factor} \approx 0\), though edge load scales with notification rate \(\lambda_{\text{notif}}\) due to DNS lookups.

Biomechanical/human layer. False negatives—spam reaching the inbox—impose manual processing costs. Baracuda Networks estimates a 1000-employee company receiving 20 spam messages per employee per day loses 30,500 hours/year, or over US$877,400, at 15 seconds per message [PAGE 19]. Microsoft Research estimates approximately 1% of legitimate mail is lost to infrastructure failure or aggressive filtering [PAGE 18–19]. These human-time costs are not eliminated by General Delivery unless the RMTA's sender classification is highly accurate; the protocol shifts filtering from content analysis to sender authentication, trading one error mode for another.

Weather/meteorological layer. No meteorological variable appears in the source text; the "Storm" botnet is a proper name and does not introduce atmospheric dependencies [PAGE 10–11]. Network partitions and DNS outages function analogously to weather events by disrupting SPF lookups and RETR path establishment, but they are topological, not meteorological, vetoes.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — SPF inflation attack. Counterfactual: a botnet operator publishes a single SPF TXT record listing ip4 mechanisms for 100,000 dynamic zombie addresses. Verifiable veto: DNS TXT payload exceeds 1300 bytes [PAGE 67], causing truncation or rejection by resolvers adhering to the single-packet guideline. RLVR prediction: RMTA SPF queries return fail or suspect for the domain; notifications are discarded [PAGE 66]; effective spam throughput from that domain falls to \(\epsilon \approx 0\) unless zombies are consolidated under a shared static mail relay with a compact SPF record. Measurable outcome: domain-level email rejection rate rises from baseline to near 100% for GDEL notifications.

Scenario 2 — NATed zombie retrieval failure. Counterfactual: a zombie PC operates behind a residential gateway with no port forwarding and a dynamic WAN address. Verifiable veto: the RMTA stores the notification IP and attempts a RETR connection to that address [PAGE 68]; the gateway drops inbound SYN packets; the connection times out after \(T_{\text{timeout}} = 300\) s [PAGE 68]. Retries over 4–5 days per RFC 5321 guidance [PAGE 68] still exceed the 48-hour availability window [PAGE 68], so the SMTA deletes the message. RLVR prediction: messages originating from NATed zombies have zero retrieval probability under General Delivery; botnet effective throughput scales only with the fraction of zombies possessing public static IPs and open inbound TCP/25.

Scenario 3 — Indirect DDoS with SPF enforcement. Counterfactual: an attacker controls a botnet that sends forged GDEL notifications to 1 million legitimate RMTAs, using the target domain's name in the UID but sending from IPs not listed in the target's SPF record. Verifiable veto: each RMTA performs an SPF check on the notifying host's IP against the domain extracted from the UID [PAGE 66]; mismatches cause the notification to be dropped before any RETR session is initiated [PAGE 66]. RLVR prediction: the indirect DDoS amplification factor \(A\) collapses to approximately zero because no retrieval connections are opened to the target SMTA [PAGE 61–62]; however, the target's DNS infrastructure still experiences lookup load proportional to \(\lambda_{\text{notif}}\), and edge MTAs bear packet-inspection cost. The SMTA is protected from retrieval-flood DoS but not from notification-ingestion load.
