The paper’s metric table is the formal core for classification and tracking claims. From Table 2 [PAGE 7], with binary labels and a fixed decision threshold, all classification metrics below are dimensionless probability-like scores in \([0,1]\), assuming the relevant denominator is nonzero:

\[
\mathrm{ACC}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}
\]

\[
\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad
\mathrm{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{TNR}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}
\]

\[
F_1=2\cdot\frac{\mathrm{PPV}\cdot\mathrm{TPR}}{\mathrm{PPV}+\mathrm{TPR}}
=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FN+FP}
\]

These are cited in the paper as ACC, PPV, TPR, TNR, and F1 [PAGE 7]. Breakdown regimes: \(\mathrm{PPV}\) is undefined when \(TP+FP=0\); \(\mathrm{TPR}\) is undefined when \(TP+FN=0\); \(\mathrm{TNR}\) is undefined when \(TN+FP=0\); \(F_1\) is undefined when \(2TP+FN+FP=0\). Even when defined, ACC can be misleading under class imbalance, which is directly relevant to the paper’s warning that “positive-to-negative sample ratios used in each study are different” [PAGE 13].

The regression metric in Table 2 is:

\[
\mathrm{MSE}(y,\hat y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2
\]

If \(y\) is measured in physical units \(u\), then \(\mathrm{MSE}\) has units \(u^2\); the root mean squared error \(\sqrt{\mathrm{MSE}}\) has units \(u\). Breakdown: undefined for \(n=0\); sensitive to outliers and non-identically distributed test data. The paper lists MSE among its core metrics [PAGE 7].

Multi-object tracking uses:

\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{\sum_t(FN_t+FP_t+IDSW_t)}{\sum_t GT_t}
\]

This is dimensionless. Assumptions: \(GT_t\) is the ground-truth object count at frame \(t\), \(FN_t\) and \(FP_t\) are missed and false detections, and \(IDSW_t\) is identity switches, as defined in the Table 2 note [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: undefined when \(\sum_t GT_t=0\); MOTA can be negative when cumulative errors exceed the ground-truth count. The localization term is:

\[
\mathrm{MOTP}=\frac{\sum_{t,i}d_{t,i}}{\sum_t c_t}
\]

where \(d_{t,i}\) is the Intersection-over-Union between target \(i\) in frame \(t\) and its designated ground truth, and \(c_t\) is the number of matches [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: undefined when \(\sum_t c_t=0\). The underlying IoU is:

\[
\mathrm{IoU}(B_p,B_g)=\frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|}
\]

which is dimensionless and undefined when \(|B_p\cup B_g|=0\). The paper’s note explicitly defines IoU in the MOTP context [PAGE 7].

For action recognition, the paper defines the task as decoding “the ‘when and what action’ of an individual or a group from the data source” [PAGE 8]. A source-independent necessary condition for that decoding is positive measurement information. Let \(A\) be the latent action class and \(X\) the observed sensor/video representation. Then:

\[
I(A;X)=H(A)-H(A\mid X)\ge I_{\min}
\]

where \(I(A;X)\) is mutual information in bits when base-2 logarithms are used, \(H(A)\) is action entropy in bits, and \(H(A\mid X)\) is conditional entropy in bits. Assumption: finite action alphabet or quantized continuous observations; differential entropy for continuous \(X\) is not absolutely unit-invariant without quantization. Breakdown: if \(I(A;X)=0\), the observation is independent of the action and no decoder can beat the prior. Fano’s inequality gives the corresponding error floor:

\[
H(A\mid X)\le H_b(P_e)+P_e\log_2(|A|-1)
\]

where \(P_e\) is probability of error and \(H_b\) is the binary entropy function in bits. Breakdown: \(|A|=1\) trivializes the bound; \(|A|=0\) is undefined. This formalizes why occlusion, lighting, and camera-view failures are not cosmetic: they can drive \(I(A;X)\) toward zero even if the neural network is large.

Fast-motion validity requires a sampling condition. Let \(f_s\) be the camera frame rate in \(\mathrm{Hz}\) and \(f_{\max}\) the highest relevant biomechanical motion frequency in \(\mathrm{Hz}\). Then:

\[
f_s>2f_{\max}
\]

is the Nyquist condition for avoiding aliasing. Breakdown: at \(f_s=2f_{\max}\), reconstruction is marginal; below it, fast limb or ball motion is aliased and action phase is not identifiable. The paper repeatedly notes fast motion and occlusion as limiting conditions [PAGE 11][PAGE 13].

Validation identity leakage can be expressed set-theoretically. Let \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{train}}\) and \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{test}}\) be the sets of athlete identities, sessions, or match clips used in training and testing. A clean split requires:

\[
\mathcal{I}_{\text{train}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{\text{test}}=\varnothing
\]

Breakdown: if the intersection is nonempty, the model may memorize identity- or clip-specific cues, exactly the leakage risk the paper flags when it says high values “may hide data leakage or sample bias issues” [PAGE 16]. The generalization gap is:

\[
\Delta_g=R_{\text{test}}(f)-R_{\text{train}}(f)
\]

where \(R\) is expected loss in the units of the chosen loss function. Breakdown: \(\Delta_g\) is not a clean generalization estimate when the test distribution is not independent and identically distributed, when identities overlap, or when hyperparameters were tuned on the test set. The paper explicitly warns that without independent test sets and cross-validation, “the metrics will be grossly over-represented” [PAGE 16].

For probabilistic prediction, the Bayesian neural-network form is:

\[
p(y\mid x,\mathcal{D})=\int p(y\mid x,\theta)\,p(\theta\mid \mathcal{D})\,d\theta
\]

where \(y\) is the target, \(x\) is input, \(\theta\) are model parameters, and \(\mathcal{D}\) is training data; \(p(y\mid x,\mathcal{D})\) is a probability density or mass. Breakdown: the integral is generally intractable and is approximated; under model misspecification or distribution shift, the posterior can be confidently wrong. The paper cites Bayesian neural networks for probabilistic prediction and reports precision \(0.948\) with \(F_1=0.858\) in one football shooting-event application [PAGE 16], but that number remains conditional on the validation regime.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Let \(B_i^{(k)}(R)\) denote agent \(i\)’s probability that claim \(R\) is true after \(k\) recursive modelling steps, in probability units. The relevant agents are the analyst, the coach, and the model builder. A crisis habit regression occurs when \(k\) collapses under pressure: the coach regresses to a single headline ACC number or to manual video intuition; the analyst regresses to “the model is black-box, so trust the benchmark”; the model builder regresses to test-set tuning. The paper’s warning that the black-box nature of models makes it difficult “for coaches and analysts to understand and trust AI decisions” [PAGE 16] is exactly the epistemic condition that triggers regression to habit. The counter-trap is the opposite over-correction: distrust every model and retreat to unverified manual annotation. That feels cautious, but it replaces one unverifiable metric with another, because manual frame-by-frame labelling is described as “time-consuming and laborious” [PAGE 8] and is itself prone to observer error. The clock is the third pressure: deadlines, in-season decisions, and data drift make stale validation look current. A model trained on previous seasons can appear precise because the clock has not yet exposed distribution shift. The correct recursive stance is not infinite distrust; it is calibrated trust conditioned on identity-disjoint validation, physical observability, and domain review. The paper’s claim that output must be “interpreted alongside domain expertise to avoid over-reliance” [PAGE 18] sets the recursion depth: the coach should believe the model only to the extent that the analyst can verify the measurement chain, and the analyst should believe the benchmark only to the extent that the validation split excludes athlete and clip leakage. If \(B_{\text{coach}}^{(2)}(R)\) is high while \(B_{\text{analyst}}^{(2)}(R)\) is low because leakage is unresolved, the system is in false consensus, not reliable agreement.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The veto adjudication is layered because a high metric cannot repair a broken measurement. Physical veto: if the observation process has \(I(A;X)=0\) or \(f_s\le 2f_{\max}\), the action is not identifiable; no downstream model should be credited. The paper supports this with occlusion and lighting warnings [PAGE 10] and with the statement that ambient lighting, self-occlusion, and cross-camera alignment challenge practical applicability [PAGE 11]. Biomechanical veto: skeleton-based models require accurate pose estimation. The paper’s table states “Requires accurate pose estimation; real-time performance unverified” for a multi-sport skeleton model [PAGE 9], and it notes that skeletal keypoint approaches depend heavily on pose-estimation accuracy [PAGE 10]. Formally, with joint position error \(e_j=\|\hat p_j-p_j\|_2\) in metres, a biomechanical claim is vetoed when \(\max_j e_j>e_{\text{tol}}\) for the task-specific tolerance \(e_{\text{tol}}\). Weather/environment veto: outdoor performance claims require sensor and visibility conditions above a signal-to-noise threshold. With \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}=10\log_{10}(P_{\text{signal}}/P_{\text{noise}})\) in decibels, a weather claim is vetoed when \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}<\mathrm{SNR}_{\min}\). The paper reports that environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity impacted marathon performance [PAGE 14], and it explicitly says forecasting must consider “environmental variables such as weather” [PAGE 17]. Proof-style adjudication: if the physical layer vetoes, model comparison is invalid because the Bayes error floor is unknown and potentially near chance; if the biomechanical layer vetoes, skeleton or injury-prediction claims are invalid because the input joint coordinates are outside tolerance; if the weather layer vetoes, outdoor generalizability claims are invalid because the test distribution is not the deployment distribution. The veto order is physical, then biomechanical, then weather, then model. The paper’s conclusion that applicability and stability in real-match settings “remain to be established” [PAGE 18] is therefore not a rhetorical caveat; it is the correct adjudication rule.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
A verifiable reward \(r(s)\in\{0,1\}\) should be produced by a deterministic verifier, not by a self-reported metric. Define the veto indicator \(V_k=1\) only if verifier \(\mathcal{V}_k\) passes at threshold \(\tau_k\); otherwise \(V_k=0\) and the claim is vetoed. The three counterfactual scenarios below are testable.

Scenario 1: identity-disjoint replication veto. Verifier: retrain or re-evaluate on a split with \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{train}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{\text{test}}=\varnothing\), where identities are athletes, sessions, or match clips. Veto condition: reported ACC or F1 is not reproduced within the pre-registered confidence interval. Prediction: many high-ACC action-recognition and classification claims will lose rank and show wider variance; studies with small samples and frame-level random splits will fail most often. This follows directly from the paper’s leakage warning [PAGE 16] and its statement that differences in datasets and protocols limit direct comparison [PAGE 9]. If \(V_1=0\), the claim is not deployment evidence, regardless of headline accuracy.

Scenario 2: occlusion-and-lighting stress veto. Verifier: apply real and synthetic occlusion, illumination shift, and camera-view perturbation; measure F1, MOTA, and identity switches. Veto condition: performance falls below the task threshold or IDSW rises beyond the operational bound. Prediction: RGB-only CNN and single-camera appearance models will degrade more than multimodal or pose-fusion models; YOLO-based trackers will show identity drift under occlusion. The source explicitly warns that vision methods are sensitive to occlusion and light changes [PAGE 10] and that identity drift remains obvious in complex scenes [PAGE 11]. If \(V_2=0\), the model is not robust enough for live competition analysis even if its clean-set metric is high.

Scenario 3: edge-latency and Nyquist veto. Verifier: measure end-to-end latency
\[
T_{\text{e2e}}=T_{\text{capture}}+T_{\text{preprocess}}+T_{\text{infer}}+T_{\text{track}}+T_{\text{post}}
\]
in seconds, and require \(T_{\text{e2e}}\le 1/f_s\) with \(f_s\) the operational frame rate in \(\mathrm{Hz}\), plus \(f_s>2f_{\max}\). Veto condition: the pipeline misses the real-time bound on target hardware or aliases fast motion. Prediction: heavy YOLO-LSTM or multi-camera fusion pipelines will fail 30 fps edge deployment; lightweight pruned detectors may pass. The paper reports a pruned badminton YOLO variant with “4 MB weights” and “29.2 fps” [PAGE 12], while another multi-sport pipeline is marked “Heavy computation” [PAGE 12] and a skeleton model has “real-time performance unverified” [PAGE 9]. If \(V_3=0\), the model is an offline analysis tool, not a real-time decision aid. In all three cases, the veto is counterfactual and verifiable: the claim must survive a test that can falsify it, not merely a narrative that celebrates its highest reported metric.
