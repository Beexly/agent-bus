The first multi-hop step is to discretize \(d_t\) into an ordinal category \(y_t\in\{0,\ldots,J\}\), with thresholds such as \(\Delta\) separating weak and strong wins [PAGE 12]. The second step is to place an Adjacent Categories model on those categories: \(\Pr\{Y_t=h|z_t\}\propto 10^{\alpha_h+\delta_h z_t/\sigma}\) [PAGE 13]. The third step is to identify \(z_t=\theta_{t,i_t}-\theta_{t,j_t}\) as the pre-game skill difference [PAGE 14]. The fourth step is maximum-likelihood: the log-likelihood derivative with respect to \(z_t\) collapses to a score residual \(\tilde y_t-G(z_t)\), where \(G(z)\) is the expected score [PAGE 16, PAGE 18]. The fifth step is stochastic-gradient ascent, giving the G-Elo update equations that retain Elo’s form but redefine score and expected score. The source explicitly says: “the equations of the Generalized Elo (G-Elo) rating algorithm we propose in this work are the same as those defining the Elo algorithm in (1), except that the score and the expected score ... are redefined” [PAGE 8].

The gray-zone audit begins when one asks what the model has silently absorbed. Elo’s ternary form is recovered as a special case, but the source warns that “the Elo algorithm is implicitly based on the draw model proposed by Davidson (1970) and assumes a particular value of the draw coefficient that can be related to frequency of draws equal to 50%” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, a return to plain Elo under stress is not a neutral simplification; it imports an implicit draw prior. This is transparent and closed-form, but it is singular when a category frequency is zero, and it cannot estimate \(\delta_h\) when \(\eta=0\). <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines an ordinal \((J+1)\)-ary outcome \(Y_t\in\{0,\ldots,J\}\). The margin variable \(d_t\) is in game-points, e.g. goals or American-football points. The skill difference is
\[
z_t=\theta_{t,i_t}-\theta_{t,j_t},
\]
where \(\theta\) and \(z_t\) are on an arbitrary rating-point scale, and \(\sigma \gt 0\) is a scale parameter in the same rating-point units [PAGE 14]. The AC probability model is
\[
\Pr\{Y_t=h|z_t\}
=
\frac{10^{\alpha_h+\delta_h z_t/\sigma}}
{\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}10^{\alpha_\ell+\delta_\ell z_t/\sigma}},
\quad h=0,\ldots,J,
\]
with dimensionless coefficients \(\alpha_h,\delta_h\) [PAGE 13, PAGE 14]. The log-odds are linear in \(z_t/\sigma\):
\[
\log_{10}\frac{\Pr\{Y_t=h|z_t\}}{\Pr\{Y_t=\ell|z_t\}}
=
(\alpha_h-\alpha_\ell)+(\delta_h-\delta_\ell)\frac{z_t}{\sigma}.
\]
The symmetry constraints are
\[
\alpha_h=\alpha_{J-h},\quad
\delta_h=-\delta_{J-h},\quad
\alpha_0=\alpha_J=0,\quad
\delta_J=-\delta_0=1.
\]
For ordinal interpretation one assumes \(\delta_h\) increases monotonically with \(h\); the paper notes that this is not imposed in optimization and is expected to emerge “naturally” from sufficiently large data [PAGE 23, PAGE 27].

The log-likelihood and its derivative are
\[
L_t(\theta_t)=\log\Pr\{Y_t=y_t|\theta_t\},
\]
\[
\frac{dL_t}{dz_t}
=
\frac{\log 10}{\sigma}
\left[
\delta_{y_t}
-
\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}\delta_\ell 10^{\alpha_\ell+\delta_\ell z_t/\sigma}}
{\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}10^{\alpha_\ell+\delta_\ell z_t/\sigma}}
\right]
=
\frac{2\log 10}{\sigma}
\left[
\tilde y_t-G(z_t)
\right],
\]
where
\[
\tilde y_t=\tilde\delta_{y_t},\quad
\tilde\delta_h=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_h-\delta_0),
\]
\[
G(z)=
\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}\tilde\delta_\ell 10^{\alpha_\ell+2\tilde\delta_\ell z/\sigma}}
{\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}10^{\alpha_\ell+2\tilde\delta_\ell z/\sigma}}.
\]
The transformed score satisfies \(\tilde y_t\in[0,1]\) and \(G(-z)=1-G(z)\). The paper states that “\(G(z)\) has the meaning of the expected score” [PAGE 18]. The stochastic-gradient updates are
\[
\theta_{t+1,i_t}\leftarrow \theta_{t,i_t}+\tilde K\sigma[\tilde y_t-G(z_t)],
\]
\[
\theta_{t+1,j_t}\leftarrow \theta_{t,j_t}-\tilde K\sigma[\tilde y_t-G(z_t)],
\]
or compactly,
\[
\theta_{t+1,i}\leftarrow \theta_{t,i}+\tilde K\sigma[\tilde y-G(z)],
\quad i\in\{i_t,j_t\}.
\]
Here \(\tilde K\) is dimensionless and \(\tilde K\sigma\) has rating-point units; the source says the multiplication by \(\sigma\) makes normalized skills independent of \(\sigma\) [PAGE 17].

The binary and ternary reductions are important for lineage. For \(J=1\),
\[
G(z)=\frac{10^{z/\sigma}}{10^{-z/\sigma}+10^{z/\sigma}},
\]
which recovers binary Elo. For \(J=2\),
\[
G(z)=\frac{10^{z/\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa}{10^{-z/\sigma}+10^{z/\sigma}+\kappa},
\quad \kappa=10^{\alpha_1},
\]
which is the Elo-Davidson expected score. Setting \(\kappa=2\) produces
\[
G(z)=\frac{10^{z/(2\sigma)}}{10^{-z/(2\sigma)}+10^{z/(2\sigma)}},
\]
i.e. binary Elo after rescaling \(\sigma\leftarrow\sigma/2\) [PAGE 19]. This is why Elo’s transition from binary to ternary games appears effortless, while the draw prior remains hidden.

Frequency-based coefficient estimation uses observed category frequencies \(f_h\), dimensionless:
\[
f_h=
\frac{1}{T|S_{\rm train}|}
\sum_{s\in S_{\rm train}}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
I\{y_t^{(s)}=h\}.
\]
The home-field offset is estimated from the ratio of the strongest home and away categories:
\[
\xi=\sqrt{f_0 f_J},
\quad
\eta=\frac{1}{2}\log_{10}\frac{f_J}{f_0},
\]
and then
\[
\alpha_h=\frac{1}{2}\log_{10}(f_h f_{J-h})-\log_{10}\xi,
\quad
\delta_h=\frac{1}{2\eta}\log_{10}\frac{f_h}{f_{J-h}},
\quad h=1,\ldots,J-1.
\]
The home-field advantage enters as \(\Pr\{Y_t=h|z_t\}=P_h(z_t+\eta\sigma)\) [PAGE 24]. Here \(\eta\) is dimensionless and \(\eta\sigma\) is in rating-point units.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. If \(\eta=0\), equivalently \(f_0=f_J\), then \(\delta_h\) in the frequency formula has division by zero and is not identifiable from those equations [PAGE 27].
2. If any \(f_h=0\), then \(\log_{10} f_h\) is undefined; if \(f_0=0\) or \(f_J=0\), then \(\xi=0\) and the normalization becomes singular.
3. If \(\sigma\to 0\), the exponent \(z_t/\sigma\) can overflow whenever \(z_t\ne 0\); if \(\sigma\to\infty\), then \(z_t/\sigma\to 0\) and the model degenerates toward prior category frequencies, losing skill discrimination.
4. If \(\tilde K\to 0\), the rating does not update; if \(\tilde K\) is too large, the stochastic-gradient recursion can oscillate or fail to converge. The paper assumes convergence after half a season, \(\tau=T/2\) [PAGE 33].
5. If \(\delta_h\) is not monotone in \(h\), the AC model’s ordinal interpretation breaks even if the algebra runs.
6. If a discretization threshold \(\Delta\) yields an empty category in training, frequency estimation is impossible without smoothing or pooling.
7. The linearization behind frequency equations is valid only when \(\theta_i-\theta_j\) is small relative to \(\sigma\) [PAGE 25]; large skill dispersion biases \(\eta,\alpha,\delta\).
8. If the schedule is not approximately balanced or equiprobable, the averaging argument behind the frequency equations is misspecified [PAGE 25].
9. If physical, biomechanical, weather, or officiating variables change the category mapping or the slopes \(\delta_h\), the constant-coefficient AC likelihood is misspecified. The model includes only a constant home offset, described as “artificially increasing the rating of the home team by a constant factor \(\theta_{t,i_t}\leftarrow\theta_{t,i_t}+\eta\sigma\)” [PAGE 21].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from Bradley-Terry binary paired comparison to Davidson’s tie extension, then to Elo, then to Elo-Davidson, then to G-Elo. The paper states that the AC model generalizes Bradley-Terry for \(J=1\) and Davidson for \(J=2\), and that these underpin Elo rating [PAGE 15]. It also notes that the ternary Elo algorithm is implicitly Davidson with a draw coefficient tied to a 50% draw frequency [PAGE 4]. Elo-Davidson made the draw parameter and home-field offset explicit; G-Elo moves further by discretizing the MOV into multiple ordinal categories. The statistical ancestry is Agresti’s Adjacent Categories model; the paper contrasts this with the Cumulative Link model used in earlier sport-rating work [PAGE 8]. The modern alternative is Ingram’s Bayesian Gaussian MOV extension, which the paper explicitly separates from its AC approach [PAGE 7, PAGE 8].

This genealogy creates multi-week traps. First, a team may look stronger after a large MOV, but the category threshold \(\Delta\) determines how much of that MOV is treated as “strong.” If \(\Delta\) is chosen after inspecting test seasons, the rating is retrocausally tuned. Second, frequency-based coefficients assume that home-win, draw, and away-win frequencies are stable “locally (within season) and globally (across seasons)” [PAGE 24]. If the league changes scheduling, rules, or competitive balance, the coefficients become stale. Third, the paper’s training-test split uses five seasons for training and five for testing [PAGE 29]; a deployment that recalibrates coefficients on the same season being rated gains adaptivity but loses the transparency argument made for classification [PAGE 21, PAGE 22]. Fourth, optimization-based coefficient estimation can overfit; the paper conjectures that frequency-based estimation “regularizes the estimates” [PAGE 36]. Fifth, using a higher \(J\) adds coefficients and categories, which can improve fit on training data while failing out-of-sample when categories are sparse. These are multi-week and multi-season traps because they are invisible in a single update but accumulate through coefficient drift, threshold drift, and schedule imbalance.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression is the operator’s fallback to plain Elo under uncertainty: ternary or binary Elo feels safe because its update is familiar and has no visible coefficients. But the source shows that this fallback hides a Davidson draw model with an implicit 50% draw frequency [PAGE 4]. Thus the habit is not coefficient-free; it is coefficient-denial. The counter-trap is the opposite overreaction: adding many MOV categories and optimizing \(\alpha,\delta,\eta\) on the same data until the crisis “disappears.” The paper warns that such optimization may overfit and that frequency-based estimation may generalize better [PAGE 36], while frequency equations have singularities when categories are empty or \(\eta=0\) [PAGE 27]. The clock is the disciplined temporal frame: estimate coefficients off-line for transparency, validate on held-out seasons, choose \(\tilde K\) on training data, and allow half-season convergence before judging the rating [PAGE 33]. If prediction is the goal, the paper permits real-time coefficient adjustment [PAGE 21]; if classification and promotion/relegation consequences are the goal, off-line coefficients preserve transparency because “the coefficients are known before all the games” [PAGE 22]. Recursive self-audit therefore asks three questions: Is the operator regressing to hidden Elo priors, is the operator trapped in overfitting complexity, or is the operator respecting the clock of calibration and validation? The correct posture is neither habit nor counter-trap but clocked model selection.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather veto can be proved conditionally. The G-Elo likelihood conditions only on \(z_t\) and the discretized category \(y_t\). If a cross-layer variable \(X_t\)—fatigue, injury status, surface, temperature, wind, travel, or officiating state—affects either the category mapping or the slope coefficients, then the true conditional law is not the constant-coefficient AC law. Formally, if
\[
\Pr\{Y_t=h|z_t,X_t\}
\propto
10^{\alpha_h+\delta_h(z_t/\sigma+\beta_h^\top X_t)},
\]
then the model that omits \(X_t\) estimates a mixture over \(X_t\). This mixture equals the AC form only under special conditions: \(\beta_h=0\), or \(X_t\) is independent of \(Y_t\) given \(z_t\), or the distribution of \(X_t\) is constant across training and deployment. Otherwise the fitted \(G(z)\) is an average over environments and is not invariant to a weather shift, travel regime, or injury wave. A hard veto applies to causal interpretation whenever \(X_t\) changes \(\delta_h\) or \(\alpha_h\); a soft veto applies to prediction when \(X_t\) shifts but the mixture remains calibrated.

The source itself supplies a non-weather example: empty-net goals and referee errors can change the MOV category without reflecting stable skill, and “we might reclassify the event, e.g., from sH to wH” [PAGE 13]. That is direct evidence that the category label is not a pure skill measurement. The only cross-layer variable explicitly modeled is a constant home-field offset \(\eta\sigma\) [PAGE 21]. Weather and biomechanics are absent. Therefore, the veto proof is: physical and weather variables are omitted; if they have nonzero conditional effect on \(Y_t\) and their distribution changes between training and deployment, the AC model’s constant \(\alpha,\delta\) and constant \(\eta\) are misspecified. If they only shift the latent skill \(\theta_t\), stochastic-gradient tracking can partially absorb the shift. If they alter category discrimination or base rates, the model must be re-estimated or vetoed for the affected regime.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
