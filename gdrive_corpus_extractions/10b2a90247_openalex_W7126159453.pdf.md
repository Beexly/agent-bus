f_c(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{c,i}y_iK(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x})+b_c.

On standardised features, \(\gamma\) is dimensionless because the squared Euclidean norm is dimensionless. On raw mixed-unit features, \(\gamma\) would need inverse squared feature-norm units, which is one reason standardisation is required. After standardisation, \(d\) is dimensionless. Three recursive modes are visible. The first is crisis habit regression. When the classifier’s boundary is uncertain—especially between defence and midfield, where “The majority of misclassifications occurred” [PAGE 5]—the coach’s fallback is likely to be the older habit: eye test, prior reputation, and current role. The paper itself positions the method as an aid: quantitative data “supports the coach’s intuition” [PAGE 1], and it contrasts ML with the tradition in which “playing position determination relied largely on the experience of coaches and subjective assessments based on observation” [PAGE 2]. Under crisis, the decision-maker can regress to that tradition and treat the model as a confirmatory ornament. That is not irrational; it is a natural response to a model that is strong for forwards but only moderate for defence and midfield.

The second is the counter-trap. The analyst tries to counter coach subjectivity with an “objective” ML pipeline, but the target is the coach’s label [PAGE 1]. The model therefore learns a compressed image of coach belief plus measured performance. If the coach then uses the model to allocate positional training, the athlete’s behavior changes, and the next label is co-produced by the model. The recursive belief structure is: coach belief shapes label; label shapes model; model shapes coach belief; coach belief shapes athlete opportunity; athlete opportunity shapes future performance and future label. The paper’s claim that early prediction can give athletes “more experience in their current playing positions” [PAGE 2] is exactly the feedback channel. The counter-trap is that a tool intended to reduce subjectivity can harden the very prior it was meant to check.

The third is the clock. The developmental clock is not a metaphor here; the sample is aged 15–17, and the paper states that “playing positional roles are not yet fully established in young players (aged 15–17)” [PAGE 8]. A classifier trained at one developmental moment has a shelf life. Over a multi-week training block, athletes may mature, change club role, recover from minor injury, or alter technical habits. The correct theory-of-mind posture is therefore probabilistic and time-indexed: the coach should treat the output as a current-label recovery estimate, not as a fixed aptitude diagnosis. The athlete should not internalize the label as identity. The analyst should pre-register re-test intervals and external holdouts. The clock also creates an ethical recursion: a forward-labeled player may receive different coaching, which changes the probability of remaining a forward. The paper’s own limitations—missing decision-making, game reading, acceleration, agility, and aerobic/anaerobic capacity [PAGE 10]—mean the clock can outrun the feature set. The safe recursive rule is: use the model to generate questions and testing schedules, not to close the athlete’s positional identity.

The cross-layer veto is proven by variable inventory. The source’s modelled variables are age, height, weight, BMI, mixed juggling, head juggling, shooting, dribbling, and 20 m dribbling [PAGE 1][PAGE 6]. The paper says “The variables used in the study are largely based on technical performance tests” [PAGE 10]. It also states that “performance components such as decision-making, game reading, playing positioning, acceleration, agility, and aerobic/anaerobic capacity were not measured” [PAGE 10]. Therefore any physical-capacity claim beyond the measured anthropometry and technical tests is unidentified. The study can say that height, BMI, dribbling, and heading differ by coach-assigned group; it cannot say that aerobic capacity, sprint mechanics, or match external load caused the classification, because those variables are absent.

The biomechanical veto is even sharper. The source contains no joint kinematics, no kinetics, no force-plate data, no electromyography, no centre-of-mass modelling, and no injury-mechanics assay. The tests are field tasks: juggling, slalom dribbling, 20 m dribbling, and shooting at target circles [PAGE 3]. The participants were also selected as healthy, with exclusions for orthopaedic problems, heart and lung problems, and recent musculoskeletal injury [PAGE 3]. That healthy-volunteer selection further blocks biomechanical generalization to injured or load-compromised athletes. A claim that the SVM has learned a biomechanical mechanism is therefore vetoed: the model has learned associations among performance outcomes and anthropometric summaries, not mechanism.

The weather and environment veto is similarly absolute for this source. No temperature, humidity, wind, rain, altitude, surface type, footwear, or time-of-day covariates appear in the variable list [PAGE 1][PAGE 6]. The tests are described on a flat track and a goal setup [PAGE 3], but no environmental stratification or interaction term is reported. Therefore the paper cannot support a claim that positional classification is weather-robust, weather-sensitive, or transferable across climates. The cross-layer adjudication rule is: if a proposed layer \(Z\) is not in the measured set and is not randomized or stratified, its causal effect is confounded with unmeasured context. Here \(Z_{\mathrm{weather}}\) and \(Z_{\mathrm{biomech}}\) are both outside the design. The only defensible conclusion is a measured-variable classification result under the study’s specific age, sex, health, and testing conditions. Any stronger physical, biomechanical, or weather claim is vetoed.
