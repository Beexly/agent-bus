The core operator is the latent-permutation observation equation from [PAGE 7]: \(Y=\Pi X\), where \(Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times2}\) contains player locations, \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times2}\) contains role locations, and \(\Pi\) is a random permutation matrix independent of \(X\). The role law is \(X_k\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_k,\Sigma_k)\) independently for \(k=1,\dots,d\). With \(d=11\), raw coordinates are in the tracking coordinate system; normalized coordinates are dimensionless, so \(\mu_k\) is in coordinate units and \(\Sigma_k\) in coordinate-squared units. If raw tracking is in metres, then \(\mu_k\) is m and \(\Sigma_k\) is m\(^2\); after per-frame centering and scaling by standard deviation, both are dimensionless. The joint density of \(X\) is
\[
g(x;\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^d\sqrt{\prod_{k=1}^d\det(\Sigma_k)}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac12\sum_{k=1}^d(\mu_k-x_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mu_k-x_k)\right),
\]
with density units coordinate\(^{-2d}\). The observed mixture is
\[
f(y;\mu,\Sigma,w)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_d}w_Q\,g(Q^\top y;\mu,\Sigma),
\]
where \(w_Q\ge0\), \(\sum_Qw_Q=1\), and weights are dimensionless. The log-likelihood on [PAGE 7] is
\[
L_\theta(y)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\!\left[\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_d}w_Q
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^d\sqrt{\prod_{k=1}^d\det(\Sigma_k)}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac12\sum_{k=1}^d(\mu_k-(Q^\top y^{(i)})_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mu_k-(Q^\top y^{(i)})_k)\right)\right].
\]
The cardinality breakdown is exact: the support has \(d!\) permutations, so storing all weights requires \(d!-1\) free probabilities; for \(d=11\), \(11!-1=39{,}916{,}799\) weights. The paper avoids this by selecting a sparse support. The shared-component construction on [PAGE 8] and [PAGE 9] has, for player component \(l\),
\[
f_l(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{11}\pi_{l,k}\,\mathcal{N}(z\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k),
\quad
\pi_{l,k}=\mathbb{E}(\Pi_{l,k})=\mathbb{P}(\Pi_{l,k}=1),
\]
with \(\sum_k\pi_{l,k}=1\) but no column-sum constraint because one player is sampled per constructed frame. Its log-likelihood is
\[
L_\theta(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/11\rfloor}\sum_{l=1}^{11}
\log\!\left[\sum_{k=1}^{11}\pi_{l,k}\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det(\Sigma_k)}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac12(z_l^{(j)}-\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(z_l^{(j)}-\mu_k)\right)\right],
\]
with component densities in coordinate\(^{-2}\) units and \(\pi_{l,k}\) dimensionless.

Permutation selection uses overlap. Define
\[
v(p,q)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times2}}\min(p(x),q(x))\,dx,
\]
which is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1]\). Proposition 2.1 on [PAGE 9] and [PAGE 10] gives
\[
v(p,q)\ge \min(\mathbb{P}(\Pi=Q^\top),\mathbb{P}(\Pi=\mathrm{Id}))
+\min(\mathbb{P}(\Pi=Q),\mathbb{P}(\Pi=\mathrm{Id})).
\]
Thus if \(v(p,q)\le o_{\mathrm{tresh}}\), then \(\mathbb{P}(\Pi=Q)+\mathbb{P}(\Pi=Q^\top)\le o_{\mathrm{tresh}}\); for a transposition or disjoint transposition product, \(Q=Q^\top\), so the necessary probability bound is \(o_{\mathrm{tresh}}/2\). The paper sets \(o_{\mathrm{tresh}}=5\%\) and \(p_{\mathrm{tresh}}=2.5\%\) on [PAGE 13]. The classifier operator is on [PAGE 30]:
\[
a_\Phi=\frac12\mathbb{P}(\Phi(Z_1)&lt;0)+\frac12\mathbb{P}(\Phi(Z_2)\ge0)\ge\frac12 v(p,q),
\]
so \(2a_\Phi\) upper-bounds overlap; the empirical version is
\[
\hat a_\Phi=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(1_{\Phi(Z_{1,i})&lt;0}+1_{\Phi(Z_{2,i})\ge0}\right),
\]
and the asymptotic upper bound is
\[
2\hat a_{\hat\Phi}+\frac{z_{1-\alpha}}{\sqrt{2n}},
\]
which is dimensionless. QDA uses
\[
\Phi(y)=h(y;\mu_1,\Sigma_1)-h(y;\mu_2,\Sigma_2),
\]
with \(h\) the Gaussian density [PAGE 31]. The Bayesian Gaussian mixture replaces each class density by a mixture, with separate training and test samples to preserve the upper-bound logic [PAGE 32]. The regime extension on [PAGE 11] has
\[
L_\theta(y)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\!\left[\sum_{r=1}^l v_r\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_d}w_{r,Q}
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^d\sqrt{\prod_{k=1}^d\det(\Sigma_{r,k})}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac12\sum_{k=1}^d(\mu_{r,k}-(Q^\top y^{(i)})_k)^\top\Sigma_{r,k}^{-1}(\mu_{r,k}-(Q^\top y^{(i)})_k)\right)\right],
\]
with \(v_r=\mathbb{P}(R=r)\), \(w_{r,Q}=\mathbb{P}(\Pi=Q\mid R=r)\), and dimensionless regime and permutation weights. The expected role assignment in regime \(r\) is
\[
\hat\pi_r=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}_d}\hat w_{r,Q}Q,
\]
the frame regime posterior is
\[
\hat v_{i,r}=
\frac{\sum_{Q}g(Q^\top y^{(i)};\hat\mu_r,\hat\Sigma_r)\hat w_{r,Q}\hat v_r}
{\sum_{s=1}^l\sum_{Q}g(Q^\top y^{(i)};\hat\mu_s,\hat\Sigma_s)\hat w_{s,Q}\hat v_s},
\]
and the frame permutation posterior is
\[
\hat w_{i,r,Q}=
\frac{g(Q^\top y^{(i)};\hat\mu_r,\hat\Sigma_r)\hat w_{r,Q}}
{\sum_{Q}g(Q^\top y^{(i)};\hat\mu_r,\hat\Sigma_r)\hat w_{r,Q}}.
\]
The Bhattacharyya coefficient used on [PAGE 17] is
\[
\mathrm{BC}(f_1,f_2)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\sqrt{f_1(x)f_2(x)}\,dx,
\]
dimensionless. The Mixture-Wasserstein formation distance on [PAGE 22] has units of coordinate if raw coordinates are used; for two Gaussians it is closed-form via means and covariance matrices. The model assumptions are: independent frames within a game [PAGE 7]; independent Gaussian roles; sparse permutation support; non-negligible identity probability; identity alignment; classifier independence where required; finite regimes; substitution-free, at-least-five-minute segments; one frame every five frames [PAGE 5]; and no explicit temporal dependence. Singularities/breakdown regimes are: \(\det(\Sigma_k)=0\) or EM covariance collapse; \(d!\) support explosion; \(o_{\mathrm{tresh}}\) or \(p_{\mathrm{tresh}}\) mis-set so true permutations are discarded or too many retained; violation of \(\mathbb{P}(\Pi=\mathrm{Id})>o_{\mathrm{tresh}}\); classifier leakage in the Bayesian mixture; label switching under role permutation; overfitting the number of regimes; non-identifiability of causal claims not represented in the likelihood; and temporal autocorrelation violating frame independence.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is visibly layered. The historical narrative on [PAGE 1] moves from early 2-3-5 attacking shapes to 4-4-2 and 4-3-3, then to more flexible systems; the statistical lineage then recasts those historical formations as latent Gaussian role areas. The K-means role-assignment procedure of Bialkowski et al. is the immediate predecessor: the source describes it on [PAGE 2] as an iterative assignment of players to roles by maximizing total likelihood, and later on [PAGE 15] notes that hard assignment “is particularly sensitive to overlapping classes” while also saying that K-means is equivalent to EM in the well-separated-cluster limit. This is a retrocausal relation: the newer soft-mixture model contains the older hard-assignment method as a limiting case, not as a separate theory. A second lineage branch is formation-phase detection: Bauer et al. and Shaw and Glickman are cited on [PAGE 2] for segmenting phases and identifying formations, while Narizuka and Yamazaki are cited for Delaunay-graph adjacency. The source’s regime variable generalizes phase segmentation into a latent mixture rather than a supervised or CNN-segmented phase label. A third branch is permutation-space probability: Huang et al. and Plis et al. are cited on [PAGE 3] as Fourier and von Mises–Fisher approaches; this paper’s contribution is sparse support selection rather than full permutation density estimation. A fourth branch is overlap and classifier theory: García et al., Vuttipittayamongkol et al., Johno and Nakamoto, Freedman and Diaconis, and Póczos and Schneider are cited on [PAGE 3] and [PAGE 4] for overlap and density estimation; QDA is chosen because it needs only means and covariances and scales to repeated permutation tests. A fifth branch is optimal-transport formation comparison: Delon and Desolneux’s Gaussian-mixture Wasserstein distance is cited on [PAGE 22], and the paper’s embedding for K-means is tied to sliced-Wasserstein geometry on [PAGE 23]. A sixth branch is finite-mixture identifiability: Yakowitz and Spragins is cited on [PAGE 29] and used in the appendix proof on [PAGE 35] to establish identifiability up to role permutation.

The multi-week traps are the persistent analytic errors that would survive a single good match and corrupt a season-scale study. One trap is treating normalized relative positions as absolute tactical zones: [PAGE 2] explicitly normalizes by subtracting frame mean and dividing by standard deviation, so a multi-week comparison of “where” a role stands must either undo normalization or compare only shape. A second trap is ignoring substitutions: [PAGE 5] says segments are cut where no substitution has occurred and short segments are discarded, so pooling across substitutions without re-segmentation can create artificial role swaps. A third trap is threshold drift: \(p_{\mathrm{tresh}}=2.5\%\) and \(o_{\mathrm{tresh}}=5\%\) on [PAGE 13] are fixed for the reported application, but across weeks and teams the sparse-support assumption may fail; a threshold that is too high discards real permutations, while one too low re-inflates the support. A fourth trap is classifier leakage: the Bayesian Gaussian mixture must use separate training and test data [PAGE 32], or the upper bound is not trustworthy; this is exactly the kind of error that can persist across many weekly fits because it produces plausible-looking but under-pruned supports. A fifth trap is regime-count overfitting: the two-regime model yields an outlier cluster [PAGE 17], and the three-regime model labels one regime transitional or disorganized [PAGE 18]; without penalized model selection, adding regimes can always absorb noise. A sixth trap is misreading Bhattacharyya overlap: [PAGE 17] says high overlap may be true role intersection or estimation failure, so a multi-week trend in overlap is not automatically a tactical trend. A seventh trap is treating the no-swap probability as a psychological trait rather than a positional statistic: [PAGE 22] reports team-level no-swap values such as Troyes and Nice at the high end, but the quantity is an average over frames and matches, weighted by segment length; it is not a direct measure of coach intent. An eighth trap is formation clustering that ignores covariance: [PAGE 23] says the embedding uses “the mean of each role” and projects along 12 directions, so the K-means clusters in [PAGE 24] and [PAGE 25] are centroid-shape clusters, not full distributional clusters. A ninth trap is temporal dependence: [PAGE 27] names hidden Markov models as an extension, which means weekly independent-frame fitting can understate persistence. These traps are “multi-week” because each one is locally invisible in a single frame but accumulates when the analyst aggregates match segments, teams, and scouting profiles.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer must be stated carefully: the paper measures positions, permutations, and regimes; it does not measure beliefs, intent, panic, or morale. A defensible recursive reading is an observer–agent–opponent hierarchy. The coach selects a formation and tactical regime; players execute role assignments and may swap; the opponent’s possession state changes the observed regime; the analyst infers \(R\) and \(\Pi\) from \(Y\). In the model, the analyst’s belief is the posterior \(\hat v_{i,r}\) and \(\hat w_{i,r,Q}\); the coach’s “habit” is not directly observed but can be proxied by the identity probability \(w_{\mathrm{Id}}\) and by regime persistence. “Crisis habit regression” would be the hypothesis that under pressure a team reverts to a high-\(w_{\mathrm{Id}}\), low-swap formation. The source gives only indirect evidence: [PAGE 22] says “Teams exhibiting higher no-swap probabilities, such as Troyes and Nice, demonstrate a strong adherence to their role assignments throughout the game,” and [PAGE 20] says “the goalkeeper, center-backs, defensive midfielder, and center-forward maintain consistent roles,” while wingers swap more. That is positional rigidity, not proof of crisis. A crisis interpretation requires an external pressure variable, such as score state, opponent quality, or time under pressure; those are absent from the likelihood.

The “counter-trap” is better grounded. The paper’s overlap criterion is explicitly not sufficient: [PAGE 10] warns that overlap can arise because of other permutations, and [PAGE 17] warns that high Bhattacharyya overlap may be either true intersection or estimation failure. In recursive terms, an opponent can create a transitional or disorganized regime by ceding possession or by forcing unassigned possession; the analyst may then mistake the resulting outlier regime for a deliberate tactical phase. The source supports this: [PAGE 18] says “the second regime shows a positive correlation with unassigned possession, supporting the hypothesis that it captures outlying frames,” while “Regime 1 is correlated at 51.06% with Nantes holding the ball, while Regime 3 exhibits a 63.98% correlation with Rennes possession.” Thus the counter-trap is to reify every latent regime as an intentional plan. A verifiable analyst should ask whether the regime posterior correlates with possession, transition, or missing structure, not whether the regime “looks clever.”

The “clock” is substitution time and match time. The paper segments matches by substitution-free intervals and discards segments shorter than five minutes [PAGE 5], which means the clock is already a data-construction variable. On [PAGE 23] the paper reports that “the last substitution typically results in larger formation changes,” while the first substitution is usually player-for-player. This is a clock-dependent tactical pattern: early changes preserve roles, late changes disrupt structure. A recursive theory-of-mind statement should therefore be temporal: the analyst’s posterior over \(R\) at minute \(t\) is conditioned on the substitution segment, the possession state, and the estimated no-swap probability; it should not be treated as a static team “identity.” The source’s own limitation is that it assumes frame independence [PAGE 7] and proposes hidden Markov models [PAGE 27], so the clock enters only through segmentation and post-hoc regime plots, not through a fully dynamic belief update. The safe conclusion is: crisis habit regression is an interpretive hypothesis requiring external pressure data; counter-trap is a real model-risk arising from permutation/overlap ambiguity; clock is a measurable conditioning variable through substitution segments and the first-versus-last substitution distance result.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is straightforward. The model is fitted to \(Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times2}\) after optional normalization; [PAGE 2] says normalization is done by subtracting the frame mean and dividing by the standard deviation along each axis, so the analysis is about “relative positions rather than absolute positions.” Any claim about absolute pitch zones, absolute distance run, or absolute physical location is not identified by the normalized likelihood. The raw tracking data are 25 frames per second, but [PAGE 5] says the data are sub-sampled “keeping one frame every 5 frames,” and frames with missing players are excluded in the reported empirical section [PAGE 15]. Therefore a physical claim at sub-five-frame resolution or across missing-player intervals is vetoed by the preprocessing. Substitutions impose another physical veto: [PAGE 5] says the match is segmented into sequences where no substitution has occurred and segments shorter than five minutes are discarded. A formation estimate is valid within such a segment; it is not automatically valid across a substitution boundary.

The biomechanical-layer veto is also direct. The source contains no sprint load, acceleration, heart rate, muscle activation, or injury variable. The only biomechanical-adjacent interpretation is the statement on [PAGE 20] that “the wingers’ positions are significantly influenced by their dominant foot.” That is a tactical–anatomical conjecture about wide-player side preference, not a measured biomechanical load model. The likelihood in Equation (5) has parameters \((\mu_{r,k},\Sigma_{r,k},w_{r,Q},v_r)\) and no biomechanical covariate; therefore a claim that fatigue, dominant-foot load, or repeated sprint exposure caused a regime is not identifiable from the source. It would require adding a biomechanical variable \(B\) and showing \(\partial L/\partial B\neq0\) or a controlled comparison. Absent that, the biomechanical attribution is vetoed.

The weather-layer veto is even stronger: there is no weather variable, no temperature, no precipitation, no wind, and no pitch-condition covariate in the model. The source’s own causal language is deliberately cautious: [PAGE 17] says high Bhattacharyya overlap “may result either from truly intersecting roles or from the estimation process failing to disentangle role swaps from player locations.” If an analyst attributes a high-overlap regime to weather, the required variable is missing from the likelihood; the model cannot distinguish weather from role-swap ambiguity, outlier frames, or possession-state effects. The correct adjudication is not “weather is false”; it is “weather is non-identifiable in this source and therefore vetoed as an explanation.”

A formal veto proof can be stated as follows. Let the source-supported data be \(D=(Y,S,T)\), where \(S\) is possession state and \(T\) is substitution-segment/time information. The fitted likelihood is \(p(D\mid\mu,\Sigma,w,v)\). Let \(W\) be weather and \(B\) be biomechanical load. The source model has no factor \(p(W)\) or \(p(B)\), and no conditional \(p(Y\mid W,B,\mu,\Sigma,w,v)\) beyond the latent permutation/regime structure. Therefore
\[
p(D\mid\mu,\Sigma,w,v,W,B)=p(D\mid\mu,\Sigma,w,v)
\]
for all \(W,B\) in the source’s observation space. Any claim \(C\) that requires \(W\) or \(B\) to change the likelihood has zero identified signal in this source; it can only be added as an external prior or a new measurement layer. Physical claims about absolute position are similarly vetoed by the normalization step, and substitution-crossing claims are vetoed by the segmentation rule. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: use the model for relative role geometry, permutation probabilities, regime posteriors, and possession-conditioned structure; do not use it as proof of weather effects, biomechanical fatigue, absolute physical load, or psychological crisis. Those require additional layers and explicit covariates.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR check 1: remove the latent permutation and force identity. Counterfactual: set \(w_{\mathrm{Id}}=1\) or fit the hard-assignment baseline instead of the Gaussian mixture with permutations. The source’s simulation on [PAGE 14] uses two Gaussian roles with mean vectors \(\mu_1=-\mu_2=(1.5,0)\), then applies a random permutation “with probability p = 0.2.” The prediction is verifiable: for small separation \(\delta\) between role means, the forced-identity or hard-assignment estimator should have higher mean squared error in recovering \((\mu_1,\mu_2)\) than the soft permutation mixture, because [PAGE 15] states that “the hard-assignment approach is particularly sensitive to overlapping classes.” Verification metric: replicate the 100-run MSE curve in Figure 4 across \(\delta\in[0.1,2]\). Veto condition: if forced identity matches or beats the permutation mixture at small \(\delta\), the paper’s central claim that latent permutations disentangle overlap is falsified for that regime.

RLVR check 2: replace held-out Bayesian-mixture overlap estimation with same-sample training. Counterfactual: train the Bayesian Gaussian mixture on the same frames used to estimate the classifier error rate, contrary to the separation rule described on [PAGE 32]. Prediction: the same-sample classifier overfits, the empirical error rate \(\hat a_{\hat\Phi}\) falls toward zero, the upper bound \(2\hat a_{\hat\Phi}+z_{1-\alpha}/\sqrt{2n}\) becomes too small, and the selection procedure discards more permutations, including some with non-negligible true probability. Verification metric: in a simulation with known \(\mu_k,\Sigma_k\) and known \(w_Q\), measure the false-discard rate for the true permutation and the number of retained permutations under same-sample versus held-out training. Veto condition: if same-sample training does not increase false discards and does not shrink the retained support relative to held-out training, the claimed necessity of the independence split is not supported. This is a direct RLVR counterfactual because the source explicitly warns that without separate data “we lack garantees that the resulting estimation provides an asymptotic upper-bound to the overlap” [PAGE 32].
