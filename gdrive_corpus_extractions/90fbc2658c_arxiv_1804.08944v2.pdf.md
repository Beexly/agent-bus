The source is an operator chain, not a single model. The paper first defines a pose as a 14-joint 2D point set, \(p=\{(x_k,y_k)\}_{k=1}^{N}\), with \(N=14\) and frame-indexed time under a constant frame-rate assumption [PAGE 2]. The first nontrivial operator is a similarity alignment: for a reference pose \(p_r\) and a candidate pose \(p\), the paper solves for scale \(s\), rotation \(\theta\), and translation \((t_x,t_y)\) by minimizing a mean-square error over reshaped joint coordinates, using the pseudo-inverse \(t_{\mathrm{opt}}=(A^T A)^{-1}A^T p_{r,\mathrm{reshaped}}\) [PAGE 2]. This is the correct place to start a gray-zone audit because the raw MSE is not a metric in the mathematical sense: the paper explicitly states, “It is not symmetric, i.e., generally MSE(pr ,p) , MSE(p,pr ).” [PAGE 2], and it also states, “Its magnitude depends on the scale of the reference pose. Doubling the reference pose’s scale will quadruple the MSE value.” [PAGE 2]. Therefore any downstream threshold applied directly to raw MSE is scale-inconsistent. The paper’s remedy is the scale normalization in Eq. (8): each pose is normalized by its own average joint-to-center-of-mass distance \(s_p\), then the two directed MSEs are averaged with an arbitrary reference scale \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}\) [PAGE 3]. This converts the operator into a symmetric, translation-, rotation-, and scale-normalized distance, but it is still expressed in squared coordinate units because \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}\) carries units. With \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\) and threshold \(49\), the paper’s own interpretation is “on avg. 7 pixels in each direction for each joint” [PAGE 3]. Thus a plate-scale change from 100 to 200 without a corresponding threshold rescaling would silently invalidate the matching graph.

The swimming pipeline is a multi-hop graph over this distance. For each pose \(p\), the method compares against every other pose \(p'\), keeps matches below threshold in \(L_p\), temporally clusters the matched frame numbers, consolidates each cluster to a representative pose, discards clusters whose temporal width is too large, and in the experiments sets that width threshold to 10 frames [PAGE 4]. It then keeps only recurrence sequences in the longest 50% by count [PAGE 4], estimates frame differences between consecutive matches, removes differences deviating more than 10% from a local median, and fits a piecewise degree-5 polynomial over roughly three cycles with a smoothness condition at boundaries [PAGE 4]. The resulting time-continuous cycle length is converted to strokes per minute by Eq. (9), where the inverse of frames per stroke is multiplied by frames per second and 60 seconds per minute [PAGE 3]. The gray zone is that every step assumes the athlete is mostly visible and that cyclic motion dominates the recording. The paper admits missing poses are possible, says “we discard all poses that are only partially detected to make mining simpler” [PAGE 2], and later uses a 10-second minimum cyclic range because shorter ranges indicate detection difficulty from “strong reflections, water splashes, spray and/or air bubbles in the water” [PAGE 6]. A 10-second rule is therefore a physical-support veto, not a performance statement.

The long-jump branch uses a different operator grammar: unsupervised k-Medoids clustering over the normalized pose distance, a cluster-to-phase probability \(P(c|h(p))\), transition probabilities from observed phase transitions, and Viterbi decoding of the most likely phase sequence [PAGE 7]. The paper reports mAP \(0.89\) at IoU threshold \(0.5\), with flight phase \(0.94\) and final landing \(0.97\), and average run-up duration error \(60\,\mathrm{ms}\) [PAGE 8]. The gray-zone audit must separate phase recognition from kinematic inference: a correct phase label does not prove joint-level accuracy under camera pan, spectators, or background false detections. The largest traps are scale-threshold coupling, partial-pose discard, temporal-support minimum, edit-distance denominator degeneracy, and the difference between coach-visible salient angles and system-visible temporal salience. The paper itself notes that human-salient poses “typically focus on one or two characteristic angles” while its striking pose is “a pose which is easy to localize temporally by our system” [PAGE 6]. Any claim that system salience equals coaching salience is therefore a category error unless independently validated.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**Pose representation.** The paper defines a 2D pose as a sequence of \(N\) points, \(p=\{(x_k,y_k)\}_{k=1}^{N}\), with \(N=14\) joints and frame-indexed time under constant frame rate [PAGE 2]. Coordinates are image-plane coordinates; when the paper sets \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\) and threshold \(49\), it is effectively using pixel coordinates and squared-pixel error [PAGE 3].

**Similarity alignment.** For reference pose \(p_r\) and candidate \(p\), the reshaped candidate is \(p'_{\mathrm{reshaped}} = A\,t_{\mathrm{trans}}\), with \(t_{\mathrm{trans}}=(a,b,t_x,t_y)^T\), \(a=s\cos\theta\), \(b=s\sin\theta\) [PAGE 2]. The least-squares transformation is
\[
t_{\mathrm{opt}}=(A^T A)^{-1}A^T p_{r,\mathrm{reshaped}},
\]
and the aligned point is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
+
\begin{pmatrix}t_x\\t_y\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The raw objective is
\[
\mathrm{MSE}(p_r,p)=\frac{1}{2N}\|p_{r,\mathrm{reshaped}}-p'_{\mathrm{reshaped}}\|_2^2,
\]
with units of squared image coordinates, i.e. \(\mathrm{px}^2\) under the paper’s pixel interpretation [PAGE 2]. Assumption: the pseudo-inverse is taken over real coordinates and the reference and candidate have the same joint correspondence. Breakdown regimes: \(A^T A\) is singular or ill-conditioned when the joint configuration is degenerate, most obviously when all joints coincide and scale/rotation are unidentifiable; raw MSE is also non-symmetric and scale-dependent, as the paper explicitly states [PAGE 2].

**Pose scale and normalized distance.** The pose scale is
\[
s_p=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x_k\\y_k\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}c_{p,x}\\c_{p,y}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_2,
\quad
\begin{pmatrix}c_{p,x}\\c_{p,y}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix}x_k\\y_k\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This has units of image length, \(\mathrm{px}\) under pixel coordinates [PAGE 3]. The normalized distance is
\[
\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{norm}}(p_1,p_2)
=
\frac{s_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{2s_{p_1}^2}\mathrm{MSE}(p_1,p_2)
+
\frac{s_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{2s_{p_2}^2}\mathrm{MSE}(p_2,p_1).
\]
Because the prefactors are dimensionless but \(\mathrm{MSE}\) is in \(\mathrm{px}^2\), \(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{norm}}\) is also in \(\mathrm{px}^2\) when \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is in pixels. The paper uses a target scale \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\) and threshold \(49\) [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: \(s_{p_1}=0\) or \(s_{p_2}=0\) makes the expression undefined; \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=0\) collapses the distance to zero; changing \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}\) by a factor \(\lambda\) multiplies the distance by \(\lambda^2\), so threshold \(49\) is only valid for the stated reference scale unless rescaled. This is not a toy ratio; it is the paper’s own scale-normalization equation.

**Cycle speed.** The paper defines strokes per minute by
\[
\frac{\#\mathrm{strokes}}{\mathrm{minute}}
=
\left(\frac{\#\mathrm{frames}}{\mathrm{stroke}}\right)^{-1}
\cdot
\frac{\#\mathrm{frames}}{\mathrm{second}}
\cdot
60\ \frac{\mathrm{seconds}}{\mathrm{minute}}.
\]
Units: \((\mathrm{stroke}/\mathrm{frame})(\mathrm{frame}/\mathrm{s})(\mathrm{s}/\mathrm{min})=\mathrm{stroke}/\mathrm{min}\) [PAGE 3]. Assumption: constant video sampling rate and a detected reoccurrence of the same pose one cycle earlier. Breakdown: zero frames per stroke, missing poses around the cycle boundary, or a false match at one-half the true cycle length produce a doubled or halved estimate; the paper reports one such failure in Figure 5 where stroke length was “incorrectly estimated twice as high as it actually was” [PAGE 6].

**Recurrence and robust cycle estimation.** The match list is
\[
L_p=\left\{\left(f_{p'},p',\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{norm}}(p,p')\right)\right\}_{f_{p'}},
\]
with thresholded matches, temporal clustering, consolidation to \(L'_p\), deletion of clusters with temporal width greater than 10 frames, retention of the 50% longest recurrence sequences, and local median filtering with a \(\pm 10\%\) deviation cut [PAGE 4]. The paper states, “We decided to keep only those reoccurrence sequences L′′p which belong to the 50% longest ones” [PAGE 4], and “We remove all difference values at frame number f , which deviate more than 10% from the median” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: too few cycles make the median and polynomial unstable; a stationary non-cyclic pose can create close matches; and the 10-frame cluster-width threshold is an experimental setting, not a universal physical constant.

**Saliency.** The saliency operator sums normalized or raw MSE over temporal offsets \(\Delta w_l\) and \(\Delta w_s\), divided by \((2w_s+1)(2w_l+1)\) [PAGE 4]. The paper says, “Both were arbitrarily set to 4” [PAGE 4]. Units are squared image coordinates if raw MSE is used. Breakdown: a degenerate pose, repeated identical frames, or a stationary segment can make all saliency values equal; the paper’s own use is not a coach-defined key pose but a pose “easy to localize temporally by our system” [PAGE 6].

**Edit-distance stability.** The bounded distance function is
\[
\mathrm{dist\_fct}(p_1,p_2)=
\begin{cases}
0, & \mathrm{MSEn}(p_1,p_2)\le th_{\mathrm{same}},\\
\frac{\mathrm{MSEn}(p_1,p_2)-th_{\mathrm{same}}}{th_{\mathrm{diff}}-th_{\mathrm{same}}}, & \mathrm{MSEn}(p_1,p_2)\ge th_{\mathrm{diff}},\\
1, & \mathrm{else}.
\end{cases}
\]
The paper states, “The cost of transforming one pose into another is 0 for poses which are considered the same (MSEn(p1,p2) ≤thsame) and 1 for poses which are considered different (MSEn(p1,p2) ≥thdif f ).” [PAGE 5]. The function is dimensionless only if \(\mathrm{MSEn}\), \(th_{\mathrm{same}}\), and \(th_{\mathrm{diff}}\) share units; the ratio is dimensionless. Breakdown: \(th_{\mathrm{diff}}=th_{\mathrm{same}}\) gives division by zero; an empty pattern or zero virtual matching length makes normalization undefined; and a matching score is not a probability.

**HMM phase decoding.** The long-jump phase model uses \(P(c|h(p))\), emission \(P(h(p)|c)=\alpha P(c|h(p))P(h(p))\), transition probabilities from observed transitions, and
\[
c^*_{1:T}=\arg\max_{c_{1:T}}P(c_{1:T}|h(p)_{1:T}).
\]
These probabilities are dimensionless in \([0,1]\); mAP and AP are dimensionless [PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. Assumptions: similar poses often belong to the same phase, athletes stay in phases for some time, and transitions follow a fixed sequential pattern [PAGE 7]. Breakdown: an unseen pose cluster with zero emission for a phase, a disconnected transition graph, or phase labels with unequal support can make Viterbi path selection brittle; the paper’s reported mAP is \(0.89\) at IoU \(\tau=0.5\), not a guarantee for all cameras [PAGE 8].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit. The paper builds on DNN-based human pose estimation, citing stacked hourglass networks, convolutional pose machines, and benchmark work on 2D pose estimation [PAGE 1][PAGE 9]. It also inherits the older sequence-matching tradition: Levenshtein distance and approximate substring matching are used to compare pose clips to longer pose sequences [PAGE 5], while affinity propagation is used to cluster salient poses [PAGE 4]. The long-jump branch inherits hidden Markov models and Viterbi decoding, with phase transitions constrained by a fixed sequential pattern [PAGE 7]. This genealogy matters because the paper’s novelty is not a new pose detector; it is the mining layer that converts noisy, annotation-free pose streams into time-continuous kinematic descriptors.

A retrocausal reading is warranted: downstream mining can diagnose upstream detection. The paper reports that “the length of the detected cyclic motion range(s) per video was an excellent indicator to identify unstable and/or erroneous pose detection results” [PAGE 6]. That is a feedback loop from mining output back to detector quality. The same loop appears in athlete recognition: while matching pose clips across a video database, the authors “accidentally discovered” that scorematch can identify a swimmer, with cross-swimmer scores “4 to 8 times higher” than same-video scores [PAGE 5]. These are not mere applications; they are retroactive validation signals.

Multi-week implementation traps follow from that lineage. First, a team may port the normalized MSE but forget that the paper’s threshold \(49\) is tied to \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\); doubling the reference scale quadruples the distance and shrinks the match graph [PAGE 3]. Second, a team may treat missing frames as zero-speed frames instead of frame-indexed gaps; the paper’s data structure explicitly pairs pose \(p\) with frame number \(f_p\), and poses may be absent [PAGE 3]. Third, a team may tune the 10-frame temporal-width cutoff, the 50% recurrence filter, the 10% median deviation cutoff, and the degree-5 polynomial on the same videos used for evaluation; the paper says all mining was performed before ground-truth annotations were created [PAGE 5], which is a data-leakage warning. Fourth, a team may overfit the long-jump HMM to one track; the paper uses six different long-jump tracks and six-fold cross evaluation on 60 videos [PAGE 8]. Fifth, a team may equate coach salience with temporal salience; the paper explicitly says human-salient poses focus on one or two characteristic angles, whereas its striking pose is the one easy for the system to localize in time [PAGE 6]. The lineage therefore predicts a recurring failure mode: the equations are simple, but the experimental controls are the hard part.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive model has three agents: the athlete, the coach, and the mining system. The athlete’s crisis habit regression is described in the paper’s motivation for cycle stability: a decisive trait of winning athletes is “a very stable stroke pattern over time, under increasing fatigue and at different pace” [PAGE 5]. Under fatigue, the athlete may regress to a habitual motor pattern, and the stability score is meant to detect that regression. The counter-trap is that the coach and the system may agree on rank order for different reasons. In breaststroke, the coach judged the third-ranked video as equivalent to the second because the swimmer was “getting tired, drifting regularly with his lower legs out of the picture during the stretching phase” [PAGE 6]. In freestyle, the coach ranked the second video slightly better, but the paper explains the discrepancy by “water flow speed was higher than normal, leading to a slightly higher error frequency in the automatically detected poses” [PAGE 7]. Thus a stability score can be numerically correct and causally wrong.

The clock is the time-continuous cycle-speed estimate. It supplies the temporal coordinate against which stability is measured, and the paper deliberately avoids penalizing pace in the edit-distance cost: “Pace is already captured by the cycle speed. Here we only want to focus on the stability of the stroke pattern, no matter how fast the stroke is executed” [PAGE 5]. This is a theory-of-mind claim about what the coach cares about: not absolute cycle duration, but pose succession consistency. The recursive trap is that the system’s “temporally striking pose” is defined by system localizability, not by coach-visible biomechanical landmarks [PAGE 6]. Therefore the correct recursive adjudication is: use the clock to separate pace from pattern; use the coach only as an external criterion, not as a label for salience; and veto any stability comparison where the athlete is partially out of frame, where water flow is abnormal, or where the detected cyclic range is shorter than 10 seconds [PAGE 6]. The paper’s fly example adds another recursive layer: the system picked up athletes “breathing every other stroke” with a strong difference between breathing and non-breathing cycles, while coaches typically emphasize minimizing that difference [PAGE 6]. A one-cycle stability score can therefore miss a two-cycle habit; the model of the athlete must include the breath cycle, not just the stroke cycle.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
**Physical veto.** The physical layer vetoes any pose-derived metric when the detection support is invalid. The paper discards partially detected poses [PAGE 2], and it states that a cyclic motion range of less than 10 seconds indicated detection difficulty “due to strong reflections, water splashes, spray and/or air bubbles in the water” [PAGE 6]. Therefore a physical-support predicate \(V_{\mathrm{phys}}\) should fire when the cyclic range is shorter than 10 s, when the athlete is not fully visible, or when the pose stream contains only partial detections. The paper’s own action was to reduce the swimming set from 233 to 213 videos for stability experiments [PAGE 6]. That is a veto proof: the authors did not attempt to interpret stability under insufficient physical support.

**Biomechanical veto.** The biomechanical layer vetoes when the phase definition or joint visibility makes the pose metric non-identifiable. Breaststroke is the clearest case: the swimmer pulls the heels toward the buttocks, bringing the feet back into the image and suddenly providing a complete pose, which causes over-detection of the stroke range up to 6% relative to the early cut-off ground truth [PAGE 6]. That over-detection is described as “primarily an artifact of how we determined the ground truth range of the stroke, but no real error” [PAGE 6]. A biomechanical veto should therefore distinguish a true cyclic continuation from a re-entry artifact. Freestyle flutter with an “unsteady and irregular flutter flick” is another case where the coach and system agreed on the third-ranked video but for different reasons [PAGE 7]. The veto is not “the athlete is bad”; it is “the pose sequence does not support a stable cycle comparison.”

**Weather and environment veto.** The source does not model weather variables at all. It does model environmental confounds: water flow speed, reflections, splashes, spray, air bubbles, background spectators, and camera panning for long jump [PAGE 6][PAGE 7]. Therefore any claim that weather caused a pose failure is outside the paper’s evidence. The correct adjudication is to log the environment as an uncontrolled covariate and veto causal attribution, not to invent a weather equation. For long jump, the paper notes that spectators and other background objects “are likely to cause regular false detections of body joints” [PAGE 7]. That is an environmental false-detection veto. The cross-layer proof is therefore: physical visibility is necessary; biomechanical phase identifiability is necessary; environmental false detections must be treated as a confound. If any of these fail, the derived cycle speed, stability score, or phase label is not admissible as a performance claim.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto 1: Scale-degenerate normalized distance.** Counterfactual: take a valid 14-joint pose and collapse all joints to the center of mass so \(s_p=0\). Prediction: Eq. (8) becomes undefined or numerically infinite, and any downstream threshold match must be rejected. Verifiable test: construct synthetic poses with \(s_p\le \epsilon\), run the normalized-distance operator, and require a hard failure rather than a silent large value. This veto is grounded in the paper’s definition of \(s_p\) as the average joint-to-center-of-mass distance and in the normalized distance’s denominator \(s_{p_1}^2,s_{p_2}^2\) [PAGE 3]. A system that returns a finite similarity for a collapsed pose is invalid.

**Veto 2: Temporal-support insufficiency.** Counterfactual: retain a swimming clip whose detected cyclic range is shorter than 10 seconds and compute a stability score. Prediction: the score should be unstable or causally uninterpretable because the paper explicitly excludes such sequences: “only cyclic motion sequences of 10 seconds or longer were used” [PAGE 6]. Verifiable test: split the 233-video set into the 20 short-range videos and the 213 retained videos, compute stability rankings, and require that short-range rankings fail a pre-registered stability criterion or disagree with coach ordering more often. If short-range clips produce the same ranking quality as long-range clips, the 10-second veto is not supported on that dataset; if they produce unstable rankings, the veto is verified.

**Veto 3: Reference-scale threshold inconsistency.** Counterfactual: keep \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\) and threshold \(49\), then change \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}\) to 200 without changing the threshold. Prediction: because Eq. (8) scales with \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}^2\), the normalized distance quadruples and the match graph becomes sparse; the threshold must become \(49\times 4=196\) to preserve the same pixel interpretation. Verifiable test: on a fixed annotated pose pair with known \(\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{norm}}\) at \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\), recompute at \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=200\); require the ratio to be exactly 4 and require the thresholded match set to be invariant only after threshold rescaling. This veto is directly supported by the paper’s statement that “Doubling the reference pose’s scale will quadruple the MSE value” [PAGE 2] and by its use of \(s_{\mathrm{ref}}=100\), threshold \(49\) [PAGE 3]. A pipeline that changes reference scale without rescaling its threshold is formally inconsistent.
