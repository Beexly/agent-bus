The model’s stay probability ρ is high for tackles (0.97) and guards (0.97), indicating that under crisis the offensive line tends to remain attached to its original rusher rather than pass off [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. This is a habit regression: when the play accelerates, linemen default to man-on-man responsibility even if a defensive stunt would warrant a switch. The per-blocker ρ_b, partially pooled via Beta(αρ, βρ), quantifies how readily an individual overrides this default [PAGE 5].

Counter-trap. Defensive stunts and twists are designed to induce exactly the opposite behavior: feint toward one blocker to draw assignment mass, then loop to a second rusher whose original blocker has released [PAGE 8]. The HMM’s switch probability (1 − ρ) / (n_i − 1) treats every non-stay transition as equally likely across the remaining rushers [PAGE 5], which is a coarse approximation for rapid, coordinated stunts. Blocker assignment entropy captures this: interior linemen show the highest normalized entropy (e.g., Ben Cleveland 0.368, Netane Muti 0.336) because they routinely pass off twists and looping rushers, whereas tackles are the “stickiest” (Patrick Ricard 0.042, Dion Dawkins 0.134) [PAGE 8, Table 3]. A successful counter-trap appears in the model as a switch event with elevated entropy; if the switch probability is underestimated, the HMM misattributes credit to the wrong blocker.

Clock. The play operates under a hard temporal constraint: the snap-to-throw horizon is approximately 4 seconds, and most blocks are right-censored at the quarterback event [PAGE 8]. RMST sits near this ∼4 s horizon for nearly all tackles, with elite protectors beaten on only 3–6% of blocks [PAGE 8]. The dwell coefficient ĝ_1 > 0 in the block-hold hazard confirms that the longer a block must last, the more likely it is to fail [PAGE 8]. The continuous-time model’s one-step-ahead response ΔSTRAIN with mean reversion ϕ̂ = −0.059 reflects pressure building within this clock [PAGE 9]. Recursive theory of mind enters because the blocker anticipates the rusher’s path, the rusher anticipates the blocker’s set, and the quarterback anticipates pressure—all conditioned on the same shrinking window before the throw. The model filters assignments using data only up to frame t (filtered posterior), respecting the causal clock; smoothed assignments use future frames and thus look backward from the play’s end [PAGE 9].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

Physical layer veto. The emission assumes O_tb is on the segment between R_tj and Q_t with isotropic noise σ_D² I₂ [PAGE 4]. For tackles σ_D² = 1.41 yd² (σ_D ≈ 1.19 yd). A veto triggers if a blocker’s perpendicular offset from the rusher–QB segment exceeds 3σ_D (≈ 3.6 yd), which occurs on wide sets, kick slides, or when a blocker anchors deep. In such frames the Gaussian emission assigns negligible density, and the HMM may force a false engagement or push mass into the disengaged state. This is a physical veto: the “between” geometric assumption is invalid for non-anchored blocking.

Biomechanical layer veto. Orientation is modeled as von Mises(η_tj + π, κ = 1), expecting the blocker to face opposite the rusher [PAGE 4]. Because κ = 1 is near-uniform, orientation cannot strongly veto a positional match. A biomechanical veto should trigger when |η_tb − (η_tj + π)| mod 2π > π/2 (blocker facing roughly away from assigned rusher) while θ(b,j,t) remains high; this indicates either a missed assignment or a broken down block, yet the emission product may still dominate if position is favorable. The model lacks an explicit engagement-intensity layer (actively impeding vs. coincidentally present), which the authors acknowledge as a limitation [PAGE 15].

Weather and surface layer veto. The Kalman filter assumes constant acceleration over δ = 0.1 s with process spectral density q = 1 yd²/s⁵ and observation noise σ_p² = 10⁻³ yd² [PAGE 16–17]. These parameters are calibrated for normal outdoor conditions. On low-friction surfaces (wet turf, rain, snow) or under high wind, abrupt slips and decelerations violate the Gaussian jerk assumption, causing smoothed velocity and acceleration to lag true kinematics. Since STRAIN depends on first and second derivatives of distance, weather-induced smoothing lag injects correlated error into rusher pressure estimates. No weather or surface covariate appears in the model; therefore all-weather generality is not established, and extreme-condition data should be treated as a breakdown regime.

Absorbing-state veto. ρ_null ≈ 0.97–1.00 makes the disengaged state effectively absorbing within a play [PAGE 7]. A veto triggers on any play where a blocker disengages (slip, chip-and-release, official whistle) and then re-engages before the quarterback event; the HMM cannot represent this recovery, and all subsequent frames are misclassified as disengaged. This biases p_fail,b upward and hold ratings −u_b downward for players on plays with transient disengagement.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement-learning-from-verifiable-rewards) counterfactual veto scenarios with testable predictions, derived strictly from the paper’s equations and reported parameter ranges.

Scenario 1 — Orientation-position decoupling veto.
Condition: On any frame where assignment posterior θ(b,j,t) > 0.7 but blocker orientation deviates from the rusher-facing ideal by more than 90°, i.e., |η_tb − (η_tj + π)| mod 2π > π/2 [PAGE 4].
Verification: Compute from tracking orientation streams; the von Mises density at this angular deviation is exp(−κ cos(Δ)) / (2π I₀(κ)) with κ = 1, which is close to the background density and thus cannot override a strong positional likelihood [PAGE 4].
Prediction: If the forward-backward algorithm maintains θ > 0.7 despite the orientation anomaly, the conditional independence of position and orientation emissions is violated. The veto would flag these frames for manual review or for a two-layer engagement-status HMM as suggested in the discussion [PAGE 15].

Scenario 2 — STRAIN-distance singularity veto.
Condition: STRAIN_ijt > 0.5 s⁻¹ while d_ijt > 6 yd [PAGE 3].
Verification: Since STRAIN = −v/d, high STRAIN at large distance requires extreme closing velocity v that is physically implausible for NFL rushers given the Kalman-smoothed kinematics (q = 1 yd²/s⁵) [PAGE 3, PAGE 16].
Prediction: Such frames are noise artifacts from numerical differentiation. The continuous-time plus-minus regressor ΔSTRAIN_ijt would then inject spurious pressure into R^Δ_j. The veto excludes them; prediction is that rusher-effect correlations with PFF pressure (currently Pearson 0.57 [PAGE 12]) should remain stable or improve after removal, confirming these outliers were driving false signal.

Scenario 3 — Disengagement misclassification veto (“won” vs “beaten”).
Condition: Blocked b transitions to disengaged at t (engaged → disengaged via p_fail,b) but within a window w = 5 frames the assigned rusher’s distance to the quarterback increases and STRAIN is negative or near zero (rusher moving away) [PAGE 8].
Verification: Per the paper’s own rule, a blocker who disengages while the assigned rusher moves away with low/negative STRAIN has “won” the matchup, not been beaten [PAGE 8]. The survival model, however, counts any p_fail,b transition as a block failure in the hazard logit h_b,t [PAGE 8].
Prediction: For blockers with high p_fail,b but many “won” disengagements, the beat rate BR_b is inflated and the hold rating −u_b is biased downward. Applying the veto (reclassifying rusher-away disengagements as successes) should increase the oriented correlation between blocker-side survival metrics and PFF pressures allowed (currently only 0.19 for RMST and 0.17 for beat rate [PAGE 12]), demonstrating that the current hazard conflates shedding losses with legitimate wins.
