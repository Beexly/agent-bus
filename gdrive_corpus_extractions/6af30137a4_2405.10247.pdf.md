The fifth step is embedding: in the double-Poisson model, \(\omega_n\) enters the home and away log-mean equations with opposite signs, \(\frac{\phi}{2}\omega_n\) and \(-\frac{\phi}{2}\omega_n\), while in random forests, ANN, and MARS it enters as a feature. The main gray-zone audit is temporal and inferential. The source says the models are trained “on a continuously updated dataset encompassing international matches from 2018 to 2023” [PAGE 8]. For a genuine pre-tournament forecast of the 2022 World Cup, using data through 2023 would be future leakage unless the implementation applies match-specific cutoffs. The paper does not, in the provided text, fully specify the cutoff discipline for every tournament match, so the retrospective Brier comparisons should be read as predictive-model comparisons under the paper’s protocol, not necessarily as deployable pre-event forecasts. A second gray zone is that the Bayesian BTD posterior is collapsed to a posterior median before downstream use; this discards posterior uncertainty and does not propagate ranking uncertainty into the Brier score. A third gray zone is small test-sample size: tournament stages contain few matches, and many Brier differences are hundredths of a point, so ranking-system superiority is stage- and model-dependent rather than universal. \[
p^{W}_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\psi_i)}{\exp(\psi_i)+\exp(\psi_j)+\exp(\gamma+(\psi_i+\psi_j)/2)},
\]
\[
p^{D}_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\gamma+(\psi_i+\psi_j)/2)}{\exp(\psi_i)+\exp(\psi_j)+\exp(\gamma+(\psi_i+\psi_j)/2)},
\]
\[
p^{L}_{ij}=\frac{\exp(\psi_j)}{\exp(\psi_i)+\exp(\psi_j)+\exp(\gamma+(\psi_i+\psi_j)/2)}.
\]
Here \(p^{W}_{ij},p^{D}_{ij},p^{L}_{ij}\in[0,1]\) are dimensionless probabilities and sum to one. \(\psi_i,\psi_j\) are latent log-strength parameters; under the natural-log parameterization they are interpretable on a log scale and are dimensionless, with additive-constant non-identifiability removed by a sum-to-zero constraint. \(\gamma\) is the dimensionless draw parameter. The breakdown regimes are explicit: as \(\gamma\to+\infty\), \(p^{D}_{ij}\to1\); as \(\gamma\to-\infty\), \(p^{D}_{ij}\to0\); at \(\gamma=0\), the three probabilities depend only on the strengths [PAGE 4]. The Bayesian likelihood is
\[
w_{ij}\mid p^{W}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p^{W}_{ij}),\quad
d_{ij}\mid p^{D}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p^{D}_{ij}),
\]
with
\[
\psi\sim N(\mu_\psi,\sigma^2_\psi),\quad
\gamma\sim N(\mu_\gamma,\sigma^2_\gamma),
\]
as given in [PAGE 5]. The prior variances are dimensionless on the log-strength and draw-parameter scales. A proper prior is required for a proper posterior; the source cites Whelan’s desirable prior properties and the Gaussian prior choice [PAGE 5]. If the prior were improper or the sum-to-zero constraint were omitted, the posterior ranking could be non-identifiable. The source also imposes a sum-to-zero constraint on attack and defense effects: [PAGE 6] states that “A sum-to-zero constraint (Baio and Blangiardo, 2010) is imposed on the attack and defense parameters to ensure model identifiability.”

from [PAGE 7], with dimensionless log-scale variances. If \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{att}}=0\) and \(\sigma^2_{\mathrm{def}}=0\), the dynamic prior collapses to a static model; that is a mathematical limit, not a claim made by the paper.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is layered. The pairwise-comparison core descends from Bradley and Terry (1952), cited at [PAGE 3]. Draw handling descends from Rao and Kupper (1967) and Davidson (1970), with Davidson’s ratio-scale formulation adopted here [PAGE 4]. Bayesian paired-comparison work descends from Davidson and Solomon (1973), Leonard (1977), Chen and Smith (1984), Caron and Doucet (2012), Whelan (2017), and others [PAGE 3]. Dynamic paired-comparison and football-score models descend from Fahrmeir and Tutz (1994), Glickman (1999, 2001), Cattelan et al. (2012), and Tian et al. (2023) for dynamic ranking, and from Maher (1982), Dixon and Coles (1997), Karlis and Ntzoufras (2003), Rue and Salvesen (2000), Owen (2011), and Koopman and Lit (2015) for goal-based modeling [PAGE 2]. Machine-learning result-based work descends from Breiman’s random forests, Friedman’s MARS, and football-specific random-forest studies by Schauberger and Groll and Groll et al. [PAGE 2, PAGE 7]. The FIFA ranking itself was revised after 2018 to account for opponent strength before each match, as the source notes [PAGE 2].

Retrocausally, the 2022 and 2023 tournament results reframe the earlier ranking debate. The paper’s conclusion is not “BTD always beats FIFA” but “stage and tournament heterogeneity matter.” [PAGE 13] states that “The FIFA ranking tends to be more accurate during the group stages of the 2022 World Cup,” while [PAGE 14] states that “the Bayesian BTD derived ranking is particularly effective in the group stage of the 2023 AFCON.” The same BTD object therefore receives opposite retrospective verdicts depending on tournament structure. This is the central retrocausal lesson: a ranking that looks superior in one competition’s knockout stage can look inferior in another competition’s group stage because the strength dispersion, sample size, and match-generation process differ.

The multi-week traps are practical. First, ranking-publication timing: FIFA points are a snapshot, and a multi-week gap between publication and tournament play can make the covariate stale. Second, training-window timing: the BTD model is estimated on 2018–2023 data [PAGE 8], so any retrospective evaluation must check whether the window includes matches after the forecast target. Third, normalization timing: MAD normalization uses the evaluation sample’s median and median absolute deviation, so the transformation can change when the tournament sample changes. Fourth, stage aggregation: group-stage and knockout-stage matches have different selection mechanisms, and pooling them can hide opposite ranking effects. Fifth, model-class timing: random forests and ANNs can absorb nonlinear interactions but are data-hungry; the AFCON knockout random-forest Brier values of 0.834 and 0.884 [PAGE 12] are a warning that flexible learners can degrade on small stage samples. Sixth, computational timing: the BTD route is a two-step procedure, and [PAGE 14] states that “it is more computationally intensive compared to the standard FIFA ranking.” The multi-week trap is therefore not a single bug; it is a chain of snapshot, cutoff, normalization, and stage-selection decisions that can reverse the apparent ranking winner.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression is the analyst’s default under uncertainty: fall back to the FIFA ranking because it is familiar, published, and cheap. The paper partially supports this habit in the 2022 World Cup group stage: [PAGE 13] says “The FIFA ranking tends to be more accurate during the group stages of the 2022 World Cup.” But the habit is not globally rational. In the 2023 AFCON group stage, the BTD ranking is particularly effective [PAGE 14], and in knockout stages the BTD ranking often improves statistical models. A purely habitual FIFA-first policy therefore misreads the tournament context.

The counter-trap is the opposite overcorrection: treat BTD as a universal replacement because it is Bayesian, pairwise, and draw-aware. The paper vetoes that overcorrection through mixed table evidence. In the 2022 World Cup group stage, Table 1 shows FIFA Brier scores lower than BTD for Diag. Infl. (0.620 versus 0.629), Biv. Pois. (0.617 versus 0.618), Double Pois. (0.622 versus 0.623), MARS (0.640 versus 0.660), ANN (0.627 versus 0.660), and Random Forest (0.713 versus 0.745) [PAGE 10]. In the 2023 AFCON knockout stage, Table 2 shows BTD better for Diag. Infl. (0.677 versus 0.681), Double Pois. (0.656 versus 0.670), and ANN (0.661 versus 0.666), but FIFA better for Biv. Pois. (0.658 versus 0.660), MARS (0.645 versus 0.650), and Random Forest (0.834 versus 0.884) [PAGE 12]. The counter-trap is therefore model- and stage-specific, not a global law.

The clock is the tournament stage and the information clock of the ranking snapshot. Group stages are more heterogeneous in strength, while knockout stages contain survivors with compressed strength differences [PAGE 9, PAGE 11]. The BTD model is designed to capture shifts in team strength [PAGE 14], but the source also warns that the approach is computationally heavier [PAGE 14]. A clock-aware decision rule would use FIFA when the ranking snapshot is fresh and the stage is heterogeneous, and would test BTD when the stage is compressed or the competition has many similar-strength teams. The recursive theory-of-mind point is that the analyst must model three agents: the ranking publisher, the competing teams whose strengths drift, and the evaluator whose Brier score is sample-dependent. The source’s own conclusion is recursive: ranking choice depends on tournament type, stage, and model class, and no single agent’s prior should dominate all clocks.
