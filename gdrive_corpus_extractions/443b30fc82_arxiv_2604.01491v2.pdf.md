<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s estimand is not a physical-force score. It is a latent opponent-adjusted logit strength for rushers and blockers, identified through linked blocking matchups. The core identification problem is stated directly: “observed win rates reflect both player performance and the difficulty of the blocking assignments each player faces” [PAGE 1], and “Comparing linemen therefore requires accounting for opponent strength and protection help” [PAGE 2]. The model solves this by a paired-comparison likelihood. For matchup \(k\), with rusher \(i(k)\), blocker set \(J_k\), \(m_k=|J_k|\), early win indicator \(Y_k\), and help indicator \(D_k\), the source specifies
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p_k)=\alpha+r_{i(k)}-\frac{1}{m_k}\sum_{j\in J_k}b_j+\delta D_k .
\]
Thus \(r_i,b_j,\alpha,\delta\) are dimensionless logit-scale parameters. A single-blocker matchup has logit shift \(r_i-b_j\); a shared assignment penalizes the rusher by the mean blocker effect, not the sum. This mean construction is important: it keeps protection on the same scale for single and shared assignments, and it means one helper contributes only \(b_j/m_k\). The source says “Each matchup contributes one binary observation, regardless of the number of participating blockers” [PAGE 5]. The estimator is “ridge-penalized maximum likelihood” [PAGE 5], so \(\lambda>0\) stabilizes players with sparse or separated records. Without ridge, complete separation—e.g., a player with all wins or all losses—pushes coefficients toward infinity. With ridge, estimates are finite but shrunk; the paper selects the penalty by five-fold game-grouped cross-validation over “120 logarithmically spaced ridge penalties from \(10^{-6}\) to \(1\)” [PAGE 20].

A multi-hop audit exposes several gray zones. First, the matchup graph must connect players through shared opponents. If the graph is disconnected, no amount of ridge creates cross-component information; ridge only shrinks within components. The paper motivates linkage—“these matchups link players through shared opponents” [PAGE 2]—but a production audit should check component structure. Second, the help term is binary overlap. The source defines help when “another blocker’s recorded block overlaps the ongoing matchup for a positive interval before the rusher wins” [PAGE 3]. That does not distinguish a chip from sustained double-team protection; the authors admit “a primary blocker and a teammate providing a brief assist receive equal weight” and “the help indicator does not distinguish a chip from sustained double-team protection” [PAGE 16–17]. Third, the early outcome is clock-truncated. A win requires the rusher to be closer to the quarterback than every joined blocker “within 2.5 seconds of the snap and before pass release or a sack” [PAGE 3]. Therefore “a recorded loss does not necessarily imply protection sustained for the full 2.5 seconds” [PAGE 16]. Fourth, the multinomial extension uses category-specific profiles:
\[
\eta_{k,c}=\alpha_c+r_{i(k),c}-\frac{1}{m_k}\sum_{j\in J_k}b_{j,c}+\delta_cD_k,
\quad
\Pr(C_k=c)=\frac{\exp(\eta_{k,c})}{\sum_{d\in C}\exp(\eta_{k,d})}.
\]
Identification requires constraints; the paper imposes “each coefficient profile sums to zero across categories” [PAGE 6]. Sparse pressure and sack categories will be shrunk toward the intercepts, so category-specific rankings are less stable than the binary early model.

The EPA layer is associative, not causal. The source fits
\[
EPA_\ell=\beta_0+\beta_{win}W_\ell+\beta_{pressure}P_\ell+\beta_{sack}S_\ell+f(z_\ell)+\varepsilon_\ell,
\]
where \(W_\ell,P_\ell\) count rushers in those categories and \(S_\ell\) is sack credit. Because “Several defenders can record rushing outcomes on the same play, but those outcomes share a single EPA value” [PAGE 6], each play enters once. The weights are
\[
w_c=\frac{\hat\beta_c}{\hat\beta_{sack}},\quad w_{loss}=0,\quad w_{sack}=1.
\]
The breakdown regime is explicit: if \(\hat\beta_{sack}\approx0\), the ratio is unstable; the paper anchors \(w_{sack}=1\). The reported weights are pressure \(0.103\) with interval \([0.071,0.140]\), and win-only \(0.009\) with interval \([-0.009,0.025]\) [PAGE 12]. That win-only interval includes zero, so a pure early-win advantage should not be treated as a reliable full-play disruption value. Finally,
\[
Q_a=\sum_{c\in C}w_c\bar p_{a,c}
\]
is an expected sack-equivalent disruption rate under a fixed observed assignment reference. It is not points caused by the player, and it is not a biomechanical force measure. The bootstrap resamples games “with replacement and keeping all of their observations together” [PAGE 8]; it captures sampling and refitting variation, but “they do not quantify uncertainty from outcome recording, sample selection, or model assumptions” [PAGE 17].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are: \(Y_k\), the early win indicator; \(C_k\in\{loss,win,pressure,sack\}\), the final category; \(m_k=|J_k|\), the number of blockers sharing the initial assignment; \(D_k\), the overlap-help indicator; \(d_{a,t}\), the straight-line distance from player \(a\) to the quarterback in snapshot \(t\); and \(\bar p_{a,c}\), the reference-averaged predicted probability of outcome \(c\) for player \(a\).

The early model is
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p_k)=\alpha+r_{i(k)}-\frac{1}{m_k}\sum_{j\in J_k}b_j+\delta D_k,
\qquad p_k=\Pr(Y_k=1),
\]
with larger \(r_i\) increasing win probability and larger \(b_j\) reducing it [PAGE 5]. The help indicator is defined by overlapping blocks: “If another blocker’s recorded block overlaps the ongoing matchup for a positive interval before the rusher wins, he shares the assignment and is included in the same observation” [PAGE 3]. The early win condition is: “Let \(d_{a,t}\) denote the straight-line distance from player \(a\) to the quarterback in snapshot \(t\); for rusher \(r\), we require \(d_{r,t}&lt;d_{b,t}\) for every blocker \(b\) who has joined the assignment by \(t\)” [PAGE 4]. If positions, timing, or assignment boundaries are unclear, “we leave the matchup ungraded” [PAGE 4].

The multinomial model is
\[
\eta_{k,c}=\alpha_c+r_{i(k),c}-\frac{1}{m_k}\sum_{j\in J_k}b_{j,c}+\delta_cD_k,
\qquad
\Pr(C_k=c)=\frac{\exp(\eta_{k,c})}{\sum_{d\in C}\exp(\eta_{k,d})},
\quad c\in C,
\]
with category-specific player profiles [PAGE 6]. For reporting, “each coefficient profile sums to zero across categories” and player effects are centered within roles [PAGE 5–6].

The EPA regression is
\[
EPA_\ell=\beta_0+\beta_{win}W_\ell+\beta_{pressure}P_\ell+\beta_{sack}S_\ell+f(z_\ell)+\varepsilon_\ell,
\]
where \(f(z_\ell)\) includes context, quarterback, defensive team, protection-participant count, and shared-sack adjustments [PAGE 6–7, PAGE 19]. The disruption weights are
\[
w_c=\frac{\hat\beta_c}{\hat\beta_{sack}},
\qquad w_{loss}=0,\qquad w_{sack}=1,
\]
so \(w_c\) is dimensionless relative sack-equivalent value [PAGE 7]. The player rating is
\[
Q_a=\sum_{c\in C}w_c\bar p_{a,c},
\]
an expected sack-equivalent disruption rate per matchup under the fixed reference assignment mix [PAGE 7].

Units: logit strengths \(r_i,b_j,\alpha,\delta\) are dimensionless logit units; probabilities are dimensionless; \(d_{a,t}\) is in the tracking coordinate distance units, and the source does not report an SI conversion; EPA and \(\beta_c\) are in offensive points or points per outcome count; \(w_c\) is dimensionless; \(Q_a\) is expected sack-equivalents per matchup; log loss is an average negative log probability, i.e. nats per matchup if natural logarithms are used. The paper reports held-out log-loss reductions of \(4.5\%\) and \(3.6\%\) against fixed historical baselines, with \(95\%\) intervals above zero [PAGE 10]. A numerical probability floor of \(10^{-15}\) is used in baseline pooling and log-loss evaluation [PAGE 21].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes:
1. \(m_k\ge1\) and assignment boundaries are observed. If not, the matchup is ungraded [PAGE 4].
2. Shared protection is represented by the arithmetic mean \(m_k^{-1}\sum b_j\), not by a primary/assist hierarchy. This breaks down for chip blocks versus sustained double teams [PAGE 16–17].
3. Ridge \(\lambda>0\) is required for finite estimates under sparse or separated records. At \(\lambda=0\), complete separation yields divergent coefficients; at very large \(\lambda\), player effects shrink toward the common intercept and rankings lose resolution [PAGE 5, PAGE 20].
4. The multinomial model needs the sum-to-zero constraint for reporting identification; without it, category profiles are not uniquely presented [PAGE 6].
5. The ratio \(w_c=\hat\beta_c/\hat\beta_{sack}\) is undefined or unstable if \(\hat\beta_{sack}=0\); the paper fixes \(w_{sack}=1\) by construction [PAGE 7].
6. EPA attribution is associative after recorded context, not causal isolation: “The EPA weights describe associations after adjustment for recorded play context, without isolating points caused by a player’s actions” [PAGE 17].
7. Game bootstrap intervals do not cover recording error, sample selection, or model misspecification [PAGE 17].
8. The matchup graph must be connected for cross-player comparisons; otherwise only within-component contrasts are identified. The source’s linkage argument is “these matchups link players through shared opponents” [PAGE 2].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from manual line evaluation to paired-comparison tracking models. Alamar and Weinstein-Gould (2008) charted whether linemen sustained blocks and how quarterback time related to completion [PAGE 2, PAGE 18]. ESPN’s pass block and pass rush win rates then operationalized a 2.5-second window: a blocker wins by sustaining protection for 2.5 seconds, and a rusher wins by beating a block within that interval [PAGE 2]. Nguyen et al. (2024) moved from binary wins to STRAIN, using defender distance from the quarterback and the rate at which that distance shrinks [PAGE 2]. The present paper combines that tracking tradition with the Bradley–Terry paired-comparison model [PAGE 18] and ridge-penalized generalized linear models via glmnet [PAGE 7, PAGE 18]. The data lineage is Hudl coordinates and blocking annotations for 2021, linked to nflverse play-by-play; the 2023 Big Data Bowl is a related but shorter tracking source covering only Weeks 1–8 [PAGE 4–5].

The retrocausal trap is explicit in the weekly paths. The authors state: “The paths are retrospective: weekly fits retain the season fit’s ridge penalties, player pool, assignment references, and EPA weights” [PAGE 9]. Thus a Week 5 rating is not a purely prospective Week 5 estimate; it uses season-level tuning and a fixed reference chosen with later information. This creates a multi-week trap for analysts who read the weekly trajectories as live forecasts. The paper’s own weekly correlations show why patience matters: early pass rush ratings correlate with Week 18 ratings at \(0.762\) for rushers and \(0.611\) for offensive linemen in Week 5, rising to \(0.891\) and \(0.848\) by Week 10, and \(0.971\) and \(0.958\) by Week 15 [PAGE 25]. A second multi-week trap is exposure. The leading linemen have different accumulation paths; for example, Kevin Zeitler has 114 eligible matchups by Week 4 and 581 by Week 18, while Lane Johnson has 79 and 285 [PAGE 25]. Early-season rankings are therefore partly exposure rankings.

A third trap is model choice over time. The paper’s primary early and full-play rankings are related but distinct: Spearman correlations are \(0.61\) for rushers and \(0.53\) for offensive linemen, and “the two models share no top-five offensive linemen” [PAGE 12]. A fourth trap is single-season stability. “With data from 2021 alone, the stability of these findings across seasons also remains untested” [PAGE 17]. A fifth trap is smoothing and baseline choice: the historical baseline’s smoothing strength is selected by cross-validation, with fixed choices \(\tau=2\) for early outcomes and \(\tau=10\) for final outcomes [PAGE 21]. The retuned and fixed comparisons both show positive reductions, but the grid in Appendix G shows that the baseline’s strength materially changes the percentage reduction [PAGE 27]. The historical lesson is that opponent adjustment is not a one-time correction; it is a lineage of assignment definitions, tracking resolution, help coding, and validation design.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when an evaluator, facing noisy early results, reverts to raw win rates, reputation, or All-Pro memory. The paper shows why that habit is tempting but incomplete: raw and fitted early rankings have Spearman correlations of \(0.85\) for rushers and \(0.77\) for offensive linemen [PAGE 11], so the model often preserves the broad ordering. But individual cases move sharply: DeForest Buckner rises from 78th by raw win rate to 25th by fitted strength, while Corey Linsley moves from 25th by raw protection rate to 117th [PAGE 11]. A pure crisis habit would miss those opponent-adjustment corrections. The counter-trap is not “trust the model absolutely.” It is to use the joint model with its intervals, exposure thresholds, and two distinct questions: early matchup strength versus full-play disruption. The paper warns that “early pass rush strength and full-play disruption answer different football questions” [PAGE 16], and for linemen the two models identify substantially different leaders [PAGE 12–13]. The counter-trap also requires respecting uncertainty: Zeitler’s early rank interval is \([1,20]\), and the rankings are “more stable for rushers than for offensive linemen, even though rushers generally have fewer observed matchups” [PAGE 10–11]. An evaluator who models other evaluators’ reactions should expect herd overreaction to a top-five list and should instead publish rank intervals and sensitivity checks.

The clock has two scales. The micro-clock is the 2.5-second early window. A quick release can end the observation before a win can occur, so “a recorded loss does not necessarily imply protection sustained for the full 2.5 seconds” [PAGE 16]. The macro-clock is the season accumulation. Weekly correlations rise substantially from Week 5 to Week 15 [PAGE 25], and the game bootstrap uses 1,000 draws to quantify refitting variation [PAGE 8]. A recursive evaluator should therefore separate three mental models: the player’s latent strength, the opponent/help assignment process, and the evaluator’s own impatience. The first is estimated by \(r_i,b_j\); the second by \(m_k^{-1}\sum b_j\) and \(D_k\); the third is disciplined by intervals and by refusing to collapse early and full-play ratings into one crisis ranking. The Garrett versus Tryon-Shoyinka example is the clean counter-trap: both rank in the top ten for initial matchup strength, but their full-play disruption ranks are fourth and 53rd; their early win rates are similar, yet “Garrett recorded pressures more often and accumulated nearly three times as much sack credit per matchup” [PAGE 13]. The correct response is not to declare one player better in all respects, but to ask which clock and which outcome the evaluation is rewarding.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical tracking layer supports only a narrow claim: a rusher is graded as winning when his tracked distance to the quarterback is less than every joined blocker’s distance within the early window and before release or sack [PAGE 3–4]. The source’s snapshots are generally no farther apart than 0.1 seconds in the comparable Big Data Bowl data, and Hudl supplies block start and end annotations [PAGE 4–5]. That is sufficient for a discrete event definition, but it is not a contact-force measurement. A biomechanical veto must therefore reject any claim about Newtons, leverage, mass, tendon load, fatigue, or injury risk. The paper has no such variables; its “strength” is a latent logit parameter, not a physical unit. The authors also admit that “other differences in protection scheme, route timing, or quarterback movement remain unmeasured and may enter the player effects” [PAGE 16–17]. Thus a scheme-adjusted causal claim is vetoed unless the analyst adds external protection-design and quarterback-movement data.

A weather veto is even clearer: the EPA regression’s recorded context includes down, second half, overtime, logarithm of yards to go, field position, seconds remaining, score differential, quarterback, and defensive team [PAGE 19]. Weather, temperature, wind, surface, and precipitation are absent. Therefore no source page supports a weather explanation for a rating change. A weather claim must be treated as an external hypothesis, not a finding of this model. The physical layer also has a missingness veto: if positions, event timing, or assignment boundaries prevent grading, the matchup is omitted [PAGE 4]. Omission is not neutral if missingness correlates with quick passes, play-action, or particular protections.

The help/scheme layer has its own veto. The model uses \(D_k\) as a binary overlap indicator and averages blocker effects with equal weight. The authors state that the help indicator “does not distinguish a chip from sustained double-team protection” [PAGE 16–17]. Therefore a claim that the model separates a brief assist from a true double team is vetoed. The EPA layer is also non-causal: the weights are “associations after adjustment for recorded play context, without isolating points caused by a player’s actions” [PAGE 17]. A claim that a pressure “caused” a specific EPA change is vetoed unless supported by a separate design. The adjudication rule is: allow claims about opponent-adjusted latent strength, early win probability, and expected sack-equivalent disruption under the observed reference; veto claims about physical force, biomechanical fatigue, weather causation, exact protection scheme, or isolated causal EPA. The uncertainty layer reinforces this: bootstrap intervals capture sampling and refitting, but “do not quantify uncertainty from outcome recording, sample selection, or model assumptions” [PAGE 17].
