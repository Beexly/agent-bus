However, the rise of Gestalt psychology and Tolman’s own "Purposive Behavior" challenged this by introducing *meaning* and *goals* into the equation. The bibliography lists Watson (1914), representing the opposing extreme: Radical Behaviorism, which sought to purge any mention of internal states ("purposiveness") entirely, reducing behavior to conditioned reflexes.

The "multi-week trap" emerges from the false dichotomy between Thorndike’s S-R bonds and Watson’s environmental determinism. Both camps ignored the *cognitive map*—the intermediate representation that allows an animal to plan. Tolman utilized the very studies cited (e.g., Peterson on frequency, Wakeham on habit formation) to demonstrate that behavior could change instantly based on new information (e.g., blocking a preferred path) without new reinforcements, proving that learning had occurred *latentely* prior to performance. This retrocausal insight—that future performance reveals past learning—redefined the field. The "gray zone" historians often miss is how physiological factors (like Wang’s "ton" influence [PAGE 370]) were gradually sidelined in favor of purely psychological variables, only to resurface decades later in neuromodulation research (dopamine, serotonin acting as global gain controls).
