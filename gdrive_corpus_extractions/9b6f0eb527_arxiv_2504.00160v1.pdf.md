\[
\Delta \mathrm{HR}_{ij}=\mathrm{HR}_{ij}-\mathrm{HR}_{i}^{\mathrm{base}}\quad [\mathrm{bpm}].
\]

The paper reports baseline \(77.78\) bpm, free \(120.20\), coupled \(134.34\), and concurrent \(130.12\) bpm [PAGE 9]. The repeated-measures decomposition, when the normal-theory ANOVA is used, is:

\[
\Delta \mathrm{HR}_{ij}=\mu+\tau_j+\pi_i+\varepsilon_{ij},
\]

with \(\sum_j \tau_j=0\), \(\pi_i\) a participant effect, and \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) residual error. The condition effect is tested by \(F=MS_{\tau}/MS_{\varepsilon}\), with partial effect size:

\[
\eta_p^2=\frac{SS_{\tau}}{SS_{\tau}+SS_{\varepsilon}},
\]

which is dimensionless. For non-normal repeated measures, the paper used Friedman’s test and Kendall’s \(W\) [PAGE 7][PAGE 8]. A standard Friedman statistic is:

\[
\chi_F^2=\frac{12}{nk(k+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}R_j^2-3n(k+1),
\]

where \(R_j\) is the rank sum for condition \(j\), \(n\) is the number of participants, and \(k\) is the number of conditions; \(W=\chi_F^2/[n(k-1)]\), dimensionless. The paper reports \(\chi^2(3)=47.80\), \(p \lt .001\), \(W=.759\) for HR across baseline and the three conditions [PAGE 8][PAGE 9]. It reports \(\chi^2(2)=6.16\), \(p=.046\), \(W=.128\) for PENS Relatedness and \(\chi^2(2)=10.36\), \(p=.006\), \(W=.216\) for CCPIG Team Identification [PAGE 8].

T_{ik}^{\mathrm{RT}}=t_{ik}^{\mathrm{tap}}-t_{ik}^{\mathrm{spawn}}\quad [\mathrm{s}],

\left|t_{A,k}^{\mathrm{tap}}-t_{B,k}^{\mathrm{tap}}\right|\le 0.5\ \mathrm{s},
\]

where \(A\) and \(B\) are the two players and \(k\) indexes the linked orb dyad [PAGE 6]. Arm movement is a telemetry path-length operator:

\[
A_{ij}=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{ij}-1}\left\|\mathbf{p}_{i,m+1}-\mathbf{p}_{i,m}\right\|_2,

where \(\mathbf{p}_{i,m}\) is the tracked wrist position at sample \(m\). The source reports “total arm movement” but does not assign SI units to that telemetry value [PAGE 7][PAGE 9]. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The relevant theory-of-mind dynamics are not abstract empathy; they are time-bounded inferences about a partner’s next action under a clock. Three regimes appear in the source.

**Crisis habit regression.** Under time pressure, players regress to overlearned spatial habits: claim territory, protect one’s own movement lane, and compete for access. The paper quotes a player’s frustration: “I got really [frustrated] and thought, ‘you move out of my way so I can get to my spot!’” [PAGE 10]. This is not evidence that cooperation failed as a design; it is evidence that the clock and shared space activate egocentric spatial habits. The paper also notes that players select the easiest and most direct solutions [PAGE 12]. In free and concurrent, many dyads divided the space in half to reduce movement [PAGE 12]. That is a low-cost coordination heuristic, but it also reduces the intended full-body cooperation.

**Counter-trap.** The counter-trap is the designers’ assumption that stricter rules automatically produce richer social coordination. In coupled, the color rule prevents helping even when help is physically trivial: “I could have just pressed [the orb] beside me to help. But [in coupled], I could not” [PAGE 10]. The rule creates interdependence, but it also blocks the natural prosocial impulse. In concurrent, players create a trigger phrase to solve the timing problem: “1, 2, 3, go!” or simply “go!” [PAGE 10]. That is a compensatory communication protocol. It works, but it can also produce stress: “‘do I do it now?’ [...] ‘is it too late?’” [PAGE 10]. The counter-trap is that a mechanic intended to force mutual modeling can instead force a shallow ritual that satisfies the timer without deep partner modeling.

**Clock.** The clock is the boundary condition on recursive theory of mind. Free and coupled spawn every 1.5 s and despawn after 3 s; concurrent spawns every 2.25 s and despawns after 4.5 s, with a 0.5 s synchronization window [PAGE 5][PAGE 6]. If the available communication latency \(L_{\mathrm{comm}}\) exceeds the task deadline \(D\), players cannot negotiate a plan and must rely on deictic shouts, gestures, or pre-agreed triggers. The paper quotes the difficulty of “not having ‘enough time to communicate [the plan] back and forth’” [PAGE 10]. If \(L_{\mathrm{comm}}\ll D\), players can recursively model: “I think that you think that I will take the high orb.” Friends exploit this: “I already knew that we are seeing this [game] similarly” [PAGE 9] and can “guess what [the other player] is trying to do” [PAGE 9]. Strangers are more reserved and seek to “confirm and [prevent] miscommunication” [PAGE 9]. Thus the clock sets the recursion horizon. Too fast, and theory of mind collapses to reactive habit. Too slow, and players conserve energy and territorialize. The paper’s moderate timing sits between those failure modes, which is why the social effects appear but are not uniform.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
