The paper states that discrimination uses “the number of non-zero frequency (AC) Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) coefficients” [PAGE 2]. The feature set is \(\{Fv1\}=\{\text{Crowd Image Confidence}\}_{\text{I-frames}}\) [PAGE 2]. Units: coefficient count, normalized to a dimensionless confidence if a monotone map is used; the paper does not specify the exact mapping. Assumption: crowd texture has higher AC activity than grass or shirt regions.

Speech-band energy is sampled at \(\Delta t=0.5\,\mathrm{s}\): \(Fv2(t_j)\) at \(t_j=j\Delta t\). The paper defines “a feature set {Fv2} of audio speech band energy levels, estimated at 0.5s intervals” [PAGE 2]. Units: scalefactor-derived energy proxy, dimensionless in the MPEG bitstream domain. Assumption: commentator voice energy is separable from crowd and music.

Scoreboard presence is tracked by \(Fv3(I_n)=\sum_{p\in P_c}|Y(I_n,p)-M(p)|\), where \(P_c\) is the critical-pixel set and \(M(p)=\operatorname{mode}_n Y(I_n,p)\) over I-frames. The paper states “a SAD operation between its critical pixels and the mode values should provide a good indication of whether the graphic is present/absent” [PAGE 2]. Units: luminance-level times pixel count, often arbitrary digital units. Assumption: scoreboard location is static and mostly on-screen, so the temporal mode is representative.

Near-field motion activity is \(Fv4(P_n)=\sum_{m\in \mathrm{MB}(P_n)}\mathbf{1}[\|\mathbf{v}_{m,n}\|>\tau_{\mathrm{NFMA}}]\), where \(\mathbf{v}_{m,n}\) is the motion vector of macroblock \(m\) in P-frame \(P_n\). The paper describes “counting the number of macroblocks in the frame whose motion vector length is greater than a pre-selected ‘zero’-threshold” [PAGE 2]. Units: macroblocks per P-frame. Assumption: \(\tau_{\mathrm{NFMA}}\) rejects slow far-field motion but retains jerky near-field celebratory motion.

Field-line orientation uses the Hough transform: \(Fv5(I_n)=\theta_n^*=\arg\max_{\theta} H(\rho,\theta)\) for the most prominent line. The paper says the angles “mainly lie within the interval 5º-25º, relative to the camera” [PAGE 3]. Units: degrees. Assumption: end-zone camera geometry and visible pitch lines.

Aggregation: \(SFV_s=[Vcc1,Vcc2,Vcc3,Vcc4,Vcc5]\). The paper defines \(Vcc1=\operatorname{Max}\{Fv1\}_{\mathrm{CSW}}\), \(Vcc2=\operatorname{Avg}\{Fv2\}_{\mathrm{CSW}}\), \(Vcc3=\operatorname{Max}\{Fv3\}_{\mathrm{CSW}}\), \(Vcc4=\operatorname{Max}\{Fv4\}_{\mathrm{CSW}}\), and \(Vcc5=i/6\), where \(i\) is the number of critical I-frame orientations within the key range [PAGE 3]. The classifier is an SVM with RBF kernel: \(f(x)=\sum_i \alpha_i y_i K(x_i,x)+b\), \(K(x,x')=\exp(-\gamma\|x-x'\|^2)\), and \(\hat y=\operatorname{sign} f(SFV)\). The paper says “an SVM (with radial basis function kernel) was chosen” [PAGE 3]; it does not specify \(\gamma\), feature normalization, or class priors. Evaluation metrics are \(\mathrm{ERR}=\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})\) and \(\mathrm{CRR}=\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})\), described as true-positive and true-negative statistics [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: (1) close-up detector failure forces \(R(s)=0\) and caps ERR at the preprocessor limit, e.g. rugby 97%, soccer 95%, hockey 90%, Gaelic Football 96% [PAGE 4]; (2) scoreboard motion or animation invalidates the mode-based SAD; (3) speech-band and NFMA thresholds are broadcast-style dependent; (4) field-line angles outside \(5^\circ\)-\(25^\circ\) or weak line contrast invalidate \(Vcc5\); (5) reaction or replay delay beyond 25 s truncates evidence; (6) metric denominators are undefined when a test set lacks positives or negatives; (7) the paper itself notes the hockey preprocessor retained “only 90% of events” although “52% of content was rejected” [PAGE 3].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from early image-processing primitives to late-1990s and early-2000s video indexing. The Roberts edge detector is cited from 1965, the Hough transform from 1989, and DCT/MPEG bitstream processing from the MPEG era; the paper’s own references include shot-boundary evaluation from 1999 and MPEG audio bitstream processing from 2002 [PAGE 4]. The retrocausal point is that the “generic” event detector is built from older low-level operators whose semantics are inherited: DCT AC counts become crowd texture, MPEG motion vectors become celebratory turbulence, Hough angles become end-zone geometry, and scalefactor weights become commentator speech. The SVM layer is a later statistical wrapper over these inherited perceptual proxies. The paper positions itself against genre-specific systems for soccer, basketball, Formula-1, baseball, cricket, and tennis [PAGE 1], and claims genericity by restricting scope to field sports with shared characteristics: two teams plus referees, enclosed playing area, grass pitch, field lines, commentator voice-over, spectator cheering, scoreboard, three camera styles, and territorial objective [PAGE 1]. The multi-week traps are therefore not merely implementation bugs; they are conceptual drift traps. First, treating the Table-1 percentages as universal priors rather than corpus statistics will overfit to the 100-hour MPEG-1 corpus [PAGE 1]. Second, leaking test-corpus tuning into the preprocessor—especially the 25 s CSW and close-up retention rule—will inflate apparent ERR. Third, conflating the preprocessor limit with classifier performance will misread Table 2: rugby’s 97% ERR at 38% CRR is not the SVM’s best balanced operating point; the paper separately reports a saturation limit of “78% CRR and 68% ERR” [PAGE 3]. Fourth, the genre-pace explanation is post hoc: “faster paced games tend to contain more live action” and slower games include “more contextual content e.g. more close-up shots, more crowd shots, more replays, more dissolves” [PAGE 4]; this is plausible but not a controlled causal test. Fifth, annotation ambiguity around “event” (goal, try, conversion, penalty, point) can create label noise across genres. Sixth, feature independence is assumed by the SVM input vector, yet close-up, crowd, audio, scoreboard, and NFMA are director-driven and correlated. Multi-week replication should freeze preprocessor thresholds, report confidence intervals, separate preprocessor and classifier limits, and test on unseen broadcast styles before claiming genericity.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper encodes a three-agent recursive model. The director is modeled as an editor who “utilizes this reaction-phase to capture the responses of players and/or crowds to the significance of the event” [PAGE 1]. The system is modeled as an inferential observer that reads the director’s reaction as evidence. The user is modeled as a retrieval user for whom “high recall is paramount since a user would be more likely to tolerate the inclusion of non-events as opposed to event omissions” [PAGE 3]. Crisis habit regression occurs when the system’s habitual assumptions—close-up within the CSW, static scoreboard, 5º-25º field lines, near-field turbulence, commentator speech band—become the default under distribution shift. In a crisis, the model regresses to the preprocessor: if the close-up is absent, the shot is rejected even if the event occurred. That is the habit of trusting the 97% close-up statistic as a law rather than a corpus frequency [PAGE 2]. The counter-trap is the opposite failure: if the operator reacts to missed events by lowering thresholds or biasing the SVM toward high ERR, the system may accept non-events, reducing CRR; the paper shows this trade-off by varying \(C\) from the preprocessor limit toward “78% CRR and 68% ERR” [PAGE 3]. The clock trap is temporal: the 25 s CSW and 0.5 s audio sampling define when evidence is admissible. If the director’s reaction is delayed, if the replay is late, or if the close-up falls just outside the shot-end window, the clock veto overrides the event. Recursive theory of mind therefore requires modeling the director’s timing policy, the user’s recall preference, and the system’s own threshold habit simultaneously. A robust detector should ask: is the close-up missing because no event occurred, or because this director used a different reaction grammar? Is the scoreboard SAD high because the graphic is absent, or because the broadcast uses an animated overlay? Is the field-line angle outside the interval because play is not end-zone, or because the camera is reversed? The paper’s own conclusion points toward this audit: future work should quantify “the individual contribution of each feature” and analyze “the effect of feature threshold selection on overall system performance” [PAGE 4].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof must be stated carefully because the source contains no direct physical sensor, force plate, weather station, or biomechanical tracking data. The corpus is “100-hours of content ... captured in MPEG-1 format” [PAGE 1], and the operators are DCT coefficients, scalefactor weights, SAD luminance values, motion vectors, and Hough angles [PAGE 2-3]. Therefore any claim that weather, biomechanics, or physical forces directly caused an event-detection outcome is not verifiable from this source. The admissible cross-layer veto is observational and proxy-based: a physical or weather condition can veto event inference only if it corrupts the feature distributions that the paper uses. Formally, let \(D(SFV)\) be the detector decision and let \(V_{\mathrm{layer}}\) be a cross-layer veto. The veto is admissible only when \(V_{\mathrm{layer}}\) changes \(p(Fv_i\mid \mathrm{event})\) or \(p(Fv_i\mid \mathrm{non-event})\) enough to cross the learned boundary, or when it invalidates the preprocessor condition \(R(s)=1\). Weather can veto through pitch hue segmentation, field-line contrast, crowd clothing texture, and audio noise. The paper assumes “colours of the playing surface and the players/referee shirts usually consist of one or two (striped) dominant colours” [PAGE 2], so rain, snow, mud, or floodlight color shifts can break the hue model. Biomechanics can veto through NFMA: celebratory motion is assumed to be jerky and near-field, but camera zoom and normal player acceleration can also produce high motion-vector counts [PAGE 2]. Physical camera geometry can veto through field-line angles: the \(5^\circ\)-\(25^\circ\) interval is an end-zone perspective assumption [PAGE 3], so a reverse-angle or midfield camera is a physical-geometry veto. No algebraic singularity is created by these layers; the veto is a distributional breakdown. The proof is therefore negative and operational: the paper cannot prove a physical causal link, but it does prove that its detector depends on lighting, color, camera geometry, and motion scale. If those proxies are corrupted, the correct adjudication is to suspend the event claim, not to infer a physical cause.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Preprocessor-limit veto for rugby. Veto condition: a report claims rugby ERR above 97% under the paper’s same close-up retention preprocessor and same corpus. The paper states the “maximum ERR value achievable is 97%” and the corresponding CRR is 38% [PAGE 3]. Prediction: any reported rugby ERR above 97% is a measurement error, threshold leak, or preprocessor change; a verifier should reject the claim unless the close-up retention rule or CSW was modified. This is verifiable by recomputing \(R(s)\) and checking whether the reported positives exceed the preprocessor’s retained set.

2. Static-scoreboard-mode veto. Veto condition: the scoreboard bounding box moves across I-frames, or the overlay is animated during updates, so \(M(p)=\operatorname{mode}_n Y(I_n,p)\) is not representative. The paper assumes the scoreboard “location is generally static within the frame” and uses “the temporal mode values” for SAD [PAGE 2]. Prediction: under a moving or animated scoreboard, \(Fv3\) loses its intended meaning; event scenes with scoreboard removal will not raise \(Vcc3\), and non-event scenes with animated graphics may falsely raise it. A verifier can test this by tracking the graphic’s critical-pixel variance; if the variance exceeds the training-corpus mode stability, the scoreboard feature claim is vetoed.

3. Hockey operating-point veto. Veto condition: a report claims hockey CRR above 72% at 90% ERR under the same preprocessor and corpus. Table 2 gives Hockey preprocessor ERR 90% and CRR 52%, and the maximum CRR for 90% ERR is 72% [PAGE 4]. Prediction: a claimed hockey result of, for example, 80% CRR at 90% ERR is inconsistent with the reported table unless the evaluation set, preprocessor, or class definition changed. The verifier should recompute ERR and CRR from the shot decisions and confirm that the hockey preprocessor retained only the stated fraction; the paper notes that for hockey “although 52% of content was rejected, only 90% of events were retained” [PAGE 3]. These three vetoes are verifiable because they compare an external claim against the paper’s explicit upper bounds, static-mode assumption, and tabulated operating point, and they yield testable predictions about false negatives, scoreboard-feature invalidation, and impossible metric combinations.
