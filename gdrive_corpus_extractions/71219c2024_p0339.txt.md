**Multi-hop derivation:**
1. **Habit Strength ($sE_r$):** We define the simple positive growth function as $sE_r = 1 - e^{-A sIr}$, following Hull's earlier formulations. This represents an asymptotic approach to maximum potential association based on reinforcements ($r$). However, this is an internal state, not observable behavior.
2. **Threshhold of Evocation ($V_s$):** The initial horizontal phase at zero probability implies a latent variable—reaction potential must exceed a threshold before manifesting. We model this using a logistic transformation of the difference between current reaction potential and a dynamic threshold: $P(evocation) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-k(V_s - V_{th})}}$. Initially, $V_s < V_{th}$, resulting in near-zero probability.
3. **Saturation Plateau:** As reinforcements continue, $sE_r$ increases, driving $V_s$ upward. Once $V_s$ significantly exceeds $V_{th}$, the probability saturates at 1.0. At this point, further learning (increase in $sE_r$) yields no visible change in evocation probability—a phenomenon known as ceiling effects or asymptotic saturation.
4. **Stimulus Generalization & Fatigue:** Real-world deviations from the ideal sigmoid (e.g., fluctuations in the middle section) arise from interfering variables like fatigue (D drive variations) or stimulus generalization gradients. These introduce noise into the mapping between internal strength and external probability.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
We formalize the transition from internal habit strength to observable evocation probability using Hullian operators extended with probabilistic and information-theoretic constraints.

**1. Habit Strength Accumulation (The Simple Positive Growth Function)**
Based on Hull's fundamental postulate of learning (Chapter VI), the rate of change in habit strength ($sE_r$) with respect to reinforcements ($n$) is proportional to the remaining unsaturated capacity:
$$ \frac{dsE_r}{dn} = K_1 (1 - sE_r) D $$
Assuming constant drive $D$, the solution is the exponential growth curve cited in the text:
$$ sE_r(n) = 1 - e^{-\lambda n} $$
*   **Units:** $sE_r$ is dimensionless (potential ranging 0 to 1); $n$ is discrete reinforcement count; $\lambda$ is a learning-rate constant with units $[reinforcement]^{-1}$.
*   **Breakdown Regime:** This equation assumes no fatigue or inhibition. If $n \to \infty$, $sE_r \to 1$. It fails to predict the initial zero-probability lag observed in Figure 73.

**2. Reaction Potential Threshold Model**
To map $sE_r$ to the sigmoid evocation curve $P(e)$, we introduce a reaction potential $V$ and a threshold $V_{th}$. Following the principles of psychophysics and neural firing rates:
$$ V(n) = sE_r(n) \cdot D \cdot (1 - I) $$
where $I$ is inhibitory potential. The probability of evocation follows a cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the noise surrounding the threshold decision process. Using the logistic function (a proxy for the normal ogive mentioned in the text):
$$ P(e|n) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\beta (V(n) - V_{th})}} $$
*   **Units:** $V$ and $V_{th}$ share energy-like units (potential); $\beta$ is inverse potential $[Potential]^{-1}$ (related to slope/precision).
*   **Singularity/Breakdown:** 
    *   If $V(n) \ll V_{th}$, $P(e) \approx 0$ (Initial Horizontal Phase, Corollary I).
    *   If $V(n) \gg V_{th}$, $P(e) \approx 1$ (Final Horizontal Phase). 
    *   The derivative $\frac{dP}{dn}$ peaks at $V = V_{th}$, defining the steepest learning period.

**3. Information-Theoretic Constraint**
The "indefinite extent" of the final plateau suggests diminishing returns in information gain. We can quantify the mutual information $I(S;R)$ between stimulus context $S$ and response $R$:
$$ I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R|S) $$
As $P(e) \to 1$, the entropy $H(R) \to 0$ (certainty), and thus the capacity to convey new information about the subject's state via the binary response vanishes. Continued learning at this stage shifts to optimizing execution speed or metabolic efficiency rather than changing the response selection probability.

*Citation:* Hull’s text explicitly notes the deviation from the "true ogive" (Fig 70) and the "horizontal... standing at zero probability" (Fig 73) [[PAGE 332]].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of these curves stretches back to Ebbinghaus’s forgetting curves and Fechner’s laws of sensation. Hull’s specific formulation is a retrocausal resolution of the conflict between Thorndike’s Law of Effect (strength grows linearly with satisfaction) and Watson’s S-R bond theory. By positing a hidden variable—habit strength—that evolves continuously, while behavior emerges discontinuously (sigmoidally), Hull bridged the gap between gradual synaptic modification and abrupt behavioral manifestation. 

This framework prefigured modern computational neuroscience models of spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) and winner-take-all networks, where a neuron's firing probability is sigmoidal relative to its input current. Historically, this was a trap for early behaviorists who equated "learning" directly with "performance." The discovery that performance plateaus while learning continues forced a paradigm shift toward latent trait modeling. It also anticipated the concept of "overlearning" in skill acquisition, where practice beyond mastery enhances retention but not immediate accuracy.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From a crisis habit regression perspective, the initial zero-probability phase represents a defensive "freeze" or hesitation state. The organism is assessing the environment; reaction potential is building but remains below the threshold for action due to high perceived risk or uncertainty (high $V_{th}$). This is a maladaptive trap if the threat requires immediate response. Conversely, counter-trapping strategies involve lowering the threshold through prior exposure or stress inoculation, effectively shifting the sigmoid leftward. 

The clock mechanism here is time-on-task or trial number. As trials proceed without negative feedback, the system recursively updates its estimate of safety, lowering $V_{th}$. Once the threshold is crossed, the behavior erupts. The final plateau represents a state of automated cognition—the "clock" of conscious monitoring stops ticking as the habit becomes procedural. Crisis regression occurs if a novel disturbance pushes the system out of this basin of attraction, forcing a return to the uncertain, slow-learning regime.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
