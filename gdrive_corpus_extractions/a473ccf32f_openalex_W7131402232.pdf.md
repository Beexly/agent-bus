<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s first-stage operator is logistic recalibration. For trade \(i\), \(p_i\in(0,1)\) is the execution price converted to the yes-claim price in cents divided by 100, and \(y_i\in\{0,1\}\) is the binary outcome. The paper fits
\[
\operatorname{logit}\!\left(\Pr(y_i=1)\right)=a+b\,\operatorname{logit}(p_i),\qquad 
\operatorname{logit}(x)=\log\!\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right),
\]
with log-likelihood
\[
\ell(a,b)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\log\pi_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\pi_i)\right],\qquad
\pi_i=\sigma\!\left(a+b\,\operatorname{logit}(p_i)\right),\quad
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}.
\]
These are Equations (1) and (2) [PAGE 5]. Units: \(p_i\), \(y_i\), \(\pi_i\), \(a\), and \(b\) are dimensionless; \(b\) is a dimensionless logit-scale slope; \(N\) is a count of trades. The source states: “When \(a\) is close to zero, \(b>1\) corresponds to underconfidence: favorites are underpriced, longshots are overpriced and prices are compressed toward 50%. Conversely, \(b<1\) corresponds to overconfidence, with prices that are too extreme” [PAGE 5]. Assumptions: prices are restricted to 5–95 cents, markets with fewer than 10 trades are excluded, each analysis cell requires at least 200 trades, and mild \(L2\) regularization \(C=10\) is used; the paper reports that \(C=1\), \(C=10\), and \(C=100\) give the same decomposition to three decimals [PAGE 6]. The full grid is \(6\) domains \(\times\) \(9\) time bins \(\times\) \(4\) size bins \(=216\) cells [PAGE 6]. Time bins are \([0,1h),[1h,3h),[3h,6h),[6h,12h),[12h,24h),[24h,48h),[2d,1w),[1w,1mo),[1mo,\infty)\), and size bins are Single (1 contract), Small (2–10), Medium (11–100), Large (more than 100) [PAGE 6]. \(\tau\) is measured in hours or days; size is measured in contracts.

The second-stage operator is additive:
\[
\theta(d,\tau,s)=\mu(\tau)+\alpha_d+\kappa(s)+\beta_d(\tau)+\gamma_d(s)+\varepsilon,
\]
Equation (3) [PAGE 10], where \(\theta(d,\tau,s)\) is the calibration slope in domain \(d\), horizon bin \(\tau\), size bin \(s\). All terms are dimensionless slope components. Identification is by sum-to-zero constraints:
\[
\sum_{d=1}^{D}\alpha_d=0,\qquad
\sum_{d=1}^{D}\beta_d(\tau)=0\ \forall\tau,\qquad
\sum_{\tau}\beta_d(\tau)=0\ \forall d,
\]
\[
\sum_{s=1}^{S}\kappa(s)=0,\qquad
\sum_{d=1}^{D}\gamma_d(s)=0\ \forall s,\qquad
\sum_{s=1}^{S}\gamma_d(s)=0\ \forall d,
\]
Equations (4)–(7) [PAGE 11]. The variance decomposition uses
\[
SS_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{d,\tau,s}\left[\theta(d,\tau,s)-\bar{\theta}\right]^2,\qquad
SS_{\mathrm{res}}=\sum_{d,\tau,s}\left[\theta(d,\tau,s)-\hat{\theta}(d,\tau,s)\right]^2,
\]
Equations (8)–(9) [PAGE 12]. The paper reports total \(R^2=0.873\), adjusted \(R^2=0.810\), permutation-null mean \(0.329\) with 95th percentile \(0.406\), leave-one-cell-out \(R^2=0.715\), and 10-fold \(R^2=0.704\) [PAGE 12]. Units: sums of squares are dimensionless slope-squared quantities; \(R^2\) is dimensionless.

The scale-effect estimand is
\[
\Delta_d=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\tau=1}^{T}\left[\theta(d,\tau,s_L)-\theta(d,\tau,s_S)\right],
\]
Equation (11) [PAGE 13], where \(s_L\) is Large and \(s_S\) is Single, and \(T=9\) time bins. This is a within-horizon average, so it is not a raw cross-horizon difference. For Kalshi Politics the paper reports \(\Delta=0.53\) with trade-level interval \([0.29,0.75]\), market-clustered \([0.14,1.32]\), and event-clustered \([0.12,1.80]\) [PAGE 8, PAGE 13]. For Sports the corresponding gap is \(0.07\) and is null under clustering [PAGE 8]. The event-clustered interval is widest because there are only 850 event clusters [PAGE 13].

The Bayesian measurement-error operator is
\[
\theta_{\mathrm{obs}}(d,\tau,s)\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(
\mu(\tau)+\alpha_d+\beta_d(\tau)+\delta_d\tilde{s},\ 
\sigma^2+\mathrm{se}^2_{d\tau s}
\right),
\]
Equation (12) [PAGE 17], where \(\tilde{s}=\log s-\overline{\log s}\), \(\mathrm{se}^2_{d\tau s}\) is the known first-stage event-clustered estimation variance, \(\sigma^2\) is residual structural heterogeneity, and all slope-scale quantities are dimensionless. Priors are \(\mu(\tau)\sim\mathcal{N}(1.0,0.5^2)\), \(\alpha_d\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\alpha^2)\), \(\beta_d(\tau)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\beta^2)\), \(\delta_d\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_\delta^2)\), with half-Cauchy hyperpriors [PAGE 16–17]. The paper reports total structural share \(0.456\) with 95% credible interval \([0.315,0.620]\), and horizon share \(0.193\), domain share \(0.084\), domain-by-horizon share \(0.111\), scale share \(0.068\) [PAGE 18]. The practical recalibration operator is
\[
p^{*}=\sigma\!\left(\hat{a}+\hat{b}\operatorname{logit}(p)\right),
\]
Equation (18) [PAGE 20]. For a political price \(p=0.70\) one week before resolution, using \(\hat{b}\approx1.83\) as a slope-only illustration gives
\[
p^{*}=\frac{0.70^{1.83}}{0.70^{1.83}+0.30^{1.83}}\approx0.83,
\]
Equation (19) [PAGE 20]. The paper explicitly warns: “A price of 70 cents in a political market one week before resolution should not automatically be reported as 70%” [PAGE 20].

Singularity and breakdown regimes: \(\operatorname{logit}(p)\to\pm\infty\) as \(p\to0\) or \(p\to1\), so the analysis filters to 5–95 cents to avoid tick-size domination on the logit scale [PAGE 6]. Cells with fewer than 200 trades are not estimated; all 216 Kalshi cells pass, with the smallest domain cell 472 trades in Weather and the largest 22,518 in Sports [PAGE 6, PAGE 26]. Polymarket timestamps have approximately 3-hour noise from block-number bucketing, so the 0–1h and 1–3h bins are unreliable for cross-platform work [PAGE 3, PAGE 6]. Leave-one-domain and leave-one-size-bin extrapolation breaks down [PAGE 12]. The measurement-error model ignores cross-cell covariance from events contributing to multiple cells, so its intervals may be mildly optimistic [PAGE 17]. The Polymarket snapshot is locked to one deposited version and differs from the original submission, so snapshot-specific replication is required [PAGE 22].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage runs from probability verification and calibration measurement to modern prediction-market microstructure. Brier (1950), Murphy and Winkler (1977), and Lichtenstein et al. (1982) supply the calibration and scoring framework; Platt (1999), Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005), and Gneiting and Raftery (2007) supply logistic recalibration and proper scoring; Satopää et al. (2014), Guthrie and Franck (2024), and Palley and Satopää (2024) extend logit-space aggregation and recalibration [PAGE 2]. Prediction-market lineage includes Hayek (1945), Galton (1907), Surowiecki (2004), Forsythe et al. (1992), Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004), and Manski (2006); the paper notes that Manski shows “prices need not equal mean beliefs under heterogeneous preferences” [PAGE 2]. The favorite–longshot regularity is traced through Griffith (1949), Ali (1977), Thaler and Ziemba (1988), Snowberg and Wolfers (2010), and Page and Clemen (2013), while Ottaviani and Sørensen (2008) is used to insist that multiple explanations exist and that “no single explanation should be assumed without market-specific evidence” [PAGE 2]. Microstructure lineage is Kyle (1985), Glosten and Milgrom (1985), and Easley and O’Hara (1987); the paper’s large-trade result runs opposite to the standard private-information interpretation for Kalshi politics, so the author treats it as “a diagnostic fact requiring institutional explanation, not as direct evidence of a particular causal mechanism” [PAGE 2–3]. The closest modern comparators are Becker (2026), Bürgi et al. (2025), and Reichenbach and Walther (2025) [PAGE 2]. Multi-week traps: the horizon bins [1w,1mo) and [1mo,\infty) are where slopes rise most, with the universal horizon component rising from 0.99 at 0–1h to 1.32 beyond one month [PAGE 6]; Sports reaches 1.74 beyond one month and Politics 1.73 [PAGE 8]. These long-horizon cells are also where contract availability changes: a balanced-panel restriction leaves Politics underconfident at every coarse horizon with slopes 1.34, 1.24, 1.34, 1.63, but Crypto and Weather become erratic on the small long-lived subset [PAGE 28]. The Polymarket Politics 2-day-to-1-week local maximum of 2.077 is plausibly tied to 2024 US election daily-resolution markets whose peak trading fell 2–7 days before resolution [PAGE 30]. Thus the historical lineage supports conditional recalibration, while the multi-week trap is to read long-horizon slopes as universal contract properties rather than as properties of the contracts that happen to survive to those horizons.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive-agent audit has three modes. Crisis habit regression is the default under deadline: a journalist or policymaker sees a price and reports it as a probability. The paper’s motivating example is exactly this: contracts trading near 62 cents are often translated directly into probabilities, but “The translation is natural but not automatic” [PAGE 1]. The counter-trap is overcorrection: after learning that politics is compressed, an agent applies the political slope to every platform, horizon, and trade size. That is blocked by the paper’s own cross-platform result: Kalshi Politics has a monotonic size gradient from 1.188 to 1.741, while Polymarket Politics is non-monotonic and the market-clustered interval crosses zero [PAGE 33]. The clock is the horizon and resolution schedule: \(\tau\) is time-to-resolution, and the paper’s practical example is a political contract one week before resolution, where a 70-cent price should not automatically be reported as 70% [PAGE 20]. A recursive theory-of-mind agent should therefore maintain a nested belief: first, what is the raw price \(p\); second, what is the cell \((d,\tau,s)\); third, what is the platform and clustering regime; fourth, what is the first-stage uncertainty \(\mathrm{se}_{d\tau s}\); fifth, what is the posterior structural share. The agent’s belief about another agent’s belief must not assume that the other agent has already done this. In crisis mode, the other agent likely treats \(p\) as \(\Pr(y=1)\). In counter-trap mode, the other agent may over-apply a single slope. In clock mode, the agent recognizes that the same price can carry different probabilistic content at 1–3 hours, 2 days–1 week, and beyond one month. The paper’s composition audit is a recursive warning: at the 1–3 hour political bin, Trump Administration markets are 63% of trades with a moderate slope of 1.08, while Electoral College and Other Politics remain strongly underconfident but are diluted; removing Trump Administration drops the aggregate from 0.93 to 0.88, and removing Electoral College drops it to 0.69 [PAGE 9]. A recursive agent must therefore ask which subpopulation is speaking through the aggregate. The correct counter-trap is not “always extremize” or “always moderate,” but “apply the conditional recalibration and retain the intercept when it is not near zero” [PAGE 20]. The clock also constrains uncertainty: event-clustered standard errors are about 50 times naive Fisher errors, so confidence must be clocked to the clustering regime, not to the nominal trade count [PAGE 13].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication requires four proofs. Physical-layer veto: Polymarket trade timestamps are derived from Polygon block numbers with approximately 3-hour noise, and the paper states that “the two shortest time bins (0–1h and 1–3h) are unreliable for cross-platform comparison” [PAGE 3, PAGE 6]. Therefore any cross-platform claim about 0–1h or 1–3h calibration is vetoed unless it uses the seven reliable bins from 3–6h through 1mo+ [PAGE 6]. Biomechanical/execution-layer veto: Kalshi records executions but not parent orders; exact-timestamp burst aggregation reduces the Politics large-minus-single effect from 0.531 to 0.417, but it remains positive with interval \([0.141,0.664]\), and wider one-second and five-second windows give +0.42 and +0.45 [PAGE 27]. Therefore the large-trade association is not merely a one-timestamp fragmentation artifact, but a claim that it is a pure information effect is vetoed because same-price child executions and order splitting cannot be fully ruled out [PAGE 27]. Weather-layer veto: short-horizon weather is the opposite of political compression. The paper reports weather slopes of 0.69–0.97 within 48 hours, “where prices are too extreme” [PAGE 7], and the isotonic estimate at a raw price of 0.75 is 0.691 in Weather versus 0.886 in Politics [PAGE 6, PAGE 27]. Weather’s base rate is 24.0%, reflecting threshold-exceedance contracts [PAGE 4]. Therefore a claim that a short-horizon weather price is a direct probability is vetoed in the opposite direction from politics: the price may overstate the predicted outcome, and the veto must be horizon-specific because weather converges to the universal underconfidence pattern at longer horizons, reaching 1.37 beyond one month [PAGE 8]. Platform-layer veto: the Kalshi political scale effect survives trade-level, market-clustered, and event-clustered resampling, but the Polymarket Politics effect is positive at the cell level and not robust to market clustering [PAGE 8, PAGE 33]. Therefore a universal “large trades compress political prices” claim is vetoed; the retained claim is Kalshi-specific. The cross-layer proof chain is: physical timestamp resolution must be adequate; execution fragmentation must be bounded; domain-horizon composition must be disaggregated; platform clustering must be respected. Only then can a price be recalibrated with Equation (18).
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
