<THINK_TRACE>
The source presents ShuttleArena as a coupled shot-and-recovery learning environment, not as an isolated stroke generator. The key gray-zone move is to treat the shuttle trajectory as a physically simulated high-drag projectile while keeping player motion and interception abstract enough for fast self-play. The paper explicitly states the coupling: “The central challenge is that shot selection and recovery are not separable: the best recovery depends on the shot-induced opponent response, while the value of the shot depends on whether the hitter can cover the reply.” [PAGE 1]. That sentence forces a multi-hop causal chain: hitter contact produces a shot vector; the shot vector determines feasible receiver interception candidates; the receiver’s possible reply changes the hitter’s optimal post-shot recovery; and the value of the original shot is then conditional on that recovery and the opponent’s response. The environment therefore cannot be reduced to a one-step shot classifier. It is a rally-level decision problem where the action space is factorized into shot geometry, speed, and recovery target.

The policy factorization is central. A monolithic hitter action would contain “11 × 8 × 5 × 25 = 11,000 combinations,” whereas the policy emits “11 + 8 + 5 + 25 = 49 component logits” [PAGE 2]. This is not merely a scalability trick. It changes what can be audited. Because the policy factors azimuth, elevation, speed, and recovery, the authors can hold a contact state fixed and ask how the conditional shot distribution changes over training. They can also hold a shot and an opponent response fixed and ask how recovery changes. The source says the factorization “matches the sport’s decision structure and allows individual components to be visualized under fixed contact states” [PAGE 3]. This is a gray-zone because the structured interface is deliberately discrete. The authors warn that the resulting tactical richness should be read “as variation within this interpretable discretized control interface, not as evidence that the same policy would be unchanged under a continuous-action formulation” [PAGE 4]. That caveat matters: many apparent tactical simplifications may be artifacts of binning, action masking, or recovery-grid resolution.

The physics layer is also a gray zone. The source says ShuttleArena “models shuttle flight as a high-drag projectile under gravity and drag-square dynamics” and uses “kh = 0.20 and kv = 0.16” with a numerical integration step of 0.01 s and maximum initial shuttle speed 100.0 m/s [PAGE 3]. These constants are not arbitrary because the appendix ties them to external reference checks: “kh = 0.20 gives a horizontal speed-halving distance ln 2/kh = 3.47 m, close to the measured 3.35 m scale reported by Collet (2026), while kv = 0.16 gives a vertical terminal speed sqrt(g/kv) = 7.83 m/s, close to published feather-shuttle terminal-speed values.” [PAGE 12]. The validation is intentionally limited: it checks shuttle flight, not full biomechanics, racket contact, or perception. The source states that the validation “is limited to the shuttle-flight layer and is not intended as a full model of biomechanics or racket contact” [PAGE 12]. Therefore, any claim about ShuttleArena reproducing real professional human motor behavior must be treated as an inference beyond the model’s declared scope.

The learning problem introduces another layer. The authors use PPO self-play against a checkpoint pool. They first use pure recency-biased sampling, then switch to a broader linear-recency continuation after 3.0M steps. The reason is that “Pure-recency evaluation tends to plateau near this point, consistent with over-emphasis on the newest opponents and possible overfitting to a narrow local style.” [PAGE 4]. The source also warns against single scalar evaluation: “A policy can improve against the current pool while becoming worse against an older style” [PAGE 4]. This is a crucial gray-zone in self-play evaluation. Win-rate matrices can show non-transitive structure because “different checkpoints may represent different tactical styles, and a policy can be strong against recent opponents while remaining vulnerable to an older style.” [PAGE 5]. The Bradley–Terry/Elo fit is therefore used as a retrospective summary, not as a precise small-difference measurement. The authors explicitly say the ratings “are used only as a retrospective summary of the win-rate matrix, not as a training reward.” [PAGE 5].

Recovery is treated as a credit-assignment problem, not a cosmetic add-on. The Counterfactual Recovery Advantage compares the selected recovery with alternative recoveries under the same shot and sampled opponent response [PAGE 9]. This is where the environment becomes genuinely interactive: the recovery head is evaluated in a context generated by the shot, not by a static state alone. The source states that the learned policy “does not simply return to court center; it discovers context-dependent targets” [PAGE 6]. The ablation at 6.0M steps reports learned recovery at approximately 1683 Elo versus centered recovery at approximately 1429 Elo [PAGE 6]. The authors correctly interpret this as “the total competitive consequence of the intervention; it is not a local causal decomposition in which future states are otherwise identical.” [PAGE 6]. That distinction is important. Replacing recovery changes downstream state distribution, so the intervention is a policy-level perturbation, not a clean counterfactual holding all future states fixed.

The deepest audit concern is whether ShuttleArena’s abstractions create tactical artifacts. The source admits that “some tactical structure may reflect the chosen action parameterization and simplified opponent-response dynamics” [PAGE 8]. It also says the model lacks “full biomechanics, deceptive preparation, fatigue, perception uncertainty, spin-dependent aerodynamics, or detailed racket contact mechanics.” [PAGE 8]. Those omissions do not invalidate the paper’s narrow claim, because the claim is about a physics-based testbed for interpretable self-play, not about a fully calibrated replica of professional badminton. The human-data sanity check is similarly bounded: “This comparison is not an imitation target, an external-policy comparison, or a calibrated realism score.” [PAGE 6]. It is a sanity check, and it reveals a clear rally-length gap and shallower learned recovery targets relative to ShuttleSet22 [PAGE 6]. The correct inference is therefore modest: ShuttleArena learns recognizable badminton-like structure inside its simulator, while the simulator’s abstractions still shape the behavior.

The source gives a compact set of formal operators. The first is the hitter shot vector:

\[
a_{\text{shot},t}=(\phi_t,\theta_t,v_t)
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\phi_t\) is azimuth, \(\theta_t\) is elevation or polar angle, and \(v_t\) is initial speed [PAGE 3]. Units: \(\phi_t\) and \(\theta_t\) are angles, interpretable as radians in the physical simulator or as discrete bin indices in the policy; \(v_t\) has units \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The trained run discretizes this into “11 azimuth bins, 8 elevation bins, and 5 speed bins” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regime: if a selected bin is invalid under the simulator’s action-validity logic, ShuttleArena “clips or projects invalid choices to feasible shot outcomes” [PAGE 3]; the learned categorical distribution is therefore not the executed physical distribution until validity masking or projection is applied.

The policy factorization is:

\[
\pi_{\text{hit}}(a_t\mid s_t)
=
\pi_\phi(\phi_t\mid s_t)
\pi_\theta(\theta_t\mid s_t,\phi_t)
\pi_v(v_t\mid s_t,\phi_t,\theta_t)
\pi_{\text{rec}}(r_t\mid s_t,\phi_t,\theta_t,v_t)
\tag{2}
\]

[PAGE 3]. Each conditional probability is dimensionless; the joint hitter distribution is also dimensionless. The receiver branch instead produces

\[
\pi_{\text{int}}(I_t\mid s_t,a_t^{\text{in}},M_t)
\]

where \(a_t^{\text{in}}\) is the incoming shot and \(M_t\) is the feasible-interception mask [PAGE 3]. Assumption: receiver and hitter decisions occur on consecutive role-specific simulator steps, “rather than sampled as one joint action” [PAGE 3]. Breakdown: if the mask \(M_t\) is empty, no legal interception exists and the rally terminates or is projected to a miss according to simulator logic.

The drag model is described qualitatively and then calibrated by two scaling relations. The source says ShuttleArena uses “drag-square dynamics” and separate coefficients \(k_h=0.20\), \(k_v=0.16\) [PAGE 3]. A reduced formal representation consistent with the source’s validation equations is:

\[
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{v},
\qquad
\dot{\mathbf{v}}=-g\hat{\mathbf{z}}-\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{v}),
\]

with quadratic drag magnitude \(D=\kappa \lVert \mathbf{v}\rVert^2\) and separate effective coefficients \(\kappa_h,\kappa_v\) in \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\). For one-dimensional horizontal motion, quadratic drag gives

\[
\frac{dv}{dt}=-\kappa v^2,
\qquad
\frac{dv}{dx}=-\kappa v,
\qquad
v(x)=v_0 e^{-\kappa x}.
\]

The speed-halving distance is therefore

\[
d_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{\kappa_h}
\]

[PAGE 12]. With \(k_h=0.20\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(d_{1/2}=3.47\ \mathrm{m}\), matching the source’s reported scale [PAGE 12]. For vertical equilibrium,

\[
\kappa_v V_T^2=g,
\qquad
V_T=\sqrt{\frac{g}{\kappa_v}},
\]

with \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). With \(k_v=0.16\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(V_T=7.83\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 12]. Units: \(\kappa_h,\kappa_v\) are \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(d_{1/2}\) is \(\mathrm{m}\), \(V_T\) is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Breakdown regimes: if \(\kappa\to 0\), the model degenerates toward ballistic motion and \(d_{1/2}\to\infty\); if \(\kappa\) becomes too large relative to the integration step, explicit integration can become unstable. The source uses \(\Delta t=0.01\ \mathrm{s}\) and maximum initial speed \(100.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 3], so the coarse discrete stability condition \(\kappa v \Delta t < 1\) is satisfied for the nominal horizontal case because \(0.20\cdot 100\cdot 0.01=0.2\), but it would break for much larger speeds or drag coefficients.

The fast-reaction miss model is also formalizable. Let \(T\) be flight time before contact, in seconds. The source says: “contacts with less than 0.1 s of flight time are missed with probability 0.8; between 0.1 and 0.5 s, this probability decreases linearly to zero, and it remains zero thereafter.” [PAGE 3]. Thus:

\[
p_{\text{miss}}(T)=
\begin{cases}
0.8, & 0<T<0.1,\\
0.8\cdot\dfrac{0.5-T}{0.4}, & 0.1\le T<0.5,\\
0, & T\ge 0.5.
\end{cases}
\]

The probability is dimensionless; \(T\) is in seconds. Breakdown: at \(T=0\) the model is undefined because flight time is zero; the source avoids this by conditioning on actual contact candidates.

The ranking operator is the Bradley–Terry model:

\[
P(i\succ j)=\frac{\exp(\rho_i)}{\exp(\rho_i)+\exp(\rho_j)}
\tag{3}
\]

[PAGE 5]. Here \(\rho_i,\rho_j\) are scalar retrospective ratings, dimensionless on an Elo-style scale. The win probability is dimensionless. Assumption: each pairwise comparison is summarized by a scalar latent rating. Breakdown: numerical overflow can occur if raw exponentiated ratings are too large; only rating differences \(\Delta\rho=\rho_i-\rho_j\) are identifiable. The source explicitly treats the ratings as “only a retrospective summary” and not as precise small-difference measurements [PAGE 5].

The Counterfactual Recovery Advantage uses critic values. The critic estimate before contact is

\[
v_t=V_\psi(s_t)
\tag{4}
\]

[PAGE 9]. The recovery learning target is

\[
y^{\text{rec}}_t=
\begin{cases}
R_t, & \text{Rally terminates},\\
V_\psi(s_{t+1}), & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\tag{5}
\]

The transitional recovery advantage is

\[
A^{\text{trans}}_{\text{rec},t}=y^{\text{rec}}_t-v_t
\tag{6}
\]

[PAGE 9]. For candidate recovery target \(r\), the simulator constructs post-shot state \(x_t(r;a^{\text{shot}}_t,\hat{o}_t)\), and the critic scores it:

\[
q_{\text{rec}}(r)=V_\psi\!\left(x_t(r;a^{\text{shot}}_t,\hat{o}_t)\right)
\tag{7}
\]

[PAGE 9]. The counterfactual baseline is

\[
b_{\text{rec}}=\frac{1}{|R'|}\sum_{r'\in R'}q_{\text{rec}}(r')
\tag{8}
\]

where \(R'\) contains sampled alternatives and excludes the selected recovery [PAGE 9]. The counterfactual term is

\[
A^{\text{cf}}_{\text{rec},t}=q_{\text{rec}}(r_t)-b_{\text{rec}}
\tag{9}
\]

and the recovery advantage used in the PPO surrogate is

\[
\tilde{A}^{\text{rec}}_t=A^{\text{trans}}_{\text{rec},t}+\lambda_{\text{cf}}A^{\text{cf}}_{\text{rec},t},
\qquad
\lambda_{\text{cf}}=0.05.
\tag{10}
\]

[PAGE 10]. Units: values and advantages are dimensionless returns if terminal reward is dimensionless; \(\lambda_{\text{cf}}\) is dimensionless. Assumptions: one sampled opponent response \(\hat{o}_t\) is reused for all candidate recoveries; the analyzed run uses 24 alternative recovery samples [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: if \(R'=\varnothing\), equation (8) is undefined; if the critic is poorly calibrated, \(q_{\text{rec}}(r)\) may rank recovery cells incorrectly; if the single opponent-response sample is unrepresentative, the counterfactual baseline is biased.

The probe diagnostics use information units. For an empirical categorical distribution \(p\), entropy is

\[
H(p)=-\sum_k p_k\ln p_k
\]

in nats if natural logarithms are used. Effective support is

\[
\mathrm{supp}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\exp(H(p))
\]

with dimensionless count interpretation. The Jensen–Shannon divergence is measured in nats. The source reports “mean joint shot–landing Jensen–Shannon divergence is about 0.17 nats” and “mean Jensen–Shannon divergence reaching about 0.48 nats by 6.0M steps” [PAGE 15]. Breakdown: if a category has zero empirical frequency across all probes, the distribution is undefined unless smoothing is specified; if \(H=0\), effective support is 1, indicating deterministic behavior.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
ShuttleArena’s lineage is explicitly game-AI and self-play. The introduction traces a path “from TD-Gammon (Tesauro 1995) through AlphaGo and AlphaZero-style self-play (Silver et al. 2016, 2018)” [PAGE 1]. It then situates its own training stack in modern practice: “policy-gradient optimization such as PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), competitive or population-based training (Bansal et al. 2018; Jaderberg et al. 2019), retrospective skill ratings (Elo 1978; Herbrich, Minka, and Graepel 2006), and game-theoretic population views such as policy-space response oracles (Lanctot et al. 2017).” [PAGE 1]. The paper’s scientific genealogy is therefore not badminton-specific first; it is self-play evaluation plus structured action spaces plus counterfactual credit assignment.

A key lineage trap is the assumption that average scalar performance is sufficient. The paper explicitly rejects that simplification: “aggregate handcrafted metrics, such as average shot speed, may saturate early or fluctuate, while policy quality remains fundamentally relative to the opponent population.” [PAGE 1]. This is a multi-week trap because during training, a policy may appear to improve on a local average while becoming brittle against older styles. The win-rate matrix is designed to expose this: “Later checkpoints usually dominate earlier checkpoints, but the matrix contains off-diagonal structure rather than a perfectly monotonic ordering.” [PAGE 5]. The non-transitive structure is not a bug; it is a known feature of population-based self-play. The source says this is “expected in non-transitive self-play populations” [PAGE 5].

Another lineage trap is the overfitting of opponent sampling to recency. The source describes a two-stage schedule: “The main training schedule first runs to 3.0M self-play timesteps with pure recency-biased sampling from the continuation checkpoint pool.” [PAGE 4]. It then notes that “Pure-recency evaluation tends to plateau near this point, consistent with over-emphasis on the newest opponents and possible overfitting to a narrow local style.” [PAGE 4]. The continuation uses “a broader linear-recency scenario” [PAGE 4]. This is a retro-causal correction: the evaluation plateau at 3.0M suggests that the training distribution, not the agent alone, was causing saturation. The source says broader sampling “delays apparent self-play saturation in this setup.” [PAGE 5].

A further historical trap comes from treating recovery as cosmetic. In racket sports, the value of a shot is often judged by immediate trajectory, but ShuttleArena’s ablation shows recovery matters. The paper states: “the learned recovery behavior is competitively important.” [PAGE 6]. At 6.0M steps, learned recovery reaches approximately 1683 Elo while centered recovery reaches approximately 1429 Elo [PAGE 6]. This updates a lineage assumption from classical stroke selection: the immediate stroke is not separable from the post-stroke body-state distribution.

The paper also inherits the parameterized-action lineage. It cites parameterized, branched, and hybrid action spaces (Masson, Ranchod, and Konidaris 2016; Tavakoli, Pardo, and Kormushev 2018; Fan et al. 2019) [PAGE 2]. ShuttleArena extends this by making the factors scientifically probeable: shot azimuth, elevation, speed, and recovery target can be fixed or varied independently. The CRA method is explicitly related to counterfactual baselines: “The counterfactual part of this signal is related in spirit to counterfactual baselines for credit assignment, particularly Counterfactual Multi-Agent (COMA) policy gradients (Foerster et al. 2018), but applies the comparison to one action factor rather than to one agent in a cooperative multi-agent team.” [PAGE 2].

The multi-week traps are therefore: first, over-trusting average metrics; second, over-trusting recent opponents; third, underestimating non-transitive style drift; fourth, assuming recovery is secondary; fifth, conflating simulator plausibility with human realism. The source handles the last trap carefully: the ShuttleSet22 comparison “is not an imitation target, an external-policy comparison, or a calibrated realism score.” [PAGE 6]. The genealogical lesson is that ShuttleArena’s validity depends on keeping competitive claims inside the population and keeping behavioral claims inside the probeable simulator.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
ShuttleArena requires a shallow but explicit recursive theory of mind. The hitter does not merely choose a trajectory; the hitter chooses a trajectory that anticipates the receiver’s interception set. The source says the policy must “choose a physically feasible shuttle trajectory, anticipate the opponent’s interception, and recover to a court position whose value depends on the opponent’s next response.” [PAGE 1]. This is a two-level recursive loop: shot \(\rightarrow\) opponent reply \(\rightarrow\) hitter recovery value. The environment enforces this by making the receiver’s feasible interception depend on “movement constraints, reaction time, acceleration, racket length, and maximum hitting height.” [PAGE 3].

Crisis habit regression appears in the fast-shot regime. The simulator includes a fast-reaction miss model because otherwise interception could become “unrealistically perfect” on shots that leave little time [PAGE 3]. If flight time is under 0.1 s, the miss probability is 0.8; between 0.1 s and 0.5 s it decreases linearly to zero [PAGE 3]. In crisis states, a naive policy might regress to habitual high-speed attacks because they create immediate pressure. The source shows that learned agents do not simply repeat a universal preference: “The high-probability modes differ in direction, depth, speed, and trajectory geometry, indicating that the policy conditions on opponent state rather than repeating a universal shot preference.” [PAGE 6]. This is a counter-habit signal. When the opponent is displaced, fast deep trajectories become high-probability options; when the opponent is already waiting in the backcourt, “softer and shorter alternatives receive more probability.” [PAGE 6].

The counter-trap mechanism has three parts. First, opponent-pool sampling prevents the agent from overfitting to one local style. The source retains “a small probability of heuristic opponents … to anchor basic play and avoid collapse.” [PAGE 4]. Second, the continuation uses broader historical anchors: “most opponent samples are drawn from historical anchors with linear recency weighting” [PAGE 4]. Third, the recovery head receives a factor-specific counterfactual signal. The source says the recovery factor uses “a recovery-specific PPO advantage augmented by Counterfactual Recovery Advantage” [PAGE 4]. This helps prevent the recovery head from being credited or blamed for outcomes caused mainly by shot selection or opponent strength.

The clock is explicit in the evaluation. Training is organized around “3.0M self-play timesteps” and a “3.0M–6.0M continuation” [PAGE 4]. Checkpoints are evaluated at fixed intervals, including “30 fixed-pool opponents sampled every 0.2M timesteps from 0.2M to 6.0M” and “200 rallies per cell” [PAGE 5]. The robustness sweep uses “1000 side-balanced rallies” per cell [PAGE 15]. These clock choices matter because self-play behavior can change rapidly. A policy that looks strong at 3.0M may be vulnerable after a branch in opponent distribution. The source observes that after the broader sampling switch, “later policies—now trained against a more varied opponent distribution rather than only the newest local opponent style—obtain high win rates against many earlier pure-recency checkpoints.” [PAGE 5].

The recursive model is still limited. CRA uses “one opponent-response sample conditioned on the selected shot, and the same response context is reused when comparing recovery targets.” [PAGE 9]. The source admits that “using multiple opponent-response samples could reduce estimator variance or bias in the recovery baseline and is a natural extension.” [PAGE 9]. Thus the agent’s opponent model is not a full distribution over future replies; it is a sampled bridge between shot and recovery evaluation. A gray-zone failure would occur if the single sampled opponent response systematically favored certain recovery cells, causing the recovery head to learn a response-specific artifact rather than a robust tactical rule. The fixed-context probes partially guard against this because they hold the shot-response context fixed, but they cannot prove optimality under the full distribution of human-like opponent responses.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer does not veto ShuttleArena’s core claims, provided the claims remain inside the simulator’s declared scope. The source validates the shuttle-flight layer against four canonical shot families: clear/lift, drop, smash, and drive [PAGE 12]. For the clear/lift, the reference is the BWF shuttle-speed test: “a correct-speed shuttle, hit with a full underhand stroke from the back boundary line, should land 0.53–0.99 m short of the opposite back boundary line” [PAGE 12]. The simulated example “lands 0.74 m short of the far back line,” inside that window [PAGE 12]. For the smash, the source compares to Collet (2026): “approximately exponential velocity decay, a velocity-halving distance near 3.35 m, and a roughly 0.44 s time-of-flight over 10 m for a 300 km/h smash.” [PAGE 12]. The simulated smash “reaches 10 m in 0.40 s and has speed ratio V(3.35m)/V0 = 0.51” [PAGE 12]. These checks are physically plausible, not perfect calibration. The paper says so directly: the checks are “intended as plausibility validation of the shuttle-flight model, not as detailed calibration of player biomechanics or racket contact.” [PAGE 13].

A physical veto would arise if a learned tactic required a shuttle speed or trajectory outside the simulator’s legal action or physics envelope. The source gives maximum initial shuttle speed \(100.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 3], which is above the Collet reference smash speed of 300 km/h, or \(83.33\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Thus the simulated smash validation is inside the maximum-speed envelope. A physical veto would also arise if drag constants implied impossible terminal speeds or halving distances. The nominal values pass: \(d_{1/2}=3.47\ \mathrm{m}\) and \(V_T=7.83\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [PAGE 12]. The drag robustness sweep shows smooth changes when both coefficients are scaled from 0.8 to 1.2 times nominal values; clear depth is the most sensitive metric, while smash time-to-10 m and drop landing depth change smoothly [PAGE 12]. This does not veto the main claims; it bounds their sensitivity.

The biomechanical layer partially vetoes any claim of human motor equivalence. The source states that the model “does not yet include full biomechanics, deceptive preparation, fatigue, perception uncertainty, spin-dependent aerodynamics, or detailed racket contact mechanics.” [PAGE 8]. Therefore, any conclusion that ShuttleArena learned human-like fatigue management, deceptive racket preparation, or spin control would be vetoed. The movement model is also simplified: players move in two dimensions, with speed 5.0 m/s, acceleration \(8.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), racket length 1.6 m, maximum hitting height 2.6 m, and reaction time 0.15 s [PAGE 3]. The racket reach is height-dependent, contracting as contact height approaches the maximum hitting height [PAGE 3]. These are lightweight abstractions, not full biomechanics. The human-data sanity check reflects this: ShuttleArena rallies are shorter than ShuttleSet22 rallies, and recovery targets are systematically closer to the front court [PAGE 6]. The source lists plausible contributors, including “simplified player motion and interception, exact execution without human motor or perceptual noise and fatigue, simulator-specific tactical exploitation, randomized rally initialization rather than full match context, and optimization for rally reward rather than human imitation.” [PAGE 6]. A biomechanical veto therefore applies to any claim that the learned policies reproduce human physical constraints beyond the simulator.

The weather layer is absent. ShuttleArena does not model wind, humidity, air density changes, arena pressure, or thermal effects on shuttle drag. Since drag coefficients are fixed and shuttle flight is simulated under nominal constants, any claim about weather-adjusted shuttle flight, wind-dependent shot selection, or humidity-sensitive terminal velocity is vetoed by the source’s declared state and simulator scope. The source’s physical constants are fixed at \(k_h=0.20\), \(k_v=0.16\), player speed 5.0 m/s, acceleration \(8.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), reaction time 0.15 s, and fast-reaction miss probability 0.8 below 0.1 s [PAGE 12]. The robustness sweep perturbs these constants at evaluation time, but it does not add weather. It tests “whether the main qualitative conclusions persist when these constants are perturbed at evaluation time” [PAGE 12]. The sweep preserves all three main conclusions: late beats early, learned recovery beats centered recovery, and the CRA branch remains higher in the matched comparison [PAGE 15]. Thus, within the simulator, the conclusions are robust to several local parameter changes, but outside the simulator’s declared layer inventory, physical realism claims are vetoed.

The adjudication is therefore layered. Physical flight claims pass the source’s plausibility checks. Biomechanical and perceptual claims are vetoed unless explicitly restated as simulator-internal claims. Weather claims are vetoed outright because the environment does not include weather. The correct proof is: let \(C\) be a conclusion about learned ShuttleArena behavior. If \(C\) depends only on the simulated shot, interception, recovery, and rally reward, then \(C\) is admissible under the source’s own validation and ablation evidence. If \(C\) depends on unmodeled layers such as fatigue, spin, perception uncertainty, full scored matches, or weather, then \(C\) is not supported by the source and must be vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios for ShuttleArena’s main claims, framed as reinforcement-learning checks with observable predictions. They do not rely on external performance claims; they rely on the source’s reported metrics, probes, and ablations.

Veto 1: Recovery-is-cosmetic veto. Scenario: if the learned recovery factor is replaced by a centered recovery override while the shot policy is held fixed, and the learned recovery policy does not win more than half of rallies across seeds and robustness variants, then the claim that recovery is competitively important is vetoed. Verification: compare learned recovery versus centered recovery using shared fixed opponent pools, with 200 rallies per cell for main evaluations and 1000 side-balanced rallies for robustness cells. Prediction from source: learned recovery wins. At 6.0M steps, “the learned-recovery variant reaches approximately 1683 Elo on average, while the centered-recovery variant reaches approximately 1429 Elo.” [PAGE 6]. The robustness sweep reports learned recovery beating centered recovery with win rates between 0.738 and 0.805 across variants [PAGE 15]. Therefore, this veto is not triggered by the reported evidence.

Veto 2: CRA-no-effect veto. Scenario: if disabling Counterfactual Recovery Advantage, by using zero alternative recovery samples and coefficient 0, produces checkpoints statistically indistinguishable from the CRA lineage under a common mixed opponent pool, then the claim that factor-specific counterfactual recovery credit helps is vetoed. Verification: freeze checkpoints from both lineages, evaluate against a common mixed pool, fit one Elo scale, and bootstrap pair outcomes. Prediction from source: CRA is higher after early training. The source reports “at 3.2M steps, the default run is about 1660 Elo versus about 1506 Elo without CRA.” [PAGE 10]. It also reports that in paired bootstrap resampling, the CRA run remains above the ablated run in 100% of 1,000 resamples, with a 95% bootstrap interval of roughly 9–25 percentage points for mean win-rate advantage [PAGE 10]. In the evaluation-time robustness table, the CRA branch remains higher across all listed perturbations [PAGE 15]. Therefore, this veto is not triggered within the source’s reported lineage comparison, though it would become plausible if the single opponent-response sample in CRA were systematically biased.

Veto 3: Interpretability-or-opponent-conditioning veto. Scenario: if fixed-context probes show that shot selection does not condition on opponent state, or that recovery collapses to a single default return-to-center target, then the claim that ShuttleArena exposes interpretable opponent-conditioned tactical behavior is vetoed. Verification: hold contact states fixed and estimate empirical distributions over shot type, landing zone, joint shot-type–landing-zone, and recovery-grid cells. Compute entropy \(H\), effective support \(\exp(H)\), and Jensen–Shannon divergence from the initial checkpoint. Prediction from source: shot policy remains sparse but changes relative to initialization; recovery support expands. The source says that under fixed backcourt states, “when the opponent is displaced or moving away from the target side, fast deep trajectories become high-probability options; when the opponent is already waiting in the backcourt, softer and shorter alternatives receive more probability.” [PAGE 6]. It also reports that recovery “support grows more clearly, from about 1.69 effective cells at initialization to about 2.15 cells by 6.0M steps” and that recovery distribution divergence reaches about 0.48 nats [PAGE 15]. For shots, mean effective joint support remains near 1.3 bins while divergence reaches about 0.17 nats [PAGE 15]. Therefore, this veto is not triggered: the learned policy changes which sparse modes are preferred, and recovery does not collapse to a single center target.

A secondary veto condition concerns environment mismatch. If the main qualitative conclusions reversed under plausible evaluation-time perturbations of drag, player speed, reaction time, or fast-reaction miss probability, then the claims would be fragile. The source tests this directly. The most vulnerable comparison is the late-versus-early lower-drag cell, with win rate 0.540, which is directionally positive but close to the threshold [PAGE 15]. The learned-recovery comparison remains between 0.738 and 0.805 [PAGE 15]. Removing the fast-reaction miss mechanism “does not reverse the conclusions” [PAGE 14]. Thus the verifiable counterfactual record supports the main claims inside the tested perturbations, while identifying lower-drag late-versus-early margin as the narrowest empirical edge.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
