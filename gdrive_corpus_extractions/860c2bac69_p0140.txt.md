Crisis habit regression operates as follows. Under high arousal—“Critical situations,” goal-line stands, or closing seconds [PAGE 113]—players regress to the earliest consolidated motor schema rather than executing the most recently refined diagnostic. In Daly’s hierarchy, “Stance” and “Breaking through (stance, use of hands, body charge)” [PAGE 113] are likely the first-line, highest-repetition patterns; thus a lineman under pressure abandons “Diagnosing plays (end runs, passes, line drives, splits)” [PAGE 113] and defaults to a simple body charge into the nearest gap. This is a collapse to a low-dimensional attractor: working memory (limited to roughly four chunks) can no longer maintain the full play-diagnosis tree, so the system selects the most over-learned response.

The counter-trap is recursive. If the offensive coordinator knows the defense will regress to “end through” on goal-line “Special situations” [PAGE 113], they can run a trap or delayed handoff to the backside. The defensive coordinator, anticipating this, may instruct contain rather than penetrate. The offense, anticipating the contain, may run a direct snap dive. This infinite regress is terminated only by an exogenous clock trigger: down, distance, field position, and game clock force a binarized decision (e.g., 4th and goal, under two minutes). Daly’s isolation of “Critical situations” [PAGE 113] reflects an intuitive recognition that the clock collapses the recursion by external deadline.

Recursive theory of mind across the staff: the head coach models the position coach’s model of the player’s model of the opponent’s tendency. Without film or scouting reports beyond advance notices, the 1921 coaching loop relied on “Starting signal drill” [PAGE 113] to reduce pre-snap uncertainty and on repeated “Diagnosing plays” reps to keep the defensive estimator online. The failure mode is a fixed point in which both teams regress to their base plays because neither can sustain higher-order modeling under fatigue and clock pressure.
