The first hop is data acquisition: standard cameras or mobile devices record a side-profile view encompassing the athlete's entire body [PAGE 3]. The second hop is anthropometric calibration. The operator inputs the athlete's actual height H_real in meters and the vertical offset between ear and top of head. MediaPipe detects the ear landmark, which the authors claim is more stable than the top-of-head landmark for estimating total stature [PAGE 3]. From this, the software estimates pixel stature H_pixel and derives a scale factor κ = H_real / H_pixel, with units m/px. A gray zone appears here: the pixel distance is measured between ear and heel landmarks, then adjusted for head offset, but no uncertainty distribution for H_pixel is given, nor is the sensitivity of κ to ear-landmark jitter quantified.

The third hop is ground detection. The algorithm scans all frames, tracks heel and big-toe Y coordinates, and selects the maximum pixel Y (lowest physical point in image coordinates) as the fixed ground coordinate y_ground [PAGE 3]. This baseline is prerequisite for height and contact time. The fourth hop is pose estimation via MediaPipe, producing time-series landmarks for hip, knee, ankle, shoulder, and foot [PAGE 4]. Joint angles are computed from landmark triples. For the knee, with vectors u = r_hip − r_knee and v = r_ankle − r_knee, the angle is θ_knee = arccos( (u·v) / (|u||v|) ). Similar constructions apply to hip and ankle; the ankle uses an imaginary point parallel to foot movement [PAGE 4]. A gray zone: this is strictly 2D sagittal-plane geometry. Any out-of-plane rotation collapses the 3D joint angle into a projected 2D angle, and the paper provides no correction for frontal-plane deviation.

The fifth hop extracts performance metrics. Ground contact time Δt_contact = (n_toeoff − n_contact) / FPS, where n_contact is the first frame with foot contact and n_toeoff is the last frame before loss of contact [PAGE 8]. Jump height h_jump = (y_ground − y_foot_peak) · κ, where y_foot_peak is the minimum pixel Y of the foot during flight [PAGE 6]. Reactive Strength Index RSI = h_jump / Δt_contact, units m/s (or cm/s as tabulated) [PAGE 4].

\]
Units: px. Breakdown regime: if the foot never contacts the visible frame or the heel is occluded at landing, y_ground is undefined and height/contact time cannot be computed.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage plus multi-week traps.

The intellectual genealogy traces from Verhoshansky's "shock method" depth jumps through the modern stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) formalism, to Flanagan and Comyns (2008), who defined the Reactive Strength Index using contact time and jump height to optimize fast-SSC training [PAGE 1, Ref 1]. Loturco et al. (2017) extended this to power development in team sports [PAGE 10, Ref 2]. The present work inherits this lineage but replaces force platforms and optical motion capture with monocular MediaPipe pose estimation, a shift justified by Niu's posture-recognition argument and related computer-vision works in karate, muaythai, weightlifting, and volleyball tracking [PAGE 1–2, Refs 3–7, 10–12]. Simoes et al. (2024) established MediaPipe accuracy for physiotherapy pose estimation at 99.22% for upper limbs, which the authors extend to lower-limb plyometrics [PAGE 2, Ref 12].

Multi-week traps emerge when this tool is embedded in a training microcycle. First, RSI optimization can select for minimal contact time via increased lower-limb stiffness; over weeks, this raises patellar-tendon and Achilles loading without a concomitant eccentric-capacity metric, creating an injury-debt trap. Second, because the system is 2D sagittal-only, a coach may week-after-week reinforce symmetric-looking sagittal angles while ignoring frontal-plane knee valgus that the camera cannot see, producing latent asymmetry. Third, daily RSI monitoring without fatigue adjustment creates a regression trap: an athlete whose RSI drops due to accumulated neuromuscular fatigue may be pushed to "rebound faster," precisely when stretch-shortening cycle integrity is compromised. Fourth, the <1.0° angular precision claim is anchored to N=3 validation; over a multi-week block, inter-day landmark-detection variance (lighting, clothing, camera angle) likely exceeds intra-session error, so week-to-week "progress" may be measurement noise. The paper's own conclusion calls for high-speed cameras (>120 FPS), depth sensors, and frontal/diagonal views to mitigate these traps [PAGE 9], acknowledging the current POC is not yet robust for longitudinal prescription.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Recursive theory of mind models the nested beliefs of athlete, coach, and algorithm. Under crisis (competition pressure, perceived fatigue, or a failed previous jump), the athlete regresses to a crisis habit: longer ground contact, reduced knee flexion, or a conservative drop height. The coach, observing through the computer-vision interface, sees the algorithm's annotated angles and contact time. First-order belief: the coach believes the numbers. Second-order belief: the coach believes the athlete trusts the numbers. Third-order belief: the athlete knows the coach is monitoring RSI and may intentionally shorten contact time by landing stiffer to game the metric, even if that strategy increases injury risk.

The counter-trap is epistemic overfitting to the tool. Because the paper reports sub-degree angular accuracy [PAGE 5], a coach may treat the algorithm's output as ground truth and prescribe drop heights or volume based on RSI deltas that are within the parallax tolerance of ±5 cm [PAGE 7] or within one camera frame at low FPS [PAGE 9]. The counter-trap fires when the model is trusted beyond its breakdown regime: prescribing training from a monocular, sagittal-only, frame-quantized proxy as though it were a force-platform-derived dynamic index.

The clock is the physical deadline imposed by FPS. Contact events shorter than one inter-frame interval are invisible; at 30 FPS the clock resolution is 33.3 ms, while meaningful reactive-strength differences can be smaller [PAGE 8–9]. The algorithm's real-time window is therefore bounded by a hard temporal ceiling: events below the clock granularity are aliased, and the coach's mental model updates on a delayed, quantized signal. Recursive instability arises when the athlete adapts to the coach's clocked feedback, the coach adapts to the athlete's adaptation, and neither accounts for the unobserved sub-frame and out-of-plane degrees of freedom. The result is a meta-stable loop in which measured RSI improves while true reactive capacity and injury risk diverge.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Layer 2 — Biomechanical (plane and symmetry). Veto condition: out-of-sagittal-plane deviation exceeding ~10°, or inability to view both lower limbs simultaneously. The system is built for side-profile capture and computes 2D joint angles [PAGE 3–4]. It cannot detect knee valgus, foot rotation, or left/right asymmetry; the authors themselves propose adding frontal/diagonal views for future symmetry analysis [PAGE 9]. If this veto fires, hip/knee/angle outputs are projections, not true joint angles, breaching the <1.0° claim [PAGE 5].

Layer 3 — Environmental/weather. Veto condition: variable outdoor lighting, precipitation, wind-induced camera vibration, or lens condensation that degrades MediaPipe landmark confidence. Though not explicitly quantified in the paper, computer-vision pose estimation depends on image quality; occlusion or poor contrast breaks landmark tracking [PAGE 4]. If this veto fires, the time series of angles and foot Y positions contains gaps or jumps, corrupting h_jump and Δt_contact.

Layer 4 — Anthropometric calibration. Veto condition: error in athlete's stated height H_real exceeding ~2 cm, or incorrect ear-to-head offset entry. Since κ = H_real/H_pixel [PAGE 3], a 2 cm error in a 1.8 m athlete propagates as ~1.1% length bias, shifting h_jump by ~0.5 cm at 50 cm jump height—within but consequential relative to the ±5 cm parallax tolerance [PAGE 7].

Adjudication order: Physical > Biomechanical > Environmental > Calibration. If Layer 1 vetoes, the dataset is inadmissible regardless of biomechanical clarity. If Layer 1 passes but Layer 2 vetoes, angle and RSI outputs are suppressed while contact time may remain usable only under strict sagittal compliance. If Layer 3 vetoes intermittently, affected frames must be excised rather than interpolated. A veto at any layer renders the POC output non-diagnostic for injury-prevention decisions; the system may only return to advisory status after the violating condition is remediated and re-validated.
