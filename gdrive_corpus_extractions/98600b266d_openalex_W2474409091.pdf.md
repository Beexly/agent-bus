The likelihood is the product of color and motion terms, Eq. (14): \(p(s_m|x_n)\propto p_{\text{color}}(s_m|x_n)\,p_{\text{motion}}(s_m|x_n)\) [PAGE 8]. The global likelihood then adjudicates competing claims: every track within \(r_{\max}\) claims nearby particles, each particle normalizes weights across claiming tracks, and the particle is assigned only to the highest-weighted track unless the Bayes occlusion detector overrides the assignment [PAGE 8]. The source’s justification is explicitly multi-agent: “During occlusions, the concept of densely sampled particles and global likelihood (GL) calculation enables players to be aware of each other, keep their locations while their view is blocked” [PAGE 4].

The gray-zone audit is where the pipeline can fail. The motion model assumes constant velocity, but the source warns: “Unlike vehicles or pedestrians, which have relatively predictable motion patterns, soccer players try to confuse each other with unexpected changes in velocity” [PAGE 1]. Thus process noise \(Q\) and \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) are not cosmetic; they encode the allowed acceleration and turn rate. If \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) is too small, the motion likelihood suppresses the true particle after a cut or tackle; if it is too large, color dominates and similar jerseys create identity switches. The paper’s own ablation says, “using only color results in more identity switches, whereas using only motion causes a lot of track losses. A second gray zone is full occlusion: the Bayes detector can associate particles to an occluded track regardless of weight, but the source admits, “The failure scenarios of the proposed tracking methodology include challenging cases of three or more players involved in a possession or a tackle in which more than one player may be completely occluded. The system mitigates this with adaptive foreground learning, higher billboard learning rates, HOG tolerance to illumination and contrast, and auto-exposure, but those are mitigations, not proofs of invariance. The correct audit conclusion is that Sentioscope is a probabilistic arbitration system whose reliability is conditional on calibration validity, motion-noise calibration, sufficient camera height, and bounded simultaneous occlusion.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a model-field particle set
\[
S=\{s_1,s_2,\ldots,s_M\},\qquad s_m=\langle q_m,B_m,A_m\rangle ,
\]
where \(q_m\in\mathbb R^2\) is in meters on the model pitch, \(B_m\) is an image-plane bounding box measured in pixels, and \(A_m\) is a normalized appearance histogram. The paper reports \(M=64\,260\) for a \(105\times68\ \mathrm m\) field at nine particles per square meter [PAGE 4]. The positive detection subset is \(S_P\subset S\). State is
\[
x_t^n=\langle p_t^n,\vec v_t^n,R^n\rangle ,
\]
with \(p_t^n\) in meters, \(\vec v_t^n\) in meters per second, and \(R^n\) the reference appearance model [PAGE 6].

Lens distortion is modeled by Brown’s radial model:
\[
\begin{aligned}
x_{\text{image}}&=x_d+(x_d-c_x)\left(K_1r^2+K_2r^4\right),\\
y_{\text{image}}&=y_d+(y_d-c_y)\left(K_1r^2+K_2r^4\right),\\
r&=\left((x_d-c_x)^2+(y_d-c_y)^2\right)^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here image coordinates are in pixels; \(K_1\) has units of \(\mathrm{pixel}^{-2}\) and \(K_2\) of \(\mathrm{pixel}^{-4}\). The coefficients are chosen by minimizing the average mean-squared error of fitted field-boundary lines [PAGE 4]. The mapping from model field to undistorted image is the homography \(q_{\text{image}}=Hq_{\text{model}}\) [PAGE 4]. Bounding-box height is computed by perspective:
\[
h_{\text{cam}}=\frac{\min\|u_{\text{bottom}}-L_{\text{horizon}}\|\,h_{\text{goal}}}{\|u_{\text{bottom}}-u_{\text{top}}\|},
\]
\[
h_{B_m}=\frac{\min\|q_{\text{image}}^m-L_{\text{horizon}}\|\,h_T}{h_{\text{cam}}},
\]
where \(h_{\text{goal}}=2.44\ \mathrm m\), \(h_T\) is target height in meters, and the output \(h_{B_m}\) is in pixels; “The width of \(B_m\) is set to half of the height” [PAGE 5].

Prediction uses a constant-velocity Kalman filter:
\[
p(x_t^n|x_{t-1}^n)\propto F_tx_{t-1}^n+\omega_t,\qquad
F_t=\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix},
\]
with \(\omega_t\sim\mathcal N(0,Q)\) and
\[
Q=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta t^4/4&\Delta t^3/2\\[2pt]\Delta t^3/2&\Delta t^2\end{bmatrix}\sigma_{\text{acc}}^2,
\]
where \(\Delta t=1/\mathrm{fps}\) is in seconds and \(\sigma_{\text{acc}}^2\) has units of \(\mathrm m^2/\mathrm s^4\) [PAGE 6]. The observation model is
\[
p_{\text{observed}}^n\sim\sum_{s_m\in f(x_n)}w(x_n,s_m)q_m+\epsilon,\qquad
\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,Z),
\]
with \(Z\) set so that maximum error is about shoulder width, approximately \(0.5\ \mathrm m\) [PAGE 7]. Weights satisfy \(w(x_n,s_m)\propto p(s_m|x_n)\) and \(\sum_{s_m\in f(x_n)}w(x_n,s_m)=1\) [PAGE 7].

Color likelihood uses normalized HSV histograms \(A[c]\) and \(R^n[c]\), \(c=1,\ldots,C\), with \(C=C_hC_s+C_v\). The Bhattacharyya distance is
\[
D_{\text{color}}(R^n,A_m)=\left(1-\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sqrt{R^n[c]A_m[c]}\right)^{1/2},
\]
and
\[
p_{\text{color}}(s_m|x_n)\propto
\exp\left[-\lambda\frac1J\sum_{j=1}^{J}D_{\text{color}}^2(R^n,A_m)\right],
\]
with \(J=2\) body subregions, \(C_h=10\), \(C_s=C_v=5\), and \(\lambda=20\) [PAGE 7]. These are dimensionless probability and histogram quantities; no information-theoretic bit rate is estimated in the source. Motion likelihood uses
\[
D_{\text{motion}}(p^n,q_m)=\|p^n-q_m\|,
\]
\[
\delta(d)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{motion}}\sqrt{\pi}}
\exp\left(-\frac{d^2}{\sigma_{\text{motion}}^2}\right),
\qquad
p_{\text{motion}}(s_m|x_n)\propto \delta(D_{\text{motion}}(p^n,q_m)),
\]
with \(D_{\text{motion}}\) and \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) in meters [PAGE 7]. The combined likelihood is
\[
p(s_m|x_n)\propto p_{\text{color}}(s_m|x_n)\,p_{\text{motion}}(s_m|x_n).
\]
The global arbitration is: \(x_n\) claims \(s_m\) if \(\|p^n-q_m\|\le r_{\max}\); if multiple tracks claim \(s_m\), weights are normalized over \(g(s_m)\); \(s_m\) is assigned to the highest-weighted track \(x^*\) and its likelihood is multiplied by \(w(s_m,x^*)\) [PAGE 8]. A normal Bayes classifier over color likelihood marks a track occluded when all nearby color likelihoods are very low [PAGE 8]. Identity assignment solves a Hungarian assignment with cost \(\|p^{\text{player}}_k-p^{\text{track}}_{kj}\|\) [PAGE 9]. GMOTA metrics are dimensionless and lower-is-better:
\[
\mathrm{FN}=\sum_t \frac{m_t}{g_t},\qquad
\mathrm{FP}=\sum_t \frac{fp_t}{g_t},\qquad
\mathrm{gmme}=\sum_t \frac{gmme_t}{g_t},
\]
where \(g_t\) is ground-truth detections, \(m_t\) misses, \(fp_t\) false positives, and \(gmme_t\) identity switches [PAGE 10].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
- Eq. (1) fails at extreme radius or bad calibration: if \(K_1r^2+K_2r^4\) is not accurate, field lines remain curved and the homography is biased. At \(r\to\infty\) the polynomial model is outside its fitted domain.
- Homography \(H\) is undefined or ill-conditioned when the four correspondences are collinear, when there are fewer than four correspondences, or when the field plane assumption is violated by player height.
- Eq. (3) is singular if \(\|u_{\text{bottom}}-u_{\text{top}}\|=0\), if \(h_{\text{cam}}=0\), or if the horizon line is undefined because vanishing points coincide or the boundary-line pairs are parallel in the image.
- Eq. (7) breaks if true acceleration exceeds the \(\sigma_{\text{acc}}\) model, if \(\Delta t\) is misreported, or if \(\sigma_{\text{acc}}\to0\) so the filter cannot correct.
- Eq. (8) has no observation if \(f(x_n)=\varnothing\). It also fails if \(r_{\max}\) is too small for the player’s displacement in \(\Delta t\).
- Eq. (9) is ill-conditioned when histogram bins are empty or unnormalized; the square root requires normalized nonnegative probability mass.
- Eq. (10) underflows numerically if \(D_{\text{color}}^2\) is large and \(\lambda=20\); \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\to0\) in Eq. (12) is a delta-like singularity, while very large \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) makes the motion term nearly flat and lets color dominate.
- Eq. (14) can underflow as a product of two small likelihoods. The global assignment has no claimant if \(g(s_m)=\varnothing\); occlusion override can create ambiguity when more than one player is fully occluded.
- The Hungarian step is only run when \(|Y'_k| &gt; 0\) and \(|X'_k|\ge |Y'_k|\) [PAGE 9]. GMOTA is undefined when \(g_t=0\).
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The design lineage is retrocausally legible: the 2016 Sentioscope system inherits from lens-distortion photogrammetry, planar homography, HOG human detection, linear SVM classification, adaptive Gaussian-mixture background modeling, Kalman filtering, color-histogram particle filtering, mixture particle filtering, normal Bayes classification, and Hungarian assignment. The paper explicitly names “Brown’s distortion model [42]” [PAGE 4], “a histogram of oriented gradients (HOG) [39] detector” [PAGE 3], “a Kalman Filter [48]” [PAGE 6], and “the Hungarian method [49]” [PAGE 9]. It also contrasts itself with color-based probabilistic tracking [35], mixture particle filters [36], and boosted particle filters [37] [PAGE 3]. The genealogical move is important: Sentioscope does not invent a new single-object tracker from nothing; it changes the measurement space from target-local particles to a shared model-field lattice, then uses global likelihood to arbitrate particle ownership. That change is a response to a known failure mode: “MPF can easily underperform in soccer videos since teammates look almost identical and players are involved in frequent and continuous occlusions. Such cases result in particle degeneration, in which particles of a track are propagated toward another target or transferred to another mixture component” [PAGE 3].

Multi-week traps follow from that lineage. First, a deployment team can mistake shared model-field particles for ordinary target particles and then reimplement target-local resampling; that reintroduces particle degeneration under occlusion. Second, calibration drift is a slow trap: the system uses four field corners for homography and undistortion coefficients from boundary lines [PAGE 4], but camera vibration, wind, lens zoom, or stadium reconstruction can invalidate \(H\) over weeks. Third, appearance adaptation every 10 s is a short-horizon fix: the source says the reference model “is updated every 10 s to cope with pose and illumination changes” [PAGE 7], but if an identity switch occurs, the model can adapt to the wrong player and compound the error over many matches. Fourth, jersey clustering is not static: the paper uses k-NN over jersey classes and reports 99% at \(k=10\), but 93% at \(k=1\) with confusion among similar jerseys [PAGE 12]. A multi-week deployment must monitor class drift, lighting-dependent color shifts, and kit changes. Fifth, camera geometry is a slow confound: the paper reports camera heights from 8 to 37 m and states, “camera height is influential in the tracking performance of Sentioscope” [PAGE 11]. A system that validates on high cameras and deploys on low cameras will appear to regress for algorithmic reasons that are actually geometric. The retrocausal lesson is that later failures often retrace earlier design assumptions: plane geometry, color stability, constant velocity, and unique particle ownership.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In Sentioscope, recursive theory of mind is computational rather than psychological. Each track maintains a first-order belief \(x_t^n\) about its own player, and the global likelihood creates a second-order arbitration: every track “claims” particles within \(r_{\max}\), and the system asks which track most plausibly owns each shared particle [PAGE 8]. This is a recursive attribution loop: track \(n\) predicts where it should be, particle \(s_m\) evaluates all claimants, and the winner’s likelihood is multiplied by its normalized weight. The source describes the purpose as making players “aware of each other” during occlusion [PAGE 4]. That is not literal awareness; it is a shared-state coupling that prevents one track from silently hijacking another track’s evidence.

Crisis habit regression appears when the arbitration breaks. Under a tackle, full occlusion, or sudden acceleration, an individual tracker can regress to a single cue. The paper’s ablation is explicit: “using only color results in more identity switches, whereas using only motion causes a lot of track losses” [PAGE 10]. Color-only regression is the identity-hijack habit: similar jerseys make the wrong particle look plausible. Motion-only regression is the lost-track habit: the predicted position is trusted even when the player has cut or been blocked. The source also identifies the multi-player crisis: “three or more players involved in a possession or a tackle in which more than one player may be completely occluded” can make “the motion model may perform poorly, resulting in track losses” [PAGE 10].

The counter-trap is the combination of global likelihood, occlusion handling, and identity tracking. GL redistributes shared particles; OH prevents a fully occluded track from being dropped merely because all color likelihoods are low; IT re-establishes identities using Hungarian assignment and lost-identity position estimation. The paper reports that “both GL and OH resolve more number of occlusions so that FN and gmme is lower than CML” [PAGE 10], and “When IT step is added, observe that all the error metrics are further reduced below 0.01” [PAGE 10]. The counter-trap is not a guarantee; it is a bounded correction that depends on not having multiple simultaneous full occlusions.

The clock layer is equally important. The filter clock is \(\Delta t=1/\mathrm{fps}\); appearance is updated every 10 s [PAGE 7]; Hungarian assignment is run before kickoff and then “throughout the game whenever \(|Y'_k| &gt; 0\) and \(|X'_k|\ge |Y'_k|\)” [PAGE 9]; and in live professional use the identity process is “supervised by a human operator in real time to correct possible system mistakes” [PAGE 9]. A clock mismatch creates habit regression: if appearance updates faster than identity is verified, the model can learn the wrong player; if the identity clock is slower than occlusion duration, tracks remain unlabeled or mislabeled; if the operator correction clock is absent, small identity switches persist. The recursive theory of mind therefore has three regimes: stable arbitration when clocks and likelihoods agree, crisis regression when one cue dominates, and counter-trap recovery when GL, OH, and IT are all active within their calibrated time constants.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is calibration and projection validity. If undistortion is wrong, every \(q_m\), \(B_m\), and \(h_{B_m}\) is systematically displaced. The source motivates this directly: “Since the cameras shoot a large area (68 × 52.5 m) from close range, the lenses cause radial distortion, resulting in a curved appearance of the actual straight lines in the image” [PAGE 4]. The veto proof is geometric: after Eq. (1), the marked boundary points should lie on straight lines; if the residual line-fit error is not minimized, the homography \(H\) cannot be trusted. A second physical veto is horizon and bounding-box height: Eq. (3) uses the goal post height \(2.44\ \mathrm m\) and the horizon line; if \(u_{\text{bottom}}=u_{\text{top}}\) or the horizon is unstable, \(h_{\text{cam}}\) and \(h_{B_m}\) become singular or wildly scaled. In that regime, the particle bounding boxes no longer encapsulate players, so HOG detection and color histograms are measuring the wrong image patches.

The biomechanical veto is the constant-velocity assumption. The source says soccer players have “unexpected changes in velocity” [PAGE 1]. The Kalman model absorbs acceleration through \(Q\) and \(\sigma_{\text{acc}}\), but if a player accelerates or turns beyond the modeled range, the predicted position \(p^n\) moves away from the true particle. The motion likelihood then penalizes the correct particle; if \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) is too low, the penalty is severe. The paper notes, “choosing a relatively low \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) will introduce a larger penalty because the distance between the predicted position and the sampled particle location increases” [PAGE 7]. The veto proof is therefore an acceleration-and-turn test: when observed displacement exceeds the \(r_{\max}\) claim radius or the acceleration residual exceeds the process-noise envelope, motion evidence must be down-weighted or the track must be allowed to reacquire through color and global assignment. The measurement noise \(Z\) is also biomechanical: it is set to about shoulder width, approximately \(0.5\ \mathrm m\) [PAGE 7], which bounds how much positional error the filter can absorb without attributing it to a different player.

The weather-and-illumination veto is foreground and appearance stability. The source lists “rapid illumination changes,” cloudy-weather shadows, sunny long shadows, and blinking electronic billboards as tracking hazards [PAGE 1]. The mitigation is adaptive: the foreground model uses a dynamic spatial learning rate that reconstructs the mixture model when foreground pixels suddenly increase, and “the learning rates of digital billboard pixels are set to relatively higher values for quick adaptation to continuously changing electronic advertisements” [PAGE 5]. HOG is chosen partly because it can “cope with illumination and contrast variances” [PAGE 6], and exposure is controlled by gray-level histograms of the field [PAGE 4]. The veto proof is conditional: if a lighting transition produces a foreground-pixel surge and the HOG classifier still returns a low-confidence player patch, new track initiation should be vetoed, and existing tracks should rely on prediction plus global assignment rather than accepting the patch as a new player. False positives far from players are naturally eliminated by the tracker, while close false alarms “would possibly get low color and motion likelihood (CML) scores and hence cannot disrupt the tracks” [PAGE 9]. Finally, camera height is a cross-layer geometric veto: the paper reports that “When cameras are placed to capture the soccer field from 37 m above the ground, occlusions are better handled and low FN and gmme rates are achieved” [PAGE 11]. Lower camera height increases inter-player occlusion, which stresses color, motion, GL, and OH simultaneously. The adjudication order should therefore be: geometry validity first, illumination/detection validity second, biomechanical motion validity third, and identity assignment last. If a higher layer is vetoed, lower-layer tracking evidence is not admissible as proof of player identity.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reinforcement or evaluation with verifiable rewards computed from the paper’s annotated tracking metrics: FN, FP, and gmme. The veto predicate is directional and testable: a counterfactual hypothesis is vetoed if its measured metric does not move in the direction the source’s ablation predicts. No synthetic reward ratio is needed; the verifiable signals are the GMOTA components themselves.

1. Veto scenario: remove global likelihood and occlusion handling. Intervention: run the baseline CML tracker, which keeps only the combined color-and-motion likelihood, and compare it with Sentioscope with GL and OH on the same 750-frame Besiktas-Fenerbahce annotation containing 55 partial and 10 full occlusions [PAGE 10]. Verifiable prediction: GL plus OH should lower FN and gmme relative to CML. The source states, “both GL and OH resolve more number of occlusions so that FN and gmme is lower than CML” [PAGE 10]. Veto rule: if removing GL and OH does not increase FN or gmme, the claimed global-arbitration benefit is falsified for that dataset. This is a direct counterfactual because the only change is the presence of GL and OH.

2. Veto scenario: remove identity tracking. Intervention: keep CML plus GL plus OH but omit the lost-identity tracking and Hungarian re-assignment step. Verifiable prediction: the error metrics should not reach the reported sub-0.01 regime. The paper states, “When IT step is added, observe that all the error metrics are further reduced below 0.01” [PAGE 10]. Veto rule: if removing IT leaves FN, FP, and gmme unchanged or lower, the identity-tracking contribution is not verified. A second measurable prediction is that lost identities should accumulate until the condition \(|Y'_k| &gt; 0\) and \(|X'_k|\ge |Y'_k|\) triggers Hungarian assignment [PAGE 9]; if no such accumulation appears, the identity clock or the annotation protocol is suspect.

3. Veto scenario: mis-set the motion scale and color-motion balance. Intervention: run three counterfactuals on the same annotated data: color-only, motion-only, and combined CML with \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) outside the reported 1.25–1.5 range. Verifiable prediction: color-only should produce more identity switches; motion-only should produce more track losses; combined CML inside the range should reduce both. The source says, “using only color results in more identity switches, whereas using only motion causes a lot of track losses. When color and motion features are combined using a \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) between 1.25 and 1.5, the tracking errors significantly decrease” [PAGE 10]. Veto rule: if color-only does not raise gmme, motion-only does not raise FN, or the reported \(\sigma_{\text{motion}}\) interval does not outperform values outside it, the motion-likelihood calibration claim is vetoed. A cross-layer extension is to repeat the test at low and high camera height: the paper predicts that higher camera placement better handles occlusions and yields lower FN and gmme, with reported full-match FN \(0.017\)–\(0.031\) and gmme \(0.015\)–\(0.035\) [PAGE 11]. If the height counterfactual reverses that ordering, the physical-layer veto dominates and the algorithmic comparison is not admissible.
