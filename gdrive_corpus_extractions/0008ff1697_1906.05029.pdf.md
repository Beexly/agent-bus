The source is a ten-page KDD-style paper describing a Bayesian in-game win probability model for soccer. The first multi-hop step is to reject the direct-outcome shortcut: the paper states, “The task of an in-game win probability model is to predict the probability distribution over the possible match outcomes” given the game state at time t [PAGE 2]. That outcome variable is win-draw-loss, and the paper emphasizes that soccer makes this nonstandard because of “the frequent occurrence of ties and changes in momentum” [PAGE 2]. The second hop is the paper’s key reframing: “we model the number of future goals that a team will score and then map that back to the win-draw-loss probability” [PAGE 3]. This is not a cosmetic change. It introduces a count process, preserves uncertainty over final scorelines, and lets the model represent momentum through the future goal rate rather than through a single binary classifier. The third hop is the Poisson construction: “We model the expected number of goals that the home ... and away ... team will score after time ... as independent Poisson distributions” [PAGE 3]. The fourth hop is the temporal coefficient model: scoring intensity is not fixed but follows a random-walk process, because “At the start of the game, the prior estimated strengths of each team are most informative, while near the end the features that reflect the in-game performance become more important” [PAGE 3]. The fifth hop is the state representation: “we split each game into T = 100 time frames, each corresponding to a percentage of the game. Halftime always corresponds to T = 50” [PAGE 3]. This is a clock normalization trick, not a claim that soccer has exactly 100 minutes. The sixth hop is feature engineering: base features, team-strength features, and contextual features, where “The seven contextual features that we propose are capable of doing this” [PAGE 4]. The seventh hop is inference: the model is trained with variational inference rather than MCMC, using PyMC3 ADVI and batched data [PAGE 4]. The eighth hop is calibration and scoring: the model is evaluated with reliability diagrams, multi-class expected calibration error, and ranked probability score [PAGE 4-5]. The ninth hop is empirical verdict: “Only our Bayesian model has good probability calibration curves” [PAGE 5], and “our proposed Bayesian model achieves an ECE of 0.002” in the final ten percent of games [PAGE 5]. The tenth hop is deployment: stoppage time must be predicted separately, and missing event-stream features force a simplified real-time model [PAGE 6].

The gray-zone audit is where the model’s authority must be bounded. First, calibration is a population property over many game states, not a guarantee for any single state. A 3 percent win probability is not falsified by a comeback; it is only evaluated by aggregating states with similar predicted probabilities. The paper operationalizes this through bins and ECE [PAGE 4], with M = 5, so sparse bins and coarse binning are part of the inference regime. Second, the Poisson model assumes independence between home and away future goals, despite the paper noting “a (relatively low) correlation between the number of goals scored by the two opponents” and that “this has been ignored since it demands more sophisticated techniques” [PAGE 3]. That is a deliberate approximation, not an exact physical law. Third, the model’s clock is a percentage of game, not real elapsed minutes. “Soccer games rarely last precisely 90 minutes” [PAGE 2], so real-time deployment needs an added-time predictor; the paper reports “mean absolute errors of 46.2s in the first half and 59.4s in the second half” [PAGE 6]. Fourth, the model is associational. It does not estimate a structural causal effect of a tactical change, a substitution, or a weather event. The feature set includes game time, score differential, Elo rating differential, team goals, red cards, yellow cards, goal-scoring opportunities, attacking passes, expected threat, and duel strength [PAGE 3-4], but no weather, biomechanical load, or tactical intent variable. Fifth, the paper itself contains a counterintuitive sign: “winning more duels has a negative effect on the win probability, which is not what one would intuitively expect” [PAGE 6]. That is a warning against reading feature weights as causal coaching advice. The gray-zone verdict is therefore: the model is a well-calibrated conditional probability engine for the historical distribution it was trained on, not a universal simulator of soccer causality. Its “well-calibrated” claim must be restricted to the tested leagues, seasons, feature pipeline, and clock treatment, and it must be recalibrated when those layers shift.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core outcome target is the conditional distribution over match outcomes:
\[
P(Y \mid x_t)
\]
where \(Y\) is win, draw, or loss for the home team, and \(x_t\) is the game state at time frame \(t\) [PAGE 2]. The paper’s central operator is a future-goal count model. Let \(T=100\) be the number of normalized game frames, with halftime fixed at \(T=50\) [PAGE 3]. For each team, the future goals after frame \(t\) are modeled as:
\[
y_{>t,\mathrm{home}} \sim \mathrm{Pois}\bigl((T-t)\theta_{t,\mathrm{home}}\bigr),
\]
\[
y_{>t,\mathrm{away}} \sim \mathrm{Pois}\bigl((T-t)\theta_{t,\mathrm{away}}\bigr).
\]
Here \(y\) is a dimensionless goal count, \((T-t)\) is a dimensionless number of remaining normalized frames, and \(\theta\) is a scoring intensity in goals per frame. The paper states these are “independent Poisson distributions” [PAGE 3]. The intensities are linked to features through:
\[
\theta_{t,\mathrm{home}}=\mathrm{invlogit}\bigl(\alpha_t x_{t,\mathrm{home}}+\beta+\mathrm{Ha}\bigr),
\]
\[
\theta_{t,\mathrm{away}}=\mathrm{invlogit}\bigl(\alpha_t x_{t,\mathrm{away}}+\beta\bigr),
\]
with priors:
\[
\alpha_0 \sim N(0,2),\quad
\alpha_t \sim N(\alpha_{t-1},2),\quad
\beta \sim N(0,2),\quad
\mathrm{Ha}\sim N(0,2).
\]
The source says the zero-mean normal priors with variance two correspond to “weak information regarding the true parameter values” [PAGE 3]. Unit note: the linear predictor is on the logit scale and is dimensionless; the \(\alpha_t\) coefficients carry inverse units of their associated features, and \(\beta\) and \(\mathrm{Ha}\) are dimensionless offsets on that scale. Because features are heterogeneous, raw coefficient magnitudes are not comparable across features; sign, posterior uncertainty, and time variation are the interpretable quantities. The final score is the observed current score plus future goals:
\[
S_{\mathrm{home}}=y_{<t,\mathrm{home}}+y_{>t,\mathrm{home}},\quad
S_{\mathrm{away}}=y_{<t,\mathrm{away}}+y_{>t,\mathrm{away}}.
\]
Win, draw, and loss probabilities are obtained by summing the independent Poisson probabilities over the scoreline regions where \(S_{\mathrm{home}}\) is greater than, equal to, or less than \(S_{\mathrm{away}}\). The paper’s evaluation uses expected calibration error:
\[
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{N}\left|\bar y(B_m)-\bar p(B_m)\right|,
\]
where \(N\) is the number of samples, \(B_m\) is the \(m\)-th probability bin, \(|B_m|\) is bin size, and \(\bar p(B_m)\) and \(\bar y(B_m)\) are average predicted probability and observed proportion [PAGE 4]. ECE is dimensionless probability error; the experiments use \(M=5\) [PAGE 4]. The ranked probability score at time \(t\) is:
\[
\mathrm{RPS}_t=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{t,j}-\sum_{j=1}^{i}e_j\right)^2,
\]
where \(p_t=[P(Y=\mathrm{win}\mid x_t),P(Y=\mathrm{draw}\mid x_t),P(Y=\mathrm{loss}\mid x_t)]\), and \(e\) encodes the final outcome as win \([1,1,1]\), draw \([0,1,1]\), or loss \([0,0,1]\) [PAGE 4]. RPS is dimensionless and respects the ordered nature of win-draw-loss. The paper’s Added Goal Value metric is:
\[
\mathrm{AGVp90}_i=
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K_i}\left(3\Delta P(\mathrm{win}\mid x_{t_k})+\Delta P(\mathrm{draw}\mid x_{t_k})\right)}{M_i}\times 90,
\]
where \(K_i\) is goals scored by player \(i\), \(M_i\) is minutes played, and \(t_k\) is the goal time [PAGE 7]. The factor 3 converts win probability into expected league points; the metric is therefore in expected-points-per-90-minutes units. The paper notes that pre-game strength is removed for this analysis: “We remove the pre-game strength from our win probability model for this analysis” [PAGE 7].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes are explicit. At \(t=T\), the Poisson mean \((T-t)\theta\) is zero, so the model degenerates to the current score and the outcome probabilities collapse toward deterministic values; there is no future-goal uncertainty left. The invlogit link prevents negative intensities, but large linear predictors can saturate \(\theta\) near zero or one, creating vanishing gradient and overconfident rates. The random-walk prior allows coefficient variance to grow with time; if the prior variance interpretation is too loose, late-game coefficient estimates can become weakly identified. Calibration bins can be empty or sparse, making ECE unstable even though the formula has no singularity. The independence assumption is a known approximation because the paper ignores the low correlation between the two teams’ goal counts [PAGE 3]. The clock normalization \(T=100\) is not a physical time; it compresses stoppage time, so real-time deployment needs the separate added-time model [PAGE 6]. Finally, missing event-stream features force a simplified model, which the paper says “would slightly reduce the RPS from 0.134 for the offline model to 0.138 for the real-time model” [PAGE 6].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy runs from early baseball win-probability work to soccer’s low-scoring problem. The paper states, “In-game win probability models emerged for Major League Baseball in the 1960s” [PAGE 8]. Baseball’s discrete state space and large historical sample made cumulative historical averages practical. Ice hockey followed with a similarly sparse score-state representation, while American football and basketball moved to regression and random-forest models over richer state descriptions [PAGE 8]. The paper also traces a simulation lineage: Rosenheck for the NFL, Štrumbelj and Vračar for basketball, Buttrey et al. for NHL scoring rates via a Poisson process, and Stern’s Brownian-motion model for score progress [PAGE 8]. The proposed model is placed in the “time-varying coefficient models” class [PAGE 8], which is a statistical lineage rather than a soccer-specific invention. The pre-game strength input comes from Elo ratings, which the paper calls “a highly significant predictor of match outcomes” in soccer [PAGE 5]. The contextual feature lineage includes expected threat, or xT, and the paper cites xT and VAEP as valuing on-ball actions [PAGE 4]. The Added Goal Value metric extends the “clutch” performance idea by weighting goals by their change in win probability [PAGE 7]. This is a retrocausal genealogy in the sense that later metrics such as xT, VAEP, and AGV are used to reinterpret earlier game states: a goal’s value is not just its count but its position in the probability trajectory. The paper explicitly warns that win probability is a “story stat” that gives historical context to in-game situations [PAGE 7], and that fans and commentators otherwise “have to rely on their intuition and limited experience” [PAGE 7]. The retrocausal trap is to treat the final outcome as retroactively validating every earlier probability. A late comeback does not prove the model wrong; calibration is aggregated over states. A second multi-week trap is hindsight weighting in AGV: because goals in tense states get larger \(\Delta P\), scouts may overread “clutch” as a stable player trait rather than a state-dependent contribution. The paper’s removal of pre-game strength for AGV is a guard against dominant teams starting with high win probability and flattening every goal’s value [PAGE 7]. A third trap is model-class transfer: the paper shows logistic regression, multiple logistic regression, and random forest baselines are poorly calibrated in late-game tie states; the random forest “never predicts a high probability of a tie” and has final-ten-percent ECE of 0.101 [PAGE 5]. A fourth trap is distribution drift: rule changes, added-time directives, league changes, provider changes, and roster turnover can shift the mapping from features to scoring intensity, so a model trained on 2011/12 through 2017/18 and tested on 2018/19 [PAGE 4-5] should not be assumed calibrated indefinitely. A fifth trap is feature leakage through rolling windows; the paper specifies previous 10 or 12 frames for several contextual features [PAGE 4], which is a leakage-control detail that must be preserved in any reimplementation.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive layer is not a formal game-theoretic equilibrium in the paper; it is a behavioral reading of how teams, coaches, fans, and analysts update under pressure. “Crisis habit regression” is the tendency of a leading team to fall back on habitual late-game behavior: deeper defending, more clearances, more duels, more fouls, and more time management. The model can see some consequences through yellow cards, red cards, duel strength, attacking passes, goal-scoring opportunities, and xT [PAGE 3-4]. But the paper’s caution is important: “winning more duels has a negative effect on the win probability, which is not what one would intuitively expect” [PAGE 6]. That sign means a naive crisis narrative— “we are winning duels, so we are in control” —can be a counter-trap. A team may win many duels in harmless areas while the opponent protects central zones; xT is described as “more fine-grained” and “probably better at capturing a team’s offensive intentions during the last part of a game” [PAGE 6]. The counter-trap is recursive: the trailing team anticipates that the leader will sit deep, so it increases circulation and crosses; the leader anticipates the crosses and defends the box; the model sees the resulting pass, xT, and opportunity traces but not the intentions behind them. The clock is the third layer. The paper models scoring intensity as a temporal stochastic process because feature importance is non-linear over time [PAGE 3]. It reports that “the effect of yellows increases as the game progresses” [PAGE 6], and that around the 80th frame, “The importance of the xT feature increases, while the other two features lose importance” [PAGE 6]. That is a clock-sensitive shift in the model’s attention, not a claim that xT causes goals. The recursive theory-of-mind verdict is therefore: crisis habit regression is observable only through its behavioral residue; counter-traps can invert the intuitive meaning of duel and possession signals; and the clock changes which residue is informative. A coach who uses the model should not ask “does this player win duels?” but “does this state, at this frame, with these cards and this xT profile, change the future goal rate?” The model does not simulate the opponent’s counterfactual adjustment. It only conditions on the observed state. That limitation is precisely why the paper’s calibration focus matters: the model can be a good probability tracker without being a causal theory of tactical deception.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto is a proof of scope: the model’s probability is licensed only for the layers represented in its features and training distribution. The feature list is game time, score differential, Elo rating differential, team goals, red-card difference, opponent yellow cards, goal-scoring opportunities per frame, rolling attacking passes, rolling xT, and rolling duel strength [PAGE 3-4]. Weather, pitch condition, temperature, altitude, travel, biomechanical load, injury severity, sleep, and tactical instruction are not in that list. The paper’s real-time stoppage-time model uses “the time lost due to injuries and time-wasting” [PAGE 9], but that is a clock-estimation layer, not a biomechanical or weather layer. Therefore, if a physical layer changes the mapping from the observed features to future goals, the model has no internal mechanism to correct it. Formally, the paper’s calibration claim is an empirical statement about the conditional distribution \(P(Y\mid x_t)\) in its training and test leagues. It is not a structural claim that \(x_t\) d-separates outcome from weather or fatigue. If an external variable \(Z\) changes the goal-rate process, then the deployed conditional \(P(Y\mid x_t,Z)\) can differ from the learned \(P(Y\mid x_t)\), and the model’s ECE can degrade even if its internal equations are unchanged. The veto proof is therefore: no weather or biomechanical variable appears in the ten-feature state description [PAGE 3-4]; no such variable appears in the calibration evaluation [PAGE 4-5]; and the paper’s own feature-importance analysis warns that feature signs are not intuitive causal effects, as shown by the negative duel-strength effect [PAGE 6]. Consequence: in extreme heat, heavy rain, poor pitch conditions, or a suspected injury-driven mobility loss, the model’s win probability should be flagged as uncalibrated for that layer. The correct adjudication is not to “adjust by feel” inside the Poisson model, but to treat the output as a baseline conditional probability and require recalibration or additional covariates. The same veto applies to biomechanical red-card risk: the model includes red cards ex post, but not the movement quality or fatigue state that predicts them. Weather can also confound xT and passing features: if rain reduces pass completion and changes duel frequency, the model may attribute the resulting state shift to its learned xT or duel weights, which are associational. The physical-layer veto is thus not a refutation of the paper; it is a boundary condition on its use.
