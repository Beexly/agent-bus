Crisis habit regression: under cue conflict—convex lenses or atropine—the observer regresses to the dominant habitual mapping. Normally “we accommodate our eyes for an object as it approaches us” and “the object throws a larger retinal image” [PAGE 93], so the habit is A-up/\theta-up ⇒ approach. When atropine weakens accommodation, the same voluntary strain is emitted “as if it were a great deal nearer,” producing micropsy because the retinal image fails to confirm the approach prediction [PAGE 93]. The crisis habit is to trust the efferent strain signal over the weakened accommodative optics, thereby shrinking the inferred object.

Counter-trap: an informed observer who knows convex lenses relax accommodation while enlarging the retinal image may overcorrect, deliberately judging the object as approaching rather than receding, or may suppress the illusion entirely by attending to the constant physical size. This is a second-order reversal: instead of the naive “receding and grew” percept [PAGE 93], the counter-trap subject imposes a model-based correction that can overshoot and produce macropsis or distance underestimation. The trap within the trap is that explicit knowledge does not eliminate the low-level oculomotor signal; it only changes the decision criterion, so reaction-time and confidence measures dissociate from verbal reports.

Clock: the dynamics run on multiple time scales. Accommodative relaxation occurs in seconds; the perceptual “vacillation as to the recession” [PAGE 93] unfolds over seconds to minutes as the posterior samples modes. Atropine’s cycloplegic effect persists for hours to days depending on dose, so micropsy is not a momentary artifact but a pharmacologically extended regime. Adaptation to convex lenses or to reversed size-distance relations requires days to weeks of exposure, consistent with slow prior updating in g(A). The moon illusion recurs nightly, tied to circadian horizon exposure rather than to a one-time recalibration. Any model that treats the accommodation cue as a fixed clock-independent gain is falsified: the effective weight w_A in the Bayesian fusion is itself a function of elapsed exposure t, recent cue history, and pharmacological state.
