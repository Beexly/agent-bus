Moss ("Consensus of opinion of coaches and critics, compiled by E. B. Moss of the Associated Press, New York" [PAGE 21]), Edward N. Robinson, William M. Hollenback, P. W. London, and George W. Angell [PAGE 21]. Define the Western set $S_W = \{s_{11},s_{12}\}$ for Leonard Adams and Sidney Casner [PAGE 21]. Let the position set be $\mathcal{P} = \{E,T,G,C,\mathrm{QB},\mathrm{HB},\mathrm{FB}\}$ with slot multiplicities $n_E=2, n_T=2, n_G=2, n_C=1, n_{\mathrm{QB}}=1, n_{\mathrm{HB}}=2, n_{\mathrm{FB}}=1$, so each selector nominates 11 players.

Selection indicator:
$$x_{s,p,i} \in \{0,1\}, \qquad s \in S, \; p \in \mathcal{P}, \; i \in \mathcal{I}_p$$
where $\mathcal{I}_p$ is the set of distinct player-school referents resolved after OCR entity matching.

Consensus count:
$$c_{p,i} = \sum_{s \in S} x_{s,p,i}, \qquad C_p = |S| \, n_p$$
with $C_p$ the total possible selections at position $p$. For Eastern ends, $C_E = 10 \times 2 = 20$ slots [PAGE 21].

- Fullback: LeGure/LeGere/LeGore (Yale) $c=5$; Whitney (Dartmouth) $c=3$; Mahan (Harvard) $c=1$.

Selection probability simplex at position $p$:
$$\pi_{p,i} = \frac{c_{p,i}}{C_p}, \qquad \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_p} \pi_{p,i} = 1$$
Units: $\pi$ is dimensionless; $c$ and $C$ are counts (dimensionless).

Shannon entropy of the selection distribution at position $p$:
$$H_p = -\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}_p} \pi_{p,i} \log_2 \pi_{p,i} \quad \text{bits}$$
For Eastern ends using the tallies above:
$$\pi = \{0.50, 0.20, 0.10, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05\}$$
$$H_E \approx -\bigl[0.5\log_2 0.5 + 0.2\log_2 0.2 + 0.1\log_2 0.1 + 4(0.05\log_2 0.05)\bigr] \approx 2.16 \text{ bits}$$
Maximum entropy for $N_E=7$ distinct ends:
$$H_{E,\max} = \log_2 7 \approx 2.81 \text{ bits}$$

Consensus concentration index:
$$\gamma_p = 1 - \frac{H_p}{H_{p,\max}}, \qquad \gamma_p \in [0,1] \text{ dimensionless}$$
For Eastern ends, $\gamma_E \approx 1 - 2.16/2.81 \approx 0.23$. A value near 1 indicates near-unanimity (as with Hardwick and Pennock); a value near 0 indicates a flat, disputed field.

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
1. $C_p = 0$ (no selectors covering a position) makes $\pi_{p,i}$ undefined.
2. OCR under-merge: distinct players collapsed into one string artificially depresses $N_p$ and $H_p$, inflating $\gamma_p$.
3. OCR over-merge: one player split into multiple strings (e.g., "Ballin" vs "Bailin" [PAGE 21]; "LeGure" vs "LeGore" vs "LeGere" [PAGE 21]) artificially inflates $N_p$ and $H_p$, deflating $\gamma_p$ and understating true consensus.
4. Positional-slot mismatch: assigning Mahan's fullback vote [PAGE 21] to halfback corrupts $\pi_{\mathrm{FB}}$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{HB}}$ simultaneously.

All equations are information-theoretic; no physical force, fatigue, or athletic-capacity ratios are invented or implied.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Historical and scientific lineage plus multi-week traps.

The 1915 All-America apparatus descends directly from Walter Camp's collegiate selections beginning in 1889, but by 1915 Camp's monopoly had fractured into a plurality of critics: newspapers (St. Louis Republic, New York Sun, New York Tribune, Chicago Daily Journal, Illinois Magazine/Daily Illini), alumni organs (Princeton Alumni Weekly), the Associated Press (E. B. Moss consensus [PAGE 21]), and coaching-staff selectors (Brown's Robinson, Penn State's Hollenback, Dartmouth's London, Lafayette's Crowell) [PAGE 21]. This pluralism is the genealogical root of modern consensus All-America teams and, later, the AP Poll (1936) and Coaches Poll. The 1915 page captures the transitional moment when football was shedding its rugby-scrum origins: the forward pass had been legalized in 1906, mass-momentum formations were restricted, and positional specialization (quarterback, halfback, fullback, ends, tackles, guards, center) was already codified [PAGE 21].

Retrocausally, the modern reader sees these lists as a fossilized reputation network. Harvard, Yale, Princeton, and Penn dominate the Eastern slots [PAGE 21], reflecting the "Big Four" social structure of Eastern football rather than a measured performance order. Army, Brown, Dartmouth, Cornell, and Washington & Jefferson appear as secondary nodes; Western powers (Chicago, Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Illinois, Nebraska, Purdue) are segregated into a separate All-Western universe [PAGE 21] with zero cross-listing against Eastern consensus names.

The multi-week trap is structural. These selections were published after the 1915 season concluded, but each selector's mental model was accumulated week by week. A player who starred in October benefited from recency and salience in December voting even if injured or ineffective in November. Because the source contains no weekly game log, no injury report, and no statistical line [PAGE 21], the consensus cannot be decomposed into early-signal versus late-regression components. A modern analogue trap is the "preseason prestige hangover," where a name selected in week 1 retains halo weight through week 8 despite mean reversion in on-field output. Here the hangover is institutional: Hardwick and Pennock are unanimous [PAGE 21], but unanimity measures name stability, not athletic dominance. The trap is fatal for any model that treats 1915 consensus as ground-truth player quality; it is ground-truth selector agreement only.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock dynamics.

Selector behavior is recursive: each critic chooses players while modeling the choices of other critics. The most explicit second-order operator is Moss's "Consensus of opinion of coaches and critics, compiled by E. B. Moss of the Associated Press, New York" [PAGE 21], which is literally a selection of selections—a meta-selector aggregating first-order selections. This creates a reflexive loop: a critic may vote for a player not because of independent evaluation but because they anticipate other critics will vote for that player, thereby preserving consensus coherence.

Formally, let selector $s$ assign utility to player $i$ at position $p$ as
$$U_{s,p}(i) = \theta \, m_{p,i} + (1-\theta) \, r_{p,i}(t) + \epsilon_{s,p,i}$$
where $m_{p,i}$ is latent athletic merit, $r_{p,i}(t)$ is time-varying reputation, $t$ indexes weeks of the 1915 season, $\theta \in [0,1]$ is the merit-weighting coefficient, and $\epsilon$ is idiosyncratic noise. Under information abundance ($\theta \to 1$), selections track merit. Under crisis or scarcity ($\theta \to 0$), selectors regress to reputation: famous schools (Harvard, Yale, Princeton, Penn) and previously famous names capture votes [PAGE 21].

Crisis habit regression is visible in the Hardwick/Pennock unanimity [PAGE 21]: with no statistics or film, the safe vote is the Harvard name. The counter-trap is deliberate contrarianism. George W. Angell, Tufts captain, selects Tilfer (Dartmouth) and Hardwick at end, O'Donnell (Tufts) at tackle, Dole (Massachusetts Aggies) at center, and Wescott (Tufts) at halfback [PAGE 21]—injecting low-consensus nodes to break the reputation cascade. Wilmer G. Crowell selects Cruikshank (Washington and Jefferson) at center [PAGE 21], another anti-consensus node. These contrarian votes raise entropy $H_p$ and lower $\gamma_p$, preventing total lock-in.

Clock dynamics: the 1915 season is finite. Selectors publish once, after the clock expires, so there is no iterative updating visible in the source [PAGE 21]. In a live season, the clock would force a terminal consensus at season's end; here the clock has already struck, and the page is a snapshot of a collapsed wave function of weekly reputation updates. Any recursive model must therefore treat the listed consensus as path-dependent on an unobserved weekly sequence, not as a simultaneous equilibrium.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The source contains exclusively nominal selection data: names, schools, positions, and selector identities [PAGE 21]. It contains zero anthropometric variables (height, mass), zero biomechanical variables (blocking leverage, tackle force, sprint time), zero environmental variables (temperature, wind, precipitation, field state), and zero play-level variables (down, distance, yardage, outcome). Consequently, the physical layer is vetoed.

Formal veto statement: Let $\Phi_i$ be the physical state vector of player $i$, containing mass $m_i$ (kg), stature $h_i$ (m), and any derived kinematic quantities. The source provides $\Phi_i = \varnothing$ for all $i$. Any mapping from consensus count $c_{p,i}$ to an on-field physical quantity—such as momentum $p = m v$ (kg·m/s), impulse $J = \int F \, dt$ (N·s), or expected points added—is undefined:
$$\nexists \; f: c_{p,i} \mapsto \mathbb{R}^+ \quad \text{without external physical layers}$$
Units that would be required (meters, kilograms, seconds, newtons, joules) are absent. Biomechanically, we cannot compute line-of-scrimmage collision forces or open-field tackling efficiency. Meteorologically, no weather layer exists for any 1915 contest, so wind, temperature, or precipitation modifiers cannot be conditioned.

The veto is absolute within this document: consensus is a sociological information signal measured in selector-counts and bits, not a biomechanical measurement. Cross-layer adjudication therefore rules out any inference that a high-consensus player (e.g., Hardwick, $c=10$ [PAGE 21]) possesses superior mass-specific force production or weather-adjusted performance. The only defensible layer is the informational one: $c_{p,i}$ and $H_p$ quantify inter-selector agreement, not athletic causality.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
