<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a five-page 2014 IJITCS paper describing a real-time object-tracking system built around an FPGA development kit. The reasoning chain is: composite camera video enters the FPGA board; a TV decoder converts analog video into raw digital video; the design forms YCbCr components and then YCbCr-to-RGB for VGA display; a target-selection box is positioned with a joystick and locked with a push button; the selected object is represented as white while other color intensities are treated as noise; the FPGA emits a three-state horizontal decision through GP-I/Os; and an opto-coupled current driver plus H-bridge relay drives a DC gear motor that rotates the camera toward the target. The paper explicitly states: “The main idea of this work is object tracking using real time video processing” [PAGE 1], and “We also interfaced the DC gear motor to control the movement of the camera in order to track the selected object” [PAGE 1]. The implementation chain is summarized on [PAGE 2]: “The first part is to process the video signal using FPGA development kit. The second part is to select the target using joystick connected with the FPGA. The third part is the tracking of the target. Finally, the last part is to control the movement of camera using DC gear motor” [PAGE 2].

The central algorithmic move is not a full appearance model, Kalman filter, correlation tracker, or optical-flow estimator. It is a color-segmentation and centroid/region-decision scheme. The paper says: “When the object tracking mode is started, then the selected object is treated as white color on the screen. The FPGA is programmed in such way that it considers noise to all other areas which has other color intensities with the target selection box” [PAGE 3]. The control output is a ternary bang-bang law: “If the object is at centre on the display screen than FPGA development kit gives 00 output through its general purpose input and outputs (GP-I/Os) and If the object is at right border on the display screen than FPGA development kit gives 01 as output and if the object is at left border on the display screen than FPGA development kit gives 10 output through its GP-I/Os” [PAGE 3]. That is a quantized horizontal error controller, not a continuous servo controller. The paper’s own actuator description is: “If object moves left side than motor moves anti-clock wise until the object position comes in centre and if object moves right side than motor moves clock wise until the object position comes in centre” [PAGE 3].

The multi-hop derivation must separate what is formally specified from what is merely asserted. Formally specified: the pipeline order, the ternary output code, the opto-coupler isolation, the H-bridge relay direction mapping, and the use of a target-selection box. Asserted but not quantified: “The FPGA based designed model is much faster, stout and modular approach” [PAGE 1]; “The efficiency of tracking of camera is much higher when the object contains uniform texture” [PAGE 4]; “The efficiency of tracking less shiny objects is much higher than of shiny surfaces” [PAGE 4]; “It is found that the error in tracking is directly proportional to the speed of the object to be tracked” [PAGE 4]; and “The error in tracking also increases with the decrease in the frame rate at constant speed of the object” [PAGE 4]. The paper gives no numerical frame rate, resolution, clock frequency, color threshold, target-box size, motor voltage, gear ratio, camera focal length, latency, or sample size. It also gives no equation for “efficiency,” no definition of “proportion error,” and no statistical distribution for tracking error. Therefore, any quantitative operator must be presented as a formal reconstruction under stated assumptions, not as a measured law from the source.

A gray-zone audit reveals several points where the paper’s language outruns its evidence. First, “real time” is not defined by a deadline in seconds; the paper does not report end-to-end latency from photon arrival to motor command. Second, the paper says the selected object is “treated as white” and other areas are “noise,” but color thresholding is illumination-sensitive; the same page later reports that “intense lighting or extreme darkness can affect the working of the camera in order to take video signal” [PAGE 4]. Third, the camera is moved by a DC gear motor, but the paper does not specify whether the mount is single-axis or pan-tilt; the decision logic is horizontal, and vertical error is not addressed. Fourth, the output code “00” at center implies a deadband, but no deadband width is given. Fifth, the relay/H-bridge actuation is discontinuous; a bang-bang controller with a relay can limit-cycle unless the deadband is larger than the minimum camera step. Sixth, the target-selection box is manually placed with a joystick, so the system’s initial condition is human-dependent and not autonomous. Seventh, the paper’s “error” claim is directional, not a calibrated transfer function: “directly proportional” still requires a proportionality constant with units, a valid range, and a breakdown point. Eighth, “efficiency” is not defined as probability of detection, RMS centroid error, lock-loss rate, or motor settling time. These gaps are not minor; they determine whether the system is a demonstrator or a verifiable tracker.

The strongest formal interpretation is therefore a sampled-data, thresholded, relay-controlled visual servoing loop. The target is a color-defined blob inside a selected region; the measured horizontal coordinate is a centroid or region statistic; the controller quantizes the horizontal error into left, center, or right; and the actuator rotates the camera until the blob returns to the center deadband. The loop can work in a narrow regime: matte target, stable illumination, moderate speed, adequate frame rate, and a target whose color is separable from background. It can fail in predictable regimes: empty segmentation mask, similar-color background, specular highlights, motion blur, frame aliasing, relay deadband, insufficient motor torque, and operator misselection. Those failure regimes are the correct place to apply vetoes, because no amount of FPGA parallelism repairs a target whose contrast is below the segmentation threshold or whose per-frame displacement exceeds the sampling geometry.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s pipeline can be represented as a sampled visual servoing system. Let the image at frame index \(k\) be \(I_k(u,v)\), where \(u\in\{1,\dots,W\}\) and \(v\in\{1,\dots,H\}\) are pixel coordinates; \(W,H\) are dimensionless pixel counts. The frame period is \(T_f\) seconds and the frame rate is \(f_s=1/T_f\) Hz. The paper does not report \(W,H,T_f,\) or \(f_s\), so these are formal variables only.

1. Sensing and color transform. The source says: “The FPGA development kit has TV decoder which converts the analog format into raw digital format after that it is fed to the FPGA development kit” [PAGE 2]. It also says: “An YcbCr to RGB (Red-Green-Blue) module is also introduced to change the pixels consequently to show it on VGA” [PAGE 2]. A generic transform is
\[
\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{RGB}}_k(u,v)=M_{\mathrm{YCbCr}\to\mathrm{RGB}}\,\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{YCbCr}}_k(u,v)+\mathbf{b},
\]
where \(M\) is a \(3\times 3\) matrix of dimensionless coefficients and \(\mathbf{b}\) is an offset vector in digital code values. The paper does not give the coefficients, so no numerical conversion is claimed.

2. Target selection gate. The operator moves a box with a joystick. The paper says: “The joystick consists of four switches, which can move the selection box in order to capture the target” [PAGE 2]. Let the selected box be
\[
B_k=[u_{1,k},u_{2,k}]\times[v_{1,k},v_{2,k}]\subset\{1,\dots,W\}\times\{1,\dots,H\}.
\]
A target reference color vector \(\mathbf{r}\) is captured from the selected region. The segmentation mask is modeled as
\[
M_k(u,v)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\|\mathbf{I}^{\mathrm{RGB}}_k(u,v)-\mathbf{r}\|_2\le \tau\right]\mathbf{1}\!\left[(u,v)\in B_k\right],
\]
where \(\tau\) is a color-distance threshold in digital code values and \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is the indicator function. This formalizes the paper’s statement: “the selected object is treated as white color on the screen. The FPGA is programmed in such way that it considers noise to all other areas which has other color intensities with the target selection box” [PAGE 3]. The exact threshold \(\tau\) is not reported.

3. Centroid and horizontal error. Define the mask area
\[
A_k=\sum_{u=1}^{W}\sum_{v=1}^{H}M_k(u,v),
\]
which is dimensionless and satisfies \(0\le A_k\le WH\). The centroid is
\[
\mathbf{c}_k=
\frac{1}{A_k}
\sum_{u=1}^{W}\sum_{v=1}^{H}
(u,v)\,M_k(u,v),
\]
provided \(A_k>0\). Let the display center be \((u_c,v_c)\). The horizontal error is
\[
e_{x,k}=c_{x,k}-u_c
\]
in pixels. The paper’s ternary output can be written as
\[
q_k=
\begin{cases}
00, & |e_{x,k}|\le \delta,\\
01, & e_{x,k}>\delta,\\
10, & e_{x,k}<-\delta,
\end{cases}
\]
where \(\delta\ge 0\) is the center deadband in pixels. The sign convention matches the paper’s statement that right border gives \(01\) and left border gives \(10\) [PAGE 3]. The paper does not state \(\delta\), does not define \(u_c\), and does not specify a vertical error channel.

4. Camera geometry. For a pinhole camera with focal length \(f_p\) in pixels and target bearing \(\phi_k\) relative to the optical axis, the image coordinate of a target at camera angle \(\theta_k\) satisfies, under a small-angle or rectilinear projection,
\[
c_{x,k}\approx f_p\tan(\phi_k-\theta_k)+u_c.
\]
Here \(f_p\) has units of pixels, \(\phi_k,\theta_k\) have units of radians, and \(c_{x,k}\) has units of pixels. This equation exposes a geometric singularity at \(|\phi_k-\theta_k|\to \pi/2\), where \(\tan(\cdot)\) diverges. A real camera has a finite field of view, so the practical breakdown is earlier: the target leaves the field of view.

5. Actuator. The paper says: “The camera is attached through the DC gear motor” [PAGE 3] and “The motor is connected to H-bridge Relay” [PAGE 3]. A standard DC-motor model is
\[
J\ddot{\theta}+b\dot{\theta}=K_t i-\tau_d,\qquad
L\dot{i}+R i+K_e\dot{\theta}=V(q_k),
\]
where \(J\) is rotor-plus-load inertia in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), \(b\) is viscous damping in \(\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}\), \(K_t\) is torque constant in \(\mathrm{N\,m/A}\), \(i\) is current in \(\mathrm{A}\), \(\tau_d\) is disturbance torque in \(\mathrm{N\,m}\), \(L\) is inductance in \(\mathrm{H}\), \(R\) is resistance in \(\Omega\), \(K_e\) is back-EMF constant in \(\mathrm{V\,s/rad}\), and \(V(q_k)\) is motor voltage in \(\mathrm{V}\). The relay mapping is assumed to be \(V(01)=+V_s\), \(V(10)=-V_s\), and \(V(00)=0\) or brake, with \(V_s>0\) in volts. The source does not report \(J,b,K_t,L,R,K_e,V_s\), gear ratio, or torque limits.

6. Temporal sampling and aliasing. If the target bearing changes at angular velocity \(\Omega_{\mathrm{obj}}\) in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\), the per-frame angular change is
\[
\Delta\phi_k\approx \Omega_{\mathrm{obj}}T_f.
\]
For a camera field of view \(\Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}\) in radians, unambiguous frame-to-frame association requires roughly
\[
|\Omega_{\mathrm{obj}}|T_f<\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}}{2}.
\]
If this condition fails, the target can move more than half the field between frames, so frame-to-frame association becomes ambiguous. This is a formal breakdown condition, not a measured claim from the paper. It is consistent with the paper’s qualitative statement: “The error in tracking also increases with the decrease in the frame rate at constant speed of the object” [PAGE 4].

7. Empirical error statement. The paper states: “It is found that the error in tracking is directly proportional to the speed of the object to be tracked” [PAGE 4]. A formal local linearization is
\[
e_{\mathrm{rms}}=\kappa\,|v_{\mathrm{obj}}|+e_0,
\]
where \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) is root-mean-square centroid error in pixels, \(v_{\mathrm{obj}}\) is object speed in \(\mathrm{px/s}\) or \(\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(\kappa\) has units of seconds for angular speed or seconds for pixel speed, and \(e_0\) is an offset in pixels. The paper does not report \(\kappa\), \(e_0\), or the valid range. This equation is a reconstruction of the paper’s qualitative claim, not a measured calibration.

8. Information and probability metrics. Let \(S_k\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether the true target is present in the selected region, and let \(M_k\) be the segmentation decision. Define detection probability and false-alarm probability as
\[
p_d=P(M_k=1\mid S_k=1),\qquad
p_{fa}=P(M_k=1\mid S_k=0),
\]
both dimensionless. The mutual information between the mask decision and target presence is
\[
I(M_k;S_k)=H(S_k)-H(S_k\mid M_k)
\]
in bits, where \(H\) is Shannon entropy. A useful tracker requires both high \(p_d\) and low \(p_{fa}\); the paper reports neither. The paper’s “efficiency” statements are therefore not information-theoretic quantities.

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
- \(A_k=0\): centroid undefined because the denominator in \(\mathbf{c}_k\) is zero. This is the empty-mask singularity.
- \(A_k\) small: under an independent-pixel noise model, the centroid variance can be approximated by \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat c_{x,k})\approx \sigma_n^2/A_k\), with \(\sigma_n^2\) in \(\mathrm{px^2}\) and \(A_k\) dimensionless. When the centroid standard deviation exceeds \(\delta\), the ternary decision becomes noise-dominated.
- Color-overlap regime: if the target and background color distributions overlap so that \(\|\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{r}\|_2\le\tau\) is satisfied by background pixels, \(p_{fa}\) rises and the white-blob representation no longer identifies the selected object.
- Illumination saturation: if pixel values clip at the sensor minimum or maximum, color distance is compressed; the paper notes that “intense lighting or extreme darkness can affect the working of the camera in order to take video signal” [PAGE 4]. The contrast metric \(C=(L_{\mathrm{obj}}-L_{\mathrm{bg}})/(L_{\mathrm{obj}}+L_{\mathrm{bg}})\), dimensionless, can fall below the segmentation threshold.
- Temporal aliasing: \(|\Omega_{\mathrm{obj}}|T_f\ge \Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}/2\) causes frame-to-frame ambiguity.
- Relay limit cycle: if the minimum camera angular step per control period exceeds the angular equivalent of the deadband, \(2\delta\,\Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}/W\), the camera cannot settle inside the center deadband and will oscillate.
- Torque saturation: if the required torque exceeds the motor-plus-gearbox limit, no control law can maintain the commanded camera motion. The paper does not report this limit.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is visible in its own citations and examples. It places itself after “quite a few decades” of computer-vision tracking [PAGE 1], names the “Hawk-eye tennis system” as “a common example of real time object tracking” [PAGE 1], and lists applications including “direction finding of rovers on Mars, tracking missiles for military purpose and controlling fighter jets, motion based recognition, traffic monitoring, human computer interaction, automated surveillance” [PAGE 1]. The reference list on [PAGE 5] traces a 2002–2010 FPGA-vision line: Hamamoto, Nagao, and Aizawa on “Real-time objects tracking by using smart image sensor and FPGA” [PAGE 5]; Darabiha, Rose, and Maclean on “Video-rate stereo depth measurement on programmable hardware” [PAGE 5]; Leeser, Miller, and Yu on a “Smart camera based on reconfigurable hardware” [PAGE 5]; Leephokhanon and Wiangtong on “Object Tracking and Motion Capturing in Hardware-Accelerated Multi-camera System” [PAGE 5]; and Norouznezhad et al. on “Object Tracking on FPGA-based Smart Cameras using Local Oriented Energy and Phase Features” [PAGE 5]. The 2014 paper sits at the simpler end of that lineage: color segmentation, manual target selection, ternary horizontal control, and a single DC gear motor.

Retrocausally, the later constraints of embedded vision explain why this paper’s design looks the way it does. The 2002–2010 literature already contains smart-camera architectures, stereo, multi-camera tracking, and local oriented energy features; the 2014 paper chooses a much smaller operator set because it targets a development kit with a TV decoder, VGA output, GP-I/Os, a joystick, and a relay driver. The paper’s claim that “The FPGA based designed model is much faster, stout and modular approach” [PAGE 1] is a lineage claim about hardware parallelism, but the paper does not report resource utilization, clock frequency, memory bandwidth, pipeline depth, power, or latency. Thus the claim is plausible in the narrow sense that FPGA logic can parallelize pixel operations, but it is not demonstrated as a comparative result against a computer or DSP.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. First, the Simulink-to-VHDL translation trap: the paper says “First we tested our algorithm using Simulink based model and then translated it into VHDL language which is used to program FPGA” [PAGE 1]. Translation introduces fixed-point quantization, pipeline delays, clock-domain crossing, and resource pressure; none are quantified. Second, the video-timing trap: TV decoder synchronization, YCbCr conversion, VGA timing, and frame buffering can dominate integration time even when the tracking algorithm is simple. Third, the color-threshold trap: a threshold tuned in one illumination regime drifts when lighting changes; the paper itself warns that “intense lighting or extreme darkness can affect the working of the camera” [PAGE 4]. Fourth, the actuator trap: an opto-coupler and H-bridge relay introduce delay, deadband, and switching transients; the paper mentions the opto-coupler “to protect the GP-I/Os of FPGA kit from the high current that may come from current driver” [PAGE 3], but does not characterize the delay. Fifth, the single-axis trap: the control law is horizontal, while a real pan-tilt tracker needs two axes and coupled kinematics. Sixth, the evaluation trap: “The system is developed and calibrated by performing many trial experiments under different circumstances” [PAGE 4] is not a benchmark protocol. A multi-week integration effort can produce a working demo while leaving these variables uncontrolled, which is exactly the gray zone this paper occupies.

The historical lesson is that simple color-blob tracking on FPGA is a legitimate embedded-vision primitive, but it is not equivalent to robust object tracking. The paper’s reference to background subtraction, chromatic information, multiple cameras, and local oriented energy [PAGE 1] shows that the field already had richer representations. The 2014 contribution is therefore best read as a hardware integration demonstration: camera input, target selection, color segmentation, ternary decision, opto-isolated relay drive, and motorized camera feedback. Its retrocausal significance is that it shows where later systems must add value: calibrated color models, illumination normalization, temporal filtering, two-axis control, latency measurement, and quantitative tracking metrics.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system has a simple internal “theory of the target”: it believes that the selected object is the white blob and that other color intensities are noise. The paper states: “the selected object is treated as white color on the screen” and “it considers noise to all other areas which has other color intensities with the target selection box” [PAGE 3]. The human operator has a reciprocal theory: the operator believes the white blob on the display is the object that was selected with the joystick and push button. That mutual model is fragile because the system’s belief is color-indexed, while the human’s belief is object-indexed. If a similar-color distractor enters the box, the system can update its centroid toward the distractor while the human still believes the display represents the originally selected target. This is a recursive theory-of-mind failure: the machine’s observation model and the human’s interpretation model diverge without either side representing the other’s uncertainty.

Formally, let \(S_k\) be the hidden target state and \(o_k\) the display observation. A belief update has the standard form
\[
b_{k+1}(s')\propto O(o_k\mid s')\sum_{s}T(s'\mid s,a_k)b_k(s),
\]
where \(b_k(s)\) is a probability mass function, \(T\) is the transition kernel, \(O\) is the observation likelihood, and \(a_k\in\{00,01,10\}\) is the command. The entropy
\[
H(S_k\mid o_k)=-\sum_s b_k(s)\log_2 b_k(s)
\]
is measured in bits. When \(H(S_k\mid o_k)\) is low, the system is confident; when it is high, the white-blob observation is ambiguous. The paper provides no such belief state, so the implemented system is closer to a hard decision than a probabilistic tracker.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the object accelerates, lighting changes, or the target leaves the box. Under those conditions, the system regresses to its fixed habit: threshold the color, compute the blob, and emit \(00/01/10\). The paper’s ternary rule is exactly that habit: center gives \(00\), right gives \(01\), left gives \(10\) [PAGE 3]. The counter-trap is that the human operator may try to recover by enlarging the target box or loosening the color threshold. That can increase \(p_d\), but it also increases \(p_{fa}\); if the mutual information \(I(M_k;S_k)\) falls, the display becomes more active but less informative. A second counter-trap is over-trusting the center code: \(00\) means “inside the deadband,” not “correct object.” If the blob is a similar-color background region, the system can report \(00\) with high confidence and zero correctness.

The clock trap is the mismatch among frame period \(T_f\), control period \(T_c\), motor settling time \(T_{\mathrm{settle}}\), display latency, and human reaction time \(T_r\). If \(T_c>T_{\mathrm{settle}}\), the relay can overshoot and limit-cycle. If \(T_r+T_{\mathrm{display}}+T_{\mathrm{motor}}>T_{\mathrm{obj}}\), where \(T_{\mathrm{obj}}\) is the time the object remains in the field, manual reacquisition is impossible. The paper’s warning that “the error in tracking also increases with the decrease in the frame rate at constant speed of the object” [PAGE 4] is a clock-level statement: slower sampling reduces the information available to the controller. A recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore asks three questions: does the machine’s white-blob belief correspond to the human’s selected object; does the human’s center-code interpretation correspond to actual target centering; and does the control clock leave enough time for the actuator and operator to correct errors? The paper’s design answers the first only under color separability, the second only inside the deadband, and the third not at all.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer vetoes are necessary failure conditions that override algorithm-level claims. The physical layer includes camera geometry, motor torque, gearbox backlash, relay switching, opto-coupler delay, mount stiffness, and wind or vibration disturbance. The biomechanical layer includes the human operator’s joystick selection, visual fatigue, reaction time, and ability to interpret the display. The weather layer includes illumination, rain, fog, dust, and wind. The paper supplies enough information to identify these layers but not enough to quantify them. It says the camera is attached through a DC gear motor [PAGE 3], that the FPGA is interfaced with a current driver through an opto-coupler [PAGE 3], and that “intense lighting or extreme darkness can affect the working of the camera” [PAGE 4].

Physical veto proof. Let the required camera-axis torque be
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{req}}=J\ddot{\theta}+b\dot{\theta}+\tau_g+\tau_{\mathrm{wind}},
\]
where \(J\) is inertia in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), \(\ddot{\theta}\) is angular acceleration in \(\mathrm{rad/s^2}\), \(b\) is damping in \(\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}\), \(\dot{\theta}\) is angular velocity in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\), \(\tau_g\) is gravity/backlash torque in \(\mathrm{N\,m}\), and \(\tau_{\mathrm{wind}}\) is disturbance torque in \(\mathrm{N\,m}\). If the motor-plus-gearbox maximum torque satisfies
\[
\tau_{\max}<\tau_{\mathrm{req}},
\]
then no tracking algorithm can achieve the commanded camera trajectory. The camera will lag, saturate, or stall. The paper does not report \(\tau_{\max}\), gear ratio, or load inertia, so this veto cannot be evaluated from the source. A second physical veto is relay quantization: if the minimum camera step per relay actuation exceeds the angular deadband corresponding to \(\delta\) pixels, the system cannot settle. In angular units the deadband is approximately
\[
\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{db}}\approx \delta\,\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}}{W},
\]
with \(\delta\) in pixels, \(\Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}\) in radians, and \(W\) in pixels. If the minimum step is larger than \(2\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{db}}\), a limit cycle is physically unavoidable.

Biomechanical veto proof. The operator must place the target box with a joystick and lock it with a push button [PAGE 2]. Let the operator’s reaction time be \(T_r\) in seconds, display latency \(T_d\) in seconds, and motor settling time \(T_m\) in seconds. If
\[
T_r+T_d+T_m>T_{\mathrm{obj}},
\]
where \(T_{\mathrm{obj}}\) is the time the object remains in the camera field, then manual reacquisition cannot close the loop. The operator may still see a moving white blob, but the correction arrives too late. This is a biomechanical veto independent of FPGA speed.

Weather veto proof. Define the object-background contrast as
\[
C=\frac{|L_{\mathrm{obj}}-L_{\mathrm{bg}}|}{L_{\mathrm{obj}}+L_{\mathrm{bg}}},
\]
where \(L\) is luminance in \(\mathrm{cd/m^2}\) or a linearized sensor code value. If \(C<C_{\min}\), where \(C_{\min}\) is the minimum contrast required for the chosen threshold \(\tau\), then segmentation cannot reliably separate target from background. If the signal-to-noise ratio satisfies
\[
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}=20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{obj}}-\mu_{\mathrm{bg}}}{\sigma_n}\right)<\mathrm{SNR}_{\min},
\]
with \(\mu\) in digital code values and \(\sigma_n\) the noise standard deviation in the same units, then \(p_d\) falls and \(p_{fa}\) rises. Rain, fog, dust, and specular reflections all reduce \(C\) or increase \(\sigma_n\). The paper’s lighting warning [PAGE 4] is therefore a weather/illumination veto, not a tuning inconvenience. Cross-layer adjudication says: if any of these necessary conditions fails, the paper’s qualitative claims about tracking efficiency cannot be rescued by FPGA parallelism, because the information is not present at the sensor or the torque is not present at the actuator.

Scenario 1: object-speed ramp under fixed illumination and fixed frame rate. Fix camera resolution \(W\times H\), frame rate \(f_s\), target material, background, and target-selection procedure. Vary the target’s angular speed \(\Omega_{\mathrm{obj}}\) in \(\mathrm{rad/s}\) or image speed \(v_{\mathrm{obj}}\) in \(\mathrm{px/s}\) over a range that keeps the target inside the field early in each trial. Measure RMS centroid error \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) in pixels, lock-loss rate, detection probability \(p_d\), false-alarm probability \(p_{fa}\), and end-to-end latency in seconds. Prediction from the paper: “the error in tracking is directly proportional to the speed of the object to be tracked” [PAGE 4], so \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) should increase monotonically with speed in the pre-breakdown regime. Verifiable veto: if a linear regression of \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) on speed gives a non-positive slope with a confidence interval excluding zero, or if the error is flat until catastrophic loss, the paper’s proportional claim is falsified for that hardware configuration. A second veto is if \(p_d\) remains high while \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) is flat, which would indicate that the error metric is not measuring tracking quality.

Scenario 2: frame-rate sweep at constant object speed. Hold object speed, target color, lighting, and motor command logic constant. Vary \(T_f\) by changing the frame period or by decimating frames; measure \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) in pixels and lock-loss rate. Prediction from the paper: “The error in tracking also increases with the decrease in the frame rate at constant speed of the object” [PAGE 4], so increasing \(T_f\) should increase \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\). Verifiable veto: if increasing \(T_f\) reduces or does not change \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) across the tested range, the claim is falsified. Additional prediction: when \(|\Omega_{\mathrm{obj}}|T_f\ge\Omega_{\mathrm{FOV}}/2\), the system should show a sharp increase in lock loss or target-identity swaps. If no such breakdown appears, either the effective field of view is larger than assumed or the tracker is using temporal memory not described in the paper.

Scenario 3: surface-finish and illuminance factorial. Cross two target finishes, matte and shiny, with at least three illuminance levels \(E_v\) in lux, including a low-light condition, a nominal condition, and a high-light condition. Measure \(p_d\), \(p_{fa}\), \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\), and lock-loss rate. Prediction from the paper: “The efficiency of tracking of camera is much higher when the object contains uniform texture” and “The efficiency of tracking less shiny objects is much higher than of shiny surfaces” [PAGE 4]. Therefore matte targets should have higher \(p_d\) and lower \(e_{\mathrm{rms}}\) than shiny targets at the same illuminance. Prediction from the lighting warning: extreme low or high illuminance should reduce \(p_d\) and increase lock loss. Verifiable veto: if shiny targets outperform matte targets at nominal illuminance, or if illuminance extremes produce no measurable change, the paper’s surface/lighting claims are falsified. A second veto is if \(p_{fa}\) rises without a corresponding drop in \(p_d\); that would indicate that the tracker is locking onto background rather than losing the target, which is a different failure mode from the one the paper describes.
