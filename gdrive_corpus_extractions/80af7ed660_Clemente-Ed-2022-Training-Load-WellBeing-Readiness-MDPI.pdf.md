<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a heterogeneous MDPI special-issue corpus, not a single experiment. Its through-architecture is stated in the Preface: monitoring should individually track “internal and external load,” analyze “accumulated load and peripherical factors on well-being,” and assess “the athlete’s readiness” to reduce injury risk and improve performance [PAGE 10]. The multi-hop derivation is therefore: (1) training exposure is measured as internal load, external load, or both; (2) exposure is compressed into weekly or rolling indices such as monotony, strain, and ACWR; (3) those indices are interpreted against well-being, fitness, biomechanics, and injury history; (4) decisions alter future exposure; (5) the loop closes through injury or performance outcomes. The soccer paper makes the load distinction explicit: “The external load can be considered as the physical demands that occur in the players in response to the implemented drill/task, while the internal load corresponds to the psychophysiological responses to the external load” [PAGE 59]. This is not a trivial distinction: s-RPE, distance covered, and high-speed running are different measurement operators, and their indices cannot be averaged as if they were the same SI quantity.

A gray-zone audit is mandatory because each article supplies its own veto. The Wushu study has only 12 injured athletes, no healthy controls, and a 20-month injury cut-point derived from a Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel risk expression; the authors themselves state that “athletes with an injury length of 20 months or more were classified as having a high risk of repeated injury” [PAGE 14] and later admit that “this study had a small sample size due to the limited number of Wushu players in Korea. In addition, healthy Wushu players were not included in the study” [PAGE 26]. The AAS survey is male-only, self-report, and without blood work: “it was only performed among male gym members, no blood work was included to investigate potential adverse implications of AAS, and finally, we used a self-reported questionnaire” [PAGE 46]. The women’s soccer cohort did not meet its own recommended sample size: “the recommended sample size (N = 80 players) as per Gregson et al. was not met” [PAGE 86]. The cycling myometry study is cross-sectional, uses an incremental test rather than a stage race, and finds baseline stiffness similarity across performance groups [PAGE 97]. These constraints mean the correct inference is conditional and local, not a universal athlete-readiness law.

Information-theoretically, monitoring has value only if it reduces uncertainty about the future state. Let \(S_{t+1}\) be a discretized readiness/injury/performance state and \(M_{\leq t}\) the monitoring history. The epistemic gain is
\[
\Delta I_t = H(S_{t+1}) - H(S_{t+1}\mid M_{\leq t}) \quad [\text{bits or nats}],
\]
with the assumption that the state definition and priors are stable. This breaks if \(S\) is redefined mid-season, if labels are missing, or if the monitor is a deterministic function of a variable already known. In practical terms, a dashboard that reports monotony, strain, and ACWR without denominator checks can add zero information and may add false confidence. The gray-zone conclusion is that the source supports a layered, veto-based monitoring system: use the load indices as weak evidence, then veto conclusions when biomechanical, temporal, or population constraints conflict.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Training monotony is defined in words as “mean of training load during the seven days of the week divided by the standard deviation of the training load of the seven days” [PAGE 61]. For daily session loads \(x_{w,d}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) in arbitrary units (AU), with \(d=1,\dots,7\),
\[
\mathrm{TM}_{w}=\frac{\bar{x}_{w}}{s_{x,w}},\qquad
\bar{x}_{w}=\frac{1}{7}\sum_{d=1}^{7}x_{w,d},\qquad
s_{x,w}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{7-1}\sum_{d=1}^{7}(x_{w,d}-\bar{x}_{w})^2}.
\]
Units: AU/AU, dimensionless. Assumption: seven comparable daily loads and a meaningful SD. Exact breakdown: if \(s_{x,w}=0\) (all seven daily loads equal), \(\mathrm{TM}_{w}\) is undefined or diverges; if \(s_{x,w}\to 0^+\), monotony becomes numerically unstable. A zero-denominator guard must be explicit; otherwise dashboards silently produce infinity or NaN.

Training strain is defined as “sum of the training loads for all training sessions during a week multiplied by training monotony” [PAGE 61]:
\[
\mathrm{TS}_{w}=\left(\sum_{d=1}^{7}x_{w,d}\right)\mathrm{TM}_{w}.
\]
Units: AU if \(x\) is AU; if \(x\) is distance in m, the product is m, not a universal “strain” unit. Assumption: the same load metric is used in both the sum and the monotony term. Breakdown: if \(\mathrm{TM}_{w}\) is undefined, \(\mathrm{TS}_{w}\) inherits the singularity; if the sum is zero and monotony is undefined, strain is not zero but undefined. Cross-metric aggregation is invalid because s-RPE AU, distance in m, and high-speed running in m are not commensurable.

ACWR is defined as “dividing the acute workload, i.e., the 1-week rolling workload data, by the chronic workload, i.e., the rolling 4-week average workload data” [PAGE 61]. Let \(A_t\) be the acute 1-week rolling load and \(C_t\) the 4-week rolling mean:
\[
\mathrm{ACWR}_t=\frac{A_t}{C_t}.
\]
Units: dimensionless. Assumption: \(C_t>0\), the chronic window is complete, and the load metric is stationary enough for a ratio to be interpretable. Exact breakdown: \(C_t=0\) gives division by zero; \(C_t\to 0^+\) gives extreme ratios; fewer than four chronic weeks make the denominator unstable. Because the chronic window is four weeks, the index has a built-in multi-week memory trap: a spike can be “diluted” or “amplified” by earlier exposure, not just by the current week.

Blood flow is computed as “Blood ﬂow (mL/min) = Cross sectional area (cm2) × TAV (cm/s) × 60 s/min” [PAGE 30]:
\[
Q_{\mathrm{BF}}(t)=A_{\mathrm{CSA}}(t)\,\bar v_{\mathrm{TAV}}(t)\,60\ \mathrm{s/min}.
\]
Units: \(A\) in cm\(^2\), \(v\) in cm/s, \(Q\) in mL/min. Assumption: the vessel cross-section is adequately represented by the measured diameter and the time-averaged velocity is representative. Exact breakdown: if \(\bar v_{\mathrm{TAV}}=0\), flow is zero; if the Doppler angle deviates from the maintained 60°, the measured velocity scales approximately with \(\cos\theta\), and at \(\theta=90^\circ\) the Doppler shift vanishes. The source states that “Angle of insonation of the ultrasound probe was maintained at 60◦” [PAGE 30]; this is an operator constraint, not an optional detail.

The arterial occlusion pressure regression is “AOP (mmHg) = 40.4 + (3.23) Leg Circumference (cm)” [PAGE 32]:
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{AOP}}=40.4+3.23\,C_{\mathrm{leg}}.
\]
Units: mmHg for AOP, cm for circumference. Assumption: linearity over the observed limb-size range and exchangeability of the sampled population. Breakdown: extrapolation far outside the observed circumference range is unsupported; the model is a population-average regression, not an individual guarantee. The paper also reports sex and limb differences: “There was a signiﬁcant difference in the AOP between males and females in the dominant (230 ± 41 vs. 191 ± 27 mmHg; p = 0.002) and non-dominant (209 ± 37 vs. 178 ± 21 mmHg; p = 0.004) legs” [PAGE 32]. Therefore a single absolute cuff pressure cannot be transferred across sexes or limbs without a veto check.

Relative blood flow is “Percent Blood Flow = 99.46 −0.85 (Occlusion Pressure; %AOP)” [PAGE 32]:
\[
\%Q_{\mathrm{BF}}=99.46-0.85\,(\%\mathrm{AOP}).
\]
Units: percent. Assumption: linearity over the tested 0–100% AOP range and a population-average response. Exact breakdown: at \(\%\mathrm{AOP}>99.46/0.85\approx117.0\%\), the equation predicts negative blood flow, which is physically inadmissible; at 100% AOP it predicts 14.46%, not zero, so it cannot be treated as an individual deterministic law. The reported dispersion is large: \(R=-0.842\) and residual standard error \(25.3\) [PAGE 32]. The paper contrasts this with nonlinear reports and calls for caution, so the operator is a weak population summary, not a precise prescription.

The women’s soccer paper gives a power operator for repeated sprint testing:
\[
P=\frac{m\,d^2}{t^3},
\]
with \(m\) in kg, \(d\) in m, \(t\) in s, giving W when coherent SI units are used [PAGE 78]. Exact breakdown: \(t\to 0\) or timing-gate failure makes \(P\) diverge; the cubic denominator amplifies timing noise. The fatigue index is given as “Fatigue = maxpower−minpower / Sum of 6 sprints (s)” [PAGE 78], schematically
\[
\mathrm{FI}=\frac{P_{\max}-P_{\min}}{\sum_{i=1}^{6}t_i}.
\]
Units depend on the numerator and denominator; the source reports a percentage in the table, so unit conversion must be explicit. Breakdown: denominator zero from missing sprint times, or \(P_{\min}>P_{\max}\) producing a negative index.

The Yo-Yo IR1 estimate is “VO2max (mL/min/kg) = IR1 distance (m) × 0.0084 + 36.4” [PAGE 77]:
\[
\dot V\mathrm{O}_{2\max}\approx0.0084\,D_{\mathrm{IR1}}+36.4.
\]
Units: mL·kg\(^{-1}\)·min\(^{-1}\) and m. Assumption: the tested population and protocol match the validation context. Breakdown: extrapolation beyond validated distances and individual variability; this is an estimate, not a gas-exchange measurement.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is traceable: session-RPE and monotony/strain descend from Foster-style load monitoring, ACWR from the acute:chronic workload tradition, and the current special issue applies them across soccer, women’s soccer, Wushu, cycling, BFR, gait, and AAS surveillance. The soccer study monitored a 41-week competition period with 52 matches and ten mesocycles [PAGE 60]; it found that in the mesocycle with more matches, monotony, ACWR, and some strain indices rose, while ACWR values remained below 1.3 [PAGE 68]. That is a multi-week trap: the chronic denominator carries four weeks of history [PAGE 61], so a single congested block is not interpretable without the preceding month. The women’s soccer cohort used three assessments over 22 weeks, with intervals of four weeks and 18 weeks [PAGE 75], and concluded that “the usefulness of some ﬁtness data may become outdated (or at least its relevance might be reduced) after approximately a month in women’s soccer” [PAGE 85]. This is a clock trap: a test’s predictive validity decays with the test-to-match interval.

The Wushu paper adds an injury-history trap. The 20-month cut-point was chosen because “athletes with an injury length of 20 months or more were classified as having a high risk of repeated injury” [PAGE 14]. The retrospective logic is plausible but fragile: injury length is not randomized, and the “less injured” group may simply be better trained or better rehabilitated. The BFR line has its own genealogy: early absolute cuff pressures gave way to relative %AOP because a fixed mmHg value means different occlusion for different limbs [PAGE 28]. That is a retrocausal correction: the older absolute-pressure habit is vetoed by the later limb-circumference and sex evidence [PAGE 32]. The AAS survey shows a public-health lineage: prevalence estimates in gyms, then awareness and source-of-supply questions; the reported 21.3% prevalence [PAGE 43] is a surveillance signal, not a causal effect estimate.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under crisis, athletes and coaches regress to habitual patterns. The Wushu data suggest that longer injury history is associated with lower lower-extremity myofunction for aerial rotation: “when the length of the injuries of the athletes is long, the muscle function of the lower extremities to cause rotation in the air is low” [PAGE 25]. The recursive trap is that the athlete may avoid high-rotation attempts, the coach may read that avoidance as readiness, and the avoidance itself reduces the exposure needed to rebuild the capacity. Counter-traps are equally important. A heel-first strike cue lowers knee external rotation and adductor moments [PAGE 48], but it also raises the peak vertical force at initial contact: “The mean value for the peak vertical force at the initial contact was signiﬁcantly higher during walking with heel-ﬁrst strike” [PAGE 51]. An athlete who feels less knee pain may therefore increase volume and load the impact pathway more. That is a counter-trap: the local knee-load reduction can coexist with a global force redistribution.

The clock matters because beliefs are updated on different time scales. Coaches may treat a fitness test as valid for months, but the women’s soccer evidence says predictive usefulness may decay after about a month [PAGE 85]. Non-starters present a distinct recursive problem: the soccer discussion warns that “particular attention should be paid to non-starters since they presented a high monotony” [PAGE 68]. A non-starter may train harder to earn selection, the coach may see high monotony as poor load management, and the athlete may hide fatigue to avoid being labeled unready. The safe theory-of-mind model is therefore not “athlete reports readiness” but a joint inference over athlete belief, coach belief, selection pressure, and measurement clock. Any model that assumes truthful self-report and stationary test validity is vetoed by the source’s own limitations.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical layers veto simple “load up or down” conclusions. Heel-first strike reduces knee flexion angle and knee adduction moment [PAGE 51], but the same condition increases peak vertical force at initial contact and toe-off [PAGE 52]. Therefore a claim that heel-first strike “reduces mechanical load” is false as stated; it redistributes load from the knee frontal plane toward impact pathways. The correct claim is joint-specific and phase-specific. BFR provides a second veto: AOP differs by sex and limb, with dominant-leg means of 230 ± 41 mmHg in males versus 191 ± 27 mmHg in females [PAGE 32]. A fixed cuff pressure is therefore not a transferable physical prescription. The operator must either measure AOP per limb or explicitly accept a relative-pressure approximation.

Weather and environment veto broad generalization. The women’s soccer tests were performed at “a mean temperature of 19.5 ± 3.4 ◦C and a relative humidity of 63 ± 4%” [PAGE 77]. The cycling laboratory was at “a mean temperature of 21 ± 2 ◦C and mean relative humidity of 52% ± 9%” [PAGE 94]. These are bounded laboratory or field conditions; extrapolation to extreme heat, cold, altitude, or rain is not licensed by the source. The Wushu study is also physically bounded by landing mechanics and a small injured sample [PAGE 26]. The AAS survey is bounded by male gym users and self-report [PAGE 46]. The cycling study is bounded by an incremental test, not a stage race, and by a cross-sectional design [PAGE 97]. These are cross-layer vetoes: no amount of statistical significance in one layer can override a biomechanical contradiction, a missing denominator, or a population mismatch.
