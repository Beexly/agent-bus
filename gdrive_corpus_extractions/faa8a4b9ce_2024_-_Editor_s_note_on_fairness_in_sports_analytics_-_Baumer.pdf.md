That sentence is the drive source for the whole reasoning problem: the editorial’s object is not a player, team, or biomechanical system, but the semantic and methodological status of the word “fair” in sports analytics. The first multi-hop derivation is therefore: if a paper claims a scoring system is more fair, and if “fair” can mean either equality of treatment or equity of opportunity, then the claim is underdetermined until the author supplies criteria. Baumer makes that explicit: “I pressed the authors to carefully define their notion of fairness and list the criteria they use to evaluate it” and “Without specific criteria, I argued that any claims of superiority would be unsupported” [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit begins here: a paper can be mathematically rigorous about optimization while still making an unsupported normative claim, because optimization criteria are not automatically fairness criteria. Paper B is the clean example: the authors “already had specific optimization criteria” and the “designation of ‘fair’ was not necessary, and had become a distraction from the contributions of the paper” [PAGE 1]. That is a counter-trap: the correct editorial move is not to force every paper into a fairness vocabulary, but to force every fairness claim into explicit criteria or remove the claim.

The second derivation step is the equality/equity split. On [PAGE 1], equality is “treating each participant the same,” while equity “requires that we give each person a box of the appropriate height such that they have the same opportunity (to see the game) as everyone else.” On [PAGE 1-2], Baumer says “both systems might reasonably be described as ‘fair,’ and we don’t agree on which notion of ‘fairness’ we want.” This creates a formal breakdown regime: if the criteria set is empty, the claim is undefined; if the criteria set contains both equality and equity constraints that cannot be jointly maximized, the ranking is only a partial order, not a total order. The editorial then supplies a sports-specific illustration: golf handicaps make amateur events “fair” by equity, while professional events make them “fair” by equality [PAGE 2]. The example is exact: “a player with a 12-handicap who shoots an 80 will win against a player with a 4-handicap who shoots a 78” [PAGE 2]. That example is not a toy ratio; it is a net-score comparison in strokes, and it shows why “fair” cannot be evaluated without the treatment variable and the opportunity variable.

The third derivation step is the predictability/uncertainty layer. Baumer cites Kovalchik and Ingram: “longer matches favor the better player and make match outcomes more predictable” [PAGE 2]. He also cites the sports-economics finding that “fan interest (and thus opportunities for teams and leagues to make money) is highest when the outcome of a match is more uncertain” [PAGE 2]. This is a multi-objective gray zone: longer formats may increase the probability that the better player wins, but they may reduce outcome uncertainty, and the editorial does not claim a universal social welfare function that ranks those objectives. The same tension appears in best-of-5 versus best-of-3 tennis and in MLB playoff expansion: “longer series favor the stronger team” and MLB “has only expanded the playoffs with more shorter series” [PAGE 2]. The audit conclusion is that “fair” is not a single scalar; it is a vector of criteria whose weights are normative, institutional, and sport-specific. Paper A is closer to equity because it proposes “a scoring system for para and master’s swimming that accounts for age and disability class” [PAGE 2]; Paper B is closer to equality because it “raises the probability of the best two players meeting in the final” [PAGE 2]. Paper C is the hardest case because “the gender of the athletes was a central consideration,” and Baumer insisted that notions of fairness be informed by “the extensive literature on women’s sports” and “women and gender studies” [PAGE 1]. The gray-zone audit therefore rejects any claim that the editorial endorses one universal fairness metric. It endorses criteria explicitness, contextual scholarship, and real-world relevance.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source contains no formal equations and no SI-unit measurements. It does contain operational concepts that can be formalized without inventing irrelevant ratios. The formalization below is an audit language derived from the editorial’s own criteria demand, not a claim that Baumer wrote these equations.

Let \(S_0\) be the existing scoring or prize system and \(S_1\) the proposed system. Let \(C=\{c_1,\dots,c_n\}\) be a finite set of explicit fairness criteria. Define the dimensionless satisfaction vector
\[
\mathbf{s}_k(C)=\left(\mathbf{1}[S_k\models c_1],\dots,\mathbf{1}[S_k\models c_n]\right)\in\{0,1\}^n,
\]
where \(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) is the unitless indicator function. A claim that \(S_1\) is more fair than \(S_0\) is well-posed only if \(C\neq\varnothing\) and evidence \(E\) supports a declared ordering on \(\mathbf{s}_1(C)\) and \(\mathbf{s}_0(C)\). This formalizes Baumer’s statement that “Without specific criteria, I argued that any claims of superiority would be unsupported” [PAGE 1]. The exact breakdown regime is \(C=\varnothing\): then the fairness claim is not false, but undefined. A second breakdown regime is criteria conflict: if \(C\) contains incompatible equality and equity constraints, \(\mathbf{s}(C)\) may be only partially ordered, so no unique “more fair” conclusion follows. This matches “both systems might reasonably be described as ‘fair,’ and we don’t agree on which notion of ‘fairness’ we want” [PAGE 1-2].

For equality and equity, define unitless operators
\[
\mathrm{Eq}(i,j)=1 \iff \mathrm{treatment}_i=\mathrm{treatment}_j,
\]
\[
\mathrm{Ey}(i,j)=1 \iff O_i=O_j,
\]
where \(O_i\) is the opportunity to see the game or to compete under equivalent conditions. The source states that equality is “treating each participant the same” and equity gives “the same opportunity (to see the game) as everyone else” [PAGE 1]. The operators are ordinal and dimensionless; the source supplies no cardinal utility scale for \(O_i\).

The golf handicap operator is source-derived. Let \(G_i\) be gross strokes over 18 holes and \(P\) be par strokes. Then
\[
H_i=\mathbb{E}[G_i-P],
\]
with \(G_i,P,H_i\) measured in strokes per 18-hole round. This follows Baumer’s definition: “A handicap is an estimate of the average number of strokes that a player exceeds par over the course of 18 holes” [PAGE 2]. The net-score comparison in amateur handicap play is
\[
N_i=G_i-H_i,
\]
with lower \(N_i\) winning. The editorial’s example gives \(80-12=68\) versus \(78-4=74\) [PAGE 2]. The breakdown regime is that \(H_i\) must be estimated under a defined course and population; the editorial does not provide an estimation model, so the equation is descriptive, not an empirical estimate.

For tennis and series length, let \(m\) be match format length in sets and \(p_b(m)=\Pr(\text{better player wins}\mid m)\), dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). Baumer cites the relation “longer matches favor the better player and make match outcomes more predictable” [PAGE 2]. A minimal monotone formalization is
\[
m_1>m_2 \implies p_b(m_1)\ge p_b(m_2)
\]
under ceteris paribus and a fixed player pair. The source does not give a functional form, coefficient, or SI unit. The breakdown regime is non-identifiability: if \(m\) is correlated with player selection, tournament round, or format rules, the monotone statement is not causal from this editorial alone. For series, let \(L\) be games in a series and \(p_s(L)=\Pr(\text{stronger team wins series}\mid L)\), dimensionless. The source reports that “longer series favor the stronger team” [PAGE 2], but the MLB claim that the strongest team was “least likely to win the championship” relative to other leagues is a comparative empirical result cited from Lopez et al., not a universal equation [PAGE 2].

For Paper B’s tournament proposal, let \(T\) be the tournament system and \(p_{12}(T)=\Pr(\text{best two players meet in the final}\mid T)\), dimensionless. Baumer says Paper B “raises the probability of the best two players meeting in the final” [PAGE 2]. The formal claim is therefore a comparison \(p_{12}(T_1)>p_{12}(T_0)\), but the source does not provide the probability values or the tournament graph. For fan interest, let \(U\in[0,1]\) be outcome uncertainty. The source claims fan interest is “highest when the outcome of a match is more uncertain” [PAGE 2], but gives no monotone function and no information-unit measurement such as bits or nats. Any entropy equation would be an import, not a source operator. The main cross-objective breakdown regime is that \(p_b(m)\), \(p_s(L)\), \(p_{12}(T)\), and \(U\) can move in different directions; without explicit weights, there is no unique fairness optimum.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of the editorial’s fairness problem runs through at least three lineages. The first is the equality/equity distinction illustrated by the Interaction Institute for Social Change cartoon: equality gives “each person a box of equal height,” while equity gives “a box of the appropriate height such that they have the same opportunity” [PAGE 1]. That lineage is older than sports analytics, but Baumer places it inside a sporting event so that the normative question becomes measurable in treatment and opportunity terms. The second lineage is handicap governance in golf: par and handicap convert gross strokes into net competition, so amateur events are “fair” by equity while professional events are “fair” by equality [PAGE 2]. The third lineage is the sports-analytics literature on format and uncertainty: Kovalchik and Ingram on tennis match duration [PAGE 2, PAGE 3], Buraimo and Simmons on outcome uncertainty and audience interest [PAGE 2, PAGE 3], Lopez, Matthews, and Baumer on randomness and best-team championships [PAGE 2, PAGE 3], Hall and Liu on tournament opponent choice [PAGE 2, PAGE 3], and Bartneck and Moltchanova on para and masters swimming points [PAGE 2, PAGE 3].

A retrocausal reading shows why the editorial is not merely a style note. Earlier papers may have treated “fair” as a post hoc persuasive label, then optimized a system, then looked for a justification. Baumer reverses that order: criteria must come before superiority claims. The multi-week trap is that revision cycles reward polishing a system while leaving the normative term untouched. A paper can spend weeks improving a tournament graph, a points formula, or a playoff structure and still fail the first gate if “fair” is undefined. Paper A escapes by generating criteria in revision [PAGE 1]. Paper B escapes by removing the unnecessary fairness label [PAGE 1]. Paper C exposes the deepest trap: when “gender of the athletes was a central consideration,” a fairness claim without women’s-sports and gender-studies scholarship “would severely weaken the paper by abdicating our responsibility to include relevant scholarship” [PAGE 1]. The authors “respectfully decided to withdraw” [PAGE 1]. The retrocausal lesson is that fairness claims are not free-floating; they inherit the history of the athletes, categories, and institutions they classify.

Another multi-week trap is the confusion between “more predictable” and “more fair.” The tennis and MLB passages show that longer competition can favor the stronger competitor, while fan interest can rise with uncertainty [PAGE 2]. If authors optimize only for predictability, they may produce a system that is defensible for selection but unattractive to audiences or harmful to underdog opportunity. If they optimize only for uncertainty, they may produce a system that weakens meritocratic selection. The editorial does not resolve that tradeoff; it says “there are no one-size-fits-all solutions” and recommends framing notions of fairness “using existing goalposts” [PAGE 2]. A final trap is contextual omission. Baumer rejects the “just focus on sports” posture and insists that scholarship engage “political, social, and historical context to the extent that it is relevant” [PAGE 2]. The beach-volleyball image is rhetorical: “perhaps on a beach volleyball court” [PAGE 2]. It is not weather evidence and should not be retrofitted into a physical-model claim.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The editorial is a recursive belief system. At level 0, an author has an internal notion of fairness, often tacit. At level 1, the author predicts that reviewers will accept “fair” as a persuasive label, so the author claims the new system is “more fair” [PAGE 1]. At level 2, the editor predicts that the author may not have explicit criteria, so the editor demands a definition and a criteria list. Baumer records exactly that move: “I pressed the authors to carefully define their notion of fairness and list the criteria they use to evaluate it” [PAGE 1]. At level 3, the author predicts the editor’s prediction and chooses a revision strategy: Paper A supplies criteria; Paper B removes the fairness claim; Paper C withdraws after being asked to engage women’s-sports and gender-studies literature [PAGE 1]. At level 4, the community infers from those outcomes what JQAS will accept: explicit criteria, real-world relevance, and contextual scholarship.

Crisis habit regression appears when the clock is short. Under revision pressure, authors may regress to the habit of using “fair” as a slogan because it sounds normatively strong. The editorial’s Paper B is the counter-trap: the authors “already had specific optimization criteria,” and the fairness label “had become a distraction” [PAGE 1]. The correct response was not to invent a fairness theory after the fact, but to drop the label and preserve the contribution. Paper A is the constructive counter-trap: the authors “were able to come up with a reasonable list of criteria in their revision” and showed their system satisfied them “in ways that were importantly better than the existing scoring system” [PAGE 1]. Paper C is the hard counter-trap: when gender is central, a generic fairness definition is insufficient; the editor’s request for external scholarship raises the cost of revision, and the authors withdraw [PAGE 1]. The clock matters because revision rounds are finite: “This paper is in another round of revision and will be published in the next issue” for Paper A, while Paper B also moves forward after removing the claim [PAGE 1]. The recursive lesson is that the editor is not merely checking math; the editor is checking whether the author can model the reader’s normative expectations. A paper that treats “fair” as self-evident is modeling no reader beyond the author. A paper that states equality versus equity, criteria, and evidence is modeling a skeptical reader, a reviewer, and a future practitioner at once.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical/biomechanical/weather veto must be handled conservatively because the source is an editorial. The only physical or category-level variables explicitly named are age, disability class, gender, and “innate physical attributes, excellent training, mental acuity” [PAGE 2]. Paper A is specifically about “para and master’s swimming that accounts for age and disability class” [PAGE 2]. Therefore a physical-layer veto is justified when a claimed equity system omits the very classification variables that make the competition equitable. If a swimming points system claims fairness while failing to define age class or disability class, the equity claim is under-specified; the source gives no disability-class measurement model, so no quantitative veto can be computed from the editorial alone. The veto is logical, not biomechanical: the source names the variables but supplies no coefficients, no load equations, and no injury model.

The tennis example creates a biomechanical gray zone. Baumer cites “longer matches favor the better player and make match outcomes more predictable” [PAGE 2]. A longer match may also change physiological load, recovery, and injury exposure, but the editorial does not provide any biomechanical variable, unit, or equation. Thus a biomechanical veto can be triggered only if a paper infers “more fair” from match length while ignoring a stated physical constraint that its own design changes. The editorial itself does not make that inference. The same caution applies to golf: “innate physical attributes, excellent training, mental acuity” are listed as possible sources of superiority [PAGE 2], but no swing biomechanics or weather model is given. The handicap operator is a scoring convention, not a physical model.

The weather layer has no evidentiary support in the source. The only weather-adjacent phrase is the rhetorical “perhaps on a beach volleyball court” [PAGE 2], which is a metaphor about ignoring context, not a dataset. There is no temperature, wind, precipitation, humidity, or altitude variable in the editorial. Therefore any weather-based veto of a source-derived fairness claim would be unverifiable from this document. The cross-layer adjudication is: physical/category vetoes are admissible only where the source names the category variable, as with age and disability class in para and masters swimming; biomechanical vetoes are inadmissible without an explicit load or injury variable; weather vetoes are inadmissible here because the source contains no weather measurement. That is the proof of the veto boundary.
