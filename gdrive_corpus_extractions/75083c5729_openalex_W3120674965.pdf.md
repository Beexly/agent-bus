First, the source defines the game state as “the current situation in the match, which may include the locations of the players and the ball, the scoreline, and the time remaining in the match” [PAGE 4]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(s_t\) denote the game state at time \(t\), with \(s_t\) containing ball and player locations, scoreline, and time remaining [PAGE 4]. Let \(V:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}\) be a state-value function. The action-value operator is
\[
\Delta V(a_t)=V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t),
\]
with units equal to the units of \(V\): expected goals if \(V\) is goal probability, or expected league points if \(V\) is a points value. Assumption: \(s_t\) is a sufficient state and the action’s effect is captured by the immediate transition. Breakdown regime: hidden variables, non-Markov dynamics, and multi-player credit assignment invalidate the simple difference; the source notes the difficulty of dividing credit “among all 22 players on the pitch” [PAGE 5].

For shots, let \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) be the goal indicator and \(C_i\) the shot context. Then
\[
xG_i=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid C_i),\qquad r_i=Y_i-xG_i,
\]
where \(xG_i\) is dimensionless probability and \(r_i\) is a dimensionless shot residual or goals per shot. The source describes xG as “the likelihood of a shot yielding a goal” [PAGE 2] and notes evolution from location-only models to models using opponent locations and “the height of the ball at the time of the shot” [PAGE 3]. Assumption: conditional exchangeability and calibration within \(C_i\). Breakdown regime: sparse shots, unobserved pressure, goalkeeper positioning, and player finishing effects; the source states, “if two players take an identical shot, the model will assign a higher xG to the one that is a better finisher” [PAGE 6], which is a modeling assumption rather than an observed identical-shot experiment.

For goalkeepers, let \(S_i\in\{0,1\}\) be a save indicator on an on-target shot with post-shot context \(C_i^{S}\). Then
\[
xS_i=\Pr(S_i=1\mid C_i^{S}),\qquad e_i=S_i-xS_i,
\]
with \(xS_i\) dimensionless and aggregated expected saves in saves. Assumption: evaluation is conditioned on on-target shots and includes post-shot direction information [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regime: selection on on-target shots, small season samples, and unobserved shot placement or goalkeeper biomechanics.

For execution rating, let \(O_i\in\{0,1\}\) be action success and \(\hat p_i=\Pr(O_i=1\mid C_i^{E})\). Then
\[
E_i=O_i-\hat p_i,
\]
dimensionless. The source states: “The execution rating is then simply the difference between the result of the action ... and the predicted probability that the action would be successful” [PAGE 7]. Assumption: success is well-defined and \(C_i^{E}\) includes the relevant context, including “the body part used to execute the action” [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: binary success misclassifies decision quality, and unobserved pressure or tactical instruction biases \(\hat p_i\).

For Added Goal Value, let \(P_{\text{win}}(s_t)\) and \(P_{\text{draw}}(s_t)\) be in-game win and draw probabilities. Then
\[
AGV_i=3\left[P_{\text{win}}(s_{t+1})-P_{\text{win}}(s_t)\right]
+1\left[P_{\text{draw}}(s_{t+1})-P_{\text{draw}}(s_t)\right],
\]
with units of expected league points. This follows the source’s description of the metric as “the sum of the change in win probability multiplied by three and the change in draw probability” [PAGE 10]. Assumption: the 3-1-0 points rule and no other state changes. Breakdown regime: saturated win probabilities, miscalibration, and infinitesimal effects for most non-goal actions.

For pressure, define a hypothetical goal augmentation \(s_t\oplus g_H\) for the home team and \(s_t\oplus g_A\) for the away team. A formal proxy is
\[
\pi_t=\left|P_{\text{win}}(s_t)-P_{\text{win}}(s_t\oplus g_H)\right|
+\left|P_{\text{win}}(s_t)-P_{\text{win}}(s_t\oplus g_A)\right|,
\]
dimensionless probability. This operationalizes the source’s use of “the difference in win probability between the current game state and the two hypothetical game states where the home or away team has scored an additional goal” [PAGE 10]. Assumption: win-probability model calibrated and goal augmentation well-defined. Breakdown regime: probabilities near 0 or 1, unmodeled red cards, and time effects. The source also notes that “Pressure mounts in close games, particularly as time winds down because a goal would increase the chances of a favorable outcome” [PAGE 9].

For pre-game pressure, let \(m\) be a match and \(F(m)\) a feature vector over team ambition, game importance, recent performance, and game context. A learned ordinal score is
\[
p_{\text{pre}}(m)=f\left(F(m)\right),
\]
with ordinal score units. The source trains this from pairwise expert rankings and notes that “Team ambition” and “Game importance” are among the factors [PAGE 12]. Assumption: expert rankings are monotone and features generalize across leagues. Breakdown regime: expert bias, league-specific ambition, and unobserved squad mental state.

Calibration should be audited with log-loss,
\[
L=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\right],
\]
with units of nats when using the natural logarithm and bits when using base 2. Breakdown regime: perfect separation, sparse positives, and distribution shift.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under pressure, three mechanisms must be separated: crisis habit regression, counter-trap anticipation, and clock compression. Crisis habit regression means that as pressure rises, a player may fall back on overlearned action policies rather than deliberative choice. Counter-trap means the opponent anticipates that regression and positions to punish the habitual action. Clock compression means time remaining changes the marginal value of actions because a goal’s effect on win probability depends on when it occurs. A formal decision model is
\[
a_t^\star=\arg\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[U(s_{t+1})\mid s_t,a,b_t^{(2)}\right],
\]
where \(U\) is expected league points or win probability, and \(b_t^{(2)}\) is the player’s second-order belief about the opponent’s belief about the player’s action policy. Units are expected points or probability. Assumption: the player forms a recursive belief and the action set is feasible. Breakdown regime: pressure saturates, beliefs are misspecified, or the player has no reliable second-order model.

The source supplies the observable pressure skeleton. It distinguishes in-game context from pre-game context [PAGE 9]. In-game pressure is tied to score differential and time: “Pressure mounts in close games, particularly as time winds down because a goal would increase the chances of a favorable outcome” [PAGE 9]. Pre-game pressure is tied to rivalry, relegation impact, and stakes [PAGE 9]. The source also warns that clutch definitions are contested and that “up to 75% of all football games end with a goal difference below one” [PAGE 10], which makes binary clutch labels fragile. Recursive theory of mind therefore should not be treated as a directly observed variable. It is a latent state that must be inferred from action timing, opponent positioning, and repeated interactions. Habit regression is a hypothesis about policy collapse; counter-trap is a hypothesis about opponent response; clock is an observed state variable. The correct audit is to ask whether the pressure proxy changes the predicted action before the outcome is known. If not, the mental explanation is post hoc.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication requires that a higher-layer performance claim be rejected when a lower-layer physical, biomechanical, or environmental constraint makes the action infeasible or changes its conditional probability. Let \(X\) be the observed performance context and \(Z\) be an unobserved or weakly observed lower-layer state such as injury, fatigue, traction, ball-flight conditions, or biomechanical limitation. The true success probability is
\[
\Pr(O=1\mid X)=\int \Pr(O=1\mid X,Z)\,dP(Z\mid X).
\]
If a model estimates \(\hat p=\Pr(O=1\mid X)\) but assigns individual credit as if \(\hat p=\Pr(O=1\mid X,Z)\), then the omitted-variable bias is
\[
B(X)=\mathbb{E}\left[V(X,Z)\mid X\right]-V(X),
\]
with units of the value function. Assumption: \(Z\) is correlated with \(X\) or with player identity. Breakdown regime: if \(Z\) is independent of \(X\), the marginal model is unbiased for group averages but still invalid for individual counterfactuals. A hard veto occurs when \(a\notin\mathcal{A}(X,Z)\), because then \(\Pr(O=1\mid X,Z)=0\) and any positive estimated success probability is physically impossible. The source gives biomechanical examples: the “body part used to execute the action” [PAGE 6], the “height of the ball at the time of the shot” [PAGE 3], and goalkeeper “ability to time a jump” [PAGE 6]. It also gives a physical-fatigue example: “mental fatigue due to the quick succession of games” [PAGE 8]. Weather is not discussed in the supplied pages, so any weather-layer causal claim must be vetoed as unsupported by this source; it can be admitted only as an explicitly unmodeled environmental variable requiring external data. The adjudication rule is therefore: first check feasibility, second check conditional exchangeability, third check calibration, and only then interpret the metric as player performance.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: finishing-skill veto. Counterfactual: preserve each shot’s context \(C_i\) but randomly permute shot-taker identities across players. Prediction: if player-specific finishing skill is real, the observed between-player variance in \(r_i=Y_i-xG_i\) will exceed the permutation null; if the apparent skill is small-sample noise, the excess will collapse. Verification: held-out-season permutation test with log-loss and residual-variance statistics. The source itself warns that few players take large shot samples and that identical shots are practically unavailable [PAGE 6], so a positive result must survive this veto.

Scenario 2: execution-context veto. Counterfactual: score the same completed pass twice, once with pressure, body-part, location, and previous-action features, and once with those features removed. Prediction: the context-aware execution rating will rank a difficult completed through ball above an easy completed lateral pass, while the context-free model will invert or flatten that ranking. Verification: paired event audit against tracking-derived pressure and pass-lane features. This follows the source’s execution-rating definition as the difference between action result and predicted success probability [PAGE 7].

Scenario 3: Added Goal Value timing veto. Counterfactual: hold the number of goals fixed but move each goal to a neutral early-game state with the same score differential. Prediction: the original AGV will be higher for goals that produce large win-probability swings, while raw goal counts will be unchanged. Verification: recompute AGV on the event timeline using an in-game win probability model and compare original versus neutralized timestamps. This is exactly the source’s win-probability logic: AGV is the sum of the change in win probability multiplied by three and the change in draw probability [PAGE 10]. All three vetoes are verifiable because they specify a counterfactual transformation, a metric, and a predicted directional change before looking at the outcome.
