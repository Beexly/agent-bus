The source text does not report soundproofing or mechanical silence, so the visual threshold model \(\theta(I,P)\) is not veto-proof without an acoustic control.

Biomechanical layer: Albinism is associated with reduced retinal pigment epithelium, abnormal retinal development, and photophobia; pigmented eyes possess greater absorptive capacity and better performance at low luminance. The reported advantage for pigmented rats—perceiving the door "at a greater distance"—is consistent with a lower visual detection threshold [PAGE 129]. However, rats also use vibrissae for proximate tactile guidance and olfaction for goal localization. A biomechanical veto applies if albinos compensate for poor vision by running more slowly or using whisker contact earlier, which would compress \(d_{\mathrm{albino}}^*\) without any difference in cognitive expectancy.

Weather/environmental layer: The text says illumination was reduced "until supposedly complete darkness was reached" [PAGE 129], but provides no irradiance measurement and no statement that the room was light-sealed against outdoor weather or daylight leakage. If ambient daylight entered during the "darkness" phase, \(I_{\mathrm{leak}}>0\), and the claimed \(I\to0\) regime is falsified. A weather veto therefore invalidates any inference that turning-back behavior in the R curves is driven by non-visual cognition rather than residual photopic vision.

Adjudication: The visual-sign model is provisionally accepted only under the conjunction of (a) measured scotopic illumination \(I<10^{-3}\,\mathrm{lx}\), (b) acoustic isolation of the cord-door mechanism, and (c) matched running speed and whisker-use profiles across pigmentation groups. If any one fails, the characteristic distance \(d_i^*\) must be reinterpreted as a multimodal detection distance rather than a purely cognitive sign-to-goal mapping [PAGE 129].

Three verifiable RLVR veto scenarios with predictions.

Veto 1 — Affordance substitution. Replace the closed door with a visually identical dummy that does not block passage (e.g., a transparent or open-frame door matching visual contours). Prediction under the sign-Gestalt model [PAGE 129]: rats continue to turn back at \(d_i^*\) because they perceive the door as a negative goal-object. Prediction under an affordance/contact model: rats proceed through the dummy and only turn at physical contact, so \(d_i^*\to0\). Verifiable by measuring \(d_{i,t}\) before and after substitution; a collapse to \(d\approx0\) vetoes the pure visual-sign interpretation.

Veto 2 — Contingency decoupling. Open the unentered alley door on a random schedule independent of whether the rat has entered, turned, and started back, breaking the protocol described in [PAGE 129]. Prediction under mnemonics: rats cannot form a reliable association between closed door and non-availability, so \(\lambda_i\to0\) and \(d_{i,t}\) remains near \(L\) with no asymptotic reduction. Prediction under simple stimulus-response chaining: some turning may persist if local cues remain, but the characteristic distance \(d_i^*\) should fail to stabilize across days. Verifiable by fitting the first-order learning model; non-convergence of \(d_i^*\) vetoes the claim that the closed door functions as a learned negative goal-object.

Veto 3 — Scotopic isolation with olfactory marker. Test in independently measured darkness (\(I<10^{-3}\,\mathrm{lx}\), verified by photodiode) while adding an odorant exclusively to the closed door. Prediction under the visual-threshold account [PAGE 129]: the pigmented-eye advantage disappears because the retinal channel is saturated by noise; albino and pigmented rats converge on the same \(d^*\) if they use olfaction, or both regress to contact turning if no cue supports distance perception. Prediction under a retinal-pigment account: the advantage persists, contradicting the visual model. Verifiable by comparing \(d_{\mathrm{albino}}^*\) and \(d_{\mathrm{pigmented}}^*\) under measured darkness; persistence of a gap vetoes the visual-threshold explanation, while convergence supports it.
