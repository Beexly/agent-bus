<THINK_TRACE>
The benchmark’s causal chain is: an image is shown with the ball removed; human or model must infer the hidden ball’s grid cell from player gaze, pose, orientation, and sport context. The paper states the core premise directly: “Humans excel at visual social inference, the ability to infer hidden elements of a scene from subtle behavioral cues such as other people’s gaze, pose, and orientation” [PAGE 1]. The task is to “localize a removed sports ball from soccer, basketball, and volleyball images” [PAGE 1]. The headline result is a large human-model gap: humans are “two to three times more accurate (20–34%) than models (≤17%) across all sports” [PAGE 1].

A multi-hop audit shows why accuracy alone is not sufficient evidence for social inference. First, the ground-truth ball distribution is spatially clustered. The paper reports that “52.2% of ground truth balls are near players, 20.9% of the ground truth balls are near players by overlap” [PAGE 6], and that center-window ground-truth mass is “soccer (36.3%), volleyball (46.2%), basketball (56.3%)” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, a center-seeking or player-seeking policy can score above a uniform 60-cell baseline without using gaze or recursive ToM. The paper itself observes that models “rely on superficial spatial heuristics—such as guessing near the image center or nearby players—while humans leverage social cues like gaze direction and body pose” [PAGE 1]. The behavioral metrics confirm this: models are “90%” likely to predict near a player versus humans at “65–75%,” and several models have center ratios above 1.2 [PAGE 6]. The gray-zone issue is that the paper reports a uniform-distribution baseline but does not, in the main text, report an explicit nearest-player or center-window heuristic baseline. Thus the strongest causal claim—models fail at social cue composition—is supported but not fully isolated from simpler spatial priors.

Second, if models actually used gaze and pose composition, then Cue-Directed and chain-of-thought prompts should improve final localization. The paper finds only inconsistent gains: Cue-Directed prompting “yields some improvement over Base prompting in some cases. However, these gains are inconsistent and don’t close the large gap with human performance” [PAGE 7]. CoT sometimes degrades accuracy, and LLaMA reaches “272.6 ± 50.7 pixels in volleyball” under CoT [PAGE 5]. More tellingly, CoT “leads to more attention on gaze, but doesn’t improve accuracy” [PAGE 7]. This is a dissociation between textual rationale and behavioral use of the cue. The model can narrate gaze without converting gaze into a better ball-cell posterior.

Third, the sport-difficulty inversion is diagnostic. Humans perform best in basketball, worse in volleyball, and worst in soccer; models perform similarly in basketball and soccer but struggle most in volleyball [PAGE 5–6]. The paper explains that basketball has fewer but larger players, yielding clearer postural and gaze information, while volleyball has nearly twice as many players with much lower per-player pixel density [PAGE 6]. Volleyball also breaks the near-player heuristic because “the ball is rarely in contact with players as it is struck rather than held, making a ‘guess-near-player’ heuristic unreliable” [PAGE 6]. This supports the interpretation that model errors are driven by heuristic-prior mismatch rather than generic lack of sport knowledge.

Fourth, the static-image design removes motion but also removes trajectory evidence: “We use static images to isolate social reasoning from motion dynamics” [PAGE 2]. Inpainting controls artifacts only by manual inspection: “Images were manually checked to remove any remaining ball shadows or artifacts” [PAGE 3]. That is a reasonable control but not a quantitative artifact-leakage test. Finally, sampling and human-baseline differences are gray zones: models use “n = 50 predictions per image at Base Prompt and Cue-Directed Prompt, and n = 20 at Chain-of-Thought Prompt, all at temperature T = 0.6” [PAGE 4], while humans are N=150, 50 per sport, with three guesses and mean completion time 17.3 minutes [PAGE 4]. Overall, the evidence strongly supports a human-model gap and heuristic reliance, but the claim that models lack social inference per se requires stronger fixed-heuristic and artifact controls.

Accuracy is a dimensionless proportion over N images:
\[
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat y_i\in G_i\right].
\]
Source: “For image i with ground-truth cells Gi and prediction ˆyi: Accuracy = …” [PAGE 4]. Assumptions: \(G_i\) is nonempty; \(\hat y_i\) is one valid grid cell; multi-cell ground truth is allowed. Breakdown: if a model outputs an invalid or non-grid cell, the indicator cannot be evaluated without a parsing rule; if \(G_i\) is empty, the expression is undefined.

Euclidean error is a mean minimum distance:
\[
d_i=\min_{g\in G_i}\left\|c(\hat y_i)-c(g)\right\|_2 .
\]
Source: “Mean minimum distance (in pixels) from predicted cell center c(ˆyi) to nearest ground-truth cell” [PAGE 4]. Units: pixels when \(c(\cdot)\) maps cells to image coordinates. Assumptions: cell centers are well-defined; the nearest valid ground-truth cell is the relevant target. Breakdown: empty \(G_i\); coordinate-system mismatch; a prediction between two valid adjacent cells can be penalized only to the nearest center even if the cell overlaps the ball region.

Wasserstein distance compares model and human distributions:
\[
W(P,Q)=\min_{\gamma\ge0}\sum_{j,k}\gamma_{jk}D_{jk}
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\sum_k\gamma_{jk}=P_j,\qquad
\sum_j\gamma_{jk}=Q_k .
\]
Source: “We compare model and human prediction distributions P and Q using Earth Mover’s Distance” [PAGE 4]. Units: pixels if \(D_{jk}\) is Euclidean distance between cell centers in pixel coordinates; otherwise grid-cell units. Assumptions: \(P\) and \(Q\) are normalized probability masses over the 60-cell grid; \(\gamma\) is a transport plan. Breakdown: unnormalized \(P\) or \(Q\); zero-mass degeneracies; differing support conventions; if \(D_{jk}\) is not a metric, the “distance” interpretation weakens.

Point-to-box distance is:
\[
\mathrm{dist}(x,b)=
\begin{cases}
0, & x\in\mathrm{supp}(b),\\
d(x,\mathrm{supp}(b)), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]
where \(d\) is Euclidean distance to the box support. Units: pixels. Source: “Define point-to-box distance” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: empty player set; box support boundary conventions.

\]

\mathbf{1}\left[
\max_{b\in B_i}

{\mathrm{area}\left(c(\hat y_{i,t})\right)}
\ge\theta
\right].
\]
Source: “Fraction of predictions whose grid cell intersects a player box by ≥θ of cell area (θ = 0.02)” [PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless. Breakdown: zero cell area; empty player set; overlap saturation if cells are much larger than player boxes.

\[
\mathrm{CR}=\frac{\sum_{j\in S}p_j}{\sum_{j\in S}q_j},
\]
where \(S\) is the central window and \(p\) is model mass, \(q\) is ground-truth mass. Source: “Ratio of prediction mass to ground-truth mass in central window S = … (3×5 region)” [PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless. Assumptions: \(S\) is fixed; \(q\) has nonzero mass in \(S\). Breakdown: zero denominator; source text has a typographical extra comma in the row set, so the intended \(S\) should be read as rows {2,3,4} and columns {3,…,7}. \hat H(p)=\frac{-\sum_{j=1}^{60}p_j\log p_j}{\log 60}.
\]
Source: “Normalized entropy measures prediction spread” [PAGE 5]. Units: dimensionless; log base cancels under normalization. Assumptions: \(p_j\ge0\), \(\sum_j p_j=1\), and \(0\log0=0\). Breakdown: unnormalized \(p\); all mass on one cell gives 0; uniform over 60 cells gives 1.

\]
Source: “O(τ, θ) = 1/2 (NR(τ, θ) + OR(τ, θ))” [PAGE 12]. Units: dimensionless. Assumptions: equal weighting of proximity and overlap; metrics are comparable. Breakdown: saturation near median distance 0 and median overlap 0.56 [PAGE 12], which limits discriminability.

The paper’s lineage runs from classic theory-of-mind and social-perception work to modern multimodal benchmarks. The introduction grounds the task in ToM and cites Dennett’s intentional stance, Baron-Cohen’s autism ToM work, and Wellman’s developmental account [PAGE 1]. The related-work section traces adult social inference from “moving geometric shapes,” “gaze,” “facial expressions,” and “body language” [PAGE 2], citing Heider and Simmel, Kleinke, and de Gelder among others. The benchmark is therefore retrocausally connected to a long experimental tradition: social perception is inferred from sparse visual cues, not from explicit labels.

The benchmark lineage is equally important. The paper argues that existing social-reasoning benchmarks are mostly text-only, evaluating ToM, empathy, moral reasoning, deception detection, and negotiation, while visual benchmarks either show “fully observable scenes” or focus on “physical occlusion without social cues” [PAGE 2]. The authors claim: “No prior work evaluates whether models can infer hidden information purely from visual social cues” [PAGE 2]. This retroactively reframes earlier text-based ToM successes: the abstract warns that “such behavior may rely primarily on linguistic pattern matching rather than perceptual grounding” [PAGE 1]. In other words, high text-only social reasoning scores may not transfer to visually grounded inference.

The engineering genealogy is modular: YouTube retrieval, OpenCV decoding at approximately 1 FPS, CLIP filtering, YOLOv8 detection, Stable Diffusion inpainting, and a 6×10 alphanumeric grid [PAGE 2–3]. This pipeline is scalable and produced “3,000 additional soccer images for training and analysis” [PAGE 2]. But scalability introduces multi-week traps. First, pretraining contamination is not quantitatively ruled out: sports footage from YouTube may appear in web-scale pretraining corpora, so a model could memorize a famous frame or learn sport-specific ball priors. The 150-image curated evaluation set and the 3,000-image soccer set are useful, but a contamination audit on entirely new footage is still needed.

Second, inpainting artifacts are a persistent trap. Stable Diffusion is used to remove the ball, and “Images were manually checked to remove any remaining ball shadows or artifacts” [PAGE 3]. Manual checking is a reasonable control, but it is not a quantitative test for subtle texture, shadow, or compression cues that could leak location. A model could learn to detect the inpainted region rather than reason about players. Third, grid and annotation conventions create metric sensitivity: the average ball spans 2.27 soccer cells, 2.08 volleyball cells, and 2.42 basketball cells [PAGE 4, Table 1]. Multi-cell ground truth increases the chance that a coarse guess is counted correct, and the central window already contains large ground-truth mass [PAGE 6].

Fourth, prompt and sampling choices are traps for causal interpretation. Models are sampled at temperature 0.6 with 50 or 20 predictions per image [PAGE 4]; human participants make three guesses per test image and average 17.3 minutes [PAGE 4]. A model’s lower entropy could reflect a genuinely narrower strategy, or it could reflect decoding collapse. Fifth, the embedding-based rationale analysis classifies reasoning as pose-aligned or gaze-aligned using similarity to templates [PAGE 7]. That measures textual framing, not visual fixation or internal cue use. The paper’s own result—CoT increases gaze language but not accuracy [PAGE 7]—shows why rationale text cannot be treated as a causal explanation of behavior.

Finally, the human baseline has its own traps: Prolific recruitment, attention checks, exclusion of 26 participants, final N=150, and 50 per sport [PAGE 4]. Human performance is not a fixed ceiling; it depends on instructions, sport familiarity, and time. The retrocausal lesson is that the benchmark’s value is not just a leaderboard gap. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
I use “crisis habit regression” as an audit label, not as the paper’s terminology. Recursive ToM in this task requires at least two nested inferences: first, infer what a player sees from gaze and body orientation; second, infer what that player expects other players to do, and therefore where the ball is likely to be. A volleyball setter-hitter sequence is a clear example: the setter’s pose and gaze constrain the set, and the hitter’s approach constrains the attack. A model that only detects “player is jumping” or “player is near the net” is not performing recursive ToM; it is performing a body-level heuristic.

Crisis habit regression appears when ambiguity is high. The paper’s failure modes are exactly this: “Neglect of gaze,” “Role confusion,” and “Default-to-center heuristic” [PAGE 7–8]. Models “frequently misidentify which player has possession or is about to act” and “often resort to simply guessing near a player” [PAGE 7]. The quantitative signature is strong: models are “90%” likely to predict near a player versus humans at “65–75%” [PAGE 6], and several models show center ratios above 1.2 [PAGE 6]. Under uncertainty, the model collapses to a low-level prior—near a person or near the image center—rather than maintaining a structured belief about what each player sees.

The counter-trap is the prompt manipulation. Cue-Directed prompting explicitly tells the model that “The location of the players, where they are looking and their positions can help you infer the location of the ball” [PAGE 11]. Chain-of-Thought asks one-shot questions about player location, pose, and gaze before prediction [PAGE 4]. If the model had latent recursive ToM, these prompts should elicit better final cell predictions. Instead, CoT “leads to more attention on gaze, but doesn’t improve accuracy” [PAGE 7], and sometimes degrades performance [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap is that the model learns to produce a more human-like narrative—mentioning gaze and pose—without improving the underlying spatial belief. This is a classic dissociation between verbalized reasoning and control policy.

The clock dimension is the static-image constraint. The paper says: “We use static images to isolate social reasoning from motion dynamics” [PAGE 2]. That removes trajectory and timing, which are central to real recursive ToM: beliefs update as the ball moves, players reorient, and expectations change. The human experiment has a real clock—mean completion time 17.3 minutes, SD 7.4 [PAGE 4]—while model sampling is n=50 or n=20 at T=0.6 [PAGE 4]. The model sees a frozen frame and must infer a hidden state without temporal evidence. In that setting, a center prior can dominate because the image center is a safe Bayesian default when cue reliability is low. The paper’s conclusion points toward this limitation: progress may require “integrating perceptual priors, temporal information, or architectures explicitly designed to capture agentive and relational dynamics” [PAGE 8]. The benchmark therefore tests a snapshot of recursive ToM, not the full update loop.

A cross-layer veto asks whether physical, biomechanical, or environmental factors can explain the model-human gap without invoking social inference. The physical layer gives a clear veto against a universal “near-player” prior. In volleyball, “the ball is rarely in contact with players as it is struck rather than held, making a ‘guess-near-player’ heuristic unreliable” [PAGE 6]. This is why a model that predicts near players will fail in volleyball even though that heuristic may help in soccer or basketball. The physical structure of the sport is a legitimate cross-layer constraint, and the paper’s sport comparison uses it correctly.

The biomechanical layer is also diagnostic. Basketball has fewer but larger players, “offering clearer postural and gaze information” [PAGE 3]. The table reports average player coverage of 13,718.87 soccer pixels, 6,600.26 volleyball pixels, and 20,067.43 basketball pixels [PAGE 4, Table 1]. Volleyball has the most players on average, 9.92, but the lowest per-player pixel area [PAGE 4, Table 1]. This is a biomechanical visibility veto: if gaze and pose are too low-resolution, a model cannot extract fine social cues even if it has the capacity. The paper’s finding that models disproportionately rely on pose [PAGE 7] is consistent with this: coarse body orientation survives low resolution better than eye gaze. Basketball’s larger players should therefore be easier, and humans do perform best there [PAGE 5–6].

The weather and environment layer is weaker in the source. The paper does not report weather metadata, lighting controls, or outdoor-versus-indoor stratification. Soccer footage may include sun, shadow, rain, or crowd clutter; volleyball and basketball are typically indoor. Weather could affect gaze visibility, contrast, and pose extraction. However, because humans and models see the same images, weather cannot by itself explain a paired human-model gap unless model vision encoders are less robust to those conditions. The paper controls one major artifact source—ball shadows and inpainting residue—by manual inspection: “Images were manually checked to remove any remaining ball shadows or artifacts” [PAGE 3]. That is a partial veto against artifact leakage, but not a quantitative proof. A proper cross-layer adjudication would need an artifact-only classifier, weather/lighting metadata, and a low-level visibility score per image.

The static-image design is another cross-layer control: “We use static images to isolate social reasoning from motion dynamics” [PAGE 2]. This vetoes motion-blur and trajectory confounds, but it also removes a legitimate source of human inference. The model cannot use ball flight, player acceleration, or anticipation over time. So the benchmark is conservative: it tests inference from a frozen social snapshot. The cross-layer conclusion is that the human-model gap is not plausibly explained by sport rules alone, because the sports are heavily represented in pretraining. It is better explained by a combination of heuristic priors, low-resolution social cues in volleyball, and failure to compose gaze with pose. But the claim that models lack social inference is not fully adjudicated until fixed-heuristic baselines and artifact-only controls are reported.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
For this audit, RLVR means reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: the reward is a deterministic function of held-out benchmark outcomes, not a human preference score. Three counterfactual vetoes can falsify overstrong claims.

Veto 1: Artifact-only leakage. Train a classifier or reward model on the inpainted image patch, players-masked image, or background texture, and evaluate top-1 cell prediction. If the artifact-only model exceeds the uniform baseline by a pre-specified margin with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval, then the benchmark is leaking ball location through inpainting or compression artifacts, and the social-inference claim is vetoed. Prediction: because the authors manually checked for “remaining ball shadows or artifacts” [PAGE 3], artifact-only top-1 should be near the uniform expectation of approximately \(\mathbb{E}[|G_i|]/60\), about 3.5–4.0% given average spans of 2.08–2.42 cells [PAGE 4, Table 1]. If artifact-only top-1 is materially above that, re-inpainting and re-evaluation are required.

Veto 2: Fixed-heuristic sufficiency. Evaluate deterministic policies: uniform over 60 cells, center-window only, nearest-player only, and nearest-player plus center prior. If the best fixed heuristic matches model accuracy and distributional metrics within confidence intervals, then model performance is explained by spatial priors, not social cue composition. Prediction: because “52.2% of ground truth balls are near players” and center-window ground-truth mass is 36.3–56.3% [PAGE 6], nearest-player and center policies should be competitive in soccer and basketball. In volleyball, the near-player heuristic should degrade because the ball is “rarely in contact with players” [PAGE 6], which matches the paper’s finding that models struggle most in volleyball. If models are not significantly above the best fixed heuristic, the “social inference” interpretation is vetoed.
