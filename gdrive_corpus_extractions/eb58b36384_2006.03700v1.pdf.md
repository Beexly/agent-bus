The source builds a multi-hop inferential chain from raw pedestrian motion to a behavioral label called “leadership.” The first hop is kinematic: head positions are recorded at 60 Hz with a 16-camera infrared-reflective system, reduced to the xy-plane, filtered with a forward-backward fourth-order Butterworth filter, then differentiated to obtain speed and heading [PAGE 5]. The second hop is directional alignment: for pedestrians i and j, the cosine between i’s heading at time t and j’s heading at delayed time t+τ is computed as \(h_{ij}(t,\tau)\), a dimensionless quantity bounded in \([-1,1]\) [PAGE 6]. The third hop is temporal smoothing: \(h_{ij}\) is averaged over a symmetric window \(2\omega\Delta t\), producing the time-dependent delayed directional correlation \(C^d_{ij}(t,\tau)\) [PAGE 6]. The fourth hop is leadership assignment: i is said to lead j at t if the delay \(\tau_{ij}(t)\) maximizing \(C^d_{ij}(t,\tau)\) is positive [PAGE 6]. The speed analogue uses absolute speed differences and minimization, with a shorter window because the cited human visual-locomotor delay to speed changes is about 500 ms [PAGE 7]. The fifth hop aggregates positive-delay time into an individual leadership index and into directed network weights \(w_{i,j}\in[0,1]\) [PAGE 7]. The sixth hop applies mixed ANOVA and post hoc tests to group trials by position and participant [PAGE 3].

The gray-zone audit is crucial. A positive delay in \(C^d\) or \(C^s\) is evidence of temporal precedence and alignment, not proof of intention, authority, or causal control. The source itself frames “leader” operationally: “a leader is defined as the participant whose heading direction influences the others the most, in the sense that the other participants match their headings with that of the leader after a time delay corresponding to the natural human visual-locomotor delay” [PAGE 6]. That definition is kinematic and delay-based. It cannot distinguish a true initiator from a pedestrian who happens to move first because of a shared external cue, or from a follower whose motion is visually salient. The paper’s strongest contextual result is that position matters: a three-way Mixed ANOVA found a significant main effect of Position, \(F(3,117)=19.865\), \(p &lt; 0.05\), \(\eta^2=0.337\), while Condition and Condition × Position were not significant [PAGE 3]. The source concludes: “Thus, the factor Position alone dominates percent leadership” [PAGE 3]. That is a statistical dominance claim over the measured index, not a claim that position is the only causal factor in all walking groups.

The individual-level result is narrower but important: in direction-change trials for Group 2, a one-way ANOVA found a main effect, and “pedestrian P2.4 became the leader independently of his/her position in the group” [PAGE 3]. The paper attributes this to “a characteristic behaviour, the tendency to initiate a change by acting first” [PAGE 3]. Yet the explanatory mechanism is visual: on direction changes P2.4 “often moved into the peripheral field of view of other group members,” whereas on speed changes P2.4 “remained directly behind the front row, out of view” [PAGE 3]. This is a post hoc field-of-view account, not a direct gaze or visibility measurement. The audit therefore separates three layers: (1) robust statistical pattern, front-row advantage; (2) trial-specific participant effect, P2.4 in Group 2 direction trials; (3) explanatory hypothesis, peripheral visual coupling. Only layer 1 has strong model-based statistical support. Layer 2 is supported by a group-specific ANOVA. Layer 3 is plausible but under-instrumented because the study records head position, not gaze direction or field-of-view occupancy.

Additional breakdown regimes are metric and design related. If \(\|v_i(t)\|=0\) or \(\|v_j(t+\tau)\|=0\), the heading unit vector is singular, so \(h_{ij}\) and \(C^d_{ij}\) are undefined at standstill. If the argmax delay is non-unique or broad, leader labels are sensitive to tie-breaking and window length. The speed correlation \(C^s_{ij}\) has units of m/s, not dimensionless, because it averages absolute speed differences. The leadership index is a percentage of time, not a probability, and the network weights are normalized fractions, not bits. The design has only two groups of four participants, 27 trials per group, and sequence/IPD were collapsed after no IPD effects were found [PAGE 5]. These are boundary conditions on generalization, especially to evacuation crowds or multi-week teams.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s primary operators are kinematic vectors, delayed cosine alignment, delayed absolute-speed mismatch, temporal-window averaging, delay-extremum selection, percentage-time aggregation, directed network weighting, and mixed ANOVA. I preserve the source’s units and page citations.

Let \(v_k(t)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) be pedestrian k’s planar velocity in m s\(^{-1}\). The heading unit vector is
\[
u_k(t)=\frac{v_k(t)}{\|v_k(t)\|},
\]
which is dimensionless and singular when \(\|v_k(t)\|=0\). The delayed directional cosine is, from [PAGE 6],
\[
h_{ij}(t,\tau)=\frac{v_i(t)}{\|v_i(t)\|}\cdot\frac{v_j(t+\tau)}{\|v_j(t+\tau)\|}\in[-1,1],
\]
dimensionless, where \(\tau\) is a time delay in seconds. The time-dependent delayed directional correlation is
\[
C^d_{ij}(t,\tau)=\frac{1}{2\omega+1}\sum_{k=-\omega}^{\omega}h_{ij}(t+k\Delta t,\tau),
\]
also dimensionless. The source states \(\Delta t=1/60\,\mathrm{Hz}^{-1}\), sets \(\omega=40\), and notes \(2\omega\Delta t>1\,\mathrm{s}\), giving a symmetric window of about \(1.33\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 6]. The human visual-locomotor delay to heading change is cited as approximately \(1000\,\mathrm{ms}\) [PAGE 6]. Pedestrian i leads j at time t if
\[
\tau^d_{ij}(t)=\arg\max_{\tau} C^d_{ij}(t,\tau)
\]
is positive; otherwise i is interpreted as following j [PAGE 6].

For speed, the source defines
\[
\Delta s_{ij}(t,\tau)=\left|\|v_i(t)\|-\|v_j(t+\tau)\|\right|,
\]
with units m s\(^{-1}\), and
\[
C^s_{ij}(t,\tau)=\frac{1}{2\omega+1}\sum_{k=-\omega}^{\omega}\Delta s_{ij}(t+k\Delta t,\tau),
\]
also in m s\(^{-1}\) [PAGE 7]. Here \(\omega=20\), so the window is \(2\omega\Delta t\approx0.667\,\mathrm{s}\), based on the cited approximately \(500\,\mathrm{ms}\) visual-locomotor delay to speed change [PAGE 7]. Pedestrian i leads j at time t if
\[
\tau^s_{ij}(t)=\arg\min_{\tau} C^s_{ij}(t,\tau)
\]
is positive, and follows if negative [PAGE 7]. The asymmetry between heading and speed metrics is explicit: heading uses a cosine and maximization; speed uses absolute difference and minimization.

The individual leadership index is defined as “the overall average percentage of time for which the computed \(\tau_{ij}(t)\) is positive” [PAGE 7]. Formally, for a given pair and trial,
\[
L_{i\to j}=100\cdot\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \mathbf{1}\{\tau_{ij}(t)>0\}\,dt,
\]
with units percent, and the paper aggregates this over pairs and trials as its reported percent leadership. The leadership index is a time-proportion, not a probability distribution and not an information-theoretic quantity. Network reconstruction uses the edge weight \(w_{i,j}\in[0,1]\), “the normalised percentage of time in which pedestrian i leads pedestrian j” [PAGE 7]. The source then applies data processing inequality style refinement: links with null weights are discarded, and for triplets i, j, k, if \(w_{i,k}\) is lower than both \(w_{i,j}\) and \(w_{j,k}\), then \(w_{i,k}\) is set to zero [PAGE 7].

The statistical operator is a three-way Mixed ANOVA on percent leadership with 2 Conditions, 4 Positions, and 3 IPDs; Condition and IPD were treated as between-subject factors, Position as within-subject, and Group as a random effect [PAGE 3]. The source reports Position \(F(3,117)=19.865\), \(p &lt; 0.05\), \(\eta^2=0.337\); Condition \(F(1,39)=1.334\), \(p=0.255\), \(\eta^2=0.033\); Condition × Position \(F(3,117)=0.196\), \(p=0.899\), \(\eta^2=0.005\); IPD \(F(2,39)=0.133\), \(p=0.876\), \(\eta^2=0.007\); IPD × Position \(F(6,117)=0.906\), \(p=0.493\), \(\eta^2=0.044\); and three-way interaction \(F(6,117)=1.301\), \(p=0.262\), \(\eta^2=0.063\) [PAGE 3]. The source concludes: “Thus, the factor Position alone dominates percent leadership” [PAGE 3]. Bonferroni post hoc tests found front versus back differences significant at \(p &lt; 0.05\), while front-front and back-back differences had \(p=1\) [PAGE 3]. For individual behavior, a one-way ANOVA by participant found a main effect only in direction-change trials for Group 2, \(F(3,24)=4.663\), \(p &lt; 0.05\), \(\eta^2=0.368\) [PAGE 3].

Breakdown and singularity regimes are explicit. Heading correlation breaks at zero velocity because the unit heading vector is undefined. The delay argmax or argmin can be non-unique or unstable when correlation surfaces are flat, and the fixed windows assume a stationary delay near 1000 ms for heading and 500 ms for speed. The speed metric’s units are m s\(^{-1}\), so it is not directly comparable to the dimensionless directional correlation. The design has only two groups of four, and IPD/sequence effects were collapsed after no IPD effects were found [PAGE 5]. The study records head position, not gaze, so the field-of-view explanation for P2.4 is an inference from position and trial condition, not a measured visibility operator [PAGE 3]. Finally, no information-theoretic entropy, mutual information, or bits are computed; the network weights are normalized time fractions.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage runs from animal collective motion to human walking groups, then back to human applications such as evacuation. The introduction states that leadership has been studied mostly in animal groups and that “only a small proportion of informed individuals is needed to guide the group behaviour” as group size grows [PAGE 1]. It then positions human work in consensus decision making, orchestra conduction, dance, and pedestrian walking [PAGE 1]. The method is explicitly adapted from a time-dependent cross-correlation metric used for trawling bats [PAGE 2], and the paper claims it is applied “for the first time to a human group in order to predict leader-follower patterns” [PAGE 3]. That lineage matters because the metric was designed for delayed biosonar or movement coordination, not for human intention or social role. The walking-group application inherits the bat-method’s assumptions: a characteristic delay, local visual coupling, and pairwise temporal precedence.

The retrocausal genealogy also contains a human-factors lineage: local visual coupling between neighbors, heading alignment, speed control in pedestrian following, and collective motion [PAGE 1, PAGE 3]. The paper’s front-row effect follows directly from this lineage: “one of the pedestrians in the front row to act as the leader because they can influence participants in the back row (i.e. the back row is visually coupled to the front row), but not vice versa” [PAGE 2]. The P2.4 case adds a personal-factor lineage: a participant who “assumed a leadership role more than 60% of the time, initiating a change in direction irrespective of their position in the group” and who, by turning first, “exploited the mechanism of collective motion to seize the leadership, a self-appointed leader” [PAGE 3]. This is the paper’s bridge from contextual position to personal locomotor style.

Multi-week traps are serious because the experiment is not longitudinal. The test session lasted “about 40 min” [PAGE 5], with one practice trial and 27 test trials per group [PAGE 5]. There is no multi-week retention, no role stability across days, no team formation, and no measurement of verbal communication or social history. A reader could mistakenly convert a trial-level leadership index into a stable trait. The paper does not support that: it reports group-specific and condition-specific effects, especially the Group 2 direction-change result for P2.4, and it explicitly says the influence “is absent in the speed-change condition” [PAGE 3]. Another trap is treating the front-row effect as a universal social hierarchy rather than a visual-coupling geometry. The source itself says position dominates the measured index [PAGE 3], but that is within a square configuration with 1, 2, or 4 m IPD and shuffled positions [PAGE 2]. A third trap is collapsing sequence and IPD after no IPD effects, then overgeneralizing to all distances and turn sequences [PAGE 5]. A fourth trap is the post hoc field-of-view explanation: P2.4’s directional influence is attributed to peripheral visibility, while speed influence is absent because P2.4 “remained directly behind the front row, out of view” [PAGE 3]. That is a testable visibility hypothesis, not a multi-week social role. A fifth trap is evacuation extrapolation: the paper cites evacuation as motivation [PAGE 1] and notes relevance where “verbal communication among group members is limited” [PAGE 4], but it does not run an evacuation experiment, a stress manipulation, or a crowd-density manipulation. The genealogy therefore supports a narrow claim: delayed visual-locomotor coupling can generate front-row and occasional first-mover leadership in small walking groups. It does not support stable multi-week leadership or crisis-role persistence without new evidence.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind reading of the paper must be labeled as an interpretive layer because the study measures kinematics, not mental-state inference. The behavioral loop is still recursive in structure: each walker must represent a neighbor’s likely heading or speed from prior motion, then adjust; a first mover can become a leader if others represent and follow that change after the visual-locomotor delay. The paper’s own language is behavioral: P2.4 had “the tendency to initiate a change by acting first” and became “a self-appointed leader” by exploiting collective motion [PAGE 3]. That is a first-order behavioral strategy, but it can support higher-order coordination: followers infer that the first mover’s turn is informative, and the first mover can anticipate that visibility and timing will make the turn propagative.

Crisis habit regression is the risk that under stress, people default to habitual visual following rather than deliberative leadership selection. The paper motivates evacuation applications [PAGE 1] and notes that in emergencies some people may have higher leadership probability because of attitude, charisma, confidence, or ability to act first [PAGE 1]. But the walking data show a simpler mechanism: front-row walkers are visually coupled to back-row walkers, while the reverse is not true [PAGE 2]. In a crisis, that habitual front-row following could regress to following whoever is visible, even if that person lacks global knowledge. The counter-trap is to assume that a designated or expert leader will be followed from any position. The P2.4 result warns that a back-row leader can influence direction changes only when entering peripheral view; on speed changes, P2.4 “remained directly behind the front row, out of view,” and the influence was absent [PAGE 3]. Thus the counter-trap is positional invisibility: a legitimate leader who is behind the front row may be behaviorally silent unless they move into view or use another channel.

The clock layer constrains both regression and counter-trap. The heading metric uses a window around a cited 1000 ms visual-locomotor delay, with \(\omega=40\) and \(2\omega\Delta t>1\,\mathrm{s}\) [PAGE 6]. The speed metric uses a shorter window around a cited 500 ms delay, with \(\omega=20\) [PAGE 7]. A recursive follower cannot infer and act instantaneously; the measured leader-follower relation is delay-structured. If a crisis intervention assumes faster propagation than the 0.5 to 1 s coupling delay, it may misclassify normal following as failed leadership. If it assumes a stable leader across both heading and speed, it may miss the condition-specific result: P2.4’s directional leadership did not appear in speed-change trials [PAGE 3]. The clock also creates a measurement trap: a fixed window can label a slow reactor as a follower and a fast reactor as a leader even when both are responding to a third cue. The source’s operational definition is explicitly delay-based, not intention-based [PAGE 6]. Therefore recursive theory of mind should be used as a hypothesis about visible first-mover inference, not as a measured mentalizing capacity.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The square configuration and visual coupling are not optional context; they are the mechanism the paper invokes. Participants were positioned at FL, FR, BL, and BR vertices with 1, 2, or 4 m IPD, and positions were shuffled between trials [PAGE 2]. The paper’s expected physical effect is that front-row walkers can influence back-row walkers because the back row is visually coupled to the front row, “but not vice versa” [PAGE 2]. Any interpretation that ignores front-back visibility is vetoed by this geometry. The P2.4 exception is also physically bounded: P2.4 “often moved into the peripheral field of view” on direction trials but stayed “directly behind the front row, out of view” on speed trials [PAGE 3]. The study records head position, not gaze, so a stronger claim about exactly what each walker saw is not verifiable from the source. The physical veto is therefore: visual-coupling claims must respect position, occlusion, and field-of-view plausibility; correlation alone cannot override those constraints.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The participants had “normal or corrected vision, and none reported a motor impairment” [PAGE 4]. That exclusion matters: the observed leadership index depends on walking, turning, speed modulation, and visual locomotor delay. If a population has motor impairment, visual field loss, or gait instability, the same correlation metric may not identify the same leader-follower structure. The biomechanical measurement layer also includes 60 Hz head tracking, 3D recording with only xy analyzed, fourth-order Butterworth filtering, 1.0 Hz cutoff before speed, and 0.6 Hz cutoff before heading [PAGE 5]. These filters reduce gait oscillations and tracker error, but they also smooth out high-frequency movement that could matter for fast reactions. The cited delays, about 1000 ms for heading and 500 ms for speed [PAGE 6, PAGE 7], are biomechanical constraints on interpretation: a leadership claim that assumes instantaneous following is vetoed. A claim that treats speed leadership and heading leadership as the same operator is also vetoed because the source uses different metrics, windows, and extrema [PAGE 6, PAGE 7].

Weather-layer veto. The source provides no weather variables and no outdoor exposure. The experiment was carried out “at Brown University in a large hall with a 14m × 20m tracking area marked on the floor” [PAGE 5]. There is no wind, rain, temperature, surface friction, or visibility-weather manipulation. Therefore any weather-based explanation of the observed leadership patterns is non-identifiable and should be vetoed. If one wanted to extrapolate to outdoor evacuation or street crowds, weather could alter speed, heading stability, clothing, surface friction, and visual exposure, but that would require a new experiment. The cross-layer adjudication is thus: physical occlusion and geometry can veto visual-coupling claims; biomechanical exclusion and filter/delay assumptions can veto instantaneous or universal-leadership claims; weather cannot be invoked at all from this indoor dataset. The valid claim is the one the source supports: in a small indoor walking group, position and individual locomotor behavior both contribute to a delay-based leadership index, with front-row and peripheral-visibility effects [PAGE 1, PAGE 3].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
For an RLVR-style audit, each counterfactual should have a pre-registered prediction and a verifiable reward: the analysis passes only if held-out or perturbed data satisfy the prediction; otherwise the explanatory claim is vetoed. Three scenarios follow from the source’s own operators.

1. Occlusion and field-of-view counterfactual. Manipulate visibility between rows, either with an opaque barrier, a gaze-forward instruction, or a controlled head-mounted occlusion, and recompute the same \(C^d_{ij}\) and \(C^s_{ij}\) indices. Prediction: if the front-row advantage is caused by back-row visual coupling to the front row [PAGE 2], then blocking that coupling should reduce or eliminate the FL/FR advantage in both heading and speed conditions. Prediction for the P2.4 case: if P2.4’s directional leadership depends on entering the peripheral field of view [PAGE 3], then blocking peripheral visibility should remove P2.4’s back-row direction-change advantage, while leaving any true front-row positional effect intact. Veto condition: if the front-row advantage and P2.4’s back-row directional advantage persist under full occlusion, the visual-coupling explanation is falsified. This is verifiable by comparing leadership-index effect sizes and Bonferroni-adjusted front-versus-back contrasts against a pre-registered null.

2. Delay-sign and window-perturbation counterfactual. Recompute leadership after phase-randomizing trajectories, shuffling time delays, or constraining the delay search to the wrong sign or a mismatched window. Prediction: true delay-based following should peak near positive delays of about 1000 ms for heading and about 500 ms for speed, consistent with the source’s windows and cited delays [PAGE 6, PAGE 7]. If the original leader labels are genuine, then phase-randomized or sign-flipped data should yield leadership indices near chance, and the original indices should exceed the permutation null. Veto condition: if leadership assignments remain unchanged when delays are randomized or when the sign of \(\tau\) is inverted, then the metric is not identifying delayed following; it is tracking some other shared motion or smoothing artifact. This scenario directly tests the operational definition “a leader is defined as the participant whose heading direction influences the others the most” through delay matching [PAGE 6].

3. Participant-trait versus position-swap counterfactual. Repeat the experiment with new four-person groups, or analytically swap P2.4’s position across many held-out trials, and test whether individual identity predicts leadership after controlling for position. Prediction: if P2.4’s effect is a personal “tendency to initiate a change by acting first” [PAGE 3], then that participant should show elevated direction-change leadership across positions and across trials, but the effect should be condition-specific because it was absent in speed-change trials [PAGE 3]. Prediction for new groups: some first-mover individuals may emerge, but there is no basis in the source for stable multi-week or universal leader identity. Veto condition: if leadership is fully explained by position in held-out trials, the personal-factor claim is unnecessary; if a participant’s advantage appears in both heading and speed despite being out of view, the field-of-view account is incomplete. A verifiable reward can be defined as out-of-sample prediction of held-out trial leadership from participant identity plus position, with a pre-registered threshold above the position-only model.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
