Level L1: the product signals trustworthiness through premium command-center aesthetics, source cues, and confidence-safe language rather than casino/tout affect [PAGE 1]. Level L2: the user infers the product's model of the user—whether the product treats them as a prospect to be converted, a PRO/ELITE member to be retained, or a mark to be sold certainty. Level L3: the product adjusts copy and compare presentation to the user's inferred state without exposing source weights or private methods [PAGE 1].

Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap. Under uncertainty, users regress to a crisis habit: seeking guaranteed outcomes, fake certainty, or tout-style assurance [PAGE 1: no fake certainty, no guaranteed-betting language]. The counter-trap is to supply confidence-safe language paired with last-verified/source cues, which satisfies the user's need for orientation without violating the no-hype-without-proof rule [PAGE 1]. Failure mode: if confidence-safe language becomes too vague, the user regresses further and exits to less responsible sources; if it becomes too specific, it risks method leakage or implied guarantees.

Clock constraint. The 10-second clarity deadline functions as a hard temporal boundary: T_understand <= 10 s [PAGE 1]. If the clock expires before comprehension, trust does not form and PRO/ELITE value is not communicated. Recursive belief update can be expressed probabilistically: posterior trust P(H | D_t) ∝ P(D_t | H) P(H), where H is the hypothesis that the product is trustworthy and D_t is the sequence of interface signals observed by time t [probability units dimensionless]. Each session updates P(H); repeated clarity failures drive P(H) toward zero and increase churn. The design must therefore maximize the likelihood of trustworthy signals D_t within the 10-second window while keeping I(M; C_public) ≈ 0 [bit] to avoid leaking the model that generates those signals [PAGE 1].
