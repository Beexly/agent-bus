<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits at the end of a crashworthiness-to-sports lineage. Reference [1] is the All Japan Judo Federation safety instruction (3rd edition, 2011, pp. 5-9). Reference [2] is Zhang, Yang, Kin (2004), “A Proposed Injury Threshold for Mild Traumatic Brain Injury,” J. Biomech. Eng. Trans. ASME Vol. 126, pp. 226-236, which supplies the mTBI threshold logic. References [3] and [7] are Willinger & Baumgartner (2003), “Human head tolerance limits to specific injury mechanisms,” Int. J. of Crashworthiness Vol. 8 Issue 6, pp. 605-617, the source of the 7.8 kPa and -135 kPa tolerance values. Reference [4] is Majoux, Baumgartner, Deck, Willinger (2008), “Head Injury Prediction Capability of the HIC, HIP, SMon and ULP criteria,” Accident Analysis & Prevention Vol. 40 Issue 3, pp. 1135-1148. Reference [5] is Declercq & Willinger (2008), “Improved head injury criteria based on head FE model,” Int. J. of Crashworthiness Vol. 13 No. 6, pp. 667-678. Reference [6] is the Nihon University American football team, source of the video-recorded collision case [PAGE 5].

This genealogy matters because the thresholds were derived for automotive crash dummies and human head FE models under impact conditions qualitatively different from judo tatami or football helmetless collision. Transplanting them creates a multi-week trap: [PAGE 1] documents that over Heisei 10-22 (14 years) there were 167 school-sport accidents under school management, with 57 deaths and 110 disabilities, and 90% of deaths were head trauma. Acute subdural hematoma can declare itself only after a time lag—vomiting and headache appear post-accident [PAGE 1]—so the clinical clock extends from minutes at impact to days or weeks of observation. The paper’s own judo epidemiology shows 32 serious head injuries in 2003-2010, half occipital, frequently from o-gari throws [PAGE 2]. The trap is that a biomechanical model computed at t = 0-35 ms [PAGE 4] is being used to justify return-to-play or activity-cessation decisions whose clinical consequences unfold over weeks, yet the model contains no cumulative damage variable, no second-impact susceptibility term, and no delayed hematoma evolution equation.

Counter-trap within the lineage: the references are explicit that these are tolerance *limits* and prediction *capabilities*, not individual diagnostic criteria. Treating a population 50% risk threshold (7.8 kPa) as an individual clearance line is a retrospective overreach that the source literature does not authorize.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: In contact sports, the default habit after a head strike is rapid subjective assessment—does the athlete lose consciousness, can they walk, do they report feeling well?—followed by quick return to play. [PAGE 1] states that concussion shows no clear pathological findings on CT or MRI, and that deciding when to stop activity or permit return is extremely difficult with no effective means available. This absence of visible biomarkers regresses decision-making to pre-injury habits: athletes minimize symptoms, coaches prioritize availability, and the “invisible” mTBI is played through.

Counter-trap: The FE simulation introduces an objective counter-trap by asserting that von Mises stress and CSF negative pressure exceed thresholds even when the athlete is ambulatory. If the model is trusted, the athlete’s subjective “I feel fine” is overridden by \sigma_{vm}^{max} = 14.1 kPa > 7.8 kPa and |p_{CSF}^{min}| = 324 kPa > 135 kPa [PAGE 3]-[PAGE 4]. The counter-trap is to make removal-from-play contingent on biomechanical threshold crossing rather than self-report.

Clock: There are two incompatible clocks. The impact transient resolves in milliseconds (LS-DYNA time axis 0-35 ms in Fig. 7-8, [PAGE 4]), whereas clinical classification uses GCS/AIS at 24 h [PAGE 6] and delayed subdural symptoms may emerge days later [PAGE 1]. The model therefore answers “what happened at impact” but not “when is it safe to return.” A recursive theory of mind must track that the athlete’s internal state (symptom reporting) is regressing toward habitual play while the external biomechanical state (tissue stress) remains in a high-risk regime, and the 24 h clinical clock is too slow to prevent practice during the vulnerable window.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The judo impact occurs on a tatami indoors; the American football case is reconstructed from video of a practice collision [PAGE 5]. Neither scenario admits weather variables (precipitation, wind, ambient temperature), so meteorological veto does not apply. However, the physical surface compliance is unmodeled in detail: tatami foam stiffness, mat age, moisture content, and temperature-dependent modulus are absent from the LS-DYNA boundary conditions. Because stress scales with impact duration and surface deformation, an unmodeled mat-property change can veto the absolute 14.1 kPa / -324 kPa predictions.

Biomechanical layer veto (strongest): Three structural vetoes invalidate direct transfer. (i) Sex/size mismatch: the head FE model is built from an adult female MRI dataset [PAGE 2], yet it is applied to judoka and American-football players who are predominantly male; no scaling law for skull thickness, brain mass, or neck stiffness is reported. (ii) Single-body vs. two-body: the judo simulation models only the falling judoka (one MADYMO human model); the thrower’s mass, grip forces, and reaction are explicitly deferred to future work [PAGE 2], so the input head kinematics omit interaction dynamics. (iii) Helmet absence: the American football model currently uses the head model only, with helmet modeling explicitly planned as future work [PAGE 5]; helmet padding would lengthen impact duration and reduce peak acceleration, likely lowering \sigma_{vm} below the 7.8-18 kPa concussion band. Until a sex-matched, helmeted, two-body model is validated against instrumented data, the kPa outputs are not transferable to individual athletes.

Weather veto proof: Because both case domains are sheltered (indoor dojo; recorded practice), there is no rain, wind, or temperature gradient to adjudicate. The only environmental veto is the playing surface itself, treated as a fixed boundary. The absence of weather dependence is a robustness feature for cross-case comparison but does not cure the biomechanical vetoes above.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
