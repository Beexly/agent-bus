Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock.

Recursive theory of mind here models an observer reasoning about another system’s tendency to return to habitual baseline after a crisis-level deviation. “Crisis habit regression” is the hypothesis that after an extreme shock, behavior/stature/performance reverts toward the individual’s or population’s habitual mean at rate β per observation interval (Galton’s β = 1/3 across generations; analogously β per interval within a series). The observer’s model of the system includes the system’s model of the observer: if the system expects the observer to correct for crisis deviations, the observed reversion rate is itself strategic.

The counter-trap is the inverse error: observing one extreme realization and predicting the next will be even more extreme, or treating regression-to-the-mean as evidence that the baseline has shifted permanently. Galton’s text blocks this by asserting the mid-parent “is not likely to be more exceptional than the son” [PAGE 1] and that exceptional sons arise mostly from mediocre parents [PAGE 1]; the counter-trap would claim the opposite direction of surprise. A second-order counter-trap is to assume that because regression is real, every extreme must be followed by correction in the very next interval, ignoring that the conditional expectation E[y|x] = βx permits the next draw to remain extreme with probability governed by the residual variance σ_Y²(1−ρ²) [m²], not by a deterministic pull.

The clock is the temporal operator: under stationarity, E[deviation at t+k | deviation at t] = β^k × deviation at t, with β dimensionless and k measured in intervals (generations, or weekly observation slots if the process were applicable). The clock stops or breaks when β is time-varying (secular changes in stature), when ρ → 1 (singular, no regression), or when the process leaves the linear Gaussian regime (bounds, truncation, measurement error). Recursive theory of mind therefore specifies: observer believes system regresses at β; system knows observer believes this; observer corrects predictions accordingly; the stable fixed point of this recursion is the population mean P, but only if the clock assumptions (A1–A5) hold across the recursion depth. Crisis habits that violate homoscedasticity—e.g., stress-induced variance inflation—break the clock even if the mean still points at P.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

The stature variable is bounded by physical law, so any regression coefficient must survive cross-layer veto checks. Physical layer: human stature has a species-level ceiling and a viability floor; linear regression E[y|x] = βx cannot hold arbitrarily far from P because deviations cannot exceed skeletal and hormonal limits (e.g., growth-plate closure, GH/IGF-1 axis saturation). As |x| approaches those bounds, the conditional variance σ²_{y|x} = σ_Y²(1−ρ²) [m²] cannot remain homoscedastic; truncation at the physical boundary induces skewness and violates (A1)/(A4), vetoing a constant one-third slope in the tails.

Biomechanical layer: stature is the integrated output of longitudinal bone growth, epiphyseal plate activity, nutrition, and disease during childhood and adolescence. Mid-parent stature is a proxy for shared genotype and shared childhood environment, not a mechanical lever. If parental stature differs because one parent experienced famine or illness (environmental) rather than genetic potential, the parent–offspring correlation ρ is attenuated and the regression slope no longer estimates heritability; a weather-driven or famine-driven cohort shift (e.g., historical nutritional crises altering mean stature by centimeters) changes μ and σ across cohorts, violating stationarity (A2) and vetoing cross-cohort application of Galton’s 1/3.

Weather layer: short-term meteorological variation has no direct biomechanical effect on adult stature; the veto is indirect. Weather affects stature only through long-horizon channels (agricultural yield → childhood nutrition → growth), operating over seasons to years, not within a single parent–son measurement. Thus a model that regresses son stature on mid-parent stature while conditioning on concurrent weather commits a layer error: weather is not a contemporaneous operator on stature deviations [m] but, at most, a lagged cohort-level shifter of μ. Any claimed weather-by-regression interaction must demonstrate a pathway through developmental nutrition; otherwise it is vetoed as a category error.

Adjudication rule: Galton’s linear regression with β = 1/3 is admissible only in the interior of the stature distribution, under Gaussian assumptions, with independence across parents, no truncation, and stable cohort parameters. At the physical bounds (giantism/dwarfism extremes), under selection truncation, under assortative mating, or across cohorts with different nutritional histories, the coefficient is vetoed and replaced by a bounded, possibly nonlinear, cohort-specific conditional mean.
