Define formalism.

Let G=(V,E) be a directed graph with |V|=N nodes (web pages) and edge set E (hyperlinks). Adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N}, A_{ij}=1 if a hyperlink exists from node j to node i (column convention). Out-degree d_j = ∑_{i=1}^N A_{ij}. The column-stochastic transition matrix S ∈ R^{N×N} is defined by:

S_{ij} = 
{ A_{ij}/d_j, if d_j > 0;
  1/N,         if d_j = 0 (dangling-node repair). }

Each column sums to one: ∑_i S_{ij} = 1, so S is a stochastic matrix on the probability simplex Δ^{N-1} = { r ∈ R^N : r_i ≥ 0, ∑_i r_i = 1 }.

PageRank vector r ∈ Δ^{N-1} satisfies the fixed-point equation:

r = α S r + (1 − α) v,

where α ∈ (0,1) is the dimensionless damping factor (random-surfing continuation probability), v ∈ Δ^{N-1} is the personalization/teleportation vector (often v_i = 1/N), and (1−α) is the teleportation probability. Equivalently, r is the stationary distribution π of the Markov chain with transition matrix P = α S + (1−α) 1 v^T, where 1 is the all-ones column vector. P is strictly positive if v > 0 and α < 1, hence irreducible and aperiodic; by the Perron–Frobenius theorem, a unique stationary distribution exists and power iteration r^{(k+1)} = P r^{(k)} converges geometrically at rate governed by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus |λ_2|.

Units and dimensions: r_i is dimensionless probability in [0,1]; ∑_i r_i = 1 exactly; α dimensionless; v dimensionless; time is measured in discrete surfer steps k ∈ N; mixing time τ_mix(ε) = min{ k : ||P^k(x,·) − π||_TV ≤ ε } has units of steps and depends on α, N, and graph expansion.

Assumptions: (A1) the web graph is observable and static during computation; (A2) dangling nodes are repaired by uniform teleportation; (A3) α < 1 ensures strict positivity of P; (A4) the personalization vector v is fixed and known.

Breakdown/singularity regimes: (R1) α → 1: P may become reducible or periodic; dangling nodes with d_j=0 cause substochastic columns and probability leakage unless repaired; no unique stationary distribution if multiple closed communicating classes exist. (R2) N → ∞ with power-law out-degree distribution: numerical underflow for low-rank pages and hub dominance distort finite-precision power iteration. (R3) Directed acyclic subgraphs act as absorbing states when α = 1, concentrating all rank mass in sinks. (R4) If the graph is not strongly connected and v has zero entries in some components, those components receive no teleportation mass and their rank remains undefined under the standard formulation.

Crisis habit regression. When a search-ranking system enters crisis—sudden spam campaign, index corruption, freshness emergency, or adversarial link-farm detection—operators and algorithms tend to regress to lower-complexity heuristics: raw in-degree counting, keyword density, or manual whitelisting. This is habit regression because the system abandons the eigenvector fixed point (computationally expensive, globally coupled) in favor of local, computationally cheap standing proxies that were dominant before eigenvector methods became standard.

Counter-trap. Sophisticated adversaries anticipate this regression. If they expect the system to fall back to in-degree ranking during a crisis, they pre-construct link cycles and doorway pages that maximize in-degree without organic authority. If they expect keyword-density fallback, they seed term-stuffed mirror networks. The counter-trap is therefore recursive: the adversary models the operator's model of the adversary, and optimizes edge creation for the regressive heuristic rather than for the eigenvector equilibrium. PageRank's resistance depends on whether α and the teleportation vector v can be adjusted faster than the adversary can redeploy link structures.

Clock. Two temporal clocks govern this recursion. The mixing clock τ_mix(ε) measures how many random-surfing steps are required for the rank vector to forget its initialization and converge to π; for the web graph, τ_mix scales with graph diameter and conductance, typically on the order of dozens to hundreds of iterations. The crawl clock T_crawl measures the interval between index refreshes (historically weekly during the “Google Dance" era). If T_crawl ≫ τ_mix in wall-clock terms, the ranking is stale relative to the web's edge dynamics; if a crisis persists for fewer weeks than T_crawl, the system may operate on a crisis-heuristic for an entire refresh cycle before the eigenvector recomputation restores equilibrium. The multi-week mismatch between T_crawl and adversarial iteration speed is the decisive vulnerability: the rank vector is a slow state variable in a fast adversarial environment.
