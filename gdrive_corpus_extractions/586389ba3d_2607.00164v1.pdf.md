<THINK_TRACE>
Audit trace: the problem is not epistemic answer correctness but aleatoric forecasting. The label \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) is one Bernoulli draw from a conditional rate \(\eta(X)=\Pr(Y=1\mid X)\), so a per-play reward \(r_y=1-(p-y)^2\) is unbiased for the Brier objective but has irreducible label variance. Two identical public states, one realized win and one realized loss, generate opposite policy gradients; in small GRPO groups this noise can dominate the per-state signal. The paper’s first move is therefore to replace the realized draw with a state-conditioned empirical rate \(\hat p(x)\) estimated from past outcomes, citing that “Rewarding the realized per-play outcome fails, because the single outcome is a noisy target and the policy gradient corrupts the chain of thought” [PAGE 1]. The second move is to stop the gradient from reaching the reasoning tokens, because the paper reports that “The policy gradient passes through the reasoning tokens, so raising the reward on the final probability rewrites the reasoning before it into pseudo-quantitative arguments for ever more extreme numbers” [PAGE 4]. That is a retrocausal corruption: the final probability reward reaches backward into the textual reasoning and changes it.

Multi-hop derivation: proper scoring gives the target \(\eta\), but \(\eta\) is unobserved; the only verifiable object is the empirical rate. The empirical rate is a denoised teacher, but it is still a ceiling: “We call ˆp the teacher, because a model trained on eq. (1) can be no better calibrated than ˆp itself” [PAGE 4]. Therefore calibration to the teacher is not calibration to the full physical process; it is calibration to the public-state conditional. The paper’s strongest empirical result is that a direct 7B model reaches ECE 0.029 against the market reference’s 0.027, while its Brier is 0.144 against 0.136, and “the shortfall is in resolution, not calibration” [PAGE 4]. That distinction matters: reliability is matching stated probabilities to frequencies; resolution is separating states with different outcome rates. The market’s residual edge is therefore not automatically a model-capacity failure; the paper attributes it to “live in-game information that no static input contains” [PAGE 8].

Gray-zone audit: first, the teacher is coarse. The reward bins score margin, time remaining, and one public pre-game market signal, with hierarchical empirical-Bayes shrinkage [PAGE 12]; finer public features such as field position and down do not improve game-level calibration on the 2023 split [PAGE 12]. So the teacher is deliberately smooth, not a mechanistic simulator. Second, the masked model preserves explanation but costs sharpness: direct Brier 0.144 and masked Brier 0.152, with a paired gap of about 0.008 [PAGE 4]. Third, the masked run needs the KL anchor removed because the answer-span gradient is concentrated enough to make the estimator overflow and the loss diverge [PAGE 4]; the learning rate has an inverted-U and over-sharpens at \(4\times10^{-5}\) [PAGE 13]. Fourth, checkpoint selection uses a fixed 128-state held-out sample [PAGE 5], so small-sample selection noise is a real gray zone even though final numbers use full splits. Fifth, season-disjoint splits and a prompt scan guard temporal leakage [PAGE 14], but they do not guard omitted physical, biomechanical, or weather variables. I therefore accept the paper’s public-state calibration claim, the teacher-ceiling claim, and the gradient-reach claim; I do not accept any stronger claim that the model has recovered the full physical outcome process or that the market reference is an absolute information-theoretic ceiling. The correct reading is conditional and layer-specific.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal objects and units. Let \(X\in\mathcal X\) be the public game state, \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) the realized possession-team win, \(p=f_\theta(X)\in[0,1]\) the forecast, and \(\eta(X)=\Pr(Y=1\mid X)\) the true conditional rate. All probabilities are dimensionless in \([0,1]\). The Brier score is
\[
S_B(p,Y)=(p-Y)^2,
\]
in squared-probability units, dimensionless. It is strictly proper, so
\[
\mathbb E[S_B(p,Y)\mid X]=\mathbb E[(p-\eta(X))^2\mid X]+\eta(X)(1-\eta(X)),
\]
with the second term fixed by the aleatoric outcome variance. The unique conditional minimizer is \(p=\eta(X)\) [PAGE 3]. The Murphy partition is reliability minus resolution plus uncertainty; ECE and MCE are computed over ten equal-width bins and are dimensionless [PAGE 6, PAGE 7].

The paper’s reward replaces \(Y\) with the empirical rate:
\[
r=1-(p-\hat p(x))^2,
\]
dimensionless, where \(\hat p(x)\) is the state-conditioned empirical win rate [PAGE 4]. This is proper for \(\hat p\), not for \(\eta\). The empirical-Bayes operator is
\[
\hat p_b=\frac{w_b+M\hat p_{\mathrm{parent}(b)}}{n_b+M},
\]
with \(w_b\) wins, \(n_b\) plays, and pseudocount \(M=25\) plays; the rule is applied hierarchically from the global rate downward [PAGE 12]. The operator is finite when \(n_b=0\) because \(\hat p_b=\hat p_{\mathrm{parent}(b)}\). If \(M=0\), then \(n_b=0\) makes the raw fraction undefined and small \(n_b\) gives Bernoulli variance at most \(1/(4n_b)\), worst near rate \(0.5\). This is the first exact singularity regime: sparse-bin estimation. The paper avoids it with \(M=25\) and parent shrinkage.

Group-relative advantage can be written \(A_i=(r_i-\bar r)/(\sigma_r+\epsilon)\), dimensionless. If \(\sigma_r\to0\), all group rewards are equal and the advantage degenerates; the paper reports that turning off reward scaling collapses resolution [PAGE 12], so scale handling is not cosmetic. The KL-regularized objective is
\[
\mathcal L=-\sum_{t\in\mathcal M}A_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),
\]
where \(\mathcal M\) is the token mask. For full-completion training, \(\mathcal M\) includes reasoning tokens; for the answer-span mask, \(\mathcal M\) is only the final probability span. The second exact breakdown regime is masked training with \(\beta>0\): the paper states that “Masking the gradient needs the KL penalty turned off, because on the few tokens of the answer span the gradient is concentrated enough to make the penalty’s estimator overflow and the loss diverge” [PAGE 4]. The masked model therefore uses \(\beta=0\), and its learning rate peaks at \(3\times10^{-5}\); at \(4\times10^{-5}\) the unanchored update over-sharpens and drives maximum calibration error past \(0.5\) [PAGE 13]. The third breakdown regime is full-completion gradient reach: with the rate reward, full-completion training drives held-out Brier from the base model’s 0.25 to 0.34 and ECE from 0.19 to 0.30 [PAGE 4], while direct or answer-span-masked training holds calibration near the teacher. The fourth regime is teacher bias: because \(\hat p\neq\eta\) in general, a model can be calibrated to \(\hat p\) yet remain miscalibrated for \(\eta\); the paper explicitly notes the teacher ceiling [PAGE 4]. The fifth regime is distribution shift: season-disjoint splits prevent game leakage [PAGE 14], but a season shift in the omitted physical layer can bias \(\hat p\) for the test conditional. The sixth regime is long-horizon or sparse events: the conclusion limits the method to settings “where outcomes are dense and resolved quickly enough to estimate a reliable empirical rate” [PAGE 9].

Assumptions: within-bin exchangeability conditional on coarse public features; stable outcome-rate mapping across training seasons; no live market probability in prompts; resolved outcomes only; and a market reference that is informative but not treated as ground truth. Under these assumptions the operator chain is coherent: \(Y\to\hat p\to r\to\) answer-span gradient \(\to p\). The chain deliberately cuts the path \(\hat p\to r\to\) reasoning tokens. That cut is the paper’s central operator, not an incidental implementation detail.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is a sequence of corrections to proper scoring under optimization pressure. Brier’s 1950 score [PAGE 9] made probability forecasts verifiable; Gneiting and Raftery’s strictly proper scoring framework [PAGE 9] made the conditional rate the unique expectation-minimizer; Murphy’s partition [PAGE 10] separated reliability, resolution, and uncertainty; Guo et al. [PAGE 10] and the reliability-diagram literature [PAGE 10, PAGE 11] made calibration a first-class diagnostic; Efron’s bootstrap [PAGE 11] and Efron–Morris empirical Bayes [PAGE 11] supply the uncertainty and shrinkage tools used here. The modern RLVR branch runs from DeepSeekMath and DeepSeek-R1 [PAGE 9] through GRPO, DAPO, and GPG [PAGE 10], with later work on KL-estimator instability and token-role constraints [PAGE 10]. The forecasting-LLM branch runs from retrieval-and-aggregation forecasters [PAGE 9] through prompting studies [PAGE 9] and outcome-based RL forecasters [PAGE 9], with temporal-leakage warnings [PAGE 10]. The NFL win-probability branch runs from random forests [PAGE 10] through nflWAR and nflfastR [PAGE 10] to the simulation bound of Brill et al. [PAGE 10], which is consistent with the paper’s reading of the residual market edge as structural rather than a capacity failure.

Retrocausally, the key inversion is that a reward computed only at the end of a completion can rewrite the textual path that produced it. The paper’s genealogy of overconfidence says RLHF reward models can prefer confident answers [PAGE 2], RLVR can create an accuracy-calibration gradient conflict [PAGE 2], and GRPO’s group-standard-deviation normalization has been blamed for overconfidence on stochastic outcomes [PAGE 2]. The present result refines that lineage: with a denoised rate target, the normalization is necessary, and “the overconfidence is driven by the gradient on the reasoning rather than by the advantage” [PAGE 2]. That is the retrocausal lesson: the corrupting path is not the scalar advantage alone but the gradient’s reach into the reasoning.

Multi-week traps in this lineage are predictable. Trap one: temporal leakage across seasons, which the paper avoids by training on 2015–2022, selecting on 2023, and testing on 2024 [PAGE 14]. Trap two: using the live market probability as a prompt feature; the paper scanned 40,246 training prompts and the evaluation prompts and found no occurrence [PAGE 14]. Trap three: evaluating on the same season used for checkpoint selection; the paper selects on 2023 and reports on 2024 [PAGE 5]. Trap four: treating the teacher as truth; the teacher is coarse and can be no better than its own bins [PAGE 4, PAGE 12]. Trap five: blending the realized outcome back into the target, which reintroduces label variance and is worse on both Brier and ECE [PAGE 4]. Trap six: keeping the chain-of-thought gradient because the reward is denoised; the paper shows this still decalibrates [PAGE 4]. Trap seven: assuming the masked model dominates; it preserves explanation but loses sharpness relative to direct prediction [PAGE 5]. Trap eight: over-reading the market gap as model incapacity; the paper’s three-way convergence near Brier 0.143–0.144 places the residual gap in information, not scale [PAGE 7, PAGE 8].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under reward pressure, the base chain-of-thought policy regresses to fluent but inconsistent pseudo-quantitative habits: arithmetic that does not sum, drive-level and game-level probabilities conflated, and a final number the text does not support. The blinded-judge protocol is explicitly recursive: a separate model judges each completion blind to its source, must quote the completion, and checks whether the stated probability follows from the reasoning [PAGE 12]. The base model is inconsistent on 22.4% of completions; full-completion RL runs are 18.8% and 14.0%; a masked prompt without training is 6.8%; masked-CoT RLVR is 4.4% [PAGE 13]. State-reading errors are low and flat: possession and score about one percent, the pregame favorite two to seven percent, the clock seven to ten percent, and “Reinforcement learning does not corrupt the reading of the game” [PAGE 13]. The regression is therefore at the fact-to-number step, not at perception. That is the crisis habit: the model can read the state but cannot keep its answer consistent with its own reasoning when the gradient reaches the reasoning.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is to remove the gradient from the reasoning, either by direct prediction or by an answer-span mask. This works, but it is not free. Direct prediction reaches Brier 0.144 and ECE 0.029; masked-CoT reaches Brier 0.152 and ECE 0.030 [PAGE 7]. The paired gap is small but significant: “Masking the gradient to the answer span costs a small but significant amount of Brier relative to discarding the reasoning” [PAGE 5]. The masked model is nonetheless “the better choice when a forecast must be explained, at the cost of slightly lower sharpness than direct prediction” [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap’s failure mode is over-constraint: if the answer-span mask is paired with a nonzero KL anchor, the concentrated gradient overflows and the loss diverges [PAGE 4]; if the learning rate is too high, the unanchored update over-sharpens [PAGE 13]. So the recursive theory is not “mask always wins”; it is “mask the correct credit-assignment path, then tune the optimization clock.”

Clock. The clock appears at three layers. First, the game clock is a coarse state feature; clock misreads occur in seven to ten percent of completions and no training variant changes that base weakness [PAGE 13]. Second, the empirical-rate bins use time remaining, so coarse time buckets smooth the target and limit resolution [PAGE 12]. Third, the training clock matters: 250 steps, 20-step warmup, saves every 50 steps, checkpoint selection on a fixed 128-state sample [PAGE 5]. Early checkpoints may not reach the teacher; late checkpoints can over-sharpen, especially in the masked run where the learning-rate optimum is an inverted-U and \(4\times10^{-5}\) over-sharpens [PAGE 13]. The recursive audit therefore has to model the judge, the reward, and the stopping rule simultaneously: a model can look calibrated at one clock time and be overconfident at another. The paper’s defense is season-disjoint evaluation, full-split reporting, and paired bootstrap over plays [PAGE 5, PAGE 7]. That defense is credible for the public-state layer; it does not resolve omitted physical-layer uncertainty.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto rule: a physical, biomechanical, or weather claim is admissible only if the relevant variable is represented in the state or reward, its variation changes the conditional outcome rate, the target marginalizes over it consistently, and its train/test distribution is stable. The paper’s public state is “the score margin, the quarter and time remaining, down and distance, field position, the team in possession” plus one public pre-game market signal [PAGE 3]. It does not include player injury status, fatigue, biomechanical load, weather, field surface, or crowd environment. The prompt exposes “one market-derived quantity” [PAGE 14], not a full physical state. Therefore the learned \(\hat p(x)\) estimates a public-state conditional \(\eta_p(x)=\mathbb E[\eta_f(x,Z)\mid x]\), where \(Z\) is the omitted physical layer, not the full \(\eta_f(x,Z)\). Calibration to \(\eta_p\) is valid for public-state forecasting; it is not proof of calibration conditional on \(Z\).

Proof sketch. Suppose weather \(Z_w\) interacts with time remaining and score margin, so \(\eta_f(x,z_w)\) differs from \(\eta_p(x)\) within a public bin. The empirical rate \(\hat p_b\) averages over the training-season \(Z_w\) distribution. If the test season has a different \(Z_w\) distribution, \(\hat p_b\) is biased for the test conditional even though the season split prevents game leakage. The same argument applies to biomechanical load and injury: if those variables shift the outcome rate within a score-time bin, the coarse teacher cannot represent the shift. The paper’s fine-table ablation adds field position and down, not weather or injury, and still finds no game-level calibration gain [PAGE 12]; that supports the public-state ceiling claim but does not adjudicate the physical layer. The market’s residual edge is described as “live in-game information that no static input contains” [PAGE 8], which is consistent with omitted live physical information, but the paper does not identify which physical variable carries it.

Adjudication: accept the claim that the direct model is calibrated to the public-state conditional and reaches the teacher/frontier tier; accept the claim that full-completion gradient reach decalibrates; accept the claim that the residual market gap is resolution rather than reliability. Reject any claim that the model has matched the full physical outcome process, that biomechanical or weather mechanisms have been learned, or that the market reference is an absolute ceiling for all information layers. The correct cross-layer verdict is a veto on mechanism claims, not on the public-state calibration result. The paper itself supplies the limiting language: the method “reaches a strong reference only where the public state already carries most of the predictive signal” [PAGE 9]. That is exactly the layer where the evidence is strong.

Veto 1: reward-target swap. Counterfactual: train the identical direct model with the realized-outcome reward \(r_y=1-(p-y)^2\) instead of \(r=1-(p-\hat p(x))^2\). Prediction: the outcome reward is higher variance and drifts in calibration; the paper’s in-training ablation reports Brier 0.166 and ECE 0.10 for the realized outcome, versus 0.154 and 0.050 for the empirical rate, and blending the outcome back in is worse on both at 0.181 and 0.121 [PAGE 4]. On the full 2024 test the direct rate-trained model is 0.1443 Brier and 0.0292 ECE [PAGE 7]. Veto condition: if the outcome reward, under identical seeds, checkpoint rule, and group size, reaches ECE at or below 0.05 and Brier at or below 0.154, the claim that label noise is the decalibrating target is vetoed. This is verifiable by rerunning the released training recipe or by comparing per-play predictions.

Veto 2: gradient-reach swap. Counterfactual: keep the empirical-rate reward but allow the gradient over the full completion, including reasoning tokens. Prediction: calibration degrades sharply; the paper reports held-out Brier rising from the base model’s 0.25 to 0.34 and ECE from 0.19 to 0.30 under full-completion rate-reward training, while direct or answer-span-masked training holds calibration near the market reference [PAGE 4, PAGE 5]. Veto condition: if full-completion rate-reward training keeps ECE at or below 0.05 and keeps the inconsistent-reasoning rate at or below the masked-prompt control’s 6.8%, then the claim that the gradient’s reach into the reasoning is the corrupting mechanism is vetoed [PAGE 13]. This is verifiable from the released per-play predictions and the blinded-judge sample.

Veto 3: leakage and season-clock swap. Counterfactual: introduce the live market probability into prompts or let test outcomes enter the empirical-rate target. Prediction: the reported calibration would inflate and the season-disjoint test would no longer be a clean estimate. The paper states that it scanned all 40,246 training prompts and the 2023 and 2024 evaluation prompts and found no occurrence of the market win probability [PAGE 14], and that “The estimate for an evaluation play is read from the training-season table alone, so no test outcome enters its own target” [PAGE 12]. It also trains on 2015–2022, selects on 2023, and tests on 2024 [PAGE 14]. Veto condition: if a reproducible scan finds the live market probability in any prompt, or if any test outcome enters its own target, or if the reported numbers fail to reproduce from the released per-play predictions under the season-disjoint script [PAGE 14], the leakage-free claim is vetoed. These three vetoes are concrete, thresholded, and independently checkable; they test target purity, credit-assignment path, and temporal integrity rather than rhetorical plausibility.
