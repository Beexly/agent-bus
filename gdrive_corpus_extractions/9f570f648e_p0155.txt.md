H(d,N) = m'(d)\,\Bigl[1 - \bigl(1 - F(d)\bigr)^N\Bigr].

Units: seconds (SI base unit of time) for \(d\); dimensionless count for \(N\); habs (Hullian habit unit) for \(H\) and \(m'\); \(F\) is per-trial (dimensionless).

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
1. Finite-sample regime: if \(N\) is too small that \(H(d,N) \ll m'(d)\), the asymptotic difference of 18.68 habs is not yet expressed, and the test choice reflects transient strength rather than \(m'\). This is not a mathematical singularity but an empirical non-identification regime.
2. Learning paralysis: if \(F(d) \to 0\), then \(H(d,N)\) never approaches \(m'(d)\) within finite \(N\), producing a singularity in effective learnability.
3. Delay prohibitive regime: if there exists a critical delay \(d_c\) such that \(m'(d_c) = 0\), habit strength collapses and the alley is functionally abandoned; the text provides no evidence for or against such a \(d_c\).
4. Drive instability: the preparation assumes hungry animals [PAGE 155]; if hunger drive \(D\) declines across the session, the effective reinforcement value changes, violating the "exactly similar food reinforcement" premise and making \(m'\) time-varying rather than constant.

Choice operator at test (both entrances open): the text states that a competition between habit strengths occurs, "the stronger habit of course dominating" [PAGE 155]. Formally:
\[
\mathcal{C}(N_{30}, N_{60}) =
\begin{cases}
\text{30-s alley} & H(30,N_{30}) > H(60,N_{60}),\\[2mm]
\text{60-s alley} & H(60,N_{60}) > H(30,N_{30}),\\[2mm]
\text{no preference} & H(30,N_{30}) = H(60,N_{60}).
\end{cases}
\]
This is a deterministic winner-take-all operator as written in the source; no stochastic matching function is specified on this page.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The passage sits inside Hull’s 1943 *Principles of Behavior*, a hypothetico-deductive system intended to derive behavioral laws from a small set of postulates [PAGE 155]. The goal-gradient hypothesis itself descends from Hull’s earlier formulations concerning the relation between goal proximity and behavior strength, later elaborated by Spence and by subsequent learning theorists. The lineage runs from Thorndike’s law of effect and Pavlovian conditioned reflexes through Hull’s deductive behaviorism, through Spence’s mathematical reformulations, and forward to Rescorla-Wagner associative strength and temporal-difference models in which delay to reward is explicitly discounted.

A multi-week trap is embedded in the design. If the experiment runs across many sessions, the delay chambers themselves become predictive stimuli. A 30-second chamber and a 60-second chamber differ not only in duration but in the temporal contingency they signal; over days or weeks the rat may learn that one chamber predicts food after a short interval and the other after a long interval, producing Pavlovian temporal conditioning superimposed on instrumental habit strength. The longer delay also provides more time for within-trial extinction, distraction, or habituation within the chamber, which could depress the asymptotic habit strength independently of any goal-gradient principle.

Another longitudinal trap concerns drive. The subjects are "hungry albino rats" [PAGE 155], but over a multi-week protocol body weight, handling stress, circadian phase, and metabolic state will drift. Because the asymptote \(m'\) is theoretically tied to reinforcement conditions under a fixed drive state, slow changes in hunger drive across weeks could masquerade as a delay effect if one group happens to be tested during a phase of higher or lower deprivation. Finally, randomization of alley order [PAGE 155] protects against order effects within a session, but it does not protect against the animal developing a stable spatial or contextual bias ("left alley = short wait") that hardens over weeks and becomes resistant to the very delay manipulation the experiment is meant to isolate.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: Under intensified deprivation or stress, an organism may regress to the habit with the greatest historical momentum rather than the one with the highest current asymptote. If the 60-second alley was trained first or under more salient conditions, a crisis state could re-activate that alley despite its lower \(m'(60\,\mathrm{s}) = 31.61\) habs [PAGE 155]. Regression is therefore a recursive readout of acquisition history, not solely of the instantaneous habit-strength comparison at the test point.

Counter-trap: The experimenter’s randomization of alley order and equating of reinforcement counts [PAGE 155] is a methodological counter-trap against experimenter-induced order bias. The animal, however, may construct its own counter-model: because one alley always imposes 30 seconds and the other 60 seconds, the rat can learn a stable cue-delay mapping. If the animal prefers the 30-second alley because it expects a shorter wait, the measured preference reflects temporal expectation and cue-based choice, not merely the competition of two habit strengths. The counter-trap is thus vulnerable to the subject’s own counter-inference.

Clock: The choice at the entrance is prospective. The animal must represent the upcoming delay chain before entering. A biological clock imposes a temporal calculus on the effective value of each alley; the habit strengths \(H(30,N)\) and \(H(60,N)\) are read through a temporal discounting or interval-timing lens. If the internal clock dominates, the decision variable is not raw habit strength but something like \(H(d,N)\) weighted by expected delay to food. In that recursive architecture, the preference for the shorter delay is generated by the clock's prospective evaluation of the delay chain, while habit strength supplies only the asymptotic amplitude of each branch.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: The alleys are stipulated to be "exactly similar" apart from the delay chamber [PAGE 155]. A physical veto is triggered if length, width, floor texture, illumination, or odor differ between alleys. Required veto check: verify \(L_{30} = L_{60}\) within millimetric tolerance and equal photometric intensity at the entrance and food box. If the 60-second alley is physically longer or darker, the preference cannot be attributed to delay duration alone.

Biomechanical layer: Inside the delay chamber the animal is "retained for a certain period" [PAGE 155]. If the retention mechanism in one chamber imposes a different posture, restraint stress, or locomotor cost upon release, the biomechanical cost of the delay differs between alleys. Veto condition: identical restraint mechanics and equal latency to resume locomotion after the chamber door opens. Differential immobility cost would confound delay duration with physical effort.

Weather/laboratory layer: Ambient temperature \(T\), relative humidity, and barometric pressure affect basal metabolic rate and hence hunger drive \(D\). Because both alleys occupy the same apparatus, common-mode weather variation cancels in a within-subject choice. However, if one delay chamber is adjacent to an exterior wall with a different thermal profile, a differential thermal veto arises. Required check: monitor temperature at both chambers; require \(|\Delta T| < 0.5\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) across sessions. Uncontrolled differential temperature would alter drive asymmetrically and invalidate the equal-reinforcement premise.

Veto adjudication: The design successfully randomizes alley order and equates reinforcement numbers [PAGE 155], thereby vetoing order and frequency confounds. It does not, however, blind the animal to the delay duration nor rule out differential Pavlovian conditioning within the chambers. Consequently, the inference from \(m'\) differences to a preference for shorter delay is not veto-proof without additional controls for chamber cues, restraint equivalence, and thermal uniformity.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 (Delay-equivalence control): Configure both alleys with 30-second delay chambers while preserving the distinct visual and tactile cues used in the original experiment. Verifiable record: alley choice over 50 unreinforced test trials. Prediction: no significant deviation from 50/50 choice if delay duration is the sole causal variable; any preference exceeding 60% for one alley vetoes the delay-duration model and implicates cue or location confounds.

Scenario 2 (Delay reversal after asymptote): Train both habits to asymptotic strength, then swap the delays so that the formerly 30-second alley imposes 60 seconds and vice versa, keeping alley identities fixed. Verifiable record: choice proportion before and after the swap across at least 20 test trials. Prediction: preference should follow the delay (subjects switch to the alley now imposing 30 seconds); if preference remains attached to the original alley identity, habit is bound to spatial/contextual cues rather than the delay parameter, vetoing a pure \(m'(d)\) account.

Scenario 3 (Drive depletion / satiation): Pre-feed subjects to reduce hunger drive below the training deprivation level, then administer the standard choice test. Verifiable record: alley selection and choice latency. Prediction: if Hullian habit strength \(H\) is independent of drive but performance is multiplicatively scaled by motivation, satiation should reduce overall response vigor without reversing the alley preference; if preference disappears entirely or choice latency diverges under satiation, a motivational performance term vetoes a pure habit-strength interpretation of the choice.

Bonus verifiable condition: introduce a 120-second delay chamber. Prediction under a monotonic delay penalty: \(m'(120\,\mathrm{s}) < m'(60\,\mathrm{s}) < m'(30\,\mathrm{s})\). If \(m'(120\,\mathrm{s}) \geq m'(60\,\mathrm{s})\), the monotonic delay model is falsified and a non-monotonic or ceiling effect must be posited.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
