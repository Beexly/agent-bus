Because the T-Former fine scale dominates attention at 0.52 weight during transitions [PAGE 50], a feint can trigger spurious transition outputs, degrading precision. Page 46 notes the hardest category is "formation restructuring transitions" involving multiple simultaneous positional lines (F1 = 0.889), precisely where an adversary could concentrate feints to maximize false positives. The model has no explicit adversarial robustness layer; it assumes ground-truth transitions are honest tactical states rather than strategic deception.

The clock operates on three coupled timescales. First, the inference clock: the system must process each 10 Hz frame within a 100 ms budget to support live coaching feedback [PAGE 25], and measured end-to-end latency is 49.7 ms [PAGE 37], leaving ~50 ms slack before missing the sampling deadline. Second, the buffering clock: the 60-second sliding window with 1-second stride [PAGE 31] means the model always classifies with up to 60 seconds of lookback, creating a fundamental causal lag between live pitch events and stabilized classification. Third, the match clock: transition detection MAE of 1.73 seconds [PAGE 46] means the model localizes a formation change nearly two seconds after it stabilizes, which in elite football is sufficient time for multiple progressive passes and a spatial reorganization. Recursive theory of mind enters because the model models players who model roles; coaches then model the model’s output; and opponents model the coach’s reaction. Without joint adversarial formation modeling—explicitly flagged as future work on page 62–63—the system remains a first-order observer vulnerable to second-order deception.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The pitch is a bounded Euclidean domain with regulated dimensions; player sprint speeds in elite football reach roughly 10 m/s, so over Δt = 0.1 s a player can displace ~1 meter. Finite-difference velocity and acceleration at 10 Hz are therefore numerically well-conditioned only if tracking noise is well below meter scale. Page 31 explicitly injects "Gaussian perturbation on positional coordinates, which represents the measurement uncertainty of real-world tracking systems," acknowledging that the physical measurement layer is noisy. If optical tracking suffers occlusion or dropout, the graph G(t) loses nodes; at τ = 15 m (or 25 m), isolated players receive zero message-passing gradient, collapsing the ST-GAT representation to the self-loop/identity term and breaking relational invariance. There is no explicit masking or imputation operator described for missing detections, which is listed as an untested robustness gap on page 60–61.

Biomechanical layer veto: The model treats players as kinematic points with no fatigue, injury, or substitute-status variable. Page 60 states the model relies on positional tracking data and does not integrate physical load monitoring except as a future training application [PAGE 59]. A player at the end of a 90-minute match exhibits reduced acceleration magnitude and altered inter-player spacing; because acceleration features are part of the 12-dimensional input [TABLE 1, PAGE 21], fatigue-induced kinematic drift shifts the feature distribution away from training conditions, inducing silent covariate shift. No biomechanical veto exists to downweight or flag fatigued players, so formation recognition may misattribute fatigue-driven spacing changes to tactical role changes.

Weather/environmental layer veto: The source text contains no weather variables (precipitation, wind, temperature, pitch moisture). Optical tracking systems can be degraded by adverse weather, and rain alters ball-surface and player-surface friction, changing acceleration profiles. Because the model was validated only on elite men’s professional football datasets without domain shift evaluation to different contexts [PAGE 55], and no environmental covariates appear in Table 1 or Algorithm 1, the weather veto is absent by construction. Any deployment in variable climates is unvalidated.

Adjudication: The veto hierarchy is (1) physical sensor failure/occlusion → immediate model invalidity (graph breaks); (2) biomechanical fatigue → silent accuracy decay, detectable only via drift monitoring not implemented; (3) weather → unmodeled external validity risk. Breakdown singularity occurs when tracking frequency < 5 Hz (aliasing), when >1 player is occluded for >5 seconds (graph fragmentation), or when ground-truth transition labels are ambiguous (κ = 0.85–0.87 is high but not perfect; page 22). Under these conditions the formal operators lose their domain of validity and the system defaults to prior-biased classification, eroding the reported 94.7% accuracy toward the 87.3% Transformer-Traj baseline or below.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable reinforcement-learning-verifiable (RLVR) counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a falsifiable prediction:

Veto Scenario 1 — Sub-critical tracking sampling and node dropout.
Counterfactual: Resample all input streams to 5 Hz (Δt = 0.2 s) and randomly occlude two outfield players for 8-second intervals during match windows, simulating tracking dropout. The ST-GAT graph construction depends on continuous node availability and the 10 Hz Nyquist rate established on page 11; halving the rate aliases acceleration features (finite differences over doubled Δt amplify noise by √2) and removes message-passing context for missing nodes.
Verification protocol: Retrain HDS-SGT on the same 847-match split with corrupted inputs; evaluate on the untouched test partition.
Prediction: Classification accuracy falls below 85.0% (at or below the standard GNN baseline of 83.1% on page 40) and transition F1 drops below 0.80; the model fails the real-time accuracy contract relative to the reported 94.7%.

Veto Scenario 2 — Goalkeeper node injection into the role-embedding space.
Counterfactual: Include the goalkeeper as an 11th graph node while retaining the 10-role DRAM embedding table. Page 11 explicitly excludes the goalkeeper because its behavior follows different patterns; forcing it into the 10-role soft-assignment matrix P ∈ ℝ^{11×10} creates a dimensional mismatch and skews the team-centroid normalization (the goalkeeper’s position is systematically outside the outfield convex hull, biasing c(t)).
Verification protocol: Run inference on the primary dataset with 11 nodes and DRAM role embeddings unchanged; compare per-formation F1 against the outfield-only configuration.
Prediction: Macro-F1 for formations with high goalkeeper variance (e.g., 5-4-1 vs 4-4-2 when the sweeper-keeper acts as an 11th outfielder) drops by more than 0.05 absolute, and confusion between defensive-line variants increases; the DRAM’s role-coverage assumption is violated.

Veto Scenario 3 — Adversarial feint transitions within the fine-scale attention window.
Counterfactual: An opponent executes deliberate shape feints lasting 1.5–2.5 seconds (within the 2-second fine-scale window) followed by immediate reversion, repeated 20 times per match. Page 50 shows fine-scale attention weight reaches 0.52 during transitions, so the T-Former will assign high posterior probability to these spurious events; page 46 already identifies multi-line restructuring as the hardest transition category (F1 = 0.889).
Verification protocol: Inject synthetic feint events into test sequences with ground-truth labels marked as non-transitions; measure transition precision and false-positive rate.
Prediction: Transition precision falls below 0.85 (from the reported 0.921) and the false-positive transition rate exceeds 15%, causing spurious coaching alerts; the system lacks an adversarial robustness veto, confirming the need for joint adversarial formation modeling flagged as future work on page 62–63.
