The third hop is inferential: one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD post-hoc and Cohen’s d effect sizes, where p ≤ 0.05 defines significance and d thresholds run from trivial (<0.20) to very large (>2.00) [PAGE 2]. The observed effect hierarchy—central midfielders versus central defenders d = 1.79 (large), forwards versus central defenders for lost balls d = 1.89 (large), forwards versus central defenders for dribbles d = 1.92 (large)—indicates that positional specialization, not raw volume, differentiates South American match profiles [PAGE 1; PAGE 3].

Gray-zone audit. Several breakdown regimes limit causal extrapolation. First, the sample is a sub-sample: only 13 of the tournament’s matches, which restricts generalization to knockout rounds where situational stakes rise [PAGE 5]. Second, exclusions remove goalkeepers, substituted players, and anyone not finishing 90 minutes, inducing survivor bias toward high-availability players [PAGE 2]. Third, InStat measurement error is non-negligible: typical errors from 0.019 to 0.036 and total errors from 0.020 to 0.037 [PAGE 2], meaning sprint distances such as central defenders’ 67 ± 42 m versus wide midfielders’ 214 ± 170 m sit within instrument uncertainty bands at the lower end [PAGE 1]. Fourth, normality was checked via Kolmogorov–Smirnov, but the small wide-midfielder cell (n = 17) risks violating ANOVA homoscedasticity assumptions [PAGE 2]. Fifth, the authors explicitly state environmental factors, match outcome, opposition quality, and game importance were not assessed [PAGE 5]; therefore the 9,903 m tournament average cannot be decomposed into tactical versus climatic drivers. Sixth, effect-size interpretation depends on the pooled standard deviation, which is not printed for every variable in Table 2, so some d-values must be treated as reported rather than independently recomputable from the printed means and SDs alone.

The multi-hop chain ends at tactical prescription: coaches should individualize conditioning by position because wide defenders now cross more than wide midfielders (d = 0.62) and forwards dribble more but lose possession more (d = 1.92 versus central defenders) [PAGE 3; PAGE 4]. However, the technical–physical coupling is asymmetric: central midfielders cover the greatest total distance (10,553 ± 763 m) yet the fewest sprint metres (91 ± 66 m), implying their load is accumulative and aerobic rather than explosive, whereas wide midfielders combine high total distance (10,390 ± 844 m) with the highest sprint volume (214 ± 170 m) [PAGE 1; PAGE 3]. This dissociation means a training prescription based solely on total distance would misallocate metabolic conditioning across positions.

Multi-week traps. A tournament such as COPA América spans roughly four to six weeks with condensed recovery microcycles between fixtures. The study’s 13-game sub-sample likely captures group-stage intensity but may underrepresent knockout-stage physical ceilings [PAGE 5]. Positional load accumulates across matches: wide defenders’ dual attacking–defensive role elevates total distance (9,929 ± 633 m) and sprint load relative to central defenders (9,226 ± 720 m), suggesting cumulative fatigue manifests first in the repeated-sprint domain (>25 km·h⁻¹) rather than in total volume [PAGE 1; PAGE 4]. Because environmental factors were not assessed, heat accumulation across match days remains an unmeasured variable that could attenuate high-speed running (>20–25 km·h⁻¹) in later tournament weeks [PAGE 5]. The multi-week trap is therefore a survivorship-and-stakes trap: players who complete all 90 minutes in the sampled 13 games are systematically more durable, and the stakes of the sampled matches may be lower than those of the omitted knockout fixtures.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under match pressure, positional roles regress to domain-specific risk preferences. Forwards, operating in dense zones and often outnumbered by defenders, exhibit the highest lost-ball count (10.55 ± 5.60) and the highest dribble volume (4.50 ± 3.02), indicating a habitual regression to individual penetration when collective passing options collapse [PAGE 3; PAGE 5]. Central defenders regress to the opposite extreme: fewest dribbles (0.31 ± 0.60), fewest lost balls (2.67 ± 1.80), and high passing accuracy (86 ± 9%), reflecting risk aversion because a defensive error directly threatens the goal [PAGE 3; PAGE 4].

Counter-trap. The wide-defender role has undergone a tactical inversion: wide defenders now complete more crosses (2.07 ± 2.02) than wide midfielders (1.06 ± 1.14), a moderate effect (d = 0.62), forcing opponents to defend the final third against traditional defenders [PAGE 3; PAGE 4]. Central midfielders function as the circulation hub (52.36 ± 15.12 passes), becoming the press-resistance counter-trap to high-intensity defensive schemes [PAGE 3]. This creates a recursive dilemma: pressing the central midfielder cuts off circulation but opens space behind for the wide defender’s cross; dropping off concedes circulation but nullifies the cross.

Clock. The 90-minute inclusion criterion imposes a hard temporal boundary: only players completing full match duration enter the analysis, so late-game fatigue substitutions are excluded [PAGE 2]. Sprint metrics (>25 km·h⁻¹) are therefore full-match aggregates and cannot resolve whether peak sprint output occurs in opening or closing minutes; the clock acts as a censoring operator on the time-to-fatigue curve, and the study provides no minute-by-minute decay function for high-speed running.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer. The sprint threshold is strictly v > 25 km·h⁻¹ [PAGE 2, Table 1]. Wide midfielders sprint 214 ± 170 m versus central defenders 67 ± 42 m (d = 1.19), a separation exceeding InStat typical-error bounds (0.019–0.036) [PAGE 1; PAGE 2]. Total-distance differences are robust because the effect sizes are large (d = 1.79 central midfielder versus central defender; d = 1.45 central midfielder versus forward) [PAGE 1].

Biomechanical layer. Total distance alone masks deceleration and change-of-direction load; the study does not report accelerometer-derived PlayerLoad or metabolic power, so the biomechanical cost of wide defenders’ repeated sprint-and-recover cycles is unquantified [PAGE 2]. Technical load (crosses, passes, lost balls) is recorded as counts without spatial coordinates, so pitch-location-adjusted external load cannot be computed from the source.

Weather veto. The authors list environmental factors among unassessed variables [PAGE 5]. The 2019 COPA América was hosted in Brazil; if wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) exceeded approximately 26–28 °C during match hours, sprint output and high-speed running (>20–25 km·h⁻¹) would be expected to degrade nonlinearly, potentially invalidating cross-climate comparisons with the UEFA EURO average of 10,350 m [PAGE 1; PAGE 3; PAGE 5]. Because no WBGT data appear in the source, a weather-driven veto applies to any generalization of the 9,903 m South American average to cooler or hotter environments.

Veto synthesis. The positional ranking of sprinting (wide midfielders > wide defenders > forwards > central midfielders > central defenders) is veto-proof only within the observed environmental envelope of the 13 sampled matches; outside that envelope, thermoregulatory constraints may disproportionately affect wide defenders’ repeated high-intensity efforts and invert the ranking.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Veto scenario 1 (substitution censoring). Counterfactual: include players substituted before minute 90 and goalkeepers. The source excludes all non-full-90-minute players [PAGE 2]. Prediction: mean total distance for central midfielders falls below 10,553 ± 763 m because partial-match observations contribute less absolute distance; the effect size versus central defenders (d = 1.79) attenuates toward the moderate/small boundary (d < 1.20). Verification: re-run one-way ANOVA on an intent-to-treat sample including all minutes played; if the F-ratio drops below the p ≤ 0.05 threshold, the original position effect is an artifact of survivor censoring.

Veto scenario 2 (knockout-stage escalation). Counterfactual: extend the sample from 13 group-stage games to semifinals and final. The source analyzes only 13 matches and explicitly excludes situational variables such as match importance [PAGE 1; PAGE 5]. Prediction: central-midfielder total distance exceeds 10,553 m and wide-defender sprinting exceeds 152 ± 199 m because elimination matches raise tactical intensity and reduce possession conservatism. Verification: collect InStat data from knockout matches; if central-midfielder distance does not exceed the group-stage mean, the tournament-stage veto is falsified.

Veto scenario 3 (thermal suppression of HSR). Counterfactual: impose wet-bulb globe temperature > 28 °C. The source records no environmental data [PAGE 5]. Prediction: central-midfielder high-speed running (739 ± 202 m) drops below ~600 m and sprinting (91 ± 66 m) falls below ~60 m, collapsing the separation from central defenders (sprinting 67 ± 42 m) and inverting the high-intensity hierarchy. Verification: match WBGT logs to InStat speed-zone outputs; a significant negative regression coefficient for WBGT on high-speed-running distance confirms the veto. Each RLVR veto is verifiable from the source’s own exclusion criteria, measurement-error bounds, and acknowledged limitations.
