<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system's recursive theory of mind contains a crisis-habit regression: upon detecting a suspicious second input, the aggregator regresses to weighting the first input more heavily [PAGE 2]. This is an anchor-reversion habit: the algorithm treats the initial preference as a stable prior and the new input as a potential contamination. However, this creates a recursive vulnerability. A strategic evaluator with theory of mind about the detector can first establish a clean history of non-suspicious inputs, then introduce a biased preference precisely when the system's guard is lowered by the "more weight to first input" rule. The evaluator regresses the system's own trust model: by mimicking legitimate behavior early, the adversary gains credibility, then exploits the asymmetry in weight assignment to inject a manipulated preference that disproportionately shifts the global order.

The counter-trap is meta-detection of history manipulation. If the system only examines "a history of inputs" without modeling evaluator intent, it cannot distinguish a genuine change of mind from a cultivated-then-exploited trust trajectory. A robust counter-trap would maintain not just input values but input provenance, response latency, intra-evaluator consistency across query classes C(q), and cross-evaluator agreement on identical pairs.
