The source passage is Frank Knight, *Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit*, page 176. Knight begins by rejecting what he calls the "illicit procedure of funding productive agents into 'factors'" and insists on dealing with "the actual competing units on their own account" [PAGE 176]. This is the foundational move: neoclassical imputation treats labor, land, and capital as homogeneous, infinitely divisible factors whose remuneration equals marginal product. Knight argues that this procedure collapses when the agent is a concrete human individual. He writes that the human individual "is a very effective unit; not only does he bargain as a unit, but he cannot practically be divided up between different establishments, and the range of occupations in which he can engage in any short interval of time is also very narrowly restricted" [PAGE 176]. Three constraints bind simultaneously: (1) indivisibility across establishments, (2) restricted occupational mobility over short intervals, and (3) uniqueness—"he does not always shade off by imperceptible gradations from one variety of another to the extent that perfect competitive imputation demands" [PAGE 176]. Because the number of such individuals is not always large relative to the number of variants, "an individual [is not] a negligible fraction of a group of similars" [PAGE 176].

The multi-hop derivation proceeds as follows. In competitive imputation theory, the income of agent i is w_i = p · MP_i = p · ∂F/∂a_i, where F is the production function over agents a = (a_1, …, a_n) and p is output price. This equation requires two limiting conditions: the continuum/infinitesimal condition a_i / Σ_j a_j → 0, and the homogeneity/substitutability condition that for every agent i there exists a dense set of near-substitutes j such that the distance d(a_i, a_j) → 0. Knight denies both for labor. The individual is not infinitesimal; he is an appreciable dimension of the organization. Consequently, the partial derivative ∂F/∂a_i is not a market-determined scalar independent of the allocation; it becomes allocation-dependent and bargaining-dependent. When a group consists of "small numbers of rather unique agents which very effectively supplement each other and are not so effectively demanded elsewhere," competition "does not afford means of distributing the entire yield of the group among its members; an appreciable part of it resists automatic division and remains a joint product, dependent on" [PAGE 176]—the sentence trails into the next page, but the logical terminus is clear: residual income is not imputable by marginal productivity alone.

Knight’s own footnote supplies the crucial boundary condition: "It is not necessary that he be an infinitesimal fraction of the productive power of a particular establishment. The imputation process works itself out through the competition of establishments for the different agents. If a number of establishments exist in which a certain type of agencies is on an indifference margin, the income of all similar agencies will be accurately determined" [PAGE 176n1]. This means Knight’s critique is not a blanket denial of imputation; it is a conditional breakdown. Imputation survives only when (a) multiple establishments compete for the same agent type, and (b) that type sits on an indifference margin across establishments. When those conditions fail—when there are few establishments, when the agent is unique, and when mobility is restricted over the relevant short interval—marginal-productivity wages cease to be a well-defined equilibrium object. The residual, or joint product, becomes the return to the agent who bears the uncertainty of coordinating the complementary group. This is Knight’s bridge to profit: profit is not the imputed return to capital or labor but the residual accruing to uncertainty-bearing under conditions where automatic division fails.

Gray-zone audit. The theory weakens precisely at the boundary between "risk" and "Knightian uncertainty." Risk admits an objective probability measure P(ω) over outcomes ω ∈ Ω with known variance σ²; under risk, expected-utility maximization can price contingent claims and restore imputation through actuarial fairness. Knightian uncertainty obtains when P is not objectively known or when multiple non-nested subjective priors μ_1, μ_2 are compatible with the same evidence. In the passage, the "appreciable dimensions" of the natural agent and the "very narrowly restricted" range of short-run occupations create an environment where the decision-maker cannot reduce the coordination problem to a known lottery. The singularity regime is therefore: (i) I(labor) = 1 (indivisible), (ii) δ(uniqueness) ≫ 0 (distance from substitutes bounded away from zero), and (iii) the set of competing establishments is finite and small, so no indifference margin pins down a unique wage. Under these three conditions, w_i = MRP_i has no unique solution; any wage within a bargaining interval is consistent with individual rationality, and the residual R = Y_group − Σ w_i a_i is positive and indeterminate by competition alone. The theory recovers neoclassical imputation only in the thick-market limit N → ∞ with homogeneous agents, where the infinitesimal condition and substitutability condition are approximately satisfied and uncertainty collapses to measurable risk.

The counter-trap is the mirror-image overcorrection: management overestimates the uniqueness \(\delta\) of a particular agent and pays a quasi-rent to prevent departure, thereby surrendering the residual R to the agent even when a thick market of substitutes exists but is not searched because \(\Delta t_{\text{search}}\) exceeds the crisis window. The clock layer is decisive. Knight’s restriction that the range of occupations is "very narrowly restricted" in "any short interval of time" [PAGE 176] defines a tactical clock: \(\Delta t_{\text{bargain}}\) (time to negotiate and adjust contracts) versus \(\Delta t_{\text{adjust}}\) (time to physically reassign or replace the agent). Because the human individual cannot be divided and mobility is slow, \(\Delta t_{\text{bargain}} > \Delta t_{\text{adjust}}\) frequently fails in the direction that traps the firm: the contract must be settled before the agent can be substituted. The firm therefore pays for immediate availability, not for marginal product evaluated at a long-run competitive equilibrium. This clock asymmetry converts Knightian uncertainty into a hold-up premium embedded in the joint product.
