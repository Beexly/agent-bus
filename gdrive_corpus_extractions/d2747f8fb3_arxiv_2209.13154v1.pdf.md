<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a computer-vision tracking paper, not a wagering document. The multi-hop derivation begins with detection: YOLOX-X produces bounding boxes, then Observation-Centric SORT (OCSORT) associates detections to tracklets using a Hungarian assignment whose cost includes IoU and motion-direction similarity [PAGE 2]. The paper’s central claim is that the linear-motion assumption of a Kalman filter is a failure mode for sports players: “Current motion-based tracking algorithms often use Kalman Filter to predict the motion of an object, however, its linear movement assumption can cause failure in tracking when the target is not moving linearly” [PAGE 1]. OCSORT addresses this with observation-centric online smoothing and momentum, but the authors add a second recovery step because IoU-based recovery fails when a fast player’s new detection does not overlap the last observation. The added central-distance recovery uses Euclidean distance between detection centers and the last observation of unassociated tracklets [PAGE 3]. After tracking, sport-specific ReID post-processing merges or suppresses fragment tracklets: basketball uses a 10-player cap and appearance EMA; football uses three greedy association rounds with position gates depending on time gap; volleyball uses a 12-tracklet cap and position-only re-entry matching [PAGES 3–6]. Finally, linear interpolation fills gaps [PAGE 4]. The evaluation chain is therefore detector quality -> association quality -> recovery quality -> post-processing merge quality -> interpolation -> HOTA and related metrics. The reported HOTA progression is 67.107 for original OCSORT, 71.764 after central-distance recovery, and 73.968 after ReID post-processing, with AssA 63.460, DetA 86.316, MOTA 94.832, IDF1 78.271, IDS 2754, and Frag 3592 [PAGE 6]. That progression is an ablation-like chain, but it is not a full factorial ablation for every sport-specific threshold.

The gray-zone audit is substantial. First, the paper does not give explicit OCSORT equations; it describes the strategy qualitatively. Second, the central-distance thresholds are scalars whose coordinate units are not stated; under an image-coordinate convention they are pixel-like, but the source never says “pixels.” Third, the thresholds were chosen by testing performance: “The choice of threshold is based on the evaluation performance on the Sportsmot testing set” [PAGE 5]. That is a legitimate engineering practice but a gray zone for generalization because test-set tuning can overfit sport, camera, resolution, and frame-rate specifics. Fourth, the football position gate is piecewise in time gap: less than 100 frames gives distance threshold 100; 100 to 500 frames gives 250; beyond 500 frames gives 400 [PAGE 6]. These discontinuities are not physically calibrated to player speed, field scale, or camera homography. Fifth, weather is entirely absent; the dataset description says only that videos were collected from Olympic Games, NCAA Championship, and NBA games on YouTube, with 720P, 25 FPS, and official recordings [PAGE 5]. Therefore any weather-dependent veto is underdetermined by the source. Sixth, the tracklet age is fixed at 30 frames for all sports [PAGE 5], which at 25 FPS is 1.2 seconds; that clock is a design choice, not a derived physical constant. Seventh, the paper reports a final leaderboard rank and aggregate metrics, but not per-sport error decompositions or confidence intervals, so threshold sensitivity and variance are not auditable from the source alone. The correct audit conclusion is not that the method is invalid; it is that the method is empirically gated by unstated units, unshown per-sport ablations, and test-set threshold selection.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are detection, Hungarian association, IoU, motion-direction similarity, Euclidean central distance, cosine similarity, exponential moving average, sport-specific gating, and linear interpolation. Formalizing them with explicit units:

Bounding-box IoU is dimensionless:
\[
\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\in[0,1].
\]
The paper keeps OCSORT’s “0.3 IoU threshold” [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: if \(|A\cup B|=0\), IoU is undefined; if \(\mathrm{IoU}=0\), there is no overlap and IoU-only recovery cannot associate, which is exactly the motivation for central-distance recovery [PAGE 3].

The Hungarian assignment solves a minimum-cost matching over tracklet-detection pairs:
\[
\sigma^{*}=\arg\min_{\sigma\in\Pi}\sum_{i} C_{i,\sigma(i)},
\]
where \(\Pi\) is the set of valid one-to-one assignments and \(C_{i,j}\) is the association cost. The paper states that OCSORT includes “moving direction similarity between detection and tracklets into the Hungarian Algorithm cost” [PAGE 2]. The source does not print the exact \(C_{i,j}\), so any numerical reconstruction beyond the stated IoU threshold and motion-direction term is an assumption, not a source equation.

Central-distance recovery uses the Euclidean distance between a detection center and the last observation center of an unassociated tracklet:
\[
D_{i,j}=\left\|\mathbf{c}_{i}-\mathbf{c}_{j}\right\|_{2}.
\]
Under image-coordinate convention, \(D_{i,j}\) has pixel-like units; the paper reports scalar thresholds without naming the unit. The recovery gate is \(D_{i,j}\le \tau_{s}\), where the paper sets basketball \(\tau=200\), football \(\tau=80\), and volleyball \(\tau=80\) [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regimes: \(D_{i,j}=0\) can mean either the same player or two players with coincident centers, so zero distance is not proof of identity; \(D_{i,j}>\tau_{s}\) vetoes recovery; and if the camera scale changes, a fixed \(\tau_{s}\) is not physically invariant.

Football’s position gate is explicitly piecewise by disappear-reappear time gap \(g\) in frames:
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{pos}}(g)=100\ \text{if }g\in[0,100),\quad
\tau_{\mathrm{pos}}(g)=250\ \text{if }g\in[100,500],\quad
\tau_{\mathrm{pos}}(g)=400\ \text{if }g\in(500,\infty).
\]
This is stated verbatim: “For the tracklets that share a time gap of fewer than 100 frames, the distance threshold is 100. For the tracklets that share a time gap between 100 to 500 frames, the distance threshold is 250, and for those tracklets that share a time gap bigger than 500 frames, the distance threshold is set to 400” [PAGE 6]. Units are frames for \(g\) and pixel-like image units for distance. Breakdown regimes occur at the discontinuities \(g=100\) and \(g=500\), where a one-frame change can jump the allowed distance from 100 to 250 or from 250 to 400.

Appearance matching uses cosine similarity between embedding vectors:
\[
s_{\cos}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}}\in[-1,1],
\]
with cosine distance \(d_{\cos}=1-s_{\cos}\) or an equivalent dissimilarity. The paper uses cosine-distance thresholds 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 in three football association rounds [PAGE 6]. Breakdown regime: if either embedding has zero norm, cosine similarity is undefined; if all same-team jerseys are visually similar, \(s_{\cos}\) has low discriminative information, which is why the paper adds position and time gates.

Basketball appearance memory is an exponential moving average:
\[
\mathbf{f}_{t}=\alpha\mathbf{f}_{t-1}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{f}_{t}^{\mathrm{new}},
\]
with \(\alpha\in(0,1]\) dimensionless. The paper says “We keep updating the tracklets appearance feature using exponential moving averages” [PAGE 4], but does not report \(\alpha\). Breakdown regimes: \(\alpha=0\) erases memory; \(\alpha=1\) freezes memory; neither is validated in the source.

Interpolation fills missing boxes:
\[
\mathbf{b}_{t}=(1-\lambda)\mathbf{b}_{t_0}+\lambda\mathbf{b}_{t_1},\quad \lambda=\frac{t-t_0}{t_1-t_0}.
\]
The paper says “we use linear interpolation as our last step” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown regime: if \(t_1=t_0\), interpolation is singular; if the gap spans a direction change, linear interpolation is physically wrong even if it improves box continuity.

The reported HOTA is dimensionless and is commonly decomposed as a balance of detection and association:
\[
\mathrm{HOTA}=\sqrt{\mathrm{DetA}\cdot\mathrm{AssA}}.
\]
The source reports HOTA 73.968, AssA 63.460, DetA 86.316, MOTA 94.832, IDF1 78.271, IDS 2754, and Frag 3592 [PAGE 6]. Units: HOTA, AssA, DetA, MOTA, and IDF1 are dimensionless percentages or fractions; IDS and Frag are counts. The source does not print the HOTA formula itself, so the formula above is the standard external definition from the cited HOTA reference [PAGE 7], not a printed equation in the paper.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is explicit in the references. The Hungarian method dates to 1955 [PAGE 7], the Kalman filter to 1960 [PAGE 7], MOT16 to 2016 [PAGE 7], DeepSORT to 2017 [PAGE 7], FairMOT to 2020 [PAGE 7], YOLOX to 2021 [PAGE 7], ByteTrack to 2021 [PAGE 7], OCSORT to 2022 [PAGE 7], and HOTA to 2021 [PAGE 7]. The retrocausal story is: older tracking inherited a linear Kalman habit from pedestrian MOT; pedestrian scenes are described as mostly slow and linear with distinguishable appearance [PAGE 1]; sports scenes violate both assumptions because players “sprint, jump and change directions in a short time” [PAGE 2] and same-team jerseys are similar [PAGE 1]. OCSORT is the bridge: it rethinks SORT by adding observation-centric smoothing and momentum [PAGE 2–3]. The paper’s contribution is then a second bridge, central-distance recovery, plus sport-specific ReID post-processing [PAGE 3–4].

The multi-week traps are not single-frame bugs; they are persistent design inheritances. Trap one is linear-motion regression: when detections are noisy or occluded, a tracker can fall back to constant-velocity prediction, which is a crisis habit. Trap two is IoU-only recovery: fast players can move far enough that boxes do not overlap, so the tracker creates a new identity. Trap three is appearance-overconfidence: same-color jerseys make cosine similarity ambiguous, so pure ReID can merge different players. Trap four is test-set threshold tuning: the paper says thresholds were chosen based on SportsMOT testing performance [PAGE 5], so a multi-week benchmark campaign can overfit sport-specific constants. Trap five is tracklet inflation followed by over-merging: the paper notes that results usually have more identities than ground truth because re-entering players are treated as new [PAGE 3], and the post-processing then tries to merge fragments; if the merge gate is too loose, ID switches replace fragmentation. Trap six is interpolation masking: linear interpolation can hide occlusion gaps and make trajectories look smoother than the underlying motion [PAGE 4]. Trap seven is camera-cut and resolution dependence: the dataset is 720P, 25 FPS, with no shot change in clips [PAGE 5], so thresholds may not transfer to other resolutions, frame rates, or broadcast cuts. These traps are exactly why the paper’s ablation chain matters: 67.107 to 71.764 to 73.968 in HOTA [PAGE 6] is evidence for the added stages, but it is not evidence that every threshold is physically calibrated.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Treat the tracker as an agent with a belief state over identity and motion. Its “theory of mind” is the internal prediction of where a player should be and which tracklet the player belongs to. Crisis habit regression occurs when occlusion, crowding, or low detection confidence pushes the agent back to its oldest habit: linear Kalman prediction and IoU matching. The paper identifies this exact failure: the Kalman filter’s “linear movement assumption can cause failure in tracking when the target is not moving linearly” [PAGE 1]. In a crisis, the tracker predicts a straight path, the player cuts or jumps, IoU collapses, and the tracklet is lost. OCSORT’s observation-centric online smoothing rebuilds a virtual trajectory when a lost target is re-associated, “preventing error accumulation in the Kalman filter” [PAGE 3]. That is a counter-habit, not a new physical model.

The counter-trap is the opposite failure: after a player re-enters, the post-processor may over-merge different same-team players because appearance is ambiguous. Basketball uses a 10-player cap and candidate queue with cosine similarity [PAGE 4]; football uses three greedy rounds with cosine-distance thresholds 0.1, 0.2, and 0.4 plus position gates [PAGE 6]; volleyball limits to 12 players and uses only disappear-reappear distance with threshold 400 [PAGE 6]. Each is a counter-trap against ID inflation, but each can become an over-merge trap if the gate is too permissive.

The clock is the temporal control layer. The maximum tracklet age is 30 frames for all sports [PAGE 5]. At 25 FPS [PAGE 5], 30 frames is 1.2 seconds; 100 frames is 4 seconds; 500 frames is 20 seconds. Those clocks define when the agent forgets, when it searches for re-entry, and how far it allows a reappearance to be. The football gate makes the clock explicit: short gaps allow 100, medium gaps allow 250, long gaps allow 400 [PAGE 6]. The recursive theory-of-mind audit is therefore: under crisis, does the tracker regress to linear habit? Under re-entry, does it over-merge to avoid new IDs? Under clock expiry, does it forget too early or hold too long? The source gives evidence for the first two but not a full sensitivity curve for the third.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: central-distance recovery must not violate a kinematic bound. If a player’s maximum speed is \(v_{\max}\) in meters per second, the time gap is \(\Delta t\) in seconds, and the image scale is \(s_{p}\) in meters per pixel, then the maximum plausible image displacement is
\[
D_{\max}=\frac{v_{\max}\Delta t}{s_{p}}.
\]
An association with \(D_{i,j}\) substantially above \(D_{\max}\) is physically suspect even if it passes the empirical threshold. The source does not provide \(v_{\max}\) or \(s_{p}\), so this is an audit bound, not a source calibration. The paper’s thresholds of 200, 80, 80, and 100/250/400 are scalar gates [PAGES 5–6], not physically normalized quantities. That is a gray zone: a fixed 200 can be generous in a wide view and restrictive in a close-up.

Biomechanical veto: the paper’s own description of sports motion vetoes constant-velocity certainty. Players “need to sprint, jump and change directions in a short time during the game, causing the movement to be unpredictable” [PAGE 2]. OCSORT’s momentum helps with “sudden direction changes” [PAGE 3], but a biomechanical audit still says that linear interpolation across a jump or cut is invalid. The correct veto is: if the gap spans a known direction change, do not treat linear interpolation as physical evidence; treat it as a display fill.

Weather veto: no weather layer is present. The dataset description says videos were collected from Olympic Games, NCAA Championship, and NBA games on YouTube, 720P, 25 FPS, official recordings, cut into clips of average 485 frames with no shot change [PAGE 5]. There is no temperature, wind, precipitation, humidity, or surface variable in the source. Therefore any claim that weather explains tracking failure is underdetermined and must be vetoed as unsupported. The only defensible cross-layer statement is that indoor and outdoor sports differ in scene geometry and lighting, but the source does not measure those either.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Prediction: HOTA should fall toward the reported original OCSORT level of 67.107 rather than the 71.764 reported after central-distance recovery [PAGE 6]. Verifiable test: rerun the same split with the same detector and OCSORT configuration, report HOTA, AssA, DetA, IDS, and Frag. If HOTA does not drop materially, the claimed contribution of central-distance recovery is vetoed for that split. If it drops but Frag and IDS also worsen, the mechanism is supported.

2. Counterfactual removal of ReID post-processing. Keep central-distance recovery but disable basketball, football, and volleyball post-processing and interpolation. Prediction: HOTA should fall from 73.968 toward 71.764, and identity-count errors should increase, because the paper reports that results usually end with more identities than ground truth when re-entry players are not re-identified [PAGE 3]. Verifiable test: compare final HOTA, IDS, Frag, and IDF1 against the reported 73.968, 2754, 3592, and 78.271 [PAGE 6]. If IDS and Frag do not worsen after removing post-processing, the claimed post-processing benefit is vetoed. If AssA drops while DetA stays roughly fixed, the association story is supported.
