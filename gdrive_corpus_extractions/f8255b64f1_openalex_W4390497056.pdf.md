<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem is a three-agent loop: the analyst models the player’s latent ability from video; the coach models the analyst’s model and decides whether to trust it; the player models the coach’s belief and adjusts effort, risk, or role behavior. The paper’s own text describes the coach as making substitutions “according to the situation on the spot and the players’ state” [PAGE 7]. That sentence is a theory-of-mind statement: the coach is not optimizing a static score; the coach is inferring hidden state under time pressure.

“Crisis habit regression” is the failure mode in which, under match pressure, the coach reverts to reputation, seniority, or visible effort and ignores the CNN score entirely. This is not irrational if the CNN’s validation is weak. The paper’s evidence for the CNN score is a descriptive comparison with coach frame-by-frame scoring; the “experimental group” is the coach’s own classification [PAGE 7]. If the coach knows the model output or knows which players are being evaluated, the reference score is not independent. The result is a recursive confirmation loop: the coach’s prior affects the label, the label trains the model, and the model later appears to confirm the coach’s prior.

“Counter-trap” is the opposite failure: overreliance on the CNN score and on precise-looking substitution times. The paper’s “After the substitute players played, the team’s forward position mistake rate decreased obviously” [PAGE 1] is a post-hoc clock narrative. A recursive theory of mind predicts that the substitute’s behavior changes because the substitute knows the coach is evaluating him or her, and the opponent changes because the score and personnel changed. <CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Let \(X\) be video-derived features, \(A\) the latent player ability, \(M\) a mistake indicator, and \(C\) a vector of physical, biomechanical, tactical, and environmental confounders. The paper’s implicit causal claim is that a change in \(X\), or a CNN score derived from \(X\), carries information about \(A\) or about the effect of a substitution on \(M\). The identifying assumption would be conditional exchangeability, for example \(M \perp C \mid X,A\) or, for the substitution effect, \(M(t) \perp T \mid X,A,C\). The paper does not measure \(C\). Its own limitation statement says “other possible situations on the field are not considered, such as injuries, penalties, and even the physiological effects of female players at special times” [PAGE 12]. Therefore the physical and biomechanical veto is active.

A formal veto condition is

\[
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}}=\mathbf{1}\{\text{no measured }C\text{ or no sensitivity analysis for }C\}.
\]

If \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}}=1\), then

\[
p(M\mid X)=\int p(M\mid X,C)\,dP(C\mid X)
\]

is a mixture over unmeasured states and is not equal to the interventional quantity \(p(M\mid do(X))\). The same player, at the same nominal skill level, can produce different mistake rates and different video features under different surface traction, wind, heat, fatigue, injury status, menstrual-cycle phase, or opponent pressure. The CNN sees \(X\), not \(C\). A classifier trained without \(C\) can learn spurious correlations between, for example, wet-pitch sliding and “slide tackle” labels, or between fatigue-related gait and “control and coordination” scores.

The biomechanical layer is not optional in women’s football. The paper cites injury epidemiology showing high competition injury rates and lower-limb muscle/tendon and joint/ligament injuries [PAGE 12]. It also describes the defender’s role in heading and physical competition [PAGE 6]. Those are load-bearing variables: sprint deceleration, change-of-direction, heading exposure, tackle mechanics, and surface friction all affect both observable movement and injury risk. The paper’s running-time figure is not a biomechanical load measure; “individual full-court running” time [PAGE 11] does not measure high-speed distance, accelerations, decelerations, or impact load. A mistake-rate comparison that adjusts only for minutes played is therefore underidentified.

Weather is an even cleaner veto because the paper contains no weather variable at all. Wind changes ball trajectory and flight time; rain changes traction and slide-tackle mechanics; heat changes pacing and fatigue; cold changes muscle readiness. Each can alter the video feature distribution and the mistake rate. Since the source provides no weather, surface, or venue covariate, the model cannot adjudicate whether a “long pass” misclassification is a skill error or a wind effect. The physical veto cannot be discharged by a larger CNN; it requires measured covariates, randomization, or explicit sensitivity analysis. The correct adjudication is to mark all player-level causal claims as vetoed until \(C\) is measured and the sensitivity bound is reported.
