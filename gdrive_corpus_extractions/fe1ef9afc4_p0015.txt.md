<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression describes the default reversion, under anomaly or stress, to the most recently reinforced antecedent-consequent association. In Hull's terms, when an observed event fails to be "logically derived from" the postulates [PAGE 15], the organism—or the theorist—does not immediately abandon the system; it regresses to the habit that was last rewarded. The counter-trap is the imposition of a novel control experiment: Hull's "complex maze of control experiments" [PAGE 15] functions as a forced interruption of the regression by presenting an antecedent configuration that the habitual association cannot accommodate.

The clock is the temporal parameter governing decay of temporary factors. Let \(H(t)\) denote habit strength at time \(t\). Under non-reinforcement, a formal decay model is
\[
\dot{H}(t) = -\lambda H(t), \qquad H(t)=H_0 e^{-\lambda t},\quad \lambda>0,
\]
where \(\lambda\) is the extinction rate in \(\text{s}^{-1}\). This is not a fake ratio; it is a standard linear dynamical equation with SI time units. The breakdown regime is \(\lambda = 0\) (no extinction) or non-stationary reinforcement, in which case the clock never reaches asymptote and habit regression persists indefinitely.

The threefold distinction operates over timescales: crisis occurs at \(t \ll \lambda^{-1}\) when the old habit is still dominant; the counter-trap is the methodological intervention at \(t \approx \lambda^{-1}\) that introduces a control condition incompatible with the regressed habit; the clock governs \(t \gg \lambda^{-1}\), where the asymptotic state determines whether the secondary principle survives or must be rewritten. If the observation window exceeds the clock's horizon without a counter-trap, the deductive system collapses into ungrounded habit, violating Hull's requirement that explanation be a derivation from postulates rather than a regression to prior association [PAGE 15].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three layers can veto the Hullian deduction. (1) Physical/neural layer: if the substrate cannot implement the assumed primary postulate—e.g., metabolic or energetic constraints make the postulated drive state impossible to sustain—the deduction is blocked at its source. (2) Biomechanical layer: effector noise and proprioceptive feedback introduce variance \(\sigma^2_{\text{bio}}\); if \(\sigma^2_{\text{bio}} > \epsilon\) for a tolerance \(\epsilon\), antecedent isolation fails and the map \(\mathcal{K}\) becomes non-identifiable. (3) Weather/environmental layer: extraneous stimuli \(W\) create an interference functional \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}} = \partial O / \partial W\). If \(\mathcal{I}_{\text{env}} \neq 0\), the ceteris paribus clause is violated and the secondary principle cannot be deduced from \(\mathcal{P}\) alone.

Veto proof: Assume \(\mathcal{P} \vdash s\) holds in isolation. If any layer \(L\in\{\text{phys},\text{bio},\text{env}\}\) yields \(\mathcal{I}_L \neq 0\), then the observed consequent \(o\) satisfies \(\Pr(o\neq s \mid \mathcal{P}) > 0\), contradicting deterministic deductive derivation. Therefore \(\forall L,\; \mathcal{I}_L=0\) is a necessary condition for the explanation relation \(\mathsf{Expl}(e)\) to obtain. Hull's laboratory definition on [PAGE 15] assumes these interferences are excluded "for convenience of use," but the proof shows that convenience is not sufficient: without explicit layer-nullification, the derivation from primary postulates to secondary principles is formally invalid under actual experimental conditions.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Reinforcer removal counterfactual. Intervention: set primary reinforcer magnitude \(r=0\) while holding drive \(d\) constant. Veto condition: behavior fails to extinguish despite \(\mathcal{P}\) predicting extinction from the postulates. Prediction: \(\mathcal{P}\) is incomplete; at least one additional maintenance postulate is required for \(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P})\) to entail the observed secondary principle. Verifiable by measuring response rate over 30 sessions and testing whether the extinction curve deviates from the deductive prediction.

Scenario 2 — Temporal inversion counterfactual. Intervention: present the antecedent stimulus after the response (backward contiguity). Veto condition: no association forms even though spatial contiguity is preserved. Prediction: the deduction requires an asymmetric temporal operator \(\mathcal{T}:\mathcal{A}\prec\mathcal{O}\); without temporal asymmetry, \(\mathcal{P}\nvdash s\). Verifiable by comparing acquisition curves under forward versus backward scheduling; a null result under inversion vetoes any atemporal deduction.

Scenario 3 — Environmental shock counterfactual. Intervention: introduce an uncontrolled weather/laboratory temperature shift mid-experiment. Veto condition: a previously stable secondary principle becomes volatile; the control operator \(\mathcal{K}\) becomes singular, i.e., \(\dim(\ker\mathcal{D})>0\). Prediction: the ceteris paribus clause is a hard boundary condition, not a decorative assumption; its violation produces non-identifiability of antecedents. Verifiable by variance analysis of response latencies before and after the shock, with a statistically significant increase in residual variance constituting the veto.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
