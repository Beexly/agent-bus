Let \(t\) index teams, \(s\) index seasons, and \(d\) index games. Let \(n_{t,s}\) be the number of games played by team \(t\) in season \(s\). For each eligible interior blocker \(i\), let \(o_{i,d}\in[0,1]\) be the offensive snap fraction in game \(d\), and let \(P_i\in\{T,G,C\}\) be the position class. The source filters to “T”, “G”, and “C” and selects the five highest snap-fraction players [PAGE 35]. A formal construction is:
\[
S_{t,d}=\operatorname{Top5}\left(\{o_{i,d}: P_i\in\{T,G,C\}\}\right).
\]
The season-level unique combination count is:
\[
C_{t,s}=\left|\left\{S_{t,d}: d=1,\ldots,n_{t,s}\right\}\right|.
\]
Assumptions: snap fractions are measured without missingness; each game has at least five eligible interior blockers; ties are resolved deterministically, here by alphabetical ordering as in the source [PAGE 36]. Breakdown regime: if fewer than five eligible blockers exist, \(\operatorname{Top5}\) is undefined. If \(n_{t,s}=0\), \(C_{t,s}\) is undefined. If tie-breaking is unstable, \(C_{t,s}\) can be non-identifiable across small perturbations.

For pressure rate, let \(K_t\) be pressures allowed and \(B_t\) be dropbacks. The probability-scale pressure rate is:
\[
r_t=\frac{K_t}{B_t}.
\]
A generative model consistent with the source’s scatter-plot logic is:
\[
K_t\mid B_t,p_t\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(B_t,p_t),
\]
\[
\operatorname{logit}(p_t)=\beta_0+\beta_1 C_{t,s}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{T}X_t,
\]
where \(X_t\) can include opponent strength, down distribution, pass depth, and quarterback release time. Units: \(p_t\) and \(r_t\) are dimensionless probabilities in \([0,1]\). Breakdown regime: if \(B_t=0\), \(r_t\) is undefined. If \(B_t\) is very small, binomial variance is large and \(\beta_1\) is weakly identified. The source’s visual claim of little correlation corresponds to failing to reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) in a low-power, 32-team sample [PAGE 43].

For adjusted sack rate, let \(S_j\in\{0,1\}\) indicate a sack on pass play \(j\). The source says adjusted sack rate accounts for down, distance, pass attempts, and opponent context [PAGE 45]. A formal weighted estimator is:
\[
\widehat{ASR}_t=
\frac{\sum_{j\in D_t} w_j S_j}
{\sum_{j\in D_t} w_j},
\]
where \(w_j=f(\text{down}_j,\text{yards\_to\_go}_j,\text{opponent}_j)\). Units: \(\widehat{ASR}_t\) is a probability in \([0,1]\). Breakdown regime: if \(\sum_{j\in D_t}w_j=0\), the estimator is undefined. If weights are estimated from the same data, standard errors must propagate weight uncertainty.

For Adjusted Line Yards, the source describes responsibility weights: a loss is assigned 120 percent responsibility, a 0-to-4-yard rush is assigned 100 percent, a 5-to-10-yard rush is assigned 50 percent, and a rush over 11 yards is assigned 0 percent [PAGE 48]. One formalization in yard-credit units is:
\[
h(y)=
\begin{cases}
1.20y, & y \text{ negative},\\
y, & 0\le y\le 4,\\
4+0.50(y-4), & 4<y\le 10,\\
7.00, & y>10.
\end{cases}
\]
Then:
\[
\mathrm{ALY}_t=\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{j=1}^{N_t} h(y_j),
\]
where \(N_t\) is rushing attempts and \(y_j\) is yards gained. Units: yards per rush. Breakdown regime: if \(N_t=0\), \(\mathrm{ALY}_t\) is undefined. If the source’s prose is interpreted as applying percentages to whole runs rather than marginal yards, the numeric value changes; the equation therefore encodes an explicit assumption.

For the weighted Adjusted Line Yards construction, the source standardizes average elusive rating using `scale()` and subtracts the resulting z-score from Adjusted Line Yards [PAGE 53]. Let \(E_t\) be the team average elusive rating, \(\bar E\) the league mean, and \(s_E\) the standard deviation:
\[
z_t=\frac{E_t-\bar E}{s_E},
\]
\[
\mathrm{WALY}_t=\mathrm{ALY}_t-z_t.
\]
Units: \(\mathrm{ALY}_t\) is in yard-credit units, while \(z_t\) is dimensionless. The subtraction is unit-mixed, but it is the source’s explicit elementary feature-engineering choice [PAGE 53]. Breakdown regime: if \(s_E=0\), \(z_t\) is undefined. If \(E_t\) is measured with error, \(\mathrm{WALY}_t\) inherits attenuation bias.

For explosive plays per touchdown drive, the source groups by team, game, and drive, computes maximum yards gained on a drive, and defines an explosive play as:
\[
E_d=\mathbf{1}\{M_d\ge 20\},
\]
where \(M_d\) is the maximum single-play yardage in touchdown drive \(d\), after excluding special teams, fumbles, and interceptions [PAGE 55]. The team-level probability is:
\[
\hat p_t=\frac{1}{n_t}\sum_{d=1}^{n_t}E_d.
\]
A binomial model is:
\[
X_t\mid n_t,p_t\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n_t,p_t),
\]
with log-likelihood:
\[
\ell(p_t)=\sum_{d=1}^{n_t}
\left[E_d\log p_t+(1-E_d)\log(1-p_t)\right].
\]
Units: \(\ell(p_t)\) is in nats; dividing by \(\ln 2\) converts it to bits. Breakdown regime: if \(n_t=0\), \(\hat p_t\) is undefined. If \(\hat p_t\) is near 0 or 1, Wald intervals are unreliable and exact or Wilson intervals are preferable.

\mathrm{AIC}=2k-2\ell(\hat\theta),
\]
where \(k\) is the number of estimated parameters and \(\ell\) is the maximized log-likelihood. Units: nats; bits are obtained by division by \(\ln 2\). Breakdown regime: AIC comparison is invalid when models are fit on different filtered samples or when the outcome definitions differ.

The proper recursive model therefore includes priors about opponent behavior, quarterback clock, game state, and the analyst’s own adjustment choices.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical vetoes constrain any claim that unique interior blocker combinations directly cause pressure, sack, or run-blocking outcomes. The first veto is a structural counting veto. The source says a team’s starting offensive front is conceptually five players [PAGE 34]. If the eligible set after filtering to T, G, and C contains fewer than five players, the combination variable is undefined. Proof: \(\operatorname{Top5}\) requires a domain of cardinality at least five. If \(|\{i:P_i\in\{T,G,C\}\}|<5\), no five-player set exists. This veto is not merely statistical; it is a set-theoretic breakdown.

The second veto is a temporal biomechanical veto. A sack is not only a blocking failure; it is a race between pass-rush arrival and quarterback release. Let \(T_p\) be the arrival time of the first effective rusher and \(T_q\) the quarterback’s release time. A necessary condition for a sack is \(T_p\le T_q\). If \(T_q\) is below the minimum plausible pressure-arrival time, the sack probability is driven toward zero regardless of blocker continuity. The source supports this with the 2020 observation that Roethlisberger and Rivers had extremely quick releases, making it difficult for defenses to reach them despite line issues [PAGE 47]. Therefore, any claim of the form “more combinations imply more sacks” is vetoed unless \(T_q\) is conditioned upon.

The third veto is a run-attribution veto. The source quotes Football Outsiders’ warning that “a team with a very good running back will appear higher no matter how bad their line, and a team with a great line will appear lower if the running back is terrible” [PAGE 25]. Let observed run success \(Y\) be a function of line quality \(L\) and back elusiveness \(E\): \(Y=g(L,E)\). If \(E\) varies strongly and is omitted, the estimated effect of \(L\) is biased. The source’s weighted Adjusted Line Yards attempts to address this by subtracting a standardized elusive rating from Adjusted Line Yards [PAGE 53]. However, if \(E\) is measured with error, the adjustment can overcorrect or undercorrect. The veto is valid: run-blocking conclusions are not physically identifiable without separating blocker responsibility from runner elusiveness.

The fourth veto is a weather-layer veto. The source does not provide explicit weather covariates for the 2022 offensive-line analysis. If wind, precipitation, temperature, or surface conditions affect passing accuracy, run success, or pressure conversion, then any unadjusted association between blocker combinations and outcomes may be confounded by environmental state. Let \(W\) be weather state. For a causal claim \(C\to Y\) to be identified, we need conditional ignorability: \(Y(c)\perp C\mid X,W\). If \(W\) is omitted and associated with both game conditions and outcome measurement, the claim is not identifiable. This is a cross-layer veto because the physical environment can change the mapping from blocker continuity to observed football outcomes.

The final adjudication is therefore restrictive. Unique combinations can be counted cleanly, but they cannot be treated as a standalone causal force. They are compatible with poor pass protection only when quarterback release time, opponent pass-rush quality, and structural availability are accounted for. They are compatible with poor run blocking only when back elusiveness and run direction are accounted for. The source’s own sequence of examples supports this nested veto structure: raw pressure and hit metrics show weak association; adjusted sack rate shows a broader but context-sensitive relationship; adjusted line yards require runner adjustments; explosive-drive analysis requires drive-level binary modeling and game-script awareness.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
