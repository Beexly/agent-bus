<THINK_TRACE>
The reasoning task is to transform the ActorLens paper into a formal analytic chain without adding betting constructs or fabricated equations. The source defines a two-stage detection pipeline: first remove low-level actors using deterministic thresholds, then expose high-level actors through interactive visual analytics and human judgment. The critical gray zone is that high-level actors are not primarily defined by extreme aggregate statistics. The paper states that their “in-game statistics of high-level actors, particularly the combination of kills, deaths, and assists (KDA), may appear no different from those of regular players” [PAGE 2]. Therefore, the inference cannot be reduced to a single scalar anomaly score. It must combine temporal inactivity, event priority, transition rarity, cohort comparison, and match replay evidence.

The first derivation layer is the low-level filter. The paper formalizes AFK detection through accumulated idle time and a threshold of 120 seconds [PAGE 13]. It formalizes feeding detection through suspected death types and a damage-ratio test [PAGE 15]. These rules are exact, threshold-based, and break down when actors evade the thresholds. The second layer is the high-level actor metric. The paper defines an inactivity percentage using 20-second intervals, averaging hero damage contribution and economic contribution, with inactivity declared when the activeness score is below 0.1 [PAGE 5]. This produces a dimensionless probability-like score in [0,1]. The paper also defines priority events by an expert-derived ranking: turret destruction, dragon killing, hero killing, death, assist, poke, monster killing, minion killing, inaction [PAGE 5]. When multiple events occur in a minute, the highest-ranked event becomes the representative event. This is a compression operator that loses information, but it makes temporal behavior inspectable.

The third layer is cohort comparison. The system compares the focal player against three cohorts: selected players, the focal player’s historical matches, and matches of other players using the same hero [PAGE 6]. This is essential because the paper explicitly says that determining whether a player is a high-level actor requires “longitudinal analyses” and “cross-sectional analyses” [PAGE 2]. A low-probability event is not inherently toxic; it becomes suspicious when it is rare relative to the player’s own history and relative to hero norms. The formal object is therefore a context-conditioned anomaly, not an unconditional outlier.

The fourth layer is interactive prediction. The paper uses XGBoost to propagate user labels to unlabeled players [PAGE 6]. This introduces a supervised probability estimate over feature vectors. However, the paper also admits that “the training of interactive models requires many training instances while the number of given labels is typically small” [PAGE 9]. Thus, the model must be treated as a recommendation operator, not an autonomous adjudicator. The empirical result supports this caution: accuracy was reported as 74.17±7.12, with duration 25.92±10.48 minutes [PAGE 9]. The system improves speed but does not eliminate human uncertainty.

The final gray-zone audit concerns singularities. The inactivity percentage breaks down if the denominator of intervals is zero or if team damage/economy totals are zero. The priority-event operator breaks down when event logs are missing or when multiple events tie under the expert ranking. The UMAP distance breaks down when normalized metrics have zero variance or identical vectors, requiring collision avoidance [PAGE 6]. The feeder damage-ratio rule breaks down when the denominator of received damage is zero. The AFK rule breaks down for evasion below 120 seconds, as shown by the case where “idle time was only 111 seconds, below the threshold of 120 seconds for the AFK actor detection algorithm” [PAGE 8]. These breakdown regimes define the boundary where the formal system must hand control to human inspection.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let a match be indexed by \(m\), a player by \(i\), and a time interval by \(\tau\). The source states that multivariate time series data are “captured at 20-second intervals during the entirety of a given match” [PAGE 12], so the elementary temporal unit for inactivity measurement is \(\Delta t = 20\ \mathrm{s}\).

1. Inactivity operator.  
The paper says: “The metric computation involves determining the percentage of hero damage and economic contribution made by the player within each 20-second interval” [PAGE 5]. Let

\[
D_{i,m}(\tau) \in [0,1]
\]

be the player’s fraction of team hero damage in interval \(\tau\), and let

\[
E_{i,m}(\tau) \in [0,1]
\]

be the player’s fraction of team economic contribution in the same interval. The activeness score is defined by the paper: “The resulting activeness score is obtained as the average of these two percentages” [PAGE 5]. Therefore,

\[
A_{i,m}(\tau)
=
\frac{D_{i,m}(\tau) + E_{i,m}(\tau)}{2}.
\]

The inactivity indicator is

\[
I_{i,m}(\tau)
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
A_{i,m}(\tau) < 0.1
\right],
\]

because “If the activeness score is below 0.1, the player is deemed inactive during that interval” [PAGE 5]. If a match has \(N_m\) intervals, the inactivity percentage is

\[
P^{\mathrm{inact}}_{i,m}
=
\frac{1}{N_m}
\sum_{\tau=1}^{N_m}
I_{i,m}(\tau).
\]

Units: dimensionless. Range: \([0,1]\).  
Assumptions: team damage and economy totals are positive in each interval.  
Breakdown regime: if team damage or team economy is zero, \(D_{i,m}(\tau)\) or \(E_{i,m}(\tau)\) is undefined unless a missing-data rule is imposed. If \(N_m = 0\), the percentage is undefined. If the player is inactive in all intervals, \(P^{\mathrm{inact}}_{i,m}=1\); if never inactive, \(P^{\mathrm{inact}}_{i,m}=0\).

2. Priority-event operator.  
The source defines the expert ordering: “The resulting prioritization sequence is as follows: turret destruction, dragon killing, hero killing, death, assist in killing, poke, monster killing, minion killing, and inaction” [PAGE 5]. Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be the event set and let \(r(e)\) be the rank, with \(r(\text{turret})=1\), \(r(\text{dragon})=2\), …, \(r(\text{inaction})=9\). For player \(i\) in minute \(t\), let \(S_{i,m}(t)\subseteq \mathcal{E}\) be the observed event set. The priority event is

\[
e^{*}_{i,m}(t)
=
\arg\min_{e \in S_{i,m}(t)} r(e).
\]

If \(S_{i,m}(t)=\varnothing\), the system uses inaction, since inaction is the terminal fallback in the ranking.  
Units: categorical event type.  
Breakdown regime: if two events share the same expert rank or if event logs are missing, the operator is ambiguous. If a player has multiple events in the same minute, lower-ranked events are hidden.

3. UMAP projection distance.  
The glyph representation uses eleven metrics: nine priority-event counts, inactive percentage, and reports [PAGE 6]. The source states: “The distance between the glyphs is computed by summing the squares of the discrepancies among the normalized metrics” [PAGE 6]. Let

\[
z_{i,m} \in \mathbb{R}^{11}
\]

be the normalized metric vector for player \(i\) in match \(m\). Then

\[
d^2(i,m; j,n)
=
\sum_{k=1}^{11}
\left(
z_{i,m,k} - z_{j,n,k}
\right)^2.
\]

Units: dimensionless after normalization.  
Assumptions: each metric is scaled to comparable range.  
Breakdown regime: if a metric has zero variance, normalization is undefined. If two vectors are identical, \(d^2=0\), so collision avoidance is required, as the paper notes UMAP with collision avoidance is used [PAGE 6].

4. Low-level AFK rule.  
The paper provides Algorithm 1 with “threshold = 120” and the rule “if idle_time ≥threshold then” [PAGE 13]. Formally,

\[
L^{\mathrm{AFK}}_{i,m}
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
t^{\mathrm{idle}}_{i,m} \geq 120\ \mathrm{s}
\right].
\]

Units: seconds.  
Breakdown regime: evasion below 120 seconds. The source gives a concrete example: “idle time was only 111 seconds, below the threshold of 120 seconds for the AFK actor detection algorithm” [PAGE 8].

5. Low-level feeder rule.  
Algorithm 2 sets “threshold = 0.4” and “count = 3” [PAGE 15]. For each death \(d\), let

\[
p_h = \text{player damage to hero},
\quad
p_t = \text{player damage to turret},
\]

\[
h_p = \text{hero damage to player},
\quad
t_p = \text{turret damage to player}.
\]

The disguise-resistance test is

\[
\frac{p_h + p_t}{h_p + t_p}
\leq
0.4.
\]

This is the source rule: “if (player_to_hero+ player_to_turret)/(hero_to_player +turret_to_player) ≤threshold then” [PAGE 15]. Let \(S_i\) be the number of suspected deaths. Then

\[
L^{\mathrm{feed}}_{i,m}
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
S_i \geq 3
\right].
\]

Units: dimensionless damage ratio and death count.  
Breakdown regime: if \(h_p+t_p=0\), the ratio is undefined. If the player has fewer than three suspected deaths, the rule does not fire even if behavior is suspicious.

6. Human-machine posterior operator.  
The paper states: “The XGBoost model is trained on the current labels and features extracted, as described in Appendix E, which allows it to predict a label” [PAGE 6]. Let \(Y_i \in \{0,1\}\) denote normal versus actor, and let \(X_i\) be the feature vector. The interactive model estimates

\[
\hat{p}_i
=
\widehat{\Pr}(Y_i=1 \mid X_i).
\]

A decision rule can be written as

\[
\hat{Y}_i
=
\mathbf{1}
\left[
\hat{p}_i \geq \theta
\right],
\]

where \(\theta\) is a user-set acceptance threshold.  
Information-theoretic interpretation: if \(E\) is visual evidence and \(H(Y)\) is label entropy, expected evidence gain is

\[
I(Y;E)
=
H(Y) - H(Y \mid E),
\]

with units in bits if logarithms are base 2.  
Breakdown regime: with too few labels, \(\hat{p}_i\) is unreliable. The paper explicitly warns: “the training of interactive models requires many training instances while the number of given labels is typically small” [PAGE 9].

7. Evaluation metrics.  
The user study reports: “The accuracy for using the system to label players is 74.17±7.12, and the duration is 25.92±10.48 minutes” [PAGE 9]. The comparison condition is described as “which requires 52 seconds on average for labeling one player in one match” [PAGE 9]. These are empirical performance operators, not theoretical guarantees. The system also has a scalability boundary: “scalability is a critical concern for Projection View, as it is currently limited to a maximum of 300 players” [PAGE 9].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogical origin of ActorLens lies in the failure of simple toxic-behavior detection in MOBA games. The paper situates actors within a broader history of toxic behavior research: players may “act contrary to the team’s overall objectives” [PAGE 1], and low-level actors are classically detected through AFK or feeding [PAGE 2]. This creates a historical trap: early detection systems optimize for overt behaviors, but high-level actors adapt. The paper states that high-level actors “deliberately avoid participating in crucial events of the game, such as team fights” [PAGE 2]. This is a direct counter-move against event-based detectors.

The second lineage is anomaly detection. The related-work section lists statistical-based, distance-based, density-based, and cluster-based methods [PAGE 2]. However, the paper notes that automated anomaly detection is “impeded by unclear boundaries and insufficient labeled data” [PAGE 2]. This explains why ActorLens does not rely on a single anomaly score. Instead, it builds a human-in-the-loop visual analytics environment. The lineage moves from unsupervised outlier detection toward interactive evidence assembly.

The third lineage is game data visualization. The paper reviews gameplay visualization for training, developer insight, and role analysis [PAGE 3]. It identifies a gap: prior systems analyze overall game state but do not filter or identify abnormal individual behavior [PAGE 3]. ActorLens inherits the visual grammar of match summaries, event charts, and movement maps, but repurposes them for actor labeling.

The fourth lineage is longitudinal and cross-sectional comparison. The paper emphasizes that “it is necessary to track the player over a longer period of time and with greater granularity” [PAGE 2]. This is the multi-week trap: a single bad game may be involuntary, while repeated deviation from personal history and hero norms is more diagnostic. The three cohort modes operationalize this: selected cohort, history mode, and hero mode [PAGE 6].

A further trap is scalability. The projection view is limited to 300 players [PAGE 9]. If the input population exceeds this limit, the system must filter or partition. Another trap is label scarcity. The interactive XGBoost model can amplify early labeling errors if trained on too few examples. The empirical accuracy of 74.17±7.12 shows that the system is useful but not definitive [PAGE 9]. Thus the genealogy of the system is not a march toward automation; it is a disciplined retreat from overautomated detection toward inspectable evidence.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind problem is that both the inspector and the high-level actor model each other. The inspector expects abnormal behavior to appear in obvious statistics; the high-level actor knows this and avoids obvious abnormality. The paper states that high-level actors “evade detection through simple rules and measures” [PAGE 2]. Therefore, the detection system must model not only the player’s behavior but also the inspector’s likely habits under time pressure.

Crisis habit regression occurs when inspection time is compressed. The traditional workflow requires reviewing entire game videos, with an average game duration of 20 minutes [PAGE 3]. The paper says manual review is “time-consuming and subjective” [PAGE 2]. Under time pressure, an inspector may regress to salient cues: deaths, AFK flags, KDA, or obvious feeding. This is precisely the habit that high-level actors exploit. If the inspector relies only on KDA, the actor can maintain a normal-looking KDA while avoiding crucial events [PAGE 2].

The counter-trap is the ActorLens design. Instead of asking “is the statistic extreme?”, it asks “is the event rare in context?” The system uses three comparison frames: selected cohort, player history, and same-hero cohort [PAGE 6]. It also uses minute-level priority events and transition flows [PAGE 6]. A suspicious pattern is not merely “death occurred”; it is “death transition probability is low in the relevant cohort” or “inaction occurs when same-hero players almost never have inaction.” The paper gives an example where “the percentage of inaction events in the beginning was low, indicating that the player’s behavior was atypical” [PAGE 8]. This is a theory-of-mind move: the system treats rarity relative to expected player behavior as evidence.

The clock layer is explicit. Matches are summarized by minute [PAGE 5]. Time-series data are captured at 20-second intervals [PAGE 12]. Movement data are logged at 10-second intervals [PAGE 12]. The AFK rule uses a 120-second threshold [PAGE 13]. These temporal resolutions create the observation window. If the clock is too coarse, short evasive actions disappear. If the clock is too fine, the analyst is overloaded. The system chooses minute-level priority events for overview and finer replay brushing for verification.

The recursive loop is therefore: inspector habit -> actor counter-habit -> visual evidence that exposes context rarity -> updated label -> model recommendation -> inspector verification. The loop breaks if the inspector ignores cohort rarity or if the model is trained on too few labels. The paper acknowledges that interactive models may not be reliable with small label sets [PAGE 9]. Thus, the safest recursive policy is conservative: use the system to narrow candidates, but require replay evidence before final labeling.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto concerns whether physical, biomechanical, or weather explanations can override the in-game evidence. In this source, the adjudication layer is the game’s event abstraction. The paper describes the environment as a virtual MOBA scene with bases, turrets, minions, jungle areas, drakes, and baron nasher [PAGE 3]. The analytical objects are server-side or replay-derived events: player information, match summaries, multivariate event sequences, multivariate time series, movement data, and algorithmic derived data [PAGE 5]. There is no measured weather variable, no biomechanical sensor, and no physical environmental covariate in the abstraction.

Formally, let \(Y_i\) be the actor label, \(S_i\) be in-game evidence, \(W_i\) be weather, and \(B_i\) be biomechanical state. The source’s evidence model is

\[
P(Y_i \mid S_i, W_i, B_i)
=
P(Y_i \mid S_i)
\]

under the assumption that \(W_i\) and \(B_i\) have no direct measurement channel and can affect \(Y_i\) only through \(S_i\). This is not a claim that weather or biomechanics are irrelevant in the physical world; it is a claim that the paper’s inferential layer does not observe them directly. Therefore, any veto argument that says “the player was inactive because of weather” or “the player died because of biomechanical fatigue” is inadmissible unless it is translated into observed in-game evidence such as idle time, movement, damage, death location, or event sequence.

Weather veto proof. The virtual match has no weather operator in the data abstraction. Movement is logged at 10-second intervals [PAGE 12], and time-series metrics are logged at 20-second intervals [PAGE 12]. If weather affected behavior, its effect must appear as changed movement, inactivity, damage, or economy. Direct weather attribution is vetoed because no weather variable exists in the formal evidence set.

Biomechanical veto proof. Suppose fatigue affects hand movement or reaction time. The system can observe the consequence only through event probabilities, movement trajectories, or idle time. The source does not measure tremor, reaction latency, or physiological state. Therefore, biomechanical explanations are not independent evidence. They are admissible only as latent-cause narratives that must be encoded in observable features. If a player has idle time below 120 seconds but still shows abnormal inactivity and deaths, the biomechanical explanation does not override the event evidence; it merely suggests an unobserved cause behind the observed pattern.

Physical-location veto proof. The match summary chart and map chart reconstruct battle progress and trajectories [PAGE 7]. The paper uses trajectories to determine whether a player participated in team combat or “solely focused on his own lane” [PAGE 8]. Physical location in the real world is not observed. Therefore, physical-world location claims are vetoed unless they correspond to in-game hero position.

The final adjudication rule is: evidence from \(S_i\) controls. If a proposed physical, biomechanical, or weather cause does not change any element of \(S_i\) — idle time, damage share, economy share, priority event, transition probability, trajectory, death context, or cohort comparison — it has zero decision weight in the source’s formal framework.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The RLVR counterfactual veto asks whether a reward model or reinforcement-learned detector would be invalidated by verifiable source-grounded counterexamples. The following three veto scenarios are directly supported by the paper.

Veto Scenario 1: AFK threshold evasion.  
A model that labels actors only when idle time is at least 120 seconds will fail on the documented case where “idle time was only 111 seconds, below the threshold of 120 seconds for the AFK actor detection algorithm” [PAGE 8]. The player was still judged suspicious because of inaction, deaths, and trajectory evidence.  
Formal veto: if detector \(f\) uses only \(t^{\mathrm{idle}}\geq120\), then for the case \(t^{\mathrm{idle}}=111\), \(f=0\), but the expert label can be \(Y=1\). Therefore, the detector is not sufficient.  
Prediction: adding combined inaction and death features increases true positives for AFK-like evasion. Adding trajectory evidence reduces false positives from players who are briefly idle but not malicious.

Veto Scenario 2: KDA-only normal appearance.  
A model that uses KDA alone will fail because high-level actors may have KDA that appears normal. The paper says the KDA of high-level actors “may appear no different from those of regular players” [PAGE 2]. The formal KDA operator is “KDA (kills plus assists divided by deaths plus one)” [PAGE 7].  
Formal veto: let \(KDA_i\) be in the normal range but let \(P^{\mathrm{inact}}_{i,m}\) be high and let priority-event transitions be rare. A KDA-only detector returns normal, while the multi-evidence detector returns suspicious.  
Prediction: event-transition rarity and inactivity will identify actors that KDA misses, especially when nonparticipation occurs during team combat or objective control.

Veto Scenario 3: Isolated damage or economy without team context.  
A model that rewards high damage or high economy without team context can misclassify a player who attacks unimportant targets or farms while avoiding crucial fights. The paper notes that a high-level actor “may appear to perform well with a high damage indicator, despite intentionally attacking only unimportant opposing heroes” [PAGE 2]. In the dragon case, the player “solely focused on his own lane” while teammates died and the dragon failed [PAGE 8].  
Formal veto: if detector uses only individual damage or economy, it may score the player as active. But cohort comparison shows low-rank priority events and failure to participate in team combat. The source defines team combat as an event when opposing players are in close proximity and engage in combat [PAGE 5].  
Prediction: a detector that includes team-combat proximity, objective participation, and cohort-relative event rarity will veto false positives from isolated economic activity.

These vetoes imply a design rule: RLVR reward functions must not reward only low-level thresholds or aggregate statistics. They must include context-conditioned evidence, especially longitudinal history, hero cohort comparison, event transitions, and replay trajectory. The reported accuracy of 74.17±7.12 [PAGE 9] indicates that even a strong visual analytics system remains uncertain. Therefore, any learned reward model should be treated as a candidate generator, not a final adjudicator, unless it satisfies the source’s evidence hierarchy.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
