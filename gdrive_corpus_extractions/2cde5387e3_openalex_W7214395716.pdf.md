The sabotage model is quadratic in standardized rivalry:

\[
F_{it}=\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\beta_1 Z_{it}+\beta_2 Z_{it}^{2}+\gamma^{\top}X_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},
\]

with reported \(\beta_1=-0.3508^{***}\) and \(\beta_2=0.1685^{***}\) for disruptive-intent fouls [PAGE 21]. The marginal effect is

\[
\frac{\partial \mathbb{E}[F_{it}\mid Z_{it}]}{\partial Z_{it}}
=\beta_1+2\beta_2 Z_{it}
\quad [\text{fouls game}^{-1}\text{ per SD of rivalry}],
\]

and the turning point is

\[
Z^{*}=-\frac{\beta_1}{2\beta_2}
=\frac{0.3508}{2(0.1685)}
\approx 1.0404\ \text{SD}.
\]

Because \(\bar R\approx22.58\) and \(s_R\approx34.5\) rivalry points [PAGE 34–35],

\[
R^{*}\approx 22.58+34.5(1.0404)\approx58.5\ \text{rivalry points},
\]

matching the paper’s statement that “the minimum number of fouls is reached at 58.5 rivalry points” [PAGE 20]. The second derivative is \(2\beta_2=0.337>0\), so the estimated quadratic is locally convex. Breakdown regimes: if \(\beta_2\le0\), no U-shape exists; if \(\beta_2=0\), the vertex is undefined; if \(Z^{*}\) lies outside the observed support, the U-shape is an extrapolation; if rivalry has zero within-team variance, the within estimator is collinear with \(\alpha_i\); if the normalization denominator (the team’s maximum raw rivalry score) were zero, the relative score would be undefined, though the survey design allows 1–10 named rivals and avoids that case by construction [PAGE 10]. The symmetric/asymmetric moderator equation is

\[
Y_{it}=\alpha_i+\lambda_t+\beta Z_{it}
+\theta_H Z_{it}H_{it}
+\theta_L Z_{it}L_{it}
+\gamma^{\top}X_{it}+\varepsilon_{it},
\]

where \(H_{it}=1\) if the focal team rates the rivalry at least 10 points above the opponent and \(L_{it}=1\) if the opponent rates it at least 10 points above the focal team; the omitted category is symmetric rivalry within 10 points [PAGE 19]. For yards, the baseline symmetric coefficient is \(-4.1164^{**}\), the higher-rivalry interaction is \(2.4406\) (not significant), and the lower-rivalry interaction is \(7.2073^{**}\); for offensive points, the corresponding values are \(-0.3835^{*}\), \(0.1363\), and \(1.1219^{**}\) [PAGE 18]. Propensity-score matching defines treatment as \(D_{it}=1[Z_{it}\ge\bar Z_i]\) and estimates

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{ATT}}
=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{it}(1)-Y_{it}(0)\mid D_{it}=1\right],
\]

using 1:2 matching [PAGE 17]. Its breakdown regime is common-support failure: if \(\Pr(D_{it}=1\mid X_{it})\) is near 0 or 1 for a subgroup, the ATT is not identified by matching. The paper reports that matching yields substantively similar estimates [PAGE 17–18]. No information-theoretic estimator is used in the source; any Shannon entropy in nats would be an external construct, not a source operator.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is layered. Lazear and Rosen’s rank-order tournament model supplies the incentive logic that a larger winner–loser prize differential raises effort, while Lazear’s sabotage model supplies the countervailing logic that contestants may reduce an opponent’s output [PAGE 4; PAGE 36–38 references]. Social comparison theory and social identity/categorization theory explain why similar, familiar, repeatedly encountered opponents become ingroup–outgroup symbols and why defeating a rival can raise self-esteem [PAGE 2–4]. Yerkes–Dodson arousal theory supplies the inverted-U pressure story, and Kilduff et al.’s rivalry program supplies the modern measurement and sports evidence [PAGE 5–7]. The retrocausal trap is temporal: the rivalry survey is from 2015 onward, but the performance panel begins in 2009 [PAGE 9–10]. The paper justifies backward application by arguing that “rivalry has been shown to be stable over time” [PAGE 11]. That is plausible but not verified for every team-year; if a rivalry intensified or decayed after 2009, the fixed score becomes a multi-week, multi-season measurement-error trap. A second trap is fan–player projection: the source concedes that “fans’ opinions do not necessarily correspond to players’ opinions” and that players may identify less with a team than fans [PAGE 11]. A third trap is roster turnover: the quarterback is treated as the face of the franchise and the most likely conduit of fan rivalry, but the paper also controls for quarterback rookie status and quarterback change because those alter performance [PAGE 13–14]. A fourth trap is the COVID-affected 2020 season; the paper repeats analyses without 2020 and reports similar results [PAGE 18]. The genealogy therefore supports a conditional reading: the result is not “rivalry always harms,” but “under this fan-derived, time-stable rivalry proxy, symmetric rivalry predicts lower offensive output and a U-shaped disruptive-foul count.”
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive structure is explicit in the asymmetry test. Let \(R_{it}\) be the focal team’s first-order rivalry perception and \(R_{ji,t}\) the opponent’s perception of the focal team. The difference \(\Delta_{it}=R_{it}-R_{ji,t}\) operationalizes a second-order belief: what the focal team thinks the opponent feels, and whether that feeling is reciprocated [PAGE 19]. In symmetric rivalry, both sides hold high first-order and second-order stakes; the paper argues this “has a reinforcing effect, maximizing psychological pressure on both sides” [PAGE 20]. That is the crisis-habit-regression channel: high arousal narrows attention and shifts behavior from automatic execution to controlled, effortful processing, which is consistent with the negative performance association [PAGE 5]. However, the paper rejects the simplest “choking under pressure” story because “a team did not perform significantly worse than expected in rivalry games” [PAGE 19–20]. So crisis-habit regression is not supported as a general performance mechanism; it is better supported for sabotage, where the U-shape “may reflect a transition from impulsive misconduct at low rivalry to strategic misconduct at high rivalry, with a period of cautious restraint in between” [PAGE 23]. The counter-trap is the asymmetric case: when the opponent rates the rivalry higher, the focal team “is under less pressure and may be able to use the tension caused by the rivalry to create a clear focus and to perform better” [PAGE 20]. That is a theory-of-mind advantage: the less rivalrous team can exploit the more rivalrous team’s arousal. The clock layer is underidentified. The panel uses game days and season controls, and dynamic models with lagged dependent variables show “coefficients of the lagged dependent variables are always insignificant at the 10% level” [PAGE 18]. But the source does not model within-game clock, score margin, or drive-level time pressure, so a “clock” mechanism—late-game narrowing, hurry-up pressure, or fourth-quarter arousal—remains a residual trap rather than a tested operator.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto is partial. The models control “weather influences (1 = outdoors, 0 = indoors), field condition (1 = turf, 0 = grass)” plus overtime, attendance, stadium, quarterback rookie status, quarterback change, Pro Bowl players, and average experience [PAGE 13]. In the reported performance table, “Outdoors” has a positive coefficient of \(1.7332^{**}\) for offensive points but is not significant for yards or passer rating; “Turf” is negative but not significant; overtime is large and positive for yards (\(47.0367^{***}\)) and points (\(2.5729^{***}\)); quarterback change is negative for yards (\(-10.7709^{***}\)) and points (\(-1.4253^{***}\)) [PAGE 14]. A weather-veto proof therefore cannot be absolute: the binary outdoors/indoors control is too coarse to rule out temperature, wind, precipitation, or field-surface microstate. The biomechanical veto is also partial: net yards subtract sacks and yard loss [PAGE 11–12], and passer rating decomposes completion percentage, yardage, touchdowns, and interceptions [PAGE 12], but the source has no direct load, injury, collision-force, or fatigue measure. The strongest cross-layer adjudication is discriminant: if a physical or weather factor were the true driver, it should produce a consistent sign across net yards, points, passer rating, and defensive performance. Instead, defensive performance also “deteriorates in rivalry games” [PAGE 15], while the opponent’s rivalry score is not significant for focal defensive performance [PAGE 16]. That pattern is more consistent with a focal-perception channel than with a pure opponent-quality or weather channel, but it does not eliminate biomechanical mediation. The correct proof statement is therefore a veto of the simple confound, not a veto of all physical mechanisms.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Opponent-score swap. Replace the focal team’s standardized rivalry score with the opponent’s standardized rivalry score in the defensive-performance equation. Prediction: the focal team’s own score remains negative for defensive performance, while the opponent’s score is null; the paper reports the opponent-score coefficient \(0.1784\) with standard error \(0.1482\), not significant, while the focal score is \(-0.3189^{**}\) [PAGE 16]. Veto condition: if the opponent-score coefficient becomes significantly negative and the focal-score coefficient collapses, the focal-perception interpretation fails.
2. Geography substitution. Replace the survey rivalry score with geographic proximity between the two teams. Prediction: the rivalry effect disappears; the paper states that with geographic proximity “no significant effects are apparent” [PAGE 18]. Veto condition: if geography produces the same negative performance and U-shaped sabotage pattern with comparable precision, the survey score is not doing independent work.
3. Temporal/dynamic stress test. Exclude the 2020 season and add a lagged dependent variable via the Arellano–Bond style dynamic specification. Prediction: the rivalry coefficients retain sign and approximate magnitude, and the lagged dependent variable remains insignificant at the 10% level; the paper reports that dynamics are negligible and that excluding 2020 leaves results similar [PAGE 18]. Veto condition: if the rivalry coefficient flips sign, loses significance, or the lagged term becomes significant, the claimed effect is a persistence artifact rather than a rivalry association.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
