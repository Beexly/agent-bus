The formal operator chain in the paper can be written as a metadata-conditioned projection operator followed by a gated distance-matching rule.

Let a detection at frame t have bounding-box center in pixel coordinates

\[
x_t^i = \begin{bmatrix} u_t^i \\ v_t^i \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\]

where \(u\) and \(v\) are in pixels. The paper defines the 2D-to-3D projection as

\[
X_t^{3D} = H_{m_t}\left(\widehat{X}_t^{2D}\right),
\]

where \(H_{m_t}\) is derived from the metadata \(m_t\) at frame t [PAGE 4]. The source quote is: “For each detection at frame t, the world coordinate X3d_t is computed using the projection H(·) derived from the metadata mt” [PAGE 4].

The intrinsic matrix is obtained from field-of-view. The paper’s Eq. (1) introduces the camera model from image width \(w\), height \(h\), and field-of-view \(\mathrm{fov}\) [PAGE 3]. In a conventional homogeneous pinhole form, the formal matrix is

\[
K =
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{w/2}{\tan(\mathrm{fov}/2)} & 0 & w/2 \\
0 & \dfrac{w/2}{\tan(\mathrm{fov}/2)} & h/2 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Units: \(w,h\) are pixels, \(\mathrm{fov}\) is radians in the tangent function, and \(K\) maps homogeneous pixel coordinates to normalized camera coordinates. The extracted source prints Eq. (1) with a zero-like final row, but Eq. (3) uses \(K^{-1}\), so the operational assumption must be that the homogeneous lower-right entry is nonzero. If that entry is literally zero, \(K\) is singular and the projection operator is undefined. This is the first breakdown regime: \(\tan(\mathrm{fov}/2)=0\), \(w=0\), \(h=0\), or a singular homogeneous row makes the inverse projection invalid.

The rotation matrix from camera coordinates to world coordinates is given by Eq. (2):

\[
R(\alpha,\beta,0) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\alpha\cos\beta & -\sin\alpha & \cos\alpha\sin\beta \\
\sin\alpha\cos\beta & \cos\alpha & \sin\alpha\sin\beta \\
-\sin\beta & 0 & \cos\beta
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Here \(\alpha\) is yaw and \(\beta\) is pitch. The paper specifies, “\(\alpha = 90^\circ -\) gimbal heading and \(\beta =\) gimbal pitch, where gimbal heading and gimbal pitch are given by metadata” [PAGE 3]. Units: angles must be converted to radians before evaluating trigonometric functions. Assumptions: roll is zero or ignored; gimbal heading and pitch are synchronized with the frame; heading wrap-around is handled correctly. Breakdown: if metadata heading is off by a large angle, \(R\) rotates the ray to an incorrect world direction. If roll is nonzero but omitted, the camera ray is systematically biased.

The directional ray is

\[
\mathrm{ray}_i = R \cdot K^{-1} \cdot \frac{x_i}{\lVert x_i \rVert} \in \mathbb{R}^3.
\]

This corresponds to the paper’s Eq. (3) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(x_i/\lVert x_i\rVert\) is a normalized pixel vector, \(K^{-1}\) maps it into a camera-frame direction, and \(R\) rotates it into the world frame. The resulting ray is dimensionless as a direction vector. The paper assumes that the object lies on the sea plane \(z=0\). The intersection is

\[
X_i^{3D} = \mathrm{Loc}_{\mathrm{object}}
= \mathrm{Loc}_{\mathrm{drone}} + \frac{Z_{\mathrm{drone}}}{Z_{\mathrm{ray}_i}} \, \mathrm{ray}_i.
\]

This is Eq. (4) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(\mathrm{Loc}_{\mathrm{drone}}\) and \(X_i^{3D}\) are world positions, preferably meters in a local tangent plane; \(Z_{\mathrm{drone}}\) is altitude above the sea plane in meters; \(Z_{\mathrm{ray}_i}\) is the third component of the ray. The source’s Table 2 reports latitude and longitude with a relative center, altitude, gimbal pitch, gimbal heading, and speeds [PAGE 4]. For formal SI use, latitude and longitude in degrees must be converted to local East-North-Up coordinates; altitude and position are then in meters.

The first-order projection error can be written as

\[
\delta X \approx
\frac{Z_{\mathrm{drone}}}{Z_{\mathrm{ray}}}\,\delta \mathrm{ray}
+
\frac{\mathrm{ray}}{Z_{\mathrm{ray}}}\,\delta Z_{\mathrm{drone}}
-
\frac{Z_{\mathrm{drone}}\,\mathrm{ray}}{Z_{\mathrm{ray}}^2}\,\delta Z_{\mathrm{ray}}.
\]

This shows that the world-coordinate uncertainty grows as \(|Z_{\mathrm{ray}}|\) becomes small. The exact singularity regime is

\[
Z_{\mathrm{ray}} \to 0,
\]

which corresponds to a ray nearly parallel to the sea plane. In that regime, the intersection distance diverges and small angular metadata errors create large world-coordinate errors. Another singularity is

\[
Z_{\mathrm{drone}} \le 0,
\]

which violates the assumption that the camera is above the sea plane. If the ray points away from the plane, the computed intersection is physically invalid.

\[
C_{\mathrm{dis}}(T_i,T_j)
=
\left\|
X^{3D}_{t_{\mathrm{exit}},i}
-
X^{3D}_{t_{\mathrm{entry}},j}
\right\|_2.
\]

This is Eq. (6) [PAGE 4]. Units: meters if world coordinates are in meters. The matching rule is

\[
\text{match}(i,j)
\iff
C_{\mathrm{dis}}(T_i,T_j) \le \tau_{\mathrm{match}},
\]

and if the cost exceeds \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\), the entering track is treated as new. The threshold \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\) has units of meters. Breakdown occurs if \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\) is too small: legitimate re-entries become fragments; if too large: distinct objects merge.

=
X^{3D}_{t_{\mathrm{exit}},i}
+

\]

valid if

\[
\Delta t \le \tau_{\mathrm{memory}},
\]

where

\[
\Delta t = t' - t_{\mathrm{exit}}.
\]

This corresponds to Eq. (7) [PAGE 4]. The exit velocity is estimated by a finite difference:

\[
V^{T_i}_{\mathrm{exit}}
=
\frac{
X^{3D}_{t_{\mathrm{exit}},i}
-
X^{3D}_{t_{\mathrm{exit}}-w,i}
}{w}.
\]

This is Eq. (8) [PAGE 4]. Units depend on the time unit of \(w\). If \(w\) is measured in frames and \(\Delta t\) is also in frames, velocity has units of meters per frame. If \(\Delta t\) is converted to seconds, velocity is meters per second. The paper warns that extrapolation is bounded because “the world coordinates after such duration are often unreliable and may be confused with some new tracks” [PAGE 4]. The exact memory breakdown is

\[
\Delta t > \tau_{\mathrm{memory}}.
\]

Backward extrapolation uses the same logic with entry terms substituted for exit terms [PAGE 4].

Matching Threshold Expansion is given by Eq. (9):

\[
\tau'_{\mathrm{match}}
=
\lambda \cdot
\left(
\Delta t_{\mathrm{exit}}
+
\Delta t_{\mathrm{entry}}
\right)
\cdot
\tau_{\mathrm{match}}.
\]

Here \(\lambda\) is a scalar expansion factor. Dimensional consistency requires care. If \(\Delta t\) is in frames, then \(\lambda\) must carry units of inverse frames if \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\) is in meters. If \(\Delta t\) is converted to seconds, then \(\lambda\) must carry units of inverse seconds. The paper uses this expansion because tracks that disappear or reappear need a larger spatial gate after longer absence [PAGE 4]. Breakdown occurs when the expansion grows without bound or when \(\lambda\) is too large: the gate eventually accepts implausible matches.

Class-wise ReID introduces class-specific parameters:

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{match}}^{(c)}, \quad
\lambda^{(c)}, \quad
\tau_{\mathrm{memory}}^{(c)},
\]

where \(c\) is a class such as boat or swimmer. The paper says it sets “a larger \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\) for boats due to their potential for higher relative velocity” [PAGE 4]. This is formalizable as a class-conditioned gating rule. However, because the testing split is evaluated without class separation, “testing only considers all tracks as the same classes during evaluation” [PAGE 5], the metric may not fully expose class-association errors unless they cause ID switches.

The evaluation metrics are dimensionless fractions, usually reported as percentages. HOTA and IDF1 are higher-is-better; MOTP is a distance-like error; FP and FN are counts; IDs and Frag are identity-change and fragmentation counts. These are not betting quantities; they are tracking-quality metrics.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of MG-MOT comes from tracking-by-detection. The paper situates itself among “DeepSORT [22], ByteTrack [25], and BoT-SORT [2]” [PAGE 2]. Those methods descend from an older architecture: detect first, then associate detections using motion, appearance, or hybrid cues. DeepSORT introduced appearance embeddings into the SORT lineage; ByteTrack emphasized low-confidence detections; BoT-SORT added camera motion compensation. MG-MOT inherits this lineage but changes the association substrate: instead of relying primarily on image-plane IoU or appearance embeddings, it projects detections into a world coordinate frame using UAV metadata.

The camera-motion lineage is also relevant. The paper notes that BoT-SORT uses global motion compensation to estimate background motion, while StrongSORT incorporates enhanced correlation coefficient maximization for camera motion compensation [PAGE 3]. MG-MOT’s initial tracker is explicitly “BoT-SORT [2] with sparse optical flow as the Generalized Motion Compensation (GMC) method” [PAGE 5]. Thus MG-MOT is not a replacement for motion compensation at the image layer; it is a higher-level correction that uses metadata to bypass some of the instability caused by drone motion.

A second lineage is metadata-aided tracking. The paper cites prior work using vehicle metadata for multi-camera tracking, camera calibration via corresponding points and PnP, and UAV memory maps of object locations in world coordinates [PAGE 3]. The key conceptual shift is that metadata can become a spatial prior. The paper quotes the relevant claim from prior work: metadata from UAVs can “create a memory map of object locations in actual world coordinates” [PAGE 3]. MG-MOT operationalizes that memory map not as a static map but as a dynamic association field.

The maritime benchmark lineage is SeaDronesSee and the MaCVi challenge. The paper says the dataset is designed to assess “detection and tracking of humans, boats, and other objects in open water” and introduces “the novel aspect of long-term tracking” [PAGE 1]. This matters because the benchmark deliberately includes disappearances and reappearances. The paper also notes a dataset limitation: “the training and validation sets do not contain long-term tracking labels, i.e., objects that have gone missing are assigned new IDs when they reappear” [PAGE 4]. That means the model cannot directly learn long-term identity continuity from supervised labels in those splits; it must rely on geometric and motion priors.

The multi-week traps in this lineage are subtle. First, a detector tuned for one altitude distribution can fail at another. Table 2 shows altitude ranges from 4.60 to 149.49 in testing metadata [PAGE 4]. A detector trained mostly on higher-altitude views may underperform when the drone flies low, exactly the condition where swimmers become small, overlapped, and visually merged with sea clutter. The paper admits this: “Our detector performs poorly when detecting swimmers while the drone flew low to the sea surface with a considerable heading angle” [PAGE 5].

Second, a tracking threshold tuned on validation clips can become unsafe across weeks or sequences if sea state, boat speed, or gimbal behavior changes. The paper’s threshold ablation shows that IoU threshold 0.5 is balanced, while a higher matching threshold improves performance because swimmers can group and follow similar trajectories [PAGE 7]. However, that tuning is dataset-specific. If the operational environment changes, the same threshold can become either over-conservative or over-permissive.

Third, metadata synchronization is a long-horizon trap. The paper shows that “the metadata is not synchronized with the actual drone gimbal” [PAGE 6]. A method that assumes synchronization can produce confident but wrong world coordinates. This is more dangerous than a simple missing detection because it creates a false spatial hypothesis.

Fourth, appearance-based ReID is a trap in maritime scenes because boats and swimmers can share visual characteristics. The paper says long-term ReID is challenging because “traditional appearance features, often effective in pedestrian tracking scenarios, may not perform well” [PAGE 2]. MG-MOT reduces reliance on appearance, but it does not eliminate the need for appearance when metadata fail or when objects reappear after long intervals.

The retro-causal genealogy therefore explains why the paper’s performance is high but conditional. MG-MOT succeeds because it inherits strong detection and motion compensation, then adds a world-frame geometry layer. It fails or degrades when the inherited layers break: detector misses, metadata errors, unsynchronized gimbal state, or motion extrapolation beyond the reliable memory horizon.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind view of MG-MOT asks what assumptions the tracker makes about the world, about the drone, and about the target, and what happens when those assumptions are violated. The baseline tracker implicitly believes that image-plane motion and appearance are sufficient to infer identity. In maritime UAV video, that belief is weak. The paper says the environment includes “the presence of small-sized objects, challenging visibility conditions due to waves and sun reflections, and the dynamic nature of objects caused by gimbal movements and altitude changes” [PAGE 1]. Those conditions invalidate the baseline belief.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the tracker falls back to the most familiar cue under stress. In ordinary MOT, that cue is often IoU or appearance embedding. In MG-MOT, the fallback is replaced by world-coordinate distance. The paper’s short-term method directly uses Hungarian assignment on world-coordinate distance [PAGE 4]. This is a counter-trap: instead of asking whether two boxes look alike, the tracker asks whether two detections correspond to nearly the same physical location on the sea plane. That is more robust when the camera moves abruptly, because the world coordinate can remain stable even when the pixel coordinate jumps.

However, the counter-trap can itself become a crisis habit if metadata are trusted too strongly. The recursive failure is this: the tracker assumes metadata are correct; metadata are sometimes incorrect; therefore the tracker confidently associates wrong tracks. The paper acknowledges this with “We find the metadata of the drone is not that reliable” [PAGE 6]. The correct recursive policy is therefore not “trust metadata always” but “use metadata as a gating prior, then verify with independent cues when the gate is near its boundary or when the time gap is long.”

The clock layer is central. MG-MOT has several clocks:

1. Detection clock: each frame t has detections with confidence. The paper uses high-confidence threshold 0.5 and low-confidence threshold 0.1 [PAGE 5].
2. Memory clock: tracks are kept in a buffer. The paper uses “a buffer of 100 frames to remove those unmatched tracks” [PAGE 5].
3. Extrapolation clock: forward or backward motion extrapolation is valid only if \(\Delta t \le \tau_{\mathrm{memory}}\) [PAGE 4].
4. Matching-expansion clock: the allowed world-distance gate grows with disappearance and reappearance times via Eq. (9) [PAGE 4].
5. Interpolation clock: the paper applies linear interpolation twice, “one before and one after the ReID step” [PAGE 5].

These clocks can conflict. A long memory clock helps recover re-entries but increases false-match risk. A large matching-expansion clock helps tracks that moved far while absent but can merge distinct objects. A short extrapolation clock prevents wild predictions but may fragment legitimate tracks. The paper’s ablation reflects this trade-off: “Results illustrate that a higher matching threshold improves performance” [PAGE 7], but this is not universal; it is tied to grouped swimmer trajectories.

The recursive habit of the baseline is to preserve continuity at all costs. The recursive counter-trap is to allow a new track when the physical gate fails. MG-MOT implements that by treating an entering track as new if the cost exceeds \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\) [PAGE 4]. This prevents identity hallucination. But it also creates fragments if the gate is too tight. The method’s post-processing tries to repair fragments through interpolation [PAGE 5].

A deeper recursive issue is class-aware reasoning. The paper says boats and swimmers require different thresholds because of different movement patterns [PAGE 4]. A recursive tracker should infer not only “where is this object now?” but also “what kind of motion is physically plausible for this object?” If the tracker ignores class, a fast boat can be confused with a swimmer or vice versa. Yet the testing evaluation ignores class labels, so the tracker’s internal class model may not be directly rewarded [PAGE 5]. This creates a mismatch between the tracker’s theory of the world and the benchmark’s theory of success.

The counter-trap versus clock adjudication is therefore:

- If the time gap is short and metadata are reliable, trust the world-coordinate gate.
- If the time gap is moderate, use BiME and MTE but require the extrapolated position to remain physically plausible.
- If the time gap is long, do not trust pure extrapolation; readopt appearance, class constraints, or search-zone expansion.
- If metadata are unreliable, reduce reliance on the world gate and fall back to motion-compensated image association or appearance confirmation.

This is exactly the recursive lesson of the paper: the best counter-trap is not a single rule but a policy that changes its belief as the clock and physical context change.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof must decide when MG-MOT should be allowed to assert identity continuity and when it must be vetoed. The layers are physical geometry, biomechanics, weather/visibility, metadata integrity, and evaluation semantics.

Layer 1: Physical geometry veto.
The core projection is

\[
X^{3D}
=
\mathrm{Loc}_{\mathrm{drone}}
+
\frac{Z_{\mathrm{drone}}}{Z_{\mathrm{ray}}}
\mathrm{ray}.
\]

This is valid only if the ray intersects the sea plane in front of the camera. The formal veto condition is

\[
Z_{\mathrm{ray}} \le \varepsilon_z
\quad
\text{or}
\quad
Z_{\mathrm{drone}} \le 0.
\]

Here \(\varepsilon_z\) is a small positive threshold. If \(Z_{\mathrm{ray}}\) is near zero, the projection amplifies angular error. The first-order error term

\[
-\frac{Z_{\mathrm{drone}}\,\mathrm{ray}}{Z_{\mathrm{ray}}^2}\,\delta Z_{\mathrm{ray}}
\]

diverges as \(Z_{\mathrm{ray}}\to 0\). Therefore, any association whose cost depends on \(X^{3D}\) is physically unreliable in that regime. The source supports the need for a sea-plane model: the method projects “the location of the object from image coordinates (x, y) to world coordinates (X′, Y′, 0)” [PAGE 3]. If the plane assumption fails due to waves or camera roll, the veto must be extended: the sea is not exactly z = 0 at all times, so the projection becomes a local approximation rather than an exact inverse.

Layer 2: Metadata integrity veto.
The projection depends on \(\mathrm{Loc}_{\mathrm{drone}}\), \(Z_{\mathrm{drone}}\), yaw, pitch, and field-of-view. A veto should trigger if metadata are unsynchronized or inconsistent. The paper provides direct evidence: “the metadata is not synchronized with the actual drone gimbal” [PAGE 6]. It also shows a pitch error example where “the two frames have nearly identical pitch, but the drone’s directions are clearly different” [PAGE 6]. A formal veto can be defined using a reprojection residual on stable references, such as a buoy, shoreline, or sea horizon. If the residual exceeds a threshold, reject the projection for association.

Let \(r_t\) be the reprojection residual in meters. A conservative veto is

\[
r_t > \tau_{\mathrm{meta}}
\implies
\text{do not trust } X_t^{3D}.
\]

If no stable reference is available, use a consistency check across consecutive frames: abrupt jumps in inferred camera ray direction not explainable by platform dynamics imply metadata error. The source’s statement “We find the metadata of the drone is not that reliable” [PAGE 6] justifies treating metadata as a noisy sensor rather than ground truth.

Layer 3: Biomechanical and class-based veto.
Boats and swimmers have different motion envelopes. The paper says, “we conduct short-term and long-term re-identification (Re-ID) in a class-specific manner” [PAGE 4], and it sets “a larger \(\tau_{\mathrm{match}}\) for boats due to their potential for higher relative velocity” [PAGE 4]. A formal class-conditioned velocity veto is

\[
\lVert V_i \rVert > v_{\max}^{(c)}
\implies
\text{reject constant-velocity extrapolation for class } c.
\]

Similarly, an acceleration or turn-rate veto can be written as

\[
\left\|
\frac{V_i(t)-V_i(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t}
\right\|
>
a_{\max}^{(c)}
\implies
\text{reject extrapolation}.
\]

This prevents a swimmer track from being extrapolated with boat-like speed or a boat track from being constrained with swimmer-like slowness. The paper also identifies a lifejacket-induced split-track failure: “a swimmer takes off his/her life jacket during the tracking process, which causes a ‘split tracks’ situation” [PAGE 6]. This is an appearance and class-continuity veto: if the visual class or appearance changes abruptly, identity association should require stronger evidence.

An information-theoretic veto can be expressed using class posterior entropy:

\[
H(C) = -\sum_c p(c) \log_2 p(c).
\]

If \(H(C)\) is high, the tracker is uncertain about class. If class-conditioned thresholds are used, high entropy should either widen the gate conservatively or defer to appearance. The unit is bits. This is not a toy equation; it is a standard uncertainty measure for class probability distributions.

Layer 4: Weather and visibility veto.
The paper states that the maritime environment includes “challenging visibility conditions due to waves and sun reflections” [PAGE 1]. These conditions increase false negatives and can create false positives from glint or wave texture. A weather/visibility veto should trigger when detection confidence is low and the detector is operating outside its reliable domain. The paper admits low-altitude swimmer failure: “Our detector performs poorly when detecting swimmers while the drone flew low to the sea surface with a considerable heading angle” [PAGE 5].

A formal low-visibility veto can use detection confidence \(s\) and overlap ratio. If

\[
s < s_{\min}
\]

and the object is in a high-overlap cluster, then the tracker should not create a long-term identity from interpolation alone. The paper’s post-processing uses interpolation to recover missing detections [PAGE 5], but interpolation can also fabricate false continuity. Therefore, interpolation should be vetoed for identity assignment if it spans a long gap, crosses a class discontinuity, or occurs under low-confidence detection.

Layer 5: Evaluation semantics veto.
The testing split evaluates without class separation: “testing only considers all tracks as the same classes during evaluation” [PAGE 5]. Therefore, a class-association error may not appear as a class error in the metric; it appears only if it causes ID switches, fragments, or FN/FP changes. This means the adjudication layer must distinguish physical correctness from benchmark reward. A physically correct class-aware veto may not improve the metric if the benchmark does not expose the error, but it should still be retained for operational safety.

Final cross-layer adjudication:
MG-MOT is valid when all of the following hold:

1. The projection ray intersects the sea plane with non-small \(Z_{\mathrm{ray}}\).
2. Metadata are synchronized and internally consistent.
3. The object class is known or reliably inferable.
4. The time gap is within the memory horizon.
5. Detection confidence is sufficient.
6. Extrapolated motion is class-plausible.

If any condition fails, the association should be vetoed or downgraded. The strongest vetoes are geometric singularity, metadata desynchronization, and excessive time gap. The weakest but still important veto is appearance ambiguity caused by lifejacket removal or similar boats.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
