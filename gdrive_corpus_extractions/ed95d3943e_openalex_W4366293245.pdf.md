<THINK_TRACE>
This source is not a game-event document; it is an exploratory molecular-biomarker study of concussion in collegiate football players. The reasoning problem is therefore to convert a clinical injury event into a measurable serum signal, then adjudicate whether that signal supports inference about acute injury, persistent neuronal injury, or artifact. The first derivation hop is pathophysiological: the paper states that mild traumatic brain injury involves “external mechanical forces on the brain” and that “the dominant pathological injury in mild TBI is to the white matter” [PAGE 1] [PAGE 2]. The second hop is temporal: concussion is diagnosed clinically on the sideline, and blood is drawn very quickly after injury. The protocol says, “Blood was drawn from the concussed player within 1 h of the injury” and the matched control was sampled “at the end of that day’s practice or game” [PAGE 2]. The third hop is molecular: circulating microRNAs are treated as stable post-transcriptional regulators that may reflect neuronal injury, inflammation, apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, or repair-related transcriptional states [PAGE 1] [PAGE 2]. The fourth hop is assay-specific: serum RNA is extracted, reverse-transcribed, and quantified by RT-qPCR, producing cycle-threshold values, Ct, where lower Ct means higher abundance and higher Ct means lower abundance [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4]. The fifth hop is statistical: the authors compare concussed versus position-matched controls at combined timepoints and at discrete timepoints using two-sided Mann-Whitney tests, Bonferroni correction, ROC analysis, and miRNA-pair analysis [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4].

The central gray-zone audit concerns identifiability. The observed miRNA difference is not a pure injury signal. It is the sum of injury-related biological change, assay noise, hemolysis, exercise exposure, circadian or training-load effects, RNA degradation, and sampling-time effects. The paper acknowledges that “physical exercise profoundly affects miRNA expression,” which justifies using position-matched non-concussed controls exposed to similar practice/game load rather than relying only on preseason baselines [PAGE 5]. However, the study also lacks preseason samples for each concussed player, and the control design is therefore a concurrent-exposure control rather than a within-person baseline control [PAGE 12]. This creates a major counterfactual issue: if a miRNA is lower in concussed players, the difference could be due to concussion, but it could also reflect subtle differences in recovery behavior, medical removal from play, altered exertion, or hidden subclinical injury in a control player. The paper itself notes that one control sample mapped with concussed players in unsupervised clustering and “may be indicative of a non-diagnosed concussion” [PAGE 5], and later says this “may indicate an undiagnosed brain injury” [PAGE 12].

A second gray zone is the multiple-timepoint structure. The acute phase includes 2 h and 18 h samples, but the clinically important inference extends to return-to-play and end-of-season. The paper reports that “miRNAs whose changes persist up to 4 months after concussion” include miR-17-5p and miR-22-3p [PAGE 1]. It also reports that players returned to play “about 3 weeks after concussion” [PAGE 2]. This creates a multi-week temporal trap: a biomarker that is useful at 2 h may vanish by 18 h, while a biomarker that is useful at the end of season may not be diagnostically useful in the acute sideline window. The paper handles this by separating timepoint-specific effects, but the small sample sizes mean that temporal persistence claims must be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

A third gray zone is normalization. The target miRNAs are normalized to miR-23a-3p, and hemolysis is assessed using the difference in Ct between miR-23a-3p and miR-451a [PAGE 3]. This is rational, but normalization assumes the reference miRNA is stable across injury, exercise, hemolysis, and sample handling. If hemolysis or degradation alters miR-23a-3p, the normalized values become biased. The paper reports that one 18 h concussed sample was discarded because of poor RNA quality [PAGE 4], which shows awareness of this failure mode, but it does not provide a complete sensitivity analysis for hemolysis-adjusted inference.

A fourth gray zone is statistical power. The study enrolled 57 players, but the primary concussion/control comparison used 10 concussed and 11 non-concussed players [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4]. The combined analysis used 27 concussed samples and 29 control samples [PAGE 4], but the samples are not fully independent because players contribute multiple timepoints. The Bonferroni threshold is severe: “Bonferroni correction was applied to determine statistically significant miRNA and miRNA pairs (p < 0.0001)” [PAGE 3]. This protects against false positives but also makes the study vulnerable to false negatives, especially for miRNAs with moderate effect sizes.

The final reasoning target is therefore not “predict the game” but “adjudicate the biomarker claim.” A valid inference must preserve the chain: witnessed head impact, clinical concussion diagnosis, timed blood draw, acceptable RNA quality, stable normalization, statistically robust differential expression, and temporal consistency. If any link breaks, the inference must be vetoed. The paper provides a plausible chain, but because the sample is small, observational, and not preseason-baselined, the safest conclusion is that the miRNA signature is exploratory evidence of association, not yet a deployable diagnostic oracle.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper uses quantitative PCR and statistical discrimination operators. These can be formalized without inventing toy ratios.

1. Cycle-threshold operator.
For sample \(s\) and miRNA \(i\), let \(Ct_{s,i}\) be the measured quantification cycle. The unit is the PCR cycle, a dimensionless logarithmic abundance proxy. The paper states that fluorescent data were converted into cycle threshold measurements [PAGE 3], and that “higher numbers in the y-axis mean less expressed miRNA” [PAGE 4]. Thus:

\[
Ct_{s,i} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}, \quad \text{unit: PCR cycles}.
\]

Lower \(Ct\) means higher abundance; higher \(Ct\) means lower abundance.

2. Hemolysis operator.
The paper says, “Degree of hemolysis was determined as the difference in Ct of miR-23a-3p (a miRNA not affected by hemolysis) and miR-451a (an indicator of hemolysis)” [PAGE 3]. A formal hemolysis index is:

\[
H_s = Ct_{s,\text{miR-23a-3p}} - Ct_{s,\text{miR-451a}},
\]

with units of PCR cycles. If hemolysis increases miR-451a abundance, the sign and magnitude of \(H_s\) become an assay-integrity covariate. The paper does not provide a universal cutoff, so any threshold must be treated as assay-specific.

3. Reference-normalized expression operator.
The target miRNAs were normalized to miR-23a-3p: “The amount of target miRNAs was normalized relative to the amount of miR-23a-3p reference gene” [PAGE 3]. Define:

\[
\Delta Ct_{s,i} = Ct_{s,i} - Ct_{s,\text{miR-23a-3p}}.
\]

A common relative expression transform is:

\[
E_{s,i} = 2^{-\Delta Ct_{s,i}},
\]

with arbitrary normalized expression units. The paper’s phrase “relative miRNA expression (calculated as 2−1Ct)” [PAGE 4] is best interpreted as an inverse-cycle transform where higher \(Ct\) yields lower expression.

4. Case-control fold-change operator.
For a comparison between concussed and control groups, define:

\[
\Delta\Delta Ct_i =
\left(Ct_{\text{conc},i} - Ct_{\text{conc,ref}}\right)
-
\left(Ct_{\text{ctrl},i} - Ct_{\text{ctrl,ref}}\right).
\]

Then:

\[
FC_i = 2^{-\Delta\Delta Ct_i}.
\]

The paper’s figure caption gives “FC: fold change (2−11Ct)” [PAGE 6], which appears to be a typographical rendering of a \(\Delta\Delta Ct\)-style transform. If \(FC_i < 1\), the miRNA is downregulated in concussed players; if \(FC_i > 1\), it is upregulated.

5. Mann-Whitney operator.
The paper states, “Mann-Whitney tests were two-sided and set at 5% level and 95% confidence intervals” [PAGE 3]. For two independent groups, the Mann-Whitney \(U\) statistic is computed from rank sums. The p-value is:

\[
p = 2 \min\left[P(U \leq u_{\text{obs}} \mid H_0), P(U \geq u_{\text{obs}} \mid H_0)\right],
\]

with dimensionless probability units. The null hypothesis is that the two groups have the same distribution of normalized miRNA expression.

6. Bonferroni operator.
The paper applied Bonferroni correction: “Bonferroni correction was applied to determine statistically significant miRNA and miRNA pairs (p < 0.0001)” [PAGE 3]. If \(m\) hypotheses are tested, the family-wise error rate target \(\alpha\) is controlled by:

\[
\alpha_{\text{Bonf}} = \frac{\alpha}{m}.
\]

The reported threshold \(p < 0.0001\) is dimensionless and highly conservative for a 50-miRNA panel and multiple pair tests.

7. ROC and AUC operators.
The paper used ROC analysis to assess sensitivity and specificity [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4]. Let \(X\) be a biomarker score. Sensitivity and specificity are:

\[
\text{Sens} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad
\text{Spec} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}.
\]

Both are dimensionless probabilities. The area under the ROC curve can be written as:

\[
AUC = P(X_{\text{conc}} > X_{\text{ctrl}}) + \frac{1}{2}P(X_{\text{conc}} = X_{\text{ctrl}}).
\]

The AUC is dimensionless and ranges from 0 to 1. An AUC of 1 indicates perfect rank separation; an AUC of 0.5 indicates no rank discrimination.

8. miRNA-pair operator.
The paper used miRNA-pair analysis following Sheinerman et al. [PAGE 3]. A formal pair score can be defined as:

\[
L_{s,(i,j)} = \Delta Ct_{s,i} - \Delta Ct_{s,j}.
\]

This has units of PCR cycles and represents the log2 relative abundance difference between two miRNAs within the same sample. Equivalently:

\[
L_{s,(i,j)} = \log_2\left(\frac{E_{s,i}}{E_{s,j}}\right).
\]

The paper reports that “miRNA pair analysis increases sensitivity and specificity of miRNA biomarkers” [PAGE 9], and identifies miR-181c-5p/miR-338-3p as the best overall pair [PAGE 4] [PAGE 9] [PAGE 11].

9. Logistic discriminant operator.
If a multivariable classifier is desired, define feature vector \(x_s\) containing normalized miRNA levels or pair scores. A logistic model is:

\[
P(Y_s = 1 \mid x_s) = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta^T x_s),
\]

where \(Y_s=1\) denotes concussion, \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), and \(\beta\) has units inverse to the features. If \(x\) is in cycles, \(\beta\) has units cycles-inverse.

10. Information operator.
The diagnostic information in a biomarker \(X\) about injury status \(Y\) can be expressed as mutual information:

\[
I(Y;X) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X),
\]

with units of bits if logarithms are base 2. This is not a toy ratio; it is an information-theoretic measure of uncertainty reduction.

Assumptions:
- The reference miRNA miR-23a-3p is stable across groups [PAGE 3].
- Hemolysis is either absent or controlled [PAGE 3].
- RNA quality is adequate; poor samples are excluded [PAGE 4].
- The concussion diagnosis is clinically valid [PAGE 2].
- Exercise exposure is balanced by position-matched controls [PAGE 5].
- PCR efficiency is approximately constant and near doubling per cycle.

Breakdown regimes:
- Non-detect or censored Ct values invalidate simple fold-change estimation.
- Low-copy-number stochasticity makes Ct noisy.
- Hemolysis changes miR-451a and possibly reference stability [PAGE 3].
- Batch effects can mimic biological group effects.
- Small sample size makes ROC confidence intervals wide.
- Multiple testing inflates false positives unless corrected [PAGE 3].
- Return-to-play timing and end-of-season timing are confounded by training load, healing, and behavior [PAGE 2] [PAGE 4].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of this paper runs from macroscopic head trauma to microscopic molecular regulation. The authors begin with the clinical fact that concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury caused by mechanical force [PAGE 1]. They then invoke white-matter axonal injury: “Axonal injury results from the rapid stretching of the axons, which then induce an uncontrolled flux of ions, increased intra-axonal Ca2+, release of glutamate and further depolarization of the neurons” [PAGE 2]. This is important because it supplies a mechanistic reason why circulating miRNAs might change after concussion: axonal stretch, ionic flux, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and inflammation are all molecular events that can alter miRNA expression [PAGE 2].

The paper also inherits a lineage from prior TBI biomarker work. It notes that changes in plasma or serum miRNAs have been associated with animal and human severe TBI and sport-related TBI [PAGE 2]. It cites prior studies on salivary miRNA, plasma miRNA, cerebrospinal fluid miRNA, and miRNA-pair analysis [PAGE 2] [PAGE 11]. The specific pair-analysis method comes from Sheinerman et al., and the authors state that they previously used this type of analysis for cerebrospinal fluid and circulating miRNAs in HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment [PAGE 3] [PAGE 11]. This genealogy matters because the current study is not starting from zero; it imports an existing analytic grammar: normalization, pair ratios, ROC discrimination, and temporal sampling.

A second genealogical thread is chronic neurodegeneration. The paper connects concussion to chronic traumatic encephalopathy and Alzheimer-like pathology: “Repeated concussions are thought to lead to long-term brain damage including chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)” [PAGE 1]. It also notes that repeated head trauma in boxers leads to a syndrome with similarities to Alzheimer’s disease [PAGE 2]. This gives the study a long-horizon motivation: not merely diagnosing acute concussion, but detecting molecular traces that might persist after clinical recovery. The persistence claim is explicit: “miRNAs associated with the acute phase” and “those whose changes persist up to 4 months after concussion” [PAGE 1].

A third genealogical thread is exercise confounding. The authors explicitly state that “physical exercise profoundly affects miRNA expression,” which is why they chose concurrent position-matched controls rather than only preseason baselines [PAGE 5]. This reflects awareness that sports biomarker studies can be confounded by exertion, musculoskeletal injury, dehydration, and training load. The paper contrasts itself with a prior study that used each player’s preseason sample as control and therefore “did not control for miRNA from cumulative musculo-skeletal injury” [PAGE 12].

Multi-week traps are central. The first trap is the acute-phase trap: a miRNA that is strongly changed at 2 h may normalize by 18 h or end of season. The paper shows that miR-181c-5p is the most downregulated miRNA at 2 h but becomes less statistically significant later [PAGE 4] [PAGE 10]. The second trap is the persistence trap: a miRNA that is only mildly changed acutely may become important later. The paper highlights miR-17-5p and miR-22-3p as persistent signals through return-to-play and end-of-season sampling [PAGE 1] [PAGE 5]. The third trap is the return-to-play trap: clinical recovery does not necessarily mean molecular recovery. The paper says the persistence of miR-17-5p and miR-22-3p “may indicate long-lasting molecular changes even after full clinical recovery” [PAGE 10]. The fourth trap is the undiagnosed-control trap: if a control player has an unrecognized concussion, the control group is contaminated. The paper acknowledges this possibility in the clustering analysis [PAGE 5] [PAGE 12].

The retrospective genealogy therefore warns against collapsing the study into a single “concussion biomarker.” The correct historical reading is that the paper is a time-resolved, serum-based, miRNA-pair-enhanced exploratory assay situated between acute sports medicine and long-term neurodegeneration research.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
This section models the beliefs and incentives of the actors in the study: players, athletic trainers, team physicians, control players, and researchers. The central recursive problem is that the observable clinical report is not identical to the hidden biological state.

Let \(H_t\) be the hidden injury state at time \(t\), \(S_t\) the reported symptom state, \(D_t\) the clinical diagnosis, and \(B_t\) the biomarker state. The naive model assumes:

\[
D_t = f(H_t), \quad B_t = g(H_t).
\]

But the recursive model recognizes:

\[
S_t = r(H_t, U_t),
\]

where \(U_t\) is the player’s utility from continuing to play. A player may underreport symptoms if removal from play has high personal cost. The paper describes a formal concussion management protocol, including sideline evaluation, Graded Symptom Checklist, SCAT-3 modification, ImPACT testing, and removal from play if concussion is diagnosed [PAGE 2]. This protocol is a counter-trap against crisis habit regression.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency under acute competitive stress to revert to the habit of playing through discomfort. In football, this habit is reinforced by position competition, team identity, scholarship concerns, and short-cycle recovery expectations. The paper’s design tries to counter this by requiring medical evaluation when a significant impact is witnessed, when a teammate reports symptoms, or when the player requests evaluation [PAGE 2]. Still, the recursive risk remains: if the player hides symptoms, the clinical diagnosis may miss the injury, and the biomarker may either reveal the hidden injury or be confounded by the player’s continued exertion.

The counter-trap has three layers. First, medical protocol: trained personnel evaluate the player and remove them if concussion is suspected [PAGE 2]. Second, biological measurement: serum miRNA profiling may detect molecular injury even when symptoms are ambiguous [PAGE 1]. Third, statistical design: position-matched controls exposed to similar exercise reduce the chance that the signal is merely due to practice load [PAGE 5].

The clock is the fourth actor. The study samples at 2 h, 18 h, return-to-play, and end-of-season [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4] [PAGE 5]. Each clock creates a different belief problem. At 2 h, the key question is whether the acute molecular response is detectable before symptoms fully evolve. At 18 h, the key question is whether the acute signal is stable or already decaying. At return-to-play, the key question is whether the player is biologically recovered, not merely clinically recovered. At end-of-season, the key question is whether the molecular trace persists despite apparent recovery.

A recursive belief matrix can be stated as follows:
- If the player believes symptoms are mild but the medical staff suspects concussion, the player may underreport, while the biomarker may detect injury.
- If the medical staff clears the player but the biomarker remains abnormal, the biomarker may indicate incomplete molecular recovery.
- If the control player is truly uninjured, the biomarker difference supports concussion association.
- If the control player has an undiagnosed concussion, the biomarker difference may be attenuated or the control may cluster with concussed players.

The paper explicitly entertains this last possibility: “one sample in the control group mapped with group 2 … may be indicative of a non-diagnosed concussion” [PAGE 5]. This is a crucial recursive insight: the control group is not epistemically innocent. It may contain hidden injury, and the clustering result may be detecting that hidden injury.

The safest theory-of-mind conclusion is that miRNA biomarkers are most useful as decision-support evidence, not as autonomous truth. They can flag possible persistent injury when clinical recovery has occurred, but they cannot by themselves resolve the player’s incentive to return, the physician’s diagnostic threshold, or the hidden injury state of a control player. The correct counter-trap is a multi-layer protocol: clinical assessment, timed sampling, hemolysis control, reference normalization, pair analysis, and longitudinal follow-up.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is valid when a lower physical layer invalidates a higher biological or statistical inference. Here the layers are: clinical event, biomechanical exposure, sample integrity, molecular assay, statistical discrimination, and temporal persistence.

Physical/biomechanical veto.
The clinical label “concussion” requires a plausible head-impact mechanism and medical evaluation. The paper states that the physician makes the diagnosis “based on a combination of history, physical exam, and these tests” [PAGE 2]. If no significant head impact is documented, or if the clinical evaluation does not support concussion, then a miRNA signature cannot be causally assigned to concussion. Formally, let \(A\) be the injury event, \(M\) the measured miRNA vector, and \(C\) the clinical diagnosis. The causal claim requires:

\[
P(A \mid M, C, \text{valid assay}) > P(A \mid M, C, \text{invalid assay}).
\]

If \(A\) is absent or not clinically supported, the posterior collapses. The paper’s protocol depends on witnessed impact, medical evaluation, and sideline testing [PAGE 2]. Therefore, absence of documented impact vetoes concussion attribution.

Sample-integrity veto.
The paper uses serum RNA and RT-qPCR. RNA quality and hemolysis are physical-layer constraints. The authors state that one sample was discarded because of poor RNA quality [PAGE 4], and they compute hemolysis from the Ct difference between miR-23a-3p and miR-451a [PAGE 3]. If hemolysis is high, miR-451a is altered, and normalization may be biased. If RNA is degraded, Ct values increase across targets, mimicking downregulation. Therefore:

\[
\text{If } H_s \text{ exceeds assay validity threshold, or RNA quality fails, then } M_s \text{ is not admissible for injury inference.}
\]

The paper does not provide a universal hemolysis cutoff, so the veto must be applied using assay-specific validation criteria.

Cold-chain and transport veto.
Blood samples were “transported at 4°C in an iced biohazard cooler” and centrifuged to separate serum [PAGE 3]. If the cold chain fails, RNA degradation or hemolysis may occur. A temperature excursion is a physical veto because it changes the molecular layer before statistical analysis begins. The paper’s protocol depends on controlled transport and storage at −80°C after serum separation [PAGE 3]. If those conditions are not met, the measured miRNA pattern is not interpretable.

Exercise-confounding veto.
The paper acknowledges that “physical exercise profoundly affects miRNA expression” [PAGE 5]. This means that a miRNA difference between concussed and control players is only interpretable if the control group has comparable exercise exposure. The study addresses this with position-matched concurrent controls [PAGE 5]. However, if a concussed player is removed from play while a control continues practicing, the groups diverge in exercise exposure. That divergence can veto the injury interpretation because the biomarker difference may reflect altered exertion rather than brain injury.

Weather veto.
The source does not report weather variables such as ambient temperature, humidity, wind, or field surface conditions. Therefore, any inference that requires weather adjustment is not verifiable from the provided text. A weather-based veto is valid in the following sense: if external weather data are not collected, they cannot be used to explain or correct miRNA variability. If weather data are later introduced, they must be treated as unmeasured confounders unless explicitly modeled. Because the paper’s design uses same-day control sampling and cold-chain transport [PAGE 2] [PAGE 3], the primary weather-related risk is indirect: extreme environmental conditions could alter exertion, dehydration, hemolysis risk, or sample transport timing. Without direct measurement, weather cannot be adjudicated as a causal factor.

Veto proof schema.
Let \(V\) be the validity predicate:

\[
V = V_{\text{impact}} \land V_{\text{diagnosis}} \land V_{\text{sample}} \land V_{\text{cold chain}} \land V_{\text{exercise control}}.
\]

If \(V = 0\), the inference from miRNA to concussion is not admissible. If \(V = 1\), the inference may proceed to statistical evaluation. This is not a toy equation; it is a gate condition. The paper satisfies many gate conditions by design: witnessed impact, medical diagnosis, timed sampling, matched controls, hemolysis assessment, and RNA quality control [PAGE 2] [PAGE 3] [PAGE 4]. But the small sample size and lack of preseason baseline mean that even when \(V = 1\), the statistical layer must remain cautious.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Here “RLVR” is treated as reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: a counterfactual reasoning claim is admissible only if it generates a verifiable prediction that can be checked against assay or clinical data. Three veto scenarios follow.

Scenario 1: Persistent miR-17-5p veto.
Counterfactual claim: “miR-17-5p remains downregulated at the end of the season because of persistent neuronal injury after concussion.”
Verifiable prediction: In a new cohort or in the existing cohort, concussed players should have lower normalized miR-17-5p expression than controls at the end-of-season timepoint. Using \(E_{s,\text{miR-17-5p}} = 2^{-\Delta Ct_{s,\text{miR-17-5p}}}\), the prediction is:

\[
E_{\text{conc, end}} < E_{\text{ctrl, end}}
\]

with a statistically significant difference after multiple-testing correction. The paper supports this prediction by reporting that miR-17-5p was downregulated “near or >3-fold at every timepoint, including the end of the season” [PAGE 10], and that miR-17-5p and miR-22-3p persisted as discriminators [PAGE 1] [PAGE 5].

Veto condition: If miR-17-5p expression is not lower at end-of-season, or if the difference disappears after adjusting for hemolysis, batch, or exercise load, the persistence claim is vetoed.

Scenario 2: Hemolysis-artifact veto.
Counterfactual claim: “The apparent downregulation of several miRNAs is caused by concussion.”
Verifiable prediction: After adjusting for the hemolysis index \(H_s = Ct_{s,\text{miR-23a-3p}} - Ct_{s,\text{miR-451a}}\), or after excluding samples with excessive hemolysis, the case-control separation should remain approximately stable. If the signature is injury-related, the AUC for top miRNAs or pairs should not collapse toward 0.5. The paper’s hemolysis operator makes this test possible [PAGE 3].

Veto condition: If hemolysis adjustment removes the discrimination, the injury interpretation is vetoed. In that case, the observed miRNA pattern is better explained by blood-cell contamination than by brain injury.

Scenario 3: Undiagnosed-control contamination veto.
Counterfactual claim: “One control sample mapped with concussed players because that control player had an undiagnosed concussion.”
Verifiable prediction: If the control sample is re-evaluated clinically or with imaging, follow-up symptom reports, or additional biomarkers, it should show evidence of injury. In the clustering analysis based on miR-17-5p, the control sample mapped into the concussed group, and the authors state this “may indicate an undiagnosed brain injury” [PAGE 12]. A verifiable prediction is that the clustered control sample should have a higher posterior probability of hidden injury than control samples in group 1.

Veto condition: If the control sample is later confirmed to be uninjured, or if the clustering disappears under resampling, the undiagnosed-injury interpretation is vetoed. The paper itself frames this cautiously: “may be indicative” and “may indicate” [PAGE 5] [PAGE 12].

These three vetoes are not speculative; each maps to a measurable quantity: persistent miR-17-5p expression, hemolysis-adjusted discrimination, and cluster membership of the control sample. The paper provides enough protocol detail to make these tests plausible, but the small sample size and exploratory nature mean that any positive prediction should be treated as provisional until replicated in a larger, preseason-baselined, hemolysis-controlled cohort.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
