<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a four-arm randomized controlled trial in 40 untrained, obese young women, with 10 participants per arm: polarized training (POL), high-intensity interval training (HIIT), threshold training (THR), and non-exercising control (CON). The design claim is not merely that exercise works; it is that the <em>distribution</em> of training intensity across ventilatory-threshold zones changes the magnitude of adaptation. The first multi-hop step is therefore to separate the intervention’s total dose from its distribution. The paper states that “The weekly intensity distribution was 75% Z1 and 25% Z3 for POL, 100% Z3 for HIIT, and 50% Z1 and 50% Z2 for THR” [PAGE 395]. That sentence is the core operator: three different probability-like allocations of training time over three physiological zones. If total weekly time \(T_g\) were identical and internal load were identical, then any between-group difference could be attributed more cleanly to distribution. But the paper also admits that “due to the inherent characteristics of the different training models, some variation in the resulting weekly training volume and intensity distribution may have occurred” [PAGE 403]. Thus the strongest causal claim—distribution alone—is partially underdetermined by volume and by the practical difficulty of matching HIIT, THR, and POL exactly.

The second step is physiological. POL combines high-volume low-intensity work in Z1 with a smaller Z3 component. The authors argue that Z1 supports “sustainable fat oxidation and mitochondrial biogenesis” while Z3 provides “potent stimuli for central cardiovascular adaptation, including increases in stroke volume and maximal cardiac output” [PAGE 401]. That is a plausible two-signal model: peripheral adaptations from volume and central adaptations from high-intensity bursts. The observed variables fit the story: VO2max, VT1, VT2, Qmax, and SVmax rise in all training groups, but POL shows larger changes [PAGE 399-PAGE 400]. Yet the paper did not perform muscle biopsies or mitochondrial assays, and it states that “direct mechanistic measurements ... were not performed” [PAGE 403]. Therefore the mechanistic chain is inferential, not directly measured.

The third step is statistical. The design uses a 4 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA and Hedges’ g. The interaction effects are the key inferential object: POL is reported as superior for body mass, fat mass, HR, VO2max, VT1, VT2, Qmax, SVmax, fasting glucose, cholesterol, LDL, and triglyceride [PAGE 399-PAGE 402]. But many standardized mean differences have 95% confidence intervals that cross zero. For example, POL versus THR for HR is reported as SMD = -0.64 with 95% CI = -1.54 to 0.26, described as “Moderate differences” [PAGE 400]. The point estimate favors POL, but the interval is compatible with no difference. This is a gray-zone audit issue: statistical significance of the omnibus interaction does not guarantee that every pairwise contrast is precise. The sample is small, n = 10 per group, and the paper’s a priori power calculation targeted N = 8 per group for detecting HIIT effects on CRF, not for all cardiometabolic pairwise contrasts [PAGE 397]. Multiple outcomes increase the risk that some “superiority” claims are less stable than the summary language suggests.

The fourth step is internal validity. The paper reports “total compliance” and “no incidents of injury” [PAGE 399]. That is unusually clean for a 10-week, 30-session trial in untrained obese women. It strengthens pragmatic feasibility but raises a gray-zone question: were participants highly selected or highly supervised? The methods say training was “under the guidance of a professional trainer” and that speed was adjusted by no more than 0.5 km/h to keep HR in zone [PAGE 398-PAGE 399]. This level of supervision may not generalize to unsupervised public-health settings. Also, intensity was monitored “primarily through HR responses, without verification through blood lactate measurements as a metabolic control” [PAGE 403]. Because HR can lag during intervals, zone misclassification is possible, especially in Z3. The authors acknowledge this limitation directly.

The fifth step is body composition. The paper reports greater body mass and fat mass reductions in POL, but exercise-only trials are vulnerable to dietary confounding. The authors themselves note that “exercise alone typically produces modest reductions in body weight compared with dietary interventions” [PAGE 401]. CON was “advised against participating in formal physical activities or making dietary changes” [PAGE 398], but dietary intake was not apparently measured. Therefore body-composition superiority is the weakest causal claim: randomization helps, but differential energy intake or hydration status could still affect BIA-derived fat mass. The CRF and blood-pressure/HR results are less dependent on energy balance, though still affected by training dose.

The sixth step is external validity. The sample is exclusively female, aged roughly 19–23, BMI roughly 30–32, untrained, and non-diabetic. The conclusion is explicitly limited to “young, untrained women with obesity” [PAGE 395]. Any extension to men, older adults, athletes, or clinical cardiometabolic populations is a separate inferential leap. The 10-week duration is also a clock limitation: the paper states that “The intervention lasted 10 weeks, which may not fully capture the long-term sustainability of the observed benefits” [PAGE 403]. Early adaptations such as plasma-volume expansion, glycogen storage, and neural coordination may inflate short-term gains; longer mitochondrial and structural adaptations may follow a different time course. Thus the correct reading is: within this sample and this 10-week supervised window, POL produced the most favorable aggregate profile; the mechanistic and long-term causal claims remain probabilistic and partially underdetermined.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines training zones relative to ventilatory thresholds. The formal zone-membership operator is
\[
Z_{1,g}(t)=\{t:\dot V O_{2,g}(t)&lt;\dot V O_{2,VT1,g}\},\quad
Z_{2,g}(t)=\{t:\dot V O_{2,VT1,g}\le \dot V O_{2,g}(t)\le \dot V O_{2,VT2,g}\},\quad
Z_{3,g}(t)=\{t:\dot V O_{2,g}(t)&gt;\dot V O_{2,VT2,g}\},
\]
with \(\dot V O_2\) in mL O2·kg\(^{-1}\)·min\(^{-1}\) or L O2·min\(^{-1}\). Assumption: VT1 and VT2 are stable and identifiable from the incremental test. Breakdown regime: if VT1 and VT2 are not separable, if HR lag misassigns intervals, or if \(\dot V O_{2,VT1}=\dot V O_{2,VT2}\) so that Z2 has zero width. The paper’s operational HR approximations are quoted verbatim as “Zone 1 (Z1, &lt; VT1 [~65-67% HRmax]), Zone 2 (Z2, between VT1 and VT2 [~81-82% HRmax]), and Zone 3 (Z3, &gt; VT2 [~92-93% HRmax])” [PAGE 398].

The weekly distribution operator is
\[
p_{g,z}=\frac{T_{g,z}}{T_g},\qquad \sum_{z=1}^{3}p_{g,z}=1,\qquad T_g=\sum_{z=1}^{3}T_{g,z},
\]
where \(T_{g,z}\) is weekly zone-specific training time in seconds or minutes and \(p_{g,z}\) is dimensionless. Assumption: the weekly block is closed and all training time is assigned to one zone. Singularity: \(T_g=0\) makes \(p_{g,z}\) undefined. The source gives the intended values: “75% Z1 and 25% Z3 for POL, 100% Z3 for HIIT, and 50% Z1 and 50% Z2 for THR” [PAGE 395]. It also states: “In the POL group, approximately 75% of the total weekly exercise time was performed in Z1 and 25% in Z3, with almost no time spent in Z2” [PAGE 398].

Internal-load monitoring can be represented as
\[
\ell_{g,s}=RPE_{g,s}\,t_{g,s},\qquad \bar\ell_g=\frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{s=1}^{N_s}\ell_{g,s},
\]
with \(RPE_{g,s}\) on the Borg 0–10 scale and \(t_{g,s}\) in minutes, giving arbitrary units \(AU\cdot \min\). Assumption: RPE is recorded 10 minutes after each session and is comparable across modes. Breakdown: if \(t_{g,s}=0\), if RPE is missing, or if RPE is systematically under-reported because participants anticipate speed increases. The source reports RPE values after intervention: “POL = 8.7 ± 1.1, HIIT = 8.2 ± 0.9, and THR = 7.9 ± 1.3” [PAGE 399].

Heart-rate fraction is
\[
h_{g,s}=\frac{HR_{g,s}}{HR_{max,g}},
\]
dimensionless. Assumption: \(HR_{max}\) is measured or estimated consistently. Breakdown: age-predicted \(HR_{max}\) error; HR lag during short intervals; and HR drift under heat or dehydration. The source states that training groups performed at “66% to 93% heart rate max” and that load was matched by “%HRmax and RPE” [PAGE 395, PAGE 398-PAGE 399].

The central physiological operator is the Fick equation:
\[
\dot V O_2=\dot Q\left(C_aO_2-C_vO_2\right),\qquad \dot Q=HR\times SV,
\]
where \(\dot V O_2\) is mL O2·min\(^{-1}\), \(\dot Q\) is L·min\(^{-1}\), \(C_aO_2-C_vO_2\) is mL O2·L\(^{-1}\), HR is beats·min\(^{-1}\), and SV is L·beat\(^{-1}\). Assumption: steady-state or acceptable averaging during incremental exercise; impedance cardiography valid. Breakdown: if \(C_aO_2=C_vO_2\), the equation predicts zero oxygen consumption, which is physiologically incompatible with exercise and indicates measurement error; if SV or Q is invalid, the inferred central-adaptation mechanism fails. The paper states: “cardiac hemodynamics were continuously monitored throughout the test using impedance cardiography ... allowing for the non-invasive recording of cardiac output (Q̇max) and stroke volume (SVmax)” [PAGE 398].

The statistical operator is the repeated-measures ANOVA model
\[
y_{ijt}=\mu+G_i+T_t+(G\times T)_{it}+\varepsilon_{ijt},\qquad \varepsilon_{ijt}\sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),
\]
with variable-specific units. Assumptions: independent errors, normality, homoscedasticity, and sphericity for the repeated factor. Breakdown: if sphericity is violated and not corrected; if variance is zero; or if differential dropout creates non-random missingness. The source reports Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests but does not report a sphericity correction [PAGE 399].

Effect size is Hedges’ g:
\[
g=J\,\frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{s_p},\qquad J=1-\frac{3}{4(n_1+n_2)-9},
\]
dimensionless, with \(s_p\) the pooled standard deviation. Singularity: \(s_p=0\). The paper classifies g as trivial, small, moderate, large, or very large and reports 95% confidence intervals [PAGE 399]. Probability units are dimensionless \(p\in[0,1]\); the paper uses \(p&lt;0.001\) time effects and interaction \(p\le 0.002\) in the abstract [PAGE 395]. The source reports, for example, POL VO2max 28.3 ± 1.7 to 32.7 ± 2.3 mL·kg\(^{-1}\)·min\(^{-1}\), versus HIIT 27.8 ± 2.0 to 30.7 ± 2.0 and THR 28.2 ± 1.8 to 30.9 ± 2.1 [PAGE 400].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The genealogy of this paper runs through the polarized-training literature: Stöggl and Sperlich’s 2014 comparison of polarized, threshold, high-intensity, and high-volume training; Muñoz et al.’s 2014 recreational-runner trial; Zapata-Lamana et al.’s 2018 polarized-training trial in overweight and obese young women; Rosenblat et al.’s 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis; and Chiang et al.’s 2023 randomized trial with equal training impulse. The present paper positions itself as an extension to untrained obese females and directly cites the mixed evidence: “other studies have reported no significant improvement in CRF following POL interventions” [PAGE 396]. That sentence matters because it prevents a triumphalist reading. The historical lineage is not a clean march toward POL; it is a contested series of protocols with different populations, zone definitions, volume matching, and adherence contexts.

The retrocausal trap is to read the 2026 result backward as proof that POL was always superior. In fact, earlier reviews and trials often found no advantage for POL over HIIT or threshold training. The present study’s positive result may depend on its specific 75/25 POL distribution, its 10-week duration, its supervised treadmill setting, and its obese untrained female sample. The authors themselves note that observational endurance data suggest the high-intensity component may range from “approximately 5% to 15% of total training volume,” whereas their POL group used about 25% in Z3 [PAGE 403]. Therefore the exact dose of Z3 is a moving target, not a universal constant.

Multi-week traps are central. First, early adaptations are not the same as long-term adaptations: plasma-volume expansion, glycogen storage, and skill acquisition can produce rapid VO2max and HR changes before mitochondrial or structural remodeling is complete. The paper acknowledges that the 10-week window “may not fully capture the long-term sustainability of the observed benefits” [PAGE 403]. Second, thresholds drift. VT1 and VT2 were determined from the pre-test incremental exercise test and used to prescribe zones; if fitness improved during the 10 weeks, the same absolute speed or HR may have moved into a different relative zone. Third, HR and RPE adaptation can create a feedback trap: the methods state that “if a participant's RPE or average HR (5 - 10 bpm) decreased over two consecutive weeks of training ... the initial speed was raised by 0.5 km/h” [PAGE 399]. That rule preserves load, but it can also convert intended Z1 work into Z2 work if the adaptation is transient or fatigue-related. Fourth, adherence is a clock-dependent outcome: the paper argues that “alternating easy and hard training days could enhance enjoyment, reduce perceived exertion, and improve long-term adherence” [PAGE 403]. If true, POL’s advantage may be behavioral as much as physiological, and a shorter or monotony-heavy protocol could reverse the ranking.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how participants, trainers, and investigators model each other over the 10-week clock. In untrained obese women, a crisis habit regression is plausible: when a session becomes aversive, the participant may revert to sedentary behavior, skip sessions, or reduce effort. The counter-trap is overcorrection by the trainer: if HR falls, the trainer may raise speed to keep the participant in the prescribed zone, but the fall could reflect genuine training adaptation, fatigue, or measurement lag. The paper’s monitoring rule is explicit: speed was adjusted by “≤0.5 km/h” to keep HR in the target zone, and speed was raised by “0.5 km/h” after two consecutive weeks of lower RPE or average HR [PAGE 399]. This is a feedback controller with a built-in risk of overshoot.

The clock matters because the 30-session schedule has three phases: familiarization, adaptation, and endpoint testing. In the first phase, participants learn the laboratory and trainer expectations. In the middle phase, they may anticipate that lower RPE will trigger harder sessions, creating an incentive to report effort strategically. In the final phase, the approaching post-test may alter effort or adherence. The paper reports 100% compliance and no injuries [PAGE 399], which suggests the supervisory model was effective, but it also means the behavioral counter-trap was not observed in this sample. The practical lesson is that POL may reduce perceived exertion by alternating easy and hard days, but the same alternation requires a trainer who can distinguish adaptation from fatigue. If the trainer misreads the signal, the counter-trap converts a low-stress polarized plan into a threshold-heavy plan.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: body-mass and fat-mass changes require an energy-balance mediator,
\[
\Delta m=\frac{\int (E_{in}-E_{out})\,dt}{\rho_E},
\]
where \(E\) is in joules, \(t\) in seconds, and \(\rho_E\) is the energy density in J·kg\(^{-1}\). Assumption: energy intake and expenditure are measured or randomized. Breakdown: if dietary intake changed differentially, the body-composition superiority of POL is confounded. The paper notes that “exercise alone typically produces modest reductions in body weight compared with dietary interventions” [PAGE 401], and CON was only “advised against ... making dietary changes” [PAGE 398]. Therefore the body-composition claim survives as a supervised intervention effect, but not as a clean isolated effect of intensity distribution.

Biomechanical veto: the intervention used treadmill running with speed adjustments of no more than 0.5 km/h and reported no training-related injuries [PAGE 399]. The biomechanical proof is therefore minimal but positive: the protocol was tolerable in this sample. The veto condition is any injury, gait breakdown, or orthopedic limitation that prevents zone-targeted running. If present, the observed cardiometabolic differences could reflect injury-related volume loss rather than training distribution.

Weather/environmental veto: the laboratory was controlled at “ambient temperatures maintained between 26 and 28° C and humidity levels ranging from 45 to 55%” [PAGE 397]. This matters because HR and RPE are thermoregulatory-sensitive. If the same protocol were performed in heat, humidity, or cold outside that range, HR would drift upward or downward and zone assignment would misclassify. The weather veto is thus satisfied for the indoor lab but not for outdoor translation. The paper’s internal validity is strengthened by environmental control; its external validity is narrowed by the same control.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
