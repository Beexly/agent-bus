The core of Tolman’s molar analysis, as detailed in [PAGE 29], is the assertion that behavior is not an elemental reaction but a "molar phenomenon" defined by three descriptive features: (1) directionality toward a goal-object; (2) the necessity of engaging with "means-objects" in a specific "pattern of commerce"; and (3) a selectivity for shorter, easier means-routes to achieve that goal. This third feature—the preference for minimal effort—is presented as a fundamental law of purposive behavior, but its formalization requires moving beyond descriptive psychology into the domain of information theory and optimal control. The "gray-zone" arises when we consider the computational cost of identifying and executing this minimal route versus the physical energy saved. Is the "shorter route" always selected if the cognitive map required to navigate it is more complex?

To bridge this gap, we employ a multi-hop derivation starting from Shannon's rate-distortion theory. If the environment is a noisy channel, the animal's internal representation (the cognitive map) is a compressed signal $R$ of the environmental state $S$. The distortion $D$ is the error in predicting or navigating the environment. Rate-distortion theory provides the fundamental limit on how much one can compress a source while keeping the distortion below a certain threshold. We posit that the "selectively greater readiness for short means activities" is not merely about spatial distance but about minimizing the total expected cost, which includes both the physical effort and the informational cost of maintaining the necessary cognitive map resolution. This transforms Tolman’s observation into a constrained optimization problem where the objective function balances spatial/temporal/gravitational distance against the mutual information $I(S;R)$ required to execute the path. The singularity occurs at the point where marginal reduction in physical effort equals the marginal increase in cognitive load, leading to a phase transition in route selection. This framework allows us to mathematically dissect Tolman's examples: the rat choosing an alley is optimizing a joint cost function of distance and certainty; the man driving home is minimizing fuel and time subject to his mental model of traffic patterns; the psychologist reading nonsense syllables is minimizing the experimental error in measuring learning curves, all while adhering to the constraints of their respective cognitive maps. The critique lies in whether this unified variational principle fully captures the qualitative "commerce" Tolman describes or reduces it to a mechanistic calculus of forces.

We formalize the "third descriptive feature" using a Lagrangian approach incorporating both classical mechanics and information-theoretic costs. Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of all possible paths from an initial state to a goal-object in a configuration space $\mathcal{X}$. A path $\gamma \in \mathcal{P}$ is a sequence of interactions with means-objects.

**Operator Definition:** The *Molar Effort Operator* $\hat{\mathcal{E}}$ acts on a path $\gamma$ to yield the scalar effort cost:
\[
\hat{\mathcal{E}}[\gamma] = \int_{t_0}^{t_f} \left( \alpha L(\dot{x}, t) + \beta I(S_t; R_t) \right) dt + C_{\text{cog}}[R]
\]
Where:
- $L(\dot{x}, t)$ is the instantaneous Lagrangian (kinetic minus potential energy), measured in Joules (J).
- $I(S_t; R_t)$ is the mutual information between the true environmental state $S_t$ and the agent's internal representation $R_t$, measured in nats (natural units of information). This term quantifies the real-time sensory processing and predictive coding load.
- $C_{\text{cog}}[R]$ is the static maintenance cost of the cognitive map structure $R$, also in nats, representing long-term memory storage overhead.
- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are scaling factors converting physical work and information rates into a common utility dimension (e.g., metabolic equivalents or subjective disutility).

**Fundamental Equilibrium Condition:** Following Tolman’s observation of "selectively greater readiness," the chosen path $\gamma^*$ minimizes the expected effort operator:
\[
\gamma^* = \arg\min_{\gamma \in \mathcal{P}} \langle \hat{\mathcal{E}}[\gamma] \rangle_{\pi}
\]
where the expectation is taken over the stochastic policy $\pi$ conditioned on the current cognitive map.

**Singularity/Breakdown Regime:** The equation exhibits a singularity when the derivative of the information cost with respect to path complexity approaches infinity. This occurs in environments with fractal boundaries or chaotic dynamics where arbitrarily fine-grained sensing is required to distinguish between viable and fatal trajectories. In such regimes, the "shortest" spatial route may demand infinite information capacity to navigate reliably, causing the system to collapse into a local minimum where a longer, simpler route is selected despite higher physical effort. This is the **Information-Capacity Bottleneck**: when $I(S;R)$ hits the maximum channel capacity of the nervous system, further reductions in physical distance $L$ become impossible without catastrophic failure in navigation.

As stated explicitly on [PAGE 29]: *"behavior-acts are to be characterized, also, in terms of a selectively greater readiness for short (i.e., easy) means activities as against long ones... he will within limits select the shorter."* Our formulation reveals that these "limits" correspond precisely to the breakdown regime of the variational principle above.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s introduction of "purpose" and "goals" into behaviorism was a direct revolt against the S-R mechanism of Watson and Thorndike, whose laws of effect were purely associative and lacked teleonomy. However, Tolman’s own concept of "selectiveness towards means-objects" is historically indebted to two earlier scientific traditions. First, the **Principle of Least Action** in physics (Maupertuis, Euler, Lagrange), which dictates that dynamical systems evolve along paths that minimize the action integral. Tolman effectively biologized this principle, suggesting organisms act as "least-effort machines." Second, and more subtly, it connects to the economic concept of **rational choice theory**, which assumes agents maximize utility subject to constraints.

The **multi-week trap** emerges when we view this through a retrocausal lens. Modern neuroscience often treats "effort minimization" as a dopamine-mediated reinforcement signal, implying that the brain calculates future rewards and discounts them by effort. This creates a temporal paradox: if the goal is future-oriented, how does the present moment contain enough information to compute the integral over all future paths? Tolman hinted at this with his "cognitive map," but the full genealogical debt is owed to **retrocausal models in quantum mechanics** (like the Two-State Vector Formalism), where future boundary conditions influence present states. By framing behavior as always already oriented toward a goal ("getting to or from"), Tolman implicitly posited a form of biological retrocausality where the future goal-state acts as an attractor that constrains present motor patterns. The trap is assuming this computation happens forward-in-time via prediction; instead, it might be better modeled as a constraint satisfaction problem where the future goal imposes a boundary condition that restricts the present solution space. This historical entanglement explains why modern AI planning algorithms (which look ahead) struggle with the fluid adaptability seen in Tolman’s rats—biological systems appear to solve the inverse problem, inferring the necessary present actions directly from the pull of the goal.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When an organism faces the choice between a known short route and an uncertain long route, it is not simply computing distance; it is engaging in a recursive simulation of its own future competence. This involves a **crisis habit regression vs. counter-trap dynamic**. 
In a novel environment, the agent enters a state of high uncertainty (high entropy). The default response, according to Tolman’s early observations but reinterpreted here, is not blind trial-and-error but a rapid regression to primitive, high-bandwidth sensorimotor habits (e.g., wall-following, thigmotaxis). This is "crisis habit regression"—a fallback to safe, albeit inefficient, means-activities to stabilize the cognitive map.

However, once a preliminary map is established, the agent may encounter a **counter-trap**: a situation where the locally optimal move (following the shortest vector) leads to a dead end or a previously encountered failure state. To avoid this, the agent must perform a second-order simulation: "If I take the shortcut, what will my knowledge state be in five seconds?" This recursive theory of mind allows the agent to reject the physically shortest path because it predicts a future state of confusion or entrapment. The **clock** element is critical here: the decision is bounded by metabolic urgency. As time passes without achieving the goal, the weight $\beta$ in our effort operator dynamically increases, forcing a trade-off where the risk of a complex shortcut becomes too high compared to the certainty of a long, slow grind. Thus, behavior oscillates between exploiting a simplified map (for speed) and exploring to refine the map (for safety), governed by an internal clock that measures the opportunity cost of time.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Even if the variational principle identifies a globally optimal path $\gamma^*$, execution can be vetoed by lower-level constraints. We define a **Cross-Layer Veto Function** $V: \mathcal{P} \times \Omega \rightarrow \{0,1\}$, where $\Omega$ represents physical and biomechanical state variables.

**Biomechanical Veto:** Let the agent's musculoskeletal system have a maximum sustainable force $F_{max}$ and a fatigue accumulation rate $\dot{f}$. If the optimal path requires a gradient climb exceeding the agent's power-to-weight ratio such that the required torque $\tau(t) > \tau_{lim}$, then $V=1$ (veto). For example, a rat might computationally prefer a straight line across rough terrain, but ankle proprioceptive feedback vetoes the motion due to instability risks, forcing a switch to a compliant gait along a longer ridge.

**Weather/Environmental Veto:** External fields can override internal optimization. Consider wind velocity field $\vec{w}(x,y)$ and rain intensity $r(x,y)$. The effective cost of traversing a segment is modified by a drag coefficient dependent on these vectors. If the "short" route crosses a zone of extreme turbulence or visibility < 0.1 meters, the sensory noise term in $I(S;R)$ spikes dramatically. The system detects that $d(I)/d(dist) \to \infty$ in that region. The veto triggers a rerouting algorithm that prioritizes sheltered paths, effectively decoupling geometric distance from behavioral preference.

**Proof of Necessity:** Without these vetoes, the model would predict animals leaping off cliffs to save horizontal distance or driving cars through rising floodwaters to beat traffic. These vetoes prove that Tolman’s "selective readiness" is not a monolithic law but a hierarchy of constraints, where lower-level physiological and environmental realities can suspend higher-level purposive calculations.
