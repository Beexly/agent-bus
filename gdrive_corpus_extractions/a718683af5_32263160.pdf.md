<THINK_TRACE>

The paper's causal skeleton is a two-outcome test of whether pre-snap information can be converted into decision-relevant prediction. Reading it as a pure estimation problem rather than a football problem, the whole document collapses onto two questions: (a) does the conditional distribution of yards given pre-snap covariates differ materially from the marginal distribution; and (b) is the observed test-set accuracy separation on play-calls resolvable at the sample size used.

Gray-zone audit of the yardage result. Page 3 defines the training objective as Equation (1), $J=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\hat y^{(i)}-y^{(i)}|$. Under a squared-integrable conditional distribution $Y\mid X=x$ with conditional median $m(x)$, the population minimizer of expected absolute error is exactly $m(x)$, and the unconditional constant predictor achieves $\mathbb{E}|Y-m_Y|$. So the benchmark on [PAGE 4], "Benchmark: Guess Median of Training y", with mean absolute yards 6.18 and median absolute yards 3.00, is not a straw man — it is the Bayes-optimal predictor inside the class of constants. Every learned model on [PAGE 4] must beat 6.18 by exploiting $X$ alone. Random Forest lands at 6.16; LASSO, Shallow CNN, and VGG19 land at 6.18, 6.18, and 6.21. The VGG19 result is *worse* than a zero-parameter constant. That is the single most informative number in Table 1 and it is stated on [PAGE 5]: "the shallow CNN model is able to reproduce the benchmark metrics, but at a substantially larger cost than just guessing a constant for all plays, which has essentially 0 computational cost."

The decisive audit question is whether 0.02 yards is a signal. With $m_{\text{test}}=\lfloor 1049 \times 0.125 \rfloor = 131$ plays ([PAGE 2]: "I collected images from 1,055 plays and end up having 1,049 valid plays to train, validate and test my models on. I use a 75/12.5/12.5 split"), the standard error of a mean absolute error is $\sigma_{|e|}/\sqrt{m}$. NFL pre-snap yards have a heavy right tail; if $\sigma_{|e|}\approx 5.5$ yards, then $\text{SE}\approx 0.48$ yards, and the RF-versus-benchmark difference of 0.02 yards is roughly $0.04\sigma$. This is not evidence of absence of signal; it is absence of resolution. The design can only resolve differences on the order of one yard. That distinction matters because the paper's conclusion on [PAGE 1] is strong: "there is very little signal in the features I collect for the predicting yardage." A more defensible statement is: the collected features plus a 0–3-second pre-snap snapshot do not resolve a conditional median away from the unconditional median at a resolution of $\sim$1 yard.

The play-call result has the mirror-image problem. Table 2 on [PAGE 5] reports 0.546 benchmark, 0.568 LASSO, 0.614 Random Forest, 0.606 Shallow CNN, 0.606 VGG19. The text claims "a substantial increase of 6 percentage points in accuracy over benchmark methods" and that "the data used provides valuable signal." At $m=131$ with $p\approx 0.6$, the single-model binomial standard error is $\sqrt{0.6\times0.4/131}=0.0428$. Because all models are evaluated on the *same* 131 test plays, the correct comparison is paired (McNemar), not independent, which tightens the interval somewhat, but the discordant-cell count is small — roughly 15–20 plays out of 131 — so the test has very low power. A 6.8-point gap between 0.546 and 0.614 is approximately $1.5\sigma$ on an independent-pooled approximation. It is suggestive, not established. Yet it is directionally consistent with a large and well-known literature: situational down-and-distance dominates play-call selection. The paper never exploits the one check available to it, which is to re-estimate the majority-class rate on a fresh sample.

The image-data question is where the paper is most careful and most interesting. Equation (2) on [PAGE 3], "marginal value of image $\approx$ accuracy deep learning with image $-$ accuracy machine learning w/o image," is a heuristic estimator of the conditional information gain $I(Y; X_{\text{img}} \mid X_{\text{sit}})$. The heuristic's defect is that it subtracts accuracies produced by models trained under different loss functions (MAE for deep nets, MSE for the LASSO and Random Forest, stated on [PAGE 3]: "They use a loss of MSE to be trained") and different capacities. A cleaner operationalization is to hold the architecture fixed and toggle only the image branch. The paper does not do that; it compares RF-on-situational to CNN-on-situational-plus-image. The reported equality (0.606 vs 0.614) therefore bounds the information gain only up to model-misspecification slack.

Two secondary findings deserve flagging. First, hyperparameter selection: "I examined about a dozen different models" for the CNN and "about 20" for VGG19, selecting on a validation set of about 131 plays ([PAGE 4]). Under pure noise with $K=20$ candidates and validation SE $\approx 0.043$, the expected upward selection bias in reported validation performance is on the order of $0.043 \times \mathbb{E}[\max_{k\le 20} Z_k] \approx 0.043 \times 1.87 \approx 0.08$ — larger than the entire effect being claimed. The test set insulates the final number from this, but the *chosen architecture* is noise-fitted. Second, target censoring: the author states "I intentionally sampled plays with a yardage outcome instead of a penalty or other outcome" [PAGE 2]. Dropping penalty plays (roughly 5–8 percent of NFL snaps, and systematically negative or zero-yard) shifts the empirical median upward and shrinks the right tail that MSE-based models would chase. Both effects mechanically compress the benchmark's advantage and therefore shrink the apparent room for improvement.

Net adjudication: the yardage null is a resolution failure, not a measured null; the play-call positive is real in sign but under-powered in magnitude; and the "image adds nothing" claim is the best-supported of the three, though for a reason the paper only half-states — a single pre-snap RGB frame is a near-deterministic function of the situational features already in the Kaggle table (down, distance, yard line, score, clock), so it carries little *conditional* information by construction, not by failure of the CNN.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

The lineage here runs from LeCun et al. [6] (LeNet-5, 1998) through Simonyan & Zisserman [8] (VGG19, arXiv:1409.1556, April 2015) to this CS230 Winter 2020 project. The architecture on [PAGE 3] is described as "inspired by LeNet-5 [6]" — two conv layers with pooling, concatenation with tabular features, one dense layer, linear or sigmoid head. That design decision is a 2020 student correctly applying 1998 wisdom to a 1998-sized dataset.

The more consequential genealogy is the statistical one, and it runs backwards through the football-analytics literature. The paper's own related-work paragraph on [PAGE 2] names the neighborhood: play classification from image data [3, 1, 10, 5], within-play valuation with tracking data [11], continuous-time valuation (Yurko et al., JQAS 2020), and optimal passing strategy from positional data [4]. What the author does not cite, and should, is the older tradition that already established the ceiling: the demonstration that down, distance, field position, score, and clock explain a large and *bounded* share of play-call variance, and that the residual is dominated by personnel, tendency, and in-play reaction that pre-snap data cannot see.

The "multi-week trap" the paper walks into is a specific and recurring one in sports prediction research: the pre-snap framing is *guaranteed* to produce an uninformative image branch, because at 0–3 seconds before the snap, the formation is already determined by the situational features. If you know it is 3rd-and-12 from your own 20 with 2:00 left and you're down 10, the formation tells you almost nothing new. The features are collinear by construction. Any paper that predicts play outcome from a pre-snap snapshot *and* from the situation will find that the snapshot adds nothing, and will then write it up as a discovery about football rather than as a tautology about information. Taylor half-escapes this by noting on [PAGE 5] that "there are two important caveats. First the dataset was small. Second, my image data was severely limited since it was a single snapshot a few seconds before the play." That is the correct diagnosis, arrived at from the empirical side.

The historical trap this study *avoids* is worth naming. Predicting total game outcomes [9, 2] is a low-information task — a single NFL game is a near-symmetric coin with a small edge. Predicting a single play's yards is a high-variance task with a very tight conditional distribution. The author correctly identifies on [PAGE 1] that game outcome prediction is "not directly useful in improving strategy, the main motivation for this project." That is the right instinct: the leverage is in the play-level, not the game-level. But the leverage is also where the noise floor is highest, and the paper's own numbers demonstrate the noise floor winning.

**Crisis-habit regression.** The play-call result on [PAGE 5] is the only genuinely exploitable finding, and the reason it works is habit, not information. Offensive coordinators are predictable conditional on down and distance because they are optimizing against a known tendency landscape, and they are *most* predictable in crisis configurations. Third-and-long with a lead in the fourth quarter collapses toward pass with near-deterministic regularity. The Random Forest achieving 0.614 on situational data alone ([PAGE 5]) is measuring the strength of that regularity. A defensive coordinator with a laminated sheet of opponent tendencies on 3rd-and-7-plus already has most of that 0.614 in hand, which is the reason the paper's practical value is lower than its headline.

**The counter-trap the author falls into.** Equation (2) on [PAGE 3] invites the reader to interpret a small $\Delta_{\text{img}}$ as evidence that "seeing the players on the field may not provide much useful information for the tasks at hand" [PAGE 6]. This is an inversion. The correct reading is that the *task* has been made information-free by the pre-snap constraint, not that vision is uninformative about football. Play-call prediction from a formation is nearly a lookup; play-call prediction from *tendency and game state* is what the Random Forest actually does. The author's own future-work paragraph recognizes this by proposing bounding boxes on players and a two-stage position-then-outcome pipeline [PAGE 6] — which is exactly the right escape from the trap, and which would convert the problem from image classification into tracking, where the literature cited on [PAGE 2] ([1, 7, 3]) already has momentum.

**The clock dimension.** The feature list on [PAGE 6]–[PAGE 7] includes "Time passed in the game (categorical on the minute - one-hot encode)" and "Quarter of the game" but *no* continuous game-clock-in-seconds, no play-clock, no timeout state, and no score-differential-with-time interaction. This is the most consequential missing variable in the entire paper for play-call prediction. Two-minute drill, four-minute offense, and hurry-up no-huddle are qualitatively different play-call regimes with radically different pass rates, and a minute-resolution one-hot cannot express "we are inside 40 seconds with no timeouts." The one-hot encoding also imposes a hard discretization: a play with 0:45 remaining and one with 1:15 remaining fall in the same bucket despite being different strategic worlds.

**The 6-point claim under selection pressure.** "the deep learning methods provide a substantial increase of 6 percentage points in accuracy over benchmark methods" [PAGE 5]. The selection procedure on [PAGE 4] — roughly a dozen CNN configs and about 20 VGG19 configs, all tuned on a validation set of about 131 plays — means the *reported validation numbers that drove model choice* carry an expected upward bias of roughly $0.08$ under pure-noise conditions. The test set insulates the final reported figure, but the architecture selected is the one that happened to look best on 131 noisy plays. In a recursive framing: the model-selection process is itself fitting the validation noise, and the test set is too small to detect the resulting overfitting.

Three verifiable scenarios, each with a checkable prediction, derived from the paper's own tables.

**Veto 1 — Image ablation with architecture held fixed.**
Setup: train Random Forest on situational features only, then the same Random Forest on situational plus a 256-dimensional embedding from the frozen VGG19 pool-5 layer, with identical hyperparameters, identical folds, and 5× repeated 75/12.5/12.5 splits.
Prediction: accuracy delta in $[-0.02, +0.02]$, not significantly different from zero. This is a stronger test than Equation (2) as written, because it removes the architecture confound. If the delta is positive and outside that band, the "image adds nothing" conclusion is falsified. Expected outcome per the paper: no movement. Rationale: conditional MI of a pre-snap frame given down/distance/yard-line/score/clock is near zero by construction.

**Veto 2 — Power-replication on play-call accuracy.**
Setup: pool the 1,049 collected plays into a single test set (or re-collect on 3× more games), re-fit the Random Forest with the exact [PAGE 5] hyperparameters (2 trees, max depth 7) and the majority-class benchmark.
Prediction: benchmark reproduces near $0.546 \pm 0.05$; Random Forest lands in $[0.57, 0.65]$. If the Random Forest falls below $0.57$ on the larger sample, the 6-point gain is a small-sample artifact. If it holds above $0.60$, the effect is real and the paper under-sold its own result by using a test set of 131. The author's own caveat on [PAGE 5] that "the dataset was small" applies with equal force to the positive finding as to the negative one — a point the paper does not make.

**Veto 3 — Penalty-inclusive outcome distribution.**
Setup: re-collect without the stated sampling filter — the author writes "I intentionally sampled plays with a yardage outcome instead of a penalty or other outcome" [PAGE 2] — so that penalty and no-play outcomes enter the target.
Prediction: the empirical median of $Y$ shifts downward (penalties contribute negative or zero yards), the unconditional MAE benchmark degrades, and the *gap* between the constant predictor and any learned model widens, because the penalty outcome is partially predictable from situation (e.g., defensive holding on 3rd-and-long). If the gap stays at $\le 0.05$ yards under penalty-inclusive sampling, the "no signal for yardage" conclusion is robust. If it widens materially, the null is an artifact of censoring.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

**Physical / biomechanical veto layer.** The paper's own numbers supply the proof. If the image branch had access to pre-snap player geometry, it would at best recover alignment — which is already encoded in the situational features (down, distance, yard line). What it cannot recover is post-snap biomechanics: blocking-module interaction, pad level, double-team sliding, edge-setting, and the QB's pocket mechanics. These are the dominant determinants of yards on a run play and they are unobservable at $t=-3\text{s}$. Formally, the image feature $X_{\text{img}}(t=-3\text{s})$ is measurable with respect to the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the pre-snap formation; yards $Y$ are a function of the post-snap trajectory. The conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_{\text{img}}, X_{\text{sit}}]$ therefore reduces to $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X_{\text{sit}}]$ to within the formation's own marginal informativeness. This is the biomechanical veto and it is not defeasible by more data — only by a different *time* sample.

**Weather / surface veto layer.** The feature list on [PAGE 6]–[PAGE 7] contains no weather, wind, temperature, or turf-surface variable. Wind above $\sim$15 mph measurably reduces deep-pass attempt rate and shifts play-call distribution; precipitation and turf type shift run/pass mix. None of this is in the model. The result is that the play-call model's 0.614 is a mixture over weather regimes, and its apparent skill is partly a proxy for game script. A regime-stratified re-fit (dome vs. outdoor, wind < 10 vs. > 15 mph) would likely show the accuracy concentrated in the dome/low-wind stratum — a checkable prediction.

**Cross-layer adjudication.** The two layers above jointly establish that the binding constraint is the *sampling time*, not the model class. The paper's conclusion that "the image data I collected was not useful over the other pre-play features" [PAGE 6] is correct as a statement about a single pre-snap frame and incorrect as a statement about pre-snap information generally, because the situational features already exhaust the pre-snap information set at minute resolution. The veto is therefore: no increase in CNN capacity, no amount of transfer learning, and no larger pre-snap image corpus will break the yardage ceiling, because the ceiling is set by post-snap physics that is not a function of pre-snap inputs.

Each veto above is operationalized: Veto 1 isolates the image branch with the architecture held fixed and predicts a null; Veto 2 scales the test set and predicts the 6-point play-call gain survives as a smaller, better-estimated effect; Veto 3 removes the penalty-censoring filter and predicts the yardage null either survives or dissolves depending on whether the penalty outcome carries situation-conditional signal. The three are mutually exclusive in what they would establish: Veto 1 tests the paper's headline negative, Veto 2 tests its headline positive, Veto 3 tests its target-definition validity.

The final adjudication of the source document: this is a well-scoped, honestly-reported student project whose *negative* yardage result is stated too strongly ("very little signal" [PAGE 1] when the design can only resolve $\sim$1 yard) and whose *positive* play-call result is stated too weakly relative to its own evidence, since a 0.546-to-0.614 move on 131 plays with a Random Forest on situational data alone is a real and practically useful finding that the author undersells by framing it as a deep-learning success. The correct summary is: the situational features carry the play-call signal; the image carries almost none of it at pre-snap; and the yardage question is unresolved by this design, not answered by it. The paper's own future-work paragraph [PAGE 6] — bounding boxes, two-stage position-then-outcome, more image data, YOLO-style detection — is the correct next step and would convert an inconclusive image study into a tractable tracking study.
