<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a compact negative-result paper. The first multi-hop derivation is: the paper asks whether pre-snap situational data plus a single pre-snap image can predict two NFL outcomes, yardage and pass-versus-run play call. The abstract states the headline: “The models show that while the features have no signal for yardage outcomes, offensive play calls can be predicted with much higher accuracy than benchmark models. However, the marginal value of the image data and sophisticated deep learning models is very low for both tasks.” [PAGE 1 of 7]. This already separates two estimands: yardage is a continuous-or-count outcome measured in yards, while play call is a binary classification outcome measured by accuracy. A second derivation concerns data scale: the paper manually collected “1,055 plays” and retained “1,049 valid plays,” using a “75/12.5/12.5 split” [PAGE 2 of 7]. That implies a test set near 131 plays, so a one-play change in accuracy is about 0.008. The reported play-call gap between Random Forest at 0.614 and Shallow CNN at 0.606 is therefore approximately one test play, not a robust architectural advantage. The 6 percentage-point gain over the 0.546 benchmark is larger, about eight test plays, but still requires confidence intervals and grouped validation.

A third derivation is methodological: the paper’s “marginal value of image” heuristic is written as “marginal value of image ≈accuracy deep learning with image−accuracy machine learning w/o image” [PAGE 3 of 7]. This is not a clean causal ablation because it contrasts a deep model with image against a different machine-learning model without image. It confounds modality, architecture, regularization, hyperparameter search, and training budget. A stronger audit would hold the architecture family, optimizer, epoch budget, and split fixed while toggling only the image tensor. The paper’s own results support the weaker conclusion: “the Random Forest model on only situational data achieves a higher test accuracy than the other models” [PAGE 5 of 7], and “the image data I collected was not useful over the other pre-play features for both tasks addressed” [PAGE 5 of 7]. The gray-zone audit is that this negative result is sample-bound and instrument-bound: the image is a single screenshot 0 to 3 seconds before the snap, from nine 2016 games, and the paper explicitly caveats that “the dataset was small” and “my image data was severely limited since it was a single snapshot a few seconds before the play” [PAGE 5 of 7]. Thus the correct inference is not that player images are universally useless, but that this particular single-frame, small-sample, sideline-film representation did not beat situational features under the tested models. The yardage result is even more constrained: the benchmark median is 6.18 mean absolute yards and 3.00 median absolute yards, while the Shallow CNN reproduces 6.18 and 3.00, and the Random Forest improves mean absolute yards by only 0.02 yards [PAGE 4 of 7]. The paper itself calls the results “quite striking in how negative they are” [PAGE 4 of 7]. The audit therefore treats the yardage claim as well supported for this dataset but not as a law of football; it is a statement about signal-to-noise, representation, and sample size under a specific experimental design.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let play \(i\) have yardage label \(y_i \in \mathbb{Z}\) with unit yards, and let a model output \(\hat y_i \in \mathbb{R}\) with unit yards. The paper’s deep yardage models use mean absolute error:
\[
J_{\mathrm{MAE}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\hat y_\theta(x_i)-y_i\right|,
\qquad [J_{\mathrm{MAE}}]=\mathrm{yd}.
\]
The paper writes this as “\(J = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\hat y^{(i)}-y^{(i)}|\)” and states that all deep models except LASSO used “a cost function of mean absolute yards” [PAGE 3 of 7]. Because “yards must be integer valued,” final predictions are integer-rounded [PAGE 4 of 7]. The evaluation also uses median absolute yards:
\[
\mathrm{MedAE}=\operatorname{median}_{i}\left|\hat y_i-y_i\right|,
\qquad [\mathrm{MedAE}]=\mathrm{yd}.
\]
Breakdown regimes: \(J_{\mathrm{MAE}}\) is non-differentiable when \(\hat y_i=y_i\), where the subgradient is set-valued; it is linear in large residuals, so rare long plays do not dominate as they would under squared error. The median metric is non-smooth and can be insensitive to tail errors, so a model can match the median benchmark while failing on long-yardage plays.

For play call, let \(c_i \in \{0,1\}\) encode run or pass, and let \(\hat p_\theta(x_i)\in(0,1)\) be the predicted pass probability. The binary cross-entropy is
\[
L_{\mathrm{BCE}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[c_i\log \hat p_\theta(x_i)+(1-c_i)\log\left(1-\hat p_\theta(x_i)\right)\right],
\qquad [L_{\mathrm{BCE}}]=\mathrm{nat/play}.
\]
Accuracy is
\[
A(\theta)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat c_i=c_i\right],
\qquad [A]=1,
\]
where \(\hat c_i=\mathbf{1}[\hat p_\theta(x_i)\geq 0.5]\). The paper states that play call is “either a pass or a run,” uses “binary cross-entropy as my loss,” and uses “accuracy” because classes are “close to balanced” [PAGE 4 of 7]. Breakdown regimes: BCE has logarithmic singularities as \(\hat p_\theta(x_i)\to 0\) when \(c_i=1\), or as \(\hat p_\theta(x_i)\to 1\) when \(c_i=0\); implementations clip probabilities by a small \(\epsilon\). Accuracy is a step function, non-differentiable almost everywhere, and high variance on small test sets. With 1,049 valid plays and a 12.5 percent test fraction, the test set is about 131 plays [PAGE 2 of 7], so one play changes accuracy by about 0.008.

The paper’s marginal-value operator is
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{image}}\approx A_{\mathrm{DL,image}}-A_{\mathrm{ML,no\ image}},
\qquad [\Delta_{\mathrm{image}}]=1,
\]
quoted as “marginal value of image ≈accuracy deep learning with image−accuracy machine learning w/o image” [PAGE 3 of 7]. A cleaner causal estimand would be
\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{image}}^{\mathrm{causal}}
=\mathbb{E}_{(S,I,y)\sim P_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[\ell\left(f_{\theta^*}(S,I),y\right)\right]
-\mathbb{E}_{(S,I,y)\sim P_{\mathrm{test}}}\left[\ell\left(f_{\theta'}(S),y\right)\right],
\]
with the same architecture family, optimizer, epoch budget, and validation protocol, and with \(\ell\) in yards for MAE or nats for BCE. Breakdown regimes: the paper’s heuristic is undefined as a modality effect when the two models differ in architecture; it is metric-mismatched for yardage because the paper reports yards, not accuracy; and it is non-identifiable under temporal leakage or game-level dependence. The source’s table values are benchmark 6.18 mean absolute yards and 3.00 median absolute yards, LASSO 6.18 and 4.00, Random Forest 6.16 and 3.66, Shallow CNN 6.18 and 3.00, and VGG19 6.21 and 3.00 [PAGE 4 of 7]. For play call, the source reports benchmark 0.546, LASSO 0.568, Random Forest 0.614, Shallow CNN 0.606, and VGG19 0.606 [PAGE 5 of 7].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is explicit. It borrows the shallow convolutional architecture from LeNet-5 [PAGE 2 of 7], uses “transfer learning from VGG19 trained on ImageNet” [PAGE 3 of 7], and positions itself near the 2020 NFL Big Data Bowl and player-tracking work [PAGE 2 of 7]. The retrocausal reading is that the negative image result is not a refutation of computer vision in football; it is a consequence of the 2020-era instrument: a single pre-snap screenshot from nine games, merged with 2009 to 2018 Kaggle play-by-play rows. The paper notes that other work uses “positional data provided by the NFL on where players are located on the field, whereas my approach utilizes more end-to-end deep learning, inputting only the raw image data” [PAGE 2 of 7]. That genealogy matters because ImageNet features are optimized for object categories, not for player leverage, coverage shells, or biomechanical pre-snap cues.

The multi-week traps are fourfold. First, random 75/12.5/12.5 splitting can leak same-game or same-drive dependence if plays from one game appear in both training and test; a grouped game-level or season-level holdout would be stricter. Second, the manual collection rule “0-3 seconds before the snap” and the choice to sample “plays with a yardage outcome instead of a penalty or other outcome” [PAGE 2 of 7] creates selection bias and timing variance. Third, hyperparameter search over a small dev set, with about a dozen shallow-CNN models and about twenty VGG19 variants [PAGE 4 of 7], invites winner’s-curse optimism; the reported gaps are within one or two test plays for play call. Fourth, the marginal-value heuristic compares models of different complexity, so a multi-week research program could incorrectly conclude that “images do not matter” rather than that “this single-frame image pipeline did not matter under this sample and this ablation.” The paper’s future-work list, including YOLO, more image data, and bounding boxes [PAGE 5 of 7], is the correct retrocausal repair: move from raw pixels to measured player state before declaring a modality dead.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper predicts play call from situational features including down, distance, quarter, time, score, team, and yard line [PAGE 6 of 7], but it does not model recursive opponent adaptation. In a strategic setting, the offense chooses a mixed play-call policy \(\pi_O(a\mid s)\) and the defense chooses a response \(d\). A defense that uses the model’s estimated \(\hat\pi_O(a\mid s)\) will alter its best response; the offense can then counter by shifting away from the historical habit distribution. The fixed-point object is not a single accuracy number but a best-response cycle:
\[
\pi_O^{\mathrm{BR}}
=\arg\max_{\pi_O}\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_O,\ d\sim \mathrm{BR}_D(\hat\pi_O)}\left[U_O(a,d)\right].
\]
“Crisis habit regression” is the gray-zone hypothesis that under high-leverage states, such as late-clock, red-zone, or third-down situations, play callers regress toward stable habits. That can temporarily raise model accuracy because habit is predictable. “Counter-trap” is the opposite risk: if a defense overfits to the habit model, the offense exploits the overcommitment with a counter call, so historical accuracy overstates deployable accuracy. “Clock” is the state variable that changes risk preference: with time remaining \(\tau\) and score differential \(\Delta\), the offense’s effective utility is state-dependent, not stationary. The paper’s features include time and score [PAGE 6 of 7], but it does not estimate these recursive interactions. The adjudication is therefore: the reported 0.614 Random Forest accuracy [PAGE 5 of 7] is a historical-prediction result, not proof of stable strategic advantage. A recursive audit should stratify by pressure state and test whether accuracy rises because of habit regression, then test whether that rise disappears under counter-trap conditions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto asks whether a prediction is physically, biomechanically, and meteorologically admissible before it is used for strategy. Let the observed state be \(O=(S,I)\), with situational data \(S\) and image \(I\), and let unobserved weather and biomechanical state be \(U=(W,B)\). The paper’s model estimates \(p(y\mid O)\). A deployable decision requires \(p(y\mid O,U)\). If \(U\) is not independent of \(y\) given \(O\), then
\[
p(y\mid O)=\int p(y\mid O,U)\,dP(U\mid O)
\]
is a marginalization over unmodeled conditions, not a condition-specific prediction. A veto is warranted when two admissible \(U\) values induce different optimal actions under the decision loss. The paper’s feature appendix lists drive, quarter, down, time, yard line, yards to go, teams, and scores, but no explicit weather, field-surface, or player-tracking variables [PAGE 6 of 7]. The single screenshot could contain visual weather cues, but the paper’s ablation finds no marginal image value for either task [PAGE 4 of 7] [PAGE 5 of 7], so the image cannot be credited with resolving those layers.

Physical veto proof: with line of scrimmage at \(y_{\mathrm{LOS}}\in[0,100]\) yards from the offensive goal line, a play’s yardage must satisfy
\[
-y_{\mathrm{LOS}}\leq y_i\leq 100-y_{\mathrm{LOS}},
\qquad [y_i]=\mathrm{yd}.
\]
Any model output outside this interval is geometrically impossible and is automatically vetoed. Biomechanical veto proof: required player acceleration is bounded by available traction,
\[
\|\vec a_{\mathrm{req}}\|\leq \mu_s g,
\qquad [a]=\mathrm{m/s^2},
\]
where \(\mu_s\) is dimensionless and \(g=9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). If a route or cut demands acceleration beyond \(\mu_s g\), the resulting slip risk changes the outcome distribution, and a prediction that ignores surface state is not certified. Weather veto proof: aerodynamic force on the ball is
\[
\vec F_{\mathrm{aero}}=\frac{1}{2}\rho C_D A\|\vec v_{\mathrm{rel}}\|\vec v_{\mathrm{rel}},
\qquad [F]=\mathrm{N},
\]
so wind, density, and precipitation alter trajectory and catch probability. The paper’s negative image marginal value does not prove that weather and biomechanics are irrelevant; it proves only that this single-frame image pipeline did not reduce loss on this sample. The cross-layer veto therefore blocks any overgeneralization from “image data had no marginal value here” to “physical context is unnecessary for strategy.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Same-architecture image ablation. Train the shallow CNN and VGG19 twice, once on \(S\) only and once on \(S\) plus \(I\), holding split, seeds, optimizer, epoch budget, regularization, and hyperparameter search fixed. The verifiable reward is \(+1\) for a valid no-image advantage and \(0\) otherwise. Veto condition: if the 95 percent confidence interval for \(\Delta A\) contains 0 and \(\Delta\mathrm{MAE}\geq -0.02\) yards, veto the claim that image data has positive marginal value. Prediction: \(\Delta A\) will be between 0.000 and 0.008, roughly zero to one test play, and \(\Delta\mathrm{MAE}\) will be between 0.00 and 0.03 yards, consistent with the reported Shallow CNN 0.606 versus Random Forest 0.614 and the 6.18 versus 6.16 yard means [PAGE 4 of 7] [PAGE 5 of 7].

Scenario 2: Grouped game or season holdout. Re-run the benchmark, LASSO, Random Forest, Shallow CNN, and VGG19 with leave-one-game-out or season-level splits rather than a random play-level split. Veto condition: if the Random Forest’s advantage over the benchmark is not robust across folds, or if the deep image models do not beat the no-image Random Forest, veto the stronger claim that deep learning plus images improves play-call strategy. Prediction: the 0.614 accuracy will shrink toward the 0.55 to 0.60 range under grouped validation, and the image models will remain at or below the no-image Random Forest. This follows from the small nine-game image base and the paper’s own statement that “the Random Forest model on only situational data achieves a higher test accuracy than the other models” [PAGE 5 of 7].

Scenario 3: Multi-frame video, tracking, or weather-covariate upgrade. Collect multiple pre-snap frames, player bounding boxes, or explicit weather and field-surface variables, then test whether the added representation beats the no-image Random Forest on yardage and play call. Veto condition: if the upgraded image or tracking model improves mean absolute yards by at least 0.10 yards or play-call accuracy by at least 2 percentage points over the no-image model, veto the paper’s general conclusion that image data is not useful. Prediction: single-frame raw pixels will remain near zero marginal value, while tracking or multi-frame video may add a small play-call gain but will not solve yardage prediction; the paper’s own caveat says multiple images or video “could have more signal” but the challenge is collecting enough examples [PAGE 5 of 7]. These three counterfactuals are verifiable because each has a fixed split, a defined metric, a threshold, and a directional prediction.
