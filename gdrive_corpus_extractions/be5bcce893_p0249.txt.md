A veto proof is required to validate Camp's reliance on auditory cues. He states the baseman can tell "by sight or hearing just where the runner is" [PAGE 226]. From a biomechanical and physics perspective, sound travels significantly slower than light (~343 m/s vs ~3x10^8 m/s). In a stadium environment with significant ambient noise (wind, crowd), the auditory signal of a sliding runner (cleats scraping dirt, body impact) is often masked or delayed by the time the ball arrives at the plate. Relying on hearing introduces a latency error that could result in a missed tag or a collision. Furthermore, the biomechanics of "swinging so low... almost sweep the ground" [PAGE 226] impose a severe torque load on the shoulder joint and lumbar spine, especially if the player is fully extended. While effective for ensuring contact with a sliding runner, this motion increases injury risk during practice repetitions. Thus, a cross-layer veto might suggest prioritizing visual occlusion cues over auditory ones, and limiting the depth of the swing to preserve long-term joint health, even if it slightly reduces the margin for error on the tag.
