<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. Under acute stress—“the most exciting moments” or “foreseen possible physical danger” [PAGE 22]—cognitive resources collapse and behavior reverts to the most overlearned schema. If the dominant schema is external obedience (“obey toth [both] quickly and quietly” [PAGE 22]), crisis triggers heteronomous action motivated by fear of “disgrace” to self or school [PAGE 22], bypassing deliberation. If the dominant schema is internalized self-control (“perfect control of his te:rper,” “use his head all the time” [PAGE 22]), crisis triggers autonomous regulation.

Recursive theory-of-mind layers: Layer 0 = player’s own impulse; Layer 1 = player models coach’s demand for obedience; Layer 2 = coach models player’s courage and trustworthiness; Layer 3 = both model spectator and institutional judgment (“disgrace himself or his school” [PAGE 22]). In crisis, the recursive loop can short-circuit from Layer 3 (institutional shame) directly to motor action, skipping Layer 0 deliberation. The counter-trap is deliberate practice of calm under simulated excitement so the default recursive model becomes “use his head” rather than “fear authority.”

Clock layering: circadian clock (sleep regularity [PAGE 22]), game/season clock (fall term termination), academic clock (first semester away from home [PAGE 22]), and biological recovery clock (~48–72 h for tissue and cognitive recovery). When these clocks are misaligned—early practices in darkness, late travel, academic deadlines—sleep regularity S(t) falls below S_crit, W(t) depletes, and the system regresses to the regressive Layer 1 fear schema even in players who appeared disciplined earlier in the season.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: [PAGE 22] explicitly requires running into “foreseen possible physical danger.” In the 1905 context this meant lethal and catastrophic injury risk was non-trivial. Let ι [injuries/1000 exposures] be the expected injury rate. The moral-development claim is vetoed by non-maleficence whenever E[harm] = ι · severity > E[benefit] = κ·M(T), i.e., when physical trauma outweighs courage accumulation. Because [PAGE 22] acknowledges danger but does not bound it, the unqualified claim fails this veto unless rule changes or conditioning reduce ι below ι_max.

Biomechanical layer veto: cumulative load L(t) = ∫ λ_impact(t) dt [impact·d] versus recovery rate R [d⁻¹]. Overtraining singularity occurs when L(t) > R·t_recovery, producing injury and performance collapse rather than character development.

Weather/environmental layer veto: fall photoperiod decline and cold temperatures challenge “regular rising and retiring” [PAGE 22]. If ambient conditions or scheduling prevent S(t) > S_crit (e.g., pre-dawn practices in cold darkness disrupting circadian entrainment), sleep-dependent willpower recovery fails, vetoing the self-control mechanism.

Adjudication: The physical/biomechanical veto is hard and unconditional on [PAGE 22] alone, because the page never establishes an upper bound on acceptable danger. The sleep/diet/obedience layer supports the claim only conditionally—bounded temptation, intact authority, and circadian stability. Overall verdict: the moral-development mechanism is conditionally valid (bounded physical risk, adequate sleep, internalized rather than coerced obedience) and is vetoed outright when injury risk is unbounded, when recovery is compromised by scheduling/weather, or when external authority is removed after the season ends.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios with formal predictions.

Scenario A — Sleep-deprivation veto of temper control. Independent variable: actigraphy-verified sleep < 6 h/night for 7 consecutive nights in-season. Dependent variable: temper-control incidents per game λ_temp [events/game] (benchings, penalties for unsportsmanlike conduct, coach removals). Model threshold: W(t) < W_crit = 0.3 per the dW/dt equation. Prediction: λ_temp rises nonlinearly above λ_crit = 2× the rested baseline by week 4; players with S < 0.5 show significantly more disciplinary events than players with S > 0.8. Veto condition: if λ_temp exceeds λ_crit under sleep restriction, the claim that football develops temper control is vetoed for that subpopulation. Verification: prospective cohort with actigraphy and coach logs across a 10-week season.

Scenario B — Dietary autonomy/reactance veto of off-field obedience. Independent variable: freshman away from home for the first time [PAGE 22] whose assigned diet conflicts with a cultural or religious food prohibition. Dependent variable: diet compliance D(t) and covert consumption events per week. Model threshold: D(t) < 0.4 after two weeks of enforced external regulation. Prediction: controlled obedience without autonomy support produces reactance; D(t) falls and covert violations rise, falsifying the generalization of obedience “off the gridiron” [PAGE 22]. Veto condition: if D < 0.4 and covert events exceed baseline, off-field obedience is not internalized. Verification: daily compliance logs plus biomarkers (e.g., urinary cotinine for tobacco adherence where applicable).

Scenario C — Post-season regression veto of persistent moral effects. Independent variable: days since final game Δt [d], with no coach authority and no game excitement. Dependent variable: self-control reserve W(t), sleep regularity S(t), diet compliance D(t). Model threshold: W(Δt) < 0.5 W(0). Prediction: post-season dW/dt = −λ_off W with half-life τ₁/₂ ≈ ln(2)/λ_off ≈ 14 days; habits regress toward baseline by Δt ≈ 30–45 d unless a continuation structure replaces the season. Veto condition: if W falls below 0.5 of its in-season value within ~6 weeks, the claim that “moral effects cannot be overestimated” [PAGE 22] does not persist beyond the season. Verification: longitudinal tracking from end of fall season through spring term.
