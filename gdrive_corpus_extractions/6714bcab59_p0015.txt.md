<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Edward Tolman’s 1932 manifesto emerges from a specific historiographical tension: the dominance of Watsonian behaviorism, which sought to explain psychology solely through measurable stimuli and responses, rejecting any reference to internal states. Tolman’s genealogy is dual-natured. 

Scientifically, he inherits from Gestalt psychology (Köhler, Koffka) the notion of *insight* and *configuration*, arguing that animals perceive relations between stimuli (means-end relationships) rather than isolated bits. However, he also borrows from cybernetics (Wiener) and early AI concepts, framing behavior as information processing toward a goal.

The "retrocausal" aspect lies in how his theory was received and subsequently reshaped by later developments. In the 1950s and 60s, B.F. Skinner’s operant conditioning seemed to offer a simpler, purely mechanistic alternative to Tolman’s cognitively laden "maps." Yet, Tolman’s ideas resurfaced in the cognitive revolution (Miller, Galanter, Pribram) and, more recently, in computational neuroscience and reinforcement learning. Modern "model-based RL" is essentially a mathematical implementation of Tolman’s "cognitive map."

Multi-week traps manifest in experimental designs. For instance, in maze learning, an animal might form a correct map over days. If the maze is then altered (a blocked path), the animal exhibits "perseveration"—continuing down the old route. This is a failure of the map update algorithm, a trap where past success dictates future error. Historically, a trap for behaviorists was interpreting such errors as "frustration-aggression" cycles rather than computational failures, missing the opportunity to study the plasticity of the underlying representation.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Tolman’s framework implicitly requires a recursive model of the world: the agent models the environment, models its own actions within it, and anticipates outcomes. This hierarchy is fragile.

Crisis habit regression occurs when computational load or threat level spikes. In a familiar maze, a rat uses its cognitive map ($M$) to navigate efficiently. Introduce a loud noise or predator scent (threat), and the behavior may revert to a low-level startle response or freezing—an ancestral S-R circuit bypassing the cortex. This is a hard-wired veto on deliberation, prioritizing speed over accuracy. The "purpose" of finding food is overridden by the "purpose" of avoiding death.

Conversely, a counter-trap strategy involves meta-cognition. An animal might learn that a certain cue usually precedes a shock, but occasionally it does not. It must model the probabilities of these contingencies. If it suspects a rival is observing its choices (theory of mind), it might deviate from its optimal path to mislead the observer, even at a personal cost. This requires simulating the rival’s expectations—a second-order model. The "clock" here is the decision latency: simple S-R reactions take milliseconds; map-based planning takes seconds; deceptive strategizing takes minutes and incurs high metabolic costs. In a crisis, the clock runs out for higher functions, forcing regression.
