Source-constrained multi-hop derivation. The paper starts from a counterfactual missing-data problem: at each frame only one movement is realized, while many alternative movements could have occurred under the same spatial context. The source states: “At any given moment within a play, we observe only one realized movement among the many movements that could have occurred under the same context.” [PAGE 7]. The proposed solution is generative: model frame-level movement, simulate alternative next steps, map each location to a play-value quantity, and compare the observed movement to the simulated distribution. The first representational hop is the step-and-turn decomposition: step length is Euclidean displacement between successive locations, and turn angle is the change in bearing between successive displacement vectors [PAGE 8]. The second hop is a conditional two-model decomposition: step length is modeled first with a transformed Gaussian multilevel model, then turn angle is modeled conditionally on the current step length using a von Mises response with a log-linked concentration parameter [PAGE 9, PAGE 10]. The third hop is posterior predictive simulation: draw parameters, draw a new random effect for a generic player, draw step length, draw turn angle conditional on that step length, and convert the pair into a hypothetical next location [PAGE 11, PAGE 12]. The fourth hop is valuation: a CatBoost multinomial model estimates a distribution over ending yard lines, the expected ending yard line is a probability-weighted sum, and the observed-minus-hypothetical difference is averaged over simulations [PAGE 12, PAGE 13]. The fifth hop is aggregation into player metrics such as yards success rate and explosiveness [PAGE 18, PAGE 19].

The gray-zone audit follows from the source’s own scope statements. The simulation is explicitly one-step-ahead and holds all other players fixed: “we only simulate the individual player movement while holding the observed tracking information of all other players fixed.” [PAGE 11]. The paper calls the result “hypothetical player behavior instead of a fully multi-agent replay of the actions within a play.” [PAGE 11]. Later, it says forward trajectory simulation would require “a well-calibrated tackle probability model” and modeling “the movements of other players on offense and defense,” and that “holding non-ball carrier trajectories fixed would introduce systematic bias in the simulation results.” [PAGE 21]. Therefore, any interpretation of \(\delta\) as a full-play causal counterfactual, or as a defender-response counterfactual, is outside the source’s warrant. A second gray zone is geometric: bearing is defined by \(\operatorname{atan2}\), so a zero-length step makes the bearing undefined; the turn angle is a difference of bearings in \([-\pi,\pi]\), so an unwrapped subtraction can create artificial \(2\pi\) jumps at the branch cut [PAGE 8]. A third gray zone is the step-length transform: the paper uses a “scaled arcsine transformation” after normalizing step length to between 0 and 1 [PAGE 25], but the excerpt does not print the exact scaling constants or a clipping rule, so endpoint sensitivity is unresolved. A fourth gray zone is the simulation of the defensive-team random effect \(v_k\) in the step-length model: the algorithm explicitly draws \(u_{\text{new}}\), but the simulation description does not explicitly mention drawing or marginalizing \(v_k\) [PAGE 11]. A fifth gray zone is valuation support: the ending-yard-line model is a multinomial classifier over yard lines 10 through 110 [PAGE 12], so the expected-value sum is a restricted-support expectation, not an unrestricted real-line expectation. A sixth gray zone is Monte Carlo tail resolution: results use 100 hypothetical steps per frame [PAGE 18], so the 95th-quantile indicator has finite simulation noise. A seventh gray zone is generalization: the data are 5,400 plays over 136 games from weeks 1 through 9 of 2022 [PAGE 5], and the end-of-play model is trained on all data from that sample [PAGE 31]; the paper’s prior sensitivity checks address priors, not out-of-sample seasonal stability [PAGE 29]. The source therefore supports conditional, one-step, observed-other-agent movement evaluation. It does not support full multi-agent trajectory claims, biomechanical force claims, weather attribution, or causal multi-season ability claims.

The source’s geometry is two-dimensional field tracking. Let \((x_t,y_t)\) be the observed player location at frame \(t\), with horizontal axis in the end-zone direction and vertical axis in the sideline direction [PAGE 8]. With 10 frames per second [PAGE 2], the inter-frame interval is \(0.1\,\mathrm{s}\). Step length is
\[
s_t=\sqrt{(x_{t+1}-x_t)^2+(y_{t+1}-y_t)^2},
\]
in field-coordinate distance units, interpreted as yards in the paper’s yard-based feature system [PAGE 8, PAGE 6]. Under the assumption of uniform motion between samples, the implied average speed over the frame interval is \(10s_t\ \mathrm{yd\,s^{-1}}\), but \(s_t\) itself is a per-frame displacement, not a speed. Bearing is
\[
b_t=\operatorname{atan2}(y_{t+1}-y_t,\ x_{t+1}-x_t),\qquad b_t\in[-\pi,\pi],
\]
and turn angle is
\[
\phi_t=b_t-b_{t-1},
\]
in radians [PAGE 8]. The source does not print an explicit modulo-\(2\pi\) wrapping operator, so the branch-cut correction must be assumed for circular consistency.

The step-length model is
\[
\tilde{s}_{ijt}\sim N\!\left(\mu^{(SL)}_{ijt},\sigma^2\right),
\qquad
\mu^{(SL)}_{ijt}=\alpha^{(SL)}_0+X^{(SL)}_{ijt}\beta^{(SL)}+u_j+v_k,
\]
with
\[
u_j\sim N(0,\tau_u^2),\qquad v_k\sim N(0,\tau_v^2),
\]
where \(i\) indexes play, \(j\) indexes ball carrier, \(t\) indexes frame, and \(k\) indexes defensive team [PAGE 9]. Here \(\tilde{s}_{ijt}\) is the transformed step length, \(u_j\) is a ball-carrier random intercept, and \(v_k\) is a defensive-team random intercept. The response is dimensionless on the transformed scale; \(\sigma,\tau_u,\tau_v\) are also on that transformed scale. The fixed-effect coefficients \(\beta^{(SL)}\) have units of transformed-step response per covariate unit, and the covariates mix yards, yards per second, radians, and counts [PAGE 6].

The turn-angle model is
\[
\phi_{ijt}\sim \operatorname{von Mises}\!\left(\mu^{(TA)}_{ijt},\kappa_{ijt}\right),
\]
with
\[
\tan\!\left(\frac{\mu^{(TA)}_{ijt}}{2}\right)
=\alpha^{(TA)}_0+X^{(TA)}_{ijt}\beta^{(TA)},
\qquad
\log\kappa_{ijt}=\gamma_0+\gamma_1s_{ijt}+w_j,
\]
and
\[
w_j\sim N(0,\tau_w^2),
\]
where \(\phi_{ijt}\), \(\mu^{(TA)}_{ijt}\), and \(\beta^{(TA)}\) are on angular or link scales in radians, \(\kappa_{ijt}\) is a dimensionless concentration, \(s_{ijt}\) is in yards, \(\gamma_1\) has units of inverse yards, and \(w_j\) is a ball-carrier random intercept on the log-concentration scale [PAGE 10]. The source’s ordering assumption is explicit: step length dictates how much distance is covered, and turn angle then determines directional change relative to the previous step; “longer steps make it more difficult to perform sharper turns” [PAGE 10].

Posterior predictive simulation uses
\[
\int_{\Theta_{\tilde s}}p(\tilde s^{\text{new}}\mid \theta_{\tilde s})\pi(\theta_{\tilde s}\mid D_{\tilde s})d\theta_{\tilde s},
\qquad
\int_{\Theta_{\phi}}p(\phi^{\text{new}}\mid \theta_{\phi})\pi(\theta_{\phi}\mid D_{\phi})d\theta_{\phi},
\]
for step length and turn angle respectively [PAGE 11]. For a generic player, the algorithm draws \(u^{(h)}_{\text{new}}\sim N(0,\tau_u^{2(h)})\) and later \(w^{(h)}_{\text{new}}\sim N(0,\tau_w^{2(h)})\), then draws step length, then turn angle conditional on that step length [PAGE 11]. The hypothetical next location is
\[
(x^{(h)}_{t+1},y^{(h)}_{t+1})
=
\left(
x_t+s^{(h)}_{t+1}\cos(\phi^{(h)}_{t+1}+b_t),
\ y_t+s^{(h)}_{t+1}\sin(\phi^{(h)}_{t+1}+b_t)
\right),
\]
where the hypothetical step and turn define a single next step [PAGE 12].

The valuation layer uses a multinomial classifier over ending yard lines \(l\in\{10,11,\dots,110\}\), with
\[
\ell_{ijt}=E[L_{ijt}\mid X_{ijt}]
=\sum_{l=10}^{110}l\,P(L_{ijt}=l\mid X_{ijt}),
\]
in yards [PAGE 12]. The frame-level comparison is
\[
\delta^{(h)}_{ijt}=\hat\ell_{ijt}-\hat\ell^{(h)}_{ijt},
\qquad
\bar\delta_{ijt}=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\delta^{(h)}_{ijt},
\]
in yards [PAGE 13]. The yards success rate is
\[
\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbf{1}\!\left(\delta^{(h)}_{ijt}>0\right),
\]
a dimensionless frame-level proportion [PAGE 18], and explosiveness is
\[
\mathbf{1}\!\left(\hat\ell_{ijt}>q_{0.95}\!\left(\hat\ell^{(h)}_{ijt}\right)\right),
\]
where \(q_{0.95}\) is the 95th quantile of the hypothetical ending-yard-line distribution [PAGE 19].

Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. If \(s_t=0\), then \(\operatorname{atan2}(0,0)\) is not a meaningful bearing, so \(\phi_t\) is undefined or implementation-dependent; any turn-angle inference at zero displacement is vetoed [PAGE 8].
2. If \(\phi_t\) is computed by raw subtraction across the \(\pm\pi\) branch cut, it can jump by approximately \(2\pi\); circular wrapping is required but not printed in the source [PAGE 8].
3. The scaled arcsine transform has endpoint sensitivity. The source says only that step length is normalized to between 0 and 1 and then arcsine-transformed [PAGE 25]; it does not print the exact scaling or a clipping rule. If the normalized value reaches an endpoint, an arcsine-type derivative can diverge or become numerically unstable, so endpoint handling is a gray zone.
4. In the turn model, \(\kappa_{ijt}\to0\) yields a nearly uniform circular distribution, while \(\kappa_{ijt}\to\infty\) yields a degenerate point mass; the log link \(\log\kappa=\gamma_0+\gamma_1s+w_j\) can overflow for extreme linear predictors [PAGE 10].
5. The yard-line expectation in Eq. (5) is a restricted-support sum over 10 through 110; if the classifier places mass outside that support or returns unnormalized probabilities, the “expectation” is a truncated or biased functional [PAGE 12].
6. The Monte Carlo average in Eq. (7) uses finite \(H\), with \(H=100\) in the reported results [PAGE 18]; the 95th-quantile indicator has finite tail resolution.
7. The simulation is one-step-ahead with non-ball-carrier trajectories fixed, so multi-step or multi-agent breakdown is explicitly acknowledged: forward simulation would need a tackle probability model and other-player movement models, and fixed non-ball-carrier trajectories “would introduce systematic bias” [PAGE 11, PAGE 21].

This means the choice of counterfactual baseline is part of the scientific claim, not a neutral preprocessing step. Fifth, the end-of-play yard-line model is trained on all data from the provided sample and tuned with game-level grouped cross-validation [PAGE 31], so the valuation layer is not a held-out future-season test. The historical lineage is coherent, but the multi-week inference trap is to convert a nine-week, one-step, fixed-other-agent evaluation into a season-long causal ranking.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper does not implement a recursive theory of mind. Its covariates are spatial and kinematic: locations, speeds, angles, distances, and counts of players in directions [PAGE 6]. The simulation holds other players fixed [PAGE 11]. There is no belief state for a defender, no anticipation model, no response policy, and no recursive “I think that you think that I will cut” layer. The discussion proposes a future action-selection extension with \(p(a_t\mid X_t)\) and a conditional step-selection distribution \(p(s_{t+1},\phi_{t+1}\mid a_t,X_t)\) [PAGE 20, PAGE 21], but that is explicitly future work, not an estimated component.

Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock:
- Crisis habit regression: the estimated player effects \(u_j\) and \(w_j\) are static random intercepts for step length and turn-angle concentration [PAGE 9, PAGE 10]. They are not state-dependent contact, fatigue, or congestion effects. The example play discusses first contact and post-contact movement [PAGE 16], but the equations shown do not include a first-contact indicator or a crisis-state interaction. Thus the model can describe an average movement profile, but it cannot separate a stable habit from a transient crisis regression under contact.
- Counter-trap: the generic baseline draws new random effects centered at zero, representing an average player [PAGE 11]. If a defender adapts to the observed ball carrier, the fixed-other-agent ghost is strategically non-responsive. The source itself shows baseline sensitivity: relative to a generic baseline versus the player’s own expectation, “some ball carriers exhibit higher success rates against generic alternatives than against their own simulated distributions,” and Christian McCaffrey is “penalized when compared to a generic baseline” [PAGE 31]. That is the counter-trap: the evaluation can reward or punish a player depending on which counterfactual distribution is treated as the baseline.
- Clock: the clock is the frame index and event sequence, not a causal decision clock. The ball-carrier window runs from handoff to tackle, out-of-bounds, or touchdown [PAGE 5], and the model predicts one step ahead at each frame [PAGE 11]. The frame-level \(\bar\delta\) series can rise and fall within a play, and the source notes that the interval band widens as the play develops [PAGE 16]. But there is no explicit play-clock, fatigue clock, or forward termination clock in the simulation. The model therefore has a temporal index but not a recursive temporal theory of opponent response.

The correct interpretation is kinematic and statistical: \(s_{ijt}\) is a per-frame displacement, and \(\phi_{ijt}\) is a bearing change.

The paper interprets higher concentration as lower variability in directional changes and links it to a player’s “ability to display variable change of direction” [PAGE 10], but that is a statistical movement trait, not a biomechanical diagnosis. Any claim about muscle activation, tendon load, proprioception, or rehabilitation status is vetoed by the absence of those variables.

Weather-layer veto. Table 2 lists the tracking features used: horizontal and vertical field position, distance from first-down line, speed, angle of motion relative to ball carrier, relative positions, and distance from ball carrier [PAGE 6]. Weather, temperature, wind, precipitation, humidity, and field-surface condition are not listed. The source does not include a weather covariate or a weather random effect. Cross-layer adjudication. The model conditions on the observed spatial configuration and holds non-ball-carrier trajectories fixed during simulation [PAGE 11]. It does not include a tackle-termination model, out-of-bounds termination model, or collision model in the simulation; the paper says forward simulation would require “a well-calibrated tackle probability model” [PAGE 21]. It also does not model the other players’ reactions, and the source warns that holding non-ball-carrier trajectories fixed “would introduce systematic bias” for forward simulation [PAGE 21]. Thus the valid cross-layer claim is narrow: the source supports a within-play, one-step, conditional-on-observed-others comparison of ball-carrier movement against a distribution of hypothetical next steps. It does not support full physical simulation, biomechanical inference, weather attribution, or multi-agent strategic counterfactuals.
