The objective is to maximize $W(S)$, the win probability function. A play $a$ induces a transition distribution $P(S'|S,a)$ and immediate reward $r(a) \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ (loss, tie, win). The value of action $a$ is $Q(S,a) = \mathbb{E}[r(a) + \gamma W(S')]$. For Kempton's case, $S_0 = (30, 4\text{min}, 0)$. Standard run $a_{std}$ yields $S' \approx (33, 3.8\text{min}, 0)$ with high prob, leading eventually to $W \approx 0.5$ (tie). Lateral $a_{lat}$ has outcomes: success to $x_s=80$ with prob $p$, leading to $W \approx 0.9$ after drive; failure to opponent at $x_f=70$ with prob $1-p$, leading to $W \approx 0.05$. Then $Q(S_0, a_{lat}) = p(0.9) + (1-p)(0.05)$. Break-even when $0.9p + 0.05(1-p) = 0.5 \Rightarrow 0.85p = 0.45 \Rightarrow p \approx 0.53$. Roper claims Kempton saw a "fair chance," implying $p > 0.53$ in his estimation, possibly due to unique situational advantage.

Incorporating masking: Define defense coverage quality $C \in [0,1]$ as prob they stop any given receiver. With two potential receivers in lateral scheme, if they are "masked" (indistinguishable pre-snap), defense assigns attention $c_1, c_2$ with $c_1 + c_2 = C_{total}$. Success prob for lateral $\approx (1-c_1)(1-c_2)$ if independent. Maximizing success occurs when $c_1 = c_2 = C_{total}/2$, i.e., perfect masking equalizes coverage. Roper’s “perfectly masked” implies minimizing $\max(c_i)$, thus maximizing $\prod (1-c_i)$.

Units: Field position in yards, time in seconds, probabilities dimensionless. Information units: Surprise of play choice measured by Kullback-Leibler divergence $D_{KL}(P_{true} || P_{expected})$, where $P_{true}$ is actual play distribution and $P_{expected}$ is defender’s prior.
