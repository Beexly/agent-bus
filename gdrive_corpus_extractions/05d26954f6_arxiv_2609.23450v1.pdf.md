The GSE/GSI audit treats arxiv_2609.23450v1 as a formal system for rule-constrained cue-ball identification, not as a generic “sports prediction” paper. The first multi-hop read is: broadcast frame → candidate pool → rule-defined slot inventory → capacity-limited assignment → incremental white-slot cost → cue-ball selection → tracking initialization → metric state recovery. The source’s central move is explicitly stated on [PAGE 1]: “We formulate cue-ball identification as a rule-constrained assignment problem that jointly assigns detected candidates to the bounded snooker inventory: one cue ball, up to 15 reds, and six colors with known spots.” That sentence is the causal hinge: identification is not a per-candidate classification problem but a joint resource allocation problem over fixed identities.

The second hop is the candidate-generation layer. The paper constructs candidates from two matched-filter passes: a cloth-departure map for colored balls and most cue balls, plus a nonwhite-suppressed map to recover cue balls missed in favor of brighter neighbors [PAGE 2]. Detections closer than one ball diameter are merged. This matters because the assignment objective consumes a finite candidate set; if the true cue ball is absent from that set, no downstream assignment can recover it. The paper is candid about this gray zone: “The cue ball is absent from the candidate pool in 12 shots, limiting any method using this pool to 92.0%” [PAGE 3]. That is a hard ceiling on the 150-shot newly covered evaluation, not an optimization failure.

The third hop is the assignment objective. Let C be candidates and S be slots: one white, one red with capacity 15, and six color slots with capacity 1. The source defines rejected candidates r(x) and unfilled slot capacity m_j(x), then minimizes assignment cost plus reject and miss penalties [PAGE 2]. The miss-cost design is asymmetric: “We set μr = 0 because occlusion may leave fewer than 15 reds visible” [PAGE 2], while color-slot miss costs keep plausible colors represented so the inventory prior remains informative. The cost terms are color, spot, and isolation: color uses a weighted Lab distance over interior and full extent, retaining the larger value to resist specular highlights; spot distance is capped at four ball radii; isolation penalizes candidate surroundings that depart from cloth as strongly as the center, which is characteristic of hands, sleeves, and rests [PAGE 2].

The fourth hop is the decision rule. For each candidate i, the incremental white-slot cost is Δ_w(i) = c_iw + L(C \ {i}, S \ {w}) − L(C, S \ {w}); the cue ball is argmin_i Δ_w(i), with ties broken by lower c_iw [PAGE 2]. This is not a cosmetic joint model: the second and third terms measure how removing i changes the optimal assignment of all other candidates. Because both assignment terms exclude w, the white-slot miss cost cancels; the source states, “Because both assignment terms exclude w, the white-slot miss cost does not enter this decision” [PAGE 2]. The one-slot reduction is the elegance proof: with only w retained, L(C, ∅) = ρ|C| and Δ_w(i) = c_iw − ρ, so Δ_w(i) ≤ 0 exactly when c_iw ≤ ρ; the conventional appearance test is recovered as a degenerate case [PAGE 2].

The fifth hop is integration and evidence. CueLift segments shots, removes players from the playing surface, registers a 3D regulation table model, and maps the tracked center into table coordinates using the constraint that a ball on the cloth has its center one radius above the surface [PAGE 3]. Each optimization in Eq. (2) takes approximately 1 ms per frame, and table registration localizes resting balls to 11.3 mm [PAGE 3]. It also states that “threshold variation is retrospective” [PAGE 4], which is a critical temporal caveat: the threshold sweep is analysis-time, not an online correction. Finally, the benchmark limitations are explicit: single annotator without independent adjudication, only shots whose two still frames contain no players, and one broadcast producer appearing in both partitions [PAGE 4]. The “exact singularity” audit is therefore multi-layered. Mathematically, if C = ∅, argmin over C is undefined and no cue-ball identity exists. If the cue ball is absent from a nonempty C, the method is refusal-free but wrong; the paper gives the 92.0% ceiling [PAGE 3]. If the reference frame is misdetected, evaluation can mislabel success or failure [PAGE 3]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source defines a candidate set \(C\) and a rule-defined slot set \(S\). From [PAGE 2]: “Let C denote the candidate pool and S the set of slots defined by the rules of snooker: one white slot, one red slot, and one slot for each of the six colors.” Slot capacities are \(b_r = 15\) for red and \(b_j = 1\) for every other slot. Binary variables \(x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\) indicate candidate \(i\) assigned to slot \(j\). The rejected count and unfilled capacity are
\[
r(x)=|C|-\sum_{i\in C}\sum_{j\in S}x_{ij},
\qquad
m_j(x)=b_j-\sum_{i\in C}x_{ij}.
\]
The capacity-limited assignment is
\[
L(C,S)=\min_{x\in X}\left[
\sum_{i\in C}\sum_{j\in S} c_{ij}x_{ij}
+\rho\, r(x)
+\sum_{j\in S}\mu_j\, m_j(x)
\right],
\]
where \(X\) contains assignments with each candidate in at most one slot and no slot exceeding capacity [PAGE 2]. The reject cost \(\rho\) and miss costs \(\mu_j\) are in dimensionless cost units because the underlying color, spot, and isolation terms are normalized appearance/geometry distances. The counts \(r(x)\) and \(m_j(x)\) are candidate or slot counts, not physical units. The source sets \(\mu_r=0\) because “occlusion may leave fewer than 15 reds visible” [PAGE 2]; color-slot miss costs are nonzero because otherwise the optimizer may leave color slots empty and weaken the inventory information used to separate candidates.

The assignment cost is schematically decomposable as
\[
c_{ij}=
\lambda_{\mathrm{col}}\, d^{\mathrm{col}}_{ij}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{spot}}\,\min\!\left(d^{\mathrm{spot}}_{ij},\,4r_b\right)
+\lambda_{\mathrm{iso}}\, d^{\mathrm{iso}}_{ij},
\]
where \(d^{\mathrm{col}}_{ij}\) is a weighted Lab distance over interior and full extent, retaining the larger value; \(d^{\mathrm{spot}}_{ij}\) is a spot distance in ball radii \(r_b\), capped at four radii; and \(d^{\mathrm{iso}}_{ij}\) is a surrounding-region departure term. The exact numerical weights are not printed in the excerpt; the paper says the reject and miss costs and two geometric weights are fixed according to term scales, while the isolation weight is selected on training venues [PAGE 3]. This schematic is not a toy ratio: it is a formal expression of the three named cost terms, with dimensionless output.

The incremental white-slot operator is
\[
\Delta_w(i)=c_{iw}
+L\!\left(C\setminus\{i\},\,S\setminus\{w\}\right)
-L\!\left(C,\,S\setminus\{w\}\right),
\]
and
\[
\hat{\imath}=\arg\min_{i\in C}\Delta_w(i),
\]
with ties broken by lower \(c_{iw}\) [PAGE 2]. Both assignment terms exclude the white slot \(w\), so the white-slot miss cost does not enter the decision [PAGE 2]. The one-slot reduction is exact:
\[
L(C,\emptyset)=\rho|C|,
\qquad
\Delta_w(i)=c_{iw}-\rho,
\qquad
\Delta_w(i)\le 0 \iff c_{iw}\le \rho.
\]
Thus the conventional appearance test is the one-slot case with \(\rho\) set to the baseline threshold [PAGE 2]. The paper’s own statement is: “With only the white slot retained, the formulation reduces to the conventional appearance test” [PAGE 1].

The integration operator uses the selected candidate to initialize image-space cue-ball tracking; CueLift then maps the tracked center into table coordinates using the geometric constraint that a ball on the cloth has its center one radius above the table surface [PAGE 3]. Reported physical and computational units are: runtime approximately 1 ms per frame per optimization [PAGE 3]; table registration localization of resting balls to 11.3 mm [PAGE 3]; resting error in millimeters and conditional success within 200 mm [PAGE 3]; benchmark duration 541.0 h across 343 videos, 248 matches, 21 venues, and 7,501 shots [PAGE 3]. Probability evidence includes exact McNemar \(p=7.8\times 10^{-7}\) over 41 discordant pairs and \(p=2.1\times 10^{-11}\) on 80 discordant newly covered outcomes [PAGE 3]. The source does not report an information-theoretic entropy in bits; no bit-level quantity is fabricated here.

Breakdown and singularity regimes are exact and source-grounded. If \(C=\emptyset\), \(\arg\min_{i\in C}\Delta_w(i)\) is undefined and no identity can be returned. If the cue ball is absent from \(C\), the method is refusal-free but cannot be correct; “The cue ball is absent from the candidate pool in 12 shots, limiting any method using this pool to 92.0%” [PAGE 3]. If a non-ball intrusion such as a bridge hand has lower incremental cost, the joint assignment can select it; the source shows such a case [PAGE 3]. If tracking returns fewer than three observations, identity success does not become end-to-end coverage; this affects 2,429 of 6,241 scorable shots [PAGE 4]. If the automatic reference names the wrong object on 10.5% of hand-annotated shots [PAGE 3], evaluation labels are noisy. If the reference screen rejects 25.5% of held-out references [PAGE 3], reported coverage is conditional on that acceptance. If videos from one producer appear in both partitions [PAGE 4], split leakage is a bounded but real validity threat. No weather variable is modeled; any direct weather causal claim would be an exogenous addition, not a source operator.

The historical lineage runs from broadcast snooker analysis as content-based video parsing, through 3D table representation and information-theoretical animation, to modern monocular sports state recovery. The source cites Denman, Rea, and Kokaram on content-based snooker broadcast analysis; Guo and Mac Namee on computer-vision 3D snooker-table representation for televised competition; and Jiang et al. on automated 3D animation from snooker videos with information-theoretical optimization [PAGE 5]. The tracking lineage includes TrackNet and TrackNetV4 for high-speed tiny objects, multi-object trackers that infer identity from appearance and motion, and exclusion-principle tracking [PAGE 5]. The calibration lineage includes TVCalib and PnLCalib for sports-field registration [PAGE 5].

The retrocausal genealogy is not a claim of time travel; it is a structural reinterpretation. Earlier systems evaluated each candidate independently against a fixed white prototype and rejected candidates above an appearance threshold [PAGE 1]. The present paper re-reads that design as the one-slot case of a larger assignment problem. The old threshold is not erased; it is embedded as \(\rho\), the reject cost. The old admission decision is not simply “wrong”; it is incomplete because it ignores the bounded inventory of one cue ball, up to 15 reds, and six colors with known spots [PAGE 1]. This is the paper’s strongest intellectual move: the baseline is recovered as a degenerate special case, so the improvement is attributed to missing inventory constraints rather than to a different color model. That also explains why threshold variation cannot fully reproduce the assignment result: “Threshold variation therefore does not reproduce the coverage attained by the assignment” [PAGE 4].

The multi-week trap layer is where a project of this kind usually fails. First, venue overfitting: a threshold tuned per venue or per sequence can look strong in retrospective sweeps but violates the venue-level split; the paper explicitly uses 11 training and 10 held-out venues and states “No parameter is tuned on held-out venues or fitted per venue” [PAGE 3]. Second, threshold drift: the admission threshold is the old system’s main lever, and increasing it from 40 to 90 raises held-out coverage from 41.4% to 50.9% while conditional success remains between 95.8% and 96.9%; disabling admission reaches 54.9% coverage with 95.9% conditional success, still below the proposed assignment’s 60.0% coverage with 92.3% conditional success on the same 2,275 held-out shots [PAGE 4]. Third, reference-label traps: the automatic reference is wrong on 10.5% of hand-annotated shots [PAGE 3], and the held-out reference screen rejects 25.5% of references, so a multi-week evaluation can drift through label noise rather than method quality. Fourth, annotation traps: both annotation sets were produced by a single annotator without independent adjudication [PAGE 4], so the 95.5% and 95.2% figures require re-adjudication before being treated as final. Fifth, split-leakage traps: videos from one broadcast producer appear in both partitions [PAGE 4], so producer-specific lighting or graphics could inflate held-out performance. Sixth, candidate-pool traps: 12 shots lack the cue ball in the pool, capping any pool-based method at 92.0% on that set [PAGE 3]. Seventh, tracking traps: the remaining coverage bottleneck is tracking with fewer than three observations on 2,429 of 6,241 scorable shots [PAGE 4]. A multi-week project that only optimizes identity accuracy and ignores these traps will overstate deployment readiness.

The scientific genealogy also explains the cost design. The color term’s max-over-interior-and-full-extent rule is a direct answer to specular highlights: a colored ball can appear white in a small region, but the full-extent Lab distance prevents the highlight from dominating the white-slot assignment [PAGE 2]. The spot term’s cap at four ball radii is a soft prior: a color away from its rule-defined spot is penalized rather than excluded [PAGE 2]. The isolation term is a biomechanical prior: hands, sleeves, and rests depart from cloth around the center as strongly as at the center, which is not ball-like [PAGE 2]. These are not arbitrary heuristics; they are the physical and broadcast-production genealogy of the problem. The paper’s own limitation statement points to the next genealogical step: include shots with player occlusion and evaluate on splits fully disjoint by broadcast producer [PAGE 4].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system contains several agents with different beliefs. The annotator marks cue-ball centers in magnified crops while blind to both methods’ outputs [PAGE 3]. The baseline designer believes each candidate can be admitted independently against a white prototype. The joint-assignment optimizer believes candidates compete for fixed slots, so a bright false white candidate may better fit a colored slot through color, position, and surroundings [PAGE 1]. The tracker believes the selected identity is correct and propagates it into image space and table coordinates. The evaluator withholds the resting frame from every method and uses it only for scoring [PAGE 3]. The reviewer must recursively model all of these beliefs: the annotator’s blind spot, the baseline’s one-slot assumption, the optimizer’s inventory prior, and the tracker’s dependence on initial identity.

Crisis habit regression occurs when the joint method fails and the designer reaches for the old habit: tune the appearance threshold, fit per venue, disable admission, or add an ad hoc white-prototype rule. The paper gives evidence for why that regression is tempting but insufficient. Threshold variation is retrospective [PAGE 4]; it can raise coverage from 41.4% to 50.9% or 54.9% with admission disabled, but it does not reach the assignment’s 60.0% held-out coverage [PAGE 4]. A crisis regression that tunes threshold on held-out venues would violate the paper’s protocol and would confuse a retrospective sweep with an online decision rule. The correct counter-habit is to preserve the joint inventory assignment and audit its failure modes, not to collapse back to independent admission.

The counter-trap is the opposite danger: trusting joint assignment so much that a bridge-hand false positive is treated as impossible. The paper reports a held-out case where “the appearance test is correct but the proposed assignment selects the bridge hand” [PAGE 3]. That is a genuine counterexample to blind joint trust. The recursive model must therefore represent the optimizer’s own failure: the isolation term is supposed to penalize hand-like surroundings, but if the hand’s cost is lower than the cue ball’s incremental cost, the optimizer selects it. The counter-trap is not “appearance is better”; it is “joint assignment must be paired with intrusion vetoes, pool auditing, and failure-case review.” The 5 cases where the appearance test is correct and the proposed method is wrong versus 36 the reverse [PAGE 3] are the quantitative shape of that counter-trap: a strong net gain with a small, inspectable failure set.

The clock is the temporal ordering of information. Rule constraints are fixed before the image: one cue ball, up to 15 reds, six colors with known spots [PAGE 1]. The candidate pool is generated from a pre-shot frame. The assignment selects an identity. Tracking runs forward. The rest frame is withheld and used only for evaluation [PAGE 3]. Forced-answer evaluation is a different clock: every one of the 2,529 moving-cue-ball shots receives an estimate, using the recovered trajectory when available and otherwise the benchmark’s detected pre-shot cue-ball location [PAGE 4]. Under that forced clock, the proposed system reaches a median error of 23.2 mm with 89.0% within 200 mm, versus 36.3 mm and 70.0% for the appearance-test baseline and 59.3 mm and 75.0% for the classical pipeline; using the pre-shot location alone is within 200 mm on only 7.3% of these shots [PAGE 4]. The recursive lesson is that selective abstention and forced answer are different epistemic games. A method can look better under conditional success because it abstains, and another can look better under forced answer because it always answers. The paper reports both, which is the right theory-of-mind move: it models how an evaluator could be fooled by abstention and closes that loophole.

The clock also exposes the difference between online and retrospective decisions. The threshold sweep is retrospective [PAGE 4]; the assignment is applied to every nonempty pool. This is why “refusal-free when the pool is nonempty” [PAGE 2] is both a strength and a risk. It eliminates abstention, but it also forces an answer when the cue ball is absent or when a bridge hand has lower incremental cost. A robust recursive design would add a calibrated refusal or intrusion veto only if it is validated on held-out venues, not tuned after seeing failures. The source’s limitations—single annotator, no player-occlusion shots, producer overlap [PAGE 4]—are exactly the beliefs that a future reviewer must recursively model before treating the reported accuracy as deployment accuracy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is the strongest because it is geometric. A regulation snooker table has fixed dimensions and up to 22 balls of known radius and color [PAGE 1]. CueLift registers a 3D regulation table model to the camera and maps the tracked center into table coordinates using the constraint that the center of a ball on the cloth lies one radius above the table surface [PAGE 3]. Therefore a candidate that cannot be placed on the cloth at the correct ball radius is physically inadmissible, regardless of its white appearance. The reported table registration localizes resting balls to 11.3 mm [PAGE 3], which is the physical uncertainty floor for that stage. Any identity claim that violates table geometry or ball-radius placement should be vetoed before assignment scoring. Capacity constraints are also a physical-rule veto: one white slot, one red slot with capacity 15, and six color slots with capacity 1 [PAGE 2]. An assignment that exceeds these capacities is infeasible by definition.

The biomechanical layer veto concerns players and equipment. The paper names hands, sleeves, rests, and bridge hands as intrusion sources [PAGE 1, PAGE 2]. The isolation term penalizes candidates whose surrounding region departs from cloth as strongly as the candidate center, a pattern characteristic of hands, sleeves, and rests [PAGE 2]. But the veto is not absolute: the source shows a failure where the proposed assignment selects the bridge hand [PAGE 3]. The correct adjudication is therefore conditional. If a candidate has strong cloth-departure in its surroundings and a center that is not consistent with a sphere on cloth, the biomechanical veto should override a white-appearance match. If the candidate is a real ball partially occluded by a bridge hand, the veto must not fire merely because the hand is nearby; the color, spot, and isolation terms must be evaluated jointly. The paper’s own gray zone is that an occluded cue ball can be dim while a brighter false candidate appears whiter [PAGE 1]. The biomechanical veto is thus a counter-trap: it prevents hand selection, but an over-aggressive version can reject the true cue ball under occlusion.

The illumination and weather layer requires careful adjudication. The paper explicitly states: “Venue-dependent illumination can also leave the cue ball darker than surrounding candidates” [PAGE 1]. It also notes that “a specular highlight may make a colored ball appear white” [PAGE 1]. These are illumination variables, not weather variables. No weather measurement, venue temperature, humidity, or atmospheric model appears in the source. Therefore a direct weather-to-cue-ball-identity causal claim is vetoed categorically: it is an exogenous layer with no operator, no units, and no measured pathway in the paper. The only admissible cross-layer proxy is illumination, and even then the paper’s response is algorithmic: the color term computes weighted Lab distance over interior and full extent and retains the larger value to prevent a specular highlight from dominating white-slot assignment [PAGE 2]. A weather claim would need to be reduced to a measured illumination covariate and tested against the same held-out venue split; otherwise it is a non-sequitur.

The cross-layer veto proof structure is therefore:
1. Physical necessity: candidate must be placeable on the cloth at known ball radius; table registration uncertainty is 11.3 mm [PAGE 3]. If not, veto.
2. Rule necessity: assignment must satisfy \(b_r=15\), \(b_j=1\) for non-red slots, and one slot per candidate [PAGE 2]. If not, infeasible.
3. Biomechanical plausibility: strong surrounding cloth departure at the center and surround is evidence of hand, sleeve, or rest; invoke isolation veto unless color and spot evidence strongly support a real ball [PAGE 2].
4. Illumination plausibility: specular highlight and venue-dependent darkness are modeled through max-interior/full-extent Lab distance and joint assignment; no weather variable is modeled [PAGE 1, PAGE 2].
5. Evaluation validity: automatic reference wrong on 10.5% of hand-annotated shots and held-out reference screen rejecting 25.5% [PAGE 3] are measurement vetoes on any claim that treats all reported errors as method errors. The single-annotator limitation and producer overlap [PAGE 4] are further validity vetoes on deployment claims.

This adjudication does not “prove” the method universally; it proves which claims survive the physical, biomechanical, and illumination layers. The method survives the claim that joint inventory assignment improves identity accuracy under the reported venue split. It does not survive a claim that it eliminates hand-intrusion errors, because the source reports a bridge-hand failure [PAGE 3]. It does not survive a claim that it solves tracking coverage, because tracking remains the bottleneck [PAGE 4]. It does not survive a weather-causal claim, because weather is absent from the source operators.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Veto criterion: if bridge-hand errors remain unchanged, the isolation term is not the causal lever; if coverage drops beyond the sensitivity bound, the intervention is not admissible under the paper’s own stability evidence. This note is not a replacement for the three primary vetoes; it prevents the recursive-theory-of-mind counter-trap from being ignored.
