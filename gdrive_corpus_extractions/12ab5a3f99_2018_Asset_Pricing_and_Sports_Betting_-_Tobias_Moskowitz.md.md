FRONTIER REASONING FOUNDRY audit of the supplied source: the multi-hop derivation begins with the identification problem. The paper’s first move is to escape the joint hypothesis problem by choosing a market where the stochastic discount factor is not doing cross-sectional work: “Two unique features of this market – no systematic risk and terminal values exogenous to betting activity – evade the joint hypothesis problem and allow mispricing to be detected” [PAGE 1]. The second move is to convert quoted contract prices into expected payoffs using estimated state probabilities, then compute returns as normalized payoff changes. The third move is to regress the post-price-movement terminal return on the pre-terminal price movement: if information is priced rationally, the coefficient is zero; if pure noise drives the price movement, the coefficient is negative one; if information is mispriced, the sign separates underreaction from overreaction. The paper states the rational case as “Prediction 1: If prices move (P0 = P1) on information and markets respond rationally, then β1 = 0” [PAGE 11], the pure-noise case as “Prediction 2: If prices move (P0 = P1) for non-information reasons, then β1 = −1” [PAGE 11], and the misreaction case as “if markets overreact to the news, then the open-to-close return will negatively predict the close-to-end return” [PAGE 11]. The empirical coefficient is around negative one-half, and the paper reads it as “about half of that movement gets reversed/corrected by the game outcome” [PAGE 18].

The fourth hop is cross-sectional: define analogues of momentum, value, and size, then estimate two reduced-form regressions, one for initial-to-final price movement and one for final-to-terminal price movement. The fifth hop is theoretical: overreaction implies a positive first-stage response and a negative second-stage reversal; value should be the mirror image; size should be irrelevant if efficiently priced or unimportant. The sixth hop is uncertainty: “stronger momentum effects and weaker value effects when there is greater uncertainty” [PAGE 4]. The seventh hop transfers the uncertainty mechanism to equities, where “price momentum is much stronger among the stale earnings (e.g., higher uncertainty) firms, exhibiting an 11.81 percent annual return compared to only a 1.98 percent annual return for price momentum among firms whose earnings announcement occurred within the last two weeks” [PAGE 31]. The eighth hop is limits to arbitrage: “trading costs in sports betting wipe out any profits to trading on these patterns” [PAGE 37]. The ninth hop is covariance: sports contracts are idiosyncratic, and the paper states “The lack of common risk among value and momentum contracts in sports betting markets is further evidence that these markets are devoid of systematic risk” [PAGE 36].

Gray-zone audit. First, the source text’s H3 statement contains an internal tension: it says noise gives β1 = 0 and βT = −β1 while also requiring |β1 − βT| positive; mathematically, a price-moving noise signal requires β1 nonzero. This must be treated as a typographical ambiguity, not a new theory. Second, probability extraction from sparse extreme prices is a real estimator-risk zone; the paper itself notes that the non-parametric estimator is less reliable at extreme winner-contract prices and uses a probit estimator there [PAGE 60]. Third, the evidence is associational: the paper rules out several confounds, including microstructural volume proxy tests, confidence-combination tests, exchange data, and alternative biases [PAGE 18, PAGE 19, PAGE 27, PAGE 28], but it does not prove a structural causal mechanism. Fifth, the cost result is a threshold result: gross alpha can be positive while net alpha is negative when commission friction exceeds gross expected return. These are the exact gray zones to keep live.

Let contract class \(c\in\{A,B,C\}\) denote, respectively, a differential-payoff contract, a winner-payoff contract, and an aggregate-score contract. For \(t<T\), define the normalized price operator
\[
P_t^{(c)}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_c}\pi_t^{(c)}(s)\,X_s^{(c)},
\]
where \(X_s^{(c)}\) is the payoff in normalized payoff units per unit stake, and \(\pi_t^{(c)}(s)\) is a dimensionless probability. At the terminal date \(T\), \(\pi_T^{(c)}(s)=\mathbf{1}\{s=s_T\}\), so \(P_T^{(c)}=X_{s_T}^{(c)}\). The return operator over horizon \(t:t'\) is
\[
R_{t:t'}^{(c)}=\frac{P_{t'}^{(c)}}{P_t^{(c)}}-1,
\]
which is dimensionless. The source describes this conversion as estimating “the probability, \(\pi(S_t)\), of a payoff occurring” and using the expected value of payoff states [PAGE 9]. Units: prices are normalized payoff units per unit stake; probabilities and returns are dimensionless; horizons are measured in games, weeks, and seasons; annualized returns are percent per year; Sharpe and information ratios are dimensionless.

The core identification regression is
\[
R_{j,1:T}=\alpha+\beta_1 R_{j,0:1}+\varepsilon_j,
\]
where \(R_{j,0:1}\) is initial-to-final pre-terminal price movement and \(R_{j,1:T}\) is final-to-terminal movement. The source’s testable regimes are: \(\beta_1=0\) under rational information pricing; \(\beta_1=-1\) under pure non-information price movement; \(\beta_1\) positive under underreaction; \(\beta_1\) negative under overreaction [PAGE 11]. Breakdown/singularity regimes: \(P_t=0\) makes the return undefined; \(\pi_t(s)\in\{0,1\}\) makes the contract degenerate; \(\beta_1=-1\) is the full-reversal boundary; \(\beta_1=0\) is the no-predictability boundary.

The cross-sectional operators are
\[
R_{j,0:1}=\alpha_1+\beta_1\,\mathrm{Char}_j+\epsilon_{j,0:1},
\qquad
R_{j,1:T}=\alpha_T+\beta_T\,\mathrm{Char}_j+\epsilon_{j,1:T},
\]
with \(\mathrm{Char}_j\in\{\mathrm{Mom}_j,\mathrm{Val}_j,\mathrm{Size}_j\}\). Here \(\beta_1-\beta_T\) measures the absolute price movement plus reversal, while \(\beta_1+\beta_T\) measures the cumulative initial-to-terminal relation. The source’s hypotheses are: no relevance or efficient information gives \(\beta_1=\beta_T=0\); pure noise gives \(\beta_T=-\beta_1\); underreaction gives same-sign predictability; overreaction gives opposite-sign predictability [PAGE 13, PAGE 14]. The source text’s H3 typo should be read carefully: its stated \(\beta_1=0,\beta_T=-\beta_1\) conflicts with its own requirement that \(|\beta_1-\beta_T|\) be positive; logically, a price-moving noise characteristic must have nonzero \(\beta_1\). This is a typographical breakdown regime, not an empirical result.

Momentum is constructed from past wins, past point differentials, past dollar returns, and past point returns over 1 to 8 games, with a principal-component index [PAGE 20, PAGE 21]. Value is constructed from negative long-term past performance over 1 to 3 seasons, fundamental-to-price measures, and a model-implied expected price relative to observed price [PAGE 21, PAGE 22]. Size is franchise value, ticket revenue, revenue, and payroll [PAGE 22]. Uncertainty operators are early-season timing, combination-bet activity, stale versus recent earnings announcements, and analyst forecast dispersion [PAGE 29, PAGE 31, PAGE 32]. Commission friction is a cost operator \(C_j\); the net-return condition is \(E[R_j^{\text{gross}}]-C_j\). The source reports that net returns are negative because the commission is large relative to gross predictability [PAGE 33, PAGE 34]. Breakdown regimes: sparse extreme prices make non-parametric probability estimates unstable; overlapping test assets and factors generate mechanical covariance; uncertainty proxies fail if they correlate with the characteristics; and arbitrage is non-viable when \(C_j\) exceeds gross expected return.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: the bettor treats recent performance as if it were a persistent quality signal, especially when true quality is uncertain. The observed pattern is “bettors seem to chase recent performance and push the price in the direction of momentum, which gets reversed by the game outcome” [PAGE 24]. The recursive belief is not merely “team A is good”; it is “other bettors will treat team A as good,” which pushes the price further and creates the reversal. Under high uncertainty, the source predicts stronger momentum and weaker value [PAGE 4], because the agent has less precise fundamentals to anchor to: “greater uncertainty implies less precise information to update to, which allows the overreaction to continue unchecked” [PAGE 29].

Counter-trap: the sophisticated arbitrageur sees the overreaction but is trapped by commission friction. If gross expected return is positive but less than the cost of taking and unwinding the position, the rational counter-trap is not to trade. The source’s net-return evidence is exactly this: gross predictability exists, but “trading costs in sports betting wipe out any profits to trading on these patterns” [PAGE 37]. The recursive theory-of-mind layer is therefore two-level: the naive bettor extrapolates; the sophisticated bettor knows the naive bettor extrapolates; but the sophisticated bettor also knows the cost structure and declines to correct the price. This is why the anomaly survives.

Clock: the terminal date \(T\) is short and exogenous. The clock has three scales: the contract clock from initial price to final price to game outcome; the season clock from early-season high uncertainty to late-season lower uncertainty; and the earnings clock from recent announcement to stale announcement. The source’s equity transfer test shows the same clock logic: momentum is stronger for stale earnings firms and value is stronger for recent earnings firms [PAGE 31]. The clock also disciplines the interpretation: the correction must occur by \(T\), not by an unobserved infinite-horizon discount-rate adjustment.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
