On [PAGE 4] the authors define \(f_i=s_i(p_i(t+T))-s_j(p_j(t+T))\) and note that “drone racing can be seen as a zero-sum differential game because clearly from (8) one has f1 + f2 = 0.” This is the formal entry point into Nash equilibrium. Third hop: discretisation. Problem (9) converts continuous control into piecewise-constant inputs \(u_i^k=v_i\delta t\), dynamic equality \(p_i^k=p_i^{k-1}+u_i^k\), collision inequality \(\|p_j^k-p_i^k\|\ge d_i\), track inequality \(|n(p_i^k)^T[p_i^k-\tau(p_i^k)]|\le w_\tau\), and speed inequality \(\|u_i^k\|\le \bar u_i\). Fourth hop: Nash condition. A strategy profile \((\theta_1^*,\theta_2^*)\) is Nash if no player gains by unilateral change, eq. (11), and a best-reply map is defined by eq. (12). Fifth hop: the key gray-zone correction. The authors state on [PAGE 5] that “a direct application of IBR to (9) does not allow to fully capture the implications of the collision avoidance constraints (9c).” Therefore they add the sensitivity term \(\alpha \mu_j^l\,\partial\gamma_j/\partial\theta_i\), eq. (15), where \(\mu_j^l\) is the vector of Lagrange multipliers for the joint collision constraints and \(\alpha\ge0\) is an aggressiveness factor. Sixth hop: the reduced drone-racing form, eq. (16), contains the arc-length term plus \(\alpha\sum_{k=1}^N \mu_j^{k,l}{\beta_{ij}^{k,l}}^T p_i^k\). On [PAGE 6] the interpretation is explicit: “player i can win the race by either going faster along the track or by getting in the way of player j, thus obstructing its motion along the path.” Seventh hop: numerical linearisation. The derivative of \(s_i\) with respect to terminal position is \(ds_i/dp_i^N=t^T/[1-\kappa(p_i^N-\tau)^T n]:=\sigma(p_i^N)\), eq. (18), with \(\kappa\) in inverse metres and the denominator dimensionless. The exact singularity is \(1-\kappa(p_i^N-\tau)^T n=0\), where the gradient is unbounded and the QCLP linearisation can break. Eighth hop: orientation and estimation. Yaw is set to keep the opponent bearing centred, and the opponent relative state is estimated by a Kalman filter with dynamics eq. (19) and measurement eq. (20). The paper’s real-time claim is “at 20Hz, on standard hardware” [PAGE 2], and the simulation planner rate is “20 Hz” [PAGE 8].

The gray-zone audit is where the paper must be read against itself. It proves only that if the iteration converges, the tuple satisfies necessary Nash conditions [PAGE 2]; it does not prove global convergence. It explicitly says that because of non-convexity “the optimization algorithm can converge to local minima” [PAGE 10]. It also says that the planner “assumes that the opponent is using a similar planning strategy with a known cost function” [PAGE 10], and that the planner can “overestimate” the intelligence of the adversary and generate “overly conservative trajectories” [PAGE 10]. The sensitivity lemma assumes that the active constraint set stays the same in a neighbourhood; if the collision constraint toggles on and off, the derivative is no longer a valid first-order model. The QCLP step is a local approximation, and the paper admits “we opt for a local optimization strategy thus potentially sacrificing optimality for the sake of increasing performance” [PAGE 7]. The vision layer adds another gray zone: if the bearing violates \(\beta_{ij}^T e_1\ge\cos\alpha\), measurement (20) is unavailable or degraded, and the Kalman filter must coast on a constant-velocity model. Weather is not modelled at all; the filter explicitly assumes “constant world-frame linear velocities for both robots” [PAGE 8]. Thus the correct audit separates three claims: the physical constraints are hard vetoes; the Nash claim is conditional on convergence and active-set stability; the performance claim is empirical and opponent-model dependent.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source operators are stated in SI-compatible variables and probability/information variables. The translational state is \(p_i\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in metres; yaw is \(\psi_i\in S^1\) in radians; body linear velocity is \(v_i\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in metres per second; angular rate is \(\omega_i\in\mathbb{R}\) in radians per second. The nominal dynamics on [PAGE 3] are
\[
\dot p_i=R_i v_i,\qquad \dot\psi_i=\omega_i,\qquad R_i=R_z(\psi_i)\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2},
\]
with the actuator bound \(\|v_i\|=\|\dot p_i\|\le \bar v_i\), where \(\bar v_i\) has units of metres per second. The track operator is a twice continuously differentiable immersed closed curve \(\tau:[0,l_\tau]\to\mathbb{R}^2\), with \(l_\tau\) in metres, \(\tau(0)=\tau(l_\tau)\), \(t=\tau'\), \(n\kappa=\tau''\), \(\kappa\) in inverse metres, and the local track coordinate
\[
s_i(p_i)=\arg\min_s \frac12\|\tau(s)-p_i\|^2.
\]
The hard track and collision constraints are
\[
|n(s_i)^T[p_i-\tau(s_i)]|\le w_\tau,\qquad \|p_i-p_j\|\ge d_i,
\]
with \(w_\tau\) and \(d_i\) in metres. The relative-position measurement is
\[
p_{ij}=R_i^T(p_j-p_i),
\]
and the field-of-view condition is
\[
\beta_{ij}^T e_1\ge \cos\alpha,\qquad \beta_{ij}=\frac{p_{ij}}{\|p_{ij}\|},
\]
where \(\alpha\) is the camera half-field-of-view in radians and \(e_1=(1,0)\) is the optical axis [PAGE 4]. The paper’s zero-sum payoff is
\[
f_i=s_i(p_i(t+T))-s_j(p_j(t+T)),\qquad f_1+f_2=0,
\]
with \(s_i\) and \(s_j\) in metres and planning horizon \(T\) in seconds [PAGE 4]. The discretised planner solves
\[
\max_{\theta_i}\ s_i(p_i^N)
\]
subject to
\[
p_i^k=p_i^{k-1}+u_i^k,\qquad \|p_j^k-p_i^k\|\ge d_i,\qquad |n(p_i^k)^T[p_i^k-\tau(p_i^k)]|\le w_\tau,\qquad \|u_i^k\|\le \bar u_i,
\]
where \(u_i^k=v_i\delta t\), \(\delta t\) is the sampling interval in seconds, and \(\theta_i=(p_i^1,\ldots,p_i^N,u_i^1,\ldots,u_i^N)\) [PAGE 4]. The Nash condition is
\[
\theta_i^*=\arg\max_{\theta_i\in\Theta_i(\theta_j^*)} s_i(\theta_i),
\]
and the best-reply return is
\[
s_i^*(\theta_j)=\max_{\theta_i\in\Theta_i(\theta_j)} s_i(\theta_i).
\]
The sensitivity operator is the paper’s Lemma 1:
\[
\left.\frac{ds_j^*}{d\theta_i}\right|_{\theta_i^{l-1}}
=
-\mu_j^l\left.\frac{\partial\gamma_j}{\partial\theta_i}\right|_{(\theta_i^{l-1},\theta_j^l)},
\]
where \(\mu_j^l\) is a row vector of Lagrange multipliers for the joint inequality constraints \(\gamma_j\le0\) [PAGE 5]. The modified cost is
\[
\max_{\theta_i\in\Theta_i^l}\left[s_i(\theta_i)+\alpha\mu_j^l\left.\frac{\partial\gamma_j}{\partial\theta_i}\right|_{(\theta_i^{l-1},\theta_j^l)}\theta_i\right],
\]
with \(\alpha\ge0\) dimensionless [PAGE 5]. In the drone-racing reduction, eq. (16) is
\[
\max_{\theta_i\in\Theta_i^l}\left[
\arg\min_s \frac12\|\tau(s)-p_i^N\|^2
+
\alpha\sum_{k=1}^N \mu_j^{k,l}{\beta_{ij}^{k,l}}^T p_i^k
\right],
\]
where
\[
\beta_{ij}^{k,l}=\frac{p_j^{k,l}-p_i^{k,l-1}}{\|p_j^{k,l}-p_i^{k,l-1}\|}
\]
is dimensionless [PAGE 6]. For a locally linear track aligned to unit vector \(t\), the paper rewrites the effective objective as
\[
t^T p_i^N-\alpha\sum_{k=1}^N \mu_j^{k,l}{\beta_{ij}^{k,l}}^T(p_j^{k,l}-p_i^k),
\]
and explains that the extra term rewards reducing distance from the predicted opponent along the bearing direction [PAGE 6]. The terminal arc-length gradient is
\[
\frac{ds_i}{dp_i^N}
=
\frac{t^T}{1-\kappa(p_i^N-\tau)^T n}
:=\sigma(p_i^N),
\]
eq. (18), with \(\sigma\) dimensionless because \(s_i\) and \(p_i^N\) are both in metres [PAGE 7].

Assumptions and exact breakdown regimes must be stated. The sensitivity result assumes differentiability, KKT regularity, and that “in the vicinity of c, the set of active constraints remains the same” [PAGE 11]. If an active collision constraint toggles, eq. (14) is not a valid local derivative. Problem (16) is non-linear and non-convex; the paper says “we cannot guarantee the uniqueness of an optimal solution,” and left-versus-right overtaking can produce multiple equilibria [PAGE 6]. The IBR scheme gives only necessary Nash conditions if it converges [PAGE 2]. The exact curvature singularity is
\[
1-\kappa(p_i^N-\tau)^T n=0,
\]
where \(\sigma(p_i^N)\) is unbounded and the QCLP linearisation can fail. The track parameterisation also requires \(\tau'(s)\neq0\); the immersed-curve assumption rules this out in the source, but a numerical implementation that estimates \(\tau'\) can still lose rank. The visibility constraint breaks when \(\beta_{ij}^T e_1<\cos\alpha\); then measurement eq. (20) is unavailable or corrupted [PAGE 4]. The collision-feasibility breakdown occurs when no \(\theta_i\) can satisfy both \(\|p_j^k-p_i^k\|\ge d_i\) and the track-width inequality simultaneously; the paper notes \(\Theta_i^l\) is never empty only under the assumption that the robots “do not start from a position that violates (9c) and (9d)” [PAGE 6]. The Kalman-filter model on [PAGE 8] uses \(w\sim\mathcal N(0_6,Q)\) and \(v\sim\mathcal N(0_3,R)\) with additive Gaussian noise; \(p_{ij}\) is in metres, \(v_{ij}\) in metres per second, \(R\) has units of square metres, and \(Q\) has mixed position-velocity covariance units. The filter assumes constant world-frame linear velocities, so unmodelled wind or acceleration is a model-breakdown regime.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from differential-game theory and Nash equilibrium through model predictive control, reciprocal collision avoidance, and sensitivity-based bilevel optimisation. The paper cites Başar and Olsder for dynamic noncooperative game theory [PAGE 2 and PAGE 12], Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs reachability for antagonistic disturbance models [PAGE 3], Stackelberg formulations for autonomous driving [PAGE 3], generalized Nash equilibrium for intersection coordination [PAGE 3], and the RVO library as a reactive multi-agent benchmark [PAGE 7]. The retrocausal point is that the later real-time approximation retroactively defines what the earlier game “means” in practice: the exact Nash equilibrium is computationally difficult, so the paper’s IBR with sensitivity analysis becomes the operational object. On [PAGE 5] the authors say they use an “iterated best response algorithm (IBR)” and on [PAGE 2] they say “if our algorithm converges, the output satisfies necessary conditions for a Nash equilibrium.” That is a conditional, not a global, genealogy: the exact equilibrium is the ancestor, the fixed-L IBR is the descendant, and the descendant’s active-set and local-optimisation assumptions determine which ancestor is actually approximated.

The multi-week traps are concrete. Trap one: treating the Nash label as a global guarantee. The paper only proves necessary conditions under convergence, and it admits local minima on [PAGE 10]. A multi-week experiment that assumes global equilibrium can mistake a local left-side overtake for the unique solution. Trap two: ignoring active-set changes. The sensitivity lemma is valid only while the same joint constraints remain active [PAGE 11]; if the collision constraint chatters, the derivative term is not trustworthy and the planner can oscillate. Trap three: conflating “20 Hz” with robust online control. The source reports 20 Hz on standard hardware [PAGE 2] and 20 Hz planner rate in simulation [PAGE 8], but it also says the planner uses a constant number of iterations \(L\) and local QCLP steps [PAGE 6 and PAGE 7]. A multi-week test that increases the number of obstacles, players, or vision latency can silently change the effective recursion depth. Trap four: overfitting to the opponent model. On [PAGE 10] the authors state that the planner “assumes that the opponent is using a similar planning strategy with a known cost function,” and on [PAGE 9] they observe that GTP “often tends to ‘overestimate’ its opponent.” This is a retrocausal failure: the planner’s model of the opponent changes the planner’s own apparent aggressiveness. Trap five: forgetting that the track geometry is an input. The arc-length derivative has a curvature denominator [PAGE 7]; a tight curve or large lateral offset can push the denominator near zero even when the physical track constraint is satisfied. Trap six: treating RVO as a fair proxy for reactive intelligence. The paper says RVO is “the least effective one among the three alternatives” because it is “a reactive (instantaneous) control strategy” while GTP and MPC use an extended planning horizon [PAGE 9]. A multi-week benchmark that uses only RVO can overstate the value of planning without testing the active-blocking regime.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind ladder in this source has four useful levels. Level 0 is RVO: instantaneous reciprocal avoidance with no extended planning horizon; the paper calls it “a reactive (instantaneous) control strategy” [PAGE 9]. Level 1 is MPC: the ego predicts the opponent as moving straight at maximum speed along the local track direction, then solves a single optimal-control problem [PAGE 7]. Level 2 is the paper’s GTP: each player runs IBR, solves its own problem and the opponent’s problem alternately, and uses sensitivity analysis to approximate how the opponent’s best response changes with the ego action [PAGE 5]. Level 3 is the recursive fixed point: both players run the same recursion, each modelling the other’s modelling, truncated after a constant number \(L\) of iterations [PAGE 6]. The paper does not claim unbounded recursion; it claims a real-time approximation.

Crisis habit regression appears when the clock or perception budget collapses. If \(L\) is reduced, or if the QCLP inner iterations stop early, the planner regresses toward level 1: it still has a horizon but loses the opponent-reaction term. If the vision measurement is lost, the Kalman filter coasts on the constant-velocity model [PAGE 8], which is effectively a level-1 prediction. If compute is tight, RVO-like reactive behaviour is the fallback. The paper’s own warning is relevant: GTP can “overestimate” the opponent when the opponent is not actually using GTP, producing “overly cautious” trajectories [PAGE 9 and PAGE 10]. That is a theory-of-mind error, not a physical error: the ego assigns a higher recursion level to the opponent than the opponent actually uses.

The counter-trap is the active-collision-constraint exploit. On [PAGE 6] the authors explain that when \(\mu_j^{k,l}>0\), player \(i\) can improve its reward by reducing distance from the opponent’s predicted position along \(\beta_{ij}^{k,l}\); the paper’s own summary is that player \(i\) can “get in the way of player j, thus obstructing its motion along the path.” This is a counter-trap because it uses the opponent’s safety constraint against it. But the trap has a counter-counter: a non-reactive opponent that ignores collisions forces the GTP to make room, and the GTP’s assumption of reciprocal rationality can make it too cautious. The paper’s simulation comparison shows this asymmetry: when GTP is faster than MPC, it overtakes in approximately 30 percent of races, and the authors attribute the caution to overestimating the opponent [PAGE 9]; when GTP is slower, it “manages to defend its initial advantage for the vast majority of the races” by moving sideways to block [PAGE 9].

The clock layer is the receding-horizon scheduler. The planner discretises the horizon into \(N\) steps with sampling interval \(\delta t\), solves a finite number of IBR iterations \(L\), and outputs the first control before replanning. The simulation uses a 10 ms simulation step but a 20 Hz planner [PAGE 8]; the hardware experiment uses a forward-facing 752 by 480 pixel camera at 15 fps [PAGE 10]. The yaw controller is also on the clock: it aligns the camera axis to \(\beta_{ij}\) using \(\psi_i^k=\operatorname{atan2}(e_2^T\beta_{ij}^k,e_1^T\beta_{ij}^k)\) and differentiates consecutive samples for \(\omega_i^k\) [PAGE 7]. Thus the recursive ToM is bounded by three clocks: the planning horizon \(T\), the IBR iteration budget \(L\), and the perception frame period. When any clock dominates, the system’s effective recursion depth drops.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is valid when a lower layer makes the upper-layer plan physically or informationally impossible. Physical veto: the actuator bound \(\|v_i\|\le\bar v_i\) and the collision bound \(\|p_i-p_j\|\ge d_i\) are hard constraints; if a proposed trajectory requires \(\|u_i^k\|>\bar u_i\), the QCLP is infeasible or must be clipped, and the game-theoretic payoff is not realisable. The track-width constraint \(|n(s_i)^T[p_i-\tau(s_i)]|\le w_\tau\) is also hard; a Nash plan that exits the track is vetoed regardless of its payoff. The paper’s simulation parameters give \(d=0.8\) m, \(w_\tau=1.5\) m, robot radius 0.3 m, and speed limits 0.5 and 0.6 m/s [PAGE 8]; the hardware experiment gives \(d=0.6\) m, \(w_\tau=0.9\) m, a 2.3 m square track with rounded corners, and 0.6 m/s maximum speed with a 0.3 m/s assumed opponent limit [PAGE 10]. Any counterfactual that violates these numbers is not a valid test of the source algorithm; it is a different physical problem.

Information veto: the visibility condition \(\beta_{ij}^T e_1\ge\cos\alpha\) must hold for the camera measurement \(y_i=p_{ij}+v\) to be useful [PAGE 4 and PAGE 8]. If the bearing leaves the field of view, the Kalman filter loses its measurement and the opponent state estimate is prediction-only. The hardware camera has 752 by 480 pixels at 15 fps [PAGE 10], so the information layer has finite angular resolution and finite temporal sampling. A plan that assumes continuous perfect relative-position knowledge is informationally vetoed. The estimator also assumes constant world-frame linear velocities for both robots [PAGE 8]; if the opponent accelerates or if wind adds an unmodelled disturbance, the innovation sequence will be biased and the covariance \(R\) or \(Q\) no longer describes the residual. Probability units matter: \(R\) is a measurement covariance in square metres, \(Q\) is a process covariance with mixed position-velocity units, and the Gaussian densities are defined over the corresponding state space.

Biomechanical veto: the source contains no biomechanical state variable. It mentions that “human racers need years of training” [PAGE 1], but the model is a quadrotor with holonomic translational dynamics, not a human body. Therefore any biomechanical veto applied to this paper is extra-source and should be rejected unless a human-pilot model is explicitly added. The nearest physical substitutes are rotor thrust authority, airframe radius, and camera mounting geometry, not muscle or joint dynamics. Weather veto: the source does not include wind, drag, or aerodynamic disturbance in eq. (1) or in the Kalman model eq. (19). The filter’s “constant world-frame linear velocities” assumption [PAGE 8] is a direct weather-vulnerability. A crosswind that exceeds the available control authority or that invalidates constant-velocity prediction vetoes the plan; the paper offers no wind-robustness proof. The correct adjudication order is therefore: first check physical feasibility against \(d_i\), \(w_\tau\), and \(\bar v_i\); second check information feasibility against \(\cos\alpha\), camera frame rate, and estimator covariance; third check unmodelled disturbance against weather and acceleration; and only then evaluate the Nash payoff. If the first two layers veto, the game-theoretic layer’s equilibrium claim is irrelevant.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1: \(\alpha=0\) ablation. In eq. (15) and eq. (17), setting \(\alpha=0\) removes the sensitivity term \(\alpha\mu_j^l\partial\gamma_j/\partial\theta_i\). The paper states that if no collision-avoidance constraints are active, \(\mu_j^{k,l}=0\), and the modified problem reduces to the original optimal-control problem [PAGE 6]. Prediction: with \(\alpha=0\), GTP’s active blocking and defensive sideways motion should decrease toward the MPC-like baseline, especially in the “slow GTP versus fast MPC” case where the paper reports that GTP defends “for the vast majority of the races” [PAGE 9]. Verifiable test: rerun the 150 sampled initial conditions from [PAGE 8] with \(\alpha=0\), same track, same speed limits, and same minimum distance; measure final arch-length difference and lateral deviation from the center line. Falsifier: if the defence frequency and lateral blocking traces are unchanged, the sensitivity term is not the operative cause in that regime.

Veto scenario 2: curvature-singularity stress. Place the terminal predicted position near the denominator zero \(1-\kappa(p_i^N-\tau)^T n=0\) in eq. (18), or tighten the curvature and widen the track so the robot can approach the center of curvature while still satisfying the track-width constraint. Prediction: \(\sigma(p_i^N)\) grows without bound, the QCLP cost linearisation becomes ill-conditioned, and the planner either fails to find a feasible 20 Hz solution or produces a terminal arc-length update that violates the track constraint. Verifiable test: log the denominator, the condition number of the QCLP, the solve time against the 50 ms real-time budget, and the number of infeasible solves while sweeping curvature and lateral offset. Falsifier: if the denominator approaches zero but the planner remains numerically stable and constraint-feasible, the singularity is not reached in that parameter range.

Veto scenario 3: field-of-view and estimator starvation. Reduce the camera half-field-of-view \(\alpha\) or disable the yaw-alignment controller so that \(\beta_{ij}^T e_1<\cos\alpha\) occurs during overtaking or blocking. Prediction: measurement eq. (20) becomes unavailable, the Kalman filter coasts on the constant-velocity model eq. (19), relative-position error grows, and the collision constraint \(\|p_i-p_j\|\ge d_i\) is more likely to be violated because the planner is acting on a stale opponent state. Verifiable test: use the hardware camera parameters from [PAGE 10], record bearing angle, measurement availability, filter covariance trace, and minimum inter-robot distance; compare a full-FOV run with a reduced-FOV run. Falsifier: if the estimator error and minimum distance are statistically indistinguishable between the two runs, the visibility layer is not the limiting factor in that trajectory set.
