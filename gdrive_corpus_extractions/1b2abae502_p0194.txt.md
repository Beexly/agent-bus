<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Stimulus generalization traces back to early experimental psychology. Pavlov (1927) demonstrated dogs salivating to tones similar to conditioned tone. Tolman (1932) introduced cognitive maps, suggesting rats navigated based on relational knowledge rather than rigid stimulus-response bonds. Skinner (1938) incorporated generalization into operant conditioning, noting pigeons pecked keys of varying colors after being trained on one color.

Hull (1943) provided the first quantitative treatment, fitting exponential decay curves to GSR data. His work influenced later theorists: Rescorla-Wagner (1972) added attentional mechanisms, where surprise reduces association strength, potentially altering gradient shape. Pearce (1987) proposed elemental models where each stimulus element contributes independently, allowing for asymmetrical gradients through configural cues.

Modern machine learning inherited these ideas. Kernel methods explicitly define similarity functions akin to generalization gradients. Support Vector Machines use radial basis function kernels $K(x,x')=e^{-\gamma \|x-x'\|^2}$ directly mirroring Hull’s decay function. Deep neural networks implicitly learn such kernels through representation learning (Jacot et al., 2018 Neural Tangent Kernel).

However, there are **multi-week traps**. Researchers often conflate two types of generalization: *stimulus* generalization (responding to similar inputs) versus *response* generalization (producing varied outputs). Also, many assume generalization is purely automatic, ignoring top-down modulation by goals or expectations. Another trap is treating all gradients as symmetric—ecological validity suggests asymmetries should exist (e.g., brightness increases matter more than decreases for detecting predators).

Furthermore, historical accounts sometimes oversimplify by attributing discovery solely to individual scientists, overlooking collaborative networks and technological advances enabling precise measurement (like electrometers for GSR). The genealogy reveals not just theoretical progress but also methodological innovations shaping what phenomena became visible for study.

Understanding this lineage prevents presentist errors and highlights persistent challenges: integrating multiple scales (neural, behavioral, environmental), handling non-linear interactions, and distinguishing between proximate mechanisms and ultimate evolutionary causes. Only by appreciating both continuity and rupture can we build truly cumulative science of learning.

Over time, this creates competing associations that weaken original gradient slopes.

A crucial factor is the **internal clock**—our sense of elapsed time affects generalization dynamics. Memories consolidate differently depending on recency. Recently acquired associations show broader gradients that narrow with extinction training. Moreover, anticipation builds over time; waiting for a feared event heightens arousal and amplifies responding to ambiguous cues.

Recursive loops arise when individuals reflect on their own thought patterns: “Why am I afraid?” → introspection → hypothesis generation → testing against internal evidence → updating beliefs. Such metacognition enables adaptation beyond pure associationism. However, excessive rumination can reinforce dysfunctional gradients, creating vicious cycles typical of anxiety disorders.

Therapeutic interventions must therefore target not only surface-level behaviors but also deeper narrative structures sustaining irrational fears. Narrative exposure therapy encourages retelling traumatic events until emotional impact diminishes—a form of spaced repetition optimizing memory reconsolidation windows. Meanwhile mindfulness practices cultivate non-judgmental awareness, disrupting habitual identification with arising thoughts/feelings.

Ultimately, human cognition operates as a multi-agent system balancing efficiency demands (relying on gradients) against flexibility needs (updating representations). Mastery comes from developing meta-skills to monitor and adjust this balance appropriately according to context.
