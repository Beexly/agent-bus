**Multi-week trap formalization.** The corpus spans 17 weeks. If officiating crews differ in their latent threshold for "significantly hinders an eligible player's opportunity to catch the ball" [PAGE 1], then the label is not a function of the play alone but of the play crossed with the crew-week. Under that model the Bayes error is inflated by the between-crew variance, and any random week-split validation will *understate* the generalization gap because both train and validation share the same crew mixture. The trap is silent: a model validated on weeks 1–10 and tested on 11–17 can appear stable while having learned crew-invariant noise. The three architectures' common regression to test recall 0.884 (Table III [PAGE 4]) is consistent with a shared ceiling that a week-stratified split would expose more sharply. A second, slower trap is rule-emphasis drift across seasons: the DIRECT proposal exists precisely because the standard is unstable, so a model trained on 2018 may not transfer to a later season even with more data.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

**Crisis habit regression.** A DPI is not a random act; it is a stress-response attractor. When a cornerback is beaten at the break, the trained counter — turn, locate the ball, play it — requires a head and hip rotation that costs 200–400 ms. Under time compression the defender regresses to the habitual motor program: extend, grab, restrict the receiver's arm swing. The paper's own play narrative encodes this: "Ball arrives and DPI is committed" [PAGE 2, Fig. 1c] — the foul is the terminal state of a losing race, not an independent decision. The tracking corpus captures the *precursor* (closing rate, hip separation angle) but not the *act*, because the act is sub-Nyquist (E6).

**Counter-trap.** The label is co-produced. The receiver's stem and late push-off induce the defender's grab; the same physical contact can be legal or illegal depending on who initiated and whether the defender was playing the ball. The paper's rule quotation — an act that "significantly hinders an eligible player's opportunity to catch the ball" [PAGE 1] — is inherently relational: it is a property of the *pair trajectory*, not of either agent. This is why the feature engineering collapses attacker, defender and ball into one row [PAGE 3]: the authors correctly identify that the unit of analysis is the interaction. But the tracking channels (speed, acceleration, orientation, direction — one vector per agent) carry no term for *intent*, and the counter-trap is an intent variable.

**Clock.** Three clocks run simultaneously. (i) The rule clock: DPI "applies from the time the ball is thrown until the ball is touched" [PAGE 1] — before the throw, contact is legal; after the touch, it is not interference. (ii) The ball-flight clock: time-to-arrival \(T_{\text{arr}}\) sets whether the defender can arrive in phase. (iii) The sampling clock: 10 Hz. Only the third is observed precisely. The first is recoverable from the "pass forward" event flag; the second is only weakly encoded — the paper retains "pass arrived" as a binary static variable [PAGE 3], discarding the continuous timing that would make \(T_{\text{arr}}\) a feature. That is a concrete, fixable representational loss.

**The recursive loop.** Full theory of mind would require the defender modeling the receiver modeling the defender. The corpus captures one round of this: position and velocity of both agents, sufficient to infer the receiver's break and the defender's commit. It captures zero rounds of the intentional layer — whether the defender's hand was going for the ball or the man. The authors state the resulting ceiling plainly: "there is just not enough information to determine whether a foul was committed" [PAGE 1]. Framed recursively, the model has the physical state but not the belief state, and DPI is a belief-state predicate.

</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

**Physical/geometric veto.** The field is a bounded domain: 91.44 m × 48.8 m [PAGE 1]. Near the sideline, the receiver's usable release space is truncated, the defender can use the boundary as an extra defender, and the geometric prior on contact changes discontinuously. The paper includes "out of bounds" as a binary event [PAGE 3] but does not encode distance-to-sideline as a continuous covariate. **Verdict:** the corpus *permits* a geometrical interaction test but the published feature set *vetoes* it — any claim about boundary effects is unidentifiable from the reported model.

**Biomechanical veto.** The channels are "acceleration, speed, orientation and direction information" [PAGE 3]. Human deceleration is bounded; a defender at full sprint cannot reduce closing speed fast enough to avoid contact once the receiver breaks. This creates a hard physical regime: above a critical approach velocity, contact is biomechanically unavoidable and DPI risk is near-deterministic conditional on the geometry. **Verdict:** the layer is present and informative, but the decisive variable — the defender's *head orientation relative to the ball vector* (i.e., whether he is "playing the ball") — is sampled at 10 Hz while the head-turn occurs in under 200 ms, so the biomechanical veto is under-resolved by roughly a factor of two in the Nyquist sense (E6). The claim "no information when players are close" [PAGE 4] should therefore be restated as "no *resolved* information in the band \(f\le5\) Hz."

**Weather veto.** The paper reports no meteorological covariate anywhere in the five pages — no temperature, wind, precipitation, or surface variable appears in the data-acquisition section, the processing section, or the feature list [PAGES 1–3]. **Verdict:** weather is an *absent layer*, not a controlled one. Because the coefficient on weather cannot be estimated from these data, any inference that conditions the DPI probability on weather is unsupported by this corpus. Two consequences follow. First, the 17-week 2018 season mixes indoor and outdoor venues without a stratifier, so residual venue/climate variance is absorbed into the play-level random effect and inflates the apparent irreducible error. Second, a future replication must add the covariate, not merely more seasons — otherwise the added data inherit the same confounding.

**Adjudication rule applied.** A cross-layer veto is upheld only if the layer is (a) physically capable of generating the outcome, (b) represented in the feature set, and (c) resolved above the sampling floor. Geometry passes (a) but fails (b) as a continuous covariate; biomechanics passes (a) and (b) but fails (c); weather passes (a) but fails (b) entirely. Hence none of the three layers can be adjudicated as a *cause* of model failure from this paper — only biomechanical under-resolution can, and only as a sufficient contributor, not a proven one.

</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
