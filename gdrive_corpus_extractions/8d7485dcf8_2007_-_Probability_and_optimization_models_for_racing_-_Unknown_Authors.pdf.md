<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a 22-page symposium deck, not a live-race dataset and not a predictive lock. Page 1 names Victor S. Y. Lo, University of British Columbia and Fidelity Investments, with a disclaimer that the work was completed at the University of British Columbia and the University of Hong Kong. The deck has two coupled halves: (1) a favorite-longshot bias model, and (2) ordering-probability and optimal-investment models. The multi-hop derivation is: consensus probabilities \(P_i\) are transformed by a logit-power model into objective probabilities \(\pi_i\); the exponent \(\beta\) is interpreted through risk preference and power utility; those \(\pi_i\) then feed ordering probabilities through running-time distributions; the ordering probabilities then feed an expected-log-wealth allocation rule. Every downstream object inherits bias or misspecification from the upstream object. That is the central audit point.

First hop: the deck defines \(P_i\) as “Bet fraction on horse i, i.e. consensus win probability” [PAGE 4]. The model proposes \( \pi_i = P_i^\beta / \sum_{j=1}^n P_j^\beta \), with \(\beta\) as a risk-preference parameter. Page 6 labels \(\beta =1\) as “risk-neutral” and separates risk-prefer from risk-averse regimes. Page 7 states “US racetracks consistently have a risk-prefer bias with β > 1” [PAGE 7]. But page 9 immediately cautions: “Favorite-longshot bias exists in many US racetracks (but not huge)…” and “but does not exist in some Asian racetracks” [PAGE 9]. So the correct multi-hop conclusion is not “bias is universal”; it is “bias is sample- and market-dependent, and the deck itself flags pool size as a possible moderator.”

Second hop: the deck’s utility interpretation is not a proof of a structural preference. Page 8 says “Expected utility maximizer is indifferent between betting on any horses in a race:” [PAGE 8] and connects the logit exponent to power utility and a Pratt-Arrow absolute-risk-aversion measure. There is a sign-convention gray zone: a positive absolute-risk-aversion measure usually indexes risk aversion, while the deck’s logit label is “risk-prefer” for \(\beta >1\). The safe reading is that \(\beta\) is an empirical curvature parameter, not a direct psychological law. A model can fit \(\beta >1\) without proving that every participant is risk-loving.

Third hop: ordering probabilities require a running-time distribution. Page 10 says “Running time distribution (Ti’s) is key to determine ordering probabilities:” [PAGE 10]. Page 11 says the models assume “all assuming independent running times:” [PAGE 11]. Page 12 adds “Strictly speaking, we only need g(T) ~ Exponential” [PAGE 12]. Page 13 admits “The formulas are complex, as one has to solve (3), a system of integral equations” [PAGE 13]. The gray-zone audit here is severe: the integral equations require identification of location or scale parameters, and the deck later questions whether independence is adequate. Page 15 says “Harville model has a systematic bias in estimating ordering probabilities based on Hong Kong data and Henery model is clearly superior” [PAGE 15], while also reporting that “Stern model with r=4 is better than both Henery and Harville using Japan data!” [PAGE 15]. Thus there is no universal winner. Page 19 confirms: “There appears to be no universal best distribution but Henery (Normal) and Stern (Gamma) are competitive” [PAGE 19].

Fourth hop: optimal investment via expected log wealth. Page 18 says “maximize expected log wealth →growth rate of capital:” [PAGE 18]. This is the Kelly criterion family. But if \(\pi_i\) is biased, the allocation is biased; if ordering probabilities are wrong for exotic combinations, the allocation is wrong; if the running-time model is misspecified, the ordering probabilities are wrong; if correlations are omitted, the independence assumption is wrong. Page 19 concludes “(Fractional) Kelly is promising for optimal betting” [PAGE 19], which implicitly acknowledges that full-Kelly aggressiveness is fragile. The deck is therefore a hierarchy of conditional models, not a single validated predictor.

The most important trap is retroactive overreading: because page 7 reports \(\beta>1\) for several US tracks, an analyst may later treat \(\beta>1\) as a stable law across weeks. But page 7 itself shows p-values and sample sizes varying by track, and page 9 explicitly asks whether pool size matters. Another trap is model-selection overfitting: page 17’s non-constant-correlation table reports “Non-constant correlation (γ only) γ = 0.58 0.06” [PAGE 17] and “Non-constant variance κ = 0.08 0.06” [PAGE 17]; those are suggestive, not decisive. A third trap is treating the first-order Taylor approximation as exact. Page 17 says “First order Taylor series approx employed for complexity” [PAGE 17]. Approximation error is largest in tail orderings and in fields with heterogeneous running times.

No race-specific physical, biomechanical, or weather claim can be validated from this deck. The deck contains distributional abstractions, not track-surface measurements, horse physiology, or weather covariates. Therefore any inference that a particular horse will improve or decline because of weather, fatigue, or biomechanics is outside the source’s evidential layer. The correct posture is to treat physical variables as potential omitted variables that can invalidate independence and parameter stability, not as facts supplied by the paper.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s core operator is a power-logit map from consensus probabilities to objective probabilities:
\[
\pi_i=\frac{P_i^\beta}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_j^\beta},\qquad
\pi_i,P_i\in[0,1],\quad \sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_i=1,\quad \beta\in\mathbb{R}.
\]
Here \(P_i\) is defined as “Bet fraction on horse i, i.e. consensus win probability” [PAGE 4]. The deck says “Propose to use a simple logit model” [PAGE 5]. Units: \(P_i\) and \(\pi_i\) are dimensionless probabilities; \(\beta\) is dimensionless curvature. The singularity regimes are exact: if all \(P_i=0\), the map is undefined; if any \(P_i=0\) and \(\beta<0\), \(P_i^\beta\) diverges; if \(\beta=0\), the map collapses to the uniform distribution \(\pi_i=1/n\); if \(\beta\) is large, small-probability states underflow numerically and the denominator dominates. The deck labels \(\beta =1\) “risk-neutral” [PAGE 6] and reports “US racetracks consistently have a risk-prefer bias with β > 1” [PAGE 7], but the heterogeneity caution on page 9 means \(\beta\) must be estimated per market and checked for stability.

The utility bridge is:
\[
U'(x)=x^{-\beta},\qquad
A(x)=-\frac{U''(x)}{U'(x)}=\frac{\beta}{x}.
\]
The deck frames this under “Power utility” and “Measure of Absolute Risk Aversion” [PAGE 8]. Units: \(U\) is dimensionless utility; \(x\) is wealth in currency units; \(A(x)\) has inverse-currency units; \(\beta\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: \(x=0\) is singular for \(\beta\ge 1\); the sign convention is a gray zone because positive \(A\) conventionally indicates risk aversion, while the deck’s logit label calls \(\beta>1\) risk-prefer. The defensible interpretation is empirical curvature, not a universal psychological sign. The deck also states “Expected utility maximizer is indifferent between betting on any horses in a race:” [PAGE 8], which is a conditional equilibrium statement, not a validated law.

Ordering probabilities are defined through independent running times. The generic first-place probability is:
\[
P\!\left(T_i=\min_{j\ne i}T_j\right)
=
\int_0^\infty f_i(t;\theta_i)\prod_{j\ne i}\left[1-F_j(t;\theta_j)\right]dt.
\]
The deck says “Running time distribution (Ti’s) is key to determine ordering probabilities:” [PAGE 10] and assumes “all assuming independent running times:” [PAGE 11]. Units: \(T_i\) is seconds; \(f_i\) is \(\mathrm{s}^{-1}\); \(F_j\) is dimensionless; the integral result is dimensionless probability. To estimate \(\theta_i\), one solves the system of integral equations; page 13 warns “The formulas are complex, as one has to solve (3), a system of integral equations” [PAGE 13]. Singularities: common shifts in all \(\theta_i\) are non-identifiable unless a location anchor is fixed; bounded-support distributions create zero-probability regions; discrete or tied times violate the continuous-density derivation; dependent times violate the product term.

For exponential running times, the Harville-style ordering operator is:
\[
P_{ij}=\frac{\pi_i\pi_j}{1-\pi_i},\qquad
P_{ijk}=\frac{\pi_i\pi_j\pi_k}{(1-\pi_i)(1-\pi_i-\pi_j)}.
\]
These are dimensionless probabilities. Breakdown occurs when \(1-\pi_i=0\) or \(1-\pi_i-\pi_j=0\), i.e., when the remaining field has zero mass. Page 12 says “Strictly speaking, we only need g(T) ~ Exponential” [PAGE 12], meaning the exponential form is a monotone-transform approximation, not a literal claim about raw stopwatch times. The deck also reports model competition: “Harville model has a systematic bias in estimating ordering probabilities based on Hong Kong data and Henery model is clearly superior” [PAGE 15], but “Stern model with r=4 is better than both Henery and Harville using Japan data!” [PAGE 15]. Page 19 concludes “There appears to be no universal best distribution but Henery (Normal) and Stern (Gamma) are competitive” [PAGE 19].

The correlation extension weakens independence. Page 16 treats constant and non-constant correlation cases; page 17 reports “Non-constant correlation (γ only) γ = 0.58 0.06” [PAGE 17] and “Non-constant variance κ = 0.08 0.06” [PAGE 17]. Units: \(\gamma\) and \(\kappa\) are dimensionless covariance-structure parameters. Breakdown: if correlation is strong, the independent-product integrals are invalid; if variance depends on field strength, the location parameters are confounded with scale; first-order Taylor approximations can fail for tail orderings. Page 17 explicitly says “First order Taylor series approx employed for complexity” [PAGE 17].

The investment operator is expected-log-wealth maximization:
\[
X^*=\arg\max_{X}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log W_T\right],
\quad X_i\ge 0,\quad \sum_i X_i\le 1,
\]
with \(W_T\) terminal wealth. The deck says “maximize expected log wealth →growth rate of capital:” [PAGE 18]. Units: \(\log W_T\) is in nats when wealth is normalized to a unit base; the growth rate is nats per race; \(X_i\) are dimensionless fractions. Singularity: if any outcome with positive probability yields \(W_T=0\), then \(\mathbb{E}[\log W_T]=-\infty\); full-Kelly can produce severe drawdowns, which is why page 19 calls “(Fractional) Kelly is promising for optimal betting” [PAGE 19]. The source also cites long-run asymptotic optimality from Breiman, Thorp, and Algoet-Cover [PAGE 18], but that optimality is conditional on correct probability and payoff models.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a chain of model corrections, not a single discovery. Page 20 anchors the bias literature with “Ali, M.M. (1977)” and the economic framing of risk preference. Page 21 anchors the ordering literature with “Harville, D.A. (1973)” and “Henery, R.J. (1981)”. Page 22 anchors the growth-optimal investment lineage with “Breiman, L. (1960)” and Thorp’s Kelly work. The retrocausal pattern is: early models assumed simple probability transforms; later work found systematic ordering bias; then correlated-running-time and approximation models appeared; finally those probability models were plugged into expected-log-wealth allocation. The deck’s own citations show that the “best” model depends on the dataset: Hong Kong favored Henery over Harville [PAGE 15], while Japan favored Stern with \(r=4\) over both [PAGE 15]. This is a multi-week trap: a model selected on one meet or one jurisdiction can regress toward a different model when the field composition, pool size, or correlation structure changes. Page 9’s phrase “would it depend on the Pool Size?” [PAGE 9] is the explicit warning against universalizing \(\beta\). A second multi-week trap is approximation drift: first-order Taylor approximations can look adequate in average orderings but fail on rare permutations. A third is overfitting the correlation table: the reported \(p\)-values near 0.06 [PAGE 17] are suggestive, not decisive, and repeated weekly refitting can turn noise into a narrative.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three recursive failure modes dominate. First, crisis habit regression: under uncertainty, the analyst collapses to a single remembered rule, e.g., “favorites are underbet and longshots are overbet” from page 3, ignoring page 9’s qualification that the pattern is “(but not huge)” and absent in some Asian racetracks [PAGE 9]. Second, counter-trap overcorrection: after seeing heterogeneity, the analyst flips to “no bias exists anywhere,” which contradicts page 7’s US evidence and page 9’s market-specific caveat. Third, clock/identifiability failure: the analyst forgets that \(\beta\), running-time parameters, and correlation parameters are estimated on finite samples with different clocks. Page 13’s warning about solving integral equations [PAGE 13] and page 17’s approximation note [PAGE 17] mean that a weekly refit can move parameters for numerical reasons, not because the underlying process changed. The correct recursive stance is to model the analyst’s own fallback habits: do not promote a sample-specific \(\beta\) to a universal law; do not dismiss the bias entirely; and do not treat a first-order approximation as a clock-free truth. The deck’s own structure—bias, ordering, then allocation—implies that any self-audit must propagate uncertainty upward from \(\pi_i\) to ordering probabilities to allocation, not just check the final result.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather claims are vetoed unless the source contains explicit covariates or identifiable parameters for them. It does not. The source’s running-time layer is distributional: page 10 says the running-time distribution is “key to determine ordering probabilities” [PAGE 10], and page 11 says the models assume “all assuming independent running times” [PAGE 11]. Weather or track-surface effects would enter as common shocks to \(T_i\), violating the product structure and inducing correlation; biomechanical fatigue would enter as nonstationary or non-Gamma tail behavior; physical interactions between horses would enter as dependence beyond the constant-correlation and non-constant-correlation cases mentioned on page 16. The deck’s correlation table reports only “Non-constant correlation (γ only) γ = 0.58 0.06” and “Non-constant variance κ = 0.08 0.06” [PAGE 17], not weather or physiology. Therefore a race-specific claim such as “horse A will improve because of weather” has zero likelihood support in this source. Veto proof: if the model is \(T_i\sim F_i(\theta_i)\) with fixed \(\theta_i\), then a weather variable \(W\) that is not in the likelihood cannot update \(\theta_i\); if \(W\) is added as a common scale, only relative parameters are identified, so absolute speed claims remain untestable; if \(W\) changes correlation, the independent-product integrals on page 11 are invalid. The only defensible cross-layer statement is conditional: weather or biomechanics could explain why Henery and Stern disagree across datasets, but this deck does not test that explanation.
