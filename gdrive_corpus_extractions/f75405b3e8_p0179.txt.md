\[
H(t) = H_0 \exp\left(-\frac{t}{\tau}\right) + H_r,
\tag{1}
\]

where \(H_0\) is the initial habit strength when perfect contiguity (\(t = 0\)), \(\tau\) is the characteristic time constant for decay, and \(H_r\) is the residual habit strength attainable if contiguity were indefinitely prolonged. \(H_0\) and \(H_r\) are expressed in percent (\%).

For experimental data in Figure 39, fitting yields \(H_0 \approx 6.5\%\), \(H_r \approx 6.5\%\), and \(\tau \approx 0.2\) s, consistent with physiological constraints on neurotransmission and synaptic delay.

\[
\Delta S_{tot} = \Delta S_{sys} + \Delta S_{env} \ge 0,
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\Delta S_{sys}\) is the entropy change of the neural system and \(\Delta S_{env}\) the environmental contribution. Minimization of \(\Delta S_{tot}\) occurs when the sensory prediction error \(\varepsilon(t) = |V_{expected}(t) - V_{actual}|\) is minimized. For a given US-evoked potential \(V_{US}\), assuming a Gaussian prior \(N(V_{US}, \sigma^2)\) over predicted values,

\[
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon(t)] = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \cdot \text{std}(V_{actual}) \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\tag{3}
\]

where \(\sigma\) captures uncertainty in sensory processing. Hence, \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]\) exhibits a Gaussian peak at \(t = 0\) and monotonic decrease for \(t > 0\); this form closely matches the exponential decay of \(H(t)\) observed experimentally.

Additionally, consider a topological interpretation using Morse theory. Define a Morse function \(f: M \to \mathbb{R}\) on the neural manifold \(M\) whose critical points correspond to stationary states of synaptic weights. The index of a critical point equals the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix \(\text{Hess}(f)\). For short \(t\), the gradient flow \(\dot{x} = -\nabla f\) exhibits a single sink (global attractor) representing converged synaptic efficacy. For long \(t\), additional saddles emerge, corresponding to alternative local optima, destabilizing the trajectory. The Morse polynomial \(P_f(t) = \sum_k m_k t^k\), where \(m_k\) counts critical points of index \(k\), therefore changes as \(t\) varies, reflecting a topological phase transition in the learning landscape. Explicitly,

\[
\chi(M_t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} (-1)^k m_k(t)
\tag{4}
\]

evaluates the Euler characteristic, which must equal a constant independent of \(t\) for smooth families of functions. Thus, as \(t\) increases, new index-1 critical points (saddles) must appear to balance lost sinks, explaining the observed plateau in \(H(t)\) for large \(t\).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The Wolfle experiments did not exist in a vacuum. They crystallize a pre-existing intellectual tradition stretching back through Külpe's formal Psychology (1893) and Wundt's Elements of Physiological Psychology (1874), which conceptualized mental acts as temporary, dynamic configurations susceptible to decay and reformation. Pavlov's own conditioning research (1927) had already revealed that timing profoundly influences acquisition rate, though he attributed this primarily to response fatigue and inhibition rather than memory-trace disruption. Thorndike (1932) explicitly linked contiguity to habit formation, positing that connection strengths increase proportionally with paired presentations; however, Thorndike worked exclusively within the realm of instrumental learning with humans and animals, lacking the precise operational definitions needed for direct quantitative comparison.

The crux of the historical tension lay in reconciling seemingly contradictory findings. Miller and Montague (1955) demonstrated that rats could learn delayed versions of a two-lever pressing task, implying robustity against significant gaps. Yet Bush and Mosteller (1951) showed that for discrete multi-stage decision problems, intermediate delays diminished performance—a paradox resolved only later by introducing concepts of working memory load and processing capacity limits. Thus, the Wolfle study (1940) positioned itself at the hinge of paradigms: it used a classical reflexive paradigm (hand-withdrawal), eschewing complex cognitive tasks, yet deliberately manipulated a medium-length delay range (prolonged relative to simple licking responses but brief enough to avoid total forgetting). 

Retrospectively, the concentration on short delays (including zero) reflects a broader zeitgeist of the 1930s. It mirrored advances in electronic engineering and communication theory, where signal distortion due to latency was being solved via amplifier design and filtering techniques. Wolterstorff’s treatment of information flow (1938) influenced both cognitive psychologists and physicists studying brain function. Consequently, the exponential decay model wasn't merely derived from scratch; it was steeped in precedents from electrical circuit analysis, notably Kirchhoff's RC circuits, which exhibit exactly this type of voltage relaxation across a capacitor. McCulloch and Pitts' foundational logical-calculus paper (1943) further popularized the idea that neural networks could mimic such relaxation dynamics.

Crucially, the multi-week controversy in May-August 1940 among U.S. continental psychometricians didn't undermine the core finding of Wolfle. Instead, it created a climate of methodological suspicion that inadvertently stimulated replication efforts. The Chicago school led by Lorge actively sought to falsify earlier claims of strong temporal effects, conducting their own experiments on word-pair association with much longer retention intervals (weeks). Their failure to replicate rapid decay at day-long delays actually bolstered Wolfle's localized observation about millisecond-second scales, revealing that the phenomenon was scale-dependent: fast-decaying processes dominate neurophysiologically, whereas slower cultural or linguistic memories endure longer.

A common trap today is the assumption that all forms of memory decay identically, mistaking the rapid fading of primary sensory traces for the resilience of episodic or semantic memories. Another trap stems from mechanistic reductionism: treating the exponential fit as an ontological law rather than an emergent property valid within specific parameter ranges, ignoring potential higher-order corrections for extremely short (\(\sim\) microseconds) or extremely long (\(\sim\) years) delays.
