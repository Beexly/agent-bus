<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s core multi-hop argument is not merely “Elo beats UEFA coefficient.” The first hop is structural: the UEFA club coefficient is defined over European competitions and ignores domestic league and cup results, while the Football Club Elo Rating is continuously updated from a broader match stream. The source states that the coefficient “ignores most matches played by the clubs since it does not depend on the outcomes of matches in the national leagues and cups at all” [PAGE 2]. The second hop is that a broader information set should improve prediction only if domestic matches contain signal about Champions League performance; the paper tests this through logistic regressions on 19 seasons, 2003/04–2021/22 [PAGE 2, PAGE 5]. The third hop is model comparison: the paper uses pseudo-\(R^2\), classification accuracy at a 0.5 cut, and area under the ROC curve, not just parameter significance [PAGE 6–7]. The fourth hop is robustness: group matches, knockout qualification, group ranking, draws included or excluded, and split samples all point the same way, with the partial exception that the UEFA coefficient retains some additional significance for group ranking when the dependent variable is defined by the higher UEFA coefficient [PAGE 8–9].

The gray-zone audit matters because the paper is a reduced-form predictive comparison, not a causal proof that changing the coefficient would produce fairer schedules. The Elo ratings are frozen at 30 June each year to match the season-start UEFA coefficient, because “The Elo ratings are continuously updated once a match is played by the teams. On the other hand, the UEFA club coefficient is calculated at the beginning of each season and does not change during the season” [PAGE 4]. That is a good comparability choice, but it leaves a clock problem: transfers, injuries, managerial changes, and preseason form after 30 June are omitted. The paper also notes COVID-19 disruption: “Almost all matches were played behind closed doors in the 2020/21 season, which could have affected home advantage” [PAGE 5], and the 2019/20 single-leg neutral-field matches are removed from the knockout sample [PAGE 5]. Those are not minor details; they are cross-layer variables that can veto a pure rating story.

Another gray zone is scale. The paper explicitly warns that “The scale of the two measures is not the same, the difference between the Elo ratings is about three-four times higher than the difference between the club coefficients” [PAGE 6]. Therefore, one must not compare the raw logistic coefficients \(0.019\) for UEFA and \(0.007\) for Elo as if they were on the same unit scale; they are log-odds per UEFA coefficient point and log-odds per Elo point, respectively. The correct comparison is fit, AUC, classification, and out-of-sample behavior. The paper follows that logic and does not standardize the variables because it focuses “primarily on comparing the performance of the models rather than the interpretation of the estimated parameters” [PAGE 6]. That is defensible for prediction, but it means the coefficients are not directly interpretable as effect sizes across systems.

A further audit point is the printed Elo formula. The source gives \(W=1/(1+10^{\Delta/s})\) [PAGE 4]. If \(\Delta\) is literally “the difference between the Elo rating of the home and the away team” and one defines \(\Delta=E_{\mathrm{home}}-E_{\mathrm{away}}\), the printed expression is decreasing in home strength, which is the opposite of the intended monotonicity. The standard monotone form requires either \(\Delta=E_{\mathrm{away}}-E_{\mathrm{home}}\) or a minus sign in the exponent. This is a sign-convention trap, not a reason to reject the paper’s empirical results, because the fitted signs in Tables 4–8 are positive for Elo and the AUC values exceed 0.5. But any implementation must audit the sign before using the formula.

The paper’s strongest evidence is not a single table. It is the convergence across outcomes. In Table 3, the simple “higher rating wins” rule gives 70.68% versus 73.32% for group matches, 61.92% versus 65.00% for knockout qualification, and 75.22% versus 78.51% for group ranking, with Elo higher in every case [PAGE 7]. In Table 4, for group matches, model (1) with UEFA has AUC 0.784, model (2) with Elo has 0.814, and model (3) with both has 0.815; Elo is significant while the club coefficient “has no additional value here” [PAGE 7]. In Table 5, for knockout qualification, AUC rises from 0.617 to 0.690 to 0.693, and the paper concludes that “using the club coefficients is not able to improve the predictions based on Elo ratings” [PAGE 8]. In Table 6, for group ranking, Elo again dominates, but the UEFA coefficient retains a significant coefficient when both are included [PAGE 8–9]. That exception is important: it suggests the coefficient may proxy European experience or squad familiarity with European competition, as the paper notes: “better performance in previous European competitions can provide some experience for the squads that cannot be obtained by playing against domestic teams” [PAGE 9].

The final gray-zone question is whether the result is “Elo is better” or “the particular Football Club Elo Ratings freeze is better.” The paper uses one Elo variant, with \(k=400\), \(K=20\), home advantage, and margin of victory [PAGE 4]. It does not claim that this is the unique best predictor; it says the Elo algorithm “has many variants” and that future work could weight European games differently [PAGE 4, PAGE 14]. Thus the defensible conclusion is narrower: under the tested freeze-date protocol and the tested variants, the Elo-based measure has higher explanatory and predictive power than the official UEFA club coefficient. A different Elo specification, a different freeze date, or a different handling of draws could change the magnitude, though the paper’s robustness tables make a complete reversal unlikely.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(E_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}\) denote an Elo rating in Elo points for team \(i\) at freeze time \(t\), and let \(C_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) denote a UEFA club coefficient in UEFA coefficient points. For a match with home team \(h\) and away team \(a\), define
\[
\Delta E_i = E_h(t)-E_a(t), \qquad \Delta C_i = C_h(t)-C_a(t).
\]
The units are Elo points for \(\Delta E_i\) and UEFA coefficient points for \(\Delta C_i\). These are not directly comparable; the paper states that “the difference between the Elo ratings is about three-four times higher than the difference between the club coefficients” [PAGE 6].

The paper’s Elo expected-score formula is
\[
W_i=\frac{1}{1+10^{\Delta_i/s}},
\]
where \(s>0\) is a scaling parameter in Elo points and \(W_i\in(0,1)\) is dimensionless [PAGE 4]. With the paper’s printed sign convention, monotonicity in home strength requires \(\Delta_i\) to be interpreted as opponent-minus-reference or the exponent to carry a negative sign; otherwise the printed expression decreases with the home team’s advantage. The update is
\[
E_i^{+}=E_i^{-}+K(R_i-W_i),
\]
where \(E_i^{\pm}\) are Elo points, \(K>0\) is an Elo-point update weight, \(R_i\in\{0,0.5,1\}\) is the dimensionless result, and \(W_i\) is the dimensionless expected score [PAGE 4]. The update is bounded by \(|E_i^{+}-E_i^{-}|\le K\). The Football Club Elo Ratings variant used in the paper “applies the values k = 400 and K = 20, and accounts for home advantage and the margin of victory” [PAGE 4].

The logistic comparison uses a binary outcome \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) and
\[
p_i=\Pr(Y_i=1\mid \Delta_i)=\sigma(\beta_0+\beta_1\Delta_i),\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]
Here \(\beta_0\) is in log-odds and \(\beta_1\) is in log-odds per rating point: per Elo point for \(\Delta E_i\), per UEFA coefficient point for \(\Delta C_i\). The paper estimates these models by maximum likelihood for group matches, knockout qualification, and group ranking [PAGE 5–8]. Because the rating units differ, \(\beta_1^{\mathrm{Elo}}\) and \(\beta_1^{\mathrm{UEFA}}\) are not directly comparable; the paper compares pseudo-\(R^2\), classification at cut 0.5, and area under the ROC curve instead [PAGE 6–7].

The paper reports three pseudo-\(R^2\) measures. With \(L_0\) the likelihood without predictors, \(L_M\) the fitted-model likelihood, and \(n\) observations,
\[
R^2_{\mathrm{CoxSnell}}=1-\left(\frac{L_0}{L_M}\right)^{2/n},
\]
with upper bound
\[
1-L_0^{2/n}=1-\left[p^p(1-p)^{1-p}\right]^2,
\]
where \(p\) is the sample event proportion [PAGE 6]. Nagelkerke’s measure divides by that upper bound:
\[
R^2_{\mathrm{Nagelkerke}}=
\frac{R^2_{\mathrm{CoxSnell}}}{1-L_0^{2/n}}.
\]
McFadden’s measure is
\[
R^2_{\mathrm{McFadden}}=1-\frac{\ln L_M}{\ln L_0}.
\]
Likelihoods are dimensionless; natural-log likelihoods are in nats. The AUC is dimensionless, with 0.5 random and 1 perfect [PAGE 6–7].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes:
\begin{itemize}
\item Elo formula: \(s=0\) is a singularity. If \(s<0\), the monotonicity reverses relative to the intended positive scale. If \(K=0\), ratings never update; if \(K<0\), a win lowers the rating, which is perverse. For very large \(|\Delta/s|\), numerical overflow can occur unless the exponent is clipped or computed in log-space. Extreme \(\Delta\) drives \(W\to 0\) or \(W\to 1\), so the update saturates.
\item Logistic model: \(\beta_1=0\) gives no discrimination. Complete or quasi-complete separation makes \(|\beta_1|\to\infty\) and the maximum-likelihood estimate unstable. Omitted variables can bias \(\beta_1\) if they affect \(Y\) and correlate with \(\Delta\).
\item Pseudo-\(R^2\): if \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), the upper bound is zero and Nagelkerke’s measure is undefined. If \(L_0=1\), \(\ln L_0=0\), so McFadden’s ratio is undefined. If \(L_M=L_0\), all pseudo-\(R^2\) values are zero.
\item AUC: 0.5 is the random-ranking benchmark; 1 is perfect separation. Tied predicted probabilities require a tie convention; the paper reports standard AUC values, so ties are not a practical breakdown here.
\end{itemize}
The paper’s empirical estimates are finite and have the expected positive signs: for group matches, Table 4 gives Elo coefficient \(0.007\) with AUC 0.814 versus UEFA AUC 0.784 [PAGE 7]; for knockout qualification, Elo AUC 0.690 versus UEFA 0.617 [PAGE 8]; for group ranking, Elo AUC 0.776 versus UEFA 0.704, with both significant in model (3) [PAGE 8]. The paper’s conclusion is therefore “a variant of the Elo method provides a higher accuracy in terms of explanatory power in the Champions League matches” [PAGE 1], not that Elo is universally causal or universally optimal.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage runs from Árpád Élő’s chess rating system to football. The paper states that “The Elo method has been developed by a Hungarian-born American physics, Árpád Élő, as an improved chess rating system” and that it is “now widely used in many sports, including association football, as shown by the FIFA World Ranking since 2018” [PAGE 4]. The UEFA club coefficient has a different genealogy: it is a five-season European-competition points system, with a floor at the association coefficient: “The UEFA club coefficient is either the sum of all points won in the previous five seasons of European competitions ... or the association coefficient over the same period, whichever is the higher” [PAGE 3]. That floor is a structural trap: it mixes club-specific and association-level信息, so a club from a strong association can receive a coefficient boost not earned by its own European results.

The retrocausal chain is therefore: chess Elo → football Elo variants → clubelo.com → FIFA World Ranking reform in 2018 → the paper’s proposal for UEFA club competition seeding. The paper explicitly notes the FIFA precedent: “FIFA has developed and introduced a new model for calculating the FIFA World Ranking based on the Elo method in 2018” [PAGE 14], and UEFA’s use of Guyon’s proposal for Euro 2020 [PAGE 14]. The multi-week traps are temporal. First, the UEFA coefficient is fixed at the start of the season, while Elo is continuously updated; the paper freezes Elo at 30 June to avoid leakage [PAGE 4]. Second, the five-season UEFA window is long and backward-looking, so a club’s current strength can diverge sharply from its coefficient. Third, COVID-19 created a multi-week and multi-season distortion: the 2020/21 season was largely behind closed doors, and the 2019/20 European season was incomplete by 30 June 2020, so “the Elo ratings used for 2020/21 do not contain the results of all matches played in the previous seasons” [PAGE 5]. Fourth, the 2019/20 knockout quarterfinals and semifinals were single-leg neutral-field matches, and the paper removes those six matches from the knockout sample [PAGE 5]. Fifth, pot allocation itself is endogenous: pots are “primarily determined by the ranking of the teams based on their UEFA club coefficients,” with titleholder and association-champion exceptions [PAGE 3]. That means the coefficient is not an exogenous predictor; it shapes the schedule that produces future results.

The 2024/25 reform sharpens the genealogy. The new format has 36 teams in one league, each playing eight matches, with seeding into four pots of nine based on club coefficients [PAGE 12–13]. The paper warns that without an accurate rating, “the league phase has a high probability of becoming unbalanced” [PAGE 13]. Historically, the old group stage had one team from each pot, which reduced the chance that similar-coefficient teams met [PAGE 3]. The new format increases the number of cross-pot matches and therefore increases the cost of a stale or biased coefficient. Retrocausally, the reform creates a deadline: the rating formula must be chosen before the schedule is drawn, and the schedule then locks in the consequences.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive stakeholder problem is that clubs, administrators, fans, and media all anticipate how ratings are used. UEFA’s current coefficient rewards European results and ignores domestic league results [PAGE 2], so clubs have an incentive to prioritize European matches and to treat domestic form as partially invisible to seeding. If UEFA switches to an Elo-based formula that includes domestic matches, the incentive set changes: domestic results become seeding-relevant, and clubs may re-optimize squad rotation, transfer timing, and European-versus-domestic prioritization. That is the recursive loop: the rating formula changes behavior, and changed behavior changes the data that feed the rating.

“Crisis habit regression” appears in the temptation to retreat to the legacy coefficient because it is familiar and easy to explain. The paper acknowledges the trade-off: “the calculation of the ratings should be relatively simple without requiring the estimation of difficult regression models even if they would have theoretically a higher predictive power” [PAGE 2]. During COVID-19, home advantage was disrupted [PAGE 5], which could tempt administrators to conclude that all ratings are unstable and that the old coefficient is safer. That is habit regression: using a known-but-biased instrument because the crisis makes novelty feel risky.

The “counter-trap” is the opposite error: adopting one Elo variant as a final answer without auditing its assumptions. The paper uses Football Club Elo Ratings with \(k=400\), \(K=20\), home advantage, and margin of victory [PAGE 4], but it also says the Elo algorithm “has many variants” and that future work could weight European games more heavily [PAGE 4, PAGE 14]. A counter-trap would be to treat the clubelo.com freeze as a universal truth, ignore the sign convention in the printed formula, or fail to test draw handling and dependent-variable definition. The paper itself shows why this matters: when the group-ranking target is redefined by Elo rather than by the UEFA coefficient, the UEFA coefficient’s additional significance disappears [PAGE 9]. That is a warning against circular target construction.

The “clock” is the season-start freeze and the reform deadline. The paper fixes Elo at 30 June each year because the coefficient is calculated at the beginning of the season [PAGE 4]. The clock creates staleness: injuries, transfers, managerial changes, and preseason form are not in the rating. The new 2024/25 format makes the clock more consequential because the draw depends on pots built from the rating [PAGE 13]. A recursive theory of mind must therefore model not only “what does the rating predict?” but “what do clubs know about when the rating is frozen, and how will they respond before and after the freeze?” The paper’s evidence supports a predictive switch to Elo, but the implementation should be transparent about freeze dates, update rules, and the strategic responses those rules induce.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The paper’s model is a reduced-form predictor, not a physical, biomechanical, or meteorological model. A cross-layer veto is therefore not proven by the source; it is a proof obligation that the source cannot discharge. Formally, let the true conditional logit be
\[
\operatorname{logit}\Pr(Y_i=1\mid \Delta_i,Z_i)
=\beta_0+\beta_1\Delta_i+\gamma^\top Z_i,
\]
where \(Z_i\) collects physical load, biomechanical fatigue, injury status, travel, weather, pitch condition, and venue effects. If the estimated model omits \(Z_i\), the linear-projection bias is approximately
\[
\widehat{\beta}_1\approx
\beta_1+\gamma^\top
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta_i,Z_i)}
{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_i)}.
\]
A cross-layer veto requires (i) \(\gamma\neq 0\), (ii) \(\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta_i,Z_i)\neq 0\), and (iii) the omitted layer differs systematically across the compared rating regimes. The paper provides no such \(Z_i\), so its estimates are predictive associations, not structural causal effects.

The source does contain layer-shift evidence. It notes that “Almost all matches were played behind closed doors in the 2020/21 season, which could have affected home advantage” [PAGE 5]. It removes the 2019/20 single-leg neutral-field knockout matches [PAGE 5]. Those are venue and crowd layers that can veto a home-advantage coefficient. Weather is not modeled; extreme heat, cold, rain, wind, or pitch quality could change the mapping from rating difference to outcome. Biomechanical factors are not modeled; injuries, congestion, recovery time, and squad rotation can make the frozen Elo rating stale. Physical factors such as travel distance and rest days are not modeled. The paper’s own freeze at 30 June [PAGE 4] means that in-season physical and biomechanical changes are invisible.

The adjudication is therefore layered. At the predictive layer, the paper’s evidence is strong: Elo outperforms the coefficient across group matches, knockout qualification, and group ranking [PAGE 7–8]. At the causal layer, the evidence is weaker: a switch to Elo could change club incentives and schedule balance, and the paper does not simulate that equilibrium. At the physical/biomechanical/weather layer, the paper is silent. A valid veto would be a measured \(Z_i\) that is correlated with \(\Delta_i\) and changes the sign or magnitude of the Elo advantage. Absent that, the correct conclusion is conditional: Elo is the better reduced-form predictor under the paper’s protocol, but it is not a complete model of match causation. The paper’s COVID caveat [PAGE 5] is the clearest admission that cross-layer shocks can distort even a well-frozen rating.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
