The paper’s simple difference-in-differences estimator interacts an absence indicator with a post-announcement indicator: “𝐴𝑏𝑠𝑖 is a binary variable =1 if there was a superstar absence for matchup 𝑖, and 𝑃𝑜𝑠𝑡_𝐴𝑛𝑛ℎ is a binary variable =1 if the announcement had already been made at hours-to-game ℎ” [PAGE 13]. The coefficient of interest is “𝛽3, which approximately represents the percentage change in ticket prices associated with a superstar absence” [PAGE 13]. The identifying logic is multi-hop: first, hedonic pricing treats the ticket as a bundle of attributes, including the expected presence of a superstar [PAGE 3]; second, the announcement changes the expected attribute bundle; third, differencing within matchup removes matchup-invariant components and hours-to-game fixed effects remove common time-to-game price trajectories; fourth, the same-team counterfactual controls for team-specific price trends; fifth, the generalized event-study version checks whether treated and counterfactual prices were parallel before the announcement and whether the response appears after the announcement [PAGE 13-14]. The paper reports that the DID design is only interpreted causally for players whose pre-trends are parallel; the text says results “only include players where pre-trends in ticket prices between the counterfactual and treated matchups prior to a superstar’s injury announcement were parallel” [PAGE 16]. It also reports a delayed response: “typically the effects begin to happen 3-6 hours after an announcement is made” [PAGE 19].

The gray-zone audit is extensive. First, listed prices are not necessarily transaction prices or true reservation values; the paper says changes in listed prices “should reflect the combined effect of sellers’ and buyers’ lower value of attending the corresponding matchup” [PAGE 15], but it also notes that non-adjusting sellers may make the estimate a “lower bound” because “40% of sellers have a ‘negative scrap value’” and “20% of sellers value their tickets above the franchise’s face value” [PAGE 4]. Second, the aggregate per-player counterfactual requires random assignment of announcement times from the treated pool, and the paper repeats this only “3 times, each with a different random counterfactual pairing” [PAGE 14]. Third, injury announcements are treated as random, but rest announcements are not: the paper explicitly warns that rest may depend on “the second night of back-to-back games or fourth game in five nights,” opponent competitiveness, and home vs. away status [PAGE 15]. Fourth, the analysis restricts to announcements within 3 days and to the immediately following game: “we only examine the game immediately following the announcement” and “if a player tears their ACL this impacts not just the next game, but all remaining games in the season” [PAGE 14]. Fifth, overlapping superstar absences, missing marketplace data from “server restarts and changes of event-names mid-season” [PAGE 7], and excluded cases such as Joel Embiid’s back-to-backs and LaMarcus Aldridge’s Kawhi Leonard uncertainty [PAGE 11] are boundary conditions. Sixth, the welfare calculation is an extrapolation, not a structural estimate: the Curry example uses “$25 x 20,000 x 82 = $41,000,000” [PAGE 18], assuming constant per-attendee loss, fixed attendance, and 82 games. Seventh, heterogeneity by home/away and market size is descriptive and triple-difference-based; the paper interprets “for every $1 million increase in the market size of the home team, the absence effect for superstar player ‘Y’ changes by XX%” [PAGE 22], with NBA average market size “$1.65 billion” [PAGE 22]. Thus the defensible causal claim is narrow: injury-driven, explicitly announced, single-superstar absences within three days, in games whose treated and same-team counterfactual price paths satisfy parallel pre-trends, and with sufficient post-announcement observation time. The paper’s own conclusion states the main effect range as “7-25% ($9-$25) reduction in the average ticket price” [PAGE 1, PAGE 23]. Outside that box, the estimate becomes an association or a lower bound.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(P_{ish}>0\) be the average listed ticket price in USD for section \(s\), matchup \(i\), and hours-to-game \(h\). Let \(Abs_i\in\{0,1\}\) indicate a superstar absence for matchup \(i\), and let \(PostAnn_h\in\{0,1\}\) indicate that the absence announcement has already occurred by hours-to-game \(h\). The paper’s simple DID specification is, verbatim from [PAGE 13]:

\[
\log(P_{ish})=\beta_1 Abs_i+\beta_2 PostAnn_h+\beta_3\left(Abs_i \times PostAnn_h\right)+\alpha_s+\alpha_h+\varepsilon_{ish}.
\]

The source states: “The DID treatment coefficient is represented by 𝛽3, which approximately represents the percentage change in ticket prices associated with a superstar absence, and is our primary coefficient of interest” [PAGE 13]. Units: \(P_{ish}\) is USD; \(Abs_i\) and \(PostAnn_h\) are binary; \(\alpha_s\) are section fixed effects, “which are matchup-specific as well”; \(\alpha_h\) is an “hours-to-game fixed effect” [PAGE 13]; \(\beta_3\) is in log points. The exact proportional effect is \(100(e^{\beta_3}-1)\%\), while \(100\beta_3\%\) is the small-coefficient approximation used by the paper [PAGE 13, footnote 15].

The generalized event-study specification is, verbatim from [PAGE 13]:

\[
\log(P_{isht})=\sum_{t=-7}^{7} D_t\, Absence_{t,ih}+\alpha_s+\alpha_h+\varepsilon_{isht}.
\]

The paper states: “Event-time 𝑡 is in the hours-to-game unit, but is normalized to 𝑡=0 based on the hours-to-game value when the announcement of a superstar’s absence takes place” [PAGE 13]. It also states: “we restrict our event-time horizon to 𝑡=[−7, 7], where the left (right) binned endpoint coefficient represents the average treatment effects for all pre- (post-) periods” [PAGE 14]. The omitted period is \(t=-1\) [PAGE 14]. Thus the event-study coefficients \(D_t\) are differential log-price gaps between treated and same-team counterfactual games at event-time \(t\), relative to \(t=-1\). The identifying assumption is parallel pre-trends: \(D_t\approx 0\) for \(t=-7,\dots,-2\). The paper verifies this visually in the generalized DID figures and says coefficients “statistically insignificantly different from zero prior to an absence announcement … suggest that parallel pre-trends in ticket prices hold” [PAGE 19].

For aggregate per-player estimation, the paper randomly assigns counterfactual announcement times: “we randomly assign announcement times for all matchups in the counterfactual sampling from the pool of announcement times observed for the treated matchups” [PAGE 14]. It repeats the aggregate pairing “3 times, each with a different random counterfactual pairing” [PAGE 14]. A representative formalization is:

\[
\widehat{AnnTime}^{cf}_{j}\sim \widehat{F}_{AnnTime\mid i},\qquad j=1,\dots,m_i,
\]

where \(\widehat{F}_{AnnTime\mid i}\) is the empirical distribution of observed announcement times for treated matchups of player \(i\), and \(m_i\) is the number of same-team counterfactual matchups assigned to that treated matchup. This is not a structural equation; it is the paper’s resampling operator for aligning counterfactual event-time.

For the market-size heterogeneity test, the paper describes a triple-differences estimator rather than printing its full LaTeX. A representative triple-difference form consistent with the text is:

\[
\log(P_{ish})=\beta_1 Abs_i+\beta_2 PostAnn_h+\beta_3(Abs_i PostAnn_h)
+\beta_4(Abs_i PostAnn_h \times MktSize_{home})+\alpha_s+\alpha_h+\varepsilon_{ish}.
\]

The paper’s interpretation is: “for every $1 million increase in the market size of the home team, the absence effect for superstar player ‘Y’ changes by XX%” [PAGE 22]. \(MktSize_{home}\) is in USD millions; the paper notes the NBA average is “$1.65 billion” [PAGE 22]. A positive \(\beta_4\) means the negative absence effect is smaller in larger home markets; the paper states “a positive triple-diff coefficient indicates that prices respond less negatively to absence announcements in larger markets” [PAGE 22].

The welfare extrapolation is:

\[
\widehat{\Delta W}_{\text{season}}
= -\widehat{\beta}_3 \times \bar P \times A \times G
\quad\text{or exactly}\quad
\widehat{\Delta W}_{\text{season}}
= -\left(1-e^{\widehat{\beta}_3}\right)\bar P\,A\,G,
\]

where \(\bar P\) is average price in USD, \(A\) is attendance per game, and \(G\) is games per season. The paper’s Curry example is: “If each attendee for Golden State Warriors games loses $25 when Stephen Curry is absent, and there are on average 20,000 fans in an NBA arena for these games, and 82 Golden State Warriors games in an NBA season, then the total value of watching Stephen Curry play in person to NBA fans is $25 x 20,000 x 82 = $41,000,000” [PAGE 18]. Assumptions: constant per-attendee loss, fixed attendance, fixed 82-game season, no general-equilibrium price incidence, and no distinction between listed and transaction price. This is explicitly “back-of-the-envelope” [PAGE 18], not a structural welfare theorem.

Singularity/breakdown regimes:
1. \(P_{ish}=0\) or near-zero: \(\log(P)\) is undefined or unstable; the paper uses log-level because “prices cannot fall below zero” [PAGE 13], but zero-price listings break the log transformation.
2. No within-matchup variation in \(PostAnn_h\): if the announcement occurs after the observation window or outside the \([-7,7]\) event horizon, the interaction is unidentified; the paper bins endpoints at \(-7\) and \(+7\) [PAGE 14].
3. Empty event-time bins or perfect collinearity with section/hours fixed effects: the event-study coefficient is not estimable.
4. Simultaneous superstar absences: \(Abs_i\) is no longer player-specific; the paper excludes games where “another superstar was announced as out for that qualifying game as well” [PAGE 11].
5. Long-term injury: if the absence affects many future games, the per-game DID is not identified by a 3-day window; the paper explicitly restricts to announcements within 3 days and the immediately following game [PAGE 14].
6. Rest endogeneity: rest announcements may correlate with back-to-backs, fourth-in-five, opponent, and home/away status, so \(\beta_3\) is not causal for rest without controls [PAGE 15].
7. Pre-trend failure: if \(D_t\neq 0\) pre-announcement, DID is biased; the paper only reports estimates for players satisfying parallel pre-trends [PAGE 16].
8. Market-size extrapolation: triple-difference interpretation is per $1 million, while the observed market-size scale is billions [PAGE 22]; out-of-sample extrapolation is fragile.
9. Sold-listing selection: sold listings are a selected subsample; the paper reports them as a robustness check and says results are “even more suggestive of price declines” [PAGE 20], but selection into sale is endogenous.
10. Missing marketplace observations: “server restarts and changes of event-names mid-season” can induce non-random missingness [PAGE 7].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s estimate is genealogically downstream of two older research lines. The first is hedonic pricing: Rosen’s 1974 model treats a good’s value as the sum of attribute values, and the paper claims to be “the first to look at a hedonic component of event ticket values, namely the marginal contribution of a superstar player to the value of attending an NBA game” [PAGE 3]. The second is superstar economics: Rosen’s 1981 model explains how talented individuals obtain differentially higher salaries, and Adler’s 1985 work distinguishes “popularity” from “skill” [PAGE 4]. The paper then links those lineages to NBA attendance and viewership studies: Hausman and Leonard found “substantial impacts for these players, especially in the case of away games” [PAGE 4]; Berri and Schmidt estimated marginal effects of All-Star votes on away-game attendance [PAGE 4]; Jane found larger attendance effects for “popular players” than “skill players” [PAGE 4]; Humphreys and Johnson found higher attendance at both home and away games when a superstar is present [PAGE 4]. The paper’s novelty is to replace attendance counts with high-frequency secondary-market listing prices and to treat an absence announcement as an information shock. The literature review also places it in dynamic ticket pricing: Courty’s work on ticket resale and demand uncertainty [PAGE 3], Sweeting’s dynamic pricing evidence from secondary markets [PAGE 4], and Clarke’s finding of heterogeneous seller strategies, including “negative scrap value” sellers [PAGE 4].

The retrocausal genealogy matters because later methods can retroactively weaken earlier causal claims. The paper itself proposes synthetic controls as future work: “a synthetic control approach would impose a weighting scheme for counterfactual games that requires pre-treatment prices between the two groups to follow the same trends” [PAGE 16], following Abadie, Diamond, and Hainmueller [PAGE 16]. This is a retrocausal correction: the current same-team DID only “observe[s] in the generalized DID framework whether or not the pre-trends do indeed satisfy the parallel condition” [PAGE 16], whereas synthetic control would force the pre-treatment fit. If synthetic control attenuates the estimates, the genealogical chain would reinterpret the current DID estimates as upper bounds or as selected-by-pre-trend associations rather than clean causal effects.

Multi-week traps are central. First, the paper restricts to a 3-day pre-game window and the immediately following game [PAGE 14]. It explicitly warns that a long-term injury such as a torn ACL “impacts not just the next game, but all remaining games in the season” [PAGE 14]. Therefore a one-game absence effect cannot be multiplied into a multi-week injury effect without a separate model of expectation updating, replacement-player quality, and schedule effects. Second, rest is a multi-week strategic phenomenon: rest may be more likely on “the second night of back-to-back games or fourth game in five nights,” and may depend on opponent competitiveness and home/away status [PAGE 15]. Third, the paper excludes Joel Embiid because “he did not play in the second night of all back-to-back games, and thus those announcements were not plausibly exogenous” [PAGE 11], and excludes LaMarcus Aldridge because of “the season-long uncertainty surrounding Kawhi Leonard’s playing status in San Antonio” [PAGE 11]. These exclusions are not minor; they show that multi-week team-level uncertainty can contaminate the treatment. Fourth, the paper’s future-work agenda flags “the impact of ‘uncertainty’ associated with some players’ timelines in returning from injury or rest on ticket prices for future, potentially impacted games,” citing LeBron James’s “lingering groin injury during the middle of the 2018-19 season, which led to a highly uncertain timetable for his return” [PAGE 24]. Fifth, midseason trades and free-agent moves are another multi-week trap: if a superstar joins or leaves a team, the attribute bundle changes for many future games, not just one announcement [PAGE 24]. Sixth, market-size and home/away heterogeneity are not stable across multi-week windows: the paper finds Curry and Durant have larger away-game absence effects, while Anthony Davis and Kristaps Porzingis have larger home-game effects [PAGE 21], and larger markets attenuate the effect [PAGE 22]. A multi-week extrapolation that ignores schedule composition, home/away mix, and market size will overstate or understate welfare.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The marketplace is a recursive game of seller and buyer beliefs. At level 0, a seller posts a listed price based on prior comparable listings. At level 1, a buyer observes the absence announcement and updates willingness-to-pay downward. At level 2, the seller anticipates the buyer’s updated valuation and may lower the listing price, withdraw the listing, or hold firm. At level 3, the buyer anticipates seller capitulation near game time and may delay purchase to extract a lower price. The paper’s price response is therefore a equilibrium object, not a direct readout of fan welfare.

“Crisis habit regression” appears as stale listing behavior. The paper notes that some sellers do not adjust because of “transaction costs that are too high” or the “sunk cost fallacy,” and that observed negative price effects “may reflect a lower bound” [PAGE 4]. A habit-regressing seller anchors on the pre-announcement price \(L_{t-}\) and posts \(L_{t+}\approx L_{t-}\) despite lower expected attendance value. This attenuates the DID estimate. It also predicts that active-listing prices will respond more slowly than sold-listing prices, because sold listings require a buyer and seller to agree on the new valuation, whereas active listings can remain stale. The paper’s sold-listing robustness check is consistent: using “only the subset of tickets that are actually sold,” the results “are even more suggestive of price declines associated with absence announcements for these high-impact superstars” [PAGE 20].

The “counter-trap” is strategic timing. A buyer who knows the superstar is out may wait for last-minute seller discounts; a seller who anticipates waiting buyers may lower the price preemptively or withdraw inventory. The paper documents a last-minute spike in sales: “the average number of tickets sold spikes in the 10 or so hours before a game” [PAGE 8]. It also treats listing disappearance as a sale or seller self-purchase: “we assume the disappearance of a listing on the marketplace implies that this listing was sold, either to a buyer or to the ‘seller’ of the listing who decided to go themselves” [PAGE 7-8, footnote 9]. This assumption is a theory-of-mind boundary: a withdrawn listing is not necessarily a market-clearing sale. If sellers counter-trap by withdrawing rather than repricing, the observed active-listing price effect is censored. If buyers counter-trap by waiting, the sold-listing price effect may appear later and larger near game time.

The “clock” is the disciplining device. Event-time is normalized to \(t=0\) at the announcement, and the event-study window is \([-7,+7]\) hours [PAGE 13-14]. But the physical clock of hours-to-game is not symmetric: announcements are concentrated close to game time. The paper reports that “most of the announcements occur within 10 hours of a game, some coming as close as a few minutes beforehand,” with many around 24 hours and “rarely … more than 36 hours prior to a game” [PAGE 10]. If the true repricing delay is 3-6 hours [PAGE 19], then an announcement made 2 hours before game time cannot be fully observed. That is a clock-induced null, not evidence of zero welfare loss. The recursive prediction is: late announcements should show attenuated or statistically insignificant effects; early announcements with sufficient post-window should show larger effects; sold listings should show stronger effects than active listings; and players with high popularity but low replacement-team competitiveness should show larger home-game effects, as the paper finds for Davis and Porzingis [PAGE 21]. The counter-trap prediction is that sellers who can withdraw inventory will do so when the expected sale price falls below their reservation value, producing missingness in active listings and a possible upward bias in observed listed prices.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a condition under which a lower-layer mechanism breaks the causal interpretation of the price DID. The paper’s object is economic, but three lower layers can veto it: physical/technical marketplace constraints, biomechanical player-availability constraints, and weather/transport shocks.

Physical/technical layer. The ticket is a perishable good: the paper states that tickets “represent a ‘perishable good’ and have no value once a matchup is completed” [PAGE 8]. This is not a veto to the log-price DID because hours-to-game fixed effects absorb the common perishability trajectory. But it is a veto to naive welfare extrapolation because the marginal value of a ticket after game time is zero. Arena capacity is also a physical constraint: the welfare calculation assumes “on average 20,000 fans in an NBA arena” [PAGE 18], but actual attendance, comp tickets, and capacity constraints are not modeled. A capacity-bound game can censor price variation. The marketplace technical layer is a more direct veto: the paper acknowledges missing data from “server restarts and changes of event-names mid-season on the secondary ticket marketplace that were not automatically identified by our data collection program” [PAGE 7, footnote 8]. If outages are correlated with treated games, the DID is biased. The adjudication is: no blanket physical veto for the price regression conditional on non-outage matchups and hours-to-game fixed effects; but a welfare veto for capacity-bound or outage-affected games.

Biomechanical layer. The paper’s key identification claim is that “injury announcements are random (the occurrence of an injury is not predictable)” and that it only looks at price movements “3 days prior to a matchup” [PAGE 15]. That claim is plausible for acute injury, but not for load management. The paper itself warns that rest decisions may depend on “the second night of back-to-back games or fourth game in five nights,” opponent competitiveness, and home vs. away games [PAGE 15]. This is a biomechanical veto for the rest subsample: the absence is endogenous to fatigue management and schedule. The exclusions reinforce the veto: Joel Embiid is excluded because “he did not play in the second night of all back-to-back games, and thus those announcements were not plausibly exogenous” [PAGE 11], and LaMarcus Aldridge is excluded because of “the season-long uncertainty surrounding Kawhi Leonard’s playing status” [PAGE 11]. The adjudication is: retain the causal claim for acute, explicitly announced injury absences; veto causal interpretation for rest, back-to-back, fourth-in-five, and team-uncertainty cases unless a selection model is added.

Weather/transport layer. The paper contains no weather variables, no travel variables, and no local demand shocks. The same-team counterfactual compares treated games to other games of the same team, often on different dates. Weather could therefore differentially affect the treated game and the counterfactual games. If a snowstorm or transit disruption reduces demand for the treated game after the announcement, the DID would attribute that to the superstar absence. The paper provides no test to reject this. The adjudication is a conditional veto: the price DID is not weather-proof in general; it is defensible only for games without severe weather/transport shocks or after adding date-and-market fixed effects and weather controls. The paper’s “same day counterfactual” is mentioned as an alternative [PAGE 12], but it is not the preferred design, and it would not solve weather if the treated and control games are in different cities. Thus the cross-layer verdict is: physical capacity and technical outages veto welfare extrapolation; biomechanical rest vetoes causal interpretation outside acute injury; weather/transport remains an unresolved external-validity veto that the paper does not measure.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Pre-trend falsification veto. Verification: reproduce the generalized DID in equation (2), estimate \(D_t\) for \(t=-7,\dots,-2\), and jointly test \(H_0: D_{-7}=\cdots=D_{-2}=0\) for each player. The paper’s own criterion is that “coefficients statistically insignificantly different from zero prior to an absence announcement … suggest that parallel pre-trends in ticket prices hold” [PAGE 19], and it only reports players whose pre-trends are parallel [PAGE 16]. Prediction: if the joint test rejects at conventional levels, or if pre-event coefficients are economically large relative to the post-event decline, the causal claim for that player is vetoed; the synthetic-control approach proposed in [PAGE 15-16] should attenuate the estimate toward zero. If the joint test fails to reject and the post-event decline appears only after \(t=0\) with the documented 3-6 hour delay [PAGE 19], the causal claim survives this veto.

Scenario 2: Placebo announcement-time randomization veto. Verification: implement the paper’s own random-counterfactual-announcement-time operator [PAGE 14] as a placebo. For each treated matchup, randomly assign announcement times drawn from the observed announcement-time pool to same-team counterfactual matchups, estimate the placebo DID, and repeat many times to build an empirical null distribution. The paper reports only “3 times, each with a different random counterfactual pairing” [PAGE 14]; a verifiable RLVR test should use enough permutations to estimate a tail probability. Prediction: for players with a genuine effect, the observed \(\widehat{\beta}_3\) should lie outside the central 95% placebo interval, and the placebo distribution should be centered near zero. If the observed effect is indistinguishable from the placebo distribution, the estimate is vetoed as an artifact of random pairing or event-time alignment. If the observed effect is outside the placebo interval but the pre-trend test in Scenario 1 fails, the estimate is downgraded to association rather than causation.

Scenario 3: Sold-listing market-clearing veto. Verification: replicate the event-study on the subset of listings that actually sold, as the paper does in Figure 8, and compare the sold-listing event-study path to the active-listing path. The paper states that using only sold listings, “the results are even more suggestive of price declines associated with absence announcements for these high-impact superstars” [PAGE 20]. Prediction: if the active-listing estimate is negative but the sold-listing estimate is null or positive, the listed-price effect is not a market-clearing willingness-to-pay effect and the welfare interpretation is vetoed. If the sold-listing estimate is negative and at least as large in magnitude, the market-clearing interpretation survives. A secondary prediction from the clock layer is that late announcements, especially those within the 3-6 hour repricing delay [PAGE 19], should show attenuated sold-listing effects; if they do not, the delay mechanism is questionable. A third prediction is that high-impact players such as Stephen Curry should show larger sold-listing declines than low-impact players such as James Harden or Kevin Durant in the simple DID, consistent with the paper’s reported heterogeneity [PAGE 16-20]. If the ordering reverses, the sold-listing veto fires.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
