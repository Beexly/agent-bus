That change is both application-driven and computational: players do not observe teammates’ mental states, and the observed-only conditioning makes the E-step sampleable rather than requiring variational inference [PAGE 2].

The sports-analytics lineage runs from additive player ratings and regression plus-minus to network ranking and latent-space team embeddings, which the paper summarizes as models that “ignore dependencies between the players” [PAGE 2]. The model’s “pillars” are a formalization of asymmetric dependence: “We call such players pillars” [PAGE 1], and non-pillars are “more susceptible to be influenced by their teammates’ observed performance” [PAGE 1].

so \(H_t\) is inferred using observations after \(t\). That is smoothing, not real-time filtering. <RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source’s implicit theory of mind is not a nested belief hierarchy; it is a one-step inference from observed performance to hidden mental state. Players “don’t know the hidden state of their teammates; they only know their teammates’ observed performance” [PAGE 2]. That is why \(O_{t-1}^j\), not \(H_{t-1}^j\), enters Eq. (1). The model therefore captures second-order social inference only in a weak sense: player \(i\)’s mental state responds to what \(i\) can see of \(j\)’s performance, and the E-step reconstructs the unseen mental path from the observed sequence [PAGE 6]. There is no explicit representation of \(i\)’s belief about \(j\)’s belief about \(i\). Any “recursive theory of mind” language must be an interpretive overlay, not a formal property of \(\Theta\).

Crisis habit regression is represented by \(N_i\) and \(r_i\). Remark 1 says “Self-dependent players, i.e., players that primarily depend on their own hidden and observed states, improve the resilience of teams” [PAGE 5]. In the extreme \(H\) profile, all players depend only on their previous hidden state; the team rarely collapses because independent choking is required, but when collapse does occur, “the average duration of the collapse is longer” [PAGE 17] because the absorbing habit is hard to break. That is the habit-regression tradeoff: high self-dependence lowers the probability of a coordinated collapse but lengthens recovery once the system is trapped.

Counter-trap behavior is represented by dependence between pillars and by anti-dependence. Remark 3 states “Dependence between pillars makes teams more vulnerable to team collapse” [PAGE 5]. The \(2Pillars+D\) profile is less resilient because dependent pillars “have a tendency to under-perform at the same time” [PAGE 4]. The anti-dependence construction on [PAGE 12] is even more explicit: “when one player’s performance improves, another player’s performance drops.” The Bad Teammate pillar has “significantly negative impact when his/her observed state is \(-1\), and weakly positive impact when their observed state is \(1\)” [PAGE 12]; the Great Teammate pillar has the reverse asymmetry. These are counter-trap profiles because a non-pillar’s best response to a teammate’s improvement can be a relative decline, sustaining a low-performance equilibrium.

The clock is the third layer. More pillars lower collapse probability but “the more pillars a team has, the longer the maximum window of team collapse is” [PAGE 16]. That is not a contradiction; it is a probability-versus-duration tradeoff. A one-pillar team has a single point of failure [PAGE 4], but once the pillar recovers, “once the pillar player gets out of the choke, non-pillar players are likely to do the same” [PAGE 17]. A three-pillar team is harder to collapse, but when multiple pillars are simultaneously low, the exit time can be longer. The 3-minute windowing in NBAgames [PAGE 7] makes this clock observable, but the model does not include substitution timing, timeouts, or fatigue clocks, so the clock layer is only partially represented.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The lower-layer veto is physical and biomechanical. The source model has no operator for minutes, fatigue, injury, position, opponent pressure, or substitution. Yet its real-data setup selects starting lineups by minutes [PAGE 6] and its conclusion admits that players “do not change over time” is a shortcoming [PAGE 8]. Formally, let \(B_t\) be a common biomechanical or matchup factor, such as opponent defensive pressure or cumulative fatigue, that affects multiple players’ observed PIE. The source likelihood is
\[
Pr(\mathbf O_t|\Theta)
=
\sum_{\mathbf H_t}\prod_i E_i(O_t^i|H_t^i)\,Pr(\mathbf H_t|\Theta),
\]
with no \(B_t\) term. If the true generative process contains \(B_t\), then the source’s d-separation claim \(O_t^i\perp O_t^{-i}|H_t^i\) [PAGE 17] fails because \(B_t\) is a common cause. The model then attributes to teammate influence \(R_{ij}\) what is actually a shared physical shock. This is a non-identifiability veto: for any estimated \(\hat R_{ij}>0\), there exists an alternative model with \(R_{ij}=0\) and a common \(B_t\) that reproduces the same observed likelihood. The source cannot adjudicate between “teammate influence” and “common physical cause” without conditioning on \(B_t\).

The substitution veto is exact. Eq. (1) is written for fixed \(n\) and a fixed row set of \(R\). If a substitution changes the on-court five at time \(t\), the dimension and meaning of \(R\) change. The paper’s own conclusion says a dynamic version “where players can get substituted” is future work [PAGE 8]. Therefore any inference that a team collapsed because of mental contagion, when a substitution or injury occurred at the same window, is vetoed unless the lineup is held fixed or the model is extended to \(n_t\) and time-varying \(R_t\).

The weather veto is domain-specific. For the studied NBA data, games are indoor, and the source contains no weather variable in \(\Theta\). Weather is therefore not an admissible causal layer for these games; a weather attribution would be unsupported. If the model were ported to an outdoor sport, weather \(W_t\) would be a common cause of observed performance and would have to enter the emission or transition model. Without \(W_t\), the same non-identifiability argument applies: rain, wind, heat, or field conditions could produce correlated underperformance that the model would misread as teammate influence.

The adjudication rule is therefore: a latent mental-state explanation is admissible only after lower-layer variables are held constant, conditioned, or shown to be irrelevant. The source’s own evidence supports this rule indirectly: team structure is “just one characteristic of a team” [PAGE 8], and Dallas, Boston, and Houston show that structure alone does not determine success. Physical and matchup layers can veto a mental-contagion interpretation even when the graphical model fits the observed sequence.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Pillar-forced-choke counterfactual. Scenario: generate synthetic data from the source’s 1Pillar profile with \(n=5\), \(T=100\), and \(\alpha=1\), as in [PAGE 4]. Intervention: at a fixed \(t_0\), force the pillar’s \(H_{t_0}=-1\) versus force a randomly chosen non-pillar’s \(H_{t_0}=-1\), with all else equal. Verifiable reward: difference in downstream \(\alpha\)-collapse frequency and recovery time over many independent simulations. Prediction from the source: the pillar intervention produces a larger collapse probability and a longer recovery window than the non-pillar intervention, because the paper says the one-pillar team has “a single point of failure” [PAGE 4]. Veto condition: if the pillar intervention is not significantly worse than the non-pillar intervention under a pre-registered confidence interval, the single-point-of-failure mechanism is falsified for that profile.

2. Common-shock confound counterfactual. Scenario: use a controlled synthetic team with known \(R\), then inject a common exogenous factor \(X_t\) that flips all players’ observed states with probability \(q\), independent of the hidden states. Fit the source model with and without \(X_t\). Verifiable reward: held-out log-likelihood and the estimated off-diagonal mass \(\sum_{i\neq j}\hat R_{ij}\). Prediction: the unadjusted source model will inflate off-diagonal \(R\) estimates as \(q\) rises, because common shocks look like teammate influence. The adjusted model should recover the true \(R\) and improve held-out likelihood. Veto condition: if the source model’s \(\hat R\) is invariant to \(q\), then the common-shock critique is not empirically material for that regime; if \(\hat R\) inflates, the source’s causal attribution is vetoed in the presence of unmeasured common causes.

3. Dynamic-substitution counterfactual. Scenario: take NBAgames-style 3-minute windows [PAGE 7] and compare the fixed-lineup source model against a dynamic model with \(n_t\) and \(R_t\) that changes at substitution boundaries. Verifiable reward: held-out predictive log-likelihood on later windows and error in predicting recovery time after a substitution. Prediction: the fixed-lineup model will fit worse at substitution boundaries and will misestimate collapse duration, because the source explicitly assumes players “do not change over time” [PAGE 8]. Veto condition: if the fixed-lineup model matches the dynamic model on held-out windows and recovery-time error, the substitution critique is not material in that dataset; if it fails, the source’s collapse-duration estimates are vetoed for games with material lineup changes.
