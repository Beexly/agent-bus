Under temporal crisis—the two-minute warning [PAGE 10, 3-41] and the final 40 seconds of a half [PAGE 21, 4-7-3]—coaches regress to conservative habit structures: hoarding timeouts, avoiding substitution risks, and refusing to decline penalties that would stop the clock. Rule 4-5-1 limits teams to three charged timeouts per half and prohibits a second timeout in the same dead-ball period [PAGE 12]; under pressure, a coach may request a forbidden second timeout, incurring a 5-yard delay-of-game penalty and a charged timeout [PAGE 12, Item 3]. The officiating crew, modeled recursively by the head coach, faces its own regression: Rule 4-5-1 Item 3 states that if a team requests and is granted a second timeout in the same dead-ball period, the timeout is charged and 5 yards are assessed [PAGE 12], but crew workload in the final minutes increases the probability of missed or double-granted timeouts, creating an exploitable enforcement gap.

Counter-trap structure: The offended team may decline a 5-yard penalty to preserve possession or to avoid a 10-second runoff [PAGE 12, Item 1; PAGE 15, 4-7-4]. For example, if the defense commits a foul that stops the clock after the two-minute warning, the offense can decline the yardage and accept the snap-start clock, denying the defense a stoppage [PAGE 12, Item 1]. Conversely, the defense may decline an offensive penalty to keep the clock running [PAGE 14, 14-1-2 Item 1]. This creates a recursive model in which each team predicts the other’s acceptance/decline decision based on clock state, score differential, and timeout inventory.

The clock itself is an adversarial agent with asymmetric information. Rule 4-4 [PAGE 11-12] designates the stadium electric clock as official time; the Side Judge supervises and can take over if the clock fails [PAGE 11]. Rule 15-5-1 states the game clock is reviewable “for purposes of restoring time to the clock but not for purposes of taking time off the clock” [PAGE 62]. This one-way temporal restoration means the leading team benefits from clock errors that stop time (restorable) but is insulated from errors that start time (not restorable), breaking time-reversal symmetry. In overtime, Rule 16-3-3(d) caps the regular-season extra period at 10 minutes with a possible tie [PAGE 65], while postseason overtime continues until a winner [PAGE 66, 16-4-4(d)]; the recursive theory of mind must therefore model different terminal conditions based on season phase, altering fourth-down and two-point conversion calculus.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto layers.

Physical layer: Rule 1-1-1 fixes the field at 360 ft by 160 ft [PAGE 8]. Goal posts: crossbar top face 10 ft above ground, uprights extending 35 ft above the crossbar, 3–4 in diameter [PAGE 8]. Ball pressure \(P \in [12.5, 13.5]\) psi [PAGE 9]; the Referee is sole judge and may withhold non-conforming balls [PAGE 9]. Field markings must be laid out with tolerance of plus one-fourth inch [PAGE 9, 1-2-3]; hash marks at 70 ft 9 in [PAGE 8]. Any deviation outside tolerance is a physical veto on the validity of the playing surface; the Commissioner must approve deviations [PAGE 8, 1-2-2].

Biomechanical layer: Mandatory equipment includes helmet, shoulder pads, thigh pads, knee pads [PAGE 18-19, 5-4-3]. Knee pads must be at least 1/4 inch thick [PAGE 19]. Prohibited items: metal or hard projecting objects, adhesive/slippery substances, cleats with cutting edges, tear-away jerseys [PAGE 19-21]. Illegal blocks below the waist are prohibited outside the tight-end box except against runners or pass catchers [PAGE 46-47, 12-2-4]. Chop blocks, crackback blocks, peel-back blocks, blindside blocks, and hip-drop tackles draw 15-yard penalties [PAGE 45-50]. Defenseless-player posture covers passers, receivers in the act of catching, kickers, and players on the ground [PAGE 47-48]; prohibited contact includes helmet-to-head/neck, lowering the head, and illegal launching [PAGE 48]. These rules constitute biomechanical vetoes: a block or tackle crossing the force/angle threshold is nullified by penalty and may trigger disqualification if flagrant [PAGE 47-50].

Weather/environmental layer: Rule 17 governs emergencies [PAGE 67-68]. If spectators enter the field such that the game cannot continue, the Referee declares timeout, records down/distance/position/time, and orders the field cleared [PAGE 67, 17-1-2]. If resumption is impossible due to severe weather, lightning, flooding, or power failure, the Commissioner may terminate the game if resumption would not change the result [PAGE 67, 17-1-4]. Postseason interruptions are rescheduled at alternate sites if necessary [PAGE 68, 17-1-10]. These environmental vetoes operate independently of team strategy: a lightning delay can void a field-goal attempt or a drive, and the Commissioner’s termination authority is final [PAGE 67, 17-1-3].

Veto proof: A play is physically vetoed if the ball fails pressure specification [PAGE 9], if field markings exceed tolerance [PAGE 9], or if an emergency stops play [PAGE 67]. Biomechanically, a play is vetoed by personal foul enforcement (15 yards, possible disqualification) [PAGE 45-50]. Procedurally, Rule 19-2 permits disqualification consultation before the next snap [PAGE 70], and Rule 15 permits replay review of possession, goal line, and boundary rulings [PAGE 60-63], creating layered vetoes over on-field outcomes.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (rule-based verifiable counterfactual) veto scenarios with predictions.

Scenario 1 — Late onside declaration with formation mismatch. Counterfactual: With 1:15 remaining in the fourth quarter, Team A trails and lines up for a standard kickoff from its 35-yard line. Team B aligns nine players in the 5-yard setup zone per Rule 6-1-3 [PAGE 22]. After the Referee signals ready for play but before the 25-second play clock starts, Team A declares an onside kick under Rule 6-1-6 [PAGE 23] and lines up six players on one side of the ball, violating Rule 6-1-6(c)(2)’s limit of five players per side [PAGE 23]. Veto operator: The kicking team’s illegal formation is a dead-ball/free-kick foul; enforcement is loss of 5 yards from the previous spot and the free kick is made again under Rule 6-3-1 [PAGE 25], unless Team B was also illegally formed (double foul, down replayed). Prediction: Because Team B had no notice of the onside declaration until after it committed to a deep alignment, officials should grant Team B a reasonable re-set window under Rule 6-1-3(d)’s spirit [PAGE 22]. If the kick occurs before re-set, the veto isolates Team A’s formation foul; Team B takes possession at its 40-yard line (previous spot + 5) or the kick is re-taken from Team A’s 30-yard line. Verifiable: Penalty enforcement 5 yards; down replayed; no score on the play.

Scenario 2 — Excess injury timeout after two-minute warning with no timeouts. Counterfactual: With 0:45 left in the second half, Team A has the ball and no timeouts. Team A’s quarterback goes down claiming injury; no Team B foul caused it, and there was no change of possession or score [PAGE 13-14, 4-5-4(b)]. Team A has used all three timeouts, so an excess team timeout is called [PAGE 13]. Veto operator: Rule 4-5-4 penalizes the second and each subsequent excess timeout after the two-minute warning with loss of 5 yards from the succeeding spot [PAGE 13]. Under Rule 4-5-4 Note 3, because the excess timeout is charged to the team in possession and stops the clock, the defense may choose a 10-second runoff [PAGE 13]. Prediction: If the defense elects the runoff, the game clock goes from 0:45 to 0:35; if Team A then snaps immediately, 25 seconds remain. If instead the clock was at 0:09 when the injury occurred, the 10-second runoff ends the half [PAGE 13, Note 4], vetoing any further offensive play. Verifiable: 5-yard penalty, 10-second runoff optional for defense, possible half termination if \(\tau_{\text{game}} \le 10\) s.

Scenario 3 — Illegal wedge block on kickoff return inside the end zone. Counterfactual: Team A kicks off from its 35. Team B’s returner catches the ball at the 2-yard line. Before the ball is touched in the landing zone, three Team B blockers align shoulder-to-shoulder within two yards at the 5-yard line and move forward, constituting an illegal wedge block under Rule 6-2-1 Item 2(d) [PAGE 24]. The returner advances to the 40-yard line. Veto operator: Rule 6-2-1 states that if the wedge foul occurs during the kick, enforcement is from the spot of the foul; if the foul occurs in the receiving team’s end zone during the kick, it is enforced from the previous spot [PAGE 24]. Because the wedge formed at the 5-yard line (in the field of play, not the end zone) during the kick, enforcement is from that spot [PAGE 24]. Prediction: Team B’s return is nullified; the ball is placed at Team B’s 5-yard line (spot of foul), first-and-10, with the 15-yard yardage walked off from that spot. If the wedge had formed inside the end zone, the ball would revert to the previous spot (Team B’s 20-yard line touchback spot or 35-yard line depending on kick type [PAGE 22, 6-1-5]). Verifiable: 15-yard penalty from spot of foul; return yardage erased; possession retained by Team B at the enforcement spot.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
