Recursive theory of mind here concerns what the experimenter assumes the rat assumes about the choice structure. Under error crisis—repeated incorrect choices—the organism may regress from purposive expectancy (the white curtain leads to reward) to habitual stimulus-response alternation or side bias. This crisis habit regression is especially likely when the spatial field is weak: if alleys are spaced far apart, each trial feels like an isolated problem, and after errors the rat falls back on motor habits rather than field-based sign-gestalts.

The counter-trap is the experimenter's inference that spatial contiguity is intrinsically facilitating. Proximity may instead create a perceptual trap: if the two curtains are within a single whisker or visual gestalt, the animal cannot form separate sign-gestalts for "white curtain here" and "black curtain there," so initial acquisition may look faster because the animal is responding to a unified local cue rather than learning a transferable discrimination. On reversal, the contiguous group should show more perseveration because the rigid field-whole resists updating; the spaced group, having encoded more allocentric, separable relations, reverses faster.

For delayed reaction, the contiguity advantage holds only while \(\tau_d < \tau_c\), a critical retention ceiling (on the order of tens of seconds for a rat).
