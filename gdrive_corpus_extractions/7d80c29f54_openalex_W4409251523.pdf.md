<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Formal measurement model. Let \(\mathbf p(t)\in\mathbb R^2\) be the true field position in metres and let \(\hat{\mathbf p}(t)\) be the estimated position. Under the assumption of an additive systematic bias \(\mathbf b\) and zero-mean random error \(\boldsymbol\epsilon(t)\),
\[
\hat{\mathbf p}(t)=\mathbf p(t)+\mathbf b+\boldsymbol\epsilon(t),
\quad [\hat{\mathbf p}]=[\mathbf p]=[\mathbf b]=[\boldsymbol\epsilon]=\mathrm{m}.
\]
This aligns with the source’s distinction between systematic and random error [PAGE 5]. Breakdown regime: if \(|\mathbf b|\) is not estimated by calibration or criterion testing, every downstream distance and derivative inherits the bias; if \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is heavy-tailed or non-stationary, reported mean error alone is insufficient.

Kinematic differentiation. For uniform sampling interval \(\Delta t\) in seconds, central differences give
\[
\hat v_k=\frac{\hat p_{k+1}-\hat p_{k-1}}{2\Delta t},
\quad [\hat v]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\hat v_k)=\frac{\sigma_p^2}{2\Delta t^2},
\quad [\mathrm{Var}]=\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}},
\]
and
\[
\hat a_k=\frac{\hat p_{k+1}-2\hat p_k+\hat p_{k-1}}{\Delta t^2},
\quad [\hat a]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\hat a_k)=\frac{6\sigma_p^2}{\Delta t^4},
\quad [\mathrm{Var}]=\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-4}}.
\]
Breakdown singularity: as \(\Delta t\to 0\), both derivative-error variances diverge; therefore finite sampling plus random position error makes acceleration inherently less trustworthy than distance. This is the formal reason the review recommends independent validation of instantaneous velocity and warns about aliasing [PAGE 34].

Nyquist condition. Let \(f_s\) be sampling frequency in Hz and \(f_{\max}\) the highest frequency present in the athlete’s movement signal. The source states: “if the sampling rate is not at least twice the highest frequency present in the signal, it may lead to distorted or misleading data [232]” [PAGE 34]. Formally,
\[
f_s \ge 2 f_{\max},\quad [f_s]=\mathrm{Hz}.
\]
Breakdown regime: when \(f_s\) is below \(2f_{\max}\), aliasing occurs; peak speed and acceleration can be systematically misestimated even if mean speed appears acceptable.

LPS effective sampling. The review states: “if the sampling rate is 1,000 Hz but 22 players are connected with their transponders (e.g., during football matches), the resulting sampling frequency is ~45 Hz” [PAGE 19]. Let \(f_{\mathrm{true}}\) be the true system rate and \(N_T\) the number of active transponders. Under the source’s reciprocal relationship,
\[
f_{\mathrm{res}}=\frac{f_{\mathrm{true}}}{N_T},\quad [f_{\mathrm{res}}]=\mathrm{Hz}.
\]
Breakdown regime: as \(N_T\) increases, \(f_{\mathrm{res}}\) decreases; if \(f_{\mathrm{res}}\) falls below the Nyquist threshold for the movement, LPS-derived acceleration and high-frequency change-of-direction metrics are not identifiable from the sampled trajectory.

GNSS geometry and DOP. The source states: “DOP is inversely proportional to the volume of a cone defined by satellites and the receiver position. The better the arrangement of satellites, the lower the DOP value, which results in higher accuracy” [PAGE 9]. A compact formal representation, under the source’s proportionality assumption, is
\[
\mathrm{DOP}\propto \frac{1}{V_{\mathrm{cone}}},
\quad [\mathrm{DOP}]=1,\quad [V_{\mathrm{cone}}]=\mathrm{m^3}.
\]
The review notes: “DOP values may range from 0 to 50, but ideally, it is less than 1 [94]” [PAGE 9]. Breakdown singularity: as \(V_{\mathrm{cone}}\to 0\), \(\mathrm{DOP}\to\infty\) under the stated inverse proportionality; clustered or collinear satellites are the physical geometry that drives this regime [PAGE 9].

Camera homography and calibration. The review says field coordinates are obtained “by camera calibration or homography parameters [54]” [PAGE 5]. Let \(\mathbf x_{\mathrm{img}}\) be image coordinates and \(\mathbf X_{\mathrm{plane}}\) field-plane coordinates. Under a planar homography,
\[
\mathbf x_{\mathrm{img}}\sim \mathbf H\,\mathbf X_{\mathrm{plane}},
\quad [\mathbf H]=1.
\]
Breakdown regime: if calibration points are collinear or otherwise degenerate, \(\mathbf H\) is singular or ill-conditioned, and back-projected field position is unbounded or unstable. The source warns that “Poor camera calibration is one of the key factors that result in systematic errors during video-based tracking methods” [PAGE 5].

Detection and tracking metrics. The source defines precision and recall as “the ratio between the TP and the sum between the TP and the FP” and “the ratio between the TP and the sum between the TP and the FN” [PAGE 29]. In dimensionless probability/count form,
\[
P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad
R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad
F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R},
\]
with \(TP,FP,FN\in\mathbb N_0\), \(P,R,F_1\in[0,1]\), and all quantities dimensionless. The source states that MOTA “combines three error types: FPs, FNs, and identity switches (IDSWs)” [PAGE 29]. Under the usual count convention,
\[
\mathrm{MOTA}=1-\frac{FN+FP+IDSW}{GT},
\quad [\mathrm{MOTA}]=1,
\]
where \(GT\) is the number of ground-truth identities. This is a performance indicator, not a position-accuracy metric; the source explicitly warns that detection and re-ID success “is not a guarantee of good position measurements” [PAGE 33].

Identity information. For a time index \(t\), let \(X_t\) be the true identity variable and \(Y_t\) the observed detection/association evidence. Shannon mutual information is
\[
I(X_t;Y_t)=H(X_t)-H(X_t\mid Y_t),
\quad [I]=[H]=\mathrm{bits}.
\]
Under occlusion and similar-appearance teammates, \(H(X_t\mid Y_t)\) increases; IDSW counts and IDF1 are practical proxies. The source defines IDSW as “the number of times the identification (ID) changes in the same trajectory” [PAGE 32]. Breakdown regime: as the visible area fraction of a player approaches zero, identity evidence collapses; if \(I(X_t;Y_t)\to 0\), identity-dependent tactical variables are not identifiable from that frame.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage in the review runs from time-motion analysis to wearable and AI systems. The source states: “At the end of the 1970s, the development of tools capable of monitoring athletes during matches and competitions was presented in the literature” [PAGE 2]. It describes early methods as quantifying stride length at different speeds and later filming athletes to estimate steps at each speed [PAGE 2]. The genealogy then branches into potentiometers, video-based tracking, GPS/GNSS, LPS, and AI. The review notes that “Some methods, such as potentiometers [8], were limited to the analysis of a single player per match and were neglected by the scientific community in further studies” [PAGE 3]. This is an early example of a multi-week trap: a method can appear adequate in a single-player protocol and then fail as a team-scale measurement system.

The video-based lineage carries a time-cost trap. The review states: “Barros et al. [9] reported more than 12 hours to accomplish computational processes for automatic tracking and more than 6 hours of manual corrections for each player analyzed in a football match” [PAGE 3]. A later AI wave can look superior because detection metrics improve, but the review warns that “in the early 2000s, the literature already reported high values of automatic tracking rates, like those currently reported by AI-based systems” [PAGE 34]. The retrocausal lesson is that a modern mAP or MOTA score does not by itself prove a reduction in total processing time or an improvement in coordinate accuracy. The recommendation is to report “the automatic tracking rate, and the total duration of pre- and post-data processing for all players” [PAGE 34].

The wearable lineage has a regulatory and environmental hinge. The review states: “In 2015, the use of these systems was allowed during official matches [13]” [PAGE 3], but also notes that “some GPSs may be inaccurate when recording performances at high velocities [14]” [PAGE 3]. LPS entered as a high-accuracy indoor alternative: “The LPS proved to be accurate, with errors lower than 25 cm for the determination of the player’s position in football [15, 16]” [PAGE 3], but it “requires antennas installed around the pitch and usually has a higher cost compared to other systems” [PAGE 3]. The multi-week trap is procurement drift: a system validated in one stadium with optimal antenna placement can be reused in another venue without repeating calibration, and the error budget changes without any change in the advertised product.

The AI lineage adds pose estimation and transformer-based detection. The review states: “With the advent of artificial intelligence (AI) and GPU, video-based tracking systems have continued to improve and have become more promising in terms of accuracy and cost” [PAGE 29]. But the historical trap repeats at a higher layer: detection benchmarks are mostly pedestrian or generic object environments, while sport contexts add “teammates have very similar appearances in the image, image blur during fast movements, complex movements, limited video resolution, and severe occlusions” [PAGE 33]. A method can win a generic benchmark and still fail on a sports field.

Multi-week traps in longitudinal monitoring are therefore of four kinds. First, calibration drift: a camera or LPS antenna array can shift over weeks, producing a systematic bias that mimics a real change in athlete output. Second, sampling aliasing: if weekly sprint data are collected at a rate below the Nyquist threshold for the movement, the reported weekly “peak speed” can fluctuate because of phase alignment rather than athlete adaptation [PAGE 34]. Third, device-position drift: the review reports that “lower back is the best location for the accelerometer to estimate an external training load in badminton compared to hands and legs [79]” and that lower-back peak reliability is higher than upper-back during jumping and landing [PAGE 6]. If a monitoring program changes vest placement between weeks, accelerometer-derived load is not longitudinally comparable. Fourth, satellite-geometry drift: DOP varies with venue and satellite arrangement, and the review notes that DOP and satellite count are “underreported” in sports science [PAGE 9]. A multi-week dataset can therefore mix low-DOP and high-DOP sessions without documentation, making apparent trends partly geodetic rather than physiological.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has three agents: the analyst \(A\), the coach \(C\), and the athlete \(P\), plus the tracking system \(S\). \(A\) believes that \(C\) needs immediate performance information; \(C\) believes that \(A\) has already validated \(S\); \(P\) may adapt behavior because a device is worn or because feedback changes training. The source supplies the clock: “Considering that performance data guide coaches and trainers about training parameters and match analytics, these professionals need the performance information in a short period or even in real-time” [PAGE 33]. It also supplies the contrast: video-based systems are “very time-consuming” [PAGE 33], while GPS/GNSS devices are “time-efficient” and provide “fast and/or live data [14]” [PAGE 33]. The recursive trap is that \(A\) anticipates \(C\)’s clock pressure and chooses the fastest available number, even when that number is the least validated for the question being asked.

Crisis habit regression. Under time pressure, \(A\) regresses to familiar low-dimensional metrics: total distance, mean speed, or a generic “player load.” These are habitual because they are easy to explain and often less sensitive to momentary occlusion than tactical variables. But the source shows the danger: GPS distance measures “may be less reliable as the running speed increases, with lower accuracy for a nonlinear path [91]” [PAGE 9]. Accelerometer-derived load is also not a universal currency: “the accelerometers showed poor reliability, high variability, and were not valid when used to estimate vertical acceleration of the center of gravity and the thoracic segment” [PAGE 6]. Habit regression therefore produces a false sense of safety: the metric is familiar, but it may be answering a different question than the coach’s high-speed or biomechanical question.

Counter-trap. The opposite failure is overcorrection: \(A\) abandons the habitual metric and adopts an AI pose pipeline because it produces rich limb kinematics and visually impressive overlays. The source supports the promise—pose tracking “allows a more in-depth interpretation and assessment of human movements of biomechanical and physiological demands” [PAGE 33]—but the same page warns that pose estimators “struggle with motion blur” and that “occlusions between nearby individuals can mislead estimators, resulting in incorrect predictions” [PAGE 33]. The counter-trap is treating a detection benchmark as a biomechanical validation. The source’s recommendation is explicit: “good performance during detection and re-ID procedures is not a guarantee of good position measurements” [PAGE 33].

Clock. The clock is not only computational; it is institutional. A coach may need a halftime adjustment, a strength coach may need a next-day load report, and a federation may need a season-long audit. The source’s recommendation to report “the automatic tracking rate, and the total duration of pre- and post-data processing for all players” [PAGE 34] is a clock-aware validity rule. The recursive theory-of-mind resolution is to make the clock explicit in the validity predicate: if the required decision latency is shorter than the validated processing latency for the required variable, the system must return a lower-dimensional validated metric or a “not yet valid” flag, not an unvalidated high-dimensional metric. This prevents \(A\) from projecting \(C\)’s urgency onto \(S\)’s accuracy and prevents \(C\) from assuming that \(A\)’s output has passed a criterion test that was never run.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
