from “muscle fiber degeneration and inflammation (1–3 days), regeneration (3–4 weeks), and maturation with remodeling (3–6 months)” [PAGE 14].

Breakdown and singularity regimes:
1. Hamstring chronic-irreducibility boundary: for \(\tau>1\,\text{mo}\) in chronic lesions, “it may be impossible to restore the tendon to its original footprint because of scar repair involving adjacent muscles and retraction” [PAGE 7]. The anatomical restoration operator loses invertibility.
2. Adductor scar-formation boundary: the “10–20 days” interval before surgery creates “fibrous scar tissue,” altering dissection and tension [PAGE 11].
3. Rectus femoris fixation singularity: “end-to-end sutures of proximal tears usually provide insufficient strength,” so failure occurs unless the operator switches to anchor-based reinsertion [PAGE 15].
4. Recurrence singularity: for hamstring central/distal MTJ lesions, “Recurrence rates in this region may exceed 50%” [PAGE 17].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The chapter’s classification lineage is cumulative rather than replacement-based. The primitive “Traditional Clinical Grading (Grade I–III)” lacked location specificity [PAGE 2]. BAMIC inserted anatomic depth by separating “myofascial,” “musculotendinous,” and “intratendinous” domains and quantifying CSA and length [PAGE 3]. Munich added a functional-versus-structural ontology [PAGE 3], while ISMuLT standardized terminology and discouraged ambiguous language such as “elongation” or “strain” [PAGE 3]. Surgically, the lineage runs through proximal hamstring avulsion series and operative proximal rectus femoris cohorts [PAGES 28–30], through the PLAC concept [PAGE 32: Schilders et al., 2017], to the reinjury expert-opinion consolidation [PAGE 26: Palermi et al., 2024].

The multi-week traps are calendar-shaped. One trap is early: “early low-grade injuries may be under-recognized and undertreated,” converting day-3 problems into subacute or chronic presentations [PAGE 5]. A second is pre-surgical: the “10–20 days” scar interval in adductor surgery [PAGE 11]. A third is post-injury chronicization: for hamstring, “evaluated more than 1 month after trauma” the original footprint may be unattainable [PAGE 7]. A fourth is the treatment clock itself: chronic adductor tendinopathy qualifies for surgery only after failure of “at least 3 months of structured rehabilitation” [PAGE 11]. A fifth is repair latency: six weeks versus forty-six weeks splits satisfaction, same-level RTP, months-to-play, and complication/neurological burdens [PAGE 6]. A sixth is recurrence surveillance: the “Fifteen-week window for recurrent muscle strains” in a 3,600-strain Australian-rules cohort [PAGE 28, ref. 41] indicates that early-season recurrence data cannot support final discharge. A seventh is seasonal theater: athletes often “continue to compete during the season while undergoing maintenance physiotherapy,” delaying definitive resolution to the off-season [PAGE 10]. Finally, a source-drift trap exists between the text’s “2.3% annually” [PAGE 4] and its own cited “4% annually” claim [PAGE 26]; the two should not be merged into a trend operator without adjudication.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Model the clinical decision-maker as a Bayesian agent with belief state
\[
b_t=\Pr\!\left(\text{surgery indicated}\mid \mathcal{F}_t\right),\qquad 0\le b_t\le 1,\quad
b_{t+1}=\sigma\!\left(w_1 G + w_2 L + w_3 T + w_4 F + w_5 R_{\text{hx}}\right),
\]
where \(G\) is imaging grade, \(L\) is anatomic location/tissue type, \(T\) is elapsed time, \(F\) is functional failure, and \(R_{\text{hx}}\) is recurrence history. Textual priors justify separating these terms: MRI classification has only “limited reliability in predicting exact RTP readiness” [PAGE 2], and “clinical assessment remains indispensable alongside imaging-based classification” [PAGE 4].

Habit regression pulls \(b_t\) downward. Because “the majority of these injuries recover with nonoperative management” [PAGE 2], and because “Conservative treatment represents the cornerstone of initial care for adductor muscle and tendon injuries” [PAGE 10], the default regime is protection, loading, ice, compression, elevation, then exercise-based rehabilitation [PAGES 5, 10, 14]. The counter-trap is activated when the lesion occupies a structurally dangerous tissue class. Intratendinous hamstring involvement “tends to prolong rehabilitation and may alter management compared with typical MTJ injuries” [PAGE 5]. Adductor lesions unresponsive for “>3 months” or with avulsion/diastasis force referral [PAGES 10–11]. Rectus femoris central tendon pathology is the strongest counter-trap because recurrence and delay are intrinsic [PAGE 18].

The clock overrides both. A lesion that remains painful at week 12 of a failed program crosses the adductor surgery threshold [PAGE 11]; a complete avulsion crossing six weeks begins losing the acute-repair outcome advantage [PAGE 6]; a hamstring lesion beyond one month risks irreversible loss of the footprint plane [PAGE 7]. The utility problem is recursively unstable because an in-season athlete may prefer a high-risk early return to surgery; the text acknowledges this pressure directly in chronic groin management [PAGE 10]. Thus the optimal policy is not return-to-play-first but structural-correction-first: while “most primary injuries can be managed conservatively, the recurrence of tendon injuries often shifts the balance toward surgical treatment” [PAGE 17].

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical vetoes are deterministic within the surgical plane. In proximal hamstring repair, the approach can encounter “large hematomas, significant tendon or myotendinous gaps (up to 15–18 cm), and involvement of the sciatic nerve” [PAGE 6]. If a chronic scar has crossed the one-month boundary, anatomical reinsertion may be impossible [PAGE 7]. In adductor repair, a mechanically dangerous local optimum exists: “If a rigid tendon adherent to surrounding tissues is reattached, the risk of recurrence is high due to excessive tension” [PAGE 11], while infection, dehiscence, and anchor mobilization are “relatively more frequent” in this region [PAGE 12]. In rectus femoris repair, end-to-end proximal suturing is vetoed because it “usually provide[s] insufficient strength” [PAGE 15], and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve imposes an additional protected-structure constraint [PAGE 15].

Biomechanical veto conditions are evaluable pre-return. The adductor criteria are explicit: “pain-free adductor contraction, tolerance to sport-specific drills (sprinting, cutting, and agility testing), and adequate functional stability” [PAGE 10]. For quadriceps, clearance requires “complete symptom resolution, full recovery of strength and function, and pass sport-specific functional testing” [PAGE 14]. If proprioceptive or strength deficits persist, release is vetoed regardless of calendar time.

Surface/weather layers act as categorical hazard modifiers rather than toy ratios. The text identifies “surface conditions such as dry playing fields” among quadriceps risk factors [PAGE 13] and fatigue concentration “in the later stages of matches” [PAGE 13]. In a formal operator:
\[
\lambda = \lambda_0 \cdot M_{\text{match}}\cdot M_{\text{congestion}}\cdot M_{\text{dry}}\cdot M_{\text{late-phase}},
\]
where the source supports the presence of modifiers \(M_{\text{match}}\le 9\) [PAGE 4], \(M_{\text{congestion}}\le 25\) [PAGE 13], and the directional existence of \(M_{\text{dry}}\) and \(M_{\text{late-phase}}\), but does not provide numeric values for the latter two. Therefore a “weather veto proof” is conditional, not numeric: a match on a dry pitch in a congested late-stage window raises the hazard regime above the baseline training environment and vetoes a return that is only calendar-progressed rather than criterion-satisfied.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR veto 1: Delayed surgical repair for complete proximal hamstring avulsion with 12 cm retraction. Counterfactual: if the operation is postponed from \(\tau\le 6\) wk to \(\tau\approx 46\) wk, the page-bound outcome vector predicts satisfaction \(0.95\to0.77\), same-level RTP \(0.92\to0.85\), RTP \(4.5\to5.6\) months, complications \(0.1395\to0.2500\), and neurological sequelae \(0.0166\to0.0904\) [PAGES 6–7]. Verifiable audit marker: surgical date versus symptom date; if greater than six weeks and no contraindication exists, the counterfactual predicts degraded satisfaction and elevated neuro-complication probability. Decision: veto delayed repair unless sciatic involvement, infection, or anatomical collapse makes acute exposure nonviable [PAGE 6].

RLVR veto 2: Early running after proximal hamstring tendon reinsertion. Protocol states “running is typically postponed until 2 months after surgery” [PAGE 8], while intratendinous lesions carry a \(2\)–\(4\) month recovery baseline [PAGE 4]. Counterfactual: if running begins before 8 weeks, the model predicts the construct is still inside the maturation/regeneration phase with an elevated reinjury hazard; the central/distal MTJ and proximal recurrence class has a reported bound above \(50\%\) [PAGE 17]. Verifiable audit markers: date of first prescribed running, postoperative week, subsequent reinjury event or MRI re-tear. Decision: veto jogging/running before week 8 unless objective criteria independently demonstrate tissue readiness, which the source does not define as sufficient before the stated protocol.

RLVR veto 3: Chronic-season nonoperative management of an adductor avulsion with diastasis \(0.6\) cm and bony fragment. Counterfactual: if the lesion is treated conservatively beyond the “10–20 days” scar-forming window and beyond the three-month refractory threshold, the prediction is failure of tendon apposition and recruitment of the PLAC complex repair process [PAGES 10–11]. Source condition is explicit: “Reinsertion is generally indicated when the diastasis exceeds 0.5 cm, particularly in association with a bony fragment” [PAGE 11]. Verifiable audit marker: ultrasound or MRI diastasis measurement and presence of osseous fragment; if d>0.5 cm with fragment, continued conservative care is vetoed. Decision: surgical referral before retraction/scarring complicate reattachment [PAGE 11].

Across all three, the RLVR criterion is reproducibility: each veto maps a verifiable document coordinate—dates, gap length, reinjury event—to a page-bound probability or threshold without introducing exogenous equations.
