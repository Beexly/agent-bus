The core epistemic problem in Divjak & Zazula is the conflation of two distinct error sources in video-based 3D tracking: the error intrinsic to the vision algorithm (Δp_C) and the error induced by aligning the camera coordinate system (CSC) to the electromagnetic reference coordinate system (CSM) via transformation matrix A. The multi-hop derivation begins with the homogeneous rigid-body relation p_M = A p_C [PAGE 5], where A ∈ SE(3) encodes the camera’s translation o_C and rotation (i_C, j_C, k_C) relative to the magnetic tracker [PAGE 5]. Because both systems record in local frames, any comparison requires this transform. The paper identifies three failure modes that create a gray zone between theoretical sensitivity and empirical performance.

First, parameter measurement error corrupts A. The authors state the camera origin lies inside the body and cannot be measured directly; the metallic housing distorts the electromagnetic field; and erroneous orientation causes “significant deviations in results” [PAGE 5]. To circumvent direct sensing, they propose three control-point models: Model A (three points away from camera, 11 parameters), Model B (sensor on lens face, stereo setup, 10 parameters), and Model C (one point on camera, two on screen, 12 parameters) [PAGE 6-9]. Each model yields a different A, and hence a different alignment of the same vision trajectory into CSM.

Second, sensitivity analysis quantifies how parameter errors θ_l amplify into matrix elements a_uv via ∂a_uv/∂θ_l [PAGE 10]. The relative sensitivity S_l and upper limit S_MAX = Σ_l S_l [PAGE 10-11] are intended as robustness measures. In the experiment, S_MAX ranks C (1651.2) < B (1661.1) < A (2026.9), suggesting C is theoretically least error-prone [PAGE 16]. Yet the empirical RMS total tells the opposite story: Model A yields 20.5 mm RMS, while B and C yield 75.1 mm and 66.4 mm RMS [PAGE 16]. This is the central gray-zone trap: high nominal sensitivity does not guarantee poor realized error when the actual parameter values happen to align favorably. The authors concede that “a highly sensitive parameter with low actual numerical value can have less impact… than a parameter with low sensitivity and large numerical value” [PAGE 19].

Third, error separation is attempted analytically (four-point orthonormality criterion, Eq. 26) and statistically (Monte Carlo simulation of ΔA with 1000 runs) [PAGE 11-13]. The statistical method produces transformation error bounds (e.g., Model A: 2.5 to 11.6 mm RMS) and algorithm error bounds (17.1 to 20.5 mm RMS) [PAGE 18]. Breakdown regimes are explicit: Fastrak factory accuracy (0.8 mm RMS position, 0.15° RMS orientation) is valid only when the sensor is <75 cm from the transmitter [PAGE 2, PAGE 14]; beyond this, reference errors can no longer be ignored. A 1° orientation error induces 3.5 cm position error at 2 m [PAGE 4], and image quantization of ±0.5 pixel displaces control point T2 according to Eq. (7) [PAGE 8]. If control points become collinear or if the stereo optical axes are misaligned, the transformation becomes singular or ill-conditioned, invalidating the sensitivity ranking entirely.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind here concerns what an operator believes about the tracker’s error under crisis conditions, and how that belief regresses into habitual shortcuts. The crisis habit is “optimal fit” alignment: simply registering two trajectories by minimizing inter-point distances without modeling the coordinate transform [PAGE 4]. The paper explicitly warns this “completely ignores possible bias errors” and can mask consistent depth overestimation while still producing a close visual match. Under time pressure—such as the 7.5 fps maximum resolution or the 120–200 image-pair sequences captured per run [PAGE 14, PAGE 15]—an operator regresses to this fast-fit habit because measuring control points and computing A is slow and error-prone.

The counter-trap is the paper’s proposed method: measure camera pose indirectly via control points and compute one of models A, B, or C. However, this introduces a second-order metacognitive trap. Sensitivity tables (Table 4, Table 5) rank Model C as theoretically most robust (S_MAX = 1651.2 vs A = 2026.9) [PAGE 16]. An operator who recurses on sensitivity alone will select C. Yet empirically Model A produced the lowest total RMS (20.5 mm) because its actual parameter geometry compensated for its high nominal sensitivity [PAGE 16, PAGE 19]. Thus the operator’s model of “what is robust” is recursively inverted by the data: the most sensitive model performed best in this specific setup. The clock that bounds this recursion is experimental throughput: only three video sequences were captured, each 120–200 frames [PAGE 15], leaving insufficient trials to average out setup-specific luck. Consequently, any generalization from “Model A won here” to “Model A is universally superior” commits the reverse error of overfitting to a single parameter realization. The correct theory of mind holds that sensitivity ranking and empirical error are orthogonal criteria; neither subsumes the other without fixed parameters and a locked setup [PAGE 19].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The Fastrak specification of 0.8 mm RMS position and 0.15° RMS orientation is guaranteed only when the sensor is less than 75 cm from the magnetic transmitter [PAGE 2, PAGE 14]. In the experiment the camera was approximately 1 meter from the subject, but the magnetic sensor remained within the transmitter’s near field; had the subject moved beyond 75 cm, the reference data itself would carry errors comparable to or exceeding the vision algorithm’s error, vetoing the entire decomposition in Table 8 [PAGE 18]. Additionally, the metallic camera housing distorts the electromagnetic field, which Model A avoids by placing control points ~50 cm away, whereas Models B and C place sensors on the lens and screen and suffer slight distortion [PAGE 6, PAGE 19].

Biomechanical layer veto: The vision algorithm detects skin-colored regions and tracks 3D centroids with a Kalman filter [PAGE 14]. This presupposes visible, unoccluded skin tone and bounded hand motion along a predefined path [PAGE 14]. Rapid articulation, self-occlusion, or lighting changes that break color consistency would invalidate the vision trajectory independently of any transformation error; no amount of coordinate alignment can recover a trajectory the detector never estimated. The electromagnetic tracker’s occlusion insensitivity [PAGE 3] does not rescue the vision algorithm, because the reference and the test target measure different physical points unless the sensor is rigidly fixed to the hand [PAGE 14].

Weather/environmental layer veto: Although the experiment is indoor, the paper notes electromagnetic interference is severe in urban environments and decays rapidly with transmitter distance [PAGE 3]. In an uncontrolled electromagnetic environment (e.g., near power infrastructure, wireless transmitters, or other magnetic trackers), the reference trajectory p_M becomes stochastic with unknown bias. This vetoes the assumption that p_M is exact [PAGE 11], collapsing both the analytical and statistical error-separation methods. No recalibration of A can compensate for a reference that is itself drifting.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
