The first multi-hop move is Reed’s own: football “aspires to the condition of warfare” [PAGE 35 of 295], and “There are both strategy and tactics in football as in war—the season is a campaign, the big game a battle” [PAGE 13 of 295]. The second move is Reed’s use of military theory: Von Moltke’s strategy definition and du Teil’s “Concentration of the destructive elements on the decisive point” [PAGE 43 of 295] become, in football, play selection under field position, down, distance, wind, and personnel. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Define the game state as \(s=(x,d,y,\mathbf w,\mu,C(t),p)\), where \(x\) is field position in yards from the attacking team’s own goal line, \(d\in\{1,2,3,4\}\) is down, \(y\) is yards to go, \(\mathbf w\) is wind velocity in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\mu\) is dimensionless surface friction, \(C(t)\) is a condition state in condition-units, and \(p\) is personnel state. Let \(a\in\mathcal A(s)\) be a play call and \(G(a,s)\) be random gain in yards. The strategic operator is a Bellman-style scoring-value recursion:
\[
V(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal A(s)}\left\{\mathbb E[G(a,s)]+\mathbb E[V(s')\mid s,a]\right\},
\]
with \(V\) in scoring points. Assumptions: the state is Markov, the transition kernel is stationary within a rule regime, and personnel and weather are observed. Breakdown regimes: rule changes, hidden injury, unobserved fatigue, or nonstationary opponent adaptation make the kernel misspecified. Reed’s strategic language is consistent with this operator: “Strategy,” said Von Moltke, “is the practical adaptation of the means placed at a general’s disposal to the attainment of the object in view” [PAGE 43 of 295], while du Teil’s “Concentration of the destructive elements on the decisive point” [PAGE 43 of 295] is the local concentration principle inside the recursion.

The signal operator is an information channel. Let \(S\) be the signal sequence and \(A\) the intended play. Then
\[
H(S)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2 p_i\quad\text{bits},
\]
and
\[
I(A;S_{\mathrm{obs}})=H(A)-H(A\mid S_{\mathrm{obs}})\quad\text{bits}.
\]
Cognitive latency is modeled as
\[
C_{\mathrm{sig}}=n_{\mathrm{ops}}\tau_{\mathrm{op}}+\tau_{\mathrm{comm}}\quad\text{seconds},
\]
with the hard constraint \(C_{\mathrm{sig}}\leq \tau_{\mathrm{snap}}\). Reed’s “simplest signal system is the best” [PAGE 184 of 295] and “No signal system, I believe, should require a man actually to think” [PAGE 186 of 295] are exactly this constraint. Breakdown regimes: \(C_{\mathrm{sig}}>\tau_{\mathrm{snap}}\) produces delay, false starts, and misalignment; \(H(S)\to 0\) means the code is predictable; high channel noise makes \(I(A;S_{\mathrm{obs}})\) too small for reliable execution.

The shift/slide operator is a relative-motion control law. Let \(x_A(t)\) and \(x_D(t)\) be lateral positions in yards, with velocities \(v_A,v_D\) in \(\mathrm{yd\,s^{-1}}\). Then
\[
x_A(t+\tau)=x_A(t)+v_A\tau,\qquad
x_D(t+\tau)=x_D(t)+v_D\tau.
\]
The defensive slide maintains alignment when \(v_D>v_A\) and \(|\Delta x_D|\leq|\Delta x_A|\) over the snap window \(\tau\). Reed states the principle: the basic principle of shifts is “to catch the defense in lateral motion at the instant the ball is snapped” [PAGE 192 of 295], while the defense can “slide” faster than the attack can side-step [PAGE 197 of 295]. Breakdown regimes: timing jitter \(\sigma_\tau\) larger than the snap window, poor surface friction, or a weak defensive spot that cannot be reinforced. The “pairing” advantage is a local force problem: if local contact capacity is \(F_A\) and \(F_D\) in newtons, an opening requires \(F_A>F_D+F_{\mathrm{threshold}}\); if \(F_{\mathrm{threshold}}\) is unknown, the model is not identifiable.

The forward-pass operator is a joint-event probability. Let \(D\) be deception, \(L\) delay, and \(R\) receiver-open/protected. By the chain rule,
\[
P(C)=P(R\mid D,L)P(D\mid L)P(L),
\]
dimensionless. Reed’s “The elements of the forward pass are three—deception, delay, protection” [PAGE 213 of 295] is the design decomposition. Breakdown regimes: \(P(R\mid D,L)\) near zero because the defense covers all eligibles; protection failure; wind or wet ball reducing throw accuracy; or interception cost dominating expected gain. The pass is a threat as well as a play, so the value includes the defensive dispersion induced even when the pass is not thrown.

The kicking operator is expected field-position change:
\[
\mathbb E[\Delta x]=\mathbb E[K]-\mathbb E[R]-\mathbb E[P]\quad\text{yards},
\]
where \(K\) is kick distance, \(R\) is return, and \(P\) is penalty/coverage loss. Reed’s “it is far better to kick too much than run too much” [PAGE 220 of 295] is a conservative policy under uncertainty. Breakdown regimes: wind speed approaching or exceeding the kicker’s control envelope, wet surface, poor cleats, or a return threat that makes \(R\) large. Reed’s weather claim is explicit: “The wind is the most important factor in football generalship to-day” [PAGE 236 of 295].

The trainer/condition operator is a recovery-load ODE:
\[
\frac{dC}{dt}=r-\lambda C-\kappa L(t),
\]
with \(C\) in condition-units, \(L(t)\) in load-units per day, \(r\) in condition-units per day, and \(\lambda,\kappa\) as positive coefficients. The feasibility constraint is \(C(t)\geq C_{\min}\). Reed’s warning that overworked men become “mentally and physically ‘dead’” [PAGE 103 of 295] and his mid-season “slump” diagnosis that men are “overfootballed” [PAGE 105 of 295] are breakdown regimes: when the load term dominates recovery, the strategic plan is physically infeasible regardless of play-calling quality.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Reed’s lineage runs from nineteenth-century military theory into early twentieth-century football coaching and then, retrocausally, into modern operations research, information theory, Bayesian inference, and human-factors engineering. The military lineage is explicit: Von Moltke, du Teil, Wagner, and Napoleon are cited as sources for strategy, concentration, preparation, assault, and completion [PAGE 43 of 295; PAGE 47 of 295]. Reed’s three-phase translation of Wagner’s attack—preparation by long-range fire, assault at effective range, and resumption after the score—is a proto-operations-research sequence: shape the battlespace, concentrate force, then reorganize for the next cycle [PAGE 47 of 295]. The information lineage is later but structurally present: Reed’s signal chapter is a latency-and-complexity critique before Shannon. His “simplest signal system is the best” [PAGE 184 of 295] and his story of the injured player who answers signals correctly but cannot count goal posts [PAGE 187 of 295] are early human-factors observations: overlearned codes survive stress while deliberate arithmetic does not. The control lineage is also present: the shift-and-slide passage is a relative-velocity and timing problem, and the forward-pass passage is a decomposition into deception, delay, and protection [PAGE 213 of 295]. The recursive-game lineage appears in Reed’s future-strategy chapter, where innovation becomes “a case of matching coins all over again” [PAGE 268 of 295]. 

The multi-week traps are the places where retrocausal reading can go wrong. First, the analogy trap: battlefield metaphors are heuristic, not causal proof. Reed’s claim that football “aspires to the condition of warfare” [PAGE 35 of 295] does not imply that military doctrine transfers without modification; the text itself notes differences in terrain, supply, duration, and concealment [PAGE 41 of 295; PAGE 42 of 295]. Second, the rule-change trap: Reed writes after the radical changes of 1911 and during the forward-pass era, and he repeatedly refers to shifting rules, the restored quick kick, and the new open game [PAGE 53 of 295; PAGE 55 of 295; PAGE 56 of 295]. Any model that omits the rule regime will misread his advice. Third, the elite-sample trap: the book generalizes from Yale, Harvard, Princeton, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Minnesota, Carlisle, and a few schools; it is not a random sample of football. Fourth, the genius-attribution trap: Reed’s “genius” chapter celebrates Cooney, Poe, DeWitt, Hinkey, Coy, Brickley, and others [PAGE 270 of 295; PAGE 272 of 295; PAGE 279 of 295], but genius language often absorbs unmodeled variance—diagnosis speed, proprioception, social authority, and luck. Fifth, the social-capital trap: the “football family” and graduate coaching network are institutional assets, not playbook operators [PAGE 48 of 295; PAGE 63 of 295]. Sixth, the overfitting trap: Reed warns against “the premature use of the team’s ‘best stuff’” [PAGE 94 of 295] and against overtraining [PAGE 103 of 295]; both are multi-week failure modes where early success or excessive load corrupts later performance. These traps do not invalidate the source; they define the audit boundary.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Reed’s quarterback is a recursive opponent modeler under clock pressure. Formalize level-\(k\) reasoning: level-0 assigns a prior over opponent actions; level-1 best-responds to level-0; level-2 best-responds to level-1; and so on. Let \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) be available decision time, \(\tau_{\mathrm{exec}}\) execution time, and \(\tau_{\mathrm{model}}\) the time cost of one modeling increment. Then the maximum feasible depth is
\[
k_{\max}=\left\lfloor\frac{\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}-\tau_{\mathrm{exec}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{model}}}\right\rfloor,
\]
dimensionless. If \(k>k_{\max}\), the player is overmodeling and will either delay or regress. This matches Reed’s signal axiom: “No signal system, I believe, should require a man actually to think” [PAGE 186 of 295]. It also matches the starting-signal discussion, where the offense tries to gain a fraction of a second by starting with the ball [PAGE 187 of 295; PAGE 188 of 295]. The clock is not decorative; it bounds recursion.

Crisis habit regression is the first failure mode. Under stress, players revert to overlearned habits. Reed’s injured player answers signal sequences correctly while giving absurd answers to simple questions [PAGE 187 of 295], which is a precise image of habit preservation under cognitive degradation. The practical prediction is that complex level-2 or level-3 plans collapse before simple level-1 habits do. A coach should therefore train the simple key-number and starting-signal system to automaticity, not rely on in-game arithmetic. The counter-trap is the second failure mode: if the opponent expects the expected, the best response may be the unexpected. Reed’s future-strategy passage frames this as a guessing match: “Simply put, it is a case of matching coins all over again” [PAGE 268 of 295]. But counter-trap reasoning itself consumes clock and model depth. A team that tries to be unpredictable at too high a level may outsmart itself. The disciplined rule is: use counter-trap reasoning only when the clock permits \(k_{\max}\geq2\), the opponent model is supported by observed tendencies, and the execution cost of the surprise is low. Otherwise, use the trained habit. The third failure mode is clock collapse: when \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) shrinks, the optimal policy is not more recursion but lower-variance execution. Reed’s preference for simple formations and “a few sound formations with simple plays therefrom” [PAGE 93 of 295] is consistent with this. The recursive theory of mind therefore yields three testable predictions: high-stress conditions favor simpler signals and formations; counter-trap plays have higher variance and require more practice; and teams that overmodel under time pressure show more delay, misalignment, and procedural penalties.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Lower layers veto strategic plans when the state leaves the feasible set
\[
\mathcal F=\{s:C(t)\geq C_{\min},\ \mu\geq\mu_{\min},\ \|\mathbf w\|\leq W_{\max},\ \text{visibility}\geq V_{\min},\ \text{injury load}\leq I_{\max}\}.
\]
If \(s\notin\mathcal F\), then \(V(\pi\mid s_0)\) estimated under nominal conditions is out-of-distribution and the plan is infeasible. This is not a metaphor. Reed’s trainer chapter gives the proof by counterexample: on a muddy field, “all the generalship in the world and the finest of individual play, will not serve to win the game” [PAGE 131 of 295]. The biomechanical veto is explicit in the friction constraint. The maximum horizontal force before slipping is
\[
F_{\max}=\mu m g\quad\text{newtons},
\]
where \(m\) is mass in kilograms, \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and \(\mu\) is dimensionless. If the required cutting or braking force \(F_{\mathrm{req}}>\mu m g\), the player slips, and any shift, cut, or tackle that depends on that force is vetoed. Reed’s cleat passage is a direct operational response to this inequality [PAGE 131 of 295]. The weather veto is similarly concrete: wind affects kick distance, hang time, and return coverage; Reed calls wind “the most important factor in football generalship to-day” [PAGE 236 of 295]. The 1911 Yale-Princeton mud example, where “the condition under foot reminded one of the first week’s work on the Panama Canal” [PAGE 237 of 295], is a verified source case where a drop-kicking plan was physically undermined. The condition veto is the ODE constraint from the trainer operator: if \(C(t)<C_{\min}\), the player cannot sustain the required repeated efforts. Reed’s warning against overtraining and his “slump” diagnosis are the source’s own veto language [PAGE 103 of 295; PAGE 105 of 295]. The adjudication rule is: strategy may choose among feasible plans, but it may not overrule a failed physical, biomechanical, or weather constraint. When the constraint fails, the correct response is to simplify, shorten the play menu, change cleats or coverage, reduce load, or shift to a lower-variance plan—not to demand more “generalship.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
