Multi-hop derivation and gray-zone audit. The source chain is explicit: static calisthenics evaluation depends on the duration of a hold, because “The evaluation of static skills is based on their difficulty level and the duration of the hold” [PAGE 1]. Automated support therefore requires temporal segmentation of a video into skill and non-skill intervals, because “Automated tools able to recognize isometric skills from a video by segmenting them to estimate their duration would be desirable to assist athletes in their training and judges during competitions” [PAGE 1]. The observable used by the paper is the athlete’s 2-D body pose: “we extracted the spatial coordinates of the athletes’ joints in the videos using OpenPose (Cao et al., 2019)” [PAGE 2]. The feature construction is 25 joints times three values, X, Y, and confidence, giving 75 normalized numerical features per frame [PAGE 3]. The classifier is a 10-way MLP: nine skills plus “NONE,” because “The output layer has 10 nodes, corresponding to the 9 skill classes, plus an additional ‘NONE’ background class” [PAGE 4]. Temporal reasoning then refines the per-frame labels either by a heuristic sliding-window mode pipeline or by a Markov/Viterbi probabilistic model.

The heuristic operator contains a non-trivial scale derivation. The paper defines “the base window size is defined as: wb = ⌊((1−s)·m⌋ with m = 32” and then defines \(s\) as a weighted sum of same-label and different-label transitions, with \(up=0.14/n\) and \(dw=-0.11/n\) [PAGE 4]. The expression as printed has a missing closing bracket, so the operational reading must be \(w_b=\lfloor(1-s)m\rfloor\). The units are frames: \(m=32\) frames, \(n\) is the number of frames, \(s\) is dimensionless, and \(w_b\) is dimensionless but interpreted as a frame count. If every adjacent pair is equal, \(s\approx0.5+0.14=0.64\), so \(w_b\approx11\); if every adjacent pair differs, \(s\approx0.5-0.11=0.39\), so \(w_b\approx19\). The paper states that this “adjusts the window range from [0.39,0.64] to [11,19]” [PAGE 4]. At 24 frames per second, that is roughly 0.458 s to 0.792 s. This is not a harmless smoothing constant: the shortest video in the dataset lasts 0.83 s [PAGE 3], about 19.9 frames at 24 fps. A base window of 11–19 frames can therefore cover nearly the entire shortest hold. That is a real breakdown regime for boundary estimation.

The probabilistic operator has an exact degeneracy that the paper’s bound hides. The model is \(P(L|F)\propto\prod_{i=2}^{n}P(y_i|y_{i-1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(y_i|f_i)\), with transition probability \(\varepsilon\) for a label change and \(1-M\varepsilon\) for persistence, and the paper states “The constant ε is hence defined as: ε ≤ 1/(M+1)” [PAGE 5]. For a sequence with \(T\) transitions, the transition factor is \((1-M\varepsilon)^{n-1-T}\varepsilon^{T}\). If \(\varepsilon=1/(M+1)\), then same-label and different-label transitions both have probability \(1/(M+1)\), so the product over all adjacent pairs is constant. Viterbi then reduces exactly to per-frame argmax; there is no temporal smoothing left. If \(\varepsilon=0\), no transitions are allowed. If \(\varepsilon>1/M\), the same-label probability becomes negative, which is invalid. If \(1/(M+1)<\varepsilon<1/M\), the model actually penalizes persistence. Thus the “smoothing” regime is \(0<\varepsilon<1/(M+1)\), and the paper’s upper bound is precisely the no-smoothing point, not merely a numerical convenience.

The empirical audit is consistent with this reading. Table 2 reports probabilistic mASF1 0.674, heuristic 0.631, and raw 0.114 [PAGE 7]. Temporal reasoning is doing most of the work, but the per-class failures are diagnostic: probabilistic Iron Cross is 0.847, while One Arm Handstand is 0.492 and NONE is 0.476 [PAGE 7]. The paper explains the Iron Cross advantage by pose visibility: “Iron Cross is a vertical skill and it is not upside down, with full visible body (without limbs overlapping). One Arm Handstand, on the other hand, is the most challenging because of the upside down body position” [PAGE 6]. The edge audit is also explicit: “frames closer to the edges of the segments generally have a lower accuracy than those with a more centered position” because of “higher human and model uncertainty when classifying frames representing a transition” [PAGE 6]. The gray-zone is therefore not the existence of the pipeline; it is the inferential leap from clip-level segmentation scores to judge-grade hold duration. Random video splitting is one leakage risk: “We divided the dataset randomly assigning 80% of the videos to the training split and 20% of the videos to the test split” [PAGE 4]. With crawled social-media and ad-hoc recordings, the same athlete, gym, background, camera, or recording session can appear in both splits. The paper does not report athlete-grouped or scene-grouped splits. Also, OpenPose is run with “number people max” set to 1, which “does not ensure that the recognized person is the athlete in a multi-person scene” [PAGE 3]. Those are sufficient reasons to treat the reported 0.674 as a feasibility result, not as an adjudicated timing proof.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is a stack of borrowings. OpenPose descends from part-affinity-field 2-D multi-person pose estimation [PAGE 2, PAGE 3], skeleton-based human action recognition supplies the idea that joint trajectories are the relevant signal, and temporal action segmentation supplies the boundary problem. The paper explicitly follows the factorization of Furnari et al. 2018: “We base our experiments on the approach presented in (Furnari et al., 2018), which factorizes temporal video segmentation into per-frame processing and probabilistic temporal reasoning on top of per-frame predictions” [PAGE 2]. That lineage brings a hidden prior: per-frame classifiers are noisy, so a transition penalty should clean the timeline. The Viterbi/Markov layer is therefore not a neutral post-processor; it encodes a belief that labels persist. That belief is exactly what can over-merge two distinct holds or extend a failed skill beyond its true end.

Retrocausal traps are visible in the dataset construction. The paper uses crawled and ad-hoc recordings, then splits videos randomly 80/20 [PAGE 4]. If the same athlete appears in a training and test video, the test set is partly a memory test for body shape, clothing, background, and habitual pose. If the same recording session is split across videos, camera angle and lighting leak. If the same background appears, the classifier can learn scene context even though the paper’s stated motivation is pose. A grouped split by athlete, session, source URL, and scene would be a stronger retrocausal control. The paper does not report it. A second trap is label provenance: temporal boundaries are manually labeled, but the paper does not report inter-annotator agreement or a boundary tolerance protocol. The metric ASF1 averages over thresholds, which can mask a systematic boundary lag: a method can score well at loose thresholds while being late at strict thresholds. A third trap is class imbalance and the NONE class. NONE is not a skill, but it is a class in the 10-way output [PAGE 4] and has probabilistic ASF1 0.476 [PAGE 7]. Low NONE performance directly threatens duration estimates, because the start of the hold is a NONE-to-skill transition and the end is a skill-to-NONE transition.

Multi-week traps. Over a multi-week training block, athletes change technique, fatigue tolerance, body composition, and camera placement. A model trained on short social-media clips can learn the editing style of a source rather than the skill. Over a multi-week competition cycle, judges may apply different hold criteria or athletes may adopt variants of a skill. The dataset’s 839 videos and 4,826.79 seconds [PAGE 3] are not a longitudinal sample; they are a cross-sectional crawl. The shortest video is 0.83 s and the longest is 27.83 s [PAGE 3], so the window scale and the Viterbi persistence prior are being asked to operate across nearly two orders of duration. The correct genealogical conclusion is that this paper is an initial feasibility study, not a validated measurement instrument.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under fatigue, occlusion, or unusual camera angle, an athlete’s pose can regress to compensatory habits: shoulder elevation, elbow flexion, hip piking, or torso rotation. The MLP sees only joints and confidence, so it can classify the compensation rather than the intended skill. The paper’s per-class result is a concrete instance: Iron Cross is high because it is vertical and fully visible, while One Arm Handstand is low because it is upside down [PAGE 6]. In recursive terms, the athlete intends a skill, the judge recognizes a hold boundary, and the model predicts a label sequence. The model’s “theory” of the athlete is only a pose-likelihood model; it has no explicit intention state. When the pose distribution shifts, the model’s habit regression is to assign the nearest trained class or NONE. That is a crisis behavior, not a semantic understanding of the athlete’s plan.

Counter-trap. The natural counter-trap is to increase temporal smoothing. A low transition probability \(\varepsilon\) makes persistence cheap, so Viterbi suppresses flicker. But the same prior can merge two separate skills, extend a hold beyond the true release, or hide a brief legitimate hold. At \(\varepsilon=1/(M+1)\), the transition factor is constant and the model has no smoothing at all [PAGE 5 derivation]. At very small \(\varepsilon\), the model can collapse to one label for a long span. The counter-trap is therefore to treat smoothing as a solution rather than as a bias-variance tradeoff. A better counter-trap is abstention: if pose confidence is low or the skill duration is below the window resolution, the system should output “not adjudicable” rather than a duration.

Clock. Duration is \(\hat d=\hat t_{\text{end}}-\hat t_{\text{start}}\), with error \(\Delta d=\Delta t_{\text{end}}-\Delta t_{\text{start}}\). If smoothing delays both boundaries equally, the duration error cancels; if it delays the start more than the end, the hold is underestimated; if it delays the end more, it is overestimated. At 24 fps, one frame is \(1/24\) s ≈ 41.7 ms. The heuristic window is 11–19 frames, or 0.458–0.792 s, and the shortest video is 0.83 s [PAGE 3, PAGE 4]. The clock layer therefore has a resolution floor: boundary errors of one window are comparable to the entire shortest hold. Recursive theory of mind must include the judge’s tolerance: if the judge’s start/end convention differs from the model’s persistence prior, the model will be systematically early or late even when its frame accuracy is high. The paper’s edge-accuracy result [PAGE 6] is exactly a clock-layer warning.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. Let \(c_{i,j}\in[0,1]\) be the OpenPose confidence for joint \(j\) at frame \(i\), and let \(J_{\text{crit}}\) be the joints required by the claimed skill, for example wrists, elbows, shoulders, hips, knees, and ankles for levers and planches. Define the critical-joint minimum confidence \(c_i^{\min}=\min_{j\in J_{\text{crit}}}c_{i,j}\). A physical veto is
\[
V_{\text{pose}}(i)=1\ \text{if }c_i^{\min}\lt \tau_c\ \text{or if any }j\in J_{\text{crit}}\text{ is missing}.
\]
The paper does not supply \(\tau_c\); it must be calibrated on a validation set. The proof is data sufficiency: if a critical joint is unobserved or low-confidence, the 75-dimensional pose vector does not identify the body configuration, so the skill label is not causally attributable to the athlete’s pose. This is a sufficient veto, not a claim that every low-confidence frame is wrong.

Biomechanical veto. The paper gives a directional proof: Iron Cross is “a vertical skill and it is not upside down, with full visible body (without limbs overlapping),” while One Arm Handstand is “the most challenging because of the upside down body position” [PAGE 6]. If the skill requires an inverted body and the head, shoulders, or support arm are occluded, OpenPose’s 2-D skeleton is ambiguous. A biomechanical veto is
\[
V_{\text{biomech}}=1\ \text{if the claimed skill requires inversion and the inverted-chain joints are occluded or overlapping beyond the calibrated tolerance}.
\]
This veto directly explains the per-class gap: probabilistic Iron Cross 0.847 versus One Arm Handstand 0.492 [PAGE 7]. Any duration output for an inverted skill with occluded support chain should be rejected or downgraded.

Environmental and weather veto. The dataset includes “outdoor areas such as parks or streets, as well as indoor locations like gyms or the athletes’ homes” [PAGE 3]. Outdoor rain, wind, loose clothing, moving backgrounds, and lighting changes can corrupt pose extraction. The paper sets “number people max” to 1 and warns that this “does not ensure that the recognized person is the athlete in a multi-person scene” [PAGE 3]. An environmental veto is
\[
V_{\text{env}}=1\ \text{if multi-person scene and athlete identity is not tracked, or if background motion or lighting invalidates the pose confidence model}.
\]
The proof is identity: if the selected skeleton belongs to a background person, the features are not the athlete’s features, so the segmentation is invalid regardless of classifier confidence.

Clock and camera veto. The videos are 960×540 pixels at 24 fps [PAGE 3]. The heuristic base window is 11–19 frames [PAGE 4], and the shortest video is 0.83 s, about 19.9 frames [PAGE 3]. Define \(N_{\text{skill}}\) as the number of frames in the claimed skill. A resolution veto is
\[
V_{\text{clock}}=1\ \text{if }N_{\text{skill}}\lt 2w_b\ \text{or if the boundary uncertainty exceeds the judge’s tolerance}.
\]
The proof is observability: if the window length is comparable to the hold length, the sliding-window mode and the transition prior cannot separately resolve start and end. Final adjudication should accept a duration only when
\[
A=1\ \text{iff }V_{\text{pose}}=0,\ V_{\text{biomech}}=0,\ V_{\text{env}}=0,\ V_{\text{clock}}=0.
\]
Otherwise the system should abstain. This is a conservative veto algebra: it does not prove correctness, but it proves that a rejected segment lacks sufficient physical, biomechanical, environmental, or temporal evidence.
