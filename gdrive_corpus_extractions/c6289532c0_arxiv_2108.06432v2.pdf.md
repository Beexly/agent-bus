The paper must be read as a probabilistic morphological segmentation and geometric classification system, not as a conventional detector that assumes straightness, fixed line counts, or clean edges. The central derivation chain is: input RGB image, grayscale contrast enhancement, stochastic watershed aggregation, thresholded boundary mask, connected-region extraction, primitive fitting, merging, refinement, and finally evaluation by pixel-level and object-level metrics. Each step introduces a distinct failure mode, and the paper’s design choices are best understood as countermeasures to those failures.

First, the authors observe that simple brightness is insufficient because grass texture and capture noise create false edges [PAGE 8]. They therefore use a Top-Hat transform to preserve bright thin structures. The formal operator is \(X_{TH}=X-X\circ e_{sd}\), where \(X\) is a grayscale image, \(\circ\) is morphological opening, and \(e_{sd}\) is a structuring element whose diameter must be slightly larger than the maximum line width [PAGE 8]. In the database, line width is typically under 10 pixels, so an 11-pixel structuring element is used [PAGE 8]. This is not a toy heuristic: it is a morphological scale-selection rule. If the structuring element is too small, line-like bright bands survive but thick non-line bright regions may also survive; if too large, thin line marks may be suppressed. The white-line constraint is then enforced by \(I_{GS}=\min(R_{TH},G_{TH},B_{TH})\), so that a pixel must be bright in all three channels, reducing non-white false positives [PAGE 8].

Second, the watershed stage must handle occlusion and non-straightness. A single watershed segmentation is unstable because small changes in seeds can change boundaries, especially when players join regions that a human would separate [PAGE 4]. The authors therefore repeat the seeded watershed many times. If \(Z_i(r,c)\in\{0,1\}\) denotes the indicator that pixel \((r,c)\) lies on a watershed line in experiment \(i\), then the aggregated image is
\[
I_{RWS}(r,c)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z_i(r,c).
\]
The paper explicitly interprets this value as akin to the probability of a pixel being a true watershed line [PAGE 6]. Under an independence approximation, if \(Z_i(r,c)\) is Bernoulli with parameter \(p(r,c)\in[0,1]\), then \(\mathbb{E}[I_{RWS}]=p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(I_{RWS})=p(1-p)/M\). This gives a formal breakdown regime: if experiments are highly correlated, for example because the seed lattice origin is fixed, the variance does not fall as \(1/M\) and lattice-shaped artifacts appear [PAGE 7]. The proposed windowed random seed generation breaks this correlation by giving each experiment a different random lattice origin [PAGE 7].

Third, the threshold stage is a hard decision rule:
\[
M_B(r,c)=1 \quad \text{iff} \quad I_{RWS}(r,c)\ge T_{RWS} \text{ and } M_{PF}(r,c)=1,
\]
otherwise \(M_B(r,c)=0\). The playing-field mask \(M_{PF}\) is a geometric veto: no matter how line-like a pixel is outside the pitch, it is rejected [PAGE 6]. The authors report \(T_{RWS}=0.8\) and claim low sensitivity around this value [PAGE 6]. However, the gray zone is clear: a high threshold suppresses weak lines in shadow or distance, while a low threshold admits grass texture, players, goals, and broadcast artifacts.

Fourth, classification is performed after removing line intersections and extracting connected regions. The region set is \(R=\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{N_R}\). Each region is tested against two primitive models: ellipse and straight line. The ellipse fit uses least squares [PAGE 9], while the line fit uses Deming regression because both coordinates may be noisy [PAGE 9]. The initial acceptance rule is RMSE-based: a line fit is accurate enough when RMSE is lower than 2 [PAGE 10]. Small regions, such as those with fewer than 50 pixels, are initially unassigned because they are not representative enough [PAGE 10]. This is a major gray-zone source: short penalty-arc fragments, distant touchline fragments, or partially occluded center-circle fragments can be discarded even if they are physically true line marks.

Fifth, merging relaxes the RMSE criterion to allow radial distortion and long segments. For straight-line merging, maximum RMSE is allowed to be 4 px; for ellipse merging, the resulting fit RMSE must be at most 4 [PAGE 10]. Refinement then removes pixels whose distance to the estimated model exceeds the same 4-pixel criterion [PAGE 10]. This creates a two-scale tolerance structure: strict 2-pixel acceptance for initial primitive evidence, looser 4-pixel tolerance for structural merging and outlier removal. The formal singularity regime appears when a region is too short, too noisy, or too occluded to produce a stable primitive model. If the ellipse design matrix is rank-deficient, for example because points are collinear, duplicated, or fewer than five nondegenerate points are available, the ellipse fit becomes undefined. If a region contains only a tiny fragment of a curved arc, its local curvature may be indistinguishable from a straight line, causing ellipse underdetection or straight-line false positives [PAGE 15].

The empirical results support this derivation chain. Segmentation achieves overall F-score 0.97 [PAGE 13]. False positives are mainly due to white kits and white goal elements; false negatives are mainly due to occlusion or distant low-contrast lines [PAGE 14]. Object-level straight-line results show 402 ground-truth straight lines, 22 misdetected, and 59 false detections, many from the straightest visible parts of penalty arcs being misclassified as small straight lines [PAGE 15]. Ellipses show 71 ground-truth objects, 12 misdetected, and no false detections [PAGE 15]. The Hough baseline requires a target line count; low counts miss lines, high counts create false lines, and even the best global configuration reaches F-score 0.78, while the proposed method reaches 0.90 for straight-line object detection [PAGE 16]. This is the core gray-zone conclusion: the proposed method is not a magic invariance detector; it is a robust probabilistic and morphological compromise whose residual errors are concentrated in short, occluded, low-contrast, or geometrically ambiguous regions.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal operators can be separated into seed generation, stochastic watershed aggregation, morphological preprocessing, thresholded segmentation, geometric fitting, and evaluation metrics. All coordinates are in pixels. Probability-like values are dimensionless. Grayscale values may be normalized to \([0,1]\) or represented as integer intensities, but the thresholding logic is scale-invariant once normalized.

1. Uniform seed generation. The first possible seed set is
\[
S_i=\left\{s_{i,j}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{i,j}\\ c_{i,j}\end{bmatrix} : r_{i,j}=U(1,H),\; c_{i,j}=U(1,W),\; j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\right\},
\]
where \(H\) is image height in rows, \(W\) is image width in columns, \(N\) is the number of seeds, and \(U(a,b)\) is a uniform draw from the interval or discrete range specified by context [PAGE 5]. This operator assumes that random coverage will eventually sample all regions. Its breakdown regime is small \(N\): uncovered regions produce missing watershed boundaries and therefore line misdetection [PAGE 6].

2. Stochastic watershed aggregation. Let
\[
\operatorname{SeededWS}(I_{GS},S_i)\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}
\]
be the binary watershed-line image for experiment \(i\). The aggregated stochastic watershed image is
\[
I_{RWS}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\operatorname{SeededWS}(I_{GS},S_i),
\]
where \(M\) is the number of experiments [PAGE 5]. Pixel values in \(I_{RWS}\) lie in \([0,1]\) and are interpreted as empirical probabilities of being a true watershed line [PAGE 6]. If \(Z_i(r,c)\in\{0,1\}\) is the Bernoulli indicator for pixel \((r,c)\) in experiment \(i\), and the experiments are independent, then
\[
\mathbb{E}[I_{RWS}(r,c)]=p(r,c),
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(I_{RWS}(r,c))=\frac{p(r,c)(1-p(r,c))}{M}.
\]
This gives a principled convergence regime: variance decreases with \(M\) only if seed sets are sufficiently decorrelated. Fixed lattice origins violate this assumption and produce structured artifacts in flat regions [PAGE 7].

3. Windowed random seed generation. To guarantee coverage, the image is divided into \(N_r\times N_c\) rectangular cells. Assuming divisibility for notational simplicity, the seed coordinates are
\[
r_{i,j,k}=1+\operatorname{mod}\left(r_{0,i}+j\frac{H}{N_r}+U\left(1,\frac{H}{N_r}\right),H\right),
\]
\[
c_{i,j,k}=1+\operatorname{mod}\left(c_{0,i}+k\frac{W}{N_c}+U\left(1,\frac{W}{N_c}\right),W\right),
\]
with random lattice origins
\[
r_{0,i}=U\left(1,\frac{H}{N_r}\right),
\qquad
c_{0,i}=U\left(1,\frac{W}{N_c}\right),
\]
for \(j\in\{0,\ldots,N_r-1\}\) and \(k\in\{0,\ldots,N_c-1\}\) [PAGE 7]. Units: \(r_{i,j,k}\) and \(c_{i,j,k}\) are pixel row and column indices. Breakdown regime: if \(N_r=0\) or \(N_c=0\), the operator is undefined; if \(H\) or \(W\) is not divisible by \(N_r\) or \(N_c\), boundary cells require a non-simplified modulo implementation; if \(N_rN_c\) is too small relative to the number of spatial regions in the pitch image, some regions may still receive insufficient seed coverage.

4. Thresholded boundary mask. The final segmentation mask is defined by
\[
M_B(r,c)=
\begin{cases}
1, & I_{RWS}(r,c)\ge T_{RWS}\ \text{and}\ M_{PF}(r,c)=1,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
The paper uses \(T_{RWS}=0.8\) in all reported experiments [PAGE 6]. Units: \(T_{RWS}\) is dimensionless because it thresholds an empirical probability. Breakdown regimes: if \(T_{RWS}\) is too high, weak true lines are removed; if too low, spurious watershed lines from texture, players, or goals survive. The playing-field mask \(M_{PF}\) is a binary veto operator; if \(M_{PF}(r,c)=0\), then \(M_B(r,c)=0\) regardless of \(I_{RWS}\).

5. Top-Hat preprocessing. For a grayscale image \(X\),
\[
X_{TH}=X-X\circ e_{sd},
\]
where \(\circ\) is morphological opening and \(e_{sd}\) is a structuring element whose diameter must be slightly greater than the maximum width of the features to be preserved [PAGE 8]. In the reported database, line width is typically under 10 pixels, so the structuring element diameter is 11 pixels [PAGE 8]. For white line markings, the three channels are combined as
\[
I_{GS}=\min(R_{TH},G_{TH},B_{TH}),
\]
where \(R_{TH},G_{TH},B_{TH}\) are Top-Hat responses in the red, green, and blue channels [PAGE 8]. Breakdown regime: if line width exceeds the structuring-element diameter, line marks may be treated as background and lost; if the structuring element is too small, thick bright non-line objects may remain. If illumination changes abruptly, local thresholding can create false contours, which is why the paper uses Top-Hat instead of naive local thresholding [PAGE 8].

6. Connected-region segmentation. After removing line intersections, defined as line points with more than two neighbors, the mask \(M_B\) yields connected regions
\[
R=\{r_i\}_{i=1}^{N_R}.
\]
The paper’s example reports \(N_R=26\) connected regions before classification [PAGE 9]. Breakdown regime: if intersections are removed incorrectly, a single continuous line may be split into too many regions; if occlusion merges two distinct structures, the resulting region may fail to fit either primitive cleanly.

7. Ellipse fitting operator. The paper applies the least-squares ellipse-fitting algorithm in [36] [PAGE 9]. A standard algebraic conic model is
\[
q^{T}u(x,y)=0,
\]
where
\[
q=[a,b,c,d,e,f]^{T},
\qquad
u(x,y)=[x^{2},xy,y^{2},x,y,1]^{T}.
\]
The least-squares problem is
\[
\min_q \sum_{m=1}^{n}\left(q^{T}u(x_m,y_m)\right)^2
\]
subject to a scale constraint to avoid the trivial solution \(q=0\). Units: \(x_m,y_m\) are in pixels; \(x_m^2\), \(x_my_m\), and \(y_m^2\) are in square pixels; \(x_m\), \(y_m\), and \(1\) are in pixels or dimensionless; the coefficients in \(q\) carry reciprocal units so that each term is homogeneous. Breakdown regimes: fewer than five nondegenerate points; collinear points; duplicate points; nearly collinear arcs; or a rank-deficient design matrix. The paper notes that ellipses are difficult when only short arcs are visible, especially for penalty arcs or incomplete central circles [PAGE 4][PAGE 15].

8. Straight-line fitting operator. The paper uses Deming regression for straight-line fitting because errors may exist in both coordinates [PAGE 9]. For a line represented in normal form,
\[
\mathbf{n}^{T}\mathbf{x}=d,
\]
where \(\mathbf{n}\) is a unit normal vector and \(d\) is a scalar offset, Deming regression can be understood as an errors-in-variables fit that minimizes weighted orthogonal residuals when both \(x\) and \(y\) are noisy. Units: residuals and RMSE are in pixels. Breakdown regime: if the region contains only one or two points, line orientation is undefined; if the region is strongly curved, a line fit may have high RMSE and trigger subdivision or rejection.

9. RMSE acceptance operators. The paper does not print the RMSE formula, but the operative definition is root mean square residual distance:
\[
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}d_m^2},
\]
where \(d_m\) is the residual distance of point \(m\) to the fitted model, in pixels. The initial classification rule says a fit is accurate enough when RMSE is lower than 2 [PAGE 10]. Merging relaxes this to a maximum RMSE of 4 px for straight lines and at most 4 for ellipse merging [PAGE 10]. Refinement removes pixels whose distance to the estimated model is greater than 4 [PAGE 10]. Breakdown regime: if \(n=0\), RMSE is undefined; if all points are identical, model orientation may be non-unique; if residuals are dominated by occlusion or partial primitive fragments, RMSE thresholds can discard true structure.

10. Evaluation operators. The metrics are
\[
\operatorname{rec}=\frac{tp}{tp+fn},
\qquad
\operatorname{pre}=\frac{tp}{tp+fp},
\qquad
F=\frac{2tp}{2tp+fp+fn}.
\]
All metrics are dimensionless and lie in \([0,1]\) [PAGE 12]. The paper reports overall segmentation F-score 0.97 [PAGE 13]. It also reports object-level straight-line detection: 402 straight lines, 22 misdetected, 59 false detections; ellipse detection: 71 ellipses, 12 misdetected, no false detections [PAGE 15]. The Hough comparison states that the best target-line setting is 8 lines per image, with F-score 0.78, while the proposed straight-line object F-score is 0.90 [PAGE 16].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of the paper is not a single invention but a convergence of several older traditions. The watershed transform itself descends from terrain modeling. The paper cites J. C. Maxwell, “On hills and dales,” as an early conceptual source for watershed-like reasoning [PAGE 18]. In classical watershed segmentation, a grayscale image is interpreted as elevation, and catchment basins are separated by ridge lines. The paper explicitly states that the usual purpose of a watershed transform is to segment regions separated by higher ground [PAGE 4]. However, the authors invert the objective: they are not interested in regions themselves but in the boundaries that delimit them [PAGE 4].

A second lineage is stochastic watershed. The paper builds on Angulo and Jeulin’s stochastic watershed segmentation [PAGE 2][PAGE 18]. Instead of relying on a single deterministic seed placement, the method repeats seeded watershed experiments with random seed sets and averages the resulting boundaries. The key conceptual shift is from “exact region segmentation” to “stable boundary evidence.” The paper says that spurious lines change across experiments, but true lines arise again, allowing reliable detection by averaging [PAGE 5]. This is a historical move from deterministic morphology to Monte Carlo morphological evidence.

A third lineage is mathematical morphology for thin bright structures. The Top-Hat transform is attributed to F. Meyer [PAGE 8][PAGE 18]. The paper uses it to preserve thin bright features while suppressing background and thick structures. This connects to earlier field-line detection methods that used edge detectors, Top-Hat transforms, or morphological operations [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. The historical trap is that edge detectors such as Sobel, Canny, or LoG require careful thresholds and are sensitive to grass texture, shadows, and players [PAGE 2][PAGE 3]. The paper’s related-work review states that simple methods have “the important drawback of the correct selection of a threshold” [PAGE 3].

A fourth lineage is geometric primitive fitting. Straight lines have traditionally been detected with the Hough transform [PAGE 3]. Ellipses or circles have been detected with Hough variants, least-squares fitting, or model-based methods [PAGE 4]. The paper cites Fitzgibbon, Pilu, and Fisher’s direct least-squares ellipse fitting [PAGE 9][PAGE 18] and Deming regression for line fitting [PAGE 9][PAGE 18]. The historical trap is that Hough methods require the number of lines to be specified, are sensitive to false edges, and produce duplicate lines under radial distortion [PAGE 3]. The paper’s Hough comparison demonstrates this directly: low target counts miss lines, high target counts create false lines, and the best global setting reaches F-score 0.78 [PAGE 16].

A fifth lineage is random sampling and coverage. The proposed windowed seed generation resembles Poisson-disk sampling in its goal of bounded seed separation, but the authors claim lower computational cost and better parallelizability [PAGE 7]. The paper cites Bridson’s fast Poisson-disk sampling [PAGE 7][PAGE 18]. The historical trap is naive uniform sampling: it can leave image regions uncovered, causing line misdetection [PAGE 6]. The proposed lattice-plus-random-origin method is a multi-week-style engineering trap avoidance: fixed lattices create visible artifacts, while fully uniform sampling requires many experiments.

A sixth lineage is camera calibration and field registration. The paper frames line-mark detection as a precursor to registering images to a playing-field model and calibrating cameras [PAGE 1][PAGE 2]. Historically, many methods assumed known camera position, master-camera geometry, or vanishing-point structures [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. The paper’s contribution is to avoid those assumptions at the low-level segmentation stage. It leaves viewpoint, distribution, and higher-level geometric interpretation to later stages [PAGE 16]. This retro-causal genealogy explains why the method emphasizes robustness to radial distortion and viewpoint variation: the downstream registration stage cannot recover if the initial line mask is biased by straightness assumptions.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer here concerns nested models between the image, the algorithm, the pitch geometry, and the human broadcast viewer. At level zero, the image contains raw pixel intensities. At level one, the segmentation algorithm models the image as a terrain of bright thin structures separated by darker grass. At level two, the stochastic watershed models the uncertainty of level-one boundaries by imagining many possible seed configurations. At level three, the classifier models the segmented regions as evidence for geometric primitives, namely straight lines and ellipses. At level four, the broadcast or analytics system models the primitives as field markings that can later support camera calibration, field registration, player tracking, or tactical analysis.

A crisis habit regression occurs when difficult visual conditions force the pipeline back into older, brittle habits. For example, when lighting is harsh or half the field is shadowed, a classical pipeline may regress to a fixed local threshold or a Hough search with a predetermined line count. The paper warns that low thresholds create false detections from grass texture and players, while high thresholds miss low-contrast lines [PAGE 3]. It also warns that Hough methods are sensitive to false edges and require setting the number of lines [PAGE 3]. In a crisis, an operator may habitually choose a “safe” threshold or a “standard” line count, but that regression is exactly the trap. The paper’s evidence shows that Sobel, LoG, and Top-Hat methods lack a universally good precision-recall balance across the dataset [PAGE 14].

The counter-trap is the proposed stochastic watershed plus windowed seeding plus joint primitive classification. The stochastic watershed models boundary uncertainty instead of forcing one deterministic edge map. The windowed seed generator models spatial coverage instead of hoping that uniform random seeds cover all regions. The joint classification models both straight lines and ellipses together, which is important because a short elliptic arc may locally look like a straight line. The paper notes that parts of ellipses may locally fit a straight line, and only multiple regions considered together can reveal their elliptic correspondence [PAGE 10]. This is a recursive counter-model: the classifier must consider that its own local line hypothesis may be wrong at a larger structural scale.

The clock variable is computational convergence. The paper compares uniform seeding with windowed random seeding and reports that a small number of experiments, \(M=20\), already yields results comparable to uniform seeding with \(M=200\) [PAGE 7]. This is not a cosmetic runtime detail. It is a recursive timing model: the algorithm must converge before broadcast latency, camera motion, or downstream tracking becomes stale. If the seed distribution is poorly covered, the clock forces more experiments, increasing cost. If the seed distribution is well covered, convergence can be reached with fewer experiments. The random lattice origin is also a clock-related counter-trap: fixed lattice origins create patterned artifacts, while random origins reduce temporal correlation between experiments [PAGE 7].

The deepest recursive risk is that the classifier may “believe” a false primitive because the segmentation stage has already removed or weakened the evidence needed to disambiguate it. For example, a penalty arc may be partially occluded by players. The visible fragment may be short and locally straight. The classifier may then assign it to a straight line rather than an ellipse. The paper reports that many false straight-line detections come from the straightest parts of ellipses, especially penalty arcs, being wrongly classified as small straight lines [PAGE 15]. A robust theory-of-mind system would therefore maintain a counterfactual model: “If this short segment were part of an ellipse, would a larger elliptic fit reduce RMSE below the merging threshold?” The paper’s merging and refinement steps approximate this by considering multiple regions together and by allowing straight regions to be reclassified as elliptic during ellipse merging [PAGE 10].

Thus, crisis regression is the tendency to return to fixed thresholds, edge detectors, or Hough line counts under stress. The counter-trap is to replace deterministic geometric assumptions with probabilistic boundary evidence and joint primitive ambiguity resolution. The clock is the number of experiments and the convergence behavior of the stochastic watershed. The recursive theory-of-mind requirement is to anticipate that local evidence can be globally misleading, especially under occlusion, radial distortion, and short visible arcs.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication separates physical, biomechanical, and weather/illumination constraints. A veto is valid only when it can be expressed as a formal condition that forces a particular output or removes a class of candidate detections.

Physical layer veto 1: playing-field mask veto. The equation
\[
M_B(r,c)=1 \quad \text{iff} \quad I_{RWS}(r,c)\ge T_{RWS}\ \text{and}\ M_{PF}(r,c)=1
\]
is a hard spatial veto. If \(M_{PF}(r,c)=0\), then \(M_B(r,c)=0\). Therefore, any bright line-like object outside the playing field cannot be detected by the proposed segmentation stage. This is physically and semantically valid for soccer field-line detection, because the target objects are field markings on grass, not arbitrary white objects outside the pitch. The paper uses the ground-truth playing-field mask for evaluation [PAGE 13]. Breakdown: if \(M_{PF}\) is incorrect, the veto becomes invalid and true lines inside the true field may be removed, or non-field white structures may survive if the mask is too permissive.

Physical layer veto 2: morphological scale veto. The Top-Hat operator uses a structuring element whose diameter must be slightly greater than the maximum width of features to be preserved [PAGE 8]. If the line width \(w\) exceeds the structuring-element diameter \(d_{sd}\), then the line mark is not guaranteed to survive as a thin bright feature. Formally, a necessary condition for preservation is approximately
\[
w < d_{sd}.
\]
In the database, line width is typically under 10 pixels, so \(d_{sd}=11\) pixels is used [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: if lines are wider than 11 pixels due to blur, low resolution, or unusual marking thickness, the morphological veto may remove them. If the structuring element is too small, thick bright non-line regions may survive and create false positives.

Physical layer veto 3: radial-distortion veto against straightness-only methods. The paper argues that Hough-based methods produce duplicate lines under radial distortion and that many methods assume straightness or known camera geometry [PAGE 3][PAGE 4]. The proposed watershed segmentation makes no assumptions about line straightness [PAGE 1][PAGE 16]. This is a cross-layer veto: if the camera introduces radial distortion, any method whose first stage assumes straight lines must either fail, produce duplicates, or require undistortion. The stochastic watershed avoids this by detecting bright boundary evidence before primitive classification. Breakdown: the later classifier still uses straight-line and ellipse models, so extremely strong distortion may cause classification errors even if segmentation remains good.

Biomechanical layer veto: player and ball occlusion. Players can occlude line marks, interrupt regions, or create boundaries that a single watershed experiment may follow incorrectly. The paper states that the watershed transformation can flood out irrelevant edges due to players or the ball [PAGE 2], and that even lines interrupted by players, such as the penalty arc, can be present at both sides of the interruption in a single experiment [PAGE 5]. However, occlusion is not fully removable. If a player occludes a segment long enough that the remaining visible fragments are too short for reliable primitive fitting, the classifier may discard the region. The paper notes that small regions, for example fewer than 50 pixels, are initially left unassigned [PAGE 10]. Formal veto:
\[
|R_i| < 50 \quad \Rightarrow \quad r_i \text{ is initially unassigned.}
\]
Breakdown: this protects against unstable fits but can remove true short line fragments. The paper reports non-detections associated with shortest line segments and with detected line segments not long enough to determine an ellipse better than a straight line [PAGE 15].

Biomechanical layer veto: white kits and goal elements. The paper reports false positives mainly due to players wearing white kits and white elements of the goals, which resemble white pitch lines [PAGE 14]. This is a biomechanical and equipment veto problem. The proposed method reduces but does not eliminate it because the Top-Hat and watershed stages respond to bright thin or locally bright structures, and a white kit can produce similar local contrast. A cross-layer correction would require player segmentation, semantic object recognition, or temporal tracking. The paper leaves that to higher-level stages.

Weather and illumination veto: abrupt illumination changes. The paper warns that local thresholding can create false contours under abrupt illumination changes [PAGE 8]. It uses Top-Hat to avoid this. However, extreme illumination can still reduce recall. The paper notes false negatives correspond to line points occluded by players or that barely stand out from the grass because they are very far from the camera [PAGE 14]. It also shows an image where half the field is strongly sunlit and the other half is in shadow, claiming that only the proposed strategy detects line marks in all areas while yielding the fewest false positives [PAGE 14]. Formal weather veto: if local contrast
\[
C(r,c)=I_{GS}(r,c)-\text{background}(r,c)
\]
falls below the effective detection margin after Top-Hat and watershed aggregation, the pixel cannot survive thresholding. Breakdown: heavy shadow, low sun angle, wet grass reflections, or low camera resolution can push \(C(r,c)\) below the margin.

Geometric model veto: ellipse degeneracy. If the visible points from a curved primitive are too short or too noisy, the ellipse fit may be indistinguishable from a line fit or may be rank-deficient. The paper notes that ellipses are misdetected when detected line segments are not long enough to determine that they fit an ellipse better than a straight line [PAGE 15]. Formal veto:
\[
\operatorname{RMSE}_{\text{line}} - \operatorname{RMSE}_{\text{ellipse}} \le \epsilon
\]
can cause an elliptic fragment to be assigned to a line, where \(\epsilon\) is effectively governed by the RMSE thresholds and region length. This is a geometric ambiguity veto, not a physical impossibility. The correct adjudication is not to force an ellipse but to leave the region unassigned or to re-evaluate it during merging when more regions are available.

Overall adjudication: the proposed method passes physical and biomechanical vetoes better than fixed-threshold edge detectors and Hough methods because it does not require global straightness, fixed line count, or a single threshold. It fails or weakens when line evidence is too short, too occluded, too low-contrast, or outside the playing-field mask. The empirical evidence is consistent with this: segmentation F-score 0.97 [PAGE 13], but residual errors concentrate in short segments, occlusions, white-kit false positives, and ellipse-versus-line ambiguity [PAGE 14][PAGE 15].
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios can be constructed from the paper’s formal operators. Each scenario has a predicate, a veto mechanism, and a testable prediction.

Scenario 1: playing-field-mask veto.
Predicate: a candidate pixel \((r,c)\) has high watershed probability, \(I_{RWS}(r,c)\ge 0.8\), but lies outside the playing field, \(M_{PF}(r,c)=0\).
Veto mechanism:
\[
M_B(r,c)=1 \quad \text{iff} \quad I_{RWS}(r,c)\ge T_{RWS}\ \text{and}\ M_{PF}(r,c)=1.
\]
Therefore, the candidate is vetoed and \(M_B(r,c)=0\).
Verifiable prediction: if an image contains white structures outside the pitch that produce strong watershed responses, the proposed segmentation mask will still contain zero detections in those pixels. If the ground-truth line mask contains no true field lines outside the pitch, precision outside the pitch should be undefined or perfect by absence, while recall outside the pitch is not meaningful. If the playing-field mask is corrupted, this veto becomes a failure mode: true pitch lines inside the corrupted zero region will be missed.
Test: create a counterfactual image with a bright white rectangular object outside the pitch and no true field line there. The expected output is no segmentation detection in that object. The paper’s evaluation uses the provided ground-truth playing-field mask \(M_{PF}\) [PAGE 13], making this veto directly verifiable.

Scenario 2: RMSE acceptance veto.
Predicate: a connected region \(r_i\) has an initial straight-line RMSE above 2, or a merged or elliptic model RMSE above 4.
Veto mechanism: the paper says a fit is accurate enough when RMSE is lower than 2 [PAGE 10], while merging allows a maximum RMSE of 4 px for straight lines and at most 4 for ellipse merging [PAGE 10]. Refinement removes pixels whose distance to the estimated model is greater than 4 [PAGE 10]. Therefore, a region or subregion that cannot satisfy the RMSE criterion is not accepted as a primitive and its pixels may be discarded.
Verifiable prediction: if a region is highly noisy, heavily occluded, or composed of two crossing structures that were not split correctly, its RMSE will exceed the acceptance threshold and the classifier will discard it or split it into unstable subregions. In the paper’s example, small regions with fewer than 50 pixels are initially left unassigned [PAGE 10]. In the full database, shortest line segments are associated with non-detections [PAGE 15]. Thus, if a ground-truth line mark is visible only as a short noisy fragment, the predicted outcome is lower straight-line recall or ellipse non-detection.
Test: take a true short line segment, add Gaussian noise or occlusion until the residual RMSE exceeds 4 px, and verify that the classifier removes it. Conversely, take a clean segment and verify that RMSE remains below 2 or 4 and the segment is retained.

Scenario 3: Hough target-count veto.
Predicate: a Hough-based straight-line detector is configured with target line count \(K\). If \(K\) is too low, true lines are missed; if \(K\) is too high, false lines are introduced.
Veto mechanism: the paper states that low target-line settings produce many misdetections, while high settings significantly increase false detections [PAGE 16]. It reports that the best global configuration obtains 8 lines per image, with F-score 0.78, while the proposed method reaches 0.90 for straight-line object detection [PAGE 16].
Verifiable prediction: if \(K\) is set below the number of visible true straight lines, recall decreases because some true lines are not returned. If \(K\) is set above the number of visible true lines, precision decreases because extra lines are forced. The optimum around \(K=8\) is dataset-dependent and should fail on images with unusually few or unusually many visible straight lines.
Test: run the Hough baseline on images with fewer than 8 true straight lines and with more than 8 true straight lines. The counterfactual veto predicts that a fixed \(K=8\) will produce false positives on sparse images and false negatives on dense images. The paper’s Figure 11 and discussion explicitly describe this trade-off [PAGE 16].

Additional cross-check: seed-coverage veto.
Although only three scenarios were requested, the seed-coverage veto is a useful internal validation of the stochastic watershed design. If uniform seeds are too few, some regions remain uncovered and lines are missed [PAGE 6]. If the lattice origin is fixed, artifacts appear [PAGE 7]. If the windowed random seed generator is used, \(M=20\) can approach the quality of uniform seeding with \(M=200\) [PAGE 7]. A verifiable prediction is that increasing \(M\) with fixed lattice origin will not remove lattice-shaped artifacts, while randomizing the lattice origin will. This is directly testable by comparing fixed-origin and random-origin windowed seeding outputs.

These three main counterfactual vetoes are verifiable because they map to explicit equations and reported thresholds: the playing-field mask veto maps to \(M_B\), the RMSE veto maps to the 2-pixel initial and 4-pixel merging/refinement rules, and the Hough veto maps to the target-line-count limitation and the reported F-score gap. They also expose the main gray zones of the system: spatial masking can remove valid off-field detections if the mask is wrong; RMSE thresholds can discard short but true segments; and fixed primitive-count priors can fail when the visible geometry is sparse or dense.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
