The central physical derivation is simple but decisive. If a model predicts velocity \(v_t\) in m/s and the postprocessor integrates positions at fixed \(\Delta t=0.04\) s by \(p_i=p_{i-1}+v_i\Delta t\) [PAGE 7], then a per-step velocity error \(\varepsilon_{v,k}\) accumulates as \(e_H=e_0+\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{H}\varepsilon_{v,k}\). Therefore FDE should grow with horizon unless the velocity field is nearly constant; that explains why the paper reports that Constant Velocity and Linear models are competitive at very short horizons and why “Beyond 1.3s, all models outperform the Linear model” [PAGE 9]. The same derivation explains why a 2 s input history is enough: if player direction changes faster than the 2 s window, additional history carries little mutual information about the next 2 s, and the paper explicitly notes “error variations remain minimal between 1.61 and 1.63m at input lengths between 1.0 and 3.0s” [PAGE 9]. 

A second multi-hop thread is the context transform. The paper applies \(f(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,2\,e^{-\frac12 |x|}\) to distances [PAGE 4] so that shorter distances dominate. This is a monotone bounded transform on a dimensionless argument; if \(x\) is in meters, the exponent is dimensionally ill-posed unless \(x\) is normalized or a meter scale is absorbed. That is a gray-zone: the paper reports the transform but does not state the normalization constant in the extracted text. A third thread is interaction modeling. The GAT computes attention coefficients \(\alpha_{ij}\) over neighbors and aggregates weighted features [PAGE 5 and PAGE 6]; the Transformer uses scaled dot-product attention [PAGE 7]. Both can be interpreted as first-order “theory of mind” operators—how much a target player’s future depends on each neighbor—but neither implements recursive beliefs about what opponents believe. The paper’s LSTM context model uses “velocities of the target object, teammates, and opponents” plus distances [PAGE 4], so the interaction signal is real, but higher-order recursion is absent.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**1. Uniform-time trajectory operator.** The paper states: “The NBA dataset is sampled at 25 Hz. Interpolation is applied to all trajectories to ensure a uniform time interval of 0.04 s.” [PAGE 3]. With positions \(p_t\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in meters and velocities \(v_t\in\mathbb{R}^2\) in m/s, the postprocessing integration is
\[
p_i=p_{i-1}+v_i\Delta t,\qquad \Delta t=0.04\ \mathrm{s}.
\]
The source writes “pi = pi−1 + vi∆t” [PAGE 7]. Unit check: m = m + (m/s)(s). Breakdown regime: if \(\Delta t\) is not uniform, this Euler reconstruction is biased; if \(v_i\) has per-step error \(\varepsilon_{v,i}\), then after \(H\) steps \(e_H=e_0+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{H}\varepsilon_{v,i}\). Thus FDE is not a pure model score; it is a functional of velocity error and integration horizon.

**2. Displacement metrics.** The paper defines final displacement error and average displacement error, and states that “The final displacement error (FDE) and the average displacement error (ADE) are measured in meters” [PAGE 8]:
\[
\mathrm{FDE}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|y_{i,T}-\hat y_{i,T}\right\|,
\qquad
\mathrm{ADE}=\frac1{N\cdot T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|y_{i,t}-\hat y_{i,t}\right\|.
\]
Units: meters. \(T\) is the forecast horizon in steps, \(N\) is sample count. Breakdown: FDE is dominated by the last step and is sensitive to endpoint noise; ADE averages over the whole path and can hide end-point divergence. Neither metric alone identifies whether angular heading is correct.

**3. Angular metrics.** For ground-truth velocity \(\vec v_y\) and predicted velocity \(\vec v_{\hat y}\), the paper defines
\[
\bar\theta=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\vec v_y\cdot\vec v_{\hat y}}{\|\vec v_y\|\,\|\vec v_{\hat y}\|}\right),
\qquad
\theta=\begin{cases}\bar\theta,&\vec v_y\times\vec v_{\hat y}>0,\\-\bar\theta,&\text{else},\end{cases}
\]
and then AAE and FAE as averages of \(|\theta_{i,t}|\) [PAGE 8]. Units: radians inside the arccos, converted to degrees for reporting; the paper reports AAE and FAE in degrees [PAGE 8]. Breakdown: the sign convention depends on the 2D cross-product, so a coordinate-frame flip changes the sign but not AAE/FAE magnitudes. Near-zero velocity vectors make the normalized dot product numerically unstable; that is a singularity regime requiring an epsilon floor.

**4. Distance transform for context.** The LSTM context pathway applies
\[
f(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,2\,e^{-\frac12 |x|}
\]
to distances [PAGE 4]. If \(x\) is dimensionless, \(f(x)\) is dimensionless and bounded in \((-2,2)\). If \(x\) is in meters, the exponent \(-\frac12 |x|\) is dimensionally inconsistent unless a reference length \(x_0\) is absorbed, e.g. \(f(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)\,2\,e^{-\frac12 |x|/x_0}\). The paper’s stated purpose is to “prioritizing shorter distances” [PAGE 4]. Breakdown: very large raw distances saturate toward zero influence; very small distances approach the maximum magnitude. The transform is not a probability and should not be interpreted as one.

**5. LSTM cell.** The source gives the standard gated recurrence:
\[
i_t=\sigma(W_{ii}x_t+b_{ii}+W_{hi}h_{t-1}+b_{hi}),\quad
f_t=\sigma(W_{if}x_t+b_{if}+W_{hf}h_{t-1}+b_{hf}),
\]
\[
g_t=\tanh(W_{ig}x_t+b_{ig}+W_{hg}h_{t-1}+b_{hg}),\quad
o_t=\sigma(W_{io}x_t+b_{io}+W_{ho}h_{t-1}+b_{ho}),
\]
\[
c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot g_t,\qquad h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t),
\]
with sigmoid \(\sigma\) and Hadamard product \(\odot\) [PAGE 4]. Units are inherited from inputs; gate activations are dimensionless. Breakdown: long sequences stress the cell-state memory; the paper notes recurrent models “may suffer from vanishing memory and limited scalability when processing extended sequences” [PAGE 1]. The 2 s input recommendation [PAGE 9] is an empirical boundary, not a theorem.

**6. GAT interaction operator.** The node-level attention is
\[
\alpha_{ij}=
\frac{\exp\!\left(\operatorname{LeakyReLU}\!\left(a^\top[Wh_i\oplus Wh_j]\right)\right)}
{\sum_{k\in\mathcal N(i)}\exp\!\left(\operatorname{LeakyReLU}\!\left(a^\top[Wh_i\oplus Wh_k]\right)\right)},
\]
and the aggregated feature is
\[
h_i'=\sigma\!\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}\alpha_{ij}Wh_j\right),
\]
with multi-head concatenation over \(K\) heads [PAGE 5 and PAGE 6]. Units: \(\alpha_{ij}\) is dimensionless and sums to 1 over neighbors; \(h_i\) carries the feature units chosen upstream. Breakdown: attention is permutation-invariant over neighbors and therefore cannot by itself encode absolute temporal order; the paper therefore wraps the GAT in an RNN-GAT-RNN encoder-decoder [PAGE 6].

**7. Transformer attention.** The source uses
\[
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
\]
with \(Q=XW_Q\), \(K=XW_K\), \(V=XW_V\), eight heads, \(d_k=4\), six encoder blocks, and \(d_{\text{model}}=256\) [PAGE 7]. Units: attention weights are dimensionless probabilities over keys; the \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) factor is a variance-stabilizing scale. Breakdown: without positional encoding or the paper’s CNN temporal embedding, self-attention is order-agnostic; the source explicitly uses a CNN-based temporal embedding [PAGE 3 and PAGE 7]. The paper also states the Transformer “suffers from having too little data for effective training” [PAGE 10], which is a data-scarcity breakdown regime.

**8. LMU state-space operator.** The LMU is described by
\[
\theta\dot m(t)=Am(t)+Bu(t),
\]
with discretized form \(m_t=\bar A m_{t-1}+\bar B u_t\), and hidden update
\[
h_t=f(W_xx_t+W_hh_{t-1}+W_mm_t)
\]
[PAGE 5]. Units: \(m(t)\) is a memory state; \(\theta\) is a time window and should carry seconds if \(t\) is in seconds. The paper sets \(d=256\) and \(\theta=25\) [PAGE 5]. Breakdown: the continuous-time approximation assumes a fixed sampling interval; the paper’s 0.04 s grid [PAGE 3] satisfies that only if interpolation is accurate. If \(\theta\) is treated as a pure number while \(t\) is in seconds, the ODE is dimensionally inconsistent.

**9. Reported benchmark anchors.** Table I at a 2.0 s horizon reports CNN-LSTM FDE 1.51 m and Transformer FDE 1.66 m [PAGE 10]; Table II at 0.48 s reports Linear FDE 0.20 m [PAGE 10]; Table III reports CNN-LSTM \(\Delta\)FDE 0.02 m across within-team and cross-team tests [PAGE 11]. These are the concrete operators to use in adjudication.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is retrocausal in the sense that today’s architecture choices descend from older modeling assumptions. Classical ARIMA, Kalman filters, and particle filters assume near-linear or probabilistic state evolution and are computationally efficient, but the paper states they “rely on linearity assumptions that render them inadequate for capturing sudden transitions and complex multi-agent interactions” [PAGE 1]. Recurrent networks, especially LSTM and GRU, inherit the idea of a latent state carried through time; the source traces LSTM to the gated recurrence [PAGE 4] and presents LMU as a continuous-time state-space alternative grounded in Legendre polynomials [PAGE 5]. Temporal convolutional networks inherit causal convolution and dilation; the paper uses five causal convolutional layers with dilations \(1,2,4,8\) [PAGE 3 and PAGE 4]. Graph neural networks inherit message passing and attention-based neighbor weighting; the paper’s GAT equations are a direct descendant of graph attention [PAGE 5 and PAGE 6]. Transformers inherit scaled dot-product self-attention and positional encoding, with a CNN temporal embedding grafted on [PAGE 7]. The paper’s own genealogy is explicit: it moves “from Recurrent to Graph Neural Networks and General Purpose Transformers” [PAGE 1].

The multi-week traps are mostly evaluation traps. First, a team can spend weeks tuning long input histories before discovering the 2 s plateau; the source reports that “no model benefits from an input sequence length longer than 2s” [PAGE 3 footnote] and that error is nearly flat between 1.0 and 3.0 s input length [PAGE 9]. Second, a team can leak the test game into preprocessing if it interpolates or normalizes across the full dataset rather than fitting normalization on training data; the paper says the test set is “sourced from a single, unseen game” [PAGE 3] and later describes a 70/20/10 split [PAGE 7], so the split protocol must be reconstructed before trusting a leaderboard. Third, a team can overfit to one team’s style and call it generalization; the source distinguishes within-team generalization [PAGE 9] from cross-team generalization [PAGE 10 and PAGE 11]. Fourth, a team can select on FDE alone and miss angular degradation: Table I shows LMU FDE 1.59 m but AAE 45.38°, worse angularly than CNN-LSTM’s 38.92° [PAGE 10]. Fifth, a team can treat the distance transform as a universal constant without checking normalization; the transform’s exponent is only valid for dimensionless \(x\) or with a reference length [PAGE 4]. Sixth, a team can assume a GAT or Transformer automatically encodes recursive opponent intent; the paper’s GAT is first-order neighbor attention [PAGE 5 and PAGE 6], not a recursive belief hierarchy. Seventh, a team can compare architectures trained with different feature sets; the paper says Constant Velocity, Linear, TCNN, LSTM, and CNN-LSTM “perform best on velocities” while LMU, GNN, and Transformer “exploited also positions” [PAGE 8], so architecture and input feature are confounded unless held fixed. These are multi-week traps because each one silently changes the estimand while producing plausible-looking curves.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the tendency, under time pressure or data scarcity, to fall back to the simplest motion prior. The paper documents this empirically: “For very short horizons (up to 0.12s), classical approaches such as a Constant Velocity Kalman Filter may be sufficient” [PAGE 10], and Constant Velocity and Linear models are competitive below 0.5 s [PAGE 9]. That is not a failure of complex models; it is the correct Bayesian behavior when the observation noise dominates the predictable acceleration. The regression becomes a trap when an analyst uses a 0.12 s result to justify a 2 s deployment. The source’s own horizon thresholds rebut that: beyond 0.4 s all models outperform Constant Velocity, and beyond 1.3 s all models outperform Linear [PAGE 9]. A second regression is architectural: when data are scarce, a Transformer can underperform a recurrent model, and the paper attributes its 1.66 m FDE to “too little data for effective training” [PAGE 10]. The counter-trap is equally dangerous: assuming that a GAT or Transformer must dominate because it is more expressive. The paper’s Table I does not support that; CNN-LSTM has the best FDE at 1.51 m [PAGE 10]. 

The clock is the 25 Hz / 0.04 s discretization and the forecast horizon. At 25 Hz, one step is 0.04 s; 50 steps is 2 s. The source evaluates 50 predicted timesteps [PAGE 8] and finds the 2 s input horizon near-optimal [PAGE 9]. A recursive theory-of-mind model would need to update beliefs about opponents at a rate compatible with that clock; if the belief update is slower than the player’s direction change, the model is effectively using stale intent. The paper’s interaction encoder is a first-order attention operator: \(\alpha_{ij}\) weights neighbor \(j\)’s influence on node \(i\) [PAGE 5]. That can be read as “how much should player \(i\) care about player \(j\),” but it is not a recursive “player \(i\) believes that player \(j\) believes that player \(k\) will cut.” If uncertainty is reported, a natural information unit is the predictive entropy \(H(p)=-\sum p\log_2 p\) in bits; the source does not report entropy, so any claim about calibrated uncertainty is unsupported. The safe adjudication is: use the clock to set horizon-specific thresholds, use first-order attention only as a contextual feature, and do not infer higher-order strategic reasoning from a GAT attention map.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical and biomechanical vetoes must be applied after any neural prediction. Let \(p_t\in\mathbb{R}^2\) be position in meters, \(v_t\) velocity in m/s, and \(a_t\) acceleration in m/s². The source’s integration rule is \(p_i=p_{i-1}+v_i\Delta t\) with \(\Delta t=0.04\) s [PAGE 7]. A necessary physical condition is
\[
\|p_{t+1}-p_t\|\le v_{\max}\Delta t,
\qquad
\left\|\frac{p_{t+1}-2p_t+p_{t-1}}{\Delta t^2}\right\|\le a_{\max},
\]
where \(v_{\max}\) and \(a_{\max}\) must be calibrated from the tracking data or sport-specific biomechanics; the paper does not provide those constants, so they cannot be invented here. A predicted step that exceeds the speed or acceleration envelope is vetoed regardless of its FDE. A court-boundary condition \(p_t\in\mathcal C\) is also required; the paper shows the court with context in Fig. 2 [PAGE 4] but does not give dimensions in the extracted text, so \(\mathcal C\) must come from the dataset metadata. A heading veto applies when the angular update implied by consecutive predicted velocities exceeds a biomechanical turning-rate bound; the paper’s FAE/AAE are in degrees [PAGE 8], so the threshold should be expressed in degrees per second, not as a dimensionless ratio. A collision/interpenetration veto applies when two players’ predicted positions occupy the same physical space within a body-radius margin; the source models distances to neighboring objects [PAGE 4], so this is a natural post-hoc check.

Weather veto proof is different: the NBA dataset is indoor basketball, and the paper provides no weather variable, no wind vector, no precipitation state, and no outdoor surface state. Therefore any weather-based adjustment is unsupported by the drive source. The correct adjudication is to veto weather claims as out-of-scope rather than to assign them a coefficient. If a downstream system includes weather, it must be treated as a separate layer with its own measured uncertainty; it cannot be smuggled into the distance transform \(f(x)\) [PAGE 4] because that transform is defined on distances, not atmospheric state. The cross-layer order is: neural prediction → unit check (m, m/s, degrees) → speed/acceleration veto → court-boundary veto → collision veto → horizon-specific model-selection veto. This order matters because a model can have the lowest FDE and still violate a hard physical constraint at a single step; the source’s aggregate metrics [PAGE 10] would not reveal that.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto 1: The long-context counterfactual.** Scenario: a proposed change claims that increasing input history from 2 s to 4 s or 10 s materially improves 2 s forecasting. Verifiable check: the source reports that “no model benefits from an input sequence length longer than 2s” [PAGE 3 footnote] and that at a 2 s forecast horizon “error variations remain minimal between 1.61 and 1.63m at input lengths between 1.0 and 3.0s” [PAGE 9]. Prediction: under the same LSTM protocol, a 4 s input should not beat the 2 s input by a large margin; if the counterfactual claims a gain larger than the reported plateau, veto the claim unless it also reports a new dataset, new sampling rate, or new sport. The verifiable reward is binary: accept only if the measured 2 s FDE falls outside the 1.61–1.63 m plateau in the claimed direction and the split protocol is documented.

**Veto 2: The architecture-superiority counterfactual.** Scenario: a claim that a Transformer or GAT is the best architecture for 2 s NBA player forecasting under the paper’s setup. Verifiable check: Table I reports CNN-LSTM FDE 1.51 m, LMU 1.59 m, LSTM 1.62 m, GNN 1.62 m, Transformer 1.66 m, and Constant Velocity 2.49 m at 2.0 s [PAGE 10]. The paper also states that “our CNN-LSTM model (green curve) outperforms the more complex Transformer and GNN models across all forecast horizons” [PAGE 10]. Prediction: if the counterfactual is tested on the same features, split, and horizon, the Transformer should not achieve a lower FDE than 1.51 m without a material change such as more training data, pretraining, or a different input feature set. Veto any claim that ignores the paper’s stated data-scarcity explanation for the Transformer [PAGE 10].

**Veto 3: The generalization-collapse counterfactual.** Scenario: a claim that CNN-LSTM degrades severely when tested on unseen teams or players. Verifiable check: Table III reports \(\Delta\)FDE = 0.02 m for CNN-LSTM between within-team and cross-team evaluation, and the paper reports an unseen-game FDE of 1.53 m [PAGE 11]. The source also says “The minimal differences in error (CNN-LSTM: \(\Delta\) FDE=0.02m) between the two experiments indicate that the models do not overfit to specific players or teams” [PAGE 10]. Prediction: under the same train/test protocol, CNN-LSTM’s cross-team FDE should remain near 1.53 m, not collapse to the Constant Velocity 2.49 m level [PAGE 10]. Veto a claimed collapse unless the counterfactual specifies a distribution shift not present in the source, such as new tracking hardware, new court geometry, or a different sport. The reward is verifiable by recomputing FDE on the held-out game and checking whether the difference exceeds the reported 0.02 m by a stated tolerance.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
