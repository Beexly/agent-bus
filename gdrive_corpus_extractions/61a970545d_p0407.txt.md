If an obsolete segment was dominant for many trials, it can remain the argmax of the hierarchy for weeks after reinforcement is withdrawn, producing spontaneous recovery or context renewal when the animal is re‑exposed to the original training context. A discrimination reversal trained under Spence’s single‑dimension paradigm (refs. 24, 26) likewise shows a multi‑week trap: the generalization gradient shifts slowly, so the animal continues to respond to the formerly reinforced stimulus long after the contingency has inverted. The retrocausal element is that the current goal gradient re‑weights old segments; if the goal is removed (\(V=0\)), the hierarchy does not immediately collapse, because \(sHr\) persists beyond the removal of the prospective value that originally organized it.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under elevated drive \(D\) or stress, the organism regresses to the habit with maximal \(sHr_i\) among feasible responses:
\[
r_{\text{crisis}}=\arg\max_{i:\,B_i(t)=1}sHr_i,
\]
where \(B_i(t)\in\{0,1\}\) is biomechanical feasibility (defined in the next section). Regression is “crisis” because novel, weakly reinforced segments are suppressed and the hierarchy collapses to its strongest, most overlearned rung.

Counter‑trap. A counter‑trap is a pre‑installed competing segment \(r_c\) trained to high \(sHr_c\) before the crisis. It blocks regression only if its reactive potential exceeds that of the regressive habit at decision time:
\[
sHr_c\,D\,V\,K - sIr_c \;>\; sHr_{\text{reg}}\,D\,V\,K - sIr_{\text{reg}}.
\]
Because \(D,V,K\) are common factors, the inequality reduces to a comparison of net habit strength minus inhibition. If the counter‑trap was not sufficiently overlearned (\(sHr_c\) too low) or carries high inhibition (\(sIr_c\) high), the regression wins.

Clock. Extinction/decay imposes a time constant \(\tau_{\text{ext}}\) on habit strength under nonreinforcement:
\[
sHr_i(t)=sHr_i(0)\exp(-t/\tau_{\text{ext}}).
\]
If the crisis duration \(T_c\) satisfies \(T_c\ll\tau_{\text{ext}}\), the dominant habit is essentially unchanged and regression is locked in. If \(T_c\gg\tau_{\text{ext}}\), the dominant habit decays and the counter‑trap—or a lower‑strength alternative—may capture control. The clock bounds recursive theory of mind: Agent A models B’s hierarchy; B models A’s counter‑trap; A models B’s model of A’s counter‑trap. Each ply consumes decision time; once available decision time \(T_{\text{dec}}\) falls below the cognitive cycle time, recursion halts and the system defaults to zero‑ply habit regression (\(r_{\text{crisis}}\)). The clock therefore prevents infinite regress by forcing a hardware‑level fallback to the strongest habit.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Three layers are adjudicated: psychological (Hullian), biomechanical/physical, and environmental/weather.

Layer 1 — Psychological: \(sEr_i = sHr_i D V K - sIr_i - I\) as above. This layer computes motivational excitatory potential but does not by itself guarantee emission.

Layer 2 — Biomechanical/physical veto: define feasibility \(B_i(t)\in\{0,1\}\). If \(B_i(t)=0\) (e.g., the required limb is immobilized, the maze runway is blocked, the effectors cannot execute segment \(r_i\)), then actual emitted potential is
\[
sEr_i^{\text{actual}} = sEr_i \cdot B_i(t).
\]
If \(B_i=0\), then \(sEr_i^{\text{actual}}=0\) no matter how large \(sHr_i,D,V,K\) are. Proof by contradiction: assume psychological motivation alone can produce response \(r_i\) when \(B_i=0\). Then \(sEr_i>0\) but emitted behavior \(E_i=0\), contradicting the definition of \(sEr\) as excitatory potential leading to behavior. Therefore the biomechanical layer holds veto priority over the psychological layer.

Layer 3 — Environmental/weather: an ambient parameter \(W(t)\) (temperature, precipitation, wind) modulates \(D\) (drive) and movement speed but does not create or destroy \(sHr\). If \(W\notin[W_{\min},W_{\max}]\) (outside the viable range for the organism), then effective drive \(D_{\text{eff}}=0\) or feasibility \(B=0\), producing a global veto. Weather cannot override a hard \(B=0\) veto; it can only attenuate \(D\) within the non‑vetoed regime.

Source tie: ref. 14 (Hull, 1938, “The goal‑gradient hypothesis applied to some ‘field‑force’ problems in the behavior of young children”) explicitly treats environmental forces as modifiers of behavior, and ref. 15 (trial‑and‑error learning) depends on physical maze apparatus whose geometry imposes biomechanical constraints. The veto ordering is therefore: weather/environment can modulate \(D\) and \(B\), but biomechanical feasibility \(B=0\) is a hard, layer‑dominating veto; psychological goal gradient never supersedes it.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
