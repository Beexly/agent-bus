The source is a Japanese journal paper on matching field-sport players before and after occlusion. Its central move is not “solve occlusion inside the overlap”; it is an abstention-and-reidentification policy. The abstract states that “the features of each athlete before and after occlusion are approximated” and that the authors “were able to solve the problem of degrading detection, identification and tracking accuracy in the occlusion interval” [PAGE 1]. The method chapter is more precise: “オクルージョン時の情報を細部に亘って解析せず，オクルージョンの発生前後の選手の特徴を同定することにより，プレーの中で全選手をシームレスに追跡すること” [PAGE 4]. Thus the multi-hop derivation is: background-subtraction moving blobs → bounding-box overlap classification → occlusion/separate/unchanged state → pre-occlusion feature storage → post-separation BRISK matching → Hamming-distance comparison → state-pool update → search-region tracking → displacement correction → linear player-size interpolation. The paper’s novelty is the temporal re-identification bridge, not dense per-frame identity persistence through the occluder.

The gray-zone audit must separate three regimes. First, “simple” and “complex” occlusion with separation can work when players are large and appearance changes are small: video A succeeds for patterns ⒜–⒞, and pattern ⒟ is reported at “６割（10人中の６人が同定成功）” [PAGE 14]. Second, high-density union and long multi-occlusion are brittle: B/C only robustly handle ⒜ and ⒝, while ⒞–⒠ produce many failures [PAGE 14–15]. Third, the real veto regime is small/static players and posture transitions. The paper reports a 46-pixel-high player whose “動体領域の取得が困難” because the player is small and nearly static [PAGE 15]. It also concludes that when a player changes from “立位（走り）から臥位（スライディング）のように姿勢が著しく変化した場合，選手の同定精度が低下する” [PAGE 15]. That is a biomechanical veto, not a minor edge case.

The quantitative gray zone is also important. The aggregate table reports “全体平쬣 ... 75.7％ 67.0％” [PAGE 17], but video A’s mean is lower for the proposed method than the existing method: “74.0％ 78.7％” [PAGE 17]. The paper itself notes that in video A’s remaining scenes the proposed accuracy dropped by “平쬣4.7％” [PAGE 17], attributed to shadow and backlight causing moving-region errors. Therefore the correct audit is not “proposed beats existing everywhere”; it is “proposed wins in aggregate and in the small-player regime, but loses in some near-distance, shadow/backlight, and high-occlusion scenes.” The small-player result is nevertheless strong in its niche: “特に60ピクセル以下となると，既存手法において平쬣49.2％の選手の同定の正確率に対し，提案手法が71.8％の正確率を維持できた” [PAGE 18]. But the same page says the proposed method’s accuracy is “最も低下” at 60 pixels or less [PAGE 18], so 71.8% is a relative improvement, not a robust absolute guarantee.

The computational clock is another hard veto. The paper reports “１フレームの処理時間は12から15秒” for 23 players including referees [PAGE 13], and later “１フレームごとに約10秒の処理時間” on an Intel i7 CPU with 16 GB memory [PAGE 19]. For a 90-minute match at 30 fps, the frame count is \(90 \times 60 \times 30 = 162{,}000\). At 10 s/frame that is \(1.62 \times 10^6\) s, i.e. 450 h; at 12–15 s/frame it is 540–675 h. The paper explicitly calls this “数百時間となり，実用が困難な問題” [PAGE 19]. Any real-time or tactical-live claim is therefore vetoed unless parallelization, GPU acceleration, or search-space reduction is demonstrated. The paper proposes those as future work, not as completed results [PAGE 20].

The final audit point is internal consistency: table 6 and the prose around it contain a tension. The prose says “すべてのシーンにおいて本提案手法の選手の同定の正確率は既存手法より向上している” [PAGE 17], yet the same table’s video A mean is 74.0% for the proposed method versus 78.7% for the existing method [PAGE 17]. This should be treated as a reporting inconsistency, not as evidence of universal superiority. The defensible claim is conditional: the method is useful when a separation event exists, when the player remains a sufficiently large moving blob, and when appearance does not undergo a large standing-to-lying or limb-configuration change. Outside those conditions, the paper’s own failure cases provide the veto evidence.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s operators are: adaptive background subtraction to obtain moving foreground regions; bounding rectangles and overlap ratios to classify occlusion, separation, and unchanged states; BRISK keypoint extraction and Hamming-distance matching; a state pool storing pre-occlusion position/size and latest single-player position/size; a search-region tracker; a displacement-correction grid; and a linear image-height-to-player-size model. The claims are defined on pixels, frames, seconds, and Hamming bits; the accuracy values are dimensionless empirical ratios.

Let \(F^t_{\Omega}\) be the set of matched BRISK feature points inside moving region \(\Omega\) at frame \(t\), with \(p_i^t \in \mathbb{R}^2\) in pixel coordinates. The paper’s displacement set is, in formal terms,
\[
D_t=\left\{\,d_i=\left\|p_i^{t+\Delta}-p_i^{t}\right\|_2\;\middle|\;p_i^t\in F^t_{\Omega},\;p_i^{t+\Delta}\in F^{t+\Delta}_{\Omega'},\;i=1,\dots,N\,\right\},
\]
with units of pixels per \(\Delta\) frames. The mean displacement is
\[
\bar d_t=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_i,
\]
in pixels, and the paper’s dispersion term is the mean absolute deviation
\[
s_t=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|d_i-\bar d_t\right|,
\]
also in pixels. The noise rejection rule is \( |d_i-\bar d_t|>s_t \), quoted in the source as “最後に，…＞…の特徴点…をノイズとして除去する” [PAGE 9]. This is an operator on matched feature displacements, not a physical force or fatigue ratio.

Breakdown regimes for this operator are exact. If \(N=0\), \(\bar d_t\) and \(s_t\) are undefined; the paper reports a failure case with only two matched points, where one bad point corrupts the position estimate [PAGE 15]. If \(N=1\), then \(s_t=0\) and the rule removes nothing; the estimate is a single-point estimate and is not robust. If \(N\) is small, a single mismatched feature can dominate \(\bar d_t\). If the player is static, the moving foreground region may not be obtained, because the method uses “動的背景差分法” and the paper notes that when a player is stationary, the moving region and features cannot be obtained [PAGE 8]. If shadow or backlight splits the blob, \(D_t\) can mix features from different objects, producing a large \(s_t\) or a wrong but low-\(s_t\) consensus. Thus the operator’s validity condition is \(N\gg 1\) with feature matches concentrated on one player and with a stable moving blob.

For identity comparison, the paper uses BRISK binary descriptors and Hamming distance. Formally, for binary descriptor vectors \(q,r\in\{0,1\}^m\),
\[
H(q,r)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left[q_j\neq r_j\right],
\]
with units of bits. The decision rule is that the same player should satisfy \(\bar H_{\text{same}}<\bar H_{\text{diff}}\), where the bar denotes the mean over matched feature pairs. The paper states, “ハミング距離の平쬣値が小さいほど，同定対象と類似していると判定できる” [PAGE 9]. BRISK is selected because “BRISK はこれらの手法の中で対象の回転とスケールに最も頑健” [PAGE 7]. The breakdown is visible in the pre-experiment: the “奥－左” direction has same-target mean 145.5 versus different-target mean 144.4, a difference of \(+1.1\), so the same/different ordering is violated for that direction [PAGE 7]. Therefore BRISK+Hamming is not a universal rotation/scale invariant; it is empirically adequate only for the tested small posture changes and favorable directions.

The occlusion/separation classifier uses bounding-box overlap. Let \(B_t\) be the axis-aligned bounding rectangle of a moving region at frame \(t\), and \(A(\cdot)\) be pixel area. The paper’s rule can be written as
\[
O(B_t,B_{t-1})=\frac{A(B_t\cap B_{t-1})}{A(B_t)}\ge 0.6,
\]
with the alternative denominator \(A(B_{t-1})\) possible depending on implementation. The source says, “前後フレームにおける動体領域の外接矩形の重なりが６割以上の場合にオクルージョンが発生したものとする” [PAGE 8]. Breakdown occurs when \(A(B_t)=0\) or \(A(B_{t-1})=0\), when a shadow contributes a second blob, when two players merge into one blob, or when one player is split into multiple blobs; then the 0.6 threshold classifies the wrong geometric event. The paper confirms shadow/backlight and blob splitting as failure causes [PAGE 17–18] and lists “逆光や影などによる動体領域のノイズ” as a failure source [PAGE 20].

The search-region operator is a rectangle centered on the previous tracking box with width and height expanded to twice the original size, except when the player is in occlusion or has just separated; then the moving region or separated region becomes the search region [PAGE 10]. Formally,
\[
R_t=\operatorname{Rect}\!\left(c_{t-1};\,2w_{t-1},\,2h_{t-1}\right),
\]
where \(c_{t-1}\) is the previous box center and \(w,h\) are pixel dimensions. The correction operator searches a discrete set
\[
\mathcal{V}=\left\{(\alpha\bar d_x,\beta\bar d_y)\;\middle|\;\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1,2\}\right\},
\]
with \(\bar d_x,\bar d_y\) the mean feature displacement in pixels, and chooses
\[
m^*=\arg\max_{m\in\mathcal{V}} N_F\!\left(B(c_{t-1}+m)\right),
\]
where \(N_F\) is the count of BRISK features inside the trial box. The paper reports that “全選手が特徴の平쬣移動量の２倍の枠内に収まることが分かった” [PAGE 10]. Breakdown occurs when the feature count is maximized by background texture or by a neighboring player, when \(N_F=0\), or when the true displacement exceeds \(2\bar d\). The paper identifies accumulated matching error as a tracking-failure cause and uses this correction to mitigate it [PAGE 10–11].

The player-size interpolation is
\[
S(Y)=S_{\min}+\frac{S_{\max}-S_{\min}}{Y_{\max}-Y_{\min}}\left(Y-Y_{\min}\right),
\]
where \(S(Y)\) is the player’s image height in pixels at image row \(Y\), and \(S_{\min},S_{\max}\) are the heights of the players with minimum and maximum \(Y\) coordinates [PAGE 11]. This has an exact singularity at \(Y_{\max}=Y_{\min}\); the slope is undefined and the model cannot be calibrated. It also assumes a planar field, a fixed camera, and approximately linear perspective scaling, which the paper states as “線形関係に従って変化する” [PAGE 11]. Extrapolation outside \([Y_{\min},Y_{\max}]\) is unsupported. In small-player regimes, the measured \(S(Y)\) is only 30–100 px in videos B/C, versus 70–250+ px in video A [PAGE 13], so quantization error in the height measurement becomes a large fraction of the signal.

The computational operator is the decisive deployment constraint. The paper reports 12–15 s per frame for 23 tracked persons [PAGE 13] and about 10 s per frame on an Intel i7/16 GB machine [PAGE 19]. For a 90-minute, 30 fps match, \(N_f=162{,}000\) frames and \(T\approx 10\,\mathrm{s/frame}\) gives \(T_{\text{run}}\approx 1.62\times 10^6\,\mathrm{s}=450\,\mathrm{h}\); at 12–15 s/frame it gives 540–675 h. The source calls this “数百時間となり，実用が困難な問題” [PAGE 19]. This is a hard singular regime for live use unless the search space is reduced or the pipeline is parallelized.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage is tracking-by-detection plus local-feature matching. The paper reviews SIFT, SURF, HOG, optical flow, particle filters, and sports-specific trackers [PAGE 3–4]; it selects BRISK from the SIFT/SURF/KAZE/AKAZE/ORB/BRISK family because of rotation/scale robustness and binary descriptor speed [PAGE 7]. Its tracking ancestry includes DeepSort-style detection-plus-Kalman-filter association and MOTChallenge evaluation [PAGE 3–4], and its sports ancestry includes soccer and American-football tracking attempts that degrade under occlusion [PAGE 1–4]. The retrocausal point is that later failure evidence redefines the earlier abstract claim. The abstract’s phrase “features ... before and after occlusion are approximated” [PAGE 1] is not a universal law; the experiments show it holds when the player remains a moving, sufficiently large blob and when posture changes are small, and fails when the player is static, tiny, or transitions from standing/running to sliding/falling [PAGE 15, PAGE 20].

The first multi-week trap is to read the paper as a general “occlusion solver.” It is not. It explicitly avoids detailed analysis inside occlusion and waits for separation [PAGE 4]. If no separation event occurs, the state pool cannot re-identify; the method has no dense identity guarantee through the union. The second trap is to overgeneralize the 60% bounding-box overlap rule. The paper uses it as an engineering threshold [PAGE 8], but shadow, backlight, and blob splitting make the geometric event ambiguous [PAGE 17–18]. The third trap is to treat 71.8% for sub-60-pixel players as robust. The same page says the proposed method’s accuracy is lowest in that regime [PAGE 18]; 71.8% is a relative improvement over 49.2%, not an absolute reliability claim. The fourth trap is to ignore the video-A contradiction: the proposed method’s mean is 74.0% versus 78.7% for the existing method on video A [PAGE 17], despite the prose claim of improvement in all scenes. The fifth trap is to ignore the clock: 10–15 s/frame makes offline analysis the only demonstrated mode [PAGE 13, PAGE 19]. The sixth trap is to assume BRISK’s rotation/scale invariance is complete; the “奥－左” pre-experiment reverses the same/different Hamming ordering [PAGE 7]. The seventh trap is to assume weather robustness. The paper reports illumination/shadow effects, not rain, wind, or wet-field experiments; any weather claim beyond backlight/shadow is unsupported by the source.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the analyst’s default assumption that a tracking ID persists through every frame. Under occlusion, that habit produces identity switches because the tracker keeps assigning a box through a merged blob. The paper’s counter-trap is to stop trusting the occlusion interval and to re-identify after separation [PAGE 4]. But that counter-trap has its own recursive failure: the system now “believes” that a separation will occur and that the pre-occlusion features remain valid. If the player is static, no moving region is obtained [PAGE 8]; if the player is small, only two matched features may remain and one bad point corrupts the position [PAGE 15]; if the player changes from standing to sliding, the same-player Hamming distance can exceed the different-player distance [PAGE 15, PAGE 20]. In those cases the state pool stores stale or wrong features, and the representative-player selection can anchor the wrong identity. The recursive belief “the next separation will resolve it” becomes the error source.

The clock layer is equally recursive. At 10–15 s/frame [PAGE 13, PAGE 19], a live analyst cannot close the loop within the match; the method is an offline batch procedure unless parallelized. The paper’s proposed remedies—GPU use, parallel tracking, ReID models, semantic segmentation, GAN-based shadow removal—are future work [PAGE 20], so the current clock veto is not resolved. The correct mental model is conditional: if \(\tau_{\text{occ}}\) is short and appearance drift is small, re-identification is rational; if \(\tau_{\text{occ}}\) is long, if the player remains in a union, or if posture changes, the same policy becomes a counter-trap. The analyst should therefore maintain a hierarchy: first verify a separation event exists; second verify the post-separation blob is a single-player blob; third verify matched feature count and Hamming ordering; fourth verify the processing clock. Without those checks, the method’s “ignore the occlusion” rule is not a solution but a deferred identity decision.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The sensor/optical layer vetoes broad deployment when projected player height is too small. Video A has players at 70–250+ px, while videos B/C have players at 30–100 px [PAGE 13]. The paper reports that below 100 px the existing detector degrades, and below 60 px the proposed method still reaches 71.8% versus 49.2% for the existing method, but also that the proposed method’s accuracy is lowest at ≤60 px [PAGE 18]. A 46-px static player fails because the moving region cannot be obtained [PAGE 15]. Therefore the optical veto is: if \(h_{\text{player}}\lesssim 60\) px and motion is small, the method’s input signal is insufficient; the claim is not transferable without higher resolution or a different detector.

The geometric occlusion layer vetoes high-density unions and long multi-occlusion. Pattern ⒟ is only “６割（10人中の６人）” successful in video A [PAGE 14], and videos B/C show many failures in ⒞–⒠ [PAGE 14–15]. The 60% overlap rule [PAGE 8] is a geometric proxy, not a semantic count of players; when multiple players merge, the representative-player mechanism can choose one and mark the rest as occlusion, but the post-separation matching may still confuse identities. The paper’s own failure example at frames 50–102 shows a box drifting from player 1 to player 2 and then re-identifying incorrectly after accumulation [PAGE 15–16]. Thus the geometric veto is: if the occlusion is a union with no clean separation, or if the separation is delayed long enough for appearance drift, the method’s identity bridge is not reliable.

The biomechanical layer vetoes large posture transitions. The paper’s applicability limit is explicit: walking/running with small posture changes is high-accuracy; special limb bending or raising lowers accuracy; standing-to-lying (falling/sliding) makes application difficult [PAGE 20]. The sliding failure is also reported as a cause in video B pattern ⒟ and video C pattern ⒞ [PAGE 15–16]. This is a biomechanical veto because the descriptor’s same-player Hamming distance increases as the body configuration changes, while the different-player distance may not. The paper’s future work suggests ReID models and time-series appearance databases [PAGE 20], which confirms that BRISK alone is insufficient for this regime.

The environmental layer veto is proven for backlight and shadow, but not for general weather. The paper attributes video A’s lower accuracy to “選手の影” and “逆光” causing moving-region errors [PAGE 17], and table 8 lists “逆光や影などによる動体領域のノイズ” as a failure cause [PAGE 20]. There is no reported rain, wind, or wet-field experiment, so weather claims beyond illumination are unsupported. Adjudication: uphold the method for near-distance, walking/running, low-to-moderate occlusion with a clear separation event, and stable illumination; veto real-time use at 10–15 s/frame; veto small/static players; veto high-density unions; veto standing-to-sliding transitions; and conditionally veto shadow/backlight scenes.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

This follows the paper’s observed 46-px static failure [PAGE 15] and the two-feature failure case [PAGE 15]. Verifiable prediction: measure \(N\), position error, and ID-switch count. Veto the “small-player robust” claim for this subcase if \(N<3\) and the proposed method’s identification accuracy is not above the existing method on the same annotated clip.

2. Standing-to-sliding posture counterfactual. Construct a player who runs, enters occlusion, then slides or falls before separation. Prediction: the same-player mean Hamming distance becomes greater than or equal to the different-player mean, so the same/different ordering fails; the state pool’s pre-occlusion features no longer match; the post-separation ID is wrong. This is supported by the paper’s conclusion that standing-to-lying changes make application difficult [PAGE 20] and by the sliding failure cases [PAGE 15–16]. Verifiable prediction: compute \(\Delta H=\bar H_{\text{same}}-\bar H_{\text{diff}}\). Veto if \(\Delta H\ge 0\) on the annotated sliding pair, or if the proposed method’s ID-switch rate exceeds its walking/running rate on the same camera.

Construct a scene with strong backlight and long shadows so that one player produces multiple moving-region bounding rectangles or a shadow blob overlaps the player. Prediction: the 0.6 overlap rule misclassifies occlusion/separation; one player is treated as multiple regions; the representative-player state is updated with shadow features; the box drifts. This follows the paper’s reported shadow/backlight degradation [PAGE 17–18] and the table-8 failure cause [PAGE 20]. Verifiable prediction: count moving blobs per annotated player and compute the overlap ratio \(O(B_t,B_{t-1})\).
