\[
e=(t_{\rm start},t_{\rm end},a_{\rm hand},a_{\rm dist},a_{\rm tech},a_{\rm target},a_{\rm eff}),
\]
with \(a_{\rm hand}\in\{\mathrm{Lead},\mathrm{Rear}\}\), \(a_{\rm dist}\in\{\mathrm{Long},\mathrm{Mid},\mathrm{Close}\}\), \(a_{\rm tech}\in\{\mathrm{Straight},\mathrm{Hook},\mathrm{Uppercut}\}\), \(a_{\rm target}\in\{\mathrm{Head},\mathrm{Torso}\}\), and \(a_{\rm eff}\in\{\mathrm{Effective},\mathrm{Ineffective}\}\) [PAGE 3]. Units: \(t_{\rm start},t_{\rm end}\) in seconds; other attributes categorical. The range operator uses arm-length units \(L_{\rm arm}\) in metres: long range \(d\in(1.0L_{\rm arm},\infty)\), mid range \(d\in(0.5L_{\rm arm},1.0L_{\rm arm})\), close range \(d\in(0,0.5L_{\rm arm})\), matching Table S1 [PAGE 16] and Fig. 1 [PAGE 3]. Breakdown regimes: if \(L_{\rm arm}=0\) or is uncalibrated, the zone partition collapses; at the exact boundaries \(d=0.5L_{\rm arm}\) or \(d=1.0L_{\rm arm}\), the category is ambiguous unless a tie rule is fixed.

Temporal operators. The paper defines “an attack interval as a pause exceeding one second” and classifies strikes “responding within 0.2 seconds” as counter punches [PAGE 5]. Formally, for pause \(\Delta t_{\rm pause}>1\,{\rm s}\), the first strike is proactive; for response latency \(\Delta t_{\rm response}\le 0.2\,{\rm s}\), it is a counter. Units: seconds. Breakdown: equality at \(1\,{\rm s}\) or \(0.2\,{\rm s}\) needs deterministic tie handling; latency measurement error near \(0.2\,{\rm s}\) dominates the counter indicator, consistent with the lowest correlation “Proportion of Counter Punches (r=0.579)” [PAGE 21].

Indicator aggregation. “Prop.” indicators are proportions, dimensionless in \([0,1]\), e.g. \(i_k=N_k/N_{\rm total}\); “No. (per minute)” indicators are counts per minute, units \(\mathrm{min}^{-1}\), e.g. \(\rho_k=N_k/T_{\rm match}\). Table 1 lists 18 indicators across six categories [PAGE 4]. Singularities: if \(N_{\rm total}=0\), the proportion is undefined; if \(T_{\rm match}=0\), the count rate is undefined. A zero-denominator convention must be pre-registered, otherwise downstream MLP inputs are not identified.

Graph and time-variant embedding. The source models the embedding as
\[
E_b(t)=\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}E_b^{(c)}t^c,
\]
where \(D\) is latent feature dimension and \(C\) is time order [PAGE 6]. Units: \(E_b(t)\) is a dimensionless latent vector; \(t\) should be expressed in years since a fixed reference epoch, with coefficients absorbing inverse-time powers. Breakdown: as \(t\to\infty\), polynomial extrapolation is unbounded; high \(C\) creates collinearity and overfitting; without a strict past-only mask, edges with \(t'>t\) can leak future outcomes into \(E_b(t)\).

Fusion and loss. The source fuses inputs by
\[
F_{\rm match}={\rm MLP}_{\rm fusion}(I_{b,\rm ind}\oplus E_b(t)\oplus I_{o,\rm ind}\oplus E_o(t)),
\]
where \(\oplus\) denotes concatenation [PAGE 6]. Training uses
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\rm total}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\rm MSE}(\hat I_{\rm curr},I_{\rm GT})+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}(\hat y,y_{\rm GT}),
\]
with cross-entropy for match outcome and MSE for indicator prediction [PAGE 6]. Units: \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm CE}\) in nats if natural log is used; \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm MSE}\) in squared indicator units; \(\alpha,\beta\) dimensionless weights. The source later reports CE weight \(1\) and indicator-error weight \(0.02\) [PAGE 20]. Breakdown: CE gradient saturates as \(\hat y\to0\) or \(\hat y\to1\); MSE dominates if indicator scales differ; ReLU MLPs are non-differentiable at zero pre-activations.

Units: probability per indicator unit; for proportions, probability per unit proportion; for count rates, probability times minutes. Breakdown: the gradient is a local linearization, not a causal effect; if the model is saturated or non-smooth, the ranking is unstable; if the optimizer ignores feasibility constraints, the recommended direction may be physically unrealizable.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from scalar rating systems to structured tactical representation. The paper compares against Glicko, Elo, and WHR, which “plateau at 60.3% accuracy” [PAGE 7], illustrating the historical reduction of a boxer to one scalar. Earlier boxing AI is described as “largely confined to basic action classification” [PAGE 2], while team-sport analytics already used tracking, event recognition, and tactical modeling [PAGE 2]. BoxMind’s genealogy is therefore a fusion of four lineages: performance-analysis indicator systems (Table 1) [PAGE 4], graph-based matchup modeling with learnable embeddings [PAGE 6], differentiable saliency/backpropagation for strategy recommendation [PAGE 7], and closed-loop deployment in elite sport [PAGE 8]. The retrocausal risk is that the system is presented as having generated pre-competition recommendations, but the validation narrative was assembled after the Olympic outcome. The paper’s own phrase “provides causal evidence of the system’s impact” [PAGE 10] is a retrospective causal claim, not a randomized identification strategy.

Multi-week traps are concentrated in the Li Qian case. The intervention spans “from January to July 2024” [PAGE 8], and the reported shift is “from 28.5% to 39.0%” in close- and mid-range punch proportion [PAGE 8]. Over such a multi-week window, coaching changes, opponent scouting, maturation, training load, injury status, and regression to the mean can all produce the same indicator movement. A second trap is multiple comparisons: 18 indicators are screened and the top-5 positive gradients are selected [PAGE 7], so apparent alignment with competition behavior can be a selection artifact. A third trap is ground-truth circularity in the extended analysis, because the paper uses a “majority voting protocol that integrates annotations from all four experts and the BoxMind system” [PAGE 22]. A fourth trap is temporal: the test set is “matches post-July 1, 2023” [PAGE 7], but the provided text does not explicitly prove that graph construction and embedding training were strictly past-only. Without that proof, the model’s historical lineage can contaminate its apparent foresight.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. Under fatigue, pressure, or a close score, a boxer tends to regress to the most overlearned pattern; in the paper’s indicator language this appears as high proactive rhythm and repeated combination complexity, e.g. straight-straight sequences [PAGE 4]. If BoxMind recommends increasing an already habitual indicator, it may amplify predictability rather than create advantage. The recursive model must therefore distinguish adaptive variation from stress-induced regression, not merely rank positive gradients.

Counter-trap. The gradient operator assumes the opponent’s profile is fixed: \(i_b^\star=\arg\max_{i_b\in\mathcal{F}}\hat y(i_b,I_o,E_b,E_o)\). But a thinking opponent can bait that best response, so the true problem is closer to \(i_b^\star=\arg\max_{i_b\in\mathcal{F}}\min_{i_o\in\mathcal{F}_o}\hat y(i_b,i_o,E_b,E_o)\). The paper’s counter-punch definition—responding “within 0.2 seconds” [PAGE 5]—is exactly the layer where a counter-trap operates: a recommended proactive pattern can be timed and punished. The recursive theory of mind has at least three levels: the boxer models the opponent, the opponent models the boxer’s new habit, and the coaching staff models how the opponent will adapt to the BoxMind-informed strategy. A one-step gradient is not a fixed point of this recursion.

Clock. The system’s clock is both micro and macro. Micro-clock: \(>1\,{\rm s}\) attack interval and \(0.2\,{\rm s}\) counter window [PAGE 5]. Macro-clock: tournament schedule and between-round adaptation. The paper states, “Currently, BoxMind operates as a post-match or pre-match planning tool” [PAGE 10], and real-time between-round adjustment is future work [PAGE 10]. Therefore, the recursive loop is truncated: the system cannot update the opponent model inside the bout. Breakdown occurs when the clock is faster than the model’s inference cycle, when the opponent’s adaptation is nonstationary, or when no pure best-response equilibrium exists. The correct counter-trap is not “more of the top gradient” but a constrained, mixed, and opponent-anticipating policy.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. A recommendation is admissible only if it lies in the physically realizable set \(\mathcal{F}_{\rm phys}\). The distance layer gives exact veto predicates: close range requires \(d\in(0,0.5L_{\rm arm})\), mid range \(d\in(0.5L_{\rm arm},1.0L_{\rm arm})\), and long range \(d\in(1.0L_{\rm arm},\infty)\) [PAGE 16]. If the model recommends more close-range effective punches but the boxer cannot enter that interval without being clinched, or if \(L_{\rm arm}\) is misestimated, the recommendation is vetoed. The effect layer is also physical: “Effective” requires “the knuckle area of either clenched fist” and excludes “the side of the glove, heel of the hand, palm, or any part of the glove other than the knuckle” [PAGE 16]. A gradient that increases “effective” counts without a contact-feasibility check is therefore invalid.

Biomechanical veto. Lead/rear identity is stance-dependent: “a left-foot-forward posture defines an Orthodox stance where the left hand is the Lead” and a right-foot-forward posture defines Southpaw [PAGE 15-16]. If stance classification is wrong, the lead-hand proportion indicator and its gradient are inverted. Hook versus uppercut ambiguity is acknowledged by the need for localized video evidence [PAGE 19]. Fatigue, injury, reach, hip-shoulder timing, and guard geometry can make a recommended combination mechanically unavailable even when the statistical gradient is positive.

Weather and environment veto. The provided pages contain no weather covariate; the model’s inputs are video, pose, punch attributes, indicators, embeddings, and outcomes [PAGE 3-7]. Therefore weather is an unmodeled layer. If true outcome is \(y=f(I_b,I_o,E_b,E_o,W,\epsilon)\) while the model estimates \(\hat y=f(I_b,I_o,E_b,E_o;\theta)\), then any nonzero \(\partial y/\partial W\) with unobserved \(W\) confounds the recommendation. Indoor Olympic boxing reduces direct weather exposure, but heat, humidity, hydration, sweat, glove friction, and air quality can still alter work rate and effective contact. The veto rule is: if \(W\notin\mathcal{W}_{\rm admissible}\), reject high-volume or high-range-change recommendations until the environment is conditioned or the model is recalibrated. Extraction quality is a related veto: broadcast video ranges “from 640 × 360 to 1920 × 1080 at 30 fps” [PAGE 15], and tracking deteriorates under shot transitions and occlusion [PAGE 16]. If identity or punch detection confidence falls below the validated regime, the indicator vector itself is out of distribution and the gradient must be vetoed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
