<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression: when \(\delta\) is tiny, an analyst’s habit is to regress to the familiar symmetric-Schwarz mental model—condition number, CG, volume RAS, “small overlap means slow convergence.” That habit is exactly the wrong prior here because the ICDD Schur complement is non-symmetric and the relevant object is the field of values of \(\tilde\Sigma\), not \(\kappa(\Sigma)\). The counter-trap is to exploit the weak/dual formulations: the paper reports that “the convergence rate of the dual weak ICDD is almost independent of \(\delta\)” [PAGE 26], while dual iterations cost about twice as much per iteration [PAGE 11], so the rational policy is to switch only when the iteration reduction exceeds the per-iteration factor. The clock is the iteration budget: the theorem gives a \(\delta^{-1}p^{3/2}\log p\) worst-case clock, independent of \(h\) [PAGE 1], [PAGE 22], but the empirical clock for strong ICDD is nearly \(p\)-flat and can be much weaker in \(\delta\) under coefficient jumps [PAGE 26], [PAGE 2]. A second-order theory-of-mind move is to model the reviewer’s likely objection—“your bound is pessimistic”—and answer with the source’s own distinction between upper bound and observed rate: “Corollary 8 seems to overestimate the number of GMRES iterations for ICDD” [PAGE 26]. A third move is to model the implementation agent’s temptation to use non-conforming meshes because they are easy in ICDD; the source permits that practically but vetoes the positivity guarantee [PAGE 19], [PAGE 37]. Thus the correct policy is: measure \(\delta\), \(p\), coefficient jump, and conformity; use the theorem as a conservative veto clock; use dual/weak variants as counter-traps only when their measured iteration savings beat the doubled per-iteration cost; and never infer a positive-real GMRES guarantee outside the conforming regime.
