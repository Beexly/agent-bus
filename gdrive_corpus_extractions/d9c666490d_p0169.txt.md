Crisis habit regression: under pressure—e.g., a fat-tailed shock or a regime switch—modelers habitually regress to finite-dimensional intuition. They truncate the infinite product at depth $N$, set $A_i=X_i$ for all $i>N$, and treat the tail as a neutral background of measure 1. This is exactly the rectangle approximation sanctioned by Halmos’s definition [PAGE 154]. It is valid only when the event under study depends on finitely many coordinates and when the tail factors are genuinely neutral ($\mu_i(X_i)=1$). During a prolonged crisis, the assumption that “everything after week $N$ is background” fails precisely because the crisis is defined by its duration across infinitely many future coordinates.

Counter-trap: the reflexive belief that because every marginal has been normalized to a probability measure ($\mu_i(X_i)=1$), the infinite product is automatically well-defined and every pathwise statement is legitimate. This counter-trap ignores (i) the need for projective consistency of finite-dimensional marginals, (ii) the distinction between $\bigotimes_i\mathcal{S}_i$ and the $\sigma$-algebra of all subsets, and (iii) the fact that adaptive re-normalization at each stage (each week’s posterior integrates to 1) does not preserve an unconditional product law unless the normalization constants telescope correctly across the infinite horizon. The counter-trap is therefore the belief that local probability normalization guarantees global product measurability.

Clock: the truncation depth $N$ (or equivalently the physical time horizon $T$) is the clock variable. As $N\to\infty$, the finite-cylinder measures converge to the product measure only if tightness and consistency hold. Without them, the limit is singular: reversing the order of limits—first letting $N\to\infty$ then evaluating a tail functional, versus fixing a large but finite $N$—yields different results.
