Historically, Edward Thorndike’s law of effect treated learning as a blind, trial-and-error strengthening of S-R bonds. Tolman, writing in 1932, injected the teleological concept of "purposive" behavior into this rigid materialist framework. He retro-causally re-interpreted the S-R bond not as a simple reflex arc, but as a mediated process involving "intervening variables." This intervention paved the way for cognitive science decades before its official inception. 

Scientifically, the genealogy connects to cybernetics (Wiener) and later predictive processing (Karl Friston). Tolman’s "means-end expectations" [PAGE 412] are the exact precursor to modern Reinforcement Learning (RL) value functions. However, a multi-week trap emerges when applying this historical lens to current neuroscientific validation. Researchers often fall into the trap of "post-hoc cognitive attribution"—observing complex rat navigation in a maze and assuming the presence of a "cognitive map" purely because Tolman theorized it. The trap lies in mistaking the mathematical elegance of the intervening variables for direct neuroanatomical correlates. While place cells support the idea of spatial mapping, the specific categorization of "demands" and "readinesses" remains highly abstract, making experimental isolation notoriously difficult. The trap forces researchers to invent ad-hoc neural substrates for every one of Tolman's subdivisions, leading to over-parameterized and unfalsifiable cognitive models.
