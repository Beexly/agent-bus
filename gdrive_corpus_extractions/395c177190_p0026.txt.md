Lineage. James’s page sits at the intersection of 19th-century physiological optics and early experimental psychology. Helmholtz’s Handbuch der physiologischen Optik (1866; 3rd ed. 1909) treated simultaneous contrast as partly retinal, partly cerebral, with irradiation and contrast as complementary distortions—James inherits the assumption that contrast has both a retinal modification and an attentional visibility condition [PAGE 1]. Ewald Hering’s opponent-colors theory (Zur Lehre vom Lichtsinne, 1878; 1920) provides the black–white, red–green, blue–yellow channel structure implicit in James’s separation of “contrast of brightness” from “colorcontrast” [PAGE 1]. Ernst Mach’s analysis of contrast (1865) and Mach bands anticipates the spatial-intermediate-field case: borders and shaded contours suppress local contrast through lateral interactions, prefiguring the black-line mechanism [PAGE 1].

Modern descendants. Hartline (1949) demonstrated lateral inhibition in the Limulus retina; Kuffler (1953) identified center–surround receptive fields in cat retinal ganglion cells, giving the kernel K(x) and scale λ used above. Hubel and Wiesel (1962) extended contrast selectivity to cortical simple cells. Land and McCann’s Retinex theory (1971) formalized lightness/color constancy under unequal illumination, directly addressing James’s complaint that natural objects rarely satisfy equal-brightness, homogeneous conditions [PAGE 1]. The normalization model (Heeger, 1992; Carandini & Heeger, 2012) formalizes P_C ∝ M_C/(1 + η|M_L|) as divisive contrast gain control.

Multi-week traps. Dark adaptation is not multi-week (cones ~5–10 min; rods ~30–40 min), but contingent aftereffects such as the McCollough effect can persist for days to weeks after prolonged exposure to oriented colored gratings, producing a long-lived adaptive set that violates the assumption of a neutral baseline. Another trap: assuming James’s “best contrast” optimum (equal brightness, medium intensity, homogeneous texture) is a static physical law rather than a photopic, fixated, normal-trichromat operating point. Under long-term exposure to saturated or high-luminance-range environments, the observer’s adaptation state drifts, shifting η and g_crit and making cross-session contrast judgments non-stationary.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three interlocking constructs: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

Crisis habit regression. Under cognitive load or perceptual crisis, the observer regresses from analytic, low-level feature encoding to habitual gist-based object parsing. In James’s woolen-cloth case [PAGE 1], the distant viewer registers “cloth” (homogeneous surface) and sees the spot; the close-up viewer parses “fibres” (intricate surface texture) and the spot disappears. The regression is not a failure of acuity but a prior-dominant mode: the perceptual system treats the surface as a textured object rather than a two-field contrast stimulus, raising the effective decision criterion β for spot detection.

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is that the very act of verifying contrast by closer inspection destroys the conditions that produced it. Closer examination increases spatial resolution (reduces σ_blur relative to fiber pitch), raises effective field separation Δx, and introduces black-line/shadow structure at fiber boundaries; per [PAGE 1], “such lines separate other fields too far for them to distinctly influence one another,” and ρ(Δx) collapses. The observer seeking evidence for contrast thereby removes the spatial integration (ρ → 1) that made the signal detectable—a self-defeating verification loop.

Clock. The temporal dimension bounds the observation window. Negative after-images decay with retinal recovery time constant τ_ret (seconds); attentional capture transients operate on tens-to-hundreds-of-milliseconds scales; dark and chromatic adaptation operate on minutes. If the observer’s hypothesis–test cycle (attend → inspect → judge) exceeds τ_ret, the after-image is gone before contrast can be confirmed [PAGE 1]: “a faint negative after-image … may become invisible.” Recursive structure: the observer models their own attention; under crisis, the model regresses to the habitual prior, misses the counter-trap of close inspection, and the clock expires on the retinal signal. The result is a false negative: contrast existed physically but was never available to the regressed, clock-limited observer.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Veto hierarchy across physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers, using [PAGE 1] conditions as premises.

Physical layer (retinal/optical). Absolute veto: field absorptance ρ_abs → 1 in a dark or intensely colored field nullifies induced color regardless of retinal health, H_ind ∝ (1 − ρ_abs)·I_field → 0 [PAGE 1]. Proof: measure spectral reflectance of each field; if the contrasting field’s absorptance exceeds ~0.9, predicted induction falls below detection. Secondary physical veto: photopigment bleaching/regeneration limits A_ret(t); if the inter-stimulus interval is shorter than τ_ret, residual adaptation contaminates the baseline and inverts the sign of the after-image.

Biomechanical layer (oculomotor). Fixation stability is a temporal veto gate. Saccades at ~30–70°/s induce saccadic suppression; microsaccades and drift reset retinal adaptation. Pupil diameter controls optical blur σ_blur: miosis increases depth of field, rendering woolen fibers distinguishable and destroying spot contrast via the close-examination mechanism [PAGE 1]; mydriasis blurs fibers, restoring homogeneity. Veto condition: if fixation is unstable or pupil dynamics change σ_blur across the comparison, ρ(Δx) is not stationary and contrast judgments are invalid.

Environmental/weather layer (illumination and atmosphere). Natural scenes violate the Jamesian optimum with high probability [PAGE 1]: “Such conditions do not often occur naturally, the disturbing influences being present in case of almost all ordinary objects.” Shading creates |M_L| ≫ |M_C|, triggering brightness-contrast masking of color contrast [PAGE 1]. Atmospheric path radiance and glare compress luminance range and shift illuminant chromaticity, altering E and M_C non-uniformly across the field. Veto condition: outdoor or unevenly lit scenes fail the equal-brightness, medium-intensity, homogeneous-texture premises; predicted contrast effects are vetoed unless illuminant and luminance are controlled.

Adjudication order. (1) Environmental veto first: verify homogeneous texture, approximately equal brightness, and controlled illuminant; if violated, stop—natural-scene disturbing influences dominate [PAGE 1]. (2) Biomechanical veto second: require stable fixation within the after-image decay window τ_ret and constant pupil state; if saccades or blur changes occur, the spatial kernel is non-stationary. (3) Physical veto third: check absorptance ρ_abs and photopigment state; if ρ_abs → 1 or A_ret is contaminated, induction is physically null. Only if all three layers pass does the chromatic contrast model P_C ∝ M_C/(1 + η|M_L|) apply.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable veto scenarios, each with a formal veto condition and a falsifiable prediction.

Veto Scenario 1 — Luminance-chromatic masking.
Setup: Two chromatic patches with fixed chromatic contrast M_C, independently varied luminance contrast M_L via heterochromatic photometry. Veto condition: |M_L| > 2·|M_C|. Prediction: The simultaneous hue shift predicted from M_C alone is absent, reduced, or replaced by a brightness-contrast shift; P_C ≈ 0 under the divisive model P_C ∝ M_C/(1 + η|M_L|). Null test: equiluminating the patches (M_L ≈ 0) restores the hue shift at identical M_C. Verifiable with hue cancellation or nulling. This implements James’s [PAGE 1] claim that brightness contrast interferes with color contrast and makes it imperceptible.

Veto Scenario 2 — Black-line spatial separation.
Setup: Two identical color patches at fixed center distance d; introduce a narrow black separator line of width w at the midpoint, absorptance ρ_abs > 0.9. Veto condition: effective separation Δx > 2λ (λ ≈ 0.3°) or ρ(Δx) < 0.2. Prediction: Induced contrast falls to baseline (no difference from a spatially scrambled control) even though physical luminance and chromatic contrast are unchanged; removing the line at fixed d restores induction. Verifiable with a spatial contrast sensitivity or induction-nulling paradigm. This implements [PAGE 1]: narrow dark intermediate fields separate fields “too far for them to distinctly influence one another.”

Veto Scenario 3 — Attentional capture of an after-image.
Setup: Induce a stable negative after-image by fixation, then present a central luminance transient (capture stimulus >100 cd·m⁻², 20 ms) during after-image probing. Veto condition: attentional gain drops such that d′ = g_att·|μ_signal − μ_noise|/σ < β. Prediction: Observers report the after-image as invisible despite identical retinal stimulation; the detection threshold for the after-image rises by a factor ≥ 2 relative to a no-capture control. Retinal adaptation (measured via sensitivity recovery or pupillometry) remains intact, dissociating physiology from perception. This implements [PAGE 1]: a faint negative after-image “undoubtedly due to retinal modifications” may become invisible when attention is entirely occupied.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
