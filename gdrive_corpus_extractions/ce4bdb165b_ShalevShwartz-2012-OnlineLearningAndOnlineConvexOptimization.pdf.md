The source is a mathematical survey of online learning, not a physical or event-forecasting document. Its central operator is the conversion of sequential prediction into regret minimization under convexity. The first move is negative: with no distributional assumption, an adversary can force arbitrary cumulative loss; the source states that “no algorithm can obtain a sublinear regret bound even if |H| = 2” [PAGE 8]. The two escape routes are realizability and randomization. Realizability yields mistake bounds such as Halving’s “MHalving(H) ≤log2(|H|)” [PAGE 10]. Randomization yields expected regret on the order of √(ln(|H|)T) [PAGE 12]. Both are then reinterpreted as convexiﬁcation: discrete expert selection becomes a probability-simplex prediction, and nonconvex 0–1 loss is upper-bounded by a convex surrogate [PAGE 18–20].

The second move is the online convex optimization model: predict w_t ∈ S, receive convex f_t, pay f_t(w_t), and compare with a competing u by Regret_T(u)=Σ(f_t(w_t)−f_t(u)) [PAGE 15]. Follow-the-leader is unstable: on the linear example in [PAGE 23], “the regret of FTL is T,” so FTL cannot be treated as universally safe. Follow-the-regularized-leader adds R and decomposes regret into a regularizer gap plus a stability term [PAGE 24]. Strong convexity of R converts stability into a finite regret: the source proves ∥w_t−w_{t+1}∥ ≤ L/σ and then Regret_T(u) ≤ R(u)−min R + T L²/σ [PAGE 33–34]. This is the key gray-zone: if σ=0, or if L is unbounded, the bound is not merely loose; it is undefined or infinite. Online mirror descent is the recursive form of the same operator, with link g and update θ_{t+1}=θ_t−z_t [PAGE 38]. Duality tightens the Euclidean case by a factor √2 [PAGE 47].

The third move is limited feedback. The bandit estimator z_t[j]=y_t[j]/w_t[j] when j=p_t and 0 otherwise is unbiased [PAGE 76], but it divides by w_t[j]; zero or very small probabilities are a technical singularity. The multi-armed bandit bound acquires an extra factor d relative to full-information Weighted Majority: the source explicitly notes “an additional factor of d” because the learner receives only 1/d of the feedback [PAGE 77]. **1. Regret and mistake-bound operators.** For a hypothesis h⋆ and sequence of T examples, the source defines
\[
\mathrm{Regret}_T(h^\star)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(p_t,y_t)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(h^\star(x_t),y_t),
\]
and
\[
\mathrm{Regret}_T(H)=\max_{h^\star\in H}\mathrm{Regret}_T(h^\star).
\]
These are cumulative loss differences in loss units; if losses are normalized to [0,1], regret is dimensionless. The source states the adversary can force the opposite answer [PAGE 6] and that “no algorithm can obtain a sublinear regret bound even if |H| = 2” [PAGE 8]. Halving gives the count bound
\[
M_{\mathrm{Halving}}(H)\leq \log_2(|H|)
\]
[PAGE 10]. If log is natural, the information term is nats; if base 2, it is bits. Randomization gives expected regret
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{T}|p_t-y_t|-\min_{h\in H}\sum_{t=1}^{T}|h(x_t)-y_t|
\leq \sqrt{0.5\ln(|H|)T}
\]
[PAGE 12]. These are counts or expected loss units, not ratios.

**2. Online convex optimization operator.** The model is:
\[
\text{for }t=1,\dots,T:\quad w_t\in S,\quad f_t:S\to\mathbb R \text{ convex},\quad \text{pay }f_t(w_t),
\]
with
\[
\mathrm{Regret}_T(u)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_t(w_t)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_t(u).
\]
See [PAGE 15]. Units: f_t and regret are in loss units; w_t and u are in the parameter space S; subgradients z_t have units of loss per parameter.

**3. FTL and FoReL stability.** FTL predicts
\[
w_t=\arg\min_{w\in S}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}f_i(w).
\]
The source’s failure example gives FTL regret T while u=0 has loss 0 [PAGE 23]. FoReL instead uses
\[
w_t=\arg\min_{w\in S}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}f_i(w)+R(w)\right)
\]
[PAGE 23–24]. Lemma 2.3 gives
\[
\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f_t(w_t)-f_t(u))
\leq R(u)-R(w_1)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}(f_t(w_t)-f_t(w_{t+1}))
\]
[PAGE 24]. If R is σ-strongly convex and f_t is L_t-Lipschitz, then
\[
f_t(w_t)-f_t(w_{t+1})\leq L_t\|w_t-w_{t+1}\|\leq L_t^2/\sigma
\]
[PAGE 33–34], yielding
\[
\mathrm{Regret}_T(u)\leq R(u)-\min_{v\in S}R(v)+T L^2/\sigma
\]
[PAGE 34]. **Breakdown regimes:** σ=0 makes the bound undefined or infinite; unbounded L makes it infinite; nonconvex f_t invalidates the subgradient linearization; T=0 is trivial but T unknown requires the doubling trick [PAGE 26].

**4. Online mirror descent and duality.** OMD uses a link g and update
\[
w_t=g(\theta_t),\quad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-z_t,\quad z_t\in\partial f_t(w_t)
\]
[PAGE 38]. Theorem 2.15 gives
\[
\mathrm{Regret}_T(u)\leq R(u)-\min_{v\in S}R(v)+\eta\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|z_t\|_\star^2
\]
[PAGE 38]. The dual analysis improves the Euclidean constant by a factor √2: the source states “we have obtained a factor of √2 improvement in the regret bound” [PAGE 47]. Local-norm bounds require the condition “ηz_t[i] ≥−1” [PAGE 49]; if violated, the inequality e^{-a}≤1−a+a² used in the proof does not hold, so the local-norm bound is vetoed.

**5. Bandit operator.** The unbiased gradient estimate is
\[
z_t[j]=\begin{cases}
y_t[j]/w_t[j], & j=p_t,\\
0, & j\neq p_t,
\end{cases}
\]
[PAGE 76]. The multi-armed bandit bound is
\[
\mathbb E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_t[p_t]\right]
\leq \min_i\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_t[i]+\frac{\log d}{\eta}+\eta dT,
\]
and with η=√(log(d)/(dT)) gives \(2\sqrt{d\log(d)T}\) [PAGE 77]. The extra factor d is explicit; applying the full-information Weighted Majority bound √(log d T) to bandit feedback is a theorem violation. General black-box bandit OCO yields \(O(\sqrt{BdFL}\,T^{3/4})\) [PAGE 80–81]. **Singularity:** w_t[j]=0 makes the estimator formula undefined; in practice positivity or smoothing is required.

**6. Online-to-batch operator.** With randomization,
\[
\mathbb E[C(\bar w)]=\mathbb E\left[\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_t(w_t)\right];
\]
with averaging, the same inequality requires C convex and Jensen’s inequality [PAGE 84–85]. If C is nonconvex, the averaging guarantee is vetoed; only randomization remains exact under the stated assumptions.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
**Crisis habit regression.** Under uncertainty, a learner may regress to naive FTL or Consistent. The source shows why this is a theorem-level failure, not a personality flaw: FTL can have regret T on linear losses [PAGE 23], and Consistent merely has \(M_{\mathrm{Consistent}}(H)\leq|H|-1\) [PAGE 10]. Halving improves this to log₂|H| by majority vote over the version space [PAGE 10].

**Counter-trap.** The environment can model the learner’s deterministic strategy: it can wait for the prediction and return the opposite answer [PAGE 6], and Cover’s impossibility shows no sublinear guarantee for even two hypotheses without randomization or realizability [PAGE 8]. Randomization is meaningful only if the adversary does not know the learner’s current random coins [PAGE 11]. Against a fully adversarial environment, the Standard Optimal Algorithm is optimal in the realizable case, with \(M_{\mathrm{SOA}}(H)=\mathrm{Ldim}(H)\) and no algorithm can do strictly better [PAGE 60]. In the unrealizable case, the source constructs experts by simulating SOA and assuming mistakes at selected rounds [PAGE 62], yielding regret \(O(\sqrt{\mathrm{Ldim}(H)\ln(T)T})\) [PAGE 61]. This is recursive theory of mind in algorithmic form: the learner models the environment’s shattering tree, and the environment models the learner’s prediction rule.

**Clock.** The horizon T is a control parameter. When T is unknown, the doubling trick restarts the algorithm on periods of size \(2^m\), paying a constant multiplicative factor [PAGE 26]. The clock also changes regret exponents: full-information OCO gives √T-type bounds, while black-box bandit OCO gives \(T^{3/4}\) [PAGE 81]. A long-horizon agent that ignores the clock, the feedback model, or the strong-convexity constant will overclaim safety. The correct recursive stance is: choose the operator, verify its assumptions, then interpret the bound only within those assumptions.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
