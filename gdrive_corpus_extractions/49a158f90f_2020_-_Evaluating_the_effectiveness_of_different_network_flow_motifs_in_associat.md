Let \(i=1,\dots,N\) index motifs, with \(N=203{,}208\) in the fitted model after exclusions [PAGE 7]. The binary outcome is
\[
Y_i^M \in \{0,1\},\qquad
Y_i^M=1 \iff \text{motif }i\text{ leads to a shot within }15\text{ s of its last pass},
\]
where the 15 s window is verbatim from the source: the shot “must be made within 15 s of the last pass of the sequence” [PAGE 4]. Units: \(Y_i^M\) is dimensionless; time is seconds (s).

The logistic link and inverse link are
\[
\eta_i=\operatorname{logit}(P_i)=\log\!\left(\frac{P_i}{1-P_i}\right),
\qquad
P_i=\Pr(Y_i^M=1\mid \cdot)=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}(\eta_i)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\eta_i}}.
\]
The source states that independent variables are related “via the logit link function” and that the conditional distribution is Bernoulli with inverse logit [PAGE 3]. Units: \(P_i\in[0,1]\) is a probability; \(\eta_i\) is dimensionless on the logit scale.

The GAM additive predictor is
\[
\eta_i = X_i^\top\beta + \sum_{j=1}^{J} f_j(x_{ji}),
\]
where \(X_i^\top\beta\) are fixed effects and \(f_1,\dots,f_J\) are smooth functions [PAGE 3]. The motif model uses fixed effects for motif type and smooth terms for continuous covariates; the source reports a Wald test for fixed effects and treats continuous covariates as smooth terms [PAGE 5–7]. A standard identifiability assumption is that each smooth is centered, e.g. \(\sum_i f_j(x_{ji})=0\) or \(\int f_j(x)\,dx=0\); without such a constraint the intercept and smooths are not separately identified. The source does not print the centering constraint, so this is an explicit modeling assumption, not a source quote.

The pass-ability aggregate is
\[
S_{i,m}=\sum_{r=1}^{3}\hat p_{i,m,r},\qquad m\in\{\text{difficulty},\text{risk},\text{potential}\},
\]
where \(\hat p_{i,m,r}\in[0,1]\) is the predicted success probability for pass \(r\) under criterion \(m\); hence \(S_{i,m}\in[0,3]\). The source says these are “the sum of the predicted probabilities of success for the three passes in the motif” [PAGE 5]. Units: probability sum, dimensionless.

The centrality aggregates are
\[
C_{i,q}=\sum_{s\in \mathcal{M}_i} c_{q,\pi(s)},
\]
where \(\mathcal{M}_i\) is the four-slot multiset of motif players, \(\pi(s)\) is the player in slot \(s\), and \(c_{q,p}\in[0,1]\) is player \(p\)'s team-relative centrality score for measure \(q\). Thus closeness, betweenness, and clustering sums lie in \([0,4]\); PageRank passer/recipient sums over the three relevant slots lie in \([0,3]\) [PAGE 5]. The source notes that “Players involved more than once in a motif are added accordingly to the sums considered” [PAGE 5]. Units: normalized dimensionless scores.

The zone operator is
\[
Z_i=\left|\{\,z_{1}^{\text{start}},z_{1}^{\text{end}},z_{2}^{\text{start}},z_{2}^{\text{end}},z_{3}^{\text{start}},z_{3}^{\text{end}}\,\}\right|,
\]
with \(z\in\{1,\dots,21\}\), so \(Z_i\in\{1,\dots,6\}\). The source states “The pitch is divided into 21 unique zones” and “a total of six zones may be involved in a flow motif” [PAGE 6]. Although \(Z_i\) is discrete, the source says it is “interpreted as a continuous variable” to permit a GAM smooth [PAGE 6]. Units: count of unique zones.

Assumptions and breakdown regimes:
1. Conditional Bernoulli independence is assumed by the logistic likelihood. If motifs within the same match are strongly dependent through possession state, opponent press, score state, or player availability, standard errors are too small. The source does not add match/team random effects in the motif GAM; it explicitly suggests future mixed effects and says more data would be needed [PAGE 12].
2. Complete or quasi-complete separation breaks the fixed-effect MLE: if a rare motif type perfectly predicts no shot, the coefficient diverges. The source’s sparse motif counts and retention of only two non-reference motif types make this a real gray zone [PAGE 7–8].
3. Smooth-term collinearity breaks interpretability: “Some of the network metrics are highly correlated” [PAGE 12]. In that regime, individual smooth signs and confidence bands cannot be read as independent causal effects.
4. Sparse covariate regions produce wide confidence intervals; the paper itself says low-risk-sum regions have “much larger confidence intervals, indicating fewer observations and more uncertainty” [PAGE 9].
5. The 5-s pass threshold and 15-s shot window are definitional singularities: changing them redefines the motif and outcome, so estimates are not invariant to administrative threshold changes [PAGE 4, PAGE 6].
6. Zone discretization into 21 zones and motif combinations is combinatorial; the source says a zone-motif analysis with 21 zones and motif size four was “too extensive” [PAGE 13].
7. The outcome is shot, not goal: any claim about scoring is outside the estimand [PAGE 4, PAGE 13].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from general network motifs to football-specific flow motifs and then to shot-effectiveness modeling. Milo et al. (2002) supply the network-motif concept; Gyarmati et al. (2014) introduce flow motifs in football and analyze “three consecutive passes” to study style [PAGE 1–2]. Peña and Navarro (2015) extend motifs to the player level by averaging a player’s occurrences in each motif type; Bekkers and Dabadghao (2019) separate possession motifs from motifs leading to goalscoring opportunities; Perdomo Meza (2017) argues “the optimal size of motifs is four” and adds spatial information; Malqui et al. (2019) add ball trajectories and visualization [PAGE 2]. Håland et al. (2020) build three pass-ability GAMMs for difficulty, risk, and potential; Wiig et al. (2019) convert those outputs into weighted passing networks and centrality measures [PAGE 2–3]. This paper then uses those outputs as motif-level covariates in a GAM for shot generation [PAGE 3–5]. The retrocausal point is that the later “effectiveness” result is built on earlier “style” and “pass quality” constructs: the motif is not a raw tactical object but a compressed label whose value depends on the upstream pass model and network normalization.

Multi-week traps follow directly. First, a team can look “compact” for several weeks because of opponent quality, game state, or player availability, not because compactness is a stable trait; the motif model includes opponent-Elo only inside the upstream pass model, not as a direct motif-model control [PAGE 4]. Second, the paper’s team table is a single-season descriptive snapshot, and the source says there is “ostensibly no connection” between table position and motif distribution [PAGE 12]. Third, the three teams with the highest ABCD share are at the bottom of the table, while some low-ABCD teams have high shot rates [PAGE 10]; a multi-week analyst who treats ABCD share as a quality proxy will be trapped. Fourth, because “some of the motifs are used far less than others,” short windows over-weight rare motif types and destabilize estimates [PAGE 12]. Fifth, the paper’s own limitation—one league, not among Europe’s best—means cross-league extrapolation is a multi-week trap: a motif that is shot-productive in Eliteserien may face different pressing, pitch, and player quality elsewhere [PAGE 12].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The paper does not measure player cognition, so any recursive-theory-of-mind reading is an interpretive overlay. Three candidate mechanisms can be stated and audited. First, “crisis habit regression”: under pressure, teams may regress to compact, familiar circulation—ABAB or ABCB—because those patterns require less spatial coordination. The model finds ABAB less shot-productive and ABCB also negative [PAGE 7–8], and compact motifs cover fewer zones: “ABCD covers more zones on average than the other motifs” [PAGE 8]. If crisis habit regression is real, then late-game or deficit states should raise ABAB/ABCB share and lower shot probability, but the motif model does not directly include game time or goal difference; those variables appear in the upstream pass GAMM table, not in the motif GAM table [PAGE 4–5]. That is a key gap, not a proven result.

Second, “counter-trap”: an opponent can allow compact circulation in front of a block and then press the next compact pass, converting the possession into a loss. The model’s positive zone smooth is consistent with this: “The likelihood of success increases with the number of unique zones” [PAGE 9], and the paper speculates that large movement under the 5-s rule may indicate counter-attacks [PAGE 9]. But the source has no direct pressing-intensity, opponent-shape, or trap variable, so the counter-trap story is a hypothesis. It predicts that compact motifs should be most penalized against high-pressing opponents; that interaction is not estimated.

Third, “clock”: the 5-s inter-pass limit and 15-s shot window impose a decision clock [PAGE 4, PAGE 6]. Compact motifs may be safe in the short run but burn the clock without advancing zones; spread motifs move the ball into more zones and are more shot-productive in the model [PAGE 9]. The recursive part is that passers must model both teammate availability and opponent recovery speed within 5 s; the aggregate model sees only the resulting motif label, not the internal model. The safe conclusion is conditional: in this sample, compact motifs are less associated with shots, and zone spread is positively associated with shots; crisis-habit, counter-trap, and clock mechanisms are plausible but unverified. A verifiable test would require game-state, pressing, and time-to-shot variables at the motif level, which the source does not provide.

A hard physical/biomechanical/weather veto cannot be proved from this source, but a partial veto is available. The motif GAM itself uses motif type, \(S_i\), centrality sums, and zones; it does not directly include grass type, month, header status, aerial duels, tackles, interceptions, ball recovery, or player load [PAGE 5]. Those physical and environmental variables enter the upstream pass-ability GAMMs: Table 1 includes “The match is played on artificial grass,” “Current pass is a header,” “Aerial duel in the previous event,” “Tackle in the previous event,” “Interception in the previous event,” “Ball recovery due to a loose ball in the previous event,” and “1-D smooth functions representing month of play, x9, interacting with type of grass” [PAGE 4]. Therefore, the motif model inherits some weather/pitch/biomechanical adjustment through \(\hat p_{i,m,r}\), but only as projected into pass-success probabilities. If artificial grass changes ball roll, if month changes pitch softness or weather, if headers and aerial duels alter pass execution, or if player fatigue changes sprint and recovery biomechanics, those effects are not directly identified in the motif equation. A cross-layer veto would trigger if the positive zone smooth or the ABAB penalty disappears after adding direct grass/month, header/aerial, load, or sprint-distance controls. The source’s own limitation—one league and no additional data sources—supports caution rather than a physical proof [PAGE 12]. Verdict: the tactical association is admissible as a conditional statistical result; a strong biomechanical or weather-causal claim is vetoed until direct cross-layer controls are estimated.
