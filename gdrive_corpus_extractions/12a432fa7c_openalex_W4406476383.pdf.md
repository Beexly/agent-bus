<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(i=1,\dots,I\) index plays, \(j=1,\dots,J_i\) index contact windows within play \(i\), \(t=1,\dots,T_{ij}\) index frames within window \(j\), and \(k=1,\dots,K_{ijt}\) index defenders within 1.5 yd of the ball-carrier at frame \(t\). Let \(\mathbf{x}_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) be the ball-carrier’s position in yards, and let \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_z\) be the unit vector toward the target end zone. The forward velocity operator is
\[
v_{ijt}=\dot{\mathbf{x}}_i(t)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}}_z,
\]
with units \(\mathrm{yd\,s^{-1}}\). The sampling interval is \(\Delta t=0.1\,\mathrm{s}\) because the data are recorded “at a rate of 10 Hz (i.e., 10 data points per second)” [PAGE 2]. A contact window is the maximal consecutive frame set satisfying
\[
\min_{k}\left\|\mathbf{x}_k(t)-\mathbf{x}_i(t)\right\|_2 \le D,
\]
with \(D=1.5\,\mathrm{yd}=1.3716\,\mathrm{m}\). The source selects this threshold because it “captures about 95% of the distances across both first contact and tackle events” [PAGE 3].

The window-value operator is given by the source as
\[
w_{ij}=\frac{v_{ij}^{\mathrm{start}}-v_{ij}^{\mathrm{end}}}{v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}},
\]
where \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}\) is the peak velocity before or during the window and \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}\) is the maximum velocity after the window [PAGE 4]. The ratio is dimensionless. It is not a probability and is not constrained to \([0,1]\). The source’s modification rules are:
\[
w_{ij}=0 \quad \text{if } v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}\ge v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}},
\]
because the runner “recovers all of their forward motion to essentially begin a new run” [PAGE 4]; and if \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{end}}\le v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}<v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}\), then \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{end}}\) is replaced by \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}\). If the peak occurs within the window, \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{start}}\) is replaced by \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}\) [PAGE 4].

The credit operators are:
\[
w_{ijt}=\frac{w_{ij}}{T_{ij}},
\]
which is the frame-level value after distributing “the window-level value wij equally across all frames within the window” [PAGE 5]; then
\[
w_{ijkt}=\frac{w_{ijt}}{K_{ijt}}=\frac{w_{ij}}{T_{ij}K_{ijt}},
\]
because the source says “We then divide the frame-level value wijt equally across all Kijt involved defenders” [PAGE 5]. The defender-window credit is
\[
w_{ijk}=\sum_{t=1}^{T_{ij}} w_{ijkt}.
\]
The printed source equation at [PAGE 5] shows \(w_{ijk}=\sum_{t=1}^{T_{ij}}w_{ijt}\), which omits the \(k\) subscript in the summand; the preceding frame-level equation makes the intended defender-level sum \(w_{ijkt}\) the consistent operator. This is a documentation gray zone, not a conceptual change.

Singularity and breakdown regimes are explicit. If \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}=0\), the denominator vanishes; the source does not state a convention. This occurs for backward or lateral runs, immediate penetration, or tracking error. If \(T_{ij}=0\), no window exists; if \(K_{ijt}=0\), the frame is not in a contact window by definition, but numerical jitter can create empty sets. If \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{start}}<v_{ij}^{\mathrm{end}}\), \(w_{ij}\) is negative; the source does not clamp it. If \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}\ge v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}\), credit is forced to zero even when real deceleration occurred. The 1.5 yd threshold is an empirical proxy, not a physical contact test. The 2D projection ignores pad height, arm span, force direction, and whether the defender is being blocked. Equal \(1/K_{ijt}\) splitting ignores force distribution. The metric is not normalized: the authors note that “the fractional tackle values across all windows in a play do not add up to one” [PAGE 9]. Finally, the operator depends on future frames through \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}\), so it is a retrospective within-play valuation, not a purely causal online operator.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when a defender is losing the race to the runner: the defender may revert to a low-level habit such as diving, reaching, or grabbing. That habit can put the defender inside the 1.5 yd contact window and can coincide with a velocity drop, so the metric may award credit even if the defender’s force is poorly directed or late. The source’s equal-split rule [PAGE 5] cannot distinguish a square, leveraged tackle from a desperate arm tackle. A recursive model of defender and runner intent would ask whether the defender’s action constrained the runner’s future cutback options, not merely whether the runner slowed while the defender was near.

Counter-trap behavior is the offense’s answer: a runner or blocker can invite contact, absorb it, and re-accelerate into space. The source has a partial counter-trap veto: if \(v_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}}\ge v_{ij}^{\mathrm{pre}}\), the window value is set to zero because the runner “recovers all of their forward motion to essentially begin a new run” [PAGE 4]. That rule correctly denies credit when the runner fully recovers, but it can also erase legitimate deceleration if recovery occurs after the window boundary. The recursive theory-of-mind issue is that the defender is modeling the runner’s likely path while the runner is modeling the defender’s leverage; the metric models neither. It models only the kinematic shadow of that interaction.

Clock has two meanings. The first is the sampling clock: 10 Hz gives 0.1 s frames [PAGE 2], and the average window is 1.28 s [PAGE 3], so equal frame division is a coarse temporal model. The second is the game and play clock: the source claims the metric is “model-free” and “easily implemented in the media during a live broadcast” [PAGE 9], but it contains no game-state or clock weighting. A defender may rationally trade yardage for time, or a runner may trade time for yardage; fractional tackles measures forward-motion suppression, not clock value. Crisis habit regression, counter-trap recognition, and clock management therefore form three separate layers that the current operator does not adjudicate.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto: let \(C_{\mathrm{true}}(t)=1\) denote actual contact force exchange and \(C_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)=1\) denote the observed distance condition \(\|\mathbf{x}_k(t)-\mathbf{x}_i(t)\|_2\le D\). The metric requires \(C_{\mathrm{obs}}\), but the authors admit that “we do not know if the players are actually making contact with one another based on the provided 2D tracking data” [PAGE 9]. Therefore \(C_{\mathrm{obs}}\) is necessary but not sufficient. If \(C_{\mathrm{true}}=0\) while \(C_{\mathrm{obs}}=1\), the operator can assign positive \(w_{ij}\) to a defender who never contacted the runner. That is a physical veto against reading fractional tackles as tackle counts.

Biomechanical veto: at frame \(t\), the true force contribution of defender \(k\) is \(\mathbf{F}_{k}(t)\), with impulse \(\mathbf{J}_{k}=\int \mathbf{F}_{k}\,dt\). The metric assigns \(w_{ijt}/K_{ijt}\) regardless of \(\|\mathbf{F}_{k}\|\), direction, or moment arm. A defender behind the runner, a defender being driven backward, and a defender square to the runner receive identical frame credit if all are within \(D\). The source acknowledges the simplifying assumption of “equal credit across all frames within a contact window” and equal credit to multiple defenders [PAGE 9]. This is a biomechanical veto against individual causal attribution: the operator measures shared proximity credit, not force decomposition.

Weather and surface veto: weather data are not in the provided source, but the physical requirement is general. On a low-traction surface, environmental deceleration can be modeled as \(a_{\mathrm{env}}=-\mu g + a_{\mathrm{drag}}\), where \(\mu\) is an effective friction coefficient and \(g\) is gravitational acceleration. A runner can lose forward velocity because of footing, cut mechanics, or surface conditions while a defender is within \(D\). The metric would assign that loss to the nearest defender. The 2D tracking system does not observe \(\mu\), cleat-surface interaction, or body pose. Thus weather and surface conditions are cross-layer veto variables: they can create positive \(w_{ij}\) without defensive force and can suppress \(w_{ij}\) despite defensive force. The correct adjudication is to require pose, force, or accelerometer evidence before treating a contact window as a tackle event.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
