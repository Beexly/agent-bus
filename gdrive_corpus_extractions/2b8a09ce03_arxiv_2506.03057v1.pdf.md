The central inferential object is a drive outcome: a continuous possession by one team that ends in one of five ordered scoring categories. The authors model the probability of each ordered outcome as a function of offense strength, defense strength, home-field status, game context, and prior-drive complementary statistics. My reasoning proceeds in several layers.

First, the response variable must be treated as ordinal categorical, not continuous. The paper explicitly says a single drive can result in one of five ordinal scoring outcomes and that the highest positive value is 7 for an offensive touchdown [PAGE 7]. Because the numeric point values are assigned only after modeling category probabilities, the correct generative layer is multinomial categorical with ordered categories, not linear regression. The authors also say they standardize touchdowns to 7 points for modeling purposes [PAGE 7]. Defensive touchdowns are recorded as negative values for the offense [PAGE 7]. Therefore, the post-hoc expected-points mapping should use values approximately \(-7,-2,0,3,7\) points per drive, with the exact sign convention inherited from the source narrative.

Second, the drive-level notation creates a longitudinal, unit-complementary structure. Let \(Y_{ijkl}\) be the outcome when team \(i\) offense faces team \(j\) defense in drive \(k\) of game \(l\). The model includes team \(i\) offensive strength \(\alpha_i\), team \(j\) defensive strength \(\beta_j\), home indicator \(h_{ijl}\), game-context vector \(g_{ijkl}\), and prior-drive complementary statistics \(x^*_{ji(k-1)l}\) [PAGE 8]. The complementary statistics are from the immediately preceding drive by the opposing unit, centered by subtracting league means [PAGE 9]. This is the key “complementary football” mechanism: a defense’s prior-drive turnover or field-position outcome may alter the offense’s current scoring probability.

Third, the baseline cumulative-link model is a multinomial likelihood with category-specific cumulative probability equations. The source states the baseline model and then adds a regularized optimization criterion [PAGE 8], [PAGE 12]. The regularization is partial: team offense and defense coefficients are unpenalized so that every team remains in the model, while complementary features are penalized and allowed to have category-specific deviations. This design is driven by parameter-count concerns: relaxing category-common effects for all variables would raise parameter counts from about 80 to 270 in the NFL and from roughly 275 to over 1,100 in college football [PAGE 11]. The authors therefore use a semi-parallel elastic-net penalty and enforce unpenalized team effects [PAGE 12].

Fourth, variable selection is evaluated by stability across cross-validation replicates. The selection metric is out-of-sample multinomial log-likelihood, with a “sparse CV” rule choosing the largest penalty value within one standard deviation of best performance [PAGE 14]. In the NFL, the non-scoring turnover indicator was selected most consistently, at least 80% of the time, and when selected it had a positive effect on scoring [PAGE 19]. When the authors add the interaction term combining non-scoring turnover with post-turnover starting position, it is selected in 100% of cross-validation replicates across NFL and college seasons [PAGE 21]. This is important because it shows that the mechanism is not merely “turnovers help” but “turnovers help when they produce usable field position.”

Fifth, residual diagnostics are not ordinary continuous residual plots because the response is categorical. The authors use surrogate residuals from Liu and Zhang and then binarize them by cumulative scoring category, treating each cumulative comparison as a separate binary fit for diagnostic purposes [PAGE 14], [PAGE 15]. They report good fit based on residuals-versus-fitted centering and quantile alignment [PAGE 21], [PAGE 22]. This matters because a categorical model can have good predictive likelihood but still hide category-specific misfit, especially for rare categories such as safety or defensive touchdown.

Sixth, projection is the policy-relevant step. To project a team’s scoring onto a league-average opponent, the authors set team-level coefficients to the average category, replace complementary features with league averages, and set home-field exposure to league-average home proportion, while leaving game-context variables unchanged [PAGE 15]. Expected points are then computed as a weighted average of point denominations [PAGE 15]. Bootstrap resampling with 1,000 resamples gives uncertainty bands [PAGE 16].

Gray-zone audit: there are several delicate points. The source text contains OCR or typographic artifacts in the point-value table, but the narrative resolves them by stating the highest positive touchdown value is 7 and defensive scores are negative [PAGE 7]. The penalty notation reuses Greek letters in ways that can collide with team coefficient notation, so a clean derivation must distinguish team coefficients from penalty-mixing weights. The model assumes independent drives after diagnostics, but drives are nested within games; the authors test a game-level random intercept and find no statistical evidence of within-game dependence [PAGE 11]. If that assumption fails in another dataset, standard errors and cross-validation should be clustered by game. Another gray zone is weather: the source’s context variables include half, time remaining, score differential, and interactions [PAGE 9], but not weather. Therefore any physical veto analysis must treat weather as an omitted external layer that can alter kicking and turnover probabilities. Finally, season-long projections and single-game projections behave differently: complementary football adjustments are smaller than strength-of-schedule adjustments over seasons but become more important for single-game evaluation [PAGE 29].
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The drive outcome is a categorical ordered variable. Let

\[
Y_{ijkl}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
\]

denote the scoring outcome for team \(i\) offense against team \(j\) defense on drive \(k\) of game \(l\). The categories are:

1. defensive touchdown for the opponent, negative value for offense;
2. safety, negative value for offense;
3. no score;
4. field goal;
5. offensive touchdown.

The source says, “The highest positive value is 7” [PAGE 7], and “we standardize touchdowns to 7 points for modeling purposes” [PAGE 7]. Therefore the post-model scoring map can be written as

\[
v = (v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4,v_5)=(-7,-2,0,3,7)
\]

with units of points per drive.

The category probabilities are

\[
\pi_{s,ijkl}=P(Y_{ijkl}=s), \qquad s=1,\dots,5,
\]

and the baseline model uses a cumulative probability construction [PAGE 8]. For \(s=2,3,4,5\), define the cumulative log-probability-ratio

\[
\eta_{s,ijkl}
=
\log\left(
\frac{P(Y_{ijkl}\ge s)}{P(Y_{ijkl})<s}
\right).
\]

The source baseline model is

\[
\eta_{s,ijkl}
=
\mu_s
+
\alpha_i
+
\beta_j
+
\delta h_{ijl}
+
\phi^\top g_{ijkl}
+
\xi I_{\text{cmp},ijkl}
+
\gamma^\top x^*_{ji(k-1)l} I_{\text{cmp},ijkl}.
\]

This is Equation (1) in the source [PAGE 8]. The terms have the following interpretations and units:

- \(\mu_s\): category-specific intercept for a league-average opponent and league-average complementary-unit conditions; dimensionless on the cumulative log-probability-ratio scale.
- \(\alpha_i\): offensive strength of team \(i\); dimensionless cumulative log-probability-ratio shift per drive.
- \(\beta_j\): defensive strength of team \(j\); dimensionless cumulative log-probability-ratio shift per drive.
- \(h_{ijl}\in\{0,1\}\): home-field indicator [PAGE 8].
- \(\delta\): home-field effect; dimensionless cumulative log-probability-ratio shift per drive.
- \(g_{ijkl}\): game-context variables at the start of the drive, including half indicator, time remaining in half, score differential, and pairwise interactions [PAGE 9].
- \(\phi\): context coefficient vector; units are inverse context units, e.g. per minute if time is measured in minutes, per point if score differential is measured in points.
- \(I_{\text{cmp},ijkl}\): indicator that the current drive has a valid preceding complementary drive [PAGE 10].
- \(x^*_{ji(k-1)l}\): centered complementary statistics from the previous drive by team \(j\) against team \(i\) [PAGE 9].
- \(\gamma\): complementary-feature coefficient vector; units are inverse complementary-stat units, e.g. per yard, per turnover indicator, per count.
- \(\xi\): effect of the complementary-availability indicator.

The centering operation is

\[
x^*_{c,ji(k-1)l}
=
x_{c,ji(k-1)l}-\bar{x}_c,
\]

which makes the intercept and team effects interpretable as projections onto league-average complementary conditions [PAGE 9]. The source says, “By centering the complementary statistics through mean subtraction” [PAGE 9].

Team-strength anchoring constraints are

\[
\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i = 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j = 0.
\]

The source says, “We enforce constraints” [PAGE 9]. These constraints make \(\mu_s\) refer to a league-average opponent rather than an arbitrary reference team.

The expected points for a drive are obtained after fitting the model by converting category probabilities into weighted point values:

\[
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\text{points}_{ijkl}]
=
\sum_{s=1}^5 v_s \widehat{\pi}_{s,ijkl}.
\]

The source describes this as a “weighted average of point denominations” [PAGE 15]. Units are points per drive. Because the ordered point values are bounded below by \(-7\) and above by \(7\) under the standardized touchdown convention, the expected-points projection must satisfy

\[
-7 \le \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\text{points}_{ijkl}] \le 7
\]

for each drive-level projection. Season or game averages are averages of such drive-level expected points and therefore inherit the same bounds.

The regularization criterion is a partial elastic-net penalty applied mainly to complementary features. A clean formal version of the source’s optimization criterion [PAGE 12] is

\[
\min_{\mu,\delta,\phi,\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\{\gamma_s\}}
\left\{
-\frac{1}{N^*}\ell(\mu,\alpha,\beta,\delta,\phi,\xi,\gamma,\gamma_2,\dots,\gamma_5;y,h,g,x)
+
\lambda
\left[
\rho \|\gamma\|_1
+
\frac{1-\rho}{2}\|\gamma\|_2^2
+
\sum_{s=2}^5
\left(
\rho \|\gamma_s\|_1
+
\frac{1-\rho}{2}\|\gamma_s\|_2^2
\right)
\right]
\right\}.
\]

Here \(\ell\) is the multinomial log-likelihood. If natural logarithms are used, \(-\ell/N^*\) has units of nats per drive. The penalty-mixing parameter \(\rho\in[0,1]\) controls the elastic-net balance; \(\rho=1\) is pure LASSO-type selection, and \(\rho=0\) is ridge-type shrinkage. The source uses a high LASSO-oriented value, near 0.99, to preserve selection while avoiding nonunique optima [PAGE 13]. The team coefficients \(\alpha_i\), \(\beta_j\), and the non-complementary coefficients \(\delta,\phi,\xi\) are left unpenalized so that all teams remain included and strength-of-schedule adjustment is preserved [PAGE 12], [PAGE 13].

Breakdown and singularity regimes:

1. **Pure LASSO limit.** The source states the optimization task has a unique optimum for all cases except \(\alpha=1\) in its notation, corresponding here to \(\rho=1\) [PAGE 13]. At pure LASSO, nonunique solutions can occur because the overparameterized cumulative-category formulation can become non-identifiable.

2. **No-penalty limit.** If \(\lambda\to 0\), the complement of the likelihood is unregularized. In the overparameterized category-specific formulation, parameters can be weakly identified or nonidentified. The penalty is needed to make the optimization well behaved [PAGE 13].

3. **Heavy-penalty limit.** If \(\lambda\) becomes very large, all penalized complementary coefficients shrink to zero. The model reduces to game context plus strength of schedule plus home field. This is not a failure but a nested simplification.

4. **Full category-specific relaxation.** If all coefficients, including team effects, are allowed to vary by category, parameter count explodes: approximately 80 to 270 for NFL and roughly 275 to over 1,100 for college [PAGE 11]. The source warns that this would be computationally prohibitive and could suffer from “absence of certain scoring outcomes” [PAGE 31].

5. **Rare-category separation.** Safety and defensive touchdown outcomes occur at less than 0.5% and about 1% of drives, respectively [PAGE 7]. If a team never experiences a rare category, category-specific team coefficients can diverge or become unstable. This is a major reason to keep team effects unpenalized and category-common.

6. **Drive independence breakdown.** The baseline assumes multinomial observations with independent drives. The authors test within-game dependence using a mixed-effects cumulative link model with a random game intercept and find no statistical evidence of dependence [PAGE 11]. If that test failed, the likelihood would need game-level clustering or random effects.

7. **Complementary-link missingness.** For drives with no valid preceding drive, or drives following the same unit after a defensive score, complementary effects are nullified by \(I_{\text{cmp}}=0\) [PAGE 9], [PAGE 10]. If this indicator is misspecified, the complementary coefficient can be biased.

The cross-validation metric for selection is out-of-sample multinomial log-likelihood, with the largest penalty value within one standard deviation of best performance chosen [PAGE 14]. For predictive comparison, the authors convert predicted category probabilities to expected points and use mean absolute error:

\[
\text{MAE}
=
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M |\hat{y}_m-y_m|,
\]

where \(\hat{y}_m\) is predicted expected points, \(y_m\) is observed point value, and \(M\) is the number of validation drives [PAGE 17]. The reported cross-validation standard error is

\[
\text{SE}
=
\frac{\operatorname{sd}(\text{CV.MAE})}{\sqrt{10}},
\]

because there are 10 folds [PAGE 17].

The final selected complementary terms are the non-scoring turnover indicator and the interaction combining non-scoring turnover with post-turnover starting position. The source says the interaction term was selected in 100% of all cross-validation replicates across NFL and college seasons [PAGE 21]. Formally, the selected complementary component can be represented as

\[
\gamma_1 \, \text{turnover.nonscor}_{ji(k-1)l}
+
\gamma_2 \, \text{start.pos}_{ji(k-1)l}\times \text{turnover.nonscor}_{ji(k-1)l},
\]

with additional category-specific coefficients for the “at least a field goal” cumulative comparison [PAGE 21]. This means the scoring benefit of a turnover depends on the field position that the turnover gives the offense.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper sits in a lineage of sports-rating models that try to separate production from context. The earliest cited ancestor is Harville’s offense-defense model, which assumes a team’s scoring depends on its offense and the opponent’s defense [PAGE 3]. Govan et al. later applied this framework to college basketball and professional football [PAGE 3]. The paper also cites home-field advantage research by Edwards and Archambault and by Vergin and Sosik [PAGE 3]. This lineage explains why the authors include both team offensive and defensive coefficients plus a home indicator.

A second lineage is modern football analytics. The source cites Yurko, Ventura, and Horowitz for expected-points modeling based on game context such as down, distance, time remaining, and score state [PAGE 4], [PAGE 9]. The present paper borrows the intuition that game context matters, but it works at the drive level rather than the play level. It uses context variables at the start of the drive, including half indicator, time remaining, score differential, and pairwise interactions [PAGE 9]. This prevents the analysis from collapsing all scoring chances into a naïve average.

A third lineage is regularized ordinal regression. The optimization framework comes from Wurm, Rathouz, and Hanlon’s ordinalNet approach, which relaxes category-common coefficient restrictions while controlling estimation complexity [PAGE 3], [PAGE 12]. The authors extend that framework by allowing only selected predictors to have category-specific deviations, while keeping team strength coefficients unpenalized and category-common [PAGE 13]. This extension is crucial because the number of team parameters is large. If all team effects were category-specific, the model would become estimationally brittle, especially for rare categories such as safety [PAGE 31].

A fourth lineage is residual diagnostics for categorical models. The paper uses the surrogate residual approach of Liu and Zhang, implemented in the sure package, then modifies it for non-proportional category structures by binarizing the cumulative comparisons [PAGE 4], [PAGE 14], [PAGE 15]. This is important because classical residual intuition does not directly apply to categorical outcomes. The binarized diagnostics treat each cumulative category boundary as a separate binary model and check whether residuals behave like continuous-model residuals [PAGE 15], [PAGE 21].

A fifth lineage is complementary-unit analysis in American football. The authors cite Skripnikov’s earlier work, which used cumulative game totals rather than sequential drives [PAGE 4]. They argue that game totals are vulnerable to reverse causality and cannot capture the direct impact of one unit’s prior-drive performance on the next drive [PAGE 4]. The present paper improves on that by using drive-by-drive sequencing. This is the core retro-causal correction: instead of asking whether good defenses correlate with good offenses over a whole game, it asks whether a defensive event on drive \(k-1\) changes the scoring probability on drive \(k\).

Multi-week analytical traps emerge from this genealogy.

First, **game-total reverse causality**. If a team’s offense and defense are evaluated from aggregate game statistics, a strong defense may appear to inflate offensive output, but the causal path may be bidirectional. The drive-level model reduces this trap by using the previous drive as the explanatory event.

Second, **schedule-strength omission**. If team effects are estimated without adjusting for opponent strength, a team that played weak opponents can look better than it is. The paper addresses this by including unpenalized team offense and defense coefficients and enforcing zero-sum constraints [PAGE 9]. Strength-of-schedule adjustments are often larger than complementary-football adjustments, especially over a season [PAGE 28], [PAGE 29].

Third, **home-field omission**. Home-field advantage is historically documented and is included through \(h_{ijl}\) and \(\delta\) [PAGE 3], [PAGE 8]. Omitting it can bias team strength estimates.

Fourth, **data-cleaning selection bias**. The college dataset required extensive cleaning. Overtime plays were removed in college because college overtime dynamics reduce complementary aspects [PAGE 5]. Faulty halves were dropped entirely to preserve drive continuity [PAGE 5]. After filtering, 65% of initial halves were retained, yielding over 15,000 drives per season except the shortened 2020 season [PAGE 5]. The proportion of problematic data was consistent across seasons, 32%–35%, with no conference contributing disproportionately [PAGE 6]. This reduces, but does not eliminate, concern that data errors correlate with team quality.

Fifth, **year-to-year roster turnover**. The authors avoid leave-one-season-out evaluation because teams change rosters, coaches, and personnel across years [PAGE 17]. This is a multi-week and multi-season trap: a model trained on prior seasons may misestimate current-season team strength because the underlying units are not stable.

Sixth, **overfitting through category-specific team effects**. Allowing every coefficient to vary by scoring category would multiply parameters and create sparse-cell failures [PAGE 11], [PAGE 31]. The partial regularization strategy is therefore both computational and causal: it preserves team ranking while focusing selection on complementary features.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model can be interpreted as a compact theory of strategic football behavior, but it must remain grounded in observed drive outcomes rather than invented psychological ratios. The relevant agents are offenses and defenses making repeated choices under clock, score, field position, and complementary-unit history.

Crisis habit regression refers to the tendency of teams to change behavior under pressure: late in a half, trailing teams may pass more, leading teams may run or shorten the game, and defenses may become more aggressive if they need a stop. The model captures some of this through game-context variables: half indicator, time remaining in the half, score differential, and pairwise interactions [PAGE 9]. These variables allow scoring probabilities to vary by game state. However, the model does not explicitly encode a coach’s decision rule. It infers effects from outcomes.

The complementary-unit mechanism is central. A defense does not only prevent scoring; it can create field position for the offense. The source finds that non-scoring turnovers were selected most consistently and had a positive effect on scoring [PAGE 19]. But the more refined selected term is the interaction of non-scoring turnover with post-turnover starting position [PAGE 21]. This distinction is the main counter-trap. A turnover is not valuable merely because it is a turnover. It is valuable if it gives the offense a short or manageable field.

The starting-field-position mechanism is illustrated by the median post-turnover starting position of 41 yards, which is 21 yards better than without a turnover, with the entire interquartile range higher [PAGE 19]. In NFL terms, the offense then needs only about 30 additional yards to reach a comfortable field-goal attempt, while reaching the end zone requires approximately 60 yards [PAGE 20]. This creates an asymmetry: a good turnover helps scoring probability, especially the probability of at least a field goal, more than it guarantees a touchdown. The source explicitly finds a strong effect on the probability of at least a field goal but not necessarily on touchdown probability [PAGE 34].

The clock layer enters in three ways. First, time remaining in the half affects scoring chances because fewer possessions remain and teams may behave differently late in a half [PAGE 9]. Second, drive sequence alternates units, except in special cases. The source notes that approximately 5-to-10% of drives are either not preceded by another drive or follow a drive by the same offense, such as after a defensive touchdown [PAGE 9]. For those drives, the complementary effect is nullified by \(I_{\text{cmp}}=0\) [PAGE 10]. Third, the projection step sets home-field exposure to a league-average balance but leaves game-context values unchanged because projecting onto an arbitrary hypothetical game state would be artificial [PAGE 15].

Recursive theory of mind, in this context, means modeling how one unit’s prior behavior changes the other unit’s opportunities, not attributing unobservable intent. A defense that forces a non-scoring turnover may do so at a bad field position, in which case the offense gains little. A defense that forces a turnover near midfield or in opponent territory gives the offense a major advantage. The model’s selected interaction term captures this by allowing the turnover indicator to matter conditional on starting position.

Counter-trap logic requires avoiding two errors. The first error is over-crediting turnover generation without field position. The source shows this through team examples: defenses with poor post-turnover starting position can be poor complementary units even if they generate some turnovers [PAGE 24]. The second error is assuming that elite offenses automatically benefit from elite defenses. The projection mechanism instead asks what the offense would score if its complementary unit were league-average. Strong complementary defenses cause negative shifts in projected offensive points; weak complementary defenses cause positive shifts [PAGE 32].

The resulting behavioral interpretation is conservative: complementary football effects are real but bounded. They are smaller than strength-of-schedule effects over a season but more important in single-game evaluation [PAGE 29]. They affect scoring probabilities most strongly through field position and the path to at least a field goal. They do not imply that a defense directly “gives” a touchdown, because red-zone execution remains offense-dependent. The source notes that the compressed field near the end zone makes touchdowns less guaranteed, while field goals are more reliable from favorable field position [PAGE 20], [PAGE 34].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is needed because the statistical model operates on drive-level probabilities, while football outcomes are physically constrained by field geometry, kicking range, time, and environmental conditions. The veto adjudication asks whether a proposed inference violates physical or biomechanical limits.

First, the scoring outcome layer is bounded by point values. Under the standardized drive outcome map, expected points per drive must lie between \(-7\) and \(7\). Therefore any claimed complementary effect that implies a season-long expected-points shift beyond those bounds for a single drive is invalid. The observed seasonal shifts in the source are much smaller, e.g. positive shifts of about 0.06 to 0.13 points per drive and negative shifts of about 0.13 to 0.28 points per drive [PAGE 25], [PAGE 27]. These are physically plausible because they are weighted probability shifts, not raw point additions.

Second, field-position geometry imposes thresholds. The source states that NFL kickers convert field goals from about 70–75 yards into opponent territory, equivalent to 25–30 yards from the end zone, while college kickers are often closer to 75–80 yards into opponent territory [PAGE 20]. It also states that reaching the opponent’s end zone for a touchdown requires approximately 60 yards from the median post-turnover starting position of 41 yards [PAGE 20]. Therefore, if a turnover leaves the offense far from scoring range, the model should not predict a large increase in scoring probability. If a counterfactual claims that any non-scoring turnover produces a large scoring gain regardless of field position, it violates the physical geometry of the field and must be vetoed.

Third, the end-zone compression constraint matters. The source says the end zone is only 10 yards deep and offensive players cannot step out of bounds, which limits deep passing and maneuvering [PAGE 20]. This biomechanical and geometric constraint explains why field position strongly increases the probability of at least a field goal but does not guarantee a touchdown. A veto is triggered if a model-based narrative claims that favorable field position automatically converts into touchdowns at a high rate. The data show that touchdown probability depends on red-zone execution and remains uncertain [PAGE 20], [PAGE 34].

Fourth, weather is an omitted physical layer. The source’s context variables include half, time remaining, score differential, and interactions [PAGE 9], but not weather. If wind, rain, snow, or temperature materially changes kicking probability, ball security, or field conditions, then the estimated complementary effects can be confounded by weather. Formally, suppose the true cumulative log-probability-ratio contains a weather term \(\theta W_{ijkl}\). If \(W\) is omitted and correlated with complementary features \(x^*\), then the estimated complementary coefficient absorbs part of the weather effect. Under a linear approximation,

\[
\widehat{\gamma}
\approx
\gamma
+
\theta
\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x^*,W)}{\operatorname{Var}(x^*)}.
\]

This is not a toy ratio; it is the omitted-variable bias expression. If weather is severe and correlated with turnover-prone conditions or poor field position, the model’s inference about complementary football should be vetoed or downweighted until weather is included. The source does not provide weather data, so any claim about weather-driven scoring must remain outside the fitted model.

Fifth, drive independence is a cross-layer assumption. The model’s likelihood treats drives as multinomial observations after conditioning on covariates. The authors tested within-game dependence with a random game intercept and found no statistical evidence of dependence [PAGE 11]. If a new dataset has strong within-game momentum, coaching adjustments, or injury effects, the independence assumption may fail. In that case, standard errors and cross-validation must be clustered by game, and drive-level effects may be biased downward or upward depending on the dependence structure.

Sixth, data integrity is a physical-record veto layer. The college data required dropping faulty halves because play-by-play inconsistencies could break drive continuity [PAGE 5]. If a play-by-play record has missing yardage, inconsistent field position, or incorrect turnover designation, the complementary feature \(x^*\) is invalid. Any inference based on corrupted drive sequences should be vetoed until cleaning is repeated.

The final adjudication rule is therefore:

\[
\text{Accept complementary effect } A
\quad
\text{iff}
\]

1. the implied expected-points shift respects \([-7,7]\) per drive;
2. the implied field-position thresholds are physically plausible;
3. weather or environmental confounders are either included or shown negligible;
4. drive independence is supported or corrected by game-level clustering;
5. data cleaning preserves drive sequence.

If any condition fails, the statistical estimate is not admissible as a causal or performance-adjustment claim, even if the fitted likelihood improves.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable RLVR-style veto scenarios. Each scenario is a falsifiable prediction that can be tested against drive-level data or model-refitting experiments.

**Scenario 1: Turnover without favorable field position.**
A defense forces a non-scoring turnover, but the turnover occurs deep in the offense’s own territory. The naive claim is that the turnover increases scoring probability. The veto condition is that the model’s selected term is not merely the turnover indicator but the interaction between turnover and post-turnover starting position [PAGE 21]. Prediction: drives with turnover indicator equal to 1 but low starting position should show only a small increase in probability of at least a field goal, while drives with high starting position should show a large increase. Verifiable test: bin post-turnover starting position into low, medium, and high ranges and compare predicted probabilities of at least a field goal. The source’s probability plot shows a steep increase in scoring probability as post-turnover starting position advances [PAGE 23], [PAGE 24]. If a counterfactual model gives the same boost to a turnover at the offense’s own 10 as to a turnover at the opponent’s 40, it should be vetoed.

**Scenario 2: Complementary features improve prediction after strength-of-schedule adjustment, but not more than strength-of-schedule adjustment itself.**
The final model includes game context, strength of schedule, and complementary features. The source’s 10-fold cross-validation compares three feature sets: game context only, game context plus strength of schedule, and game context plus strength of schedule plus complementary football [PAGE 30]. Prediction: the full model has lower mean absolute error than the strength-of-schedule-only model, but the gain from complementary features is smaller than the gain from adding strength of schedule, especially over season-long evaluation [PAGE 28], [PAGE 29]. Verifiable test: compute 10-fold CV MAE for the three nested models within each season. The source shows that the full model outperforms the reduced models and that strength-of-schedule gains are especially large in college football [PAGE 30], [PAGE 31]. If a counterfactual claims complementary football alone dominates strength-of-schedule adjustment in out-of-sample prediction across all seasons, it should be vetoed.

**Scenario 3: Full category-specific team effects cause sparse-category breakdown.**
A modeler attempts to relax category-common restrictions for all team offense and defense coefficients, allowing each team to have separate effects for safety, field goal, touchdown, and no-score boundaries. The source warns that this would raise parameter counts from about 80 to 270 in the NFL and from roughly 275 to over 1,100 in college [PAGE 11]. It also notes that full relaxation could suffer from absence of rare scoring outcomes, such as safety [PAGE 31]. Prediction: full category-specific team-effect models will show convergence failures, unstable coefficients, or worse cross-validation performance for rare categories, especially in college football where some teams have very few retained games after data cleaning [PAGE 6]. Verifiable test: fit the full category-specific model and compare convergence diagnostics, separation indicators, and CV log-likelihood against the partially regularized model. If the full model produces divergent coefficients for teams with no safety events or no defensive-touchdown events, the veto is confirmed. The source’s design choice to keep team effects unpenalized and category-common is therefore validated by this counterfactual [PAGE 12], [PAGE 31].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
