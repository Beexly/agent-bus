The boundary between risk and uncertainty is thus not metaphysical but cognitive and institutional.

\[

where \(H\) denotes Shannon entropy. High \(U(\omega)\) implies that \(\omega\) carries little redundant information with other events in its class—i.e., it is nearly unique.

**Insurance pricing operator**: Under perfect competition and known probabilities, the premium \(P\) for coverage over class \(C\) is:
\[
P(C) = E[X \mid C] + \alpha \cdot \text{AdminCost},
\]
where \(\alpha \geq 1\) accounts for administrative overhead. Knight’s claim that “premiums will under either system be equal to the administrative costs” corresponds to \(\alpha = 1\) and zero economic profit.

**Breakdown regimes**: The law of large numbers ensures convergence of sample means to \(E[X \mid C]\) only if the underlying distribution is stationary and i.i.d. within \(C\). If the classification \(\mathcal{C}\) is misspecified (i.e., \(C\) mixes heterogeneous sub-populations), then \(\text{Var}(X \mid C)\) inflates, violating the central limit theorem assumptions. Formally, let \(C = C_1 \cup C_2\) with \(E[X \mid C_1] \neq E[X \mid C_2]\); then the effective variance becomes:
\[
\text{Var}_{\text{true}}(X \mid C) = E[\text{Var}(X \mid C_k)] + \text{Var}(E[X \mid C_k]),
\]
where the second term (between-group variance) represents Knightian uncertainty. When this term dominates, insurance markets fail because insurers cannot set premiums that are both fair and sustainable.

This occurs when the process generating \(\omega\) is non-ergodic or structurally changing.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Knight’s distinction between risk and uncertainty has deep roots in early 20th-century epistemology. Retrospectively, it can be seen as a response to the crisis of positivism: if all knowledge must be reducible to empirical regularities, then economic phenomena that lack such regularities become irrational or outside science. Knight drew on John Maynard Keynes’s *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), which distinguished between “objective” (statistical) and “subjective” (logical) probability. However, Knight went further by tying this to organizational form and profit theory.

The genealogical trap lies in assuming that Knight’s categories are timeless. In fact, his notion of “classifiability” reflects the actuarial practices of the 1920s, particularly life insurance’s reliance on mortality tables. Today, big data and machine learning have blurred the line: algorithms can identify micro-segments that were previously considered “unique.” Thus, what was once “uncertainty” may now be reclassified as “risk,” expanding the domain of insurability. This retrocausal shift reveals that Knight’s framework is historically contingent, not universal.

Moreover, the multi-week trap in applying Knight today is to conflate statistical confidence with fundamental measurability. Modern risk models (e.g., VaR) often present complex uncertainties as if they were risks, creating false precision. The 2008 financial crisis exemplified this: mortgage-backed securities were priced using historical default correlations, ignoring tail dependencies that had never been observed—a classic case of treating uncertainty as risk.

Thus, while Knight’s logic is sound within its context, its application requires constant vigilance against technological and methodological changes that redefine the boundary between the two domains.
