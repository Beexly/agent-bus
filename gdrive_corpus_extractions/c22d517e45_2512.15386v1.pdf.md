The source paper defines a video-understanding pipeline whose central operator is not merely recognition of a completed rebound, but anticipation of a future possession outcome before the rebound is fully resolved. The first hop is to separate two temporal regimes. In the offline regime, the input clip is explicitly trimmed so that the final observed frame satisfies \(t_W = t_A - \tau_a\), where \(t_A\) is the ground-truth action frame and \(\tau_a\) is the anticipation time. This creates a controlled but artificial benchmark: the model always sees a pre-event context and must infer whether the attacking team will recover the ball, labeled OREB, or the defending team will recover it, labeled DREB. The paper states that in offline anticipation “the temporal context W is trimmed, meaning that it always ends exactly τa frames (or seconds) before the rebound action occurs” [PAGE 7]. In the online regime, by contrast, the model receives arbitrary fixed-length clips and must decide whether an event occurs inside a future anticipation window, introducing a background class and severe imbalance: “a third class (no rebound) must be introduced” [PAGE 7].

The second hop is to recognize that the task is dominated by latent physical trajectories rather than semantic labels alone. A rebound is defined as “the action in which a player gains control of the ball following a missed shot” [PAGE 2]. The annotated timestamp is “the first frame in which the ball makes contact with the hand of the player who eventually gains possession” [PAGE 8]. Therefore the target event is a conjunction: missed shot, ball trajectory, rim or backboard interaction, player reachability, and hand contact. If any necessary physical condition is absent in the observed context, an anticipatory model should be able to veto or at least strongly discount the event. This is why interpretability matters: the paper finds that “the model does not focus strongly on the ball” [PAGE 23], while human experts rely on “the trajectory of the ball after it bounces off the rim” [PAGE 25]. That mismatch creates a gray zone: the model may predict from player positioning and end-of-context motion cues, while humans may predict from ball physics when the ball is visible.

The third hop concerns evaluation and class imbalance. Offline anticipation is binary and uses accuracy. Online anticipation is three-class and uses macro-averaged F1, because “all classes — including the dominant background class — are weighted equally” [PAGE 7]. The macro-F1 metric prevents the model from receiving credit merely by predicting background. However, the online loss uses class weights “[0.49, 0.49, 0.02] for the OREB, DREB and no rebound classes respectively” [PAGE 15], which introduces a prior: action classes are heavily upweighted relative to background. This is not a toy heuristic; it is a formal compensation for sampling frequency, but it also creates a brittle operating point if background clips are extremely abundant or if the anticipation window is short.

The fourth hop is temporal. The paper shows that decreasing \(\tau_a\) makes the event closer and often exposes the ball after rim contact. At \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s, “the ball has typically already bounced off the rim or backboard” [PAGE 22]. At \(\tau_a = 1.5\) s, the ball may still be in the air or in the shooter’s hands, so the task depends more on latent player geometry and momentum. This produces a temporal crossover: short-horizon anticipation can exploit observable ball physics, whereas long-horizon anticipation must infer unobservable future dynamics from pre-contact posture. The paper’s human study confirms this crossover: humans outperform the model at \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s, but at \(\tau_a = 1.5\) s the model matches overall accuracy and exceeds human F1 because humans become biased toward the more frequent class [PAGE 24].

The fifth hop is the auditing of pseudo-labels. The dataset uses a weak spotting model to generate pseudo-labels, but the paper explicitly warns that timestamp errors alter the effective anticipation time. If a rebound is detected earlier than the true rebound, “the effective anticipation window becomes 1.5s” when nominal \(\tau_a = 1.0\) s [PAGE 24]. This is a major gray zone: increasing training data does not necessarily improve learning if the temporal labels are noisy. The paper’s result that adding pseudo-labeled samples does not consistently help [PAGE 23] is therefore not merely empirical; it follows from temporal label noise propagating into the definition of the input context. The final audit must therefore distinguish between model capacity errors, metric artifacts, dataset leakage risks, and physical impossibility.

Let a broadcast video be represented as an ordered frame sequence  
\[
V = \{t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_{T_V}\},
\]  
where \(t_i\) indexes frames at original frame rate \(f_{\text{orig}}\) in \(\text{frames}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\). The paper states that videos are “recorded at a resolution of 1280×720 pixels and a frame rate of 60 frames per second” [PAGE 8]. For temporal downsampling, the effective frame rate is  
\[
f_{\text{down}} = \frac{f_{\text{orig}}}{\text{stride}},
\]  
with stride dimensionless and \(f_{\text{down}}\) in \(\text{frames}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\) [PAGE 9]. In the implemented TEAM setting, a 2-second clip at 60 fps with stride 3 yields \(T = 40\) frames, as stated: “T = 40 (corresponding to a clip of 2 seconds at 60 fps with a stride of 3)” [PAGE 17].

Define the rebound event time as a random variable \(T_A\), measured in seconds or frame index, and the class variable  
\[
Y_A \in \{\text{OREB}, \text{DREB}\}.
\]  
For offline anticipation, the observed context is  
\[
W = \{t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_W\}, \qquad t_W = t_A - \tau_a,
\]  
with \(\tau_a\) in seconds and the constraint \(t_W < t_A < t_{T_V}\). The paper specifies: “the temporal context W is trimmed … such that \(t_W = t_A - \tau_a\)” [PAGE 7]. The offline predictor is a conditional distribution  
\[
p_\phi(Y_A \mid W),
\]  
where \(\phi\) denotes trainable parameters. The output probability is dimensionless, and the decision threshold for the baseline binary model is \(0.5\): “values above the threshold 0.5 are interpreted as offensive rebound, while values below are interpreted as defensive rebound” [PAGE 15].

For online anticipation, the source gives Equation 1:
\[
c_i = \{t_k, t_{k+1}, \ldots, t_{k+T_C-1}\} \subset V,
\]
\[
AW_i = \{t_{k+T_C}, t_{k+T_C+1}, \ldots, t_{k+T_C+\Delta-1}\}.
\]  
Here \(T_C\) is the clip length in frames, \(\Delta\) is the anticipation-window length in frames, and \(c_i\) is the observed context. The paper states: “Each clip \(c_i\) has a fixed duration of \(T_C\) frames … and for each clip we define a corresponding anticipation window \(AW_i\) of fixed length \(\Delta\)” [PAGE 7]. The online prediction is  
\[
p_\phi(Y \mid c_i), \qquad Y \in \{\text{OREB}, \text{DREB}, \text{no rebound}\}.
\]  
This is a three-class posterior over future events conditioned only on the observed clip.

The binary classification loss is the source’s Equation 2:
\[
L_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)\right],
\]  
where \(y_i \in \{0,1\}\) and \(\hat{y}_i \in [0,1]\) [PAGE 15]. If \(\log\) is natural, the loss is in nats; if \(\log_2\) is used, it is in bits. The probability \(\hat{y}_i\) is dimensionless.

For online anticipation, the source gives Equation 3:
\[
L = -\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c \, y_{v,c}\log \hat{y}_{v,c},
\]  
with class weights \(w_c\). The paper uses “[0.49, 0.49, 0.02] for the OREB, DERB and no rebound classes respectively” [PAGE 15]. The weights are dimensionless and increase the gradient contribution of action classes relative to background. The paper justifies this: “Without this weighting, the signal from action clips could be easily overshadowed by the overwhelming number of background clips” [PAGE 16].

For frame-wise spotting, the source gives Equation 4:
\[
L = -\frac{1}{\sum_{v=1}^{V} F_v}
\sum_{v=1}^{V}\sum_{f=1}^{F_v}\sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c \, y_{v,f,c}\log \hat{y}_{v,f,c},
\]  
where \(F_v\) is the number of frames in video \(v\), and the denominator normalizes over all frames in the batch [PAGE 16]. The weights are “[0.49999, 0.49999, 0.00002] for DREB, OREB, and background classes respectively” [PAGE 16]. This extreme weighting reflects “hundreds of background frames for each action frame” [PAGE 16].

The macro-averaged F1 score used for online anticipation is formally
\[
F_1^{(c)} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{Precision}^{(c)}\,\mathrm{Recall}^{(c)}}{\mathrm{Precision}^{(c)}+\mathrm{Recall}^{(c)}},
\]
\[
\mathrm{Precision}^{(c)} = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FP_c}, \qquad
\mathrm{Recall}^{(c)} = \frac{TP_c}{TP_c + FN_c},
\]
\[
F_{1,\text{macro}} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} F_1^{(c)}.
\]  
All terms are probabilities in \([0,1]\) except counts, which are dimensionless integers. A singularity occurs if \(\mathrm{Precision}^{(c)}+\mathrm{Recall}^{(c)}=0\); conventionally \(F_1^{(c)}=0\) in that case. The paper explicitly chooses macro-F1 because it ensures “all classes … are weighted equally” [PAGE 7].

The spotting metric is \(mAP@\delta\), where a prediction is correct if  
\[
\hat{y}_A = y_A
\quad\text{and}\quad
\hat{t}_A \in \left[t_A - \frac{\delta}{2}, t_A + \frac{\delta}{2}\right],
\]  
with \(\delta\) in seconds or frames [PAGE 7]. The tolerance window is a temporal uncertainty interval; if \(\delta\) is too small, precise localization is required, while if \(\delta\) is too large, the metric becomes permissive. The paper reports that “the higher the δ, the higher the mAP” [PAGE 19].

For interpretability, the source gives Equation 5:
\[
CAM_{\text{norm}}[t,h,w] =
\frac{CAM[t,h,w] - \min_{t',h',w'} CAM[t',h',w']}
{\max_{t',h',w'} CAM[t',h',w'] - \min_{t',h',w'} CAM[t',h',w'] + \epsilon},
\]  
with \(\epsilon = 10^{-8}\) [PAGE 22]. The singularity is the constant-activation case where numerator and denominator both approach zero; \(\epsilon\) prevents division by zero but also slightly compresses the dynamic range. The normalized CAM is dimensionless.

Breakdown regimes are explicit. First, as \(\tau_a \to 0\), the observed context approaches the event and the task becomes less anticipatory and more perceptual. The paper notes that at small \(\tau_a\) the ball bounce is visible [PAGE 22]. Second, as \(\tau_a\) becomes large, the model has less future information and must infer from earlier positioning. Third, for offline anticipation, videos in which the action occurs too early are removed: “A potential issue arises when the action occurs within the first \(t \le \tau_a\) frames … these videos are therefore removed” [PAGE 9]. Fourth, in online anticipation, the theoretical limit is stated: “In the theoretical limit of AW →∞, the task transitions from a multiclass classification problem with three possible classes to a binary classification problem” [PAGE 14]. Fifth, if the temporal stride is too large, the ball trajectory may be undersampled; the paper’s ablation finds optimal stride around 3 [PAGE 27]. Sixth, if pseudo-label timestamps are noisy, the nominal \(\tau_a\) no longer equals the effective anticipation time; the paper states that an early detection can make the effective anticipation window longer [PAGE 24].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper situates itself within a lineage that moves from video representation learning to event-level anticipation. The introduction frames action anticipation as a capability beyond recognition: “action anticipation … goes beyond recognizing past or ongoing events and instead focus on inferring when and what actions will happen in the future” [PAGE 1]. In sports, the paper argues that this is harder than scripted domains because “sports are characterized by open-ended interactions among multiple agents, evolving strategies, and constantly changing external conditions” [PAGE 1]. Rebound anticipation inherits this non-determinism: multiple players contest a tight region, occlusions are frequent, and “the irregular bounces of the ball off the rim introduce further uncertainty” [PAGE 2].

A direct ancestor is Felsen et al., ICCV 2017, which forecasted player moves from overhead tracking representations and found that “the random forest outperforms the CNNs” while “the Overhead CNN outperforms the Image CNN” [PAGE 5]. That result established a recurring warning: raw broadcast pixels are difficult to convert into anticipatory features without explicit geometric structure. Another ancestor is Neumann et al., CVPR Workshops 2019, which predicted “If and When” using time-to-event distributions and reported a mean TTE error of 1.42 s for their best Gaussian Mixture Model Heatmap [PAGE 5]. The paper notes that “predictions on the basketball dataset also show higher uncertainty, reflecting the inherent unpredictability of sports events” [PAGE 5]. This is genealogically important because rebound anticipation is even less temporally constrained than generic shot detection: the ball can be tipped, blocked, or redirected.

A more recent lineage is FAANTRA, adapted from FUTR for SoccerNet: “FAANTRA adapts the state-of-the-art anticipation model FUTR [20] to this new domain” [PAGE 6]. The present method TEAM is explicitly an adaptation of that sports-anticipation lineage, but simplified: “a single classification objective is used instead of combining the classification loss with an auxiliary segmentation loss” and “transformer decoder layers are omitted” [PAGE 10]. The architectural genealogy is therefore: 3D CNN backbone, temporal module, prediction head. The paper describes this general skeleton: “a backbone to extract features from the input data, a neck to further process these features … and finally a head, which is responsible for producing the final prediction” [PAGE 4]. X3D is chosen for efficiency: “X3D m … maintains the same number of parameters as the smallest versions (3.79M) while being able to process longer input sequences” [PAGE 10].

A multi-week trap in this genealogy is the temptation to treat classification pre-training as neutral. The paper uses classification as a pretext task because “the internal representations learned by this model … will be better adapted to the NBA Rebounds dataset” [PAGE 12]. However, it also warns that classification uses complete videos, including post-action frames, and therefore “it may rely excessively on information from frames occurring after the action” [PAGE 12]. To prevent leakage, the paper enforces disjoint sets: “D25K and D2K were defined as disjoint sets” [PAGE 9]. This is a crucial historical correction: one cannot pre-train on full clips and then claim that anticipation is truly blinded to the future unless the anticipation clips are temporally trimmed and the datasets are disjoint.

Another multi-week trap is pseudo-labeling. The spotting model is described as “relatively rudimentary” and its pseudo-labels are not temporally precise [PAGE 16]. The paper therefore filters by single detection, confidence \(\ge 0.99\), and class balance [PAGE 12]. Even after filtering, “25,000 pseudo-labeled samples are obtained from the original 75,000 inferred ones” and are rebalanced to 16,000 [PAGE 21]. The retro-causal lesson is that increasing dataset size without temporal calibration can create a misleading appearance of progress. The paper’s own experiment shows that adding pseudo-labeled samples “does not necessarily lead to better generalization” [PAGE 23].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The predictive agents in this paper have different theories of mind. The visual model approximates a function from observed frames to rebound class, but it does not explicitly infer player intentions unless those intentions are encoded in posture, spacing, or motion. Human experts, by contrast, use a theory of the play: they infer shooter intent, defender boxing-out behavior, offensive momentum, and likely ball rebound regions. The paper records expert cues: “the location of the shot,” “the trajectory of the ball after it bounces off the rim,” “the number of offensive players inside the three-point line,” “whether these offensive players are being boxed out,” and “whether the offensive player has momentum toward the rim” [PAGE 25]. These are recursive cues: a defender anticipates the offender’s attempt to secure the ball, while an offender anticipates the defender’s box-out.

A first regime is crisis habit regression. When temporal evidence is sparse, humans fall back on learned base rates. The paper states that at \(\tau_a = 1.5\) s, “humans display a strong bias toward the DREB class” because “defensive rebounds occur approximately two to three times more frequently than offensive ones” and “when little visual information is available, humans tend to rely on intuition and prior experience, defaulting to the most common outcome” [PAGE 24]. This is a crisis-mode regression: under uncertainty, the human predictor collapses to the prior. The model, trained on balanced offline data, does not inherit this real-world base rate, so its predictions can be more class-balanced but also may miss the empirical base-rate advantage when evidence is weak.

A second regime is the counter-trap. The model could be accused of learning superficial end-of-context motion rather than true anticipation. The interpretability result says that “the maximum activations consistently appear in the last two or three frames of the temporal context” [PAGE 22]. This is not necessarily a flaw: in a fast-changing environment, the last frames carry the most current information. But if the model becomes overly dependent on terminal frames, it may fail when the event is farther away or when the terminal frame is occluded. The paper also finds that “the model does not focus strongly on the ball” [PAGE 23], while humans rely on ball trajectory when available. Thus the counter-trap is to mistake player-position heuristics for genuine physical anticipation.

A third regime is the clock. The variable \(\tau_a\) acts as an informational clock. At \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s, the clock reveals the post-rim ball trajectory; at \(\tau_a = 1.5\) s, it hides that trajectory. The paper explains: “for a 0.5s anticipation, the ball has typically already bounced off the rim or backboard … In contrast, 1s before the action, the ball is usually still in the air or even in the hands of the player” [PAGE 22]. Human performance is clock-sensitive: experts outperform the model at \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s because they can exploit ball rebound information, but at \(\tau_a = 1.5\) s their performance drops and becomes comparable to the model [PAGE 25]. The model’s relative advantage at the longer horizon is not because it sees the ball, but because it does not rely heavily on a missing cue and maintains balanced class decisions.

The recursive audit therefore separates three predictors: the human expert, the baseline X3D, and TEAM. The human expert is a Bayesian-ish intuitive agent with strong domain priors but cue-dependent. The baseline X3D is a spatiotemporal appearance model with limited long-range temporal modeling. TEAM adds a Transformer encoder to capture longer dependencies, and indeed “TEAM consistently outperform[s] the baseline across all experiments” in one clip-length comparison [PAGE 25]. However, the ablation also shows that temporal modeling choice matters greatly: “The transformer-encoder module achieves substantially better performance compared to both the 3D CNN and the LSTM” [PAGE 27]. The theory-of-mind implication is that the winning predictor is not the one that simply knows more about basketball, but the one whose temporal module matches the horizon and uncertainty structure of the task.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical veto layer requires a rebound to be physically possible given the observed context. The paper defines a rebound as control after a missed shot [PAGE 2] and annotates it at first hand contact [PAGE 8]. Let the ball position be \(b(t)\in\mathbb{R}^3\) in meters, player hand positions be \(h_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^3\), and player positions be \(p_i(t)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) on the court plane. A necessary condition for a rebound at \(t_A\) is that there exists a player \(i\) such that  
\[
\|b(t_A)-h_i(t_A)\|_2 \le r_{\text{catch}},
\]  
where \(r_{\text{catch}}\) is a hand-control radius in meters. If no player can satisfy this inequality under kinematic constraints, a predicted rebound in the near future should be vetoed.

A second necessary condition is temporal continuity. If the ball is observed at \(b(t_W)\) with velocity \(v(t_W)\), and the maximum plausible acceleration magnitude over the unobserved interval is \(a_{\max}\), then a reachable ball position must satisfy a bounded-motion inequality such as  
\[
\|b(t_A)-b(t_W)-v(t_W)\Delta t\|_2
\le \frac{1}{2}a_{\max}(\Delta t)^2,
\]  
with \(\Delta t = t_A - t_W\) in seconds, \(v\) in \(\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\), and \(a_{\max}\) in \(\text{m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}\). This is not a toy heuristic; it is a standard bounded-dynamics feasibility condition. If a model predicts a rebound class but the observed ball state cannot reach any plausible rim-to-hand region within the physical time budget, the prediction should be rejected or heavily penalized.

A third veto is the shot-outcome condition. A rebound requires a missed shot, but online clips may contain no shot at all, or a shot that is still in flight. Let \(s(t)\) indicate shot release and \(r(t)\) indicate rim or backboard contact. If no shot release is observable before the end of the clip and the anticipation window is shorter than the minimum flight time from release to rim, then a rebound in that window is physically implausible. The paper’s human protocol enforces that test clips contain a rebound and do not end with a basket [PAGE 13], but online sampling does not enforce this: “the sampling process is exhaustive, not using the groundtruth information” [PAGE 10]. Therefore the online model must implicitly learn a veto: no shot, no immediate rebound.

A fourth veto is occlusion-induced uncertainty, but occlusion is not a veto by itself. The paper notes that rebounds involve “partial or full occlusions” [PAGE 2], and that occluded rebounds require inference from “the trajectory and speed of the ball in the last visible frame, as well as the body movement of the players” [PAGE 8]. The correct adjudication is that occlusion increases posterior variance but does not make the event impossible. Formally, if the observation likelihood is broadened by occlusion,  
\[
p(\text{observations}\mid Y_A)
\]  
becomes flatter, so the posterior should move toward the prior or toward robust positional cues. The paper’s finding that the model focuses on players rather than the ball [PAGE 23] is consistent with this: under occlusion or small ball scale, player geometry may be a more stable evidence source than ball tracking.

The weather layer is largely non-binding for NBA indoor basketball. The dataset consists of NBA games [PAGE 8], where rain, wind, and outdoor court conditions are not causal variables. A formal weather veto would state: if an environmental variable \(w\) has no causal path to ball trajectory or player motion in the domain, then conditioning on \(w\) should not alter the posterior except through domain metadata. In this corpus, weather is effectively constant or irrelevant, so any model feature based on weather would be spurious. The only environmental factors with physical relevance are indoor lighting, court friction, and broadcast camera motion. The paper’s data augmentation includes color jitter, horizontal flip, blur, random resized crop, and affine transformations [PAGE 15], which implicitly treats lighting and camera variation as nuisance variables rather than predictive cues.

The final cross-layer proof is that a valid anticipation claim must satisfy at least one of two paths. Path A is ball-physics dominance: the ball has contacted the rim or backboard, its trajectory is observable, and a player’s reachable region intersects the predicted landing zone. Path B is positional dominance: the ball trajectory is not observable, but player positions, momentum, boxing-out configuration, and shot location make one team’s possession outcome highly probable. If neither path is supported, the event should be treated as background or as high-entropy uncertainty. This adjudication explains why \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s favors humans when ball bounce is visible, while \(\tau_a = 1.5\) s can favor a model that relies on stable player-position cues and balanced classification.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Counterfactual Veto 1: Ball-tracking auxiliary loss should improve short-horizon offline anticipation.  
The source shows that humans outperform the model at \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s, with human accuracy \(0.71\) versus AI accuracy \(0.60\) [PAGE 25]. The paper explains this by saying humans use “the trajectory of the ball after it bounces off the rim” when available, while the model “does not appear to focus on the ball after the shot is released or when it bounces off the rim” [PAGE 25]. A verifiable counterfactual is: if TEAM or the baseline X3D is trained with an auxiliary ball-tracking loss predicting the ball bounding box per frame, then offline anticipation accuracy at \(\tau_a = 0.5\) s should improve relative to the current model, and the human–AI gap should shrink. The veto condition is that if ball tracking is added but the short-horizon gap remains unchanged or worsens, the hypothesis that ball trajectory is the missing cue is weakened. The source itself suggests this direction: “incorporating an explicit ball-tracking auxiliary loss … would introduce an inductive bias toward attending to the ball’s trajectory” [PAGE 28]. Prediction: the largest gain should appear at small \(\tau_a\), because that is where rim rebound information is visible; at large \(\tau_a\), the gain should be smaller because the ball has not yet bounced.

Counterfactual Veto 2: Longer anticipation windows should monotonically simplify online prediction, with diminishing returns and possible baseline convergence.  
The paper states: “the larger the anticipation window, the higher the performance for both models” except for a slight TEAM drop from 1.0 s to 1.5 s [PAGE 26]. It also predicts that as AW increases, “the performance gap narrows; in some cases (AW = 1.5s) the baseline model even slightly surpasses TEAM” [PAGE 26]. The formal reason is that a larger AW increases the chance that an action occurs inside the window, reducing the effective background burden. In the limit, the task becomes binary: “In the theoretical limit of AW →∞, the task transitions from a multiclass classification problem with three possible classes to a binary classification problem” [PAGE 14]. A verifiable prediction is: holding clip length fixed, macro-F1 should increase with AW, but the marginal gain should decrease as AW grows because most clips already contain actions at large windows. If a model performs worse at larger AW without a change in class imbalance or label noise, the monotonic simplification hypothesis is vetoed. The source also warns that the apparent gain is not because longer windows improve understanding, but because “a larger AW provides a higher margin of error” [PAGE 14].

Counterfactual Veto 3: Pseudo-label timestamp jitter should prevent consistent gains from larger training sets.  
Let the pseudo-label timestamp be  
\[
\hat{t}_A = t_A + e,
\]  
where \(e\) is timestamp error in seconds. If a clip is trimmed \(\tau_a\) seconds before the pseudo-label, the final observed frame is  
\[
\hat{t}_W = \hat{t}_A - \tau_a = t_A + e - \tau_a.
\]  
The effective anticipation time relative to the true event is therefore  
\[
\tau_{\text{eff}} = t_A - \hat{t}_W = \tau_a - e.
\]  
If the spotting model detects the rebound earlier than the true rebound, \(e < 0\), so \(\tau_{\text{eff}} > \tau_a\), making the clip harder and changing the input distribution. The paper explicitly describes this: if detection is 0.5 s early, “the effective anticipation window becomes 1.5s” when nominal \(\tau_a = 1.0\) s [PAGE 24]. If detection is late, \(e > 0\), the effective horizon becomes shorter and the task easier. Because \(e\) has nonzero variance, pseudo-labeling injects temporal label noise into the very definition of the context window. A verifiable prediction is: adding pseudo-labeled samples without timestamp refinement will not consistently improve test accuracy, and may degrade it, especially at small \(\tau_a\) where temporal precision matters most. The paper’s result supports this: “increasing the number of training samples does not necessarily lead to better generalization” and the irregular trend prevents a stable conclusion [PAGE 23]. The veto condition is that if pseudo-labels are not temporally calibrated, any claimed improvement from increased sample count is confounded by altered effective anticipation times.

These three counterfactuals provide verifiable tests. First, ball tracking should matter most when the ball bounce is visible. Second, longer anticipation windows should ease online class imbalance but with diminishing returns. Third, pseudo-label expansion without timestamp correction should fail or produce unstable gains because the effective \(\tau_a\) is randomized by annotation error. Together they enforce a strict evidentiary standard: performance claims must be separated into physical cue availability, temporal-window geometry, and label-noise artifacts.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
