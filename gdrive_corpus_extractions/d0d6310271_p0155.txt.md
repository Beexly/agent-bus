Knight’s paragraph on [PAGE 140] is a node in a long genealogy. The idea that the entrepreneur or borrower bears non-measurable uncertainty traces to Richard Cantillon’s distinction between fixed-income hirelings and entrepreneur-bearers of uncertain profit. Knight (1921) sharpened this by isolating “uncertainty” from “risk” proper: risk is insurable because it admits an objective probability distribution; uncertainty is not, because the very state space or its probabilities are not known ex ante. The capital-loan separation argument then becomes a property-rights solution to non-measurability: when \(\mathcal{P}\) cannot be reduced to a single \(P\), the saver will not hold the residual claim directly, so ownership of value equities is detached from direct control of production goods via the debt contract.

Later lineage includes Keynes (1937) on genuine uncertainty as a determinant of liquidity preference; Arrow–Debreu incomplete-markets general equilibrium, where absence of state-contingent markets recreates Knightian separation; Ellsberg (1961), which experimentally verified ambiguity aversion; and Gilboa–Schmeidler (1989) max-min expected utility, which formalizes the \(U_{\text{Knight}}\) operator above. Modern entrepreneurial finance (e.g., venture capital as equity rather than debt) is the institutional descendant: when cash flows are non-measurable, financiers take residual claims rather than fixed loan contracts.
