<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of the [PAGE 9] method traces through actuarial science, sports epidemiology, and collaborative-filtering machine learning. In the 17th century, Edmund Halley's life-table construction established the use of population counts to estimate individual mortality probabilities, a direct ancestor of computing P(S_i) as injuries divided by games played [PAGE 9]. In the 20th century, survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier, 1958; Cox proportional hazards, 1972) formalized time-to-event modeling with censoring, which the [PAGE 9] validation block 716 attempts informally via "number of days between the target date and a first subsequent injury" but without explicit hazard functions or censoring adjustments. Sports injury epidemiology (Meeuwisse model; Bahr & Maehlum) introduced multifactorial etiology—predisposing internal risk factors and precipitating external events—whereas [PAGE 9] reduces risk to a scalar similarity-weighted average. In machine learning, item-based collaborative filtering (Sarwar et al., early 2000s) computes weighted averages of peer ratings using similarity weights, mathematically analogous to P(I/S) = Σ P(S_i)M(i) / Σ M(i) [PAGE 9]. The patent US 11,049,031 B2 thus sits at the intersection of actuarial peer-rating methods and sports injury risk scoring.

Multi-week traps arise from the temporal structure in [PAGE 9]. Block 802 aggregates data "from a start of a current season to a target date" [PAGE 9], so early-season observations carry the same formal weight as late-season observations unless M encodes temporal decay (unspecified in [PAGE 9]). In team sports, injury risk exhibits clustering: reinjury of hamstring or ankle structures commonly recurs within 2–4 weeks of return to play. Because P(S_i) is a season-average ratio, it dilutes acute post-reinjury hazard windows and cannot resolve within-week risk modulation. The validation phase 701 occurs "after the target date (e.g., the game/competition has been played)" [PAGE 9], meaning model updates lag at least one competition cycle. If a player sustains an injury in week k, that information only enters the similar-player database after week k's validation, creating a multi-week feedback delay during which the model continues to generate predictions with stale peer statistics. Finally, the claim that "a previous injury is a good predictor of injury" [PAGE 9] encodes a Markovian recurrence assumption; it ignores that recovery interventions (physiotherapy, load modification) alter the underlying hazard between the previous injury and the target date, violating stationarity across multi-week horizons.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression operates when the model's own output changes the behavior it seeks to predict. If block 710 yields a high P(I) for the target player, coaching or medical staff may reduce that player's minutes or rest them entirely [PAGE 9 context: prediction report block 712 is generated]. Reduced exposure lowers the denominator of future injury rates and suppresses the observed injury count, producing a self-negating prophecy: the high-risk prediction causes the intervention that prevents the injury, after which the accuracy report at block 720 may record a "false positive" even though the intervention was causally efficacious. The model then regresses toward underestimating risk for similar players because the intervention-induced absence of injury is recorded as a non-event rather than a prevented event.

A counter-trap emerges in adversarial or strategic environments. If an opposing team or the player's own organization knows the P(I/S) ranking (e.g., via the prediction report 112 [PAGE 9]), they may deliberately alter mechanical loading: an opponent might target a flagged high-risk player in contact situations, while the player's own team might strategically rest them to manage the similarity database for future predictions. In either case, the similarity factors M and the injury counts n_{inj,i} become endogenous to the prediction itself, violating the exchangeability assumption required for P(I/S) [PAGE 9]. The model is no longer passively observing; it is participating in the system it models.

The clock mismatch compounds both effects. Physiological recovery from musculoskeletal strain operates on a 48–72 hour inflammatory and remodeling cycle, whereas the [PAGE 9] validation loop only closes "after the target date" [PAGE 9], typically at the next day or later. The model's update clock is therefore slower than the biological clock of injury risk. A player whose risk spikes due to acute fatigue in game k will not have that spike reflected in the similar-player P(S) statistics until after game k+1's validation, creating a persistent lag in which P(I) systematically underestimates transient post-load hazard.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: [PAGE 9] computes P(S_i) purely from injury counts and games played. It contains no internal-load covariate (session RPE, TRIMP, accelerometry load) and no external-load covariate (GPS distance, high-speed running, collision count). Because non-contact soft-tissue injury is strongly modulated by acute:chronic workload ratio, a player who played 90 minutes at low intensity receives the same exposure credit as one who played 90 minutes at maximal intensity. Veto: any target date featuring a sudden load spike (e.g., return from international travel, compressed fixture) invalidates P(I/S) unless load data are added; the model cannot discriminate mechanical overuse from game-count exposure.

Biomechanical layer veto: The similarity factor M(i) is referenced at blocks 804 and 810 [PAGE 9] but its defining features are never disclosed in the excerpt. If M is constructed from aggregate performance statistics (games played, position, age), two players with identical game counts but divergent kinematic risk signatures—such as dynamic valgus during landing or asymmetric hip extension—are assigned identical similarity weights. Since ACL rupture and hamstring strain are mechanism-specific, not rate-specific, the model commits omitted-variable bias at the biomechanical layer. Veto: when a target player exhibits a known biomechanical fault uncorrelated with season game count, P(I) is physically unjustified regardless of the similarity-weighted average.

Weather layer veto: Block 802 accesses only injury and game data [PAGE 9]; block 804 accesses only similarity factors [PAGE 9]. Ambient temperature, relative humidity, precipitation, and surface type (natural turf vs. artificial turf) are absent. Cold environments reduce muscle-tendon unit compliance and increase strain risk; high heat increases dehydration-mediated fatigue. Because these are exogenous modulators of injury hazard uncorrelated with peer similarity, their omission means P(I) cannot be treated as a complete physical risk estimate. Veto: extreme weather conditions on the target date constitute a structural break; the model's [0,1] probability output is not calibrated to environmental tails.

Adjudication: The [PAGE 9] architecture is a correlational prior over peer injury rates, not a causal physical model. Any one of the three veto layers—unmeasured load, unmodeled biomechanics, or omitted weather—triggers a structural-break veto: the predicted P(I) should be flagged as unreliable for that specific target date until the missing layer is integrated. No such veto logic appears in blocks 708–720 of [PAGE 9], so the system as disclosed emits a probability even when physical preconditions for valid inference are absent.
