<THINK_TRACE>
Audit trace and multi-hop derivation. The source’s causal chain is: rule text becomes referee majority label; referee label becomes supervised target; smartphone video becomes pose-estimation coordinates; coordinates become normalized biomechanical features; features become a logistic-regression decision; decision is interpreted through standard regression coefficients. Each hop is auditable and each hop has a failure mode. The first hop is stated on [PAGE 1]: “at least one foot must always be in contact with the ground and the forward knee must be extended until it is perpendicular to the ground [4].” The source then names the two violations: “Violation of the first rule is called loss of contact (LC) violation and that of the second rule is called bent knee (BK) violation” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is on [PAGE 2]: three qualified referees judged the videos and “We annotated the videos according to the majority vote of the three referees.” Therefore the model is not trained against force-plate contact or against a geometric ground truth; it is trained against a referee-consensus label. That distinction is central to every generalization claim.

The third hop is pose estimation. The system uses HigherHRNet, a bottom-up model, because “The performance of the bottom-up model is superior to that of the top-down model in multiple-person estimation” [PAGE 3]. It estimates 17 key points: “nose, left and right eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles” [PAGE 3]. The fourth hop is normalization and feature construction: “all data were normalized so that the length from the nose to the hip key point was 1” [PAGE 4], and all points were translated so that the nose is at the origin. Then the shank is the midpoint of knee and ankle, and the knee angle is “θ counterclockwise from the thigh to the calf” [PAGE 4]. Outliers are removed by standard deviation of frame-to-frame right-knee-angle change; the interval is “two steps from the most bent right knee”; and all data are interpolated to 85 frames [PAGE 4]. The fifth hop is classification: “A logistic regression model was used as the classifier for fault detection” [PAGE 4]. The sixth hop is interpretation: standard regression coefficients are averaged across cross-validation models to identify which features and frames drive BK and LC decisions.

The result is strong but not uniform. The source reports “average accuracy of over 90%” [PAGE 1], yet [PAGE 6] gives walker A BK accuracy 0.850 and LC accuracy 0.700, while walkers B–D are mostly 0.915–1.000 and walker E LC is 0.969. The explanation is not merely “Olympic medalist is hard.” The source says “The model could not detect LC in the movements of walker A well” [PAGE 8], and “the knee angle, which according to the rules should not be the focus of attention during LC detection, became more important” [PAGE 8]. The mechanism is stated: “Walker B rolled up his hind leg during LC walking, and as a result, his knee was bent more than in normal walking” [PAGE 8], and “walkers B to E tended to roll up their hind legs when they intentionally walked in LC walking, while walker A did not” [PAGE 8]. Thus the training distribution contains a non-rule cue for LC: hind-leg roll-up. A model trained on B–E can use knee angle as a shortcut for LC. On walker A, who does not produce that cue, the shortcut fails. This is the central gray-zone audit: high average accuracy can coexist with a rule-mismatched feature, and the source itself identifies that mismatch.

Source-text audit also reveals inconsistencies that must be preserved rather than smoothed away. [PAGE 2] says “The walking section was approximately 20 m long,” while [PAGE 12] says “The participants’ walking section was about 30 meters and the recording section was about 12 meters.” Those are not identical; the pipeline’s field geometry is therefore under-specified. [PAGE 5] prints a nonstandard precision sentence: “the Precision is defined as the ratio of the sum of true positives and true negatives to false positives.” That is not the usual positive predictive value. It should be treated as a source anomaly, not silently “fixed.” [PAGE 16] shows valid-data counts that differ from the nominal video counts, with the explanation that “They are different due to failures in pose estimation and differences between the instructions at the time of walking and the judgments of the referees.” These discrepancies matter because the classifier’s ground truth is referee majority, not physical contact, and the effective sample per class per walker is small. The audit conclusion is therefore: the paper demonstrates feasibility under intentional-fault, single-smartphone, side-profile conditions; it does not prove race-condition validity, multi-person occlusion robustness, weather invariance, or ground-truth contact measurement.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source’s operators are: rule-to-label annotation; HigherHRNet pose estimation; fine-tuning; nose-to-hip normalization; shank midpoint construction; knee-angle construction; standard-deviation outlier removal; two-step interval extraction; interpolation to 85 frames; logistic-regression classification; leave-one-walker-out cross-validation; accuracy and F-score; and standard-regression-coefficient feature importance. The label operator is defined by referee majority: “We annotated the videos according to the majority vote of the three referees” [PAGE 2]. The rule operator is stated as: “at least one foot must always be in contact with the ground and the forward knee must be extended until it is perpendicular to the ground [4]” [PAGE 1]. The model is not given a direct contact sensor; it receives pose coordinates and knee angles.

Pose-estimation evaluation uses OKS. The source prints [PAGE 5]:
\[
\mathrm{OKS}
=
\frac{\sum_i \exp\!\left(-d_i^2/(2s^2k_i^2)\right)\delta(v_i>0)}
{\sum_i \delta(v_i>0)}.
\]
Here \(d_i\) is the Euclidean distance between the detected keypoint and the corresponding ground truth, \(v_i\) is the visibility flag, \(s\) is object scale, and \(k_i\) is a per-keypoint falloff constant: “where di is the Euclidean distance between the detected keypoint and the corresponding ground truth, vi is the visibility flag of the ground truth, s is the object scale, and ki is a per-keypoint constant that controls falloff” [PAGE 5]. Units: \(d_i\) is in image pixels or normalized image coordinates; \(s\) must carry the same unit as \(d_i\); \(k_i\) is dimensionless; the exponent is dimensionless; OKS is dimensionless. AP is dimensionless and is computed over OKS thresholds: “the threshold value is changed in 10 steps of 0.05 from 0.50 to 0.95” [PAGE 5]. The source reports AP rising from 0.961 to 0.992 after fine-tuning [PAGE 5].

The knee-angle operator is defined verbally on [PAGE 4]: “The shank position was defined as the midpoint of the knee and ankle, and the knee angle was defined as θ counterclockwise from the thigh to the calf.” A formal realization, consistent with that definition, is:
\[
p_{\mathrm{shank}}=\frac{p_{\mathrm{knee}}+p_{\mathrm{ankle}}}{2},
\]
\[
u_t=\frac{p_{\mathrm{knee}}-p_{\mathrm{hip}}}{\lVert p_{\mathrm{knee}}-p_{\mathrm{hip}}\rVert_2},
\quad
u_c=\frac{p_{\mathrm{ankle}}-p_{\mathrm{knee}}}{\lVert p_{\mathrm{ankle}}-p_{\mathrm{knee}}\rVert_2},
\]
\[
\theta=
\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(
u_{t,x}u_{c,y}-u_{t,y}u_{c,x},
\;
u_t\cdot u_c
\right).
\]
The angle is in radians. The coordinate-axis convention must be fixed because the sign of a counterclockwise angle depends on whether image \(y\) increases downward; the source does not print the axis convention. Normalization is:
\[
p_i'=\frac{p_i-p_{\mathrm{nose}}}{\lVert p_{\mathrm{hip}}-p_{\mathrm{nose}}\rVert_2}.
\]
The resulting coordinates are dimensionless because they are divided by a nose-to-hip length.

The classifier is a logistic model. The source says only “A logistic regression model was used as the classifier for fault detection” [PAGE 4]. A standard formal statement is:
\[
p(y=1\mid x)=\sigma(w^\top x+b),
\quad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-z)}.
\]
Here \(x\) contains normalized coordinate features and knee angles; \(w\) has inverse feature units; \(b\) is in log-odds; \(p\) is dimensionless probability in \([0,1]\). The source does not print a logistic likelihood equation, so none should be attributed to it. The source’s feature-importance operator is “the standard regression coefficient for the logistic regression model as a measure of feature importance” [PAGE 5]. It does not print a standardization formula; therefore the exact normalization of those coefficients is unspecified.

The reported performance metrics are:
\[
\mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},
\]
\[
\mathrm{F\mbox{-}score}=\frac{2\times \mathrm{Precision}\times \mathrm{Recall}}{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\]
The source prints the F-score formula on [PAGE 5], but it also prints the anomalous sentence: “the Precision is defined as the ratio of the sum of true positives and true negatives to false positives” [PAGE 5]. That sentence is not the standard positive predictive value. Treat it as a source-text anomaly and do not replace it silently.

Singularity and breakdown regimes are exact and important. OKS is undefined if \(\sum_i\delta(v_i>0)=0\), that is, if no ground-truth keypoint is visible. It is also undefined or divergent if \(s=0\), \(k_i=0\), or a limb segment has zero length. The knee angle is undefined if \(\lVert p_{\mathrm{knee}}-p_{\mathrm{hip}}\rVert_2=0\) or \(\lVert p_{\mathrm{ankle}}-p_{\mathrm{knee}}\rVert_2=0\). Normalization is undefined if the nose and hip keypoints coincide. Standard-deviation outlier removal is undefined or degenerate if the frame-to-frame right-knee-angle change has zero standard deviation. Interpolation to 85 frames is ill-posed if the extracted two-step interval is too short to contain a stable phase or if keypoints are missing across the interval. Logistic regression has a separation breakdown: if a cross-validation fold is linearly separable, coefficients can diverge and feature importance becomes unstable. The source’s small per-walker folds and class counts in [PAGE 16] make that instability a real concern. No synthetic “Force divided by Fatigue” or similar toy ratio is used; the above operators are the source’s actual pipeline and standard mathematical formalizations of it.

Cross-validation is by walker: “the training walker data does not include the test data, thus the model performance for unknown walkers can be evaluated” [PAGE 5]. The BK model used walkers A–D; the LC model used walkers A–E [PAGE 5]. Table 1 on [PAGE 6] gives: walker A BK 0.850 accuracy and 0.893 F-score; walker A LC 0.700 accuracy and 0.727 F-score; walker B BK 0.966 and 0.961, LC 0.960 and 0.959; walker C BK 0.915 and 0.905, LC 0.967 and 0.967; walker D BK 0.972 and 0.966, LC 1.000 and 1.000; walker E LC 0.969 and 0.969. The source’s summary “The average accuracy was over 90%” [PAGE 6] is true at the aggregate level but masks the walker-A LC failure.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The historical lineage is retrocausal in the sense that later machine-learning design choices recast earlier rule text as a supervised label. The root is the race-walking rule: contact and knee extension, with LC and BK violations [PAGE 1]. Earlier detection attempts used body-attached sensors: piezoelectric plantar sensors, waist and lower-limb accelerometers, and inertial machine learning [PAGE 2]. The source criticizes those because “all of these studies used sensors attached to the body, which is not practical because they may affect the performance of walkers” [PAGE 2]. That criticism motivates non-contact video. The lineage then branches into image-recognition precedents from other sports: rhythmic-gymnastics scoring, figure-skating scoring, and soccer offside detection [PAGE 2], with references listed on [PAGE 10]. The pose-estimation lineage is HigherHRNet [PAGE 3], MMPose [PAGE 4], COCO Annotator [PAGE 4], and COCO keypoint evaluation [PAGE 5]. The fine-tuning precedent is track-and-field long-jump and triple-jump pose estimation [PAGE 4]. The interpretability lineage is logistic regression and standard regression coefficients [PAGE 4], chosen because “it is undesirable not to understand the process of judging sports” [PAGE 4]. Thus the paper’s genealogy is: rule text → sensor-based fault detection → non-contact sports scoring → bottom-up pose estimation → fine-tuning on race-walking images → interpretable logistic classification.

The multi-week traps are visible in the experimental design. The source says the experiment was “conducted over multiple dates to ensure that fatigue did not affect the walk” [PAGE 3]. That is a good control against acute fatigue, but it creates longitudinal risks: referee calibration can drift across dates; athletes can habituate to the instruction to walk with intentional BK or LC; clothing, lighting, and camera placement can change; and the athlete’s “intentional fault” can become a learned skill rather than a natural fault. The source itself notes that walkers B–E “rolled up” the hind leg during intentional LC, while walker A did not [PAGE 8]. Over repeated weeks, that roll-up may be a training artifact rather than a rule violation. A second multi-week trap is label leakage by instruction: participants were “instructed to randomly walk with a normal form, a BK, and LC” [PAGE 3], so the label is tied to the instruction and to referee perception, not to independently measured contact. A third trap is small-sample walker identity: only one Olympic medalist and four university athletes are included [PAGE 2], and the pose-estimation fine-tuning used only 108 training images, 25 validation images, and 52 test images [PAGE 4]. A fourth trap is class imbalance and invalid-video attrition: [PAGE 16] shows walker A with 31/20 normal, 9/20 BK, and 20/20 LC, while walker E has no BK data. These are not cosmetic; they determine which features the logistic model can learn. The retrocausal lesson is that the paper’s later “interpretable” feature importance is partly a genealogy of its earlier data-collection choices: because B–E rolled up the hind leg during LC, knee angle became a useful LC feature; because walker A did not, that feature failed on the medalist.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The nested agents are the athlete, the referee, the model, and the evaluator. The athlete knows the rule and knows the referee is watching; the referee knows the rule and must infer contact and knee extension from a side view; the model learns the referee’s majority label, not the rule directly; the evaluator reads the model’s accuracy and feature importance. “Crisis habit regression” is the athlete’s return to a trained motor habit under the artificial instruction to fault. Walker A’s habitual control apparently allowed LC without the hind-leg roll-up that B–E produced: “walkers B to E tended to roll up their hind legs when they intentionally walked in LC walking, while walker A did not” [PAGE 8]. That is a crisis-habit difference: the medalist’s overlearned gait suppresses a non-rule compensation, while less expert walkers use a visible knee-bend compensation. The “counter-trap” is the model’s learned shortcut. Because the training labels co-occur with the compensation, the model can use knee angle for LC even though the rule is about contact: “the knee angle, which according to the rules should not be the focus of attention during LC detection, became more important” [PAGE 8]. The counter-trap is that optimizing referee-label fit can make the model less rule-faithful. The “clock” is the temporal normalization: two steps from the most bent right knee, interpolated to 85 frames [PAGE 4], with 60 fps capture [PAGE 2]. The model attends to specific frame windows; [PAGE 7] reports that frames 0–9, 35–49, and 75–84 had high importance for the left knee angle, while frames 15–24 and 55–69 had high importance for the right knee angle in BK detection. If the phase clock is off—because the “most bent right knee” detector is wrong, because the walker’s cadence differs, or because interpolation distorts timing—the important windows shift and the model’s apparent rule-following becomes an artifact of alignment. Recursive theory of mind therefore requires three checks: does the athlete’s intention match the rule violation; does the referee’s majority label match physical contact and knee extension; and does the model’s feature importance match the rule rather than the athlete’s compensation habit? The source provides evidence for the third check only partially: BK feature importance is rule-consistent (“the knee angle is of utmost importance in BK detection” [PAGE 7]), but LC feature importance is contaminated by the B–E roll-up habit [PAGE 8].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Adjudication must proceed layer by layer, and a lower layer can veto a higher-layer claim. Physical-layer veto: the input is a single smartphone camera at 60 fps [PAGE 2], with a side-profile view and a walking section whose length is inconsistently reported as “approximately 20 m” [PAGE 2] and “about 30 meters” with a “recording section ... about 12 meters” [PAGE 12]. At 60 fps the frame interval is \(\Delta t=1/60\ \mathrm{s}\approx 0.0167\ \mathrm{s}\). Any contact-loss interval shorter than that cannot be reliably resolved by frame sampling alone; the source does not measure the duration distribution of contact loss. If ankle or foot keypoints are occluded, OKS excludes them through \(\delta(v_i>0)\), and if all relevant keypoints are invisible the OKS denominator is zero. Therefore a physical-layer veto holds whenever the contact-relevant keypoints are missing or the sampling interval is not demonstrably shorter than the contact-loss event. Biomechanical-layer veto: BK is defined by knee extension “until it is perpendicular to the ground” [PAGE 1], but the computed \(\theta\) depends on hip, knee, and ankle keypoints. If keypoint error is comparable to limb length, \(\theta\) is unstable; if thigh or calf length is zero, \(\theta\) is undefined. LC is defined by foot-ground contact, but the pipeline estimates ankle keypoints, not sole pressure or ground reaction force. A pose-only LC conclusion is therefore not a direct contact measurement. The walker-A result is a biomechanical veto on the LC model’s feature validity: because walker A did not roll up the hind leg, the knee-angle feature that the model used for LC was not a rule feature [PAGE 8]. Weather-layer veto: the source pages provided do not report rain, wind, glare, temperature, or illuminance covariates. There is no evidence that the pose estimator or classifier is invariant to wet ground, changing light, loose clothing, or wind-induced camera motion. A weather veto is therefore an epistemic veto: without weather metadata, an all-condition claim is unsupported. The adjudication rule is: if physical validity is not established, reject the fault conclusion; if biomechanical feature validity is not established, reject the rule interpretation; if weather invariance is not established, downgrade the system to controlled-condition feasibility. The source itself supports this caution: it says applying the system to a race is “more difficult because it is necessary to improve the performance of pose estimation for multiple persons and to solve the problem of occlusion between persons” [PAGE 9]. Thus the correct cross-layer verdict is not “the model detects faults” but “the model detects referee-majority labels under controlled side-view, single-walker, intentional-fault conditions, with a demonstrated LC feature-validity failure on the medalist.”
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>

If the pre-trained HigherHRNet is used without race-walking fine-tuning, the source’s own reported AP should return from 0.992 to 0.961, and knee-angle outliers should increase; the source says fine-tuning “improves the estimation performance” and that outliers are reduced [PAGE 5]. Verifiable prediction: rerunning the fault classifier on the pre-trained pose outputs will lower BK and LC accuracy relative to Table 1, especially in the frames where knee angle is most important. This veto tests whether the reported >90% average depends on domain adaptation rather than on the classifier alone.

Counterfactual veto 2: remove the hind-leg roll-up cue. The source states that “walkers B to E tended to roll up their hind legs when they intentionally walked in LC walking, while walker A did not” [PAGE 8]. If B–E are re-instructed or physically constrained to perform LC without hind-leg roll-up, matching walker A’s movement, then the LC model’s knee-angle importance for walker A should fall and walker A’s LC accuracy should rise above the reported 0.700 [PAGE 6]. Conversely, a model trained only on walker-A-like LC should lose accuracy on the original B–E roll-up data because the shortcut feature is absent. Verifiable prediction: the LC feature-importance rank of knee angle will decrease, and cross-walker LC F-score will become more symmetric across A–E if the cue is removed. This veto tests rule-faithfulness versus label-fitting.

Counterfactual veto 3: temporal and occlusion stress. If the 60 fps videos are downsampled to 30 fps, the frame interval doubles to \(1/30\ \mathrm{s}\approx 0.0333\ \mathrm{s}\), so short contact-loss events and the precise “most bent right knee” phase become less resolvable. If ankle or knee keypoints are masked to simulate occlusion, OKS loses those keypoints and the knee-angle and y-coordinate features become missing or unstable. Verifiable prediction: LC accuracy and F-score will degrade more than BK accuracy, because LC depends on contact timing and on y-coordinate/knee-position features, while BK depends strongly on knee angle in specific frames [PAGE 7]. The walker-A LC case should degrade first, since it already has the lowest reported LC accuracy, 0.700 [PAGE 6]. This veto tests whether the system’s apparent rule detection survives the physical sampling and occlusion limits that the source identifies for real races [PAGE 9].
