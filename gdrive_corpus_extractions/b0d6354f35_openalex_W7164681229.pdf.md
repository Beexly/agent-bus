The central reasoning task is to adjudicate the GIRL-GNN framework not as a superficial tactical classifier, but as a feasibility-constrained offline reinforcement-learning operator over synchronized tracking and event data. The source states that “At each decision moment t, the environment state is defined as:” [PAGE 12], and immediately defines the state as a heterogeneous graph plus a context vector. That framing forces a multi-hop derivation: the model must first infer a relational state from player, ball, and zone nodes; second, it must generate a hierarchical action pair, where high-level intent is separated from low-level execution; third, it must test whether that action survives physical, temporal, and rule-based feasibility; and fourth, it must score the surviving action under a calibrated risk-sensitive objective. The gray-zone audit therefore has to inspect four layers simultaneously: representation, feasibility, learning signal, and empirical validation.

A first gray-zone issue is the small match count. The source explicitly says the evaluation uses “seven professional matches” [PAGE 7] and that “The dataset comprises only seven matches at the match level, it contains over one million synchronized tracking frames and several thousand on-ball decision moments” [PAGE 20]. This creates a tension: the effective decision-level sample may be large, but match-level generalization remains fragile. The authors acknowledge this by saying the results are “within the evaluated dataset” [PAGE 29] and by interpreting p-values “descriptively rather than as strict decision thresholds” [PAGE 27]. That is scientifically cautious, but it also means the framework’s external validity is not proven across leagues, seasons, tactical schools, or fixture contexts.

A second gray-zone issue is the interaction between graph sparsity and tactical completeness. The source imposes hard sparsification constraints: “For each ball carrier ... candidate actions are restricted to a sparse subset of edges” [PAGE 14], with maximum passing and carrying candidates. The source’s own case study admits a failure mode: “Under extreme graph sparsity ... certain weak-side connections may be pruned, leaving open players unlinked to the ball” [PAGE 44]. This is not a minor implementation detail; it is a structural singularity. If the true optimal action is pruned before scoring, the policy cannot recover it, regardless of how well the value, risk, and feasibility heads are calibrated. The model therefore operates under a combinatorial veto: an action that is tactically superior but not present in the sparse candidate graph is effectively nonexistent.

A third gray-zone issue is feasibility thresholding. The source says “Each candidate control is retained only if:” and specifies a feasibility probability constraint [PAGE 15]. The reported threshold is 0.6 [PAGE 18]. This is conservative, but conservative filtering can create a hidden bias: in highly constrained states, the model may default to safe retention even when a slightly riskier action would have higher expected possession value. The source partially addresses this by noting that under dense pressure the decoder “naturally prioritizes safer alternatives such as short passes or weak-side switches” [PAGE 16]. That is desirable for robustness, but it also means the policy may systematically under-explore high-value vertical options in exactly the moments where a team might need them, such as late-game trailing states.

A fourth gray-zone issue is calibration. The paper reports an Expected Calibration Error of 0.038 [PAGE 28], which is strong, but calibration is fitted only on training/validation matches within each leave-one-match-out fold [PAGE 17]. This prevents leakage, yet it does not guarantee calibration under true distribution shift. If the held-out match contains a tactical style unseen in training, such as extreme directness, ultra-low block, or unusual set-piece dependence, temperature scaling and isotonic regression may preserve marginal calibration while failing conditionally. The source’s exploratory data analysis already shows match-specific style differences: “J03WMX features a greater number of long passes, consistent with a more direct attacking approach” whereas “In J03WOH, most passes are of medium range, suggesting patient ball circulation” [PAGE 23]. Those style shifts are exactly the kind of covariate shift that can degrade conditional feasibility estimation.

A fifth gray-zone issue is interpretability. The source defines interpretability in an “analyst-facing tactical analysis” sense [PAGE 6], not as full mechanistic transparency. It supports lane attention, counterfactual rollouts, spatial attention, and role-wise contributions [PAGE 35–36]. This is operationally useful, but attention weights are not causal proofs. A counterfactual rollout showing an approximate “ΔEPV≈0.20 after six steps” [PAGE 36] is persuasive only if the rollout model is faithful. If the world model is biased toward the same style that produced the policy, counterfactual explanations can become self-confirming. Thus interpretability should be treated as decision support, not as verification of tactical optimality.

The final audit concern is whether the framework’s claimed robustness is structural or empirical. The source reports that “GIRL-GNN degrades the least, retaining over 94% of its original performance under maximum noise, compared with approximately 88% for the sequence baseline and below 85% for the rule-based engine” [PAGE 29]. That is evidence of noise resilience, but noise injection is not the same as adversarial tactical shift. A defender set may adapt to the model’s preferred weak-side switch pattern, creating a non-stationary opponent. The paper includes opponent modeling [PAGE 14], but not explicit online adaptation to opponent learning. Therefore robustness should be adjudicated as “robust to input noise within evaluated matches,” not as robust to strategic counter-adaptation.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The formal state operator is given as

\[
s_t = (G_t, c_t)
\]

where \(G_t=(V_t,E_t)\) is a heterogeneous graph and \(c_t\) is a match-context vector [PAGE 12]. Units: graph nodes and edges are dimensionless symbolic entities, while embedded node/edge features carry spatial units such as meters for normalized pitch coordinates, meters per second for velocity, and seconds for time-derived features. The context vector summarizes “score differential, time remaining, possession status, and set-piece indicators” [PAGE 13], so its components have units of goals, seconds, binary indicators, and set-piece categorical codes.

The hierarchical action operator is

\[
a_t = (g_t, u_t)
\]

where \(g_t\) is tactical intent and \(u_t\) is executable control [PAGE 13]. The source describes \(g_t\) as “progression, switching, or retention” [PAGE 13]. Formally, \(g_t \in \mathcal{G}\), with \(\mathcal{G}\) a finite tactical-intent set, and \(u_t \in \mathcal{U}(s_t)\), where \(\mathcal{U}(s_t)\) is the feasible low-level action set after graph sparsification and rule filtering.

Graph sparsification is defined by

\[
|E_t^{pass}(i)| \le K_p, \qquad |E_t^{carry}(i)| \le K_c
\]

for each ball carrier \(i\in V_t\) [PAGE 14]. In the implementation, \(K_p=6\) and \(K_c=4\) [PAGE 18]. The source also states that “At least one weak-side or cross-field option is always retained to preserve tactical completeness” [PAGE 14]. This creates a hard combinatorial constraint:

\[
\mathcal{U}_{sparse}(s_t) = \{u : u \text{ belongs to retained pass/carry edges}\}
\]

and the true optimal action is admissible only if

\[
u^* \in \mathcal{U}_{sparse}(s_t).
\]

If \(u^* \notin \mathcal{U}_{sparse}(s_t)\), then the policy suffers an irrecoverable pruning singularity.

The candidate-control scoring operator is

\[
S(u_t|s_t) = \Delta \widehat{EPV} - \lambda_{opp}\widehat{xG}_{against} + \lambda_{coh}Coh(u_t) + \lambda_{cxc}\log \widehat{P}_{feas}
\]

[PAGE 14]. Units: \(\Delta \widehat{EPV}\) is expected possession value gain in probability-like or expected-goal-equivalent units; \(\widehat{xG}_{against}\) is opponent expected-goal risk in expected goals; \(Coh(u_t)\) is a dimensionless positional-coherence score; \(\widehat{P}_{feas}\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless feasibility probability; \(\lambda\) coefficients are trade-off weights. The logarithmic feasibility term creates a boundary singularity: as \(\widehat{P}_{feas}\to 0^+\), \(\log \widehat{P}_{feas}\to -\infty\), so infeasible actions receive arbitrarily large penalties. This is desirable for vetoing impossible actions, but numerically unstable if \(\widehat{P}_{feas}\) is not clipped.

Feasibility is modeled as a logistic probability:

\[
\widehat{P}_{feas}(u|s_t) \in [0,1]
\]

and an action is retained only if

\[
\widehat{P}_{feas}(u|s_t) \ge \tau
\]

[PAGE 15]. In the implementation, \(\tau=0.6\) [PAGE 18]. The source also defines a timing tolerance window \(\Delta t = \pm 0.3\ \text{s}\) and an offside uncertainty margin \(\delta_{off}=0.2\ \text{m}\) [PAGE 18]. A formal feasibility decomposition can be written as

\[
\widehat{P}_{feas}(u|s_t) = \sigma\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 M_{time} + \beta_2 M_{vis} - \beta_3 M_{press}\right)
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\), \(M_{time}\) is a timing-match score, \(M_{vis}\) is corridor visibility, and \(M_{press}\) is closing-pressure intensity. The source says the feasibility score “integrates timing, visibility, and local pressure” and that these components are “combined into a single logistic function” [PAGE 15]. Units: \(M_{time}\), \(M_{vis}\), and \(M_{press}\) may be dimensionless normalized scores or physically derived quantities with units of seconds, meters, or meters per second before normalization.

A physically grounded timing operator can be expressed as

\[
t_{arr} = \frac{d_{pass}}{v_{ball}}
\]

where \(d_{pass}\) is pass distance in meters and \(v_{ball}\) is ball speed in meters per second. If the receiver’s usable window has duration \(w\) seconds, timing consistency requires

\[
|t_{arr} - t_{window}| \le \Delta t.
\]

With \(\Delta t=0.3\ \text{s}\) [PAGE 18], a pass whose arrival error exceeds this tolerance should be downweighted or rejected. Breakdown occurs when \(v_{ball}\to 0\), because \(t_{arr}\to\infty\), or when \(d_{pass}\) is too large relative to the receiver’s reachable region.

Pressure closing can be formalized as

\[
t_{press} = \frac{r_{press}}{v_{close}}
\]

where \(r_{press}\) is the distance from the nearest defender or interception point in meters, and \(v_{close}\) is closing speed in meters per second. If

\[
t_{press} < t_{arr} + t_{control}
\]

then the action is under time pressure and should receive a feasibility penalty, where \(t_{control}\) is the receiver’s estimated first-touch control time in seconds. This is consistent with the source’s statement that pressure “quantifies how quickly opponents can close down the receiver or the ball carrier” [PAGE 15].

The surrogate reward operator is

\[
\tilde{r}_t = \Delta \widehat{EPV}_t - \lambda_{opp}\widehat{xG}_{against,t} - \lambda_{exe}(1-\widehat{P}_{feas,t})
\]

[PAGE 16]. Units: reward is expressed in expected-value units minus expected-goal risk units minus a dimensionless infeasibility penalty weighted by \(\lambda_{exe}\). The term \((1-\widehat{P}_{feas,t})\) acts as a feasibility deficit. When \(\widehat{P}_{feas,t}=1\), the deficit is zero; when \(\widehat{P}_{feas,t}=0\), the deficit is maximal.

The risk-sensitive actor objective is

\[
\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}\left[CVaR_{\alpha}(A^{\pi}(s,a))\right] - \lambda_{KL} KL(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s)\,\|\,\mu(\cdot|s))
\]

[PAGE 17]. Here \(CVaR_{\alpha}\) is the conditional value-at-risk at level \(\alpha\), \(A^{\pi}(s,a)\) is the advantage in reward units, \(\mu\) is the historical behavior policy, and \(KL\) is measured in nats if natural logarithms are used. The reported CVaR level is \(\alpha=0.2\) and the KL coefficient is \(\lambda_{KL}=0.1\) [PAGE 18]. This objective penalizes tail-risk advantage and divergence from observed behavior. A breakdown regime occurs if the historical policy \(\mu\) has near-zero probability on a high-value action: the KL penalty may suppress that action even when it is genuinely optimal in the held-out match.

Calibration uses temperature scaling for value and risk outputs:

\[
f_{cal} = \frac{f_{\theta}}{T}
\]

[PAGE 17], with \(T>0\). For probability-like outputs, temperature scaling changes sharpness. If \(T\to 0^+\), outputs saturate toward 0 or 1; if \(T\to\infty\), outputs flatten toward uniform uncertainty. For feasibility, isotonic regression is applied:

\[
\widehat{P}_{feas}^{cal} = \mathcal{I}(\widehat{P}_{feas})
\]

where \(\mathcal{I}\) is a monotonic piecewise-constant function [PAGE 17]. The source emphasizes that “calibration parameters are learned exclusively on the training and validation data within each LOMO fold, and the held-out match is never used during calibration” [PAGE 17]. This prevents leakage but leaves open the question of conditional calibration under match-style shift.

The offside constraint can be written as a rule-based veto. Let \(x_{rec}\) be the receiver’s longitudinal coordinate, \(x_{ball}\) the ball coordinate, and \(x_{second}\) the second-last defender’s coordinate. A simplified offside-danger condition is

\[
x_{rec} > x_{second} + \delta_{off}
\]

when the receiver is in the attacking half and ahead of the ball, with \(\delta_{off}=0.2\ \text{m}\) as the uncertainty margin [PAGE 18]. If the model’s recommended pass places the receiver within \(\delta_{off}\) of the offside line, the action should be vetoed or heavily penalized. The source acknowledges this boundary issue: “GIRL-GNN sometimes generates borderline through-balls positioned too close to the offside line” [PAGE 43].

The overall offline-learning constraint is also important. Trajectories are constructed as

\[
\mathcal{T} = \{(s_t,a_t,r_t)\}_{t=1}^{T}
\]

[PAGE 16], and mini-batches are sampled only from the same match within each training step [PAGE 16]. This prevents temporal leakage but also means the critic cannot learn cross-match transition dynamics in a single batch. The source’s match-level buffer design is therefore conservative, but it may slow adaptation to new tactical contexts.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of GIRL-GNN is a convergence of four historical streams: event-data football analytics, tracking-data spatiotemporal modeling, graph representation learning, and offline reinforcement learning. The source situates itself against earlier work that relied on “aggregated indicators such as goals or shots on target” [PAGE 8], which tended to “exaggerate the contribution of forwards while underestimating defenders or midfielders who drive buildup play” [PAGE 8]. This critique is historically important: football analytics evolved from discrete event logs to continuous tracking because the former under-sampled off-ball coordination, pressing structure, and spatial control.

The graph lineage is explicit. The source cites GoalNet as using “event-time graphs to trace player interactions and apply graph neural networks (GNNs) to measure how each action changes the expected-threat value (xT)” [PAGE 8]. It also cites TacticAI as representing players as nodes and using graph deep learning for set-piece structures [PAGE 8]. GIRL-GNN extends this lineage by moving from localized prediction toward “sequential decision optimization” [PAGE 5]. The retro-causal genealogical claim is that the paper is not inventing graphs from scratch; it is recombining graph-based tactical representation with executable control.

The reinforcement-learning lineage is equally important. The source notes that “Reinforcement learning has similarly been explored using historical match data, including offline RL formulations for in-game outcome-related prediction” [PAGE 5]. It also cites conservative Q-learning as a way to “mitigate distributional shift and value overestimation” [PAGE 6]. GIRL-GNN adopts a related logic through a “conservative double-Q objective” and a KL constraint [PAGE 16]. This places the paper inside the offline-RL tradition where the central danger is not underfitting but overestimating unobserved actions.

The calibration lineage comes from machine learning reliability research. The source cites temperature scaling and post-hoc calibration [PAGE 6], and applies temperature scaling to value/risk outputs and isotonic regression to feasibility probabilities [PAGE 17]. The genealogical trap here is to treat calibration as a final cosmetic step. The paper avoids that by making calibration part of the LOMO pipeline and by keeping the held-out match untouched during calibration [PAGE 17]. Nevertheless, calibration does not solve causal misidentification. If the model learns a spurious correlation between a tactical pattern and reward because of team-specific style, calibration can still be locally reliable while being tactically misaligned.

A multi-week trap in this lineage is temporal leakage. The source explicitly guards against it by saying “trajectories are never truncated across possessions or matches, replay buffers are constructed at the match level, and mini-batches are sampled only from the same match within each training step” [PAGE 16]. This is a strong safeguard, but it also means the model may underweight slow strategic adaptations that occur across matches. In professional football, tactical trends can evolve over weeks: a team may shift from wide overloads to central compactness, or from high pressing to mid-block containment. A match-buffered offline learner may not capture those slow drifts unless the evaluation explicitly includes them.

Another genealogical trap is style confounding. The source’s own exploratory analysis shows that “J03WMX features a greater number of long passes, consistent with a more direct attacking approach” while “J03WOH shows medium-range patient circulation” [PAGE 23]. If one match dominates training, the model may infer that long passes are inherently valuable or that patient circulation is inherently safe, when the true causal factor is opponent structure, game state, or personnel. The LOMO protocol reduces this risk by holding out entire matches, but with only seven matches, the residual confounding remains substantial.

A third retro-causal trap is metric genealogy. The source uses expected possession value, final-third progression, box entry, xG per shot, opponent xG per 90, feasibility, cohesion, robustness, and learning efficiency [PAGE 28]. These are sensible football metrics, but they inherit historical biases. For example, box entry may overvalue territorial penetration that does not produce high-quality shots; xG per shot may undervalue buildup actions that create defensive collapse; opponent xG per 90 may be unstable over small samples. The paper mitigates this by combining multiple metrics, but the genealogical dependency remains: the framework’s notion of tactical value is still shaped by historically available indicators.

The most important retro-causal insight is that GIRL-GNN is a reaction to the historical separation between “what should be done” and “what can be executed.” Earlier systems often produced idealized recommendations. The source criticizes prior models for generating “idealized decisions that overlook feasibility factors such as visibility, defensive pressure, and offside margins” [PAGE 4]. GIRL-GNN’s genealogical novelty is to fuse tactical intent with executable constraints. Its risk is that it may become too conservative: by encoding feasibility too strongly, it may reproduce safe historical behavior and underweight rare but decisive actions.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how the model represents opponents, coaches, analysts, and itself under crisis conditions. The source includes opponent pressure in edges and features: “Defensive edges capture the pressure exerted by defenders and their closing speed” [PAGE 14]. This implies at least a first-order theory of mind: the model estimates defender proximity and closing speed. The ablation supports this: “When opponent modeling is omitted (-Opp), the decline is sharper, especially in defensive metrics, feasibility, and team cohesion” [PAGE 33]. Without opponent modeling, the model recommends passes that “arrive late or are cut off” [PAGE 33]. That is a clear theory-of-mind failure: the model fails to anticipate defender interception.

A second-order theory-of-mind layer appears in game-state adaptation. The context vector includes score differential and time remaining [PAGE 13], and the model changes risk behavior by game state. The source reports: “When leading, the model recommends fewer high-risk through balls (18%) than the sequential baseline (24%), favoring possession retention and lateral buildup. When trailing, the share rises to 36% compared with 32% for the baseline” [PAGE 39]. This is not merely reactive; it reflects an implicit model of coaching incentives. A leading team is assumed to value risk reduction; a trailing team is assumed to value vertical progression. The model internalizes a simplified coach’s utility function.

The crisis-habit regression problem is whether the model regresses to habitual safe actions when uncertainty rises. The source’s feasibility threshold encourages this: under dense pressure, the decoder “prioritizes safer alternatives such as short passes or weak-side switches” [PAGE 16]. This is adaptive in many contexts, but in a late-game crisis, excessive safety can be tactically inadequate. If a team is trailing and time is low, the expected value of variance may increase. A purely risk-averse policy may then underperform. The CVaR objective and KL regularization amplify this tendency by penalizing tail-risk and deviation from historical behavior [PAGE 17]. Thus the model may exhibit crisis-habit regression: under pressure, it defaults to historically safe patterns even when the game state demands higher variance.

The counter-trap is the model’s context-aware adjustment. The source says the model “adjusts its risk preferences in line with game context” [PAGE 39]. This suggests a partial correction: when trailing, the model increases high-risk through balls from 18% to 36% [PAGE 39]. That is evidence of a counter-regression mechanism. However, the increase is modest relative to the baseline, and the sample is small. A stronger counter-trap would include explicit time-dependent risk budgeting: as remaining time decreases and score deficit increases, the feasibility threshold could be relaxed for high-value actions, or the CVaR penalty could be adjusted to allow controlled variance.

The clock layer is crucial. Football decisions are not stationary; the value of a possession depends on remaining time, score state, and substitution state. The source includes time remaining in the context vector [PAGE 13], but the equations do not show an explicit time-decay operator. A formal clock-aware value operator could be written as

\[
V_{\pi}(s_t) = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{T-t} \gamma^k r_{t+k} \mid s_t\right]
\]

where \(\gamma=0.99\) is the discount factor [PAGE 18]. This discount is mild, so long-horizon value remains important. However, in late-game crisis, the effective horizon should shrink. If the remaining time is \(T-t\), then the value of a slow buildup sequence may be overestimated if the model does not sufficiently penalize clock consumption. The source’s case study acknowledges this: during rapid counterattacks, the model “occasionally prioritizes multi-step progressions that exceed the available execution window” [PAGE 44]. That is a clock-related breakdown.

The recursive analyst layer is also relevant. The source evaluates agreement with two analysts and reports “analysts preferred the model’s recommendation in approximately 61% of the evaluated sequences” with “F1 score of 0.77 and Cohen’s κ of 0.51” [PAGE 38–39]. This is moderate agreement, not full alignment. The model is therefore not a perfect coach surrogate; it is a decision-support agent whose recommendations must be audited. A recursive theory-of-mind design would explicitly model analyst disagreement in ambiguous states. The source notes that disagreements occur in “borderline offside decisions or rapid counterattacks” [PAGE 39]. In those regimes, the model should increase uncertainty disclosure rather than issue a confident recommendation.

In summary, the recursive layer shows three competing tendencies: first, the model has enough opponent and context modeling to avoid naïve habit regression; second, its risk-sensitive objective and feasibility threshold bias it toward safe habits; third, its clock awareness is present but not fully formalized, creating breakdowns in fast transitions. The optimal adjudication is that GIRL-GNN is best viewed as a risk-aware analyst assistant, not as an autonomous crisis commander.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto test asks whether a tactically attractive recommendation must be rejected because of physical, biomechanical, temporal, or environmental impossibility. The source already includes a feasibility veto: an action is retained only if \(\widehat{P}_{feas}(u|s_t)\ge \tau\) [PAGE 15]. However, a deeper veto requires explicit physical inequalities.

First, biomechanical reachability veto. Let \(d_{reach}\) be the distance the receiver must cover to reach the pass target, and let \(v_{max}\) be the receiver’s maximum sprint speed in meters per second. If the available time is \(t_{avail}\), then the maximum reachable distance is approximately

\[
d_{max} = v_{max} t_{avail}.
\]

If

\[
d_{reach} > d_{max},
\]

the action should be vetoed. A more realistic model includes acceleration:

\[
d_{reach} \le v_0 t_{avail} + \frac{1}{2}a_{max}t_{avail}^2
\]

where \(v_0\) is initial receiver speed and \(a_{max}\) is maximum acceleration in meters per second squared. If this inequality fails, the pass is physically unreachable, even if it has high tactical value. The source’s timing tolerance \(\Delta t=\pm0.3\ \text{s}\) [PAGE 18] is a coarse proxy for this constraint, but it should be supplemented by biomechanical reachability.

Second, ball-flight feasibility veto. For a ground pass with initial speed \(v_b\) and rolling deceleration \(a_{roll}\), the stopping distance is approximately

\[
d_{stop} = \frac{v_b^2}{2a_{roll}}.
\]

If the intended receiver distance \(d_{pass}\) satisfies

\[
d_{pass} > d_{stop},
\]

the ball will not reach the target under the assumed deceleration, and the action should be vetoed or reclassified as an under-hit pass. For a lofted pass, vertical motion adds a flight time

\[
t_{flight} = \frac{2v_{z0}}{g}
\]

where \(v_{z0}\) is initial vertical velocity and \(g\approx9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\). The horizontal range is approximately

\[
R = v_{x0}t_{flight}.
\]

If \(d_{pass} > R\), the pass is out of range. These equations are not in the source in full physical detail, but they operationalize the source’s statement that feasibility integrates “timing, visibility, and local pressure” [PAGE 15].

Third, visibility veto. Let \(C\) be the passing corridor, modeled as a rectangle of width \(w_{corr}\) in meters. If a defender’s interception radius intersects the corridor, visibility is compromised. Let \(d_{def-corr}\) be the minimum distance from defender body center to corridor centerline. If

\[
d_{def-corr} < \frac{w_{corr}}{2} + r_{intercept}
\]

where \(r_{intercept}\) is the defender’s effective interception radius in meters, then the corridor is blocked. The source says visibility “assesses how much of the passing corridor is unobstructed by defenders” [PAGE 15]. A formal veto should reject actions where the corridor is geometrically blocked, not merely where the learned feasibility score is low.

Fourth, offside veto. The source includes an offside margin \(\delta_{off}=0.2\ \text{m}\) to account for tracking noise [PAGE 18]. Let \(\Delta_{off,true}\) be the true distance between the receiver and the second-last defender at the moment of the pass. If

\[
\Delta_{off,true} < \delta_{off},
\]

the action is too close to the offside boundary and should be vetoed under uncertainty. The source’s failure case is exactly this: “borderline through-balls positioned too close to the offside line” [PAGE 43]. A robust veto would require either a larger margin or an explicit uncertainty distribution over tracking positions.

Fifth, weather and surface veto. The source does not model weather, but a cross-layer adjudicator must consider it because ball motion and player traction depend on environmental conditions. For wind, lateral displacement over flight time \(t\) can be approximated as

\[
\Delta y_{wind} \approx \frac{1}{2}a_{wind}t^2
\]

where \(a_{wind}\) is lateral wind-induced acceleration in meters per second squared. If

\[
\Delta y_{wind} > \frac{w_{corr}}{2},
\]

the pass corridor is materially distorted. For wet surfaces, rolling deceleration \(a_{roll}\) may decrease, increasing pass distance; for heavy rain, ball control time \(t_{control}\) may increase. These are not included in the source, so any deployment in adverse weather would require an external veto layer.

Sixth, tracking-noise singularity. The source adds noise robustness tests and reports that GIRL-GNN retains over 94% performance under maximum noise [PAGE 29]. However, near boundaries, small noise can flip decisions. Let \(\epsilon_{pos}\) be positional error in meters. If the true feasibility margin is \(m_{feas}\), then a noisy observation can cause a false acceptance when

\[
m_{feas} < \epsilon_{pos}.
\]

This is a singularity because the model’s input graph may place a receiver onside when the true position is offside, or may place a defender outside the corridor when the defender is actually inside it. The source’s \(\delta_{off}=0.2\ \text{m}\) is one mitigation, but it is not a full probabilistic uncertainty model.

The adjudication rule is therefore hierarchical:

1. Hard rule veto: offside, out-of-bounds, impossible body control.
2. Physical reachability veto: receiver cannot reach, ball cannot reach.
3. Pressure-timing veto: defender arrival precedes control.
4. Statistical feasibility veto: learned feasibility below threshold.
5. Tactical value ranking: only after all vetoes pass.

This hierarchy prevents the model from recommending a high-value action that is physically impossible. The source’s architecture is compatible with this hierarchy because it already separates feasibility from value and risk [PAGE 14–16], but the physical inequalities should be made explicit in any operational deployment.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three RLVR counterfactual vetoes are designed to test whether the framework’s learned policy respects verifiable physical and tactical constraints. Each scenario includes a formal trigger, a model prediction, and an empirical verification condition.

Veto 1: Offside-margin veto in transition.
Trigger: A through-ball is recommended during a fast transition. The receiver’s true offside margin \(\Delta_{off,true}\) is less than the tracking uncertainty margin \(\delta_{off}=0.2\ \text{m}\) [PAGE 18]. Formal condition:

\[
\Delta_{off,true} < \delta_{off}.
\]

Model prediction: A properly feasibility-aware GIRL-GNN should either veto the through-ball, downgrade it to a lower-risk retention action, or select a different receiver with a safer margin. If the model ignores the margin, it will generate the failure mode described in the source: “borderline through-balls positioned too close to the offside line” [PAGE 43].

Verification: Inspect the model’s candidate action list and feasibility head. For each recommended through-ball, record \(\widehat{P}_{feas}\), offside flag, and estimated margin. Success criterion: no recommendation is issued when the estimated margin is below \(\delta_{off}\) and the offside flag is uncertain. Failure criterion: the model recommends a through-ball with \(\widehat{P}_{feas}\ge 0.6\) despite \(\Delta_{off,true}<\delta_{off}\). A strong test would compare the model with and without the offside margin; removal of the margin should increase borderline offside recommendations.

Veto 2: Dense-pressure timing veto.
Trigger: A line-breaking pass is recommended while a defender can arrive before the receiver controls the ball. Let \(r_{press}\) be defender distance to the intended control point, \(v_{close}\) defender closing speed, \(t_{arr}\) pass arrival time, and \(t_{control}\) receiver control time. Formal condition:

\[
\frac{r_{press}}{v_{close}} < t_{arr} + t_{control}.
\]

Model prediction: The model should veto the line-breaking pass and prefer a short safe pass, retention, or weak-side switch. The source supports this behavior: under dense pressure, the decoder “prioritizes safer alternatives such as short passes or weak-side switches” [PAGE 16]. The ablation also shows that removing opponent modeling sharply reduces feasibility and defensive performance [PAGE 33].

Verification: For each candidate pass, compute \(t_{press}=r_{press}/v_{close}\) and \(t_{arr}=d_{pass}/v_{ball}\). If \(t_{press}<t_{arr}+t_{control}\), the action should be masked or heavily penalized. Success criterion: the selected action has \(t_{press}\ge t_{arr}+t_{control}\) or has a significantly higher feasibility score than the vetoed line-breaking option. Failure criterion: the model selects a pass with \(t_{press}<t_{arr}+t_{control}\) while a safer feasible action exists. This veto tests whether the learned policy respects temporal pressure rather than merely rewarding territorial progression.

Veto 3: Counterattack clock-window veto.
Trigger: The model recommends a multi-step combination during a rapid counterattack, but the required execution time exceeds the available transition window. Let \(n_{steps}\) be the number of required passes, \(\bar{t}_{step}\) the mean pass-and-control time per step, and \(W_{trans}\) the available transition window in seconds. Formal condition:

\[
n_{steps}\bar{t}_{step} > W_{trans}.
\]

Model prediction: The model should choose a direct corridor pass or a simpler progression rather than an over-structured combination. The source explicitly identifies this failure mode: during rapid counterattacks, the model “occasionally prioritizes multi-step progressions that exceed the available execution window,” and a direct corridor pass is preferable [PAGE 44].

Verification: For each counterattack sequence, estimate \(W_{trans}\) from defender recovery speed and pitch geography. Estimate \(\bar{t}_{step}\) from historical pass-control times. If \(n_{steps}\bar{t}_{step}>W_{trans}\), the multi-step action should be vetoed. Success criterion: the selected action has estimated execution time less than or equal to \(W_{trans}\), or has higher calibrated value after clock adjustment. Failure criterion: the model selects a multi-step sequence whose estimated execution time exceeds \(W_{trans}\) while a direct option is feasible. This veto tests whether the policy internalizes clock pressure rather than treating each possession as having unlimited temporal depth.

These three vetoes are not merely statistical performance checks; they are causal sanity tests. They verify whether the framework’s feasibility module and risk-sensitive objective actually constrain the policy in the regimes where professional football decisions are most brittle: offside boundaries, dense pressing, and fast transitions. If GIRL-GNN passes these vetoes, its claim to produce “physically plausible and tactically coherent” actions [PAGE 5] is strengthened. If it fails them, the framework should be interpreted as a promising analyst-support tool rather than as a reliable executor of high-risk tactical recommendations.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
