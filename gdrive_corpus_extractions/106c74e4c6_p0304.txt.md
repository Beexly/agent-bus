Knight’s analysis emerged from the Chicago School’s critique of classical economics, responding to early 20th-century industrial consolidation. Pre-corporate eras featured direct owner-managers (e.g., family firms) where risk-bearing and control were unified. The rise of joint-stock companies post-1850s decoupled capital from management, enabling projects too large for individual wealth but creating the agency problems Knight described. The 1929 crash exemplified this: salaried executives pursued growth despite unsustainable leverage, shielded by limited liability.

The trap lies in mistaking statistical regularity (risk) for fundamental stability, while the hierarchical structure punishes deviation from plan.

This mirrors complexity theory: hierarchical systems are efficient in predictable environments but fail catastrophically under novel perturbations (e.g., neural networks vs. biological organisms). Knight’s "judgment of men" aligns with Herbert Simon’s bounded rationality—decision-making is satisficing, not optimizing, especially under uncertainty.
