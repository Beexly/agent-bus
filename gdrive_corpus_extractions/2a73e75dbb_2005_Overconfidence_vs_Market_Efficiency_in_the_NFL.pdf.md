The source’s inference chain is multi-hop and must be audited as such. First, it defines a rational-expectations null for draft-pick value: for picks i and i+k, the ratio of market values should equal the ratio of expected surplus values, as in Equation (1) on [PAGE 11]. The alternative is Equation (2): the market-value ratio declines more steeply than the surplus-value ratio, i.e., teams overpay for the right to choose early. The first hop is market-value inference from trades. The paper assumes picks exchanged in a trade are equal in value and models pick value with a Weibull function, with the first pick as numeraire. The second hop is compensation: early picks are expensive both in foregone picks and in salary. The third hop is on-field performance: performance must decline steeply enough to justify the steep market curve. The fourth hop is surplus value: estimate the market value of performance from sixth-year veteran compensation, subtract rookie compensation cost, and regress surplus on a linear spline of draft order. The fifth hop is the economic interpretation: if first-round surplus slopes are positive, then the first pick is not the most valuable pick but the least valuable in surplus terms. The paper states this sharply: “the first pick in the first round is the worst pick in the round, not the best!” [PAGE 39]. It also says: “the surplus value of the picks during the first round actually increases throughout the round” [PAGE 6], and: “The first pick is valued as much as the 10th and 11th picks combined, and as much as the sum of the last four picks in the first round.” [PAGE 6].

But the gray-zone audit begins here. The second major gray zone is measurement of performance value. The scientific lineage is explicit. The paper draws on Kahneman and Tversky’s work on non-regressive prediction [PAGE 6–7], Lichtenstein, Fischhoff, and Phillips on overconfident confidence intervals [PAGE 7], Oskamp on confidence rising with information while accuracy stays flat [PAGE 8], Capen, Clapp, and Campbell on the winner’s curse [PAGE 8], Harrison and March on expectation inflation [PAGE 9], Ross, Greene, and House on false consensus [PAGE 10], Thaler on the winner’s curse [PAGE 8], and De Bondt and Thaler on mean reversion in extreme stock performers [PAGE 11]. Later lineage includes Camerer and Weber and Staw and Hoang on NBA draft-order effects on playing time [PAGE 25], and Hall and Limm on the NFL salary cap [PAGE 19]. The historical arc is from laboratory judgment biases to organizational markets with high stakes, then to sports labor markets as a test bed where performance is unusually observable. The paper’s own historical claim is that this should be a conservative test: football teams can watch college performance, administer drills, and grade professional performance on film [PAGE 40–41]. If bias persists there, it is not because the domain is opaque.

The retrocausal trap is that the draft-pick value curve has become an institution. The paper notes that the curve is widely used in the NFL and that “This historical consensus may lend the considerable power of inertia and precedent to the over-valuation we suggest has psychological roots.” [PAGE 18]. That is a multi-week and multi-season trap: once a convention is shared, a team that owns a top pick may resist trading down at less than the conventional price, even if its own analysis says the price is too high. The paper says a team “may be reluctant to make a trade at less than ‘full value’” [PAGE 40–41]. The trap is reinforced by the absence of short selling: “There is no way to sell the early picks short” [PAGE 5]. A smart team cannot arbitrage the mispricing directly; it can only decline to participate, trade down when possible, or wait for others to learn.

A second multi-week trap is information accumulation. In the months before the draft, teams collect more drills, interviews, and scouting reports; Oskamp’s pattern implies that confidence can rise faster than accuracy [PAGE 8]. The paper applies this directly: “The more information teams acquire about players, the more overconfident they will feel about their ability to make fine distinctions.” [PAGE 39]. A third trap is learning failure: “In the absence of systematic data collection, learning will be inhibited by bad memories and hindsight bias.” [PAGE 39]. The multi-week cycle therefore does not automatically correct the bias; it can intensify it through information overload, institutional precedent, and selective memory. The counter-lineage is the Moneyball-style critique of inefficient talent markets [PAGE 11–12], but the paper’s version is more specific: the inefficiency is not merely a bad ranking of players; it is a bad price for the right to choose early.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive structure has three levels. At level 0, a team forms its own player valuation from college evidence. At level 1, it forms a belief about how other teams value the same player; false consensus predicts that it overestimates the overlap between its own ranking and others’ rankings. The paper states that false consensus means “teams will overestimate the extent to which other teams covet the same player” [PAGE 10]. At level 2, the team forms a belief about the market’s draft-pick value curve and about what a trade partner will accept. At level 3, it anticipates that other teams know it wants a particular player and may demand a premium. The draft-day clock compresses all three levels into minutes. The source gives the concrete case: “during the 15 minutes in which they had to make their selection” the Giants considered trading up for Eli Manning or moving down for Ben Roethlisberger [PAGE 4]. That is the crisis clock. Under that clock, the habitual response is to rely on the team’s private ranking, to treat its own scouting information as more diagnostic than it is, and to overpay for the right to choose the specific player. This is the crisis-habit regression: the organization reverts to non-regressive prediction and overconfidence when time is scarce.

The counter-trap is a disciplined decision rule: regress player forecasts toward prior distributions, compare the team’s board with the likely draft order, and treat trade-up premiums as prices to be tested rather than facts to be accepted. The paper’s psychological review implies this rule: “to be regressive is to admit to a limited ability to differentiate the good from the great” [PAGE 7], and that admission is exactly what scouts and general managers are professionally rewarded for avoiding. The counter-trap also includes systematic data collection on players the team did not draft, because the league allows employers to observe the performance of candidates they did not hire [PAGE 10]. But the paper argues that organizations rarely do the time-intensive analysis needed [PAGE 39]. So the recursive theory of mind predicts a split: teams may say they are regressive and market-aware, but under the draft clock their behavior reveals a higher implicit valuation of the right to choose. The observable prediction is that trade-up premiums persist even when the same teams have access to the historical evidence that late-first-round picks produce more surplus. The counter-trap is not a personality trait; it is an institutional commitment to a slower clock, with pre-committed trade-down rules and independent valuation review. The clock is both literal, the 15-minute selection window, and seasonal, the annual draft cycle that lets hindsight bias rewrite the memory of past picks.

This recursive account also explains why the bias is not arbitraged away. The team trading up must believe that its level-1 estimate of the player is better than the market’s; otherwise it would not pay the premium. The team trading down must believe that the market price is high enough to compensate for losing the specific player. Both can be internally rational at the individual level while producing an aggregate curve that is too steep. The winner’s curse adds another recursion: the winning bidder is likely to be the one who most overestimated the common value [PAGE 8]. The false-consensus effect adds another: the team overestimates how much others share its ranking, so it overestimates the risk of waiting. The result is a recursive trap in which each team’s best response to its beliefs reproduces the market pattern that the paper identifies as inconsistent with rational expectations.
