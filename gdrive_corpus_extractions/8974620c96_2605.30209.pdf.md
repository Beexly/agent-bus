The source paper is not a model for forecasting match outcomes; it is a model for forecasting normal betting-stake dynamics and then testing whether observed stakes are extreme relative to that forecast. The derivation therefore begins with a high-frequency observation process, compresses it into one-minute market-state summaries, embeds those summaries in a latent state-space model, and finally converts model-implied predictive distributions into tail-based anomaly scores. The paper states this aim directly: it uses “high-frequency in-play (live) betting data” [PAGE 1] and says that “A state-space modelling framework is employed to describe standard betting market dynamics” [PAGE 2]. The inferential chain is: raw second-by-second stakes → one-minute aggregation → team-perspective mirrored series → hurdle model for zero versus positive stakes → log-transformed positive stakes → Student-t observation distribution → latent market-activity state → covariate-adjusted predictive mean → one-step-ahead predictive quantile → pseudo-residual flag.

A first gray-zone audit concerns the unit of observation. The original data are recorded at “a very high resolution of 1 Hz.” [PAGE 8], but the authors aggregate to one-minute intervals to reduce volatility and make modelling tractable. This aggregation changes the causal granularity of manipulation detection. A fixer or an anomalous cohort could act within seconds, while the model sees only minute-level volume. The authors also “exclude observations from the injury time after the second half.” [PAGE 8], which removes the most unstable terminal segment of a match. That choice improves numerical stability but creates a blind spot exactly where late-match information and pressure may be strongest.

A second gray zone is the treatment of zeros and closed markets. The model is a hurdle model: zero stake probability is modelled separately, and positive stakes are modelled on the log scale. The paper says, “Given the strong skewness of the stakes, we consider the logarithm of these stakes” [PAGE 12]. This is statistically sensible because stake volume is highly right-skewed. But the anomaly logic depends heavily on the zero component being adequately calibrated. If the model underestimates zero-stake probability in decided matches, then a sudden positive stake can look more anomalous than it truly is. Conversely, if the model overfits the zero process, it can suppress genuine positive-volume anomalies.

A third gray zone is the latent state. The latent process is intended to capture unobservable “market activity level” or excitement. In the baseline model, the estimated persistence is extremely high, and in the richer model it remains near one. This means that betting activity is strongly serially correlated: a high-activity state can persist across many minutes even without any single covariate shock. The model therefore tries to separate ordinary persistence from unexplained positive shocks. The key anomaly statistic is not raw stake size; it is the standardized one-step-ahead predictive residual. If a stake is huge but was predictable given team sentiment, pre-match probability, score difference, red cards, expected-goal difference, minute, halftime, or latent persistence, it will not be flagged. If it is huge after conditioning on all those factors, it becomes a tail outlier.

A fourth gray zone is the absence of ground truth. The authors acknowledge that there are no confirmed fixed matches in their sample, so the analysis cannot validate manipulation directly. The paper’s own conclusion is careful: flagged observations are anomalies, not proven fixes. This is crucial. The model identifies “observations in which realised stakes exceed a given threshold – effectively a quantile – in the model’s predictive distribution” [PAGE 23], but a predictive quantile violation is not a forensic proof. It is a probabilistic alarm.

A fifth gray zone is multiple testing. The dataset is large, with 235,882 mirrored observations. Even a well-calibrated model will produce some extreme residuals by chance. The authors focus on the largest positive pseudo-residuals and inspect the top 20 in detail. The fact that many top observations share a surface pattern—away team, early match, low pre-match probability, often before or during halftime—could reflect either manipulation, herding, sentiment-driven overreaction, or model misspecification. The paper quotes this pattern: “Seventeen out of 20 bets are on away teams, and all are placed before or during halftime at a score of either 0-0 or 1-0.” [PAGE 27] This is an important gray-zone result: the pattern is suspicious enough to warrant inspection, but not sufficient to establish fraud.

The deepest multi-hop question is whether the flagged observations are evidence of manipulation or merely evidence of unobserved market heterogeneity. The model conditions on many known drivers, but cannot condition on everything: sudden injury news, inside information, coordinated liquidity, referee behavior, fatigue, weather, schedule congestion, or team-specific fan sentiment. The paper’s state-space framework is strong for describing normal dependence, but weak for proving intent. Therefore, the anomaly detector should be read as a monitoring operator, not a verdict machine. It flags tail deviations; investigators must then apply domain knowledge, physical match context, and temporal pattern analysis.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper’s formal model is a state-space model with a hurdle observation distribution and a latent AR(1) or ARX(1) state. The observation index is team-match-minute. Let \(y_t\) denote the stake placed on a given team in a given minute, measured in scaled currency units. Because raw stakes are highly skewed, the positive part is modelled on the logarithmic scale, which is dimensionless.

1. Hurdle observation model

\[
\Pr(y_t = 0) = \pi_t,
\qquad
\Pr(y_t > 0) = 1 - \pi_t.
\]

When the market is closed, the model fixes \(\pi_t = 1\), so no positive-stake density is evaluated. For positive stakes,

\[
\log(y_t) \mid y_t > 0
=
\mu_t + \sigma \sqrt{\frac{\nu - 2}{\nu}} \, \xi_t,
\qquad
\xi_t \sim t_\nu,
\qquad \nu > 2.
\]

This is a location-scale Student-t distribution on the log-stake scale. The scaling factor ensures that the variance of \(\log(y_t) \mid y_t > 0\) is \(\sigma^2\). The parameter \(\sigma\) has units of log-stakes; \(\nu\) is dimensionless. The normal distribution is the limiting case as \(\nu \to \infty\). The paper motivates this heavy-tailed specification by noting the strong skewness of stakes and by stating that “Given the strong skewness of the stakes, we consider the logarithm of these stakes” [PAGE 12].

2. Mean log-stake predictor

\[
\mu_t = \eta_t + s_t,
\qquad
\eta_t = x_t^{(1)\prime}\beta.
\]

Here \(x_t^{(1)}\) contains covariates for the state-dependent process, such as average team stakes, pre-match probability, red cards, score difference, minute, minute squared, and halftime. The latent state \(s_t\) captures unobserved market activity. The units of \(\mu_t\) are log-stakes. The covariate coefficients \(\beta\) are dimensionless when covariates are standardized or appropriately scaled.

3. Latent state process

The basic latent process is an AR(1):

\[
s_t = \phi s_{t-1} + x_t^{(2)\prime}\omega + \sigma_s \epsilon_t,
\qquad
\epsilon_t \sim N(0,1),
\qquad
\sigma_s > 0.
\]

The persistence parameter \(\phi\) is dimensionless and is constrained away from a unit root in the formal model. The innovation scale \(\sigma_s\) is also in log-stake units. The paper’s baseline estimate of persistence is extremely high, and the richer model still shows strong persistence. This means the latent state can dominate short-run stake variation if not properly separated from covariate effects.

The richer state equation adds goal surprise, expected-goal difference, and halftime effects:

\[
s_t
=
\phi s_{t-1}
+
\sum_{w=1}^{3} \omega_w \frac{I_t^w}{\text{goal\_team}_t^{(w)}}
+
\omega_4 \, \text{xg\_diff}_t
+
\omega_5 \, \text{halftime}_t
+
\sigma_s \epsilon_t.
\]

The variables \(I_t^w\) indicate whether the most recent goal was surprising, slightly surprising, or unsurprising. The denominator measures time since the most recent goal. The expected-goal difference is dimensionless in the covariate coding, but substantively it represents a physical scoring-opportunity differential.

4. Zero-stake probability model

The probability of no stake in an open market is modelled through a logistic link:

\[
\pi_t
=
\operatorname{logit}^{-1}
\left(
\alpha_0
+
\alpha_1 \text{Gini}_t
+
\alpha_2 \text{Gini}_t^2
+
\alpha_3 \text{minute}_t
+
\alpha_4 \text{minute}_t \cdot \text{Gini}_t
+
\alpha_5 \text{minute}_t \cdot \text{Gini}_t^2
\right).
\]

The Gini variable is normalized between 0 and 1, with lower values indicating greater uncertainty about the match outcome. The interaction terms allow the effect of match certainty to change as the match progresses. This component matters because a spike is only meaningful relative to the chance of observing any positive stake at all.

5. Exact likelihood and HMM approximation

The state process is continuous and analytically intractable, so the authors use Kitagawa-style discretization. The exact likelihood is written as an integral over all possible latent state sequences:

\[
L_T(\Theta)
=
\int \cdots \int
f(s_1)
f(y_1 \mid s_1, x_1^{(1)})
\prod_{t=2}^{T}
f(s_t \mid s_{t-1}, x_t^{(2)})
f(y_t \mid s_t, x_t^{(1)})
\, ds_T \cdots ds_1.
\]

Here \(\Theta\) contains regression coefficients, variance parameters, persistence, and degrees of freedom. The likelihood is dimensionless in the sense that it is a probability density product; its logarithm is measured in natural units, or nats, if natural logarithms are used.

The state space is discretized into \(m\) intervals. The approximating likelihood becomes a high-dimensional sum over discretized states. The authors note that “The computational cost of evaluating the likelihood of the approximating HMM can therefore be dramatically reduced, from the order \(O(Tm^T)\) to the order \(O(Tm^2)\), by making use of the recursive forward algorithm” [PAGE 14]. In matrix form,

\[
L_T(\Theta)
\approx
\delta P(y_1) \Gamma P(y_2) \cdots \Gamma P(y_T) \mathbf{1},
\]

where \(\Gamma\) is the state-transition matrix, \(P(y_t)\) is diagonal with state-dependent densities, \(\delta\) is the initial distribution, and \(\mathbf{1}\) is a vector of ones. The discretization step \(h\) has state-space units; \(m\) is dimensionless. If \(m\) is too small, approximation bias enters; if \(m\) is large, computation increases.

6. One-step-ahead predictive distribution and PIT residuals

For diagnostics and outlier detection, the model computes one-step-ahead state probabilities:

\[
p_{t \mid t-1}(i)
=
\Pr(s_t = i \mid y_{1:t-1}, x_{1:t}).
\]

The predictive density for positive log-stakes is a mixture over discretized latent states:

\[
f(\log y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}, x_{1:t})
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}
p_{t \mid t-1}(i)
f(\log y_t \mid s_t = i, x_t).
\]

Let \(F_t\) be the predictive cumulative distribution function. The probability integral transform is

\[
u_t = F_t(\log y_t),
\]

and the quantile pseudo-residual is

\[
r_t = \Phi^{-1}(u_t),
\]

where \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the standard normal quantile function. The residual \(r_t\) is dimensionless. Under correct specification, the paper says the “pseudo-residuals should be approximately i.i.d. standard normally distributed” [PAGE 19]. The paper states that “We compute probability integral transform (PIT) values \(u_t = F_t(\log y_t)\)” [PAGE 20].

7. Outlier flag

The operational anomaly rule is tail-based. The paper says: “We classify an observation as potentially suspicious if the realised betting stakes exceed the 99.8% quantile of our model’s predictive distribution.” [PAGE 29] Equivalently, a flag can be defined by

\[
r_t > q_{0.998}
\]

or by

\[
y_t > Q_{0.998}(F_t).
\]

This rule focuses on unusually large positive deviations. Negative residuals are less relevant to the paper’s fraud-detection aim because unusually low stakes do not directly indicate excessive betting volume.

8. Breakdown regimes and singularity conditions

Several breakdown regimes matter.

First, if the market is closed, the model sets \(\pi_t = 1\) and does not evaluate positive-stake likelihood. The paper states that “Bookmakers close the market right after major events, such as goals and red cards, and towards the end of a match if its outcome is effectively decided.” [PAGE 8] If this closure rule is inaccurate, flagged stakes near closure may reflect market microstructure rather than manipulation.

Second, the Student-t degrees of freedom must satisfy \(\nu > 2\) for finite variance. If \(\nu\) approached 2, the variance interpretation of \(\sigma\) would collapse. The fitted values are substantially above 2, indicating heavy tails but finite variance.

Third, the latent AR(1) persistence must remain below one for stationarity. If \(\phi\) approached one, the state process would become nearly nonstationary, making one-step-ahead predictive intervals unstable. The estimated persistence is close to one, so interpretation requires caution.

Fourth, the discretized state approximation can break down if \(m\) is too small or if the state distribution is highly multimodal. The forward algorithm stabilizes computation but does not remove approximation error.

Fifth, covariate extrapolation is a risk. If a match enters a rare combination of score, red card, expected-goal difference, halftime, and minute, the model may predict poorly because training data are sparse. The paper’s careful inspection of top observations is partly a response to this risk.

Sixth, the anomaly threshold is a multiple-testing regime. With many observations, extreme residuals will occur even under a correct model. The flag is therefore an alarm, not a Bayesian posterior probability of fixing.

The paper cites Kitagawa for finely discretized state-space likelihoods and cites Zucchini, MacDonald, and Langrock for HMM inference. The computational bridge is crucial: a continuous latent state is approximated by a large discrete-state Markov chain, then evaluated using the forward algorithm. This allows the authors to estimate a model with 235,882 mirrored observations and many covariates.

The sports analytics lineage is equally important. Earlier fraud-detection work often focused on pre-match markets. The paper notes that much of the literature has focused on pre-match markets, while in-play betting is now a large share of betting activity. The authors extend prior work by using in-play stake data, not merely pre-match probabilities or final results. They draw on research showing that betting activity responds to goals, red cards, score changes, expected-goal dynamics, and halftime. The paper’s model therefore treats normal betting behavior as a dynamic process, not a static equilibrium.

This is a multi-week trap: the vulnerability is not only a single-match event but a season-long economic condition.

Multi-week traps arise because manipulation risk is often seasonal. Late-season matches with little competitive consequence can lower the moral and financial cost of corruption. Teams near relegation, promotion, or mid-table safety may have different incentives from teams fighting for titles. The paper’s flagged observations are concentrated in certain periods, including a cluster between February 2020 and April 2021. This does not prove seasonality of fixing, but it suggests that calendar context matters. A detector that ignores schedule density, relegation pressure, or squad fatigue may mistake ordinary crisis-driven betting for manipulation.

Another genealogical trap is the difference between observed stake spikes and underlying intent. The paper’s model controls for many known drivers, but historical scandals show that fixers can exploit liquid markets, low monitoring, or coordinated behavior. The authors note that detecting suspicious activity remains challenging because unusual patterns may result from ordinary behavior, such as players conserving energy in low-stakes matches. This is a recurring issue in sports integrity: the same observable can arise from fatigue, poor performance, or deliberate underperformance. Without ground truth, the model must rely on probabilistic anomalies rather than causal proof.

A further lineage issue is the evolution of fraud-detection methods. Early approaches used simple comparisons, forecasting models, Bayesian networks, rule-based reasoning, or machine learning. The paper’s contribution is more modest and more statistically formal: it builds a predictive distribution for stake volume and uses tail exceedances as anomaly evidence. This places the paper in the tradition of model-based monitoring rather than black-box classification. The strength is interpretability: each covariate has an economic or football-specific meaning. This small detail shows that exogenous interruptions can break the continuity assumptions of in-play modelling. If a match is delayed, suspended, or resumed, the minute-level market process may no longer represent normal football dynamics. The historical lineage of the model is therefore not only mathematical; it is also institutional, involving league calendars, referee assignments, club finances, and monitoring systems.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory-of-mind analysis asks how a strategic manipulator might behave if they know that a detector is watching. The paper’s detector flags large positive deviations from expected stake volume. A naive manipulator might place an unusually large stake at a dramatic moment, expecting profit from a manipulated outcome. But if integrity monitors observe the same minute-level data, such a spike becomes conspicuous. The model’s top observations are indeed concentrated in high-attention windows: early match minutes, halftime, and low-score states. The paper notes that “On average, the highest stakes are placed during the initial 10 minutes of a match.” [PAGE 9] It also finds that stakes are elevated during halftime, with “average stakes additionally elevated during the halftime break by approximately 21.8%” [PAGE 17]. These are periods where a manipulator might believe that information is incomplete or that market attention is dispersed.

Crisis habit regression is the idea that under crisis, teams or fixers may fall back on familiar behavioral patterns. A club facing relegation, a player with unpaid wages, or a referee under pressure may act in ways that are predictable from historical incentives. The paper’s structural risk quote is relevant: low-compensation leagues with liquid markets are especially prone to corruption [PAGE 7]. In a crisis, the habit may be to target matches with low external attention, especially early in the match or during halftime, when the market has not yet incorporated the full match context. The detector, however, has learned that these windows normally have high baseline activity. Therefore, a crisis-driven spike must be unusually large to be flagged.

The counter-trap is that sophisticated manipulators may avoid single-minute spikes. If they know that the model uses one-step-ahead predictive quantiles, they may distribute stakes across many minutes, use smaller amounts, or target less monitored outcomes. The paper excludes draw stakes because draws are less popular and would require coordinated cooperation from both teams. This exclusion reduces the search space but also creates a blind spot. If a manipulation scheme uses draw-adjacent behavior, or if it avoids home and away win markets, the detector may miss it.

The clock layer is central. The raw data are second-by-second, but the model uses one-minute bins. This creates a temporal aliasing problem. Two actions within the same minute are collapsed into one observation. If a manipulator places several moderate stakes within a minute, the aggregate may not exceed the predictive quantile even if the pattern is suspicious at a finer timescale. Conversely, a single large stake in a minute can create a spike that looks anomalous even if it is part of a broader normal campaign. The authors acknowledge that “our outlier-detection procedure assesses observations individually rather than across consecutive periods.” [PAGE 29] This is a direct description of the clock-layer limitation.

The clock also interacts with market closure. The paper states that bookmakers close the market after major events and near the end of a decided match [PAGE 8]. A manipulator who understands this may place stakes just before closure, when the model may have less reliable covariate information. The model’s predictive distribution becomes more uncertain near closure, and the flag threshold may be less stable. The authors’ exclusion of injury time after the second half reduces noise but also removes a period where late-match manipulation risk could be highest.

A recursive adversary may also exploit the model’s covariate structure. The model includes red cards, score difference, expected-goal difference, and pre-match probability. If a manipulator knows these covariates, they may choose situations where the model expects high stakes for legitimate reasons. For example, if the opponent receives a red card, the model expects higher stakes on the remaining team. A manipulator could hide within that expected increase. The paper finds that red cards issued to the opponent increase stakes on the team under consideration, while red cards issued to the team decrease them. A sophisticated actor could therefore create a plausible narrative for elevated stakes.

The theory-of-mind analysis also applies to investigators. If integrity analysts rely only on single-observation tail flags, they may overinterpret random spikes. If they require temporal clustering, they may miss isolated but decisive acts. The optimal monitoring policy depends on the adversary’s sophistication. For low-sophistication actors, single spikes may be informative. For high-sophistication actors, distributed or context-masked behavior may be more informative. The paper’s model is therefore best viewed as one layer in a recursive detection game, not a complete solution.

The final recursive point is that the model’s own diagnostics create beliefs about normal behavior. Once a league or bookmaker knows that early-halftime spikes are flagged, manipulators may shift to later minutes, lower amounts, or less obvious score states. The detector’s thresholds then become endogenous: they influence the behavior they are meant to measure. This is a classic performative forecasting problem. The model must be continuously updated, otherwise its notion of “normal” becomes outdated as soon as adversaries respond.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto asks whether a physical, biomechanical, or weather-based explanation can invalidate an apparent betting anomaly. The paper’s model already includes several physical football covariates: red cards, score difference, expected-goal difference, minute, halftime, and a latent activity state. If a stake spike is fully explained by these variables, the model should not flag it as strongly. However, the model’s covariates are imperfect proxies for physical match states. Therefore, a physical-layer veto is necessary when an anomaly coincides with an observable event that the model does not fully capture.

The strongest physical veto comes from market closure. The paper says that “Bookmakers close the market right after major events, such as goals and red cards, and towards the end of a match if its outcome is effectively decided.” [PAGE 8] If a flagged stake occurs immediately before or after a major event, the model may be reacting to a discontinuity rather than to manipulation. The one-minute aggregation can smear the exact event timing. A goal, red card, penalty, injury stoppage, or VAR review can change market conditions abruptly. If the flagged observation is adjacent to such an event, the anomaly may be a physical-market response rather than suspicious intent.

A biomechanical veto arises from fatigue, injury, or tactical conservation. The paper notes that unusual betting patterns or poor on-field performance may result from players conserving energy in low-stakes matches. This is important because a team that is not trying to win in a low-consequence match may generate betting patterns that look abnormal if bettors expect normal competitive effort. However, the paper’s model does not directly observe effort or fatigue. It observes stakes and football covariates. Therefore, a flag based only on stake volume cannot distinguish between a manipulated match and a low-effort but legitimate match. The physical layer can veto the inference if there is independent evidence of fatigue, squad rotation, or tactical disengagement.

A weather veto is explicit in the data construction. The authors excluded one match because it was interrupted by fog: “one further match was omitted because it was interrupted by fog and resumed on the following day” [PAGE 8]. This shows that weather can break the continuity of in-play dynamics. If a match is delayed, suspended, or played in unusual conditions, the minute-level betting process may not follow normal patterns. Rain, wind, heat, or poor pitch conditions can reduce scoring opportunities, alter tempo, and change bettor behavior. The model includes expected-goal difference, but expected-goal estimates may themselves be affected by weather. Therefore, a weather-related anomaly can veto a manipulation inference unless there is corroborating evidence.

The expected-goal layer is particularly important. The paper includes expected-goal difference as a covariate in the state process, but finds that in Serie B it does not significantly affect the latent state: “we do not find the match dynamics, measured by the difference in expected goals, to significantly affect the state of the market” [PAGE 18]. This does not mean expected goals are irrelevant; it means that, after controlling for other variables, the latent betting activity in this sample is not strongly driven by that measure. However, expected goals can still affect the physical interpretation of a flag. If a team creates many high-quality chances but has not scored, bettors may increase stakes for legitimate informational reasons. If the model underweights that physical pressure, it may produce a false anomaly.

A formal veto condition can be stated as follows. Let \(F_t\) be the model predictive distribution for positive log-stakes, and let \(A_t\) be the anomaly indicator:

\[
A_t = \mathbf{1}\{ y_t > Q_{0.998}(F_t) \}.
\]

Let \(E_t\) be a physical or environmental event set, including goals, red cards, penalties, injuries, substitutions, weather interruptions, or extreme fatigue indicators. A physical veto applies if

\[
A_t = 1
\quad \text{and} \quad
E_t \neq \varnothing
\]

and the event \(E_t\) plausibly explains the stake deviation outside the model’s covariate set. In that case, the posterior probability of manipulation should be reduced. If \(E_t = \varnothing\) and no physical explanation is available, the flag retains more evidentiary weight.

The halftime layer requires special care. The model includes halftime as a covariate and finds elevated activity during the break. But halftime is also a period of tactical instruction, injury assessment, and information uncertainty. A stake spike during halftime may reflect bettors reacting to perceived tactical changes, not manipulation. The paper’s top observations often occur before or during halftime [PAGE 27]. This is a gray zone: the temporal concentration is notable, but halftime is also a naturally high-uncertainty interval.

The final veto adjudication is that the model can identify statistically abnormal stake volume, but it cannot by itself prove that physical events did not cause the abnormality. The paper’s own careful language reflects this: the flagged observations are potentially suspicious, not confirmed manipulation. A cross-layer veto is therefore valid whenever there is a credible physical, biomechanical, or weather-based explanation for the stake spike that is not fully encoded in the model. The detector should be used to prioritize review, not to assign guilt.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three reinforcement-learning-verifier-style counterfactual vetoes can be constructed to test whether the paper’s anomaly flags should be treated as evidence of match-fixing. Each veto is falsifiable and tied to the source text.

1. Temporal clustering veto

The paper’s detector flags individual observations that exceed a predictive quantile. But sustained manipulation might produce repeated or consecutive anomalies, especially before a key event. The authors explicitly state that “our outlier-detection procedure assesses observations individually rather than across consecutive periods.” [PAGE 29]

Counterfactual test: if flagged observations are genuine manipulation signals, then the probability of temporal clustering should be higher than expected under the null model. Define a cluster as \(k\) consecutive minutes, or \(k\) minutes within a short window, where the same team-match series exceeds the 99.8% predictive quantile. Let \(C_j\) be the cluster count for match-team series \(j\). Under a manipulation hypothesis,

\[
\Pr(C_j \geq k \mid \text{fixing}) > \Pr(C_j \geq k \mid \text{normal market}).
\]

Prediction: confirmed fixing cases should show more clustered flags than normal matches. If the data show mostly isolated spikes and no significant clustering after adjusting for match importance, referee, score state, and calendar week, the manipulation interpretation is vetoed. The paper’s own result that flags are largely individual and that no clear patterns emerged weakens the manipulation inference.

2. Ground-truth precision veto

The dataset contains no confirmed fixed matches. The authors state that there are no documented fixes in the sample, so flagged observations cannot be validated against ground truth. A credible detector should show high precision when applied to seasons or leagues with confirmed fixing.

Counterfactual test: let \(G\) be the set of matches with independently confirmed manipulation. A useful detector should satisfy

\[
\Pr(\text{flag} \mid G) \gg \Pr(\text{flag} \mid \neg G).
\]

Equivalently, the precision

\[
\text{Precision}
=
\frac{\text{true flagged fixes}}{\text{all flagged observations}}
\]

should be materially above the base-rate precision obtainable by random tail selection.

Prediction: if the model is applied to earlier Serie B seasons with known fixes, or to the Turkish case, it should flag known fixed matches at a higher rate than ordinary high-stakes matches. If it does not, the anomaly detector is not sufficiently diagnostic. The paper itself acknowledges this limitation: without ground truth, the approach is a proof of concept, not a validated forensic classifier. Therefore, any claim that the flagged observations in the sample prove fixing is vetoed unless external ground truth supports it.

3. Near-future scoring causality veto

If large stakes are placed because a manipulator expects a specific team to win, then the backed team should have an elevated near-future scoring hazard after the flag, assuming the fix is designed to produce the desired outcome. The paper provides a direct empirical test: “Within three minutes after a flagged observation, the backed team scores in 2.6% of cases, while the opposing team scores in 6.2%” [PAGE 25]. The corresponding rate across all observations is 3.1% for a goal within the subsequent three minutes.

Let \(H_b(t)\) be the hazard that the backed team scores within a short horizon after a flagged stake, and let \(H_o(t)\) be the hazard that the opposing team scores. Under a simple winning-fix hypothesis,

\[
H_b(t) > H_{\text{baseline}}(t).
\]

But the observed flagged data show the opposite: the backed team scores less often than the baseline, and the opponent scores more often. This is a strong counterfactual veto against the interpretation that the flagged stakes are bets on guaranteed wins.

Prediction: if manipulation is present and aimed at ensuring the backed team wins, the scoring hazard after the flag should exceed the baseline hazard. If the hazard is lower, the anomaly is more consistent with speculative, sentimental, or unsuccessful betting than with successful fixing. The paper’s top 20 observations also show that many flagged bets did not win, and that several matches ended in draws. This further weakens the hypothesis that the flagged stakes reflect informed manipulation.

These three vetoes are not independent. Temporal clustering, ground-truth precision, and scoring causality together form a minimum validation triangle. A flag becomes more credible if it is clustered, validated against known fixes, and followed by outcome-consistent scoring. If it fails one or more vetoes, the appropriate decision is to downgrade the anomaly from “suspicious” to “unexplained but not necessarily fraudulent.” The source paper’s own evidence satisfies the anomaly-detection objective but does not satisfy the stronger forensic objective of proving match-fixing.
