**1. Formal Definitions of Construct Space:**
Let $S$ be the space of environmental stimuli and $A$ be the space of possible actions (motor outputs).
Let $\mathcal{C} = \{ c_1, ..., c_n \}$ represent the set of fundamental **Appetites**, explicitly listed as: Food-hunger ($c_F$), Sex-hunger ($c_S$), Excretion hungers ($c_E$), Specific contact-hunger ($c_{SC}$), Rest-hunger ($c_R$), Sensory-motor-hungers ($c_{SM}$).

**2. Sign-Gestalt-Readiness Operators:**
We define the **Final Sign-Gestalt-Readiness** operator, $\mathcal{G}_{final}: S \rightarrow [0,1]$, which assigns a probability score indicating whether a stimulus constellation $s \in S$ matches the innate template for a specific drive.
For an aversion (e.g., Fright), as noted on [PAGE 288], the agent has a "vague final sign-gestalt-readiness... that the given type of 'threatening' object... would lead to the given disturbance." 
Mathematically, the activation level of the aversion, $V_d(t)$, at time $t$, is driven by the mismatch between the current perceptual state and the predicted safe state:
$$ V_d(t) = \mathbb{E}_{p(s)} \left[ -\log \mathcal{G}_{final}^{(d)}(s_t) \right] + \beta \cdot D_{KL}\left( P(\text{outcome}|s_t) \parallel P(\text{safe}) \right) $$
Where $D_{KL}$ measures the divergence from safety. 

The **Subordinate Sign-Gestalt-Readiness** operator, $\mathcal{G}_{sub}: S \times Q_{internal} \rightarrow A$, dictates the immediate motor response based on the current stimulus and internal drive state $Q$. As stated on [PAGE 288]: "...provided... with some vague subordinate sign-gestalt-readinesses as to how to get away from such a 'threatening' object".
This acts as a policy function $\pi(a|s, V_d)$ that minimizes exposure to $s$:
$$ \mathcal{G}_{sub}(s, Q) = \arg\min_{a \in A} \left( \gamma \cdot V_d(s') + \text{Cost}(a) \right) $$

**3. Equilibrium, Quiescence, and Disturbance:**
The text postulates that the "goings-off... will always prove to be docile relative to the proved ultimate appearances or non-appearances of such quiescences and disturbances." We formalize the organism's internal tension state $T(t)$ as a weighted sum of active drives:
$$ T(t) = \sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} w_c \cdot H(c_t) + \sum_{d \in \mathcal{D}} w_d \cdot V_d(t) $$
where $H(c_t)$ is the magnitude of the appetite (hunger). The goal of behavior is to drive $T(t)$ toward a quiescent state $Q_0$ (where $T \approx 0$).
The rate of change of quiescence, $\dot{Q}$, is governed by the success of the subordinate readinesses in achieving consummatory acts or avoiding threats.

**4. Units, Assumptions, and Breakdown Regimes:**
*Units:* $V_d$ and $H(c)$ are measured in Nats (natural units of information/surprise). Action costs are in Joules.
*Assumptions:* The mapping $\mathcal{G}_{final}$ is static over short timeframes (innate); the environment is Markovian.
*Singularity/Breakdown Regimes:* 
The model assumes "docility"—that small changes in disturbance yield proportional behavioral adjustments. The breakdown occurs when the system encounters a **bifurcation point**. For example, if $V_{d_{Fr}}$ (Fright) exceeds a critical threshold $\theta_{panic}$, the smooth policy $\mathcal{G}_{sub}$ collapses into a stochastic, non-optimal flight response (freezing or erratic running), rendering the "correct" perception impossible. Additionally, if $\mathcal{G}_{final}$ yields a persistent false positive (an optical illusion of a predator), the subordinate readinesses engage perpetually, leading to exhaustion and system failure (death).

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage presented on [PAGE 288]—"initially and perhaps most fundamentally from McDougall... much influenced by Woodworth and Kempf"—anchors Tolman’s work within the Hormic Psychology movement, representing a significant pivot from strict Watsonian Behaviorism. 

**McDougall’s Teleodynamic Core:**
William McDougall (*Outline of Psychology*, 1923) argued vehemently against mechanistic S-R (Stimulus-Response) chains, proposing instead that all behavior is inherently goal-directed ("hormic"). He postulated that emotions are psychophysiological processes preparing the organism for action aimed at a goal. Tolman inherits this teleology but strips away the subjective language, replacing McDougall's "instincts" with objectively measurable "sign-gestalt-readinesses." McDougall's influence is evident in the categorization of appetites and aversions as primary motivators rather than mere reflexes.

**Woodworth’s S-O-R Bridge and Kempf’s Autonomy:**
Robert S. Woodworth (*Dynamic Psychology*, 1918) famously inserted the Organism (O) between Stimulus and Response, emphasizing cognitive mediation. Tolman extends this by detailing exactly *what* happens inside the O—the construction of expectancies and readinesses. Edmund J. Kempf (*The Autonomous Functions of the Personality*, 1918) contributed the notion that psychological systems operate autonomously from moment-to-moment stimuli, driven by internal organizational principles. This underpins Tolman's assertion on [PAGE 288] that the animal is "provided innately" with readineses, implying a pre-wired cognitive architecture that operates independently of immediate reinforcement history.

**The Multi-Week Trap of Rigid Taxonomies:**
While historically monumental, adopting this genealogical framework introduces a "multi-week trap" for modern researchers. By anchoring motivation to a finite, rigid list of "fundamental" appetites and aversions, subsequent research often falls into the trap of reductionist categorization. For instance, framing complex social behaviors (like empathy or Machiavellian manipulation) strictly through the narrow lens of "Pugnacity" or "Specific contact-hunger" forces rich, dynamic socio-emotional data into ill-fitting Procrustean beds. Over weeks or months of studying anomalous social interactions, researchers may find their McDougall-Tolman inspired taxonomies failing to capture emergent properties, leading to theoretical stagnation. They might endlessly argue over whether a new observed behavior is a novel "appetite" or just a complex combination of existing "subordinate sign-gestalt-readinesses," thereby obscuring the continuous, fluid reality of affective experience.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
When navigating crisis situations, the recursive application of Tolman’s sign-gestalt framework exposes deep fault lines between innate programming and learned adaptation—a dynamic we can conceptualize as Crisis Habit Regression versus Counter-Traps, all regulated by a temporal clock.

**Crisis Habit Regression via Subordinate Readinesses:**
In a high-stakes, rapidly unfolding crisis (e.g., a forest fire), the sophisticated, learned "maps" of the environment constructed by a mature organism often fail due to unprecedented variables. Under extreme stress (high $V_{d_{Fr}}$), the organism undergoes regression. It bypasses the complex, cognitively demanding navigational readineses and defaults to the most primitive, "vague subordinate sign-gestalt-readinesses" mentioned on [PAGE 288]. If an animal has been trained to navigate a maze for food, a sudden loud noise might instantly override the goal of finding food and trigger a hardwired aversion readiness—pure flight toward the nearest dark hole, ignoring the previously learned path. This is habit regression: the collapse of higher-order purposive behavior into lower-order, innate reflex arcs designed purely for survival.

**The Counter-Trap Mechanism:**
A counter-trap occurs when an environment exploits this exact regression. Consider a predator using a lure (mimicry) that perfectly triggers the prey's "Food-hunger" final sign-gestalt-readiness. The prey, expecting the consummatory act of eating, engages its approach subordinate readinesses. Simultaneously, the environment is structured to exploit the prey's lack of situational awareness during this focused approach, triggering a delayed "Fright" once the prey is within striking distance. The prey's mind was recursively hacked: the strong initial signal of an appetite blinded it to the subtle cues of an impending aversion, creating a fatal trap.

**The Clock Constraint:**
Both regression and counter-traps are constrained by an internal and external clock. 
Biologically, the clock dictates the urgency of the drives. If "Food-hunger" reaches a critical physiological threshold (starvation), the "clock" on patience runs out. The animal stops cautiously evaluating the "final sign-gestalt-readiness" of a suspiciously stationary prey (which might be a trap) and simply lunges, overriding its risk-assessment protocols. Temporally, a counter-trap must execute within the window of the target's engaged readiness. If the predator strikes too slowly, the prey's attention may shift back to scanning for threats (lowering the dominance of the food-readiness and raising the fright-readiness), allowing escape. Thus, behavioral outcomes are a race between the decay of drive-specific readinesses and the execution time of environmental traps.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Tolman's elegant cognitive models of sign-gestalt-readinesses operate predominantly at the behavioral and psychological layers. However, these higher-order constructs are subject to absolute vetoes from lower-level physical, biomechanical, and meteorological constraints.

**The Physical Layer Veto: Biomechanical Limits:**
On [PAGE 288], Tolman suggests that subordinate sign-gestalt-readinesses provide the knowledge "as to how to get away from such a 'threatening' object." This implies a theoretically available repertoire of escape actions (running, jumping, climbing). However, a biomechanical veto exists. If an organism is injured (e.g., a fractured leg), the specific motor programs encoded in the subordinate readiness for "rapid locomotion" cannot be executed. Even if the "Fright" readiness perfectly identifies the threat and the brain formulates the optimal escape vector, the musculoskeletal system issues a veto. The behavior defaults not to the intended escape, but to a localized defense mechanism (e.g., playing dead or biting), demonstrating that cognitive readiness is strictly bounded by physical capability.

**The Environmental Layer Veto: Meteorological Conditions:**
Environmental factors can veto the expression of appetites and aversions entirely. Consider "Excretion hungers" or the need for water. The "final sign-gestalt-readiness" for a water source might strongly orient a desert animal toward a distant mountain range. However, if a severe sandstorm (meteorological condition) sets in, the visual cues necessary to maintain the sign-gestalt are obliterated. The weather acts as a sensory veto, temporarily blinding the cognitive map. Similarly, extreme cold might veto "Sex-hunger." Regardless of the hormonal drive and the presence of a mate (the appropriate consummatory object), the thermoregulatory demands of the body (physical layer) force the allocation of resources to maintaining core temperature, suppressing the reproductive subordinate readinesses.

**Proof of Adjudication Hierarchy:**
These examples prove that in the adjudication hierarchy of behavior, physical and environmental layers possess supreme authority. Tolman's formulation implicitly assumes a functional organism operating within a tractable environment. When these boundary conditions are violated, the "docility" of the goings-off of appetites and aversions ceases to exist, replaced by chaotic system failures or forced stasis. The cognitive map (sign-gestalt) is rendered useless if the hardware (body) or the interface (environment) refuses to cooperate.
