The concept of the "Mid-Parent" was his innovation to handle biparental inheritance, moving beyond single-parent correlations studied by Quetelet. The mathematical machinery of conditioning expectations and variances, though not formalized as probability theory until Kolmogorov (1933), was intuitively grasped by Galton through empirical frequencies. The results supported particulate inheritance, foreshadowing Mendel's rediscovery (1900). However, Galton interpreted his regression results through a lens of "pangenesis" (Darwin's hypothesis of gemmules), which blended particles from all parts of the body. This hybrid view allowed him to think of traits as continuous aggregates of many discrete factors, justifying the use of normal distributions even before the genetic basis was known.

**The Trap of Sociobiological Projection**: The critical trap embedded in this methodology is the conflation of statistical association with causal mechanism, particularly when applied to socially valued traits like "artistic" talent. By modeling inheritance mathematically, Galton lent an aura of scientific objectivity to eugenic arguments. This genealogy leads directly to the eugenics movements of the early 20th century, where similar regression analyses were used to argue for selective breeding policies. **Modern Resonance and Critique**: Today, quantitative genetics uses more sophisticated models (mixed models, GWAS) that explicitly separate genetic and environmental variance components (ACE models). Behavioral genetics emerged from twin and adoption studies to address the confounding Galton faced. The historical lesson is that while the mathematical form of regression is universal, its interpretation as *genetic* regression requires rigorous control of non-genetic familial similarity—a distinction Galton could not make with the available data. The "gray zone" of equating statistical regression with biological heredity persists in popular misunderstandings of heritability estimates.
