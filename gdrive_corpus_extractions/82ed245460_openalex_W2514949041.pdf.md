The multi-hop inference chain is: (1) a proprietary “impact diffusing” design is compared with a control; (2) small-scale Dynatup tests use a modified impactor carrying the helmet model, football padding, and a single-axis accelerometer, with a Buna-N impact pad; (3) the small-scale output is SI and a locally invented EDC; (4) ANOVA and Tukey comparisons suggest ED1 and ED2 reduce SI relative to control at 1 ft and 2 ft; (5) because ED1 and ED2 are not statistically distinguishable, ED2 is dropped; (6) a full-scale NOCSAE-style drop tower is built because Cal Poly lacked one; (7) full-scale FDM ABS prototype shells with donated football padding are dropped onto a rubber pad with a three-axis accelerometer inside an aluminum headform; (8) the full-scale experimental helmet produces much larger SI, HIC15, HIC36, and peak acceleration than the control, and the conclusion reverses to “no reduction” for the prototypes.

The small-scale accelerometer is on the impactor that carries the model and padding: “The tup was replaced for this testing with a specifically designed impactor which carried both the model prototypes, padding from a football helmet, and an accelerometer which was used to measure the acceleration versus time data to be later converted to severity index” [PAGE 13]. <SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses g-normalized acceleration and time in seconds for the empirical injury criteria. A formal operator for the triaxial resultant is

\[
a_{\mathrm{res}}(t)=\sqrt{a_x(t)^2+a_y(t)^2+a_z(t)^2},
\]

which is exactly the operation in the full-scale MATLAB code: “resdata = sqrt(data1.^2 + data2.^2 + data3.^2)” [PAGE 133]. The units are \(g\) if each axis channel is in \(g\); if the channels are in \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\), the resultant is in \(\mathrm{m/s^2}\) and must be normalized by \(g_0=9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) before using the source’s empirical thresholds. The source’s full-scale sampling frequency is 16384 Hz [PAGE 62], and the small-scale sampling frequency is also 16384 Hz [PAGE 28].

The filter operator is a 4-pole Butterworth low-pass with 1000 Hz cutoff:

\[
a_f(t)=\mathcal{B}_{4,1000\,\mathrm{Hz}}\{a_{\mathrm{res}}(t)\},
\]

with \(F_s=16384\ \mathrm{Hz}\). The source states “The data were refined first by a 4 pole Butterworth low pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 1000 Hz, as required by NOCSAE” [PAGE 20]. The MATLAB filter specification lists “N = 4; F3dB = 1000; Fs = 16384” [PAGE 97]. The threshold gate is

\[
a_g(t)=
\begin{cases}
a_f(t), & a_f(t) \gt 4g,\\
0, & a_f(t) \leq 4g,
\end{cases}
\]

because the source says it processed the data “by setting any value of acceleration that is less than 4 g’s to zero” [PAGE 20]. This gate is not a physical shock threshold; it is an event-definition convention. If a pulse hovers near 4 g, the start/end time and therefore SI become sensitive to noise and filter phase.

The Severity Index used by NOCSAE is

\[
\mathrm{SI}=\int_{0}^{T} A(t)^{2.5}\,dt,
\]

where \(A\) is the resultant acceleration, \(T\) is the time when acceleration drops below 4 g, and time zero is when acceleration rises above 4 g [PAGE 14]. The source states: “If the SI value exceeds 1200 it means that the risk of concussion is extremely great and the helmet would be considered a failure” [PAGE 14]. With \(A\) in \(g\) and \(t\) in seconds, the numerical SI carries units of \(g^{2.5}\mathrm{s}\); the threshold 1200 is an empirical criterion, not a dimensionally derived probability. A breakdown regime is \(T\to 0\): the integral can still be finite for a finite pulse, but event detection and sampling dominate, and the criterion loses physical meaning. A second breakdown is strong multi-axis motion: the resultant can prolong the above-4-g interval by adding pitch/yaw components, which the source explicitly observed [PAGE 63].

The Head Injury Criterion is

\[
\mathrm{HIC}=
\left[
\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}
\int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\,dt
\right]^{2.5}
(t_2-t_1),
\]

with \(t_2-t_1\leq 36\ \mathrm{ms}\) historically and \(t_2-t_1\leq 15\ \mathrm{ms}\) in HIC15, where \(t_1,t_2\) are chosen to maximize HIC [PAGE 13]. The source gives thresholds: “Values of HIC above 1000 are considered to be above the safe limit. Later on, the maximum length of time was changed from 36 milliseconds to 15 milliseconds” [PAGE 13]. Table 5 uses HIC15 with 700 fail and HIC36 with 1000 fail [PAGE 64]. As \(t_2-t_1\to 0\), the mean-acceleration term is finite for a finite \(a\), but the numerical maximization becomes ill-conditioned; as the window approaches the full pulse, HIC tends to a weighted impulse. HIC and SI are not probabilities and do not include neck compliance, brain deformation, or cumulative exposure; they are weighted acceleration integrals.

The source’s locally defined Energy Diffusion Coefficient is

\[
\mathrm{EDC}=100\frac{A_2^{\max}}{A_1^{\max}},
\]

where \(A_1^{\max}\) is the first impulse peak and \(A_2^{\max}\) is the second impulse peak [PAGE 20], [PAGE 90]. The source states: “The EDC is a value not used by other researchers and was created for this research. This value is the ratio of the second to first impulse, shown as a percentage” [PAGE 20]. Units are percent. Breakdown regimes: if the second impulse is absent, merged with the first, or below the 4-g gate, EDC is undefined or zero by convention; if \(A_1^{\max}\to 0\), the ratio is singular. This is why the source did not use EDC for full scale: “EDC was not used for the full scale tests because of the nature of the data. It was very hard to determine the first two main peaks” [PAGE 65].

The source’s drop-tower tolerance is a coefficient of variation:

\[
\tau=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{SI}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{SI}}},
\]

reported as 14% for 2 ft and 10% for 4 ft [PAGE 53]. This is an empirical repeatability ratio, not a certification uncertainty. It becomes singular as \(\mu_{\mathrm{SI}}\to 0\), and it is based on a small verification set: four 2-ft runs and four 4-ft runs with SI values 555.28, 681.34, 642.34, 662.80 and 1422.2, 1324.0, 1107.3, 1360.1 [PAGE 53]. The experimental-control difference in Table 5 is far larger than this tolerance, but that does not remove specimen and boundary confounds.

The small-scale stem analysis used an impulse-momentum estimate:

\[
F\approx \frac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t},
\]

with impactor speed 17.94 ft/s and estimated impact time 1 ms, giving 852 lbf; the source then reports stem stress 3736 psi with a stress concentration factor and 482 psi without it, and fatigue life over 1000 impacts with factor of safety seven [PAGE 15]. The singularity is \(\Delta t\to 0\), which would make \(F\to\infty\); real padding, rubber, and structural compliance set a finite contact time. The headform FEM used a 50 lbf load said to equal “a drop from 17 ft/s with an impact time of 17 milliseconds” and gave a yield factor of safety of 4 in Aluminum 3003 [PAGE 46-47]. That is a static-equivalent check, not a modal or contact simulation.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from the Wayne State Tolerance Curve to Gadd’s weighted-impulse criterion, then to HIC and to NOCSAE’s SI. The source states that the Wayne State curve “shows the tolerance level of humans,” that Gadd proposed formulas “consisting of the integral of the acceleration raised to a weighting factor,” and that HIC was developed by NHTSA [PAGE 13]. It then distinguishes automotive HIC from athletic SI: “Unlike the automotive industry the athletic industry is regulated to use Severity Index or SI” [PAGE 14]. The regulatory genealogy is NOCSAE Documents 001, 002, and 004, plus ASTM F429 and F717 and SAE J211-1 [PAGE 8-9]. This matters because the thesis is not testing a generic bumper; it is borrowing an automotive-style weighted acceleration criterion and applying it to a helmeted headform with athletic boundary conditions.

The prototype genealogy is ED2 as a modification of ED1, which is itself a modification of a control helmet [PAGE 10]. The small-scale Tukey analysis found ED1 and ED2 statistically indistinguishable and dropped ED2 from future testing and modeling [PAGE 30]. That is a retrocausal decision: the later full-scale result retroactively changes the meaning of the earlier small-scale “improvement.” At 1 ft, the source reports “The SI is reduced by 18% by using the design of ED1 versus the control” [PAGE 30]; at 2 ft, “The reductions in SI values versus the control are 8%” [PAGE 30]. But the source also warns that a trend between two drop heights is not justified: “an infinite number of lines can be drawn between the two points of varying curvatures” [PAGE 40]. That is a correct anti-overfitting statement. The 18% and 8% are two-point observations, not a dose-response law.

Multi-week traps are visible in the chronology. The small-scale test was attractive because it allowed “a quicker turnaround for preliminary results without having to wait for full scale models and a full scale test apparatus to be built” [PAGE 11]. That speed is exactly the trap: preliminary proxy results can harden into design decisions before the measurement chain is validated. The full-scale apparatus then had to be designed and built, and the source notes that CNC-machined SIRC stem and rotator parts “were not completed in time for testing due to manufacturing errors,” so “a simple non-articulating stem was welded in place” [PAGE 45]. Manufacturing delay also pushed the shells from the desired SLS impact-resistant nylon to FDM ABS [PAGE 55]. These are not just schedule notes; they are causal forks that change the biomechanical boundary.

A second trap is metric pluralism without calibration. The thesis uses SI, windowed SI, HIC15, HIC36, max acceleration, and EDC. The five full-scale criteria agree that the experimental prototype is worse [PAGE 64-65]. That agreement is useful internal consistency, but it is not independent validation because the criteria are all functions of the same acceleration time history. They are correlated transforms, not five independent experiments. The thesis’s statement that “statistical tests were not needed because of the great difference in performance” [PAGE 65] is reasonable for a gross prototype comparison, but it is not a substitute for uncertainty propagation, repeated builds, or inter-laboratory validation. The source itself says the machine “still needs to be validated against other laboratories” [PAGE 66]. The historical lineage therefore creates a false sense of continuity: HIC and SI are mature criteria, but their application here rests on a new drop tower, new headform, new shells, and new processing.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression in this thesis is the substitution of locally convenient hardware and processing for the missing NOCSAE-standard chain. Under clock pressure, the team substitutes Buna-N for the specified MEP pad because the MEP pad is expensive [PAGE 24], [PAGE 37]; substitutes FDM ABS for SLS impact-resistant nylon because the sponsor chose a lower-cost manufacturer [PAGE 55]; welds a non-articulating stem because the SIRC-designed stem and rotator are late [PAGE 45]; and stops relying on limit switches because they are unreliable [PAGE 68]. Each substitution is rational in isolation. Together they create a chain whose weakest link is no longer the helmet feature being tested. The recursive failure mode is that the operator begins to treat the workaround as the new normal and then interprets the output as if the original boundary condition had been preserved.

The counter-trap is to overcorrect from the small-scale result to the full-scale result. If the small-scale test says ED1 reduces SI by 18% [PAGE 30], the tempting inference is “the design works.” If the full-scale test says the experimental helmet is worse by about 2.5 [PAGE 65], the tempting counter-inference is “the design is intrinsically bad.” The source resists the second overcorrection by limiting the conclusion: the results “truly only apply to the current prototypes and an actual helmet may perform differently” [PAGE 65]. The correct theory-of-mind model is multi-agent: the inventor wants validation, the sponsor wants a low-cost prototype, the student needs a defensible thesis, the committee wants rigor, and NOCSAE wants certification-grade evidence. The thesis satisfies the committee’s local demand for statistical comparison in small scale and multi-criterion comparison in full scale, but it does not satisfy the regulator’s demand for a NOCSAE headform, side impacts, temperature conditioning, and inter-lab validation [PAGE 71], [PAGE 66].

The clock is explicit in the test sequence. The full-scale procedure raises the carriage by hand because the limit switches cannot guarantee height [PAGE 68]; the helmet is secured with duct tape [PAGE 54]; the high-speed camera is triggered manually after impact is heard [PAGE 54]. These are human-in-the-loop contingencies. They increase the chance that the measured event is not the intended event. The recursive operator model is: when a sensor anomaly appears, the operator can either stop and fix the instrument or process around it. This thesis processes around magnet spikes by deleting impulses below 3 ms [PAGE 63] and windows the multi-axis record to 25 ms using video [PAGE 63-64]. That is not dishonest; it is a crisis habit. The risk is that the same processing that removes a known artifact also removes unknown physics. The counter-trap is to demand a raw-data audit and a pre-registered spike-removal rule before accepting either the small-scale or full-scale conclusion.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer vetoes any production-helmet claim. The full-scale shells are FDM ABS with weak interlayer fusion, described as “highly anisotropic” [PAGE 55]. The experimental and control shells received different coating amounts, and the source says this “may have an effect on the results” [PAGE 67]. Support material interfered with the experimental features [PAGE 67-68]. Both helmets broke during testing: “both helmets broke during the impact tests” [PAGE 58], and they “failed when drops were attempted from 4 feet” [PAGE 58]. A shell that delaminates is not a valid proxy for a polycarbonate football helmet. The physical veto is therefore absolute for certification: the prototype hardware cannot support a claim about production helmet safety. It is not absolute for the narrower claim that the tested geometry underperformed the tested control on this tower.

The biomechanical layer vetoes direct concussion-risk mapping. The headform is aluminum and the neck is a “simple non-articulating stem” [PAGE 45]. The full-scale accelerometer is a three-axis PCB 356A02 inside the headform [PAGE 51-52], and the source observes that the headform “shakes back and forth as well as pitches and yaws” [PAGE 63]. That motion is not neck compliance; it is rigid-body motion of the headform-carriage system. The source also says the current headform and attachment allow impacts “in the front and back but not on the sides” [PAGE 71]. A helmet is not certified by front/back-only rigid-headform drops. The bent stem “may have had an influence on the test results” [PAGE 60], which is a direct biomechanical confound. The SI and HIC values are acceleration-based surrogates, not brain-tissue strain or clinical concussion probability.

The weather/environmental layer is absent. NOCSAE Document 002 “lists things such as the speeds and temperatures a football helmet must be tested at to be certified” [PAGE 9], but this thesis does not report temperature conditioning, humidity control, or thermal soak. The impact pad is Buna-N at 40A durometer, while NOCSAE specifies 38A ± 5 for the MEP pad [PAGE 24]; the source substitutes Buna-N because the MEP pad is expensive [PAGE 24], [PAGE 37]. Rubber stiffness and damping are temperature- and rate-sensitive, so an unconditioned pad is an environmental boundary, not a detail. Cable tension was “not measured” and was set by feel [PAGE 41], adding a mechanical boundary uncertainty. The weather/environmental veto means the thesis cannot claim all-weather or NOCSAE-certification performance. It can claim only room-condition, as-built, front/back-dominated prototype behavior.

The cross-layer adjudication is therefore split. Within the as-built apparatus, the full-scale experimental prototype is worse across SI, windowed SI, HIC15, HIC36, and max acceleration: control SI 326.90 versus experimental 878.52, control HIC15 169.60 versus 436.46, control max acceleration 88.92 g versus 173.56 g [PAGE 64]. That is a strong internal physical result. Across layers, the same result cannot be promoted to “the design enhancements do not work” in general because the physical shell, biomechanical neck, and environmental conditioning are all non-representative. The source’s own conclusion is appropriately narrow: “the design enhancements provide no reduction to concussion risk compared to a standard football helmet” [PAGE 66] is stated after acknowledging prototype limitations [PAGE 65]. The cross-layer veto prevents using the 2.5 factor as a production-helmet safety multiplier; it does not erase the fact that the tested experimental prototype failed relative to its control.
