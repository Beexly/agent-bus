<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage is clear from the paper’s own references. DIFT descends from dynamic taint analysis and dynamic information flow tracking: Newsome and Song’s dynamic taint analysis, Suh et al.’s DIFT architecture, Clause et al.’s Dytan, Yin et al.’s Panorama, Vogt et al.’s cross-site-scripting tainting, and Dalton et al.’s Raksha [PAGE 16, refs. 1,2,5,6,7,8]. The paper’s Table I summarizes the lineage by tag source, propagation rule, and security analysis [PAGE 3]. The “tag sanitization” operator is inherited from Schwartz et al. [PAGE 3, ref. 9]. The game-theoretic lineage includes FlipIt for stealthy takeover, control-theoretic malware games, Colonel Blotto cloud-storage APT games, zero-sum APT games, nonzero-sum defender/APT/insider games, and prior DIFT games for single-stage and multi-stage attacks [PAGE 2, refs. 10–18]. The present paper’s retrocausal move is to merge those two lineages: DIFT’s low-level information-flow tracing becomes a game over a graph, and APT’s multi-stage intrusion becomes a stage-indexed game with asymmetric information.

The multi-week trap is structural. The paper begins by saying “APTs monitor the system for long time and perform tailored attacks that consist of multiple stages” [PAGE 1], and it describes the attack chain as reconnaissance, initial compromise, privilege elevation, internal compromise, and “data exﬁltration at an ultra-low-rate” [PAGE 1]. But the empirical section uses a three-day nation-state attack and explicitly says, “We only consider the day one log data collected via RAIN for our evaluation purposes” [PAGE 14]. Therefore, the paper’s demonstrated horizon is one day of a three-day campaign, not multi-week. The model’s stationary-strategy assumption [PAGE 4] and stage-independent cost assumption \(C_D=c_1B\), \(W_D=c_2B\) [PAGE 5] are exactly the assumptions most likely to fail over weeks: traffic baselines drift, attacker tactics change, defenders update rules, and the graph’s edge set changes as software and network topology change. The paper’s own sensitivity result says the “expected utility of the defender starts decreasing exponentially when the cost of defense increases to higher values” [PAGE 15]. That is a warning that small cost drift can produce large policy changes. Thus the retrocausal genealogy is not “DIFT plus game theory solves APTs”; it is “DIFT plus game theory solves a one-day, stage-annotated, stationary-cost abstraction of an APT.” Multi-week deployment requires re-identification of \(G\), \(\lambda\), \(D_j\), \(B(s_i)\), and the rule set, followed by re-solving the game. If those are not refreshed, the equilibrium is historically stale.

A second trap is overtagging/undertagging. The paper states that conventional DIFT “results in tagging of numerous authentic ﬂows referred as overtagging” and that untagged spurious flows are “security threats” [PAGE 3]. The game is meant to balance those costs, but the balance is only as good as the declared destinations and rules. If the stage destinations are wrong, the defender tags and traps the wrong cut. If the security rules are incomplete, the tag sink fires but the rule does not classify the flow as spurious. That is a genealogical failure: the low-level taint lineage produces alerts, while the high-level game lineage assumes the alerts are meaningful. The two lineages can diverge in deployment.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursion is asymmetric. The adversary knows the attack stage and the defender does not: “while the adversary knows the stage of the attack, the defender does not know the stage of the attack” [PAGE 4]. The adversary also knows the graph and can compute the defender’s tagging, trapping, and rule probabilities; the defender knows the graph but not the adversary’s current stage. This yields a two-level theory of mind. Level 1: the adversary models the defender’s policy \(p_D(s_i)\) and chooses transitions that maximize \(U_A\). Level 2: the defender models the adversary’s transition policy \(p_A\) and selects \(p_D\) to maximize \(U_D\). At equilibrium, each model is a best response to the other. The single-stage theorem makes this concrete: the defender tags and traps the min-cut nodes with equal probability, and the adversary chooses a path that passes through exactly one tagged-and-trapped node [PAGE 10]. That is a counter-trap logic: the defender wants one trap on every attack path, while the adversary wants a path with the least detection probability. The adversary’s best response is “a sequence of transitions such that any attack path … passes through exactly one node which is tagged and also a trap” [PAGE 10, Lemma 6.3].

Crisis habit regression appears in the model as stationary strategies and uniform initialization. The paper defines a stationary strategy as one that “depends only on the current state” [PAGE 4], and Algorithm 7.1 initializes “uniform distribution over set of actions” [PAGE 13]. Under crisis or uncertainty, this is a habit regression to memoryless, symmetric priors: the defender does not build a belief over the stage, and the adversary does not need to model a long history. The cost is that a real APT can deliberately behave nonstationarily across weeks, while a real defender may anchor on yesterday’s min-cut. The recursive model therefore predicts a counter-trap: if the defender’s policy is stationary and the attacker can observe or infer it, the attacker can shift to a path outside the stale min-cut or drop out and re-enter at a different vulnerable node. The paper’s own scenario list includes “The adversary drops out of the game before reaching some destination in \(D_1\)” and intermediate drop-out cases [PAGE 5], so drop-out is not an escape from the model; but a change in the graph or stage set after the defender solves is outside it.

The clock is the stage counter \(j\). The adversary has the clock; the defender does not. The defender’s strategy “does not depend on the stage, as the defender is unaware of the stage of the attack” [PAGE 4]. The adversary’s transition probabilities do depend on the stage [PAGE 4]. That clock asymmetry is more important than the numerical utilities: it means the defender is solving a partially observed stage problem with no explicit belief state. The paper’s local correlated equilibrium is a way to coordinate actions without a global stage clock, but it is not a belief update. A recursive theory-of-mind extension would require the defender to maintain a posterior over \(j\) and the adversary to maintain a posterior over the defender’s posterior. The paper stops one level short. That is not a fatal flaw for its stated one-day experiment, but it is a fatal flaw for any claim that the policy is robust to an adaptive APT over multiple weeks.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source is a cyber-layer model. It contains no biomechanical variable and no weather variable. Therefore any cross-layer claim must be adjudicated as a veto on overreach, not as an extension of the paper. The physical layer does enter through memory and performance overhead: the defender pays \(C_D(s_i)\) for tagging, \(W_D(s_i)\) for traps, and \(\gamma_r\) for rule selection [PAGE 5], and the introduction warns that overtagging leads to “performance overhead resulting in system slowdown” [PAGE 3]. The formal physical veto is this: if the deployed system’s actual observable graph \(G_{\mathrm{obs}}\) is a strict subgraph of the modeled \(G\), because logging drops edges, memory pressure delays tag propagation, or a host is offline, then there exists an attack path \(\omega\notin\Omega_D\) that the model does not represent. The defender’s detection probability on that path is not bounded by the equilibrium value; it can be zero. Hence no min-cut or correlated-equilibrium certificate transfers to the physical system unless the graph is refreshed and the unobserved-edge set is empty or explicitly modeled.

The biomechanical/human-in-the-loop veto is similarly a feasibility constraint. The paper selects a subset of \(N\) security rules at each tag sink [PAGE 3–4]. If a human analyst can inspect at most \(R_{\max}\) rule outcomes per unit time, and the selected policy requires more than \(R_{\max}\), then the effective \(p_D^{2+r}\) for the excess rules is zero, not the nominal equilibrium value. The model has no fatigue, workload, or reaction-time variable; therefore it cannot certify a “real-time” detection claim when the human operator is part of the loop. The cyber-physical proof is: nominal policy says rule \(r\) is selected with probability \(p\), but if operator capacity is binding, the implemented probability is \(\min(p, \text{capacity allocation})\). If that implemented probability changes the cut coverage or the product \(\prod(1-w)\), the equilibrium is no longer the deployed policy.

The weather/environmental veto is infrastructure availability. Weather can cause power loss, network partition, or sensor/logging failure. The paper’s cost model depends on average traffic \(B(s_i)\) [PAGE 5]. If a weather event changes \(B(s_i)\) or removes an edge from the observed graph, then \(C_D,W_D\) and the path set change. The model’s own sensitivity analysis says defender utility falls as defense cost rises [PAGE 15], so a weather-induced cost spike can move the equilibrium away from the min-cut. The formal veto: if \(B_{\mathrm{weather}}(s_i)\neq B_{\mathrm{nominal}}(s_i)\) or if \(E_{\mathrm{weather}}\neq E_{\mathrm{nominal}}\), then the theorem’s premises are false. The model cannot be used to certify detection under that event without re-solving. In short, the cross-layer adjudication is not that physics, biomechanics, or weather “prove” the game; it is that each layer can veto the game’s deployment if its variables alter the graph, the cost vector, or the action feasibility. The paper provides no biomechanical or meteorological data, so any claim that it does is rejected.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

**Veto 1: exact adversary best response versus the sum-weight shortest path.** Construct a two-path graph from \(s_0\) to a destination. Path A has one node with \(w=0.9\). Path B has two nodes, each with \(w=0.6\). Exact undetected probabilities are
\[
p(A)=1-0.9=0.10,\qquad
p(B)=(1-0.6)(1-0.6)=0.16.
\]
An exact adversary maximizes undetected probability and chooses B. The paper’s proposed shortest path minimizes \(\sum w\): path A costs \(0.9\), path B costs \(1.2\), so the sum-weight rule chooses A. Prediction: on this counterexample, the paper’s Prop. 5.2 as transcribed returns A, while direct evaluation of \(U_A\) returns B. The veto is triggered if the exact simulation shows \(U_A(B)>U_A(A)\), because the source text says the shortest path “is a best response” [PAGE 7–8]. This is verifiable by enumerating both paths and computing the product; it does not require the full state space. The gray-zone is the log-to-sum step at [PAGE 8]. The corrected exact edge weight is \(-\log(1-w(s_i))\), not \(w(s_i)\).

**Veto 2: negative node capacities in the single-stage min-cut.** The source declares \(c_F:E_F\to\mathbb R_+\) [PAGE 9], but \(C_D(s_i)<0\), \(W_D(s_i)<0\) [PAGE 5], and Algorithm 6.1 assigns \(c_F(e)=C_D(s_i)+W_D(s_i)\) on \(\hat E_G\) [PAGE 9]. Take two candidate cuts: cut 1 contains one node-edge with \(C_D+W_D=-10\); cut 2 contains one node-edge with \(C_D+W_D=-100\). A literal minimization of negative capacities prefers \(-100\) over \(-10\), i.e., it selects the higher-overhead cut, which contradicts the stated resource-efficiency objective. A standard max-flow/min-cut implementation with nonnegative capacities will either reject the instance or require a transformation. Prediction: on a synthetic flow network with those costs, the literal signed-capacity implementation selects the more expensive cut, while the corrected positive-magnitude capacity \(-[C_D+W_D]\) selects the cheaper cut. The veto is verified if the corrected cut has lower total overhead and no greater detection coverage than the literal cut. This strikes the single-stage theorem’s operational meaning, though not necessarily its positive-magnitude version. The source’s own theorem says the defender tags and traps the min-cut nodes with equal probability [PAGE 10]; the implementation must first make the min-cut well-posed.

**Veto 3: complexity blow-up when \(\Lambda\) scales with \(N\).** The paper allows \(|\Lambda|\le N^2\) [PAGE 3] and gives the evaluation bound
\[
\mathcal O\!\left(
\frac{N^2(M+2)+N(\Lambda+1)}{\varepsilon^2}
\ln\!\frac{N^2(M+1)+N(\Lambda+1)}{\zeta}
\right)
\]
[PAGE 13–14]. If \(\Lambda=N^2\), the dominant term is \(N(\Lambda+1)=N^3+N\), not \(N^2\). The paper’s summary says the complexity is “quadratic in the number of nodes” [PAGE 14], but that holds only if \(\Lambda\) is treated as a constant or is \(O(N)\). Prediction: double \(N\) while setting \(\Lambda=N^2\); the evaluation count should grow by roughly a factor of \(8\) in the \(N\Lambda\) term, not \(4\). A verifier can count utility evaluations in Algorithm 7.1 for \(N=10,20,40\) and compare the fitted exponent. If the fitted exponent is near \(3\), the quadratic claim is vetoed. This is a clean RLVR counterfactual because it uses only the paper’s stated complexity formula and its stated bound on \(|\Lambda|\), with no hidden data.
