The derivation must be treated as a closed semi-supervised loop over video frames, where the central object is not a single prediction but a label-acquisition operator that repeatedly converts unlabelled spatio-temporal evidence into supervised training labels. The source defines the problem as follows: “Given sparsely labelled video data that consists of n different video sequences” and “assuming that x ∈X, y ∈Y, and l ≪m” [PAGE 1 of 10] [PAGE 2 of 10]. This immediately fixes the gray zone: the system is label-starved, unlabelled-dominant, and must exploit video structure rather than independent samples. The authors explicitly reject the common semi-supervised independence assumption: “Many algorithms in semi-supervised learning assume that the unlabelled data are independent samples. However, in a video sequence, the trajectory of object instances … suggests the spatio-temporal structure of subsequent labels” [PAGE 2 of 10]. Therefore, the effective unit of inference is not a single image but a tracklet, defined as “a sequence of bounding windows that share the same identity over time” [PAGE 5 of 10].

The first formal layer is detection. The paper uses latent SVM and deformable part models, with score fβ(x) = maxz∈Z(x) β · Φ(x, z) [PAGE 4 of 10]. This is not a trivial classifier because z is latent: part placements, scales, and mixture choices are unknown and must be maximized during inference and alternating-trained during learning. The score is converted into a pseudo-probability through Platt-style sigmoid calibration: P(y = 1|x) ≈ 1 / (1 + exp(fA + B)) [PAGE 4 of 10]. The gray zone here is calibration: the sigmoid is an approximation, not a guaranteed probability. If f is poorly calibrated, thresholding and label selection become unstable, especially when m is small and the initial detector is weak.

The second formal layer is team color classification. The shape DPM supplies latent part locations zβ, and color classification becomes fγ(x) = γ · Φ(x, zβ) [PAGE 4 of 10]. This is crucial because color is not modeled holistically. The paper states that “multi-part weighted histograms preserve the spatial information of colour distributions, where a single holistic representation cannot” [PAGE 4 of 10]. The histogram operator is K(R) ≜ {k(n; R)} with k(n; R) = η Σd∈R δ[b(d) − n] [PAGE 4 of 10]. The authors emphasize that “K(R) is not a probability distribution and is only locally contrast normalized” and that “max K(R) = 1.0” [PAGE 4 of 10]. This matters because the classifier is sensitive to relative contrast inside a part window, not absolute color probability. Breakdown occurs when illumination change, ice glare, or jersey shading moves pixel chromaticity outside the learned bin support.

The third formal layer is tracking. Detected windows are linked by a Kalman filter [PAGE 2 of 10] [PAGE 5 of 10]. The tracking stage imposes temporal coherence and converts noisy per-frame detections into candidate tracklets. The fourth layer is figure-ground segmentation, trained with Gentle Adaboost: “we used the OpenCV implementation of Gentle Adaboost to learn a set of 150 weighted decision trees where the maximum depth of these trees is 10” [PAGE 4 of 10]. This segmentation model produces a binary foreground decision f(di) = 1 if H(di) ≥ 0, or 0 otherwise [PAGE 6 of 10]. The fifth layer is data selection, where foreground score af = (1 / area(Rp)) Σdi∈Rp f(di) is used to accept or reject candidate windows [PAGE 6 of 10]. The acceptance interval is τlower ≤ af ≤ τupper, with τlower = μaf − σaf and τupper = μaf + σaf [PAGE 6 of 10].

The key multi-hop insight is that detection alone cannot recover its own false negatives. The paper says the selected candidates “are typically false negatives of the player detector. The detector alone cannot identify these misclassification examples, but they are quite effective at improving the classification performance” [PAGE 7 of 10]. The system therefore uses motion and field segmentation as auxiliary oracles. Tracking recovers temporally consistent detections; figure-ground segmentation verifies that the candidate window contains a plausible player-shaped foreground region. This creates a recursive loop: better detections produce better tracklets, better tracklets produce better labels, and better labels produce better detections.

The gray-zone audit reveals several fragile assumptions. First, the algorithm assumes tracklets are reliable after pruning: “we prune away short tracklets with less than 10 bounding windows because these tracklets are often produced by very sparse detection results, and often include incorrect labels” [PAGE 6 of 10]. If a true player is detected intermittently for fewer than 10 windows, the system discards it, causing false negatives. Second, the foreground threshold assumes that positive ground-truth foreground scores are approximately summarized by μaf and σaf. If foreground score distribution is multimodal—tight bounding boxes versus loose boxes—the single-mean/single-standard-deviation gate may be too permissive or too strict. Third, the paper contains an apparent notation inconsistency: Algorithm 1 says “L = L ∩Lnew” [PAGE 2 of 10], while Section VII says “The selected candidate data Lnew is added to labelled data L by simply taking the union of these two datasets, L = L ∪Lnew” [PAGE 6 of 10]. The substantive description supports union with duplicate suppression, not intersection. Fourth, the number of iterations is described inconsistently: “The number of self-learning sessions is set as ns = 5” [PAGE 2 of 10], while experiments say “up to four iterations” [PAGE 6 of 10]. This matters because stopping too early leaves gain unrealized; stopping too late risks noisy label accumulation. Finally, the method assumes non-static camera views can be handled by motion constraints and field segmentation, but sudden camera cuts or rapid pans can break Kalman gating and make foreground masks unreliable. The system is therefore strongest in low-label regimes and weakest when label noise dominates or when temporal continuity is destroyed.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Spatio-temporal dataset and label scarcity

The source defines video sequences and label sets as:

\[
V_i = \{x_1, \ldots, x_{n_i}\}, \quad i = 1,\ldots,n
\]

\[
L = \{(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_l,y_l)\}, \quad U = \{x_{l+1},\ldots,x_{l+m}\}
\]

with the explicit regime:

\[
l \ll m
\]

[PAGE 1 of 10] [PAGE 2 of 10].

Units and objects: \(x\) is an image frame or window descriptor; \(y\) is a class label; \(l\) and \(m\) are counts of labelled and unlabelled samples, in samples. The probability space is discrete for class labels and continuous for image features. Breakdown regime: if \(l\) is too small, the initial detector underfits; if \(U\) has covariate shift relative to \(L\), self-training amplifies bias.

2. Latent SVM detection operator

The detector score is:

\[
f_{\beta}(x) = \max_{z \in Z(x)} \beta \cdot \Phi(x,z)
\]

[PAGE 4 of 10].

Units: \(f_{\beta}(x)\) is a dimensionless decision score after feature normalization. \(z\) is a latent configuration, such as root filter placement and part filter placements. \(\beta\) is a parameter vector. Assumption: \(Z(x)\) is sufficiently rich to encode pose variation but not so large that inference becomes intractable. Breakdown: the max over latent values makes the objective nonconvex; poor initialization can trap training in local optima, especially for rare poses such as jumping or occluded players.

3. Sigmoid posterior approximation

The paper approximates posterior probability as:

\[
P(y = 1|x) \approx P(y = 1|f) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(fA + B)}
\]

where \(f = f_{\beta}(x)\) [PAGE 4 of 10].

Units: \(fA + B\) must be dimensionless; \(P\) is probability in \([0,1]\). Breakdown: if Platt parameters \(A\) and \(B\) are estimated from too few positive examples, the sigmoid may be overconfident. In low-label regimes, this can cause early self-learning iterations to select mostly easy positives and miss informative false negatives.

4. Color classification conditioned on shape latents

The color model is:

\[
f_{\gamma}(x) = \gamma \cdot \Phi(x,z_{\beta})
\]

[PAGE 4 of 10].

Here \(z_{\beta}\) is not learned independently; it is supplied by the shape DPM. Units: \(f_{\gamma}\) is a dimensionless color-part score. Breakdown: if the shape detector misplaces parts, the color histogram is sampled from wrong regions, causing team misclassification. The method is efficient because it avoids optimizing over a large latent space for color: “these colour models are efficiently trained without optimizing over a large space of latent values” [PAGE 4 of 10].

5. HSV histogram operator

For a bounding window \(R\), the color histogram is:

\[
K(R) \triangleq \{k(n;R)\}_{n=1,\ldots,N}
\]

\[
k(n;R) = \eta \sum_{d \in R} \delta[b(d) - n]
\]

[PAGE 4 of 10].

The bin index \(b(d) \in \{1,\ldots,N\}\), where \(N = N_h N_s + N_v\). The paper sets \(N_h\), \(N_s\), and \(N_v\) as 10 [PAGE 4 of 10]. Units: \(d\) is a pixel coordinate; \(n\) is a bin index; \(k(n;R)\) is a normalized count with maximum 1.0. The paper explicitly states: “K(R) is not a probability distribution and is only locally contrast normalized” and “max K(R) = 1.0” [PAGE 4 of 10]. Breakdown: local contrast normalization can fail under global illumination change because the maximum bin may shift, altering relative weights. It can also fail when a part window contains mostly background, because the histogram may normalize to a non-player color.

6. Team label decision

The team color classifier chooses:

\[
y^{*} = \arg\max_{y \in Y} P(y|x,z_{\beta})
\]

with:

\[
Y = \{\text{“MTL”}, \text{“NYR”}, \text{“ref”}, \text{“others”}\}
\]

[PAGE 4 of 10].

Units: \(P(y|x,z_{\beta})\) is probability. Breakdown: when two team color distributions overlap, such as red and blue uniforms, holistic color models can confuse classes. The paper reports part-based color improves false-positive suppression: “we had 79% precision and 57% recall without team classification (a) and 89% precision and 54% recall with team classification” [PAGE 4 of 10].

7. Figure-ground segmentation operator

The segmentation model \(H\) produces a decision value for pixel \(d_i\). The binary foreground indicator is:

\[
f(d_i) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } H(d_i) \geq 0 \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

[PAGE 6 of 10].

Units: \(f(d_i)\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: if the Adaboost model is trained on too few manually labeled pixels, it may overfit to rink color or ice reflections. The paper notes the model is trained with no more than 5 images: “The process is repeated a few times with no more than 5 images” [PAGE 4 of 10].

8. Foreground proportion score

For a candidate window \(R_p\), the foreground score is:

\[
a_f = \frac{1}{\operatorname{area}(R_p)} \sum_{d_i \in R_p} f(d_i)
\]

[PAGE 6 of 10].

Units: \(\operatorname{area}(R_p)\) is in pixels; \(a_f\) is dimensionless and lies in \([0,1.0]\). Breakdown: if \(R_p\) is too large, true foreground proportion decreases; if \(R_p\) is too tight, background pixels may be included or the player may be clipped, making \(a_f\) unstable.

9. Threshold gate for pseudo-label acceptance

The acceptance thresholds are:

\[
\tau_{\text{lower}} = \mu_{a_f} - \sigma_{a_f}
\]

\[
\tau_{\text{upper}} = \mu_{a_f} + \sigma_{a_f}
\]

\[
\text{accept } R_p \text{ if } \tau_{\text{lower}} \leq a_f \leq \tau_{\text{upper}}
\]

[PAGE 6 of 10].

Units: \(\mu_{a_f}\) and \(\sigma_{a_f}\) are dimensionless because \(a_f\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: if positive foreground scores are skewed by loose annotations, \(\tau_{\text{upper}}\) becomes too permissive; if variance is large, the interval may admit many false positives. The threshold is derived from labelled positive instances, so annotation quality directly controls label noise.

10. Tracklet pruning and selection budget

The paper prunes short tracklets:

\[
\text{discard } T_j \text{ if } |T_j| < 10
\]

[PAGE 6 of 10].

The maximum number of additional labels per iteration is:

\[
n_{\max} = 2000
\]

[PAGE 6 of 10].

Units: \(|T_j|\) is number of bounding windows; \(n_{\max}\) is number of labels. Breakdown: aggressive pruning removes sparse true positives; large \(n_{\max}\) can overwhelm retraining with noisy labels if earlier detectors are weak.

11. Average precision evaluation

The paper uses PASCAL VOC average precision: “We adopted the PASCAL VOC criterion … and used average precision (AP) for evaluating our detection results” [PAGE 6 of 10]. Formally, average precision is an area under a precision-recall curve:

\[
\mathrm{AP} = \int_0^1 p(r)\,dr
\]

where \(p(r)\) is precision at recall \(r\). Units: dimensionless. Breakdown: AP is sensitive to ranking quality and localization threshold; if bounding boxes are systematically loose, AP can fall even if classification confidence improves.

12. Information-theoretic audit

The self-learning loop implicitly seeks to reduce uncertainty about player labels. If \(Y\) is the true player-label variable and \(D\) is detector evidence, the relevant information quantity is:

\[
I(Y;D) = H(Y) - H(Y|D)
\]

where entropy is measured in bits if logarithms are base 2. Units: bits. The source does not explicitly optimize mutual information, but the selection process approximates it by choosing temporally consistent, segmentation-verified detections that the current detector is likely to have missed. Breakdown: if selected candidates are mostly redundant easy positives, \(I(Y;D)\) gain is small; if candidates are noisy, the update can increase \(H(Y|D)\) rather than reduce it.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of the paper is a convergence of semi-supervised learning, part-based object detection, tracking-by-detection, and interactive video annotation. The authors place their work in a tradition that “originally dates back to the work of Scudder” [PAGE 1 of 10], indicating that self-training and adaptive pattern recognition are not new. However, the paper’s novelty is not merely self-training; it is the application of self-training to “an unknown, unconstrained number of target objects in a more generalized video sequence with non-static camera views” [PAGE 1 of 10]. This is a deliberate break from single-object tracking-by-detection methods such as Babenko et al., Kalal et al., and Leistner et al., which the paper describes as having “the stringent assumption of having only one instance of the target object class in each frame of a video sequence” [PAGE 2 of 10].

A second lineage is the deformable part model tradition. The paper adopts “the recent latent SVM (LSVM) approach of Felzenszwalb et al” [PAGE 4 of 10]. This lineage matters because sports players have “wide pose variation” and “object pose” challenges [PAGE 3 of 10]. A rigid holistic template would fail under running, jumping, skating, and occlusion. The part-based model decomposes the player into localized components, allowing the detector to tolerate pose change. The color classifier is then conditioned on these parts, creating a two-stream model: shape for localization and color for team identity.

A third lineage is video annotation and crowdsourcing. The paper reviews LabelMe, VATIC, LabelMe video, and other tools, but criticizes them because they “are designed primarily for reducing the overall labelling cost in terms of time and money” and do not consider “the impact of each label for improved performance of a classification model” [PAGE 1 of 10]. This positions the paper as an active-label-discovery system rather than a passive annotation platform. The system does not ask humans for more labels; it asks the model to discover labels that are most useful under motion and field constraints.

A fourth lineage is tracking and field modeling. The paper uses Kalman filters for tracklet formation and figure-ground segmentation for validation. The field segmentation is trained with Gentle Adaboost and only a small number of interactive labels [PAGE 4 of 10]. This reflects a broader lineage in sports video analysis where the playing field is a strong prior. In hockey, the rink color and line structure provide a discriminative background model; in basketball, the court provides a different but related prior. The historical trap is to assume that generic saliency or objectness will work in sports video. The paper explicitly rejects that assumption: “objectness did not work well in a hockey video mainly due to a small size of hockey players and weak contrast of the colour of hockey players and the rink” [PAGE 5 of 10].

The multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy are precise. First, the single-object trap: if one assumes one target per frame, multi-player sports video becomes unusable. The paper avoids this by allowing an “unconstrained, unknown number of players” [PAGE 2 of 10]. Second, the stationary-camera trap: many prior semi-supervised video methods assume smooth motion from a fixed camera. The paper states that previous work “assumes a simpler form of video input that could not be applied to broadcast footage of sports” [PAGE 3 of 10]. Third, the holistic-color trap: team uniforms can share similar color statistics globally, but part-localized histograms preserve spatial arrangement and discriminative part weights [PAGE 4 of 10]. Fourth, the noisy-label trap: self-learning can collapse if each iteration adds too many false positives. The paper addresses this with tracklet pruning, foreground scoring, and a maximum addition budget, but it admits that “such an assumption may not hold if the selection process accumulates too many noisy labels” [PAGE 6 of 10]. Fifth, the scale trap: prior methods required a single object scale, but sports players vary in size due to camera zoom and player distance; the paper explicitly says it needs to improve localization “for sports players with various sizes” [PAGE 3 of 10].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The system can be interpreted as a recursive theory-of-mind loop between a detector, a tracker, a segmenter, and an implicit annotator. At iteration \(t\), the current model \(D_t\) produces detections on unlabelled frames. A naïve theory of mind would assume that the model’s highest-confidence detections are the best labels. This is the crisis habit regression: under label scarcity, the system may regress to selecting only easy positives, because high-confidence detections are abundant and cheap. This habit is dangerous because the paper’s central claim is that the most useful labels are often missed by the current detector: “many of these candidate bounding windows are typically false negatives of the player detector” [PAGE 7 of 10]. If the system only trusts its own current confidence, it reinforces existing blind spots.

The counter-trap is the use of orthogonal cues. Tracking supplies temporal persistence. A player that is detected sporadically but moves consistently across frames can be recovered even if per-frame detection confidence is low. Figure-ground segmentation supplies spatial plausibility: a candidate window should contain a foreground proportion consistent with a player. The acceptance gate \( \tau_{\text{lower}} \leq a_f \leq \tau_{\text{upper}} \) acts as a theory-of-mind correction: it asks not “did the detector like this window?” but “does this window look like a foreground object under the field model?” This prevents pure self-reinforcement.

The clock component is the temporal and iterative stopping structure. The paper sets \(ns = 5\) self-learning sessions in Algorithm 1 [PAGE 2 of 10], while experiments describe up to four iterations [PAGE 6 of 10]. The clock serves two purposes. First, it limits drift: too many iterations can accumulate noisy labels. Second, it provides convergence evidence: the paper shows that “the performance of tracking hockey players quickly converges to the best performance in the case of fully labelled images” [PAGE 7 of 10]. The clock is therefore not merely a compute budget; it is a epistemic brake.

A recursive adversarial model can be written conceptually as:

\[
D_{t+1} = \operatorname{Train}(L \cup L_{\text{new},t})
\]

\[
L_{\text{new},t} = \operatorname{Select}(D_t, K, S)
\]

where \(K\) is the Kalman tracking operator and \(S\) is the figure-ground segmentation operator. The theory-of-mind risk is that \(D_t\) may misrepresent its own uncertainty. The tracker and segmenter act as external validators, reducing self-deception. However, if the camera view changes abruptly, the clock can also become a trap: the system may stop before recovering from a transient failure. Thus the ideal policy is not fixed iteration count alone, but a convergence criterion based on validation gain: continue while selected labels improve held-out average precision or reduce estimated label noise; stop when marginal information gain approaches zero.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Cross-layer veto adjudication asks which hypotheses are physically, biomechanically, or environmentally inadmissible given the source evidence. The first veto concerns single-object assumptions. The paper states that team sports contain multiple players per frame: hockey has “6 on-field players per team”, basketball has “5 on-field players per team”, and soccer has “11 on-field players per team” [PAGE 2 of 10]. It also explicitly allows “an unconstrained, unknown number of players” [PAGE 2 of 10]. Therefore, any method that assumes one target instance per frame is vetoed for this domain. Proof sketch: if a frame contains \(N_p > 1\) players, a single-object model must label all but one as negative or ignore them. That destroys valid negative mining and suppresses multi-instance label discovery. The source confirms that multi-instance video is more useful because it captures occlusion relationships absent in single-object videos [PAGE 2 of 10].

The second veto concerns generic saliency or objectness as the primary label-validation cue. The paper tested “objectness” and found it ineffective: “objectness did not work well in a hockey video mainly due to a small size of hockey players and weak contrast of the colour of hockey players and the rink” [PAGE 5 of 10]. This is a physical veto: hockey players occupy small image regions, and their uniforms may have low chromatic separation from ice, boards, or shadows. If an objectness prior cannot distinguish player pixels from rink markings, it cannot serve as a reliable foreground validator. The system therefore uses a learned figure-ground segmentation model instead.

The third veto concerns holistic color representation. The paper says part-based color models are effective when teams have similar global colors, such as red and blue, because “multi-part weighted histograms preserve the spatial information of colour distributions, where a single holistic representation cannot” [PAGE 4 of 10]. This is a representational veto: if a player’s jersey has red sleeves and blue shorts, or if team colors are spatially distributed across parts, a single global histogram may collapse distinct classes. The part-conditioned model survives because it samples color from shape-defined regions.

The fourth veto concerns biomechanical pose variation. The paper lists “motion blur, object pose, occlusion, and sudden illumination change” as challenges [PAGE 3 of 10]. A rigid template that assumes upright walking pedestrians would be biomechanically inadmissible for hockey or basketball, where players skate, jump, lunge, and collide. The latent SVM part model is admissible because latent part placements can adapt to pose. Breakdown regime: if a pose is outside the learned mixture components, such as extreme airborne extension or full occlusion, the detector may still fail. The countermeasure is temporal tracking and field segmentation, but these cannot fully recover a player who is never detected in any frame.

The fifth veto concerns weather or environmental illumination. The source does not focus on outdoor weather, but it does identify “sudden illumination change” as a challenge [PAGE 3 of 10]. In indoor hockey, rink lighting, camera exposure, and ice reflection can create abrupt color shifts. In outdoor basketball, sun angle and cloud cover can alter court color. A color histogram with fixed bins can break if illumination shifts move pixel values outside the learned support. The figure-ground model can partially veto false positives caused by court lines or shadows, but if the segmentation model itself was trained under different lighting, its decision boundary may fail. Formal breakdown: if chromaticity \(c(d)\) changes such that \(b(d)\) maps to bins not represented in training, then \(k(n;R)\) becomes misaligned and \(P(y|x,z_{\beta})\) becomes unreliable.

The sixth veto concerns camera motion. The paper emphasizes non-static camera views [PAGE 1 of 10]. A Kalman filter assumes a motion model; if the camera pans, tilts, or cuts, the state prediction may fail. In formal terms, if the innovation covariance is too small relative to actual motion, true detections are rejected; if too large, false detections are accepted. The source does not provide the Kalman equations, but the tracking stage is treated as a black box: “we do not update parameters of a tracking model and treat player tracking as a black box” [PAGE 5 of 10]. Therefore, the tracking layer can veto labels, but its veto is only as reliable as its motion model. The overall system is robust when camera motion is smooth but vulnerable during cuts or rapid broadcast transitions.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
1. Counterfactual veto: replace figure-ground segmentation with generic objectness.

Mechanism: The current system validates candidate windows using a foreground score \(a_f\) derived from a trained figure-ground model. If objectness replaces it, validation depends on generic object saliency. The source already shows this is weak: “objectness did not work well in a hockey video mainly due to a small size of hockey players and weak contrast of the colour of hockey players and the rink” [PAGE 5 of 10]. Hockey players are small relative to the rink, and their uniforms may not separate strongly from ice or boards. Therefore, objectness would likely accept background patches and reject true but low-contrast players.

Prediction: The self-learning gain in average precision would collapse or become negative in hockey, especially when starting from 5 or 10 labels. The source reports a large gain with the proposed method: “about 20% in the mean average precision — in cases with a small number of labelled images” [PAGE 6 of 10]. Under the objectness counterfactual, the gain should be much smaller because false negatives of the detector would not be recovered reliably. Verification: train the same DPM, tracking, and data-selection loop but replace \(f(d_i)\) with an objectness saliency map. Measure AP across iterations for \(m = 5, 10, 20, 40, 100\). If objectness is inadmissible, the AP trajectory should flatten early and may degrade after iteration 2.

2. Counterfactual veto: replace part-based color histograms with holistic color histograms.

Mechanism: The paper uses part-conditioned HSV histograms because they preserve spatial information: “multi-part weighted histograms preserve the spatial information of colour distributions, where a single holistic representation cannot” [PAGE 4 of 10]. Montreal Canadiens and New York Rangers can have similar red and blue color content globally. A holistic histogram may confuse teams when the same colors appear in different spatial arrangements. The part model uses latent shape locations \(z_{\beta}\) to sample color from meaningful regions.

Prediction: Team classification would produce weaker false-positive suppression. The source reports that team classification changes performance from “79% precision and 57% recall without team classification” to “89% precision and 54% recall with team classification” [PAGE 4 of 10]. Under the holistic-color counterfactual, precision improvement should be smaller, perhaps failing to reach the same false-positive suppression, because the model cannot distinguish whether red pixels belong to a sleeve, torso, or background object. Verification: train a holistic color classifier using the same HSV bin count but no part conditioning. Compare precision and recall on detection windows, and compare final AP after self-learning. The veto is confirmed if holistic color yields lower precision improvement and higher team-confusion errors.

3. Counterfactual veto: remove tracklet pruning and widen foreground acceptance thresholds.

Mechanism: The system prunes tracklets shorter than 10 windows because “these tracklets are often produced by very sparse detection results, and often include incorrect labels” [PAGE 6 of 10]. It also accepts candidate windows only if \(\tau_{\text{lower}} \leq a_f \leq \tau_{\text{upper}}\) [PAGE 6 of 10]. If pruning is removed and thresholds are widened, many noisy detections enter \(L_{\text{new}}\). The paper warns that improvement assumes labels are beneficial: “such an assumption may not hold if the selection process accumulates too many noisy labels” [PAGE 6 of 10].

Prediction: The system will initially add more labels, but AP gain will diminish or reverse, especially for larger initial label sets. The source already shows that gain decreases with more labels: “the performance gain gradually decreases or is eliminated with larger labelled datasets” [PAGE 7 of 10]. Under the noisy-selection counterfactual, the gain should disappear even earlier, because false positives from short tracklets and loose foreground gates will dominate. Verification: run self-learning with three conditions: (a) original pruning and thresholds, (b) no tracklet pruning, (c) widened threshold interval, for example using \(\mu_{a_f} \pm 2\sigma_{a_f}\) instead of \(\mu_{a_f} \pm \sigma_{a_f}\). Measure AP after each iteration and estimate pseudo-label noise by checking selected boxes against ground truth. The veto is confirmed if condition (b) or (c) produces lower AP after iteration 2 and higher false-positive label rates.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
