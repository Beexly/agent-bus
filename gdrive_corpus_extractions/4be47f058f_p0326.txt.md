The lineage begins with Tolman's purposive behaviorism (1932), which intervened between Watsonian strict stimulus-response psychology and later cognitive expectancy theory. The sign gestalt-connection on [PAGE 300] is Tolman's mechanism for representing goal-directedness without invoking unobservable consciousness: an environmental sign stands for a significate and organizes behavior preparatorily. This position descends from Pavlovian conditioned stimulus theory and Thorndike's "stamped in" learning via the law of effect, but rejects the idea that all learning is blind mechanical stamping-in; Tolman explicitly preserves a cognitive sign phase before mechanical reduction. The dictionary citation to English's 1928 A Student's Dictionary of Psychological Terms [PAGE 300] bridges behaviorism and psychoanalysis: Hamilton's "indirect responsiveness" and Freudian symbolization in hysteria and dreams are re-descriptions of sign operations in which an idea or action partially satisfies a repressed craving without attaining the goal.

Scientific descendants include Hullian drive-reduction theory, which formalized habit strength as a function of reinforcement repetition; modern expectancy models (e.g., Rescorla-Wagner), in which prediction error governs the association between sign and outcome; and contemporary neuroscience distinctions between goal-directed action (dorsomedial striatum, outcome-sensitive) and habitual action (dorsolateral striatum, outcome-insensitive after overtraining). Tolman's fixation index phi corresponds operationally to the devaluation sensitivity gradient: as phi approaches 1, behavior becomes insensitive to changes in the value of the significate.

Multi-week traps. First, the acute-to-chronic trap: laboratory demonstrations of sign learning or short-term conditioning do not automatically extrapolate to phobias maintained over weeks or months, because fixation consolidates with time and repetition beyond the experimental session. Second, the context-renewal trap: a response extinguished in one setting may recur when the original cue context (water, exposed back) returns, because the mechanical trace was never unlearned, only suppressed. Third, the interpretation trap: an analyst may attribute a persistent symptom to unconscious symbolic meaning (sign magic) when the behavior is actually a fixed reflex operating irrespective of consequences, leading to weeks of insight-oriented work that fails to alter the response. Fourth, the incubation trap: fear responses can strengthen over the weeks following a single trauma even without additional pairings, violating simple repetition-count assumptions and challenging the linearity of phi(n).
