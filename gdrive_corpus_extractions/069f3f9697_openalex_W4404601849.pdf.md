**1. Bounding-box regression operator (as printed).** [PAGE 2] quotes: "In the output stage, GIoU Loss [11] replaces traditional IoU Loss as the bounding box loss function, offering improved detection performance by overcoming IoU's limitations. Its calculation method is:" followed by Eq. (1). [PAGE 3] defines the symbols: "where LIoU stands for the traditional Intersection over Union (IoU), which is the ratio of the intersection area of the predicted box and the ground truth box to their union area. Ac represents the area of the minimum enclosing rectangle of the predicted box and the ground truth box. U denotes the area of the union."

As printed:
$$L_{GIoU} = L_{IoU} - \frac{|A_c - U|}{|A_c|} \qquad (1)$$

Units: all areas in px²; L dimensionless. **Repair to the canonical form** (Rezatofighi et al., cited as [11]):
$$IoU = \frac{|B \cap B^{*}|}{|B \cup B^{*}|},\quad GIoU = IoU - \frac{|C \setminus (B \cup B^{*})|}{|C|},\quad L_{GIoU} = 1 - GIoU \in [0,2]$$

Assumptions: axis-aligned boxes; C is the smallest enclosing rectangle.

**Singularity / breakdown regimes:** (i) |A_c| → 0 when both boxes degenerate to points or lines ⇒ 0/0 indeterminacy; (ii) the printed form is bounded below and evaluates *negative* for disjoint boxes (IoU = 0 while |A_c − U|/|A_c| ∈ (0,1)), so it is not a proper loss and its gradient does not increase with separation; (iii) the canonical GIoU gradient vanishes when C is dominated by an already-large union (one box fully contains the other) — the containment plateau.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three agents are nested in this system, and each has a failure mode the others cannot see.

**Agent 1 — the operator/analyst (habit regression under crisis).** When the ball disappears, the reflex is to *loosen*: reduce track_thresh, widen match_thresh, extend track_buffer. This is crisis habit regression — reverting to a high-recall prior because a missing object feels worse than a mislabeled one. The formal consequence is direct: lowering the first-stage threshold admits low-score background boxes into the second stage, and since the gate is $1 - IoU$ with a 6-px box, essentially any nearby blob can claim the identity. The observable signature is an *increase* in distinct IDs per minute concurrent with flat detection rate — i.e., the fix improves the felt problem (fewer blanks) while destroying the measured one.

**Agent 2 — the counter-trap (purification reflex).** The opposite habit: raise thresholds to eliminate ID swaps. This produces track fragmentation; each occlusion beyond T_b/f_s spawns a new identity, and the analyst reads the inflated ID count as "more objects detected." The counter-trap is that both habits are locally rational and globally destructive, and the loop between them (loosen → churn → tighten → fragment → loosen) is the default multi-week failure cycle.

**Agent 3 — the clock.** The frame clock is the only impartial arbiter. All three temporal quantities are frame-denominated: track_buffer in frames, occlusion in frames ($\tau_{occ} f_s$), detection gaps geometric with mean $1/(1-R)$ frames. The clock converts every qualitative debate into one inequality, $\tau_{occ} \le T_b/f_s$, plus one dimensionless group, $\mathcal{M} \le 1/3$. Any tuning decision that does not move one of those two numbers is noise.

**Modeling the hidden fourth agent — the camera operator.** The broadcast director is an optimizer with a different objective (human watchability): tight follow-shots on the ball, fast pans on transitions, slow zooms on set pieces. This agent's policy is *anti-correlated* with the tracker's needs in a specific, predictable way: tight zoom raises d_px (helps detection) but raises f_px·v_pan/Z (hurts the motion model); wide tactical shots lower pan velocity (helps association) but push d_px under the anchor floor (kills detection). There is no camera policy that satisfies both simultaneously for a 0.22 m object. Recognizing this reframes every unexplained variance in the results as an *unmeasured covariate* — zoom state and shutter speed — rather than as model quality.

**Recursive layer — the annotator inside best.pt.** The model's high player/referee/goalkeeper scores are explained in the paper by "manual labeling, where each instance of the desired labels was meticulously annotated" [PAGE 9]. That is a second-order fact: the model's competence reflects the annotator's *attention policy*, and annotators attend to salient, large, slow, jersey-distinct objects — exactly the class of objects the model performs well on, and exactly not the ball. The training signal inherits the annotator's priors. A team that assumes "more labels" will fix the ball is assuming the annotator's attention was uniform; it was not.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
A cross-layer veto is a constraint originating below the model layer (photonics, sensor, codec, biomechanics, weather) that no reweighting, augmentation, or hyperparameter change can overturn. Five are provable from this source plus first principles.

**Veto 1 — optical resolvability (photonics → anchor geometry).** $d_{px} = f_{px}D/Z$ with the positive-assignment floor at ~10 px gives $Z_{max} \approx 31$ m for a 0.22 m ball at $f_{px} \approx 1.4\times10^3$ px. Half a pitch is 52.5 m; most broadcast frames place the ball between 30 and 90 m from the camera, i.e. at 3–10 px. **Proof of veto:** the object is smaller than the smallest anchor, so its IoU with every anchor is below the assignment floor; no gradient ever flows to it. The paper records the symptom on [PAGE 9] — "particularly in instances where the ball appears small or distant" — without identifying it as architectural.

**Veto 2 — shutter integration (sensor exposure → contrast).** $b_{px} = f_{px}v\Delta t_{exp}/Z$. At 1/50 s and 30 m·s⁻¹ the smear is ~17 px against a 6 px object; the object's energy is spread over a region ~3× its diameter and its peak contrast drops by roughly the ratio of areas. **Proof of veto:** detection cannot recover contrast that was never integrated; the information is destroyed in the sensor, not in the network. The paper names the mechanism [PAGE 10] but never reports exposure, so its ball recall is partly a measurement of the broadcaster's shutter setting.

**Veto 3 — codec quantization (compression → Nyquist).** H.264/HEVC partition a frame into 8×8 DCT blocks; a 6-px ball occupies one to four blocks. At aggressive quantization, high-frequency coefficients are zeroed and a small, fast, low-contrast object is erased or flickers block-to-block. **Proof of veto:** sub-block objects cannot be represented with less than one block of support; the transform is the floor. Any per-frame detector, however large, sees the decoded pixels only.

**Veto 4 — biomechanical ceiling (human kinematics → association).** Peak human sprint ≈ 12 m·s⁻¹, but players' *acceleration* is bounded at roughly 5–10 m·s⁻², which gives $\mathcal{M} \approx 0.01$ — two orders of magnitude inside the association budget. This is why player and goalkeeper tracking succeeds: biomechanics guarantees it. Conversely, a struck ball reaches 30 m·s⁻¹ with accelerations of order 10³ m·s⁻², giving $\mathcal{M} \approx 3.8$ — two orders outside. **Proof of veto:** the tracking-quality split is fixed by the ratio of acceleration to object scale, and object scale is fixed by optics. The paper's attribution to "erratic motion patterns" [PAGE 11] is directionally right but mis-scaled; the operative variable is $\mathcal{M}$, not the aesthetic impression of randomness.

**Veto 5 — environmental/field optics (weather → appearance model).** [PAGE 9] cites "the condition of the playing field, lighting variations, and video resolution." Concretely: wet-pitch specular highlights create high-luminance blobs that share a ball's size and brightness profile; hard shadow boundaries bisect a player into two boxes and can split one identity into two; rain adds broadband high-frequency texture that competes with small-object contrast; low sun produces long shadows that merge adjacent players. **Proof of veto:** these are scene-level radiometric transformations applied before the detector; they change the class-conditional distributions the model was trained on, and no threshold adjustment restores information lost to saturation or clipping.

**Adjudication rule.** Layer-0 vetoes (1–3) cap performance regardless of model size; layer-1 vetoes (4–5) determine which classes are affected. Applied here: the ball's ceiling is set by Veto 1 and Veto 2 jointly, and the referee's by Veto 4 plus degenerate Hungarian assignment (§7 above). Any claim of ball recall materially above ~0.75 under wide-shot, 1/50 s, low-bitrate footage should be treated as a measurement error until the exposure, bitrate, and box-size distribution are published.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
