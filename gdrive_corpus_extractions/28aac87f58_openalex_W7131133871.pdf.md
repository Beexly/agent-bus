**E1 — Biometric uniqueness (units: bits; output dimensionless probability).**
Let H be the effective entropy of a biometric template in bits, N the size of the registered population.
$$U(H)=1-\left(1-2^{-H}\right)^{N-1}\approx 1-\exp\!\left(-\frac{N^{2}}{2^{H+1}}\right)$$

A cross-layer veto is a hard constraint at one layer that voids a claim at another. Four layers, each with a proof obligation.

**Layer 1 — electromagnetic/physical (GNSS + ECG).** Veto condition: PDOP·σ_UERE > m, or σ_{f_d} > v*/0.190 where v* is the velocity resolution required by the metric. Proof: from E6, velocity error scales linearly with Doppler resolution; a stadium with partial sky view inflates both PDOP and σ_{f_d}. The article's own framing — "it is essential to know which one is being used in each case to ensure adequate protection of player data" [PAGE 10] — concedes that GNSS, LPS and optical have non-comparable error structures and that a single compliance narrative cannot cover all three. Secondary veto: the heart-rate chest strap "uses electrical sensors to detect heartbeats in real time" [PAGE 13]; motion artifact during sprinting degrades RR detection, and per E7 a 1 ms jitter is 0.54 bpm at 180 bpm. Any injury-risk inference built on artifact-contaminated RR series fails falsifiability, and by the article's own standard at [PAGE 20–21] the accuracy that matters is "the accuracy of the conclusions drawn from the data and the decisions made on the basis of those conclusions."

**Layer 2 — biomechanical (skeletal visibility).** Veto condition: for the kick instant t_k, the count of frames in [t_k − δ, t_k + δ] with all limb-relevant keypoints visible falls below k_min. Proof: the template entropy H in E1 is a function of the visible keypoint set; partial occlusion reduces the effective dimension, which reduces H, which *lowers* identifiability U while simultaneously *raising* positional variance σ_det in E5. The two effects push in opposite directions legally: occlusion makes the system less biometric but more error-prone, so a provider cannot simultaneously claim "not biometric because occluded" and "accurate because 29 points." The source supplies the architectural basis for the constraint — 10 to 14 cameras, 29 points per player [PAGE 11] — and the constraint follows from geometry alone.

**Layer 3 — weather/optical.** Veto condition: contrast transfer at the relevant spatial frequency falls below the detector threshold for keypoint regression, or specular solar saturation clips the sensor. Rain and snow add scattering; low sun produces glare across a stadium bowl whose camera placement is fixed at certification. Because certification windows are four years [PAGE 10], a system certified in July is evaluated under conditions it will not face in February. The veto is that a metric derived under non-representative conditions cannot support a decision with contractual consequences — which is exactly the Annex III §4 trigger [PAGE 9].

**Layer 4 — legal.** Veto condition: S = 1 per E2 with ¬C per E10, or the E3 bundle fails, or Article 26(7)/26(11) notice is absent [PAGE 17 fn 51]. This layer vetoes *upward*: a physically valid measurement that was lawfully unobtainable is inadmissible as a basis for the decision, regardless of its accuracy.

**Adjudication order.** Layer 4 first (is the data lawfully held?), then Layer 1–3 (is the measurement sound?), never the reverse. Adjudicating physics first produces the classic error of validating an unlawfully obtained number.
