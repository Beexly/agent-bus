Therefore the strongest defensible derivation is conditional: recreational football probably improves adiposity-related measures, TG, and BP in the sampled populations, but LBM, lipid subfractions, long-term maintenance, female and adult external validity, and weather/setting moderation remain unresolved.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source uses standard meta-analytic operators rather than invented ratios. For continuous outcomes reported on comparable scales, the mean difference is
\[
\Delta_i=\bar Y_{i,T}-\bar Y_{i,C},
\qquad
v_i=\frac{s_{i,T}^{2}}{n_{i,T}}+\frac{s_{i,C}^{2}}{n_{i,C}},
\]
where \(\Delta_i\) has units of the outcome: kg/m² for BMI, percent for BFP, cm for WC, kg for LBM, mmHg for SBP and DBP. The variance \(v_i\) has squared outcome units, and the information weight is \(w_i=1/v_i\) with inverse squared outcome units. The source states: “When outcomes were reported using comparable units and measurement conventions across studies, pooled effects were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs)” [PAGE 2]. When scales differed, it used standardized mean differences: “When outcome measures differed in scale or measurement convention, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used” [PAGE 2]. A Hedges-type operator is
\[
g_i=\frac{\bar Y_{i,T}-\bar Y_{i,C}}{s_{i,\text{pooled}}},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(g_i)\approx \frac{n_T+n_C}{n_T n_C}+\frac{g_i^2}{2(n_T+n_C)},
\]
which is dimensionless; this matches the use of SMDs for TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C [PAGE 2].

Fixed-effect pooling is
\[
\hat\Delta_F=\frac{\sum_i w_i\Delta_i}{\sum_i w_i},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\hat\Delta_F)=\left(\sum_i w_i\right)^{-1}.
\]
Random-effects pooling is
\[
w_i^{*}=\frac{1}{v_i+\tau^{2}},
\qquad
\hat\Delta_R=\frac{\sum_i w_i^{*}\Delta_i}{\sum_i w_i^{*}},
\]
with \(\tau^{2}\) in squared outcome units. The model-selection rule was explicit: “A fixed-effect model was used when heterogeneity was low (I² ≤ 50%), whereas a random-effects model was applied when substantial heterogeneity was present (I² > 50%)” [PAGE 3]. Heterogeneity was quantified as
\[
Q=\sum_i w_i(\Delta_i-\hat\Delta_F)^2,
\qquad
I^{2}=\max\left(0,\frac{Q-df}{Q}\right)\times 100\%,
\qquad
df=k-1,
\]
so \(I^{2}\) is a dimensionless percent of variance attributable to heterogeneity. These operators produced the reported BMI result: “Pooled analyses showed significant reductions in BMI (MD = -2.58 kg/m², 95% CI: -3.35 to -1.81, P &lt; 0.00001; I² = 10%” [PAGE 6]. LBM was discordant: “For LBM, the primary between-group analysis favored the control group (MD = -3.00 kg, 95% CI: -5.87 to -0.14, P = 0.04), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 74%” [PAGE 8]. SBP and DBP were: “For SBP, a random-effects model showed a significant reduction (MD = -6.62 mmHg, 95% CI: -10.16 to -3.09, P = 0.0002; I² = 55%” and “For DBP, a fixed-effect model also showed a significant reduction (MD = -2.60 mmHg, 95% CI: -4.56 to -0.65, P = 0.009; I² = 0%” [PAGE 8]. TG was significant: “TG levels were also significantly reduced in the football group (SMD = -0.57, 95% CI: -0.93 to -0.20, P = 0.002; I² = 30%” [PAGE 8]. The non-significant lipid findings were: “TC (SMD = -0.31, 95% CI: -0.62 to 0.00, P = 0.05; I² = 29%,” “LDL-C (SMD = -0.28, 95% CI: -0.59 to 0.03, P = 0.07; I² = 20%),” and “HDL-C (SMD = 0.30, 95% CI: 0.00 to 0.60, P = 0.05; I² = 44%)” [PAGE 9]. Egger’s operator was
\[
\frac{\Delta_i}{SE_i}=\beta_0+\beta_1\frac{1}{SE_i}+\epsilon_i,
\]
with \(P\) as a probability in \([0,1]\). The source reports “no significant small-study effects for BMI (t = 0.65, df = 12, P = 0.5253) or BFP (t = 1.66, df = 11, P = 0.1260)” but “Egger’s test for LBM was statistically significant (t = -2.61, df = 8, P = 0.0314)” [PAGE 9]. GRADE starts at high certainty for RCTs and downgrades for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [PAGE 3]; Table 3 records BMI, BFP, WC, and DBP as moderate, and LBM, TG, SBP, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C as low [PAGE 12].

Assumptions and breakdown regimes. The equations assume independent study-level effects, approximately normal sampling distributions, known within-study variances, and comparability of intervention and comparator constructs. Exact singularity occurs if \(v_i\to 0\) or \(n_i\to 0\), which gives infinite weights; the source does not report this, but small strata exist, e.g. WC has 132 participants and TG 146 [PAGE 12]. Breakdown regimes are explicit: \(I^{2}\ge 75\%\) without explanation triggers two-level inconsistency downgrading [PAGE 3], and LBM reaches \(I^{2}=74\%\) [PAGE 8]; total \(n<400\) triggers imprecision downgrading [PAGE 3], and every outcome in Table 3 is below 400 [PAGE 12]; Egger \(P<0.10\) triggers publication-bias concern [PAGE 3], and LBM has \(P=0.0314\) [PAGE 9]; sign discordance between between-group and within-group LBM estimates invalidates any directional claim [PAGE 8].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from football-for-health physiological reviews and small-sided-game studies into the present 2026 meta-analysis. The source cites earlier topical and narrative reviews, including “Recreational football as a health promoting activity: a topical review” [PAGE 14] and “Recreational football for disease prevention and treatment in untrained men: a narrative review” [PAGE 13-14], plus prior meta-analyses on fitness and body composition [PAGE 14]. The included RCTs span 2008 to 2023 [PAGE 3], so the 2026 synthesis is retrocausally shaped by an evidence base that was already organized around adiposity, blood pressure, lipids, and body composition. The lineage matters because the current conclusions inherit old measurement choices: DXA and BIA, stadiometers, enzymatic kits, and automated analyzers [PAGE 4-6]. Those choices create non-equivalent outcome operators across studies.

Multi-week traps are the main temporal hazard. Intervention duration “ranged from 3 to 6 months” [PAGE 3], and training frequency “varied from 2 to 4 sessions/week” [PAGE 4]. A 3-month window can capture training adaptation but not maintenance; a 6-month window can capture more exposure but also more dropout, maturation, and seasonal variation. The source admits that “the long-term sustainability of the observed benefits could not be evaluated because follow-up data were rarely reported” [PAGE 13]. Adherence is another multi-week trap: attendance ranged from approximately 42% in one trial to approximately 100% in another [PAGE 5-6], and Randers et al. reported training frequency falling “2.4 → 1.3 sessions/week” [PAGE 5]. A pooled effect therefore mixes high-exposure and low-exposure trajectories. The LBM subgroup analysis illustrates the trap: lower frequency, &lt;3 sessions/week, showed MD = -5.42 kg, while ≥3 sessions/week showed MD = -1.19 kg, with subgroup difference P = 0.05 [PAGE 9]. That is not proof that lower frequency harms lean mass; it is evidence that dose-time patterning is unresolved. Development is a second clock: children and adolescents mature over months, so adiposity and lean-mass changes cannot be attributed cleanly to football without maturation controls. The source notes that “age, sex, training dose, and comparator type may influence both the magnitude and consistency of the observed effects” [PAGE 1]. Thus the historical lineage supports plausibility, but the multi-week evidence architecture supports only short-horizon, population-specific inference.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the behavioral failure mode in which participants under fatigue, time pressure, or social stress revert to prior sedentary routines. The source gives structural evidence for this risk: attendance was approximately 42% in Weintraub et al. [PAGE 5], and Randers et al. reported training frequency dropping from 2.4 to 1.3 sessions/week [PAGE 5]. Because the pooled estimate is a post-intervention contrast, low adherence dilutes the intended dose and can make a true physiological effect appear smaller or more heterogeneous. Counter-trap is the control-side inversion: controls are not a uniform “no treatment” state. The review states that “‘Daily activity’ was not consistently defined across studies, and comparators such as health education or sedentary lifestyle may have represented different counterfactual conditions” [PAGE 4]. If controls increase activity, receive health education, or change diet, the between-group contrast shrinks; if they remain sedentary, the contrast inflates. Dietary and medication co-interventions were also inconsistently reported [PAGE 13]. The clock is the third recursive layer: children/adolescents are moving through Tanner stages and growth, adults are aging, and the intervention lasts only 3 to 6 months [PAGE 3]. A participant’s belief about the game, the social setting, and the researcher’s expectations can alter attendance, effort, and compensatory eating; the meta-analysis does not measure these beliefs directly. The recursive theory-of-mind audit therefore predicts that the most fragile outcomes are those most sensitive to adherence and comparator behavior: LBM, TG, and lipid subfractions. BMI, BFP, and WC may be more robust because they integrate long-run energy balance, but they are still vulnerable to measurement and maturation clocks. The practical counter-trap is to treat “recreational football” as a single dose when the source shows dose heterogeneity from 2 to 4 sessions/week, 50% to 83% HRmax, and 60 to 90 minutes [PAGE 7]. The correct inference is not “football works” but “football works under specified adherence, comparator, and time-window conditions.”
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto. The physical pathway is plausible because football combines intermittent high-intensity activity with recovery and multidirectional movement [PAGE 11]. The observed BMI, BFP, WC, TG, SBP, and DBP effects are consistent with that pathway [PAGE 6-8]. However, the review does not measure total energy expenditure, dietary energy intake, or direct fitness changes in the pooled models; dietary control was inconsistent [PAGE 13]. A physical veto would be triggered if a reanalysis showed that the adiposity effects disappear after adjusting for baseline physical activity, dietary change, or medication status. The source does not provide those adjusted models, so physical proof is indirect, not definitive.

Biomechanical-layer veto. The source describes “running, acceleration, deceleration, changes of direction, and intermittent high-intensity activity” [PAGE 11]. That is a biomechanical load claim, but the review does not quantify external load, ground reaction forces, playing surface, or injury exposure. Measurement differs across DXA, BIA, stadiometers, and analyzers [PAGE 4-6]. A biomechanical veto would be triggered if LBM or WC effects were explained by measurement modality rather than tissue change; the LBM between-group versus within-group contradiction is exactly such a warning [PAGE 8]. Thus biomechanical plausibility exists, but biomechanical proof does not.

Weather-layer veto. The 17-page source reports duration, frequency, session duration, and intensity in Table 2 [PAGE 7], but it does not report weather, temperature, humidity, altitude, indoor versus outdoor setting, or seasonal timing. Therefore weather cannot be adjudicated as a causal moderator from this source. A weather veto is not proven; it is unmeasured. The defensible adjudication is conditional: physical and biomechanical pathways are supported by intervention content and some outcome patterns, while weather remains an open cross-layer confound. Any claim that football effects are weather-independent would be vetoed by the absence of weather data. Any claim that weather explains the effects would also be unsupported. The source only supports a narrower claim: in the reported settings and seasons, whatever they were, the pooled effects were observed.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
