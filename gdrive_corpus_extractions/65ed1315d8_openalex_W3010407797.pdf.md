The source defines a ratings-to-probability pipeline, not a single predictive formula. The first hop is measurement: a match produces home and away attacking-performance inputs \(S_h,S_a\) in units \(U\), where \(U\) can be goals, shots, shots on target, corners, or a composite count [PAGE 8–9]. The second hop is state update: four GAP ratings per team—home attack \(H_i^a\), home defence \(H_i^d\), away attack \(A_i^a\), away defence \(A_i^d\)—are updated toward the discrepancy between observed attacking performance and the mean of the team’s own relevant rating and the opponent’s opposing defensive rating [PAGE 10]. This is a bounded exponential-forgetting rule: \(\lambda\) controls the last-match influence, while \(\phi_1,\phi_2\) split the update between home and away rating channels. The maximum operator prevents negative ratings [PAGE 11]. The third hop is probability formation: the paper uses logistic regression
\[
\hat y=\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}
=\hat\alpha+\hat\beta_1(H_i^a+H_i^d+A_i^a+A_i^d)+\hat\beta_2 r_i,
\]
where \(r_i\) is the source’s market-implied probability term [PAGE 12]. The fourth hop is evaluation: the ignorance score \(S(p(Y))=-\log_2 p(Y)\) is proper and information-theoretic, measured in bits, with all mean scores quoted relative to a climatology [PAGE 13–14]. The fifth hop is capital allocation: the Level Stakes rule selects a unit exposure only when the forecast probability exceeds the market-implied probability, while the Kelly rule sets
\[
f=\max\left(\frac{r p-1}{r-1},0\right)
\]
as a dimensionless stake fraction, later rescaled so average stake is one [PAGE 16]. The final hop is empirical adjudication: the paper compares aggregate returns, random-selection counterfactuals, Bonferroni-adjusted significance, and league-by-league stability [PAGE 18–25].

The gray-zone audit is where the derivation becomes fragile. First, the source’s headline claim depends on input choice. Goals-only is not a neutral baseline: it is explicitly the worst-performing input in the paper’s own tables, while shots-plus-corners is the strongest aggregate input [PAGE 19, PAGE 25]. That does not prove shots and corners are “causal”; it may mean they are lower-variance proxies for latent attacking process, or that the model is better specified in that input space. Second, the probability model uses a single summed rating term rather than separate attack/defence coefficients; the paper reports that separate inputs had less support by AIC [PAGE 24]. This is a specification compromise, not a theorem. Third, the market-implied probability \(r_i\) is not a probability distribution over exhaustive events; the source notes that implied probabilities may sum above one, with the excess representing bookmaker margin [PAGE 7]. So \(r_i\) is a covariate and a benchmark, not a calibrated truth. Fourth, the source’s strongest economic result is sensitive to which return multipliers are assumed executable. The paper reports a robust aggregate gain under maximum available multipliers and a substantial loss under average multipliers [PAGE 26, PAGE 30]. That is a direct execution-layer veto, not a minor caveat. Fifth, the paper’s own discussion reports recent flattening of cumulative profit and suggests that market participants may now incorporate shot and corner information [PAGE 34]. That is a retrocausal threat: publication and market adaptation can erase the very residual the model exploits. Sixth, the first-six and last-six match eligibility rules are institutional and motivational controls, not derived from the rating equations [PAGE 16–17]. They matter because early-season ratings are weakly identified and late-season incentives are heterogeneous. Seventh, parameter selection is not joint: rating parameters are optimized between seasons across leagues, while logistic parameters are updated after every round [PAGE 13–14]. Finally, the multiple-testing correction is present, but the input family is still small and hand-chosen; the Bonferroni result is reassuring but not a proof of external validity [PAGE 25]. The correct reading is therefore conditional: under the paper’s data, input family, eligibility clock, and maximum-execution assumption, the GAP pipeline has demonstrable skill and economic value; outside those conditions, the derivation does not automatically transfer.

**Breakdown and singularity regimes.**
1. \(\lambda\to0\): no learning; ratings freeze. \(\lambda\) too large: nonstationary oscillation and overreaction to single matches.
2. \(\phi_1\to0\) or \(\phi_2\to0\): cross-location channels decouple; home matches do not update away ratings, or away matches do not update home ratings. \(\phi_1\to1\) or \(\phi_2\to1\): one channel absorbs the entire update and the other is starved.
3. The \(\max(\cdot,0)\) operator is non-differentiable at zero and creates censoring. A team pinned at zero receives no gradient from further negative discrepancies, so the rating can become informationally stale.
4. \(\hat p\to0\) or \(\hat p\to1\): the logit diverges; least-squares estimation on \(\hat y\) becomes numerically unstable. If an outcome occurs with forecast probability near zero, ignorance diverges.
5. \(r\to1\): the Kelly denominator \(r-1\) vanishes. The formula is undefined at exactly \(r=1\); economically, a unit return multiplier gives no surplus to allocate.
6. Market-implied probabilities summing above one mean \(r_i\) cannot be treated as a coherent probability distribution; using it as a covariate is valid, but using it as a truth benchmark is not.
7. Input-definition break: if \(S\) changes measurement protocol—shot definition, corner attribution, composite weighting—then ratings are not comparable across the break, because the update target changes in units and meaning.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific genealogy runs from Elo’s chess rating system, implemented by the United States Chess Federation in 1960, through football-specific attack/defence Poisson models. The paper explicitly places Maher (1982) and Dixon & Coles (1997) in the lineage: Maher used fixed team ratings with a Poisson model for goals, while Dixon and Coles used attack and defence ratings in a Poisson regression and combined them with a selection rule [PAGE 4–5]. Rue & Salvesen (2000) added Bayesian time variation; Dixon & Pope (2004) extended the market test; Constantinou & Fenton (2013) supplied the pi-rating system, which the GAP system resembles but differs from because GAP separates attack and defence and allows general attacking-performance inputs [PAGE 9–12]. Goddard (2005) is the methodological fork: ratings can enter Poisson regression or can directly predict outcomes via ordered probit; the paper reports little difference between the two approaches [PAGE 6]. The GAP paper chooses the direct logistic route for the binary goal-count event [PAGE 6, PAGE 12]. The modern “expected goals” and shot-quality literature is the other parent: the paper argues that goals contain substantial chance and that shots and corners may better indicate process [PAGE 33]. The machine-learning branch is represented by the 2017 Soccer Prediction Challenge and gradient-boosted trees, Bayesian networks, and dynamic ratings [PAGE 5].

Retrocausal traps are therefore multi-week and multi-layer. The first trap is input nostalgia: because goals are the historically canonical input, an analyst may revert to goals when results disappoint, even though the paper’s own evidence says goals are the weakest input and produce a large negative aggregate return [PAGE 19]. The second trap is publication-induced adaptation: once shot and corner information becomes widely modeled, the residual signal decays. The paper itself reports that cumulative profit appears to have “levelled oﬀ” in recent years and suggests that market participants may now account for additional performance data [PAGE 34]. The third trap is parameter vintage: rating parameters are chosen between seasons across leagues, while logistic parameters are updated after every round [PAGE 13–14]. A backtest that uses end-of-season parameters for early-season matches leaks future information. The fourth trap is promotion/relegation initialization: the paper uses average ratings of relegated teams for promoted teams and vice versa, while noting that promoted teams often outperform the relegated teams they replace [PAGE 15]. This creates a systematic early-season bias. The fifth trap is the season clock: the first six matches are ineligible because ratings have not learned enough, and the last six are ineligible because motivation is heterogeneous [PAGE 16–17]. Ignoring this clock inflates variance and can create false confidence. The sixth trap is execution vintage: maximum available return multipliers are not the same as routinely executable multipliers; the paper shows a large gap between maximum and average assumptions [PAGE 26, PAGE 30]. The seventh trap is multiple testing: scanning six input families and selecting the best aggregate return can manufacture apparent skill; the paper’s Bonferroni correction and million-trial random-selection comparison are designed to address this [PAGE 24–25]. The genealogy therefore supports a conditional conclusion: GAP is a synthesis of Elo-like updating, attack/defence Poisson traditions, and process-over-outcome thinking, but its edge is historically situated and vulnerable to measurement drift, market adaptation, and clock effects.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive problem is that the forecaster is not alone. Bookmakers, rival modelers, and the public observe the same match statistics. The GAP model’s edge depends on a residual: its estimated probability must exceed the market-implied probability often enough to overcome the bookmaker margin. That is a second-order belief problem. If other agents update their beliefs toward shot and corner process faster than the model, the residual shrinks. The paper’s own flattening result is evidence consistent with this recursive adaptation [PAGE 34].

**Crisis habit regression.** Under drawdown, the natural failure mode is to regress to the habit that feels interpretable: goals. Goals are salient, historically canonical, and easy to explain. But the source’s evidence is that goals are the worst input in the tested family: “when goals scored is used as the GAP rating input, the gambling return is far lower than for each of the other measures, resulting in a large loss” [PAGE 19]. Crisis habit regression would therefore be a mislearning response: the model would abandon the lower-variance process proxy exactly when the market has become more efficient, not less.

**Counter-trap.** The opposite error is to overfit the counter-habit: assume shots and corners will always dominate, add more shot-derived features, and tune until the backtest recovers. The paper’s own AIC check on separate rating inputs found less support than the summed specification [PAGE 24], and its multiple-testing correction is a warning against input-family mining [PAGE 25]. A proper counter-trap is pre-registration: fix the input family, fix the season clock, fix the proper score, and require out-of-sample improvement in \(\Delta\mathcal I\) before changing the model. The paper’s comparison of ignorance and profit is useful here: it reports reasonable agreement between forecast skill and economic performance, but it also notes that the selection rule only needs enough correctly identified positive-surplus cases, not globally maximal accuracy [PAGE 32]. The counter-trap is therefore to optimize the proper score and the selection rule separately and then test both.

**Clock.** The season is a state variable. Early matches have weakly identified ratings; late matches have heterogeneous incentives. The source’s eligibility rules are explicit: “Bets are not placed until both teams have played at least six league games during that season in order for the ratings to have suﬃciently ‘learned’ about the attacking and defensive ratings of the teams” [PAGE 16], and “bets are never placed on the last six games for each team” [PAGE 17]. A recursive agent should model the clock explicitly, because opponents also know the clock. Late-season matches can feature asymmetric motivation: a relegation-threatened team may field a stronger lineup than a mid-table team. The model’s rating state does not encode motivation, so the clock is a veto layer. The right recursive policy is: use the clock to gate eligibility, use the proper score to detect model decay, and use a pre-registered counterfactual to distinguish “market adaptation” from “temporary variance.” Without that discipline, the analyst oscillates between crisis habit regression and counter-trap overfitting.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto asks whether a physical, biomechanical, or weather layer can invalidate the rating-to-probability mapping. The formal condition is conditional independence: for GAP updates to measure latent attacking and defensive ability, the input \(S\) should be conditionally independent of environmental and workload variables given the latent team state and opponent. If \(S=f(A,D,W,E)\), where \(A\) is attack, \(D\) is defence, \(W\) is workload, and \(E\) is environment, then the update
\[
\Delta H_i^a=\lambda\phi_1\left(S_h-\frac{H_i^a+A_j^d}{2}\right)
\]
absorbs any shift in \(S_h\) caused by \(W\) or \(E\). The rating then confounds skill with conditions. The veto is triggered when
\[
\left|\mathbb E[S\mid A,D,W,E]-\mathbb E[S\mid A,D]\right|>\sigma_S,
\]
where \(\sigma_S\) is the measurement noise in \(S\). This is a testable cross-layer condition.

**Physical veto.** Fixture congestion and travel change running capacity, pressing intensity, and chance creation. If a team’s shot volume drops because of fatigue rather than because of defensive quality, the GAP update lowers its attacking rating. The next forecast then underpredicts its attack. The veto proof is a within-team, within-opponent comparison across congestion states: if the residual \(S-\frac{H_i^a+A_j^d}{2}\) is systematically negative under congestion after controlling for opponent, the rating is not a pure skill estimate.

**Biomechanical veto.** Shot counts and corner counts are not biomechanically equivalent to shot quality. A team can generate many low-power, blocked shots and few high-quality chances. The paper acknowledges this distinction when discussing expected goals: shot data with constant weighting does not account for location or nature of each shot [PAGE 6]. If the input is shots on target, weather and pitch conditions can alter conversion mechanics—wet surface, wind, cold—so the mapping from latent attack to observed shots on target changes. The veto condition is a measurement-invariance failure: if the conditional distribution of shots on target given latent attack shifts with weather, the logistic coefficient \(\beta_1\) is unstable across weather strata.

**Weather veto.** Wind, rain, heat, and pitch quality can change corner frequency, shot accuracy, and game tempo. If weather effects are league-wide and random, they add noise but not bias. The dangerous case is weather correlated with team, venue, or season segment. For example, if a subset of leagues has systematically higher corner counts in winter, the GAP ratings for those teams absorb a seasonal weather component. The veto test is a stratified calibration check: compute mean ignorance and calibration error by weather bucket. If \(\Delta\mathcal I\) or calibration slope changes materially across buckets, the model is not weather-invariant.

**Adjudication rule.** The cross-layer veto should override a pure statistical result when the measurement model fails invariance. A practical decision rule is: if the input’s conditional distribution shifts across physical, biomechanical, or weather strata beyond the noise threshold, then either add the stratum as a covariate, restrict the model to homogeneous conditions, or downgrade the rating to a descriptive index. The source’s own input-family comparison supports this caution: goals, shots, shots on target, corners, and composites are not interchangeable measurements [PAGE 18]. A model that treats them as interchangeable is not robust to cross-layer drift.

**Scenario 1: Input-sufficiency veto.** Counterfactual: recompute the entire GAP pipeline twice on identical seasons, eligibility windows, and parameter-selection rules—once with goals-only as \(S\), once with shots-plus-corners as \(S\). Reward: held-out mean ignorance in bits and aggregate return. Veto condition: goals-only is equal to or better than shots-plus-corners on both pre-registered metrics, or the difference is within noise after Bonferroni correction. Prediction: goals-only fails; the source reports a large negative aggregate return for goals and a positive aggregate return for shots-plus-corners [PAGE 19], and the paper states that “measures of attacking performance other than goals can be more informative in predicting future goalscoring performance than goals themselves” [PAGE 35]. Expected verification: goals-only shows worse calibration and lower return; the veto is not triggered.

**Scenario 2: Season-clock veto.** Counterfactual: include the first six and last six matches for each team, which the source excludes [PAGE 16–17]. Reward: out-of-sample proper score, calibration slope, and aggregate return. Veto condition: including boundary matches improves or leaves unchanged both proper score and return at the same sample size and significance threshold. Prediction: inclusion degrades performance and increases variance, because early ratings are weakly identified and late-season motivation is heterogeneous. The source’s rationale is that the first six matches are a settling period and the last six are motivationally noisy [PAGE 16–17]. Expected verification: the boundary-inclusive run has higher mean ignorance and wider return dispersion; the clock veto is validated.

**Scenario 3: Execution-margin veto.** Counterfactual: replace maximum available return multipliers with mean consensus return multipliers while holding the model, input family, and eligibility rules fixed. Reward: aggregate return and Bonferroni-adjusted random-selection p-value. Veto condition: the strategy remains profitable with the same sign and significance under mean consensus multipliers. Prediction: the edge disappears under mean consensus multipliers because the bookmaker margin rises sharply; the source reports a substantial loss under average multipliers and a mean margin near 6.8 percent versus near 1.57 percent under maximum multipliers [PAGE 26, PAGE 30]. Expected verification: the mean-consensus run is non-positive or significantly weaker; the execution-margin veto is validated. This veto is the most important because it separates forecast skill from executable surplus.
