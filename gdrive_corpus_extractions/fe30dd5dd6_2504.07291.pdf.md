The PDF displays “vn(α, β) = e−λ(n−1)β” [PAGE 2], but the \(\alpha\) symbol is not used in the formula, and the Table 2 numbers at pick 2 require the exponent \((j-1)^\beta\) rather than \((j-1)\beta\) [PAGE 8]. \[

\qquad
V_p(Y)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\exp(-\lambda(k_i-1)^\beta)\right]^p\right)^{1/p},
\]
with the trade treated as fair when \(V_p(X)\approx V_p(Y)\). This is the \(p\)-norm aggregation described on [PAGE 2] and [PAGE 3]. The residual is
\[
\Delta(\lambda,\beta;p)=V_p(X)-V_p(Y).
\]
The paper defines \(\Delta\) as the difference between the aggregated values of the two teams’ draft picks [PAGE 3]. The standard fitting losses, using \(t=1,\ldots,N\) historical trades and \(r_t=\Delta(\lambda,\beta;p;X_t,Y_t)\), are
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MAE}}(\lambda,\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}|r_t|,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\lambda,\beta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}r_t^2.
\]
The paper says it fits parameters “using two convex and quasi-convex loss functions, Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE)” [PAGE 4]. Units: if \(r_t\) is in dimensionless normalized draft capital, MAE is dimensionless and MSE is dimensionless-squared; if rescaled to Jimmy Johnson points, MAE is in points and MSE is in points\(^2\). Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
- \(p=1\): the L1 norm is convex but “not differentiable at xi = 0” [PAGE 3]; optimization uses subgradients, and zero residuals produce non-unique subgradient choices. The paper notes this “promotes sparsity” [PAGE 3].
- \(p=2\): the L2 norm is “convex and differentiable everywhere except at the origin” [PAGE 3]; the norm itself is non-differentiable at the zero vector, although the squared-error loss is smooth everywhere.
- \(0<p<1\): the expression is not a norm because the triangle inequality fails; it is non-convex in general. The paper only uses \(p=1,2\) [PAGE 3], so \(0<p<1\) is outside the validated operator.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Under the draft clock, the relevant recursive problem is not “what is the true value of pick \(j\)?” but “what does the other decision-maker believe about the value curve, and what does that belief imply about the offer?” The paper provides no explicit belief state or game-theoretic equilibrium; it fits a fairness residual across historical trades [PAGE 3], [PAGE 4]. That omission matters. A crisis-habit regression story is plausible: when the clock is running—seven rounds and 32 clubs with one pick each round [PAGE 1]—an executive may regress to the familiar Jimmy Johnson chart because it is fast and socially legible [PAGE 6]. That is a level-0 heuristic. A counter-trap story is level-1: an opponent who expects the regression can offer later picks whose L1 values look high under the gradual MAE curve, inducing acceptance. But the source warns that the L1 curve may overstate real late-round value [PAGE 7], so the counter-trap is not automatically profitable; it depends on the omitted player-outcome distribution. A level-2 counter-counter-trap is: the first team anticipates the second team’s L1 framing and re-prices the offer using an L2/Jimmy-Johnson-like steep curve. The paper cannot adjudicate which recursive level is operative because it does not model beliefs, draft-clock latency, or strategic responses. The “clock” therefore acts as a boundary condition on reasoning depth: it favors simple charts and low-depth reasoning, but the data record only completed trades, not the internal recursion. The correct inference is modest: the fitted L1/L2 curves describe how different aggregation losses re-price the same pick set, not how sophisticated teams actually reason under time pressure.
