Crisis habit regression: Under Knightian uncertainty—situations where the distribution of outcomes is unknown—the settler or planner regresses to heuristic decision rules. The most potent regression is the linear extrapolation fallacy: “more land means more product,” which respects Ax. 1 [PAGE 1] but ignores Ax. 2 and Ax. 3. A second regression is boundary fixation: because land is described as “unlimited” [PAGE 1], the decision-maker anchors on the maximum feasible T and cultivates extensively rather than intensively, driving the system toward the Ax. 3 zone where output collapses to zero. These habits are recursive because the negative outcome of over-expansion reinforces the belief that land was “too much,” prompting a swing back to under-expansion and the opposite boundary failure.

Counter-trap: The analyst who recognizes Knight’s warning [PAGE 1] that the two principles are “badly confused” may over-correct by assuming strict global concavity and a unique interior T*. This is itself a trap: it imports regularity conditions that Knight’s text does not prove [PAGE 1], thereby erasing the possibility of multiple local maxima arising from soil heterogeneity or labor indivisibilities. The counter-trap is to treat Ax. 2’s interior maximum as a contingent equilibrium, not a theorem, and to test for non-concavity before applying competitive price logic.

Clock: The production decision unfolds on an agricultural clock. Land cleared at planting (T=0) cannot be reclaimed if weather or pest shocks arrive at mid-season. The graph on [PAGE 1] is timeless, but the settler’s problem is seasonal: an error in T at t=0 propagates through the growing season with no intra-period reoptimization. Recursive theory of mind therefore models the settler as anticipating his own future regression: he knows that if he chooses too much land now, labor dispersion later will make cultivation impossible (Ax. 3), and he knows that if he chooses too little, he forgoes the “largest amount of product” (Ax. 2). The optimal T* must be chosen under this self-referential constraint, not against a static, known production surface.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The text [PAGE 1] assumes a “virgin continent” with “unlimited supply of land” and “limited supply of labor.” A physical veto arises from soil fertility depletion: continuous cultivation of expanding acreage with fixed labor cannot maintain constant output per hectare without fallow or nutrient replacement. If soil quality is an endogenous state variable S(T,t) that declines with cumulative cultivation, then Q = f(T; L, S) and the production surface shifts downward over time. Knight’s graph holds S constant; the veto is exogenous but binding. Breakdown occurs when dS/dt < 0 faster than the settler can expand T, forcing the interior maximum T* to shift leftward within a single season.

Biomechanical layer veto: Applying “land to labor” or “labor to land” [PAGE 1] implies a spatial relationship. As T increases with fixed L, the average distance each worker must travel between plots rises, reducing effective labor hours per hectare. Let effective labor be L_eff(T) = L - k·D(T), where D(T) is mean travel distance and k is a biomechanical cost coefficient [hour/km]. Beyond a critical T, L_eff → 0 and Q → 0 even if land remains available. This provides a mechanistic microfoundation for Ax. 3’s “so much land that no product at all would be secured” [PAGE 1]. The veto is triggered when walking/transport time exceeds available daylight, a hard constraint absent from Knight’s graph.

Weather veto: Knight’s condition for “determinate division of the produce among the factors by competitive price forces” [PAGE 1] presupposes stable expected output. A weather shock (drought, flood, early frost) enters as a multiplicative shock θ on Q: Q̃ = θ·f(T;L), with θ random and not necessarily mean-reverting within the season. Because the text provides no state-contingent contracts or insurance, a low-θ realization collapses factor prices and invalidates the competitive division rule. The veto proof: if Var(θ) > 0 and contracts are written only on expected Q, the ex post division of produce is indeterminate, violating the requisite condition Knight identifies [PAGE 1]. No internal equation on [PAGE 1] absorbs this shock; it is an external veto on the model’s external validity.

Adjudication: The text itself contains no biomechanical, soil, or meteorological variables [PAGE 1]. Therefore these layers do not create internal contradictions within the page; they establish bounds on the domain where Knight’s axioms can be transported to real settler economies. The production function is valid only on the subdomain where travel costs are negligible, soil is treated as fixed fertility, and weather is either absent or perfectly insured. Outside that subdomain, Ax. 2’s interior maximum may not exist, and Ax. 3’s boundary zeros may be reached before the theoretical T limits.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1 — Lower-bound land indivisibility. Veto condition: Fix L > 0 worker-days and reduce T toward zero. Ax. 1 [PAGE 1] asserts “they can use different amounts and still get some product,” implying f(T;L) > 0 for arbitrarily small T > 0. Counterfactual: impose a minimum viable plot T_min > 0 below which seed, tools, and seedbed preparation cannot be physically accommodated. Prediction: f(T;L) = 0 for T < T_min, and the production function has a discontinuous jump at T_min. Verification: run an agronomic controlled trial with a single fixed worker allocating 0.01 ha, 0.05 ha, and 0.10 ha; if the 0.01 ha plot yields zero harvestable grain while 0.10 ha yields positive output, Ax. 1’s smooth interior positivity is falsified.

Scenario 2 — Upper-bound labor dispersion with factor mobility. Veto condition: Let T → ∞ with fixed L. Ax. 3 [PAGE 1] predicts output → 0 because “so much land” secures “no product at all.” Counterfactual: introduce land rental or labor hiring so that the settler need not personally cultivate every hectare. Prediction: f(T;L) does not approach zero; instead, marginal land is leased to others or labor is hired, keeping effective cultivation intensive on a core subset. Verification: observe historical frontier economies where land was abundant but labor scarce; if output per settler remained positive as claimed landholdings expanded (via tenancy or hired labor), then Ax. 3’s boundary collapse is not a technological necessity but an artifact of the fixed-L assumption.

Scenario 3 — Multiple interior maxima from land heterogeneity. Veto condition: Knight’s graph on [PAGE 1] implies a single-variable production function over a homogeneous quantity T. Counterfactual: stratify land into quality classes q_1 > q_2 > ... > q_n with differing productivities. Prediction: the settler cultivates the best plots first, producing a stepwise aggregate production function with kinks and possibly multiple local maxima as new quality classes are brought into use. The unique interior T* from Ax. 2 [PAGE 1] fails to exist globally; instead, the optimum is a corner solution at each quality-class threshold. Verification: conduct a field experiment with plots of known differing soil nitrogen levels; if the optimal total cultivated area jumps discontinuously when a higher-quality plot becomes available, the single-peak graph assumed on [PAGE 1] is invalid for heterogeneous land.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
