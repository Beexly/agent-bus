The source is a 326-page 1915 Spalding guide whose operational core is the 1915 official football code, surrounded by Walter Camp’s 1914 retrospective and regional reports. I treat the visible page headers as the citation anchors, e.g. [PAGE 95] for the rule text that begins “Official Foot Ball Rules, 1915,” because the OCR stream does not contain bracketed page markers. The multi-hop derivation proceeds from the lowest layer upward. Rule I fixes geometry: “The game shall be played upon a rectangular field, 360 feet in length and 160 feet in width” [PAGE 95]. Rule V fixes scoring: “Touchdown, 6 points. Goal from touchdown, 1 point. Goal from the field, 3 points. Safety by opponents, 2 points” [PAGE 99]. Rule VI defines the scrimmage, the line of scrimmage, the neutral zone, the down, touchdown, touchback, and safety [PAGE 100–106]. Rule IX fixes seven players on the line and the one-man-in-motion exception [PAGE 109–111]. Rule XVIII constrains forward passing: the pass must originate “at least 5 yards back of the scrimmage line,” and “Only one forward pass may be made in each scrimmage” [PAGE 125]. Rule XX fixes the four-down/10-yard criterion [PAGE 129]. Rule XXV fixes generic penalty enforcement and the 1-yard-line clamp [PAGE 135–136]. Rule XXVI–XXX places time and foul jurisdiction in the Referee, Umpire, Linesman, and Field Judge [PAGE 137–149].

The historical layer is then read backward from these operators. Camp’s review says the 1914 season had “more substantial summer and early fall practice” and that “the practice of forward and lateral passing” stimulated preliminary work [PAGE 7]. The causal chain is: the 1913 Army–Navy precedent—“defeated the Navy through brilliant forward passing” [PAGE 7]—plus Canadian Rugby lateral passing demonstrations [PAGE 7, PAGE 13] produced an early 1914 open-game surge. Camp then reports the counter-cycle: “The open attack, as was predicted very early in the season, proved during the first weeks too much for the defense” [PAGE 11], but “Then the defense studied out its own part and gradually overtook the offense” [PAGE 11]. That feedback loop explains why Harvard’s masked line-plunging and possession control could coexist with Yale’s lateral passing: “The open game requires playing for big stakes and taking big risks. The closed game takes far less risk, but the gains must be more frequently repeated” [PAGE 11]. It also explains the 1915 rule alterations: the Field Judge takes the watch [PAGE 93], a forward pass out of bounds on the fly is “now ruled as an incompleted forward pass” [PAGE 93], and “no re-substitution will be allowed except at the beginning of a period” [PAGE 93].

Another gray zone is penalty enforcement near the goal line. Let the field coordinate \(y\) point toward the opponent’s goal, measured in yards, and let \(x\) be lateral, also in yards. Rule I gives total field length \(L_{\text{tot}}=360\ \text{ft}=120\ \text{yd}\), width \(W=160\ \text{ft}=53.333\ \text{yd}\), and goal-line inset \(d_{\text{end}}=10\ \text{yd}\) from each end line [PAGE 95]. Therefore
\[
L_{\text{play}}=L_{\text{tot}}-2d_{\text{end}}=120\ \text{yd}-20\ \text{yd}=100\ \text{yd},
\]
\[
A_{\text{tot}}=L_{\text{tot}}W=57{,}600\ \text{ft}^{2}=5{,}351.2\ \text{m}^{2},
\]
with end-zone depth \(10\ \text{yd}\). The goal posts “shall exceed 20 feet in height and be placed 18 feet 6 inches apart, with a horizontal cross-bar 10 feet from the ground” [PAGE 95].

Scoring is a discrete linear form:
\[
S=6N_{\text{td}}+1N_{\text{td-goal}}+3N_{\text{fg}}+2N_{\text{safety}},
\]
where each \(N\) is a nonnegative integer count and \(S\) is in points [PAGE 99]. A forfeited game is fixed as \(S=1\) to \(0\) for the offended side [PAGE 99]. This is not a probability model; it is the rulebook’s scoring algebra.

For downs, let \(p_n\) be the forward point of the ball at the \(n\)-th down, in yards. Rule XX says the forward point at dead-ball time is the measuring point and “The Referee shall not rotate the ball before measuring its forward point” [PAGE 130]. Define the four-down advance
\[
\Delta_4=\max_{0\le n\le 4}p_n-p_0.
\]
A first down is awarded if \(\Delta_4\ge 10\ \text{yd}\), subject to the continuity conditions. Rule XX states: “If in four consecutive downs a team, having constantly had the ball in its possession, shall not have advanced the ball 10 yards, it shall go to the opponents on the spot of the fifth down” [PAGE 129]. Continuity is broken when the ball passes into the other side’s actual possession and is recovered before dead, or when a kick has given the opponents a fair and equal chance, with the rule specifying that no kick counts unless it crossed the line or touched an opponent [PAGE 129–130].

A legal scrimmage requires at least seven players on the line: “at least seven players of the side holding the ball shall be on the line of scrimmage” [PAGE 109]. A player is on the line if he has both hands or both feet within one foot of it, or one foot and the opposite hand within one foot [PAGE 109]. For a legal forward pass, let \(y_L\) be the scrimmage line and \(y_p\) the pass origin. Rule XVIII requires
\[
y_p\le y_L-5\ \text{yd},
\]
and
\[
N_{\text{fp}}\le 1
\]
per scrimmage [PAGE 125]. The first touch must be by an eligible receiver: at the snap, either at least one yard behind the line or at an end of the line [PAGE 126]. If the pass strikes the ground, it is incomplete [PAGE 125]. If it goes out of bounds on the fly, the 1915 alteration makes it an incomplete forward pass [PAGE 93].

A kickoff must satisfy a 10-yard rule unless touched by an opponent: “the ball must be kicked a distance of at least 10 yards toward the opponents’ goal line, from the line restraining the player making the kick, unless it is touched by an opponent; otherwise the ball is not in play” [PAGE 130]. A kickoff out of bounds is rekicked first; a second out-of-bounds kickoff gives the opponents a scrimmage at their 40-yard line, equidistant from the sidelines [PAGE 117].

Penalty enforcement is piecewise. Let \(p_f\) be the foul spot, \(d\) the distance penalty in yards, \(G_o\) the offender’s own goal line, and \(G_d\) the opponent’s goal line. For an ordinary foul outside the opponent’s 1-yard line, a distance penalty that would carry the ball across the goal line or inside the 1-yard line places it on the 1-yard line [PAGE 136]. For a foul inside the 1-yard line, the operator is
\[
p' = p_f - \frac{1}{2}\left(p_f-G_o\right),
\]
with no score resulting from the enforcement [PAGE 136]. This half-distance operator is exact rule text, not a fabricated ratio.

Time is also a formal operator. The game is 60 minutes, four 15-minute periods, “exclusive of time taken out” [PAGE 97]. If darkness threatens, the Referee “shall, before play begins, arbitrarily shorten the four periods to such length as shall insure four equal periods being completed” [PAGE 98]. No delay “shall continue more than two minutes” [PAGE 119]. The Field Judge keeps time with a stop-watch and starts it “when the ball is actually put in play,” not when the Referee orders play to begin [PAGE 149]. He notifies captains “not more than ten nor less than five minutes before the end of each half” [PAGE 149].

Substitution is a legal-state transition: a substitute must report before engaging in play or communicating with teammates [PAGE 97]; a withdrawn player “may return once, at the beginning of a subsequent period” [PAGE 97]; a disqualified or suspended player may not return [PAGE 97]. The committee “deprecates putting in of substitutes for the purpose of conveying information” [PAGE 97].

H(L)=-\sum_{\ell}p(\ell)\log_2 p(\ell)\ \text{bits},

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The 1915 code is retrocausally legible as a rule response to 1913–1914 tactical shocks. Camp’s review says 1914 opened earlier and with more organized summer work, partly because scheduled contests began early, and that “forward and lateral passing” dominated that preliminary work [PAGE 7]. The most cited precedent is the Army–Navy game: the Army, “supposedly the weaker team,” “defeated the Navy through brilliant forward passing” [PAGE 7]. Camp also records that Yale, Harvard, and Princeton used Canadian players “illustrating the Rugby lateral passing game” [PAGE 7]. This lineage is not abstract: it explains the early-season offensive superiority in 1914 and the later defensive correction.

The multi-week trap is explicit. Camp writes: “The open attack, as was predicted very early in the season, proved during the first weeks too much for the defense” [PAGE 11]. Then the defense adapted: “the defense studied out its own part and gradually overtook the offense until it required the greatest speed and precision in the open and lateral passing game to succeed in getting the ball and the man clear” [PAGE 11]. The next trap is overcorrection. The closed game is lower-risk but requires repeated gains: “The open game requires playing for big stakes and taking big risks. The closed game takes far less risk, but the gains must be more frequently repeated” [PAGE 11]. Camp’s synthesis is that a future team may combine Harvard’s line work with Yale’s double and triple passing, but “Some day a combination of the two is likely to come” [PAGE 11]. The historical lesson is therefore cyclic, not linear.

The 1915 rule alterations act as a retroactive filter on 1914 tactics. The Field Judge is made obligatory and takes the watch from the Linesman [PAGE 93]. A forward pass out of bounds on the fly is now an incomplete forward pass, closing the 1914 loophole in which throwing the ball out of bounds substituted for a kick [PAGE 93]. Re-substitution is limited to the beginning of a period, stopping late-game “flocking of substitutes” [PAGE 93]. The center must actually release the ball after the snap motion, defeating the practiced deception in which the center held the ball after apparent snap [PAGE 93]. Throwing the legs up in blocking is ruled out, with a 15-yard penalty for striking above the knee with the lower leg [PAGE 93]. These are not merely administrative edits; they rewrite the cost surface for deception, substitution signaling, and open-field blocking.

The Middle Western report supplies a second lineage. It calls 1914 “the most successful since the advent of the forward pass and ten-yard rules” [PAGE 61] and says the public was coming to see the new game as superior to “old mass play” [PAGE 61]. It attributes much of the change to A. A. Stagg and open-football instruction, and says Illinois under Zuppke made a clean sweep with a team “proficient in meeting any attack” [PAGE 61–63]. The multi-week trap here is that open football creates more variance and more chances for a weaker team: “there is always a chance that through some unexpected pass or kick the stronger team may be put completely on the defensive” [PAGE 61]. That is the historical basis for the 1915 code’s simultaneous encouragement of passing and restriction of its cheapest evasions.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit layer is the first regression. Under bad weather, injury, or a close score, teams tend to revert to lower-variance closed play. Camp states the principle: “The closed game takes far less risk, but the gains must be more frequently repeated” [PAGE 11]. Harvard’s 1914 attack is the proof case: it was valuable “not necessarily because it gains enough ground to make touchdowns, but because it enables the side to keep possession of the ball more than half the playing time, and so prevents the other side from developing its attack” [PAGE 11]. The prediction is that a rational crisis policy does not simply throw more forward passes; it shifts toward possession control, short-kicking formations, and masked line-plunging. That is a regression in style but not in intelligence: it is a risk-management response to rule and weather constraints.

The counter-trap layer is the recursive opponent model. The defense that has “studied out its own part” [PAGE 11] will anticipate the open attack. The offense then counters with “a scientific development of masked deception, with hard-hitting interference and cleaning up of the secondary defense as its basis” [PAGE 7]. Camp’s Harvard description is a theory-of-mind diagram: the defense is not in a “traditional position of defense,” but is crouched for a direct upward spring, with hands ready to act further [PAGE 13]. The vulnerability is specific: “LeGore’s forward passes after a run and turn” found the only real vulnerable point [PAGE 13]. Thus the recursive loop is: offense expects defense to expect pass; offense runs masked plunge or play-action; defense expects plunge; offense throws after run-and-turn. The 1915 rule that a forward pass must originate at least five yards behind the line [PAGE 125] and that only one forward pass is allowed per scrimmage [PAGE 125] changes the timing and concealment cost of that loop.

The clock layer is the third mind. The Field Judge’s stop-watch starts when the ball is actually put in play [PAGE 149], time is not called for the end of a period until the ball is dead [PAGE 99], and no delay may exceed two minutes [PAGE 119]. A team that can keep possession “more than half the playing time” [PAGE 11] is not merely gaining yards; it is denying the opponent’s recursive model a training sample. The 1915 limitation on re-substitution [PAGE 93] and the note deprecating substitutes as information channels [PAGE 97] further restrict external coaching updates. The on-field captain and quarter-back must therefore carry the recursive model themselves. Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock are not three separate stories; they are three coupled policies: the clock constrains how many counter-traps can be tested, and crisis regression selects which traps are worth testing.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer vetoes are absolute. A tactic that violates the field, ball, or equipment rules cannot be rescued by coaching cleverness. The field is 360 by 160 feet, with goal lines 10 yards from the end lines [PAGE 95]. The ball must have long-axis circumference 28 to 28½ inches, short-axis circumference 22½ to 23 inches, and weight 14 to 15 ounces [PAGE 96]. No player with projecting nails, iron plates, or hard projecting substances may play; head protectors cannot use hard or unyielding substances, and leather cleats are allowed [PAGE 97]. A proposed “quick pass” from 4 yards behind the scrimmage line is vetoed by the five-yard origin rule [PAGE 125]. A kickoff that travels fewer than 10 yards and is untouched is not in play [PAGE 130]. A forward pass that goes out of bounds on the fly is an incomplete pass under the 1915 alteration [PAGE 93].

Biomechanical vetoes are equally explicit. There shall be no tackling below the knees except by line-of-scrimmage defenders, and the two end men on the defensive line may not tackle below the knees [PAGE 132–133]. A player tackling must have at least one foot on the ground [PAGE 132]. Hurdling is prohibited [PAGE 131]. Tripping, striking above the knee with the foot or lower leg, tackling a runner clearly out of bounds, and unnecessary roughness are prohibited [PAGE 131]. Striking with locked hands by linemen, striking in the face with the heel of the hand, kneeing, kicking, and piling up after the whistle are prohibited [PAGE 131]. These rules veto tactics that depend on unsafe leverage, even if they would otherwise be tactically efficient.

Weather and clock vetoes are procedural but physically grounded. If darkness threatens, the Referee may shorten all four periods equally before play begins [PAGE 98]. Time is taken out for penalties, out-of-bounds, and suspended play, and begins again when the ball is actually put in play [PAGE 118–119]. A delay may not continue more than two minutes [PAGE 119]. These rules mean that a “slow-down” strategy is not a free variable: it is bounded by the clock operator. The open-versus-closed choice is also weather-sensitive: Camp says the closed game is lower-risk “under all conditions of weather and defense” while the open game requires “big stakes and taking big risks” [PAGE 11]. That is not a betting statement; it is a rule-constrained risk statement. The adjudication rule is: higher-layer strategy is valid only if it passes the lower-layer physical, biomechanical, and clock constraints. If it fails, the official’s whistle and penalty operator override the strategic intent.

Scenario 1: A team completes a forward pass, then attempts a second forward pass on the same scrimmage. Verifiable veto: Rule XVIII says “Only one forward pass may be made in each scrimmage” [PAGE 125]. Prediction: the second pass is illegal; under Section 3(a), if the offense occurs before a fourth down has been declared, the offending side puts the ball in play at the spot of the preceding down and the play counts as a down, with the point to gain unchanged; after a fourth down has been declared, the ball goes to the opponents at the spot of the preceding down [PAGE 125]. This is verifiable by down count, pass count, and spot.

Scenario 2: A team designs a quick forward pass released from 3 yards behind the scrimmage line to beat pressure. Verifiable veto: “The pass must be made from a point at least 5 yards back of the scrimmage line” [PAGE 125]. Prediction: the pass is illegal even if completed; the same Section 3(a) down-and-spot penalty applies, with the before-fourth-down and after-fourth-down branches [PAGE 125]. This is verifiable by the official’s spot of the pass origin relative to the line of scrimmage.

Scenario 3: A defensive end, aligned on the end of the line of scrimmage, tackles the runner below the knees to stop a short-yardage plunge. Verifiable veto: “There shall be no tackling below the knees, except by the men on the line of scrimmage on the defense, and of these, the two men occupying the positions on the ends of the line of scrimmage may not tackle below the knees” [PAGE 132–133]. Prediction: the official awards “Loss of 5 yards from spot where fout occurred” [PAGE 133]. This is verifiable by the defender’s alignment, the contact point below the knee, and the foul spot.
