For the [PAGE 4] kernel section, the OCR is incomplete. The standard formalization of the named integral operator is
\[
(T_K f)(x)=\int_X K(x,y)f(y)\,d\rho(y),\qquad x\in X,
\]
where \((X,\mathcal B,\rho)\) is a probability space. If \(\rho\) is finite and
\[
\int_X\int_X |K(x,y)|^2\,d\rho(x)d\rho(y)<\infty,
\]
then \(T_K\) is Hilbert–Schmidt and compact on \(L^2(X,\rho)\), with
\[
\|T_K\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=\int_X\int_X |K(x,y)|^2\,d\rho(x)d\rho(y).
\]
If \(K\) is bounded, \(\sup_{x,y}|K(x,y)|<\infty\), then \(\|T_K\|_{L^2\to L^2}\le \sup_{x,y}|K(x,y)|\). Units: if \(K\) has units \(U_K\) and \(f\) has units \(U_f\), then \(T_K f\) has units \(U_K U_f\); \(d\rho\) is dimensionless. If questionnaire features are standardized, \(U_K=1\) and \(T_K f\) is dimensionless.

For a Mercer kernel,
\[
K(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_j\phi_j(x)\phi_j(y),\qquad \lambda_j\ge 0,
\]
with \(\{\phi_j\}\) orthonormal in \(L^2(X,\rho)\), one has \(T_K\phi_j=\lambda_j\phi_j\). The \(\lambda_j\) carry \(U_K\); normalized eigenfunctions are dimensionless. The operator is positive semidefinite; it is strictly positive definite only if all \(\lambda_j>0\). The paper’s phrase “symmetric and positive definite” should be read as positive semidefinite unless strict positivity is separately assumed.

For a sampling/regression interpretation of the “weak correlation sampling questionnaire survey” heading on [PAGE 4], let \(S_n:\mathcal H_K\to\mathbb R^n\) be \((S_n f)_i=f(x_i)\), and \(K_n=(K(x_i,x_j))_{ij}\). \[
\hat f_\lambda=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal H_K}\left\{\frac1n\|S_n f-y\|_2^2+\lambda\|f\|_{\mathcal H_K}^2\right\},
\]

\hat f_\lambda(x)=K(x,X)^\top (K_n+n\lambda I_n)^{-1}y.
\]

Identification requires exchangeability \(Y(d)\perp D\mid W,B\) and positivity \(0<P(D=1\mid W,B)<1\). If \(W\) and \(B\) are unmeasured, the observed contrast \(E[Y\mid D=1]-E[Y\mid D=0]\) is biased:
\[
\text{bias}=\sum_{w,b}E[Y\mid D=1,W=w,B=b]\big(P(w,b\mid D=1)-P(w,b)\big).
\]
This is the formal veto: without weather and biomechanical covariates, the paper cannot identify a causal music/display effect.

Physical/weather veto: [PAGE 8] references altitude effects on elite track-and-field performance, but the paper reports no altitude, wind, rain, or temperature data. If a venue’s wet-bulb globe temperature, wind, or precipitation crosses the governing body’s safety or fairness threshold, the display effect is not the dominant causal factor. Biomechanical veto: [PAGE 3] recommends that “the rhythm of live music should be slightly faster than the rhythm of sports.” That could alter cadence, arousal, or pacing, but the paper provides no ground reaction force, cadence, EMG, or fatigue data. If tempo mismatch changes motor entrainment or stride variability, a survey of “hope for music” cannot adjudicate performance. The paper’s own [PAGE 4] runtime principle—“take the sports display of the track as the main body” and conduct it “without affecting the track”—is an operational constraint, not evidence of no effect. Therefore the cross-layer verdict is: the survey can describe preferences; it cannot certify a physical or biomechanical performance benefit.
