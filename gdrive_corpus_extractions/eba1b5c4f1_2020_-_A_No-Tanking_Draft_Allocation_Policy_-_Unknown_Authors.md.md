- Maintained assumption: \(\partial p_{it}/\partial e_{it} > 0\) [PAGE 5-6].
- Continuation probability of winning primary prize: \(q_i(\vec h^t)\), defined recursively [PAGE 5].

Team effort problem (Problem 1) and NTC:
- Team i chooses \(e_{it} \in [0,1]\) to maximize expected payoff given \(\vec h^{t-1}\) [PAGE 5].
- Objective can be rewritten with a constant K with respect to the effort decision [PAGE 6].
- No-Tanking Condition (NTC): if the derivative of win probability w.r.t. effort is positive, the team chooses maximum effort \(e_{it}=1\) whenever the bracketed term is satisfied [PAGE 6].
- Verbal statement [PAGE 6]: the increase in \(\Pr(D)\) from losing a game must be less than the change in \(\Pr(V)\) from winning, scaled by the relative value of the primary versus secondary prize. Violation implies \(e_{it}<1\), i.e., tanking.

Theorem 1 [PAGE 6]:
- The only rank-based, non-increasing secondary-prize rule satisfying NTC for all histories is a uniform lottery over teams not receiving V.

R-NTD rule [PAGE 7-8]:
- \(y_i(\vec h^t) = \Pr(v^{(n)}(\vec h^m) = i \mid \vec h^t)\) for \(t \le t^*(\vec h^t)\).
- \(t^*(\vec h^t)\) is the smallest \(s \le t\) such that NTC would be violated at time \(s+1\) if updates continued; if no such \(s \le t-1\) exists, \(t^* = t\) [PAGE 8].
- After \(t^*\), \(y_i(\vec h^t) = y_i(\vec h^{t^*})\) is fixed [PAGE 8-9].

Theorem 2 [PAGE 8]:
- R-NTD satisfies NTC, DC, FAIR, and PROB.
- It maximizes the expected league objective conditional on \(\vec h^{t^*}\).

Computational boundary [PAGE 7]:
- Exact optimum via backward induction requires \(2^m\) nodes; NBA regular season has \(m = 1,230\), rendering exhaustive optimization infeasible.

Empirical calibrations [PAGE 10-13]:
- Simulation: N = 3 teams, 60 games, \(B = \pi^V/\pi^D = 10\). Final wins: Team 3 = 23, Team 2 = 21, Team 1 = 16. R-NTD draft probabilities: Team 1 = 65%, Team 2 = 35%, Team 3 = 0%; uniform lottery would pay 50%/50% to the two non-primary teams [PAGE 10].
- Freeze occurs after Team 1’s losing streak makes its primary-prize chance negligible; post-freeze winning streaks (e.g., Team 2) do not alter draft odds [PAGE 10].
- Prize-ratio figure (Figure 2): expected stopping time \(t^*\) and expected gain over uniform lottery vary with B; at B = 1, R-NTD is nearly uniform; at B = 7, the worst team receives D with probability above 75% [PAGE 11].
- 1987 NBA: cutoff at game 266; L.A. Clippers (12 wins) receive D with 28.5% under R-NTD vs 14% uniform [PAGE 12, Table 1].
- 1989 NBA: cutoff at game 332; Miami Heat (15 wins) receive D with 45.5% under R-NTD vs 11% uniform [PAGE 13, Table 2].
- Extensions: multiple secondary prizes R-NTD-J [PAGE 16]; arbitrary win-probability models robust if \(\partial p/\partial e > 0\) [PAGE 16].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s intellectual lineage begins with tournament theory. Lazear and Rosen (1981) introduced rank-order tournaments in which a worker’s prize depends on output ranking rather than absolute output, with prizes decreasing in rank [PAGE 2]. Rosen (1986) extended this to sequential elimination contests, noting that prize structure must account for evolving incentives as contestants advance [PAGE 2]. Banchio and Munro depart from Rosen by studying a setting without elimination—teams continue playing even after losing—and by examining a secondary prize (the draft pick) whose value is exogenous to the planner [PAGE 2].

Empirically, Taylor and Trogdon (2002) documented NBA shirking consistent with tanking incentives [PAGE 1]. The paper situates itself against prior reform proposals: Gold (2010) awarding the top pick to the team with the most wins after playoff elimination, and Lenten (2016) and Lenten et al. (2018) awarding it to the first team eliminated from playoff contention [PAGE 2]. Banchio and Munro show these are not fully incentive compatible under their model and, in Gold’s case, do not even target the worst teams [PAGE 2]. Dagaev and Sonin (2018) analyze a distinct failure—sequential tournaments where a worse first-round seed yields a better second-round draw—which the authors explicitly set aside [PAGE 2].

The NBA’s institutional history supplies the multi-week trap structure. From 1966 to 1984, a coin flip between conference losers determined the top pick; from 1985 to 1989, a uniform lottery applied to all non-playoff teams; from 1990 to the present, weighted lotteries have been repeatedly modified (1994, 2005, 2010, 2019) [PAGE 15, Table A1]. The 1985–1989 uniform-lottery window provides the real-world control group used in Section 4.2 because, under the model, teams faced no incentive to intentionally lose during those seasons [PAGE 12].

Multi-week traps arise from the interaction of prize values and season timing. Early in the season, uncertainty about final standing is high, so the incentive-compatibility constraint has slack and teams exert effort [PAGE 9]. As the season progresses, teams that fall out of primary-prize contention face a collapsing marginal value of winning; if the draft rule remains rank-sensitive, the NTC is violated and a late-season tanking trap opens [PAGE 6]. A second trap occurs in draft years with a generational prospect: if \(\pi^D\) approaches or exceeds \(\pi^V\) (B ≤ 1), NTC binds almost immediately and only a near-uniform lottery is incentive compatible, sacrificing redistribution to the worst team [PAGE 11]. A third trap is path dependence in franchise revenue: dominant teams accumulate attendance and TV revenue, bid for better players, and widen the competitive gap unless redistributive draft mechanisms intervene [PAGE 1].
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model embeds a recursive theory of mind across three agents: the league as central planner, team i, and team i’s beliefs about team j’s effort. In Problem 1, team i chooses e_it given history \(\vec h^{t-1}\), anticipating how its effort shifts p_it and therefore its continuation values q_i (primary prize) and y_i (secondary prize) [PAGE 5-6]. Because opponents’ efforts also determine game outcomes, team i must reason about team j’s effort choice, which itself depends on j’s beliefs about i’s effort. This creates a fixed-point in effort expectations that the league resolves by imposing NTC as an exogenous constraint on the draft rule, thereby decoupling the strategic secondary-prize allocation from the effort decision.

Crisis habit regression appears when a team falls sufficiently far behind that its probability of winning V becomes effectively zero. At that point, \(\Delta \Pr(V\mid \text{win}) = 0\), so NTC requires \(\Delta \Pr(D\mid \text{lose}) \le 0\) [PAGE 6]. Any rank-based secondary prize that increases upon losing violates this, and the team regresses to a tanking habit (e_it < 1). The historical NBA weighted lotteries create exactly this regression zone for teams eliminated from playoff contention but still competing for draft position [PAGE 6].

The counter-trap is R-NTD’s endogenous stopping clock t*. Because the league freezes draft probabilities at the first history where NTC would bind, teams cannot perfectly forecast when the “draft-value” incentive switches off. A team that considers tanking in game t must weigh the immediate draft-probability gain against the risk that the league has not yet frozen probabilities and that winning remains necessary to preserve playoff hopes. The clock is path-dependent: t* is computed from the realized history \(\vec h^t\), not from a calendar date, so a team cannot condition its tanking on a known freeze date [PAGE 8].

The recursion resolves because R-NTD makes the marginal value of effort positive in every game prior to t*, while after t* the draft probabilities are sunk and effort is governed solely by the primary prize V. If a team is already eliminated from V after t*, it has no draft-based reason to tank because y_i is fixed; its only remaining motive is to win for short-term standings pride or revenue, which the model assumes is preferred absent long-run incentives [PAGE 6]. The clock thus front-loads information acquisition: the league extracts maximum ranking information from early-season games while incentive slack is abundant, then locks the allocation before the crisis habit regression can take hold.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer: Effort e_it ∈ [0,1] maps to physiological exertion. The paper abstracts effort cost as implicit in draft rules rather than an explicit physiological cost [PAGE 6]. A veto at this layer would arise if marginal physical cost of effort exceeded its benefit regardless of prize structure—e.g., injury, back-to-back fatigue, or load management—rendering \(\partial p_{it}/\partial e_{it}\) effectively zero or negative for a subset of games. The model’s main result depends on \(\partial p_{it}/\partial e_{it} > 0\) [PAGE 5-6]; if a team’s star is rested and effort cannot raise win probability, NTC loses its bite because the team cannot credibly signal competitiveness. Appendix B partially covers this by allowing replacement of the basic win-probability model with any sport-specific scoring model, provided the effort derivative remains positive [PAGE 16].

Biomechanical layer: Venue (home/away), travel, and roster composition alter p_it through the latent shock z_it and the ability term α_i [PAGE 5]. Because R-NTD is robust to any win-probability forecast satisfying monotonicity in effort [PAGE 16], biomechanical heterogeneity does not invalidate NTC; it only changes the estimated q_i continuation values and therefore the realized stopping time t*. However, if a team’s biomechanical state makes winning impossible (p_it = 0 even at e_it = 1), then \(\Delta\Pr(V\mid\text{win})=0\) and the elimination singularity triggers early.

Weather layer: Not directly modeled for the NBA (indoor arena), but for outdoor sports it would enter as an exogenous shock to p_it. The same veto logic applies: weather that collapses win-probability sensitivity to effort creates a local NTC failure unless probabilities have already been frozen.

Exact singularity/breakdown regime: The decisive veto occurs at mathematical elimination from the primary prize. Once team i cannot win V regardless of future results, q_i = 0 for all continuations, so \(\Delta\Pr(V\mid\text{win}) = 0\). NTC then reduces to \(\Delta\Pr(D\mid\text{lose}) \le 0\) [PAGE 6]. Any rule y_i that increases in rank below the playoff cutoff assigns higher D probability to the loser, strictly violating NTC. This is the veto proof: R-NTD must stop updating at or before the first history in which any active team faces this elimination singularity. Empirically, in the 1987 season this occurred after game 266 [PAGE 12], and in 1989 after game 332 [PAGE 13]. After the freeze, win streaks by non-playoff teams (e.g., Team 2 in the simulation [PAGE 10], or the Pacers in 1989 [PAGE 13]) cannot change draft odds, which is the mechanism’s enforcement against post-elimination manipulation.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: Generational draft prospect (prize-ratio collapse). Suppose a draft class contains a prospect of LeBron James caliber such that \(\pi^D \ge \pi^V\), i.e., B = \(\pi^V/\pi^D \le 1\) [PAGE 11]. Veto: R-NTD’s NTC binds almost immediately because the marginal value of D exceeds or equals that of V even before elimination. Prediction: t* occurs after only one or two games; expected stopping time as a percentage of the season approaches the lower bound; R-NTD draft probabilities converge to a uniform lottery over non-playoff teams within numerical tolerance, and the expected improvement over uniform lottery approaches zero. Verifiable by simulating Figure 2’s B-sweep at B = 1 and comparing generated draft probabilities to the uniform case.

Scenario 2: Mid-season star injury on a borderline playoff team. Suppose team i loses a star player, reducing α_i such that its win probability model p_it shifts downward, but \(\partial p_{it}/\partial e_{it} > 0\) is preserved [PAGE 16]. Veto: The injury does not by itself invalidate NTC because effort still raises win probability; however, q_i falls, shrinking the bracket term in NTC and bringing the team closer to the violation boundary. Prediction: t* occurs weakly earlier than in the no-injury counterfactual; draft probabilities freeze at an interim history with more uncertainty, so the worst team’s final R-NTD probability is lower than in the uninjured simulation. Verifiable by re-running the NBA 1987/1989 counterfactual with an injury shock applied to one borderline team and comparing freeze dates and final draft probabilities.

Scenario 3: Early mathematical elimination of the worst team. Suppose team k is clinched in last place with several games remaining (e.g., an 82-game NBA season where one team is mathematically out of avoiding last place after game 70). Veto: Once eliminated from V, \(\Delta\Pr(V\mid\text{win})=0\), so any continuation of rank-sensitive draft updates would give team k a strict incentive to lose [PAGE 6]. Prediction: R-NTD freezes all draft probabilities at t* ≤ 70; team k’s post-freeze results (wins or losses) do not alter its draft probability, and its final R-NTD probability equals the conditional probability of last place computed at the freeze history. Verifiable by applying R-NTD to a truncated 1989 season history where the Miami Heat are mathematically last before game 332 and checking that post-clinch game results leave their 45.5% probability unchanged.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
