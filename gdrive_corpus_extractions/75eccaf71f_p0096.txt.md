The footnote on [PAGE 77] invoking Miles (1927) and a narrow-path elevated maze shows that apparatus control was already a live concern in the 1920s, though the OCR corruption prevents exact source recovery.

A retrocausal genealogy trap is to project modern computational formalism—prediction errors, TD-learning, state-value functions—back onto Tolman’s pre-computational vocabulary. Tolman’s "expectancy" is not a scalar value update; it is a purposive, goal-directed representation. Treating it as a modern reward prediction error is anachronistic.

Multi-week trap: Tolman [PAGE 77] claims disruption "disappeared" with repeated experiences of the new goal-stimulus relation. Later extinction research (Bouton) shows that extinction is context-dependent and temporary, not unlearning. Original expectancies can renew after weeks, reinstate after stress, and recover spontaneously. Assuming that a brief retraining sequence permanently rewrites the goal map is a multi-week trap: the within-session disappearance Tolman describes does not license a cross-week conclusion of expectancy erasure.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and dual clocks.

When disruption and hunting appear [PAGE 77], the animal may regress to crisis habits: proximal stimulus-response chaining rather than goal-directed expectancy. Recursive theory of mind asks whether the experimenter attributes a representational mental state ("expectation of goal-object") to the animal while actually imposing that model from the outside. The inner loop—animal behavior—may be simpler than the outer loop—experimenter interpretation.

Counter-trap: "hunting" may be bottom-up exploratory search triggered by sensory novelty (the goal changed), not top-down violation of a prospective expectancy. Without a no-swap control that introduces equivalent sensory change without goal substitution, the inference to perceptual expectation is unfalsifiable; the observed behavior is consistent with a general novelty response.

Clock: two distinct temporal scales run through [PAGE 77]. The within-trial clock (seconds) supports online perceptual expectation via continuously present goal stimuli. The across-trial clock (trials to days) supports memorial expectation from "a preceding trial or trials." Tolman compresses these scales, but they obey different dynamics: sensory adaptation operates on a within-trial clock, while extinction and renewal operate on a across-trial clock. If the experimental design does not separate real-time stimulus flow from trial-indexed memory, the memorial/perceptual distinction collapses into a single undifferentiated "expectation" construct.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental veto proof.

Physical layer: Wire sides and elevated tracks alter airflow, olfaction, light gradients, and ambient sound. A veto is triggered if goal-stimulus "reach" [PAGE 77] is confounded with these physical changes. Required control: identical biomechanical path, goal visible but physically inaccessible versus actually swapped, counterbalanced side and height.

Biomechanical layer: Running on an elevated narrow path versus an enclosed maze changes gait, limb loading, and center-of-mass dynamics (SI units: m/s^2, N). A veto is triggered if disruption appears in the run-up to the goal-box rather than exclusively at goal arrival, because the signature then indexes motor adaptation to apparatus, not expectancy violation at the goal.

Weather/environment layer: Although not detailed in [PAGE 77], laboratory temperature, humidity, HVAC cycles, and experimenter scent are necessary controls. A veto is triggered if the goal-object swap coincides with an environmental drift that the animal could detect independently of goal identity.

Adjudication rule: Accept the perceptual-expectation inference only if (a) within-subject counterbalance, (b) blind scoring of hunting, (c) biomechanical equivalence between swap and no-swap trials, and (d) sensory-channel isolation (goal changed in one modality only). Any single layer failure triggers a veto of the [PAGE 77] inference.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR counterfactual veto scenarios and predictions. RLVR here denotes formal verification of counterfactual reward structures through observable behavioral variables.

Scenario 1 — Modality-specific swap. On a wire-side maze [PAGE 77], change the goal odor but preserve visual appearance (or vice versa). Prediction supporting perceptual expectancy: hunting occurs only when the violated modality matches the concurrent sensory channel (vision through wire sides). Veto prediction: if hunting occurs equally across modalities or only in the non-concurrent modality, veto the claim that concurrent goal stimuli drove the expectancy; the signature is a general novelty response, not a specific perceptual expectation.

Scenario 2 — Path-length confound on elevated track. Keep the goal object identical but insert a detour so concurrent goal stimuli remain present while arrival time shifts. Prediction: if disruption scales with arrival-time deviation \Delta t_{\text{arrival}} (seconds) rather than goal identity, veto goal-object expectancy; the animal tracks a temporal/motor expectancy. Reject if the Pearson correlation between \mathcal{D} and \Delta t_{\text{arrival}} has |r| > 0.7 while goal identity is orthogonal.

Scenario 3 — Asymptotic extinction test. After the legerdemain swap [PAGE 77], retrain for N = 100 trials and measure hunting path entropy per trial. Tolman [PAGE 77] claims disruption "disappeared." Veto prediction: if H[\text{path}] asymptotes above baseline rather than decaying to zero, the transience claim fails; persistent expectancy-habit interference or latent cognitive-map conflict is indicated. If H decays exponentially with \tau_{\text{ext}} < 10 trials, Tolman’s within-session transience is supported, but only for that session, not across weeks.
