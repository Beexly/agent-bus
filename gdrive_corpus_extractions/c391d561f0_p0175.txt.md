This regression is itself a second-order belief: agents act as if they believe other agents believe supply is fixed, which temporarily makes it self-fulfilling through speculative price pressure. Counter-trap: competitive arbitrage operates as a corrective loop. If agents overbid land because they expect scarcity, new entry on the extensive margin or substitution toward technical processes erodes the premium. The counter-trap is recursive because each round of arbitrage changes the information set, which changes expectations, which changes the next round of bids.

Clock: the model contains at least three nested time scales. The speculation clock runs in weeks to months: rapid entry and exit in land or resource concessions, driven by sentiment and psychological appeal. The appropriation clock runs in months to years: surveying, staking, development, and legal titling of new natural resources. The diffusion clock runs in years to decades: the spread of a patented or secret technical process across “the whole field of production.” A sound theory of mind must track how agents’ beliefs on the speculation clock are constrained by the slower appropriation and diffusion clocks. If a crisis hits on the speculation clock while the diffusion clock is still open (i.e., \(\beta > 0\)), the regression is temporary. If the diffusion clock is blocked (\(\beta = 0\)) or the physical supply is genuinely fixed at the intensive margin, the crisis habit regresses into a persistent rent claim. The exact breakdown of recursive equilibrium occurs when higher-order beliefs about uncertainty become common knowledge but no probability measure exists to anchor them—Knight’s non-quantifiable uncertainty producing reflexive panic rather than priced risk.</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

Physical layer veto: Knight’s claim that land is “produced” like any exchangeable good is physically vetoed at the intensive margin. A specific parcel has a fixed geographic location and cannot be replicated; improvements (drainage, leveling) are capital embedded in land, not new land itself. Biomechanical/human layer veto: The “discovery, appropriation, and development” of resources requires scarce human labor, cognition, and risk-bearing capacity. “Psychological appeal” is bounded by heterogeneous preferences and limited attention; it cannot be assumed to perfectly offset pecuniary differentials. When the labor of appropriation is itself the bottleneck, returns to resource owners include a scarcity rent to human effort, not merely a competitive return to capital.

Weather/environmental layer veto: Exogenous shocks (drought, flood, pestilence) introduce systemic uncertainty over yield that is not diversifiable across fields if the shock is covariate. Knight admits “a large speculative element” in real life; weather volatility is a primary source. When weather risk is non-insurable (no \(P\) on \(\Omega\)), land exhibits Knightian profit and speculation “on an especially large scale,” as [PAGE 1] puts it. Adjudication: Knight’s equal-return model survives only on the extensive margin with diversifiable idiosyncratic shocks; it is vetoed when the intensive margin dominates, when appropriation labor is scarce, or when weather-driven uncertainty is systemic and uninsurable. In those veto regimes, land-specific rents and speculative premiums re-emerge as structural, not transitional, phenomena.</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
