<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Three behavioral layers interact here: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock. Crisis habit regression is the defender’s stress response: when the ball-carrier is near, a defender may abandon assignment discipline and chase contact. Fractional tackles can reward proximity even when the defender is out of leverage or has been led into the contact window by the offense. The paper explicitly warns that “a defender might be in a contact window because they are being targeted by the offense” [PAGE 19]. That is the counter-trap: the offense can design a path or block release that invites a defender into the 1.5-yard radius, producing credit for the defender while the offense gains the tactical advantage. The ball-carrier can also counter-trap by cutting back into a defender’s proximity to induce a contact window, then recovering velocity; the paper notes that “a ball-carrier enters a window due to poor decision-making” [PAGE 19]. The metric’s equal-credit rule cannot distinguish a defender who actually halts forward motion from one who is merely present during a bait.

The clock layer is the 0.1 s sampling grid and the short window duration. The average window is 1.28 seconds [PAGE 8], so frame-level equal credit is a coarse clock: a defender who arrives at the last frame gets the same per-frame credit as one who sustains leverage across the whole window. Under play-clock pressure, defenders and ball-carriers make faster, less optimal decisions; the metric has no explicit clock, down, distance, or leverage term. Recursively, players anticipate the metric, coaches anticipate which defenders are credited, and analysts anticipate how coaches will game the contact window. A counter-trap against the metric is to reward assignment-correct defenders who force the ball-carrier away from the window without entering it. The paper’s own future-work direction—modeling frame-level tracking with “ghosting” techniques for baseline player behavior [PAGE 19]—is exactly a recursive theory-of-mind correction: compare observed contact to a counterfactual defender path that was not baited.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical, biomechanical, and weather layers can veto an unqualified “defender skill” interpretation even if the arithmetic is correct. The formal issue is that the metric attributes the entire observed velocity drop inside a proximity window to the defenders in that window. Decompose the observed end-zone velocity change as
\[
\Delta v_{ij}=v^{start}_{ij}-v^{end}_{ij}
=\Delta v^{def}_{ij}+\Delta v^{surface}_{ij}+\Delta v^{self}_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ij},
\]
where \(\Delta v^{def}\) is defender-induced slowing, \(\Delta v^{surface}\) is turf/weather/traction effects, \(\Delta v^{self}\) is voluntary ball-carrier deceleration or path choice, and \(\varepsilon\) is tracking/annotation error. The metric computes
\[
w_{ij}=\frac{\Delta v_{ij}}{v^{pre}_{ij}},
\]
so it assigns \(\Delta v^{surface}\) and \(\Delta v^{self}\) to the defenders whenever they occur inside a contact window. A cross-layer veto applies when
\[
\left|\Delta v^{surface}_{ij}+\Delta v^{self}_{ij}\right|
\ge \left|\Delta v^{def}_{ij}\right|
\]
or when the sign of the observed change disagrees with defender-induced slowing. In that regime, \(w_{ij}\) is a descriptive velocity-drop fraction, not a defensive-force measurement.

Biomechanically, velocity is not force, leverage, or pose. The paper concedes the 2D data do not establish actual contact [PAGE 18], and the contact definition is “player-agnostic” [PAGE 19]. A 240-pound ball-carrier’s momentum is different from a lighter ball-carrier’s, even at the same velocity; the paper notes momentum is “the product of its mass and velocity” [PAGE 20]. Because mass is constant within a play, replacing velocity with momentum leaves the ratio unchanged [PAGE 20], but that invariance does not make the metric a force metric across players. Weather and surface are not modeled at all; rain, snow, turf, and footing can produce velocity changes that are environmental rather than defensive. The correct adjudication is therefore conditional: the metric is admissible as a within-play, proximity-conditioned slowing proxy; it is vetoed as a standalone measure of tackling skill, force, or contact quality unless paired with pose/contact data, mass/momentum features, assignment/leverage context, and weather/surface controls.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
