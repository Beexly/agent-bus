The core derivation in Olson is a multi-hop inversion of the received group theory. The first hop is definitional: a common interest is not merely a sum of private interests; it is a good whose consumption cannot feasibly be withheld from members of the relevant group. Olson states: “A common, collective, or public good is here defined as any good such that, if any person X.” in a group X1 ... Xn consumes it, it cannot feasibly be withheld from the others in that group” [PAGE 27]. The second hop is behavioral: if the good is non-excludable, a rational member receives the benefit whether or not he bears cost. Olson’s central claim is that “unless the number of individuals in a group is quite small, or unless there is coercion or some other special device to make individuals act in their common interest, rational, self-interested individuals will not act to achieve their common or group interests” [PAGE 15]. The third hop is the formal individual calculus: let \(T\) be the rate or level of the collective good in good-units per period, \(S_g\) the group’s value per unit of \(T\) in monetary units, \(F_i\) the dimensionless fraction of group gain captured by individual \(i\), \(V_g=S_gT\) the total group value in monetary units, \(V_i=F_iS_gT\) the individual value, and \(C(T)\) total cost in monetary units. Individual advantage is \(A_i=V_i-C\), and the independent optimum satisfies \(dA_i/dT=F_iS_g-dC/dT=0\) [PAGE 36]. The fourth hop is the provision threshold: “the collective or group good will presumably be provided if \(F_i &gt; C/V_g\)” [PAGE 37], equivalently \(V_i &gt; C\). The fifth hop is group-size translation: with equal-sized members, \(F_i=1/N\), so the threshold becomes \(1/N &gt; C/V_g\), or \(N &lt; V_g/C\). As \(N\to\infty\), \(F_i\to0\) and the condition fails. That is the formal engine behind Olson’s statement that “the larger the group, the less it will further its common interests” [PAGE 49].

The gray-zone audit must distinguish three group regimes. A “privileged” group has at least one member for whom \(F_i &gt; C/V_g\) at the relevant level, so unilateral provision is possible. An “intermediate” group has no such member but is small enough that one member’s action is noticeable to others; here Olson says the result is indeterminate because strategic interaction and bargaining matter [PAGE 56-57]. A “latent” group is so large that no member’s contribution is perceptible; absent coercion or selective incentives, no voluntary contribution is predicted. Olson’s taxonomy is explicit: “Only a separate and ‘selective’ incentitle will stimulate a rational individual in a latent group to act in a group-oriented way” [PAGE 64]. This is not a moral claim about selfishness; Olson insists the result holds “even if there is perfect consensus” [PAGE 73]. The audit must also flag that small groups are not optimal: the largest \(F_i\) member bears a disproportionate burden, producing “a systematic tendency for ‘exploitation’ of the great by the small” [PAGE 42]. The marginal-cost-sharing condition is exact: “The marginal cost of additional units of the collective good must be shared in exactly the same proportion as the additional benefits” [PAGE 44]. If not, provision is suboptimal. Organization costs add another hurdle: “costs of organization are an increasing function of the number of individuals in the group” [PAGE 60], and “the cost of the first unit of a collective good will be quite high in relation to the cost of some subsequent units” [PAGE 60]. Thus even a large total benefit may not clear the first-unit cost. The breakdown regimes are therefore: \(N\to\infty\) with \(F_i\to0\); \(C/V_g\) rising above every \(F_i\); \(K(N)\) organization cost exceeding every member’s private gain; noticeability falling below the threshold at which others react; selective incentive value falling below the individual cost share; and physical/legal vetoes that make contribution or strike action impossible. Olson’s empirical chapters test these regimes: unions rely on compulsory membership and picket lines [PAGE 81-84]; farm organizations rely on county agents, cooperatives, and insurance [PAGE 149-158]; professional associations rely on subtle coercion and noncollective services [PAGE 137-140]; business lobbies exploit small-N industries [PAGE 143-145]. The gray zone is the by-product claim: it is powerful for latent groups but not a universal law of all organizations, and Olson concedes that non-economic, religious, philanthropic, and “lost cause” groups are less tractable [PAGE 172-173].

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
1. Public-good operator. Olson’s definition is non-excludability, not pure non-rivalry: “A common, collective, or public good is here defined as any good such that, if any person X.” in a group X1 ... Xn consumes it, it cannot feasibly be withheld from the others in that group” [PAGE 27]. Units: the good is measured in good-units per period; exclusion is a binary feasibility condition. Assumption: the relevant group is specified. Breakdown: if exclusion becomes feasible or cheap, the good behaves as a private good and the free-rider result weakens. Olson explicitly notes that “jointness of supply” is not necessary by his definition [PAGE 27, footnote 21].

2. Individual advantage. Let \(T\) be the rate or level of the collective good, \(S_g\) the group’s value per unit of \(T\) in monetary units, \(F_i\) the dimensionless fraction of group gain received by \(i\), \(V_g=S_gT\), \(V_i=F_iS_gT\), and \(C(T)\) total cost in monetary units. Then
\[
A_i(T)=V_i(T)-C(T)=F_iS_gT-C(T).
\]
The independent first-order condition is
\[
\frac{dA_i}{dT}=F_iS_g-\frac{dC}{dT}=0
\]
[PAGE 36]. Units: monetary units per unit of \(T\). Assumption: \(F_i\) and \(S_g\) constant; independent behavior; no strategic reaction. Breakdown: if \(F_i\) or \(S_g\) varies with \(T\), Olson’s footnote gives
\[
F_iS_g+F_iT\frac{dS_g}{dT}+S_gT\frac{dF_i}{dT}-\frac{dC}{dT}=0
\]
[PAGE 36, footnote 41]. If \(dC/dT=0\), the first-order condition degenerates; if \(S_g=0\), no provision is predicted.

3. Provision threshold. At the individual optimum, provision is presumed if
\[
F_i&gt;\frac{C}{V_g}
\]
[PAGE 37], equivalently \(V_i&gt;C\). Units: dimensionless fraction compared with a dimensionless cost-benefit ratio. Assumption: the individual is deciding whether any amount is worth providing. Breakdown: if \(C/V_g\ge1\), no member gains from total provision; if \(C=0\), the condition is trivially satisfied; if \(V_g\to\infty\) with finite \(C\), the threshold approaches zero and even small \(F_i\) can satisfy it.

4. Equal-size group-size threshold. If all members are equal, \(F_i=1/N\). Then
\[
\frac{1}{N}&gt;\frac{C}{V_g}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad N&lt;\frac{V_g}{C}.
\]
Thus the critical group size is \(N^*=V_g/C\). Units: \(N\) is a count; \(V_g/C\) is dimensionless. Breakdown: as \(N\to\infty\), \(F_i\to0\), so the inequality fails for any positive \(C/V_g\). This is the formal core of Olson’s large-group result [PAGE 15, PAGE 49]. If members are unequal, the relevant condition is \(\max_i F_i&gt;C/V_g\), not average \(F_i\).

5. Group optimum and suboptimality. The group optimum requires
\[
\frac{dV_g}{dT}=\frac{dC}{dT}.
\]
Voluntary independent provision generally stops short because each member sets \(F_i(dV_g/dT)=dC/dT\). Olson’s exact condition for optimal voluntary provision is that marginal costs be shared in the same proportion as marginal benefits [PAGE 44]. Units: monetary units per unit of \(T\). Breakdown: any mismatch produces suboptimality; the largest \(F_i\) member bears a disproportionate share, giving “exploitation” of the great by the small [PAGE 42]. The moral overtones are explicitly disclaimed by Olson [PAGE 42, footnote 47].

6. Cournot/oligopoly mapping. For a homogeneous industry with zero marginal production cost, let \(T\) be price, \(S_g\) industry sales volume, \(S_i\) firm \(i\)’s sales, \(F_i=S_i/S_g\), and \(E\) the absolute elasticity of demand. Olson derives
\[
F_iS_g+T\frac{dS_g}{dT}=0,\qquad \frac{dS_g}{dT}=-\frac{ES_g}{T},
\]
so
\[
S_g(F_i-E)=0.
\]
“This can happen only when \(F_i=E\)” [PAGE 38]. Units: \(E\) and \(F_i\) are dimensionless; \(S_g\) is volume. Assumption: independent output decisions, no reaction. Breakdown: if \(E\) is large relative to \(F_i\), no firm restricts output; if \(F_i=E\), the firm is at the margin of group-oriented action. Olson’s example: with a thousand equal firms, \(E\) would have to be \(1/1000\) or less before output restriction [PAGE 38].

7. Organization cost. Olson argues that organization costs rise with group size and that the first unit of a collective good is disproportionately expensive [PAGE 60]. A formal reconstruction is
\[
C_{\text{total}}(T,N)=C_0(T)+K(N),\qquad K'(N)&gt;0,
\]
with provision requiring
\[
\max_i\left[F_iS_gT-C_0(T)-K(N)\right]&gt;0.
\]
Units: monetary units. Assumption: \(K(N)\) is the cost of communication, bargaining, staffing, and maintaining agreement. Breakdown: if \(K(N)\) exceeds every member’s private gain, no organization forms even if total group benefit exceeds total cost. This is a hard veto, not a preference.

8. Selective incentives and coercion. Olson defines selective incentives as values larger in absolute magnitude than an individual’s share of the cost of the collective good [PAGE 64, footnote 72]. Let \(b_i\) be the private benefit of joining and \(c_i\) the individual cost share, both in monetary or utility-equivalent units. Mobilization requires
\[
b_i&gt;c_i
\]
for enough members, or coercion such that the punishment leaves the non-contributor on a lower indifference curve than contribution. Breakdown: if \(b_i\le c_i\), the latent group remains unmobilized. Olson’s examples include union insurance, seniority rights, grievance processing, professional journals, malpractice defense, farm cooperative patronage dividends, and veterans’ club facilities [PAGE 72-75, PAGE 139-140, PAGE 154-156, PAGE 172-173].

9. Noticeability and information. Olson says the key test is “whether the individual actions of any one or more members in a group are noticeable to any other individuals in the group” [PAGE 58]. An operationalization is to define mutual information \(I_{ij}=I(a_i;a_j)\) in bits between member \(i\)’s action and member \(j\)’s reaction, with threshold \(\theta\). If \(I_{ij}&lt;\theta\), no strategic reaction occurs and the group is latent. Units: bits. Assumption: noticeability can be improved by group arrangements, but Olson doubts much can be done beyond information improvement [PAGE 58, footnote 67]. Breakdown: if \(I_{ij}\ge\theta\) for enough pairs, intermediate-group strategic interaction becomes possible; if not, only selective incentives or coercion work.

10. By-product operator. Olson’s by-product claim is: “The large and powerful economic lobbies are in fact the by-products of organizations that obtain their strength and support because they perform some function in addition to lobbying for collective goods” [PAGE 145]. Formally, let \(O\) be a nonpolitical organization that supplies selective benefit \(b_i\), and let \(L\) be its lobbying output. The claim is that \(L\) is sustained by membership recruited through \(b_i\) or coercion, not by the collective benefit of \(L\) alone. Units: membership count, dues revenue, lobbying expenditure. Breakdown: the by-product theory applies to latent groups, not to privileged or intermediate groups that can lobby directly [PAGE 145]. It also does not explain purely political large organizations with no selective incentives, which Olson predicts will be weak or unstable [PAGE 163-165].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Olson’s argument has a long lineage. He begins with Aristotle’s claim that political association exists for “general advantages” [PAGE 19], but he radicalizes it: common advantage does not imply common action. Hume is cited for the contrast between two neighbors draining a meadow and a thousand persons trying to agree [PAGE 46], a precursor of the privileged/latent distinction. Cournot supplies the market analogue: a firm restricts output only when its share \(F_i\) is at least as large as demand elasticity \(E\) [PAGE 38-39]. Wicksell supplies the public-finance analogue: the taxpayer “will obviously pay nothing whatsoever for public purposes” because his payment affects the scope of public services too slightly to notice [PAGE 113]. Marx supplies the class analogue: classes are defined by property relations and common economic interests, but Olson argues that “class-oriented action will not occur if the individuals that make up a class act rationally” [PAGE 119]. Bentley, Truman, and Latham supply the pressure-group analogue: they assume that group interests will organize into effective pressure, but Olson identifies an “anarchistic fallacy” in that assumption [PAGE 144]. Commons supplies the institutional-economics analogue: pressure groups are celebrated as a more representative “occupational parliament” [PAGE 128-129]. The Webbs supply the union analogue: compulsory unionism is “coeval with trade unionism itself” [PAGE 81]. Perlman supplies the “job consciousness” target: Olson argues that union job control reflects organizational survival, not worker pessimism [PAGE 90-97]. Schattschneider and Key supply the empirical puzzle: business pressure politics is “essentially the politics of small groups” [PAGE 157-158], while consumers, migrants, and taxpayers remain unorganized [PAGE 165-166].

The multi-week traps are persistent because they are intuitively plausible. Trap one: treating a group interest as if it were an individual incentive. Olson’s reply is the \(F_i&gt;C/V_g\) threshold: a group can gain while every individual loses from contributing. Trap two: assuming crisis or “suffering” produces organization. Truman’s claim that “suffering and dislocation” inevitably produce associations [PAGE 136] is contradicted by Olson’s latent-group logic and by the fact that association formation has been highest in prosperous, stable periods [PAGE 136-137]. Trap three: invoking “potential groups” that would organize if seriously threatened. Olson notes this is “rather difficult to test empirically” because failure can always be explained by saying the threat was not serious [PAGE 141]. Trap four: treating the by-product theory as a conspiracy theory. It is structural: organizations recruit through selective benefits or coercion, and lobbying is a by-product [PAGE 145]. Trap five: moralizing “exploitation” in small groups. Olson explicitly says the moral overtones are “unfortunate” and that no general moral conclusions follow from the logical analysis [PAGE 42, footnote 47]. Trap six: treating the union shop as a modern invention. The Webbs quote is decisive: compulsory trade unionism is “coeval with trade unionism itself” [PAGE 81]. Trap seven: assuming the state can be financed voluntarily. Olson’s analogy is that even the national state, with patriotism and ideology, “cannot support itself through voluntary dues or contributions” [PAGE 27]. Trap eight: the anarchistic fallacy, the belief that once coercive institutions are removed, spontaneous voluntary organization will arise [PAGE 144]. Each trap survives because it confuses a functional need with an individual incentive; Olson’s genealogy replaces function with mechanism.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In a privileged group, recursive reasoning is strategic but bounded: each member knows that at least one member has \(F_i&gt;C/V_g\), so each waits for that member to provide. The large member anticipates that others will free-ride and may still provide because his private gain exceeds total cost, but he will stop short of the group optimum [PAGE 37-42]. In an intermediate group, each member knows that others notice his action, so he models their reactions; Olson says the result is indeterminate because “the rational member of such a group faces a strategic problem” [PAGE 56]. In a latent group, recursive reasoning collapses. The thought “if I do not contribute, others will not either” is not credible when \(N\) is large because no one’s withdrawal is perceptible. Olson’s competitive-market analogy makes the point: it is “quite as reasonable to argue that prices will never fall below the levels a monopoly would have charged” because one firm fears triggering others, but in fact no firm notices the effect of one output change [PAGE 25]. The same holds for a large organization: “the loss of one dues payer will not noticeably increase the burden for any other one dues payer” [PAGE 25]. Thus the recursive loop terminates in a dominant free-ride.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency of leaders and analysts to assume that emergency produces solidarity. Olson’s model predicts the opposite unless the crisis also creates selective incentives or coercion. A strike crisis may increase the value of the collective good, but it also increases the individual cost of striking and the temptation to scab; hence unions rely on picket lines, union shops, and violence [PAGE 81-84]. The counter-trap is institutional design: create selective benefits that are contingent on membership. Examples include union insurance and seniority rights [PAGE 72-75], professional journals and malpractice defense [PAGE 139-140], farm cooperative patronage dividends [PAGE 154-156], and veterans’ club facilities [PAGE 172-173]. The clock matters because selective incentives must arrive at the right time. The first unit of a collective good is expensive [PAGE 60], strikes require workers to “forego his entire income until the employer comes to terms” [PAGE 80], and “maintenance of membership” was imposed during wartime to keep unions alive between contracts [PAGE 93]. A union that must wait for a strike fund to accumulate may lose the membership clock. A farm cooperative that pays patronage dividends at harvest can time the selective benefit to the moment of membership renewal. A professional association that publishes a monthly journal can meter the benefit continuously. The recursive theory of mind therefore predicts that successful latent-group organizations will be those that make selective incentives salient, immediate, and contingent, while failed ones will rely on the collective good alone. Olson’s perfect-consensus claim is the strongest version: even if everyone agrees on the goal and the means, the recursive incentive to free-ride remains [PAGE 73].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto proof begins with the physical communication layer. Olson states that the large latent group “always contains more people than could possibly know each other” [PAGE 76]. Face-to-face social sanctions are therefore physically unavailable at scale. This vetoes any argument that social pressure alone will mobilize a latent group, except in federated structures where small constituent groups can apply social incentives [PAGE 75-76]. The biomechanical/energetic layer vetoes strikes that cannot sustain biological needs: Olson notes that the strike “requires that each worker normally forego his entire income until the employer comes to terms” [PAGE 80]. If the strike fund is below subsistence, the strike collapses regardless of the justice of the cause. Picket lines are a physical enforcement technology: “strikebreakers are legally free to cross picket lines and make any strike ineffective” [PAGE 83], which is why violence and coercion have been prominent [PAGE 83-84]. The organizational/administrative layer vetoes latent-group action when \(K(N)\) exceeds the largest private gain. Olson’s first-unit cost argument [PAGE 60] is a hard budget constraint: if the cost of creating an organization is too high, no amount of collective benefit can be captured. The legal layer can either veto or enable: the Wagner Act allowed the closed shop and made representation elections easier [PAGE 92], while right-to-work laws are “seldom enforced” [PAGE 89]. The weather/seasonal layer is visible in agriculture: the National Farmers Organization tried to maintain prices by getting farmers to withhold output, but its “holding actions” failed [PAGE 172]. Perishability, harvest windows, storage limits, and weather shocks are physical vetoes on withholding. By contrast, weather risk increases the value of mutual insurance, which is exactly the selective benefit that Farm Bureau and Farmers Union organizations supplied [PAGE 156-158]. The adjudication is therefore not a single hierarchy but a veto sequence: if the physical/biological layer says no, the collective-action layer never operates; if the legal layer forbids coercion, selective incentives must carry the load; if selective incentives are absent, the latent group remains unmobilized. Olson’s theory is conditional on these lower layers being satisfied, and its predictions are strongest where the vetoes are absent.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
