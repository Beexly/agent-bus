The paper’s behavioral operator is signal detection theory. Let \(H\) be the hit rate and \(F\) the false-positive rate, each a probability in \([0,1]\). The paper defines: “The d′ is a measure of the difference between the signal and noise distributions, calculated as d′ = z(H) - z(F), where H is “hits” or correct identifications, and F is false positives, expressed in terms of their common standard deviation (z-units)” [PAGE 5]. Formally,
\[
d' = z(H)-z(F), \qquad d' \in \mathbb{R},
\]
where \(z(\cdot)=\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)\) is the inverse standard-normal CDF. The unit is dimensionless standard-deviation units. The criterion is given as: “The criterion position c is the midpoint of the normalized hits and false positives, c = −1/2[z(H) + z(F)]” [PAGE 5]. Thus
\[
c = -\frac{1}{2}\left[z(H)+z(F)\right], \qquad c \in \mathbb{R}.
\]
The likelihood-ratio bias is described as “calculated as ecd′, where cd′ = −1/2 [z(H)2−z(F)2]” [PAGE 5], i.e.
\[
\beta = e^{c d'} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[z(H)^2-z(F)^2\right]\right), \qquad \beta \in (0,\infty).
\]
These are dimensionless quantities. The assumptions are equal-variance Gaussian signal and noise distributions, independent trials, and stable response criteria within a cell. The singularity and breakdown regimes are exact: if \(H=0\), \(H=1\), \(F=0\), or \(F=1\), then \(z(H)\) or \(z(F)\) diverges to \(\pm\infty\), so \(d'\), \(c\), and \(\beta\) are undefined without a correction such as log-linear or 0.5/N adjustment; if the equal-variance assumption fails, \(d'\) is biased and \(d_a\) or a nonparametric sensitivity measure is required; if prior probabilities or payoffs shift, \(c\) and \(\beta\) change even when sensitivity is constant. The paper’s task-specific hit and false-positive definitions matter: for deception identification, \(H\) is the proportion of correct identifications of normal moves and \(F\) is the proportion of deceptive moves incorrectly identified as normal; for direction identification, \(H\) is the proportion of correct direction judgments on normal moves and \(F\) is the proportion of incorrect direction judgments on deceptive moves [PAGE 5]. The empirical values are reported as deception \(d'\) late \(M=1.46\) versus early \(M=0.59\), and direction \(d'\) late \(M=1.07\) versus early \(M=0.39\) [PAGE 5]. For deception bias, late-occluded \(\beta\) was \(M=1.7\), early-occluded \(M=0.39\) [PAGE 5]. For direction bias, late-occluded \(\beta\) was \(M=0.70\), early \(M=0.86\) [PAGE 6]. The paper also reports ANOVA F statistics with partial eta-squared, e.g. trial type \(F(1,48)=63.5\), \(p&lt;0.0005\), \(\eta_p^2=0.59\) [PAGE 4], and group \(F(2,48)=10.4\), \(p&lt;0.005\), \(\eta_p^2=0.32\) [PAGE 4]. Formally, \(F=MS_{\text{effect}}/MS_{\text{error}}\) and \(\eta_p^2=SS_{\text{effect}}/(SS_{\text{effect}}+SS_{\text{error}})\), both dimensionless; \(p\in[0,1]\) is a probability. Assumptions include normality, homogeneity of variance, independence, and sphericity for repeated measures; breakdown occurs with \(MS_{\text{error}}=0\), \(df_{\text{error}}=0\), severe non-normality, unequal variances, or sphericity violation. For fMRI, the paper states: “The selected design matrix convolved the experimental design with a hemodynamic response function to model the hemodynamic lag behind the neuronal response” [PAGE 4]. Formally, with task indicator \(u(t)\) and HRF \(h(t)\),
\[
x(t)=(u*h)(t),\qquad y(t)=x(t)\beta+e(t),\qquad e(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
\]
where \(y(t)\) is BOLD signal in arbitrary units or percent signal change, \(\beta\) is a BOLD amplitude parameter, and the contrast estimate \(c^\top\hat{\beta}\) is tested with a t statistic, dimensionless. The acquisition parameters are TR = 3000 ms, TE = 31 ms, flip angle = 90°, 41 transverse slices, 3 mm thickness, 3 mm³ voxels, 360 volumes, and 6 mm FWHM smoothing [PAGE 4]. The breakdown regimes are motion, low degrees of freedom, HRF misspecification, scanner drift, nonstationarity, collinearity, and reverse inference from a region to a single cognitive function. The paper itself warns that scanner video viewing omits “whole-body sensory-motor coupling, affordance-rich environment, and powerful contextual cues” [PAGE 10].

The crisis-habit regression pattern is visible in the early-occlusion data: “early-occluded deceptive moves gave rise to significantly worse than chance direction identification because lower-skilled players especially were not simply responding randomly but were misdirected by the deceptive cues” [PAGE 14]. That is a regression to a habitual stimulus-response mapping under uncertainty: the observer reads the step-over’s false directional cue as if it were honest. The counter-trap is the higher-skilled male bias toward calling moves deceptive: they “showed a bias toward identifying moves as deceptive” [PAGE 6], with late-occluded deception \(\beta\) mean 1.7 [PAGE 5]. This is a metacognitive overcorrection: expecting deception inflates deceptive detections but can reduce normal-move accuracy. The clock layer is temporal occlusion. Late occlusion improved deception \(d'\) from 0.59 to 1.46 and direction \(d'\) from 0.39 to 1.07 [PAGE 5]. The clock also includes the hemodynamic response: the design matrix is convolved with an HRF [PAGE 4], so the BOLD contrast integrates neural activity over seconds. The recursive observer must update an opponent model as the action unfolds: early honest and deceptive kinematics are ambiguous, later cues disambiguate, and the observer’s criterion may shift with time. The paper’s direct task contrast supports a partial dissociation: deception greater than direction in right DLPFC, medial frontal, anterior insula, posterior cingulate, and right parietal areas; direction greater than deception in ACC and caudate [PAGE 8; PAGE 13]. The interpretation is that deception identification recruits top-down control and social-affective processing, while direction identification recruits response-conflict and stimulus-response learning circuits [PAGE 12]. But the paper reports no significant task-by-group interaction in the fMRI ANOVA [PAGE 10], so the recursive mentalizing interpretation is a network-level inference, not a group-specific demonstration. The safest formulation is: the task demands an explicit intent classification under a deceptive cue, and the observed network pattern is consistent with that demand, but recursive ToM is not independently verified.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto adjudication asks which claims survive physical, biomechanical, environmental, and neurophysiological constraints. The physical layer: the stimuli are 2-s point-light videos of “three junior international male soccer players dribbling the ball toward a video camera” at 11.5 m in an indoor sports hall [PAGE 3]. The biomechanical layer: the actors performed “a deceptive maneuver known as a step-over in 50% of runs immediately prior to a direction change” [PAGE 3], represented by “15 small disc markers on principal body joints and extremities” with a white disc for the ball [PAGE 3]. There is no surface texture, depth, orientation, or color [PAGE 4]. The environmental layer: the study is indoors, so weather, wind, turf, lighting, crowd, and outdoor ball-flight conditions are not variables. Any claim about weather or outdoor field conditions is therefore vetoed by the design. The neurophysiological layer: 3T BOLD fMRI with TR = 3000 ms, TE = 31 ms, 3 mm³ voxels, 6 mm FWHM smoothing, and HRF convolution [PAGE 4] cannot resolve millisecond neural dynamics or single-neuron mirror properties. Any claim that the study “proves mirror neurons” is vetoed; the paper itself prefers AON over MNS for exactly this reason [PAGE 2]. The ecological layer: the observer is immobile in a scanner, and the paper concedes that “whole-body sensory-motor coupling, affordance-rich environment, and powerful contextual cues in soccer field play greatly exceed what is available to an immobile viewer of videos in a scanner” [PAGE 10]. Therefore, claims about real-time interceptive action, whole-body affordance, or outdoor deceptive play are vetoed as overgeneralizations. What survives is narrower: within an indoor, point-light, 2-s, three-actor, scanner-based paradigm, higher-skilled males show higher behavioral sensitivity to normal-versus-deceptive and directional cues, and the task contrast engages a broader AON/SN pattern. The step-over is only one deceptive maneuver, so generalization to all soccer deception is also vetoed. The male-actor/female-observer mismatch further vetoes any strong sex-difference claim; the paper’s own discussion of viewer-performer gender supports that caution [PAGE 9]. The correct adjudication is thus layer-specific: behavioral expertise effects survive within the sampled laboratory task; neural localization survives as BOLD association; ecological and weather generalizations do not survive.
