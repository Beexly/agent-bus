Fifth, interval estimates are censored: the caption states “Interval per magnitude level was calculated only for games in which &gt;2 head impacts of the respective magnitude was experienced during the same game” [PAGE 89]. Sixth, the BSE/T weights are ordinal heuristics: severity numbers 1–5 and interval weights 4–1 are assigned by category [PAGE 83], not derived from a physical conservation law. The audit therefore treats the profiles as comparative exposure phenotypes, not as calibrated dose units. The correct inference is conditional: within the source’s reconstruction protocol and category scheme, line positions are high-frequency/low-magnitude, skill positions are low-frequency/high-magnitude, and hybrid positions occupy the middle [PAGE 88][PAGE 90]. Any extension to absolute individual risk requires prospective biomarker, imaging, and clinical validation.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The source provides several formal operators. Equation (5) for the UCDBTM brain-tissue shear relaxation modulus is explicitly given as:

\[
G(t)=G_{\infty}+(G_0-G_{\infty})e^{-\beta t}
\]

where \(G_\infty\) is the long-term shear modulus, \(G_0\) is the short-term shear modulus, and \(\beta\) is the decay factor [PAGE 32]. The material tables list shear modulus in kPa, decay constant in s\(^{-1}\), and bulk modulus in GPa [PAGE 33]. Assumptions: brain tissue is modeled as linearly viscoelastic in shear, compression is elastic, and the CSF/brain interface uses solid elements with high bulk modulus and low shear modulus to permit sliding, with friction coefficient 0.2 and no separation [PAGE 32]. Singularity/breakdown: the equation has no finite-time singularity; as \(t\to\infty\), \(G(t)\to G_\infty\). If \(\beta=0\), no relaxation occurs; if \(G_0=G_\infty\), the model becomes elastic in shear. The equation breaks down as a description of axonal rupture because it is a continuum viscoelastic constitutive law, not a failure criterion; the source itself states the model is “a gross representation of the brain” [PAGE 130].

Maximum principal strain is described in the source as tissue elongation relative to original length along a principal axis [PAGE 30]. A standard continuum definition consistent with that wording is:

\[
\varepsilon_{\max}(\mathbf{x},t)=\max\{\varepsilon_1(\mathbf{x},t),\varepsilon_2(\mathbf{x},t),\varepsilon_3(\mathbf{x},t)\}
\]

where \(\varepsilon_i\) are principal strain values; the result is dimensionless or reported in percent. The source’s Eq. (4) is referenced but the OCR rendering does not expose the full algebraic form; the text says: “MPS is calculated as follows; (4) where, εx, εy and εz = strains measured along corresponding axes” [PAGE 30]. Assumptions: the principal-axis decomposition is meaningful at the element scale; strain is interpreted as tensile elongation. Breakdown: near material rupture, infinitesimal strain language is inadequate; the source uses finite-element output as relative severity, not literal tissue tolerance [PAGE 130].

CSDM is an integral volume-fraction operator:

\[
\mathrm{CSDM}_{\varepsilon^*}(t)=\frac{1}{V}\int_V \mathbf{1}\{\varepsilon_{\max}(\mathbf{x},t)\ge \varepsilon^*\}\,dV
\]

where \(\mathbf{1}\) is the indicator function, \(V\) is the modeled brain volume, and \(\varepsilon^*\) is a threshold such as 0.08 or 0.17. The source describes CSDM as identifying “a volume of brain tissue that exceeds a predetermined strain threshold” [PAGE 18] and reports a 0.55 brain-volume fraction at 15% strain for 50% concussion probability [PAGE 31], plus 0.09–0.23 brain volume at 10% CSDM for 50% injury probability in various regions [PAGE 31]. Units: dimensionless volume fraction. Assumptions: the threshold is sharp and the mesh resolves the strain field. Breakdown: the indicator is discontinuous at \(\varepsilon_{\max}=\varepsilon^*\), so CSDM is mesh-sensitive near threshold; the source warns that the model does not resolve sulcus versus gyrus [PAGE 130].

BSE/T is the source’s exposure operator. The source states: “An exposure metric, brain strain exposure per time (BSE/T), utilizing the three characteristics of trauma was created to quantify cumulative brain strain exposure for each field position throughout each game. This metric was calculated by multiplying the number of impacts within each brain tissue strain magnitude category by its severity # (very low =1; low =2; moderate= 3; high= 4; very high =5), then multiplied by the time interval in which those impacts were experienced (very low =4; low =3; moderate= 2; high =1)” [PAGE 83]. Formally:

\[
\mathrm{BSE/T}_{p,g}=\sum_{m}\sum_{\tau} N_{p,g}(m,\tau)\,S(m)\,W(\tau)
\]

where \(N_{p,g}(m,\tau)\) is the count of confirmed impacts for position \(p\) in game \(g\) in MPS magnitude category \(m\) and interval category \(\tau\); \(S(m)\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}\); and \(W(\tau)\in\{4,3,2,1\}\) [PAGE 83]. Units: dimensionless ordinal weighted count per game. Assumptions: ordinal categories may be arithmetically weighted; interval category is a valid proxy for recovery opportunity. Breakdown: if category boundaries are arbitrary or if interval censoring is severe, BSE/T is not an interval-scale physical dose. The source’s interval categories are: “very low = &lt;15min, low = 15-30min, moderate = 31-90min, high = 91min+” [PAGE 75]. The MPS categories are: “very low= &lt; 8%, low= 8-16.9%, moderate= 17-25.9%, high= 26-34.9%, very high= 35%+” [PAGE 82].

Impact-frequency outputs are counts per game and per 16-game season [PAGE 84]. The source reports 3,439 total documented head impacts for eight positions over 32 regular-season games, with 2,941 confirmed and 498 suspected [PAGE 84]. Event reconstruction used four primary event types: helmet, shoulder, hip/thigh, and ground [PAGE 76]. Velocity is in m/s; linear acceleration in g; rotational acceleration in rad/s\(^2\) [PAGE 24][PAGE 28]. Probability values are dimensionless proportions or percentages; the source reports 50% concussion risk at approximately 19–30% MPS in gray and white matter [PAGE 30].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retrocausal lineage runs from clinical boxing neurology to modern football neuropathology. The source traces “punch drunk” to Martland in 1928, “dementia pugilistica” to Millspaugh in 1937, “psychopathic deterioration of pugilists” to Courville in 1962, and Critchley’s 1949 “punch-drunk syndromes” to the modern term CTE [PAGE 26]. It also notes that “Brandenburg and Hallervorden, who demonstrated Alzheimer’s disease-like changes in a 51 years-old boxer, made the first published neuropathological report on CTE in 1954” [PAGE 26]. The modern football line begins with Omalu: “In 2002, Dr. Bennet Omalu [2005] discovered neurodegeneration in the brain of a retired ASF player, consistent with brain disease initially found in boxers” [PAGE 13]. McKee’s staging describes “four identified stages of progression including focal perivascular epicenters of hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau) initiating in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortices typically in clusters within sulcal depths, to profound tauopathy in some cases involving all regions of the brain” [PAGE 38]. Mez et al. reported that “87% of the examined brains of former football players were found positive for CTE” and that “99% of those who played in National Football League were positive for CTE,” while acknowledging selection bias [PAGE 43].

The multi-week trap is that clinical recovery and neurobiological recovery are not the same clock. The source reports that “NF-L levels tend to return to baseline much sooner from one concussive impact in hockey (7-8 days) as opposed to repeated impact with knockout from boxing, which have been reported to take as long as 36 weeks to return to baseline” [PAGE 67]. It also states that with cessation, “biomarkers generally return to normal at 8-12 wks in boxers” [PAGE 67]. In football, “levels remaining above baseline values for up to 9 weeks postseason” were observed in starters [PAGE 66]. Clearance may be impaired for “up to 28 days following an insult” [PAGE 68]. At the far end, prion-like tau propagation is slow: “even the most aggressive cases of disease, only expresses decades after the insult” [PAGE 40]. Thus the genealogy is not linear in symptom time: the exposure clock may be days to weeks, the biomarker clock weeks to months, and the pathological clock years to decades. Any policy that uses same-day symptom clearance as the recovery criterion is therefore retrocausally blind to the later pathology.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive decision problem has three layers. First-order: the athlete feels symptoms or does not; under crisis-habit regression, the athlete and staff default to the visible-symptom rule. The source states the historical habit: “only major traumatic events with associated symptoms are identified as ‘injuries’” [PAGE 12]. It also notes that “less than half are identified, either from reluctance to report, or the inability to detect the vast expression of possible signs” [PAGE 12]. Second-order: the coach or clinician models the athlete as “not injured” if symptoms are absent, but the athlete may be modeling the coach as likely to remove them from play. The source documents position-specific reporting behavior: offensive line positions “have been shown to experience a higher number of symptoms that were not reported, and returning to play while symptomatic more often compared to other teammate positions” [PAGE 55]. Third-order: league policy models the team’s incentive to keep players on the field, and the team models the league’s enforcement probability. The counter-trap is the assumption that symptom cessation equals neurobiological recovery: “as individuals recover from the clinical expression of symptoms, the assumption that neurobiological recovery has also occurred is also likely to be inaccurate” [PAGE 72]. The clock trap is that interval weighting rewards short gaps: “impacts are experienced on average every 6-30 minutes during game play” [PAGE 93], and OL/DL experienced the highest frequency of lowest-magnitude impacts “on average every 6-7 minutes” [PAGE 94]. In recursive terms, the athlete’s best response to a symptom-based rule is silence; the coach’s best response to silence is continued participation; the league’s best response to visible concussion only is a policy that misses sub-concussive exposure. The source’s BSE/T operator is an attempt to break that recursion by making magnitude, frequency, and interval explicit [PAGE 83]. The counter-trap is to replace one single metric with another single metric; the source instead reports three profiles: low-frequency/high-magnitude skill positions, medium-frequency/mixed positions, and high-frequency/low-magnitude line positions [PAGE 88][PAGE 90].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical veto. The source distinguishes focal from diffuse injury: “Translational energy produced focal injuries, such as skull fracture and contusion, but diffuse brain injury was achieved only if the head had been rotated” [PAGE 24]. Therefore a linear-acceleration-only clearance rule can be vetoed by a rotational/shear mechanism even when the linear value appears tolerable. Biomechanical veto. Holbourn’s shear argument is reproduced: shear strains within the brain are the main cause of injury, and rotation produces shear because neural tissue is nearly incompressible [PAGE 24]. The helmet layer is also vetoed for rotational protection: “Helmets currently used in contact/collision sports are not effective at attenuating rotational energy” [PAGE 64]. The source further notes that increased helmet-liner compliance “changed the risk from catastrophic injury (i.e. skull fracture, severe traumatic brain injury) to concussion and repetitive head impact exposure” [PAGE 64]. Thus a helmet that passes a linear drop test does not prove protection against the diffuse/rotational pathway. Environmental/surface veto. The source’s ground-event reconstruction uses “a monorail drop tower with turf anvil” to simulate ground contact [PAGE 68], and the general principle is stated: “The level of compliance between the impacting object and the impacted body will influence how energy is transmitted to the brain” [PAGE 60]. Weather is not directly modeled in the source; the defensible environmental proxy is surface compliance. A wet or frozen surface that changes turf compliance would alter acceleration duration and peak, and therefore alter strain, but the source does not provide weather data. The veto proof is therefore conditional: if surface compliance changes the acceleration time history, then linear peak alone is insufficient; the source supports this because “events considered low risk based on acceleration magnitude still resulted in high tissue stress and strains” [PAGE 60]. Cross-layer adjudication: physical layer can veto a biomechanical prediction if skull fracture dominates; biomechanical layer can veto a helmet claim if rotational energy is unattenuated; environmental layer can veto a ground-impact generalization if surface compliance differs from the reconstructed turf anvil. None of these layers is reducible to a single scalar.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR here means reward-verifiable counterfactual falsification: each scenario specifies a measurable dataset and a prediction that would veto the source’s mechanism if violated.

1. Regional-invariance veto. Prediction from the source: after matching on MPS magnitude and impact frequency, lobe-level strain rank should remain temporal first, frontal second, with parietal/occipital lower, and position should not add a significant main effect [PAGE 111]. Verifiable test: reconstruct a new professional or collegiate cohort, match positions on MPS category and frequency, and fit a mixed model with lobe rank as outcome and position as fixed effect. If position remains significant after matching, the claim that “distribution of strain throughout the brain lobes was not significant between positions based on exemplar impact conditions” [PAGE 111] is vetoed. Prediction: the position effect should vanish or become small; the temporal-frontal dominance should persist.

2. Low-strain high-frequency pathway veto. Prediction from the source: profile 3 positions, especially OL and DL, should accumulate high-frequency low-magnitude exposure, with most impacts below 17% strain, and this should still be biologically consequential over time [PAGE 92][PAGE 94]. Verifiable test: prospective cohort with video-confirmed impacts, FE-derived MPS, and pre/post-season DTI or plasma biomarkers; stratify by total low-magnitude count while controlling for high-magnitude count. If low-magnitude frequency has no dose-response after adjustment, the frequency-dominant mechanism for profile 3 is vetoed. Prediction: low-magnitude frequency should show a positive association with white-matter or biomarker change, even if weaker than high-magnitude exposure.

3. BSE/T outcome-alignment veto. Prediction from the source: profile 1, the low-frequency/high-magnitude skill-position group, should align with higher acute concussion incidence, while profile 3, the high-frequency/low-magnitude line group, should align with higher confirmed CTE burden [PAGE 93][PAGE 94]. Verifiable test: compute BSE/T in an independent league with linked injury and neuropathology outcomes; test whether profile membership predicts concussion versus CTE patterns. If BSE/T fails to separate these outcomes, the profiling tool’s clinical claim is vetoed. Prediction: profile 1 should show stronger acute concussion association; profile 3 should show stronger cumulative pathology association; profile 2 should show mixed risk [PAGE 90]. These three vetoes are measurable, pre-registerable, and independent of any single imaging modality.
