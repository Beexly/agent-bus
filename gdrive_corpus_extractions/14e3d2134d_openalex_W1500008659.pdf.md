**Hop 1 — Object identification.** The note proposes a contractual form, not a forecast: "the IBC is a contract a team can offer its disgruntled player that is based primarily on the player's on-field performance" [PAGE 3]. Any rigorous treatment must therefore model a *mechanism*, with an incentive-compatibility side (does the player sign?) and a *cap-feasibility* side (does the club's charge stay under the ceiling?). These are separate constraint sets and the note routinely runs them together — that conflation is the first gray zone.

**Hop 2 — Extracting the accounting layer.** The document supplies a complete, checkable cap identity. The cap is 63% of projected Defined Gross Revenue divided across 30 clubs; for 1995-96, "the salary cap was approximately $42.05 million per team" [PAGE 9], derived from a DGR "more than $2 billion" [PAGE 9]. Arithmetic check: 0.63 × $2.0×10⁹ / 30 = $42.0×10⁶, and the figure decomposes as "$37.1 million for salaries and $4.95 million for benefits" [PAGE 9, n.39] → $42.05M ✓. The identity is internally consistent to rounding. This matters because the whole IBC argument is an argument about the *classification* term inside that identity, not about the salary term.

**Hop 3 — The classification channel.** The mechanism's lever is the "likely to be earned" (LTE) test: bonuses count against the cap "based upon whether the player could have satisfied the same clauses in the prior year" [PAGE 10-11]. The drafter's problem is stated with unusual candor: "create incentives that are *not* 'likely to be earned' so that any money earned by the player will not be counted against the cap, yet still attractive enough so the player believes he can attain the incentive" [PAGE 11]. That is a one-bit information channel (LTE / not-LTE) gating a continuous dollar quantity. Every design pathology in the paper descends from that quantization.

**Hop 4 — The polarization argument.** The doc's example: Barry Sanders at $5 million for 2000 rushing yards is cap-cheap but participation-infeasible, because "only two running backs have rushed for 2000 or more yards in a season" [PAGE 11, n.59]; Sanders counters at 1300 yards for $2.5 million. So the design problem is a bargaining frontier: the club wants maximum platform height (λ=0), the player wants maximum platform probability. Their intersection is where the platform sits just above the historical maximum — i.e., exactly where the estimate is least stable. This is a thin-sample extrapolation problem dressed as a drafting problem.

**Hop 5 — Auditing the central causal claim.** The note's thesis is that the IBC reduces midterm demands. But its own screening analysis says the opposite population: "once a player achieves 'superstar status,' he would never realistically sign a contract requiring him to perform as a superstar year in and year out" [PAGE 39]. Therefore the mechanism operates on the *extensive margin* — who is willing to demand renegotiation — not on the *intensity* of demands by those who do. The claimed deterrence and the claimed screening are the same effect described twice. That is not fatal, but it means the conclusion at [PAGE 43] ("many will be dissuaded from making such demands") is a selection statement and should not be read as a behavioral claim about superstars.

**Hop 6 — Reflexivity.** The doc concedes the general-equilibrium channel without modeling it: "Because there can only be a few elite players, salaries on average will drop" [PAGE 43]. If average pay falls, every player's outside option falls, which mechanically raises IBC acceptance — a positive feedback on adoption that the paper reports as a benefit to clubs without noting it also shrinks the pool the mechanism was designed to screen.

Internal tension, unresolved in the corpus.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

**Notation.** $t$ = League Year; $i$ = player; $y$ = realized statistic (yards, receptions, plays, games); $\theta_k$ = platform threshold; $b_k$ = payout at platform $k$; $\lambda_k \in \{0,1\}$ = LTE classifier; $w_i$ = base salary; $s_i$ = signing bonus; $n_i$ = contract length in League Years.

**(E1) Hard-cap identity** (CBA art. XXIV, per [PAGE 8-9]):

$$\mathrm{Cap}_t \;=\; \frac{\rho\,\mathrm{DGR}_t}{N},\qquad \rho = 0.63,\; N = 30$$

$$\mathrm{Charge}_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{s_i}{n_i} + w_{i,t} + r_{i,t} + \sum_k \lambda_{ik}\,b_{ik}, \qquad \sum_i \mathrm{Charge}_{i,t} + B_t \;\le\; \mathrm{Cap}_t$$

*Units:* USD per (team · League Year). *Check:* $0.63 \times 2.0\times10^{9} / 30 = 4.2\times10^{7}$, versus the stated "$42.05 million per team" [PAGE 9]. *Breakdown regimes:* (i) $\sum_i \mathrm{Charge} = \mathrm{Cap}$ — binding, zero residual room, all IBC negotiation must be cap-neutral; (ii) $\mathrm{Charge} > \mathrm{Cap}$ — infeasible, penalty carries forward: "the amount by which the team exceeds the cap is deducted from the team's salary cap for the next League year" [PAGE 10, n.58]; (iii) the corpus does not state a floor of zero on the carried deduction — **open parameter**, do not assume.

**(E2) LTE classifier as an OR-gate on two sufficient conditions** ([PAGE 10-11] and n.56):

$$\lambda_k = \mathbb{1}\Big[\underbrace{\exists\, \text{prior-year window satisfying clause } k}_{\text{historical satisfiability}} \;\lor\; \underbrace{\text{clause within sole player control}}_{\text{volitional}}\Big]$$

*Units:* 1 bit per clause. *Breakdown:* the first disjunct makes $\lambda_k$ a function of *last year's* realized $y$, so a career year mechanically converts next year's incentive into a cap charge — the "one-year wonder" cap trap. The second disjunct ("non-guaranteed reporting bonuses, off-season workout and weight bonuses") is deemed LTE categorically [PAGE 10, n.56], which kills the reporting-bonus maneuver the note proposes at [PAGE 30].

**(E3) Ex-post reconciliation.** If $\sum_k \lambda_{ik} b_{ik} < \sum_k \mathbb{1}[y \ge \theta_k]\,b_{ik}$, the excess is charged against the following League Year. *Units:* USD. *Singularity:* a single mid-season break-out (Esiason's 522-yard game, [PAGE 41]) can move a season total across a platform and convert a $\lambda=0$ clause into realized spend, producing a cap shock one year later with no contemporaneous signal.

**(E4) Incentive intensity — inverse-hazard (Holmström–Milgrom sufficient statistic).** For a risk-neutral principal, CARA agent, first-order approach, and density $f(y\mid e)$ satisfying MLRP:

$$w'(y) \;=\; \frac{\psi'(e)}{\partial_e \ln f(y\mid e)} \;=\; \psi'(e)\,\frac{f(y\mid e)}{f_e(y\mid e)}$$

*Units:* USD per unit of the statistic $y$. $\psi(\cdot)$ = agent's cost of effort, USD. *Assumptions that must be stated and are **violated** here:* (i) $y$ is a sufficient statistic for $e$ — false in football, since $y$ depends on linemen (the $10,000-per-lineman Sanders clause [PAGE 15]) and on coaching decisions; (ii) $f_e \ne 0$ — false at saturated platforms; (iii) no multi-task substitution — false, since chasing 1000 yards trades off against pass protection. *Breakdown regimes:* $f_e \to 0$ ⇒ $w' \to \infty$ ⇒ unbounded slope ⇒ cap-infeasible; $y$ uninformative about $e$ ⇒ $w'$ unidentifiable ⇒ the contract requires third-party adjudication, which is exactly the note's remedy: "the team should reciprocate by adding a provision to the IBC calling for an independent arbitrator to govern any and all disputes over the incentive provisions" [PAGE 40].

**(E5) Platform-cliff measure.** With $C(y) = w_{\min} + \sum_k b_k \mathbb{1}[y \ge \theta_k]$:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial y} = 0 \;\; \text{a.e.},\qquad \Delta C\big|_{\theta_k} = b_k - b_{k-1}$$

*Units:* USD jump at a point in yard-space. *Purpose:* this is the formal object the note's staggered-bin remedy addresses — "in any three games, Player shall receive a bonus of $10,000; in any four to seven games, $20,000; and for any eight or more games, $50,000" [PAGE 32] — bounding each $\Delta C$ so that a first-play injury does not zero the contract. Note honestly: staggering bounds the *jump*, not the *expected value*.

**(E6) Screening condition (single crossing).** Let $\theta$ index productivity, $O(\theta)$ the front-loaded market alternative, $E[C\mid\theta]$ the IBC expectation:

$$\text{accept} \iff E[C\mid\theta] \;\ge\; O(\theta);\qquad \text{separation at } \theta^{*} \text{ iff } \frac{dO}{d\theta} > \frac{dE[C]}{d\theta}\ \forall \theta$$

*Units:* USD. *Source support:* "once a player achieves 'superstar status,' he would never realistically sign a contract requiring him to perform as a superstar year in and year out" [PAGE 39] is precisely the claim $dO/d\theta > dE[C]/d\theta$ at high $\theta$.

**(E7) Injury participation constraint.** With per-year season-ending hazard $q$ and injured-state payout $G$:

$$\mathbb{E}[U] = (1-q)\,E[C\mid \text{healthy}] + q\,G - \pi \;\ge\; U_0$$

*Units:* USD (utility valued in certainty-equivalent dollars). *Anchors:* "$<5\%$ have injury guarantees" [PAGE 18]; average career "approximately five years" [PAGE 18]; CBA injury protection capped at $200,000 for 1997-99 [PAGE 18, n.117]. *Breakdown:* if $G = 0$ and $q$ is non-trivial, the IBC is rejected by any risk-averse player, which is why the note concedes "it would be unconscionable to compel the player to bear the entire risk of a season-ending injury" [PAGE 33].

**(E8) Cap wedge (definition, not a toy ratio).**

$$\Delta = E[C] - \mathrm{Charge} = \underbrace{\big(s - s/n\big)}_{\text{proration timing}} - \underbrace{\sum_k(\lambda_k - \mathbb{1}[y\ge\theta_k])b_k}_{\text{classification error}}$$

Rhett instance: $\Delta = \$1{,}125{,}000 - \$50{,}000 = \$1{,}075{,}000$, i.e. 96% timing, 4% classification [PAGE 37-38].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

Three lineages converge on this instrument, and each carries a multi-week trap.

**Lineage I — the mid-performance holdout doctrine.** *Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico* (9th Cir. 1902), cited at [PAGE 6, n.16], is structurally identical to the modern holdout: performance already begun, demanded increase, coercion, no unforeseen circumstance. The note's escape hatch is Restatement §89 (modification for unanticipated circumstances), flagged at [PAGE 2, n.1] — but as the note itself notes, §73's drafters failed "to distinguish between the situation in which the promisor demands more money because of opportunism and greed and the situation in which the demand is prompted by the occurrence of some unforeseen circumstances" [PAGE 6, n.20]. *Multi-week trap:* the legal question and the commercial question run on different clocks — the holdout is resolved in 94 days (Rhett, [PAGE 26, n.175]) while the doctrine resolves over years.

**Lineage II — the free-agency succession.** Reserved clause → option clause → Rozelle rule → *Freeman McNeil* → Plan B → *White v. National Football League*, 822 F. Supp. 1389 (D. Minn. 1993), *aff'd*, 41 F.3d 402 (8th Cir. 1994), whose settlement "became the blueprint for the 1993-2000 National Football League-NFLPA Collective Bargaining Agreement" [PAGE 8, n.31]. *Multi-week trap:* free agency arrived in 1993 but was immediately traded away for the hard cap — "in order to secure free agency for its members, the players union was forced to accept many restrictive provisions" [PAGE 8]. The IBC sits inside that trade, so it prices *cap space*, not market freedom.

**Lineage III — the incentive-clause drafting tradition.** From Greenberg's Harvard negotiation-seminar examples [PAGE 34] — "Ex. 10. Player shall receive the additional sum of \$___ if he gains 1,000 yards during the regular season. An extremely common mistake. One thousand yards by what means?" — through leader bonuses to escalators. The lineage is a record of *definitional* failure, not economic failure, and it is the reason the note names ambiguous drafting "the most prevalent problem with player contracts" [PAGE 31].

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

**Level 0 — player belief about own production.** Marvin Jones: "This is the year for me to go out and show people what I haven't been able to do from being hurt" [PAGE 25] — a self-model that the club's record contradicts.

**Level 1 — club belief about player type.** The Jets "would not discuss a new contract until after the 1996 season" [PAGE 26]; five weeks in they were 0-5 and Jones was on the disabled list five to six weeks with a sprained right knee [PAGE 26, n.175]. The club's level-1 estimate was correct; the player's was not.

**Level 2 — player belief about club belief about leverage.** "The worst case scenario is a rejection by the team, as the New York Jets did with Marvin Jones" [PAGE 28]. This is the note's own statement of the off-equilibrium payoff: under standard renegotiation the player's downside is bounded at rejection, so demanding is weakly dominant. The IBC is designed to break that dominance by giving the demand a *negative* payoff through the base-salary reset to the minimum [PAGE 30].

**Level 3 — owner belief about coach behavior about player incentives.** The deepest loop in the document, and the authors concede it: "what assurances does the player have that his team's owner will not order the coach to keep him out of a game in order to prevent the player from reaching a higher incentive platform?" [PAGE 40]. The remedy proposed is a third-party adjudicator. Formally this is a Stackelberg game in which the principal controls the agent's *action set* after the contract is signed — the incentive slope (E4) is meaningless when the principal can zero $f_e$.

**Crisis-habit regression.** Under stale pay, the player's default heuristic regresses to the pre-1993 behavior set — withholding performance — even though withholding is now heavily fined and cap-constrained. The document records the regression precisely: Rhett's 94-day holdout netted *no* renegotiation [PAGE 26, n.175], yet the behavior recurs. Habit outlives the institution that made it rational.

**Counter-trap.** The IBC is itself a counter-trap: it converts the player's only weapon into his own penalty, since the very act of demanding renegotiation triggers the base-salary reset to "the league's minimum base salary ... until the expiration date of the original contract" [PAGE 30]. Note the trap is not information-asymmetric — both sides know the rule — it is *commitment*-asymmetric.

**Clock dominance over both.** None of the belief layers bind if the clock binds first. Rhett's holdout was decided not by beliefs but by 94 days, $480,000 in fines, and a team that had "lost six of seven games" [PAGE 35]. The belief recursion is decorative relative to the calendar.
