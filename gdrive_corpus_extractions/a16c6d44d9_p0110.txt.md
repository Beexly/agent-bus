The production function for joint output $Q$ involving factors $x_1, ..., x_n$ is defined as $Q = F(x_1, ..., x_n)$. Under the "arbitrary specifications" [PAGE 95], we assume:
1. Continuous creation: $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t} = \text{constant}$.
2. No material productive property (no capital accumulation/depreciation): Assets are either superabundant ($\mu \to \infty$, shadow price $\lambda = 0$) or rigidly attached to persons ($x_i \equiv L_i$).
3. Absence of efficiency change: $\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}$ is constant with respect to time and usage.

In this idealized static state, the problem of "imputation" reduces to solving the Lagrangian for utility maximization subject to resource constraints:
$\mathcal{L} = U(Q) - \sum_{i=1}^n p_i (x_i - \bar{x}_i)$
However, Knight notes that reality involves joint production practically without exception. When jointness is introduced, the marginal product of factor $i$ depends on the quantity of factor $j$, i.e., $\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} \neq 0$. 
We define the Jointness Entropy $H_J$ as the uncertainty in allocating the total value $V$ among factors due to this cross-partial dependency:
$H_J = - \sum_{i} P(V_i | V_{total}) \log P(V_i | V_{total})$
Where $P(V_i | V_{total})$ is the probability distribution of factor payments given the joint product. In the strict static state with no durable goods, $H_J = 0$ because the valuation is deterministic based on current marginal utilities. However, once "productive property" is introduced (breaking assumption 2), the valuation requires discounting future flows, introducing Knightian Uncertainty. The breakdown regime occurs when the mutual information between the factors exceeds the capacity of the pricing mechanism to separate their individual contributions, necessitating an entrepreneur who absorbs the residual variance (profit).
Assumptions: Factors are continuously divisible; markets are perfectly competitive; preferences are transitive. Breakdown: If $\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$ exhibits increasing returns to scale (violating the static concavity requirement), the equilibrium solution ceases to exist, and monopolistic or organizational structures emerge to handle the coordination failure.

Specifically, the term "imputation" (Zurechnung) directly references the work of Friedrich von Wieser and the Viennese Marginalists, who attempted to derive factor prices by retroactively attributing the value of the final consumer good to its constituent elements. Knight is engaging in a genealogical critique here: while Wieser provided the mathematical apparatus for imputation, he operated within a closed, certain system. Knight pushes this lineage forward by demonstrating that the very act of organizing these factors "through free contract alone" requires bridging a temporal gap that pure mathematics cannot span. Historically, this page marks the pivot where neoclassical economics confronts the messy reality of the firm. The "multi-week trap" in modern economics stems from ignoring this pivot; contemporary asset pricing models (like CAPM) attempt to solve the "division of a joint product" using purely statistical covariances (beta), effectively pretending that Knight's "uncertainty" can be reduced to measurable "risk." They fall into the trap of believing that if they just add enough variables to the static state, they can eliminate the need for entrepreneurship. Knight’s text serves as a retrocausal veto against this reductionist path, reminding us that the mechanism of organization is fundamentally rooted in human judgment under conditions where probabilities are unknowable.
