<THINK_TRACE>
The source is a 2013 UNC–Chapel Hill MA thesis by Simon J. Haake examining salivary IL-6 and cortisol across a 6-week off-season resistance-training block in 20 Division-I American football athletes. The core multi-hop chain is: high-intensity resistance training at 85–100% 1RM [PAGE 3] produces repeated muscular contraction; contracting skeletal muscle releases IL-6; IL-6 feeds forward through the HPA axis to increase CRH/ACTH-dependent cortisol; cortisol in turn suppresses IL-6 mRNA and inflammatory signaling. If this loop is repeatedly stressed without adequate recovery, the cytokine hypothesis predicts chronically elevated resting IL-6 and cortisol, negative affect, fatigue, and performance decrement. The thesis tests a weaker, longitudinal version: do resting salivary IL-6 and cortisol rise from Week 1 to Week 6, and do they correlate within sampling weeks? The answer is mixed-but-informative: cortisol rose significantly by Week 6, IL-6 rose only after log10 transformation, correlations were significant at Week 1 and Week 6 but not Week 4, and performance/REST-Q did not show overtraining. The abstract states: “It appears that the training utilized in the study was strenuous enough to produce a positive physical response and increases in biomarkers, but did not cause overtraining” [PAGE 3].

The gray-zone audit begins with the biomarker data. Cortisol means were 0.093 ± 0.089 µg/dL at Week 1, 0.133 ± 0.124 µg/dL at Week 4, and 0.193 ± 0.179 µg/dL at Week 6 [PAGE 29]. The paper reports “a ~108% increase from baseline (p = 0.004)” for Week 1 to Week 6 [PAGE 29]. IL-6 means were 1.42 ± 1.77 pg/mL, 4.19 ± 8.27 pg/mL, and 5.60 ± 12.57 pg/mL at Weeks 1, 4, and 6 [PAGE 29]. Raw IL-6 ANOVA was not significant (p = 0.170), but “Log base 10 values showed a significant increase in IL-6 values from Week 1 to Week 6 (p = 0.0013)” [PAGE 29]. The dispersion is enormous: Week 6 SD exceeds the mean by more than a factor of two, which is exactly the regime where a log transform is defensible but also where individual outliers can drive group inference. The paper itself notes that Week 6 mean IL-6 reached 5.60 pg/mL, “above the normal baseline ranges typical for training athletes” [PAGE 46], and that “levels as low as 5 pg/mL were reported to affect athlete perceived exertion during activity” [PAGE 46]. That is a mechanistic plausibility claim, not proof of overtraining.

The correlation section contains a material internal inconsistency. The table reports Week 6 cortisol × Week 6 IL-6 as r = 0.6805* [PAGE 30], while the prose reports “Week 6 Cortisol and IL-6 values (r = 0.4495)” [PAGE 30]. The prose also says: “The levels of IL-6 and cortisol correlated significantly at both Week 1 and Week 6, but not Week 4” [PAGE 39]. A gray-zone audit must flag that one of these Week 6 values is likely a transcription or table-formatting error; without raw data, the exact coefficient cannot be adjudicated. The Week 1 coefficient r = 0.6404 is consistent across table and prose. Week 4 is near zero and non-significant. The second hypothesis in the thesis explicitly required correlations “at each of the 6 time points” [PAGE 4], but the results table only presents Weeks 1, 4, and 6; the thesis states that cortisol analysis was reduced to Weeks 1, 4, and 6 “For financial reasons” [PAGE 29]. Thus hypothesis 2 is not fully testable as originally stated. This is a sampling-design trap, not merely a statistical one.

A second audit issue is the REST-Q interpretation. The thesis reports that “All four affective categories showed significant decreases, at different times, over the course of the 6-week period” [PAGE 40], and interprets decreases in anger, depression, fatigue, and “Vigor (Motivation)” as improvement. But the “Vigor” category is composed of negatively worded items—poor sleep, feeling sick, not wanting to train [PAGE 62–63]—so a decrease in that category is indeed consistent with better recovery, even though the label “Vigor” is misleading. The aggregate REST-Q score did not change significantly [PAGE 42]. The most conservative reading is: negative affect and fatigue symptoms did not worsen; some improved; no psychometric signal of overtraining emerged.

The third audit issue is clinical versus statistical significance. The training program produced strength gains in all three main lifts: bench +4.8 ± 4.2%, squat +2.1 ± 3.1%, clean +2.0 ± 3.3% [PAGE 28], and body weight increased from Week 1 through Week 5 [PAGE 27]. If overtraining had occurred, performance and body weight would be expected to fall, not rise. The thesis concludes: “Thus, the athletes showed no overt signs of being overtrained, but the biomarkers were significantly elevated” [PAGE 50]. That is the central adjudication: the immune-endocrine response was real, but the overtraining inference is vetoed by the performance and recovery data within this 6-week window. The unresolved question is whether a longer block, higher external load, or less recovery would convert the elevated resting biomarkers into true underperformance.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Let \(C_i(t)\) be salivary cortisol for subject \(i\) at week \(t\), in \(\mu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{dL}\), and \(I_i(t)\) be salivary IL-6 in \(\mathrm{pg}/\mathrm{mL}\). The thesis reports group means at \(t\in\{1,4,6\}\) [PAGE 29]. The percent-change operator used implicitly is

\[
\Delta\%_C(t)=\frac{\bar C(t)-\bar C(1)}{\bar C(1)}\times 100,
\qquad
\Delta\%_I(t)=\frac{\bar I(t)-\bar I(1)}{\bar I(1)}\times 100,
\]

with the assumption \(\bar C(1)>0\) and \(\bar I(1)>0\). Using the reported means, \(\Delta\%_C(6)\approx 107.5\%\), matching the paper’s “~108% increase from baseline” [PAGE 29], and \(\Delta\%_I(6)\approx 294.4\%\), matching the paper’s “~294%” [PAGE 46]. Singularity/breakdown: if the Week 1 mean is at or below the assay limit of detection, the percent change is undefined or numerically explosive; if a subject has zero or negative IL-6, percent change is undefined.

The IL-6 inferential operator was a base-10 log transform:

\[
y_i(t)=\log_{10} I_i(t),\qquad I_i(t)>0,
\]

followed by within-subjects repeated-measures ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc tests at \(\alpha\le 0.05\). The paper reports raw IL-6 \(p=0.170\), but log10 IL-6 Week 1 to Week 6 \(p=0.0013\) [PAGE 29]. Assumptions: positive IL-6 values, approximate normality of log values, sphericity for repeated measures, and no informative missingness. Breakdown regimes: nonpositive or censored values; zero-inflation; assay-floor clustering; log transform masking clinically meaningful high outliers; sphericity violation; and unequal variance across weeks.

The correlation operator is Pearson product-moment correlation:

\[
r_{CI}(t)=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(C_i(t)-\bar C(t)\right)\left(I_i(t)-\bar I(t)\right)}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(C_i(t)-\bar C(t)\right)^2}
 \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(I_i(t)-\bar I(t)\right)^2}},
\]

with \(n=20\). The thesis reports \(r=0.6404\) at Week 1 and a Week 6 value that is \(r=0.6805\) in Table 6 [PAGE 30] but \(r=0.4495\) in the prose [PAGE 30]. Assumptions: linear association, no severe outlier leverage, adequate variance in both variables, and independent pairs within a week. Breakdown regimes: zero variance, range restriction, nonlinear threshold behavior, outlier dominance, and the exploratory nature of the correlation analysis. Week 4’s near-zero correlation is a regime where a simple linear model fails to capture any coupling.

A formal qualitative HPA–cytokine control scaffold is

\[
\dot I=\alpha_I S-\beta_I I-\gamma_{CI} C I,
\]

\[
\dot C=a_C\frac{I^n}{K_I^n+I^n}-b_C C,
\]

where \(I\) is IL-6 in \(\mathrm{pg}/\mathrm{mL}\), \(C\) is cortisol in \(\mu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{dL}\), \(S\) is a dimensionless normalized training-stress input, \(\alpha_I\) has units \(\mathrm{pg}\,\mathrm{mL}^{-1}\,\mathrm{week}^{-1}\), \(\beta_I\) and \(b_C\) have units \(\mathrm{week}^{-1}\), \(\gamma_{CI}\) has units \((\mu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{dL})^{-1}\,\mathrm{week}^{-1}\), \(a_C\) has units \(\mu\mathrm{g}\,\mathrm{dL}^{-1}\,\mathrm{week}^{-1}\), \(K_I\) is in \(\mathrm{pg}/\mathrm{mL}\), and \(n\) is dimensionless. This is not a fitted source equation; it is an audit scaffold consistent with the paper’s statements that IL-6 signals through the hypothalamus for CRH and cortisol release and that cortisol blocks IL-6 mRNA production [PAGE 24]. Breakdown regimes: \(I\to 0\) removes the cortisol drive; \(I\gg K_I\) saturates the Hill term; chronic stress can shift the set-point; and if negative feedback fails, the steady-state assumption collapses.

The training-stress input itself was prescribed at 85–100% of 1RM [PAGE 3], with main-lift maxima tested pre/post [PAGE 28]. The body-weight operator was a repeated-measures ANOVA across Weeks 1–6, with significant increases at Weeks 2–5 versus baseline [PAGE 27]. The REST-Q operator was a 12-item, 0–6 Likert instrument grouped into anger, depression, fatigue, and “Vigor” categories [PAGE 31–32, PAGE 62–63]. Its breakdown regime is response shift: athletes may underreport symptoms as they adapt, and negatively worded items can invert the apparent meaning of a category label.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from Selye’s general adaptation syndrome and the overreaching/supercompensation model [PAGE 10] to Smith’s cytokine hypothesis of overtraining [PAGE 14, PAGE 24], Robson’s IL-6-centered “unexplained underperformance syndrome” hypothesis [PAGE 14], and the Pedersen–Febbraio muscle-as-endocrine-organ framework [PAGE 18–19]. The thesis explicitly cites Selye (1950), Mackinnon (2000), Lehmann et al. (1993), Smith (2000), and Robson (2003) to set up the distinction between overreaching and overtraining [PAGE 10–14]. The HPA branch adds CRH, ACTH, cortisol, and negative feedback, with the paper noting that “Cortisol blocks production of IL-6 mRNA” [PAGE 24] and that recombinant human IL-6 infusion increases cortisol in healthy subjects [PAGE 24]. This is a feedback genealogy, not a one-way stress hierarchy.

The multi-week trap is built into the design. The study sampled at Week 1, Week 4, and Week 6 for cortisol because of financial constraints [PAGE 29], even though the protocol describes six weekly samples [PAGE 19–20]. That means the trajectory is under-sampled exactly where a threshold or plateau could occur. A 6-week off-season block is long enough to raise resting biomarkers, but the overtraining literature often involves more prolonged or higher-frequency loading. The thesis itself says the training “did not provoke enough of a immunological-endocrine response to induce the developing of overtraining symptoms within the 6 week time frame” [PAGE 50–51]. This is a clock-bound conclusion: it is not a claim that the program could never produce overtraining, only that it did not within this window.

A second trap is the use of salivary IL-6. The thesis acknowledges that salivary measures “may not account for minute changes in IL-6 concentrations” [PAGE 15]. Salivary cortisol is well accepted; salivary IL-6 is more fragile because oral mucosal sources, blood contamination, food residue, and flow rate can affect concentration. The thesis also notes that subjects’ mouths were not inspected before sampling [PAGE 42]. Historically, the cytokine hypothesis was built on plasma/serum IL-6 and on post-exercise kinetics; this thesis extends it to resting salivary measures across weeks. That extension is useful but not interchangeable with the original plasma evidence.

A third trap is the “biomarker elevation equals overtraining” inference. The thesis’s own performance data veto that inference: bench, squat, and clean all increased [PAGE 28], and body weight rose through Week 5 [PAGE 27]. The historical lineage predicts that chronic cytokine elevation should be accompanied by performance decrement and negative affect; here the opposite pattern appeared for performance and largely for affect. The correct retrocausal reading is that the immune-endocrine system was engaged adaptively, not that the athletes were overtrained. The open genealogical question is whether a longer block would preserve the strength gains while continuing to raise resting IL-6 and cortisol, or whether the system would eventually cross into underperformance. The thesis cannot answer that because it stops at Week 6.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer is about nested interpretations of the same biomarker signal. The athlete experiences soreness, fatigue, or poor sleep; the strength coach sees a training block that is supposed to produce supercompensation; the researcher sees IL-6 and cortisol rising; and each agent models the others’ intentions and tolerance. In a crisis, the default habit regression is: “biomarkers are up, so shut the block down.” That is the overreaction trap. The counter-trap is: “performance is up, so ignore the biomarkers.” The clock is the 6-week window: the coach and researcher must decide whether the signal is an adaptive response or an early warning before the adaptation window closes.

The thesis supplies evidence for the counter-trap and against the overreaction trap. The REST-Q data show significant decreases in anger, depression, and fatigue at various weeks, and a significant decrease in the “Vigor” category that the author interprets as “an increase in feelings of vigor and motivation” [PAGE 41]. The aggregate REST-Q score did not change significantly [PAGE 42]. If the athletes were entering an overtrained state, one would expect the opposite: rising negative affect, rising fatigue, and falling motivation. Instead, the psychological layer moved toward better recovery. That does not prove the biomarkers are harmless; it means the athletes’ self-report and the coach’s performance observation did not confirm a crisis.

The recursive trap is that each layer can over-interpret the other. The researcher may treat a statistically significant cortisol rise as clinical overtraining even though no performance decrement occurred. The coach may treat strength gains as proof that the athletes are fine even though resting IL-6 is elevated and the long-term trajectory is unknown. The athlete may underreport fatigue because he wants to keep training or because he has adapted to the routine. The thesis’s own wording is appropriately cautious: the training “was strenuous enough to produce a positive physical response and increases in biomarkers, but did not cause overtraining” [PAGE 3]. That is a clock-bound, multi-layer judgment: immune-endocrine activation occurred, biomechanical performance improved, and psychological crisis did not appear within 6 weeks.

The practical counter-trap is to avoid using one biomarker as a veto over all other evidence. A rise in cortisol and IL-6 should trigger a monitoring response, not an automatic deload. The practical overreaction trap is to ignore the biomarkers because the athletes are strong and motivated. The correct recursive stance is to track the triad—biomarker trajectory, 1RM/body-weight trajectory, and REST-Q trajectory—and to treat divergence among them as the signal that the clock may be running out. In this thesis, the triad is congruent with adaptation, not overtraining, but only for the observed 6-week window.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: The biomarker elevations are statistically supported for cortisol and log10 IL-6 [PAGE 29], but the physical layer does not support a diagnosis of overtraining. The thesis states that Week 6 IL-6 reached 5.60 pg/mL, “above the normal baseline ranges typical for training athletes” [PAGE 46], yet the athletes continued to improve. A physical veto of the overtraining claim is therefore warranted: elevated resting biomarkers alone are insufficient when performance and body weight are rising. The caveat is assay validity. Salivary IL-6 is less robust than serum IL-6, and the thesis admits possible food/drink residue and no oral inspection [PAGE 42]. That caveat weakens precision but does not reverse the direction of the cortisol finding, which is the more stable salivary measure.

Biomechanical-layer veto: The main-lift data are the strongest counter-evidence to overtraining. Bench press increased from 121.6 ± 36.3 kg to 127.4 ± 35.9 kg (+4.8 ± 4.2%), back squat from 187.2 ± 30.2 kg to 190.9 ± 28.1 kg (+2.1 ± 3.1%), and power clean from 116.8 ± 14.6 kg to 119.2 ± 14.5 kg (+2.0 ± 3.3%) [PAGE 28]. These are not the decrements expected in an overtrained state. The thesis also notes that the athletes had at least two years of resistance-training history and were not naive to the methods [PAGE 27–28], which reduces the likelihood that the gains were simply a novel-training artifact. Biomechanically, the athletes were still expressing force effectively under near-maximal loading.

Weather-layer veto: The source does not report environmental temperature, humidity, hydration status, or indoor/outdoor conditions for the conditioning runs. It does describe 100-yard runs and 300-yard shuttles at set paces, with the shuttles replaced by a speed/agility circuit in Weeks 5–6 [PAGE 31–32]. Because weather and heat stress are not measured, a weather-based explanation for the cortisol rise cannot be adjudicated from this source. The honest adjudication is “insufficient evidence,” not “weather caused the response.” If heat stress were present, it could raise cortisol and affect salivary flow, but it would not explain the simultaneous strength gains. Weather is therefore a residual confound, not a proven veto.

Cross-layer synthesis: The physical layer shows immune-endocrine activation; the biomechanical layer shows positive adaptation; the psychological layer shows no worsening and some improvement [PAGE 40–42]; and the weather layer is unmeasured. The overtraining hypothesis requires convergence of biomarker elevation, performance decrement, and negative affect. Only the first is present. The thesis’s conclusion is therefore internally consistent: “the athletes showed no overt signs of being overtrained, but the biomarkers were significantly elevated” [PAGE 50]. The cross-layer veto is not against biomarker measurement; it is against using a single layer to overrule the others.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Veto scenario 1 — raw-data correlation audit. Counterfactual: the Week 6 cortisol–IL-6 correlation is either \(r=0.6805\) as in Table 6 [PAGE 30] or \(r=0.4495\) as in the prose [PAGE 30], and the other is a transcription error. Verifiable prediction: recomputation from the 20 paired Week 6 observations should reproduce one value within rounding and fail the other; if neither reproduces, the Week 6 correlation claim is vetoed. If the raw data show a nonlinear or outlier-driven association, the Pearson claim should be replaced by a rank correlation or outlier-sensitivity analysis. This is verifiable because the thesis supplies \(n=20\) and reports both candidate coefficients.

Veto scenario 2 — salivary contamination/assay-floor audit. Counterfactual: the Week 6 IL-6 elevation is an artifact of oral contamination, food residue, or assay-floor behavior rather than a training-induced resting increase. Verifiable prediction: re-assay with oral inspection, standardized fasting, and duplicate ELISAs should attenuate the Week 1-to-Week 6 log10 difference to non-significance (\(p>0.05\)). If the log10 Week 6 difference remains significant at \(p=0.0013\) after re-assay [PAGE 29], the contamination veto fails. A further check is whether nonpositive or censored IL-6 values exist; if they do, the log transform is invalid and the claim is vetoed on statistical grounds.

Veto scenario 3 — overtraining-threshold audit. Counterfactual: the athletes were actually overtrained despite the group means. Verifiable prediction: an overtrained state should produce a pre-to-post decline in at least one main lift, a fall in body weight, and a rise in REST-Q negative affect. Observed values contradict that prediction: bench +4.8 ± 4.2%, squat +2.1 ± 3.1%, clean +2.0 ± 3.3% [PAGE 28]; body weight rose through Week 5 [PAGE 27]; and anger, depression, and fatigue categories decreased at significant points [PAGE 40–41]. If individual-level raw data revealed a hidden subgroup with 1RM decline and rising REST-Q scores, the group-level “no overtraining” conclusion would be partially vetoed. Absent that subgroup evidence, the overtraining counterfactual fails verification. Each scenario yields a concrete, checkable prediction and a clear veto condition.
