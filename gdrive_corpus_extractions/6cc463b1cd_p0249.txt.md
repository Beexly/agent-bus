<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Galton embeds a theory of mind about the marriage market: he assumes that if temper were salient in spouse selection, the observed pairing frequencies would depart from chance pairings [PAGE 232]. His crisis habit, when confronted with \(N=111\), is regression to qualitative reassurance rather than formal inference—he treats the small sample as a legitimate excuse to dismiss deviation (“as could be expected from the small number of observations”) [PAGE 232]. The counter-trap is his post-hoc pooling of mild and docile: “There is little difference between mildness and docility, so we may throw these entries together” [PAGE 232]. This is a defensive model revision triggered by anomaly, not a pre-registered theoretical claim; it protects the hypothesis of random mating by altering the category space after seeing the data.

Nested theory of mind: Galton’s ultimate scientific goal is to establish that temper is hereditary and that its natural distribution is not distorted by marital choice. If assortative mating by temper were real, the parental correlation would enter the offspring variance and complicate his regression-to-the-mean architecture. By finding none, he simplifies the hereditary model. The recursive danger is confirmation at the level of study design: the sample (“my lists”) is a convenience set of families whose temper was recorded, not a probability sample of the marriage population [PAGE 232]. Galton attributes the absence of assortment to the nature of temper, when it may equally reflect unobserved stratification (parish, class, religion) that makes temper independent of spouse only within strata but not in the aggregate. The clock is absolute: the 111 marriages are a closed historical record. No resampling, bootstrapping, or re-reading of page 232 can recover unobserved unions, so the epistemic window on whether temper guided Victorian marriage is permanently narrow.

No heart-rate variability, cortisol, startle response, or motor-action measure anchors “mild,” “docile,” “fretful,” “violent,” or “masterful” [PAGE 232]. Veto: the five classes are socially constructed self- or observer-reports; any physical-layer reduction is impossible from this page alone.

Biomechanical layer: there is no gait, grip strength, reaction-time, or facial-muscle datum in the source. Veto: no biomechanical operator can confirm or refute temper class membership, so the table’s rows and columns lack a bodily ground truth.

Weather/seasonal layer: the source collapses all marriages into a single \(5 \times 5\) matrix with no date, season, or geography [PAGE 232]. If temper correlates with birth season (seasonal affective patterning) or if marriage season correlates with temper through occupational cycles, seasonality is an unobserved confounder. Veto: the table cannot reject structural assortment mediated by parish, season, or economic cycle because those variables are absent.

Selection-bias layer: “my lists” indicates a non-random, possibly eugenically curated set of families [PAGE 232]. Veto: external validity to the general marriage population is nil; the calculated chance frequencies assume the same marginal distribution for husbands and wives, an assumption that fails if the sample over-represents particular temper classes by sex.

Aggregation veto: the pooling of mild and docile is a post-hoc statistical operation [PAGE 232]. It changes the hypothesis space after anomaly detection, so the “close approximation” of 16 vs 18 per cent is not an independent confirmation of random mating.
