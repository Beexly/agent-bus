<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s pipeline is a multi-stage filter from raw pixels to a validated homography. The first hop is color/illumination: the input image \(I(x,y)\) is converted to HSV, and a field mask is built from Hue and Value thresholds, with the stated assumption that the center region contains field ground only [PAGE 3]. The second hop is line evidence: the masked field image \(G(x,y)=I(x,y)M(x,y)\) is converted into a white-line image \(F(x,y)\) using an intensity threshold and a four-neighbor gradient test, with \(\tau=6\) pixels chosen as the maximum line width [PAGE 3]. The third hop is geometric primitive extraction: Hough segments are clustered into horizontal and vertical families, with slope split \(T_a=0\), then clustered by slope and intercept; three dominant vertical and three dominant horizontal clusters are selected by total segment length \(S_j^v=\sum_{l_i\in L_j^v}|l_i|\) [PAGE 3-4]. The fourth hop is combinatorial: three vertical and three horizontal lines produce nine intersection points and \(\binom{3}{2}\binom{3}{2}=9\) four-point camera sets [PAGE 4-5]. The fifth hop is feature matching: each intersection point is encoded by a 4-bit directional code indicating whether a line exists down, left, up, or right; seven camera-only patterns receive a wildcard, and four such codes form a 16-bit feature vector [PAGE 4]. The model side has \(\binom{7}{2}\binom{6}{2}=315\) four-point feature vectors because the field model has seven vertical and six horizontal lines [PAGE 5]. Matching uses Hamming distance one or less [PAGE 5]. The sixth hop is homography estimation and validation: candidate homographies are scored by Turkey’s biweight score \(J\) over projected model line pixels, and the maximum-score matrix is selected [PAGE 5].

The gray-zone audit begins where the paper itself flags nontrivial assumptions. First, the method’s use of outside-image intersection points is not cosmetic. The paper states: “The shorter the distance between the points used is, the larger the projection error become in the regions distant from the points.” [PAGE 2] This is a standard extrapolation/conditioning issue: a four-point DLT homography is reliable when the control quadrilateral spans the image region being mapped; when the quadrilateral shrinks, the DLT design matrix becomes ill-conditioned and projection error amplifies away from the control points. The paper’s qualitative result confirms this: inside-point-only projection shows misalignment, and outside points improve accuracy [PAGE 6]. Second, the method does not use RANSAC as a safety net. The footnote states: “Homography estimation can be performed by well-known RANSAC. In our matching problem, since almost all the matched pairs of four-points are outliers RANSAC cannot robustly estimate the optimal Homography in realistic processing time.” [PAGE 5] That means the Hamming feature filter is not merely a speed trick; it is the primary outlier-control mechanism. Third, the ground truth is not treated as infallible: “we found that the ground truth is not enough accurate through our experiments and thus we didn’t conduct the evaluation using the ground truth.” [PAGE 6] Therefore any audit that treats World Cup 2014 ground truth as absolute would be methodologically wrong. Fourth, the computational claim is not uniform: the method reduces homography evaluations by almost 90% overall [PAGE 2], but scene 14 reaches 1831 evaluations and only 35.41% reduction because two outside points were selected and the candidate set expanded [PAGE 8]. Fifth, the failure case is explanatory: scene 7 fails because of “the lines detected from the center circle and to lack of ﬁeld lines in camera images” [PAGE 7-8]. That is a model-mismatch veto: a straight-line Hough model can mistake curved center-circle evidence for a field line, and if real straight lines are too few, the selected clusters are not identifiably tied to the field model. The correct interpretation is not “more lines always help”; it is “dominant straight-line support must correspond to model lines.” The paper’s choice of three lines per family rather than two is explicitly a robustness margin: “our method selects three lines each for more robust estimation, although two lines each are suﬃcient to compute four points needed for Homography matrix estimation.” [PAGE 4] The audit therefore treats the method as a conditioned feature-matching estimator, not as a generic robust homography solver.

\[
l_i\in L_h\ \text{if}\ a_i<T_a,\qquad

\[

Directional feature and Hamming matching. Each single-point pattern is a 4-bit code for line existence in down, left, up, and right directions; four codes form a 16-bit vector \(u\in\{0,1,*\}^{16}\), where \(*\) is the wildcard for seven camera-only patterns [PAGE 4]. Matching requires
\[
d_H(u_n,u_m)\le 1,
\]
where \(d_H\) is Hamming distance in bits [PAGE 5]. Homography DLT. For four correspondences \(\mathbf{x}_i\leftrightarrow\mathbf{x}'_i\), standard DLT enforces
\[
\mathbf{x}'_i\times H\mathbf{x}_i=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,4,
\]
which gives an \(8\times9\) system \(A\mathbf{h}=0\) with \(H\) defined up to scale. The paper’s justification for four points is: “four intersection points, which are the minimum required points for estimating Homography” [PAGE 1]. \(x,y,x',y'\) are pixel coordinates; \(H\) is dimensionless up to scale. Singularity occurs if \(\operatorname{rank}(A)<8\), commonly when three points are collinear, points are duplicated, or the control quadrilateral has near-zero area. A numerical audit uses
\[
\kappa_2(A)=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)},
\]
dimensionless; as \(\sigma_{\min}\to0\), DLT error amplification diverges. This formalizes the paper’s statement that shorter point separation increases distant projection error [PAGE 2].

Accuracy score. The score is
\[
J=\sum_{FM(x,y)=255}\rho\bigl(F(x',y')-FM(x,y)\bigr),
\]
with
\[
\rho(x)=
\begin{cases}
1-(x/128)^2, & |x|\le128,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
The paper states: “Based on Turkey’s biweight method, the accuracy score \(J\) is computed” [PAGE 5]. \(J\) is dimensionless because each model line pixel contributes a dimensionless biweight; the residual is an intensity-code difference. The selected matrix is
\[
H^\star=\arg\max_H J(H).
\]
Breakdown occurs if the model line projection is systematically wrong, if \(J\) is flat across candidates, or if the model itself does not match the observed field markings.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from minimal-point homography to feature-filtered homography. Farin et al. [18] extracted two horizontal and two vertical lines and used their four intersections, which the paper describes as “the minimum required points for estimating Homography” [PAGE 1]. That approach is minimal but combinatorially expensive because it must validate many possible pairs; the paper states that Farin’s method “needs to estimate and validate all Homography matrices to all possible pairs of intersection points between camera and model images” [PAGE 1]. Farin et al. [19] is a faster version, but it uses predetermined line segments, so it “works fast only for the scene where these line segments are visible” [PAGE 1]. The authors’ previous method [14] is the immediate ancestor: it used directional patterns but with a different distance and loss function; the paper explicitly corrects the earlier decimal/Euclidean comparison by using Hamming distance and corrects the simpler loss to Turkey’s biweight [PAGE 5]. The learning-based branch is Homayounfar et al. [21], which uses semantic segmentation and vanishing points but is sensitive to vanishing-point estimation [PAGE 2], and Chen et al. [22], which uses synthetic edge images and a siamese network but “degrades the accuracy for the scenes which camera pose is not similar to any of the prepared 100,000 camera poses” [PAGE 2]. The present paper’s retrocausal contribution is to move the burden from exhaustive geometric validation to directional feature filtering, while adding outside-image virtual points as control points.

The multi-week traps are persistent experimental-design errors rather than one-off bugs. Trap one is trusting the dataset ground truth: the paper says the ground truth is “not enough accurate” [PAGE 6], so a long experiment cycle spent minimizing ground-truth error can optimize the wrong target. Trap two is assuming RANSAC will rescue bad matches: the paper states that “almost all the matched pairs of four-points are outliers” [PAGE 5], so a RANSAC-first design wastes time and may fail within realistic processing time. Trap three is assuming synthetic pose coverage is sufficient: Chen’s 100,000 synthetic poses still miss dissimilar camera poses [PAGE 2]. Trap four is assuming inside-image points are enough: scenes 7, 8, 12, and 14 fail with inside-only points because no matching pairs are found [PAGE 6]. Trap five is assuming outside points always reduce cost: scene 14 has 1831 evaluations and only 35.41% reduction because outside points expand the candidate set [PAGE 8]. These traps can persist across many weeks because each has a plausible local justification: use ground truth, use RANSAC, use synthetic data, use only visible points, use fewer evaluations. The paper’s evidence vetoes each one in a specific regime.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis habit regression is the engineer’s instinct to add capacity when a scene fails: add more lines, lower thresholds, add more matches, or call RANSAC. The paper’s scene 7 failure is a counterexample. The failure is not caused by too few candidate homographies; it is caused by “the lines detected from the center circle and to lack of ﬁeld lines in camera images” [PAGE 7-8]. Adding more clusters without a curve model can inject center-circle segments into the straight-line sets, which worsens the model mismatch. The counter-trap is the inside-point shortcut. Inside-only matching is computationally attractive: Table 2 shows very high reduction rates, often above 99% [PAGE 8]. But Table 1 shows that scenes 7, 8, 12, and 14 have no successful inside-only estimate, and other scenes are generally lower-scoring than the outside-point method [PAGE 6, PAGE 8]. The recursive model of mind here is: an operator who optimizes the visible metric (“fewer homography evaluations”) will choose the inside-only configuration and then mistake the absence of a homography for a detector bug, when the actual issue is control-point conditioning. The clock is not just wall-clock time; the paper measures the number of Homography evaluations, with 2835 as the exhaustive full-search maximum [PAGE 8]. The outside-point method often achieves about 90% reduction, but scene 14’s 1831 evaluations and 35.41% reduction show that the clock can dominate in wide-field-of-view scenes [PAGE 8]. The correct counter-trap is adaptive: retain outside points when they improve conditioning, but monitor candidate explosion; do not treat the inside-only reduction rate as a free lunch. The method’s three-line selection is also a theory-of-mind correction: the designers anticipate that two lines are mathematically sufficient but not robustly sufficient, so they use three per family [PAGE 4]. That is a deliberate margin against occlusion and mis-clustering, not redundancy for its own sake.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical/geometric veto. The homography is identifiable only if the selected line families correspond to the field model. The paper’s model has seven vertical and six horizontal lines [PAGE 5]. If the camera view lacks at least two vertical and two horizontal straight lines, the nine-intersection construction cannot produce a valid four-point set. The method’s use of three lines per family is a robustness margin, but it does not create information: it only reduces the chance that the top cluster is the wrong line. Proof sketch: if \(L_v\) or \(L_h\) is empty after Eq. (3) and the dominance test \(S_j^v\), then no intersection point exists, so no feature vector \(u_n\) exists, so no Hamming match exists, and no \(H\) can be estimated. This is a hard veto independent of the score \(J\).

Optical/weather veto. Eq. (1) assumes the center region is field ground only [PAGE 3]. Rain, snow, strong shadows, stadium lighting, or a non-green field can shift Hue and Value such that \(M(x,y)=0\) over true field areas or \(M(x,y)=1\) over non-field areas. Eq. (2) requires \(G(x,y)\ge140\) and \(\Delta(x,y)\ge10\) [PAGE 3]. If weather reduces white-line contrast, the true line pixels become \(F(x,y)=0\), which removes Hough evidence and cluster support. If overexposure or white advertising boards create high \(G\) and high \(\Delta\), false lines enter \(F\). Either failure changes the selected clusters and can make \(J\) prefer a wrong homography. This is a physical-layer veto because no downstream feature matching can recover line evidence that the optical front end deleted.

Biomechanical/occlusion veto. The paper notes that predetermined-primitive methods require all primitives to be visible and are “inevitably not robust to occlusions” [PAGE 1]. This method is more flexible because it clusters many Hough segments and selects dominant clusters, but player and referee occlusion can still break a line into short segments. If an occluder removes enough support, the true line may fall below the top-three \(S_j^v\) values, or the Hough transform may fit fragmented edges with the wrong slope/intercept. The veto is not absolute—short segments can still cluster—but it becomes hard when the remaining support is less than the competing false-line support. The paper’s failure case is related: center-circle curvature and lack of straight field lines break the straight-line model [PAGE 7-8].

Camera-pose veto. Chen’s method degrades when the camera pose is unlike any of the 100,000 synthetic poses [PAGE 2]. The present method is less dependent on synthetic pose coverage, but it still has a pose-sensitive cost: scene 14, with a wide field of view, selects two outside points and increases candidate pairs to 1831 evaluations [PAGE 8]. A pose that places too many model lines outside the image increases reliance on virtual outside points; a pose that places too few lines inside the image reduces the number of usable intersections. Thus the camera pose can veto either identifiability or computational feasibility.

Model/score veto. The score \(J\) assumes that projected field-model lines should coincide with \(F(x,y)\) [PAGE 5]. If the field model is wrong for the stadium, or if the visible markings differ from the model, maximizing \(J\) can select a systematically biased \(H\). The paper’s warning that the dataset ground truth is “not enough accurate” [PAGE 6] reinforces that validation must use projection consistency and multiple scenes, not a single authoritative label. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: optical evidence must pass Eq. (1) and Eq. (2), geometric evidence must pass Eq. (3) and Eq. (4), matching must pass the Hamming gate, and numerical estimation must avoid DLT singularity. Any layer that fails vetoes the homography before the score can be trusted.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
