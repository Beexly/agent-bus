Exact singularity and breakdown regimes:
1. f = −1/a: then 1 + f a = 0, so log(1 + fa) = −∞; the functional and its derivative are singular at this boundary [PAGE 1].
2. a = −∞: no f > 0 satisfies 1 + fs > 0 F-a.e.; the domain collapses to {0}; f* → 0 and the Kelly prescription is trivial [PAGE 1].

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage on [PAGE 1] descends from Kelly (1956), “A New Interpretation of Information Rate,” which established expected logarithmic growth as the optimal criterion for repeated favorable wagers, measuring growth in bits of information rate. Thorp extended this to blackjack (1962) and, with Kassouf, to warrant hedging and stock-index arbitrage in “Beat the Market” (1967). Merton (1969) formulated the continuous-time analog using Itô calculus, deriving the log-optimal portfolio under geometric Brownian motion. Samuelson (1979) challenged the myopic optimality of Kelly in multi-period settings, igniting the growth-optimal versus mean-variance debate that persists in portfolio theory.

The present page [PAGE 1] situates the U.S. stock market as a “continuous gambling game with a positive, one-year expected return equal to the average of the historical annual returns over a sufficiently long time span.” It admits that strict stationarity is “argumentative,” yet asserts evidence that “price changes in speculative markets behave like independent, identically distributed random variables with finite variances (see [8])” [PAGE 1]. By the Central Limit Theorem, annual price changes are treated as approximately normal, with the concession that “the lognormal distribution would provide a superior fit, but the computations are much more cumbersome to discuss here” [PAGE 1].

Multi-week traps embedded in this genealogy. The iid stationarity assumption is fragile over holding periods shorter than the full historical cycle; volatility clustering, regime shifts, and drawdown persistence violate finite-variance iid sampling. Estimating μ and a from a finite window introduces plug-in error: f* is hypersensitive to μ near zero and to a near the realized minimum. Fat-tailed left endpoints mean the sample estimate â may lie far above the true essential infimum, causing systematic over-leverage. The normal/lognormal approximation ignores gap risk; a single session in which realized s < −1/f makes 1 + fs negative, driving the logarithm outside its real domain and producing permanent capital impairment. Thus the historical lineage supplies the mathematical form, but the multi-week estimation window supplies the principal mode of model breakdown.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression. When an allocator experiences consecutive losses, behavior regresses from the abstract logarithmic utility of Kelly toward mean-variance heuristics, stop-loss rules, or complete cash withdrawal. The Kelly fraction f* is computed from μ and a, but realized drawdowns cause the agent to revise the perceived left endpoint â downward (more pessimistic), which shrinks f* in the next period. This creates a recursive habit loop: losses → pessimistic â update → lower f* → under-allocation during recovery → foregone compounding → reinforced aversion. The regression is not a recalculation within the model; it is an extra-model override that invalidates the stationarity premise on which f* was derived [PAGE 1].

Counter-trap. The counter-trap is adversarial misspecification of a. If the analyst estimates the left endpoint from a shallow historical window that excludes limit-down moves or settlement failures, then −1/â is inflated and the unconstrained f* is pushed toward the margin boundary. The text itself provides an exogenous ceiling: “margin and wager up to 1.95 times current capital” [PAGE 1]. Any computed f* exceeding this cap must be vetoed by capital adequacy, regardless of what the first-order condition G′(f*) = 0 recommends. The counter-trap is therefore to treat the 1.95× margin limit as a hard veto that dominates the Kelly root whenever the two conflict.

Clock. The source assumes annual iid returns and frames expected return as a one-year historical average [PAGE 1]. Rebalancing at a frequency faster than one year breaks the sampling clock: daily or weekly re-sampling of μ does not preserve the annual iid structure, and transaction costs, brokerage commissions, taxes, and time-varying purchasing power continuously erode realized log-growth [PAGE 1]. If the allocation clock is mismatched to the return clock—e.g., applying an annual μ to a daily rebalancing schedule—the fraction f* is mis-scaled and may exceed the admissible domain implied by the realized high-frequency left endpoint. The recursive implication is that f* is only valid when the rebalancing period matches the estimation period of μ and F.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer (market microstructure). Exchanges can halt trading, impose circuit breakers, or delay settlement precisely when the portfolio approaches the domain boundary f = −1/a. If rebalancing is impossible at the moment 1 + fs approaches zero, the theoretical maximizer f* cannot be executed. Veto rule: exchange halt or margin call ⇒ f_effective := 0 until liquidity is restored.

Biomechanical layer (human operator). Cognitive load, fatigue, and drawdown-induced risk aversion may prevent the allocator from implementing the prescribed f* after a loss sequence. If the operator cannot emotionally or operationally execute the Kelly fraction, the model is not implementable. Veto rule: measured drawdown exceeding operator tolerance ⇒ override f* to a reduced fraction or cash.

Weather layer (macroeconomic/liquidity shocks). Sudden shifts in the left endpoint a—sovereign default, commodity limit-down, systemic liquidity freeze—can produce realized s < â, which makes 1 + fs < 0 and pushes the logarithm outside its real domain [PAGE 1]. This is a structural breach of the support assumption. Veto rule: realized return crossing below the modeled essential infimum ⇒ liquidate to cash (f = 0) and re-estimate F before re-entering.

Adjudication proof. The admissible Kelly fraction is

f_admissible = min{ f*, f_cap, f_veto },

where f_cap = 1.95 from the source’s margin constraint [PAGE 1] and f_veto = 0 if any layer vetoes. If a → −∞, the domain itself vanishes and f* → 0 [PAGE 1], which is a weather/structural veto on the entire model. If lim f → (−1/a)⁻ G′(f) ≥ 0, the supremum lies at the singularity boundary; the physical margin cap vetoes entry into that regime. Thus the formal maximizer from Theorem 2 [PAGE 1] is never the final allocation unless all three layers pass; otherwise the cross-layer veto reduces f to the binding constraint, and if the domain boundary is breached, to zero.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
RLVR denotes a verifiable counterfactual reward layer in which each scenario is tied to a falsifiable predicate on observed returns and a precise prediction for f*.

Scenario 1 — Domain collapse (unbounded downside). Counterfactual world: the true left endpoint is a = −∞ (e.g., leveraged equity or derivative positions with unlimited downside). Verifiable predicate: ∃ realized return s_obs such that 1 + f s_obs ≤ 0 for the candidate f. Prediction: G(f) is undefined for all f > 0 because the logarithm leaves ℝ; the unique admissible fraction collapses to f* = 0 [PAGE 1]. Any strictly positive allocation risks total capital impairment, so the verified action is full cash allocation.

Scenario 2 — Non-positive drift. Counterfactual world: the sample annual mean over the estimation window satisfies μ̂ ≤ 0. Verifiable predicate: (1/N) Σ_{i=1}^N s_i ≤ 0. Prediction: G′(0) = μ̂ ≤ 0 and G″(f) < 0 imply G′(f) < 0 for every f > 0; by strict concavity the global maximum is at f* = 0 [PAGE 1]. Kelly prescribes no allocation to the risky asset; any positive f yields strictly lower expected log-growth than cash.

Scenario 3 — Boundary non-crossing with binding margin cap. Counterfactual world: lim f → (−1/a)⁻ G′(f) ≥ 0, so G′ never crosses zero on (0, −1/a), while the institution enforces f_cap = 1.95 [PAGE 1]. Verifiable predicate: numerical evaluation of G′(f) on a dense grid over [0, min(−1/a, 1.95)) remains strictly positive. Prediction: the unconstrained supremum is approached at the leverage boundary f → (−1/a)⁻, a singularity/ruin regime; the binding constraint is capital adequacy, not the Kelly root [PAGE 1]. Verified action: either allocate f = f_cap only if an independent risk budget permits, or veto leverage entirely and set f = 1. The model breakdown is localized at f = −1/a, where log(0) → −∞; crossing this line means the growth functional ceases to exist in real-valued terms.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
