<THINK_TRACE>
The supplied source describes STORM as an end-to-end multimodal large language model for referring multi-object tracking, or RMOT. The central claim is not merely architectural but epistemic: RMOT should be treated as a single language-conditioned spatial-temporal generation problem rather than as a pipeline of detector, tracker, and text encoder. The paper states that STORM is “an end-to-end MLLM that jointly performs grounding and tracking within a unified framework, eliminating external detectors and enabling coherent reasoning over appearance, motion, and language” [PAGE 1]. This matters because prior RMOT systems are described as decomposing “object grounding and tracking into separated modules” and suffering from “the scarcity of training videos, ambiguous annotations, and restricted domains” [PAGE 1]. The source’s multi-hop logic is therefore: first, RMOT requires language grounding and temporal identity maintenance; second, end-to-end unification allows the language model to reason over both; third, data scarcity blocks direct end-to-end training; fourth, task-composition learning, or TCL, transfers knowledge from abundant subtasks; fifth, STORM-Bench supplies cleaner RMOT supervision; sixth, the resulting model achieves state-of-the-art benchmark performance.

A gray-zone audit begins with the definition of the task. The paper formalizes RMOT as extracting all spatial locations corresponding to one or more referenced objects across a video. It says: “Given a video V = {It, t ∈{1, . . . , T}} consisting of T frames and a referring expression R” and that “the model extracts the spatial locations B = {Bk t , t ∈{1, . . . , T}, k ∈{1, . . . , K}}” [PAGE 3]. This is important because the output is not a single trajectory but a set of trajectories indexed by object identity and frame index. The source further states that STORM “directly generates bounding boxes for the text-described objects in a structured plain-text format” [PAGE 2]. The output format is explicitly textual: “Object 1: Frame 1: [x1, y1, x2, y2], Frame 2: …” [PAGE 3]. Thus the model is not emitting a probabilistic detector head in the conventional sense; it is emitting a tokenized structured sequence that can be parsed into boxes.

The second gray-zone concerns training. The paper argues that “RMOT can likewise be approached by first learning fundamental capabilities from easily accessible data, followed by a small amount of task-specific fine-tuning” [PAGE 2]. TCL decomposes RMOT into “grounding objects according to a textual description and maintaining their identities across time” [PAGE 2]. Stage 1 uses image grounding, single-object tracking, and referring single-object tracking data. Stage 2 fine-tunes on STORM-Bench with reasoning-based supervision. The model is “guided to generate intermediate reasoning traces, first grounding the described objects in the initial frame and then tracking them sequentially throughout the video” [PAGE 4]. This is not a trivial curriculum claim; it implies that the language model’s chain-of-thought can be aligned with a geometric task, where intermediate tokens serve as a computational bridge between language semantics and pixel coordinates.

The third gray-zone concerns dataset construction. STORM-Bench is introduced because “existing RMOT datasets remain far from satisfactory: they are small in scale, noisy, and lack sufficient diversity” [PAGE 2]. The paper criticizes prior datasets for ambiguity. LaSOT expressions may fail “to specify which instance should be tracked” [PAGE 3]. LaMOT exhibits “low annotation diversity and incomplete object coverage” [PAGE 3]. Elysium contains “misaligned or erroneous bounding boxes that do not accurately match the referring expression” [PAGE 3]. STORM’s response is a bottom-up pipeline: first localize objects, then caption each object, verify captions, and finally compose multi-object expressions. The source explains the asymmetry: “while locating an object given a description is challenging, generating captions for a given object is a relatively easy task” [PAGE 5]. This inversion is the causal core of the dataset contribution. Instead of asking a model to infer objects from ambiguous language, the pipeline begins with known objects and generates verifiable language around them.

The fourth gray-zone is evaluation. The paper reports RMOT performance using “HOTA [55], identity-based metrics [61], and CLEAR metrics [7] (MOTA and IDsw)” [PAGE 6]. In Table 4, STORM achieves 66.7 HOTA, 55.3 DetA, 82.6 AssA, 81.4 LocA, 78.3 IDF1, 57.1 MOTA, and 183 IDsw, while the strongest adapted baseline, LaMOT, reaches 46.7 HOTA and 48.8 IDF1 [PAGE 7]. The paper says STORM surpasses prior methods “by +21.2 HOTA and +31.2 IDF1” [PAGE 7]. However, the comparison is not a native end-to-end RMOT comparison because “no existing model supports end-to-end RMOT” and baselines were adapted [PAGE 7]. For image-grounding models, “OCSORT is used as the tracker” [PAGE 7]. This is a legitimate comparison but also a gray zone: the baselines are not optimized as unified RMOT systems, so the gain reflects both STORM’s unification and its training recipe.

The fifth gray-zone concerns failure modes. The paper is unusually explicit about limitations. It reports that “heavy occlusion in dense scenes remains challenging for referring object trackers” [PAGE 13]. It also says the remaining gap is “primarily in grounding quality, as reflected by DetA” and that the model can miss precise alignment “when prompts are highly ambiguous or when scenes are heavily crowded” [PAGE 13]. Efficiency is another boundary: “With a fixed number of targets, decoding remains stable at around 3 FPS as the video grows from 32 to 256 frames” [PAGE 13], but “as the number of tracked targets increases, throughput drops roughly linearly because the model must auto-regressively emit longer output sequences” [PAGE 13]. Finally, “very long videos or videos with more than ten target objects become challenging because the autoregressive output sequence grows quickly” [PAGE 13]. These statements convert STORM from a pure performance claim into a bounded operational system: strong on benchmark-scale RMOT, but constrained by autoregressive length, occlusion, crowding, and ambiguous language.

The source provides an explicit problem operator and an explicit training objective. I formalize only those elements, plus minimal notational scaffolding required for units and breakdown regimes.

1. RMOT input-output operator.

The paper defines a video and a referring expression:

\[
V = \{I_t\}_{t=1}^{T}, \qquad T \in \mathbb{N}, \qquad R \in \mathcal{R},
\]

where \(I_t\) is frame \(t\), \(T\) is the number of frames, and \(R\) is the referring expression. The source says: “Given a video V = {It, t ∈{1, . . . , T}} consisting of T frames and a referring expression R” [PAGE 3].

The output is a set of object tracks:

\[
B = \{B_{t,k}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T;\, k=1,\ldots,K},
\]

where \(k\) indexes the referenced objects and \(t\) indexes frames. The source states that “the model extracts the spatial locations B = {Bk t , t ∈{1, . . . , T}, k ∈{1, . . . , K}}” [PAGE 3].

Each box is represented as:

\[
B_{t,k} = (x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2) \in \mathcal{P} \cup \{\varnothing\},
\]

where \(\mathcal{P}\) is the set of pixel-coordinate tuples and \(\varnothing\) denotes an empty bounding box. The source says the output is “[x1, y1, x2, y2]” and that “When objects are temporarily absent from a frame due to occlusion or camera movement, STORM outputs an empty bounding box to indicate that the object is unobserved in that frame” [PAGE 4]. Units: pixel-coordinate units, frame index units, object index units.

2. Tokenized sequence model.

STORM uses a ViT visual encoder, an MLP projector, and an LLM. The source says: “STORM extracts the frame-based visual tokens V through a ViT-based vision encoder. A two-layer MLP projector is followed to map the visual tokens into text space. The constructed visual tokens and referring text query tokens are sent to the a LLaMA-based LLM” [PAGE 3].

Let the model input be a concatenated token sequence:

\[
z = (v_1, \ldots, v_{N_v}, r_1, \ldots, r_{N_r}),
\]

where \(v_i\) are visual tokens and \(r_j\) are text-query tokens. Let the generated output tokens be:

\[
y = (y_1, \ldots, y_L).
\]

The output sequence encodes the structured RMOT response. The paper says STORM “directly generates bounding boxes for the text-described objects in a structured plain-text format” [PAGE 2].

3. Autoregressive probability operator.

Because the LLM autoregressively generates output, the conditional probability is:

\[
p_\theta(y_1, \ldots, y_L \mid V, R)
=
\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}
p_\theta(y_\ell \mid y_{1:\ell-1}, z).
\]

Assumption: causal autoregressive decoding, with no external detector or tracker inserted during inference. This follows from the claim that STORM eliminates “external detectors” [PAGE 1] and “without relying external detectors or trackers” [PAGE 1].

Probability units: dimensionless conditional probabilities. Information units: if \(\log\) is natural log, loss is in nats; if \(\log_2\), loss is in bits.

4. Cross-entropy training operator.

The source explicitly says: “This formulation enables us to train the model as a next-token prediction task using cross-entropy loss” [PAGE 3].

For a dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) of RMOT examples, the loss is:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\theta)
=
-\sum_{(V,R,y)\in\mathcal{D}}
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
\log p_\theta(y_\ell \mid y_{1:\ell-1}, z).
\]

This is the direct formalization of the paper’s stated training objective. No additional detection loss, tracking loss, or matching loss is specified in the supplied pages. The absence of such terms is important: the model’s spatial-temporal behavior is learned through token prediction over the structured output.

5. Task-composition learning operator.

Define empirical risk for a dataset \(\mathcal{D}\):

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{(V,R,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\theta; V, R, y)
\right].
\]

TCL is a stage-wise curriculum over subtask datasets. The paper says STORM is trained on “large-scale datasets for image grounding and single-object tracking” and then “progressively extend[s] the model’s competence to RSOT and ultimately to RMOT” [PAGE 4].

Let:

\[
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{IG}} = \text{image-grounding data},
\]
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SOT}} = \text{single-object tracking data},
\]
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{RSOT}} = \text{referring single-object tracking data},
\]
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{RMOT}} = \text{STORM-Bench RMOT data}.
\]

A minimal TCL operator is:

\[

=
\arg\min_{\theta}
\mathcal{R}_{
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{IG}}
\cup
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SOT}}
\cup
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{RSOT}}

\[

\quad

Assumption: the paper does not provide exact sampling ratios, loss weights, or optimization schedules for the unioned subtask data. Therefore, no numeric mixing coefficient is introduced here. The operator is a formal curriculum abstraction, not an invented scalar objective.

6. Chain-of-thought supervision operator.

Stage 2 uses reasoning-based supervision. The source says: “To encourage explicit reasoning, we employ a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) training strategy. The model is guided to generate intermediate reasoning traces, first grounding the described objects in the initial frame and then tracking them sequentially throughout the video” [PAGE 4].

Let the target output be decomposed as:

\[

(y_{\mathrm{reason}}, y_{\mathrm{boxes}}),
\]

where \(y_{\mathrm{reason}}\) contains the intermediate reasoning trace and \(y_{\mathrm{boxes}}\) contains the final structured track output. The loss still operates over tokens, but the token sequence now includes an intermediate planning subsequence. This does not change the cross-entropy form; it changes the target distribution.

7. Long-video clipping operator.

For long videos, the source says: “For long videos, we split the input into shorter clips and stitch the resulting tracklets by using the predicted boxes from the previous clip as prompts for the next one” [PAGE 4].

Let:

V = \bigcup_{m=1}^{M} C_m,
\]

where \(C_m\) is clip \(m\). Let \(P_{m-1}\) be the predicted boxes from clip \(m-1\), used as prompt for clip \(m\). Then:

\[
\hat{B}_m
=
f_\theta(C_m, P_{m-1}),

and the stitched output is:

\[
\hat{B}
=
\bigcup_{m=1}^{M} \hat{B}_m.

Breakdown regime: if \(P_{m-1}\) contains an identity error, that error can propagate into \(C_m\) through the prompt. This is a sequential error-propagation singularity, not a stochastic aleatoric failure.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage of STORM can be reconstructed from the paper’s related-work and dataset sections. The first lineage is object grounding. The paper traces grounding from detection-style alignment methods such as MDETR, GLIP, and Grounding DINO, which “align object and text features in embedding space and subsequently retrieve object-text pairs” [PAGE 2]. It then moves to MLLM-based grounding, where models produce “object grounding with precise coordinates” [PAGE 3]. STORM inherits this lineage but extends it from static images to videos and from single-object grounding to multi-object referring tracking.

The second lineage is tracking. The paper distinguishes single-object tracking and multi-object tracking. It says traditional SOT uses “similarity-learning” [PAGE 3], while MOT “generally employs tracking-by-detection paradigm and utilizes additional tracker head to associate object bounding boxes” [PAGE 3]. STORM’s historical break is that it refuses the additional tracker head. The paper states that prior video extensions of MLLM grounding “typically attach an external tracker or detector” and that such methods “leverage the LLMs as standalone text encoder and require additional detection module, preventing the model from learning unified spatial-temporal representations” [PAGE 1]. Thus the causal lineage is: modular tracking systems had strong engineering maturity, but their separation blocked unified language-motion reasoning.

The third lineage is referring tracking. The source identifies RSOT and RMOT as descendants of referring grounding. RSOT localizes and follows “one target based on a textual query” [PAGE 1], but is “inherently limited to a single target and fail[s] to handle multiple interacting objects or relational descriptions” [PAGE 1]. RMOT expands the task to “ground and track all objects referenced by textual queries throughout a video” [PAGE 1]. STORM positions itself as the first end-to-end MLLM for this expanded task: “we introduce STORM, the first end-to-end multi-modal large language model designed specifically for referring multi-object tracking (RMOT)” [PAGE 1].

The fourth lineage is dataset evolution. The paper reviews image grounding datasets such as Flickr30k, RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg, then video-based datasets such as OTB99, Cityscapes-Ref, Talk2Car, and Refer-Youtube-VOS [PAGE 3]. It then critiques RSOT and RMOT datasets. LaSOT provides descriptions but may contain “multiple instances of the same category” without specifying which instance should be tracked [PAGE 3]. LaMOT is an early RMOT effort but has “low annotation diversity and incomplete object coverage” [PAGE 3]. Elysium has “misaligned or erroneous bounding boxes” [PAGE 3]. Refer-KITTI is limited to traffic scenes [PAGE 3]. STORM-Bench is the counter-move: “a new dataset featuring accurate multi-object trajectories and diverse, unambiguous referring expressions” [PAGE 2].

The fifth lineage is training methodology. The paper explicitly borrows from LLM pre-training and supervised fine-tuning. It says MLLMs require massive data, but constructing large RMOT datasets is impractical. Therefore, it proposes learning “fundamental capabilities from easily accessible data, followed by a small amount of task-specific fine-tuning” [PAGE 2]. This is a direct transfer of LLM pre-training logic into visual-spatial-temporal grounding. The multi-week trap here is that RMOT annotation is expensive not only because boxes must be accurate, but because expressions must uniquely identify multiple objects under relational language. STORM’s TCL is designed to bypass that trap by borrowing from cheaper subtasks.

A major multi-week trap identified by the source is top-down annotation error. Elysium uses a top-down pipeline: caption first, then Grounding-DINO boxes, then tracking. The paper says this pipeline “is highly sensitive to the performance of the expert detector and the tracker” [PAGE 5]. STORM reverses the direction. Its bottom-up pipeline “first localizes objects and then generates corresponding referring expression for each object” [PAGE 5]. It also replaces confidence-only filtering with LLM verification: “we employ an LLM-based verification process that leverages the reasoning capabilities of large language models to identify and filter incorrect annotations” [PAGE 5]. This is a historical correction: annotation should begin from observable objects, not from unreliable captions.

Another trap is domain narrowness. The source notes that existing RMOT work is “generally focused on cityscape scenes (Ref-KITTI)” [PAGE 3]. STORM-Bench is built on VidOR, which “provides ground-truth bounding boxes for diverse daily objects, beyond the limited pedestrian–vehicle focus of typical MOT datasets” [PAGE 5]. The paper reports that STORM-Bench spans “80 classes” and “averages 3 instances per expression” [PAGE 6]. This broadens the genealogical branch from traffic-centric RMOT to everyday open-vocabulary RMOT.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory of mind in STORM can be described as a contest between three forces: crisis habit regression, counter-trap, and clock.

The crisis is data scarcity and annotation ambiguity. The paper states that “constructing large-scale datasets for referring multi-object tracking is highly impractical” [PAGE 2]. It also says existing RMOT datasets are “small in scale, noisy, and lack sufficient diversity” [PAGE 2]. This creates a crisis for any MLLM approach because MLLMs normally require massive high-quality training data. The recursive problem is that the model must infer which objects a user means, but the training signal needed to teach that inference is expensive and ambiguous.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency of prior systems to respond to this crisis by decomposing the problem. The paper says prior RMOT approaches “augment a specialized detector with text encoders” [PAGE 1]. These systems “struggle to interpret complex referring expressions and cannot reason about causal or relational dependencies conveyed in language” [PAGE 1]. The regression is intuitive: if RMOT is hard, use a detector for grounding and a tracker for association. But this regression prevents unified reasoning. The LLM becomes a text encoder, not a spatial-temporal reasoner. The source explicitly criticizes this: such methods “prevent the model from learning unified spatial-temporal representations” [PAGE 1].

The counter-trap is STORM’s unified end-to-end formulation. Instead of separating grounding and tracking, STORM uses one MLLM to generate both. The model’s internal theory of mind is shaped by chain-of-thought supervision: “The model is guided to generate intermediate reasoning traces, first grounding the described objects in the initial frame and then tracking them sequentially throughout the video” [PAGE 4]. This creates an explicit temporal policy: identify referenced entities first, then maintain them. The model is not merely classifying objects; it is constructing a persistent identity hypothesis under language constraints.

The second counter-trap is TCL. The paper says TCL allows STORM “to leverage data-rich sub-tasks and learn structured spatial–temporal reasoning” [PAGE 1]. It transfers “knowledge learned from sub-tasks such as image grounding and SOT” [PAGE 3]. This is a theory-of-mind shortcut: the model first learns that descriptions map to regions, then learns that regions persist over time, then learns that multiple described regions can persist jointly. The recursive mental operation is composition: grounding plus tracking becomes RMOT.

The third counter-trap is bottom-up data construction. The source’s dataset pipeline treats the model as a verifier of object-language alignment. It generates captions for known objects, verifies them under multiple visual inputs, then composes multi-object expressions. The verification stage asks another MLLM “to predict whether the generated caption uniquely matches the intended object” [PAGE 5]. This is a recursive self-check: the dataset itself uses an MLLM to audit MLLM hallucinations. The paper notes that “occasional inaccuracies still arise due to inherent hallucinations in MLLMs” [PAGE 5]. The counter-trap is not merely more data; it is verification-aware data generation.

The clock is the cost of autoregressive output and annotation. STORM’s architecture is powerful but slow when many objects are tracked. The source reports that “throughput drops roughly linearly because the model must auto-regressively emit longer output sequences” [PAGE 13]. Table 7 shows 3.2 FPS for one target at 64 frames, but only 0.4 FPS for eight targets at 64 frames [PAGE 14]. This is a temporal clock constraint: each additional object increases the token sequence, which increases latency. The paper also says “very long videos or videos with more than ten target objects become challenging because the autoregressive output sequence grows quickly” [PAGE 13].

The clock also appears in annotation. RMOT annotations are “extremely costly and difficult to obtain” [PAGE 7]. The paper finds that “performance begins to plateau at around 12K samples” and therefore caps STORM-Bench at 15K samples [PAGE 7]. This is a data clock: after a certain point, more RMOT examples provide limited returns. TCL is the response to both clocks: it reduces the need for massive RMOT supervision and allows the model to borrow from cheaper subtasks.

In recursive terms, STORM’s theory of mind has three layers. First, it models the user’s referring expression as a constraint over objects. Second, it models each object as a persistent identity across frames. Third, it models absence, because “When objects are temporarily absent from a frame due to occlusion or camera movement, STORM outputs an empty bounding box” [PAGE 4]. This absence handling is a crucial mental operation: the model must not hallucinate presence. The supplementary analysis says STORM “does not assume referred objects always appear in the video and can reliably detect their presence or absence” [PAGE 13].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source contains no biomechanical, athletic, meteorological, or environmental physics layer. Therefore, any veto based on weather, force, fatigue, or physiological constraints is inadmissible from the supplied pages. The admissible veto layer is visual-computational: visibility, occlusion, crowding, prompt ambiguity, autoregressive length, and inference latency.

The physical-visual veto can be stated as a conditional proof.

Let successful RMOT prediction require three conditions:

1. The referring expression \(R\) maps to a determinate object subset.
2. The target objects are sufficiently visible or partially visible such that their boxes can be localized.
3. The generated token sequence remains within the model’s practical decoding horizon.

If condition 1 fails, the model cannot uniquely ground objects. The source says the remaining gap is “primarily in grounding quality, as reflected by DetA” and that the model can miss precise spatial alignment “when prompts are highly ambiguous” [PAGE 13]. Therefore, ambiguous prompts veto high grounding quality.

If condition 2 fails, physical visibility is insufficient. The source says “heavy occlusion in dense scenes remains challenging for referring object trackers” [PAGE 13]. It also gives a failure case involving “a partially visible table with a white cloth” where “all models mislocalize the table” and “STORM misses the right portion of the table” [PAGE 13]. Therefore, dense occlusion vetoes exact localization even for STORM.

If condition 3 fails, the output sequence becomes too long. The source says “very long videos or videos with more than ten target objects become challenging because the autoregressive output sequence grows quickly” [PAGE 13]. It also reports throughput degradation with target count: at 64 frames, FPS falls from 3.2 for one target to 0.4 for eight targets [PAGE 14]. Therefore, large target count vetoes high-throughput operation.

A cross-layer adjudication follows: STORM’s claimed superiority is valid under benchmark conditions where expressions are interpretable, targets are not excessively occluded, and output length is manageable. It is not valid as a universal physical guarantee under arbitrary occlusion, extreme crowding, or arbitrarily many targets. The source itself acknowledges these boundaries.

There is also a temporal-clipping veto. For long videos, STORM splits input into clips and stitches tracklets using previous boxes as prompts [PAGE 4]. This creates a dependence on prior predictions. If an earlier clip produces an identity switch or false box, the next clip may inherit that error. This is not a weather veto but a sequential state veto: the model’s future output is conditioned on its own past output.

Finally, there is an absence-handling veto. The model is trained to output an empty box when an object is unobserved [PAGE 4]. If it instead hallucinates a box during exit or re-entry, it violates the physical visibility constraint. The supplementary results state that baselines produce false positives when objects are absent, while STORM is trained to avoid this [PAGE 13]. Thus the admissible physical veto is: no visible object should receive a non-empty box unless the model has sufficient visual evidence or a valid occlusion-inference policy.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following are three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios for STORM under reinforcement-learning-style verifiable rewards. Each scenario uses only source-supported metrics and failure modes.

Scenario 1: Absence and re-entry veto.

Condition: An object referenced by the expression is temporarily absent from a frame due to exit, occlusion, or camera movement.

Verifiable reward: The model should output an empty bounding box for the absent object. A non-empty box is a false positive.

Source basis: The paper states, “When objects are temporarily absent from a frame due to occlusion or camera movement, STORM outputs an empty bounding box to indicate that the object is unobserved in that frame” [PAGE 4]. The supplementary analysis says that in an exit/re-entry example, baselines “incorrectly produce false-positive bounding boxes” while STORM “does not assume referred objects always appear in the video and can reliably detect their presence or absence” [PAGE 13].

Counterfactual veto: If STORM outputs a non-empty box for an absent target, the absence-reward veto is triggered.

Prediction: On videos with temporary target absence, STORM should produce fewer false-positive boxes than image-based baselines such as Qwen2.5-VL and VisionLLMv2, because those baselines are adapted through frame-level detection and linking rather than explicit absence-aware generation [PAGE 7]. The verifiable prediction is lower false-positive count or higher precision on absent frames for STORM.

Scenario 2: Dense-scene grounding veto.

Condition: The scene is heavily crowded or contains multiple similar objects, and the referring expression requires precise spatial alignment.

Verifiable reward: Localization quality, measured by DetA and LocA, should remain high. If DetA falls sharply, the model is failing the grounding component.

Source basis: The paper says, “The remaining gap is primarily in grounding quality, as reflected by DetA: the model can still miss precise spatial alignment when prompts are highly ambiguous or when scenes are heavily crowded” [PAGE 13]. It also says, “heavy occlusion in dense scenes remains challenging for referring object trackers” [PAGE 13]. In Figure 11, the expression “the table with white cloth and the wooden table” causes all models to mislocalize, and STORM “misses the right portion of the table” [PAGE 13].

Counterfactual veto: If evaluated on a dense-crowd subset, STORM’s DetA should be lower than on cleaner multi-object scenes, and STORM should not be expected to outperform all baselines in that subset.

Prediction: On dense crowded scenes with similar objects, STORM’s identity consistency may remain strong because AssA is high, but its localization score DetA may degrade. The source’s own Table 4 shows high AssA and lower DetA relative to AssA for STORM: AssA is 82.6, DetA is 55.3 [PAGE 7]. The verifiable prediction is that in crowded/ambiguous subsets, the gap between STORM and baselines narrows or reverses specifically on DetA/LocA, even if identity metrics remain favorable.

Scenario 3: Target-count latency veto.

Condition: The number of tracked targets increases while video length is held fixed.

Verifiable reward: Throughput, measured in FPS, or wall-clock time, should remain within an operational threshold.

Source basis: The paper reports, “As the number of tracked targets increases, throughput drops roughly linearly because the model must auto-regressively emit longer output sequences” [PAGE 13]. Table 7 gives, for 64 frames, FPS values of 3.2, 1.8, 1.0, and 0.4 for one, two, four, and eight targets respectively [PAGE 14]. It also says, “very long videos or videos with more than ten target objects become challenging because the autoregressive output sequence grows quickly” [PAGE 13].

Counterfactual veto: If an application requires at least 1 FPS at 64 frames with eight targets, STORM fails because the reported throughput is 0.4 FPS [PAGE 14]. If an application requires stable decoding with more than ten targets, the paper’s own limitation vetoes that regime [PAGE 13].

Prediction: As target count increases, wall-clock time increases and FPS decreases. At 64 frames, doubling targets from four to eight reduces FPS from 1.0 to 0.4, an absolute drop of 0.6 FPS and a relative reduction to 40 percent of the four-target value. This is directly verifiable from Table 7. The veto is not that STORM cannot track eight objects; it is that it cannot do so at the same throughput as one object.
