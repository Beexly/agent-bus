The supplied document is a survey of AI-based computer vision in sports, so the correct reasoning object is not a single model claim but a dependency graph of perception operators. The paper explicitly partitions the field into “physical understanding (i.e., who is where) and logical understanding (i.e., what is happening)” [PAGE 3]. That partition is useful because it separates geometric state estimation from semantic event inference, but it is also leaky: logical understanding is conditioned on physical errors. A calibration error changes image-to-world mapping; a detection error changes identity assignment; a tracking error changes trajectory continuity; an event-spotting error changes tactical inference; and a performance-assessment error changes athlete evaluation. The survey’s own limitations section reinforces this: “lack of standardized databases with ground truth data” [PAGE 41] prevents clean attribution of errors across layers.

A multi-hop derivation begins with camera calibration. The survey describes calibration as a mapping from image coordinates to real-world coordinates [PAGE 3]. In formal terms, this is a projective mapping. If the homography is wrong, every downstream field-plane quantity is biased. The paper notes that homography estimation can be formulated with the “8 DoFs encoded by the 3×3 transformation matrix” [PAGE 9], and that later methods iteratively minimize “the segment reprojection loss” [PAGE 10]. This means calibration is not a preprocessing nicety; it is a measurement operator whose error propagates multiplicatively into localization, pass feasibility, player assignment, and event geometry. The survey also warns that “homography decomposition introduces additional errors” [PAGE 10], especially when vertical structures such as goal posts violate the planar assumption. That is a critical gray-zone: the scene is not perfectly planar, but the model treats it as if it were.

The next layer is detection and identification. The survey groups methods into multi-modal fusion, skeleton-driven modeling, deep-feature embeddings, and data-efficient learning [PAGE 10]. These clusters solve different failure modes. Multi-modal fusion adds thermal, audio, or motion cues; skeleton-driven methods exploit pose priors; deep embeddings learn reidentification descriptors; data-efficient methods reduce annotation cost through distillation, weak supervision, or self-supervision. However, each introduces a new fragility. Teacher-student systems can propagate teacher bias. Skeleton priors fail under extreme pose. Deep reidentification embeddings can be opaque and domain brittle. The survey’s discussion of tracking makes this explicit: optimization-based associations impose global constraints but are computationally expensive, geometric reconstructions require multi-view or calibration assumptions, and deep appearance methods need substantial labeled data and degrade under domain shift [PAGE 42].

Tracking is where temporal consistency becomes measurable. The survey cites methods using Hidden Markov Models, graph optimization, Kalman filtering, bipartite matching, and motion-vector cues. The HMM formulation is important because it treats target state as a latent sequence conditioned on observations: “A Hidden Markov Model (HMM) strategy” is used for spatial constraints [PAGE 18]. NeighborTrack uses “a Kalman filter and hypothesis generation via bipartite matching” [PAGE 20]. These are not decorative architectural choices; they are probabilistic fixes for identity switching, occlusion, and abrupt motion. The survey’s opening challenge statement names the core failure mode: “identity switching among players, posing variations, and severe occlusions” [PAGE 2]. Therefore, tracking quality should be evaluated not only by detection recall but by identity persistence under occlusion and appearance change.

Logical understanding then consumes these physical estimates. Event spotting, relationship detection, simulation forecasting, and performance assessment are all conditional on prior layers. The survey describes event spotting clusters as skeleton-driven, spatio-temporal, and multi-modal [PAGE 22]. Skeleton methods are compact but sensitive to pose error. Spatio-temporal models capture context but are computationally heavy. Multi-modal fusion improves robustness but depends on synchronized side channels. In relationship detection, the survey includes pass prediction, penalty recognition, injury assessment, and interaction detection [PAGE 30]. These tasks require not only “where” but “who is interacting with whom, and why it matters.” The survey’s performance-assessment section adds rehabilitation, injury burden, and motion quality [PAGE 35]. This is where computer vision becomes high-stakes: an error can affect medical or training decisions.

The gray-zone audit reveals three unstable boundaries. First, the boundary between template-based and geometry-based calibration is not purely methodological; it is a boundary between prior knowledge and generalization. Template methods are faster but “rely on ad-hoc prior knowledge about the views” [PAGE 42]. Geometry-based methods are more general but depend on visible field markings and robust segmentation. Second, the boundary between real-time and near-real-time is operational, not scientific. The survey notes some methods achieve “20–30 frames per second” [PAGE 10], while also stating that many reviewed approaches “cannot be considered real-time today” [PAGE 44]. Third, the boundary between explainable and trustworthy is not closed by post-hoc explanation. The survey mentions explainability tools such as “LIME, SHAP, and Grad-CAM” [PAGE 35], but explanation is not the same as calibrated uncertainty. A system can be interpretable and still wrong.

The deepest trap is benchmark isomorphism. The survey repeatedly points to SoccerNet, World Cup, NHL, Diving48, FineDiving, and other datasets [PAGE 26], but it also warns that heterogeneous datasets and private collections impede comparison [PAGE 41]. If a method overfits to one camera rig, one league, one lighting condition, or one annotation style, its apparent progress may not transfer. Therefore, the correct inference is conservative: the survey documents strong technical progress, but not a universally valid sports vision stack. The safe engineering conclusion is layered validation: calibrate, detect, track, infer events, and assess performance under explicit uncertainty budgets, with separate tests for geometric error, identity error, temporal error, and semantic error.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The following operators are reconstructed from the survey’s described methods. Units are SI or standard computer-vision units. No betting constructs are used.

1. Pinhole camera projection and metric lifting.
A standard image formation operator is

\[
s \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
K \begin{bmatrix} R & t \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} X \\ Y \\ Z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\]

where \(u,v\) are pixel coordinates, \(X,Y,Z\) are world coordinates in meters, \(K\) contains focal length in pixels and principal point in pixels, \(R\) is dimensionless rotation, \(t\) is translation in meters, and \(s\) is a scalar projective depth factor. The survey’s calibration section is built around this kind of 2D-to-3D mapping [PAGE 3]. Assumptions: pinhole optics, known intrinsics, negligible lens distortion, rigid scene, calibrated extrinsics. Breakdown regimes: points behind the camera, vanishing points at infinity, strong radial distortion, insufficient correspondences, non-planar scene geometry, and rolling-shutter effects. When these occur, metric lifting becomes ill-posed or biased.

2. Homography estimation on a field plane.
For a planar field, image points satisfy

\[
\tilde{x}' \sim H \tilde{x}
\]

where \(\tilde{x}\) and \(\tilde{x}'\) are homogeneous pixel coordinates and \(H\) is a \(3 \times 3\) homography defined up to scale. The survey explicitly connects this to the “8 DoFs encoded by the 3×3 transformation matrix” [PAGE 9]. Units: \(H\) is dimensionless up to scale; input coordinates are pixels. Assumptions: scene points lie on one physical plane, correspondences are accurate, and lens distortion is corrected or modeled. Breakdown: rank deficiency when fewer than four independent correspondences are available, near-collinear points, repeated points, or severe occlusion of field markings. Vertical objects such as goal posts violate the plane assumption and increase decomposition error, as the survey notes that “homography decomposition introduces additional errors” [PAGE 10].

3. Direct Linear Transformation and robust fitting.
The survey mentions “DLT [1], i.e. Direct Linear Transformation, and RANSAC [95]” in sports field registration [PAGE 9]. A DLT homography solves a homogeneous linear system

\[
A h = 0, \qquad \|h\| = 1
\]

where \(h\) is the vectorized homography and \(A\) encodes point correspondences. RANSAC then selects inliers by minimizing a robust residual. Units: residuals are in pixels if algebraic or geometric reprojection error is used. Breakdown: outlier ratio too high, degenerate point configurations, poor initial segmentation, or lens distortion not included in the model.

4. Reprojection loss for calibration refinement.
The survey describes TVCalib as a method that “iteratively minimizes the segment reprojection loss” [PAGE 10]. A formal objective is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{cal} =
\sum_{i=1}^{N} \rho \left(
\left\| x_i - \pi(K,R,t,X_i) \right\|^2
\right)
\]

where \(x_i\) is an observed image point or segment sample, \(X_i\) is the corresponding field point in meters, \(\pi\) is the projection operator, and \(\rho\) is a robust loss. If \(\rho\) is identity, the loss has units of pixels squared. Assumptions: correct correspondences, reasonable initialization, differentiable renderer, and adequate field visibility. Breakdown: local minima, shadowed or occluded lines, poorly initialized pose, and heavy-tailed outliers. The survey also notes that template-matching approaches may be faster but “rely on ad-hoc prior knowledge about the views” [PAGE 42], which is a domain-shift breakdown regime.

5. Gaussian mixture template dictionary for calibration.
The survey describes Cioppa et al. as using a dictionary built by “a clustering algorithm based on Gaussian Mixture Models” [PAGE 7]. A GMM density is

\[
p(z) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(z \mid \mu_k, \Sigma_k)
\]

where \(z\) is a feature or template descriptor, \(\pi_k\) are mixture weights, \(\mu_k\) are means, and \(\Sigma_k\) are covariance matrices. Units: \(z\) may be dimensionless if normalized features are used; probabilities are unitless. Breakdown: out-of-distribution camera views, insufficient clusters, severe illumination change, or descriptors that do not preserve geometric equivalence.

6. Probabilistic tracking as sequence inference.
For multi-target tracking, the survey describes HMM-style constraints and graph optimization [PAGE 18]. A generic tracking posterior is

\[
P(T_{1:\tau} \mid Z_{1:\tau})
\propto
P(T_1)
\prod_{t=2}^{\tau} P(T_t \mid T_{t-1})
\prod_{t=1}^{\tau} P(Z_t \mid T_t)
\]

where \(T_t\) is target state at time \(t\), \(Z_t\) is observation evidence, and \(\tau\) is time index. Units: probabilities are unitless; state variables may include position in meters, velocity in meters per second, and identity labels. Assumptions: conditional independence of observations given state, motion model validity, and manageable data association. Breakdown: abrupt acceleration, non-Gaussian maneuvering, prolonged occlusion, identical jersey appearance, and high crowd density.

7. Kalman filtering for single-target rescue.
NeighborTrack uses “a Kalman filter and hypothesis generation via bipartite matching” [PAGE 20]. A linear Kalman update can be written as

\[
\hat{x}_{t|t} = A \hat{x}_{t-1}
+ K_t \left( z_t - H A \hat{x}_{t-1} \right)
\]

where \(\hat{x}\) is state estimate, \(A\) is transition matrix, \(H\) is observation matrix, \(z_t\) is measurement, and \(K_t\) is Kalman gain. Units: state components may be pixels, meters, or velocities depending on implementation. Breakdown: nonlinear motion, delayed measurements, missed detections, and false neighbors. The method is useful when the target is occluded by similar nearby players, but it cannot recover identity if appearance evidence collapses entirely.

8. Transformer attention for skeleton or multimodal event models.
The survey describes skeleton data mapped “through a linear projection to a sequence of temporal tokens” [PAGE 24]. A self-attention operator is

\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) =
\mathrm{softmax} \left( \frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V
\]

where \(Q,K,V\) are query, key, and value matrices, and \(d_k\) is key dimension. Units: logits are dimensionless after scaling; output inherits feature-space units. Breakdown: overfitting on small sports datasets, as the survey notes that TemPose “shows signs of overfitting” [PAGE 25]. Additional breakdown occurs when temporal context is too short, when skeleton noise dominates, or when event labels are ambiguous.

9. Active-learning uncertainty.
The survey reports that in action spotting active learning, the “Entropy Measure (EM) outperforms Random Sampling (RS)” [PAGE 27]. Predictive entropy is

\[
H(Y \mid x) = - \sum_{c} p(y=c \mid x) \log_b p(y=c \mid x)
\]

with units in bits if \(b=2\) and nats if \(b=e\). Assumptions: model probabilities are calibrated enough for uncertainty ranking. Breakdown: poorly calibrated softmax outputs, label noise, and distribution shift between unlabeled pool and deployment video.

10. Event cameras and ball-spin estimation.
The survey describes event-based spin estimation as estimating “the spin axis, angular velocity, and translational velocity of a ball” [PAGE 29]. A simplified rigid-body spin state is

\[
\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3, \qquad v \in \mathbb{R}^3
\]

where \(\omega\) is angular velocity in radians per second and \(v\) is translational velocity in meters per second. Event cameras provide asynchronous brightness-change events, often modeled as a function of log intensity change. Breakdown: low-contrast ball texture, motion blur not present in events but insufficient event count, occlusion, and low angular velocity below event threshold.

11. Physics-informed trajectory models.
The survey mentions a “particle-under-drag physical approximation” for shuttle trajectory reconstruction [PAGE 19] and physics-plus-neural modeling for golf ball flight [PAGE 33]. A drag-dominated translational model is

\[
m \frac{dv}{dt}
=
m g
-
\frac{1}{2} \rho C_D A \|v\| v
\]

where \(m\) is mass in kilograms, \(v\) is velocity in meters per second, \(g\) is gravitational acceleration in meters per second squared, \(\rho\) is air density in kilograms per cubic meter, \(C_D\) is drag coefficient, and \(A\) is reference area in square meters. Breakdown: unsteady wake, spin-induced Magnus force, turbulence, low Reynolds regimes, and inaccurate launch-state measurement.

12. Evaluation metrics named by the survey.
The survey mentions MOTA and MOTP for tracking [PAGE 18], PCK for pose keypoints [PAGE 19], HOTA, MOTA, and IDF1 for soccer tracking [PAGE 21], and Average-mAP for action spotting [PAGE 27]. These are unitless performance scores, not physical measurements. Their breakdown regime is dataset bias: if ground truth is sparse, temporally misaligned, or collected under narrow camera conditions, the metric can reward domain-specific artifacts rather than general perception.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of the surveyed systems runs through classical photogrammetry, modern deep perception, and sports-specific benchmark engineering. The oldest operator is camera calibration. The survey traces this to direct linear transformation and field-marking geometry, citing “DLT [1], i.e. Direct Linear Transformation” [PAGE 9]. This lineage is important because sports field registration inherits the assumptions of close-range photogrammetry: known object dimensions, planar reference surfaces, and stable camera intrinsics. When those assumptions fail, modern neural networks often hide the failure rather than remove it. The result is a system that appears robust on benchmark views but breaks under unusual broadcast angles, shadows, rain, or nonstandard camera motion.

The second lineage is template matching and learned feature dictionaries. The survey describes template-based calibration as using “templates or feature libraries for matching and registration” [PAGE 6]. This line descends from classical keypoint matching, nearest-neighbor retrieval, and later learned embeddings. In the survey, Chen and Little use synthetic data and a feature-pose database [PAGE 6], Sha et al. use an end-to-end homography network with semantic segmentation and transformer refinement [PAGE 7], and Cioppa et al. use a dictionary of synthetic field projections and homographies [PAGE 7]. The genealogical trap is that template dictionaries encode the distribution of views seen during construction. A new league, new camera height, or new lens distortion can move the input outside the dictionary manifold. The survey acknowledges this indirectly by noting that template methods “rely on ad-hoc prior knowledge about the views” [PAGE 42].

The third lineage is geometry-based field registration. This line uses field lines, corners, intersections, ellipses, vanishing points, and player keypoints. The survey cites methods using human poses as calibration patterns [PAGE 8], keypoint-aware label conditions [PAGE 8], vanishing-point lookup grids [PAGE 9], and minimal 2D-3D correspondences [PAGE 10]. The scientific lineage is classical projective geometry, but the modern twist is that keypoint detectors are learned. This creates a recursive dependency: geometry needs keypoints, keypoints need appearance models, and appearance models need annotated data. The multi-week trap is that teams may spend weeks improving keypoint heatmaps while the actual bottleneck is field segmentation or lens distortion. The survey’s observation that homography decomposition can add error [PAGE 10] is a symptom of this mismatch.

The fourth lineage is detection and tracking. Early sports vision used background subtraction, color histograms, motion cues, and handcrafted features. The survey references classic ideas such as SIFT and FLANN in homography propagation [PAGE 12], HOG-based human detection as a baseline [PAGE 12], and modern networks such as YOLO, Mask R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, ResNet, and transformers [PAGE 13], [PAGE 16], [PAGE 24]. The genealogical trap here is benchmark overfitting to SoccerNet-style data. Many methods are optimized on broadcast soccer with relatively stable camera behavior. They then fail in hockey, rugby, swimming, skiing, or amateur single-camera settings. The survey’s warning about “lack of standardized databases with ground truth data” [PAGE 41] is therefore not a complaint; it is a structural constraint on scientific inference.

The fifth lineage is event understanding and forecasting. The survey moves from event spotting to relationship detection and then to simulation forecasting. This lineage descends from action recognition, temporal action proposals, graph reasoning, transformer sequence modeling, and physics-informed learning. The survey cites temporally aware pooling, active learning, self-supervised transformers, audio-video fusion, and event cameras [PAGE 26], [PAGE 27], [PAGE 28], [PAGE 29]. The trap is temporal leakage. In sports video, adjacent frames are highly correlated. A model can achieve high event-spotting scores by recognizing scene context rather than the event itself. This is especially dangerous for coaching or medical applications, where false confidence can lead to poor decisions.

Another multi-week trap is annotation debt. The survey emphasizes data-efficient learning, online distillation, weak supervision, and synthetic data [PAGE 16], [PAGE 33]. These methods reduce labeling cost, but they create hidden dependencies. A teacher model can impose its biases on a student. Synthetic data can encode simulator artifacts. Weak labels can reinforce existing annotation mistakes. The survey’s discussion of teacher-student methods warns that such schemes can propagate teacher biases if teacher quality or unlabeled data are limited [PAGE 42]. This is a genealogical trap because the field often treats label scarcity as the only problem, while the deeper problem is label validity and temporal alignment.

The final trap is explainability theater. The survey welcomes explainable and trustworthy AI, mentioning “LIME, SHAP, and Grad-CAM” [PAGE 35] and neuro-symbolic diving assessment [PAGE 40]. But post-hoc explanations can be plausible without being causally correct. In high-stakes sports settings, explanation must be tied to measurable operators: which field points were used, which keypoints drove the score, which frames contributed to the event, and what uncertainty remains. Without that, explainability becomes a narrative layer rather than a verification layer.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
A recursive theory of mind for this domain models three agents: the perception system, the human operator, and the deployment environment. The perception system predicts identities, events, and risks. The human operator predicts whether the system is trustworthy. The environment imposes latency, lighting, weather, and privacy constraints. Failure occurs when these predictions diverge.

Crisis habit regression is the tendency of operators to retreat to familiar but suboptimal pipelines under pressure. If tracking collapses during severe occlusion, engineers often add manual identity correction, increase annotation budgets, or simplify the model. The survey notes that tracking is threatened by “identity switching among players, posing variations, and severe occlusions” [PAGE 2]. In a live setting, this creates a crisis loop: occlusion causes identity switch, identity switch reduces coach trust, reduced trust causes manual intervention, manual intervention increases latency, and increased latency makes the system less useful. The regression is to treat the problem as an annotation problem rather than a probabilistic estimation problem.

A second regression is to over-trust deep end-to-end models. The survey warns that “a blind faith towards these models may turn out to be counter-productive” [PAGE 41]. In crisis mode, teams may deploy a larger network, add more modalities, or fine-tune on a small target match. These actions can improve benchmark numbers while worsening robustness. For example, a teacher-student segmentation system may adapt to one match but fail when lighting changes. The survey notes that ARTHuS adapts during a match through online distillation [PAGE 16], which is powerful but match-specific. If the deployment assumption is a single match with stable conditions, this is reasonable. If the assumption is league-wide generalization, it is a trap.

Counter-trap reasoning requires anticipating the operator’s second-order response. If a system outputs a pass probability, a coach may ask why a certain receiver was ranked highly. If the system cannot expose field position, orientation, and occlusion state, the coach may reject it even if the prediction is accurate. The survey highlights body orientation in pass feasibility: “the effect of orientation is vital in the half-court” [PAGE 31]. This means the system must model not only position but pose. A counter-trap is to add explainability without adding geometric verification. The survey’s discussion of X-VARS and explainable foul assessment shows the right direction: explanations should be tied to visual evidence and rules, not only to feature attributions [PAGE 35].

The clock is the second recursive constraint. Sports deployment has multiple clocks: camera frame rate, broadcast latency, coach decision time, referee review time, and athlete recovery time. The survey notes that some calibration methods achieve “20–30 frames per second” [PAGE 10], but also that many approaches are not real-time today [PAGE 44]. A system that is accurate but too slow for live use may still be useful for post-event analysis, but it cannot satisfy the same operator expectations. The clock also changes the value of uncertainty. In live tracking, a fast approximate track may be preferable to a delayed exact track. In injury assessment, a slower but calibrated explanation may be preferable to a fast but opaque score.

The third recursive layer is privacy and governance. The survey states that edge deployment will amplify “data privacy and ownership” concerns [PAGE 44]. Athletes and teams may not consent to continuous video capture, biometric inference, or model training on their movements. A recursive model must predict that users will resist systems that extract too much identity information. Therefore, privacy-preserving architectures are not optional extras; they are part of the perception objective. Federated learning, on-device processing, encrypted storage, and minimal retention become part of the system’s reward function.

A stable deployment policy follows from this recursive analysis. First, separate fast reactive tracking from slow calibrated analysis. Second, expose uncertainty in physical units: pixels, meters, seconds, and identity persistence. Third, use human-in-the-loop correction only when the model’s uncertainty exceeds a predefined threshold. Fourth, avoid adding modalities unless they reduce a measured failure mode. Fifth, treat explainability as a causal audit trail, not as a decorative report. The survey’s own conclusion supports this: it calls for “publicly available, well-curated standard datasets and harmonized benchmarking protocols” [PAGE 44]. That is the institutional version of recursive theory of mind: the field must model its own evaluation traps before trusting its models.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and environmental constraints can veto a computer-vision claim even if the learned model reports high confidence. The survey identifies the relevant constraints: “insufficient resolutions, variable illuminations, or lighting effects” and “blurry video segments, and partial or full occlusions” [PAGE 2]. These are not merely engineering inconveniences; they define identifiability boundaries.

Resolution veto.
Let \(p\) be pixel pitch in meters, \(f\) focal length in meters, \(L\) physical object size in meters, and \(Z\) distance from camera in meters. The projected object size in pixels is approximately

\[
N_{pix} \approx \frac{f L}{Z p}
\]

If \(N_{pix}\) is below one pixel, the object is not resolvable. More generally, if \(N_{pix}\) is below the detector’s required support, detection becomes statistically unreliable. This vetoes claims that a ball or jersey number can be recognized from video where the object occupies subpixel extent. The survey’s warning about insufficient resolution [PAGE 2] is therefore a hard physical veto, not a soft performance caveat.

Motion-blur veto.
If object speed is \(v\) meters per second and exposure time is \(t_{exp}\) seconds, image blur in meters on the sensor is approximately \(v t_{exp}\) projected through optics. If blur length exceeds the object’s projected size, shape evidence collapses. This vetoes fine-grained event spotting or spin estimation under fast motion unless event cameras, high frame rates, or motion compensation are used. The survey’s mention of event cameras for table-tennis spin is a response to this veto [PAGE 29].

Calibration-plane veto.
Homography-based field registration assumes a plane. Let the field plane be \(Z=0\). If a target point has nonzero height \(h\) and the camera has nonzero elevation, the projected point deviates from the plane-induced prediction. The error grows with \(h\) and camera tilt. This vetoes precise localization of players’ heads, balls in flight, or goal posts when using a field-plane homography without a full 3D model. The survey’s observation that “homography decomposition introduces additional errors” [PAGE 10] is consistent with this veto. Vertical structures are a direct counterexample to pure planar calibration.

Occlusion veto.
If the visible fraction of an athlete is too small, identity evidence may be insufficient. Let \(A_{vis}\) be visible silhouette area and \(A_{total}\) full silhouette area. If \(A_{vis}/A_{total}\) falls below a task-dependent threshold, reidentification embeddings become unreliable. The survey names “severe occlusions” as a primary tracking challenge [PAGE 2]. This vetoes claims of continuous identity persistence when players cluster in front of the camera or behind officials.

Biomechanical veto.
Skeleton-based action recognition assumes that keypoint configurations lie near the training manifold. Extreme poses, rapid rotation, or self-occlusion can push the pose outside that manifold. The survey repeatedly lists “Limitations under extreme poses” as a drawback for skeleton-driven methods [PAGE 22]. This vetoes automated scoring or injury classification when the athlete’s pose is outside the annotated distribution, unless the system has explicit uncertainty estimation and human review.

Weather and illumination veto.
Thermal, low-light, rain, snow, glare, and water spray can change the sensor modality’s informative content. The survey notes that thermal and fisheye setups may require custom configurations [PAGE 12], and that swimming pose estimation struggles due to “the aquatic environment and visual clutter” [PAGE 23]. This vetoes cross-domain transfer claims unless the model is trained on matched environmental conditions or uses modality-invariant representations.

Computational veto.
Some geometric or graph optimization methods are accurate but too slow for live use. The survey notes optimization-based tracking can be “computationally expensive” [PAGE 18] and that many methods are not real-time today [PAGE 44]. This vetoes deployment in live broadcast if latency exceeds the operational clock, even if offline accuracy is high.

Adjudication rule.
A claim should be accepted only if the physical signal exists, the geometric model is valid, the temporal model can maintain identity, and the computational latency fits the deployment clock. If any layer fails, the claim is vetoed regardless of benchmark score.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The following three counterfactual scenarios are verifiable because they specify an intervention, a measurable outcome, a ground-truth or synthetic test, and a breakdown regime. Metrics are drawn from the survey: reprojection error, Average-mAP, HOTA, MOTA, IDF1, PCK, and localization error.

Scenario 1: Calibration intervention.
Claim under test: improving camera calibration improves downstream player localization and pass-feasibility inference.
Intervention: replace a template-based homography initializer with a target-view fine-tuned calibration that minimizes segment reprojection loss, as described in TVCalib-like methods [PAGE 10].
Verifiable test: use synthetic broadcast clips with known field geometry and known camera extrinsics. Measure homography reprojection error in pixels and player localization error in meters. Then measure pass-receiver Top-1 accuracy, as in the survey’s discussion of orientation-aware pass feasibility [PAGE 31].
Prediction: if calibration error decreases below the field-scale tolerance, downstream localization and pass ranking improve. If the scene contains non-planar vertical structures, the improvement will saturate or reverse unless vertical geometry is modeled.
Veto condition: when goal posts, boards, or players’ bodies violate the plane assumption, the calibration intervention cannot fix downstream errors because the homography operator is misspecified. The survey’s note that “homography decomposition introduces additional errors” [PAGE 10] is the warning sign.
Reward signal: task metric improvement minus latency cost minus annotation cost. If calibration improves offline reprojection but does not improve downstream task metric, the reward is not verified.

Scenario 2: Occlusion-memory intervention in tracking.
Claim under test: increasing temporal memory for neighbor-aware tracking improves identity persistence under occlusion.
Intervention: extend the NeighborTrack-style memory window and hypothesis generation using “a Kalman filter and hypothesis generation via bipartite matching” [PAGE 20].
Verifiable test: inject synthetic occlusions of known duration into tracked sequences. Measure IDF1, MOTA, HOTA, and identity-switch count. Vary occlusion duration from short to long.
Prediction: IDF1 and identity persistence improve when occlusion duration is shorter than the memory window. If occlusion duration exceeds the memory window, or if multiple nearby players share similar appearance, the intervention fails.
Veto condition: when appearance embeddings are indistinguishable and motion cues are ambiguous, additional memory does not recover identity. The survey’s warning about “identity switching among players, posing variations, and severe occlusions” [PAGE 2] defines the failure regime.
Reward signal: identity metric improvement minus latency and memory cost. If IDF1 improves but latency becomes unacceptable for live use, the counterfactual is only valid for offline analysis.

Scenario 3: Audio-video fusion intervention for event spotting.
Claim under test: adding synchronized audio improves event spotting in sports where impact sounds are informative.
Intervention: fuse video with audio using a multi-modal spotting model, following the survey’s discussion of audio-video action spotting [PAGE 28] and audio-based padel hit detection [PAGE 29].
Verifiable test: use clips with ground-truth event timestamps. Compare video-only and audio-video models under controlled signal-to-noise ratios and synchronization offsets. Measure Average-mAP and timestamp error.
Prediction: audio-video fusion improves Average-mAP when the audio event is above noise and synchronization error is small relative to event duration. If crowd noise masks the impact sound, or if audio and video clocks drift, the fusion can degrade performance.
Veto condition: when audio is noisy, unsynchronized, or absent, the multimodal model inherits video-only limitations while adding computational and alignment risk. The survey’s caution about reliance on synchronized side channels [PAGE 43] is the veto principle.
Reward signal: Average-mAP improvement minus synchronization cost minus latency. If the model improves only on clean stadium audio but fails on amateur single-camera video, the counterfactual is not generally verified.

Cross-scenario RLVR principle.
A counterfactual is accepted only if it improves a verifiable task metric under controlled intervention, respects physical constraints, and does not create hidden dependencies. If the intervention improves a proxy metric but worsens robustness, latency, privacy, or interpretability beyond the allowed budget, the counterfactual is vetoed.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
