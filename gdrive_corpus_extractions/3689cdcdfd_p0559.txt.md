<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The conceptual lineage of James's "greatest resistance" model traces back to dualistic philosophies but diverges sharply through its mechanistic framing. Historically, Stoicism and Kantian ethics posited that moral worth arises precisely from acting against inclination; however, they treated the "struggle" as a purely spiritual or rational imperative. James retrocausally grounds this in physiology. His father, Henry James Sr., explored the tension between transcendental ideals and material reality, influencing William's focus on the "stream of consciousness" as a biological process.

Scientifically, James was reacting against associationist psychology (Hartley, Mill) which viewed all behavior as passive chaining of ideas based on pleasure/pain. By introducing $E$ as an active, indeterminable reinforcement, James anticipated Volition Theory and later, the Free Energy Principle (Friston). The idea that the brain minimizes surprise (free energy) aligns with minimizing "resistance," but James uniquely identifies that *moral* action involves maximizing short-term surprise/resistance (via $E$) to satisfy long-term priors ($I$).

A multi-week trap emerges in modern interpretations: equating $E$ solely with "grit" or static personality traits. The genealogical error is forgetting that for James, $E$ is a momentary physiological surge, likely tied to adrenaline noradrenergic activation in the locus coeruleus. If one views willpower as a finite battery (Baumeister's ego depletion), one misses James's insight that $E$ is generated *by* the resistance itself—the larger the threat to the self-concept ($I$), the larger the potential mobilization of $E$, up to the singular breakdown point. This historical nuance warns against viewing moral failure merely as a lack of character; it is often a structural collapse of the $E(P)$ function under extreme $P$.
