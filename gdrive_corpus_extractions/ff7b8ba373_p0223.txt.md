<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The theoretical framework derives from Clark L. Hull’s drive-reduction theory of learning. The core equation for predicting behavior relates the D-conditioned reflex (Dcr) to the summated habit strength (V(sHr)).

Let $R$ denote the observable reaction magnitude (e.g., GSR amplitude) mediated by an aggregate stimulus. Let $f(\cdot)$ be the mapping function from effective habit strength ($H$) to reaction magnitude.

**Assumptions:**
1. **Monotonic Linear Relation:** $R = k \cdot H$, where $k > 0$ is a scaling constant. (This assumes the measurement scale is appropriately normalized).
2. **Combination Principle (Shunting):** For two independent stimulus aggregates $S_1$ and $S_2$ with respective habit strengths $H_1$ and $H_2$, the effective combined habit strength $H_{joint}$ when presented simultaneously is governed by a non-linear combination rule (often modeled as a parallel resistor network):
   $$ H_{joint} = \frac{H_1 H_2}{H_1 + H_2 - H_1 H_2 / K} $$ 
   *(Note: The exact formula depends on the specific formulation of Hull's postulate, but the crucial property is sub-additivity: $H_{joint} < H_1 + H_2$).*
3. **Strict Monotonicity:** $f'(H) > 0$.

**Formulation of Corollary I:**
Given $R(S_i) = f(H_i)$, the corollary posits:
$$ R(S_1 \cup S_2) < R(S_1) + R(S_2) $$
Substituting the operators:
$$ f(H_{joint}) < f(H_1) + f(H_2) $$

If $f$ is linear, $f(H) = cH$. Then $c \cdot H_{joint} < c \cdot H_1 + c \cdot H_2 \implies H_{joint} < H_1 + H_2$, which is exactly the premise of the combination principle.

**Units and Dimensions:**
- $H$ (Habit Strength): Dimensionless probability-like quantity ranging theoretically from 0 to 1 (or scaled 0 to 100). It represents the degree of neural pathway efficiency established through reinforcement.
- $R$ (Reaction Amplitude): Measured in microsiemens ($\mu S$) for GSR, milliliters (mL) for saliva, or degrees/grams for lid reflexes.
- The ratio $k = dR/dH$ carries units of [Reaction]/[Habit].

**Singularity and Breakdown Regimes:**
- **Ceiling Effects (Probability Domain):** As noted in the footnote, for striated muscles, amplitude is replaced by probability $p$. Here, $R \rightarrow p \in [0,1]$. The linear assumption breaks down near boundaries. If $p(H)$ approaches 1 asymptotically, adding more habit strength yields diminishing returns. The inequality still holds trivially if $p(H_1)+p(H_2)>1$ and $p(H_{joint}) \le 1$. However, if both $p(H_1)$ and $p(H_2)$ are small and $p(H)$ is highly convex initially, local violations could theoretically occur before the combination principle dominates.
- **Negative Interference:** If $S_1$ and $S_2$ compete for the same response channel but evoke opposing muscular tensions (e.g., left vs right press), vector cancellation occurs. $R_{joint}$ may approach zero while $R(S_1) + R(S_2)$ remains positive, satisfying the inequality drastically, but violating the scalar addition assumption implicitly used in simple amplitude measures.
- **Saturation of Drive (D):** If the organism's available drive $D$ is depleted such that $D < D_{threshold}$, $R \approx 0$ regardless of $H$, causing the functional form to collapse entirely.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The intellectual lineage of Corollary I traces back to the late 19th-century psychophysics of Ernst Heinrich Weber and Gustav Fechner. The fundamental insight that sensory/response magnitudes do not sum arithmetically laid the groundwork for understanding biological non-linearity. Specifically, the concept of "saturation"—where adding more stimulus intensity yields progressively smaller increases in perception—is mathematically captured by the logarithmic nature of sensation.
In the early 20th century, Pavlovian conditioning introduced the variable of 'strength' of a conditioned connection. However, it was behaviorist E.L. Thorndike's Law of Effect that first attempted to quantify the frequency of response based on satisfaction. Hull radically restructured these ideas by importing mathematical rigor from physics (specifically electrical circuit theory) into psychology. By modeling the nervous system as a set of pathways with varying resistances, he inherently adopted Ohm's Law analogies. In electrical networks, conductances in parallel combine sub-additively regarding voltage drops (or currents divide), leading directly to the intuition that two stimuli acting on overlapping neural substrates cannot double the output current.
This genealogy highlights a multi-week trap for modern researchers: assuming that because the underlying biology is electrochemical, linear superposition applies at the behavioral level. Hull demonstrated decades ago that neural convergence acts as a bottleneck. The historical shift from Wundt's associative psychology (simple addition of ideas) to Hull's field-theoretic approach marks the precise moment where "complexity" entered behavioral prediction, forcing scientists to account for interference and inhibition long before the term "connectionist" existed.

Consider a rat trained to press a lever (Response A) upon hearing Tone 1 (Stimulus 1) and another lever (Response B) upon hearing Tone 2 (Stimulus 2).
When presented with both tones simultaneously (Stimulus 1+2), a naive additive model suggests the rat should exhibit a combined vigor. However, the rat's internal state—a recursive loop evaluating past contingencies—triggers conflict resolution mechanisms. The cognitive dissonance of "Should I execute A or B?" induces hesitation (latency increase) or suppression of both actions (response inhibition). This is a higher-order manifestation of Corollary I: the *behavioral* outcome (probability of pressing either lever) is significantly dampened compared to the sum of probabilities in isolation.
In crisis situations, such as panic attacks or high-stress tactical environments, this sub-additivity becomes a maladaptive trap. An individual conditioned to react to Threat Cue X and Threat Cue Y independently may find themselves paralyzed (zero reaction) when exposed to a compound threat containing both X and Y. The habit strengths $sH_r(X)$ and $sH_r(Y)$ are massive, yet the combined reaction fails to trigger due to the shunting effect, interpreted cognitively as overwhelming ambiguity or decision fatigue. The "trap" is expecting resilience from redundant cues, only to encounter systemic failure due to interference.
