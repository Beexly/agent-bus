Lineage (backward in time from this paper):
- Noisy Student self-training, Xie et al. [18] — the paper explicitly ports its iterative generate-filter-retrain loop to HPE: "Xie et al. [18] show that this iterative process performs well on ImageNet … This paper uses this approach adapted to human pose estimation" [PAGE 3].
- Pseudo-labeling, Lee [17] (2013) — the origin of "network predictions used as annotations" [PAGE 3].
- Mean Teacher, Tarvainen & Valpola [15] (NeurIPS 2017) — EMA-teacher/consistency, "great improvements on common image classification benchmarks" [PAGE 4]; here adapted with dropout-in-fusion-layers as the perturbation operator.
- HPE-specific consistency: Xie et al. [16] (occlusion/cutout augmentation), [15]-style ensembles — cited as prior consistency regularizers [PAGE 3].
- Backbone descent: HRNet [9] → HigherHRNet [10]; the multi-resolution, highest-resolution-preserving design is why the authors call it "state-of-the-art" [PAGE 3].
- Sports-HPE application layer: ski-jump flight angles [3], triple/long-jump event detection [2], multi-person 3D from TV footage [1,11].

Multi-week traps this lineage hides:
(1) The confidence-miscalibration trap — teams spend weeks tuning a confidence threshold that the paper shows is *not monotonically related to pixel error* [PAGE 3]; the augmentation-MSE selector is the escape hatch, but only if augmentations are label-preserving.
(2) The imbalance trap — a global confidence cut silently starves low-frequency joints; the per-joint quantile design is the countermeasure, and forgetting it costs weeks of "why is the ankle never improving."
(3) The validation-size trap — the paper flags that they used the 200-image validation set "for comparability" but "verified that a validation set of 50 images leads to similar results" [PAGE 5]; practitioners who over-tune early stopping on tiny validation sets chase noise.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Modeling the decision agents around this pipeline.

The coach/practitioner (resource-bounded agent). Objective: acceptable keypoint tracking at minimal annotation cost. Crisis habit under deadline: revert to (a) annotating more frames by hand, or (b) trusting the network's confidence and shipping. Both are the paper's explicit targets. The paper reframes the coach's utility: "The expense to collect video material is very low, as it only requires a smartphone or small camera. Furthermore, annotating 50 images by hand is also done quickly" [PAGE 6] — i.e., it moves the coach from the expensive axis (annotation) to the cheap axis (video capture + frame selection).

Counter-trap (what the authors anticipate the field will do wrong). The naive move is global confidence thresholding; the paper predicts and blocks it via balanced per-joint MSE selection, so that "the detection performance of joints with a lot of pseudo labels increases, while the scores of joints with fewer labels stagnate" [PAGE 3] is avoided.

The clock. Three clocks interact: (i) annotation clock (50 vs 100 vs 250 images — diminishing returns quantified in Table 2: post-self-training gaps of only 0.6% between 50↔100 and 1.0% between 100↔250 [PAGE 6]); (ii) training clock (10k pseudo steps + 250 fine-tune steps per iteration × up to 4 iterations vs single-shot mean teacher); (iii) inference clock (student-only inference, no teacher needed at test time [PAGE 4]). The authors' revealed preference: spend the compute clock, save the annotation clock. Table 2 is the decision surface — "there is a trade-off between efficiency of the self-supervised learning and the final absolute score" [PAGE 6].
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical / biomechanical / acquisition vetoes on the reported numbers.

Veto 1 — sampling theorem vs motion blur. Capture is "a constant frame rate of 200Hz" [PAGE 4], clips "between 670 and 1900 frames." At 200 Hz the Nyquist-safe assumption is that inter-frame joint displacement stays within the PCK tolerance. For a takeoff foot moving near ~9–11 m/s, per-frame displacement ≈ 45–55 mm; PCK@0.1 tolerance ≈ 60 mm. So single-frame keypoint error budget is physically tight but not violated — the 200 Hz choice is defensible. A 30 fps smartphone capture (which the paper invites, [PAGE 6]) would give ~300 mm inter-frame motion ≫ 60 mm tolerance, so the *reported* PCK does not transfer to low-frame-rate acquisition. Proof of veto: displacement-per-frame $>$ $t\,d_{\text{ref}}$ breaks the label-quality assumption of both self-training loops.

Veto 2 — biomechanical asymmetry vs horizontal flip. The selector and mean teacher both use horizontal flip as a label-preserving augmentation ([PAGE 4]). In long/triple jump the takeoff leg is functionally asymmetric from the swing leg; a flip maps a right-footed takeoff to an anatomically atypical left-footed pose. If the network learns flip-invariance, it also learns to be indifferent to which leg leads — a biomechanical error the PCK metric (symmetric per-keypoint) will *not* penalize because left/right keypoints are swapped consistently. Veto stands unless the flip operator also swaps L/R keypoint identities (not stated in the source — flagged as underspecified).

Veto 3 — weather/illumination on outdoor pits. Long/triple jump is contested outdoors; color jitter and scale/rotation augmentation ([PAGE 4]) do not model rain, low sun, or wet-runway specularities. The 88.6% PCK is measured on a test set drawn from the same competition/training distribution [PAGE 4]; no cross-condition split is reported, so the number is in-distribution and cannot be extrapolated to adverse weather without a dedicated test. This is a distribution-shift veto, not a metric artifact.

Net adjudication: no physical constant is violated by the in-distribution result; the vetoes bind specifically on (frame-rate reduction), (left/right biomechanical semantics), and (out-of-distribution weather) — precisely the axes the evaluation does not cover.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable scenarios (each a falsifiable reward signal) that would veto generalization, with concrete predictions.

Veto A — Cross-athlete holdout. Train on the 122 training videos, test on the 46 held-out videos (as done) but additionally stratify test by *unseen athletes*. Prediction: PCK@0.1 for never-seen body morphologies drops below the 83.8 supervised-50 baseline for toe/heel keypoints, because pseudo-labels encode the training athletes' limb proportions. Falsifiable test: re-run Table 1 with athlete-disjoint folds; verify whether the 88.6 → ~83 range holds. If it holds, the method captures domain geometry, not athlete identity — reward granted; if it collapses, veto sustained.

Veto B — Frame-rate reduction (from Veto 1). Downsample input video from 200 Hz to 25/30 Hz while keeping the 50-label budget and re-run the pseudo-label loop. Prediction: pseudo-label MSE floor rises (motion blur widens $\overline{\text{MSE}}_k$), so the $Q_{0.6}$ gate admits blurrier labels; PCK@0.1 degrades by more than the 0.2–0.6% iteration-to-iteration gains reported on [PAGE 5–6], likely returning near the 83.8 baseline. Verifiable reward: monotonic PCK decline as frame rate falls below the ~100 Hz needed to keep inter-frame displacement under $t\,d_{\text{ref}}$.

Veto C — Confidence/MSE decoupling stress test. Construct a controlled set where softmax confidence is high but pixel error is large (e.g., symmetric low-texture poses — athlete against a plain sky). The paper's own premise [PAGE 3] is that confidence and error decouple; the augmentation-MSE selector is the proposed fix. Prediction: on this set, confidence-threshold selection collapses (near-random precision) while augmentation-MSE selection retains positive precision, quantifying the selector's marginal value. Verifiable reward: precision@k of pseudo-labels under each selector; if augmentation-MSE does not beat confidence-threshold here, the paper's central methodological claim is vetoed.

Summary judgment: within the reported in-distribution, 200 Hz, same-athlete-family setting, the 83.8% → 88.6% PCK@0.1 gain (≈59% of the fully-supervised gap closed with 1.585% of labels) is internally consistent and arithmetically supported, with two caveats surfaced by audit — the "more than 60%" headline is ≈59.3% strictly, and the 4,522-vs-4,416 image count does not reconcile as printed. The three RLVR vetoes are the decisive, runnable tests of whether the gain is domain-geometry transfer or dataset-specific fitting.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
