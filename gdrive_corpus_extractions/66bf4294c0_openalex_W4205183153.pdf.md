The source is not a play-calling document; it is a Bayesian statistical study of intended serve direction in professional tennis. The first multi-hop inference is that the response is categorical, not continuous: serves are classified as Wide, Body, or T, with the service court divided into three equally spaced zones and axes recorded in metres [PAGE 13]. The abstract already warns that the conclusions are about discernible differences rather than deterministic laws: “We ﬁnd discernible diﬀerences between men’s and women’s tennis, and between individual players” [PAGE 3], and it reports a general tendency: “the preference serving towards the Body on second serve and on high pressure points are revealed” [PAGE 3]. A second hop is that the paper’s risk logic is geometric and probabilistic: “serves aimed at the two corners (Wide and T) are risky and more likely to land out; by contrast, serves aimed towards the middle (Body) are safe and more likely to land in the service box” [PAGE 9]. Thus the model is not merely describing locations; it is describing a trade-off between corner-seeking and fault-avoidance.

The third hop is the model structure. With three unordered categories, the paper states: “A multinomial logit (MNL) model with C unordered response categories requires the speciﬁcation of C-1 logit equations, owing to identiﬁability concerns” [PAGE 20]. Because the fitted models use Body as the reference category [PAGE 23], the estimable contrasts are T-versus-Body and Wide-versus-Body. The Expanded Model adds player-varying intercepts and covariates: \(\eta_{itr}=\alpha_{ir}+X_{it}^{\top}\beta_r\) [PAGE 22]. The hierarchical layer assumes player intercepts are drawn from a common distribution, with Cholesky-factorized covariance and LKJ and half-Cauchy priors [PAGE 21]. A fourth hop is predictive: the paper defines \(p(\tilde y|\tilde x,x,y)=\int p(\tilde y|\tilde x,\theta)\pi(\theta|y,x)d\theta\) [PAGE 22], approximated by 4,000 posterior draws [PAGE 23], [PAGE 27].

The gray-zone audit begins with the data-generating process. The corpus contains 82 men’s and 81 women’s matches, with 23,588 and 14,862 serve observations, and 74 male and 78 female participants [PAGE 12]. But net faults were imputed, and the paper explicitly cautions: “This approximation may not be entirely valid, since players are known to impart top- and side-spin on served balls, as well as gravity that induce a non-linear ball trajectory” [PAGE 13], [PAGE 14]. Since “faults consisted of ∼30% of serve observations; of all faults, ∼25% are classiﬁed as net faults” [PAGE 14], the imputation is not a cosmetic detail. It is a source of misclassification that can shift the Wide/Body/T labels. Another gray zone is match-to-match variation: the model assumes “serve decisions do not vary significantly match-to-match” and lets \(t\) expand across matches [PAGE 20]. That is an assumption, not a demonstrated invariance; the discussion acknowledges that “players can easily change their serve tendencies on a whim” [PAGE 28]. The WAIC evidence is strong but relative: the Expanded Model has lower WAIC than the Player-Varying Intercept and Common Intercept models for both sexes [PAGE 23], but WAIC is a predictive model-comparison criterion, not a causal identification proof. Finally, the paper limits generalization: “The inferences gained cannot be generalized to amateur players” [PAGE 28]. I therefore treat the paper as evidence for professional Roland Garros serving patterns under the stated model, not as a universal theory of tennis behavior.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The core source operator is the multinomial logit link for \(C=3\) unordered serve-direction categories. Let \(Y_{it}\in\{\mathrm{Wide},\mathrm{Body},T\}\) be server \(i\)’s intended direction on serve \(t\). Following the paper’s reference-category choice, set \(C=\mathrm{Body}\), so the two estimable logit components are
\[
\eta_{itT}=\log\left(\frac{\pi_{itT}}{\pi_{it,\mathrm{Body}}}\right),
\qquad
\eta_{itW}=\log\left(\frac{\pi_{itW}}{\pi_{it,\mathrm{Body}}}\right),
\]
with \(\pi_{itr}=\Pr(Y_{it}=r)\). This is the paper’s \(C-1\) logit construction [PAGE 20], with Body as reference [PAGE 23]. The inverse softmax map is
\[
\pi_{itT}=\frac{\exp(\eta_{itT})}{1+\exp(\eta_{itT})+\exp(\eta_{itW})},
\quad
\pi_{itW}=\frac{\exp(\eta_{itW})}{1+\exp(\eta_{itT})+\exp(\eta_{itW})},
\]
\[
\pi_{it,\mathrm{Body}}=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\eta_{itT})+\exp(\eta_{itW})}.
\]
These are dimensionless probabilities. The expanded linear predictor is
\[
\eta_{itr}=\alpha_{ir}+X_{it}^{\top}\beta_r,\qquad r\in\{T,W\},
\]
where \(\alpha_{ir}\) is a player-varying intercept and \(X_{it}\) is a \(p\times 1\) covariate vector [PAGE 22]. The coefficient vector \(\beta_r\) is on the logit scale: for a continuous covariate, its unit is “logit per covariate unit”; for an indicator, it is “logit change for 0 to 1.” Lateral and longitudinal impact distances are geometric distances, with axes recorded in metres [PAGE 13]. Point importance is dimensionless on \([0,1]\): “0 represents no importance and 1 represents maximal match importance” [PAGE 16]. The intercept prior in the common-intercept model is
\[
(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{C-1})\sim N_{C-1}(0,\nu I_{C-1}),
\]
and the player-varying hierarchy uses
\[
(\alpha_{i1},\ldots,\alpha_{i,C-1})\sim N_{C-1}\left((\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{C-1}),\Sigma_{\alpha i}\right),
\]
with Cholesky factorization, a weakly informative half-Cauchy prior on scale parameters, and an LKJ prior on the correlation matrix [PAGE 21]. The \(\beta_r\) vector receives a vague multivariate normal prior with mean 0 and standard deviation \(\nu_\beta\) [PAGE 22]. The predictive distribution is
\[
p(\tilde y|\tilde x,x,y)=\int p(\tilde y|\tilde x,\theta)\pi(\theta|y,x)d\theta,
\]
approximated by posterior draws \(\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(M)}\) and sampled \(\tilde y^{(m)}\sim p(\tilde y|\tilde x,\theta^{(m)})\) [PAGE 22]. The paper uses 4,000 posterior draws [PAGE 23].

Assumptions include: a valid reference category with positive probability; exchangeable player intercepts drawn from a common distribution [PAGE 21]; no material match-to-match variation in serve decisions [PAGE 20]; approximately linear trajectory imputation for net faults [PAGE 13], [PAGE 14]; and pointwise predictive densities suitable for WAIC comparison [PAGE 23]. Exact breakdown regimes are: (1) logit singularity if \(\pi_{it,\mathrm{Body}}=0\) or if a category is perfectly separated, making the log-ratio undefined or the posterior unstable; (2) non-positive-definite or near-singular \(\Sigma_{\alpha i}\), which breaks the Cholesky parameterization [PAGE 21]; (3) collinearity or sparse interaction cells, especially for left-handed women on the Advantage court and for serve-number-by-backhand interactions, which can make main and interaction effects weakly identified; (4) imputation-validity breakdown when spin, gravity, or side-spin substantially bend the trajectory, because the net-fault imputation assumes a linear path to \(z=0\) [PAGE 13], [PAGE 14]; and (5) model-selection overreach: WAIC is described as lower-is-better [PAGE 23], but \(\Delta\)WAIC standard errors do not convert a predictive ranking into a causal proof. The reported coefficients in Table 4.2 are posterior means with standard errors [PAGE 26]; for men, Serve Number has T logit \(-2.17\) with SE \(0.07\) and Wide logit \(-1.58\) with SE \(0.06\), while Point Importance has T logit \(-1.55\) with SE \(0.55\) and Wide logit \(-0.75\) with SE \(0.53\) [PAGE 26]. For women, Serve Number has T logit \(-1.45\) with SE \(0.08\) and Wide logit \(-0.97\) with SE \(0.07\) [PAGE 26]. These values are not probabilities; they are logit-scale contrasts relative to Body.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s scientific lineage is a retrocausal chain in which later modeling choices reinterpret earlier tennis claims. Morris’s 1977 point-importance idea, later popularized by Kovalchik and Reid, becomes the \([0,1]\) match-pressure covariate in this 2021 model [PAGE 16]. Walker and Wooders’s 2001 finding that player serve decisions are not serially independent becomes the lagged covariates “Serve at \(t-1\) is T” and “Serve at \(t-1\) is Wide” [PAGE 10], [PAGE 19]. Loffing, Hagemann, and Strauss’s handedness work becomes the server-handedness term and the Court Side by Server Handedness interaction [PAGE 10], [PAGE 19]. Reid, Whiteside, and Elliott’s biomechanical work on ball position at impact becomes the lateral and longitudinal distance covariates [PAGE 9], [PAGE 19]. Unierzyski and Wieczorek’s serve-number observation becomes the empirical claim that first serves target Wide and T while Body is more common on second serve [PAGE 10]. Bailey and McGarrity’s pressure/backhand claim becomes the second interaction: Serve Number times Returner’s BH Location [PAGE 19]. Vernon, Farrow, and Reid’s elite-returner anticipation work becomes the player-specific intercept hierarchy and the predictive Federer example [PAGE 10], [PAGE 27].

This genealogy creates several multi-week research traps. The first is reifying exploratory density maps. Figure 2.3 shows different serve-location densities for men and women and for individuals, but the text treats these as motivating tendencies, not causal effects [PAGE 15]. A second trap is treating point importance as if it were a direct measure of psychological pressure. The source defines it only as an expected change in match-win probability proxy, a score in \([0,1]\) [PAGE 16]; it does not measure arousal, threat, or explicit opponent belief. A third trap is treating imputed net-fault bounces as observed data. The paper imputes net faults by a linear trajectory and then admits the approximation “may not be entirely valid” because of top-spin, side-spin, and gravity [PAGE 13], [PAGE 14]. A fourth trap is over-reading WAIC. The Expanded Model is clearly preferred: men’s WAIC is 41,147.5 versus 44,378.2 for Player-Varying Intercept and 44,744.0 for Common Intercept; women’s is 28,281.0 versus 30,009.0 and 30,587.4 [PAGE 23]. But a large \(\Delta\)WAIC relative to its SE establishes better predictive fit, not a causal mechanism. A fifth trap is pooling professional and amateur contexts. The discussion states: “The inferences gained cannot be generalized to amateur players” [PAGE 28]. A sixth trap is assuming stable player identities across weeks or tournaments: “players can easily change their serve tendencies on a whim” [PAGE 28]. A seventh trap is sex comparison without sample-structure caution. The paper notes a scarcity of dominant left-handed women compared with Nadal on the men’s side [PAGE 24], so handedness-by-court effects are not symmetric evidence across sexes. The correct lineage reading is therefore conditional: these are regularized, partially pooled descriptions of professional Roland Garros serving under a particular data regime, not timeless laws of tennis.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive-theory-of-mind layer has three competing interpretations. The first is “crisis habit regression”: under pressure, the server narrows to a safe or habitual target. The source supports a conservative shift: “On average, the second serve greatly deters players from aiming towards the two corners” [PAGE 24], and the discussion says players “shrink towards more conservative Body serves during tense situations such as on second serve and on high pressure points” [PAGE 28]. The posterior table gives the same sign: men’s Serve Number T logit is \(-2.17\), Wide \(-1.58\); women’s T is \(-1.45\), Wide \(-0.97\) [PAGE 26]. Point Importance is also negative for T and Wide in both sexes [PAGE 26]. This is the habit-regression story: pressure and second-serve risk make Body more probable.

The second is “counter-trap”: a server who models the returner’s expectation can exploit it. The paper notes that “players tend to switch from one action to another far too often from an optimal strategy point-of-view” [PAGE 10], and it describes Federer as less predictable on less important points while Djokovic shows “virtually no change” in serve direction behavior across importance bins [PAGE 16]. The footnote even raises “mixed strategy implications” for Djokovic being more willing to randomize than Federer [PAGE 16]. But the model itself does not contain an explicit returner-belief state. It contains lagged serve choice and Returner’s BH Location [PAGE 19]. Therefore counter-trap reasoning is an interpretive overlay, not an identified causal parameter. The third is “clock”: serve number and point importance act as a state clock that changes risk tolerance. The Federer prediction example makes this concrete: trailing 0–1 in sets, 5–6 in games, facing breakpoint, and having previously served T, his first-serve prediction favors Wide and T, while on second serve “there is a clear preference serving Wide, which incidentally would target the returner’s backhand” [PAGE 27]. The clock is not literal time; it is the ordered match-state variable set. A recursive returner would anticipate the second-serve backhand tendency, and a counter-trap server would deviate; but the paper’s data only support the observable conditional probabilities, not the private belief recursion. Thus the strongest defensible synthesis is: crisis habit regression is directly supported by the coefficient signs; counter-trap is suggested by player heterogeneity and the mixed-strategy footnote but not structurally identified; clock effects are supported through serve number, point importance, and lagged serve covariates, while the full theory-of-mind recursion remains a hypothesis.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical-layer veto rule is geometry first. The service court is divided into three zones, and the axes are recorded in metres [PAGE 13]. Any claim about serve direction must therefore be compatible with measured lateral and longitudinal impact position and with the court’s discrete Wide/Body/T partition. The paper reports that “higher lateral impact distances promote a Wide or Body serve over a T serve” and that “higher longitudinal impact distances are associated with serves aimed Wide or T — especially among women” [PAGE 24]. Those claims are admissible because the covariates are measured and included in Table 2.1 [PAGE 19]. The physical explanation that T is preferred because “the net is six inches lower in the center” [PAGE 24] is a plausible geometric account, but it is not a measured covariate in the model; it should be treated as interpretation, not as an estimated effect.

The biomechanical veto rule is stricter. The model includes lateral and longitudinal impact distance, but it does not include ball-toss trajectory, racket kinematics, or player position on the baseline: the paper says those features “could not be extracted from our data, and will not be considered” [PAGE 16]. Therefore any claim that a specific toss trajectory or racket-angle mechanism explains the fitted serve-direction probabilities is vetoed as unsupported by this source. The net-fault imputation is also biomechanically vulnerable: the paper assumes a linear trajectory to \(z=0\), but acknowledges that top-spin, side-spin, and gravity “induce a non-linear ball trajectory” [PAGE 13], [PAGE 14]. Because faults are about 30% of serves and net faults about 25% of faults [PAGE 14], a biomechanical objection to the imputation is not trivial. The correct adjudication is to accept the model’s conditional associations but downgrade any exact physical reconstruction of net-fault serves.

The weather-layer veto is absolute for this source. No weather variable appears in the dataset description or in the candidate predictor table [PAGE 12], [PAGE 19]. There is no measured temperature, wind, humidity, precipitation, or air-density operator, and no weather coefficient in Table 4.2 [PAGE 26]. Therefore a weather-specific causal claim about serve direction is outside the identified model. This does not prove weather is irrelevant; it proves the source cannot adjudicate it. The cross-layer veto proof is: if a claim requires a variable \(V\) that is neither measured nor included in \(X_{it}\), then the model’s posterior predictive distribution cannot distinguish that claim from an omitted-variable explanation. Weather is such a \(V\); toss trajectory is such a \(V\); net clearance is not directly modeled. Physical geometry and measured impact distances survive the veto; unmeasured biomechanical and weather stories do not.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
