The source defines the convGRU operator on an input tensor \(x_t\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times W\times H}\), with the paper’s stated assumption that \(r_t,z_t,h_t\) have compatible axes [PAGE 5]. For video experiments, frames are resized to \(180\times180\) pixels and downsampled from 60 Hz to 30 Hz; the original capture is \(1280\times720\) pixels at 60 frames per second, with videos about 5 seconds long on average [PAGE 8]. The sequence length is fixed at 60 frames, equal to 2 seconds at 30 Hz [PAGE 8–9]. These are pixel and time units, not abstract ratios.

The convGRU equations are:
\[
r_t=\sigma\!\left(W_r\ast[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_r\right),\qquad
z_t=\sigma\!\left(W_z\ast[h_{t-1},x_t]+b_z\right),
\]
\[
\hat h_t=\tanh\!\left(W_{\hat h}\ast[h_{t-1}\odot r_t,x_t]+b_{\hat h}\right),
\qquad
h_t=h_{t-1}\odot(1-z_t)+z_t\odot\hat h_t,
\]
where \(\ast\) is convolution, \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication, and \([a,b]\) is channel-wise concatenation [PAGE 5]. \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid; \(z_t,r_t\) are dimensionless gate activations in \([0,1]\); \(h_t\) is an activation tensor whose numerical units are network-dependent and not specified in the paper. For a \(k\times k\) filter, padding \((k-1)/2\) preserves spatial size when \(k\) is odd [PAGE 6].

The bounding-box head is:
\[
h^{pool}_t=W_{pool}\ast\mathrm{MaxPool}(h_t)+b_{pool},
\]
\[
bb_t=W_{bb}\cdot \mathrm{NN}_2\!\left(\mathrm{NN}_1\!\left(\mathrm{Flatten}(h^{pool}_t)\right)\right)+b_{bb},
\]
\[
\mathrm{NN}_l(x)=\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(W_{NN_l}\cdot x+b_{NN_l}\right),
\]
where \(h^{pool}_t\) has shape \(1\times W_{pool}\times H_{pool}\), and Flatten reshapes it to \(1\times(W_{pool}H_{pool})\) [PAGE 6]. \(bb_t\in\mathbb{R}^4\) is a bounding-box coordinate vector. The paper does not explicitly state whether these coordinates are in 180-pixel resized units or normalized units; if they are pixel coordinates, the smooth-L1 loss is in pixel-based units, and IOU is dimensionless.

The mask is constructed as a 3-channel zero tensor with the predicted box outline drawn at the corresponding pixel locations; the outline values are set to 255 [PAGE 6]. The weighted hidden state is:
\[
h^{mask}_t=\beta h_t+(1-\beta)mask_t,\qquad \beta\in[0,1],
\]
where \(\beta\) is dimensionless and \(mask_t\) is in 8-bit intensity units as written [PAGE 6]. This equation is the central operator. It is a convex combination only if \(h_t\) and \(mask_t\) are placed on the same numerical scale. The paper does not report such a normalization. That is a formal assumption gap and a breakdown risk.

The maskGRU recurrent equations are:
\[
r_t=\mathrm{IN}\!\left(\sigma\!\left(W_r\ast[h^{mask}_{t-1},x_t]+b_r\right)\right),
\]
\[
z_t=\mathrm{IN}\!\left(\sigma\!\left(W_z\ast[h^{mask}_{t-1},x_t]+b_z\right)\right),
\]
\[
\hat h_t=\mathrm{IN}\!\left(\tanh\!\left(W_{\hat h}\ast[h^{mask}_{t-1}\odot r_t,x_t]+b_{\hat h}\right)\right),
\]
\[
h_t=h^{mask}_{t-1}\odot(1-z_t)+z_t\odot\hat h_t,
\]
with instance normalization \(\mathrm{IN}(\cdot)\) added after each activation [PAGE 6]. The paper states: “We chose to use instance normalization, which avoids this issue, after each activation function in maskGRU as it resulted in the loss converging to a lesser value during training” [PAGE 6]. The exact IN definition and its stabilizer are not expanded in the source. Any implementation requires a positive variance stabilizer; the mathematical singularity is at per-channel variance \(\sigma^2=0\), where normalization divides by zero unless a stabilizer is used.

Teacher forcing is stochastic:
\[
\epsilon(e)=\max(1-\gamma e,0),\qquad \gamma=0.01,
\]
where \(e\) is the epoch number and \(\epsilon\) is a dimensionless probability [PAGE 9]. At \(e=100\), \(\epsilon=0\). The paper states: “At each training iteration, teacher forcing is used (i.e. the ground truth bounding box is used to produce maskt) with probability ϵ = max(1−γ(epoch number), 0), where γ is a hyperparameter” [PAGE 9]. The reported setting is \(\gamma=0.01\) [PAGE 9].

The evaluation metric is:
\[
\mathrm{IOU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}\in[0,1],
\]
a dimensionless overlap ratio. It is not a calibrated probability unless one adopts a uniform-random-point interpretation: IOU equals the probability that a point drawn uniformly from \(A\cup B\) lies in \(A\cap B\). The paper reports average IOU values: maskGRU 0.3009 training and 0.002531 validation; convGRU 0.03092 and 0.002008; convSTAR 0.007945 and 0.001509 [PAGE 9]. The validation IOU of maskGRU is approximately \(2.5\times10^{-3}\), i.e. a near-zero overlap on held-out data. No information-theoretic quantity in bits or nats is reported.

Exact breakdown regimes:
- \(\beta=1\): \(h^{mask}_t=h_t\); the mask term vanishes, and the model reduces to convGRU with instance normalization. The claimed mask benefit is absent.
- \(\beta=0\): \(h^{mask}_t=mask_t\); if the predicted box is absent, the mask is zero and the recurrent state is zero; if the outline is 255, the mask dominates the recurrent state. This is a hard switch, not a soft attention.
- Stale mask: if the object displacement between consecutive frames exceeds the mask width, \(\|\Delta p_t\|_2 \gt w_{mask}\), the previous-step mask does not overlap the object. The paper acknowledges that the mask is “from the previous time step” and “probably would not be directly overlapping the ball” [PAGE 8].
- Instance-normalization singularity: if a channel’s variance is exactly zero, \(\mathrm{IN}\) is undefined without a stabilizer.
- Teacher-forcing boundary: for \(\gamma=0.01\), \(\epsilon=0\) at \(e\ge100\); early stopping before that leaves a training-inference mismatch.
- Physical visibility: if motion blur length or projected object diameter falls below the sensor resolution, the observation is information-limited; no recurrent operator can recover the missing signal.

The scientific lineage is clear from the citations. Fully connected GRUs descend from Cho et al. [PAGE 10], LSTMs from Hochreiter and Schmidhuber [PAGE 11], convLSTM from Shi et al. [PAGE 11], and convGRU/GRU-RCN from Ballas et al. [PAGE 10]. The paper positions maskGRU as a modification of that lineage: convGRU is the recurrent backbone, STAR is the deeper-RNN alternative, and TrackNet/TrackNetv2, ATOM/COMET, and T-RexNet are the small-object tracking relatives [PAGE 4–5, PAGE 11–12]. The retrocausal question is whether the lineage’s assumptions actually transfer. The paper admits that gating is not enough: “Despite the use of a gating mechanism to help combat vanishing gradients, convGRUs are still susceptible to this problem, making training diﬃcult” [PAGE 2]. That admission is the genealogical hinge: maskGRU is not a refutation of gated recurrence; it is a patch for a failure mode that the gated-recurrence line already knew about.

Several multi-week traps follow from that lineage. The first is the gating trap: a team may spend weeks tuning convGRU depth, kernel size, and normalization before recognizing that the problem is the feedback state, not the gate. The paper’s own training note reinforces this: convGRU “was particularly diﬃcult to train” and required removing instance normalization, using batch size 1, using a shallower box predictor, and lowering \(\gamma\) [PAGE 9]. The second is the teacher-forcing trap: the paper’s schedule uses the ground-truth box to build \(mask_t\) with probability \(\epsilon\) [PAGE 9]. If a validation harness accidentally retains teacher forcing, or if training stops before \(\epsilon\) reaches zero at epoch 100, the reported gain can be an oracle-mask gain rather than a self-conditioned tracking gain. The third is the metric trap: maskGRU’s training IOU is 0.3009 while its validation IOU is 0.002531 [PAGE 9]. A team watching only training IOU could spend weeks believing the model is learning to track when it is mostly fitting the training sequences. The paper explicitly says it “struggles to track well on validation data” [PAGE 10].

The fourth trap is numerical scale. The mask uses 255-valued outlines [PAGE 6], while \(h_t\) is an activation tensor. Without a reported normalization, \(\beta\) is not a clean attention weight; it is a scale-sensitive interpolation between unlike quantities. Weeks can be lost to instability that looks like a vanishing-gradient problem but is actually a units problem. The fifth is the ablation trap. The paper’s causal claims are speculative [PAGE 7], and the conclusion says future work will “explore the reasons behind maskGRU’s eﬀectiveness through ablation experiments” [PAGE 10]. Until \(\beta\), mask presence, instance normalization, and teacher forcing are ablated independently, the genealogy of the improvement remains ambiguous: it could be mask attention, gradient damping, normalization, or regularization. The sixth is the dataset trap. The corpus is 88 videos from 15 courts, originally 1280×720 at 60 Hz, resized to 180×180 and 30 Hz, with 60-frame sequences [PAGE 8–9]. Cross-court, cross-lighting, and cross-sequence generalization are not established by a near-zero validation IOU. These traps are not hypothetical; they are the natural failure modes of a retrocausal reading that tries to infer mechanism from a single relative ranking.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In recursive terms, \(h_t\) is the model’s belief state, \(bb_t\) is its expressed intention, and \(mask_t\) is an externalized attention cue that is fed back into the next belief update. The crisis habit is regression to the prior: under motion blur, low resolution, or occlusion, the model cannot observe the ball, so it relies on the previous mask and the recurrent state. The paper’s own wording makes this explicit: the mask is “from the previous time step (and therefore probably would not be directly overlapping the ball in the current time step)” [PAGE 8]. That is a prior, not a measurement. If the prior is wrong, the loop can become self-confirming: wrong box → wrong mask → wrong hidden state → wrong next box. This is the counter-trap. Teacher forcing is the temporary escape: it substitutes the ground-truth box for the model’s own belief with probability \(\epsilon\) [PAGE 9]. But teacher forcing is an oracle habit. At inference, the oracle is gone, and the model must act on its own generated mask. If training stops before \(\epsilon=0\), the model has not practiced the inference-time recursion enough.

The clock matters in three places. First, the teacher-forcing clock: for \(\gamma=0.01\), the oracle probability reaches zero at epoch 100 [PAGE 9]. Early stopping can freeze the model in a partially oracle-dependent regime. Second, the sequence clock: each training sample is 60 frames, i.e. 2 seconds at 30 Hz [PAGE 8–9]. The hidden state is reset at every sequence boundary, so tracking across longer occlusions or across sequence cuts is not directly trained. Third, the physical clock: the original video is 60 Hz, but training uses 30 Hz [PAGE 8]. Halving the temporal sampling increases inter-frame displacement. If the ball moves more than the mask width between samples, the mask cue is temporally stale. A recursive theory of mind for this system should therefore include an uncertainty check: when the predicted box motion and the observed image motion disagree, the model should down-weight the mask rather than recursively trust it. The paper does not implement such a veto; \(\beta\) is fixed and there is no reported confidence estimate. That absence is the central recursive weakness: the model has a belief and an attention cue, but no explicit arbitration between them when they conflict.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer can veto the algorithmic claim. For a ball of diameter \(D\) meters at distance \(Z\) meters from a camera with focal length \(f_{px}\) in pixels, the projected diameter is approximately
\[
d_{px}=\frac{f_{px}D}{Z},
\]
in pixels, under the small-angle and \(D\ll Z\) assumptions. If \(d_{px}\) is only a few pixels, the ball occupies very little sensor information. Motion blur adds
\[
L_{blur}=v_{px}\tau_{exp},
\]
where \(v_{px}\) is the image-plane velocity in pixels per second and \(\tau_{exp}\) is the exposure time in seconds; \(L_{blur}\) is in pixels. If \(L_{blur}\gtrsim d_{px}\), the ball’s signal is smeared across the background. The paper directly reports this regime: “Due to a combination of motion blur and low resolution, the volleyball is not discernible to the human eye in the rightmost frames” [PAGE 2]. That is a physical visibility veto. No mask feedback can recover a ball that is not present in the sensor data; it can only propagate a prior.

The biomechanical layer adds clutter. Volleyball has six players per side, and the paper explicitly contrasts this with tennis and badminton: “since there are fewer players on the court in tennis and badminton, such sports have less complex background scenes compared to volleyball, where six players are present on each side of the court” [PAGE 5]. Player limbs, jumps, and lateral movements create large background motion. If the ball’s apparent motion is not separable from the players’ motion, a recurrent appearance model can lock onto a player. The mask cue is supposed to disambiguate the region, but it is generated from the previous prediction. If the previous prediction is on a player, the mask reinforces the wrong region. The physical and biomechanical vetoes therefore interact: low resolution weakens the observation, while high background motion strengthens the wrong prior.

Weather is not a primary variable in the paper because the footage is indoor volleyball. But the cross-layer logic still applies if the setting changes: rain, glare, or lighting transitions can reduce contrast below sensor noise, and wind can alter ball trajectory. In the paper’s indoor setting, the relevant weather-like layer is illumination and court-to-court domain shift. The corpus covers 15 courts and 88 videos [PAGE 8], but the validation IOU of 0.002531 [PAGE 9] shows that the learned representation does not transfer cleanly to unseen sequences. The paper’s own conclusion targets this: “Future work will explore how to improve maskGRU’s ability to track unseen video sequences” [PAGE 10]. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: physical visibility and biomechanical clutter can veto tracking independently of the recurrent architecture; the reported near-zero validation IOU is evidence that such vetoes are active, not merely that the optimizer needs more epochs.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: \(\beta=1\) ablation with the same convGRU, instance normalization, box predictor, optimizer, and data split. The prediction is that removing the mask term should reduce validation IOU relative to the reported maskGRU value of 0.002531 [PAGE 9] if the mask is causally useful. The verifiable reward is mean IOU on held-out videos. Veto condition: if \(\beta=1\) matches or exceeds maskGRU within a predeclared confidence interval, the mask-specific causal claim is vetoed; the improvement would be attributable to instance normalization, the box head, or training variance rather than the mask. This is directly checkable from equation (8) and the reported metric.

Scenario 2: random-mask or stale-mask control. Keep \(\beta\), the architecture, and the training schedule fixed, but replace the predicted-box mask with either a random box or a deliberately shifted previous-box mask. The prediction is that spatially misaligned masks should not improve validation IOU and should degrade it relative to the aligned predicted mask if the mechanism is attention-like. Veto condition: if random or stale masks produce equal or better held-out IOU, the “attention map” interpretation is vetoed; the effect is then likely a numerical regularizer or a scale artifact from the 255-valued mask [PAGE 6]. This control is verifiable because the mask tensor is an explicit input and the IOU is an explicit output.

Scenario 3: teacher-forcing-free training and held-out-court evaluation. Train with \(\epsilon=0\) throughout, or train with the paper’s schedule but evaluate only after \(\epsilon\) has reached zero at epoch 100 [PAGE 9], and report IOU separately on unseen courts. The prediction is that removing the oracle mask should either destabilize training or lower validation IOU; if performance is unchanged, the exposure-bias concern is vetoed and the mask loop is robust to self-generated masks. A second prediction is that held-out-court IOU should remain near the reported 0.002531 [PAGE 9] if domain shift is the dominant failure; if held-out-court IOU rises sharply, the original validation split was the problem, not the architecture. Veto condition: if the model fails to exceed the convGRU validation baseline of 0.002008 [PAGE 9] under oracle-free training on unseen courts, the general small-object tracking claim is vetoed regardless of training-set IOU.
