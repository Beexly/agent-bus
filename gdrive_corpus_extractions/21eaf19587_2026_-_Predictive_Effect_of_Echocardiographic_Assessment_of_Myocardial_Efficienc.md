The source’s central inference chain is: resting echocardiographic myocardial indices are measured under standardized conditions; athletes are split into top and bottom tertiles of a composite performance score; between-group differences and adjusted regression or logistic models show associations; the combined GLS plus GWE model improves internal classification. The first two links support association and internal discrimination, but the final link to “predictive effect” is not established by the design. The paper itself states: “The study did not test causal effects of training, longitudinal cardiac remodeling, or chronic adaptation” [PAGE 4], and the abstract states that “longitudinal validation is required before these measures can be considered predictive markers for talent identification or training decisions” [PAGE 1]. A multi-hop audit therefore separates three claims: (i) higher-performance tertile athletes differ in GLS, GWE, GWW, and MPI; (ii) GLS and GWE add incremental variance or AUC beyond covariates; (iii) these indices can predict future performance or talent. The source supports (i) and (ii) conditionally; it does not support (iii).

The most serious gray-zone issue is tertile circularity. The composite performance score incorporates anaerobic power, agility time, and competition index. The paper admits: “These differences were expected because anaerobic power, agility time, and competition index contributed to the composite performance score used for tertile classification; therefore, the results describe the magnitude and statistical consistency of group separation rather than independent causal effects” [PAGE 7]. Thus Table 2 differences of 11.7 versus 9.3 W/kg peak power, 9.0 versus 10.6 s T-test time, and 8.2 versus 5.1 competition index are not independent validation; they are constituent outcomes of the label. The logistic AUC of 0.91 for GLS plus GWE is therefore internal discrimination against a label built from related performance domains, not prospective prediction of an external competition outcome. The base covariate model already reaches AUC 0.82, and the combined model reaches 0.91 with 85.8% accuracy [PAGE 16]; the incremental gain is real within-sample but vulnerable to overfitting and label leakage.

The GLS direction is another gray zone. The source uses signed negative GLS in regression and GLS magnitude in correlation: “GLS was analysed as a signed negative percentage, with more negative values representing greater longitudinal myocardial shortening; GLS magnitude was also reported to make the direction of interpretation explicit” [PAGE 7]. Later: “GLS magnitude was used for correlation interpretation, while signed GLS was retained in regression models” [PAGE 9]. Therefore a negative βGLS and a positive magnitude-versus-performance correlation are not contradictory; they are different parameterizations. Any interpretation that treats the sign alone as a direct causal direction is invalid.

Measurement uncertainty also limits the claim. GWE and GWW are pressure-strain loop estimates: “GWE and GWW were interpreted as pressure-adjusted myocardial work indices derived from non-invasive pressure-strain loop modeling. These indices were not treated as direct measurements of whole-body metabolic energy efficiency” [PAGE 6]. They depend on cuff blood pressure, stable loading, and speckle tracking. The abstract reports GWE “96.8% vs. 92.1%, p = 0.002” [PAGE 1], while Table S1 reports GWE “92.1 ± 3.6” versus “96.0 ± 2.8” [PAGE 16]; that discrepancy is a minor but real gray zone. GWW is reported in mmHg%, and MPI uses Doppler timing, so the indices are not interchangeable metabolic measures.

Finally, the key points overstate the evidence relative to the limitations. The key points claim “robust, non-invasive predictors” and “over 80% accuracy” and advocate “integration of echocardiographic screening into athlete talent identification” [PAGE 15], while the Discussion states that “Application to athlete selection, talent identification, or development policy requires longitudinal validation, external replication, stress-imaging evidence, and proof of added value beyond established performance tests” [PAGE 13]. The defensible conclusion is narrower: cross-sectional association and internal tertile classification in a national-level martial arts cohort, with large effect sizes for GLS magnitude and GWE, but no validated predictive or talent-identification threshold.
</THINK_TRACE>

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
Global longitudinal strain is defined from the 17-segment model as
\[
\mathrm{GLS}=\frac{1}{17}\sum_{s=1}^{17}\varepsilon_s,\qquad
\varepsilon_s=\frac{L_s(t_{\mathrm{ES}})-L_s(t_0)}{L_s(t_0)}\times 100\%,
\]
where \(L_s\) is segment length, \(t_0\) is end-diastole, and \(t_{\mathrm{ES}}\) is end-systole. The source states GLS is “the average peak negative strain value from all 17 segments, expressed as a percentage” and requires tracking quality above 85 percent [PAGE 6]. Units are percent. More negative signed GLS means greater longitudinal shortening; the source explicitly notes that magnitude is used for correlation and signed GLS in regression [PAGE 7, PAGE 9]. Breakdown regimes: tracking quality below 85 percent, segment deviation above 10 percent, poor acoustic window, arrhythmia, or acute loading change invalidate the resting deformation estimate; sign conventions can also invert naive interpretation.

Global work efficiency is
\[
\mathrm{GWE}=\frac{W_{\mathrm{constructive}}}{W_{\mathrm{constructive}}+W_{\mathrm{wasted}}}\times 100\%,
\]
as “the ratio of constructive work to the sum of constructive and wasted work, expressed as a percentage” [PAGE 6]. GWE is dimensionless, reported as percent. Global wasted work is
\[
W_{\mathrm{wasted}}=W_{\mathrm{lengthening,systole}}+W_{\mathrm{shortening,IVR}},
\]
described as “energy expenditure during myocardial lengthening in systole and shortening in isovolumic relaxation” [PAGE 6]; Table S1 reports GWW in mmHg% [PAGE 16]. Assumptions: brachial blood pressure calibration, stable loading conditions, pressure-strain loop modeling, and adequate speckle tracking across all 17 segments. Breakdown regimes: cuff blood pressure error, unstable afterload, valvular disease, arrhythmia, or tracking failure; if \(W_{\mathrm{constructive}}+W_{\mathrm{wasted}}\to 0\), GWE is undefined. The source warns these are not direct whole-body metabolic efficiency measures [PAGE 6].

The myocardial performance index is
\[
\mathrm{MPI}=\frac{\mathrm{IVCT}+\mathrm{IVRT}}{\mathrm{ET}},
\]
with IVCT, IVRT, and ET in seconds or milliseconds; MPI is dimensionless. The source gives exactly “MPI = (IVCT + IVRT) ÷ ET” and uses pulsed-wave tissue Doppler at the lateral mitral annulus [PAGE 6]. Assumptions: correct Doppler alignment, three cardiac cycles, and 100 mm/s sweep speed. Breakdown regimes: angle misalignment, arrhythmia, loading change, or very small ET; as \(\mathrm{ET}\to 0\), the ratio is singular. Higher MPI means longer combined isovolumic intervals relative to ejection time; the source associates lower MPI with shorter agility time and fewer balance errors [PAGE 1, PAGE 9].

The z-score operator is
\[
Z_{i,k}=\frac{X_{i,k}-\bar X_k}{SD_k},
\]
where \(X_{i,k}\) is athlete \(i\)’s raw score in domain \(k\), \(\bar X_k\) is the sample mean, and \(SD_k\) is the sample standard deviation; T-test agility time is reverse-coded before aggregation [PAGE 5]. The extracted text then says “The final composite performance score was calculated as:” but the displayed equation is missing [PAGE 5]. The only defensible general expression is
\[
C_i=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k Z_{i,k},
\]
with weights \(w_k\) not recoverable from the supplied pages; equal weighting would be \(w_k=1/K\), but that should not be invented. Breakdown regimes: \(SD_k=0\), correlated domains, arbitrary weights, and sample-dependent tertile thresholds. The logistic model is
\[
\operatorname{logit}P(Y_i=1)=\beta_0+\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}}\mathrm{GLS}_i+\beta_{\mathrm{GWE}}\mathrm{GWE}_i+\gamma^{\top}X_i,
\]
where \(Y_i=1\) is higher-performance tertile status and \(X_i\) includes age, sex, discipline, training age, weekly training load, body fat percentage, and skeletal muscle mass [PAGE 7]. Coefficients are per percentage-point change in GLS or GWE. Discrimination is summarized by
\[
\mathrm{AUC}=P(\hat p_i>\hat p_j\mid Y_i=1,Y_j=0).
\]
Breakdown regimes: perfect separation, small events per predictor, tertile circularity, and overfitting; the source reports 1,000 bootstrap resamples and VIF below 5.0 [PAGE 7]. Table S5 gives AUC 0.91 for the combined GLS plus GWE model and 85.8% accuracy [PAGE 16], but that is internal classification, not external prediction.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The scientific lineage runs from volumetric ejection fraction to deformation imaging to pressure-strain myocardial work to composite athlete profiling. The source positions GLS as a marker of longitudinal deformation, GWE and GWW as pressure-adjusted work-efficiency indices, and MPI as a composite systolic-diastolic timing index [PAGE 1, PAGE 2]. Historically, LVEF describes volumetric output but not contractile or metabolic efficiency; the paper says traditional parameters such as LVEF “represent volumetric output without capturing contractile or metabolic efficiency” [PAGE 1]. GLS and myocardial work were developed in clinical cardiology for earlier detection of subclinical dysfunction, then transplanted into athlete monitoring through papers on endurance athletes, basketball players, handball and football players, and COVID-19 recovery [PAGE 2, PAGE 3]. The martial arts application is the newest branch, and the source admits that “Combat-sport specific evidence remains absent” [PAGE 2].

The retrocausal trap is that observed cardiac differences may be downstream of training exposure and selection rather than upstream causes of performance. The higher-performance group had longer training age, more weekly sessions, greater weekly training load, lower body fat, higher skeletal muscle mass, and lower resting heart rate and diastolic blood pressure [PAGE 8]. The paper states these variables were “retained as descriptive characteristics and covariates in subsequent models rather than interpreted as causal determinants of cardiac or performance outcomes” [PAGE 7]. That is the correct guardrail, but it also means the echo-performance associations may reflect reverse causation: better athletes train more, and more training produces or preserves favorable cardiac remodeling.

Multi-week traps are therefore central. The study controls only a 48-hour testing window: athletes avoided caffeine and alcohol for 24 hours and strenuous exercise for 48 hours before testing [PAGE 5], and cardiac and performance testing were completed within a fixed 48-hour window [PAGE 5]. That is too short to capture periodization, detraining, injury, competition congestion, menstrual cycle, sleep debt, or multi-week training-load accumulation. The competition index uses the five most recent official matches within 12 months [PAGE 6], while eligibility requires at least 5 years of training and average weekly frequency of at least 4 sessions over the past 6 months [PAGE 4]. Those windows create a retrospective mosaic, not a longitudinal prediction. The sensitivity decile analysis uses only 36 athletes and has a wide AUC interval of 0.76 to 0.98 [PAGE 17], confirming that extreme-tail claims are unstable. The genealogy therefore supports a cautious interpretation: echo indices are plausible correlates of long-term training adaptation, but the causal arrow and the multi-week predictive horizon remain unproven.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression appears when selection committees face uncertainty and fall back to familiar markers such as LVEF, VO2max, or simple physical tests. The source criticizes that habit: LVEF underperforms for nuanced adaptation, and the paper repeatedly says GLS and MPI may add information beyond volumetric output [PAGE 3]. But the counter-trap is overcorrection: treating an internal AUC of 0.91 as a ready selection tool. The key points invite this by calling GLS and GWE “robust, non-invasive predictors” and advocating “integration of echocardiographic screening into athlete talent identification” [PAGE 15]. That is precisely the counter-trap, because the same paper says longitudinal validation and external replication are required [PAGE 1, PAGE 13].

The clock pressure is practical: coaches must select athletes, periodize training, and clear medical eligibility on calendar deadlines. The study’s 48-hour testing window and 12-month competition-index window create an appearance of timely prediction, but the design is cross-sectional. A recursive theory-of-mind audit must model three perspectives. The author knows the limitations and states them in the Discussion. The coach or selector may read only the title and key points and overgeneralize. The athlete may internalize a “high myocardial efficiency” or “low efficiency” label as fixed talent. The analyst must therefore adjudicate intent against incentive: the paper’s cautious sections are scientifically correct, while the promotional key points are not equally supported.

Counter-trap avoidance requires explicit decision rules: use echo indices as adjunctive monitoring markers, never as stand-alone selection thresholds; require prospective competition outcomes after the echo; require calibration and external validation; and preserve blinding and automated scoring. The source did blind echo assessors to group assignment and performance outcomes [PAGE 5], which reduces observer expectancy bias. But blinding does not fix tertile circularity, because the label itself contains the performance domains. The clock should be reset to a multi-season validation timeline, not a single testing week. The correct theory of mind is that uncertainty creates demand for a biomarker, and that demand can convert an association into a false talent-identification narrative unless the analyst enforces the source’s own limitation clause.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical-layer veto: resting echocardiography cannot measure combat-specific myocardial stress. The source states “Resting myocardial strain and myocardial work indices do not directly measure combat skill” [PAGE 1], and GWE and GWW are not direct whole-body metabolic efficiency measures [PAGE 6]. The imaging room was controlled at 22 to 24°C and 40 to 60% relative humidity [PAGE 5], and testing was done after overnight fast and seated stabilization. That standardization is a strength for internal validity, but it vetoes any claim that the resting indices capture acute combat exertion, fatigue, or recovery during sparring. A physical-layer proof of prediction would require stress echocardiography or repeated measures across training phases; the source explicitly calls for stress-imaging evidence [PAGE 13].

Biomechanical-layer veto: the performance tests are surrogates. Wingate peak power, T-test agility, BESS errors, FTNAT repetitions, reaction time, and technical execution are not the same as martial arts competition skill. The competition index is a constructed 10-point score combining event tier, match outcome, and technical margin [PAGE 6-7], with weights chosen by the investigators. Those weights may be sensible, but they are not a biomechanical law. The tertile label also includes anaerobic power, agility time, and competition index, so the echo-group differences are partly definitional [PAGE 7]. A biomechanical prediction claim would need a held-out competition outcome, not a composite that contains the same domains.

Weather and environment veto: internal weather is controlled, so weather cannot explain the internal group differences. But the study provides no evidence for hot, cold, humid, altitude, or outdoor competition conditions. The controlled imaging room at 22 to 24°C and 40 to 60% relative humidity [PAGE 5] means the results are condition-specific. Any claim that GLS or GWE predicts performance across weather or travel conditions is vetoed by absence of environmental stratification. The source’s limitations also note that resting echocardiography does not capture myocardial responses during combat-like exertion, fatigue, and recovery [PAGE 13]. Cross-layer adjudication therefore yields a narrow verdict: physical measurement is internally standardized but not sport-specific; biomechanical outcomes are surrogate and partly circular; weather external validity is absent. The valid claim is association and internal classification, not universal prediction.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

Scenario 1: Pre-registered external validation. Freeze the GLS plus GWE logistic model and test it on an independent national or international martial arts cohort of at least 300 athletes with a future competition outcome measured after echocardiography. Verifiable veto rule: reject the “predictive” claim if the lower bound of the 95% AUC confidence interval is below 0.70, if calibration slope is outside 0.80 to 1.20, or if the incremental AUC over the covariate-only model is below 0.05. Prediction: AUC will shrink from the internal 0.91 [PAGE 16] to approximately 0.65 to 0.78, and incremental AUC will be 0.02 to 0.06, because the internal label is tertile-circular and the covariates already carry substantial signal. If the veto rule fires, the paper’s key-point claim of “robust, non-invasive predictors” [PAGE 15] is invalidated.

Scenario 2: Circularity ablation with held-out performance. Rebuild the criterion without using the echo-predicted domains in the label; for example, predict a subsequent 12-month competition index from baseline GLS and GWE while excluding current anaerobic power and agility from the training label. Verifiable veto rule: reject incremental predictive validity if the adjusted \(R^2\) or AUC gain over age, sex, discipline, training age, weekly load, body fat, and skeletal muscle mass is below the pre-specified minimal important difference, for example \( \Delta R^2 \lt 0.03 \) or \( \Delta \mathrm{AUC} \lt 0.05 \). Prediction: the gain will be small and may be non-significant after FDR correction, because the source itself says the between-group differences “describe the magnitude and statistical consistency of group separation rather than independent causal effects” [PAGE 7]. If confirmed, GLS and GWE remain adjunctive correlates, not selection predictors.

Recompute GWE, GWW, and MPI under plausible cuff-pressure error, Doppler angle variation, and tracking-quality threshold changes, then measure ICC and tertile misclassification. Verifiable veto rule: reject threshold use if GWE or GWW ICC falls below 0.80, if MPI ICC falls below 0.75, or if more than 10% of athletes change performance tertile under perturbation. Prediction: GWE will remain moderately stable because reported ICC is 0.91 and CV is 3.9% [PAGE 5], but GWW and MPI will be more fragile because GWW depends on wasted-work integration and MPI depends on Doppler alignment [PAGE 6].
