The source is a 47-page student economics paper, not an audited market report. Its usable core is an accounting and demand-shock narrative: a transfer or trophy event raises attention and salience, which shifts jersey demand; realized sales are then capped by supply; gross jersey revenue is converted into club capture only through margin or royalty rates; and residual effects feed brand goodwill and future demand. The multi-hop derivation is therefore: event [PAGE 8, PAGE 14, PAGE 25] → attention/salience → demand shift → supply-constrained sales → gross revenue → club margin or royalty → long-run goodwill. The paper often stops at gross revenue, so its strongest quantitative claims require a gray-zone audit.

The most important audit is gross revenue versus club capture. The source states for Messi: “First 24 Hours: PSG sold over 830,000 Messi jerseys within a day of the announcement.” [PAGE 25] and “Estimated Revenue: At an average price of €120, that translates to nearly €100 million in revenue in less than 24 hours.” [PAGE 25]. The arithmetic is consistent: 830,000 × €120 = €99.6 million. But this is gross retail revenue, not club profit. The source itself says: “Gross Margins: Typically 30–40% per jersey for clubs after manufacturer and distribution costs.” [PAGE 11] and “Licensing Revenue: Clubs often receive 10–15% royalties on each jersey sold, unless they have direct merchandising rights.” [PAGE 11]. Thus the club-capture range is approximately €29.9–39.8 million under the gross-margin assumption, or €10–15 million under the royalty assumption. The source’s “nearly €100 million” is therefore an admissible gross-sales figure only if the sales volume and price are verified; it is not an admissible club-profit figure.

The same audit applies to Ronaldo. The source says: “First-year sales: Over 1.2 million jerseys bearing Ronaldo’s name and number were sold globally.” [PAGE 8] and “Revenue generated: Estimated €100 million in jersey sales in just the first season.” [PAGE 8]. The implied average price is about €83.3 per jersey, which is plausible for 2009 but is not stated as a unit price by the source. At the paper’s stated 30–40% margin, club capture would be €30–40 million; at 10–15% royalty, €10–15 million. That matters because the transfer fee is given as “€94 million” [PAGE 8]. The paper’s own data therefore do not prove that first-season jersey gross sales recouped the transfer fee; they prove only a gross-sales estimate with uncertain capture.

There are also internal gray zones. Neymar’s first 24 hours are given as “more than 10,000 jerseys online (worth approx. €1 million)” [PAGE 14], while the later comparison says “First-month jersey sales: over 1 million” [PAGE 28]. \[
D_t = N_t \pi_t(p_t,A_t,S_t,G_t) + \eta_t,\qquad [D_t]=\text{jerseys}\cdot\text{period}^{-1}.
\]

\[
\varepsilon_t=\frac{\partial Q_t}{\partial p_t}\frac{p_t}{Q_t},\qquad [\varepsilon_t]=\text{dimensionless}.
\]
The source states: “Official Jerseys: Relatively inelastic for loyal fans (they buy even at €120+).” [PAGE 5]. Assumption: \(p_t\) varies while non-price demand factors are held fixed. \]
where \(C_t^{\text{prod}}\), \(C_t^{\text{ship}}\), and \(C_t^{\text{lic}}\) are production, shipping, and licensing capacities in \(\text{jerseys}\cdot\text{period}^{-1}\), and \(I_{t-1}+\Delta I_t\) is available inventory in jerseys. \]
where \(Q_t^{\text{counter}}\) is counterfeit volume in \(\text{jerseys}\cdot\text{period}^{-1}\) and \(\sigma_t\in[0,1]\) is the probability that a counterfeit buyer would instead buy an official jersey at \(p_t\). The paper’s lineage is a staged commercialization story: early jerseys were “simple cotton shirts with minimal design” and “There was no commercialization” [PAGE 6]; the post-war era introduced “lighter synthetic fabrics” and “First instances of club branding on shirts” [PAGE 6]; Eintracht Braunschweig’s Jägermeister deal in 1973 is presented as “one of the first official shirt sponsor deals” [PAGE 7]; the modern era added manufacturer logos and “home, away, and third kits” [PAGE 7]; fashion integration appears with “PSG x Jordan (2018/19)” [PAGE 7]; digital sales and NFTs follow [PAGE 8]. The superstar genealogy runs from Ronaldo to Real Madrid in 2009 [PAGE 8–9], to Neymar to PSG in 2017 [PAGE 14–15], to Messi to PSG in 2021 [PAGE 25–26], to Lamine Yamal at Barcelona in 2023–2025 [PAGE 26–27]. The retrocausal trap is reading later categories—superstar economics, fashion collaboration, NFT collectibles—back into earlier eras as if the same demand mechanism operated. The early kit did not have a global attention market; the modern kit does.

The multi-week traps are measurement-window traps. First, the paper gives Neymar “more than 10,000 jerseys online” in the first 24 hours [PAGE 14] but “over 1 million” in the first month [PAGE 28]. A 24-hour window measures announcement response plus existing inventory; a monthly window measures replenishment and sustained demand. Annualizing the first day overstates. Second, the paper gives PSG social-media growth as “20 million in one year” for Neymar [PAGE 14] but “20 million in a single week” for Messi [PAGE 25]. Attention is not a purchase probability; the same follower can be counted repeatedly and may never buy. Third, the 2025 UCL case is explicitly “hypothetical” [PAGE 30], so it cannot be retroactively used as evidence of trophy-driven merchandise. Fourth, counterfeit “lost sales” of “over $500 million annually” [PAGE 21] are not net profit losses; the relevant counterfactual is official purchase probability. Fifth, retro appreciation examples such as “€70 now sells for €700+” [PAGE 36] and “AC Milan’s 1994 kit, priced at €80 originally, can now fetch €600” [PAGE 37] are selected survivors; failed retro kits are not reported. Sixth, outlet markdowns to “€40–€60” [PAGE 33] are inventory-clearing evidence, not demand evidence. The historical lesson is that the same word—“sales”—can mean orders, deliveries, gross retail revenue, club capture, or resale value. Each has a different genealogy and a different multi-week clock.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The crisis-habit regression mode is conditional. The source says: “Loyal fans tend to buy jerseys regardless of price changes.” [PAGE 35] That supports inelastic core demand during a club crisis. But the source also says: “If a star player leaves mid-season, fans avoid jerseys with their name.” [PAGE 33] So the habit regresses to club identity if the fan is club-loyal, but it regresses away from player-specific merchandise if the fan is player-loyal. The recursive belief is: fans know that other fans may abandon a player’s kit, so they anticipate resale-value loss; clubs know fans anticipate scarcity, so they create “Limited-edition jerseys” and “Scarcity & Exclusivity” campaigns [PAGE 24]. The counter-trap is the counterfeit/outlet loop. The source says counterfeit availability “increases elasticity” [PAGE 33], and clubs respond with cheaper “stadium versions” in developing markets [PAGE 32] and outlet pricing that “also combats counterfeit sales by offering legitimate, cheaper options” [PAGE 34]. But the recursive trap is that outlet and stadium-version strategies teach price-sensitive fans to wait, which can cannibalize launch-price sales. The clock mode is the event window. The source’s key measurements are “first 24 hours” [PAGE 14], “first month” [PAGE 28], “first season” [PAGE 8], and “annual” [PAGE 26]. The clock determines which theory of mind dominates: in the first 24 hours, scarcity and loss aversion dominate; in the first month, replenishment and social proof dominate; after the season, outlet and counterfeit substitution dominate. The prediction is that short-clock, club-loyal demand is relatively price-insensitive, while long-clock, player-loyal demand is more elastic and more exposed to counterfeit and outlet leakage.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The physical layer veto is explicit in the source’s own supply list: “Manufacturer capacity (Nike, Adidas, Puma),” “Material availability and logistics,” and “Licensing agreements” [PAGE 4]. The admissible physical constraint is
\[
Q_t^{\text{delivered}}\le I_0+\int_{t_0}^{t_0+\Delta t}\min\{C^{\text{prod}}(s),C^{\text{ship}}(s)\}\,ds,
\]
in jerseys, where \(I_0\) is pre-announcement inventory. The Messi claim of “over 830,000 Messi jerseys within a day” [PAGE 25] therefore requires either pre-positioned inventory or a 24-hour production-and-shipping capacity of at least 830,000 jerseys. The source gives no inventory or lead-time data, so the claim is conditional, not physically proven. If \(I_0+R_{24}\) is below 830,000, the physical layer vetoes the claim as delivered sales; the number must then be orders, backorders, or multi-day shipments. The source’s production geography—China, Vietnam, Bangladesh [PAGE 2]—makes lead time material, but the paper provides no transit time.

The biomechanical layer is only qualitative in the source. Early kits were “heavy, uncomfortable, and not designed for performance” [PAGE 6], and later “lighter synthetic fabrics improved player comfort” [PAGE 6]. Counterfeits are said to have “potential safety issues (e.g., toxic dyes)” [PAGE 32]. But there is no quantitative biomechanical metric—no fabric mass, breathability, fit tolerance, or measured performance effect. Therefore any claim that a specific jersey design improves player output is inadmissible from this source; it would be vetoed by a controlled biomechanical test showing no difference. The weather layer is absent: the source contains no weather data, no rainfall, heat, or storm variable. Weather can physically veto shipping and retail, but no weather conclusion can be derived from these pages. The cross-layer adjudication is therefore: economic demand shocks survive only if the supply min constraint and the biomechanical/weather evidence layers do not bind; where they bind, the economic claim must be restated as a conditional gross-demand claim, not a realized-sales or profit claim.
