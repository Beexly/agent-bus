**2. Intertemporal Exchange and Dependable Obligations**
The text contrasts a *"present or early allotment of goods"* with a *"dependable obligation for the future delivery."* 
Let $G_0$ be goods consumed today and $G_T$ be the obligation at time $T$. A risk-averse agent values the obligation only if its certainty is maximized. Let $p \in [0,1]$ be the probability of fulfillment without insurance, and $p^* \approx 1$ with insurance [PAGE 149, footnote 2]. The expected utility of accepting the obligation is:
$$ E[U] = p^* \cdot U(G_T) - K_{insurance} $$
Exchange occurs when the borrower's marginal rate of substitution (MRS) between present and future goods exceeds the lender's MRS.
$$ MRS_{borrower} = \frac{\partial U / \partial G_0}{e^{-rT} (\partial U / \partial G_T)} > 1 $$
*Assumptions:* Perfect divisibility of goods, and $K_{insurance}$ does not exceed the time-value spread.

While this specific page discusses certainty ("dependable obligation"), it inherently sets up the contrast with Knight's famous distinction between calculable risk and un-insurable uncertainty, which acts as the primary barrier to capital accumulation in the modern era. Historically, the "trap" over subsequent decades has been the financialization of these "obligations." By converting illiquid, depreciable consumption goods into tradable, insured futures contracts, modern finance artificially suppresses the natural premium on present goods, creating systemic fragility where the illusion of future security masks an underlying depletion of actual material wealth.
