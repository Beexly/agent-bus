In the e-sports domain, Sainz, Collado-Mateo, and Coso (2020) previously tested acute caffeine on hit accuracy and reaction time in professional players using an aim trainer [PAGE 1, ref 12], the direct methodological ancestor of the 60-target, 2-minute static-map protocol used here [PAGE 3]. Redondo et al. (2021) linked caffeine to dynamic visual acuity [PAGE 6, ref 32], offering a mechanistic bridge to shooting moving targets. Genetic moderation enters through Nehlig (2018) on CYP1A2-driven interindividual metabolism [PAGE 6, ref 34] and Southward et al. (2018) on non-responders [PAGE 5, ref 35].

Multi-week traps embedded in the protocol. The study ran from 01/01/2022 to 30/04/2022 [PAGE 2]. Participants completed three to five familiarization tests at least 1 week before the formal experiment [PAGE 2], then underwent a 7-day washout between CAF and PL sessions [PAGE 2], while maintaining controlled diets for 3 days before each visit and abstaining from >80 mg/day caffeine for 2 weeks prior [PAGE 2]. These longitudinal controls create three distinct multi-week traps. First, the 7-day washout exceeds caffeine’s pharmacokinetic half-life by an order of magnitude, so molecular carryover is unlikely; however, behavioral learning on the aim trainer may persist across the inter-session interval, producing a period effect that a simple paired t-test cannot fully remove. Second, habitual caffeine users (even at low baseline intakes of 0–78 mg/day [PAGE 2]) may exhibit adenosine-receptor adaptation, meaning withdrawal and re-exposure dynamics across weeks could masquerade as treatment effects. Third, the fixed January–April window removes seasonal climatic variance because testing occurs indoors at 26 °C [PAGE 2], but it also limits the sample to a single climatic and academic cycle, constraining temporal external validity.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression versus counter-trap versus clock. Model the elite player as an adaptive system operating under time pressure. Crisis habit regression: under high-stress auditory and visual load, skilled operators regress to automated schemas; caffeine’s significant effect on the congruent Stroop condition (P = 0.023) but not the incongruent condition (P = 0.478) [PAGE 3] implies that the drug accelerates stimulus–response binding when the correct response is prepotent, but does not restore executive control when conflict must be inhibited. In crisis moments—ambiguous target identification, decoy-distractors, rapid target switching—the incongruent-like condition dominates, so the caffeinated player may react faster to obvious targets without gaining the inhibitory precision needed for complex decisions. Moreover, adverse somatic effects (palpitations, anxiety, tremor) at higher or sensitive doses [PAGE 4] can themselves regress fine motor control under crisis.

Counter-trap: an opposing team aware of an opponent’s caffeine protocol can exploit the load-specificity of the effect. Caffeine significantly shortened visual-search reaction time only at 20 items (P = 0.004/0.020), not at 5, 10, or 15 items [PAGE 3]. This implies no reliable advantage in sparse, early-game engagements; the edge appears only in cluttered, high-entropy scenarios. An adversary can therefore dictate tempo—keeping engagements visually simple—to nullify the pharmacological benefit, then force high-load team fights where the caffeinated player’s faster but potentially stereotyped reactions become predictable and punishable. The large effect sizes (D = 0.96–1.96 [PAGE 3]) describe average shifts in a controlled aim trainer, not a robust, context-invariant dominance.

Clock: the protocol fixes capsule ingestion at t = −3600 s relative to testing, with a 1-hour rest [PAGE 2]. This targets the expected plasma peak for acute psychostimulation [PAGE 1]. However, match duration in competitive first-person shooters can exceed caffeine’s effective psychomotor window; as plasma concentration decays according to first-order elimination (k_e term [PAGE 4]), the attentional gain attenuates over time. Overtime or extended map rotations therefore push performance into a post-peak regime where the CAF–PL difference contracts toward zero. Additionally, because the trial is single-blind rather than double-blind [PAGE 2], participants may detect somatic caffeine cues and unblind themselves, creating an expectancy component that decays on a different timescale than the pharmacodynamic clock, producing a mismatch between perceived and actual enhancement across the match clock.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical, biomechanical, and weather veto proof.

Weather layer: No weather veto applies. All testing occurred in a professional e-sports classroom with indoor ambient temperature fixed at 26 °C [PAGE 2], removing solar load, precipitation, wind, and thermal-gradient confounds. The only physical environmental variable controlled is room temperature; HVAC failure or deviations beyond the tested 26 °C would constitute an unmodeled physical veto but were not observed in the protocol.

Physical/biological layer: Cardiovascular disease and any illness were exclusion criteria [PAGE 2], and dose was scaled to body mass (3 mg/kg) [PAGE 2], reducing inter-subject hemodynamic variance. The paper notes caffeine doses above 400 mg are detrimental [PAGE 4], 500 mg provokes tremor, anxiety, and palpitations [PAGE 4], and 5 g (80–100 mg/kg) is likely fatal [PAGE 4]. The administered ~218.4 mg mean dose sits well below these breakdown regimes [PAGE 2], but CYP1A2 variability means slow metabolizers could experience effective overdoses relative to fast metabolizers at the same nominal dose [PAGE 5]. A physical veto triggers if a subject’s measured heart rate or blood pressure crosses the adverse threshold documented by Ebrahimi et al. for 5 mg/kg [PAGE 4, ref 21]; no such vital-sign data were recorded in this study, so the veto cannot be verified post hoc.

Biomechanical layer: Hardware and task parameters are tightly specified. Computers maintained ≥240 Hz frame rate; mouse scrolling speed was 1 ms [PAGE 2]. The shooting test used a static medium-difficulty map, 60 targets, 3 targets per round, 2-second engagement window, one-shot kill ideal [PAGE 3]. These constraints bound motor noise and frame-latency jitter. A biomechanical veto triggers if frame rate falls below 240 Hz or mouse latency exceeds 1 ms, because reaction-time differences on the order of tens of milliseconds (Stroop congruent effect [PAGE 3]) would be swamped by input/display lag. The crossover design controls for constant per-subject hardware, but the authors note participants used equipment they were “most accustomed to” [PAGE 2], introducing participant-specific biomechanics that are differenced out only if stable across the 7-day washout.

Verdict: The indoor setting removes weather vetoes; the hardware specs remove most biomechanical vetoes; and the sub-adverse dose removes the most severe physical vetoes. The remaining unadjudicated vetoes are genetic (CYP1A2 non-response [PAGE 5]) and behavioral (learning/expectancy across the crossover [PAGE 2]), neither of which was measured or controlled sufficiently to claim a universal ergogenic effect.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards) veto scenarios with falsifiable conditions and predictions.

Veto Scenario 1 — Genotype-stratified non-response. Verifiable condition: Genotype all participants for CYP1A2 and collect saliva/plasma caffeine concentration at t = 3600 s; define responders as those showing a statistically significant within-subject CAF > PL improvement on the primary shooting composite. Prediction: If the Southward et al. estimate holds [PAGE 5], up to 33% of subjects are non-responders. With n = 9, expect at least 2–3 non-responders; a mixed-effects model including CYP1A2 × treatment interaction should show a significant interaction (p < 0.05) and the main-effect caffeine benefit should vanish or reverse in the slow-metabolizer/non-responder subgroup. Veto trigger: significant genotype × treatment interaction with null simple effect in non-responders falsifies the blanket claim that “caffeine supplementation significantly improves e-sports players’ performance” [PAGE 1].

Veto Scenario 2 — Family-wise error correction. Verifiable condition: Pre-register a confirmatory analysis plan applying Holm–Bonferroni correction to the eight reported hypothesis tests (Stroop congruent, Stroop incongruent, visual search × 4 loads, kill ratio, accuracy, time to target) [PAGE 3]. Prediction: At α = 0.05, only average time to target (P = 0.001) is likely to survive correction; kill ratio (P = 0.020), visual search 20 items (P = 0.004 in Table 1 but P = 0.020 in narrative [PAGE 1; PAGE 3]), and congruent Stroop (P = 0.023) likely fail the adjusted threshold. Veto trigger: if fewer than two endpoints remain significant after correction, the omnibus claim of broad cognitive and shooting improvement is not supported at the family-wise level.

Veto Scenario 3 — Crossover order/carryover test. Verifiable condition: Fit a linear mixed model to shooting metrics with fixed effects for treatment, session order (CAF-first vs PL-first), period, and their interaction, and a random intercept for participant [PAGE 2]. Prediction: If aim-trainer learning or expectancy carries across the 7-day washout, the period/order term will be significant and the treatment effect will attenuate to null when order is included; specifically, the CAF-first group should show inflated PL-session performance relative to the PL-first group’s CAF session. Veto trigger: significant period × treatment interaction or elimination of the treatment main effect after adjusting for session order falsifies the assumption of clean crossover isolation and invalidates the paired t-test inference [PAGE 3].
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
