Recursive theory of mind concerns what one agent infers about another’s model of the self under pressure. Hull’s page supplies the mechanism: crisis raises drive \(D\), elevating mean \(sER\) for the dominant habit, but stress may simultaneously enlarge \(sOk\) (oscillatory interference), widening the downward swing of \(sER\).

Crisis habit regression: under acute threat, an agent regresses to the response with the highest \(sER\) relative to \(sLR\). If \(sER_{\max} > sLR\) but \(sER_{\min} < sLR\), the crisis produces intermittent emission rather than clean regression; the observer sees “regression” only if the threshold is crossed frequently enough.

Counter-trap: an observer who expects crisis-induced regression can be wrong in two directions. If the target is already in Hull’s terminal regime—“zone of maximum oscillatory interference … passed above the reaction threshold” [PAGE 342]—then \(p = 1\) and the habit will not regress even under stress; the observer’s prediction of collapse fails. Conversely, if crisis inflates \(sOk\) enough that the minimum \(sER\) falls back below \(sLR\), the observer who assumed stability will be surprised by intermittent omission.

Clock: reaction latency \(stR\) [PAGE 342] imposes a hard deadline. As \(sER \to sLR^{+}\), \(stR\) lengthens toward its ceiling; under time pressure, only responses with \(sER\) comfortably above threshold can be emitted before the clock expires. Thus the recursive loop is: Agent A models B’s \(sER\) distribution; B’s crisis changes \(D\) and \(sOk\); A must predict whether B’s response clears \(sLR\) within the available \(stR\) budget; if A misestimates the oscillation width \(sOk\), A mispredicts both regression and timing. The clock converts the probabilistic \(p\) into a binary make/miss outcome at the moment of action.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source text isolates central reaction-evocation variables \(p, stR, n, A\) [PAGE 342], but behavior requires passage through physical, biomechanical, and environmental layers. A veto proof composes these multiplicatively:

\[
p_{\text{obs}} = p_{\text{central}} \cdot V_{\text{phys}} \cdot V_{\text{bio}} \cdot V_{\text{weather}},
\qquad V_i \in \{0,1\}.
\]

Physical layer: intact afferent detection of the “stimulation immediately preceding reaction evocation” [PAGE 342] and intact efferent neural conduction. \(V_{\text{phys}} = 0\) under sensory blockade, anesthesia, or nerve conduction failure; then \(p_{\text{obs}} = 0\) even if \(p_{\text{central}} = 1\).

Biomechanical layer: \(stR\) includes electromechanical delay and movement time. \(V_{\text{bio}} = 0\) if fatigue, injury, or load prevents the effector from reaching the required amplitude or completing the movement within the latency window. Autonomic amplitude \(A\) [PAGE 342] additionally requires intact smooth muscle or glandular response; pharmacological or structural failure vetoes \(A\) without necessarily changing \(p\).

Weather/environmental layer: ambient temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure shift baseline arousal and stimulus salience, thereby moving \(\mu\) and widening \(\sigma\) (equivalently altering \(sOk\)) of the \(sER\) distribution. Extreme cold can increase \(stR\) by slowing neuromuscular transmission; extreme heat can raise drive and enlarge oscillation, pushing the minimum \(sER\) below \(sLR\) and converting \(p = 1\) into intermittent failure.

Veto adjudication rule: whenever any \(V_i = 0\), observed performance is zero regardless of central \(sER\). The [PAGE 342] terminal period of “uniformly perfect response probability (100 per cent)” [PAGE 342] is therefore only central perfection; a silent peripheral veto (e.g., weather-induced muscle stiffness or autonomic blockade) produces observed \(p_{\text{obs}} = 0\). Breakdown regime: inferring habit strength from \(p_{\text{obs}} = 1\) is valid only if all \(V_i\) are independently verified to be 1; otherwise the 100-per-cent central claim is unfalsifiable from behavior alone.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Three verifiable counterfactual veto scenarios, each with a falsifiable prediction.

Scenario 1 (autonomic veto): Administer a beta-adrenergic blocker before an autonomically mediated reaction. Central \(sER\), \(p\), and \(stR\) for the somatic component are left intact, but autonomic amplitude \(A\) is vetoed. Prediction: \(A \to 0\) (or near-baseline) while the probability of the accompanying skeletal reaction and its latency remain unchanged; heart-rate or galvanic response is absent on reinforced trials. Verifiable by simultaneous EMG/lever-press and physiological recording.

Scenario 2 (biomechanical latency veto): Cool the effector limb to reduce nerve conduction velocity and muscle contraction speed. Prediction: \(stR\) increases by a quantifiable number of milliseconds per degree of cooling while \(p\) remains at 1 until a biomechanical threshold is crossed; at that temperature the movement can no longer be completed within the required window and \(p_{\text{obs}}\) drops discontinuously to 0 even though central \(sER\) still exceeds \(sLR\). Verifiable by high-speed motion capture and thermode control.

Scenario 3 (extinction resistance veto): After the terminal perfect regime on [PAGE 342]—where \(p = 1\) because the whole oscillatory zone lies above \(sLR\)—withhold all reinforcement. Prediction: \(p\) remains at 1 for exactly \(n\) nonreinforced trials (Hull’s resistance to extinction), then declines as repeated nonreinforcement erodes \(sER\) until the minimum oscillatory value falls below \(sLR\). The length of the perfect-response plateau is predictable from pre-extinction \(sER\), \(sOk\), and the decay rate per nonreinforced trial; it is not infinite. Verifiable by cumulative response counting during extinction: a flat plateau at 100 per cent followed by a drop, with plateau length equal to the independently estimated \(n\).
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
