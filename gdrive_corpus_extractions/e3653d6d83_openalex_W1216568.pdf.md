<THINK_TRACE>
The paper’s central multi-hop claim is a mediation claim, not a simple trait-accuracy correlation. The first hop is AOT → information acquisition: “Only AOT predicted performance” among AOT, need for cognition, grit, and maximizing, and “This relationship was mediated by information acquisition: AOT predicted the tendency to collect information, and information acquisition predicted performance” [PAGE 1]. The second hop is search → accuracy, but only under a validity condition: when acquired information is positively predictive, more search improves choices; when the outcome contradicts the acquired information, more search can produce coherent but wrong predictions. Study 1 shows the first two hops in a perceptual-estimation task: participants viewed objects an average of 5.70 times, achieved a 71.86% success rate, and search correlated with choice accuracy at \(r=.415\) and with confidence-interval hit rate at \(r=.311\) [PAGE 4]. Study 1 also reports full mediation for modal-choice accuracy and partial mediation for confidence-interval hit rate [PAGE 4]. Study 2 is the gray-zone audit of the mediator: when the experimenter fixed information at 2, 5, or 8 views, AOT no longer predicted performance; the source states, “Did actively open-minded thinking predict estimate accuracy when participants could not control the amount of information? The answer is no” [PAGE 7], and “Without the opportunity to conduct a more thorough search for information, neither AOT nor any other variable was related to performance” [PAGE 7]. This is the key singularity: if the search mediator is constant or capped, the mediated path \(AOT \to search \to accuracy\) is structurally unidentified. Study 3 adds the second singularity: information validity. In non-upset games, pre-game search correlated with accuracy at \(r=.311\); in upset games it correlated negatively at \(r=-.504\) [PAGE 8]. The paper concludes that “with invalid information, this strategy backfired” [PAGE 10], and “When information was misleading, high AOT individuals were more susceptible to invalid information” [PAGE 11]. Thus the correct model is conditional: AOT raises search persistence; search raises accuracy only when the searchable information set has positive predictive validity. The gray-zone audit must also flag design limits. The studies are online, cross-sectional, self-report AOT, and do not manipulate AOT directly. Study 3 uses one NFL week from an undisclosed past season, though the source says no participant guessed the season [PAGE 8]. Expertise is self-rated and only significant in some analyses; in non-upset games AOT was “the only variable that predicted information acquisition and performance” [PAGE 8], while in upset games expertise predicted correct estimates [PAGE 9]. The paper does not report a formal sampling-variance derivation; it cites diminishing utility of search and stopping-rule work [PAGE 2]. Therefore, any stronger claim—e.g., that AOT is a general accuracy trait—is vetoed by Study 2’s fixed-information null and Study 3’s validity reversal. The defensible claim is narrower: AOT predicts persistence in search, and persistence predicts accuracy only when search is controllable and the information environment is at least somewhat predictive. The paper itself states that “as long as the information is at least somewhat predictive, coherence should have a positive relationship with accuracy, and AOT should be helpful” [PAGE 11]. That is the boundary condition to formalize.

<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
**AOT operator.** From the Appendix, respondents rate seven statements on a 1–7 scale, with “(LAST 4 should be reverse coded)” [PAGE 14]. A formal reconstruction is
\[
A=\frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=1}^{7}\tilde x_i,\qquad
\tilde x_i=x_i\ \text{for }i=1,2,3,\qquad
\tilde x_i=8-x_i\ \text{for }i=4,5,6,7,
\]
where \(x_i\in\{1,\dots,7\}\) is dimensionless. Assumption: equal item weighting; the source states reverse coding but does not specify a different aggregation rule. Units: dimensionless scale score.

**Information acquisition.** In Study 1 the source says the number of views was skewed, “therefore we used a log(10) transformation of information acquisition” [PAGE 4]. Define
\[
S_1=\log_{10}(N_v),\qquad N_v\ge 1,
\]
where \(N_v\) is the count of object-viewing choices. Units: dimensionless log-count. In Study 3, \(S_3=N_f\), the number of pre-game facts requested; the mean was 2.11 facts per prediction [PAGE 8]. Units: count of facts.

**Accuracy and calibration operators.** For Study 1’s four categorical tasks, define
\[
Y_{\text{choice}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\mathbf{1}[\hat c_k=c_k],
\]
where \(\hat c_k\) is the chosen most-frequent type and \(c_k\) is the true type. Units: proportion. For 80% confidence intervals, define
\[
H_{\text{CI}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\mathbf{1}[L_k\le T_k\le U_k],
\]
where \(T_k\) is the true total number of objects. Units: proportion. The source reports \(Y_{\text{choice}}=.7186\) and \(H_{\text{CI}}=.5205\) in Study 1 [PAGE 4]. Overconfidence in categorical confidence is
\[
O=\bar C-Y_{\text{choice}},
\]
where \(\bar C\) is mean stated confidence in proportion units; the source reports 69.04% confidence versus 71.86% accuracy, not significantly different [PAGE 4]. For interval overprecision,
\[
O_{\text{CI}}=.80-H_{\text{CI}},
\]
positive when hit rate is below the nominal 80%. Study 1’s \(H_{\text{CI}}=.5205\) implies \(O_{\text{CI}}=.2795\), i.e., 27.95 percentage points of overprecision [PAGE 4]. Study 2 reports 39.50% interval hit rate against 80%, implying \(O_{\text{CI}}=.4050\) [PAGE 6]. Study 3 reports mean confidence 70.59% and 5.65/10 correct predictions, implying “overconfidence of 14.09%” [PAGE 8].

**Mediation equations.** The source uses Baron and Kenny mediation and reports standardized coefficients in Figures 2–6 [PAGE 4], [PAGE 9]. The formal system is
\[
Y=cA+\varepsilon_Y,
\]
\[
S=aA+\varepsilon_S,
\]
\[
Y=c'A+bS+\varepsilon_Y'.
\]
The mediated effect is \(ab\), and the direct effect is \(c'\). Units: if \(Y\) and \(S\) are proportions/counts, coefficients are outcome units per one-unit AOT change; standardized versions are dimensionless. Assumptions: linearity, correct temporal order \(A\to S\to Y\), and no unmeasured confounding of \(S\to Y\). Breakdown regimes: (i) if search is experimentally fixed, \(S=\text{constant}\), then \(a=0\) and \(ab=0\), which is the Study 2 null [PAGE 7]; (ii) if \(S\) is measured with error, \(ab\) attenuates; (iii) if information validity is negative, the \(b\) path can change sign for accuracy even while remaining positive for coherence.

**Regression estimates.** Study 1’s Table 2 reports AOT predicting information acquisition at \(B=0.087\), \(p<.01\), correct choices at \(B=0.198\), \(p<.05\), and accurate confidence intervals at \(B=0.451\), \(p<.001\) [PAGE 6]. Study 3 non-upset games report AOT predicting information acquisition at \(B=0.092\), \(p<.01\), and correct estimates at \(B=0.034\), \(p<.01\) [PAGE 9]. For upset games, AOT still predicted search at \(B=0.091\), \(p<.01\), but the AOT-performance relation was negative and almost significant, \(B=-.127\), \(t(199)=-1.80\), \(p=.07\) [PAGE 8]. The paper also reports that “AOT was the only significant predictor of consistency, \(B=.217\), \(R^2=.047\), \(t(199)=3.13\), \(p=.002\)” [PAGE 9], and that this relation “was fully-mediated by the number of pre-game facts acquired” [PAGE 9].

**Coherence-validity identity.** Let \(y^{\text{info}}\in\{0,1\}\) be the outcome implied by the acquired information, \(Y\in\{0,1\}\) the actual outcome, and \(K=P(\hat y=y^{\text{info}})\) the probability that the prediction follows the information. Let \(V=P(Y=y^{\text{info}})\) be information validity in probability units. If a prediction that does not follow information predicts the opposite outcome, then
\[
P(\text{correct})=K V+(1-K)(1-V)=1-V+K(2V-1).
\]
This is a probability identity, not a fabricated ratio. Units: probability. The derivative is
\[
\frac{\partial P(\text{correct})}{\partial K}=2V-1.
\]
Breakdown: at \(V=1/2\), accuracy is independent of coherence; for \(V<1/2\), higher coherence lowers expected accuracy; for \(V>1/2\), higher coherence raises expected accuracy. This exactly matches the source’s upset pattern: search correlated positively with accuracy in non-upsets (\(r=.311\)) and negatively in upsets (\(r=-.504\)) [PAGE 8], and “with invalid information, this strategy backfired” [PAGE 10].

**Stopping/utility operator.** The source states that search utility “increases at a diminishing rate” and is “costly in time and effort” [PAGE 2]. Define
\[
n^*=\arg\max_{n\in\mathbb N}\{U(n)-c n\},
\]
where \(U(n)\) is the expected utility of an estimate after \(n\) information items and \(c>0\) is per-item cost in utility units. Assumption: \(U'(n)>0\), \(U''(n)<0\) in the region of interest. Breakdown: if \(c\) rises sharply or \(U(n)\) is flat, \(n^*\) falls; if validity \(V<1/2\), \(U(n)\) can be non-monotone or decreasing, so more search is not defensible. The source adds that “people rarely ‘overthink’ before making an estimate or forecast” [PAGE 2], but that does not override the validity and fixed-information vetoes.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The lineage runs from calibration and overconfidence research through search/stopping theory to AOT. The paper cites overconfidence as “robust and difficult to remedy” [PAGE 1] and positions AOT within Baron’s reasoning-style tradition: AOT includes “the tendency to weigh new evidence against a favored belief, to spend sufficient time on a problem before giving up, and to consider carefully the opinions of others” [PAGE 2]. Earlier work tried to reduce miscalibration by perspective-taking, scenario projection, and self-contradiction, often by reducing confidence rather than improving accuracy [PAGE 2]. The paper’s retrocausal contribution is to relocate the mechanism from confidence adjustment to search persistence: AOT predicts more information acquisition, and search predicts accuracy when information is useful. This also connects to SPIES and interval-estimation remedies: the source suggests SPIES “improved calibration of confidence intervals by preventing judges from ignoring alternative outcomes” and that such methods may make participants behave “as high AOT individuals are naturally inclined to” [PAGE 11]. The multi-week trap is training. The paper asks “Can AOT be taught?” and notes Baron’s advocacy and prior 8-month decision-making courses [PAGE 11]. A multi-week AOT intervention could raise search persistence, but if the training environment has low-validity information or delayed feedback, it may teach coherence rather than accuracy. The source warns that high AOT individuals “collected more information and used it” but “with invalid information, this strategy backfired” [PAGE 10]. Thus a training program that rewards coherent explanations in noisy domains could entrench the wrong habit. The paper itself is cross-sectional, so it cannot establish that AOT training causes long-run calibration; it only says “If these interventions can cause changes in forecasting skills, they should be used to train forecasters” [PAGE 11]. Genealogically, the safe conclusion is conditional: AOT is a search-persistence disposition with roots in belief-bias and stopping-rule research, not a domain-general accuracy guarantee.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The source does not measure recursive theory of mind, mentalizing, or strategic modeling of other forecasters. The only defensible recursive reading is metacognitive: an agent must model its own search policy and the validity of the information environment. In that sense, “crisis habit regression” is low-AOT regression to early stopping: under time pressure or stress, the cost \(c\) in the stopping equation rises and \(n^*\) falls, so the agent samples too little. The paper’s Study 2 is a clean clock/cap analogue: when the experimenter fixed information at 2, 5, or 8 views, AOT had no performance effect [PAGE 7]. That means a crisis clock that removes search control also removes AOT’s mediated advantage. The counter-trap is the upset regime: high AOT agents search more, produce more coherent predictions, and then become more wrong when the information is invalid. The source states that “higher AOT individuals performed better when games were not upsets and worse in games that resulted in upsets” [PAGE 11]. Recursively, an agent that models this trap should not merely ask “Have I searched enough?” but “Is the information I am searching predictive in this regime?” The paper’s data support the first question, not the second. The source does contain a social component in the AOT items—e.g., “People should take into consideration evidence that goes against their beliefs” and “consider carefully the opinions of others” [PAGE 14]—but it does not test whether AOT improves modeling of others’ beliefs. Therefore, any claim that AOT enhances recursive ToM or strategic deception detection is unsupported. The defensible recursive claim is narrower: AOT may increase self-monitoring of search sufficiency, and that self-monitoring is beneficial only when the clock allows search and the information environment has positive validity. In crisis, the habit regression is early stopping; the counter-trap is over-search in an invalid environment; the clock is the cost term that determines which failure dominates.

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The source has no direct biomechanical, physical, or weather measurements. Study 3 includes football outcomes and lists information facts such as records, recent performance, rankings, last meeting, and “injuries to significant players, if there were any” [PAGE 8], but it does not report weather, field surface, travel, or biomechanical load. Therefore, a cross-layer veto cannot be proved from the source alone; it can only be formalized as an external validity constraint. Let \(X\) be the searchable information set and \(Z\) a physical/biomechanical/weather variable that affects outcome \(Y\) but is not in \(X\). If \(Z\) is unobserved and random with respect to \(X\), it lowers information validity \(V=P(Y=y^{\text{info}}(X))\) but does not necessarily bias the AOT coefficient. If \(Z\) is correlated with \(X\) and with \(Y\), it can confound the search-accuracy relation. The veto rule follows from the coherence-validity identity: if the available \(X\) has \(V\le 1/2\), then more coherence cannot improve expected accuracy and may reduce it. A weather or injury shock that makes the searchable facts anti-predictive is therefore a validity veto, not merely a nuisance. The source’s own upset result is the proof-of-concept: four of ten games were upsets, and search correlated negatively with accuracy in those games [PAGE 8]. A biomechanical veto would require showing that injury or fatigue changes the outcome distribution beyond the searchable facts; if those facts do not encode the change, \(V\) falls. A weather veto would require the same: if weather is not in the fact set and changes outcomes, the fact set’s validity falls. If weather is observable and searchable, AOT could acquire it; if it is not, no amount of AOT search can recover it. The adjudication is therefore: physical/weather variables can veto an AOT-accuracy claim only when they reduce the validity of the actually searchable information set below the break-even point \(V=1/2\), or when they confound the AOT-search relation. The source does not provide the data to estimate that threshold for football, so the correct cross-layer verdict is “not established,” not “proved absent.”

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
**Veto 1: Fixed-search AOT manipulation.** Counterfactual: randomly assign participants to high- versus low-AOT training or priming, hold the number of searchable items fixed at \(N_v=5\), and measure accuracy. The paper’s mediation account predicts no AOT main effect on accuracy when search is fixed, because Study 2 found “none of the cognitive styles or other individual attributes we measured predicted performance” under fixed information [PAGE 7]. Verifiable prediction: the standardized AOT coefficient on accuracy should fall within a pre-registered equivalence bound around zero; if a significant positive AOT effect persists with search fixed and no processing mediator is found, the search-mediation account is vetoed.

**Veto 2: Validity reversal.** Counterfactual: construct two forecast sets, one where searchable facts have \(V>1/2\) and one where the same facts are anti-predictive with \(V<1/2\). The paper predicts AOT should increase coherence in both, but increase accuracy only in the high-validity set; in the low-validity set, higher coherence should predict lower accuracy. This is the formal prediction \(\partial P(\text{correct})/\partial K=2V-1\). Verifiable prediction: the AOT-by-validity interaction on accuracy should be positive, and within the \(V<1/2\) set the AOT-accuracy slope should be negative or null. If AOT improves accuracy even when the searchable facts are anti-predictive, the coherence-validity model is vetoed.

**Veto 3: Multi-week AOT training with delayed feedback.** Counterfactual: run a multi-week AOT training program with verifiable search and calibration scoring, then test on both high-validity and low-validity forecasting tasks. The paper’s genealogy suggests training could increase search persistence [PAGE 11], but the upset result predicts a trap: training may increase coherence and worsen accuracy in low-validity environments. Verifiable prediction: trained participants should request more information and show higher coherence than controls; accuracy should improve only in the high-validity task. If training improves accuracy in the low-validity task without changing search or validity, the paper’s conditional mediation account is vetoed.
