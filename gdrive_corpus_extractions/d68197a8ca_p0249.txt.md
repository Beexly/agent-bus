Crisis habit regression follows directly from the multiplicative structure: under high drive \(D\), the term \(\Psi(D)\) amplifies whichever habit \(sHR\) is strongest, so behavior regresses to the most overlearned response rather than the most adaptive one ([PAGE 249], product rule). The counter-trap is the inverse error: inferring drive strength \(D\) from observed behavior while ignoring \(sHR\). A second counter-trap is assuming the linear \(S_p(C) = \kappa C\) across all concentrations; extrapolation into the saturation regime (\(C \gg K_d\)) predicts linear growth where the system actually plateaus, causing systematic overestimation of drive at high need levels.

The clock is the temporal operator linking accumulation and discharge. Mismatching these clocks—for example, attributing a delayed response to immediate drive concentration—misplaces the causal stimulus. The regression-versus-counter-trap tension is therefore timed: crisis regression dominates on the fast conditioning clock when \(D\) is phasically high, while counter-trap misidentification accumulates on the slow allostatic clock when \(sHR\) and \(D\) have drifted apart over weeks.

Veto: the linear assumption \(S_p = \kappa C\) ([PAGE 249]) is invalidated by receptor saturation; \(S_p\) approaches \(S_{\max}\) and becomes insensitive to further increases in \(C\). Prediction: doubling \(C\) in the saturated regime will not double drive-stimulus intensity or \(sER\); the dose–response curve plateaus. Verifiable by intravenous or intraperitoneal infusion of the putative motivating substance at escalating doses while measuring receptor afferent discharge or behavioral activation; a linear model predicts continued growth, the saturation model predicts an asymptote.

Scenario 3 — Injury-reversal veto. Counterfactual: tissue injury occurs in the absence of the characteristic blood-borne need substance (no prior deprivation, no circulating chemical signal). Veto: under the injury reversal ([PAGE 249]), the injury energy itself is \(S_p\), and adrenal secretion is downstream; therefore the chemical-first model’s prerequisite (substance in blood) is not necessary for drive. Prediction: injured subjects exhibit drive-stimulus afferent discharge and motivated behavior proportional to injury energy even when blood need-substance concentration is at baseline, whereas chemical-substance injection without receptor activation or without injury produces no \(S_p\). Verifiable by comparing adrenalectomized versus sham subjects under controlled injury: if \(S_p\) is the injury energy itself, adrenal removal should not abolish the immediate drive stimulus, though downstream hormonal effects may differ; this falsifies a strictly chemical prerequisite for all primary drives.
