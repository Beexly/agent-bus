Hull’s argument on [PAGE 118] descends from the neobehaviorist interventionism of the 1930s–1940s. The temporal-causal problem—reinforcement is past, behavior is present—echoes Pavlov’s temporal contiguity constraints in classical conditioning and Thorndike’s law of effect, but Hull internalizes the consequence by positing a neural trace rather than treating reinforcement as a direct stimulus. This places him in the lineage of logical positivism (Carnap, Hempel) applied to psychology: unobservable entities are admitted only if they bridge observable antecedents (stimulus) and observables (response) while preserving causal locality. Tolman’s cognitive maps and Lashley’s engram search are adjacent traditions; Hull differs by formalizing habit as a persistent nervous organization rather than a representational map.

Multi-week traps arise when this single-page logic is extrapolated across long timescales:
1. Persistence trap: Assuming κ(u) ≈ 1 over “months and years” ([PAGE 118]) ignores synaptic turnover, systems consolidation, and interference. Over weeks, a habit acquired with N reinforcements may show savings (spontaneous recovery) or complete extinction depending on context, violating monotonic H(t).
2. Index-dissociation trap: Magnitude, latency, extinction resistance, and frequency can diverge under stress, fatigue, or drug state. Treating any one as “habit strength” produces false certainty over multi-week tracking.
3. Threshold trap: “No habits mediate overt action below the reaction threshold” ([PAGE 118], citing p. 323 ff.) means that even a strong H(t) yields R(t)=0 if τ shifts upward under arousal, anxiety, or competing drives. Longitudinal studies that ignore τ drift misattribute performance loss to habit loss.
4. Credit-assignment trap: Because reinforcement is not the immediate cause, the nervous system must bind S(t_i) to the outcome at t_i + delay. Over multi-week schedules with variable delays, this binding degrades, producing superstitious or misattributed habit chains.

Thus the passage supports a short-run, laboratory-timescale model; its extrapolation to “months and years” is an assumption, not a demonstrated theorem ([PAGE 118]).
