<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
William James's discourse on will sits at a nexus of 19th-century thought. Its immediate lineage is the Scottish Common Sense Realism (Thomas Reid) and German Idealism (Kant, Hegel), which grappled with free will versus determinism. James inherits the Kantian "fact of reason"—the unconditional "ought" that presupposes freedom—but psychologizes it, grounding it in stream-of-consciousness experience rather than transcendental logic. The phrase "on the perilous edge" echoes the Romantic sublime (Burke, Wordsworth), where terror induces a pleasurable elevation of the spirit.

Scientifically, James was contemporaneous with the rise of experimental psychology (Wundt) and early neuroscience (Brodmann areas). His concept of the "education of the will" predates B.F. Skinner's operant conditioning by decades, though James emphasized internal habit formation ("association of ideas") rather than external reinforcement schedules. The genealogy branches into two 20th-century streams: (1) Humanistic psychology (Maslow, Rogers), which valorized self-actualization and personal responsibility as James did; and (2) Cognitive neuroscience of decision-making, which maps his "effort" onto dopamine signaling in striatum and metabolic cost in prefrontal cortex.

A multi-week trap for the modern reader is **presentism**: interpreting James through the lens of contemporary "growth mindset" (Carol Dweck) or "self-determination theory" (Deci & Ryan). These frameworks reduce will to motivational orientation, missing James's metaphysical seriousness. For James, the "dumb responses" of consent/non-consent are "our deepest organs of communication with the nature of things" [PAGE 579]—a radical phenomenological claim that volition is a fundamental category of reality, not just a psychological variable. Another trap is the **neuroscientific reductionism** trap: equating the "will operator" with fMRI activations. While correlations exist, James's point is normative and experiential; the feeling of effortful choice is constitutive of moral agency, regardless of its neural substrates. Historically, James was writing in reaction to psychological hedonism (Bentham) and strict physiologism (Mill), arguing for a non-reductive mental causation. His genealogy thus serves as a warning against flattening rich, first-person concepts into third-person data points.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In crisis situations—be it personal trauma, existential dread, or collective catastrophe—the "perilous edge" narrows to a knife's edge. James's model predicts a regression in recursive theory of mind. Normally, individuals maintain a hierarchical simulation: "I believe that I believe that I should act." Under acute stress, this collapses into a zero-order, stimulus-bound state: the "weaker brethren" who are "laid low" [PAGE 579]. Their capacity for "deliberate" religion or morality (both dependent on will, per James) vanishes. They cannot sustain the effort \(e\) required to meet the threshold \(\theta\) of any future-oriented question.

This regression manifests as either passive withdrawal (non-consent to all propositions) or frantic, unreflective action (consent driven by affect, not deliberation). Both bypass the "education of the will," which relies on reflective association of ideas. The counter-trap is the emergence of the "prophet" or heroic exemplar whose "unshaken countenance" provides an external anchor. Observers do not merely copy the prophet's actions; they vicariously access the prophet's higher-order state: "He believes he should act, and therefore I too can believe I should." This is a second-order emulation that bootstraps the observer's collapsed recursion back online. The prophet's will becomes their will because the social feedback loop temporarily supplies the missing internal scaffolding.

The clock here is physiological and attentional. Sustained focus on a single intention (the "zest" to face deterrents) consumes working memory resources. In crisis, these resources are hijacked by threat monitoring. The window for deliberate will may last only seconds before being overwhelmed. Thus, James's "every hour of the day" [PAGE 579] probing question becomes impossible to answer reflectively; it must be answered by ingrained habit (the product of past education) or by borrowing from a stronger will. This dynamic explains why charismatic leadership is so potent in crises—the leader performs the sustained volitional effort for the group, allowing followers to allocate cognitive resources to survival tasks while still participating in the collective "yes."
