<SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>
The paper uses four families of operators: shooting efficiency, turnover/possession security, efficiency differential, and league-relative standardization/correlation. The source does not always state the formulas, so I separate “source-stated” from “standard operator required to make the source claim checkable.”

1. True Shooting Percentage. The standard operator is
\[
\mathrm{TS\%}=100\cdot \frac{\mathrm{PTS}}{2\left(\mathrm{FGA}+0.44\,\mathrm{FTA}\right)}
\]
where \(\mathrm{PTS}\) is points scored, \(\mathrm{FGA}\) is field-goal attempts, and \(\mathrm{FTA}\) is free-throw attempts. Units: percent. The 0.44 coefficient approximates the share of free-throw attempts that end a possession; it is a convention, not a physical constant. Assumptions: points include two- and three-point field goals and free throws; the denominator approximates true shooting possessions; league rules and scoring environment are comparable across the two seasons. Breakdown regimes: denominator \(=0\) gives an undefined TS%; very small samples produce unstable percentages; rule changes in free-throw and three-point environments break cross-season comparability. The source gives “Season 2017-2018: mean: 57.56%; SD: 1.96%; z-score: 0.563” and “Season 2021-2022: mean: 55.99%; SD: 2.42%; z-score: 0.459” [PAGE 8]. It then says “we cannot conclude Liaoning Flying Leopard is the qualified for the championship on the scale of True Shooting Percentage(TSP)” [PAGE 8], because both z-scores are “with in 2” [PAGE 8]. That is a soft screen, not a formal qualification criterion.

2. z-score standardization. The source reports z-scores but not the formula. The standard form is
\[
z_s=\frac{X_s-\mu_s}{\sigma_s},
\]
where \(X_s\) is the team’s TS% in season \(s\), \(\mu_s\) is the league mean TS% in season \(s\), and \(\sigma_s\) is the league standard deviation. Units: dimensionless. Assumptions: the league distribution is meaningfully summarized by mean and SD; the team is compared to the same league population; the z-scores are not rounded too aggressively. Breakdown regimes: \(\sigma_s=0\) makes \(z_s\) undefined; heavy skew or multimodality makes a z-score misleading; a “within 2” rule is not a ranking test. Using the reported values, the implied team TS% under the league-reference interpretation is
\[
\mathrm{TS}_{2017}\approx 57.56+0.563(1.96)=58.66\%,
\]
\[
\mathrm{TS}_{2021}\approx 55.99+0.459(2.42)=57.10\%.
\]
This is a derived implication, not a source-stated team value. The difference is about 1.56 percentage points, while the z difference is about 0.104.

3. Turnover/possession-security operator. The paper lists “Turnover Rate” [PAGE 2] and “Turnover ratio Analysis” [PAGE 6], but the extracted pages contain only headings and no formula or values. A standard box-score turnover percentage is
\[
\mathrm{TOV\%}=100\cdot \frac{\mathrm{TOV}}{\mathrm{FGA}+0.44\,\mathrm{FTA}+\mathrm{TOV}},
\]
with \(\mathrm{TOV}\) as turnovers, denominator as estimated possessions used, and units percent. An alternative is turnovers per possession,
\[
\mathrm{TOV}_{\mathrm{per\ possession}}=\frac{\mathrm{TOV}}{\mathrm{Poss}},
\]
with units turnovers per possession. The source does not state which operator it used. Assumptions for either: possessions are consistently estimated; team and opponent pace are comparable or adjusted; garbage-time and lineup composition are controlled. Breakdown regimes: denominator \(=0\) gives undefined rate; different possession estimators are not directly comparable; a “ratio” and a “rate” can differ by a factor of 100 if one is a fraction and one is a percent. Because pages 6–7 provide no numeric comparison, the turnover channel cannot verify or veto the flexibility claim in [PAGE 15].

4. Offensive efficiency, defensive efficiency, and efficiency differential. The standard operators are
\[
\mathrm{OE}=100\cdot \frac{P}{\mathrm{Poss}},\qquad
\mathrm{DE}=100\cdot \frac{P_{\mathrm{opp}}}{\mathrm{Poss}},\qquad
\Delta E=\mathrm{OE}-\mathrm{DE},
\]
where \(P\) is points scored, \(P_{\mathrm{opp}}\) is points allowed, \(\mathrm{Poss}\) is possessions, and \(\Delta E\) has units of points per 100 possessions. Assumptions: possessions are consistently counted; opponent strength and pace are adjusted or held comparable; garbage time is excluded or modeled. Breakdown regimes: \(\mathrm{Poss}=0\) is undefined; different pace and opponent distributions can reverse \(\Delta E\); a league-wide efficiency shift can change the meaning of a raw differential. The source states “The 2020-21 Leopards had a lower offensive efficiency, but their defensive efficiency got better, thereby, having a higher efficiency differential” [PAGE 14], but the extracted text does not give the numeric differentials. The paper’s own condition is: “if the efficiency differencial for 2017 to 18 is higher than the 2021-22 season, we have to account for the competitiveness of the seasons. But if not, then the conclusion will be pronounce” [PAGE 13]. Without the actual values, that condition is unresolved in the source.

5. Pearson correlation. The source reports “r=-0.45 for 2018, r=-0.69 for 2022” [PAGE 11]. The standard operator is
\[
r=\frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}
{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2}},
\]
where \(x_i\) is offensive efficiency and \(y_i\) is defensive efficiency for team \(i\). Units: dimensionless, \(r\in[-1,1]\). Assumptions: linear association; teams are exchangeable draws from the league; the defensive-efficiency sign convention is fixed; outliers do not dominate. Breakdown regimes: zero variance in either variable makes \(r\) undefined; restriction of range attenuates \(r\); nonlinearity or clustering makes \(r\) misleading; correlation is not causation. The paper calls the association “moderately strong negative” [PAGE 11], but it does not state whether “defensive efficiency” means points allowed per 100 possessions or a higher-is-better defensive rating. That coding convention changes the interpretation of the negative sign.

Information/probability note: the source contains no information-theoretic operator. If one later models late-clock action choices as a probability distribution \(p(a)\), \(p(a)\in[0,1]\) is dimensionless, and Shannon entropy \(H(p)=-\sum_a p(a)\ln p(a)\) has units of nats in the natural-log convention. That would be a diagnostic addition, not a source-reported quantity. Time variables, such as shot-clock seconds, are in seconds; probability variables are dimensionless.

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
Crisis habit regression is the tendency of players and coaches, under high leverage or late-clock pressure, to fall back on the most practiced action patterns rather than the full tactical menu. In this source, the 2017/2018 roster had a structural reason to regress: “Dejun Han had no substitute in 2018, the only substitute was Meng Wei, which is far less capable than Dejun Han. As a result, the team had to make one of the forward Xiaoxu Li play 5” [PAGE 15]. That is a habit-forming constraint. When the game tightens, the available habit is to keep Han on the floor, protect him from foul trouble, and use Li out of position. The 2022 roster, by contrast, “got Rongzhen Zhu as a substitute which made the team more flexible in game” [PAGE 15], so the crisis policy set is larger: play Han, play Zhu, or use a smaller lineup without immediately collapsing the interior. The paper’s phrase “the leap in defensive efficiency outweighs the lack of fire power” [PAGE 15] is a claim about that larger policy set, but it is not a measurement of which policy was actually chosen in close games.

Counter-trap reasoning is the recursive response: the opponent knows the Leopards’ preferred lineup and designs a trap. If the 2022 team uses “single guard lineups” [PAGE 15], the opponent can pressure the primary ballhandler, force an early pass, and test the secondary creator. The paper’s turnover pages are headed “Turnover ratio Analysis” [PAGE 6] and [PAGE 7], but the extracted text gives no turnover values. So the counter-trap is an open adversarial channel: flexibility on defense may coexist with a ball-security cost on offense, and without the turnover data the net effect cannot be adjudicated. The recursive theory of mind is: coach A expects coach B to trap the single guard; coach B expects coach A to counter with a second handler; coach A then weighs defensive mobility against turnover risk. The paper asserts the defensive side wins [PAGE 15], but the missing turnover operator is exactly the evidence that would test the opponent’s best response.

Clock state is the third layer. Late-clock possessions change the optimal action distribution: shooting efficiency becomes noisier, turnovers become more costly, and offensive rebounding or foul-drawing may shift. The 9-0 playoff run [PAGE 4] is a clutch outcome, but nine games are a small sample for estimating a stable late-clock policy. A recursive account would distinguish (i) the team’s baseline habit, (ii) the opponent’s counter-trap, and (iii) the shot-clock state that determines whether a single-guard lineup can safely initiate. The source provides the roster mechanism [PAGE 15] and the playoff outcome [PAGE 4], but not the possession-level clock data. Thus the paper’s conclusion is best read as a plausible crisis-policy hypothesis: more lineup options reduce habit regression, but the counter-trap and clock costs remain unmeasured.
