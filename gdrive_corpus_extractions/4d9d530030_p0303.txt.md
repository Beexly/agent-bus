<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
Tolman’s 1932 account sits at a crossroads between early functionalism and later cognitive behaviorism. The immediate lineage runs through William James’s notion of instinct and habit as organized physiological discharge, through John Dewey’s treatment of activity undertaken for its own immediate satisfaction, and through Edward Thorndike’s instrumental framework, which Tolman partially rejects by insisting that play and esthetic responses are not merely means to a later appetite ([PAGE 1]). The “unspent energy” concept anticipates drive-reduction models associated with Hull, but Tolman’s addition of a selective environmental object—picture, concert, hills, swim, puzzle—makes the account proto-cognitive: the organism is not passively discharged but is described as “set[ting] out to find” an appropriate object ([PAGE 1]).

Scientific descendants include intrinsic-motivation and curiosity models in reinforcement learning, where an agent seeks novelty or skill-acquisition not because of an extrinsic reward but because the act itself reduces an internal uncertainty or competence gap. In contemporary predictive-processing terms, unspent energy can be read as a precision-weighted free-energy imbalance whose resolution requires sensory or motor sampling. The historical trap is multi-week: if one treats “play” as a stable, separable reinforcement channel across days or weeks, one ignores Tolman’s own embedding clause ([PAGE 1]). Over a multi-week horizon, appetite states drift; a faculty that is easily stimulated on day one may be suppressed on day fourteen by a dominant aversion (threat, hunger, social conflict). Scheduling “play breaks” or “esthetic exposure” as if they were thermodynamically independent of the animal’s momentary metabolic neutrality is a category error. Another trap is the reverse: assuming that because play is embedded in other appetites, it has no independent regulatory function. Tolman’s argument requires both: play is embedded, yet it performs a local fatigue/disequilibrium correction when the object is available and the organism is neutral.
