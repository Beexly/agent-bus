interpreted as small (\(C=.10\)), medium (\(C=.30\)), or large (\(C=.50\)) [PAGE 3]. SMOTE generates a synthetic minority sample by convex interpolation between a minority instance \(x_i\) and a randomly chosen minority neighbor \(x_z\):
\[
\tilde{x} = x_i + \lambda (x_z - x_i), \qquad \lambda \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1),
\]
applied before the stratified 70/30 split [PAGE 6]. The Random Forest is an ensemble of \(n_{\text{estimators}}=300\) CART trees with \(\text{max\_depth}=20\), \(\text{min\_samples\_leaf}=2\), \(\text{min\_samples\_split}=2\), and \(\text{bootstrap}=\text{False}\) [PAGE 6]. Classification performance on the original test set is summarized by
\[
\text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = 0.18,\qquad
\text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN+FP} = 0.96,\qquad
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP+TN}{N} = 0.88,
\]
with AUC \(= 0.78\) (fair) versus AUC \(= 0.99\) (excellent) on the resampled set [PAGE 6 Table 4]. ROC area is formally
\[
\text{AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} \operatorname{TPR}\!\bigl(\operatorname{FPR}^{-1}(u)\bigr)\,du,
\]
bounded in \([0,1]\). SHAP attribution for feature \(j\) is
\[
\phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|N|-|S|-1)!}{|N|!}
\Bigl[f_{S\cup\{j\}}(x_{S\cup\{j\}}) - f_S(x_S)\Bigr],
\]
allocating the change in model output to each predictor [PAGE 6]. Units throughout are dimensionless counts (transitions, passes, players), minutes on the match clock, and unitless ratios for AUC, Cohen's Kappa (range \([-1,1]\)), and class probabilities.

Breakdown/singularity regimes: (i) when \(\pi_{+} < 0.15\) and \(N_{+} < 500\), the problem enters the sparse-minority regime where SMOTE manifold interpolation may invent synthetic bridges across the majority-class decision boundary; (ii) when resampled-test AUC approaches 1.0 while original-test recall remains \(\le 0.20\), the diagnostic indicates leakage or overfitting to synthetic support rather than genuine signal [PAGE 6]; (iii) when a bivariate factor returns \(p > .05\) yet is narratively asserted as causal (Period of Match, 76–90 minutes), the inferential chain suffers a significance–narrative rupture [PAGE 7; PAGE 10].
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The study's methodological lineage is multi-generational. Observational typology (nomothetic, punctual, multi-dimensional) traces to Anguera's observational designs in sport psychology [PAGE 12 refs 29, 32], and the instrument construction follows consensual-agreement reliability protocols with Cohen's Kappa benchmarked against Fleiss et al. [PAGE 5; PAGE 12 ref 33]. The classifier descends directly from Breiman's Random Forest (2001) [PAGE 12 ref 35], while the explainability layer implements Lundberg & Lee's SHAP (2017) [PAGE 12 ref 39]. Domain-specific predecessors in women's football include Iván-Baragaño et al.'s multivariate model of offensive-phase success probability [PAGE 1 ref 13; PAGE 12 ref 13], Casal et al.'s goalkeeper-participation analysis in Spain's La Liga [PAGE 12 ref 22], Scanlan et al.'s goal-scoring-opportunity work at FWWC 2015 [PAGE 12 ref 45], and Bradley's conditional-demand benchmarking at FWWC 2023 [PAGE 12 refs 8, 9].

Two multi-week retro-causal traps are visible. First, the authors attribute the apparent success of 76–90 minute transitions to "physical and mental fatigue towards the end of a match" while admitting this mechanism "was not directly analysed in the present study" [PAGE 10]; the causal link is imported from prior work [PAGE 10 refs 22, 46] without within-study measurement of load, sprint decay, or decision-error rates. Second, match-status effects are treated inconsistently across the literature base: FWWC 2015 evidence suggests match status modifies offensive strategy [PAGE 1 ref 15], whereas FWWC 2019 evidence finds consistent offensive strategies regardless of match status [PAGE 1 ref 16]. This study finds a bivariate association for Match Status (\(p < .005\)) but ultimately excludes it from the final model [PAGE 7; PAGE 10], leaving an unresolved historical contradiction about whether scoreboard state is a driver or a downstream artifact of transition success.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
From a recursive theory-of-mind perspective, the model's outputs interact with coaching cognition in three nested layers. Crisis habit regression: when a team is losing, the observed team may regress to habitual, higher-risk verticality; the model codes this as Direct Attack or Counterattack [PAGE 4 Table 1] but cannot distinguish calculated transition from panic-induced desperation, so the KPI "counterattack" may encode a crisis state rather than a repeatable skill. Counter-trap: the opponent's low defensive position (LW) functions as a defensive trap that surrenders space in exchange for compactness; the offensive counter-trap is \(\ge 3\) penetrating passes, which the SHAP analysis identifies as the most impactful predictor [PAGE 6; PAGE 7]. However, once this KPI is publicized through elite coaching networks, opposing defenses will adapt by compressing the middle third and reducing penetrable channels—a Goodhart-style counter-adaptation that decays the indicator's predictive value over a multi-week tactical cycle. Clock: the 76–90 minute window is treated as a fatigue-sensitive exploitation zone, but because Period of Match is not statistically significant (\(p > .05\)) [PAGE 7], the "clock" effect may be a selection artifact of match status rather than a temporal law.

Most critically, the original-test recall of 0.18 means the model fails to flag 82% of actual successful transitions [PAGE 6 Table 4]. A coaching staff recursively relying on this model for pre-match transition planning would systematically miss the majority of offensive successes while being overconfident in specificity (0.96) driven by class imbalance. The recursive trap is therefore not merely statistical but institutional: low recall institutionalizes false negatives in scouting, while high specificity creates false confidence that the absence of predicted KPIs implies defensive safety.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
Physical layer veto: The dataset contains no GPS, accelerometer, or player-load telemetry [PAGE 11 limitations]. The fatigue-mediated explanation for late-match transition success is therefore unverifiable within this study [PAGE 10], and the model cannot condition on physical state variables such as cumulative high-intensity distance or sprint count. Biomechanical layer veto: Penetrative Passes and Type of Initial Attack (Positional, Direct, Counterattack) are coded qualitatively from broadcast video by human observers [PAGE 4 Table 1; PAGE 5 Table 1]; there are no data on pass velocity, spin, receiver-to-defender spacing, or interception probability, so the biomechanical quality of a "penetrating pass" is inferred rather than measured. Weather layer veto: Table 1 includes no meteorological covariates (ambient temperature, precipitation, wind, pitch moisture) despite the sample spanning outdoor finals-stage matches in Australia/New Zealand (FWWC 2023), England (UEFA Women's Euro 2022), and UEFA Champions League venues [PAGE 2; PAGE 4 Table 1]. Adjudication: each layer independently vetoes real-time, in-game deployment of the model. The admissible scope is restricted to post-hoc notational review at elite level, where video-coded categories are stable and match context is fixed after the fact.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
