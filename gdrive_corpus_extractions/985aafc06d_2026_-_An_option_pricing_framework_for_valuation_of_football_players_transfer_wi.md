<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The model is a recursive decision problem in which the club knows that future offers will arrive with intensity \(\nu\), that contract expiration is fixed at \(T\), and that a release clause makes an offer mandatory at \(K\) [PAGE 3–4]. The club’s current decision therefore depends on its forecast of its own future acceptance threshold and on the acquiring club’s willingness to trigger the clause. In the baseline regime, “voluntary sellouts are not optimal (‘rational retention’)” [PAGE 1], because the continuation value of retaining a productive player exceeds \(L\). But as \(T-t\to0\), the continuation value collapses and the acceptance region expands; the club’s optimal future self becomes more willing to accept \(K\). This is the “clock” component: the deadline effect is not a change in the player’s talent but a contraction of the remaining horizon. The paper supports this by showing that the gap between constrained and unconstrained contracts narrows near expiration and that the counterfactual expiration experiment with \(T=102\) drives late opportunity cost to zero [PAGE 14, PAGE 18, Table 3]. In recursive-belief terms, the club correctly anticipates that near-expiration it will have less bargaining leverage; the release clause then transfers control to the first arriving offer rather than to the club’s later self.

The “crisis habit regression” risk is that observers mistake the deadline effect for panic or short-term performance chasing. The paper’s counterfactual evidence argues the opposite: “the deadline effect is driven by horizon contraction rather than short-term performance fluctuations” [PAGE 1]. A club that regresses to a static DCF habit near the window will underprice the option value of timing; a club that ignores the clock and extrapolates recent performance will over-retain. The “counter-trap” is the release clause itself: a low \(K\) combined with high \(\nu\) creates a danger zone in which frequent offers force the club to surrender high-performing players at a capped fee [PAGE 11–12]. The prescribed counter-trap is either to set a prohibitively high \(K\)—the safety zone where the clause is non-binding—or to refuse the clause for high-\(\mu\), high-\(\nu\) profiles [PAGE 12]. But there is a recursive caveat: the model treats \(\nu\) as exogenous and constant, while the authors acknowledge that in reality \(\nu\) likely rises with performance \(Y\) and falls with \(K\) [PAGE 15]. If so, the true opportunity cost for high performers is even larger than modeled, and the safety-zone effectiveness of a high \(K\) is even stronger. Conversely, if release clauses are renegotiable or heterogeneous, the hard-cap assumption overstates the opportunity cost [PAGE 15]. A second-order theory-of-mind issue is that the acquiring club, player, and agent all know the clause; the model does not solve a bargaining equilibrium, so it should be read as an institutional-constraint baseline rather than a full strategic game [PAGE 15]. The clock has two levels—the window close and the contract expiration—and the source distinguishes intra-window deadline pressure from inter-window jumps [PAGE 8]. The correct recursive posture is therefore: retain rationally while continuation value is high; anticipate that your future threshold will rise as the clock contracts; and treat the release clause as a transfer of future control to offer arrivals.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The model’s physical/biomechanical/weather layer is deliberately thin. Assumption A4 states that injuries occur at stochastic times \(\theta_k\sim\mathrm{Erlang}(k,\lambda)\), independent of \(Y_t\), and cause “a temporary loss of value equivalent to \(m\) matches \((mY)\)” [PAGE 3]. The generator contains no weather state, no pitch state, no travel state, no biomechanical load state, and no permanent-injury state. The paper explicitly says that injury risk “does not generate the structural regime shifts analyzed in this paper”; varying injury parameters “shifts the overall value level but leaves the qualitative properties of the optimal thresholds and the deadline effect unchanged” [PAGE 12]. Therefore the formal veto proof is straightforward: if a proposed valuation claim depends on weather, pitch condition, travel, fixture congestion, or a biomechanical load variable that changes \(Y_t\), \(\lambda\), or \(m\), the claim is outside the model’s state space. The model admits only \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\{\mathcal F_t\},Y_t)\) with independent offer and injury clocks [PAGE 3–4]; there is no \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable weather or load term in \(\mathcal L_Y\). A weather event that postpones matches or changes the transfer-window calendar is a calendar veto; a biomechanical event that causes a permanent injury is an injury-structure veto because A4 is temporary; a physical event that changes the player’s performance process from GBM to a jump or mean-reverting process is a stochastic-process veto. In each case, the correct adjudication is to reject the model-implied value as unsupported rather than to reinterpret the GBM as if it priced that layer. The only cross-layer variables the model can absorb are shifts in \(\lambda\), \(m\), \(\rho\), or \(\nu\), and even then only as exogenous parameter changes, not as endogenous physical mechanisms [PAGE 3, PAGE 12, PAGE 15]. This is a proof of scope, not a proof of empirical adequacy: the model is a contract-timing baseline, and any physical, biomechanical, or weather mechanism that violates the independence or temporary-loss assumptions vetoes the institutional-timing interpretation.
