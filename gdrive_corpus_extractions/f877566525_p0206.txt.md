The foundational operators extracted from Halmos (1950) [PAGE 1] define the probability space as a specific instance of a measure space. Let $(X, S, \mu)$ be a totally finite measure space such that $\mu(X) = 1$. Here, $X$ is the sample space, $S$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $X$, and $\mu: S \to [0,1]$ is the probability measure.

The primary equation governing stochastic independence for a class of sets $\mathcal{E} \subseteq S$ is defined as follows. The sets in $\mathcal{E}$ are independent if for every finite subclass of distinct sets $\{E_1, E_2, \dots, E_n\} \subseteq \mathcal{E}$, the following equality holds:

$$ \mu\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} E_i\right) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \mu(E_i) $$

For the specific case of two sets $E, F \in \mathcal{E}$, this reduces to the familiar product rule:

$$ \mu(E \cap F) = \mu(E) \cdot \mu(F) $$

Units: The measure $\mu$ is dimensionless, representing a normalized mass or probability density integrated over the domain. In the illustrative example provided by Halmos [PAGE 1], $X$ is the unit square with Lebesgue measure, $X = \{(x,y): 0 \le x \le 1, 0 \le y \le 1\}$. The sets $E = \{(x,y): 0 \le x \le 1, a \le y \le b\}$ and $F = \{(x,y): c \le x \le d, 0 \le y \le 1\}$ represent horizontal and vertical strips. Their independence arises because the Lebesgue measure on the plane is the product of the Lebesgue measures on the axes: $d\mu(x,y) = dx \, dy$. Thus, $\mu(E) = b-a$, $\mu(F) = d-c$, and $\mu(E \cap F) = (b-a)(d-c)$, satisfying the independence equation exactly.

Breakdown Regime: The definition provided by Halmos [PAGE 1] applies to finite subclasses. The text explicitly warns: "it is not sufficient for the independence of the sets of a class $\mathcal{E}$ (even if $\mathcal{E}$ is a finite class) that any two distinct sets of $\mathcal{E}$ be independent." Mathematically, one can construct a measure space with three sets $A, B, C$ such that $\mu(A \cap B) = \mu(A)\mu(B)$, $\mu(B \cap C) = \mu(B)\mu(C)$, and $\mu(A \cap C) = \mu(A)\mu(C)$, yet $\mu(A \cap B \cap C) \neq \mu(A)\mu(B)\mu(C)$.
