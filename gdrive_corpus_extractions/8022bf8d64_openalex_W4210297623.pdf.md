<THINK_TRACE>
The source document is an epidemiological Bayesian inference study, not a predictive gambling artifact. Its core object is the count of diagnosed concussions per NFL game. The modeling chain begins with a dependent variable that is a non-negative integer count, a set of game-level covariates, a negative binomial likelihood to accommodate overdispersion, and a Bayesian posterior that yields probability statements about incidence rate ratios. The deep derivation is therefore: define the data-generating count, define the linear predictor on the log scale, define prior distributions, define the posterior, and then transform posterior draws into incidence rate ratios. The source explicitly states that the authors “performed a retrospective cohort analysis of eight NFL seasons” and that “negative binomial regression models were developed to determine the relationship between risk factors and the number of concussions within a game” [PAGE 4], [PAGE 7]. The count is the number of diagnosed concussions in a game, with “1096 diagnosed concussions were identified in 1830 games” [PAGE 2]. That denominator is important because the model is game-level, not player-minute-level unless exposure is implicitly held constant across games. The paper does not include an explicit player-exposure offset in the supplied text; it treats each game as the observational unit. That assumption is reasonable for an initial ecological model but creates a gray zone because roster size, participation rates, snap counts, and injury reporting can vary across games and teams.

The statistical model can be reconstructed canonically as a negative binomial regression with a log link. Let \(y_i\) be the number of diagnosed concussions in game \(i\), where \(i=1,\dots,1830\). Let \(G_i\) be a binary surface indicator, with \(G_i=1\) for natural grass and \(G_i=0\) for artificial turf. Let \(W_i\) be centered week of season, and let \(Z_i\) be standardized temperature. The paper says the intercept corresponds to artificial turf at mid-season and average temperature: “The model intercept represents the log of the average of number of concussions in a game played on artificial turf in the middle of the season (between weeks 8 and 9) and at an average temperature (17.1 degrees Celsius)” [PAGE 7]. It also says temperature was modeled after standardization: the results refer to “each one standard deviation (corresponding to 7.9oC) increase/decrease in temperature” [PAGE 10]. Thus a natural formalization is:
\[
y_i \mid \mu_i,\kappa \sim \text{NegBin}(\mu_i,\kappa),
\]
\[
\log \mu_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 G_i + \beta_2 W_i + \beta_3 Z_i + \beta_4 G_i W_i + \beta_5 G_i Z_i.
\]
The parameters \(\beta_j\) are dimensionless log-rate coefficients, or log-count effects per game. Their exponentiated versions are incidence rate ratios, which are also dimensionless. The model includes two interactions because the authors argue that surface material properties may change with week and temperature: “the two-way interactions of surface × week and surface × temperature were included in the model” [PAGE 7]. The posterior probability statements are then computed by counting posterior draws where an incidence rate ratio is below or above 1.0. The source says: “We exponentiated each sample of the model parameters to compute the incidence rate ratio of all of the predictive variables in our model” [PAGE 8]. This is why the results are expressed as probability that risk is reduced or increased, not as a binary significance decision.

The gray-zone audit must separate statistical estimation, physical mechanism, and diagnostic observation. The estimated grass main effect is a 0.78 incidence rate ratio with a 95% credible interval of 0.68 to 0.89 at reference conditions [PAGE 22]. The paper reports “>99% probability that concussion risk was reduced on grass surface” [PAGE 2]. However, the coefficient is conditional on the model, the priors, the covariate centering, and the exclusion of hybrid surfaces. The authors excluded hybrid games because “games played on hybrid surfaces (n=82) were excluded from analysis” [PAGE 5]. They also restricted analysis to weeks 1–16 because “only the first 16 weeks of data (15 games per team) were analyzed” [PAGE 5]. Those exclusions narrow the inferential target. The posterior probability does not mean there is a 99% probability of causation in a physical sense; it means that, under the model and priors, the posterior mass for the grass coefficient is below zero on the log scale. Causal interpretation requires additional assumptions: no unmeasured confounding, stable measurement, consistent diagnostic practice, and a mechanism linking surface properties to head impact biomechanics. The source itself acknowledges diagnostic and reporting uncertainty: “it is possible that concussion reporting / diagnosis is biased in either direction by some of these risk factors” [PAGE 15].

Temperature and week are strongly seasonally correlated. The paper notes that “these two factors are substantially correlated and should be considered together using methods to disentangle their associations” [PAGE 3]. The Bayesian model includes both main effects and interactions, but collinearity remains a numerical and interpretive issue. The sensitivity analyses attempt to address this: indoor-only games are used to control temperature, and late-season games are used to examine temperature without week [PAGE 8]–[PAGE 9]. Those analyses are useful because they provide partial counterfactual checks. If week remained predictive in indoor games where temperature is nearly constant, that supports an independent within-season effect. If temperature remained predictive in weeks 13–16, that supports an independent temperature effect within a narrow seasonal window. The source says the sensitivity analyses “confirmed both as independent predictors” [PAGE 11]. Still, the model cannot fully separate biological fatigue, cumulative impact exposure, field degradation, diagnostic drift, and reporting behavior because those variables are not directly measured.

The priors also matter. The authors set the intercept prior to “normal with mean -0.92 (ln 0.4 = -0.92) and with standard deviation 0.3” [PAGE 7]. They set other coefficients with “normal priors” centered at zero and standard deviation 2.5 [PAGE 7]. They claim these induce a fairly broad prior on average concussions per game [PAGE 7]–[PAGE 8]. The posterior diagnostics show strong computational convergence: “all of the R-hat statistics were close to 1.0” and traceplots did not show mixing issues [PAGE 9]. Table 2 reports R-hat values of 1.00, large effective sample sizes, and Monte Carlo standard errors below 0.001 for the reported parameters [PAGE 22]–[PAGE 23]. Therefore, the numerical inference appears stable. The deeper vulnerability is not Markov chain convergence but model specification: omitted variables, exposure denominators, diagnostic bias, sparse cells, and extrapolation beyond observed game conditions. The model is strongest where data are dense, such as mid-season and common outdoor temperature ranges. It is weaker at extreme cold, warm outliers, and unobserved playoff conditions. The source explicitly states that playoff injury reports are incomplete: “accurate injury reports are not available for teams which fail to advance to the next round of playoffs” [PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16]. That prevents a full late-season inference into the highest-risk period.

where \(y_i\) is the number of diagnosed concussions in game \(i\). The unit of \(y_i\) is concussion events per game. The source states: “1096 diagnosed concussions were identified in 1830 games” [PAGE 2].

2. Negative binomial sampling model.
A negative binomial model is appropriate because concussion counts may be overdispersed relative to a Poisson model. In a common NB2 parameterization:
\[
y_i \mid \mu_i,\kappa \sim \text{NegBin}(\mu_i,\kappa),
\]
\[
\mathbb{E}[y_i \mid \mu_i] = \mu_i,
\]
\[
\text{Var}(y_i \mid \mu_i) = \mu_i + \frac{\mu_i^2}{\kappa}.
\]
Here \(\mu_i\) is the expected number of diagnosed concussions per game, with units of events per game. The dispersion parameter \(\kappa>0\) controls excess variance. As \(\kappa \to \infty\), the negative binomial approaches a Poisson model. As \(\kappa\) becomes small, overdispersion becomes severe. The probability mass function can be written as:
\[
p(y_i \mid \mu_i,\kappa)
=
\frac{\Gamma(y_i+\kappa)}{\Gamma(\kappa)y_i!}
\left(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+\mu_i}\right)^{\kappa}
\left(\frac{\mu_i}{\kappa+\mu_i}\right)^{y_i}.
\]
This formula is consistent with the source statement that the number of concussions “is well-represented by a negative binomial distribution” [PAGE 6]–[PAGE 7] and that “negative binomial regression models were developed to determine the relationship between risk factors and the number of concussions within a game” [PAGE 7].

3. Linear predictor and covariate definitions.
Let:
\[
G_i =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{natural grass},\\
0 & \text{artificial turf},
\end{cases}
\]
\[
W_i = \text{centered week of season},
\]
\[
Z_i = \frac{T_i - 17.1}{7.9},
\]
where \(T_i\) is game temperature in degrees Celsius. The source says the average temperature is approximately \(17.1^\circ\text{C}\) and one standard deviation is approximately \(7.9^\circ\text{C}\) [PAGE 7], [PAGE 10]. The linear predictor is:
\[
\log \mu_i
=
\beta_0
+
\beta_1 G_i
+
\beta_2 W_i
+
\beta_3 Z_i
+
\beta_4 G_i W_i
+
\beta_5 G_i Z_i.
\]
The coefficient \(\beta_0\) is the log expected concussion count for artificial turf at reference week and reference temperature. The source states: “The model intercept represents the log of the average of number of concussions in a game played on artificial turf in the middle of the season (between weeks 8 and 9) and at an average temperature (17.1 degrees Celsius)” [PAGE 7].

4. Prior distributions.
The source reports informative priors:
\[
\beta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(-0.92, 0.3^2),
\]
\[
\beta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2.5^2), \quad j=1,\dots,5.
\]
The intercept prior is motivated by earlier work: “we specified the prior distribution on the model intercept to be normal with mean -0.92 (ln 0.4 = -0.92) and with standard deviation 0.3” [PAGE 7]. The other priors are centered at zero because earlier Poisson regression analyses did not find clear associations [PAGE 7]. The prior predictive checks are described as inducing a broad prior on average concussions per game [PAGE 7]–[PAGE 8].

5. Posterior distribution.
Let \(\theta = (\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3,\beta_4,\beta_5,\kappa)\). The posterior is:
\[
p(\theta \mid y)
\propto
\left[\prod_{i=1}^{1830} p(y_i \mid \mu_i(\theta),\kappa)\right]
p(\theta).
\]
Because the posterior is analytically intractable, the authors use Markov chain Monte Carlo: “Since the posterior distribution is analytically intractable, we rely on Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation to approximate these quantities” [PAGE 8]. They ran multiple chains and retained posterior draws after warmup [PAGE 8].

6. Incidence rate ratios.
For a coefficient \(\beta_j\), the incidence rate ratio is:
\[
\text{IRR}_j = \exp(\beta_j).
\]
The source says: “We exponentiated each sample of the model parameters to compute the incidence rate ratio of all of the predictive variables in our model” [PAGE 8]. Because the model includes interactions, several effects are conditional:
\[
\text{Grass main effect at reference week and temperature}
=
\exp(\beta_1).
\]
\[
\text{Temperature effect on artificial turf}
=
\exp(\beta_3).
\]
\[
\text{Temperature effect on grass}
=
\exp(\beta_3+\beta_5).
\]
\[
\text{Week effect on artificial turf}
=
\exp(\beta_2).
\]
\[
\text{Week effect on grass}
=
\exp(\beta_2+\beta_4).
\]
Table 2 reports median coefficients and incidence rate ratios, including grass surface \(\beta=-0.25\), IRR \(0.78\), 95% credible interval \(0.68\) to \(0.89\) [PAGE 22]. It reports week IRR \(1.02\), temperature IRR \(0.85\), grass × week IRR \(1.02\), and grass × temperature IRR \(1.11\) [PAGE 22]–[PAGE 23].

7. Posterior probabilities.
The authors compute posterior probabilities that an incidence rate ratio is harmful or beneficial:
\[
\Pr(\text{IRR}_j > 1 \mid y)
\]
and
\[
\Pr(\text{IRR}_j < 1 \mid y).
\]
The source reports “>99% probability that concussion risk was reduced on grass surface,” “>99% probability that concussion risk was lower at higher temperatures,” and “>91% probability that concussion risk increased with each week of the season” [PAGE 2].

8. Units, information scale, and probability scale.
- \(y_i\): dimensionless count per game.
- \(\mu_i\): expected count per game.
- \(\beta_j\): log-count effects per game; dimensionless on the log scale.
- \(\text{IRR}_j\): dimensionless multiplicative rate ratio.
- \(T_i\): degrees Celsius.
- \(Z_i\): dimensionless standard deviation units.
- Posterior log density: measured in nats, because natural logarithms are used in the model and source formula \(\ln(0.4)=-0.92\) [PAGE 7].
- Posterior probabilities: dimensionless numbers in \([0,1]\).

9. Breakdown and singularity regimes.
A formal model audit must identify regimes where inference breaks down.
\[
\mu_i \to 0
\]
causes the log rate to approach \(-\infty\). This is a singularity in the linear predictor if no regularization or offset prevents extreme negative values. In count data, very low expected counts produce unstable relative comparisons.

\[
\kappa \to \infty
\]
reduces the negative binomial to Poisson. If overdispersion is real, this misspecification underestimates uncertainty.

\[
\kappa \to 0^+
\]
creates extreme overdispersion and can make the likelihood numerically unstable. The model may place excessive mass on rare high-count events.

If week and temperature are highly collinear, the Fisher information matrix can become near singular. In Bayesian terms, the posterior may still be proper because of priors, but the likelihood may weakly identify separate coefficients. The source acknowledges correlation and uses sensitivity analyses [PAGE 3], [PAGE 8]–[PAGE 9].

If a covariate value lies outside the observed support, predictions are extrapolations. For example, extreme cold combined with late week and artificial turf may be sparsely observed. The source notes substantial uncertainty at higher temperatures for Week 9 and overlap in credible intervals [PAGE 25].

If diagnostic probability depends on surface, week, or temperature, then the outcome variable is not a pure physical event count. The source acknowledges this possibility: “it is possible that concussion reporting / diagnosis is biased in either direction by some of these risk factors” [PAGE 15]. In measurement-error terms, observed \(y_i\) is a noisy, potentially biased realization of true concussion events \(y_i^*\). If \(\Pr(\text{diagnosis} \mid \text{true impact})\) varies by surface or weather, the coefficients estimate diagnosed concussion risk, not necessarily underlying biomechanical risk.

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The retro-causal genealogy of this manuscript is the lineage of hypotheses, datasets, prior studies, and unresolved mechanisms that converged into the 2026-style vault source. The immediate scientific ancestry is NFL concussion epidemiology, playing-surface biomechanics, Bayesian count modeling, and injury-reporting bias literature. The paper itself traces several threads. It says concerns about neurodegenerative disease prompted policies to reduce sport-related concussions and identifies football as important because of “the potential risk of chronic traumatic encephalopathy” [PAGE 3]. That clinical concern creates the policy pressure behind surface, temperature, and season analyses.

The first lineage is surface epidemiology. The source notes that artificial turf is often marketed as reducing concussion risk, but the NFL Players Association argues turf is detrimental to player safety [PAGE 3]. It cites prior work: an analysis of 2012–2013 NFL data found no association, a 2019 systematic review found slightly decreased concussion risk on artificial surfaces, and two American football studies found no difference or greater risk on natural grass [PAGE 3]. This conflicting past is the reason the new study uses a larger sample and a Bayesian interaction model. The paper says its large sample and model provide “a high-level of confidence in our results, which conflict with some existing literature with smaller sample sizes” [PAGE 12]. Thus the current source is retro-causally positioned as a corrective to underpowered or categorical analyses.

The second lineage is temporal epidemiology. Earlier work found increased concussion risk in the second half of the season and in colder weather [PAGE 3]. Because the NFL season begins in late summer and ends in early January, week and temperature are strongly correlated. The authors explicitly state that these factors “are substantially correlated and should be considered together using methods to disentangle their associations” [PAGE 3]. This is a key trap: a naive analysis might attribute late-season risk to temperature, or cold-weather risk to seasonal progression. The current model attempts to disentangle them through main effects, interactions, and sensitivity analyses [PAGE 7]–[PAGE 9]. The retro-causal genealogy therefore includes the earlier literature’s failure to separate seasonal, thermal, and surface effects.

The third lineage is biomechanical surface science. The source says the likely mechanism is helmet-to-ground impact biomechanics. It reports that helmet-to-ground impacts account for a meaningful fraction of concussions, citing 16% to 22% in earlier NFL video reviews and 18% in a later two-season study [PAGE 13]. It then states: “Grass tears in relation to translational force, whereas artificial turf does not – which allows the latter to have greater force transmission between the player and the surface” [PAGE 13]. It also cites laboratory reconstruction showing that helmet-to-ground impact severity is “highly sensitive to the compliance and frictional properties of the surface” [PAGE 13]. This lineage supplies the physical plausibility needed for the surface coefficient. Without it, the statistical association would remain purely epidemiological. With it, the surface coefficient can be interpreted as consistent with a mechanical pathway, though not definitively proven as the sole cause.

The fourth lineage is material degradation and weather physics. The source notes that both surfaces degrade over a season and that this can influence friction and energy absorption [PAGE 13]. It also says surface temperature influences shock absorption properties of artificial turf and soil hardness/grass quality of natural fields [PAGE 13]. Artificial surface temperature is also influenced by solar radiation, which varies with season [PAGE 13]. This lineage creates the interaction hypotheses. The model’s surface × week term reflects possible within-season field degradation. The surface × temperature term reflects possible temperature-dependent material compliance. The source says these factors may explain why previous surface literature conflicts: “The evidence that week and temperature interact with surface effects may also explain conflicting findings within the literature regarding the role of playing surface in athletic injuries” [PAGE 12].

The fifth lineage is diagnostic and reporting behavior. The outcome is not all concussions but diagnosed concussions. The source says concussions are under-diagnosed and that diagnosis depends on visible signs and honest reporting [PAGE 14]–[PAGE 15]. It notes that headache and dizziness are common symptoms and that some concussed players do not exhibit visible signs [PAGE 15]. It also cites survey evidence that a nontrivial fraction of collegiate players would not intend to report symptoms [PAGE 15]. This creates a multi-week trap: if reporting behavior changes over the season, the week effect may partly reflect disclosure propensity rather than physical injury generation. The source warns: “if players or medical staff believe that artificial turf increases concussion risk, it is possible that they are more likely to suspect/report/diagnose a concussion after a helmet-to-ground impact on artificial turf than a similar impact on natural grass” [PAGE 14]. This is a surveillance or ascertainment trap. It does not invalidate the model, but it changes the causal target from total concussion burden to diagnosed concussion burden.

The sixth lineage is data construction. The dataset combines Football Outsiders injury reports and Frontline Concussion Watch data [PAGE 4]. It excludes hybrid surfaces, practice concussions when identifiable, and week 17 missing injury reports [PAGE 4]–[PAGE 5]. Temperature is assembled from Pro-Football-Reference, official NFL reports, team media guides, and Weather Underground when necessary [PAGE 6]. Indoor games are generally assigned 20–22°C, with 20°C used when missing [PAGE 6]. Retractable-roof ambiguity leads to exclusion of eight games [PAGE 6]. These choices are genealogical because they determine which causal question is being answered. The question is not “what is the physical concussion risk of every NFL field in all conditions?” but “what is the diagnosed concussion risk across 1830 regular-season games under the coding and imputation rules used here?”

The multi-week traps are therefore: seasonal correlation between week and temperature; field degradation over time; player fatigue or cumulative impact exposure; diagnostic drift; reporting incentives; missing playoff data; hybrid surface exclusion; temperature imputation; and sparse cells at extreme conditions. The manuscript’s strength is that it confronts several of these traps directly through interaction terms and sensitivity analyses. Its limitation is that some traps remain partially unresolved because the necessary mechanistic or reporting variables are not observed.
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
The recursive theory-of-mind layer concerns how players, medical staff, coaches, statisticians, and the authors model one another’s beliefs, incentives, and reporting behavior. The outcome variable is “diagnosed concussion,” which is a social-clinical event, not a purely physical event. A diagnosed concussion occurs only if an impact is perceived, evaluated, reported, suspected, examined, and recorded. Therefore, any surface, week, or temperature effect may operate through two channels: physical injury generation and diagnostic detection. The source acknowledges this: “it is possible that these apparent risk factors may not be purely physical/biological, but may also reflect bias in suspecting, reporting, or diagnosing concussions” [PAGE 4].

Crisis habit regression refers to the tendency of actors under stress to fall back on habitual interpretations. In this context, if players or medical staff habitually associate artificial turf with danger, they may be more likely to attribute post-impact symptoms to concussion on turf. The source explicitly states: “if players or medical staff believe that artificial turf increases concussion risk, it is possible that they are more likely to suspect/report/diagnose a concussion after a helmet-to-ground impact on artificial turf than a similar impact on natural grass” [PAGE 14]. This is a recursive belief loop. Belief about turf risk increases suspicion. Increased suspicion increases diagnosis. Increased diagnosis produces epidemiological evidence that turf is riskier. The evidence then reinforces the belief. The model cannot fully break this loop without blinded adjudication, video review, or biomarker data.

Crisis habit regression can also operate across the season. As playoff races intensify, players may underreport symptoms to remain available. The source notes that NFL players diagnosed with concussions are more likely to face salary reductions and release [PAGE 14]. That creates an incentive to hide symptoms. However, medical staff may become more vigilant in high-stakes games, or conversely may face pressure to return star players. The direction of bias is not fixed. The model therefore estimates a net observed association, not a pure exposure-response function. The posterior probability statements should be read as statements about diagnosed events under the observed social system.

The counter-trap is the possibility that researchers overcorrect. If the authors believe turf is harmful, they may construct a model that detects harm. The manuscript includes several features that reduce this concern: prior specification is described and justified, posterior predictive checks are mentioned, sensitivity analyses are run, and the authors report uncertainty rather than only point estimates [PAGE 7]–[PAGE 9]. Still, counter-trap awareness requires asking whether the model’s design makes the turf-harm conclusion more discoverable. The model includes surface × week and surface × temperature interactions because the authors have prior mechanistic reasons to expect them [PAGE 7]. Those interactions are not uniformly strong: the posterior probability for grass × week is “>75%” and for grass × temperature is “>84%” [PAGE 2]. The main surface effect is much stronger, with “>99% probability” [PAGE 2]. This pattern suggests the authors are not manufacturing an interaction when the main effect is already strong, but the interpretation still depends on model assumptions.

The clock is the seasonal and biological timing structure. The source says risk increases later in the season and in colder weather [PAGE 2], [PAGE 12]. It also says extending the NFL season may increase concussion risk [PAGE 12], [PAGE 16]. The clock operates through several possible mechanisms: cumulative sub-concussive impacts, fatigue, reduced neuromuscular control, field wear, colder material properties, and diagnostic drift. The source mentions cumulative impact exposure and changes in biological threshold as possible biological explanations [PAGE 4], [PAGE 14]. It also mentions field degradation: “Both surfaces degrade over the course of the season, which can influence friction and energy absorption” [PAGE 13]. It notes that natural grass may lose protective effects later in the season due to soil compaction and reduced grass density [PAGE 14]. The clock therefore is not just calendar time; it is a latent accumulation of physical, biological, and observational changes.

A recursive decision model can be described without inventing a toy ratio. Let \(D_i\) be diagnosis, \(I_i\) be impact severity, \(R_i\) be reporting probability, and \(V_i\) be medical vigilance. The observed event is approximately a function of true injury and detection:
\[
D_i \approx f(I_i, R_i, V_i, \text{protocol}_i).
\]
If \(R_i\) or \(V_i\) varies by surface or week, the observed count conflates injury and detection. The source’s own limitation section supports this: “concussion recognition / diagnosis is highly (but not entirely) dependent on honest reporting from players” [PAGE 15]. This is the recursive layer’s central caution. The Bayesian model is coherent as a diagnosis-risk model, but it is not a complete injury-generation model unless reporting and diagnosis are conditionally independent of covariates.

The counter-trap and clock also affect intervention interpretation. If the main effect is driven by reporting bias, switching surfaces may not reduce true concussions proportionally. If the week effect is driven by cumulative biological vulnerability, extending the season could be harmful even if surface effects are small. If the temperature effect is driven by material stiffness, indoor climate control could reduce risk independent of surface. If the effect is driven by diagnostic drift, protocol standardization may change the measured association without changing underlying injury. Therefore, the recursive theory-of-mind audit recommends interpreting the model’s coefficients as conditional observational effects, not as isolated causal multipliers. The strongest claim supported by the supplied text is that diagnosed concussion risk is higher on artificial turf in this dataset and model, especially under colder and later-season conditions, while acknowledging that reporting and diagnosis may contribute to the observed pattern.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
The cross-layer veto adjudication asks whether physical, biomechanical, or weather constraints can veto a causal claim derived from the statistical model. The statistical layer says artificial turf is associated with higher diagnosed concussion risk, with median grass IRR 0.78 and >99% posterior probability of reduction relative to artificial turf [PAGE 2], [PAGE 22]. The biomechanical layer asks whether a plausible physical pathway exists. The weather layer asks whether temperature effects are physically mediated or merely seasonal correlates. The adjudication is conditional: physical mechanisms can support the statistical association, but they do not prove it unless the necessary mechanical inequalities are observed.

First, the surface-veto condition. A surface effect can be physically plausible if the surface changes impact severity. The source states: “Grass tears in relation to translational force, whereas artificial turf does not – which allows the latter to have greater force transmission between the player and the surface” [PAGE 13]. It also says helmet-to-ground impacts can involve high angular velocities and accelerations, and that impact severity is sensitive to compliance and friction [PAGE 13]. A formal physical veto can be stated in terms of impact energy and head kinematics. Let \(m\) be effective head mass, \(v\) linear velocity, \(I\) head rotational inertia, and \(\omega\) angular velocity. Translational kinetic energy is:
\[
E_{\text{lin}} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2.
\]
Rotational kinetic energy is:
\[
E_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2} I \omega^2.
\]
If a surface increases the fraction of impact energy transmitted to the head, or increases tangential impulse during helmet-ground contact, it can increase concussion risk. Tangential impulse is:
\[
J_t = \int F_t \, dt,
\]
where \(F_t\) is tangential force and \(t\) is time in seconds. If artificial turf produces greater \(J_t\) than grass for comparable impacts, the surface-veto condition is not satisfied; instead, the physical mechanism supports the turf association. The source’s statement that grass tears while turf does not is a qualitative mechanism for reduced energy dissipation on turf. However, the supplied text does not provide field measurements of \(J_t\), coefficient of friction, or compliance for the exact NFL surfaces studied. Therefore, the physical layer supports plausibility but does not independently prove the magnitude of the 0.78 grass IRR.

Second, the temperature-veto condition. Temperature can affect concussion risk if it changes surface stiffness, friction, or impact absorption. The source says surface temperature influences shock absorption properties of artificial turf and soil hardness/grass quality of natural fields [PAGE 13]. A formal material-stiffness condition can be written as:
\[
k = k(T),
\]
where \(k\) is effective surface stiffness and \(T\) is temperature in degrees Celsius. If \(k\) increases as temperature decreases, the surface may transmit more force or reduce contact time. Peak acceleration can be approximated by:
\[
a_{\text{peak}} \approx \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t},
\]
where \(\Delta v\) is change in velocity in meters per second and \(\Delta t\) is contact time in seconds. If cold artificial turf reduces \(\Delta t\) or increases \(a_{\text{peak}}\), the physical layer supports the cold-turf interaction. The source reports that “the risk of concussion seems to increase substantially on artificial turf in colder temperatures” [PAGE 10]–[PAGE 11]. But weather veto remains because the model does not directly observe surface stiffness, infill condition, moisture, or helmet-ground friction. Temperature is a proxy. Therefore, any claim that temperature itself is the causal variable must be weakened to “temperature is associated with conditions that may alter surface mechanics.”

Third, the week-veto condition. Week of season can affect risk through field degradation, cumulative biological effects, or reporting changes. The source says both surfaces degrade over the season and that this can influence friction and energy absorption [PAGE 13]. It also says natural grass may lose protective effects later in the season because of soil compaction and reduced grass density [PAGE 14]. A physical degradation veto would require showing that surface mechanical properties remain stable across weeks. If field hardness, traction, or compliance changes materially with week, then the week effect cannot be dismissed as purely biological. Conversely, if surface properties are stable and concussion risk still rises, a biological or reporting mechanism becomes more plausible. The source cannot fully adjudicate this because it lacks direct field measurements. It says it was “unable to quantify field ‘wear’ given different environmental conditions” [PAGE 15]. Therefore, the week effect remains partially ambiguous.

Fourth, the weather-data veto. The model uses game temperature, but not precipitation, humidity, wind chill, surface moisture, or pre-game weather history. The source notes that “pre-existing precipitation influence the surface even if game-time weather as dry and weather can vary considerably within a game” [PAGE 15]. This is a weather veto: temperature alone may be an incomplete physical descriptor. If the apparent temperature effect is actually moisture or prior precipitation, then the model’s temperature coefficient is a proxy for unmeasured surface condition. The statistical layer still estimates an association, but the physical mechanism is underdetermined. The cross-layer adjudication therefore vetoes any claim that the temperature coefficient is a pure causal effect of air temperature on concussion risk. It only supports a conditional claim: colder game conditions are associated with higher diagnosed concussion risk, especially on artificial turf, possibly through material property changes.

Fifth, the diagnostic-layer veto. Even if physical mechanisms exist, the observed outcome may be filtered through diagnosis. The source says diagnosis depends on visible signs and reporting, and that some concussions lack visible signs [PAGE 15]. If diagnostic sensitivity is higher on artificial turf because observers expect turf to be dangerous, the surface coefficient may be inflated. The source explicitly warns of this possibility [PAGE 14]. A diagnostic veto would require showing that diagnostic probability is invariant to surface, temperature, and week. The supplied text does not provide that proof. Therefore, the strongest admissible conclusion is that the model identifies risk factors for diagnosed concussion, not necessarily for all concussions. The source itself says: “we are confident that our model identifies risk factors for diagnosed concussions, but it is unknown if it is representative of all concussions” [PAGE 15].

The final adjudication is a hierarchy of vetoes. If surface mechanics are identical across grass and turf, the surface effect is physically vetoed. If temperature does not alter surface mechanics, the temperature effect is physically vetoed. If field wear is negligible across weeks, the week effect is physically vetoed as a surface-degradation mechanism. If diagnosis is invariant to surface and week, reporting bias is vetoed. The source’s evidence does not fully satisfy all veto conditions. It supports physical plausibility through surface biomechanics and temperature-dependent material properties, but it lacks direct mechanical measurements and lacks blinded diagnostic validation. Therefore, the cross-layer verdict is: the statistical association survives as a diagnosed-concussion association; the physical mechanism survives as plausible but not fully proven; the weather mechanism survives as conditional on unmeasured surface properties; and the diagnostic bias layer remains an unresolved alternative explanation.

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
The RLVR-style counterfactual veto audit asks whether verifiable predictions from the fitted model can falsify specific causal claims. The reward signal is not betting return; it is posterior predictive consistency with observed or held-out data. A claim is vetoed if the model’s predicted direction, magnitude, or credible interval contradicts the counterfactual scenario. The source supplies enough numerical predictions to construct three verifiable veto scenarios.

Scenario 1: Warm early-season surface veto.
Claim to test: “Artificial turf is safer than grass in warm early-season conditions.”
Model prediction: For an early-season Week 1 game at warm weather, the source reports “a concussion rate of 0.37 per game on natural grass and 0.48 per game on artificial turf” [PAGE 11]. This prediction corresponds to a grass-to-turf rate difference:
\[
\Delta_{\text{grass-turf}} = 0.37 - 0.48 = -0.11
\]
concussions per game, with grass lower than turf. If a counterfactual dataset or held-out warm early-season sample showed turf rates materially lower than grass beyond posterior uncertainty, the claim that warm early-season conditions reverse the surface effect would be supported. But if the observed pattern matches the source prediction—grass lower—then the claim “turf is safer in warm early-season conditions” is vetoed. The verifiable test is:
\[
\Pr(\mu_{\text{turf}} < \mu_{\text{grass}} \mid T=25^\circ\text{C}, W=1) < \alpha,
\]
with \(\alpha\) chosen as a small posterior tail probability. The source’s predicted values suggest this probability should be low for grass being worse, because the predicted turf count is higher.

Scenario 2: Cold late-season amplification veto.
Claim to test: “Cold weather eliminates the turf disadvantage or makes grass equally risky.”
Model prediction: For late-season Week 16 cold weather at \(5^\circ\text{C}\), the source reports “concussion rates of 0.72 and 0.96 per game, on the respective surfaces” [PAGE 11], where the order corresponds to natural grass then artificial turf. The difference is:
\[
\Delta_{\text{turf-grass}} = 0.96 - 0.72 = 0.24
\]
concussions per game, with turf higher. The source also says: “For games as cold as 1.5oC, our model is quite certain that there will be more concussions on artificial turf than grass” [PAGE 25]. A counterfactual veto arises if a cold late-season sample shows no turf elevation or a grass elevation beyond posterior predictive uncertainty. The verifiable prediction is:
\[
\Pr(\mu_{\text{turf}} > \mu_{\text{grass}} \mid T=5^\circ\text{C}, W=16) \gg 0.5.
\]
If out-of-sample counts at cold late-season turf games do not exceed grass games, the cold-amplification claim is vetoed. If they do exceed grass, the claim survives. This scenario is especially important because it tests the interaction term rather than the main effect. The source’s Figure 3 legend supports the conditional claim: cold temperatures exacerbate turf risk, while uncertainty is larger at warmer Week 9 temperatures [PAGE 25].

Scenario 3: Temperature-confounding veto using indoor games.
Claim to test: “The surface association is entirely explained by temperature, because turf games occur in colder or more variable conditions.”
Model prediction: The authors ran sensitivity analyses on indoor games to control temperature without including it as a parameter [PAGE 8]–[PAGE 9]. They also ran models restricted to moderate outdoor temperatures [PAGE 9]. The source says these models “found similar IRRs for week and its interaction with surface as the main model” and that a late-season temperature model “confirmed both as independent predictors” [PAGE 11]. The verifiable counterfactual is:
\[
\text{If } \Pr(\text{surface effect} \mid \text{indoor or temperature-controlled sample}) \le \tau,
\]
then the surface effect is not robust and the temperature-confounding claim survives. But if the indoor or moderate-temperature model still yields a surface coefficient below zero with substantial posterior probability, the temperature-confounding claim is vetoed. The source’s reported sensitivity analyses support the latter: the surface association is not wholly removed when temperature is constrained. Therefore, the claim “surface effect is only a temperature artifact” is vetoed, subject to the limitation that indoor games may differ in reporting, stadium, team quality, and diagnostic practices.

A formal RLVR reward can be written as:
\[
R =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if posterior predictive sign and magnitude match held-out counts},\\
0, & \text{if held-out counts contradict the predicted sign beyond uncertainty}.
\end{cases}
\]
For scenario 1, the predicted sign is \(\mu_{\text{grass}} < \mu_{\text{turf}}\) at \(T=25^\circ\text{C}, W=1\). For scenario 2, the predicted sign is \(\mu_{\text{turf}} > \mu_{\text{grass}}\) at \(T=5^\circ\text{C}, W=16\). For scenario 3, the predicted sign is persistence of the surface coefficient after temperature control. The source provides point predictions and probability statements that make these tests operational. However, the reward must be computed against the same outcome definition: diagnosed concussions. If the outcome were all concussions, including undocumented events, the counterfactual predictions would need recalibration because reporting bias could change the observed counts.

The RLVR audit also identifies falsification limits. If the dataset has sparse cold-turf late-season games, the posterior predictive interval may be too wide to veto a false claim. The source notes uncertainty at extreme temperatures and limited samples at certain conditions [PAGE 25]. If the counterfactual sample includes playoff games, the source says valid injury reports are unavailable, so the model cannot be tested there [PAGE 15]–[PAGE 16]. If hybrid surfaces are introduced, the model must be expanded because the source excluded hybrid games [PAGE 5]. If new artificial turf products have different infill or shock pads, the surface coefficient may not transport unchanged.
