The derivation begins from the paper’s explicit distinction between time-optimal stabilization and its own object. The paper states: “In many references, by optimal stabilization it is meant time-optimal stabilization, i.e. finding a control that steers a system to the origin in minimum time. However, here, our goal is substantially different.” [PAGE 1] The first multi-hop move is therefore to replace “reach the origin as fast as possible” by an infinite-horizon pair-selection problem: choose an equilibrium \(\xi\in S\), choose an admissible arc \(x(\cdot)\to \xi\), and minimize a discounted running cost plus an endpoint/equilibrium penalty. The source explicitly notes that “The value of the optimal cost depends not only on the equilibrium point, but also on the particular trajectory driving the system to this point.” [PAGE 1] That sentence is the central nonstandard feature: the terminal object \(\xi\) is a decision variable, not a fixed terminal state.

The second hop is the convergence constraint. The paper says that \(x(t)\to \xi\in S\) if “for some \(\gamma>0\), … \(e^{\gamma s}\|x(s)-\xi\|ds\) is finite for all \(t\in[\tau,\infty)\).” [PAGE 2] This is much stronger than ordinary asymptotic convergence. If \(\|x(t)-\xi\|\approx C e^{-\beta t}\), then the weighted integral is finite only when \(\beta>\gamma\). If \(\beta\le\gamma\), the integral diverges even though \(x(t)\to\xi\) in the usual sense. Thus the source’s “convergence” condition is an exponential-rate admissibility condition with a free rate \(\gamma\), and the paper acknowledges that “the choice of \(\gamma\) is dependent on the specific application being considered” [PAGE 2]. This is a gray zone: the paper does not state a universal rate theorem; the user of the theory must supply \(\gamma<\beta\).

The third hop is the penalized problem. The constrained infinite-horizon problem is replaced by Eq. (6), adding \(\int_{\tau+l}^{\infty} e^{\gamma s}\|x(s)-\xi\|ds\). The paper claims: “when \(l\to\infty\) we expect that … will go to zero, thus recovering the original optimization problem without the explicit restriction” [PAGE 2]. For an admissible exponentially convergent arc, the tail indeed tends to zero. For a merely asymptotically convergent arc with subexponential decay, the penalty is infinite, so the penalized formulation is not equivalent to the original verbal constraint; it is a stronger surrogate. That is the first true breakdown regime.

The fourth hop is the HJB layer. The Hamiltonian is
\[

\]
and the HJB equation is \(\varphi_t-H(t,x,-\varphi_x)=0\) [PAGE 2]. Because endpoint state constraints and nonsmoothness are present, the paper uses proximal subgradients: a lower semicontinuous \(v\) is a proximal solution if, whenever \(\partial^P v(t,x)\neq\varnothing\),

\eta_0-H(t,x,-\eta)=0\quad\forall(\eta_0,\eta)\in\partial^P v(t,x).
\]
This is the paper’s substitute for classical differentiability [PAGE 3]. The gray zone is that the condition is vacuous where the proximal subgradient is empty; the theory does not force a classical gradient to exist.

The fifth hop is the verification-function theorem. The local verification function uses the inequality
\[
\eta_0+\min_{u\in\Omega}\{\langle\eta,f(x,u)\rangle+e^{-\delta t}f_0(x,u)\}\ge0

for all proximal subgradients, together with a terminal lower-bound condition and a matching condition at \((\tau,z)\). Theorem 1 states that existence of such a function implies a strong local minimizer, and conversely under a boundedness hypothesis [PAGE 3]. The converse is not local-only: it requires boundedness of \(|g(\xi)|+|\int f_0|\) over all admissible processes, a strong global condition that is easy to overlook.
