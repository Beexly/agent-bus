The source is a methodological econometrics paper, not an event data feed. Its central object is a static binary incomplete-information game with \(I\) players, public payoff shifters \(X\), private types \(U=(U_1,\ldots,U_I)\), and binary actions \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\). The first multi-hop derivation is: if player \(i\)'s equilibrium strategy is monotone in her private type, then because the payoff \(\pi_i(Y_{-i},X)-U_i\) for action 1 is decreasing in \(U_i\), the strategy can be written as a threshold rule \(Y_i=\mathbf{1}\{U_i\le u_i^*(X)\}\) [PAGE 9]. At the cutoff, if the cutoff is interior, indifference gives \(\sum_{a_{-i}}\pi_i(a_{-i},x)\sigma_{-i}^*(a_{-i}|x,u_i^*(x))-u_i^*(x)=0\) [PAGE 9]. Because \(\alpha_i(x)=E[Y_i|X=x]=F_{U_i|X}(u_i^*(x)|x)\), the threshold is the \(\alpha_i(x)\)-quantile of the type distribution. The joint action probabilities are then generated by the copula of the joint type distribution evaluated at the marginal choice probabilities. This is the bridge from game theory to observables: the game's structural content is pushed into the copula \(C_U\), the quantile functions \(Q_{U_i}\), and the payoff functions \(\pi_i\).

The second hop is the rationalization hierarchy. Model \(M_1\) assumes Assumptions R and M and a single monotone pure-strategy Bayesian Nash equilibrium. Proposition 1 states that \(M_1\) rationalizes any distribution of \(Y\) given \(X\) except for structural zeros: [PAGE 14] says \(P(Y=a|X=x)=0\) implies \(P(Y_i=a_i|X=x)=0\) for some \(i\). The paper puts this starkly: [PAGE 5] “In a fully nonparametric setting, our baseline discrete game model is the most general one and closest to that considered in game theory. We show that such a model imposes essentially no restrictions on the distribution of players’ choices.” Therefore, \(M_1\) is not a testable restriction in any ordinary sense and is not nonparametrically identified; [PAGE 17] states “Proposition 4. M1 is not identiﬁed nonparametrically.” Adding exogeneity \(X\perp U\) gives \(M_2\), whose observable restrictions are R1–R3 in Proposition 2 [PAGE 14]: the joint choice probability depends on \(X\) only through the marginal choice probabilities, is strictly increasing in those marginals except at zero coordinates, and is continuously differentiable. Adding mutual independence of types gives \(M_3\), whose sole observable restriction is conditional independence of actions given \(X\) [PAGE 15]: “A conditional distribution FY|X can be rationalized by M3 if and only if Y1, · · · , YI are conditionally independent given X.” Thus the hierarchy is \(M_1\supset M_2\supset M_3\), but the observable restrictions collapse in a precise direction: exogeneity is testable, while exogeneity and monotonicity become redundant once mutual independence and a single equilibrium are imposed [PAGE 16].

The third hop is identification. In \(M_2\), the copula is identified on the extended support by the joint-choice probability conditional on the marginal choice probabilities, \(C_U(\alpha)=E\{\prod_i Y_i|\alpha_j(X)=\alpha_j,\forall j\in\{i:\alpha_i\ne1\}\}\) [PAGE 18]. The equilibrium belief at the margin is identified by differentiating that joint probability with respect to player \(i\)'s marginal choice probability: \(\sigma_{-i}^*(a_{-i}|x,u_i^*(x))=\partial P(Y_i=1,Y_{-i}=a_{-i}|\alpha(X)=\alpha)/\partial\alpha_i|_{\alpha=\alpha(x)}\) [PAGE 19]. This is the local-instrumental-variables/copula derivative step. Payoffs are then identified from the indifference equation only up to location and scale, and fully identified after exclusion restrictions, rank conditions, support conditions, and a normalization. The rank condition is discontinuous: rank \(2^{I-1}-1\) means no strategic effects and a singleton marginal-choice support; rank \(2^{I-1}-2\) identifies payoffs up to location and scale [PAGE 21]. Proposition 6 extends point identification over an iteratively expanding set \(C_i^\infty\) [PAGE 24].

The gray-zone audit is where the paper becomes fragile. First, structural zeros can violate Assumption R; the rationalization result excludes distributions with zero-probability action profiles unless some marginal is also zero [PAGE 14]. Second, Assumption SC requires \(S_{\alpha(X)}\) to be a convex subset of \([0,1]^I\) with full dimension [PAGE 18]; if the support is not full-dimensional, the derivative in Lemma 2 is unavailable or non-identified. Third, exclusion restrictions can fail: without them the sharp identification region is “unbounded and quite large” [PAGE 30] and “Without exclusion restrictions, we show that the sharp identiﬁcation region of players’ payoffs is unbounded” [PAGE 35]. Notation and units. \(I\) is an integer count of players. \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) is a dimensionless action indicator. \(X\in S_X\subset\mathbb{R}^d\) is a vector of public payoff shifters in their native units. \(U_i\in\mathbb{R}\) is a private type in payoff/utility units. \(\alpha_i(x)=E[Y_i|X=x]=F_{U_i|X}(u_i^*(x)|x)\) is a probability, dimensionless and in \([0,1]\). \(\sigma_{-i}^*(a_{-i}|x,u_i^*(x))\) is a conditional probability, dimensionless. \(C_U\) is a copula on \([0,1]^I\), dimensionless. \(Q_{U_i}\) is a quantile function in utility units. The derivative \(\partial P/\partial\alpha_i\) has units of probability per probability and is dimensionless. Rank conditions are integer-valued. No information-theoretic unit is used in the paper; if one externally maps beliefs to entropy, the natural unit is nats, but that is not a primitive of the source.

Payoff and threshold operator. The source defines [PAGE 7]
\[
\Pi_i(Y,X,U_i)=
\begin{cases}
\pi_i(Y_{-i},X)-U_i, & Y_i=1,\\
0, & Y_i=0.
\end{cases}
\]
A monotone pure-strategy BNE is written as [PAGE 9]
\[
Y_i=\mathbf{1}\{U_i\le u_i^*(X)\}.
\]
The mutual-consistency/indifference equation at an interior cutoff is [PAGE 9]
\[
\sum_{a_{-i}\in A_{-i}}\pi_i(a_{-i},x)\sigma_{-i}^*(a_{-i}|x,u_i^*(x))-u_i^*(x)=0.
\]
This is a threshold-crossing equation, but the “coefficient” is an equilibrium belief, not a fixed parameter. The singularity regimes are: if \(u_i^*(x)\) is at a boundary of the support, the equality can become an inequality [PAGE 43]; if Assumption M fails, the best response may not be monotone and the threshold representation can break; Example 1’s positive-correlation regime is an explicit breakdown, with non-monotonicity for \(\rho\in(\sqrt{\pi/(2+\pi)},1)\) and uniqueness only up to \(\rho\in(-1,(\pi-2)/(2+\pi)]\) [PAGE 11].

Rationalization restrictions. For \(M_2\), Proposition 2 gives, for each \(p=2,\ldots,I\) and \(1\le i_1<\cdots<i_p\le I\) [PAGE 14],
\[
R1:\quad E\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}\mid X\right\}
=
E\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}\mid \alpha_{i_1}(X),\ldots,\alpha_{i_p}(X)\right\},
\]
\[
R2:\quad E\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}\mid \alpha_{i_1}(X)=\cdot,\ldots,\alpha_{i_p}(X)=\cdot\right\}
\text{ is strictly increasing on }S_{\alpha_{i_1}(X),\ldots,\alpha_{i_p}(X)}
\]
except at values with some zero coordinate, and
\[
R3:\quad E\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}\mid \alpha_{i_1}(X)=\cdot,\ldots,\alpha_{i_p}(X)=\cdot\right\}
\text{ is continuously differentiable on }S_{\alpha_{i_1}(X),\ldots,\alpha_{i_p}(X)}.
\]
For \(M_3\), Proposition 3 reduces the restriction to [PAGE 15]
\[
E\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}\mid X\right\}
=
\prod_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{i_j}(X).
\]
Breakdown: if \(X\) is discrete, R3 is irrelevant [PAGE 14, footnote 10]; if there are structural zeros, the baseline \(M_1\) restriction is not vacuous [PAGE 14]; if mutual independence fails, the product factorization in \(M_3\) fails; if exogeneity fails, R1–R3 can fail.

Copula and belief identification. The copula is identified on the extended support by [PAGE 18]
\[
C_U(\alpha)
=
P\{U_1\le Q_{U_1}(\alpha_1),\ldots,U_I\le Q_{U_I}(\alpha_I)\}
=
E\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{I}Y_i\mid \alpha_j(X)=\alpha_j,\ \forall j\in\{i:\alpha_i\ne1\}\right\}.
\]
Lemma 2 identifies the equilibrium belief by [PAGE 19]
\[
\sigma_{-i}^*(a_{-i}|x,u_i^*(x))
=
\left.
\frac{\partial P(Y_i=1;Y_{-i}=a_{-i}\mid \alpha(X)=\alpha)}
{\partial \alpha_i}
\right|_{\alpha=\alpha(x)}.
\]
Breakdown: this requires Assumption SC, namely [PAGE 18] “The support Sα(X) is a convex subset of [0, 1]I with full dimension, i.e. dim(Sα(X)) = I.” Without full-dimensional support, the derivative cannot be taken in all directions.

Payoff identification and rank. Lemma 3 gives two regimes [PAGE 21]: if \(\operatorname{rank}R_i(x_i)=2^{I-1}-1\), then \(S_{\alpha_i(X)|X_i=x_i}\) is a singleton \(\{\alpha_i^\dagger\}\) and \(\pi_i(\cdot,x_i)=Q_{U_i}(\alpha_i^\dagger)\); if \(\operatorname{rank}R_i(x_i)=2^{I-1}-2\), then \(\pi_i(\cdot,x_i)\) is identified up to location and scale, equivalently \(\pi_i(\cdot,x_i)-\pi_i(a_{-i}^0,x_i)\) is identified up to scale. Assumption N fixes location and scale: \(\pi_i(a_{-i}^0,x_i^*)=0\) and \(\|\pi_i(\cdot,x_i^*)\|=1\) [PAGE 23]. Proposition 6 then gives point identification on \(A_{-i}\times C_i^\infty\) and on the corresponding quantile support [PAGE 24]. Breakdown: rank below \(2^{I-1}-2\) gives weaker identification; rank equal to \(2^{I-1}-1\) implies no strategic effects; nondegenerate strategic effects require the intermediate rank and enough variation in \(X_{-i}\).

Partial identification and singular determinant. Without exclusion restrictions, the sharp identification region is characterized by hyperplanes with identified slopes and an unbounded set [PAGE 29–30]. The source states [PAGE 30] “The identiﬁcation region is unbounded and quite large.” In the iterative proof, the two-equation system for location and scale has a nonzero determinant because \(Q_{U_i}(\alpha_i')<Q_{U_i}(\alpha_i)\) [PAGE 50]. Singularity occurs if those two quantiles coincide or if the rank condition collapses; then location and scale are not separately recoverable.
</SOURCE_OPERATORS_AND_EQUATIONS>

<RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>
The paper’s lineage is a convergence of four historical strands. The first is the empirical discrete-game tradition: labor-force participation, entry, and social interactions, with the paper citing Bjorn and Vuong, Bresnahan and Reiss, Berry, Tamer, and others [PAGE 2]. The second is the incomplete-information and global-games tradition: Carlsson and Van Damme and Morris and Shin are invoked to justify correlated private information in bank runs, currency crises, and bubbles [PAGE 3]. The third is the nonparametric threshold-crossing tradition: Matzkin’s binary threshold crossing model is the single-agent ancestor, and the paper explicitly says its model “can be viewed as an extension to a game theoretic setting of traditional threshold–crossing models” [PAGE 6]. The fourth is the local-instrumental-variables/copula tradition: Heckman and Vytlacil, Carneiro and Lee, Jun, Pinkse and Xu, and Darsow, Nguyen and Olsen are used to justify the derivative-with-respect-to-propensity-score identification of beliefs at the margin [PAGE 19–20].

The retrocausal trap is that later empirical practice often treats mutual independence of private information as a harmless simplification. The paper shows that this simplification is not innocuous: \(M_3\) imposes conditional independence of actions given \(X\) [PAGE 15], and exogeneity plus monotonicity become redundant once mutual independence and a single equilibrium are imposed [PAGE 16]. A second multi-week trap is to confuse the baseline model’s flexibility with testability. Because \(M_1\) rationalizes essentially all interior choice probabilities [PAGE 5, PAGE 14], a researcher who “tests the game” without exogeneity, exclusion, support, or rank conditions is testing almost nothing. A third trap is to forget that the paper’s identification is up to location and scale unless a normalization is imposed [PAGE 21, PAGE 23]. A fourth trap is to treat the partial-identification region as small: without exclusion restrictions it is unbounded [PAGE 30, PAGE 35]. A fifth trap is to ignore multiple equilibria: the paper notes that violations of R1 or R2 can indicate multiple equilibria, and in the two-player case a failure of monotonicity of \(\alpha_j(x)\) in \(\alpha_i(x)\) is diagnostic [PAGE 32]. A sixth trap is to conflate correlated types with unobserved heterogeneity plus independent types; the latter can violate R1 and is distinguishable from \(M_2\) [PAGE 35]. A seventh trap is to assume that testing requires identification; the paper says the opposite: [PAGE 36] “It is worth noting that the above tests do not rely on identiﬁcation and consequently on the assumptions used to identify the primitives of the various models.”
</RETROCAUSAL_GENEALOGY>

<RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>
In this game, recursive theory of mind is not optional. Each player’s best response depends on her belief about rivals’ actions conditional on her own private type: \(\sigma_{-i}^*(a_{-i}|x,u_i)\). When private information is correlated, the paper states [PAGE 4] “when private information is correlated, the BNE solution concept requires that each player’s beliefs about rivals’ choices depend on her private information, thereby invalidating the usual two–step identiﬁcation argument and estimation procedure.” That is the formal core of the recursive belief problem: player \(i\) reasons about \(Y_{-i}\) using \(U_i\), and the researcher must recover that type-dependent belief rather than assume it away.

The “crisis habit regression” is the fallback behavior of an analyst under stress: impose mutual independence, run a two-step estimator, and interpret the first-stage marginal choice probabilities as if they were sufficient. Under correlated types, that habit is exactly what the paper identifies as invalid. The “counter-trap” is the opposite overcorrection: try to infer the full joint type distribution directly from actions without controlling for the equilibrium belief at the margin. That also fails because the joint action probability is a copula evaluated at the marginals, and the derivative with respect to the marginal identifies the belief only under support and single-equilibrium conditions [PAGE 18–19]. The “clock” is the source of exogenous variation that breaks the trap: either repeated play of the same game across time, which can justify a single-equilibrium assumption [PAGE 31], or an instrument \(Z\) that does not affect payoffs, type distributions, or the equilibrium set but changes equilibrium selection [PAGE 33]. In that clock logic, the observed distribution is a mixture over equilibria; variations in \(Z\) move the mixture along a convex hull, and the endpoints can identify the component equilibrium distributions [PAGE 33]. Thus the recursive model has three failure modes: habit regression under correlated types, counter-trap overfitting of the joint distribution, and absence of a clock that separates equilibrium selection from structural variation.
</RECURSIVE_THEORY_OF_MIND>

<CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>
There is no physical, biomechanical, or weather layer in the source. The source is an abstract game-theoretic econometric model; its layers are the payoff layer \(\pi_i\), the type layer \(F_{U|X}\), the equilibrium layer \(\sigma_{-i}^*\), and the observable layer \(F_{Y|X}\). A physical, biomechanical, or weather variable can enter only in one of three ways. First, it can be a component of the public payoff shifter \(X\). Then the model is conditional on it, and identification requires the support condition SC [PAGE 18], the exclusion restriction ER [PAGE 20], and the rank conditions in Lemma 3 [PAGE 21]. If the physical variable is common to all players, it may fail to provide the player-specific exclusion variation that ER needs; the source notes that \(X_i\) can have common variables due to homophily, and then results hold by conditioning on those common variables [PAGE 20, footnote 15]. Second, it can be publicly observed by players but not by the researcher. That is the unobserved-heterogeneity case in Section 5.4, and the paper shows that such a model with independent types can violate R1 and can be distinguished from \(M_2\) [PAGE 34–35]. Third, it can affect only equilibrium selection, not payoffs or the type distribution; then it is an instrument \(Z\) in the Henry–Kitamura–Salanié sense [PAGE 33].

The veto proof is therefore conditional, not physical. A physical or weather layer cannot refute the theorem unless it changes \(X\), \(U\), \(\zeta\), \(Z\), or the equilibrium set. If it is omitted and payoff-relevant, it becomes unobserved heterogeneity and can break R1, as the paper’s two-player example with \(E(Y_1Y_2|X=x)\ne E(Y_1Y_2|X=x')\) while \(\alpha_i(x)=\alpha_i(x')\) demonstrates [PAGE 35]. If it is a biomechanical constraint on one player, it is just a component of that player’s \(X_i\); the model's identification then rises or falls on support, rank, and exclusion. If it is a weather event common to all players and also affects payoffs, it is not automatically a valid instrument; it must still satisfy the no-direct-payoff and equilibrium-selection-only conditions. Hence the correct cross-layer adjudication is: physical/weather vetoes are admissible only as data-layer restrictions, not as external overrides of the game-theoretic identification logic.
</CROSS_LAYER_VETO_ADJUDICATION>

<RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
Scenario 1: R1/R2 counterfactual under \(M_2\). Estimate \(\alpha_i(x)=E[Y_i|X=x]\) and compare the joint-choice regression \(E\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}|X\}\) with \(E\{\prod_{j=1}^{p}Y_{i_j}|\alpha_{i_1}(X),\ldots,\alpha_{i_p}(X)\}\). Verifiable veto: if the two differ at the same marginal-probability vector, or if the conditional joint probability is not strictly increasing on the support except at zero coordinates, then R1 or R2 is rejected, so \(M_2\) is vetoed [PAGE 14]. Prediction: under \(M_2\) the joint choice probability is a function of the marginals only; under unobserved heterogeneity or multiple equilibria it generally is not [PAGE 34–35, PAGE 32].

Scenario 2: support-and-rank counterfactual. Construct the empirical support \(S_{\alpha(X)}\) and estimate the rank of \(R_i(x_i)\). Verifiable veto: if \(S_{\alpha(X)}\) is not full-dimensional, the derivative identification of \(\sigma_{-i}^*\) fails [PAGE 18–19]; if \(\operatorname{rank}R_i(x_i)\) is below \(2^{I-1}-2\), payoff point identification fails [PAGE 21]. Prediction: in the failed-support/failed-rank regime, the sharp identification region is unbounded and the location-scale normalization cannot recover absolute payoff levels [PAGE 30, PAGE 35]. This scenario is verifiable by rank tests, support coverage diagnostics, and sensitivity of the estimated payoff function to normalization.

Scenario 3: multiple-equilibrium and instrument counterfactual. Under \(M_3\), test whether \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_I\) are conditionally independent given \(X\) [PAGE 15]. Verifiable veto: rejection means either mutual independence of types fails or a single equilibrium fails; the paper explicitly says conditional independence can be used to test a single equilibrium [PAGE 16, PAGE 32]. Then introduce \(Z\) that does not affect payoffs, the type distribution, or the equilibrium set but changes equilibrium selection [PAGE 33]. Prediction: if \(Z\) acts only through selection, the observed distributions lie on a convex hull in probability-distribution space; with sufficient variation, the endpoints identify the component equilibrium distributions [PAGE 33]. If the distributions do not move along such a hull, either \(Z\) violates the exclusion/selection-only condition or the single/multiple-equilibrium structure is misspecified.
</RLVR_COUNTERFACTUAL_VETOES>
